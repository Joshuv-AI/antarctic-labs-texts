Viral Sentry AI—Automated zoonotic surveillance and drug repurposing agent
Authors: Cristian R Munteanu, Jose Vázquez-Naya, Eduardo Tejera
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractZoonotic viruses capable of jumping from animal reservoirs into human populations represent a persistent and unpredictable menace to global health. To confront this challenge, we developed Viral Sentry AI, an autonomous agent designed to close the gap between viral emergence and therapeutic response. Unlike static analysis tools, Viral Sentry AI operates as a continuous sentinel, automatically scanning the National Center for Biotechnology Information public databases for new viral genomes and executing a three-stage agentic surveillance workflow, with distinct, specialized artificial intelligence architectures for generated text, macromolecule sequences, and drug chemical data. First, the system is using a Large Language Model (Gemma4) to parse unstructured submission records and extract the host information if it is not available in the dedicated field. In the second stage, the system employs a novel deep-learning topology, virsentai-v3-hyena-dna-16k, a fine-tuned HyenaDNA model capable of processing complete viral genomes up to 160 000 bases. This architecture captures subtle, long-range genomic dependencies to predict human infectivity with high precision. Upon predicting the possible human infection of the scanned viruses, the agent autonomously triggers a downstream therapeutic module as the stage three. It extracts National Center for Biotechnology Information RefSeq viral protein sequences and utilizes a pretrained Protein-Ligand Affinity Prediction Transformer model to calculate affinity interactions against 2092 ChEMBL-approved drugs, instantly identifying candidates for drug repurposing. In rigorous cross-validation on a curated dataset of 33 426 complete viral genomes, the surveillance module demonstrated robust discriminatory power, achieving an Area Under the Receiver Operating Characteristic Curve of 0.88 in classifying human host potential. By integrating state-of-the-art genomic modeling with automated lead compound screening, Viral Sentry AI offers a proactive, end-to-end research prototype for pandemic preparedness. The platform is freely accessible at https://muntisa.github.io/virsentai (source code: https://github.com/muntisa/virsentai).

Introduction

The prediction of viral spillover from animal reservoirs to humans represents a critical frontier in global public health security. The societal disruption caused by the COVID-19 pandemic served as a stark reminder that most emerging human pathogens, including coronaviruses, filoviruses, and influenza viruses, are of zoonotic origin. In principle, genomic sequencing of a novel animal virus could determine its potential to infect humans. In practice, the laboratory-based host-range determination is a resource-intensive and retrospective process. This limitation has catalyzed the development of computational models designed to rapidly assess zoonotic risk from viral sequence data alone, scanning vast genomic archives to identify potential threats.

The landscape of these predictive models reveals a clear evolutionary trajectory. Initial efforts produced powerful, yet highly specialized, tools for specific viral families. For instance, HostPredictor, a gradient boosting ensemble leveraging codon usage bias and k-mer frequencies, achieved a high area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUROC) of 0.95 for avian influenza viruses [1]. Similarly, Flu-CNN, a deep 1D convolutional neural network, demonstrated 99% accuracy in identifying the host specificity of Influenza A viruses [2]. While potent, the utility of such models is intrinsically narrowed to well-defined genomic contexts.

Subsequently, broader strategies emerged to capture more universal signatures of zoonotic potential. Foundational work by Babayan’s team [3] demonstrated that evolutionary signatures within viral genomes could effectively predict reservoir hosts, stimulating numerous subsequent investigations. This line of inquiry led to tools like VIDHOP, which applied deep neural networks to partial genomic sequences from diverse viral families, achieving high AUROCs between 0.93 and 0.98 for specific viruses like Rabies and Rotavirus [4]. Another approach, BiLSTM-VHP, utilized a bidirectional LSTM network to analyze patterns in short viral genome fragments [5]. A persistent tension exists in these approaches, balancing the precision of family-specific models against the broader applicability of pan-viral systems, which can sometimes sacrifice contextual depth. As highlighted in a recent review, model performance is highly context-dependent, with no single tool demonstrating universal optimality [6]. This fragmentation necessitates that researchers often navigate a complex ecosystem of tools, each with distinct data requirements and limitations.

The advent of large-scale, foundational language models trained on genomic data has introduced a paradigm shift. Evo 2, a scale-unifying model pretrained on 9.3 trillion base pairs from across all domains of life, exemplifies this new frontier [7]. Its StripedHyena 2 architecture can process sequence contexts up to 1 million nucleotides, enabling the recognition of long-range dependencies invisible to previous models. By encoding a deep representation of genomic grammar, Evo 2 provides latent features richly attuned to evolutionary constraints and regulatory motifs—attributes highly relevant for host-prediction tasks.

A significant constraint, however, is that Evo 2 was not trained on known human-pathogen viral genomes, an intentional precaution against misuse [7]. This exclusion prevents its direct application for predicting human infectivity without further fine-tuning. Nonetheless, the existence of such foundational models reshapes the development landscape.

Viral Sentry AI (VirSentAI) was developed to occupy this precise niche as a three AI model agent processing text, DNA and protein sequences, and drug SMILES. It is an AI-driven web service integrating a modern, large-scale sequence model with an automated surveillance platform. By fine-tuning a HyenaDNA architecture [8], VirSentAI leverages the power of large-scale pretraining to scan entire viral genomes, flag those with the highest likelihood of infecting humans. PLAPT pretrained model (https://github.com/Bindwell/PLAPT, accessed on 10 September 2025) was used to propose automatic drug repurposing for the viral proteins. The host virus was corrected with a pretrained large language model (LLM) such as Gemma 4 [9] using web search. VirSentAI is designed for accessibility, operating as an autonomous system that periodically scans public repositories like National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI) RefSeq [10] for new viral sequences and presents risk assessments and drug repurposing solutions without requiring bioinformatics expertise from the user. VirSentAI aims to streamline zoonosis surveillance by making an advanced, broadly applicable host-prediction model accessible to the wider virology and epidemiology community as a prototype tool.

Materials and methods

Our tool’s foundation rests on a blend of genomic insight and computational finesse. We subdivided the build to keep things clear. Behind the scenes, VirSentAI runs a set of automated scripts that routinely comb through the NCBI database, searching for newly released complete viral DNA sequences. These sequences are then passed into our updated prediction engine, virsentai-v3-hyenadna-16k, which estimates the likelihood that each virus could infect humans. The results are translated into ranked tables and clear visual summaries, highlighting the viruses that deserve a closer look. Dataset

The training dataset was constructed by aggregating viral genome data from three authoritative bioinformatics repositories: NCBI RefSeq [11], VirusHostDB [16], and BV-BRC [17].

Genetic data retrieval

This multi-source approach ensures comprehensive coverage of viral diversity while enabling cross-validation of metadata quality:

NCBI RefSeq Database—Viral genome sequences and associated metadata were retrieved from the NCBI RefSeq database using the official datasets command-line interface (CLI) tool. A hybrid discovery-enrichment strategy was employed to handle the large dataset (∼15 000+ complete RefSeq viral genomes) efficiently. First, taxonomy IDs for all complete RefSeq viruses were discovered using the genome subcommand. Subsequently, full metadata (including host information, sequence length, and collection dates) were fetched in batches of 100 via the virus subcommand to avoid HTTP 431 errors. Genomic sequences were downloaded in ZIP archives of 5000 records each to maximize network stability. All metadata were exported to JSONL format, and sequences were stored in FASTA format before merging into a unified TSV file, NCBI_raw.tsv. The download date was 16 April 2026.

Dataset construction for viral host classification

The curated database was processed through a multistage pipeline to generate a balanced, leakage-free training dataset optimized for viral host classification. The pipeline comprised filtering, under-sampling, clustering-based splitting, and validation steps.

Data filtering and labeling—Records from the curated SQLite database were filtered to retain only complete viral genomes with sequence lengths ≤160 000 bases (the maximum input length supported by the HyenaDNA model). Each record was assigned a binary label/class based on host information: Label 1: Host = Homo sapiens (human-infecting viruses), Label 0: All other host organisms (nonhuman viruses).

Class balancing via similarity diversity-aware under-sampling—The dataset exhibited significant class imbalance, with approximately 49 451 nonhuman records versus 16 713 human-infecting records. The Locality-Sensitive Hashing (LSH) parameters were tuned for optimal detection probability (∼98.8% for Jaccard similarity of 0.60). Thus, ds_160k_balanced.tsv was separated in training subset with 26 743 genomes, 13 372 for label 1 and 13 371 for label 0 (train_split_160k.tsv) and validation subset with 6683 genomes, 3341 for label 1 and 3342 for label 0 (val_split_160k.tsv), the inputs for the fine-tune step that will generate the new virsentai model (ver. 3). We use the sequence similarity criteria and not a taxonomy one in order to create a model that can understand genetic information, no matter the taxonomy of the virus. VirSentAI model

We built our model, virsentai-v3-hyena-dna-16k, by fine-tuning the powerful HyenaDNA foundation model (https://huggingface.co/LongSafari/hyenadna-medium-160k-seqlen-hf), a transformer-like architecture pretrained on billions of DNA bases to learn the fundamental “language” of genomics [8, 20]. This is where the architecture’s true advantage lies. Its use of convolutions with learnable long-range filters—the so-called Hyena operators—allows it to process entire viral genomes up to 160 000 bases without resorting to the fragmenting or windowing techniques that can obscure critical information (effectively replacing the costly attention mechanism of traditional Transformer network for subquadratic scaling). The result is an engine uniquely capable of capturing the subtle, distributed sequence signals, from codon biases to complex structural motifs, which correlate with human tropism.

To specialize this powerful foundation for zoonotic surveillance, we undertook a meticulous fine-tuning process. The fuel for this was a curated training dataset of 33 426 complete viral genomes, drawn from three cornerstone repositories: NCBI [21], VirusHostDB [16], and the VIPR, which has been integrated into the BV-BRC [17]. A critical step in this process was ensuring a strict balance between human and nonhuman host labels—a measure essential for mitigating classification bias. During training, each single-nucleotide tokenized sequence was fed into the model, which learned to predict a continuous human-infectivity score represented as a probability from 0 to 1.

The model was trained over 20 epochs using 16-bit mixed-precision on a single 24GB NVIDIA GPU. This entire 250-h regimen was carefully calibrated to maximize performance within the very real constraints of typical academic research infrastructure. Web server architecture

The backend logic of VirSentAI was implemented in Python, leveraging powerful libraries such as PyTorch for deep learning inference. The core function of the backend is to run the automated scanning and prediction pipeline. A lightweight SQLite database (virsentai.sqlite) is used for storing prediction results, virus metadata, and summary statistics. This choice supports the self-contained and easily deployable nature of the platform.

The frontend user interface is built with standard web technologies, including HTML, CSS and JavaScript, to ensure compatibility across all modern browsers. In addition, due to security reasons, the web shows limited results as stand-alone HTML that can run in any device and the pipeline is executed into a university computer, with more hardware resources. The main page includes the “Top Predicted Threat” virus with its host taxonomy and probability, a table of the 10 most-zoonotic viruses among recent inputs, and dynamic chart as a time-series of predicted zoonotic viruses. This page includes only viruses with virsentai probability of infecting human greater than 0.80. All virus IDs are linked to the details from the NCBI database. The system is designed for ease of maintenance: new sequences are queried via NCBI API, HyenaDNA inference is fast on modern GPUs, and results are updated in real time on the main dashboard. Results

The efficacy of a viral host prediction model is ultimately defined not just by its quantitative metrics, but also by its ability to distinguish a faint zoonotic signal from the overwhelming noise of viral diversity.

The optimized Virus Sentinel AI model (virsentai-v3-hyena-dna-16k) [23], leveraging the HyenaDNA architecture with a sequence length capacity of 160 000 nucleotides, demonstrated robust classification performance across all evaluated metrics (Table 1). The primary advantage of this model lies in its high discriminative capacity and its ability to generalize complex genomic patterns from training to unseen validation data. The model achieved a validation AUROC of 0.8816, indicating a strong ability to distinguish between viral and non-viral sequences. This high AUROC suggests that the model maintains a high true-positive rate across various threshold settings. Furthermore, the validation accuracy of 79.77% confirms consistent performance across the dataset.

A critical observation in the model’s performance is the balance between precision (83.56%) and recall (74.11%). The superior precision score indicates that when the model identifies a sequence as a viral target, it is correct in the vast majority of instances, thereby minimizing false-positive detections. This conservative classification profile is particularly advantageous in diagnostic or biosurveillance contexts where the cost of false alarms is high. The f1-score of 0.7855 reflects a successful harmonization of these two metrics.

The comparative analysis between training and validation metrics reveals a high degree of model stability. The training AUROC (0.9185) and validation AUROC (0.8816) show a narrow generalization gap of approximately 3.69%. Similarly, the f1-score transition from 0.8356 (training) to 0.7855 (validation) suggests that the model has effectively learned the underlying biological features of the sequences rather than merely memorizing the training set.

These figures position virsentai among leading models in the domain, even if specialized predictors report greater AUROC scores in the 0.95–0.99 range [1, 2]. The progression of the field has moved from highly specialized tools toward more generalized models (Table 2). Early models offered high precision within narrow viral families but lacked the flexibility to generalize across a broader pathogenic landscape. The subsequent wave of alignment-free classifiers and recurrent neural networks expanded this horizon, but often at the cost of losing long-range sequence context. More recently, massive foundation models like Evo 2 signal a conceptual leap toward large-scale genomic learning, although their exclusion of pathogenic viruses from training data currently limits their direct applicability for host prediction.

VirSentAI was designed to bridge the gap between narrow specialists and broad, unspecialized foundation models. Its core architectural advantage lies in its ability to process entire viral genomes as single, continuous sequences at single-nucleotide resolution. This allows the model to capture not only local mutational signatures but also the long-range dependencies across the genome that are often crucial for host adaptation—context that is frequently lost in methods relying on fragmented or feature-engineered data.

This architectural choice carries significant practical implications. The HyenaDNA framework is computationally efficient: VirSentAI is a compact model of approximately 120 million parameters, trainable in under one week on a single 24GB GPU. This efficiency contrasts sharply with the resource-intensive demands of billion-parameter models and is a key factor in its viability as an operational tool rather than a purely academic one. The resulting platform integrates scientific rigor with real-world usability. Its data pipeline, directly linked to NCBI repositories, robustly handles the ingestion and preprocessing of large-scale genomic data.

Discussion

VirSentAI is not without its limitations. The current 160k-token context window can be restrictive for the largest known viral genomes. Furthermore, the rapid convergence of the model during training suggests that extended training epochs could yield further performance improvements. The current implementation also relies solely on NCBI data. The PLAPT interaction model may introduce inaccuracies into the resulting drug repurposing predictions. The LLM employed to enhance virus metadata relies exclusively on NCBI data, which may limit its coverage and lead to failure in cases where information is absent unless external web resources are utilized. These aspects are not fundamental flaws but rather represent clear avenues for future refinement and development.

The application of a HyenaDNA-based AI model for predicting human host potential from complete viral genomes yielded a wide spectrum of results. This analysis reveals the model’s profound capabilities in genomic pattern recognition while simultaneously highlighting its critical deficiencies in biological and ecological reasoning. We have categorized the predictions to delineate the model’s successes, nuanced outputs, and theoretical failures.

Filtered zoonotic predictions

We first present the high-confidence zoonotic predictions corresponding to the data presented in Table 3, which reflects the current web results filtered for a prediction cutoff of 90% and excluding non-viral sequences. The model demonstrated a solid accuracy in identifying viruses that are well-established as human pathogens or belong to high-risk zoonotic reservoirs, effectively acting as a high-throughput genomic classifier. The top predictions generated by the Autonomous VirSentAI Agent highlight its capability to identify potential cross-species transmission risks, often-flagging non-mammalian viruses with high genomic similarity to established human pathogens. The high prediction probabilities across this diverse set underscore the robustness of the fine-tuned HyenaDNA topology in recognizing sequence patterns associated with human host adaptation, even when the known host is nonhuman or currently non-zoonotic.

For years, our reliance on alignment-heavy tools like BLAST felt difficult especially for the new topography of viral evolution. We have shifted toward neural architectures that parse the “grammar” of a genome rather than just hunting for direct matches. Thus, VirSentAI proposes a full-genome model to predict zoonotic viruses using complex genomics patterns.

Virsentai predict H10N4 Influenza strain (CY241921) from an environmental sample as possible zoonotic virus. While traditionally pigeonholed as an avian-centric curiosity, the model flagged it with an 82.81% probability of human infection. We often view environmental reservoirs as stagnant, but these genomic signatures suggest a far more fluid trajectory toward human susceptibility than we previously assumed.

The second possible zoonotic virus is the sewage-associated circular DNA virus (NC_028258). Sewage is essentially a biological soup, making it a computational nightmare for host prediction. VirSentAI assigned it an 80.75% probability for human infection. These circular DNA viruses are frequently dismissed as “dark matter” because they do not fit into our taxonomic boxes. By tagging this as potentially human infecting, the model suggests we might be ignoring a massive reservoir of viral diversity.

The performance of the model on the nonhuman-infecting cohort—a landscape heavily populated by various phages—provides a necessary baseline for assessing taxonomic fidelity. When we look at the results for Mycobacterium phage S6 or the Escherichia phage vB_EcoM_C2-3, the probabilities for human infection are vanishingly small, often plummeting below 0.15%. It suggests that VirSentAI is not just hallucinating human-compatibility signals where none exist, effectively filtering out the immense background noise of the bacterial virome. We often worry about “cross-domain leakage” in these neural architectures, yet here the model demonstrates a sharp, categorical anchoring. It recognizes that while a phage and a human virus might share a common evolutionary language, their host-specific “dialects” remain fundamentally distinct. This high degree of negative predictive value is just as critical for public health surveillance as the discovery of new zoonotic threats because it keeps us from chasing ghosts in the genomic dark.

We should be careful not to mistake a high probability score for an absolute biological truth. These models are essentially highly sophisticated pattern-matchers. This is where the methodology starts to show its limitations: a high score is a lead, not a diagnosis. We still need the wet-lab assays and the cell culture studies to confirm what the silicon is telling us. It is an iterative methodology between computational foresight and experimental proof.

In silico automatic drug repurposing for zoonotic viruses

The final stage of the Autonomous VirSentAI Agent workflow involves the application of the PLAPT AI model to perform a large-scale in silico drug repurposing analysis. This computational screening identifies potential binding interactions between the extracted proteins from predicted zoonotic viruses and a curated library of approved or clinically advanced small molecules. This step transforms the initial genomic prediction (host risk) into actionable therapeutic intelligence (drug candidates).

The affinity scores in Table 4 represent the predicted binding strength (affinity), often expressed as a pKi or equivalent metric, where higher values indicate stronger predicted binding.

The current drug repurposing screen, yielding 44 high-affinity interactions, effectively maps a landscape of theoretical vulnerabilities across these emergent viral targets. We are moving beyond the era of one-drug-one-bug serendipity and into a space where AI-driven interrogation of the existing pharmacopeia reveals where old tools might fit into new, viral-shaped holes. The data suggest a complex architecture of molecular “keys” finding “locks” in places we had not thought to look. It is a shift from blind searching to a structured search of pharmacological ghosts in the genomic machine. All the results should be confirmed in future version of virsentai with additional molecular docking, molecular dynamics, and in vitro experiments that could confirm the interactions.

The interaction profile for the H10N4 Influenza strain (CY241921) suggests a rather specific vulnerability within its nuclear export protein (NEP) and nonstructural protein 1 (NS1). While Deucravacitinib sits at the top with an exceptional affinity score of 11.58 for NEP, we see a tail of diverse molecules like Danazol (10.44) and the SGLT2 inhibitor Tofogliflozin (9.19). Thus, an androgenic steroid or a diabetes medication shows high theoretical affinity for a viral export factor. It could point to the structural plasticity of NEP, which must interact with diverse host cargo machinery. Meanwhile, NS1—the virus’s primary tool for blunting the host’s innate immune response—shows a strong affinity for Apixaban (10.72) and, interestingly, Biotin (10.22). These interactions suggest the virus is more integrated into host biochemistry than a simple replication cycle would imply.

The sewage-associated circular DNA virus-2 (NC_028258) presents a much broader, perhaps messier, pharmacological target. Alpelisib remains the standout performer, showing consistent high-affinity binding (10.99 and 10.92) across both the Rep and capsid proteins. This kind of “multi-target” profile is rare and usually indicates a common hydrophobic pocket or a highly conserved structural motif across the viral proteome. The presence of the imaging agent Flutemetamol F 18 (10.54) and the antibiotic Cefepime (10.24) in the top tier is equally provocative. While we usually think of Cefepime in the context of bacterial cell wall synthesis, here it seems to be moonlighting as a potential disruptor of viral replication. Of course, until experiments, Cefepime interaction could be just a PLAPT prediction error.

Looking closer at the remaining hits for the circular DNA virus, we see a fascinating cluster of cardiovascular and hormonal agents. Oxprenolol (9.91) and Moricizine (9.97) suggest that the viral proteins may share some conformational features with human ion channels or adrenergic receptors. In addition, Ulipristal Acetate (9.87), a progesterone receptor modulator, and Calcitriol (9.74), the active form of Vitamin D show good interactions. We even see Didanosine (9.79), an older HIV reverse transcriptase inhibitor, showing up in the mix. This provides a shred of therapeutic logic—a known antiviral scaffold interacting with a novel viral replication protein—which gives us a bit more confidence in the model’s underlying logic.

Ultimately, we have to look at these 44 results as a map of what could be, rather than a list of what is. The diversity of the drug classes involved—ranging from tyrosine kinase inhibitors to anticoagulants and vitamin derivatives—is both a strength and a source of healthy skepticism. It suggests our AI models are sensing deep structural patterns that bypass our traditional “one-drug-one-indication” mindset. However, the limitation of this computational foresight is obvious: a high binding score does not account for bioavailability, cellular localization, or the actual functional impact on the viral lifecycle. Virsenai predicts the candidates but we need to confirm with other computational methods and see if they can actually perform on the biological stage (in vitro/in vivo experiments).

Perspectives on future work

Looking ahead, we are focused on transforming VirSentAI from a powerful prototype into a robust, high-availability surveillance platform. A key first step will be migrating to a dedicated Python-based web server and application, which will allow us to scale operations far beyond the current static GitHub page.

To broaden our detection scope, we plan to significantly extend the analysis window beyond the current 160 kb to effectively process the genomes of much larger viruses. On the data front, we aim to integrate additional genomic databases by leveraging their APIs and employing LLMs to rapidly scan and process incoming data. Crucially, we will integrate LLMs to summarize comprehensive data about predicted high-risk viruses from multiple sources, providing immediate, actionable insights. The model will improve with the integration of biological, ecological, and structural data to provide the context for pure genomics predictions. Conclusion

In pushing virus discovery forward, we have created something more than just another predictive model. VirSentAI represents a deliberate shift toward a proactive, rather than reactive, stance in genomic surveillance by this innovative prototype. The core idea was to build an intelligent, end-to-end platform capable of scanning the near-infinite horizon of genomic data for brewing threats, and—crucially—to make it profoundly accessible. Pandemics, after all, do not wait for command-line expertise.

We have engineered VirSentAI to scout for zoonotic risks directly from the raw dialect of viral DNA, harnessing the power of a long-range genomic model like HyenaDNA, which excels at interpreting vast, complex sequences. This is not just an incremental improvement. It is about fundamentally changing who gets to ask the questions. By wrapping this analytical prowess within a fully automated, open-source web service, we bridge a critical gap, empowering researchers and public health officials globally, regardless of their computational background. The entire platform, from its underlying code to a live dashboard, is freely available, a deliberate choice to foster global vigilance.

Of course, the prototype platform has its limitations. No tool is a panacea. The resolution for pinpointing specific hosts can get fuzzy in certain edge cases, and the inherent biases lurking within training datasets remain a persistent shadow we must continually work to mitigate. Machine learning models, however powerful, are shaped by the data they learn from, a challenge across the field of viral prediction.

Still, the impact is a tangible step forward: bolstering surveillance, helping to spot faint patterns in the overwhelming noise of metagenomics data, and shining a light on potential epidemiological blind spots. The applications begin to sprawl from here. One could imagine synthetic virology labs using the platform to vet designs or API extensions hooking directly into global alert systems. This is where the method starts to show its true potential. By creating a tool that is both powerful and open, VirSentAI nudges the global health community toward a more democratized and anticipatory posture in the unending race against the next pandemic threat.
