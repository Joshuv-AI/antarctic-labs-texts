Regulatory roles of G-quadruplexes and G-quadruplex-binding proteins across the enhancer and promoter of the HBV genome
Authors: Lina He, Baoyue Huang, Haiyang Ma, Lin Wang, Qingsi Wu, Junling Zhang, Li Yu, Shujuan Lv
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundHepatitis B virus (HBV) infection poses a global threat to human health due to the limited availability of effective treatment options. Recent studies have shown that the structures of G-quadruplex (G4) are related to the pathogenesis of the virus and potential targets for antiviral therapy. G-quadruplex-binding proteins (G4BPs) play a key role in regulating the G4 landscape and its associated functions by anchoring, stabilizing, or unwinding G4 structures. Researchers have actively pursued the identification of G4 structures within the HBV genome, yet a comprehensive, genome-wide analysis revealing their regulatory role and that of G4BPs has remained largely elusive. Consequently, our understanding of the intricate interactions between HBV G4 and G4BPs remains quite limited.MethodsBiophysical and molecular biology approaches were employed to investigate HBV G4, focusing on core promoter and enhancer (CP/EN) activities. Mass spectrometry, DNA pull-down, and surface plasmon resonance (SPR) characterized interactions between G4 and G4BPs. Employing the dual luciferase reporter system and HBV infection model, this study utilized binding assays designed for the G4 mutant, alongside overexpression and knockdown experiments, to delve into the regulatory functions of G4 and G4BPs.ResultsWe identified two functional G4 elements located at positions 1204 and 1732 within the HBV CP/EN regulatory region. Reporter assays containing the CP/EN sequences showed that disruption of either the 1204 or 1732 G4 structure significantly reduced promoter activity. In the HBV 1.3-mer system, disruption of the 1732 G4 markedly decreased HBsAg, HBeAg and HBcAg expression, whereas total HBV RNA and 3.5 kb RNA levels were not significantly affected. Both G4 elements selectively recruited multiple host proteins in vitro, among which the 1732 displayed stronger binding to HNF4A. Functional assays further demonstrated that HNF4A enhanced CP/EN-driven reporter activity in a G4-dependent manner, and stabilization of G4 structures by BRACO-19 further promoted the interaction between G4 and HNF4A. In addition, CNBP, the most highly enriched G4-binding protein, positively regulated HBV antigen and RNA expression; however, CNBP did not enhance CP/EN promoter activity, indicating a regulatory mechanism independent of CP/EN activation.ConclusionThis study reveals that two G4 structures within the HBV CP/EN regulatory region function as structural platforms for host factor recruitment and play distinct roles in regulating viral gene expression. In particular, the 1732 G4 facilitates HNF4A-dependent activation of CP/EN activity, thereby supporting efficient viral protein production, whereas CNBP promotes HBV expression through a CP/EN-independent mechanism.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12985-026-03132-2.Keywords: HBV, G-quadruplex, Promoter, Enhancer, G4BPs, HNF4A, CNBP

Introduction

HBV can cause chronic infections, which are closely related to liver cirrhosis and hepatocellular carcinoma, and remains an important public health threat. HBV is a double-stranded, incomplete DNA virus that remains hidden in the liver and proliferates. The HBV genome contains four open reading frames (P, C, S, X) on its coding negative strand, along with four overlapping promoters: the core promoter (CP, nt1613-1849), preS1 promoter (SP I, nt2718-2808), preS2 promoter (SP II, nt2983-3210), and X promoter (XP, nt1171-1361). Additionally, there are two enhancers: Enhancer I (EN I, nt957-1361) and Enhancer II (EN II, nt1685-1773) [1]. CP plays a vital role in HBV replication directs the transcription of 3.5 kb RNA and is activated by liver-enriched transcription factors such as hepatocyte nuclear factor 1 alpha (HNF1A), hepatocyte nuclear factor 4 alpha (HNF4A), retinoid X receptor alpha (RXRα), CCAAT/enhancer- binding protein (C/EBP) [2]. HBV enhancers further contain indispensable cis-acting elements crucial for liver-specific functions. As HBV gene expression is regulated at multiple levels, including transcriptional and post-transcriptional processes, targeting regulatory elements that modulate viral gene expression represents a promising strategy for the development of novel anti-HBV therapies.

G4 are secondary structures formed by nucleic acids in G-rich sequences of DNA and RNA under physiological conditions. G4 structures involve Hoogsteen-type base pairing, resulting in the formation of G-quartets, which is the most characteristic structural element of G4 [3]. The stacking of at least two G-quartets is facilitated by π-π interactions, leading to the formation of G4 structures [4–7]. The stability and topology of G4s are influenced by multiple factors, including the number and length of G-tracts, the sequence and length of the loop, and the presence of cations (e.g., K+, Na+). Consequently, each of the four G-tracts forming the structure’s core can run parallel or diverge antiparallel relative to its immediate neighbors, creating parallel, antiparallel, or hybrid core configurations [8]. G4s play key regulatory roles in multiple essential cellular biological processes, including telomere maintenance, DNA replication, transcription, mRNA processing, and chromatin remodeling [9, 10].

Notably, numerous studies have revealed that many viruses harbor conserved potential G-quadruplex sequences (PQS), particularly in EBV, HCV, HIV, and SARS-CoV-2 [11]. G4s in the genome regulate DNA/RNA replication and transcription, playing a vital role in controlling viral invasion and infection. To date, the G4 structures identified in the HBV genome located within key regulatory sequences include: G4s existing exclusively in the pre-S promoter of genotype B virus boost S promoter activity and virion secretion [12], and they control HBV replication by promoting cccDNA transcription and RNA translation [13, 14]. Conversely, mutations in HBV precore promoter’s quadruplexes lead to increased HBsAg/HBeAg production but a decrease in HBcAg levels [15]. Thus, the precise regulatory roles and molecular mechanisms of HBV G4s in viral gene expression, including both transcriptional and post-transcriptional regulation, remain incompletely understood and warrant further investigation.

G4BPs serve as essential mediators and are crucial for the biological functions of G4 structures. Common mechanisms include unwinding G4 structures as helicases or being specifically recruited by G4s to exert cooperative functions. In recent years, an increasing number of G4BPs such as MAZ, NCL, PARP1 and DHX36, have been identified. These proteins can bind to G4 structures in regulatory regions and participate in the control of gene expression [7, 9, 16, 17]. However, whether and how HBV G4BPs interact with G4 may influence HBV infection continues to elude us.

In this study, we investigated two highly conserved G4 elements located at HBV CP/EN II (1732) and enhancer I (1204), both of which contribute to the regulation of HBV gene expression. Both the 1204 and 1732 G4 elements are capable of recruiting HNF4A and CNBP in vitro. Notably, CNBP, the most highly enriched G4-binding protein, positively regulates HBV antigen and RNA expression but does not act through activation of the CP/EN. In contrast, HNF4A enhances CP/EN-driven promoter activity through high-affinity binding to the 1732. Collectively, our study highlights the regulatory roles of highly conserved G4 structures within the HBV CP/EN regions and their associated host factors in controlling viral gene expression. Further characterization of G4 elements and their interacting host proteins in the HBV genome may facilitate the development of novel anti-HBV strategies.

Materials and methods

Analysis of full-length HBV sequences for PQS

All of genotype HBV sequences were retrieved from the HBV database (https://hbvdb.lyon.inserm.fr) [18] and National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI). The QGRS Mapper application was used to predict the sequences of all the collected genomic information, and the PQS search range was expanded. PQS prediction was based on pattern matching of the GxNy1GxNy2GxNy3Gx motif, where x represents the number of guanine quartets forming the G-quadruplex(x ≥ 2), y denotes the length of the loops connecting the guanine quartets(y ≥ 1), and the maximum allowable sequence length for this motif is 30 nucleotides [19]. Due to limited full-length HBV sequences for genotypes I and J, they were not included in the analysis.

Oligonucleotides, plasmids and reagents

The DNA oligonucleotide and mutant sequences were chemically synthesized and purified via HPLC (Sangon Biotech). Specific G4 oligonucleotides employed in this investigation are detailed in Supplementary Table 2. We utilized the luciferase reporter gene construct pHBV-Luc (MiaoLing Biology), which harbors the HBV CP, EN I, EN II elements along with the luciferase gene. SiCNBP was previously described [20]; additional siRNAs were sourced from Sangon Biotech (Supplementary Table 3). The HBV 1.3-mer plasmid, pCMV-HNF4A and pCMV-CNBP overexpression plasmids were also purchased from MiaoLing Biology.

Native gel electrophoresis

The synthesized G4 oligonucleotides were dissolved in 50 mM Tris-HCl buffer (pH 7.0) containing 100 mM K⁺ to a final concentration of 20 µM. This solution was heated to 95 °C for 5 min and then gradually cooled to ambient room temperature. The samples were subsequently electrophoresed on a 20% non-denaturing polyacrylamide gel in 0.5×TBE buffer supplemented with 50 mM KCl. Following electrophoresis, the gel was stained with an ethidium bromide solution and ultimately visualized using a gel imaging system for detailed analysis. The use of 50 mM KCl in the electrophoresis buffer instead of 100 mM KCl in the samples aims to maintain K⁺-mediated G4 stability while avoiding excessive salt concentration that could lead to band dispersion or abnormal mobility. This concentration difference ensures the G4structure remains stable during electrophoresis.

NMR spectroscopy

Standard 1H NMR spectroscopy was conducted using a Bruker Avance III spectrometer operating at a field strength of 600 MHz. Oligonucleotides were prepared containing 100 mM K⁺ and 10% D2O, with a concentration of 0.3 mM.

Circular dichroism (CD) spectroscopy and melting curve

The synthesized G4 oligonucleotides were dissolved in 10 mM Tris-HCl buffer (pH 7.0) containing 100 mM K⁺ to a final concentration of 10 µM. The solution was heated at 95 °C for 5 min and then gradually cooled to room temperature. The oligonucleotide solution was transferred into a cuvette and measured using a J-1700 CD spectropolarimeter (Japan). Under isothermal conditions, the ellipticity at different wavelengths was recorded with the following instrument settings: scanning range, 220–320 nm; scanning speed, 50 nm/min; bandwidth, 1.0 nm. Prior to testing, baseline calibration is conducted using a blank buffer solution to eliminate any background interference.

The CD melting experiments were conducted over a temperature range of 20–95 °C, employing a heating rate of 3 °C/minute while monitoring ellipticity at 295 nm (1204, 1886) or 265 nm (1732), respectively. When required, the oligonucleotide solution was incubated with the G4 ligand BRACO‑19 (Sigma) at a 1:4 molar ratio in the dark for 15 min before the corresponding measurement. Following normalization applied to both datasets, the resulting average values were subsequently fitted using the Boltzmann sigmoidal model (OriginPro 2021 software) to determine the Tm values.

Cell culture and transfection

HepG2.2.15, HepG2, and HEK-293T cells were cultivated in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM) enriched with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS). The culture medium for HepG2.2.15 cells received an additional supplement of 380 µg/ml G418. HepG2 cells underwent transfection with the HBV 1.3-mer plasmid. This transfection procedure was performed strictly according to the manufacturer’s instructions for the Lipofectamine 3000 transfection reagent (Thermo Fisher). To initiate the process, cells were seeded into 24-well plates at the desired density 24 h prior to transfection. The transfection complex was carefully prepared by combining the necessary reagents in Opti-MEM (Gibco), followed by a 15 min incubation at room temperature. This mixture was then gently added to the awaiting cells, maintained within a humidified incubator at 37 °C and 5% CO2. Four hours after transfection, the medium was replaced with fresh complete growth medium, and the cells continued incubating until harvest.

RNA preparation and qRT-PCR assay

Total RNA from HBV was extracted using the Trizol reagent, and the first strand of cDNA was reverse transcribed in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions (Accurate Biology). The mRNA expression levels of ATR, HNRNPA1, HNF4A, PRKDC, CNBP, as well as the total HBV RNA and the 3.5 kb RNA, were determined using the qPCR system Lightcycler® 96 (Roche). GAPDH was used as an internal control (Supplementary Table 4). HBV total RNA and 3.5 kb RNA primers were previously described [21].

Dual-luciferase reporter assay

Three mutant plasmids of pHBV-Luc-1204/1732/1886 were generated. The specific mutation sites are illustrated in Fig. 3. For transfection into HEK-293T cells, 1 µg of the pHBV-Luc luciferase reporter plasmid or its mutant counterparts, supplemented with 1 µg of HNF4A/CNBP overexpression plasmid as required, along with 400 ng of Renilla luciferase plasmid (pRL-TK) serving as the internal reference, were transfected. 48 h post-transfection, cells were lysed and detected according to the Dual-luciferase Reporter Gene Assay Kit (Beyotime).

ELISA

The detection of HBsAg and HBeAg in the cell supernatant was conducted using HBsAg/HBeAg ELISA Kits (Kehua Bio-Engineering), adhering to the standardized laboratory procedures detailed in the kit instructions. The results presented have been normalized according to the control antigen level.

G4 pull-down

G4 DNA pull-down assay was performed as follows [22]. First, the 3’-Biotin-TEG-labeled oligonucleotides were dissolved in 10 mM Tris-HCl buffer (pH 7.0) containing 100 mM K⁺ to a final concentration of 10 µM. The solution was heated at 95 °C for 5 min and then gradually cooled to room temperature. Subsequently, the folded oligonucleotides were incubated with 100 µl of streptavidin magnetic beads (MedchemExpress, MCE) in a binding buffer composed of 10 mM Tris-HCl (pH 7.5) and 100 mM K⁺ at room temperature for 1 h. (When required, the oligonucleotide should be incubated with BRACO-19 of 200 µM at room temperature for 1 h before binding to the magnetic beads). After incubation, the beads were washed twice with a buffer containing 10 mM Tris-HCl, pH 7.5, 100 mM K⁺, 0.05% Tween-20 to remove unbound oligonucleotides. Total cellular protein of HepG2.2.15 was obtained using HEPES lysis buffer. The lysis buffer consisted of 50 mM HEPES-KOH (pH 7.5), 100 mM K⁺, 10% glycerol, 0.3% Triton X-100, and 1×PMSF, and was finally brought with nuclease-free water. Next, the protein lysate was incubated with the oligonucleotide-bead complex at 4 °C for 1 h under gentle rotation. The complex was then washed twice with a buffer containing 10 mM Tris-HCl (pH 7.5), 200 mM K⁺, and 0.05% Tween-20 to eliminate nonspecifically bound proteins. A portion of the magnetic beads was mixed with 5×loading buffer, incubated at 100 °C for 10 min, and subsequently subjected to Western blot analysis.

Mass spectrometry

The pull-down beads underwent five washes with 1×PBS before subsequent mass spectrometry identification performed by PTM Biolab. Trypsin was then introduced for digestion. The resulting peptides dissolved in liquid chromatography mobile phase and were separated using an EASY-nLC 1200 ultra-high-performance liquid chromatography system. The DIA-NN software output multi-dimensional q-values for peptides, proteins, etc., based on spectral matching, and peptide results meeting the threshold set by the false discovery rate (FDR) were filtered accordingly (q < 0.01) [23].

Western blot analysis

Proteins were extracted using RIPA buffer and separated by SDS-PAGE gels, followed by transfer onto PVDF membranes. The membranes were blocked with 5% nonfat milk and subsequently incubated overnight at 4 °C with diluted HNF4A, CNBP (Proteintech Group) or HBcAg (Abcam) antibodies. HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies were then applied for detection. β-actin was used as the endogenous control.

Surface plasmon resonance (SPR) and molecular prediction

The synthesized G4 oligonucleotides (100 µM) were dissolved in 10 mM Tris-HCl buffer (pH 7.0) containing 100 mM K⁺/Li+, heated at 95 °C for 5 min, and then slowly cooled to room temperature before use. In the Biacore T200, the ligand protein HNF4A (MedChemExpress, MCE) was immobilized onto a CM5 sensor chip (Cytiva) via standard amine-coupling chemistry. Dilute the oligonucleotides at a 2-fold gradient, each diluted sample was then injected sequentially over the chip surface at a constant flow rate, allowing for real-time monitoring of the association and dissociation processes at each concentration. Following the assay, the resulting sensorgrams were processed and analyzed using BIAevaluation software to determine the binding kinetics and derive the equilibrium dissociation constant (KD).

Meanwhile, interactions between HNF4A (pdb_00004iqr) [24] and G4 structures were predicted via the powerful online platform AlphaFold3. These predicted complex structures were then visualized using the advanced PyMOL software.

Statistical analysis

The data were presented as mean ± sem. A two-tailed unpaired Student’s t-test was employed to assess the difference between two independent groups. P < 0.05 were considered significant.

Results

Highly conserved PQS motifs are presented in the CP/EN region of the HBV genome

Within the search range of the whole gene sequence, we mapped the formation of PQSs in the HBV full-length genomic sequences according to the G-Score, which implies the likelihood of G4 formation (Fig. 1A). Notably, high-scoring G4s were identified at positions + 1204, +1732, + 1886 and + 3021 within the HBV B type LC753640.1 genome (Supplementary Table 1). These PQSs exhibited significant potential for forming G4 structures and were consequently designated as 1204, 1732, 1886 and 3021. Based on multiple sequence comparison results, we used WebLogo software to map PQSs in full-length HBV genome genotypes A-H. The results showed that the PQSs of 1204, 1732 and 1886 were highly conserved among different HBV genotypes (Fig. 1B). PQS-3021 did not contain evolutionarily conserved sequences, as previously reported [12]. By using CLUSTAL multi-sequence alignment analysis of MEGA software, the comparison of HBV representative A to H was carried out. The results also showed that PQS-1204/1732/1886 were highly conserved except for genotype G (Supplementary Fig. 1).

Highly conserved nucleotides are represented by a larger font size, while largely variable nucleotides are denoted by a smaller font size. C The positions of the PQSs (green dots) are shown in the promoter and enhancer regions of the HBV genome

The HBV genome uses overlapping ORFs to encode more genetic information, with different PQSs found in overlapping regions of the coding regions, promoter and enhancer. Specifically, 1204 was identified at the green position of the P gene with a G-score of 19, overlapping in the XP/EN I region of the regulatory gene. Similarly, 1732 was located at the green position of the X gene with a G-score of 19 and overlapped with the CP/EN II region. Additionally, 1886 was found within the green preC/C gene with a G-score of 18 (Fig. 1C). Structurally, the guanine distribution within HBV PQS nucleic acid sequences exhibits the distinctive G2 sequence pattern (G≥2NxG≥2NyG≥2NzG≥2) as a critical feature [25]. This pattern describes DNA sequences containing 2 or more guanines within each of its four guanine cores, separated by loops of 1–7 nucleotides. Such sequences containing four contiguous runs of at least two guanine bases possess the potential to fold into two stacked quartets, thereby forming a stable two-quartet G4 structure. This specific structural motif thus constitutes the primary focus of the present investigation.

G4 structure validation of HBV PQS sequences

To verify that the high PQS scores from the HBV genome can form G4 structures, native PAGE was used to monitor the formation of the secondary structure. HBV PQSs and the guanines in the G-rich region were mutated to disrupt the formation of the G4 structure for study (Fig. 2A). The core principle of our mutation design is to disrupt G4 formation by introducing single-point mutations (G→A/C/T) in each G-repeat. Using different base substitutions validates universality, achieving a G-score of 0. The wild-type PQS migrated faster than the mutated PQS on native PAGE, suggesting that guanine mutations disrupted the formation of an intramolecular G4 (Fig. 2B).

B Native polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis confirmed G4 formation in HBV sequences and corresponding mutants. C 1H NMR spectra unveiled characteristic chemical shifts for HBV PQS and mutants, using AS1411 as a positive control. D CD spectroscopy analysis revealed the structure of the HBV PQS and its mutants. E CD thermal unfolding analysis of HBV PQS with BRACO-19. Unfolding of wild-type 1204 and 1886 was tracked at 295 nm, while 1732 unfolding was monitored at 265 nm. PQS structures and ligand BRACO-19 were combined at a 1:4 ratio. F PQS-1204/1886 adopts an antiparallel structure, while PQS-1732 assembles into a parallel conformation

The 1H NMR signals pertaining to the formation of Hoogsteen base pairing appeared in the spectra 11–12 ppm [26–28], Previous literature also indicates that certain quadruplexes display weak imine peaks at 10.1–10.5 ppm and 12.1–12.6 ppm. additionally, some weaker imine proton peaks may extend to approximately 10 ppm which indicated the formation of two-quartet G4s. We used the well-known classic G4 AS1411 [29] as the positive control to observe the NMR signals of wild-type PQS and its mutants. Within the range of 10–12.5 ppm, the characteristic signals of G4s were observed in all four PQS sequences, namely the chemical shift peaks corresponding to the amine protons of guanine, indicating that these HBV PQS sequences can form G4s. However, the peaks of 1204/1886-mut are primarily concentrated at 12.5–14 ppm, suggesting a transition of G4 folding toward Watson-Crick base pairing; the peak of 1732-mut still appears at 10–12 ppm, but its shape is less pronounced compared to the WT (Fig. 2C).

We further confirmed the ability of these PQS to form G4 structures by using CD analysis. CD spectroscopy analysis confirmed the formation of a parallel G4 by 1732, while 1204 and 1886 formed antiparallel G4s (Fig. 2F). According to the shape of its forming curve in K+ or Na+ metal monovalent cations, potassium ions are preferred for HBV G4 stabilization (Supplementary Fig. 2). We then mutated guanines to disrupt the formation of the G4 structures. As expected, 1204/1886-mut did not show CD spectral signatures corresponding to G4 formation. Meanwhile, the 1732-mut maintains a positive peak at 260 nm and a minor negative peak at 240 nm, yet exhibits a dramatic ~ 50% reduction in mdeg (Fig. 2D). Combined with diminished peak intensity in NMR, this suggests that while the mutant can still adopt a G4 topology, its folding efficiency is likely compromised, dynamic motion heightened, and structural rigidity weakened. To further validate these data, we employed BRACO-19, a known G4-stabilizing ligand [30], to confirm the reliability of the G4 structures. The results revealed that introducing BRACO-19 into the G4 solution at a concentration four times the molar amount elevated the Tm values for 1204 and 1886. For 1732, its inherently high Tm exceeded the detectable range following BRACO-19 addition (Fig. 2E). This suggests that BRACO-19 enhances the stability of the G4 structures, which again demonstrates the HBV G4 folding is very stable and luckily formed in physiological conditions.

Destroying HBV G4 1204 and 1732 reduces the HBV expression

Subsequently, we utilized pHBV-Luc to investigate the role of HBV G4 in CP/EN, where the luciferase gene expression is regulated by HBV CP, EN I, and EN II. The PQS region within the reporter plasmid shared the core G sequence with the oligonucleotide in Fig. 2A, but possessed distinct loop sequences (Fig. 3A). We further validated the G4 and mutated sequences on the pHBV-Luc plasmid and its mutant plasmid using CD results, which were consistent with the G4 oligonucleotide results shown in Fig. 2 (Fig. 3B-D). The constructed pHBV-Luc plasmid and its mutants were transfected into HEK-293T cells, co-transfected with pRL-TK as an internal reference. After 48 h, detection revealed that mutations at the 1204/1732/1886 sites significantly reduced luciferase expression levels, with the 1732 site mutation exhibiting the most pronounced downregulation effect (Fig. 3F).

Furthermore, we selected the antiparallel structure 1204 and the parallel structure 1732 for detailed investigation. Compared to the HBV 1.3-mer-WT plasmid, the construct harboring the 1732-mut exhibited significantly reduced levels of HBsAg, HBeAg, and HBcAg (Fig. 3G). Interestingly, no significant differences were observed in the levels of HBV total RNA and 3.5 kb RNA at 48 h and 72 h (Fig. 3H and I). These results collectively demonstrate that HBV 1732 promotes the expression of HBV proteins, likely not through transcriptional pathways but rather via post-transcriptional regulation. In contrast, the 1204-mut displayed no significant changes (Fig. 3J), nor were alterations observed in HBV RNA levels (Fig. 3K). Notably, 1204 resides within a genomic region overlapping EN I and the ORFs encoding DNA polymerase. To preserve the transcriptional frame, complete elimination of the amino acid sequence for key elements and the G4 structure at the 1204 site proved unfeasible. Consequently, a single G-to-A mutation within the G-tract diminished the G-score from 20 to 12. CD revealed that following the 1204-mut, the peak intensity at 290 nm weakened, while the distinct positive peak originally at 240 nm shifted to a pronounced negative peak, and the negative peak at 260 nm began to shift toward a positive peak (Fig. 3E). We hypothesize that in this mutation model, 1204 favors a parallel G4 conformation, potentially retaining residual regulatory functions of the G4 structure. Therefore, the distinct 1204 mutation patterns within HBV1.3-mer and pHBV-Luc yielded markedly divergent outcomes. These findings collectively demonstrate that HBV G4 1204 and 1732 can promote the expression of HBV effector proteins.

Identification of HBV G4-recruited proteins

Given the critical role of 1204 and 1732 in gene regulation, we further investigated which host proteins were recruited by the G4 domain for regulatory purposes. Biotinylated 1204 or 1732 oligonucleotides were selectively precipitated from HepG2.2.15 cell lysates, followed by MS analysis for identification (Fig. 4A). The results demonstrated that 619 proteins in the 1204 and 1732 groups were enriched by more than 1.5 times compared to the Non-G4 control group (Fig. 4B). Among these, 34 proteins were identified as involved in DNA repair, transcriptional regulation, RNA processing, or G4 binding (Fig. 4C). Previous studies have reported that FUS proteins can be recruited to G4 structures [13], and these findings also validated the accuracy of our MS experiments.

Among the candidate molecules, CNBP showed the highest and most significant enrichment with HBV G4. As a key transcription factor of HBV, HNF4A had not been previously reported to participate in G4 structure binding. Therefore, five candidate proteins including CNBP and HNF4A were selected for siRNA knockdown experiments (Supplementary Fig. 3). Knockdown of these candidate proteins significantly reduced the levels of HBV antigens and RNA (Fig. 4D-F). To further clarify the binding capacity of HNF4A and CNBP to HBV G4, DNA pull-down experiments demonstrated that 1204 and 1732 enriched higher concentrations of CNBP and HNF4A compared to their respective mutants and Non-G4 controls (Fig. 4G and H). Future studies will ensure that 1204 and 1732 retain the ability to fold into G4 structures in DNA pull-down buffers. CD experiments confirmed that the G4 structures maintained correct topological conformations (Supplementary Fig. 4).

HNF4A and CNBP positively regulate HBV gene expression

Subsequently, pCMV-HNF4A or pCMV-CNBP was transfected into HepG2 cells together with the HBV 1.3-mer plasmid (Fig. 5A and B). The results showed that overexpression of either HNF4A or CNBP led to increased levels of HBcAg, HBeAg and HBsAg, as well as increased HBV total RNA and 3.5 kb RNA (Fig. 5C-F). Conversely, knockdown of HNF4A or CNBP resulted in reduced levels of HBV antigens and RNA (Figs. 4D-F and 5G-I). HNF4A is a known HBV transcription factor, and our results are consistent with previous studies [31, 32]. In contrast, the identification of CNBP as a positive regulator of HBV expression has not been previously reported. The CP/EN region is a key regulatory element controlling HBV gene expression. To determine whether CNBP regulates HBV expression through the CP/EN region in a manner similar to HNF4A, pHBV-Luc was co-transfected with pCMV-HNF4A or pCMV-CNBP into HEK-293T cells. Overexpression of HNF4A markedly increased luciferase activity, indicating that HNF4A enhances CP/EN-driven promoter activity. In contrast, CNBP overexpression did not increase, but instead slightly reduced, luciferase activity (Fig. 5J-L). Collectively, these results indicate that HNF4A promotes HBV gene expression through activation of CP/EN activity, whereas CNBP positively regulates HBV expression through a CP/EN-independent mechanism.

1732 binds to HNF4A and modulates the activity of the HBV CP/EN

Neither 1204 nor 1732 represent known classical targets of the HNF4A transcription factor. To verify potential direct interactions between HNF4A and G4 structures, we first conducted structural predictions of protein-G4 complexes utilizing AlphaFold3, followed by detailed visual analysis of critical binding sites with PyMOL. These analyses revealed that both 1204 and 1732 form hydrogen bonds with specific amino acid residues within HNF4A and share partially overlapping binding sites (Fig. 6A and B). To further substantiate HNF4A binding to G4, we employed SPR analysis. This technique demonstrated direct binding of both 1204 and 1732 to HNF4A, yielding concentration-dependent binding affinity constants of KD = 2.552 × 10− 5 M and KD = 1.991 × 10− 6 M, respectively (Fig. 6C and D), with 1732 exhibiting significantly stronger affinity. Crucially, SPR experiments using Non-G4, 1204-mut and 1732-mut as mobile phases confirmed that neither the mutants nor Non-G4 displayed any binding activity towards HNF4A (Fig. 6E-G). Furthermore, replacing K+ in the buffer with equimolar Li+-known to disrupt G4 folding-abolished binding between HBV G4 and HNF4A (Fig. 6H and I). Subsequent co-incubation of 1204/1732 with BRACO-19, validated by DNA pull-down, demonstrated that BRACO-19 actively promoted the binding of 1204/1732 to HNF4A (Fig. 6J and K). Collectively, these findings confirm 1204/1732 as specific targets for HNF4A binding.

To investigate the functional role of G4-dependent recruitment of HNF4A in regulating CP/EN-driven promoter activity, HEK-293T cells were co-transfected with pHBV-Luc constructs carrying either the 1204 or 1732 G4 mutation together with pCMV-HNF4A. Both 1204- and 1732-mutated pHBV-Luc reporters exhibited markedly reduced luciferase activity. Overexpression of HNF4A partially restored and significantly enhanced CP/EN-driven promoter activity (Fig. 6L). Notably, luciferase activity in cells co-transfected with HNF4A and the 1732-mut reporter remained significantly lower than that observed with the wild-type reporter in the presence of HNF4A. These results indicate that HNF4A overexpression cannot fully compensate for disruption of the 1732 G4, and that an intact 1732 G4 structure is required for optimal HNF4A-dependent activation of CP/EN activity.

Discussion

In recent years, the impact of G4 structures on microbial replication, transcription, recombination, and virulence has been extensively explored. Within the SARS-CoV-2 genome, G4 stabilizers show potential for repurposing against COVID-19 [33]. Our study identifies highly conserved G4 sequences at positions 1204, 1732, and 1886 in the HBV genome across genotypes. The 1204 sequence resides in the XP/EN I regulatory gene, 1732 within the CP/EN II overlapping region, and HBV-1886 in the Epsilon RNA element, aligning with prior findings [13–15]. Giraud et al. reported that the PQS5 G4 facilitates cccDNA transcription, which is in line with our observation that the 1204 element supports CP/EN-driven reporter activity in a luciferase assay [13]. However, in our HBV 1.3-mer model, the 1204 mutant, which contains only a single G-to-A substitution due to its overlap with the polymerase coding region, did not produce detectable changes in viral antigen production or viral RNA levels. This may reflect incomplete disruption of the G4 structure at this site, as also suggested by the residual G4 features observed in the CD spectra. The pre-core promoter region G4 reported by Meier-Stephenson et al. corresponds to our site 1732 [15]. Unlike the strategy employed by Meier-Stephenson V et al., when designing the 1732 mutant we substituted three guanines with A/C/T to completely disrupt the G4 structure. This mutation strategy significantly reduced the activity of the HBV CP/EN luciferase reporter, and, in an HBV replication model based on the HBV 1.3-mer plasmid, the mutant also decreased the levels of HBV HBsAg, HBeAg and HBcAg. Notably, despite the marked reduction in antigen levels, HBV total RNA and 3.5 kb RNA did not show corresponding changes, suggesting that HBV 1732 may preferentially function at a post-transcriptional level, including translation and/or RNA quality, rather than exerting a significant effect on transcription. The core promoter regulates the expression of HBeAg and HBcAg. Although HBsAg is not directly controlled by the CP, it is regulated by EN. Since 1732 is located within the overlapping region of the CP and EN, structural alterations at this site can simultaneously affect the expression of all three antigens.

Consistent with the findings of Meier-Stephenson V et al. [15], the G4 mutant G1738/1748A also resulted in reduced HBcAg levels without obvious changes in viral RNA abundance, indicating that alterations in viral protein production are not necessarily correlated with RNA levels. Based on these observations, we speculate that this G4 element may indirectly influence translational efficiency. Disruption of the 1732 G4 may lead to changes in transcription start site usage of the 3.5 kb RNA, thereby affecting ribosomal scanning and initiation efficiency, or may alter RNA quality, such as RNA conformation or 5′-end processing.

For most eukaryotic mRNAs, translation initiation depends on the 7-methylguanosine cap structure at the 5′ end. However, some viral and cellular mRNAs can initiate translation in a cap-independent manner through internal ribosome entry sites. Notably, previous research has reported that HBV RNA G4 elements are involved in the regulation of cap-independent translation [14]. Given the location of the 1732 G4 within the CP/EN regulatory region, it is plausible that this structure contributes to post-transcriptional control of HBV gene expression, potentially by modulating translation efficiency, although the precise molecular mechanism remains to be elucidated.

Our research demonstrates that the disruption of G4 at positions 1204 and 1732 correlates with impaired CP/EN activity. Typically, G4s function as switches in gene expression regulation. While most DNA and RNA viruses enhance promoter activity by destabilizing or stabilizing G4s [34–37], our findings indicate that, in HBV, properly folded G4 structures within the CP/EN region are required to support optimal promoter-enhancer activity. These observations suggest that G4s play multifaceted regulatory roles in viral gene expression. In HBV, G4 formation within CP/EN regions may facilitate the recruitment of host or viral factors to fine-tune regulatory activity.

G4BPs have attracted increasing attention because of their involvement in viral replication and gene expression. Both the 1204 and 1732 were able to recruit a large number of host proteins, among which CNBP was the most enriched factor identified by MS. We further confirmed a strong and specific interaction between CNBP and the G4 elements within the HBV CP/EN region. Functional analyses using overexpression and siRNA approaches revealed that CNBP positively regulates HBV antigen production and viral RNA levels in the HBV 1.3-mer plasmid system. Notably, however, dual-luciferase assays showed that CNBP does not enhance CP/EN-driven promoter activity, indicating that its regulatory effect is independent of CP/EN activation and is mechanistically distinct from that of the transcription factor HNF4A. It is known that CNBP is a CCHC-type zinc finger nucleic acid-binding protein. Early studies also indicated that CNBP stabilizes G4s [38, 39], preferentially binds to mRNA G4 sequences, and promotes RNA translation and stability [40, 41]. Because the 1204 and 1732 sequences are also present in HBV transcripts [13, 14, 42], it is plausible that CNBP may interact with 1204 and 1732 RNA to regulate RNA G4 metabolic activities. Furthermore, CNBP may regulate gene expression by unwinding G4 structures. For instance, in SARS-CoV-2, CNBP binds to and unwinds G4s on both the positive and negative strands of the viral genome. Therefore, we hypothesize that CNBP may regulate HBV RNA G4 to stabilize RNA and enhance translation levels, or modulate HBV expression by unwinding G4 structures, among other mechanisms. The specific mechanisms warrant further investigation in future studies.

The HBV CP/EN domain is regulated by diverse host transcription factors, including HNF4A, SP1, RXRα, and C/EBP [1, 43]. Among these, HNF4A—a nuclear transcription factor capable of forming homodimers to bind DNA—critically regulates HBV replication by enhancing CP activity. Its specific target sequence consistently features tandem repeats resembling the half-site consensus sequence 5’-AGGTCA-3’ [1, 44, 45].

Our experiments collectively demonstrate that G4 mutation abolishes HNF4A binding; buffer conditions (Li+) critically affect affinity, indicating G4 structure involvement; and BRACO-19 significantly enhances HNF4A binding. We firstly revealed the G4 structure and its high affinity for HNF4A, particularly 1732, through SPR and pull-down experiments, demonstrating that G4 also serves as a binding target for HNF4A.

Although the 1204 mutant located in EN I region showed reduced binding to HNF4A compared with its wild-type counterpart, disruption of the 1204 did not significantly alter HNF4A-dependent CP/EN-driven promoter activity. This observation is consistent with previous reports showing that the G4 structure within the EN I region modulates cccDNA transcription mainly through structural perturbation rather than by reshaping the transcription-factor binding landscape [13].

In contrast, the 1732 mutant located within the CP/EN regulatory region exhibited a markedly reduced binding affinity for HNF4A. Moreover, disruption of the 1732 G4 exerted a measurable effect on HNF4A-dependent activation of CP/EN activity. As shown in Fig. 6, although overexpression of HNF4A significantly increased luciferase activity in cells transfected with the 1732-mut reporter compared with the mutant alone, the activity remained significantly lower than that observed in the wild-type 1732 reporter in the presence of HNF4A. These results indicate that HNF4A overexpression cannot fully compensate for the loss of the 1732 G4.

Taken together, our data suggest that, in addition to its classical regulation through canonical HNF4A binding sites, HNF4A can also functionally engage the CP/EN region through direct interaction with the 1732 G4. This effect is likely facilitated by the close genomic proximity between the classical HNF4A binding sites and the 1732 G4 element within the CP/EN regulatory region. Although both 1204 and 1732 contribute to the regulation of HBV gene expression, their functional cooperation with HNF4A is not equivalent. This difference may be attributed to the stronger affinity of 1732 for HNF4A, as revealed by SPR analysis, and/or to distinct G4 topologies formed by the two elements.

Importantly, our results support a dual regulatory model for HNF4A in controlling CP/EN activity. The dominant mechanism is G4-independent, in which HNF4A activates CP/EN through its classical DNA binding sites. In parallel, a secondary G4-dependent mechanism exists, whereby direct interaction between HNF4A and the 1732 G4 further enhances CP/EN activity and enables maximal promoter output. Thus, the 1732 G4 is not essential for HNF4A function but acts as a structural co-regulatory element that potentiates HNF4A-dependent activation of the HBV CP/EN region.

Although we confirmed that BRACO-19 significantly enhanced the direct interaction between the HNF4A protein and synthetic oligonucleotides containing G4 domains in the pull-down assay, future studies using HBV infection models and fully chromatinized cccDNA templates will be required to determine whether HNF4A directly associates with HBV cccDNA in vivo. Recent studies uncover that G4 structures can regulate HBV gene expression by promoting liquid-liquid phase separation [13]. Future investigations could precisely quantify G4’s impact on HBV transcription, replication, or translation by measuring nascent RNA levels or recruitment of distinct RNAP II forms. Further studies should also elucidate how CNBP recruitment modulates viral transcription and replication using expanded HBV infection models. Concurrently, mass spectrometry analysis should investigate the contributions of other host proteins recruited by the HBV CP/EN G4 in HBV gene expression.

Conclusions

We identified highly conserved two-quartet G4 structures across HBV genotypes. HBV-1732 forms a parallel G4 within the CP/EN II overlapping region, whereas HBV-1204 adopts an antiparallel G4 within the XP/EN I region. Both G4 elements contribute positively to HBV gene expression, but exert distinct regulatory effects. Notably, the 1732 G4 displays a high binding affinity for HNF4A and functions as a G4-dependent co-regulatory element that enhances HNF4A-mediated CP/EN promoter activity. In contrast, CNBP, the most highly enriched HBV G4-associated protein, promotes HBV antigen production and viral RNA accumulation through a mechanism independent of CP/EN-driven transcriptional activation.
