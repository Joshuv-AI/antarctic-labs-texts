Multiscale neural assimilation scheme for high-resolution sea surface temperature reconstruction from satellite observations
Authors: Maxime Beauchamp, Ioanna Karagali, Guisella Gacitúa, Jacob L. Høyer, Maxime Ballarotta, Ronan Fablet
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Sea Surface Temperature (SST) retrieved from satellites is a crucial variable for weather prediction and climate monitoring, yet satellite-based SST observations are often hindered by orbital constraints and cloud cover−especially in regions like the North and Baltic Seas where high-resolution data is essential to support operational monitoring and forecasting. Existing SST reanalysis products frequently lack the spatial and temporal granularity needed for these dynamic coastal areas. To address this, we explore an advanced data-driven approach to SST reconstruction using 4DVarNet, an end-to-end deep learning framework that integrates variational data assimilation with machine learning. While 4DVarNet has shown promising results in reconstructing SST fields, it lacks a robust mechanism for uncertainty quantification. We enhance the framework by incorporating a self-attention embedded variational autoencoder as a prior model, enabling probabilistic reconstruction and efficient sampling. The overall scheme includes a multiscale neural variational approach, self-supervised training, and the integration of pre-trained generative models. The proposed method demonstrates improved accuracy, resolving smaller spatial scales down to 33–45 km versus 59–69 km with current operational capabilities, faster convergence, and better representation of small-scale ocean features, offering a realistic path toward operational deployment of neural variational data assimilation techniques for high-resolution SST analysis in challenging regions.

Main

Sea Surface Temperature (SST) is a key variable in oceanographic and meteorological studies, central to understanding ocean circulation, weather forecasting, and global climate variability. SST modulates air-sea heat and moisture exchanges, playing a vital role in climate dynamics and operational services such as maritime navigation, ecosystem monitoring, and disaster risk management. Satellite-based remote sensing has enabled consistent and high-frequency SST monitoring at global scales through Infrared-based (IR) instruments such as AVHRR9, SLSTR27, and VIIRS25, or microwave-based (MW) data, see for instance AMSR232 or TMI29. These sensors provide valuable observations for capturing both large-scale patterns and finer-scale features such as coastal upwelling and mesoscale eddies.

However, satellite SST data are frequently affected by limitations−such as cloud cover for infrared sensors and rain contamination for microwave sensors−necessitating advanced gap-filling and data assimilation (DA) methods. A range of reanalysis and near-real-time (NRT) SST products has emerged over the last two decades, including global systems such as OSTIA33, TOPAZ36, and the Copernicus Marine Service (CMEMS) GLO12 product at \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$1/12^\circ$$\end{document} resolution23. These products rely on data assimilation techniques that combine satellite and in-situ observations, with some systems additionally incorporating ocean model outputs. However, for coastal regions like the North and Baltic Seas−characterized by complex coastlines and sub-mesoscale dynamics−global products often lack the spatial resolution required. To address this, regional high-resolution systems like the Danish Meteorological Institute’s DMI Optimal Interpolation (OI) SST product have been developed17,18. Operating at \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$0.02^\circ$$\end{document} \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\times$$\end{document} \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$0.02^\circ$$\end{document} resolution, DMI-OI uses Level-3 Super-collated (L3S) satellite data and a tailored OI scheme to capture regional ocean variability.

While DMI-OI performs well due to the relatively stable evolution of SST and high satellite data coverage, its interpolation framework has remained largely unchanged. In contrast, recent developments in Sea Surface Height (SSH) analysis have led to a proliferation of more advanced methods, including multiscale OI techniques2, dynamical priors rooted in quasi-geostrophic theory22, and data-driven methods such as Empirical orthogonal functions (EOF)-based reconstructions1,8 and analog techniques24,31. The convergence of machine learning (ML) and data assimilation (DA) offers new opportunities for solving high-dimensional reconstruction problems, reducing the complexity of DA system tuning. Within this context, we introduced 4DVarNet6, an end-to-end deep learning framework that leverages variational DA to jointly learn solvers and prior models. Originally designed for SSH data, 4DVarNet has shown strong performance in reconstructing sea surface states using altimetry from both conventional nadir sensors and wide swath altimeter datasets.

However, a key limitation of existing 4DVarNet implementations is the absence of uncertainty quantification. Indeed, while the baseline algorithm provides deterministic reconstructions, because trained on mean-squared error (MSE) loss, it does not natively represent uncertainty. Recent work has explored the integration of stochastic modeling using Stochastic Partial Differential Equations (SPDE) as generative approaches to incorporate prior interpretability and stochasticity into neural variational schemes7. Yet, few efforts have embedded state-of-the-art pre-trained priors into such frameworks. By embedding a VAE prior21 in the variational cost, the reconstruction process gains a probabilistic structure that enables sampling from the posterior distribution and thus explicitly quantify the uncertainty. VAE generative models are fast to sample from, produce smooth outputs, and are well-suited for efficient posterior estimation. Our main contributions are:A neural variational scheme that leverages multiscale inputs for high-resolution SST reconstruction in the North and Baltic Seas;A self-supervised training procedure involving random removal of observational patches to simulate realistic data gaps;The use of a pretrained generative prior model within 4DVarNet, enabling efficient posterior sampling and uncertainty modeling.The 4DVarNet framework outperforms the DMI-OI baseline across the North and Baltic Seas, reducing RMSE from 0.43 K to 0.41 K for the full domain on the test dataset covering daily outputs for the entire year 2021, and resolving smaller spatial scales down to 33–45 km versus 59–69 km with DMI-OI. Seasonal and regional analyses show consistent improvements, especially in spring and summer. The VAE-based extension enables efficient uncertainty quantification, with CRPS (Continuous Ranked Probability Score) values typically between 0.1–0.2, compared to 0.4 for DMI-OI. By transferring and extending methods developed in SSH-related research to SST applications, our approach offers a scalable path toward operational, uncertainty-aware SST products tailored for coastal and high-variability regions.

DMI optimal interpolation

The Operational Sea Surface Temperature Analysis (DMI-OI) system is run daily to generate L3S and L4 SST products for the Baltic Sea and North Sea using NRT infra-red SSTs from the Advanced Very High Resolution Radiometer (AVHRR) instruments on board NOAA (National Oceanographic and Atmospheric Administration) and MetOp (Meteorological Operational) satellites, the Spinning Enhanced Visible and Infrared Imager (SEVIRI) on board the Meteosat Second Generation (MSG) satellite, the Visible Infrared Imaging Radiometer Suite (VIIRS) on board the Suomi-NPP (Suomi National Polar-orbiting Partnership) and NOAA-20/21 and from the Sea and Land Surface Temperature Radiometer (SLSTR) on board the Sentinel 3-A/B satellites.

All satellite data valid for a particular day, i.e within 24 hours from the analysis are considered. The input L2P SST data undergo various QCs and processing steps to generate single-sensor Level 3 files which are bias adjusted using reference sensors and then are combined to the multi-sensor L3S product on which the OI scheme is further applied. For further details on the processing chain see17,18,20.

4DVarNet schemes

The variational formulation of the OI-related statistical inverse problem is known as 3D-Var. It often replaces the OI method based on BLUE for operational purposes, primarly because it offers greater flexibility in incorporating various types of observation, even when they have a nonlinear relationship with the model state. Additionally, it provides a more general framework for modeling all the components of the DA scheme, particularly the covariance matrices. In addition, both 3D-Var and BLUE provide the same optimal solution for Gaussian and linear dynamics. When this is not the case, 4D-Var is often considered as a variational formulation for time-dependent dynamical system extensions. In our case, the SST dynamic would generally be provided by numerical models as a background term used in the optimization process, making it computationally expensive due to the calculation of the adjoint term in the gradient of the variational cost. This explains why DL-based methods aim to combining traditional DA schemes with neural operators to bypass these issues.

Among these,13 introduces 4DVarNet, an end-to-end learning framework that builds upon variational DA to jointly learn prior models and solvers optimizing the reconstruction of a state from partial observations. In this subsection, we first recall the classical implementation of 4DVarNet schemes, then present the use of a VAE-based prior for their stochastic extension, and finally introduce a multiscale strategy that combines both approaches. Uncertainty quantification: deterministic vs. stochastic prior

In the current implementation of 4DVarNet, the scheme is fully deterministic. The outputs can be seen as the conditional expectation of the state given the observations available. We use as prior operator a two-scale UNet-like parameterization30 that helps in the bi-level optimization problem for both the variational cost and the training loss, and Eq. 3 can be restated as:4\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \min _{\Phi ,\Gamma } \mathop {\mathbb {E}}_{\textbf{x}, \textbf{y} \sim p^*} \left[ \Vert \textbf{x} - \textbf{x}^\star \Vert ^2 \right] \text{ s.t. } \textbf{x}^\star = \arg \min _{ \textbf{x}} \mathcal {J} \left[ \textbf{x}, \textbf{y} ; \lbrace {\Phi ,\Gamma \rbrace } \right] \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\Phi$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\Gamma$$\end{document} resp. denote the parameters of the UNet-based prior and iterative LSTM-based solver. This leads to a deterministic formulation of the 4DVarNet scheme. Though proved to be efficient, there is no information regarding the uncertainty associated with the reconstruction. Preliminary works suggests to train multiple 4DVarNet schemes to provide an ensemble of 4DVarNet predictions6 whose spread aligns on the posterior distribution. Although straightforward to implement, this approach captures only the uncertainty of the neural scheme. More interestingly,7 proposed to build stochastic trainable SPDE-based Gaussian priors embedded in 4DVarNet to sample prior members \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\Phi _i$$\end{document}, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$i=1,\cdots ,N$$\end{document} then conditioned by the solver as an estimation of the posterior. Such an approach can be generalized to any other state-of-the-art generative models, amongst them variational autoencoders21 that we proposed to use in this work as prior representation of the dynamics along the data assimilation window. If assuming a Gaussian likelihood, it writes:6\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} D_{KL} (p_{\Theta ^\star }(\textbf{x} \vert \textbf{y}) \Vert p_{\Theta }(\textbf{x} \vert \textbf{y}) )&= -\log p( \textbf{y}) + D_{KL} (p_{\Theta ^\star }(\textbf{x} \vert \textbf{y}) \Vert p_{\Theta }(\textbf{x} ) ) - \mathbb {E}_{\textbf{x} \sim p_{\Theta ^\star }(\textbf{x} \vert \textbf{y})} \left[ \log p_{\Theta }(\textbf{y} \vert \textbf{x})\right] \nonumber \\&\approx \Vert \textbf{y} - \textbf{H}\textbf{x}\Vert _2^2 + \Vert \textbf{x} -\textbf{x}^b\Vert _2^2 \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$D_{KL}$$\end{document} denotes the Kullback-Leiber divergence between the true and estimated posterior distributions here, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\textbf{x}^b$$\end{document} is the prior state sampled from \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$p_{\Theta }(\textbf{x})$$\end{document}. Because the number of parameters of the VAE is large, training 4DVarNet end-to-end requires GPU memory intensive computations, while making difficult the choice of the regularizations between all the training loss terms: MSE of the solver output, MSE of the VAE reconstruction and KL divergence associated to the VAE, see Eq. 8. That is why the VAE-based anomaly training can be done offline, before the 4DVarNet training of the solver, see Fig. 3. When involving the 4DVarNet solver, this will keep the features of the prior distribution far from the observations, while the classic implementation of the scheme obviously tends to zero when no observations are available. We expect the anomaly to exhibit location-dependent features. To account for this, we condition the prior scheme on additional inputs. Specifically, we use normalized coordinates, bathymetry, and the first-guess variance (as in the DMI-OI scheme) as proxies to guide the neural model in learning local adaptations

Multiscale approach

In its current configuration, 4DVarNet is not easy to train on very large domains, probably because the trainable prior and solver may be locally-specific which make the learning of this joint architecture difficult with a reasonable number of trainable parameters. Also, large domains, or small domains at high-resolution, generally implies a complex mixing of both large and mesoscale processes, which again suggests that multi-prior and or multi-solver extensions of the scheme should be developped. Results

To provide a detailed intercomparison exercise between the neural reconstruction scheme and the DMI-OI product, we use RMSE spatial and temporal metrics based on the Sentinel 3 SLSTR independent satellite dataset. Yearly and seasonally averaged analysis are provided together with corresponding spectral analysis to assess the actual scales retrieved by the two schemes, based on the original resolution targeted by DMI-OI at \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$0.02^\circ$$\end{document}. In particular, all spatial scales are significantly improved, as the high-resolution information from the L3S collated satellite observations within the 7-day assimilation window is effectively propagated into the reconstruction produced for the last day of the window, which constitutes the near-real-time product. The sequence of observations used in both interpolation schemes and their subsequent reconstructions shed light on the neural scheme’s ability to capture small-scale anomalies and temporal dynamics across various regions and conditions. This high-resolution enhancement is also visible along coastal regions (Fig. 6), which is often a limitation of traditional interpolation tools in which the space-time gaussian kernels prescribed in the OI schemes may not be fully adapted. The same applies for northern part of the interior Baltic where the delineation between ice surface temperature and sea surface temperature is intricate, or between Iceland and Norwegian seas where sharp fronts caused by the meeting of warm and cold water masses occur. The differences in the SST gradient field in the right panels arise mainly from the over-persistent propagation of the OI method in the coastal and interior Baltic, whereas the neural network has learned to propagate information not only in space but also in time, sometimes leading to smoother results when no relevant information is available for forward propagation in this specific region.

The key takeaway is that, in its current configuration, the neural scheme generally outperforms DMI-OI, with some seasonal and spatial variations. It significantly improves the baseline during spring and summer, performing well across the entire domain at all scales. In Autumn, results are more moderate in the North and Norwegian Seas, but still show a consistant improvement in the interior Baltic. It is also important to note that DMI-OI relies on a fixed set of parameters (e.g., first guess variance and spatial parametrization of the covariance kernel accross the domain). Consequently, both methods could likely benefit from seasonal or dynamical parameter adaptations. Future works could assess whether specific seasonal trainings on longer datasets, spanning around a decade for instance, might help mitigate this effect. However, when evaluating the operational feasibility of a new method, there is always a trade-off between the expected improvements over the existing operational scheme and the ease of transitioning from the former system to the new one. In this regard, the neural scheme is particularly appealing: with a single configuration, it generally outperforms DMI-OI while significantly reducing the computational costs. This is important not only for day-to-day production but even more for reanalysis and climate data records, where a neural implementation could process years of daily data in a few hours. As a reference, generating the 2021 test year of daily data on a single Nvidia GPU NVIDIA A100 with 80 GB of RAM took approximately 15 minutes. The model was trained on the same single GPU for 500 epochs, with a total training time of approximately 8 hours. Implementing parallelization of the scheme on multiple GPUs would also make the algorithm scalable at high resolution for large domains (even at a global scale) within minutes.

Uncertainty quantification

Ensemble-based methods in data assimilation provide a natural way to quantify uncertainty through the ensemble spread, see e.g4. However, they typically require a large number of members to adequately capture non-Gaussian structures, which can be computationally prohibitive for high-resolution satellite applications. In contrast, we explicitly made the link between VAE-based prior anomalies and posterior sampling in our neural scheme (Methods). This paves the way to fast generation of large ensembles of simulations in 4DVarNet schemes, for data-driven representation of uncertainty that can capture non-Gaussian and multimodal distributions at reduced computational cost. Here, we sample 50 conditional simulations from which we compute the ensemble-based posterior standard deviation, and we evaluate how it behaves w.r.t to the Gaussian uncertainty drawn from the posterior DMI-OI variance. In order to avoid the storage of large ensembles over the year, we perform the analysis only twice a month, thus leading to a test dataset of 24 days corresponding to the 1st and 16th of each month in 2021. Discussion

In this work, we present an in-depth exploration of the application and advantages of the 4DVarNet framework for reconstructing Sea Surface Temperature (SST) in the North and Baltic Seas. It emphasizes the integration of traditional data assimilation methods with neural networks to overcome the limitations of existing approaches such as the DMI-Optimal Interpolation (DMI-OI). 4DVarNet brings a significant improvement over the traditional DMI-OI technique, primarily due to its multiscale implementation, which allows for detailed reconstruction of SST anomalies and efficient handling of sparse spatio-temporal datasets. The combination of coarse approximation with fine-scale anomaly reconstructions leverages a patch-based training strategy, facilitating the handling of large domains while ensuring that the model remains sensitive to local small-scale patterns. Additionally, the integration of generative models−specifically variational autoencoder (VAE) priors−enhances the ability of the reconstructions to account for uncertainties, where traditional deep learning-based methods often fall short. This neural variational formulation not only provides smoother and more physically consistent reconstructions, but also naturally introduces stochasticity into the generative process, allowing for future extensions into uncertainty quantification and ensemble-based forecasting. Empirical results, validated against independent SLSTR observations and in-situ datasets, consistently demonstrate that the neural scheme outperforms OI in terms of reconstruction accuracy and the retrieval of smaller spatial scales. This is particularly evident in spring and summer, when both observational coverage and environmental variability align well with the training dataset. These findings are highly promising for operational SST forecasting, as they suggest that neural variational models can deliver more detailed and timely products, even in near-real-time applications. The computational efficiency and scalability of the 4DVarNet approach also facilitate integration into existing operational pipelines, offering the potential for higher-resolution monitoring without a significant increase in computational burden. However, challenges remain, particularly in winter months when sparser observational data can reduce RMSE-based performance in interior Baltic regions. Despite this, the spatial scales retrieved by 4DVarNet in these settings still surpass those captured by Optimal Interpolation methods, suggesting that performance remains operationally relevant. Nonetheless, this highlights a key limitation of the approach: model performance is tightly coupled to the quality and density of training data. Sparse or seasonally biased observational inputs can limit generalization, especially when extended to unseen conditions or poorly observed areas. Another open question concerns the robustness of the model under anomalous or extreme conditions, which may not be well-represented in the training set. To improve robustness and generalization, several directions are worth exploring. First, training on extended datasets that span multiple years and seasonal cycles could help the model better capture interannual variability and improve performance under underrepresented conditions. Second, incorporating domain adaptation strategies or physics-informed priors may enhance generalizability across different ocean regions. Third, expanding this methodology to other sea surface variables−such as Sea Surface Height (SSH)−is a natural next step. In particular, variables like SSH present additional challenges due to their inherently noisier and more incomplete satellite observations, but also offer rich dynamical information that could benefit from the same multiscale and stochastic reconstruction techniques. From a pipeline perspective, implementing 4DVarNet in a real-time or near-real-time setting would require further streamlining of preprocessing, assimilation of incoming observations, and fast inference mechanisms. The fast sampling capabilities of the model and its flexibility for fine-tuning on specific domains make it a strong candidate for integration into operational centers. Future implementations could also benefit from active learning strategies, where the model actively identifies spatio-temporal regions where additional data would be most valuable, thereby optimizing assimilation in real-time monitoring systems. Finally, this pipeline could be further extended to incorporate sparse in-situ observations, which provide a more direct measure of the ”true” SST, in contrast to satellite observations that may contain systematic errors. For example, an additional term could be added to the inner variational cost to guide the model in correcting satellite-based SST toward the in-situ measurements. Extensions can also be considered for Climate Data Records (CDRs), where observations are also available in the future. On this point, the reprocessing would be even easier than before due to the computational efficiency of the neural scheme. Finally, on the stochastic modeling side, future research could aim to improve uncertainty quantification not only for anomaly fields but also for coarse approximations. The current additive model assumption may not always hold, especially in cases where the coarse component itself is uncertain−such as during rapidly changing oceanographic conditions or in poorly observed areas. A promising direction would be to redesign the 4DVarNet architecture to jointly learn both coarse and anomaly fields, allowing the model to escape the pure additive decomposition and potentially offering new insights into multiscale coupling between large-scale and (sub)mesoscale processes.

Conclusion

The neural data assimilation framework proposed in this study demonstrates the transformative potential of combining machine learning with traditional physical modeling for environmental monitoring. By addressing limitations in current SST reconstruction approaches−particularly in high-resolution, data-sparse regions like the North and Baltic Seas−4DVarNet offers a scalable, accurate, and computationally efficient alternative. Its successful integration of multiscale modeling, uncertainty quantification, and patch-based learning underscores its promise for operational implementation. As data sources and environmental demands evolve, such hybrid frameworks are likely to play a key role in the next generation of oceanographic reanalysis and forecasting systems.
