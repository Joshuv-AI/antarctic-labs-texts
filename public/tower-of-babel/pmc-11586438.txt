Heteroatom-doped magneto-fluorescent carbon dots, a potent agent for multimodal imaging
Authors: Abolghasem Abbasi Kajani, Ali Pouresmaeili, Masoud Ayatollahi Mehrgardi, Shaghayegh Haghjooy Javanmard
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
A simple, one-pot and green method is reported for hydrothermal synthesis of highly fluorescent and magnetic carbon dots (CDs) by using D-glucose, as the carbon source. CDs were fully characterized by the UV-Vis and fluorescence spectroscopy, DLS, FTIR, TEM, EDS, XRD, and VSM. The nitrogen doping of different diamines significantly improved the fluorescence quantum yield (QY) of CDs with the maximum effect obtained by using m-phenylenediamine (mPDA). Temperature and reaction time also affected the QY of CDs with the best results obtained at 150 °C for 3 h. The heteroatom doping by innovative use of different metal sulfates including FeSO4, MnSO4, CuSO4, MgSO4, and ZnSO4, further improved the optical properties of CDs. Interestingly, the magnetic and multicolor CDs with high colloidal stability and QYs of 17.7, 16.5, and 53.9% at 460, 490, and 515 nm, respectively, were synthesized by using 0.1 M of glucose, mPDA and MnSO4. The resulted Mn-, S-, N-doped CDs represented rapid uptake and high-quality fluorescence imaging of the human fibroblast and umbilical vein endothelial cells in vitro, without significant toxicity. The CDs also displayed high r1 relaxivity of 32.3 mM− 1 s− 1 and were used for high-contrast MR and fluorescence imaging of mouse tumor models, in vivo.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-024-80531-x.

Introduction

The fluorescent nanoparticles (NPs) with the superior physicochemical properties, including bright, stable and long-time emission, have found great attention, as an appropriate alternative to the organic fluorophores1. Among different kinds of fluorescent NPs, carbon dots (CDs) have found great attention due to their prominent advantages, especially simple synthesis and functionalization, high biocompatibility, hydrophilic nature, as well as superior and stable luminescence properties2–4. The zero-dimensional CDs consisted of a sp2/sp3 carbon structure with the oxygen/nitrogen-based groups5–8. The growing applications of CDs in the various applications such as bioimaging, sensing, catalysis, theranostics, and energy harvesting, encouraged the scientists to develop the simple, efficient and low-cost methods for the synthesis of CDs with the novel and superior characteristics9.

Different organic compounds such as carbohydrates, proteins, and amino acids have been extensively used as the carbon source for the synthesis of biocompatible CDs10. Among them, the food-grade carbohydrates are excellent candidates for the large-scale and cost-effective synthesis of biocompatible CDs, owing to their high biocompatibility, availability, and water solubility. In this context, glucose has been of great attention for the synthesis of fluorescent CDs by different methods and various optical properties11–17.

Different strategies, especially heteroatom doping, surface functionalization and passivation, have been extensively used to improve the optical properties of CDs18,19. Among them, the heteroatom doping has been considered as a facile, robust and effective approach to develop CDs with the improved optical properties and functionalities3,20,21. Different metal dopants such as Mn, Mg, Cu, Zn, Gd, Ge, and Tb, as well as the non-metal dopants such as N, S, P, and B, have been used individually (single-doping) or in combination (co-doping) for the engineering of optical and electronic properties of CDs22. Among different dopants, nitrogen (N-) and sulfur (S-) have been extensively used to improve the photoluminescence of CDs23,24. However, the potential toxicity of metal-atom dopants is the main obstacle for the biomedical applications25. The co-doping approach not only considered for the improvement of optical properties of CDs, mainly via the “surface passivation-like” effects, but also could enhance their active sites that leads to the new activities of CDs25. The heteroatoms doping has also been suggested as a promising rout to improve the quantum yields (QYs) of CDs as well as to increase the functional surface groups26.

Here, based on a comprehensive and comparative study, the multicolor fluorescent CDs with the high biocompatibility and QYs were synthesized using a green, simple and efficient hydrothermal method. Glucose was used as an abundant, renewable, and cost-effective source of carbon. To improve the optical properties of CDs, different diamines and metal sulfates, were also used as co-dopants. The multicolor CDs with the high fluorescence QYs and biocompatibility obtained in the optimum condition were used in vitro and in vivo, as the fluorescent and magnetic contrast agents for the high-quality multimodal imaging.

Materials and methods

Materials

D-(+)-glucose, dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO), ethylenediamine (EDA), ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA), fluorescein, m-phenylenediamine (mPDA), o-phenylenediamine (oPDA), FeSO4.7H2O, MnSO₄.H₂O, CuSO4, MgSO4, and ZnSO4.7H2O were purchased from Merck (Darmstadt, Germany). Roswell Park Memorial Institute medium (RPMI-1640), Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM), fetal bovine serum (FBS), trypsin-EDTA solution, and antibiotic solution (penicillin-streptomycin) were prepared from Gibco (Invitrogen, Grand Island, NY). Fluorescein, 3-(4,5-dimethylthiazol-2-yl)-2,5-diphenyltetrazolium bromide (MTT), the microbial culture media including Mueller-Hinton; Blood agar; Dixon agar; and Potato-Dextrose, were purchased from Sigma (Sigma-Aldrich, Germany). All of the aqueous solutions were prepared with double-distilled water. The normal human fibroblast (HFB) and umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVEC) were provided from Pasteur Institute of Iran.

Synthesis of CDs

CDs were hydrothermally synthesized by using an aqueous solution of D-glucose (20 mL, 0.1 M final concentration) in a Teflon-lined autoclave at 150 °C. The reaction condition was optimized in several steps. The potential synthesis of fluorescent CDs was first investigated without using of nitrogen source. The effect of N-doping was then studied using different diamines including EDA, EDTA, mPDA, and oPDA, as the nitrogen sources, at different concentrations of 0.05, 0.1, and 0.2 M. In the next step, the synthesis reaction was optimized for different temperatures (150, 200, and 250 °C) and reaction times (2, 3, 4, 5, 6 and 8 h). Subsequently, several metal sulfates including FeSO4.7H2O, MnSO₄.H₂O, CuSO4, MgSO4, and ZnSO4.7H2O (in the final concentration of 0.05 M) were used in the individual reactions and the fluorescence properties of CDs were evaluated. The large particles and impurities were precipitated and removed by centrifugation at 6000 rpm for 15 min and the colloidal CDs were filtered and freeze-dried before further use. The purification of CDs from the unreacted precursors was performed by antisolvent precipitation using isopropanol and subsequent centrifugation at 6000 rpm for 30 min, according to the previous report27.

Characterization of CDs

The UV-Vis spectrophotometer (Lambda 265, Perkin Elmer) and fluorescence spectrophotometer (RF-5000, Shimadzu) were used to investigate the absorption and emission spectra of CDs. The hydrodynamic size and surface charge of CDs were determined using the SZ-100 zeta potential analyzer (Horiba, Japan). The surface functional groups of CDs were identified using a Fourier transform infrared (FT-IR) spectrometer (Jasco FT/IR-6300) at the wavelength range of 4000 cm− 1 to 400 cm− 1. The size, morphology and electron diffraction patterns of the final products were evaluated using the high-resolution transmission electron microscopy (HR-TEM) at an accelerating voltage of 80–300 kV. The elemental composition of CDs was determined by using the X-ray energy dispersive spectroscopy (EDS, Silicon drift detectors, Germany). The magnetic activity of CDs was analyzed using a vibration sample magnetometer (VSM; Lake Shore Model 7400, Japan). The fluorescence quantum yield (QY) of CDs was calculated by the following equation using fluorescein (QY = 95%) as a standard:

To minimize the re-absorption effects, the samples (CDs and fluorescein) were first diluted with water (refractive index, 1.33) until their absorbance at the excitation wavelength was less than 0.1.

In vitro studies

The biocompatibility of CDs was studied in vitro on HFB and HUVEC cell lines via the MTT assay, based on a previous report5. Briefly, 104 of as-proliferated cells with 200 µL RPMI 1640 culture medium were added into each well of a 96-well plate before overnight incubation at 37 °C in a humidified 5% CO2 incubator. The cells were then exposed with different concentrations (10, 50, 100, and 200 µg mL− 1) of CDs for 48 h at the same condition. The medium was then completely depleted from the wells and 100 µL of MTT solution (0.5 mg mL− 1 in media) was added and the plate was incubated again at the same condition for 4 h. Finally, the medium was removed and the precipitated formazan crystals were dissolved in 150 µL of DMSO and the absorbance was measured at 570 nm. The cell viability was calculated as the ratio of absorbance values from each treatment and the control. The final results were presented as the mean value of three independent experiments ± standard deviation. The morphology of cell was also evaluated by optical microscope after exposure to CDs for 48 h. An inverted microscope (Nikon, Eclipse Ti-U) was also used for the fluorescence imaging of the cells after 3 h incubation with CDs.

Relaxivity measurement

The sensitivity (relaxivity) of CDs and the commercial contrast agent of gadodiamide (Omniscan™) was measured and compared. MRI studies were carried out in the Shafa medical imaging center, Najafabad, Isfahan, Iran, by using a 1.5 T clinical MRI system (Avanto, siemens, Germany) equipped with a 32-channel head coil. CDs with different concentrations of manganese (0, 10, 25, 50, 100, 250, 500, and 1000 mM) were dispersed in double distilled water and used for T1 and T2 weighted MR imaging, according to the following parameters:

T1: TE:10 TR:150, 300, 500, 750, 1000, 2000 (ms) TE:12 TR:33, 100, 500, 750, 1000 (ms).

T2: TE:92 TR: 1500, 2000, 2500, 3000, 5000 (ms).

FoV: 120.140, slice thickness: 2 mm, Matrix size: 256.256.

The signal of each voxel was then obtained using PacsPlusCV software and the longitudinal relaxivity (R1) of Mn-CDs and gadodiamide were plotted linearly using the R1 = 1/T1 formula.

In vivo magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) and fluorescent imaging

The ability of the synthesized CDs for the in vivo MRI and fluorescent imaging was studied on the mouse models of breast cancer. For the cancer induction, the highly tumorigenic mammary carcinoma cell line of 4T1 was first cultured in the DMEM culture medium supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% antibiotics solution. The proliferated 4T1 cells (106) were then used for the injection and induction of the breast tumor in the six to eight-weeks-old female Balb/c mice. T1-weighted MR imaging of the mice was performed using a 1.5T MRI system (Avanto, siemens, Germany) equipped with a 32-channel head coil. For this purpose, the mice were first anesthetized by 60 mg/15 mg of ketamine/xylazine and then, a dose of 20 mg CDs per kg of mice (6.7 mg Mn per kg of mice) was injected into the tumors. The imaging was performed at the injection time and also after 30 min, by using Spin echo, fat sat, and Grade imaging protocols. The T1-weighted images values were as follows: for Fat sat including TR: 8.9 ms, TE: 2.4 ms, Slice thickness: 1 mm, Matrix size: 224*134, and for spin echo including TR: 344 ms, TE: 9ms, Slice thickness: 2 mm, and Matrix size: 256*164. The in vivo fluorescent imaging of mouse tumor models was performed using a KODAK in-vivo FX Pro system (Carestream Health, Rochester, NY) at Tehran University of Medical Sciences, Tehran, Iran. The excitation wavelength was set at 360 nm and the emission was recorded at 400 nm. All the animal experiments were approved by the Ethics Committee of the Isfahan University of Medical Sciences (Approval ID: IR.MUI.MED.REC.1399.770). The study is reported in accordance with ARRIVE guidelines. All the experiments were also performed in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations.

Statistical analysis

All the in vitro experiments were carried out in triplicate and the final results were presented as the mean value ± standard deviation (SD). Student’s t-test (Microsoft Excel, Microsoft Corporation, USA) was used for data analysis and P values of less than 0.05 were considered as the statistically significant.

Results and discussion

Synthesis and characterization of CDs

This study was conducted to develop a green and simple method for the synthesis of multicolor and magnetic CDs by using glucose, as the carbon source, for the biomedical applications. The well-known biocompatibility of CDs and their superior optical properties make them a promising alternative to the traditional contrast agents for in vitro and in vivo bioimaging28. The initial attempts toward the hydrothermal synthesis of fluorescent CDs by using glucose, as the only precursor of reaction, were failed and no fluorescence emission was detected from the resulted CDs. Therefore, in the second step, the N-doping approach was used by the addition of different diamines including EDA, EDTA, oPDA and mPDA, to obtain the fluorescent N-CDs. The N-doping, whether substitute the carbon atoms or participate in a pendant amine or amide group, has been reported to lead the substantial changes in the electronic properties of the CDs9. Our results (Fig. 1) also clearly showed that the N-doping led to the synthesis of fluorescent CDs with different properties, depending on the type of diamine. The synthesized N-CDs displayed excitation-dependent fluorescence emission with the highest QYs of 7.2 and 30.1% at the emission wavelengths of 470 and 520 nm, respectively, by using 0.1 M of mPDA (Table S1). The surface state is well-known as one of the most effective factors on the optical properties of CDs29. N-doping was reported to increase the defect states density of CDs by the introduction of N surface states, leading to the facilitated electron trapping and direct recombination of excitons which finally results in the improved QY of N-CDs25.

Subsequently, the effect of reaction time and temperature was studied on the QY of N-CDs synthesized with glucose and mPDA. The increase of reaction temperature from 150 to 250 °C not only had no significant effect on the fluorescence QY of N-CDs but also reduced their colloidal stability and led to the precipitation. The significant decrease in the colloidal stability and QY of CDs by the increasing of the reaction time have also been reported previously and attributed to the destruction of surface functional groups, which led to the agglomeration of NPs7,30.

The synthesized N-CDs with different reaction times displayed two remarkable emission peaks in the blue and green regions (Figure S1) with different QYs. The best results obtained by the hydrothermal reaction at 150 °C for 3 h with the highest QYs of 11.1 and 35.6% at 470 and 520 nm, respectively (Table 1). The carbonization degree plays an effective role in the structure and fluorescence properties of the CDs. Therefore, the temperature and reaction time could affect the synthesis of CDs by adjusting of the carbonization process. The enhanced carbonization increases the contribution of carbon core state in the photoluminescent of CDs than the molecular state. This condition also leads to the higher graphitization and subsequent expansion of the conjugated structures that could decrease the bandgap and leads to the red-shift emission of CDs31.

In the next step, the effect of heteroatom doping on the optical properties of the synthesized CDs was investigated by the addition of different metal sulfate salts, as a source of sulfur and metal atoms. The heteroatom doping has also been reported to improve the QY of CDs by the creation of new surface functionalities and surface defects32. The S-doping has been reported to increase the fluorescence intensity of CDs by affecting their electronic structure, owing to the different electronegativity of sulfur and its lone pair of electrons31.

The results showed that the using of metal sulfates has a significant effect, not only on the quantity (fluorescence intensity) but also on the quality (emission wavelengths) of photoluminescence of N-CDs (Table S2). For example, the addition of FeSO4 and H2SO4 led to the disappearance of blue emission wavelength, while the addition of CuSO4 resulted in the decay of green wavelength. The presence of FeSO4, MgSO4, or ZnSO4 also led to the red-shift emission (Fig. 2). The composition and surface oxidation of CDs can affect their optical properties, especially the wavelength shift, by the modification of surface charge19. The highest fluorescence QYs obtained by using MnSO4, such that Mn, S,N-CDs with the maximum QYs of 17.7, 16.5, and 53.9% at 460, 490, and 515 nm, respectively, were synthesized in the presence of 0.05 M MnSO4. Meanwhile, in the presence of sulfuric acid, the resulted S, N-CDs displayed less fluorescence intensity (maximum QY of 4% at 500–515 nm), which indicates the significant effect of Mn atoms on the improved fluorescence emission of CDs. The Mn-doping has been reported to improve the N-doping content of CDs and thus, leads to the higher QYs of Mn, N-CDs33. The blue photoluminescence of CDs has been reported to originate from the radiative recombination of electron-hole pairs in the carbon cores while the green photoluminescence has been attributed to the radiative relaxation of the excited state to ground state in CDs due to the abundance of oxygen-containing groups34. Therefore, the change in emission spectrum of CDs in the presence of sulfuric acid could be attributed to the higher oxidation degree of their surface35. Thus, the multi-color CDs with the outstanding QYs were obtained in the presence of MnSO4 (Mn, S,N-CDs) via the stepwise optimization of the synthesis process.

The S-doping has also been reported to improve the radiative recombination of CDs by increasing the density of N state, which leads to the higher fluorescence QYs and broader excitation-independent emission of N, S-doped CDs in comparison to N-doped CDs25,26,36. A quantum yield of 9.59% has been reported by Liu et al. for CDs synthesized using glucose, ethylenediamine, and phosphoric acid as C, N, and P dopants16. The optical properties of CDs are mainly determined by different electronic transitions of their electronic structure, including σ→σ*, σ→π*, π→π*, n→π*, and n→σ*. The heteroatom dopants could modulate the optical properties of CDs by affecting the interaction of π- and n-states proportional to their ability for withdrawing/donating the electrons26. As metal ions contain more unoccupied orbitals with electrons that could be easily loosed, the metal ions are more effective than nonmetallic heteroatoms as dopant to change the charge density and optical properties of CDs as well as the introduction of new functionalities. However, on the other hand, the coordination of metal ions with the surface amino and carboxyl groups of CDs could lead to the fluorescence quenching26.

HR-TEM imaging of the resulted Mn, S,N-CDs (Fig. 3a) showed that NPs have the semispherical shape with the sizes of less than 20 nm. Mn, S,N-CDs have an average hydrodynamic size of 49.4 nm and polydispersity index (PDI) of 0.478 (Figure S2) with a ζ potential value of − 58.5 (Figure S3). The synthesized Mn, S,N-CDs represented high water dispersibility and colloidal stability without any agglomeration and precipitation after six months storage at room temperature. Moreover, the fluorescence QY of CDs did not show any significant change after long-time storage at the light condition. The XRD pattern of Mn, S,N-CDs (Fig. 3b) exhibits a relatively broad and noisy diffraction peak concentrated around 13° (2θ), resembling the imperfect graphite crystals22,37. FTIR spectrum of Mn, S,N-CDs (Fig. 3c) contains several absorption peaks including 3405 cm− 1, 3241 cm− 1, 2947 cm− 1, 1768 cm− 1, 1686 cm− 1, 1611 cm− 1, 1431 cm− 1, 1373 cm− 1, 1104 cm− 1, and 879 cm− 1, corresponding to the O − H vibration of alcohols and water, N − H stretching vibration of amides, C − H stretching of alkanes, C = O stretching of carbonyl groups, COO stretching of carboxylic acids, C = C stretching of alkenes, C − O−C symmetric stretching of glycosidic linkages, C − N stretching of amines, C − O stretching of alcohols, and C − H stretching of hydrocarbons, respectively5,37,38. The results clearly showed the efficient surface functionalization of CDs with different functional groups that are necessary for the subsequent surface engineering of CDs. The high hydrophilic nature of Mn, S,N-CDs could also be attributed to the different functional groups on their surface, especially hydroxyl, carboxyl and amino groups39.

The elemental composition of Mn, S,N-CDs (Fig. 3d) includes C, N, O, Na, P, S, and Mn with the weight percentages of 14.13%, 5.57%, 19.98%, 6.87%, 2.64%, 17.04%, and (33.78%). Interestingly, Mn, S,N-CDs also represented the considerable magnetic activity with the magnetic saturation value of 0.8 emu.g− 1 at 30 ◦C (Figure S4) that could be attributed to the Mn-doping. Therefore, Mn-doping not only improved the fluorescence intensity of S, N-CDs but also induced their magnetic activity. Several studies have been reported the role of Mn-doping on the induction of magnetic activity in CDs18,27,40–42.

The UV-Vis absorption spectrum of the colloidal Mn-CDs (Figure S5) shows a strong band at 255 nm that has been attributed to the π − π* transition of the aromatic rings formed during the carbonization process via the reactions of the initial functional groups. The broad band located around 350–450 nm has been attributed to the n–π* electronic transition due to the trapping of the surface functional groups of Mn, S,N-CDs into the excited state energy37.

In vitro studies and in vivo imaging

Regarding the importance of the biocompatibility for the biomedical application of NPs, the potential toxicity of Mn, S,N-CDs on the HFB and HUVEC cells was evaluated, in vitro. The results (Fig. 4a) clearly showed the high biocompatibility of Mn, S,N-CDs, such that more than 82% and 86% viability of the HUVEC and HFB cells obtained after 48 h incubation with 200 µg mL− 1 of Mn, S,N-CDs. The results of MTT assay were also confirmed by the monitoring of cells morphology changes after 48 h exposure with CDs (Figure S6). The high-quality fluorescence imaging of the cells (Fig. 4a) was also obtained only after 2 h incubation of the cells with Mn, S,N-CDs that indicates the rapid and efficient cell uptake of CDs and their high potential as the contrast agent.

The contrast agents are used to increase the signal intensity of the abnormal areas43,44, while different factors such as size, shape, composition, surface chemistry, and the magnetic properties significantly influence the quality of contrast45. The gadolinium-based contrast agents are the most commonly used agents for MRI, while the toxic effects of gadolinium are a main concern of the human health which can cause the nephrogenic fibrosis46. Therefore, many efforts have been made to develop new, efficient and biocompatible contrast agents. In this context, the NPs-based contrast agents have been greatly considered and many reports are now available regarding the high potential of magnetic NPs as the MRI contrast agent.

The potential application of Mn, S,N-CDs as the MRI contrast agent was then investigated. Regarding the significant role of relaxivity of the contrast agents on the quality of MRI results, the r1 relaxivity of Mn, S,N-CDs was determined by the measurement of the T1 relaxation times of different concentrations of Mn, S,N-CDs. The results (Fig. 4b) showed a good linear relationship in the different TE values and also a higher contrast value of Mn, S,N-CDs than the gadodiamide. The slope of the fitted line to 1/T1 against the Mn concentrations (Fig. 4c), represents the r1 relaxivity of Mn, S,N-CDs. The calculated r1 value for the commercial gadodiamide was 8.1 mM− 1 s− 1 while a r1 value of 32.3 mM− 1 s− 1 was obtained for Mn, S,N-CDs, indicating that the r1 value of Mn, S,N-CDs is approximately 4 folds higher than that for the same concentrations of commercial gadodiamide. Such a high r1 value of Mn, S,N-CDs could be attributed to the high hydrophilicity and nanoscale size of Mn, S,N-CDs, which leads to the less distance of Mn cations with the water protons37. The relaxivity of Mn, S,N-CDs is about 5 times higher than that of the loaded gadolinium nanodroplets, reported by Maqsoodinia et al. in 202147. The relaxivity value of Mn, S,N-CDs was also 6.5 times higher than the relaxivity of gadolinium-doped CDs reported by Bouzas-Ramos et al., recently48.

The potential of Mn, S,N-CDs as the MRI contrast agent was also studied in vivo, on a breast cancer mouse model. The results (Fig. 5a) showed the high ability of Mn, S,N-CDs to clearly distinguish the tumor area from the neighboring tissues. Fat sat and T1 images of the cancer mouse model showed a higher signal in the presence of the CDs inside the tumor tissue, compared with the control (in the absence of CDs). The signal intensity of the tumor tissue before and after injection was 22.9 and 68.3 s− 1, which indicates an increase of 3.1 times in the emitted signal from the tumor area after injection. The results indicate that Mn, S,N-CDs can effectively shorten the spin-lattice relaxation time of water protons and increase the longitudinal recovery rate which leads to the excellent positive (bright) contrast after the injection to the tumor cells. Therefore, Mn, S,N-CDs can be a promising candidate, as an alternative to the toxic gadolinium-based contrast agents49,50.

The fluorescence imaging of the tumor mouse models also showed the high potential of Mn, S,N-CDs for the high quality imaging of tumors (Fig. 5b). The high-intensity signals were received from the tumors only 20 min after the injection, indicating the efficient uptake and retention of the CDs in this region such that the tumor boundaries were successfully delineated.

The dual-modal (MR/optical) imaging provides the accurate and sensitive diagnosis by combining of the advantages of the high soft-tissue contrast and spatial resolution of MR contrast agents with the rapid data collection and high sensitivity of the optical probes37. In this context, the using of dual magnetic/fluorescent nanoprobes is of great attention for the one-step imaging and thus, to reduce the time and costs of diagnosis as well as the toxicity risk of using different contrast agents.

Conclusion

In the present study, a hydrothermal method was stablished for the synthesis of high fluorescent and magnetic CDs. The present method, unlike many previously reported methods, is performed at the relatively low temperature (150 °C) and short time (3 h) by using of the easily available and cost-effective precursor of glucose, as the carbon source. Temperature and reaction time had significant effects on the physico-chemical properties of the synthesized CDs. The N-doping was recognized to efficiently improve the fluorescence QY of CDs with the maximum effect of mPDA. The innovative use of the metal sulfates for the S-doping, led to the improved fluorescence QY of CDs with the best results obtained using MnSO4. The using of manganese sulfate also led to the magnetic activity of the resulted heteroatoms-doped CDs. Based on the results, the metal-doping, especially Mn-doping, could be suggested as an efficient approach for the improvement of the fluorescence properties of CDs as well as other physico-chemical properties such as the magnetic activity. The present method is safe and green with the less concerns for the human health and environment and could be easily extended to the large-scale production of CDs. The significant fluorescence and magnetic activity, the excellent biocompatibility, high water dispersity and colloidal stability, the appropriate surface functionality, and the rapid and efficient cell uptake of the resulted Mn, S,N-CDs, make them an ideal candidate for the biomedical applications, especially for the fluorescence/MR imaging. The biocompatible and fluorescent Mn, S,N-CDs also represented the high r1 relaxivity of 32.3 mM− 1 s− 1 and were successfully used for the in vivo MR imaging of the mouse tumor models, which lead to an enhanced MR T1 contrast in the breast tumor. Unlike the commonly used gadolinium-based contrast agents that are toxic, due to the inhibitor effects of gadolinium ions on the calcium channels, Mn cations are considered as a biocompatible and appropriate candidate for development of new and efficient CDs-based contrast agent for the fluorescence/MR imaging40. The investigation of long-time in vivo biodistribution and toxicity of heteroatoms-doped CDs, the active targeting of CDs by the appropriate targeting molecules, such as antibodies and aptamers, and also the increasing of permeation time via the development of CDs-loaded nanoformulations such as liposomes can be suggested for the future studies.
