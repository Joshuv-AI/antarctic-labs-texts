A resource theoretical unification of Mpemba effects: classical and quantum
Alessandro Summer, Mattia Moroder, Laetitia P. Bettmann, Xhek Turkeshi, Iman Marvian, John Goold

Abstract
The Mpemba effect originally referred to the observation that, under certain thermalizing dynamics, initially hotter samples can cool faster than colder ones. This effect has since been generalized to other anomalous relaxation behaviors even beyond classical domains, such as symmetry restoration in quantum systems. This work demonstrates that resource theories, widely employed in information theory, provide a unified organizing principle to frame Mpemba physics. We show how the conventional thermal Mpemba effect arises naturally from the resource theory of athermality, while its symmetry-restoring counterpart is fully captured by the resource theories of asymmetry. Leveraging the framework of modes of asymmetry, we demonstrate that the Mpemba effect due to symmetry restoration is governed by the initial overlap with the slowest symmetry-restoring mode – mirroring the role of the slowest Liouvillian eigenmode in thermal Mpemba dynamics. Through this resource-theoretical formalism, we uncover the connection between these seemingly disparate effects and show that the dynamics of thermalization naturally splits into a symmetry-respecting and a symmetry-breaking term.

I.1
Resource Theories

Resource Theories (RTs)
(see Ref.
Chitambar and Gour 2019
for a review) provide the natural language for understanding and unifying diverse Mpemba effects: they formalize which state transformations are possible when only certain
free
operations are allowed, and thereby translate relaxation processes into the decay of a resource monotone
Goold et al. 2016
.
In a nutshell, a resource theory is defined by two foundational ingredients: a set of free state and a class of free operations. Free operations are
completely positive and trace-preserving (CPTP)
maps that are considered accessible at no cost, either because they are physically implementable with minimal resources or because they reflect fundamental constraints of the theory. Similarly, the set of free states, denoted
{F}\subset{S}({H}_{s})
(where
{S}({H}_{s})
indicates the set of density operators on the Hilbert space
{H}_{s}
), consists of states that can be prepared at no cost. A minimal requirement that any resource theory must satisfy is that the set of free states is closed under free operations (as defined later in this section); that is, for any free state
\hat{\pi}\in{F}
and any free operation
\mathcal{E}
, we have
\mathcal{E}[\hat{\pi}]\in{F}
. This closure condition ensures that no resource can be generated from within the free set.
All states outside the free set, i.e.,
{S}({H}_{s})\setminus{F}

2
2

2

We indicate by
{S}({H}_{s})
the set of density matrices on
{H}_{s}
.
, are called resourceful or non-free. These are states that cannot be obtained from any free state using only free operations. Their preparation requires access to non-free operations, and they are typically the carriers of some operational advantage within the theory.
The presence and quantification of such states lie at the core of resource-theoretic approaches.

To familiarize with these concepts, we recall two prominent examples of quantum resource theories: bipartite entanglement and magic state resources. In the theory of bipartite entanglement, the free states are separable states, and the free operations are
local operations and classical communication (LOCC)

Horodecki et al. 2009
. Entangled states lie outside the free set and enable tasks such as quantum teleportation or the violation of Bell inequalities, which are impossible under
LOCC
alone. In the theory of magic state resources, relevant to fault-tolerant quantum computing, the free states are stabilizer states, and the free operations are Clifford unitaries and stabilizer-preserving measurements
Gottesman 1998
. Resourceful states, such as
\ket{T}
states, are essential to promote Clifford circuits to universal quantum computation, highlighting their role as a computational resource.

A resource monotone is a function
M:{S}({H}_{s})\to[0,\infty)
satisfying two key properties:

1.

Vanishing for free states:
\hat{\rho}\in{F}\Rightarrow M(\hat{\rho})=0\,.

2.

Monotonicity under free operations: for every state
\hat{\rho}
and every
\mathcal{E}\in{O}
,

M(\mathcal{E}[\hat{\rho}])\leq M(\hat{\rho})\,,

(1)

where free operations
{O}
consist of all
CPTP
maps
\mathcal{E}:{H}_{s}\to{H}_{s}
that preserve the free set.

A Mpemba effect manifests when two initial states
\hat{\rho}_{1}
and
\hat{\rho}_{2}
satisfying
M(\hat{\rho}_{1})>M(\hat{\rho}_{2})
evolve under the same free operation
\mathcal{E}_{t}
so that

M\bigl(\mathcal{E}_{t}[\hat{\rho}_{1}]\bigr)<M\bigl(\mathcal{E}_{t}[\hat{\rho}_{2}]\bigr)\quad\text{for some }t>0\,,

(2)

indicating that the more resourceful state has dissipated the resource faster.

To quantify resourcefulness, we adopt the family of Petz-Rényi
\alpha
-divergences
Mosonyi and Hiai 2011

M_{\alpha}(\hat{\rho})=\min_{\hat{\pi}\in{F}}S_{\alpha}\bigl(\hat{\rho}\,\|\,\hat{\pi}\bigr)\,,\quad\alpha\in(0,1)\cup(1,2]\,,

(3)

where

\begin{split}S_{\alpha}(\hat{\rho}\|\hat{\pi})=&\frac{1}{\alpha-1}\!\ln\mathrm{Tr}[\hat{\rho}^{\alpha}\,\hat{\pi}^{\,1-\alpha}]\,,\end{split}

(4)

obeys the data-processing inequality under any
CPTP
map
Mosonyi and Hiai 2011
;
Chitambar and Gour 2019
and recovers the quantum relative entropy of resource for
\alpha\rightarrow 1
, i.e.,

M(\hat{\rho})=\min_{\hat{\pi}\in{F}}S\bigl(\hat{\rho}\,\|\,\hat{\pi}\bigr)\,,

(5)

where
S\bigl(\hat{\rho}_{1}\,\|\,\hat{\rho}_{2}\bigr)=\Tr[\hat{\rho}_{1}(\ln\hat{\rho}_{1}-\ln\hat{\rho}_{2})]
is the relative entropy, i.e., the quantum generalization of the
Kullback-Leibler (KL)
-divergence.
Physically,
M_{\alpha}
measures the divergence of
\hat{\rho}
with respect to the closest free state in the Rényi sense.

II
The thermal Mpemba effect

The first rigorous mathematical description of the thermal Mpemba effect was given in 2017 by Lu and Raz, who studied discrete-state continuous-time Markovian processes.
In
Lu and Raz 2017
, the dynamics is generated by a classical Liouvillian
\hat{\mathcal{L}}^{\mathrm{cl}}
, whose eigenvalues
\lambda_{k}
are non-positive, and it is convenient to order them in decreasing order of their real part as
0=\lambda_{1}>\real(\lambda_{2})\geq\real(\lambda_{3})\geq\cdots
.
The classical Liouville equation (see
Section

II.2
) dictates that the contribution to the dynamics from all eigenmodes
\mathbf{r}_{k}
of
\hat{\mathcal{L}}^{\mathrm{cl}}
decay exponentially in time with a rate given by the real part of the corresponding eigenvalue, except for
\mathbf{r}_{1}
corresponding to
\lambda_{1}=0
that describes the thermal steady state.
Thus, at long times, the equilibration speed of typical states will be proportional to
\exp(\Re(\lambda_2)t)
.
Instead, special initial states having zero overlap with the so-called slowest-decaying mode
\mathbf{v}_{2}
will equilibrate exponentially faster, with speed
\propto\exp(\Re(\lambda_3)t)
.
Moreover, if a faster equilibrating state happens to be initially further away from the steady state than a typical state, then the distance of the two states measured with some function
M
will cross in time and this is called a strong Mpemba effect.
If the fast equilibrating states have small but non-vanishing overlap with the slowest-decaying mode, the speedup will not be exponential, and the crossing indicates a weaker Mpemba effect.
To set the stage for the following examples, we first review athermality, the
RT
relevant to the thermal Mpemba effect.

II.1
Athermality as a Resource

Athermality, a state’s deviation from thermal equilibrium

Example 1: Thermalization of a spin chain

Example 2: The Davies Map

The generalization of
Eq.

10

\frac{\mathrm{d}\hat{\rho}_{s}(t)}{\mathrm{d}t}=\mathcal{L}[\hat{\rho}_{s}(t)]\,.

(18)

Here the superoperator
\mathcal{L}=\mathcal{H}_{s}+\mathcal{D}
evolving the state
\hat{\rho}_{s}
consists of a unitary part
\mathcal{H}_{s}\left[\hat{\rho}_{s}(t)\right]=-i[\hat{H}_{s},\hat{\rho}_{s}(t)]

\hat{H}_{s}
denotes the system Hamiltonian, and a dissipative part

\mathcal{D}\left[\hat{\rho}_{s}(t)\right]=\sum_{l}\hat{L}_{l}\hat{\rho}_{s}(t)\hat{L}_{l}^{\dagger}-\frac{1}{2}\left\{\hat{L}_{l}^{\dagger}\hat{L}_{l},\hat{\rho}_{s}(t)\right\}\,,

(19)

with the jump operators
\hat{L}_{l}
describing the effect of an environment on the system.
Here we focus on a Lindbladian describing the weak interaction of a system with a Markovian heat bath, known as the Davies map
Davies 1979
, which is an important example of an elementary Markovian quantum thermal operation
Lostaglio et al. 2018
.
The Davies generator is characterized by two mathematical properties: it satisfies the quantum detailed balance condition

and the superoperators corresponding to the unitary and the dissipative part of its generator commute.
These properties imply that the unique fixed point of the Davies map is the thermal state
\hat{\pi}_{\beta}=e^{-\beta\hat{H}_{s}}/\Tr[e^{-\beta\hat{H}_{s}}]
.
Moreover, in the energy eigenbasis, the Davies generator can be written in a block-diagonal form, where one block describes the thermalization of the populations and the other the thermalization of the coherences, which evolve independently from one another (as we will analyze thoroughly in
Section

III.3

III
The symmetry Mpemba effect

Besides the thermal Mpemba effect, a related phenomenon has recently drawn attention: the anomalous dynamical restoration of symmetries, first investigated by Ares, Murciano, and Calabrese
Ares et al. 2023
.
They considered unitary dynamics generated by a Hamiltonian with a continuous symmetry, where the system is initialized in a state that breaks it.
For a subsystem, the symmetry is (partially) restored during the dynamics, with the remainder of the system acting as a non-Markovian environment.
In this context, the Mpemba effect
is said to occur when stronger initial symmetry breaking leads to faster symmetry restoration.
Instances of this effect have been observed in both integrable
Rylands et al. 2024
;
Murciano et al. 2024
;
Di Giulio et al. 2025
;
Yamashika et al. 2024
and non-integrable
Turkeshi et al. 2025
;
Liu et al. 2024
;
Ares et al. 2025b
systems, and have been confirmed experimentally
Joshi et al. 2024
. The degree of asymmetry is captured by the entanglement asymmetry
Ares et al. 2023
.

To explain the occurrence of the Mpemba effect in this setting, we first review symmetries in (open) quantum systems in
Section

III.1
. We then discuss the resource of asymmetry in
Section

III.2
, and introduce modes of asymmetry, a useful tool for decomposing the dynamics in
Section

III.3
.
We show the appearance of the effect first using a classical Markov chain in
Section

III.4
and its quantum version in
Section

III.5
, then a Davies map in
Section

II.3
, and then in scenarios more closely aligned with existing literature: quantum circuits, both interacting with Markovian and with non-Markovian environments with
\mathrm{U}(1)
symmetry, as well as systems with non-Abelian (
\mathrm{SU}(2)
) symmetries in
Section

III.7
.

III.1
Symmetries in Quantum Systems

In closed systems, Noether’s theorem
3
3

3

When considering isolated quantum systems

\hat{T}=\hat{U}_{g}^{\dagger}\,\hat{T}\,\hat{U}_{g}:\forall\,g\in\mathrm{G}

In many cases of interest in physics, the group

\hat{U}_{g}=e^{i\,s_{g}\,\hat{Q}}\quad:s_{g}\in\mathbb{R}\,,

\hat{T}\hat{Q}\hat{T}^{\dagger}=\hat{Q}

\mathrm{G}
-invariant

\hat{U}_{g}=e^{\,i\sum_{\lambda=1}^{\Lambda}s_{g}^{\lambda}\,\hat{Q}_{\lambda}},\quad\mathbf{s}_{g}^{\lambda}\in\mathbb{R}\,,

(26)

where

\{\hat{Q}_{\lambda}\},\,\lambda=1,\dots\Lambda
form a Lie algebra and are, in particular, closed under commutator

[\hat{Q}_{a},\hat{Q}_{b}]=i\,\sum_{\lambda=1}^{\Lambda}\varepsilon_{ab}^{\lambda}\,\hat{Q}_{\lambda}\,,

(27)

for real
\varepsilon_{ab}^{\lambda}
.
In spin systems, four paradigmatic continuous symmetries are commonly encountered.

(i) Magnetization conservation (phase symmetry,

\hat{U}_{g}=e^{i\,\gamma_{g}\,\hat{S}_{z}},\quad\gamma_{g}\in[0,4\pi)\,,

(28)

\tfrac{1}{2}

\tfrac{1}{2}

(ii) Total angular momentum conservation, associated
with the rotation group
\mathrm{SO}(3)
or its double cover, the group
\mathrm{SU}(2)

\hat{U}_{g}=e^{i\,\theta_{g}\,\boldsymbol{\hat{n}}_{g}\cdot\hat{\mathbf{S}}},\quad\theta_{g}\in[0,4\pi),\,\boldsymbol{\hat{n}}_{g}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}\,,

(29)

where
\boldsymbol{\hat{n}}_{g}\cdot\boldsymbol{\hat{n}}_{g}=1

\hat{\mathbf{S}}=(\hat{S}_{x},\hat{S}_{y},\hat{S}_{z})
,
\hat{S}_{\alpha}=\sum_{n}\hat{s}^{\alpha}_{n},\,\alpha=x,y,z
.

(iii) Energy conservation (time-translation symmetry):

\hat{U}_{g}=e^{i\,t_{g}\,\hat{H}_{s}},\quad t_{g}\in\mathbb{R}\,.

(30)

Here,
\hat{H}_{s}
is the system Hamiltonian, and

In the special case where the ratios of energy levels are all rational numbers, that is, when the system’s time evolution is periodic, this group of unitaries is isomorphic to
\mathrm{U}(1)

\mathbb{R}

4
4

4

.
.

(iv) Another important continuous symmetry, which we

\hat{U}_{g}=e^{i\,\mathbf{v}_{g}\,\cdot\hat{\mathbf{P}}},\quad\mathbf{v}_{g}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}\,,

(31)

where
\hat{\mathbf{P}}=(\hat{P}_{x},\,\hat{P}_{y},\,\hat{P}_{z})

Instead, we will consider its discrete version.
Consider a 1D equispaced lattice with

where

A symmetry group
\mathrm{G}
acts on density matrices

via the unitary representation

\mathcal{U}_{g}[\hat{\rho}]=\hat{U}_{g}\,\hat{\rho}\,\hat{U}_{g}^{\dagger},\qquad g\in\mathrm{G}.

-invariant component is extracted by the twirling map

\mathcal{G}[\hat{\rho}]=\int_{G}\!\mathrm{d}g\;\mathcal{U}_{g}[\hat{\rho}]=\int_{G}\!\mathrm{d}g\;\hat{U}_{g}\,\hat{\rho}\,\hat{U}_{g}^{\dagger},

where

denotes the normalized Haar measure on
\mathrm{G}

\mathrm{G}
is compact. This
CPTP
map projects any input state onto its symmetric component by averaging over all group actions.
For discrete groups this becomes

\mathcal{G}[\hat{\rho}]=\frac{1}{\absolutevalue{G}}\sum_{g\in\mathrm{G}}\mathcal{U}_{g}[\hat{\rho}]\,.

(35)

As previously mentioned, the Mpemba effect occurs when the system interacts with an environment.
A
\mathrm{G}
-invariant unitary time evolution
\hat{T}

{H}_{s}\otimes{H}_{e}

In fact, let

(36)

\mathrm{G}
-invariant state on
{H}_{e}

Then, it can be easily shown (see

\mathrm{G}
-covariance condition, also known as weak symmetry

;
Albert and Jiang 2014

\mathcal{E}\circ\mathcal{U}_{g}\left[\hat{\rho}\right]=\mathcal{U}_{g}\circ\mathcal{E}\left[\hat{\rho}\right],\quad\forall\,g\in\mathrm{G},\,\forall\,\hat{\rho}\,.

(37)

Conversely, it has been shown that any

36

\mathrm{G}
-invariant unitary
\hat{T}
, and a
\mathrm{G}
-invariant state
\hat{\pi}_{e}

Keyl and Werner 1999
;
Marvian 2012
5
5

5

This is sometimes known as covariant Stinespring dilation theorem
.
Therefore, while the global evolution is a
\mathrm{G}
-invariant unitary transformation, the dynamics of the reduced state of the system is governed by a
\mathrm{G}
-covariant CPTP map. As a result, the asymmetry (i.e., symmetry breaking) with respect to the group
\mathrm{G}

This can be quantified by means of the asymmetry monotones

-covariant

Hence, while for open systems the presence of symmetry does not imply conservation laws, that is, Noether’s theorem is not applicable, asymmetry monotones still capture non-trivial implications of symmetries

.
In generic open system dynamics that respect the symmetry, the asymmetry (symmetry-breaking) in the initial state decreases, although the complete restoration of the symmetry is not apriori guaranteed
6
6

6

A broad class of maps that are guaranteed to restore a symmetry consists of quantum Markov processes with a fixed point, as shown in Ref.
Frigerio 1978

However, non-Markovian examples also exist, such as spin star systems

.
In the special case of closed system dynamics, the presence of symmetry implies the conservation of all asymmetry monotones.
That is, for any
\mathrm{G}
-invariant unitary

: in this regime, no asymmetry can be created or destroyed.
To observe Mpemba effects, thus one clearly needs to consider
\mathrm{G}
-covariant maps, i.e. systems interacting with environments.

When the symmetry group
\mathrm{G}
is the one-parameter group of time translations generated by the system Hamiltonian
\hat{H}_{s}
, in the set of
\mathrm{G}
‑covariant (also called phase‑covariant) maps. An example of such maps is the

In the open systems literature, the covariance condition is often referred to as weak symmetry, to distinguish it from a stronger notion, known as strong symmetry

.

Under the restriction to

III.2
Asymmetry as a Resource

For any symmetry group
\mathrm{G}
, the resource theory of asymmetry with respect to
\mathrm{G}
assumes that all systems under consideration are equipped with a representation of the group.
The set of free states and free operations in this resource theory are then defined as those that respect the symmetry
Marvian 2012
;
Gour and Spekkens 2008
;
Marvian and Spekkens 2014b
;
Marvian and Spekkens 2013
.

In particular, for any system
s
with Hilbert space
{H}_{s}
, the free states are the
\mathrm{G}
-invariant states,

{F}=\left\{\hat{\rho}\in{B}({H}_{s})\mid\mathcal{G}\left[\hat{\rho}\right]=\hat{\rho}\right\}\,,

(40)

where
\mathcal{G}
denotes the corresponding twirling map.

Free operations on system
s
are
CPTP
maps
\mathcal{E}
satisfying the covariance condition

\mathcal{E}\circ\mathcal{U}_{g}=\mathcal{U}_{g}\circ\mathcal{E}\quad:\forall\ g\in\mathrm{G}\,.

(
37
)

Note that, as required in all resource theories, free operations (i.e.,
\mathrm{G}
-covariant maps) map free states (i.e.,
\mathrm{G}
-invariant states) to free states.

A useful monotone in this resource theory is the

;
Vaccaro et al. 2008

\begin{split}M(\hat{\rho})&=\min_{\hat{\pi}\in{F}}S\bigl(\hat{\rho}\,\|\,\hat{\pi}\bigr)\\
&=S\bigl(\hat{\rho}\,\|\,\mathcal{G}\left[\hat{\rho}\right]\bigr)\\
&=S\bigl(\mathcal{G}\left[\hat{\rho}\right]\bigr)-S(\hat{\rho})\,,\end{split}

(41)

which corresponds to

M(\hat{\rho})=\min_{\hat{\pi}\in{F}}S\bigl(\hat{\rho}\,\|\,\hat{\pi}\bigr)\,.

(
3
)

This quantifies the minimal divergence of
\hat{\rho}
with respect to the manifold of symmetric states. Intuitively, if
\hat{\rho}
is nearly invariant, twirling induces little entropy change; if
\hat{\rho}
breaks the symmetry strongly, mixing over
\mathrm{G}
spreads its support and increases entropy substantially

.
The relative entropy of asymmetry can be extended to the
\alpha

3

\alpha

\alpha\in(0,1)\cup(1,2]
.

-covariant maps

III.3
Modes of asymmetry

Within the resource theory of asymmetry, an important tool to characterize the symmetry breaking was introduced in Ref.
Marvian and Spekkens 2014a

In this section, we outline the main ingredients to this concept, a simple example, and how this idea can be used to fully understand the symmetry Mpemba effect.

Consider a finite or compact Lie group
\mathrm{G}
acting unitarily on
{H}_{s}
via

\hat{U}_{g}:G\to{B}({H}_{s})

The full operator algebra
{B}({H}_{s})

\mathrm{G}

\mathrm{G}

\mu

{H}_{s}

One can use the irreducible tensor operator basis

\hat{X}=\sum_{\mu,q}\hat{X}^{(\mu,q)}.

Here,

\hat{X}^{(\mu,q)}=\sum_{\alpha}\Tr\bigl[\hat{T}^{(\mu,q)}_{\alpha}{}^{\dagger}\,\hat{X}\bigr]\;\hat{T}^{(\mu,q)}_{\alpha}\,.

(43)

A basis independent way to define these modes is by the group’s action

\hat{X}^{(\mu,q)}={d}_{\mu}\int_{G}\mathrm{d}g\,D_{qq}^{(\mu)*}(g)\,\mathcal{U}_{g}[\hat{X}]\,,

(44)

where

Assuming

By orthonormality of the

For discrete groups

(45)

-covariant maps and the trace norm respects the data processing inequality, the trace norm of each asymmetry mode is individually a monotone, that is

\|\mathcal{E}[{\hat{X}^{(\mu,q)}}]\|_{1}\leq\|{\hat{X}^{(\mu,q)}}\|_{1}\,.

(47)

43

(49)

where one can choose the tensor operators to be simply the outer product of two computational basis elements, or, equivalently, two eigenvectors of
\hat{S}_{z}

(50)

where

where at

Fixing the number of qubits to

\mu=0,\pm 1,\pm 2,\pm 3

To illustrate the mode decomposition, we employ the family of tilted pure states

(52)

As we discuss in the next section, this formalism helps unify the two phenomena: the thermal Mpemba effect hinges on the small overlap with the overall slowest-decaying eigenmode, whereas the symmetry Mpemba effect stems exclusively from the decay rate of the slowest eigenmode living in a (

III.4
Example 1: A classical symmetry Mpemba Effect with
\mathbb{Z}_{4}

To illustrate how an initially more asymmetric distribution can restore symmetry faster than a more symmetric one
, we consider the minimal classical setup in which distinct symmetry-breaking eigenmodes (namely eigenmodes living in
\mu\!\neq\!0
-sectors) decay at different rates: a four-site Markov chain on a ring (represented in

\mathbb{Z}_{4}

A unified description of the thermal and the symmetry Mpemba effects

An initially asymmetric state

The relative entropy of athermality of
\hat{\rho}(t)
(
\hat{\rho}_{t}

\hat{\rho}(t)
with respect to the instantaneously dephased state
\mathcal{G}\left[\hat{\rho}(t)\right]
(orange, namely the relative entropy of asymmetry) and the relative entropy of
\mathcal{G}\left[\hat{\rho}(t)\right]

\hat{\pi}

We have reinterpreted instances of both the thermal (

​) and symmetry (
) Mpemba effects in systems interacting with featureless baths as well as in non-Markovian environments in a resource theoretical approach in both classical and quantum settings.
Strikingly, the mechanisms behind the thermal and symmetry Mpemba effects are remarkably similar: the former is governed by the slowest-decaying mode, while the latter relies on the slowest symmetry-restoring mode.
Importantly, in both cases, the relevant monotone is based on the quantum relative entropy; what differs is the reference state with respect to which it is computed.
For the thermal Mpemba effect

​​, it characterizes the divergence with respect to the steady state, while for the symmetry Mpemba effect
, it quantifies the divergence with respect to an instantaneously dephased state.
It is well-known that for the Davies map, the relative entropy between a non-equilibrium state and the thermal steady state can be split into a classical and a purely quantum coherent part
Santos et al. 2019
.
In this context, the system’s Hamiltonian is the symmetry generator and, correspondingly, the twirling operation consists of dephasing the non-equilibrium state in the energy eigenbasis. Crucially, here we show that this approach can be generalized to an arbitrary symmetry as follows:
Let
\mathrm{G}
be a group with unitary representation
\{\hat{U}_{g}\}_{g\in\mathrm{G}}
and
\mathcal{G}
be the corresponding twirling map as in
Eq.

34
.
For two states
\hat{\rho},\hat{\pi}
with
\mathcal{G}\left[\hat{\pi}\right]=\hat{\pi}
, the quantum relative entropy reads

\begin{split}S(\hat{\rho}\|\hat{\pi})&=\Tr\bigl[\hat{\rho}\ln\hat{\rho}\bigr]-\Tr\bigl[\hat{\rho}\ln\hat{\pi}\bigr]\\
&\quad+\Tr\bigl[\hat{\rho}\ln\mathcal{G}\left[\hat{\rho}\right]\bigr]-\Tr\bigl[\hat{\rho}\ln\mathcal{G}\left[\hat{\rho}\right]\bigr]\\
&=S(\hat{\rho}\|\mathcal{G}\left[\hat{\rho}\right])-\Tr\bigl[\hat{\rho}\ln\hat{\pi}\bigr]+\Tr\bigl[\hat{\rho}\ln\mathcal{G}\left[\hat{\rho}\right]\bigr]\,.\end{split}

(80)

Now since
\hat{\pi}
is
G
-invariant, we have that
\Tr\bigl[\hat{\rho}\ln\hat{\pi}\bigr]=\Tr\bigl[\hat{\rho}\ln\mathcal{G}[\hat{\pi}]\bigr]
.

\mathcal{G}\circ\mathcal{G}=\mathcal{G}
and
\mathcal{G}^{\dagger}=\mathcal{G}
imply

\Tr\bigl[\hat{\rho}\ln\mathcal{G}[\hat{\pi}]\bigr]=\Tr\bigl[\hat{\rho}\,\mathcal{G}[\ln(\mathcal{G}[\hat\pi])]\bigr]=\Tr\bigl[\mathcal{G}[\hat{\rho}]\,\ln\hat{\pi}\bigr]\,.

(81)

Analogously, we have that
\Tr\bigl[\hat{\rho}\ln\mathcal{G}[\hat{\rho}]\bigr]=\Tr\bigl[\mathcal{G}[\hat{\rho}]\ln\mathcal{G}[\hat{\rho}]\bigr]
.
Hence, we find that

S(\hat{\rho}\|\hat{\pi})=S(\hat{\rho}\|\mathcal{G}[\hat{\rho}])+S(\mathcal{G}[\hat{\rho}]\|\hat{\pi})\,.

(82)

This equality holds for arbitrary
G
-invariant state
\hat{\pi}
.
In the special case where
\hat{\pi}
is the thermal state, this identity implies that the relative entropy of athermality can always be separated into the relative entropy of asymmetry (symmetry-breaking contribution) and the relative entropy of athermality for symmetry-respecting states (symmetry-preserving contribution), as illustrated in
Fig.

12

We note that, by

21

and that of
\hat{\pi}_{\beta}

Furthermore, by

Hence, in this special case,

\begin{split}F(\hat{\rho})-F(\hat{\pi}_{\beta})=&\beta^{-1}\big(S(\mathcal{G}[\hat{\rho}])-S(\hat{\rho})\big)\\
&+F(\mathcal{G}[\hat{\rho}])-F(\hat{\pi}_{\beta})\ ,\end{split}

(83)

which was previously presented in
Vaccaro et al. 2008

This decomposition reveals that for Davies maps, the quantum thermal Mpemba effect

\hat{H}_{s}

V
Conclusion

We have shown that the various manifestations of the Mpemba effect, arising from thte restoration of thermal equilibrium or symmetries, can all be cast within the unified framework of
RTs
.
By formalizing each variant as a distinct resource-theoretic phenomenon, we clarified the conceptual boundaries between thermal Mpemba and symmetry Mpemba effects.
Crucially, we demonstrated that it is not the specific physical embedding of the system (unitary versus featureless bath) that determines which Mpemba effect emerges, but rather the choice of measure used to quantify the system’s relaxation.
Indeed, by selecting different resource measures, one can observe multiple Mpemba behaviors in the very same quantum model.
Moreover, due to the absence of total ordering imposed by measures of resource in
RTs
, even when considering the same resource, the existence of the crossing characterizing the Mpemba effect depends on the considered monotone.

We highlighted how the quantum relative entropy has been used in studying the different Mpemba phenomena by taking as reference state the instantaneous projection of the state onto the free state set of the
RT
that corresponds to the Mpemba effect analyzed.
This reveals a fundamental connection between the thermal Mpemba and the symmetry Mpemba effects.
Crucially, the framework of the modes of asymmetry helps us extend the interpretation of thermal Mpemba effect to the symmetry one by shifting the attention of the initial overlap with the slowest decaying mode to that with the slowest symmetry-breaking one, which dictates the timescale of the symmetry restoration.
Following this approach, we identified the first instance of a symmetry Mpemba effect in a classical system.
Moreover, we analyzed quantum symmetry Mpemba effects for global
\mathrm{U}(1)
and
\mathrm{SU}(2)
symmetries, considering both pure and mixed initial states.

The study of Mpemba effects within the framework of
RTs
reveals how different resources can dissipate at different rates, as illustrated in
Fig.

13
.
By analyzing Mpemba dynamics, one can identify the mechanisms that cause certain resources to decay rapidly while others persist.

In this work, we have focused on quantum coherence, athermality, asymmetry, and non-stationarity, but this approach naturally extends to other resources such as entanglement, magic, and non-Gaussianity.
Beyond unifying thermal and symmetry variants, this framework offers a principled route to designing protocols that accelerate or delay resource dissipation.
Moreover, surveying diverse resource theories may uncover novel Mpemba-type phenomena in both classical and quantum domains.
In this sense, resource theories provide conceptual structure within the ubiquitous but non-universal phenomenon that is the Mpemba effect.

Appendix A
Non-Thermal Mpemba Effect and the Resource Theory of non-stationarity

In the following, we address Mpemba effects in the pure relaxation dynamics of open quantum systems. We consider states
\hat{\rho}(t)

(87)

\hat{\pi}

\mathcal{U}_{t}\circ\mathcal{E}=\mathcal{E}\circ\mathcal{U}_{t}

\mathcal{U}_{t}[\cdot]=\hat{\pi}^{it}\,[\cdot]\,\hat{\pi}^{-it}\ ,

(88)

Appendix C
Asymptotic decay of the quantum relative entropy

For both classical and quantum systems, the late time behaviour of a state is determined by its overlap with the slowest decaying eigenmode, among the whole spectrum for athermality and only among the nonzero-
\mu
-sectors for asymmetry. Here we prove the equations
Eq.

16
and
Eq.

23
.

C.1
Proof of the Asymptotic Decay of the KL-Divergence

Let
\hat{\mathcal{L}}^{\mathrm{cl}}
be a classical Liouvillian
with unique stationary distribution
\boldsymbol{\pi}
, which we assume has full support (
\pi_{i}>0
for all
i
).
Let us define the weighted inner product

\langle\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v}\rangle_{\boldsymbol{\pi}}=\sum_{i}\frac{u_{i}\,v_{i}}{\pi_{i}}\,,

(96)

and to order the eigenvalues of
\hat{\mathcal{L}}^{\mathrm{cl}}
as

0=\real(\lambda_{1})>\real(\lambda_{2})\geq\real(\lambda_{3})\geq\cdots\,.

(97)

We further assume the spectrum to not be degenerate for
\lambda_{j}
, with
j=2
in the case of athermality (see
Section

II
) and
j
corresponding to the slowest asymmetric eigenmode for asymmetry (see
Section

III
).
Denoting the corresponding eigenmodes by
\mathbf{r}_{k}
(orthonormal under
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{\boldsymbol{\pi}}
),

\hat{\mathcal{L}}^{\mathrm{cl}}\,\mathbf{r}_{k}=\lambda_{k}\,\mathbf{r}_{k}\,,\qquad\langle\mathbf{r}_{k},\mathbf{r}_{\ell}\rangle_{\boldsymbol{\pi}}=\delta_{k\ell}\,.

(98)

In this eigenbasis an initial probability distribution
\mathbf{p}
evolves as

\mathbf{p}(t)=\boldsymbol{\pi}+\sum_{k\geq 2}a_{k}\,\mathbf{r}_{k}\,e^{\lambda_{k}t},\quad a_{k}=\langle\mathbf{p},\mathbf{r}_{k}\rangle_{\boldsymbol{\pi}}\,,

(99)

so that
\delta\mathbf{p}(t)=\mathbf{p}(t)-\boldsymbol{\pi}=\sum_{k\geq 2}a_{k}\,\mathbf{r}_{k}e^{\lambda_{k}t}
.
For small deviations one finds

D(\mathbf{p}(t)\|\boldsymbol{\pi})=\sum_{k}p_{k}(t)\ln\frac{p_{k}(t)}{\pi_{k}}\sim\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k}\frac{(\delta p_{k}(t))^{2}}{\pi_{k}}\,.

(100)

As
t\to\infty
, as noted in Ref.
Polettini and Esposito 2013
only the slowest decaying mode (
j=2
) survives,
\delta p_{k}\sim a_{2}\,r_{2,k}\,e^{\lambda_{2}t}
, giving

D(\mathbf{p}(t)\|\boldsymbol{\pi})\sim\frac{1}{2}\,a_{2}^{2}\,e^{2\real(\lambda_{2})t}\sum_{k}\frac{r_{2,k}^{2}}{\pi_{k}}\,.

(
16
)

Replacing
\boldsymbol{\pi}
with
\hat{\mathcal{G}}\mathbf{p}(t)
allows to extend this to the asymptotic behaviour of the relative entropy of asymmetry.

C.2
Proof of the Asymptotic Decay of the Quantum Relative Entropy

In both the thermalization example of
Section

II.3
and the symmetry-breaking scenario of
Section

III
, our main monotone

M(\hat{\rho}(t))=S(\hat{\rho}(t)\|\hat{\pi}_{\beta})\quad\text{or}\quad S(\hat{\rho}(t)\|\mathcal{G}[\hat{\rho}(t)])

(101)

is the quantum relative entropy between the evolving state and its target (the relative entropy of athermality and of asymmetry, respectively).

At long times, its decay is governed by the slowest eigenmode of the Lindbladian
\mathcal{L}
having nonzero overlap with the initial state, following
Eq.

23
for the thermal Mpemba effect and
Eq.

58
(in the case of the
\mathbb{Z}_{4}
example studied in
Section

III.4
) for the asymmetry Mpemba effect.

Let now
\mathcal{L}
be diagonalizable.
Then there exist right eigenmodes
\{\hat{r}_{k}\}
and left eigenmodes
\{\hat{\ell}_{k}\}
satisfying

\mathcal{L}[\hat{r}_{k}]=\lambda_{k}\,\hat{r}_{k},\qquad\mathcal{L}^{\dagger}[\hat{\ell}_{k}]=\lambda_{k}^{*}\,\hat{\ell}_{k},

(102)

with the biorthonormality condition

\Tr\!\bigl[\hat{\ell}_{k}^{\dagger}\,\hat{r}_{\ell}\bigr]=\delta_{k\ell},

(103)

and corresponding spectrum ordered so that

0=\real(\lambda_{1})>\real(\lambda_{2})\geq\real(\lambda_{3})\geq\cdots.

(104)

As in
Section

C.1
, we also assume the slowest eigenmode associated to eigenvalue
\lambda_{j}
to be non-degenerate (for athermality
j=1
, for asymmetry the index
j
is chosen among eigenmodes in
\mu\!\neq\!0
-sectors as in the main text).

We now sketch the proof in three steps.

Spectral expansion.

Analogously to (
99
), any initial state decomposes as

\hat{\rho}(t)=\hat{\pi}+\sum_{k\geq 2}c_{k}\,\hat{r}_{k}\,e^{\lambda_{k}t},\qquad c_{k}=\Tr\!\bigl[\hat{\ell}_{k}^{\dagger}\,\hat{\rho}\bigr].

(105)

Quadratic approximation of
S
.

Writing
\hat{\rho}=\hat{\pi}+\delta\hat{\rho}
with
\|\delta\hat{\rho}\|\ll 1
, one shows via the integral representation of the logarithm that

S(\hat{\rho}\|\hat{\pi})=\Tr\!\bigl[\hat{\rho}(\ln\hat{\rho}-\ln\hat{\pi})\bigr]\approx\frac{1}{2}\,\Tr\!\bigl[\delta\hat{\rho}\,\hat{\pi}^{-1}\,\delta\hat{\rho}\bigr].

(106)

Dominance of the slowest eigenmode.

At late times
\delta\hat{\rho}(t)\approx c_{j}\,\hat{r}_{j}\,e^{\lambda_{j}t}
.

If
\lambda_{j}\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\mathbb{R}
, its conjugate partner
\lambda_{j}^{*}
must also be included, and one finds

\delta\hat{\rho}(t)\approx c_{j}\,\hat{r}_{j}\,e^{\lambda_{j}t}+c_{j}^{*}\,\hat{r}_{j}^{\dagger}\,e^{\lambda_{j}^{*}t}.

(107)

Substituting into (
106
) gives

\begin{split}&\Tr\!\bigl[\delta\hat{\rho}\,\hat{\pi}^{-1}\,\delta\hat{\rho}\bigr]\\
&\approx\begin{cases}\absolutevalue{c_j}^{2}\,e^{2\real(\lambda_{j})t}\,\Tr\!\bigl[\hat{r}_{j}^{\dagger}\,\hat{\pi}^{-1}\,\hat{r}_{j}\bigr]\,,&\lambda_{j}\in\mathbb{R}\,,\\
2\absolutevalue{c_j}^{2}\,e^{2\real(\lambda_{j})t}\,\real(\Tr\![\hat{r}_{j}^{\dagger}\,\hat{\pi}^{-1}\,\hat{r}_{j}])\,,&\lambda_{j}\notin\mathbb{R}\,.\end{cases}\end{split}

(108)

Hence,

S\bigl(\hat{\rho}(t)\|\hat{\pi}\bigr)\sim\begin{cases}\frac{1}{2}\,\absolutevalue{c_j}^{2}\,e^{2\real(\lambda_{j})t}\,\Tr\!\bigl[\hat{r}_{j}^{\dagger}\,\hat{\pi}^{-1}\,\hat{r}_{j}\bigr],&\lambda_{j}\in\mathbb{R}\,,\\
\absolutevalue{c_j}^{2}\,e^{2\real(\lambda_{j})t}\,\real(\Tr\![\hat{r}_{j}^{\dagger}\,\hat{\pi}^{-1}\,\hat{r}_{j}]),&\lambda_{j}\notin\mathbb{R}\,,\end{cases}

(109)

as discussed in Ref.
Cao et al. 2019
.
Note that this can be straightforwardly extended to
S\bigl(\hat{\rho}(t)\,\|\,\mathcal{G}[\hat{\rho}(t)]\bigr)
by replacing
\hat{\pi}
with
\mathcal{G}[\hat{\rho}(t)]
in the proof.

Appendix D
Quantum Fisher Information and why the measure matters

Let us assume that the dynamics of a system described by the state
\hat{\rho}
are Markovian, and that the state evolves according to

\frac{d\hat{\rho}}{dt}=\mathcal{L}\left[\hat{\rho}\right]\,,

(110)

where
\mathcal{L}
is the generator of the dynamics and takes the Lindblad form.
Following
Styliaris and Zanardi 2020
, we define the symmetries of the Lindbladian as the set of superoperators that commute with
\mathcal{L}

\mathrm{Sym}(\mathcal{L}):=\left\{\mathcal{M}\in{B}({B}({H}))\;\middle|\;[\mathcal{M},\mathcal{L}]=0\right\}\,,

(111)

where
{B}({B}({H}))
denotes the set of bounded linear operators acting on the space of bounded linear operators on the finite-dimensional Hilbert space
{H}
.

Here, we focus on the special case where the symmetry superoperator is the Lindbladian itself, i.e.,
\mathcal{M}=\mathcal{L}
. In this case, any quantum Fisher information with respect to the time parameter serves as a monotone under the evolution generated by
\mathcal{L}
.
The family of quantum Fisher information (QFI) measures about the time parameter
t
can be written in the form

I_{f}(t)=\sum_{x,y}\frac{\absolutevalue{\braket{x(t)\abs{\,\mathcal{L} [\hat{\rho}_t]\,}y(t)}}^{2}}{p_{x}(t)\,f(p_{y}(t)/p_{x}(t))},

(112)

where the density matrix is expressed in its spectral decomposition
\hat{\rho}=\sum_{x}p_{x}\ket{x}\!\bra{x}
. The set
\{p_{x}\}
defines a discrete probability distribution over eigenstates. The function
f
characterizes the particular QFI measure within the family and is required to satisfy three properties:
1) operator monotonicity: for any positive semidefinite operators
A
and
B
such that
A\leq B
, it holds that
f(A)\leq f(B)
;
2) self-inversiveness:
f(x)=xf(1/x)
for all
x>0
;
3) normalization:
f(1)=1
.

Common examples of such QFI measures include the symmetric logarithmic derivative (SLD) QFI with
f_{\mathrm{SLD}}(x)=\frac{x+1}{2}
, which is minimal in this family, the Wigner-Yanase (WY) QFI with
f_{\mathrm{WY}}(x)=\frac{1}{4}(\sqrt{x}+1)^{2}
, and the harmonic mean (HM) QFI with
f_{\mathrm{HM}}(x)=\frac{2x}{x+1}
, which is the maximal element in this class.

For a more detailed discussion of these QFI families and their properties, see
Scandi et al. 2025
.

I_{f}
for different monotones during the thermal relaxation of a single qubit. The initial states are unitarily connected, with one being incoherent (dashed line) and the other coherent (full lines) in the energy eigenbasis. Due to the structure of the Davies generator, the incoherent state’s dynamics and corresponding
I_{f}
are independent of the choice of
f
, while the coherent state’s behavior depends strongly on the monotone. The SLD and WY monotones both exhibit an Mpemba crossing at distinct times, whereas the HM monotone does not display a crossing. Parameters:
(r_{x},r_{y},r_{z})=(0.75,0,0.19)
,
J=1
,
\beta=0.1J^{-1}
,
\gamma=1J
,
\omega=5J
.

As discussed in
Styliaris and Zanardi 2020
, this perspective can be naturally integrated into the framework of quantum resource theories, particularly those in which the free operations are generated by the dynamical map
\mathcal{E}_{t}:=\exp(t\mathcal{L})
. In such a setting, the resource is identified with the degree of non-stationarity, as previously defined.

It is crucial to emphasize, however, that monotones associated with a given symmetry
\mathcal{M}
do not, in general, induce a total order on quantum states. Specifically, if a monotone
M_{a}
satisfies
M_{a}(\hat{\rho}_{1})>M_{a}(\hat{\rho}_{2})
for two states
\hat{\rho}_{1}
and
\hat{\rho}_{2}
, this does not necessarily imply that another monotone
M_{b}
associated with the same symmetry will also yield
M_{b}(\hat{\rho}_{1})>M_{b}(\hat{\rho}_{2})
. In fact, we will present an explicit counterexample illustrating this point. Moreover, we explore the consequences of this monotone dependence in the context of crossing times, particularly as it pertains to (quantum) Mpemba behavior.

To this end, we revisit the thermal quantum Mpemba effect that emerges in the pure relaxation dynamics of a single qubit coupled to a thermal bath, as discussed in
Section

II.3
of the main text.
We consider the relaxation behavior of two distinct, but unitarily connected, initial states: one of which is coherent in the energy eigenbasis, while the other (
\hat{\rho}_{2}
) is not. Owing to the structure of the Davies generator, the evolution of populations and coherences in the energy basis decouples. Furthermore, the quantity
I_{f}
naturally splits into two contributions: one incoherent and one coherent, defined with respect to the instantaneous eigenbasis of the state.

Given these two facts, it is straightforward to see that for the incoherent state, only the incoherent component contributes to
I_{f}
. Importantly, this contribution is independent of the choice of the function
f
, as illustrated by the dashed grey line in
Fig.

S3
.
In contrast, the initially coherent state yields an
f
-dependent contribution, as shown by the colored lines in
Fig.

S3
. Notably, the monotones
I_{\mathrm{SLD}}
and
I_{\mathrm{WY}}
exhibit a Mpemba crossing, though at distinct crossing times, while
I_{\mathrm{HM}}
does not show any crossing. This demonstrates that both the presence of an Mpemba crossing and the associated crossing time are strongly dependent on the specific choice of monotone, even when the underlying resource is held fixed.

Appendix E
Details on the Davies map

The Davies map is a particular Lindblad equation describing the thermalization of quantum systems weakly coupled to a Markovian heat bath
Davies 1979
.
In the main text, we have focused on its general mathematical properties, while here we will give the explicit expression of the equation that we used to compute various examples.
Consider the spectral decomposition of the system’s Hamiltonian
\hat{H}_{s}=\sum_{k}h_{k}\outerproduct{k}{k}
.
In the eigenbasis of
\hat{H}_{s}
, the Davies map reads

\begin{split}\frac{d\hat{\rho}_{s}(t)}{dt}=\mathcal{L}\left[\hat{\rho}_{s}(t)\right]=&-i[\hat{H}_{s},\hat{\rho}_{s}(t)]\\
&+\sum_{k<k^{\prime}}\mathcal{D}^{(1)}_{kk^{\prime}}[{\hat{\rho}_{s}(t)}]+\mathcal{D}^{(2)}_{kk^{\prime}}[{\hat{\rho}_{s}(t)}]\\
\end{split}

(113)

where the dissipators
\mathcal{D}^{(1)}_{kk^{\prime}}
and
\mathcal{D}^{(2)}_{kk^{\prime}}
are generated by the jump operators
\hat{L}^{(1)}_{kk^{\prime}}
and
\hat{L}^{(2)}_{kk^{\prime}}
, respectively, which couple all Hamiltonian eigenstates as

\displaystyle\hat{L}^{(1)}_{kk^{\prime}}(\beta)=\left(1\mp f^{\pm}(\beta,h_{k^{\prime}}\!-\!h_{k})\right)^{1/2}\outerproduct{k}{k'}

(114)

\displaystyle\hat{L}^{(2)}_{kk^{\prime}}(\beta)=\left(f^{\pm}(\beta,h_{k^{\prime}}\!-\!h_{k})\right)^{1/2}\outerproduct{k'}{k'}\,.

Here
f^{\pm}(\beta,h_{k^{\prime}}\!-\!h_{k})=1/(\exp(\beta(h_{k'}\!-\!h_k))\pm 1)
are the Fermi (
+
) and the Bose (
-
) distribution functions which enforce the detailed balance condition, ensuring that the unique steady state is the thermal state at inverse temperature
\beta
.
Throughout all examples in the main text, we considered bosonic canonical commutation relations.

Appendix F
From global G-invariance to local G-covariance

In this section we demonstrate how a global symmetry of a joint system-environment unitary evolution induces covariance of the reduced system dynamics under the same symmetry group, if the environment is prepared in a symmetric state.
Let us consider a
\mathrm{G}
group,
\hat{T}\in{B}({H}_{s}\otimes{H}_{e})
a
\mathrm{G}
-invariant unitary, and
\hat{\pi}_{e}\in{S}({H}_{e})
a
\mathrm{G}
-invariant state.
Then, the map

\mathcal{E}[\cdot]=\Tr_{e}[\hat{T}([\cdot]\otimes\hat{\pi}_{e})\hat{T}^{\dagger}]

(115)

will be
\mathrm{G}
-covariant, namely

\mathcal{E}[\hat{U}_{g,s}\,\hat{\rho}\,\hat{U}_{g,s}^{\dagger}]=\hat{U}_{g,s}\,\mathcal{E}[\hat{\rho}]\,\hat{U}_{g,s}^{\dagger}\quad\forall\,\hat{\rho}\in{S}({H}_{s})\,,

(116)

where
\hat{U}_{g,s}
is the unitary representation of
\mathrm{G}
on
{H}_{s}
.

To prove this let us consider a unitary representation of the group
\hat{U}_{g}=\hat{U}_{g,s}\otimes\hat{U}_{g,e}
on
{H}_{s}\otimes{H}_{e}
and begin from the left-hand side

\mathcal{E}[\hat{U}_{g,s}\,\hat{\rho}\,\hat{U}_{g,s}^{\dagger}]=\Tr_{e}[\hat{T}(\hat{U}_{g,s}\,\hat{\rho}\,\hat{U}_{g,s}^{\dagger}\otimes\hat{\pi})\hat{T}^{\dagger}]\,.

(117)

By
\mathrm{G}
-invariance we will have
[\hat{T},\hat{U}_{g,s}\otimes\hat{U}_{g,e}]=0\,\forall\,g\in\mathrm{G}
, so

\hat{T}(\hat{U}_{g,s}\,\hat{\rho}\,\hat{U}_{g,s}^{\dagger}\otimes\hat{\pi})T^{\dagger}=\hat{U}_{g,s}\otimes\hat{U}_{g,e}\,(\hat{\sigma})\,\hat{U}_{g,s}^{\dagger}\otimes\hat{U}_{g,e}^{\dagger}\,,

(118)

where
\hat{\sigma}=\hat{T}(\hat{\rho}\otimes\hat{\pi}_{e})\hat{T}^{\dagger}
, where we used
\hat{\pi}_{e}=\hat{U}_{g,e}\,\hat{\pi}_{e}\,\hat{U}_{g,e}^{\dagger}
.
Hence

\mathcal{E}[\hat{U}_{g,s}\,\hat{\rho}\,\hat{U}_{g,s}^{\dagger}]=\Tr_{e}[\hat{U}_{g,s}\otimes\hat{U}_{g,e}\,(\hat{\sigma})\,\hat{U}_{g,s}^{\dagger}\otimes\hat{U}_{g,e}^{\dagger}]\,.

(119)

Since the partial trace over the environment commutes with unitaries on the environment, one gets

\begin{split}\Tr_{e}[\hat{U}_{g,s}\otimes\hat{U}_{g,e}\,(\hat{\sigma})\,\hat{U}_{g,s}^{\dagger}\otimes\hat{U}_{g,e}^{\dagger}]&=\hat{U}_{g,s}\Tr_{e}[\hat{\sigma}]\hat{U}_{g,s}^{\dagger}\\
&=\hat{U}_{g,s}\mathcal{E}[\hat{\rho}]\hat{U}_{g,s}^{\dagger}\,.\end{split}

(120)

Thus

\mathcal{E}[\hat{U}_{g,s}\,\hat{\rho}\,\hat{U}_{g,s}^{\dagger}]=\hat{U}_{g,s}\mathcal{E}[\hat{\rho}]U_{g,s}^{\dagger}\,,

(121)

showing that
\mathcal{E}
is
\mathrm{G}
-covariant whenever
\hat{U}
is
\mathrm{G}
-invariant and
\hat{\pi}
is
\mathrm{G}
-invariant.
