Klebsiella pneumoniae, a human-dog shuttle organism for the genes of CTX-M ESBL
Authors: Eun-Jeong Yoon, You Jeong Choi, Dongju Won, Jong Rak Choi, Seok Hoon Jeong
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Antimicrobials reserved for human medicines are permitted for companion animals and it is important to understand multidrug-resistant pathogens recovered from companion animals in terms of epidemiological correlation with human pathogens and possibility of transmission to human-beings. Seventeen of each CTX-M-type extended-spectrum beta-lactamase-producing Escherichia coli (ESBL-EC) and Klebsiella pneumoniae (ESBL-KP) canine isolates were assessed. Entire genomes of the 34 isolates were sequenced. Plasmid transfer and relative growth rates were assessed at differed temperature conditions indicating the body temperature of dogs, that of human-beings, and environment. ESBL-ECs were clonally diverse, while ESBL-KPs were not. The ESBL-ECs carried the blaCTX−M−15 gene in plasmids and the blaCTX−M−14-like gene either in chromosomes or in plasmids. The ESBL-KPs possessed the blaCTX−M−15 gene in plasmids (n = 15). One of the isolates carried another blaCTX−M−15 gene in a chromosome simultaneously and the other isolate had an additional blaCTX−M−9 gene-harbouring plasmid, together. Two ESBL-KP isolates carried the blaCTX−M−14 gene in plasmids. Plasmid transfer ESBL-EC to K. pneumoniae was efficient and the differed biological costs by temperature was much more in ESBL-EC than in ESBL-KP. Intersectoral dissemination of ESBL-ECs occurred mainly by horizontal gene transfer, while that of ESBL-KPs occurred by clonal dissemination.

Introduction

Antimicrobial resistance (AMR) is an emerging threat to public health and it needs to be taken into account in a One Health aspect1. Surveillance programmes for the One Health AMR are mostly concentrated on components composing a food chain, because the food chain is known to be a major circuit of transmission of pathogens with AMR traits2. The protocol for the Global Tricycle Surveillance proposed by World Health Organization (WHO) is also focused on the food chain including pregnant women in the community for a human sector, live chickens in open markets for an animal sector and the river close to the slaughterhouse for an environment sector3. In the protocol, CTX-M-type extended-spectrum beta-lactamase (ESBL)-producing Escherichia coli (ESBL-EC) was taken as sentinel antimicrobial resistant pathogen following the suggestion of the WHO Advisory Group on Integrated Surveillance on AMR. The rationale was (i) varied rates of colonization of ESBL-EC by human in a country and between countries;4 (ii) varied prevalence among farm animals and some evidences that part of the human morbidity associated with ESBL-ECs is due to both antimicrobial usage in the food chain5 and environmental presence of ESBL-ECs;6 (iii) interventions to decrease antimicrobial exposure in animals7 or humans8 resulted in decreasing rates of ESBL-EC occurrence; and (iv) ESBL productions by the infection-causative pathogens limits treatment options9 resulting in usage of the last resort drugs10.

However, as the protocol indicated, the sentinel bacterial species with the indicated AMR trait is not representative and insufficient to understand all, and, for some animals, specific bacterial species needs to be chosen for the surveillance study. As well, the mechanism of dissemination could be differed by bacterial species. For instance, clonal dissemination between patients was observed to be a major cause of ESBL-KP spread while that of ESBL-EC spread occurred by horizontal gene transfer11.

Though the companion animals are not included in a food chain, they are an unnegligible component in the animal sector since they share living environments both outdoors and indoors with human-beings12,13. Antimicrobial-resistant bacteria may take an advantage of dissemination through direct and indirect interactions between human-beings and companion animals. Actually, multidrug-resistant bacteria from companion animals were often reported globally14,15, highlighting the companion animals’ position in the current One Health AMR framework. Furthermore, veterinarians often use antimicrobial drugs reserved for human-beings to treat companion animals with infectious diseases16 and it may lead selection pressure promoting inter- and intra-sectoral dissemination either by clonal dissemination or by horizontal gene transfer of AMR genes to bacterial pathogens17. Particularly AMR in common enteric bacteria, which may cause infectious diseases in the host, i.e., ESBL-EC and ESBL-producing K. pneumoniae (ESBL-KP) are considered the most critical group of pathogens posing a considerable threat to human health18.

This study evaluated the multidrug-resistant pathogens, ESBL-EC and ESBL-KP recovered from companion dogs for its clonal relatedness to clinical isolates of human origin and their viability at the conditions of the varied sectors. Distinctive strategies of dissemination taken by the two relevant Enterobacterales species, emphasize the importance of collecting extensive scientific evidences for better designing the One Health AMR surveillance programme.

Results

CTX-M-type ESBL-producing Enterobacterales isolated from dogs

A total of 34 Enterobacterales, including 17 E. coli and 17 K. pneumoniae isolates carrying either the blaCTX−M−15 or the blaCTX−M−14-like gene, were assessed. All the isolates were recovered from rectal swabs of companion dogs in three veterinary hospitals, except the K. pneumoniae Z0117KP0043 isolate recovered from a nasal swab. The E. coli isolates belonged to various STs: four to ST38, two to ST131, and each one to ST485, ST372, ST1146, ST95, ST648, ST155, ST88-like, ST12, ST961, ST295, and ST602 (Fig. 1A). None of the STs were grouped into a clonal complex by their similarity to a central allele type. In the case of K. pneumoniae isolates, more AMR genes were identified, with a median value of 22 genes per genome, than E. coli with a median value of eight genes per genome: the genome of Z0117KP0001 (ST17) had the fewest, with 17 AMR genes, whereas the genome of Z0117KP0017 (ST950) had the most, with 39 genes (Fig. 2A). All the K. pneumoniae isolates carried the blaCTX−M gene-harbouring plasmid: 15 isolates possessed the blaCTX−M−15 gene-harbouring plasmids and three isolates had the blaCTX−M−14–like gene-harbouring plasmids. The Z0117KP0017 (ST950) isolate carried two blaCTX−M−15 gene-harbouring plasmids, the Z0117KP0019 (ST307) isolate had one blaCTX−M−15 gene-harbouring plasmid and two copies of the blaCTX−M−15 gene in chromosome, simultaneously, and the Z0117KP0020 (ST307) isolate had two plasmids together, one carrying the blaCTX−M−15 gene and the other carrying the blaCTX−M−9 gene.

The blaCTX−M gene-possessing plasmids in K. pneumoniae carried more AMR genes with the median value of 13 genes per genome than those in E. coli with the median value of 5.5 genes per genome (Fig. 2B).

Plasmids carrying theblaCTX−M gene

In E. coli, four plasmids possessed the blaCTX−M−15 gene, and each plasmid belonged to the IncH, IncI, IncFIB/FII, and IncFII families, respectively (Table 1; Fig. 5A). Among the six blaCTX−M−14 gene-harbouring plasmids, two were unidentifiable incompatibility type through the nucleic acid sequence of the replication origin, three belonged to the IncI1-I family, and the other belonged to either family of the IncHI2/HI2A. One blaCTX−M−9 gene-harbouring plasmids in the ESBL-EC belonged to the IncFIB/FIC. A recently developed artificial intelligence-based host range estimation system for plasmids, namely, PlasmidHostFinder, was used to assess the host ranges of each plasmid. Since the homologous scores against the non-Enterobacterales were all near zero, only the scores against Enterobacterales are presented in Fig. 6. To order the bacterial species belonging to Enterobacterales, a phylogenetic tree based on the rpoB sequences of each species was constructed, and a heatmap was drawn. As expected, the scores of each plasmid were the highest against the natural host bacterial species. Generally, E. coli plasmids presented relatively high scores to a wide range of host bacterial species, including Salmonella enterica and K. pneumoniae, and less frequently Enterobacter spp., Shigella flexneri, Pantoea spp., and Serratia marcescens. Three of those, both plasmids in Z0117EC0036 and a plasmid in Z0117EC0131, likely originated from Enterobacterales species other than E. coli with the score lower than 0.1. Most of the K. pneumoniae plasmids presented a relatively conserved host range on the natural host bacterial species K. pneumoniae, except for two cases, pZ0117KP0042-2 and pZ0117KP0043-2. Among those, the pZ0117KP0042-2 was more likely originated in other Enterobacterales than E. coli, although it was IncFII plasmid, which is more common in E. coli. TheblaCTX−M gene integrated into chromosomes

Among the ESBL-ECs, seven isolates possessed one to three copies of the blaCTX−M−14 gene in the chromosome (Table 1). The site of integration was varied. Two isolates having two or three copies of the blaCTX−M−14 gene in the chromosome possessed each transposable element of its own size (Fig. 3), and if a sequential copy-and-paste integration occurred following the first acquisition of the transposable element in the chromosome, the order of insertion in the chromosome could be estimated. The shortened portion was identified downstream from the blaCTX−M−14 gene, the locus of the latter insertion was downstream from the first site of insertion, and the direction was fairly random. The copy number of the blaCTX−M−14 gene in E. coli chromosome corresponded to the value of MICs of cephalosporins: cefotaxime MICs between 4 µg/ml and 32 µg/ml in isolates with a single copy of the gene, and 64 µg/ml in those having three copies of the gene; cefepime MICs of 2 µg/ml in isolates with a single copy and 8 µg/ml in those with three copies of the gene.

Among the 17 ESBL-KP isolates, one isolate carried a pair of blaCTX−M−15 genes in the chromosome, together with a plasmid carrying the blaCTX−M−15 gene (Table 1). The transposable elements were bracketed by direct repeats either of five GATCA or six ATTAAC nucleotides, and, in contrast to the E. coli cases, the size of the integrated portion was identical (Fig. 3).

Plasmid transfer efficiency and relative growth rates

To assess the ability to transfer the blaCTX−M gene-harbouring plasmid horizontally at different temperature conditions, plasmid transfer efficiency was evaluated at three different temperatures, 28 °C reflecting the condition of the environment, 36 °C fitting to the human body temperature, and 39 °C reflecting the canine body temperature (Table 2). The IncFII plasmid in E. coli was transferrable to any recipient, regardless of the bacterial species; however, notably, transfer efficiency to E. coli ST73, which has never been found to carry the gene, was always under the detection limit at 36 °C. The transfer efficiency of the plasmids carried by E. coli was often better at 39 °C than at 36 °C. Discussion

One Health concept needs to be considered to overview the emergence and dissemination of AMR for prevention and intervention strategies to control AMR, and surveillance is a key component to build an evidence-based strategy. The AMR surveillance study including multiple sectors is complex and methodologic aspects is critical for further interpretation of the surveillance data including selection of a convincing sentinel bacterial host with AMR traits. Thus, scientific evidences are needed for the One Health compositions. As human medicines are allowed to use for companion animals, ESBL-producing Enterobacterales leads more use of reserved antimicrobial drugs for human use, such as carbapenems and, in this study, possible mechanisms of transmission between sectors were assessed using CTX-M ESBL-producing canine Enterobacterales.

The canine ESBL-EC isolates were clonally diverse, and only two STs, ST38 and ST131, were identified in multiple isolates. All four ESBL-EC ST38 isolates possessed the blaCTX−M−14 gene in their chromosomes and, among those, one isolate possessed three copies of the gene. Similarly in UK, chromosomal integration of the blaOXA−28 gene in a carbapenem-resistant E. coli ST38 isolate has been reported in201522. In addition, dissemination of the ESBL-EC ST38 clone in a One Health interphase was reported in Europe, through identification of the E. coli ST38 isolates possessing the blaCTX−M gene identified both in human samples and in broiler production samples21. The ST131 isolates belonged to subtype H41 is rare to carry the blaCTX−M gene among human blood isolates19, but a dominant among human urine isolates to carry the blaCTX−M−14-like gene23. The blaCTX−M gene-harbouring plasmids in E. coli isolates were 15-kb- to 230-kb-sized and grouped into various incompatibility families and carried less than eleven AMR-associated genes. The wide range of the bacterial host species of the E. coli plasmids, together with the lively inter-species plasmid transfer, indicated lively occurring horizontal gene transfer in this bacterial species. On the other hand, K. pneumoniae canine isolates were grouped into three or four clones at most. Two biggest group clones, ST307 and ST392, are often recovered from blood specimens of human patients in our previous study24 and are well known as antimicrobial drug-resistant clones producing K. pneumoniae carbapenemases (KPC)24. Of note, none of the canine isolates used in the study possessed genes for carbapenemases. All the canine K. pneumoniae isolates possessed more than fourteen acquired genes for AMR in the genome. All possessed the blaCTX−M gene, one or more copies, on plasmids and the plasmids possessed more genes for AMR than those in E. coli. Among them, an isolate of ST307 carried two copies of an identical blaCTX−M−15 gene-harbouring transposable element at the late semicircle of the chromosome. Although the integration locus was different, K. pneumoniae ST307 carrying the blaCTX−M−15 gene on the chromosome has also been often identified among clinical isolates all over the world24–26. The plasmids carrying the blaCTX−M gene possessed by K. pneumoniae identified in the study were much larger than those in E. coli, over 156 kb in size, and mostly belonged to the IncF family. These plasmids were so-called multidrug resistance plasmids carrying multiple genes for AMR up to 18 and were identical to the dominant plasmids in K. pneumoniae blood isolates recovered from human patients in South Korea and in Taiwan, e.g., 100% na identity 99% na coverage of 157,059-bp pE16K0268-2 (GenBank accession CP052270),24 and 100% na identity 100% na coverage of 238-816-bp pSC-KP169-2 (GenBank accession CP119185) to pZ0117KP0004-1. The plasmids presented a relatively narrow range of bacterial hosts and the K. pneumoniae isolates carrying the plasmid were clonal, it was likely that spreading the ESBL-KP between human beings and companion dogs was mainly due to clonal dissemination using the bacterial host as a shuttle for the delivery of the AMR determinant.

Temperature is one of the factors, which could be differed by sectors composing the One Health interface and, in this study, ESBL-EC isolates presented great differences in growth rates among the three differed temperatures: elevated growth rates by 12–20% at the canine body temperature, and decreased growth rates by 24–34% at the environmental temperature, comparing to those at the human body temperature. The ESBL-KP isolates presented lower biological advantage at the canine body temperature than ESBL-EC isolates with elevated growth rates by 4–10%, while the decreased growth rates at the environmental temperature were alike to those of ESBL-EC. It is likely that the ESBL-EC at the canine body temperature has shorter generation time with accelerate metabolism than the ESBL-KP including construction of conjugation-associated structures. The plasmid transfer efficiency by presenting general agreement of higher rates of transfer at higher temperature, especially the ESBL-EC with FII plasmids carrying the blaCTX−M−14 gene.

Owing to the notorious clone ST131, the ESBL-EC is now considered as a principal spreader of the ESBLs in the One Health interface. However, taking the history of emerging ESBL into account, K. pneumoniae was the bacterial species amenable to the beginning of ESBL dissemination as the primary bacterial host species of the genes for SHV-2-type ESBL in favour of the selection pressure by 3rd generation cephalosporins27. In the field of AMR surveillance studies using One Health perspectives, E. coli is preferred. Considering the ability of K. pneumoniae to move among sectors and its cost-free acclimatization in varied conditions, the shuttle bacterial species should be seriously considered as a vehicle to disseminate AMR.

Simplification of the sector-specific conditions limited by temperature could present limited information. However, temperature is one of the important factors that make the sector-specific condition allowing a simple but solid experimental model to compare the pathogens originated each sector at a better surveillance study. Of course, the target bacteria could be multiple to have an insight for the dissemination of antimicrobial-resistant bacteria in One Health interface since each bacterial species can spread in a differed way as the E. coli and K. pneumoniae species presented in the study. However, since an AMR surveillance system of delicate design is often costly, such a preliminary study is necessary to find out a better bacterial model fit to the One Health composition.

Materials and methods

Bacterial strains

A total of 17 E. coli and 17 K. pneumoniae isolates, which were recovered from 33 rectal swabs and a nasal swab of companion dogs in three veterinary hospitals throughout South Korea between July and November 2017, were used in the study. The 34 isolates were all ESBL-producing Enterobacterales recovered from the three veterinarians for six months and first-collected 17 isolates of each bacterial species were used for the study.

Microbiological assessment and testing for an antimicrobial susceptibility

Bacterial species of the isolates were identified by using MALDI-TIF/MS using Bruker Biotyper™ system (Billerica, MA, USA). The MICs for cefotaxime (Sigma, Burlington, MA, USA), ceftazidime (Sigma), and cefepime (Sigma) were assessed by the broth microdilution methods using Mueller-Hinton (MH) broth (Difco Laboratories, Detroit, MI, USA), following Clinical Laboratory Standards Institute guidelines (CLSI)28. Escherichia coli ATCC 25,922 was used for quality control and the susceptibility was determined through the CLSI guidelines29. The genes for beta-lactamase genes were identified by PCR and direct sequencing using gene specific primers30.

Whole genome sequencing

From the 34 Enterobacterales isolates, genomic DNA was extracted with the GenElute bacterial genomic DNA kit (Sigma–Aldrich, St. Louis, MO). Following quality control of the extracted DNA, libraries were prepared using the SMRTbell® Express Template Prep Kit 2.0 (Pacific Biosciences of California, Inc., Menlo Park, CA) following the manufacturer’s instructions. The entire genomes were sequenced using PacBio Sequel Systems technology. Reads were assembled using the Microbial Assembly application of SMRT® Link v10.1. Annotation for the complete sequences was conducted using prokka 1.13.7 (https://github.com/tseemann/prokka).31

In silico molecular epidemiology of the isolates

For multilocus sequence typing (MLST), allele numbers of seven housekeeping genes, adk, fumC, gyrB, icd, mdh, purA, and recA, of E. coli were extracted, and each allele number was determined through the scheme of Wirth et al.32 to obtain the corresponding STs using the software provided by the Center for Genomic Epidemiology (ver. 2.0.8)33. For K. pneumoniae, partial sequences of the gapA, infB, mdh, pgi, phoE, rpoB, and tonB genes of each isolate were extracted, and the allele numbers were used to determine the corresponding ST through the scheme of Diancourt et al.34 For both bacterial species, core genome MLST (cgMLST) was conducted through BIGSdb-Kl (https://bigsdb.pasteur.fr) for K. pneumoniae35 and Enterobase (https://enterobase.warwick.ac.uk) for E. coli36, using a total of 2537 and 2513 loci, respectively. The relatedness of each isolate was inferred by constructing minimum spanning trees using PHYLOViZ37. Identification of AMR determinants was performed by using ResFinder (https://cge.cbs.dtu.dk//services/ResFinder/).38 The incompatibility type of the blaCTX−M gene-harbouring plasmid and the plasmid MLST (pMLST) were determined by plasmid finder (https://cge.cbs.dtu.dk//services/PlasmidFinder/) and pMLST (https://cge.cbs.dtu.dk//services/pMLST/), respectively39. Estimation of the range of bacterial hosts of each plasmid was conducted using PlasmidHostFinder (https://cge.cbs.dtu.dk/services/PlasmidHostFinder/).40 Subtyping of the blaCTX−M gene was conducted using the National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI) Reference Sequence Database, RefSeq (last updated in Oct. 2021).

Estimation of the plasmid transfer efficiency

For bacterial conjugation, spontaneous mutants resistant to both nalidixic acid and sodium azide were generated for recipient isolates from two K. pneumoniae and five E. coli isolates susceptible to all clinically available antibiotics. The K. pneumoniae isolates comprised one each of ST29 and ST384, which rarely carry the blaCTX−M gene20. The E. coli isolates comprised one each of ST131 H30R, H30Rx, H30, and non-H30 and of ST73. E. coli isolates of ST131 are often found to carry the blaCTX−M gene in human-origin clinical isolates, and those of ST73 rarely carry the blaCTX−M gene19. The donor strains were composed of four E. coli isolates carrying either the blaCTX−M−14 gene-harbouring plasmid belonging to the IncI1-I family or the blaCTX−M−14 gene-harbouring plasmid of the IncFII family and three K. pneumoniae isolates carrying either the IncFIB, IncHI2, or IncFIB/FII plasmid carrying the blaCTX−M−15 gene. Equal amounts of exponential cultures of the donor and recipient isolates were mixed, incubated in Mueller-Hinton broth (Becton Dickinson and Company (BD), Franklin Lakes, NJ) devoid of any drug for 12 h at 28 °C, 36 °C, and 39 °C, and spread on brain heart infusion agar (BD) containing nalidixic acid (30 µg/ml), sodium azide (100 µg/ml), and cefotaxime (10 µg/ml). Each colony was confirmed by PCR for the blaCTX−M gene, and the plasmid transfer frequency was calculated as the number of transconjugants per donor. The experiments were performed in duplicate and repeated at least three times.

Relative growth rate measurements

Growth rates were determined in 96-well microplates coupled to a Multiscan spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA). Isolates were grown overnight at temperatures of 28 °C, 36 °C, and 39 °C, and the cultures were diluted to inoculate approximately 5 × 103 bacteria into 200 µl of Luria-Bertani broth (BD) in a 96-well microplate that was incubated at 36 °C with shaking, and the optical density at 600 nm was measured every 3 min. Each culture was replicated three times in the same microplate. Growth rates were determined at the beginning of the exponential phase, and relative growth rates were calculated through three independent experiments as the ratio of the growth rate of the isolates versus that of the isolates used for comparison.
