Disparities in digital information exchange and interoperability among family medicine physicians in the United States
Authors: Kate E Trout, Jeongyoung Park
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveThis study examines disparities in the use of interoperability features within the EHR among family medicine physicians in the United States, particularly in areas with higher social deprivation index and rural settings.Materials and MethodsUsing data from the 2022 American Board of Family Medicine Continuous Certification Questionnaire, we assessed the association between self-reported interoperability and social deprivation.ResultsAdjusting for other confounders, there is a noticeable trend of declining odds to report utilizing key interoperability features associated with higher social deprivation, including receiving and sending information, and having patient information available that are external to their organization. Family medicine physicians are more likely to report sending electronic information when collaborating with local public health agencies (OR = 1.573). They also have higher odds of reporting the integration electronic information when partnering with behavioral health agencies (OR = 1.838) and schools (OR = 1.742). Exploratory analyses show that rural family medicine physicians may have significantly lower odds of reporting sending (OR = 0.0891), searching (OR = 0.362), and having external patient encounters available (OR = 0.0774) compared to urban physicians, though the small sample size (n = 6) limits estimate stability and generalizability.DiscussionProviders serving populations with higher social deprivation and practice in rural areas may face unique challenges regarding digital information exchange and interoperability. Programs and structures should facilitate community collaborations, such as partnerships with local public health agencies, schools, and behavioral health organizations, that were associated with improved odds of interoperability.ConclusionSeamless information exchange is crucial for reducing health disparities and improving care coordination, particularly within family medicine as a frontline point of patient care. Further research should examine how family physicians can leverage funding and partnerships to enhance interoperability and digital access, and assess their impact on reducing disparities.Keywords: electronic health records, health information interoperability, health equity, family medicine, rural health services

Background

Ensuring seamless interoperability in healthcare systems is essential for advancing health equity by enabling efficient and high-quality care through robust information exchange across platforms and institutions. Interoperability facilitates the exchange of critical health information, such as medical records, lab results, and treatment histories, which is vital for providing comprehensive and coordinated care to all individuals, regardless of their socioeconomic status or geographical location. Since the enactment of the Health Information Technology for Economic and Clinical Health Act (HITECH) in 2009, the nation has invested more than $30 billion in adopting Electronic Health Records (EHRs) with the aim of enhancing the quality of care and clinical decision-making through interoperability and digital information exchange across the continuum of care. Most existing research focuses on hospital-based settings, yet family medicine represents the front line of patient care (eg, entry point for preventive and continuous care) and a vital gateway for equitable digital health infrastructure. Framing family medicine in this context underscores the importance of examining interoperability and digital equity at the primary care level, where differences in access and technology adoption most directly influence patient outcomes and population health. However, the adoption of health information technologies (HITs) to facilitate the exchange of digital health information has proven to be a major challenge, particularly for small and rural healthcare organizations with fewer resources, which lag behind in digital modernization.1–7

Disparities in digital information exchange and interoperability are likely to deepen existing healthcare inequities, as evidence already points to significant gaps in access to these critical technologies across different populations.5,7–9 Despite the considerable expansion of HIT capacity among primary care physicians, practices integrated into delivery systems or those sharing resources with other practices exhibit higher rates of EHR adoption, multifunctional HIT, electronic information exchange, and electronic patient access while small private practices fall behind.5 One study in Georgia found disparities in EHR adoption among primary care physicians, between large practices and community health centers (CHCs), who were more likely to adopt EHRs (>80%), and health clinics in rural and other underserved settings, who were far less likely to do so (53%).

In addition to adoption barriers focused on organizational and structural differences, disparities also emerge based on the patient populations served. A significantly lower proportion (68.9%) of Medicaid predominant providers had adopted EHRs than private insurance predominant practices regardless of location.7 Specifically, primary care practices serving underserved communities, such as African American and Latino/a/x patients in rural and underserved communities, are less likely to be seen in practices that have adopted digital technology programs and are therefore less likely to engage in patient portals and other patient engagement tools and consumer technologies.7 These gaps severely limit the benefits of digital technologies to reach vulnerable populations facing long-standing health and social health disparities,8 but at the same time hold the potential to improve health equity. These differences in EHR adoption and functionality may exacerbate existing disparities in health outcomes, which will require targeted support for those healthcare organizations that are disadvantaged. Without interoperability, individuals in underserved or rural areas particularly, who often rely on fragmented healthcare services, may experience gaps in care, misdiagnoses, or delayed treatments. Studying equitable access to interoperability is vital to ensure that all populations, especially those in underserved or marginalized communities, benefit equally from advancements in healthcare technology and experience improved health outcomes.

Coordinated exchange of information plays a crucial role in advancing health equity by addressing the digital divide and social determinants of health. Given the historical fragmentation of health data in the United States, we do not fully understand if populations facing higher social inequities have equal access to digital exchange and interoperability capital among family medicine physicians, regardless of size of practice and rurality. Most of the research has focused on hospital settings,2–4,10,11 but less is known about the digital and interoperable capital of family medicine physicians in other settings. A recent hospital-based study revealed potential disparities for marginalized communities, indicating that hospitals serving areas with significant social deprivation were less likely to participate in interoperable exchange.11 As family medicine serves as the first line of care, it plays a crucial role in ensuring whole-person care and reducing healthcare costs. However, the gap in knowledge regarding the digital and interoperable capital of family medicine practices, particularly those serving vulnerable populations, limits the potential for leveraging HIT to improve health equity. Primary care, which includes family medicine, is essential to providing whole person care with the potential to improve health care outcomes and, ultimately, reduce health care costs. In this context, emphasis has been placed on the importance of primary care practices effectively utilizing digital information to coordinate with other healthcare providers. Considering the fragmentation challenges and the potential of primary care, family medicine could play a significant role in facilitating digital exchange and increasingly serve as digital health information hubs for their patients.12 There is a gap in knowledge about the digital exchange and interoperability capital for primary care providers serving populations with greater population vulnerabilities using national samples.

To date, disparities in digital exchange and interoperability capital among family medicine providers serving different populations in the United States remain poorly understood. The American Board of Family Medicine (ABFM) survey has allowed us to study elements of interoperability across a nationally representative sample of family medicine physicians. This study will examine disparities that exist in family medicine interoperability capital. The objective of this study is to examine disparities in the use of interoperability features within the EHR among family medicine physicians in the United States, particularly in areas with a higher social deprivation index and rural settings. We hypothesize that family medicine physicians serving areas with more population vulnerabilities will have decreased interoperability. This evidence is important to direct future investment and planning to strengthen health equity and digital capital for family medicine providers serving vulnerable populations with high needs. Delivery system innovations, payment reforms, and federally funded extension programs could offer promising pathways for further diffusion and potentially reduce disparities in functionality.5

Materials and methods

Study design and study sample

We conducted a cross-sectional analysis of family medicine physicians who completed the 2022 ABFM Continuous Certification Questionnaire (CCQ). The CCQ survey is completed by all diplomates seeking to continue certification upon exam registration.6 The 2022 CCQ was completed by 5458 family medicine physicians in the US seeking to continue their ABFM certification, with a 100% response rate. Each respondent was randomized to different sets of sub-questions. In this study, we will analyze 808 family medicine physicians who were randomized to questions that included meaningful use and interoperability with their EHRs. The Robert Graham Center’s Social Deprivation Index (SDI) was used to construct zip code tabulation area (ZCTA)-level deprivation based on 7 demographic characteristics used to quantify the socioeconomic variation in health outcomes (https://www.graham-center.org/maps-data-tools/social-deprivation-index.html). The Rural-Urban Continuum Codes were used to identify county-level rurality.

Study measures

The primary outcome of this study is physicians’ self-reported rating on how often they report utilizing elements of interoperability, including sending patient health information (PHI), receiving PHI, integration of received PHI, searching patients from outside their organization, and external patient data availability. Interoperable exchange was measured from five primary domains of reported utilization, including (1) searching, (2) sending, (3) receiving information, (4) integrating information into the EHR, and (5) viewing encounters of patients external to their organization. Similar categories have been used previously in the literature to study reported utilization of interoperability as described by the Office of the National Coordinator of Health Information Technology.10 Each dependent variable was dichotomized as yes (often/sometimes) or no (rarely/never) (Table 1). It should be noted that outcomes reflect self-reported frequency of use rather than verified technical capability. As such, observed differences may be influenced by variations in workflow, institutional incentives, user awareness, or underlying infrastructure, rather than true differences in system availability or functionality. We followed ABFM reporting conventions to ensure comparability; however, future studies could apply Likert-scale or ordinal modeling approaches to capture finer variations in response patterns with larger sample sizes. The primary independent variables are (1) area social vulnerabilities using the SDI as area indicators at the ZIP Code Tabulation Areas (ZCTA) level, modeling as quartiles, and (2) county-level rurality indicators.

Statistical analysis

We used 5 separate multivariate logistic regressions to understand the associations between interoperability and population deprivation, after adjusting for organizational size by number of providers, site affiliation, participation in value-based care, collaborations with local public health agencies, behavioral health, schools, and neighborhood organizations, and EHR vendor. Collaboration in the CCQ includes formal or informal partnerships with community organizations (eg, public health agencies, schools, behavioral health providers), which is independent of electronic health information exchange activities. The number of observations in each model varied due to missing data with key variables, including “don’t know” responses to five outcome variables: 97 for external access, 72 for search, 234 for integrate, 81 for receive, and 153 for send. Across the five interoperability domains, missing responses were significantly more likely among practices not participating in value-based payment (VBP) models, and response patterns differed significantly by health IT vendor. A sensitivity analysis using multinomial logistic regression that included a missing-response category yielded results that were not substantially different from those of the primary analyses (see Supplementary Tables). Models included robust standard errors clustered by the EHR vendor, and the 95% confidence interval (CI) was used to estimate the precision of the odds ratio (OR). This study was ruled exempt by the American Academy of Family Physicians Institutional Review Board. All analyses were performed using Stata version 17 (StataCorp LLC, College Station, TX).

Study results

Characteristics

Among the nationally representative sample of family medicine physicians, the majority were from hospital/HMO (43.1%), a practice size of 2-5 providers (35.6%), and participated in value-based purchasing programs (67.5%). Only 6 physicians (0.7%) were located in rural areas. Over half had collaborations with the local public health agencies (55.8%) and behavioral health organizations (58.0%). Collaborations with schools were reported by less than half of physicians (39.9%), while even fewer reported collaborations with neighborhood organizations (31.9%).

Within the 806-physician sample integrated with ZCTA for SDI quartile determination, approximately a quarter of physicians were situated in each quartile concerning social deprivation. Detailed physician and practice characteristics are presented in Table 2.

EHR interoperability

The primary domain of interoperability most commonly reported by family medicine physicians was the reception of health information from external sources (71.9%), closely followed by seeking information outside their organization (70.4%), and accessing electronic clinical data for patients from external sources (68.9%; see Figure 1). Conversely, family medicine physicians reported less frequent instances (63.4%) of transmitting patient information outside their organization using their primary outpatient EHRs.

Family medicine physicians have increased odds to integrate electronic information among those that collaborate with behavioral health agencies (Odds ratio = 1.838; 95% CI, 1.182-2.857, P = .007) and schools (Odds ratio = 1.742; 95% CI, 1.232-2.465, P = .002) compared to those that do not have collaborations, respectively.

Discussion

Coordinated information exchange is critical in addressing the digital divide and incorporating social determinants of health (SDH) into patient care. Incorporating SDH into EHR systems can help healthcare providers better understand and address the social factors affecting patient health, but the lack of standardization and interoperability remains a challenge.13 This study identified significant disparities in EHR interoperability among family medicine physicians, particularly among those serving populations with higher social deprivation. Although similar challenges were observed in rural areas, these findings should be considered preliminary given the small sample size.

This evidence supports our hypothesis that family medicine physicians in areas with greater social deprivation have lower odds of reporting key interoperability features such as receiving and searching patient information, and having health information available for patients external to their organization. These disparities present a critical challenge to health equity, as access to and sharing of comprehensive patient data are essential for improving quality of care through digital information exchange, particularly among underserved populations.

Our findings also reflect ongoing inequities in HIT interoperability access. The association between social deprivation and reduced interoperability is consistent with prior research indicating that healthcare providers serving disadvantaged communities often face resource constraints that limit their ability to adopt advanced health information technologies.8 Practices in these areas are less likely to implement interoperable systems, resulting in fragmented care and limited access to patient information, which can exacerbate existing health disparities.10 Mack et al. similarly reported disparities in EHR adoption between larger, well-funded practices and smaller, resource-limited providers, particularly in underserved areas.7 Our results provide additional evidence that this digital divide persists, with family medicine physicians in high-deprivation areas being significantly less likely to report engaging in critical elements of health information exchange, such as receiving and searching patient information, or having patient data available that are external to their organization.

Notably, not all interoperability functions exhibited the same sensitivity to social deprivation, which is supported by our study. Specifically, reporting “receive data” appeared less affected by socioeconomic context, as indicated by only a modest reduction in effect size from the third SDI quartile (OR = 0.692) to the fourth SDI quartile (OR = 0.558). This may reflect the influence of centralized health information exchange infrastructures and standardized EHR vendor functionalities that facilitate incoming data regardless of local resource capacity. In contrast, functions requiring greater local initiative (such as sending or integrating data) may depend more heavily on practice-level investments, staff training, and technological readiness, thereby amplifying disparities in resource-limited or socially deprived settings.

While we acknowledge the small sample size of rural physicians, our exploratory analysis suggests that rural practitioners may face distinct barriers to interoperability. However, studies with larger samples of rural providers are needed. Our study indicates that rural family medicine physicians have significantly lower odds to report sending and searching for patient information, as well as accessing patient data external to their organization compared to their urban counterparts. This is in line with studies that document the persistent digital divide in rural healthcare, where providers often lack the resources and infrastructure needed to fully integrate EHR systems.4 For example, rural hospitals and clinics frequently struggle to fully integrate EHR systems due to the high costs of and lack of access to broadband technology, which hampers their ability to share and access patient data efficiently.14 The limited capacity for information exchange in rural areas compromises the ability of healthcare providers to deliver timely and coordinated care, potentially leading to poorer health outcomes and widening disparities for rural populations. These findings highlight the importance of targeted interventions to support the adoption and overcome barriers to EHR interoperability in underserved areas, as seamless information exchange is crucial for enhancing care coordination and reducing the quality gap in healthcare delivery.

One promising finding is the role of community collaborations in improving interoperability. In the context of this survey, collaboration is defined as the routine exchange of patient information or joint programmatic activity between the physician’s practice and the organization. The role of community collaborations, such as partnerships with local public health agencies, schools, and behavioral health organizations, emerged as a facilitator for improved interoperability. Physicians who collaborate with local public health agencies were more likely to report sending and searching information. Physicians who collaborate with behavioral health organizations and schools were more likely to report integrating any type of patient health information received electronically into their EHR systems. These collaborations likely facilitate resource sharing and align reporting requirements, making it easier for providers to adopt and use interoperable technologies.8 This suggests that fostering partnerships between healthcare providers and community organizations may be a key strategy for expanding the use of EHR interoperability in underserved areas, while better incorporating information about the social determinants of health. In addition to enhancing interoperability, collaborations between family medicine physicians and community-based organizations offer several other benefits. These partnerships can help improve patient care by integrating social services with clinical services, addressing social determinants of health such as housing, food insecurity, and mental health. Such collaborations can also expand access to preventive services and screenings, ultimately leading to earlier interventions and improved health outcomes. Moreover, community partnerships can support care coordination for complex patients, particularly those with chronic diseases, by facilitating communication and resource-sharing between healthcare providers and community services. This integrated approach has the potential to reduce healthcare costs and improve overall patient satisfaction by addressing both medical and non-medical needs.15,16

Limitations

Due to the cross-sectional nature of this study, we cannot infer a causal association between family medicine physician’s EHR interoperability and social deprivation of populations they serve. Nevertheless, the results of this study offer evidence and emphasize the existence of disparities in interoperability resources among family medicine physicians serving communities with higher levels of social deprivation. Consequently, we may not see gains in population health and healthcare quality that were envisioned with the advanced use of EHRs and interoperability. In fact, some literature indicates that existing disparities might worsen in the absence of widespread adoption of these advanced functionalities.7 Second, we use the SDI quartile as the area-level composite measure as a proxy to determine population deprivation that family medicine physicians serve. As a composite measure, future studies should determine the impact of individual indicators included in the index and explore additional vulnerabilities across the social determinants such as food insecurity, housing stability, etc., that are not included in the SDI. Another limitation is the absence of key contextual variables that may influence interoperability, such as state-level EHR support policies, broadband access, and regional health information exchange participation. Incorporating publicly available datasets (such as the FCC Broadband Data and the ONC State HIE Policy Index) in future analyses could help disentangle whether observed disparities stem primarily from infrastructural barriers or practice-level characteristics. Next, prospective trials should include the collection of social determinants and vulnerabilities in EHR data to comprehensively study the actual patients served by family medicine physicians. We acknowledge that not all patients will have access to the family medicine provider in their area, especially those lacking health insurance. Outcome measures are based on self-reported use rather than verified technical capability; observed differences may reflect workflow, incentives, awareness, or infrastructure. Hence, reliance on self-reported measures of interoperability is a limitation, and responses represent physicians’ perceptions of how often they “send,” “receive,” or “integrate” data, which may be influenced by recall bias, social desirability, knowledge, or differing interpretations of question wording. Future studies should incorporate objective measures of interoperability, such as EHR audit logs or data exchange records, to validate self-reported responses and provide a more accurate understanding of interoperability practices across clinical settings. Because the number of rural physicians was small (n = 6), these findings should be considered preliminary, limiting the ability to stratify analyses or draw definitive conclusions about rural–urban differences. Future national studies should seek to prioritize expanding the sample size of rural family medicine physicians when investigating interoperability and digital health technologies, considering the substantial evidence indicating rurality as a confounding factor. Nevertheless, in our study, we did incorporate rurality into our model and exploratory analysis suggests that rurality may be a significant factor associated with decreased odds of reporting the use of several interoperability domains, but cannot be confirmatory due to the small sample size. In addition, the question set was randomly assigned across the ABFM sample to enhance generalizability. However, missing data on interoperability measures may introduce nonresponse bias, as physicians without experience or awareness of specific interoperability functions may have been more likely to skip these questions, resulting in underrepresentation of certain groups in the analysis. Across all five domains of interoperability, missing responses were significantly more likely among practices not participating in value-based payment models, and the prevalence of missingness also differed significantly by EHR vendor. Results from sensitivity analyses using multinomial logistic regression with an explicit missing-data category were not materially different from the primary analyses, indicating robustness of the findings. Detailed results are provided in the supplementary tables. Last, we considered hierarchical modeling but determined that sample size constraints limited feasibility. Models were instead clustered by EHR vendor to account for potential within-group correlations. Hierarchical approaches are recommended for future analyses with larger or multilevel datasets to better capture the nested structure of physicians within practices and organizations.

Policy implications

To improve EHR interoperability and address the digital divide in healthcare, programs should implement targeted efforts at federal, state, and practice levels. At the federal level, funding should continue to be allocated to support broadband expansion in areas with higher social deprivation and rural areas (such as federal Communications Commission [FCC] Connect America Fund and Rural Digital Opportunity Fund) and incentivize EHR systems within the Promoting Interoperability program to incorporate standardized fields for SDH data. Additionally, federal funding could be provided to underserved and rural practices for adopting interoperable EHRs, including providers serving patients with high shares of social deprivation. At the state level, partnerships with community organizations, including public health agencies, schools, and behavioral health services, should be encouraged and funded to promote resource sharing and alignment of reporting requirements that facilitate data sharing through interoperability. At the practice level, technical assistance and training should be available to help providers effectively implement and use interoperable EHR systems, with a focus on using community collaborations to integrate SDH data and improve care coordination for complex, underserved populations. These coordinated efforts would promote health equity by enabling a more comprehensive, data-driven approach to patient care.

Conclusions

Equitable access to utilizing digital resources and interoperability functionalities could reduce health disparities by ensuring that all healthcare providers have equal access to critical data, leading to more informed clinical decisions, better health outcomes, and efficient resource use. This study identifies disparities in interoperability utilization across family medicine physicians in the United States, which will require intentional support from current and future programs and workforce development to overcome and strive toward health equity. These findings highlight the need for targeted funding and cross-sector partnerships to strengthen interoperability in resource-limited settings; however, further research is needed to determine the extent to which such interventions may reduce underlying disparities. Achieving interoperability will be key to coordinating care and improving quality, especially for populations that face social challenges impacting mental and physical health. Future research should measure the extent to which family medicine physicians are improving quality of care with interoperability, particularly for those physicians serving populations with high social deprivation.
