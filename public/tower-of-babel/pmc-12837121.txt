Insights into extracellular vesicle-based platforms on the way to cancer treatment
Authors: Elnaz Bagheri, Elahehsadat Aminyazdi, Mohammad Ramezani, Marzieh Mohammadi, Khalil Abnous, Seyed Mohammad Taghdisi, Mona Alibolandi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundExosomes are nanoscale extracellular vesicles (EVs) (≈30–150 nm) defined by a lipid bilayer that encloses an aqueous interior. This amphiphilic architecture permits the simultaneous encapsulation of hydrophobic, hydrophilic, and amphipathic compounds. Their endogenous biogenesis results in high biocompatibility, negligible immunogenicity, and the capacity to evade rapid clearance while crossing biological barriers. Additionally, the Surface antigens retained from the cell of origin provide exosomes with innate specificity, enabling efficient uptake by specific cell subpopulations. Studies have successfully engineered exosomes to transport inorganic nanoparticles, chemotherapeutic drugs, nucleic acids, and contrast agents, resulting in enhanced therapeutic efficacy and diagnostic sensitivity. Hybrid exosome systems, which integrate exosomes with supplementary carriers such as polymers, lipids, and nanoparticles, have been developed to further enhance their efficacy and expand the translational potential of exosome-based nanomedicine.MethodsA comprehensive literature search was conducted across PubMed, Scopus, and Google Scholar to identify relevant studies focusing on the therapeutic and diagnostic applications of exosomes in cancer, including engineered and hybrid exosome systems. It was extended to include the most recent publications up to and including those released in 2025. Full texts were reviewed to extract key information regarding study design, exosome source and modification, cargo type, delivery mechanism, and reported outcomes.ResultsResearch has shown that exosomes can efficiently deliver therapeutic agents, greatly improving the effectiveness of targeted cancer treatments and the accuracy of diagnostics. Particularly, hybridized exosome-based systems have exceeded traditional techniques. Despite the limitations, such as the high expense and difficulty of accurate characterization, exosome-based systems have successfully penetrated various domains, including medication and gene delivery, diagnostics, and the development of hybrid carriers.ConclusionExosomes represent a flexible and promising platform for cancer diagnostics and treatment. Recent developments, particularly of hybrid exosome systems, have further boosted their therapeutic versatility and addressed some of the most significant drawbacks of native exosome technologies. Nevertheless, several challenges remain to be overcome, including the costly nature of purification, standardization of characterization methods, and safety issues and potential side effects. Overcoming these obstacles will ensure the clinical translation of exosome-based therapy.Graphical Abstract

Introduction

Exosomes are nanoscale EVs secreted by various eukaryotic and prokaryotic cells [1, 2]. with diameters ranging from 30 to 150 nm. Many bioactive components, like proteins and nucleic acids, enclosed by the lipid bilayer membrane, constitute these vesicles [3].

Recent advances in research methodology have established exosomes as important and active players in cell-to-cell communication. In contrast, earlier concepts, dating back to the late 1980s, described them as merely cellular waste products [4, 5]. They were recognized for contributing to several physiological processes, including the maintenance of tissue homeostasis and the regulation of the immune response [6]. They also have a crucial role in pathogenic mechanisms and disease-associated processes such as cancer [7]. Besides, exosomes initiate anti-tumor responses by presenting antigens and stimulating the immune system [8]. New evidence suggests that tumor-derived exosomes (TDXs) play a crucial role in cancer. These exosomes and their cargo may serve as prognostic markers for cancer, therapeutic targets, or even as carriers for anticancer drugs [9].

Because they are secreted into body fluids (such as bile, blood, urine, and breast milk) and due to their biological origin, they can overcome physiological defenses and barriers while maintaining their stability [10]. Due to their biocompatibility, these entities have been utilized as carriers for the precise transfer of genes, delivery of drugs, and even as diagnostic tools for the early detection of illnesses [11]. Exosomes carry lipids, metabolites, proteins, and nucleic acids to target cells, determining their biological functions [12].

To further enhance the encapsulation efficiency or modify the surface properties of the exosomes, recent studies have focused on the fusion of liposomes or polymersomes into the exosome structure [13]. In this regard, hybrid exosome platforms have gained tremendous interest. It was revealed that the hybrid vesicles enhanced cellular uptake and translation efficiency, and improved endosomal escape, a necessary step for gene delivery [13, 14]. The endosomal system is the site of exosome production, and its properties are influenced by the specific donor cell type [15]. Their inherent targeting capabilities are attributed to the distinctive biogenesis, composition, and surface markers of exosomes [16].

Their specific adherent membrane properties, derived from their parental origin, enable them to interact selectively with target cells that exhibit compatible receptors [16]. Tetraspanins, including CD9, CD63, and CD81, are expressed on the surface of exosomes. Due to their role in cell adhesion and signaling, they have been identified as remarkable biomarkers for targeting [17]. Epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR) and EGFRvIII have also been utilized as biomarkers for cancer detection, particularly in gliomas [18]. Several studies have utilized exosomal microRNAs, such as miR-21, miR-141, and miR-214, as diagnostic markers [19].

Due to the similarity between the morphologies of the EVs and their size, the extraction and purification of exosomes from the parental cells is considered an important and challenging process. Additionally, most separation methods do not provide high purity and recovery rates. The current study will introduce the isolation methods and discuss their advantages and adverse effects. Herein, we will discuss the role of exosomes as multifunctional delivery systems used in drug/gene delivery. Also, their role as diagnostic agents will be addressed. This review offers a comprehensive overview of exosome therapy. It covers the production and loading of exosomes, as well as their functions in drug and gene delivery. A significant aspect is the detailed comparison of different hybrid exosome structures, including those combined with liposomes or polymers. The review classifies these structures based on their benefits, drawbacks, and clinical potential. By connecting these previously separate topics, the article highlights the key differences among various methods and provides a new perspective that addresses an existing gap in the literature.

Exosomes

Based on biogenesis, biophysical, and size properties, numerous types of membrane vesicles, known as extracellular vesicles (EVs) [20], are classified into two categories: exosomes and ectosomes [20]. Ectosomes, with diameters of 100 to 500 nm, are ubiquitous vesicles. Exosomes are produced by the exocytosis of intraluminal vesicles (ILVs), which are contained within multivesicular bodies (MVBs) generated through the continuous activity of the plasma membrane (PM) [21]. The secreted exosomes, ranging in diameter from 30 to 150 nm [22], are classified based on features such as content (cargo molecules), size, and the source cells [23]. They play a significant role in intercellular communication, profoundly impacting the recipient cells. Exosomes possess specific surface biomarkers, including CD81, CD63, and CD9, which are part of the tetraspanin transmembrane-4 family. They also express transferrin and caveolin receptors, which are considered distinctive biomarkers, as well as a series of conserved proteins reflecting the characteristics of the parent cells [5, 24]. Exosomes serve as valuable tools for therapy and diagnosis in various diseases, including cancer, due to their unique ability to trigger biological responses. Specific biomarkers enable targeting of certain tumor sites [25], and their capacity to cross biological barriers, such as the blood-brain barrier (BBB), makes them suitable for delivering drugs to brain tumors [26]. Derived exosomes (TEXs) are a subtype used in the development of cancer treatments. They have tumor-promoting properties, such as supporting metastasis and helping tumors evade immune responses [27]. However, TEXs also carry Heat Shock Proteins (HSP70, HSP90) and Major Histocompatibility Complex I (MHC I), which can stimulate the immune system and potentially serve as candidates for vaccine development [28]. Exosomes are utilized as therapeutic and diagnostic tools because of their ability to elicit biological responses. They can also transport various cargoes, including metabolites, proteins, lipids, and nucleic acids. Once inside cells, these cargo molecules can activate specific pathways while evading immune detection, as exosomes originate from biogenic sources [29].

Purification and extraction of exosomes

Several methods are used to isolate exosomes from EVs. There are many challenges to using economical and efficient methods for the purification of exosomes. Consequently, it is crucial to develop a standardized technique for extracting exosomes [30]. Different methods are available to isolate exosomes, such as differential ultracentrifugation [31, 32], size-exclusion chromatography (SEC) [33], immunoaffinity capture [34], sucrose-gradient centrifugation [35], polymer-based precipitation [36, 37], microfluidics-based isolation [38, 39], and commercially available kits [40]. Among these methods, ultracentrifugation is the standard and most common method for isolating exosomes [41]. The advantages of this method include its speed, low equipment cost, and high yield. On the other hand, after repeated centrifugation, exosomes may be damaged. Among other disadvantages of this method, we can point out that this method is time-consuming and needs costly instrumentation [31]. SEC, based on a gel-like Sepharose, is used to sort based on specific molecular sizes and separate exosomes. In this method, the integrity and biological activity of the exosomes are preserved. However, the processing of numerous samples is challenging [42, 43]. Handling numerous samples in SEC can be challenging for several reasons, including time-consuming sample preparation, column saturation, volume limitations, and optimization requirements. These factors make it difficult to efficiently process a large number of samples while maintaining accuracy and reliable results. Antibody-based affinity capture is another method that can be used in numerous immune separation assays for rapid isolation of the exosomes. These methods are expensive and difficult for additional analyses [36, 44, 45]. The most significant disadvantage of commercial kits is that they lead to high protein impurity with exosomes; however, the yield of this method is high [46]. In polymer-based precipitation, the solubility of exosomes is decreased by polymers, such as PEG, and the exosomes can be isolated through low-speed centrifugation. In this method, exosomes might be contaminated with proteins [47]. The achievement of extracellular vesicles (EVs) as therapeutics is primarily dependent on two major technical challenges: first, the ability to separate EVs that are both pure and biologically active, and second, the ability to efficiently load them with therapeutic agents such as proteins, nucleic acids, or drugs. The optimization of exosome isolation is universally constrained by a compromise between the yield of particles and the purity of the final preparation [48]. Precipitation techniques (e.g., ExoQuick) offer the highest yield methods, but this is counterbalanced by remarkably compromised purity, as they non-specifically co-precipitate protein contaminants [49]. Although ultracentrifugation (UC) is a criterion method for preparing samples of high purity, it is disadvantaged by a lengthy workflow, the potential for exosome damage, and low yields [50]. SEC is ideal for its high purity extraction and exosome integrity, but with the essential drawback of sample dilution [51]. Immunoaffinity capture is an accurate tool that offers remarkable purity for specific subpopulations but proves to be a low-yield and costly approach [49]. To achieve the best results, combined methods such as SEC followed by ultrafiltration are being developed as superior methods, proposing high purity, reduced processing time, and high yield [52]. Therefore, there is no universally superior isolation process; the optimal procedure is entirely dependent on the intended downstream application.

No single isolation method is superior in all aspects; the downstream application dictates the optimal choice. However, for clinical translation, Size-Exclusion Chromatography (SEC) and optimized combination methods are generally more reliable due to their vital role in biological characterization. For biomarker-sensitive studies, high-yielding methods, such as precipitation, are not ideal due to a lack of absolute purity, and SEC and a combination of ultrafiltration/SEC are preferred [49].

A critical step for exosome utilization is a practical loading step that should ensure the integrity of EVs while maintaining the appropriate yield and consistency. Incubation as a passive loading method is affected by the temperature and pH [65]. Active physical methods also have benefits and drawbacks. Electroporation, although an effective technique for nucleic acids, may encounter challenges related to aggregation [66]. Sonication yields a reduced result while presenting a high loading [67]. Emerging methods, such as Tension-controlled loading, have the potential to produce better loading while minimizing damage to the integrity of the EV [66].

Exosomes against cancer

Due to the high mortality of cancer, several therapeutic approaches are applied for its treatment and diagnosis. Additionally, exosomes are a promising candidate for cancer treatment [68]. Recent studies have shown that tumor cells leak more exosomes than normal cells. The secreted exosomes are involved in tumor development by reducing the immune system’s efficiency and improving angiogenesis [69, 70]. Cancer development is significantly influenced by these entities, which can serve as nanocarriers by encapsulating therapeutic agents and attaching targeting ligands [71], thereby facilitating the diagnosis and treatment of tumors [72], They are suitable for targeted delivery of proteins, genes, small molecules, and contrast agents, as well as chemotherapeutics, radiotherapeutics, phototherapeutic agents, and immunomodulators to tumor tissues [73]. A summary of the exosomes’ classification, sources, and related utility for cancer treatment

In this review, we will discuss the clinical applications of exosomes for delivering therapeutic or diagnostic agents, with a focus on recent research in cancer treatment.

Exosomes for drug delivery

A suitable drug delivery technique should possess several key characteristics, including targeted delivery, minimal systemic side effects, biocompatibility, the ability to overcome biological barriers, and ease of administration. In addition to the properties above, exosomes possess unique characteristics, including targeting capability, controlled release patterns, natural origin, and stability in circulation, which make them suitable for drug delivery [113]. TAMs and cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs) are the main cells present in the stroma of tumor cells. The tumor microenvironment (TME) plays an essential role in tumor progression [114, 115]. Consequently, the inhibition of chemokine and cytokine secretion by CAFs, such as TGF-β, CXCL, and IL-6, could prevent cancer development [116]. Because exosome secretion from tumor cells is 10 times more than in normal cells, exosomes play a crucial role in cell-to-cell communication by delivering nucleic acids, signaling molecules, lipids, and proteins within the TME and facilitating the metastasis process [117]. Since traditional methods have indicated many disadvantages, including adverse side effects, multiple drug resistance, and low therapeutic indexes, an effective drug delivery system to dominate delivery-related challenges and targeted therapy is essential for clinical application.

Small-molecule delivery

With the ability to transport various chemicals and biological substances, exosomes have been studied as conventional vehicles for drug delivery. They are currently being considered as potential alternatives to other targeted carriers. Exosomes may be one of the best candidates for targeted drug delivery systems (DDSs) due to their intrinsic ability to cross the blood-brain barrier with high biocompatibility and specificity at target sites [118]. The exosome can be attached to target cancer cells via specific surface proteins, facilitating their effective cellular uptake. This vehicle can be applied to load photosensitizers or antitumor drugs for cancer therapy [119]. The selective accumulation of this carrier in tumor tissue enhances efficacy and reduces toxicity in normal cells. Artificial exosomes coated with aptamers, siRNA, and antisense oligonucleotides are a suitable alternative to natural exosomes for improving their accumulation in cancer cells [120]. Given the vital role of exosomes in disease treatment and as intercellular messengers, there has been a growing interest in exosome studies recently [121, 122]. Gemcitabine is a chemotherapeutic agent utilized in the treatment of pancreatic cancer. Systemic administration of GEM causes severe side effects in patients. Li et al. isolated exosomes from Panc-1 cancer cells for use as an autologous drug delivery system. Exosomes were isolated from Panc-1 cells at 70–80% confluency using centrifugation steps. Incubation and sonication techniques were employed to incorporate Gemcitabine into the exosomes. At the same time, the efficiency of the loading was later determined using high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) after the removal of unbound drugs. The autologous exosomes expressed CD47, a characteristic that inhibits their phagocytosis and enhances their persistence within the bloodstream. These exosomes were used for the targeted administration of GEM in cancer treatment. The therapeutic efficacy of this drug-loaded carrier was enhanced due to its accumulation in cancerous tissues, while simultaneously reducing side effects on normal cells [86].

In another study, Bagheri et al. successfully isolated MSC-derived exosomes and loaded the chemotherapeutic agent DOX using the electroporation technique. These exosomes were then conjugated with the MUC1 aptamer (5TR1) via a covalent bonding process. In contrast, the 5TR1 aptamer has been developed for MUC1-overexpressing tumors. Since MUC1 is overexpressed in many cancers, this aptamer was targeted during the process of drug delivery to allow for the targeted inhibition of proliferation. This result showed cellular uptake enhancement. It was tested in vitro on C26 cells and in vivo on a C26 tumor model in BALB/c mice. Therefore, the study concluded that the DOX@exosome-apt platform has the potential to be applied as a therapeutic in a clinical setting [132].

Similarly, exosomes derived from HEK293 cells were used to encapsulate DOX through an incubation technique and then post-functionalized with the AS1411 Aptamer (DOX-Apt-Exo) via covalent bonding for targeting colorectal cancer (CRC) cells. Results from in vitro and in vivo experiments demonstrated that this targeted nanocarrier could successfully permeate into target cells through ligand-receptor interactions, thereby enabling tumor treatment. The authors concluded that this targeting delivery system was a promising candidate for localized drug delivery in the treatment of colon cancer and warranted further investigation [133].

Among the proteins overexpressed in exosomes, one essential protein is metalloproteinase 15 (A15), which contains Arg-Gly-Asp (RGD) motifs. This protein interacts with integrin αvβ3, which is also overexpressed on tumor cells, facilitating increased cellular uptake of exosomes [134]. Gong et al. developed A15-modified exosomes derived from THP-1 cells incorporated with Chol-miRNA159 and DOX. The above nanocarrier has been used as a co-delivery vehicle in treating breast cancer. Based on the in vivo experiments, it can be said that the gene TCF-7 has been silenced without any side effects. Therefore, the synergistic effect of this targeted exosome, which was loaded with miR159 and DOX, was utilized in the therapeutic strategy for TNBC [135].

CSCs possess a characteristic differentiation potential that significantly contributes to tumor growth and metastasis. In contrast, conventional chemotherapy is ineffective in eradicating CSCs because these cells often turn quiescent and thus escape through the therapeutic net. The process of metastasis involves a phenomenon called epithelial to mesenchymal transition, or EMT, in which the motility of cells is significantly enhanced due to the detachment of tumor cells from their original sites. Targeting CSCs is one of the most promising approaches toward the elimination of cancer metastasis. Among the chemotherapeutic agents frequently used in the treatment of TNBC, DOX increases the metastasis and recurrence of the disease. 3,3′-Diindolylmethane (DIM) is a key bioactive compound in cruciferous vegetables and has been shown to mitigate the adverse effects of DOX while enhancing its efficacy against TNBC. In this respect, Sarkar et al. prepared mesoporous silica nanoparticles encapsulating both DOX and DIM, coated with exosomes derived from the B16 cell line, and termed them as e-DDMSNP. Results obtained in both in vitro and in vivo experiments confirmed that exosomes in this formulation successfully guided DOX and DIM to the CSC microenvironment. This consequently led to a decline in the EMT-inducing CSCs, thus impeding tumor metastasis. The integral membrane proteins characteristic of FA thus made it an effective marker for identifying tumoral cells since FR-elevated expression levels were observed in cancerous tissues. Therefore, FA-functionalized nanocarriers are particularly interesting in drug delivery because they exhibit a strong affinity for heparan sulfate proteoglycans and folate receptors. Besides, CQDs are also water-soluble, biodegradable, and fluorescent, hence useful as drug transport agents. Thus, there has been a rising interest in recent years regarding its utilization as a drug delivery system in the context of cancer treatment. In one of the significant works, Kazeminava et al. prepared FA-CQDs loaded with methotrexate and coated with exosomes derived from breast cancer cells, known as Ex@MTX-CQDs, specifically for targeting breast cancer treatment. Such a novel structure allowed the targeted delivery of MTX into tumor cells, as both FA-CQDs and exosomes were able to bind selectively to the folate and heparan sulfate proteoglycan receptors, respectively, thereby facilitating the precise delivery of MTX into the breast cancer cells [74].

MSCs release antiangiogenic factors, which metastasize to tumors and lead to tumor growth by inhibiting tumor angiogenesis. In addition, the functions of various cell types related to immunity were modulated by exosomes derived from MSCs by incorporating the therapeutic drug. Though efficient drug delivery occurs, low exosomal drug-loading efficiency exists. A combination of exosomes and liposomes for loading antitumor drugs was proposed to overcome that problem. Wang et al. developed a hybrid nanocarrier composed of exosome-derived MSCs and folate-targeted liposomes. In this way, the targeted nanocarrier was loaded with PTX as an anticancer drug to deliver drugs to malignant sites effectively. The result of the in vivo study showed that the growth of the tumor was suppressed in the CT26 tumor-bearing mouse. ELP injected into the cancer intratumorally is known to induce CD4+ and CD8+ T cells, polarize TAMs toward M1 type, and decrease the number of regulatory T cells. Terminalia chebula Retz (EaTCE), obtained from plants in India, has shown anti-inflammatory and anticancer activities. In one study, exosomes were extracted from lemon juice. After mixing EaTCE and exosomes with a homogenizer probe, the exosomes were incubated to recover their cell membrane. EaTCE was encapsulated in the membrane of the exosome, named Ex-EaTCE. They were applied for antitumor activity in the HepG2 cell line. Tests on cell viability and investigation into apoptosis indicated a practical therapeutic effect [138].

One of the challenges facing the employment of exosomes in treatment is their active role in cancer progression and metastasis [139]. Tumor-derived exosomes (TEXs) contribute to metastatic colonization by their molecules, such as integrin, which impede effective cancer treatment. Research indicates that the horizontal transfer of αvβ5 to the liver may result in metastases to this organ, while the horizontal transmission of α6β4 and α6β1 integrins leads to lung involvement. They also have a role in Epithelial-Mesenchymal Transition (EMT), which results in stromal cell development [119]. Studies have also shown that platelet-derived exosomes contributed to lung cancer angiogenesis and metastasis by their CD41 integrin [140, 141]. To avoid these challenges, researchers could turn to non-tumor cell sources, such as dendritic cells [139], and aim for an optimized purification method, such as the combination of ultracentrifugation (UC) and density gradient separation, as well as microfluidic platforms [139, 142]. Another strategy involves targeted neutralization to silence the undesired process. The use of anti-PD-L1 antibodies has been effectively employed to counteract the immunosuppressive action [140]. Zhao and colleagues effectively formulated a combination of doxorubicin-encapsulated platelet-derived small extracellular vesicles (Pex) named PexD and an anti-PD-L1 monoclonal antibody (aPD-L1) in the form of an in situ sprayable gel, which effectively hindered metastasis and reduced cancer recurrence when applied post-resection surgery [143]. When administered intravenously, exosomes cannot effectively reach tumor cells, as a high percentage of them may be eliminated by the mononuclear phagocyte system (MPS) of the spleen and liver. To overcome these challenges, alternative approaches were designed to produce an effective exosome. CD9, as an exosome biomarker, is expressed on the surface of the exosome. Integrin αvβ3 is a receptor expressed in various cancer cells, including those of pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC), which facilitates the metastasis and invasion of tumor cells. The RGD peptide, as a ligand, can bind to the αvβ3 integrin, indicating potential applications in antiangiogenesis and targeted therapies. The rapid clearance of exosomes from the circulation after in vivo injection is one of the barriers to exosomal drug delivery. After i.v. administration, exosomes are accumulated in the lungs, liver, and spleen, and phagocytosed by macrophages. To overcome this obstacle, CD47, as a “don’t eat me” signal, was added to the surface of the exosome, minimizing MPS clearance and phagocytosis. In this study, the N-terminus of CD9 was engineered to facilitate the effective loading of proteins into exosomes. This strategy improved the display of ligands on the surfaces of exosomes. RGD peptide and CD47p110–130 were placed on CD9-engineered exosomes (ExoSmart). The approach of CD47p110–130 reduced MPS clearance through SIRPα-mediated interaction with CD47p110–130 on macrophages. Moreover, RGD was used to target αvβ3 on pancreatic cells, which enhanced specific cellular uptake. Paclitaxel (PTX) as an anticancer drug was also loaded in ExoSmart, which enhanced the therapeutic effect by up to 50-fold [144].

Arginine-glycine-aspartic acid, or RGD, is a targeting tripeptide utilized for diagnostic purposes; it is overexpressed in tumor cells, particularly integrins, hence improving targeting accuracy. Wu et al. synthesized RGD-modified exosomes, designated as RGD-EXO, for the encapsulation of indocyanine green and cisplatin, termed ICG-DDP, to attain a synergistic impact in imaging and therapy. Indocyanine green is an imaging agent that exhibits fluorescent properties, while cisplatin is a chemotherapeutic agent. RGD-EXO was generated by culturing HEK-293 T cells in the presence of Ac4ManNAz for 48 hours. The exosomes secreted with azide groups at their surface, N3-EXO, were then collected by ultracentrifugation. Afterward, the azide groups were utilized to facilitate bioorthogonal reactions through ‘click’ chemistry with DBCO-RGD, and the RGD-EXO was successfully formulated. Later, ICG-DDP was incorporated into the resultant exosomes via electroporation, resulting in ID@RGD-EXO. The therapeutic efficacy of this engineered system was tested in vitro and in vivo using Cal-27 cells. FLI, PAI, and IRT imaging after treating ID@RGD-EXO showed marked improvements. Under laser radiation, the viability of Cal-27 cells incubated with ID@RGD-EXO was reduced by 30%. In contrast, the Cal-27 cells incubated with I@RGD-EXO retained almost 100% viability, demonstrating the efficiency and low toxicity of exosomes in the delivery of therapeutic agents [145].

TRAIL is an inducer of apoptosis due to its interaction with specific receptors. In a creative method, Ye et al. developed the therapeutic application of exosomes containing TRAIL and cisplatin (DDP) to facilitate the co-delivery of therapeutic agents toward treating cervical cancer. Human chorion-derived mesenchymal stem cells were infected with lentivirus to generate TRAIL-engineered hCD-MSCs (hCD-MSCsTRAIL). Subsequently, the exosomes were extracted from the transformed cells by centrifugation and ultracentrifugation. The DDP was ultimately introduced into hCD-MSCs-ExoTRAIL via electroporation to create the DDP & hCD-MSCs-ExoTRAIL complex. Both in vitro and in vivo studies demonstrated that the DDP & hCD-MSCs-ExoTRAIL-treated group exhibited a better induction of apoptosis and inhibition of proliferation in cervical cancer models compared with either DDP or hCD-MSCs-ExoTRAIL treatment alone. Moreover, the lowest expression of survivin was observed in the DDP & hCD-MSCs-ExoTRAIL-treated group, suggesting that the use of exosomes may be a feasible method for co-delivering DDP and TRAIL into cancer cells to elicit a synergistic therapeutic effect [146].

Another study demonstrated the delivery of DDP via exosomes derived from NK cells to address OC, effectively overcoming the resistance of cancer cells to the drug, a prevalent cause of mortality among OC patients. Here, the exosomes were isolated from expanded NK cells, namely eNK-EXO, via ultracentrifugation, after which DDP was loaded using electroporation methods. These findings confirmed that only eNK-EXO inhibited the growth of OC cells without showing any toxic effect on normal ovarian epithelial cells. Moreover, significantly increased cell death was detected after the combination treatment with eNK-EXO and DDP compared to the treatments with either DDP or eNK-EXO alone. Significant inhibitory action was observed even in DDP-insensitive OV-90 cells and the DDP-resistant COC1/DDP cell line. 5-fluorouracil (5-FU) stands as one of the pivotal therapeutic agents in the treatment of colon cancer, a disease with one of the highest mortality rates. Given the aggressive nature of this form of cancer, much interest remains in enhancing its treatment modalities. Shekh et al. employed exosomes to deliver 5-FU against human colon cancer HCT116 cells. Targeted drug delivery initially considered ultracentrifugation-isolated exosomes from HCT116 cells, which were incubated with 5-FU to facilitate their incorporation into these exosomes. By quantification, the drug loading and entrapment efficiencies were determined to be 21.36% ± 2.6% and 72.65%, respectively. According to the results of the drug release analysis, the effective release of 5-FU was observed in the acidic pH of the TME. In vitro, it was demonstrated that 5-FU-loaded exosomes induced cell death at a higher rate and arrested the cell cycle in the G0/G1 phase at a higher rate compared to an equal dose of free 5-FU, accompanied by an increase in nuclear fragmentation of HCT116 cells [77].

In another study, Meng et al. used exosomes from murine embryonic stem cells and fibroblasts that stably expressed granulocyte-monocyte colony-stimulating factor (GM-CSF) as a vaccine for lung cancer. Using exosomes instead of live embryonic stem cells improves the vaccine’s safety. The study results showed that using GM-CSF exosomes as a vaccine protected the mice against metastasis of pulmonary tumors [148].

Gene delivery

Gene therapy combined with exosome technology is one of the most exciting frontiers of biological research and its possible clinical applications. Hybrid systems combining genes and exosomes rely on the special properties of exosomes, which enable the targeted delivery of genetic materials, such as DNA or RNA, to specific cells. Functional genes in exosomes provide a strategy to increase gene stability and availability while decreasing various adverse effects associated with standard means of application. Such an enormous treatment novelty has the potential to open the way towards more personalized therapies for the coming decades. The composition of exosomes includes integrins, which play a significant role in organotropic metastasis. Exosomes derived from breast cancer that are enriched with α6β4 and α6β1 integrins bear characteristics that may enable targeting of the lungs, especially in an environment rich in laminin. This may allow the development of more personalized therapeutic approaches [72]. S100A4 protein is highly overexpressed within the TME and plays a vital role in enhancing the process of tumor growth and metastasis. Treatment with S100A4 siRNA (siS100A4) results in the targeting and silencing of the S100A4 protein. Zhao et al. extracted exosomes from the breast cancer cells tagged with specific targets for lung cells. In the present study, CBSA was conjugated with siS100A4 to form a core, which was then enveloped by an exosomal membrane, resulting in the CBSA/siS100A4@Exosome structure. The composite structure exhibited remarkable accumulation in lung cancer cells within mice that had developed lung tumors, and it was found that gene silencing reduced both malignancy progression and metastasis [75].

In a closely related study, Hosseini et al. reported the successful isolation of exosomes from mesenchymal stem cells and their use as an effective and harmless delivery method in combating multiple sclerosis. Their results showed that the LJM-3064 aptamer, covalently attached to the surface of the exosome, had a high affinity for myelin and thus acted as an anti-inflammatory agent that favored remyelination. The in vitro experiments demonstrated that this therapeutic aptamer increased the proliferation of the oligodendroglial cell line, OLN93. Besides, the in vivo studies presented reduced inflammatory responses and decreased demyelination within the central nervous system among female C57BL/6 mice [149].

The risk of developing brain metastases (BM) is high in people with advanced non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) [150]. The available treatments for BM are limited because of the blood-brain barrier (BBB). One of the cytosolic enzymes that plays an essential role in developing BM of lung cancer through the PI3K/AKT/MYC pathway is lysophosphatidylcholine acyltransferase 1 (LPCAT1). Consequently, gene silencing of LPCAT1 can prevent the development and metastasis of lung cancer cells. In this regard, Jiang and coworkers designed the scFv and lamp2b fusion protein cloned into the pcDNA3.1(-) vector. When scFv genes on the surface of HEK-293-derived exosomes were expressed, targeted delivery of therapeutic molecules to the EGFR of lung cancer occurred. Next, electroporation loaded LPCAT1 siRNA (siLPCAT1) into these engineered exosomes (exoscFv). These engineered vesicles effectively crossed the BBB, and siLPCAT1 was delivered to the BM site. The results showed that the malignant development of BM was inhibited by silencing the LPCAT1 gene. Generally, RNA interference (RNAi)- loaded exosome vesicles were used to treat lung cancer-related BM [151].

Umbilical mesenchymal stem cell-derived exosomes (uMSC-Exo) can be utilized in regenerative medicine to inhibit HCV (Hepatitis C virus) infection and viral replication. Studies have shown that miR-221, miR-145, let-7f, and miR-199a, which exhibit distinct expression patterns, are secreted from uMSC-Exo, where they interact with HCV RNA and suppress the RNA replication of HCV. These miRNAs were released from exosomes that interacted with the binding sites of HCV RNA. Actually, the U.S. Food and Drug Administration approved telaprevir or interferon-α with uMSC-Exo as adjuvants, which demonstrated a synergistic effect for future HCV therapy applications [152].

One of the lethal brain cancers is glioblastoma. Temozolomide (TMZ), as a chemotherapy and radiotherapy drug, is used for glioblastoma treatment. Due to the impressive side effects of TMZ [153], gene therapy-based microRNAs (miRNAs) have been noticed recently. One of the oncogenic miRNAs is microRNA-21 (miR-21), which inhibits the expression of phosphatases, particularly phosphatase and tensin homolog (PTEN) and programmed cell death protein 4 (PDCD4). Consequently, the proliferation of tumor cells and their apoptosis process can be controlled by knocking down miR-21. Antisense miRNA oligonucleotides against miR-21 (AMO-21) are used as a therapeutic agent for glioblastoma therapy. Kim and coworkers designed T7 peptide-surface-decorated exosomes for the targeted delivery of AMO-21 through systemic injection. Due to the overexpression of the transferrin receptor (TfR) on the glioblastoma tumor cell surface, the T7 peptide (ligand of this receptor) was bound. It induced the transfer of exosomes through the BBB more efficiently. In addition, exosomes functionalized with rabies virus glycoprotein (RVG) were also used as targeted carriers to neuronal cells. Besides, the loading of AMO-21 into exosomes was followed by electroporation. In vitro studies indicated that T7-Exo had a higher AMO-21 delivery efficiency compared to RVG-modified exosomes. Besides, the expression of PTEN and PDCD4 in tumor cells of rat models that received intravenous injections of T7-exo-loaded AMO-21 was reduced after interaction with miR-21. Consequently, the study revealed that T7-exo is a suitable carrier for AMO-21 delivery for glioblastoma therapy [154].

In another study, Wang and coworkers designed a chemo/gene/photothermal system for cancer therapy. In the first step, after transforming THP-1 cells into macrophages, they were incubated with exosome-depleted FBS and DSPE-PEG-Biotin (50 μg/mL) to be modified with biotin. After ultracentrifugation, the biotin-labeled exosomes were detached from the supernatant of the medium. In the second step, DOX, as the chemotherapeutic drug, was loaded into engineered exosomes via the electroporation method. After that, this carrier was covered with Fe3O4@PDA, conjugated with molecular beacons and avidin. In this system, molecular beacons (MBs) were modified with the target miR-21 sequence and used for responsive molecular imaging. In the absence of miR-21, the MB structure was designed based on a stem-loop hairpin. Thus, after the hybridization of miR-21 with target miR-21, the fluorescence group of MB was separated from the Fe₃O₄@PDA as the quencher agent, and the hairpin structure was opened. An external magnetic field was applied to accumulate this platform at the tumor site. After that, under near-infrared radiation (NIR), hyperthermia was localized, and DOX was released, resulting in the killing of 91.04% of cancer cells following Exo-DOX-Fe3O4@PDA-MB treatment. The in vivo study results demonstrated a 97.57% reduction in tumor cell size when utilizing this carrier in a magnetic field (87).

As siRNAs can be used as a new generation of nucleic acid drugs for cancer treatment, their chemical modifications are necessary for effective delivery. Exosomes were extracted from bovine milk and loaded with 5′ −32P-labeled siKRAS by the electroporation method for gene silencing. SiRNA against specific genes, such as mutated KRAS (KRASG12S) (which plays a vital role in improving lung cancer), knock down the expression of this gene by 2 to 10-fold. Besides, loading siRNA into exosomes could prevent degradation. The in vitro study of exosomes decorated with folic acid and carrying siRNA against KRAS in A549 cells indicated that proliferative activity was inhibited. Also, the size of A549 tumor xenografts in mice was decreased. Consequently, this platform can be a suitable natural nanocarrier [155].

In another study, patients with CRC were treated with exosomes loaded with 5-FU and miR-21 inhibitor (miR-21i) through the electroporation method. Studies have shown that the human colon cancer cell line (HCT-1165FR) was 5-FU-resistant, overexpressing miR-21. An engineered exosome derived from HEK-293T cells, expressing Her2 fused with LAMP2, was used for efficient delivery of miR-21 and 5-FU to HCT-1165FR cells. The expressed Her2 on the surface of exosomes interacted with the EGFR receptor of HCT-1165FR cells, resulting in targeted delivery. The engineered platform was used for synergistic drug and gene delivery [156].

Immunotherapy with prolonged high-efficiency and low-toxicity properties is used for malignant melanoma (MM) treatment as a destructive skin cancer. Due to the TME, immunotherapy is not an effective treatment option for MM. DCs in the MM microenvironment lack stimulating factors, resulting in the inability of CD8+ T cells to activate. Furthermore, because of particular gene mutations, MM patients show a poor reaction to immunotherapy, leading to metastasis. One of the most important genes in the development and proliferation of this cancer is the BRAF V600E mutated gene, which plays a crucial role in the angiogenesis of tumor cells. Consequently, to improve the efficacy of immunotherapy against MM, maturation of DCs to activate T-cells and silence the BRAF V600E mutation gene must be performed. To achieve this aim, Lin and coworkers utilized mature dendritic cell (mDexos)-derived exosomes as carriers for inducing immune system function. Besides, BRAF siRNA (siBRAF) was loaded into mDexos through the electroporation method and applied for gene therapy by silencing mutated BRAF in MM. SiBRAF inhibited VEGF, IL-10, and IL-6 fabrication, and returned DCs to a mature state. Since siBRAF was quickly degraded in the body environment by nucleases, mDexos as nanocarriers were used to encapsulate siBRAF to achieve greater stability. The result of the study indicated that the BRAF gene silencing efficiency of siBRAF-mDexos was enhanced. Also, the development of tumors in B16–F10 mouse melanoma cells was significantly decreased. Thus, this platform was utilized for the simultaneous delivery of gene therapy and immunotherapy to MM patients [157].

Overexpression of the membrane-associated RINGCH finger protein 1 (MARCH 1) contributes to cell migration and progression in osteosarcoma, a common type of bone cancer. Exosome-enriched miR-486 3p was isolated by high-speed centrifugation. Through coculture studies, the proliferative index of OS cells was demonstrated to be suppressed by targeting the circKEAP1/MARCH 1 pathway, which is associated with elevated levels of exosomal miR-486-3p. Yang et al. extracted exosomes from human fetal osteoblasts, which were rich in miR-486-3p, and these were then incubated with MNNG/HOS and U2OS cell lines. Cell viability was significantly reduced after 48 hours of incubation. This effect is due to circKEAP1 acting as a sponge for miR-486-3p, which downregulates MARCH1 expression. Consequently, with the increased levels of exosomal miR-486-3p, the circKEAP1/miR-486-3p/MARCH1 pathway in the progression of OS is effectively inhibited [158].

Antisense oligonucleotide therapy is a new strategy in cancer treatment. Evidence has demonstrated that miR-146b-5p plays an important role in the development of colorectal cancer and may serve as a promising target for treatment. Yu et al. employed exosomes as a carrier to deliver anti-miR-146b-5p ASO in their work, PMO-146b. CP05-P was used for anchoring PMO-146b onto the surface of exosomes derived from hUC-MSC. CP05-P binds to CD63, a marker present on the exosomal surface, forming a complex referred to as ePPMO-146b. In vitro assays in SW620 cells revealed that ePPMO-146b effectively restrains the migration and proliferation of cells. Further, PPMO-146b overexpression in the tumorous tissue significantly diminished tumor growth and volume in a mouse xenograft model. This study demonstrates the feasibility of exploiting exosomes for cancer therapies targeting miRNA [159].

Gliomas are the most common intracranial tumors that originate from glial cells, comprising about 80% of malignant brain tumors. The DACT2 gene, located on chromosome 6q27, functions as a tumor suppressor. DACT2 has been implicated in glioma treatment by directly inhibiting cell growth and facilitating apoptosis or by enhancing the efficacy of the chemotherapeutic agent temozolomide. Besides, the VAR2CSA malaria protein (RVP) expression is also reported to be overexpressed in gliomas. In one study, Xu et al. prepared a modified cationic lipid exosome to deliver the dapper homolog 2 (DACT2) gene, which acts as an antineoplastic agent. These exosomes were altered by the incorporation of GHDC and NH₂-PEG-MAL to bear a positive charge, enabling them to be used as a gene vector for DACT2. At the same time, RVP antibodies were also conjugated to exosomes to enhance the tumor-specific targeting of modified exosomes. Results proved significant growth inhibition of glioma cells in in vitro and in vivo studies, representing the potential of modified cationic exosomes for effective gene delivery [160].

The blood-brain barrier (BBB) is crucial for the targeted delivery of chemotherapeutic drugs in the treatment of central nervous system (CNS) malignancies. Because MSC-derived exosomes can pass across the BBB into tumor cells, they are a suitable vehicle for effective and safe drug delivery. Focused ultrasound (FUS) can enhance the accumulation of therapeutic substances in brain tissue and delay tumor progression. The result of this study indicated that low-intensity focused ultrasound (LIFU) increased the accumulation and permeability of stem cell-derived exosomes in the tumor region after IV administration and was used for CNS therapy. Without microbubbles, LIFU was unable to successfully open the BBB after exposure durations of 20, 40, or 60 minutes. The dextran study estimated the effect on the BBB endothelium both with and without LIFU, investigating whether the duration of LIFU exposure influenced the interruption of the BBB. A dextran solution comprising antibodies for RHO70 and FITC10 was injected into the tail vein of each rat immediately after managing LIFU for 0, 20, 40, or 60 minutes into the right hippocampus. One of the most common CNS diseases is glioma, with a poor diagnosis and a high rate of recurrence. One of the common epigenetic modifications in malignant glioma is N6-methyladenine (m6A), which contributes to the progression of this type of tumor. Methyl transferase-like 3 (METTL3) is overexpressed in malignant glioma and increases the methylation level of m6A. Besides, miR-1208, as a tumor-suppressive microRNA, can attach to the 3’ untranslated regions (3’UTR) of the targeted protein and prevent gene expression in such cancers. Still, the primary function of miR-1208 in glioma cancer is unclear. Zhan and coworkers used MSC-extracted exosomes as a carrier and loaded them with miR-1208, a tumor suppressor gene. They understood that the expression level of miR-1208 in glioma tissues was decreased, and the pathological grade of the glioma was increased. On the other hand, when the expression level of miR-1208 was enhanced, the proliferation of glioma cells decreased, and the malignant performances of glioma cells were inhibited. Actually, miR-1208 induced apoptosis in glioma cells. Using the CCK-8 method, the cell viability of glioma cells co-cultured with Exos-miR1208 at varying ultrasonic levels was investigated. The result indicated that cell viability was decreased by enhancing the irradiation time and ultrasonic intensity. Besides, in the presence of 0.5 W/30 s ultrasound parameters, the maximum uptake of miR-1208 in exosomes was observed in glioma cells. Consequently, the combination of FUS with Exos-miR-1208 is an appropriate method for glioma treatment [162].

The advantages of exosomes in LIFU diagnostics include non-invasive delivery, increased permeability, and targeted therapy. Additionally, the method has several disadvantages, including technical complexity, potential tissue damage, and its effectiveness, which depends on the duration, intensity, and type of tissue.

Their instability significantly hampers the clinical use of exosomes. As delicate biological carriers, they are susceptible to changes in pH and temperature, which can damage their membranes, lead to degradation, and ultimately reduce their therapeutic effectiveness [155, 156]. Exosomes must be stored stably at around −80 °C, a logistical challenge for clinical and pharmaceutical use [157]. Additionally, the process of freezing and thawing exosomes can cause aggregation, which reduces their therapeutic potency [155, 156]. However, the use of cryoprotectants, such as trehalose and sucrose, offers a potential solution to minimize this damage [158–162]. Allogeneic exosomes and repeated injections are associated with immunogenic risks, including the stimulation of systemic immune responses that lead to opsonization and rapid clearance, thereby decreasing therapeutic potency and in vivo half-life [163]. The inability to isolate exosomes with consistent purity and yield presents a major manufacturing obstacle, hindering the scale-up of exosome therapies for clinical use [163–165]. Large-scale, pharmaceutical-grade production of exosomes faces several significant practical challenges. Major limitations include their short lifespan in the bloodstream, limited ability to reach specific targets, and a lack of clear control systems—all of which need to be addressed for practical application [164, 166, 167].

Hybrid exosomes

Due to the limitations of exosomes as drug delivery vehicles, including challenges in loading large molecules, low production yields, low encapsulation efficiency, and the need for high concentrations of exosomes to achieve effective therapeutic properties, the combination of exosomes with organic or inorganic nanomaterials is necessary to overcome these issues. Furthermore, by modifying organic/inorganic nanomaterials with exosomes, their uptake by immune cells can be minimized. Hybrid organic/inorganic exosome nanoparticles can specifically transport and encapsulate drugs at specific concentrations, protect drugs from unpredictable biological interactions, and reduce the required dose. These hybrid nanoparticles enable concurrent drug delivery and tracking using ultrasound signals, luminescence, or magnetic resonance imaging. These hybrids provide the advantages of exosome biocompatibility along with the customizable features of nanoparticles [168].

As collaborations between researchers and clinicians in different fields expand, exosome-based methods are gradually evolving from initial proof-of-concept studies to practical, precision delivery systems suitable for clinical use. With ongoing scientific rigor and a focus on translation, exosomes hold the promise of transforming the next wave of targeted treatments and furthering the progress of personalized medicine. Liposome-exosome hybrids

The loading efficiency of exosomes can be improved, and the formation of exosome-liposome hybrids can address the problem of low yield of exosomes. Studies have shown that these hybrids increase the circulation time of liposomes and enhance their ability to cross biological barriers. Therefore, this hybrid has been effectively applied to encapsulate drugs, genes, and gene editing systems. Liposomes act as biocompatible carriers that can accommodate hydrophobic, hydrophilic, and amphiphilic drugs [177]. They face problems such as rapid clearance from the mononuclear phagocytic system (MPS) and limited targeting capabilities [178, 179].

In this regard, a study applied exosome-liposome hybrid nanoparticles to enhance drug delivery efficiency, thereby evading elimination by the MPS system. In this study, triptolide (TP) and miR497 were encapsulated in an exosome-liposome hybrid and used for the treatment of cisplatin-resistant ovarian cancer (OC). Studies have shown that TP is potentially effective in drug-resistant ovarian cancer. It inhibits the growth of chemotherapy-resistant cell lines across various tumors and acts synergistically with several chemotherapeutic agents to combat tumor resistance [180]. TP efficiently prevented the PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathway. Furthermore, TP improved the conversion of M2 macrophages into M1 macrophages, aiding in reversing tumor resistance. Overexpression of microRNA-497 (miR497) improves the apoptosis pathway and inhibits tumor growth [181]. Besides, TDEs with nanosized properties as DDSs expressed CD47 (a transmembrane protein), avoiding phagocytosis by the MPS. This hybrid was decorated with cRGD peptide. As the PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathway is known to play a crucial role in inhibiting apoptosis, the designed structure aimed to function through this regulatory mechanism. TP was used as a therapeutic agent in this platform, and the presence of calcium phosphate (CaP) facilitated the adsorption of miR497 onto the surface of the structure. Exosomes and cRGD in this structure were used to target this platform. In the tumor acidic microenvironment, this platform was disassembled, releasing TP and miR497, which triggered apoptosis in OC cells by inhibiting the PI3K/AKT/mTOR signaling pathway. GSH levels in OC cells were reduced, and TP increased intracellular ROS. Additionally, TP addressed drug resistance by modifying macrophage polarization. The in vivo result indicated that tumor size was significantly decreased in mice treated with this platform, and the survival rate was improved. This platform may offer a promising strategy for treating chemotherapy-resistant tumors [182].

In another study, a hybrid platform of fibroblast-derived exosomes and Clodronate (CLD)-loaded liposomes was designed to deliver drugs to fibrotic lung tissue. Pulmonary fibrosis is a severe and often chronic disease of the lungs that results in the uncontrolled growth of fibrous tissue [183]. Recent treatments are primarily based on anti-fibrotic drugs; however, their lack of selectivity limits their effectiveness in preventing the development of fibrosis. Approximately 90% of injected nanoparticles accumulate in the liver, while less than 1% reach the target site, resulting in drug removal in fibrotic areas. In addition, the interaction between the extracellular matrix (ECM) and collagen prevents nanoparticle penetration. CLD is a bisphosphonate that was entrapped in liposomes (clodrolip) and can kill macrophages through apoptosis [184]. After diminishing macrophages with clodrolip, targeted delivery of nanoparticles to the tumor site and therapeutic effect occurred. Besides, exosomes exhibited unique surface proteins that tended to their original cells, thereby reducing external influences. However, low drug loading capacity limits its application as a drug delivery vehicle. CLD delivered from liposomes in this platform reduced the Kupffer cells. Additionally, the presence of exosomes facilitated the specific targeting of fibroblast tissue, thereby increasing their accumulation and involvement in pulmonary fibrosis. Additionally, nintedanib (NIN), a triple angiokinase inhibitor for pulmonary fibrosis, was loaded into a hybrid of EL-CLD, significantly enhancing its therapeutic effects. Besides, a reduced inflammatory response was triggered by macrophages. Therefore, the EL-CLD hybrid system was considered an effective platform for delivering anti-fibrotic drugs to the lungs and should be further improved as a targeted therapy for fibroblasts [185].

Zhou et al. proposed the development of an exosome-liposome hybrid with surface modifications to create more effective vesicles loaded with dasatinib for suppressing pancreatic cancer cells. First, dasatinib was encapsulated in liposomes prepared by EPC, cholesterol, and PEG2000-DSPE through the thin lipid film hydration method. Then, the product was sonicated and extruded to reach an appropriate size of 118.9 nm. Then, liposomes were mixed with exosomes derived from human pancreatic cancer cells, which had a size of 106.47 nm. After a freeze-thaw cycle, the resulting dasatinib-containing hybrid exosome had a diameter of 119.90 nm and an encapsulation efficiency of 88.70 ± 1.53%, with a drug-loading capacity of 2.16 ± 0.04%. Additionally, pH-sensitive drug release in acidic environments further increased drug delivery to the acidic milieu of cancerous tissues. The study found that at higher concentrations of dasatinib than 0.123 µmol/L (p < 0.05), the hybrid exosome had a significantly higher inhibiting action against HuP-T3 and Panc0403 cells compared to liposomes and free dasatinib. Moreover, the treatment with hybrid exosomes resulted in an apoptosis rate of 69.53 ± 0.70% in HuP-T3 cells and 15.43 ± 1.07% in Panc0403 cells. Glutathione is overexpressed in the TME due to resistance to oxidative stress and thus can be a potential target for some tumor-specific therapies. Nguyen Cao et al. developed the exosomes comprising diselenide-bearing lipids (DSe) conjugated with triphenylphosphonium and chlorin e6 (Ce6), along with glycolysis inhibitors FX11. Drug-loaded redox-cleavable lipids with diselenide linker incorporation into exosomes resulted in drug release, even in the presence of elevated glutathione levels at the tumor site. Disintegration of diselenide bonds through reduction reactions involving GSH enables the controlled release of the mitochondria-targeted molecule TPP and the photosensitizer Ce6 at a specific site. Second, near-infrared fluorescence imaging has been a breakthrough in medical imaging, allowing deep tissue penetration and generating high-resolution images by tracking the NIR-II fluorophores, such as cyanine dyes. The inherent targeting abilities of exosomes further increased the targeting for pathological brain tissues. Fluorescence microscopy analyses demonstrated that the exosomes significantly enhanced fluorescence intensity in target cells, and under laser irradiation, the in vivo photothermal therapy efficacy of the NIR-C12-EL hybrid was remarkable, amounting to 62.28%. PDAC is a highly aggressive gastrointestinal malignancy responsible for more than 90% of pancreatic cancer diagnoses [189]. The cornerstone chemotherapeutic agent used in managing this disease is gemcitabine (2,2-difluoro-2′-deoxycytidine), although it suffers from several clinical limitations due to rapid clearance and inadequate uptake by target cells. A gemcitabine-palmitic acid prodrug was synthesized to increase its retention time in the bloodstream and was called GEMC16. The objective of the present study was to prepare an exosome-liposome hybrid system to improve tumor site-directed delivery. Palmitic acid was bound via an amide bond to gemcitabine, forming GEMC16 with increased lipophilicity characteristics essential for integration into the lipid bilayer of liposomes. A significant advantage of GEMC16 is that it does not depend on the hENT1 transporter for cellular entry. Then, preparation of liposomes comprising GEMC16 (L-GP) was done with DOPC, DSPE-PEG2K, cholesterol, and GEMC16, which ensured stable incorporation due to increased lipophilicity. Exosomes isolated from PANC1 cells using centrifugation and ultracentrifugation techniques were used. Thus, an exosome-liposome hybrid system developed in this study ensured enhanced targeting to the cancer site. A hybrid system was prepared by mixing exosomes with L-GP and then sonicating and extruding the mixture through membranes, termed H-GP. Cellular uptake studies in PANC1 cells showed that H-GP had a 3.1-fold increase in MFI compared to standard L-GP, indicating increased targeting capability. Findings that may unravel the potential of liposome-exosome hybrids in the treatment of PDAC, based on their enhanced capabilities for the improvement of therapeutic efficacy with reduced adverse effects [190].

Microbubble-hybridized exosomes

Ultrasound, a noninvasive clinical imaging technique, is used to get significant information about the soft tissues [191, 192]. Several researchers indicated that ultrasound can influence the secretion of exosomes and enhance the theranostic features of exosomes.

Microbubbles (MBs) are composed of phospholipids, proteins, and polymers filled with perfluorocarbon gas, which are used as ultrasound (US) contrast agents for the identification of several tissues and organs [193]. MBs are also used as suitable carriers for gene and drug delivery. The cavity produced under external irradiation in the US provides a specific area for drug and gene trapping. Besides, MBs collapse or burst under higher pressure and temperature in the blood vessel. After that, the gas released within the bubbles occurred. Consequently, therapeutic agents or other substances loaded in MBs were diffused [194]. Consequently, under the US contrast signal, most of the MBs are degraded after a few minutes, and the release of the cargo occurs. On the other hand, the modification of the surface of the MBs for active targeting seems to be difficult due to their instability. To overcome these limitations, Jang and coworkers designed exosomes fused with MBs (Exo-MBs) and loaded with a photosensitizer, Ce6, as a photodynamic agent with therapeutic effects at the tumor site. The activation of DCs increased the immunotherapeutic effect of Exo-MBs. Consequently, this platform was applied for drug delivery and US imaging. Under external US irradiation, the cavitated Ce6-Exo-MBs were formed and deviated out to the blood vessels. Consequently, Ce6-Exo-MBs could impressively deliver Ce6 with higher therapeutic efficacy through ROS generation [171].

Polymeric nanoparticle-exosome hybrids

The design of hybrid nanoplatforms with polymeric nanoparticles (PNPs) and exosomes offers a practical remedy to the hindrances created by drug delivery systems, which are solely based on exosomes or PNPs. One of the main advantages of this strategy is its ability to prevent the rapid elimination of exosomes, thus improving its therapeutic efficacy by extending its circulation time in the blood. Furthermore, using exosomes prevents the activation of the immunological response against PNPs within the organism. PNPs are synthesized from polymers that can generate nanoparticles measuring between 10 and 1000 nanometers. The suitability of PNPs for drug delivery is guaranteed by their characteristics: biocompatibility, biodegradability, simplicity of fabrication, efficient drug loading, and potential for tailored therapy. The development of hybrid nanoplatforms incorporating PNPs and exosomes provides an effective solution to the challenges posed by DDSs that rely exclusively on either exosomes or PNPs. Poly (lactic-co-glycolic acid) (PLGA), which is an FDA-approved polymer [195], is generally applied in pharmaceutical formulations for sustained release applications. Hemangioma is the most common cancer in children and usually affects females. The current treatment is Hemangeol™ (propranolol), but it has side effects and requires repeated injections [196]. Rapamycin is effective against hemangioma due to its ability to inhibit mTOR by preventing the proliferation of hemangioma endothelial cells and reducing the production of VEGF. Still, it has side effects and is not internally targeted [197]. Different types of rapamycin nanoparticles are developed for the sustained release of rapamycin for hemangioma treatment. To overcome the rapid clearance of rapamycin by the immune system, rapamycin was encapsulated in exosome-mimetic nanoparticles derived from macrophages (RNM) for targeted therapy of hemangiomas [198]. The structural integrity of (PM) proteins for targeted delivery was sustained. Also, integrin α4β1 on the surface of the (PM) of macrophages targeted VCAM-1 of endothelial cells. In the first step, the rapamycin entrapped in exosome-mimicking nanoparticles (RN) was initially produced by means of an extrusion-based manner from a human macrophage cell line (U937 cells). Next, RN was embedded in poly lactic-co-glycolic acid (PLGA) microspheres to form RNM. Actually, PLGA is a crucial polymer used for the prolonged treatment duration of RN for hemangiomas. The potential targeting and release profile of RN and RNM were investigated on hemangioma stem cells (HemSC). The diameter of RN was approximately 100 nm with an efficiency (EE) of 83%. Besides, the particle size of RNM microspheres was about 30 µm, and the EE was 34%. The result indicated that the rapamycin from RNM was released in 40 days. When RN and RNM were expressed in HemSCs, cell proliferation was inhibited, expression of angiogenic factors was reduced, and significant apoptosis was induced. Consequently, RNM is a promising method for the long-term release and effective delivery of rapamycin in hemangiomas [199].

External antigens are captured by antigen-presenting cells (APCs), and their co-expression with primary histocompatibility complex class II molecules causes activation and proliferation of T cells [200]. By modifying the surface roughness of MS with exosomes, their intracellular uptake was increased. A fluorescent dye (coumarin 6) was incorporated into the MS, and intracellular uptake of EXO-coated Dopa MS (EXO-Dopa MS) was investigated by fluorescence microscopy. Increasing the surface roughness of MS by coating with EXO led to the enhancement of their intracellular uptake. Han et al. examined the improvement of polymeric nanoparticles’ effectiveness in drug delivery by applying exosome membranes (EMs) on biological cells. This coating enables nanoparticles to evade immune detection, thus prolonging their circulation time within the body. EMs contain components from both plasma and endosomal membranes [202]. The presence of proteins such as CD55 and CD59 on exosomes protects them from being destroyed by the complement system, allowing for stable circulation in the bloodstream [203]. Moreover, the cell membrane proteins on exosomes improved their targeted delivery to the originating cells. Consequently, nanoparticles coated with exosomes sourced from tumor cells have been utilized for therapeutic applications and targeted delivery. Furthermore, incorporating aptamers as targeting ligands to alter these nanoparticles generates new opportunities for designing drug delivery systems. The AS1411 aptamer, with its G-rich sequence property that targets nucleolin, has been widely reported in various cancers, including lung and breast cancers. The use of particles containing the drug and coated with AS1411 increased tumor targeting and cytotoxicity [204]. As the process of attachment of aptamer to the modified nanoparticles involved complex steps, microfluidic techniques were used for the fabrication and functionalization of nanoparticles based on a core-shell structure. In this study, the platform was synthesized with PLGA as the core and an EM as the shell, conjugated with cholesterol-modified AS1411 aptamers (AS-EP), using microfluidic sonication and surface modification for targeted tumor imaging. Creating these modified nanoparticles, termed AS-EP, was very efficient and took approximately 10 minutes due to a combination of microfluidic sonication and functionalization with lipid-modified aptamers. These aptamer-functionalized biomimetic nanoparticles showed significant potential for drug delivery applications. AS-EP demonstrated a prolonged circulation time in vivo due to the natural properties of exosomes and high tumor-targeting efficiency, achieved through specific binding to nucleolin on tumor cell membranes [205].

As hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) poses significant risks to human health [206], sorafenib has presented new hope for patients with liver cancer [207]. In this regard, Mo et al. discussed a new approach to improve the effectiveness of sorafenib delivery in advanced liver cancer, primarily hepatocellular carcinoma. This research investigated the application of mPEG-PLGA nanoparticles containing the drug sorafenib, subsequently incubated with exosomes obtained from liver cancer cells. The encapsulation process employed pulsed ultrasound to enhance the drug’s targeting efficacy in specific bodily regions. This study estimated the toxic effects of nanoparticles (Exo-Sora-NP) on liver cancer cells and their ability to be taken up by these cells in the tumor tissue. The results showed that nanoparticles encapsulated in exosomes were highly targeted, safe, and exhibited antitumor effects with minimal side effects. These findings suggest that nanoparticles containing drug-enhanced exosomes may be an effective method for liver cancer drug delivery, providing a promising approach to enhance the efficacy of cancer treatment [208].

Periprosthetic osteolysis is the leading cause of failure of total joint prostheses, often requiring surgical intervention [209]. In this regard, Li et al. declared that using exosomes, small vesicles that facilitate cell-to-cell communication, as therapeutic agents can support tissue repair. Specifically, the researchers developed nanoparticles (NPs) made of poly(lactic-co-glycolic) acid (PLGA) to carry exosomes derived from urine-stem cells (USC-Exos) for targeting and treating polyethylene-induced osteolysis in a murine model. In this study, PLGA nanoparticles were fabricated using a mechanical double-emulsion method to encapsulate USC-Exos. Sustained release of exosomes occurred from this platform over 12 days. In vivo studies confirmed that this platform decreased osteolysis compared to USC-Exos alone. Additionally, the results of micro-CT analysis showed that bone mineral density (BMD) and bone volume fraction (BV/TV) in NP-treated mice were improved. Furthermore, histological staining revealed a decrease in osteoclasts in the platform-treated group. Consequently, bone resorption was decreased. Thus, PLGA NPs may be favorable for delivering therapeutic agents to treat periprosthetic osteolysis and potentially improve outcomes after joint replacement [210].

To date, the use of plasmid DNA in treating brain tumors has been limited due to its inability to penetrate the blood-brain barrier. The most promising gene therapy method in treating glioblastoma uses the thymidine kinase gene of the herpes simplex virus. However, systemic delivery of plasmid DNA encoding HSVtk to the brain faces an extraordinary challenge due to pDNA’s inability to penetrate the blood-brain barrier. The challenge has prompted Lee et al. to propose a polymer-based hybrid complex coated with exosome membranes for the targeted delivery of pDNA into glioblastoma cells. It was prepared by the electrostatic interaction between histidine/arginine-linked polyamidoamine and pHSVtk to form the core, enveloped by exosomes derived from C6 cells. The complex was subsequently extruded ten times. Attachment of the T7 peptide to the surface of the complex of pDNA with PHR-EM increased glioblastoma-targeting; hence, the product is designated as pDNA/PHR-EM-T7. The expression of HSVtk and the induction of apoptosis in tumors from the pHSVtk/PHR-EM-T7 group were significantly higher than in the control group. Fluorescence imaging and TUNEL assays demonstrated that this complex enhanced brain permeability and induced apoptosis in tumor cells, with promising results. Using a polymer-plasmid-EM approach may act as an effective inducer for gene expression [211].

Inorganic nanoparticle-exosome hybrid

Gold nanoparticles incorporated into exosomes

Integrating gold nanoparticles with exosomes presents a modern approach involving nanomedicine and biomedical applications. Gold nanoparticles represent unique optical, electronic, and catalytic properties that make them widely used in biomedical applications, including imaging, diagnostics, and therapy. The combination of gold nanoparticles with exosomes increases the efficacy and therapeutic potential of such vesicles.

As an extensively applied photosensitizer in photodynamic therapy (PDT), Ce6 not only generates near-infrared fluorescence for real-time imaging in clinical diagnostic applications but also produces a significant amount of singlet oxygen (1 O₂) to trigger cell apoptosis when subjected to 633 nm laser light [212].

Due to poor biocompatibility and the short half-life time of Ce6, it must be loaded in nanoparticles to control the release rate in the cancer microenvironment. In this regard, Pan et al. coated ultra-small gold nanoparticles (approximately 4–5 nm) with an amphiphilic polymer (PMA) and transferred them into an aqueous solution. A BSA layer was formed on the surface of the gold nanoparticle. Consequently, Ce6 was entrapped in the BSA networks, and PMA/Au-BSA@Ce6 was prepared. Following this, the PMA/Au-BSA@Ce6 nanocomposites were embedded into exosomes derived from gastric cancer patients’ urine using electroporation. Thus, Exo-PMA/Au-BSA@Ce6 nano-vehicles were successfully synthesized [212]. The results showed that their penetration into the cancer cells was increased, phagocytosis by macrophages was reduced, and blood circulation was prolonged due to the role of membrane structure and antigens. The results showed that under 633 nm laser irradiation and acidic conditions, the nanocarrier structure was disrupted, and large PMA/Au-BSA@Ce6 nanoparticles were released into cancer cells, leading to oxygen deprivation and inhibiting tumor growth. In vivo experiments on MGC-803 tumor-bearing nude mice demonstrated that the Exo-PMA/Au-BSA@Ce6 nanocarriers could penetrate deeply into tumor cells and possess high retention within the tumors. This study claimed that this process provided a reliable and non-invasive labeling method for tracking exosomes derived from human urine. With the same idea, Lee et al. explored the development of fluorescent gold nanoclusters (AuNCs) using exosomes as templates, a novel approach for imaging and sensing technology. These light metal nanoclusters have gained attention due to their fluorescence and compatibility with biological systems. The research team has combined two types of exosome-based AuNCs under neutral and alkaline conditions to create nanoclusters exhibiting either blue or red fluorescence emission. This novel process is environmentally friendly, as it does not use hazardous chemicals or employ harsh conditions (133). The study demonstrated, for the first time in the literature, the effective synthesis of AuNCs within exosomes using various analytical methods. AuNCs that emit red light show stronger fluorescence and larger Stokes shifts compared to AuNCs that are blue. Both strains were evaluated for biocompatibility with multiple cancer cell lines, which included ones derived from breast, cervical, and colon cancers. Blue-light-emitting exo-AuNCs showed cytotoxicity at higher concentrations. Additionally, red-light-emitting exo-AuNCs were more compatible and capable of staining cell nuclei, making them suitable for use with other staining dyes in cellular tissue imaging. This investigation represented the first example of using exosomes to create fluorescent nanomaterials for cell imaging. Since almost all cell types naturally produce exosomes, this method offers a cost-effective way to make a variety of nanomaterials for various bioimaging applications, which could support the development of advanced and sustainable imaging technologies [214].

The high density of AuNPs has made them one of the most common nanoparticles in cancer treatment and imaging. However, the major obstacle to their use is precisely delivering these nanoparticles to the tumor cells. Ping et al. developed an exosome-based nanoparticle with AuNPs, having exosomes from macrophages as the outer shell and AuNPs as the inner core. In this work, ultrasonically sensitive hybrid-targeted nanoparticles were composed of AuNPs, exosomes as active carriers, and the S11e aptamer for precise delivery into NSCLC cells. Therefore, AuNPs were synthesized by mixing HAuCl4 and NaBH4 with a CTAB solution and adding ascorbic acid. Next, exosomes from RAW 264.7 cells were mixed with S11e. Finally, AuNP@EXO-S11e was prepared by combining EXO-S11e with GNPs. So far, sonodynamic therapy has demonstrated excellent tissue permeability and much less damage to normal tissues; thus, it presents high promise for cancer treatment. The structured sonodynamic nanomachine demonstrated distinct in vitro and in vivo effects, targeting and preventing the development of NSCLC after ultrasound treatment. This experiment demonstrates the effectiveness of using exosomes as camouflage for nanoparticles in sonodynamic therapy [215].

SDT is a non-invasive deep tumor treatment modality that combines chemical sonosensitizers with low-intensity US. The sonosensitizers inside the US produce reactive oxygen species (ROS) and induce necrosis and apoptosis in tumor cells [216, 217]. Besides, the TME plays a vital role in tumor progression by stimulating macrophage-mediated tumor cell killing [218, 219]. Besides, some research showed that SDT can enhance the reprogramming of TAM. Gold nanoparticles (GNPs) with properties of the acoustic resonance phenomenon can be used to directly affect cancerous cells. Besides, the uncoated GNPs may be eliminated by the mononuclear phagocyte system. So, Tang and colleagues used GNPs as a core, coated them with exosomes derived from macrophages, and decorated them with AS1411 aptamer for tumor-targeting delivery. After entering the lysosomes of the cancer cells, GNP@EXO-Aps accumulated, and a lysosomal-dependent cell death process was initiated with US stimulation. The induction of the US reprogrammed the TME TAMs toward an antitumor phenotype. Therefore, the combined use of GNP@EXO-APs with US irradiation could effectively impede tumor cell proliferation and further reprogram TAMs [220].

Exosomes incorporated with quantum dots (QDs)

Quantum dot-exosome hybrids can improve the stability of QDs and reduce their toxicity.

This approach could lead to a higher concentration of the drug delivery structure at the targeted site while reducing immune system activation. QDs are tiny particles or nanocrystals composed of semiconducting materials, with sizes ranging from 2 to 10 nanometers [221].

QDs indicated greater photostability and strong fluorescence features. QDs are used in fluorescence imaging of animal models. Due to the poor transmission of the NIR therapeutic window (700–1700 nm) through the biological tissue, QDs improve deep-tissue imaging [222]. QDs exhibit electronic and distinctive optical features, including resistance to photobleaching, size-tunable light emission, enhanced signal brightness with photostability, relatively low cost, and the ability to produce multiple fluorescence colors with excellent contrast [223].

Breast cancer remains a global public health problem, accounting for increasing cancer-related deaths among women. In this regard, Tiwari et al. presented a new method to develop the delivery of decarbonize (DC), a chemotherapy prodrug loaded with carbon quantum dots (CQDs) that were covered with exosomes. Exosomes were isolated from breast cancer cells (Ex -DC@CQD). The concept here was to use the natural targeting capability of exosomes, which bind with specific receptors on cancerous cells, thereby acting as an effective drug delivery system. The results indicated that Ex-DC@CQDs significantly facilitated the transport of DC to cancer cells via the HSPG receptor, while increasing ROS generation, inducing loss of mitochondrial membrane potential, and promoting apoptosis. In vivo imaging studies after injection revealed high tumor targeting and superior pharmacokinetic profiling of Ex-DC@CQDs upon comparison with free DC, and thus significant amelioration of therapeutic outcome associated with a diminished side effect [224].

Bladder cancer is a common malignant tumor of the urinary system, the incidence of which is increasing yearly (200). Treatment of non-invasive bladder cancer usually involves electrosurgical resection and postoperative infusion chemotherapy to prevent recurrence, while invasive cases are treated with surgical resection and chemotherapy [225]. Cobalt sulfide quantum dots (CoS QDs) possess both photothermal therapy (PTT) and chemodynamic therapy (CDT) capabilities, yet they encounter challenges in achieving targeted delivery. Exosomes are a suitable career path in drug delivery for bladder cancer. Targeting peptides increases the accuracy of exosome delivery to cancer cells. The study suggested a dual-targeting quantum dot system (PEP/Exo/CoS) for the treatment of bladder cancer, which provided enhanced therapeutic outcomes and decreased non-specific uptake, addressing limitations of previous therapies. An in vitro study using this platform indicated that CoS QDs were entrapped within mesenchymal stem cell-derived exosomes via electrotransfection. A dual-targeting peptide was considered to bind both exosomes and bladder cancer cells, thus promoting targeted delivery. Besides, PEP/Exo/CoS significantly increased reactive oxygen species (ROS) levels in bladder cancer cells while leaving normal epithelial cells unaffected. It also improved apoptosis rates in cancer cells compared to the control groups. Additionally, the PEP/Exo/CoS platform demonstrated remarkable anticancer properties, as evidenced by reduced tumor volume and induced apoptosis in animal models [226]. Actually, after laser irradiation, the temperature of the exosome/CoS QDs group rose to 48°C in comparison with the control group and caused tumor cell death. Furthermore, exosome/CoS QDs kept suitable stability after five laser irradiations. Besides, the mean fluorescence intensity (MFI) of ROS in the PEP/Exo/CoS group was enhanced by 70.11% compared to the control group.

The poor drug-loading capacity and related efficacy of exosomes have limited their applications in therapeutic contexts. To address this challenge, graphene nanosheets, particularly graphene quantum dots, have been utilized to facilitate the loading of both hydrophilic and hydrophobic drugs into the exosomal lipid bilayer by leveraging their two-dimensional structure. Zhang et al. initially synthesized GQDs from carbon nanofibers and then added L-cysteine and D-cysteine to provide chirality to the GQD skeleton. The right-handed chirality of the D-cysteine-modified GQDs enabled higher permeability through the exosomal lipid membrane, resulting in a higher drug loading efficiency. D-Cys-GQDs were mixed with Dox to form a complex of D-Cys-GQD/Dox, which was incubated with 3T3 cell-derived exosomes to obtain D-Cys-GQDs/Dox-loaded exosomes, Exo-Dox. The drug loading efficiency of the exosomes loaded with doxorubicin was determined as 66.7 ± 9.5%. The in vitro uptake study using 3T3 cells revealed the accumulation of Exo-Dox in the nucleus. Further tests were conducted with human hepatocellular carcinoma cells, HepG2, and demonstrated a 44.2 ± 9.2% inhibition of cell proliferation. D-cysteine-GQDs were also utilized for the intracellular delivery of Pygo2 siRNA to DU145 cells, resulting in a 62% reduction in Pygo2 mRNA levels and demonstrating their effectiveness in suppressing prostate cancer via gene therapy. Therefore, this novel approach suggests the potential for cysteine-GQDs to facilitate extensive exosomal drug loading without compromising the integrity of the exosomes [227].

Exosomes incorporated with magnetic nanoparticles

The biocompatible and biodegradable nanoparticles, known as superparamagnetic Fe₃O₄ nanoparticles (FeNPs), can be magnetized by an external magnetic field. When the magnetic force exceeds the blood flow rate, FeNPs can remain at the application site, thereby improving their targeting efficacy. Additionally, these nanoparticles can respond to magnetic fields and undergo micro-deformation, thereby generating mechanical forces on cells [228].

MRI is a critical non-invasive method for tumor diagnosis, especially when high-performance contrast agents based on Fe-, Gd-, and Mn-nanomaterials are used. However, their accumulations in the body elevate the potential toxicity concern. Although renal clearance effectively reduces toxicity, nanoparticles smaller than 6–8 nm usually present poor tumor accumulation. There is an increasing interest in stimuli-responsive nanomaterials whose responses are linked to external triggers (such as light and heat) or internal triggers (such as pH and glutathione). Large nanoparticles may accumulate at tumor sites and decompose into smaller particles for effective elimination.

Another approach was taken by Wei et al., who used exosomes prepared from macrophage cells and complexed them with small iron oxide nanoparticles via electroporation, producing magnetic exosomes that would activate bacterial secretions. Magnetic exosomes may circulate in the bloodstream, accumulate in tumor tissue, and react to the hypoxic environment of malignant tissue. Once bacteria released PFTs, the exosomes were degraded, and the ESIONs were released. These triggers dynamically altered the MRI imaging, highlighting the contrast in tumor tissues. This approach was so sensitive that even the tumor cells as small as 10 mm3 could be depicted. On the contrary, ESIONs have been quickly removed from the body after imaging through the kidneys, resulting in minimized potential toxicity [229].

Carbon dots (CDs) are recognized for their eco-friendliness and solubility, exhibiting potential in cancer theranostics [230]. They can be applied in photothermal therapy (PTT) [231], which leverages the heat sensitivity of tumor cells. Dopamine can be converted into CDs, and polydopamine (PDA) layers develop on the surface of the CDs through self-polymerization. This work applied exosomes and CDs as a multitargeting carrier in imaging-guided cancer treatment. The exosome surface was decorated with the tumor-targeting peptide RGD, which demonstrated a high affinity for integrin αvβ3 overexpressed in tumor cells and enhanced receptor-mediated endocytosis. Then Gd- and Dy-doped (CDs) as imaging and therapeutic agents associated with the nucleus-targeting peptide TAT (CDs:Gd,Dy-TAT) were encapsulated in Exo-RGD. This platform was developed based on photothermal therapy and cancer imaging under the irradiation of the NIR laser. The carrier is designed to effectively accumulate at the tumor site through exosome entrapment and ligand modification. In this platform, Gd and Dy served as imaging agents for MRI and CT, thereby enhancing cancer diagnostic imaging and therapeutic efficacy. Moreover, both in vitro and in vivo studies showed that the modification of targeting facilitated the accumulation of CDs:Gd,Dy-TAT@Exo-RGD at the tumor site. Mice treated with it showed improved therapeutic effects, as their tumor temperatures exceeded 50 °C during NIR irradiation, resulting in significant tumor destruction and 100% survival after 60 days. Moreover, the contrast of MRI/CT imaging of modified exosomes was highly improved compared to the control group. Prostate cancer is the most common malignancy among men, and its primary target in imaging is the prostate-specific membrane antigen. Recent studies involving cancer patients have focused on the potential applications of exosomes in fluorescence molecular imaging and magnetic particle imaging for targeted delivery to tumor sites. Herein, fluorescent dye Cy7 as FMI agent absorbed near-infrared light and transformed it into heat sources as an essential element for photothermal therapy, while superparamagnetic iron oxide nanoparticles (SPIONs) as MPI agents were loaded in the exosome as carriers, generating a dual-modal imaging system named SPIONs@EXO-dye. The current exosome-based platform was utilized here as a theranostic carrier through a combination of magneto-thermal and photothermal treatments. Herein, a system loaded with exosomes was precisely localized at the tumor site by combining magnetic hyperthermia and photothermal therapy for image-guided therapy with considerable promise for targeted therapies in prostate cancer [233].

Tumor cell-derived exosomes facilitate cell communication, thereby creating a supportive environment that promotes tumor progression and metastasis. The study describes a self-labeling technique for observing tumor cell-derived exosomes with an intrinsic MRI reporter. The self-labeling approach described herein enhances the current understanding of exosomes in various pathophysiological contexts and provides insight into tumor formation and its development. Liu et al. used a fusion protein between FTH1 and lactadherin to detect exosomes, where FTH1 served as the MRI reporter and lactadherin as a surface protein on exosomes. To prepare these imaging-labeled exosomes, the FTH1 reporter gene was inserted into B16 cells using FTH1-expressing lentivirus (the proliferation of cells was hindered by adding FTH1), and then exosomes were collected from the B16/FTH1 cell culture medium. The T2-weighted magnetic resonance (T2W MR) was employed to evaluate the in vitro MR imaging capability of the Exosome-B16/FTH1. Indeed, the results indicated that with increased concentration, the negative contrast of the images developed more strongly. This carrier enabled MRI imaging in vivo, as overexpression enhances the paramagnetic content and increases nuclear relaxation rates. This effectively enhances cell-endogenous MRI reporter protein-based imaging, promising a promising future in MRI research [234].

MRI using exosomes is a research domain that still holds promise for therapeutic and diagnostic applications. This can be considered a platform for medical imaging and even targeted therapies. The advantages of this technique are as follows: it is a non-invasive process, has high spatial resolution, and allows for real-time monitoring. However, this technique is countered by limiting factors, such as limited contrast and misinterpretation of the signal.

TNF-α was the first cytokine used for tumor treatment and was found to significantly inhibit the VEGF-induced growth of HUVEC, thereby decreasing tumor angiogenesis [235]. So far, TNF-α has limited clinical applications due to its nonspecific tumor targeting, low in vivo stability, and significant side effects. In the current work, Zhuang and co-authors developed a novel delivery platform that combines TNF-α with exosomes and superparamagnetic iron oxide nanoparticles (SPIONs) to enhance targeting and reduce toxicity. At the first stage, recombinant plasmids bearing cytokine peptide (CPP) and TNF-α sequences were generated to obtain TNF-α-encoding stable MSC cell lines. The exosomes derived from these MSCs expressing TNF-α were conjugated with SPIONs, forming CTNF-α-exosome-SPIONs. The binding of CTNF-α-exosome-SPIONs to the TNF-α receptor TNFR1 occurred, and this facilitated apoptotic pathways, thereby enhancing the therapeutic effects. Hence, the exosome-based delivery platform effectively targeted the tumor tissue under an external magnetic field, reducing tumor growth. Besides, this platform minimized the systemic toxicity of TNF-α [236].

In this regard, Jia et al. developed glioma-targeting nanovesicles for theranostic applications. They loaded SPIONs into the exosomes and conjugated the vesicle membrane with neuropilin-1-targeted peptide using click chemistry. They also loaded curcumin, an antineoplastic and anti-inflammatory agent, into the vesicles. The study revealed that the engineered exosomes could successfully pass the blood-brain barrier and showed a synergistic antitumor effect through SPION-mediated magnetic flow hyperthermia and curcumin delivery. Also, it provided targeted imaging of the glioma [237].

The mixed effect of exosomes with magnetic nanoparticles or quantum dots enhances drug delivery and imaging methodologies. This collaboration can enhance therapeutic effectiveness and improve analytical accuracy. Quantum dots are employed in diagnostics and imaging due to their tunable fluorescence and high-sensitivity detection abilities. Besides, magnetic nanoparticles (MNPs) can be controlled using magnetic fields with high contrast. Besides, the adverse effects of QD can point out prolonged buildup in tissues and metal toxicity. Prolonged retention and inflammatory responses are adverse effects of magnetic nanoparticles.

Exosomes incorporated with carbon dots

So far, the boron complexes used in clinical BNCT have mainly been restricted to BPA and BSH [238]. However, several disadvantages, including poor BBB penetration, rapid metabolic degradation, poor targeting, and associated toxicity, severely limit the therapeutic efficacy of BPA and BSH in glioblastoma treatment. BPA exhibits higher clinical efficiency due to its preferential uptake by tumor cells through the large l-amino acid transport (LAT) systems on the membranes of cancer cells. More recently, newly synthesized CDs derived from d-glucose and l-aspartic acid have been shown to cross the BBB and target glioma tissues specifically via LAT systems and glucose transporters (GLUT), as assessed by fluorescent imaging studies. Li et al. proposed exosomes-encapsulated boron-containing carbon dots for targeting glioma cells. Incubation of the exosomes from RAW264.7 cells with BCDs for two hours increased their delivery and decreased clearance. In vitro studies of U87-MG glioma cells revealed that the cellular intake of BCD-Exosomes was drastically higher, with over 80% localization, which is very promising for BNCT applications. In addition, treatment with BCD-Exosomes in glioma-bearing mice dramatically increased tumor progression, leading to an exceptional 100% survival rate within 30 days. In contrast, the free BCDs-administered group resulted in only a 50% survival rate within 21 days. These data suggest that exosomes may play a key role in future developments of BNCT and the improvement of therapeutic outcomes [239].

Exosomes incorporated with MnCO

Recent evidence has shown that CO interacts with H2O2, leading to significant mitochondrial dysfunction, impairing oxygen binding in hemoglobin, exhibiting antitumor effects, and increasing sensitivity to RT [240–242]. However, the therapeutic use of CO has been limited by issues such as low CO loading levels, rapid leakage, and its inherent toxicity. In this context, Zhu et al. developed an MnCO-exosome hybrid nano-platform, MMV, to enhance targeted low-dose RT in cancer treatment. Its small size makes it difficult for macrophages to phagocytose these nanovesicles, allowing them to penetrate deeply into tumor tissues. MnCO is electroporated into tumor-derived exosomes, achieving a loading efficiency of 31.3%. In vitro experiments showed that when MnCO was delivered to target cells, it reacted with the excess H2O2 in the tumor microenvironment (TME) to produce CO gas, causing acute damage to mitochondria and selectively inducing apoptosis in hypoxic tumor cells without harming normal tissue. Additionally, cell viability after RT irradiation decreased to 5.8% when treated with 50 μg/mL MnCO. An in vivo study in 4T1 mice demonstrated a remarkable 90% cancer suppression rate using MnCO-loaded exosome nanovesicles (MMV) combined with low-dose RT at 2 Gy. This study provides evidence of the emerging potential of exosomes in mediating targeted CO gas therapy [243].

Summary of clinical trials on exosome-based applications in oncology

The translation from laboratory to clinic for exosome-based therapeutics faces multiple challenges, including batch-to-batch consistency and Good Manufacturing Practice (GMP)-compliant processing that controls equipment, personnel, and documentation while maintaining scalable production for cell-derived products [113]. Regulatory organizations worldwide, including the European Medicines Agency (EMA), classify exosomes as biological medicinal products, subjecting them to advanced therapy medicinal products (ATMPs) [245, 246]. The U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) requires Investigational New Drug (IND) submissions for clinical studies that address various aspects, including potency standards and safety data, such as endotoxin levels [247].

Conclusions and future prospects

Exosomes are EVs derived from endosomes and play an important role in cell-cell communication and activation of various signaling pathways. Their role is implicated in several key processes, including tumor invasion, reprogramming of stromal cells, chemoresistance, and regulation of immune response and cell death [248, 249]. Exosomes are membrane vesicles with sizes ranging from 40 to 150 nm, which can be selectively loaded with genes, chemotherapeutic agents, and diagnostic substances. They can be modified with targeting ligands to deliver precise cargo to the tumor microenvironment. Moreover, exosomes may have the capability of acting as cell-free vaccines in the battle against cancer. Unlike liposomes or polymeric nanoparticles, exosomes possess the exclusive property of being internalized into target cells without relying on endosomal pathways, thereby avoiding lysosomal degradation and allowing them to release their cargo directly into the cytoplasm [250]. These entities are a good source for biomarkers because they resemble the originating cells and are suitable for targeted drug delivery [251].

Despite notable progress in exosome biology, including improved purification methods and diverse applications, many hurdles still exist. Producing large amounts of high-purity exosomes is essential for effective drug delivery. However, low purification yields and high costs of extraction present significant challenges. Furthermore, exosomes are diverse, originating from different cellular components, and using unsuitable donor cells may cause immune reactions and serious side effects. The heterogeneity of cell sources continues to impede consistent exosome production. To address this, recent strategies involve using clonally engineered cell lines, such as human embryonic kidney (HEK293) cells or immortalized mesenchymal stem cells, which are designed for stable, uniform exosome output with reduced immunogenicity. Some platforms now incorporate genetic circuits to regulate exosome biogenesis, control secretion rates, or specifically load desired cargo into the intraluminal space [252].

Looking ahead, the field is approaching major breakthroughs. Innovative methods, such as synthetic exosome mimetics, engineered delivery systems like CRISPR and mRNA, AI-driven optimization, and advanced stimuli-responsive technologies, offer solutions to long-standing challenges. Moreover, employing stem cell-derived or genetically standardized producer lines can enhance product consistency and satisfy regulatory standards. As collaboration grows among researchers, clinicians, regulators, and industry stakeholders, exosome-based technologies are gradually shifting from conceptual ideas to practical clinical applications. With continued scientific rigor and translational research, exosomes have the potential to revolutionize targeted, cell-free therapies and propel personalized medicine forward.
