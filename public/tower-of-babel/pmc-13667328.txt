Introduction

Phase contrast magnetic resonance imaging enables the quantitative assessment of blood flow, which is valuable for the clinical evaluation of cardiovascular disease [1, 2]. The velocity encoding limit (VENC) is a phase‐contrast sequence parameter that defines the range of measurable velocities. Maximum flow velocity varies from subject to subject based on patient physiology and the presence of flow jets [3]. According to the most recent consensus statements [4, 5], the choice of VENC should be close to the maximum velocity found in the region of interest: ideally 10% greater than the maximum, and not exceeding 25%. The VENC has a direct impact on the precision of the flow measurements [6] as described by the standard deviation of the velocity (σv) [7] and the Velocity‐to‐Noise Ratio (VNR):

where SNR is the signal‐to‐noise ratio in the region of interest, and v is the measured velocity. If the VENC is set lower than the maximum velocity imaged, phase aliasing results in incorrect flow measurements. If it is too high, measurement precision is reduced which results in the need for a longer scan time [8]. Because velocity precision scales inversely with SNR, VENC selection becomes particularly important on low‐ and mid‐field MRI systems (e.g., 0.55 T) where reduced intrinsic SNR amplifies σv or diminishes VNR.

In current practice, VENC is operator‐dependent and is typically set manually by the MR technologist prior to the flow acquisition. In some centers, a rapid velocity scout sequence is performed to guide VENC selection; however, this workflow requires an experienced cardiac MR technologist. More commonly, a fixed, patient‐invariant high VENC value is used (e.g., 150 or 200 cm/s for the aorta) to ensure adequate coverage across cases [9, 10].

Automated MRI workflows are increasingly being explored to automate exam planning [11] and to enable subject‐specific image quality control [12] during scanning. These approaches could reduce the need for repeat imaging and facilitate non‐expert centers to perform advanced MR exams.

In this study, an autonomous workflow is proposed to enable estimation and prescription of the optimal VENC. The aim was to provide subject‐specific VENC selection without operator interaction and without substantial scan‐time penalty.

Material and Methods

Study Design

Institutional Review Board approval as obtained from the National Institutes of Health (IRB Number 18‐H‐0011, 18‐H‐0118) and written informed consent was obtained from all study participants, after trial registration (ClinicalTrials.gov identifier NCT03331380, NCT03581318). Study participants were eligible if they were 18 years of age or older, had no contraindication to MRI, were not pregnant, and either had no significant medical history (healthy volunteers) or hemodynamically stable disease (patients).

Retrospective Study: Impact of VENC Selection

This proof‐of‐concept study focused on the ascending aorta (AAo). To assess the impact of manual VENC selection, 254 2D Cartesian phase contrast MRI from three MRI systems acquired in 113 patients (47 ± 14 years, 58 female) and 18 healthy volunteers (34 ± 15 years, 10 female) were analyzed. All enrolled subjects underwent a cardiac MRI exam at 1.5 T (MAGNETOM Aera, Siemens Healthineers, Forchheim, Germany) and/or 0.55 T (prototype MAGNETOM Aera). Details of the enrollment by MRI scanner are provided in Table S1. Flow imaging of the ascending and descending aorta was performed on an axial slice at the level of the main pulmonary artery bifurcation during free‐breathing. The following parameters were used for 0.55 T: TE/TR = 4.3/14.1 ms, flip angle (FA) = 30° (1.56–2.2 mm)2 resolution, 6 mm slice thickness, through plane velocity encoding, FOV = 270 × 360 mm2, 3 averages, and at 1.5 T: TE/TR = 2.7/10.0 ms, FA = 20° (1.40–1.56 mm)2 resolution, 6 mm slice thickness, through plane velocity encoding, FOV = 270 × 360 mm2, 3 averages. The default VENC was chosen to be high enough to avoid aliasing in the majority of cases (200 cm/s), although the operator could modify the VENC as desired. Retrospective analysis was performed to compare the selected VENC with the maximum measured velocity (Vmax) in the ascending aorta (AAo) using the ratio VENC:Vmax. According to recent consensus statements, the ratio VENC:Vmax should fall within the interval [1.1, 1.25] [4, 5].

Inline Image Processing

The automatic VENC calibration relies on the performance of the vessel segmentation and phase unwrapping algorithms. Automatic segmentation of the AAo was performed using a trained 2D nnUnet previously published [12] which provided accurate segmentation with a mean Dice Similarity Coefficient of 0.95 ± 0.02 on the test cohort.

For phase unwrapping, three state‐of‐the‐art open‐source algorithms (Laplacian unwrapping [16], graph‐cut unwrapping [17] and non‐continuous path with reliability sorting, SRNCP [18]) were assessed on synthetic data. Two types of aliasing artifacts were generated on the retrospective dataset for each 2D non‐aliased phase contrast: (1) constant offset: an offset (ϕoffset) in the ascending aorta was added on all cardiac phases; (2) multiplication factor: a factor (α) was used to multiply the phase (ϕ) in the ascending aorta. In both cases, the generated aliasing region represented 10% of the vessel area at peak flow.

Algorithm performance was compared on both the original and synthetic phase images. The number of total failure cases (Φunwrapped−Φmax>εwithε=0.01) and number of underestimation cases (Φunwrapped−Φmax<εwithε=0.01) was measured. The computation time was also calculated to ensure that the selected algorithm could be deployed inline.

Prospective Application in Healthy Volunteers and Patients

Ten healthy volunteers and five patients with suspected abnormal aortic flow were prospectively imaged on a 0.55 T MRI scanner (prototype MAGNETOM Free.Max, Siemens Healthineers, Forchheim, Germany) with prototype high‐performance gradients (peak gradient amplitude = 33 mT/m, peak gradient slew = 160 T/m/s). The vendor body array and the spine array coils, totaling up to 21 channels, were used in this study.

The same free‐breathing Cartesian gradient echo phase‐contrast sequence was used for both VENC scout and flow measurement (scout: TE/TR = 2.7/9.9 ms, FA = 15°, 1.8 mm2 resolution, VENC = 150 cm/s, GRAPPA 2, FOV = 260 × 350 mm2, averages = 2, acquisition time = 30 s; free‐breathing flow measurement: TE/TR = 4/12.2 ms, FA = 15°, 2.3 mm2 resolution, VENC = optimal VENC, GRAPPA 2, FOV = 300 × 300 mm2, averages = 6, acquisition time = 2 min 20 s, chosen to be long enough to ensure precise flow measurement). The sequence was modified to (1) listen to the feedback message, (2) store the optimal VENC calculated by the image reconstruction calibration analysis, and (3) check, read, and apply the optimal VENC if available.

In five healthy subjects, an additional scout with VENC = 75 cm/s was acquired to test the performance of the phase unwrapping on the estimation of the optimal VENC. Two free‐breathing flow measurements with commonly used patient‐invariant VENCs (150 and 200 cm/s) were additionally acquired.

Rapid inline image reconstruction and processing were performed using Gadgetron on a computer equipped with four GPUs (2× NVIDIA RTX A6000 48 GB and NVIDIA A100‐SXM, 80 GB) and 128 CPUs cores (2× AMD EPYC 7763 64‐core processors).

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted using R (version 4.3.0). The metrics' distribution normality was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test.

For the retrospective analysis, the VENC:Vmax ratio was compared with consensus recommendations. Equivalence was assessed using a nonparametric two‐sided bootstrap with a 90% confidence interval due to deviation from normality.

For the prospective study, Wilcoxon signed rank or paired t‐test was applied, depending on the normality of the distribution, to compare the VENC:Vmax ratio between the autonomous VENC selection and patient‐invariant VENC.

p‐values for the pairwise comparisons were adjusted using the Holm method. A p value < 0.05 were considered significant.

Results

Retrospective Study: Impact of VENC Calibration

Table S2 summarizes the study population included in the retrospective evaluation of the VENC calibration. The retrospective analysis of the phase contrast database showed that only 5.5% of the prescribed VENCs had been modified from the default value of 200 cm/s, with 4 cases at 100 cm/s, 8 cases at 150 cm/s, and 2 cases at 300 cm/s. Across all scans, the ratio of VENC:Vmax was 1.97 ± 0.50 for the AAo (Vmax = 99.8 ± 23.68 cm/s). In 96.1% of cases, the ratio VENC:Vmax was higher than recommended by consensus statements (i.e., VENC:Vmax > 1.25) and 0.4% of the cases contained aliasing artifact (i.e., VENC:Vmax < 1). Consistent with this observation, the nonparametric bootstrap did not demonstrate equivalence at the population level. The bootstrap 90% confidence interval for the mean was [2.03, 2.13], which was outside of the equivalence bounds [1.1, 1.25]. Figure 2 provides a histogram of VENC:Vmax and example images from a well‐prescribed VENC and one which is too high. Overall, the use of a VENC that was too high resulted in a velocity standard deviation 78% ± 45% higher than what would be achieved with optimal theoretical VENC:Vmax = 1.10, meaning there was a significant loss in measurement precision.

Across all types of aliasing tested, the Laplacian method had the smallest number of failure cases compared to SRNCP method and graph‐cut method. Autonomous VENC Selection Workflow

Table 2 summarizes the study population included in the prospective evaluation of the automatic VENC selection method, and participant‐level data for all 15 individuals are provided in Table S3. Automatic inline estimation of the optimal VENC was successful for phase contrast imaging for the entire prospective population using the scout with VENC 150 cm/s (Figure 4), including in the patients with abnormal flow patterns. Computation of optimal VENC required 6 s (Supporting Information) on the scanner. The optimal VENC calculated (VENCoptimal = 124.87 ± 28.9 cm/s) was strictly higher than the maximum velocity of the subsequent flow measurement (Vmax = 105.6 ± 25.7 cm/s). The ratio VENCoptimal:Vmax was 1.19 ± 0.10, and was significantly lower compared to subject‐invariant VENC (VENC150:Vmax = 1.49 ± 0.31, VENC200:Vmax = 1.99 ± 0.41) (Figure 5). Thus, 73% of the subsequent flow measurements following automatic VENC calibration were within the recommended VENC guidelines (VENC:Vmax ratio of 1.0–1.25) as opposed to 7%/13% using subject‐invariant VENC 150/200 cm/s. After automatic calibration, none of the flow measurements contained aliasing whereas 13% of subject‐invariant VENC 150 cm/s contained aliasing and may require a repeat flow measurement.

Analysis of Precision and Scan Time

Compared to a fixed VENC of 150 and 200 cm/s, the automatic VENC decreased the standard deviation of the velocity (σv) across the prospective population by 16.8% ± 19.3% and 37.6% ± 14.5%, respectively (Figure 7A). Lower σv equates to improved measurements precision, which can be traded for shorter scan time. In other words, to obtain an equivalent σv to the automatic VENC, it would have been necessary to acquire data for longer using default patient‐invariant VENC settings. Compared to VENC of 150 cm/s, scan duration could be reduced by approximately 5 s or 1 min for a breath hold scan or free‐breathing scan, respectively. The Cartesian acquisition time is enforced by the number of averages, so for example, if 6 averages are required for VENC = 200 cm/s, only 2 averages would be required with VENCoptimal = 111 cm/s (Figure 7B), resulting in a scan time that is 1/3 of the original time.

Evaluating the retrospective cohort of 254 scans, if an ideal VENC had been selected (ratio VENCideal:Vmax = 1.1), the scan time could be reduced by 66.9% ± 17.6% to achieve this same level of precision. Considering that a free breathing scan is 2 min 30 s at 0.55 T, it would have been possible to reduce the scan duration to 50 ± 26 s scan instead. That is a time savings of 7 h 4 min over the 254 scans.

Discussion

This proof‐of‐concept study demonstrated the feasibility of an autonomous and inline selection of subject‐specific VENCs for phase‐contrast MRI. In the prospective cohort, this autonomous approach selected VENC values closer to recommended VENC‐to‐peak‐velocity ratios (110%–125% Vmax) than routinely used fixed prescriptions, while avoiding aliasing in the subsequent flow acquisitions. These findings suggest that patient‐specific VENC selection may improve the efficiency of phase‐contrast imaging and reduce the errors in quantitative cardiac MRI.

Although recommendations for VENC prescription are well established [4, 8, 19], evidence describing routine clinical practice of performing a peak velocity “scout” followed by the quantitative acquisition remains limited. For example, UK Biobank CMR protocol [20] used a fixed VENC of 200 cm/s with upward adjustment when turbulence is identified whereas another multicenter study protocol [21] relied on operator‐selected VENC with values ranging from 80% to 200% of Vmax. Assessment of real‐world VENC selection may therefore be valuable to determine how closely current practice aligns with these recommendations. The single‐center retrospective analysis showed that VENC prescription was rarely individualized in routine clinical practice and was frequently higher than recommended, with a 90% confidence interval for the VENC of 203%–213% Vmax. In the prospective cohort, the automatically selected VENC values were consistently lower than standard subject‐invariant prescriptions, yet remained above the measured peak velocity in all cases. These findings suggest that the proposed autonomous workflow was able to maintain a practical balance between avoiding aliasing and preserving velocity precision, in closer agreement with recent consensus recommendations.

Another potential implication of this work is reduced operator dependence. In routine clinical practice, VENC is often prescribed conservatively, particularly when the expected peak velocity is uncertain. An automated workflow may help standardize this step and reduce variability related to user experience. This concept is consistent with prior developments in cardiac MRI workflow automation, including automated cardiac plane prescription [22] and fully automated inversion‐time selection for late gadolinium enhancement imaging [23], which have been proposed to reduce operator dependence, simplify examination planning, and improve reproducibility of image acquisition. Similar benefits may be expected from autonomous VENC selection in centers with limited expertise in quantitative cardiac MRI, although this will require validation in broader clinical settings.

The effect of VENC on phase‐contrast precision is well established theoretically and supported by prior experimental studies [6, 24, 25]. Because the precision depends on the relationship between velocity range and noise, reducing VENC overestimation may also provide greater flexibility in protocol design. For example, improved VENC prescription could translate into shorter acquisitions while maintaining equivalent precision. This may be especially valuable in examinations requiring multiple flow measurements, such as interventional cardiac MRI [26] or complex congenital heart disease [27], where cumulative inefficiencies become more consequential. In this sense, automated VENC selection may be viewed not only as a calibration tool, but also as a mechanism for improving overall protocol efficiency and patient comfort.

Autonomous VENC selection may be especially relevant for real‐time phase contrast MRI, where accelerated acquisitions are inherently more noise limited. Prior work [28] has shown that halving VENC in real‐time flow MRI can double VNR after phase unwrapping, suggesting that subject specific VENC selection may improve velocity precision while avoiding the scan time penalties associated with dual‐ or multi VENC approaches [29, 30].

Limitations

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, both the VENC scout and the flow measurement were acquired using Cartesian imaging. As a result, the achievable scan‐time reduction was constrained by the discrete number of averages used in the sequence. More flexible sampling strategies, such as golden angle non‐Cartesian approaches, may allow finer adjustment of acquisition time and could further improve the efficiency of subject‐specific VENC prescription. Non‐Cartesian approaches [31, 32] could also be used to shorten the acquisition of the VENC scout. Second, estimation of the optimal VENC relies on phase‐unwrapping to recover velocities above the VENC scout. Although the selected unwrapping method performed robustly overall, failure of phase unwrapping in one case resulted in a suboptimal VENC estimate, indicating that this step remains a potential source of error. Alternative approaches, such as multi‐VENC [29] scouting, may improve robustness, but would likely increase calibration time. Third, the current implementation assumes a predefined target vessel and does not yet include automated slice prescription [33] or automatic identification of the vascular territory to be measured. Extending the framework to include these steps would further increase automation and may improve clinical usability. Fourth, the free‐breathing phase‐contrast acquisitions were respiratory averaged; therefore, reducing the scan time may affect not only noise behavior but also the degree of respiratory motion averaging, which was not separately assessed in the present study. Fifth, the scout was acquired at higher spatial resolution than the subsequent flow scan. Although this facilitated robust inline velocity estimation, it may also have contributed to differences between scout‐derived and subsequently measured peak velocities [34]. Sixth, the retrospective cohort was not designed as a formal test–retest study. In patients with repeated examinations, the interval between scans and the presence of potential physiological changes were not controlled, such that variation in estimated optimal VENC may reflect true hemodynamic differences rather than methodological variability. Accordingly, this dataset is informative regarding routine clinical prescribing behavior, but does not allow reliable assessment of within‐patient reproducibility or generalizability across scanners and field strengths. Dedicated prospective repeatability studies will be needed to address these questions. Finally, this was a single‐center study with a relatively small prospective cohort including healthy volunteers and a limited number of patients with abnormal flow. Therefore, the present results should be interpreted as an initial feasibility demonstration rather than definitive evidence of clinical benefit. Further evaluation in larger and more diverse patient cohorts, including populations with valvular disease and more complex hemodynamics, will be needed to assess the robustness and generalizability of the method.

Conclusions

The proof of concept of inline automatic VENC selection demonstrates accurate selection of this subject‐specific parameter without any operator interaction. By reducing unnecessary VENC overestimation, this approach may improve standardization by ensuring precise flow measurements, reducing operator dependence, and increasing workflow efficiency. Further studies in larger clinical populations will be required to establish its robustness and clinical impact.
