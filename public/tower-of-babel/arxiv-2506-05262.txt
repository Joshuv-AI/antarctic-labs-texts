Tuning Shear Rheology through Active Dopants
Amir Shee, Ritwik Bandyopadhyay, Haicen Yue

Abstract
We numerically investigate the shear rheology of mixtures of active and passive Brownian particles, with varying fractions of active components. We find that even a small fraction of active dopants triggers fluidization with comparable efficiency to fully active systems. A combined parameter, active energy, given by dopant fraction multiplied by propulsion speed squared controls the shear rheology and glass transition of the active-passive mixtures. These results together provide a quantitative strategy for fine-tuning the mechanical properties of a soft material with small amounts of active dopants.

Aiming to develop a general understanding of fluid-glass-jamming transitions in amorphous materials, the role of activity, as another source of energy to overcome local energy barriers, has also been compared to temperature

Previous work has shown that small amounts of active particles can induce motility-driven phase separation

However, the quantitative role of various fractions of active doping in triggering the solid-to-fluid transition remains an open question.

Here, we investigate how small-amount active doping controls the glass‑to‑fluid transition. We identify “active energy”, given by the dopant fraction multiplied by propulsion speed squared as the control parameter of transition. Remarkably, even a small dopant fraction (well below 50 percent) suffices to fluidize systems with comparable efficiency to fully active systems, revealing the non-negligible role of even a minimal amount of active components and suggesting a strategy to finetune soft materials’ properties with them.

Together, these results underscore the role of active energy

In summary, we have demonstrated that the glass transition density
\phi_{g}
in active-passive mixtures exhibits a highly nonlinear dependence on the active fraction, with a more pronounced effect at low active fractions. This underscores the substantial impact that even a small number of active components can have on the mechanical properties of mixed soft materials, such as soils containing a small population of living organisms, or extracellular matrices embedded with a few living cells. This finding also suggests a cost-effective strategy for fine-tuning the mechanical response of soft materials through minimal active doping. We also show that the shear rheology of active-passive mixtures is governed by a combined parameter
\alpha\mathrm{Pe}^{2}
, which quantifies the total active energy input. This combined parameter carries both theoretical and practical significance. Theoretically, it points to a natural direction for extending existing active glass theories to include an additional variable – the active fraction
\alpha
– in systems composed of both active and passive components. The “mean-field” approach, such as in the active random first-order transition (RFOT) theory proposed in
Nandi et al. 2018
, may still apply by substituting the active strength
f_{0}
in fully active systems with an effective “average” activity
\sqrt{\alpha f_{0}^{2}}
in mixtures. This substitution is reasonable in our systems, where active particles are uniformly distributed. If this uniformity is broken, as is often the case in many real mixed systems where active components aggregate or form structures, the situation becomes more complex and requires further investigation. Practically, a systematic scanning in the parameter space of at least four dimensions (
\phi
,
\alpha
,
\mathrm{Pe}
,
\dot{\gamma}
) is computationally expensive. By collapsing the influence of
\alpha
and
\mathrm{Pe}
into a single combined parameter, the dimensionality of the problem is reduced. This simplification allows for a more efficient and detailed exploration and prediction of the rheology of active-passive mixtures. For example, it is reported in
Wiese et al. 2023
that a fully active system with
\mathrm{Pe}=3
at
\phi=0.66
has a yield stress of
\sigma_{Y}\approx 3.49\times 10^{-4}
. According to our findings, this corresponds to an active energy
\alpha\mathrm{Pe}^{2}=9
, and should be equivalent to any mixed system with different combinations of
\alpha
and
\mathrm{Pe}
that yield the same active energy. This can be cross-validated using our fitted empirical form (Eq. (
4
)) for the
\phi=0.66
case
\sigma_{Y}(9)=\sigma_{0p}/\left[1+\left(9/(\alpha\mathrm{Pe}^{2})_{G}\right)^{3}\right]\approx 3.47\times 10^{-4}
, which closely matches the reported value. Thus, with a detailed characterization of the rheology in fully active systems, it becomes possible to predict the rheological behavior of mixed systems or to select appropriate combinations of
\alpha
and
\mathrm{Pe}
for active dopants to achieve desired rheological properties, without the need for additional simulations.

As the first systematic study on the glass–fluid rheology of active–passive mixtures, this work remains idealized, relying on simplified assumptions such as frictionless and spherical particles, negligible hydrodynamic interactions, equal sizes for active and passive particles, and uniformly distributed and isotropically directed active dopants. Future applications in specific systems will require more nuanced models that relax some of these assumptions to better capture system-specific complexities. In particular, the non-uniform distribution of active dopants, accounting for the complex structures or organization of active components, is of special interest. Theoretically, the potential interplay between such spatial heterogeneity and the intrinsic dynamical heterogeneity of glassy systems presents a compelling direction for future investigation. Additionally, size disparities between active and passive particles, which affect static structure and contact geometry, also raise important theoretical questions.

In the active‐glassy regime, stress fluctuations result from a mix of thermally activated hops and activity‐driven, localized rearrangements, all confined by the amorphous energy landscape. Consequently: 1) Symmetric fluctuations
(\mathcal{S}_{\sigma}\approx 0)
: Escape events whether via Brownian motion or an active “kick” produce stress increments and decrements with nearly equal probability, yielding a symmetric distribution. 2) Suppressed extremes (
\mathcal{K}_{\sigma}<0
): Finite energy barriers and elastic constraints limit large‐amplitude stress excursions. This truncation of the tails makes the distribution platykurtic (flatter than Gaussian), i.e. excess kurtosis below zero.

Even in the presence of activity, the glassy network’s structural constraints enforce near‐zero skewness and negative excess kurtosis, indicating symmetric, tightly bounded shear stress fluctuations.
