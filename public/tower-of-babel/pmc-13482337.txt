Preserved and non-preserved social attentional processes in Autism and developmental language disorder at early ages
Authors: Adoración Antolí, Julia Vacas, Araceli Sánchez-Raya, Carolina Pérez-Dueñas, José Juan Cañas, Fátima Cuadrado
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Differentiating between Autism Spectrum Disorder (ASD) and Developmental Language Disorder (DLD) in early childhood, when language and behavioral repertoires are not yet fully developed, remains a major challenge in clinical practice. This study aimed to examine which aspects of social attention are preserved or altered in each condition, using eye-tracking. We analyzed how children explored faces and objects in three groups: children with ASD, children with DLD, and non-clinical children. A paired-preference task was used in which children viewed images with one face and one object. Variables included stimulus type (face vs. object), object interest (related vs. unrelated to autistic circumscribed interests), and facial emotional expression (neutral, happy, angry). The sample included 69 children aged 32 to 74 months (23 per group). Eye-tracking measures captured how quickly children oriented to each stimulus (time to first fixation; prioritization index), how long they looked at it (total fixation duration; preference index), and how sustained their visual engagement was (visit duration; duration index). The ASD group showed preserved orientation toward social stimuli, with no differences relative to the non-clinical group. The ASD and DLD groups differed in the duration index, indicating stronger visual engagement with objects in the ASD group. Both prioritization and preference indices differentiated between clinical and non-clinical groups. These findings characterize how children in each group attend to social and non-social information, highlighting distinct patterns that may inform early differential diagnosis and clinical assessment.

Introduction

Neurodevelopmental disorders are defined as a group of conditions with onset during the early developmental period, typically before school age1. They are characterized by deficits in neurodevelopmental processes that produce significant impairments in personal, social, academic, or occupational functioning1,2.

According to the DSM-5-TR, neurodevelopmental disorders frequently co-occur with one another and with other emotional and behavioral conditions in childhood1. Symptoms may manifest as behaviors that are more frequent or more severe than expected for the child’s age and sex and/or as delays in developmental milestones. Dimensional approaches to symptom assessment indicate that these features vary along a continuum of severity, without a clear boundary between typical and atypical development, which contributes to phenotypic overlap across conditions1. These factors complicate differential diagnosis, particularly given the high rates of comorbidity and overlapping clinical presentations3,4.

In this context, further examination of specific neurodevelopmental disorders is warranted, particularly those that pose challenges for differential diagnosis in early developmental stages, such as Autism Spectrum Disorder (ASD) and Developmental Language Disorder (DLD; previously referred to as Specific Language Impairment or Language Disorder)5,6.

ASD is a neurodevelopmental disorder characterized by persistent difficulties in social communication and interaction, as well as restricted and repetitive patterns of behavior, interests, or activities1,3. In contrast, DLD is clinically defined by persistent difficulties in language acquisition and/or use of language across modalities (i.e., spoken, written, or signed), affecting vocabulary, grammar, and discourse. These difficulties are substantially below age expectations and have a significant impact on daily functioning1–3,7–9.

The DSM-5-TR classifies ASD and DLD as distinct neurodevelopmental disorders. However, despite differences in core clinical features (i.e., restricted and repetitive patterns of behavior, interests, or activities), both conditions may present with language difficulties, particularly during early developmental stages when language skills are still emerging and behavioral manifestations are less differentiated. This overlap contributes to challenges in differential diagnosis. Individuals with ASD typically present with difficulties in pragmatic language, whereas impairments in structural language (i.e., phonology, morphology, and syntax) are a defining feature of DLD. Although these differences are more readily identifiable in older children and adolescents, they are less evident in early development, when diagnostic evaluation is often conducted as early as 2–3 years of age6. Moreover, both conditions are characterized by substantial heterogeneity, and linguistic profiles may overlap across groups, as widely reported in the literature10–16.

In addition, social difficulties have been documented in both populations10–14, and some studies have reported the presence of ASD-related features in individuals with DLD12,13. These findings indicate that the boundaries between these conditions may be less clearly defined during early developmental stages14–19.

In this context, some authors have noted that a substantial proportion of individuals do not conform to prototypical clinical presentations, instead showing mixed profiles10,17,19,20. These cases pose particular challenges for assessment, diagnosis, and intervention10, and are often associated with delays in the initiation of intervention. Such delays are clinically relevant given the extensive evidence supporting the effectiveness of early intervention21–25.

These considerations have led to calls for an increased number of comparative studies, with a particular emphasis on improving differential diagnosis7,17,26–28.

Social attention in children with ASD or DLD. Studies with eye-tracking

Given the challenges in distinguishing these conditions based on language and behavioral profiles at early ages, the examination of socio-cognitive domains may inform differential diagnosis. In particular, research has focused on social attention (SA), defined as the preferential allocation of visual attention to socially relevant stimuli relative to non-social stimuli29,30. In recent decades, the use of eye-tracking methodology has advanced the study of SA by enabling the quantification of visual attention patterns. Eye-tracking provides objective measures of attention allocation, including fixation duration, proportion of looking time, number of fixations, and latency to first fixation31–36.

Eye-tracking studies of SA in children with ASD have consistently reported reduced attention to social stimuli, as reflected in shorter fixation durations, a lower proportion of looking time, fewer fixations, and longer latencies to first fixation on social stimuli, together with a relative attentional bias toward non-social elements27,35,37,38,30,39–42. In addition, atypical attentional patterns have been observed in infancy, including longer fixation durations on objects and increased latency to disengage attention and orient toward social stimuli35,43. The analysis of visual scanning pattern (VSP), defined as the sequence of gaze shifts, fixations, and spatial locations during visual exploration, has further shown that individuals with ASD exhibit more dispersed and less structured attention to social content, characterized by greater spatial dispersion of fixations and increased variability in scanpaths44.

Faces constitute a primary source of social information, providing cues for emotion perception, recognition, and interpretation45,46. Accordingly, they have been extensively studied in research on SA. Evidence suggests that attention to faces is a natural mechanism which emerges early in development and supports the specialization of neural systems involved in social processing46–49. In contrast, children with ASD show reduced attention to faces27,42,50–52. However, some studies indicate that certain aspects of emotional reactivity may be relatively preserved under specific conditions, particularly at a basic or physiological level (i.e., automatic responses to emotional expressions or discrimination of affective cues), despite difficulties in using this information to guide social behavior27,51,53,54. For example, Jones et al. reported atypical face processing in two-year-old children with ASD, characterized by a reduced proportion of looking time to the eye region and increased attention to other facial regions, such as the mouth55. This pattern has led to the hypothesis that reduced attention to socially informative regions of the face may limit social learning opportunities from early infancy, with potential downstream effects on social development and neural specialization43,46,49,55–58.

Beyond its linguistic profile, DLD has also been associated with difficulties in the use of social-pragmatic cues and the integration of social information during communication. Several studies have shown that children with DLD may experience challenges in using and integrating social-pragmatic cues, such as contextual information and communicative intent, which are critical for language learning and comprehension10,11,8,59. These studies suggest that the processing of socially relevant information may be less efficient in children with DLD, providing a rationale for examining SA in this population. However, empirical evidence in this area remains limited, as most eye-tracking studies in DLD have focused on narrative processing (expressive or receptive) using visual prompts60,61, as well as word recognition and prediction62,63. Two studies have examined SA from a comparative perspective64,65, reporting similar VSP in children with DLD and their non-clinical peers when viewing dynamic and ecologically valid stimuli. This VSP was characterized by socially oriented and focused attention. In the same studies, children with ASD showed atypical gaze behavior, including reduced attention to faces, specifically to the eye region, as well as a bias toward non-social elements66,67. In addition, both DLD and non-clinical groups showed appropriate gaze shifts when discourse was introduced (i.e., alternating gaze toward the relevant speaker), while children with ASD did not consistently follow the speakers with their gaze68.

Despite these similarities, findings of both studies diverge regarding attention to the mouth region in children with DLD. Hosozawa et al. reported increased attention to the mouth, whereas Hanley et al. did not find statistically significant differences68,67. This pattern has been interpreted as a potential compensatory mechanism supporting language processing in children with DLD. However, to date, no comparative study have directly examined attention to faces versus objects or the role of facial emotional content across these populations.

The role of competing objects in social attention

The influence of the saliency of competing non-social objects on visual attention in children with ASD is an important factor to consider when assessing SA in this population43,57,58. Several studies have reported a restricted attentional bias toward non-social stimuli in children with ASD, which may be associated with the cognitive mechanisms underlying restricted and repetitive behaviors, particularly the circumscribed interests (CI)57,58,69. A substantial body of research has examined how non-social objects compete with social stimuli for attentional resources in ASD. For example, Sasson et al. reported restricted visual attention to CI-related objects (CIOs) in school-aged children with ASD43. This pattern was not observed for non-CI-related objects (non-CIOs) or social stimuli. Similar findings have been reported in preschool-aged children with ASD57. In these studies, VSP was characterized as circumscribed, perseverative, and detail oriented.

Using a paired-preference paradigm, Sasson and Touchstone further examined SA in preschool-aged children with ASD by presenting faces displaying different emotional expressions (i.e., happiness, sadness, fear, anger, and neutral expressions) alongside CIOs and non-CIOs58. The authors reported that children with ASD showed a typical preference for faces when these were paired with non-CIOs; however, this preference was reduced when faces were paired with CIOs.

In addition, using a similar paradigm, Vacas et al. have reported comparable VSP between ASD and non-clinical groups when faces were paired with non-CIOs, characterized by faster prioritization, greater preference, and longer visit duration (i.e., the time between gaze entry into and exit from the area of interest). However, children with ASD showed reduced overall attention to faces across all conditions70.

Given that CI are a hallmark of ASD, and this pattern of restricted visual attention appears to be specific to this population, the saliency of competing non-social stimuli may differentially affect SA in children with ASD compared to those with DLD.

Aims and hypotheses

Previous eye-tracking research on SA suggests that children with ASD and those with DLD may exhibit distinct VSP, contributing to the characterization of these conditions. In addition, the influence of CIOs on attention to faces in children with ASD may be relevant for differential diagnosis, particularly in mixed cases where autistic features cooccur with language difficulties. Given the limited number of studies examining VSP in children with DLD, further investigation is needed to characterize their visual attention to social and non-social stimuli. Accordingly, the aim of the present study was to compare attention to faces and objects in young children with ASD, DLD, and non-clinical peers, while examining the effects of facial emotional expressions and competing non-social stimuli on VSP.

To this end, an eye-tracking paired preference task was used in which social stimuli (faces displaying happy, angry, or neutral expressions) were presented alongside non-social ones (CIOs or non-CIOs).

Based on previous findings, it was hypothesized that young children with ASD would show significantly reduced attention to faces, particularly when faces were paired with CIOs (H1). No specific directional hypothesis was formulated for children with DLD due to the limited available evidence. In addition, the effect of emotional expression on SA was examined across groups. It was hypothesized that emotional faces (happy and angry) would elicit different visual processing compared to neutral faces in all groups (H2). In particular, angry faces were expected to show increased salience, in line with evidence of emotional sensitivity in children with ASD27,51,53,54 and prior findings on the preferential processing of threat-related stimuli71. Finally, based on results from58, it was hypothesized that the type of object would differentially affect attention to faces in children with ASD (H3), such that CIOs would be associated with reduced attention to faces, whereas non-CIOs would not. Given the lack of prior research in children with DLD, the influence of CIOs in this group was examined in an exploratory manner.

These hypotheses guided the planned analyses, including comparisons between groups and experimental conditions following significant interaction effects, to identify specific differences in attentional patterns.

Methods

Participants

Sixty-nine children completed the eye-tracking paired-preference task: 23 with ASD (age range = 32–73 months), 23 with DLD (age range = 32–72 months), and 23 non-clinical (age range = 39–74 months). An a priori sensitivity analysis conducted in G*Power (version 3.1.9.7) indicated that a minimum sample of 54 participants was required to detect between-group differences with 95% power and an effect size of 0.25. The final sample exceeded this threshold.

Participants in the clinical groups were recruited from Early Childhood Intervention (ECI) centers in Córdoba (Spain). Inclusion criteria for both groups were: (1) a prior clinical diagnosis of ASD or DLD established according to DSM-5/ICD-11 criteria following the standardized diagnostic protocol implemented by the Infant Mental Health Unit within the public health system; (2) regular attendance at an ECI center; and (3) absence of additional mental or medical conditions.

As part of this clinical protocol, children who screened positive on the Modified Checklist for Autism in Toddlers (M-CHAT)72 underwent a comprehensive multidisciplinary assessment conducted by experienced licensed clinicians. This assessment included the Autism Diagnostic Observation Schedule, Second Edition (ADOS-2)73, as well as standardized developmental measures such as the Battelle Developmental Inventory, Second Edition (BDI-II)74 and the Bayley Scales of Infant Development, Third Edition (Bayley-III)75. Children who screened negative on the M-Chat72 were assessed using developmental measures only. Inclusion in the ASD group was based on a prior clinical diagnosis supported by this standardized assessment process, which routinely includes ADOS-2 administration. Children in the DLD group were identified based on persistent language impairments, as reflected in low performance on language subscales of the developmental assessments. In addition, all participants completed the Peabody Picture Vocabulary Test, Third Edition (PPVT-III)76 to obtain a standardized measure of receptive vocabulary across groups.

Children in the non-clinical group were recruited from preschool classrooms in a public school in Córdoba (Spain). Inclusion required no history of developmental concerns, as reported by parents in the informed consent form. Children with reported concerns or prior diagnoses were excluded from participation. This group was matched to the clinical groups on chronological age and gender. Following previous research, matching was conducted on chronological age rather than mental age, given the low cognitive demands of the task, as passive-observational paradigms do not require complex cognitive processing27,58. Gender matching was also implemented to control for its potential effect on attentional preferences toward CIOs58,77–79.

Despite matching on chronological age and gender, group differences were observed in basic attention (defined as the proportion of valid gaze samples within the screen area relative to total recording time, as recorded by the eye-tracker), and receptive vocabulary (PPVT-III standard scores)76(see Table 1). Non-clinical children showed higher levels of basic attention and higher PPVT-III scores than both clinical groups. In addition, children with ASD scored higher on the PPVT-III than children with DLD.

Stimuli

A paired-preference task was implemented to assess participants’ VSP toward faces and objects, as well as the effect of competing stimuli on attention to faces across groups27,58. The task included facial stimuli displaying three emotional expressions (happiness, anger, and neutral) and two types of objects (CIOs and non-CIOs).

By pairing each emotional expression with both object types, six experimental conditions were generated. Each condition was presented six times using different facial identities, resulting in a total of 36 trials. The gender of the posers was counterbalanced to ensure equal representation of male and female faces. The spatial position of stimuli (left/right) was also counterbalanced across trials to control for potential location effects.

Facial stimuli were selected from the Amsterdam Dynamic Facial Expression Set (ADFES)80, a standardized database that ensures consistency in key visual properties. A total of 36 images from 12 posers were used, with each poser displaying the three emotional expressions, although no poser was repeated within the same emotion condition. These stimuli were paired with 36 objects images (18 CIOs and 18 non-CIOs; see Fig. 1A), obtained from the Pixabay database (Creative Commons CC0 license) or created by photographing toys and puzzles commonly used at the ECI center of the University of Córdoba (Spain). Objects were classified as CIOs based on previous literature on CI in ASD43,57,81. The CIO category included toys, puzzles, means of transport, animals, and blocks, whereas the non-CIO category included plants, furniture, musical instruments, tools, school materials, and clothing. To minimize potential confounding effects, CIO and non-CIO stimuli were selected to be comparable in size, shape, and general visual properties. All images were edited to remove background information and standardized in size.

Stimulus pairs were created using Microsoft PowerPoint. We used PowerPoint to design the pairs. Images were resized (to approximately 15.3 × 11.3 cm), positioned symmetrically on the screen, and arranged in a consistent spatial configuration across trials. Final compositions were rescaled to 1500 × 843 pixels to match the display resolution of the presentation device. In addition, the order of stimuli presentation was counterbalanced across participants.

Procedure

This study was approved by the Research Ethics Committee of the University of Córdoba (Spain). ECI centers were contacted, and professionals provided information about the study to families. Written informed consent was obtained from parents or legal guardians prior to participation, and evaluation sessions were scheduled according to family and center availability.

Assessments were conducted in quiet rooms at the participating centers to minimize potential distractions. All children were evaluated individually by a trained researcher. Parents were present during the session only if requested by the child or the parents. All assessments were completed in a single session lasting approximately 45 min.

During the session, children first completed the paired-preference task (approximately 3.5 min). Eye movements were recorded using Tobii X2-30 (Tobii Technology AB, Stockholm, Sweden), which samples gaze data at 30 Hz with a spatial accuracy of 0.4°. Participants were seated approximately 60 cm away and 36º above a 15-inch laptop screen. Instructions were limited to directing children to look at the screen throughout the task. Prior to the task, a nine-points calibration procedure was conducted using an animated stimulus. Calibration was repeated when necessary if the child did not adequately fixate on the calibration targets. The task consisted of 36 trials, each presented for 5 s and preceded by a central fixation stimulus displayed for 1 s to orient their attention.

Following the eye-tracking task, receptive vocabulary was assessed using the PPTV-III76 in order to further characterize group differences. This measure was selected due to its suitability for both clinical and research purposes and its availability of normative data for diverse populations, including children with ASD or DLD. At the end of the session, all children received a small reward (a craft medal) for their participation.

Measures

Following established approaches in the literature, three indexes were defined to characterize participants’ VSP: (1) Prioritization, operationalized as the latency to first fixation (Time to First Fixation, TFF), indexing initial attentional orienting to both faces and objects, (2) Preference, operationalized as total fixation duration (Total Fixation Duration, TFD) on each stimulus type (faces or objects), reflecting the relative allocation of visual attention, and (3) Duration, operationalized as mean visit duration (VD) to each stimulus type, indexing the depth of visual engagement27,58.

These indices were derived from eye-tracking data processed using Tobii Studio (Tobii Technology AB, Stockholm, Sweden). A fixation was defined as a period during which gaze remained relatively stable within a predefined spatial area (Area of Interest; AOI) for a minimum duration, reflecting sustained visual attention. Fixations were identified using the default I-VT (Identification by Velocity Threshold) filter, with a velocity threshold of approximately 30°/s and a minimum duration of 75 ms. Raw gaze data were processed using standard noise-reduction procedures implemented in the software.

A visit was defined as a sequence of one or more consecutive fixations within a given AOI, bounded by gaze entry and exit. A new visit was recorded each time gaze re-entered the AOI after leaving it.

Five AOIs were defined based on the experimental conditions: happy faces, angry faces, neutral faces, CIOs, and non-CIOs.

The Prioritization index was derived from TFF values, and the Preference index from TFD values. The Duration index was calculated as the total visit duration within a given AOI divided by the number of visits to that AOI. All indexes were treated as dependent variables.

Following previous studies82,83, indices were computed separately for face and object AOIs and aggregated across trials.

Data analysis

Data analysis was conducted using the Statistical Package for the Social Sciences, version 25 (SPSS, 25, IBM, Armonk, NY, United States of America).

To examine VSP toward target stimuli across groups, separate repeated-measures analyses of variance (ANOVAs) were conducted for each dependent variable (Prioritization, Preference, and Duration). Group (ASD, DLD, non-clinical) was included as a between-group factor, while Type of Stimulus (Face, Object), Interest (CIO, Non-CIO), and Emotion (Happiness, Anger, Neutral) were included as within-subjects factors. ANOVA assumptions (normality, homogeneity of variance, and sphericity) were assessed; when violated, appropriate corrections were applied. A Greenhouse–Geisser correction was applied when the assumption of sphericity was violated, as indicated by Mauchly’s test (p < .05).

Receptive vocabulary (PPVT-III standard score) was initially included as covariate. However, as it was not significantly associated with the dependent variables, it was excluded from the final analysis to improve model parsimony. Effect sizes were estimated using partial eta-squared (ηp2), with values of 0.01, 0.06, and 0.14 conventionally interpreted as small, medium, and large effects, respectively84. Significant interactions were further examined using planned pairwise comparisons with Bonferroni correction.

Results

Prioritization

The ANOVA conducted on the Prioritization index revealed a significant main effect of Type of Stimulus (F(1,66) = 5.671, p=.02, ηp2 = 0.079), indicating shorter latencies to first fixation for objects (M = 836) than for faces (M = 941). Significant interactions were also found for Type of Stimulus × Interest (F(1,66) = 10.101, p=.002, ηp2=0.133), Type of Stimulus × Group (F(2,66) = 3.964, p=.024, ηp2 = 0.107), and Interest × Group (F(2,66) = 3.171, p=.048, ηp2 = 0.088). In addition, significant three-way interaction was observed for Group × Type of Stimulus × Interest (F(2,66) = 8.067, p=.001, ηp2 = 0.196). This third-order interaction was further examined by conducting separate 3 (Group) × 2 (Interest) ANOVAs for each stimulus type (Faces and Objects; see Fig. 2).

‘Face condition’. The follow-up ANOVA revealed a significant main effect of Interest (F(1,66) = 11.982, p<.001, ηp2=0.154) with longer latencies to first fixation for faces paired with CIOs (M = 836) than for faces paired with non-CIOs (M = 941). A significant Group × Interest interaction was also observed (F(2,66) = 4.514, p=.015, ηp2=0.154). Planned contrasts between groups indicated that the DLD group showed shorter latencies to faces than the ASD group in the CIO condition (t(66) = 3.16, p=.002, d = 0.99), whereas no group differences were found in the Non-CIO condition (p>.05). Planned contrasts within-group showed longer latencies to faces paired with CIOs compared to Non-CIOs in the ASD group (t(22) = 3.43, p=.002, d = 0.77) and non-clinical group (t(22) = 3.27, p=.003, d = 0.73), but not in the DLD group (p>.05).

‘Object condition’. The follow-up ANOVA revealed a significant Group × Interest interaction (F(2,66) = 6.96, p=.002, ηp2=0.174). Planned contrasts between groups indicated that the non-clinical group showed longer latencies to non-CIOs than both the ASD group (t(66) = 4.32, p<.001, d = 1.31) and the DLD group (t(66) = 3.35, p=.001, d = 0.92) groups, with no differences between the ASD and DLD groups (p>.05). Planned contrasts within-group showed longer latencies to non-CIOs compared to CIOs in the non-clinical group (t(22)=-3.73, p=.001, d = 1.03), but not in the ASD or DLD groups (p>.05) (Fig. 2).

Highlights

Prioritization index. Non-clinical participants and those with ASD showed comparable orienting to faces. In both groups, longer latencies to first fixation were observed for faces paired with CIOs relative to non-CIOs. In contrast, children with DLD exhibited shorter latencies to faces across conditions, irrespective of the type of competing object. This pattern differentiates the DLD group from both the ASD and non-clinical participants, which showed similar orienting profiles. Regarding orienting to objects, children with ASD and DLD showed comparable patterns, characterized by short latencies to first fixation regardless of object type. By contrast, non-clinical participants exhibited shorter latencies to CIOs relative to non-CIOs.

Preference index. This index did not differentiate between participants with ASD and DLD but distinguished clinical from non-clinical groups. Both clinical groups showed longer total fixation durations for objects than for faces, whereas the non-clinical group showed the opposite pattern. In addition, emotional expressions elicited similar patterns across groups, with angry faces associated with longer fixation durations and reduced allocation of attention to objects.

Duration index. Only participants with ASD showed longer visit durations for objects than for faces. This pattern differentiated the ASD group from both the DLD or non-clinical groups.

Discussion

Despite the challenges associated with the early differential diagnosis of ASD and DLD, relatively few studies have directly compared VSP toward social stimuli in these populations67,68. This study examined three characteristics commonly reported in ASD to differentiate it from DLD: (1) reduced attention to social relative to non-social stimuli27, 35,37,38,40,42; (2) decreased attention to social stimuli when competing with CIOs43; (3) and relatively preserved emotional sensitivity to emotional expressions, with differential responses to emotional valence27,51,54.

To this end, VSP was examined in children with ASD, DLD, and non-clinical peers using three indexes to capture attentional orienting (Prioritization), allocation of visual attention (Preference), and depth of processing (Duration).

Results from the Prioritization index indicate that children with ASD and non-clinical showed comparable orienting to faces but differed in orienting to objects. In contrast, children with ASD and DLD showed similar orienting to objects but diverged in orienting to faces. These findings suggest that, in contexts involving competing social and non-social stimuli, atypical orienting to faces in ASD may not reflect a generalized deficit in face processing, but rather increased attentional capture by objects. In contrast, children with DLD exhibited rapid and relatively undifferentiated orienting to both social and non-social stimuli. This pattern may have developmental implications, as efficient and flexible orienting to salient environmental information has been linked to social-cognitive development85.

Results from the Preference index showed clear differences between non-clinical and clinical groups, suggesting a potential nonspecific marker of atypical attention. Both clinical groups showed greater allocation of visual attention to objects than to faces, whereas non-clinical participants showed the opposite pattern, consistent with H1. Regarding emotional processing, no group differences were observed, but visual attention varied as a function of emotional expression. In line with H3 and previous research27,51,53,54,86, angry faces elicited longer fixation durations than happy or neutral faces and were associated with reduced attention to competing objects. This pattern is consistent with the well-documented anger superiority effect87,88, often attributed to the evolutionary salience of threat-related stimuli71,89. The presence of this effect across all groups suggests preserved sensitivity to emotional salience in both children with ASD and DLD. Thus, this index appears useful for distinguishing clinical from non-clinical groups, but not as a specific marker for differentiating between ASD and DLD.

Finally, results from the Duration index indicate that only children with ASD showed greater depth of processing for objects relative to faces. This pattern differentiated the ASD group from both the DLD and non-clinical groups. This index may be a valuable marker to differentiate between ASD and DLD. As this index reflects sustained engagement with stimuli90, it may be particularly interesting for individuals with ASD, given their characteristic patterns of restrictive, repetitive, and stereotyped behavior, interests, and activities1,2. Thus, these findings may point to enhanced engagement with non-social stimuli in ASD91. This interpretation is consistent with prior work linking attentional engagement and difficulties in disengagement to the restricted and repetitive behavioral patterns characteristic of ASD92.

In summary, children with DLD differed from those with ASD primarily in the Prioritization index, showing faster orienting to faces irrespective of the competing stimulus. In contrast, children with ASD exhibited delayed orienting to faces when paired with CIOs and greater sustained attention to objects. With respect to clinical versus non-clinical groups, the Preference index indicated that both clinical groups showed reduced overall allocation of visual attention and a bias toward objects, whereas non-clinical children showed greater overall attention and a preference for faces.

Beyond group-level differences, distinct VSP profiles emerged for each group:

Non-clinical children showed (1) flexible orienting adapted to stimulus characteristics, (2) greater overall allocation of visual attention, particularly to faces, and (3) comparable depth of processing for faces and objects.

Children with ASD showed (1) context-dependent orienting to faces influenced by competing objects, alongside rapid orienting to objects, (2) reduced overall allocation of visual attention, particularly to faces, and (3) greater depth of processing for objects, suggesting increased engagement with non-social stimuli (specific marker for this group).

Children with DLD showed (1) relatively inflexible orienting, with limited modulation by stimulus characteristics, (2) reduced overall allocation of visual attention similar to the ASD group, and (3) comparable depth of processing across stimulus types.

Finally, across all groups emotional modulation of attention was observed, with longer and more sustained processing of angry faces.

Limitations

This study has several limitations that should be considered when interpreting the findings. First, the scope of the experimental design and the relatively small sample size may limit the generalizability of the results. Future research including larger samples and broader comparisons across neurodevelopmental disorders would help to further delineate condition-specific profiles. Given the similarities of children with ASD and DLD in early development, the consistent replication of VSP differences across studies could provide clinicians with an additional tool to support differential diagnosis in complex cases. The present findings also have potential clinical implications. In particular, they suggest that interventions for children with DLD may benefit from incorporating social components alongside linguistic targets. The inclusion of SA measures in intervention contexts may contribute to improving social interactions and increasing exposure to socially relevant input, which is critical for language development At the same time, the limited evidence regarding visual behavior in children with DLD highlights the need for further research in this area. In the present study, only receptive vocabulary was assessed; future work should include more comprehensive language and cognitive measures to better characterize participant profiles. In addition, the classification of CIO stimuli was based on categories derived from the literature rather than on individual preferences. This approach may not fully capture variability in children’s interests and could reduce the ecological validity and sensitivity of the task. It should also be noted that some of the diagnostic instruments used (i.e., M-CHAT, BDI-II, PPVT-III) have since been updated. Although these were the versions available at the time of data collection, future studies should employ the most recent versions to ensure greater accuracy and comparability. Finally, diagnoses at early ages are inherently provisional and may change over time. Therefore, the present findings should be interpreted with appropriate caution. Nevertheless, research focusing on early developmental stages remains essential for advancing the understanding of neurodevelopmental trajectories.

Conclusions

In conclusion, this study provides evidence that three complementary indices capture distinct aspects of VSP in children with ASD, DLD, and non-clinical. These findings contribute to a more precise characterization of attentional profiles, with potential implications for understanding everyday strengths and difficulties, as well as for informing intervention approaches. In addition, the identification of distinct patterns across indices may support the development of behavioral markers to aid early differential diagnosis. However, further research is needed to validate these findings and determine their clinical applicability.
