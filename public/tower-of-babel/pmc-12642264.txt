The relationship between growth mindset and adolescent psychological well-being: the parallel mediating roles of grit and learning engagement
Authors: Wenhui Wang, Suning Jia, Lili Zhang, Yan Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundWhile existing research has established associations among growth mindset, grit, learning engagement, and psychological well-being, the underlying mechanisms—specifically, whether grit and learning engagement serve as parallel mediators in the relationship between growth mindset and psychological well-being—remain underexplored.MethodsThis cross-sectional study investigated the relationship between growth mindset and adolescent psychological well-being, focusing on the potential parallel mediating roles of grit and learning engagement. In February 2025, 1142 adolescents from Shandong and Jiangsu provinces completed questionnaires assessing these constructs. Data were analyzed using structural equation modeling (SEM) with AMOS 24.0 and SPSS 24.0.Results(1) Growth mindset was significantly and positively associated with psychological well-being; (2) Grit mediated the relationship between growth mindset and psychological well-being; (3) Learning engagement mediated this relationship between growth mindset and psychological well-being.ConclusionThese findings provide a novel theoretical perspective for understanding the determinants of adolescents’ psychological well-being. Additionally, they offer important implications for educational practice: targeted interventions (e.g., growth mindset training, grit cultivation, and learning engagement) are feasible approaches to improving adolescents’ psychological well-being.

Introduction

Psychological well-being is defined as the positive psychological state that individuals experience in terms of self-growth, life goals, and interpersonal relationships [1]. Adolescents (aged 13–18) represent a crucial developmental population undergoing significant transitions from childhood to adulthood [2]. During this developmental stage, adolescents face multifaceted challenges across various domains. Academically, they must navigate intense competitive pressures, particularly surrounding high-stakes standardized testing systems like the Zhongkao (secondary school entrance examination) and Gaokao (national college entrance examination) [3]. In the realm of social relationships, they often contend with pressure from high parental expectations and social comparisons with peers [4]. Additionally, due to the incomplete maturation of the prefrontal cortex [5], they are more likely to experience identity crises, characterized by confusion and doubt about their self-worth and life goals [6]. The combined effect of these developmental challenges is associated with lower levels of psychological well-being in this group compared to other developmental stages [7]. Lower psychological well-being not only increases the risk of psychological problems such as depression and anxiety [8], but also significantly affects learning engagement and academic efficacy [9]. Therefore, investigating the psychological well-being of adolescents holds significant theoretical and practical importance.

Growth mindset is considered a key indicator of psychological well-being [10]. Growth mindset is defined as the conviction that personal attributes are amenable to development [11]. This mindset helps individuals maintain a positive attitude when facing challenges, promotes adaptive behavior, and thereby enhances psychological well-being [12]. Grit, defined as the persistent effort and passion in pursuing long-term goals [13], can help adolescents stay focused and resilient in the face of academic pressure, which in turn improves psychological well-being [14]. Meanwhile, learning engagement, which refers to the cognitive, emotional, and behavioral involvement of individuals in the learning process [15], can promote positive learning experiences and a sense of achievement, thus enhancing psychological well-being. Therefore, growth mindset, grit, and learning engagement may be important factors influencing psychological well-being. Although existing research has established associations among growth mindset, grit, learning engagement, and psychological well-being, the mechanisms underlying these relationships—specifically, whether grit and learning engagement serve as parallel mediators—remain underexplored.

Self-Determination Theory (SDT) offers a theoretical framework for understanding how growth mindset may influence psychological well-being through grit and learning engagement [16]. As a macro-theory of human motivation and personality, SDT posits three innate and universal psychological needs: autonomy (the need to volitionally initiate one’s own actions), competence (the need to feel effective in one’s interactions with the environment), and relatedness (the need to feel connected and cared for by others) [16]. The satisfaction of these needs is essential for optimal functioning, fostering intrinsic motivation, facilitating the internalization of extrinsic goals, and promoting vitality, well-being, and personal growth [17].

A growth mindset, as a positive cognitive belief, supports the fulfillment of these basic psychological needs by shaping motivational and behavioral patterns [18]. This dynamic suggests a coherent causal sequence: growth mindset serves as the key driver, while the satisfaction of basic psychological needs constitutes the central underlying mechanism. Within this process, grit and learning engagement function as parallel manifestations of this need-satisfaction mechanism at the behavioral and experiential levels. This is evidenced by findings that grit depends on confidence in one’s abilities (competence) and alignment with personal goals (autonomy) [14], while learning engagement is enhanced in autonomy-supportive environments, through challenging tasks (competence), and via positive learning interactions (relatedness) [19]. Thus, a growth mindset may promote psychological well-being both by cultivating grit and by increasing learning engagement—with each pathway facilitating need satisfaction. This theoretical perspective justifies the examination of grit and learning engagement as parallel mediators in the relationship between growth mindset and psychological well-being, providing a solid conceptual foundation for the proposed model (Fig. 1).

Guided by this framework, the present study aims to address three research questions: (1) Does growth mindset directly predict psychological well-being? (2) Does grit mediate the relationship between growth mindset and psychological well-being? (3) Does learning engagement mediate the relationship between growth mindset and psychological well-being? The study is expected to provide a scientific basis for designing targeted psychological intervention programs. Specifically, if grit is identified as a significant mediator, this would suggest that interventions aimed at fostering grit—such as through perseverance-building exercises, long-term goal-setting activities, and resilience training—could enhance adolescents’ psychological well-being. Similarly, if learning engagement serves as a key mediator, interventions that promote active and meaningful participation in learning—for example, through project-based learning, autonomous task design, and supportive feedback mechanisms—may be effective. By clarifying these mediating pathways, the study will offer evidence-backed guidance for developing structured intervention plans (e.g., growth mindset workshops, grit cultivation programs, and learning engagement strategies) tailored to the specific psychological and educational needs of adolescents. This study, situated within the distinctive context of China’s examination-oriented system, aims to illuminate how nurturing a growth mindset can improve students’ mental health by strengthening grit and boosting learning engagement, thereby contributing meaningful insights for educational reform.

The relationship between growth mindset and psychological well-being

Aligned with Self-Determination Theory, a growth mindset is posited to enhance psychological well-being through the satisfaction of individuals’ basic psychological needs [20]. Specifically, individuals who believe in the malleability of their abilities are more likely to regard challenges as opportunities for growth rather than threats. This positive cognitive orientation boosts their sense of autonomy in making behavioral choices [21]. Moreover, the attitude of persisting through difficulties and learning from failures effectively strengthens individuals’ sense of competence [22]. Additionally, a growth mindset mitigates the pressure from social comparison and fosters cooperative interpersonal relationships, thereby satisfying the need for relatedness [23]– [24]. Recent empirical studies have provided cross-cultural evidence for this theoretical mechanism. For instance, Zeng et al. examined 1,260 Chinese primary and middle school students and revealed that a higher level of growth mindset predicts greater psychological well-being [10]. Similarly, Chan et al. surveyed 856 Hong Kong adolescents and found that different dimensions of growth mindset have distinct impacts on various aspects of psychological well-being, beyond the effects of gender and age [25]. In another study, Purwaningsih et al. employed a quasi-experimental design and demonstrated that growth mindset training significantly enhances the psychological well-being of high school students [26]. Whereas existing Chinese studies have often focused on specific regions or educational stages, this research encompasses a broader adolescent demographic within Mainland China’s unique examination-oriented context. Overall, as a crucial cognitive factor in promoting psychological well-being, the mechanism of a growth mindset exhibits cultural and developmental stage specificity. While prior studies establish a general association between growth mindset and psychological well-being, several unresolved issues necessitate further investigation—particularly in the context of Chinese adolescents. Thus, we posit the following hypothesis:

H1: A growth mindset is positively and significantly associated with psychological well-being among adolescents.

The mediating role of grit

A growth mindset is a positive predictor of grit. Within the Self-Determination Theory framework, a growth mindset encourages students to believe that effort can enhance ability (competence), to autonomously choose challenging tasks (autonomy), and to seek support from others (relatedness) [16]. The satisfaction of these psychological needs strengthens intrinsic motivation, making students more willing to persist in long-term goals and exhibit higher levels of grit. Moreover, numerous studies both domestically and internationally have shown that individuals with a growth mindset tend to have higher levels of grit [27]– [28]. For example, Wang et al. provided theoretical support from a neuroanatomical perspective [29]. Through magnetic resonance imaging scans of Chinese adolescents, they found that a growth mindset affected the neural bases of grit and acted as a mediator linking brain structure to grit. Several cross-sectional studies have also shown a significant positive correlation between a growth mindset and grit [30–32]. In a 2-year longitudinal study, Park et al. conducted four consecutive follow-up surveys on 1,667 junior high school students from different states in the United States [33]. The results indicated that grit and a growth mindset have a mutually reinforcing effect during adolescence, with a growth mindset having a positive impact on grit.

Grit demonstrates a strong positive association with psychological well-being [34]. From a Self-Determination Theory perspective, grit contributes to psychological need satisfaction by fostering sustained goal-directed behavior, which in turn enhances well-being [16]. Empirical studies have shown that grit positively affects individuals’ psychological well-being. Through two studies examining grit in relation to psychological well-being, satisfaction with life, and harmony in life, Vainio and Daukantaitė found that grit not only positively correlates with psychological well-being but also influences it through the mediating roles of sense of coherence and authenticity [35]. Vinothkumar and Prasad investigated adolescents aged 14–17 and revealed that grit not only directly affects psychological well-being but also influences adolescents’ overall well-being through resilience [34]. Furthermore, Tang and Zhu surveyed 968 Chinese EFL college students and indicated that grit emerged as a direct predictor of psychological well-being [36].

While existing research has provided theoretical and empirical support for the relationship between growth mindset, grit, and psychological well-being, several critical gaps remain. Most studies have focused on college students, with limited attention to the unique developmental stage of adolescents. Therefore, to better understand how this mechanism operates during this specific developmental period, this study proposes the following hypothesis:

H2: Grit mediates the relationship between a growth mindset and psychological well-being among adolescents.

The mediating role of learning engagement

A growth mindset is closely related to learning engagement [37]. Self-Determination Theory suggests that the satisfaction of basic psychological needs—autonomy, competence, and relatedness—promotes motivation and behavioral engagement [19]. For instance, when students experience a sense of choice and interest in learning (autonomy), perceive tasks as manageable (competence), and feel connected to teachers and peers (relatedness), they are more likely to exhibit higher learning engagement [38]– [39]. Empirical evidence supports this link. Zhao et al. conducted online questionnaires with 1,040 Chinese college students from various universities and indicated a positive correlation between a growth mindset and learning engagement, as well as an inverse association with both perceived COVID-19 event strength and stress [40]. Xiao et al. surveyed 1,065 nursing students from five schools in China and discovered that a growth mindset was positively correlated with learning engagement (r = 0.272, p < 0.01) [41]. Li investigated 413 Chinese EFL learners and found that a growth mindset indirectly affected student engagement through the mediation of foreign language enjoyment [42].

Empirical studies have consistently shown that learning engagement positively impacts psychological well-being [43]– [44]. Self-Determination Theory conceptualizes learning engagement as both an outcome of need satisfaction and a facilitator of further psychological growth, thereby enhancing overall well-being [16]. For example, in a longitudinal survey investigating the impact of student engagement on well-being, Boulton et al. revealed a significant positive relationship between the two among a sample of 175 UK undergraduates [45]. Kilgo et al. surveyed 4,402 college students and showed that student engagement is positively associated with psychological well-being [46]. Pan et al. conducted a questionnaire survey with 1,968 Chinese EFL learners and found that psychological well-being and learning engagement were positively correlated [43].

While existing research has preliminarily established the relationship between growth mindset and learning engagement through the Self-Determination Theory, further exploration is needed regarding adolescent populations. Current studies predominantly focus on college students, with relatively insufficient attention given to this unique developmental stage. Although cross-cultural studies have demonstrated associations between learning engagement and psychological well-being, evidence remains limited concerning the mediating role of learning engagement between growth mindset and well-being in secondary education. Therefore, this study examines the mediating mechanism of learning engagement among adolescents to broaden the research perspective in this field. Based on the above theoretical and empirical evidence, the following hypothesis is proposed:

H3: Learning engagement mediates the relationship between growth mindset and psychological well-being among adolescents.

Method

Participants

Given its cross-sectional survey design, the study was appropriate for investigating the relationships among variables at a particular point in time. This type of design is useful for collecting data from a large and diverse sample to test the hypothesized mediation model of growth mindset, grit, learning engagement, and psychological well-being.

The determination of a target sample size of approximately 1300 participants was guided by the methodological requirements of Structural Equation Modeling (SEM) to ensure robust parameter estimates, adequate statistical power, and stable model fit indices [47]. The study employed a convenience sampling method to collect data from eight schools (four junior high schools and four senior high schools) in Jiangsu and Shandong provinces in February 2025. These provinces were selected as they represent regions with distinct yet comparable socioeconomic and educational development levels in eastern China, thereby supporting sample diversity while maintaining structural comparability.

Prior to data collection, parental consent procedures were implemented: all parents or guardians received written information about the study’s purpose and procedures, and were informed of their right to decline their child’s participation. Only students with no parental opt-out took part in the survey. To minimize response bias, all participants were assured of the anonymity and confidentiality of their responses, and were encouraged to answer honestly based on their actual feelings and experiences. Teachers present during administration were instructed not to interfere with or observe individual answers.

A multi-step cleaning protocol was rigorously applied to the 1,300 initially collected paper questionnaires. The cleaning process involved: first, questionnaires with illegible handwriting (n = 25) were excluded; next, those with more than 5% missing values (n = 78) were removed; finally, duplicate submissions, identified based on identical demographic information and response patterns (n = 55), were also discarded. As a result, 158 invalid responses were excluded, yielding a final sample of 1,142 valid questionnaires and an effective response rate of 87.85%. The sample composition was as follows: 585 males (51.2%) and 557 females (48.8%), with a mean age of 14.93 ± 1.553 years. The grade distribution was as follows: 261 first-year junior high school students (22.9%), 358 s-year junior high students (31.3%), 252 first-year senior high students (22.1%), and 271 s-year senior high students (23.7%). In terms of residence, 558 students (51.5%) lived in urban areas, while 554 (48.5%) resided in rural regions. The study was supported by the Ethics Committee of Liaocheng University (No. 20250011).

Materials

The scales employed in this study, though originally developed in Western contexts, have been rigorously translated and widely validated for use with Chinese adolescent populations. All scales underwent formal back-translation to ensure conceptual and linguistic equivalence. This process involved initial translation into Chinese by a bilingual expert, followed by blind back-translation into English by an independent translator. The resulting versions were compared against the originals, with discrepancies resolved through iterative revision. To further ensure clarity and contextual appropriateness for our specific sample, we invited a panel of experts, including a psychology professor and an experienced secondary school teacher, to conduct a content validity review. The panel confirmed that all items were well-aligned with Chinese middle school students’ cognitive and linguistic patterns. Subsequently, we conducted a pilot test with 50 students from a secondary school outside the formal sample. Results demonstrated good internal consistency, with Cronbach’s α coefficients for all scales exceeding the commonly accepted threshold of 0.70.

Growth mindset scale

The Growth Mindset Scale, developed by Dweck et al. [48], was adopted in this study, consisting of six items. Items 1–3 represent a fixed mindset (e.g., “You can learn new things, but you can’t really change your basic intelligence level.”), while items 4–6 represent a growth mindset (e.g., “You can change your basic intelligence level to a great extent.”). The scale uses a 5-point Likert scale, ranging from 1 (“Strongly disagree”) to 5 (“Strongly agree”). Items 1–3 are reverse-scored.

After reverse scoring was applied, the participant’s growth mindset score was obtained by averaging the six items, with higher scores denoting a stronger growth mindset. The reliability and validity of this scale have been confirmed in previous studies with Chinese students [49]. In this study, the Cronbach’s alpha for fixed mindset was 0.844, and for growth mindset it was 0.874, indicating high internal consistency. The measurement model demonstrated a good fit to the data: χ2/df = 1.354 (χ2 = 12.186, df = 9), IFI = 0.999, CFI = 0.999, TLI = 0.999, GFI = 0.997, AGFI = 0.999, and RMSEA = 0.018.

Grit scale

The Triarchic Model of Grit Scale [48] was selected for its theoretical relevance to the Chinese educational context. Unlike earlier grit scales [13]– [50], the TMGS incorporates adaptability to situations in addition to perseverance and consistency. This third dimension is critical in capturing how Chinese adolescents navigate dynamic and competitive academic environments, thereby strengthening the scale’s contextual validity. The Chinese version of the scale, translated and validated by Datu and Zhang [51], was used. The scale consists of three dimensions with 10 items: perseverance of effort, consistency of interests, and adaptability to situations. Items are rated on a 5-point Likert scale, ranging from 1 (“Not like me at all”) to 5 (“Very much like me”). Higher scores, after reverse scoring, indicate higher levels of grit. The reliability and validity of this scale have been confirmed in previous studies with Chinese students [52]. In this study, the Cronbach’s alpha for perseverance of effort was 0.907, for consistency of interests was 0.910, and for adaptability to situations was 0.923. The scale demonstrated a good model fit: χ2/df = 2.084 (χ2 = 72.945, df = 35), IFI = 0.996, CFI = 0.996, TLI = 0.995, GFI = 0.993, AGFI = 0.984, and RMSEA = 0.031.

Learning engagement scale

Students’ learning engagement was measured using the questionnaire developed by Salmela and Upadaya [53], which comprises three subscales: vigor (e.g., “I feel energetic at school.”), dedication (e.g., “I am passionate about learning.”), and absorption (e.g., “I feel happy when studying intensively.”), with a total of nine items. The questionnaire uses a 7-point Likert scale, ranging from 0 (“Never”) to 6 (“Always”), with higher scores indicating higher levels of learning engagement. The operational definition and dimensional structure of this learning engagement questionnaire align with those established in studies by Yin et al. [38] and Teuber et al. [54]. The scale has demonstrated good reliability and validity, which has been established in studies involving Chinese samples [38]. In this study, the Cronbach’s α for vigor was 0.85, for dedication was 0.85, and for absorption was 0.886. The scale showed excellent model fit indices: χ2/df = 2.771 (χ2 = 74.825, df = 27), IFI = 0.995, CFI = 0.995, TLI = 0.993, GFI = 0.992, AGFI = 0.976, and RMSEA = 0.039.

Psychological well-being scale

Psychological well-being was assessed using the scale developed by Diener et al. [55]. The scale consists of eight items (e.g., “I am engaged and interested in my daily activities.”), rated on a 7-point Likert scale from 1 (“Strongly disagree”) to 7 (“Strongly agree”). Higher total scores indicate higher levels of psychological well-being. The reliability and validity of this questionnaire have been confirmed in Chinese samples [36]. In this study, the Cronbach’s α coefficient was 0.957, reaffirming its reliability in measuring psychological well-being among Chinese students. The measurement model demonstrated a good fit to the data: X2/df = 1.487 (χ2 = 29.74, df = 20), IFI = 0.999, CFI = 0.999, TLI = 0.998, GFI = 0.997, AGFI = 0.983, and RMSEA = 0.021.

Procedure

Paper-based questionnaires were administered to assess students’ growth mindset, grit, learning engagement, and psychological well-being. The survey implementation strictly followed ethical guidelines. Approval was obtained from the ethics committees of each participating school. Prior to data collection, written informed consent was secured from all parents. Students were also informed of their right to withdraw from the study at any time without penalty.

The questionnaires were administered during regular class hours in computer labs under the organization of homeroom teachers, who served as test administrators. All administrators received uniform training regarding survey procedures. Specifically, they introduced the research purpose, emphasized the principles of anonymity, confidentiality, and voluntary participation, and provided clear instructions. Participants completed the questionnaires independently within approximately 12–15 min under teacher supervision, without intervention.

Data collection and analysis

This study adopted a cross-sectional design to efficiently collect data from a large sample of adolescents, allowing for the examination of relationships among the key variables at a specific point in time. The research employed SPSS 24.0 and Amos 24.0 for data processing. Initially, common method variance was evaluated through Harman’s single-factor examination. Subsequent statistical procedures included computing descriptive statistics and conducting normality assessments using SPSS. Given that the study involves latent variables (growth mindset, grit, learning engagement, and psychological well-being) measured by multiple indicators and aims to examine the complex mediating relationships among them, Covariance-Based Structural Equation Modeling (CB-SEM) was deemed the most appropriate method. Amos 24.0 was selected to perform the CB-SEM analysis due to its strength in simultaneously estimating the measurement model (relationships between latent variables and their indicators) and the structural model (paths between latent variables), while accounting for measurement error. Ultimately, a structural equation model incorporating latent variables was constructed, with mediation effects verified using Bootstrap resampling techniques.

Testing of common method bias

Common method bias (CMB) refers to the potential spurious covariance that can arise when variables are measured using the same method (e.g., self-report scales), which may inflate or deflate the observed relationships between constructs [56]. Since this study utilized self-report scales for data collection, common method bias may be present. To assess common method bias for the variables, the full collinearity test approach proposed by Kock [57] was employed. This method requires that the variance inflation factor (VIF) values be below 3.3, indicating the absence of severe multicollinearity issues in the model. In accordance with the recommendations of Becker et al. [58] and Hair et al. [59], we conducted variance inflation factor (VIF) analyses for the key latent constructs—growth mindset, grit, learning engagement, and psychological well-being. As shown in Table 1, all constructs in this study exhibited VIF values below 3.3, thereby confirming the absence of significant common method bias.

Testing for mediation effect

To examine whether the effect of growth mindset on psychological well-being is mediated by grit and learning engagement, a structural equation model was constructed using Amos 24.0. The model included growth mindset as the independent variable, grit and learning engagement as mediating variables, and psychological well-being as the outcome variable. Gender and age were controlled for in the model. The overall model showed a good fit to the data and explained a substantial proportion of the variance in psychological well-being (R² = 0.426).

Two indirect paths were identified through which growth mindset affects psychological well-being: (1) Growth mindset → Grit → Psychological well-being; (2) Growth mindset → Learning engagement → Psychological well-being. The direct paths from growth mindset to grit (β = 0.434, p < 0.001) and from grit to psychological well-being (β = 0.275, p < 0.001) were significant. Similarly, the direct paths from growth mindset to learning engagement (β = 0.458, p < 0.001) and from learning engagement to psychological well-being (β = 0.319, p < 0.001) were significant, collectively supporting the proposed parallel mediation model.

To further validate the parallel mediation effect of grit and learning engagement, a bias-corrected nonparametric percentile Bootstrap test was conducted with 5,000 random resamples and a 95% confidence interval. The results are shown in Table 6. The standardized indirect effect through grit was 0.143 (95% CI = [0.103, 0.192]), supporting Hypothesis 2. The standardized indirect effect through learning engagement was 0.164 (95% CI = [0.132, 0.213]), supporting Hypothesis 3. The total standardized indirect effect was 0.307 (95% CI = [0.258, 0.373]), accounting for 46.5% of the total effect. These effect sizes indicate that both mediating pathways represent meaningful mechanisms through which growth mindset influences psychological well-being. Therefore, Hypotheses 1, 2, and 3 are all supported.

Discussion

This study investigated the relationship between growth mindset and adolescent psychological well-being through a parallel mediation model, examining the roles of grit and learning engagement. The results revealed a significant positive association between growth mindset and psychological well-being, with grit and learning engagement acting as significant mediators. These findings offer important implications for identifying pathways to enhance adolescents’ psychological well-being.

The strong link between growth mindset and psychological well-being in adolescents

This study examined the relationship and underlying mechanisms between growth mindset and psychological well-being among adolescents, finding a positive relationship between growth mindset and psychological well-being. This finding is consistent with previous research [25]– [26], indicating that a growth mindset is linked to the psychological well-being of adolescents. More importantly, it specifies and operationalizes how a growth mindset facilitates basic psychological need satisfaction—a core proposition of SDT that has been largely theoretical in this context. For example, in terms of autonomy, a growth mindset encourages students to embrace challenges and view failures as learning opportunities, which may enhance their sense of control over personal development [67]. Regarding competence, students with a growth mindset are more likely to invest effort and perceive progress, thereby strengthening their self-confidence and sense of efficacy [68]. Regarding relatedness, such students often view interpersonal difficulties as opportunities for growth, potentially promoting prosocial behaviors and supportive relationships, thereby reducing loneliness [69]. Notably, while these mechanisms are consistent with Western findings, the emphasis on interpersonal harmony and collective goal orientation within Chinese culture may amplify the role of relatedness need satisfaction, distinguishing it from more individualistically oriented contexts. When a growth mindset is well-developed, individuals are more likely to enhance their social adaptability and academic performance through the satisfaction of psychological needs [18]. This positive developmental cycle also enhances individuals’ perception of their potential realization and development, thereby improving their psychological well-being.

The mediating role of grit

This study revealed that grit mediates the relationship between growth mindset and psychological well-being among adolescents, indicating that a strong growth mindset is associated with the development of grit, which in turn is linked to psychological well-being. This finding corroborates previous research [30–32]. Students with a strong growth mindset typically exhibit higher autonomous motivation, stronger self-efficacy, and more positive problem-solving approaches. From a SDT perspective, this pathway illustrates how growth mindset supports need satisfaction through sustained goal pursuit: They are more willing to accept challenges and persist through setbacks, which helps mitigate the negative emotions associated with academic stress and enhances the fulfillment of basic psychological needs (autonomy, competence, and relatedness). In the Chinese context, where high-stakes exams like the Gaokao create a prolonged and intense pressure environment, the capacity of growth mindset to foster grit may be particularly critical—potentially more so than in educational systems with less centralized academic pressure. In short, a growth mindset builds the psychological resources for grit development, enabling individuals to respond more positively and persistently in the face of challenges, thereby enhancing grit. Moreover, individuals with high grit possess more positive cognitive schemas and emotional regulation abilities, allowing them to view problems in a more flexible and constructive manner [50]. They actively seek support and effectively allocate resources to solve problems, increasing their likelihood of success and personal growth. Research shows that when facing academic setbacks, high-grit individuals have a broader mindset, correctly perceive stress, and learn from failure experiences, discovering their growth value [64]. Through persistent goal pursuit, high-grit individuals enhance their confidence in their abilities, developing resilience, optimism, and other positive qualities, accompanied by increased self-efficacy, which is associated with improved psychological well-being. This finding confirms the role of grit in linking growth mindset to psychological well-being in adolescents.

The mediating role of learning engagement

The study confirmed learning engagement as a mediator between growth mindset and psychological well-being in adolescents, which not only aligns with existing studies [40–42] but extends their implications by situating this pathway within the theoretical framework of SDT. While prior research has established the correlation between growth mindset and engagement, our model specifies the motivational mechanism: a growth mindset fosters the belief in ability malleability, which in turn enhances autonomous motivation and facilitates the satisfaction of three basic psychological needs. Specifically, through autonomy (volitional engagement in learning), competence (mastery of challenges), and relatedness (meaningful classroom interactions), students with a growth mindset are more likely to set and pursue challenging goals, thereby exhibiting sustained learning engagement. This process takes on unique characteristics in Chinese classrooms: the cultural emphasis on diligent learning as a moral virtue may strengthen the pathway from growth mindset to engagement, while the collective learning atmosphere provides a distinct form of relatedness support compared to the more individualized engagement often observed in Western settings [70]. This pathway provides an operationalization of how SDT’s theoretical propositions translate into measurable educational outcomes. The experience of flow and mastery in learning contexts [39, 71] serves as direct evidence of need satisfaction, further reinforcing self-efficacy and consolidating the link between engagement and well-being. Furthermore, during the critical adolescent stage of identity formation, active learning engagement becomes a primary venue for realizing autonomy, competence, and relatedness. Thus, our finding underscores that learning engagement functions not merely as an academic outcome but as a vital behavioral conduit through which growth mindset beliefs support psychological well-being via need fulfillment. This finding underscores the role of learning engagement are linked to growth mindset to psychological well-being in adolescents.

Implications and limitations

While existing research has established correlations among growth mindset, grit, learning engagement, and psychological well-being, empirical exploration of the mechanisms linking growth mindset to psychological well-being through grit and learning engagement remains limited. The implications of this study extend beyond theoretical advancement to provide actionable insights for real-world practice.

Theoretically, this research aimed to explore the relationship between growth mindset and psychological well-being among adolescents, by investigating their association and underlying mechanisms from the perspective of Self-Determination Theory. This study not only identifies key variables associated with the psychological well-being of adolescents but also tests a parallel mediation model involving grit and learning engagement, thereby adding new theoretical depth to well-being research. By doing so, the model extends the application of growth mindset in education and provides a novel analytical framework for understanding how it relates to student well-being.

Practically, this study provides certain insights for interventions targeting psychological well-being. First, given the positive effect of growth mindset on psychological well-being, educators should focus on creating supportive and empathetic learning environments [72] and providing process-oriented feedback, while parents should aim to foster a family atmosphere that encourages exploration and tolerates failure, thereby supporting the satisfaction of basic psychological needs and enabling children to grow through challenges. Second, the significant mediating roles of grit and learning engagement suggest the value of incorporating structured growth mindset training into school-based programs. Such training—teaching students that intellectual abilities can be developed through effort, strategy, and guidance [73]—not only strengthens grit by reinforcing the link between effort and growth [74] but also boosts learning engagement by shifting students’ focus from performance outcomes to learning processes. Educators can further help students set personally meaningful goals, manage setbacks constructively, and internalize the learning potential of difficulties. Finally, at the institutional level, schools are encouraged to integrate growth mindset principles into daily teaching practices, curriculum design, and teacher professional development. By establishing an educational environment that consistently emphasizes the malleability of ability, the value of effort, and the importance of challenge, schools can more effectively translate these theoretical insights into sustainable improvements in students’ psychological well-being.

This study also has some limitations that need to be further improved in future research. First, although practical for data collection, the use of a convenience sampling method may limit the generalizability of the findings, as participants were recruited from specific schools in Jiangsu and Shandong, which may not fully represent the broader population of Chinese adolescents. Future studies would benefit from employing randomized or stratified sampling strategies to enhance external validity. Second, the cross-sectional nature of the design means that the relationships observed between variables are associative, and causal inferences cannot be established. Although prior theoretical and empirical research provides a solid foundation for the hypothesized model, future research utilizing experimental or longitudinal designs would be valuable to further examine the causal mechanisms among the variables. Third, the reliance on self-reported measures, while common in the field, may introduce the potential for common method bias, despite our statistical checks indicating it was not a significant issue in this dataset. Incorporating multi-source data (e.g., teacher reports, behavioral observations) in future work would help to enhance the robustness of the findings. Finally, although the parallel mediation model is consistent with the data and theory, it is worth noting other plausible mechanisms. For example, a sequential pathway whereby growth mindset strengthens grit, which facilitates learning engagement, in turn elevating psychological well-being, also merits theoretical consideration. Future research could employ longitudinal or experimental designs to directly compare the parallel and serial mediation models to unravel the more nuanced temporal and causal relationships among these constructs.

Conclusions

This study demonstrates that growth mindset enhances adolescent psychological well-being through both direct and indirect pathways. The main findings indicate that: (1) Growth mindset significantly and positively predicts psychological well-being among adolescents, confirming the foundational role of malleability beliefs in adolescent mental health; (2) Grit mediates the relationship between growth mindset and adolescent psychological well-being, suggesting that part of its benefit arises from fostering sustained effort and long-term goal commitment; (3) Learning engagement also serves as a mediator, indicating that growth mindset further contributes to well-being by promoting cognitive and emotional involvement in learning. Collectively, these results underscore the practical value of adopting a combined educational approach that integrates growth mindset principles, grit cultivation, and engaging pedagogical strategies to effectively support adolescents’ psychological well-being.
