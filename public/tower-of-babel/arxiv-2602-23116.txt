Regularized Online RLHF with Generalized Bilinear Preferences
Junghyun Lee, Minju Hong, Kwang-Sung Jun, Chulhee Yun, Se-Young Yun

Abstract
We consider the problem of contextual online RLHF with general preferences, where the goal is to identify the Nash Equilibrium. We adopt the Generalized Bilinear Preference Model (GBPM) to capture potentially intransitive preferences via low-rank, skew-symmetric matrices. We investigate general preference learning with any strongly convex regularizer and regularization strength η − 1 \eta^{-1} , generalizing beyond prior work limited to reverse KL-regularization. Central to our analysis is proving that the dual gap of the greedy policy is bounded by the square of the estimation error, a result derived solely from strong convexity and the skew-symmetry of GBPM. Building on this insight and a feature diversity assumption, we establish two regret bounds via two simple algorithms: (1) Greedy Sampling achieves polylogarithmic, e 𝒪 ⁡ ( η ) e^{{\mathcal{O}}(\eta)} -free regret 𝒪 ~ ​ ( η ​ d 4 ​ ( log ⁡ T ) 2 ) \tilde{{\mathcal{O}}}(\eta d^{4}(\log T)^{2}) . (2) Explore-Then-Commit achieves poly ⁡ ( d ) \mathrm{poly}(d) -free regret 𝒪 ~ ​ ( η ​ r ​ T ) \tilde{{\mathcal{O}}}(\sqrt{\eta rT}) by exploiting the low-rank structure; this is the first statistically efficient guarantee for online RLHF in high-dimensions.

Introduction

RLHF Theory.

Aligning large language models (LLMs) with human values has emerged as a central challenge in modern AI, driven by the success of models like Llama, Qwen, and GPT-4
(
81
;
99
;
94
)
, which rely heavily on human preference feedback
(
26
;
96
)
.
This framework, known as
Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF)
, necessitates a rigorous statistical foundation.
The theoretical landscape of RLHF and Preference-based RL (PbRL;
128
) has advanced rapidly, primarily under the Bradley-Terry-Luce (BTL) model
(
18
;
98
)
, where each item
i
possesses a latent reward (utility)
r_{i}\in{\mathbb{R}}
.

A standard approach is to model this reward linearly as
r_{i}=\langle\bm{\phi}_{i},\bm{\theta}_{\star}\rangle
.
Because this formulation naturally mirrors (contextual) linear bandits
(
1
;
27
;
75
)
and linear MDPs
(
60
)
, it enables the direct adaptation of powerful tools from the bandit literature to interactive decision-making problems.
Consequently, these techniques have been successfully extended to tackle various facets of LLM alignment, including dueling bandits and RL
(
92
;
153
;
107
;
132
;
142
;
78
)
, active learning from human feedback
(
30
;
56
;
130
)
, and principled exploration strategies
(
40
;
120
)
.
Despite their simplicity, such (generalized) linear model-based frameworks remain crucial for deriving concrete algorithmic intuition and often serve as the starting point for theoretically principled algorithm design.
Furthermore, these foundational works have paved the way for more expressive, abstract function-approximation frameworks
(
135
;
150
;
149
;
129
;
141
;
52
)
.

General Preference Learning.

Despite its success, reward-based RLHF is fundamentally limited in modeling complex, cyclic preferences prevalent in human psychology
(
83
;
121
)
, as well as diverse human preferences
(
7
;
88
)
.
This calls for
General Preference Learning
(or
Nash Learning
), which targets the Nash Equilibrium (NE) directly without assuming an underlying utility
(
89
;
122
;
84
;
88
)
, and has demonstrated empirical promise in LLMs
(
139
;
5
;
29
;
102
)
.
Broadly, research in this domain can be divided into two perspectives: optimization and statistical learning.
The majority of recent progress has focused on the optimization perspective
(
113
;
88
;
117
;
148
;
147
;
126
;
152
)
: specifically, how to find the NE in a computationally efficient manner given a
known
game
(
91
)
.
However, in contrast to reward-based RLHF, the statistical perspective of general preference learning remains sparse. Here, the game is
unknown
, requiring the learner to estimate the underlying preference model while simultaneously identifying the NE from limited (e.g., bandit) feedback.
The limited literature in this setting primarily relies on general function approximation
(
25
;
127
;
139
;
129
)
or restricts focus to tabular matrix games
(
50
;
97
;
53
)
.

While recent works by
137
;
90
have explored linear function approximation, they rely on
item pair-wise
features and a linear link function.
Beyond the restriction to a linear link, the assumption of access to valid item pair-wise features
(
57
;
35
)
is often impractical, as elaborated in
145
.
First, this necessitates
\binom{K}{2}
features for
K
items, compared to just
K
for item-wise features.
Second, constructing pairwise features that ensure an inherently antisymmetric preference model is often ad-hoc and nontrivial; for instance, supervised preference models often exhibit positional bias (e.g., preferring the first response) when inputs are concatenated
(
109
)
.
Thus, analogous to the linear contextual BTL model, it is highly desirable from both theoretical and empirical perspectives to study general preference learning using
item-wise
features.

Generalized Bilinear Preference Model (GBPM).

To bridge this gap, we adopt the
Generalized Bilinear Preference Model (GBPM)
, introduced by
69
;
145
: given
item-wise
features
\bm{\phi}^{1},\bm{\phi}^{2}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
, the preference probability is modeled as

P^{*}(\bm{\phi}^{1}\succ\bm{\phi}^{2}):=\mu\left((\bm{\phi}^{1})^{\top}\bm{\Theta}_{\star}\bm{\phi}^{2}\right),

(1)

where
\mu(\cdot)
is a link function satisfying
\mu(z)+\mu(-z)=1
, and
\bm{\Theta}_{\star}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d\times d}
is a potentially low-rank,
skew-symmetric
matrix.
This extends linear BTL: the bilinear form captures pairwise relationships as in bilinear bandits
(
61
)
, and skew-symmetry ensures that
P^{*}
is anti-symmetric.

Problem Setting

2.1
(Self-Play) Interaction Protocol

We consider a game defined by a ground-truth, contextual preference model
P^{*}({\bm{a}}^{1}\succ{\bm{a}}^{2}\mid{\bm{x}})
, where
{\bm{x}}\in{\mathcal{X}}
is a context (question), and
{\bm{a}}^{1},{\bm{a}}^{2}\in{\mathcal{A}}
are two actions (responses). The notation
{\bm{a}}^{1}\succ{\bm{a}}^{2}\mid{\bm{x}}
indicates that response
{\bm{a}}^{1}
is preferred to
{\bm{a}}^{2}
given
{\bm{x}}
.
Crucially,
P^{*}
is anti-symmetric:
P^{*}({\bm{a}}^{1}\succ{\bm{a}}^{2})+P^{*}({\bm{a}}^{2}\succ{\bm{a}}^{1})=1
.
A policy is a mapping
\pi:{\mathcal{X}}\rightarrow\Delta({\mathcal{A}})
, where
\pi(\cdot|{\bm{x}})
denotes the conditional distribution over actions. We denote the class of all such policies by
\Pi
, and define the
\ell_{1}
-distance on
\Pi
as follows:

\left\lVert\pi-\pi^{\prime}\right\rVert_{1}:=\sum_{({\bm{x}},{\bm{a}})\in{\mathcal{X}}\times{\mathcal{A}}}|\pi({\bm{a}}|{\bm{x}})-\pi^{\prime}({\bm{a}}|{\bm{x}})|.

(2)

We adopt the
self-play
framework, where the learner controls
both
players to learn by playing against itself. Since the breakthrough of AlphaGo
(
111
;
110
)
, self-play has been extensively studied in the RL theory literature
(
9
;
8
;
80
;
59
;
136
)
, and recently in LLM alignment
(
114
;
133
;
147
)
.
A key feature of this framework is that the learner can achieve high performance even in the absence of an expert or adversarial opponent.

Interaction Protocol.

We define the preference between
\pi^{1}
(
max-player
) and
\pi^{2}
(
min-player
) given
{\bm{x}}\in{\mathcal{X}}
as:

P^{*}(\pi^{1}\succ\pi^{2}\mid{\bm{x}}):=\mathbb{E}_{\begin{subarray}{c}{\bm{a}}^{1}\sim\pi^{1}(\cdot|{\bm{x}})\\
{\bm{a}}^{2}\sim\pi^{2}(\cdot|{\bm{x}})\end{subarray}}\left[P^{*}({\bm{a}}^{1}\succ{\bm{a}}^{2}\mid{\bm{x}})\right].

Given a fixed context distribution
d_{0}\in\Delta({\mathcal{X}})
(unknown to the learner), we denote the population preference as
P^{*}(\pi^{1}\succ\pi^{2}):=\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{x}}\sim d_{0}}[P^{*}(\pi^{1}\succ\pi^{2}|{\bm{x}})]
.

The online contextual RLHF protocol proceeds as follows: At each
t=1,\dots,T
, a context
{\bm{x}}_{t}\sim d_{0}
is revealed. The learner chooses policies
{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\hat{\pi}_{t}^{1}(\cdot|{\bm{x}}_{t})}
and
{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\hat{\pi}_{t}^{2}(\cdot|{\bm{x}}_{t})}
, samples actions
{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{\bm{a}}_{t}^{1}}
and
{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}{\bm{a}}_{t}^{2}}
, and receives a bandit feedback
r_{t}\sim\mathrm{Ber}(P^{*}({\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{\bm{a}}_{t}^{1}}\succ{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}{\bm{a}}_{t}^{2}}\mid{\bm{x}}_{t}))
.
This constitutes a contextual symmetric two-player zero-sum game
(
10
)
with bandit feedback.

Feature Diversity.

We consider the following assumption, considered in prior literature on contextual bandits (see
Theorem

3.1
and
Section

6
for more discussions):

Assumption 1

(Feature Map and Diversity)
.

The learner has access to a known feature map
\phi:{\mathcal{X}}\times{\mathcal{A}}\rightarrow{\mathcal{B}}^{d}(1)
and an exploration policy
\rho(\cdot|{\bm{x}})
such that for a
C_{\min}>0
:

\lambda_{\min}\left(\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{x}}\sim d_{0}}\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{a}}\sim\rho(\cdot|{\bm{x}})}\left[\phi({\bm{x}},{\bm{a}})\phi({\bm{x}},{\bm{a}})^{\top}\right]\right)\geq C_{\min}.

(3)

Remark 2.1

(Scaling of
C_{\min}
)
.

Well-conditioned sets (e.g., hypercubes) allow
C_{\min}\asymp 1
, while for ill-conditioned sets (e.g., standard basis),
C_{\min}^{-1}\asymp d^{-1}
or worse.
In this paper, our regime of interest is when
C_{\min}^{-1}\asymp 1
, although for completeness, we keep track of this quantity throughout.

2.2
Generalized Bilinear Preference Model (GBPM)

We now introduce the low-rank contextual general preference model that we consider in this work.

First, we define
\mathrm{Skew}(d;2r,S)
as the following:

\left\{\bm{\Theta}\in\mathrm{Skew}(d):\mathrm{rank}(\bm{\Theta})\leq 2r,\left\lVert\bm{\Theta}\right\rVert_{\mathrm{nuc}}\leq S\right\},

(4)

where
\mathrm{Skew}(d):=\left\{\bm{\Theta}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d\times d}:\bm{\Theta}^{\top}=-\bm{\Theta}\right\}.

Now the definition of
GBPM

(
145
;
69
)
:

Definition 2.2

(
Generalized Bilinear Preference Model
)
.

Let rank
r\leq\lfloor d/2\rfloor
and norm bound
S>0
be known. For any
{\bm{x}}\in{\mathcal{X}},{\bm{a}}^{1},{\bm{a}}^{2}\in{\mathcal{A}}
, the ground-truth preference under
GBPM
is:

P^{*}({\bm{a}}^{1}\succ{\bm{a}}^{2}\mid{\bm{x}}):=\mu\left(\phi({\bm{x}},{\bm{a}}^{1})^{\top}\bm{\Theta}_{\star}\phi({\bm{x}},{\bm{a}}^{2})\right),

where
\bm{\Theta}_{\star}\in\mathrm{Skew}(d;2r,S)
is an unknown
skew-symmetric, low-rank
matrix, and
\mu:\mathbb{R}\rightarrow[0,1]
is a known link satisfying: for some
\kappa>0
,

1.

\mu
is twice differentiable, monotone increasing, and symmetric (
\mu(z)+\mu(-z)=1
).

2.

\kappa\leq\dot{\mu}\left(\phi^{\top}\bm{\Theta}\phi^{\prime}\right)\leq L_{\mu},\ \left|\ddot{\mu}\left(\phi^{\top}\bm{\Theta}\phi^{\prime}\right)\right|\leq L_{\mu},\ \ \forall\bm{\phi},\bm{\phi}^{\prime}\in{\mathcal{B}}^{d}(1),\bm{\Theta}\in\mathrm{Skew}(d;2r,S)
.
2
2

2

We assume the same constant
L_{\mu}
for the upper bounds of
\dot{\mu}
and
|\ddot{\mu}|
for simplicity of the exposition.

The conditions for
\mu
are standard in logistic and generalized linear (dueling) bandits
(
38
;
2
;
71
;
70
;
69
;
132
;
13
)
.
The logistic link
\mu(z)=(1+e^{-z})^{-1}
satisfies the above with
R_{s}=1
and
L_{\mu}=\frac{1}{4}
.
The linear link
\mu(z)=\tfrac{1}{2}+z
is also covered, in which case
R_{s}=0
and
L_{\mu}=1

(
45
;
132
)
.

Throughout, we denote
J(\bm{\phi}_{t}^{1},\bm{\phi}_{t}^{2};\bm{\Theta}):=\mu((\bm{\phi}_{t}^{1})^{\top}\bm{\Theta}\bm{\phi}_{t}^{2})
and its expectation w.r.t.
{\bm{x}}_{t}\sim d_{0}
and
\bm{\phi}_{t}^{i}\sim\pi^{i}
as
J(\pi^{1},\pi^{2};\bm{\Theta})
.
We also denote
J(\cdot,\cdot):=J(\cdot,\cdot;\bm{\Theta}_{\star})
.

2.3
Regularized NE and Regret Definitions

For a
{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}\eta}\in(0,\infty]
and a
\beta^{-1}
-strongly convex regularizer
\psi:\Pi\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}_{\geq 0}
w.r.t.
\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert_{1}
, we define a
symmetric
, regularized game objective
J_{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}\eta}:\Pi\times\Pi\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}
as follows:

J_{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}\eta}(\pi,\pi^{\prime};\bm{\Theta}):=J(\pi,\pi^{\prime};\bm{\Theta})-{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}\eta^{-1}}\psi(\pi)+{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}\eta^{-1}}\psi(\pi^{\prime}).

Standard solution concepts (e.g., Condorcet winners) may not exist in general preference learning
(
36
;
12
;
88
;
114
)
.
Thus, as in many recent literature in online RLHF
(
88
)
, we consider
(regularized) Nash Equilibrium (NE)

(
89
;
84
)
:

Definition 2.3

(Nash Equilibrium)
.

A pair
(\pi^{1}_{\star},\pi^{2}_{\star})\in\Pi\times\Pi
is a
Nash equilibrium (NE)
if for all
\pi^{1},\pi^{2}\in\Pi
:

J_{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}\eta}(\pi^{1},\pi^{2}_{\star})\leq J_{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}\eta}(\pi^{1}_{\star},\pi^{2}_{\star})\leq J_{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}\eta}(\pi^{1}_{\star},\pi^{2}).

If
\pi^{1}_{\star}=\pi^{2}_{\star}=:\pi_{\star}
, we refer to
\pi_{\star}
as a
symmetric NE (SNE)
.
By the minimax theorem
(
122
;
112
)
, any
SNE

\pi_{\star}
is equivalently characterized as follows:

\pi^{\star}\in\argmax_{\pi^{1}\in\Pi}\min_{\pi^{2}\in\Pi}J_{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}\eta}(\pi^{1},\pi^{2}).

(5)

We also have the following important technical lemma, which will be critically used later:

Lemma 2.4
.

For any
\bm{\Theta}\in\mathrm{Skew}(d)
, the value of the game,
\max_{\pi^{1}}\min_{\pi^{2}}J_{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}\eta}(\pi^{1},\pi^{2};\bm{\Theta})
, is always
\frac{1}{2}
.

Proof.

For any
\bm{\Theta}
, there always exists a
SNE

(
114
, Lemma 2.1)
.
As the game value of
SNE
is
\frac{1}{2}
, it must be so for any
NE

(
122
;
112
)
.
∎

We define the
(symmetric) dual gap
of a policy
\hat{\pi}\in\Pi
as

\mathrm{DGap}_{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}\eta}(\hat{\pi}):=\frac{1}{2}-\min_{\pi^{2}\in\Pi}J_{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}\eta}(\hat{\pi},\pi^{2}).

(6)

Intuitively, this quantifies how close
\hat{\pi}
is to an
SNE
.

We evaluate any resulting policy sequence
\{({\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\hat{\pi}_{t}^{1}},{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\hat{\pi}_{t}^{2}})\}_{t\in[T]}
using the following two regrets:

Definition 2.5
.

For any
\{({\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\hat{\pi}_{t}^{1}},{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\hat{\pi}_{t}^{2}})\}_{t\in[T]}
,

(a) Average-Best-Response Regret:

\mathrm{ABR\text{-}Reg}_{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}\eta}(T):=\sum_{t=1}^{T}\left\{\mathrm{DGap}_{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}\eta}({\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\hat{\pi}_{t}^{1}})+\mathrm{DGap}_{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}\eta}({\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\hat{\pi}_{t}^{2}})\right\}.

(b) Max-Best-Response Regret
:

\mathrm{MBR\text{-}Reg}_{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}\eta}(T):=\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathrm{DGap}_{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}\eta}({\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\hat{\pi}_{t}^{1}}).

Remark 2.6
.

These regrets are stronger than those considered in the standard adversarial bandit literature. The above regrets can be converted to sample complexities for finding an NE via online-to-batch conversion
(
44
)
; see
Appendix

D
for detailed discussions.

A New Analysis of Regularized Regret

We present our main technical contribution: a new bound on the instantaneous dual gap of any greedy NE policy for the max-player.
Denoting
\bm{\phi}\sim\pi
as sampling a
\bm{\phi}({\bm{x}},{\bm{a}})\in{\mathcal{B}}^{d}(1)
from
{\bm{a}}\sim\pi(\cdot|{\bm{x}})
and
{\bm{x}}\sim d_{0}
,

Theorem 3.1
.

For
any
estimator
\widehat{\bm{\Theta}}_{t}\in\mathrm{Skew}(d)
at time

Relations to Prior Works

For the remainder of this section, for simplicity, we assume that
{\mathcal{X}}=\{{\bm{x}}\}
and omit any dependency on the context.

Contextual General Preference Learning.

Real-world contexts exhibit high diversity, spanning recommender systems
(
74
)
to mobile edge computing
(
24
)
.
While recent works have introduced contextual bandit frameworks for general preference learning
(
137
;
90
;
132
)
, they predominantly rely on
item pair-wise
feature maps (linearizing payoffs as
\langle\varphi({\bm{a}}^{1},{\bm{a}}^{2}),{\bm{\theta}}\rangle
for each pair of actions
{\bm{a}}^{1},{\bm{a}}^{2}\in{\mathcal{A}}
) or tabular structures
(
97
)
; the former is conceptually similar to
132
, though the connection is not explicitly drawn in the literature.
This formulation contrasts with practical RLHF scenarios where only
item-wise
features
\phi({\bm{a}})
are available, further motivating our choice of
GBPM
as the primary object of study.
Additionally, unlike their approaches which scale with
|{\mathcal{A}}|^{2}
(rendering them intractable for infinite
{\mathcal{A}}
), we only track the NE policy and the
d\times d
parameter, which is efficient.

Beyond KL-Regularization via Self-Bounding Analysis.

The theoretical landscape of RLHF is currently dominated by reverse KL-regularization
(
135
;
150
;
149
;
129
)
, often leveraging KL-specific properties of the exponential family (e.g., bounded log-density ratios) to enable oracle reductions to least square regressions
(

;

;
143
)
; see
Appendix

F
for an illustration where we rigorously instantiate the regret bound of
129
to
GBPM
.
We show that the specific geometry of KL is not strictly necessary for fast rates; rather, the
strong convexity
of the regularizer drives our results.
This covers various regularizers: Shannon entropy
(
84
;
85
;
21
)
, Tsallis entropy
(
118
;

;

;

)
,

(

(

The key theme of our proof is a

(Eqn. (

(

that exploits the landscape curvature to penalize deviations.
The high-level intuition is similar, but with different technical components: such strong convexity ensures that the deviations are penalized more heavily, enabling tighter control over the dual gap (

While generic formulations have appeared in recent works such as

Crucially, our analysis unifies these diverse regularizers in the online setting: we demonstrate that

Similar assumptions on the diversity of the feature mapping have been considered in the vast literature on greedy sampling for contextual bandits
(

Under such conditions, in which exploration is naturally facilitated, near-optimal performance of order

We further note that the literature on semi-parametric generalized linear bandits relies on similar assumptions
(

;

to facilitate exploration via
T

Crucially, this assumption is standard in the high-dimensional contextual bandit scenario
(

(

In this regime, the intuition is that sufficient exploration of the feature space is requisite to identify the underlying low-rank structure to be exploited.
We lastly remark that for specific choices of the regularizer

7
Conclusion

We have established a statistical framework for regularized online RLHF under the Generalized Bilinear Preference Model (GBPM) for
any
choice of strongly convex regularizer, which subsumes the standard reverse KL-regularization.
By leveraging the skew-symmetry of the preferences and the strong convexity of the regularizer, we derive a novel self-bounding quadratic inequality for the dual gap, which serves as the cornerstone of our analysis.
Under the feature diversity assumption, we proved that a simple
Greedy Sampling
strategy attains a polylogarithmic regret of
\tilde{\mathcal{O}}({\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}\eta}d^{4}(\log T)^{2})
without
{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}\exp({\mathcal{O}}(\eta))}
dependency, partially addressing an open problem by
129
.
Furthermore, addressing the high-dimensional regime, we demonstrated that
Explore-Then-Commit
achieves a
\text{poly}(d)
-free regret of
\tilde{\mathcal{O}}(\sqrt{{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}\eta}rT})
by effectively exploiting the low-rank structure of the underlying preference matrix.
We believe that this opens up various future directions, which we discuss in detail in
Appendix

H
.

B.2
Proof of
Lemma

B.2
: Coverage Lemma

We begin with this simple yet effective matrix lemma that will be useful throughout (we provide its proof at
Section

B.3
for completeness):

Lemma B.4
.

If
{\bm{A}}\succeq{\bm{0}}
and
{\bm{B}}\succeq{\bm{C}}\succeq{\bm{0}}
of compatible sizes, then
{\bm{A}}\otimes{\bm{B}}\succeq{\bm{A}}\otimes{\bm{C}}
and
\tr({\bm{A}}{\bm{B}})\geq\tr({\bm{A}}{\bm{C}})
.

We begin from the expectation on the RHS and work our way to the LHS:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\tilde{\bm{\phi}}\sim\rho}\left[(\bm{\phi}\otimes\tilde{\bm{\phi}})^{\top}{\bm{M}}(\bm{\phi}\otimes\tilde{\bm{\phi}})\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\tilde{\bm{\phi}}\sim\rho}\left[\tr\left((\bm{\phi}\otimes\tilde{\bm{\phi}})^{\top}{\bm{M}}(\bm{\phi}\otimes\tilde{\bm{\phi}})\right)\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\tilde{\bm{\phi}}\sim\rho}\left[\tr\left({\bm{M}}(\bm{\phi}\otimes\tilde{\bm{\phi}})(\bm{\phi}\otimes\tilde{\bm{\phi}})^{\top}\right)\right]

(Cyclic property of
\tr(\cdot)
)

\displaystyle=\tr\left({\bm{M}}\mathbb{E}_{\tilde{\bm{\phi}}\sim\rho}\left[(\bm{\phi}\otimes\tilde{\bm{\phi}})(\bm{\phi}\otimes\tilde{\bm{\phi}})^{\top}\right]\right)

(Linearity of expectation)

\displaystyle=\tr\left({\bm{M}}\mathbb{E}_{\tilde{\bm{\phi}}\sim\rho}\left[\bm{\phi}\bm{\phi}^{\top}\otimes\tilde{\bm{\phi}}\tilde{\bm{\phi}}^{\top}\right]\right).

(Mixed-product property of
\otimes
)

Now, let us examine
\mathbb{E}_{\tilde{\bm{\phi}}\sim\rho}\left[\bm{\phi}\bm{\phi}^{\top}\otimes\tilde{\bm{\phi}}\tilde{\bm{\phi}}^{\top}\right]
.
Due to the linearity of the expectation and our assumption, we have

\mathbb{E}_{\tilde{\bm{\phi}}\sim\rho}\left[\bm{\phi}\bm{\phi}^{\top}\otimes\tilde{\bm{\phi}}\tilde{\bm{\phi}}^{\top}\right]=\bm{\phi}\bm{\phi}^{\top}\otimes\mathbb{E}_{\tilde{\bm{\phi}}\sim\rho}\left[\tilde{\bm{\phi}}\tilde{\bm{\phi}}^{\top}\right]\succeq C_{\min}\bm{\phi}\bm{\phi}^{\top}\otimes{\bm{I}}_{d},

where the last Lowener order follows from
Lemma

B.4
.
Then, again by
Lemma

B.4
, we have that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\tilde{\bm{\phi}}\sim\rho}\left[(\bm{\phi}\otimes\tilde{\bm{\phi}})^{\top}{\bm{M}}(\bm{\phi}\otimes\tilde{\bm{\phi}})\right]

\displaystyle=\tr\left({\bm{M}}\bm{\phi}\bm{\phi}^{\top}\otimes\mathbb{E}_{\tilde{\bm{\phi}}\sim\rho}\left[\tilde{\bm{\phi}}\tilde{\bm{\phi}}^{\top}\right]\right)

\displaystyle\geq C_{\min}\tr\left({\bm{M}}\bm{\phi}\bm{\phi}^{\top}\otimes{\bm{I}}_{d}\right)

\displaystyle=C_{\min}\tr\left({\bm{M}}\bm{\phi}\bm{\phi}^{\top}\otimes\left(\sum_{j=1}^{d}{\bm{e}}_{j}{\bm{e}}_{j}^{\top}\right)\right)

\displaystyle=C_{\min}\sum_{j=1}^{d}\tr\left({\bm{M}}\bm{\phi}\bm{\phi}^{\top}\otimes{\bm{e}}_{j}{\bm{e}}_{j}^{\top}\right)

\displaystyle=C_{\min}\sum_{j=1}^{d}\tr\left({\bm{M}}(\bm{\phi}\otimes{\bm{e}}_{j})(\bm{\phi}\otimes{\bm{e}}_{j})^{\top}\right)

(Mixed-product property of
\otimes
)

\displaystyle=C_{\min}\sum_{j=1}^{d}(\bm{\phi}\otimes{\bm{e}}_{j})^{\top}{\bm{M}}(\bm{\phi}\otimes{\bm{e}}_{j}).

∎

B.3
Proof of
Lemma

B.4
: PSD Matrix Lemma

Let
{\bm{D}}:={\bm{B}}-{\bm{C}}
. Since
{\bm{B}}\succeq{\bm{C}}
, we have
{\bm{D}}\succeq{\bm{0}}
.

We first show that
{\bm{A}}\otimes{\bm{B}}\succeq{\bm{A}}\otimes{\bm{C}}
. By bilinearity of the Kronecker product,

{\bm{A}}\otimes{\bm{B}}-{\bm{A}}\otimes{\bm{C}}={\bm{A}}\otimes({\bm{B}}-{\bm{C}})={\bm{A}}\otimes{\bm{D}}.

It therefore suffices to prove that
{\bm{A}}\otimes{\bm{D}}
is positive semidefinite.

Let
\{\lambda_{i}({\bm{A}})\}_{i=1}^{n}
and
\{\lambda_{j}({\bm{D}})\}_{j=1}^{m}
denote the eigenvalues of
{\bm{A}}
and
{\bm{D}}
, respectively. A standard property of Kronecker products implies that the eigenvalues of
{\bm{A}}\otimes{\bm{D}}
are

\bigl\{\lambda_{i}({\bm{A}})\lambda_{j}({\bm{D}})\bigr\}_{i\in[n],\,j\in[m]}.

Since
{\bm{A}}\succeq{\bm{0}}
and
{\bm{D}}\succeq{\bm{0}}
, all eigenvalues
\lambda_{i}({\bm{A}})
and
\lambda_{j}({\bm{D}})
are nonnegative, hence so are all products
\lambda_{i}({\bm{A}})\lambda_{j}({\bm{D}})
. Therefore
{\bm{A}}\otimes{\bm{D}}\succeq{\bm{0}}
, which yields
{\bm{A}}\otimes{\bm{B}}\succeq{\bm{A}}\otimes{\bm{C}}
.

Now we prove
\tr({\bm{A}}{\bm{B}})\geq\tr({\bm{A}}{\bm{C}})
. Since
{\bm{A}}\succeq 0
, there exists a symmetric square root
{\bm{A}}^{1/2}
such that

{\bm{A}}={\bm{A}}^{1/2}{\bm{A}}^{1/2}
. Using cyclicity of trace,

\tr({\bm{A}}{\bm{D}})=\tr({\bm{A}}^{1/2}{\bm{A}}^{1/2}{\bm{D}})=\tr({\bm{A}}^{1/2}{\bm{D}}{\bm{A}}^{1/2}).

Moreover,
{\bm{A}}^{1/2}{\bm{D}}{\bm{A}}^{1/2}
is positive semidefinite. For any
{\bm{x}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
letting
{\bm{y}}:={\bm{A}}^{1/2}{\bm{x}}
, we have

{\bm{x}}^{\top}{\bm{A}}^{1/2}{\bm{D}}{\bm{A}}^{1/2}{\bm{x}}={\bm{y}}^{\top}{\bm{D}}{\bm{y}}\geq 0,

where the inequality uses
{\bm{D}}\succeq{\bm{0}}
. Hence
{\bm{A}}^{1/2}{\bm{D}}{\bm{A}}^{1/2}\succeq{\bm{0}}
, and therefore

\tr({\bm{A}}^{1/2}{\bm{D}}{\bm{A}}^{1/2})\geq 0
. This implies
\tr({\bm{A}}{\bm{D}})\geq 0
, completing the proof.

\hat{\pi}_{t}

Case I.

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{T_{0}}\sum_{t=1}^{T_{0}}\langle\Delta,{\bm{X}}_{t}\rangle^{2}\int_{0}^{1}(1-z)\underbrace{m^{\prime\prime}\left(\langle\bm{\Theta}_{\star}+z\Delta,{\bm{X}}_{t}\rangle\right)}_{=\dot{\mu}\left(\langle\bm{\Theta}_{\star}+z\Delta,{\bm{X}}_{t}\rangle\right)}dz.

We now divide into two cases:

\mu
is self-concordant.

If this is the case, then we can utilize the following self-concordance control lemma:

Lemma C.5

(Lemma 9 of
2
)
.

For any
R_{s}
-self-concordant
\mu
that is monotone increasing, the following holds: for any
z_{1},z_{2}\in{\mathbb{R}}
,

\dot{\mu}(z_{2})\exp(-R_{s}|z_{1}-z_{2}|)\leq\dot{\mu}(z_{1})\leq\dot{\mu}(z_{2})\exp(R_{s}|z_{1}-z_{2}|).

(32)

We choose
R=\frac{1}{\sqrt{d}}
, which implies that by Hölder’s inequality,
|\langle\Delta,{\bm{X}}_{t}\rangle|\leq 1
.
Thus, by the above self-concordance lemma,

\int_{0}^{1}(1-z)\dot{\mu}\left(\langle\bm{\Theta}_{\star}+z\Delta,{\bm{X}}_{t}\rangle\right)dz\geq\int_{0}^{1}(1-z)e^{-R_{s}z}\dot{\mu}\left(\langle\bm{\Theta}_{\star},{\bm{X}}_{t}\rangle\right)dz\geq\kappa_{\star}\tilde{R}_{s},

where we define the constant

\tilde{R}_{s}:=\begin{cases}\frac{1}{2},&\quad$R_{s}=0$\\
\frac{R_{s}-1+e^{-R_{s}}}{R_{s}^{2}},&\quad$R_{s}>0$.\end{cases}

Otherwise.

If not, then we simply lower bound

\int_{0}^{1}(1-z)\dot{\mu}\left(\langle\bm{\Theta}_{\star}+z\Delta,{\bm{X}}_{t}\rangle\right)dz\geq\kappa\int_{0}^{1}(1-z)dz=\frac{\kappa}{2}.

With a slight overload of notation, if self-concordance does not hold, we define
\tilde{R}_{s}=\frac{1}{2}
.

Concluding the proof.

Let us define the vectorized population and empirical design matrices:

{\bm{V}}:=\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{X}}}\left[\mathrm{\mathrm{vec}}({\bm{X}})\mathrm{\mathrm{vec}}({\bm{X}})^{\top}\right],\quad\widehat{{\bm{V}}}:=\frac{1}{T_{0}}\sum_{t=1}^{T_{0}}\mathrm{vec}({\bm{X}}_{t})\mathrm{vec}({\bm{X}}_{t})^{\top}.

(33)

Observe that

\displaystyle{\bm{V}}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\bm{\phi}^{1},\bm{\phi}^{2}}\left[(\bm{\phi}^{2}\otimes\bm{\phi}^{1})(\bm{\phi}^{2}\otimes\bm{\phi}^{1})^{\top}\right]

(
\bm{\phi}^{1}\overset{d}{=}\bm{\phi}^{2}

and thus,
\lambda_{\min}\left({\bm{V}}\right)=\lambda_{\min}\left(\mathbb{E}_{\bm{\phi}}\left[\bm{\phi}\bm{\phi}^{\top}\right]\right)^{2}\geq C_{\min}^{2}
by

Thus, we have that

\displaystyle{\mathcal{L}}_{T_{0}}(\bm{\Theta}_{\star}+\Delta)-{\mathcal{L}}_{T_{0}}(\bm{\Theta}_{\star})-\langle\nabla{\mathcal{L}}_{T_{0}}(\bm{\Theta}_{\star}),\Delta\rangle

\displaystyle\geq\tilde{R}_{s}\tilde{\kappa}\mathrm{vec}(\Delta)^{\top}\widehat{{\bm{V}}}\mathrm{vec}(\Delta)

\displaystyle=\tilde{R}_{s}\tilde{\kappa}\mathrm{vec}(\Delta)^{\top}{\bm{V}}\mathrm{vec}(\Delta)+\tilde{R}_{s}\tilde{\kappa}\mathrm{vec}(\Delta)^{\top}(\widehat{{\bm{V}}}-{\bm{V}})\mathrm{vec}(\Delta)

Appendix D
Discussions on Regrets

D.1
Four Regret Definitions and Discussions

A standard measure of performance in online learning is
regret
. However, because the interaction is two-player and self-play, there are several ways to define regret, arising from different yet closely related communities: no-regret learning in games, dueling bandits/RL, and RL in two-player zero-sum games.
In the main text, we consider only two regrets (
\mathrm{ABR\text{-}Reg}_{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}\eta}
and
\mathrm{MBR\text{-}Reg}_{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}\eta}
) for brevity, and so in this Appendix, we provide the deferred discussions regarding four regrets:

Definition D.1
.

The four regrets are defined as follows.

(a)

Average-Nash Regret
:

\mathrm{AN\text{-}Reg}_{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}\eta}(T):=\max_{\pi^{1},\pi^{2}\in\Pi}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\left\{J_{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}\eta}(\pi^{1},{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\hat{\pi}_{t}^{2}})-J_{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}\eta}({\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\hat{\pi}_{t}^{1}},\pi^{2})\right\}.

(b)

Average-Best-Response Regret
:

\mathrm{ABR\text{-}Reg}_{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}\eta}(T):=\sum_{t=1}^{T}\max_{\pi^{1},\pi^{2}\in\Pi}\left\{J_{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}\eta}(\pi^{1},{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\hat{\pi}_{t}^{2}})-J_{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}\eta}({\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\hat{\pi}_{t}^{1}},\pi^{2})\right\}.

(c)

Max-Nash Regret
:

\mathrm{MN\text{-}Reg}_{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}\eta}(T):=\max_{\pi\in\Pi}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\left\{\frac{1}{2}-J_{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}\eta}({\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\hat{\pi}_{t}^{1}},\pi)\right\}.

(d)

Max-Best-Response Regret
:

\mathrm{MBR\text{-}Reg}_{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}\eta}(T):=\sum_{t=1}^{T}\max_{\pi\in\Pi}\left\{\frac{1}{2}-J_{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}\eta}({\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\hat{\pi}_{t}^{1}},\pi)\right\}.

The unregularized variants are defined similarly and denoted without the
{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}\eta}
subscript.

Categorization Criteria.

There are two criteria that determine each regret definition.
The first criterion is, at each time
t
, whether to consider the regrets of both players simultaneously, or to consider the regret of the max-player only.
This distinguishes between
Average
or
Max
.
The second criterion is whether to compare against a fixed comparator or to compare against the best response at each time
t
, which is usually time-varying.
This distinguishes between
Nash
and
Best-Response
.

Intuitively, the
Average
regret definitions consider the “suboptimality” of both policies simultaneously. The difference between
Nash
and
Best-Response
is whether the regret is defined w.r.t. a fixed comparator (
Nash
) or a dynamically changing comparator (
Best-Response
).
Thus, in classical literature, they are also known as
external
and
internal (swap)
regrets.

Average Regrets.

\mathrm{AN\text{-}Reg}(T)
is the notion originally considered in the seminal work of
44
, followed by numerous works on no-regret learning dynamics in games
(
31
;
32
;
33
;
100
;
101
;
115
)
, recently adopted to game-theoretic LLM alignment
(
147
)
.

\mathrm{AN\text{-}Reg}(T)
is precisely the regret considered in contextual dueling bandits
(
36
, Eqn. (3))
and dueling RL
(
107
, Eqn. (4))
; indeed, for any
\pi\in\Pi
, utilizing the anti-symmetry of
J(\cdot,\cdot)
, we can rewrite

J(\pi,{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\hat{\pi}_{t}^{2}})-J({\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\hat{\pi}_{t}^{1}},\pi)=J(\pi,{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\hat{\pi}_{t}^{1}})+J(\pi,{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\hat{\pi}_{t}^{2}})-1.

This also slightly resembles Borda regret in dueling bandits
(
105
;
132
)
, and average regret in dueling bandits under linear stochastic transitivity
(
108
;
12
;
13
)
.

On the other hand,
\mathrm{ABR\text{-}Reg}(T)
strongly resembles the notion of
best response regret
in adversarial dueling bandits
(
106
, Eqn. (1))
, but there is a key difference.
In
106
, the comparator at time
t
is the same for both players, whereas in our regret setting, it differs for each player.
Basically, each player must compete with the worst-case (strongest) adversary from her perspective, who chooses the best response from his perspective.

Max Regrets.

The notion of considering the regret of the
max
player dates back to the self-play framework for RL in two-player zero-sum games
(
9
;
8
;
80
;
59
;
136
)
; this idea has been recently applied to theoretical analyses of online RLHF under general preference
(
139
;
129
)
.
Basically, the intuition is that the learner only cares about obtaining the NE policy for the max-player, which is the policy that is actually deployed in practice.

Note that the min-player’s policies
{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\hat{\pi}_{t}^{2}}
do not contribute to the regret at all, and thus, often, the min-player acts as an exploration agent whose sole role is to collect as much information as possible to facilitate the learning of the max-player
(
9
;
8
;
80
;
59
;
136
;
135
;
139
)
.

D.2
Online-to-Batch Conversion

A standard consequence of no-regret learning in repeated zero-sum games is an
online-to-batch conversion
: the time-averaged (mixed) policies form an approximate Nash equilibrium. We formalize this statement in the following proposition.

Proposition D.2

(Online-to-batch conversion)
.

Let
\{(\hat{\pi}_{t}^{1},\hat{\pi}_{t}^{2})\}_{t=1}^{T}\subseteq\Pi\times\Pi
be any policy sequence.
Define the uniform mixture policies as
\bar{\pi}_{T}^{i}:=\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\hat{\pi}_{t}^{i}
for
i\in\{1,2\}
and for simplicity, let us denote
\bar{\pi}_{T}:=\bar{\pi}_{T}^{1}
.

(a) Average regrets.

For average regrets,
(\bar{\pi}_{T}^{1},\bar{\pi}_{T}^{2})
is a
\frac{\mathrm{Reg}(T)}{T}
-approximate symmetric NE:

\max_{\pi^{1},\pi^{2}\in\Pi}\Bigl\{J_{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}\eta}(\pi^{1},\bar{\pi}_{T}^{2})-J_{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}\eta}(\bar{\pi}_{T}^{1},\pi^{2})\Bigr\}\;\leq\;\frac{\mathrm{AN\text{-}Reg}_{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}\eta}(T)}{T}\;\leq\;\frac{\mathrm{ABR\text{-}Reg}_{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}\eta}(T)}{T}.

(b) Max regrets.

For max regrets,
\bar{\pi}_{T}
is a
\frac{2\mathrm{Reg}(T)}{T}
-approximate symmetric NE:

\max_{\pi\in\Pi}\Bigl\{J_{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}\eta}(\pi,\bar{\pi}_{T})-J_{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}\eta}(\bar{\pi}_{T},\pi)\Bigr\}\;\leq\;\frac{2\mathrm{MN\text{-}Reg}_{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}\eta}(T)}{T}\;\leq\;\frac{2\mathrm{MBR\text{-}Reg}_{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}\eta}(T)}{T}.

Proof.

(a)
Fix any
\pi^{1},\pi^{2}\in\Pi
.
By the bilinearity of
J
and Jensen’s inequality w.r.t.
\psi(\cdot)
,

J_{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}\eta}(\pi^{1},\bar{\pi}_{T}^{2})=J(\pi^{1},\bar{\pi}_{T}^{2})-{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}\eta^{-1}}\psi(\pi^{1})+{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}\eta^{-1}}\psi(\bar{\pi}_{T}^{2})\leq\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}J_{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}\eta}(\pi^{1},\hat{\pi}_{t}^{2}),

and similarly,

J_{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}\eta}(\bar{\pi}_{T}^{1},\pi^{2})=J(\bar{\pi}_{T}^{1},\pi^{2})-{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}\eta^{-1}}\psi(\bar{\pi}_{T}^{1})+{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}\eta^{-1}}\psi(\pi^{2})\geq\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}J_{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}\eta}(\hat{\pi}_{t}^{1},\pi^{2}).

Subtracting the two inequalities and taking
\max_{\pi^{1},\pi^{2}}
yields

\displaystyle\max_{\pi^{1},\pi^{2}}\Bigl\{J_{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}\eta}(\pi^{1},\bar{\pi}_{T}^{2})-J_{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}\eta}(\bar{\pi}_{T}^{1},\pi^{2})\Bigr\}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{T}\max_{\pi^{1},\pi^{2}}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\Bigl(J_{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}\eta}(\pi^{1},\hat{\pi}_{t}^{2})-J_{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}\eta}(\hat{\pi}_{t}^{1},\pi^{2})\Bigr)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\max_{\pi^{1},\pi^{2}}\Bigl(J_{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}\eta}(\pi^{1},\hat{\pi}_{t}^{2})-J_{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}\eta}(\hat{\pi}_{t}^{1},\pi^{2})\Bigr)

(b)
By the same averaging argument (bilinearity of
J
and Jensen’s inequality),
for every
\pi\in\Pi
,

J_{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}\eta}(\pi,\bar{\pi}_{T})-J_{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}\eta}(\bar{\pi}_{T},\pi)\leq\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\Bigl(J_{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}\eta}(\pi,\hat{\pi}_{t}^{1})-J_{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}\eta}(\hat{\pi}_{t}^{1},\pi)\Bigr).

Since for each
t
,
J_{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}\eta}(\pi,\hat{\pi}_{t}^{1})-J_{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}\eta}(\hat{\pi}_{t}^{1},\pi)=2\Bigl(\tfrac{1}{2}-J_{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}\eta}(\hat{\pi}_{t}^{1},\pi)\Bigr)
,
taking
\max_{\pi}
and substituting yields

\displaystyle\max_{\pi}\bigl\{J_{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}\eta}(\pi,\bar{\pi}_{T})-J_{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}\eta}(\bar{\pi}_{T},\pi)\bigr\}\leq\frac{2}{T}\max_{\pi}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\Bigl(\tfrac{1}{2}-J_{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}\eta}(\hat{\pi}_{t}^{1},\pi)\Bigr)\leq\frac{2}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\max_{\pi}\Bigl(\tfrac{1}{2}-J_{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}\eta}(\hat{\pi}_{t}^{1},\pi)\Bigr).

∎

E.1
Upper Bounding the Eluder Dimension of

To instantiate the regret bound of

in
Appendix

F
, we first recall their specific notion of the eluder dimension:

Definition E.1

(Eluder dimension, General Preference Model
(
129
)
)
.

Under the general preference model, for any
\mathcal{D}_{t-1}=\{({\bm{x}}_{i},{\bm{a}}^{1}_{i},{\bm{a}}^{2}_{i})\}_{i=1}^{t-1}
, we define the uncertainty of
({\bm{x}},{\bm{a}}^{1},{\bm{a}}^{2})
with respect to
\mathcal{P}
as

U_{\texttt{GP}}(\lambda,{\bm{x}},{\bm{a}}^{1},{\bm{a}}^{2};\mathcal{P},\mathcal{D}_{t-1})=\sup_{P_{1},P_{2}\in\mathcal{P}}\frac{|P_{1}({\bm{a}}^{1}\succ{\bm{a}}^{2}\mid{\bm{x}})-P_{2}({\bm{a}}^{1}\succ{\bm{a}}^{2}\mid{\bm{x}})|}{\sqrt{\lambda+\sum_{s=1}^{t-1}(P_{1}({\bm{a}}^{1}_{s}\succ{\bm{a}}^{2}_{s}\mid{\bm{x}}_{s})-P_{2}({\bm{a}}^{1}_{s}\succ{\bm{a}}^{2}_{s}\mid{\bm{x}}_{s}))^{2}}}.

Then the eluder dimension of
{\mathcal{P}}
is defined as

d(\mathcal{P},\lambda,T):=\sup_{{\bm{x}}_{1:T},{\bm{a}}^{1}_{1:T},{\bm{a}}^{2}_{1:T}}\sum_{t\in[T]}\min\left\{1,\left[U_{\texttt{GP}}(\lambda,{\bm{x}}_{t},{\bm{a}}^{1}_{t},{\bm{a}}^{2}_{t};\mathcal{P},\mathcal{D}_{t-1})\right]^{2}\right\}.

Using the standard elliptical potential arguments, we now derive an upper bound for this complexity measure under the GBPM:

Proposition E.2
.

For GBPM, the eluder dimension
d(\mathcal{P},\lambda,T)
is bounded as

d(\mathcal{P},\lambda,T)\leq\frac{2d^{2}L_{\mu}^{2}}{\kappa^{2}}\log\left(1+\frac{4\kappa^{2}S^{2}T}{d^{2}\lambda}\right).

Proof.

For the proof, let us arbitrarily fix a sequence of context-action-action pairs
({\bm{x}}_{1:T},{\bm{a}}^{1}_{1:T},{\bm{a}}^{2}_{1:T})
, and let us denote the induced sequence of features as
\bm{\phi}_{1:T}^{1}
and
\bm{\phi}_{1:T}^{2}
, where
\bm{\phi}_{t}^{i}:=\bm{\phi}({\bm{x}}_{t},{\bm{a}}_{t}^{i})
for
t\in[T]
and
i\in\{1,2\}.

Let us also denote
\bm{\Phi}_{t}:=\bm{\phi}_{t}^{1}(\bm{\phi}_{t}^{2})^{\top}
.
For any
\bm{\Theta}\in\Theta
, the induced preference model is defined as

P_{\bm{\Theta}}(\bm{\phi}_{t}^{1},\bm{\phi}_{t}^{2}):=\mu\left((\bm{\phi}_{t}^{1})^{\top}\bm{\Theta}\phi_{t}^{2}\right)=\mu\left(\langle\bm{\Theta},\bm{\Phi}_{t}\right).

(35)

We bound the uncertainty via the elliptical potential lemma (
Lemma

G.3
).
Let us denote
P_{i}=P_{\bm{\Theta}_{i}}
for some arbitrary
\bm{\Theta}_{i}\in\Theta.

We first upper bound the numerator as follows:
denoting
\alpha({\bm{x}},{\bm{\theta}}_{1},{\bm{\theta}}_{2})\coloneqq\int_{0}^{1}\dot{\mu}\bigl({\bm{x}}^{\top}{\bm{\theta}}_{1}+z\,{\bm{x}}^{\top}({\bm{\theta}}_{2}-{\bm{\theta}}_{1})\bigr)\,dz
for
{\bm{x}},{\bm{\theta}}_{1},{\bm{\theta}}_{2}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
,

\displaystyle\left|\mu\left(\langle\bm{\Theta}_{1},\bm{\Phi}_{t}\rangle\right)-\mu\left(\langle\bm{\Theta}_{2},\bm{\Phi}_{t}\rangle\right)\right|

\displaystyle\quad=\left|\alpha\!\left(\operatorname{vec}(\bm{\Phi}_{t}),\operatorname{vec}(\bm{\Theta}_{1}),\operatorname{vec}(\bm{\Theta}_{2})\right)\langle\bm{\Theta}_{1}-\bm{\Theta}_{2},\bm{\Phi}_{t}\rangle\right|

(Mean-value theorem)

\displaystyle\quad=\left|\left\langle\bm{\Theta}_{1}-\bm{\Theta}_{2},\underbrace{\alpha\!\left(\operatorname{vec}(\bm{\Phi}_{t}),\operatorname{vec}(\bm{\Theta}_{1}),\operatorname{vec}(\bm{\Theta}_{2})\right)\bm{\Phi}_{t}}_{\triangleq\bar{\bm{\Phi}}_{t}(\bm{\Theta}_{1},\bm{\Theta}_{2})}\right\rangle\right|

\displaystyle\quad\leq\left\lVert\mathrm{vec}(\bm{\Theta}_{1}-\bm{\Theta}_{2})\right\rVert_{{\bm{G}}_{t}(\bm{\Theta}_{1},\bm{\Theta}_{2})}\left\lVert\mathrm{vec}(\bar{\bm{\Phi}}_{t}(\bm{\Theta}_{1},\bm{\Theta}_{2}))\right\rVert_{{\bm{G}}_{t}(\bm{\Theta}_{1},\bm{\Theta}_{2})^{-1}}

(Cauchy-Schwarz inequality)

for some matrix
{\bm{G}}_{t}(\bm{\Theta}_{1},\bm{\Theta}_{2})\succ{\bm{0}}
to be determined later.

For the denominator squared:

\displaystyle\lambda+\sum_{s=1}^{t-1}\left(\mu\left(\langle\bm{\Theta}_{1},\bm{\Phi}_{s}\rangle\right)-\mu\left(\langle\bm{\Theta}_{2},\bm{\Phi}_{s}\rangle\right)\right)^{2}

\displaystyle=\lambda+\sum_{s=1}^{t-1}\left[\alpha\!\left(\operatorname{vec}(\bm{\Phi}_{s}),\operatorname{vec}(\bm{\Theta}_{1}),\operatorname{vec}(\bm{\Theta}_{2})\right)^{2}\langle\bm{\Theta}_{1}-\bm{\Theta}_{2},\bm{\Phi}_{s}\rangle^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=\lambda+\operatorname{vec}\!\left(\bm{\Theta}_{1}-\bm{\Theta}_{2}\right)^{\top}\left[\sum_{s=1}^{t-1}\left(\alpha\!\left(\operatorname{vec}(\bm{\Phi}_{s}),\operatorname{vec}(\bm{\Theta}_{1}),\operatorname{vec}(\bm{\Theta}_{2})\right)^{2}\operatorname{vec}(\bm{\Phi}_{s})\,\operatorname{vec}(\bm{\Phi}_{s})^{\top}\right)\right]\operatorname{vec}\!\left(\bm{\Theta}_{1}-\bm{\Theta}_{2}\right)

\displaystyle\geq\mathrm{vec}(\bm{\Theta}_{1}-\bm{\Theta}_{2})^{\top}\underbrace{\left[\frac{\lambda}{4S^{2}}{\bm{I}}+\sum_{s=1}^{t-1}\!\left[\alpha\!\left(\operatorname{vec}(\bm{\Phi}_{s}),\operatorname{vec}(\bm{\Theta}_{1}),\operatorname{vec}(\bm{\Theta}_{2})\right)^{2}\operatorname{vec}(\bm{\Phi}_{s})\operatorname{vec}(\bm{\Phi}_{s})^{\top}\right]\right]}_{\triangleq{\bm{G}}_{t}(\bm{\Theta}_{1},\bm{\Theta}_{2})}\mathrm{vec}(\bm{\Theta}_{1}-\bm{\Theta}_{2})

\displaystyle=\left\lVert\mathrm{vec}(\bm{\Theta}_{1}-\bm{\Theta}_{2})\right\rVert_{{\bm{G}}_{t}(\bm{\Theta}_{1},\bm{\Theta}_{2})}^{2}.

Combining the above two inequalities, we have that

\displaystyle U_{\texttt{GP}}(\lambda,{\bm{x}},{\bm{a}}^{1},{\bm{a}}^{2};\mathcal{P},\mathcal{D}_{t-1})

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{\bm{\Theta}_{1},\bm{\Theta}_{2}\in\Theta}\frac{\left\lVert\mathrm{vec}(\bm{\Theta}_{1}-\bm{\Theta}_{2})\right\rVert_{{\bm{G}}_{t}}\left\lVert\mathrm{vec}(\bar{\bm{\Phi}}_{t}(\bm{\Theta}_{1},\bm{\Theta}_{2}))\right\rVert_{{\bm{G}}_{t}(\bm{\Theta}_{1},\bm{\Theta}_{2})^{-1}}}{\left\lVert\mathrm{vec}(\bm{\Theta}_{1}-\bm{\Theta}_{2})\right\rVert_{{\bm{G}}_{t}(\bm{\Theta}_{1},\bm{\Theta}_{2})}}

\displaystyle=\sup_{\bm{\Theta}_{1},\bm{\Theta}_{2}\in\Theta}\left\lVert\mathrm{vec}(\bar{\bm{\Phi}}_{t}(\bm{\Theta}_{1},\bm{\Theta}_{2}))\right\rVert_{{\bm{G}}_{t}(\bm{\Theta}_{1},\bm{\Theta}_{2})^{-1}}

We can lower-bound

in the Löwner sense using the fact that

,

{\bm{G}}_{t}(\bm{\Theta}_{1},\bm{\Theta}_{2})\succeq\frac{\lambda}{4S^{2}}{\bm{I}}+\sum_{s=1}^{t-1}\kappa^{2}\operatorname{vec}({\bm{\Phi}}_{s})\operatorname{vec}({\bm{\Phi}}_{s})^{\top}=\frac{\lambda}{4S^{2}}{\bm{I}}+\sum_{s=1}^{t-1}\operatorname{vec}(\kappa{\bm{\Phi}}_{s})\operatorname{vec}(\kappa{\bm{\Phi}}_{s})^{\top}\triangleq{\bm{V}}_{t}.

Then, as
\alpha(\cdot,\cdot,\cdot)\leq L_{\mu}

U_{\texttt{GP}}(\lambda,{\bm{x}},{\bm{a}}^{1},{\bm{a}}^{2};\mathcal{P},\mathcal{D}_{t-1})\leq\frac{L_{\mu}}{\kappa}\left\lVert\operatorname{vec}(\kappa\bm{\Phi}_{t})\right\rVert_{{\bm{V}}_{t}^{-1}}.

(36)

We then conclude the proof via the elliptical potential lemma (
Lemma

G.3

\displaystyle d({\mathcal{P}},\lambda,T)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{t=1}^{T}\min\left\{1,\frac{L_{\mu}^{2}}{\kappa^{2}}\left\lVert\operatorname{vec}(\kappa\bm{\Phi}_{t})\right\rVert_{{\bm{V}}_{t}^{-1}}^{2}\right\}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{L_{\mu}^{2}}{\kappa^{2}}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\min\left\{1,\left\lVert\operatorname{vec}(\kappa\bm{\Phi}_{t})\right\rVert_{{\bm{V}}_{t}^{-1}}^{2}\right\}

(
\kappa\leq L_{\mu}
)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2d^{2}L_{\mu}^{2}}{\kappa^{2}}\log\left(1+\frac{4\kappa^{2}S^{2}T}{d^{2}\lambda}\right).

{\mathbb{P}}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{t}(P_{\widehat{\bm{\Theta}}_{\varepsilon,t}}({\bm{z}}_{i})-P_{\bm{\Theta}_{\star}}({\bm{z}}_{i}))^{2}\leq\log\frac{|\Theta_{\varepsilon}|}{\delta}+\sum_{i=1}^{t}\log\frac{P_{\bm{\Theta}_{\star}}(r_{i}\mid{\bm{z}}_{i})}{P_{\widehat{\bm{\Theta}}_{\varepsilon,t}}(r_{i}\mid{\bm{z}}_{i})}\right)\geq 1-\delta.

Using the inequality
(a-b)^{2}\geq\frac{1}{2}(a-c)^{2}-(b-c)^{2}
and the optimality of the MLE, with probability at least
1-\delta
the following holds:

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{t}(P_{\widehat{\bm{\Theta}}_{t}}({\bm{z}}_{i})-P_{\bm{\Theta}}({\bm{z}}_{i}))^{2}-\sum_{i=1}^{t}(P_{\widehat{\bm{\Theta}}_{t}}({\bm{z}}_{i})-P_{\widehat{\bm{\Theta}}_{\varepsilon,t}}({\bm{z}}_{i}))^{2}

\displaystyle=\log\frac{|\Theta_{\varepsilon}|}{\delta}+\sum_{i=1}^{t}\log\frac{P_{\bm{\Theta}_{\star}}(r_{i}\mid{\bm{z}}_{i})}{P_{\widehat{\bm{\Theta}}_{t}}(r_{i}\mid{\bm{z}}_{i})}+\sum_{i=1}^{t}\log\frac{P_{\widehat{\bm{\Theta}}_{t}}(r_{i}\mid{\bm{z}}_{i})}{P_{\widehat{\bm{\Theta}}_{\varepsilon,t}}(r_{i}\mid{\bm{z}}_{i})}

\displaystyle\leq\log\frac{|\Theta_{\varepsilon}|}{\delta}+\sum_{i=1}^{t}\log\frac{P_{\widehat{\bm{\Theta}}_{t}}(r_{i}\mid{\bm{z}}_{i})}{P_{\widehat{\bm{\Theta}}_{\varepsilon,t}}(r_{i}\mid{\bm{z}}_{i})}.

Now, note that for any
\bm{\Theta}
,

\log P_{\bm{\Theta}}(r_{i}\mid{\bm{z}}_{i})=r_{i}\log P_{\bm{\Theta}}({\bm{z}}_{i})+(1-r_{i})\log(1-P_{\bm{\Theta}}({\bm{z}}_{i})),

which is
L
-Lipschitz in
\bm{\Theta}
by given.
With this, we can bound the log sum on the right as

\sum_{i=1}^{t}\log\frac{P_{\widehat{\bm{\Theta}}_{t}}(r_{i}\mid{\bm{z}}_{i})}{P_{\widehat{\bm{\Theta}}_{\varepsilon,t}}(r_{i}\mid{\bm{z}}_{i})}\leq Lt\left\lVert\widehat{\bm{\Theta}}_{\varepsilon,t}-\widehat{\bm{\Theta}}_{t}\right\rVert_{F}\leq Lt\varepsilon.

Since we assumed that
\log P_{\bm{\Theta}}
is
L
-Lipschitz, it follows that
P_{\bm{\Theta}}
is also
L
-Lipschitz.
12
12

12

As the Lipschitz constant is the maximum gradient norm by the Rademacher’s theorem,
\left\lVert\nabla_{\bm{\Theta}}P_{\bm{\Theta}}\right\rVert=P_{\bm{\Theta}}\cdot\left\lVert\nabla_{\bm{\Theta}}\log P_{\bm{\Theta}}\right\rVert\leq L
.
Therefore,

\sum_{i=1}^{t}(P_{\widehat{\bm{\Theta}}_{t}}({\bm{z}}_{i})-P_{\widehat{\bm{\Theta}}_{\varepsilon,t}}({\bm{z}}_{i}))^{2}\leq Lt\varepsilon^{2}.

We now bound the cardinality of the
\varepsilon
-net
|\Theta_{\varepsilon}|
by bounding the covering number of a slightly larger set:

Lemma F.7

(Lemma 3.1 of
19
)
.

Let
\Theta(d,2r;S)\coloneqq\{{\bm{X}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d\times d}\mid\|\mathbf{X}\|_{F}\leq S,\operatorname{rank}({\bm{X}})\leq 2r\}\supseteq\mathrm{Skew}(d,2r;S)
. For any
\varepsilon>0
, there exists an
\varepsilon
-net
\Theta_{\varepsilon}
of
\Theta(d,2r;S)
w.r.t.
\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert_{F}
with
|\Theta_{\varepsilon}|\leq\left(\frac{9S}{\varepsilon}\right)^{2(2d+1)r}.

Putting the bounds together, we have:

{\mathbb{P}}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{t}(P_{\widehat{\bm{\Theta}}_{t}}({\bm{z}}_{i})-P_{\bm{\Theta}_{\star}}({\bm{z}}_{i}))^{2}\lesssim\log\frac{1}{\delta}+Lt\varepsilon+Lt\varepsilon^{2}+dr\log\frac{S}{\varepsilon}\right)\geq 1-\delta.

Choosing
\varepsilon\approx\frac{1}{(Lt)^{2}}
,

{\mathbb{P}}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{t}(P_{\widehat{\bm{\Theta}}_{t}}({\bm{z}}_{i})-P_{\bm{\Theta}_{\star}}({\bm{z}}_{i}))^{2}\lesssim\log\frac{1}{\delta}+dr\log(LSt)\right)\geq 1-\delta.

Setting
\delta=\delta/T
and taking the union bound over
t
, we have:

{\mathbb{P}}\left(\forall t\in[T],\,\sum_{i=1}^{t}(P_{\widehat{\bm{\Theta}}_{t}}({\bm{z}}_{i})-P_{\bm{\Theta}_{\star}}({\bm{z}}_{i}))^{2}\lesssim\log\frac{T}{\delta}+dr\log(LST)\right)\geq 1-\delta.

which concludes the proof.
∎

Appendix H
Future Directions

Relaxing the Feature Diversity Assumption.

Our current regret bounds rely on the feature diversity assumption (
1
), characterized by the minimum eigenvalue
C_{\min}
.
While this assumption is standard in the contextual bandits literature that involves either greedy sampling (e.g., algorithms without sophisticated exploration strategies) or high dimensions, it may still be restrictive for general RLHF applications.
Recent works have investigated minimal assumptions required for greedy strategies, such as the local anti-concentration (LAC) property proposed by
66
.
Investigating the impact of such relaxed conditions on online RLHF with
GBPM
(e.g., whether we can still obtain
{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}e^{{\mathcal{O}}(\eta)}}
-free polylogarithmic regret with
GS
) remains an important open question.

Instance-Specific Guarantees for Unregularized Regret.

While our work establishes
\tilde{{\mathcal{O}}}(\sqrt{T})
guarantees for
unregularized
regret (via
Theorem

4.2
), these bounds reflect worst-case hardness.
For instance,
53
demonstrated that in tabular games with bandit feedback, the Nash regret for the Tsallis-INF algorithm
(
118
;
3
;
154
)
scales with the “sparsity” or “entropy” of the NE set, potentially achieving logarithmic regret
{\mathcal{O}}(\log T)
when the NE is unique and deterministic (a pure strategy), and even rates of the form
{\mathcal{O}}(T^{c})
for some
c\in(0,1)
, depending on the geometry of the set of Nash Equilibria.
Adapting such instance-dependent guarantees to the contextual
GBPM
setting is non-trivial, even when the link function
\mu
is linear.
Recent advances in “Best-of-Both-Worlds” algorithms for linear contextual bandits
(
67
;
64
)
may provide a promising starting point.

Computationally Efficient Algorithms.

Our current theoretical framework assumes access to a computational oracle for finding the NE (
3
), which may be computationally expensive in practice.
Developing efficient variants of our algorithms is a practical priority.
Promising approaches include leveraging online estimation techniques such as Online Mirror Descent (OMD)
(
146
)
, minimax optimization techniques such as optimistic OMD
(
100
;
115
;
147
)
, or reductions to offline/online regression oracles
(
42
)
.
