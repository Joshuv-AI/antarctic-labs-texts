Automated PET tracer classification in multi-site, multi-cohort studies using deep learning
Authors: Robel K. Gebre, Robert I. Reid, Matthew L. Senjem, Jeffrey L. Gunter, Val J. Lowe, Kejal Kantarci, Ronald C. Petersen, Jonathan Graff-Radford, Clifford R. Jack, Prashanthi Vemuri, Christopher G. Schwarz
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundRadiotracer labels in positron emission tomography (PET) DICOM metadata are manually entered and often unreliable or missing, requiring expert visual verification for downstream analyses. We developed and validated deep learning models to classify PET tracers directly from image data across multiple sites and cohorts.ResultsWe used PET scans obtained from the Alzheimer’s Disease Neuroimaging Initiative (ADNI, n = 7429) and the Standardized Centralized Alzheimer’s & Related Dementias Neuroimaging study (SCAN, n = 4869) to classify 10 tracers. We evaluated generalizability on an external test set of Mayo Clinic Study of Aging and ADRC scans containing four tracers (n = 10558). ADNI and SCAN data were combined and split into training (60%), validation (20%), and internal test (20%) sets. We trained two ConvNeXt-Large models initialized with ImageNet weights: a 10-class model (AV1451, FBP, FBP-Early, FBB, FBB-Early, FDG, MK6240, NAV4694, PI2620, PIB) and a 3-class model grouping tracers by biological target (Amyloid, FDG, Tau). Performance was evaluated using macro-F1, average precision (AP), and confusion matrices, Integrated Gradients for attribution, with Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP) and cosine similarity to assess model interpretability and multi-cohort effects on the embedding structure. The 3-class model achieved macro-F1 ≥ 0.99 across all test sets. The 10-class model achieved macro-F1 of 0.91 on the internal test and 0.93 on the external test set where only four tracers were available. Lower performance was observed for NAV4694 (AP 0.70; misclassified as FBB, 17.2%), PI2620 (0.79; AV1451, 20.0%), and FBB (0.80; FBP, 23.3%). The attribution maps indicated the models learned some tracer specific uptake patterns as well as general imaging features. Class embeddings in the UMAP projection showed clear separation, with limited overlap among underrepresented tracers with similar binding characteristics. Cohort-level differences in learned representations were most pronounced among tau tracers, with higher within-class cosine similarity in ADNI than in SCAN (mean [SD], 0.26 [0.10] vs. 0.01 [0.06]), while amyloid tracers showed minimal shift across cohorts.ConclusionsClassification was near-perfect when grouping tracers into three biological targets, and the 10-class model generalized well to the four tracers available in the independent external cohort. A few tracers with similar binding characteristics showed some overlap in latent space, with minimal impact on overall classification performance. Our results demonstrate that deep learning models can identify PET tracers from image appearance alone and have the potential to support semi-automated tracer identification and data curation in large multi-site PET studies. Introduction

Positron emission tomography (PET) is a core imaging modality in Alzheimer’s disease (AD) and related dementia research and clinical practice. It allows in vivo assessment of beta-amyloid deposition, tau aggregation, and cerebral glucose metabolism. Large multi-site initiatives such as Alzheimer’s Disease Neuroimaging Initiative (ADNI) [1] and Standardized Centralized Alzheimer’s & Related Dementias Neuroimaging initiative (SCAN) [2] have enabled PET data sharing through standardized acquisition, quality control (QC), harmonization, and data-sharing infrastructure. However, PET workflows remain vulnerable to labeling errors because tracer identity is not known to the scanner and is manually entered at acquisition using free-text fields with potentially infinite variations that are challenging to parse. Technicians are also often forced to enter the wrong tracer when new tracers are scanned before software updates enable them as choices. Tracer information in DICOM metadata may also be missing or incorrect due to over anonymization by site-specific software. Consequently, workflows often rely on expert confirmation of tracer type, which is time-consuming and can introduce additional operator error, particularly for tracers targeting the same biological process that appear similar on visual inspection. For example, amyloid PET can be acquired using Pittsburgh compound B (PIB), Florbetapir (FBP), Florbetaben (FBB), etc., where each one can produce similar cortical uptake patterns that can be difficult to distinguish visually.

Despite this need, automated image-based tracer identification has received limited attention, particularly compared to MRI sequence classification [3–10]. To date, only one related study has been reported, where Ghafari et al. trained a DeepCNN on 2D axial slices taken from 20 PET scans to classify two tracers (Fluorodeoxyglucose (FDG) and Gallium-68-prostate-specific membrane) and identify four body regions [11]. However, a dedicated approach to discriminate among multiple brain PET tracers and to generalize across studies is still needed. To address this gap, we developed a deep learning (DL) model to predict tracer type directly from scan appearance, independent of DICOM metadata. Our primary aim was to test whether brain PET tracers could be distinguished from image appearance alone across large multi-cohort and multi-site datasets. To account for real-world heterogeneity in sites, study duration, scanner vendor, protocol, and reconstruction algorithms, we assembled more than 12,000 PET scans from ADNI and SCAN to train models for ten distinct tracer types (Fig. 1). Performance was evaluated on an internal held-out test set and an external test set containing the tracer classes available for external validation. These models are intended to provide semi-automated tracer verification and data curation support for large PET imaging studies and have the potential to provide a more efficient and reliable multi-site PET research and clinical workflows.

Materials and methods

ADNI

Data used in the preparation of this article were obtained from the ADNI database. ADNI was launched in 2003 as a public-private partnership. The goal of ADNI is to determine whether serial MRI, PET, other biologic markers, and clinical and neuropsychological assessments can be combined to measure the progression of mild cognitive impairment (MCI) and early Alzheimer’s disease (AD), while also validating biomarkers for clinical trials, improving the generalizability of ADNI data through increased cohort diversity, and providing data on the diagnosis and progression of AD to the scientific community.

For this study, we included AV1451 [12] (n = 1663), FBB [13] (n = 740), FBB-Early [14] (n = 53), FBP [15] (n = 2397), FBP-Early [16] (n = 117), FDG [17] (n = 2162), MK6240 [18] (n = 119), NAV4694 [19] (n = 78), PI2620 [20] (n = 42), and PIB [21] (n = 58) PET scans from all available ADNI phases (ADNI-1, ADNI-GO, ADNI-2, ADNI-3, ADNI-4) that had passed ADNI QC, regardless of participant demographics (Fig. 1). We denote early frame dynamic FBB and FBP scans, acquired for cerebral perfusion imaging, with the suffix “-Early.” Scans contributed by Mayo Clinic ADRC to ADNI were removed to avoid overlap. ADNI PET acquisition protocols have been described previously [22].

SCAN

SCAN [2] was established to harmonize PET and MRI acquisition and processing across U.S. ADRCs funded by the National Institute on Aging. It defines core acquisition protocols and analysis pipelines to align imaging outcomes across sites and with other multi-site efforts, including ADNI, and links imaging with broader ADRC data through the National Alzheimer’s Coordinating Center. SCAN PET protocols are identical to ADNI’s PET protocols. For this study, we included all available AV1451 (n = 1168), FBB (n = 1102), FBP (n = 256), FDG (n = 134), MK6240 (n = 612), NAV4694 (n = 168), PI2620 (n = 258), and PIB (n = 1171) PET scans except for those contributed by the Mayo Clinic ADRC, to avoid overlap across our datasets (Fig. 1). Our sample includes participants recruited across 22 ADRCs (Supplementary Fig. 1).

Mayo (MCSA and ADRC)

MCSA [23] is a population-based study in Olmsted County, Minnesota, and the Mayo Clinic ADRC is a longitudinal clinical practice study at Mayo Clinic. Both were approved by the Mayo Clinic and Olmsted Medical Center Institutional Review Boards. Participants span cognitively unimpaired (CU), MCI, and clinical AD dementia across the combined MCSA and ADRC. Imaging protocols evolved over time: MRI, FDG PET, and amyloid PET were acquired from 2006 to 2015, tau PET was added in 2015, and FDG PET became optional thereafter, resulting in fewer PET participants under age 50, and longitudinal imaging is primarily available for participants aged ≥ 50 [24]. AV1451 (n = 4043), FBP (n = 16), FDG (n = 2518) and PIB (n = 3981) PET scans acquired from 2015 onward were used as the external test set (Fig. 1). Mayo PET protocols have been previously published [24].

Model development

We performed two separate classification tasks, one with 10 classes and one with 3 classes. The 10-class labels were AV1451, FBB, FBB-Early, FBP, FBP-Early, FDG, MK6240, NAV4694, PI2620, and PIB. The 3-class labels were Amyloid (n = 6140), FDG (n = 2296) and Tau (n = 3862), created by categorizing tracers according to the biological targets they bind to (Table 1). Amyloid included FBP, FBP-Early, FBB, FBB-Early, NAV4694, and PIB, FDG was its own group, and Tau included AV1451, MK6240, and PI2620. A ConvNeXt-Large [25] architecture initialized with ImageNet-pretrained weights [26] was used as the primary classifier. The final classification layer was replaced to match the number of classes for each task. A pictorial summary of model development is given in Fig. 2.

Data standardization and splitting

The same standardization process was followed for all three cohorts. Individual frames from original, unfiltered (no smoothing during on-scanner reconstructions) dynamic PET image DICOM files were first converted to NIfTI files. All PETs were then resampled to a standard isotropic voxel size of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$1\times1\times1\times\; mm^{2}$$\end{document} prior to modeling, using ANTs [27]. For 4D time-series images, one 3D volume was selected before resampling: the middle frame for Early protocol scans (after sufficient perfusion for imaging) and the first late-uptake frame for all other scans (which only acquired standard late-uptake time windows). The nominal acquisition windows used across cohorts and tracers are summarized in Supplementary Table 1. These ranged from early-frame dynamic perfusion acquisitions near the time of injection to late-uptake acquisitions beginning approximately 30–110 min post injection, depending on tracer and protocol. The resulting 3D images were used for preprocessing and model input generation.

Data used for model development was a combined ADNI and SCAN dataset. SCAN did not include Early protocol PETs, whereas ADNI had relatively fewer PIB, PI2620, NAV4694, and MK6240 scans. Thus, a SCAN-only model would miss tracer categories present only in ADNI, while an ADNI-only model would be limited by sparse classes and reduced generalizability. Combining these two cohorts increased the overall number of scans per tracer in the training, validation and test splits used to develop effective models.

The combined dataset was partitioned into training, validation, and internal test sets using participant-level stratified splitting to prevent data leakage. All scans from a given participant were assigned to a single split, with participant ID used as the grouping variable. To account for study site differences (Supplementary Fig. 1), stratification was performed jointly by tracer label and site ID to preserve marginal distributions of both factors across splits. To reduce dependence on a one-time split and assess model stability, the splitting procedure was repeated across ten random seeds which resulted in ten trained instances for the 10-class model and ten trained instances for the 3-class model. We then report result statistics across the seeds.

Input preparation

Each input consisted of three orthogonal views (axial, coronal, sagittal) which provided a 2.5D field of view and preserved the 3-channel format that the pretrained convolutional network (which were trained on color images) required. A similar approach had previously been implemented by Roth et al. for lymph node detection using computed tomography images [28].

Three important pre-processing steps were performed for input preparation. First, for each orthogonal view, ten mid-slices centered on the mid-plane were extracted and averaged to produce a single representative slice. During initial experiments, we used a single mid-plane slice from each view to train the classifiers. However, this proved to be sensitive to small differences in slice placement and provided limited within-subject variation, particularly in the presence of minor differences in patients’ scan positioning and noise. The slab approach improved performance by reducing this influence of local noise. Second, the per-view averaged image was resized to the same size of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:256\times\:256$$\end{document}. Third, slice pixel values were normalized using percentile-based intensity stretching [29]. Values were linearly mapped using the 1st and 99th percentiles of the non-zero intensity distribution and then clipped to the range [0, 1] [26] (Fig. 3). This percentile range performed best among those evaluated and reduced sensitivity to extreme outliers and preserved tracer-related contrasts.

Optimization was performed using AdamW [30] with an initial learning rate of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:3\:\times\:\:10⁻⁴$$\end{document}, batch size of 64, and up to 500 epochs. Training runtime per epoch was on average ~ 2 min.

Statistical analysis

After model training, performance was assessed by macro-F1 and Matthews correlation coefficient (MCC). MCC was used as a complementary metric as it uses all entries of the multiclass confusion matrix and can be informative under class imbalance [32].

Classification performance

Classification performance was summarized using the mean confusion matrix and precision-recall (PR) analysis of the predicted probabilities. For each seed run, predicted labels were assigned to the class with the highest predicted probability. We then normalized the confusion matrices so that each row summed to 1 and averaged them across seeds to obtain a single mean normalized confusion matrix. Per-tracer performance was evaluated using average precision (AP), defined as the area under the PR curve. We then summarized AP across seed runs as the mean and standard deviation (SD) and displayed the SD on the mean PR curve to show variability in precision at each recall value.

Model interpretation: attribution

We used integrated gradients to examine which image features contributed to tracer prediction in both the 10-class and 3-class models. Integrated gradients estimate the contribution of each input feature by integrating the model’s gradients along a path from a baseline image to the observed image [33]. We focused the attribution analysis on the 10-class model, because this model provided class resolution and therefore allowed interpretation of image features relevant for classification of the individual radiotracers. A baseline image was defined as a zero-valued image with the same dimensions as the model input. Attribution maps were generated in the same standardized model-input space used for classification, consisting of the axial, coronal, and sagittal 2.5D slices.

Because internal train, validation, and test partitions were randomly generated for each seed, an image that appears in a test set for one seed was not necessarily part of the test set for the other seeds. This design was appropriate for evaluating model performance across repeated random splits, but it created a challenge for model interpretation because attribution maps would not be directly comparable across seed-specific models if each model were explained using different test images. We therefore created a fixed explanation image set and passed the same candidate images through each independently trained seed model. For ADNI and SCAN, where internal splits differed across random seeds, we selected candidate images for each tracer from the internal test set of one seed. For the external Mayo test set, which was fixed across seeds, the same candidate images were selected from the external test set. Each selected candidate image was then passed through all independently trained seed models, regardless of whether that image belonged to the corresponding seed model’s original test split. The resulting maps per image were averaged across seed-specific models. Using the same image across seed models was important for two reasons. First, it allowed us to assess whether independently trained models used similar image features to support the same tracer prediction. Second, it avoided the anatomical blurring that would occur if maps from different images were averaged together, because the images were in patient native space.

Model interpretation: latent space

For model interpretation, we focused on the 10-class model because it was the more challenging of the two tasks, and on the internal test set because it was the only test split where all tracers were available. We examined the model’s latent space using Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP) [34] and cosine similarity [35]. UMAP is a nonlinear dimensionality reduction method that projects high dimensional embedding into a lower dimensional space to visualize class clustering and overlap. where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\mathbf{u}$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\mathbf{v}$$\end{document} are the mean embedding vectors for the tracer pair being compared, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\mathbf{u}}^{\mathrm{T}}\mathbf{v}$$\end{document} is their dot product, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\parallel\mathbf{u}\parallel\:and\:\parallel\mathbf{v}\parallel$$\end{document} are their norms. These matrices were then averaged across seeds to produce a final matrix which we plotted as a heatmap. The values ranged from − 1, 0 and 1 for opposing, orthogonal, and aligned representations, respectively. The larger the value, the more similar the learned representations of the two tracer classes.

Investigating multi-cohort effects

To assess potential effects of ADNI and SCAN on model learning, we compared cosine similarity distributions across the seeds between the two cohorts for tracer pairs with the same target, amyloid-to-amyloid and tau-to-tau. This was done to determine whether the model learned different cohort-related embedding structures for tracers that target the same biological target (Table 1). We also examined the distributions for individual tracer pairs when the cosine similarity means differed visibly between cohorts. Apart from mean and standard deviation descriptive statistics, formal hypothesis testing was not performed because these distributions were derived from the same underlying dataset (split using different seeds) and therefore did not represent independent observations.

Then, to investigate whether acquisition year affected model performance, we repeated the classification performance (summarized across the seeds) stratified by acquisition year. This analytic step was needed because of the historical differences between ADNI and SCAN. Classification performance metrics were calculated only using tracer classes present in that year, so missing classes did not contribute to the performance metrics. To quantify how often a model missed tracers present in a given year, we also calculated sample size weighted recall by acquisition year.

Classification performance of the 3-class model

Performance of the 3-class model was uniformly excellent across cohorts and test sets (Table 4). For the internal test set, AP was 1.00 for Amyloid and FDG and 0.99 for Tau in ADNI, while SCAN showed AP of 1.00 for Amyloid and Tau with a small reduction for FDG (0.99 [0.02]). For the external test set, AP was 1.00 for all three classes and the largest misclassification error was Tau classified as Amyloid in SCAN (1.6%), whereas all other errors were < 0.8% (Table 4).

Model interpretation

In Fig. 3, we show the positive attribution maps of the 10-class model, created by explaining a single image per tracer across the ten seed models and averaging the resulting maps. The positive attributions represent image regions that increased the model output for the true tracer class, and we interpret them as model-supporting image features rather than voxelwise localization of tracer binding or disease pathology.

When looking at the maps we can see that the models were able to learn some tracer specific uptake patterns that were consistent with known tracer and target related image distributions. For instance, they were able to learn the meningeal off-target signal for MK6240, the characteristic cortical signal for FDG, and the prominent white matter signal in amyloid tracers (Fig. 3). Similar attribution patterns were observed across ADNI, SCAN, and the Mayo external test set which suggested that the models could have learned reproducible image features across cohorts. Some attributions were also observed outside the brain, indicating that non-brain off-target signal features were sometimes informative for tracer classification.

The UMAP embeddings analysis showed similar class separation in ADNI and SCAN, with overlap found in the same tracer pairs that showed weaker classification performance (Fig. 4). UMAP analysis for Mayo was not conducted since the purpose of the embedding analysis was to investigate cohort effects (of ADNI and SCAN) used for model development.

Next, in order to investigate whether study history differences had an effect on model performance, we conducted year- and phase-stratified analyses. We found that performance was generally stable across acquisition years in SCAN and Mayo, with balanced accuracy, macro-F1, and MCC remaining high for both the 10-class and 3-class models (Fig. 6). ADNI showed lower and more variable 10-class performance across study phases, particularly in later years, whereas 3-class performance remained high and stable. Tracer’s recall showed that variability was most evident for visually similar or underrepresented tracer classes, but the ADNI performance differences were not completely explained by scan year alone and were not limited only to later-introduced tracers. These findings suggest that the ADNI-SCAN model performance difference likely reflects multiple factors, including tracer similarity, class imbalance, and scanner- or site-related technical variations, rather than a simple acquisition year effect.

Discussion

We developed DL models to classify PET scans into ten radiotracer types, as well as a three-class grouping, directly from image appearance and independent of DICOM metadata. Using standardized preprocessing and participant-level stratified splitting across ten random seeds, the 10-class models achieved very good performance on the internal test set and generalized well to an external cohort where only four tracers were available. The 3-class (amyloid vs. tau vs. FDG) models showed excellent performance throughout. Per-tracer AP was consistently high in SCAN and Mayo, whereas ADNI showed greater variability for a small subset of tracers.

The models developed in this study were designed for radiotracer type prediction rather than pathology interpretation or mechanistic inference, which is important when interpreting the findings. Because the ground truth was tracer identity rather than disease stage or diagnosis, attribution patterns (image regions that contribute most to the model’s prediction) should not be expected to localize to established pathological regions [36], as the models may rely on multiple uptake patterns (i.e. off-target regions only for some images, and more true binding for others), white matter retention, extracerebral signal, global intensity distribution, and head profile features to distinguish tracers. This interpretation is supported by the integrated gradients analysis, which showed broad patterns rather than focal localization to a single anatomical region (Fig. 3). For example, the maps highlighted features consistent with known tracer related image distributions, including meningeal signal for MK6240, cortical signal for FDG, and prominent white matter signal in amyloid tracers. Some attribution was also observed outside the brain, suggesting that extracerebral or nonbrain image features may have contributed to classification.

Several tracers share protein targets and related chemical properties [37–39], which can produce similar binding patterns [14, 16, 40–43] and, consequently, similar overall image contrast. Effectively, the classifier was trained to differentiate between some pairings of radiotracers that were originally designed to look the same. As a result, the 10-class problem is inherently more challenging than the 3-class grouping. Nonetheless, the 10-class models showed strong classification performance even in presence of significant class imbalance and non-biological heterogeneity across the multi-site cohorts, including differences in acquisition year, study phase, scanner generation, scanner manufacturer, and site-related imaging variation. Overall, our results align with prior work by Mahmutoglu et al., who showed that DL models trained on large multicenter datasets can generalize across sites when participant-level stratified splitting is used [7]. In that work, a ResNet-18 ensemble trained on mid-slices to identify brain MRI sequence types across 249 hospitals and 29 scanner models achieved high test accuracy across nine sequence types (84%–99.8%) [7].

Among the amyloid tracers, the dominant misclassification error of the 10-class model was between FBB and FBP, which is expected given their known commonality in uptake characteristics and image contrast driven in part by white matter and myelin-related off-target binding [40–42], in addition to shared binding to amyloid pathology. Confusion among tracers targeting the same amyloid pathology also affected NAV4694, which was misclassified as FBB [44, 45], though its limited representation (Table 2) makes it difficult to determine whether this was driven by greater class imbalance, non-biological heterogeneity within the cohorts, or both. This explanation also applies to FBP-Early and FBB-Early, where we found misclassification rates of 2.6% and 14.5%, respectively. In these early frames only seconds after injection, the distribution likely reflects perfusion more than tracer binding properties or anything amyloid-related. We expected that it would likely be impossible for the model to differentiate between the two, but it still performed well for this task (AP > 0.93, Table 4). In addition, due to the close association between perfusion and glucose metabolism, prior works had proposed FBP-Early as an FDG surrogate [16, 46], which is exactly what our model interpretation analysis revealed (Fig. 5).

The lower AP and slight confusion among the tau tracers PI2620, MK6240, and AV1451 are also biologically plausible (Table 4), and the stronger misclassification error suggests the combined influence of limited class representation for PI2620 and MK6240. Nonetheless, despite the increased difficulty of the 10-class task, there was clear separation among tau, amyloid, and FDG tracers, as shown in Fig. 3. The models captured the characteristic cerebral uptake pattern of FDG, with predominant gray matter uptake and relatively low white matter signal, in contrast to amyloid tracers, which retain white matter signal even in amyloid negative scans and show additional cortical uptake when amyloid positive, and the tau tracers, which are more heterogenous in uptake patterns across participants and have starker differences between each of the different tracers.

We trained our models using the combined ADNI and SCAN data. However, performance differed between the two cohorts due to several sources of non-biological technical heterogeneity. These two cohorts use similar PET acquisition protocols (Supplementary Table 1), and many sites participate in both, so protocol differences alone are unlikely to explain the lower performance observed for some tracers in ADNI compared to SCAN. A sensitivity analysis of acquisition-window start time showed little to no association with classification correctness in the held-out internal test data and external Mayo test set (Supplementary Fig. 2). Therefore, the more likely explanation is a combined influence of limited sample size, technical heterogeneity, and tracer chemistry and target protein similarities. We have already discussed how tracer similarity may contribute to misclassification and how this interpretation is supported by existing literature. The year- and phase- stratified analysis support the influence of limited sample size, particularly for sparse ADNI tracers introduced in later study phases unlike SCAN where the available tracers were more consistently represented throughout its history (Fig. 6). In neural network classification tasks, when a class is represented by fewer data points, its learned representation may be more vulnerable to overlap with other classes in the embedding space under class imbalance. Greater embedding overlap, however, does not necessarily imply poor classification accuracy [47]. Instead, it may reflect reduced separability of learned class representations and should be interpreted together with AP and the confusion matrix.

Another important factor to consider is the multi-site structure within each cohort. The number of ADNI PET imaging sites increased across study phases, whereas SCAN site expansion occurred more gradually year by year (Supplementary Fig. 3). In the final year (2025) of each cohort included in this study, ADNI included roughly three times as many contributing PET imaging sites as SCAN (55 vs. 19), considerably increasing its technical heterogeneity. The increase in ADNI sites, together with the introduction of rarer tracers in later ADNI phases, may help explain the year and phase associated performance pattern observed in Fig. 6. Although we stratified by tracer label and study site ID to reduce cross-site imbalance across data splits, ADNI introduced additional sites in its later phases which likely increases the complexity of technical heterogeneity during model training and evaluation. Even with careful split design, the independent effects of site, study phase, and tracer distribution cannot be completely disentangled, which is a limitation of the current analysis. Lastly, it is worth noting that when tracer sample sizes were large, cohort differences appeared to have limited effect on model performance. The UMAP embedding structure and integrated gradients attribution maps showed that the models learned broadly similar embedding patterns and voxel-wise imaging features across cohorts, with only modest cohort-specific effects. Similarly, the cosine similarity analysis showed that the models made quantitatively different but very similar within-tracer similarity errors in both ADNI and SCAN. This suggests that, when sufficient training examples were available, the model could learn stable tracer-specific representations despite multi-cohort technical heterogeneity. Therefore, the lower ADNI performance for selected tracers is more likely to reflect the combined effects of sparse tracer classes, tracer similarity, and study phase associated technical heterogeneity, rather than failure of the models to generalize across cohorts.

Our goal was to examine whether PET tracer data can be distinguished using conventional deep learning models from image appearance alone. The results were promising but need further investigation, and so our future work will focus on three areas. First, we will improve model performance by implementing better loss functions that could account for class imbalance and by training 3D models that use the full spatial extent of the PET volumes. Second, we are working on obtaining larger samples of underrepresented tracers, to retrain the models with additional datasets to potentially improve the 10-class model performance and help separate tracer-specific image features from multi-cohort and multi-site related effects. Third, we are considering a practical deployment strategy in which the model functions as a review support tool. To this end, we performed a preliminary investigation of the 10-class model’s confidence when correct vs. incorrect, to evaluate whether confidence measures could identify a subset of image classifications that would be sufficiently accurate without human review. We found when the models were correct, they were usually highly confident, however, when they were wrong, many errors still had high probabilities (Supplementary Fig. 4). Overall, higher top-1 softmax probabilities were associated with a greater likelihood of correct classification, but confidence did not perfectly align with correctness. This is a well reported characteristic of neural networks, in which softmax probabilities often do not directly match the empirical likelihood of correctness, even though maximum softmax probability can still be useful for identifying predictions that may require review [48–50]. We then examined for each misclassification whether the true tracer was still retained among the highest ranked candidate labels. We found that the true tracer was usually present among the top three candidate labels, which suggests that a ranked output could still guide human review based on a confidence threshold (Supplementary Fig. 5). Although building a validated human review support system (threshold validation, probability calibration assessment, expert-reader agreement, workflow testing, etc.) is well beyond the scope of this study, these results suggest that a future QC workflow is possible in which confidence thresholds are used as a feature to help prioritize scans for review and the top candidate tracer labels are presented for expert confirmation. For studies where DICOM-level tracer information is available, it may also be feasible for human experts to only review cases where the model disagrees with DICOM.

This study has some limitations. First, class imbalance and tracer sparsity could have inflated uncertainty for underrepresented tracers. Second, external testing was limited to the four tracers available at Mayo, so generalization to additional sites, vendors, and tracers could not be assessed, and should be interpreted cautiously. These limitations will be explored when more data becomes available in future studies.

Conclusion

We demonstrate that deep learning models can accurately classify PET radiotracers across multiple cohorts using a standardized preprocessing pipeline. Most errors were among tracers with overlapping biological targets or similar binding profiles, including known off-target binding, and were further influenced by similarities in acquisition protocol design. Despite the multi-site and multi-cohort nature of the data, performance remained stable for well represented tracers. This was partly due to these studies using similar acquisition time windows for each tracer and standardized reconstruction parameters. These models offer a practical approach for large-scale tracer verification by potentially reducing manual burden and improving reliability of downstream analyses in multi-site PET studies, while uncertain or underrepresented cases may still require expert review.
