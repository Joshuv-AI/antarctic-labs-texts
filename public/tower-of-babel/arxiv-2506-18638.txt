A Selection of Distributions and Their Fourier Transforms with Applications in Magnetic Resonance Imaging
Kaibo Tang

Abstract




1
Introduction

This note presents a rigorous introduction to a selection of distributions along with their Fourier transforms, which are commonly encountered in signal processing, and, in particular, magnetic resonance imaging (MRI). In contrast to many textbooks on the principles of MRI which place more emphasis on the signal processing aspect
[
1
,
2
]
, this note will take a more mathematical approach. In particular, we will make explicit the underlying topological space of interest and clarify the exact sense in which these distributions and their Fourier transforms are defined. Key results presented in this note involve the Poisson summation formula and the Fourier transform of a Gaussian function via an ordinary differential equation (ODE) argument, etc.

Although the readers are expected to have prior exposure to functional analysis
[
3
]
and distribution theory, this note is intended to be self-contained. To this end, we will begin with a brief review of some of the basic definitions and results.

Definition 1.1

(Schwartz function)
.

A Schwartz function is a function
f\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
such that for all
N\in\mathbb{N}
we have
q_{N}(f)<\infty
where

q_{N}(f)=\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n},|\alpha|\leq N}(1+|x|)^{N}|\partial^{\alpha}f(x)|.

(1)

We denote by
\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}^{n})=:\mathcal{S}
the Fréchet space of Schwartz functions whose topology is induced by the countable family of seminorms
\{q_{N}:N\in\mathbb{N}\}
.

Definition 1.2

(Tempered distribution)
.

A tempered distribution is a continuous linear functional
u:\mathcal{S}\to\mathbb{C}
. We denote by
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{n})=:\mathcal{S}^{\prime}
the space of tempered distributions.

As usual, we use
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
to denote the dual pairing on
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}\times\mathcal{S}
. The following proposition gives a convenient criterion for checking whether a linear functional is continuous.

Proposition 1.3
.

Let
u:\mathcal{S}\to\mathbb{C}
be linear. Then,
u
is continuous if and only if there exists
N,C_{N}
such that

|\langle u,\varphi\rangle|\leq C_{N}q_{N}(\varphi)\quad\forall\varphi\in\mathcal{S}.

(2)

Corollary 1.4
.

Every Lebesgue integrable function is a tempered distribution in the distributional sense.

Proof.

Fix
f\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})=:L^{1}
and fix
\varphi\in\mathcal{S}
. It is clear that
f:\mathcal{S}\to\mathbb{C}
is linear. Hence, we only need to check continuity. Notice that

|\langle f,\varphi\rangle|=\left|\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}f(x)\varphi(x)\ dx\right|\leq\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}|f(x)||\varphi(x)|\ dx\leq\|f\|_{L^{1}}\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}|\varphi(x)|.

(3)

Take
N=1
and
C_{N}=\|f\|_{L^{1}}
to finish.
∎

2
The Fourier transform

In this section, our aim is twofold. We will first outline the typical procedure of defining the Fourier transform on the space of tempered distributions
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}
. Later, we will clarify how the Fourier transform defined using the mathematical convention differs from the engineering convention.

2.1
The Fourier transform on the space of tempered distributions

We begin by defining the Fourier transform for function in
L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})=:L^{1}
, which is given by an absolute convergent integral. To avoid confusion, we will denote the Fourier transform defined here as
\mathcal{F}_{1}
or
\hat{\cdot}_{1}
.

Definition 2.1

(Fourier transform I)
.

Let
f\in L^{1}
. For
\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, define the Fourier transform of
f
, denoted
\mathcal{F}_{1}f
or
\hat{f}_{1}
, by

\mathcal{F}_{1}f(\xi)=\hat{f}_{1}(\xi):=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}e^{-ix\cdot\xi}f(x)\ d^{\prime}x,\quad\text{where }d^{\prime}x:=(2\pi)^{-n/2}\ dx.

(4)

The following formula describes what is frequently referred to as the ‘‘inverse Fourier transform’’.
1
1

1

Use the Fourier inversion formula with
caution
since it is valid only for a subset of functions!

Theorem 2.2

(Fourier inversion formula)
.

For
g\in\mathcal{S}\subset L^{1}
, we have

g(x)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}e^{ix\cdot\xi}\hat{g}_{1}(\xi)\ d^{\prime}\xi,\quad\text{where }d^{\prime}\xi:=(2\pi)^{-n/2}\ d\xi.

(5)

Corollary 2.3
.

The Fourier transform
\mathcal{F}_{1}
is a homeomorphism of
\mathcal{S}
onto itself.

We now use the dual pairing on
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}\times\mathcal{S}
to extend
\mathcal{F}_{1}
to all of
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}
.

Proposition 2.4
.

For
u\in\mathcal{S}^{\prime}
, define the linear map
\mathcal{F}_{1}u=\hat{u}:\mathcal{S}\to\mathbb{C}
by

\langle\hat{u},\varphi\rangle:=\langle u,\hat{\varphi}\rangle\quad\text{for }\varphi\in\mathcal{S}.

(6)

Then,
\mathcal{F}_{1}u\in\mathcal{S}^{\prime}
and the map
\mathcal{F}_{1}:\mathcal{S}^{\prime}\to\mathcal{S}^{\prime}
is a homeomorphism.

2.2
Different conventions of the Fourier transform

In the engineering realm, the Fourier transform is defined differently as follows. To avoid confusion, we will denote the Fourier transform defined here as
\mathcal{F}_{2}
or
\hat{\cdot}_{2}
.

Definition 2.5

(Fourier transform II)
.

Let
f\in L^{1}
. For
\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, define the Fourier transform of
f
, denoted
\mathcal{F}_{2}f
or
\hat{f}_{2}
, by

\mathcal{F}_{2}f(\xi)=\hat{f}_{2}(\xi):=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}e^{-2\pi ix\cdot\xi}f(x)\ dx.

(7)

For the ease of comparison, we will recall the Fourier transform defined following the mathematical convention. Let
f\in L^{1}
. For
\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, the Fourier transform of
f
, defined using the mathematical convention, denoted
\mathcal{F}_{2}f
or
\hat{f}_{1}
, is given by

\mathcal{F}_{1}f(\xi)=\hat{f}_{1}(\xi):=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}e^{-ix\cdot\xi}f(x)\ d^{\prime}x,

(8)

where
d^{\prime}x:=(2\pi)^{-n/2}\ dx
.

Similar to the mathematical convention, we can extend
\mathcal{F}_{2}
from
\mathcal{S}
to all of
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}
such that the Fourier transform of a distribution, e.g., the Dirac comb, can be computed.

The mathematical and engineering conventions are reconciled by the following relationships:

\displaystyle\hat{f}_{1}(\xi)

\displaystyle=(2\pi)^{-n/2}\hat{f}_{2}\left(\frac{\xi}{2\pi}\right);

(9)

\displaystyle\hat{f}_{2}(\xi)

\displaystyle=(2\pi)^{n/2}\hat{f}_{1}(2\pi\xi).

(10)

Sometimes, the engineering convention gives a slightly more elegant expression. Hence, for the rest of the note, we will denote by
\mathcal{F}
and
\hat{\cdot}
the Fourier transform in the engineering convention. To obtain the expression for the mathematical convention, apply Equation
9
.

Proposition 2.7

(Conjugation)
.

Let
\varphi\in\mathcal{S}
. If

\hat{f}(\xi)=\overline{\hat{\varphi}(-\xi)}.

(15)

\hat{f}(-\xi)=-\overline{\hat{f}(\xi)}.

Remark 2.8
.

Equation (

k

(Real and imaginary parts)

Let

3.2
Gaussian function

We consider a particular example of Gaussian function of the form

g(x)=e^{-\pi x^{2}}.

(26)

Integrating
g
over the real line shows that
\|g\|_{L^{1}}=1
. Hence, we see that
g\in L^{1}
and, by Corollary
1.4
, that
g:\mathcal{S}\to\mathbb{C}
is a tempered distribution.

Proposition 3.4
.

The Fourier transform of

g(x)=e^{-\pi x^{2}}

(27)

is given by

\hat{g}(\xi)=e^{-\pi\xi^{2}}.

(28)

Proof.

We show that
g
and
\hat{g}
satisfy the same initial value problem for which the uniqueness theorem applies, and
g=\hat{g}
follows immediately.

1.

(
g
). Differentiating both sides of (
27
), we see that
g
is a solution of the initial value problem given by

f^{\prime}(x)=-2\pi xf(x),f(0)=1.

(29)

2.

(
\hat{g}
). The Fourier transform of
g
is given by

\hat{g}(\xi)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}g(x)e^{-2\pi ix\xi}\ dx=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{-\pi x^{2}}e^{-2\pi ix\xi}\ dx.

(30)

Differentiating the integrand, we see that

\left|\frac{d}{d\xi}[e^{-\pi x^{2}}e^{-2\pi ix\xi}]\right|=|e^{-\pi x^{2}}(-2\pi ix)e^{-2\pi ix\xi}|=2\pi|x|e^{-\pi x^{2}},

(31)

which is Lebesgue integrable over
\mathbb{R}
. Hence, the dominated convergence theorem applies and we may differentiate under the integral sign, in which case, we have

\hat{g}^{\prime}(\xi)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{-\pi x^{2}}(-2\pi ix)e^{-2\pi ix\xi}\ dx=i\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}(-2\pi x)e^{-\pi x^{2}}e^{-2\pi ix\xi}\ dx.

(32)

Integrating by parts and noticing that the boundary term vanishes, we get

\displaystyle\hat{g}^{\prime}(\xi)

\displaystyle=-i\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{-\pi x^{2}}(-2\pi i\xi)e^{-2\pi ix\xi}\ dx

(33)

\displaystyle=-2\pi\xi i\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{-\pi x^{2}}e^{-2\pi ix\xi}\ dx

(34)

\displaystyle=-2\pi\xi\hat{g}(\xi).

(35)

Finally, notice that

\hat{g}(0)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}g(x)\ dx=1.

(36)

Hence,
\hat{g}
is also a solution of the initial value problem in (
29
).

∎

3.3
Dirac delta and constant function

In this section, we will see that the Dirac delta
\delta
and the constant function
1
are related by Fourier transform. As you will notice before long, one direction (
\hat{\delta}=1
) is quite trivial while the other direction (
\hat{1}=\delta
), despite still being quite trivial, requires some additional tricks.

We start by defining the Dirac delta and checking both
1
and
\delta
are tempered distributions.

Proposition 3.6
.

The constant function
1:\mathcal{S}\to\mathbb{C}
is a tempered distribution.

Proof.

Fix
\varphi\in\mathcal{S}
. It is clear that
1:\mathcal{S}\to\mathbb{C}
is linear. Hence, we only need to check continuity. Notice that

\displaystyle|\langle 1,\varphi\rangle|=\left|\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\varphi(x)\ dx\right|

\displaystyle\leq\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}|\varphi(x)|\ dx

(37)

\displaystyle=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{1}{(1+|x|)^{2}}(1+|x|)^{2}|\varphi(x)|\ dx

(38)

\displaystyle=\left(\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{1}{(1+|x|)^{2}}\ dx\right)\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}}[(1+|x|)^{2}|\varphi(x)|]

(39)

\displaystyle=2\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}}[(1+|x|)^{2}|\varphi(x)|].

(40)

Take
N=2
and
C_{N}=2
in Proposition
1.3
to finish.
∎

Here, we define the Dirac delta, also known as the unit impulse.

Definition 3.7

(Dirac delta)
.

Define the Dirac delta, denoted
\delta:\mathcal{S}\to\mathbb{C}
, by

\langle\delta,\varphi\rangle=\varphi(0)\quad\text{for }\varphi\in\mathcal{S}.

(41)

Proposition 3.8
.

The Dirac delta
\delta:\mathcal{S}\to\mathbb{C}
is a tempered distribution.

Proof.

Fix
\varphi\in\mathcal{S}
. It is clear that
f:\mathcal{S}\to\mathbb{C}
is linear. Hence, we only need to check continuity. Notice that

|\langle\delta,\varphi\rangle|=|\varphi(0)|\leq\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}}|\varphi(x)|.

(42)

Take
N=1
and
C_{N}=1
in Proposition
1.3
to finish.
∎

We will tackle the less trivial direction first, i.e.,
\hat{1}=\delta
.

Proposition 3.9
.

The Fourier transform of the constant function
1
is
\delta
.

Proof.

For
\varphi\in\mathcal{S}
, we have

\langle\hat{1},\varphi\rangle=\langle 1,\hat{\varphi}\rangle=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\hat{\varphi}(\xi)\ d\xi.

(43)

Since
\varphi\in\mathcal{S}
, the Fourier inversion formula applies, in which case,

\langle\hat{1},\varphi\rangle=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{2\pi i0\xi}\hat{\varphi}(\xi)\ d\xi=\varphi(0)=\langle\delta,\varphi\rangle.

(44)

∎

Now, the other direction is easier to show.

Proposition 3.10
.

The Fourier transform of the Dirac delta
\delta
is
1
.

Proof.

Notice that

\langle\hat{\delta},\varphi\rangle=\langle\delta,\hat{\varphi}\rangle=\hat{\varphi}(0)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{-2\pi ix0}\varphi(x)\ dx=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\varphi(x)\ dx=\langle 1,\varphi\rangle.

(45)

∎

3.5
Dirac comb

The Dirac comb is a powerful distributional tool. It enables us to model sampling of a function. For example, in MRI, we are usually concerned with sampling in the frequency domain, i.e.,
k
-space, where the acquired raw data in
k
-space can be modeled as a pointwise product between the underlying function in
k
-space and the Dirac comb with an appropriately chosen sampling period. Additional applications of the Dirac comb involves aliasing and the discrete Fourier transform (DFT) among many others.

Definition 3.13

(Dirac comb)
.

Define the Dirac comb, denoted
\Sh
, by

\Sh(x):=\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}\delta(x-n).

(51)

Proposition 3.14
.

The Dirac comb
\Sh:\mathcal{S}\to\mathbb{C}
is a tempered distribution.

Proof.

It is clear that
\Sh:\mathcal{S}\to\mathbb{C}
is linear. We only need to check continuity using Proposition
1.3
. Indeed, for any fixed
\varphi\in\mathcal{S}
, we have

\displaystyle|\langle\Sh,\varphi\rangle|=\left|\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}\varphi(n)\right|

\displaystyle=\left|\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{1}{(1+|n|)^{2}}(1+|n|)^{2}\varphi(n)\right|

(52)

\displaystyle\leq\left(\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{1}{(1+|n|)^{2}}\right)\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}}|(1+|x|)^{2}\varphi(x)|.

(53)

Finally, take
N=2
and

C_{N}=\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{1}{(1+|n|)^{2}}<\infty,

(54)

and apply Proposition
1.3
to finish.
∎

Now, we will state and prove the Poisson summation formula, which is a powerful tool and can be used to derive the Nyquist–Shannon sampling theorem. And we will derive the Fourier transform of the Dirac comb as a corollary of the Poisson summation formula.

Proposition 3.15

(Poisson summation formula)
.

If
\varphi\in\mathcal{S}
, then

\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}\varphi(x+n)=\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}\hat{\varphi}(n)e^{2\pi inx}.

(55)

In particular, setting
x=0
,

\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}\varphi(n)=\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}\hat{\varphi}(n).

(56)

Proof.

Define

f(x):=\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}\varphi(x+n).

(57)

It is clear that
f
is a periodic function with period
1
(check!) and the infinite series converge uniformly on compact sets (check!), and, in particular, on
[0,1]
.

It suffices to check that
\hat{\varphi}(n)
