Harmonizing biomarker testing in NSCLC: The critical role of cytopathology in delivering comprehensive biomarker testing for patients with NSCLC
Authors: Sinchita Roy‐Chowdhuri, Haresh Mani
Published: 2026

The era of precision oncology has transformed the management of non‐small cell lung cancer (NSCLC). The discovery of actionable biomarkers and the development of targeted therapies, immune checkpoint inhibitor therapies, and antibody–drug conjugates have transformed the clinical management of patients with NSCLC. 1 Therapeutic decisions increasingly depend on comprehensive molecular profiling and biomarker assessment based on the testing recommendations from clinical practice guidelines. 2 In a recent article in Cancer, 3 Patel and colleagues present a timely analysis of biomarker testing recommendations across major professional society guidelines and highlight an often‐overlooked challenge in clinical decision‐making: variability in guideline interpretation and implementation despite broad agreement on fundamental principles. The work, conducted through the American Cancer Society National Lung Cancer Roundtable Biomarker Consensus Committee, highlights an issue of growing relevance as the number of actionable biomarkers continues to expand and the complexity of testing strategies increases.

Using a modified RAND Appropriateness Method, the authors compared recommendations from the National Comprehensive Cancer Network (NCCN), American Society of Clinical Oncology (ASCO), and CAP/IASLC/AMP molecular testing guidelines. 4 , 5 , 6 The clinical scenarios used in the guideline comparisons centered around who to test, what to test, and how/where to test. Their findings demonstrate that although substantial concordance exists among contemporary guidelines, particularly between NCCN and ASCO, important differences in wording, scope, and framing may lead to variability in clinical practice. The CAP/IASLC/AMP guidelines were included in the initial review by the panel; however, unlike the NCCN and ASCO guidelines, which are regularly updated, the CAP/IASLC/AMP guidelines have not been updated since 2018, causing significant discordance in specific areas (for instance, the CAP/IASLC/AMP guidelines recommend only EGFR, ALK, ROS1 for testing, whereas NCCN and ASCO recommend broad panel‐based testing for BRAF, HER2, RET, MET exon 14 skipping, NTRK, and KRAS alterations in addition to EGFR, ALK, and ROS1). The authors did note that the CAP/IASLC/AMP guidelines are currently being updated and a comparison with the updated guideline could have significant changes to their conclusions.

One of the key findings in this study by Patel and colleagues is that even though NCCN and ASCO recommendations were concordant in nearly 90% of evaluated clinical scenarios, consensus among expert reviewers was achieved in only approximately half of these scenarios. This highlights how subtle differences in language can influence clinical decision‐making and that concordance between guidelines does not necessarily translate into how guideline recommendations are applied in clinical practice. The work by Patel et al. is also noteworthy in recognizing that barriers to implementing comprehensive biomarker testing extend far beyond guideline development. Tissue limitations, turnaround times, provider awareness, reimbursement challenges, and access to appropriate testing platforms remain major obstacles. 2 , 7 These challenges frequently manifest first within pathology departments.

For practicing cytopathologists, the importance of this study extends well beyond guideline harmonization. It highlights the increasingly central role of cytopathology in ensuring that every patient with advanced NSCLC receives timely and comprehensive biomarker testing. Cytopathologists function not only as diagnosticians but also as stewards of the entire biomarker testing process. 8 From guiding collection of an adequate specimen during tissue acquisition, to specimen triage, processing and downstream handling, to evaluating molecular adequacy and testing strategies, the cytopathology laboratory serves as the operational hub through which precision oncology becomes possible. 9

This is particularly relevant in advanced‐stage NSCLC, where cytology specimens often represent the only diagnostic material available. Endobronchial ultrasound‐guided fine needle aspiration, transthoracic needle aspiration, aspirations from metastatic sites, and pleural fluid cytology increasingly represent the small specimens available for biomarker testing. 10 , 11 , 12 The study's findings reinforce the need for pathology teams to move toward a comprehensive stewardship model in which specimen adequacy is defined not only by diagnostic sufficiency but also by molecular adequacy. 13 Given the rapid advances in identifying newer targets for therapy, every cytology preparation (including smears, cell block sections, liquid‐based cytology) represents a valuable and finite testing substrate whose handling and management may directly influence therapeutic options for patients with NSCLC. 9 , 14 , 15 , 16 Cytopathology departments should ensure optimal specimen handling to maximize cell‐block adequacy and should be prudent in using cell‐blocks for immunohistochemistry. As minimally invasive sampling techniques continue to expand, cytology specimens are increasingly expected to support diagnosis, immunohistochemistry, comprehensive genomic profiling, and emerging biomarker assays. 9 Pathology leadership will need to play an essential role in developing standardized protocols, ensuring efficient laboratory workflows, and establishing testing strategies for precision oncology programs to function equitably and efficiently.

Another key observation in this study by Patel and colleagues is the recognition that implementation of guidelines may often depend on the linguistic variability within guidelines. 17 The use of terminology such as “should,” “consider,” “may,” and “must” can influence clinician interpretation and subsequent testing behavior. The use of specific terminology in evidence‐based guidelines is often based on the Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development and Evaluation approach during the guideline development process. 18 , 19 Although such differences in terminology may appear subtle, they frequently have important consequences when translated into institutional workflows, electronic ordering systems, reflex testing protocols, and payer policies. 7 For cytopathology laboratories that are developing reflex testing algorithms, biomarker pathways, and decision‐support tools these distinctions may be especially relevant when translating guideline language into operational workflows. The challenge for guideline developers will be to simplify language and avoid ambiguity in deciding “who to test, what to test, and how/where to test.”

This study serves as a reminder that comprehensive biomarker testing is not merely a laboratory procedure; it is a multidisciplinary process requiring coordination among interventional pulmonologists, interventional radiologists, thoracic surgeons, medical oncologists, pathologists, and molecular laboratory professionals. 20 The success of precision oncology therefore depends on harmonized recommendations, multidisciplinary coordination, and establishing workflows capable of delivering reliable molecular results from limited specimens that can be easily translated into therapeutic decisions. The continued engagement of the cytopathology community and laboratory professionals will be essential in translating guideline‐driven recommendations into practical, efficient, and equitable testing pathways.
