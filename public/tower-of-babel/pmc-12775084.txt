NGS and the design of an optimized phage display workflow for peptide discovery
Authors: Babak Bakhshinejad, Andreas Kjaer
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPhage display is a powerful technology that has demonstrated great potential in identifying peptides with high binding affinity and specificity toward a broad spectrum of biological targets. The undesired enrichment of nonspecific binders remains a major challenge in phage display selection. The integration of next-generation sequencing (NGS) into phage display has expanded the horizons of ligand discovery by significantly enhancing our ability to interrogate the massive sequence space of combinatorial phage display libraries and providing quantitative information about their composition and evolution during biopanning. NGS findings have provided strong support for the notion that the selection output still contains a large number of nonspecifically enriched peptide sequences that could not be removed or identified by traditional strategies for biopanning optimization. Despite its great potential for increasing the strength of peptide discovery, the routine NGS-based phage display workflow, which relies on analyzing the biopanning output, fails to effectively distinguish thousands of nonspecific peptides from specific target-binding sequences. By incorporating precise control experiments—including the NGS characterization of the unamplified and amplified naïve libraries and the outputs of targetless and replicate selections—alongside the thoughtful data analysis and interpretation, we propose an optimized workflow of NGS-based phage display that would be capable of distinguishing many target-specific peptides from the overwhelming background of nonspecific binders. Applying such a systematic approach will not only advance fundamental research for peptide discovery but also hold promise for the clinic, where these peptides can serve as the foundation for next-generation diagnostic and therapeutic platforms in precision medicine.Keywords: Biopanning, Combinatorial libraries, Enrichment factor, Next-generation sequencing, Peptide discovery, Phage display

Introduction

Phage display is a powerful and versatile technology that has significantly advanced the field of molecular selection by linking genotype and phenotype within a bacteriophage particle. Since its inception (Smith 1985), phage display has been widely adopted for the discovery of peptides with high specificity and affinity toward a broad spectrum of biological targets (Bakhshinejad and Ghiasvand 2025). The capacity of peptides to act as modulators of protein-protein interactions and their structural adaptability make them attractive candidates for various biomedical applications. A critical factor underpinning the success of phage display lies in the extraordinary diversity of the peptide libraries it employs (Lindner et al. 2011). These highly diverse libraries, encompassing millions of unique sequences (up to 109), ensure that virtually any target of interest—be it a recombinantly produced protein, cell surface receptor, or even a complex cellular structure—can be explored for specific and high-affinity peptide binders. To provide context, phage display is one of several in vitro display technologies used for peptide discovery. Other systems, such as mRNA display and ribosome display, can achieve much higher theoretical library sizes (up to 10¹⁴) because they bypass the transformation step required in phage display (Ullman et al. 2011; Galán et al. 2016). However, despite their enormous sequence space coverage, these entirely cell-free systems are technically demanding, prone to instability of large RNA–protein complexes, and often less compatible with selections on complex or cellular targets. Synthetic (nonbiological) combinatorial libraries, on the other hand, represent smaller library sizes but can expand chemical diversity beyond natural amino acids (Gray and Brown 2014). But they lack the direct genetic linkage that enables iterative rounds of selection and amplification. Together, these distinctions highlight why phage display remains a widely used and experimentally tractable platform for peptide discovery despite the existence of other alternatives.

In recent years, the integration of next-generation sequencing (NGS) into phage display workflow has significantly enhanced our ability to interrogate the massive sequence space of phage display libraries (Dias-Neto et al. 2009; Ernst et al. 2010). By enabling the simultaneous analysis of millions of peptide sequences, high-throughput sequencing allows for the comprehensive investigation of phage pools, revealing the abundance and enrichment patterns of peptides that would have remained hidden using traditional methods (Sloth et al. 2023). This integration has not only increased the veracity and depth of peptide discovery efforts but also streamlined the process by providing an unprecedented level of detail in sequence diversity. Despite these advances, challenges remain. The inherent complexity of phage display selections and the massive sequence data generated by NGS, coupled with the presence of a vast number of nonspecific binders in the selection output, can hinder the accurate identification of true target-specific peptides. Routine NGS-based phage display relies on subjecting the output phage population obtained from biopanning to high-throughput sequencing. This routine workflow, while offering great promise, often overlooks critical aspects of selection dynamics and fails to distinguish nonspecific from specific binders effectively. To address these challenges, it is essential to move beyond the routine NGS-based workflow and develop more refined strategies. As a result, there is a growing need for an optimized approach that harnesses the full power of phage display and NGS while minimizing the confounding factors associated with library propagation and nonspecific interactions. In this review, we delve more deeply into the landscape of peptide phage display and explore how the integration of an optimized workflow, encompassing careful biopanning design, appropriate controls, and rigorous NGS data analysis, can enhance the reliability and utility of phage display-based peptide discovery. By refining these processes and applying such a systematic approach, we can unlock new dimensions of phage display technology, ultimately paving the way for the development of peptide-based diagnostics and therapeutics with meaningful clinical potential.

Peptide phage display

Phage display was first introduced in the mid-1980s by George Smith (Smith 1985). M13 is the most widely utilized bacteriophage in phage display technology (Pande et al. 2010). This filamentous bacteriophage has five coat proteins. The distal parts of the phage body are capped with five copies of each of the four minor coat proteins (pIII, pVI, pVII, and pIX), and the central part of the filament is made up of 2500–2700 copies of pVIII (each copy with 33 residues) as the major coat protein (Hamzeh-Mivehroud et al. 2013; Rakonjac et al. 2017; Loh et al. 2019). Among coat proteins of M13 phage, pIII is the most frequently used for the display of foreign biomolecules (Kehoe and Kay 2005). In the phage display technique, the sequence of an exogenous DNA is inserted into the gene for one of the phage coat proteins, typically gene III (encoding pIII). This leads the exogenous amino acid sequence to be genetically fused to the endogenous amino acids of the coat protein, forming a hybrid fusion protein. During the translation of phage proteins and assembly of the phage particle within the membrane of the host bacterium, the hybrid coat protein is incorporated into the phage particle (Paschke 2006). As the phage particle is extruded from the host bacterium, the exogenous peptide is, therefore, displayed on the outer surface of the M13 phage. Figure 1 schematically illustrates the life cycle of the M13 phage and the mechanism of peptide display on the phage surface. A central characteristic of phage display is its ability to provide a direct physical connection between the phenotype (the displayed peptide) and its corresponding genotype (the peptide-encoding DNA sequence) (Kay and Hoess 1996). This genotype-phenotype link is the underlying principle of phage display technology and means that each peptide-encoding DNA insert acts like an addressable tag for its displayed peptide. Also, it enables researchers to identify an isolated peptide via sequencing its encoding oligonucleotide in the phage genome.

Peptides derived from phage display libraries have become increasingly important for biomedical applications, and their utilization continues to expand in various fields (Fig. 2). From a clinical perspective, they are at the interface of small molecules and large antibody fragments. Peptides offer several advantages for translation from bench to bedside. They can be produced in large quantities at low cost and easily conjugated with radionuclides, proteins, toxins, nanocarriers, or cytotoxic drugs. Their small size enables efficient diffusion, effective penetration into cells, tissues, and organs, low immunogenicity, and rapid blood clearance. Additional benefits include high activity per mass, ease of customization, and improved stability through cyclization at the N- and C-termini or incorporation of D- or unnatural amino acids. Peptides also face fewer patent restrictions and a simpler intellectual property landscape, facilitating faster clinical translation (Li and Cho 2010; Bozovičar and Bratkovič 2019; Lingasamy and Teesalu 2021). It is worth mentioning that romiplostim, ecallantide, and pegcetacoplan are important examples of peptides derived from phage display selections that have reached routine clinical use. Romiplostim is a peptibody (a fusion of a peptide and antibody Fc fragment) that functions as a thrombopoietin receptor (TOP-R) agonist and is used for the treatment of chronic immune thrombocytopenic purpura (ITP) (Chalmers and Tarantino 2015). Ecallantide is a potent peptide inhibitor of plasma kallikrein, developed to treat acute hereditary angioedema (HAE) (Duffey and Firszt 2015). Pegcetacoplan is a pegylated cyclic peptide that acts as an inhibitor of the complement component C3, thereby modulating complement activation. This peptide drug is used for the treatment of paroxysmal nocturnal hemoglobinuria (PNH) (Horneff et al. 2024). These peptides provide concrete evidence of the clinical translatability of phage display technology.

Applications of peptide phage display in diverse biomedical fields. Peptides identified through phage display technology have a wide array of diagnostic and therapeutic uses, including targeted drug delivery (guiding nanoparticles carrying therapeutic agents to specific cells), molecular imaging (enabling in vivo visualization of diseased tissues), vaccine development (designing vaccines that elicit targeted immune responses), and tissue regeneration (serving as essential components for constructing tissue scaffolds)

In phage display, the exogenous DNA encoding the displayed peptide is first inserted into the phage genome (shown in red in Fig. 1) in frame with gene III (shown in light blue in Fig. 1) before infection; however, the peptide itself is not displayed at this stage. The peptide is expressed as a fusion to pIII during production of phage proteins within the host cell and displayed on the phage during assembly. As a result, the newly formed phage particles extruded from the bacterial cell display the exogenous peptide on their surface. It is important to note that M13 phage infection of E. coli depends on the presence of F-pilus on the bacterial surface. It has been demonstrated that F-pilus expression is highly temperature dependent: production peaks at 37 °C but decreases sharply at lower temperatures, with cells kept on ice losing functional pili and becoming resistant to infection by male-specific phages (Novotny and Lavin 1971). Based on this, the efficiency of phage infection—and consequently the outcomes of phage display experiments—can be markedly affected by the growth conditions and handling temperatures of the host bacteria.

Different systems are employed to display biomolecules on the M13 phage surface, each influencing display efficiency and introducing potential biases during library selection. In the phage vector system (already mentioned), the direct insertion of the exogenous DNA into the full phage genome in frame with gene III leads to polyvalent display (up to five pIII fusions per virion). Although this generates strong binding signals, large peptide inserts can interfere with pIII function, reducing the efficiency of phage assembly and infectivity (Sioud 2019). Phagemid vectors present a solution to overcome the limitations associated with the size of the displayed ligand. A phagemid is a hybrid plasmid that combines the features of a plasmid (a bacterial origin of replication, an antibiotic resistance gene, and a multiple cloning site) with elements of a phage genome (a phage origin of replication and a copy of gIII that is used for cloning of exogenous DNA). A phagemid can be converted to a filamentous phage by co-infection with a helper phage, which provides all the structural and functional proteins required for phage assembly. Because the helper phage carries its own replication origins for plus- and minus-strand synthesis, the replication machinery acts on both the phagemid and the helper phage. However, due to defective origins in the helper, the amount of helper-derived ssDNA produced is much lower than that of the phagemid (Qi et al. 2012). In the phagemid-helper phage system, the exogenous ligand fused with pIII is displayed on the phage surface as a mixture with wild-type pIII, resulting in mosaic display. A major advantage of the phagemid vector system is its capacity to produce only one copy of the fusion ligand per phage particle (monovalent display). This prevents avidity effects caused by the simultaneous expression of multiple peptide copies in phage vectors and thereby increases the stringency of selection (O’Connell et al. 2002; Castel et al. 2011). Compared to phage vectors, phagemids are smaller and can accommodate larger foreign DNA inserts, exhibit higher transformation efficiency, facilitate DNA recombination and gene manipulation due to the presence of a multiple cloning site, allow for more easily controlling and adjusting the expression level of the fusion ligand, and are generally more stable than recombinant phages during multiple rounds of propagation (Qi et al. 2012). Despite these advantages, the wild-type pIII from the helper phage can outcompete the phagemid-encoded fusion pIII during packaging and assembly, leading to representation bias in the library reflected by mostly non-displaying phage particles and low display valency. To address this limitation, a type of helper phage known as hyperphage has been developed. In the hyperphage system, the pIII-encoding gene is deleted from the helper genome, leaving it incapable of producing a functional pIII. Consequently, only the phagemid-encoded fusion pIII can be incorporated into the phage particle during the virion assembly. This modification in the helper phage system has led to a remarkable increase in the fraction of phages carrying a fusion ligand on their surface (Rondot et al. 2001).

Affinity selection of phage display peptide libraries

A phage display library is a diverse repertoire of phage clones, each harboring a unique exogenous DNA insert and displaying the peptide encoded by the inserted DNA on its surface (Cwirla et al. 1990; Devlin et al. 1990; Scott and Smith 1990; Smith and Scott 1993). Peptides of the library possess two attributes that are essential for in vitro evolution: replicability and mutability. The replicability of a peptide is achieved when the phage particle displaying the peptide infects a new bacterial cell, generating a large number of identical progeny phages that display the same peptide. The mutability of a peptide means that a mutation in the DNA sequence encoding the peptide is inherited by the progeny phages and can alter the structure of the displayed peptide (Smith and Petrenko 1997).

Biopanning is the affinity selection process to identify target-specific binders from phage display peptide libraries (Wysocki and Sato 1978; Parmley and Smith 1988; Smith and Petrenko 1997). During biopanning, a highly diverse initial population of phage-borne peptides (selection input) is subjected to selection against a target to produce a peptide subpopulation (selection output) with increased fitness (e.g., high binding affinity to the target). Selection is conducted by passing the phage library over the target (e.g., a recombinant protein tethered to or cells seeded onto a solid support). Bound phages are captured, while unbound phages are washed away. The bound phages are then eluted, yielding the output subpopulation that represents only a small fraction of the input population. This selection process aims to increase the copy number of fitter clones (those displaying peptides with higher binding affinity to the target). The output subpopulation still contains infective phage particles and can be amplified by infecting a fresh culture of the host bacterial cells. The resulting amplified eluate can be used as input for the next round of selection and amplification. After several cycles of biopanning, the eluate from the final round is spread over an agar plate, and a number of phage clones are subjected to sequencing to identify the sequences of peptides displayed by them. The ultimate goal of iterative biopanning rounds is to help fitter clones become incrementally predominant in the output subpopulation, resulting in a pool of clones with the highest fitness. Construction of a phage display library and affinity selection through biopanning. The process begins with the generation of a synthetic oligonucleotide library, containing a diverse collection of DNA sequences encoding a large number of random peptides. These sequences are cloned into a phage display vector (e.g., phage DNA genome), followed by transformation into host bacterial cells. Inside the host bacteria, phage particles displaying the peptide library are amplified to create a large, diverse phage population. This amplified phage library serves as the selection input for affinity selection through biopanning. In biopanning, the library is incubated with the target of interest, allowing phages that display peptides with affinity to the target to bind. Subsequently, stringent washing is performed to remove unbound or weakly bound phages, enriching for those with higher affinity. The target-bound phages are then eluted and amplified, producing an enriched phage population that can undergo further rounds of selection if desired. Finally, the phage DNA from the selected population is subjected to sequencing and analysis of retrieved sequences, enabling identification of peptide sequences with specific binding for the target

Traditional strategies of biopanning optimization: beneficial but insufficient for removing or identifying nonspecific binders

Phage display has demonstrated great promise in identifying peptides with high binding affinity and specificity toward a variety of biologically relevant targets, and peptides gained from the affinity selection of combinatorial phage display libraries have offered enormous potential for clinical adoption (Krumpe and Mori 2006; Brown 2010; Teesalu et al. 2012; Deyle et al. 2017; Saw and Song 2019; Zambrano-Mila et al. 2020; Li et al. 2021; Xu et al. 2021; Zhang et al. 2022; Ning et al. 2024). However, the appearance of false-positive peptides continues to be a major challenge in phage display selection. False-positive hits are represented by phages whose displayed peptides have no binding affinity toward the target (Vodnik et al. 2011; Newton-Northup and Deutscher 2012; Bakhshinejad et al. 2016). Many phage display selections are plagued by the unintentional isolation of these target-unrelated ligands. The enrichment of these nonspecific binders can be explained by the fact that phage display selection is a blind process, meaning that target-binding sequences are not known a priori, and the outcome emerges through trial and error. The reliance on semi-random selection from a vast library of peptides, along with inherent biases in the selection steps, leads the process of biopanning to have an element of unpredictability, resulting in the identification of irrelevant or undesired sequences (Bakhshinejad et al. 2016). Nonspecific binders appear in the biopanning output due to (1) the binding of a phage clone through its body to either the background (components of the selection apparatus other than the target) or the target, as well as the binding of a phage clone through its displayed peptide to the background and (2) target-unrelated enrichment of phages with higher propagation rates (known as fast-propagating clones) compared to other phages of the library (known as slow-propagating clones) (Bakhshinejad and Kjaer 2025).

Using an optimized phage display selection protocol is crucial for reducing nonspecific binding and enhancing the likelihood of the selection of high-affinity target-specific binders. Several strategies have been widely utilized to optimize various parameters involved in the different steps of biopanning for improving the yield and specificity of target-binding sequences (Levitan 1998; Mcconnell et al. 1999; D’mello and Howard 2001; Zhuang et al. 2001; Molek et al. 2011; Lakzaei et al. 2019; Lim et al. 2019; Yun et al. 2019; Panagides et al. 2022; Thota et al. 2024). Adjusting the presentation mode and quantity of the target, using blocking agents along with increasing blocking duration as well as blocking buffer concentration, fine-tuning the incubation time of the target with the phage library, enhancing the duration and harshness of washes, and applying specific elution are some of the standard modifications to the biopanning protocol. Gradual alteration of these parameters during successive rounds of biopanning is performed with the intention of augmenting the stringency of selection conditions and raising selection pressure toward the isolation of target-binding sequences, thus improving the quality of biopanning. These optimizations can, to some extent, increase the success rate of a phage display selection. However, using high-throughput sequencing to analyze the composition of eluted phage populations has provided strong support for the notion that the biopanning output still contains a massive number of peptides, many of which are enriched in a target-independent manner (Ernst et al. 2010; Ac’t Hoen et al. 2012; Rentero Rebollo et al. 2014; He et al. 2022; Lisowska et al. 2022; Sell et al. 2024). The retention of such a great number of nonspecific binders in the output subpopulation has roots in the nature of phage display selection, which is mediated via the tremendous diversity of the naïve phage display library and the biased selection of this vast peptide pool, making it impossible to deplete the library of all nonspecific binders. This demonstrates that even well-optimized protocols cannot entirely eliminate nonspecific peptides. This complex repertoire of target-irrelevant sequences in the biopanning output can interfere with the genuine identification of target-specific binders, mislead the discovery of true target binders, and ultimately misguide the interpretation of biopanning data. These new lines of growing evidence highlight that while traditional strategies of biopanning optimization have undeniably improved the yield and specificity of target-binding sequences, they remain insufficient to fully address the persistent issue of the enrichment of nonspecific binders in phage display experiments and are not potent enough to remove or identify many nonspecific binders in the biopanning output.

Next-generation sequencing: providing a deeper perspective of the sequence composition of phage display libraries

The sequencing platform used for analyzing the results of a phage display selection plays a pivotal role in the accuracy of the obtained data and the successful identification of target-specific binders. A major drawback of the conventional phage display selection using the standard Sanger sequencing approach is that only the sequences of displayed peptides by a limited number of clones become available at the end of selection (Ravn et al. 2010). This low-throughput nature of peptide identification provides a shallow representation of the total peptide pool in the biopanning output. Substantial breakthroughs in sequencing technologies have taken our understanding of the peptide population in the biopanned library to a new level by moving from Sanger sequencing of DNA from a few isolated plaques to high-throughput sequencing of DNA from an extremely large number of isolated phage clones. Using the high-throughput platform of next-generation sequencing (NGS) allows for a deeper representation of the total peptide population in the biopanning output (Ryvkin et al. 2012; Matochko and Derda 2015; Braun et al. 2020; Sloth et al. 2023). Among the different types of NGS platforms available, Illumina sequencing has been used most frequently for deducing the DNA sequences of peptides isolated at the end of phage display selections. Compared to the Sanger approach, NGS can capture an extraordinarily larger coverage of peptide diversity within the output of a phage display selection, enabling a more meticulous census of the sequences remaining in the output subpopulation. Such quantitative information yields high-resolution and in-depth insights into the composition and evolution of phage display libraries during biopanning and holds potential for revealing trends in clone abundance and binding motifs correlated with target specificity over successive biopanning rounds. These insights ultimately facilitate the identification as well as prioritization of promising candidate hits for downstream validation assays and improve the overall efficiency of ligand discovery in phage display selections (Sloth et al. 2023). Comparative illustration of Sanger sequencing and next-generation sequencing (NGS) for analyzing the sequence space of a biopanning output. The entire sequence space generated from a biopanning experiment is represented as an iceberg. Traditional Sanger sequencing can reveal only the tip of the iceberg, capturing a very small portion of the sequence diversity while the vast majority of sequences remain hidden. In contrast, NGS acts like a magnifying glass that uncovers a much larger portion of the hidden sequence space, enabling high-throughput, deep profiling of the peptide pool and exposing a substantially larger portion of the underlying sequence space. Although NGS still does not capture the entire sequence space, it significantly increases the sequence coverage compared to Sanger sequencing, offering a more comprehensive characterization and higher-resolution view of the peptide population in the biopanned phage pool

Comparative studies that have simultaneously applied Sanger sequencing and NGS to analyze the same phage populations provide critical insights into the advantages of high-throughput sequencing for accurate characterization of phage pool composition and efficient identification of target-specific binders. In one study, NGS was able to detect the enrichment of polypropylene-binding peptides as early as the first round of biopanning, whereas this enrichment became evident only after the third round when analyzed by Sanger sequencing. This study also demonstrated the superior ability of NGS to assess residue-level enrichment and depletion patterns in selected peptide pools—for example, arginine-rich sequences among polypropylene-binding peptides were revealed by NGS but remained undetected with Sanger sequencing (Juds et al. 2020). Such positional enrichment analyses lay a statistically robust foundation for identifying consensus motifs and sequence patterns. Similarly, comparative analysis of arsenic-binding peptide selections using both sequencing approaches highlighted the limitations of Sanger sequencing in detecting key target-binding motifs and quantifying sequence abundances concerning the underlying selection pressure. These results support the notion that critical information about sequence variation and selection bias remains obscured when using conventional low-throughput sequencing methods (Braun et al. 2020). In another study focused on identifying peptides targeting murine M2 macrophages, NGS provided markedly deeper coverage and a more accurate representation of the selected phage library than Sanger sequencing. The two most abundant sequences identified by NGS—constituting over 40% and 30% of the phage pools in replicate experiments—were each represented only once and three times, respectively, among the Sanger-sequenced clones. Moreover, Sanger sequencing failed to detect a valid consensus motif in one replicate. These findings underscore the high degree of variability and sampling size constraints inherent in Sanger-based analyses, which are overcome by the extensive sequence coverage afforded by NGS (Liu et al. 2015). In a recent comparative study from our group, we further demonstrated the power of NGS in uncovering phage pool corruption during biopanning—a common but often underreported cause of selection failure. Such corruptions occur when nonspecifically enriched clones, often those with a propagation advantage, dominate the phage population and mask genuine target-specific binders. Many failed selections remain unreported in the literature, and their underlying causes are unexplored, leaving the scientific community unaware of this valuable information. In our study, 57 clones from the third round of biopanning against human CD4 were analyzed by Sanger sequencing, and two candidate peptides were selected for further validation based on phage ELISA results. However, subsequent binding assays confirmed that neither peptide exhibited specific binding to CD4. In our efforts to investigate the reasons behind the failure of phage display selection to identify target-specific binders, the same phage pool was analyzed by NGS, which revealed that approximately 95% of the third-round eluate consisted of a single nonspecific clone (Sell et al. 2024). In a previous study, we had indicated that this phage possesses a strong propagation advantage resulting from a genomic deletion in the lacZ gene (Kamstrup Sell et al. 2022). This corruption, undetectable by Sanger sequencing due to its limited depth, provided a clear mechanistic explanation for the selection failure, leading to the misidentification of target-binding peptides. Early detection of this corruption in the third-round eluate could have prompted a reassessment—or even discontinuation—of downstream analyses of the selected peptides. The findings of this study provide an interesting example of how reliance on Sanger sequencing can lead to a distorted interpretation of selection outcomes and misidentification of nonspecific peptides as apparent binders. In contrast, NGS offers a more comprehensive and quantitative view of phage pool composition, enabling the early detection of artifacts such as propagation biases and nonspecific enrichment. These lines of evidence collectively highlight the huge potential of high-throughput sequencing to refine biopanning workflows, guide data-driven decision-making, and improve the reliability of peptide discovery in phage display experiments.

Limitations of the routine NGS-based phage display workflow in identifying target-specific peptides

Despite the great potential of NGS in increasing the accuracy and strength of phage display studies, offering more comprehensive information about the selection process, and portraying a broader picture of the compositional features of phage display libraries, some complications in using the routine workflow of NGS in phage display analysis remain to be addressed. Apart from biases that can occur during the different steps of sample preparation for NGS, there are some fundamental issues in how NGS is used for the investigation of biopanning output and how NGS data are interpreted for peptide identification. A significant drawback of the routine NGS workflow is that it does not allow for identifying thousands of nonspecific binders still present in the biopanning output and distinguishing them from target-binding sequences. The routine NGS-based phage display is primarily based on subjecting the biopanning output (the eluted and amplified phage population from one or more rounds) to NGS, which allows for counting how many times a given clone’s sequence appears among thousands or millions of sequences generated by performing NGS on the selected phage pool(s). This absolute value is called a “read number” and represents the abundance (frequency or copy number can also be used interchangeably) of the given clone. This absolute number can be converted into a relative value by dividing it by the total number of reads, which yields prevalence (relative abundance or relative frequency can also be used interchangeably). The abundance and prevalence data of a large number of clones are typically used to determine those that most probably display peptides with high binding affinity for the target (Smith 2024). However, NGS-based characterization and binding affinity measurements of peptides enriched during the selection of display libraries reinforce the notion that abundance and binding affinity of a sequence are not directly interconnected. Consistent with this, the high-throughput measurement of binding kinetics of over 20,000 peptide sequences within the selection output of an mRNA display library has demonstrated that the affinity of peptides is not directly correlated with their abundance rank. This is because sequence enrichment depends not only on target affinity but also on various factors during selection that are unrelated to target binding capacity (Jalali-Yazdi et al. 2016). The lack of correlation between the abundance rank of a peptide sequence and its binding affinity for the target is also a very likely scenario for the affinity-enriched pool of sequences obtained from phage display selection and has been reported by analyzing NGS datasets from biopanning of a phage display library on murine mesenchymal progenitor stem cells (Ac’t Hoen et al. 2012). It is important to note that although the lack of correlation between sequence abundance and binding affinity has been well established in previous studies, it has not yet received sufficient attention in the literature and remains an overlooked methodological limitation in the interpretation of NGS datasets obtained from phage display selections. The lack of differences in the enrichment kinetics between specific and nonspecific binders can be attributed to the fact that many high-ranked sequences are actually displayed by fast-propagating clones, and thus, their rank alone cannot precisely and reliably predict their true affinity for the target.

Optimized NGS-based workflow for identifying target-specific peptides in phage display libraries

With the soaring popularity of NGS in analyzing the results of phage display selections, significant efforts have been made in recent years to extend NGS-based phage display beyond merely analyzing the biopanning output. Researchers have begun designing NGS-based phage display selections that incorporate appropriate controls, such as unamplified naïve library to detect overrepresented peptides before selection on the target (Ac’t Hoen et al. 2012), amplified naïve library to identify peptides displayed by fast-propagating clones (Brinton et al. 2016; Miki et al. 2022), targetless selection to reveal potential binders to the selection apparatus itself (Sevenich et al. 2022), and duplicate selection to focus on sequences common between two independent selections for further binding characterization (Rentero Rebollo et al. 2014; Liu et al. 2015). In light of scarce and fragmented but informative lines of evidence from the literature, we believe that the routine NGS workflow for phage display should be revised to identify and filter out as many nonspecific binders in the biopanning output as possible, thus increasing the success rate of peptide discovery. NGS characterization of the (unamplified) naïve library

In the routine mode of NGS-based phage display, the eluted phage pool from biopanning is amplified and subjected to NGS analysis (Fig. 5). The main criterion for selecting candidate peptides for downstream characterization is the abundance- or prevalence-based rank of sequences. A major shortcoming of this library selection and data analysis strategy is that it overlooks the initial abundance of peptides at the origin in the unselected naïve library. In an earlier report, we indicated that peptide sequences in the naïve library present a broad heterogeneous range of abundances, and many peptides have significantly higher copy numbers than expected (Sloth et al. 2022). The non-uniform distribution of peptide abundances arises from clones with a propagation advantage that amplify faster than other clones of the library and thus increase their copy numbers in the short amplification step during library construction (Matochko et al. 2014; Zade et al. 2017). NGS-based characterization of the (unamplified) naïve library aids in identifying sequences displayed by such clones. In addition, the abundance values of sequences in the naïve library can be used as a reference for normalization of sequence abundances in datasets derived from the amplified naïve library, as well as the outputs of target-dependent and targetless selections (Fig. 6). Thus, to obtain a more accurate understanding of the target-specific enrichment of sequences as well as make a differentiation between their specific (target-dependent) and nonspecific (target-unrelated) enrichment during selection, it is highly necessary to conduct NGS on the naïve library and specify the abundances of sequences in both unselected naïve and selected phage pools. To consider the abundance or prevalence of sequences in the analysis pipeline for peptide selection, an optimal approach is to compute the enrichment factor (EFBP) for each sequence. EFBP (the subscript BP for biopanning) is calculated by dividing the abundance of a sequence in the biopanning output by the abundance of the same sequence in the (unamplified) naïve library (Fig. 6). To determine EFBP, a given sequence should be present in both datasets. EFBP represents the number of times the copy number of a sequence has changed during selection on the target and amplification of the selected phage pool and thus is a measure of the sequence’s enrichment in the biopanned phage pool. In the case of running more than one round of biopanning, EFBP can be calculated for sequences obtained from each round. By doing so, it becomes feasible to monitor the evolution of the peptide content of the library as well as track alterations in the enrichment status of clones and their displayed sequences during successive rounds of selection and amplification. EFBP calculation allows for realizing the true enrichment of sequences in the biopanned phage pool independent of their original abundance in the naïve library.

Calculation of enrichment factors (EFs) in the optimized NGS-based phage display workflow. This figure depicts how the various enrichment factors employed in the optimized NGS-based phage display workflow—EFBP, EFAMP, and EFTLS—are calculated. To determine any EF value, a given sequence must be present in both datasets that form the numerator and denominator of the calculation. In all cases, the abundance of a sequence in a specific dataset (biopanning output, amplified naïve library, or targetless selection) is normalized to its original abundance in the naïve unselected library. When multiple rounds of biopanning, amplification, or targetless selection are performed, EF values can be calculated for each sequence across successive rounds. By tracking these EF values, it becomes possible to monitor the evolutionary trajectory of each sequence as well as the entire peptide pool—through iterative cycles of target-dependent selection, amplification, and target-independent selection. These calculations help distinguish the portion of enrichment resulting from true target binding from that due to propagation advantage or nonspecific background binding, thereby improving the reliability of candidate peptide identification.

Sequencing the entire naïve library is not feasible, and some sequences might escape detection due to the limited sequence coverage of NGS. A restriction of the EF approach is that it requires a sequence to be present in both datasets; consequently, sequences absent in the naïve library but appearing in the biopanning output are excluded from the analysis. To address this, a small uniform pseudo-abundance value can be assigned to these missing sequences. For example, if N total reads represents the total number of reads in the naïve library, the pseudo-abundance can be set as: Ab pseudo = 1/N total reads. The main limitation of this approach is that it assumes all missing sequences had the same abundance in the naïve library, which may lead to under- or overestimation of EF for some sequences. Nevertheless, its simplicity and conservative nature ensure that highly enriched sequences emerging during biopanning are not excluded from the analysis. This is highly important, as these sequences have high EFBP and are likely true target-specific binders.

NGS characterization of the amplified naïve library

The enrichment of sequences during biopanning is driven by two factors: selective pressure for binding affinity toward the target and selective pressure for amplification rate of phage clones (Matochko et al. 2014). Therefore, EFBP of a sequence not only exhibits its binding to the target but also indicates the amplification capacity of the phage clone displaying that sequence (Fig. 6). Thus, it is highly important to gain a more rigorous understanding of how much of the observed enrichment is due to target binding and how much of the observed enrichment is associated with the propagation capacity of the peptide-displaying phage clone. To achieve this purpose, the naïve library is amplified with no intermediate selection step and subjected to NGS analysis (Fig. 5). Amplification of the naïve library is carried out for one or more rounds. Multiple rounds of amplification of the naïve library are called serial amplification (Nguyen et al. 2014). A preferred setup is to amplify the naïve library the same number of rounds as the biopanning experiment. Both the unselected naïve and selected (biopanned) phage populations should be amplified under similar experimental conditions, in particular, the same or very close multiplicity of infection (MOI) values. MOI is defined as the ratio of the number of infective phage particles to the number of host bacterial cells and specifies the number of phage particles that can infect each bacterial cell during amplification of a phage display library (Abedon and Bartom 2013). Inappropriate MOI values can skew the enrichment of phage clones in the phage pool and affect the outcome of a phage display selection. A low MOI ensures proper and efficient amplification of the phage population with less competition between phage clones, reducing the extent of unavoidable amplification-associated bias (Sinkjaer et al. 2025).

When the NGS datasets of a (serially) amplified naïve library are sorted based on the abundance (or prevalence) values of sequences, peptides at the top are considered as potential propagation-related nonspecific sequences. Instead of abundance, a more optimal approach is to calculate the EFAMP (the subscript AMP for amplification) of sequences obtained from (serial) amplification of the naïve library by dividing the abundance of each sequence in the NGS dataset of the amplified naïve library by its abundance in the NGS dataset of the (unamplified) naïve library (Fig. 6) and then sort all sequences based on EFAMP. Sequences with the highest EFAMP are most probably propagation-related nonspecific sequences displayed on the surface of fast-propagating clones and should be excluded from the list of peptides with potential target-binding capacity. In contrast, sequences with high EFBP but with no (not present in the NGS dataset from the amplified naïve library) or low EFAMP are highly likely to possess target-binding capacity and can be included in the final list of peptides for synthesis and binding characterization. Overall, amplification of the naïve library can help in identifying sequences displayed by fast-propagating phage clones and filtering them out of the list of promising peptides.

NGS characterization of the targetless selection output

Many phages and their displayed peptides appear in the biopanning output due to binding to the components of the selection apparatus other than the target (Vodnik et al. 2011; Bakhshinejad et al. 2016). The routine NGS-based characterization of phage display does not allow for identifying sequences in the biopanning output that arise from these off-target interactions. Targetless (empty) selection of the library can identify many such peptides that bind to the background (non-target components of the selection system) and are enriched during biopanning in a target-independent manner. For this purpose, a parallel selection of the library is conducted on the well of a microtiter plate that does not contain the target but contains all other components of the selection apparatus, and the output of this targetless selection is NGS analyzed. The same experimental conditions used for biopanning are applied to the targetless selection, and the eluted phage population is subjected to NGS analysis (Fig. 5). Similar to other samples, the abundances of sequences in the NGS dataset(s) of a targetless selection are divided by their initial abundances in the (unamplified) naïve library, and EFTLS (the subscript TLS for targetless selection) is calculated for each sequence (Fig. 6). It is optimal to have the same number of rounds as biopanning for targetless selection with EFTLS calculation for sequences from each round. The sequences with high EFTLS values in the NGS datasets from the output of targetless selection are likely to be selection-related nonspecific binders and need to be removed from the final list of candidate peptides for further characterization. Thus, we need to consider sequences with low EFTLS but high EFBP values and select them as candidate target-binding peptides. A modified version of targetless selection has been reported by combining target-dependent selection, targetless selection, and another control selection first performed on the target and then continued in the next round without the target (called direct control) (Sevenich et al. 2022). A target-binding sequence exhibits a significantly increased EFBP during successive rounds of target-dependent selection as well as a significantly higher abundance in the target-dependent selection compared to the respective direct control and targetless selection. In addition, the researchers of the group developed the TSAT (target sequence analysis tool) program to filter out potential nonspecific binders identified by including the above-mentioned controls, thus enhancing the probability of finding target-specific binders in the biopanning output (Altendorf et al. 2024). In addition to NGS-based analyses, comparing the percentage of phages eluted (elution efficiency) from target-dependent and targetless selections provides an important complementary measure of binding specificity. The elution efficiency value is calculated as the fraction of phages recovered from the elution step relative to those applied during the binding step (output phage number/input phage number). To ensure meaningful comparison, the same number of input phages must be applied for both the target-dependent and targetless selections. This parameter reflects the proportion of phages that successfully bound and were eluted, representing, at the population level, the subpopulation of phages enriched for binding activity. However, this type of analysis does not provide information about which individual sequences are enriched toward the target. Instead, it yields population-level information about the overall binding behavior of the phage pool. If elution efficiency is markedly lower for the targetless selection compared to the target-dependent selection, it suggests that the biopanned phage population is more likely enriched for target-specific binders. Conversely, if the elution efficiency values are approximately equal in both target-dependent and targetless selections, the enrichment observed in the biopanned phage pool is more likely due to nonspecific binding to background components. Therefore, while elution efficiency data cannot identify sequence-level enrichments, it offers valuable insight into the global specificity of the phage population and can be used in conjunction with NGS-based analyses of targetless selections to obtain a comprehensive understanding of the specificity landscape of the biopanned phage pool.

As noted, targetless selection aims at identifying phages whose displayed peptides bind to the background. However, a pitfall of this approach is the enrichment and isolation of phages that bind to the background through their main bodies, not their displayed peptides. Compared to the size of a displayed peptide, the body of the phage particle is a very large entity with various copies of five coat proteins on the surface (Newton et al. 2019). As previously described, the main body of the phage is composed of tens of thousands of amino acids, orders of magnitude larger than the displayed peptide. This creates great potential for the phage particle to interact with the background through the main body, regardless of the displayed peptide. In addition, the large body of M13 phage can cause steric hindrance for binding of the displayed peptide to the background (Wang et al. 2023). Overall, undesired interactions between the phage main body and the background components can potentially result in misidentifying sequences found in the NGS datasets of targetless selection as background binders and consequently excluding them from the final list of target-binding sequences, while they might display peptides with target-binding capacity. This ultimately leads to undesirable down-weighting or the complete loss of many clones displaying potential target-specific binders from the pool of library phages. Due to the potential elimination of many useful candidates, as well as to avoid misinterpretation of NGS results, it is important to analyze data from NGS analysis of the targetless selection with caution.

NGS characterization of the replicate selection outputs

The high background noise, represented by the vast number of nonspecific binders in the biopanning output, poses significant challenges to the identification of those binders that are enriched specifically during library selection (Rentero Rebollo et al. 2014; Petry et al. 2023). Conducting replicate biopanning experiments, followed by NGS analysis on the outputs of these parallel selections, offers an effective approach to identify target-specific binders within the selection output (Fig. 5). The use of replicate selection remains parochial, and there are very few studies in the literature reporting multiple replicates of library selection. One possible explanation might be the cost of running and NGS-based characterization of several independent parallel selections. Interestingly, the NGS analysis of independent selections of the same phage display library on the same target has demonstrated that among the large repertoires of sequences obtained from parallel selections, containing several hundred thousand peptides, only a very small number are common (Rentero Rebollo et al. 2014; Brinton et al. 2016). Apart from some technical biases (e.g., sampling bias), one of the major reasons for the low degree of reproducibility among replicate selections might be the tremendous diversity of the naïve library (used as input for selection) and the resulting complexity of molecular interactions between the library virions and the target molecules, leading to a vast variability in the sequence composition of peptides retained in the biopanning output of selection replicates. However, multiple selections of the same library on the same target can provide a means for reducing the undesirable effects of such biological variability on the interpretation of biopanning data by identifying the limited number of common sequences between selection replicates. In this manner, replicate selection aids in fishing out the sporadic target-binding sequences from a dense population of target-irrelevant sequences. Independent parallel selections need to be performed under identical experimental conditions. Afterward, the eluted phages from each replicate are subjected to NGS analysis (Fig. 5), and the generated datasets are searched for identical sequences with high EFBP values. These common sequences present a high likelihood of being target-specific binders. It is worth mentioning that including a higher number of replicates is an optimal scenario, as it can increase the accuracy of data analysis and successful peptide discovery. However, the number of replicates depends on the financial resources available for the project.

Selection criteria of candidate peptides in routine versus optimized NGS-based phage display workflows

In this subsection, we recap the criteria used by routine and optimized workflows (described in detail in the previous subsections) to select candidate peptides for downstream characterization of their target-binding affinity:

In the routine workflow, candidate peptides are chosen primarily based on their abundance- or prevalence-based rank in the NGS dataset(s) obtained from target-dependent selection. In this scheme, sequences are first sorted by abundance or prevalence (from the highest to the lowest), and the highest-ranked peptides are then selected for further binding assays to validate their affinity toward the target.

In the routine workflow, candidate peptides are chosen primarily based on their abundance- or prevalence-based rank in the NGS dataset(s) obtained from target-dependent selection. In this scheme, sequences are first sorted by abundance or prevalence (from the highest to the lowest), and the highest-ranked peptides are then selected for further binding assays to validate their affinity toward the target.

In the optimized workflow, candidate peptides are chosen based on multiple selection criteria: In the first step, EFBP, EFAMP, and EFTLS are calculated for each sequence in the NGS datasets obtained from target-dependent selection, amplified naïve library, and target-dependent selection, respectively. Thereafter, sequences with high EFBP (enrichment factor in the biopanning output), low EFAMP (enrichment factor in the amplified naïve library), and low EFTLS (enrichment factor in the output of targetless selection) are prioritized for further investigation.

In the optimized workflow, candidate peptides are chosen based on multiple selection criteria: In the first step, EFBP, EFAMP, and EFTLS are calculated for each sequence in the NGS datasets obtained from target-dependent selection, amplified naïve library, and target-dependent selection, respectively. Thereafter, sequences with high EFBP (enrichment factor in the biopanning output), low EFAMP (enrichment factor in the amplified naïve library), and low EFTLS (enrichment factor in the output of targetless selection) are prioritized for further investigation.

Additionally, replicate biopanning experiments allow identification of sequences common across replicates, which further increases confidence in the target specificity of candidate peptides. Therefore, sequences with the above-mentioned EF values and common across replicates (with preferably high EFBP values) are more likely to be true target-specific binders. It is of interest to note that sequences with high EFAMP and high EFTLS are very likely to be propagation-related and selection-related nonspecific binders, respectively, and thus are excluded from the list of candidate peptides.

Complimentary considerations for optimized NGS-based phage display

In this section, we explore additional practical and analytical considerations that can further strengthen the success of peptide discovery using NGS-based phage display. The experimental and methodological aspects discussed here—including the incorporation of negative selection into the biopanning protocol, the number of iterative biopanning cycles, and the choice of elution strategy for recovering target-bound phages—can certainly be treated as standalone optimizations. Their implementation is not strictly required for every NGS-based phage display experiment, nor are they prerequisites for the core optimized scheme outlined earlier. However, integrating these complementary elements into the design of an optimized NGS-guided biopanning workflow can further increase the likelihood of identifying true target-specific binders amidst high background noise.

Negative selection

Negative selection, which is also known as counterselection or subtractive selection, is performed by incubating the phage library with non-target molecules or surfaces before or after exposing it to the desired target (Tan et al. 2018; Lipok et al. 2019; Nafian et al. 2019; Yun et al. 2019). The main goal of negative selection is to effectively deplete the library of nonspecific binders (phages whose displayed peptides bind to unintended targets). This approach is broadly used while working with complex targets, like whole cells (Romanov et al. 2001; Hu et al. 2006; Zhao et al. 2013; Wang et al. 2014; Zhang et al. 2014; Bakhshinejad and Sadeghizadeh 2022), whose surfaces contain a heterogeneous array of off-target molecules and possess high potential to cause off-target interactions between the library phages and the target surface. In whole-cell biopanning, different control cells can be employed in negative selection. For example, normal cells can be used in negative selection when a cancer cell is the target in library selection. The most optimal condition is when the only difference between the target and control cells would be the surface presence (on the target cell) or absence (on the control cell) of the target molecule. This can be achieved by constructing a knock-out version of the target cell, which is obtained by disrupting the sequence encoding the target molecule in the genome, and using it in negative selection. To further enhance the efficiency of whole-cell biopanning, a knock-in version of the target cell can be made by incorporating the sequence encoding the target molecule into the genome. The knock-in and knock-out versions of the target cell can be used in positive and negative selections, respectively. Negative selection is not restricted to whole-cell biopanning and can also be incorporated into the biopanning setup while performing library selection on recombinant proteins. In this condition, a version of the protein, which does not contain the desired epitope(s), can serve as the control (Sakamoto et al. 2017). However, these optimal controls are not always available for cells and recombinant proteins used as the target for biopanning, leading to the reduced efficacy of negative selection. The stringency of negative selection is also important, as over-stringent subtraction of the library can inadvertently discard many phages with desirable target-binding properties. Over-stringent negative selection occurs when counterselection is carried out several times during biopanning (multiple subtractions in one round of selection, as well as single subtractions in each round of selection).

Despite its broad use in phage display selection, in particular, biopanning on complex targets, negative selection comes with the risk of an over-reduction in the library diversity due to the loss of many potentially promising clones. The diversity loss arises primarily from the unintended interaction between the large surface of phage particles (parts other than the displayed peptide) and off-target molecules during negative selection (Wang et al. 2023), which excludes many phages with potential target-binding peptides from the library pool. This is more likely to occur in biopanning on whole cells than recombinant proteins when the vast surface of control cells, with a diverse mix of off-target molecules (exposing a wide variety of epitopes), is used in negative selection. In addition, compromised diversity associated with negative selection removes many competitors from the library, giving a higher chance to fast-propagating clones to amplify exponentially and become predominant in the (output) phage subpopulation.

Optimal number of biopanning rounds

Iterative selection, which mimics natural evolution, forms the cornerstone of classical phage display. The repetitive cycles of library selection on the target aim to progressively expand the proportion of target-specific binders in the phage pool, leading to increased fitness for binding in the eluted phage population in the biopanning output. NGS characterization of the output phage from each round allows for tracking changes in the enrichment of sequences as well as in the composition of the library during the biopanning cycles. However, each round of selection is accompanied by amplification of the eluted phage population, which results in clonal bias featuring over-enrichment of fast-propagating clones that may not necessarily display optimal target-binding peptides and under-enrichment (de-enrichment) of binders with desirable properties in the biopanning output. Furthermore, NGS analysis of the biopanning output during rounds has demonstrated significant enrichment after the first round, as well as a strong correlation between sequence abundances in the output of the first round and the outputs of subsequent rounds. It seems that a strong enrichment of sequences occurs in the first round (Ac’t Hoen et al. 2012; Juds et al. 2020). This might be ascribed to the collapse of the library diversity in the first round, which is an inherent feature of all display-based selection platforms like phage display. By including different controls suggested in the optimized workflow of phage display selection and analyzing the results with NGS, only one round of biopanning can provide us with the required information for finding potential target-binding peptides. Further rounds might be needed for affinity maturation of promising peptide(s) identified in the first round. The already selected hit(s) can serve as the starting point for partial or complete randomization of specific residues to optimize binding properties toward the target (Smith and Yu 1996; Yu and Smith 1996; Bakhshinejad and Ghiasvand 2025). For this purpose, based on the analysis of NGS data from the first round, some amino acid positions of promising peptide(s) are selected for mutagenesis, and a secondary (focused) library, with a narrower sequence space as well as a smaller background noise, is constructed that is more likely than the primary library to contain peptide variants with desired binding characteristics. Based on results from the selection of the primary library, mutagenesis is directed toward residues whose modification is predicted to be associated with increased binding affinity. The affinity selection of such a secondary library through biopanning maximizes the likelihood of finding high-affinity, target-specific binders.

Alternative elution strategies

Elution is a critical step of library selection and is performed to recover bound phages. However, elution not only recovers phages bound to the target but also retrieves phages bound to off-target molecules (background), which have not been removed from the phage pool during the previous steps of washing. The type and stringency of elution can influence the peptide composition of the output phage subpopulation. Nonspecific elution with pH shift (acidic pH) is frequently used to recover bound phages (Smith and Petrenko 1997). Instead, binders can be specifically eluted using a competitor (the free target molecule or a known ligand of the target) (Pande et al. 2010; Colazet and Chames 2018). The eluates obtained from both strategies can be subjected to NGS analysis. Comparing the results of nonspecific and specific elutions can offer further insights into the sequence composition of the target-selected library, increasing the efficiency of the discovery of target-binding peptides. For example, peptides present in the specific eluate but not found in the nonspecific eluate might hold potential for target binding and can be considered for synthesis and further binding characterization.

Computational approaches for analyzing phage display data

A range of curated databases and computational tools has been developed to support the analysis of peptides identified through phage display (He et al. 2019). The Artificially Selected Proteins/Peptides Database (ASPD) represented the first dedicated resource for organizing biopanning data (Valuev et al. 2002), compiling information from 195 individual selection experiments. Despite its pioneering role, ASPD has not been upgraded since 2002. Another resource, RELIC (REceptor LIgand Contacts), provides a collection of computational modules for the analysis of combinatorial peptide data and for mapping protein–ligand interaction sites (Mandava et al. 2004). The MOTIF system integrates a peptide motif database with an epitope mapping tool, allowing users to compare peptide motifs with structural features of target antigens (Batori et al. 2006). The PepBank database compiles peptide sequences obtained from phage display studies and other sources (Shtatland et al. 2007). It was created using text mining of peptide-related publications and public repositories and includes peptides no longer than 20 amino acids. Similarly, the TumorHoPe database, assembled through extensive literature curation, catalogs tumor-associated peptides, some of which originate from phage display screenings (Kapoor et al. 2012). MimoDB (version 2.0) expanded the scope of biopanning data collection by integrating results from random peptide libraries generated through multiple display platforms, including phage display (Huang et al. 2012). This resource was later updated and renamed the Biopanning Data Bank (BDB) (He et al. 2016). BDB is a specialized archive for biopanning data from combinatorial peptide libraries, including phage display. In addition to these databases, other computational frameworks have been developed for processing and interpreting biopanning results. Certain algorithms are designed to eliminate target-unrelated peptides (TUPs) from experimental datasets (Huang et al. 2010), while others focus on extracting meaningful sequence patterns and relationships from enriched pools (Huang et al. 2006; Negi and Braun 2009). Popular tools used for motif discovery, sequence alignment, and epitope mapping include MIMOP (Moreau et al. 2006), PEPTIDE (Pizzi et al. 1995), SiteLight (Halperin et al. 2003), Multiple Em for Motif Elicitation (MEME) (Bailey et al. 2009), and the Short Linear Motif Finder (SLiMFinder) (Edwards et al. 2007). It is important to note that most of these resources and algorithms were originally developed to handle relatively small datasets generated by conventional phage display experiments based on Sanger sequencing.

The introduction of NGS has created a pressing need for advanced computational frameworks capable of managing and interpreting the vast datasets produced by modern biopanning experiments. The enormous volume of sequence information generated through NGS presents both a challenge and an opportunity for researchers. On one side, it demands high-performance analytical pipelines that can process data with greater speed and computational efficiency; on the other, it enables remarkable analytical depth, allowing the detection of subtle variations in peptide populations and the identification of multiple binding specificities (He et al. 2019). In response, numerous computational approaches have been developed in recent years to handle NGS-derived phage display data. These include translation utilities for decoding sequencing outputs, tools for pinpointing candidate peptides with potential target-binding activity, and motif discovery algorithms for pattern identification. In this regard, a MATLAB-based analysis pipeline has been developed to convert sequencing data from phage display libraries into peptide sequences. This single-end processing software parses FASTQ files, identifies constant regions (adapter sequences), and filters reads based on Phred quality scores, retaining only those with scores above 5 in all variable region bases. High-quality reads are then grouped, counted, and translated into corresponding peptide sequences, with all intermediate and final outputs stored as plain text files (Matochko et al. 2012). The group also introduced a paired-end processing pipeline that merges and aligns forward and reverse FASTQ reads, detects adapter sequences with limited mismatch tolerance, trims constant regions, and translates variable regions into peptides. The program outputs text files listing peptide sequences and abundances, along with intermediate alignment data. This improved version replaces the earlier single-end pipeline by resolving mismatches between paired reads, thereby reducing false point mutations (He et al. 2018b).

Beyond decoding sequences, computational tools have been developed to identify candidate peptides with potential target-binding capacity. These tools analyze peptide abundance, enrichment patterns across selection rounds, and sequence features to rank sequences according to their likelihood of binding to the target. By prioritizing promising candidates, such computational frameworks reduce the number of peptides that need to be experimentally tested, streamlining the discovery of high-affinity binders from large NGS-derived datasets. An important example of such a tool is PHage Analysis for Selective Targeted PEPtides (PHASTpep), a MATLAB-based software specifically designed to identify cell-selective peptides (Brinton et al. 2016). Selectivity is critical for clinical applications, as peptides must preferentially bind to the target cell while minimizing off-target interactions. PHASTpep features a user-friendly graphical interface and performs analysis in two main stages. In the first stage, sequences are imported directly from FASTQ files, the DNA segments corresponding to displayed peptides are extracted and translated into amino acids, and the frequency of each unique peptide is determined. In the second stage, the data are normalized to account for sequencing depth and a reference library derived from an unselected amplified library. The software then generates a matrix, with rows representing individual peptides and columns corresponding to positive and negative screens, and ranks peptides based on the ratio of normalized frequencies in positive versus negative screens, prioritizing the most selective candidates for further evaluation.

Several computational tools have been developed for clustering and motif discovery in large NGS datasets. One such tool, the Multiple Specificity Identifier (MUSI), efficiently analyzes extensive peptide or nucleotide datasets to identify target-specific motifs or consensus sequences (Kim et al. 2012). It employs multiple Position Weight Matrix (PWM) models fitted through mixture modeling and maximum likelihood estimation. MUSI produces both PWMs and corresponding sequence logos for intuitive visualization, though it does not report sequence frequencies in its output. Similarly, a MATLAB-based software has been designed for homology finding and the discovery of target-binding motifs (Rentero Rebollo et al. 2014). This software ranks phage display-selected peptides by abundance and aligns them pairwise to reveal sequence similarities, identifying consensus sequences and subfamilies of related motifs. It reads raw FASTQ files from high-throughput sequencing platforms, and the outputs are groups of 3–20 peptides sharing common motifs while retaining both nucleotide sequence and abundance information of each peptide sequence. This tool is compatible with both commercially available and custom-designed libraries, providing a more complete representation of NGS datasets compared to MUSI. FASTAptamer is another versatile toolkit applicable to any NGS-compatible selection strategy (Alam et al. 2015). It organizes sequences into groups, calculates frequencies, compares sequence distributions between populations, and generates clusters or families of closely related sequences to facilitate motif discovery. Another tool is Hammock, which uses a Hidden Markov Model (HMM)-based clustering approach to identify consensus motifs in NGS data from phage display selections (Krejci et al. 2016). The program begins with a fast greedy clustering of highly similar sequences, then converts each cluster into an HMM profile using HMMER to search for additional matching sequences. Local alignments are performed with Clustal Omega, and similar clusters are iteratively merged. The process ultimately produces multiple sequence clusters with consensus motifs and corresponding multiple sequence alignments. Together, the above-mentioned tools provide a range of complementary strategies for motif identification, sequence clustering, and visualization in high-throughput phage display datasets, enabling deeper insights into peptide specificity and diversity.

One of the recent advances in the interpretation of phage display-derived sequence data is the development of analysis pipelines based on machine learning (ML). In line with this, ML has been used to optimize peptides identified by phage display selection. The goal of this study was to generate an agonist peptide for free fatty acid receptor 1 (FFAR1), a promising target for the treatment of type 2 diabetes, with reduced toxicity and enhanced activity. Following the initial identification of the FFAR1 agonist peptide STTGTQY via phage display, a sophisticated ML strategy was used to rationally enhance its biological potency, moving beyond traditional iterative selection. The researchers first generated a focused training dataset by performing comprehensive single amino acid substitutions on the minimized hexapeptide lead, STTGTQ, and experimentally quantifying the agonist activity of each variant. Rather than training the model on raw sequences, they featurized the peptides using 120 diverse physicochemical properties of their constituent amino acids, thereby enabling the algorithm to discern the underlying chemical principles governing receptor activation. A logistic regression (LR) model, refined via least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) for feature selection, was subsequently trained on this data to correlate physicochemical profiles with biological output. This validated model was then deployed for in silico screening of a vast theoretical library of over 5,000 double amino acid-substituted peptides, predicting a prioritized set of high-potency candidates. This ML-driven prioritization proved highly effective; experimental validation confirmed that the top predicted peptide, STKGTF, exhibited significantly enhanced agonist activity, demonstrated by a lower EC₅₀ and superior glucose-stimulated insulin secretion in pancreatic beta cells at lower concentrations compared to the original phage-derived hit (Yoshioka et al. 2021). This work exemplifies a powerful paradigm in which ML serves as a force multiplier for phage display, transforming an initial binding peptide into a therapeutically optimized lead by decoding the complex structure-activity relationships that dictate functional efficacy. ML has also been used to facilitate the discovery of target-specific peptides from phage display selections by designing predictive computational models that accelerate the identification of binders to a certain target. To this end, the vast NGS data from the biopanning of a phage display library on programmed death ligand 1 (PD-L1) was employed to generate a robust training dataset containing high-confidence PD-L1-binding peptides. PD-L1-binding peptides hold potential as anti-tumor therapeutics. To enable a computational model to discern the complex patterns defining a binder, each peptide sequence was translated into a numerical feature space using a comprehensive set of physicochemical descriptors. This multi-dimensional encoding allowed the algorithm to learn from both global composition and local sequence-order information. The authors then undertook a systematic benchmarking of five feature selection methods and twelve distinct ML algorithms, ultimately identifying a Support Vector Machine (SVM) model, optimized with features selected by Pearson’s correlation coefficient, as the top performer. To enhance predictive stability and generalization, the final model, called PDL1Binder, was implemented as an ensemble of ten such SVM sub-models, whose predictions are aggregated to produce a final probability score. This model was not designed to optimize a single lead compound but to act as a high-throughput in silico filter, capable of prioritizing potential binders from vast peptide libraries. The model could successfully identify 83.33% of known PD-L1 binders while filtering out 53.29% of non-binders, thereby offering a substantial reduction in experimental overhead (He et al. 2022). Furthermore, this work suggests that predictive models of PD-L1 binding can be learned from deep sequencing data. By deploying this ensemble model as a freely accessible web server, the study provides a practical resource that takes advantage of ML to intelligently screen and efficiently prioritize vast peptide libraries, accelerating the identification of novel peptide-based immune checkpoint inhibitors and underscoring the role of ML-empowered computational pre-screening as an indispensable first step in modern drug discovery.

Challenges of NGS

Although NGS has driven significant breakthroughs in phage display selection by enabling deep characterization of peptide repertoires, its utilization is not without difficulties. A primary challenge of this technology is the generation of massive amounts of sequence data. Storing, processing, analyzing, and interpreting such large datasets requires an increased amount of computational power, advanced in silico tools, and a high level of bioinformatic expertise (Vekris et al. 2019). This can pose a barrier for laboratories without the necessary infrastructure and dedicated resources. One of the other challenges in NGS-based phage display is the bias that can be introduced during the various steps of sample preparation. This type of bias can arise during withdrawal of phages from the stocks (sampling bias), DNA extraction from phages, PCR amplification, purification of PCR products, indexing, loading of a subset of phage-derived DNA onto the flow cell, and DNA amplification steps inherent to sequencing (Sinkjaer et al. 2025). Such artifacts are especially troublesome in phage display, where accurate quantification of sequence abundance and enrichment is essential for distinguishing true binders from background noise. Some of this bias can be mitigated by minimizing the number of PCR cycles, using high-fidelity polymerases, and incorporating technical replicates. Sequencing errors are one of the most crucial points that need to be addressed while working with NGS datasets (Baum et al. 2012; Matochko and Derda 2013; Ma et al. 2019). Introducing base-calling errors during the experimental part of NGS, as well as defining improper criteria for error identification and elimination during bioinformatic processing of NGS information, can create spurious sequencing errors in datasets. Sequencing errors are more likely to come up from highly abundant peptides (sequences with the highest copy numbers). These errors generate a series of erroneous sequence variants with lower abundances that can be easily mistaken for independent sequences (Rentero Rebollo et al. 2014). The emergence of sequencing errors leads to overestimation of the diversity of the phage pool as well as underestimation of the frequency of high-abundance sequences. Accordingly, sequencing errors possess the potential to obfuscate the enrichment pattern of peptides in the NGS datasets from phage display libraries, which leads to wrong conclusions about the sequence composition of peptide pools, selection of the library, and target specificity of peptides. Therefore, preventing the occurrence of errors and removing them from NGS datasets can hinder the misidentification of target-specific binders. This can be achieved by employing high-fidelity sequencing platforms, optimal experimental design, and robust computational tools (e.g., sophisticated error-correcting algorithms). In particular, paired-end sequencing and corresponding read-merging strategies enhance data quality by cross-validating overlapping read regions, thereby improving the identification and removal of errors within the peptide-coding sequences. Paired-end processing offers advantages, including increased read confidence by sequencing each region twice, the ability to analyze longer libraries without any change in sequencing or bioinformatic workflow, and the possibility of double indexing using two independent barcodes (He et al. 2018b). Additionally, ensuring that custom analysis pipelines, scripts, and raw datasets are properly documented, stored, and made publicly accessible can substantially improve reproducibility and facilitate further analyses by the broader scientific community. Comprehensive documentation of data-processing steps enables other researchers to reproduce results accurately and to adapt analytical strategies to their own datasets. Openly sharing code and data not only supports transparency but also fosters collaboration, accelerates methodological improvements, and lowers technical barriers for researchers with limited computational expertise. For long-term storage and accessibility, raw sequencing reads and metadata can be deposited in repositories such as the NCBI Sequence Read Archive (SRA) (Leinonen et al. 2010) and Biopanning Data Bank (BDB) (He et al. 2018a). Moreover, the establishment of standardized data-sharing practices within the phage display community—such as depositing raw sequencing reads and providing well-annotated metadata—would create a valuable collective resource. Such practices would allow cross-study comparisons, benchmarking of new analytical tools, and meta-analyses aimed at uncovering general principles of peptide enrichment and selection dynamics.

Conclusion

The convergence of phage display and NGS has opened exciting new perspectives in peptide discovery, offering a unique window into the vast sequence diversity of display libraries and the molecular interactions driving selection. Our exploration of this landscape underscores the capacity of NGS-based phage display to expand the horizons of peptide identification, enabling the systematic mapping of enriched sequences with unprecedented depth and precision. The challenge of phage display selection is often compared to finding a needle in a molecular haystack (Rodi and Makowski 1999). Yet, even after one or more rounds of biopanning and recovery of the output phage pool, this metaphor still applies, though now we are seeking a needle within a smaller, enriched molecular haystack. Despite enrichment, high-throughput sequencing analyses consistently show that the eluted phage populations remain heavily populated with nonspecific binders (Sell et al. 2024; Bakhshinejad and Kjaer 2025), arising from the immense diversity of the naïve library and the inherently biased nature of selection that does not fully eliminate irrelevant sequences. As a result, even the selected biopanning output harbors a vast number of peptides, complicating the accurate identification of true target-specific binders and emphasizing the need for more rigorous, integrated strategies to navigate this enriched but still complex molecular landscape. The optimized workflow we propose here builds upon advances shaped by the integration of NGS with phage display technology, offering a comprehensive framework that addresses limitations of the routine phage display workflow. By incorporating careful control experiments—such as the sequencing of the (unamplified) naïve library, amplified naïve library, targetless selection, and replicate selections—alongside thoughtful data interpretation, our approach aims to distinguish target-specific binders from the overwhelming background of nonspecific peptides. This refinement not only increases the accuracy of peptide discovery but also elevates the utility of phage display as a platform for translational research. Importantly, the comprehensive integration of multiple control strategies proposed in our workflow represents a novel approach in the field of phage display. While individual controls have been applied independently in previous studies, their combined application within a single, unified experimental setup has not yet been reported. Although this integrated setup may appear costly at first glance, it can actually be more economical in the long run, especially for research groups aiming for peptides with direct clinical translation. This is because many phage display selections might fail (Smith 2024), leading to wasted financial resources and time. By incorporating all these controls simultaneously, the risk of failed experiments is significantly reduced. This holistic incorporation of controls is expected to significantly enhance the accuracy and reliability of peptide selection, thereby streamlining and accelerating the discovery process in phage display experiments and ensuring that efforts are directed toward the most promising peptide candidates. It is worth mentioning that phage display selection is a complex process with several inherent limitations. Given the vast diversity of peptide libraries—often comprising tens to hundreds of millions of variants—the presence of numerous phage clones with high propagation capacity, the complexity of molecular interactions within the selection environment (e.g., a microtiter plate well), and the high likelihood of nonspecific interactions between phages and off-target molecules, the selection process is far from straightforward. Consequently, even an optimized workflow may still be prone to errors in peptide discovery, occasionally resulting in unsuccessful selections. However, the inclusion of the aforementioned controls in the optimized workflow can mitigate many of the intrinsic limitations of phage display and address several bottlenecks associated with the conventional NGS-based workflow. Compared to the routine workflow, the optimized approach can improve experimental reproducibility across laboratories and enhance the robustness of peptide discovery. This, in turn, can markedly reduce the likelihood of selection failure and increase the probability of identifying truly target-specific peptides from the vast background of nonspecific binders. An appropriate sequencing depth is a critical factor in NGS-based phage display analysis (Sloth et al. 2023). While phage display libraries often possess theoretical complexities on the order of 10⁹ variants (Galán et al. 2016), current sequencing technologies are incapable of capturing this full complexity in a single run. However, exhaustive coverage is not required to derive meaningful biological insights. Rather, the goal is to achieve sufficient sampling to capture enrichment patterns and dominant sequence motifs. In most cases, sequencing depths yielding approximately 10⁵–10⁶ unique peptide reads per sample provide adequate representation of the evolving phage population, allowing for reliable assessment of sequence convergence and enrichment. Importantly, the optimized workflow proposed in this study facilitates comparative analysis of multiple samples based on their sequence composition. Such comparative evaluation can reduce the required sequencing depth and, consequently, the overall cost of NGS analysis of phage display experiments.

The implications of this optimized workflow are profound. Peptides identified through this enhanced approach hold promise as candidates for a variety of clinical applications, including diagnostic assays, therapeutic interventions, and imaging agents. The ability to more precisely and reliably identify target-specific peptides can accelerate the development of new therapeutic modalities, offering tailored solutions for a spectrum of diseases. Going forward, further technological innovations—such as more sophisticated sequencing platforms, advanced bioinformatic tools, and the development of machine learning-based analysis pipelines—will continue to strengthen the combination of phage display with NGS (Croce et al. 2025; Kawada et al. 2025). These advances, coupled with the rigorous selection strategies, will propel peptide phage display into a new era of precision and translational impact, enhancing its potential to uncover clinically relevant peptides and opening new avenues for therapeutic and diagnostic applications. In summary, by embracing an optimized workflow that utilizes the full capacity of NGS and phage display, we can realize the true potential of phage display in peptide discovery. This not only advances fundamental research but also holds remarkable promise for the clinic, where these peptides can serve as the foundation for next-generation diagnostic and therapeutic strategies in precision medicine. Another promising perspective lies in the democratization of NGS technology in the coming years. The rapid evolution and decreasing costs of NGS platforms have already led to a surge in the number of studies employing these powerful tools to analyze the complex outputs of phage display selections. As accessibility to these advanced sequencing technologies continues to expand, a broader range of research groups and institutions will be able to utilize their capabilities, regardless of resource constraints. This democratization of NGS will not only accelerate the pace of peptide discovery but also foster a more inclusive and collaborative scientific landscape, empowering researchers around the world to contribute to the field’s ongoing progress and unlock new opportunities for translational applications.
