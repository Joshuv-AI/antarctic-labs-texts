Spatial structure based deep feature fusion network for autism spectrum disorder classification
Authors: K S Shwetha, G Deepak, S P Chaitra, C K Raghavendra, Bhupender Singh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAutism spectrum disorder (ASD) is a neuro-developmental disorder that has serious impact on person’s social interaction, communication, and behavior. Non intrusive detection through analysis of electroencephalogram (EEG) signals with deep learning techniques has been the prominent area of research in ASD diagnosis. The current deep learning techniques processed EEG signals without considering their spatial structure. They treat EEG features as independent or grid-like sequences, ignoring the non-Euclidean structure of brain connectivity. As the result, the features do not capture the intricate relationship between signals obtained from multiple electrodes considering both spatial and long term temporal dependencies. To solve this problem, this work proposes a spatial structure based feature fusion network (SSFFN) which considers two attributes of spatial proximity and functional connectivity to capture local and long range interactions between brain regions as fused features. These fused features are used for ASD classification using long short term (LSTM) predictor considering the long term temporal dependencies. Experimental analysis of proposed solution against Sheffield dataset and KAU dataset showed a 1% higher accuracy and 4.4% higher recall compared to most recent time frequency synergy networks.Keywords: Autism spectrum disorder, Multiple electrode, Functional connectivity, Feature fusionSubject terms: Computational biology and bioinformatics, Engineering, Neuroscience

Introduction

Autism spectrum disorder (ASD) is a multifaceted neuro-developmental disorder marked by difficulties in social interaction, communication, and the presence of restricted or repetitive behaviors1,2. According to the World Health Organization, approximately one in every hundred children globally is affected by ASD, although prevalence rates vary widely across regions and socioeconomic contexts, reflecting disparities in diagnostic resources and healthcare accessibility3. The underlying causes of ASD remain not fully understood; however, existing research indicates that it arises from intricate interactions between genetic predispositions and environmental influences4,5.

Symptoms of Autism spectrum disorder (ASD) typically emerge during early childhood, often before the age of two. Initially, these signs may resemble general developmental delays in motor or cognitive skills, making early diagnosis challenging6. At present, ASD diagnosis primarily relies on clinical evaluation, where multidisciplinary teams assess behavioral observations and standardized questionnaires7,8. However, this approach is frequently prone to subjectivity, leading to diagnostic inaccuracies and delays in intervention. Towards this problem various computer aided diagnostics techniques using different biomarkers were experimented. Biomarkers like magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), computed tomography (CT), EEGetc were used in combination with various machine learning techniques for detection of ASD. Recently deep learning has been used for ASD diagnosis since it avoids the need for handcrafted feature extraction. Compared to other biomarkers, EEG offered various advantages like cost-effectiveness, high temporal resolution, and noninvasiveness and this research restricts its scope to EEG based ASD diagnosis9. Most existing studies based on EEG analysis for ASD diagnosis do not incorporate a mechanism to jointly represent time- and frequency-domain EEG features, which restricts their ability to effectively capture the comprehensive and complementary information embedded in the signals.Addressing this problem Shi et al.10 introduced a Time–Frequency Synergy Network (TFSNet), designed to integrate and fuse time- and frequency-domain features for accurate classification of EEG signals in individuals with autism.TFSNet incorporated dynamic convolution within the time-domain feature extraction process, enabling adaptive adjustment of convolution kernel parameters to more effectively capture temporal variations in EEG signals. Furthermore, an attention-based fusion mechanism is introduced to integrate time- and frequency-domain features, allowing the model to uncover deeper dynamic changes and complex patterns in EEG activity while jointly representing both domains in a unified framework. Both TFSNet and existing works treated EEG features as independent or grid-like sequences, ignoring the non-Euclidean structure of brain connectivity. EEG data are naturally spatially structured; each electrode on the scalp corresponds to a brain region, and brain activity involves interactions between these regions. The exiting feature extraction methods do not capture the intricate relationship between signals obtained between multiple electrodes considering both spatial and long term temporal dependencies. This reduces the ASD diagnostics accuracy. As a solution to this problem, this work proposes a spatial structure based feature fusion network (SSFFNet) which can capture local and long range interactions between brain regions as fused features. Following are the contributions of this work.

(i)SSFFNet which encodes the spatial/functional relations between the brain regions (signal captured from different electrodes) as a graph. The time and frequency features of each brain region activity are encoded as node features in the graph. The inter regional dependencies is modeled using graph neural network to finally generate a aggregate graph representation.

Related work

Tang et al.11 introduced a hybrid graph convolutional network, termed Rest-HGCN, for Autism spectrum disorder (ASD) diagnosis using resting-state EEG signals. The proposed model extracted stable and discriminative brain connectivity patterns by constructing functional brain graphs grounded in prior cognitive knowledge. It employed a dual-branch graph convolutional structure to capture both common and distinctive connectivity features, which are then fused through an attention mechanism to enhance feature representation. Experimental evaluation on the ABC-CT achieved an accuracy of 87.12%. But this method did not consider the long term temporal dependencies between each EEG segments. Also this approach did not consider the spatial structure correlation between the brain regions for ASD diagnosis.

Xu et al.12 proposed a deep learning framework for Autism spectrum disorder (ASD) detection using EEG signals, integrating convolutional and recurrent neural networks with an attention mechanism and data augmentation to improve model robustness. The study utilized EEG recordings from 60 participants (30 ASD and 30 controls) collected under two experimental conditions: eyes-open resting state and a visual biomotion stimulation task. The proposed CNN-LSTM architecture was designed to capture both spatial and temporal dynamics of the EEG signals, while the attention layers emphasized the most discriminative temporal regions. To mitigate overfitting and enhance generalization, data augmentation techniques such as random cropping, noise injection, and signal permutation were applied. The method achieved best accuracy of 75.68%. This method did not consider spatial structure correlation between the brain regions.

Dede et al.13 analyzed resting-state EEG data from 776 participants across multiple datasets. Their findings indicated that univariate biomarkers alone are inadequate for reliably distinguishing individuals with ASD from controls. This motivates identification of complex interdependencies among EEG features, potentially providing more sensitive and robust classification performance compared to conventional univariate approaches.

Ullah et al.14 proposed a deep learning-based weighted ensemble model for Autism spectrum disorder (ASD) classification using EEG data. In their approach, the raw EEG signals were transformed into multi-channel two-dimensional spectrograms through Short-Time Fourier Transform (STFT). A grid search strategy was subsequently applied to optimize the combination of model weights, leading to highly effective classification performance.Though the method made better usage of spatial characteristics, it does not consider the long term temporal characteristics in the signal and spatial correlation between the different electrode signals.

Ardakani et al.15 segmented EEG signals from individuals with autism and healthy controls into non-overlapping windows, converted these segments into image representations, and classified them using a two-dimensional deep convolutional neural network (2D-DCNN). But the method did not consider temporal characteristics in ASD classification.

Baygin et al.16 proposed an automated approach for detecting Autism spectrum disorder (ASD) using EEG signals. They first extracted one-dimensional Local Binary Pattern (1D-LBP) features and generated spectrogram images through Short-Time Fourier Transform (STFT). Deep features were then obtained using pretrained MobileNetV2, ShuffleNet, and SqueezeNet models. Finally, a two-layer ReliefF algorithm was applied for feature selection, leading to strong classification performance.The features did not consider the spatial correlation between the signals across electrodes.

Ari et al.17 introduced a method that integrates the Douglas–Peucker algorithm, sparse coding-based feature mapping, and a convolutional neural network (CNN). In this approach, EEG rhythms are sparsely encoded during feature extraction and subsequently fed into the CNN for model training.

Tawhid et al.18 proposed an efficient diagnostic framework for Autism spectrum disorder (ASD) based on EEG spectrogram analysis. The processed EEG signals were transformed into two-dimensional spectrogram images using Short-Time Fourier Transform (STFT), which were subsequently analyzed through machine learning techniques for classification.

Ali et al.19 used a 6 layer CNN network to classify ASD from EEG signals. The multi electrode EEG signals were processed as such without considering the correlation between the signals. Also the approach did not consider long term temporal characteristics.

Abdolzadegan et al.20 extracted various linear and non linear features using power spectrum, wavelet transform, Fast Fourier transform, fractal dimension etc. From the features, best set of features are found using mutual entropy. The features are then classified to ASD classes using traditional machine learning classifiers like K-nearest neighborhood and support vector machine. This technique dod not consider spatial and temporal characteristics across different electrode signals for feature extraction.

Most of deep learning based approaches discussed above, are based on conventional CNN which could not capture the temporal variations in the EEG signal. Addressing this problem, Shi et al.10 introduced a Time–Frequency Synergy Network (TFSNet), which was able to capture both time and frequency domain characteristics of EEG signal using a dynamic convolution. Authors also proposed an attention mechanism to fuse time domain and frequency domain features. But a problem in this method is that it does not exploit the spatial/functional relations between the brain regions spread out as signals across multiple electrodes. Based on this observation, this work proposes a spatial structure based fusion to exploit the spatial/ functional relations between electrode readings along with joint time/frequency domain characteristics to obtain better feature representation for ASD detection.

Methodology

Traditional deep learning architectures, such as convolutional neural networks (CNNs) and recurrent models, often treat EEG features as independent temporal or spatial sequences, overlooking the non-Euclidean topology of the brain’s functional organization. This limitation constrains their ability to model the true interdependencies and communication pathways that underlie cognitive and behavioral functions in ASD. Each EEG electrode corresponds to a specific cortical area, and their interactions encode important information about neural synchronization and connectivity patterns that are often disrupted in individuals with ASD. Capturing these spatial relationships is therefore essential for building diagnostic models that reflect the underlying neurophysiology rather than purely statistical feature correlations.

Graph Neural Networks (GNNs)21 have recently emerged as an effective solution to model such complex, non-grid structured data. Unlike conventional convolutional approaches, which operate on regular image-like representations, GNNs generalize the concept of convolution to graph domains, allowing direct learning on networks of connected nodes. In the context of EEG analysis, each electrode can be represented as a node, while the edges between them represent either physical proximity or functional connectivity—defined through correlation, coherence, or phase synchronization between signals. This structure allows GNNs to perform message passing, where each electrode node aggregates information from its connected neighbors, enabling the model to learn how brain regions interact during neural processing. Through this process, GNNs can capture both local and long-range dependencies across the scalp, modeling how activity in one region may influence or coordinate with another, which is crucial for characterizing the atypical connectivity patterns frequently observed in ASD.

TFSNet10 effectively captures temporal and spectral characteristics of EEG signals through dynamic convolution and attention-based fusion but treats each electrode channel independently. By embedding a GNN module after time–frequency feature extraction, the network can explicitly model spatial and functional dependencies among electrodes, creating a unified representation that encodes temporal, spectral, and topological information simultaneously. This approach allows the model to move beyond feature-level concatenation, achieving structure-aware fusion that mirrors real brain network interactions. This improvised TFSNet modeled is referred as SSFFNet in this work. The architecture of the SSFFNet is given in Fig. 1.

Architecture of proposed SSFFNet.

The SSFFNet introduces a GNN-based module positioned after the time- and frequency-domain feature extraction stages, enabling structure-aware feature fusion that reflects both domain-specific characteristics and topological correlations across electrodes. Initially, the raw EEG signals are preprocessed and split into 2 s segments. Each segment is decomposed into two parallel branches: the time-domain and frequency-domain feature extraction pathways. The time-domain branch employs a Temporal Dynamic Residual Block (TDRB)with dynamic convolution to adaptively adjust kernel parameters and capture non-stationary temporal fluctuations in neural activity. Simultaneously, the frequency-domain branch transforms EEG signals using the Short-Time Fourier Transform (STFT) to obtain spectral representations. These are subsequently processed by a Convolutional Block Attention Module (CBAM) and a Transformer encoder, which together enhance salient frequency patterns while preserving temporal dependencies across frequency bands. The outputs of both branches are then concatenated along the feature dimension, forming a unified representation that contains complementary temporal and spectral information for each electrode channel.

At this stage, the concatenated feature matrix serves as the node feature input to the GNN fusion block. The adjacency matrix is constructed using joint spatial proximity and functional connectivity. Functional connectivity represents statistical dependencies between EEG signals recorded at different electrodes, representing neural co-activation or synchronization. Result

Experimental evaluation of proposed SSFFNet is conducted against two datasets of Sheffield dataset22 and KAU dataset23.The Sheffield dataset comprises EEG recordings from 28 individuals diagnosed with Autism spectrum disorder (ASD) and 28 neurologically typical control participants, aged between 18 and 68 years. Each recording was acquired during a 2.5-minute (150-second) resting-state session with participants keeping their eyes closed. The KAU dataset consists of EEG recordings from 17 participants, including 12 individuals with Autism spectrum disorder (ASD) (boys and girls aged 6–20 years) and 5 neurotypical control subjects (boys aged 9–13 years). For the purpose of evaluation of proposed solution against larger dataset ABC-CT26 having 280 participants with ASD and 119 participants with TD, aged 6–11 years is used. The EEG signals in ABC-CT dataset were collected with the 128-channel EEG acquire equipment and the sampling rate is 1000 Hz.

Performance is measured in terms of accuracy, precision, recall, specificity and F1-score. Experimentation is conducted using fivefold cross validation by splitting the EEG dataset into 5 random parts and results are averaged. For comparison, the TFSNet10 in combination with random forest, KNN and SVM are used. In addition, the deep learning networks of EEGNet24, DeepConvNet25, ShallowConvNet26 used in TFSNet10 for comparison are also used for comparison against proposed SSFFNet.

The experimental results presented in Tables 2, 3, 4, 5 and 6 clearly demonstrate the superior performance of the proposed Spatial Structure Based Feature Fusion Network (SSFFNet) compared to both traditional classifiers combined with TFSNet and existing deep learning models. Across both the Sheffield and KAU datasets, SSFFNet consistently achieved the highest accuracy, precision, recall, specificity, and F1-score, highlighting its robustness and generalization capability. The improvements, though numerically small when compared to TFSNet, are significant given that the baseline models already exhibit strong performance. The enhanced accuracy of SSFFNet can be attributed to its ability to jointly learn temporal, spectral, and spatial dependencies within EEG signals, which conventional architectures fail to fully capture.

For the Sheffield dataset, SSFFNet achieved an accuracy of 99.56%, outperforming the next best model, TFSNet, by 0.88%. The increase in precision and recall indicates that the proposed model not only identifies ASD cases more accurately but also minimizes false detections. A similar trend is observed in the KAU dataset, where SSFFNet achieved 98.67% accuracy, improving upon TFSNet by approximately 1.5%. This consistent gain across datasets of varying sizes and participant demographics indicates that the integration of spatial–functional relationships through the GNN fusion block contributes significantly to model stability and generalization. In contrast, traditional classifiers such as Random Forest, KNN, and SVM, when combined with TFSNet features, achieved slightly lower performance, emphasizing the advantage of end-to-end trainable deep fusion architectures like SSFFNet.

When compared with deep learning models such as EEGNet, DeepConvNet, and ShallowConvNet, SSFFNet achieved substantial improvements. The CNN-based models primarily focus on spatial or spectral features in isolation and neglect inter-electrode relationships, resulting in reduced discriminative capability. While TFSNet addressed the fusion of time and frequency domains, it still treated electrode signals independently. The inclusion of a GNN module in SSFFNet allowed the network to model spatial and functional dependencies between brain regions, enriching each node’s feature representation through message passing. This structure-aware learning provided a more holistic understanding of brain activity, enabling the model to recognize the complex connectivity disruptions characteristic of ASD. The subsequent LSTM layer further enhanced the model by capturing long-term temporal dependencies across EEG segments, leading to a more temporally coherent decision process.

SSFFNet achieves 97.85% true positive rate with only 2.15% false negatives, while maintaining an extremely low false positive rate of 0.75%. The proposed solution had an accuracy of 97.8% which is atleast 9% higher compared to Rest-HGGN. Thus the proposed solution achieves higher accuracy even for larger dataset. Sensitivity analysis is conducted to determine the optimal balance between anatomical proximity and functional connectivity for ASD classification by varying the in Eq. Results show that purely functional or purely spatial graphs are suboptimal. The best performance is consistently achieved at α ≈ 0.4 across both datasets, indicating that functional connectivity should dominate while spatial proximity provides effective regularization. The stability of performance within α ∈ [0.3, 0.5] further demonstrates the robustness of the proposed fusion strategy. The optimal α value confirms that ASD-related EEG patterns are primarily functional in nature but are best modeled when constrained by anatomical structure.

Functional connectivity heat maps for control and ASD groups based on the 10–20 EEG electrode layout is given in Fig. 5. The control group exhibits symmetric and stable connectivity patterns, whereas the ASD group shows disrupted long-range connections and increased inter-regional variability, reflecting atypical neural synchronization which is consistent with known ASD neurobiological markers. The contrasting heat map patterns justify the hypothesis of explicitly modeling both spatial constraints and functional connectivity to robustly capture ASD-specific neural disruptions in the proposed SSFFNet.

Overall, the results confirm that the proposed SSFFNet effectively integrates time–frequency features with spatial and functional brain connectivity, offering a more complete representation of neural dynamics. The marginal yet consistent improvements across datasets suggest that GNN-based fusion provides meaningful physiological insights rather than merely overfitting the data. Moreover, the near-perfect precision and F1-scores indicate that the model achieves a balanced trade-off between sensitivity and specificity, making it suitable for real-world diagnostic support. These findings validate the hypothesis that incorporating spatial structure and functional relationships within EEG data can significantly enhance the diagnostic accuracy of ASD detection models, paving the way for more reliable and interpretable computer-aided diagnostic systems.

Though the proposed solution was tested against small to large datasets, the suitability of solution for demographic variability and EEG recording conditions cannot be tested due to unavailability of datasets with these characteristics. Since the proposed solution achieved more than 98% accuracy, the features refinements and attention based temporal improvement were not explored. Improvising the proposed fused features using advanced attention based convolutional models proposed in27–29 and improving temporal modeling efficiency using advanced attention based LSTM models proposed in30,31 can be explored in scope of future work.

Conclusion

This work proposed a Spatial Structure Based Feature Fusion Network (SSFFNet) for Autism spectrum disorder (ASD) classification using EEG signals. The proposed network integrates time-, frequency-, and spatial-domain features to capture comprehensive neural representations. A Graph Neural Network (GNN) fusion block was introduced to model spatial proximity and functional connectivity among electrodes. The fused features were further processed using an LSTM network to account for long-term temporal dependencies in EEG signals. Experimental evaluation with Sheffield and KAU datasets shows atleast 1.5% increase in accuracy and 4.4% increase in recall with the proposed solution in comparison to most recent TFSNet. The spatial functionality dependency proposed in proposed SSFFNet is found to have higher correlation to known ASD biomarkers. Experimental evaluation of spatial functionality dependency model propped in SSFFNet to larger set of ASD biomarkers over large dataset and analyzing the impact of hyper parameter optimization over SSFFNet are in future work scope.
