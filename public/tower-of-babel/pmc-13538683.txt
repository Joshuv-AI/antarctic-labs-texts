Multi-objective optimization in the context of generative chemistry
Authors: Paula Torren-Peraire, Nadine Schneider, Raquel Rodríguez-Pérez, Robin A. Fairhurst, Jessica Lanini
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Designing new medicines requires balancing multiple competing objectives, including biological activity, pharmacokinetics and safety. As generative chemistry expands our ability to explore chemical space, the choice of how objectives are defined, combined and evaluated becomes increasingly important. In this Perspective, we discuss the role of multi-objective optimization in molecular generation, highlighting practical considerations, methodological challenges and emerging opportunities for translating these approaches into effective drug discovery tools.

Introduction

The search for new drugs requires finding molecules that can simultaneously satisfy multiple, often competing, objectives. These objectives can be combined to define a target product profile that outlines the essential properties a compound must possess for a drug discovery campaign to succeed1,2, ultimately aiming to yield treatments that are both effective and safe. This includes critical aspects such as the biological activity, pharmacokinetics, safety, and toxicity of the drug3. Given the vastness of chemical space, identifying compounds with the required balance of properties requires careful trade-offs. These properties are interrelated, often serving as indicators of more complex biological processes, and thus can significantly impact one another4.

In the past, compound optimization more often occurred in a sequential manner5, and potency or absorption, distribution, metabolism, excretion, or toxicity (ADMET) characteristics were improved successively. However, this approach proved slow, resource-intensive, and prone to late-stage trade-offs that compromised overall compound quality6. Thus, today drug discovery requires working in multi-dimensional spaces, considering diverse properties simultaneously and navigating an intricate network of interdependent factors. Consequently, scientists have increasingly turned to formalized approaches to address the challenge of compound optimization in such multi-dimensional spaces7. The design of blood-brain barrier penetrant drugs is an example where such approaches have been extensively explored to overcome the additional challenge of achieving an effective drug concentration at the site of action8.

Multi-objective optimization (MOO) is employed as a systematic framework designed to balance and prioritize competing goals, guiding the selection and refinement of drug candidates based on complex desired features. These approaches use mathematical models to evaluate molecules across multiple parameters. In principle, MOO frameworks allow several properties to be simultaneously optimized, avoiding the limitations of sequential approaches9,10. These methods are well established in fields such as materials science, engineering, and finance11–16, where they are employed to efficiently explore intricate design and optimization landscapes.

Within the realm of pharmaceutical research, a variety of MOO tools and strategies have been introduced, each tailored to help manage the complexity inherent to drug discovery. While MOO approaches are frequently employed to prioritize compounds in conventional drug design settings, they may encounter challenges when applied to modern drug discovery methods that leverage high-throughput data and machine learning (ML) for chemical space exploration. These new strategies, often referred to as generative chemistry, rely on computational techniques to design novel molecules. Traditional drug design follows the well-known paradigm of Design-Make-Test-Analyze (DMTA) cycle, where compound designs are sequentially explored (Fig. 1). Generative methods carry out an iterative process to optimize molecules within a single experiment, improving upon ideas from previous iterations, before continuing the process. Despite its promise, the integration of MOO into generative chemistry is an emerging area and remains largely underexplored in the literature10,17–19. This Perspective highlights the use of MOO in drug design, with particular focus on its combination with generative chemistry. From a pharmaceutical industry perspective, it examines opportunities, challenges, and implementation considerations for its successful adoption. As generative chemistry begins to increasingly influence decision‑making in the pharmaceutical industry, a clearer discussion of how MOO should guide generative designs becomes timely and necessary.

Foundations of MOO in drug discovery

In traditional drug discovery, many different MOO approaches exist to handle high-dimensional property spaces. As drug discovery projects evolve across multiple distinct stages, different design approaches are typically required. In the following, MOO strategies and common applications at various stages of drug design are discussed.

MOO approaches from hit identification to lead optimization

In the early phase of hit identification, the focus is on broad exploration to uncover compounds that can be validated as interacting in the desired manner, and where even a minimum level of biological activity is acceptable. Even though MOO may appear to play a limited role here, alternative starting points can be prioritized to select the ones with the highest potential for later optimization20. At this stage, critical features start to become apparent, such as the minimal pharmacophore, desirable molecular size, or accounting for known competing targets, leading to the definition of the first targeted profile for property optimization.

One commonly used approach is filtering9, establishing a set of desired criteria and discarding those compounds which do not comply with the defined requirements (Fig. 2A). These criteria often introduce knowledge from past experience, which can be project-specific or based on well-known guidelines, such as Lipinski’s rule of five21. Parameters can be derived from successful compounds to identify attributes associated with favorable outcomes. For example, rigorous analysis was carried out to identify parameters that distinguish clinically successful positron emission tomography ligands22, enabling a more focused identification of interesting PDE2A inhibitors. In these filtering schemes, all compounds must comply with a minimal property profile to be considered, and those that stand on the cusp of property limits are excluded. Consequently, a permissive filtering approach is often preferred23, prioritizing candidates that meet the primary goal of a baseline activity signal, while ensuring a minimal set of property criteria such as high solubility or permeability20. Or to select compounds of interest from a virtual library based on on-target potency, synthetic accessibility and favorable ADMET properties24. These filters can be continuously refined as project needs evolve. Once an initial hit is identified, the project focus shifts to exploring structure-activity relationships surrounding the selected starting points. Here, diverse compounds spanning different property combinations are desired, maximizing the breadth of chemical space investigated around each hit. Calculated metrics such as those assessing the relationships between potency, lipophilicity, and molecular size are essential for appropriately balancing potency with ADMET considerations26. For instance, although two compounds may exhibit comparable potency, they can vary markedly in predicted clearance or permeability. MOO helps identify the best overall balance. Some commonly applied calculated metrics are LipE27, LipMetE28, and Ligand Efficiency29, which assess the relationship between lipophilicity and potency (Fig. 2B), lipophilicity and clearance, and potency and molecule size, respectively. These provide interpretable metrics on the most relevant properties and often help to maintain a project in a realistic property space whilst biological activity is optimized. However, these calculated metrics are limited in the number of properties they can consider without losing interpretability. Moreover, they are static in their definition and do not fully assess the profile of a molecule design.

The lead optimization phase offers an opportunity for detailed analysis, where subtle structural changes can yield substantial improvements in molecular properties. Desirability functions, which map the raw value of a property to a normalized space (Fig. 2C), become particularly useful at this stage. Importantly, such desirability functions allow researchers to define the target ranges and optimization direction, whether increasing or decreasing a value is favorable. This provides great flexibility in setting property objectives, adapting these goals to specific projects, and evolving project priorities. By placing all properties onto the same scale, desirability functions enable direct comparison of potential property combinations and facilitate the selection of compounds that align with multiple objectives. For example, compounds for central nervous system (CNS) use can be scored based on desirability functions30, taking into consideration six physicochemical properties: molecular weight, lipophilicity, distribution, pKa, number of hydrogen bond donors, and topological polar surface area. The sum of these is used to quantify the quality of the candidate compounds. However, the selection of these desirability functions is intricate and can lead to markedly different outcomes depending on their definition31.

From hit identification to lead optimization, compound prioritization is constantly applied and constitutes a pivotal step in drug design, as decisions significantly influence the future direction of the project. In this context, MOO is indispensable for evaluating the advantages and disadvantages of candidate compounds. Pareto-based methods, which identify sets of equally optimal solutions across competing properties, are particularly indicated for maximizing molecular features while addressing inherent trade-offs and limitations4,32. These approaches recognize multiple equally favorable solutions rather than a single optimum, known as the Pareto front. These can be useful in understanding how different properties may interact with each other. Since multiple compounds may be the best performing, this can lead to many options for exploration but can also require additional work to consider all options33. Additionally, some minimum values may be required in each property for a candidate; as such, the use of a constrained Pareto front may be considered (Fig. 2D). Minimum realistic values for each property can be established to avoid undesirable property profiles. For example, a Pareto front might be constructed for three-dimensional (3D) similarity, synthetic complexity and ADMET risk34, allowing teams to visualize trade-offs and select compounds that best align with project priorities. However, this must be valued together with the number of objectives, since at higher dimensionalities the Pareto front becomes harder to compute or no longer meaningful for prioritization10.

Throughout all these stages, traditional drug design relies on a sequential exploration of compounds, in which hypotheses are generated, tested, and refined. In this context, MOO can guide the ranking and selection of subsequent candidates. This iterative process, while systematic, is often slow and subject to the biases inherent in the explorative strategies of medicinal chemists. These limitations highlight the potential of emerging approaches such as generative chemistry coupled with MOO, which can dynamically reshape the chemical search space, enabling a broader exploration and accelerating convergence toward optimal solutions whilst reducing the number of compounds required for synthesis.

Emerging ideation methods to explore optimal candidates

Generative chemistry35–37 approaches focus on proposing chemical structures based on a lower-dimensional representation of chemical space (Fig. 3). This is known as the latent space, where complex molecular structures can be described numerically. Computational methods are trained on historical compound data to identify patterns that define this vast space to facilitate its effective exploration. Search algorithms are implemented to navigate through38–44, or adapt45–49 this latent space and have been extensively discussed in the literature35,50–54. It is essential for these methods to determine the optimal direction in which to move or adjust within the space. However, guiding algorithmic exploration and scoring candidate compounds remains a central challenge in generative chemistry. MOO addresses this requirement by guiding the search towards areas that exhibit enhanced properties according to the user’s interest, rather than traversing the latent space randomly. Coupling generative chemistry with MOO introduces an adaptability that traditional optimization frameworks lack, while avoiding inherent design biases experienced in traditional drug design. Unlike, for example, static compound libraries, generative models can continuously reshape the chemical search space in response to evolving project priorities or constraints. This brings the promise of transforming drug discovery by enabling a more efficient design of molecules fulfilling specific targeted properties. Generative chemistry creates an iterative feedback loop between generation and optimization, continuously updating sampling toward the desired property profile (Fig. 1). This contrasts with more traditional approaches, where compounds are manually designed and assessed sequentially, focusing on different property dimensions at each iteration. Generative chemistry may foster faster convergence towards lead compounds in a more efficient and automated manner, potentially broadening the explored chemical space and reducing the number of synthesized compounds. However, realizing this impact fully in drug design depends on overcoming current methodological challenges and open questions.

MOO in the new era of generative chemistry

Generative chemistry is increasingly used for molecular design32,35–37,55, for varied applications and has been shown to be beneficial for various design phases56. Commonly, researchers employ generative chemistry to create a large library of compounds, which are then filtered down and further optimized by medicinal chemists. Cui et al. used this approach to identify a dual inhibitor for JAK3 and TEC kinases57. Thomas et al. combined a generative chemistry approach with structure-based drug design approaches to obtain nanomolar adenosine A2A receptor ligands58. Bachorz et al. employed generative chemistry to design novel RORγt inverse agonists, followed up by an additional MOO selection based on multicriteria decision analysis to select the most promising compounds for synthesis59. Munson et al. explored a polypharmacology setting focusing on designing compounds that target multiple proteins simultaneously60. Similarly, Isigkeit et al. fine-tuned a generative chemistry method61 to enable polypharmacology for metabolic syndrome-associated proteins62. Many other examples exist of generative chemistry use in medicinal chemistry, including proteolysis targeting chimera (PROTAC) design63 and epidermal growth factor receptor inhibitors64, yet they typically focus on a single optimization cycle using generative chemistry as an initial idea generation tool.

However, the practical integration of generative chemistry into drug discovery projects remains complex and non-trivial. For instance, due to the interdependent nature of the property space, modifications made to improve one objective can inadvertently negatively impact another (Fig. 4), or the need to follow up with the manual curation of properties. The complexity also arises as different stages of the pipeline may have differing priorities. In some cases, the goal may be to increase the number of molecules matching a targeted profile, explore a broader chemical space, or identify compounds with the best balance of properties. Choosing the right objectives

Chemical space ideation using generative chemistry methods usually begins by defining what needs to be improved, namely, the optimization objectives. This step is critical as it sets the foundation for the entire generative process. The selection of these objectives is guided by the current explorations of the medicinal chemistry team. As a result, these objectives typically align with the desired target profile and the current priorities of the drug discovery project.

Explicit vs implicit objectives

The selection of objectives may seem straightforward at first, focusing on properties that must be improved in a project. However, medicinal chemists routinely design molecules with multiple factors in mind that are not explicitly articulated. Yet generative chemistry models can only account for the criteria they have been trained or optimized for. Consequently, optimization objectives can be grouped into “explicit” and “implicit”. Explicit criteria represent the objectives at the forefront of an exploration and require direct optimization. Implicit criteria, however, are those objectives that must be additionally controlled for and can account for essential aspects of molecule design which may seem obvious to a medicinal chemist. For instance, carrying out a scaffold-hopping exercise based on 3D similarity (explicit criteria), while also optimizing for molecular weight, polar surface area, and lipophilicity to ensure molecules remain within a reasonable property space (implicit criteria)65. Thus, introducing implicit criteria into the generative chemistry framework helps ensure that designed molecules match the expectations of medicinal chemists.

Taken together, both explicit and implicit criteria must be stated to ensure that useful molecules are designed, emulating the intuition or experience of a medicinal chemist, a notoriously hard concept to model66. Multiple experiments are often required to determine whether additional implicit objectives are needed to obtain the desired outcome. Handling these different types of objectives is essential to proposing chemically relevant compounds, but understanding which implicit criteria need to be introduced to improve upon explicit objectives is an open discussion and may vary depending on the application.

Machine-learning models as objectives

Since generative chemistry approaches sample molecules from a virtual space, objectives are generally based on calculated or predicted values. ML models are increasingly used for compound property predictions (e.g., physicochemical properties67,68). These models relate the molecular structure to a property of interest, also known as quantitative structure-property relationship (QSPR) models, and have shown their applicability for various endpoints, including activity, permeability, lipophilicity, among others69. As such, ML models are typically used as objectives in generative chemistry explorations70–72. However, little has been explored regarding how model characteristics, such as training data coverage or generalization ability, impact the generation process.

Though developing ML models for ADMET tasks is a highly studied field68,73 there is an open question whether the metrics used to validate or select property prediction models for traditional applications should be the same when selecting models for generative chemistry. For example, models with similar accuracy yet different training sets could lead to substantially distinct outcomes given that generative chemistry aims to explore beyond known compounds. This raises the question of whether to use models trained on a specific drug discovery project or chemical series (local) or on the full corpus of available data (global) for predicting compound properties74. There is the possibility that global models, which have learnt more general patterns within the data, may provide more valuable information to a generative chemistry pipeline. With ML models, it is especially important to consider additional aspects such as uncertainty estimation and reward hacking, which are discussed later in this Perspective75,76. In summary, machine-learning models used as objectives in generative chemistry experiments may require a different set of evaluation criteria, such as a broader application domain and highly reliable predictions.

Number of objectives

Though there may be a focus on improving a specific subset of properties, in a drug discovery project, multiple molecular properties are considered throughout the design process. Drug discovery projects are always working with a high number of objectives10 at any one time. However, current generative chemistry methodologies often validate approaches using a limited set of simple objectives77–79. As a result, current benchmarks often fail to reflect realistic drug discovery settings, complicating method selection and evaluation. This could be mitigated by the exploration of a higher number of objectives in generative chemistry research. However, there are no defined criteria for the number of objectives to incorporate for generative chemistry exploration. Increasing the number of objectives can allow for identifying molecules with more complex and diverse property profiles, yet poses the risk of designing an unfeasible optimization. Moreover, objectives are heterogeneous in their definition; objective quality and complexity can be as important as objective count. This can additionally contribute to selecting which objectives are relevant in a generative chemistry experiment.

Combination of objectives

Selecting the right combination of objectives is a non-trivial aspect to consider when setting up a generative chemistry experiment or a traditional drug discovery exploration, as discussed in previous sections. Certain objectives are highly correlated, requiring specific considerations to avoid overemphasizing them10. Additionally, some objectives may be more complex and require significantly more steps for proper optimization than other objectives, which can lead to a slow process when using generative chemistry for multiple objectives. To address this, it can be beneficial to support the optimization by including additional simpler objectives in the optimization72. By taking this approach, the generative chemistry model can more efficiently learn to design relevant molecules, first learning to optimize simpler properties from closely related objectives. This staged approach helps the method to gain information to then tackle more complex objectives, such as designing potent inhibitors by additionally including a 3D shape similarity objective as an easier task80, following a curriculum learning paradigm80–82. However, the way to select these objectives and their combination remains an unresolved challenge, with the risk of forgetting the initial task when optimizing for later objectives. The selection of these objectives must be carefully considered together with the objective criteria and MOO approach, and is currently explored largely through trial and error. Developing more rigorous frameworks to establish which objectives to select and how they should be defined and combined would be a solid first step towards ensuring successful generative chemistry experiments.

Constraints and penalties

In standard MOO, all objectives are maximized or minimized continually. However, this is not the case in drug discovery, where certain properties must fall within specific ranges, and further improvement beyond a threshold might no longer be relevant. For example, once solubility exceeds an acceptable level, further improvement might add little value. Therefore, the list of properties of the targeted profile does not necessarily translate into a list of optimization objectives. Some properties may need to be optimized, while others only need to remain within a specific range or meet a minimum threshold83. In these cases, they can be treated as constraints or penalties rather than objectives.

Constraints or penalties are thus considered to better delimit the searchable space. This aspect introduces a new dimensionality to the exploration by generative methods and can ensure that molecules meet the targeted profile and comply with the expectations of medicinal chemists. Such as constraining LogP to a desired range while optimizing for docking score and other drug-like properties84. Importantly, this use of constraints allows for a reduction in the number of objectives to be considered, potentially simplifying the optimization problem. Such a combination of objectives, constraints, and penalties can be further exploited and could introduce unexplored ways of considering multiple objectives.

Selecting an MOO approach

Once the objectives have been selected, the next step is to determine how they will be combined. This is handled through MOO. However, this aspect remains largely underexplored and is often treated as a default setting rather than a deliberate design choice.

Default and alternative MOO methods

In most cases, as reflected in the literature, mean aggregation methods such as arithmetic mean and geometric mean17 are considered to incorporate multiple objectives. These bear the caveat that they often require weighting vectors to explore the objective space. These weighting vectors are subjective and might add unwanted effects due to correlation of objectives85. Importantly, the characteristics that may appear vital to a practitioner may not align with the optimal generative chemistry exploration. Additionally, multiple MOO approaches are generally not tested to explore how these dimensions can be combined. Therefore, the question remains whether alternative MOO methods that have not yet been studied could offer new insights, improve current generative chemistry workflows, and ensure that optimal solutions are explored. Techniques such as Chebyshev scalarization86 and ε-constraint method87, well known in parallel fields, have not yet been applied to molecular design. Employing alternative MOO methods could broaden exploration into distinct regions of chemical space, leading to new combinations of desired properties. Further investigation is needed, as the added value of these methods has not yet been quantified, particularly in more realistic objective and drug discovery settings.

Interpretability of MOO functions

Within traditional drug discovery settings, MOO approaches like LIPE27, rule-of-521, and CNS MPO score30 are usually selected for their interpretability, enabling users to efficiently prioritize compounds of interest by understanding the relationship between key parameters or simplifying numerous property values. Yet when considering MOO within generative chemistry, there is a trade-off to be established. In order to explore the property space, MOO methods frequently employ complex parameter combinations to improve molecular designs at times at the cost of user interpretability. This inadvertently means that understanding why MOO approaches prioritize certain explorations can be more complex to evaluate. Therefore, when choosing and developing MOO strategies, it can be important to consider whether added complexity or lack of interpretability is warranted by the resulting molecular property enhancements88.

Combining MOO approaches

An essential aspect is how MOO approaches are incorporated into generative chemistry experiments. Importantly, MOO techniques do not operate in isolation. The question is not just which MOO approach is best, but also whether combining MOO approaches can generate molecules that best match the targeted profile. One common example is the combination of desirability functions with scalarization methods (see Table 1). Implementing desirability functions as an initial step allows normalization of objectives and facilitates the incorporation of values of interest by project teams; the output of the desirability functions is then used to calculate the scalarization89. In the same way that desirability functions and scalarization are combined to ensure the most effective use, further combinations of MOO strategies may be considered to improve upon molecule design10,76. This can involve combining novel and traditional MOO approaches, or MOO approaches with different strengths, for example combining calculated metrics such as LIPE with Pareto-based methods. Understanding the shortcomings of individual MOO approaches is essential to determine effective MOO combinations that improve upon outcomes.

Running generative chemistry

After defining the objectives and the MOO approach, a generative chemistry experiment is performed to identify molecules that meet the specified criteria. Working in high-dimensional spaces poses the advantage of addressing a complex joint optimization task simultaneously. When the experiment is well-defined, it is possible to directly address multiple objectives throughout the generative process, while, in principle, moving within a space not biased by the known chemical series.

Uncertainty estimation

Since generative chemistry typically aims to explore a wider chemical space, objective models must be reliable and accurate across a sufficiently large applicability domain. This is particularly critical in ML-based objective models and is a requirement for the ability to design novel molecules beyond the training data. This implies a delicate trade-off between exploring novel chemical space unknown to a project and remaining within an area where models are reliable. To address this, uncertainty estimation could prove vital in the exploration, where models can quantify confidence in predicted values90. Currently, the combination of uncertainty estimation in objectives and generative chemistry is addressed in a few works52. Where some explorations have focused on using the uncertainty associated with objective predictions to calculate the likelihood of an objective threshold being met76. In drug discovery, weighing risks against benefits is crucial. Pursuing confident predictions can increase the likelihood of a successful optimization, such as selecting compounds with a higher confidence, leading to a higher hit rate in antibiotic discovery91. Proper uncertainty quantification would allow the generative chemistry approach to venture beyond the training set chemical space while being aware of the associated risks (e.g., synthesis of an inactive compound), a particularly attractive feature for medicinal chemists when exploring sampled molecules.

Sampling the chemical space

The way in which generative chemistry methods explore the chemical space has not yet been fully characterized. MOO methods can either concentrate on exploitation within a specific area of the chemical space to produce an optimal solution, or sample various regions while evaluating different property combinations, thereby focusing on exploration50,78,92,93. Generally, a generative chemistry method yields a sample of potential property combinations present in a certain region. Determining whether sampled molecules represent optimal solutions or merely examples of available possibilities could improve understanding of structure–activity relationships and scaffold potential. Coverage metrics have explored the concept of the molecular diversity of the explored space94, though they do not consider the property space. Additionally, they conclude that methods tend to explore a small sample of the available chemical space. Assessing potential within a region of the property space can provide valuable information for identifying new compound designs, as well as assessing the quality of an explored scaffold. Determining whether a sampled molecule represents an optimal combination of properties or simply an example from the available possibilities could offer insight into the structure-activity relationship within a project or the potential of an explored substructure.

Intent vs effect

When starting the generative chemistry exploration, practitioners may define an experiment that reflects their desired optimization criteria. However, this may not necessarily result in desirable compounds. Firstly, there may be combinations of objectives in which the criteria are not optimizable or solvable, which would demonstrate a shortcoming in the design of the experiment, not the methods themselves. This can lead to a high compute cost due to few or no compounds adhering to the desired criteria. Another aspect is that molecules may not appear to be the desired structures that chemists wish to see. This can be influenced by objective selection, chemical space definition, and practitioner preferences. One method to better inform generative chemistry methods of desirable structures is to directly include the practitioners in the generative chemistry iterations, known as human-in-the-loop. These methods allow the user to define whether the compounds proposed at certain iterations are structures of interest95,96. This allows practitioners to guide exploration towards regions of interest.

Identifying relevant molecules

With the generative chemistry experiment finalized, it is then a case of reviewing and assessing the sampled molecules. Presently, the output often requires substantial amounts of project-specific filtering and post-processing to identify the molecules that are valuable to project teams. This is often specific to the interests and explorations of a project. Yet it often includes removing molecules with unwanted moieties, unstable molecules, or unfeasible combinations of properties. For generative chemistry to be successful, ensuring that useful molecules are proposed is imperative to benefit from these approaches.

Reward hacking

A known side effect of the combination of generative chemistry and optimization approaches is reward hacking, where the search algorithm identifies molecular features that maximize the score yet produce irrelevant molecules from a drug discovery standpoint75,97. For example, the addition of long chains of heteroatoms to maximize the objective score75. These cases are particularly difficult to account for numerically, as they seem promising from a scoring perspective. Reward hacking is a common phenomenon, meaning generative workflows often require constraints, penalties, medicinal chemistry filters, or manual review to identify unsuitable molecules. Avoiding this loophole while still addressing unexplored areas of the chemical space is a delicate combination since it can be hard to assess which changes will lead to useful molecules and which are disconnected from the intention of the generative chemistry exploration98. Current approaches have focused on quantifying the applicability domain of selected objectives to promote compounds that fall within reliable regions of the objectives97.

Exhaustive library enumeration

A natural comparison arises between generative chemistry and exhaustive library enumeration. Within a drug discovery project, it is often possible to enumerate a large number of compounds within a region of the chemical space. Such enumeration ensures that all boundaries of an area have been explored, within the constraints of known or available building blocks. In contrast, the breadth of exploration achieved by generative chemistry is not yet fully understood. Therefore, it is not possible to discard an area of the chemical space as in exhaustive enumeration since we are not yet able to identify whether a given area was not explored due to unfavorable properties or because it is simply not sampled by the generative model99. Generative chemistry allows scalability beyond the combinatorial limits of library enumeration, into areas of chemical space not limited by specific reaction types or known building blocks. However, particularly as the project progresses into making smaller changes, as in the late stages of lead optimization, the broader exploration of generative chemistry with a lack of interpretability may pose a disadvantage. Within these limited spaces, it can be harder to assess the benefit of generative chemistry exploration over an exhaustive enumeration.

Synthesizability

A vital aspect of these designs is their synthetic accessibility. If a molecular design cannot be synthesized in a timely manner, then the compound must offer a significant improvement to justify the investment or is inherently not useful. The combination of synthesis-aware generative chemistry and MOO approaches may open new avenues for ensuring the synthetic accessibility of molecule designs35,100. These new approaches could contribute to efficient property and chemical exploration together with acceptable synthetic complexity. Methods are starting to be explored that incorporate the synthetic accessibility of a molecule into the generative process, by basing the generative process on known building blocks and compatible reactions101,102. Alternatively this can also form part of the MOO search. By quantifying the accessibility of certain compounds, we can better assess the potential risks of attempting a synthesis if the predicted properties are beneficial.

Validating the approach

Evaluating generative chemistry approaches involves a dual computational validation task. On the one hand, the objectives should be evaluated to ensure that the predicted properties are reliable, but on the other hand, the generative chemistry methods should also be considered, enabling an understanding of the chemical and objective space. Failures arising from inaccurate objectives should be distinguished from limitations of the generative method itself. In line with this, benchmarks have been developed to quantify and compare generative chemistry methods, such as MolScore79, enabling the comparison of generative chemistry methods under the same optimization scenario, and PMO103, comparing generative chemistry approaches under equal computational conditions. This latter work addresses an important aspect with concerns for computational budget, to increase the reliability of generative chemistry explorations, more computationally expensive objectives may be employed. At this point, the frequency with which these methods are called and the impact they have on an exploration are vital. Different generative chemistry approaches may be appropriate depending on the computational budget allowed for an exploration.

Validating molecular designs

Generative chemistry methods must also be validated experimentally71, to assess whether the designed molecules match the targeted profile. Objectives and generative chemistry methods should be validated as frequently as possible to ensure that the predictions are reliable; validated compounds can be used not just to identify potential limitations or triumphs but, importantly to inform later objectives and future experiments. For example, methods can be developed to incorporate the new information into the objective models, as in an active learning paradigm104,105. This can allow generative methods to become more informed of uncertain areas and potentially increase the breadth of the accessible chemical space106. Continued validation will help clarify how these methods can best support medicinal chemists. Throughout generative chemistry experiments, drug discovery scientists should consider whether the generative chemistry approach enables them to identify undiscovered regions within chemical space or new avenues for exploration.

Future outlook

A central question for the next generation of generative chemistry is not whether algorithms can generate molecules that satisfy standard objectives, but whether they can support the design of drug candidates. Molecular design balances competing objectives whose importance changes throughout a project. While current generative workflows often treat these objectives as fixed mathematical targets, medicinal chemists continuously reinterpret their priorities as new data becomes available. Capturing this adaptive decision-making process remains one of the largest outstanding challenges for the field.

Progress will therefore require moving beyond viewing multi-objective optimization primarily as a scoring problem. Future frameworks must become capable of representing different forms of knowledge simultaneously, combining quantitative predictions with subjective preferences, project-specific constraints and evolving hypotheses. In this setting, the role of MOO may shift from identifying a single optimal molecule towards helping scientists understand the trade-offs that define a design space, navigating the high-dimensional space and revealing alternative solutions that would otherwise remain unexplored.

This challenge places the interaction between human expertise and computational systems at the center of future development. Human-in-the-loop approaches95,96, closed-loop DMTA cycles107, uncertainty-aware optimization76,91 and emerging agentic workflows108,109 may provide mechanisms for incorporating medicinal chemistry intuition directly into the optimization process, further blurring the boundaries between molecular generation, property prediction, expert knowledge and decision support. As these capabilities become increasingly integrated, the divide between designing molecules and designing experiments may start to become indistinguishable.

Ultimately, the future of generative chemistry may depend on the ability of optimization methods to augment scientific reasoning. The most impactful systems may not be those that identify the highest-scoring molecule, but those that help scientists decide which questions to ask next, which trade-offs are worth pursuing, and which regions of chemical space merit further exploration. Developing multi-objective optimization frameworks that support this broader role represents, in our view, one of the most important challenges and opportunities for molecular design research.
