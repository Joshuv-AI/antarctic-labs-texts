Modulation Schemes for Functionalized Vesicle-based MC Transmitters
Teena tom Dieck, Lukas Brand, Sebastian Lotter, Kathrin Castiglione, Robert Schober, and Maximilian Schäfer

Abstract
Molecular communication enables information exchange through the transmission of signaling molecules and holds promise for many innovative applications. However, most existing molecular communication studies rely on simplified transmitter models that do not account for the physical and biochemical limitations of realistic biological hardware. This work extends previous efforts toward developing models for practical molecular communication systems by proposing a more realistic TX model that incorporates the delay in signaling molecule release and transmitter noise introduced by biological components. Building on this more realistic, functionalized vesicle-based transmitter model, we propose two novel modulation schemes specifically designed for this transmitter to mitigate transmitter-induced memory effects that arise from delayed and imperfectly controllable signaling molecule release. The proposed modulation schemes enable low-complexity receiver designs by mitigating memory effects directly at the transmitter. Numerical evaluations demonstrate that the proposed schemes improve communication reliability under realistic biochemical constraints, offering an important step toward physically realizable molecular communication systems.



I
Introduction

Molecular communication is a bio-inspired communication paradigm in which signaling molecules serve as information carriers
[
1
]
. molecular communication shall enable communication in environments where conventional communication approaches are impractical and envisions applications in health monitoring, targeted drug delivery, and agriculture
[
2
,
3
,
4
]
. Despite its promise, most existing molecular communication research remains theoretical and is often detached from practical realization, making oversimplifying assumptions that neglect physical and biochemical limitations of biological hardware. Consequently, there is a lack of accurate models for practically realizable transmitters and receivers, which are essential for the practical implementation of molecular communication systems.
Realistic transmitter models must account for the effects imposed by biological hardware, including delay, buffering, and imperfect control of signaling molecule release.
Delay
is caused by the finite reaction rates of the cascades of biochemical processes that are facilitated by biological components
[
5
]
.
Buffering effects
, which are expected in many molecular communication-relevant biological environments such as the bloodstream
[
6
]
, further impact these reaction dynamics. Moreover, signaling molecules may leak from the transmitter or be released in an uncontrolled manner, resulting in undesired
transmitter noise
.
Several efforts have been made toward more realistic transmitter designs. In
[
7
]
, a vesicle-based transmitter was proposed, where signaling molecule release is controlled by pH-induced changes in membrane permeability. In
[
8
]
, a membrane-fusion-based transmitter inspired by natural exocytosis processes was proposed. Recently, in
[
9
]
, an imperfect molecule shift keying transmitter that accounts for energy consumption during signaling molecule replenishment was investigated. In our previous work
[
10
]
, we presented a realistic, functionalized vesicle-based transmitter model incorporating ion pumps as biological modules for energy generation via ion gradients, and symporters as biological modules for signaling molecule release. The energy module can be externally controlled by light, while the ion gradient generated by this module governs the activity of the symporters. As these processes are cascaded, i.e., this transmitter must first establish a concentration gradient before signaling molecules are released, it inherently causes a delay between the light signal and the signaling molecule release, which leads to memory effects at the transmitter. Additionally, in
[
10
]
, the impact of monoprotic pH buffers was considered, which further influence the transport processes of ions and, thus, induce additional memory effects. In this work, we extend the transmitter model proposed in
[
10
]
by introducing a more realistic model for signaling molecule release. Specifically, the release of signaling molecules in the envisioned vesicle-based transmitter cannot be perfectly controlled, as unintended signaling molecule leakage through the vesicle membrane and undesired release by the symporters may occur.
Because of their specific properties, realistic transmitter models require tailor-made modulation and detection schemes. For example, delays introduced at the transmitter by biological hardware induce memory deteriorating communication performance. Typically, memory in molecular communication systems is attributed to inter-symbol interference caused by stochastic signaling molecule propagation. Common mitigation strategies include equalization
[
11
]
, sequence estimation
[
12
]
, and channel coding
[
13
]
. However, such methods often reduce throughput and/or demand significant computational resources at the receiver side.

In contrast, in some molecular communication systems, memory effects can be mitigated directly at the transmitter side, thereby avoiding any increase in receiver complexity. A practical strategy is to employ an inter-symbol interference-aware modulation scheme, which can often be easily adapted by the user in molecular communication systems
[
10
,
14
,
15
]
. Several modulation schemes for inter-symbol interference mitigation have been proposed in the literature (see
[
16
]
for an overview); however, most require modifications at the receiver. In contrast, this work presents two novel modulation schemes tailor-made for realistic biological transmitter hardware. These schemes tackle the memory effects inherent to realizable transmitters on the transmitter side such that an unaltered low-complexity receiver can be used. Such an approach is especially useful when the receiver is set or cannot be tuned adequately, as is assumed here.

The main contributions of this paper are as follows:

•

We extend the realizable transmitter model from
[
10
]
to account for the kinetics of the release module more accurately and to include transmitter noise caused by signaling molecules leakage and the soft activation of the release module.

•

We propose two novel modulation schemes for mitigating transmitter-induced memory effects.

•

We compare the proposed modulation schemes to conventional benchmark schemes and analyze their performance in different system settings.

Buffering Effects

The transmitter model accounts for buffering effects. In the biological context, buffers are solutions capable of resisting changes in
\mathrm{H^{+}}
concentration. The presence of such buffers ensures that the pH value remains stable even when pH-altering reactions occur. Buffers are typically present in biological environments, and thus their effect must be considered in molecular communication systems employing
\mathrm{H^{+}}

10

\mathrm{H^{+}}

3

denote the dissociation constant of the buffer in
\mathrm{mol}\text{\,}{\mathrm{m}}^{-3}

\mathrm{mol}\text{\,}{\mathrm{m}}^{-3}

Leakage

Synthetic vesicles, also those with polymeric membranes, are never perfectly impenetrable and, thus, lose some of their cargo due to leakage of molecules over their membranes
[
18
]
. We consider the membrane to be permeable to
\mathrm{H^{+}}
with permeability coefficient
\gamma_{\mathrm{L}}
, resulting in leakage flux
i^{\mathrm{H^{+}}}_{\mathrm{L}}(t)=\gamma_{\mathrm{L}}\left(C^{\mathrm{H^{+}}}_{\mathrm{in}}(t)-C^{\mathrm{H^{+}}}_{\mathrm{out}}(t)\right)

[
10

\gamma^{\mathrm{SM}}_{\mathrm{L}}

[
19
]

\mathrm{H^{+}}

i^{\mathrm{SM}}_{\mathrm{L}}(t)=\gamma^{\mathrm{SM}}_{\mathrm{L}}\left(C^{\mathrm{SM}}_{\mathrm{in}}(t)-C^{\mathrm{SM}}_{\mathrm{out}}(t)\right)

C^{\mathrm{SM}}_{\mathrm{out}}(t)
denotes the extravesicular
\mathrm{SM}
concentration. The signaling molecule leakage flux varies with time as the signaling molecule supply in the vesicle diminishes.

To summarize, there are several effects that occur in practical transmitters, which the proposed model captures. Due to their finite rates, the
cascaded reactions
facilitating the release of signaling molecules implicitly add delay to the release signal. The
buffer
directly influences changes in
C^{\mathrm{H^{+}}}_{\mathrm{in}}(t)
, thus, it also influences the delay of signaling molecules release. Additionally, the
signaling molecule leakage
and
soft activation of the symporters
lead to transmitter noise.

III-A

On-Off Keying

On-Off keying is a common modulation scheme, widely used in various communication systems, as it is straightforward and universally applicable. In our system, On-Off keying is realized by continuous light illumination during the entire bit “1” intervals of length
T_{\mathrm{b}}
, and no illumination during bit “0” intervals (see
Fig.

4
a), i.e., the light-emitting diode signal for On-Off keying,

, becomes

l_{\mathrm{OOK}}(t)=\sum_{i=0}^{L-1}b_{i}p_{\mathrm{OOK}}(t-iT_{\mathrm{b}}),\quad p_{\mathrm{OOK}}(t)=\mathds{1}_{[0,T_{\mathrm{b}})}(t),

(4)

where
p_{\mathrm{OOK}}(t)
and
\mathds{1}_{\mathcal{X}}(x)
denote the pulse shape for a single bit and the indicator function, which is 1 if
x\in\mathcal{X}
(i.e., if
x
is an element of set
\mathcal{X}
) and 0 otherwise.
In the context of the proposed transmitter, On-Off keying is subject to the delay in the release of signaling molecules caused by cascaded biochemical reactions. This can be seen in
Fig.

4

Section

III-C1
) and the beginning and end of the bit intervals.

III-B

On-Off Keying with Guard Interval

One possible approach for reducing memory effects and inter-symbol interference at the transmitter is the use of a GI, which we denote by OOK with guard interval (GI). The guard interval ensures a fixed period of time
T_{\mathrm{g}}<T_{\mathrm{b}}
of non-excitation at the end of each bit interval (see
Fig.

4
b). Therefore, the light-emitting diode signal for OOK with guard interval (GI),
l_{\mathrm{OOK-GI}}(t)
, reads

l_{\mathrm{OOK-GI}}(t)=\sum_{i=0}^{L-1}b_{i}p_{\mathrm{OOK-GI}}(t-iT_{\mathrm{b}}),

(5)

where
p_{\mathrm{OOK-GI}}(t)=\mathds{1}_{[0,T_{\mathrm{b}}-T_{\mathrm{g}})}(t)
denotes the pulse shape for a single bit for OOK with guard interval (GI).
The guard interval gives the memory-prone system time to re-establish the initial equilibrium state
C^{\mathrm{H^{+}}}_{\mathrm{in,0}}
, such that the transmitter ideally produces the same release signal independent of the previously transmitted sequence. Therefore, the guard interval is expected to improve the communication reliability in comparison to On-Off keying. To achieve this effect, it is crucial to adequately choose
T_{\mathrm{g}}
, as a large guard interval comes at the cost of reduced received signal strength. Here, we assume that
T_{\mathrm{g}}=0.5T_{\mathrm{b}}
.

reaches a predefined threshold,

We refer to this approach as memory-aware modulation (MAM) and provide details next.

III-D

Memory-Erasing Modulation

The principle underlying memory-aware modulation can be extended to erase all memory induced by previous transmissions. We denote this modulation scheme as memory-erasing modulation (see
Fig.

4
d). The required extension ensures that
C^{\mathrm{H^{+}}}_{\mathrm{in}}(t=iT_{\mathrm{b}})=C^{\mathrm{H^{+}}}_{\mathrm{in},\xi},\;\forall i\in\{0,...,L-1\}
is enforced at the beginning of all bit intervals — in contrast to memory-aware modulation, which only takes effect for bit switches. The light-emitting diode signal for memory-erasing modulation,
l_{\mathrm{MEM}}(t)
, becomes

l_{\mathrm{MEM}}(t)=\sum_{i=0}^{L-1}\begin{cases}\mathds{1}_{[0,T_{\mathrm{b}}-T_{\mathrm{d}})}(t-iT_{\mathrm{b}}),&\text{if }b_{i}=1,\\
\mathds{1}_{[T_{\mathrm{b}}-T_{\mathrm{l}},T_{\mathrm{b}})}(t-iT_{\mathrm{b}}),&\text{if }b_{i}=0.\end{cases}

(8)

This ensures that the transmitter states at the beginning of all bit interval are as similar as possible. In turn, memory-erasing modulation exhibits more loading and deloading periods than memory-aware modulation, which increases the average number of signaling molecules released during bit “0”s and decreases the average number of signaling molecules released during bit “1”s. Hence — depending on the detector — memory-aware modulation can ease the distinguishability of the two symbols.

V
Conclusion and Future Work

In this work, we tackle the transmitter-induced delay in signaling molecule release in molecular communication systems. The delay may stem from biological building blocks, which are expected to be key components in future molecular communication transmitter implementations. We proposed two different modulation schemes to mitigate the related undesired and performance-deteriorating effects directly at the transmitter without introducing a loss in data rate. These modulation schemes were then compared with respect to their performance in simulations. The proposed memory-aware modulation and memory-erasing modulation schemes improved the performance of the communication link for different data rates. Additionally, they proved effective for different buffer molarities that are expected in realistic molecular communication systems. In future work, the possibility of further performance enhancement by suitable encoding schemes for realizable molecular communication systems will be considered. Such schemes may be able to further reduce memory effects or replace tailor-made modulation schemes, e.g., when the modulation block in an molecular communication system is difficult to control.
