Cavity Multimodes as an Array for High-Frequency Gravitational Waves
Diego Blas, Yifan Chen, Yuxin Liu, Yanfei Shang, Jing Shu

Abstract
Microwave cavities operated in the presence of a background magnetic field provide a promising avenue for detecting high-frequency gravitational waves (HFGWs). We demonstrate for the first time that the distinct antenna patterns of multiple electromagnetic modes within a single cavity enable localization and reconstruction of key properties of an incoming HFGW signal, including its polarization ratio and frequency drift rate. Using a 9-cell cavity commonly employed in particle accelerators as a representative example, we analyze the time-domain response of 18 nearly degenerate modes, which can be sequentially excited by a frequency-drifting signal. The sensitivity is further enhanced by the number of available modes, in close analogy to the scaling achieved by a network of independent detectors, enabling sensitivity to astrophysically plausible binary sources.

IV
Discussion

HFGWs remain a largely unexplored frontier in gravitational physics, carrying potential signatures of phenomena beyond the Standard Model. EM resonant cavities, widely used in axion searches, provide a powerful platform for HFGW detection owing to their analogous microscopic coupling to EM fields. As tensorial excitations, GWs encode rich information about their sources and production mechanisms in their waveform, polarization, and propagation direction. In this work, we exploit multiple nearly degenerate modes within a single cavity to reconstruct the waveform properties of incoming HFGWs. Achieving this reconstruction requires at least
\displaystyle 5
sufficiently loud modes, which motivates the use of multi-cell cavities to ensure an adequate number of detectable modes. The sensitivity scaling with the number of modes is analogous to that of a network comprising the same number of independent detectors, making a single cavity significantly more capable of probing astrophysically plausible binary sources.

Our method of dissecting GW signals through comparisons among modes with distinct antenna patterns demonstrates that a single cavity can already function as an effective detector array, without the need for phase synchronization. The multi-cell configuration can be naturally generalized to a network of multiple detectors
Schmieden and Schott 2024
;
Schneemann et al. 2024
. Combining internal modal diversity with spatially separated cavities, such as in a long-baseline configuration
Jiang et al. 2024
, would enable even richer searches and reconstructions of HFGWs.

While we have focused on HFGWs from inspiraling PBH binaries with relatively simple waveforms, more complex signals, including merger and ringdown phases
Maggiore 2018
, can be naturally incorporated within our framework. In such cases, upgrading the cavity to a readout system capable of simultaneous resonant and broadband operation could significantly enhance sensitivity
Chen et al. 2022b
;
Wurtz et al. 2021
;
Jiang et al. 2023

Appendix A
Cavity Response to High Frequency Gravitational Waves

A.1
Cavity Mode Equations in the Presence of High Frequency Gravitational Waves and a Magnetic Field Background

We focus on the direct coupling of gravitational waves (GWs) to electromagnetic (EM) fields and their conversion into photon signals in the presence of a static background EM field. This process, known as the inverse Gertsenshtein effect, originates from the minimal coupling between electromagnetism and gravity,

S=\int\mathrm{d}^{4}x\,\sqrt{-g}\,\left(-\frac{1}{4}\,g^{\mu\rho}g^{\nu\sigma}F_{\mu\nu}F_{\rho\sigma}\right),

(S1)

where
\displaystyle F_{\mu\nu}
is the EM field-strength tensor. Under a weak gravitational perturbation, the spacetime metric is expanded as

\displaystyle g_{\mu\nu}=\eta_{\mu\nu}+h_{\mu\nu}+\mathcal{O}(h^{2})
, with

\displaystyle\eta_{\mu\nu}\equiv\mathrm{diag}(-1,1,1,1)
.
In the presence of a background EM field
\displaystyle F^{\mu\nu}_{0}
, the interaction term relevant for GW–EM conversion can be written as

S\supset\int\mathrm{d}^{4}x\,j^{\mu}_{\mathrm{eff}}A_{\mu},\qquad j^{\mu}_{\mathrm{eff}}=\partial_{\nu}\!\left(\frac{1}{2}\,hF_{0}^{\mu\nu}+h^{\nu}{}_{\sigma}F_{0}^{\sigma\mu}-h^{\mu}{}_{\sigma}F_{0}^{\sigma\nu}\right),

(S2)

where
\displaystyle j^{\mu}_{\mathrm{eff}}\equiv(\rho_{\mathrm{eff}},\vec{j}_{\mathrm{eff}})
is the effective current induced by the GW in the background EM field, and

\displaystyle h\equiv\eta_{\mu\nu}h^{\mu\nu}
. The equation of motion for the EM field in vacuum is accordingly modified to

(\nabla^{2}-\partial_{t}^{2})\vec{E}=\partial_{t}\vec{j}_{\mathrm{eff}}+\vec{\nabla}\rho_{\mathrm{eff}}.

(S3)

The signal EM field of interest corresponds to resonant modes inside the cavity, which receive contributions only from the effective three-current
\displaystyle\vec{j}_{\mathrm{eff}}

Hill 2009
. We therefore decompose the electric field in terms of a complete set of orthogonal cavity eigenmodes
\displaystyle\vec{E}_{a}
with eigenfrequencies
\displaystyle\omega_{a}
,

\vec{E}(t,\vec{x})=\sum_{a}e_{a}(t)\,\vec{E}_{a}(\vec{x}),\qquad\nabla^{2}\vec{E}_{a}+\omega_{a}^{2}\vec{E}_{a}=0,\qquad\int\mathrm{d}V\,\vec{E}_{a}\cdot\vec{E}_{b}^{*}=\delta_{ab},

(S4)

where
\displaystyle e_{a}(t)
encodes the time dependence of each mode. The volume integral is taken over the interior of the cavity. These cavity modes also approximately satisfy the conducting boundary conditions on the inner cavity surface.

Projecting Eq. (
S3
) onto a given eigenmode and including cavity dissipation, we obtain the equation of motion for
\displaystyle e_{a}(t)
,

\ddot{e}_{a}(t)+\frac{\omega_{a}}{Q_{a}^{L}}\,\dot{e}_{a}(t)+\omega_{a}^{2}e_{a}(t)=-\int\partial_{t}\vec{j}_{\mathrm{eff}}\cdot\vec{E}_{a}^{*}\,\mathrm{d}V,

(S5)

where
\displaystyle Q_{a}^{L}
is the loaded quality factor of the cavity mode. Here, we retain only the contribution from the effective three-current
\displaystyle\vec{j}_{\mathrm{eff}}
.

We work in the transverse-traceless (TT) gauge for the GW and expand it in terms of the two polarization states
\displaystyle A=+,\times
,

h^{ij}(\vec{k})=\bigl(h_{+}\,\epsilon^{ij}_{+}(\hat{k})+h_{\times}\,\epsilon^{ij}_{\times}(\hat{k})\bigr)e^{-\mathrm{i}\vec{k}\cdot\vec{x}}.

(S6)

Here
\displaystyle h_{+/\times}
denote the time-dependent amplitudes of the GW strain. The polarization tensors for a plane GW propagating in the direction

\displaystyle\hat{k}\equiv(\theta,\phi)
are given by

\epsilon^{+}_{ij}(\hat{k})=\hat{l}_{i}\hat{l}_{j}-\hat{m}_{i}\hat{m}_{j},\qquad\epsilon^{\times}_{ij}(\hat{k})=\hat{l}_{i}\hat{m}_{j}+\hat{m}_{i}\hat{l}_{j},

(S7)

where
\displaystyle\hat{k}
,
\displaystyle\hat{l}
, and
\displaystyle\hat{m}
form an orthonormal triad. In the cavity Cartesian frame
\displaystyle(x,y,z)
, expressed in terms of spherical coordinates, these unit vectors take the form

\displaystyle\hat{k}

\displaystyle=\left(\sin\theta\cos\phi,\,\sin\theta\sin\phi,\,\cos\theta\right)^{\mathrm{T}},

(S8)

\displaystyle\hat{l}

\displaystyle=\left(\cos\theta\cos\phi,\,\cos\theta\sin\phi,\,-\sin\theta\right)^{\mathrm{T}},

\displaystyle\hat{m}

\displaystyle=\left(-\sin\phi,\,\cos\phi,\,0\right)^{\mathrm{T}}.

In our setup, we consider a static background magnetic field of uniform strength
\displaystyle B_{0}
. The effective current induced by the GW can then be decomposed as

\vec{j}_{\mathrm{eff}}(\vec{k})\equiv B_{0}|\vec{k}|^{2}V^{1/3}\bigl(h_{+}\hat{j}_{+}+h_{\times}\hat{j}_{\times}\bigr),

(S9)

where
\displaystyle\hat{j}_{+/\times}
are dimensionless spatial profiles determined by the GW polarization and momentum. The induced signal in a given cavity mode is proportional to the overlap between the effective current and the cavity electric-field distribution. This is characterized by the dimensionless overlap function, or antenna pattern,

\eta^{a}_{+/\times}(\vec{k})\equiv\frac{1}{V^{1/2}}\int_{V}\hat{j}_{+/\times}\cdot\vec{E}_{a}^{*}\,\mathrm{d}V,

(S10)

where the integral is taken over the cavity volume.

In general, the antenna pattern depends on both the GW propagation direction
\displaystyle\hat{k}
and the GW frequency
\displaystyle|\vec{k}|
. In the main text, however, we evaluate
\displaystyle\eta^{a}_{+/\times}
at the cavity resonance,

\displaystyle|\vec{k}|\simeq\omega_{a}
, since the relevant signal is confined to a narrow bandwidth around the resonant frequency.

With these reparameterizations, Eq. (
S5
) can be rewritten as

\ddot{e}_{a}(t)+\frac{\omega_{a}}{Q_{a}^{L}}\,\dot{e}_{a}(t)+\omega_{a}^{2}e_{a}(t)\simeq-\mathrm{i}\,\omega_{a}^{3}V^{5/6}B_{0}\sum_{A=+,\times}h_{A}\,\eta^{a}_{A}.

(S11)

A.2
Time-Domain Waveforms of GW-Excited Cavity Modes

We next compute the time evolution of GW-excited cavity modes driven by the waveform given in Eq. (III.1) of the main text, which is motivated by a binary inspiral. To this end, we first reparameterize the source term on the right-hand side of Eq. (
S11
) as

\sum_{A=+,\times}h_{A}\,\eta_{A}^{a}\equiv h_{0}\,\eta_{\rm eff}^{a}\,\exp\left[{\mathrm{i}(\varphi_{a}+\omega_{1}(t-t_{0})+\alpha(t-t_{0})^{2}/2})\right],

(S12)

where
\displaystyle h_{0}
is the GW strain amplitude,
\displaystyle\eta_{\rm eff}^{a}
is the effective overlap function for mode
\displaystyle a
, and
\displaystyle\varphi_{a}
encodes the relative phase.
The relevant quantities are defined as

\displaystyle\displaystyle h_{0}

\displaystyle\displaystyle\equiv(h_{+}^{2}+h_{\times}^{2})^{1/2},

(S13)

\displaystyle\displaystyle\eta^{a}_{\rm eff}

\displaystyle\displaystyle\equiv\frac{1}{\sqrt{1+\kappa^{2}}}\sqrt{(\eta_{+}^{a})^{2}+(\kappa\eta_{\times}^{a})^{2}+2\left|\kappa\eta_{+}^{a}\eta_{\times}^{a}\right|\cos{(\Omega^{a}_{\times}-\Omega^{a}_{+}+\xi)}},\qquad\kappa\equiv|h_{\times}/h_{+}|,\qquad\Omega_{+/\times}^{a}\equiv{\rm arg}[\eta^{a}_{+/\times}],

(S14)

\displaystyle\displaystyle\varphi_{a}

\displaystyle\displaystyle\equiv\delta_{0}+\Omega_{+}^{a}+{\rm arctan}\left[\frac{\kappa\left|\eta^{a}_{\times}\right|\sin{(\Omega^{a}_{\times}-\Omega^{a}_{+}+\xi)}}{\left|\eta^{a}_{+}\right|+\kappa\left|\eta^{a}_{\times}\right|\cos{(\Omega^{a}_{\times}-\Omega^{a}_{+}+\xi)}}\right],

(S15)

Here,
\displaystyle t_{0}
denotes the time at which the GW frequency reaches the first cavity resonance with initial phase
\displaystyle\delta_{0}
,
\displaystyle\kappa
and
\displaystyle\xi
are the two polarizations’ ratio and relative phase, and
\displaystyle\alpha
is the linear angular frequency drift rate.

Substituting Eq. (
S12
) back into Eq. (
S11
), the mode amplitude can be solved using the Green’s function method:

e_{a}(t)=-\mathrm{i}\omega_{a}^{3}B_{0}V^{5/6}h_{0}\eta^{a}_{\rm eff}\exp[{\mathrm{i}\varphi_{a}}]\int_{-\infty}^{t^{\prime}}G_{a}(t^{\prime}-\tau)\exp[{\mathrm{i}(\omega_{1}\tau+\alpha\tau^{2}/2})]\text{d}\tau,\qquad G_{a}(t)=\exp[{-\gamma_{a}t}]\frac{\sin\left(\sqrt{\omega_{a}^{2}-\gamma_{a}^{2}}t\right)}{\sqrt{\omega_{a}^{2}-\gamma_{a}^{2}}}H(t),

(S16)

where
\displaystyle t^{\prime}\equiv t-t_{0}
,
\displaystyle\gamma_{a}\equiv\omega_{a}/(2Q_{a}^{L})
is the dissipation rate and
\displaystyle H(t)
is the Heaviside step function.
This expression admits a closed-form analytic solution,

e_{a}(t)=\frac{-\omega_{a}^{2}B_{0}V^{5/6}h_{0}\eta^{a}_{\rm eff}}{2}\exp[{\mathrm{i}\varphi_{a}}]\left(\exp[{-\lambda_{-}^{a}t^{\prime}}]I_{-}^{a}(t^{\prime})-\exp[{-\lambda_{+}^{a}t^{\prime}}]I_{+}^{a}(t^{\prime})\right),

(S17)

where we have defined

I_{\pm}^{a}(t^{\prime})\equiv\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{2\alpha}}\exp\left[{\mathrm{i}\left(\frac{\pi}{4}+\frac{(b_{\pm}^{a})^{2}}{4p}\right)}\right]\left(\text{erf}\left[\sqrt{p}(t^{\prime}-\frac{b_{\pm}^{a}}{2p})\right]+1\right),

(S18)

with
\displaystyle\lambda^{a}_{\pm}=\gamma_{a}\pm\sqrt{\omega_{a}^{2}-\gamma_{a}^{2}}
,
\displaystyle b_{\pm}^{a}=\gamma_{a}+\mathrm{i}(\omega_{1}\pm\sqrt{\omega_{a}^{2}-\gamma_{a}^{2}})
, and
\displaystyle p=-\mathrm{i}\alpha/2
.

A.2.1
Approximate Waveforms

The exact analytic expression for the cavity-mode response derived above is rather involved. To highlight the dominant physical features of the signal, we focus here on its approximate scaling behavior and time-domain structure.

Each cavity mode can be modeled as a damped harmonic oscillator driven by a chirping, nearly monochromatic GW with instantaneous angular frequency

\omega_{g}(t)=\omega_{1}+\alpha(t-t_{0}).

(S19)

As the GW frequency sweeps across the cavity resonance
\displaystyle\omega_{a}
, the mode response is governed by the interplay of three characteristic time scales:
(i) the inverse detuning
\displaystyle|\omega_{g}(t)-\omega_{a}|^{-1}
,
(ii) the cavity damping time
\displaystyle 1/\gamma_{a}
, and
(iii) the frequency-sweep time scale
\displaystyle\alpha^{-1/2}
.
The interplay of these time scales leads to three distinct stages in the response: off-resonant tracking, resonant growth, and post-resonance decay.

To make this structure explicit, it is useful to define the instantaneous saturation amplitude, namely the steady-state response that would be obtained if the GW frequency were held fixed at
\displaystyle\omega_{g}(t)
:

e_{a}^{\rm sat}(t)\equiv\left|\frac{\omega_{a}^{3}B_{0}V^{5/6}h_{0}\eta_{\rm eff}^{a}}{\omega_{a}^{2}-\omega_{g}(t)^{2}-2\mathrm{i}\omega_{g}(t)\gamma_{a}}\right|.

(S20)

The actual signal amplitude
\displaystyle e_{a}(t)
attempts to follow this moving saturation envelope but may lag behind when the frequency sweep is sufficiently rapid.

In what follows, we first focus on the intermediate regime

2\gamma_{a}^{2}\;\leq\;\alpha\;<\;\gamma_{a}\omega_{a},

(S21)

for which the frequency evolution is fast enough to invalidate adiabatic tracking near resonance, yet slow enough that the signal remains nearly monochromatic at any given time.

•

Stage I: off-resonant tracking.

At early times, when
\displaystyle|\omega_{g}(t)-\omega_{a}|\gg\sqrt{\alpha/2\pi}
, the GW drive is far off resonance and the saturation amplitude is small. In this regime the cavity mode remains effectively saturated, and the signal amplitude closely follows the instantaneous steady-state value,

|e_{a}(t)|^{\rm(I)}\simeq e_{a}^{\rm sat}(t).

(S22)

Because the Lorentzian envelope in Eq. (
S20
) grows only slowly far from resonance, the signal remains negligible during this stage.

As the GW frequency approaches
\displaystyle\omega_{a}
, the saturation amplitude increases rapidly. Stage I ends at the time
\displaystyle t_{a}^{s}
when the cavity can no longer adiabatically track this growth. A useful estimate for
\displaystyle t_{a}^{s}
is obtained by equating the rate of change of the saturation amplitude to the intrinsic growth rate of the cavity mode,

\left.\frac{{\rm d}|e_{a}|}{{\rm d}t}\right|_{t=t_{a}^{s}}\sim\alpha\left.\frac{{\rm d}e_{a}^{\rm sat}}{{\rm d}\omega_{g}}\right|_{t=t_{a}^{s}}\sim\omega_{a}^{2}B_{0}V^{5/6}h_{0}\eta_{\rm eff}^{a}.

(S23)

•

Stage II: resonant growth.

For
\displaystyle t>t_{a}^{s}
, the instantaneous saturation amplitude exceeds the actual signal amplitude, and the cavity mode undergoes resonant energy accumulation. During this stage, the signal grows approximately as an effectively monochromatic response within the resonant bandwidth, with the growth controlled by the cavity dissipation rate
\displaystyle\gamma_{a}

|e_{a}(t)|^{\rm(II)}\simeq\left[1-e^{-\gamma_{a}(t-t_{a}^{s})}\right]\left(\omega_{a}B_{0}V^{5/6}h_{0}\eta_{\rm eff}^{a}Q_{a}^{L}\right).

(S24)

This growth persists only while the GW frequency remains sufficiently close to resonance. Once
\displaystyle\omega_{g}(t)
exceeds
\displaystyle\omega_{a}
, the saturation amplitude
\displaystyle e_{a}^{\rm sat}(t)
decreases rapidly. Stage II ends at time
\displaystyle t_{a}^{e}
, defined by the condition

e_{a}^{\rm sat}(t_{a}^{e})\sim|e_{a}(t_{a}^{e})|^{\rm(II)}.

(S25)

The duration of the resonant growth window is primarily controlled by the frequency sweep rate and can be estimated as

t_{a}^{e}-t_{a}^{s}\sim\frac{1}{\sqrt{\alpha}}.

(S26)

This timescale is longer than the naive estimate based on the interval during which the GW frequency remains within the cavity bandwidth,
\displaystyle\sim\gamma_{a}/\alpha
. This indicates that significant signal growth can occur even when the GW frequency lies outside the nominal resonance bandwidth, as the cavity mode has not yet reached its saturation limit.

•

Stage III: post-resonance decay.

For
\displaystyle t>t_{a}^{e}
, the driving frequency has moved off resonance and the cavity mode undergoes free decay with damping rate
\displaystyle\gamma_{a}
, accompanied by weak beat oscillations. The signal amplitude in this stage is approximately

|e_{a}(t)|^{\rm(III)}\simeq\left[1-e^{-\gamma_{a}(t_{a}^{e}-t_{a}^{s})}\right]\left(\omega_{a}B_{0}V^{5/6}h_{0}\eta_{\rm eff}^{a}Q_{a}^{L}\right)e^{-\gamma_{a}(t-t_{a}^{e})}.

(S27)

Since the signal growth during Stage I is negligible, the maximum oscillation amplitude is well approximated by the value reached at the end of Stage II,

e_{a}^{\rm amp}\equiv|e_{a}(t_{a}^{e})|\simeq\left[1-e^{-\gamma_{a}/\sqrt{\alpha}}\right]\left(\omega_{a}B_{0}V^{5/6}h_{0}\eta_{\rm eff}^{a}Q_{a}^{L}\right).

(S28)

At any given time, the signal remains nearly monochromatic, allowing the time-integrated power to be estimated by averaging over oscillations. One finds

\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e_{a}(t)^{2}\,{\rm d}t

\displaystyle\approx\int_{t_{a}^{s}}^{t_{a}^{e}}\frac{1}{2}\left[e_{a}^{\rm amp}\left(1-e^{-\gamma_{a}(t-t_{a}^{s})}\right)\right]^{2}{\rm d}t+\int_{t_{a}^{e}}^{\infty}\frac{1}{2}\left[e_{a}^{\rm amp}e^{-\gamma_{a}(t-t_{a}^{e})}\right]^{2}{\rm d}t

(S29)

\displaystyle\simeq\frac{(e_{a}^{\rm amp})^{2}}{4\gamma_{a}}\left[1+\mathcal{O}\!\left(\left(\frac{\gamma_{a}}{\sqrt{\alpha}}\right)^{3}\right)\right].

For sufficiently slow frequency evolution,

\alpha\leq 2\gamma_{a}^{2},

(S30)

the cavity mode remains adiabatically saturated throughout the resonance crossing. In this regime, the full time-domain response is well approximated by

|e_{a}(t)|\simeq e_{a}^{\rm sat}(t),

(S31)

and no distinct resonant growth stage develops.

B.2
Overlap Functions

Figure 2 in the main text shows the overlap functions
\displaystyle\eta_{A}^{a}
for GWs incident from direction
\displaystyle\hat{k}=(\phi,\theta)
, for the two polarizations
\displaystyle A=+,\times
. In this subsection, we explain the origin of their angular dependence and the symmetry relations connecting different regions of the sky.

We first note that the overlap functions for each cavity mode exhibit a pronounced preference for specific ranges of the polar angle
\displaystyle\theta
. This behavior can be understood as a phase-matching condition between the electric-field distributions in the 9 cavity cells and the variation of the GW plane-wave phase along the
\displaystyle z
direction. Denoting the distance between the centers of two adjacent cells by

\displaystyle L_{z}=0.114~\mathrm{m}
, we obtain the parametric scaling

\left|\eta^{a}_{+/\times}\right|\propto\left|\sum_{j=1}^{N}e^{\mathrm{i}kjL_{z}\cos\theta}\sin\!\left[(j-\tfrac{1}{2})\frac{a\pi}{N}\right]\right|\propto\left|\frac{\sin\!\left(\tfrac{N}{2}\psi\right)}{\sin\!\left(\tfrac{\psi}{2}\right)}\right|,\qquad\psi\equiv kL_{z}\cos\theta-\frac{a\pi}{N}.

(S32)

This expression naturally leads to the multi-peak structure observed in
\displaystyle\theta
, with the global maximum attained when the phase-matching condition
\displaystyle\psi=0
is satisfied.

In addition, the azimuthal dependence can be understood by projecting the effective current onto the
\displaystyle x
direction, along which the electric field of the
\displaystyle\mathrm{TE}_{111+}
modes predominantly lies. One finds

\hat{j}_{+}\cdot\hat{x}\propto\sin\theta\,\sin\phi,\qquad\hat{j}_{\times}\cdot\hat{x}\propto\sin\theta\,\cos\theta\,\sin\phi.

(S33)

This
\displaystyle\phi
dependence is in good agreement with the numerical overlap functions. The additional factor of
\displaystyle\cos\theta
in
\displaystyle\hat{j}_{\times}\cdot\hat{x}
further explains the relatively suppressed amplitude of
\displaystyle\eta^{a}_{\times}
compared to
\displaystyle\eta^{a}_{+}
.

In Fig. 2 of the main text, we display the overlap functions
\displaystyle\eta_{A}^{a}(\hat{k})
only over one-eighth of the sky, corresponding to
\displaystyle\phi\in[0,\pi/2]
and
\displaystyle\theta\in[0,\pi/2]
, for the
\displaystyle\mathrm{TE}_{111+}
modes. The overlap functions in the remaining regions of the sky, as well as those for the
\displaystyle\mathrm{TE}_{111-}
modes, can be obtained straightforwardly using discrete symmetry transformations, as detailed below.

The combined system of the nearly axisymmetric cavity, the background magnetic field, and an incident plane-wave GW admits three independent discrete parity symmetries acting on the celestial angles. These symmetries induce a
\displaystyle Z_{8}
degeneracy in the overlap functions defined in Eq. (
S10

(i)

\displaystyle\phi\rightarrow\pi+\phi
, corresponding to a parity transformation in the
\displaystyle x
–
\displaystyle y
plane;

(ii)

\displaystyle\phi\rightarrow\pi-\phi
, corresponding to a parity transformation along the
\displaystyle x

Due to the above symmetries, the overlap functions in eight distinct sky octants are identical up to an overall sign, resulting in an apparent eightfold degeneracy in the GW arrival direction. When multiple cavity modes with different indices
\displaystyle a
are measured, their distinct symmetry behaviors, particularly under transformation (iii), allow one to access the relative phase between the two GW polarizations. Since
\displaystyle 4

\displaystyle 8

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\times

\displaystyle 4

The remaining fourfold degeneracy could, in principle, be lifted by introducing controlled inhomogeneities in the background magnetic field, which would break the discrete symmetries discussed above and yield quadrant-dependent overlap functions. Implementing and calibrating such magnetic-field configurations would require additional experimental complexity, and we defer a detailed investigation of this possibility to future work.
