Subarachnoid puncture via the anterior fontanelle with intrathecal vancomycin for refractory purulent meningitis: a pediatric case report
Authors: Yan Li, Xinghui Yang, Yongping Xie, Guolan Huang, Xiaohui Fang, Lisu Huang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis case report presents a novel treatment approach for refractory purulent meningitis in a 1-month-old infant caused by penicillin-sensitive group B Streptococcus. Despite initial treatment with intravenous antibiotics, including penicillin and vancomycin, the infant experienced persistent symptoms and bilateral subdural effusions. Conventional therapies failed to resolve these issues, leading to the use of a new technique: subarachnoid puncture via the anterior fontanelle combined with intrathecal vancomycin administration. This minimally invasive procedure allowed for targeted antibiotic delivery, significantly reducing subdural effusions and improving cerebrospinal fluid parameters. The infant showed substantial clinical improvement and was discharged after 68 days with no complications during a 3-month follow-up. This approach offers a promising alternative to more invasive treatments such as craniotomy or subdural external drainage, though further research is needed to confirm its efficacy and safety in broader clinical contexts.Keywords: Subarachnoid puncture, Intrathecal vancomycin, Purulent meningitis, Anterior fontanelle, Pediatric neurosurgery, Group B Streptococcus meningitis

On April 8, 2024, a 1-month-old male infant with persistent fever lasting 12 h was admitted to the Children’s Hospital affiliated with Zhejiang University School of Medicine. Clinical evaluation revealed that the infant experienced occasional staring episodes, a bulging anterior fontanelle, and a liver palpated 3 cm below the costal margin. He also presented with lethargy, decreased activity, and reduced milk intake. Physical examination revealed increased muscle tone, elicitable reflexes, cool extremities, diffuse petechiae, and a prolonged capillary refill time of 4 s.

Diagnostic tests confirmed the presence of penicillin-sensitive Streptococcus agalactiae (group B Streptococcus, GBS) in both blood and cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) cultures. CSF analysis revealed elevated white blood cell counts and protein levels. Imaging studies, including cranial ultrasound and MRI, revealed bilateral subdural effusions and widening of the frontal extracerebral spaces. The initial treatment regimen included intravenous penicillin (550,000 units every 6 h) for 3 weeks. This was replaced with vancomycin (67 mg every 8 h) after the patient experienced hearing loss in both ears. Subsequently, linezolid was administered, but it also proved ineffective. To manage inflammation, steroids were given. Mannitol was used to reduce intracranial pressure, and phenobarbital was provided for seizure control. Despite these interventions, the patient’s condition did not improve satisfactorily.

A follow-up contrast-enhanced magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) revealed not only persistent bilateral frontal and temporal widening but also newly developed subdural effusion in the left occipital region, along with thickening and enhancement of multiple meninges. Owing to the unsatisfactory decrease in cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) cell count and protein levels, as well as persistent hypoglycemia, we performed bilateral subdural effusion burr hole irrigation and drainage. However, follow-up MRI revealed no improvement (Fig. 1a–b). With parental consent, vancomycin was administered directly into the subdural space under ultrasound guidance to achieve higher local concentrations. Due to the bilateral nature of the effusions, a decision was made to puncture only one side, allowing the medication to diffuse into the contralateral subdural space through the communication between the two spaces. A solution containing vancomycin (10 mg) and dexamethasone (1 mg) diluted in 5 ml of normal saline was administered via a 45–60° angle puncture into the lateral anterior fontanelle at 1 ml/min under ultrasound guidance. Prior to the injection, 5 ml of CSF was aspirated for routine examination, and the vancomycin solution was then slowly administered to ensure that intracranial pressure remained stable without significant changes. After the procedure, the vancomycin concentration, the patient’s vital signs, and neurological status were closely monitored.

a–b Pre-intrathecal injection enhanced cranial MRI before intrathecal injection: Meningitis with bilateral frontal–temporal subdural effusions, meningeal thickening, and enhancement. c–d Post-intrathecal injection follow-up enhanced cranial MRI: Improvement in meningeal thickening and enhancement, with a reduction in bilateral subdural effusions

Over the next 2 weeks, the patient underwent five subarachnoid punctures and intrathecal vancomycin injections via the anterior fontanelle. The CSF progressively clarified (Fig. 2), with a corresponding decrease in cell count and protein levels, which paralleled the clinical improvement in neurological conditions. The white blood cell count and protein levels in the CSF steadily decreased (Fig. 3a–b). His body temperature and C-reactive protein (CRP) level gradually returned to normal. Follow-up MRI revealed reduced subdural effusion and meningeal enhancement (Fig. 1c–d). After a 68-day hospitalization, the patient was discharged with satisfactory outpatient follow-up.

Discussion

This case highlights the complexities of treating neonatal GBS infection, particularly when complicated by subdural effusions, as observed in this 1-month-old infant with penicillin-sensitive Streptococcus agalactiae meningitis. Despite initial treatment with intravenous antibiotics, the infant exhibited persistent subdural effusions and CSF cell counts and protein levels, demonstrating inadequate improvement. In this instance, subarachnoid puncture via the anterior fontanelle combined with intrathecal vancomycin effectively eradicated GBS meningitis.

Even with effective antibiotic treatment, the complication rate and mortality of bacterial meningitis remain high. Subdural effusion is present in 10–45% of children with acute bacterial meningitis [1]. Traditional craniotomy is often used to address complications such as subdural effusion and hydrocephalus resulting from purulent meningitis. This procedure involves directly removing the infection, particularly the abscess, effusion, or clots, to reduce intracranial pressure [2]. However, craniotomy carries significant risks, including secondary infection, bleeding, and neurological damage, and usually requires a prolonged hospital stay, which is particularly challenging for infants [3–5]. As an alternative, burr hole drainage offers a less invasive option. Compared with craniotomy, this technique involves creating a small hole in the skull to drain effusions or pus, thereby relieving pressure and managing infection with less overall trauma.

Subarachnoid puncture via the anterior fontanelle offers a minimally invasive and effective method for direct drug delivery, particularly in infants with an open fontanelle. This approach allows targeted antibiotic administration directly to the site of infection, achieving high local drug concentrations and enhancing therapeutic efficacy [6]. In this case, alternative methods such as lumbar puncture, intraventricular catheterization, and subdural external drainage were considered [7]. However, while subdural external drainage is sometimes a viable option for managing effusions, the patient’s open fontanelle provided a more accessible and less invasive route for drug delivery. Given the bilateral nature of the effusions, a single-sided puncture enabled diffusion across the subdural spaces. Ultimately, we chose this approach over subdural external drainage due to its ability to manage the infection effectively without the need for prolonged drainage, minimizing physical trauma, reducing infection risk, and lowering postoperative discomfort and medical costs.

Our study demonstrated that subarachnoid puncture via the anterior fontanelle combined with intrathecal vancomycin can effectively manage complex purulent meningitis. This approach may be particularly beneficial when conventional antibiotic therapies fail and traditional surgical methods do not adequately address intracranial pressure. Due to the neurotoxic side effects associated with high concentrations of vancomycin, we closely monitored the CSF vancomycin levels to prevent adverse events [8]. Further research and clinical trials are essential to establish standardized protocols and confirm the long-term safety and efficacy of this treatment strategy.
