Splitting the Forward-backward Algorithm: A Full Characterization
A. Åkerman ∗ and E. Chenchene †,c and P. Giselsson ∗ and E. Naldi ‡

Abstract
. We study frugal splitting algorithms with minimal lifting for solving monotone inclusion problems involving sums of maximal monotone and cocoercive operators. Building on a foundational result by Ryu, we fully characterize all methods that use only individual resolvent evaluations, direct evaluations of cocoercive operators, and minimal memory resources while ensuring convergence via averaged fixed-point iterations. We show that all such methods are captured by a unified framework, which includes known schemes and enables new ones with promising features. Systematic numerical experiments lead us to propose three design heuristics to achieve excellent performances in practice, yielding significant gains over existing methods.

1.
Introduction

Splitting algorithms lie at the hearth of nonsmooth optimization. They define a class of iterative method that allow to solve highly structured problems by decomposing them into simpler components, each handled through efficient and easily computable operations. In this paper, we consider monotone inclusion problems of the form:

(1.1)

\text{Find}\ x\in\mathcal{H}\ \text{such that:}\quad 0\in\sum_{i=1}^{n}A_{i}(x)+\sum_{i=1}^{m}C_{i}(x),

where
\mathcal{H}
is a real Hilbert space, each operator
A_{i}:\mathcal{H}\rightarrow 2^{\mathcal{H}}
is maximal monotone, and
C_{i}:\mathcal{H}\rightarrow\mathcal{H}
is
\frac{1}{\beta_{i}}
-cocoercive for some
\beta_{i}\in\real_{+}
. If the resolvent
J_{\gamma A}:=(\Id+\gamma A)^{-1}
with
A:=A_{1}+\dots+A_{n}
can be computed efficiently for any given step size
\gamma>0
, one can address (
1.1
) through the celebrated
forward-backward
algorithm
[
16
]
that, from a starting point
x^{0}\in\mathcal{H}
, iterates:

(1.2)

x^{k+1}=J_{\gamma A}(x^{k}-\gamma C(x^{k})),\quad\text{for all}\ k\in\mathbb{N},

where
C:=C_{1}+\dots+C_{m}
. The sequence generated by (
1.2
) is well known to converge weakly to a solution to (
1.1
), provided that one exists and
\gamma<\frac{2}{\beta}
. However, in practice the operator
J_{\gamma A}
can rarely be computed efficiently. The classical alternative if
n=2
and
m=0
is the
Douglas–Rachford
splitting (DRS) algorithm
[
16
]
and if
m=1
, the so-called
three-operator splitting
, or
Davis–Yin
method
[
14
,
21
]
, that, given
z^{0}\in\mathcal{H}
, iterates:

(1.3)

\left\{\begin{aligned} &x_{1}^{k+1}=J_{\gamma A_{1}}(z^{k}),\\
&x_{2}^{k+1}=J_{\gamma A_{2}}(2x_{1}^{k+1}-\gamma C(x_{1}^{k+1})-z^{k}),\\
&z^{k+1}=z^{k}+(x_{2}^{k+1}-x_{1}^{k+1}),\end{aligned}\right.\quad\text{for all}\ k\in\mathbb{N}.

The method reduces to DRS if
C=0
, and to (
1.2
) if
A_{1}=0
and, in particular, is such that
\{x_{1}^{k+1}\}_{k}
and
\{x_{2}^{k+1}\}_{k}
converge weakly to the same solution to (
1.1
) provided that
\gamma<\frac{2}{\beta}

[
14
]
.

In the general case, one possible approach to tackle (
1.1
) is using a classical lifting trick
[
20
]
, i.e., applying (
1.2
) to a suitable product-space reformulation of (
1.1
), see, e.g.,
[
21
]
. As these are formulated on product spaces, they usually feature
n
, or, in some specific instances
[
13
,
11
]
,
n-1
variables instead of only one as in (
1.2
) and (
1.3
). More recently, a number of new splitting algorithms with
n-1
variables have been discovered, without using reformulations on product spaces but proximal-point-based designs. Notable examples include the graph-DRS algorithmic framework
[
10
]
for
m=0
, or the graph-forward-backward algorithm
[
2
]
for
m=n-1
.

The need to introduce
n-1
(or more) variables is not a coincidence. A breakthrough result by Ryu
[
22
]
, later extended in
[
17
,
18
]
, demonstrated that
frugal splitting methods
—those relying solely on individual resolvent evaluations
J_{\gamma_{1}A_{1}},\dots,J_{\gamma_{n}A_{n}}
with
\gamma_{1},\dots,\gamma_{n}>0
, forward evaluations
C_{1},\dots,C_{m}
, and simple algebraic operations—cannot solve arbitrary instances of (
1.1
) by storing fewer than
n-1
variables between iterations. Consequently, methods achieving this bound are said to exhibit
minimal lifting
. Even more striking, if
n=2
and
m=0
, the only frugal resolvent method with minimal lifting that is so-called
unconditionally stable
(i.e., producing a weakly converging sequence to a solution for any starting point and any operator choice) is indeed DRS
[
22
, Corollary 1]
. For
n>2
, however, the landscape of such methods becomes richer, giving rise to a variety of structurally diverse algorithms that have attracted significant attention in recent years
[
17
,
18
,
1
,
3
,
11
]
. These can all be understood as fixed-point iterations with respect to
averaged
operators, which lead us to the core question of this paper:

Can we characterize all averaged frugal splitting methods with minimal lifting to solve (
1.1
)?

To be more precise, we consider fixed-point iterations with respect to
T:\mathcal{H}^{n-1}\rightarrow\mathcal{H}^{n-1}
to solve arbitrary instances of (
1.1
), with the following properties:

(P1)

Splitting
: Each evaluation of
T
is constructed solely from resolvent evaluations of
A_{i}
, direct evaluations of
C_{i}
, and arbitrary linear combinations of their inputs and outputs.

(P2)

Frugality
: Each operator is evaluated exactly once per evaluation of
T
, either directly for
C_{i}
or through a resolvent for
A_{i}
.

(P3)

Minimal lifting
: The method only needs to store
n-1
variables between iterations. This property is automatically satisfied for
T
defined on
\mathcal{H}^{n-1}
.

(P4)

Fixed-point encoding
: Fixed points of
T
correspond to solutions of (
1.1
) and vice versa, as made precise in Section
3
.

(P5)

Averaged nonexpansive
: The operator
T
is
\theta
-averaged on
\mathcal{H}^{n-1}
,
\theta\in(0,1)
, equipped with the product norm.

In this paper, we give a positive answer to the question showing that the entire class of methods satisfying properties (
P1
)–(
P5
) is given by Algorithm
1
, meaning that all methods covered by Algorithm
1
satisfy (
P1
)–(
P5
) and that no other methods satisfy all these requirements. This represents the first, yet partial, answer to a challenging open problem proposed by Ryu in
[
22
]
, on the characterization of all
unconditionally stable
methods.

Although the central question is of pure intellectual interest, its resolution comes with significant practical implications. Indeed, while Algorithm
1
can be shown to encompass several methods in the literature, it also allows us to devise new ones with desirable numerical properties. In particular, in Algorithm
2
, we propose a special case of Algorithm
1
that can easily handle data heterogeneity and admits fully distributed implementations on general networks, without requiring knowledge of a global cocoercivity constant. We further conduct a systematic numerical study examining the influence of the four parametrizing matrices
M
,
P
and
H,K
and propose three heuristics to achieve excellent performance in practice. The resulting method enhanced by our heuristics exhibits unparalleled performance, significantly outperforming all existing variants.

Notation

Let
\mathbb{N}
denote the set of nonnegative integers,
\mathbb{R}
the set of real numbers, and
\mathbb{R}_{+}
,
\mathbb{R}_{++}
the set of nonnegative and positive real numbers, respectively. Let
\llbracket i,j\rrbracket:=\{i,i+1,\ldots,j\}
with the convention that
\llbracket i,j\rrbracket=\emptyset
if
j<i
,
\kron
denote the tensor product,
\mathcal{H}
denote a real Hilbert space with identity operator denoted by
\Id
. For a matrix
M\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}
we denote by
M_{ij}
its
(i,j)
component. We denote by
\slt(M)\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}
the strictly lower triangular matrix extracted from
M
, and by
\diag(M)\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
the vector extracted from the main diagonal of
M
. With a mild overload of notation, if
v\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
\diag(v)\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
denotes the diagonal matrix with the diagonal being
v
.

For a multivalued map
A:\mathcal{H}\to 2^{\mathcal{H}}
, its
graph
is the set
\gra(A):=\{(x,a)\in\mathcal{H}^{2}\ :\ a\in A(x)\}
, while its
domain
is
\dom(A):=\{x\in\mathcal{H}\ :\ A(x)\neq\emptyset\}
. An operator
A:\mathcal{H}\to 2^{\mathcal{H}}
is
monotone
if

(1.4)

\langle a-a^{\prime},x-x^{\prime}\rangle\geq 0,\quad\text{for all}\ (x,a),\ (x^{\prime},a^{\prime})\in\gra(A),

and is
maximal montone
if its graph is maximal among the class of monotone operators. The
inverse
of
A
is the unique operator
A^{-1}
such that
\gra(A^{-1})=\{(a,x)\ :\ (x,a)\in\gra(A)\}
and the
resolvent
of
A
is the operator
J_{A}:=(\Id+A)^{-1}
, which is single-valued if
A
is monotone, see
[
5
, Proposition 23.8]
. By Minty’s theorem
[
5
, Theorem 21.1]
a monotone operator is maximal if and only if its resolvent
J_{A}
has full domain. In this case, it defines a map from
\mathcal{H}
to itself. In such cases, we identify multivalued operators with functions on
\mathcal{H}
. A monotone operator
C:\mathcal{H}\to 2^{\mathcal{H}}
is
\frac{1}{\beta}
-
cocoercive
if it is single-valued, has full domain, and satisfies:

(1.5)

\beta\langle C(x)-C(x^{\prime}),x-x^{\prime}\rangle\geq\|C(x)-C(x^{\prime})\|^{2},\quad\text{for all}\ x,x^{\prime}\in\mathcal{H},

in particular, it is
\beta
-Lipschitz continuous and if
\beta=0
it is necessarily constant. Eventually, we denote by
\mathcal{A}_{n}
and by
\mathcal{C}_{m}
the families of
n
and
m
tuples of maximal monotone and cocoercive operators, respectively.

We identify matrices
M\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}
with block-operators
(M\kron\Id):\mathcal{H}^{m}\to\mathcal{H}^{n}
, when clear by context. In particular, for any
z\in\mathcal{H}^{m}
, we abbreviate
(M\kron\Id)z
by
Mz
. Similarly, if
\boldsymbol{C}:\mathcal{H}^{n}\to\mathcal{H}^{n}
and
\boldsymbol{C}^{\prime}:\mathcal{H}^{m}\to\mathcal{H}^{m}
are operators, we interpret the products
\boldsymbol{C}M
and
M\boldsymbol{C}^{\prime}
as
\boldsymbol{C}\circ(M\kron\Id)
and
(M\kron\Id)\circ\boldsymbol{C}^{\prime}
, respectively. Further, we denote by
\mathbb{S}_{+}^{n}
and
\mathbb{S}_{++}^{n}
the spaces of positive semidefinite and positive definite matrices in
\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
. Given Hilbert spaces
\mathcal{H}_{1},\dots,\mathcal{H}_{n}
and
A_{i}:\mathcal{H}_{i}\to 2^{\mathcal{H}}_{i}
, we denote by
\boldsymbol{A}(\boldsymbol{x}):=\diag(A_{1},\dots,A_{n})
the
diagonal
operator defined by
\boldsymbol{A}(\boldsymbol{x}):=A_{1}(x_{1})\times A_{2}(x_{2})\times\cdots\times A_{n}(x_{n})
, for all
\boldsymbol{x}=(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})\in\mathcal{H}_{1}\times\dots\times\mathcal{H}_{n}
.

2.
Algorithmic model

To capture the entire class of methods with properties (
P1
) to (
P5
), we start out by defining the entire class of
frugal splitting methods with minimal lifting
satisfying (
P1
)–(
P3
).

Definition 2.1

(Frugal splitting operators with minimal lifting)
.

Let
\Gamma:=\diag(\gamma)\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
with
\gamma\in\mathbb{R}^{n}_{++}
,
L\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
,
V^{T},M\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times(n-1)}
,
U\in\mathbb{R}^{(n-1)\times(n-1)}
,
H^{T},K\in\real^{m\times n}
,
Z^{(1)},\in\real^{m\times(n-1)}
,
Z^{(2)}\in\real^{m\times m}
, and
Z^{(3)}\in\real^{(n-1)\times m}
. A
frugal splitting operator with minimal lifting
is an operator
T:\mathcal{H}^{n-1}\to\mathcal{H}^{n-1}
defined by
\boldsymbol{z}^{+}:=T(\boldsymbol{z})
with:

(2.1)

\left\{\begin{aligned} &\boldsymbol{x}^{+}=J_{\Gamma\boldsymbol{A}}(\Gamma L\boldsymbol{x}^{+}-\Gamma H\boldsymbol{u}^{+}+\Gamma M\boldsymbol{z}),\\
&\boldsymbol{u}^{+}=\boldsymbol{C}(K\boldsymbol{x}^{+}+Z^{(2)}\boldsymbol{u}^{+}+Z^{(1)}\boldsymbol{z}),\\
&\boldsymbol{z}^{+}=U\boldsymbol{z}-V\boldsymbol{x}^{+}-Z^{(3)}\boldsymbol{u}^{+},\end{aligned}\right.

and such that
\boldsymbol{z}^{+}
can be computed only employing additions and scalar multiplications in
\mathcal{H}
, and exactly one evaluation of
J_{\gamma_{i}A_{i}}
and
C_{j}
for each
i\in\llbracket 1,n\rrbracket
and
j\in\llbracket 1,m\rrbracket
, and for each
A\in\mathcal{A}_{n}
,
C\in\mathcal{C}_{m}
and any real Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
. Given
T
as in (
2.1
), a
frugal splitting method with minimal lifting
is a fixed-point iteration defined by

(2.2)

\boldsymbol{z}^{k+1}=T(\boldsymbol{z}^{k}),\quad\text{for all}\ k\in\mathbb{N},\ \boldsymbol{z}^{0}\in\mathcal{H}^{n-1}.

Observe that each resolvent evaluation automatically produces elements
a_{i}^{+}\in A_{i}(x_{i}^{+})
for all
i\in\llbracket 1,n\rrbracket
. The evaluation of
T
as in (
2.1
) at some
\boldsymbol{z}\in\mathcal{H}^{n-1}
can be thus characterized as the
unique
solution to the following system of nonlinear equations:

(2.3)

\displaystyle\text{Find $\boldsymbol{z}^{+}\in\mathcal{H}^{n-1}$, $\boldsymbol{x}^{+}\in\mathcal{H}^{n}$, $\boldsymbol{u}^{+}\in\mathcal{H}^{m}$, $\boldsymbol{a}^{+}\in\boldsymbol{A}(\boldsymbol{x}^{+})$ such that:}

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{aligned} &(\Gamma^{-1}-L)\boldsymbol{x}^{+}+\boldsymbol{a}^{+}=M\boldsymbol{z}-H\boldsymbol{u}^{+},\\
&\boldsymbol{u}^{+}=\boldsymbol{C}(K\boldsymbol{x}^{+}+Z^{(2)}\boldsymbol{u}^{+}+Z^{(1)}\boldsymbol{z}),\\
&\boldsymbol{z}^{+}=U\boldsymbol{z}-V\boldsymbol{x}^{+}-Z^{(3)}\boldsymbol{u}^{+}.\end{aligned}\right.

In the following, we say that
\boldsymbol{z}^{+}\in\mathcal{H}^{n-1}
,
\boldsymbol{x}^{+}\in\mathcal{H}^{n}
,
\boldsymbol{u}^{+}\in\mathcal{H}^{m}
,
\boldsymbol{a}^{+}\in\boldsymbol{A}(\boldsymbol{x}^{+})

solve
(
2.3
) at
\boldsymbol{z}
. Alternatively, we say that
\boldsymbol{x}^{+}
,
\boldsymbol{a}^{+}
and
\boldsymbol{u}^{+}
are
generated
by the evaluation of
T(\boldsymbol{z})
.

The operator in (
2.1
) is
frugal
(
P2
) since each resolvent of
A_{i}
and each
C_{i}
can be evaluated exactly once per iteration and it has
minimal lifting
(
P3
) since the corresponding fixed-point iteration lies in
\mathcal{H}^{n-1}
. Being able to explicitly evaluate
T
immediately induces a specific structure on
L
, and the matrices
H
,
K
and
Z^{(2)}
, a property that we refer to as
causality
. Obviously, up to a permutation,
L
and
Z^{(2)}
should be strictly lower triangular. For
H
and
K
the situation is more subtle—we discuss their structural properties in Section
2.1
. Additionally, our algorithm model includes methods that may or may not satisfy (
P4
) and (
P5
), i.e., that may or may not have the
fixed-point encoding property
, and be
averaged nonexpansive
. As we proceed, we will provide necessary and sufficient conditions for each of these properties, in terms of structural constraints of the involved matrices.

2.1.
Causality

In order for an operator
T
defined as in (
2.1
) to be well-defined, specifically to only requiring single evaluations of
J_{\gamma_{i}A_{i}}
and
C_{j}
for each
A\in\mathcal{A}_{n}
and
C\in\mathcal{C}_{m}
, the matrices
L
,
H
,
K
and
Z^{(2)}
need to meet certain structural properties.

First, let us observe that any frugal splitting method automatically defines a partial order (denoted by
\hookrightarrow
) on the evaluations of operators
A_{1},\dots,A_{n}
,
C_{1},\dots,C_{m}
. As this ordering is implicit in Definition
2.1
, we will assume it is explicitly given, and we require further structure on the matrices of (
2.1
) in order to not violate it. Additionally, note that, up to relabeling, we shall also assume that
A_{i}\hookrightarrow A_{j}
and, similarly,
C_{i}\hookrightarrow C_{j}
, for each
i<j
. To comply with (
2.1
), we necessarily have
A_{1}\hookrightarrow C_{j}
and
C_{j}\hookrightarrow A_{n}
for all
j\in\llbracket 1,m\rrbracket
.

Second, we find that the ordering
\hookrightarrow
is uniquely characterized by a vector
F\in\mathbb{N}^{n}
with
F_{1}=0
,
F_{n}=m
and
F_{i}\leq F_{i+1}
for each
i\in\llbracket 1,n-1\rrbracket
, a notion that we refer to as a
(m,n)
-nondecreasing vector
(see Proposition
A.6
). In our algorithms, we will interpret
F_{i}
as the number of forward operators that are evaluated before applying the
i
th backward operator in each iteration. In (
2.1
), backward steps are evaluated both first and last, which forces
F_{1}=0
and
F_{n}=m
.

With this notation, we can show the following, whose proof is postponed to the appendix.

Proposition 2.2
.

Each frugal splitting operator with minimal lifting as parameterized by (
2.1
) is well-defined if and only if
L
and
Z^{(2)}
are strictly lower triangular and
H,K^{T}
is a causal pair of matrices according to Definition
A.3
.

2.2.
Equivalence of algorithms

Following
[
22
]
, we now introduce a notion of
equivalence
between frugal splitting operators that immediately reduces the degrees of freedom in (
2.1
).

Definition 2.3

(Equivalent frugal splitting algorithms)
.

Two frugal splitting methods with minimal lifting are
equivalent
if the corresponding frugal splitting operators
T
and
\bar{T}
satisfy at least one of the following:

(i)

There exists an invertible matrix
\mathfrak{E}\in\mathbb{R}^{(n-1)\times(n-1)}
such that
\bar{T}=\mathfrak{E}^{-1}T(\mathfrak{E})
for any choice of
A\in\mathcal{A}_{n}
and
C\in\mathcal{C}_{m}
and any real Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
,

(ii)

\bar{T}
can be obtained from
T
by swapping the order of
A_{1},\dots,A_{n},C_{1},\dots,C_{m}
, or by scaling these monotone operators by some
\tau>0
.

Frugal splitting methods with respect to equivalent operators generate sequences that indeed coincide modulo an isomorphism of
\mathcal{H}^{n-1}
, or can be obtained with a different step size
\tau>0
or by swapping the order of the operators. Since in Section
2.1
we fixed the operators and their order of evaluation, in what remains, two equivalent methods are always understood in the sense of Item
(i)
. In the following result, we show that this is tightly related to a change of metric in
\mathcal{H}^{n-1}
. We will use this result in Section
4
to fix a-priori the canonical norm.

Proposition 2.4
.

A frugal splitting operator
T
with minimal lifting according to Definition
2.1
is nonexpansive in the norm
\|\cdot\|_{Q}
for
Q\in\mathbb{S}_{++}^{n-1}
if and only if there exists an equivalent operator
\bar{T}
that is nonexpansive w.r.t. the canonical norm.

Proof.

Let
\mathfrak{E}:=Q^{-\frac{1}{2}}
,
T
be nonexpansive in the norm
\|\cdot\|_{Q}
, and define
\bar{T}:=\mathfrak{E}^{-1}T(\mathfrak{E})
. Then
\bar{T}
is nonexpansive with respect to the canonical norm since for all
\boldsymbol{z},\boldsymbol{z}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{H}^{n-1}

(2.4)

\displaystyle\|\bar{T}(\boldsymbol{z})-\bar{T}(\boldsymbol{z}^{\prime})\|

\displaystyle=\|Q^{\frac{1}{2}}T(Q^{-\frac{1}{2}}\boldsymbol{z})-Q^{\frac{1}{2}}T(Q^{-\frac{1}{2}}\boldsymbol{z}^{\prime})\|=\|T(Q^{-\frac{1}{2}}\boldsymbol{z})-T(Q^{-\frac{1}{2}}\boldsymbol{z}^{\prime})\|_{Q}

\displaystyle\leq\|Q^{-\frac{1}{2}}\boldsymbol{z}-Q^{-\frac{1}{2}}\boldsymbol{z}^{\prime}\|_{Q}=\|\boldsymbol{z}-\boldsymbol{z}^{\prime}\|.

Additionally,
\bar{T}
is an instance of (
2.1
) with
\bar{U}:=\mathfrak{E}^{-1}U\mathfrak{E}
,
\bar{V}:=\mathfrak{E}^{-1}V
,
\bar{Z}^{(3)}:=\mathfrak{E}^{-1}Z^{(3)}
,
\bar{Z}^{(1)}:=Z^{(1)}\mathfrak{E}
, and
\bar{M}:=M\mathfrak{E}
. Therefore,
T
and
\bar{T}
are equivalent according to Definition
2.3
. The converse is similar.
∎

3.
Necessary and sufficient conditions for fixed-point encoding

Not all minimal lifting frugal splitting methods, as parameterized by (
2.1
), are useful for solving the inclusion problem (
1.1
). Consider for example the algorithm parameterized by
U=I
,
V=0
, and
Z^{(3)}=0
, which gives the fixed-point iteration
\boldsymbol{z}^{k+1}=\boldsymbol{z}^{k}
. This fixed-point iteration does not depend on the operators defining the inclusion problem, and fixed points of this fixed-point iteration give no meaningful information about solutions of (
1.1
). We are interested in the exact subset of algorithms of the form (
2.1
) that have the fixed-point encoding property, meaning solutions to (
1.1
) can easily be extracted from a fixed point of (
2.1
) and that the zero set of (
1.1
) is nonempty if and only if the fixed-point set of (
2.1
) is. In the following definition, a simple adaptation of
[
17
,
22
]
, we concretize the fixed-point encoding property outlined in (
P4
).

Definition 3.1

(Fixed-point encoding)
.

A frugal splitting operator
T
according to Definition
2.1
is a
fixed-point encoding
if there exist
s_{z}\in\mathbb{R}^{n-1}
,
s_{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, and
s_{u}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
such that the map
\mathfrak{s}:\mathcal{H}^{n-1}\to\mathcal{H}
defined by

(3.1)

\mathfrak{s}(\boldsymbol{z}):=s_{z}^{T}\boldsymbol{z}+s_{x}^{T}\boldsymbol{x}^{+}+s_{u}^{T}\boldsymbol{u}^{+},\quad\text{for all}\ \boldsymbol{z}\in\mathcal{H}^{n-1},

where
\boldsymbol{x}^{+},\boldsymbol{u}^{+}
are obtained through evaluation of
T(\boldsymbol{z})
, satisfies, for all
A\in\mathcal{A}_{n}
,
C\in\mathcal{C}_{m}
and real Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
,

(i)

\zer\left(A_{1}+\dots+A_{n}+C_{1}+\dots+C_{m}\right)\neq\emptyset\iff\Fix(T)\neq\emptyset
,

(ii)

\boldsymbol{z}^{\star}\in\Fix(T)\implies\mathfrak{s}(\boldsymbol{z}^{\star})\in\zer\left(A_{1}+\dots+A_{n}+C_{1}+\dots+C_{m}\right)
.

In this case,
\mathfrak{s}
is called
solution map
.

Indeed, adapting
[
17
, Proposition 1]
, we can immediately simplify the setting, since for any fixed-point encoding, the solution map is necessarily of a specific form:

Proposition 3.2
.

Let
T
be a frugal splitting operator with minimal lifting according to Definition
2.1
. Suppose that
T
is a fixed-point encoding according to Definition
3.1
and let
\mathfrak{s}:\mathcal{H}^{n-1}\to\mathcal{H}
be the corresponding solution map. Then, for all
\boldsymbol{z}\in\Fix(T)

(i)

a_{1}^{+}+\dots+a_{n}^{+}+u_{1}^{+}+\dots+u_{n}^{+}=0
,

(ii)

\mathfrak{s}(\boldsymbol{z})=x_{1}^{+}=\dots=x_{n}^{+}
,

where
\boldsymbol{x}^{+}\in\mathcal{H}^{n}
,
\boldsymbol{a}^{+}\in\mathcal{H}^{n}
and
\boldsymbol{u}^{+}\in\mathcal{H}^{m}
are obtained through evaluation of
T(\boldsymbol{z})
.

Proof.

Fix
\mathcal{H}
,
A\in\mathcal{A}_{n}
and
C\in\mathcal{C}_{m}
with
\zer(A_{1}+\dots+A_{n}+C_{1}+\dots+C_{m})\neq\emptyset
. Let
\boldsymbol{z}\in\Fix(T)
, and
\boldsymbol{x}^{+},\boldsymbol{a}^{+},\boldsymbol{u}^{+}
be the elements obtained by evaluation of
T
, i.e., those solving (
2.3
). Consider now for
j\in\llbracket 0,n\rrbracket
the tuples of operators
A^{(j)}\in\mathcal{A}_{n}
and
C^{(j)}\in\mathcal{C}_{m}
defined as follows. Set
C_{i}^{(j)}(x)=u_{i}^{+}
for all
i\in\llbracket 1,m\rrbracket
and
j\in\llbracket 0,n\rrbracket
. Regarding
A^{(j)}
, set, for all
x\in\mathcal{H}
and all
i\in\llbracket 1,n\rrbracket
,

(3.2)

A^{(0)}_{i}(x):=a_{i}^{+},\quad\text{and for all $j\in\llbracket 1,n\rrbracket$}\quad A^{(j)}_{i}(x):=\begin{cases}a_{i}^{+}&\text{if $i\neq j$},\\
a_{i}^{+}+x-x_{i}^{+}&\text{if $j=i$}.\end{cases}

Let
T^{(j)}
be the operator with respect to
A^{(j)}
and
C^{(j)}
. By construction,
\boldsymbol{z}\in\Fix(T^{(j)})
for all
j\in\llbracket 0,n\rrbracket
since
\boldsymbol{z},\boldsymbol{x}^{+},\boldsymbol{u}^{+}
and
\boldsymbol{a}^{+}
still solve (
2.3
) at
\boldsymbol{z}
with
\boldsymbol{A}
replaced by
\boldsymbol{A}^{(j)}:=\diag(A^{(j)})
, or, in other words,
\boldsymbol{x}^{+}
,
\boldsymbol{a}^{+}
and
\boldsymbol{u}^{+}
obtained via evaluation of
T^{(j)}(\boldsymbol{z})
all coincide for each
j\in\llbracket 0,n\rrbracket
.

Consider the case
j=0
. By Item
(i)
in Definition
3.1
, as
\Fix(T^{(0)})\neq\emptyset
we must have
\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}^{+}+\sum_{i=1}^{m}u_{i}^{+}=0
, which shows Item
(i)
. Consider now the case
j\in\llbracket 1,n\rrbracket
. From Item
(ii)
in Definition
3.1
,
x^{\star}:=\mathfrak{s}(\boldsymbol{z})=s_{z}^{T}\boldsymbol{z}+s_{x}^{T}\boldsymbol{x}^{+}+s_{u}^{T}\boldsymbol{u}^{+}
must solve for all
j\in\llbracket 1,n\rrbracket

(3.3)

0\in\sum_{i=1}^{n}A^{(j)}_{i}(x^{\star})+\sum_{i=1}^{m}C^{(j)}_{i}(x^{\star})=\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}^{+}+\sum_{i=1}^{m}u_{i}^{+}+(x^{\star}-x_{j}^{+})=x^{\star}-x_{j}^{+},

which shows Item
(ii)
and concludes the proof.
∎

Proposition
3.2
shows that if
\boldsymbol{z}\in\Fix(T)
then
x^{+}_{1}=\dots=x_{n}^{+}
and
x^{\star}:=x_{1}^{+}
solves (
1.1
). Therefore, we shall set aside the notion of solution map, as it is from now on implicitly encoded in the operator itself, without requiring any further operation.

3.1.
The case of pure resolvent splitting

To arrive at the necessary and sufficient conditions for (
2.1
) to have the fixed-point encoding property, we start by providing necessary conditions for fixed-point encoding of pure
resolvent
splitting methods with minimal lifting, i.e., when
m=0
. In this case, a frugal splitting operator
T
(
2.1
) is referred to as a
frugal resolvent splitting operator
and reduces to:

(3.4)

\boldsymbol{z}^{+}:=T(\boldsymbol{z}),\quad\text{with}\quad\left\{\begin{aligned} &\boldsymbol{x}^{+}=J_{\Gamma\boldsymbol{A}}(\Gamma L\boldsymbol{x}^{+}+\Gamma M\boldsymbol{z}),\\
&\boldsymbol{z}^{+}=U\boldsymbol{z}-V\boldsymbol{x}^{+}.\end{aligned}\right.

Lemma 3.3
.

Let
T
be a frugal resolvent splitting operator according to (
3.4
) with minimal lifting. Suppose that
T
is a fixed-point encoding. Then, the following hold:

(i)

\Null(V)=\Ran(\mathbbm{1})
,

(ii)

U=I
,

(iii)

\mathbbm{1}^{T}\left(\Gamma^{-1}-L\right)\mathbbm{1}=0
,

(iv)

\Null(M^{T})=\Ran(\mathbbm{1})
.

Proof.

In this proof, we argue with
\mathcal{H}=\mathbb{R}
.

Item
(i)
: Since
V\in\mathbb{R}^{(n-1)\times n}
,
\Null(V)\neq\{0\}
. Let
\boldsymbol{x}=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})\in\Null(V)
and take

(3.5)

A_{i}:=\partial\iota_{\{x_{i}\}},\quad\text{for}\ i\in\llbracket 1,n\rrbracket.

Observe that
\boldsymbol{z}=0
is a fixed point of
T
. Since
\Fix(T)\neq\emptyset
, we get
\zer(\sum_{i=1}^{n}A_{i})\neq\emptyset
, and thus
\boldsymbol{x}\in\Ran(\mathbbm{1})
. Therefore,
\Null(V)=\Ran(\mathbbm{1})
.

Item
(ii)
: Assume by contradiction that
U\neq I
. Pick
\boldsymbol{v}:=(I-U)\boldsymbol{z}^{\star}\neq 0
and
\boldsymbol{x}^{\star}\notin\Null(V)
such that
-V\boldsymbol{x}^{\star}=\boldsymbol{v}
. This is indeed possible since
V
is full rank by
(i)
. Consider now
A_{i}=\partial\iota_{\{x^{\star}_{i}\}}
for
i\in\llbracket 1,n\rrbracket
. Then,
\boldsymbol{z}^{\star}\in\Fix(T)
but
\zer\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}A_{i}\right)=\emptyset
, which yields the desired contradiction.

Item
(iii)
: Let us now consider constant operators defined by:

(3.6)

A_{i}(x)=v^{\star}_{i},\quad\text{for all}\ i\in\llbracket 1,n\rrbracket,\quad\text{with}\quad\boldsymbol{v}^{\star}=(v^{\star}_{1},\dots,v^{\star}_{n})=-(\Gamma^{-1}-L)\mathbbm{1}\in\real^{n}.

Then
\boldsymbol{z}^{\star}:=0\in\Fix(T)
since, with
\boldsymbol{x}^{\star}:=\mathbbm{1}
, we get by conditions
(i)
and
(ii)

that:

(3.7)

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{z}^{\star}=\boldsymbol{z}^{\star}-V\boldsymbol{x}^{\star}=0-0=0,

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=M\boldsymbol{z}^{\star}=(\Gamma^{-1}-L+\boldsymbol{A})(\boldsymbol{x}^{\star})=(\Gamma^{-1}-L-(\Gamma^{-1}-L))\mathbbm{1}=0.

Thus,
\zer(\sum_{i=1}^{n}A_{i})\neq\emptyset
, i.e.,
\mathbbm{1}^{T}(\Gamma^{-1}-L)\mathbbm{1}=0
.

Item
(iv)
:
Given arbitrary
\boldsymbol{v}^{\star}=(v_{1}^{\star},\dots,v_{n}^{\star})\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
such that
\mathbbm{1}^{T}\boldsymbol{v}^{\star}=0
define the operators as:

(3.8)

A_{i}(x)=x+v_{i}^{\star},\quad\text{for}\ i\in\llbracket 1,n\rrbracket\text{ and each }x\in\mathbb{R}.

Clearly, for any such choice of
\boldsymbol{v}^{\star}
,
\zer(\sum_{i=1}^{n}A_{i})=\{0\}\neq\emptyset
, implying through the fixed-point encoding assumption that the fixed-point set is nonempty. By Proposition
3.2
, we know that
\boldsymbol{x}^{\star}=0
and by (
2.3
), we know that
\boldsymbol{z}^{\star}
is a fixed point if and only if it satisfies:

(3.9)

M\boldsymbol{z}^{\star}=(\Gamma^{-1}-L+\boldsymbol{A})(\boldsymbol{x}^{\star})=0+\boldsymbol{v}^{\star},

As
\boldsymbol{v}^{\star}\in\Ran(\mathbbm{1})^{\perp}
is an arbitrary point in
\Ran(\mathbbm{1})^{\perp}
, and
\Fix(T)\neq\emptyset
by fixed-point encoding,
\Ran(M)\supseteq\Ran(\mathbbm{1})^{\perp}
, which is equivalent to
\Null(M^{T})\subseteq\Ran(\mathbbm{1})
. Since
M^{T}\in\mathbb{R}^{(n-1)\times n}
has a non-trivial null space, we conclude that
\Null(M^{T})=\Ran(\mathbbm{1})
.
∎

Remark 3.4
.

Observe that the counterexample in Item
(iv)
and specifically (
3.9
) can be used to show that if
T
is a fixed-point encoding, it cannot be defined on a
d
-fold product space with
d<n-1
, providing another explicit proof of
[
17
, Theorem 1]
.

3.2.
The general case

We now deal with the general case with
m>0
. We first note that a frugal splitting method with minimal lifting, for which
\boldsymbol{C}=0
, simplifies to a pure resolvent splitting, also with minimal lifting, so the conditions of Proposition
3.3

are necessary also for fixed-point encoding frugal splitting methods.

Lemma 3.5
.

Let
T
be a frugal splitting operator with minimal lifting according to (
2.1
) and satisfying the fixed-point encoding property. Then, all the conditions of
Lemma

3.3
hold and additionally:

(i)

H^{T}\mathbbm{1}=K\mathbbm{1}=\mathbbm{1}
,

(ii)

Z^{(1)}
,
Z^{(2)}
and
Z^{(3)}
are zero.

Proof.

Let
C_{i}=0
for
i\in\llbracket 1,m\rrbracket
. Then (
2.1
) reduces to (
3.4
), from which we conclude that the conditions
Lemma

3.3
are necessary also for general fixed-point encoding as in (
2.1
). Pick
\mathcal{H}=\mathbb{R}
.

Item
(i)
:
Let
\boldsymbol{A}=0
and let
C_{i}(x)=u^{\star}_{i}
for
i\in\llbracket 1,m\rrbracket
for any
\boldsymbol{u}^{\star}\in\real^{m}
. The corresponding inclusion problem is solvable if and only if
\mathbbm{1}^{T}\boldsymbol{u}^{\star}=0
and there exists a fixed point if and only if there exists
\boldsymbol{z}^{\star}\in\real^{n-1}
and
\boldsymbol{x}^{\star}\in\Ran(\mathbbm{1})
satisfying

(3.10)

\left(\Gamma^{-1}-L+\boldsymbol{A}\right)(\boldsymbol{x}^{\star})+H\boldsymbol{u}^{\star}\in\Ran(M),\quad\text{i.e.,}\quad 0+\mathbbm{1}^{T}H\boldsymbol{u}^{\star}=\mathbbm{1}^{T}M=0,

due to the necessary conditions of Lemma
3.3
.
Item
(i)
in the fixed-point encoding property implies that
\mathbbm{1}^{T}H\boldsymbol{u}^{\star}=0
if and only if
\mathbbm{1}^{T}\boldsymbol{u}^{\star}=0
, from which
\mathbbm{1}^{T}H=\mathbbm{1}^{T}
.

Consider instead
C_{i}(x)=\beta_{i}\left(x-K_{i}\mathbbm{1}\right)
for a fixed
i\in\llbracket 1,m\rrbracket
,
C_{j}=0
for
j\in\llbracket 1,m\rrbracket\setminus\{i\}
and let
A_{j}(x)=-(\Gamma^{-1}-L)_{j}\mathbbm{1}x
for
j\in\llbracket 1,n\rrbracket
and all
x\in\mathbb{R}
. Observe that, using Lemma
3.3
(iii)
,
x^{\star}=K_{i}\mathbbm{1}
is the unique solution to the corresponding inclusion problem. Further,
(\bar{\boldsymbol{z}},\bar{\boldsymbol{x}},\bar{\boldsymbol{u}},\bar{\boldsymbol{a}})=(0,\mathbbm{1},0,0)
solves (
2.3
) at
\bar{\boldsymbol{z}}
, since
\bar{\boldsymbol{z}}^{+}=U\boldsymbol{z}-V\boldsymbol{x}^{+}-Z^{(3)}\boldsymbol{u}^{+}=0
,

(3.11)

\bar{\boldsymbol{u}}=\boldsymbol{C}(K\mathbbm{1}+0+0)=0,\quad\text{and}\quad 0=\left(\Gamma^{-1}-L+\boldsymbol{A}\right)(\bar{\boldsymbol{x}})=M\bar{\boldsymbol{z}}-H\bar{\boldsymbol{u}}=0.

In particular,
\bar{\boldsymbol{z}}\in\Fix(T)
. Therefore, Proposition
3.2
(ii)
yields
x^{\star}=1
, hence
K_{i}\mathbbm{1}=1
. As
i
was arbitrary, we get
K\mathbbm{1}=\mathbbm{1}
.

Item
(ii)
: Let
A_{i}=\iota_{\{x^{\star}\}}
for
i\in\llbracket 1,n\rrbracket
and
x^{\star}\in\real
. Pick any
i\in\llbracket 1,m\rrbracket
, let
C_{i}=1
and let
C_{j}=0
for
j
in
\llbracket 1,m\rrbracket\setminus\{i\}
. The corresponding inclusion problem is solvable but there cannot exist a fixed point
\boldsymbol{z}^{\star}\in\mathbb{R}^{n-1}
with
\boldsymbol{x}^{\star}=\mathbbm{1}x^{\star}
, unless

(3.12)

\boldsymbol{z}^{\star}=\boldsymbol{z}^{\star}-V\boldsymbol{x}^{\star}-Z^{(3)}_{i},\quad\text{i.e.,}\quad Z^{(3)}_{i}=0,

where we used Lemma
3.3
(i)
.
Since the index
i
was arbitrary, we get
Z^{(3)}=0
. Let instead
C_{i}=\beta_{i}I
for some
i\in\llbracket 1,m\rrbracket
and let
C_{j}=0
for
j\in\llbracket 1,m\rrbracket\setminus\{i\}
. For any
\boldsymbol{z}^{\star}\in\real^{n-1}
let
A_{j}(x):=(M\boldsymbol{z}^{\star}-\beta_{i}H\diag(e_{i})Z^{(1)}\boldsymbol{z}^{\star})_{j}
for
j\in\llbracket 1,n\rrbracket
. We note that
(\boldsymbol{z}^{\star},\boldsymbol{x}^{\star},\boldsymbol{u}^{\star},\boldsymbol{a}^{\star}):=(\boldsymbol{z}^{\star},0,\beta_{i}\diag(e_{i})Z^{(1)}\boldsymbol{z}^{\star},\boldsymbol{A}(0))
solves (
2.3
) at
\boldsymbol{z}^{\star}
as

(3.13)

\displaystyle\left(\Gamma^{-1}-L+\boldsymbol{A}\right)(0)

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{A}(0)=M\boldsymbol{z}^{\star}-\beta_{i}H\diag(e_{i})Z^{(1)}\boldsymbol{z}^{\star}=M\boldsymbol{z}^{\star}-H\boldsymbol{u}^{\star},

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{u}^{\star}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{C}(0+Z^{(1)}\boldsymbol{z}^{\star}+0)=\beta_{i}\diag(e_{i})Z^{(1)}\boldsymbol{z}^{\star}.

Thus,
\boldsymbol{z}^{\star}\in\Fix(T)
. However,
\boldsymbol{x}^{\star}=0
does not solve the corresponding inclusion unless

(3.14)

0=\sum_{j=1}^{n}A_{j}(0)+\sum_{j=1}^{m}C_{j}(0)=\mathbbm{1}^{T}M\boldsymbol{z}^{\star}-\beta_{i}\mathbbm{1}^{T}H\diag(e_{i})Z^{(1)}\boldsymbol{z}^{\star}=e_{i}^{T}Z^{(1)}\boldsymbol{z}^{\star}.

Since
\boldsymbol{z}^{\star}
and
i
were arbitrary, we conclude that
Z^{(1)}=0
. For the last counterexample, let
C_{i}(x)=1
and
C_{j}(x)=\beta_{j}x
for all
x\in\mathbb{R}
and some fixed
i<j
,
i,j\in\llbracket 1,n\rrbracket
, and let
C_{h}=0
for
h\in\llbracket 1,m\rrbracket\setminus\{i,j\}
. Let
\boldsymbol{u}^{\star}:=e_{i}+\beta_{j}Z^{(2)}_{ij}e_{j}
and let
A_{i}(x)=-H_{i}\boldsymbol{u}^{\star}
for
i\in\llbracket 1,n\rrbracket
. We note that
(\boldsymbol{z}^{\star},\boldsymbol{x}^{\star},\boldsymbol{u}^{\star},\boldsymbol{a}^{\star}):=(0,0,e_{i}+\beta_{j}Z^{(2)}_{ij}e_{j},-H\boldsymbol{u}^{\star})
solves (
2.3
) at
\boldsymbol{z}^{\star}
since

(3.15)

\left(\Gamma^{-1}-L+\boldsymbol{A}\right)(0)=-H\boldsymbol{u}^{\star}=M\boldsymbol{z}^{\star}-H\boldsymbol{u}^{\star},\quad\text{and}\quad\boldsymbol{u}^{\star}=\mathbf{C}(0+Z^{(2)}\boldsymbol{u}^{\star}+0).

However, since
\boldsymbol{x}^{\star}=0
does not solve the corresponding inclusion problem unless
0=\sum_{i=1}^{m}C_{i}(0)+\sum_{i=1}^{n}A(0)=1-\mathbbm{1}^{T}H\boldsymbol{u}^{\star}=1-1-\beta_{j}Z^{(2)}_{ij}
, fixed-point encoding is violated unless
Z_{ij}^{(2)}=0
whenever
i<j
. Note that
Z^{(2)}_{ij}=0
also for
i\geq j
from Proposition
2.2
. Thus,
Z^{(2)}=0
.
∎

We next show that the necessary conditions are also sufficient, and the fixed-point operator with matrices satisfying the conditions of
Lemma

3.5
and Proposition
2.2
thus gives an exact characterization of the methods satisfying (
P1
), (
P2
), (
P3
), and
(
P4
)
.

Theorem 3.6
.

A frugal splitting operator with minimal lifting, as parameterized by (
2.1
) has the fixed-point encoding property if and only if it can be represented as

(3.16)

\boldsymbol{z}^{+}:=T(\boldsymbol{z}),\quad\text{with}\quad\left\{\begin{aligned} &\boldsymbol{x}^{+}=J_{\Gamma\boldsymbol{A}}(\Gamma L\boldsymbol{x}^{+}-\Gamma H\boldsymbol{C}(K\boldsymbol{x}^{+})+\Gamma M\boldsymbol{z}),\\
&\boldsymbol{z}^{+}=\boldsymbol{z}-V\boldsymbol{x}^{+},\end{aligned}\right.

where
M^{T},V\in\mathbb{R}^{(n-1)\times n}
,
\Gamma:=\diag(\gamma)\succ 0
,
L\in\real^{n\times n}
, and
H,K^{T}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}
satisfy:

(i)

\Null(M^{T})=\Null(V)=\Ran(\mathbbm{1})
,

(ii)

H
and
K
are causal in the sense of Definition
A.3
and satisfy
H^{T}\mathbbm{1}=K\mathbbm{1}=\mathbbm{1}
,

(iii)

L\in\real^{n\times n}
is strictly lower triangular such that
\mathbbm{1}^{T}\left(\Gamma^{-1}-L\right)\mathbbm{1}=0
.

In this case, if
\boldsymbol{z}\in\Fix(T)
,
\boldsymbol{x}^{\star}=\mathbbm{1}x^{\star}
and
x^{\star}
solves (
1.1
).

Proof.

Necessity follows directly from Proposition
2.2
and
Lemma

3.5
. For sufficiency, assume that conditions
(i)
–
(iii)
hold.
Then by
(i)
, there exists a fixed point of
T
if and only if there exists
x^{\star}\in\mathcal{H}
such that

(3.17)

\Ran(M)\cap\left(\Gamma^{-1}-L+\boldsymbol{A}+H\boldsymbol{C}K\right)(\mathbbm{1}x^{\star})\neq\emptyset.

Since
\Ran(M)=(\mathbbm{1}^{T})^{\perp}
and Items
(ii)
and
(iii)
hold, (
3.17
) is in turn equivalent to

(3.18)

0\in\mathbbm{1}^{T}\left(\Gamma^{-1}-L+\boldsymbol{A}+H\boldsymbol{C}K\right)(\mathbbm{1}x^{\star})=\sum_{i=1}^{n}A_{i}(x^{\star})+\sum_{i=1}^{m}C_{i}(x^{\star}).

There thus exists a fixed point of
T
if and only if the inclusion problem is solvable, and at a fixed point
\boldsymbol{z}^{\star}
we obtain
\boldsymbol{x}^{\star}:=\mathbbm{1}x^{\star}
for
x^{\star}
solving the inclusion problem.
∎

4.
Necessary and sufficient conditions for nonexpansiveness

Theorem
3.6
exactly captures the class of all frugal splitting methods with minimal lifting that have the fixed-point encoding property. This property guarantees that fixed points of the algorithm correspond to solutions of the underlying inclusion problem. However, an iteration of the form
\boldsymbol{z}^{k+1}=T(\boldsymbol{z}^{k})
, where
T
is an operator as in (
3.16
), does not necessarily find a fixed point and is therefore not useful. In this section, we will give necessary and sufficient conditions on the algorithm matrices for that
T
is averaged nonexpansive, implying convergence to a fixed point. Instead of imposing structural constraints on the involved matrices, like fixed-point encoding does, it constrains the sizes of involved matrices in the form of a linear matrix inequality. Note that we treat nonexpansiveness with respect to the canonical norm in
\mathcal{H}^{n-1}
. As we have shown in
Section

2.2
, this entails no loss of generality.

4.1.
The case of pure resolvent splitting

As in Section
3
, we first tackle the case of pure resolvent splitting, i.e., with
m=0
. So let us consider (
3.16
) with
\boldsymbol{C}=0
:

(4.1)

\boldsymbol{z}^{+}:=T(\boldsymbol{z}),\quad\text{with}\quad\left\{\begin{aligned} &\boldsymbol{x}^{+}=J_{\Gamma\boldsymbol{A}}(\Gamma L\boldsymbol{x}^{+}+\Gamma M\boldsymbol{z}),\\
&\boldsymbol{z}^{+}=\boldsymbol{z}-V\boldsymbol{x}^{+}.\end{aligned}\right.

We start with the following necessary condition:

Lemma 4.1
.

If
T
as defined in (
4.1
) is nonexpansive, then

(4.2)

V=\alpha M^{T},\quad\text{for some}\ \alpha\in\mathbb{R}_{++}.

Proof.

Also in this case we argue in
\mathcal{H}=\mathbb{R}
. We make the following choice: Fix an index
i\in\llbracket 1,n\rrbracket
, and let
A_{h}=\partial f_{h}
for all
h\in\llbracket 1,n\rrbracket
, with

(4.3)

f_{i}=0,\quad\text{and}\quad f_{j}=\iota_{\{0\}},\quad\text{for all}\ j\neq i.

Since the proximity operator of
f_{j}
with
j\neq i
is simply the zero function, in this case, for each
\boldsymbol{z}\in\mathbb{R}^{n-1}
, we get
x_{i}^{+}=\gamma_{i}m_{i}^{T}\boldsymbol{z}
and
x_{j}^{+}=0
for all
j\neq i
, where
m_{i}
is the
i
th row of
M
. Therefore, denoting by
v_{i}
the
i
th column of
V
,
T
writes as

(4.4)

T(\boldsymbol{z})=\boldsymbol{z}-V\boldsymbol{x}^{+}=\boldsymbol{z}-\gamma_{i}v_{i}m_{i}^{T}\boldsymbol{z}=\left(I-\gamma_{i}v_{i}m_{i}^{T}\right)\boldsymbol{z}.

Since
T
is nonexpansive,
I-T=\gamma_{i}v_{i}m_{i}^{T}
is monotone, implying
v_{i}=\alpha_{i}m_{i}
for some
\alpha_{i}\geq 0
. Repeating the argument with
i\in\llbracket 1,n\rrbracket
, we find that
V=M^{T}\diag(\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{n})
. Since
\Null(V)=\Null(M^{T})=\Ran(\mathbbm{1})
from Lemma
3.3
, it must hold

The class of averaged operators is in a one-to-one correspondence with that of nonexpansive operators. Indeed, by definition

4.2.1.

4.2.2.

We now show necessity of the condition in (

(4.17)

\displaystyle\underset{A,C,\boldsymbol{z},\boldsymbol{z}^{\star}}{\text{maximize}}

\displaystyle\|T(\boldsymbol{z})-T(\boldsymbol{z}^{\star})\|^{2}-\|\boldsymbol{z}-\boldsymbol{z}^{\star}\|^{2},

\displaystyle\text{subject to}

\displaystyle A\in\mathcal{A}_{n},\ C\in\mathcal{C}_{m},\ \text{$\textstyle\zer\big(\sum_{i=1}^{n}A_{i}+\sum_{i=1}^{m}C_{i}\big)\neq\emptyset$},\ \boldsymbol{z},\boldsymbol{z}^{\star}\in\mathcal{H}^{n-1}.

\min_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\ \|x-\xi_{1}\|+\dots+\|x-\xi_{n}\|+H_{\delta_{1},\delta_{2}}(\Psi x-y),

where
H_{\delta_{1},\delta_{2}}:\mathbb{R}^{p}\to\mathbb{R}
is a Huber-like smooth function defined for all
z:=(z_{1},\dots,z_{p})\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
by:

H_{\delta_{1},\delta_{2}}(z):=\sum_{i=1}^{p}h_{\delta_{1},\delta_{2}}(z_{i}),\quad\quad h_{\delta_{1},\delta_{2}}(z_{i}):=\begin{cases}0&\text{if}\ |z_{i}|\leq\delta_{1},\\
\frac{1}{2}(z_{i}-\delta_{1})^{2}&\text{if}\ |z_{i}|\in[\delta_{1},\delta_{2}],\\
(\delta_{2}-\delta_{1})|z_{i}|-\frac{1}{2}(\delta_{2}^{2}-\delta_{1}^{2})&\text{else}.\end{cases}

The function
h_{\delta_{1},\delta_{2}}
is differentiable with
1
-Lipschitz continuous gradient and thus
H_{\delta_{1},\delta_{2}}
has
\|\Psi\|_{2}
-Lipschitz continuous gradient. Instead of computing or estimating the norm of
\Psi
, we can split the forward term into
m\leq p
terms. Doing so, since
h_{\delta_{1},\delta_{2}}
is
1
-Lipschitz, we can take, for all
i\in\llbracket 1,m\rrbracket
,
\beta_{i}:=\|\Psi_{I_{i}}\Psi_{I_{i}}^{T}\|_{2}
where
\Psi_{I_{i}}\in\mathbb{R}^{|I_{i}|\times d}
denotes the
i
th block formed with the rows of
\Psi
indexed by
I_{i}\subset\llbracket 1,p\rrbracket
. Note that if
I_{i}=\{i\}
and
m=p
, then
C_{i}(x):=h_{\delta_{1},\delta_{2}}^{\prime}(\Psi_{i}x-y_{i})\Psi_{i}^{T}
where
\Psi_{i}
is the
i^{th}
row of
\Psi
and
\beta_{i}=\|\Psi_{i}\|^{2}
, which can be computed easily. Regarding the nonsmooth terms, we consider
A_{i}:=\partial g_{i}
with
g_{i}(x):=\|x-\xi_{i}\|
for all
i\in\llbracket 1,n\rrbracket
. Observe that for all
\tau>0
,
J_{\tau A_{i}}
coincides with
\prox_{\tau g_{i}}
, which admits a simple closed-form expression via a standard soft-thresholding.

6.1.2.
Algorithm design

To capture the generality of Algorithm
1
, with the aim of systematically testing the influence of the parametrization matrices, we follow construction principles that allow us to randomly generate instances of
M,H,K,
and

guaranteeing that all the hypotheses are met. This setup enables extensive numerical experiments and leads us to three heuristics that consistently deliver strong performances. In the following, it will be convenient to denote by
\mathcal{U}(I)

.

Defining
M
and
\mathcal{L}:=MM^{T}

We can easily define a matrix

satisfying the properties in Algorithm

by setting

(6.2)

M:=\left(I_{n}-\tfrac{1}{n}\mathbbm{1}\mathbbm{1}^{T}\right)\hat{M},\quad\text{for}\ \hat{M}\ \text{sampled from}\ \mathcal{U}(I_{M})^{n\times(n-1)},\ I_{M}\subset\mathbb{R}.

This yields a full rank matrix with probability
1
, and allows us to compare different randomized choices of

, cf. Section

described in Section

as in (

with

]

Heuristic 6.1

In Algorithm

, pick

Defining

To define a matrix

Defining causal

and

To define

and
\hat{K}^{T}
from

(6.3)

\hat{H}_{ij}=0,\ \hat{K}_{hi}=0,\quad\text{for all}\ h\leq F_{i}\leq j.

Eventually, we normalize the sum to one as:
H_{ij}:=\hat{H}_{ij}/\sum_{h=1}^{n}\hat{H}_{hj}

The

Application to portfolio optimization with decarbonization

We now consider a convex portfolio optimization problem using real market data. We select

p:=123

4
4

4

stockdata.org
.
. The corresponding asset returns are stored in a matrix
r\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times d}
. Each asset

has a carbon footprint encoded in three indexes
C_{i}^{1}
,
C_{i}^{2}
,
C_{i}^{3}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
, quantifying direct, indirect and other greenhouse gas emissions, which we take from
[
15
]

The standard Markowitz portfolio optimization problem consists in finding a portfolio, i.e.,
x\in\Delta
, where
\Delta\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
is the standard unit simplex in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, that maximizes the expected return
\hat{r}^{T}x
while penalizing the expected risk
x^{T}\hat{\Sigma}x

\hat{r}
is the vector of mean returns and
\hat{\Sigma}
is the covariance matrix of asset returns. In our experiment, we consider decarbonization constraints according to the 2015 Paris agreement on climate change. These require an average of
7\%

[
15
]
, can be imposed as
\sum_{i=1}^{d}C^{i}x_{i}\leq 0.93\sum_{i=1}^{d}C^{i}x^{0}_{i}
with
C^{i}:=C^{i}_{1}+C^{i}_{2}+C^{i}_{3}
. To further showcase the flexibility of our algorithmic approach, we impose that the carbon intensity decreases within each scope
j
of a percentage
\zeta_{j}
by imposing the corresponding constraint
x\in\mathcal{C}_{j}
for
j\in\llbracket 1,3\rrbracket
. In our model problem, we further account for the so-called one-way turnover of the portfolio, i.e., an
\ell_{1}
penalization with the current position
x^{0}
, which is standard in portfolio optimization, see, e.g.,
[
8
]
. Putting all together, our problem reads as:

(6.5)

\displaystyle\underset{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}{\text{minimize}}

\displaystyle x^{T}\hat{\Sigma}x-\hat{r}^{T}x+\|x-x^{0}\|_{1},

\displaystyle\text{subject to}

\displaystyle x\in\mathcal{C}_{1}\cap\mathcal{C}_{2}\cap\mathcal{C}_{3},\ x\in\Delta.

To tackle (
6.5
), we introduce the following non-smooth functions
g_{1}(x):=\|x-x^{0}\|_{1}
,
g_{2}(x):=\iota_{\Delta}(x)
,
g_{3}(x)=\iota_{\mathcal{C}_{1}}(x)
,
g_{4}(x):=\iota_{\mathcal{C}_{2}}(x)
and
g_{5}(x):=\iota_{\mathcal{C}_{3}}(x)
for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Then, we split the smooth function
x\mapsto x^{T}\hat{\Sigma}x-\hat{r}^{T}x
into four terms
f_{i}(x):=x^{T}\hat{\Sigma}_{i}x-\frac{1}{4}\hat{r}^{T}x

\hat{\Sigma}_{i}
is obtained by splitting
r
evenly along the first axis into four chunks

and setting
\hat{\Sigma}_{i}
as being the corresponding covariance matrix. The rationale for this split is illustrated in Figure
4(b)

We can observe that the second chunk of data, corresponding to the Covid-19 crisis, exhibits markedly different behavior compared to the other periods. In light of the considerations above, isolating this segment may prove beneficial. Finally, note that the number of chunks is not restricted to four.

In summary, (
6.5
) can be written as the sum of
n=5
non-smooth functions with simple proximity operators, and
m=3
smooth ones. Then, for
20

and

7.
Conclusions

In this paper, we provide the first, albeit partial, result toward resolving an open problem posed by Ryu in
[
22
]
. We characterize all averaged frugal resolvent splittings with minimal lifting and demonstrate compelling practical applications. Whether Algorithm
1
also captures all
unconditionally stable
methods remains a challenging open problem, for which we currently have no indication. Nevertheless, our results up to Remark
4.2
may prove instrumental in addressing it. Our work opens further avenues for future research: We aim to adapt our method to accommodate monotone Lipschitz (but not necessarily cocoercive) operators
C_{1},\dots,C_{m}
, and provide a full characterization of averaged splitting methods involving compositions with linear operators
à la
Chambolle–Pock.

Causality

Definition A.1

(
(m,n)
-nondecreasing vector)
.

Let

. We say that

F=(F_{1},F_{2},\ldots,F_{n})\in\{0,1,\ldots,m\}^{n}

is an
(m,n)
-nondecreasing vector
if
F_{1}=0
,
F_{n}=m
and
F_{i}\leq F_{i+1}
for each
i\in\llbracket 1,n-1\rrbracket
.

Definition A.2

(Staircase structure)
.

Let
n,m\in\mathbb{N}
and
F

be an
(m,n)
-nondecreasing vector.
Let
\mathcal{S}(F)
denote the set of matrices

that have a

w.r.t.
F
, i.e.,

M_{ij}=0\quad\text{for all }i=1,\ldots,n\text{ and }j>F_{i}.

Conversely,
\mathcal{S}^{c}(F)
denotes the set of matrices
M\in\real^{n\times m}
that have a complement staircase structure w.r.t.
F
, i.e.,
M_{ij}=0
for all
i=1,\ldots,n
and
j\leq F_{i}
. The staircase and complement staircase definitions are used to define what we call a causal pair of matrices.

Definition A.3

(Causal pair of matrices)
.

A pair of matrices
H,K^{T}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}
is said to be

if there exists an
(m,n)
-nondecreasing vector
F
such that

(A.1)

H\in\mathcal{S}(F)\,\quad\text{and}\quad K^{T}\in\mathcal{S}^{c}(F).

Remark A.4
.

The concept of causal pairs of matrices captures the full class of matrices

and
