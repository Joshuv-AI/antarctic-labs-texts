The zero-dispersion limit for the Benjamin–Ono equation on the circle
Ola Mæhlen

Abstract
. Using the explicit formula of P. Gérard, we characterize the zero-dispersion limit for solutions of the Benjamin–Ono equation on the circle 𝕋 ≔ ℝ / 2 ​ π ​ ℤ \mathbb{T}\coloneqq\mathbb{R}/2\pi\mathbb{Z} with bounded initial data u 0 ∈ L ∞ ​ ( 𝕋 , ℝ ) u_{0}\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{T},\mathbb{R}) . The result generalizes the work of L. Gassot, who focused on periodic bell-shaped data, and complements the work of Gérard and X. Chen who identified the zero-dispersion limit on the line with u 0 ∈ L 2 ∩ L ∞ ​ ( ℝ ) u_{0}\in L^{2}\cap L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}) . Here, as well as in the mentioned cases, the characterization agrees with the one first obtained by Miller–Xu for bell-shaped data on the line: The zero-dispersion limit is given as an alternating sum of the characteristics appearing in the (multivalued) solution of Burgers’ equation. From this characterization, we compute regularity properties of the zero-dispersion limit, including maximum principles and an Oleinik estimate.

2.1.
The Hardy spaces and Toeplitz operators

Let
C^{\omega}(\mathbb{D})
denote the set of holomorphic functions on the open unit disc
\mathbb{D}=\{z\in\mathbb{C}\colon|z|<1\}
. We introduce the two Hardy spaces

\displaystyle L^{2}_{+}(\mathbb{T})=

\displaystyle\,\{f\in L^{2}(\mathbb{T},\mathbb{C})\colon\hat{f}(k)=0,\,\,\forall k<0\},

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}^{2}(\mathbb{D})=

\displaystyle\,\{F\in C^{\omega}(\mathbb{D})\colon\textstyle\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}|F^{(k)}(0)|^{2}<\infty\}.

We endow

with its Hilbert space structure inherited from

\widehat{\Pi f}(k)=\mathbbm{1}_{k\geq 0}\hat{f}(k)
. The canonical bijection

is the mapping
f\mapsto F_{f}
defined by

(6)

F_{f}(z)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\hat{f}(k)z^{k},

whose inverse is the

-limit

. We will make use of the following important observation: For any real-valued
g\in L^{2}(\mathbb{T},\mathbb{R})\subset L^{2}(\mathbb{T},\mathbb{C})

g=\Pi g+\overline{\Pi g}-\langle\Pi g,1\rangle,

that is,

is completely determined by
\Pi g\in L^{2}_{+}(\mathbb{T})
which again is completely determined by
F_{\Pi g}\in\mathcal{H}^{2}(\mathbb{D})
; for brevity sake, we will simply write

in stead of

.

Next, with

we define the Toeplitz operator

by

T_{b}f\coloneqq\Pi(bf),

. Note that

Two particularly useful Toeplitz operator are the right-shift

S\coloneqq T_{q},\qquad\qquad S^{*}\coloneqq T_{\overline{q}},\qquad\text{where $q(x)\coloneqq e^{ix}$.}

g\colon\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{C}
. In particular,
e^{it\mathcal{L}^{\varepsilon}}\to e^{-2tT_{u_{0}}}
strongly; in fact, we claim that the convergence holds uniformly in
t\in I
for bounded
I\subset\mathbb{R}
. To see this, pick arbitrary sequences
t_{n}\to t
and
\varepsilon_{n}\to 0
, and set
\mathcal{L}_{n}\coloneqq\mathcal{L}^{\varepsilon_{n}}
. For
f\in H^{1}_{+}(\mathbb{T})
we infer

\lim_{n\to\infty}\big\|\big(e^{it_{n}\mathcal{L}_{n}}-e^{-2itT_{u_{0}}}\big)f\big\|_{L^{2}_{+}(\mathbb{T})}=\lim_{n\to\infty}\big\|{\textstyle\int_{t}^{t_{n}}}e^{is\mathcal{L}_{n}}\mathcal{L}_{n}f\,ds\big\|_{L^{2}_{+}(\mathbb{T})}\leq\lim_{n\to\infty}|t-t_{n}|\|\mathcal{L}_{n}f\|_{L^{2}_{+}(\mathbb{T})}=0.

And because
H^{1}_{+}(\mathbb{T})
is dense in
L^{2}_{+}(\mathbb{T})
, the claim is demonstrated. For any
k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
it follows that

\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\Big(e^{it\mathcal{L}^{\varepsilon}}S^{*}\Big)^{k}\Pi u_{0}=\Big(e^{-2itT_{u_{0}}}S^{*}\Big)^{k}\Pi u_{0}\quad\text{in }L^{2}_{+}(\mathbb{T}),

locally uniformly in
t
; this implies the sought convergence of Fourier coefficients, and so the weak limit
ZD[u_{0}]
satisfies (
11
). Finally, expanding
(\mathrm{id}-ze^{-2itT_{u_{0}}}S^{*})^{-1}
in
z
as Neumann series, one sees that the series expansion of (
12
) in
z
results in (
11
) when substituting
z\mapsto e^{ix}
, as claimed.
∎

Remark 2.2

(On the restriction
u_{0}\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{T},\mathbb{R})
)
.

Since both (
BO
) and the mapping
u_{0}\mapsto ZD[u_{0}]
are well-posed for

data, one may naturally expect that Theorem
1.1
extends to
u_{0}\in L^{2}(\mathbb{T},\mathbb{R})
. This could be true, but requires a different proof: If
u_{0}\in L^{2}(\mathbb{T},\mathbb{R})
is unbounded, then the operator
T_{u_{0}}
is also unbounded, as first shown by Toeplitz
[
47
,
46
]
. This is unproblematic for the explicit formula since
T_{u_{0}}
is still a well-defined symmetric operator on
H^{1}_{+}(\mathbb{T})
which is dominated by
D
, and so
D-T_{u_{0}}
is selfadjoint on

by the Kato–Rellich theorem. The same is true for
\varepsilon D-T_{u_{0}}
for any
\varepsilon>0
, but the
\varepsilon\downarrow 0
-limit becomes ambiguous, because
T_{u_{0}}
need not be essentially self-adjoint on

[
24
, Appendix A]
. In the previous proof, it is then no longer clear if the strong limit of

exists as
\varepsilon\downarrow 0
or, if so, what this limit is.

On the other hand, we note that there exist unbounded
u_{0}\in L^{2}(\mathbb{T},\mathbb{R})
such that

is still essentially selfadjoint on
H^{1}_{+}(\mathbb{T})
. As pointed out in
[
45
]
, this holds whenever
u_{0}
is locally semibounded, that is

(14)

\overline{u_{0}^{-1}((-\infty,-R])}\cap\overline{u_{0}^{-1}([R,\infty))}=\emptyset,\quad\text{for sufficiently large }R>0.

For such
u_{0}
, Theorem
1.1
applies — the proof being the same as for bounded
u_{0}
.

Remark 2.3

(Letting the initial data depend on
\varepsilon
)
.

In the previous proof, the steps would be exactly the same if the initial data was
\varepsilon
-dependent, i.e. if the family of solutions
(u^{\varepsilon})_{\varepsilon>0}
had initial data

such that the following

would still converge strongly to

. Proposition

thus holds true for

.

2.3.
The generalization of Miller–Xu’s alternating sum formula

The characterization (
2
) of
ZD[u_{0}]
by Miller and Xu, requires that
u_{B}
is a finite set for a.e.
(t,x)\in\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{T}
. This is true for sufficiently smooth
u_{0}
(finite variation will do), but in general false for
u_{0}\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{T},\mathbb{R})
. We here introduce an appropriate generalization of their formula.

Consider for now
u_{0}\in C^{1}(\mathbb{T},\mathbb{R})
, and let
u_{B}
denote the corresponding classical solution of Burgers’ equation (
Bu
) which exists for sufficiently small times
t\in(-\varepsilon,\varepsilon)
. In the spirit of the kinetic formulation for hyperbolic conservation laws we introduce the
\chi
-function

(15)

\chi(t,x,y)=\mathbbm{1}_{u_{B}(t,x)>y>0}-\mathbbm{1}_{u_{B}(t,x)<y<0}.

Intuitively speaking,
\chi(t)
represents the
(x,y)
-region enclosed by the graph
\{(x,y)\colon y=u_{B}(t,x)\}
and the line
y=0
; inside the region
\chi=\pm 1
depending on
y\gtrless 0
, and outside the region
\chi=0
. The transformation
u_{B}\mapsto\chi
is invertible, because
u_{B}(t,x)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\chi(t,x,y)\,dy
, and it has the benefit that (
Bu
) reduces to a linear transport equation

(16)

\partial_{t}\chi+2y\partial_{x}\chi=0,\quad\implies\quad\chi(t,x,y)=\chi(0,x-2ty,y),

where the former equation holds in weak sense. We use the right-hand side of (
16
) to extend
\chi
to all
t\in\mathbb{R}
, including those for which
u_{B}
becomes multivalued in the sense of (
1
). The value
\int_{\mathbb{R}}\chi\,dy
will no longer coincide with
u_{B}
when the latter is multivalued, however the following holds

(17)

\int_{\mathbb{R}}\chi(t,x,y)\,dy=\sum_{n=0}^{N}(-1)^{n}u^{n}_{B}(t,x),

with
u^{B}(t,x)=\{u_{B}^{0}(t,x)<u_{B}^{1}(t,x)<\dots<u_{B}^{N}(t,x)\}
and where
(t,x)\in\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{T}
must avoid the null set of caustics arising from the characteristics
y=u_{0}(x-2ty)
; a proof of this identity is given in the appendix, and it is illustrated in Figure
1
.

Figure 1
.
The graph (black curve) of the multivalued solution
u_{B}=u_{0}(x-2tu_{B})
of Burgers’ equation, drawn at
t=0
and a later time
t>0
. Red color implies
\chi=1
while blue implies
\chi=-1
. The dashed vertical line intersects the graph once at first, and later seven times; the alternating sum of these seven
y
-values coincides with the integral of
\chi
along the dashed line.

The benefit of (
17
) is that the left-hand side extends canonically to all data
u_{0}\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{T},\mathbb{R})
. Naturally, we expect it to coincide with the zero-dispersion limit from Propositon
2.1
, that is, we aim to prove that

(18)

ZD[u_{0}](t,x)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle?}}{{=}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\chi(t,x,y)\,dy\eqqcolon\int_{\mathbb{R}}\chi_{0}(x-2ty,y)\,dy,

where we used that
\chi(t,x,y)=\chi(0,x-2ty,y)
by its definition. Since
ZD[u_{0}]
is given as a Fourier series, we compute the same for the right-hand side of (
18
):

Proposition 2.4

(The generalized alternating sum formula)
.

For
u_{0}\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{T},\mathbb{R})
, set

AS[u_{0}](t,x)\coloneqq\int_{\mathbb{R}}\chi_{0}(x-2ty,y)\,dy,\qquad\chi_{0}(x,y)\coloneqq\mathbbm{1}_{u_{0}(x)>y>0}-\mathbbm{1}_{u_{0}(x)<y<0}.

Then
\Pi\big(AS[u_{0}]\big)
admits the Fourier expansion

(19)

\Pi\big(AS[u_{0}]\big)(t,x)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\bigg\langle\frac{1-e^{-2itku_{0}}}{2itk},q^{k}\bigg\rangle e^{ikx},

where
q(x)\coloneqq e^{ix}
, and where the coefficients must be interpreted in limit sense when
kt=0
. Equivalently,
AS[u_{0}]
can be represented in
\mathcal{H}^{2}(\mathbb{D})
by
F_{AS}(t,z)\coloneqq F_{AS[u_{0}](t)}(z)
which reads

(20)

F_{AS}(t,z)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\mathbb{T}}u_{0}\,dx+\frac{1}{4\pi it}\int_{\mathbb{T}}\mathrm{Log}\bigg(\frac{1-ze^{-i(x+2tu_{0})}}{1-ze^{-ix}}\bigg)\,dx,

where
\mathrm{Log}
denotes the principal branch logarithm.

Remark 2.5
.

The
\mathrm{Log}
-term is well defined because
w\in\mathbb{D}\implies\mathrm{Re}[1-w]>0
, which means that

\frac{1-w_{1}}{1-w_{2}}\in\mathbb{C}\setminus(-\infty,0],\quad\forall w_{1},w_{2}\in\mathbb{D}.

We also point out that the analogous expression for (
20
) on the real line
[
20
,
9
]
is

(21)

(t,\tilde{z})\mapsto\frac{1}{4\pi it}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\mathrm{Log}\bigg(1+\frac{2tu_{0}(x)}{x-\tilde{z}}\bigg)\,dx,

with domain
(t,\tilde{z})\in\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{C}_{+}
and
\mathbb{C}_{+}\coloneqq\{w\in\mathbb{C}\colon\mathrm{Im}[w]>0\}
.
It is indeed possible to obtain (
21
) as a long-period-limit of (
20
) (as one might expect), but we will not do so here.

Proof of Proposition
2.4
.

For
k\geq 0
, we compute

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{x}\Bigg[\int_{\mathbb{R}}\chi_{0}(x-2ty,y)\,dy\Bigg](k)=

\displaystyle\,\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\int_{\mathbb{T}}\chi_{0}(x-2ty,y)e^{-ikx}\,dx\,dy

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\,\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\int_{\mathbb{T}}\chi_{0}(x,y)e^{-ik(x+2ty)}\,dx\,dy

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\,\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\mathbb{T}}\bigg(\int_{0}^{u_{0}(x)}e^{-2itky}\,dy\bigg)e^{-ikx}\,dx

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\,\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\mathbb{T}}\bigg(\frac{1-e^{-2itku_{0}(x)}}{2itk}\bigg)e^{-ikx}\,dx=\bigg\langle\frac{1-e^{-2itku_{0}}}{2itk},q^{k}\bigg\rangle.

Thus, (
19
) is clear. And since

\mathrm{Log}\bigg(\frac{1-z\overline{q}e^{-2itu_{0}}}{1-z\overline{q}}\bigg)=\mathrm{Log}\Big(\frac{1}{1-z\overline{q}}\Big)-\mathrm{Log}\Big(\frac{1}{1-z\overline{q}e^{-2itu_{0}}}\Big)=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{(z\overline{q})^{k}}{k}\big(1-e^{-2itku_{0}}\big),

we see that the series expansion of (
20
) in
z
leads to (
19
) when setting
z\mapsto e^{ix}
as claimed.
∎

3.1.
Characterizing the zero-dispersion limit

In this section we prove Theorem
1.1

Proposition 3.1

(Series expanding the Fourier coefficients)
.

Let

,

, and
u_{0}\in H^{1}(\mathbb{T},\mathbb{R})
. Then we have the following series expansions written in notation introduced below or in Section

(22)

\displaystyle\Big\langle\big(e^{-2itT_{u_{0}}}S^{*}\big)^{k}\Pi u_{0},1\Big\rangle=

\displaystyle\,\sum_{d=0}^{\infty}\Bigg(\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}m\in X_{d,k}\\
n\in Y_{d,k}\end{subarray}}\langle S(m,n)1,1\rangle\frac{\hat{u}_{0}(m)}{n!}\Bigg)(-2it)^{d},

(23)

\displaystyle\Big\langle\frac{1-e^{-2itku_{0}}}{2itk},q^{k}\Big\rangle=

\displaystyle\,\sum_{d=0}^{\infty}\Bigg(\frac{k^{d}}{(d+1)!}\sum_{m\in X_{d,k}}\hat{u}_{0}(m)\Bigg)(-2it)^{d}.

The two sets

and

X_{d,k}\coloneqq\Big\{m\in\mathbb{Z}^{d+1}\colon{\textstyle\sum_{j=1}^{d+1}m_{j}=k}\Big\},\qquad Y_{d,k}\coloneqq\Big\{n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{k}\colon{\textstyle\sum_{\ell=1}^{k}n_{\ell}=d}\Big\},

and we have employed the multi-index notation

\hat{u}_{0}(m)\coloneqq\hat{u}_{0}(m_{1})\cdots\hat{u}_{0}(m_{d+1})\qquad n!\coloneqq n_{1}!\cdots n_{k}!.

Moreover, defining
N_{\ell}\coloneqq n_{1}+\dots+n_{\ell}
(and

is the following composition of shifts

S(m,n)\coloneqq\Bigg(\prod_{\ell=1}^{k}\Bigg[\Bigg(\prod_{j={1+N_{\ell-1}}}^{N_{\ell}}S_{m_{j}}\Bigg)S^{*}\Bigg]\Bigg)S_{m_{d+1}},\quad S_{r}\coloneqq T_{q^{r}}=\begin{cases}S^{r},&r=0,1,2,\dots\\
(S^{*})^{-r},&r=-1,-2,\dots\end{cases}

\prod_{j=a}^{b}c_{j}\coloneqq c_{a}\cdots c_{b}

\prod_{j=a}^{b}c_{j}\coloneqq\mathrm{id}

.

Proof.

Replacing

\displaystyle\Big(e^{-2itT_{u_{0}}}S^{*}\Big)^{k}T_{u_{0}}=\sum_{n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{k}}\frac{(-2it)^{d}}{n!}(T_{u_{0}})^{n_{1}}S^{*}\cdots(T_{u_{0}})^{n_{k}}S^{*}T_{u_{0}},

where

and

\displaystyle(T_{u_{0}})^{n_{1}}=\sum_{m\in\mathbb{Z}^{n_{1}}}\hat{u}_{0}(m)S_{m_{1}}\cdots S_{m_{n_{1}}},

where, as before, we use the short-hand notation

By similarly expanding

\displaystyle\Big\langle\big(e^{-2itT_{u_{0}}}S^{*}\big)^{k}T_{u_{0}}(1),1\Big\rangle=\sum_{n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{k}}\sum_{m\in\mathbb{Z}^{d+1}}\langle S(m,n)1,1\rangle\frac{(-2it)^{d}}{n!}\hat{u}_{0}(m).

We skip the proof of (

The only obstacle in comparing the two series (

3.2.
Proving the Raney-type lemma

Throughout the subsection, all finite sequences will be endowed with cyclic indexing. For a
\mathbb{Z}
-labeled sequence of real numbers
y=(\dots,y_{-1},y_{0},y_{1},\dots)
and
j\in\mathbb{Z}
we introduce the signed sum

\Sigma(y,j)\coloneqq\begin{cases}y_{1}+\dots+y_{j},&j>0,\\
-(y_{j+1}+\dots+y_{0}),&j<0,\\
0,&j=0,\end{cases}

which is easily seen to satisfy

(28)

\Sigma(y,j_{1}+j_{2})=\Sigma(y,j_{1})+\Sigma(T^{j_{1}}y,j_{2}),\quad\forall j_{1},j_{2}\in\mathbb{Z}.

Lemma 3.3

(Raney’s lemma,
[
26
]
)
.

If
y\in\mathbb{Z}^{d+1}
sums to one, that is,
\Sigma(y,d+1)=1
, then there is a unique cyclic shift
T^{r}\in\{T,\dots,T^{d+1}\}
such that the partial sums of
T^{r}y
are strictly positive:

(29)

\Sigma(T^{r}y,j)>0,\qquad j\in\{1,\dots,d\}.

Proof.

Define the
\mathbb{R}
-valued sequence
\tilde{y}_{j}\coloneqq y_{j}-\frac{1}{d+1}
. Since
y
is integer valued, we infer both the equivalence
\min_{1\leq j\leq d}\Sigma(T^{r}y,j)>0
if, and only if,
\min_{1\leq j\leq d}\Sigma(T^{r}\tilde{y},j)>0
, and that the numbers

r_{j}\coloneqq\Sigma(\tilde{y},j)

are all different for
j\in\{1,\dots,d+1\}
so that
r\coloneqq\min_{1\leq j\leq d+1}r_{j}
is well-defined. By(
28
),

(30)

\Sigma(T^{s}\tilde{y},j)=\Sigma(\tilde{y},s+j)-\Sigma(\tilde{y},s),

for all
s\in\{1,\dots,d+1\}
and
j\in\{1,\dots,d\}
. Because
\tilde{y}
sums to zero,
j\mapsto\Sigma(\tilde{y},j)
is
(d+1)
-periodic, and so if
s\neq r
, then there is some
j\in\{1,\dots,d\}
such that
s+j=r
mod
\,(d+1)
, making the right-hand side of (
30
) negative. Conversely, the right-hand side is positive for all
j\in\{1,\dots,d\}
if
s=r
.
∎

We shall prove Lemma
3.2
from the above Raney’s lemma (they are in fact equivalent). A first step in this direction is to obtain a more tangible description of when
\langle S(m,n)1,1\rangle
is one/zero:

Lemma 3.4
.

Let
d\geq 0
,
k\geq 1
, and
X_{d,k}\times Y_{d,k}
be as in Proposition
3.1
. For every
(m,n)\in X_{k,d}\times Y_{d,k}
we have the equivalence

(31)

\langle S(m,n)1,1\rangle=1,\qquad\quad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad\quad\Sigma(n,\Sigma(m,j))<j,\,\,\,\forall j\in\{1,\dots,d\},

and otherwise
\langle S(m,n)1,1\rangle=0
.

Proof.

For brevity set
N_{\ell}=\Sigma(n,\ell)
. Since
S^{*}=S_{-1}
, it is evident from (
24
) (or from (
26
) where the order of the indexed products is more clear) that

(32)

\langle S(m,n)1,1\rangle=\langle S_{y_{1}}S_{y_{2}}\cdots S_{y_{k+d+1}}1,1\rangle,

for a (canonically defined) sequence
y\in\mathbb{Z}^{k+d+1}
. More specifically, we can characterize
y
by first introducing the disjoint (possibly empty) index sets

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}^{*}\coloneqq

\displaystyle\,\{N_{1}+1,\dots,N_{k}+k\},

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}_{0}\coloneqq

\displaystyle\,\{r\in\mathbb{N}\colon N_{0}+0<r<N_{1}+1\},

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}_{k-1}\coloneqq

\displaystyle\,\{r\in\mathbb{N}\colon N_{k-1}+k-1<r<N_{k}+k\}

\displaystyle\mathcal{\mathcal{R}}_{k}\coloneqq

\displaystyle\,\{k+d+1\}.

The set
\mathcal{S}^{*}
represents the
k
left-shifts
S^{*}
appearing in (
24
), while
\mathcal{R}_{0},\dots\mathcal{R}_{k}
represents the remaining blocks of
S_{m_{j}}
-shifts separated by
S^{*}
. The implied sequence in (
32
) is then given by

(33)

y_{r}=\begin{cases}-1,&\text{if }r\in\mathcal{S}^{*},\\
m_{r-\ell},&\text{if }r\in\mathcal{R}_{\ell}.\end{cases}

The entries of
y
sum to zero, and so (
32
) is one (and otherwise zero) provided that
y_{r}+\dots+y_{k+d+1}\geq 0
for all
r\in\{1,\dots,k+d+1\}
, or equivalently if

(34)

y_{1}+\dots+y_{r}\leq 0,\quad\forall r\in\{1,2,\dots,k+d+1\}.

Since
y_{r}=-1
for
r\in\mathcal{S}^{*}
, these indices may be removed from the condition (
34
) without altering its validity. The same goes for the last index
\mathcal{R}_{k}=\{y_{k+d+1}\}
since
y
sums to zero. And so (
34
) may be written

(35)

\Big(y_{1}+\dots+y_{r}\leq 0,\quad\forall r\in\mathcal{R}_{\ell}\Big),\quad\forall\ell\in\{0,\dots,k-1\}.

Note that
\Sigma(y,r)=\Sigma(m,r-\ell)-\ell
for
r\in\mathcal{R}_{\ell}
and so we may further write (
35
) as

(36)

\Big(\Sigma(m,r-\ell)\leq\ell,\quad\forall r\in\mathcal{R}_{\ell}\Big),\quad\forall\ell\in\{0,\dots,k-1\}.

In (
36
), the index
r-\ell
ranges injectively over the values
\{1,\dots,d\}
: For
j\in\{1,\dots,d\}
there is a unique
\ell\in\{0,\dots,k-1\}
and
r\in\mathcal{R}_{\ell}
such that
j=r-\ell
; they are computed from the formulas

(37)

\ell\coloneqq\max\{\ell^{\prime}\in\mathbb{Z}\colon N_{\ell^{\prime}}<j\},\quad r\coloneqq j+\ell,

where the first formula is meaningful because
\ell^{\prime}\mapsto N_{\ell^{\prime}}
is nondecreasing and
N_{k}=d
.
Because of this, we can in (
36
) range over the index
j
instead of the pair
(r,\ell)
in which case (
36
) reads

(38)

\Sigma(m,j)\leq\ell(j),\quad\forall j\in\{1,\dots,d\},

where
\ell(j)
is as given by (
37
). The final observation is that

(39)

N_{p}<j\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad p\leq\ell(j),

for all
p\in\mathbb{Z}
and
j\in\{1,\dots,d\}
. In particular, (
39
) holds for the choice
p\coloneqq\Sigma(m,j)
in which case (
39
) gives the equivalence between (
38
) and the right-hand side of (
31
); tracing these equivalences back to (
32
), we see that the lemma is proved.
∎

Before proving Lemma
3.2
, we will show how to convert it into the framework of Raney’s lemma: For
(m,n)\in X_{d,k}\times Y_{d,k}
(this set is defined in Proposition
3.1
) we associate a vector
y=y(m,n)\in\mathbb{Z}^{d+1}
, endowed with cyclic indexing and defined by

(40)

y(m,n)_{j}=1+\Sigma(n,\Sigma(m,j-1))-\Sigma(n,\Sigma(m,j)),\quad j\in\mathbb{Z}.

We stress that the right-hand side of (
40
) is
(d+1)
-periodic in
j
(the definition is therefore compatible with the cyclic indexing of
y
); this is easily seen from (
28
) if one exploits that
T^{d+1}m=m
,
T^{k}n=n
,
\Sigma(m,d+1)=k
, and
\Sigma(n,k)=d
. This vector has various good properties:

Lemma 3.5
.

For
(m,n)\in X_{d,k}\times Y_{d,k}
let
y=y(m,n)\in\mathbb{Z}^{d+1}
be as in (
40
). Then the following holds:

i)

The vector
y
sums to one,
y_{1}+\dots+y_{d+1}=1
.

ii)

The weight
\langle S(m,n)1,1\rangle
is one (and otherwise zero) if, and only if, the associated vector has strictly positive partial sums,
y_{1}+\dots+y_{j}>0
for all
j\in\{1,\dots,d\}
.

iii)

A cyclic shift of
y
is related to cyclic shifts of
(m,n)
through the formula

(41)

T\big(y(m,n)\big)=y(Tm,T^{m_{1}}n).

Proof.

Since
\Sigma(n,\Sigma(m,0))=0
, we have

y_{1}+\dots+y_{j}=j-\Sigma(n,\Sigma(m,j)),\qquad j\geq 1.

Thus
i)
follows because
\Sigma(n,\Sigma(m,d+1))=\Sigma(n,k)=d
, while
ii)
follows from Lemma
3.4
.
We get
iii)
by using (
28
):

\displaystyle(Ty)_{j}=

\displaystyle\,1+\Sigma\Big(n,\Sigma(m,j)\Big)-\Sigma\Big(n,\Sigma(m,j+1)\Big)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\,1+\Sigma\Big(n,m_{1}+\Sigma(Tm_{1},j-1)\Big)-\Sigma\Big(n,m_{1}+\Sigma(Tm,j)\Big)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\,1+\Sigma(n,m_{1})+\Sigma\Big(T^{m_{1}}n,\Sigma(Tm_{1},j-1)\Big)-\Sigma(n,m_{1})-\Sigma\Big(T^{m_{1}}n,\Sigma(Tm,j)\Big)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\,y(Tm,T^{m_{1}}n)_{j}.\qed

We are finally sufficiently equipped to prove the Raney-type lemma.

Proof of Lemma
3.2
.

On
X_{d,k}\times Y_{d,k}
we define the permutation
\sigma(m,n)\coloneqq(Tm,T^{m_{1}}n)
. We then have

(42)

\sum_{j=1}^{d+1}\sum_{\ell=1}^{k}\langle S(T^{j}m,T^{\ell}n)1,1\rangle=\sum_{j=1}^{d+1}\sum_{\ell=1}^{k}\langle S(\sigma^{j}(m,T^{\ell}n)1,1),

because
\sigma^{j}(m,T^{\ell})=(T^{j}m,T^{\ell+s(j)}n)
where
s(j)\coloneqq\Sigma(m,j)
, and because the
s(j)
-perturbation makes no difference to the inner sum since
T^{k}n=n
. Next, in light of Raney’s lemma (Lemma
3.3
) and
i)
from Lemma
3.5
, we infer for
y=y(m,T^{\ell}n)
that there is precisely one
r\in\{1,\dots,d+1\}
such that
T^{r}y
has strictly positive partial sums. And by
ii)
and
iii)
in Lemma
3.5
it follows that

\langle S\big(\sigma^{j}(m,T^{\ell}n)\big)1,1\rangle=\delta_{jr}\qquad\implies\qquad\sum_{j=1}^{d+1}\langle S\big(\sigma^{j}(m,T^{\ell}n)\big)1,1\rangle=1.

The lemma then follows by interchanging the inner and outer sum in (
42
).
∎

3.3.
Properties of the zero-dispersion limit

In this section we prove Theorem
1.2
; the proof will rely solely on properties of the mapping
AS[\cdot]
introduced earlier, but which we define again here in equivalent, but more convenient, notation:

(43)

AS[v](t,x)\coloneqq\int_{0}^{\infty}f_{v}(x-2ty,y)\,dy,\qquad f_{v}(x,y)\coloneqq\mathbbm{1}_{v(x)>y>0}-\mathbbm{1}_{v(x)<-y<0},

for any
v\in L^{1}(\mathbb{T},\mathbb{R})
.

Proof of Theorem
1.2
.

We prove the maximum principle first as it will be useful later.

Maximum principles:
For
v\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{T})
it is obvious from definition (
43
) that

-\mathbbm{1}_{\inf v<-y<0}\leq f_{v}(x,y)\leq\mathbbm{1}_{\sup v>y>0},

and so the maximum principle follows. In particular one gets
\|AS[v](t)\|_{L^{\infty}}\leq\|v\|_{L^{\infty}}
.

Joint time- and
L^{p}
-continuity:
Let
p\in[1,\infty)
,
v,w\in L^{p}(\mathbb{T},\mathbb{R})
,
t\in\mathbb{R}
, and let
[h]^{p}\coloneqq h|h|^{p-1}
denote the signed power. Using a layer cake representation, one finds that

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}py^{p-1}|(f_{v}-f_{w})(x,y)|\,dy=

\displaystyle\,\big|[v]^{p}-[w]^{p}\big|(x),

Using this identity, the bound
\|f_{v}-f_{w}\|_{L^{\infty}}\leq 2
, and Lemma
A.2
, we have

(44)

\begin{split}\|\big(AS[v]-AS[w]\big)(t)\|_{L^{p}(\mathbb{T})}^{p}\leq&\,\|\,\|(f_{v}-f_{w})(x-2ty,y)\|_{L^{1}_{y}(\mathbb{R}_{+})}\|_{L^{p}_{x}(\mathbb{T})}^{p}\\
\leq&\,2^{p-1}\int_{\mathbb{T}}\int_{0}^{\infty}py^{p-1}|(f_{v}-f_{w})(x-2ty,y)|\,dy\,dx\\
=&\,2^{p-1}\int_{\mathbb{T}}\int_{0}^{\infty}py^{p-1}|(f_{v}-f_{w})(x,y)|\,dy\,dx\\
=&\,2^{p-1}\int_{\mathbb{T}}\big|[v]^{p}-[w]^{p}\big|\,dx\\
\leq&\,2^{p-1}\int_{\mathbb{T}}p\big(|v|+|w|\big)^{p-1}|v-w|\,dx\\
\leq&\,p2^{p-1}\big(\|v\|_{L^{p}(\mathbb{T})}+\|w\|_{L^{p}(\mathbb{T})}\big)^{p-1}\|v-w\|_{L^{p}(\mathbb{T})}.\end{split}

That is,
v\mapsto AS[v](t)
is locally uniformly
\frac{1}{p}
-Hölder continuous on
L^{p}(\mathbb{T})
with a
t
-independent coefficient. Let
v_{n}\to v\in L^{p}(\mathbb{T})
and
t_{n}\to t\in\mathbb{R}
. Using (
44
) and the triangle inequality we get

\displaystyle\limsup_{n\to\infty}\|AS[v_{n}](t_{n})-AS[v](t)\|_{L^{p}(\mathbb{T})}\leq

\displaystyle\,\limsup_{n\to\infty}\|AS[v](t_{n})-AS[v](t)\|_{L^{p}(\mathbb{T})}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\,\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\limsup_{n\to\infty}\|AS[v^{\varepsilon}](t_{n})-AS[v^{\varepsilon}](t)\|_{L^{p}(\mathbb{T})},

where
v^{\varepsilon}\in C^{1}(\mathbb{T})
is such that
\|v-v^{\varepsilon}\|_{L^{p}(\mathbb{T})}<\varepsilon
. Thus, it remains to show that
AS[\varphi]\in C(\mathbb{R},L^{p}(\mathbb{T}))
for any
\varphi\in C^{1}(\mathbb{T})
: For this, note first that
|f_{\varphi}(x,y)-f_{\varphi}(z,y)|=0
, if either
|y|>\|\varphi\|_{L^{\infty}}
or if
|y-\varphi(x)|>|x-z|\|\varphi^{\prime}\|_{L^{\infty}}
. Using both of these two observations, and setting
z=x+\delta y
, it holds that

\displaystyle|f_{\varphi}(x,y)-f_{\varphi}(x+\delta y,y)|

\displaystyle\,=0,\text{ if }|y-\varphi(x)|>|\delta|\|\varphi\|_{L^{\infty}}\|\varphi^{\prime}\|_{L^{\infty}},

\displaystyle\implies\int_{0}^{\infty}|f_{\varphi}(x,y)-f_{\varphi}(x+\delta y,y)|

\displaystyle\,\,dy\leq|\delta|\|\varphi\|_{L^{\infty}}\|\varphi^{\prime}\|_{L^{\infty}}.

Picking
s,t\in\mathbb{R}
, we get the desired time-continuity

\displaystyle\|AS[\varphi](t)-AS[\varphi](s)\|_{L^{p}(\mathbb{T})}^{p}\leq

\displaystyle\,(2\|\varphi\|_{L^{\infty}})^{p-1}\|AS[\varphi](t)-AS[\varphi](s)\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{T})}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\,(2\|\varphi\|_{L^{\infty}})^{p-1}\int_{\mathbb{T}}\int_{0}^{\infty}|f_{\varphi}(x-2ty,y)-f_{\varphi}(x-2sy,y)|\,dy\,dx

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\,(2\|\varphi\|_{L^{\infty}})^{p-1}\int_{\mathbb{T}}\int_{0}^{\infty}|f_{\varphi}(x,y)-f_{\varphi}(x+2(t-s)y,y)|\,dy\,dx

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\,2\pi(2\|\varphi\|_{L^{\infty}})^{p}\|\varphi^{\prime}\|_{L^{\infty}}|t-s|.

Norm control and
L^{1}
contractions:
The control of the
L^{\infty}
norm was demonstrated at the beginning. The
p\in[1,\infty)
case is dealt with using Lemma
A.2
:

(45)

\|AS[v](t)\|_{L^{p}(\mathbb{T})}\leq\int_{\mathbb{T}}\int_{0}^{\infty}py^{p-1}|f_{v}(x-2ty,y)|\,dy\,dx=\int_{\mathbb{T}}\int_{0}^{\infty}py^{p-1}|f_{v}(x,y)|\,dy\,dx=\|v\|_{L^{p}(\mathbb{T})}.

The
L^{1}
-contractions are obtained from (
44
) when setting
p=1
.

Oleinik estimate:
It will here be more convenient to use the original notation for
AS
, namely
AS[v](t,x)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\chi_{v}(x-2ty,y)\,dy
where
\chi_{v}(x,y)\coloneqq\mathbbm{1}_{v(x)>y>0}-\mathbbm{1}_{v(x)<y<0}
. This is because of the pointwise bound
\chi_{v}(x,y+h)\leq\chi_{v}(x,y)+\mathbbm{1}_{0>y>-h}
, which is valid for
h>0
,
x\in\mathbb{T}
, and
y\in\mathbb{R}
. Suppose
t>0
, then

\displaystyle AS[v](t,x+h)=

\displaystyle\,\int_{\mathbb{R}}\chi_{v}(x+h-2ty,y)\,dy=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\chi_{v}\big(x-2ty,y+\tfrac{h}{2t}\big)\,dy

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\,\int_{\mathbb{R}}\chi_{v}(x-2ty,y)+\mathbbm{1}_{0>y>-h/2t}\,dy=AS[v](t,x)+\frac{h}{2t}.

The remaining cases,
h,t<0
,
h<0<t
, and
t<0<h
, can be dealt with similarly; some of the inequalities will flip, but in all cases the resulting estimate can be written as in Theorem
1.2
.

Strong convergence before breaking:
We need only prove that
\|AS[u_{0}](t)\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T})}=\|u_{0}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T})}
for
t\in[T_{-},T_{+}]
since weak convergence plus convergence of norms implies strong convergence in
L^{2}(\mathbb{T})
. For fixed
t\in(T_{-},T_{+})
and
x\in\mathbb{T}
, note that there is a unique solution
y_{*}
to the equation
0=h(y)\coloneqq y-u_{0}(x-2ty)
since
h^{\prime}(y)>1
with
h(\pm\infty)=\infty
. As a consequence, we get that
y\mapsto f_{v}(x-2ty,y)
is of the form
y\mapsto\mathrm{sgn}(y_{*})\mathbbm{1}_{0<y<|y_{*}|}
. This further implies that
|\int_{0}^{\infty}f_{v}(x-2ty,y)\,dy|=\int_{0}^{\infty}|f_{v}(x-2ty,y)|\,dy
and that the bound of Lemma
A.2
is in-fact an equality when applied to
y\mapsto f(x-2ty,y)
. Thus, the inequality in (
45
) becomes an equality for all
p\in[1,\infty)
; in particular for
p=2
. By the
L^{2}
-continuity of
AS[\cdot]
, this extends up to the endpoints
t\in\{T_{-},T_{+}\}
as well.

Entropy solution from Trotter’s formula:
The mapping
(t,u_{0})\mapsto ZD[u_{0}](t)
coincides with the transport-collapse map
(u_{0},t)\mapsto T(t)u_{0}
in
[
8
]
when the flux-function is defined
B(u)=u^{2}
. Strictly speaking, it is assumed in
[
8
]
that
B
is globally Lipschitz,
x\in\mathbb{R}
, and
u_{0}\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R})
. However if
u_{0}
is bounded, then by maximum principles and finite speed of propagation (both of the map
T
and of the entropy solution
u_{\mathrm{ent}}
), the result in
[
8
]
carries over to our setting. In particular, we get from
[
8
, Theorem 1]
that

(46)

u_{\mathrm{ent}}(t)=\lim_{n\to\infty}T(\tfrac{t}{n})^{\circ n}u_{0},\quad\text{in }L^{1}(\mathbb{T}),

for all
t\geq 0
, and
u_{0}\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{T})
. The extension to
u_{0}\in L^{1}(\mathbb{T})
follows by density when using the
L^{1}
-contractions.
∎

Appendix A
Auxiliary lemmas

The following lemma is a justification of the identity (
17
).

Lemma A.1
.

Let
u_{0}\in C^{1}(\mathbb{T},\mathbb{R})
, and let
(t,x)
denote points in
\in\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{T}
. Define the multivalued solution of the Burgers equation (
Bu
) and the set of caustics (the envelopes of the characteristics) by

\displaystyle u_{B}(t,x)\coloneqq

\displaystyle\,\{y\colon y=u_{0}(x-2ty)\},

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}\coloneqq

\displaystyle\,\big\{(t,x)\colon\exists z\in\mathbb{R},\,g_{t}(z)=x,\,g^{\prime}_{t}(z)=0\big\},

where
g_{t}\in C^{1}(\mathbb{R},\mathbb{T})
is defined
g_{t}(z)=z+2tu_{0}(z)
.
Then
\mathcal{C}
is a Lebesgue null set. Moreover, if
(t,x)\notin\mathcal{C}
, then
u_{B}(t,x)
contains an odd number of values
u_{B}^{0}(t,x)<\dots<u_{B}^{N}(t,x)
satisfying

\sum_{n=0}^{N}(-1)^{n}u^{n}_{B}(t,x)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\chi_{0}(x-2ty,y)\,dy,\quad\text{where }\chi_{0}(x,y)\coloneqq\mathbbm{1}_{u_{0}(x)>y>0}-\mathbbm{1}_{u_{0}(x)<y<0}.

Proof.

Since
u_{0},u_{0}^{\prime}
are continuous,
\mathcal{C}
is a closed set and therefore measurable. Thus,
\mathcal{C}
is a null set in
\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{T}
because each fiber
\mathcal{C}_{t}=\{x\colon(t,x)\in\mathcal{C}\}
, which is precisely the critical values of
g_{t}
, is a null set in
\mathbb{T}
by Sard’s theorem.

Next, fix
(t,x)\notin\mathcal{C}
, then any root
y_{*}
of the function
h\in C^{1}(\mathbb{R},\mathbb{R})
, defined

h(y)\coloneqq y-u_{0}(x-2ty)
,
must be simple, because if
h(y_{*})=h^{\prime}(y_{*})=0
then
(t,x)\in\mathcal{C}
with corresponding
z=x-2ty_{*}
. Furthermore, since
\lim_{y\to\pm\infty}h(y)=\pm\infty
, it follows that
h
has an odd number of roots that we label
y_{0}<\dots<y_{N}
with
N\in 2\mathbb{N}_{0}
; these roots make up the set
u_{B}(t,x)
. Using the trivial identity
y_{n}=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\mathbbm{1}_{y_{n}>y>0}-\mathbbm{1}_{y_{n}<y<0}\,dy
we get that

(47)

\sum_{n=0}^{N}(-1)^{n}y_{n}=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\sum_{n=0}^{N}(-1)^{n}\mathbbm{1}_{y_{n}>y>0}\,dy-\int_{\mathbb{R}}\sum_{n=0}^{N}(-1)^{n}\mathbbm{1}_{y_{n}<y<0}\,dy.

Since
N
is even, one sees that the integrand of the first integral is equal to one (and otherwise zero) precisely when both
y>0
and an odd number of the roots
y_{0},\dots,y_{N}
exceed
y
; the latter condition coincides with the condition
0>h(y)
(the roots are simple and
h(\infty)=\infty
) which is to say that
u_{0}(x-2ty)>y
. Thus,
\sum_{n=0}^{N}(-1)^{n}\mathbbm{1}_{y_{n}>y>0}=\mathbbm{1}_{u_{0}(x-2ty)>y>0}
.
Reformulating the second integrand in (
47
) similarly, one gets the desired identity of the lemma.
∎

Lemma A.2
.

Let
f\in L^{1}\cap L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}_{+},\mathbb{R})
, and
p\in[1,\infty)
. Then

(48)

\|f\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}_{+})}\leq p^{\frac{1}{p}}\|f\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}_{+})}^{1-\frac{1}{p}}\||\cdot|^{p-1}f\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}_{+})}^{\frac{1}{p}},

with equality whenever
f(x)=\alpha\mathbbm{1}_{0<x<R}
for
R>0
and
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
.

Proof.

Set
F(x)\coloneqq\int_{0}^{x}|f(y)|\,dy\leq x\|f\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}_{+})}
. Then

\|f\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}_{+})}^{p}=F(\infty)^{p}=p\int_{0}^{\infty}F^{p-1}(x)F^{\prime}(x)\,dx\leq p\|f\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}_{+})}^{p-1}\int_{0}^{\infty}x^{p-1}|f(x)|\,dx.

The last part of the lemma is trivial.
∎
