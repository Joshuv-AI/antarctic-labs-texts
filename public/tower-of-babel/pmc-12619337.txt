Veterinary medicine in reintroduction and reinforcement of the European pond turtle Emys orbicularis: a review
Authors: Carolin EICHERT, Kathrin THEISSINGER, Benoît QUINTARD, Jean-Yves GEORGES
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The European pond turtle Emys orbicularis is among the most endangered reptile species in Europe, facing severe population decline due to habitat loss and environmental degradation. Conservation efforts increasingly rely on translocation-based strategies, including species reintroduction and reinforcement. Veterinary medicine plays a crucial role in ensuring the health and survival of captive-bred and translocated individuals, yet standardized health assessment protocols remain scarce. This review based on 418 publications synthesizes current veterinary practices related to freshwater turtle conservation, focusing on health monitoring before, during, and after translocation. We evaluate key medical concerns across different life stages, including prevention of zootechnical issues, pathogen screening, microbiome health, and adaptation challenges in captive and wild populations. Additionally, we propose standardized health assessment guidelines to improve diagnostic accuracy and long-term monitoring, and provide a global overview of the common health issues as well as normal blood reference values. By fostering transdisciplinary collaboration between veterinarians, researchers and conservationists, this study aims to refine best practices and ensure sustainable, evidence-based conservation efforts for E. orbicularis and other freshwater turtle species.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12917-025-05097-w.

Background

In the context of the ongoing sixth mass extinction crisis, population reinforcements and species reintroductions are increasingly recognized as effective nature-based solutions to mitigate biodiversity loss [1–3]. Reinforcement involves the translocation of individuals to bolster existing local populations [4], whereas reintroduction aims to restore a species to its former natural habitat from which it has disappeared [5]. Both approaches are ex situ conservation strategies whose success depends on the suitability of the release habitat and the ability of translocated individuals to survive and reproduce in the wild after being, in most cases, raised in captivity under artificial conditions [6].

Zoos and breeding facilities, both institutional and private, play a crucial role in such conservation efforts. The European Association of Zoos and Aquaria (EAZA) reported 240 reintroduction or translocation projects involving 156 species across Europe, with zoological parks actively participating [7]. Moreover, zoos provide critical expertise in veterinary science and medicine to ensure optimal health conditions for individuals before and after translocation [8]. Health assessments serve as essential tools integrating veterinary medicine and wildlife ecology to enhance conservation planning and species protection [9]. These assessments provide key indicators at individual, population, and ecosystem levels, aiding in the refinement of management strategies [9–11]. Veterinary monitoring is essential to establishing healthy, self-sustaining populations in both captivity and the wild, while minimizing the risk of infectious disease transmission to newly established and existing populations [12–15]. Additionally, private breeders have developed specialized and effective practices; however, these are often restricted to specific species or not aware of certain health issues concerning the species and are rarely disseminated beyond individual projects or citizen science initiatives [16–19]. As a result, numerous veterinary and husbandry practices exist worldwide, yet their limited dissemination makes it challenging for new projects to identify standardized and appropriate protocols.

A key issue in veterinary medicine is the complex definition of health, especially in turtles and the need of compliance of translocated and recipient populations’ health states, making a on-site situation-adjusted approach inevitable in chelonian species [20, 21]. At a first stage it is critical to determine a turtles’ individual health, which has to be evaluated in the context of external and internal influencing factors like reproductive state, the concomitance of co-infections and cumulation of environmental stressors [22]. Besides, with chelonians as patient with little signs of externally detectable disease often advanced techniques of investigation appear inevitable, which in turn means more intense financial and temporal effort [21]. Even if those additional tests have been made, the results have to be interpreted with caution, as blood parameter shifts can have various physiological reasons or be absent, antibody titers can increase with delay and many latent infectious agents exists [23]. Added to this, for several infectious agents, which continuously emerge newly, molecular diagnostic techniques still have to be adapted and improved for different species [24]. In a next step, the heath screening has to be widened to an appropriate number of individuals in a population to define the communities’ health state [25]. Finally, the potentially detected health impairments of either the translocated or the receiving group of animals should not harm each others health in terms of facultatively pathogenic infectious agents or non-infectious health issues that become clinically relevant in case of co-morbidities or other health deteriorations that are not clinically aberrant or relevant in captivity only and in the wild respectively (e.g., [26]).

Notably, for chelonian species the health and wellbeing of individuals involved in reintroduction initiatives, needs to be acknowledged to a greater extent than in other species due to their high life expectancy, slow pace of life and reproduction cycle and low effective population sizes inherent to endangered species [27].

This review synthesizes key health concerns and veterinary practices related to translocation-based conservation initiatives for the European pond turtle (Emys orbicularis, hereafter referred as Emys). Freshwater turtles face significant threats globally, primarily due to habitat loss and degradation of wetlands, their primary habitat [28, 29]. Emys (Box 1) has experienced one of the most severe declines among European reptiles, reaching extinction or near-extinction in several regions [30, 31]. In response, numerous ex situ conservation initiatives, including both reinforcements and reintroductions, have been established throughout Europe [32–34]. Both headstarting, where turtle eggs are collected in the wild, so contamination of the captive facilities by theses eggs, as well as contamination of eggs during collection in the wild, but also contamination of captive facilities by those eggs collected in the wild has to be prevented, and captive breeding involving breeder animals held under controlled conditions in captivity and artificial egg incubation are approved procedures (e.g., [35]). Few more steps in preparing and accomplishing headstarting initiatives differ from reintroduction via captive breeding, like the genetic considerations in selection of breeder animals in the wild, the impact of climate conditions on wild populations or urban-wildlife interactions [36].

While many of these initiatives include health checks, detailed descriptions of these protocols are often lacking, with most studies limited to biometric assessments [37–39].

Based on an extensive assessment of 418 scientific articles published until April 2025, this review aims to: 1) summarize the current literature on health assessments in translocation-based ex situ conservation initiatives focusing on Emys, 2) examine the methodologies and specificities of health checks at different life stages of captive-bred freshwater turtles at large and throughout the translocation process, and 3) provide recommendations for future research needed to standardize health assessments before and after translocations, with a specific focus on Emys. By compiling and evaluating best practices, this review seeks to offer critical insights for present and future freshwater turtle ex situ conservation initiatives.

Freshwater turtle veterinary issues by age class

Freshwater turtle reintroductions rely either on headstarting protocols where eggs are collected from the wild for being hatched in captivity for limiting natural mortality, or on captive breeding populations that usually consist in a relatively small pool of breeders. Since headstarting protocols and benefits have been extensively described, especially for the Blanding’s turtle [19, 35, 40], this review focuses on protocols based on captive breeding. The key concerns associated with multigenerational captivity include: i) reduced immunogenetic diversity due to potential inbreeding, ii) genetic and behavioural adaptations to captive environments, and iii) the absence of exposure to native or exotic parasites due to the controlled and isolated nature of captive habitats [41]. To address the long-term genetic and behavioural consequences of multigenerational captivity, several management strategies have been proposed. These include conducting comprehensive assessments of genetic diversity within the captive population to guide the selection of maximally diverse breeding groups [42], as well as promoting the exchange of genetically compatible individuals among institutions to reduce inbreeding and maintain adaptive potential [43]. Here, it is mandatory along the IUCN (International Union for Conservation of Nature) guidelines for species reintroductions [3] to guarantee breeding animals are of the same subspecies (for Emys there are currently 14 subspecies described in Europe) [32], as animals from the receiving population [44, 45]. They thus should be pure-bred and not genetically admixed. This allows best adaptability to local environmental conditions and consequently higher chances of persistence of the population in the wild based on historically adapted phenotypic characteristics [44], i.e., overall increased conservation value of the translocation [46]. Currently, genetic assignment of subspecies is determined using a mitochondrial sequence marker (cytochrome B) and, because mtDNA is only inherited from females, also using microsatellites which permits to exclude hybrids between subspecies or genetic lineages [45]. Within such a pure genetic lineage, preferably high levels of genetic diversity are desired, holding the opportunity for further adaptation to novel external conditions under global climate change (e.g., [47]). In addition, removing reproductively dominant males – whose disproportionate mating success can skew genetic contributions – has been suggested as a mean to equalize reproductive output. However, this latter approach requires detailed knowledge of individual reproductive success, which can be obtained through either genetic parentage analysis or systematic observations of mating behaviour [48–50]. In the following, we present the varying health issues of captive-bred Emys per age class.

Egg collection and incubation

Optimizing conditions for egg collection, incubation, and subsequent hatchling management is critical for ensuring the health and survival of individuals intended for reintroduction. While zootechnical aspects have been addressed in previous studies [51–54], this review focuses on key health-related considerations. First, when collecting eggs from nests, it is essential to preserve the maternal mucus coating on the eggshell, as it plays a vital role in supporting the external microbiota that develops during incubation. This has been demonstrated in freshwater turtles, sea turtles, and lizards [55–58]. Mechanical or chemical cleaning of eggs should therefore be strictly avoided. Second, artificial incubation requires precise control of temperature and humidity to prevent developmental abnormalities. Excessively high temperatures can accelerate embryonic development, leading to morphological defects (e.g., scutellation abnormalities, missing digits, vertebral deformities, anophthalmia), neurological impairments, and even sterility [59]. Elevated humidity levels can promote mold, fungal, and bacterial infections, which may result in embryonic mortality, immunodeficiencies, and congenital disorders [59–64]. Conversely, insufficient humidity can lead to limb deformities, missing toes, and reduced body size [59]. The effects of fluctuating incubation temperatures remain debated. Some studies suggested benefits for immune function in snapping turtles Chelydra serpentina [65], while others reported reduced hatching success in western saw-shelled turtles Myuchelys bellii [66]. Notably, freshwater turtles incubated near their optimal temperature range exhibit higher post-release survival rates compared to those incubated at intermediate temperatures [67]. Based on this, we recommend incubating Emys eggs in moistened vermiculite at 28–30 °C with a nocturnal temperature drop of 4–6 °C and maintaining 80–90% humidity [68, 69]. Finally, eggs may harbour pathogens (e.g., viruses, bacteria, and parasites such as astigmatic mites [Acaridae]) that remain undetected during incubation. Pathogens can infiltrate eggshells (e.g., Salmonella sp. [70]) or be maternally transmitted (Testudinid herpesvirus 3 [71]). For several other bacterial and viral pathogens this route of vertical transmission has also been proposed, however it is not yet proven (e.g., Nidovirus, Adenovirus, Turtle Fraser Virus 1 [TFV1], Bohle iridovirus, and blood parasites like Haemogregarina stepanowi [72–78], Mycoplasmopsis sp. [73, 79]). For the latter pathogen a vertical route of transmission has been described for mammals and birds, so that a potential analogy in reptiles and freshwater turtles becomes more likely [80–82]. To mitigate these risks, we recommend: i) daily inspections during incubation to promptly remove infertile, dead, or decaying eggs; ii) immediate removal of hatchlings; iii) systematic sterilization (e.g., autoclaving) or replacement of vermiculite between incubation cycles (see also [54]).

Health aspects of hatchlings, neonates, and juveniles

Hatching is a critical phase for newborn turtles, presenting challenges in both immunity and nutrition. Immunologically, hatchlings primarily benefit from maternal antibodies provided through the yolk sac [83, 84] and from the maternal microbiota deposited on the eggshell during oviposition, which plays a crucial role in immune defence [56, 85, 86]. Additionally, the incubation substrate influences immune development. Since the turtle immune system is largely adaptive and takes time to fully develop after exposure to specific pathogens [23], young freshwater turtles are particularly vulnerable to infectious diseases (e.g., Stromsland and Zimmerman, 2017 [87], for sea turtles see: Flint et al., 2010 [88]). Rana-, Adeno- and Herpesviruses, secondary bacterial septicaemia (Aeromonas spp. and Pseudomonas spp.), Mycoplasmosis, and opportunistic fungal (Saprolegnia spp., Aspergillus spp.), as well as protozoal (Hexamita spp., Eimeria spp.) and parasitological (Spirurida, Trematoda) infections are considered particularly dangerous for young freshwater turtles [89–93].

Hatching and early developmental issues

One common issue in neonate freshwater turtles is yolk sac retention. If not fully absorbed, it can lead to infections by opportunistic bacteria (e.g., Aeromonas sp., Citrobacter sp.) or fungi, potentially resulting in septicaemia and systemic infection [59, 94]. To prevent infections, hatchlings should be removed from the incubation substrate immediately after complete plastron closure. If the retained yolk appears abnormal in texture or colour, indicating infection, surgical intervention is recommended [59]. A rarer but problematic condition is the persistence of the chorioallantoic membrane (CAM), often associated with the absence of an egg tooth, which can delay hatching significantly [95]. The occurrence of persistent CAM in multiple eggs within a clutch may indicate maternal health problems or genetic factors, potentially influenced by environmental pollutants or hereditary defects [96–98].

Growth and metabolic disorders

Continuous growth during the first year of life is considered to be an indicator of good health and should be ensured. Growth is primarily influenced by temperature (through ectothermy), natural light (for vitamin D synthesis and bone mineralization), and diet [86, 99–101]. A common health issue in young freshwater turtles is nutritional secondary hyperparathyroidism, a metabolic bone disease (MBD) caused by calcium deficiency or inadequate UV-B exposure [102, 103]. Symptoms include paralysis, muscle tremors, shell deformations, and pathological fractures [104]. Hypocalcaemia can also lead to prolapses (e.g., cloacal or urogenital prolapse), while in adult females, it may result in egg-laying issues such as follicular stasis or dystocia [105]. Preventive measures include providing a near-natural diet with adequate calcium supplementation (e.g., cuttlebone) while maintaining an optimal calcium-to-phosphorus ratio [106]. Daily exposure to appropriate UV-B light (290–320 nm wavelength) is strongly advised [107]. UV-A light (320–400 nm wavelength) is also required for freshwater turtles kept indoor, as it contributes to the Previtamin D3 synthesis, regulates circadian rhythm and therefore reproduction along with positive effects on phenotype and behaviour [108–110]. Another potential cause of MBD is impaired calcium absorption due to intestinal disorders caused by high endoparasite loads, which increase calcium consumption [111]. Other dietary inhibitors of calcium absorption include phytates, oxalates, high-fat diets, and high dietary acid contents [112]. Diagnosis is based on anamnesis, clinical examination, and blood calcium levels (< 2.4 mg/dL or < 0.6 mmol/L; reference range: 1.3–2 mmol/L) [113, 114]. In addition to infusion therapy (hereafter fluids), vitamin, and mineral supplementation, husbandry conditions should be corrected, and parasitic infections treated accordingly.

In detail, the species-specific environmental temperature should be appropriately adjusted along seasonal and daily variations (generally preferred optimal temperature zone between 24 °C and 28 °C [115]; details for Emys temperature recommendations in [116]), as it influences (calcium) metabolism via vitamin D synthesis [117]. Basking in UV-B light (see also [69]) should be enabled for the conversion of pre-vitamin D3. UV-B lightning and sufficient humidity should be maintained to promote calcification during neonate development [117].

Parasitic load and hygiene

Young freshwater turtles are more susceptible to high endoparasite loads compared to adults, similar to other testudines [118–120]. Clinical signs of endoparasitic infections include inappetence, regurgitation, diarrhea, intussusception, weight loss, and cloacal prolapse [59]. Diagnosis should be confirmed via microscopic faecal analysis [121, 122] to ensure appropriate treatment (see Supplementary Table 1). To reduce parasitic risks, good hygiene and the avoidance of overcrowding are recommended [123, 124]. Implementing these measures helps optimize the health of hatchlings and juveniles, ensuring better survival rates and preparing them for eventual reintroduction into the wild.

Freshwater veterinary issues in all age classes

Endoparasites

A comprehensive overview of parasitic infections in European freshwater turtles is provided in Supplementary Table 1. A recent global review on infectious agents in freshwater turtles identified Platyhelminthes (n = 719), Trematoda (n = 440), Monogenea (n = 272), and Cestoda (n = 5) as the most common endoparasites [125]. Studies on free-living Mediterranean pond turtles Mauremys leprosa have documented helminths (Oxyuroidea and Ascarididae) and protozoa (Entamoebidae) [122], while Trematoda, such as Spirorchiidae (blood flukes, which can cause peracute death) and Polystomoidea (which attach to mucosal surfaces in various body cavities) have been recorded in Emys [126, 127]. Additionally, species like Trachemys venusta venusta in Mexico have been found to host Ascarididae, Ancylostomatidae, Physalopteridae, and Trichuridae [128], with potential spillover and host-switching events occurring in translocated individuals of native European species [129]. Haemoparasitic infections, particularly Haemogregarina stepanowi, are commonly reported in Emys but are often subclinical [130, 131]. In contrast, intranuclear coccidiosis in tortoises (TINC) remains rare and has not yet been documented in wild European turtle populations [132, 133].

Parasites can cause localized or systemic infections, affecting the digestive tract, lungs, heart, liver, bile ducts, gallbladder, connective tissue, muscles, kidneys, urinary bladder, or blood. Host susceptibility depends on several factors, including immune status, co-infections, and environmental stressors such as inadequate husbandry or overcrowding. For example, Spirorchis sp. infections may remain asymptomatic but can also be fatal [126]. Similarly, Myxidium sp. infections can lead to severe necrotizing nephritis, while nematodes such as Serpinema rispinosus and Siroxys contorta can cause granulomas in the small intestine [123, 134–139].

Ectoparasites

Ectoparasitic infestations in freshwater turtles are relatively rare, with leeches of the genus Placobdella being the most frequently observed, also in Mauremys leprosa [140]. High infestation levels, such as those caused by Placobdella costata, can lead to lethargy, circular skin lesions, anaemia, restlessness, and restricted limb and tail mobility in Emys [141]. Ticks, such as those of the genus Amblyomma, have been recorded in Colombian freshwater turtles of the genus Rhinoclemmys [142], though reports of pathological effects are scarce. However, these ectoparasites may serve as vectors for haemoparasites [143].

To effectively manage parasitic infections, proper species identification and post-treatment monitoring are essential. Diagnostic methods typically include light microscopy of fresh faecal samples or cloacal flushes using direct smear, flotation, and sedimentation techniques, both before and after treatment. All infected individuals within the same enclosure or water circulation system should be treated simultaneously, and husbandry conditions must be optimized to prevent reinfection. This includes thorough cleaning of enclosures and equipment, water and substrate replacement, and increased water changes following treatment to eliminate medication residues and parasitic debris [144]. Infected individuals should be quarantined and managed according to established protocols (see Sect. 6.1).

Viruses

Herpesvirus infections have been documented in various freshwater turtle species, including Chelonid Herpesvirus 2, 3, and 4, as well as Glyptemys herpesvirus 1 and 2 [145, 146], and Emydoidea herpesvirus 1 in Emydoidea Blandingii [147]. Emydid herpesvirus 1 is particularly relevant for Emys [148], with additional reports of uncharacterized herpesvirus strains (Emydid herpesvirus 2) in Pacific pond turtles Actinemys marmorata, painted turtles Chrysemys picta, and map turtles Graptemys pseudogeographica [138, 146, 149–152]. In many cases, chronic herpesvirus infections remain asymptomatic or present with stomatitis, rhinitis, blepharedema, subcutaneous oedema, lethargy, and anorexia. However, severe cases can result in lethal lesions in the liver, kidneys, and spleen, as seen in Graptemys sp. [78].

Similar to fibropapillomatosis in marine turtles, which is caused by Herpesviruses, Siadenovirus has been implicated in lymphoid leukaemia in Sulawesi turtles Indotestudo forsteni [153]. Additionally, a novel herpesvirus strain (Emydoidea herpesvirus 2) was detected in a wild Blanding’s turtle Emydoidea blandingii with concurrent squamous cell carcinoma [154] (see also Sect. 3.4 Neoplasia). Moreover, as Herpesviruses tend to cause more severe clinical diseases in aberrant hosts [155] an infection with Trachemys herpesvirus 1 in case of shared enclosures or water pipelines, as well as contact with infected invasive Trachemys turtles in wild environments represent potential sources of infection and clinical disease [156].

Adenovirus infections have been associated with cachexia, anorexia, lethargy, ulcerative stomatitis, nasal and ocular discharge, and diarrhea, with survival rates influenced by environmental temperature [157, 158] and have also recently been investigated in Emys (Marschang et al., 2025 published [159]). A novel Adenovirus strain, related to Sulawesi tortoise adenovirus 1, was identified in ornate box turtles Terrapene ornata ornata from Hungary [160]. Sulawesi tortoise adenovirus 1 infections have recently been reported in multiple turtle species (Blandings’ turtle, painted turtle, red-eared slider (Trachemys scripta elegans) [161].

Indeed, different Adenoviruses like Box turtle Adenovirus 1 have next to ornate box turtles (Glyptemys muhlenbergii) [162], been detected in Blanding’s turtles and red-eared-slider, so it is likely not a host-adapted adenovirus [158], which might point to the general potential of host-jump.

Ranaviruses (family Iridoviridae) generally induce severe systemic effects, including cutaneous and mucosal ulceration, skin abscesses, stomatitis, pharyngitis, glossitis, tracheitis, pneumonia, hepatitis, splenomegaly, conjunctivitis, esophagitis, enteritis, pancreatitis, vasculitis, and cystitis [132, 163–165]. Ranavirus rana 1, a Ranavirus subspecies, has been linked to cutaneous abscesses, oral ulceration, respiratory disorders, anorexia, and fatalities in captive Eastern box turtles Terrapene carolina carolina [166]. Furthermore, co-pathogens, such as Terrapene Herpesvirus 1, Adenovirus, Mycoplasmopsis sp., and polymicrobial necrotizing bacterial infections, were identified as contributors to morbidity and mortality events in wild T. c. carolina populations in Illinois [167]. For viral detection usually PCR from oral-cloacal swabs and or blood samples are favoured [168].

Turtle fraservirus 1 (Tosoviridae) has been detected in Florida softshell turtles (Apalone ferox), but also Florida red-bellied cooter (Pseudemys nelsoni) and Peninsular cooter (Pseudemys peninsularis) causing oral and or cloacal plaques, respiratory and neurological disorders, ocular and skin defects and anorexia with a low rate of mortality [76, 169].

Since specific antiviral treatments are unavailable or need further practical testing (Lysine and Lactoferrin [170]), management strategies focus on addressing co-infections, implementing strict quarantine protocols, and providing supportive care. Fluid therapy (like lactated Ringer’s solution with 5% dextrose, for details see e.g., Gibbons, 2009 [171]), assisted feeding (see [172] for feeding tube placement in turtles), prophylactic broad spectrum antibacterial treatment like Enrofloxacin 10 mg/kg sc./im./po. q48; [173]) to prevent secondary septicaemia and antiviral agents such as Valacyclovir (40 mg/kg every 24 h [174]), Famciclovir (e.g., 20 mg/kg po. q24h [173]), or Acyclovir (≥ 80 mg/kg q48h [175]) may offer some therapeutic benefit.

Many viruses are known to be stable for a long time period, even under extreme climate conditions like freezing and/or are supposed to be shed intermittently (e.g., Ready, 2025 [158]), Ranavirus, for example, is suspected to be spread by several pathways including insect vectors and is known to stay in the environment for weeks to months [176, 177]. Strict and appropriate disinfection regime of field items as well as enclosures and equipment used in a captive setting is required (2% chlorhexidine gluconate, soak items overnight in 5% sodium hypochlorite and UV light; [151]). Adenovirus disinfection regime includes the use of formaldehyde, chlorine, bleach, and quaternary ammonium compounds as well as intense UV light [178], Herpesvirus disinfection includes high heat (56 °C, 5—10 min, pH < 5), or sodium hypochlorite (3%) [151].

Bacteria

Mycoplasmopsis sp. is a well-documented pathogen responsible for Mycoplasmosis, which is often triggered by environmental stressors such as cold temperatures, aging, or immunosuppression, particularly in combination with co-pathogens [179]. Initial symptoms include conjunctivitis and rhinitis, which can progress to nasal obstruction, erosion, and depigmentation of the nares, leading to upper respiratory tract disease (URTD [180]; Three-toed box turtle Terrapene carolina triunguis). In the USA an Emydid mycoplasmopsis sp. has been found in wild bog, spotted, and eastern box turtles, representing another subspecies of Mycoplasmopsis, which was considered as a commensal bacterium in the given setting [181]. Mycoplasmosis infections are typically chronic, with mortality resulting from secondary infections and multimorbidity rather than the pathogen itself [167, 182].

Mycobacterial infections, most commonly caused by Mycobacterium marinum and M. chelonae, lead to chronic granulomatous disease affecting the lungs, coelomic organs, skin, bones, and joints [183]. Acute manifestations include haemorrhagic limb and pulmonary lesions, coelomic effusion, disseminated intravascular coagulation, and nasal bleeding, with lethargy as a common symptom ([184] for Eastern spiny softshell turtle Apalone spinifera spinifera; [185] for Eastern box turtle).

Salmonella sp., often considered an opportunistic pathogen, has been widely detected in both wild and captive aquatic turtles worldwide and is of significant zoonotic concern due to turtle-associated human salmonellosis (TAHS [186, 187]). With up to 85% of chelonians harbouring multiple serotypes [188], turtles can intermittently shed Salmonella without clinical symptoms. However, in some cases, infections result in shell and skin lesions, septicaemia, anorexia, pneumonia, and enteritis [183].

Dermatophilus chelonae is a common bacterial pathogen affecting turtle skin, leading to cutaneous lesions and abscesses. In severe cases, the infection can spread systemically, causing granulomas in the liver and coelomic cavity as well as septic arthritis [74, 183]. Macroscopically, lesions present as raised, yellow, hyperkeratotic nodules on the head and neck [189].

Although not yet confirmed causing clinical disease neither in wild nor captive freshwater turtles, reports of Leptospira sp. infestations from several freshwater turtle species including Emys in Europe indicate turtles as potential vectors for the zoonotic infectious agent, thus further investigation regarding the clinical relevance for freshwater turtles becomes apparent [190–193].

Routine testing for Mycoplasmopsis a sp. is recommended during pre- and post-release health screenings to prevent disease transmission. For Eastern box turtles in captivity Daleo et al., 2025 [194] recently provided guidelines for optimal pathogen detection via oral-cloacal swabs either monthly in case of shorter stays or in a three month rhythm respectively. This can be assumed for a recommended course of action for other freshwater turtles as well. Mycobacterial and Dermatophilus sp. infections should be investigated when skin lesions, or ocular and or nasal discharge, as well as systemic infections are present. Salmonella screening should be prioritized in systemic infections and zoonotic outbreaks but should not replace comprehensive bacteriological and fungal diagnostics in infected wounds or organ systems. As always, antibiotic treatment should be guided by resistance testing, with dosing and duration determined based on the latest literature (e.g., [175]).

Fungi

Mycoses can be classified into superficial dermatomycoses (SD) and visceral or systemic mycoses (SM) (like Phaeohyphomycosis [79, 183, 195, 196]). SCUD-like conditions are referred to as necrotizing scute disease (NSD [197]). Secondary fungal infections, often in conjunction with bacterial infections, may develop in areas where scutes fail to dry completely or have been compromised due to previous trauma [198, 199]. In the wild, septicaemic cutaneous ulcerative disease (SCUD) has been associated with environmental pollution or parasites as contributing factors [200–202], whereas in captivity stressors that weaken the local and systemic immune system and therefore promote disease progression are predominantly bad water quality, nutritional imbalance, superficial traumata and overcrowding (e.g., [203]). Fungi such as Mucor sp., Paecilomyces sp., Fusarium sp., Ochroconis sp., and Exophiala sp. have been associated with the disease complex [183, 204]. Also, bacterial pathogens, like Citrobacter freundii, Pseudomonas sp. (e.g. Pseudomonas aeruginosa) and Aeromonas sp. (e.g., Aeromonas hydrophila) (e.g., [203, 205]) are commonly involved in the disease complex. Besides, next to algae (Chamaesiphon sp., Phormidium sp., Oscillatoria sp.), ectoparasites such as leeches and even Herpesvirus associated dermatosis had been isolated [201]. In the United States of America, invasive lesions in freshwater turtles have been documented due to Nannizziopsiaceae (Emydomyces testavorans), resulting in coalescing areas of scute ulceration, keratin discoloration, deformation, and necrotizing osteomyelitis [206–208].

Common symptoms include irregular scute ecdysis, carapace and plastron pitting, and in severe cases, signs of septicaemia such as erythema, anorexia, lethargy, limb paralysis, and cutaneous abscesses [183, 209]. Additionally, systemic fungal infections caused by Nannizziopsis sp. have been reported in African side-neck turtles Pelomedusa subrufa in Illinois [210]. Systemic dissemination by fungi such as Nannizziopsiaceae with synergistic bacteria (e.g., Citrobacter freundii, Serratia sp., Beneckea sp.) can lead to necrosis in the liver, spleen, kidneys, coelomic cavity, bones, and heart [183].

Diagnostic testing involves physical examination, PCR or fungal culture from swabs from skin lesions, histopathological investigations from biopsies, as well as radiographs or even CT, as especially E. testavorans is known to cause deep defects and coelomic inclusion cysts [206, 208, 211].

In captivity, fungal overgrowth can be prevented by providing sufficient dry retreat areas. Additional preventive measures include maintaining high water quality and avoiding overcrowding and overfeeding [203, 212]. If fungal infections occur, husbandry conditions should be optimized, and vitamin A supplementation should be considered [175]. Local treatment options include antiseptic solutions such as ethacridine lactate (Rivanol 0.1%), propolis, medical honey, povidone-iodine (Braunol® 7.5%), or black tea baths [213]. Besides, infected wounds should be subjected to topical treatment with clotrimazole or silver sulfasalazine based ointments along resistance testing [214, 215]. More severe lesions may necessitate systemic antibiotics and antifungal agents [205, 216, 217]. In critical cases, surgical debridement, or the application of extracellular matrix powder for wound healing may be beneficial [218–220]. Against E. testavorans effective disinfection regime involves bleach 3% or 10%, Betadine, undiluted Lysol All-Purpose Cleaner, F10 SC, F10 SCXD, Roccal-D Plus, Rescue, chlorhexidine, as well as heating to 60 °C [221].

Algae

Algae of the families Methylomonaceae, Saprospiraceae and Nostocaceae are commonly found as external epibionts on the carapace and, to a lesser extent, on the skin of freshwater turtles [222]. Nevertheless, algal infestations can also promote shell disease [223, 224] or in the case of algal blooms induce severe internistic disorders [225]. Macroscopic skin lesions, including degenerative, inflammatory, or neoplastic diseases, have been observed in approximately 50% of individuals in a wild Emys population in Serbia [226]. Degenerative and inflammatory shell injuries were the most frequently detected, while the skeletal system was predominantly affected by mechanical injuries and osteopathies of various origins. About two-thirds of examined turtles exhibited abnormalities of the skeletal system and shell. In France, a study on diatoms found on the carapace of Emys suggested a neutral relationship, viewing algae as commensals and, to some extent, mutualistic organisms contributing to turtle camouflage [227]. Consequently, the application of a broad spectrum anti-parasitic should be carefully considered, as disrupting the co-evolutionary adaptation and resistance to parasites provided by algae may lead to long-term issues, potentially leaving future generations vulnerable to natural parasites in the wild.

In captivity, preventing overcrowding and overfeeding, maintaining regular water changes or water flow, and providing access to direct (or artificial) sunlight with sufficient drying time for the shell should help prevent excessive algal growth on external body surfaces.

Health impacts of non-infectious and environmental factors in captive-bred turtles

Wild turtles and captive-bred individuals released into natural habitats are subjected to markedly different non-infectious and environmental health challenges compared to those in controlled captive conditions. These include factors such as fluctuating environmental parameters, causing for example severe drought, variable food availability, and exposure to novel abiotic stressors like environmental toxic load, all of which can significantly influence physiological health and survival (e.g., [228–230]).

Kidney disorders

The most common non-infectious health impairments in both captive and wild freshwater turtles include nutritional and chronic kidney diseases, such as chronic kidney disease-metabolic bone disorder (CKD-MBD). Renal disorders, including reduced renal perfusion, dehydration, and excessively protein-rich diets, may lead to gout in freshwater turtles [231]. Other predisposing factors of the disease that occurs in a visceral or articular form are vitamin A deficiency, nephrotoxic substances (antibiotics, heavy metals), nephritis for example due to a hexamitiasis or too low rearing temperatures [232, 233]. While more frequently observed in tortoises, gout has also been reported in cold-stunned wild sea turtles [234]. Unlike the precipitation of urate salts in tissues, pseudogout is characterized by the deposition of other crystal types, such as calcium hydroxyapatite, typically within joints [235]. Underlying causes of nephrocalcinosis again comprise traumata, hypercalcemia due to hypervitaminosis D, primary or secondary hyperparathyroidism and osteolysis [232].

To mitigate these risks, breeding and husbandry practices should aim to replicate the natural and diverse diets of the species in accordance with current literature (e.g., [236]; Meyer et al., submitted article). Dietary supplementation with vitamins and minerals [237, 238] is recommended to prevent nutritional imbalances. Toxic aetiologies, such as medication overdose, misuse, or chemical contamination of water sources, should be avoided in captivity. While congenital or age-related degenerative processes may require symptomatic management, infectious causes of renal impairment may be treatable with pathogen-directed therapy, depending on the extent of tissue damage.

Metabolic disorders

In captivity, overfeeding, imbalanced diet (low fiber, high protein content), or reduced activity levels can contribute to obesity and hepatic lipidosis, particularly during reproductive periods or hibernation [204]. However, fatty liver syndrome particularly can also be the consequence of non nutrition-related predispositions like a follicular statis or dystocia, chronic infections, wrong hibernation temperature, dark rearing conditions, low essential amino acid supplementation, iron-rich diets, intoxications, or thyroid dysfunctions [239–243]. Conversely, anorexia can lead to fat mobilization, resulting in hepatic lipidosis. Chronic conditions such as hepatic inflammation, heart failure, biliary obstruction, or hepatic lipidosis may lead to hepatic fibrosis and hepatocellular nodular hyperplasia (cirrhosis). Protein malnutrition, hepatic trauma, and systemic disorders have been reported in captive populations of Emys [244]. Fish-eating freshwater turtles are susceptible to thiamine (vitamin B1) deficiency, which can result in neurological disorders such as torticollis, opisthotonus, muscle tremors, incoordination, blindness, and jaw gaping, potentially with fatal outcomes if fed frozen fish containing high levels of thiaminase [111, 245]. To prevent thiamine depletion, proper fish storage and rapid thawing methods are crucial. Fast thawing in 80 °C water for five minutes effectively denatures thiaminase, while thawing in a refrigerator prevents its activation. Supplementing thawed fish with 20 mg of thiamine per kilogram or small amounts of brewer’s yeast is recommended. Additionally, fish species with high thiaminase content, such as goldfish Carassius auratus and fathead minnow Pimephales promelas, should be avoided in turtle diets.

As previously mentioned, MBD is a common disorder in captive freshwater turtles, affecting both juveniles and adults [246]. In older turtles, renal secondary hyperparathyroidism represents an additional aetiology, wherein kidney dysfunction disrupts calcium-phosphorus homeostasis, leading to severe neurological symptoms such as seizures and collapse [103, 247]. Nutritional management for MBD includes a dietary calcium content of 2–3 mg/kcal, with approximately 1.4% calcium and 0.7% phosphorus on a dry matter basis. If exposure to unfiltered sunlight for 12–14 h per day is not feasible, full-spectrum lighting including UV-B radiation (285–320 nm) should be installed, with appropriate placement according to voltage output [116]. Initial treatment in severe cases involves fluid supplementation (Jarchow’s modification: two parts 2.5% dextrose in 0.45% saline and one part lactated Ringer’s solution, 278 mOsm/L, administered at 10–30 mL/kg q24h [248]. Supportive care includes temperature regulation, seizure control, and stabilization of bone fractures. Calcium gluconate (100 mg/kg) can be administered intravenously or subcutaneously every six hours until clinical improvement, followed by oral calcium gluconate (23 mg/kg q12h). Vitamin D3 supplementation (100 IU/kg, administered intramuscularly or subcutaneously once weekly for two weeks) may be required. If normo-calcaemia is not achieved after 3–7 days of oral calcium therapy, synthetic calcitonin (50 IU, administered intramuscularly twice weekly) can be introduced, alongside assisted feeding if necessary. Long-term management requires individual monitoring of blood calcium and vitamin D3 levels.

Aural abscesses are relatively common in freshwater turtles, often resulting from inadequate husbandry and poor sanitary conditions, particularly vitamin A deficiency. Secondary colonization by gram-negative aerobic bacteria occurs via the Eustachian tube [249]. Vitamin A deficiency leads to squamous metaplasia of the lacrimal and Harderian glands, cyst formation in the third eyelid, and persistent eyelid closure [235]. However, periocular swelling may also result from systemic disorders involving the liver, kidneys, or heart [250, 251]. Therefore, biochemical blood analyses are recommended for individuals presenting with generalized weakness. Therapeutically surgical removal of the abscess mass and capsula as well as applying of tests of antimicrobial resistance for further associated treatment are indicated. Thyroid disorders and goiter, though rare, have been reported in response to dietary iodine deficiencies, excessive iodine intake, or consumption of goitrogenic plants such as kale, broccoli, and cabbage [252].

Prolapse of urogenital organ structures, the rectum, or cloacal mucosa can occur due to various underlying causes, including calcium deficiency, imbalanced nutrition with low crude fibre content, parasitic overloads leading to tenesmus, dystocia, or the presence of foreign bodies [232]. It should be considered a differential diagnosis to cloacal neoplasia, which is relatively rare. Additional contributing factors include debilitation, neurological dysfunction, excessive libido, trauma, constipation, or cystic calculi [253]. Therapeutic management is contingent upon the specific diagnosis and underlying aetiology. In addition to initial stabilization of vital functions through fluid therapy, pain management, and wound care, treatment focuses on addressing the primary cause. This is followed by either repositioning the prolapsed structures or, if necessary and unavoidable, their surgical amputation [253].

Intoxications are observed from time to time in captive freshwater turtles, although such incidents occur less frequently in well-managed husbandry settings. Several factors contribute to the risk of intoxication. First, exposure to hazardous chemicals, including chlorinated water, cleaning agents, pesticides, insecticides, coumarin, metaldehyde, and nicotine, can induce severe clinical symptoms. Second, an inappropriate diet, particularly the ingestion of toxic plants such as avocado and rhubarb, as well as spoiled food, poses a significant risk. Third, heavy metal toxicity from lead, copper, or cadmium, along with medication overdoses (especially involving potentially nephrotoxic antibiotics such as gentamicin, amikacin, or metronidazole) can result in severe intoxication. Additionally, the administration of inappropriate medications, such as levamisole in tropical terrapins, may have detrimental effects. Lastly, environmental toxins, including ammonia or nitrate accumulation due to poor tank hygiene, represent a substantial health hazard. Common clinical signs of intoxication include lethargy, anorexia, impaired swimming ability, swelling of the extremities or eyes, skin lesions, seizures, and other neurological disorders [204, 233, 254, 255]. General treatment regimes include the removal of the turtle from water and cleaning of body surfaces with water. Possibly anaesthesia can be necessary if a gastric lavage or endoscopic removal of toxic material should be performed. Supportive care including fluid therapy, assisted feeding and oxygen therapy can help recovery as well as in more specialized cases forced diuretic medication, activated charcoal, or specific antidotes [204, 233, 254, 255].

Traumatic disorders

Various aetiologies can lead to traumatic injuries in captive freshwater turtles, including bite wounds from conspecifics (especially in cases of overcrowding), shell abrasions due to rough surfaces on basking spots, or sharp edges on hiding structures in the tank [256]. In the wild, bite wounds from predators, human interactions (collisions with boats, fishing gear, garden tools, unattended dogs), or rodent bites during hibernation are common causes of trauma and even mass mortality events (e.g., in Graptemys pseudogeographica and Sternotherus odoratus [257–259]). In critically ill patients, stabilization of blood circulation and pain management should be prioritized. In later stages, microbial pathogen identification may be performed, followed by wound debridement and closure, including standard wound management practices, administration of anti-inflammatory and analgesic medications, and anti-infective therapy based on resistance testing [260, 261]. In severe cases of shell necrosis with wide or deep defects of the carapace of plastron, tamponades soaked in iodine or ethacridine solution, as well as gelatine sponges coated with silver ions can be placed into the wound cavity and changed at the beginning of treatment daily to less frequent exchanges while more granulation tissue is building up. The use of negative pressure to enhance granulation via vacuum sponges can be installed, however technical requirements can exceed their use [248, 262, 263]. Additionally, defects can be treated with therapeutic laser light [264], small defects can temporarily be closed with coral glue or intra oral bandages from human medicine (ora-aid®). Proteolytic enzymes containing ointments as well as preparations including insulin [265] may support continuous wound care and debridement.

Promising treatment methods for Trachemys scripta elegans with deeper shell defects and detachment of scute segments besides include the use of two-component epoxy glue [260], photopolymerizable nano-hybrid dental composite [218], and advanced techniques such as photobiomodulation via low-level laser therapy [266].

Neoplasia

In addition to previously reported fibropapillomatosis (see Sect. 3.2. Viruses), other neoplastic disorders described in freshwater turtles include dermal fibroma, cutaneous mast cell tumour, squamous cell carcinoma, thyroid adenoma and carcinoma, parathyroid adenoma, pulmonary fibroma and fibroadenoma, gastric carcinoma, intestinal lymphosarcoma, gallbladder papilloma, testicular interstitial cell tumour, and renal adenocarcinoma. These conditions have been documented in species such as the Chinese box turtle Cuora flavomarginata [267], the common snapping turtle Chelydra serpentina [268], and the Matamata turtle Chelus fimbriatus [269].

In wild populations, incidences of neoplasia remain sporadic and are more frequently observed in older captive breeding animals. External abnormalities, such as mass lesions or appendages, should be surgically removed and subjected to histopathological examination. However in a case of substantial suspicion, animals can undergo diagnostic imaging like sonography or x-rays in the process of diagnosis confirmation. Captive animals diagnosed with neoplastic conditions should be isolated, especially in case of Herpesvirus-associated neoplastic lesions, like detected in a wild Blanding’s turtle [154], or in case of intense individual care needed, and/or provided with supportive therapy, if necessary (see for example [270]).

Cold stunning

Cold stunning may occur in turtles overwintering in outdoor enclosures from which they cannot escape to warmer areas [241]. It leads to deviations in blood cell counts and composition, potentially causing irreversible damage to the neurological and respiratory systems (e.g., negative buoyancy, ataxia [271]).

Treatment approaches are adapted from sea turtle medicine, although they often yield limited success. Affected individuals should be gradually warmed and subsequently provided with prophylactic antibiotic therapy, fluid therapy, and nutritional support. This includes correcting hyperkalaemia via calcium and sodium bicarbonate administration, ideally monitored through blood gas and electrolyte evaluations [272]. Mechanical ventilation may be required to compensate for respiratory acidosis, while metabolic acidosis can be managed with small doses of sodium bicarbonate, with necessary carbon dioxide monitoring [273].

Health checks in freshwater turtles

Health assessment methods should always strive to be as accurate and effective as possible while minimizing invasiveness and handling time for the animals [274, 275]. General information on methods and protocols for reptile examination and diagnostic testing can be found in current literature [196, 235, 276–278]. The following section summarizes the main visual, biochemical, haematological, and microbiological assessments in freshwater turtles.

Non-invasive visual examinations

Pre- and post-release health assessments of freshwater turtles regularly include a physical examination, preceded by an evaluation of available background information (e.g., individual history, former captive conditions, previous tests [279]). Visual examinations are prioritized non-invasive methods for regularly documenting the general health status (body mass or condition, physical abnormalities), posture, and behaviour [279–281].

As a first step the investigation consist in observing the individual animal behaviour [282]. For instance, normal behaviour comprises hiding/predator avoidance, exploratory behaviour, feeding and basking, yet with potential variations related to individual personality, which in turn can have consequences for wild animals and/or on survival after translocation [283–285]. Abnormal behaviours have been reported in captivity, including stereotype behaviours e.g., repeated swimming along tank boundaries [286, 287]. Due to the cryptic behaviour of freshwater turtles, recent technologies such as animal-borne behaviour data-recorders can be used in the wild [288]. Especially in the captive environment stereotype behaviours as repeated swimming along tank boundaries are documented in freshwater turtles as abnormal behaviour [282, 286]. In a second step, in a physical examination normal locomotion (including buoyancy changes), other body functions (breathing changes, any abnormal sounds, defecation), normal evasive behaviour including vision and hearing, and absence of neurological disorders (like tremor and opisthotonos) can be documented. However, as all other reptiles, freshwater turtles do not follow rules of routine neurological check ups like performed in mammal patients (e.g., Xiao et al., 2025 [289]), so interpretation of absence of certain reflexes is not necessarily pathologic [271]. All these changes can indicate husbandry and nutritional problems as well as clinical diseases, indicating the need for more detailed investigations, convoking for more detailed investigations, but should also be considered in the context of seasonal and reproductive stage to avoid misinterpretation of generally abnormal behaviour as loss of appetite or courtship behaviour [290, 291].

External examinations should focus on the carapace and plastron, as well as the skin (condition and integrity), the musculoskeletal system (trauma, signs of infection such as parasites, ulcers/plaques, haemorrhaging, or swelling), and the limbs and tail. Additional assessments should include an inspection of the oral cavity (mucosal coloration, plaques, foreign bodies, haemorrhaging), the eyes (discharge, hydration/nutritional condition, parasites, trauma), respiratory and cardiovascular parameters (nasal discharge, signs of unusual respiratory effort), and the genitourinary system.

Furthermore, palpation of the coelomic cavity through the hind limb openings can be performed to estimate overall fat reserves as an indicator of body condition and, in females, to check for eggs as a sign of active reproduction [292]. Pignatelli et al., 2023 [293] emphasized the importance of documenting carapace damage in freshwater turtle population monitoring, as it provides vital information regarding conservation concerns such as predation pressure, population viability trends, intraspecific interactions, stress caused by traffic and pollution, and pathogen exposure. This documentation can also help identify individual- or population-specific issues.

Faecal examination

Sample-based methods are often more precise but may also be invasive. Faecal analyses are a well-established, low-invasive approach for diagnosing endoparasites [148, 294] and for conducting microbial screenings (e.g., pathogen detection or microbiome composition analysis; [222, 295, 296]). However, especially for examinations of individual animal, the turtle has to be removed from water and placed into a lattice box, which constitutes a considerable amount of stress.

Tissue-based analyses

Samples obtained from the respiratory, intestinal, or reproductive tracts, as well as from the skin, enable microbiological testing for infectious agents or assessment of bacterial and fungal flora, including antibiograms for resistance profiling. This is particularly crucial in cases of infected wounds and serves as a diagnostic tool for guiding medical treatment of diseased individuals [297]. Explicit sampling of either the intestinal or reproductive tract via the cloaca is only feasible ante mortem through endoscopic techniques; thus, swab samples typically contain microbial communities from both body cavities.

Tracheal and/or pulmonary lavage in turtles follows a protocol detailed in [175]. Under anaesthesia, the animal is intubated, and a radiopaque catheter is inserted into the affected lung lobe. The lung is then infused with sterile saline solution at a dose of 5–10 ml/kg. The aspirated fluid is subsequently analysed for the presence of infectious agents.

When testing for infectious diseases, it is important to consider that disease susceptibility is influenced by host-related factors such as immunocompetence, pathogen-related virulence factors, and environmental factors including habitat connectivity and population structure, which may affect pathogen transmission dynamics [298].

Blood analyses

Blood samples provide valuable insights into general health status by enabling assessments of blood cell counts (e.g., via blood smear analysis [299]), genetic health impairment [300], biochemical and nutrient/vitamin levels, endocrine parameters, toxic or pollutant loads, and infection status [167, 204]). Samplings comprise blood smears for leucograms and detection of blood parasites, inclusion bodies or blood morphological abnormalities, haematology, blood biochemistry and blood cultures of bacterial pathogens in septicaemic patients (e.g., [22, 301]). However, blood biochemical and haematological parameters exhibit substantial variation depending on population, sex, age, reproductive state, stress, food availability, venipuncture site, season, and environmental factors [20, 302, 303]. Therefore, reference values should be derived from individuals of the same population and sampled at the same time of year. For Emys, reference biochemical and haematological values for both captive and wild individuals are available in [148, 300, 304] (compare also Supplementary Table 2). Generally, the collection site of choice is the dorsal coccygeal vein, but in smaller individuals or those with an inaccessible tail the subcarapacial venous sinus can be selected, however the risk of uncontrolled bleeding or damage of neck and cervical vertebrae have to be considered (e.g., [305]). Additionally, the jugular vein puncture represents a well accepted blood collection site, without lymphatic and/or cerebrospinal fluid dilution [306]. A safe blood collection and regular parameters is possible in animals weighting over 80 g [241] and serial sampling of one population however, should follow an uniform setting with allowance of the same blood collection site, equipment and consideration of intrinsic and extrinsic factors, as mentioned above [306].

Although not yet widely established for all freshwater turtle species, studies on acute-phase proteins, such as erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR) and haemoglobin-binding protein (HBP), in Box turtles Terrapene sp. have shown promising results as indicators of non-specific illness and inflammation in wild populations [307]. In Blanding’s turtles inflammation has been assessed via ESR using both microhematocrit tubes and commercial kits [308]. While the two methods yielded different reference intervals, they exhibited similar response patterns to ecological impacts. Additionally, specific blood parameters—lactate, packed cell volume (PCV), total solids, glucose, in conjunction with vital parameters such as temperature and heart rate—has been identified as reliable prognostic indicators of mortality following traumatic injuries in five species of North American freshwater turtles [309]. The study found significant correlations between hyperlactatemia, elevated lactate levels over 24 h (initial concentration > 5 mM/L), anaemia, and hypoglycaemia in males with increased mortality risk. Regarding blood lactate concentration, a point-of-care-testing has been recommended [310] to avoid inaccurate measurements. Furthermore, stress assessments in freshwater turtles can be conducted by measuring eosinophil concentrations, heterophil-to-lymphocyte (H:L) ratios, and plasma glucose levels [311–313].

Medical imaging

Medical imaging represents an advanced set of diagnostic techniques for the targeted investigation of specific health issues in freshwater turtles, including radiography, ultrasonography, endoscopy and computed tomography [148, 271, 272, 314]. Radiographic imaging is commonly employed to assess conditions affecting the digestive, respiratory, and reproductive tracts, such as the presence of foreign bodies or constipation, as well as to evaluate musculoskeletal integrity, particularly in cases of fractures [315, 316]. For the assessment of fractures or other traumatic injuries, craniodorsal-caudoventral oblique and caudodorsal-cranioventral oblique radiographic views taken at a 45° angle to the frontal plane can reveal fractures in the limbs that may otherwise be obscured by the shell [317, 318]. Ultrasonography is particularly valuable for diagnosing preovulatory egg binding as well as congenital and acquired heart diseases [319]. Additionally, it is useful for planning targeted biopsy sampling [320]. Computed tomography has become an essential diagnostic tool in modern turtle medicine, allowing for the measurement of internal body fat as an index of individual condition [321] and for evaluating the severity and extent of traumatic injuries. It also aids in pre-surgical planning, such as in cases of shell fractures in Trachemys scripta elegans [322]. Additionally, coelioscopy has been highlighted as a valuable diagnostic tool for assessing internal organs for biopsy sampling in freshwater turtles [320]. Cystoscopy, involving endoscopic examination via the urinary bladder, serves as a minimally invasive method for gender identification including assessment of reproductive state, visceral organ evaluation, and parasitic infestation assessment in turtles, including smaller post-hatchlings (e.g., 7-month-old Chinese three-banded box turtle Cuora trifasciata) [323–326]. Furthermore, endoscope-assisted minimal invasive surgery can be applied to neuter selected animals for example in captive breeding programs [327]. Lastly, digital image processing can support (regular) health monitoring of wild and captive specimen allowing for documentation of individual clinical history of shell lesions and growth performance in less time and therefore stress of handling for the animal than conventional records [328].

Necropsy

Post-mortem examinations serve as a critical tool for determining unknown causes of death, preventing the spread of infectious diseases, and identifying potential errors in captive management practices [329, 330]. Technical guidelines and protocols for conducting necropsies in freshwater turtles can be found in [331].

Advanced health surveys

Hormone-associated diagnostics

Serum androstenedione levels can serve as an indicator of reproductive fitness in male turtles, providing insights into both the quality of captive care and the individual’s overall health status [332]. Similarly, it has been demonstrated, that correlations between threshold concentrations of oestradiol, testosterone, and progesterone, along with various blood chemistry parameters, in female Chelonia mydas, enable a predictive monitoring to enhance captive breeding success [333]. A non-lethal method for assessing hatchling fitness through yolk sample analysis has been developed [334], particularly for measuring steroid hormone concentrations in Trachemys scripta sp. While approximately 70% of hatchlings survived this procedure, the range of parameters that can be analysed remains limited, suggesting that this method may be most applicable for scientific research rather than routine health assessments. In addition to non-invasive hormone assays for fitness evaluations, stress assessment could offer a valuable approach for post-release health monitoring. However, the application of chronic stress evaluation via non-invasive corticosterone sampling in freshwater turtles remains insufficiently explored. Other authors [335, 336] investigated corticosterone accumulation in turtle claws, providing potential insights into habitat suitability, the presence of persistent stressors, and long-term reintroduction success. Studies on corticosterone metabolite analysis in faecal samples of lizards (Smaug giganteus [337]) and sea turtles (Chelonia mydas [338]) have shown promising results, though further research is needed to refine interpretations in ecological contexts. Conducting a non-invasive corticosterone assay using claw tips, researchers assessed chronic stress levels in midland painted turtles Chrysemys picta marginata living in proximity to roads [339]. The potential significance of the glucocorticoid-to-DHEA(S) (dehydroepiandrosterone and its sulfate ester) ratio has been emphasized as a more comprehensive measure of animal welfare and stress [340]. However, to date, standardized protocols for applying this approach to freshwater turtles remain unavailable.

Advanced infectious agent diagnostics

Whole genome sequencing (WGS) of infectious pathogens, particularly viruses, presents significant potential for elucidating epidemiological characteristics in pathogen screening, including emerging infectious diseases (EIDs) in animals [341]. Combining long-read sequencing technologies with target enrichment is recommended for optimal pathogen characterization. Sample types include swabs, faecal samples [342], and organ tissues [343] on which environmental DNA (eDNA) based methods can be applied [344, 345]. Next-generation sequencing (NGS) is already applied in amphibian pathogen screening, such as in monitoring the chytrid fungi Batrachochytrium dendrobatidis (Bd) and Batrachochytrium salamandrivorans (Bsal) [346]. Similarly, metagenomic or metabarcoding approaches could be employed for pathogen detection in bulk samples of released turtles or through scanning eDNA for potential pathogen presence or population monitoring [347–351].

The spectrum of screened pathogens can be expanded to include vector-borne diseases detected in blood samples [352]. Studies have identified Rickettsia and Ehrlichia sp. in ticks from wild invasive Yellow-margined box turtles in Okinawa, though the clinical consequences remain unclear [353]. Other reports describe Rickettsia sp. in Amblyomma dissimile ticks from Colombia [354], Rickettsia helvetica in Amblyomma sabanerae from freshwater turtles in Mexico [355], and the transmission of Eastern equine encephalitis virus (EEEV) by Culiseta melanura mosquitoes in the USA [356].

Deep sequencing of infectious agents' subspecies may be warranted when disease signs do not align with known pathogens, allowing for the identification of novel or emerging contagious diseases, such as new Herpesvirus and Adenovirus strains [145]. Similarly, virulence assessments of bacterial strains, such as Chryseobacterium indologenes [357], provide valuable epidemiological insights. A recently identified potential EID, systemic Caryospora-like coccidiosis, was found post-mortem in Trachemys scripta elegans, with related genotypes detected in sea turtles; further research is required to determine its pathogenicity [358]. Strain-level investigations have also been conducted in farmed turtles has been examined host–pathogen interactions and gut microbiome shifts in Pelodiscus sinensis infected with Morganella morganii in aquaculture [359].

Advanced diagnostic testing

Advanced diagnostic methods infrequently used in wildlife species include immune gene and biomarker analysis (e.g., bactericidal capacity and natural antibody agglutination) [299], and pathohistological examinations for emerging diseases, unclear causes of death, and pathological lesions from biopsy samples [329]. An emerging diagnostic approach, though currently experimental in wild fish species, involves the development of an environmental microbiota health index (microHI) based on external (water) and internal (gut) microbial communities. This index has been proposed to identify key bacterial taxa indicative of microbiome health disruptions in fish [360]. Lastly and possibly less relevant for wild living individuals, except for monitoring vital parameters during general anaesthesia the technical performance of electrocardiogram has been described [361].

Euthanasia of freshwater turtles

Following a medical examination, certain freshwater turtles may be deemed unsuitable for release due to severe injuries or infections that are untreatable and not yet established in wild populations. Immediate euthanasia is justified in cases where animals are near death, beyond medical help, and suffering [362]. Additionally, severe injuries such as open fractures or extensive wounds involving multiple body parts, particularly those affecting sensory organs, warrant critical evaluation. Prolapsed or ruptured tissues exposing deeper structures, especially chronic cases, should also be assessed carefully.

Some systemic infectious diseases, such as severe Mycobacteria infections, pose a zoonotic risk and are often untreatable; in such cases, euthanasia is advisable [235]. Severe myiasis with deep tissue invasion is frequently untreatable due to the rapid spread of toxins and tissue damage [363]. Similarly, severely injured or moribund hatchlings, as well as individuals with severe congenital deformities, require assessment to determine whether treatment and recovery are feasible. Permanent disabilities that impair movement, reproduction, or the ability to evade predators may also justify euthanasia if alternative long-term care solutions, such as adoption by rescue centres, zoos, or responsible private caretakers, are unavailable. However, if euthanasia is considered solely due to the inability to return the turtle to the wild, alternative options such as captivity for breeding or educational purposes should also be explored.

For severely injured or suffering turtles identified during routine monitoring or medical interventions, recommended euthanasia protocols include deep anaesthesia—such as an intramuscular injection of 120 mg/kg ketamine hydrochloride and 2 mg/kg diazepam [364]—followed by an intravenous or intracoelomial injection of pentobarbital [365]. Alfaxalone (20 mg/kg intramuscular) is another option for general anaesthesia, though its analgesic efficacy in major surgical interventions remains debated [366].

Euthanasia should always be performed by a veterinarian when possible and, at a minimum, by trained personnel in accordance with the Association Guidelines for the Euthanasia of Animals [367] or relevant national regulations [368]. Additionally, post-mortem examinations by specialized veterinarians can provide valuable insights into disease monitoring, benefiting the conservation of remaining wild populations [329, 369].

Health issues in the context of translocations

Pre-translocation quarantine

Health assessment is crucial for optimizing captive conditions and ensuring successful translocation and reintroduction into the wild [370, 371]. Quarantine is essential for thorough health evaluation and for preventing the introduction of pathogens at the release site, protecting both resident and translocated populations. However, translocation-induced stress can trigger clinical manifestation of subclinical diseases [372, 373], such as infections with Adeno- and Herpesviruses or Mycoplasmosis [374–376]. It may also facilitate co-infections, leading to disease emergence in previously exposed but asymptomatic individuals [377, 378]. Pre-release health checks have been emphasized as a fundamental component of rehabilitation and rewilding, particularly since turtles frequently carry subclinical infections that may threaten naïve wild populations [379].

Quarantine primarily serves to assess the health status of translocated individuals. The duration should exceed the incubation period of monitored diseases and depend on the time required for diagnostic test results [74]. For reptiles, quarantine recommendations vary from 90 days [13] to 9–12 months [380], allowing sufficient time for repeated pathogen testing. Other authors suggests a minimum one-year quarantine period for turtles, including two Herpesvirus antibody tests at a minimum six-week interval [74, 232]. However, guidelines for many pathogens remain incomplete, underscoring the need for expanded disease screening in wild populations.

Reptiles rely significantly on innate immune responses, mediated by complement proteins and antimicrobial peptides [83]. Their adaptive immune system exhibits a prolonged response compared to mammals, with peak antibody production occurring 6–8 weeks post-antigen exposure and persisting for up to 34 weeks [381]. Given that secondary antigen exposure does not always enhance antibody titers or affinity, repeated testing is necessary [381].

Preventing pathogen transmission from translocated individuals to resident populations is the highest priority during reintroduction [382, 383]. For Emys, a recommended protocol includes faecal sample analysis on three consecutive days at both the beginning and end of quarantine to detect bacterial, and parasitic pathogens as well as oral-cloacal swabs for viral and bacterial pathogen screening such as Herpesvirus and Ranavirus, and Mycoplasmopsis sp. [69, 384]. Haemoparasites can be identified via blood smears [54, 372]. Others recommend screening for protozoans and helminths, either through collective faecal samples for larger groups or individual samples for smaller groups [232]. Specific parasite families of interest include Oxyuridae [385], Ascarididae [386], Camallanidae [387, 388], Gnathostomatidae [389], and Kathlaniidae [390]. Additionally, trematodes have been reported in Mauremys leprosa [126, 391]. Although cestodes and pentastomids are rarely found in freshwater turtles [128, 392], their prevalence may increase with the introduction of exotic species [127, 294, 388]. Necropsy of any individual that dies during quarantine is crucial for determining the cause of death and preventing the spread of subclinical infections [74].

Overcrowding also represents an issue, next to elevated stress levels leading to immune deficiency, also facilitating disease transmission due to increased contact rates (e.g., Rangel-Mendoza et al., 2014 [200]). Hence, recommended minimum tank sizes for adult single animals according to the Federal Ministry of Agriculture, Food and Regional Identity in Germany (BMLEH) should be at least five times the carapace length in length and 2.5 times in width for up to two turtles, with an additional 10% surface area for the 3rd–4th animal and 20% from the 5th onward, and a water depth of at least twice the carapace width (https://www.bmleh.de/SharedDocs/Downloads/DE/_Tiere/Tierschutz/Gutachten-Leitlinien/HaltungReptilien.pdf?__blob=publicationFile&v=2). In their first year, animals can be housed in 50L tanks filled with 5 cm water at 25 °C, with plants and cork for hiding and basking [54]. One adult animal can be kept at 300-400L water, with plus 150-200L per additional animal, whereas juveniles could be kept at ten animals per square meter water surface if filtering, water quality, hiding and basking space and enrichment is available to an appropriate extent [255, 393].

Minimal handling of quarantined animals is recommended to reduce stress, disease transmission risks and encourage natural behaviours before reintroduction [74]. Strict hygiene protocols must be followed at all handling stages, including the use of disposable gloves, surface disinfection (e.g., l-propanol, ethanol, glyoxal), and heat treatment of equipment at 200 °C for 15 min [394]. Enclosures should be minimally furnished to facilitate observation and capture when necessary [372]. Equipment should be designated for specific groups and disinfected after each use [74]. Additionally, turtles should be housed separately during hibernation and for 4–6 weeks post-hibernation to mitigate infection risk, as immune suppression at lower temperatures increases vulnerability to outbreaks, particularly Herpesvirus [74]. However, brumation is not recommended for previously sick or injured individuals, those that underwent chemical treatment during the last six weeks and animals involved in reintroduction programs, where turtles should be released in early ages, so that a continuous growth over winter is ensured [54, 235] or an optimal body condition index during hibernation of Emys see Carina et al., 2021 [280].

Veterinary management in soft versus hard release scenarios

Soft release consists in placing captive individuals in a temporary, natural-like place on the translocation site before they are actually released, or let dispersing by themselves, into the wild. In contrast, hard release consists in translocating captive animals directly into the wild. For soft-release, extended holding periods facilitate repeated captures, health examinations (followed by targeted treatments if needed), and implementation of preventive measures, as animals remain readily available for clinical assessment [395]. This approach also allows for gradual adaptation to environmental conditions, potentially improving growth rates, behavioural acclimatization, and predator avoidance skills before complete release [396]. Furthermore, by retaining individuals near the intended release site, soft release can increase the likelihood of settlement within suitable habitat containing adequate resources, reducing the risk of immediate and/or widespread dispersal. In addition, the nesting or breeding area can be maintained under closer observation, enabling more effective long‐term health and population monitoring. However, prolonged captivity may carry disadvantages, such as increased risk of pathogen transmission in the case of overcrowding or high animal exchange, unbalanced nutrition, stress‐related behavioural disorders and modifications that could impair skills survival in the wild [397].

Post-translocation monitoring

A key challenge in translocation efforts is ensuring adequate post-release monitoring while minimizing disturbance to the individuals. In some cases, a deliberate decision to limit direct interaction with the translocated population is made to reduce human impact and the risk of pathogen introduction [398]. However, limited resources may also constrain the implementation of comprehensive long-term monitoring (https://cinea.ec.europa.eu/programmes/life/nature-and-biodiversity_en). The IUCN recommends continuous health and mortality monitoring in reintroductions and conservation translocations to detect potentially high disease prevalence or welfare impairments that could jeopardize reintroduction success or negatively affect neighbouring populations [3, 287].

Population monitoring typically relies on standard capture-mark-recapture protocols [399]. Implanting PIT tags (Passive Integrated Transponder) subcutaneously between foreleg and neck or subcutaneously into the hind limb for a permanent marking and cataloguing of individuals in conservation programs is mostly favoured, while shell notches are more invasive and can become unreadable after severe shell necrosis or traumatic injuries [400]. Handling of translocated individuals for identification and biometric assessments provides an opportunity for health evaluation. To minimize stress and handling time, such procedures should be conducted by experienced personnel following established veterinary guidelines [401]. Fieldworkers are encouraged to refer to resources such as the World Organisation for Animal Health (WOAH) Training Manual on Wildlife Diseases and Surveillance, the European Wildlife Disease Association (EWDA) Diagnosis Cards, and research projects like WildTech (http://www.wildtechproject.com) and APHAEA (Harmonized Approaches in monitoring wildlife Population Health, And Ecology and Abundance) – a project to monitor species abundance and pathogen distribution within a European network (http://www.aphaea.eu). The importance of educating future scientists in wildlife health monitoring has also been emphasized [401].

For freshwater turtle reintroductions, health monitoring recommendations have been developed [204, 402]. However, gaps remain in best practices, particularly concerning (i) standardized quarantine protocols for European turtle species, (ii) diagnostic procedures and methodologies, and (iii) frequency and timing of pathogen testing. Limited research exists on the prevalence of relevant pathogens throughout different life stages [87, 403]. Additionally, financial constraints have restricted regular screenings for less common, but potentially emerging infectious agents [404].

Invasive turtle species pose significant threats to translocated populations through direct biotic interactions, such as predation and competition for resources, as well as indirect ecological impacts and disease transmission [294, 405]. Invasive species often exhibit higher reproductive efficiency and aggressive behaviour, disrupting food webs and predator–prey dynamics [406, 407]. Hatchlings and adult turtles are particularly vulnerable to predation by invasive fish [408] and mammals, including raccoons (Procyon lotor) and raccoon dogs (Nyctereutes procyonoides) which pose increasing challenges for reintroduction programs [409]. Veterinarians play a crucial role in advising on eradication management while ensuring ethical and welfare considerations are met [410].

Most importantly, the protocols and field methods as well as practical procedures before release have to be transferred, made coherent and applicable to all stakeholders involved in the knowledge transfer of the reintroduction/translocation effort, to ensure comparability and continuity of successful reintroduction management methods [3, 411]. The synergistic roles of wildlife veterinarians lies in coordinating and educating the collaborative work of all veterinarians, scientists, field workers, zoo keepers, or managers involved in the reintroduction process, so that on the long run protocols are executed correctly along animal welfare claims, but also adjusted along new scientific findings (e.g., Sutton 2010 [412]).

Proposed guidelines for pre- and post-translocation health surveys of Europe-native freshwater turtles Emys orbicularis and Mauremys leprosa

Based on our review, a standardized pre-release health assessment—independent of specific diseases or site-related factors—should include a comprehensive physical examination, biometric measurements, and molecular diagnostics (see also Fig. 1). At a minimum, PCR-based testing should target Ranavirus, Herpesvirus, Adenovirus, Mycoplasmopsis sp., and Spirorchis sp. using combined oral and cloacal swabs, performed twice: once at the start of quarantine and again at least six weeks later, shortly before release. Additionally, bacteriological and microscopic faecal analyses are recommended to identify potential bacterial pathogens and parasitic infections. In cases of skin or shell ulcerations, swab or tissue samples should be submitted for microbiological examination, primarily for bacterial and fungal pathogens. Blood sampling should be considered in response to unexplained morbidity or mortality events, including toxicological, biochemical, and hematological analyses. Post-mortem examinations should be conducted in cases of unexpected deaths to determine underlying causes and potential epidemiological implications.

Alongside clinical symptomatology, it is crucial to assess the significance and incidence of co-infections that may exacerbate disease progression. Ideally, long-term epidemiological monitoring should include representative population screenings over several decades (e.g., [224, 413, 414]). Unlike emergency response protocols in human medicine [415], universal guidelines for systematic EID surveillance in turtles—such as standardized screening methods or optimal testing frequencies—are currently lacking. While specific recommendations remain unavailable, passive surveillance via molecular screening (e.g., PCR-based assays) could facilitate the detection of novel strains or subspecies of known pathogens. At a minimum, such screening should be implemented in cases of cumulative mortality (e.g., [416, 417]).

Conclusion

In this review, we exhaustively synthesized scientific articles and proposed guidelines for pre-and post-release health assessments of Emys orbicularis as part of translocation-based conservation initiatives. Wildlife veterinarians are catalysts in the early phase of reintroduction or reinforcement, aiming at creating self-sustaining systems that run independently from any controls or interventions after translocation. Such conservation actions are implemented in a wide diversity of ecological, sociological and economical contexts, involving actors from private individuals to institutions, that disseminate their protocols and outcomes in their distinctive ways. This highlights the urge for better, systematic dissemination of conservation initiatives, and a strengthened bridge between herpetology and herpetoculture for ensuring best practices in future initiatives [418]. Reintroductions would specifically benefit from operational transdisciplinary collaborations between veterinarians and involved stakeholders, including local populations, field scientists and land managers for broader engagement for nature conservation, best integrated monitoring in the wild and appropriate operational decision making [54]. Such goal can be achieved through participatory action-oriented research projects (e.g. [34]), that consolidates existing network of stakeholders for best sharing of knowledge and expertise on wildlife monitoring and management and for long term sustainable, socially supported, effective conservation initiatives.
