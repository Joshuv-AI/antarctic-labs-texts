Determinants of agency in family planning among marginalized and non-marginalized women: an analysis of the Nepal demographic and health survey 2022
Authors: Rita Devi Karki, Govind Subedi, Bidhya Shrestha, Kamala Devi Lamichhane, Tek Mani Karki
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundWomen’s agency in family planning, as reflected in informed decision-making, negotiation, and cognitive control over their reproductive choices, is vital for sexual and reproductive health and rights. This study examined the prevalence of these dimensions of agency and their socio-demographic, economic, and relational determinants across marginalized and non-marginalized groups, an area that has received limited scholarly attention.MethodsThis study utilized nationally representative data from the 2022 Nepal Demographic and Health Survey. The analysis included 4,769 currently married women aged 15–49 years (3,357 marginalized and 1,412 non-marginalized) who were using modern contraception. Women’s agency in family planning, assessed through decision-making on contraceptive use, condom negotiation, and cognitive autonomy, served as the outcome variable, whereas socio-demographic, economic, and household decision-making factors were considered exposure variables. Associations were examined using bivariate and multivariable logistic regression.ResultsDisparities existed between marginalized (66%) and non-marginalized (79%) women, with a lower proportion among the former. Higher education was strongly linked to increased agency in family planning in both marginalized [AOR = 19.40, (95% CI: 2.61–144.33)] and non-marginalized [AOR: 4.44, (95% CI: 2.05–9.62)] groups. The other determining variables, such as age, caste/ethnicity, wealth index, and mobile/telephone ownership, were significant determinants for the marginalized group, while occupation and pressure from husband or family members were specific determinants for the non-marginalized group.ConclusionAgency in family planning appeared more limited among marginalized women compared to non-marginalized women. Education, especially higher education, stands out as the strongest predictor of agency in family planning in both groups. Still, age, caste/ethnicity, wealth, and technology access are crucial for marginalized women, while occupation and familial pressure are significant for non-marginalized women.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12905-025-04135-x.Keywords: Women’s agency, Family planning, Marginalization, Education, Nepal

Background

The focus on reducing fertility has given family planning programs a supply orientation to contraceptive access rather than promoting women’s agency to exercise their reproductive choice to access and use family planning [1, 2]. In Nepal, family planning has long been a cornerstone of public health policy with widespread service availability across public and private facilities [3]. Despite policy commitments and strategic plans such as the National Family Planning Costed Implementation Plan (NFPCIP) (2015–2020) [4] and Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs), aligned targets to increase modern contraceptive use to 60% by 2030 [5], progress has plateaued with the contraceptive prevalence rate among currently married women remaining stagnant at 43% from 2011 to 2022 [6]. In response, efforts have continued to focus on improving supply-side mechanisms. For instance, in 2022, the United Nations Population Fund (UNFPA), in collaboration with national and local governments, worked to strengthen family planning supply chain management to ensure the widespread availability of contraceptive commodities [7]. However, such efforts, while necessary, may not fully capture or address women’s agency in family planning. Although rights-based family planning certainly includes agency, and both the government of Nepal and development partners such as UNFPA strongly advocate advancing women’s agency, translating this commitment into concrete programming remains challenging. These trends indicate that expanding access alone is not sufficient; understanding and enhancing women’s agency in family planning is critical to ensuring meaningful reproductive autonomy and rights.

The concept of reproductive choice and autonomy is closely related to yet distinct from agency [8]. While choice refers to making decisions and autonomy to acting independently, agency encompasses a broader set of capacities, including the ability to define goals and act purposefully within social and structural constraints [9–11]. Autonomy and choice are therefore dimensions of agency, which also involve the power to negotiate, resist, reflect, and act with intent. Drawing on Kabeer’s concept of agency as a form of power, particularly the “power within”, agency is understood not just as the capacity to act, but also as the internal motivation to define goals and make meaningful choices, even in the face of structural constraints [12]. Although often measured through decision-making, Kabeer argues that agency also encompasses negotiation, resistance, and cognitive processes such as reflection and self-assessment. In the context of family planning, this broader conceptualization shifts the focus from mere access or contraceptive uptake to women’s ability to exert control and intentionality in their reproductive lives. Accordingly, this study examines three interrelated but distinct dimensions of agency, reflecting concerns related to both fertility regulation and Sexually Transmitted Infection (STI) prevention: (1) decision-making regarding contraceptive use, reflecting women’s participation in shared or independent reproductive choices; (2) condom negotiation, capturing relational power and the ability to influence partner behavior, reflecting a woman’s agency to express concerns about both STIs risk and pregnancy prevention, highlighting her capacity to safeguard her reproductive health; and (3) cognitive autonomy, exemplified by internalized beliefs, such as whether a woman feels justified in asking her husband to use a condom if he has an STI. Together, these dimensions provide a more comprehensive understanding of reproductive agency, including observable actions and underlying motivations, shaped by individual capacity and social context. In the context of Nepal, where social norms constraints continue to limit women’s reproductive choices [13], measuring decision-making, negotiation, and cognitive processes is particularly important for assessing women’s agency in family planning.

Active participation and informed decision-making in contraceptive use are crucial for attaining complete reproductive autonomy [14]. Condoms are unique among family planning methods as they offer protection against both STIs, including Human Immunodeficiency Virus (HIV), and unintended pregnancy [15–17]. When appropriately utilized, condoms demonstrate 98% effectiveness in preventing both STIs and unplanned pregnancies [18]. Therefore, it is crucial for women to have the ability to choose a contraceptive method based on their needs and to consider adding condoms if at risk for STIs [16]. In situations where males are unable to use a condom correctly or deny their use, females become vulnerable to STIs, HIV, and unintended pregnancy [19]. Research indicates that increased self-efficacy in condom use is associated with a higher likelihood of consistent condom use among adolescents and young adults in Nigeria [20]. Nonetheless, societal gender norms and structures influence men’s perception of their responsibilities regarding contraception [21], often resulting in women being assigned subordinate roles in decision-making, particularly in developing countries [22] like Nepal. In this context, a relational power dynamic exists that may constrain women’s reproductive agency [12]. Despite women bearing the primary physical burden of contraception, patriarchal norms frequently grant men greater decision-making power, thereby limiting women’s autonomy [23, 24]. Studying agency in the context of Nepal is particularly important, as women’s reproductive choices are shaped by rooted patriarchal norms, unequal gender relations, and structural barriers that restrict their ability to exercise power within intimate relationships. Nevertheless, collaborative family planning decisions between partners have been shown to enhance women’s empowerment [23].

Despite growing attention to women’s reproductive issues, such as fertility [25–29], maternal health [30–33], and contraceptive use and unmet need [34–39], empirical studies in South Asia, and Nepal in particular, have often focused on quantitative indicators with limited attention to women’s agency in family planning. This gap is especially critical in the context of the SDGs, which, unlike the Millennium Development Goals (MDGs), emphasize the principle of “leaving no one behind,” calling for inclusive and equitable development efforts [40].

Caste/ethnicity has been the primary aspect in Nepal utilized by researchers to delineate poverty levels, educational attainment, health status, and other developmental concerns [41–43]. Marginalized women in Nepal from historically disadvantaged caste and ethnic groups, such as those from Dalit, Janajati, Madhesi, and Muslim communities, continue to face systemic exclusion, while non-marginalized women from dominant groups like Brahmin and Chhetri generally enjoy greater access to resources, opportunities, and decision-making power [41, 43]. Findings from the 2022 Nepal Demographic and Health Survey (NDHS) indicate persistently lower levels of agency in family planning, including decision-making on contraceptive use, condom negotiation, and cognitive autonomy (e.g., believing a wife is justified in asking her husband to use a condom if he has an STI), among women from Dalit, Muslim, Madheshi, and Janajati groups compared to Brahmin/Chhetri women, who are generally known as ‘upper castes’ [44]. These disparities highlight the need for disaggregated analysis to better understand and address the structural inequalities that undermine reproductive agency. Despite national commitments to equity and reproductive rights, these disparities remain underexplored. This study examines the determinants of women’s agency in family planning across caste and ethnic groups, generating critical evidence to inform more inclusive and context-sensitive policies and programs that advance reproductive empowerment for all women in Nepal.

Methods

Data source and sample

This study utilized data from the 2022 NDHS, a nationally representative survey conducted by New ERA under the Ministry of Health and Population the Government of Nepal. The dataset was obtained from a then publicly available website (https://dhsprogram.com/data/dataset/Nepal_Standard-DHS_2022.cfm?flag=1) after registering as a DHS data user [45]. The NDHS received ethical approval from the Nepal Health Research Council (NHRC) and the ethical review board at ICF Macro International for conducting the study.

The survey initially selected 14,243 households, of which 13,786 were interviewed, resulting in a high response rate of 99.7%. Data was collected through face-to-face interviews using a structured questionnaire between January 5 and June 22, 2022. Within the interviewed households, 15,238 women aged 15–49 years were identified as eligible respondents, defined as usual residents or those who had spent the night prior to the survey in the household. Of these, 14,845 women completed the individual interviews, including 11,180 who were currently married. For this study, the analysis focuses on 4,769 currently married women aged 15–49 who reported using modern family planning methods which include “sterilization, intrauterine contraceptive devices (IUCDs), injectables, implants, contraceptive pills, male condoms, emergency contraception, the standard days method, and the lactational amenorrhea method” [6].

The main aim of this study is to analyze the prevalence and determinants of women’s agency in family planning based on their marginalized status. The Constitution of Nepal broadly defines marginalized groups, including those disadvantaged by gender, age, caste or ethnicity, geography, disability, and economic status, and it emphasizes eliminating discrimination through inclusive and participatory principles [46]. This study focuses specifically on caste and ethnic dimensions of marginalization, which remain deeply embedded in Nepal’s social structure and are widely used in both academic and policy research as key determinants of inequality [33, 41–43, 47–49]. Following these classifications, non-marginalized groups in this study include Brahmin/Chhetri (both Hill and Terai) and Newar, while marginalized groups comprise Janajati (Hill and Terai), Dalits (Hill and Terai), Muslims, and Other Terai Castes as outlined in Table 1. This categorization reflects historical exclusion and practical considerations related to data availability in the NDHS, allowing for a disaggregated analysis of social inequalities in reproductive agency. Based on these classifications, the analyses indicate that the total weighted sample consists of 4,769 currently married women participating in this study, with 3,357 (70.4%) belonging to marginalized groups and 1,412 (29.6%) classified as non-marginalized.

Study variables

This study used both dependent and independent variables. The dependent variable, women’s agency in family planning, was measured through three dimensions and combined into a composite index. Independent variables included key socio-demographic, economic, and interpersonal relational factors, selected based on theoretical relevance and prior research.

Dependent variable

The dependent variable in this study is women’s agency in family planning, measured through three key dimensions: (1) decision-making on contraceptive use, (2) condom negotiation, and (3) cognitive autonomy. These dimensions align with Kabeer’s conceptualization of agency, encompassing both observable behaviors and internal motivations. Decision-making refers to whether the woman alone or jointly with her partner makes decisions about using contraception. Condom negotiation captures the woman’s ability to ask her husband to use a condom. Cognitive autonomy is assessed based on whether the woman believes she is justified in asking her husband to use a condom if he has an STI. Each outcome variable was coded as a binary indicator (1 = yes, 0 = no), reflecting the presence or absence of agency in each domain. These three items were then combined into a composite index to measure overall agency in family planning: women who reported agency in all three domains were coded as 1 (indicating having agency in family planning), while all others were coded as 0 (no agency in family planning). This approach reflects the multidimensional nature of reproductive agency by requiring consistent agency across decision-making, negotiation, and cognitive autonomy (Table 1).

Independent variables

In terms of background characteristics, women with marginalized status may have different experiences of agency in family planning. Independent variables were selected based on existing literature on women’s agency in family planning. These categorical predictors include socio-economic and demographic factors such as age, occupation, education, caste/ethnicity, wealth index, place of residence, birth order (i.e., order of the child born to a woman), and media exposure [50–53], as well as women’s participation in three major domains of household decision-making (e.g., decisions regarding their health care, large household purchases, and visits to relatives). While household decision-making reflects a form of agency, it is conceptualized here as a separate dimension from reproductive agency. In line with previous studies [52, 54], we treat household decision-making as a potential enabling factor influencing women’s agency in family planning. These variables were assessed to explain variation in, and identify factors significantly associated with, women’s agency in family planning [55, 56]. A woman is considered involved in household decision-making when she participates either independently or jointly with her husband in making decisions across the three domains mentioned above. Decisions made solely by someone else on her behalf are not considered her involvement.

In addition to these background characteristics, we included the variable “husband or family member pressured respondent to become pregnant” to reflect a relational or interpersonal factor that may influence women’s agency in family planning. While not a socio-demographic trait, this variable captures potential reproductive coercion and power dynamics within intimate or family relationships. The classification and coding of all independent variables are provided in Table 1.

Methods of data analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using the Statistical Package for Social Sciences (SPSS) version 27.0. Descriptive analysis was carried out for each of the variables in the study. Bivariate analysis was employed to examine variations in women’s agency regarding family planning use based on independent variables, considering both marginalized and non-marginalized women. Collinearity among variables was assessed, and it was determined that none of the independent variables exhibited strong correlations. Consequently, all variables selected in the bivariate analysis were retained for the binary and multivariable logistic regressions. These regressions were employed to ascertain the causal impact on women’s agency in family planning usage. The outcomes of the logistic regressions were presented using Odds Ratios (OR) along with 95% Confidence Intervals (CIs).

In terms of age, the majority of women in both groups fall within the 25–34 and 35 + age brackets, with 36.5% aged 25–34 and 52.5% aged 35 or older overall. A larger share of non-marginalized women (55.4%) are aged 35 and above, compared to 51.3% of marginalized women. Analysis of birth order shows that the second birth is the most common among both groups, particularly among non-marginalized women (43.6%). Marginalized women have higher proportions in third (25.4%) and fourth-or-more (25.9%) birth orders compared to their non-marginalized counterparts. Education levels show striking disparities; that is, 45.2% of marginalized women have no education compared to 26.1% among non-marginalized women, and only 1.2% of marginalized women attained higher education versus 7.9% of non-marginalized women. Additionally, 62.5% of marginalized women are engaged in agriculture, a higher proportion than among non-marginalized women (53.0%).

Further, household and technological indicators reflect disparities. A significantly higher proportion of marginalized women fall in the poor wealth category compared with non-marginalized women (43.1% vs. 35.5%). Over half (51.3%) of non-marginalized women belong to the rich quintile, compared to only 31.8% of marginalized women. Mobile phone ownership is also much higher among non-marginalized women (89.5%) than that of marginalized women (73.5%). Residence patterns reveal that while the majority of women from both groups reside in urban areas, urban residence is more common among non-marginalized women (69.1%) compared to marginalized women (62.4%). Analysis of the data indicates that marginalized women report a slightly higher level of pregnancy pressure (10.7%) than non-marginalized women (6.3%). These demographic and socio-economic disparities and relational status indicate systemic inequalities that may influence women’s agency and access to reproductive health resources.

Regarding the decision-making, Table 3 demonstrates that joint decision-making is the most common pattern across all three domains: health care (53.7%), large household purchases (40.9%), and visits to family or relatives (44.3%), with minimal variation between marginalized and non-marginalized women overall. Solo decision-making is consistently higher among non-marginalized women, especially regarding visits to family or relatives (30.2% vs. 27.1%) and health care (23.1% vs. 18.8%). In contrast, decision-making by others is more frequently reported among marginalized women across all areas, most notably in large household purchases (37.2% vs. 32.5%) and health care (27.8% vs. 22.5%). These differences suggest that marginalized women may have relatively less autonomy in household decision-making compared to their non-marginalized counterparts.

Among the non-marginalized women, there was a slight variation in decision-making patterns regarding the use of contraception across all age groups. However, there is a noticeable lower (72%) rate, particularly among older age marginalized women groups compared with young women (79%), regarding the ability to ask their partner to use a condom. In terms of women who believe that a woman is justified in asking that they use a condom if she knows that her husband has an STI, the same status is found in all age groups among non-marginalized groups. In this group, almost all (97.0%) young women (15–24) exhibit agency in deciding on the use of contraception, while those in the 25–34 age group, nine out of ten women were comfortable asking their partners to use condoms. Additionally, 94% of women believe that it is justified to request condom use if she knows that her husband has an STI in the age group 25 and above. Marginalized women in the 25–34 age group display higher decision-making abilities in contraception (94%) and partner condom requests (92%) compared to younger (15–24) (93%) and older age (35–49) (91%) groups.

Among the marginalized group, only around three-fifths of women with a fourth or higher birth order (60.9%), identify as Muslim or belong to other Terai castes (60.2%), have no education (63.2%), and do not own a mobile telephone (61.9%) demonstrated the ability to request their partners to use condoms. This proportion was notably lower compared to their counterparts with a lower birth order, belonging to Janajati and Dalit castes, having an education, and owning a mobile/telephone.

Interestingly, women with no children reported relatively limited agency in using modern contraception in both marginalized (83%) and non-marginalized groups (90%) in comparison to women having children. The proportion of Hill Brahmin/Chhetri women who participated in decision-making regarding contraceptive use (92%) was lower than that of Terai Brahmin/Chhetri and Newar women (97%). In terms of requesting their husbands to use condoms, especially in cases of STIs, the non-marginalized group had an overall perception rate of 94% with women within their respective castes at 95%. Women with higher education had the highest rate of decision-making in using contraception, with 98% in marginalized and 94% in non-marginalized groups. There seemed to be a similar level of agency in both marginalized and non-marginalized groups when it comes to requesting partners to use condoms and justifying asking husbands to use condoms in case of STIs.

Women in lower wealth index categories, without personal mobile phones, exhibited more limited agency in family planning compared to their wealthier counterparts in both groups. Urban areas showed relatively limited agency in requesting partners’ condom use and in considering themselves justified in requesting their partners’ condom use in the presence of an STI. Meanwhile, in rural and urban regions, decision-making agency for using contraception was almost similar between marginalized (93%) and non-marginalized (92%) groups.

The study revealed that non-marginalized women who reported being pressured by their husbands or family to conceive demonstrated lower levels of agency in family planning across all three domains. In contrast, this pattern was not observed among marginalized women, who maintained relatively high levels of agency (92% and 91%) despite experiencing similar pressure. This finding highlights the importance of interpersonal or relational dynamics, such as reproductive pressure from family members, which may influence women’s reproductive agency differently across social groups.

Compared to those who are unable to ask their partner to use condoms, across both groups, women who actively participate in decisions regarding using contraception and women who believe that they request their husband to use a condom if he has an STI demonstrate notably higher levels of agency. For instance, among non-marginalized women, 94% who decided alone and jointly on their own healthcare reported agency in decision on use of contraception, compared to 86% when decisions were made by others. A similar pattern is seen among marginalized women, where about 95% of those deciding jointly on healthcare and 94% deciding alone on large household purchases reflected higher agency, whereas agency dropped to about 87% when others decided.

The data further reveal that non-marginalized women who decide alone or jointly with their husbands on household matters, such as their own health care, large household purchases, and visits to relatives, report consistently higher levels of family planning agency across all three dimensions. In terms of occupation, both non-agricultural [AOR: 1.56 (95% CI: 1.03–2.38)] and agricultural [AOR: 1.64 (95% CI: 1.11–2.43)] occupations were significantly associated with higher odds of agency in the non-marginalized groups. This suggests that individuals engaged in these sectors had increased odds of agency compared to those not working. Additionally, higher levels of education were strongly correlated with increased odds of agency in both groups. The AORs consistently indicated that as the education level increased, the odds of agency significantly rose. Specifically, the odds of agency in family planning were 1.82 times (95% CI: 1.50–2.20) higher in basic, 2.53 (95% CI: 1.91–3.36) higher in secondary, and 19.40 times (95% CI: 2.61–144.33) higher for those with higher education in the marginalized group. Similarly, in the non-marginalized group, women with secondary education had four times higher odds of agency [AOR: 3.54 (95% CI: 2.32–5.40)] and those with higher education had 4.44 times the odds (95% CI: 2.05–9.62) compared to women who had no education.

Individuals from marginalized groups, specifically Dalit and Muslim/other Terai castes, have notably lower odds of agency in family planning, as indicated by adjusted odds ratios (AOR) of 0.63 (95% CI: 0.51–0.71)] and 0.44 (95% CI: 0.36–0.54)] respectively. Factors such as higher wealth status [Middle; AOR: 1.27 (95% CI: 1.04–1.55)] and [Rich; AOR: 1.40 (95% CI: 1.14–1.73)] and ownership of mobile/telephone [AOR: 1.35 (95% CI: 1.14–1.61)] were associated with slightly increased odds of agency in marginalized groups, but not significant in the non-marginalized group. Joint decision-making in healthcare and visiting family or relatives are associated with higher odds of agency among marginalized women, while decisions made by others regarding large household purchases are linked to reduced agency. The rural residence is connected to lower odds of agency in marginalized groups, although no significant association was found between rural residence and agency in family planning.

Among non-marginalized women, those who experienced pressure to conceive had significantly lower agency in family planning [AOR: 0.49 (95% CI: 0.30–0.79)], indicating a 51% reduction in the odds of agency compared to those not pressured. In contrast, no significant association was found among marginalized women, suggesting that interpersonal pressure may influence these groups differently in shaping agency in family planning.

Discussion

This study examines the determinants of women’s agency in family planning, which is an essential aspect of health, rights, and development, with a focus on disparities across caste and ethnic groups. It comprises over two-thirds (70%) of marginalized women and nearly one-third (30%) of non-marginalized women, all of whom were currently married and using modern contraception. Notably, the percentage for marginalized women aligns closely with the national average of 68.2% [57].

Nepal is consistently working to enhance the social standing of women, though disparities and inequalities persist, especially among marginalized communities [53]. This is evident in the data, which indicates that women’s agency in family planning in all three domains was lower (66%) among marginalized women (Janajati; 76%, Dalit; 61% and Muslim, and Other Terai Caste; 53%), compared to (79%) non-marginalized group of women (Hill Brahmin/Chhetri; 89%, Terai Brahmin/Chhetri; 73% and Newar; 82%). Nonetheless, agency in contraceptive decision-making was nearly identical in both groups, with 92% among the non-marginalized and 93% among the marginalized, reflecting a consistency observed in a study by UNFPA [58] and slightly surpassing the national average of 91% [57]. Differences were evident in women’s ability to ask their partner to use a condom: 74% of marginalized women reported being able to do so, compared to 88% of non-marginalized women. This figure is lower than the national average of 78% for marginalized women and higher for non-marginalized women [57]. Furthermore, a marginal distinction, exceeding the national average of 90% [57], was noted in both groups regarding wives offering a valid rationale for advocating condom use in cases of STIs: 94% for non-marginalized women and 91% for marginalized counterparts.

These findings suggest that Nepal’s family planning policies, including the NFPCIP (2015–2020) [4], the Nepal Health Sector Strategy (2015–2020) [59], and the Family Planning 2030 commitments [5], have been successful in ensuring equitable access to contraceptive services. However, the lower levels of condom negotiation and cognitive autonomy among marginalized women highlight gaps in addressing deeper gender and social power dynamics. Policymakers should therefore care about these findings because they reveal that equal access to contraception alone is insufficient to guarantee women’s agency. To meet national and global family planning goals, programs must move beyond contraceptive uptake and explicitly address structural inequalities by empowering marginalized women through targeted interventions, community engagement, and gender-transformative strategies that challenge restrictive norms and promote equitable decision-making within Nepal’s health system.

This study reveals that increasing age is associated with higher decision-making power regarding contraception among marginalized women. Similar findings have been documented in studies conducted by researchers in different regions in South Ethiopia [60], in sub-Saharan Africa [61], in Ethiopia [62], in Mozambique [63], and in Bangladesh [64]. As women aged, they may become more confident in managing discussions with their husbands and making decisions regarding family planning. Conversely, younger women might feel hesitant to challenge their older husbands and are expected to defer to their opinions, resulting in diminished decision-making power for younger women in family planning [61, 65].

Moreover, consistent with studies from other low- and middle-income countries [52, 66], this study also finds that joint household decision-making is positively associated with women’s agency in family planning. These findings reinforce the importance of shared decision-making in enhancing reproductive autonomy. However, this study adds to the literature by highlighting how this association plays out specifically among marginalized women in Nepal, where caste-based and ethnic hierarchies intersect with household power dynamics. Similarly, as expected, an increase in wealth index and having media (phone) corresponds to stronger agency in family planning in this group [52, 61, 64]. Regarding caste/ethnicity, Muslim, other Terai Caste, and Dalit women had significantly lower odds of having agency in family planning compared with Janajati women. However, interestingly, all these mentioned significant factors (age, household decision-making, wealth index, phone ownership, and caste/ethnicity) for marginalized women were not significant factors in family planning agencies among the non-marginalized group, indicating that their relevance may vary between marginalized and non-marginalized populations. This suggests that the influence of age and household decision-making on family planning agency may not be uniformed across diverse population groups [67]. Addressing these disparities is not only essential for achieving national family planning and reproductive health targets but also for advancing Nepal’s constitutional commitment to social inclusion and reducing inequalities.

Higher educational level was also a significant predictor of increased women’s agency in family planning, as informed by several authors [60, 61, 65, 66, 68] in both groups. Notably, the effect of education on women’s agency in family planning appears stronger among marginalized women, with those having higher education corresponding to approximately 19 times higher odds of agency compared to those with no education, while the corresponding figure is 4 times higher among non-marginalized women. Although the strength of association differs, education remains a crucial factor in enhancing agency across both groups. This result underscores the importance of investing in women’s education as a policy priority for advancing family planning goals in Nepal. By enhancing knowledge, confidence, and critical awareness, education strengthens women’s agency, especially among marginalized groups, helping them overcome restrictive norms and structural barriers that limit reproductive choice. Expanding equitable access to quality education is therefore central to achieving both national family planning targets and broader gender equality commitments. Additionally, the association between women’s occupation and agency in family planning was significant only among non-marginalized women, where those engaged in non-agricultural or agricultural work had higher odds of agency compared to those not working. This finding aligns with the positive effects of employment on reproductive autonomy [64]. However, the same association was not statistically significant among marginalized women.

A lower adjusted odds ratio for agency in family planning was observed among non-marginalized women who reported pressure from their husband or family members to become pregnant, and when others made decisions about visiting family or relatives. This pattern was not evident among marginalized women, indicating that spousal and family dominance, along with restrictions on mobility, may have a stronger influence on reproductive agency among non-marginalized women. One possible explanation is that non-marginalized women, who are generally more educated and have a better socio-economic status, may have higher expectations of autonomy. Therefore, when they face pressure or external control in reproductive or personal decisions, it results in a more significant reduction in their agency. In contrast, such restrictions may be more normalized among marginalized women, whose agency is already limited by structural disadvantage.

This study has some limitations. Firstly, although the study considers caste/ethnicity, class, and education, it does not examine how these factors intersect with each other to influence women’s agency in family planning. Future research could employ an intersectional approach to investigate the combined effects of these factors, rather than examining each factor separately. Secondly, the study relies on data from a single survey, limiting the ability to compare changes in agency over time. Lastly, the study is purely quantitative and would benefit from a qualitative approach to explore participants’ perspectives on family planning decisions. These issues highlight areas for future research.

Conclusion

This study highlights that women’s agency in family planning differs between marginalized and non-marginalized groups in Nepal. While education and decision-making on family visits were common determinants for both groups, factors such as age, caste/ethnicity, wealth, mobile ownership, and household decision-making (such as the respondent’s health care and large household purchases) mattered mainly for marginalized women, whereas occupation and pregnancy pressure were significant for non-marginalized women. These variations highlight that a one-size-fits-all approach is insufficient.

To strengthen agency, policies must adopt inclusive and context-specific strategies, with special focus on marginalized women, particularly Muslim and Other Terai caste groups, the poor, and the young. Education emerges as a key lever, suggesting targeted investments in schooling, vocational training, and awareness programs. Aligning with Nepal’s FP2030 and SDG commitments, to ensure healthy, prosperous lives where all can fully exercise their sexual and reproductive rights, addressing these disparities is vital for advancing equitable agency in family planning. In this regard, future studies should employ intersectional and mixed-methods approaches to capture the nuanced experiences and structural barriers affecting women’s agency across diverse contexts.
