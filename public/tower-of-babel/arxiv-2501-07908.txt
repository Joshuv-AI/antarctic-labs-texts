Vacuum Propellion
Yu-Song Cao, YanXia Liu, Ding-Fang Zeng

Abstract
Investigations show that a time-varying δ − δ ′ \delta-\delta^{\prime} mirror gives rise to asymmetrical vacuum radiation on its two sides, enabling one to extract propelling forces from the vacuum fluctuation. In this work, we propose a design of Casimir device to gain propulsions out of vacuum with conventional δ \delta mirrors. We call this device a “vacuum propellion”, which is experimentally feasible. It consists of a cavity made up of a perfectly reflective left mirror and a right mirror with time dependent transparency. All particles generated from this propellion are preferentially right-moving, so the cavity obtains a left-pointing propelling force.

Introduction

In 1948, Casimir effect was proposed as an observable consequence of quantum fluctuation in vacuum
[
1
]
. About ten years later, this effect was reported to be directly observed in experiment
[
2
]
. In 1969, G. T. Moore predicted that particles can be generated from the quantum vacuum inside a cavity with variable length
[
3
]
. This phenomenon is known as dynamical Casimir effect (DCE), sometimes also called vacuum radiation. Several years later, calculation suggests that DCE can also occur with just one oscillating mirror
[
4
,
5
,
6
]
.

Unfortunately, the experimental observation of DCE has proven to be much more challenging than its static sibling. Estimation shows that to obtain a detectable DCE signal, the oscillation frequency of a microwave cavity wall is required to be
\sim 1
GHz, which is far beyond the reach of current mechanical technology. Recently, it is proposed that a flying plasma mirror accelerated by intense laser may serve as a candidate to meet such extreme requirement, whose experimental and theoretical analysis were discussed in
[
7
,
8
]
. Faced with such a road block, researchers devised an alternative way to observe DCE experimentally. Instead of a moving mirror, they proposed using a spatially fixed mirror with time-varying transparency to do the trick. Because it would be much more practical to modulate the mirror’s transparency with high frequency
[
9
,
10
]
. Working along this line, it is noteworthy that in 2011 the DCE radiation was observed in superconducting quantum interference device (SQUID), where the mirror’s transparency is simulated by the inductance of SQUID
[
11
]
. For more recent advances the readers are referred to
[
12
,
13
]
.

In DCE, the energy of radiated particles comes from the external driving source of the mirror. In return, the mirror experiences a back-reaction force against its change during the DCE emission. This back-reaction force is called vacuum friction
[
4
,
6
,
14
,
15
,
16
]
. Generally, in the context of DCE, the mirrors are modelled by Dirac
\delta
function potentials in the lagrangian
[
17
,
18
]
, which can perfectly simulate the transparence and reflection of the conventional mirror on the macroscopic level. For a
\delta
mirror, the transparencies and reflectivities are identical on its both sides. Consequently the emission spectra of DCE on both sides are also the same
[
19
,
20
]
. This implies that during the DCE emission, the averaged back-reaction force on the mirror should be zero. While the fluctuation part of the force will cause the mirror to undergo Browinian motion around the initial position
[
14
,
21
,
22
]
. Recently, a novel type of mirrors modelled by so called generalized Robin boundary condition attracts the researchers’ interests. In mathematical form, this boundary condition serves as a
\delta-\delta^{\prime}
potential
[
20
,
23
,
24
,
25
,
26
,
27
,
28
]
. The most profound feature of the
\delta-\delta^{\prime}
mirror is the asymmetry of transparencies on its different sides
[
20
,
29
]
. As a result, the emission spectra of DCE are also different on the two sides
[
20
,
30
,
31
]
. In
[
31
]
, the authors pointed out this asymmetric DCE spectra will lead to non-vanishing averaged back-reaction force on the mirror, giving the motion of the mirror a preferred direction. So far, discussions on
\delta-\delta^{\prime}
mirrors are purely mathematical and theoretical. Their experimental realization remains a challenge
[
32
]
.

In this work, we design a vacuum propellion with conventional
\delta
mirrors, whose experimental realization is already well known. The lesson we learned from the
\delta-\delta^{\prime}
mirror is that, as long as the DCE spectra on the different sides of the device is asymmetrically engineered, a non-zero averaged back-reaction force would be possible
[
31
]
. Here, instead of one
\delta-\delta^{\prime}
mirror, we use two conventional
\delta
mirrors, one with time-varying transparency and one with fixed transparency. The mirror with fixed transparency will modify the DCE spectra of the other and reflect the emitted particles, resulting in asymmetric particle flux, and more importantly, momentum flux on different sides of the cavity. By momentum conservation,the mean back-reaction force will be non-zero. For simplicity, we let the mirror on the left be perfectly reflective, while the mirror on the right have a time-varying transparency. It is found that no particles are created on the left side of the cavity and all the particles created travel rightward ultimately. Furthermore, the calculation shows the particle number and radiation energy does not grow monotonically with the driving frequency. This is different from the one-mirror models. Another feature we find is as a result of resonance transparency, the radiated particles does not contain components with resonance frequencies of the cavity. Finally, we present a unified method to compute the averaged propelling force on the mirrors in the domains of time as well as frequency.

This paper is organised as follows. In Sec.2, after a brief introduction of the model, we solve the field equation via scattering approach and do the quantization. In Sec.3, we compute the DCE spectra of particle number, energy and momentum. In Sec.4, we calculate the propelling force. Some conclusion remarks and discussion are given in Sec.5.

Throughout the whole paper, the units will be chosen so that
\hbar=c=1
.

2
General framework of the scattering approach

Figure 1:
Schematic of the system. The subscript denotes the ingoing, outgoing and cavity field, respectively. When the coupling strength of the right mirror varies, particles are created from vacuum and ultimately travels rightward, as shown by the wavy lines and the black dots.

In
(1+1)
dimensional quantum field theory, the field can be written as the combination of left-moving and right-moving part

\displaystyle\phi(t,x)=\varphi(t-x)+\psi(t+x).

(1)

This can be rearranged as a column vector

\displaystyle\Phi(t,x)=\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\varphi(t-x)\\
\psi(t+x)\end{array}\right).

To characterize scattering process, the partial Fourier transformation is required, which reads

\displaystyle\phi(t,x)=\int\frac{d\omega}{2\pi}\phi[\omega,x]e^{-i\omega t},

(5)

where functions in the domains of time and frequency are distinguished by the parenthetic and square brackets. This notation will be used as default in the sequel.

The system we are interested in is a cavity interacting with a real massless scalar field. For simplicity and without loss of generality, we work in
(1+1)
dimensions as sketched in Fig.
1

\delta

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}=\frac{1}{2}\partial_{\mu}\phi\partial^{\mu}\phi+\frac{g}{2}\delta(x+L)\phi^{2}+\frac{\lambda(t)}{2}\delta(x)\phi^{2},

(6)

where
g

\phi

\lambda(t)
. The field equation following this lagrangian can be written as

\displaystyle\square\phi(t,x)=g\delta(x)\phi(t,x)+\lambda(t)\delta(x)\phi(t,x),

(7)

where
\square
is the d’Alembert operator. Solutions to this equation can be decomposed into three spatial parts

\displaystyle\phi(t,x)=\theta[-(x+L)]\phi_{-}(t,x)+\theta(-x)\theta(x+L)\phi_{cav}(t,x)+\theta(x)\phi_{+}(t,x),

(8)

After partial Fourier transformation (

\displaystyle\phi_{+}(t,x)=\int\frac{d\omega}{2\pi}[\varphi_{out}[\omega]e^{i\omega x}+\psi_{in}[\omega]e^{-i\omega x}]e^{-i\omega t},

(9a)

\displaystyle\phi_{cav}(t,x)=\int\frac{d\omega}{2\pi}[\varphi_{cav}[\omega]e^{i\omega x}+\psi_{cav}[\omega]e^{-i\omega x}]e^{-i\omega t},

(9b)

\displaystyle\phi_{-}(t,x)=\int\frac{d\omega}{2\pi}[\varphi_{in}[\omega]e^{i\omega x}+\psi_{out}[\omega]e^{-i\omega x}]e^{-i\omega t},

(9c)

as one can infer from Fig.

\displaystyle\phi_{+}[\omega,x]=\varphi_{out}[\omega]e^{i\omega x}+\psi_{in}[\omega]e^{-i\omega x},

(10a)

\displaystyle\phi_{cav}[\omega,x]=\varphi_{cav}[\omega]e^{i\omega x}+\psi_{cav}[\omega]e^{-i\omega x},

(10b)

\displaystyle\phi_{-}[\omega,x]=\varphi_{in}[\omega]e^{i\omega x}+\psi_{out}[\omega]e^{-i\omega x}.

(10c)

The scattering on the left mirror

\displaystyle\Phi_{out}^{L}[\omega,-L]=S^{L}[\omega]\Phi_{in}^{L}[\omega,-L],

\displaystyle\Phi_{in}^{L}[\omega,-L]=\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\varphi_{in}[\omega]e^{-i\omega L}\\

By references

\displaystyle S^{L}[\omega]=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}s_{+}[\omega]&r_{+}[\omega]\\
r_{-}[\omega]&s_{-}[\omega]\end{array}\right),

is an arbitrary function of time satisfying

. The total reflective limit

can be identified with the Dirichlet boundary condition. Working in this limit will reduce the computation load greatly. In the following, this limit will be taken so that

\displaystyle\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\varphi_{cav}[\omega]\\
\psi_{out}[\omega]\end{array}\right)

\displaystyle\hskip-14.22636pt=e^{i\eta\omega L}\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}&-1\\
-1&\end{array}\right)e^{-i\eta\omega L}\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\varphi_{in}[\omega]\\

\displaystyle\hskip-14.22636pt=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}&-e^{2i\omega L}\\

Vacuum radiation

Now we ask the modulation function to satisfy
f(t)=0
outside the time interval
[-T,T]
. In this case, before
t=-T
, we have a cavity with total transparent right mirror and a field in its vacuum state. After
t=T
, the right mirror of the cavity is also totally transparent and there will be a bunch of right moving particles in the field state, as sketched in Fig.(
1
). The spectra of the vacuum radiation can be calculated via
[
34
]

\displaystyle n(\omega)=2\omega\text{Tr}\bra{0_{in}}\Phi_{out}[-\omega]\Phi_{out}^{T}[\omega]\ket{0_{in}}=\int_{-\infty}^{0}d\omega^{\prime}\frac{\omega}{\omega^{\prime}}\text{Tr}(S[-\omega,\omega^{\prime}]S^{T}[\omega,-\omega^{\prime}]),

(47)

where the property

\displaystyle S[-\omega,\omega^{\prime}]=S^{*}[\omega,-\omega^{\prime}]

(48)

is used. Eq.(
2
) tells us that no particles are emitted in the left side of the cavity
x<-L
. With Eq.(
43
), the emission spectra can be calculated to the second order of
\lambda_{0}

\displaystyle n(\omega)=\frac{\lambda_{0}^{2}}{4\omega}|1-e^{2i\omega L}|^{2}\int_{0}^{+\infty}d\omega^{\prime}\frac{|f[\omega+\omega^{\prime}]|^{2}|1-e^{2i\omega^{\prime}L}|^{2}}{\omega^{\prime}}.

(49)

The right hand side of Eq.(
49
) vanishes when
\omega_{n}=\frac{n\pi}{L}
and
\omega^{\prime}_{n}=\frac{n\pi}{L}
, suggesting that particles of frequencies in resonance with the cavity take no part in the scattering process. This can be understood by the hole theory in which the vacuum is described as a multi-particle state, where all the negative energy levels are occupied. The DCE can thus be viewed as a scattering process of negative energy
\omega^{\prime}
particle scattered into positive energy
\omega
state, leaving a hole in the negative energy sea. This hole, from the perspective of an observer, behaves like a particle with positive energy
-\omega^{\prime}
. In this picture, it can be seen from Eq.(
42
) that the modes with frequencies
\frac{n\pi}{L}
does not participate in the interaction with the right mirror because the interaction
\lambda(t)\delta(x)\phi^{2}(x)
is always equal to
0
for those frequencies. With Eq.(
49
), the averaged total energy and momentum of the radiated particles can thus be calculated as

\displaystyle E=\int_{0}^{+\infty}d\omega\omega n(\omega),

(50a)

\displaystyle P=\int_{0}^{+\infty}d\omega\omega n(\omega).

(50b)

The fact that
P\neq 0
gives the evidence that the averaged propelling force is not null. For illustration, we consider the damped sinusoidal modulation function

\displaystyle f(t)=e^{-\frac{|t|}{T}}\cos(\Omega t),

(51)

where
\Omega
is the driving frequency and the condition

\displaystyle\lambda_{0}\Omega\gg 1

(52)

is required to avoid mathematical complexity
[
5
]
. In this case, the total number of particles radiated and their total momentum are given by

\displaystyle N=\int_{0}^{\Omega}d\omega\frac{\lambda_{0}^{2}}{4\omega}|1-e^{2i\omega L}|^{2}\frac{|1-e^{2i(\Omega-\omega)L}|^{2}}{\Omega-\omega},

(53a)

\displaystyle P=\int_{0}^{\Omega}d\omega\frac{\lambda_{0}^{2}}{4}|1-e^{2i\omega L}|^{2}\frac{|1-e^{2i(\Omega-\omega)L}|^{2}}{\Omega-\omega}.

(53b)

Numerical features of these functions are plotted in Figs.(

4
Propelling force

Eq.(
50
) has demonstrated the existence of non-zero averaged propelling force. In this section, we will derive its explicit formula. To begin with, the momentum density can be written as

\displaystyle T_{01}(t,x)=T_{10}(t,x)=|\varphi^{\prime}(t-x)|^{2}-|\psi^{\prime}(t+x)|^{2}=P(t,x).

(54)

The corresponding vacuum expectation value can be obtained by

\displaystyle\langle P(t,x)\rangle=\text{Tr}[\eta\partial_{t}\partial_{t^{\prime}}\langle\Phi(t,x)\Phi^{T}(t^{\prime},x)\rangle]|_{t=t^{\prime}}.

(55)

In the frequency domain, we have

\displaystyle\langle P[\omega,x]\rangle=-\int\frac{d\omega^{\prime}}{2\pi}\omega^{\prime}(\omega-\omega^{\prime})\text{Tr}[\eta\langle\Phi[\omega^{\prime},x]\Phi^{T}[\omega-\omega^{\prime},x]\rangle].

(56)

The force on the mirrors comes from the difference of the radiation pressure on the left and right side. The averaged force can be expressed in terms of
P(t,x)

[
31
]

\displaystyle F(t,-L)=\langle P_{in}(t,-L)-P_{out}(t,-L)\rangle,

(57a)

\displaystyle F(t,0)=\langle P_{in}(t,0)-P_{out}(t,0)\rangle,

(57b)

where
F(t,-L)
is that exerted on the left mirror and
F(t,0)
is that on the right mirror. With Eqs.(
11
) and (
2

\displaystyle\langle P_{in}(t,-L)\rangle=\text{Tr}[\eta\partial_{t}\partial_{t^{\prime}}\langle\Phi_{in}^{L}(t,-L)(\Phi_{in}^{L}(t^{\prime},-L))^{T}\rangle]|_{t=t^{\prime}},

(58a)

\displaystyle\langle P_{out}(t,-L)\rangle=\text{Tr}[\eta\partial_{t}\partial_{t^{\prime}}\langle\Phi_{out}^{L}(t,-L)(\Phi_{out}^{L}(t^{\prime},-L))^{T}\rangle]|_{t=t^{\prime}},

(58b)

\displaystyle\langle P_{in}(t,0)\rangle=\text{Tr}[\eta\partial_{t}\partial_{t^{\prime}}\langle\Phi_{in}^{R}(t,0)(\Phi_{in}^{R}(t^{\prime},0))^{T}\rangle]|_{t=t^{\prime}},

(58c)

\displaystyle\langle P_{out}(t,0)\rangle=\text{Tr}[\eta\partial_{t}\partial_{t^{\prime}}\langle\Phi_{out}^{R}(t,0)(\Phi_{out}^{R}(t^{\prime},0))^{T}\rangle]|_{t=t^{\prime}}.

(58d)

\displaystyle F(t)=F(t,-L)+F(t,0).

43

Expressions in Eq.(

For real mirrors, they become totally transparent when the field mode frequency exceeds their plasma frequency. Thus, to obtain a physically meaningful expression for the propelling force, a cutoff should be introduced in Eq.(

\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}dtF^{(1)}(t)=0.

(62)

This is consistent with Eq.(

5
Conclusion and discussion

In this work, we designed a Casimir device called vacuum propellion made up of two conventional
\delta
mirrors, instead of one
\delta-\delta^{\prime}
mirror. The experimental realisation of this device is feasiable. So it has the potential to bring the verification of vacuum driving into laboratory in the near future. Our calculation shows that the averaged back-reaction force from DCE is non-null. Thus it will propel the system towards a preferred direction. This follows from the asymmetry of the DCE spectra on the left and right side of the cavity. We found that the particle number created from DCE does not grow monotonically as the driving frequency increases. Instead, it tends to decrease above a critical value
\omega_{c}
. Above this critical value, the propelling force will not significantly grow by further increasing the driving frequency.

We can define the efficiency of the propellion as
\eta=\frac{|P|}{E}
to quantify how much of the energy invested to DCE is converted into propelling force, where
|P|
and
E
are the absolute value of the total momentum and energy of the particles created from DCE. In our calculation, the propellion works at the highest efficiency
\eta=100\%
according to Eq.(
50
). In the following we briefly discuss two factors that may influence this efficiency: the transparency of the left mirror and the mass of the fluctuating field.

When the transparency of the left mirror is non-zero, some of the particles created from DCE will ultimately travel leftward. So the emission spectra of the DCE will become
n(\omega)=n_{L}(\omega)+n_{R}(\omega)
, where
n_{L,R}(\omega)
are the emission spectra on the left and right side, respectively. As a result, the efficiency will become

\eta=\frac{\int_{0}^{+\infty}d\omega[n_{L}(\omega)-n_{R}(\omega)]}{\int_{0}^{+\infty}d\omega[n_{L}(\omega)+n_{R}(\omega)]},

(65)

which is less than
1
as long as
n_{L}(\omega)\neq 0
. In the limit
g\to 0
where the left mirror becomes totally transparent. In this case, the emission spectra become symmetric
n_{L}(\omega)=n_{R}(\omega)
, and the efficiency drops to
\eta=0\%
. This is consistent with the previous results obtained in
[
19
]
.

When the mass of the vacuum fluctuating field is introduced, the efficiency of the propellion can be calculated by our procedure routinely

\eta=\frac{\int_{0}^{+\infty}d\omega\sqrt{\omega^{2}-m^{2}}}{\int_{0}^{+\infty}d\omega\omega}<1.

(66)

This means that if we bump equal amount of energy into the vacuum, the one uses massless field as working medium will acquire stronger propelling force than that with massive field. This is because part of the energy will become the rest mass of the radiated particles, which contributes negatively to the propelling force.
