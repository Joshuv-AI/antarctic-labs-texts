Semiclassical kinetic equations for composite bosons
A. Kudlis, I. A. Aleksandrov, Y. S. Krivosenko, I. A. Shelykh

Abstract
We derive semiclassical Boltzmann equations describing thermalization of an ensemble of excitons due to exciton-phonon interactions taking into account the fact that excitons are not ideal bosons but composite particles consisting of electrons and holes. We demonstrate that with a standard definition of excitonic creation and annihilation operators, one faces a problem of the total particle number nonconservation and propose its possible solution based on the introduction of operators with angular momentum algebra. We then derive a set of kinetic equations describing the evolution of the excitonic density in the reciprocal space and analyze how the composite statistics of the excitons affects the thermalization processes in the system.

In the most general case, this set should account for a variety of mechanisms governing the redistribution of the excitons in the reciprocal space, which include impurity scattering, exciton-exciton collisions, and interaction with phonons. The latter mechanism is of specific importance as it leads to the thermalization of the excitonic system.

Kinetic equations used for description of the excitonic dynamics routinely use the representation of an exciton as an elementary boson lacking any internal structure. However, being composed of individual fermions, excitons can be treated as bosons only if the average distance between them is much larger than the exciton Bohr radius
a_{\text{B}}
, so that the following condition is satisfied:

n_{\text{X}}a_{\text{B}}^{d}\ll 1,

(1)

where

The composite nature of excitons leading to deviations from a truly bosonic behavior has attracted attention since the early 2000s.
An efficient approach based on the concept of composite bosons (

[

,
31
,

[

[

However, the problem of a derivation of semiclassical kinetic equations describing the time-resolved dynamics of the thermalization process in the excitonic system which accounts for the composite statistics of the excitons has been never been analyzed, to the best of our knowledge. Here we present an attempt to construct the corresponding theory. Our main goal is to obtain a general version of the Boltzmann kinetic equations incorporating the statistical effects regarding the exciton composite structure. To this end, we further advance the approach based on the use of the master equation for the excitonic density matrix, presented in Refs.
[
15
,
27
,
25
,
31
]
, generalizing it for the case of a nonbosonic statistics of the particles.

The paper is structured as follows. In Sec.
II
, we recall the standard derivation of semiclassical Boltzmann equations (BBEqs) for exciton-phonon interaction, discuss the related problem of the conservation of the total number of the composite excitons, introduce the excitonic operators in terms of high angular momentum algebra, and derive the corresponding semiclassical Boltzmann equations (BEqs) conserving the total number of the particles. In Sec.
III

IV

II
Theoretical model

II.1
Model Hamiltonian

A system of excitons interacting with acoustic phonons can be described by the following model Hamiltonian:

\hat{H}=\hat{H}_{0}+\hat{H}_{\rm SR}.

(2)

Here the first term corresponds to free excitons and phonons,

\hat{H}_{0}=\sum\limits_{{\bf\it k}}\varepsilon_{{\bf\it k}}\,\hat{N}_{{\bf\it k}}+\sum\limits_{{\bf\it k}}\varepsilon_{{\bf\it k}}^{\rm ph}\,\hat{b}^{\dagger}_{{\bf\it k}}\hat{b}_{{\bf\it k}}

(3)

with
\hat{N}_{{\bf\it k}}
being the operators of the number of excitons in a state with momentun
\bm{k}
and energy
\varepsilon_{{\bf\it k}}
, while
\hat{b}^{\dagger}_{{\bf\it k}}
and
\hat{b}_{{\bf\it k}}
the bosonic operators of creation and annihilation of phonons with momentun
\bm{k}
and energy
\varepsilon_{{\bf\it k}}^{\rm ph}
, and the term

\hat{H}_{\rm SR}=\sum\limits_{{\bf\it k},{\bf\it q}}D({\bf\it q})\left(\hat{X}^{\dagger}_{{\bf\it k}+{\bf\it q}}\hat{X}_{{\bf\it k}}\hat{b}_{{\bf\it q}}+\hat{X}^{\dagger}_{{\bf\it k}}\hat{X}_{{\bf\it k}+{\bf\it q}}\hat{b}^{\dagger}_{{\bf\it q}}\right)

(4)

is the Hamiltonian describing the interaction between the excitonic system and the phonon reservoir leading to emission and absorbtion of the phonons and corresponding redistrubution of excitons in the reciprocal space.

In Eq. (
4
)
\hat{X}^{\dagger}_{{\bf\it k}}
and
\hat{X}_{{\bf\it k}}
stand for the excitonic creation and annihilation operators, respectively. In the diluted limit, when the condition (
1
) is satisfied, the excitons behave as bosons, so that
\big[\hat{X}^{\dagger}_{{\bf\it q}},\hat{X}_{{\bf\it k}}\big]=\delta_{{\bf\it k}{\bf\it q}}
leading to

\hat{N}_{{\bf\it k}}=\hat{X}^{\dagger}_{{\bf\it k}}\hat{X}_{{\bf\it k}},

(5)

and the dynamics of the excitonic occupancies
n_{\bm{k}}^{\rm X}
defined as the mean values of the corresponding operators (
5
) in Born-Markov approximation
[
35
,
36
,
15
]
is described by a set of semiclassical Boltzmann equations which involve the terms corresponding to the final state bosonic stimulation:

\partial_{t}n_{\bm{k}}^{\rm X}=\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\sum_{\bm{q},\varepsilon_{\bm{k}}<\varepsilon_{\bm{k}+\bm{q}}}\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!W_{\bm{k},\bm{k}+\bm{q}}\!\!\left[n_{\bm{k}+\bm{q}}^{\rm X}(n^{\rm ph}_{\bm{q}}+1)(n_{\bm{k}}^{\rm X}+1)-n_{\bm{k}}^{\rm X}n^{\rm ph}_{\bm{q}}(n_{\bm{k}+\bm{q}}^{\rm X}+1)\right]\\
+\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\sum_{\bm{q},\varepsilon_{\bm{k}}>\varepsilon_{\bm{k}+\bm{q}}}\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!W_{\bm{k}+\bm{q},\bm{k}}\left[n_{\bm{k}+\bm{q}}^{\rm X}n^{\rm ph}_{-\bm{q}}(n_{\bm{k}}^{\rm X}+1)-n_{\bm{k}}^{\rm X}(n^{\rm ph}_{-\bm{q}}+1)(n_{\bm{k}+\bm{q}}^{\rm X}+1)\right].

(6)

Here
n_{{\bf\it q}}^{\rm ph}
are the occupation numbers of the acoustic phonons with momentum
{\bf\it q}
defined by a standard Bose-Einstein distribution function and
W_{\bm{k},\bm{k}+\bm{q}}
are the transition rates determined by the parameters of the Hamiltonian and line broadenings.

However, as we show below, taking into account the composite nature of the excitons makes the relation (
5
) obsolete as its use leads to the problem of the particle nonconservation in the process of exciton-phonon scattering.

II.2
The problem of total number conservation for composite particles

The system of equations (
6
) ensures the conservation of the total number of the excitons, which represents a direct consequence of the commutativity between the system Hamiltonian and the operator of the total number of the excitons defined as

\hat{N}=\sum\limits_{{\bf\it k}}\hat{X}^{\dagger}_{{\bf\it k}}\hat{X}_{{\bf\it k}}=\sum\limits_{{\bf\it k}}\hat{N}_{{\bf\it k}},

(7)

for the case when the operators
\hat{X}^{\dagger}_{{\bf\it k}}
and
\hat{X}_{{\bf\it k}}
obey a standard bosonic algebra.

This is not the case, however, if the composite nature of the excitons consisting of individual electrons and holes is accounted for
[
31
]
.

To show this, let us consider the standard expression for exciton creation operator defined as
[
31
]

\hat{X}^{\dagger}_{{\bf\it k}}=\sum\limits_{{\bf\it\varkappa}}\widetilde{\chi}({\bf\it\varkappa})\,\hat{e}^{\dagger}_{{{\bf\it k}}/{2}+{\bf\it\varkappa}}\,\hat{h}^{\dagger}_{{{\bf\it k}}/{2}-{\bf\it\varkappa}},

(8)

where
\hat{e}^{\dagger}_{{\bf\it k}}
and
\hat{h}^{\dagger}_{{\bf\it k}}
denote the electron and hole creation operators that obey
fermionic
anticommutation relations. In Eq. (
8
),
{\bf\it\varkappa}
and
{\bf\it k}
denote the electron-hole relative and center-of-mass momenta, respectively, and
\widetilde{\chi}({\bf\it\varkappa})
is the exciton relative-motion wave function in the momentum space. The latter is assumed to correspond to the ground state (
1s
) of the bound electron-hole system as we neglect here possible excitations of the internal degrees of freedom.

The commutator involving the cobosonic operators
\hat{X}_{{\bf\it k}}
and
\hat{X}^{\dagger}_{{\bf\it k}}
defined in Eq. (
8
) is known to differ from the Kronecker delta
\delta_{{\bf\it k}{\bf\it k}^{\prime}}
, which is valid for bosons, and reads
[
31
]

\commutator\big{\Xop_{\vk}}{\Xop^\dagger_{\vk'}}=\delta_{{\bf\it k}{\bf\it k}^{\prime}}-\hat{\Delta}_{{\bf\it k},{\bf\it k}^{\prime}},

(9)

where

\hat{\Delta}_{{\bf\it k},{\bf\it k}^{\prime}}=\sum\limits_{{\bf\it\varkappa}}\Big[\widetilde{\chi}({\bf\it\varkappa}-{\bf\it k}/2)\widetilde{\chi}^{*}({\bf\it\varkappa}-{\bf\it k}^{\prime}/2)\,\hat{e}^{\dagger}_{{\bf\it\varkappa}-({\bf\it k}-{\bf\it k}^{\prime})/2}\,\hat{e}_{{\bf\it\varkappa}+({\bf\it k}-{\bf\it k}^{\prime})/2}+\widetilde{\chi}({\bf\it\varkappa}+{\bf\it k}/2)\widetilde{\chi}^{*}({\bf\it\varkappa}+{\bf\it k}^{\prime}/2)\,\hat{h}^{\dagger}_{-{\bf\it\varkappa}-({\bf\it k}-{\bf\it k}^{\prime})/2}\,\hat{h}_{-{\bf\it\varkappa}+({\bf\it k}-{\bf\it k}^{\prime})/2}\Big].

(10)

By a direct analogy with the case of bosonized excitons, one can introduce the operator of the total exciton number by Eq. (
7
). However, the explicit calculation shows that the deviation from the bosonic statistics described by the term
\hat{\Delta}_{{\bf\it k},{\bf\it k}^{\prime}}
yields

\!\![\hat{H},\hat{N}]=\sum\limits_{{\bf\it k},\bm{p}}\left(\varepsilon_{{\bf\it k}}-\varepsilon_{\bm{p}}\right)\hat{X}^{\dagger}_{\bm{p}}\hat{\Delta}_{\bm{p},\bm{k}}\hat{X}_{\bm{k}}+\sum\limits_{{\bf\it k},\bm{p},\bm{q}}D(\bm{q})\\
\times\left(b_{\bm{q}}+b_{-\bm{q}}^{\dagger}\right)\left(\hat{X}^{\dagger}_{\bm{p}}\hat{\Delta}_{\bm{p},\bm{k}+\bm{q}}\hat{X}_{\bm{k}}-\hat{X}^{\dagger}_{\bm{p}}\hat{\Delta}_{\bm{p}-\bm{q},\bm{k}}\hat{X}_{\bm{k}}\right).\!\!\!\!

(11)

From the physical viewpoint, this means that the total number of the particles in the system is not anymore a conserving quantity. This is a severe problem requiring a proper redefinition of the excitonic operators.

II.3
Definition of the excitonic operators

To simplify the following analysis, let us start with a two-level model describing the phonon-assisted redistribution of particles between a pair of states and governed by the following model Hamiltonian:

\!\!\!\hat{H}=\varepsilon_{1}\hat{N}_{1}+\varepsilon_{2}\hat{N}_{2}+\varepsilon^{\rm{ph}}\hat{b}^{\dagger}\hat{b}\\
+D(\hat{\tilde{X}}^{\dagger}_{2}\hat{\tilde{X}}_{1}\hat{b}+\hat{\tilde{X}}^{\dagger}_{1}\hat{\tilde{X}}_{2}\hat{b}^{\dagger}),

(12)

where
\hat{N}_{1}
and
\hat{N}_{2}
are the particle number operators of the two states, and
\hat{\tilde{X}}^{\dagger}_{1}
and
\hat{\tilde{X}}^{\dagger}_{2}
are corresponding excitonic creation operators. We assume that the energy levels are not degenerate and obey
\varepsilon_{2}=\varepsilon_{1}+\varepsilon^{\rm{ph}}>\varepsilon_{1}
.

The condition
[\hat{H},\hat{N}]=0
is satisfied if the operators have the following algebraic properties:

a)
[\hat{X}_{i},\hat{X}_{j}]=0
,
[\hat{X}_{i},\hat{X}^{\dagger}_{j}]=\delta_{ij}
, and
\hat{N}_{i}=\hat{X}^{\dagger}_{i}\hat{X}_{i}
. This corresponds to the already considered bosonic case.

b)
\{\hat{X}_{i},\hat{X}_{j}\}=0
,
\{\hat{X}_{i},\hat{X}^{\dagger}_{j}\}=\delta_{ij}
, and
\hat{N}_{i}=\hat{X}^{\dagger}_{i}\hat{X}_{i}
, where
\{...\}
stands for an anticommutator. This corresponds to the case of fermionic particles.

c)
[\hat{X}_{i},\hat{X}_{j}]=0
,
[\hat{X}_{i},\hat{X}^{\dagger}_{j}]=\delta_{ij}(1-\hat{N}_{i}/J_{i})
,
[\hat{X}_{i},\hat{N}_{i}]=\hat{X}_{i}/\sqrt{2J_{i}}
, and
[\hat{X}^{\dagger}_{i},\hat{N}_{i}]=-\hat{X}^{\dagger}_{i}/\sqrt{2J_{i}}
. In this case, the excitonic operators can be defined via the operators of angular momentum
\hat{\bm{J}}
:

\displaystyle\hat{X}^{\dagger}_{i}=\dfrac{\hat{J}_{i}^{+}}{\sqrt{2J_{i}}},\quad\hat{X}_{i}=\dfrac{\hat{J}_{i}^{-}}{\sqrt{2J_{i}}},

(13)

where
J_{i}
are positive integer or half-integer numbers and

\hat{N}_{i}=\hat{J}_{i}^{z}+J_{i}.

(14)

In the case of nonbosonic excitons, the physical meaning of the parameter
J_{i}
can be established as following. We note that
J_{i}
is the absolute value of the angular momentum, which has
2J_{i}+1
different projections. One of these projections corresponds to the vacuum state, so the maximal possible number of excitons in a given state
i
is nothing but

N_{i,{\rm max}}=2J_{i}.

(15)

The Hamiltonian rewritten in terms of the angular momentum operators reads

\hat{H}=\varepsilon_{1}\left(\hat{J}^{z}_{1}+\dfrac{N_{1,\rm max}}{2}\right)+\varepsilon_{2}\left(\hat{J}^{z}_{2}+\dfrac{N_{2,\rm max}}{2}\right)\\
+\varepsilon^{\rm{ph}}\hat{b}^{\dagger}\hat{b}+\tilde{D}(J_{2}^{+}J_{1}^{-}\hat{b}+J_{1}^{+}J_{2}^{-}\hat{b}^{\dagger}),

(16)

where
\tilde{D}=D/(2\sqrt{J_{1}J_{2}})
. Direct check shows that it indeed commutes with
\hat{J}^{z}=\hat{J}_{1}^{z}+\hat{J}_{2}^{z}
, which ensures the conservation of the total number of the excitons
\hat{N}=\hat{N}_{1}+\hat{N}_{2}=\hat{J}_{1}^{z}+\hat{J}_{2}^{z}+J_{1}+J_{2}
.

We can relate the maximal possible occupancy of a single level to the microscopic parameter characterizing an exciton, i.e., its Bohr radius. In our further discussion, we will make an assumption that the change of momentum
{\bf\it k}
does not affect the exciton wave function, so that in terms of the maximal occupancy, all states in our system are equivalent to the state
{\bf\it k}
.

Let us consider
{\bf\it k}=0
and estimate now the maximum occupation number
N_{\rm max}
. To this end, we inspect the connection between the commutation relation (
9
) for the excitonic operators written in terms of the standard coboson operators for
\bm{k}=0
[see Eq. (
8
)] and its counterpart for the excitonic operators expressed via the angular momentum algebra,

[\hat{X}_{0}^{\dagger},\hat{X}_{0}]=1-\dfrac{2\hat{N}_{0}}{N_{\rm max}},

(17)

one immediately gets:

\displaystyle\dfrac{2\hat{N}_{0}}{N_{\rm max}}=\hat{\Delta}_{0}=\sum_{{\bf\it\varkappa}}|\widetilde{\chi}({\bf\it\varkappa})|^{2}(\hat{n}^{e}_{{\bf\it\varkappa}}+\hat{n}^{h}_{-{\bf\it\varkappa}}).

(18)

We can now construct a state consisting
N_{0}
excitons as:

\ket{N_0}=(\hat{X}_{0}^{\dagger})^{N_{0}}\ket{\varnothing}

(19)

and average Eq.(
18
) with to get

\displaystyle\dfrac{2N_{0}}{N_{\rm max}}=\dfrac{\bra{N_0}\hat{\Delta}_{0}\ket{N_0}}{\innerproduct{N_0}{N_0}}=

(20)

\displaystyle=\dfrac{\bra{\varnothing}(\hat{X}_{0})^{N_{0}}[\hat{\Delta}_{0},(\hat{X}_{0}^{\dagger})^{N_{0}}]\ket{\varnothing}}{\bra{\varnothing}(\hat{X}_{0})^{N_{0}}(\hat{X}_{0}^{\dagger})^{N_{0}}]\ket{\varnothing}}=N_{0}C,

where
C
is the following overlap integral

C=\int\!{\rm d}\mathbf{r}_{\alpha_{1}}{\rm d}\mathbf{r}_{\alpha_{2}}{\rm d}\mathbf{r}_{\beta_{1}}{\rm d}\mathbf{r}_{\beta_{2}}\\
\times\phi^{*}_{0}(\mathbf{r}_{\alpha_{2}},\mathbf{r}_{\beta_{2}})\phi^{*}_{0}(\mathbf{r}_{\alpha_{1}},\mathbf{r}_{\beta_{1}})\phi_{0}(\mathbf{r}_{\alpha_{1}},\mathbf{r}_{\beta_{1}})\phi_{0}(\mathbf{r}_{\alpha_{2}},\mathbf{r}_{\beta_{2}}),

(21)

and for the commutator in the numerator of Eq.(
20
) we have used the following relation
[
37
]
:

[\hat{\Delta}_{0},(\hat{X}_{0}^{\dagger})^{N_{0}}]\approx N_{0}(\hat{X}^{\dagger}_{0})^{N_{0}}C,

(22)

which is valid in the linear order by
N_{0}
. Here,
\phi_{0}(\mathbf{r}_{\alpha 1},\mathbf{r}_{\beta 1})
is the corresponding exciton wave function in the real-space representation.

The integral (
21
) can be calculated analytically, if one represents the excitonic wavefunction as a product of the center-of-mass and relative-motion components,

\displaystyle\phi_{0}(\mathbf{r}_{\alpha},\mathbf{r}_{\beta})=\frac{e^{{\rm i}\mathbf{K}_{0}\mathbf{R}_{\alpha\beta}}}{\sqrt{S}}\varphi_{0}(\mathbf{r}_{\alpha\beta}),

(23)

where
\mathbf{R}_{\alpha\beta}
and
\mathbf{r}_{\alpha\beta}
are the center-of-mass and relative coordinates of the
e
-
h
pair and
\mathbf{K}_{0}
is the total momentum of the coboson,
S
is an area of the sample, and then uses a hydrogen-type approximation for the wavefunction of the relative motion
\varphi_{0}(\mathbf{r}_{\alpha\beta})
:

\varphi_{0}(\mathbf{r}_{\alpha\beta})=\sqrt{\frac{8}{\pi a_{\rm B}^{2}}}e^{-2r/a_{\rm B}},

(24)

where
a_{\rm B}
is the corresponding Bohr radius.

In this case, for the maxamal possible excitonic occupancy we obtain

\displaystyle N_{\rm max}=\dfrac{5}{4\pi}\dfrac{S}{a_{\rm B}^{2}}.

(25)

Note that accorging to this expression the condition (
1
) is equivalent to
N_{0}\ll N_{\rm max}
, which exactly corresponds to the limit when bosonization becomes possible.

In what follows, we will use this final estimate in our numerical simulations.

II.4
Equations of motion

Let us now derive kinetic equations describing the dynamics of the level occupancies. We start with a simple two-level case and then generalize our derivation for a more realistic case involving excitons distributed in
{\bf\it k}
space.

We will work in the interaction picture, where the corresponding coupling Hamiltonian reads

\hat{H}_{\rm SR}=\tilde{D}e^{-{\rm i}\hat{H}_{0}t/\hbar}(\hat{J}_{2}^{+}\hat{J}_{1}^{-}\hat{b}+\hat{J}_{1}^{+}\hat{J}_{2}^{-}\hat{b}^{\dagger})e^{{\rm i}\hat{H}_{0}t/\hbar}\\
=\tilde{D}\big[\hat{J}_{2}^{+}\hat{J}_{1}^{-}\hat{b}e^{{\rm i}(\varepsilon_{2}-\varepsilon_{1}-\varepsilon^{\rm{ph}})t/\hbar}+\hat{J}_{1}^{+}\hat{J}_{2}^{-}\hat{b}^{\dagger}e^{-{\rm i}(\varepsilon_{2}-\varepsilon_{1}-\varepsilon^{\rm{ph}})t/\hbar}\big]\\
=\tilde{D}(\hat{J}_{2}^{+}\hat{J}_{1}^{-}\hat{b}+\hat{J}_{1}^{+}\hat{J}_{2}^{-}\hat{b}^{\dagger})\equiv\hat{H}^{-}+\hat{H}^{+},

(26)

where

\hat{H}_{0}=\varepsilon_{1}\left(\hat{J}^{z}_{1}+\dfrac{N_{1,\rm max}}{2}\right)+\varepsilon_{2}\left(\hat{J}^{z}_{2}+\dfrac{N_{2,\rm max}}{2}\right).

(27)

The Liouville–von Neumann equation for the density matrix in the interaction picture then has the form

{\rm i}\partial_{t}\hat{\chi}=\dfrac{1}{\hbar}\big[\hat{H}_{\rm SR},\hat{\chi}\big],

(28)

which after formal time integration yields

\displaystyle\hat{\chi}(t)=\hat{\chi}(-\infty)+\dfrac{1}{{\rm i}\hbar}\int\limits_{-\infty}^{t}\!{\rm d}t^{\prime}\big[\hat{H}_{\rm SR}(t^{\prime}),\hat{\chi}(t^{\prime})\big].

(29)

Assuming that the interaction is adiabatically switched,
[\hat{{H}}_{\rm SR},\hat{{\chi}}(-\infty)]=0
, we can substitute Eq. (
29
) into Eq. (
28
) and thus obtain

\partial_{t}\hat{\chi}(t)=-\dfrac{1}{\hbar^{2}}\int\limits_{-\infty}^{t}\!{\rm d}t^{\prime}\big[\hat{H}_{\rm SR}(t),\big[\hat{H}_{\rm SR}(t^{\prime}),\hat{\chi}(t^{\prime})\big]\big].

(30)

Now we employ the Born-Markov approximation by replacing

\hat{\chi}(t^{\prime})\rightarrow\hat{\chi}(t)
, which allows one to explicitly integrate over
t^{\prime}
and find the following energy-conservation relation:

\dfrac{1}{\hbar}\int\limits_{-\infty}^{t}\!{\rm d}t^{\prime}\hat{H}_{\rm SR}(t^{\prime})=\tilde{D}(\hat{J}_{2}^{+}\hat{J}_{1}^{-}\hat{b}+\hat{J}_{1}^{+}\hat{J}_{2}^{-}\hat{b}^{\dagger})\delta(\varepsilon_{2}-\varepsilon_{1}-\varepsilon^{\rm ph})\\
\approx\dfrac{\tilde{D}}{\Delta_{E}}(\hat{J}_{2}^{+}\hat{J}_{1}^{-}\hat{b}+\hat{J}_{1}^{+}\hat{J}_{2}^{-}\hat{b}^{\dagger})=\frac{1}{\Delta_{E}}\left(\hat{H}^{-}+\hat{H}^{+}\right).

(31)

In the last line, we replaced the Dirac delta function with an inverse broadening of the levels according to the conventional regularization procedure
[
35
,
36
]
.

Neglecting the anomalous terms such as those containing
\hat{H}^{+}\hat{h}^{+}
, we finally obtain the following master equation for the density matrix:

\partial_{t}\hat{\chi}=\dfrac{1}{\hbar\Delta_{E}}\Big(\commutator\big{\hat{H}^+}{\comm\big{\hat{\chi}}{\hat{H}^-}}+\commutator\big{\hat{H}^-}{\comm\big{\hat{\chi}}{\hat{H}^+}}\Big).

(32)

To derive a kinetic equation for any physical observable which does not explicitly depend on time, it suffices just to take a trace of the master equation (
32
) with the corresponding operator. For the operators
\hat{J}^{z}_{i}
determining the occupancies of the energy levels, one finds

\partial_{t}\big\langle\hat{J}^{z}_{1}\big\rangle=-\partial_{t}\big\langle\hat{J}^{z}_{2}\big\rangle=\frac{2}{\hbar\Delta_{E}}\,\text{Re}\,\big\langle\commutator\big{\hat{H}^-}{\comm\big{\hat{J}_1^z}{\hat{H}^+}}\big\rangle\\
=\frac{2\tilde{D}^{2}}{\hbar\Delta_{E}}\left[\langle\hat{J}_{2}^{+}\hat{J}_{2}^{-}\rangle\langle\hat{J}_{1}^{-}\hat{J}_{1}^{+}\rangle\left(1+n^{\rm ph}\right)\right.\\
+\left.\langle\hat{J}_{1}^{+}\hat{J}_{1}^{-}\rangle\langle\hat{J}_{2}^{-}\hat{J}_{2}^{+}\rangle n^{\rm ph}\right],

(33)

where the angular brackets denote the mean value
\langle\hat{J}^{z}_{1}\rangle=\mathrm{Tr}\,(\hat{J}^{z}_{1}\hat{\chi})
. We used Born approximation, i.e., represented the density operator as a direct product of the density operators corresponding to the excitonic states and phonons,
\hat{\chi}=\hat{\chi}_{\text{X}}\otimes\hat{\chi}_{\rm ph}
and introduced
n^{\rm ph}=\langle b^{\dagger}b\rangle
being the phonon occupancy defined by a standard Bose distribution function. Note that if one uses bosonic operators instead of
\hat{J}^{\pm}
, one obtains from (
33
) the standard bosonic Boltzmann equations.

Using the angular momentum algebra

\displaystyle\hat{J}_{i}^{+}\hat{J}_{i}^{-}

\displaystyle=\hat{\bm{J}}_{i}^{2}-(\hat{J}_{i}^{z})^{2}+\hat{J}_{i}^{z}

\displaystyle=\hat{\bm{J}}_{i}^{2}-(\hat{N}_{i}-J_{i})(\hat{N}_{i}-J_{i}-1),

(34)

\displaystyle\hat{J}_{i}^{-}\hat{J}_{i}^{+}

\displaystyle=\hat{\bm{J}}_{i}^{2}-(\hat{J}_{i}^{z})^{2}-\hat{J}_{i}^{z}

\displaystyle=\hat{\bm{J}}_{i}^{2}-(\hat{N}_{i}-J_{i})(\hat{N}_{i}-J_{i}+1),

(35)

we obtain

\displaystyle\langle\hat{J}_{i}^{+}\hat{J}_{i}^{-}\rangle=N_{i}(N_{i,{\rm max}}+1-N_{i}),

(36)

\displaystyle\langle\hat{J}_{i}^{-}\hat{J}_{i}^{+}\rangle=(N_{i}+1)(N_{i,{\rm max}}-N_{i}),

(37)

where we used an approximation
\langle\hat{N}_{i}^{2}\rangle\approx\langle\hat{N}_{i}\rangle^{2}=N_{i}^{2}
, which is valid if the statistics of the occupancies is close to Poissonian. This allows one to derive a closed-form system of equations governing the occupation numbers:

\displaystyle\partial_{t}N_{1}=W_{12}\left\{N_{2}\left(1-\dfrac{N_{2}-1}{N_{2,{\rm max}}}\right)\left[1+N_{1}\left(1-\dfrac{N_{1}+1}{N_{1,{\rm max}}}\right)\right]\left(1+n^{\rm ph}\right)-N_{1}\left(1-\dfrac{N_{1}-1}{N_{1,{\rm max}}}\right)\left[1+N_{2}\left(1-\dfrac{N_{2}+1}{N_{2,{\rm max}}}\right)\right]n^{\rm ph}\right\}

(38)

and
\partial_{t}N_{2}=-\partial_{t}N_{1}
, where

W_{12}\equiv\dfrac{2D^{2}}{\hbar\Delta_{E}}.

(39)

If
N_{i}\gg 1
, so that
N_{i}\pm 1\approx N_{i}
, this yields

\displaystyle\partial_{t}N_{1}=W_{12}\left\{N_{2}\left(1-\dfrac{N_{2}}{N_{2,{\rm max}}}\right)\left[1+N_{1}\left(1-\dfrac{N_{1}}{N_{1,{\rm max}}}\right)\right]\left(1+n^{\rm ph}\right)-N_{1}\left(1-\dfrac{N_{1}}{N_{1,{\rm max}}}\right)\left[1+N_{2}\left(1-\dfrac{N_{2}}{N_{2,{\rm max}}}\right)\right]n^{\rm ph}\right\},

(40)

In the bosonic imit, one puts
N_{i,{\rm max}}\to\infty
, and reaches the standard rate equations with bosonic stimulation term

\displaystyle\!\!\partial_{t}N_{1}\!=\!W_{12}\left[N_{2}(1+N_{1})(1+n^{\rm ph})-N_{1}(1+N_{2})n^{\rm ph}\right].

(41)

In the opposite limit
N_{i,{\rm max}}=1
one gets the following equation:

\partial_{t}N_{1}=W_{12}\left[N_{2}(2-N_{2})(1-N_{1}^{2})(1+n^{\rm ph})\right.-\left.N_{1}(2-N_{1})(1-N_{2}^{2})n^{\rm ph}\right].

(42)

Note, that although it contains the factors accounting for the Pauli exclusion principle, it differs from the standard fermionic kinetic equation

\displaystyle\!\!\partial_{t}N_{1}\!=\!W_{12}\left[N_{2}(1-N_{1})(1+n^{\rm ph})-N_{1}(1-N_{2})n^{\rm ph}\right].

(43)

Eq. (
42
) describes the dynamics of the so-called paulions, which were first introduced by Agronovich in his seminal paper describing nonlinear properties of Frenkel excitons
[
38
]
. Paulionic operators combine the algebraic properties of bosons and fermions: they commute for the case of two different states as bosonic operators do, but anticommute for the same state as fermionic operators, which means that similar to the case of fermions one can not put more then one paulion to a given quantum state.

It follows from Eq. (
42
) that in the linear regime (
N_{1}
,
N_{2}

\ll 1
), Eq. (
42
) it contains a total factor
2
, which means that spontaneous relaxation rate for paulions is twice bigger, then for elementary bosons and fermions.

In the opposite limit
N_{1}
,
N_{2}

\lesssim 1
Eq. (
42
) turns to

\partial_{t}N_{1}\approx 2W_{12}\left[N_{2}(1-N_{1})(1+n^{\rm ph})-N_{1}(1-N_{2})n^{\rm ph}\right],

(44)

which is nothing but a fermionic rate equation (
43
) with twice bigger scattering rate.

In the case of multiple coupled energy levels corresponding to different values of momentum
\bm{p}
, the kinetic equations are straightforwardly generalize, and the corresponding system reads (see Appendix
V
for the details of the derivation):

\displaystyle\partial_{t}N_{\bm{p}}=

\displaystyle\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\sum\limits_{\bm{q},\varepsilon_{\bm{p}+\bm{q}}>\varepsilon_{\bm{p}}}\!\!\!\!\!\!\!W_{\bm{p},\bm{p}+\bm{q}}\left\{N_{\bm{p}+\bm{q}}\left(1-\dfrac{N_{\bm{p}+\bm{q}}-1}{N_{\bm{p}+\bm{q},{\rm max}}}\right)\left[1+N_{\bm{p}}\left(1-\dfrac{N_{\bm{p}}+1}{N_{\bm{p},{\rm max}}}\right)\right]\left(1+n^{\rm ph}_{\bm{q}}\right)\right.

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\left.-N_{\bm{p}}\left(1-\dfrac{N_{\bm{p}}-1}{N_{\bm{p},{\rm max}}}\right)\left[1+N_{\bm{p}+\bm{q}}\left(1-\dfrac{N_{\bm{p}+\bm{q}}+1}{N_{\bm{p}+\bm{q},{\rm max}}}\right)\right]n^{\rm ph}_{\bm{q}}\right\}

\displaystyle-

\displaystyle\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\sum\limits_{\bm{q},\varepsilon_{\bm{p}+\bm{q}}<\varepsilon_{\bm{p}}}\!\!\!\!\!\!\!W_{\bm{p}+\bm{q},\bm{p}}\left\{N_{\bm{p}}\left(1-\dfrac{N_{\bm{p}}-1}{N_{\bm{p},{\rm max}}}\right)\left[1+N_{\bm{p}+\bm{q}}\left(1-\dfrac{N_{\bm{p}+\bm{q}}+1}{N_{\bm{p}+\bm{q},{\rm max}}}\right)\right]\left(1+n^{\rm ph}_{-\bm{q}}\right)\right.

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\left.-N_{\bm{p}+\bm{q}}\left(1-\dfrac{N_{\bm{p}+\bm{q}}-1}{N_{\bm{p}+\bm{q},{\rm max}}}\right)\left[1+N_{\bm{p}}\left(1-\dfrac{N_{\bm{p}}+1}{N_{\bm{p},{\rm max}}}\right)\right]n^{\rm ph}_{-\bm{q}}\right\}.

(45)

III.2
Narrow channel

We consider now a narrow two-dimensional strip of gallium arsenide. The exciton dispersion relation and cutoff momentum are given by

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{\bm{k}}=E_{g}-E_{b}+\dfrac{\hbar^{2}k^{2}}{2\mu},\quad k_{\rm max}=\sqrt{\dfrac{2\mu E_{b}}{\hbar^{2}}},

(46)

where
\mu

E_{b}

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{\bm{k}}^{\rm ph}=\hbar kv_{s},

(47)

where

\displaystyle n_{\bm{k}}^{\rm ph}=\dfrac{1}{1-e^{\varepsilon^{\rm ph}_{\bm{k}}/(k_{\rm B}T)}}.

In what follows, we can vary
T

It remains to define the transition rate constant

II

For the multilevel case, we utilize the following expression for the transition rate involving different wave vectors, which can be obtained by Fermi’s golden rule

\displaystyle W_{p,p+q}=|q|\alpha\delta_{\Delta_{E}}(\varepsilon_{p+q}-\varepsilon_{p}-\varepsilon_{q}^{\rm ph}),

1

and

Instead, in our calculations we use some reasonable values, to avoid problems with determining the volume, mass, etc. of the sample. This is enough to demonstrate the main effect. Also, we assume that the energy profile

\delta_{\Delta_{E}}(\omega)=\frac{\Delta_{E}}{\Delta_{E}^{2}+\omega^{2}}.

We assume further that strip length is

IV
Conclusion

We developed a theoretical model describing exciton-phonon relaxation dynamics accounting for the composite structure of the excitons. We discussed the problem of the proper definition of excitonic creation and annihilation operators based angular momentum algebra and derived modified semiclassical Boltzmann equations which capture the corresponding statistical effects.

Our analysis demonstrates that the composite nature of the excitons significantly alters the kinetic equations governing their dynamics. The inclusion of statistical factors reflecting the finite maximum occupancy of the exciton states results in deviations from the ideal bosonic behavior, especially at high exciton densities. By performing numerical simulations based on our equations, we showed that the thermalization processes are influenced by the nonbosonic properties of the excitons, which affects observable quantities such as the final energy distributions and relaxation times.

Our findings highlight the importance of considering the internal structure and statistical properties of excitons in theoretical models, particularly for systems operating in regimes where the exciton densities are high. Future research may extend the developed approach and include additional types of the interactions, such as exciton-exciton scattering, providing a more comprehensive understanding of the dynamics of complex excitonic systems.
