Correspondence of Papillary Thyroid Cancer With Cutaneous Metastasis in Thyroidology
Authors: Ilker Sengul, Demet Sengul
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThe recent report by Gao and colleagues highlights an infrequent yet clinically significant manifestation of papillary thyroid carcinoma, PTC: isolated cutaneous metastasis appearing 8 years post‐resection. While the rarity of the case is undeniable, this critique explores the nuances of the diagnostic discordance observed—specifically the thyroglobulin, Tg, negativity in the metastatic lesion despite PAX‐8 and TTF‐1 positivity—and challenges the authors' hypothesis regarding the role of thyrotropin suppression. Furthermore, we examine the therapeutic decision to pursue conservative local excision over comprehensive systemic intervention. In high‐stakes oncological management, the balance between functional preservation and radical eradication remains a subject of intense debate. This correspondence underscores the necessity for comprehensive molecular profiling, such as BRAF and TERT promoter analysis, to better understand the aggressive biological behavior of seemingly “low‐risk” primary tumors and to refine follow‐up protocols for early detection of atypical recurrence.

The documentation of cutaneous metastasis in papillary thyroid carcinoma (PTC) by Gao et al. [1] provides a salient reminder of the unpredictable nature of differentiated thyroid carcinoma (DTC), published in July 2026 in the World Journal of Otolaryngology‐Head and Neck Surgery, in thyroidology [2, 3, 4]. Although PTC typically follows an indolent course with a favorable 10‐year survival rate exceeding 90%, the emergence of distant skin metastases—occurring in less than 2% of cases—signals a significant shift in the disease's biological trajectory. While the authors provide a meticulous chronological account of the patient's 8‐year follow‐up, several aspects regarding the diagnostic interpretation and therapeutic rationale warrant a more critical, high‐level academic appraisal.

A primary point of interest is the histopathological profile of the metastatic lesion. The authors report that the tumor cells expressed TTF‐1 and PAX‐8 but were notably negative for thyroglobulin (Tg). In the context of thyroid‐derived metastases, Tg is traditionally viewed as the “gold standard” marker for confirming cellular origin. The absence of Tg expression in the skin nodule, contrasted with its positivity in the primary tumor, suggests a potential “dedifferentiation” or a loss of functional thyroid‐specific protein synthesis within the metastatic clone. While PAX‐8 and TTF‐1 remain robust markers for thyroid lineage, the loss of Tg expression often correlates with more aggressive behavior and may have implications for the efficacy of future radioactive iodine (RAI) therapy.

It would have been academically prudent to explore whether this immunophenotypic shift represents a more primitive subclone that escaped initial detection during the 2016 surgery. Furthermore, the authors' emphasis on suboptimal TSH suppression as a primary driver for this metastasis requires careful scrutiny. They note that the patient's TSH was 2.84 µLU/L in December 2024, which is slightly above the recommended low‐normal range of 0.5–2 µLU/mL. While TSH suppression is a cornerstone of PTC management, attributing an isolated, contralateral cutaneous metastasis solely to a TSH level of 2.84 µLU/L may be an oversimplification. In clinical practice, such a marginal elevation is common and rarely correlates with sudden, aggressive distant dissemination in the absence of other high‐risk factors.

The focus might more appropriately be shifted toward the underlying genomic landscape. Although the authors discuss BRAF and TERT mutations in their literature review, the absence of molecular testing on this specific patient's metastatic tissue is a missed opportunity to provide definitive prognostic clarity. Of note, a TERT promoter mutation, for instance, could explain the late‐stage recurrence and the aggressive phenotype far more convincingly than a transiently suboptimal TSH level. The therapeutic approach chosen—isolated surgical resection of the skin nodule while maintaining the contralateral thyroid lobe—is perhaps the most controversial element of the report. The decision to avoid total thyroidectomy and RAI to “preserve function” and avoid surgical risks is understandable from a quality‐of‐life perspective, particularly in a young patient. However, in the presence of confirmed distant metastasis, the standard oncological paradigm generally shifts toward systemic control. By leaving the right thyroid lobe intact, the authors may have limited the future utility of serum Tg as a highly sensitive marker for surveillance, as the remaining normal tissue will continue to produce Tg, potentially masking early signs of further systemic spread. While the authors advocate for PET‐CT and regular imaging, the loss of the “biochemical compass” provided by unstimulated Tg levels in an athyrotic patient is a significant trade‐off.

In essence, while the authors are to be commended for highlighting this rare clinical entity, this case serves as a call for a more nuanced integration of molecular pathology and radical systemic thought and approach in the management of “atypical” thyroid recurrences. The transition from a local surgical problem to a systemic oncological challenge requires a shift in strategy that transcends mere local excision. In the vast landscape of oncology, a cutaneous metastasis from a DTC is akin to a distant, flickering beacon on a shoreline that was previously thought to be secure; it might indicate that the “sea” of the systemic circulation has carried the disease far beyond its original harbor. This issue merits further investigation. We thank Gao et al. for their work in World J Otolaryngol‐Head Neck Surg on HNcSCC in head & neck surgery [1, 5].
