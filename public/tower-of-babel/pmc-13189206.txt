Spectrally Resolved Fundus Autofluorescence as a Biomarker of Retinal Metabolic Integrity in Intermediate and Atrophic AMD
Authors: Stela Vujosevic, Emma Zanzottera, Luigi Brotto, Gabriele Piccoli, Camilla Alovisi, Vanda Bucceri, Chiara Rui, Enea Poletti, Marco Brambilla, Michele Coppola, Paolo Nucci, Usha Chakravarthy
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeTo evaluate the association between spectrally resolved fundus autofluorescence (Sr-FAF) signals and four macular lesion types identified on spectral-domain optical coherence tomography (SD-OCT): (1) drusen, (2) incomplete retinal pigment epithelial and outer retinal atrophy (iRORA) and complete retinal pigment epithelium and outer retinal atrophy (cRORA), (3) retinal pigment epithelium (RPE) and outer retinal atrophy, and (4) nascent geographic atrophy (nGA) in eyes with intermediate and atrophic age-related macular degeneration (AMD).Methods This cross-sectional observational study analyzed 775 SD-OCT B-scans from 62 eyes (62 patients) with AMD. Lesions were classified according to established OCT criteria (290 drusen, 49 iRORA, 390 cRORA, 46 nGA). All eyes underwent SD-OCT imaging (97 B-scans) and Sr-FAF using a 450-nm wavelength. Quantitative Sr-FAF metrics (average red emission fluorescence component [REFC] and average green emission fluorescence component [GEFC]) were determined using custom software, and lesion groups were compared via one-way analysis of variance with Scheffé post hoc testing.Results Significant differences among lesion types were observed for REFC (F = 59.3, P < 0.001) and GEFC (F = 13.5, P < 0.001). Drusen exhibited higher REFC intensities than iRORA, cRORA, and nGA (all P < 0.001), and higher GEFC intensities than cRORA (P < 0.001) and nGA (P = 0.002). Wavelength (nm) progressively decreased from drusen to iRORA, nGA, and cRORA. Lesion size (in pixels) differed significantly (iRORA < nGA < drusen < cRORA; P < 0.001) and was the strongest predictor (β = −0.31, P < 0.001) in the regression analysis.ConclusionsSr-FAF reveals distinct metabolic signatures across AMD lesion types. Higher fluorophore signals in drusen suggest preserved photoreceptor–RPE activity, whereas reduced signals in cRORA and nGA reflect advanced atrophy. Combined SD-OCT and Sr-FAF may enhance the detection of early atrophic changes and improve AMD risk stratification.

Age-related macular degeneration (AMD) is a leading cause of irreversible central vision loss among individuals over the age of 55 years. Globally, the number of affected individuals is projected to increase from approximately 196 million in 2020 to 288 million by 2040.1 This escalating prevalence reflects the aging global population and underscores AMD as a major public health concern. Although early and intermediate AMD are often asymptomatic or associated with mild visual disturbances, late-stage AMD—including neovascular (wet) AMD and geographic atrophy (GA)—can lead to profound and irreversible visual impairment, severely affecting activities of daily living such as reading, driving, and facial recognition.2,3

Historically, AMD severity has been classified using color fundus photography (CFP), with limits including subjective interpretation and limited structural information.4,5 In contrast, modern multimodal imaging—including optical coherence tomography (OCT), fundus autofluorescence (FAF), and OCT angiography—has enabled a more detailed understanding of retinal morphology, allowing for quantitative assessment of drusen volume, retinal pigment epithelium (RPE) alterations, outer retinal integrity, and choriocapillaris perfusion.6

New classification frameworks, such as the Classification of Atrophy Meeting (CAM) consensus, has introduced precise definitions, using OCT, for early atrophic changes—incomplete RPE and outer retinal atrophy (iRORA) and complete RPE and outer retinal atrophy (cRORA)—which serve as anatomical biomarkers of progression toward GA.7,8 Such multimodal criteria are increasingly considered essential for risk stratification and for selecting appropriate study populations in interventional trials.

FAF imaging offers a noninvasive way to examine RPE metabolism by detecting signals from natural fluorophores (mainly lipofuscin and advanced glycation end products), the levels of which indicate cellular activity and degeneration. FAF enhances our understanding of the underlying mechanisms of AMD and provides insights that other imaging methods cannot offer.9 Spectrally resolved fundus autofluorescence (Sr-FAF) provides metabolic insights that complement structural OCT and conventional FAF, offering a more detailed view of retinal fluorophores by separating the green emission fluorescence component (GEFC) and red emission fluorescence component (REFC). Recent studies indicate that this technique can detect subtle biochemical changes across various retinal diseases, improving diagnostic accuracy and risk assessment.10–13

In AMD, Borrelli et al.14,15 found GEFC signals within the atrophic core in over half of examined eyes, correlating with hyperreflective material on Bruch's membrane. This suggests the presence of drusenoid remnants or sub-RPE deposits containing minor fluorophores such as advanced glycation end products (AGEs), which can represent metabolic information that is unseen with standard FAF. Therefore, Sr-FAF can identify residual fluorophores within atrophy and enhance interpretation of atrophic lesions.

When combined with OCT, Sr-FAF enhances the comprehensive evaluation of retinal diseases and may aid in personalized management and better characterization of atrophic and other processes. This study aimed to integrate OCT-based morphologic staging with quantitative Sr-FAF analysis to investigate whether distinct lesion types in intermediate and atrophic AMD exhibit characteristic autofluorescence signatures. By linking structure and metabolism, we sought to provide novel insights into the pathophysiological continuum of retinal degeneration and identify imaging biomarkers that could refine early disease detection and risk stratification.

Methods

Study Design and Participants

This observational, cross-sectional study included 62 eyes of 62 patients with intermediate and non-exudative AMD evaluated at the Medical Retina Unit, IRCCS MultiMedica, San Giuseppe Hospital, Milan, Italy, from January 2025 to July 2025. The study adhered to the tenets of Declaration of Helsinki, and written informed consent was obtained from all study participants. The study was approved by the Institutional Ethics Committee of IRCCS MultiMedica (no. 264/23).

Diagnosis, classified according to Ferris et al.,5 was established by two retinal specialists (EZ, SV) and confirmed by a senior grader (UC). Exclusion criteria included choroidal neovascularization, macular edema, prior retinal treatment (laser photocoagulation, vitrectomy, intravitreal steroids, or anti-vascular endothelial growth factor [VEGF] agents), refractive error > 6 diopter (D), uveitis, other retinal diseases, or media opacities precluding imaging in the study eye.

Ophthalmic Evaluation and Imaging

All patients underwent a comprehensive ophthalmic examination, including best-corrected visual acuity (BCVA), CFP and FAF with an EIDON device (CenterVue, Padua, Italy) and spectral-domain OCT (SD-OCT) macular volume scans (SPECTRALIS HRA+OCT; Heidelberg Engineering, Heidelberg, Germany). OCT scans were used to determine the presence of AMD lesions. Only the right eye was analyzed when both eyes met image-quality criteria. The macular OCT volume scan consisted of a 20° × 20° (6 × 6 mm) cube with 97 raster lines, scanned at 60-µm intervals with high-resolution and automatic real-time (ART 25) scans. Only lesions entirely included in the volumetric map were included. The largest, intersecting B-scan was selected for analysis.

Lesion Classification on OCT Images

Lesions were classified according to OCT-based definitions: 1.Drusen—Lesions > 125 µm in diameter.2.cRORA—Choroidal hypertransmission > 250 µm, RPE attenuation or loss > 250 µm, and overlying photoreceptor degeneration.3.iRORA—Same criteria as cRORA but < 250 µm.4.Nascent geographic atrophy (nGA)—Presence of one or both of subsidence of the inner nuclear layer (INL) and outer plexiform layer (OPL), or hyporeflective wedge-shaped feature in the Henle fiber layer (HFL).

All measurements were performed in microns using the OCT scaling based on the standard calibration of the device (angular-to-linear conversion). In addition, high refractive errors (>6 D) were excluded to limit scaling variability.

Sr-FAF Images

Sr-FAF images of the posterior pole were acquired using the EIDON device (CenterVue).16 Each image covered a 60° × 55° field of view at 3680 × 3288-pixel resolution. The device employs a confocal scanning system with blue-light light-emitting diode (LED) illumination (excitation peak, 450 nm; range, 440–475 nm) and a blue barrier filter (cut-off, 500 nm). Exposure time was 60 ms. The system produces red, green, and blue (RGB) color images; the blue channel is disregarded because its pixel values are zero due to the barrier filter. According to the quantum efficiency function of the sensor,17 the red channel corresponds to the REFC (peak sensitivity, 600 nm), and the green channel to the GEFC (peak sensitivity, 540 nm).

Quantitative Sr-FAF Analysis

Each lesion was measured in clearly delineated areas; partially visible or vessel-obscured regions were excluded (Fig. 1). The analysis involved identifying the lesion location on the Sr-FAF image based on its segmentation on the corresponding OCT B-scan. This process was performed using custom-developed software. The software automatically segmented these two red points and calculated their relative positions along the green line. Using this information, it determined the corresponding positions, b1 and b2, on the B-scan image (Fig. 1B), drawing vertical reference lines. These markers allowed the grader to visually confirm or adjust the lesion boundaries if necessary. Any manual adjustment was recorded by the software, which updated the coordinates of a1 and a2 accordingly.

When the positions had been confirmed, the software registered the IR image onto the Sr-FAF image (Fig. 1C). A scaling parameter (IR [SPECTRALIS] vs. Sr-FAF [EIDON]) based on visual inspection of a reference image using image editing software was applied. Scaling, rotation, and translation were adjusted to align retinal landmarks, and the resulting scaling factor was then applied uniformly to all images. Translation and rotation parameters were then estimated using phase correlation, a frequency-domain image registration technique. This rigid roto-translation transformation was applied automatically or manually refined (x, y, angle) at the operator's discretion. Automatic registration proved sufficient for all analyzed images, and manual correction was not required.

After registration, the software applied the registration parameters (x, y, angle, scale) to the points a1 and a2 in order to localize the corresponding coordinates, c1 and c2, representing the lesion boundaries on the Sr-FAF image (Fig. 1C). To compute intensity values, a three-pixel-wide strip (14.7 µm) was defined along the lesion axis between c1 and c2; all pixels within this strip were included in the averaging of REFC and GEFC. The three-pixel width was chosen as an arbitrary parameter to reduce the noise influence and improve measurement reliability. In addition to the mean intensity (range, 0–255), the software also calculated the standard deviation, minimum, and maximum values for both REFC and GEFC within the sampled region. In addition to intensity metrics, the software estimated the equivalent wavelength (range, 500–600 nm) based on the mean REFC and GEFC values using an equivalence model that relates the REFC/GEFC ratio to the wavelength. The model parameters were not developed within this study but are embedded in the software as part of its standard functionality. They were previously defined through a calibration process that involved acquiring several autofluorescent targets, each with known emission spectra, and measuring their corresponding REFC and GEFC values from the images acquired with the device.17 This information is included here to document the underlying principle of the wavelength estimation feature.

Statistical Analysis

Normality was assessed by the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test. One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) with Scheffé post hoc tests compared REFC, GEFC, and average wavelength across lesion types. When distributions were non-normal, Kruskal–Wallis tests were applied. Lesion dimensions (number of pixels) were compared by Kruskal–Wallis with post hoc rank tests. Pearson correlations and multiple linear regression assessed relationships between lesion size (length, pixel count) and autofluorescence intensity. To explore lesion heterogeneity, iRORA and nGA lesions were subdivided by REFC and GEFC quartiles; first versus fourth quartiles were compared for lesion size using unpaired t-tests. All statistical analyses were performed using STATISTICA 12.0 (StatSoft, Tulsa, OK, USA), with a significance threshold set at P ≤ 0.05.

Results

A total of 775 B-scans from 62 eyes (62 patients) were included in the study. Of these, 290 were obtained over drusen, 49 iRORA, 390 cRORA, and 46 nGA lesions. The mean age was 81.93 years (range, 69–96 years) with a median of 85 years (interquartile range [IQR] = 81.0–89.0). In our cohort, 41 participants were females (66%), and 21 were males (34%). The mean BCVA was 73 ± 16 Early Treatment Diabetic Retinopathy Study (ETDRS) letters, and 60 eyes were pseudophakic (97%) (Table 1). Both REFC and GEFC intensities followed a normal distribution (Kolmogorov–Smirnov P = 0.15). The mean values are shown in Table 2. The post hoc analysis conducted using the Scheffé test showed that, in REFC, the difference was driven by the drusen group, in which it was significantly higher than in the iRORA group (P < 0.001), and the difference was even greater when compared to cRORA and nGA. In GEFC, the post hoc analysis showed significant differences between the nGA group and the drusen group (P = 0.002) and between the cRORA group and the drusen group (P < 0.001) (Fig. 2).

Discussion

This study revealed a distinct metabolic gradient across OCT-defined lesion types in AMD, measured through quantitative Sr-FAF. The disparities in Sr-FAF signals likely indicate different levels of retinal metabolic activity throughout the AMD spectrum. Drusen presented significantly higher REFC and GEFC intensities compared to atrophic lesions, indicating active RPE metabolism and fluorophore buildup. In contrast, these signals gradually decreased in iRORA, nGA, and especially cRORA, signifying photoreceptor (PR) and RPE loss. The iRORA group showed metabolic autofluorescence levels that were between drusen and cRORA, indicating partial preservation of RPE/PR, but the metabolic profile of nGA aligned more closely with cRORA, indicating a more advanced degeneration stage despite minimal structural changes. This suggests that metabolic decline could occur before clinically visible atrophy.

As previously reported by Borrelli et al.,14,15 our findings also revealed that the GEFC signal was detectable even within atrophic regions, likely originating from sub-RPE deposits and Bruch's membrane, as correlated with hyperreflective material observed on SD-OCT in this area. This supports the hypothesis that Sr-FAF signals, particularly GEFC, may denote not only active RPE metabolism but also the presence of extracellular deposits and remnants within the outer retina–Bruch's membrane complex. Furthermore, lesions with higher REFC and GEFC intensities tended to be smaller, likely suggesting a diseased phase preceding atrophy and the metabolic silence of retinal cells.

The OCT distinction between nGA and iRORA remains difficult in current clinical practice. Although both are early structural changes along the atrophic continuum of AMD, their definitions do not completely overlap, and multimodal imaging increasingly indicates significant biological differences. According to CAM classification, iRORA is characterized on OCT by the simultaneous presence of (1) a choroidal hypertransmission region, (2) a corresponding zone of RPE attenuation or discontinuity, and (3) overlying photoreceptor degeneration. In contrast, nGA, first described by Wu et al.18 in 2014, requires at least one of the following OCT features: subsidence of the INL and OPL or a hyporeflective wedge-shaped band in the HFL. These criteria emphasize early photoreceptor disorganization and structural collapse rather than RPE attenuation; thus, nGA can occur with or without the OCT hallmarks required for iRORA. Similarly, iRORA may or may not display the subsidence or wedge-shaped band typical of nGA. Consequently, neither lesion is a strict subset of the other, which explains why clinical differentiation based solely on OCT can be challenging, especially when structural changes are subtle or incomplete. Sr-FAF offers crucial metabolic insights that help clarify this ambiguity. Whereas iRORA usually retains some minor fluorophores—indicating only partial RPE damage but a diseased status—nGA often shows more extensive atrophic-like changes on Sr-FAF, including reduced minor fluorophores, a metabolic pattern more similar to cRORA than to iRORA, and greater disruption of the fluorophore balance even when OCT changes are mild. These findings suggest that nGA likely represents a more advanced metabolic stage of atrophic disease than iRORA, despite its appearance on OCT suggesting a less severe or earlier pathology. The loss of minor fluorophores suggests substantial cellular stress and reduced biochemical activity in the RPE–photoreceptor complex, leading to cell death. Therefore, Sr-FAF is a valuable complementary imaging tool for distinguishing lesions with ambiguous OCT features; it offers additional insights into fluorophore composition and metabolic activity, potentially highlighting differences between these lesions. Using both techniques simultaneously can help identify eyes that are at higher risk of progression to GA with potential to improve case selection when preventative treatments become available.

The prognostic significance of nGA is well established. Large prospective studies show that nGA accounts for over 90% of the variance in time to develop frank GA in individuals with bilateral large drusen.19 Eyes with nGA have a 56- to 78-fold higher risk of progressing to GA compared to eyes without nGA.20 These findings support considering nGA as a potential surrogate endpoint for atrophic AMD onset in early-stage interventional trials.21,22 Although the risk associated with nGA regarding the advanced stage of the disease is well recognized, the progression risk linked to iRORA remains less clear, necessitating additional longitudinal research to determine its predictive role compared to nGA.22 This suggests that iRORA may still be a reversible or partially salvageable stage, whereas nGA and cRORA both reflect more advanced degeneration with limited therapeutic options. Clinically, distinguishing among nGA, iRORA, and cRORA is crucial for prognosis, risk assessment, and treatment planning.

Finally, the observed inverse relationship between autofluorescence metrics—REFC, GEFC, and average wavelength—and lesion size likely reflects the progressive loss of metabolically active retinal tissue, including RPE and photoreceptor cells, with increasing lesion extent. This results in reduced fluorophore accumulation and a shift in fluorophore composition as lesions develop from early to advanced atrophic stages.

In conclusion, this study supports a multimodal imaging approach that combines structural OCT and Sr-FAF to enhance diagnostic precision, thus offering the possibility to assess progression risk in intermediate AMD. This method allows for earlier detection of high-risk lesions, clarifies uncertain OCT findings, and provides a deeper understanding of the metabolic environment before atrophy occurs. Such a comprehensive perspective is crucial not only for disease monitoring but also for improving trial design and guiding preventive or therapeutic strategies for AMD.

Limitations

The cross-sectional design precludes longitudinal assessment of lesion evolution, and the modest sample size—particularly for nGA and iRORA—limits the statistical power of subgroup analyses. Lesion measurements were performed on the selected B scan with the greatest horizontal extent passing through the lesion to ensure precise projection onto the Sr-FAF image. Although this approach may not fully capture the entire three-dimensional extent of the lesion, it promotes standardized and reproducible measurements across all cases, thereby aligning with the study objective of evaluating individual B-scan lesions.

In addition, the intrinsic characteristics of Sr-FAF imaging should be considered when interpreting the results. Sr-FAF provides an en face signal that represents the combined contribution of autofluorescent fluorophores across multiple retinal layers, predominantly within the RPE and outer retina. As a result, it is not possible to precisely assign the detected signal to a specific anatomical layer identified on OCT. Therefore, the observed relationships between Sr-FAF metrics and OCT-defined lesion types should be interpreted as reflecting overall metabolic alterations within the outer retina–RPE complex rather than layer-specific changes. Future multimodal and depth-resolved imaging techniques may aid in further clarifying the spatial origin of these signals.

Conclusions

Quantitative Sr-FAF provides a metabolic perspective on AMD progression. By distinguishing active, partially atrophic, and end-stage regions, this multimodal approach could refine disease staging and identify lesions at risk for progression to cRORA.
