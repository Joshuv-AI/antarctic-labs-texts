Refinement of renal T lymphocytes enrichment strategies: advancing the in-depth study of renal immune responses
Authors: Zhen Li, Hao Wang, Simeng Lei, Lei Huang, Xihao Shen, Yangkai Fu, Bo Zhang, Lei Yang, Wei Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe kidney T lymphocytes emerge as central agents in both physiological surveillance and pathological processes. In this study, we aimed to establish an optimized protocol for isolating and purifying T lymphocytes from mouse and human kidneys to provide support for renal diseases research. To develop an optimized protocol, we evaluated different tissue fragmentation strategies, enzymatic digestion conditions, digestion times and Percoll density gradients to maximize cell yield and viability while preserving surface markers. Subsequently, we applied this method to various kidney disease models and performed single-cell sequencing analysis by using public database. Our analyses revealed mechanical mincing combined with 0.2% collagenase IV and 0.02% DNase I for 45 min, followed by 33–80% Percoll gradient centrifugation, yielded the highest number of viable mononuclear cells with preserved surface markers. This protocol outperformed mechanical grinding and high concentration collagenase digestion, which caused greater mechanical and chemical damage. Flow cytometry confirmed optimal isolation of CD4+ T cells, CD8+ T cells, and double-negative T cells with minimal impact on cell viability and marker expression. This protocol has been applied in ischemia–reperfusion injury, renal fibrosis, cisplatin-induced acute kidney injury models, and normal human kidney tissue, demonstrating its potential for both preclinical and clinical research. In addition, public single-cell RNA-seq data were used as a complementary reference to support the T lymphocyte subset proportions obtained by our method. This optimized method provides a reliable and scalable approach for renal T lymphocytes isolation, advancing kidney diseases research and potential therapeutic applications.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-51954-5.Keywords: Renal immunology, T lymphocytes, Double-negative T cells, Collagenase IV digestion, Percoll gradient centrifugation

Introduction

The kidney, which plays a central role in maintaining systemic homeostasis and regulating metabolism, is the most frequently transplanted solid organ worldwide and a common target of autoimmune responses1. Renal diseases are typically characterized by immune cell infiltration, persistent inflammation, and progressive fibrosis, processes that ultimately lead to irreversible renal dysfunction2. T lymphocytes drive the development and progression of acute kidney injury (AKI) and chronic kidney disease (CKD)3–5. T lymphocytes in the kidney include subsets such as CD4+ T cells, CD8+ T cells, regulatory T cells (Tregs), and unconventional T cells like double-negative T cells (DNT cells). Under physiological conditions, T cell subsets in the kidney coordinate to maintain immune homeostasis and facilitate tissue repair.

CD4+ T cells are pivotal in kidney immune responses, with Th1 and Th17 subsets driving inflammation through cytokines like IFN-γ and IL-17, leading to tubular injury and fibrosis6. In contrast, Tregs suppress excessive immune activation and promote repair via IL-10 and TGF-β7. CD8+ T cells induce apoptosis in tubular epithelial cells via Fas ligand-Fas signaling, contributing to renal damage in ischemia–reperfusion injury (IRI) and the AKI-to-CKD transition8. DNT cells represent a significant subset of the resident αβ+ T cell population in the mammalian kidney and serve as early responders to AKI, exhibiting anti-inflammatory properties9. Additionally, γδ T cells and NKT cells participate in immune surveillance, with NKT cells having context-dependent roles in inflammation and repair10. These dysregulated T cell responses significantly impact immune inflammation, repair, and fibrosis processes, suggesting that a deeper understanding of T cell dynamics is essential for developing novel therapeutic strategies.

Consequently, the effective isolation and purification of kidney immune cells are essential for accurately characterizing immune responses during the development and progression of kidney diseases. Nevertheless, the techniques for isolating and purifying renal immune cells reported in different studies vary considerably, involving different collagenase concentrations (e.g., 1 mg/mL, 2 mg/mL, 5 mg/mL) and distinct Percoll density gradients for purification (e.g., 36%-72%, 37.5%, 40%-80%)4,11–15. Moreover, approaches for kidney tissue disruption (e.g., mechanical mincing, grinding) and the duration of tissue digestion (e.g., 30 min, 40 min, 45 min) significantly influence the isolation and purification efficiency of immune cells12,13,16. These methodological differences directly impact the quantity, quality, membrane molecule integrity, and antigenic epitopes of isolated T cells. For instance, inadequate digestion leaves tissue fragments, while excessive enzyme concentration or prolonged incubation degrades surface markers (e.g., CD3, CD4, CD8) and impairs cell functionality.

This study aims to establish an optimized protocol for isolating and purifying renal T lymphocytes from murine and human kidneys. Unlike previous studies that optimized only single parameters, we systematically and simultaneously evaluate tissue dissociation, enzymatic digestion (collagenase IV/DNase I concentrations and digestion time), and Percoll density gradients. We seek to enhance the yield, viability, and surface marker integrity of renal T cells to provide a reliable and reproducible method for studies on T cell-mediated mechanisms in different kidney diseases.

Materials and methods

Animals

Eight-week male C57BL/6 wild type (WT) mice were purchased from Beijing SiPeiFu Laboratory (Beijing, China) with a body weight of 20 ~ 25 g, and housed under specific pathogen-free (SPF) environment at the animal facilities of Beijing Chaoyang Hospital, Capital Medical University. A total of 44 mice were utilized in this study, with 4 mice per group. The experimental procedures were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Ethics Committee of Beijing Chaoyang Hospital (Ethics No. 24–2016). This study was conducted in accordance with the ARRIVE guidelines (https://arriveguidelines.org). Animal handling, experimentation, and maintenance were done in accordance with the institutional guidelines for animal experimentation.

Antibodies and reagents

The main reagents used in this experiment included Gibco RPMI Medium 1640 Basic (1 ×), Bovine Serum Albumin (BSA), Phosphate-Buffered Saline (PBS), Hanks’ Balanced Salt Solution (HBSS 1 × and 10 ×), Collagenase IV (50 mg/mL), Deoxyribonuclease I (DNase I, 50 mg/mL), Red Blood Cell Lysis Buffer and Percoll. All reagents were obtained from Service Biotech (Beijing, China). 7-amino-actinomycin D (7AAD) Viability Staining Solution was obtained from BioLegend (San Diego, CA, USA). The fluorochrome-conjugated antibodies against mouse used for flow cytometry analysis were 7AAD-PerCp-Cy5, CD3-FITC, CD4-PE, CD8-APC-Cy7, CD45.2-BV421, and NK1.1-PE-Cy7, which were purchased from Dakewe Biotech (Beijing, China). We used the following monoclonal antibodies to human antigens: 7AAD-PerCp-Cy5, CD3-APC, CD4-PE-Cy7, CD8-FITC, CD45-APC-Cy7, and CD161-PE.

Assessment of renal function

Renal function in IRI and AKI models was evaluated by measuring serum blood urea nitrogen (BUN) and serum creatinine (SCr) levels. SCr Colorimetric Assay Kit (Cat #ADS-W-FM034, AIDISHENG Biotech) was used to analyze SCr levels by sarcosine oxidase. BUN level was measured by BUN assay kits (Cat #C013‐2‐1, Jiancheng Biotech). All procedures were performed according to instructions of the kit.

Histology and immunohistochemistry (IHC)

Kidney tissues were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde solution for 24 h and embedded in paraffin. Paraffin sections (4 μm) were stained with hematoxylin and eosin (HE) and Masson’s trichrome, following the manufacturer’s instructions. Renal sections were deparaffinized in xylene, hydrated in ethanol solutions, rinsed for 5 min in PBS, and immersed in 3% hydrogen peroxide methanol for 15 min to block the activity of endogenous peroxidase. Different types of primary antibodies, such as anti-CD45, anti-CD3, anti-CD4, and anti-CD8, were used in the first overnight incubation, followed by incubation with appropriate secondary antibodies. Finally, the sections were incubated with DAB for detection and counterstained with hematoxylin. After dehydration and coverslips, the images were obtained by using optical microscope.

Isolation of lymphocytes from mouse kidney

The experimental design and flow chart are shown in Fig. 1. Preparation of digestive solution and Percoll solution. Prepare 4 mL of digestive solution per kidney, consisting of RPMI 1640 medium, 2% BSA, collagenase IV at concentrations of 0.05%, 0.1%, 0.2%, or 0.5%, and DNase I at concentrations of 0.005%, 0.01%, 0.02%, or 0.05%. Make Percoll solution at 6 different concentrations: 100%, 80%, 40%, 37%, 33%, and 30%. Keep solutions at room temperature.

Experimental design flow chart. We systematically evaluated tissue dissociation methods (mechanical mincing vs. mechanical grinding), enzyme concentrations (0.05–0.5% collagenase IV with 0.005%–0.05% DNase I), digestion time (15–60 min) and Percoll gradients (30%–40%–80%) to optimize renal T cells yield and surface marker preservation.

Fragmentation and digestion of renal tissue. Methods of kidney tissue fragmentation include mechanical mincing as well as mechanical grinding. The renal tissue was placed in gentle-MACS™ C tubes, and the configured digestive solution was added to the tubes. An equal volume of PBS was added to the control group without the digestion solution. The tissue was incubated for 15 min, 30 min, 45 min, or 60 min at 37 ℃. The mixture was then dissociated using a gentle-MACS Dissociator (Miltenyi Biotec, Bergisch-Gladbach, Germany). Subsequently, 10 mL cold PBS was added to terminate the digestion. The cell suspension was filtered through a 70 μm nylon cell strainer and centrifuged at 500 × g for 10 min at 4 ℃ to precipitate cells, and the supernatant was removed.

Percoll gradient centrifugation and cell count. After lysis of erythrocytes, cells were resuspended in 4 mL of 30%, 33%, 37%, or 40% Percoll solution and gently covered with 4 mL of 80% Percoll solution. Centrifuge at 1,500 × g for 30 min at room temperature with the centrifuge off in brake mode. The micro-white translucent layer at the interface of the two phases was collected with a pipette and transferred to a 15 mL conical tube. Add PBS to 10 mL and centrifuge at 400 × g for 10 min at 4℃, followed by discarding the supernatant. Resuspend the samples in 1 mL of PBS. Viable lymphocyte counts were determined via trypan blue exclusion assay using a hemocytometer, with results expressed as mononuclear cells (MNCs) number per mL per kidney.

Isolation and purification of human T lymphocytes

We isolated immune cells from human healthy kidney samples by the optimal protocol obtained by the method described above. Briefly, renal tissue was dissociated by mechanical dissection and digested with 0.2% collagenase IV and 0.02% DNase I for 45 min and the cell suspensions were purified by 33%-80% Percoll density gradient centrifugation. Healthy renal tissue was derived from three patients with urothelial carcinoma of the upper urinary tract undergoing radical nephroureterectomy. All participants provided written informed consent to participate in the study. The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Beijing Chaoyang Hospital. All procedures were performed in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and relevant guidelines and regulations.

Flow cytometric analysis

All samples were acquired on an Aria II flow cytometer (BD Biosciences). Data were analyzed using the FlowJo V10 software (Treestar Software). The gating strategies used for flow cytometry are shown in Fig. S1.

Renal IRI model

WT mice were anesthetized with pentobarbital (30 mg/kg, i.p.). After abdominal hair removal, mice were placed on a temperature-controlled heating pad to maintain body temperature at 37 ℃. A midline abdominal incision was made to expose the kidneys, and microvascular clamps were applied to the left renal artery and vein for 45 min. Clamps were then removed, and reperfusion of the left kidney was confirmed visually. Following abdominal closure, mice were allowed to recover with free access to food and water. Sham mice underwent identical procedures without vascular clamping. After 24 h, mice were anesthetized again with pentobarbital (30 mg/kg, i.p.), and blood samples were collected via enucleation of the eyeball for SCr and BUN measurements. Kidneys were excised for immune cell isolation/purification and histological assays. Mice were finally euthanized via cervical dislocation.

Renal fibrosis model

The surgical procedure of this model was consistent with the IRI model. After 2 weeks, measure SCr and BUN concentrations and collect the affected kidney for histological analysis as well as immune cells isolation.

Cisplatin-induced AKI model

Cisplatin was dissolved in normal saline at a concentration of 1 mg/mL. The cisplatin AKI model was induced by intraperitoneal injection with a single dose of cisplatin (30 mg/kg body weight). This dose of cisplatin was derived from a study that resulted in minimum mortality with a significant decline in kidney function in this strain of mice. At 24 h after cisplatin injection, the blood and renal tissue samples were collected for measurement of SCr and BUN concentrations. The control group was compared by intraperitoneal injection of equal doses of normal saline.

Single cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) and bioinformatic analysis

The mouse (7 samples) and human (62 samples) healthy kidney scRNA-seq datasets were respectively retrieved from the GEO database (GSE107585; https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/query/acc.cgi?acc=GSE107585) and CELLxGENE Discover (https://cellxgene.cziscience.com). T cell identification and analysis were performed using the “Seurat” package in R17. Quality control processes were conducted according to established protocols in the literature18,19. “RunHarmony” function was utilized to remove batch effects. We applied Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection to reduce dimensionality. Differential gene expression analysis was conducted using the “FindAllMarkers” function in Seurat, with a significance threshold set at an absolute log2 fold-change > 0.25 and an adjusted P < 0.05 to identify differentially expressed genes. Single-cell annotations for subpopulations were assigned based on established references from the literature and the "CellMarker2.0" database, which provides curated markers for various cell types across different tissues and species.

Statistical analysis

Data are expressed as means ± standard deviation. Two group means were compared with a two-tailed t test. Comparisons between multiple groups were performed by a one-way or two-way ANOVA test followed by the Tukey’s or Sidak’s multiple comparison test. Statistical analysis was performed using the GraphPad Prism 10. P < 0.05 was considered a statistically significant difference.

No explicit power calculation was performed prior to the study due to the exploratory, methodological optimization nature of this work. Each experimental group consisted of n = 4 biologically independent replicates, representing individual mice from distinct litters and housing cages. All data presented reflect biological replicates (independent animals), and no technical replicates (repeated measurements of the same sample) were included in the statistical analysis.

Results

Higher collagenase IV concentration yields more T lymphocytes but reduces cell viability

During the study period, all mice remained alive prior to euthanasia, and there were no instances of mortality. We first explored the optimal collagenase IV concentration for in vitro renal digestion. A total of four different collagenase concentrations (0.05%, 0.1%, 0.2%, and 0.5%) were set, along with 0.05% DNase I, and a 40%-80% Percoll gradient centrifugation was performed after 30 min of digestion. The 0.1% collagenase IV digestion yielded about 4.24 ± 0.46 × 106 MNCs per gram of kidney, and we obtained approximately 6.20 ± 0.53 × 106 MNCs per gram with 0.2% collagenase IV, which was not statistically different from 0.5% collagenase IV (Fig. 2A). The data suggested that elevating the concentration of collagenase IV leads to a higher yield of cells. However, CD45+ cells that were isolated using a higher concentration of collagenase exhibited increased 7AAD+ cells, which was related to potential chemical damage (Fig. 2B).

With increasing collagenase concentration, the frequency of NK cells and NKT cells gradually increased with the highest proportion of both at 0.2% collagenase concentration (Fig. 2C). In addition, the flow cytometry images showed that CD4+ T cell, CD8+ T cell, and DNT cell percentages of NK1.1-CD3+ T cells were stable at both 0.2% and 0.5% collagenase concentrations, and absolute number of all three cells were highest at 0.2% collagenase (Fig. 2C–E). The mean fluorescence intensity (MFI) of CD8 indicated that higher concentration of collagenase led to a more significant reduction in CD8 expression in CD8+ T cells. Compared with 0.05% or 0.1% collagenase IV, the 0.2% and 0.5% collagenase IV concentration resulted in a marked decrease in CD8 fluorescence intensity. Furthermore, similar findings were noted in the MFI of CD4 (Fig. 2F,G).

Furthermore, mouse spleen tissues were incubated with different concentrations of collagenase IV for 30 min to confirm the effect of collagenase on the expression of CD4 and CD8 cell surface markers. As shown in Fig. 2H–J, the proportions of splenic CD4+ T cells, CD8+ T cells, and DNT cells were not significantly affected by different collagenase concentrations, but the MFI of CD4 and CD8 decreased significantly with the increase of collagenase concentration, which is similar to kidney T lymphocytes. Taken together, higher concentrations of collagenase IV can cause chemical damage to immune cells. Therefore, we used 0.2% collagenase IV to digest kidneys and collect kidney MNCs in subsequent studies.

Effective renal immune cell isolation with 45 min digestion and 0.02% DNase I

To further investigate whether digestion time and DNase I concentration had any effect on the production of T lymphocyte, we set up different times (15 min, 30 min, 45 min and 60 min) and DNase I concentrations (0.005%, 0.01%, 0.02% and 0.05%) for this study. After the tissues were minced, the collagenase IV concentration and Percoll gradient centrifugation were adjusted to 0.2% and 40%-80%. In this section, the first step was to ascertain the optimal digestion time, while setting the DNase I concentration at 0.05%. As shown in Fig. 3A, the number of kidney MNCs per gram gradually increased with time, but there was no difference between the groups digested for 45 min and 60 min. However, the longer the digestion time, the more severely cell viability was affected. Compared with other groups, the 7AAD+ cells in CD45+ cells digested for 60 min significantly increased (Fig. 3B). The distribution of T lymphocytes clusters in mice was clearly shown in Fig. 3C as well as 3D, and the proportion of each fraction was stable in the 45 min and 60 min groups. All cell types had a trend towards an increase and then a decrease in absolute numbers, and the numbers were all at a high level in the 45 min group (Fig. 3E). As shown in Fig. 3F,G, the MFI of CD4 from renal CD4+ T cells decreased slowly over time, whereas the MFI of CD8 showed an increase followed by a decrease.

Compared with mechanical mincing alone, combined enzymatic digestion had a greater quantity of T lymphocytes

Four strategies for kidney fragmentation were established: mechanical mincing alone, mechanical grinding alone, mechanical mincing + enzymatic digestion and mechanical grinding + enzymatic digestion. Digestion conditions were set to 0.2% collagenase IV, 0.02% DNase I, with a digestion time of 45 min, followed by 40%-80% Percoll gradient centrifugation. As shown in Fig. 5A, enzymatic digestion combined with mechanical mincing and grinding produces a higher yield of renal MNCs, approximately 7.38 ± 0.35 × 106 MNCs per gram and 9.73 ± 0.50 × 106 MNCs per gram, respectively. However, MNCs isolated by mechanical grinding with or without the addition of digestion solution showed increased 7AAD staining, suggesting that the grinding process resulted in increased mechanical damage. In contrast, mechanical mincing with enzymatic digestion exhibited the lowest 7AAD positive cells (Fig. 5B).

Moreover, the frequency of CD3+ T cells was highest with mechanical grinding, whereas there was no significant difference when enzymatic digestion was combined with mechanical mincing and grinding (33.3 ± 1.76% vs. 33.0 ± 3.00%, P > 0.05), but no significant difference in the proportion of NK cells was observed among the four groups (Fig. 5C). Furthermore, enzymatic digestion increased the NKT cells percentage of CD45+ cells. As shown in Fig. 5D–F, with the exception of mechanical mincing alone, the other three methods of kidney fragmentation indicated a trend toward stabilization in the frequency of CD4+ T, CD8+ T, and DNT cells. Additionally, the absolute number exhibited a significant increase with the incorporation of digestion solution. Therefore, given that mechanical grinding had higher cell yield but lower cell viability, mechanical mincing + enzymatic digestion is a better way to fragment kidney tissue.

High-yield and high-purity isolation of T lymphocytes can be achieved by 33–80% Percoll density gradient centrifugation

To investigate the most efficient method of purifying T lymphocytes after enzymatic digestion, we assessed the ability of four different Percoll density gradients (30–80%, 33–80%, 37–80%, and 40–80%) to enrich T lymphocytes from digested tissue. Tissues were fragmented and digested with 0.2% collagenase and 0.02% DNase I for 45 min, and then purified by four different Percoll density gradient centrifugation or without Percoll gradient centrifugation, respectively. As shown in Fig. 6A, the flow analysis graphs showed a higher proportion of lymphocytes and CD45+ cells in the obtained cell suspension after Percoll gradient centrifugation. The lowest number of renal MNCs (3.29 ± 0.34 × 106 MNCs per gram) was obtained at 40%-80% Percoll gradient. Meanwhile, the absolute number of kidney MNCs after 30%-80% and 33%-80% Percoll gradient centrifugation was significantly higher than that of the other two groups, and the proportion of 7AAD+ cells in each group ranged from 15% to 20% (Fig. 6B,C). As shown in Fig. 6D and 6Ea, flow cytometry analysis showed that the proportion of CD3+ T cells and NKT cells in 7AAD-CD45+ cells after 33%-80% Percoll gradient centrifugation was significantly higher than those after other Percoll gradient centrifugation. Furthermore, a comparison of the proportions and absolute numbers of CD4+ T cells, CD8+ T cells, and DNT cells in the CD3+NK1.1− T cells at different Percoll gradients was shown in Fig. 6Eb–G. We observed progressively lower proportions of CD4+ T cells in the 30%-80%, 33%-80%, and 37%-80% Percoll gradient centrifugation, which was opposite to the change in the proportion of CD8+ T cells, with no statistically significant difference in DNT proportions among the three groups. Percoll gradient centrifugation of 33%-80% resulted in higher absolute numbers of enriched CD4+ T cells, CD8+ T cells and DNT cells compared to the other groups. These data suggested that the 33%-80% Percoll gradient centrifugation is optimal for maximizing the enrichment of kidney immune cells.

The proportions of renal T lymphocytes during mice kidney diseases

By isolating and purifying renal T lymphocytes with an optimal protocol, we also examined the proportion of T lymphocytes in different kidney disease models. We established three renal disease models including severe IRI, renal fibrosis model, and cisplatin-induced AKI. As shown in Fig. 7A,B, both the severe IRI model and the cisplatin-induced AKI model showed renal tubular injury such as tubular necrosis, cast formation, and tubular dilatation, which were accompanied by a significant increase in SCr and BUN. In the renal fibrosis model, HE staining and Masson’s trichrome staining showed features such as collagen fiber proliferation in the renal interstitium, tubular atrophy, and inflammatory cells infiltration (Fig. 7A). As expected, the levels of SCr and BUN were not different between sham and renal fibrosis model because of the remaining functional kidney (Fig. 7B). In the renal IRI model, immunohistochemistry and flow cytometry analysis showed a significant decrease in the proportion and number of CD4+ T cells in CD3+NK1.1− cells, in contrast to the changes in DNT cells, while there were no significant differences observed in the CD8+ T cell population (Fig. 7C–E). Then, we also observed the proportions and absolute numbers of CD8+ T cells and CD4+ T cells decreased, but DNT cells increased significantly in the cisplatin induced AKI mice (Fig. 7C,D,F). This suggests that DNT cells may play a crucial role in regulating the process of AKI. In addition, we performed immunohistochemical staining of CD45, CD3, CD4 and CD8 in the kidney tissues of normal control mice and post-IRI 2W mice. As shown in Fig. 7C, CD45+ cells, CD3+ cells, CD4+ cells, and CD8+ cells in fibrotic renal tissue markedly increased compared with that in sham mice, while the percentages and numbers of DNT cells substantially decreased (Fig. 7D,G). It indicates that DNT cells are significantly involved in the repair mechanisms and fibrotic processes following AKI. Meanwhile, we also digested human healthy renal samples through this method. Through flow cytometry analysis, Fig. 7D clearly showed different clusters of renal lymphocytes. The proportions of CD4+ T cells, CD8+ T cells, DNT cells in CD3+ cells were 49.70 ± 3.23%, 26.93 ± 3.39%, and 20.93 ± 2.05% in human kidney tissues respectively.

Complementary scRNA-seq analysis using public datasets

To validate the reliability of our cell extraction method, we analyzed single-cell RNA sequencing datasets derived from healthy mice and human kidneys. After meticulous quality control and filtering procedures, we classified the total cell population into distinct clusters representing the major cell types of the kidneys, as corroborated by well-established marker genes for each cell type (Supplementary Fig. S2A, S2D). Next, we isolated the cells labeled as T cells and performed further dimensionality reduction and clustering (Supplementary Fig. S2B, S2E). By leveraging the "CellMarker2.0" database and the expression profiles of key T cell marker genes, we identified three major T cell subpopulations. CD4+ T cells are marked by the genes Cd4 and CD4. CD8+ T cells are marked by the genes Cd8a and CD8A. DNT cells are marked by the gene Ikzf2 or IKZF220. The proportion of each T cell subset was then quantified, revealing that CD4+ T cells comprised 43.72% (470 cells), CD8+ T cells accounted for 30.33% (326 cells), and DNT cells represented 25.95% (279 cells) in mouse kidneys (Supplementary Fig. S2G). In human kidneys, the corresponding proportions were 57.43% (2248 cells) for CD4+ T cells, 23.74% (929 cells) for CD8+ T cells and 18.83% (737 cells) for DNT cells (Supplementary Fig. S2H). These proportions closely mirrored the results obtained through flow cytometry, thereby providing robust single-cell sequencing-level confirmation of the effectiveness of our cell extraction method.

Discussion

Renal T cells and their subsets play pivotal roles in diverse kidney diseases. Hence, reliable and efficient isolation of kidney MNC populations is essential for studying the immunological problems associated with kidney diseases. Herein, we describe a reliable and efficient method for the isolation of immune cells from kidney tissue. A major advance of this study is that we performed simultaneous, systematic optimization of four critical components: tissue fragmentation, collagenase IV concentration, DNase I concentration and digestion time, and Percoll density gradients. Previous studies have generally focused on single-parameter optimization, which frequently compromises yield, viability, and marker stability. Our analyses identified that 33%-80% Percoll gradient centrifugation subsequent to mechanical mincing and digestion with 0.2% collagenase IV plus 0.02% DNase I for 45 min yields the optimal number of viable MNCs with intact surface marker expression. Compared with traditionally reported methods4,13, our protocol can achieve a 1.3 ~ 1.8‐fold higher yield of viable MNCs, improve cell viability by approximately 10%, and reduce the degradation of CD4 and CD8 surface markers.

In collagen fiber digestion, higher collagenase concentrations damage cytomembrane integrity and reduce cell viability; a study showed collagenase inhibited peripheral blood mononuclear cells’ natural killer activity by 25 ± 4% and viability by 11 ± 2% at 3 h21. Van Damme N et al. found collagenase reduces CD25, L-selectin, αEβ7 and α4β7 expression on T lymphocytes22. This study showed 0.2% collagenase IV was optimal for yield-viability balance, while 0.5% increased 7AAD+ cells (potential damage) and diminished CD4/CD8 detection. DNase I degrades released genomic DNA to prevent clumping23, insufficient concentration biases surface marker detection. Here, 0.02% DNase I enhanced MNCs yield without affecting viability. Optimal digestion time was 45 min: insufficient digestion increases aggregates, over-digestion reduces viability and increases debris24, and prolonged isolation activates innate immune cells, biasing data interpretation25. As reported, mechanical tissue dissociation inevitably causes cellular damage26. Grinding produces stronger mechanical forces than mincing, leading to severe and irreversible cell injury24. In line with the above results, our data verified that grinding obtained a higher MNCs yield but markedly elevated 7AAD⁺ cell ratio, reflecting poor cell viability. Overall, mechanical mincing combined with enzymatic digestion is a more effective approach to isolate viable renal MNCs than grinding-based digestion protocols.

Percoll is a widely used density gradient centrifugation medium in cell biology, consisting of colloidal silica particles coated with polyvinylpyrrolidone and characterized by low viscosity and negligible cytotoxicity27. Percoll is extensively employed for the isolation of immune cells from diverse tissues. Following tissue digestion, Percoll density gradient centrifugation effectively removes cellular debris, thereby significantly enhancing lymphocyte purity and viability28,29. However, the Percoll density gradients reported in different studies for the purification of renal immune cells vary considerably, including configurations such as 36%-72%, 37.5%, and 40%-80%13–15. In this study, we evaluated a total of four Percoll density gradients for cell isolation: 30%-80%, 33%-80%, 37%-80%, and 40%-80%. The results indicated that the 33%–80% Percoll gradient provides an optimal density window for MNCs separation. Lower-density gradients (30%-80%) cause more tissue debris and parenchymal cell contamination, while higher-density gradients (37%-80%, 40%-80%) lead to excessive loss of viable MNCs. In contrast, the 33%-80% gradient effectively removes dead cells and debris while maximally preserving CD4+ T cells, CD8+ T cells, and DNT cells with high purity and minimal loss.

Public scRNA-seq datasets were analyzed as a complementary, independent reference to illustrate the physiological proportions of T cell subsets in mouse and human kidneys. The consistent subset distribution between our flow cytometry data and public scRNA-seq profiles supports the reliability of our isolation method, though this bioinformatic analysis does not represent direct functional validation of the protocol. Additionally, this protocol was applied to three murine renal disease models—IRI, renal fibrosis, and cisplatin-induced AKI—to explore dynamic changes in T lymphocyte populations. The proportion of renal DNT cells increased significantly in AKI models but decreased in the renal fibrosis model; in contrast, CD4+ T cells exhibited the opposite trend, a finding consistent with previously published studies4,9–11,30,31. These findings underscore the pivotal immunomodulatory role of T cells in the progression of acute and chronic kidney diseases. Importantly, the demonstrated applicability of our protocol to human tissues supports translational research efforts, enabling detailed profiling of the immune landscape which may aid in the discovery of novel therapeutic targets.

This optimized protocol offers clear practical advantages for renal immunology research. It enables the robust and reproducible isolation of T lymphocytes from both mouse and human kidneys, with high yield, good viability, and preserved surface marker expression. By standardizing previously variable procedures, this method reduces experimental variability and facilitates a more accurate characterization of renal immune landscapes in health and disease status. These technical improvements enhance its utility for mechanistic studies and translational research in AKI and CKD.

However, several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, our validation was primarily restricted to mouse models of AKI and early-stage fibrosis. The efficacy of this protocol in advanced fibrotic kidneys remains to be evaluated; such severe pathological changes may alter tissue stiffness and enzymatic accessibility, potentially necessitating further optimization of digestion conditions. Second, this study focused exclusively on T lymphocytes; the applicability of this protocol to other immune cell subsets (e.g., macrophages and neutrophils) warrants further investigation. Third, the application of this protocol to human kidney tissues was only validated in a very small cohort of histologically normal-appearing renal tissue specimens, which were obtained from patients with upper urinary tract urothelial carcinoma undergoing radical nephroureterectomy. Although macroscopically and histologically non-tumorous regions were carefully selected, these tissues cannot be regarded as completely normal human kidney parenchyma in a strict physiological sense, and may still be affected by the adjacent tumor microenvironment. Fourth, the protocol has not been tested in clinical samples from patients with AKI or CKD. Therefore, future studies are recommended to enroll larger patient cohorts with diverse clinical backgrounds, including acute and chronic kidney injury samples, to further verify the broader applicability and clinical translational value of this optimized protocol. Finally, although we observed changes in T-cell subsets in kidney disease models, the detailed mechanisms, especially for DNT cells, remain unclear. Future studies will further explore these mechanisms to provide more biological insights into renal immune regulation.

Conclusions

We have established a systematically optimized protocol for isolating and purifying T cells from mouse and human kidneys. This optimal strategy is characterized by mechanical mincing combined with 0.2% collagenase IV and 0.02% DNase I digestion for 45 min, followed by 33%-80% Percoll density gradient centrifugation. The 33%-80% Percoll gradient achieves the best balance between lymphocyte enrichment and purity. This method achieves high cell yield and viability while preserving surface markers. Crucially, the protocol’s robustness was validated across multiple murine disease models and human tissues. These findings provide a reliable, standardized tool that is expected to advance research on renal immunity and the pathogenesis of kidney diseases, with significant potential for both preclinical and clinical applications.
