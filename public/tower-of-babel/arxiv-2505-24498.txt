Efficient Neural and Numerical Methods for High-Quality Online Speech Spectrogram Inversion via Gradient Theorem
Andres, Juan, Çağdaş, Jesus

Abstract
Recent work in online speech spectrogram inversion effectively combines DL with the GT to predict phase derivatives directly from magnitudes. Then, phases are estimated from their derivatives via least squares, resulting in a high quality reconstruction. In this work, we introduce three innovations that drastically reduce computational cost, while maintaining high quality: Firstly, we introduce a novel neural network architecture with just 8k parameters, 30 times smaller than previous state of the art. Secondly, increasing latency by 1 hop size allows us to further halve the cost of the neural inference step. Thirdly, we propose a linear-complexity solver for the least squares step that leverages tridiagonality and positive-semidefiniteness, achieving a speedup of several orders of magnitude. We release samples online.

2.1
Online Spectrogram Inversion via Gradient Theorem

Consider the

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial\omega}\Arg(Y_{y,\varphi_{\lambda}}(\omega,t))

\displaystyle=-\lambda\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\log\lvert Y_{y,\varphi_{\lambda}}(\omega,t)\rvert

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\Arg(Y_{y,\varphi_{\lambda}}(\omega,t))

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\lambda}\frac{\partial}{\partial\omega}\log\lvert Y_{y,\varphi_{\lambda}}(\omega,t)\rvert+2\pi\omega

Assuming knowledge of magnitudes, this provides dense information about phases. It is also particularly useful for

In this setting,

3.2
Reducing CNN Computation by Increasing Latency

The arithmetic cost of running the
CNN
is proportional to the number of frequency bins and time frames.
Typically, lowering frequency and time resolution decreases output quality, exposing a trade-off between computation and performance.
In this section, we show that
it is possible to keep full quality while halving computation by introducing a look-ahead frame
.

In a nutshell, the idea is to run a modified version of the
CNN
only once every 2 frames (
. . .
,
\tau_{\text{-}2}
,
\tau_{0}
), but training the network to also provide outputs for the skipped frames (
. . .
,
(\tau_{\text{-}3},\tau_{\text{-}2})
,
(\tau_{\text{-}1},\tau_{0})
).
This can be implemented with the following minimal changes, marked with

\bigstar
in Figure
\xspace
2
:

i)
In the stem, the temporal stride of the initial convolution is set to 2, effectively skipping every other frame.

ii)
In the head, the last two convolutions are modified to produce 2 output channels, one for the skipped frame and one for the current frame.
This allows to restore the original number of frames, so second stage and training work the same way as with the unmodified
CNN
.

With this technique,
CNN
computation is roughly halved (0.14 GMAC/s) without affecting memory (8.57k parameters) or output quality (Figure
\xspace
4(b)
).
The tradeoff is that the refreshing rate of the online processing pipeline is also halved, thus latency is increased by one hop size
4
4

4

More generally,
k
hops correspond to
\frac{1}{k\!+\!1}
speedup.
.
This technique is suited for scenarios where a large hop size is hindering performance, or latency is not as important as energy or computation.

3.3
Orders of Magnitude Faster Second Stage

Equation
\xspace
11
solves a linear system in the form
{\bm{A}}{\bm{z}}\!=\!{\bm{b}}
for
{\bm{A}}\!=\!{\bm{\Lambda}}_{\tau_{0}}\!+\!{\bm{D}}_{\tau_{0}}^{\mathsf{H}}{\bm{\Gamma}}_{\tau_{0}}{\bm{D}}_{\tau_{0}}
and
{\bm{b}}\!=\!{\bm{\Lambda}}_{\tau_{0}}({\bm{Y}}[\omega,\tau_{\text{-}1}]\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{0.8}{$\odot$}}}\mathfrak{v}_{\tau_{0}})
.
In general, solvers require
\mathcal{O}(\kappa(L+1)^{2})
arithmetic for some
\kappa\in\{1,\makebox[10.00002pt][c]{.\hfil.\hfil.},L+1\}

[
35
, ch. 6]
.
Here, we observe that the matrix
{\bm{A}}
is tridiagonal, since
({\bm{\Lambda}}_{\tau_{0}}\!\in\!\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{(L+1)\times(L+1)},{\bm{\Gamma}}_{\tau_{0}}\!\in\!\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{L\times L})
are diagonal and:

\displaystyle{\bm{D}}_{\tau_{0}}^{\mathsf{H}}{\bm{\Gamma}}_{\tau_{0}}{\bm{D}}_{\tau_{0}}=

\displaystyle\sum_{l=1}^{L}\gamma_{l}(\bar{d}_{l}{\bm{e}}_{l}\!+\!{\bm{e}}_{l+1})(d_{l}{\bm{e}}_{l}\!+\!{\bm{e}}_{l+1})^{\top}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{l=1}^{L}\gamma_{l}\big(|d_{l}|^{2}\underbrace{{\bm{e}}_{l}{\bm{e}}_{l}^{\top}}_{\text{diag.}}+\underbrace{{\bm{e}}_{l+1}{\bm{e}}_{l+1}^{\top}}_{\text{diag.}}\big)

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\sum_{l=1}^{L}\gamma_{l}d_{l}\underbrace{{\bm{e}}_{l+1}{\bm{e}}_{l}^{\top}}_{\text{subdiag.}}+\sum_{l=1}^{L}\gamma_{l}\bar{d}_{l}\underbrace{{\bm{e}}_{l}{\bm{e}}_{l+1}^{\top}}_{\text{superdiag.}}

(12)

where
\gamma_{l}\!\coloneq\![{\bm{\Gamma}}_{\tau_{0}}]_{l,l}
and
d_{l}\!\coloneq\![{\bm{D}}_{\tau_{0}}]_{l,l}\!=\![\text{-}\mathfrak{u}_{\tau_{0}}]_{l}
are scalar entries, and
{\bm{e}}_{l}\!\in\!\{0,1\}^{L+1}
is the
l^{\textnormal{th}}
standard basis vector.
Crucially, tridiagonal linear systems can be solved in
\mathcal{O}(L)
arithmetic and memory
[
36
, 4.3.2]
, which is optimal in the sense that we process
L\!+\!1
individual entries.
Additionally,
{\bm{A}}
is positive semi-definite, since it is the sum of a nonnegative diagonal and a Gram matrix.
This allows us to solve Equation
\xspace
11
via more efficient methods like Thomas’ Algorithm
[
36
, 4.3.6]
.
Last but not least, Equation
\xspace
12
provides a recipe to directly construct the diagonals of
{\bm{A}}
.
These changes lead to a solver that is
orders of magnitude faster than other matrix-free or direct solvers (Figure
\xspace
3
), while remaining exact (Figure
\xspace
4(a)
)
.

5
Limitations and Future Work

In this work we introduced three innovations on top of the framework from
[
21
]
in order to achieve drastic reduction in computation, while maintaining low latency and high quality.
While results are good for our settings, other window and hop sizes, as well as enhanced/inconsistent spectrograms, remain to be evaluated.
It would also be informative to incorporate subjective metrics via participatory studies.
Including noisy phase information as in
[
45
]
, and further cost reduction via downsampling of frequency dimension, may also be explored.
Our proposed second stage is particularly amenable for differentiable implementations, paving the way for single-stage, end-to-end approaches, including extensions of the loss function (e.g.
\xspace
by using
({\bm{\Lambda}},{\bm{\Gamma}})
as
\ell_{2}
regularizers) and ideas from
[
46
]
.
