Introduction (FIRST DRAFT DONE)

Recent observations from JWST, thanks to its unprecedented sensitivity and resolution, have been able to probe the most distant galaxies ever observed, offering a direct glimpse into the first stages of galaxy formation and evolution. These observations have also been fundamental to study the process of cosmic reionization (EoR) between
\sim 300
million and 1 billion years after the Big Bang (i.e., approximately between redshift
14
and
6
), during which the ultraviolet (UV) light emitted by the first stars and galaxies reionized the surrounding neutral hydrogen in the intergalactic medium (IGM).

A critical observational diagnostic to understand early galaxy evolution and the reionization mechanism is the UV slope, denoted as
\beta
. The UV slope quantifies the spectral energy distribution (SED) of a galaxy in the UV range, which is dominated by the emission of massive O/B-type main sequence stars. Defined by the relation
f_{\lambda}\propto\lambda^{\beta}
, where
f_{\lambda}
is the flux density at wavelength
\lambda
,
\beta
(typically a negative value) provides essential insights into the physical properties of galaxies, including the dust content and the stellar population properties. Indeed,
\beta
is primarily sensitive to the presence of dust, which can redden the SED and result in a less negative or even positive UV slope (Meurer et al. 1999, Calzetti et al., 2000, Wilkins et al., 2016). However,
\beta
is also directly influenced by the age and metallicity of the stellar populations within a galaxy (see e.g. Bouwens et al. 2012, Tacchella et al. 2022). For example, younger and more metal-poor galaxies tend to have bluer (more negative) UV slopes, reflecting the predominance of hot, massive stars. Furthermore,
\beta
is also a crucial quantity to infer in an indirect way the fraction of Lyman continuum photons escaping from a galaxy (f
esc
, Mascia et al. 2023, Chisholm et al. 2021), whose direct estimation becomes prohibitive at
z>4
as due to the increasingly high opacity to the ionizing radiation of the IGM.

The extensive imaging campaigns conducted by recent surveys with JWST NIRCam have made possible to assemble a large statistical sample of photometrically selected galaxies at redshifts
\geq 4
, and to directly measure their UV slope from the photometry. Recent studies have investigated the evolution of the (photometry-based) UV slope across cosmic time until the EoR (e.g., Topping et al. 2022, Cullen et al. 2023a, Nanayakkara et al. 2023, Austin et al. 2024, Nanayakkara et al. 2024, Roberts-Borsani et al. 2024), finding a decreasing trend of
\beta
toward higher redshifts, potentially reaching the lower theoretical limit of
\sim-2.6
at
z\geq 10
, indicative of minimal or negligible dust attenuation. Moreover, some JWST studies have hinted at the possibility of extremely low
\beta
values dropping below
-3
in some galaxies at these early cosmic epochs (e.g., Atek et al. 2023; Austin et al. 2023), even though these observations may be affected by photometric uncertainties and by the limited number of photometric bands used for the
\beta
estimation.

Meanwhile, spectroscopic observations with JWST NIRSpec have enabled the confirmation of an increasing number of galaxies in the EoR. These observations have paved the road to a statistical investigation of their physical properties from emission lines, including star formation rates (Calabro et al. 2024), ionization properties (Reddy et al. 2023, Sanders et al. 2023), and ISM metallicities (Curti et al. 2023, Nakajima et al. 2023, Sanders et al. 2024). The NIRSpec spectra also allow for the first time to derive
\beta
estimates that are independent from those obtained from the photometry alone.

Measuring the UV slopes from the spectra has several advantages.
First, we can precisely define the wavelength range for the fit, for example following the original range between
1250
and
2750
Årest-frame defined by Calzetti et al. (1994), in order to exclude the Lyman break on the bluer side and the Balmer break on the redder side.
Secondly, we can identify and mask bright UV rest-frame emission lines, which might cause systematic deviations from the true UV slope of the stellar continuum up to
0.5
-
0.6
, as shown by Austin et al. (2024).
Finally, we can check in the spectra for the presence of Lyman-
\alpha
damping wing absorptions redward of Ly-
\alpha
, which can yield a redder UV slope if the bluest photometric band includes a substantial region close to the Ly-
\alpha
line.

In this work, we assemble a large spectroscopic sample of galaxies at
z>4
to study the evolution of the spectroscopic beta during (and close to) the reionization epoch, and compare the results with previous studies based on photometry alone. To this aim, we put together JWST NIRSpec observations available from multiple surveys, including the CEERS and GLASS early release surveys (Finkelstein et al. 2023, Treu et al. 2023), the JADES survey (Eisenstein et al. 2023), and all other public surveys whose spectra have been reduced and made available through the DAWN JWST Archive

Methodology (TBD)

2.1
Spectroscopic observations

We first collected JWST NIRSpec observations available from the CEERS and GLASS Early Release Science (ERS) surveys (Finkelstein et al. 2023, Treu et al. 2023). The GLASS-JWST ERS Program (PID 1324, PI: Treu; Treu et al. 2022) targeted galaxies residing over the central regions of the Frontier Field galaxy cluster Abell 2744. Observations were taken in high-resolution grating mode with three spectral configurations (G140H/F100LP, G235H/F170LP, and G395H/F290LP), covering a wavelength range from 1.5 to
5.14\mu
m at
R\sim 2000-3000
, and with a total integration time (per grating) of 4.9 hours. Since GLASS-JWST observations target sources behind a lensing cluster, we initially adopt the magnification factors (
\mu
) listed by Mascia et al. (2023), based on the latest lensing model of Bergamini et al. (2023). Later on, in order to avoid considering additional uncertainties on our parameter estimates arising from magnification, we will eventually discard those galaxies from our analysis. Nonetheless, they provided an initially test-sample to calibrate our analysis procedure and steps.

We also consider the spectra obtained by the CEERS survey through the NIRSpec MSA mode. The CEERS spectroscopic data were acquired over 3 different observing epochs and in 8 different pointings in the CANDELS Extended Groth Strip (EGS) field. The NIRSpec pointings named p4, p5, p7, p8, p9, and p10 were observed with the three NIRSpec medium resolution (G140M, G235M, and G395M) gratings, covering the observed-frame wavelength range
(0.97-5.10)\mu
m with a resolving power
R\sim 1000
. In addition, p4, p5, p8, p9, p11, and p12 were also observed with the low-resolution prism (
R\sim 100
), which are the ones we will use in this work. In all cases, the exposure time is of
3107s
per spectrum. Three-shutter slitlets with a three-point nodding pattern were also used to enable accurate background subtraction.

We then further increased our sample by adding sources from the JWST Advanced Deep Extragalactic Survey (JADES), a collaboration of the Near-Infrared Camera (NIRCam) and Near-Infrared Spectrograph (NIRSpec) Instrument Development Teams. The JADES strategy divided the survey time between two tiers: medium-depth and deep observations (for the least deep and widest tier of the NIRSpec GTO see Maseda et al. 2024). All observations are in the two GOODS fields (Great Observatories Origins Deep Survey; Giavalisco et al. 2004), but the medium tier is divided between the GOODS South and North fields (hereafter, GOODS-S and GOODS-N), whereas the deep tier is in GOODS-S only. The JADES NIRSpec MOS data uses the low-resolution prism as well as several gratings, with grating spectra available for most of the prism targets.

Lastly, we complete our final sample by adding additional galaxies taken from the DAWN JWST Archive (DJA), an online repository containing reduced images, photometric catalogs, and spectroscopic data for public JWST data products. The DJA spectroscopic archive (DJA-Spec) is, at the time of writing, comprised of observations taken from some of the large Early Release Science (ERS), General Observer (GO) and Guaranteed Time (GTO) Cycle 1 & 2 programs. All data processing is performed with the publicly available Grizli (Brammer 2023a) and MSAExp (Brammer 2023b) software modules, and released for use by anyone.

The total sample of galaxies selected is 1679 galaxies. Other than the GLASS program, also some others listed in the DJA repository present a high level of magnification due to gravitational lensing effects. These programs are the one targeting the
z=11.1
MACS0647-JD galaxy which is strongly lensed by a foreground cluster to form three images (PI D. Coe), the RX J2129 galaxy-cluster field which provides observations of a strongly lensed supernova (PI P. Kelly), and the SN H0pe program which provides independent measurement of
H_{0}
by the time delay of a multiply-imaged supernova (B. Frye).

Since we prefer to avoid considering additional uncertainties on our parameter estimates arising from magnification, we discarded all those galaxies from our analysis. We furthermore exclude the pearls-transients program since it focuses on JWST/NIRSpec confirmation of
z\sim 3
transients found by JWST/NIRCam.

To further check our total sample, we performed matching procedures between each galaxy, with a matching parameter of angular separation
\leq 1arcsec
. This was useful since many galaxies added from DJA were already present in other programs that surveyed the same fields as targets, like GOODS South and North which were also sampled in JADES. With this additional step, we obtain a sample where no galaxies have been sampled more than once.

Considering also the fact that many source files with the spectra data were not available in the release data set, we then obtained a total sample of 1137 galaxies. This includes also the 15 GLASS galaxies initially obtained, which were later discarded again because of magnification-arising uncertainties, further restricting our sample to 1122 galaxies which are free from relevant magnification.

2.2
Spectral slope evaluation

The rest-frame UV continuum slope for each galaxy in our sample can be characterised by a power-law of the type
f_{\lambda}\propto\lambda^{\beta}
, as modelled by
Calzetti et al. 1994
. Since we used the spectra, we could precisely define the boundaries of the wavelength range that we wanted to consider; as a UV band, we considered the rest-frame wavelength range
1270\AA<\lambda<2600\AA
. The lower value of
1270\AA
was extended from the original value of
1250\AA
of the
Calzetti et al. 1994
paper, and it has been chosen mainly to avoid possible biases to the slope caused by Lyman break features (
912\AA
). The higher value of
2600\AA
was instead defined to avoid the spectral feature characterized by the Balmer break (
3646\AA
).

The spectra considered in this UV portion, still presents many spectral features that disturb the continuum and can introduce systematic deviations from the true value of its slope. Additionally, we then further restricted the portion of the spectra utilized to determine
\beta
, in 3 different ways. In the first method we considered only the spectra inside the 10 windows defined by
Calzetti et al. 1994
, to avoid the presence of many stellar and interstellar absorption features, which affect the fitting procedure and artificially alter the shape of the continuum. The 10 windows have been designed to remove from the fitting procedure all the relevant absorption features and the
2175\AA
dust feature. The second method we utilized was to consider the full spectra in the UV range, as defined in our work as the rest-frame wavelength range
1270\AA<\lambda<2600\AA
. To prevent biases on the slope estimate, we decided to subtract from the considered spectra just 4 narrow bands (of wide
20\AA
), centred around these 4 main emission lines that we detected in most of our sample galaxies: Si (
1400\AA
), CIV (
1550\AA
), OIII (
1666\AA
) and CIII (
1909\AA
). Lastly, as a last, third method, we adopted, instead of the whole spectra inside the 10 Calzetti windows (as for the first method), only the mean value points inside each window, resulting in 10 total points.

By studying the correlations between the 3 methods just presented, we concluded that the last, third method had a scatter of values significantly higher compared to the 1st and 2nd methods. We then discarded considering just the mean value points inside each Calzetti window, and we kept using in our analysis only the first two methods.

If we express the spectra in a log-log setup, the power-law relation turns into a simpler and more easy to estimate linear relation, of the type
\log f_{\lambda}=\beta\log\lambda+q
. Of these 2 parameters, we will focus on discussing only
\beta
, since it is the one that contains the most physical information, compared to the intercept q. To estimate the parameter
\beta
, we then fit this linear relation into the portions of the UV log-log spectra, based on which one of the 3 methods previously discussed we choose to use. As a fitting technique, we once more use 2 different procedures.

The first consists of a polynomial fit using the SciPy function
scipy.optimize.curve_fit
, which uses non-linear least squares. Optimal values for the parameters
\beta
(and q) are obtained so that the sum of the squared residuals is minimized. The uncertainty on
\beta
originating from the fit is computed as a standard deviation.

The second procedure consists of a Monte Carlo fit. To achieve this, we ran 500 simulations, wherein each of one we randomly perturbed each value of the spectra with a perturbation scale (standard deviation of the distribution) equal to the error associated with that spectra element. The random perturbation within the error scale is done with the NumPy function
numpy.random.normal
. The final estimate on
\beta
(and q) are taken by performing the median among the 500 values produced with the simulations. The uncertainty on
\beta
originating from the fit is again computed as a standard deviation.

We performed various consistency checks to show potential biases and evaluate the precision and accuracy of both polynomial and Monte Carlo fits. As a general result, we obtain that the uncertainties related to the polynomial fits are smaller than those associated with the Monte Carlo fitting, of about 1 order of magnitude. This result seems to remain in place even by changing some parameters values, like for example the number of simulations of the Monte Carlo approach. At the same time, even if the polynomial fit seems more precise, by visually inspecting the fits to a sample of spectra, we found that in general the Monte Carlo fit is slightly more accurate and representative of the true continuum shape in the region of the spectra we studied. Lastly, it’s important to note that since the Monte Carlo procedure involves simulations and averages while the polynomial one doesn’t have any variability, the parameters estimated with the Monte Carlo can slightly change each time the analysis is run, while with the polynomial fit estimates always the same number for each parameter involved (starting from
\beta
and
\beta_{err}
). Considering this, we continued performing our following analysis using both polynomial and Monte Carlo fitting procedures, but the plots and tables reported in this paper will show the results using the polynomial approach to avoid the slight change of results that characterizes the Monte Carlo fitting procedure at each analysis run.

2.3
Final sample selection

We now briefly describe additional selections on our sample, which have the aim of increasing the robustness of our
\beta
estimates, and their accuracy with the real UV slopes. The final sample we will get after the discussion in this subsection will be the sample used in the rest of our work.

2.3.1
AGN identification and removal

A galaxy which is also an AGN has significantly different spectra than a galaxy which doesn’t show any AGN features. Since we are interested in the UV spectra of regular galaxies, we identify and remove from our sample galaxies which shows AGN properties. The JWST, since its first results, has revealed a significant number of AGN in the early universe (e.g., Harikane et al. 2023b; Larson et al. 2023; Greene et al. 2023; Goulding et al. 2023; Maiolino et al. 2023; Kocevski et al. 2023). While optimal identification methods and the nature of some objects have yet to reach consensus, we follow the procedure described in Roberts-Borsani et al. 2024, which we briefly explain here. Other than removing all AGN objects identified in the literature (i.e., objects identified as AGN in Harikane et al. 2023b, Larson et al. 2023, Greene et al. 2023, Goulding et al. 2023, Maiolino et al. 2023, Kocevski et al. 2023), additional AGN were flagged via visual inspection based on whether a two-component (i.e., broad plus narrow) model was clearly required to reproduce the
H\alpha
profile when the
[OIII]\lambda\lambda 4960,5008
Å  profile was well-fit by a single, narrow component (characteristic of Type 1 AGN). After applying this procedure, we found and removed from our sample a total of 25 AGN, obtaining a total sample size of 1097 galaxies (see column "#GALAXIES" in Table

2.3.2
Conditions on
\beta_{err}
and
S/N

Visually inspecting the spectra of the galaxies in our sample with their UV portion fitted both with the Monte Carlo and polynomial procedures, not only leads to the conclusions already mentioned in section
2.2
regarding the different behaviour of precision and accuracy between the two procedures, but is also useful to further reduce our sample by checking the consistency between the estimated slope and the actual slope of the UV spectra and maintaining only those galaxies where the estimated slope can reproduce correctly the real slope. First, we notice that for approximately 50 galaxies, the spectra in the UV range seem absent or not detected correctly (we consequently remove these galaxies from our sample). For many other galaxies, additional spectral features and noises (both intrinsic and derived from the detection layout) can furthermore alter the estimated
\beta
from the real slope of the UV region of the spectra itself.

There are 2 parameters whose values correlate with this effect. The first one is the uncertainty on
\beta
estimated during the fitting procedure; for both the Monte Carlo and the polynomial fit the uncertainties are computed as a standard deviation. We found the value of
\beta_{err}\approx 1
as a good threshold value between an accurate description of the slope and a value which is not close to the actual slope (while keeping the sample statistical size sufficiently large). As already mentioned in section
2.2
, there is an order of magnitude difference between the uncertainty on
\beta
estimated with the Monte Carlo compared with the one which uses a polynomial procedure: we have
\langle\beta_{MC,err}\rangle\sim 2.25
, while
\langle\beta_{poly,err}\rangle\sim 0.28
. Applying alone a threshold of
\beta_{err}\approx 1
discard away
\sim 36\%
of galaxies with the highest
\beta_{err,MC}
, while leaving unchanged the samples defined with
\beta_{poly}
(only
\sim 1.1\%
of the galaxies has
\beta_{poly}>1
).

The second parameter is not connected with the fit itself but it’s intrinsic to the spectra. A higher noise and variability of the spectra, which is commonly quantified as signal-to-noise ratio S/N, will surely deviate the estimated
\beta
from the real value, regardless of the precision of the fitting procedure. We estimate S/N values by performing mediums and standard deviations of the spectra inside our defined UV range (but excluding the 4 main emission lines regions described in section
2.2
that we identified). We found a mean value of
\langle S/N\rangle\sim 4.68
; by imposing a minimum value of
S/N\approx 3
as a good threshold to discriminate between spectra that are too noisy to be fitted correctly and those whose spectra allow for a robust estimate of
\beta
, we exclude from our sample galaxies with the lowest S/N ratio, which accounts for
\sim 30\%
of the total sample.

To resume, we notice that applying the cut
\beta_{err}\leq 1
has a greater impact on the
\beta
s estimated using the Monte Carlo procedure. In turn, the cut
S/N\geq 3
has an equivalent impact on the
\beta
s estimated using the polynomial procedure, since the condition
\beta_{err}\leq 1
didn’t discard them before. In the end, using both the conditions
\beta_{err}\leq 1
and
S/N\geq 3
leaves us with approximately the same galaxy sample, regardless of the procedure used for the fit (although the polynomial procedure leaves us with approximately
\sim 100
galaxies more); this final sample, after all the selections are done, allow us to have the confidence that the estimated
\beta
s are robustly close to the real UV slope of the galaxies in the sample itself. In Table
1
, column "#SELECTED GALAXIES", is reported this final sample of 733 galaxies obtained by applying the condition
\beta_{err}<1
to the slopes estimated using the polynomial procedure and the condition
S/N>3
.

2.4
Stacking methods

For the stacking procedure, we adopted the same methodology as already used in our previous works (Calabro +22a, +22b). In detail, we first converted all the spectra of XBGs to rest-frame using the spectroscopic redshift. We then normalized them to the median flux calculated between XX and XX, and resampled them to a common grid of XX Å per pixel. Finally, for each pixel, we median averaged all the fluxes after a
3\sigma
clipping rejection. The stacked noise spectrum was instead derived with a bootstrap resampling procedure.

When traversing the IGM in the Epoch of Reionization (EoR), Ly
\alpha
photons from the source galaxy are absorbed by neutral hydrogen along the line-of-sight to the observer. This, combined with attenuation from the Ly
\alpha
forest bluewards of
1216
Å  (which is though far from the wavelength range we use to determine
\beta
), produces the characteristic red-skewed Ly
\alpha
damping wing. The most widely used prescription of the Ly
\alpha
damping wing is presented in Miralda-Escudé (1998). This model assumes that the IGM has a constant (volume-averaged) neutral hydrogen fraction,
x_{HI}
, between the source redshift,
z_{gal}
, and that the end of reionization occurs at
z_{Re}
. Recent comparisons to theorized damping wing profiles assume instead a more realistic patchy reionization process (e.g. Keating et al. 2023).

In addition, Damping Lyman
\alpha
(DLA) systems with high column densities of neutral hydrogen
N_{HI}>2\cdot 10^{20}cm^{-2}
(Lanzetta 2000), arising from dense gas clouds in the vicinity of starburst galaxies, have been observed at high-redshift both in quasar (Totani et al. 2006) and galaxy spectra (Heintz et al. 2023a). In these systems, the central region of the absorption profile is completely saturated. At the same time, the Lorentzian wings take on a fundamental role in the shape of the profile since they are present until large distances from the central wavelength. The UV
\beta
slopes of these systems are then often biased red due to the reduction in flux around and redwards of
\approx 1250
Å. To check for this potential bias in our spectroscopic sample, we perform the following procedure. When calculating
\beta
using the first method presented in
2.2
(considering the flux inside the ten
Calzetti et al. 1994
windows), we additionally define
\beta_{DLA}
as the slope calculated with the flux inside the
Calzetti et al. 1994
windows except the first two. When using instead the second method (considering the entire flux in
1270\AA<\lambda<2600\AA
range, except around four main emission lines), we calculate
\beta_{DLA}
by restricting the range to
1342\AA<\lambda<2600\AA
. The aim of calculating also
\beta_{DLA}
other than
\beta
is to estimate the UV slope excluding the region of the spectra that can potentially be affected by flux reduction due to DLA systems Lorentzian wings.

To present our results, we will focus only on the first method. Our variable of interest is the difference
\beta-\beta_{DLA}
; if in the range
1270\AA<\lambda<1342\AA
the flux is slightly reduced,
\beta_{DLA}
will result bluer than
\beta
, and therefore the difference
\beta-\beta_{DLA}
will be positive. To further correlate this variable with the physical interpretation we’re giving, we study
\beta-\beta_{DLA}
as a function of redshift. To see if there’s a substantial value change around the EoR, we divide our sample along a redshift threshold value of
z_{th}=7.5
. To have consistency in our analysis we keep in our sample only galaxies with
S/N>3
,
\beta_{err}<1
, and
M_{UV}<-17.6
, consisting of a total sample of 522 galaxies.

The first interesting result is that, for any bins of
z
defined, the medium value is always
\beta-\beta_{DLA}>0
, with a medium point for the entire sample
\langle\beta-\beta_{DLA}\rangle_{tot}=0.047\pm 0.002
.

Furthermore, between
4<z<6
the medium value is around
\langle\beta-\beta_{DLA}\rangle_{4<z<6}=0.033\pm 0.006
, while for
z>7.5
the medium value is
\langle\beta-\beta_{DLA}\rangle_{z>7.5}=0.053\pm 0.009
. To see if this increase around
z_{th}
is due to statistical effects or instead highlights a different population distribution (i.e. one with a different medium point), we perform a two-sample Kolmogorov-Smirnov test. The null hypothesis is that the two distributions are identical,
F(x)=G(x)
for all x (with x here being
\beta-\beta_{DLA}
), with the alternative being that they are not identical. We obtain a p-value of 0.025; if we choose a
2\sigma
confidence level, a p-value less than 0.05 rejects the null hypothesis in favour of the alternative. The data seems to be not drawn from the same distribution, indicating that above
z>7.5
they seem to have a higher value of
\beta-\beta_{DLA}
(which means that for those galaxies, not including the part of the spectra closest to a potential DLA system feature generate an even bluer
\beta
than at lower redshifts). We repeat the KS test after increasing
z_{th}
from 7.5 to 8,
S/N
from 3 to 5, and both simultaneously. In all three cases we get a p-value greater than 0.05, indicating that the distribution is instead the same (we have to keep in mind though that in all these three last cases the number of high redshift galaxies,
z>z_{th}
, is smaller than the first test). In Figure
3
we show
\beta-\beta_{DLA}
vs z having imposed
z_{th}=7.5
and
S/N>5
.

Finally, we highlight an asymmetry in the distribution of
\beta-\beta_{DLA}
vs z. For
z<z_{th}
, we notice that there are more galaxies with
\beta-\beta_{DLA}<-0.2
(reaching values of -1), than there are respective galaxies with
\beta-\beta_{DLA}>-0.2
(omitting for simplicity the slightly positive medium value of
\langle\beta-\beta_{DLA}\rangle
). This asymmetry though seems to drastically reduce by applying the cut
M_{UV}<-17.6
, with the number of galaxies with
\beta-\beta_{DLA}<-0.2
passing from 31 to 12. Additionally, increasing the
S/N
from 3 to 5 further reduces this effect. Considering those variations, and the fact that it’s an effect mostly present when performing the fitting through the polynomial procedure instead of the Monte Carlo one, we conclude that it’s an asymmetry mostly caused by the fitting method and its accuracy, and not by a physical reason.

In the end, since much is still to be determined about Damping Lyman
\alpha
systems, we conclude that it is likely that some of our sources are biased red, however, the extent of this in our spectroscopic sample is largely unknown.

Extremely blue galaxies

5.1
Selection

We define extremely blue galaxies (XBG) as those systems with a UV spectral slope lower than
-2.6

This threshold corresponds to the theoretical lower limit expected for young stellar populations in case of zero dust attenuation and maximum contribution from the nebular continuum, assuming also that no ionizing photons escape from the galaxy (Robertson +10, Cullen +17, Reddy +18, Topping et al. 2022). This limit is also in agreement with observations of dust-poor local star-forming galaxies (Chisholm et al. 2022).
In our sample, we find
51

-1.8

\pm 0.5
