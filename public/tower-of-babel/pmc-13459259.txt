A novel homozygous variant causing fatal neonatal adenylosuccinate lyase (ADSL) deficiency presenting with respiratory failure and encephalopathy
Authors: Keisha Wolfe, Laura Armstrong, Caroline Wambach, Eyby Leon, Katelinn Ohrtman, Ilana Miller, Tucker Pyle, Jason Schroeder, Jamie Fraser, Youssef Kousa
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractNeonatal adenylosuccinate lyase (ADSL) deficiency is a rare neurodegenerative disorder that is associated with epileptic encephalopathy, diffuse hypotonia, and respiratory failure. Loss of enzymatic function of ADSL leads to toxic buildup of succinylaminoimidazole carboxamide riboside (SAICAr) and succinyladenosine (S-Ado). We describe a female term neonate who presented with respiratory failure and encephalopathy. Neuroimaging revealed microencephaly, a simplified gyral pattern, and diffuse white matter edema. Genetic testing showed novel homozygous loss of function mutations of ADSL, the adenylosuccinate lyase gene, consistent with fatal neonatal ADSL deficiency. We find this to be one of less than a dozen cases reported of the fatal neonatal phenotype of ADSL deficiency. The case highlights ADSL deficiency as a rare cause of neonatal encephalopathy and respiratory failure. Clinical recognition of epileptic encephalopathy, pertinent findings on MRI, and testing for S-Ado can confirm the diagnosis and support discussions about goals of care.Keywords: ADSL Deficiency, Neonatal epileptic encephalopathy, Neurometabolic disorder, Neonatal seizures

Background

We identified a term neonate who suffered from neonatal encephalopathy, seizures, and respiratory failure due to ADSL deficiency. The case highlights how the combination of whole exome sequencing, MRI, EEG, and fluid testing for S-Ado are needed to confirm this rare diagnosis and guide difficult goals of care discussions. Our case adds important information about types of abnormal movements, seizure semiology, and continuous EEG findings in fatal neonatal ADSL deficiency.

Case report

A neonate was born at 40 weeks and 5 days gestation via Caesarean-section due to non-reassuring fetal heartrate. The pregnancy had otherwise been uncomplicated. After birth, the infant was noted to have a normal cord gas pH of 7.33. At birth, her weight was 2870 g (21st percentile for age), her length was 48 cm (27th percentile for age), and her head circumference measured 34 cm (54th percentile for age). Over the first two to three weeks of life her head circumference did not grow, but instead fell into the microcephalic range, measuring 33.5 cm at three weeks old (0.7th percentile for age). Family history was notable for consanguinity of the parents (first cousins), maternal history of two prior miscarriages, a paternal uncle who died at two years old due to respiratory difficulties, and a paternal aunt who died at one month of age. Social history revealed that the infant’s family immigrated from Afghanistan.

At delivery, the neonate was noted to have a weak cry, hypotonia, and central cyanosis. She was given respiratory support via continuous positive airway pressure (CPAP) with improvement and was transitioned off respiratory support to room air. However, she then began to have oxygen desaturations with seizure-like activity, which was described as right upper extremity clonic jerking, bilateral myoclonic jerks, bilateral leg extension, and bicycling-like leg movements. Treatment with benzodiazepines and phenobarbital did not stop these movements. The patient’s initial electroencephalography (EEG) was negative for seizures but showed mild to moderate encephalopathy. She required re-initiation of CPAP and eventually required endotracheal intubation.

On exam, she was noted to have encephalopathy, appendicular, axial and facial hypotonia, weak deep tendon reflexes, weak suck reflex, absent gag reflex, reduced spontaneous movements, and minimal reaction to tactile stimulation. Her appearance was notable for down-slanting epicanthal folds, temporal muscle wasting, a posteriorly rotated and malformed right ear, and a single palmar crease of the left hand. She also had decreased respiratory drive leading to recurrent apnea that required endotracheal intubation.

Her initial work–up, including cerebral spinal fluid (CSF) cell counts, CSF amino acids, acylcarnitine profile, free and total carnitine, plasma amino acids, urine organic acids, beta-hydroxybutyrate, and very long chain fatty acids, was unremarkable. Brain magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) was significant for microencephaly with decreased volumes of the bilateral cerebral hemispheres and brainstem (particularly the pons), a shortened corpus callosum, a simplified gyral pattern, and diffuse white matter edema. Magnetic resonance spectroscopy showed a low N-acetylaspartate (NAA) peak and high choline peak but no discernable glycine or lactate peaks (Fig. 1). Her clinical picture was concerning for a neurogenetic disorder, so a rapid whole exome sequence (WES) was performed.

Her initial EEG, continuously monitored for two days, revealed excessive multifocal sharp wave discharges and excessive discontinuity with interburst intervals of 8–30 s. There was evidence of state changes, overall consistent with mild to moderate encephalopathy (Fig. 2A-B). There were no seizures on the study and no EEG correlate for the myoclonic jerks or trembling movements. She developed new tonic posturing with head tilting on day of life (DOL) 11. Repeat EEG demonstrated numerous electrographic seizures with onset from the bifrontal regions (R > L), multifocal sharp wave discharges, and burst suppression background suggestive of severe diffuse cerebral dysfunction and evolving epileptic encephalopathy (Fig. 2C-D). There was no EEG correlate for the tonic posturing. She required phenobarbital and fosphenytoin for seizure control.

Due to decreased respiratory drive and apneic episodes, the patient continued to require increasing respiratory support, from CPAP to non-invasive positive pressure ventilation to intubation by DOL 4. Ultrasound of her diaphragm demonstrated symmetric but impaired motion. She remained intubated for the remainder of hospitalization. Patient’s treatment was complicated by malrotation of the small intestine that required an exploratory laparotomy, hypotension requiring prolonged vasoactive support, and bilateral hydroureters with transient urinary retention.

On DOL 22, her rapid whole exome sequencing identified homozygous variants of uncertain significance (VUS) in ADSL and KIF7 and one heterozygous likely pathogenic variant in NDUFS2 (Table 1).

A heterozygous variant in NDUFS2 (likely pathogenic) is consistent with carrier status of the autosomal recessive mitochondrial complex I deficiency, nuclear type 6 but was not consistent with the patient’s clinical presentation. Variants in KIF7 can cause a spectrum of autosomal recessive ciliopathies that are associated with well-characterized brain imaging findings that were not seen in this patient, making them likely incidental in this context. Biallelic variants in ADSL cause autosomal recessive ADSL deficiency, which is consistent with this patient’s clinical presentation. The variant reported in this patient is a novel missense variant, not typically observed in healthy populations, although there has been limited sequencing of Afghani populations. As ADSL deficiency was consistent with the clinical presentation, urine purine and pyrimidines were evaluated. Results showed high excretion of adenosine and succinyladenosine, confirming the diagnosis of ADSL deficiency (Table 1). After discussion with the family about the prognosis and expected clinical course of ADSL deficiency, the patient was compassionately extubated on DOL 33.

Discussion

ADSL deficiency is an extremely rare metabolic disorder (incidence ~ 0.00125/100,000) [1]. The disorder is categorized into three distinct phenotypes based on severity and age of presentation. The fatal neonatal form, consistent with our case, represents the most severe manifestation and is characterized by hypotonia, respiratory failure requiring mechanical ventilation, intractable seizures, and early death within the first weeks of life [2–4]. There may also be associated prenatal signs and symptoms, including growth restriction, hypokinesia, and minimal heart rate variability [5]. Type I ADSL deficiency (severe form) typically presents within the first months of life with severe neurodevelopmental disability, early-onset seizures, and acquired microcephaly [2, 4]. Type II ADSL deficiency (mild/moderate form) typically presents in the first few years of life and has a more indolent course with mild to moderate neurodevelopmental disability, difficulty maintaining eye contact and processing speech and visual stimuli, and later, a less severe seizure disorder [2, 4].

The fatal neonatal form of ADSL deficiency is rare. To our knowledge, less than a dozen cases have been reported in the English literature [4–10]. Of note, a few previous cases of severe Type I have overlapping features with our case, including severe epilepsy, encephalopathy, feeding difficulties, and hypotonia present. However, Type I may not have evidence of prenatal onset, severe respiratory distress or early lethality.

Mouchegh et al. described a series of six infants with the fatal neonatal form of ADSL, all of whom had an abnormal fetal heart tracing during labor. Four of six neonates presented with congenital microcephaly, and two of six neonates developed microcephaly after birth [5]. Our patient’s birth similarly was complicated by abnormal fetal heart rate leading to caesarean delivery. The infant had evidence of microencephaly on imaging and developed early postnatal microcephaly. At birth, the fatal neonatal form presents with hypotonia and respiratory distress, complicated by frequent and progressive apnea; required respiratory support following birth varied from non-invasive to mechanical ventilation support. Our neonate had respiratory failure and apnea. Unlike other cases, however, our imaging showed poor diaphragm function, which has not been reported previously.

Although seizures are an almost universal feature of all types of ADSL deficiency, the specific EEG findings and seizure types are variable and have not been well-described, especially in the fatal neonatal form [11]. Seizure semiology has been described as myoclonic, focal and generalized tonic-clonic, and apneic [6–9]. This case is the first to describe detailed EEG findings in the fatal neonatal form and is further strengthened by having multiple EEG studies in the same patient. Evolution of the EEG over time has been described in cases of Type I and Type II ADSL deficiency, including severe Type I which can present in the neonatal period [11]. However, many of the EEG findings are nonspecific and are neither helpful for distinguishing ADSL deficiency from other causes of epileptic encephalopathy nor for distinguishing the fatal neonatal form from Type I or Type II. Abnormal background activity, including burst suppression as seen in our case, is a frequent finding in ADSL deficiency as well as other epileptic encephalopathies. Even the most severe background abnormalities are not unique to the neonatal form and can be seen in Type I ADSL deficiency, making the diagnosis and discussion of prognosis difficult. Interestingly, there may be a relative frontal predominance to the epileptic discharges in ADSL deficiency, possibly correlating with the MRI findings in the frontal lobes [11]. Our patient had focal seizures emanating from the bifrontal region, consistent with these previously reported findings. Abnormal movements are a prominent feature of the fatal neonatal form, and can include opisthotonus, myoclonus, tremors, tongue twitching and hiccups [3, 7, 10, 11]. In our case, myoclonus, trembling, and posturing movements are consistent were confirmed to be non-epileptic on continuous EEG. The later development of both new abnormal movements and new electrographic seizures is likely reflective of the progressive nature of ADSL deficiency.

The diffuse abnormalities seen on brain MRI imaging in ADSL deficiency are consistent with the indiscriminate damage to all elements of brain tissue confirmed by pathology [12]. Supratentorial atrophy and white matter abnormalities can be seen in all types of ADSL deficiency and can be progressive [11–13]. However, abnormal gyrification (pachygyria, immature gyration) has only been described in cases of fatal neonatal and severe type I ADSL deficiency [3, 5, 14]. Our case adds further support for the theory put forth by Mouchegh et al. that dysfunction may start prenatally in the most severe cases [5].

ADSL deficiency results from an inherited defect of purine metabolism due to decreased functional activity of the enzyme ADSL. The ADSL enzyme normally catalyzes two pathways of purine nucleotide metabolism: de novo purine synthesis, catalyzing conversion of succinylaminoimidazole carboxamide ribotide into aminoimidazole carboxamide ribotide, and the purine nucleotide cycle, catalyzing the formation of adenylate from adenylosuccinate during the conversion of inosine monophosphate into adenine nucleotides [2, 15]. Deficiency of ADSL blocks these pathways resulting in an increase in succinylaminoimidazole carboxamide riboside (SAICAr) and succinyladenosine (S-Ado). [13] Biochemically, ADSL deficiency can be identified by elevated levels of SAICAr and S-Ado in biologic fluids such as plasma, urine, and CSF [15, 16]. While the underlying pathophysiology of ADSL deficiency is not well understood, one of the leading hypotheses is that the accumulating SAICAr and its metabolites have neurotoxic effects caused by loss of ADSL function [5, 17, 18]. Diagnosis of ADSL deficiency requires demonstration of SAICAr and S-Ado in urine, CSF, or serum and identification of a mutation in the ADSL gene [3, 15, 16, 19]. In cases with novel variants or while awaiting confirmatory testing, the diagnosis can be supported by evidence of typical clinical and imaging findings.

Inheritance of ADSL deficiency is autosomal recessive [2, 19]. Most of the identified pathogenic variants are missense mutations. Current findings indicate that the phenotypic severity reflects the degree of structural stability and residual enzyme activity of the corresponding ADSL enzyme [2, 19]. Our patient’s novel homozygous ADSL c.1325 A > C; p.(H442P) missense variant, was initially designated a variant of unknown significance. However, based on the clinical exam, prominent white matter changes on MRI, evidence of evolving epileptic encephalopathy on EEG, and ultimately confirmatory urine metabolites, we were able to diagnosis this patient with ADSL deficiency and give family timely prognostic information. The variant was not observed at significant frequency in large population cohorts (gnomAD) and has not been previously published as pathogenic or benign.

Guidelines for clinical variant interpretation protocols published by the AMP (Association for Molecular Pathology) and ACMG (American College of Medical Genetics) include population-level information (e.g. allele frequency in population databases), among other data (e.g., functional evidence) to guide decisions on whether to designate a variant as benign, likely benign, likely pathogenic, pathogenic, or uncertain significance. For variants detected in European populations, the genomic knowledge base is robust and reliable enough such that the absence of a variant from population databases can be considered evidence for pathogenicity. However, there is currently insufficient genomic sequencing data from the Afghan population to make a similar determination for our patient. Thus, in silico methods were unsuccessful at identifying how this missense variate might alter protein structure/function.

The prognosis for ADSL deficiency varies significantly based on the phenotype. The fatal neonatal form invariably leads to early death possibly due to respiratory failure and severe encephalopathy from the build of the toxic intermediates [2, 5]. Currently, no specific treatment exists for the neonatal form of ADSL deficiency given progressive severity of symptoms. Therapies such D-ribose and ketogenic have been trialed in type I and type II without sufficient and consistent benefit [20, 21]. Management remains primarily supportive, focusing on seizure control. We recommend shared decision making with families for goals of care particularly in the rapidly progressing neonatal form.

This case of ADSL deficiency shows a novel genetic mutation and underscores the importance of including comprehensive neuroradiologic, neurometabolic and genetic screening, and multidisciplinary medical management in neonates presenting with early-onset encephalopathy or seizures, particularly when accompanied by an abnormal neurologic exam or abnormal EEG background. Early genetic diagnosis along with ancillary tests such as EEG and MRI enables appropriate counseling regarding prognosis and appropriate targeted therapeutic approaches.
