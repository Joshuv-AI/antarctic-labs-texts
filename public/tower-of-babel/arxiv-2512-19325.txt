High dimensional matrix estimation through elliptical factor models
Xinyue Xu, Huifang Ma, Hongfei Wang, Long FengSchool of Statistics and Data Science, LEBPS, KLMDASR, LPMC, Nankai University, School of Statistics, Data Science, Nanjing Audit University

Abstract
Elliptical factor models play a central role in modern high-dimensional data analysis, particularly due to their ability to capture heavy-tailed and heterogeneous dependence structures. Within this framework, Tyler’s M-estimator ( 35 ) enjoys several optimality properties and robustness advantages. In this paper, we develop high-dimensional scatter matrix, covariance matrix and precision matrix estimators grounded in Tyler’s M-estimation. We first adapt the Principal Orthogonal complEment Thresholding (POET) framework ( 13 ) by incorporating the spatial-sign covariance matrix as an effective initial estimator. Building on this idea, we further propose a direct extension of POET tailored for Tyler’s M-estimation, referred to as the POET-TME method. We establish the consistency rates for the resulting estimators under elliptical factor models. Comprehensive simulation studies and a real data application illustrate the superior performance of POET-TME, especially in the presence of heavy-tailed distributions, demonstrating the practical value of our methodological contributions. keywords : Covariance matrix estimation; High dimensional data; Spatial-sign; POET.

Introduction

Estimating the covariance matrix is a fundamental problem in multivariate statistical analysis. Accurate estimation plays a crucial role in numerous tasks such as principal component analysis (PCA), clustering, and discriminant analysis. When the data dimension
d
is fixed and the sample size
n
is large, the sample covariance matrix performs efficiently under sub-Gaussian assumptions. However, as the dimensionality grows and
d
becomes comparable to or even larger than
n
, the sample covariance matrix becomes unstable and non-invertible. To address this issue, a substantial body of research has proposed various regularized or sparse covariance estimators, leveraging structural assumptions such as sparsity in the true covariance matrix
(
4
;
5
;
7
;
8
;
32
)
. See an overview in
12
.

Most of the aforementioned methods for covariance matrix estimation rely on the assumption that the covariance matrix is sparse. While this assumption is plausible in many practical settings, it does not always hold. For instance, financial assets often share common market factors, leading to strong correlations among their returns. Similarly, genes within the same biological pathway may be co-regulated by a limited number of transcriptional factors, resulting in highly correlated gene expression patterns. Moreover, when genes are stimulated by cytokines, their expressions tend to exhibit strong co-movements. In such scenarios, the sparsity assumption becomes clearly unrealistic. To address the limitations of the sparsity assumption,
13
proposed an approximate factor model and developed the Principal Orthogonal complEment Thresholding (POET) framework. The POET estimator decomposes the covariance matrix into a low-rank component, capturing the pervasive dependence driven by a few common factors, and a sparse idiosyncratic component that represents weak local correlations. This unified framework encompasses several popular estimators as special cases, including the sample covariance matrix, the factor-based covariance matrix, the thresholding estimator, and the adaptive thresholding estimator. Despite its theoretical and computational appeal, the original POET procedure is constructed based on the sample covariance matrix, which can be highly sensitive to heavy-tailed distributions and outliers. Consequently, subsequent research has focused on developing robust extensions of the POET framework, such as those based on rank-based or M-estimation approaches, to enhance performance under non-Gaussian or contaminated data settings (see, e.g.,
14
).

Elliptical distributions have become a central modeling tool in modern multivariate analysis due to their ability to capture heavy-tailed behavior, tail dependence, and deviations from Gaussianity. They are widely used in financial return modeling
(
40
;
18
)
, gene expression data analysis
(
3
;
38
)
, and neuroimaging studies
(
19
)
. When the dimension is fixed, numerous robust estimators of the scatter matrix are available for elliptical distributions, including Tyler’s M-estimator
35
, the spatial-sign covariance matrix, and multivariate Kendall’s tau estimators
(
25
)
, all of which enjoy desirable properties such as affine equivariance and robustness to heavy tails.

However, in high-dimensional regimes where the dimension exceeds the sample size, classical robust scatter estimators become singular, non-invertible, or computationally unstable, which necessitates new approaches. Considerable effort has been dedicated to developing regularized scatter and covariance matrix estimators under elliptical models. For sparse covariance estimation,
34
introduced a regularized Tyler’s M-estimator, and
17
further enhanced this approach via thresholding techniques.

Parallel to Tyler-based methods, robust high-dimensional scatter estimation has also progressed through the study of spatial-sign covariance matrices, which naturally encode directional information and possess strong robustness properties. Recent contributions include linear shrinkage estimators
(
30
;
29
;
26
;
27
)
specifically designed for high-dimensional settings, as well as sparse precision matrix estimators constructed from spatial-sign covariance matrices
(
23
)
. These works demonstrate that combining sign-based methods with regularization can yield stable and interpretable estimators even under heavy-tailed distributions.

For nonsparse high-dimensional elliptical structures, factor-based modeling has emerged as a powerful alternative.
14
generalized the POET framework to elliptical factor models by leveraging the multivariate Kendall’s tau matrix, providing a robust means of extracting latent low-rank dependence. More recently,
10
proposed an idiosyncratic-projected self-normalization method based on the spatial-sign covariance matrix, which offers improved performance under heavy-tailed noise and complex dependence structures.

In this paper, we extend the POET framework to the setting of spatial-sign covariance
matrices for nonsparse high-dimensional factor models, named as POET-SS. We first establish the consistency rates of the proposed estimators under mild regularity conditions. Although the spatial-sign covariance matrix is robust to heavy-tailed distributions, its nonsparse structure leads to a approximation bias with respect to the true covariance matrix, which may affect the efficiency of the resulting estimators. To remedy this issue, we further incorporate Tyler’s M-estimator by using the spatial-sign covariance estimator as an initial input, and propose a new
two-step procedure, termed POET-TME. Simulation studies and real data
analysis show that the proposed POET-TME method achieves the best overall
performance for high-dimensional elliptical factor models.

This paper is organized as follows. Section 2 introduces the POET-SS method and presents the corresponding theoretical results. Section 3 develops the POET-TME procedure. Section 4 reports the simulation studies, and Section 5 provides a real data application. All technical proofs are collected in the Appendix.

\displaystyle\|\hat{{\bm{V}}}_{0u}-{\bm{V}}_{0u}\|_{\max}=O_{p}(w_{n})=\|\hat{{\bm{V}}}_{0}-{\bm{V}}_{0}\|_{\max},~~\text{and}

\displaystyle\|\hat{{\bm{V}}}_{0u}-{\bm{V}}_{0u}\|_{2}=O_{p}(w_{n}^{1-v}M_{d})=\|\hat{{\bm{V}}}_{0}-{\bm{V}}_{0}\|_{2}.

2.3
The number of spiked eigenvalues estimation

Recalling the spiked structure of
{\bm{\Sigma}}_{0}
given in Assumption
1
, by Theorem
1
and Wely’s Theorem, we have

\displaystyle\lambda_{j}(\hat{{\bm{\Sigma}}}_{0})=\begin{cases}c_{j}d,&~~\text{if}~~1\leq j\leq m,\\
c_{j}dw_{n},&~~\text{if}~~m+1\leq j\leq d,\end{cases}

with probability tending to one, where
c_{j}
’s are some positive constants. Then we estimate the number of spiked eigenvalues
m
via the following eigenvalue ratio or growth ratio,

\displaystyle\hat{m}_{ER}=\mathop{\arg\max}\limits_{1\leq j\leq M}\frac{\lambda_{j}(\hat{{\bm{\Sigma}}}_{0})}{\lambda_{j+1}(\hat{{\bm{\Sigma}}}_{0})},~~\hat{m}_{GR}=\mathop{\arg\max}\limits_{1\leq j\leq M}\frac{\ln\{1+\lambda_{j}(\hat{{\bm{\Sigma}}}_{0})/V_{j-1}\}}{\ln\{1+\lambda_{j+1}(\hat{{\bm{\Sigma}}}_{0})/V_{j}\}},

(5)

where
M
is a predetermined upper bound of the true
m
, and
V_{j}=\sum_{l=j+1}^{\min(n,d)-1}\lambda_{l}(\hat{{\bm{\Sigma}}}_{0})
.
1
recommended two methods to choose a suitable value for
M
, which are also suitable for our estimators. In the simulation study,
M
is set as
8
but this can be replaced with any other reasonable values. The following theorem show the consistency of
\hat{m}_{ER}
and
\hat{m}_{GR}
.

Theorem 4
.

Suppose the conditions in Theorem
1
hold, then as
\min(n,d)\to\infty
, for
m\leq M\leq\min(n,d)-1
,
\mathbb{P}(\hat{m}_{ER}=m)\to 1
and
\mathbb{P}(\hat{m}_{GR}=m)\to 1
.

3
Large Matrix Estimation based on Tyler’s M-estimator

Although the high-dimensional spatial-sign covariance matrix can serve as a good approximation to the true scatter matrix under mild conditions, a theoretical bias still remains between the two. In contrast, as shown in
35
, the classical Tyler’s M-estimator coincides exactly with the scatter matrix. Motivated by this property, we also extend the POET framework to incorporate Tyler’s M-estimator.

If the location parameter is known,
35
showed that the scatter matrix

\hat{\bm{\Sigma}}
can be estimated by solving the nonlinear equation

\displaystyle\frac{d}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{(\mathbf{X}_{i}-\bm{\mu})(\mathbf{X}_{i}-\bm{\mu})^{\intercal}}{(\mathbf{X}_{i}-\bm{\mu})^{\intercal}\hat{\bm{\Sigma}}^{-1}(\mathbf{X}_{i}-\bm{\mu})}=\hat{\bm{\Sigma}}.

Let
\bm{x}_{i}=\mathbf{X}_{i}-\bm{\mu}
.

35
suggested solving this equation iteratively via

\displaystyle\hat{\bm{\Sigma}}_{k+1}=\frac{d\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{\bm{x}_{i}\bm{x}_{i}^{\intercal}}{\bm{x}_{i}^{\intercal}\hat{\bm{\Sigma}}_{k}^{-1}\bm{x}_{i}}}{\operatorname{tr}\!\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{\bm{x}_{i}\bm{x}_{i}^{\intercal}}{\bm{x}_{i}^{\intercal}\hat{\bm{\Sigma}}_{k}^{-1}\bm{x}_{i}}\right)}.

20
proved that, under mild regularity conditions, this algorithm admits a unique solution and converges globally. The resulting Tyler’s M-estimator (TME) is also the maximum likelihood estimator of the shape matrix for the angular central Gaussian distribution
(
36
)
and for generalized elliptical distributions
(
16
)
.

However, when
d>n
, the classical TME becomes ill-posed. To address this issue,
34
considered the regularized TME
\hat{\bm{\Sigma}}(\alpha)
, defined as the solution to

\displaystyle\hat{\bm{\Sigma}}(\alpha)=\frac{1}{1+\alpha}\frac{d}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{\bm{x}_{i}\bm{x}_{i}^{\intercal}}{\bm{x}_{i}^{\intercal}\hat{\bm{\Sigma}}(\alpha)^{-1}\bm{x}_{i}}+\frac{\alpha}{1+\alpha}\mathbf{I}_{d},

where
\alpha>0
is a regularization parameter. They further proposed the iterative algorithm

\displaystyle\hat{\bm{\Sigma}}_{k+1}(\alpha)=\frac{1}{1+\alpha}\frac{d}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{\bm{x}_{i}\bm{x}_{i}^{\intercal}}{\bm{x}_{i}^{\intercal}\hat{\bm{\Sigma}}_{k}(\alpha)^{-1}\bm{x}_{i}}+\frac{\alpha}{1+\alpha}\mathbf{I}_{d},

which converges rapidly in practice. Based on this estimator,
17
proposed a thresholding estimator for sparse scatter matrices. Although these approaches are computationally efficient, they fundamentally rely on sparsity assumptions, which may lead to inefficiency when the true scatter matrix is nonsparse. Motivated by this limitation, we develop new POET-type estimators based on TME that remain effective in nonsparse settings.

By the Tyler’s M-estimator matrix definition, we proposed a new naive estimator of the scatter matrix
{\bm{\Sigma}}_{0}
as follows

\displaystyle\hat{\bm{\Sigma}}_{T}=\frac{d}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{({\bm{X}}_{i}-\hat{{\bm{\mu}}})({\bm{X}}_{i}-\hat{{\bm{\mu}}})^{\intercal}}{({\bm{X}}_{i}-\hat{{\bm{\mu}}})^{\intercal}\hat{{\bm{V}}}_{S}({\bm{X}}_{i}-\hat{{\bm{\mu}}})}

(6)

where
\hat{{\bm{V}}}_{S}
is the above POET estimators of
{\bm{V}}_{0}={\bm{\Sigma}}_{0}^{-1}
based on spatial-sign covariance matrix, i.e.,
\hat{{\bm{V}}}_{S}=(\hat{{\bm{\Sigma}}}_{0}^{\tau})^{-1}
or
\hat{{\bm{V}}}_{0}
. Theorem
2
and
3
yield

\displaystyle\|\hat{{\bm{V}}}_{S}-{\bm{V}}_{0}\|_{2}=O_{p}(a_{n})~~\text{with}~~a_{n}:=\begin{cases}w_{n}^{1-v}m_{d},&~~\text{if}~~\hat{{\bm{V}}}_{S}=(\hat{{\bm{\Sigma}}}_{0}^{\tau})^{-1},\\
w_{n}^{1-v}M_{d},&~~\text{if}~~\hat{{\bm{V}}}_{S}=\hat{{\bm{V}}}_{0}.\end{cases}

To proceed, we also adopt the POET procedure for the naive Tyler’s estimator
\hat{{\bm{\Sigma}}}_{T}
to obtain the final estimator
\hat{{\bm{\Sigma}}}_{TP}
for
{\bm{\Sigma}}_{0}
, or adopt the CLIME or GLASSO procedures to obtain the final estimator
\hat{{\bm{V}}}_{T}
for
{\bm{V}}_{0}
. The next theorem establishes the convergence rate of the POET-TME estimator
\hat{{\bm{\Sigma}}}_{TP}
and CLIME-TME or GLASSO-TME estimators
\hat{{\bm{V}}}_{T}
.

Theorem 5
.

Under Assumption
1
-
3
and the assumption that
\log d=o(n)
, if
\|\hat{{\bm{V}}}_{S}-{\bm{V}}_{0}\|_{2}=O_{p}(a_{n})
and
a_{n}=O(w_{n})
, then

\displaystyle\|\hat{{\bm{\Sigma}}}_{T}-{\bm{\Sigma}}_{0}\|_{\max}=O_{p}(w_{n}),

\displaystyle\|\hat{{\bm{\Sigma}}}_{TP}-{\bm{\Sigma}}_{0}\|_{{\bm{\Sigma}}_{0}}=O_{p}(d^{1/2}w_{n}^{2}+w_{n}^{1-v}m_{d}),

\displaystyle\|(\hat{{\bm{\Sigma}}}_{TP})^{-1}-{\bm{\Sigma}}_{0}^{-1}\|_{2}=O_{p}(w_{n}^{1-v}m_{d}).

Additionally, if Assumption
4
also holds, we have

\displaystyle\|\hat{{\bm{V}}}_{T}-{\bm{V}}_{0}\|_{\max}=O_{p}(w_{n}),\|\hat{{\bm{V}}}_{T}-{\bm{V}}_{0}\|_{2}=O_{p}(w_{n}^{1-v}M_{d}).

Remark 1
.

A natural idea is to iterate the estimating equation (
6
) by replacing it with

\displaystyle\hat{{\bm{\Sigma}}}_{TP,k+1}^{(0)}=\frac{d}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{({\bm{X}}_{i}-\hat{{\bm{\mu}}})({\bm{X}}_{i}-\hat{{\bm{\mu}}})^{\intercal}}{({\bm{X}}_{i}-\hat{{\bm{\mu}}})^{\intercal}\hat{{\bm{\Sigma}}}_{TP,k}^{-1}({\bm{X}}_{i}-\hat{{\bm{\mu}}})},

(7)

where
\hat{{\bm{\Sigma}}}_{TP,k}
denotes the estimator obtained at the
k
-th step.
After computing the intermediate estimator
\hat{{\bm{\Sigma}}}_{TP,k+1}^{(0)}
,
one may further apply the POET procedure to obtain the refined estimator

\hat{{\bm{\Sigma}}}_{TP,k+1}
. Repeating these two steps yields an iterative algorithm
that in principle can be continued until convergence.

However, our simulation studies indicate that this fully iterative scheme
does not provide substantial improvement over the proposed POET–TME estimator.
In fact, the estimator obtained after the first POET refinement already achieves
a level of accuracy comparable to that of the fully iterated sequence.
This suggests that the primary gain comes from the initial POET correction,
and additional iterations produce only marginal benefits while incurring extra
computational cost. Therefore, in practice, performing this replication step once
is sufficient to obtain a statistically efficient and computationally scalable estimator.

Remark 2
.

10
develop an idiosyncratic-projected self-normalization
method for estimating the scatter matrix.
Intuitively, their approach replaces the initial estimator
{\bm{V}}_{S}
in (
6
)
with the matrix
\hat{\mathbf{P}}^{\intercal}\hat{\mathbf{P}}
, where
\hat{\mathbf{P}}
is a
(d-m)\times d

projection matrix satisfying

\hat{\mathbf{P}}\hat{{\bm{\Gamma}}}_{KED}=0
and
\hat{\mathbf{P}}\hat{\mathbf{P}}^{\intercal}={\bm{I}}_{d-m}
.
Here,
\hat{{\bm{\Gamma}}}_{KED}
denotes the
m
leading eigenvectors of the
multivariate Kendall’s tau matrix.
In essence, the estimator
\hat{\mathbf{P}}^{\intercal}\hat{\mathbf{P}}
is used as a surrogate for
the precision matrix
{\bm{\Sigma}}^{-1}
.
However, this construction focuses solely on the leading
m
principal components
and consequently discards the information contained in the residual component

{\bm{\Sigma}}_{u}
.

As shown in Theorems
2
and
3
, the POET-based estimator
constructed from the spatial-sign covariance matrix provides a more accurate
and stable initial estimator for the scatter matrix, as it retains information
from both the factor structure and the idiosyncratic component.
This highlights the importance of choosing a high-quality initial estimator in
the Tyler-type fixed-point iteration (
6
).
Moreover, the same principle suggests that other well-performing scatter estimators
may also serve as effective substitutes for
\hat{{\bm{V}}}_{S}
in (
6
),
leading to procedures analogous to our proposed method.
Exploring such alternatives may further broaden the applicability and robustness
of POET-enhanced Tyler estimators in high-dimensional heavy-tailed settings.

In addition,
10
employ the Huber estimator to estimate the
location parameter
{\bm{\mu}}
. Although the Huber estimator is well known for its
robustness under heavy-tailed distributions, it is not the most natural choice
for elliptically symmetric distributions. In such settings, the sample spatial
median is widely regarded as a more suitable and efficient estimator of location;
see
(
25
)
.
The spatial median enjoys affine equivariance and high robustness, and it aligns
well with the geometric structure of elliptical distributions.
Therefore, when the underlying model adheres to elliptical symmetry, replacing the
Huber estimator with the sample spatial median may lead to improved statistical
efficiency and better compatibility with scatter estimation procedures based on
self-normalization or spatial-sign transformations.

Furthermore, we also proposed an POET-TME estimator of the covariance matrix
{\bm{\Sigma}}_{{\bm{X}}}:=\mathrm{cov}({\bm{X}}_{i})
. In detail, note that

\displaystyle{\bm{\Sigma}}_{{\bm{X}}}=d^{-1}\mathbb{E}(\xi_{i}^{2}){\bm{\Sigma}}=d^{-1}\mathbb{E}(r_{T,i}^{2}){\bm{\Sigma}}_{0},~~\text{where}~~r_{T,i}:=\|{\bm{V}}_{0}^{1/2}({\bm{X}}_{i}-{\bm{\mu}})\|_{2}.

We estimate the
d^{-1}\mathbb{E}(r_{T,i}^{2})
by the following robust estimator
d^{-1}\widehat{\mathbb{E}(r_{T,i}^{2})}
that solves

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}H_{h}\left(d^{-1}\hat{r}_{T,i}^{2}-d^{-1}\widehat{\mathbb{E}(r_{T,i}^{2})}\right)=0~~\text{where}~~\hat{r}_{T,i}:=\|\hat{{\bm{V}}}_{S}^{1/2}({\bm{X}}_{i}-\hat{{\bm{\mu}}})\|_{2},

H_{h}(x)=\min\{h,\max(-h,x)\}
is the Huber function, and
h
is a tuning parameter. The estimator of
{\bm{\Sigma}}_{{\bm{X}}}
is then simply

\displaystyle\hat{{\bm{\Sigma}}}_{{\bm{X}},T}=d^{-1}\widehat{\mathbb{E}(r_{T,i}^{2})}\hat{{\bm{\Sigma}}}_{T},~~\text{and}~~\hat{{\bm{\Sigma}}}_{{\bm{X}},TP}=d^{-1}\widehat{\mathbb{E}(r_{T,i}^{2})}\hat{{\bm{\Sigma}}}_{TP}.

The next proposition gives the convergence rate of
d^{-1}\widehat{\mathbb{E}(r_{T,i}^{2})}
and
\hat{{\bm{\Sigma}}}_{{\bm{X}},T},\hat{{\bm{\Sigma}}}_{{\bm{X}},TP}
.

Proposition 6
.

Suppose the conditions in Theorem
5
hold, in addition,
\mathbb{E}(|\xi/\sqrt{d}|^{2+\varepsilon})<\infty
for some
\varepsilon\in(0,2)
, and
h=cn^{2/(2+\varepsilon)}
for some constant
c>0
. Then,

\displaystyle\left|d^{-1}\widehat{\mathbb{E}(r_{T,i}^{2})}-d^{-1}\mathbb{E}(r_{T,i}^{2})\right|=O_{p}(w_{n}+n^{-\frac{\varepsilon}{2+\varepsilon}}),

\displaystyle\|\hat{{\bm{\Sigma}}}_{{\bm{X}},T}-{\bm{\Sigma}}_{{\bm{X}}}\|_{\max}=O_{p}(w_{n}+n^{-\frac{\varepsilon}{2+\varepsilon}}),

\displaystyle\|\hat{{\bm{\Sigma}}}_{{\bm{X}},TP}-{\bm{\Sigma}}_{{\bm{X}}}\|_{{\bm{\Sigma}}_{{\bm{X}}}}=O_{p}(d^{1/2}w_{n}^{2}+w_{n}^{1-v}m_{d}+n^{-\frac{\varepsilon}{2+\varepsilon}}),~~\text{and}

\displaystyle\|(\hat{{\bm{\Sigma}}}_{{\bm{X}},TP})^{-1}-{\bm{\Sigma}}_{{\bm{X}}}^{-1}\|_{2}=O_{p}(w_{n}^{1-v}m_{d}+n^{-\frac{\varepsilon}{2+\varepsilon}}).

5
Real data application

The concept of the global minimum variance portfolio (MVP) originates from the seminal work of
24
, who established the foundation of modern portfolio theory. Markowitz showed that, under mean–variance preferences, investors should choose portfolios that optimally balance expected return and risk. Within this theory, the MVP represents a special and fundamental portfolio: it is the portfolio on the efficient frontier that achieves the lowest possible variance among all portfolios of risky assets, regardless of expected returns. In practice, the MVP is widely used in risk management, pension fund allocation, and asset allocation strategies that emphasize capital preservation.

The global minimum variance portfolio aims to minimize the variance of portfolio returns subject to a specified expected return level, which solves

\displaystyle\min\bm{w}^{\intercal}{\bm{\Sigma}}\bm{w},\text{subject to}~~\bm{w}^{\intercal}\bm{1}=1,

where
\bm{w}
is a length
d
vector of portfolio weights, and
\bm{1}
is a length
d
vector of ones.
The minimum variance portfolio has the closed-form solution

\mathbf{w}^{*}=\frac{1}{\mathbf{1}^{{\intercal}}\bm{\Sigma}^{-1}\mathbf{1}}\bm{\Sigma}^{-1}\mathbf{1}.

Under an elliptical model, the same expression remains valid when replacing the covariance matrix by the scatter matrix, that is,

\mathbf{w}^{*}=\frac{1}{\mathbf{1}^{{\intercal}}\bm{\Sigma}_{0}^{-1}\mathbf{1}}\bm{\Sigma}_{0}^{-1}\mathbf{1}.

Therefore, using
\bm{\Sigma}^{-1}
or
\bm{\Sigma}_{0}^{-1}
yields identical minimum variance portfolio weights. Given an estimator of the scatter or covariance matrix, denoted by
\hat{\bm{\Sigma}}_{0}
or
\hat{\bm{\Sigma}}
, the MVP weights are computed as

\displaystyle\hat{\bm{w}}^{*}=\frac{1}{\mathbf{1}^{\intercal}\hat{\bm{\Sigma}}_{0}^{-1}\mathbf{1}}\hat{\bm{\Sigma}}_{0}^{-1}\mathbf{1},\qquad\text{or}\qquad\hat{\bm{w}}^{*}=\frac{1}{\mathbf{1}^{\intercal}\hat{\bm{\Sigma}}^{-1}\mathbf{1}}\hat{\bm{\Sigma}}^{-1}\mathbf{1}.

(8)

Thus, the performance of a covariance or scatter matrix estimator can be assessed by examining the out-of-sample risk of the resulting MVP
(
11
;
22
;
9
;
10
)
.

To evaluate the proposed procedures, we construct MVPs using daily returns of S&P 500 constituent stocks from January 1995 to December 2023. Portfolio weights for each month are obtained from covariance matrices estimated using the daily returns over the preceding 120 months (10 years). Only stocks that have continuously remained in the S&P 500 during this estimation window are included. Using these monthly weights, we compute daily portfolio returns and aggregate them to obtain the annualized risk for each calendar year from 2005 to 2023, enabling a comprehensive comparison of different estimators.

In addition to the proposed POET-TME and POET-SS methods, we include IPSN, FLW, and SAMPLE as competing estimators. The equal-weight portfolio
(1/d,\ldots,1/d)^{T}
, denoted EW, is also reported as a benchmark. The number of factors for each method is determined using the GR procedure, applied separately to the covariance or scatter matrix estimated by that method. In other words, for every estimator under comparison, we first compute its corresponding estimated matrix and then use the GR method to obtain the data-driven estimate of the factor dimension subsequently used in portfolio construction.

To formally assess whether the MVP constructed using POET-TME exhibits statistically lower risk than portfolios based on alternative estimators, we conduct the following one-sided hypothesis test:

\displaystyle H_{0}:\sigma\geq\sigma_{0}\quad\text{vs.}\quad H_{1}:\sigma<\sigma_{0},

(9)

where
\sigma
denotes the standard deviation of the portfolio constructed via POET
-
TME, and
\sigma_{0}
represents that of a competing method. The test is implemented using the methodology of
21
. The standard errors required for the test statistic are estimated using heteroskedasticity- and autocorrelation-consistent (HAC) estimators.

Overall, the results in Table
5
demonstrate that the POET-TME procedure delivers consistently superior out-of-sample performance relative to a wide range of competing covariance and scatter matrix estimators. Across nearly all years from 2005 to 2023, POET-TME attains one of the lowest annualized risks among the minimum variance portfolios, and it exhibits remarkable robustness during both tranquil and turbulent market conditions, including the 2008 financial crisis and the COVID-19 period.

Formally, the one-sided tests based on
21
reveal that the risk of the POET-TME portfolio is significantly lower than that of portfolios constructed using POET-SS, IPSN, FLW, and SAMPLE in many years, as indicated by the frequent appearance of significance markers in Table
5
. These results imply that the efficiency gains of POET-TME are not due to random fluctuations but represent systematic improvements in estimating the underlying scatter structure.

Compared with classical principal-component-based methods or robust scatter estimators, POET-TME effectively integrates factor modeling with Tyler’s M-estimator, yielding scatter estimates that remain stable under heavy-tailed returns while still capturing the low-rank factor structure intrinsic to large financial panels. This balance between robustness and structural efficiency appears to be the key reason why POET-TME achieves substantially lower portfolio risk and demonstrates consistently strong performance across market regimes.

Collectively, the empirical evidence shows that POET-TME is a highly reliable and effective tool for minimum variance portfolio construction, outperforming both traditional and modern competing estimators in terms of realized out-of-sample risk.

6
Conclusion

In this paper, we proposed two high-dimensional matrix estimation procedures—POET-SS and POET-TME—constructed within the POET framework and motivated by the spatial-sign covariance matrix and Tyler’s M-estimator. The POET-SS estimator serves as an effective and computationally efficient initial estimator for POET-TME, providing stable performance even in challenging high-dimensional settings. Building on this foundation, POET-TME demonstrates clear superiority over several existing methods, particularly under heavy-tailed distributions where robust estimation is essential.

The proposed methodology opens several promising directions for future research. One natural extension is to incorporate these robust estimators into classification frameworks, such as linear discriminant analysis
(
41
)
and quadratic discriminant analysis
(
33
)
. Another avenue lies in developing robust procedures for canonical correlation analysis
(
28
)
under elliptical models. Additionally, the robust scatter estimation techniques explored in this work may offer substantial benefits for high-dimensional hypothesis testing problems
(
37
)
. Investigating these applications could further enhance the understanding and utility of Tyler’s M-estimator-based estimators in modern multivariate analysis.

\displaystyle{\bm{U}}_{i}=\frac{{\bm{X}}_{i}-{\bm{\mu}}}{\|{\bm{X}}_{i}-{\bm{\mu}}\|_{2}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}\frac{{\bm{Z}}^{0}}{\|{\bm{Z}}^{0}\|_{2}},

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}(|{\bm{U}}_{i}^{\intercal}{\bm{e}}_{j}|^{2k})\}=\mathbb{E}\left(\left|\frac{\bm{w}_{j}^{\intercal}{\bm{Z}}}{({\bm{Z}}^{\intercal}{\bm{A}}{\bm{Z}})^{1/2}}\right|^{2k}\right)\leq\mathbb{E}\left(\left|\frac{\tilde{\bm{w}}_{j}^{\intercal}{\bm{Z}}}{({\bm{Z}}^{\intercal}{\bm{Z}})^{1/2}}\right|^{2k}\right)=\mathbb{E}\left(\left|\frac{\check{\bm{w}}_{j}^{\intercal}{\bm{Z}}}{({\bm{Z}}^{\intercal}{\bm{Z}})^{1/2}}\right|^{2k}\right),

\tilde{\bm{w}}_{j}=\bm{w}_{j}/\sqrt{\lambda_{d}({\bm{\Sigma}})}

, and the last equality follows from the fact that for any constant

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\left|\frac{\tilde{\bm{w}}_{j}^{\intercal}{\bm{Z}}}{({\bm{Z}}^{\intercal}{\bm{Z}})^{1/2}}\right|>c\right)=\mathbb{P}\left({\bm{Z}}^{\intercal}(\tilde{\bm{w}}_{j}\tilde{\bm{w}}_{j}^{\intercal}-c^{2}{\bm{I}}_{d}){\bm{Z}}>0\right)=\mathbb{P}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{d}l_{i}Z_{i}^{2}>0\right),\text{and}

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\left|\frac{\check{\bm{w}}_{j}^{\intercal}{\bm{Z}}}{({\bm{Z}}^{\intercal}{\bm{Z}})^{1/2}}\right|>c\right)=\mathbb{P}\left({\bm{Z}}^{\intercal}(\check{\bm{w}}_{j}\check{\bm{w}}_{j}^{\intercal}-c^{2}{\bm{I}}_{d}){\bm{Z}}>0\right)=\mathbb{P}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{d}l_{i}Z_{i}^{2}>0\right),

\displaystyle l_{i}=\lambda_{i}(\tilde{\bm{w}}_{j}\tilde{\bm{w}}_{j}^{\intercal}-c^{2}{\bm{I}}_{d})=\begin{cases}\tilde{\bm{w}}_{j}^{\intercal}\tilde{\bm{w}}_{j}-c^{2}=\Sigma_{jj}/\lambda_{d}({\bm{\Sigma}})-c^{2},&~~\text{for}~~i=1,\\
-c^{2},&~~\text{for}~~i=2,\ldots,d.\end{cases}

\displaystyle\|{\bm{U}}_{i}^{\intercal}{\bm{e}}_{j}\|_{\psi_{2}}\leq

\displaystyle\sup_{k\geq 1}(2k)^{-1/2}\sqrt{\frac{\Sigma_{jj}}{\lambda_{d}({\bm{\Sigma}})}}\left\{\mathbb{E}\left(\left|\frac{Z_{j}^{2}}{\sum_{i=1}^{d}Z_{i}^{2}}\right|^{k}\right)\right\}^{\frac{1}{2k}}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle Cd^{-1/2},

for some constants
C>0

Z_{j}^{2}/\sum_{i=1}^{d}Z_{i}^{2}\sim\text{Beta}(\frac{1}{2},\frac{d-1}{2})
and
\{\mathbb{E}(|Z_{j}^{2}/\sum_{i=1}^{d}Z_{i}^{2}|^{k})\}^{\frac{1}{2k}}\leq\sqrt{2k/d}
(see, for example, Lemma B.9 in
19
). In addition, we have
\mathbb{E}({\bm{U}}_{i}^{\intercal}{\bm{e}}_{j})=0
and
\mathrm{var}({\bm{U}}_{i}^{\intercal}{\bm{e}}_{j})=O(d^{-1})
, then, by the Bernstein inequality,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\max_{1\leq j\leq d}\left|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}{\bm{U}}_{i}^{\intercal}{\bm{e}}_{j}\right|\geq t\right)\leq

\displaystyle d\mathbb{P}\left(\left|\sum_{i=1}^{n}{\bm{U}}_{i}^{\intercal}{\bm{e}}_{j}\right|\geq nt\right)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle d\exp\left\{-\frac{nt^{2}/2}{d^{-1}(1+C^{2}t)}\right\}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle d\exp(-3ndt^{2}/8),

for small enough
t\leq(3C^{2})^{-1}
. Setting
t=\sqrt{8(\log d+\log\alpha^{-1})/(3nd)}

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\max_{1\leq j\leq d}|n^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}{\bm{U}}_{i}^{\intercal}{\bm{e}}_{j}|\geq t)\leq\alpha.

Hence,
\max_{1\leq j\leq d}|\zeta_{1}(\mu_{j}-\hat{\mu}_{j})|=O_{p}\{\sqrt{(\log d)/(nd)}\}
.

\displaystyle\left\{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(\frac{\xi_{i}^{2}}{d}\right)^{\frac{2+\varepsilon^{\prime}}{2}}\right\}^{\frac{2}{2+\varepsilon^{\prime}}}\cdot\left\{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(\frac{{\bm{z}}_{i}^{\intercal}{\bm{\Sigma}}{\bm{z}}_{i}}{d}\right)^{\frac{2(2+\varepsilon^{\prime})}{\varepsilon^{\prime}}}\right\}^{\frac{\varepsilon^{\prime}}{2(2+\varepsilon^{\prime})}}\cdot\left\{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(\frac{d}{{\bm{z}}_{i}^{\intercal}{\bm{z}}_{i}}\right)^{\frac{2(2+\varepsilon^{\prime})}{\varepsilon^{\prime}}}\right\}^{\frac{\varepsilon^{\prime}}{2(2+\varepsilon^{\prime})}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle O_{p}(1).

Hence,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}|I_{i}|\leq

\displaystyle\|\hat{{\bm{V}}}_{S}-{\bm{V}}_{0}\|_{2}\cdot\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}d^{-1}r_{i}^{2}+2\|\hat{{\bm{V}}}_{S}\|_{2}\cdot\zeta_{1}\|\hat{{\bm{\mu}}}-{\bm{\mu}}\|_{2}\cdot\max_{1\leq i\leq n}\nu_{i}\cdot\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}d^{-1}r_{i}^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle O_{p}\left(a_{n}+\sqrt{\frac{\log n}{n}}\right).

Similarly, by (
A.10
), we have

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}|II_{i}|=

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{tr}({\bm{\Sigma}})}{d}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{\xi_{i}^{2}}{d}\cdot\left|1-\frac{\|{\bm{z}}_{i}\|_{2}^{2}}{d}\right|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\left\{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(\frac{\xi_{i}^{2}}{d}\right)^{\frac{2+\varepsilon^{\prime}}{2}}\right\}^{\frac{2}{2+\varepsilon^{\prime}}}\cdot\left\{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left|1-\frac{\|{\bm{z}}_{i}\|_{2}^{2}}{d}\right|^{\frac{2+\varepsilon^{\prime}}{\varepsilon^{\prime}}}\right\}^{\frac{\varepsilon^{\prime}}{2+\varepsilon^{\prime}}}=O_{p}(d^{-1/2}).

Therefore, we obtain

\displaystyle\left|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}H_{h}\left(d^{-1}\hat{r}_{T,i}^{2}-d^{-1}\mathbb{E}(r_{T,i}^{2})\right)\right|=O_{p}\left(n^{-\varepsilon/(2+\varepsilon)}+a_{n}+\sqrt{\frac{\log n}{n}}+d^{-1/2}\right).

(D.4)

Step I. Analyze
\tilde{p}_{h}
.

When
\widehat{\mathbb{E}(r_{T,i}^{2})}\leq\mathbb{E}(r_{T,i}^{2})
, we have

\displaystyle\gamma_{i}=

\displaystyle\mathbbm{1}\left(d^{-1}\hat{r}_{T,i}^{2}-d^{-1}\widehat{\mathbb{E}(r_{T,i}^{2})}<h,d^{-1}\hat{r}_{T,i}^{2}-d^{-1}\mathbb{E}(r_{T,i}^{2})>-h\right)

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\mathbbm{1}\left(\left|d^{-1}\hat{r}_{T,i}^{2}-d^{-1}\mathbb{E}(r_{T,i}^{2})\right|\leq\frac{h}{2},d^{-1}\widehat{\mathbb{E}(r_{T,i}^{2})}\geq 0\right)

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle 1-\mathbbm{1}\left(\left|d^{-1}\hat{r}_{T,i}^{2}-d^{-1}\mathbb{E}(r_{T,i}^{2})\right|>\frac{h}{2}\right)-\mathbbm{1}\left(d^{-1}\widehat{\mathbb{E}(r_{T,i}^{2})}<0\right),

where the first inequality follows from the fact that
h=cn^{2/(2+\varepsilon)}\to\infty
and
d^{-1}\mathbb{E}(r_{T,i}^{2})<h/2
. Similarly, when
\widehat{\mathbb{E}(r_{T,i}^{2})}\geq\mathbb{E}(r_{T,i}^{2})
, we have

\displaystyle\gamma_{i}\geq

\displaystyle 1-\mathbbm{1}\left(\left|d^{-1}\hat{r}_{T,i}^{2}-d^{-1}\mathbb{E}(r_{T,i}^{2})\right|>\frac{h}{2}\right)-\mathbbm{1}\left(d^{-1}\widehat{\mathbb{E}(r_{T,i}^{2})}>\frac{h}{2}\right).

Hence,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}(\gamma_{i})\geq 1-\mathbb{P}\left(\left|d^{-1}\hat{r}_{T,i}^{2}-d^{-1}\mathbb{E}(r_{T,i}^{2})\right|>\frac{h}{2}\right)-\mathbb{P}\left(d^{-1}\widehat{\mathbb{E}(r_{T,i}^{2})}<0\right)-\mathbb{P}\left(d^{-1}\widehat{\mathbb{E}(r_{T,i}^{2})}>\frac{h}{2}\right).

(D.5)

Note that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\left|d^{-1}\hat{r}_{T,i}^{2}-d^{-1}\mathbb{E}(r_{T,i}^{2})\right|>\frac{h}{2}\right)\leq\mathbb{P}\left(\left|III_{i}-d^{-1}\mathbb{E}(r_{T,i}^{2})\right|>\frac{h}{6}\right)+\mathbb{P}\left(|II_{i}|>\frac{h}{6}\right)+\mathbb{P}\left(|I_{i}|>\frac{h}{6}\right).

By (
D.3
) and Markov’s inequality, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\left|III_{i}-d^{-1}\mathbb{E}(r_{T,i}^{2})\right|>\frac{h}{6}\right)=O\{h^{-(1+\varepsilon/2)}\}=o(1).

Using similar arguments as those used when analyzing
n^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}|I_{i}|
and
n^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}|II_{i}|
, by Markov’s inequality again, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(|II_{i}|>\frac{h}{6}\right)+\mathbb{P}\left(|I_{i}|>\frac{h}{6}\right)=o(1).

Hence,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\left|d^{-1}\hat{r}_{T,i}^{2}-d^{-1}\mathbb{E}(r_{T,i}^{2})\right|>\frac{h}{2}\right)=o(1),

(D.6)

which implies that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(d^{-1}\hat{r}_{T,i}^{2}-d^{-1}\mathbb{E}(r_{T,i}^{2})<h\right)\geq 1-\mathbb{P}\left(\left|d^{-1}\hat{r}_{T,i}^{2}-d^{-1}\mathbb{E}(r_{T,i}^{2})\right|>\frac{h}{2}\right)\to 1,~~\text{and}

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(d^{-1}\hat{r}_{T,i}^{2}-d^{-1}\mathbb{E}(r_{T,i}^{2})>-h/2\right)\geq 1-\mathbb{P}\left(\left|d^{-1}\hat{r}_{T,i}^{2}-d^{-1}\mathbb{E}(r_{T,i}^{2})\right|>\frac{h}{2}\right)\to 1.

Thus,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\left(h/2-d^{-1}\mathbb{E}(r_{T,i}^{2})\right)\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbbm{1}\left(d^{-1}\hat{r}_{T,i}^{2}-d^{-1}\mathbb{E}(r_{T,i}^{2})<h,d^{-1}\hat{r}_{T,i}^{2}>-h/2\right)>h/4\right)\to 1.

(D.7)

By (
D.1
) and the fact that
d^{-1}\mathbb{E}(r_{T,i}^{2})<h/2
, we have

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}H_{h}\left(d^{-1}\hat{r}_{T,i}^{2}-h/2\right)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}H_{h}\left(d^{-1}\hat{r}_{T,i}^{2}-d^{-1}\mathbb{E}(r_{T,i}^{2})\right)

\displaystyle-\left(h/2-d^{-1}\mathbb{E}(r_{T,i}^{2})\right)\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbbm{1}\left(d^{-1}\hat{r}_{T,i}^{2}-d^{-1}\mathbb{E}(r_{T,i}^{2})<h,d^{-1}\hat{r}_{T,i}^{2}>-h/2\right),

which, combining with (
D.4
) and (
D.7
), yields

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}H_{h}\left(d^{-1}\hat{r}_{T,i}^{2}-h/2\right)<-h/5\right)\to 1.

Similarly, we can prove

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}H_{h}\left(d^{-1}\hat{r}_{T,i}^{2}\right)>h/5\right)\to 1.

Furthermore, by the definition of
d^{-1}\widehat{\mathbb{E}(r_{T,i}^{2})}
and the continuity of
H_{h}(\cdot)
, we obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(0<d^{-1}\widehat{\mathbb{E}(r_{T,i}^{2})}<h/2\right)\to 1,

which, combining with (
D.5
) and (
D.6
), implies
\mathbb{E}(\gamma_{i})=1-o(1)
. Furthermore, by the Markov’s inequality and the fact that
\gamma_{i}\leq 1
, we have

\displaystyle\tilde{p}_{h}=1+o_{p}(1).

Combining the conclusions from the two steps, we get the desired result.
∎
