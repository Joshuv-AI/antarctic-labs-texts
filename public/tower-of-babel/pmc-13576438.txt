Calvarial erosion is a potentially life-threatening condition that requires prompt recognition and intervention. A broad spectrum of pathologies can involve the calvarium, including primary and secondary neoplasms,1,2 inflammatory and metabolic bone diseases,3 congenital abnormalities,4 and iatrogenic processes.5 Careful radiographic evaluation is essential for narrowing the differential diagnosis.2 External mechanical factors can also contribute to calvarial remodeling and erosion, as illustrated in reports of skull thinning after prolonged scalp tissue expansion; however, these mechanisms are typically iatrogenic and closely monitored.6 To the best of our knowledge, atraumatic circumferential erosion of an otherwise intact calvarium secondary to constrictive clothing, such as a headband, has not been reported in the English-language literature.

Although calvarial injuries may occur in any patient population, it is important to recognize that patients with severe mental illness carry a disproportionate burden of physical comorbidities and premature mortality, with preventable medical conditions frequently being underrecognized and undertreated. These vulnerabilities may delay the diagnosis of serious cranial pathology, particularly in institutionalized individuals with impaired self-care and limited collateral history, which may result in more severe presentations of the disease.

This report describes a woman with schizoaffective disorder, cared for at a skilled nursing facility (SNF), who developed circumferential scalp breakdown and complete calvarial erosion due to a chronically worn elastic headband. We aimed to highlight the characteristic imaging findings that facilitated the diagnosis, review the relevant surgical anatomy at risk, particularly the superior sagittal sinus and torcula, and discuss key considerations for operative management, postoperative care, and systems-level awareness in other vulnerable patients.

Illustrative Case

A 45-year-old woman with schizoaffective disorder residing in an SNF was transferred for evaluation of a progressively worsening circumferential scalp wound and acute neurological changes. Approximately 7 months prior to presentation, she sustained an unwitnessed fall with a scalp wound and thereafter wore a very tight black elastic headband that reportedly remained in place for at least 2 months, during which the wound intermittently drained purulent material. She had undergone scalp washout and closure at an outside hospital with acute care surgery 179 days before her presentation, when the defect was reported to extend only to the galea without skull involvement, and was then discharged to the SNF. The plastic and reconstructive surgery team recommended healing by secondary intention and close follow-up in the office, but the band remained in place. Silver nitrate was applied in the office for 3 consecutive weeks, resulting in improvement of the hypertrophied tissue. Her remaining wound care was managed by the nursing staff with mupirocin and sulfasalazine cream. Neglect or facility-level care failure was assumed given her presentation, particularly as no documented notes by the SNF staff expressed concerns for her head wound.

According to her mother, she remained at her neurological baseline until the day before the presentation, when she was found in the bathroom, not conversant or ambulatory, with new bleeding and purulent drainage from her circumferential scalp wound. On arrival to our level I trauma center’s emergency department, she was afebrile but lethargic, with minimal spontaneous speech and difficulty following complex commands. Her vital signs and initial laboratory evaluation revealed mild leukocytosis (white blood cell count of 11,000 cells/µL), raising concerns for sepsis in the setting of a chronically infected cranial wound.

Scalp examination revealed a circumferential wound encircling the calvarium with granulation tissue, areas of exposed bone, and overlying crusting, partially obscured by the embedded headband (Fig. 1A–C). Cross-sectional imaging with CT demonstrated a circumferential scalp and calvarial erosive defect with a continuous linear hyperdense structure within the defect, raising the possibility of a retained foreign body (Fig. 2A–F). MRI confirmed the calvarial defect and retained band-like structure but did not reveal a discrete intracranial abscess or pneumocephalus, although patchy subcortical edema concerning infection or congestive changes was noted (Fig. 2G and H). She was started on empiric broad-spectrum antibiotics with meningitic coverage, including vancomycin, ceftriaxone, and acyclovir, given the concern for ongoing intracranial infection, exposed bone, and suspected osteomyelitis.

The patient was taken to the operating room for lumbar puncture, wound exploration, debridement, foreign body removal, and complex closure with the neurosurgery and plastic surgery teams. First, the patient was placed in the left lateral decubitus position and a lumbar puncture was performed, obtaining clear CSF, which was sent for analysis (Table 1). The opening pressure was 13 mm H2O. The head was completely shaved to comprehensively assess the wound. The patient was then repositioned supine with the neck flexed, and the head was secured in a manner that allowed complete visualization of the circumferential scalp wound (Fig. 1D). The wound was noted to be subdivided by bridges of intact healed skin between areas of granulation tissue (Fig. 1E). Over the right temporal region, a larger wound was identified with no palpable skull underneath. Multiple intraoperative cultures were obtained (Table 2). The wound was copiously irrigated with sterile saline. Areas of granulation tissue were probed, and devitalized skin edges were debrided. Within the larger right temporal wound, a black elastic band was identified. The band was cut, and gentle traction was applied to slowly remove the band from the wound in two pieces (Fig. 1F). The open areas of the wound were probed further and carefully inspected, revealing no further evidence of a retained foreign body. Given the multiple intact healed skin bridges, granulation tissue, and evidence of satisfactory blood supply to the wound edges, the open areas of the wound were each closed primarily by the plastic surgery team by advancing local superior and inferior flaps of skin, subcutaneous tissue, and galea to facilitate a tension-free closure. Horizontal mattress sutures were utilized for closure (Fig. 1G). All open areas of the wound were closed successfully in this manner. Areas with healthy granulation tissue were left to continue to heal.

Postoperatively, the patient completed a full course of targeted antibiotics (ceftriaxone given her susceptibilities and vancomycin for osteomyelitis coverage) and soon returned to her preadmission neurological baseline. Her circumferential scalp wound healed well (Fig. 1H). She was discharged to her facility with recommendations from the plastic and reconstructive surgery team for daily dressing of wound gel, Telfa, and Kerlix, and follow-up with the neurosurgery team for surveillance imaging in 2–4 weeks; however, she was subsequently lost to follow-up. A telephone call by our team at 8 months postoperatively revealed that she was back to her “usual self.”

Discussion

Bony erosion of the calvarium arises from a diverse group of neoplastic, infectious, inflammatory, metabolic, congenital, and iatrogenic processes and may present with pain, localized edema, cosmetic deformity, or neurological deficits.7 Radiographic evaluation is critical to narrow this broad differential, as imaging can help distinguish lytic from sclerotic lesions, delineate intra- versus extraosseous components, and assess intracranial involvement.5 Wolff’s law states that bone adapts to external mechanical factors, which in the calvarium can result in remodeling and erosion.8 Calvarial thinning and resorption have been described in cases of prolonged scalp tissue expansion, where continuous tension alters bone turnover at the outer table and diploe.9–11 Additionally, bony erosion has been reported at the skull base and sinuses in the setting of idiopathic intracranial hypertension, cholesteotomas, osteomyelitis, neoplasms, and iatrogenic causes (i.e., radiation, hardware), which can present in severe cases with encephaloceles and CSF leaks.12–15 However, a gap in the literature remains, with no reports to date of atraumatic circumferential calvarial erosion from everyday clothing.

In this case, chronic external compression from a tight elastic headband superimposed on a poorly healing scalp wound resulted in full-thickness calvarial erosion that also traversed the superior sagittal sinus and torcula. The pattern and progression of injury from the initial scalp trauma with subsequent persistent constrictive headwear, chronic wound drainage, and eventual circumferential bone loss were most compatible with pressure necrosis and foreign body retention rather than primary neoplastic or metabolic bone disorders. Cross-sectional imaging was a major key in determining appropriate operative management in this case, as it demonstrated the extent of osseous destruction, the relationship to the dural venous sinuses, and the presence of a linear hyperdense foreign body, while confirming the absence of an underlying abscess, pneumocephalus, or CSF leak. These imaging findings allowed for prompt operative intervention focusing on debridement with venous sinus preservation.

Intraoperatively, since no CSF leak was identified, there was low concern for CNS involvement, and the patient’s altered mental status was attributed to an underlying metabolic encephalopathy in the setting of sepsis, as an example of blood culture–negative sepsis (Table 1).16 However, we acknowledge that while the patient’s mental status improved postoperatively, the patient’s baseline mental status and presenting neurological examination may have been confounded by preexisting schizoaffective disorder. Patients with schizoaffective disorder often experience delusions and command hallucinations that, in the case of wounds, may distort their perception of the severity of the injury or the risk of infection. In this case, the patient may have believed that the head wound would not harm her if it remained covered by a headband or may have experienced command hallucinations instructing her not to allow others to see or touch the wound.17 These delusional beliefs would have contributed to her perception of the headband as protective, delaying presentation and hindering an earlier diagnosis that might have prevented progression to bony erosion of the skull.17

Individuals with severe mental disorders experience marked reductions in life expectancy, largely due to preventable health conditions, such as cardiovascular disease, respiratory disease, and infections.18,19 This is often attributed to a lack of access to medical care and resources.20 Physical comorbidities in this population are frequently underrecognized and undertreated, reflecting a combination of patient-, physician-, and system-based barriers to care. In this case, the patient had multiple encounters with healthcare providers (wound care nurses) for a chronic scalp wound but did not undergo cranial imaging or neurosurgical evaluation despite the severity of her wound until she became acutely lethargic, suggesting missed opportunities for earlier recognition of intracranial pathology and targeted antimicrobial intervention.21

Observations

A woman with a severe mental illness who was cared for at an SNF presented with a chronic circumferential scalp wound and acute neurological decline. Cross-sectional imaging revealed a circumferential calvarial defect traversing the superior sagittal sinus region, with a linear structure consistent with a retained foreign body but no evidence of an intracranial abscess, pneumocephalus, or CSF leak. Operative exploration revealed an elastic headband mesh embedded within the wound, resulting in a full-thickness scalp injury and calvarial erosion; however, no CSF leak or dural violation was identified. The patient’s encephalopathy improved with antibiotic therapy and source control and was most consistent with a metabolic/infectious process, although the interpretation of her neurological examination was limited by her underlying schizoaffective disorder with depression and an uncertain mental baseline. A remote fall may have initiated a scalp laceration; however, the pattern and progression of the injury were most compatible with chronic pressure necrosis and foreign body retention. These observations have not been previously reported.

Lessons

This case demonstrates that prolonged circumferential external compression of the scalp from a seemingly benign device, such as an elastic headband, can cause progressive soft tissue breakdown, which can progress to full-thickness circumferential calvarial erosion, risking injury to the underlying dural venous sinuses. The possibility of dural sinus injury should be considered in any patient with a chronic circumferential scalp wound, especially with a history of constrictive headwear or retained foreign material. An important lesson is the need to establish a low threshold for early CT and MRI in poorly healing cranial wounds, as cross-sectional imaging can identify retained foreign bodies, delineate the extent of osseous destruction, and detect intracranial complications such as abscesses, pneumocephalus, or CSF leaks, thereby guiding antimicrobial selection, operative planning, and the extent of intervention required for definitive source control. Lastly, this case underscores the importance of heightened clinical awareness, early neurosurgical involvement, and coordinated multidisciplinary care for medically and socially vulnerable patients with severe mental illness, in whom serious wound complications may progress substantially despite nominal access to care.

There are several limitations inherent to the nature of this patient’s presentation. As this is a single case with an unreliable narrator, the causality between the headband and calvarial defect cannot be definitively established, and a remote fall with an initial scalp laceration may have contributed to the evolution of the wound. The patient’s altered mental status was likely multifactorial, with sepsis and metabolic encephalopathy playing a major role, although the interpretation of her neurological examination was complicated by schizoaffective disorder and a potential baseline of low activity and limited verbal output. The long-term outcomes of this case are unknown because the patient was lost to outpatient follow-up. Despite these limitations, this case broadens the spectrum of calvarial erosion causes, highlights a preventable mechanism of cranial injury, and supports early multidisciplinary involvement and the use of advanced imaging in at-risk populations with atypical or nonhealing scalp wounds.
