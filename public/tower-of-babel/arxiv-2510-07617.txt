1.
Introduction

It is well-known that in a monoid
M,
if a product
xy

is invertible (has a -sided inverse), the
product
yx
need not be.
The bicyclic monoid
\begin{picture}\put(0.11,0.33){\rotatebox{60.0}{\line{1}{0}{0.55}}}\put(0.11,0.33){\rotatebox{300.0}{\line{1}{0}{0.55}}}\end{picture}x,\,y~|~xy=1\begin{picture}\put(0.09,0.33){\rotatebox{120.0}{\line{1}{0}{0.55}}}\put(0.09,0.33){\rotatebox{240.0}{\line{1}{0}{0.55}}}\end{picture}

is a standard example.
That example likewise shows that if

is invertible, none

yxy,
or
yxyx
need be.

On the other hand, if a product

is invertible, we see
that
x
has both a right and a left inverse, hence it
is invertible, hence so is
y,
so the submonoid
of
M
generated by
x,

y
and
(xyx)^{-1}
is a group.

What is going on here?

In this note, we study the structure of the monoid
M
obtained by
starting with the free monoid
\begin{picture}\put(0.11,0.33){\rotatebox{60.0}{\line{1}{0}{0.55}}}\put(0.11,0.33){\rotatebox{300.0}{\line{1}{0}{0.55}}}\end{picture}X\begin{picture}\put(0.09,0.33){\rotatebox{120.0}{\line{1}{0}{0.55}}}\put(0.09,0.33){\rotatebox{240.0}{\line{1}{0}{0.55}}}\end{picture}
on a set
X,
and
for some subset

of its elements, adjoining universal inverses
to the members of
S.

In §
3
we develop a simple algorithm for
obtaining a well-behaved normal form for
M
if
S
is finite,
and in §
4
make some further observations
on this construction.

If
S
is not assumed finite,
M
will be the direct
limit of the monoids arising in this way from finite subsets

S^{\prime}\subseteq S,
and in §
5
we show that

inherits most of the properties obtained in the finite case

can be computed algorithmically from the set

In §

with results in the literature on monoids presented using

2.
General conventions and observations

We make here some straightforward
conventions and observations, so that
we will be able to call on them subsequently without digressing.
First, a convention.

(2.1)

The words “inverse” and “invertible”, unless modified by
“left”, “right” or “one-sided”, will mean two-sided inverse
and two-sided invertible.

Now some trivial observations.
Let

(2.2)

If an element

All one-sided inverses of

(2.3)

If an element

If an element

On the other hand, a

For any

The next result makes use of a tool for establishing normal forms,

Though the title of

Proposition 3.4
.

Then every element
of
\begin{picture}\put(0.11,0.33){\rotatebox{60.0}{\line{1}{0}{0.55}}}\put(0.11,0.33){\rotatebox{300.0}{\line{1}{0}{0.55}}}\end{picture}X:\,\mathbf{i}\hskip 0.50003pt(T)\begin{picture}\put(0.09,0.33){\rotatebox{120.0}{\line{1}{0}{0.55}}}\put(0.09,0.33){\rotatebox{240.0}{\line{1}{0}{0.55}}}\end{picture}

(w\in T)

Proof.

That every element of
\begin{picture}\put(0.11,0.33){\rotatebox{60.0}{\line{1}{0}{0.55}}}\put(0.11,0.33){\rotatebox{300.0}{\line{1}{0}{0.55}}}\end{picture}X:\,\mathbf{i}\hskip 0.50003pt(T)\begin{picture}\put(0.09,0.33){\rotatebox{120.0}{\line{1}{0}{0.55}}}\put(0.09,0.33){\rotatebox{240.0}{\line{1}{0}{0.55}}}\end{picture}

Indeed, by definition of

\begin{picture}\put(0.11,0.33){\rotatebox{60.0}{\line{1}{0}{0.55}}}\put(0.11,0.33){\rotatebox{300.0}{\line{1}{0}{0.55}}}\end{picture}X:\,\mathbf{i}\hskip 0.50003pt(T)\begin{picture}\put(0.09,0.33){\rotatebox{120.0}{\line{1}{0}{0.55}}}\put(0.09,0.33){\rotatebox{240.0}{\line{1}{0}{0.55}}}\end{picture},

\mathbf{i}\hskip 0.50003pt(w)

immediately preceded or followed by

then the product obtained by dropping the substring

or
\mathbf{i}\hskip 0.50003pt(w)\,w
represents the same element,
and is shorter.
Repeating such rewritings, we must eventually get an expression
of the desired sort.

To show that such representations are unique, we
use the Diamond Lemma
[
2
]
.
Given
X
and
T,
the defining identities for

\begin{picture}\put(0.11,0.33){\rotatebox{60.0}{\line{1}{0}{0.55}}}\put(0.11,0.33){\rotatebox{300.0}{\line{1}{0}{0.55}}}\end{picture}X:\,\mathbf{i}\hskip 0.50003pt(T)\begin{picture}\put(0.09,0.33){\rotatebox{120.0}{\line{1}{0}{0.55}}}\put(0.09,0.33){\rotatebox{240.0}{\line{1}{0}{0.55}}}\end{picture}
yield the reduction system

(w\,\,\mathbf{i}\hskip 0.50003pt(w),~1)

for all

Note that each generator of
the form
\mathbf{i}\hskip 0.50003pt(w)

(w\in T)
appears at the end
of just one of the
2\,\mathrm{card}(T)

reducible strings shown in (

),
and at the beginning of just one such string,
and nowhere else.
Hence the only overlaps among such strings in which
the overlapping substrings involve an

correspond to the

and it is clear that the two ways

whose overlapping portions involve an

are resolvable.

What about overlaps involving the strings

By assumption, no two members of

not, overlap; this leaves only

of a product

and the overlapping subword is in the whole string

and both reductions yield

So all ambiguities of our reduction-system are resolvable, hence

[

]

of
\begin{picture}\put(0.11,0.33){\rotatebox{60.0}{\line{1}{0}{0.55}}}\put(0.11,0.33){\rotatebox{300.0}{\line{1}{0}{0.55}}}\end{picture}X:\,\mathbf{i}\hskip 0.50003pt(T)\begin{picture}\put(0.09,0.33){\rotatebox{120.0}{\line{1}{0}{0.55}}}\put(0.09,0.33){\rotatebox{240.0}{\line{1}{0}{0.55}}}\end{picture}
has a unique reduced expression;
i.e., a unique expression of the asserted form.
∎

We shall first show that every invertible element

These results clearly imply the remaining assertions.

Let

If

so let

and assume inductively that
all invertible elements having shorter normal forms

Since

\mathbf{i}\hskip 0.50003pt(T).

Let us first consider the case where

Then

The former case clearly satisfies our desired conclusion,

It remains to consider the case where

By left-right symmetry, it suffices to assume

is invertible; hence both of the factors

As for the uniqueness of the decomposition

suppose the normal form for

of

of the other.

and conclude as before that the

What does the monoid

Clearly, if

If, more generally,

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
X
be a set, and
S
a finite subset of
\begin{picture}\put(0.11,0.33){\rotatebox{60.0}{\line{1}{0}{0.55}}}\put(0.11,0.33){\rotatebox{300.0}{\line{1}{0}{0.55}}}\end{picture}X\begin{picture}\put(0.09,0.33){\rotatebox{120.0}{\line{1}{0}{0.55}}}\put(0.09,0.33){\rotatebox{240.0}{\line{1}{0}{0.55}}}\end{picture}.

Then there is an element

such
that the singleton

is inverse-equivalent to

If
S
is empty, we can take
w=1,
and if
S
is
a singleton

we can take
w=w_{1},
so assume

S=\{w_{1},\,\dots,\,w_{n}\}
with

and let

w\ =\ w_{1}\,\dots\,w_{n-1}\,w_{n}\,w_{n-1}\,\dots\,w_{1}.

I claim that the technique used in
the last part of the proof of Proposition

,
applied repeatedly, shows

inverse-equivalent to

Indeed, note that

overlaps itself
noninclusively via the initial and final factors

Hence as in the last part of the proof of Proposition

we can get an inverse-equivalent set by replacing the

I will mention here a curious observation not directly related to the
subject of this paper.

out a reference, I will delete the result.

Lemma 4.3
.

If two elements
x
and
y
of a monoid

satisfy

but

then

(4.4)

the map

given by

is a one-to-one endomorphism of

as a semigroup, but is
not a monoid homomorphism.

That (

) respects multiplication, but does
not send

to

is immediate.

by

on the left and

on the right, we get

so the map is one-to-one.
∎

The corresponding observation is also easily seen to hold for rings.

6.
Comparison with special monoids

There has been considerable research on what are called
“special monoids”: monoids presented by a finite set of generators
and a finite family of relations of the form
w_{i}=1.

A striking result from
[
1
]
, summarized in

[
3
, p. 1876, statement (A1), and
Theorem 1.1 and following paragraph]
,
is that any special monoid presented by just
one
such
relation has solvable word problem.

The solvability of the word problem in any monoid
of the form
\begin{picture}\put(0.11,0.33){\rotatebox{60.0}{\line{1}{0}{0.55}}}\put(0.11,0.33){\rotatebox{300.0}{\line{1}{0}{0.55}}}\end{picture}X:\mathbf{i}\hskip 0.50003pt(S)\begin{picture}\put(0.09,0.33){\rotatebox{120.0}{\line{1}{0}{0.55}}}\put(0.09,0.33){\rotatebox{240.0}{\line{1}{0}{0.55}}}\end{picture}
with
X
and
S
finite,
shown in §
3
above, can
also be deduced from that result of
[
1
]
: If we
form as in Lemma
4.1
above a word
w\in\begin{picture}\put(0.11,0.33){\rotatebox{60.0}{\line{1}{0}{0.55}}}\put(0.11,0.33){\rotatebox{300.0}{\line{1}{0}{0.55}}}\end{picture}X\begin{picture}\put(0.09,0.33){\rotatebox{120.0}{\line{1}{0}{0.55}}}\put(0.09,0.33){\rotatebox{240.0}{\line{1}{0}{0.55}}}\end{picture}

such that
\begin{picture}\put(0.11,0.33){\rotatebox{60.0}{\line{1}{0}{0.55}}}\put(0.11,0.33){\rotatebox{300.0}{\line{1}{0}{0.55}}}\end{picture}X:\mathbf{i}\hskip 0.50003pt(w)\begin{picture}\put(0.09,0.33){\rotatebox{120.0}{\line{1}{0}{0.55}}}\put(0.09,0.33){\rotatebox{240.0}{\line{1}{0}{0.55}}}\end{picture}\cong\begin{picture}\put(0.11,0.33){\rotatebox{60.0}{\line{1}{0}{0.55}}}\put(0.11,0.33){\rotatebox{300.0}{\line{1}{0}{0.55}}}\end{picture}X:\mathbf{i}\hskip 0.50003pt(S)\begin{picture}\put(0.09,0.33){\rotatebox{120.0}{\line{1}{0}{0.55}}}\put(0.09,0.33){\rotatebox{240.0}{\line{1}{0}{0.55}}}\end{picture},

to

that the above monoid
can also be presented as
\begin{picture}\put(0.11,0.33){\rotatebox{60.0}{\line{1}{0}{0.55}}}\put(0.11,0.33){\rotatebox{300.0}{\line{1}{0}{0.55}}}\end{picture}X\cup\{z\}\ |\ w\,z\,w=1\begin{picture}\put(0.09,0.33){\rotatebox{120.0}{\line{1}{0}{0.55}}}\put(0.09,0.33){\rotatebox{240.0}{\line{1}{0}{0.55}}}\end{picture}.

to monoids of the sort studied in §

On the other hand, special monoids defined using more than one
relation
w_{i}=1
are much less well-behaved.
Indeed, it is proved in
[

that whether such
a monoid is a group is undecidable.

is
a group, it can
have undecidable word problem, since
any finitely presented group can be finitely presented as a
special monoid, and there are known to be finitely presented
groups
