Addressing Model Inaccuracies in Transmission Network Reconfiguration via Diverse Alternatives
Paul Bannmüller 1 , Périne Cunat 1 7 , Ali Rajaei 1 , Jochen Cremer 1 7

Abstract
The ongoing energy transition places significant pressure on the transmission network due to increasing shares of renewables and electrification. To mitigate grid congestion, transmission system operators need decision support tools to suggest remedial actions, such as transmission network reconfigurations or redispatch. However, these tools are prone to model inaccuracies and may not provide relevant suggestions with regard to important unmodeled constraints or operator preferences. We propose a human-in-the-loop modeling-to-generate alternatives (HITL-MGA) approach to address these shortcomings by generating diverse topology reconfiguration alternatives. Case studies on the IEEE 57-bus and IEEE 118-bus systems show the method can leverage expert feedback and improve the quality of the suggested remedial actions.

I
Introduction

The operation of the power grid plays a crucial role in enabling the energy transition.
As the electrification of the energy demand and the share of decentralized variable renewable energy sources in our energy supply increase, more pressure is placed on the transmission system, regularly leading to grid congestion, as in the Netherlands
[
1
]
.
Reconfiguring the topology of the transmission grid as a remedial action can mitigate grid congestion cost-effectively. Such topology reconfiguration increases the flexibility of the transmission grid by switching lines, splitting busbars or reconfiguring substations
[
2
]
.

The combinatorial complexity of the topology reconfiguration problem poses a major challenge for the transmission system operators and the necessary decision support tools, even if simplified modeling assumptions, such as direct current power flow (DC-PF), are applied
[
3
,
4
,
5
]
.
Modeling details that are necessary to apply topology reconfiguration in power system operation, like system stability or the aleatoric uncertainty in load, generation, and contingencies, further increase complexity
[
6
,
7
,
8
]
. Consequently, approaches proposed in the literature leveraging techniques of reduction and decomposition
[
9
,
10
]
, fail to decrease complexity sufficiently to include all necessary modeling details.
Further approaches apply various forms of learning to reduce computational complexity while maintaining a certain level of model accuracy. These approaches aim to learn, for example, parts of optimization models and policies or to switch actions directly
[
11
,
12
,
13
,
14
]
. However, learning approaches often fail to generalize sufficiently therefore cannot fully mitigate model inaccuracies
[
14
,
15
]
.

Recently, a new paradigm has emerged to face model inaccuracies: exploring diverse alternative actions, instead of increasing the modeling detail.
For example, the GridOptions tool
[
16
]
proposes a workflow to generate a set of topological reconfiguration alternatives that satisfy different trade-offs between various objectives and are behaviorally diverse. However, the GridOptions workflow is built on a brute-force evaluation of all possible actions followed by several sequential filtering steps, including metaheuristics. The integration of different objectives at various stages of the workflow complicates the quantification of their individual impact.

[
12
]
propose an approach based on AlphaZero using tree search to provide different topological alternatives from which the human operator can choose. Their alternatives were generated according to the best performance with respect to a primary value function and do not explicitly aim for diversity to account for model inaccuracies.

In this paper, we adapt the human-in-the-loop modeling-to-generate-alternatives (HITL-MGA) approach of
[
17
]
to generate diverse alternatives in transmission network reconfiguration. The paradigm of MGA builds on the observation that complex decision-making problems often can not be fully captured by mathematical models
[
18
]
. In this case, diverse near-optimal solutions may perform nearly as well as the optimum, while performing better with respect to unmodeled aspects. MGA is typically applied in long-term planning of energy capacity expansion, to provide alternative investment plans within a predefined budget
[
19
]
. To the author’s knowledge, the present work is the first application of MGA to network topology reconfiguration.

The adapted HITL-MGA approach uses feedback from a postprocessing step that ranks the performance of the proposed alternatives with respect to specific model inaccuracies, and guides the generation of further alternatives to address the specific model inaccuracies.
This feedback could be provided by a human operator or power system simulations that exceed the tractable level of complexity of the decision support tool. This paper simulates the feedback by using evaluation functions that rate the performance of the generated alternatives regarding three typical types of model inaccuracies: the missing integration of (1) preferences and (2) expert knowledge of the operators due to difficulties in explicitly modeling them, and (3) simplifying modeling assumptions, because higher modeling detail would make the model intractable.

The main contributions of this paper are:

•

A first application of MGA to a network topology reconfiguration problem, and insights in the specificity of this application,

•

Adapting HITL-MGA approach to address model inaccuracies in the topology reconfiguration optimization process with diverse alternatives

II-C

Proposed Dynamic HITL-MGA Weights

There are two major shortcomings in the original HITL-MGA method
[
17
]
: (i) the dependence on a distinctive threshold
\tau
, which needs to be tuned to the problem, and (ii) the discrete weights in
{\mathbf{w}}^{(k)}_{\text{HITL}}
, neglecting the degree of distinctiveness of the corresponding variables
\mathbf{z}
, as shown in (
3
).
While tuning in (i) may not be an issue for one-time planning problems, it is a burden for the recurring topology reconfiguration problem in power system operation. In this case, the weights would need to be tuned each time the scenario changes, e.g., for different load scenarios. (ii) provides an opportunity to incorporate more feedback information into the HITL-MGA objective.
Another shortcoming when applying the approach to topology reconfiguration is that solving the optimization problem (
1
) for different sets of weights
\mathbf{w}_{d}
may lead to the same alternatives. This is also the case when applying MGA to linear programs, however, more profound for MILP formulations with sparsely covered solution spaces.

This paper addresses the shortcomings (i) and (ii) by modifying the encoding of the feedback in the weights as follows: we propose a HITL-MGA V1, which takes the mean over all top-ranked alternatives instead of generating a set of weights
\mathbf{w}_{\text{HITL}}
for each of them. In that way, V1 produces only one set of weights
\mathbf{w}_{\text{HITL}}
that includes the feedback of all top-ranked alternatives:

\delta_{j}=\mathbb{E}_{i}\!\left[z_{i,j}\right]-\mathbb{E}_{k}\!\left[z_{k,j}\right]

(4)

While V1 does not address the mentioned shortcomings of the baseline directly, it represents an intermediate step to HITL-MGA V2. Instead of using discrete weights, V2 scales the weights
\mathbf{w}_{\text{HITL}}
dependent on the difference between the mean of the top alternatives and the mean of all alternatives. This renders the distinctive threshold
\tau
obsolete and includes more information in the feedback:

w_{\text{HITL},j}=\mathbb{E}_{i}[z_{i,j}]-\mathbb{E}_{k}[z_{k,j}]\\

(5)

III-A

Settings and Test Networks

The case studies focus on IEEE 57-bus and 118-bus test networks. Congestion is enforced by reducing line capacities so that operating below rated limits requires topological actions, while a line is considered overloaded above 90% of its limit.
We consider two topological action scenarios. In the base case, only line switching is possible; in the second case, covered in Section
III-F
, both line switching and substation switching are possible; up to three line switching actions and three busbar splitting actions are allowed. We only consider one snapshot of time with a fixed load for each test network.
The electricity generation cost serves as the primary objective.
We assume
\varepsilon=0.05
which is typical, and
\tau=0.15

[
17
]
. By sampling weights
\mathbf{w}_{d}
, 100 alternatives are generated, of which the top 10 HITL-MGA feedback. Each experiment is repeated with four random seeds. The optimality MIP gap of the solver is set to
0.001
.

For each evaluation function, we also
compute its optimum within the
\varepsilon
-feasible set of (1b) to serve as a performance bound for the alternatives generated by MGA and HITL-MGA. This has not been done for switching sequence feasibility, as it is intractable to optimize for the evaluation function.

III-B

Diverse Alternatives with MGA

In all tested configurations, MGA generates topological alternatives within the near-optimiality bound
\varepsilon
. Figures
4
and
5
show the IEEE 118-bus results with line switching for the cumulative quadratic load (
7e
) and overload (
7d
) evaluation functions.
In Figure
4
, most alternatives achieve better performance in the evaluation function compared to the least-cost topology, while slightly trading off performance in the primary objective. This behavior, where near-optimal solutions can perform better in unmodeled objectives than the optimal solution, is a core feature of MGA
[
19
]
.
For other evaluation functions, such as topological distance or switching sequence feasibility, the MGA finds few or no alternatives improving upon the least-cost solution on the 118-bus system (Table
III
). We identified two reasons for this: first, some evaluation functions contradict the scope of the MGA objective formulation. For example, while the MGA objective aims at generating alternatives that are diverse in the switching action space, minimizing topological depth rewards low topological distance from the base topology.
Second, for some cases MGA finds a larger number of improving alternatives for the 57-bus system (Table
II
).
This is due to the challenge of sampling high-dimensional spaces that appears when sampling the weights
\mathbf{w}_{d}
.

The performance of all generated alternatives is generally closer to the least-cost solution than to the evaluation function optimum, due to the augmentation term (
2
) and the resulting cost-optimal dispatch.
This aligns with Figure
5
, where the Hamming distance is the number of all switching actions
z
that are different between two topologies.
Here, alternatives with the same topology as a minimal load solution do not necessarily perform better than alternatives with a larger topological distance, due to the differing dispatch.

III-C

Reducing Model Inaccuracies with HITL-MGA

Figure
6
compares the performance of the different HITL-MGA approaches regarding three evaluation functions.
The figure reveals that the alternatives found by the HITL-MGA approaches outperform the original MGA in the median for the shown evaluation functions.
Comparing the HITL-MGA approaches for the different evaluation functions, alternatives generated by V1 and V2 perform slightly better in the median than the baseline approach.
In Figure
6b
and
6c
, the performance of the HITL-MGA approaches appears to be loosely bounded by the initial MGA.
This indicates that the exploratory part
A
of the HITL-MGA objectives, with the chosen hyperparameters, is not enough to explore alternatives that perform drastically better than the top-ranked alternatives of the initial MGA.
Furthermore, there is a significant performance gap for evaluation functions in Figure
6b
and
6c
comparing the evaluation function optimum and the HITL-MGA approaches. Two factors influence the size of this gap: (i) the lack of exploration in the initial MGA, and (ii) the cost-optimal dispatch variables
\mathbf{x}
ensured by the augmentation term, as observed in Section
III-B
.

In Figure
6
, the color of the alternatives marks if its topology is unique, meaning that none of the MGA alternatives has the same topology
\mathbf{z}
, and no other unique alternative generated by the same approach has the same topology.
However, the non-unique alternatives with the same
\mathbf{z}
can still perform very differently, as the values in
\mathbf{x}
may vary. Hence, the number of unique alternatives generated by a HITL-MGA approach serves as an indicator for its capability to generate alternatives with diverse topologies.
With the selected hyperparameters, the HITL-MGA approaches generate a significant fraction of redundant non-unique alternatives. This is likely encouraged by the small solution space due to the heavy congestion in the case studies.

As shown in Figure
7a
, increasing
\tau
from the proposed
15\%

[
17
]
to
75\%
for the HITL-MGA baseline barely changes the outcomes.
However, the alternatives generated for threshold values of
5\%
and
95\%
exhibit a larger spread and more unique alternatives. This behavior is due to the binary nature of the topology reconfiguration problem. Here, the switching variables in the MGA objective can only take values between zero and one.
As the mean of the switching variables over all MGA-generated alternatives is usually close to zero or close to one for the highly congested case studies,
\delta
(
3a
) is also close to zero or close to one.
Only if the threshold comes close to these values does it significantly influence the results. Regarding diverse alternatives with continuous decision variables the V2 shows potential for further improvement, as the distribution of variable values should be more uniform than for binary variables.

Another important hyperparameter is the ratio of the HITL-MGA objective parameters
a/b
. This ratio sets the trade-off between exploration and the incorporation of feedback in these approaches. Figure
7b
shows that increasing the
a/b
ratio yields more unique topologies and slightly better best alternatives, with similar medians.
When using the HITL-MGA approaches to generate alternatives for topology reconfiguration, a human operator enforce alignment with the provided ranking with small
a/b
ratio if feedback is trusted, or preserve exploration if feedback is uncertain.

When a human expert provides the HITL-MGA feedback, the number of alternatives should be limited to reduce complexity and time effort.
Reducing the number of alternatives for the HITL-MGA V2 approach to ten alternatives with three top-ranked alternatives slightly worsens median performance, shown in Figure
7c
. Also, the best- and worst-performing alternatives that were found in the base case in Figure
6c
are lost. Nevertheless, the approach still yields valuable alternatives.

The larger switching action space enables the approach to find more diverse alternatives than it does for line switching. III-G

Discussion

We consider the presented HITL-MGA approach a first step towards a structured generation of valuable alternatives for topology reconfiguration that address specific model inaccuracies. The approach generates valuable alternatives for most of the exemplary model inaccuracies.
The improvements to the HITL-MGA baseline approach
[
17
]
in V1 and V2 result in a similar performance for the topology reconfiguration problem, while V2 makes the parameter of the distinctive threshold redundant and shows potential to improve further for continuous problems.

However, some limitations of the approach should be noted.
In our case study, if the approach was unable to generate valuable alternatives, the cumulative exploration in the initial MGA and HITL-MGA was not sufficient.
Whether the approach succeeds depends on how the evaluation function for the model inaccuracy relates to the MGA and HITL-MGA objective formulation, and how the weights
\mathbf{w}_{d}
are determined. If the exploratory part of the MGA and HITL-MGA cannot explore good solutions for the evaluation function, e.g., for topological depth, the HITL-MGA approach does not find any valuable alternatives.

Interestingly, the HITL-MGA approaches can work for model inaccuracies with evaluation functions in the form of
U(\mathbf{z})
and
U(\mathbf{x,z})
. The performance of the alternatives generated by the approaches for evaluation functions of the form
U(\mathbf{x,z})
, however, is limited, as the variables
\mathbf{x}
are optimized for the primary objective through the augmentation term in the MGA objective.
By incorporating dispatch variables
\mathbf{x}
into MGA variables
\mathbf{z}
and eliminating the augmentation term, this limitation could be circumvented. However, this would further increase dimensionality, creating a problem when sampling
\mathbf{w}_{d}
in high-dimensional spaces. Consequently, it may be difficult to find a dispatch configuration that performs well in either the primary objective or the evaluation function within a reasonable number of alternatives for the different topological alternatives.

Under heavy congestion, the feasible space is small, favoring redundancy; larger action spaces (e.g., substation switching) alleviate this but increase the demand on exploration.

IV
Conclusion

This paper presents an approach for generating topological alternatives to address specific model inaccuracies in topology reconfiguration, covering both line and substation switching. The human-in-the-loop MGA (HITL-MGA) approach was adapted and applied to the IEEE 57-bus and IEEE 118-bus systems. Three types of model inaccuracies were investigated through six evaluation functions, considering both switching variables and operational variables such as dispatch. The approach successfully generates valuable alternatives for all evaluation functions except topological depth, which was limited by the design of the MGA objective. The exploration capability of the initial MGA was identified as a bottleneck of the HITL-MGA approach and should be examined further. In addition, future research could investigate alternative feedback mechanisms to enhance the effectiveness of the approach.
