Tensor Spin Polarization Induced by Curved Freeze-Out Hypersurface
Zhong-Hua Zhang, Xu-Guang Huang

Abstract
We investigate how the curvature of the freeze-out hypersurface polarizes massive vector bosons in relativistic heavy-ion collisions. Starting from the Proca Lagrangian and using the Wigner function formalism, we perform a systematic gradient expansion to obtain a covariant spin-polarization tensor expressed in terms of hydrodynamic fields and the curvature tensor of the freeze-out hypersurface. Analytic results for Bjorken and Gubser flows show that curvature anisotropy generates a non-zero tensor polarization. For ϕ \phi meson, we estimate the curvature contribution to its spin alignment as δ ​ Θ y ​ y ∼ − 10 − 4 \delta\Theta_{yy}\sim-10^{-4} to − 10 − 3 -10^{-3} . We also find that the curvature contribution grows as the system size decreases, suggesting that spin polarization measurements in small systems may provide a clean probe of this geometric effect.

When describing the spin polarization in the relativistic fluids, researchers often use the local equilibrium density operator
Zubarev et al. 1979
;
van Weert 1982
;
Becattini et al. 2019a

To determine this extended grand canonical ensemble, we need the values of the conserved charges (e.g., energy-momentum density) on a 3D hypersurface.
Most past theoretical works assume the major part of the hypersurface is isochronic

;
Liu and Huang 2022
;
Zhang et al. 2025
;

But in numerical simulations, the hypersurface is chosen as the freeze-out hypersurface in order to compare with the experimental data.
Taking the Bjorken expansion as an example

Since the thermal current field
\beta_{\mu}(x)
generally violates the Killing equation
\partial_{(\mu}\beta_{\nu)}=0

Thus, there may appear a novel contribution to the spin polarization due to the geometrical shape of the freeze-out hypersurface. By analyzing the parity and time-reversal properties of the gradient expansion

;
Sheng et al. 2024a

The effect of the curvature would appear at the next-to-leading order for vector polarization. (See a recent study of this issue for fermions in Ref.
Sheng et al. 2025
.) To highlight how the shape of hypersurfaces matters, we study the tensor spin polarization of massive vector bosons, which, as we will show, would be affected by the curvature at the leading order.

For the spin polarization of vector mesons, STAR Collaboration measured the global spin alignment of

{\mathcal{S}}^{\mu}(x,k)=-\frac{1+n_{B}}{3m}\epsilon^{\mu\nu\rho\sigma}k_{\nu}\left[\varpi_{\rho\sigma}-\mathchoice{\Xi^{{{\alpha}\mathchoice{\makebox[6.41289pt][c]{$\displaystyle$}}{\makebox[6.41289pt][c]{$\textstyle$}}{\makebox[3.89449pt][c]{$\scriptstyle$}}{\makebox[2.78175pt][c]{$\scriptscriptstyle$}}}}_{{\mathchoice{\makebox[5.19876pt][c]{$\displaystyle$}}{\makebox[5.19876pt][c]{$\textstyle$}}{\makebox[3.13454pt][c]{$\scriptstyle$}}{\makebox[2.23895pt][c]{$\scriptscriptstyle$}}{[\rho}}}}{\Xi^{{{\alpha}\mathchoice{\makebox[6.41289pt][c]{$\displaystyle$}}{\makebox[6.41289pt][c]{$\textstyle$}}{\makebox[3.89449pt][c]{$\scriptstyle$}}{\makebox[2.78175pt][c]{$\scriptscriptstyle$}}}}_{{\mathchoice{\makebox[5.19876pt][c]{$\displaystyle$}}{\makebox[5.19876pt][c]{$\textstyle$}}{\makebox[3.13454pt][c]{$\scriptstyle$}}{\makebox[2.23895pt][c]{$\scriptscriptstyle$}}{[\rho}}}}{\Xi^{{{\alpha}\mathchoice{\makebox[6.41289pt][c]{$\displaystyle$}}{\makebox[6.41289pt][c]{$\textstyle$}}{\makebox[3.89449pt][c]{$\scriptstyle$}}{\makebox[2.78175pt][c]{$\scriptscriptstyle$}}}}_{{\mathchoice{\makebox[5.19876pt][c]{$\displaystyle$}}{\makebox[5.19876pt][c]{$\textstyle$}}{\makebox[3.13454pt][c]{$\scriptstyle$}}{\makebox[2.23895pt][c]{$\scriptscriptstyle$}}{[\rho}}}}{\Xi^{{{\alpha}\mathchoice{\makebox[6.41289pt][c]{$\displaystyle$}}{\makebox[6.41289pt][c]{$\textstyle$}}{\makebox[3.89449pt][c]{$\scriptstyle$}}{\makebox[2.78175pt][c]{$\scriptscriptstyle$}}}}_{{\mathchoice{\makebox[5.19876pt][c]{$\displaystyle$}}{\makebox[5.19876pt][c]{$\textstyle$}}{\makebox[3.13454pt][c]{$\scriptstyle$}}{\makebox[2.23895pt][c]{$\scriptscriptstyle$}}{[\rho}}}}\left(\xi_{\sigma]\alpha}+\delta\Omega_{\sigma]\alpha}\right)\right]+\mathcal{O}(\partial,\overline{\kappa}^{2})

(51)

and the spin polarization tensor

\begin{split}{\mathcal{T}}^{\mu\nu}(x,k)=&~\frac{1+n_{B}}{6(n\cdot k)}B_{\alpha\beta}\bigg\{-\xi_{\rho\sigma}\left(\eta^{\rho\sigma}+\frac{k^{\rho}k^{\sigma}}{2m^{2}}\right)\left[\Xi^{\alpha\mu}\Xi^{\beta\nu}-\frac{1}{3}\Delta_{(k)}^{\mu\nu}\left(\eta^{\alpha\beta}+\hat{k}^{\alpha}\hat{k}^{\beta}\right)\right]\\
&+\xi_{\rho\sigma}\left[\Xi^{\alpha\rho}\Xi^{\beta(\mu}\Delta^{\nu)\sigma}_{{(k)}}-\frac{1}{3}\Delta_{(k)}^{\mu\nu}\Xi^{\alpha\rho}\Xi^{\beta\sigma}\right]-\delta\Omega_{\rho\sigma}\Xi^{\alpha\rho}\Xi^{\beta(\mu}\Delta^{\nu)\sigma}_{{(k)}}\bigg\}+\mathcal{O}(\partial,\overline{\kappa}^{2})\,.\end{split}

(52)

These expressions describe the polarizations generated by first-order hydrodynamic fields in
\partial
.
Equation (
52
) presents the tensor spin polarization induced by first-order hydrodynamic fields with a curved hypersurface.
The curvature appears at leading order.
We treat curvature as an independent order, since it is a general feature in our analysis.
In practice, the freeze-out hypersurface is often chosen as a constant-
T
surface.
For this case, the curvature is an
\mathcal{O}(\partial)
term.
Therefore, tensor polarization in Eq. (
52
) is of the order of
\mathcal{O}(\partial^{2})
, same as the ones in Ref.
Zhang et al. 2025
;
Yang et al. 2024
.
However, the curvature-related term is new and absent in prior literature.
This term originates from statistical effects and depends on the definition of the local equilibrium on the freeze-out hypersurface.

V
Freezeout in Bjorken Expansion

In the following, we estimate Eq. (
52
) in the flow with Bjorken expansion
Bjorken 1983
.
Since in the Bjorken expansion, the fluid preserves the longitudinal boost invariance, we should expect the freezeout to take place on a hypersurface with a constant “proper time”
\tau=\sqrt{t^{2}-z^{2}}
, which is described by

2F(x)=g^{\parallel}_{\mu\nu}x^{\mu}x^{\nu}-\tau_{f}^{2}=0\,,

where
g^{\parallel}_{\mu\nu}={\rm{diag}}\left(1,0,0,-1\right)
.
Based on
F(x)
, we have the normal vector

n^{\mu}=x_{\parallel}^{\mu}/\tau_{f}

(53)

with
x^{\parallel}_{\mu}=g^{\parallel}_{\mu\nu}x^{\nu}

and the curvature tensor

B^{\mu\nu}=(n^{\mu}n^{\nu}-g_{\parallel}^{\mu\nu})/\tau_{f}\,.

(54)

As hinted by Bjorken expansion, the fluid velocity is
u_{\mu}=x^{\parallel}_{\mu}/\tau
and the gradient of thermal velocity reads

\partial_{\mu}\beta_{\nu}=\frac{1}{T}\left(\partial_{\mu}u_{\nu}-\frac{u_{\mu}u_{\nu}}{T}\frac{dT}{d\tau}\right)

with
\partial_{\mu}u_{\nu}=\left(g^{\parallel}_{\mu\nu}-u_{\mu}u_{\nu}\right)/\tau
and the cooling rate
dT/\left(Td\tau\right)=-c_{s}^{2}/\tau
, where
c_{s}
is the speed of spound.
The cooling rate is obtained in Ref.
Bjorken 1983
by using the ideal hydrodynamic equation and assuming the zero chemical potential, which is applicable for higher collision energy in RHIC.
Therefore, the thermal vorticity is zero, and the thermal shear reads

\xi^{\mu\nu}=\frac{1}{T\tau}\left[g_{\parallel}^{\mu\nu}-(1-c_{s}^{2})u^{\mu}u^{\nu}\right]\,

(55)

Plugging Eqs. (
54
) and (
55
) into Eq. (
52
) and turning the net spin potential to zero, one can write out the tensor polarization in Bjorken expansion.
To estimate the result, we take another primary assumption that the particle produced is static inside the fluid cell, namely,
k_{\mu}=mu_{\mu}
.
Then, we obtain

{\mathcal{T}}^{\mu\nu}(x,k)=-\frac{(1+n_{B})c_{s}^{2}}{4mT_{f}\tau_{f}}B^{\left<\mu\nu\right>}\,,

(56)

where
T_{f}
is the temperature when the vector mesons freeze out.

B_{\left<\mu\nu\right>}
implies that in this typical situation, the tensor polarization comes from the anisotropy of the curvature of the freezeout hypersurface.
By taking
T_{f}
to be
150\text{ MeV}
,
\tau_{f}
to be
5\text{ fm}
, and
m
to be
\phi
meson’s mass
1019\text{ MeV}
,
we estimate the magnitude of the spin alignment

\delta\Theta_{yy}=-\frac{(1+n_{B})c_{s}^{2}}{12mT_{f}\tau_{f}^{2}}\sim-10^{-4}\,.

(57)

From the symmetry between
x
and
y
, we can also conclude that
\delta\Theta_{xx}<0
, and thus,
\delta\Theta_{zz}>0
.
Therefore, Eq. (
56
) seems to imply that in this situation, the longitudinal polarization state of a vector meson tends to align with the direction where the curvature is greater than the average, namely, the direction where the hypersurface curves more towards the normal vector, in this case,
z
direction.
This leads to a smaller probability of longitudinal polarization in
y
direction, namely,
\delta\Theta_{yy}<0
.

VI
Freezeout in Gubser Expansion

Gubser flow is an analytic solution to the conformal invariant Navier-Stokes equation
Gubser 2010
.
It possesses longitudinal boost invariance and rotational symmetry about the z-axis (corresponding to the central collisions).
However, compared to the Bjorken flow, it lacks transverse translational symmetry,
which accommodates the finite transverse size in realistic collisions and thus qualitatively depicts the radial flow.

Gubser flow contains a free parameter
q
, whose inverse characterizes the transverse size of the system.
In the following, we adopt
1/q=4.3\text{ fm}
, which is obtained by estimating the gold-gold central collisions at RHIC energy
Gubser et al. 2008
;
Gubser 2010
.
To cooperate with these symmetry properties, we adopt a reference frame
(\tau,\eta,\rho,\phi)
with
\tau
the “proper time” defined in Sec.
V
,

\eta
the space-time rapidity,
\rho=\sqrt{x^{2}+y^{2}}
the transverse radius, and
\phi
the azimuthal angle.
We follow the result of inviscid fluid in Ref.
Gubser 2010

\displaystyle T

\displaystyle=\frac{\tilde{T}_{0}}{\tau(1+g^{2})^{1/3}}\,,

(58)

\displaystyle u^{\mu}

\displaystyle=\frac{1+q^{2}\tau^{2}+q^{2}\rho^{2}}{2q\tau\sqrt{1+g^{2}}}\frac{\partial x^{\mu}}{\partial\tau}+\frac{q\rho}{\sqrt{1+g^{2}}}\frac{\partial x^{\mu}}{\partial\rho}\,,

(59)

where

g=\frac{1-q^{2}\tau^{2}+q^{2}\rho^{2}}{2q\tau}\,,

and
{\partial x^{\mu}}/{\partial\tau}
and
{\partial x^{\mu}}/{\partial\rho}
stand for unitary vectors in
\tau
and
\rho
directions, namely,

\frac{\partial x^{\mu}}{\partial\tau}=\frac{1}{\tau}x^{\mu}_{\parallel}\,,\quad\frac{\partial x^{\mu}}{\partial\rho}=\frac{1}{\rho}x_{\perp}^{\mu}

with
x_{\perp}^{\mu}=g^{\mu\nu}_{\perp}x_{\nu}
and
g_{\perp}^{\mu\nu}={\rm{diag}}(0,-1,-1,0)
.
According to a quasirealistic estimation in Ref.
Gubser 2010
, for gold-gold collisions at RHIC energy,
\tilde{T}_{0}=2.99
.
Letting
q\rightarrow 0
properly (keeping
q\tilde{T}_{0}
a constant), one would recover the Bjorken flow.

If we assume the freezeout temperature to be
150\text{ MeV}
, given by Eq. (
58
),
q\tau\sim 0.91
in the paraxial region (
q\rho\ll 1
) of the freezeout hypersurface.
Therefore, to simplify the calculation, we focus on the paraxial region of the hypersurface, and thus,
we assume
\delta\tilde{\tau}=q\tau-1
and
\delta\tilde{\tau}=q\rho
as small quantities
\delta\sim 0.1
and continue our calculation by performing these expansions.
For example, for temperature and fluid velocity, we have

\displaystyle T

\displaystyle=\tilde{T}_{0}q\left[1-\delta\tilde{\tau}+\frac{2}{3}\delta\tilde{\tau}^{2}-\frac{1}{3}\delta\tilde{\tau}\left(\delta\tilde{\tau}^{2}-\delta\tilde{\rho}^{2}\right)\right]+\mathcal{O}(\delta^{4})\,,

\displaystyle u^{\mu}

\displaystyle=\left(1+\frac{1}{2}\delta\tilde{\rho}^{2}\right)\frac{\partial x^{\mu}}{\partial\tau}+\delta\tilde{\rho}\frac{\partial x^{\mu}}{\partial\rho}+\mathcal{O}(\delta^{4})\,.

Since we will use the second-order spatial derivative of the temperature field, we have to expand it up to
\delta^{3}
.

With the equation of hypersurface,
\tau^{3}(1+g^{2})=\text{const.}
, we obtain

n^{\mu}=\frac{\partial x^{\mu}}{\partial\tau}-\frac{2}{3}\delta\tilde{\tau}\delta\tilde{\rho}\frac{\partial x^{\mu}}{\partial\rho}+\mathcal{O}(\delta^{3})\,,

and thus we obtain the curvature tensor

B^{\mu\nu}\equiv-\Delta^{\mu\alpha}_{(n)}\frac{\partial n^{\nu}}{\partial x^{\alpha}}=\frac{1}{\tau}\left(n^{\mu}n^{\nu}-g^{\mu\nu}_{\parallel}\right)-\frac{2}{3}q\delta\tilde{\tau}g_{\perp}^{\mu\nu}+\mathcal{O}(\delta^{2})\,.

(60)

As for the thermal shear tensor, we first calculate the cooling rate

\frac{(n\cdot\partial)T}{T}=-q\left(1-\frac{1}{3}\delta\tilde{\tau}\right)+\mathcal{O}(\delta^{2})

and the kinetic shear
\partial_{(\mu}u_{\nu)}
, then we have

\displaystyle\xi^{\mu\nu}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{T}\left(\partial^{(\mu}u^{\nu)}-u^{(\mu}n^{\nu)}\frac{(n\cdot\partial)T}{T}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\tilde{T}_{0}}\left[\eta^{\mu\nu}+\delta\tilde{\tau}\left(g^{\mu\nu}_{\perp}+\frac{2}{3}n^{\mu}n^{\nu}\right)\right]+\mathcal{O}(\delta^{2})\,.

(61)

As in the previous section, we assume
k_{\mu}=mu_{\mu}
. Plugging Eqs. (
60
) and (
61
) into Eq. (
52
), we have

{\mathcal{T}}^{\mu\nu}(x,k)=-\frac{1+n_{B}}{6m\tilde{T}_{0}}\left(\frac{7}{2}+\delta\tilde{\tau}\right)B^{\left<\mu\nu\right>}(x)\,,

(62)

where
B^{\left<\mu\nu\right>}
implies that in the Gubser flow and with all those assumptions, an anisotropic curvature would induce tensor polarization.
Using the definition of
\epsilon_{y}(k)
, we obtain the spin alignment measured in the
y
direction

\delta\Theta_{yy}(x,k)=-\frac{(1+n_{B})q}{6m\tilde{T}_{0}}\left(\frac{7}{6}-\frac{29}{18}\delta\tilde{\tau}\right)\sim-3\times 10^{-3}\,.

(63)

Therefore, similar to the Bjorken flow, in this situation, the spin
\pm 1
state of a vector meson tends to align in the direction with smaller curvature than the average.
Thus, the probability of longitudinal polarization in
x
and
y
directions is lower than
z
.

From Eq. (
63
), one can also observe a property by choosing a system with a different size
L
,
such as a lead-lead collision or a slightly noncentral gold-gold collision.
Since
1/q
depicts the typical transverse length of the system, one should expect
q\sim L^{-1}
.
And according to Ref.
Gubser 2010
,
\tilde{T}_{0}\sim(dN/d\eta)^{1/3}
. Since
dN/d\eta\sim L^{3}
, we have
\tilde{T}_{0}\sim L
.
By considering the leading term of Eq. (
63
), one should expect a larger influence from the curvature for a smaller system.

VII
Conclusion and Discussion

In this work, we formulate a covariant description of spin polarization for massive vector bosons using
{\mathcal{S}}^{\mu}
and
{\mathcal{T}}^{\mu\nu}

(defined in Eqs. (
19
)-(
21
)) through the Wigner function.
For instance, the spin polarization tensor is given by the traceless and symmetric part of the projected Wigner function

{\mathcal{T}}^{\mu\nu}(x,k)=\frac{W^{\left<\mu\nu\right>}_{\perp}(x,k)}{W^{\rho}_{\perp\rho}(x,k)}\,.

(64)

By evaluating its average over the hypersurface via the Cooper-Frye formula (
23
),
we obtain the expectation value of the spin polarization tensor (
13
).
Our results show that the curved freeze-out hypersurface significantly affects the spin alignment of massive vector bosons in relativistic fluids.
We identify the curvature tensor
B_{\mu\nu}
defined in Eq. (
33
) as a key contributor to the tensor polarization.
Specifically, curvature-induced contributions arise from terms proportional to first-order hydrodynamic fields, such as the thermal shear
\xi_{\rho\sigma}
and the net spin potential
\delta\Omega_{\rho\sigma}

{\mathcal{T}}^{\mu\nu}(x,k)=\frac{1+n_{B}}{6(n\cdot k)}B_{\alpha\beta}\bigg[-\xi_{\rho\sigma}\left(\eta^{\rho\sigma}+\frac{k^{\rho}k^{\sigma}}{2m^{2}}\right)\Xi^{\alpha\langle\mu}\Xi^{\beta\nu\rangle}\\
+\xi_{\rho\sigma}\Xi^{\alpha\rho}\Xi^{\beta\langle\mu}\Delta^{\nu\rangle\sigma}_{{(k)}}-\delta\Omega_{\rho\sigma}\Xi^{\alpha\rho}\Xi^{\beta(\mu}\Delta^{\nu)\sigma}_{{(k)}}\bigg]\,.

(65)

We estimate the tensor polarization for both Bjorken and Gubser flows, revealing that anisotropic curvature induces tensor polarization.
Notably, the longitudinal polarization state prefers to align with directions of greater curvature.
Consequently, the spin alignment along the
y
direction,
\delta\Theta_{yy}
, is negative and with a magnitude of
10^{-4}-10^{-3}
.
Further analysis of the Gubser flow suggests that curvature effects on spin alignment are more significant in systems with smaller transverse sizes.

We stress that the above discussion applies specifically to the space-like freeze-out hypersurface.
For implementation in numerical simulations, future research must address the time-like part of the freeze-out hypersurface.
Additionally, the above result depends on our choice of pseudo-gauge. In particular, for the tensor polarization, we emphasize that the results differ from the Belinfante pseudo-gauge even if we neutralize the net spin potential, namely, setting the spin potential to be the thermal vorticity. This occurs because the canonical spin tensor for the vector boson is not fully antisymmetric
Becattini et al. 2019b
. These issues will be considered in future studies.

Appendix A
Spin polarization tensor

For a single particle state in relativistic quantum mechanics, the spin polarization vector
{\mathcal{S}}^{\mu}(k)
defined by Eq. (
10
) is the expectation value of the vectorial spin operator
\widehat{{\mathcal{S}}}^{\mu}
defined by Eq. (
12
), which has been proved in Ref.
Becattini 2021
.
In this appendix, we follow Ref.
Becattini 2021
to demonstrate that for a single massive particle state,
{\mathcal{T}}^{\mu\nu}(k)
defined by Eq. (
11
) is the expectation value of the tensor spin operator
\widehat{{\mathcal{T}}}^{\mu\nu}
defined by Eq. (
13
).

For a single massive particle, the eigenspace of the momentum operator
\widehat{P}_{\mu}
is formed by
\ket{k}
that satisfy
\widehat{P}_{\mu}\ket{k}=k_{\mu}\ket{k}
,
where
k_{\mu}
is on the mass shell,
k^{0}=E_{\bm{k}}
.
Noticing that
[\widehat{{\mathcal{S}}}_{\mu},\widehat{P}_{\nu}]=0
, we can restrict
\widehat{{\mathcal{S}}}_{\mu}
to the eigenspace of
\widehat{P}_{\mu}
labeled by
k_{\mu}
.
As
\widehat{{\mathcal{S}}}\cdot\widehat{P}=0
, for all the
\ket{k}
in the eigenspace, we have
\widehat{{\mathcal{S}}}\cdot k\ket{k}=0
.
Hence, we can conclude that

\widehat{{\mathcal{S}}}(k)\cdot k=0\,,

where
\widehat{{\mathcal{S}}}_{\mu}(k)
represents the restriction of
\widehat{{\mathcal{S}}}_{\mu}
mentioned earlier.
Therefore, we can decompose
\widehat{{\mathcal{S}}}_{\mu}(k)
by polarization vectors
\epsilon^{\mu}_{i}(k)

\widehat{{\mathcal{S}}}^{\mu}(k)=\sum_{i=1}^{3}\widehat{{\mathcal{S}}}^{i}(k)\,\epsilon_{i}^{\mu}(k)\,.

(66)

Similarly,
\widehat{{\mathcal{T}}}^{\mu\nu}
defined by Eq. (
13
) turns into

\widehat{{\mathcal{T}}}^{\mu\nu}(k)=\widehat{{\mathcal{S}}}^{(\mu}(k)\widehat{{\mathcal{S}}}^{\nu)}(k)+\frac{2}{3}\Delta^{\mu\nu}_{(k)}

after being restricted in the eigenspace, which can also be decomposed by polarization vectors

\widehat{{\mathcal{T}}}^{\mu\nu}(k)=\sum_{ij}\left(\widehat{{\mathcal{S}}}^{(i}(k)\widehat{{\mathcal{S}}}^{j)}(k)-\frac{2}{3}\delta^{ij}\right)\epsilon_{i}^{\mu}(k)\epsilon_{j}^{\nu}(k)\,.

(67)

Since
[\widehat{P}^{\mu},\widehat{{\mathcal{S}}}^{i}(k)]=0
, we can choose to diaganalize one of
\widehat{{\mathcal{S}}}^{i}(k)
in the eigenspace of
\widehat{P}_{\mu}
.
Instead, we choose the adjoint basis, namely,

\widehat{P}_{\mu}\ket{k,i}=k_{\mu}\ket{k,i}\,,\quad\widehat{{\mathcal{S}}}^{i}(k)\ket{k,i}=0\,,\qquad\text{with }i=1,2,3\,,

where we choose to characterize the eigenspace by the longitudinal states of
\widehat{{\mathcal{S}}}^{i}(k)
.
Since
\widehat{{\mathcal{S}}}^{i}(k)
are the generators of the little group of massive particles, namely, the
SU(2)
group, we have

\bra{k,r}\widehat{{\mathcal{S}}}^{i}(k)\ket{k,s}=(s^{i})_{rs}\,,

(68)

where
s^{i}
are defined by
(s^{i})_{jk}=-i\epsilon^{ijk}
as we take the adjoint representation here.
Similarly, we obtain

\bra{k,r}\big[\widehat{{\mathcal{S}}}^{(i}(k)\widehat{{\mathcal{S}}}^{j)}(k)-\frac{2}{3}\delta^{ij}\big]\ket{k,s}=(s^{(i}s^{j)})_{rs}-\frac{2}{3}\delta^{ij}\delta_{rs}=(\Sigma^{ij})_{rs}

(69)

for the tensor spin operator.

Let
\widehat{\rho}
be the density operator of the single particle in the Hilbert space, and its restriction to the eigenspace of
\widehat{P}_{\mu}
labeled by
k_{\mu}
be
\widehat{\Theta}(k)
, which is precisely the spin density operator of a particle with momentum
{\bm{k}}
.
Evaluating the expectation value of
\widehat{{\mathcal{T}}}^{\mu\nu}(k)
, we obtain

\displaystyle\Tr{\opt{\H}(k) \opt{\mT}^{\m\n}(k)}=

\displaystyle~\sum_{r,s}\bra{k,r}\widehat{\Theta}(k)\ket{k,s}\bra{k,s}\widehat{{\mathcal{T}}}^{\mu\nu}(k)\ket{k,r}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle~\sum_{r,s,i,j}\Theta_{rs}(k)(\Sigma^{ij})_{sr}\epsilon_{i}^{\mu}(k)\epsilon_{j}^{\nu}(k)=\sum_{i,j}{\mathfrak{T}}^{ij}(k)\epsilon_{i}^{\mu}(k)\epsilon_{j}^{\nu}(k)

where we utilize Eq. (
9
) in the last equation.
Comparing this with Eq. (
11
), we conclude that for a single particle state,
{\mathcal{T}}^{\mu\nu}(k)
defined by Eq. (
11
) is the expectation value of the tensor spin operator
\widehat{{\mathcal{T}}}^{\mu\nu}
defined by Eq. (
13
).

Appendix B
Derivation of the curvature tensor

In the following, we demonstrate Eq. (
32
) by assuming
n_{\mu}(x)=(1,\bm{0})
. One can always restore the general case by a Lorentz transformation. According to the
implicit function theorem
, for a certain point
x
on
\Sigma
, we have

\frac{\partial y^{0}}{\partial y^{i}}\bigg|_{y=x}=-\frac{\partial F(y)}{\partial y^{i}}\bigg/\frac{\partial F(y)}{\partial y^{0}}\bigg|_{y=x}=0.

Therefore, up to leading order, we obtain,

n\cdot(y-x)=0+\mathcal{O}\big(|y-x|_{\perp}\big)^{2}.

As for
y^{0}
’s second-order spatial derivatives, we have

\displaystyle\frac{\partial^{2}y^{0}}{\partial y^{i}\partial y^{j}}\bigg|_{y=x}=

\displaystyle-\frac{\partial}{\partial y^{j}}\left[\frac{\partial F(y)}{\partial y^{i}}\bigg/\frac{\partial F(y)}{\partial y^{0}}\right]\bigg|_{y=x}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{\partial F(y)/\partial y^{0}}\frac{\partial^{2}F(y)}{\partial y^{i}\partial y^{j}}\bigg|_{y=x}=B_{ij}(x).

Noticing that

y^{0}-x^{0}=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i,j}\frac{\partial^{2}y^{0}}{\partial y^{i}\partial y^{j}}\bigg|_{y=x}(y-x)^{i}(y-x)^{j}+\mathcal{O}\big(|y-x|_{\perp}\big)^{3},

(70)

we finally obtain Eq. (
32
).

The Lorentz tensor
B_{\mu\nu}
is a curvature tensor for multiple reasons. The first one is that it describes the changing rate of the norm vector
n_{\mu}(x)
when
x
is moving on the hypersurface
\Sigma
, namely

B_{\mu\nu}=-\Delta^{(n)}_{\mu\alpha}\frac{\partial n_{\nu}(x)}{\partial x_{\alpha}}\,.

Recall that one of the Frenet–Serret formulas for a curve in 3D Euclidean space reads

\frac{d\bm{N}(s)}{ds}=-\kappa(s)\bm{T}(s)+\tau(s)\bm{B}(s)

(71)

with
s
the arclength of the curve,
\kappa
the curvature,
\tau
the torsion,
\bm{T}
the unit tangent vector,
\bm{N}
the unit norm vector, and
\bm{B}
the cross product of
\bm{T}
and
\bm{N}
.
These two equations resemble each other because we consider a 3D hypersurface embedded in the
(1+3)D
Minkowski space, where torsion will not appear.

On the other hand, we can examine the meaning of
B_{\mu\nu}
by a plane curve
C
on the hypersurface
\Sigma
.
Suppose
x
is a point on
C
, within a neighborhood of
x
, the curve is defined as the intersection of
\Sigma
and a plane
P
.

P
is spanned by
n_{\mu}(x)
and a vector
\xi_{\mu}
perpendicular to
n_{\mu}(x)
, where
\xi_{\mu}
satisfies
\xi^{2}=-1
.
Namely,

(y-x)_{\mu}=a~\xi_{\mu}+b~n_{\mu}(x),\,\forall y\in P

with
a,\,b\in\mathbb{R}
. Therefore, the curve
C
can be locally expressed by implicit function
b(a)
defined by

F(x_{\mu}+a~\xi_{\mu}+b~n_{\mu})=0.

As
x\in C
and
n\cdot\xi=0
, the function satisfies
b(0)=b^{\prime}(0)=0
, where
b^{\prime}(a)\equiv db(a)/da
.
In a neighborhood of
x
,
dy_{\mu}/da
is a space-like vector because it’s not zero and
(\partial F/\partial y_{\mu})(dy_{\mu}/da)=0
, which can be used to define the unit tangent of the curve
C

T_{\mu}=\frac{dy_{\mu}/da}{\sqrt{-\frac{dy_{\mu}}{da}\frac{dy^{\mu}}{da}}}=\frac{\xi_{\mu}+n_{\mu}db/da}{\sqrt{1-(db/da)^{2}}}.

(72)

Considering all the points on
C
, we conclude that it is a space-like curve.
Therefore, the infinitesimal arclength of the curve can be defined as

ds=\sqrt{-\frac{dy^{\mu}}{da}\frac{dy_{\mu}}{da}}da,

which means at point
x
,
ds=\sqrt{-\xi^{2}}da=da
.
We evaluate
dT_{\mu}/ds
at point
x

\frac{dT_{\mu}}{ds}\bigg|_{a=0}=\frac{dT_{\mu}}{da}\bigg|_{a=0}=n_{\mu}\frac{d^{2}b}{da^{2}}\bigg|_{a=0}.

With the
implicit function theorem
, we obtain

\frac{db}{da}=-\frac{\xi_{\mu}(\partial F/\partial y_{\mu})}{n_{\mu}(\partial F/\partial y_{\mu})},

and utilizing
d/da=(dy_{\mu}/da)(\partial/\partial y_{\mu})
, we finally have

\frac{d^{2}b}{da^{2}}\bigg|_{a=0}=-\frac{1}{n^{\mu}\frac{\partial F}{\partial y^{\mu}}|_{y=x}}\xi^{\mu}\xi^{\nu}\frac{\partial^{2}F(y)}{\partial y^{\mu}\partial y^{\nu}}\bigg|_{y=x}=\xi^{\mu}\xi^{\nu}B_{\mu\nu}(x).

Following the Frenet–Serret formulas, the curvature of the space-like curve
C
,
\kappa
, should be defined as
dT_{\mu}(s)/ds=\kappa(s)n_{\mu}(s)
.
Therefore, we conclude that the curvature of
C
is

\kappa=\xi^{\mu}\xi^{\nu}B_{\mu\nu}.

(73)
