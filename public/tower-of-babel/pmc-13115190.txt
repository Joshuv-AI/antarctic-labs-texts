Non-Metricity in Information Geometry
Authors: Tatsuaki Wada, Antonio Maria Scarfone
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The importance of non-metricity in information geometry is described through studies on gradient flow from the perspective of Weyl geometry. The Amari–Centsov tensor Ckij is the non-metricity tensor with respect to the α connection ∇(α) and Fisher metric gij. We derive the explicit expression of the α connection based on the non-metricity ∇k(α)gij=αCkij. The scalar field obtained from a potential function in information geometry plays a key role and characterizes Weyl’s gauge field, Weyl’s non-metricity, and the rate of the potential function during the associated gradient flow.

1. Introduction

Information geometry (IG) [1,2,3] provides a powerful, interdisciplinary framework that bridges information theory, statistics, and physics by applying differential geometry to the space of probability distributions. The theoretical information structure in IG is called α geometry. Let us begin with a brief explanation of α geometry. IG has been developed by mainly considering a parametrized statistical model(1)pθ(x)=expθiFi(x)−Ψ(θ), which is called an exponential probability distribution function (pdf). Here the θ potential Ψ(θ) is determined from the normalization of pθ(x) as Ψ(θ)=ln∫dxexp(θiFi(x)), i.e., the cumulant generating function. The expectation coordinates {ηi} are obtained from Ψ(θ) as(2)ηi=∂Ψ(θ)∂θi, which are the dual functions of the natural coordinates {θi}. The Legendre dual potential function Ψ⋆(η):=θiηi−Ψ(θ) is the (negative sign of) entropic function(3)Ψ⋆(η)=Epθ[lnpθ(x)], and the natural coordinates satisfy θi=∂Ψ⋆(η)/∂ηi.

By assuming Ψ(θ) or Ψ⋆(η) is convex, the positive definite matrices gij(θ) and gij(η) are obtained from the Hessian matrices as(4)gij(θ)=∂2Ψ(θ)∂θi∂θj,gij(η)=∂2Ψ⋆(η)∂ηi∂ηj, respectively. These matrices satisfy the relation gij(η)gjk(θ)=δki, where δki denotes Kronecker’s delta.

As one-parameter (α) extensions of the Levi–Civita connection ∇(0), the α connection ∇(α), and its dual ∇⋆(α) are introduced [1,2,3] and defined by their coefficients(5)Γ(α)ijk(θ):=(1−α)2Cijk(θ),Γ⋆(α)ijk(η):=(1+α)2C⋆ijk(η), respectively. Here Cijk(θ) and its dual C⋆ijk(η) are called Amari–Centsov tensors defined by(6)Cijk(θ):=∂3Ψ(θ)∂θi∂θj∂θk,C⋆ijk(η):=∂3Ψ⋆(η)∂ηi∂ηj∂ηk, respectively. It is known that the Fisher metric gij(θ) and Amari–Centsov tensor Cijk(θ) are characterized by Centsov’s theorem [4]. A coordinate system in which all connection coefficients become zero is called affine coordinate system. It is well-known that α=±1 plays a central role [1,2], in which the connection coefficients Γ(1)ijk(θ) or Γ⋆(−1)ijk(η) vanish as shown from the factor (1−α) or (1+α) in (5). Hence the θ coordinates (η-coordinates) are affine coordinates for the connection ∇(1) (∇⋆(−1)). This feature is known as the dually flat structure in IG.

Incidentally, for a given metric gjk and an independent connection ∇, the non-metricity tensor(7)Qijk:=∇igjk, characterizes the deviation from the metricity condition ∇igjk=0. Unlike in the case of metricity, raising or lowering the indices of a vector vi (or tensor) under the covariant derivative ∇ is not straightforward. Since(8)∇kvi=∇k(gijvj)=(∇kgij)vj+gij∇kvj=Qkijvj+gij∇kvj, it follows that(9)gij∇kvj=∇kvi−Qkijvj.In other words, a connection ∇ and a metric g are non-commutatative. For a given velocity ui, the acceleration ai is defined by(10)ai:=uk∇kui, and the anomalous acceleration ai˜ is defined by(11)a˜i:=uk∇kui=uk∇k(gijuj)=gijuk∇kuj+ukuj∇kgij=gijaj+ukujQkij.Hence lowering the index of the acceleration ai:=gijai and the anomalous acceleration a˜i are different when the non-metricity tensor Qkij are non-zero.

In IG, because both curvature and torsion are zero in dually flat spaces, non-metricity must play a role. However, as far as we know, the technical word “non-metricity” is almost never used in the literature on IG, except by Hirano and Nagahama [5]. They pointed out in their Section 2.3 [5] that the non-metricity tensor associated with the α connection ∇(α) is proportional to the Amari–Centsov tensor Cijk(θ), i.e.,(12)Qijk(α)(θ):=∇i(α)gjk(θ)=αCijk(θ).Here, the definition (7) of a non-metricity tensor is different (opposite in sign) from the definition in Ref. [5]. It is well-known in the field of IG [2,3] that the Amari–Centsov tensor Cijk(θ) is important for defining the α connection ∇(α) with its coefficients(13)Γi(α)jk(θ):=ΓiLCjk(θ)−α2giℓ(θ)Cℓjk(θ), where ΓiLCjk(θ) denotes the coefficients of the Levi–Civita connection ∇LC with respect to (w.r.t.) the Fisher metric gij(θ). However, in the conventional explanations in IG, the α connection ∇(α) is introduced by definition (13). We shall explore the importance of the non-metricity in IG in Section 4.

Non-metricity is also a key ingredient in Weyl geometry [6,7,8], which is a generalization of Riemannian geometry. In Weyl geometry, a parallel transported vector changes its length, which is characterized by Weyl’s non-metricity(14)Qijkw(θ):=∇Wigjk(θ)=ωi(θ)gjk(θ),∇Wigjk(θ)=−ωi(θ)gjk(θ), where ωi(θ) is called a Weyl gauge field, and ∇W is Weyl’s connection. The coefficients ΓiWjk(θ) of Weyl’s connection ∇W are obtained [7,8] from Weyl’s non-metricity (14) as follows:(15)ΓiWjk(θ)=ΓiLCjk(θ)−12δjiωk(θ)+δkiωj(θ)−giℓ(θ)ωℓ(θ)gjk(θ).It is worth noting that the non-metricity tensor Qijk(α)(θ) in (12) and Qijkw(θ) in (14) are different. The former is a tensor w.r.t. the α connection ∇(α) and proportional to Cijk(θ). In contrast, the latter is a tensor w.r.t. the Weyl connection ∇W and proportional to gjk(θ).

Together with our collaborators, gradient flow [9,10,11,12] in IG had been studied from different perspectives and is related to some different fields of physics: anisotropic Huygens’ equations in geometric optics [13]; analytical mechanics and black hole thermodynamics [14]. Furthermore Ref. [15] studied gradient flow w.r.t. the divergence functions from the motion of a null (light-like) particle in curved spacetime. It was the first study [16] of gradient flow from the perspective of Weyl geometry. Ref. [17] studied gradient flow in IG in spacetime and showed Weyl’s gauge symmetry. Research focusing on the mathematical and scientific structures common to different fields lies at the heart of SUURI engineering. Prof. Amari is widely recognized as the founder of SUURI engineering, and IG is a field of SUURI engineering. Many papers and books concerning the foundation of IG have been published, but it is impossible to list them all, as they are too many. Caticha [18] developed IG as a foundation for physics, particularly in the fields of statistical inference and thermodynamics. We would also like to develop a physical foundation of IG.

In this paper, we show the importance of non-metricity in IG through some studies on gradient flow from the perspective of Weyl geometry and α geometry. The next section gives a quick review of gradient flow equations in IG. In Section 3, we first briefly review Weyl geometry, Weyl’s symmetry, and Weyl proper time, all of which are characterized in terms of a Weyl gauge field ωi. Then in Section 3.1, we show that gradient flow equations are obtained from the variational principle of the Weyl symmetric action Sa in (30). The importance of the scalar field η2(θ) is emphasized from some different points of view. In Section 4, we show the importance of non-metricity in IG. After confirming the non-metricity relation of (12), we show that the second term in (13) represents a disformation tensor. In Section 4.1, we derive the coefficients Γi(α)jk(θ) of the α connection ∇(α) based on the non-metricity tensor (12) characterized by the Amari–Centsov tensor. Section 4.2 shows that gradient flow equations are characterized by autoparallel equations in IG. The final Section 5 is devoted to our conclusion and perspective. Appendix A shows the derivation of the autoparallel equations in α geometry.

2. Gradient-Flow Equations

We briefly explain gradient flow equations [9,10,11,12] in IG. For the sake of simplicity, we consider gradient flow equations w.r.t. the θ potential Ψ(θ), i.e.,(16)dθidt=gij(θ)∂Ψ(θ)∂θj, in the θ-coordinate system. The right-hand side expresses the natural gradient [19] w.r.t. the Fisher metric. By using (4), the left-hand side of (16) is rewritten as(17)dθidt=∂θi∂ηj︸gij(θ)dηjdt=gij(θ)dηjdt, and using (2) the right-hand side of (16) leads to gij(θ)ηj. Consequently, the gradient flow Equation (16) in the θ-coordinate system are equivalent to linear differential equations(18)dηi(t)dt=ηi(t), in the η-coordinate system. This linearization is a merit due to the dually flat structure [1,2,3] in IG.

The other set (or the dual) of gradient flow equations are given by(19)dηidt=−gij(η)∂Ψ⋆(η)∂ηj, in the η-coordinate system. Similarly, they are equivalent to the linear differential equations(20)dθi(t)dt=−θi(t), in the θ-coordinate system In previous works [13,14,16], gradient-flow equations w.r.t. the θ- or η-potential functions were considered. It is worth emphasizing that the two sets of differential Equations (16) and (19) describe different processes in general [13,14]. In addition, the evolutional parameter t in the gradient flow Equations (16) and (19) is a non-affine parameter.

Gradient flow (16) and (19) can be related to Hamilton flow characterized by the following Hamiltonians(21)H(θ,η)=12gij(θ)ηiηj−12η2(θ),H(η,−θ)=12gij(η)θiθj−12θ2(η), respectively. Here η2(θ) and θ2(η) are given by(22)η2(θ):=gij(θ)∂Ψ(θ)∂θi∂Ψ(θ)∂θj,θ2(η):=gij(η)∂Ψ⋆(η)∂ηi∂Ψ⋆(η)∂ηj, respectively. Note also that H(θ,η) and H(η,−θ) are related through the canonical transformation (θi,ηi) to (ηi,−θi) [14].

It is worth noting that the scalar field η2(θ) characterizes the rate of the θ potential, since it is related to the θ-potential function Ψ(θ) as follows:(23)dΨ(θ)dt=∂Ψ(θ)∂θidθidt︸gij(θ)∂Ψ(θ)/∂θj=gij(θ)∂Ψ(θ)∂θi︸ηi∂Ψ(θ)∂θj︸ηj=η2(θ). where the relations (2) and (16) are used. Similarly, the scalar field θ2(η) characterizes the rate of the η potential as(24)dΨ⋆(η)dt=−θ2(η).Since −Ψ⋆(η) is the entropy S(η) w.r.t. the pdf pθ(x), the scalar field θ2(η) characterizes the rate of the entropy dS(η)/dt in the gradient flow (19).

3. Weyl Geometry

As briefly mentioned in the Introduction, Weyl geometry is a generalization of Riemannian geometry based on the gauge field ωi(x), which specifies the non-metricity (14). The Weyl connection (15) is an extension of the Levi–Civita connection with a given gauge field ωi(x). A parallel transported vector changes in length according to the non-metricity (14). When ωi(x)=0, Weyl geometry reduces to Riemannian geometry.

Weyl [6,20] introduced the concept of Weyl’s symmetry and the equivalent classes of (gjk,ωi). An action or equation, which consists of a metric gjk(x) and a scalar field φ(x), has Weyl’ symmetry, if it is invariant under the Weyl transformation(25)gjk(x)→Ω2(x)gjk(x),φ(x)→Ω−1(x)φ(x),ωi(x)→ωi(x)+∂ilnΩ2(x), which makes a local rescaling of gjk(x), φ(x) and the Weyl gauge field ωi(x) with a positive scalar function Ω(x). It is worth noting that the Weyl connection (15) is invariant under the Weyl transformation (25).

Each equivalent class (gjk(x),ωi(x)) is related to another class by the Weyl gauge transformation (25). Fixing the gauge incolves choosing a certain equivalent class of (gjk(x),ωi(x)). A special class of (gjk(x),ωi(x)=0) is called the Einstein gauge, in which non-metricity (14) reduces to metricity, and consequently Weyl geometry reduces to Riemannian geometry.

Next, for an arbitrary time parameter λ, the most general form [7,17] of the autoparallel equations in Weyl geometry is given by(26)uk(λ)∇Wkui(λ)=12u2(λ)du2(λ)dλ−ωk(θ)uk(λ)u2(λ)ui(λ), where ui(λ):=dxi/dλ. For a given Weyl gauge field ωi(x) and tangent vector uj(τ):=dxj(τ)/dτ, parameter τ is called Weyl proper time [21] if it satisfies(27)du2(τ)dτ=u2(x)ωi(x)ui(τ), where u2(τ):=gjk(x)uj(τ)uk(τ). Note that the tangent vector uj(x) depends on parameter τ, and the right-hand side of (26) becomes zero for Weyl proper time τ.

Having briefly explained Weyl geometry, the next subsection reconsiders gradient flow in IG from the perspective of Weyl geometry.

3.1. The Gradient-Flow in Weyl Geometry

We introduce the infinitesimal line element ds of the arc-length parameter s in θ space as(28)ds2:=gij(θ)dθidθj.Then infinitesimal line element dτ of Weyl proper time τ is related [16,17] as(29)dτ=η2(θ)ds, where the positive scalar function η2(θ) is defined in (22). Then the action integral is given [17] by(30)Sa=∫dτ=∫η2(θ)ds=∫η2(θ)gij(θ)dθidθj=:∫Lθ,dθdλdλ, where L(θ,dθ/dλ) denotes the Lagrangian for arbitrary time parameter λ. It is worth noting that action Sa is invariant to the reparametrization of λ. Therefore it is crucial to specify Sa. Later we specify the time parameter t in order to make the Lagrangian L(θ,dθ/dt) describe gradient flow Equation (16). Note that η2(θ) should not be confused with η2:=gij(θ)ηiηj. They have different functional dependences but the same value after taking the variation of Sa.

In addition, action Sa is invariant to the local rescaling of(31)ds→Ω(θ)ds,andη2(θ)→η2(θ)Ω2(θ), with a positive scalar function Ω(θ). The square root form of the Lagrangian in (30) involves a technical difficulty, especially for a massless particle, which corresponds to the case η2(θ)=0. It is convenient to introduce an einbein field e(λ) [22] as then the Lagrangian becomes(32)Lθ,dθdλ=12e(λ)gij(θ)dθidλdθjdλ+e(λ)2η2(θ).Here the expression of einbein e(λ) is obtained from the Euler–Lagrange (EL) equation ∂L/∂e(λ)=0 w.r.t. einbein e(λ) as follows:(33)e(λ)=1η2(θ)gij(θ)dθidλdθjdλ=1η2(θ)dsdλ.Note that if we substitute this expression of e(λ) into (32), the square root form in (30) is recovered. One readily finds from (33) that any set of parameters λ and λ′ is related as(34)e(λ)dλ=e(λ′)dλ′.

Now let us choose parameter t such that the einbein e(t) in (33) is unity, i.e.,(35)e(t)=1η2(θ)dsdt=1, which means that ds=η2(θ)dt. Combining this relation with (29), we have dτ=η2(θ)dt. Then the canonical momenta ηi:=∂L/∂(dθi/dt) and EL equations of Lagrangian (32) w.r.t. θi(t) become (36) ηi=gij(θ)dθjdt,(37)dηidt=12∂gjk(θ)∂θidθjdtdθkdt+12∂η2(θ)∂θi.The relations (36) are equivalent to the gradient flow equations (16). By differentiating both sides of η2(θ) in (22), we have12∂η2(θ)∂θi=12∂gjk(θ)∂θi∂Ψ(θ)∂θj︸ηj∂Ψ(θ)∂θk︸ηk+gjk(θ)∂2Ψ(θ)∂θi∂θj︸gij(θ)∂Ψ(θ)∂θk︸ηk,which are rewritten by using (36) as follows:(38)12∂η2(θ)∂θi=12∂gjk(θ)∂θigkm(θ)︸−∂igkm(θ)gjk(θ)dθmdtgjℓ(θ)dθℓdt+gjk(θ)gij(θ)︸δikηk=−12∂gkm(θ)∂θigjk(θ)gjℓ(θ)︸δkℓdθmdtdθℓdt+ηi=−12∂gjk(θ)∂θidθjdtdθkdt+ηi.Substituting this into (37) leads to linearized Equation (18). In this way, we can derive both gradient flow Equations (16) and (18) from the Lagrangian (32). We hence show that the variational principle holds for gradient flow in IG.

At this point, some comments are required. Firstly, the scalar function η2(θ) is key in action Sa in (30), which is invariant to local rescaling (31).

Secondly, in previous work [16], we showed that, for gradient flow Equation (16) in θ space, the Weyl gauge field is(39)ωi(θ)=−∂ilnη2(θ), in which ωi(θ) is the i-th component of the gradient of scalar function −lnη2(θ), i.e., this is Weyl integrable geometry. Again, quantity η2(θ) is key in Weyl gauge ωi(θ), which characterizes Weyl’s non-metricity (14).

Thirdly, as mentioned in the Introduction, quantity η2(θ) characterizes rate (23) of θ-potential function Ψ(θ) during gradient flow (16). Rearranging the well-known relation dΨ(θ)=(∂Ψ(θ)/∂θi)dθi=ηidθi and using (23), it follows that(40)0=ηidθi−η2(θ)dt=:ωIG, which has the same form as the Poincaré–Cartan form ωPC:=pidqi−E(t)dt in analytical mechanics. Ref. [15] discussed complete integrability on the Pfaffian Equation (40) and obtained the null Hamiltonian describing gradient flow (16) in the following form: H(θ,η)=gij(θ)ηiηj−η2(θ), which is the square root version of H(θ,η) in (21). For details, please refer to Section 3 in [15]. As Cartan [23] emphasized the importance of ωPC, in our case, ωIG is important for describing gradient flow. By taking the exterior derivative of −ωIG, we have(41)−dωIG=dθi∧dηi+dη2(θ)∧dt, which is an extension of the standard symplectic form −d(ηidθi)=dθi∧dηi. Here again, quantity η2(θ) plays an important role. In standard symplectic geometry, the value of any Hamilton function Hsym=Hsym(θ,η) is a constant of motion (the total energy of the system). Hence any Hsym(θ,η) cannot describe a dissipative system.

Finally, let us focus on the Lagrangian (32) for choosing the time parameter t. Since the canonical momenta is ηi=gij(θ)(dθj/dt), the associated Hamiltonian is(42)H(θ,η):=ηidθidt−Lθ,dθdt=12gij(θ)ηiηj−12η2(θ), which is the Hamiltonian H(θ,η) in (21).

3.2. Relation to the α-Connection

Here we discuss the relation [16] between the Weyl connection and the α connection when they describe the same gradient flow Equation (16).

Gradient flow Equation (16) are equivalently expressed [16] in terms of ui(t)=dθi/dt as autoparallel equations(43)uk(t)∇k(−1)ui(t)=ui(t), in α geometry with α=−1 connection ∇(−1).

Next, let us choose the Weyl gauge field ωi(θ)=−∂lnη2(θ)/∂θi as (37) and the time parameter t, which satisfies (35). Then it follows that u2(t):=ui(t)ui(t)=η2(θ), where ui(t):=gij(θ)uj(t)=ηi. The corresponding Weyl autoparallel Equation (26) are(44)uk(t)∇Wkui(t)=−ωkuk(t)ui(t), which have a clear physical interpretation. The left-hand side represents acceleration w.r.t. velocity ui(t)=dθi/dt. The right-hand side represents force proportional to velocity ui(t), whose proportional factor is a function of velocity uk(t) and Weyl gauge field ωk(θ).

In addition, by applying the Weyl connection to both sides of (36) and contracting with uk(t), we have(45)uk(t)∇Wkηi=uk(t)uj(t)∇Wkgij(θ)︸ωk(θ)gij(θ)+gij(θ)uk(t)∇Wkuj(t)︸−ωk(θ)uk(t)ui(t)=0, which means that ηi is parallel-transported along a trajectory θi=θi(t) of Equation (44). In other words, recalling that the right-hand side of (45) expresses anomalous acceleration (11), we see that Equation (45) states that the anomalous acceleration is zero. In the Weyl gauge (gjk(θ),ωi=−∂ilnη2(θ)), Equations (44) and (45) characterize gradient flow (16).

As shown in [16,17], gradient flow (16) is equivalently described bynboth the autoparalell Equation (44) in Weyl geometry and by the autoparallel Equation (43). Consequently, Weyl connection ∇W is related to the α connection of α=−1 as(46)ΓiWjk(θ)uj(t)uk(t)=Γ(−1)ijk(θ)uj(t)uk(t)+∂lnη2(θ)∂θjuj(t)ui(t)−ui(t).

4. Non-Metricity in the α-Geometry

As stated in the Introduction, non-metricity tensor (7) characterizes the deviation from metricity (or metric condition) ∇igjk=0 in general. Because both curvature and torsion are zero in dually flat spaces in IG, a non-metricity tensor must play a key role. As Hirano and Nagahama [5] pointed out, the non-metricity tensor (12) associated with the α connection ∇(α) is proportional to the Amari–Centsov tensor Cijk(θ). This can be confirmed by using the definition (13) of the α connection as follows:∇i(α)gjk(θ)=∂igjk(θ)−Γ(α)ℓij(θ)gℓk(θ)−Γ(α)ℓik(θ)gjℓ(θ)=∇LCigjk(θ)︸0+αCijk(θ)=αCijk(θ), where metricity ∇LCigjk(θ)=0 is used.

Generally, an affine connection is characterized by curvature, torsion, and non-metricity, and the coefficients Γiaffjk(x) of a general affine connection can be decomposed [24] as(47)Γiaffjk(x)=ΓiLCjk(x)+Kijk(x)+Lijk(x), where ΓiLCjk(x) are the coefficients of the Levi–Civita connection, and Kijk(x) denotes a contortion tensor:(48)Kijk(x):=12Tijk(x)−Tjik(x)−Tkij(x), with the torsion tensor Tijk(x):=Γiaffjk(x)−Γiaffki(x), and Lijk(x) denote a disformation tensor(49)Lijk(x):=12Qijk(x)−Qjik(x)−Qkij(x).The well-known Levi-Civita connection has a non-zero curvature, zero torsion (or torsionless), and zero non-metricity. The Weizenböck connection [25] has a non-zero torsion, zero curvature (or tele-parallel), and zero non-metricity.

We see that non-metricity tensor Qijk(α)(θ) (12) is totally symmetric when changing indices i,j,k because Cijk(θ):=∂3Ψ(θ)/∂θi∂θj∂θk. Thus the disformation tensor (49) w.r.t. Qijk(α)(θ) is(50)L(α)ijk(θ):=12Q(α)ijk(θ)−Q(α)jik(θ)−Q(α)kij(θ)=−12Q(α)ijk(θ)=−α2giℓ(θ)Cℓjk(θ).Consequently, we find that the second term in the (13) coefficients of the α connection expresses the disformation tensor L(α)ijk(θ). This interpretation gives a physical meaning to the α connection.

4.1. Derivation of the Coefficients of the α-Connection

Starting from the non-metricity (12) with the totally symmetric Amari–Centsov tensor (6), we derive the coefficients of the α connection ∇(α) in a similar way to obtain the coefficients of the Levi–Civita or Weyl connection.

Since the α connection is torsion-free, as explained in (47), the Γi(α)jk(θ) coefficients are decomposed as(51)Γi(α)jk(θ)=ΓiLCjk(θ)+Aijk(θ).Here Aijk(θ) are the components of the unknown tensor we want to obtain and are symmetric when exchanging subscripts, i.e., Aijk(θ)=Aijk(θ).

Now let us write the non-metricity (12):(52)αCijk(θ)=∇i(α)gjk(θ)=∂igjk(θ)−Γ(α)ℓik(θ)gℓj(θ)−Γ(α)ℓjk(θ)giℓ(θ)=∇LCigjk(θ)︸0−Aℓik(θ)gℓj(θ)−Aℓjk(θ), where we use the metricity of the Levi–Civita connection ∇LC. By permuting indices (i,j,k), we obtain(53)αCjki(θ)=−Aℓji(θ)gℓk(θ)−Aℓki(θ).Again permuting the (i,j,k) indices leads to(54)αCkij(θ)=−Aℓkj(θ)gℓi(θ)−Aℓij(θ)gkℓ(θ).By calculating α(Cijk(θ)+Cjki(θ)−Ckij(θ)) by utilizing the torsion-free condition (Aℓij(θ)=Aℓji(θ)) and from the fact that the Amari-Centsov tensor Ckij(θ) is totally symmetric, we obtain(55)Aijk(θ)=−α2giℓ(θ)Cℓjk(θ), which is equivalent to the disformation tensor L(α)ijk(θ) (50). We have derived expression (13) of the α connection from non-metricity (12).

4.2. Autoparallel Equations Describing Gradient Flow

Based on non-metricity (12), we can obtain the general autoparallel equations in α geometry(56)uk(λ)∇k(α)ui(λ)=12u2(λ)du2(λ)dλ−αCjkℓ(θ)uj(λ)uk(λ)uℓ(λ)ui(λ).Appendix A provides the derivation of this autoparallel equation. Similarly, we obtain(57)uk(λ)∇k(α)ui(λ)=12u2(λ)du2(λ)dλ+αCjkℓ(θ)uj(λ)uk(λ)uℓ(λ)ui(λ).By using (38) and u2(t)=ui(t)ui(t)=η2(θ), we have(58)du2(t)dt=∂η2(θ)∂θidθidt=2ηi︸ui(t)−∂gjk(θ)∂θi︸Cijk(θ)dθjdtdθkdtui(t)=2u2(t)−Cijk(θ)ui(t)uj(t)uk(t).By substituting this relation into the right-hand side of (56) with λ=t and α=−1, it follows that(59)uk(t)∇k(−1)ui(t)=ui(t), which is equivalent to the autoparallel Equation (43) describing gradient flow (16). In this way we derived (43) as a special case (α=−1,λ=t) of the general form (56) of autoparallel equations.

In addition, by choosing (α=1,λ=t) and substituting (58) into (57), it follows that(60)uk(t)∇k(1)ui(t)=ui(t), which is equivalent to (18) because ui(t)=ηi and uk(t)∇k(1)ui(t)=dηi/dt.

Finally, recall that the scalar field η2(θ)=u2(t) characterizes the rate of the θ-potential function Ψ(θ), as shown in (23). 5. Conclusions and Perspective

We studied some aspects of the fundamental geometrical structures in IG from the perspectives of non-metricity and Weyl’s symmetry. The importance of non-metricity was emphasized. The Amari–Centsov tensor specifies the non-metricty Qijk(α)(θ) in (12) w.r.t. the α connection ∇(α), meanwhile Weyl’s non-metricity in (14) specifies it w.r.t. the Weyl connection ∇W. The scalar field η2(θ) in (22) plays a key role hereand characterizes Weyl’s gauge field ωi=−∂ilnη2(θ), Weyl’s non-metricity Qijkw(θ) in (14), and the rate of the θ-potential function, as shown in (23) during gradient flow (16). Starting from the non-metricity (12) characterized by the Amari–Centsov tensor, we derived the explicit expression of the α connection, for the first time. We showed that, for gradient flow in IG, the variational principle holds based on action Sa in (30), which is invariant to local rescaling (31). This enabled us to determine gradient flow in IG as having Hamilton (or Lagrangian) dynamics. In addition, Caticha [27] pointed out that “IG describes the conformal geometry of space”, and it allows an arbitrary choice of the local scale. The arbitrariness of the choice of local scale corresponds to the invariance under the local rescaling (31) in this work. They also pointed out that the distance described in IG, e.g., ds in (28), is dimensionless. This means that the parameter space, e.g., θ or η space in IG, is dimensionless, and hence IG has a conformal structure.

Recently, non-metricity formulations of gravity, particularly the Symmetric Teleparallel Equivalent of General Relativity (STEGR) [28] and its extensions of gravity, have attracted attention. As is well-known, general relativity is based on curvature, whereas STEGR is based on non-metricity. We hope our results open the door for the further development of the fundamental structures in IG from the perspective of non-metricity.
