Single-molecule kinetic exploration of functional sub-states in an evolving phosphotriesterase
Authors: Morito Sakuma, Dhani Ram Mahato, Ferran Feixas, Colin J Jackson, Eiji Nakata, Sílvia Osuna, Nobuhiko Tokuriki
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractEnzymes can exist in multiple stable functional states or sub-states, revealing substantial molecule-to-molecule heterogeneity in catalytic rates even within populations expressed from a clonal gene. Increasing evidence suggests that biological systems have evolved to exploit these functional sub-states to regulate reaction rates and enzyme multi-functionality or promiscuity. Thus, understanding such heterogeneity in evolutionary dynamics is central to elucidating the mechanisms underlying enzyme adaptability and evolvability. To address this, we analyze the evolutionary changes in functional sub-states using single-molecule kinetics. We measure the functional sub-states of wild-type phosphotriesterase (PTE) and 18 evolved variants spanning a trajectory from native PTE activity to promiscuous arylesterase function. This is further supported by an investigation into the conformational sub-states of selected variants using molecular dynamics simulations. Our results reveal that functional optimization is tightly coupled with the redistribution of underlying functional and conformational sub-states, particularly involving the open and closed dynamics of functionally essential loop 7. These findings provide direct evidence that enzyme evolution involves coordinated shifts in conformational heterogeneity and its functional manifestations, together shaping enzyme function.Subject terms: Enzyme mechanisms, Single-molecule biophysics, Molecular evolution

Introduction

Unraveling how enzymes catalyze chemical reactions remains a central and enduring pursuit in the life sciences, driven by a fundamental interest in the molecular foundations of biological function. Enzymes have long been regarded as remarkably robust molecular machines, owing to their high catalytic specificity and their essential roles in sustaining metabolic networks, generating energy, and facilitating processes such as DNA replication and transcription. This perceived robustness has supported a classical view of enzymes as highly optimized, deterministic catalysts.

However, a growing body of evidence reveals that proteins and enzymes, though robust at the systems level, are intrinsically heterogeneous at the molecular level1–14. Remarkably, even proteins expressed from a clonal gene can exhibit long-lived variations in their functional states and catalytic rates. These variations, referred to as functional sub-states, are shaped by a variety of factors, including pH and temperature changes11,15, post-translational modification6,16,17, ligand and cofactor binding8,18,19, different oligomerization12,14,20–22, and metastable conformational states1–5,7,9,10,13,23–26. These functional sub-states are not transient noise but instead remain stable over thousands of functional events. In enzymes, their existence has profound implications: they may govern reaction rates1,2,5,9,23–25,27–31, shape fluxes in the metabolism of diverse cellular components8,11,32–35, affect organismal survival36–38, and even contribute to epigenetic variation39. Collectively, these findings indicate that molecular heterogeneity is not merely tolerated but has likely been harnessed through evolution to enhance an organism’s functional flexibility and adaptivity.

Previous kinetic studies, both at the ensemble and single-molecule levels, have significantly contributed to uncovering this hidden layer of complexity. Pre-steady-state kinetics have captured functional sub-state transitions as biphasic behaviors in various enzymes36–38,40,41. In single-molecule kinetic assays, micron-sized reactor arrays and related technologies have enabled the simultaneous measurement of thousands of functional sub-states in enzyme populations5,9,11,42–44. Intriguingly, single-molecule kinetic assays of β-glucuronidase and alkaline phosphatase reveal that variants sampling a broader spectrum of functional sub-states exhibit enhanced catalytic promiscuity, suggesting that enzyme populations with greater heterogeneity might be enriched in molecules capable of high reactivity toward multiple substrates42,44. However, these studies have largely relied on a limited number of variants along the evolutionary trajectory or on variants differing from the wild-type by only one or a few mutations. Consequently, how functional sub-states are optimized during evolution—and whether their redistribution reflects the accompanying structural transitions—remains unexplored. Thus, directly probing these sub-states along an experimentally reconstructed evolutionary trajectory could provide deeper insights into the mechanisms underlying enzyme evolvability (Fig. 1).

Protein evolution at the single-molecule level.

Enzymes can evolve such that their promiscuous functions increase to the same level as their original native function through bifunctional intermediates exhibiting comparable activity in both their native and promiscuous functions. During this process, active site structure and conformational dynamics are optimized to accommodate new function48. This study aims to clarify the evolutionary dynamics of functional sub-states, as reflected in single-molecule turnover rate distributions, and to examine potential links to the optimization of catalytic activity and conformational fine-tuning.

To address this, we focused on Pseudomonas diminuta phosphotriesterase (PTE), a highly active Zn2+-dependent metalloenzyme well-characterized for its evolutionary dynamics45–47. Previously, we evolved the wild-type PTE (R0) to enhance its promiscuous arylesterase (AE) activity through 22 rounds of directed evolution (forward evolution), generating a highly efficient and specialized AE enzyme (R22)45,46. A further 12 rounds of evolution from R22 to restore PTE activity (reverse evolution) resulted in a bifunctional enzyme (revR12) exhibiting high efficiency for both activities46. This functional transition was underpinned by gradual, evolution-driven changes in the dynamics of active-site loop 7. In the wild-type enzyme, loop 7 is highly mobile, sampling both open (for substrate binding and/or product release) and closed (catalytic) conformational sub-states, which are essential for PTE activity. During forward evolution, the open state was progressively frozen out, resulting in predominantly sampling of the closed state, which was optimized for efficient AE activity. Subsequently, loop mobility was restored during reverse evolution, recovering PTE activity45,46,48. These well-characterized, bidirectional evolutionary trajectories—linking 105-fold activity changes with systematic rewiring of conformational dynamics—establish PTE as a powerful model for elucidating how functional sub-states emerge and are optimized through evolution, particularly when coupled with single-molecule kinetic measurements (Fig. 1).

Here, we measured the single-molecule kinetics of wild-type and 18 evolved variants for both PTE and AE activities, enabling us to characterize how functional sub-states shift throughout this evolutionary process. Notably, we identify a tight coupling between the emergence of optimized functions and the redistribution of underlying functional sub-states. Moreover, a molecular dynamics (MD) simulation uncovered conformational dynamics that link to the observed functional sub-states. Our study highlights that enzyme evolution involves coordinated shifts in conformational heterogeneity and in the functional sub-states that arise from them.

Results

Development of a single-molecule kinetic assay of PTE

We opted for a single-molecule kinetic assay of PTE variants using resorufin-diethylphosphate (RDP) and resorufin-butyrate (RB) for measuring functional sub-states (Fig. 2A and Supplementary Methods). These substrates have relatively similar leaving groups (resorufin), comparable to those of the original substrates used in the directed evolution (naphthol; Fig. 2A and Supplementary Fig. 1A)45,46. We confirmed that RDP and RB largely recapitulated the changes in the activity observed during the directed evolution in an ensemble kinetic assay (Fig. 2B, Supplementary Fig. 1B, and Supplementary Data1). R0 displayed a highly efficient PTE activity (kcat/Km = 1.3 × 108 M−1 s−1) and a weaker AE activity (kcat/Km = 3.6 × 104 M−1 s−1). Throughout evolution, these activities gradually reversed, with R6 displaying high activities toward both substrates (kcat/Km for RDP and RB were 3.5 × 106 and 2.5 × 107 M−1 s−1, respectively). Eventually, the last variant in the forward evolution, R22, became highly specific to AE activity (kcat/Km = 3.5 × 107 M−1 s−1 for RB vs. 1.4 × 103 M−1 s−1 for RDP). In the reverse evolution, PTE activity was restored while AE activity was largely unaffected; consequently, revR12 exhibited high PTE and AE activities (kcat/Km for RDP and RB were 1.2 × 107 and 1.1 × 107 M−1 s−1, respectively).

We established single-molecule kinetic assays for RDP and RB hydrolysis using a microreactor array device (Fig. 2A and see Methods)43,44,49. Each device displays over 30,000 reactors, into which an assay solution with highly diluted enzyme and 200 μM substrate was applied. This allowed the reaction rates of more than 700 single-enzyme reactions to be monitored without leakage of enzyme and substrates (Supplementary Fig. 2A and Supplementary Fig. 3). The histograms of fluorescence intensity at 10 min typically showed three peaks (peak 0, 1, and 2) (Fig. 2C). Peak 0 corresponds to reactors without enzyme molecules, peak 1 to single-molecule activity, and peak 2 represents reactors with two or more enzyme molecules. The relative populations of these peaks were consistent with a Poisson distribution43,44,49, with the majority of the reactors falling into peak 0, followed by peak 1 and peak 2 (79%, 18%, and 2% of molecules belonged to peak 0, peak 1, and peak 2 when λ was 0.23) (see Supplementary Methods).

We measured single enzyme kinetics of wild-type and 18 PTE variants across the laboratory evolution trajectories (Supplementary Fig. 4). 14 of them were measured with both RDP and RB substrates while three variants (R0, R2, R3) were measured with only RDP, and two (R18, R22) were measured with only RB due to the detection limit of the single-molecule kinetic assay. We followed the reaction over time and found that each reactor with a single enzyme molecule showed a linear increase in fluorescence intensity for at least 10 min, which is consistent with previous studies with other enzyme systems5,9,11,42–44,49. This suggests that each enzyme molecule retains a metastable functional sub-state (see Supplementary Methods). We calculated catalytic activity as the mean turnover rate using a calibration curve that correlates the concentration of resorufin dye with its fluorescence intensity in the microreactor array (Supplementary Fig. 5B). The average catalytic activities observed in the single-molecule kinetic assay were consistent with those in the ensemble kinetic assay (Supplementary Fig. 6), indicating that the environment of the device and the method of measurement did not affect the function of PTEs or introduce any experimental artifacts.

Gradual shift of functional sub-state heterogeneity in PTE variants

The time-course measurements of the various reactors identified peak 0 and peak 2 populations, where either zero or more than two enzyme molecules were present, respectively. However, it also revealed that peak 1 exhibited a bimodal distribution that could be separated into two distinct populations (Fig. 3A and Supplementary Fig. 4), and this distribution was not associated with peak 2 according to Poisson statistics (see Supplementary Methods); we refer to them as peak 1H and peak 1 L. Notably, the bimodal distribution changes for each variant along the trajectory (Supplementary Fig. 7A). In addition, the population ratio of the two peaks was nearly constant regardless of the measured activity, and the turnover rate of peak 1H was ~1.6 times higher than that of peak 1 L (Supplementary Fig. 7B, C). Thus, rather than a functionally relevant difference, it appears that this “splitting” of the peak 1 activity into two populations may be a systematic artifact of the experiment. Indeed, using single-molecule measurements along with molecular characterization methods, including fast protein liquid chromatography and blue native PAGE, we determined that this apparent splitting of peak 1 activity actually reflects heterogeneity in the active-site metal ions (Supplementary Fig. 7–11, Supplementary Methods), i.e., peak 1H represents fully active states with zinc bound at the active site, whereas metal loss or incorporation of non-zinc metals leads to the low-activity state, peak 1 L. This type of metal-dependent heterogeneity has also been observed in other metalloenzymes, including alkaline phosphatase43,44. Regardless, for the following analysis, we focused on the most active peak 1H, while also performing analyses on the combined peak 1 L and peak 1H as a control.

The second type of heterogeneity we observed in the functional sub-states was the width of the activity distribution. To quantify the degree of heterogeneity, we calculated the coefficient of variation (CV1H, %) or relative standard deviation of peak 1H (Fig. 3A, see “Methods”). Most of the enzyme variants exhibited a wider distribution (CV1H = 10-23% in Fig. 3B) than the detection limit with our system (CVlimit = 9.3 ± 1.0%, mean ± SE, n = 4), which is calculated from the spontaneous reaction rate of the substrate. Interestingly, we found that the CV1H values gradually changed throughout the evolution, and the change correlated with the functional transition (Fig. 2B and Fig. 3B). For instance, the CVPTE remained around 15% for the first eight rounds of evolution, during which variants exhibited PTE-specific or bifunctional features, before dropping rapidly to around 10% as the AE activity increased (Fig. 3B). The same trend is observed in reverse evolution, with the CVPTE increasing to 15% as PTE activity was restored. The changes in CVAE exhibited a similar trend, with a gradual decrease to 10% at R22, and then an increase during the reverse evolution (Fig. 3B). Note that enzyme molecule adsorption onto the reactor surface did not change these observations (Supplementary Fig. 12, see Supplementary Methods).

The CV showed a clear trend when compared to the activity ratio (kcat/Km; PTE/AE): AE-specialized variants (PTE/AE < 10−1) exhibited low CV1H, while bifunctional variants (10−1 < PTE/AE < 101), as well as PTE-specialized variants (PTE/AE > 101), displayed higher CV1H values (Fig. 3C, D). It is worth mentioning that extending the analysis to include peak 1 L yielded the same overall trends since the width of the activity distribution is essentially comparable across peaks (Supplementary Fig. 7D and Supplementary Fig. 13). Overall, the observed changes in the heterogeneity of peak 1 were associated with the evolutionary shift toward AE specialization. As enzymes evolved in this direction, their activity distributions became progressively narrower. Conversely, functional transitions toward bifunctionality increased heterogeneity.

Correlated alterations between conformational sub-states and functional sub-states

During the evolution of AE activity, the heterogeneity of functional sub-states gradually decreases, as indicated by a narrowing activity distribution. We hypothesized that this trend could also be coupled with optimization of conformational sub-states, as has been proposed in enzyme evolution: those required for recognizing multiple substrates were likely progressively purged during forward evolution to favor sub-states that enhance AE activity and were subsequently restored during reverse evolution7,50,51.

Indeed, the conformational dynamics of local structures, such as loops 4, 5, and 7 (Fig. 4A), undergo modulation during PTE evolution. Loop 7 is highly mobile in the PTE-specific and bifunctional variants and undergoes functionally essential conformational dynamics between open (for substrate binding and/or product release) and closed (catalytic) conformations for PTE substrates, but not for AE substrates52. In the forward evolution, a key mutation (His254Arg) was introduced to stabilize the naphthol-leaving group of AE substrates, and the stabilization of a bent Arg254 conformer by loop 7 in the closed state was central to the emergence of high AE specificity through freezing out the open state that is required for PTE45,46,48. In the reverse evolution, the mobility of loop 7, i.e., sampling of the open state, was restored to regain PTE activity45,46,48. These trends were observed in the B-factors of loop 7, which decreased during forward evolution and increased during reverse evolution (Fig. 4B and Supplementary Fig. 14A). Loops 4 and 5, on the opposite side of the active site cleft, likely gained mobility to compensate for the rigidification of loop 7 in AE-specialized variants. However, the magnitude of their conformational fluctuation is much smaller than that of loop 7 (Fig. 4B and Supplementary Fig. 14A).

To gain further insight into the evolution of the conformational sub-states, evolutionary alteration of dynamics in loops 4, 5, and 7 was assessed by performing MD simulations with RDP and RB substrates binding or apo (substrate-free) states (Supplementary Fig. 15). Specifically, for loop 7, we measured the distance between Ala270 and Trp131, which is located on the opposite side of loop 7 with less fluctuation in the MD trajectory (as a proxy for the sampling of the open state), whereas for loops 4 and 5, distances between the central residues of loop 4 (Thr173–Val176) and loop 5 (Ala203–Gln206) were used (see Methods). From the distance changes, the conformational landscapes were reconstructed (Fig. 4D, Supplementary Fig. 16, and Supplementary Fig. 17; see “Methods”). In the conformational landscape of loop 7 in the apo state, R0 exhibited two distinct peaks at 14 and 21 Å, corresponding to catalytic (closed) and substrate binding/product release (open) states (Supplementary Fig. 16 and Supplementary Fig. 17B). In the forward evolution, the population of the open conformation significantly decreased, with the mean distance reduced from 16 ± 4 Å in R0 to 9 ± 4 Å in R22. In the reverse evolution (to improve PTE activity), the sampling of the open state was restored, with the mean distance increasing from R22 (9 ± 4 Å) to revR12 (14 ± 4 Å). Loops 4 and 5, however, displayed only limited local fluctuations (Supplementary Fig. 16 and Supplementary Fig. 17C, D). Note that the evolutionary trends observed in the MD simulations were almost consistent with those inferred from crystallographic B-factors (Supplementary Fig. 14), supporting the reliability of the MD-based analysis of loop dynamics.

We then quantitatively analyzed the conformational heterogeneity of the loops in substrate-bound states by calculating the coefficients of variation (CVloop7 and CVloop4–5) from the 1D conformational landscapes (Supplementary Fig. 17; see Methods) and plotting them against evolutionary round (Fig. 4C), with heterogeneity defined as the breadth of the distance distributions sampled by each loop. The overall trends of CV changes in the MD simulation were almost identical to those of crystal structures; CVloop7 decreased in the forward evolution and was then restored in the reverse evolution. CVloop4-5 showed trends opposite to those of CVloop7.

The alteration of loop 7 heterogeneity was further analyzed using shortest path maps (SPMs), which identify residue pairs with correlated motions during MD simulations (Supplementary Fig. 18A and Supplementary Data 3)51,53–55. In forward evolution, an increase in the number of correlated edges in substrate binding states was observed (Supplementary Fig. 18A and Supplementary Fig. 19). This increase reflects reduced conformational flexibility, as stronger dynamical coupling synchronizes residue motions and limits independent fluctuation, consistent with observations from the laboratory evolution of enzymatic activity53,56. Moreover, edges between Arg254 and Phe/Leu271—key for stabilizing the closed state—were present in bifunctional and AE-specialized enzymes (Supplementary Fig. 18B). In contrast, reverse evolution resulted in fewer edges and the loss of Arg254–Leu271 interactions in the RDP-bound state, consistent with the restored flexibility of loop 7 (Supplementary Fig. 18 and Supplementary Fig. 19).

The freezing out of functionally essential loop 7 dynamics during AE evolution, and their recovery in reverse evolution, closely resembled the changes observed in the functional sub-states (Fig. 3B). Over the course of evolution, as genetic mutations accumulated, changes in the functional sub-states and conformational sub-states or B-factors obtained by crystal structural analysis exhibited similar trends (Supplementary Fig. 20). Thus, both conformational and functional heterogeneity exhibited positive correlations (Fig. 4E, F). These observations imply that enzyme evolution may be driven by selection for reduced structural heterogeneity, which in turn shapes the landscape of functional sub-states toward optimized activity.

Discussion

By leveraging high-resolution single-molecule approaches, we reveal that functional sub-states undergo co-evolution with the optimization of function, providing direct evidence that this form of molecular heterogeneity is actively modulated during evolution. Such optimization of the functional sub-states has been anticipated, as enzyme promiscuity is understood to arise from ensembles of functional states within the enzyme population7,50,51. Each enzyme molecule can exhibit distinct functional states toward native or promiscuous substrates, and mutations can progressively shift the population balance, selectively stabilizing states that enhance a specific activity. Consequently, functional states not subject to selective pressure—including those linked to unrelated or disfavored activities—may be gradually purged in enzyme evolution, resulting in a decrease in overall functional sub-states. In the PTE case, the reduction of functional sub-states during AE specialization reflects the purging of sub-states that were not under selective pressure, such as PTE activity. In contrast, during reverse evolution, phenotypic reversion enabled the enzyme to regain bifunctionality, resulting in a restoration of these previously lost functional sub-states for PTE activity.

The observed correlation between functional sub-states and structural dynamics—specifically, the association between narrower functional sub-state distributions in enzymes with high AE activity and reduced loop 7 dynamics—may indicate a possible mechanistic connection between them that occurs on very different timescales. In terms of the energy landscape, the metastable functional sub-states correspond to deep energy wells separated by high barriers, whereas the shallow regions within these wells correspond to rapidly interconverting local conformational fluctuations. Within each metastable well, small variations in local motions of individual molecules may modulate catalytic activity, generating the observed ensemble of functional sub-states at the single-molecule level. In other words, the sampling of long-lived metastable states by individual molecules allows for subtle, molecule-specific variations in local motions, some of which may manifest as distinct substrate binding affinities and variations in promiscuous activity57. Thus, as the number of metastable states increases, the ensemble of enzymes collectively explores a broader spectrum of local conformational states. Consistent with this interpretation, during the evolution of bifunctional enzymes toward AE specialization, the diversity of functional sub-states diminishes, accompanied by a reduction in the accessible conformational space of loop 7, which is critical for catalytic function.

Further investigation of long-lived conformational states will be essential to elucidate the structural principles underlying functional sub-states. This understanding can be further strengthened by examining additional evolutionary trajectories, including those trapped in suboptimal states, to provide a more comprehensive view of how functional sub-states contribute to enzyme evolution56. Measuring biophysical characteristics at the single-molecule level—such as dynamic interconversions between functional sub-states—would provide a more mechanistically grounded description of enzyme ensembles, enabling the disentanglement of static heterogeneity and dynamic transitions. Moreover, alternative molecular mechanisms—such as metal heterogeneity, post-translational modifications, and cofactor binding—may also modulate functional sub-states and enzymatic promiscuity57, highlighting the importance of analyzing evolutionary alterations from multiple molecular perspectives. Together, these efforts will deepen our understanding of the structural and biophysical basis of functional sub-states, clarify the evolutionary significance of heterogeneous enzyme ensembles, and provide key insights to guide enzyme engineering and protein design.

Methods

Expression and purification of PTEs

Genes encoding wild-type PTE (EC 3.1.8.1, txid293) and its variants were cloned into the pET-Strep vector used in previous studies1,2. The pET-Strep-tag-PTE plasmid was transformed into BL21DE3 cells by electroporation. The transformed cells were pre-cultured overnight at 37 °C in 5 mL of LB medium containing 100 μg/mL ampicillin. For protein expression, 0.5 mL of the bacterial culture was transferred to 10 mL of Overnight Express Instant TB medium (MilliporeSigma, Burlington, MA, USA) supplemented with 100 µg/mL ampicillin and 200 µM ZnCl2 in a 50 mL tube. Bacteria were cultured at 30 °C for 8 h, and then protein expression was induced at 16 °C for 12 h. Cells were harvested by centrifugation and stored at −80 °C for at least 1 day. We followed a previously developed method for lysate preparation1,2. Cells were lysed with 2 mL of a lysis buffer (1:1 mixture of B-PER and 50 mM Tris-HCl (pH 8.5) containing 100 mM NaCl, 200 µM ZnCl2, 100 µg/mL lysozyme, and 0.2 µL of benzonase). After incubation for 1 h at room temperature, cell debris was spun down, and the lysate containing the expressed protein was collected. MagStrep beads (IBA Lifesciences, Goettingen, Germany) were used for purifying strep-tagged PTE. The beads were pre-equilibrated with wash buffer 1 (50 mM Tris-HCl (pH 8.5) containing 100 mM NaCl and 200 µM ZnCl2) and mixed with the lysate for 30 min on ice with shaking. After binding, the beads were washed three times with wash buffer 1 and wash buffer 2 (50 mM Tris-HCl (pH 7.5) containing 100 mM NaCl, 200 µM ZnCl2). The protein was eluted with 50 mM biotin dissolved in wash buffer 2 by incubating it on ice with shaking for 10 min. Finally, the eluted protein solution was desalted by using a spin desalting column (Bio-Gel, Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA, USA) equilibrated with wash buffer 2. The collected protein solution was stored at 4 °C until use.

For large-scale expression and purification, 200 mL of Overnight Express Instant TB medium was prepared in a 2 L flask. 30 mL lysis buffer per 8 g bacterial pellet was used for lysate preparation. The cell lysate was passed over a Strep-Tactin column (IBA Lifesciences) with a 2 mL resin volume for protein binding. The column was pre-equilibrated with 6 mL of wash buffer 1. After binding, the column was washed with 6 mL wash buffer 1 and wash buffer 2. Then, the protein was eluted with 10 mL of 50 mM biotin solution. After elution, the protein solution was desalted using a desalting column (Econo-Pac 10DG, BioRad, Hercules, CA, USA), and the protein solutions were concentrated using a spin filter (Spin filters 10k, Pall Laboratory, Port Washington, NY, USA).

Fabrication of a microreactor array device

The microreactor array device was fabricated with photolithography and etching4–6. The cover glass (24-40 mm, thickness 0.13-0.17 mm, Millipore Sigma) was incubated in 10 M KOH solution (Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc., Waltham, MA, USA) for 30 min. After washing with deionized water and drying, hexamethyldisilazane was coated onto the glass at 3000 rpm for 30 s using a spin coater. After 5 min baking at 98 °C on a hot plate, fluoroacrylic polymer (FluoroPel, CYTONIX) was coated onto the glass at 3000 rpm for 30 s. The glass was then baked at 98 °C for 5 min. A positive photoresist (AZ-4903, Merck KGaA, Darmstadt, Germany) was coated onto the glass at 500 rpm for 10 s, then 8500 rpm for 40 s. The glass was baked at 98 °C for 5 min. Photolithography was then performed using a mask aligner (NxQ4006 aligner, Neutronix-Quintel, Morgan Hill, CA, USA) with a chrome photomask containing a 4 μm hole array. The photoresist on the glass was then developed using a photoresist developer (AZ300 MIF, Merck KGaA) for 5 min. The patterned glass coverslip was washed with deionized water. After drying, the glass was etched with O2 plasma using a reactive ion etching machine (PE-50, Plasma-Etch, Carson City, NV, USA). Then, the remaining photoresist on the glass was entirely removed using 2-propanol and ethanol, and the glass was rinsed with deionized water. The diameter and thickness of the reactor were measured using a 3D interferometer (Polytec, Irvine, CA, USA). The diameter and thickness of the reactor array used in these experiments were ~5.0 μm and 100 nm, respectively.

Single-molecule assay

Reaction buffer (100 mM Tris-HCl (pH 8) supplemented with 200 µM ZnCl2, 0.1% Triton X-100, 25 µg/mL bovine serum albumin (MilliporeSigma), 20 µM Resorufin) was used for the single-molecule kinetic assay. Resorufin-diethylphosphate (RDP), resorufin-butyrate (RB, MilliporeSigma), and resorufin dye (MilliporeSigma) were dissolved in dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) and stored at −20 °C until use.

The flow channel was constructed on the surface of the microreactor array device using double-sided adhesive tape (CAPLINQ, Assendelft, Netherlands) and a top cover made of polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS, Dow Corporate, Midland, MI, USA). The silicone elastomer and curing agent were mixed at a 1:10 ratio using a mixer (Thinky, Tokyo, JAPAN). After being degassed for 30 min, PDMS was solidified on a petri dish at 60 °C for 1 day. PDMS was cut to the size of the device, and the inlet and outlet holes were punched with a puncher. The double-sided tape was cut out in the shape of the flow path and was applied to the surface of the device. Then, the PDMS lid was attached on top of the double-sided tape.

For the single-molecule kinetic assay, the diluted enzyme (final ~5 pM) and reaction buffer were first mixed, followed by the addition of the fluorogenic substrate (final 200 µM), just before applying the reaction mix to the flow channel. After introducing the reaction mix, air was applied from the same inlet to remove the excess solution and seal the reactor. Measurements began 30 sec after sealing. A new flow channel was used for each assay. The same setup as described above was used in the reaction solution exchange experiment. To prevent x-y drift during sample loading, the device was fixed on the microscope stage, and the reaction solution was applied using a gel-loading chip (VWR, Radnor, PA, USA). After measuring the first reaction, the device was washed twice with 200 μL of fresh reaction buffer, except for the substrate. Following a 10-min incubation to ensure the complete removal of the reactants, the second reaction was measured by applying the reaction buffer with the substrate.

All images of single-molecule assays were acquired using an inverted fluorescence microscope (Eclipse Ti-E, Nikon, Tokyo, Japan) equipped with a 40× objective lens (Nikon, Plan Apo, NA = 0.9), multi-wavelength LED illumination (SpectraX, Lumencor, Beaverton, OR, USA), EM-CCD camera (imagEM, C9100-13, Hamamatsu Photonics, Shizuoka, Japan), an incubator for temperature control, and a Perfect Focus System (Nikon). The fluorescence of the fluorescein dye was measured using filter sets (excitation 550/15 nm, dichroic 573 nm, emission 598/25 nm, Nikon). All measurements were performed using the NIS-Element software (AR 5.21.01, Nikon). The temperature of the incubator was set to 22 °C. Time-course images were taken at 2-min intervals for 10 min. The cross-reactor diffusion of the fluorescein dye was evaluated by photobleaching using a field diaphragm (Nikon) to limit the area of excitation light (Supplementary Fig. 3).

Analysis of single-molecule data

The single-molecule activity was determined by plotting the histogram of the fluorescence intensity distribution at the end-point (Fig. 2C). We typically observed an enzyme-free population (peak 0), a bimodal distribution next to peak 0 (peak 1 L and peak 1H), and an additional minor peak (peak 2). Firstly, we fitted the peak 0 with a single Gaussian function, and the mean intensity (MI0) and standard deviation (SD0) were calculated. Enzyme activity was observed above a threshold value defined as MI0 + 10 × SD0. Then, we statistically identified peak 2 and removed it from the data to accurately analyze single-molecule activity4–6. After removing peak 0 and peak 2, the time trajectories of enzyme molecules were linearly fitted, and slopes (arb. units/min) were calculated. Slopes can be converted to the turnover rate (s−1) by using a calibration curve that provides the concentration of the resorufin as a function of fluorescence intensity (Supplementary Fig. 5B). After the conversion and subtracting autohydrolysis rates of RDP and RB substrates (16 and 26 (s−1)), the distribution of the turnover rate was plotted (Supplementary Fig. 4).

We fitted the distribution of turnover rates with a double Gaussian and extracted the mean turnover rate (MT) and standard deviation (SD) for peak 1H (MT1H and SD1H) and peak 1 L (MT1L and SD1L). Subsequently, the variation of the turnover rate, or functional sub-states, of each peak was quantified as the coefficient of variation (CV): CV1H or 1L (%) = SD1H or 1L/MT1H or 1L × 100. Bulk ensemble kinetic assay

Bulk ensemble kinetic assay of PTE was performed in a 384-well black plate using a plate reader (Synergy H1, BioTek, Winooski, VT, USA) operated with Gen5 6.04 software (BioTek). For measurements with the resorufin dye, the excitation and emission wavelengths were set to 550 nm and 590 nm, respectively. Changes in fluorescence intensity were acquired at thirty-second intervals at 26 °C. A reaction solution (50 μL) was prepared by mixing PTE and substrate in the reaction buffer (100 mM Tris-HCl (pH 8) supplemented with 200 µM ZnCl2, 0.1% Triton X-100, 25 µg/mL bovine serum albumin (MilliporeSigma)), and the mixture was then applied to the plate wells. Three independent measurements were taken to analyze the mean and SE of enzyme activity. Enzyme activity was determined from a linear fit of the increase in fluorescence intensity over time and converted to the turnover rate with a calibration curve (Supplementary Fig. 5A). The initial rate was measured using six different concentrations and fitted to the Michaelis-Menten equation.

Calculation of B-factor from crystal structures

We calculated the B-factor using the previously solved crystal structures of R0 (4PCP), R2 (4XD5), R6 (4XAG), R8 (4XAY), R18 (4XAZ), R22 (4PCN), revR6 (4PBE), and revR12 (4PBF). In each subunit of the dimer, Cα B-factors were normalized by the mean Cα B-factor of the entire polypeptide chain. Normalized B-factors for loop 4 (residues 170–175), loop 5 (residues 200–210), and loop 7 (residues 257–275) were then extracted and averaged across both subunits, with each subunit treated as an independent replicate to evaluate measurement reproducibility.

MD simulations of PTEs

Several PTEs, including PTE-R0, R6, R8, R22, revR6, and revR12, were simulated using the Amber18 package, with both substrate-free and substrate-bound states (RDP and RB)13. Structures of PTE were obtained from the RCSB Protein Data Bank with the following PDB IDs: 4PCP (R0), 4XAG (R6), 4PBE (revR6), 4XAY (R8), 4PBF (revR12) and 4PCN (R22). The structure of resorufin butyrate (RB) was obtained from the PubChem database (CID 500716). Diethylphosphate group was added to the resorufin (CID 69462) to create resorufin diethylphosphate (RDP) using ChemDraw and further optimized with Gaussian09 (see below). These two substrates were used to superpose onto the naphthalene ring of the transition state analog of the AE substrate bound to R18 (PDB ID: 4E3T) to determine the binding positions of RDP and RB. Further, these substrates were superimposed onto both chains A and G. Topology and parameters of the substrates were generated using the Gaussian09 software package by utilizing B3LYP/6-31 G* level of theory. The protonation states of the protein were corrected using H + + server14. Further, these systems were prepared using the tleap program to generate a system with the protein inserted in a cubic water box of 15 Å. The system was neutralized using counter ions (Na + , Cl-). For R0 and revR12, one Na+ was added. For R8 and R22, one Cl- was added. For revR6, two Cl- ions were added. For the R8 system, there were no neutralizing ions added (Supplementary Table 1). One OH group was position-restrained with the two Zn atoms at a distance of 2 Å. Additionally, the OH group of ligands was distance-constrained with one of the Zn atoms with force constant of 60 kcal/mol/Å². The simulation was performed for the apo and holo (RB and RDP) structures. Minimization was performed for 10,000 steps. The heating process was performed stepwise by increasing the temperature from 50 K to 300 K. Equilibration and production runs were performed at 300 K. The equilibration process was done in two steps for 20,000 steps. These equilibrated structures were simulated using Gaussian-accelerated molecular dynamics (GaMD) for 500 ns, which adds a harmonic boost potential to enhance conformational sampling. This approach preserves the underlying thermodynamic and kinetic information while enabling the exploration of slow conformational rearrangements that would otherwise require orders of magnitude longer timescales. Typically, GaMD can accelerate conformational sampling by one to two orders of magnitude relative to classical MD15–17, depending on the magnitude of the applied boost potential and the system’s energy landscape. This makes it particularly effective for capturing loop motions and domain rearrangements in enzymes such as PTE, where catalytically relevant conformational sub-states are separated by barriers on the microsecond-to-millisecond timescale. A dual boost for dihedral energy and potential energy was applied, with the threshold energy set to the upper bound. The upper limit of the standard deviation for the dihedral and total energy boost potentials was set to 6.0 kcal/mol. First, a 10 ns conventional MD simulation was run to calculate the GaMD acceleration parameters. In a second step, a 50 ns GaMD equilibration run was performed. The average and standard deviation of boost potentials were collected every 0.5 ns. Furthermore, a 500 ns GaMD production run was performed in three independent replicas. Similarly, the apo structures were also simulated for 500 ns (in three replicas) using the Amber18 program, with the ff14SB force field and TIP3P water model13 used as the solvent. ff14SB force field gives balanced and validated representation of both backbone and side-chain conformational behavior. Further, ff14SB in combination with TIP3P water model yields stable protein dynamics with realistic RMSF profiles and h-bond dynamics.

Shortest path maps (SPMs) were calculated considering for the Cα atoms of the protein residues along the trajectory. The distance and correlation matrix were used for building the SPM networks, which display the pairs of nearby (within 6 Å) residues that are highly correlated during the molecular dynamics trajectory18–21. The significance threshold, which determines the positions represented in the final SPM graph, was kept at the default (value of 0.3). MD trajectories were analyzed using MDTraj and PyTraj, and SPM results were visualized in PyMOL. The total number of edges represents the combined edges observed in the two subunits. The initial coordinates, simulation input files, and the final output coordinates are provided in a public repository (10.5281/zenodo.17744955)58.

Conformational landscapes were calculated based on loops 4, 5, and 7 dynamics. All atoms of four residues from Loop 4 (Thr173, Gly174, Lys175, and Val176) and four residues from Loop 5 (Ala203, Ala204, Ser205, and Gln206) were selected to calculate inter-loop distances between loops 4 and 5. These residues vary slightly among the enzyme variants and across chain G; however, comparable residue ranges were consistently chosen to accurately capture the dynamics of loops 4 and 5. To monitor Loop 7 motions, the distance between the Cβ atom of Ala270 (located in Loop 7) and the CZ atom of Trp131 (which lies within the catalytic site) was measured. This distance typically ranges from 7 to 16 Å and serves as a sensitive indicator of loop 7 dynamics, which are strongly influenced by ligand heterogeneity and local residue interactions22. We plotted the distributions of inter-residue distances at each MD simulation frame (Supplementary Fig. 16), calculated the median (Med) and median absolute deviation (MAD), and determined the coefficient of variation (CV, %) as MAD divided by Med.
