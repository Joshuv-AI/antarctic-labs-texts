Complex odontoma associated with an impacted upper central incisor: a case report and review of literature
Authors: Nourelhoda Alim, Fatema Akhter, Sally Elhaddad, Amal Alasadi, Maryam Naser Alabudulwahab
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundComplex odontomas are noncancerous growths made of dental tissues such as enamel, dentin, and cementum. They are usually found in the back part of the jaw and mostly affect children and young adults. This case is different because it involves an older adult and a less common location, namely the front upper jaw.Case presentationA 35-year-old North African man came to the clinic with a painless swelling near his upper front tooth (central incisor). After a clinical check-up, imaging tests (cone-beam computed tomography), and laboratory tests on the removed tissue, he was diagnosed with a complex odontoma. The tumor was removed through surgery performed with local anesthesia. The patient healed well and had no problems after the procedure. He was then referred to the orthodontist to retract the impacted central incisor, as previously discussed before the surgery.ConclusionThis case shows that complex odontomas can also occur in older adults and in unusual areas such as the front of the mouth or upper central incisors. This contributed to new information to existing medical literature, which usually focuses on younger patients and back teeth. Diagnosis early in the process, choosing the right surgical intervention, and proper follow-up helped ensure a good result for the patient.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13256-025-05657-6.Keywords: Complex odontoma, Tooth impaction, Upper jaw, Odontogenic hamartomas, Hamartomatous growth, Unerupted incisor, Surgical removal

Introduction

Odontomas are among the most prevalent types of tumors created in tooth-formation tissues. According to a study of 1089 cases, odontomas constitute about 42.5% of all odontogenic or tooth-related tumors.1 These tumors are widely benign hamartomas and are generally classified into two types, namely compound odontomas, which resemble rudimentary teeth, and complex odontomas, which are characterized by a disorganized mass of jumbled tissue that does not morphologically look like normal teeth. While compound odontomas are more commonly observed, complex odontomas make up for about 5–30% of all odontogenic tumors.2

Typically lacking symptoms, odontomas are often discovered by incident during routine radiographic examinations. However, they can slow down the formation of permanent teeth, causing clinical manifestations such as delayed eruption, retention of primary teeth, or displacement of adjacent teeth.3 According to the World Health Organization (WHO) classification of odontogenic and maxillofacial bone tumors, odontomas show no preference for either sex.4 Notably, the anterior maxilla is a site frequented by odontomas, with an apparent attachment for the incisor region. In spite of this, the occurrence of complex odontomas with impacted maxillary central incisors is relatively rare. These abnormalities are noncancerous, and no instances of recurrence have been documented. The formation of odontomas may be affected by various factors, such as trauma during the eruption of primary teeth, infections, the persistence of periodontal Malassez remnants, and increased activity of odontoblasts, along with hereditary disorders and genetic mutations [10]

Dentists rarely find complex odontomas near the front teeth, and when they do, diagnosis and treatment prove to be a hassle. The case selected for this study is of a 35-year-old North African man who had a complex odontoma causing impaction of his upper left central incisor. This case is noteworthy as the patient was older, since odontomas usually occur in children. Despite the rarity of the occurrence, the case resulted in successful surgery. There is a significant lack of literature concerning older patients in terms of odontomas.

Hence, this study aims to target this case and provide a comprehensive review comparing its findings with previous ones. By conducting a thorough review, better treatment options and diagnosis can be unearthed for patients, regardless of their age.

Literature review

As we have noted, complex odontomas are uncommon odontogenic tumors that hinder permanent teeth formation, especially in the areas of anterior maxilla. Although odontomas are a well-reported topic, there are several aspects that are not covered, such as odontomas specifically associated with impacted maxillary central incisors. Therefore, this review aims to analyze existing literature on the odontomas and their cases, along with their clinical presentation, diagnostic challenges, and treatment options. By evaluating past case studies and reviews, we will be able to highlight the gap and provide rationale for the selected case study and review.

To begin, one study provided evaluation of an 8-year-old female patient whose chief complaint was hard swelling in the left lower dento-alveolar region, which later proved to be a case of complex odontoma.5 The study claimed that timely diagnosis is necessary to avoid complications. Secondly, another study centered on a case of a 19-year-old female with a swelling in her left cheek, and, as the study showed, clinical examination revealed that it was a case of large-sized odontoma. This showed that although these cases are rare, the growth needs to be removed from the outer area of the face before the bones can be properly treated.6

Another study reported a case of a 13-year-old male who, on his first visit to the dentist, had a complaint of pain and swelling in his anterior maxillary region, which was discovered to be complex odontoma.7 The study also showed that early diagnosis of odontomas is necessary to ensure recovery, and the sole way to treat it is through surgical removal. Literature is not always linear, because another study reported an unusual case of giant maxillary complex odontoma.8 As per its evaluation, the tumor was discovered while examining the patient for maxillofacial cellulitis, and this showed that systematic examinations are necessary to pick up any hidden abnormalities that may get overlooked otherwise.

Moreover, a study reviewed a case of an 8-year-old child who had complex odontoma in the upper front jaw, and treatment was performed through conventional surgical practice.9 The key takeaway from the study was that despite variations in normal tooth eruptions, it is vital to note slight deviations. Regarding age, another study found a large compound-complex odontoma in the anterior maxilla of a 15-year-old male.10 The clinical examination revealed delayed tooth eruption, and the patient received surgery with 1 year follow-up.

Similarly, a study carried out on a 16-year-old Iranian female showed another case of complex odontoma in posterior mandible and that, too, was removed and emphasized the significance of early detection and careful observation.11

Furthermore, a review study evaluated an unusual case where both types—complex and compound—were discovered in a 24-year-old male patient, and surgery was performed on the lesion.12 This showed that both types can occur concurrently as well, although it is rare. In the same sense, a study found multiple odontomas, both complex and compound, and concluded that these tumors can be multiple and involve various quadrants of the jaw.13

Moreover, another case reported a large infected complex odontoma in the left mandibular third molar region and, interestingly, it mimicked osteomyelitis, which proves that these tumors can be hidden.14 Another study described the case of a 9-year-old female whose tumor in the anterior maxilla included various calcified structures resembling mini teeth.15 In another case of an 8–9-year-old individual, it was observed that complex-compound odontoma can be caused by the remodeling of the alveolar bone.16

However, perhaps the most unique study evaluated for this review was the one carried out on a 42-year-old male patient, proving that odontoma does not only occur in youth.17 The study reported that the patient, despite having no significant medical history, experienced this rare occurrence at an older age; regardless of his age, surgical excision was a success.

Another unusual case was reported in a 28-year-old woman, who suffered from a giant odontoma in the right mandibular third molar area, which is the most common site of occurrence for this type.18

A particularly interesting study took a different approach—by analyzing the cases of patients ranging from ages 9 to 32 years, all of which reported complex odontomas.19 This study stressed the necessity of quick treatment right after diagnosis to ensure and preserve facial symmetry. Moreover, a study showed that surgery for odontomas is not always smooth.20 The case study showed that out of the nonextracted teeth, 33 were displaced, 29 were aligned via surgery, and 4 erupted in the follow-up period.

Hence, after evaluating these different studies and the cases they presented, it is clear that they were thorough in their findings and stressed importance in the way both complex and compound odontomas are treated and what complications, if any, can arise. However, a gap is apparent, as the investigation of complex odontomas associated with impacted maxillary central incisors, especially in patients above 30 years, is significantly missing. This gap is particularly evident in the means of late diagnosis, where delayed eruption can lead to unwelcome lesions. As a result, this study aims to report a case that can fill this gap and contribute to scientific literature.

Case presentation

The selected case aims to meet the rationale and evaluate an area of these odontogenic tumors that has not been covered. The details are provided below.

Patient information

The targeted patient in the case study is a 35-year-old North African male. He presented to the clinic concerning strange spacing in the upper front teeth and expressed his desire to correct it for aesthetic and facial symmetry purposes, particularly in the means of leveling out irregularities in tooth alignment. The patient reported no prior dental trauma, and his medical history was clean of any class of illness or any relevant medical conditions. His family and psychosocial history were reported to be unremarkable, and there was no sign of any genetic disorder either. He had not undergone any dental interventions prior to the visit in the case study. He also reported that the issue had been persisting for a long time, but he did not pay a visit to the clinic sooner since there were no symptoms worth taking note of nor any pain. However, there was a noticeable swelling in the anterior maxillary region, which caused him to report to the clinic.

Clinical findings

Upon examination of the patient, several findings were uncovered. Firstly, the intraoral examination revealed that the maxillary left central incisor had not erupted. Instead, an observation of painless swelling, which extended from the anterior maxillary ridge to the vestibular region, was made. The overlying mucosa appeared healthy and in place. However, what was worth noting was that the swelling drove displacement of the adjacent teeth. The maxillary right central and both lateral incisors were pushed back because of the expanding lesion. These clinical findings from a provisional diagnosis ruled either odontoma or a dentigerous cyst to be the main factor of the swelling and the suspected facilitator of the impacted upper left central incisor.

Diagnostic assessment

Moving on, as the patient was brought in for diagnosis, the assessment uncovered certain aspects. For starters, upon performing a cone-beam computed tomography (CBCT) scan, which is a special three-dimensional (3D) X-ray that gives detailed images of the teeth and tissues, a dense mass, measuring approximately 21 mm × 13 mm, was observed in the midline region of the maxilla. The lesion, in a swollen state, expanded and slightly thinned the facial bone, causing issues in facial symmetry.

In terms of radiography, an analysis of the patient’s scans resulted in the discovery of a substantial radiopaque mass, which was surrounded by a radiolucent rim (Fig. 1). A wide array of irregular radiopacities were noted within the mass, and there was a noticeable deviation of adjacent incisors (Fig. 2A, B). These similarities further confirm that the presentation in this case aligns with established imaging characteristics of complex odontomas. Moreover, in the current case, the maxillary left central incisor was horizontally impacted (Fig. 3A, B), and the maxillary right central incisor and both lateral incisors deviated distally because of the expansive behavior of the lesion (Figs. 4, 5).

Therapeutic intervention

Secondly, in terms of therapeutic intervention in the case, major strides were made. The patient in question went through surgical treatment for the complex odontoma impacting the upper central incisor. He was kept under local anesthesia using 2% lignocaine with 1:200,000 adrenaline. Two vertical incisions and a gingival crevicular incision were made to lift a thick and full mucoperiosteal flap. The complex odontomas, once located properly, were then carefully removed. The flap was then arranged back into position and sutured. During the process of the operation, the odontomas appeared as irregular hard masses rather than tooth-like structures (Fig. 6). After the surgery was performed, the patient was prescribed antibiotics and analgesics, along with a set of postoperative care instructions, which were sufficiently detailed to ensure his healing process proceeded smooth. The dental care team also made plans for further dental aesthetic management for full treatment.

Odontomas presenting as irregular hard masses instead of tooth-like structures

This surgical approach aligns closely with the method described by da Rocha Leódido et al. (2015), where a similar mucoperiosteal flap was used for access, and the lesion was removed under local anesthesia while keeping a cautious eye on the surrounding anatomical structures [11]. Their case also included successful surgical management and was followed by detailed aftercare instructions and routine follow-up, similar to the current case.

As per the case’s findings, surgical treatment of the lesion was planned. The observed specimen was sent for histopathological examination, which proved and confirmed the diagnosis of a complex odontoma, rather than mere spacing in the frontal teeth, as the patient had originally suspected. The histology examination also showed a mass of disorganized dental tissues, including bits of enamel, dentin, and cementum (Fig. 5). In the process of this entire assessment, no diagnostic challenges related to costs, personal or cultural beliefs, or access to care were encountered; the process was smooth.

Follow-up and outcomes

Finally, the case came to a close with the follow-up sequence and the outcomes of the treatment. The patient was called back to the clinic once 10 days had passed since the surgical treatment to evaluate the healing process of the wound. Upon examination, the surgical site showed satisfactory healing with no signs of any infection or complications. The patient followed the instructions given to him dutifully, which resulted in this satisfactory follow-up assessment. No problems related to resistance to medicine were present. The histopathological results confirmed the clinical and radiographic impression of a complex odontoma. The prognosis process was satisfactory as well, and the patient was scheduled for aesthetic dental rehabilitation as the next phase of treatment. Since the odontoma was completely removed and had a benign nature, its recurrence was not observed over the 10 days.

Notably, this case emphasized the benign nature of the odontoma and the low likelihood of recurrence, which strengthens confidence in surgical removal as a definitive treatment method when performed correctly.

Meanwhile, Kadri et al. [7] reported a case with a much bigger odontoma situated in the posterior maxilla extending into the sinus cavity. Although the follow-up care in that case required more careful monitoring owing to the lesion’s size and location, the follow-up period still resulted in successful healing and no recurrence. The case demonstrated how even large, complicated odontomas, when removed properly, tend to have a favorable outcome, similar to the present case.

Together, these comparisons affirm that this patient’s outcome is similar to the past documented success of complex odontoma treatments reported in literature, further confirming the effectiveness of timely diagnosis, proper surgery, and appropriate follow-up care.

Discussion

It is noted that understanding and managing odontogenic tumors is an important part of dental and maxillofacial care. Among these, complex odontomas are relatively common but often underdiagnosed when they present in unusual locations or older age groups. This discussion aims to place the current case, which was of a 35-year-old male with a complex odontoma associated with an impacted upper central incisor, within the wider context of existing literature. The focus is on highlighting similarities and differences in presentation, diagnosis, treatment, and outcomes.

This case stands out primarily owing to the location of the lesion in the anterior maxilla and the age of the patient. Most studies on complex odontomas report occurrences in younger individuals, typically in the first two decades of life. For example, Adhikari et al. [4] and Shetty et al. (2013) reported cases involving children or teenagers, often with delayed tooth eruption or impaction as a symptom. In contrast, the current case involved a fully grown adult with no recent history of dental trauma, raising the possibility of a slow-growing, long-standing lesion that only became clinically significant owing to facial asymmetry and swelling.

Moreover, the study’s findings contrast with the case reported by Park et al. [17], where a much larger complex odontoma was found in the posterior mandible, which was significantly bigger and caused cortical expansion and root resorption of nearby teeth [20]. Although both cases targeted bone expansion, the location and severity of involvement were notably different.

Interestingly, as noted in the literature review of this study, a similar case was reported by Samieirad et al. [10] in which a massive complex odontoma also caused facial asymmetry and displacement of nearby anatomical structures [12]. However, their case involved a lesion measuring over 60 mm in the posterior mandible of a young female individual, with broad involvement of the surrounding jaw. Compared with our case, the lesion here was significantly smaller and less significant, though the facial impact and bone expansion showed the same clinical concerns.

Anatomical location further emphasizes this case. While complex odontomas tend to develop more often in the posterior regions of the mandible [1] (Dodamani et al. 2024), anterior maxillary involvement is rare. Dutta et al. [6] presented one such case in the anterior maxilla, but it involved an erupted lesion, unlike the current case where the odontoma remained impacted and asymptomatic for a long time. Similarly, Shetty et al. (2013) described an impacted upper central incisor hindered by an odontoma, which aligns more closely with the current findings. However, their patient was much younger, and their lesion was smaller in comparison.

Radiographically, the current case showed a radiopaque mass with a radiolucent border, where features were similar to most odontomas. Studies by Preoteasa and Preoteasa [14] and Kadri et al. [7] also describe similar radiological patterns, with Kadri et al. noting the significance of CBCT imaging for locating and evaluating complex odontomas, especially when they are large or positioned near vital structures. In the present case, CBCT was helpful in understanding the lesion’s size (21 mm × 13 mm), density, and effect on adjacent teeth. This matches findings in da Rocha Leódido et al. (2015), where CBCT was preferred for detailed 3D imaging of similar lesions.

Moreover, in the case report by Torul et al. [15], surgical intervention required a slightly different flap design owing to the position of the odontoma in the posterior region. However, the authors stressed, as in this case, the importance of removing the lesion completely and ensuring proper placement and suturing of the flap [17]. Their findings also emphasized the irregular nature of complex odontomas, which often present as calcified, non-anatomical masses. These findings were highly similar to what occurred in the current case. Hence, by comparing this case with the case studies of past literature, it can be observed that albeit the sharing of complex odontomas as the targeted dental concern, ways to handle them may vary.

Therapeutically, the lesion in this case was managed with surgical removal under local anesthesia using standard incisions and a mucoperiosteal flap, which is similar to the surgical approach reported by Goswami and Johar (2020) and da Rocha Leódido et al. (2015). During surgery, the odontoma appeared as a hard, irregular element, which matches with the descriptions in Aschaitrakool et al. [5], who removed a large mandibular odontoma. However, unlike in their report, where the lesion had caused structural changes in the jaw, the current case involved only slight thinning of the bone, with no severe complications.

Interestingly, in the case series by Sowmya et al. [18], multiple patients underwent similar surgical treatment under local anesthesia []. However, in some instances, general anesthesia was used depending on the tumor’s location, size, or the comfort level of the patient. While the lesion in this case was limited to the maxillary midline and manageable under local anesthesia, their study provides a broader view of how anesthesia selection may vary on the basis of different clinical elements.

Furthermore, post-surgery outcomes were positive, with the patient showing good healing and no complications. This reflects patterns in multiple studies, including those by Dutta et al. [6] and Alhazmi [9], where odontoma excisions led to complete recovery with low chances of recurrence. Complex odontomas are benign, and when fully removed, they rarely return [7]. This was confirmed in the current case, where follow-up showed healthy healing and a clear path toward aesthetic rehabilitation.

A similar pattern of positive follow-up outcomes was also observed in the case presented by Dutta et al. [6], where an erupted anterior maxillary complex odontoma was removed, followed by smooth wound healing, with no complications arising post-surgery [7]. Their patient, as our patient, worked well with the prescribed care instructions and did not show any signs of infection or tissue reaction, thus also stressing the importance of compliance in a positive follow-up process.

One significant lesson from this case is the importance of timely and accurate diagnosis, especially when odontomas occur in less common sites or at later ages. Kokubun et al. [1] noted in their large study of over 1000 cases that early diagnosis plays an important role in preventing complications such as tooth displacement, facial asymmetry, or cystic transformation. In the present study, though the patient delayed seeking help, the absence of infection and the localized nature of the lesion allowed for effective management without much reconstruction.

Another notable aspect is the psychological and functional effect of anterior maxillary lesions. The upper central incisor is important both for aesthetics and speech. Although the patient had become used to the functional limitations, the impact on facial symmetry was the main factor for seeking treatment. This highlights the role of patient perception in healthcare decisions and matches observations made by Aschaitrakool et al. [5] regarding patient concerns in visible areas of the mouth.

The histopathological findings, namely the disorganized masses of enamel, dentin, and cementum, were in line with definitions of complex odontomas in literature [4] (Shetty et al. 2013). While some cases present mixed features of compound and complex odontoma, such as in the study by Alhazmi [9], the present lesion was confirmed to be purely complex in type, on the basis of both its structure and lack of identifiable tooth-like elements.

One important aspect is the management of the horizontally impacted upper central incisor after the removal of the complex odontoma. The impacted tooth was extracted and aligned orthodontically. This is a critical point, especially for long-term functional and aesthetic outcomes.

In similar cases, studies have shown that orthodontic alignment is often possible and preferred, particularly when the impacted tooth is in a favorable position and the patient is young. For example, Shetty et al. (2013) reported a case where the impacted central incisor was successfully brought into proper alignment after the odontoma was surgically removed. This not only restored function but also improved the patient’s smile and self-esteem.

However, Khan et al. [20] described a case where the impacted incisor could not be saved owing to its position and was extracted. They emphasized the importance of early diagnosis and treatment planning to increase the chances of preserving the impacted tooth.

In our case, the horizontally impacted central incisor was planned to be retracted and realigned by the orthodontist.

Conclusion

This study was limited by a short follow-up period of only 10 days, which was not enough to evaluate the possibility of recurrence, and by the absence of additional case reports that could provide broader data for stronger conclusions. However, it still offers valuable insight into the diagnosis and management of complex odontomas, especially those located near impacted upper central incisors, in an unusual site and age group. The findings encourage clinicians to consider complex odontomas in older patients and in less typical locations. Future research should focus on long-term follow-up to assess recurrence rates, include more cases for comparison, and explore differences in outcomes based on the location of the lesion. Further studies could also investigate the timing of surgical intervention and the role of orthodontic treatment following odontoma removal.
