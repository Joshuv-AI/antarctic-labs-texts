Effect of a digital intervention on sexual and reproductive health knowledge, behaviour, and service uptake among university youths in Lagos, Nigeria: a quasi-experimental study
Authors: Anirejuoritse Alero Chima-Oduko, Kofoworola A. Odeyemi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundYouths in sub-Saharan Africa, particularly Nigeria, face significant barriers to accessing sexual and reproductive health (SRH) information and services because of limited awareness, confidentiality concerns, restrictive policies, and provider attitudes. These barriers contribute to poor SRH knowledge and high rates of HIV infection, unintended pregnancies, and unsafe sexual practices. Digital interventions offer a promising approach to improve SRH outcomes, but evidence of their effectiveness in Nigerian university settings remains limited. This study assessed the impact of a mobile health (mHealth), internet-based, comprehensive sexuality education (CSE) intervention on SRH knowledge, sexual behavior, and service utilization among university students in Lagos, Nigeria.MethodsA quasi-experimental study was conducted among 600 undergraduate students (300 in intervention group and 300 in control group) who were selected from two public universities in Lagos, Nigeria, via multistage sampling. The intervention consisted of weekly live CSE webinars via Google Meets, peer-led WhatsApp discussions, and digital linkages to youth-friendly SRH services. Quantitative data were collected through self-administered digital questionnaires before and after the intervention. Data analysis included baseline and end-line comparisons via Pearson chi-square tests and difference-in-differences (DID) analysis to estimate intervention effects.ResultsAt baseline, 57.7% of the participants in the intervention group and 49.7% of the participants in the control group had good knowledge of SRH (p = 0.05). A total of 41.0% of the respondents in the intervention group and 37.0% in the control group had ever had sex, and consistent condom use was low (23.6% in the intervention group and 28.8% in the control group). Only 26.0% of the intervention group and 23.3% of the control group respondents had access to SRH services. Post-intervention, SRH knowledge improved by 10.2% in the intervention group compared with 2.6% in the control group. Condom use during the last sexual encounter increased significantly by 11.7% (from 32.5% to 44.2%, p < 0.05), in the intervention group but decreased by 7.5% in the control group. Uptake of SRH services in the intervention group rose by 9% (from 26.0% to 35.0%, p < 0.05), alongside increased use of teaching hospitals and primary health care centres (PHCs). Sexual behaviour outcomes were mixed, with some indicators (e.g., multiple sexual partners) worsening post-intervention.ConclusionThe mHealth CSE intervention improved SRH knowledge, increased condom use, and enhanced the utilisation of SRH services among university students. However, its effect on risky sexual behaviours was mixed. For greater effectiveness, digital CSE should be integrated with broader SRH services.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12978-025-02201-z.

Background

Young people aged 10–24 years constitute over 1.8 billion people globally, with about 90% residing in developing countries [1]. Despite being in a generally healthy life stage, adolescents and youths in sub-Saharan Africa (SSA) face significant sexual and reproductive health (SRH) challenges, including unintended pregnancies, sexually transmitted infections (STIs), and unsafe abortions [2–4], compounded by limited awareness of SRH services, confidentiality concerns, restrictive policies, provider bias, and cultural norms [3–5].

In Nigeria, these challenges remain pressing. Adolescents and youths face high risks of early and unplanned pregnancies, unsafe abortions, STIs (including HIV), and maternal complications [6–10]. The country has one of the world’s highest adolescent birth rates, with 15% of girls pregnant or parenting [9, 11] and an adolescent birth rate of 107.3 per 1000 among 15–19-year-olds [12]. Adolescent girls also contribute significantly to Nigeria’s maternal mortality burden—the second highest risk globally [6, 10].

Unsafe abortion remains a major contributor to maternal deaths, largely due to inadequate access to contraception and youth-friendly sexual reproductive health (YFSRH) services [6, 13, 14]. HIV prevalence is also a concern, with young women aged 15–24 years bearing nearly twice the infection burden of males [8, 15].

SRH knowledge among Nigerian youths is low and often shaped by misinformation [14, 16]. For example, although 89% of the adolescents in a study in Benin city were aware of HIV and some contraceptives, their overall knowledge was fragmented [16]. Among university students, fewer than 20% could identify contraceptive methods other than condoms, and over 93% reported inconsistent condom use [17], reflecting deficiencies in sexual and reproductive health (SRH) education, even in tertiary institutions [18, 19].

Sexual behaviour among Nigerian youths is marked by early sexual initiation—over 24% report debuting before age 14—multiple partnerships, and low, inconsistent contraceptive use, often due to distrust, stigma, and perceived reduction in pleasure [19, 20]. Transactional and unprotected oral and anal sex are also increasingly reported, further increasing STI risk [21].

Despite growing awareness, SRH service use among young people in Nigeria remains low due to structural, cultural, and economic barriers [22]. These include limited knowledge of service availability, cost, provider bias, fear of stigmatisation, lack of privacy, confidentiality breaches, and sociocultural taboos [22–27]. Similar trends are observed in South Africa, where negative provider attitudes deter service use [26].

Digital interventions, particularly mobile health (mHealth), are gaining attention as strategies to address these challenges [5, 28]. Increasing smartphone and internet access among Nigerian youths presents opportunities to deliver comprehensive sexuality education (CSE) and link users to services privately and at scale [4, 5, 28–30]. Evidence from other low-and-middle-income countries (LMICs) shows that mHealth can enhance SRH knowledge, promote safer behaviours, and improve service uptake [31–33].

However, in Nigeria, evidence for internet-based mHealth interventions targeting university students is limited. Most prior interventions rely on mobile short message service (SMS) or voice calls, with little or no use of the internet, interaction or peer engagement [29, 30]. These are also targeted at secondary or out-of-school youths. However, university students, who have the digital skills and access needed, remain underrepresented in research—even though they face unique risks from newfound autonomy, reduced supervision, and peer pressure [18, 34].

This study therefore evaluated the effect of a digital, internet-based, CSE intervention on SRH knowledge, behaviour, and service uptake among university students in Lagos. It informs policy and offers context-specific evidence for youth-centred mHealth programming in SSA.

Methods

Study design and setting

A quasi-experimental study with intervention and non-equivalent control groups was conducted over three months, with pre- and post-intervention assessments. The study took place at two public universities in Lagos State, Nigeria—University of Lagos (UNILAG) and Lagos State University (LASU)—selected through simple random sampling (balloting) from all five public universities in the state. Public universities were specifically chosen to be studied—instead of private universities—as they enrol students from diverse socio-economic backgrounds across Nigeria, many of whom are primarily seeking affordable education and face challenges such as limited access to youth-friendly SRH services and economic constraints. UNILAG and LASU were randomly assigned as intervention and control sites, respectively.

Participants

The participants were full-time undergraduate students aged 16–24 years from the selected universities. The eligibility criteria included smartphone ownership with internet access and consent to participate. Students who were on academic leave or who were unavailable for follow-up were excluded.

Sample size

The sample size was calculated via a formula for comparing two proportions in experimental studies [35], assuming 90% power, a baseline contraceptive use of 39.3% [36], and a projected 15% increase [37]. The minimum required sample size was 228 per group. Adjusting for 10% non-response, the target sample was 253 per group. To strengthen the study power, 300 students were ultimately enrolled in each group.

A four-stage multistage sampling process was used:Universities: UNILAG and LASU were selected via simple random sampling from the public universities in Lagos. Study phases

This quasi-experimental study was conducted in three phases: pre-intervention, intervention, and post-intervention.

Pre-intervention phase

Following institutional approval, university lecturers introduced the study to students across academic levels. Systematic sampling was used to recruit participants from student lists provided by class governors. Focus group discussions (FGDs) in both study arms informed the design and digital delivery of the intervention. Based on FGD insights, WhatsApp and Google Meet were chosen as platforms. Separate WhatsApp groups were created for each study arm, and access was granted after completion of an online baseline questionnaire with electronic consent, which was administered via Google Forms.

Partnerships were established with the UNILAG Medical Centre and the Community Health Department of Lagos University Teaching Hospital (LUTH) to facilitate SRH service linkages. Youth-friendly service training was provided for UNILAG staff, and a list of alternative providers in Lagos was compiled. The intervention content was adapted from UNESCO’s International Technical Guidance on Sexuality Education [34] and Nigeria’s Family Life and HIV Education (FLHE) curriculum [38] and included slides, videos, and infographics tailored to adolescents and youth.

Intervention phase

A conceptual framework created by the lead author, combining a theory of change (ToC) and the socioecological (SEC) model [39], grounded in the information-motivation-behavioural skills (IMB) and health belief models [40], guided the intervention. The theory of change (ToC) guiding this digital SRH intervention outlines a sequence from identified challenges—such as poor SRH knowledge, risky sexual behaviour, and limited service access due to stigma, cost, or availability—to targeted interventions and desired outcomes. In response, the digital comprehensive sexuality education (CSE) program was developed to include live webinars, peer-led WhatsApp discussions, and digital referrals. Expected short-term outcomes included improved SRH knowledge, self-efficacy, and safer behaviours, whereas long-term goals aimed at reducing STIs, HIV, unplanned pregnancies, unsafe abortions, and maternal mortality.

The socioecological model (SEC) provides a multilevel framework for implementation. At the individual level, the intervention aimed to enhance knowledge and skills. At the interpersonal level, peer-led discussions promoted positive norms. At the community level, service linkages address access barriers, whereas at the policy level, this study aims to generate evidence to inform youth-focused SRH policy.

The intervention was delivered over six weeks, and it comprised three core components. Live webinars, held weekly via Google Meet, covered SRH topics adapted from UNESCO’s guidance [34] and Nigeria’s FLHE curriculum [38]. Each 60–90-min session included interactive and skills-based activities and was led by the researcher, with 1 GB of mobile data provided to the participants. Peer-led WhatsApp discussions reinforced the webinar content and encouraged dialogue, facilitated by trained volunteers. Service linkages connected participants to curated SRH providers, with referrals to the Department of Community Health at Lagos University Teaching Hospital (LUTH) to support service access.

This combined ToC and SEC approach offered a comprehensive framework for influencing SRH behaviours and increasing health-seeking behaviour among university youths.

Post-intervention phase

Three months after the intervention, end-line data were collected via the same online questionnaire administered at baseline. To ensure ethical fairness, control group participants received two CSE classes covering essential content after end-line data collection.

Data collection and outcomes

Data were collected at baseline and three months postintervention via a pretested, self-administered Google Form. The questionnaire covered sexual and reproductive health (SRH) knowledge, sexual behaviour, and service uptake.

SRH knowledge was assessed via 31 items across five domains: anatomy and pregnancy (8 items), contraception (5), HIV (8), other STIs (6), and condom use (4). Each correct response earned one point, with composite scores calculated as percentages. Scores ≥ 50% indicated good knowledge, whereas those < 50% were classified as poor, based on Fubam et al. [41].

Sexual behaviour included questions on activity, age at debut, number of partners, condom use, and high-risk practices. Service uptake was assessed through self-reported visits to SRH clinics and the use of contraception, STI testing, and counselling. These were analysed descriptively via proportions, following Adedini et al. [42].

Statistical analysis

The data were analysed via SPSS version 25 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY). Descriptive statistics summarised the baseline characteristics. Chi-square tests and t tests were used to compare categorical and continuous variables, respectively. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

The intervention’s effect was estimated via difference-in-differences (DID) analysis to compare changes between the intervention and control groups over time. In addition, age, which was a significant socio-demographic variable, was included as a covariate in multivariate logistic regression models to adjust for potential confounding effects (Supplementary file).

Results

A total of 600 students were enrolled, with 300 in each group. A total of 91.3% of the intervention group (n = 274) and 86.6% of the control group (n = 260) completed the follow-up. Baseline differences were observed in age, religion, and income-generating activities (p < 0.05). The intervention participants were generally older (mean age 21.3 vs. 19.3 years), with more identifying as Christian (76.0% vs. 61.7%) and self-employed (39.7% vs. 29.3%). No significant differences were found in sex, marital status, ethnicity, or allowance. (See Table 1 in the Appendix.)

Post-intervention, the proportion of participants with good SRH knowledge increased significantly in the intervention group (57.7% to 67.9%, p = 0.012) compared with a nonsignificant 2.6% increase in the control group, yielding a difference-in-differences (DID) of + 7.6%. Knowledge gains were most notable in the domains of human anatomy and pregnancy (+ 13.2%), HIV (+ 13.3%), and STIs (+ 12.3%) (all p < 0.05). Smaller or nonsignificant improvements were observed for contraception (+ 6.3%) and condom use (+ 3.9%). The corresponding DID estimates were + 6.2% for anatomy, + 21.2% for HIV, + 3.7% for STIs, + 8.1% for contraception, and + 0.4% for condom use. (See Table 2 in the Appendix).

Sexual behaviour outcomes have shown mixed results. Ever having sex was reported by 38% of the participants in the intervention group and 33.1% of those in the control group. Condom use at last sex improved significantly in the intervention group (+ 11.7%) but declined in the control group (–7.5%), with a net DID of + 19.2% (p = 0.05). Conversely, reports of multiple sexual partners worsened in the intervention group (e.g., from 4.1% to 19.2% in the past 30 days), but decreased in the control group, resulting in significant DIDs of + 22.4% and + 24.7% for the 30-day and 3-month periods, respectively. (See Table 3 in the Appendix).

SRH service utilisation improved in both groups, with visits increasing from 26.0% to 35.0% in the intervention group and from 23.3% to 33.8% in the control group. However, the net DID was negligible (–1.5%). Notably, the use of teaching hospitals (+ 13%) and PHCs (+ 9%) increased in the intervention group, whereas private hospital visits decreased. The DID estimates for teaching hospitals, PHCs, and private hospitals were + 10.5%, + 18.1%, and –29.1%, respectively (p < 0.05). (See Tables 4 and 5, in the Appendix).

Significant increases in SRH service uptake were recorded in the intervention group: HIV counselling and testing (25.6% to 43.8%, DID + 19.5%, p < 0.05), infertility counselling (2.6% to 16.7%, DID + 14.7%, p < 0.05), post-abortion care, breastfeeding counselling, anaemia testing, and parenting classes all had DIDs > 9% and were statistically significant.

While contraceptive counselling had a high DID of + 24.8%, the increase in the intervention group approached but did not reach statistical significance (p = 0.05). Conversely, Pap smear uptake significantly declined in the intervention group (20.8% to 9.0%, p < 0.05), resulting in a negative DID of –14.3%.

Discussion

This study evaluated the impact of a digital, internet-based CSE intervention on SRH knowledge, sexual behaviour, and service uptake among university students in Lagos, Nigeria. The intervention significantly improved SRH knowledge, increased condom use, and enhanced the use of SRH services—highlighting the potential of mHealth strategies in meeting youth sexual and reproductive health needs in public university settings.

Although participants in the intervention group were slightly older, this difference was adjusted for in the analysis by including age as a covariate in the multivariate regression models. Therefore, the observed improvements in knowledge and behaviour are unlikely to be attributable to age differences between groups.

With respect to sexual and reproductive health knowledge, a 10.2% increase in overall SRH knowledge in the intervention group supports evidence that digital platforms are effective for delivering youth-friendly education —especially in university settings with students from diverse socio-economic backgrounds —complementing in-person programs [4, 5, 29, 43, 44]. Notably, knowledge gains were strongest in the HIV (13.3%) and STI (12.3%) domains, which is consistent with reviews showing improved HIV/STI knowledge following digital interventions [45]. Similar gains have been reported in Ghana and Peru using mobile-based SRH programs [31, 32]. In Nigeria, prior research has shown that although awareness of SRH topics is relatively high, knowledge is often fragmented or inaccurate [14, 16, 17]. For example, a study conducted among university students reported high awareness of condoms but low awareness of other contraceptive methods and generally poor understanding of fertility and STI prevention [17]. The improvements observed in this study suggest that digitally delivered CSE—when well-structured and interactive—can fill existing knowledge gaps.

However, increases in contraception knowledge (6.3%) and condom knowledge (3.9%) were not statistically significant (p > 0.05). The World Health Organisation (WHO) noted that digital platforms may be less effective than in-person discussions when engagement is limited or personal concerns cannot be addressed in real time [46, 47]. Digital interventions often struggle to overcome cultural stigma and misconceptions around contraception and condom use, which may require interactive or real-time approaches [47]. Thus, digital health should complement, not replace, existing health infrastructure and education [47] and be tailored to audience needs.

Behavioural outcomes have shown mixed results. While overall sexual activity remained stable, condom use during the last sex improved significantly (+ 11.7%), indicating safer sexual practices. This is critical in reducing HIV, STIs, and unplanned pregnancies [6, 14, 16, 18, 19]. This is particularly important given that inconsistent condom use has been widely reported among Nigerian adolescents and youths, even among those aware of its benefits. Consistent condom use is a fundamental aspect of safe sex [6, 14, 16, 18, 19]. This improvement in this key indicator shows the effectiveness of the intervention to an extent and can be the beginning of sustained efforts to improve sexual behaviour among the target population. These findings are supported by evidence from other settings, which shows that mHealth interventions that combine education with peer interaction and access to services are more likely to shift behaviour than education alone [4, 5, 28]. Peer-led WhatsApp discussions likely reinforced positive norms, as supported by previous mHealth studies using similar behaviour change models [39, 40]; but the WhatsApp-based messages themselves were not analysed as part of the quantitative data.

However, an increase in reports of multiple sexual partners is concerning, as it heightens STI risk. However, this does not invalidate the effectiveness of the intervention, as sexual behavioural changes have been found to be more complex, difficult to attain and slower to manifest than knowledge acquisition, particularly in short-duration interventions [28, 32, 48, 49]. A scoping review noted that few SRH mHealth interventions focus on behavioural outcomes, and even fewer demonstrate significant behavioural changes [50]. In this study, it is possible that a longer time was needed for the intervention to resonate with the target audience enough to cause behaviour change. Nevertheless, the gains in condom use suggest that even brief, targeted interventions can lead to measurable behavioural impacts in specific domains.

Importantly, the intervention led to a 9% increase in SRH service utilization. The participants in the intervention group shifted toward using public facilities (teaching hospitals and PHCs), likely because of targeted service referrals. The use of specific services such as HIV counselling (18.2%), infertility counselling (14.1%), and post-abortion care (13.1%) also increased, suggesting that mHealth can improve service access [5, 28, 33, 51].

While current contraceptive use did not significantly increase, greater use of daily oral pills suggests a gradual shift. As noted in prior research, increased knowledge does not always lead to immediate behavioural change [50]. Barriers such as stigma, cost, and access persist [22]. However, the inclusion of service linkages and discussion around care-seeking in the intervention likely contributed to improved service uptake—a key lesson echoed in other mHealth studies [28, 30, 37].

This study is among the few quasi-experimental evaluations of an internet-based CSE intervention among university youths in Nigeria. It combined digital education with peer engagement and service linkages, enhancing real-world applicability. Baseline differences between the intervention and control groups were accounted for using a difference-in-differences analytical approach, while age, a significant socio-demographic factor, was further adjusted for in the multivariate regression models to control for potential confounding. However, the three-month follow-up period limited the ability to observe long-term behavioural changes. Sustainability also remains uncertain, as the study provided internet data support and facilitation that may not be easily replicated without institutional integration.

Overall, the findings reaffirm that while digital SRH interventions—particularly those offering referrals and confidential information on where to access services—can help circumvent barriers, sexuality education is most effective when integrated with broader SRH services. Digital tools should complement and enhance existing systems rather than serve as standalone interventions. In addition, university authorities should integrate digital sexuality education with existing SRH services to improve SRH outcomes among their students. Also, future studies with longer follow-up and built-in scalability models are recommended.

Conclusion

The study demonstrated that a digital health intervention—comprising mHealth-based comprehensive sexuality education (CSE), online peer discussions, and linkages to youth-friendly SRH services—significantly improved SRH knowledge, increased condom use at last sexual intercourse, and enhanced the utilisation of SRH services.

Digital platforms offer accessible, confidential, and scalable avenues for SRH education and service delivery. The integration of digital CSE into university curricula is recommended. Furthermore, digital SRH services should be embedded within broader health service delivery systems, complementing existing physical health services rather than operating as standalone interventions.
