Depemokimab: A twice yearly anti–IL 5 biologic for chronic rhinosinusitis with nasal polyps (CRSwNP) – A narrative review
Authors: Marn Joon Park, Joseph K Han, Kent K Lam
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTDepemokimab is a monoclonal antibody that targets interleukin-5 with enhanced binding affinity and an extended terminal elimination half‑life of 6–8 weeks. The unique pharmacodynamic features of depemokimab enable sustained suppression of type 2 inflammation with twice‑yearly subcutaneous dosing. This narrative review provides details of depemokimab’s molecular profile, pharmacokinetics, and pharmacodynamics and results regarding the clinical efficacy and safety of depemokimab from recently published Phase 3 clinical trials for the treatment of chronic rhinosinusitis with nasal polyposis (CRSwNP). In 528 severe adult CRSwNP patients (ANCHOR‑1/‑2 trials), depemokimab achieved Δ total endoscopic nasal polyp score by −0.70 (p < .001) and Δ nasal obstruction verbal response scale by −0.24 (p = .003) at week 52, compared to placebo. Twice‑yearly 100 mg depemokimab provides durable eosinophil suppression and with a safety profile comparable to placebo in Phase 3 trials for CRSwNP. Future work should refine biomarkers and assess comparative cost‑effectiveness and long‑term adherence, safety, and effectiveness.KEYWORDS: Depemokimab, mepolizumab, interleukin‑5, IL-5, eosinophil, chronic rhinosinusitis with nasal polyposis, CRSwNP, biologics, type 2 inflammation

Introduction

Chronic rhinosinusitis with nasal polyposis (CRSwNP) is a chronic inflammatory disease of the sinonasal mucosa.1 Over the past decade, research on the endotyping of CRSwNP has delineated type 1, type 2, and type 3 inflammatory patterns, each with characteristic cytokine and biomarker signatures.2,3 The importance of endotyping CRSwNP lies in the fact that each endotype correlates with different clinical factors, including responsiveness to various treatments, and treatment strategies are thus generally tailored to specific CRSwNP endotypes.1–3 Among the 3 CRSwNP endotypes, type 2 CRSwNP is distinguished by eosinophilic inflammation with elevated blood and tissue eosinophils, increased immunoglobulin (Ig) E levels, and upregulated interleukin (IL)‑4, IL‑5, and IL‑13 and commonly coexists with allergic asthma or nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drug (NSAID)‑exacerbated respiratory disease.4,5

Clinically, type 2 CRSwNP often presents with extensive bilateral polyposis that is ethmoid‑predominant and is associated with higher rates of recurrence following endoscopic sinus surgery.6 Nonetheless, type 2 CRSwNP typically responds to corticosteroid therapies, highlighting the use of topical intranasal corticosteroids as first-line and long-term maintenance therapy for CRSwNP.7,8 In cases of type 2 CRSwNP that are refractory to topical corticosteroid therapy, short courses of systemic corticosteroids can be considered; however, the serious risks of repeated or prolonged systemic corticosteroid exposure limit their long‑term use.8,9

Driven by the fact that type 2 CRSwNP patients are characterized by elevated levels of specific cytokines, including IL-4, IL-5, and IL-13, serum IgE, and blood eosinophil counts, monoclonal antibodies (mAbs) that are designed to have a high affinity to biomarkers have become important therapeutic agents for CRSwNP through the suppression of the type 2 inflammatory biomarkers.2,4,9 For CRSwNP, dupilumab (targeting the IL‑4 receptor α [IL‑4 Rα]), omalizumab (targeting of IgE), and mepolizumab (targeting of IL‑5) have shown efficacy and safety in randomized controlled trials (RCTs) across short‑ and long‑term follow‑up, leading to regulatory approvals in North America, Europe, and selected Asian countries.10–12 These biologic therapies have significantly changed the management approaches for CRSwNP when the chronic disease has been especially refractory to routine standard medical and surgical therapies.10–12

Despite the advances in biologic therapies for CRSwNP, practical obstacles and limitations still affect the decision-making in initiating and continuing biologic therapies for CRSwNP patients.9,13–15 One major issue is the need for frequent, repeated administration: dupilumab, omalizumab, and mepolizumab are administered every 2 weeks, every 2–4 weeks, and every 4 weeks, respectively.10–12,16 Real‑world data from a large U.S. severe‑asthma cohort (CHRONICLE) show that while adherence to biologics is generally high [median proportion of days covered 87%; 74% with proportion of days covered (PDC) ≥75%], patients required a mean of 423 d to receive a 52‑week equivalent number of doses (≈58‑d delay), and shorter dosing intervals and at‑home administration were associated with worse adherence, underscoring the clinical rationale for longer interval regimens.17 From a health‑economic perspective, cost‑utility modeling in Italy estimated that dupilumab with supportive care yields a +1.02 quality‑adjusted life‑years (QALY) gain versus best supportive care alone with an incremental cost–utility ratio of ~€21,800 per QALY, demonstrating the potential value impact of effective biologic therapy in CRSwNP.18 However, when biologic therapy was compared with endoscopic sinus surgery (ESS) over a long horizon, ESS yielded 9.80 QALYs versus 8.95 QALYs for dupilumab and dominated in base‑case analyses.19 Furthermore, a Canadian analysis comparing approved biologics concluded that omalizumab was most cost‑effective at current prices and explicitly found that altering dosing frequency materially affected cost‑effectiveness.20 Moreover, the fact that these agents are given as subcutaneous injections may reduce acceptance and adherence in a chronic disease that requires long‑term control.5,21 To overcome the burden of short dosing intervals, many clinicians have explored extending administration intervals with currently available biologics while maintaining adequate control of the disease process.22–24

In parallel, new drug development has focused on biologics engineered with longer half‑lives and therefore longer dosing intervals.25,26 For asthma, benralizumab, an IL‑5 receptor-binding antibody, has demonstrated efficacy with an 8‑weekly maintenance schedule after three initial four‑weekly doses.22,23 Nevertheless, for type 2 CRSwNP, there remains a need for biologic therapies that allow longer dosing intervals while maintaining efficacy comparable to, or better than, currently available agents.5,27,28 Depemokimab was conceived to address these limitations as an anti‑IL‑5 mAb with extended duration of action, formulated for subcutaneous administration twice yearly (every 26 weeks), and has demonstrated efficacy and safety in eosinophilic asthma through the Phase 3 RTCs (SWIFT-1 and SWIFT-2 trials) and in CRSwNP through the Phase 3 RTCs (ANCHOR-1 and ANCHOR-2 trials).29,30 This ultra‑long‑acting, twice‑yearly subcutaneous biologic is intended to overcome adherence and care‑delivery barriers associated with higher frequency biologics and may reduce overall treatment burden, as suggested by early extension data on treatment persistence.21,30

Prior depemokimab reviews are limited allergic asthma, and neither focuses on depemokimab for CRSwNP treatment, nor links YTE‑mediated Fc engineering to the bi-annual 100‑mg SC injection formula.31,32 Our review addresses this gap by integrating the molecular engineering, pharmacokinetics (PK), pharmacodynamic (PD) potency, safety, and efficacy profile of depemokimab in type 2 inflammatory airway diseases, specifically eosinophilic asthma and the CRSwNP, with evidence from Phase 1 and key Phase 3 RCTs.25,26,29,30,33

Materials and methods

This work is a narrative review aiming to scope on the molecular, PK, PD, and clinical evidence for depemokimab in CRSwNP, with contextual comparisons to anti–IL‑5 benchmarks where relevant. As our review article is a narrative review article, hence the limitations inherent to non‑systematic approaches (e.g., selection bias) should be acknowledged.

We searched PubMed/MEDLINE, Embase, the Cochrane Library, and ClinicalTrials.gov from database inception through December 2025. The search terms are a combination of controlled vocabulary-free texts, including depemokimab, GSK3511294, anti‑IL‑5, and/or chronic rhinosinusitis with nasal polyps, CRSwNP, eosinophilic asthma, and severe asthma. Additionally, we supplemented database searches with targeted reviews of regulatory documents/labels (e.g., agency prescribing information and clinical reviews) and major congress abstracts in recent years. Reference lists of key articles were hand‑searched to identify additional sources.

For the eligibility criteria, we included peer‑reviewed phase 1–3 clinical studies of depemokimab in asthma or CRSwNP, pivotal randomized controlled trials and their prespecified extensions, trial registry entries with protocol‑level details, and primary regulatory documents reporting dosing, efficacy, safety, or immunogenicity. Mechanistic and PK–PD reports were included when they provided direct data on depemokimab (or the clinically identical candidate) relevant to dose‑interval rationale. We excluded non‑English publications; animal‑only or in‑vitro‑only studies without translational relevance, narrative pieces lacking primary data, modeling‑only reports without empirical validation, and duplicate/overlapping reports, with only retaining the most complete/primary sources.

To improve consistency, pharmacokinetic half‑life values are reported in days throughout. For phase‑3 results, we report co‑primary endpoints and their p‑values as provided in the source publications; secondary‑endpoint p‑values are labeled nominal unless multiplicity adjustment is explicitly reported in the source.

Depemokimab pharmacology: mechanistic and pharmacologic overview

Among many distinctive features of depemokimab, the most distinguishable pharmacological characteristic is its markedly prolonged terminal half‑life (t1/2) in human serum, compared with any other prior anti-IL‑5 biologics such as mepolizumab, reslizumab, and benralizumab, and even compared with other commercially available anti-IL-4/13 and anti-IgE biologics.26,33–36 Pharmacologically, depemokimab acts as a ligand‑neutralizing antibody similar to that of mepolizumab, sequestering free IL‑5 in the serum and local tissues, preventing engagement of IL‑5 receptors on eosinophils, and thereby attenuating downstream type 2 inflammatory signaling.37,38 On the contrary, benralizumab targets IL‑5 Rα and depletes eosinophils via antibody‑dependent cell‑mediated cytotoxicity.39,40

How Fc engineering extends depemokimab’s elimination half‑life

Depemokimab’s extended t1/2 derives from Fc‑region engineering (Table 1).41 Like all IgG1 mAbs, mepolizumab and depemokimab contain two parts: the first part involves the binding Fab domain that recognizes and binds specifically with IL‑5, and the second part constitutes the constant Fc domain that all mAbs possess, which is responsible for interacting with Fc receptors on the lysosomes.42–46

Since IgG1 is a high-weighted molecule, its metabolism and distribution within the human body work differently from various pharmacological drugs that contain less complicated structures and are lighter in molecular weight.47 IgG1 that is distributed in the blood is continuously taken up from plasma into endothelial and hematopoietic cells by fluid‑phase pinocytosis and trafficked to early endosomes where the pH is about 6.0, creating a condition in which the IgG Fc portion can be easily bound to the neonatal Fc receptor (FcRn) on the endosomal surface.48 IgG1s that are bound to the FcRn on the endosome surface are rescued and returned to the cell surface via transcytosis mechanism.49 Then, the neutral pH of 7.4 of the cell membranes helps to disbound the FcRn-IgG1 complex, and the free IgG1 is released back into the bloodstream, which creates a salvage cycle of the IgG1 molecule; the native IgG’s t1/2 is approximately 21 d.50 However, intracellular IgG1 that fails to bind the FcRn on the endosome surface is sorted onward to lysosomes for proteolysis, followed by disintegration of the IgG1.47–49

In terms of chemical structure, depemokimab’s M252Y/S254T/T256E “YTE” substitution sits at the CH2-CH3 interface of the Fc domain, which strongly increases the affinity of depemokimab to FcRn, specifically at endosomal pH (approximately 6.0).51 Depemokimab has an even lower affinity at the physiological pH of 7.4 than the natural IgG1.51 This pH‑selective enhancement increases the fraction of intracellular depemokimab that is captured by the FcRn on the endosome, thereby diverting the depemokimab molecule away from lysosomal degradation.42 Since the release from FcRn at the cell surface remains even more efficient at pH 7.4, depemokimab can undergo multiple salvage cycles without becoming trapped intracellularly, effectively maintaining stationary serum concentration.42,52 Therefore, the overall effect of the YTE modification achieves the lower systemic clearance, higher blood concentration, and a longer terminal elimination half‑life.42,52

Enhanced IL‑5 binding affinity of depemokimab

Beyond its extended half‑life, depemokimab exhibits higher in‑vitro binding affinity for IL‑5 than mepolizumab.44,45,53,54 The equilibrium dissociation constant (KD) of depemokimab is about 10–40 pM, while mepolizumab exhibits a KD at approximately 110–260 pM (Table 1).44,45,53,45 By definition, KD equals to kd/ka, and a lower KD, which reflects stronger binding, signifies either a faster association rate (ka), a slower dissociation rate (kd), or both.55 In practical terms, a lower KD of a pharmacological agent indicates that the mAb-cytokine bound complex is more stable, and less likely to dissociate at therapeutic concentrations.56 This increased binding affinity of depemokimab is achieved by targeted changes in the complementarity‑determining regions (CDRs) of the mAb, while preserving the parental mepolizumab paratope.26,45 Specifically, the short hypervariable loops in the antibody variable domains (i.e., VH CDR3 and VL CDR1) are responsible for making direct contact with antigen.45,57 These focused CDR substitutions improve shape complementarity and electrostatic interactions with IL‑5, simultaneously enhancing the on‑rate and modestly slowing the off‑rate without altering epitope specificity.45,57

Taken together, the combination of extended half‑life and higher affinity to IL‑5 binding enables the sustained pharmacodynamic activity of depemokimab with a 100‑mg subcutaneous dose administered every 26 weeks (6 months), producing rapid and durable eosinophil suppression throughout the dosing interval.26,33

Depemokimab in eosinophilic asthma: lessons and key findings from trials and early real‑world experience

Depemokimab underwent its initial clinical evaluation in eosinophilic asthma before advancing to studies in CRSwNP, given that the IL‑5-driven inflammation is distinctively well characterized in eosinophilic asthma, and the IL‑5 blockade is a validated therapeutic strategy in those patients.58,59

In 2021, Singh et al. conducted a Phase 1 clinical trial first‑in‑human study with depemokimab on eosinophilic type asthma patients (NCT03287310). In a randomized, placebo‑controlled single‑ascending‑dose study, 48 adults with mild‑to‑moderate asthma (pre‑bronchodilator FEV1 ≥60% predicted; blood eosinophils ≥200/µL) received placebo or subcutaneous (SC) depemokimab at 2 mg, 10 mg, 30 mg, 100 mg, or 300 mg dosages and were followed for 40 weeks.26 Following a single 100 mg SC injection of depemokimab, the drug exposure increased proportionally with dose, t1/2 was 38–53 d, and blood eosinophils fell >48% within 24 hours at every dose with ≥80% suppression sustained for 26 weeks.26 No serious adverse events or neutralizing antibodies were observed, and the side effects were mostly mild nasopharyngitis or headache, establishing a tolerability margin compatible with low‑frequency dosing.26

Based on the Phase 1 data, Phase 3 RCTs were directly proceeded with 100 mg SC depemokimab per 26 weeks with severe eosinophilic-type asthma patients.33 The replicate multicenter, double‑blind, Phase 3A SWIFT‑1, and SWIFT‑2 RCTs (NCT04719832; NCT04720099) enrolled 792 adults with severe eosinophilic asthma (blood eosinophils >300/µL in the prior year or ≥150/µL at screening) and recent clinically significant exacerbations despite high‑dose inhaled corticosteroid (ICS) plus long-acting beta-agonist (LABA).33 The single primary endpoint was the annualized rate of clinically significant exacerbations over 52 weeks, and the inferential procedures followed the prespecified multiplicity strategy. Unless otherwise specified, p‑values for secondary endpoints (e.g., SGRQ, ACQ‑5, pre‑bronchodilator FEV1, and symptom diary measures) were nominal.33

At 52 weeks, the annualized severe asthma exacerbation rate was reduced by 58% in SWIFT‑1 [rate ratio (RR) of 0.42, 95% confidence interval (CI) 0.30–0.59] and 48% in SWIFT‑2 (RR of 0.52; 95% CI 0.36–0.73) (both p < .001), with sustained mean blood‑eosinophil suppression at week 52 in 90% of subjects.33 Safety and treatment‑emergent adverse events were comparable with placebo (3–4% vs. 1–4%, respectively), and no drug‑related serious adverse events were reported. The incidence of injection‑site reactions was less than <1–2%, and the anti‑drug antibody (ADA) formation rate was between 5% and 12%, in which only two cases (0.3%) resulted in detection of neutralizing antibody to depemokimab.33

The studies of depemokimab in eosinophilic asthma served as an efficient starting point for proof-of-concept and dose-selection in subsequent studies of depemokimab for CRSwNP, which encompasses a more heterogeneous inflammatory landscape in which type 2-high, type 2-low, and mixed endotypes coexist. Validated stratification of biomarkers for CRSwNP also remains limited.1,60–62

Depemokimab in CRSwNP: state-of-art evidence

On the strength of Phase 1 evidence showing that a single 100 mg subcutaneous dose of depemokimab yields a terminal half‑life of approximately 40–50 d and sustains near‑complete eosinophil suppression for 26 weeks in eosinophilic asthma, it was advanced directly to Phase 3 clinical trials for depemokimab in CRSwNP patients (the ANCHOR trials), without additional dose‑finding studies.26–29,33 Nonetheless, with a background knowledge that IL‑5–driven inflammation in CRSwNP coexists with broader endotypic heterogeneity than in asthma, the ANCHOR trials had a more strict criterion for eosinophil-dependent type 2 inflammation, as the inclusion cutoff had slightly stricter eosinophil counts and disease‑severity thresholds, including a higher screening blood‑eosinophil cutoff (≥250 /µL in ANCHOR versus ≥150 /µL in SWIFT) (Table 2).26–29,33

Furthermore, when comparing depemokimab’s efficacy, pharmacodynamics, and safety in CRSwNP patients, mepolizumab currently serves as the most appropriate benchmark.63 Among IL‑5-pathway inhibitors, mepolizumab is presently the only biologic agent with an approved indication for CRSwNP patients across major regulatory associations (United States, European Union, United Kingdom, Canada, Australia, and Japan), thereby providing a validated reference standard for comparing the pharmacologic profile and clinical performance of newer anti‑IL‑5 targeting biologics, such as depemokimab.63

Phase 3 RCTs for depemokimab in CRSwNP: the ANCHOR trials (ANCHOR‑1 and ANCHOR‑2)

The ANCHOR trials were intentionally designed as mirror‑image Phase 3 RTCs to generate two independent confirmatory data sets for regulatory review; hence, their protocols are virtually identical.29 The ANCHOR‑1 and ANCHOR‑2 trials together recruited and randomized 528 adults with severe bilateral CRSwNP who showed refractory symptoms, despite prior systemic corticosteroid usage or previous history of an endoscopic sinus surgery.29 Eligible participants were required to have an endoscopic nasal polyp (NP) score ≥5 (scale from 0 to 8), a clinician‑rated nasal obstruction verbal response scale (VRS) ≥2 (scale from 0 to 3), history of prior usage of systemic corticosteroid or sinus surgery, and blood‑eosinophil thresholds of ≥250/µL at screening or ≥300/µL in the preceding year.29 Enrolled patients were guided to continue their conservative treatment for CRSwNP, as in use of topical corticosteroid therapy with nasal irrigation throughout the study period.29

ANCHOR‑1 and ANCHOR‑2 each used a computer‑generated, block randomization scheme (1:1) to assign participants to depemokimab 100 mg or matched placebo; participant allocation was stratified by history of prior endoscopic sinus surgery (yes versus no) and geographic region (North America/Europe versus Latin America/Asia Pacific), to ensure the balanced distribution and to minimize potential geographic cofounding.29 In the ANCHOR studies, two co‑primary end points were prospectively defined as following: (1) change from baseline in total endoscopic NP score at week 52 and (2) mean change from baseline in clinician‑rated nasal obstruction VRS, assessed between weeks 49 and 52.29 These co-primary endpoints were analyzed under the prespecified multiplicity framework in the statistical analysis plan. The main secondary end points comprised (1) proportion of patients requiring a rescue systemic corticosteroids or sinus surgery through week 52, (2) composite responder rate, which indicates achieving both a ≥1‑point reduction in NP score and a ≥1‑unit improvement in VRS, (3) change in Sino‑Nasal Outcome Test‑22 (SNOT‑22) total score, and (4) change in Lund–Mackay (LM) score on computed tomography (CT) scans.29 Unless otherwise specified, p-values for secondary endpoints were nominal (multiplicity not adjusted).

Across the final study participants (n = 528), depemokimab met both coprimary end points with CRSwNP patients. The differences of endoscopic NP score changes and nasal obstruction VRS score changes at week 52 between the depemokimab arm and the placebo arm were −0.70 points (95% CI −0.91 to −0.49, p < .001) and −0.24 (95% CI −0.39 to −0.08, p = .003), respectively, indicating that depemokimab arm had much more significant polyp score reduction and improved nasal obstruction scores.29

For the secondary outcomes, the changes in the SNOT-22 scores and the VRS score for rhinorrhea and loss of smell were all significantly more reduced in the depemokimab arm than in placebo arm (all p < .05). Additionally, the SNOT‑22 score changes were −14.4 points with depemokimab, which showed superior results compared to −6.3 points with placebo (p = .007).29 For the CT image changes, the LM score change was significantly more reduced for the depemokimab group compared to the placebo group (−3.1 vs. −0.6, p < .001). Regarding the necessity for the rescue sinus surgery at week 52, there were no significant risk reductions between the depemokimab and placebo groups (16% vs. 22%, p = .128). However, the necessity for 1 or more courses of systemic corticosteroid usage was significantly reduced in the depemokimab arm than in the placebo arm (26% vs. 36%, p = .006).29

Furthermore, 70% of patients in the depemokimab arm did not require any systemic corticosteroids during the 52-week study duration, while 59% in the placebo arm did not require any systemic corticosteroids.29 Of note, 44% of patients in the depemokimab arm achieved the anatomic–symptom composite, which indicates a combination of improved anatomical (NP score) and symptomatic improvements of more than 1-point reduction, compared with 26% in the placebo group (p < .001).29 The timing of clinically relevant NP score reduction emerged by week 16 and persisted to week 52, and the peripheral blood eosinophils were suppressed by ≥90% from week 4 onwards, confirming sustained pharmacodynamic activity and clinical response of depemokimab.29,64

Regarding on the safety profiles, the results were comparable between depemokimab and placebo, with no meaningful differences in the depemokimab- or placebo-related adverse events.29 The treatment‑emergent adverse events (TEAEs) occurred in 74% of depemokimab and 79% of placebo recipients, most commonly nasopharyngitis and headache; serious adverse events were reported in <1% of participants, none of which were related with the drug, and no death was reported.24 Given the fact that depemkimab is administered SC, the injection‑site reactions were infrequent (<3%) and mild, and anti‑drug antibodies (ADA) were detected in <1% of patients with no impact on eosinophil suppression.29

Beyond the numeric effects, the integrated Δ endoscopic NP score −0.70 at 52 weeks reflects a directional reduction of bilateral polyp burden on endoscopy, and the concurrent ΔNO‑VRS −0.24 indicates perceptible relief in nasal obstruction rather than random score fluctuation. The higher responder proportion (≥1‑point endoscopic NP score reduction: 41–44% vs 24%) shows a broader “responder tail,” consistent with clinically obvious regression in roughly two of five treated patients. Importantly, more patients were reassessed as not requiring sinus surgery at Week 52 (24% vs 14%; p = .002), suggesting that a twice‑yearly (q26 wk) depemokimab regimen can defer procedural rescue in a meaningful subset.

Taken together, the ANCHOR Phase 3 RTCs supported efficacy of the 100 mg twice‑yearly SC depemokimab regimen in providing clinically meaningful benefit to CRSwNP management compared to placebo, while maintaining a safety profile comparable to placebo.29 These effects, modest in mean magnitude but consistent across endpoints and reinforced by categorical response and surgical avoidance, support clinical relevance in a condition where incremental reductions in polyp size and nasal obsturction scores translate into fewer rescue interventions over a year.

Establishing the PK and PD in depemokimab for CRSwNP: an open‑label analysis among ANCHOR participants

Immediately following the ANCHOR trials, an open-label trial was conducted to analyze and establish the pharmacokinetic (PK) and pharmacodynamic (PD) characteristics of depemokimab for CRSwNP.29,64 A total of 272 participants from both ANCHOR trials were followed from week 0 and 26.64 Through serial blood sampling among 118 patients in the ANCHOR-1/-2 trials, the pharmacokinetics data, represented as the plasma concentrations of depemokimab, and the pharmacodynamics data, which consisted of the ratio of log-transformed blood eosinophil counts (BEC) of each timepoint to baseline, were analyzed.64

The plasma concentration of depemokimab showed a geometric‑mean serum trough of 10.65 ± 2.98 mg/mL at week 4 (post-administration), followed by a gradual decrease. The pre-dose concentrations at week 26 and 52 were 1.02 ± 0.41 mg/mL and 1.25 ± 0.72 mg/mL, respectively, establishing the PK of twice-per-year SC depemokimab in CRSwNP.64 For the PD analysis, the baseline BEC showed 344/mL in the depemokimab group and 322/mL for the placebo group. At week 4, BEC ratio to baseline for depemokimab versus placebo (95% CI) was 0.104 (0.091–0.118; p < .001). Relative reductions were sustained throughout the first dosing (at week 26: 0.168 (0.148–0.191); p < .001) and second dosing intervals [at week 52: 0.153 (0.133–0.176); p < .001].64 The established PK and PD align with the Phase 1 clinical trial for asthma, in which two doses of 6-monthly depemokimab achieved sufficient concentrations to effectively suppress BEC over 52 weeks. The sustained anti-IL-5 effect and the attenuated BEC throughout the year validate the efficacy of twice-per-year 100 mg depemokimab in CRSwNP.26,64

Conclusively, data derived from RTCs suggest that a twice‑yearly 100 mg regimen of depemokimab produces clinically meaningful reductions in polyp burden and nasal obstruction, in addition to reducing the need for rescue surgery or systemic steroids, and maintains a safety profile that is similar to that of placebo.

Discussion

Biologics for type 2 inflammatory airway diseases

With advances in molecular biology and precision medicine, the paradigm has moved from phenotyping to precise endotyping of asthma and CRSwNP.39 Patients who express type 2 inflammation have clinical characteristics exhibiting higher levels of eosinophils, IgE, type 2 related cells, and cytokines: IL‑4, IL‑13, and IL‑5.29,39,62 Clinically, distinguishing patients who have type 2 airway inflammation is important, since these patients tend to have frequent exacerbations or disease relapse; however, they respond well to anti-inflammatory therapy.30 Thus, the classical mainstay of these disorders has been mainly topical and/or systemic corticosteroids, which attenuate the inflammatory response.1,9,30 However, the severe side effects of systemic corticosteroids, especially when used long term or frequently for chronic inflammatory conditions, raise concerns and limit the use of systemic corticosteroids. The development of biologics has provided an alternative to systemic corticosteroids, as these mAbs are engineered to precisely target the type 2 inflammatory cytokines and receptors.1,9,30

Biologics that are developed, approved, and adopted widely across the globe to target the type 2 inflammation are dupilumab, omalizumab, and mepolizumab, targeting IL‑4 Rα/13, IgE, and IL‑5, respectively.65 Although there are some new drugs targeting different molecules such as tezepelumab (anti-TSLP) in the pipeline, all of these biologics require a 2–4-week interval for repeated subcutaneous administrations. The dosing route and frequency of these biologics undoubtedly influence patient adherence.17,66–68 Additionally, given that asthma and CRSwNP are chronic inflammatory airway diseases, a long-term commitment to the use of these drugs is generally needed to maintain a controlled disease state.69 Therefore, newer drug development has led to a drug that requires less frequent dosing while providing sustained efficacy and a tolerable safety profile.70,71 In this context, depemokimab, which distinguishes itself from the IL‑5 inhibitor mepolizumab with a more extended t1/2 in the human body and increased affinity for IL-5, has the potential to improve long-term patient adherence with its twice-yearly dose interval.26,29,30

Depemokimab versus other anti-IL‑5 pathway targeting biologics: prospects for head‑to‑head comparison

Mechanistically, depemokimab and mepolizumab neutralize soluble IL‑5 and are injected SC, whereas benralizumab, also an injectable formula, binds IL‑5 Rα and depletes eosinophils through antibody‑dependent cell‑mediated cytotoxicity. These distinctions among these anti-IL biologic agents may yield subtle differences in tissue pharmacodynamics and onset of action.26,39,40 When it comes to real-world practice, the differences in these pharmacological characteristics will reflect not only the interval dosing of each of these biologic agents but also may affect the patient adherence among asthma and CRSwNP patients.30 For a scientifically solid head-to-head comparison across programs, it is important to consider (1) mechanistic class (ligand neutralization vs receptor-directed cell depletion), (2) dosing burden (q4–8 wk vs q26 wk), (3) trial design and rescue policies (e.g., allowance of systemic steroids/surgery), and (4) endpoint frameworks and multiplicity control, because these features materially influence observed effect sizes and real-world persistence.

To date, no head‑to‑head trial has compared depemokimab directly with other IL‑5 pathway biologics in asthma or CRSwNP, so decision‑making presently relies on mechanisms, dosing burden, and cross‑trial benchmarking.12,29 By contrast, there have been some head‑to‑head comparison studies in asthma; NIMBLE is designed to provide the first rigorous comparison by randomizing patients who are well‑controlled on mepolizumab or benralizumab to either continue their current biologic or switch to depemokimab 100 mg every 26 weeks under double‑blind, double‑dummy conditions.72 The primary endpoint is the annualized severe‑exacerbation rate at 52 weeks with key secondary outcomes including lung function, symptom control, and safety, which together will test the non‑inferiority of a twice‑yearly schedule against higher frequency.72

In the interim, the SWIFT effect sizes for exacerbation reduction are numerically similar to those reported for monthly mepolizumab and 8‑weekly benralizumab. However, differences in design (baseline severity, rescue policies, endpoint frameworks, and p‑value adjustments) and populations preclude inferences about comparative value or efficiency.22,30 However, any cross-trial comparisons should be interpreted cautiously given heterogeneity in baseline disease severity (e.g., polyp burden and symptom scores), allowed rescue interventions, and whether p-values for secondary endpoints are nominal or adjusted.

Whether parity extends to upper-airway endpoints is unclear; endoscopic NP score gains with depemokimab were numerically smaller than those in dupilumab and mepolizumab trials, and heterogeneity limits direct comparison.10,12,29 Furthermore, there are no direct comparative studies that compare safety, immunogenicity, and differences in eosinophil kinetics on long-term treatment or treatment withdrawal.30 Also, from a health system perspective, biologics that have non‑inferiority efficacy with far fewer administration intervals may shift treatment algorithms toward longer interval therapy, but definitive positioning will require head-to-head trials and real-world adherence data cost-effectiveness analysis.73 Currently at this point, when selecting the most appropriate biologics to treat type 2 inflammatory airway diseases, relevant biomarkers, patient comorbidities, patient preferences, and cost–benefit effects should be considered. Prescribing habits across different geographic regions will likely influence selection of biologic therapies for type 2 inflammatory airway diseases.9 Furthermore, post‑marketing registries should be prepared in terms of switch durability, patient adherence, as well as on the economic endpoints, which remain to be evaluated.21 Accordingly, the adherence implications of q26‑week dosing remain unstudied and should be addressed in future research.

Interpreting 3 RCTs with anti-IL-5 biologics in CRSwNP patients: why the results differed among the ANCHOR, SYNAPSE, and MERIT trials

Direct numerical comparisons across the Phase 3 RTCs for anti-IL-5 biologics for refractory CRSwNP, including ANCHOR (depemokimab), SYNAPSE (mepolizumab), and MERIT (mepolizumab).12,29,75 For interpretation across these studies, it must be acknowledged that result differences reflect (1) different symptom score scales used in each trial, (2) differences in baseline symptom severity of CRSwNP, and (3) regional differences that may affect endotypic composition.12,29,74

First, ANCHOR used a 0-to-3 VRS for obstruction, whereas MERIT and SYNAPSE used a 0-to-10-cm visual analogue scale (VAS). The different methods used to measure similar outcome metrics limit the direct side-by-side comparison of symptom outcome.12,29,74 In detail, a difference of 1 on the 0–3 VRS is roughly equivalent to about 3 cm on the 0–10 cm on the VAS. Given this, the average VRS improvement seen in ANCHOR will look smaller in raw numbers after converting to VAS in MERIT or SYNPSE trials.12,29,74 Second, the severity of the baseline disease burden also differed among trials: ANCHOR enrolled patients with slightly lower baseline nasal obstruction score and marginally smaller polyps than SYNAPSE, which might explain the more pronounced symptom improvement with mepolizumab in SYNPASE.12,29 Interestingly, ANCHOR participants had a lower proportion of patients with high type 2 biomarker levels [blood eosinophils, IgE, nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drug (NSAID)-exacerbated respiratory disease (NSAID-ERD)] than several other CRSwNP biologic trials, which should also come into consideration upon comparing each trial outcome.

Another important factor for consideration when comparing the different RTCs is the difference in regional distribution and associated endotype heterogeneity of the subject pools. The proportion of type 2-low or mixed endotypes is known to be higher in East Asian cohorts than the Western population, which typically expresses type 2-high or genuine type-2 endotypes. Differences in inflammatory profiles due to geographic differences may affect clinical responses noted with anti-IL‑5 biologics.62,75,76 In MERIT, which randomized patients from Japan, China, and Russia to monthly mepolizumab, the nasal obstruction improved significantly, while the polyp‑size endpoint narrowly missed significance, mirroring the pattern seen in East Asian population subgroups in ANCHOR.29,74

In parallel to CRSwNP, the subgroup analyses for the SWIFT trials for asthma also suggested lower asthma exacerbation rates in East Asian participants, compared with non‑Asian participants despite identical dosing, underscoring the role of population mix.77 Hence, no conclusion regarding drug superiority can be drawn from the current trials, due to differences across enrollment mixes. This further supports the necessity of biomarker‑guided patient recruitment for study enrollment and unified symptom measurement outcome scales for future CRSwNP trials.2

Managing the relapsing CRSwNP: revision surgery versus anti – IL-5 biologics

In relapsing CRSwNP after prior endoscopic sinus surgery (ESS), the treatment choice between revision ESS and anti–IL-5 biologic therapy hinges on complementary strengths and gaps: ESS offers rapid anatomic debulking, restoration of sinus ventilation, and improved access for topical corticosteroids but is susceptible to recurrence in type-2–high disease; anti–IL-5 agents (e.g., mepolizumab, benralizumab, and depemokimab) provide systemic control of type-2 inflammation, reduce polyp burden and systemic-steroid use, and may defer further surgery but require ongoing dosing and entail cost and access considerations.1,13,78

There are currently no head-to-head randomized trials directly comparing ESS with anti–IL-5 biologics in CRSwNP; available evidence comprises placebo-controlled biologic RCTs and surgical series or indirect comparisons, making cross-trial contrasts vulnerable to heterogeneity in baseline severity, rescue policies, and endpoints.79 In that respect, a pragmatic, biomarker-guided approach is therefore reasonable: patients with early post-ESS relapse, high type-2 biomarkers (eosinophilia, IgE), or comorbid asthma may preferentially benefit from anti–IL-5 therapy to delay or avoid revision ESS, whereas focal obstructive disease, complications, or limited type-2 signal may favor revision ESS, with combined or staged strategies considered case-by-case.13,80

Future comparative-effectiveness work should randomize relapsing patients to revision ESS vs anti–IL-5 (with allowance for cross-over) and use endpoints that matter to patients and systems, i.e., time to revision, cumulative oral-corticosteroid exposure, symptom, patient-reported outcome gains, safety, and cost-effectiveness.81

Long-term patient adherence and cost‑effectiveness in depemokimab

Real‑world evidence shows that patient adherence to anti-inflammatory biologics for treating respiratory diseases declines as injection frequency increases. Multiple claims‑based and registry analyses have shown that patient compliance drops to less than 60% at 12 months, for regimens dosed every 2–8 weeks.21 The real concern with this matter is that the non‑persistence to these biologics is associated with higher disease exacerbation rates, which eventually results in a roughly 1.7‑fold rise in exacerbation‑related all‑medical costs. Lower patient adherence can thus negatively affect clinical outcomes and also increases the medical‑cost economic burden.82 Noticeably, a recent study suggests that reducing the number of administrations from 13 to 26 per year to 2 per year can improve quality‑adjusted life‑years (QALY); assuming the per‑dose list price is unchanged, fewer clinic visits and better persistence can yield in higher QALY, especially when the costs of per-dosing are expensive, such as for depemokimab or mepolizumab.73 In this regard, depemokimab’s twice‑yearly schedule carries a plausible adherence advantage. The 1‑y patient adherence for depemokimab was observed in the AGILE extension study to be 89%.30 Similarly, in other studies with other chronic diseases necessitating biologic treatments, longer intervals for drug administrations can improve patient adherence, as seen with 6‑monthly denosumab in osteoporosis and rituximab in rheumatoid arthritis.76

Moreover, in terms of CRSwNP management, reducing clinic visits with biologic therapies can improve adherence to other baseline treatments like saline irrigation and topical cortisteroids, ultimately leading to better symptom control and a more positive outcome.9 These effects can be especially pronounced in regions with limited access to healthcare specialists or for patients with mobility difficulties, where fewer injections lower the barrier to sustained care.21 Although formal cost‑effectiveness modeling tailored to depemokimab in asthma and CRSwNP will be needed to quantify these advantages, the improved patient adherence provides a rationale for considering longer interval therapy that is equally efficacious.73

Exploring various potential drug‑delivery modalities for anti‑IL‑5 biologics

One of the drawbacks for depemokimab and all other biologics used in CRSwNP treatment is that they are available only as injectable therapies, mostly by SC administration via self‑injectable prefilled syringe. This factor may greatly act as a burden in some patients with a low pain threshold or those who are unable to self‑inject, in addition to the risk of local injection-site reactions.83 Therefore, in a perfect world, it would be optimal if these biologics could be administered orally, or topically at the site of pathology, provided that the efficacy is comparable to currently available injectables (Table 3).84 Nonetheless, when developing a drug that can directly bind to cytokines, including IL‑5, and inhibit its pathway, molecular characteristics and pharmacological considerations should be understood, as they can act as a hurdle to converting the SC injectable to other routes of administration.85

When administering a drug that is designed to inhibit the cytokine and its receptor‑bound complex, which is relatively large, flat, and hydrophilic, such as the IL‑5-IL‑5 Rα complex, there are hurdles and challenges that limit the discovery of orally bioavailable ligands with high affinity for the cytokine or its receptor.86 Because the cytokine-receptor complex lacks deep hydrophobic pockets that allow ligand binding, it may require ligands of large molecular weight that are highly polarized.86 Since highly polar molecules are poorly absorbed via the gastrointestinal tract, peptides and large polar compounds such as mAbs that are administered orally will be degraded by gastric acid and proteases and also exhibit poor transepithelial permeability, all resulting in low systemic bioavailability. Even if potent target binders were identified and engineered, their poor bioavailability and distribution would impede the agents from reaching effective target serum concentrations.87

On the other hand, topical delivery has some merits since it directly administers the drug at the site where the pathology takes place (i.e., inhalers for asthma and topical nasal sprays for CRSwNP).88 The intranasal or inhaled delivery can achieve high local concentrations while minimizing systemic absorption and side effects; however, the high-weighted molecule IgG1 (~150 kDa) penetrates mucus poorly and is rapidly removed by mucociliary clearance of the sinonasal mucosal epithelium, making sustained tissue exposure difficult without repeated dosing or specialized formulations.89 To overcome these limitations, there has been a proof‑of‑concept study demonstrating that thin‑film freeze‑dried antibody powders can be delivered via intranasal sprays to the posterior nasal cavities in animals. These neutralizing antibodies on the film protect against respiratory viral infections, but translating these results to chronic inflammatory diseases in humans has yet to be successful, and thus topical anti‑IL‑5‑targeting IgG1 delivery remains preclinical at this point.90

Intravenous (IV) administration of biologic therapies may give an opportunity for increasing the dose-interval through higher levels of bioavailability than SC injections.91 However, given depemokimab’s Fc engineering and extended half-life, IV administration is not expected to provide a clinically meaningful pharmacokinetic advantage over SC dosing for such long‑acting IgG1 Abs.92 Additionally, IV administration requires venous access and a trained health‑care professional in an infusion‑capable setting, and the placement and use of peripheral IV catheters introduce device‑related risks; by contrast, SC formulations avoid venous cannulation, often support at‑home self‑injection, and can substantially reduce costs relative to IV infusion.93

To overcome patient discomfort associated with the SC injection formulation of current anti‑IL‑5 biologics, drug‑delivery devices such as autoinjectors (AIs) have been suggested to improve patient adherence compared with the prefilled safety syringe device (SSD) for delivery of these biologics. AIs can be an option for patients with a low pain threshold or who are unable to self‑inject, in addition to addressing concerns about local injection‑site reactions.94 In a randomized Phase 1 device‑bridging study, a single 100 mg dose of depemokimab showed bioequivalent pharmacokinetics when delivered by either a prefilled SSD or an AI, suggesting that use of an at‑home AI self‑injection device may reduce patient discomfort and improve long‑term adherence.94

SC delivery remains the practical route for IL‑5 biologics, and especially with the ultra-long extended t1/2 of depemokimab, it enables the self-delivered, home administration with established pharmacokinetics.9,26 Although technical innovations may eventually expand to intranasal delivery development in the future, no anti‑IL‑5 intranasal biologic has entered human testing.

Current limitations and future study directions for depemokimab in CRSwNP

Although depemokimab has proven its clinical efficacy and safety, with improved long-term patient adherence in eosinophilic asthma and type 2 CRswNP, there are some current limitations with depemokimab. First, absolute efficacy on anatomic endpoints in CRSwNP is relatively modest, with a mean of 0.70 point reduction in total endoscopic NP score versus placebo at week 52, which is smaller than changes reported with twice-weekly dupilumab or monthly mepolizumab in their respective trials; whether this reflects endotype mix, differences in the local drug concentrations, or both remains unresolved.10,12,29,74 Second, response heterogeneity by geography and biomarker profile persists, with East Asian cohorts showing numerically smaller effects to anti-IL-5 biologics.62,75,77,81 Third, long‑term immunogenicity and durability beyond two dosing cycles are not yet fully established; although anti‑drug antibodies were uncommon through the first year, extended observation in AGILE will be needed to exclude late‑emerging effects on clearance or efficacy.30 Fourth, head‑to‑head comparison trials in CRSwNP are lacking. Although the ongoing NIMBLE trial is aimed to test non‑inferiority of twice‑yearly depemokimab versus established IL‑5 pathway biologics in well‑controlled asthma, the analogous studies in CRSwNP would be critical with great value.72 Lastly, unifying the outcome scales across CRSwNP trials would facilitate clearer cross‑study comparisons and reduce the need for post‑hoc mapping between VRS and VAS metrics, enabling the direct head-to-head symptomatic changes across studies.27 Addressing these gaps through longer term surveillance, biomarker‑stratified trials, and direct comparative studies will define the optimal clinical niche for depemokimab and clarify whether twice yearly IL‑5 blockade can supplant higher frequency biologics across the type 2 airway disease spectrum.29,72

Conclusion

Depemokimab is an ultra-long‑acting anti-IL‑5 IgG1 engineered for high IL‑5 affinity and the extended half-life. The Phase 3 RCTs (ANCHOR-1 and ANCHOR-2 trials) have established that twice-yearly 100 mg SC injections of depemokimab yielded significant improvements in endoscopic NP scores and nasal obstruction scores in type 2 CRSwNP at week 52 with a safety profile similar to placebo. Future studies should aim for head‑to‑head and long‑term comparative outcomes versus other type 2 biologics, biomarker‑defined subgroup analyses, and real‑world cost‑effectiveness.
