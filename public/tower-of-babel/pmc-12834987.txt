CCTO–TiO2 nanoparticle composite based PVA/PTFE multilayer triboelectric nanogenerator for energy harvesting and methylene blue (MB) dye degradation
Authors: Ashok Kumar Swami, Deepak Verma
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Triboelectric energy harvesters provide a reliable way of transforming mechanical energy obtained from routine bodily functions into electrical energy. In the development of wearable, flexible, and portable electronics as well as self-powered sensor applications, triboelectric nanogenerators (TENGs) present a fascinating alternative to power supply challenges. In this study, we report the synthesis and application of Calcium Copper Titanate (CaCu3Ti4O12, (CCTO)) and its Titanium Dioxide (TiO2)-modified composites for high-performance triboelectric nanogenerators (TENGs). CCTO and its variants 5T-CCTO and 10T-CCTO were synthesized using a modified sol-gel fuel combustion method, and their structural and chemical properties were confirmed through XRD, FTIR, XPS, and FESEM analysis. A multilayer contact-separation TENG was fabricated using the synthesized composites dispersed in a PVA matrix as the tribo-positive layer and a PTFE/rGO film as the tribo-negative layer. In comparison to the PVA/PTFE TENG device, systematic investigation revealed that incorporating CCTO and its TiO2-modified composites as fillers (10 and 20 weight percent) significantly enhanced the short circuit current and power by 37.7 and 12.7 fold, respectively. Furthermore, the practical utility of the TENG was demonstrated by integrating it into a Fenton reaction for the degradation of methylene blue (MB) dye. The TENG-assisted Fenton process achieved 98.5% degradation of MB within 60 minutes, marking an approximately 20% increase in the reaction rate constant, indicating significantly faster degradation kinetics compared to the conventional Fenton reaction. This work highlights the potential of CCTO-based composites for developing efficient TENGs for self-powered environmental remediation systems.

Introduction

Rapid technological breakthroughs, including the Internet of Things (IoTs), 5G and soon-to-be 6G networks, big data, humanoid robots, artificial intelligence (AI), and the rise of wearable, flexible, and portable electronics, are becoming part of our day-to-day life. These technologies are crucial for applications including wearable sensors, implantable microsystems, nanorobotics, and epidermal electronics. They typically operate in the milliwatt (mW) to microwatt (µW) power range1,2. There is a growing interest in incorporating environmentally friendly, self-powered, and sustainable energy sources to support autonomous operation, which can also eliminate the need for large, cumbersome batteries and enhance the longevity and usability of devices. To address the issues and sustainable energy demand in various modern electronics, different types of energy harvesting technologies, such as piezoelectric, triboelectric, electromagnetic, and thermoelectric nanogenerators, have been developed extensively to transform ambient environmental energy into usable electricity. Among these, triboelectric nanogenerators (TENGs) attracted particular fascination as viable alternatives for self-sufficient systems. This is ascribed to its straightforward architecture, ease of manufacturing, wide range of materials, lightweight nature, environmental friendliness, and capacity to provide a sizable electrical output appropriate for powering sensors and low-energy devices.

Z. L. Wang and his group (2012) pioneered the triboelectric nanogenerator (TENG) first, an efficient device that can convert mechanical stimulus into electrical output3. A triboelectric nanogenerator works on the combined effect of contact electrification and electrostatic induction, also called triboelectrification. Triboelectrification is the process in which two different electron affinities of triboelectric materials come into physical contact, driven by an external force, whether by squeezing, sliding, or separating. Interfacial electron transfer produces equal and opposite surface charges. After the release of the external force, both electrodes move apart, resulting in an abrupt drop in potential. To counteract this, electrons travel from one electrode to the other, completing the half-cycle of the AC output of TENG4,5. TENGs are devices with very high voltage output and high energy conversion efficiency. However, the electrical output performance of TENGs still needs improvement, as the current output of TENG devices remains very low. To improve electrical performance, numerous studies to date have focused on modifying triboelectric materials, such as patterning electrode surfaces, introducing porosity to polymers, and employing physical or chemical surface functionalization approaches. To efficiently increase the electrical production of TENGs, readily scalable, and cost-effective, processes are usually required6,7.

There are two effective methods for boosting the electrical properties of TENG devices. The first is nano-micro patterning, which expands the surface area by creating a high contact area through texturing hierarchical surface structures using various molding and lithography techniques. The structures can be nano- or micro-pillars, pyramids, and pores. The second is adding high dielectric materials to polymer matrices, such as CaCu3Ti4O12 (CCTO), BaTiO3, and TiO2. These fillers increase the composite’s dielectric constant, thereby increasing surface charge density and enhancing output performance.

CaCu3Ti4O12 (CCTO) is a cubic double-perovskite ceramic material with a general formula of AA′BO3, in which Ca+2 and Cu+2 are located on the A-site and A′-site, and Ti+4 resides at the B-site inside the network of corner-sharing TiO6 octahedra. Remarkably, without any doping CCTO exhibits extremely high dielectric permittivity (ε) up to 300,000, over a large frequency range from 102 – 106 Hz and temperature range from 100 – 600 K, making it promising dielectric filler for TENG. Open-shell Cu+2 and Ti+4 ions create strong interfacial polarization when they are incorporated into the perovskite lattice. Specifically, the d9 electronic configuration of Cu+2 causes a Jahn–Teller distortion that extends the TiO6 octahedra to create a square-planar motif, improving local charge separation8,9. In addition, each oxygen atom creates effective charge-transfer channels by forming strong covalent interactions with both A′- Cu+2 and B- Ti+4 ions. CCTO dramatically boosts the composite’s surface charge density and effective dielectric constant when it is distributed as dielectric fillers inside a polymer matrix10,11. This results in noticeably improved electrical performance in TENG devices. CCTO ceramic materials are frequently employed by researchers for the construction of TENG devices due to their excellent dielectric, semiconducting, and perovskite properties, as well as their non-toxicity. Furthermore, CCTO particles implanted in composite films exhibit a strong dielectric permittivity, providing significant benefits for various electronic applications. Recently, Manchi et al.12, fabricated CCTO/PDMS flexible composite film (FCF) based TENG device with cellulose paper and the addition of the CCTO particles into the poly-dimethylsiloxane (PDMS) polymer metrics it improved electrical conductivity of TENG. Furthermore, TiO2 nanoparticles have also been used as dielectric filler. Jian et al.13, designed a flower-like TiO2-filled polymethyl methacrylate (PMMA) composite as a tribo-positive layer and PDMS as a tribo-negative layer. They have optimized TENG performance by TiO2 loading amount and found that 40 wt% TiO2 loading have highest performance.

Graphene oxide (GO) is rich in oxygen-containing groups, shows high electron accepting properties and this group enhances negative tribo-layer formation and improve TENG charge density, but its weak conductivity and high dielectric loss limit charge retention14. Reduced graphene oxide (rGO) significantly improves electrical conductivity by restoring a large portion of the sp2 carbon network, while maintaining defect-mediated trap sites. Efficient electron trapping, which prevents negative tribo-charges from recombining with positive electrode charges, and quick charge transport are thus combined in rGO, which is embedded in the negative tribo-layer and produces a higher current than both pristine graphene and GO15.

In this comprehensive study, we synthesized CCTO and CCTO–TiO2 nanocomposites by a modified sol–gel fuel-combustion approach, Using citric acid as a combustibility source. This allowed for phase-pure production at relatively low temperatures (800 °C) without the presence of secondary impurities including CuO or CaTiO3. This approach is more controlled, energy-efficient, and appropriate for large-scale preparation than traditional solid-state synthesis, which makes it advantageous for device integration.

The primary novelty of this study lies in the combined use of CCTO and TiO2 NPs composite as synergistic co-fillers in the tribo-positive polymer matrix. CCTO is recognized for its high permittivity and pronounced Maxwell–Wagner interfacial polarization in polymer composites, features that considerably boost composite capacitance and interfacial charge storage capacity16. In addition to strengthening mechanical stability and environmental durability, TiO2 nanoparticles enhance this behaviour through the addition of numerous surface defect states (oxygen vacancies, Ti+3 centers), defect-mediated polarization at particle–polymer interfaces and these defect states serve as charge trapping regions that promote carrier retention in the triboelectric layer17,18. When CCTO and TiO2 coupled as co-fillers, both work through synergistic yet complementary mechanisms: CCTO amplifies bulk interfacial polarization and effective permittivity, while TiO2 improves localized trap state density and stabilizes interfacial charge accumulation (increasing charge retention). The improved charge generation and extended retention produced by this dual-filler approach surpass the capabilities of each material used alone. This synergistic dual-filler strategy therefore provides a rational approach for improving triboelectric performance within multilayer polymer-based TENG architectures.

To enhance device performance further, we constructed multilayer electrodes with an A–B–A sandwich configuration. The tribo-negative electrode consisted of PTFE/rGO-PTFE/PTFE. Tribo-negative electrode is constant in each TENG device. Here, rGO served as a charge-trapping and fast-transporting layer, effectively reducing recombination and enabling higher current outputs without compromising voltage stability. The tribo-positive electrode was designed as PVA/CCTO-PVA/PVA. CCTO and CCTO-TiO2 NPs composites and their different weight ratios based TENG devices were fabricated and characterized. The combination of dual-filler engineering and interfacial charge management presents a distinctive device architecture that advances TENG performance beyond conventional single-filler designs.

Furthermore, the practical utility of the contact separation mode TENG based on finger tapping was demonstrated by integrating it into a Fenton reaction for the degradation of methylene blue (MB) dye. This demonstration highlights the potential of the developed TENGs not only for efficient energy harvesting but also as self-powered systems for environmental remediation.

Experimental section

Materials

Calcium nitrate(Ca(NO3)2.4H2O), and Copper Nitrate(Cu(NO3)2.3H2O), Citric acid anhydrous (C6H8O7) Polyvinyl Alcohol (PVA), Hydrogen peroxide (H2O2 30%), Isopropyl Alcohol (IPA) and Ethanol (C2H5OH) was purchased from LOBA Chemie, Mumbai INDIA. FeCl2, Titanium Isopropoxide (Ti{OCH(CH3)2}4), Polytetrafluoroethylene (PTFE) 60 wt% dispersion in Milli-Q water has been purchased from Sigma Aldrich. TiO2 nanoparticles (rutile phase), ZnO NPs, reduced graphene oxide (rGO) and Silver conductive paste purchased from Techinstro industries Maharastra, INDIA.

Synthesis of CCTO particles

The CCTO particles were synthesized using new modified sol-gel fuel-combustion process. In the beginning, a stoichiometric ratio 1:3:4 of calcium nitrate, copper nitrate and titanium isopropoxide (TTIP) were used as precursors. The calcium and copper nitrites were dissolved in Milli-Q water in a 50 ml beaker with continuous stirring until a clear blue solution appeared. In a separate 50ml beaker, TTIP was also stirred in ethanol and slowly poured into the nitrate solution under continuous stirring. Subsequently, pre-dissolved in Milli-Q, 445 mg of citric acid anhydrous also added as combustion fuel. The whole solution mixture was stirred at 90 °C till homogenous light blue gel formed. After that, for combustion process, the gel was transferred to a muffle furnace and heated to 300°C for 3 hours. Later then, the black porous substance was collected and finely powdered. In order to improve phase purity and crystallinity, this powder was calcined for 3 hours at 800°C and sintered for 3 more hours at 900°C. The heating ramp rate of the furnace during the combustion initiation (300 °C), calcination (800 °C), and sintering (900 °C) stages were same that was 5 °C/min. Synthesis of CCTO and TiO2 nanoparticles composite

CCTO and TiO2 NPs composite was prepared by introducing TiO2 NPs into precursor solution after the addition of TTIP. To target two composite formulations, two separate reactions has been done by adding 50 mg (5%) and 100 mg (10%) of commercial rutile phase (20-50 nm) TiO2 NPs in whole precursor solution. Resulting samples were designated 5T-CCTO and 10T-CCTO, respectively. The gelation, combustion (300 °C), calcination (800°C), and sintering (900°C) processes used for the pure CCTO were also used to these combinations.

Preparation of CCTO/PVA dispersion

To disintegrate agglomerates and encourage uniform dispersion CCTO particles were sonicated for 1 hour in IPA and dried in hot air oven. 1.25 g PVA was dissolved separately in 25 ml of Milli-Q water at continuous heating up to 80 °C under continuous stirring. After proper dissolution, CCTO particles were added to the hot PVA solution to achieve dispersions of 10 wt% and 20 wt% relative to the polymer mass. To ensure homogeneity, the resultant mixes, designated 10%CCTO/PVA and 20%CCTO/PVA, were agitated for 12 hours at room temperature.

Preparation of 5T-CCTO/PVA dispersion

CCTO and 5% TiO2 NPs composite (5T-CCTO) powder was sonicated and then added to a 25 mL PVA solution using the same procedure as in Section “Preparation of CCTO/PVA dispersion". This resulted in filler loadings of 10 wt% and 20 wt%. The dispersions were designated as 10%5T-CCTO/PVA and 20%5T-CCTO/PVA, respectively. And stirred for 12 h at room temperature to achieve uniform particle distribution.

Preparation of 10T-CCTO/PVA dispersion

Similarly, 10T-CCTO powder was blended with PVA solution to achieve 10 wt% and 20 wt% filler loadings. The suspensions labelled as 10%10T-CCTO/PVA and 20%10T-CCTO/PVA. And to ensure proper dispersion, suspensions were constantly agitated for 12 hours.

Preparation of rGO/PTFE dispersion

Commercially available 60 wt% PTFE in Milli-Q water dispersion and rGO were employed to prepare the rGO/PTFE suspension. First 100 mg rGO has been dispersed in 10 ml of IPA by ultra-sonication for 30 min. Then 60 wt% PTFE dispersion made to 30 wt% by adding Milli-Q water. The rGO in IPA and diluted PTFE suspensions were then combined in a 1:5 volume ratio and stirred for 30 min to yield a homogeneous rGO/PTFE dispersion suitable for subsequent spin coating.

Spin-coating of triboelectric layers

All the films used in TENG device fabrication has been prepared by spin coating using NXG-P1 spin coater. Commercially available ITO-coated glass slides (2.5 × 2.5 cm2) were used as substrates for the TENG. Before spin coating, the ITO coated substrates were thoroughly cleaned and ultrasonicated in liquid soap, Milli-Q water, IPA, and acetone for 10 minutes each. Silver conductive ink was then applied as contacts and dried in hot air oven. The films of the prepared suspensions were deposited on the ITO-coated glass substrates using the NXG-P1 spin coater. The contact part of ITO substrate already covered by kapton (polyimide) tape.

TENG device assembly

The TENG device, based on the vertical contact separation mode, was assembled using two plastic platforms supported by four springs at the corners, for vertical movement of the springs. The space between the tribo-positive and negative multilayer electrodes has been calculated to be 3 cm, ensuring sufficient room for tapping. The used spring has a free length of 4 cm, an outer coil diameter of 5 mm, and a wire diameter of ~ 0.8 mm. These settings were selected to ensure consistent contact-separation behaviour and structural stability throughout time. The two substrates are then fixed onto the plastic platforms with the triboelectric surfaces facing each other. Characterization techniques

Synthesized samples of CCTO and composites were characterized for their structural, morphological, and adsorption studies. X-ray diffraction patterns were obtained from an X-ray diffractometer (Bruker, AXS D8 Focus P-XRD) using CuKα (1.54 Å) in the range of 10–80°. The absorption spectra were obtained in the wavelength range of 200–800 nm using UV–Vis spectrophotometer (Shimadzu UV1800ENG 240V). Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spectra were recorded from 4000 to 400 cm−1 using a FTIR spectrophotometer (PerkinElmer spectrum 65 series, Massachusetts, United States). FESEM, EDX, and elemental mapping (Sigma 300, ZEISS Gemini, Germany) were used to capture the surface morphology and elemental composition of the samples. Thermo Fisher Scientific’s X-Ray Photoelectron Spectrometer (XPS) has been used for elemental analysis. Furthermore, the Keithley source meter 2604B model was used for all electrical characterization.

Result and discussion

Synthesized CCTO and composites characterization

For structural characterization of CCTO particles, it has been grinded well and dark brown colour powder obtained. To investigate the crystallographic information and phase identification of CCTO particles XRD characterization was employed, as shown in Fig. 3a and 3b. The X-ray diffraction pattern of CCTO powder has all the significant peaks in the 2θ range of 10°-80°, which perfectly matches the standard JCPDS card #75–2188 displayed in Fig. 3a. The achieved major diffraction peaks at 28.97°, 33.74°, 37.90°, 41.87°, 45.37°, 48.67°, 60.91°, and 71.80° were attributed to the crystalline planes of (211), (220), (013), (222), (321), (400), (422), and (440) respectively. The absence of residual CuO, CaTiO3 and TiO2 peaks using this synthesis technique indicates the single-phase nature of the CCTO sample. The strong reflections observed at 33.74° (220), 48.67° (400), and 71.80° (422) highlighted the high crystallinity of the CCTO phase. Figure 3b further compares the combined XRD patterns of pure CCTO with its TiO2-containing composites (5T-CCTO and 10T-CCTO). An enlarged XRD profile of the (220) plane (Fig. 3d) revealed a slight shift of this peak toward higher 2θ values for the 5T-CCTO and 10T-CCTO samples. Furthermore, an enlarged graph of the plane (220) shown in Fig.3d, reveals a small shift of this peak toward higher 2θ for 5T-CCTO and 10T-CCTO composite samples. Although no new secondary phases were detected, this rightward shift suggested a minor variation in lattice parameters, which can be attributed to lattice distortion or interfacial strain introduced by TiO2 nanoparticle incorporation into the CCTO matrix. Notably, no distinct diffraction peaks corresponding to TiO2 were observed in the composites, indicating that the structural integrity and crystalline framework of CCTO remained intact upon TiO2 addition. M. Chinnathambi et. al (2021) previously reported the similar effect on neodymium doped CCTO particles did not exhibit any neodymium peaks in XRD19.

The crystallite size was estimated using Scherrer’s equation:

D = Kλ/βcosθ

Where D is the crystalline size, K is 0.9 (Scherrer constant), λ is 1.5406 nm, the wavelength of X-ray source used in XRD, β is FWHM and θ is the position of the peak in the formula11. The calculated crystalline size of the CCTO, 5T-CCTO and 10T-CCTO were estimated to be 33.9, 32.42 and 40.56nm respectively, shown in Fig 3c.

FTIR spectra of CCTO and the 5T-CCTO, 10T-CCTO composite samples were recorded; to investigate alterations in the local bonding environment, the complete and magnified spectra are displayed in Fig. 3e and 3f, respectively. The distinctive metal–oxygen vibrational characteristics of the CCTO perovskite are present in each sample. Specifically, the Ti–O–Ti stretching mode is observed at approximately 470 cm⁻1 for CCTO, shifting to approximately 451 cm⁻1 for 5T-CCTO and 445 cm⁻1 for 10T-CCTO. This suggests that the Ti–O bonding environment is altered when TiO2 nanoparticles are added. In CCTO, the Cu–O bending mode is detected at 515 cm⁻1, and in both composites, it shifts slightly to 518 cm-1. The pure CCTO has a band at 604 cm⁻1 that is attributed to Ca–O21,22. However, the TiO2 NPs-containing samples exhibit a considerable attenuation (or lack of clear resolution) of this band, indicating that the local Ca–O vibrational signature is masked or its intensity diminished during composite creation. Furthermore, for both 5T- and 10T-CCTO, a metal–oxygen (M–O; M = Ti or Cu) stretching characteristic is observed at 563 cm⁻1 and 553 cm⁻1 for CCTO23. The observed intensity alterations and peak shifts are compatible with interfacial interactions and minor lattice perturbations between the inserted TiO2 nanoparticles and the CCTO matrix, even though no new absorption bands attributed to secondary phases are observed. Collectively, the FTIR measurements show slight bond-level changes brought on by the addition of TiO2 and validate the preservation of the perovskite-related vibrational framework.

Fabricated TENG devices and electrical characterization

Fig. 6(a) demonstrates a multilayer contact-separation TENG constructed on two glass substrate electrodes covered with ITO. With a sandwiched structure of the A-B-A type, one electrode has a stack of PVA, a high-ε CCTO/PVA composite, and a capping layer PVA film. The other electrode features a PTFE layer and a reduced graphene oxide/PTFE (rGO/PTFE) composite layer underneath a top PTFE film. Neither an electric potential difference nor a charge is produced at the initial stage shown in Fig. 6 (a-1). As seen in Fig. 6 (a-2) force is applied to the TENG device to bring both triboelectric layers into physical contact. The contact region generates and overlaps opposite and equivalent charges. As a result, charges proceed from the state with higher energy to the state with lower energy. As illustrated in Fig. 6 (a-3), two electrodes produce opposing charges when the two triboelectric materials are gradually separated by eliminating force33–35. The potential difference causes electrons to travel from the one electrode to another through the outer circuit, producing an electrical signal. After the completion of electron transfer, the electric potential is in an equilibrium state as shown in Fig. 6 (a-4). The equilibrium of electrostatic induction is disrupted when the devices come into contact again, as a result of the force supplied to the TENG device (Fig. 6 (a-5)), which causes the electrons to flow in the opposite direction and creates a reversible electric signal. Following PVA/PTFE TENG, CCTO, and TiO2 NPs modified CCTO composites based TENG devices exhibit significantly increased electrical current production, as shown in Fig. 8. There are two main explanations for the significantly increased current output. The first is the addition of rGO to the PTFE tribo-layer. Due to its SP2 carbon network, which exhibits higher electrical conductivity than GO, rGO serves as both a charge conducting layer and a charge trapping layer in the PTFE tribo-layer, greatly increasing the short-circuit current output of TENGs while maintaining an equivalent open-circuit voltage44,45. Reduced graphene oxide produces localized trap states and quick electron routes when it is incorporated into the PTFE tribo-layer46. During charge transfer, this lowers internal resistance and increases short-circuit current because of the highly conductive environment of rGO47. Additionally, well-dispersed rGO introduce defect/edge states and residual oxygen functions that serve as stable electron traps. As a result, the instantaneous current may rise even when the Voc is the same or somewhat decreased. Second is the addition of high-permittivity ceramic fillers48 (CCTO, TiO2 NPs modified CCTO composites) to the polymer matrix enhances the composite dielectric response by enhancing the Maxwell–Wagner–Sillars interfacial polarization49 at particle–polymer interfaces explained in Fig. 746. In ultimately, TENG device efficiency is affected by two competing variables first is the higher displacement-current and charge capacity advantages due to high-dielectric fillers50 (CCTO and TiO2 NPs modified CCTO composites), and second is the the more rapid charge extraction and lower series resistance provided by rGO in PTFE tribo-layer. The fabricated TENG, which is based on multilayer PVA/CCTO–TiO2 tribo-positive and PTFE/rGO tribo-negative electrodes, has considerable practical applicability, because of its improved charge generation capacity, steady electrical output, and mechanical durability. The device’s electrical characteristics make it appropriate for varied applications including low-power electronics, self-powered wireless sensor nodes, and wearable energy harvesting devices. The enhanced rate of Fenton-based methylene blue (MB) degradation is the primary application shown in the present study.

To illustrate the degradation of organic pollutants, dye degradation was carried out using carbon cloth and a Pt electrode that was powered by a TENG device. Figure 9 shows the schematics of the TENG-assisted system utilized to degrade MB dye. The target pollutant for illustrating deterioration was MB dye. A 100 ml MB dye solution has been taken for the degradation process, and a stock solution containing 10 ppm of MB dye in 500 ml of Milli-Q water has been made. A 100 mL borosilicate beaker was filled with the electrodes and MB dye (Figure 9). Inside the reaction container, 2 cm distance region was created by positioning the Pt wire electrode and carbon cloth (3x1 cm2) vertically and away from one another. The TENG device was connected to the AC side of rectifier, and the electrodes submerged in the reaction container were connected to the full wave rectifier at the DC output and manual tapping63,64 done for 60 min. Three distinct kinds of catalytic reactions were carried out in order to illustrate the TENG device’s improved pollutant degrading capabilities. Using 100 mg of ZnO NPs and 600 ppm of H2O2 in an acidic environment at pH 3 without light, the first catalytic reactions were conducted. The second catalytic degradation, known as the Fenton reaction65,66 and TENG-assisted Fenton catalytic degradation, or electro-Fenton degradation67,68, was carried out using 5 mg of FeCl2, ZnO NPs, and 600 ppm of H2O2 at pH 3. This systematic study enabled a direct evaluation of the TENG’s contribution in enhancing advanced oxidation processes for organic pollutant degradation.

Proposed mechanism of MB dye degradation using the TENG device

The triboelectric nanogenerator (TENG) acts as a sustainable driving source to initiate and accelerate the degradation of methylene blue (MB) dye through electro-Fenton and advanced oxidation pathways. Upon mechanical activation, the TENG generates an alternating current (AC) output that is rectified to provide a pulsed direct current (DC) potential across the electrodes. This potential difference facilitates the reduction and oxidation reactions required for dye degradation.

At the cathode, the electrons generated by the TENG reduce dissolved O2 into superoxide radicals (O2•⁻), which further participate in the formation of hydroxyl radicals (•OH). Simultaneously, in the presence of Fe+2 ions, the reduction–oxidation cycling between Fe+2 / Fe+3 accelerates the continuous generation of •OH radicals through the Fenton reaction69. Conclusion

In this comprehensive study, we have synthesized pure-phase CCTO particles using a modified sol-gel fuel combustion method and prepared TiO2 NPs-modified CCTO composites (5T-CCTO and 10T-CCTO) as dielectric fillers for TENG devices. We have fabricated multi-layer tribo-positive and tribo-negative electrodes following an A-B-A sandwich-shaped structure of PVA/CCTO-PVA/PVA, and PTFE/rGO-PTFE/PTFE, respectively. Fillers wt% changed in every device by 10 to 20% for CCTO and TiO2 NPs modified CCTO composites. While the base PVA/PTFE-based TENG device exhibited the highest peak-to-peak voltage of 2.73 V, the incorporation of high-dielectric fillers led to a substantial improvement in charge storage and transfer. This increased the short-circuit current, with the 10% 10T-CCTO-based TENG achieving a peak of 3.62 µA. Moreover, other dielectric filler-based TENGs also show an improvement in current compared to the PVA/PTFE TENG, which ranges from 1 μA to 3 μA. This TENG device has been utilized in MB degradation with the Fenton reaction. Without the TENG device, the Fenton reaction shows 97.8% of MB degradation within 60-minute time duration. In the TENG integrated Fenton reaction, MB degradation shows 98.5% of MB removal in 60 min. Furthermore, the TENG-assisted process accelerated the degradation kinetics, achieving an approximately 20% faster reaction rate, as evidenced by the increase in the pseudo-first-order rate constant (k).
