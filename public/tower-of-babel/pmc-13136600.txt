Introduction

According to the World Health Organization (WHO), acute pediatric critical illness involves significant airway, respiratory, or circulatory issues, as well as a sudden decline in consciousness [1]. The Pediatric Intensive Care Unit (PICU) is a specialized unit designed to care for children with life‐threatening illnesses requiring advanced medical and surgical interventions [2].

Approximately 1.5% of hospitalized children require ICU services [3]. The primary goal of PICUs is to reduce child mortality through continuous care, although this also imposes financial burdens on families and communities. Mortality rates in PICUs vary significantly, ranging from 2.6% to 37%, influenced by factors such as care quality, geographical location, and patient demographics [4].

In high‐income countries, advancements in medical care have led to notable reductions in mortality and morbidity rates in PICUs over recent decades. In contrast, children in low‐ and middle‐income countries continue to face higher mortality rates due to a lack of specialized pediatric ICUs, insufficient trained medical personnel, and limited access to essential medications and equipment [1]. For instance, research in Iran shows regional variations in mortality rates, with figures reported at 6.6% in the north, 9% in the northwest, and 16.5% in the southwest [4].

Mortality among critically ill children admitted to PICUs remains a major global challenge despite significant advancements in medical care. Ongoing efforts to improve the quality of care have rarely succeeded in alleviating the notably high mortality rates in PICUs [5]. A variety of studies have investigated the factors contributing to mortality, including age, the presence of septic shock, multiple organ dysfunction syndrome (MODS), duration of stay in the PICU, length of mechanical ventilation, pre‐existing chronic conditions, and infections caused by antibiotic‐resistant pathogens. The overall mortality rate in the PICU remains alarmingly high due to the inherent vulnerabilities of the pediatric population and the constraints on available resources. Therefore, it is crucial to assess and predict the risk of mortality and its underlying causes [4].

The accurate prediction of mortality risk among critically ill children represents a major challenge in PICUs. If fulfilled, it can help physicians make informed clinical decisions and deliver more optimized and targeted care. Moreover, it can assist physicians in identifying patients at higher risk of death, enabling them to implement appropriate preventive and therapeutic interventions [5].

Conventional approaches to predicting mortality in the PICU, including scoring systems, frequently lack accuracy and reliability. These systems typically rely on restricted demographic and clinical data but fail to consider the unique complexities of each patient. As a new tool, Artificial Intelligence (AI) has demonstrated significant promise in more accurately predicting mortality among critically ill children in the PICU. AI can find intricate patterns linked to mortality risk by examining large volumes of clinical data. This can help physicians better predict mortality risk and provide more efficient care [5, 6].

While AI holds considerable promise, its application in the PICU presents a number of challenges. A key challenge is the absence of compelling evidence regarding the effectiveness and accuracy of AI models in predicting mortality. Challenges also exist in interpreting the results of AI models and integrating them into the clinical setting. A systematic review can summarize the current evidence regarding the role of AI in predicting mortality among critically ill children in the PICU [6, 7].

Unlike prior reviews that broadly examine AI use across Neonatal Intensive Care Unit (NICU) and PICU settings or encompass multiple clinical outcomes, our study is exclusively dedicated to mortality prediction in the PICU context. This narrower scope enables a more targeted evaluation of model performance and clinical relevance.

Methods

Search Strategy

A comprehensive and systematic search was conducted across three major electronic databases: PubMed, Scopus, and Web of Science. These databases were selected for their comprehensive coverage of biomedical and scientific literature to ensure a thorough search for relevant studies. In developing our search strategy, we adhered to the principles of systematic review methodology and reporting transparency as outlined in Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta‐Analyses (PRISMA) and Population, Exposure, Comparison, and Outcome (PECO) frameworks. Our primary objective was to identify studies specifically addressing mortality prediction in critically ill pediatric populations using AI within PICU settings. Keywords and terms from the Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) were carefully selected to capture the core concepts of the study. Accordingly, we selected search terms—“mortality,” “pediatric intensive care unit,” “artificial intelligence,” and “prediction”—that most directly reflected the core components of our research question.

To ensure a comprehensive search, advanced search techniques were used, including the use of Boolean operators (OR and AND) to combine terms and refine the results. Searches were conducted across the Title, Abstract, and Keywords fields of each database to maximize the search for relevant records. Eligibility Criteria

The database searches were conducted on December 2, 2024 by a member of the research team. No time restrictions were applied to ensure that all relevant studies published up to the search date were included. English‐language sources that specifically focused on the role of AI in predicting mortality among critically ill children admitted to the PICU and employed an AI model for pediatric mortality prediction were deemed suitable for inclusion. The study excluded review articles, editorials, conference papers, commentaries, case reports, and qualitative studies.

Screening and Study Selection

All records identified by the database searches were imported into EndNote X8 software for systematic management and organization. Duplicate records were removed.

The screening process was conducted in two stages:

1.Title and abstract screening: Two independent members of the research team reviewed the titles and abstracts of the remaining datasets to assess their relevance to the study objectives. Studies that clearly did not meet the eligibility criteria were excluded at this stage.

Title and abstract screening: Two independent members of the research team reviewed the titles and abstracts of the remaining datasets to assess their relevance to the study objectives. Studies that clearly did not meet the eligibility criteria were excluded at this stage.

2.Full‐text review: Records that passed the initial screening were retrieved for full‐text evaluation. The same two researchers independently assessed the full texts to determine eligibility based on the predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria.

Full‐text review: Records that passed the initial screening were retrieved for full‐text evaluation. The same two researchers independently assessed the full texts to determine eligibility based on the predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria.

Disagreements between the two reviewers on the eligibility of studies were resolved by consensus. If consensus could not be reached, a third researcher was consulted to make the final decision.

This systematic review followed the PRISMA guidelines. A PRISMA flow diagram was used to document the study selection process, including the number of records identified, screened, excluded, and included in the final analysis. The results of the different studies were summarized in tables based on the outcome of interest.

Study Evaluation and Data Extraction

The quality of the studies was assessed by two reviewers. Disagreements were resolved by a third researcher. The Prediction model study Risk Of Bias Assessment Tool (PROBAST) was used to assess the risk of bias and concerns regarding applicability of the included studies. Risk of bias was judged in the following four domains: participants, predictors, outcome, and analysis. Applicability concerns were assessed for the first three domains. This tool offers 3 degrees of bias and applicability: low, high, and unclear.

A checklist was developed to collect details on author, year, country, number of samples, outcome, variables, methodology, AUROC, AUPRC, sensitivity, specificity, PPV, NPV, accuracy, and F1‐score. A research team member extracted and entered the relevant data into this checklist.

Results

A preliminary search of the three databases resulted in 85 records, which were subsequently imported into EndNote. After duplicates were removed, 53 records remained for further examination. The title and abstract screening process identified 19 articles that met the eligibility criteria for full‐text review. The full‐text review excluded six articles due to their review or editorial article types or the lack of pediatric mortality prediction. Eventually, thirteen articles fulfilled the final inclusion criteria. Additionally, the reference lists of the included articles were hand‐searched, yielding nine potentially relevant articles, five of which were excluded based on eligibility criteria. Kim et al. (2019) used a convolutional neural network (CNN) to analyze real‐time predictions of all‐cause mortality and achieved an area under the AUROC of 0.85–0.95 for predictions made between 6 and 60 h before death. Similarly, Che et al. (2016) used gradient boosting trees (GBT) with a focus on static and temporal features and achieved an AUROC of 0.79. Aczon et al. (2021) further improved the prediction landscape by using a recurrent neural network (RNN) to continuously assess mortality risk across 12,516 episodes, achieving an AUROC of 0.94 at 12 h and an exceptional value of 0.99 at 24 h post‐assessment. This demonstrates that RNNs can handle large datasets with diverse variables effectively, providing dynamic predictions that adapt to changing patient conditions. Other studies, such as Aczon et al. (2017), also used RNNs to predict ICU mortality and reported an AUROC of 0.93, further demonstrating the efficacy of RNNs in this area.

In contrast, machine learning models such as random forests (RF) and artificial neural networks (ANN) also showed robust performance in various studies. For example, Wang et al. (2024) achieved an AUROC of 0.96 with ANN for predicting sepsis‐related mortality in children, while Daley et al. (2022) reported an AUROC of 0.90 with RF for traumatic brain injury outcomes. These results suggest that while deep learning models are increasingly prominent, established methods still have significant predictive power, especially when tailored to specific clinical contexts.

Except for the AUROC, which was reported in all studies, other performance metrics—such as AUPRC, sensitivity, specificity, PPV, NPV, accuracy, and F1‐score—were reported in only a subset of the studies. The AUPRC, reported in ten studies, fell within the poor performance range. In 71% of the studies that measured sensitivity (the proportion of patients who actually died) and specificity (the proportion of patients who actually survived), the performance of the prediction models was deemed acceptable. The PPV, defined as the ratio of correctly predicted instances (true positives and true negatives) to the total number of instances, was satisfactory only in the study by Prithula et al. In contrast, the NPV was reported as satisfactory in all six studies that included this metric. Accuracy, the ratio of correctly predicted instances to the total number of instances, showed favorable performance in studies that assessed the accuracy of predictive models. The F1‐score, representing the harmonic mean of precision and recall, it was reported as poor in three studies and satisfactory in two others.

The most frequently used algorithm in the studies was random forest. Overall, the AUROC was greater than 0.8 in 88% of the studies and less than 0.8 in 12% of the studies.

Discussion

In recent years, AI has emerged as a novel tool in the medical field, particularly in the PICU. Predicting mortality in critically ill children presents a significant clinical challenge that can substantially influence treatment decisions. This systematic review analyzed the role of AI in this field and evaluated the results of various studies.

Critical illness in pediatric populations leads to millions of admissions to PICUs [25, 26]. The PICU team provides care for critically ill children by implementing early, comprehensive interventions and delivering high‐quality treatments to achieve enhanced clinical outcomes. However, PICU‐admitted children may experience complications that rapidly deteriorate their health status and result in death despite high‐quality care [27]. Hence, identifying predictive or determinant factors of mortality in PICU is of significant clinical relevance [28].

Mortality prediction in the PICU has implications for clinical care [21]. Mortality risk is commonly employed in research to classify disease severity and to enhance ICU quality [20]. Models predicting mortality risk in critically ill children are essential for evaluating the efficacy and efficiency of pediatric intensive care [29]. Additionally, reliable prognostic estimates enable providers to inform families about the likely outcomes for patients admitted to the intensive care unit [21].

Therefore, the timely deployment of mortality prediction tools can prove highly beneficial in contexts involving critically ill, mechanically ventilated children. Preferably, such predictive instruments should be developed using basic clinical data and have a high level of specificity to identify patients with a good prognosis [27]. Since the inception of the PICU, intensive care researchers have been continually evaluating mortality risk prediction systems. Currently, the most widely used systems in PICUs are pediatric risk of mortality (PRISM) score, pediatric index of mortality (PIM), and pediatric logistic organ dysfunction (PELOD) score [28].

The PRISM score evaluates disease severity by utilizing 17 commonly monitored physiological variables and their respective ranges. It assesses physiological status through defined ranges of these factors and incorporates categorical variables to facilitate an accurate evaluation of mortality risk [30].

PIM‐3 is a simple and inexpensive system that predicts the outcomes for pediatric patients based on data collected during the first hour of their PICU admission, even before any additional diagnosis or treatment [31].

The PELOD score is a commonly utilized system for evaluating multiple organ dysfunction in children. The PELOD‐2 score provides a reliable means to assess the severity of multiple organ dysfunction syndrome. It includes 10 variables that correspond to 5 different organ dysfunctions. An increased PELOD‐2 score is associated with a greater number of organ failures and an elevated mortality rate [32].

Studies [10, 11, 12, 15, 17, 20, 21] were compared against conventional scoring systems such as PRISM, PIM, and PELOD. In these investigations, AI‐based models employed a wide range of clinical, laboratory, demographic, and therapeutic features to predict mortality risk in PICUs. The selected features demonstrated superior predictive performance compared to traditional tools.

Chan et al. (2006) employed variables aligned with the PIM, supplemented by pre‐ICU intubation data. Their ANN model achieved an AUROC of 0.96, outperforming PIM (AUROC 0.81), suggesting that even a limited set of well‐curated features can yield high predictive accuracy when modeled appropriately [10].

Aczon et al. (2021) utilized a RNN trained on 430 distinct features encompassing physiologic, laboratory, demographic, and therapeutic data. Their model achieved an AUROC of 0.94 at 12 h and 0.99 at 24 h, significantly surpassing PIM‐2 (AUROC 0.88), PRISM III (AUROC 0.89) and PELOD daily (AUROC 0.85) in the same cohort [11]. Aczon et al. (2017) similarly leveraged RNNs to generate temporally dynamic predictions using a wide array of sequential inputs. Their findings reinforce the notion that time‐series modeling of physiologic and therapeutic data can enhance predictive granularity beyond static scores [12].

Hong et al. (2021) applied ensemble step‐wise feature selection to identify 11 high‐impact features from an initial set of 397. These included demographics, vital signs, and laboratory values. Their linear regression model achieved an AUROC of 0.75, outperforming PRISM III (AUROC 0.69), and underscoring the value of targeted feature selection in interpretable models [15].

Lee et al. (2021) developed a machine learning model using early‐stage ICU data (within 72 h), including demographic, physiologic, and laboratory variables. Their random forest model achieved an AUROC of 0.91, indicating strong early predictive capability in contrast to PIM‐3 (AUROC 0.84) [17].

In the seven analyses examined [10, 11, 12, 15, 17, 20, 21], AI systems used in predicting deaths within PICUs mainly relied on body function measures and lab test results as top performance factors ‐ some also added treatment‐related data. Although patient traits like age or gender appeared in many of these tools, their impact tended to be smaller when set against real‐time clinical indicators.

One key aspect was how the RNN used sequences of data, so changes in vital signs and lab results over time helped form predictions. As more information became available during ICU care, this method got better at distinguishing outcomes. In contrast, the random forest didn't track timing closely but highlighted which inputs mattered most, regularly pointing to platelet levels, acid‐base balance, potassium, and age‐adjusted vital signs. Even simpler artificial networks tested under tight resources showed similar performance by relying only on basic yes‐or‐no factors like planned admission, breathing support, and pupil reaction, showing machine learning can adjust well across varying conditions.

In comparison, conventional scoring tools like PIM, PRISM, and PELOD use a narrow, preset group of indicators gathered at specific intervals. While PIM considers entry details—such as systolic pressure, oxygenation index, elective versus emergency admission, and pupil reaction—PRISM includes certain lab results along with vital signs; meanwhile, PELOD centers on organ failure markers. Although these models remain unchanged once built, their reach is narrow due to reliance on linear statistical methods that fail to reflect complex patterns or dynamic clinical courses. As a result, they might assign too low a risk for individuals whose condition worsens quickly ‐ or too high a score for those recovering after an acute episode.

Taken together, the comparison shows AI models gain predictive strength through three main aspects: first, wider input types—covering physiological, lab, treatment, and patient background data instead of just limited ones; second, ongoing time‐based processing, especially in recurrent network designs, updating risk levels whenever new data becomes available; third, ability to capture intricate non‐straight patterns, letting minor combined effects shape forecasts. Such traits clarify why these systems often beat standard tools in accuracy and reliability, also suggesting potential for personalized, real‐time monitoring in intensive pediatric care.

Machine learning is a subfield of artificial intelligence. More specifically, it focuses on developing computer algorithms that learn and predict based on data. These algorithms analyze data to identify patterns and relationships, enabling them to make informed decisions when presented with new data [33]. Deep learning is a subset of machine learning that uses multilayered artificial neural networks to learn data representations with multiple levels of abstraction. Deep learning algorithms are designed to automatically extract hierarchical features from complex data, optimizing weighted parameters in the process. This results in a more efficient learning experience and reduced prediction errors [34]. The studies reviewed indicated that machine learning and deep learning algorithms demonstrated high accuracy and speed in predicting the mortality of critically ill children.

The accurate and comprehensive evaluation of the predictive performance of developed models is a critical step in their development. Performance is often expressed in terms of the Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) curve and the AUROC curve. These measures provide a visual representation of model performance [21]. Most algorithms used in research have reported an AUROC curve greater than 0.8. These findings highlight the huge potential of AI in improving clinical outcomes.

Nevertheless, studies exhibit inconsistent outcomes. Some studies have reported superior performance, while others have struggled with challenges such as insufficient data. As a result, the developed models may not be generalizable to other facilities [35]. The specific clinical conditions, the algorithms employed, and the type of input data contributed to these discrepancies. Several factors can influence the predictive performance of AI models. For instance, the quality and type of clinical data (such as vital signs, medical histories, and laboratory results) significantly affect the algorithms' performance. Furthermore, the proper feature selection for training the model can impact the outcomes significantly. Data from the reviewed studies were collected retrospectively over a relatively long period to attain a large sample size. It is possible that the weighting of specific risk factors incorporated into the model may have altered over time, and the database contained a limited number of daily variables. Incorporating more variables and employing a different modeling approach may result in superior predictive models [35].

Over the past decade, significant progress in applying AI techniques has been documented. These techniques are deployed across diverse data sets. The expanding availability of structured and unstructured data derived from electronic health records and digital technologies, including portable sensors, has rapidly boosted the scope of AI applications. These techniques can potentially improve the diagnosis of critically ill patients, facilitate understanding of pathophysiological pathways, and refine therapeutic goals during critical care management. Although AI has enormous potential to improve the management of critically ill children, significant obstacles must be overcome before it can be successfully implemented to transform healthcare delivery. The unique challenges of AI warrant immediate attention to identify and address the disproportionate impact of various factors on pediatric mortality [36].

AI deployment can facilitate informed clinical decision‐making and reduce the workload of physicians. Enhanced predictive accuracy afforded by AI can enable physicians to optimize patient treatment planning and resource allocation, potentially resulting in reduced mortality and improved quality of healthcare services. Future research should further examine AI algorithms relative to conventional mortality prediction techniques. Furthermore, the development of extensive and varied databases is essential for the training of AI models. These databases may encompass clinical, genetic, and environmental data that enhance the accuracy of predictions.

The studies emphasize the crucial role of machine learning and deep learning in improving mortality prediction in PICUs. The variability in AUROC values between different methods shows that while certain models excel in certain contexts, the choice of algorithm and feature selection significantly affect prediction accuracy. This highlights the need for ongoing research to refine these models, validate their clinical applicability and ultimately improve patient outcomes in critical care. Furthermore, the widespread adoption of these technologies will require interdisciplinary collaboration between researchers, clinicians and information technology specialists to ensure that these tools are both effective and can be seamlessly integrated into clinical workflows.

Applications of AI in healthcare, similar to other medical tools and interventions, require thorough testing prior to their integration into routine clinical practice. The adoption of AI in pediatric critical care may be slower compared to other medical specialties due to concerns regarding the safety and effectiveness of these technologies in particularly vulnerable populations. Research has indicated that certain AI algorithms have outperformed traditional methods and may have had an indirect effect on mortality rates. However, it remains uncertain whether these AI applications will result in tangible reductions in patient mortality. Future research in AI should employ prospective, experimental study designs to evaluate its impact on mortality, utilizing reliable, validated measures and implementation frameworks [37].
