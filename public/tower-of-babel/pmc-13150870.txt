Correlation between recognition of autologous tumor organoids by tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes from metastatic epithelial cancers and clinical response
Authors: Alexandra M Gustafson, Aarushi Bhasin, Billel Gasmi, Brian D Bui, Catherine Marie Ade, Ken-ichi Hanada, Hyunmi K Halas, Jared J Gartner, Todd D Prickett, Jon Salazar, Maria R Parkhust, Paul Robbins, Nicholas D Klemen, Mei Li Kwong, Stephanie L Goff, Steven A Rosenberg, James C Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundNeoantigen-specific tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes (TILs) have the ability to mediate responses in patients with metastatic epithelial cancers. While some identified factors are associated with response, there is a need for ways to determine which patients will benefit from adoptive cell therapy with TIL. Patient-derived tumor organoids (PDTO) retain the genetic makeup of the tumor from which they are derived, and are a useful tool in personalized medicine to test the effectiveness of anticancer therapies.MethodsTumor organoids were grown from patients with metastatic epithelial cancers treated on a TIL protocol. TIL recognition of autologous PDTO by enzyme-linked immunosorbent spot assays and 4–1BB upregulation, as well as objective Response Evaluation Criteria in Solid Tumors (RECIST) responses to treatment, were measured in a cohort of treated patients. The most common histologies were colorectal (20 patients, 67%), pancreatic (4 patients, 13%), and breast (2 patients, 7%) cancer.ResultsRecognition of PDTO was driven by major histocompatibility complex (MHC) class I-restricted reactivity and upregulation of MHC in response to interferon-γ was not associated with response. There were no responders in the 9 patients who lacked PDTO recognition on their initial TIL screening compared with seven responses in 18 patients with initial reactivity (p=0.059). After TIL were selected and expanded for administration, 15 infusion products showed organoid recognition, with 8 of these patients achieving objective responses (53%), while only 1 of 15 patients without organoid recognition responded (7%; p=0.014).ConclusionsTIL reactivity against autologous tumor organoid appears to be associated with objective clinical response in patients with metastatic epithelial cancers.Keywords: Tumor infiltrating lymphocyte - TIL, Solid tumor, Immunotherapy, Escape/evasion, Colorectal Cancer

Background

Adoptive cell transfer of tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes (TIL) has the ability to shrink tumors in selected patients with common epithelial cancers.1,3 While treatment with TIL has proven successful in patients with metastatic melanoma, with objective response rates of 50–70%,4 the response rate in patients with gastrointestinal cancers is lower at 7–23%.5 A major task in developing TIL therapy has been identifying T cells which recognize safe tumor-specific antigens. Data from patients with cancer treated with checkpoint inhibitors or T-cell adoptive transfer has established the importance of the products of tumor-specific mutations as antigens in the immune response to human cancers.6,8 Highlighting this discovery is the fact that cancers with high mutational burdens, such as melanoma and all tumors with DNA repair deficits, have the highest rates of response to T-cell-based immunotherapies. The availability of whole exome sequencing (WES) has revealed the enormous heterogeneity of tumor mutational profiles with very few shared mutations between tumors, even of the same histological type. In addition to this, our study of T-cell responses to tumor-specific mutated proteins showed that only 1–2% of aberrant proteins encoded by a non-synonymous tumor-specific mutation could generate a documented T-cell response and truly be called a “neoantigen”.9 Among 75 patients with gastrointestinal cancers whose tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes were analyzed for neoantigen reactivity, 124 T-cell responses were discovered. All antigens were unique to an individual patient with two exceptions—KRAS and p53 mutations generated T-cell responses in more than one patient TIL, although most were against different amino acid substitutions or used different human leukocyte antigen (HLA) restrictions. These data illustrate the extreme degree of personalization in the T-cell responses to patient cancers. Given this situation, having a patient’s autologous tumor as a laboratory reagent would be extremely valuable in analyzing T-cell responses. Although melanomas can be established with some frequency as two-dimensional tumor cell cultures (contributing to much of our early understanding of human T-cell responses to cancer), this is not true of most of the common epithelial cancers. A solution to this has been the use of three-dimensional organoid culture for these cancers.10

Patient-derived tumor organoids (PDTO) are three-dimensional cancer cell lines grown from surgically resected tumors, which retain the histopathologic and genomic features of the original tumor.11 12 PDTO can successfully be grown from multiple histologies, including colorectal, breast, pancreatic, liver, and ovarian cancers, and have been used to study tumor biology, but have been less explored as immunological reagents. These represent ideal ex vivo reagents to test for immunoreactivity, as they faithfully display most of the mutational profile of the tumor of origin and may retain important tumor-specific problems with antigen processing and presentation, which are masked when normal professional antigen presenting cells are used in place of autologous tumor. Additionally, organoids typically have little normal tissue contamination,12 improving their ability to identify issues such as HLA loss more accurately than sequencing fresh tumor. Current PDTO uses in immunotherapy include co-cultures to generate tumor-reactive cells,13 14 co-cultures to test the efficacy of chimeric antigen receptor T (CAR-T) cells15 16 and drug-screening to evaluate immune checkpoint inhibitors.17 18 We have previously shown that PDTO can be used to identify neoantigen-specific T cells for adoptive cell transfer,12 but there is a paucity of data assessing the correlation between clinical response and autologous organoid recognition by adoptively transferred neoantigen-specific TIL. We also sought to use autologous organoids to determine whether tumor expression of major histocompatibility complex (MHC) class I and class II, especially under interferon gamma (IFN-γ) stimulation, had bearing on the likelihood of response to TIL therapy. Direct recognition of intrinsic antigens presented by MHC class I to CD8 TIL would be predicted to be well evaluated by direct testing with tumor organoids, while extrinsic antigens presented by MHC class II would require organoids to act as antigen presenting cells and express class II. This same paradigm has been seen when screening TIL for neoantigen reactivity using dendritic cells (DCs). Electroporating minigenes encoding neoantigens into DC (leading to cytoplasmic production of antigens) has been superior for detecting CD8 cell reactivity, while exogenously pulsing mutated long peptides has been more sensitive for detecting CD4 TIL reactivity.9 The mechanisms of tumor elimination by MHC class I and class II restricted T cells appear to differ. CD8+T cells directly recognize peptide MHC complexes on their target cells and induce killing by either receptor-mediated apoptosis or a perforin and granzyme exocytosis-dependent process. This paradigm is supported by clinical evidence that class I MHC loss through B2M loss is found in some tumors that escape immunotherapy.19 This biallelic loss is uncommon, but recent data show that for specific CD8+immune attack, the loss of the single allele encoding the relevant class I heavy chain also occurs and is likely more common.20 Murine data from multiple investigators and our own unpublished data show that complete deletion of class II genes from the B16 tumor does not alter its in vivo response to adoptive T-cell transfer using CD4 cells from the anti-TRP1 T-cell receptor (TCR) transgenic mouse.21 We wished to use PDTO to understand the relative roles of CD8 and CD4 cells in tumor rejection and determine the impact of tumor MHC expression. The hope is that evaluating TIL by recognition of autologous PDTO will lead to a better understanding of the T cells participating in cancer rejection and reveal tumor-specific mechanisms that benefit or prevent clinical response.

Methods

Study design

The aim of this study was to explore if there is a correlation between TIL reactivity against autologous patient organoid and patient clinical outcomes after treatment with TIL. Following treatment, samples of patient TIL infusion product were co-cultured with autologous tumor organoid that was grown at the time of metastasectomy for TIL harvest. PDTO and TIL samples were derived from study participants who were registered in ongoing clinical trials to harvest cells (NCT00068003) and undergo treatment with TIL (NCT01174121).

Establishment of patient-derived tumor organoids

PDTO were established according to previously published methods.12 Briefly, fresh specimens obtained from resections of solid tumors were mechanically and enzymatically digested (Miltenyi Biotec). After processing of tumor cells, they were then suspended in 80% Matrigel (Corning) and 20% histology-specific media. Tumor-cell suspensions were plated at the edges of T75 or T175 flasks and placed into a 37° CO2 incubator. Once Matrigel solidified, warmed histology-specific media was added to the flasks, which were placed back into a 37° CO2 incubator. If large enough tumors were harvested, multiple organoids from different areas of the tumor were grown for investigations of tumor heterogeneity.

As organoid cultures grew to confluence or aggregates formed, PDTO lines were passaged, typically every 2 weeks. In brief, to passage lines, they were treated with Dispase II (Gibco) at a final concentration of 1 mg/mL for 2 hours at 37°. Pelleted cells were then treated with 1 mL TrypLE Express (Gibco) for 5 min. After washing with Advanced Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM) (*Gibco), cells were replated in Matrigel and media and placed back in a 37° CO2 incubator.

Generation and selection of TILs for treatment

TILs were cultured as previously described.22 Metastatic lesions resected from patients were divided into multiple 2–3 mm3 fragments under sterile conditions, then separately cultured in T-cell medium supplemented with 6,000 IU/mL recombinant interleukin (IL)-2 to allow lymphocyte outgrowth. After 2–4 weeks of growth allowing for adequate expansion (four confluent wells of 24-well plate; approximately 8E6 cells/fragment), TIL were cryopreserved for future clinical use and samples were tested for patient-specific neoantigen recognition according to previously published protocols.9 In brief, samples from each fragment were cultured for 24 hours with autologous patient antigen-presenting cells that had either been pulsed with pools of 25-mer synthetic peptides (PP) encompassing mutations found on whole exomic sequencing or electroporated with messenger RNA transcribed in vitro from tandem minigenes (TMGs) encoding the same patient-specific mutations. When autologous PDTO were available, they were included as a target in the screening of TIL fragment cultures, along with an allogeneic PDTO control. Fragments for patient treatment were selected based on IFN-γ release on enzyme-linked immunosorbent spot assays (ELISpot) and 4–1BB% upregulation on flow cytometry. All patients undergoing adoptive cell therapy had to have clear recognition of autologous neoantigens by TIL fragment cultures co-cultured with their DCs expressing their mutated TMGs or synthetic long peptides, as previously described.5 The reactive fragments that exhibited strong reactivity to patient-specific neoantigens by IFN-γ and/or 4–1BB were thawed and rapidly expanded with anti-CD3, irradiated feeder cells and IL-2 for patient treatment. Eligible patients were administered a TIL cell infusion after undergoing a non-myeloablative lymphodepleting chemotherapy regimen of high-dose cyclophosphamide and fludarabine, in concert with pembrolizumab and high-dose IL-2.5 Cell dose (table 1) varied due to the constraints of the chemotherapy initiation and the concurrent rapid expansion schedule and ranged from 2.5 to 15 E10 cells.

In vitro co-culture assays using PDTO and TIL

To perform co-culture assays, PDTO were harvested as described above 24 hours prior to assay. They were treated with 10 ng/mL IFN-γ and rested in ultra-low adhesion 24-well plates at 37o. On average, organoids used in co-cultures had been passaged six times, with a range of 2–14; when multiple passage numbers were present, the earliest passage was used. On the day of co-culture, organoids were washed in advanced DMEM three times, then transferred to the assay plate at 4E4 cells per well in 100 µL of T-cell media. Aliquots of cryopreserved TIL infusion products received by patients were thawed 24 hours prior to co-culture and incubated overnight in T-cell media Roswell Park Memorial Institute (RPMI) with 10% human AB serum and antibiotic-antimycotic) containing 300 U/mL IL-2, in 24-well tissue-culture treated plates at 37o. CD8+T cells were selected from infusion products using EasySep Human CD8+T cell Enrichment Kit (STEMCELL Technologies). T cells were added to wells containing organoid at 4E4 cells per well in 100 µL of T-cell media. Allogeneic organoids were used as a negative control, and T cells were treated with phorbol 12-myristate 13-acetate (PMA)/ionomycin as a positive control. Co-culture was performed for 18–20 hours at 37o and 5% CO2. Organoid reactivity of TIL was determined by IFN-γ release by ELISpot and 4–1BB% upregulation by flow cytometry performed on Attune CytPix Analyzer (Thermo Fisher) and analyzed using FlowJo (V.10.10.0, TreeStar). T-cell reactivity was based primarily on % of 4–1BB+cells as compared with negative controls, with reactivity defined as twice background controls and >3% (with allogeneic PDTO subtracted). An example of the gating strategy is shown in online supplemental figure 1. IFN-γ ELISpot assays were used as a qualitative confirmation of the 4–1BB data due to high intra-assay variability in positive spot size and density. PDTO reactivity assays on infusion products were performed at least two times with concordant results in 28 of 30 duplicates. The remaining two patients had their assay repeated a third and a fourth time with agreement in 2 of 3 and 3 of 4 replicates.

Response assessment

Patients’ response to TIL therapy was measured using Response Evaluation Criteria in Solid Tumors (RECIST) V.1.0, with first imaging performed 4–6 weeks after TIL infusion, followed by imaging every 8–12 weeks until disease progression. Patients must exhibit a >30% shrinkage in the sum of longest diameters of measured tumors on two consecutive visits to be considered objective responders. This cohort of patients is a subset of those treated on trial NCT01174121. Results of the ongoing phase 2 trial have been recently reported;5 however, this analysis includes patients with additional histologies besides gastrointestinal cancers.

Flow cytometry antibodies

Following overnight co-culture, T cells were stained with the following antibodies using the manufacturer’s suggested concentration (BD Biosciences): CD8-Alexa Fluor700 (clone RPA-T8), CD3-FITC (clone SK7), CD4-PE (clone SK3), CD137-APC (clone 4B4-1). To evaluate MHC expression, organoids (with or without IFN-γ pretreatment for 48 hours) were stained with anti-human HLA-A, B, C–FITC (clone W6/32, BioLegend) and anti-human HLA-DR-PE (clone LN3, BioLegend).

Statistical analysis

All statistical analysis was done using GraphPad Prism V.10.1.1 for Windows (GraphPad Software). Groups were compared using Fisher’s exact test for categorical variables and Mann-Whitney test for non-parametric continuous variables. P2 value <0.05 was considered significant.

Results

Patient characteristics

A total of 30 patients with metastatic epithelial cancer treated with TIL at our institution between 2022–2025, who also had available autologous organoid, were tested. Median age was 46 years and 50% of patients were female (table 1). The majority of patients had metastatic colorectal adenocarcinoma (67%), while 4 (13%) had pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma, and 2 (7%) had ductal carcinoma of the breast. The study group also included patients with cholangiocarcinoma, ovarian adenocarcinoma, hepatocellular carcinoma, and esophageal adenocarcinoma. The most common site of TIL and organoid procurement was the lung (67%) (table 1). Other sites included liver (20%) and omentum or adjacent abdominal wall (17%); one patient (patient 4550) received TIL derived from both liver and lung.

Organoid recognition by MHC class I and MHC class II restricted T cells

Using previously described methods, TIL were established from up to 24 separate tumor fragments for each patient and expanded independently. 27 of 30 PDTO were available for use by the time TIL cultures were expanded and frozen (prior to final clinical expansion—typically 2 months) while three additional PDTO grew later. The success of establishing PDTO (defined by sufficient cells to passage and perform co-culture screening (1E6) within 60 days) varied by tumor histology—colorectal cancer 90%, pancreatic cancer 52%, breast cancer 49% and all others 66%. TIL fragment cultures were screened for neoantigen reactivity with autologous DCs presenting mutations defined by WES using either electroporated in vitro transcribed RNA encoding mutations in TMGs or using pools of synthetic 25-mer peptides encompassing the mutations.1 9 TIL subcultures showing mutation recognition were then selected to undergo a rapid expansion protocol (REP) prior to patient lymphodepletion and cell infusion. Presenting mutated epitopes by electroporation of DCs using in vitro transcribed minigenes favors presentation in the MHC class I pathway due to cytoplasmic translation of the minigenes. In contrast, incubation of dendritic cells with 25-mer synthetic peptides favors presentation of neoepitopes via the extrinsic pathway on MHC class II. Direct in vitro recognition of tumor organoids by class II restricted T cells would require MHC class II expression by those PDTO. Therefore, we wished to determine if class II restricted T cells could recognize PDTO in vitro and have any significant association with clinical response.

Direct labeling of PDTO from epithelial cancers with pan class I-specific antibody showed variable degrees of staining (figure 1A). Direct labeling with pan-MHC class II DR antibody showed variations in staining, but all expressed lower levels compared with two positive control organoids transduced with class II transactivator (CIITA) (figure 1B). Pre-incubation of PDTO in high levels of IFN-γ for 48 hours induced variable degrees of MHC class I upregulation in most samples (figure 1A) and had little effect on class II DR expression (figure 1B). There did not appear to be any association between clinical response and baseline expression of MHC class I or II on PDTO nor their upregulation by pre-incubation in IFN-γ (figure 1A,B). To investigate the functional significance of MHC class II expression by PDTO, we studied five samples from patients with HLA-DR alleles that matched the restriction of reference TCRs with defined tumor neoantigens from other patients with cancer. These five PDTO were then pulsed with the mutated peptides for these reference TCRs, either with or without IFN-γ pretreatment, and co-incubated with peripheral blood lymphocytes (PBL) expressing the relevant reference TCR. In two cases, a CIITA transduced version of the PDTO was available as a positive control and four patients had autologous B cells. No PDTO stimulated the reference TCRs and only one of five induced reactivity after IFN-γ pretreatment (figure 2). Both positive controls with CIITA transduced PDTO did present the reference neoantigen successfully, as did all four autologous B cells. These results imply that MHC-class II expression by most PDTO is not functionally significant and IFN-γ stimulation had only occasional benefit. In general, during initial screening of TIL subcultures, CD4 dominant TIL cultures that recognized DCs exogenously pulsed with mutated 25-mer synthetic peptides (typical of MHC class II restricted activity) did not recognize PDTO. In contrast, those TIL cultures that did recognize PDTO were mostly CD8 predominant and were reactive with DCs electroporated with minigene RNA encoding the mutations (typically a class I restricted phenomenon). Organoid reactivity in TIL fragment cultures selected for administration prior to final rapid expansion

All patients receiving TIL in this protocol are selected for treatment because they had TIL fragments which recognize mutated epitopes by TMG or synthetic peptide display on their DCs. Patients reported here also had autologous PDTO available to include in that screening assay and in approximately half of patients those results differed from that of TMG and peptide testing (ie, reactivity was not seen). Results of this screening of TIL fragment cultures versus PDTO prior to our clinical REP are shown in online supplemental table 1 and table 2. 4–1BB was used as the primary measure of reactivity as IFN-γ ELISpot showed high heterogeneity in spot size and density making quantitative comparisons difficult. ELISpot was used primarily as a qualitative confirmation of results from fluorescence-activated cell sorting (FACS) measurements of 4–1BB. Of the 27 patients who had autologous organoid available at the time of pre-REP TIL screening, 18 had at least one TIL fragment culture reactive to autologous organoid by 4–1BB upregulation on flow cytometric analysis. 7 of these 18 patients (39%) with pre-REP organoid reactivity went on to have a partial response after adoptive cell therapy with TIL while none of the 9 patients without organoid reactivity had an objective clinical response (p=0.059, negative predictive value=100%). Three patients (4462, 4493, 4526) had slower growing organoids that were not available at the time of TIL screening (but became available for later testing). Because TIL can change as a consequence of the rapid expansion protocol, we tested the final infusion products against their PDTO as well.

Organoid recognition by the TIL infusion product is associated with a higher clinical response rate

Infusion products contained highly variable percentages of CD8 and CD4 cells (table 3). To minimize this variable and investigate the class I restricted reactivity against PDTO, we enriched for CD8+cells from the infusion to test against PDTO. We co-cultured an aliquot of the final TIL infusion product CD8+cells with PDTO for 30 patients and positive recognition was defined as 4–1BB% more than double background and greater than 3% (table 3). Of 15 patients whose infusion product showed organoid recognition, 8 achieved objective responses (53%; 95% CI 26% to 74%) while only one patient without organoid recognition responded (7%; p=0.014, figure 4A). For 10 patients with multiple autologous organoids available, separate co-cultures were performed of TIL with each organoid. In these cases, eight showed concordant reactivity results across all organoids while two had organoid recognition against one of two and one of three of their PDTO. The percentages of 4–1BB upregulation of CD8+T cells for those 10 patients were averaged to maintain equal weighting for each patient in the analyses against clinical response. The median 4–1BB expression (minus allogenic control values) after co-culture from responders was 10.9% and 1.4% for non-responders (p=0.0377, figure 4B). An exploratory receiver operating characteristic curve analysis of this reactivity value identified 3.2% 4–1BB reactivity as the optimal value separating responders from non-responders in this data set, with a sensitivity of 0.89 and specificity of 0.67. Infusion product 4–1BB reactivity to the allogeneic organoid control was generally low (median 1.36%; IQR 0.62–1.79%) with one outlier at 26.3%. An example of a patient with organoid reactive TIL who experienced a clinical response is shown in figure 5. The patient was diagnosed with metastatic colon adenocarcinoma 3 years prior to TIL therapy and had metastases to lung and liver. A metastatic lung lesion was harvested for TIL and organoid, and the patient subsequently underwent TIL therapy, which caused a 90% decrease in measurable disease (figure 5A). The patient had four separate autologous organoids grown; TIL infusion product displayed reactivity to all organoids by both IFN-γ release and 4–1BB upregulation of CD8+T cells (figure 5B,C). An example of a non-responding patient with no organoid reactivity is shown in figure 6. The patient was diagnosed with metastatic colon adenocarcinoma 3.5 years prior to TIL therapy and had metastases to lung and liver. TIL and organoid were grown from a metastatic lung lesion. At the patient’s 2-month follow-up after treatment, progressive disease was noted in the lung and liver (figure 6A). Evaluation of the patient’s TIL infusion product showed no reactivity to either of the patient’s two autologous organoids despite showing strong reactivity to a private neoantigen (mutated AFAP1L2) encoded by a TMG when presented by autologous DCs (figure 6B–D).

Discussion

Organoids have become a valuable tool in preclinical assessment of cancer therapies. While many studies have established the use of organoids to predict clinical response to chemotherapy23,25 and investigated their use in immunotherapy,26 27 the applicability of organoids to assess cell-based treatments remains unclear. A recent study by Logun et al28 provided evidence that organoids are a reliable model to mirror patients with glioblastoma clinical courses when treated with CAR-T cell therapy. This phase 1 trial showed a reduction in expression of target antigens when co-culturing organoid with autologous patient CAR-T cells, as well as a similar cytokine profile between co-cultures and patient cerebrospinal fluid samples post-treatment. These findings are promising, but unlike this study, there was no correlation between objective clinical response and organoid co-culture data. We believe this work represents the first study to report such a correlation for adoptive T-cell therapy.

One persistent weakness of organoid immunoassays was the lack of consistent and relevant negative controls. We used allogeneic PDTO in this role but never had HLA-matched PDTO to test. Even having an autologous normal tissue-derived PDTO is usually not perfect as a colon cancer metastasis harvested from the lung only provides a normal lung PDTO, not one from normal colonic epithelium. Nevertheless, we seldom encountered significant background reactivity and were still able to produce useful information.

We found that direct in vitro recognition of PDTO in co-culture is driven by class I restricted reactivity as PDTO from epithelial cancers express low levels of MHC class II, which are seldom upregulated after IFN-γ stimulation and are usually inadequate to present a reference class II peptide epitope. The expression of MHC class I and class II by PDTO did not associate with clinical responses in any obvious manner nor was there predictive value in the response to IFN-γ in vitro. The finding that class II restricted reactivity is largely not detected in PDTO co-culture combined with the finding that epithelial cancers contain more CD4+reactive TIL versus CD8+might predict that co-culture assays would not be informative when it comes to clinical response. Yet this study indicates that they are and implies that there is a significant and perhaps dominant class I restricted contribution to clinical response to TIL. This finding appears to differ from a recent publication by Lowery et al from our group, which included eleven of the 30 patients reported in this work.5 That manuscript found that the number of mutation-reactive CD4+cells in gastrointestinal TIL infusion products correlated with a higher chance of clinical response while the number of reactive CD8+cells did not. Reactivity in that work was defined by recognition of neoepitopes presented by normal autologous DCs while here, it was by reactivity with autologous organoid, suggesting that the latter may better detect tumor-related mechanisms of class I immune evasion. In addition, that work was confined to GI TIL and looked at the total number of reactive cells infused while this study used a binary qualitative measure to report organoid reactivity. Efforts to incorporate PDTO for the detection of class II restricted anti-tumor reactivity are being pursued with the use of PDTO lysates presented by autologous antigen presenting cells and this may clarify the importance of CD4 cells. This type of investigation may improve our ability to select TIL for administration and better predict TIL responses using in vitro PDTO assays.

Organoid reactivity in pre-REP fragment cultures was of borderline statistical significance when correlated with clinical outcomes. One explanation for this is that there are phenotypic and clonotypic changes during the rapid expansion phase of TIL growth. Chronic stimulation leads to dysfunctional and exhausted T cells, as evidenced by previous findings that terminally differentiated T cells can lose their neoantigen recognition ability.29,31 Additionally, rapid expansion with IL-2 and OKT3 provides non-specific activation, which can cause proliferation of bystander T cells, thus degrading the T-cell repertoire of the infusion product. For example, one study found that the relative frequencies of neoantigen reactive breast cancer TIL decreased in the final infusion product after rapid expansion, with many reactivities showing a greater than 10-fold reduction.32 Therefore, organoid reactivity of pre-REP fragment cultures may not be as reliable a predictor of TIL clinical efficacy as the actual infusion product.

The major conclusion of this study was that there is a significant association between in vitro PDTO recognition by a TIL infusion product and the clinical response to those TIL. Nevertheless, there were examples of patients with PDTO recognition who did not respond. It is not always possible to produce TIL and PDTO from the exact same tumor fragment, so intratumoral heterogeneity may account for some of this discrepancy. The single site from which a PDTO is established (or TIL procured) may also not represent other tumors within the patient. Finally, there are also multiple T-cell associated factors, such as state of differentiation and checkpoint inhibition that may prevent reactive TIL from achieving tumor regression. One patient had a clinical response without detectable PDTO reactivity. Again, tumor heterogeneity may be one explanation, but the fact that PDTO do not detect MHC class II restricted TIL activity is another possible explanation. Several clinical studies support the fact that class II restricted T cells can be sufficient to induce tumor regression.1 33 34 A better understanding of how they accomplish this is needed to interpret this observation.

Another area of future investigation with PDTO is the study of discrepancies between the recognition of neoantigens presented by normal antigen presenting cells and their recognition in PDTO. These discrepancies occur in both directions with some PDTO not being recognized despite documented expression of the antigen and presenting MHC (eg, figure 6) and some PDTO stimulating T-cell recognition despite a completely negative TMG screen. The former situation can assist in the discovery of new tumor-related escape mechanisms and evaluate off-the-shelf T-cell receptors on an individual patient basis while the latter may expand our understanding of tumor rejection antigens beyond known paradigms. Both of these areas are being actively pursued using autologous PDTO.

Increases in the availability of genetic information on a patient’s tumor and better methods for dissecting human antitumor immune responses on a molecular level have revealed the intensely personalized nature of the tumor-T-cell interaction in each patient. The advantage of having the autologous cancer as an immune reagent is compelling. Yet, as immunotherapy moves towards treating the common epithelial cancers that cause the vast majority of cancer deaths, autologous tumor lines have been harder to obtain. This study looked at the potential use of PDTO to provide this reagent and test the value of the reagent when administering a selected T-cell adoptive therapy. The finding that PDTO recognition was associated with clinical response was encouraging. Further studies with PDTO have the potential to improve their predictive value, improve the TIL we administer and reveal new insights in our understanding of T-cell mediated tumor rejection.
