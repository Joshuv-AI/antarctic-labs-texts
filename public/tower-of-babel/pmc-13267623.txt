Potential of ultrasonication to produce low-salt surimi from blue mussel (Mytilus edulis) meat
Authors: M Moghadam, TR Heyn, K Schwarz, JK Keppler
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBlue mussel (Mytilus edulis) is a promising sustainable alternative protein source due to its high protein content and minimal environmental impact. The application of mussel myofibrillar proteins (MPs) in surimi products is limited due to poor solubility under low-salt conditions. This study investigated the potential of using high-intensity ultrasonication (HIUS) to improve the properties of MP for low-salt surimi from mussel meat. To address this, a factorial experimental design was used to evaluate the combinatory effects of NaCl concentration (1, 2 and 3%), sonication amplitude (20, 55 and 90%), and sonication duration (5, 32.5 and 60 min) on the properties of mussel surimi. The findings indicated that increasing both, sonication amplitude and duration, improved protein solubility remarkably by up to 104% and reduced the mean particle size by up to 73%. Results of SDS-PAGE and SEC-HPLC analyses showed that HIUS cleaved large protein aggregates to protein subunits and smaller peptides. ATR-FTIR demonstrated structural modifications, with an increase in intermolecular beta-sheets and a reduction in intramolecular beta-sheets and random coils. In addition, increased water holding capacity (WHC) by up to 124% was observed at the highest sonication intensity and longest time period, although excessive sonication at high salt level (3%) negatively affected gel properties. Additionally, lower gel viscosity and enhanced gel whiteness were observed after more intense HIUS treatment. Significant interactions were found between salt concentration, sonication amplitude, and time, affecting the structural and functional properties of mussel surimi. Overall, this study systematically demonstrated how ultrasound processing and salt concentration synergistically improve the quality of mussel surimi, offering a novel low-salt formulation approach with potential applications in the seafood and functional food industries.Keywords: Sonication, Seafood processing, Gelation, Low-salt surimi, Non-thermal processing technique

Introduction

Mytilus edulis is a marine shellfish widely grown in European waters [1] and favored by customers worldwide having 43 to 62% high-quality proteins on ash-free dry basis of freeze-dried material [[2], [3]] . Proteins in mussel meat are comprised of 17% salt-soluble myofibrillar proteins (MPs), 48% water-soluble sarcoplasmic proteins, and 11% w/w acid-soluble matrix proteins [[4], [5]]. Mussel meat reportedly contains various bioactive compounds with high nutritional value and above 90% of digestibility [4], making it a potential alternative protein source for meat, poultry, and fish [6]. Other benefits of mussels consist of low greenhouse gas emission, non-polluting, preventing ocean eutrophication, and having no negative impact on the environment [6]. Due to these benefits, there is an increasing interest in the consumption of farmed mussels [6]. According to the European Market Observatory for Fisheries and Aquaculture Products (EUMOFA), the global mussel production amounted to 2.2 million tonnes in 2020, primarily from aquaculture. The usage of mussel proteins, however, is restricted as 27% of the produced mussels are categorized as by-products because the mussels are too small or damaged. In addition, poor functional properties of mussel MPs under the low ionic strength aqueous conditions limits their application in food formulations and beverages [6]. Mussel meat may be used for surimi-like products [7]. Surimi is one of the most important seafood products, predominantly constituted of fish meat. The major constituents in surimi are MPs that are obtained through the purification steps comprising washing minced meat with cold water and drainage/dewatering of washing water from meat [[8], [9]]. There are many studies focusing on the production of surimi from different marine sources such as silver carp [[8], [10], [11]], Alaska pollock surimi [[7], [12]], tilapia surimi [13], frozen hairtail surimi [9], and shrimp surimi [[14], [15]]. Thus far, there is only few information regarding production of surimi from freshwater mussel meat [16], and therefore, in this study, blue mussel (Mytilus edulis) was selected as the raw material to produce surimi.

Surimi normally relies on 2–3% salt to solubilize myofibrillar proteins (mainly myosin) and form strong elastic gel [7]. Reducing salt improves healthfulness but directly harms gel strength [17], water holding [18], texture [17], flavor [17] and shelf life [19], so low-salt surimi requires technological and formulation innovations. Alternatives for using high salt in surimi products are non-thermal processing techniques such as ultrasound [20], high pressure homogenization [21] and microwave treatments [[22], [23]]. High-intensity ultrasonication (HIUS) (with the intensity range of 10–1000 W/cm2 and frequency of 20–100 kHz) is a safe, eco-friendly and economic technique that attracted the attention of many researchers recently [24]. Ultrasonication generates strong physical forces by cavitation [24], transient cavitation bubbles are formed and collapsing of the formed bubbles causes accumulation of energy in hot spots and formation of high-shear energy waves and turbulence. Besides, water molecules produce a huge number of highly active free radicals during the sonication process that can easily react with active groups, other molecules, and the sulfhydryl group of myosin in MPs [25]. Several studies indicated that HIUS can alter the structure of proteins and improve their functional properties. Yu et al. [26] have reported that heat-induced gelation of ultrasonicated whey proteins showed enhanced water-holding capacity and strong gels compared with non-treated sample. Amiri et al. [27] reported that a reduction in particle size of bovine MPs and improvement of functional and rheological properties after treatment with HIUS applying the power of 100 and 300 W for 30 min. According to Wang et al. [28], applying ultrasound treatment for chicken MPs caused alterations in rheological properties of proteins. Tang and Yongsawatdigul [29], reported that HIUS treatment induced the physicochemical changes of actomyosin in fish. As expressed by Zhang et al. [14], the gelling properties of shrimp surimi improved significantly after the treatment by HIUS at 360 W for 20 min. In addition, it has been reported that HIUS can decrease alpha-helix structure and increase beta-sheet regions in the secondary structure of proteins and hence improve the gel strength of surimi [30]. Furthermore, ultrasonication technique can activate the endogenous transaminases and proteases in surimi and improve the cross-linking of proteins resulting in better gel strength [22]. Thus far, however, there is no systematic investigation on the interaction of various factors such as sonication amplitude (%), sonication duration (min), and salt concentration (%) for surimi production from mussel meat and the interactive effect of mentioned factors is missing. We hypothesize that these factors act synergistically on the MP, and understanding these correlations will allow minimal processing for maximizing the effect on obtaining salt reduced mussel-based surimi products. A two level factorial design was used based on preliminary tests and process parameters reported in the literature [[20], [25], [31]] for similar experimental setups. This design systematically evaluated the combination of sonication amplitude (20, 55 and 90%), duration (5, 32.5 and 60 min) and NaCl concentration (1, 2 and 3%) and the effects of their interaction before and after heat treatment (60° C for 40 min). We compared functional, physio-chemical properties and structural characteristics to obtain deeper insights into the underlying mechanism influencing the gel-based surimi system.

Materials and methods

Materials

Fresh mussels (Mytilus edulis) were grown for two years in longline mussel farms with the depth between 1 and 4 m and harvested in 2023 by Kiel Marine Farm (Kiel, Germany). The mussels were frozen at −20 °C within ∼4 h of being harvested to stop their bioactivity. The standard protein mix for size exclusion chromatography and Folin-Ciocalteu were purchased from Merck KGaA (Darmstadt, Germany). Glycerol, and β-mercaptoethanol were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich Chemie GmbH (Taufkirchen, Germany). Hydrochloric acid 37% v/v (HCl) and isopropanol were purchased from VWR International GmbH (Darmstadt, Germany). The other chemicals were from Carl Roth GmbH + Co. KG (Karlsruhe, Germany) with analytical grades.

Mussel surimi powder preparation

Frozen mussels were thawed gently in fridge at + 4 °C for an overnight and were cleaned initially with tap water to remove sands, barnacles and other impurities attached to mussel shells. The mussels consisted of 20–30% mussel meat and 70–80% shell. Byssal threads and extracellular fluid was not taken in account. The mussel meat was separated manually from mussel shell and mixed with a hand mixer (Bosch, CleverMixx, 600 W, Slovenia) for 2 min, as described by Moghadam et al. [3]. The mussel meat was mixed/washed with cold distilled water (4 °C) at the ratio of 1:5 w/v (mussel meat to distilled water) and stirred for 5 min on magnetic stirrer. During the stirring step the temperature of mixture was kept below 10 °C. The dewatering step was performed using a cheesecloth for squeezing washed mussel meat. After that, the obtained samples were freeze-dried for further use. The freeze-drying step was conducted to improve the handling properties of surimi. The resulted powders were stored at – 20 °C for further analysis. In addition, the washing water was collected to recover water-soluble sarcoplasmic fractions as a side stream in surimi production for further investigations in the future. The protein content of surimi powder was 85% measured by AGROLAB LUFA GmbH (Kiel, Germany), using Kjeldahl method (§64 LFGB L 17.00–15:2013–08) with the conversion factor of 6.25 for nitrogen according to Stangierski et al. [16]. In addition, the Lowry method was used for small sample sizes and showed a protein concentration of 67% for surimi samples. The low solubility of MPs in surimi might be the reason for indicating less protein content in Lowry method while in Kjeldahl method the solubility is not important as reported previously [3].

Mussel surimi treatment

To produce mussel surimi gels, samples with a concentration of 50 mg protein/mL dest. water were heated at the pH value of 6.5 in a water bath at 60 °C for 40 min and then cooled down in the mixture of ice and water for 15 min. The prepared surimi gels were kept in the fridge overnight at 4 °C. Sonication treatment was conducted prior to the surimi production process. Therefore, the effect of three different factors including salt concentration (Factor A), ultrasonication amplitude (Factor B), and sonication duration (Factor C) on gelling properties, protein structure and its profile, and solubility of surimi powder dispersions were investigated. Parameters and various factor combinations can be seen in Table 1A, Table 1B, respectively. 1, 2, and 3% NaCl (based on the total weight of surimi) were considered to produce low-, medium- and high- salt mussel surimi. Ultrasonication was performed at 20, 55, and 90% amplitude and the samples were treated for different time intervals (5, 32.5, and 60 min) using ultrasound device (BANDELIN SONOPULS GM 2070, 70 W, 20 kHz, Germany). The factor combination of 2% NaCl, 55% amplitude of sonication for 32.5 min was considered as center point in this experiment. The total energy uptake per volume (Ev in J/mL) of sonication treatment was calculated according to Heyn et al. [32] from the increase in temperature during sonication of pure water and the specific thermal capacity of water (4185.1 J/kg). This calculation is shown in Eq. (1), where Is is the sonication amplitude in % and ts is the sonication time in s.

The calculated Ev ranges from 169 J/mL for amplitude 20% (5 min) to 6512 J/mL for amplitude 90% (60 min). The energy uptake for the center point was 1952 J/mL. The ultrasonic titanium probe MS 73 (3 mm diameter) was immersed in the surimi sample (7 mL) with the depth of 1.5 cm to the end of 15 mL tube. During the sonication treatment, the sample batch was kept in ice-water bath to prevent high temperature and oxidation causing by ultrasonication. In addition, a break for 5 min was considered every 15 min for the samples with longer than 5 min ultrasonication to cool down samples. Solubility of ultrasonicated samples

Changes in the solubility of treated surimi samples was investigated according to the method described in previous research [3], with slight modifications. First, surimi powder was dissolved in distilled water (with the protein concentration of 5 mg/mL) containing 1% NaCl (as having a higher NaCl concentration like 2 or 3% may cause higher solubility for MPs and making them incomparable with 1% NaCl surimi) and stirred at 700 rpm for 1 h at room temperature using a magnetic stirrer. Second, the ultrasonication treatment was performed at 20, 50 and 90% amplitude for different time intervals (5, 32.5 and 60 min). Afterwards, the samples were diluted with 1% NaCl solution to the concentration of 1 mg/mL (which corresponds to the specification of the UV/Vis spectroscope used for protein concentration measurement), and stirred for further 30 min at 700 rpm. Particle size

Static light scattering (SLS) measurement was conducted according to the method of Liu et al. [33] to analyze the effect of Factor A, B and C on the droplet size of mussel surimi particles using a particle size analyzer (LA-950 V2 HORIBA, Retsch Technology GmbH, Haan, Germany). 500 µL of treated surimi samples with the protein concentration of 50 mg/mL were added to the water batch. The rotation and the circulation speed of device were both set on 7. To characterize the droplet size, the refractive indices for oil and water was considered 1.45 and 1.33, respectively.

Sodium dodecyl sulfate–polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE)

SDS-PAGE was used to compare the protein profile of unwashed mussel meat (MM) and washed mussel meat (MS = mussel surimi) as well as surimi samples (MS1-20-5, MS1-20-60, MS1-55-32.5, MS1-90-5, and MS1-90-60). For this, a 10% separating gel and 4% stacking gel under the reducing condition was prepared according to [3]. The sample buffer was prepared by mixing 7.1 mL MilliQ water, 2.5 mL 0.5 M Tris-HCl at pH 6.8, 5 mL glycerol, 4 mL 10% (w/v) SDS, and 0.4 mL 0.5% (w/v) bromophenol blue. Then, the sample buffer stored at –20 °C. To complete the sample buffer preparation under the reducing condition, 950 µL of sample buffer was mixed with 50 µL β-mercaptoethanol and vortexed for 10 s. Next, mussel surimi samples were prepared with the protein concentration of 5 mg/mL (including 1% NaCl), treated with different sonication amplitudes and durations. Then, treated samples were mixed with the sample buffer with the ratio of 1:1 v/v and heated for 5 min at 95 °C in a Thermo shaker (Thermal shake life, VWR, Darmstadt, Germany). The salt concentration (Factor C) was kept constant on 1% NaCl as considering different salt levels may cause smiling effect in gel pattern and the samples cannot be compared properly. Hence, only the effect of Factor A and B was considered here. 5 µL of commercial standard protein mix (Precision Plus Protein All Blue Prestained Protein Standard, Bio-Rad) with the molecular weight of 10–250 kDa was loaded as marker and 20 µL of US-treated surimi samples were loaded into each cell. The voltage was adjusted first to 40 V for 10 min and after that increased to 100 V for approximately 1.5 h utilizing Mini-PROTEAN tetra cell (Bio-Rad, Hercules, USA). The gels were immersed in the staining solution containing 2 g Coomassie Brilliant Blue R-250, 50 mg Coomassie Brilliant Blue G-250, 100 mL EtOH, 400 mL MeOH, 20 mL glycerol, and 480 mL MilliQ water for 4 h and then the staining solution was replaced by destaining solution (100 mL acetic acid, 50 mL EtOH and 850 mL MilliQ water) as much as needed to eliminate the extra blue color and enhance the visibility of the protein bands. The quantification of protein bands was performed using Image Lab 6.1.

Size exclusion chromatography (SEC-HPLC)

SEC-HPLC was used to analyze the effect of Factor A and Factor B on size of various protein fractions in mussel surimi. For this, an Agilent 1100 HPLC equipped with a diode array detector set to 205 nm, 214 nm, 280 nm, and 380 nm wavelength was employed according to Heyn et al. [34]. Mussel surimi samples were prepared with the protein concentration of 5 mg/mL and NaCl concentration of 1% and then treated with sonication for comparison to the results of SDS-PAGE. Then, samples were filtered by syringe filters (CHROMAFIL® Xtra PET-20/13) with a pore size of 0.2 µm before injection to the column. The measurement was conducted at + 25 °C using two in-line SEC columns called VivoSep SEC 250 XL column (with the separation range of 0.1–––100 kDa) as well as VivoSep SEC Multipore column (with the separation range of 1 to 1000 kDa). The injection volume was set to 50 µL with the flow rate of 0.3 mL/min using 10 mM phosphate buffer containing 0.14 M NaCl (pH 7.4) as eluent. The Advance Bio SEC 300Å Protein Standard (Agilent Technologies Deutschland GmbH, Germany) was utilized for molecule-size-calibration.

Attenuated-total-reflection Fourier-transform infrared spectroscopy (ATR-FTIR)

To investigate the effect of sonication treatment on the secondary structure of proteins (proportions of alpha-helix, beta-sheet, beta-turn, and random coils in amide I area) in surimi gels, ATR-FTIR spectroscopy was utilized. The analysis was performed before and after heating of mussel surimi samples using a Tensor 2 (Bruker Optik GmbH, Ettlingen, Germany) FTIR-system, fitted with a nitrogen cooled MCT detector and a thermally controlled BioATR Cell 2 at room temperature. 30 µL of sonicated samples with a protein concentration of 50 mg/mL were placed on the ATR crystal. The spectra were recorded and averaged over 120 scans at a resolution of 0.7 cm−1 at room temperature. Distilled water was used as the blank sample. After atmospheric correction for absorbance of CO2 and H2O, the obtained spectra were normalized from the wavenumber of 4000 to 800 cm−1 and after Fourier self-deconvolution, the data were integrated in order to calculate the quantity of various proportions of protein secondary structures using OPUS 7.5 (Bruker Optics GmbH, Ettlingen, Germany).

The WHC of surimi samples (differently treated) was measured according to the method of Sun et al., [25] with slight amendments. For this the surimi gels were centrifuged at 4700 rpm for 10 min at 20 °C and the supernatants were discarded as unbonded water. Texture profile analysis (TPA) of surimi gels

The strength of mussel surimi gels was evaluated by compression test using a texture analyzer (AMETEK Brookfield CTX with 1000G LOAD CELL, Hadamar-Steinbach, Germany) equipped with a cylinder plunger (82.94 mm length) according to Zhang et al. [14] with slight amendments. All of the samples were equilibrated at room temperature for 1 h before the measurement. 6 ml of the samples were held in 15 ml Falcon tubes during the TPA. Breaking force (g) and deformation at hardness (mm) were measured with the test speed of 2 mm/s, trigger load of 0.50 g, and distance of 15 mm were applied as the testing conditions. Measurements were conducted in triplicate.

Viscosity measurement

The effect of ultrasonication and salt concentration on the viscosity of heated surimi gels (with the concentration of 50 mg/mL heated at 60 °C for 40 min) was investigated using IKA ROTAVISC HI-VI I S000 (Fa. IKA®-Werke, Staufen, Germany) viscometer equipped with single VOL-SP-6.7 spindle. The measurement was carried out at 19 ± 2 °C with the rotation speed of 2 rpm for spindle with at least three replicates.

Statistical analysis

The results were analyzed using Design Expert 13.0.15 (Stat-Ease Inc., Minneapolis, USA). A two-level factorial design, with three center points and three replicates per parameter combination was conducted via 27 randomized experiments considering the factors NaCl concentration (A), sonication amplitude (B) and sonication time (C) as mentioned in Table 1A, Table 1B. Analysis of the responses were conducted by an ANOVA for selected factorials models Relevant terms with p-values <0.05 were used to calculated the coefficients. For all models a curvature test was conducted. The significant difference between samples, where the salt concentration was kept constant on 1% NaCl was assessed by one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) and the significant differences between samples were investigated with the Duncan test (post hoc test) at a significance level of 0.05 using SPSS statistics 16 (IBM, Armonk, USA). All the values are expressed as the mean ± SD with at least three independent replicates for each sample. The graphs were designed using GraphPad PRISM 10.2.0 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, USA).

Influence of ultrasound treatment on solubility of mussel surimi

Results of HIUS-treated surimi samples with 1% NaCl are shown in Fig. 2. The maximum solubility (42%) was reached at 90% amplitude for 60 min (as the maximum factors for both HIUS intensity and time). Solubility of samples treated with 20% amplitude for 60 min and 55% amplitude for 32.5 min were statistically at the same level of significance with solubility of ∼26%. In addition, a low solubility of ∼22% and ∼21% was observed for samples treated with 90% and 20% amplitude both for 5 min, respectively. According to these results, sonication intensity and treatment time both affected the solubility of proteins representing higher solubility at max intensity and duration.

Effect of sonication amplitude (%) and duration (min) on solubility of low-salt surimi samples.

Effects of salt concentration and ultrasound treatment on particle size distribution

According to Table 2, factor A (salt concentration) did not affect the mean particle size of surimi while factor B (sonication amplitude) and C (sonication time) negatively affected the mean particle size meaning the higher the sonication intensity and time, the lower the mean particle size. For example, increasing the sonication amplitude by 35% decreased the mean particle size by 31 µm and the median by 207 µm, and increasing the sonication time by 27.5 min decreased the mean particle size by 16 µm. Regarding the effect of factor combinations, no marked effect was found on mean particle size. Salt concentration positively affected the D10 particle size about 1.88 µm while factor B, C, AB, AC, BC had a negative effect of 2.68, 4.14, 0.87, 0.95, and 0.86 µm on D10, respectively. Increase in salt concentration about 1% led to an increase of the smallest 10% (D10) of the analyzed particles by 1.88 µm and a decrease of the largest 10% (D90) of the particles by 63 µm, which created a more homogeneous particle size distribution in the disperse system. The factor combination of salt concentration and sonication amplitude had a negative impact on the D90 value. The negative correlation of WHC and D10 revealed that by increasing D10, the WHC will decrease (Fig. 8). According to Fig. 8, there is a negative correlation between the particle size (especially the D10) and the properties of the surimi gels (e.g., WHC, breaking force and whiteness).

Influence of ultrasound treatment on protein association, dissociation and hydrolysis (SDS-PAGE)

The gel electrophoresis pattern and the quantification of bands from sonicated surimi samples with 1% NaCl content are shown in Fig. 3 A and B, respectively. The relative proportions (%) of each band can be found in Table 3. According to Fig. 3 A, all surimi samples displayed a typical protein pattern comprising myosin heavy chain (MHC = 315 kDa), paramyosin (220 kDa), alpha-actinin (99 kDa), actin (42 kDa), tropomyosin (34 kDa), and myosin light chain (MLC = ∼ 10 kDa), which was reported previously by Naik and Hayes. [5]. Alpha-actinin and actin were major bands in the gel pattern for all mussel surimi samples. By increasing the duration of sonication from 5 to 60 min at both 20% and 90% amplitude, a decrease in paramyosin, alpha-actinin, actin, and tropomyosin was observed, indicating that having a longer duration of sonication may break down proteins into lower molecular weight peptides. Interestingly, in MS1-90-60, some bands between 100–150 kDa and 25–37 kDa are nearly to be vanished and may be transferred to proteins with lower molecular weight due to high intensity of sonication (90%) for long time (60 min). Quantification of bands which is a mean value of 3 individual gel replicates in Fig. 3 B, showed no significant impact of sonication at all various sonication factors for MHC (315 kDa), in the area between 200–300 kDa (except for MS1-20-5), and between 100–200 kDa. For the area of 37–100 kDa, the maximum and minimum portion (in %) was found in MS1-90–60 and MS1-20-5, respectively, and other samples were in the same range. It can be concluded that using a high amplitude of sonication for 60 min, cleaved the proteins resulting MW of above 100 kDa to lower MW between 37–100 kDa. This trend was also observed for proteins with < 15 kDa.

Influence of ultrasound treatment on surimi protein size (SEC-HPLC)

The protein profile of surimi particles under sonication treatment at different sonication amplitude (%) and duration (min) are shown in Fig. 4 (with the constant NaCl concentration of 1%). The area 1 is related to the proteins with high molecular weights like MHC (315 kDa), paramyosin (220 kDa), alpha-actinin (99 kDa), actin (42 kDa), as well as tropomyosin (34 kDa), observed in the retention time from 25 to 35 min. In this area, the lowest intensity is related to samples sonicated at high amplitude (MS1-90–5 and MS1-90–60) while the surimi samples treated at low amplitudes of 20% and 55% had higher intensity in this area. Influence of salt concentration and sonication on protein secondary structure before and after heat-gelation

The alteration in protein secondary structure of mussel surimi before and after heat-gelation was compared. According to Table 2 before heat-gelation increase in salt concentration (A), sonication amplitude (B) and time (C) by one level, enhanced the intermolecular beta-sheet structures in washed mussel meat by approximately 3.3%, 1.9% and 2.9%, respectively. In addition, all factor combinations (AB, AC, BC, and ABC) also had a positive impact on the intermolecular beta-sheet structure. On the other hand, the amount of intramolecular beta-sheet structure was affected negatively by salt concentration (A), sonication amplitude (B) and time (C), as well as factor combination AC by about 2%, 1.8%, 3% and 1.8%, respectively. Changes according to the effect of single factors (A, B and C) in the amount of random coil structure were not significant, however, all the factor combinations indicated a significant negative impact on random coil structure. By increasing the salt concentration (A) by 1%, the alpha-helix structure decreased by 1% and by increasing the sonication time (C) by 27.5 min, the mentioned structure increased by 0.7%, remarkably. Among the factor combinations, only AB indicated a negative effect of 0.41% to alpha-helix proportion. As shown in Fig. 8, a negative correlation was observed between the alpha-helix portion before heating and the measured breaking force as well as a positive correlation with the whiteness of the gels.

The alterations in secondary structure elements were also recorded after heat-gelation, signed as H- (Table 2 and Fig. 8). Despite the fact that all single and combination factors affected the amount of intermolecular beta-sheet structure before heating, only the salt concentration (by 1%) and the factor combinations AB, AC and BC had a significantly positive effect (by 3.58%, 3.72% and 3.92%, respectively) on the amount of intermolecular beta-sheets after heating. The amount of intramolecular beta-sheets after heating were influenced positively by factor A (2.78%) and negatively by factor combination AC (−1.33%). Furthermore, the amount of random coils after heating was not remarkably affected by single factors A, B and C, but was affected by the factor combinations AB, AC, BC and ABC by −6.49%, −4.75%, −4.49% and 1.90% respectively. The amount of alpha-helix elements decreased by 0.62% after heating due to an increase in factor A by one level, but increased due to combinations of factors AB, AC and BC by 1.92%, 0.93% and 1.49%, respectively.

Influence of salt concentration and sonication on WHC of surimi gels

WHC was significantly influenced by salt concentration, and the ultrasonic treatment. As demonstrated by the coefficients presented in Table 2 and illustrated in Fig. 5, Fig. 6, Fig. 7, Fig. 8, the water-holding capacity (WHC) of the mussel surimi gels exhibited a 1% increase in response to an increase in salt concentration (factor A) by 1%. In addition, the WHC enhanced by an average of 7% when the sonication amplitude (factor B) increased by one level (35%) and by 9% when the sonication time (factor C) increased by one level (27.5 min). At the same time, positive effects occurred in the combination of factors including salt (AB, AC and ABC) and negative impact in the combination of sonication amplitude and time (BC) (See Table 2 and Fig. 5).

Gel texture

The effect of sonication treatment in presence of salt, on breaking force and deformation at hardness of mussel surimi gels are presented in Table 2. For the texture measurements using the penetration test, salt concentration (factor A) had a positive effect on breaking force meaning that by increasing the salt concentration from 1 to 2%, the breaking force increased by 2.48 g and sonication time (factor C) showed a significant negative impact of 2.39 g with breaking force of gels. The factor combinations such as AC, BC, and ABC indicated remarkable decrease of the breaking force as well, with approximately 1.3 g, 2.35 g, and 1.3 g per factor level, respectively.

Viscosity

According to Table 2, the sonication amplitude (factor B) and the factor combination of amplitude and time had a negative impact (but weakly significant) on the viscosity of surimi gels measured at 2 RPM (Fig. 6). On the basis of the viscosity measurements with the lowest error values, it could be concluded that the viscosity correlates negatively with the WHC (see Fig. 8). The gels thus appear to become softer and more fluid as more water is bound in the gels.

Gel whiteness

The salt concentration had a negative effect on gel whiteness (−1.64 a.u.), while sonication amplitude and time had a positive effect on it with 0.81 and 2.18 (a.u.), respectively (Table 2) (see Fig. 7). In addition, the combination factor of AB increases the gel whiteness by 0.45 (a.u.) remarkably.

Discussion

The results of the DOE demonstrated that sonication intensity and time, as well as salt concentration, each and in combination, affected the solubility of proteins and the resulting gel properties of mussel-based surimi.

Solubility and particle size: Solubility reflects the extent of protein denaturation and aggregation which is a key indicator of functional properties in proteins. High solubility enhances the potential application of food proteins in different products [28]. The observed increase in the solubility of HIUS-treated surimi samples (Fig. 2) may be attributed to conformational changes in proteins. The physical forces and shockwaves produced by cavitation can break down the hydrogen bonds and hydrophobic interactions in proteins, resulting in unfolding that exposes more hydrophilic groups to water [35]. This is in line with Sun et al. [25], who found similar results for porcine myosin. A smaller particle size provides a larger contact area with water and may also result in the exposure of more charged groups (NH4+, COO–) and increased water-protein interactions [36]. In general, sufficient protein solubility is considered as a prerequisite for gel formation. Also, others reported increasing MP solubility with increasing ultrasound power or time (e.g., 300 W for 30 min for Holstein bulls MPs [27], 250 W for the durations of 3, 6, 9 and 12 min for silver carp myosin [33], and 600 W for 16 min for mussel protein dispersions [36].

Particle size decrease: HIUS treatment leads to break down of protein aggregates and consistently reduced the protein particle size (Table 4). The smallest particle size among surimi samples treated with 1% NaCl corresponds to MS1-90–60, indicating that high-intensity sonication for an extended duration may reduce the particle size of MPs in mussel surimi. This is in agreement with decreasing peaks for high molecular size proteins in the size exclusion chromatogram at high levels of ultrasonication (amplitude and time) (Fig. 4) and decreasing molecular weight in SDS-PAGE (Fig. 3). These results were also in a good agreement with the reduced mean particle size and the increased solubility of mussel surimi samples.

Nevertheless, it is unclear if the particle size decrease is caused by dissociation or breaking of covalent bonds of MPs or if this is because of aggregation and loss of solubility of larger particles. By rapid generation of cavitation bubbles during ultrasonication and their growth and collapse, high shear forces, temperatures, pressure and turbulence occur. Hence, cavitation may disperse the aggregates and decompose polymers [37]. It is worth to note that in case protein aggregation is happening during ultrasonication, this might not be proved/visible by either SDS-PAGE or SEC-HPLC due to the limitations of methodology. For example, in SDS-PAGE, large aggregates might be stuck on top of the gel and not go through it and in SEC-HPLC aggregates might be removed by filtration of samples prior to injection. However, while some also noted a decreased particle size of proteins after ultrasound treatment, such as myosin heavy chain (MHC) and actin degradation into low molecular weight peptides [37], and MHC degradation into smaller molecules at higher power > 100 W and longer durations > 3 min [38], others observed no molecular weight changes in walnut protein isolates treated at 200 W for 15–30 min [39], or soy protein isolates [40]. These differences may stem from protein origin (e.g., pig, beef, or chicken), species variations or sonication conditions [[37], [39]]. Thus far, there are no data for size exclusion chromatography available that how sonication affects the size of proteins under sonication treatment, however, our findings of SEC-HPLC revealed break down of high molecular weight proteins (in retention time of 25 to 35 min) to smaller ones (areas of 2, 3, 4, 5, and 6) by increasing the sonication parameters and were well supported by SDS-PAGE.

Protein secondary structure: The most sensitive area for the changes in secondary structure composition is amide I area according to Zhang et al. [14]. In our study for mussel meat before heat-gelation, by increasing the sonication amplitude, duration, and salt level, the intermolecular beta-sheet structure increased while the intramolecular beta-sheet structure decreased. The transition from intra- to intermolecular beta-sheets can be explained by acoustic cavitation, which generates high shear forces, turbulence, shockwaves, high temperatures and water sonolysis [[36], [41], [42], [43], [44]]. This mechanical forces and chemical mechanism are powerful enough to rupture the intramolecular (internal) non-covalent bonds, such as hydrogen bonds and hydrophobic interactions, that stabilize the proteins native state [[41], [42]]. As these internal bonds break, the protein unfolds [[35], [41]], and the now exposed peptide chains can then interact with neighboring molecules, leading to the formation of intermolecular beta-sheet aggregates [[31], [45]]. The random coil portion correlated positively with the measured breaking strength of the gel (Fig. 8). This observation might suggests that sonication-induced microjetting and microstreaming act as mechanical “slicers” fragmenting large protein aggregates into smaller particles [[31], [41], [46]] and might induce unfolding of the proteins to a more disordered but flexible and open conformation [42]. This open conformation exposes highly reactive functional groups (e.g. hydrophobic residues and free sulfhydryl groups) which exposed reactive sites allow an extensive formation of a dense three dimensional network [[31], [35]]. This results in a gel with significantly higher resistance to mechanical breakage [47]. Additionally, by increasing the salt concentration, the portion of alpha-helix structure was reduced, which illustrates the combined chemical and physical stress on the proteins stability. While Ionic strength alters the electrostatic interactions, the ultrasonic cavitation specifically targets the internal hydrogen bonds stabilize the alpha-helix [[46], [47]]. This can cause the closed form of the alpha-helix to uncoil to more unordered structures like random coils or beta-turn [[41], [48]]. The observation that ultrasound effects on beta-sheets become insignificant after heating is due to the overwhelming energy of thermal denaturation. The intense thermal energy applied during the gelation of surimi forces the proteins into a stable, inter-beta-sheet-dominated network [[31], [42]]. This process effectively “masks” the more subtle structural modifications induced by prior sonication amplitude or duration, as the final protein and gel structure reaches a state of thermal saturation where initial sonochemical effects are no longer statistically significant [[42], [44]].

WHC of the surimi gels: One of the most important factors to qualify the surimi gel is the WHC, which reflects the ability of a protein gel network to bind/retain water [49]. HIUS treatment of MP increased the WHC of the resulting gels (after heating), whereby sonication time had a stronger impact at high amplitude level (Fig. 5 F). In contrast, increasing the salt concentration affected the WHC of the gels less strongly at higher sonication and duration levels (Fig. 5 D and E). For samples treated at 90% amplitude for 60 min, 3% NaCl can be replaced by 1% NaCl still having a higher WHC in mussel surimi. Our results indicated that the higher the WHC, the more water is maintained in the protein structure and the softer the gels. In fact, Zhang et al. [50], showed that protein gels with homogenous and fine structure have a higher WHC due to small pores that can retain water molecules more firmly in 3D network in comparison with non-homogenous structures. High salt concentrations enhance protein-water interactions via hydrogen bonding, improving the WHC by allowing more water to be trapped within the gel network [22]. The presence of high salt can shield some surface charges of myosin and therefore weakening the electrostatic attraction among protein molecules [22]. Meanwhile, elevated salt concentrations facilitate myosin dispersion, exposing reactive groups and strengthening protein-water interactions and increasing the gelling properties and WHC [22]. In contrast to salt, ultrasonication treatment can promote the formation of free radicals from water molecules, which react with active protein groups thereby enhancing the WHC of surimi [22]. In our study, the sonication energy uptake ranged from 169 J/mL (20% amplitude for 5 min) to 6512 J/mL (90% amplitude for 30 min), a lower range compared to Amiri et al. [27] who reported a notable increase in the WHC and solubility of MPs of Holstein bulls after sonication. They applied sonication of 100 and 300 W at 20 KHz for up to 30 min corresponding to a max. energy uptake of 10800 J/mL. Xing et al. [51] observed that the WHC in chicken meat batter increased with higher HIUS amplitudes in both 1% and 2% NaCl solutions. The structural changes (Table 4) caused by ultrasonication including potential dissociation of protein structure (Fig. 3), increase the solubility of MPs (Fig. 2) and therefore results in gels with higher WHC.

Gel texture: Gel strength is another factor to evaluate the quality of surimi gel products, meaning that a greater breaking force and breaking distance result in higher gel strength [14]. The gels strength, analyzed by the breaking force, was clearly affected by all factors (sonication and salt concentration) and all factors significantly interacted. Samples containing 3% NaCl showed higher breaking force than samples with 1% NaCl considering the same HIUS parameters (amplitude and duration), except for samples treated at 90% amplitude for 60 min. The moderate HIUS treatment caused improvement in gel strength, however, excessive treatment (90% amplitude for 60 min) caused a reduction in both WHC and breaking force in sample containing 3% NaCl. Therefore, MS1-90–60 showed higher WHC and breaking force at 1% NaCl for low-salt samples compared with 3% NaCl high-salt samples. In fact, Tang et al. [29] also found, that moderate HIUS treatment can enhance gel properties by promoting uniform gel formation, prolonged or excessive exposure −especially under high-salt conditions- can lead to diminishing returns, indicating that structural degradation may outweigh the functional benefits. Zhang et al. [14] found that Penaeus vannamei surimi gel strength initially increased with sonication power and time but declined at higher intensities. Sun et al. [25] suggested that moderate ultrasound treatment improves gelling properties in low-salt products.

Viscosity: Viscosity refers to a fluid’s resistance to flow, which is determined by the interaction between components [27]. Flow behavior and viscosity are critical functional properties of food proteins ensuring the physical stability of emulsions and other suspended particles in foods while also enhancing the mouthfeel of foods. Our results indicated that increasing sonication amplitude (Factor B) and the factor combination of sonication amplitude and time (Factor BC), negatively affected the viscosity. Reduced viscosity resulted in softer gels with higher WHC. Protein concentration, pH as well as ionic strength may affect the protein solubility and viscosity [51]. Moreover, ultrasonic treatment can result in the reduction of viscosity due to the physical forces produced by cavitation. During cavitation, the liquid medium is subjected to intense forces capable of breaking the bonds between MP strands. When the shear rate increases sufficiently to surpass Brownian motion and break chemical bonds, protein strands align with the flow field, reducing resistance to flow and hence lowering the viscosity [51].

Gel whiteness: The Whiteness is an essential quality factor of surimi products, directly influencing consumer preference meaning the whiter surimi is more popular for consumers [[6], [52]]. Gel whiteness improved with higher sonication intensity and time but decreased with salt concentration. This effect can be related to the formation of more homogeneous gel network that retains water, clung later to the surface of proteins, increasing light reflection from water molecules bound to the protein surface [53]. Tong et al. [49] observed that ultrasonically emulsified lard in silver carp surimi gels increased whiteness due to the reduced particle size of oil droplets providing uniform distribution within the gel network. Tang et al. [29] reported that low-salt (0.5% NaCl) threadfin bream surimi gels exhibited greater whiteness with higher sonication intensity. However, at 1–2% NaCl, high sonication intensity and excessive unfolding of surimi proteins led to increased aggregation, a coarser and more irregular gel network, reducing luminosity in surimi gels [29].

Conclusion

In the present research, HIUS was utilized as a novel approach to reduce the salt content needed for solubilizing MPs as the major (salt-soluble) ingredient in protein-rich seafood named surimi. The interactive effect of different factors such as salt concentration, sonication amplitude and duration on physiochemical, structural, and functional characteristics of mussel surimi was systematically investigated by experimental design using DOE software. Our findings showed that sonication amplitude and duration both influenced the key quality parameters of surimi such as protein solubility, WHC, gel strength, and gel whiteness. HIUS increased the solubility of MPs by disrupting protein aggregates and reducing the particle size. Utilization of HIUS led to significant alterations in protein conformation, with structural rearrangements confirmed through multiple methods, including SDS-PAGE, SEC-HPLC, and ATR-FTIR. These alterations resulted in improvement of functional properties such as WHC (+20–30% units) and gel whiteness (+1–4 units), while reducing the NaCl concentration from 3 to 1%. The moderate HIUS treatment improved gel performance, however, prolonged or excessive treatment had diminishing returns, particularly under high-salt conditions led to diminishing functional improvements, likely due to excessive structural disruption outweighing the benefits. This study offers a practical pathway to improve the functional properties of mussel proteins and valorize underutilized mussels wasted as by-products in low-sodium seafood formulations. Future studies could explore integrating other non-thermal processing methods, additives, or protein fractions (e.g. sarcoplasmic proteins) to complement the benefits of ultrasound. This would enable greater control over gel characteristics, sensory properties, nutritional profiles, and sustainability.
