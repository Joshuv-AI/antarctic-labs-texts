Adapting Offline Reinforcement Learning with Online Delays
Simon Sinong Zhan, Qingyuan Wu, Frank Yang, Xiangyu Shi, Chao Huang, Qi Zhu

Abstract
Offline–to–online deployment of reinforcement learning (RL) agents often stumbles over two fundamental gaps: (1) the sim-to-real gap , where real-world systems exhibit latency and other physical imperfections not captured in simulation; and (2) the interaction gap , where policies trained purely offline face out-of-distribution (OOD) issues during online execution, as collecting new interaction data is costly or risky. As a result, agents must generalize from static, delay-free datasets to dynamic, delay-prone environments. Standard offline RL therefore learns from delay-free simulator logs yet must operate in delay-riddled reality, where the Markov assumption collapses and performance degrades. In this work, we introduce DT-CORL ( D elay T ransformer belief policy C onstrained O ffline RL ), a novel offline RL framework explicitly designed to handle potential online delayed dynamics. DT-CORL (i) synthesises delay-robust actions utilizing a transformer based belief prediction function without ever seeing delayed observations during training, and (ii) slashes learning inefficiency relative to naive history augmentation approaches. Empirically, we validate DT-CORL on various benchmark tasks from the D4RL dataset under multiple delay configurations, demonstrating that our method outperforms both history augmentation approaches and vanilla belief-based baselines, bridging the sim-to-real latency gap while preserving the data efficiency.

Introduction

Real-world decision-making systems, including autonomous robots embodied assistants
(

;
wang2023joint
;
wang2023enforcing
;
wei2017deep
;

)

;
mahmood2018setting
;
sun2022microfluid
)
.
In this challenging setting, the Markovian property does not even hold, thus leading to significant performance degeneration.
To address this issue, existing delayed reinforcement learning (RL) methods usually reformulate this challenging problem as the augmented Markov decision process (MDP)
(
altman1992closed
)
, a special form of partial-observable MDP (POMDP), since the delay makes the true state fully intractable.
Unfortunately, collecting
online

(
li2023behavior
;
yang2024case
;
zou2015automatic
)

At the same time, many entities already possess large

(
fu2020d4rl
;
mu2021maniskill
)
. These observations capture the task’s reward structure and nominal transitions but omit the latency found in deployment hardware. Consequently, we ask:

How can we train a robust policy
offline
—using only pre-collected, delay-free trajectories—so that it performs reliably when executed in a
delayed
real-world environment?

This question extends to two pressing needs: (i) leveraging static offline data without further interaction with the environment (offline RL), and (ii) robustly coping with latency at online deployment (delayed RL). Achieving both simultaneously promises safer development cycles, reduced sample complexity, and smoother sim-to-real transfer for latency-sensitive robotic and autonomous systems.

Delayed RL arises whenever perceptual or actuation delays disrupt the Markovian property, forcing agents to reason explicitly about the latency between true system states and available observations. Typical solutions involve either
state augmentation
kim2023belief
;
dida
;
wu2024boosting
;
wu2024variational
, which stacks multiple past history into an extended state space at the cost of significantly increased sample complexity, or
belief-state approaches
(
chen2021delay
;
karamzade2024reinforcement
;
wu2025directly
)
, which compress historical information into latent beliefs but suffer from compounding prediction errors as beliefs are propagated forward in time
(
wu2025directly
)
and distribution mismatch when directly deployed.
Meanwhile,
offline RL
levine2020offline
seeks to derive effective policies purely from static, pre-collected datasets without additional online interaction. Its primary challenge lies in balancing policy conservatism, which avoids unsafe extrapolation, with exploration of actions potentially absent from the original data, leading to overly cautious or suboptimal policies. When delay handling meets offline training constraints, these challenges compound. Naive augmentation approaches create artificial, highly complex delay distributions rarely covered well by static offline datasets, further exacerbating sample inefficiency and dimensionality issues. Simultaneously, vanilla belief-state methods become vulnerable to unchecked drift, as offline data provides no corrective online feedback: at test time, the policy may query action–state pairs that are slightly outside the logged support; the frozen belief model must extrapolate there, propagating its error forward at every single step.

In this work, we propose
DT-CORL
(
D

T
ransformer-belief
C
onstrained
O

RL
), a novel belief-based framework explicitly designed to tackle the compounded challenges arising in the offline delayed RL setting. Leveraging a transformer to predict compact belief states, DT-CORL converts the policy-constrained offline problem in a delayed MDP into an ordinary MDP optimization task. This end-to-end formulation both improves sample efficiency and contains model error during training, thereby sidestepping the distribution-mismatch and compounding-error issues that plague vanilla belief-state pipelines. Empirically, comprehensive experiments on the D4RL locomotion suite

across various delay scenarios—including varying delay lengths, deterministic delays, and stochastic delays—consistently confirm the superiority of DT-CORL over baseline methods, encompassing both augmented-state approaches and vanilla belief-based methods (i.e., direct integration of pretrained belief models with offline RL algorithms).

Preliminaries

MDP.

An RL problem is typically formulated as a finite-horizon Markov Decision Process (MDP), defined by the tuple
\langle\mathcal{S},\mathcal{A},\mathcal{P},r,H,\gamma,\rho_{0}\rangle
(
sutton1998reinforcement
)
. Here,
\mathcal{S}
denotes the state space,
\mathcal{A}
the action space,
\mathcal{P}:\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A}\times\mathcal{S}\rightarrow[0,1]
the probabilistic transition kernel,
r:\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
the reward function,
H
the horizon length,
\gamma\in(0,1)
the discount factor, and
\rho_{0}
the initial state distribution. At each timestep
t
, given the current state
s_{t}\in\mathcal{S}
, the agent selects an action
a_{t}\sim\pi(\cdot|s_{t})
according to policy
\pi:\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A}\rightarrow[0,1]
. Subsequently, the MDP transitions to the next state
s_{t+1}\sim\mathcal{P}(\cdot|s_{t},a_{t})
, and the agent receives a scalar reward
r_{t}:=r(s_{t},a_{t})
. We further introduce several mild regularity assumptions commonly adopted in the RL literature

;

Delayed MDP.

A delayed RL problem can be reformulated as a delayed MDP with Markov property based on the augmentation approaches
(
altman1992closed
)
. Assuming the delay being
\Delta
, the delayed MDP is denoted as a tuple
\langle{\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{A}},\mathcal{P}_{\Delta},r_{\Delta},H,\gamma,\rho_{\Delta}\rangle
, where the augmented state space is defined as
{\mathcal{X}}:={\mathcal{S}}\times{\mathcal{A}}^{\Delta}
(e.g., an augmented state
x_{t}=\{s_{t-\Delta},a_{t-\Delta},\cdots,a_{t-1}\}\in{\mathcal{X}}
),
{\mathcal{A}}
is the action space, the delayed transition function is defined as

\mathcal{P}_{\Delta}(x_{t+1}|x_{t},a_{t}):=\mathcal{P}(s_{t-\Delta+1}|s_{t-\Delta},a_{t-\Delta})\delta_{a_{t}}(a^{\prime}_{t})\prod_{i=1}^{\Delta-1}\delta_{a_{t-i}}(a^{\prime}_{t-i})
where
\delta
is the Dirac distribution, the delayed reward function is defined as
r_{\Delta}(x_{t},a_{t}):=\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{s_{t}\sim b(\cdot|x_{t})}\left[r(s_{t},a_{t})\right]
where
b
is the belief function defined as
b_{\Delta}(s_{t}|x_{t}):=\int_{{\mathcal{S}}^{\Delta}}\prod_{i=0}^{\Delta-1}\mathcal{P}(s_{t-\Delta+i+1}|s_{t-\Delta+i},a_{t-\Delta+i})\mathrm{d}{s_{t-\Delta+i+1}}
, the initial augmented state distribution is defined as
\rho_{\Delta}=\rho\prod_{i=1}^{\Delta}\delta_{a_{-i}}
. Noted that delayed RL is not necessarily only observational delay, there could be action delay as well. However, it has been proved that action-delay formulated delay RL problem is a subset of observation-delay RL problem
katsikopoulos2003markov
. Thus, without loss of generality, we only consider the general observation delay in the remaining of the paper.

3
Problem Definition

We consider the offline delayed RL setting in which the agent learns solely from a pre-collected static dataset,
\mathcal{D}=\{(s_{t}^{i},a_{t}^{i},r_{t}^{i},s_{t+1}^{i})\}_{t=0}^{H},i=1,\dots,K\}
, consisting of
K
trajectories collected by the behavior policy
\mu
in a delay-free environment, such as a simulator or other idealized demonstration system.
However, at deployment time, the policy is expected to operate under delayed feedback—a setting in which observations and rewards arrive after a fixed latency or max latency up to
\Delta
steps. In offline learning under this delayed MDP formulation, the agent must construct augmented state–action pairs from the delay-free dataset
\mathcal{D}
, enabling training in the delayed setting without new environment interaction. However, standard offline RL methods suffer from
out-of-distribution
(OOD) generalization issues when the learned policy queries actions not well-covered in
\mathcal{D}

(
levine2020offline
)
. To address this, policy-constrained approaches such as BRAC
(
wu2019behavior
)
and ReBRAC
(
tarasov2023revisiting
)
enforce similarity between the learned policy and the dataset’s behavior policy, often by bounding a divergence measure
D(\pi,\mu)
.
This motivates the policy-constrained learning objective under the delayed MDP:

\displaystyle\hat{Q}_{\Delta}^{\pi_{\Delta}}

\displaystyle\leftarrow\arg\min_{Q_{\Delta}}\mathbb{E}_{(x,a,x^{\prime})\sim\mathcal{D}}\left[\left(Q_{\Delta}(x,a)-\bigl(r_{\Delta}(x,a)+\gamma\mathbb{E}_{a^{\prime}\sim\pi_{\Delta}(\cdot|x^{\prime})}Q_{\Delta}(x^{\prime},a^{\prime})\bigr)\right)^{2}\right]

(1)

\displaystyle\pi^{k+1}_{\Delta}

\displaystyle\leftarrow\arg\max_{\pi_{\Delta}}\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathcal{D}}\left[\mathbb{E}_{a\sim\pi_{\Delta}(\cdot|x)}[\hat{Q}_{\Delta}^{\pi_{\Delta}}(x,a)]\right]\quad\text{s.t.}\quad D(\pi_{\Delta},\mu_{\Delta})\leq\epsilon

(2)

Here,
Q_{\Delta}
is the action-value function defined over augmented states, and
\mu_{\Delta}
is the dataset’s behavior policy lifted to the augmented space. The constraint margin
\epsilon
controls the allowable divergence between the learned policy
\pi_{\Delta}
and
\mu_{\Delta}
, mitigating OOD queries in the offline setting.

4
Our Approach

We now present the
DT-CORL
, a novel offline RL framework for adapting online delayed feedback. DT-CORL integrates transformer-based belief modeling with policy-constrained offline learning to address the challenges outlined in previous sections. This section describes how we construct delay-compensated belief representations, train a policy using belief prediction, and incorporate policy regularization to ensure effective deployment under delay. Specifically, we introduce a belief-based policy iteration framework that infers latent, delay-compensated states through a belief function, providing a compact and semantically grounded alternative. Then, we provide detailed algorithmic implementations. We further support this approach with a learning efficiency discussion to explain its efficiency benefits comparing with the augmented approach.

Remark 4.4
.

For deterministic MDP,
b_{\Delta}
is also deterministic, meaning that
\mathbb{E}_{\hat{s}\sim b_{\Delta}(\cdot|x)}\left[r(\hat{s},a)\right]=r_{\Delta}(x,a)
and
\mathbb{E}_{\hat{s}\sim b_{\Delta}(\cdot|x)}\left[Q^{\pi}(\hat{s},a)\right]=Q_{\Delta}^{\pi_{\Delta}}(x,a)
. Since
b_{\Delta}
becomes a injection mapping under this setting,
\mathbb{E}_{\hat{s}\sim b_{\Delta}(\cdot|x)}\left[r(\hat{s},a)\right]=r(s,a)
also should hold. Thus, we can directly leverage existing offline delay-free data tuples for update, except for the policy related terms.

A seemingly simpler alternative is a
two–stage
pipeline:
(i) train a belief model offline;
(ii) freeze it and apply any delay-free offline-RL algorithm to the original delay-free samples, deploying the resulting policy with that fixed belief function.
This strategy, however, suffers from several drawbacks that DT–CORL avoids.

(i) Biased value targets.

In DT–CORL, the Bellman target in (
3
) is computed
through
the belief
b_{\Delta}(\cdot|x)
, so the critic learns on exactly the latent states the policy will later encounter.
By contrast, the two–stage method treats the filter as error-free; residual belief error becomes unmodelled noise, forcing the critic to fit a moving target and biasing the learned
Q
-values.

(ii) Distribution mismatch.

Because the critic of the two-stage baseline is trained under
(s,a)\!\sim\!\mathcal{D}
while the policy, evaluated through the frozen belief, quickly drifts to new action choices, the training and deployment distributions diverge.
This mismatch is magnified in delayed environments and leads to sub-optimal returns, a phenomenon confirmed in our experiments (see
Section

5
).

(iii) Inferior sample efficiency in stochastic MDPs.

For stochastic dynamics, each offline tuple
(s,a,r,s^{\prime})
reveals only a single next state, whereas DT–CORL reuses the same transition to generate
\Delta
latent states along the belief rollout, extracting richer supervision.
Similar gains from “multiple synthetic samples per transition” have been observed in model-based offline RL
kidambi2020morel
;
yu2020mopo
.
In short, embedding belief prediction
inside
the policy-iteration loop both contains model error and keeps the critic, policy, and belief model aligned—benefits a frozen two-stage pipeline cannot match, in addition to avoiding the dimensional explosion of raw state augmentation.

Remark 4.5
.

Existing work has compared the sample-complexity of augmentation- and belief-based methods in the
online
delayed-RL setting
wu2024variational
;
wu2024boosting
. The same intuition should carry over to offline learning, but a formal analysis would require assumptions—e.g., uniform data-coverage, linear value realisability, and bounded rewards—that are standard in offline-RL theory
bai2022pessimistic
;
shi2022pessimistic
;
xie2021bellman
yet do not hold in our setting. Establishing tight offline bounds without these restrictive conditions is therefore an open problem that we leave for future work.

4.2
Practical Implementation

Figure 1
:
Overall pipeline of DT-CORL. In the Offline Training phase, trajectory data are augmented to train the transformer belief, and with the trained transformer belief, we conduct belief-based PI in the offline setting. In the Online Adaptation, we utilize the transformer belief to predict the current state from delayed observation, and adapt with offline-trained policy.

Belief Prediction.

The belief prediction can be taken as a dynamic modeling problem. With a given offline trajectory
\{(s_{t},a_{t},r_{t},s_{t+1})\}_{t=0}^{H}
, we can manually create the augmented state by stacking states and actions in
\Delta
steps from start to end, where
x_{t}=\{s_{t-\Delta},a_{t-\Delta},a_{t-\Delta+1},\cdots,a_{t-1}\}
and the true state is
s_{t}
. Thus, the problem becomes training a belief function
b_{\Delta}
which takes in
x_{t}
and predict
s_{t}
. To reduce the potential compounding loss from belief prediction, we employ a transformer structure
vaswani2017attention
;
wu2025directly
, where with the given augmented state
x_{t}
transformer predicts a sequence from
\hat{s}_{t-\Delta+1}
to
\hat{s}_{t}
. Based on the deterministic or stochastic nature of MDP, the transformer belief is updated with either MSE or MLE objectives.

Multi-step belief-based policy optimisation.

Although many offline RL methods regularise the policy with KL, MMD, or Wasserstein distances
levine2020offline
;
wu2019behavior
, computing these divergences exactly is costly. Following the pragmatic approach of TD3 +BC and ReBRAC
fujimoto2021minimalist
;
tarasov2023revisiting
, we approximate the policy–behaviour divergence by the mean-squared error between actions sampled from the learned policy and the augmented behavior policy. The policy-improvement step therefore, becomes

\pi^{k+1}\;=\;\arg\max_{\pi}\;\mathbb{E}_{(x,a)\sim\mathcal{D}}\!\Bigl[\mathbb{E}_{\begin{subarray}{c}\hat{s}\sim b_{\Delta}(\cdot|x)\\
\hat{a}\sim\pi(\cdot|\hat{s})\end{subarray}}\bigl[\hat{Q}^{\pi^{k}}(\hat{s},\hat{a})\bigr]-\alpha\bigl\lVert a-\hat{a}\bigr\rVert_{2}^{2}\Bigr]

where
\alpha>0
trades off exploitation and conservatism. This surrogate avoids training an explicit delay-augmented behaviour model while retaining a simple quadratic penalty that is trivial to compute in continuous control. To speed up value propagation, we further employ
n
-step returns and Retrace(
\lambda
) targets
peng1994incremental
;
munos2016safe
;
kozuno2021revisiting
, which empirically accelerate convergence without additional environment interaction. Besides, for deterministic MDPs the immediate reward
r(s_{t},a_{t})
can be read directly from the dataset, so no separate reward model is required (cf.
Eq.

3
,
Eq.

4
, and
Remark

4.4
).

Online adaptation.

At deployment time, we maintain a circular
action buffer
of length
\Delta
, initialised with random actions drawn from
\mathcal{A}
.
At timestep
t
the agent receives the delayed observation
o_{t}
from the plant, appends the most recent action
a_{t-1}
to the buffer, and forms the augmented input

x_{t}=\{o_{t},a_{t-\Delta},\dots,a_{t-1}\}
.
This sequence is passed to the trained belief transformer, which returns a delay-compensated state estimate
\hat{s}_{t}
.
The policy
\pi(\cdot\,|\,\hat{s}_{t})
then selects the next action
a_{t}
.
During the first
\Delta
steps (and symmetrically near episode termination), the buffer is not yet full. We handle these boundary conditions by inserting a special
[MASK]
token for missing actions and enabling the transformer’s built-in masking mechanism, ensuring consistent state prediction throughout the episode. Detailed neural network structures and hyper-parameters of DT-CORL can be found in

5
Experiments

We benchmark DT -CORL on the standard MuJoCo locomotion tasks from the D4RL offline-RL suite
fu2020d4rl
;
todorov2012mujoco
.
Because no existing method learns a delay-robust policy solely from delay-free data, we construct three baselines:
(i)
Augmented-BC
, which applies vanilla behavioural cloning
torabi2018behavioral
in the
\Delta
-step augmented state space;
(ii)
Augmented-CQL
, which runs Conservative Q-Learning
kumar2020conservative
on the same augmented states; and
(iii)

, which feeds the CQL policy the transformer belief used by DT -CORL instead of raw augmentation.

6
Related Works

Delayed RL.

Delayed reinforcement learning arises in domains such as high-frequency trading
hasbrouck2013low
and transportation
cao2020using
. While reward delay has been extensively analysed
han2022off
;
arjona2019rudder
;
zhang2023multi
, we focus on the more challenging
observation/action
delay. Existing methods fall into two categories:
Augmentation-based
, these algorithms concatenate the past
\Delta
actions (and sometimes states) to restore the Markov property, then optimise a policy in the enlarged state space. Notable examples include DIDA
dida
, DC/AC
bouteiller2020reinforcement
, ADRL and BPQL, which bootstrap large-delay learning with auxiliary small-delay tasks
wu2024boosting
;
kim2023belief
, and VDPO, which frames delayed control as variational inference
wu2024variational
. A key drawback is that the augmented state dimension grows with
\Delta
, sharply increasing sample complexity.
Belief-based
, these approaches predict the latent current state and act in the original space. DATS adopts a Gaussian belief update
chen2021delay
; D​-Dreamer leverages recurrent world models
karamzade2024reinforcement
; D​-SAC blends delayed information via causal-transformer attention
liotet2021learning
; and DFBT replaces the recurrent filter with a sequence-to-sequence transformer to reduce compounding error
wu2025directly
. Although more compact, belief rollouts still accumulate approximation error over time.
All of the above methods assume online interaction with a delayed environment. Aside from some imitation-style studies that pre-train on delay-free logs and then fine-tune online
dida
;
chen2021delay
, we are unaware of any work that learns delay-robust policies
offline
using only delay-free data.

Offline RL and Online Adaptation.

Offline reinforcement learning aims to learn high-performing policies from fixed replay datasets without additional environment interaction, addressing safety and data-collection constraints common in robotics, healthcare, and recommendation systems
levine2020offline
. Early work focused on
value conservatism
, e.g., CQL
kumar2020conservative
and IQL
kostrikov2021offline
, which lowers the Q-value of out-of-distribution (OOD) actions to prevent overestimation. Complementary
policy-constraint
methods such as BRAC
wu2019behavior
and TD3 +BC
fujimoto2021minimalist
regularise the learned policy toward the behaviour policy to stay within the support of the dataset.
Although purely offline training mitigates OOD failure, policies may remain sub-optimal if the dataset lacks trajectories near the optimum. A growing line of
offline-to-online
(a.k.a. hybrid) methods therefore initialise from an offline policy and continue fine-tuning with limited online interaction. Notable examples include AWAC
nair2020awac
, Conservative Fine-Tuning
nakamoto2023cal
, and Hy-Q
song2022hybrid
. In additions, there are also works trying to close the gap between the possible dynamic difference between simulation and real world scenarios
feng2023finetuning
;
niu2022trust
;
tiboni2023dropo
. However, none of those works try to close the possible delay gaps between offline simulation/data and online real environments.

7
Conclusion

We introduced
DT–CORL
, the first offline–to–online delay adaption framework that learns delay-robust policies
solely
from delay-free trajectories.
By coupling a transformer–based belief predictor with a conservative, behaviour-regularised policy-iteration loop, DT–CORL eliminates the state-dimensional explosion of action-history augmentation while guarding against out-of-distribution actions that plague standard offline RL.
Extensive experiments on the D4RL locomotion benchmark confirm these gains: across deterministic and stochastic delays of up to 16 steps, DT–CORL consistently outperforms strong augmented-state and vanilla belief-state baselines, achieving much better learning efficiency.
However, in our setting, DT-CORL assumes a known delay length
\Delta
and deterministic actuation latency.
Extending the framework to
adaptive
or
heterogeneous
delays—where latency varies across tasks or even within an episode—remains open.
Moreover, while our transformer belief predictor copes well with the locomotion tasks studied here, scaling to vision-based manipulation may require integrating spatial attention or world-model priors.
Finally, an exciting direction is to combine DT-CORL with limited online fine-tuning, leveraging our conservative value estimates as an uncertainty-aware exploration bonus to further narrow the sim-to-real gap.
We hope DT–CORL and its accompanying analysis will spur broader exploration of delay-aware, data-efficient reinforcement learning.
