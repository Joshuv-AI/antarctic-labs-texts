l’Hôpital rules for complex-valued functions in higher dimensions
Albert Chern, Sadashige Ishida

Abstract
In calculus, l’Hôpital’s rule provides a simple way to evaluate the limits of quotient functions when both the numerator and denominator vanish. But what happens when we move beyond real functions on a real interval? In this article, we study when the quotient of two complex-valued functions in higher dimension can be defined continuously at the points where both functions vanish. Surprisingly, the answer is far subtler than in the real-valued setting. We provide a complete characterization for the continuity of the quotient function. We also point out why extending this result to smoother quotients remains an intriguing challenge.

Introduction

The classical Bernoulli–l’Hôpital rule for real functions
f(x)
and
g(x)
on a real interval states conditions under which the ratio
f(x)/g(x)
is well-defined at a common zero of
f
and
g
.
Specifically, if
f(a)=g(a)=0
,
g^{\prime}(a)\neq 0
, and the limit
L=\lim_{x\to a}f^{\prime}(x)/g^{\prime}(x)
exists, then
\lim_{x\to a}f(x)/g(x)=L
.

The result was further extended to complex-valued functions on a real interval by
[
2
]
and, more recently, to real-valued multivariable functions by
[
3
]
.

While the classical l’Hôpital rule and its extensions focus on the limit of
f(x)/g(x)
at the common zeros of
f
and
g
, a stronger theorem can be stated in terms of regularity. If we define
f(a)/g(a)\coloneqq L
, then
f(x)/g(x)
remains a smooth function as long as both
f
and
g
are smooth.

Theorem 1
(Smooth quotient of real functions).

Let
f,g\in C^{\infty}(I)
be smooth real-valued functions on a real interval
I
. Suppose
f,g
have only simple zeros in
I
and that their zero sets are identical. Then there exists a non-vanishing smooth function
\varphi\in C^{\infty}(I)
such that
f=g\varphi
and
\varphi=f^{\prime}/g^{\prime}
at the zero level sets.

Here, a real function
f
on
I
is said to have simple zeros if
f^{\prime}(a)\neq 0
whenever
f(a)=0
.
An elementary proof of
Theorem 1
is provided in
Appendix A
and
B
.

Intuitively, functions with common zeros can be thought of as sharing common factors, which cancel out in their quotient. A similar result holds for real-valued multivariable functions. Let
\Omega\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
be an open set, with
n\geq 2
, serving as the domain. The zero set of a real-valued function
f\colon\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
is denoted by
\Sigma_{f}=\{\mathbf{a}\in\Omega\,|\,f(\mathbf{a})=0\}\subset\Omega
.
A smooth function
f\colon\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
is said to have simple zeros if
\nabla f|_{\mathbf{a}}\neq 0
for all
\mathbf{a}\in\Sigma_{f}
. Geometrically, by the implicit function theorem, the zero set
\Sigma_{f}
of a function with simple zeros forms a smooth hypersurface.

Theorem 2
(Smooth quotient of real functions in arbitrary dimension).

Let
f,g\in C^{\infty}(\Omega)
be smooth real-valued functions with simple zeros and a common zero set
\Sigma_{f}=\Sigma_{g}
. Then there exists a non-vanishing smooth function
\varphi\in C^{\infty}(\Omega)
such that
f=g\varphi
.

A proof of
Theorem 2
is provided in
Appendix C
.

One might ask whether a similar result holds for complex-valued functions in arbitrary dimension. Specifically, do two complex-valued functions on an open set
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
with a common zero set always have a smooth complex-valued quotient? The answer to this question is surprisingly different from the real-valued case.

As we will see in Section
2
, having common simple zeros is generally insufficient. It turns out that the existence of a continuous quotient requires an additional algebraic relation between the gradients of the functions at the zero set. We describe this condition in the following.

2
Quotient of Complex Functions

Most existing generalizations of the l’Hôpital theorem for complex-valued functions are formulated on a real interval
[
2
]
or for holomorphic functions on a complex plane, a restrictive class of functions studied in complex analysis
[
1
]
.
In the latter case, the quotient
f/g
is globally defined and it coincides with
f^{\prime}(\mathbf{a})/g^{\prime}(\mathbf{a})
at each common simple zero
\mathbf{a}
.

Of course, a theory that guarantees the existence of a quotient should be much more general than simply requiring both functions to be locally holomorphic. For instance, the quotient
f/f=1
is smooth even when
f
is not holomorphic.
Moreover, holomorphy is not invariant under smooth coordinate transformations, whereas the existence of a smooth quotient is independent. Additionally, it is unclear how the notion of holomorphy should make sense in a domain of general real dimension.

So, can we generalize the l’Hôpital theorem for complex-valued functions in a space of arbitrary dimension? This is the main question we explore in this article.

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
be an open set with
n\geq 2
. For a complex-valued function
f\colon\Omega\to\mathbb{C}
, the zero set is defined as
\Gamma_{f}\coloneqq\{\mathbf{a}\in\Omega\,|\,f(\mathbf{a})=0\}\subset\Omega
,
and the notion of simple zeros for complex-valued functions is characterized by a non-degeneracy condition on the first derivative.

Definition 1
(Simple zero).

For
n\geq 2
, a differentiable function
f\colon\Omega(\subset\mathbb{R}^{n})\to\mathbb{C}
is said to have
simple zeros
if, at each zero
\mathbf{a}\in\Gamma_{f}
, the derivative
Df|_{\mathbf{a}}\colon\mathbb{R}^{n}\xrightarrow{\rm linear}\mathbb{C}\cong\mathbb{R}^{2}
, considered as a real linear map, has rank 2.

By the implicit function theorem, the zero set
\Gamma_{f}
of a function with simple zeros forms a codimension-2 submanifold embedded in
\Omega
.

Problem 1
(Smooth quotient of complex multivariable functions).

Let
f,g\in C^{\infty}(\Omega;\mathbb{C})
be smooth complex-valued functions with a common zero set
\Gamma_{f}=\Gamma_{g}
consisting of simple zeros. Does there exist a nonvanishing complex-valued function
\varphi\in C^{\infty}(\Omega;\mathbb{C})
so that
f=g\varphi
?

In most cases, the answer to
1
is negative, as demonstrated in the following examples.

Example 1
.

Let
\Omega=\mathbb{R}^{2}
,
f(x,y)\coloneqq x+y+\mkern 1.5mu\mathbbold{i}\mkern 1.5muy
, and
g(x,y)=x+\mkern 1.5mu\mathbbold{i}\mkern 1.5muy
. They have a common simple zero at the origin. The quotient of these two functions is given by

\varphi(x,y)=\frac{f(x,y)}{g(x,y)}=\frac{x+y+\mkern 1.5mu\mathbbold{i}\mkern 1.5muy}{x+\mkern 1.5mu\mathbbold{i}\mkern 1.5muy}.

This function is constant along any straight line passing through the origin, yet is undefined at the origin. To see this, let us take a line
(x(t),y(t))=(t,ct)
with some real constant
c
and a parameter
t
. Then we have
\varphi(t,ct)=\frac{t+ct+\mkern 1.5mu\mathbbold{i}\mkern 1.5muct}{t+\mkern 1.5mu\mathbbold{i}\mkern 1.5muct}=\frac{1+c+\mkern 1.5mu\mathbbold{i}\mkern 1.5muc}{1+\mkern 1.5mu\mathbbold{i}\mkern 1.5muc}
and the value at the origin depends on
c
, as shown in
Fig.
2
.

Example 2
.

Let
\Omega=\mathbb{R}^{2}
,
f(x,y)=x-\mkern 1.5mu\mathbbold{i}\mkern 1.5muy
, and
g(x,y)=x+\mkern 1.5mu\mathbbold{i}\mkern 1.5muy
, which have a common simple zero at the origin. Their quotient is given by
\varphi(x,y)=\frac{f(x,y)}{g(x,y)}=\frac{x-\mkern 1.5mu\mathbbold{i}\mkern 1.5muy}{x+\mkern 1.5mu\mathbbold{i}\mkern 1.5muy}
. Just as
1
, this function is also constant along any line passing through the origin but is discontinuous at the origin.

In Examples
1
and
2
, the quotient
f/g
has a well-defined l’Hôpital limit at the origin when restricted to a one-dimensional path approaching the origin (
e.g.
, along the real axis), as studied in
[
2
]
. However,
f/g
is not continuous as a function of two variables.

The real part of the quotient of
f(x,y)=x+y+\mkern 1.5mu\mathbbold{i}\mkern 1.5muy
and
g(x,y)=x+\mkern 1.5mu\mathbbold{i}\mkern 1.5muy
. Blue lines are level lines, showing that
f/g
takes a constant value along any line passing through the origin.

The real part of the quotient of

f(x,y)=x+x^{2}+\mkern 1.5mu\mathbbold{i}\mkern 1.5mux+\mkern 1.5mu\mathbbold{i}\mkern 1.5muy
and
g(x,y)=x+\mkern 1.5mu\mathbbold{i}\mkern 1.5mux+\mkern 1.5mu\mathbbold{i}\mkern 1.5muy
. The function
f/g
is continuous but not differentiable at the origin.

Example 3
.

Let
\Omega=\mathbb{R}^{2}
,
f(x,y)=x+x^{2}+\mkern 1.5mu\mathbbold{i}\mkern 1.5mux+\mkern 1.5mu\mathbbold{i}\mkern 1.5muy
, and
g(x,y)=x+\mkern 1.5mu\mathbbold{i}\mkern 1.5mux+\mkern 1.5mu\mathbbold{i}\mkern 1.5muy
.
This pair defines a continuous quotient,
although it is not differentiable at the origin, as seen in
Fig.
2
.

This example defines a continuous quotient
f/g
unlike the previous two examples. We now investigate what makes the difference.

3
Ratio of derivatives

Similar to the classical l’Hôpital’s rule, the behavior of the quotient of two functions near their common zero is closely related to the quotient of their derivatives. In this section, we explore a more intricate structure that arises near a common simple zero of two complex-valued functions.

Let
f,g\in C^{\infty}(\Omega;\mathbb{C})
be smooth complex-valued functions with simple zeros and a common zero set
\Gamma
.
Then, for all
\mathbf{a}\in\Gamma
, the derivatives
Df|_{\mathbf{a}}
and
Dg|_{\mathbf{a}}
are surjective real linear maps to
\mathbb{C}\cong\mathbb{R}^{2}
.
This defines a unique real matrix
\mathbf{A}|_{\mathbf{a}}\in\mathbb{R}^{2\times 2}
(an endormorphism on
\mathbb{C}\cong\mathbb{R}^{2}
) that relates the two real linear operators via

\displaystyle Df|_{\mathbf{a}}=\mathbf{A}|_{\mathbf{a}}Dg|_{\mathbf{a}}.

(1)

The matrix representation
\mathbf{A}|_{\mathbf{a}}
of this relationship depends only on the choice basis for
\mathbb{C}\cong\mathbb{R}^{2}
, which is taken to be
1
and
\mkern 1.5mu\mathbbold{i}\mkern 1.5mu
. In particular, the matrix
\mathbf{A}|_{\mathbf{a}}
is independent of the choice of coordinate system for
\Omega
.

If a change of coordinate
\Phi\colon\tilde{\Omega}\to\Omega
is applied, where
\Phi(\tilde{\mathbf{a}})=\mathbf{a}
, and we define
\tilde{f}\coloneqq f\circ\Phi
and
\tilde{g}\coloneqq g\circ\Phi
, then the derivatives transform as
D\tilde{f}|_{\tilde{\mathbf{a}}}=Df|_{\mathbf{a}}D\Phi|_{\tilde{\mathbf{a}}}
and
D\tilde{g}|_{\tilde{\mathbf{a}}}=Dg|_{\mathbf{a}}D\Phi|_{\tilde{\mathbf{a}}}
.
In particular, the relation
D\tilde{f}|_{\tilde{\mathbf{a}}}=\mathbf{A}|_{\tilde{\mathbf{a}}}D\tilde{g}|_{\tilde{\mathbf{a}}}
still holds in the transformed coordinates.
That is, the matrix
\mathbf{A}
remains invariant in this change of coordinate.

Definition 2
(Ratio of derivatives).

The function
\mathbf{A}\colon\Gamma\to\mathbb{R}^{2\times 2}
defined in (
1
) is denoted by

\displaystyle\left(\frac{Df}{Dg}\right)_{\mathbf{a}}\coloneqq\mathbf{A}|_{\mathbf{a}},\quad\text{for }\mathbf{a}\in\Gamma.

(2)

While in general
f/g
is discontinuous at
\Gamma
, as demonstrated in Examples
1
and
2
, the nature of the discontinuity is such that the directional limit of
f/g
at a point in
\Gamma
depends on the path of approach. This path dependency can be described in terms of the quotient of derivatives (
2
).

Theorem 3
(Path dependent l’Hôpital rule).

Let
f,g\in C^{\infty}(\Omega;\mathbb{C})
be smooth complex-valued functions with simple zeros and share a common zero set
\Gamma
.
Suppose
\gamma\colon(-\varepsilon,\varepsilon)\to\Omega
is a smooth path such that
\gamma(0)\in\Gamma
and
\gamma^{\prime}(0)
is transversal to
\Gamma
, meaning that
g_{\gamma}^{\prime}\coloneqq\lim_{t\to 0}g(\gamma(t))/t=Dg|_{\gamma(0)}\gamma^{\prime}(0)\in\mathbb{C}
is nonzero. Then the directional limit of
f/g
along
\gamma
exists and is given by

\displaystyle\lim_{t\to 0}\frac{f(\gamma(t))}{g(\gamma(t))}={1\over g_{\gamma}^{\prime}}\left(\frac{Df}{Dg}\right)_{\gamma(0)}g_{\gamma}^{\prime}.

(3)

Here,
\left(\frac{Df}{Dg}\right)_{\gamma(0)}g_{\gamma}^{\prime}
represents the action of the
\mathbb{R}^{2\times 2}
matrix
{Df\over Dg}
on
g_{\gamma}^{\prime}
seen as a real two dimensional vector, while division by
g_{\gamma}^{\prime}
is interpreted in terms of complex numbers. Moreover, the quotient
f/g
remains smooth when restricted to the path, meaning that there exists a smooth nowhere-vanishing function
\varphi\in C^{\infty}((-\epsilon,\epsilon);\mathbb{C})
such that
f(\gamma(t))=g(\gamma(t))\varphi(t)
.

We give a proof in
Appendix D
.
Example
1
and
2
in the previous section fall onto the case of
Theorem 3
and their path-wise limits at the zero is given as in (
3
).

4
Continuous Quotient of Complex Multivariable Functions

The limit (
3
) for the quotient
f/g
of two complex-valued functions with a common simple zero set
\Gamma
is generally path-dependent unless the linear operator
({Df\over Dg})
satisfies additional conditions.

Definition 3
(Complex linearity).

Two smooth complex-valued functions
f,g\in C^{\infty}(\Omega,\mathbb{C})
with simple zeros and a common zero set
\Gamma
are said to be
complex linearly related
if the quotient of their derivatives,
({Df\over Dg})\colon\Gamma\to\mathbb{R}^{2\times 2}
, is a scaled rotation at every point in
\Gamma
. That is,
({Df\over Dg})
takes the form

\displaystyle\left({Df\over Dg}\right)_{\mathbf{a}}=\begin{bmatrix}u(\mathbf{a})&-v(\mathbf{a})\\
v(\mathbf{a})&u(\mathbf{a})\end{bmatrix},\quad\mathbf{a}\in\Gamma,

(4)

for some functions
u,v\colon\Gamma\to\mathbb{R}
.

Matrices of the form (
4
) can be rewritten as
\begin{bmatrix}u&-v\\
v&u\end{bmatrix}=r\begin{bmatrix}\cos\theta&-\sin\theta\\
\sin\theta&\cos\theta\end{bmatrix}
where
r=\sqrt{u^{2}+v^{2}}
and
\theta=\arctan{v\over u}
, showing that the transformation represents a scaled rotation.
Applying the matrix
\begin{bmatrix}u&-v\\
v&u\end{bmatrix}
to an
\mathbb{R}^{2}
vector is equivalent to complex multiplication by
u+\mkern 1.5mu\mathbbold{i}\mkern 1.5muv=re^{\mkern 1.5mu\mathbbold{i}\mkern 1.5mu\theta}
.
Another equivalent characterization of a matrix being a scaled rotation is that its two singular values are equal and its determinant is positive.

Theorem 4
(Continuous quotient of complex functions in arbitrary dimension).

Let
f,g\in C^{1}(\Omega;\mathbb{C})
with simple zeros and a common zero set
\Gamma
. Then the following statements are equivalent:

(i)

There exists a non-vanishing continuous function
\varphi\in C^{0}(\Omega)
such that
f=g\varphi
.

(ii)

f
and
g
are complex linearly related.

We give a proof in
Appendix E
. The following corollary is an immediate consequence of
Theorem 4
.

Corollary 1
.

The notion of complex linearity relation, as defined in
Definition

3
, constitutes an equivalence relation for functions sharing common simple zeros.

Remark 1
.

Higher regularity of
f
and
g
does not, in general, improve the regularity of the quotient in
Theorem 4
. Let us consider the pair
f(x,y)=x+x^{2}+\mkern 1.5mu\mathbbold{i}\mkern 1.5mux+\mkern 1.5mu\mathbbold{i}\mkern 1.5muy
and
g(x,y)=x+\mkern 1.5mu\mathbbold{i}\mkern 1.5mux+\mkern 1.5mu\mathbbold{i}\mkern 1.5muy
given in Example
3
. Its quotient of derivative
Df/Dg
is an identity matrix at
(0,0)
, hence
Theorem 4
applies. But the quotient is merely
C^{0}
although both
f
and
g
are smooth, as illustrated in
Fig.
2
.

Open problem

At this point we are unaware of the exact condition for the existence of a smooth quotient. An obvious sufficient condition is that the complex linearity of
Df/Dg
(
i.e.
, it defines a complex number) extends to an open neighborhood of
\Gamma
. In 2D, this amounts to defining
f
by multiplying a nowhere vanishing holomorphic function with
g
.

This, of course, is not a necessary condition. For example, consider the pair
f(x,y)=(x+\mkern 1.5mu\mathbbold{i}\mkern 1.5muy)e^{\mkern 1.5mu\mathbbold{i}\mkern 1.5mu(x^{2}+y^{2})},g(x,y)=x+\mkern 1.5mu\mathbbold{i}\mkern 1.5muy
, and the pair
f(x,y)=(x+\mkern 1.5mu\mathbbold{i}\mkern 1.5muy)(1+x-\mkern 1.5mu\mathbbold{i}\mkern 1.5muy)
(near the origin) and
g(x,y)=x+\mkern 1.5mu\mathbbold{i}\mkern 1.5muy
. They both have the derivative ratios
Df/Dg
that are complex linear but do not define a complex number anywhere around the origin. Nevertheless, they both yield smooth quotients.
We invite the reader to take up the challenge of finding a sharper condition for the existence of a smooth quotient.

5
Generalizations

The results of Theorems
2
,
3
and
4
remain valid under any smooth change of coordinates in
\Omega\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and do not depend on the Euclidean structure of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Consequently, the domain
\Omega
in these theorems can be replaced by any smooth manifold.

Additionally, the assumption of
C^{\infty}
-smoothness for the given functions
f,g
in all statements throughout this article can be relaxed to
C^{k}
regularity for any
k\geq 1
, with the corresponding regularity of
\varphi
adjusted to
C^{k-1}
accordingly. In fact, the proofs we give in
Appendix A
to
E
are all valid for the general
C^{k}
regularity case.

Appendix A
First Order Taylor Expansion

The smoothness statements in Theorems
1
–
3
follow from the regularity of the remainder terms in the Taylor expansions.

Suppose
f\colon I\to\mathbb{R}
is a continuously differentiable function of one real variable over an open interval
I\ni a
. Then
f
can be expressed using the first-order Taylor formula as
f(x)=f(a)+f^{\prime}(a)(x-a)+(x-a)h(x)
, where the function
h
satisfies
\lim_{x\to a}h(x)=0
. This form of the Taylor remainder is known as
Peano’s form
, first characterized by
Giuseppe Peano in 1889
[
5
,
6
]
.

If we further assume that
f\in C^{\infty}(I)
, then the Peano remainder function
h
is also
C^{\infty}
-smooth. This regularity result is known as Hadamard’s Lemma, which states that
f(x)=f(a)+(x-a)g(x)
for some
g\in C^{\infty}(I)
. An elementary proof of Hadamard’s lemma can be found in
[
4
]
.

More generally, if
f\in C^{k}(I)
for some
k\geq 1
, then the first-order Peano remainder function
h(x)
(or
g(x)=h(x)+f^{\prime}(a)
in the form of Hadamard’s lemma) belongs to
C^{k-1}(I)
. This was shown by Hassler Whitney in 1943
[
7
]
.

Lemma 1
(Whitney 1943
[
7
]
).

Suppose
f\in C^{k}(I)
with
k\geq 1
on an open interval
I\ni 0
. Then there exists a function
g\in C^{k-1}(I)
such that

f(x)=f(0)+xg(x),

with
g^{(j)}(0)={1\over j+1}f^{(j+1)}(0)
for
0\leq j\leq k-1
.

Proof.

For
x\neq 0
, the finite difference quotient
g={f(x)-f(0)\over x}
can be written as
g(x)={1\over x}\int_{0}^{x}f^{\prime}(t)\,dt
. The derivatives of
g(x)
admit the following integral formula:

\displaystyle{d^{j}\over dx^{j}}g(x)={1\over x^{j+1}}\int_{0}^{x}t^{j}f^{(j+1)}(t)\,dt,\quad j=0,\ldots,k-1.

This identity can be verified by repeated integration by parts and mathematical induction; we omit the details here.
Since
f\in C^{k}(I)
, it follows that
g\in C^{k}(I\setminus\{0\})\subset C^{k-1}(I\setminus\{0\})
.
It remains to show that
g^{(j)}(x)
has a limit as
x\to 0
, for
0\leq j\leq k-1
.
Define
L_{j}\coloneqq{1\over j+1}f^{(j+1)}(0)
, which can also be expressed as
L_{j}={1\over x^{j+1}}\int_{0}^{x}t^{j}f^{(j+1)}(0)\,dt
for any
x\neq 0
. Then we compute:

\displaystyle\left|g^{(j)}(x)-L\right|

\displaystyle=\left|{1\over x^{j+1}}\int_{0}^{x}t^{j}\left(f^{(j+1)}(t)-f^{(j+1)}(0)\right)\,dt\right|

\displaystyle\leq\underbrace{{1\over x^{j+1}}\int_{0}^{x}t^{j}\,dt}_{{1\over j+1}}\max_{t\in[0,x]}\left|f^{(j+1)}(t)-f^{(j+1)}(0)\right|.

Since
f^{(j+1)}
is continuous at
0
, this expression tends to zero as
x\to 0
. Therefore, each
g^{(j)}
extends continuously to
x=0
, with
g^{(j)}(0)=L_{j}
. Hence,
g\in C^{k-1}(I)
.
∎

Whitney also extended the result of
Lemma

1
to multidimensions in
[
7
]
. Suppose
f\colon\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
is defined in a neighborhood of the origin. Then by fixing each
x_{2},\ldots,x_{n}
and applying the same argument as
Lemma

1
on the variable
x_{1}
, we obtain

\displaystyle f(x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{n})=f(0,x_{2},\ldots,x_{n})+x_{1}g_{1}(x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{n})

(5)

where
f(0,x_{2},\ldots,x_{n})
is still
C^{k}
and
g_{1}\in C^{k-1}
. The continuity of
{\partial^{i_{1}+\cdots+i_{n}}\over\partial x_{1}^{i_{1}}\cdots\partial x_{n}^{i_{n}}}g_{1}
for
i_{1}+\cdots+i_{n}\leq{k-1}
is the result of applying
Lemma

1
on the function
{\partial^{i_{2}+\cdots+i_{n}}\over\partial x_{2}^{i_{2}}\cdots\partial x_{n}^{i_{n}}}f
.

Repeating the process of (
5
) yields

\displaystyle f(\mathbf{x})=f(\mathbf{0})+x_{1}g_{1}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})+x_{2}g_{2}(x_{2},\ldots,x_{n})+\cdots+x_{n}g_{n}(x_{n})

(6)

where
g_{i}\in C^{k-1}
and
g_{i}(\mathbf{0})={\partial f\over\partial x_{i}}(\mathbf{0})
.

The result (
6
) also holds for vector-valued functions
\mathbf{f}=\begin{bmatrix}f_{1}\\
|\\
f_{m}\end{bmatrix}\colon\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{m}
by applying (
6
) component-wise, which is concluded by the following lemma.
In the lemma we single out the factors
\sum_{i}{\partial\mathbf{f}\over\partial x_{i}}(\mathbf{0})x_{i}=D\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{0})\mathbf{x}
to resemble Peano’s form of Taylor expansion.

Lemma 2
(First-order Taylor formula with regularity).

Let
\mathbf{f}\colon U\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{m}
be a
C^{k}
-continuous vector-valued function on a neighborhood
U
of a given point
\mathbf{a}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
Then there exists
\mathbf{H}\in C^{k-1}(U;\mathbb{R}^{m\times n})
matrix-valued function so that
\mathbf{H}(\mathbf{a})=\mathbf{0}
and

\displaystyle\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{x})=\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{a})+D\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{a})(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{a})+\mathbf{H}(\mathbf{x})(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{a}),\quad\mathbf{x}\in U.

Appendix B

Proof of
Theorem 1
.

Let
\Gamma
be the zero set of
f\in C^{k}(I)
and
g\in C^{k}(I)
. For
x\in I\setminus\Gamma
we define
\varphi
by
\varphi(x)=f(x)/g(x)
, which belongs to
C^{k}(I\setminus\Gamma)\subset C^{k-1}(I\setminus\Gamma)
and non-vanishing. It remains to show that
\varphi
is
C^{k-1}
-smoothly defined in a neighborhood of
\Gamma
.
By
Lemma

1
, near a simple zero

of both
f
and
g
, we can express

f(x)=f^{\prime}(a)(x-a)F(x),\quad g(x)=g^{\prime}(a)(x-a)G(x)

for
x
in a neighborhood

of

, where
F,G\in C^{k-1}(U)

F(a)=G(a)=1
, and both

and

are nonzero on

.
Thus, for

, we define

\varphi(x)\coloneqq\frac{f^{\prime}(a)}{g^{\prime}(a)}\cdot\frac{F(x)}{G(x)},

which is smooth, non-vanishing,

∎

.

\Sigma

. On

\varphi

C^{k-1}

.
∎

Proof of Theorem

.

Consider smooth functions

on the interval

.

,

Appendix E
Proof of Theorem
4

On a 2D domain,
Theorem 4
is a direct consequence of
Theorem 3
.
If
f
and
g
are complex linearly related,
with the quotient of derivative

({Df\over Dg})=\begin{bmatrix}u&-v\\
v&u\end{bmatrix}
with some
u,v\in\mathbb{R}
at each zero,
then (
3
) simplifies to

\displaystyle\lim_{t=0}{f(\gamma(t))\over g(\gamma(t))}={1\over g^{\prime}}(u+\mkern 1.5mu\mathbbold{i}\mkern 1.5muv)g^{\prime}=u+\mkern 1.5mu\mathbbold{i}\mkern 1.5muv

which is a single complex number that depends only on the point
\gamma(0)
and is independent of the path direction
\gamma^{\prime}
. This gives a proof of
Theorem 4
in 2D.

In higher dimensions, however,
Theorem 4
does not automatically follow from
Theorem

3
.
Let us consider any 2D plane which transversely intersects
\Gamma
, on which the situation reduces to the 2D setting discussed above. Hence the quotient function
\varphi
is continuous along any path that intersects
\Gamma
transversely. In addition,
\varphi
is smooth when restricted either to
\varphi|_{\Gamma}
or to
\varphi_{\Omega\setminus\Gamma}
by construction. These properties, however, are not sufficient to conclude the continuity of
\varphi
. In fact, we can construct a function that satisfies all these properties that is still undefined on
\Gamma
, as the following example illustrates.

Example 4
.

Consider a collection of smooth bump functions
\{b_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
on
\mathbb{R}^{3}
such that each
b_{n}
takes its maximum value
1
at the center
(2^{-n},0,2^{1-{n\over 2}})
of its support, given as an open ball of radius
2^{-n}
. Their centers lie on the curve
z=2\sqrt{x}
, and the supports touch
z
-axis, as visualized in the right image.
Using these bump functions, let us define
\varphi(x,y,z)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}b_{n}(x,y,z)
.
Notice that any path intersecting
z
-axis transversely passes through only finitely many of the above balls. Hence, along such paths
\gamma:(-\epsilon,\epsilon)\to\mathbb{R}^{3}
with
\gamma(0)=\mathbf{0}
, we have
\lim_{t\to 0}\varphi(\gamma(t))=0
. However, any curve
(t,0,a\sqrt{t})
with
a>2
, intersects these balls infinitely many times as
t\to 0
, making
\varphi
discontinuous at the origin.

We now prove
Theorem 4
in arbitrary dimension. The key component is the simple zero condition of
f
and
g
. It ensures that the leading order of these functions on
\Omega\setminus\Gamma
consists of the coordinates perpendicular to
\Gamma
. This results in the continuity of the quotient along any path approaching to
\Gamma
, possibly non-transversely, as in Example
4
.

(i)\to(ii)
