Crystalline Structure of Lacrimal Fluid During Goblet Cell Transformation of Squamous Epithelium of Pterygium at Various Clinical Stages
Authors: Zarina Rakisheva, Talgat Omarov, Kenzhekyz Manekenova
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeTo identify the features of the crystalloscopic picture of native tears and goblet cell transformation of squamous epithelial cells of the pterygium at various clinical stages in young patients.Patients and MethodsNative tears and surgical material from 26 patients with different clinical stages of pterygium aged 18–38 years. Tears for crystallography were obtained by capillary collection. Pterygium biopsies obtained intraoperatively were subjected to traditional histological processing with embedding in paraffin blocks with subsequent staining of histological sections with hematoxylin and eosin. Crystalloscopy of lacrimal fluid and microscopy were performed on an Olympus microscope with software for microphotography and image archiving.ResultsCrystallization of native tears of patients with pterygium differs from crystallization of tears of healthy individuals, and the crystalloscopic picture of tears changes as the disease progresses. Destruction of crystalline structures of native tears begins already in the second clinical stage of pterygium, when pronounced goblet cell transformation of squamous epithelial cells develops in the pterygium epithelium. However, as pterygium growth increases to the third and fourth clinical stages, the number of goblet cells decreases, and destructive processes in the crystalloscopic picture of native tears, on the contrary, progress, leading to an increase in the number of mucous inclusions in the crystallogram of native tears. The volume fraction of goblet cells differed significantly between clinical stages (Kruskal–Wallis test, p < 0.05), with the highest values observed at the second clinical stage.ConclusionCrystallization of native tears differs by clinical stage of pterygium and parallels stage-dependent changes in goblet cell distribution. In the second clinical stage, the marked increase in goblet cell volume fraction and early crystal dissociation are hypothesized to reflect altered mucin secretion occurring against a background of chronic inflammation and microcirculatory disturbance; however, as mucin composition and microcirculation were not directly assessed in this study, this interpretation remains speculative and requires biochemical and functional confirmation.Keywords: crystalloscopy, native tears, crystallization, mucin

Introduction

Pterygium is a common disease affecting the ocular surface. It is a fibrovascular proliferation of subconjunctival tissue, which is frequently described as wing-shaped or triangular-shaped and located in the nasal conjunctiva. Risk factors for developing pterygium often include environmental irritants, such as exposure to UV rays and hot, dry weather, as well as genetic predisposition and an impaired immune system.1 Although people can develop pterygium worldwide, the prevalence is higher in the “pterygium belt”, being a region 30 degrees north and south to the equator, and ranges from almost 0.5% up to 30%.2 Patients with pterygium might be asymptomatic or have symptoms of eye discomfort, change in visual acuity, aesthetic problems and corneal irregularities.3 By causing the corneal aberration changes, pterygium results in astigmatism, while further tissue growth can cause a physical overlay of visual fields.4 Moreover, study by Xu et al revealed that patients with pterygium have worse vision in low-light condition rather than natural light.5 Taking into consideration the cosmetic impact and health issues, patients might report anxiety and stress of having pterygium, so it is also associated with a psychological burden.

There are different staging systems used to describe the severity of pterygium. For example, classification according to Verma et al, based on the size of the tissue (Grade I: 0–2 mm, Grade II: 2–4 mm and Grade III >4 mm).6 On the other hand, Tan et al classify pterygium based on morphological composition, considering the presence of episcleral changes, state of tissue itself.7

According to pathological presentation, pterygium can be “benign” (self-limiting), “bad” (recurrent) and ugly (preneoplastic transformation signs).8 Scholars mainly report histopathology of pterygium to be an elastotic fibro-vascular tissue, covered with epithelium.1 Routine hematoxylin–eosin evaluation of resected pterygium tissue would present signs of reactive atypia, solar elastosis, epithelial hyperplasia and dysplastic changes, reflecting the chronic UV exposure and environmental stress. Although pterygium is clinically considered as a mostly benign lesion, dysplasia can still be found in some of the excised specimens, with abnormalities ranging from mild epithelial involvement to near–full–thickness changes approaching carcinoma in situ.8 The interpretation of these findings is complicated by overlapping morphological features. For example, characteristics such as increased nuclear-to-cytoplasmic ratio, loss of polarity and nuclear crowding are common for atypia, adaptive epithelial responses and true dysplasia. The continued use of the term “squamous metaplasia” further adds to the confusion, as the conjunctival epithelium is already a non-keratinised squamous epithelium and inconsistent definitions across impression cytology, morphological criteria and cellular processes contribute to ambiguous classification.8

In case of layer-by-layer analysis, the epithelial cover of pterygium demonstrates morphological variability, with most specimens being covered by conjunctival epithelium, while the advancing head often presents a modified corneal-like squamous morphology.9 Here, pronounced cellular and nuclear polymorphism, particularly within the superficial layers, is frequently accompanied by fibrinoid changes in the underlying lamina propria, forming the pathological substrate of clinically visible Fuchs’ patches. The connective tissue stroma shows a spectrum of changes ranging from newly formed, immature fibrovascular tissue in progressive pterygia to more compact, corneal-like lamellae in stationary lesions, with marked variability in elastotic degeneration attributed to abnormal fibroblastic activity.9 These structural changes are further supported by ultrastructural studies revealing disturbed elastogenesis, accumulation of abnormal elastic fibers and remodeling of extracellular matrix components. Vascularity also varies significantly, with progressive pterygia and those exhibiting Fuchs’ patches demonstrating higher vessel density, increased VEGF expression and more pronounced fibrinoid changes. These features collectively reflect active tissue growth and may contribute to recurrence risk.9

Pterygium development is induced by UV-induced biochemical signaling pathways, particularly the ERK-MAPK and NF-κB pathways. The ERK pathway regulates the proliferation of cells, their migration and apoptosis, contributing to hyperproliferation and invasion in pterygium. The NF-κB pathway, in comparison, plays a role in inflammation, new vessel generation, and epithelial-to-mesenchymal transition (EMT), further facilitating the pathological progression of pterygium.8 DNA damage, including pyrimidine dimers and oxidative stress, also causes the pathogenesis of pterygium. While UV-induced cellular reactions include impaired DNA repair mechanisms, which exacerbate cellular instability, autophagy and apoptosis of the cell.8

Pterygium should be evaluated considering its location, size, vascularity and area it covers. Physically, it has a cap (at the leading point, avascular), a head as a periphery of the cap and body being the rest parts.1 Surgical excision remains the primary treatment for visually significant pterygium, however recurrence continues to be the major postoperative challenge, particularly with the widely used bare sclera technique, which carries high recurrence despite various adjunctive therapies.2 Conjunctival autografting from the superior bulbar conjunctiva has emerged as the standard approach, providing recurrence rates of 2–12%, while more extensive procedures such as P.E.R.F.E.C.T. (Pterygium Extended Removal Followed by Extended Conjunctival Transplantation) have achieved near-zero recurrence. Among current practices, careful excision of the pterygium body and head, thorough Tenon’s removal and precise preparation of a thin, Tenon-free conjunctival autograft anchored with sutures or fibrin glue form the essential steps toward achieving stable anatomical and functional outcomes.2

Crystalloscopy (also termed tear ferning or crystallography) of native tears is a low-cost, non-invasive method based on the principle that the drying of a biological fluid produces a characteristic crystalline pattern that reflects its qualitative composition, including electrolytes, proteins and mucins.10 The tear ferning test has previously been explored as a marker of tear film quality in dry eye disease, where reduced or atypical fern formation is associated with tear film instability and mucin deficiency. To date, however, this method has rarely been applied to pterygium, and its relationship with the underlying histopathological changes of the disease, in particular goblet cell transformation, has not been systematically described. This represents a specific knowledge gap: whether stage-dependent alterations in tear crystallization parallel, and could therefore serve as a non-invasive surrogate marker of, the histopathological changes occurring in the pterygium epithelium. We hypothesized that the crystalloscopic picture of native tears would change progressively with advancing clinical stage of pterygium, in parallel with stage-dependent changes in goblet cell density and morphology. The analysis was restricted to a relatively young adult population (18–38 years) because this age range is when pterygium is most frequently diagnosed and surgically treated in our clinical setting, and because it limits confounding by age-related changes in tear film composition and ocular surface epithelium that are independent of pterygium itself; this restriction should nonetheless be considered when interpreting the generalizability of the findings to older patients.

The aim of the study is to identify the features of the crystalloscopic picture of native tears and goblet cell transformation of squamous epithelial cells of the pterygium at various clinical stages in young patients.

Materials and Methods

This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its later amendments or comparable ethical standards. Ethical approval was granted by the Local Bioethics Committee of NJSC “Astana Medical University” (Astana, Kazakhstan), under Protocol No. 4, dated 20 December 2024 (Ethical Approval Number: Protocol No. 4, 20.12.2024). All participants were fully informed of the aims, methods, anticipated benefits, and potential risks of the study, as well as their right to withdraw at any time without consequence. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to enrollment and sample collection. No identifying information is disclosed in this publication.

The study was conducted between January and December 2024. The study material presents in a form of native tears and surgical material of 26 patients with different clinical stages of pterygium aged 28–38 years, including 14 men and 12 women. Patients were recruited consecutively among those referred for surgical treatment of pterygium; the sample size was determined by the number of consecutive, eligible patients seen during the study period rather than by a priori power calculation, and the resulting distribution of clinical stages was therefore uneven, with the second stage disproportionately represented. This is acknowledged as a limitation affecting the statistical power and generalizability of stage-specific comparisons, particularly for stages III and IV. The first clinical stage of pterygium was diagnosed in 3 patients, the second stage - in 20, the third stage - in 2, and the fourth stage was diagnosed only in one patient of this age group. The clinical stage of pterygium was determined by the degree of spread of the formation to the cornea.

Crystallography

Crystallograms of tears from patients with pterygium were compared with crystallograms of native tears from 10 healthy volunteers aged 28–38 years, recruited among hospital staff and accompanying persons with no clinical signs of pterygium, dry eye disease, active ocular surface or systemic inflammatory disease, and no use of topical ocular medication at the time of sampling. The tears for crystallography were obtained by capillary collection using a thin capillary tube (micropipette) used to collect natural (non-stimulated) tear fluid. A sterile glass capillary tube measuring 85×4 mm was used. When the capillary tube was brought to the outer corner of the lower lacrimal meniscus, without touching the cornea, the tear filled the capillary due to capillary tension. A tear in the amount of 30 μL was placed on a glass slide and dried at room temperature, under standard conditions.10 Crystallographic patterns were classified descriptively according to the morphological criteria of Shabalin and Shatokhina10 into fern-like, snowflake-like and spruce-branch-like crystal types in intact tears, and dissociated, dust-like and amorphous patterns in destroyed crystal zones, as illustrated in Figures 1–15. Two investigators independently reviewed the crystallograms and histological sections; discrepancies were resolved by consensus with a third, senior investigator. A formal statistical assessment of interobserver agreement (eg, kappa statistic) was not performed, which is acknowledged as a limitation of the descriptive morphological assessment.

Histological Analysis

Pterygium biopsies obtained intraoperatively were subjected to traditional histological processing with embedding in paraffin blocks. Histological sections of 5–7 microns thick were made from paraffin blocks and stained with hematoxylin and eosin.11 Microscopic examinations of lacrimal fluid and histological preparations of pterygium were performed on an Olympus microscope with a digital camera.

In order to identify the degree of expression of goblet transformation of cells of the integumentary epithelium in the examined pterygium tissues, the volume fraction of goblet cells in the thickness of the integumentary epithelium in the examined pterygium tissues was determined. Preliminary, a point count of goblet cells was performed using a 100-point ocular grid of Avtandilov G.G. then the volume fraction of goblet cells was calculated using the formula.12

where P1 is the number of points that fall on a separate tissue structure or a separate cell; P2 – total number of test system points. The quantitative indicators obtained in this way were subjected to statistical processing using the IBM SPSS Statistics 31 computer program. Continuous variables are presented as median with interquartile range, reflecting the non-parametric distribution of the data. The nonparametric Kruskal–Wallis criterion was used to assess the statistical reliability of differences in the volume fraction of goblet cells between clinical stages of pterygium; the significance threshold was set at p < 0.05. Given the small number of patients in stages III and IV, formal post-hoc pairwise comparisons were not performed, and stage-specific statistical comparisons should therefore be interpreted with caution.13

Results

Healthy Sample

As a result of microscopic examination of the lacrimal fluid, it was revealed that the crystalline structure of the lacrimal fluid of volunteers without clinical signs of pterygium has a compact fern-like structure, forming a reproducible baseline pattern for further comparison. At the same time, the fern branches are thin, have branches, in places bush-like (Figure 1a and b).

Clinical Stage 1

In patients with pterygium of the first clinical stage, crystallograms of lacrimal fluid revealed snowflake-shaped crystals, compactly located and distributed evenly (Figure 2a), as well as elongated crystals in the form of spruce branches, located almost parallel to each other (Figure 2b).

Among patients with clinical stage 1, in the pterygium tissues, histologically, signs of focal hyperplasia of the cells of the integumentary epithelium with signs of moderate acanthosis were detected (Figure 3). These patterns indicate early changes within organization of tear without significant destruction, which aligns with clinical presentation of the condition.

Goblet cells were distributed unevenly in the thickness of the integumentary epithelium and were located predominantly in the basal layer of the epithelial layer (Figure 4). Goblet cells had clearly defined rounded nuclei and clear, slightly enlarged cytoplasm.

In the thickness of the subepithelial fibrous tissue of the pterygium, signs of mucoid swelling of fibrous fibers and many newly formed thin-walled vessels of the capillary type were determined (Figure 5).

Here the crystallographic and histological characteristics of stage 1 pterygium suggest early epithelial redistribution without depletion of goblet cells, while stroma is activated, but preserved.

Clinical Stage 2

In the second clinical stage of pterygium, snowflake-shaped crystals and branched crystalline structures of native tears underwent certain destructive changes. A violation of the compact and uniform distribution of crystalline structures with the formation of areas of snowflake dissociation was observed. However, the functional significance of these changes cannot be determined from this analysis only.

At the same time, zones of depletion appeared between the crystals with focal accumulations of mucin; these mucin accumulations are indicated by the black arrow in the corresponding figure (Figure 6).

The “fir-tree branch” crystals were also altered: the rhythmic divergence of the lateral branches was disrupted, areas with unilateral or bilateral loss of lateral branches appeared, and empty spaces containing material, morphologically consistent with mucin droplets were observed between the exposed branches of the crystals (Figure 7). Although the biochemical confirmation was not done.

The results of the histological examination of the pterygium of the second clinical stage determined unevenly expressed hyperplasia of the integumentary epithelium on one side of the wing-shaped fibrovascular growth, and on the opposite side, on the contrary, flattening of the integumentary epithelium (Figure 8).

Histological examination of the integumentary epithelium of the second clinical stage pterygium revealed a significant increase in the number of goblet cells, which were located in groups in the middle and superficial layers of the hyperplastic integumentary epithelium, and in the flattened layer of the stratified squamous epithelium they were located superficially and scattered (Figure 9). Quantitatively, this stage represents the highest volume fraction of goblet cells within the epithelium of the pterygium.

In this case, in the thickness of the hyperplastic squamous epithelium, the goblet cells were enlarged in size, with large fine-grained cytoplasm, their nuclei were shifted to the periphery. Goblet cells located in the superficial layer of the flattened squamous epithelium were predominantly small in size, their nuclei were also shifted to the periphery. Subepithelial signs of edema, moderate lymphocytic infiltration, vascular congestion with erythrocyte stasis and sludge in the lumen of the capillaries were noted (Figure 10).

This combination of increased number of goblet cells with changed morphology and disruptions in microcirculation suggest the stage 2 to be a peak of secretory activation with developing inflammatory and vascular stress.

Clinical Stage 3

In cases of the third clinical stage of pterygium, in crystallograms of native tears, against the background of destructive changes in crystalline structures, areas of complete destruction of crystalline structures were found, where they turned into clusters of finely dispersed “dust-like” particles. In the center of such clusters, cavities filled with small particles of crystals were often formed (Figure 11).

Histological examination revealed that in the area of the pterygium head there was a pronounced proliferation of cells of the multilayered squamous epithelium. Groups of goblet cells in the thickness of the layer of integumentary epithelium showed signs of degenerative changes and cytolysis with the formation of cystic cavities containing parietal mucous masses (Figure 12).

A noticeable decrease in the number of goblet cells in the thickness of the integumentary epithelium was noted. This quantitative reduction is the opposite to stage 2 and reflects the transition from hyperplastic pattern to degenerative goblet-cell changes. In the fibrovascular stroma, along with vascular plethora and perivascular sclerosis, signs of chronic inflammation were detected in the form of uneven lymphocytic infiltration with an admixture of histiocytes. Lymphocytic infiltrates in places formed focal accumulations in the subepithelial zone, like lymphoid follicles (Figure 13).

Accordingly, the stage 3 can be considered as a turning point of chronic inflammation and fibroblastic remodeling in addition to decrease in goblet cells and disruption of tear-crystal architecture.

Clinical Stage 4

In the case of clinical stage IV pterygium diagnosed in a 38-year-old female patient, the crystallogram of native tears showed predominantly atypical, branched crystals of varying thickness and configuration, overlapping in places, among which were cruciform crystals, as well as accumulations of mucous masses with an admixture of small crystal particles in areas of their destruction (Figure 14).

At the same time, there were areas represented by small deformed crystals, separated by extensive areas of devastation, filled with homogeneous amorphous masses (Figure 15).

The histological picture of pterygium of the fourth clinical stage was dominated by fibrous-sclerotic changes in the fibrovascular stroma, pronounced sclerotic changes in the vessels (Figure 16) and dystrophic changes in the cells of the integumentary multilayered epithelium. These findings align to chronically remodeled tissue despite the young age of the participants.

Pterygium of the fourth clinical stage: sclerotic changes in the fibrovascular stroma, in the thickness of which “balls” of small sclerotic arteries are determined. Hematoxylin and eosin staining. Magnification x160.

The integumentary epithelium had an uneven thickness with areas of atrophy, was characterized by dystrophy of epithelial cells and, as a rule, pronounced atrophy of the integumentary epithelium was combined with the presence of foci of photoelastosis in the thickness of the fibrovascular stroma of the pterygium (Figure 17).

Black arrows indicate the individual foci of solar (actinic) collagenosis within the stroma. Staining with hematoxylin and eosin. Magnification x100.

Goblet cells were practically not detected in the thickness of the integumentary multilayer epithelium. In the area of the pterygium head, single deformed goblet cells were found, which were located either in the basal or in the superficial layer of cells of the epithelial layer. Wrinkling and deformation of goblet cells and their nuclei were noted (Figure 18).

Pterygium of the fourth clinical stage: the covering epithelium is of uneven thickness, in the basal layer there are single deformed goblet cells. Stained with hematoxylin and eosin. Magnification x160.

As a result of the morphometric study, statistically significant differences in the volume fraction of goblet cells in the thickness of the integumentary epithelium were revealed between clinical stages of pterygium (Kruskal–Wallis test, p < 0.05). Because stages III and IV were represented by only two and one patients, respectively, these stage-specific comparisons should be regarded as exploratory rather than definitive. The results of the morphometric study of the volume fraction of goblet cells in the integumentary epithelium at different stages of pterygium are presented in the form of a diagram (Figure 19).

Volumetric proportions of goblet cells in the pterygium epithelium at different clinical stages. X-axis: clinical stage of pterygium (stages I–IV); Y-axis: volume fraction of goblet cells, Pi (%). Data are presented as group values; differences between stages were assessed using the Kruskal–Wallis test (p < 0.05).

It follows from the diagram data that the highest content of goblet cells in the pterygium covering epithelium is observed in the second stage of the disease, and starting from the 3rd stage, there is a decrease in the volume fraction of goblet cells, and in the 4th stage, this indicator approaches the similar indicator of the pterygium of the first clinical stage. The decrease in the quantitative indicators of goblet cells of the pterygium is apparently associated with progressive fibrous-sclerotic changes in the stromal vessels and degenerative changes in the goblet cells themselves. Overall, results reveal non-linear pattern, where the increase of goblet cells can be observed at stage 2 and progressive reduction at stages 3 and 4.

Discussion

These findings present one of the first detailed stage-stratified description of tear crystallography in addition to epithelial goblet-cell morphology in young patients. Analysis of the results of our studies demonstrates that in healthy individuals aged 28–38 years, the crystallogram of native tears is represented by fern-like structures with the presence of “bush-like” branching of individual processes.

In correlation to fern-like pattern in healthy controls, in patients with the first clinical stage of pterygium, there are no destructive changes in the crystallograms of native tears, but the crystalline structures differ from the tears of healthy individuals in that the crystalloscopic picture is represented by figures in the form of snowflakes and “Christmas tree” branches.

In cases of pterygium of the second clinical stage, the crystalloscopic picture of native tears is characterized by signs of destruction of crystalline structures through their dissociation, and small focal accumulations of mucin appear in the resulting zones of devastation. These findings indicate that tear crystallization patterns are different depending on clinical stage. However, this study cannot evaluate what was the role of these changes in disease progression and whether it was a reflection of epithelial and stromal changes.

So, the stage -specific crystallographic and histological findings show dynamic sequence:

1 stage: there is a morphological reorganization of tear crystals without destruction in addition to epithelial hyperplasia

2 stage: significant hyperplasia of goblet cells and secretory activation in addition to early crystal dissociation and mucin accumulation

3–4 stages: progressive destruction of crystalline structures, inflammatory changes, decrease and degeneration of goblet cells in addition to stromal sclerosis. So, there is a goblet cell change and instability peak of tear crystals.

It should be emphasized that this sequence is inferred from a cross-sectional comparison of different patients grouped by clinical stage, and not from longitudinal follow-up of the same individuals over time. Accordingly, this “dynamic sequence” describes an association between clinical stage and crystallographic/histological pattern, not a demonstrated temporal or causal progression; the same patient was not observed to pass through successive stages, and unmeasured differences between patients at different stages cannot be excluded as an alternative explanation for the observed pattern.

Histologically, the tissues of the pterygium itself show signs of focal proliferation of the integumentary epithelium and uneven distribution of goblet cells, located predominantly in the basal layer of the stratified squamous epithelium. The subepithelial fibrous stroma contains numerous newly formed capillary vessels. Although the increased mucin-containing cells were noted, the study did not analyze the chemical composition of mucin, therefore only morphological, not biochemical abnormality of mucin can be concluded.

In the middle and superficial layers of the pterygium epithelium, histological examination reveals signs of hyperplasia of goblet cells distributed unevenly. In this case, goblet cells change their shape due to the increased mucin content in the cytoplasm, and their nuclei shift to the periphery of the cells. In the fibrovascular stroma of the pterygium, histological features that are morphologically consistent with impaired microcirculation (stasis, lymphocytic aggregation, edema and erythrocyte aggregation) were observed; however, these are static, descriptive histological findings from a single time point, not a functional assessment of microcirculation, and they should not be interpreted as direct evidence that microcirculatory impairment causes or drives the observed changes in goblet cell secretion or tear crystallization. The functional evaluation of microcirculation was not performed in this study.

The third clinical stage of pterygium is characterized by the progression of destructive changes in the crystalline structures of the lacrimal fluid with areas of complete destruction of crystals, the formation of clusters of finely dispersed “dust-like” particles. These structural changes are likely align to change in the tear component, however biochemical confirmation was not conducted. In the center of such clusters, cavities are formed filled with small particles of crystals. Despite the fact that in the area of the head of the pterygium, the proliferation of cells of the integumentary epithelium increases, the number of goblet cells in it is significantly reduced. Degenerative changes in some goblet-cell group were observed, including cytolysis-like appearances, although the detailed mechanisms were not studied. Fibroplastic processes in the stroma of the pterygium and pronounced lymphocytic infiltration with the formation of false lymphoid follicles indicate the development of a chronic inflammatory process in the pterygium. These stage 3 histological findings are based on only two patients; given this very small subgroup, they should be regarded as preliminary and descriptive rather than representative of the third clinical stage in general.

The crystallogram of a native tear of a patient with pterygium of the fourth clinical stage is represented by atypical branched and cruciform crystals of various thickness and configuration, overlapping in places. In the areas of crystal destruction, mucin clusters with an admixture of small crystal particles are found. Destruction of the crystalline structure of the tear is also accompanied by a decrease in size and pronounced deformation of the crystals, which are separated by extensive areas of depletion, filled with homogeneous amorphous masses. Histologically, signs of atrophy of the pterygium epithelium are common, a sharp decrease in the14 number of goblet cells with phenomena of their degeneration is noted. In the fibrovascular stroma, sclerotic changes in the vascular wall of small arteries progress, destruction of collagen fibers occurs with the formation of multiple foci of photoelastosis. These observations are consistent with advanced, chronically remodeled tissue. However, as this study is cross-sectional and the fourth clinical stage was represented by a single patient, these findings are anecdotal, cannot be generalized to stage IV pterygium as a whole, and it is impossible to establish cause-effect relationships between epithelial atrophy, stromal sclerosis and tear-crystal morphology.

Chui et al, 2011 provide typical and common findings in pterygium, being proliferative epithelial front and goblet cell hyperplasia, together with fragmented Bowman’s layer, which is close to Stage 2 described in results, particularly due to goblet cell proliferation. In addition, Stage 1 and 2 derived from patients show proliferative and metaplastic tendencies, but do not show atypia and dysplasia reported by literature. Stage 2 in this study can be characterized by goblet cell hyperplasia and fibrovascular stromal activation, which corresponds with progressive and growing pterygia described by Dzunic et al.9 The literature emphasizes solar elastosis and elastotic degeneration as hallmark findings, but in this study Stage 1–2 samples show no elastosis, suggesting predominantly early-stage or active but non-degenerative disease or younger age of participants. This difference can be partially explained by the fact that patients involved in this study are young.

This study has some limitations, which can be addressed and improved for future research. First, the sample size was relatively small and limited to young adults, which may affect the generalizability of the findings. Next, there was a pronounced uneven distribution of clinical stages (3 patients in stage I, 20 in stage II, 2 in stage III, and only 1 in stage IV); this imbalance is a major limitation, and the stage III and, in particular, stage IV findings described above should be regarded as preliminary observations rather than reliable characterizations of these stages, given the very small number of patients they are based on. From a statistical standpoint, no a priori sample size or power calculation was performed, so the study may be underpowered to detect true differences at the less-represented stages, and non-significant or borderline comparisons for stages III–IV cannot be interpreted as evidence of no difference. Only an omnibus Kruskal–Wallis test was used; formal post-hoc pairwise comparisons with correction for multiple testing were not performed because of the small subgroup sizes, so the specific stage-to-stage comparisons implied in the Results and Discussion are descriptive rather than formally tested and should be interpreted cautiously. The cross-sectional design prevents assessment of how tear crystallography changes over time or after intervention and limits establishment of causal relationships between histological changes and crystallography; the stage-associated pattern described in this manuscript is therefore an association across different patients rather than a demonstrated causal or temporal progression. Prospective follow up of patients after the surgical treatment and without treatment would be an informative for confirmation of changes in crystallographic patterns. Moreover, study focused on morphological features without analyzing biochemical or molecular tear composition, which could provide deeper insight into pterygium pathogenesis; consequently, the interpretations regarding altered mucin properties and impaired microcirculation offered in this manuscript are hypotheses based on morphological observation alone and are not directly demonstrated by the present data. Furthermore, there was no clinical metrics of dry eye such as Schrimer test, therefore the correlation between the crystallography. And ocular-surface function cannot be made. In addition, tear crystallography has no universally standardized criteria for interpretation, so there might be some variations. Finally, there was no measurement of environmental and occupational factors, such as UV exposure.

Future studies should include larger, more diverse populations, longitudinal follow-up, and integration of biochemical analyses to better understand early disease mechanisms and identify potential predictive biomarkers.

Conclusion

Overall, crystallization of native tears of patients with pterygium, aged 18–38 years, differs from crystallization of tears in healthy individuals and the crystalloscopic picture of tears changes as the disease progresses. This study demonstrates stage-associated differences in tear-crystal morphology and distribution of epithelial goblet cells.

Destruction of the crystalline structures of native tears could be observed in the second clinical stage of pterygium, when pronounced goblet cell transformation of squamous epithelial cells develops in the pterygium epithelium. However, as pterygium growth increases to the third and fourth clinical stages, the number of goblet cells decreases, and destructive processes in the crystalloscopic picture of native tears, on the contrary, progress, leading to an increase in the number of mucous inclusions in the crystallogram of native tears.

One possible explanation is that, against the background of microcirculation disorders and in conditions of a chronic inflammatory process, goblet cells of the pterygium in the second clinical stage secrete a greater amount of mucin with altered properties. Such mucin, if confirmed, might in principle disrupt the stability of organic and inorganic components of the lacrimal fluid, with potential aggravation of dry eye manifestations that could in turn contribute to disease progression. However, this remains a hypothesis derived from morphological observation alone: neither mucin composition nor microcirculatory function was directly assessed in this cross-sectional study, and no causal relationship can be established from the present data. Biochemical and functional confirmation in prospective studies is required before this mechanism can be considered established.
