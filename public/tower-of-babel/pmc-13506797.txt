Myocilin drives cardiomyocyte mitochondrial dysfunction via SLC3A2-dependent redox imbalance in heart failure
Authors: Xiaoyue Yu, Junxia Zhang, Junfang Wu, Mingming Zhao, Hongtu Cui, Sen Huang, Jiawen Liu, Minwen Long, Yandong Pei, Aiming Liu, Jinwei Tian, Chen Chen, You-yi Zhang, Er-dan Dong, Han Xiao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Heart failure (HF) remains a leading cause of morbidity and mortality worldwide. A hallmark of HF progression is profound metabolic remodeling accompanied by mitochondrial dysfunction in cardiomyocytes. Impaired mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation, excessive reactive oxygen species (ROS) production, and disrupted redox homeostasis collectively drive oxidative damage and compromise mitochondrial integrity, ultimately leading to contractile failure, for which no viable strategies currently exist. Although mitochondrial dysfunction is now recognized as a central driver of HF pathogenesis, the upstream molecular regulators that initiate or amplify these defects remain incompletely understood. Here, we identify myocilin as a fibroblast-derived mediator that drives cardiomyocyte mitochondrial dysfunction and ROS production in HF. Myocilin was consistently upregulated in patients with HF and in murine HF models induced by transverse aortic constriction and isoproterenol, and was predominantly expressed in cardiac fibroblasts. In vivo study using male mice showed that myocilin overexpression exacerbated cardiac dysfunction and fibrosis, whereas genetic ablation markedly alleviated pathological remodeling. Using transwell systems and recombinant protein stimulation, we found that fibroblast-derived myocilin impaired mitochondrial function in cardiomyocytes, as evidenced by reduced ATP production, increased ROS, and loss of membrane potential. Mechanistically, myocilin directly interacted with SLC3A2, the heavy chain that pairs with SLC7A11 to form the cystine/glutamate antiporter, on cardiomyocytes and promoted its degradation, thereby impairing cystine uptake. This led to glutathione depletion and redox imbalance, subsequently triggering ferroptosis-associated mitochondrial dysfunction in cardiomyocytes. Collectively, these findings identify a fibroblast–cardiomyocyte signaling axis in which myocilin disrupts cardiomyocyte metabolic homeostasis. Targeting the myocilin–SLC3A2 pathway may represent a potential therapeutic strategy for HF.

Introduction

Heart failure (HF) remains a leading cause of morbidity and mortality worldwide despite substantial advances in pharmacological and device-based therapies [1]. A hallmark of HF progression is profound metabolic remodeling accompanied by mitochondrial dysfunction in cardiomyocytes, which precedes overt contractile failure and structural remodeling [[2], [3], [4]]. Impaired mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation, excessive reactive oxygen species (ROS) production, and disrupted redox homeostasis collectively create a vicious cycle that drives oxidative damage, compromises mitochondrial integrity, and ultimately leads to contractile failure [2,5]. Although mitochondrial dysfunction is now recognized as a central driver of HF pathogenesis, the upstream molecular regulators that initiate or amplify these defects remain incompletely understood.

To date, mechanistic studies have predominantly focused on cardiomyocyte-intrinsic pathways. For instance, metabolic imbalances, such as the pyruvate-lactate axis dysregulation driven by reduced mitochondrial pyruvate carrier (MPC) activity and enhanced monocarboxylate transporter 4 (MCT4)-mediated lactate export, perturb substrate utilization from the early stages of HF [6]. Similarly, the DYRK1B-STAT3-PGC-1α axis impairs mitochondrial bioenergetics in failing cardiomyocytes [7]. In the context of obesity-related cardiomyopathy, Ulk1-Rab9-mediated alternative mitophagy constitutes an essential quality-control pathway, the loss of which aggravates both mitochondrial injury and cardiac dysfunction [8,9]. While these studies have illuminated important intracellular regulatory nodes, they do not account for the potential influence of the cardiac microenvironment. Emerging evidence has increasingly highlighted intercellular crosstalk as a critical layer of mitochondrial regulation in the failing heart [[10], [11], [12], [13]]. In particular, cardiac fibroblasts, the most abundant non-cardiomyocyte cell type in the myocardium, are known to secrete a plethora of paracrine factors that can modulate cardiomyocyte function under pathological stress [14,15]. However, the specific contributions of fibroblast-derived secreted factors to cardiomyocyte mitochondrial dysfunction remain largely unexplored.

This gap in knowledge prompted us to search for unrecognized regulators of cardiomyocyte mitochondrial dysfunction in HF. Through a transcriptomic survey of failing hearts, focusing on secreted factors correlated with mitochondrial gene signatures, we identified myocilin, a glycoprotein previously studied primarily in the context of glaucoma and ocular hypertension [16], as a consistently upregulated candidate whose cardiac function has never been explored. Notably, within the heart, myocilin is predominantly expressed by cardiac fibroblasts, raising the possibility of a paracrine effect on neighboring cardiomyocytes.

In the present study, we aimed to determine whether fibroblast-derived myocilin exacerbates cardiomyocyte mitochondrial dysfunction in HF, and to elucidate the underlying mechanistic pathway. Elucidating this intercellular crosstalk may uncover a previously unrecognized layer of redox regulation in the failing heart and provide new opportunities for therapeutic intervention.

Methods

Human subjects

This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Tongji Hospital, Tongji Medical College, Huazhong University of Science and Technology (Wuhan, China) (Approval No. TJ-IRB202401047) and conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and the International Conference on Harmonization Guidelines for Good Clinical Practice. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants. A total of 70 participants were enrolled from Tongji Hospital, consisting of 32 heart failure (HF) patients and 38 non-HF controls. HF was diagnosed according to the 2021 ESC guidelines. The inclusion criteria for the HF group were symptomatic heart failure (NYHA class II–IV), a reduced left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF ≤ 40%), and elevated plasma NT-proBNP levels (>125 pg/mL for chronic HF). The median NT-proBNP level was 3273.0 pg/mL [396.7, 13584.0] in the HF group, compared with 19.0 pg/mL [10.0, 42.7] in the non-HF group. The non-HF controls comprised age- and sex-comparable individuals with no history of heart failure and preserved LVEF (>50%). There were no statistically significant differences in age, sex distribution, and major cardiovascular risk factors (such as hypertension and diabetes) between the two groups. Participants with active infections, malignant tumors, or severe hepatic and renal dysfunction were excluded from the study. Cardiac function was assessed by echocardiography, and LVEF was recorded. Peripheral venous blood samples were collected from fasting participants (≥8 h) via the median cubital vein into EDTA-containing anticoagulant tubes. Plasma was separated by centrifugation at 4000 rpm for 10 min and stored at −80°C until further analysis. Detailed clinical characteristics of the study human cohort are summarized in Table S1.

Animals

All animal experiments were performed in accordance with the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals published by the U.S. National Institutes of Health (NIH Publication No. 85–23, revised 2011). Experimental protocols were approved by the Peking University Animal Ethics Committee (Approval No. PUIRB-LA2023533) and conducted in compliance with ARRIVE guidelines.

Myoc knockout mice

Myoc knockout (Myoc-KO) mice were generated using gene editing technology by Cyagen Biosciences (Suzhou, China). The mouse Myoc gene (NCBI Reference Sequence: NM_010865; Ensembl: ENSMUSG00000026697), located on chromosome 1, consists of three exons, with the coding sequence spanning exons 1–3. To disrupt gene function, a genomic region of approximately 10.5 kb encompassing exons 1–3 was deleted, resulting in the complete removal of the coding sequence. Genotyping was performed by PCR using genomic DNA extracted from mouse tail biopsies. Detailed primer sequences and representative genotyping results are provided in Fig. S4. All mice were maintained on a C57BL/6 N background.

AAV9-mediated gene delivery

For in vivo gene manipulation, adeno-associated virus serotype 9 (AAV9) vectors were obtained from Hanbio Biotechnology (Shanghai, China). For overexpression studies, AAV9 vectors encoding myocilin (AAV9-Myoc) and corresponding negative control vectors (AAV9-NC) were used. For periostin (postn) promoter-driven Myoc knockdown, AAV9 vectors carrying short hairpin RNA (shRNA) targeting Myoc under the control of the postn promoter (AAV9-postn-shMyoc) were employed, with AAV9-NC as a control. The target sequence for Myoc shRNA was 5′-GGACAGCACAGTTCCGAAA-3′. The postn promoter enables preferential targeting to activated cardiac fibroblasts. Eight-week-old male C57BL/6 N mice were administered AAV9 via tail vein injection. Three weeks after viral delivery, mice were subjected to transverse aortic constriction (TAC) or isoproterenol (ISO) treatment to induce cardiac remodeling as described below. Cardiac function and remodeling were assessed at the indicated time points.

TAC -induced heart failure model

Male C57BL/6 N mice (8 weeks old, 24–26 g) were randomly assigned to sham or TAC groups. The sample size was determined based on our previous experience with the TAC model and preliminary experiments, ensuring a sufficient number of animals (n = 6 per group) to achieve statistical significance. To account for potential post-operative mortality, we initially performed surgery on 10 mice per group. Mice were anesthetized by 2% isoflurane exposure, and a thoracotomy was performed to expose the transverse aorta. A 6-0 silk suture was placed around the aorta between the brachiocephalic trunk and the left common carotid artery and tied against a 28-gauge needle, which was subsequently removed to generate a defined constriction, as previously described [[17], [18], [19]] Sham-operated mice underwent the same procedure without ligation. After surgery, the chest was closed, and mice were allowed to recover on a heating pad before being returned to their cages. Cardiac function was assessed by echocardiography 4 weeks after TAC.

ISO-induced heart failure model

Male C57BL/6 N mice (10 weeks old) were randomly assigned to the control or ISO (Sigma–Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) group. Based on our previous experience and preliminary experiments, we initially enrolled 8 mice per group to ensure a final sample size of n = 6 per group for statistical power, accounting for potential attrition during the 8-week injection period. Mice in the ISO group received subcutaneous injections of ISO at 10 mg/kg/day for 8 weeks, whereas control mice received an equal volume of saline. Cardiac function was evaluated by echocardiography before tissue collection.

Echocardiography

Cardiac function was non-invasively evaluated using a Vevo 2100 high-resolution micro-imaging system (Fujifilm VisualSonics, Toronto, ON, Canada) equipped with a high-frequency transducer (MS400, 30 MHz). Mice were anesthetized with 1.5–2.0% isoflurane, and body temperature was maintained at 37°C. During echocardiography, the heart rate was monitored and data were acquired when the heart rate stabilized within the range of 450–550 beats per min (bpm) by adjusting the concentration of isoflurane anesthesia (1–1.5%). Left ventricular (LV) systolic function was assessed via M-mode at the papillary muscle level. Diastolic function was evaluated by Pulse Wave Doppler (PWD) (E and A velocities) and Tissue Doppler Imaging (TDI) (e' and a'velocities) at the mitral valve level. The E/e' ratio was calculated to assess LV filling pressure. All analyses were performed blinded to the experimental groups.

Tissue harvesting

At the designated endpoints (4 weeks after TAC or 8 weeks after ISO administration), mice were euthanized, and hearts were rapidly excised, washed in ice-cold phosphate-buffered saline (PBS, pH 7.4), and gently blotted dry. Each heart was transversely sectioned into three parts using a razor blade: the apex (∼10–15 mg) was snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at −80°C for RNA extraction and quantitative real-time PCR (qPCR); the mid-ventricular section (a 2-3 mm thick ring) was further subdivided: one piece was fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) for 24 h at 4°C and then embedded in paraffin for Masson's trichrome and wheat germ agglutinin (WGA) staining; another piece was embedded in OCT compound (Sakura Finetek) and frozen in liquid nitrogen for subsequent dihydroethidium (DHE) staining to detect reactive oxygen species (ROS); the remaining portion of the mid-ventricle, together with the basal part, was snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at −80°C for protein extraction (Western blot), malondialdehyde (MDA) assay, and ATP measurement.

Masson's trichrome staining

To evaluate myocardial fibrosis, the mid-ventricular tissue was fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, embedded in paraffin, and sectioned at a thickness of 5 μm. Masson's trichrome staining was performed using a commercial kit according to the manufacturer's instructions (Servicebio, Wuhan, China). Briefly, sections were deparaffinized and rehydrated through a graded series of ethanol. The slides were incubated in Masson A solution at 65°C for 30 min, followed by nuclear staining with a mixture of Masson B and C solutions. Subsequently, the sections were stained with Masson D and F solutions to distinguish muscle fibers from collagen. Representative images were captured using a digital pathological slide scanner (Pannoramic MIDI, 3DHISTECH, Hungary). In the stained sections, collagen fibers appeared blue, while myocardium and cytoplasm were red. The degree of cardiac fibrosis was quantified using ImageJ software (NIH, Bethesda, MD, USA). The area of blue-stained collagen was measured and expressed as a percentage of the total myocardial area.

Evaluation of myocardial ROS production

Myocardial ROS levels were assessed using DHE fluorescent probes (G1746, Servicebio, Wuhan, China). Briefly, the heart frozen sections (8 μm thick) were thawed at room temperature and air-dried. A hydrophobic barrier was drawn around the tissue using an IHC pen. The sections were then incubated with DHE working solution (1:500 dilution in PBS) in a humidified dark chamber at 37°C for 30 min. Following incubation, the slides were washed three times with PBS (5 min each) on a shaker at low speed to remove unbound probes. Sections were then mounted with an anti-fluorescence quenching mounting medium containing DAPI (ab104139, Abcam, Cambridge, UK) to visualize nuclei. Images were captured using a digital slide scanner (LG-FS80, Servicebio, Wuhan, China). The red fluorescence intensity was quantified using ImageJ software and expressed as the fold change relative to the control group to reflect the myocardial ROS levels. All procedures were performed under light-shielded conditions to prevent probe photo-oxidation.

Wheat germ agglutinin staining

To evaluate cardiomyocyte hypertrophy, the mid-ventricular paraffinsections (5 μm thick) were stained with WGA to visualize the cell membranes. Briefly, Paraffin sections (5 μm) underwent antigen retrieval in citrate buffer for 15 min. Sections were incubated with Alexa Fluor™ 555-conjugated WGA (5 μg/mL, W32464, Thermo Fisher) at 4°C overnight. After mounting with DAPI-containing medium (ab104139, Abcam), images were captured via fluorescence microscopy. The cross-sectional area of at least 100 transversely sectioned myocytes per heart was quantified using ImageJ software to evaluate hypertrophy.

Measurement of malondialdehyde levels

The MDA concentration in heart tissues was determined using a commercial MDA Assay Kit according to the manufacturer's instructions (M496, Dojindo Laboratories, Kumamoto, Japan). Briefly, snap-frozen LV tissue (approx. 20 mg) was homogenized on ice in PBS containing the antioxidant provided by the assay kit to prevent unintended oxidation. After centrifugation at 10,000 × g for 5 min, the supernatant was collected and reacted with thiobarbituric acid (TBA) at 95°C for 15 min. The fluorescence intensity (Ex/Em 540/590 nm) was measured using a microplate reader (Spark, Tecan Group Ltd., Männedorf, Switzerland). The MDA concentrations were calculated based on a standard curve and normalized to the total protein concentration as determined by a BCA Protein Assay Kit. Final results were expressed as nmol per mg of protein.

Transmission electron microscopy

Small pieces of fresh LV heart tissues (approx. 1 mm3) were rapidly excised and fixed in 2.5% glutaraldehyde in 0.1 M phosphate buffer (pH 7.4) at 4°C for 24 h. After washing with PBS, the samples were post-fixed in 1% osmium tetroxide for 2 h, dehydrated through a graded series of ethanol, and embedded in epoxy resin. Ultrathin sections (60–80 nm) were cut using an ultramicrotome and double-stained with uranyl acetate and lead citrate. The ultrastructure of cardiomyocytes, focusing on mitochondrial morphology (such as cristae density and membrane integrity) and myofibril organization, was observed and captured using a transmission electron microscope (TEM) (HT7700, Hitachi, Tokyo, Japan).

Primary ventricular cardiomyocyte isolation and culture

Adult mouse cardiomyocytes were isolated using a Langendorff perfusion system as described by Kabaeva et al. [20]. Briefly, 8–12-week-old male C57BL/6 N mice were heparinized (100 U per mouse; Sigma–Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) and anesthetized by 2% isoflurane exposure. Hearts were rapidly excised and immersed in ice-cold Tyrode's solution, followed by aortic cannulation and retrograde perfusion at 37°C. The hearts were initially perfused with Ca2+-free Tyrode's solution for 3 min, followed by enzymatic digestion using type II collagenase (300 U/mL; Worthington Biochemical Corporation, Lakewood, NJ, USA). Calcium was gradually reintroduced during digestion until the hearts became pale and flaccid. The ventricles were then dissected, minced, and gently triturated to dissociate single cardiomyocytes. The cell suspension was filtered and allowed to settle by gravity. Ventricular cardiomyocytes were subjected to stepwise calcium reintroduction to a final concentration of approximately 1.4 mM. Cells were plated on laminin-coated culture dishes (10–20 μg/mL; Sigma–Aldrich) and maintained in MEM-based medium (Gibco, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) supplemented with bovine serum albumin (BSA) (0.02%; Sigma–Aldrich), creatine monohydrate (10 mM; Sigma–Aldrich), HEPES (10 mM; Sigma–Aldrich), NaHCO3 (4 mM; Sigma–Aldrich), insulin–transferrin–selenium (1%; Gibco), penicillin/streptomycin (1%; Gibco), and blebbistatin (10 μM; Sigma–Aldrich, freshly added) to inhibit spontaneous contraction.

Isolation and culture of adult mouse cardiac fibroblasts

Adult cardiac fibroblasts were isolated from male C57BL/6 N mice (8 weeks old) by enzymatic digestion. Briefly, fresh ventricular tissue was rapidly excised, minced into small fragments (∼1 mm3), and washed with cold phosphate-buffered saline (PBS; Gibco, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). The tissue fragments were subjected to serial enzymatic digestion using collagenase type II (300 U/mL; Worthington Biochemical Corporation, Lakewood, NJ, USA) at 37°C. Each digestion step lasted 6 min, and the supernatants were collected and immediately neutralized with Dulbecco's modified Eagle's medium (DMEM; Gibco) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; Ausbian, AusGeneX, Brisbane, Australia) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin (Gibco). Collected cells were centrifuged and treated with red blood cell lysis buffer (Beyotime Biotechnology, Shanghai, China) to remove erythrocytes, followed by filtration through a 70 μm cell strainer to obtain a single-cell suspension. Cells were then seeded into culture dishes and maintained in high-glucose DMEM (Gibco) containing 10% FBS and 1% penicillin/streptomycin at 37°C in a humidified atmosphere with 5% CO2. After 24 h, non-adherent cells were removed by medium replacement. The primary cardiac fibroblasts were identified by their rapid adherence to the plastic substrate and their typical spindle-shaped morphology under phase-contrast microscopy. This identification was further validated by immunofluorescence staining for vimentin, a marker of fibroblasts. Cardiac fibroblasts were cultured to 80–90% confluence and passaged for subsequent experiments.

Culture and treatment of cardiomyocyte cell line

The AC16 human cardiomyocyte cell line and HEK-293T cell line were obtained from Procell Life Science & Technology (Wuhan, China). Cells were cultured in DMEM (Gibco, USA) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (TransGen Biotech, Beijing, China) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin at 37°C in a 5% CO2 incubator. Cells were treated with erastin (10 μM, MCE, USA) to induce ferroptosis. For mechanistic studies, AC16 cells were pretreated with ferrostatin-1 (Fer-1) (1 μM, MCE, USA) or stimulated with recombinant human myocilin protein (rh-Myocilin) (10 ng/mL,OriGene, USA) for 24 h. The HEK-293T cell line was utilized for co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP) assays and TurboID-based proximity labeling assays. Cells were maintained in the same culture medium as AC16 cells and were transiently transfected with indicated plasmids using jetPRIME transfection reagent (Polyplus-transfection, Illkirch, France) according to the manufacturer's instructions.

Evaluation of mitochondrial function and oxidative stress

To systematically evaluate mitochondrial function, AC16 cells were labeled with various organelle-specific fluorescent probes. Briefly, intracellular ROS were detected using DCFH-DA (5 μM; MCE). Mitochondrial membrane potential (MMP) was assessed by TMRM (100 nM; Thermo Fisher) or a JC-1 assay kit (5 μM; ab113850, Abcam). For mitochondrial superoxide and morphology, cells were incubated with MitoSOX Red (5 μM; MCE) and MitoTracker Deep Red FM (200 nM; MCE), respectively. Hoechst 33342 (5 μM; Thermo Fisher) was used for nuclear counterstaining. Labeled cells were incubated at 37°C for 30 min in the dark. After washing three times with pre-warmed serum-free medium, live-cell images were immediately captured using a confocal laser scanning microscope (Zeiss LSM 800) or a high-content imaging system (CellVoyager CV8000, Yokogawa, Tokyo, Japan). Fluorescence intensities and ratios (e.g., JC-1 red/green) were quantified using ImageJ software.

Cystine uptake assay

The activity of the cystine/glutamate antiporter was evaluated using a Cystine Uptake Assay Kit (UP05, Dojindo Laboratories, Japan). Briefly, AC16 cells were incubated with cystine analog (CA) uptake solution at 37°C for 30 min. After lysis, a fluorescent probe and a reducing agent were added to react with the internalized CA. Fluorescence intensity was measured at Ex/Em = 490/535 nm. To ensure accuracy, results were normalized to cell counts using the Cell Count Normalization Kit (C544, Dojindo) via Hoechst 33342 (5 μM; Thermo Fisher) staining.

Measurement of GSH/GSSG ratio

Intracellular levels of total glutathione and oxidized glutathione (GSSG) were quantified using the GSH/GSSG-Glo™ Assay (V6611, Promega, USA). Briefly, AC16 cells were lysed with either Total or Oxidized Glutathione Lysis Reagent; the latter contained N-ethylmaleimide (NEM) to rapidly block reduced GSH. Following a 30-min incubation with Luciferin Generation Reagent, Luciferin Detection Reagent was added to initiate the bioluminescent reaction. Luminescence was measured using a microplate reader (Spark, Tecan Group Ltd., Männedorf, Switzerland), and the GSH/GSSG ratio was calculated based on a GSH standard curve.

Western blot analysis

Total protein from heart tissue or cultured cells was extracted using ice-cold RIPA lysis buffer supplemented with a protease and phosphatase inhibitor cocktail (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). The lysates were centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 10 min at 4°C, and the supernatants collected. Protein concentrations were quantified using a BCA protein assay kit (A55864, Thermo Fisher Scientific). Equal amounts of protein samples were separated by denaturing SDS-PAGE (10% or 12% gels) and subsequently transferred onto nitrocellulose (NC) membranes (#HATF00010, Millipore) via electroblotting (200 mA, 60–90 min). The membranes were blocked with 5% nonfat milk at room temperature for 1.5 h and then incubated overnight at 4°C with primary antibodies against Collagen I (Abcam, Cat#ab260043, 1:2000), α-SMA(Abcam, Cat#ab7817, 1:4000), GAPDH (CST, Cat#5174, 1:2000), Myocilin (Proteintec, Cat#60357-1-Ig, 1:1000), Flag (CST, Cat#14793S, 1:2000), Total Oxidative Phosphorylation (OXPHOS) Cocktail (Abcam, Cat#ab110413, 1:5000), GPX4 (Abmart, Cat#T56959,1:2000) or SLC3A2 (Proteintech, Cat#15193-1-AP, 1:2000). The next day, the membranes were incubated with corresponding HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies for 1–2 h at room temperature. Protein bands were visualized using an ECL plus luminescence system and captured by a ChemiDoc™ MP Imaging System (Bio-Rad). The optical density of the bands was quantified using ImageJ software, with GAPDH serving as the internal control for normalization.

Quantitative real-time PCR

Total RNA from heart tissue and cultured cells was isolated using TRIzol reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). Reverse transcription was performed using the TransScript® One-Step gDNA Removal and cDNA Synthesis SuperMix (AT311, TransGen Biotech, Beijing, China). qPCR was subsequently conducted using a SYBR Green-based fluorescent quantitative PCR kit. Specific primers utilized in this investigation were designed and synthesized by Ribobio Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China), and all sequences are provided on Table S2. The relative mRNA expression levels of target genes were normalized to 18S rRNA as the internal control and calculated using the 2-△△Ct method.

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)

The concentrations of NT-proBNP and myocilin in mouse plasma, as well as MYOC levels in human plasma, were quantified using specific ELISA kits according to the manufacturers' instructions. For mouse samples, plasma NT-proBNP was measured using a kit from Elabscience (E-EL-M0834, Wuhan, China), and plasma Myoc levels were detected using a kit from Abbexa (abx534269, Cambridge, UK). For human clinical samples, the concentration of human MYOC in plasma was determined using an ELISA kit from Biorbyt (orb1293312, Cambridge, UK). All assays were performed based on the sandwich ELISA principle. The optical density (OD) was measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader. Target protein concentrations were calculated against a standard curve generated for each assay.

CCK-8 assay

AMCFs were seeded into 96-well plates and subjected to the indicated adenoviral infections and ISO stimulation. Cell viability was subsequently assessed using a Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8; TransGen Biotech, Beijing, China; Cat# FC101-01) according to the manufacturer's instructions. Briefly, the culture medium was removed, and 100 μL of working CCK-8 solution was added to each well in the dark. After incubation for 1-2 h at 37°C, the absorbance at 450 nm was measured using a microplate reader.

EdU assay

Cell proliferation was assessed using a 5-ethynyl-2′-deoxyuridine (EdU) incorporation assay (Beyotime, China; C0075S) according to the manufacturer's instructions. Primary adult mouse cardiac fibroblasts (AMCF) were infected with adenoviral vectors (Ad-Myoc or Ad-NC) and treated as indicated. Cells were incubated with EdU (40 μM) for 2 h prior to fixation. Following incubation, cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde and EdU incorporation was detected using a fluorescent azide-based click chemistry reaction. Nuclei were counterstained with Hoechst 33342. Images were acquired using a high-content imaging system (CellVoyager CV8000, Yokogawa, Japan), and the percentage of EdU-positive cells was quantified as the ratio of EdU-positive nuclei to total nuclei.

Seahorse

Mitochondrial OXPHOS and respiratory capacity were evaluated by measuring the oxygen consumption rate (OCR) in real time using a Seahorse XFe24 extracellular flux analyzer (Agilent Technologies, USA). Briefly, AC16 human cardiomyocytes were seeded in an XF24 cell culture microtiter plate at a density of 5 × 103 cells per well with 10% DMEM. After 24 h of culture, cells were stimulated with BSA or rh-Myocilin (10 ng/mL) for 24 h. On the day before the assay, the XFe24 sensor cartridge was hydrated with Seahorse XF calibration solution at 37°C in a CO2-free incubator overnight.

On the day of the assay, the culture medium was replaced with freshly prepared Seahorse XF DMEM assay medium (Cat#103575-100, Agilent Technologies) supplemented with 10 mM glucose, 2 mM glutamine, and 1 mM pyruvate. The cells were washed twice and equilibrated in 500 μL of the completed assay medium at 37°C in a CO2-free incubator for 1 h prior to measurement. To determine key respiratory parameters, the injection ports of the sensor cartridge were loaded with the following compounds from the Seahorse XF Cell Mito Stress Test Kit (Cat# 103015-100, Agilent Technologies): oligomycin (oligo, 1.5 μM), FCCP (1.0 μM), and rotenone/antimycin A (Rot/AA, 0.5 μM). Individual respiratory parameters, including basal respiration, ATP production, maximal respiration, and spare respiratory capacity, were quantified using Wave software (Agilent Technologies). To eliminate variations in cell number, the OCR values in each well were normalized to the total protein content determined by a BCA Protein Assay Kit.

Transcriptomic data acquisition and analysis

Transcriptomic datasets were integrated from multiple sources, including NCBI's Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) (GSE141910 and GSE62689) (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/) and an in-house transcriptomic dataset derived from a mouse heart failure model induced by subcutaneous ISO injection. The in-house dataset has been deposited in the SRA database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/sra/) (BioProject accession no. PRJNA1458123). Differential expression analysis was conducted using the GENE DENOVO (OmicShare) platform (https://www.omicshare.com/tools/). Additionally, transcriptomic sequencing of AC16 cells treated with rh-Myocilin protein was performed by BGI (Beijing Genomics Institute, China) (BioProject accession no. PRJNA1459041).

Statistical analysis

All quantitative data are presented as mean ± SD (for clinical characteristics) or mean ± SEM (for experimental data). Statistical analyses and graphing were performed using GraphPad Prism 10 (GraphPad Software, USA). Normality of data distribution was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. For normally distributed data, comparisons between two groups were performed using an unpaired Student's t-test or Welch's t-test (in cases of unequal variances). For comparisons among multiple groups, one-way ANOVA was used. When variances were equal, Tukey's post hoc test was applied for all pairwise comparisons or Dunnett's post hoc test was used for comparisons against a control group, as appropriate. When variances were unequal, Welch's ANOVA followed by Dunnett's T3 post hoc test was used. For non-normally distributed data, the Mann-Whitney U test was used for two-group comparisons and the Kruskal–Wallis test followed by Dunn's multiple comparisons test for multiple groups. Data involving two or more independent variables were analyzed using two-way ANOVA followed by Tukey's multiple comparisons test. Categorical variables were presented as numbers (percentages) and compared using Fisher's exact test. All statistical tests were two-tailed, and P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Myocilin is consistently upregulated in heart failure and positively correlates with pathological remodeling

To discover key upstream mediators linking mitochondrial dysfunction to pathological cardiac remodeling, we performed an integrated transcriptomic analysis. We aligned RNA-sequencing (RNA-seq) datasets from human dilated cardiomyopathy (DCM) tissues (GSE141910) with two murine heart failure models: TAC (GSE62689) and ISO infusion (internal dataset) (Fig. 1A). To precisely focus on mitochondrial functional alterations relevant to HF pathology, a mitochondrial-related gene set was obtained from the supplementary materials of Kong et al. [21] and used for subsequent analyses. This dataset was constructed based on GO annotations, anchored by the core term Mitochondrion (GO:0005739) and prioritized energy metabolism-related sub-terms, including Mitochondrial Respiratory Chain complex I (GO:0005747), Complex IV (GO:0005762), and Electron Transport (GO:0006120). By intersecting the differentially expressed genes (DEGs) (|fold change (FC)| > 1.5 and false discovery rate (FDR) < 0.05) from these datasets with the mitochondrial GO list, we identified a small group of shared candidate genes associated with mitochondrial regulation across species and HF models (Fig. 1A). Among the overlapping genes, MYOC, HMGCS2, and GATM were present in the common intersection. However, further comparison of their expression patterns showed that MYOC was the only gene consistently upregulated in all three datasets, whereas HMGCS2 was downregulated in DCM, TAC, and ISO datasets. And GATM displayed less consistent changes across datasets (Fig. 1B). To validate these bioinformatic findings, we established TAC and ISO-induced HF mouse models. Consistent with the transcriptomic data, qPCR analysis confirmed that Myoc mRNA levels were significantly elevated in the hearts of both TAC and ISO-treated mice compared to their respective controls (Fig. 1C and D). Furthermore, Western blotting demonstrated a concomitant increase in myocilin protein levels in the failing hearts (Fig. 1E and F). To further evaluate whether this pathological response is sex-dependent, we also evaluated cardiac myocilin expression in female mice subjected to TAC. Notably, a similar and significant upregulation of myocilin protein was observed in female failing hearts (Fig. S1A), suggesting that the heart failure-associated elevation of myocilin is a consistent phenotype across different sexes. We next evaluated the correlation between Myoc expression and pathological severity. Transcript levels of Myoc were positively correlated with the expression of established cardiac remodeling markers, including Nppa (ANP), Nppb (BNP), Myh7 (β-MHC), and Acta2 (α-SMA), whereas a negative correlation was observed with Myh6 (α-MHC), a marker that is typically downregulated during pathological cardiac remodeling (Fig. 1G). Importantly, since myocilin is a secreted protein, we measured its circulating levels.

Correlation analysis demonstrated that plasma myocilin levels were significantly and positively correlated with NT-proBNP levels, a canonical biomarker of heart failure, in the recruited human cohort and in both TAC- and ISO-induced murine HF models (Fig. 1H–J). Notably, plasma myocilin levels were inversely correlated with left ventricular ejection fraction (EF) across the human cohort and both murine HF models, further supporting its association with cardiac dysfunction (Fig. 1K–M). Detailed baseline characteristics of the human cohort are provided in Table S1. Furthermore, to discern potential sex-specific differences in clinical settings, we performed a sex-stratified analysis of the human cohort. Consistently, plasma myocilin concentrations were significantly and robustly elevated in the HF group compared with the non-HF control group in both male and female subjects (Fig. S1B–C). Collectively, these data identify myocilin as a clinically relevant secreted factor that is upregulated during HF progression and closely associated with pathological remodeling.

Myocilin overexpression exacerbates cardiac remodeling and mitochondrial dysfunction in TAC- and ISO-induced heart failure

We first confirmed successful AAV9-mediated myocilin overexpression. At the histological level, WGA staining revealed that myocilin overexpression markedly amplified cardiomyocyte hypertrophy, as evidenced by a profound increase in cardiomyocyte cross-sectional area compared to controls (Fig. 2E and F). Beyond hypertrophy, Masson's trichrome staining demonstrated severe cardiac fibrosis in the AAV9-Myoc group. (Fig. 2G and H). These structural changes were corroborated by the upregulated mRNA expression of hypertrophic (Nppa, Nppb) and fibrotic (Col1a1, Col3a1) markers (Fig. 2I).

Given the strong correlation between myocilin and mitochondrial signatures identified in our earlier screening, we next examined mitochondrial function and observed significantly reduced cardiac ATP production levels (Fig. 2J). TEM further revealed that mitochondria in control hearts displayed well-organized cristae and intact membranes, whereas those in AAV9-Myoc-treated hearts exhibited marked ultrastructural abnormalities, characterized by decreased mitochondrial area and reduced cristae density (Fig. 2K–M). To further characterize this mitochondrial impairment, we evaluated oxidative stress. DHE staining showed a robust increase in ROS accumulation in myocilin-overexpressing hearts compared to the AAV9-NC group (Fig. 2N and O). Collectively, these gain-of-function data demonstrate that myocilin accelerates heart failure by driving pathological remodeling and promoting mitochondrial oxidative damage.

To assess whether the detrimental effects of myocilin are universal across different etiologies of heart failure, we replicated the gain-of-function experiments in an ISO-induced HF model (Fig. S3A). Consistent with the TAC model, successful AAV9-mediated myocilin overexpression was also confirmed in the ISO model. Endogenous myocilin protein levels likewise exhibited a progressive increase across the four experimental groups, consistent with the aggravated cardiac remodeling (Fig. S2C–D). Notably, AAV9-mediated myocilin overexpression significantly worsened ISO-induced cardiac dysfunction, as evidenced by impaired systolic and diastolic parameters (Fig. S3B–C). At the tissue level, myocilin-overexpressing hearts exhibited more pronounced hypertrophy and fibrosis compared to the control group (Fig. S3D–I). Furthermore, a significant reduction in ATP production (Fig. S3J) was observed in the AAV9-Myoc group under ISO stress, accompanied by severe ultrastructural abnormalities, including decreased mitochondrial area and reduced cristae density (Fig. S3K–M), as well as a robust increase in ROS accumulation (Fig. S3N–O). These supplementary data confirm that myocilin acts as a broad driver of pathological remodeling and mitochondrial dysfunction.

Genetic ablation of myocilin confers broad cardioprotection against TAC- and ISO -induced heart failure

Western blot analysis further verified the absence of myocilin protein expression in the hearts of Myoc-KO mice (Fig. S4D). Histological analysis via WGA staining demonstrated that the pressure overload-induced increase in cardiomyocyte cross-sectional area was significantly attenuated in Myoc-KO hearts (Fig. 3E and F). Moreover, Masson's trichrome staining revealed a marked reduction in cardiac fibrosis in the knockout group compared to WT controls (Fig. 3G and H). We next sought to determine whether myocilin deletion preserves mitochondrial integrity. Consistently, the TAC-induced reduction in cardiac ATP production was significantly attenuated in the knockout group(Fig. 3J). In contrast to the shrunken mitochondria and diminished cristae observed in WT + TAC hearts, mitochondria in Myoc-KO + TAC mice exhibited relatively normal cristae density and ultrastructure (Fig. 3K–M). Concomitantly, DHE staining confirmed that myocilin deficiency markedly attenuated the TAC-induced ROS accumulation (Fig. 3N and O). These loss-of-function data collectively suggest that endogenous myocilin serves as a critical driver of pathological remodeling and mitochondrial dysfunction in heart failure.

To determine whether the cardioprotective benefits of myocilin ablation extend beyond the TAC model, we subjected the Myoc-KO mice and their WT littermates to chronic ISO infusion (Fig. S5A). Strikingly, myocilin deficiency afforded profound resilience to ISO-induced heart failure. Echocardiography assessment revealed preserved systolic and diastolic function in Myoc-KO mice compared to WT controls (Fig. S5B–C). Furthermore, the genetic ablation of myocilin substantially mitigated ISO-induced cardiac hypertrophy and cardiac fibrosis (Fig. S5D–I). Importantly, consistent with observations in the TAC model, Myoc-KO mice maintained cardiac ATP production (Fig. S5J), were highly resistant to ISO-induced mitochondrial structural damage (Fig. S5K–M), and exhibited significantly reduced ROS accumulation (Fig. S5N–O). These supplementary data further reinforce the conclusion that endogenous myocilin is a fundamental driver of heart failure progression across different pathological stimuli.

Myocilin is predominantly expressed in cardiac fibroblasts but does not drive fibroblast-to-myofibroblast transdifferentiation

Based on the established role of myocilin in HF progression, we next sought to identify its cellular origin. Analysis of a human heart single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) dataset (GSE183852) revealed that MYOC expression was predominantly enriched in the fibroblast cluster, and its expression is upregulated in DCM patients, with minimal detection in cardiomyocytes, endothelial cells, or immune cells, as shown by UMAP clustering and cell type annotation (Fig. 4A and B). To validate this expression pattern in situ, we performed immunofluorescence co-staining on mouse heart sections. Myocilin signals strongly co-localized with the fibroblast marker Vimentin, while spatially distinct from the cardiomyocyte marker α-actinin (Fig. 4C). This fibroblast-specific enrichment was further confirmed by Western blot analysis of primary cells isolated from adult WT mouse hearts, which showed marked higher myocilin protein levels in adult mouse cardiac fibroblasts (AMCF) compared to adult mouse cardiomyocytes (AMCM) (Fig. 4D). These findings suggest that myocilin is predominantly derived from cardiac fibroblasts and may act as a secreted mediator in the heart. To examine whether myocilin secretion was inducible,we treated AMCF with ISO. ISO stimulation significantly upregulated myocilin protein levels in both the cell lysate and the culture supernatant, indicating that myocilin secretion is inducible under ISO stimulation (Fig. 4E). We next examined the effects of myocilin in cardiac fibroblasts. Western blot analysis confirmed efficient myocilin overexpression in AMCF (Fig. S6A–B). Fibroblast-derived myocilin directly impairs cardiomyocyte mitochondrial function via paracrine signaling

Building on our observation that myocilin does not directly drive fibroblast-to-myofibroblast transdifferentiation but instead promotes fibroblast proliferation, we next investigated whether cardiac fibroblasts influence cardiomyocyte function via paracrine signaling. Using a transwell co-culture system, AMCF were cultured in the upper chamber, while AMCM were seeded in the lower chamber, allowing exchange of soluble factors without direct cell–cell contact (Fig. S7A). Under ISO stimulation, AMCM exhibited a marked reduction in mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS)-related gene expression, i.e., those encoding mitochondrial respiratory complexes I–V. Notably, co-culture with AMCF further exacerbated this reduction compared with AMCM cultured alone (Fig. S7B), indicating that fibroblast-derived factors aggravate cardiomyocyte mitochondrial dysfunction under stress conditions.

To determine whether fibroblast-derived myocilin functions as a paracrine mediator of cardiomyocyte injury, we established a similar transwell system using AMCF and AMCM isolated from either WT or Myoc-KO mice (Fig. 5A). Strikingly, following ISO stimulation, the transcriptional downregulation of key mitochondrial OXPHOS genes, i.e., those encoding mitochondrial respiratory complexes I–V in AMCM was observed exclusively upon co-cultured with WT AMCFs. In contrast, co-culture with Myoc-KO AMCFs markedly attenuated this ISO-induced suppression of OXPHOS gene expression, regardless of whether the cardiomyocytes were WT or Myoc-KO (Fig. S8A). To further verify these findings at the protein level, we examined representative subunits from complexes I–V by Western blot. Consistent with the mRNA profiles, ISO-induced downregulation of these subunits in cardiomyocytes was largely prevented when co-cultured with Myoc-KO AMCFs, in contrast to WT AMCFs (Fig. 5B). To determine whether myocilin directly affects cardiomyocytes, we treated AC16 human cardiomyocytes with recombinant human myocilin protein (rh-Myocilin) (Fig. 5C). rh-Myocilin treatment significantly reduced intracellular ATP levels at concentrations ≥5 ng/mL (Fig. 5D) and downregulated the same OXPHOS complex subunits (Fig. S8B), indicating impaired cellular energy metabolism. Consistently, the suppressive effect of extracellular myocilin was further confirmed at the protein level, as direct incubation with rh-Myocilin successfully recapitulated the down-regulation of all five mitochondrial respiratory complex subunits compared to the BSA control (Fig. 5E). Seahorse OCR analysis showed that rh-Myocilin significantly decreased basal respiration, ATP production, maximal respiration, and spare respiratory capacity in AC16 human cardiomyocytes, confirming that extracellular myocilin directly impairs mitochondrial bioenergetic function (Fig. 5F). Consistent with this, JC-1 staining revealed a marked reduction in the red-to-green fluorescence ratio, reflecting loss of mitochondrial membrane potential (Fig. 5G), which was further confirmed by reduced TMRM fluorescence (Fig. S8C). In addition, MitoSOX staining with MitoTracker labeling revealed a significant increase in mitochondrial superoxide production (Fig. 5H), accompanied by elevated intracellular ROS levels as detected by DCFH-DA fluorescence (Fig. S8D). Collectively, these results indicate that myocilin directly impairs mitochondrial function and redox homeostasis in cardiomyocytes.

Fibroblast-derived myocilin knockdown preserves redox homeostasis and attenuates mitochondrial dysfunction in pressure-overloaded hearts

To assess the in vivo contribution of fibroblast-derived myocilin to cardiac redox dysregulation, we employed a postn promoter-driven gene silencing strategy that preferentially targets activated fibroblasts. An AAV9 vector encoding a shRNA targeting myocilin under the control of the postn promoter (AAV9-postn-shMyoc) was generated to target activated fibroblasts. Mice were injected with AAV9-postn-shMyoc for 3 weeks prior to sham or TAC surgery, followed by 4 weeks of pressure overload (Fig. 6A). Efficient knockdown of myocilin by AAV9-postn-shMyoc was confirmed by Western blot analysis under TAC conditions (Fig. S9), whereas no significant reduction was observed in sham hearts, consistent with the injury-inducible activity of the postn promoter [22,23]. Targeted silencing of myocilin attenuated TAC-induced cardiac dysfunction and mitochondrial oxidative stress, phenocopying the protective effects observed in global Myoc-KO mice. Echocardiographic assessment four weeks post-TAC showed that, compared with AAV9-NC-treated mice, mice treated with AAV9-postn-shMyoc exhibited improved systolic and diastolic function (Fig. 6B and C). Gravimetric and histological analyses further demonstrated that this fibroblast-targeted intervention attenuated TAC-induced cardiac hypertrophy and reduced collagen deposition (Fig. 6D–H), accompanied by attenuated induction of hypertrophic (Nppa, Nppb) and fibrotic (Col1a1, Col3a1) marker gene expression (Fig. 6I). Consistent with improved cardiac function, AAV9-postn-shMyoc treatment maintained cardiac ATP levels (Fig. 6J), preserved mitochondrial ultrastructural integrity (Fig. 6K–M), and reduced ROS accumulation (Fig. 6N and O). To determine whether ferroptosis contributes to myocilin-induced cardiomyocyte injury, we first assessed cell viability in the presence of the ferroptosis inducer erastin as a positive control. Similar to erastin treatment, rh-Myocilin treatment significantly reduced cell viability(Fig. 7D). To further confirm the involvement of ferroptosis, we performed rescue experiments using the ferroptosis-specific inhibitor ferrostatin-1 (Fer-1). The rh-Myocilin-induced reduction in cell viability was partially but significantly rescued by Fer-1 treatment (Fig. S10B), supporting the role of ferroptosis in this process. Consistently, rh-Myocilin treatment decreased the GSH/GSSG ratio (Fig. 7E) and reduced cystine uptake (Fig. 7F), suggesting functional impairment of the system xc−, a cystine/glutamate antiporter composed of SLC7A11 and SLC3A2 that is essential for cystine uptake and glutathione biosynthesis [24,25].

To validate these findings in vivo, we assessed lipid peroxidation levels in different mouse models. Myocilin overexpression (AAV9-Myoc) increased MDA levels under TAC, whereas myocilin knockout (Myoc-KO) or postn promoter-driven Myoc knockdown (AAV9-postn-shMyoc) attenuated TAC-induced lipid peroxidation (Fig. 7G). Given that GPX4 is a central regulator of ferroptosis, we next examined its expression. rh-Myocilin reduced GPX4 protein levels in vitro (Fig. 7H). Consistently, in vivo myocilin overexpression (validated by Western blot, Fig. S2) decreased GPX4 expression (Fig. 7I), and myocilin deficiency or postn promoter-driven Myoc knockdown increased GPX4 levels (Fig. 7J). Collectively, these findings indicate that myocilin impairs the system xc−–GSH–GPX4 antioxidant axis, leading to enhanced lipid peroxidation and ferroptosis in cardiomyocytes.

To further assess the potential interaction between myocilin and SLC3A2, molecular docking analysis was performed, predicting a favorable binding interface with multiple potential interaction residues (Fig. 8C). This interaction was subsequently examined by co-immunoprecipitation assays. SLC3A2 was detected in myocilin immunoprecipitates, supporting an association between myocilin and SLC3A2 (Fig. 8D).

We next examined whether myocilin regulates SLC3A2 expression. Western blot analysis showed that rh-Myocilin treatment reduced SLC3A2 protein levels (Fig. 8E), whereas qPCR analysis revealed no significant change in SLC3A2 mRNA expression (Fig. 8F), suggesting that myocilin regulates SLC3A2 at the post-transcriptional level. To determine whether myocilin affects SLC3A2 protein stability, cycloheximide (CHX) chase assays were performed. SLC3A2 protein levels declined more rapidly in the presence of rh-Myocilin compared with CHX treatment alone (Fig. 8G), indicating that myocilin accelerates SLC3A2 protein degradation.

Consistent with these findings, in vivo analyses showed that myocilin overexpression (AAV9-Myoc) decreased SLC3A2 protein levels under TAC conditions (Fig. 8H), whereas Slc3a2 mRNA levels remained unchanged (Fig. 8I). Conversely, postn promoter-driven Myoc knockdown (AAV9-postn-shMyoc) significantly increased SLC3A2 protein expression without significantly affecting its mRNA levels (Fig. 8J and K).

Collectively, these results demonstrate that myocilin interacts with SLC3A2 and promotes its protein degradation, thereby reducing its stability.

Discussion

In this study, we identified myocilin as a previously unrecognized regulator of mitochondrial dysfunction in heart failure. Through integrative transcriptomic screening and multi-model validation, we demonstrated that myocilin is markedly upregulated in failing hearts and originates predominantly from cardiac fibroblasts. Using loss- and gain-of-function approaches in vivo, we established that myocilin exacerbates TAC-induced cardiac hypertrophy, fibrosis, and contractile dysfunction, whereas its genetic ablation confers protection. Mechanistically, we uncovered that secreted myocilin binds directly to SLC3A2, a membrane transporter in cardiomyocytes, and promotes its degradation. This disruption impairs system xc-function, which normally mediated cystine uptake, thereby reducing cysteine availability for GSH synthesis. As a result, antioxidant capacity is compromised, culminating in mitochondrial dysfunction. These findings address a critical gap in understanding how non-myocyte-derived signals contribute to mitochondrial impairment in cardiomyocytes, and establish the myocilin–SLC3A2 axis as a novel mediator of redox dysregulation in HF.

Myocilin, encoded by the MYOC gene, was initially identified in association with glaucoma. Pathogenic mutations in this gene lead to protein misfolding and endoplasmic reticulum stress, thereby causing trabecular meshwork dysfunction and elevated intraocular pressure [26]. Although myocilin is highly expressed in the trabecular meshwork and can be upregulated by glucocorticoids [27], it is also distributed across various tissues throughout the body, suggesting that it may have broader biological functions. Notably, myocilin is a secreted protein, and while traditional research has focused primarily on its role in the eye, its biological functions beyond the ocular system, particularly its potential impact on cellular stress, proteostasis, and mitochondrial function, are increasingly gaining attention [28]. Based on this, the present study investigates the regulatory role of myocilin in mitochondrial dysfunction in cardiomyocytes and its potential involvement in the pathogenesis of heart failure.

The cellular origin of myocilin in the heart warrants particular attention. While previous studies have focused on cardiomyocyte-intrinsic mechanisms of mitochondrial dysfunction, our results highlight cardiac fibroblasts as an unexpected source of a paracrine factor that profoundly influences cardiomyocyte redox status. This fibroblast-to-cardiomyocyte signaling axis expands the current understanding of intercellular communication in the failing heart, complementing established paradigms centered on ECM remodeling and inflammatory mediators [29,30]. The observation that myocilin secretion is enhanced under pathological stimuli such as TAC and isoproterenol further suggests that it may serve as a stress-responsive signal integrating neurohumoral activation with metabolic dysfunction. These findings align with an emerging paradigm repositioning fibroblasts as active regulators of metabolic and redox remodeling rather than merely structural cells. Recent studies have demonstrated that fibroblast-derived factors, including IGF-2, FGF-2, and exosomal miRNAs, directly modulate cardiomyocyte substrate utilization and mitochondrial function [[31], [32], [33]]. Our identification of myocilin adds a new dimension to this conceptual shift, positioning fibroblasts as gatekeepers of redox homeostasis and underscoring the importance of considering non-myocyte populations when investigating the molecular basis of HF progression. However, the incomplete restoration of OXPHOS gene expression following myocilin deletion in AMCFs under ISO stimulation suggests that myocilin-independent mechanisms may also contribute to mitochondrial suppression. This residual effect may be attributed, at least in part, to the direct actions of ISO on cardiomyocytes through sustained β-adrenergic stress, which can independently impair mitochondrial function [[34], [35], [36], [37]].

The heart is highly dependent on mitochondrial function to meet its substantial energy demands. Mitochondria occupy approximately 30% to 40% of cardiomyocyte volume and generate roughly 90% of the ATP required for sustained contraction through OXPHOS, a process that inevitably produces ROS as a byproduct [38,39]. In HF, mitochondrial homeostasis is profoundly disrupted. Key pathological features include reduced mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) content, impaired activity of respiratory chain complexes, and excessive oxidative stress, all of which compromise ATP production and exacerbate cellular injury [4,40,41]. Furthermore, mitochondrial dynamics, the balance between fusion and fission, is critically dysregulated in HF, typically shifting toward excessive fission [[42], [43], [44]]. This is accompanied by decreased expression of fusion mediators such as OPA1 and MFN2, and aberrant activation of the fission regulator DRP1, resulting in mitochondrial fragmentation and bioenergetic failure [[45], [46], [47], [48], [49]]. Given that antioxidant defense mechanisms are essential for preserving mitochondrial integrity, disruption of GSH homeostasis, like impairment of the system xc− transporter, can severely compromise the capacity to counteract oxidative stress [[50], [51], [52]], thereby accelerating mitochondrial dysfunction and the progression of HF [53,54]. These observations support the concept that pathways regulating redox homeostasis and mitochondrial quality control are intimately linked to the pathogenesis of heart failure.

At the mechanistic level, our study reveals a direct link between extracellular myocilin signaling and intracellular antioxidant capacity. The identification of SLC3A2 as a cardiomyocyte binding partner for myocilin provides a molecular explanation for the observed redox imbalance. SLC3A2, as the heavy chain of system xc−, is essential for cystine import and subsequent glutathione synthesis [55]. SLC3A2 forms a functional heterodimeric complex with SLC7A11, the light chain of system xc−, and this interaction is essential for cystine/glutamate exchange activity [24,25]. By interfering with this transporter, myocilin impairs the cell's primary defense against oxidative stress, leading to excessive ROS accumulation, mitochondrial membrane potential dissipation, and ATP depletion. This mechanism aligns with the growing recognition that amino acid transport and metabolism, particularly the cystine–glutamate antiporter system xc−–GSH–GPX4 axis, are critical determinants of cellular redox homeostasis and ferroptosis susceptibility. Notably, the myocilin–SLC3A2 interaction represents a previously uncharacterized mode of regulation for system xc−. The system has traditionally been studied in the context of cancer and neurodegenerative diseases, but rarely in cardiovascular pathology. In oncology, system xc− has emerged as a key regulator of ferroptosis and chemotherapy resistance [[55], [56], [57]]; in neuroscience, it modulates ischemia-reperfusion injury and neurodegeneration. Our findings extend this landscape into cardiovascular disease, establishing a mechanistic link between amino acid transport dysfunction and myocardial redox imbalance, which contribute to HF progression [[55], [56], [57], [58]]. This non-genomic mode of redox regulation, whereby an extracellular secreted factor directly modulates amino acid transporter stability, differs fundamentally from classical receptor-kinase cascades activating Nrf2 or NF-κB transcriptional programs. By bypassing gene expression changes, myocilin enables rapid suppression of antioxidant capacity within hours of pathological stress, complementing slower transcriptional adaptations. This mechanism resembles cytokine-induced trafficking of metabolite transporters (e.g., TNF-α-mediated GLUT4 internalization) and highlights previously unrecognized post-translational redox control in cardiomyocytes.

From a broader perspective, this work contributes to an emerging paradigm in which secreted factors from non-myocyte cells directly modulate cardiomyocyte metabolism and mitochondrial function. Our findings reveal that, in contrast to its established role as an intracellular protein aggregate in glaucoma pathogenesis, myocilin in the heart functions as a fibroblast-derived paracrine signal that disrupts cardiomyocyte redox homeostasis without directly inducing fibroblast transdifferentiation. This tissue-specific functional dichotomy, whereby a single protein exerts divergent pathogenic mechanisms in distinct organs, adds to the growing recognition of moonlighting functions for canonical disease-associated proteins and raises intriguing questions about potential cross-organ crosstalk between the heart and the eye under pathological conditions. From a therapeutic perspective, targeting fibroblast-derived myocilin—via neutralizing antibodies, small molecules disrupting its interaction with SLC3A2, or fibroblast-specific gene silencing, may offer a complementary strategy to conventional approaches that focus exclusively on cardiomyocyte-autonomous pathways. The fibroblast origin of myocilin offers a potential advantage: targeting a non-cardiomyocyte population may avoid direct interference with cardiomyocyte function and reduce on-target cardiotoxicity.

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, while we have identified SLC3A2 as a direct interactor of myocilin, the precise binding interface and the structural basis of their interaction remain to be elucidated. Second, our experiments were performed in rodent models, and translational validation in human cardiac tissue and primary human cells will be essential to establish clinical relevance. Third, although we validated the consistent pathological elevation of myocilin in female TAC model and both sexes in human cohort, our in vivo genetic and viral manipulations were performed exclusively in male mice. Future studies using female interventional models are warranted to fully delineate potential sex-specific regulatory networks. Fourth, although we focused on the myocilin–SLC3A2 axis, we cannot exclude the possibility that myocilin exerts additional effects through other binding partners or signaling pathways. Fifth, given the physiological role of myocilin in the eye and the widespread expression of SLC3A2 in tissues, including the blood-brain barrier and kidney, the potential on-target/off-tissue effects of systemic myocilin inhibition warrant careful consideration in future therapeutic development. Future studies employing structural biology approaches to map the myocilin-SLC3A2 interface, human iPSC-derived cardiomyocyte-fibroblast co-culture systems to validate mechanistic findings and longitudinal patient cohort analyses to assess biomarker utility will be necessary to address these questions and advance toward clinical translation.

In conclusion, this study identifies myocilin as a fibroblast-derived paracrine factor that disrupts cardiomyocyte redox homeostasis through SLC3A2-dependent inhibition of cystine transport and glutathione synthesis (Fig. 9). The myocilin-SLC3A2 axis represents a novel mechanism of intercellular communication in the failing heart and a potential therapeutic target for restoring mitochondrial function and mitigating HF progression.
