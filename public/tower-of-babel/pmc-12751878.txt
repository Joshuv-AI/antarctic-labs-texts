China precision nutrition biobank: protocol of a prospective cohort study on diet, human phenotype/genotype, and early-onset chronic diseases
Authors: Jingjing He, Miao Xie, Jia Liu, Yu Dong, Huiyu Chen, Ran Wang, Weili Xu, Keji Li, Zhihong Fan, J. Alfredo Martinez, Anne Raben, Fazheng Ren, Jie Guo, Ruixin Zhu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe global burden of early-onset chronic diseases, especially early-onset type 2 diabetes, is increasing, particularly in China. Diet is a key factor and emerging evidence highlights substantial inter-individual variability in metabolic responses to diets, highlighting the need for precision nutrition.MethodsThe China Precision Nutrition Biobank (CPNB) is a prospective, longitudinal, cohort study designed to investigate diet-phenotype/genotype interactions and develop precision dietary strategies for early prevention and intervention of chronic diseases, with a particular focus on early-onset diseases. CPNB consists of three phases: the alpha (pilot cohort), beta (transition cohort), and gamma (main cohort) phases. Approximately 200, 1450, and 20,000 adults aged 18–40 years from urban and rural areas in China including Beijing, one city each in Heilongjiang, Shandong, Zhejiang, Guangxi, and Hainan provinces, and one or more villages each in Henan, Gansu, Sichuan, Zhejiang, and Hunan provinces will be recruited during the alpha, beta, and gamma phases, respectively, between 2025 and 2035. Sociodemographic information, medical records, read-time weighed food records and corresponding continuous glucose monitor (CGM) readings, objective physical activity, food challenges, genes, gut and oral microbiota, metabolites from blood, stool, urine, saliva, and hair, and questionnaires will be collected at baseline survey. The follow-up survey will be conducted every five years to repeat these assessments until participants’ death (the follow-up period may extend up to 80 years). Outcomes of interest are common early- and late-onset chronic diseases and their preclinical stages.DiscussionThe CPNB data can be used to develop prediction models for personalized metabolic responses and risks of early-onset chronic diseases among Chinese people. It will also provide new evidence on interactions of diet with human phenotypes/genotypes during preclinical stage, onset, and progression of early-onset diseases. CPNB aims to inform the development of precision nutrition strategies aligned with the principles of predictive, personalized, preventive, and participatory medicine in the Chinese population.Trial registrationCPNB was registered at Chinese Clinical Trial Registry (http://www.chictr.org.cn/) on June 3rd, 2025, under the registration number ChiCTR2500103621.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12937-025-01255-w.

Background

Globally, the burden of disease is projected to shift to chronic non-communicable diseases between 2022 and 2050 [1]. Chronic diseases, particularly type 2 diabetes, cardiovascular disease, chronic kidney disease, cognitive disorders, and cancer, are leading causes of global deaths [1]. China has a large population and the burden of chronic diseases is heavier than that of most regions in the world [2, 3]. Early-onset chronic diseases, especially early-onset type 2 diabetes, defined as diagnosis before age of 40 [4] or 45 years [5], among young people have recently emerged as a major public health issue in China and around the world [4, 6–11]. From 1990 to 2021, the age-standardized rate of incidence of early-onset type 2 diabetes in China increased from 140.20 to 315.97 per 100,000 [12]. Therefore, there is an urgent need for prevention and intervention of chronic diseases in early adulthood based on scientific evidence.

Unhealthy dietary patterns are major drivers of the onset and development of multiple chronic diseases [13]. High intakes of red and processed meat and sugar-sweetened beverages as well as low intakes of whole grains are major attributable risk factors for disability-adjusted life years of early-onset type 2 diabetes in China [12]. Dietary interventions are effective in prevention and treatment of chronic diseases [14]. Emerging evidence, however, demonstrates substantial inter-individual heterogeneity in responses to diets and conventional healthful diets may not be optimal for all, highlighting the potential of precision nutrition to enhance the effectiveness of dietary interventions [15]. Prediction models for personalized postprandial glycemic response (PGR), accounting for personal characteristics especially gut microbiota, have been developed based on large populations from Israel, the UK, and the US [15, 16]. To date, there are no prediction models based on large Chinese general population data, though the Westlake Precision Birth Cohort study aimed to develop personalized dietary recommendations for Chinese women with gestational diabetes mellitus [17]. In addition, the existing models do not account for food properties and processing [18].

The association of diets with chronic diseases is influenced by personal characteristics (e.g., phenotypes and genotypes) [19]. The gene-diet interaction has been observed by existing large-scale cohorts such as the US All of Us project, the UK Biobank, the European Prospective Investigation into Cancer and Nutrition cohort study (EPIC), the China Kadoorie Biobank, the US Nurses’ Health Study, and the US Health Professionals Follow-up Study [20–23]. However, most of these studies rely on 24-hour dietary recall or food frequency questionnaires (FFQ), which are subject to recall bias and measurement error, thereby limiting the precision of dietary exposure assessment [24]. In addition, most existing precision medicine cohorts focus on middle-aged and older populations, restricting the ability to study early-onset chronic diseases, especially early-onset type 2 diabetes, and limiting insight into diet and biomarker dynamics during the health-to-disease transition.

Long-term follow-up of large-scale cohorts involving young and middle-aged adults with comprehensive baseline data (e.g., weighed food records, continuous glucose monitoring (CGM), genes, microbiota, metabolites, and food challenges) enables in-depth exploration of interactions of diets and phenotype/genotype during preclinical stage, onset, and progression of chronic diseases, especially early-onset diseases, moving beyond the narrow focus on acute postprandial response. In China, there is a need to establish a prospective cohort to explore diet-phenotype/genotype interactions and develop precision nutrition strategies for early prevention and intervention of multiple chronic diseases, especially early-onset diseases (e.g., early-onset type 2 diabetes), which may benefit from well-designed machine learning approaches.

Study objectives

The China Precision Nutrition Biobank (CPNB) is a prospective, longitudinal, cohort study of the general population, designed to advance precision nutrition research in China. The objectives of CPNB are as follows:

To develop prediction models for personalized metabolic responses to foods and risks of multiple chronic diseases, especially early-onset diseases (e.g., early-onset type 2 diabetes), in Chinese people and to explore the associations of CGM-derived glycemic patterns and metrics with preclinical stage, onset, and progression of chronic diseases, especially early-onset diseases (Work package 1).To investigate the associations of food/dietary intake with preclinical stage, onset, and progression of multiple chronic diseases, especially early-onset diseases (e.g., early-onset type 2 diabetes). Dynamics prediction models for disease risks will be developed. In addition, baseline and long-term changes in lifestyle and dietary patterns of participants from different geographical regions of China will also be explored (Work package 2).To explore the role of genotype, phenotype, and environmental and social factors in the associations of food/dietary intake with preclinical stage, onset, and progression of multiple chronic diseases, especially early-onset diseases. For genotype, the salivary amylase gene copy number and polymorphism of metabolic regulatory genes are major focuses. Methods

Study design and setting

The CPNB project is a three-phase, multi-center, prospective, longitudinal, cohort study. CPNB aims to explore the interaction between foods/dietary patterns and human phenotype/genotype in the preclinical stage and progression of multiple chronic diseases, especially early-onset diseases (e.g., early-onset type 2 diabetes) in China. CPNB consists of three phases: an alpha phase (n = 200 participants) followed by a beta phase (n = 1450 participants) followed by a gamma phase (over 20,000 participants).

In the alpha phase, we will conduct a small-scale, initial engagement, and pilot cohort study (the pilot cohort) to set up assessment centers, preliminarily test the feasibility of the baseline survey workflow, and preliminarily develop the prediction model for personalized PGR. The data collected during the alpha phase will not be integrated to the CPNB database and the samples will be discarded after use and will not be stored for a long time. During the beta phase, we will establish a cohort study (the transition cohort) for final testing and we will further fix the workflow. In the gamma phase, we will conduct a large-scale cohort study (the main cohort) and officially recruit participants from multiple cities of China. Data from the beta and the gamma phases will be stored at the CPNB database. The alpha and the beta phases will be conducted in Beijing (the West Campus and the East Campus of China Agricultural University), the capital of China. The gamma phase will be conducted in a manner similar to the China Kadoorie Biobank, with sampling sites established in both urban and rural areas in China including Beijing, one city each in Heilongjiang, Shandong, Zhejiang, Guangxi, and Hainan provinces, and one or more villages each in Henan, Gansu, Sichuan, Zhejiang, and Hunan provinces.

The CPNB protocol (version 1.0, March 10th) was prepared according to study protocol guidelines and checklists (i.e., the SPIRIT checklist) (Supplementary Table 1). CPNB will be carried out in full compliance with the relevant requirements of the latest version of the Declaration of Helsinki (75th WMA General Assembly, Helsinki, Finland, October 2024), and the International Conference on Harmonization for Good Clinical Practice (ICH-GCP) to the extent that this is possible and relevant. As the maximum validity period of the ethical approval is 5 years, we applied and obtained the ethical approval from the Human Research Ethics Committee of China Agricultural University for the alpha and the beta phases of CPNB only (No. CAUHR-20250410 on 15th April, 2025). An ethics coordinator will be responsible for continuously monitoring updates to relevant ethical guidelines and regulations in China. In addition, an annual review mechanism will be established to regularly assess and update data return protocol. The updated protocol will re-approved by the Human Research Ethics Committee of China Agricultural University. The CPNB alpha and beta phases were registered at Chinese Clinical Trial Registry (http://www.chictr.org.cn/) on June 3rd, 2025, under the registration number ChiCTR2500103621. Prior to launching the gamma phase, additional ethical approval, clinical trial registration, and authorization from the Human Genetic Resources Administration of China will be obtained.

Enrollment

The project flow is shown in Fig. 1. Participants will be recruited using multiple methods (e.g., posters, social media platforms such as Xiaohongshu and Weibo, and direct contact with primary and occupational health care providers). CPNB aims to be broadly representative of the target adult population. While we do not enforce a strict 1:1 enrollment ratio, we will set sex-specific recruitment targets based on the demographic profile (e.g., age and sex distribution) of the source population. Moreover, as dietary patterns, especially the consumption of fresh fruits, vegetables, and other seasonal foods, may vary significantly across different seasons, we will employ a continuous enrollment strategy throughout the entire year and ensure that the number of participants recruited in each season is approximately equal to minimize the potential bias introduced by seasonal variation. Additionally, we will schedule follow-up assessments for each participant in the same season as their baseline assessment to maintain consistency. Interested individuals will be contacted for the screening interview. In the interview, the inclusion and exclusion criteria will be queried. Potential participants will be given written and oral information.

Sample size

Sample size calculation was based on PASS software (version 15, NCSS, Utah, USA) and R (version 4.3.0, R Project for Statistical Computing). Since the alpha phase is a pilot study, we do not calculate the sample size for this phase. During the alpha phase, 200 participants will be recruited from Beijing (Fig. 1) and 150 of them will be recruited from the West Campus of China Agricultural University, serving as the training dataset for the PGR prediction model, and 50 of them will be recruited from the East Campus of China Agricultural University, serving as the test set for external validation of the prediction model. For the beta and gamma phases, the sample size was estimated based on the primary outcome (i.e., early-onset type 2 diabetes) and the requirement for developing a prediction model. The final sample size was determined by adopting the larger value from the two calculation methods. According to a previous cohort study [25], assuming that the hazard ratio is 1.43, the incidence rate of the non-exposed group is 13%, and the dropout rate is 10%, a total of 1463 participants need to be recruited for each of the two phases. According to a previous interventional study [26], assuming that R2 is 0.54, a total of 80 features are included, the intercept is 2237.5, and the standard deviation is 917.6, a total of 90 participants (i.e., 897 meals) need to be recruited as a training set for each of the two phases. Therefore, approximately 1450 participants will be enrolled from Beijing in the beta phase (approximately 1150 from the West Campus of China Agricultural University and approximately 300 from the East Campus of China Agricultural University). In the gamma phase, around 20,000 participants will be recruited from multiple cities in China. The number of participants recruited in the beta and gamma phases is sufficient to explore the association between dietary patterns and the risk of early-onset type 2 diabetes and to establish a prediction model. Also, the number of participants recruited in the alpha phase meets the requirements for establishing the prediction model. If feasible, we will use other cohorts, such as the PREDICT 1 study [27], as an external validation set to test the generalizability of the prediction model.

Participants’ sociodemographic information and exposures

Participants’ basic information (e.g., demographics and socioeconomic status data), examination data (e.g., anthropometric data), biological specimens, dietary data, physical activity data, sleep data, CGM data, and questionnaire data will be collected at baseline survey and each re-survey (Fig. 2; Table 1). Demographics and socioeconomic status data include sex, age, place of birth, population mobility, length of residence, gender identity and sexual orientation, education, employment, retirement, siblings, marital status, home ownership, family size, family income, social support, family vehicles, cooking methods at home, handedness, smoking status, second-hand and third-hand smoking, indoor air pollution, pesticide use, alcohol consumption, tea drinking, coffee drinking, food allergy, nutritional environment (e.g., food availability and affordability, dietary norms, nutrition literacy, etc.), use of electronic devices, use of substance (i.e., alcohol use disorder and tobacco use disorder), etc. Medical information includes medical history and family history, medication use (preventive use of aspirin included), vaccination (COVID-19 vaccines and other vaccines included), history of COVID-19 and other infections, allergy history, reproductive history, reproductive health (e.g., age of menarche, age of menopause, artificial assisted reproduction, etc.), early life experiences, and weight history (e.g., birth weight, body weight-one year ago, body weight-five years ago, body weight-ten years ago), current health status, health insurance, etc.

Anthropometric and examination data include height, sitting height, body weight, waist circumference, hip circumference, head circumference, thigh circumference, fat percentage, basal metabolic rate, grip strength, resting blood pressure, heart rate, etc. If permitted, facial and tongue images will be collected. As well as anthropometric data, the examination data also include vision and visual impairment tests, hearing tests, olfactory and gustatory tests, and helicobacter pylori tests. Biological specimens include fasting blood samples (blood routine test, blood biochemistry, metabolomics, allergens, environmental pollutants, etc.) and oral glucose tolerance test (blood glucose and insulin from the antecubital vein at 0 and 120 min), urine samples (routine urine test and metabolomics), stool samples (gut microbiota and metabolomics), saliva samples (salivary amylase content, salivary amylase activity, whole exome sequencing or whole genome sequencing, salivary amylase gene copy number, polymorphism of digestive enzyme genes, epigenomics, proteomics, transcriptomics, etc.), oral microbiota, and hair samples.

A detailed, step-by-step standard operating procedures (SOP) for collection, processing, and temporary storage of biospecimens will be established. Blood routine tests will be conducted immediately upon collection at each assessment center. Other blood samples will be drawn into pre-chilled vacuum tubes containing specific preservatives appropriate for different analyses (e.g., EDTA tubes for targeted metabolomics). Tubes will be transported on ice to the lab and processed within a strict 2-hour window. They will be centrifuged under refrigerated conditions (4 °C) to separate plasma/serum, buffy coat, and red blood cells. Only plasma and serum will be aliquoted. Aliquots of plasma will be snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen and then transferred to long-term storage at −80 °C or in liquid nitrogen to ensure stability of labile metabolites over decades. In CPNB, we will primarily employ liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry-based untargeted metabolomics for broad hypothesis generation, complemented by targeted mass spectrometry assays for precise quantification of key metabolites related to nutritional status. In terms of fecal samples, participants will be provided with a standardized, anaerobic sample collection kit that stabilizes the microbial composition at ambient temperature for several days. This eliminates the immediate need for freezing and facilitates transportation. Multiple aliquots will be prepared and immediately stored at −80 °C in freezers. We will employ 16 S rRNA sequencing for large-scale profiling due to its cost-effectiveness, complemented by shotgun metagenomics in 1450 participants from the beta phase to gain deeper functional insights. As for the hair, we only preserved the hair shafts (the hair strands) rather than the hair follicles (the roots). According to China’s “Implementation Rules for the Management of Human Genetic Resources”, samples (including urine, feces, serum, and plasma) that may contain trace amounts of cells or genetic material are no longer classified as human genetic resources materials. Samples containing genetic information (i.e., saliva samples) will be transported to Biobank of Life Science Park (with the license of preservation of human genetic resources) or other institutions for long-term storage. Other samples not containing genetic information will be initially stored at −80℃ at each center and then will be transported to China Agricultural University or Biobank of Life Science Park (tentatively) for long-term storage. For the alpha phase, the samples will be discarded after use and will not be stored long-term. Samples will be tested centrally if needed. All samples will remain within the territory of the People’s Republic of China and will not be transferred abroad. CGM readings and PGR to food challenges (i.e., glucose, sucrose, fructose, and starch or starchy foods, each containing 50 g of available carbohydrates) will be recorded. Objective, device-measured physical activity and sleep data will be collected. Participants will be asked to record dietary intake and eating time in real time using a weighing scale. Participants will be advised to take photos of every meal, if applicable. The diet information will be entered into a nutrition calculator for further analysis.

Valid questionnaires for Chinese population include physical activity questionnaires (e.g., the modified Chinese Baecke questionnaire and the international physical activity questionnaire), gastrointestinal function questionnaires (e.g., functional dyspepsia, functional constipation, irritable bowel syndrome, etc.), the cognitive function questionnaire (the Montreal Cognitive Assessment scale), eating behavior and early life nutrition status questionnaires (e.g., three-factor eating questionnaire), emotional questionnaires (e.g., stress [the perceived stress scale and the depression anxiety and stress scale-21], depression [the Beck depression rating scale], anxiety [the self-rating anxiety scale and the Beck anxiety inventory], sleep [the Pittsburgh sleep quality index], loneliness [the De Jong Gierveld loneliness scale], quality of life [The World Health Organization Quality of Life], etc.). The questionnaires also include olfactory, gustatory, and visual questionnaires, renal function questionnaires, oral health and dental questionnaires (e.g., masticatory function), etc. Other emerging exposures will be collected as well.

Outcomes

Primary outcome

The primary outcome of interest is early-onset type 2 diabetes, defined as diagnosis before age of 40 years [4]. In sensitivity analysis, early-onset type 2 diabetes is defined as diagnosis before age of 45 years [5]. The occurrence of the primary outcome will be ascertained by a panel of medical doctors and epidemiologists via physical examination as well as medical records from outpatient and inpatient service at each re-survey.

Secondary outcomes

Secondary outcomes of interest are early-onset and late-onset chronic diseases including insulin resistance, prediabetes, and late-onset type 2 diabetes, hyperlipidemia, bone loss, prehypertension, pre-mild cognitive impairment, pre-chronic kidney disease, pre-chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, hyperuricemia, mild cognitive impairment, and subclinical hypothyroidism, overweight/obesity, hypertension (early-onset and late-onset), metabolic liver disease (early-onset and late-onset), polycystic ovary syndrome, osteoporosis (early-onset and late-onset), cardiovascular and cerebrovascular diseases (early-onset and late-onset), chronic kidney disease (early-onset and late-onset), chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (early-onset and late-onset), gout (early-onset and late-onset), frailty, sarcopenia, thyroid diseases (Hashimoto’s thyroiditis included), dementia (early-onset and late-onset), female and male reproductive and offspring-related diseases, insomnia, depression, anxiety, cancer, other emerging chronic diseases, and death. The occurrence of the above secondary outcomes will be ascertained by a panel of medical doctors and epidemiologists via physical examination, medical records from outpatient and inpatient service, and death certificate at each re-survey.

The diagnostic criteria for the primary and secondary outcomes will adhere to Chinese and international clinical guidelines. Any deaths occurring among participants are coded using the 11th International Classification of Diseases (ICD-11) by trained staff. Premature death is defined as deaths occurring before the age of 70 years according to the World Health Organization criteria. Causes of death and premature death from official death certificates will be supplemented, if necessary, by review of medical records.

Study procedure

Eligibility screening

We will screen potential participants who are interested in the research using an online screening survey based on the inclusion and exclusion criteria. Individuals eligible for participation will receive an invitation from an assessment center.

Baseline survey

The baseline survey and data collection for each participant will last for 30 days. The schedule is shown in Fig. 3; Table 1. At day 1, eligible participants will be asked to bring their unique national identity cards to the assessment center at 8.00–8.30 a.m. after fasting for at least 10 h. In addition to meeting the inclusion/exclusion criteria, participants enrolled in the baseline survey must meet the following conditions: no use of antibiotics or probiotics within the preceding three months, no current pregnancy, and not currently undergoing fertility treatments. If participants do not meet the above two conditions at the time, the baseline survey can be conducted once both conditions are met. Then, participants will be asked to provide an informed consent form, which allows access to participants’ medical records and long-term storage of biological specimens for anonymized and non-specified medical research purposes. After providing the informed consent form, each participant will be given a personal profile and assigned an eight-digit code for CPNB identification. Then, participants will provide their stool sample collected in the early morning, sit for at least 15 min, and have their baseline information and data collected. For each participant, a 25 mL fasting blood sample drawn from their antecubital vein will be collected and an oral glucose tolerance test with 75 g of glucose will be conducted. Trained staff will instruct participants to complete the electronic questionnaires and examinations. Anthropometry will be measured and basal metabolic rate will be estimated using bioelectric impedance analysis. Participants will have lunch at the assessment center and be instructed how to use weighing scales and conventional household measurements to record the dietary intake in detail (e.g., type of foods and cooking methods) by trained staff. Participants will get timely feedback on accuracy of food records from the staff. Before leaving the assessment center, participants will be given a scale for weighing foods. In addition, they will wear a sleep and heart rate variability monitoring band on their wrist, an accelerometer on their waist, and a CGM on their arm. From day 2 to day 14, participants will not be required to visit the assessment center, but they will be asked to wear the CGM, the sleep and heart rate variability monitoring band and the accelerometer every day and 14-day CGM, sleep, heart rate variability, and physical activity data will be recorded. From day 3 to day 11, participants will be asked to record foods and diets consumed every day. CGM data and dietary data collected between days 3 and 11 will be used for development and validation of the prediction model for personalized PGR. At day 12 and day 14, participants will be provided 50 g of glucose (food challenges) and asked to drink glucose with 250 mL of water at 8.00–8.30.00.30 a.m. after fasting for at least 10 h. Participants will sit for at least 3 h after the food challenge. The PGR after food challenges will be recorded by CGM. At day 16, participants will come to the assessment center again at 8.00–8.30 a.m. after fasting for at least 10 h, sit for at least 15 min, and a 25 mL fasting blood sample drawn from each participant’s antecubital vein. Participants will return their scanner and the scanner will be connected to the computer and the CGM data will be exported. Participants will wear a new CGM on their arm and another 14-day CGM data will be recorded. From days 18–29, participants will have food challenges. The will instructed to consume 50 g of carbohydrates (fructose, sucrose, and starch, each repeated once, in a random order) with 250 mL of water at 8.00 a.m. every other day and sit for at least 3 h. The PGR after food challenges will be recorded by CGM. At day 30, participants will come to the center and return the scanner. The scanner will be connected to the computer and CGM data will be exported. If funding permits, we will provide participants with meals for 8–10 days in the alpha phase, along with an additional CGM to reduce food record-related bias/errors in development of prediction models.

Re-survey and long-term follow-up

Re-survey will be conducted every five years (Table 1), while the food frequency questionnaire will be completed every year. If funding permits, we will shorten the follow-up interval to three years. Data collection and survey procedures are much the same as those in the baseline survey, except for CGM and food challenges. If funding permits, we will conduct repeated CGM monitoring and food challenges at each re-survey for the approximately 1450 participants recruited in the beta phase. The participants will be followed up until death.

Data management and monitoring

Governance structure

CPNB is led by Dr Ruixin Zhu at China Agricultural University, in collaboration with Dr Jie Guo and Dr Jingjing He at China Agricultural University. Dr Ruixin Zhu is also served as the project coordinator. Dr Ruixin Zhu, Dr Jie Guo, and Dr Jingjing He constitute the Steering Committee, who are responsible for the funding, decision-making, coordination, and regulation. The Domestic Advisory Committee consists of Professor Zhihong Fan, Professor Keji Li, Professor Fazheng Ren, and other potential professionals. The International Advisory Committee consists of Professor J. Alfredo Martinez, Professor Anne Raben, Professor Weili Xu, and other potential professionals. The Domestic Advisory Committee members are responsible for giving advice and suggestions to the study protocol, SOP, and research papers. The Data Management Committee comprises a group of experts in computer science, medical statistics, and data science; Dr Miao Xie will take charge in development of the central project database platform, data management, quality control, and data storage; Dr Jie Guo and Dr Yu Dong will be responsible for data analysis. The Outcome Ascertainment Committee consists of medical doctors and epidemiologists. The internal Oversight Committee is responsible for evaluation of data collection and quality control (e.g., checking if the sample collection, packaging, processing, and analyzing meet the standards). The external Oversight Committee (i.e., the Human Ethics Committee) is in charge of protection of human rights and the well-being of participants. Moreover, specific working groups are formed to discuss and agree on questions related to diet, CGM, genomics and microbiome, and other methodological and medical issues.

Data management

The REDCap system or other data management systems will be used for participant registration, follow-up, and sociodemographic and questionnaire data collection. The participants’ diet, exercise, and sleep data will recorded using an AI Agent developed by our team. A key feature of this Agent is its integration with a comprehensive knowledge base of Chinese food composition, which enables the accurate quantification of intakes of nutrients and foods from diverse Chinese diets. This approach ensured the collection of high-quality, contextually relevant lifestyle data. In addition, a central project database platform (cloud storage) will be developed. The platform will enable data sharing and transmission across multiple assessment centers. All data will be stored in the central project database at China Agricultural University. The central project database will ensure standardized handling and storing of data and the possibility for easy data extraction. All questionnaires will be collected by the questionnaire delivery platform and participants will enter their own data into the platform. Experts in computer science and data science will back up and encrypt the data and strip the privacy data.

Data availability

Except for data involving genetic information, all other data shall be accessible for use and analysis by all members within the CPNB collaborative group. Data related to genetic information shall only be used and analyzed by Chinese members or, in compliance with the Regulations of the People’s Republic of China on the Management of Human Genetic Resources, by international members. External researchers must obtain approval by submitting a study protocol for data access. Internal reviewers will assess whether deidentified, non-genetic data can be released.

Quality control and monitoring

The Steering Committee and the Data Management Committee are collaborating intensively to ensure data collection of high-quality and consistency of study procedures and data collection across all assessment centers. Data quality control will be implemented throughout the entire study. SOP (including food records, CGM, genome and microbiome, outcome ascertainment, packaging, storage, transportation of samples, data collection, management, quality control, and storage, and other methodological and medical issues) will be created by professionals according to the documents and materials from the China National Standardization Management Committee and the International Organization for Standardization. The study procedures across all assessment centers will be in line with the study protocol and SOP. The SOP will be reviewed by the Steering Committee and the Advisory Committee members and revised as needed. New SOP will be prepared, if necessary. Staff members from each assessment center will participate in several training sessions on study procedures before the commencement of CPNB. All examination instruments will be centrally commissioned to reduce systematic bias and ensure the accuracy, stability, and comparability of data collected. The core personnel from each center will meet annually at a three-day general assembly for the full CPNB consortium. For the nationwide gamma phase, we will use a centralized coordination and regional execution framework, which includes mandatory, standardized training for all regional center staff, the use of dietary assessment tools that combine a key national food list with region-specific items, and a secured logistics network with contingency plans for sample transport. Furthermore, a rigorous multi-level quality control system, including regular audits and real-time data checks, will be employed to continuously monitor. Efforts will be made to ensure data homogeneity and quality across all assessment centers.

Prior to its use and distribution, the questionnaire’s reliability and validity will be confirmed. Data logic checks will be performed to identify outliers, missing values, abnormal distribution ratios, and logical inconsistencies in the data. A randomized sampling approach will be employed to select representative centers and samples for data quality control and logic checks.

Privacy and security protection

Regarding privacy protection, we will make every effort to prevent identification of individuals or unauthorized exposure of sensitive data. Participants will receive documents explaining how their data will be used, stored, shared, and protected. Informed consent forms must be obtained from participants prior to data collection. Participants will be de-identified by replacing their name with a unique identification number. In addition, only essential information/data closely related to the aims of this study will be collected. Given the sensitivity of genetic and microbiome data, only validated incidental findings with clear clinical actionability, for which participants have explicitly opted-in during the informed consent process, will be considered for return. Specifically, during the informed consent process, participants will be informed that the majority of genetic and microbiome data will be used for research only, and individual reports will not be provided. Feedback will only be considered in rare circumstances (e.g., a pathogenic variant in genes associated with hereditary cancer risk, such as BRCA1/2). An independent expert committee comprising genetic counselors, clinicians, and ethicists will be established to determine which specific findings meet the criteria for feedback. The return of results will be conducted through professional genetic counseling sessions to ensure participants completely understand the implications and limitations of the findings and receive appropriate guidance for subsequent medical consultation.

For security protection, in order to protect cohort data from unauthorized tampering, leaks, or destruction, the technical and administrative controls will be implemented. For instance, we will use encryption technology and data will be transformed into unreadable formats using algorithms such as AES-256 for stored data and TLS/SSL protocols for data being transferred. Furthermore, data access permissions will be role-restricted. We will ensure only authorized personnel can view specific datasets. Audit logs will be created to maintain detailed records of all data interactions, including who access what data and when.

Statistical analysis

The results will be presented as means with standard deviation for approximately normally-distributed variables, medians with interquartile range for non-normally distributed variables, and frequency with percentage for categorical variables. To address the misaligned measurement timepoints, we define time zero as the baseline survey date for each individual participant. For the alpha and the beta phases, Cox proportional hazards regression models will be used to calculate the hazard ratio and 95% confidence interval for the association of exposures especially dietary variables with outcomes of interest. The interaction term of phenotypes/genotypes with dietary variables will be added into the models. Proportional hazards assumptions will be checked statistically using Schoenfeld residuals and visually using Kaplan-Meier curves. As the gamma phase of this study will expand across China, the clustering effect of participants from urban and rural areas and diverse geographical regions (such as different provinces) will be accounted for using fragile models or generalized mixed linear models with provinces as random effects. If applicable, we will employ age-period-cohort models and compare the outcomes with those derived from alternative models. Sex, age, and phases will be adjusted for as covariates in all pooled analyses as well as sex- and age-stratified analyses to investigate potential effect modification. The season of the baseline survey will be included as a categorical covariate in models to account for residual seasonal effects on dietary reporting and health status. A formal attrition bias analysis will be conducted after each re-survey cycle. Specifically, the baseline characteristics (e.g., genetic, socioeconomic, and health) of those who are lost versus those who remain, will be compared to quantify and adjust for this potential bias.

Models for prediction of glycemic responses to foods will be developed using classic machine learning models (e.g., XGBoost) or deep learning models. Dynamics prediction models for disease risks will be developed using advanced modeling approaches (e.g., joint models, landmark models, dynamic Bayesian networks, temporal convolutional networks, recurrent neural networks, and attention-based Transformer, etc.). Novel subtypes of chronic diseases will be identified using classic machine learning models (e.g., XGBoost) or deep learning models. All statistical analyses will be performed using SAS software (version 9.4, SAS Institute, Cary, NC), Python (version 3.8.10, Python Software Foundation), and R (version 4.3.0, R Project for Statistical Computing). All P values will be two-sided, and statistical significance will be defined as P < 0.05.

Discussion

We initiate the CPNB project, a three-phase, multi-center, prospective cohort study targeting Chinese young and middle-aged adults. Given that the prevalence of chronic diseases is increasing in young people in China and traditional healthy dietary patterns cannot benefit all populations, our project is designed for precision nutrition and early prevention and intervention of chronic diseases, especially early-onset diseases. To be more specific, to explore the interaction between diet and phenotype/genotype, identify specific subgroups, and develop precision dietary recommendations for early prevention and intervention of chronic diseases. Our project aims to address contemporary medial challenges and carries substantial public health implications.

Globally, early-onset chronic diseases are a growing global health problem among young individuals. In particular, there is a greater burden of early-onset type 2 diabetes, obesity, cardiovascular disease, chronic kidney disease, cancer, dementia, and gout [8–11, 28–30]. Generally, early-onset chronic diseases present with a more aggressive disease progression and are associated with poorer quality of life, more severe complications, and shorter life expectancy with lower life quality compared with late-onset diseases [8–11, 28, 30]. There is an urgent need to prevent early-onset chronic diseases. Genetic predisposition and lifestyle, especially diets, have a vital role to play [31]. Precision nutrition, based on personal characteristics such as genetic make-up, may help prevent and reduce the burden of early-onset chronic diabetes [32]. Prospective cohort studies serve as an excellent approach to investigating gene-nutrition interactions in the development and progression of chronic diseases. Nonetheless, most existing large-scale cohorts with diet, gene, and multi-omics data collected such as the UK Biobank, the China Kadoorie Biobank, the US Health Professionals Follow-up Study, and the Israel 10 K cohort involve middle-aged and older people only [33–36]. Middle-aged and elderly population limits the capture of early-onset chronic diseases and the entire disease spectrum, leaving the dietary and biomarker dynamics during the health-to-disease transition poorly characterized, especially early-onset diseases. In CPNB, it is planned to enroll young and middle-aged participants for long-term follow-up until their death so that it would be possible to observe and screen both early- and late-onset chronic diseases and compare them. Monitoring participants’ dietary intake through weighed food records will be conducted every five years. The cumulative average method will be used to calculate the average dietary intake during the long-term follow-up. The findings from CPNB will facilitate the development of precision nutrition interventions for early-onset chronic diseases and the identification of biomarkers at the preclinical stage.

Notably, given that the critical roles insulin resistance and hyperglycemia play in the development and progression of various chronic diseases [37], as well as the decisive role of carbohydrate intake in PGR, we plan to perform food challenge tests to assess participants’ PGR to different types of carbohydrates. The food challenge tests enable us to understand individual variations in responses to different types of carbohydrates and how these individual variations may influence chronic disease pathogenesis, particularly early-onset diseases. Moreover, we are able to investigate the interaction between habitual consumption of carbohydrate-rich foods and individual variations in PGR to different types of carbohydrate, in the context of chronic disease prevention.

The gene-diet interaction has been explored by many existing large cohorts and biobanks such as the US All of Us project, the UK Biobank, the China Kadoorie Biobank, the US Nurses’ Health Study, and the US Health Professionals Follow-up Study [20–22]. Nevertheless, methodological constraints potentially exist in the dietary assessment approaches used by these cohort studies. For instance, the China Kadoorie Biobank used non-quantitative FFQ [38] and the US Nurses’ Health Study and the US Health Professionals Follow-up Study used semi-quantitative FFQ [39]. In the UK Biobank, dietary intake was assessed based on the FFQ and 24-hour dietary recall [40]. Inaccurate estimation of food intake and recall bias may induce significant measurement errors [24]. This may distort the relationship between dietary intake and outcomes. In order to minimize the errors, at the baseline visit of CPNB, participants will have lunch at the assessment center and instructed to record dietary intake by trained staff members. The staff will compare the actual dietary intake with participants’ food record and provide feedback to participants. Participants will be advised on enhancing data accuracy if necessary. Moreover, participants will be given a weighing scale and asked to weigh the food they consume and record dietary intake in real time. In addition, participants will be encouraged to take photos of every meal. The findings from CPNB may provide a more precise interpretation of diet-phenotype/genotype interactions in the development and progression of chronic diseases.

Also, we will use baseline data, particularly gut microbiota and cooking methods, to develop personalized PGR prediction models and conduct external validation. The models will be constructed based on a large-scale Chinese population, which may enhance their applicability to Chinese people. In addition to the influence of individual characteristics on PGR, we will carefully consider the impact of food properties, Chinese cooking methods, and food processing, which are not accounted for in previous models.

CPNB has some strengths. First, as early-onset chronic diseases are emerging public health problems globally and in China, our cohort study focuses on early-onset and preclinical chronic diseases by recruiting young and middle-aged people. Second, we will make every effort to accurately assess participants’ dietary intake, collect this dietary information frequently (annually), and consider using food biomarkers (metabolomics) to evaluate dietary intake. Most importantly, CPNB will collect extensive and comprehensive baseline data, including not only dietary intake, nutrition-related indicators (such as nutritional environment and nutritional status), and multi-omics information, but also gastrointestinal function, salivary amylase, mental health, and SDOH data. The CPNB database enables a more holistic exploration of the interaction between diets/nutrition and these factors in influencing the development and progression of chronic diseases, particularly early-onset diseases, as well as individual variations.

This project has several limitations as well. First, in CPNB, the baseline data collection requires an extended period of 30 days, which may pose challenges for participant recruitment and complete data acquisition. To address these potential difficulties, we will emphasize the significant scientific value of this research to participants and provide appropriate monetary compensation. Second, CPNB involves a relatively long follow-up period, during which some participants, particularly younger adults, may be lost to follow-up, as in China, young adults tend to relocate across cities more frequently than older populations. To mitigate attrition due to population mobility, this project plans to set up assessment centers across multiple cities in China, so that participants who relocate can be continued to follow-up by staff members in their new locations. We are also aware of the complexities in multi-center studies, including but not limited to standardization of operational procedures across centers, specimen transportation, and data sharing. To address the challenges, we will develop SOP and have all staff members trained to ensure the consistency of data acquisition procedures and the reliability of data. Additionally, a pilot cohort will be implemented to enable modifications and enhancements where suboptimal procedures are identified.

Conclusion

CPNB will be a large database with comprehensive baseline and long-term follow-up data, particularly objective device-measured PGR and physical activity, genes, microbiota, metabolites, and food challenges as well as comprehensively phenotype/genotype as features. This database can be used to develop prediction models for personalized metabolic responses and risks of early-onset chronic diseases, especially early-onset type 2 diabetes, in Chinese people and provide new evidence on interactions of diets with phenotypes/genotypes during preclinical stage, onset, and progression of early-onset diseases. Findings from CPNB can be used to develop precision nutrition recommendations with predictive, personalized, preventive, and participatory medicine objectives.
