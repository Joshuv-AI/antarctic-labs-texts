Survey of Rift Valley Fever Virus Antibodies and Risk Factors in Small Ruminants in Maiduguri, Borno State, Nigeria: A Cross‐Sectional Serological Study
Authors: Sani Mohammed, Abdulrahman Mohammed, Adamu Saleh Saidu, Muhammad Mamman Gashua, Shuaibu Gidado Adamu, Ibrahim Malami Yusuf, Daniel Jigbin, Abdulyekeen O. Tijani, Hassan Ismail Musa
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundRift Valley fever (RVF) is a zoonotic viral disease transmitted through mosquito bites commonly reported in low‐ to medium‐income countries such as Nigeria. It impacts livestock productivity and trade, causing abortions and high rates of mortality, particularly among young animals.ObjectivesA cross‐sectional study was aimed to determine the presence of RVF virus antibodies (anti‐RVFV IgG antibodies) and to identify related risk factors in small ruminants within Maiduguri Metropolis, Borno State, Nigeria.MethodsNinety‐six blood samples were collected; information on species, age and sex of the animals was recorded; history of flooding was assessed based on farmer report and observation. The study used a commercial ELISA kit (competitive, multispecies) to identify the presence of RVF virus antibodies, in accordance with the manufacturer's guidelines.ResultsThis study established an overall seroprevalence of RVFV antibodies of 47.9% (95% confidence interval [CI]: 37.9–57.9) in small ruminants. The prevalence rate for sheep was higher (52.1%) compared to goats (43.8%). Females (68.6%), young animals (51.2%) and places flooded (68.3%) showed a higher seroprevalence. The findings indicate that age (OR: 2.4; 95% CI: 1.1–5.2), sex (OR:2.8; 95% CI: 1.2–6.2) and proximity to flood‐prone areas (OR: 2.1; 95% CI: 1.0–4.3) were significant (p ≤ 0.05) risk factors for the RVF virus.ConclusionThe studies indicate previous exposure and potential epidemiological role of small ruminants in RVF virus transmission in Maiduguri Metropolis. It is recommended that regular monitoring and effective surveillance be carried out; mosquito vector populations be regulated during flooding or rainy seasons and the public be informed about the disease's impact on humans, livestock and the economy.

Introduction

Rift Valley fever (RVF) is a viral infection spread by mosquitoes and caused by the Rift Valley fever virus (RVFV). It primarily affects farm animals such as sheep, goats, cattle, and camels, but it can occasionally infect humans, resulting in severe economic losses and healthcare issues during outbreaks. These outbreaks occur primarily in low‐ and middle‐income countries in Africa and Asia (Gerdes 2004; Linthicum et al. 2016; Wright et al. 2019; Akash et al. 2023). The disease RVF was first identified in Kenya in the 1930s and has since caused significant outbreaks in Africa and the Middle East, including Egypt (1977), Kenya (1997 and 2007), Saudi Arabia, and Yemen (2000–2001) (Gerdes 2004; Chevalier et al. 2010; McMillen and Hartman 2018; Wright et al. 2019).

The disease is particularly catastrophic in young livestock species, where mortality rates can approach 100% in severe outbreaks (Kwasnik et al. 2021), and extensive abortion may occur in pregnant animals (Gerdes 2004; Wright et al. 2019; Elhassan et al. 2019). In humans, RVF typically presents as a mild febrile illness; however, severe cases may result in haemorrhagic fever, complications of the neurological system, blindness, and mortality, with aggregated estimates indicating a 21% fatality rate among severe hospitalised cases (Ikegami and Makino 2011; Wright et al. 2019; Anywaine et al. 2022). Transmission primarily occurs via mosquito bites; however, humans may also become infected via direct contact with the blood or tissues of infected animal species (Wright et al. 2019; Akash et al. 2023). The World Health Organization has prioritised RVF for research and development because of its significant pandemic potential and the absence of licensed human vaccines or viable interventions (Wright et al. 2019; Akash et al. 2023). Climate change and increased livestock trade have contributed to the frequency and geographic expansion of RVF outbreak (Chevalier et al. 2010; Linthicum et al. 2016). Nonetheless, there exists a paucity of published data concerning the RVF in small ruminants in Maiduguri Metropolis, Borno State, Nigeria.

There are limited published data regarding the prevalence of RVF among small ruminants in the study location. Previous studies conducted by Olaleye et al. (1996) carried out an extensive statewide survey in Ibadan and Ile‐Ife, Southwestern Nigeria, revealing RVFV seroprevalence rates of 18.7% in sheep, 10.4% in goats, and 10.2% in cattle, alongside seroconversions of 4.8% in sentinel animals during the wet season. Research conducted by Opayele et al. (2019) in the Bodija Municipal abattoir in Ibadan, southwestern Nigeria, revealed that 0.7% of the ruminants examined possessed RVFV‐specific IgM antibodies. This indicates that these animals had recently encountered RVFV, despite RT‐PCR failing to detect any viral RNA. Serological studies of RVFV antibodies in camels in Maiduguri, Northeastern Nigeria, showed a 20.7% seroprevalence (Musa et al. 2021). Consistent with the circulation of RVFV in animal populations, a study by Bukbuk and Baba (2017) in Borno State indicated that the seroprevalence of RVF virus antibodies in humans was significantly higher than that of other viral haemorrhagic fevers, including the Crimean–Congo haemorrhagic fever virus (CCHFV) and the Lassa virus in the region. This gap in knowledge highlights the need for further research to better understand the disease's impact on livestock health and productivity. Improved seroprevalence data could inform vaccination strategies and control measures in the region. Therefore, this study aimed to determine the prevalence of antibodies to RVF among small ruminants and to identify the associated risk factors within the Maiduguri Metropolitan Area of Borno State.

Materials and Methods

Study Area

The research was conducted in Maiduguri Metropolis, Borno State, Northeastern Nigeria. Maiduguri, the capital city, is a significant urban centre with a population estimated around 1.9–2 million people in the metropolitan area as of recent estimates (African Cities Research Consortium 2021), though, the 2006 census recorded about 521,492 people in the core city area (NPC 2006). The population is concentrated in urban wards with varying densities, and the city has experienced growth driven by rural–urban migration (African Cities Research Consortium 2021). Small ruminants such as sheep and goats are important livestock in Maiduguri, typically kept under a semi‐intensive management system. These animals are integral to local livelihoods, adapted to the semi‐arid environment, and contribute to food security through meat, milk, and other products. Maiduguri lies in a semi‐arid zone characterized by two major seasons: a rainy season from June to September and a dry season from October to May. Temperatures range widely from about 13°C to 41°C. The climate is generally hot and dry, with an average temperature around 31°C reported in some sources (Jimme et al. 2016; Olorunmaiye and Awolola 2018, Akpootu et al. 2019). Maiduguri is situated at a latitude of 11.8311° north and a longitude of 13.1510° east, close to the equator (Jimme et al. 2016). Small ruminants such as sheep and goats are important livestock in the Maiduguri metropolis, typically kept under an extensive and semi‐intensive management system, and these animals are integral to local livelihoods, adapted to the semi‐arid environment, and contribute to food security through meat, milk, and other products.

Study Design

A cross‐sectional study was designed to determine RVFR antibodies prevalence in small ruminant populations and to identify the associated risk factors within the Maiduguri Metropolitan Area of Borno State using a two‐stage cluster probability sampling method to determine RVFR antibodies. Initially, flocks within the study area were randomly chosen as primary sampling units. In the second stage, samples were obtained from randomly selected individual sheep and goats from the enrolled flocks, ensuring each animal had a defined likelihood of participation.

Sample Collection and Handling

Ethical approval was sought before the commencement of sample collection from Borno State Livestock Development Agency (BOLMA) with a reference (DVS/BOLMA.AUEC/2025/005). In addition, an explicit consent of livestock owners (small ruminant farmers) was sought before including their animals into the research. Farms or animals are excluded if owners decline participation, this indicates respect for farmers autonomy and property. Approximately 5 mL of blood samples were aseptically obtained from each sheep and goats in the selected flock at the study area using a 21‐gauge syringe and needle, with each sample assigned a unique number. The samples were processed according to established protocols (Olaleye et al. 1996).

Samples were left to coagulate at ambient temperatures and thereafter transported to the Veterinary Public Health Laboratory at the University of Maiduguri in a cold box with ice packs inside. The sera had been separated from the coagulated blood using centrifugation at 1500 × g for 10 min. The collected sera were preserved at −20−C until analyses. During the sample collection age, sex and history of recent flooding were recorded. The age of small ruminants was ascertained via rostral dentition using the processes of eruption of teeth and attrition (Pitassi et al. 2022). The age was classified according to years for efficient data processing. Individuals aged ≤ 2 years were classified as young, and those older than 2 years were classified as adults. The sex was determined via a visual assessment of the external genitalia. Flooding was assessed based on farm history and observation of the environments.

Serological Test

The test utilised an enzyme‐linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) kit to detect anti‐RVFV nucleoprotein antibodies by competitive ELISA in serum samples. The commercial kit utilised the ID Screen RVF competition multispecies from Innovative Diagnostics, ID VET, Montpellier, France. This company indicates that the kits exhibited a sensitivity of 100% (95% CI: 99.5–100%) as established by validation studies conducted in endemic areas. The testing process was outlined according to the manufacturer's instructions. The optimum densities of the test solutions were assessed and recorded at 450 nm utilising an ELISA reader.

Interpretation of the Result of the Test

For each test sample, the competition percentage (S/N %) was computed as follows: SN%=OptimaldensityofsampleOptimaldensityofnegativecontrol×100

Samples presenting percentage competition of less than or equal to 40% were considered positive while those with greater than 40% were considered negative based on the manufacturer's instructions.

Data Analysis

Data were presented in the form of table. All variables (species, sex, age and flood areas) generated were analysed using IBM‐SPSS V23.0 to establish a possible association with the presence of RVF virus antibodies using a chi‐squared test of association. The risk estimates and its corresponding CIs were calculated at the 95% level. A value of p ≤ 0.05 was considered significant throughout the study.

Discussion

RVF is a disease that spreads through mosquitoes and is harmful for humans as well as animals. It is a threat to public health and the economy in an endemic area of Africa. In Northeastern Nigeria, especially in Borno, Yobe, and Adamawa states, RVF spread was driven by environmental, social, economic, weather, and conflict factors. These vectors are what trigger RVF outbreaks. When farmers rear a large number of livestock, such as in nomadic pastoralism and moving animals via borders, viruses spread and multiply more quickly among humans and animals. Armed conflict, displacement and a lack of veterinary infrastructure are examples of socio‐political factors that make surveillance and response to diseases systems unsuccessful. This contributes to the biological and ecological threats becoming more severe. The loss of wildlife habitats, unhygienic slaughterhouses and a lack of awareness among people are the risks drivers for the transmission of RVF. The transmission dynamics of RVF, which involves multiple hosts and vectors in Northeastern Nigeria, have been investigated alongside additional factors that hinder successful management, notably climate variability and livestock trade routes. The 47.9% seroprevalence of RVF virus antibodies reported in this study is higher than the 20.7% found in camels in Maiduguri (Musa et al. 2021), the 11% reported in livestock in Jos, North Central Nigeria (Anejo‐Okopi et al. 2020) and the 0.7% detected in an abattoir‐based study in Ibadan, Nigeria (Opayele et al. 2019). This variation might be linked to differences in techniques of sampling employed, environmental condition, species, investigative methodology, the time of sampling frame or regional outbreak trends. The established prevalence of RVF in this current study was higher than the findings from some African countries, particularly Senegal and Gambia, in small ruminant species, with a 2%–14% prevalence range. (Gahn et al. 2024; Jarra et al. 2025). Likewise, Sado et al. (2022) in Cameroon reported that sheep and goats had 2.7% and 2.4% prevalence of RVF, respectively.

Consequent the 2008 outbreak of RVF in Madagascar, a seroprevalence of 24.7% in small ruminants was reported (Jeanmaire et al. 2011). The previously mentioned prevalence rates were all much lower than the 47.9% found in this study. This means that the seroprevalence discovered might be due to a recent previous outbreak, a very high‐risk area, or differences between the ways samples were taken or tests had been done. Prior studies, such as those conducted by Ezeifeka et al. (1982) and Olaleye et al. (1996) frequently reported lower prevalence rates, thereby substantiating the assertion that a 47.9% incidence is remarkably high for small ruminants in the majority of African contexts. A 52.1% prevalence of RVF in sheep is substantially elevated compared to rates documented in numerous African studies; however, the trend towards greater seroprevalence in sheep relative to goats remains consistent. For Instance, Sindato et al. (2015) in Tanzania found RVF seroprevalence rates of 29.7% in sheep and 22% in goats, with the difference not statistically significant, indicating both species are similarly susceptible to infection (Ngoshe et al. 2020). In Mozambique, sheep had a seroprevalence rate of 44.2%, while goats had a rate of 25.1%. In South Africa, the rates were 28% for sheep and 9.3% for goats (Blomström et al. 2016; Ngoshe et al. 2019, 2020). Some areas, like Ethiopia and Uganda, have reported rates in goats that are either the same or even higher. This suggests that local factors, management practices and outbreak history can affect the prevalence of a specific species (Megenas et al. 2024; Ndumu et al. 2021; Ibrahim et al. 2020). In general, sheep tend to have higher RVF seroprevalence than goats, but both species are clearly at risk, and the differences are not always big enough to matter. This supports the idea that both should be considered at risk in areas where the disease is common (Blomström et al. 2016; Ngoshe et al. 2019, 2020).

Several studies have found that females small ruminants are more likely to develop RVFV antibodies compared to males. This increased prevalence is partially ascribed to the tendency of females to remain in herds for extended durations, thereby augmenting their cumulative exposure to the virus over time, in contrast to males, who are frequently sold or slaughtered at younger ages, thereby diminishing their infection risk (Sindato et al. 2015; Anejo‐Okopi et al. 2020). Pregnancy along with other physiological factors may weaken the immune system in females, making them more likely to contract the infections like RVF (Anejo‐Okopi et al. 2020). Anejo‐Okopi et al. (2020) reported that the trend of RVF prevalence was higher in female's small ruminants among different regions of Africa. Additionally, Sindato et al. (2015) and Anejo‐Okopi et al. (2020) reported that environmental factors and age of the animals were significant risk determinants that might influenced the occurrence of the disease; though, immunosuppression associated with advanced pregnancy and extended herd rearing may likely revealed the reason for the established sex differences in RVF prevalence among the species. RVF has a greater detrimental effect on younger small ruminants, such as lambs and kids, than on adults, reflected by increased mortality rates and more pronounced clinical manifestations in the young. This increased susceptibility is due to persistent viral circulation and early‐life exposure, as young animals are more prone to encounter the virus during active transmission phases, particularly after significant rainfall that enhances mosquito vectors population (Kwasnik et al. 2021; Tinto et al. 2023). Antibody studies routinely demonstrate that adults exhibit a greater overall antibody prevalence, reflecting cumulative exposure over time. In contrast, the detection of antibodies in younger animals may be an excellent marker of recent or current viral circulation in the environment (Kwasnik et al. 2021; Tinto et al. 2023).

Detection of RVFV antibodies in young ruminants may indicate recent exposure, although this observation must be evaluated in conjunction with other epidemiological indicators when investigating viral circulation trends. This trend persists throughout multiple regions in Africa, where RVF is prevalent, and outbreaks can lead to substantial economic and public health repercussions due to abortion events in pregnant females and elevated mortality rates in young animals (Kwasnik et al. 2021; Tinto et al. 2023).

Consequently, it is essential to monitor antibody levels in both young and adult animals to better understand RVF epidemiology and promote control strategies. This study concludes that small ruminants in Maiduguri Metropolis exhibit evidence of exposure to RVF virus, which may indicate potential transmission risk to susceptible livestock and the public, particularly pastoralists or individuals residing in flood‐prone areas or regions experiencing heavy rainfall where vectors are in abundance. This study found that 47.9% of small ruminants in the area studied were seropositive for RVFV antibodies, which is an unusual rate. Age, sex and flooding were recognised as significant risk factors for RVF virus infection. To minimise the chances of RVF transmission, it is suggested that the study area regularly monitor and control the trade of livestock, as well as raise public awareness about the disease's effects on animals and the economy. Controlling the population of mosquitoes that spread the RVF virus and vaccinating small ruminants during floods can greatly lower the risk of the virus spreading. Moreover, additional research is required in the study area to ascertain the potential sources of RVF infection. Further research using longitudinal study designs and molecular diagnostic techniques (RT‐PCR, virus isolation and genomic sequencing) is necessary to further understand the role of small ruminants in the transmission dynamics of RVF virus within the study area. These methods could aid in identifying present infections, assessing past exposure and distinguishing specific virus strains. This would enhance surveillance systems and facilitate the development of evidence‐based intervention programs.

Limitations of the Study

The study exclusively employed univariate logistic regression, which is incapable of controlling for confounding variables or analysing the interactions among predictors.
