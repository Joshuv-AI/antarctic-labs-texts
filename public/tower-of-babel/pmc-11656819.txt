Combined inflammation-related biomarkers and clinicopathological features for the prognosis of stage II/III colorectal cancer by machine learning
Authors: Lei Liang, Ning Xu, Lanfei Ding, Xin Li, Chengxun Jiang, Jianhua Zhang, Jun Yang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundInflammation-related biomarkers, such as systemic inflammation score (SIS) and neutrophil–lymphocyte ratio (NLR), are associated with colorectal cancer prognosis. However, the combined role of SIS, NLR, and clinicopathological factors in stage II/III colorectal cancer remains unclear. This study developed a nomogram to predict long-term prognosis for these patients.MethodsThis retrospective study included 1540 patients (training set) from the First Affiliated Hospital of Kunming Medical University and 152 patients (testing set) from The Honghe Third People's Hospital. Cox regression identified independent prognostic factors, and machine learning established predictive models. Model performance was evaluated by the C-index, area under the curve (AUC), and decision curve analysis (DCA).ResultsIn the training set, a total of 1540 patients with stage II/III colorectal cancer were included. More than 70 years old (HR = 1.830, p = 0.000); SIS = 2 (HR = 1.693, p = 0.002); Preoperative CEA more than 5 ng/mL (HR = 1.614, p = 0.000); and Moderately differentiated (HR = 1.438, p = 0.011); or Low/undifferentiated (HR = 2.126, p = 0.000); The pN1 (HR = 2.040, p = 0.000) and pN2 (HR = 3.297, p = 0.000) stages were considered independent prognostic risk factors of stage II/III colorectal cancer. Negative perineural invasion (HR = 0.733, p = 0.014) and NLR less than 4 (HR = 0.696, p = 0.022) were considered independent prognostic protective factors of stage II/III colorectal cancer. Background

Colorectal cancer (CRC) is the third most common gastrointestinal tumor and is the second highest cause of cancer death [1]. Previous studies have suggested that the prognosis of CRC was related to age and pathological factors, such as TNM stage. Ongoing research has studied corresponding biomarkers (e.g., KRAS [2], BRAF [3], and MSI [4]) for the long-term prognosis of CRC and preoperative inflammation biomarkers reflect the progression of tumor cells [5]. Previous studies have reported that preoperative inflammation biomarkers were correlated with the prognosis of various tumor types [6–8], including CRC. For example, the neutrophil–lymphocyte ratio (NLR) is an independent prognostic factor of CRC and shares an inverse correlation with the prognosis of CRC [9]. Lymphocyte-monocyte ratio (LMR) is another independent prognostic factor for CRC, with a direct correlation with the prognosis of CRC [10]. Platelet lymphocyte ratio (PLR) is also a prognostic factor of stage III/IV CRC and shares an inverse correlation with the prognosis of CRC [11]. The combined score of circulating blood cells and albumin (Alb) in peripheral blood was also reported in previous studies for the prognosis of different tumors. For example, the systemic inflammatory score (SIS), which consists of LMR and Alb, was first used for the prognosis of kidney cancer. Likewise, SIS shares an inverse correlation with the prognosis of CRC [12]. However, the prognosis of stage II/III colorectal cancer by SIS, NLR, and clinicopathological factors remained unreported.

While molecular biomarkers such as KRAS and MSI are well recognized for their prognostic value in colorectal cancer, this study focused on readily available clinicopathological factors and preoperative inflammation biomarkers that can be easily obtained from routine blood sampling to develop a practical and economical prognostic model. Hence, we analyzed the correlation between SIS, NLR, and the clinicopathological factors and the prognosis of stage II/III CRC. We collected the clinicopathological factors and preoperative inflammatory data from 1540 patients with stage II/III colorectal cancer for analysis, and we subsequently established a survival prediction model and 152 patients were used for validate the efficacy of model.

Data and methods

Patients

The retrospective study included 1540 patients with stage II or III CRC as pathologically confirmed by the First Affiliated Hospital of Kunming Medical University from January 2014 to September 2019. These patients were considered the “training” set. A total of 152 patients were recruited from the Honghe Third People's Hospital, and they were considered the “testing” set.

This study was performed in line with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. This retrospective study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the First Affiliated Hospital of Kunming Medical University (the ethical approval code was 2023-L-150) and the Ethics Committee of the Honghe Third People's Hospital (the ethical approval code was 2023-LW-28), which exempted the requirement for informed consent from the patients. The patients were excluded if they had received preoperative neoadjuvant therapy or concurrent fever and blood system diseases, among others. The flow chart of the study is displayed in Fig. 1.

Flow chart of the study. Illustrates the selection process of patients included in the study, detailing inclusion and exclusion criteria and the final number of patients analyzed

Data collected

We collected data from the hospital medical record system, and this included inflammation-related biomarkers (i.e., neutrophil, lymphocyte, monocyte, platelet, Alb, fibrinogen (fib), preoperative alpha-fetoprotein (AFP), preoperative carcinoembryonic antigen (CEA), and preoperative carbohydrate antigen 19–9 (CA19-9) and clinicopathological factors (i.e., gender, age, mismatch repair (MMR), body mass index (BMI), pathological types of tumor tissue, degree of tumor tissue differentiation, tumor size, pT stage, pN stage, Union for International Cancer Control (UICC) stage, vascular invasion, perineural invasion, and obstruction).

NLR, LMR, and PLR were determined accordingly. SIS was determined by the absolute values of LMR and Alb, such that when LMR was less than 4.44, and Alb was less than 40 g/L, SIS was 0 points, and when LMR was more than 4.44, and Alb was more than 40 g/L, SIS was 2 points. Under other conditions, SIS was 1 point [5]. While the SIS score is practical, does not account for dynamic changes in inflammation and nutrition over time due to the retrospective design of this study.

Follow-up

In the training set, follow-ups were conducted until March 2022 or patient death. In the testing set, 152 patients were recruited from July 2017 to July 2021, and follow-ups were conducted until September 2023 or patient death.

Statistical analysis

SPSS version 22.0 and R (V4.1.3) were used to perform the statistical analyses and visualization. Survival figures and graphs were drawn using the GraphPad Prism 8.0 software. Continuous variables (age, NLR, PLR, Fib, and tumor size) were analyzed using the X-tile version 3.6.1 software. The cut-off value for age was 70 years old, the cut-off value for tumor size was 3.8, and the cut-off value for NLR and PLR fib were 4 and 3.2, respectively.

The Kaplan–Meier approach was used to generate the overall survival (OS) curves, and the Log-rank test was used to compare the differences between variables. Variables with p < 0.05 in univariate analysis were included in the multivariate Cox regression model using a conditional forward stepwise regression method. This approach identified the independent prognostic factors by excluding variables without significant impact on overall survival.

Independent prognostic survival factors (NLR, age, SIS, CEA, tumor differentiation, pN stage, and perineural invasion) were used to construct predictive models. We further evaluated the data and model using seven machine-learning algorithms. The machine learning algorithms, including random survival forest (RSF), Stepwise Cox, generalized boosted regression modeling (GBM), supervised principal components (SuperPC), survival support vector machine (survivalSVM), partial least squares regression for Cox (plsRcox), and CoxBoost.

The cohort data from the First Affiliated Hospital of Kunming Medical University and Hong He Third People's Hospital were used to establish and validate the above models. The results were sorted by the validation set consistency index (C-index), and the algorithm with the highest validation set C-index was selected as the algorithm for model establishment. The cohort was divided into high- and low-risk groups according to the median model risk score, and the survival curve of high- and low-risk groups was plotted accordingly. A one- to five-year prognostic ROC curve was plotted using the “timeROC package”. Nomograms and calibration curves of clinical data were plotted using “rms packages”. The models were further evaluated with DCA.

Stage II CRC was reported for 789 patients, while stage III CRC was reported for 751 patients (Table 1). Development of the prognosis model of stage II/III colorectal cancer

Machine learning was used to establish a stage II/III CRC prediction model and nomogram based on the independent prognostic factors in stage II/III CRC (Fig. 3B). We then divided the patients into high- and low-risk groups (p < 0.001) based on the median of risk scores (–2.630534) and plotted the KM survival curve (Fig. 3D). The C-index of the best prediction model was 0.746 (Fig. 3A), and the AUCs of the 1-, 3-, and 5-year OS were 0.8, 0.76, and 0.75, respectively (Fig. 3C). Calibration curves of the prognostic model reported consistent OS rates with the actual observed results (Fig. 3F).

In addition, the clinical utility of the predictive model was evaluated using DCA. The results indicated that the model provided a greater net gain and a wider threshold probability range in predicting the OS than the traditional UICC stage (Fig. 3E), demonstrating its effectiveness for prospective clinical application.

Validation of stage II ~ III colorectal cancer model

We collected external data from 152 cases for validation. The C-index of the best prediction model was 0.708 (Fig. 3A). The ROC curves for the 1-, 3-, and 5-year OS were plotted, and the AUCs were 0.7, 0.73, and 0.63, respectively (Fig. 4A). Next, we also plotted the KM curve of the model to prove significant differences between high- and low-risk groups (p < 0.001) (Fig. 4B). The calibration curve in Fig. 4C indicated a good consistency between the 3-year and 5-year OS, but this was not observed in the 1-year OS. These results highlight the need for additional validation with longer follow-up and larger datasets to improve the accuracy of long-term predictions.

Discussion

CRC is a common digestive tract tumor with high mortality and morbidity. Hence, it is important to make a simple model clinicopathological used to predict the poor prognosis of colorectal cancer population. In this study, SIS, NLR, age, preoperative CEA, tumor differentiation, pN stage, and perineural invasion were independent prognostic factors for stage II/III CRC. Preoperative inflammatory markers can be easily obtained from routine blood draws and are important prognostic factors for CRC. For example, NLR is a common marker of tumor prognosis. Previous studies have suggested that NLR is also an important prognostic indicator of CRC [13]. In this study, NLR was validated as an independent prognostic factor for stage II/III CRC when it was less than 4 (p = 0.022), consistent with previous studies. The decreased NLR may be caused by neutropenia or lymphocytosis. Vega et al. [14] reported that in the neuroblastoma model, tumor-infiltrating cells were detected in peripheral blood, and Wu TD et al. [15] suggested that lymphocytes detected in peripheral blood were also detected in tumor tissue. In this regard, an increase in peripheral blood lymphocytes may enhance the infiltration of lymphocytes into tumor tissue, potentially improving the prognosis of stage II/III CRC.

The preoperative nutritional index is also a prognostic factor for cancer, and poor nutrition may reflect tumor-induced malnutrition. Serum Alb reflects the nutritional status of the body and liver function, and serum Alb has been used to predict the prognosis of malignant tumors in recent years [16]. To a certain extent, a low serum Alb level may lead to a poor survival prognosis of CRC. As wei Y et al. [17] reported, low albumin are associated with PFS prognosis of mCRC and Li Z et al. [18] found that low albumin levels were positively associated with shorter OS and PFS in patients with non-metastatic colorectal cancer. In this study, low Alb (< 40 g/L) indicated poor nutritional status and its combination with LMR could be considered an indepence prognosis factor in stage II/III CRC patients.

SIS was initially used by Chang et al. for the prognosis of renal cancer [19]. Subsequently, SIS was used for the prognosis of all stages of CRC, and results indicated that the prognosis of CRC was poor when SIS was 1 or 2 [12]. In this study of stage II/III CRC, SIS of 0, 1, and 2 corresponded to 468 patients (30.39%), 595 patients (38.64%), and 477 patients (30.97%), respectively. Survival analysis suggested that SIS was an independent prognostic factor for stage II/III CRC. When SIS was 1, there was no significant difference in the OS of stage II/III colorectal cancer as compared to when SIS was 0 (p = 0.063). When SIS was 2, there was a significant difference in the OS (p = 0.002). In stage II/III CRC, when LMR is more than 4.44, the survival prognosis will be decreased, as LMR is an independent prognostic factor for colorectal cancer [10]. When SIS is 2, meaning LMR is less than 4.44 and Alb less than 40 g/L, LMR is less than 4.44 may be mononucleosis or lymphocytopenia. Several studies have shown that lymphopenia is associated with poor survival [20, 21]. When lymphocytes are reduced, there is less lymphocyte infiltration in the tumor tissue, and the prognosis is poor. Therefore, SIS, including LMR, may be a index for counting the infiltration of immune cells in tumor tissues [12].

It is known that age, pN stage, preoperative CEA level, tumor differentiation, and perineural invasion are correlated with CRC. Poor CRC prognosis is common with older patients, patients with lymph node metastasis, and patients with abnormal preoperative CEA levels (> 5 ng/mL) than normal patients. In this study, patients over 70 years old, with lymph node metastasis and abnormal CEA levels (> 5 ng/mL), had a worse survival prognosis for stage II/III CRC, which was consistent with previous studies [22]. In this study, patients with low or undifferentiated tumors had a poor prognosis, which was consistent with previous studies [22, 23]. In this study, patients with stage II/III CRC with perineural invasion reported a poor prognosis, which was related to a high recurrence rate and was consistent with previous studies [14].

Visualization of nomogram can better predict the probability of disease occurrence and subsequently inform better clinical decisions [24]. In this study, independent prognostic factors (age, SIS, NLR, CEA, tumor differentiation, pN stage, and perineural invasion) in COX regression were included to build the clinical prediction model. The prediction model reported good predictive efficacy (C-index of 0.748) and consistency for the long-term survival prognosis of patients with stage II/III CRC, and the model could potentially be used for better clinical decision-making. The model is economical, applicable and the model data can be easily and quickly obtained from clinical and pathological factors, and preoperative routine blood testing, which may provide a reference for clinicians to make decisions, but it needs to be further verified by a larger external validation dataset.

This was a retrospective study with several limitations, as follows: 1. The external data validation was small; 2. The follow-up time was short; 3. The sample size was small. 4. A lack of molecular biomarker data. Specifically, information on molecular biomarkers such as KRAS and MSI is restricted. These limitations could be addressed in the future with a larger sample size, long-term prognosis and having a multi-center and prospective study. 5.The model has poor performance in predicting long-term predictions of colorectal cancer in external validation data sets, and there are challenges in long-term prognosis. other studies needed External validation with longer follow-up period and larger data sets, to improve the accuracy of long-term prognosis prediction. 6.The use of the preoperative SIS scoring system in this study may omit the complex interrelationship between inflammation, nutrition, and tumor prognosis, and future studies should incorporate dynamic measures of these factors to better understand their temporal impact prognosis of CRC.

Conclusions

In summary, a nomogram combining SIS, NLR, and clinicopathological factors provides an effective, cost-efficient tool for predicting the prognosis of stage II/III colorectal cancer. Future studies will validate its long-term predictive performance in larger, multicenter cohorts.
