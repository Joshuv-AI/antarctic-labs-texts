The vaginal microecological evaluation of human papillomavirus (HPV) infection in uterine leiomyoma patients
Authors: Lingna Chen, Yuying Ruan, Xiaoqing Zhu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveWe evaluated the vaginal microecology and human papillomavirus (HPV) subtype distribution in patients with uterine leiomyoma (UL). The study also assessed the correlation between HPV infection and vaginal microecology dysbiosis and uterine leiomyoma occurrence. We hypothesized that disruption of vaginal microecology and high-risk HPV subtype infections are associated with the occurrence of UL in women of reproductive age.Materials and methodsThe observational study involved 444 women, divided into two groups: uterine leiomyoma (n = 226) and those without leiomyoma (n = 218). Assessment of vaginal microecological and HPV infection was done on the control and study groups. Spearman correlation was used to analyse differences between the two groups.ResultsThe incidences of uterine leiomyoma in patients aged between 20 and 29 years and 30–39 years were significantly high. UL patients had reduced vaginal cleanliness level (I-II), but a higher number of III-IV degree than in the healthy group. The vulvovaginal candidiasis incidence, vaginal disorders, and infections with HPV16, HPV18, and HPV52 subtypes were also remarkably increased in the patients compared to the controls. The high-risk HPV infection prevalence and vaginal disorders were elevated in the patients compared to the normal group.ConclusionsOur findings suggest that HPV infection and vaginal microecological imbalance are associated with the occurrence of uterine leiomyomas. Strategies aimed at maintaining vaginal microecological balance and preventing HPV infection may merit further investigation in relation to uterine leiomyoma management.Keywords: Human papillomavirus, Lactobacilli, Uterine leiomyoma, Vaginal microecological evaluation, Vulvovaginal candidiasis.

Introduction

Human Papillomavirus (HPV) is a small, double-stranded DNA virus, with its subtypes categorized into low or high-risk groups, and it primarily affects women of reproductive age [1, 2]. Newborns may become infected with HPV during delivery. At the same time, other notable routes of infection include direct contact with lesions of infected individuals or indirect contact through object surfaces contaminated with the virus. Women of reproductive age may contract one or more subtypes of HPV at least once in their lifetime, but the virus often clears on its own within two years [3, 4].

The vaginal microecology, including vaginal anatomy, its microbiota, and the immune mechanisms [5], plays a crucial role in supporting female reproductive health and may also indicate potential disease outcomes [3]. However, an infection in the vagina can disturb the typical balance of the vaginal microecology, potentially weakening its defence mechanisms and increasing vulnerability to HPV infection [6]. Conversely, uterine leiomyomas (uterine fibroids) are the most prevalent benign tumours of the uterus and contribute to significant health issues in women of reproductive age [7]. They are responsible for the majority of gynaecological surgeries and account for 39% of hysterectomies performed yearly [8]. The majority of uterine leiomyoma cases do not present symptoms, but some individuals might experience pelvic discomfort, painful menstruation, abnormal uterine bleeding, or difficulty conceiving [9].

High-risk human papillomavirus (HR-HPV) is a necessary cause of cervical cancer and plays a role in other anogenital neoplasms. Whether HR-HPV contributes to benign gynecologic conditions is less clear. Persistent HR-HPV infection is shaped by the cervicovaginal microenvironment, with bacterial vaginosis, Trichomonas vaginalis, vulvovaginal candidiasis, and vaginal microbial community structures influencing viral clearance or persistence. However, little is known about whether these factors extend their influence to uterine tissues or fibroid development. Therefore, we hypothesized that persistent HR-HPV infection and coexisting vaginal dysbiosis are associated with the presence of uterine fibroids. This study aimed to utilize the Vaginal Microecology Evaluation System (VMES) tool to investigate the association between vaginal microecological factors, HR-HPV infection (including specific subtypes), and the risk of uterine leiomyoma.

Materials and methods

Study design

The prospective study employed a passive case detection methodology, focusing on women aged 20 to 69 years who attended the gynecology department of Hangzhou Women’s Hospital for medical examination between November 2022 and November 2024.

Study participants

The study participants who complained of gynaecologic pain were examined by hysteroscopy and enrolled in the study by the clinicians. The sample size of the study was obtained using a single population ratio formula by assuming that the proportion of uterine leiomyoma patients in Hangzhou Women’s Hospital was 8%. The precision and confidence interval were 5% and 95% (Zα/2 = 1.96), respectively. From this model, 496 participants were assessed for possible eligibility for the study. However, 14 subjects were unwilling to participate, 14 had privacy concerns regarding sensitive tests of HPV and vaginal microbiota, while 24 people perceived the vaginal swabs as embarrassing and invasive. Eventually, 444 patients were recruited, comprising 226 patients with uterine leiomyoma cases and 218 patients without uterine leiomyoma serving as the control group, as shown in the flow diagram in Fig. 1. Cases were defined as women with ultrasound-confirmed uterine leiomyoma, identified during routine gynecological screening. Controls were recruited from the same gynecological screening population during the same study period. Eligible controls were age-matched women with no evidence of uterine leiomyoma on ultrasound. Control selection was based solely on imaging findings and not on the presence or absence of symptoms.

Experimental flow diagram

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

The inclusion criteria in this study were as follows: women aged > 18 years, with a history of sexual activity, women who underwent a colposcopic examination, women with no sexual activity within 3 days before sampling, non-smokers, and no vaginal irrigation or administration within 7 days before sampling.

Women were excluded in the study if they had: Prior surgical removal of the uterus (hysterectomy) or myomectomy that removes or alters the uterus such that the current assessment of fibroid presence/characteristics is not possible. Prior treatments that preclude assessment of current fibroid status (for instance, recent uterine artery embolization) within the last eight months, active pelvic malignancy or severe comorbidity precluding participation, and inability or refusal to provide informed consent. We also excluded women who had ever taken medication to treat lupus, Graves’ disease, Sjögren’s scleroderma, or multiple sclerosis; or any cancer type treated with radiation or chemotherapy. Women were also excluded if they were pregnant or in the lactating period, had a history of HPV preventive vaccination, received treatment for HPV infection or cervical lesions in the past, or had recently used antibiotics. All patients who consented to participate in the study signed a written consent form before the collection of clinical data and before undergoing the uterine leiomyoma test.

Sample collection

All vaginal and cervical swab samples were taken by trained medical personnel. A sterile long-handle swab was used to collect a vaginal discharge sample for smear examination, pH measurement, and hydrogen peroxide content testing. In addition, a cervical brush was used to collect a sufficient number of epithelial cells, which were then transferred to an elution tube. The study was performed according to the guidance of the Helsinki Declaration, and it was approved by the ethics committee at Hangzhou Women’s Hospital (Date: 20th, Jan, 2021, Approval no: 2021051-TX-10). All the methods were performed as per the relevant guidelines and regulations. The reporting of this study conforms to the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines [10].

Detection of uterine leiomyoma

The subjects were initially interviewed about their reproductive history, followed by a pelvic examination. A gynecologist then performed a transvaginal ultrasound on each of the registered participants. We videotaped and examined each ultrasound by a second gynecologist to ensure quality control was maintained. Blood flow analysis was performed utilizing a 6-MHz pulse Doppler system, while imaging was executed with the Aloka color Doppler 680, SSD 2000, and a 5-MHz transvaginal probe. For those patients whose test results revealed the existence of fibroids, the gynecologist documented the following details on a coded data form: The size of the fibroid, its position on the uterine axis, the number of fibroids, and its location with relation to the uterine layers. Furthermore, the volume in cm3 for each myoma was determined using the formula for a prolate ellipse, as previously reported [11]. In cases with several fibroids, the total fibroid volume was estimated by adding the volumes of each fibroid.

Microecology evaluation

The analysis of vaginal microecology included both morphological and functional variables. Morphological assessment involved the identification of dominant bacterial flora, bacterial density, spore count, flora density, and hyphal forms associated with Vulvovaginal Candidiasis, as previously described [12]. Functional indicators include the presence of enzymes such as hydrogen peroxide, leukocyte esterase activity, coagulase activity, β-glucuronidase, and sialidase activity. Those diagnosed with vaginal infections received the proper treatment.

Morphological assessment

The vaginal smear was prepared on clean slides with a dry cotton swab and then subjected to Gram staining as previously described [13]. Assessment of flora diversity, bacterial density, dominant bacterial flora, and nugents cores was determined using a 100x oil immersion microscope. To ensure quality, trained technicians cross-evaluated every slide. The diagnosis of vaginal health, bacterial density, trichomonas vaginitis (TV), vulvovaginal candidiasis (VVC), and bacterial vaginosis (BV) was performed under established reference standards [14]. The criteria for assessing morphological indicators were defined following the methodology described previously [15].

Functional analysis

Under the manufacturer’s instructions, the AV/BV combined detection kit was utilized to assess the primary functional indicators. The collected swab was subsequently transferred to the sample tube, followed by the addition of 8–10 droplets of sample diluent. The sample liquid was obtained from the swab by squeezing, and it was then carefully dispensed into the five holes on the test card using a straw. A single drop of chromogenic liquid was introduced into each reaction hole. The setup was incubated for 10 min at 37 °C. The response in each well was monitored and evaluated against the colorimetric chart for analysis.

Genotyping of HPV

We collected exfoliated human cervical cell samples and tested them for HPV using a human papillomavirus genotyping detection kit with a gene chip (Genetel Pharmaceuticals, Shenzhen). Internal quality controls, including positive and negative controls, were included in each run. Approximately 10% of samples were randomly retested to confirm reproducibility. The laboratory also participates in external quality assurance programs for HPV detection, ensuring the accuracy and reliability of results. The kit can identify 24 HPV genotypes, including the high-risk subtypes 16, 18, 31, 33, 35, 39, 45, 51, 53, 56, 58, 59, 66, 68, 73, and 82, as well as the low-risk subtypes 6, 11, 42, 43, 81, and 83. We isolated DNA from exfoliated human cervical cell samples using the isolation reagent specified by the kit and following the manufacturer’s instructions. Roche Lightcycler 480 nucleic acid detector was used for the reaction of DNA samples and amplification of nucleic acid. The reaction consisted of 2 µl of DNA, 27.6 µl of HPV PCR mix (including specific primers), and 0.4 µl of Taq/UNG (final volume: 30 µl). The PCR conditions as described by Wang et al. [16], were adopted in this study. Briefly, 1 cycle of hot-start activation at 95 °C for 10 min; followed by 40 cycles of denaturation at 95 °C for 30 s, annealing at 52 °C for 30 s, and extension at 65 °C for 30 s; and a final extension at 65 °C for 5 min. Then, the amplified product was specifically amplified again by an HPV gene chip. The chip detection and reading system was used to scan and analyze the hybridized chip image and finally obtain the HPV genotyping results.

Data analysis

Data analysis was done using SPSS 23.0 statistical software (IBM, Chicago, USA). Continuous variables were tested for normality using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Normally distributed variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) and were compared using the t-test. Non-normally distributed variables were compared using the Mann–Whitney U test. Categorical variables are presented as frequencies and percentages, and comparisons between groups were performed using the χ² test or Fisher’s exact test where appropriate.

Spearman’s rank correlation was used to assess the association between HPV infection, vaginal microecological parameters, and uterine leiomyomas. Variables that were statistically significant in univariate analyses (P < 0.05) were entered into a multivariable logistic regression model. Results are reported as adjusted odds ratios (aOR) with 95% confidence intervals (CI) and two-sided P values, which are reported to four decimal places or as P < 0.0001 where appropriate. Variables entered into the multivariable logistic regression model were selected based on two criteria: statistical significance in univariate analyses (P < 0.05), and established or potential clinical relevance reported in the literature (age, hormonal and reproductive history, BMI, HPV infection, and vaginal microecological imbalance). This approach ensured that both empirically significant and clinically meaningful variables were considered in the adjusted model.

Uterine leiomyoma multivariable logistic analysis

We carried out a multivariable logistic regression analysis on patients with uterine leiomyoma. Uterine leiomyoma occurrence (0, no:1, yes) was regarded as the dependent variable. The independent variables were BMI, age, uterine curettage ≥ 2, abortion ≥ 2, gravidity ≥ 2, vaginal pH ≥ 4.5, vaginal flora density (grade I, IV), diversity (grade I, IV), lactobacillus dominant bacteria, trichomonas vaginitis positive, VVC positive, BV positive, hydrogen peroxide positive (≤ 2 µmol/L), SNA (≥ 7 U/L), LE (≥ 9 U/L), NAG (≥ 0.1 U/mL), and GUS (≥ 0.5 U/mL). The irrelevant items were excluded using a stepwise method. The multivariable logistic regression was adjusted for age, gravidity, and abortion history.

According to our findings, Uterine curettage ≥ 2 showed an aOR of 4.66 (95% CI 2.4–8.5), indicating a statistically significant association. Distribution of HPV subtypes in the uterine leiomyoma patients and controls

We used a human papillomavirus genotyping detection kit with a gene chip to investigate the distribution of HPV infection subtypes in women with uterine leiomyoma and the control groups. We only detected HPV11 and HPV42 under the low-risk HPV infections. However, most of the participants reported high-risk HPV genotypes, including HPV16, HPV18, HPV31, HPV33, HPV35, HPV39, HPV45, HPV51, HPV52, HPV53, HPV56, HPV58, HPV59, HPV66, and HPV82. There was no significant difference in the prevalence of infections with low-risk HPV11 and HPV42 subtypes between the uterine leiomyoma and the normal groups. The incidence of infections with high-risk HPV16, HPV18, and HPV52 was significantly higher in the uterine leiomyoma group than in the normal group. We did not observe any significant difference between the uterine leiomyoma and the control groups regarding other high-risk HPV types, including HPV31, HPV33, HPV35, HPV39, HPV45, HPV51, HPV53, HPV56, HPV58, HPV59, HPV66, HPV68, HPV73, and HPV82, as depicted in Table 5.

Discussion

Uterine leiomyoma has recently been reported as the most prevalent female reproductive system tumour that occurs in approximately 30% of females aged between 30 and 50 years [17]. The clinical symptoms of uterine leiomyoma include dysregulated uterine bleeding, frequent urination, increased menstrual flow, constipation, and pelvic pain. An unchecked uterine leiomyoma is a risk factor for adverse perinatal outcomes, including obstructed labour, restriction of intrauterine growth, preterm birth, placental abruption, post-partum haemorrhage, and low birth weight. Consequently, proper prevention of uterine leiomyoma is important. The main cause of uterine leiomyoma remains unclear. However, its development is greatly linked to a network of genetic, hormonal, and environmental factors.

During the pathogenesis of uterine leiomyoma, alterations in the uterine microenvironment occur, which might contribute to the bacterial microbiota and pathogenic invasion of the female reproductive. The phenomenon might lead to endometrial polyps, uterine adhesions, and eventual infertility. According to the previous reports, the reproductive tract bacterial flora changes and the pathogenic bacterial infections, including Candida albicans and Mycobacterium tuberculosis, might enhance uterine leiomyoma occurrence [18]. Similarly, our present investigation reports that the VVC infection rate and the vaginal cleanliness level are remarkably different in uterine leiomyoma patients compared to the normal subjects. Our findings are also corroborated by the previous reports [19, 20].

To a great extent, vaginal cleanliness dysbiosis might cause bacterial vaginitis, leading to the floral imbalance, hence creating a precedent for uterine leiomyoma. Inflammatory factors, including inflammatory cytokines (TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-6), contribute to fibrosis, cell proliferation, and angiogenesis, which entirely enhance fibroid growth and remodelling of tissues, hence contributing to uterine leiomyoma [21]. Since Candida albicans is directly linked to fibrosis and inflammation, it might be a factor indirectly leading to the occurrence of uterine leiomyoma.

The co-existence of various microorganisms occurs in the female reproductive tract of healthy individuals. Consequently, good maintenance of balance and regulation of the pathogenic microbes’ invasion is necessary for the good health of the female reproductive tract. Our study reported a significantly frequent occurrence of uterine leiomyoma among women aged 20–29 and 30–39 years. Our age-related findings should be interpreted with caution. Unlike population-based studies, our clinic-based sampling may have disproportionately included younger women attending for HPV testing or reproductive consultations. Older women with fibroids may be underrepresented due to prior hysterectomy, myomectomy, or reduced attendance for HPV screening. Thus, the observed age pattern is likely an artifact of sampling bias rather than a reflection of true fibroid incidence. Interestingly, our observations are consistent with the HPV infection rate in the uterine patients, since women between 20 and 29 and 30–39 years had the most significantly elevated HPV infection incidences. Further, we also reported significant HPV infections in women between 40 and 49 years and 50–59 years. As corroborated by the previous reports, HPV infection is strongly correlated with age [22]. Age and several other factors, including nulliparity and family history, can increase uterine leiomyoma incidence [23].

To assess the robustness of our findings, we performed a sensitivity analysis restricted to women aged 30–49 years, the age range with the highest fibroid prevalence in many populations, to reduce potential artifacts introduced by age-structure differences. Results of this restricted analysis were directionally similar to the primary analysis but with wider confidence intervals due to reduced sample size, suggesting that age distribution alone does not fully explain the observed association.

Lactobacilli are considered the most essential flora to maintain the vaginal microbiome balance, generating bacteriocins and lactic acid that regulate the growth of bacteria [24]. The disappearance or a drop in lactobacilli numbers might result in various microbial imbalances, including the vaginal alkaline pH, miscellaneous bacterial growth, elevated white blood cell counts, and increased level of cleanliness [25]. The vaginal anatomical position and its adjacent nature to the cervix, and the cervix’s external orifice, also have a growth of the normal vaginal flora [26]. Consequently, the cervix is correlated with the vaginal microecology. Our present study reports significantly reduced lactobacilli among uterine leiomyoma patients. Additionally, the increased H2O2 positive rate confirmed the vaginal lactobacilli downregulation. The increased NAG and SNA positive rate demonstrated an increased anaerobic infection probability. Additional investigations found that the risk factors for uterine leiomyoma were gravidity, uterine curettage, and abortion (all ≥ 2), pH >4.5, non-Lactobacillus dominant bacteria, vagina flora diversity (grade I, IV), density (grade I, IV), BV, H2O2, and NAG positive.

According to the previous literature, vaccination against HPV is significantly preventive, and HPV screening and prevention modalities are constantly being enhanced. Nevertheless, the incidences of HPV infection remain high [20]. According to the recent findings, oxidative stress, inflammation, and dysregulation of autophagy have a significant role in uterine leiomyoma pathogenesis [27]. In the chronic inflammation process, HPV might disturb the host cells’ dynamic redox balance, further impacting the oxidative stress, hence enhancing the continued release of several molecules from the inflammatory cells, such as macrophages, leading to cell damage.

In this clinic-based, cross-sectional study, we observed an association between persistent high-risk HPV (HR-HPV) and markers of vaginal microbial dysbiosis among women undergoing gynecologic evaluation. While these findings are consistent with the hypothesis that viral persistence and altered vaginal ecology may be related, the cross-sectional design precludes inference of temporality or causality. Our results should therefore be interpreted as descriptive and hypothesis-generating rather than evidence of a causal pathway.

High-risk HPV subtypes, especially HPV16, HPV18, and HPV52, play a role in cervical carcinogenesis through E6 and E7 oncoproteins expression, which disrupt p53 and pRb tumor suppressor pathways [28]. Although uterine leiomyomas are benign smooth muscle tumors, integration of HPV DNA into host cells could theoretically dysregulate the cell cycle, impair apoptosis, and promote abnormal proliferation in myometrial tissue [29].

HPV may also interact with hormonal pathways that drive leiomyoma growth. Experimental studies suggest that E6/E7 expression can influence estrogen and progesterone receptor activity, potentially enhancing steroid-mediated proliferation and extracellular matrix deposition [30]. In addition, persistent infection may trigger local inflammation, cytokine release, and immune evasion, creating a microenvironment supportive of fibroid development [31].

Finally, while HPV52 is less extensively studied than HPV16 and HPV18, it is increasingly detected in gynecologic pathology, particularly in Asian populations [32]. Its co-occurrence with HPV16 and HPV18 in our cohort underscores the possibility of subtype-specific differences in tissue tropism and biological behavior. Taken together, these mechanisms provide a biologically plausible, though still unconfirmed, framework for understanding potential links between HPV infection and uterine leiomyoma pathogenesis. Importantly, our findings are associative and hypothesis-generating; current evidence does not establish a causal role for HPV infection in leiomyoma development.

Since there is an existing association between inflammation, vaginal microecology, and fibrosis, the link between vaginal microecological disorders and HPV infection and uterine leiomyoma was further studied. We first assessed vaginal microecology disorder and HPV infection in patients and reported significantly increased high-risk HPV infection and vaginal microecological disorders in the uterine leiomyoma group. The occurrence of uterine leiomyoma is positively correlated with the HPV infection, but negatively correlated with vaginal pH and the imbalance of vaginal microbiota. Our finding agrees with the reported direct association between vaginal microecological imbalance and HPV infection and infertility, and maternal, cervical cancer, and the pregnant women’s neonatal outcomes [6].Further, the study investigated HPV infection and vaginal microecology of uterine leiomyoma patients at various ages. Our findings demonstrate an association between HPV and the presence of uterine leiomyoma. Even though the observations suggest that HPV infection is more common in women with uterine leiomyomas, this should not be interpreted as HPV being the dominant microorganism in the genital tract. Rather, HPV may represent one of several microbial factors that could be associated with leiomyoma presence, and its relative contribution within the broader vaginal microecological community remains to be established.

While the causal role of high-risk HPV infection in cervical carcinogenesis is firmly established, evidence linking HPV to the pathogenesis of uterine leiomyomas remains weak. Current data are largely cross-sectional, and although some in vitro studies suggest that HPV oncoproteins can alter smooth muscle cell proliferation, these findings do not establish an in vivo pathogenic pathway. Our study demonstrates an association between persistent HPV and fibroid detection, but this should be interpreted with caution. Longitudinal studies integrating virologic, genomic, and mechanistic data will be required to determine whether HPV persistence and sustained dysbiosis precede or contribute to fibroid development.

This study had several limitations. Firstly, due to the cross-sectional nature of the study, causal inferences or underlying mechanisms cannot be established. Dysbiosis and HPV infection may also occur as a consequence of uterine fibroids, for example, due to altered anatomy or abnormal bleeding. Furthermore, as cases were predominantly identified among women presenting with symptoms, there is potential selection bias that may limit the generalizability of our results to asymptomatic populations. These factors should be considered when interpreting the findings, and future longitudinal and mechanistic studies are needed to further explore the temporal and causal relationships between vaginal microbiota and uterine leiomyoma.

Additionally, an important limitation of this study is the use of passive case detection, whereby participants were women who presented to the hospital with their own complaints. This approach may have introduced selection bias, as asymptomatic women without clinical manifestations of uterine leiomyomas were not included. Dysbiosis and HPV infection may be consequences rather than causes of leiomyoma development. Consequently, the findings may not be fully generalizable to the broader population. Future research using active, population-based case detection methods would be valuable to capture both symptomatic and asymptomatic cases, thereby reducing selection bias and improving external validity. In addition, subgroup analyses of less common HPV subtypes, such as HPV42, are constrained by the very small number of positive cases. As such, the statistical estimates for these subgroups are unstable and should be considered exploratory. Larger multicenter studies are required to confirm whether these rare subtypes have any meaningful association with uterine leiomyomas.

Next, the study was conducted in a single-center setting, which may restrict the generalizability of the findings to broader populations with different demographic or clinical characteristics. In addition, although we adjusted for several potential covariates, complete exclusion of residual confounding was impossible. Important factors, such as detailed hormonal status (including serum estrogen and progesterone levels, use of hormone therapy), family history of leiomyoma, lifestyle factors, smoking, and socioeconomic variables, were not comprehensively captured and may have influenced the observed associations. Furthermore, because exposure and outcome were measured at the same time, reverse causality is possible. For instance, preexisting uterine fibroids or gynecologic care-seeking could influence vaginal microbiota or HR-HPV detection patterns.

Our analyses were also constrained by the available data and sample size, which prevented simultaneous adjustment for an extended set of covariates and precluded stable calculation of variance inflation factors for the fully specified models. Consequently, residual confounding and collinearity remain possible explanations for the observed associations. Future investigations with larger, population-based samples should re-evaluate these associations with full covariate adjustment and formal collinearity diagnostics or apply alternative variable-reduction strategies.

Finally, longitudinal cohort studies with repeated measures of HR-HPV status, microbiome composition, and standardized fibroid assessment are needed to establish whether persistent HR-HPV infection and sustained dysbiosis precede the development or progression of fibroids. Such studies should collect detailed behavioral and clinical covariates, capture health-care seeking and treatment events, and include community-recruited samples to improve external validity. Until then, our findings should be viewed as preliminary evidence that motivates prospective investigation rather than proof of a causal relationship.

Conclusion

According to our study, HPV infection and vaginal microecological imbalance are associated with uterine leiomyoma in this clinic-based cohort; causality cannot be inferred. The Gravidity, abortion, uterine curettage (all ≥ 2), vaginal pH value > 4.5, diversity of vaginal microbiota (grade I, IV), dominant bacteria not Lactobacillus, vaginal bacteria density (grade I, IV), Bacterial vaginosis positive, H2O2 < 2 µ mol/L and the NAG ≥ 0.1 U/mL are all risk factors associated with uterine leiomyoma. The HPV subtypes and the vaginal microecology imbalance are associated with the presence of uterine leiomyoma.
