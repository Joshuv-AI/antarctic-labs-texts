Comparative molecular surveillance of polymorphisms in chloroquine resistance transporter, multidrug resistance 1, and Kelch 13 genes associated with antimalarial drug resistance in Plasmodium falciparum isolates from Western Kenya
Authors: Josephat Bungei, Collins Ouma, Daibin Zhong, Job Oyweri, Ming-Chieh Lee, Guofa Zhou, Harrysone Atieli, John Githure, Chloe Wang, Guiyun Yan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMalaria treatment interventions play a central role in the prevention and control of Plasmodium falciparum infections worldwide. However, the emergence and spread of resistant P. falciparum parasites pose a serious public health challenge, as they reduce the effectiveness of antimalarial drugs and contribute to an increased global malaria burden. This study conducted a comparative molecular surveillance of antimalarial drug resistance in P. falciparum isolates from the Lake endemic and highland epidemic-prone regions of western Kenya.MethodsA cross-sectional study design was employed to screen patients presenting with malaria-associated symptoms among residents of the lake-endemic region of Nyando sub-County in Kisumu County and the highland epidemic-prone region of Marani sub-County in Kisii County. Single-nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) were genotyped in the chloroquine resistance transporter (Pfcrt), multidrug resistance 1 (Pfmdr1), and Kelch 13 (Pfk13) genes to identify validated molecular markers associated with resistance to chloroquine, artemisinin derivatives, and artemisinin partner drugs. A comparative analysis was conducted to assess mutation frequencies, genetic diversity, and selection pressure on molecular markers associated with antimalarial drug resistance between the Lake-endemic region and the highland epidemic-prone region.ResultsGenotyping of molecular markers detected M74I, N75E, and K76T mutations each at a frequency of 5.4% in Pfcrt, N146I (0.9%), Y184F (48.2%), and D1246Y (3.6%) in Pfmdr1, and S522C (0.9%), R561H (0.9%), and A675V (12.8%) in Pfk13. Comparative analysis revealed a significant prevalence of the Pfcrt M74I, N75E, and K76T alleles (11.5%) and the Pfk13 A675V allele (27.5%) in the Kisii region (p < 0.05). Pfcrt CVIET (5.4%), Pfmdr1 NFD (46.7%), and Pfk13 SRV (12.8%) were the dominant haplotypes. Additionally, a novel Pfmdr1 N146I mutation and rare Pfk13 S522C and R561H mutations were detected in the Kisumu region. Although the Kisii region presented greater genetic diversity of Pfk13, neutrality statistics, Tajima’s D (−1.8474, p < 0.05), Fu’s Fs (−3.835), and Fu and Li’s F (−3.5041, p < 0.02), suggest significant population expansion in the Kisumu region, potentially driven by the emergence of the rare S522C and R561H alleles.ConclusionOur findings reveal the persistence of the Pfcrt CVIET haplotype and elevated Pfk13 A675V mutation in Kisii, with a moderate prevalence of Pfmdr1 Y184F mutation in both regions. Crucially, we detected emerging novel Pfmdr1 N146I and rare Pfk13 S522C and R561H mutations in Kisumu. Given that artemether-lumefantrine is Kenya's first-line treatment, continuous molecular surveillance is essential to elucidate the selection pressures driving the spread of resistant P. falciparum in western Kenya.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12936-025-05755-2.

Background

The sustained evolution, emergence, and spread of antimalarial drug-resistant Plasmodium falciparum constitute major contributors to the malaria-related burden in Kenya, the sub-Saharan Africa region, and globally. Among the approximately 263 million cases and 597 thousand deaths reported in 2023, 94% occurred in the sub-Saharan region, with Kenya experiencing approximately 3.2 million cases and 11,500 deaths [1]. To overcome the changing trends of P. falciparum resistance, the World Health Organization (WHO), in collaboration with respective ministries of health, has adjusted drug policies over time in response to emerging parasite resistance and declining drug efficacy. Historically, Kenya’s first-line antimalarial drug policy has changed from chloroquine (CQ) (1986–1998) to sulfadoxine-pyrimethamine (SP) (1998–2004), and currently to artemether-lumefantrine (AL), owing to the sustained occurrence of drug-resistant P. falciparum parasites in the population [2, 3]. Consequently, the WHO has established molecular markers associated with the historical use of antimalarial drugs on the basis of therapeutic efficacy studies in various regions across the world and recommends regular surveillance in health centers and communities to detect and monitor their trends among populations [4]. Regular molecular surveillance plays a crucial role in detecting and monitoring trends in P. falciparum resistance within populations, informing evidence-based decisions in drug policy, and ensuring the effective and sustainable use of available antimalarial regimens.

Continuous surveillance of resistant P. falciparum has led to the identification and validation of molecular markers related to resistance against chloroquine, artemisinin derivatives, and artemisinin partner drugs. The emergence and spread of antimalarial-resistant strains of P. falciparum in Southeast Asian countries, as well as in the sub-Saharan African region, reflect the great extent of the public health risk posed by malaria [5–8]. The identification and validation of single-nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) in P. falciparum genes as biomarkers have become the cornerstone of molecular surveillance of resistant strains of parasites [9]. The common molecular markers associated with drug resistance in P. falciparum are the chloroquine resistance transporter gene (Pfcrt), multidrug resistance gene 1 (Pfmdr1), dihydrofolate reductase (Pfdhfr), dihydropteroate synthetase (Pfdhps), and Kelch 13 (Pfk13) [4, 9]. Therefore, the use of these molecular markers in the assessment of drug resistance trends in malaria patients is critical for informing the development and use of effective treatment interventions.

Among mutations in the Pfcrt gene (PF3D7_0709000), the validated molecular markers commonly used for detecting chloroquine resistance are C72S, M74I, N75E, and K76T [4]. Numerous studies performed in the Democratic Republic of Congo, Nigeria, Burkina Faso, Ethiopia, Uganda, and Kenya have identified the existence of Pfcrt mutants in populations dominated by CVIET (K76T) haplotypes [10–13]. A combination of wild-type and mutation alleles of validated molecular markers in the Pfmdr1 gene (PF3D7_0523000) has been linked to resistance against chloroquine (86Y), amodiaquine (N86, 1246Y), and lumefantrine (N86, 184F, and D1246) [4]. Molecular surveillance in Kenya indicated that chloroquine-resistant strains are present on Ngodhe Island in Lake Victoria [14], Siaya [15], and Asembo [16]. Among the 13 validated mutations in the Pfk13 gene (F446I, N458Y, C469T, M476I, Y493H, R539T, I543T, P553L, R561H, P574L, C580Y, R622I, and A675V), six of them (M476I, P553L, R561H, P574L, C580Y and A675V) have been identified in four African countries, namely, Kenya, Tanzania, Uganda, Rwanda, and Ghana [4, 17]. Therefore, continued monitoring of molecular markers in Pfcrt, Pfmdr1, and Pfk13 genes is necessary to identify trends in antimalarial resistance.

This study addresses the critical challenge of antimalarial drug resistance in P. falciparum by conducting a comparative molecular surveillance in two distinct epidemiological regions of western Kenya. Specifically, we aim to: (1) Determine the prevalence and distribution of known SNPs associated with antimalarial drug resistance in the Pfcrt, Pfmdr1, and Pfk13 genes among P. falciparum isolates from the Lake endemic (Kisumu County) and highland epidemic-prone (Kisii County) regions. (2) Compare genetic diversity and assess the selection pressure acting on these key drug resistance molecular markers between P. falciparum populations in the Lake endemic and highland epidemic-prone regions. (3) Identify novel or emerging mutations in Pfcrt, Pfmdr1, and Pfk13 genes that may confer resistance to current antimalarial treatments in both study areas. (4) Evaluate the dominant haplotypes of Pfcrt, Pfmdr1, and Pfk13 associated with drug resistance and analyze their regional distribution.

Methods

Study sites and target population

This study was conducted across three health facilities targeting populations in regions characterized by distinct epidemiological zones in western Kenya: Eramba Health Center (EHC) and Marani sub-County Hospital (MSCH) in Kisii County, and Ahero sub-County Hospital (ASCH) in Kisumu County (Fig. 1). Two study health facilities in Marani sub-County, Kisii County, EHC (0° 32′ 57.84″S, 34° 46′ 51.24″ E) and MSCH (0° 34′ 32.39″ S, 34° 48′ 06.08″ E), are highland areas classified as epidemic-prone regions with altitudes of approximately 1700 m above sea level. On the other hand, ASCH (0° 10′ 24.60′′ S, 34° 55′ 24.96′′ E) in Nyando sub-County, Kisumu County, is a lowland area classified as the Lake endemic region with an altitude of approximately 1100 m above sea level. Kisumu is a holoendemic region characterized by high and persistent transmission, with a P. falciparum prevalence of about 20%, whereas Kisii is a hypoendemic region characterized by low and unstable transmission, with a prevalence of nearly 1% [2, 18]. Study design and sample collection

A cross-sectional research design was used to conduct comparative genotyping of drug resistance markers in the Pfcrt, Pfmdr1, and Pfk13 genes in P. falciparum isolates from the Kisumu and Kisii regions. These study regions were purposively selected to capture variations in the prevalence of drug resistance across regions with varying malaria transmission intensities. Between June and August 2024, 1,818 individuals presenting with signs and symptoms associated with malaria were screened and enrolled in the study after providing informed consent. Through a finger-prick, approximately 200 µl of capillary blood was collected and used to prepare thin and thick Giemsa-stained smears for microscopy and dried blood spot (DBS) on Whatman paper (GE Healthcare, Cardiff, UK) for preservation and molecular analysis. The DBS samples were subsequently stored under desiccating conditions in Ziploc bags with silica beads and transported in a cooler box to the sub-Saharan-Africa International Center of Excellence for Malaria Research (ICEMR) laboratory, where they were preserved at − 4 °C for analytical procedures. Microscopy-based diagnosis indicated that malaria positivity was highest in the endemic Kisumu region at ASCH (88/472, 18.64%), compared to lower rates in the hypoendemic Kisii region at MSCH (53/808, 6.56%) and EHC (38/538, 7.06%). Malaria-positive samples were selected from individuals with no reported travel history within the past month, following a multistage sampling strategy [20].

Molecular detection of parasites

An optimized saponin-Chelex method [21, 22] was used to extract deoxyribonucleic acid (DNA) from malaria-positive DBS samples, which were then stored at −20 °C. Real-time/quantitative polymerase chain reaction (RT/qPCR) was performed on QuantStudio 3 system (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Carlsbad, CA) using species-specific primers and probes to detect the P. falciparum var gene acid terminal sequence (PfvarATS) (PF3D7_0617400) [23]. The qPCR was set up using species-specific primers (0.4 µL of 10 µM each for forward and reverse) and probes (0.5 µL of 2 µM for each species) targeting P. ovale, P. malariae, and P. falciparum. The reaction mixture also included 6 µL of 2X PerfeCTa qPCR ToughMix (QuantaBio, Beverly, MA), 2 µL of sample DNA, and 0.1 µL of nuclease-free water, resulting in a final reaction volume of 12 µL. The qPCR thermal profile consisted of initial incubation at 50 °C for 2 min, followed by 45 cycles of denaturation at 95 °C for 2 min, denaturation at 95 °C for 3 s, and annealing at 58 °C for 30 s [24]. DNA samples from isolates with cycle threshold values below 32 were selected for genotyping of the Pfcrt, Pfmdr1, and Pfk13 genes because they contained adequate parasitemia.

Target amplification and sequencing

Bio-Rad T100 Thermal Cycler® (Bio-Rad Laboratories, Inc., Hercules, CA, USA) was used to perform standard PCR to amplify the Pfcrt and Pfmdr1 genes, and nested PCR was used to amplify the Pfk13 gene. In a 25 µL reaction mixture, optimized thermal cycling conditions and primers targeting the corresponding genes were used for target amplification (Additional file 1, Table S1). Gel electrophoresis was performed using 1.5% agarose, 0.2 µL RedSafe™ (New England Biolabs, Ipswich, MA, USA), and a 100 bp DNA ladder (New England Biolabs, Ipswich, MA, USA). SmartDoc™ Imaging System (Accuris, Edison, NJ, USA) was used to check the expected amplicon sizes of Pfcrt (315 bp), Pfmdr1 (448 bp, 300 bp), and Pfk13 (849 bp). QIAquick PCR Purification Kit® (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany) and the Sanger method (GENEWIZ, Inc., La Jolla, CA) were used for the purification and sequencing of amplicons in both the forward and reverse directions, respectively.

Sequence data analysis

The raw data obtained from the Sanger sequencing technique were analyzed using ChromasPro® software version 2.2.0 (Technelysium Pty Ltd, South Brisbane, Australia) by visualizing trace files, trimming low-quality reads, generating contigs, aligning with references, inspecting mutations, and creating a multi-FASTA file [25]. P. falciparum 3D7 reference sequences for the Pfcrt gene (PF3D7_0709000), the Pfmdr1 gene (PF3D7_0523000) and the Pfk13 gene (PF3D7_1343700) were obtained from PlasmoDB [26]. Single-nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) and mixed genotypes were identified from chromatograms based on base call quality, with SNPs appearing as single peaks and mixed genotypes as double peaks, where the minor peak exceeded 30% of the major peak. Alignment, translation, and visualization of sequences were done using Multiple Sequence Comparison by Log-Expectation (MUSCLE) in Molecular Evolutionary Genetics Analysis (MEGA 12) to reveal synonymous and nonsynonymous SNPs [27]. Frequencies and permutations of validated SNPs in Pfcrt at codons 72–76, Pfmdr1 at codons 86, 184, and 1246, and Pfk13 at codons 446–675 were used to calculate frequencies of genotypes and haplotypes. The chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test was used to compare the frequencies of identified validated SNPs in the Kisumu and Kisii regions (alpha < 0.05). Tajima’s D, Fu’s Fs, and Fu and Li’s F tests for diversity and selection [28] were conducted using DNA Sequence Polymorphism software (DnaSP v6). The nucleotide sequence datasets for Pfcrt, Pfmdr1, and PfK13 resistance markers were deposited in GenBank under the accession numbers PX439136-PX439247, PX359901-PX360124, and PX360125-PX360233, respectively.

Results

Comparative molecular surveillance of P. falciparum isolates (N = 112), which were obtained after screening 1,818 individuals for malaria in the Kisumu and Kisii regions, revealed both shared and region-specific patterns of drug resistance mutations in the Pfcrt, Pfmdr1, and Pfk13 genes. Validated mutations in these genes associate with resistance to chloroquine, artemisinin partner drugs, and artemisinin derivatives. Genotyping of the Pfcrt gene at five key codons (C72S, V73, M74I, N75E, and K76T) identified resistance-associated mutations indicative of historical chloroquine use (Table 1). While no polymorphisms were detected at codons 72 and 73, mutations at codons M74I, N75E, and K76T were each detected in 5.4% of the isolates. Notably, these mutations were exclusively observed in isolates from the Kisii region (11.5%), whereas none were detected in Kisumu isolates (0/60), indicating a statistically significant regional difference in the prevalence of Pfcrt mutations (M74I/N75E/K76T) (p < 0.05) (Table 1). Genotyping of the Pfmdr1 gene revealed the absence of a mutation at codon N86Y but the presence of mutations at codons N146I (0.9%), Y184F (48.2%) with mixed genotypes (12.5%), and D1246Y (3.6%) with mixed genotypes (4.5%). The N146I mutation (437A > T) was identified as a novel variant in the Kisumu region. The Y184F was the most prevalent mutation, with similar frequencies observed in Kisumu (48.3%) and Kisii (48.1%). In contrast, the prevalence of the D1246Y mutation was significantly higher in Kisumu (6.7%) compared with Kisii, where it was not detected (p < 0.05) (Table 1). In the genotyping analysis of the PfKelch13 gene, mutations at codons S522C, R561H, and A675V were identified. The S522C mutation (1.7%) and R561H mutation (1.7%) were detected exclusively in the Kisumu region, while the wild-type alleles were dominant in both regions. The A675V mutation demonstrated a significantly higher prevalence in the Kisii region (27.5%) (p < 0.05), while the Kisumu region showed mixed genotypes (3.4%) (Table 1).

Haplotype analysis was performed on five Pfcrt alleles (C72S, V73, M74I, N75E, and K76T), four Pfmdr1 alleles (N86Y, N146I, Y184F, and D1246Y), and three Pfk13 alleles (Table 2). For the Pfcrt gene, the CVIET haplotype was exclusively common in the Kisii region (11.5%) but absent in the Kisumu region. The NNYD wild-type haplotype (48.2%) and the single-mutant NNFD haplotype (47.3%) comprised approximately half of the isolates (48.2%), with almost the same prevalence between the two study regions. The single-mutant NNYY haplotype (5%), the novel-mutant NIYD haplotype (1.7%), and the double-mutant NNFY haplotype (1.7%) were present only in the Kisumu region. The SRA wild-type haplotype (85.3%) was the most dominant haplotype, with a significantly higher prevalence in Kisumu (96.6%) than in Kisii (85.3%) (p < 0.05) In contrast, the SRV single-mutant haplotype (12.8%) was significantly more dominant in Kisii (27.5%) than in Kisumu, where none were detected (p < 0.05). CRA and SHA were single-mutant haplotypes observed only in the Kisumu region. The analysis of combined haplotypes revealed variations in the prevalence of drug resistance markers in P. falciparum isolates from the Kisumu and Kisii regions (Table 2). The wild-type CVMNK-NNYD-SRA was the most dominant haplotype in the overall population (40.4%), but it had a lower prevalence in the Kisii region (35.3%) than in the Kisumu region (44.8%). The single-mutant CVMNK-NNFD-SRA haplotype ranked second in prevalence (37.6%), with a significantly higher prevalence in Kisumu (43.1%) than in Kisii (31.4%) (p < 0.05). The single-mutant CVMNK-NNYD-SRV (13.7%) and double-mutant CVMNK-NNFD-SRV (7.8%) were significantly dominant in Kisii but absent in Kisumu (p < 0.05). While the single-mutant CVMNK-NNYY-SRA (5.2%) was only present in Kisumu, the CVIET-associated quadruple-mutant CVIET-NNFD-SRA (5.9%), quintuple-mutant CVIET-NNFD-SRV (3.9%), and quadruple-mutant CVIET-NNYD-SRV (1.9%) were detected only in Kisii. The remaining haplotypes associated with the novel mutation (N146I), S522C, and R561H were all linked to the CVMNK wild-type.

The results of cluster analysis showed similarities and differences in the distributions of genotypes and haplotypes associated with antimalarial drug resistance in the Pfcrt, Pfmdr1, and Pfk13 genes of P. falciparum isolates from the Kisumu and Kisii regions in western Kenya (Fig. 2). Discussion

This study conducted comparative genotyping of drug resistance markers in the Pfcrt, Pfmdr1, and Pfk13 genes associated with antimalarial drug resistance among P. falciparum isolates from the Kisumu and Kisii regions in western Kenya. The findings demonstrated variation in the prevalence of resistance markers in the Pfcrt and Pfk13 genes but similarities in the Pfmdr1 gene. These findings are in tandem with those of previous studies on Ngodhe Island [14], a systematic review of sub-Saharan studies [8], and multicounty studies [29, 30], and a comparison of Kenya and Ethiopia [18], suggesting shared and varied selection pressures on the occurrence, evolution, and spread of resistant P. falciparum parasites in the population.

Chloroquine resistance markers in the Pfcrt gene

Genotyping analysis of the Pfcrt gene confirmed the presence of chloroquine-resistant P. falciparum strains at a low prevalence rate of 5.4% in western Kenya, with an exclusively significant prevalence in Kisii (11.5%). Since the withdrawal of chloroquine as a first-line antimalarial treatment in 1999, the prevalence of resistant parasites in the population has gradually declined. A comparative study among schoolchildren in western Kenya revealed a sustained decline in the prevalence of mutant haplotypes (CVIET) associated with chloroquine resistance from approximately 39% in 2010 to 7% in 2013 [31]. A retrospective study conducted in Kilifi among 1993–2006 clinical samples demonstrated a decline in the prevalence of P. falciparum parasites with the chloroquine resistance marker (Pfcrt K76T) from approximately 95% to 63% over 13 years [32]. A recent study conducted in the endemic region of Lake Victoria reported a similar low prevalence of Pfcrt CVIET haplotypes on Ngodhe Island (4.4%) and Mfangano Island (7.4%), which are lower than the average prevalence reported in East Africa (17%) and West Africa (13%) [14]. Therefore, the current overall low prevalence of 5.4% indicates the continued presence of resistant Pfcrt genotypes (M74I, N75E, K76T), and the resistant haplotype CVIET in western Kenya, possibly signaling either a continuing decline or potential resurgence.

The exclusive detection and significant prevalence of the Pfcrt CVIET haplotype in the Kisii region suggest the localized persistence of chloroquine-resistant P. falciparum within the population, despite the withdrawal of chloroquine as first-line antimalarial therapy more than two decades ago. In contrast, a cross-sectional survey conducted in 2005 among schoolchildren in western Kenya reported the lowest prevalence in Kisii (~ 70%), relative to Kakamega (~ 80%) and Kombewa (~ 90%) [33]. A similar trend was observed in a longitudinal study performed among schoolchildren in western Kenya, which revealed a differential decline in the prevalence of the Pfcrt K76T mutant from about 80% in 2003 to 11.8% in Kakamega and 2.7% in Kombewa in 2015 [34]. Evidently, the complete absence of the resistant haplotype (CVIET) in Kisumu aligns with those from an extensive study conducted among schoolchildren in eight counties in the Lake endemic region, which reported a fixation of the wild-type CVMNK haplotype, signifying the re-emergence of chloroquine-sensitive P. falciparum populations [35]. The higher prevalence of the Pfcrt CVIET haplotype in Kisumu than in to Kisii may be due to a greater drug pressure of lumefantrine associated with higher malaria endemicity, leading to selection for the Pfcrt K76 genotype [4, 8]. Additionally, regional differences in treatment practices, transmission intensity, and epidemiological factors could account for the variation in resistant genotypes and haplotypes.

Multiple drug resistance markers in the Pfmdr1 gene

In the genotyping analysis of mutations associated with chloroquine resistance in the Pfmdr1 gene, this study detected the absence of the N86Y mutation, but it identified a low prevalence of D1246Y (3.6%). These two validated Pfmdr1 mutations are important molecular markers for chloroquine and amodiaquine resistance in P. falciparum populations [36]. The absence of the N86Y mutation in both the Kisumu and Kisii regions corroborates the findings from a previous study conducted across eight counties in the western region [35]. Based on the decreasing prevalence of the N86Y mutation, a temporal study indicated the progressive re-emergence of chloroquine-sensitive P. falciparum strains in the coastal region of Kenya over the past two decades [37]. Additionally, the low prevalence of the D1246Y mutation in Kisumu and the existence of mixed genotypes in Kisii signal the disappearance of chloroquine-resistant P. falciparum strains in these study populations [30, 37]. Haplotype analysis revealed a low prevalence of NYY haplotypes (2.8%) in western Kenya, as confirmed by previous studies [14, 15, 31, 38]. A 2000–2021 systematic review in six sub-Saharan countries highlighted a consistent decreasing trend in Pfmdr1 N86Y and D1246Y genotypes, with a greater decrease observed in East Africa compared with other regions [8]. The low traces of Pfmdr1 NYY haplotypes predict the re-emergence of chloroquine-sensitive P. falciparum parasites in the population and a decline in resistance.

Furthermore, genotype analysis detected a high prevalence of the Pfmdr1 Y184F mutation (48.2%) and mixed genotypes (12.5%). This mutation is a validated molecular marker associated with drug pressure from artemisinin, lumefantrine, or mefloquine resistance [4, 39]. As Kenya has been using artemether-lumefantrine (AL) as a first-line treatment for the past two decades, the prevalence of this mutation in the population has increased. The findings of this study show that both the Kisii and Kisumu regions have the highest prevalence of Pfmdr1 Y184F mutations (48.2%) compared to those reported in previous studies on Ngodhe Island (47.1%), Mfangano Island (31.7%), East Africa (39%), and West Africa (15.5%) [14]. A therapeutic efficacy study demonstrated an increase in the prevalence of the Pfmdr1 Y184F from 35% in pretreatment samples to 45.8% among recrudescent samples after treatment with AL [15]. A systematic review of studies conducted in the past two decades in the sub-Saharan region revealed an increasing prevalence of the Pfmdr1 Y184F mutation in Kenya, Uganda, and Nigeria [8]. Further analysis revealed that Pfmdr1 Y184F mutation contributes meaningfully to the mutant NFD haplotype (47.3%) and AL resistance in the population. The detection of a novel mutation (N146I) signifies the continuous evolution of P. falciparum strains and highlights the need for constant monitoring and validation to determine its phenotypic effects on drug efficacy.

Artemisinin resistance markers in the Pfk13 gene

Among the 13 validated molecular markers for partial artemisinin resistance in Pfk13 (F446I, N458Y, C469Y, M476I, Y493H, R539T, I543T, P553L, R561H, P574L, C580Y, R622I, and A675V) [4], R561H (0.9%) and A675V (12.8%), as well as an unvalidated S522C (0.9%) mutation, were detected in western Kenya. The detection of R561H as a rare mutation in the Kisumu region revealed that this mutation exists in the Lake endemic region, as detected by a previous study, which reported it as the first detection in Kenya among P. falciparum isolates from Busia, Kisumu, Kisii, Kericho, Nakuru, Baringo, and Laikipia [29]. A 2012–2020 review indicated that African countries have reported a low prevalence of M476I, P553L, R561H, P574L, C580Y, and A675V mutations associated with artemisinin resistance [17]. Pfk13 R561H is an emerging mutation in Tanzania [40] and Uganda [41], but it is more prevalent in Rwanda, where it originated [42]. The detection of S522C in this study is in line with the findings of therapeutic efficacy studies conducted between 2014 and 2018 across nine sub-Saharan countries report the presence of the S522C mutation in Kenyan samples, indicating the persistent occurrence of this mutation [6]. A recent study conducted in 2022 among schoolchildren in 15 counties identified the same mutation at a low prevalence of 3.3% as a mixed genotype [30]. The detection of Pfk13 R561H and S522C shows that the emergence and spread of new resistant genotypes in Kenya continue to occur in the population, presenting serious challenges in the prevention and control of malaria both locally and globally.

The detection of Pfk13 A675V, with a significantly higher prevalence in Kisii (27.5%) than in Kisumu (3.4% mixed genotypes), revealed variation in the selection for this genotype in western Kenya. The emergence and spread of this mutation in the sub-Saharan region has led to its validation as a molecular marker for artemisinin resistance, as established in Uganda to have a significant delay in parasite clearance [5] and supported by a review [43]. As a newly validated molecular marker for artemisinin resistance, a recent study conducted among P. falciparum isolates from western Kenya identified that the Pfk13 A675V mutation had a low prevalence of 2.38% in Kakamega but was absent in the Kombewa, Homa Bay, and Kisii regions [18]. Another recent study also detected A675V mutation as a mixed genotype with a low prevalence (1.7%) among schoolchildren across 15 counties in Kenya [30]. In contrast, a study in Uganda detected the A675V mutation at a relatively high frequency (5%), indicating an increasing trend of artemisinin resistance in the population [41]. The detection of this mutation in Baringo County, a seasonal transmission zone, at a higher prevalence compared with low frequencies observed in the Busia and Kisii regions suggests an independent emergence in the population [29]. Haplotype analysis suggested that the A675V mutation could be linked to CVMNK-NNYD-SRV (13.7%) and CVMNK-NNFD-SRV (7.8%) in the population.

Selection and diversity

The analysis of genotypes and haplotypes showed that the Pfcrt, Pfmdr1, and Pfk13 genes presented varied levels of diversity and selection trends in P. falciparum isolates from the Kisumu and Kisii regions. Pfcrt exhibited low haplotype diversity and moderate nucleotide diversity with a balancing selection of CVIET haplotype (Fu's Fs = 3.314) in the Kisii region. Pfmdr1 had greater genetic diversity, haplotype diversity, and nucleotide diversity in Kisumu relative to Kisii, due to the exclusive presence of the novel N146I and validated D1246Y mutations as singletons. In contrast, Pfk13 exhibited a higher number of haplotypes, genetic diversity, haplotype diversity, and nucleotide diversity in Kisii than in Kisumu due to the presence of the A675V mutation. However, neutrality tests [28] indicated significant population expansion in Kisumu due to the presence of S522C and R561H singletons as indicated by Tajima’s D (− 1.8474), Fu’s Fs (− 3.835), and Fu and Li’s F (− 3.5041). Directional and localized selection of resistant genotypes and haplotypes calls for regional drug policies for effective treatment and elimination of malaria [44]. In this case, the unique occurrence of Pfcrt CVIET and Pfk13 A675V in Kisii and the emergence of novel Pfmdr1 N146I mutation and validated Pfk13 S522C and R561H mutations in Kisumu require close monitoring to establish their selection trends.

Limitations

The study limitations relate to the small sample size and cross-sectional research design. The small sample size limits the generalization of the findings to P. falciparum populations in the Lake endemic and highland epidemic-prone regions. The use of a multistage sampling procedure in various analytical procedures outlined in malaria molecular surveillance enhanced the representation of genotypes and haplotypes in the target population [20]. Given that the cross-sectional research design lacks causal inference between drug pressure and resistance, validated molecular markers of the Pfcrt, Pfmdr1, and Pfk13 genes were used [4]. Nevertheless, the findings highlight the comparative prevalence of resistant P. falciparum strains in the Lake endemic and highland epidemic-prone regions.

Conclusion

Our comparative molecular surveillance of P. falciparum in western Kenya's Lake endemic (Kisumu) and highland epidemic-prone (Kisii) regions revealed both shared and distinct patterns of antimalarial drug resistance. Key findings include: Regional Chloroquine Resistance: Pfcrt mutations (M74I, N75E, K76T) and the CVIET haplotype were exclusive to Kisii, indicating localized selection pressures. Shared Artemisinin Partner Drug Resistance: The comparable prevalence of the Pfmdr1 Y184F mutation in both regions likely reflects common artemether-lumefantrine use. Re-emerging Chloroquine Sensitivity: The fixation of the Pfmdr1 N86 wild-type allele and low prevalence of Pfmdr1 D1246Y mutation suggest a return of chloroquine-sensitive strains. Emerging Resistance Markers: The detection of novel Pfmdr1 N146I mutation, the appearance of Pfk13 S522C and R561H mutations in Kisumu, and the predominance of Pfk13 A675V mutation in Kisii signal divergent evolutionary paths for artemisinin resistance. We recommend intensified molecular surveillance of Pfmdr1 Y184F and Pfk13 A675V mutations due to their prevalence and validated role in resistance. Further research is crucial to understand the selective pressures and ecological drivers underlying the localized dominance of the Pfk13 A675V mutation in Kisii and the emergence of the novel Pfmdr1 N146I mutation, and the spread of Pfk13 S522C/R561H genotypes in Kisumu.
