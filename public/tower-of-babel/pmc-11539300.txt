Agricultural trade liberalization, governance quality, and technical efficiency in the agricultural sector of Southeast Asia
Authors: Veasna Trakem, Hongzhong Fan
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractInternational trade has been regarded as an essential factor in enhancing a country's productivity and efficiency. Nevertheless, developing countries squander money and resources as a result of their deficient institutional quality, impeding their ability to profit from specialization and trade. Therefore, this study intends to investigate the impact of agricultural trade liberalization and governance quality on technical efficiency in Southeast Asia's agricultural sector using balanced panel data spanning from 2002 to 2021. The research utilizes translog stochastic frontier analysis (SFA) with a single-stage maximum likelihood estimation (MLE) to simultaneously calculate the time-varying technical efficiency scores and explore the core factors influencing agricultural inefficiency. The findings reveal that the average output-oriented technical efficiency for ASEAN-8 countries was 94 %. This suggests that there is significant potential to enhance technical efficiency in agricultural production by up to 6 % by addressing the adverse impacts of technical inefficiency. The research findings further point out that Malaysia is the most technically efficient country, having a technical efficiency score of 99.82 %, followed by Vietnam (99.75 %), Thailand (99.70 %), Lao PDR (98.90 %), Myanmar (95.42 %), Indonesia (91.64 %), the Philippines (90.65 %), and Cambodia (76.11 %). The results of disaggregated agricultural trade liberalization demonstrate a significant reduction in agricultural inefficiency in Southeast Asia through agricultural exports and imports. The findings also emphasize that improvements in the rule of law positively contribute to agricultural efficiency, whereas enhancements in terms of voice and accountability and regulatory quality appear to reduce it. Based on these findings, the government should consider enlarging open and liberalized trade policies in order to facilitate the exchange of technology and knowledge within the agriculture sector. Additionally, the government should involve farmers, agricultural cooperatives, local communities, and other relevant stakeholders in the decision-making process to ensure that policies address the specific needs and constraints faced by the sector.Keywords: Agricultural trade liberalization, Governance quality, Agricultural technical efficiency, Translog stochastic frontier analysis, Single-stage maximum likelihood estimation, Southeast Asia

Introduction

The world population is estimated to reach nine billion by 2050, which poses a significant challenge for agricultural experts in order to secure an adequate food supply while preventing the adverse environmental effects associated with increased food production [1,2]. The COVID-19 pandemic, also well-known as the coronavirus pandemic, has caused a substantial global food crisis as a result of some preventive measures such as social distancing, trade restrictions, and statewide lockdowns [[3], [4], [5]]. The invasion of Ukraine by Russia on February 24, 2022, has also caused major interruptions to Ukraine's agricultural exports and brought about a substantial increase in the price of agricultural products. These concerns about energy supply and inflation led to a reassessment of agriculture's role in ensuring food security. Furthermore, the escalating consequences of climate change are having a substantial influence on productivity, resulting in changes in global trade patterns and the relative benefit of agricultural goods [6]. Besides, as per the Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs) set by the United Nations, second sustainable development goal (SDG-2) seeks to end hunger, achieving food security, improving nutrition, and promoting sustainable agriculture [7]. The objective of this SDG-2 is to reduce poverty and hunger, improve the livelihoods and productivity of farmers, conserve biodiversity, and encourage the adoption of sustainable agricultural techniques that are resilient in the long term [8]. According to the United Nations Development Programme (UNDP), this necessitates the mitigation of trade barriers, distortions, and inefficiencies in global agricultural markets, which can be achieved by doubling agricultural productivity [9].

The Association of Southeast Asian Nations (ASEAN) is a regional intergovernmental organization including Brunei Darussalam, Cambodia, Indonesia, Lao PDR, Malaysia, Myanmar, the Philippines, Singapore, Thailand, and Vietnam, with a population of 671 million in 2022 [10,11]. Agriculture is a way of life for the Southeast Asian region due to the fact that 8 out of 10 ASEAN member states are dependent on it for food and production. This sector has significantly contributed to the socio-economic development of ASEAN countries by providing employment opportunities, enhancing food security, and alleviating poverty [12]. Recently, ASEAN has experienced considerable economic transformations, including strong economic growth, agricultural production improvements, and significant agricultural sector restructuring. This transformation has turned the region into a net agricultural exporter, significantly enhancing food security [13]. During the 1960s and 1970s, the majority of the Southeast Asian population resided in rural areas. However, there has been a notable change since most of the population now lives in urban areas. This upward trend is also observable in less urbanized countries such as Cambodia, Laos, and Myanmar, where urbanization rates are increasing dramatically [14]. This transformation has significantly impacted the labor markets in the region as its economy has grown. From 1991 to 2022, the proportion of the Southeast Asian population employed in agriculture decreased from 60 % to 29.1 %. The lower income ASEAN countries such as Lao PDR, Myanmar, and Cambodia, agriculture contributed, respectively, 69.57 %, 45.54 %, and 36.64 % to overall employment in 2022.

The agricultural sector plays a crucial role in the economy of ASEAN countries, accounting for around 11 % of the region's GDP in 2022. Countries like Cambodia and Myanmar have a high reliance on agriculture, which contributes to more than 22 % of their total national GDP. Fig. 1 illustrates the contribution of agriculture, forestry, and fishing to the gross domestic product (GDP) expressed as a percentage for the ASEAN-8 countries over the period from 2003 to 2022. Although the proportion of agriculture to GDP and employment has been declining, it remains an essential driver of future economic progress, poverty reduction, and food safety in Southeast Asia. Robust and ongoing investments in the agriculture sector are necessary for sustained economic growth and structural transformation. In order to achieve long-term success in decreasing and sustained improvements in food security, it is crucial to promote an emerging economy that efficiently combines labor and capital markets in both rural and urban regions, resulting in increased production [15]. Therefore, it's important to prioritize improving agricultural productivity in Southeast Asia to foster sustainable agricultural development in the region.

The World Trade Organization (WTO) was established in 1995 as the successor of the General Agreement on Tariffs and Trade (GATT). At the present time, there are 164 member states, including all 10 members of ASEAN [16]. Nugroho et al. [17] claim that both the GATT and the WTO have played a significant role in fostering an effective and beneficial trading system via trade agreements. Indeed, Asia has demonstrated an unwavering commitment to embracing free trade policies and now holds the position of being the world's greatest trading region. Accordingly, in 2017, Asia represented 38 % of the total global exports and 31 % of the total global imports [18]. Discussions about economic openness and its potential to reduce poverty have focused on agriculture, leading to the implementation of numerous agreements and policies to promote an open economy in this area [17]. These measures encompass the discontinuation of local production assistance operations, immediate government intervention, impediments in agricultural decision-making processes, labor market restructuring, land reform, farm conversion, and the establishment of market and collective-action institutions [[19], [20], [21], [22]].

Following its inception in 1967, ASEAN has served as the basis for several collaborative accords, including the establishment of the ASEAN Free Trade Area (AFTA) in 1992 and the ASEAN Economic Community (AEC) in 2015. The AEC framework's purpose is to improve the efficiency of production factors in order to establish a continuous market within the region by facilitating unrestricted movement of commodities, skilled labor, services, and investment among its members. More precisely, the implementation of the AEC has generated a beneficial effect on the agricultural sector. It has enhanced the performance and competitiveness of agricultural trade among nations within the Southeast Asian region, leading to a steady expansion in the intra-ASEAN markets [18]. The implementation of the AEC has also facilitated market access for agricultural products in the ASEAN region. Prior to the implementation of the AEC, ASEAN member states also officially approved and implemented the ASEAN Trade in Goods Agreement (ATIGA) in February 2009, which came into force in May 2010. The objective of the agreement was to establish unrestricted trade in products, reducing trade barriers, decreasing business costs, and creating a broader market to facilitate enterprises in operating with greater economies of scale. Subsequently, ASEAN nations have successfully eradicated nearly all import taxes on trade inside the ASEAN region. The current focus is on resolving non-tariff measures (NTMs) that may significantly hinder regional trade, especially in the agriculture sector [23].

As a result of these endeavors, the agricultural and food trade of ASEAN has been consistently growing, representing 8.2 % of worldwide exports and 6.5 % of worldwide imports in 2018. More precisely, the value of exports has increased from $21.6 billion in 1995 to $99.6 billion in 2018 [24]. Thailand and Vietnam, among other ASEAN nations, play a crucial role in exporting certain agricultural products, primarily rice. These two nations consistently hold the position of leading global rice exporters, accounting for approximately 70 % of the world's rice exports [25]. In 2022, ASEAN's agricultural exports to China reached a value of around $35 billion, making up 17.6 % of ASEAN's overall agricultural exports. This makes China the second-largest market for ASEAN's agricultural products, behind intra-ASEAN trade [11]. Conversely, ASEAN's agricultural imports have also experienced a significant rise, expanding from $14.9 billion in 1995 to $79 billion in 2018. The United States is the world's greatest importer, with a total import value of $119 billion. Following closely behind is ASEAN, which is the second-largest importer, surpassing Germany, with an import value of $76.6 billion [24]. In light of the fact that agriculture plays a crucial role not solely for the ASEAN region but also for the world as a whole, it is imperative to implement policies that enhance efficiency and productivity in this sector.

Adopting advanced production technologies is a highly effective strategy to ensure that producers engage in optimal and financially profitable endeavors [26]. This underscores the need for efficiency in light of the limited availability of natural resources and budgetary limitations. Efficiency measurement is essential for producers to assess how productivity may be enhanced by optimizing resource use, resulting in increased output and profitability. Furthermore, efficiency is a crucial factor in determining whether the company should prioritize enhancing productivity or developing new technical solutions to become a frontrunner in different areas of agricultural production [27]. From this standpoint, this perspective underscores the significance of resource management, given its direct impact on productivity levels. Increased technical efficiency leads to improved output in the agriculture industry, where environmental and regulatory standards limit and regulate resources. As a result, the evaluation of efficiency becomes of utmost importance, as it serves as the primary measure for the acceleration of production and the mitigation of inefficiency. Farmers benefit from understanding the efficacy of their production operations because it enables them to use resources wisely, mitigate soil degradation, reduce costs, and increase profitability [26].

International trade is commonly acknowledged as a key factor in improving a nation's productivity. An assessment of productivity growth involves the analysis of two separate and interconnected elements, such as technological change (TC) and technical efficiency change (TEC) [[28], [29], [30], [31]]. Technological change refers to a shift in the production possibility frontier (PPF), while technical efficiency change involves movement towards or away from the PPF [42]. In essence, TEC quantifies the disparity between potential and actual outputs. Furthermore, the nexus between trade liberalization and technical efficiency has become a subject of increasing debate in global, regional, and national development policy discussions. Disagreements over the impact of trade liberalization on technical efficiency stem from a lack of systematic conceptualizations establishing a connection between trade policy and technical efficiency [32]. The lack of an obvious and comprehensive theory providing an exclusive solution to the problem has led scholars to carry out empirical research [33]. Consequently, the impact of trade liberalization on agricultural technical efficiency remains uncertain, despite its documented positive effect on productivity.

In addition, the performance of agriculture is affected by social and political factors [34]. Empirical evidence suggests that the efficiency of production largely depends on institutional quality when agricultural trade is liberalized. For instance, Islam et al. [35] have demonstrated that trade liberalization significantly impacts institutional quality but not governance measures such as voice and accountability as well as political stability. They confirmed that both natural and policy measures of trade openness are essential. Trade concerning natural resource exports continues to be correlated with poor governance after trade liberalization is accounted for. Similarly, in their analysis of 79 countries, Rodrik et al. [36] provide evidence to support the positive correlation between trade openness and institutional quality. Additionally, Rigobon et al. [37] discovered that the openness of trade negatively affects income levels and democracy but positively influences the rule of law. They claimed that higher income leads to greater openness and better institutions, although these effects are not particularly strong. While several studies offer evidence supporting the positive correlation between trade openness and institutions, the exact impact of governance quality on technical efficiency remains a topic of discussion.

There is a dearth of research on how agricultural trade liberalization affects the technical efficiency of the agricultural sector, despite some studies suggesting that it can accelerate the transfer of technology and innovation, leading to increased growth in total factor productivity (TFP) [30,38,39]. Even though there have been several studies conducted on this particular subject, there is a lack of conclusiveness and generalizability due to varying sampling parameters, regional disparities, country-specific socio-economic dynamics, and econometric models. Notably, several studies, including Miljkovic et al. [33], Hart et al. [40], and Sunge and Ngepah [41], have specifically investigated the impact of trade liberalization on agricultural technical efficiency. These studies did not differentiate between agricultural exports and imports in their analysis. However, Shaik and Miljkovic [42] were the only ones to break down trade openness into agricultural exports and imports within a single country. Thus, the distinctive effect of splitting down trade openness into separate categories of agricultural exports and imports across different nations has not yet been given enough scrutiny. An in-depth comprehension of the precise impacts of agricultural exports and imports on technical efficiency is of utmost importance, as it may provide valuable insights for policymakers and stakeholders in the agricultural sector. All studies on this issue have failed to simultaneously compute output elasticities and assess other external factors that affect agricultural technical efficiency. As a result, our current research adds significant value to the existing body of knowledge by concurrently computing the technical efficiency score, calculating output elasticities, and disaggregating trade openness into agricultural exports and imports. Furthermore, according to Olson [43], developing nations waste money and resources due to their inadequate institutional framework, which prevents them from properly capitalizing on specialization and trade. Therefore, our work adds to the existing literature by integrating aspects of agricultural trade with government quality all at once.

This has motivated us to investigate the role of agricultural trade liberalization and governance quality on agricultural technical efficiency in Southeast Asia using translog stochastic frontier analysis (SFA) in a single-stage maximum likelihood estimation (MLE), as proposed by Battese and Coelli [44]. This approach allows us to calculate the technical efficiency score for each Southeast Asian country, measure their ability to produce output using the same resources, and simultaneously evaluate the impact of agricultural trade liberalization and governance quality on technical efficiency in the region. An innovative aspect of this study lies in our estimation of technical efficiency at the country level, extending beyond the conventional focus solely on firm-level analysis. This approach offers a comprehensive perspective on the overall efficiency landscape within a country's agricultural sector. Additionally, the study aims to identify the pivotal influencing factors of technical efficiency by incorporating agricultural exports and imports, along with three governance indicators. This thorough investigation aims to explore how improving efficiency at the national level might enhance profitability and productivity in agricultural production. The findings of this study are deemed valuable for individuals engaged in both farm-level and national decision-making processes. Moreover, the outcomes of the study are expected to provide useful insights and recommendations to improve the technical efficiency of the agricultural sector in the Southeast Asian region, particularly ASEAN-8 countries.

The rest of this paper is organized as follows. Section 2 reports the literature review which including conceptual framework of technical efficiency and empirical literature review. In Section 3, we detail the research methodology of this study, including the specification of the stochastic frontier analysis (SFA) model and its functional forms, the technical inefficiency model, variable description, and data sources. The results presentation and discussion are presented in Section 4. Finally, in Section 5, we conclude with key findings and policy implications of the study.

Literature review

The concept of technical efficiency

Technical efficiency (TE) assesses how well a farmer applies a variety of agricultural inputs to achieve the highest possible yield in a production [45]. Improving technical efficiency, or reducing technical inefficiency, leads to heightened productivity by enabling more effective utilization of resources, leading to higher output [46]. Farrell [47] introduced the term of TE, delineating it as the deviation of an observation from the PPF assessed by a firm's output. In essence, it signifies, at a given technological level, the proficiency of a firm in converting inputs into outputs. Furthermore, Farrell conducted an analysis of a firm that employs two components of production, X and Y, to produce an output, P, while functioning under the paradigm of continuous return to scale. A single isoquant SS΄, which reflects different combinations of the two inputs of production that an efficient business can use to make an output, as seen in Fig. 2. The parameter P denotes the merger of two inputs that may be employed by the firm to produce one unit of output. The lower boundary of the SS' isoquant displays the same level of output as indicated by points Q and P, which are located on the same isoquant. Point Q illustrates an efficient combination of two factors that the company employs in the same ratio as P. Utilizing only a portion of each input, specifically OQ/OP, the company achieves the same output as P, maximizing the output (OP/OQ) from the same inputs. Consequently, the TE of the company is represented by the ratio OQ/OP. The gap QP depicts the company's technical inefficiency, indicating the extent to which all factors can be proportionally reduced without diminishing output. In a technically efficient company, the ratio remains stable at 1, whereas inefficiency is indicated by a ratio below 1. Fig. 2 shows that the budget line, represented by AA΄, has a slope comparable to the ratio of the prices of the two production components. Hence, the optimal combination of two production factors is determined at the position where the isoquant is parallel to the budget line AA΄. Point Q′ represents the optimal combination of the two production variables at which the company achieves technical efficiency. TE is a quantitative metric that evaluates the level of output with respect to the highest level that may be effectively achieved by a farmer with the same inputs. The optimal production frontier takes into account both-sided error terms, which include external influences that are outside of the cultivators' control. Attaining an optimal output level is not feasible for farmers; so, an additional error term becomes crucial in demonstrating technical efficiency [74].

Empirical literature review

Adam Smith [48] initially acknowledged the significance of trade liberalization, arguing that it promotes the extension of the division of labor, resulting in increased productivity. Today's society continues to widely accept that theory [40]. The impact of trade liberalization on TC and TEC is not equivalent. Although trade generally does not cause negative TC, it can result in either positive or negative TEC, as proven by Iyer et al. [49]. The absence of agreement over the influence of trade liberalization on technical efficiency stems from the absence of coherent ideas that connect trade policy to technical efficiency [32]. The lack of a definitive and transparent theory that could provide a single solution to the problem pushed the profession to pursue empirical research on the subject. Only a limited number of studies have extensively examined the simultaneous relationship between agricultural trade liberalization, governance quality, and technical efficiency. For instance, Shaik and Miljkovic [42] and Miljkovic et al. [33] employed an SFA model with a Cobb-Douglas functional form to assess the impact of trade liberalization on agricultural technical efficiency. Their findings clearly indicate that trade liberalization does not significantly influence the technical efficiency of the agricultural sector in the United States and Brazil, respectively. Shaik and Miljkovic [42] further disaggregated trade openness into the share of imports and exports, discovering that the strengthening of export protection did not affect technical efficiency, while imports appeared to mitigate technical inefficiency. In the context of EU agriculture, Hart et al. [40] furnish evidence that trade liberalization yields a sudden and adverse effect on efficiency. Efficiency is, however, enhanced by trade openness as time progresses. On the other hand, Sunge and Ngepah [41] adopted a quasi-translog SFA and single-stage MLE of a true fixed-effects panel data model and demonstrated that agricultural trade openness in Africa statistically enhances the technical efficacy of rice and maize throughout technology transfer. Additionally, research findings indicate that RTAs have favorable technical efficiency effects, although the extent of these effects may vary depending on the product and membership.

Scholars have acknowledged that an inadequate or inappropriate governmental structure can lead to unsatisfactory economic performance, despite the fact that a country possesses the capacity to produce and use modern technology [50]. Olson [43] proposes that many emerging countries squander money and resources due to their inadequate institutional framework to reap the full benefits of specialization and trade. At various stages of a value chain, institutions play a crucial role in facilitating contractual agreements, as per Lin et al. [51]. Proper governance is crucial for the growth of demand bases, which subsequently creates a conducive atmosphere for a nation's agricultural competitiveness. Additionally, it is imperative to establish quality standards for processed agricultural products, which may be effectively implemented by quality institutes. Insufficient institutional quality in nations may prevent them from meeting these requirements, leading to low earnings [52]. There are several studies attempting to explore the relationship between governance quality and agricultural technical efficiency. For example, Méon and Weill [53] employed a sample including 62 nations to examine the correlation between six governance aspects. Upon integrating these six factors into the inefficiency model, it was seen that only government effectiveness had statistical significance, whereas political stability, control of corruption, and quality of regulations revealed no significant impact. Furthermore, Lio and Hu [50] conducted a study on the years 1996, 1998, 2000, and 2002 to examine the potential association between agricultural efficiency and governance indicators. By employing a SFA in a single-stage MLE, the findings indicate that enhancements in the rule of law, corruption control, and government performance have a substantial impact on increasing agricultural output. Based on the results of this study, developing nations have the potential to significantly enhance their agricultural efficiency by reinforcing the government's and the public's adherence to the institutional framework. Another significant discovery is that nations with higher levels of democracy exhibit lower rates of agricultural output efficiency. Added to that, Bayyurt and Yılmaz [54] adopted a DEA model to analyze agricultural efficiency in the first stage. In the second stage, they used panel regression analysis to figure out how the quality of government affected the technical efficiency of agriculture. It has been shown that the level of regulatory quality has a beneficial impact on agricultural efficiency in 64 countries. Additionally, it is evident that agricultural production in developing nations significantly lags behind that of developed nations. This outcome can be attributed to the variation in physical assets and technology across different regions. In the same vein, Bayyurt et al. [55] implemented the DEA-based malmquist index to determine agricultural technical efficiency scores during the initial phase and implemented panel tobit regression during the subsequent phase to evaluate the primary governance indicators that accounted for the observed variations in efficiency levels for a sample of 24 countries. The findings indicated that the institutional framework serves as an incentive for producers to maximize the utilization of their resources, while voice and accountability have a beneficial impact on agricultural technical efficiency.

Given the existing research, the precise impact of agricultural trade liberalization on technical efficiency remains ambiguous, despite its proven ability to enhance agricultural output. As of yet, no research has been conducted to calculate output elasticities and evaluate other external variables that impact agricultural technical efficiency simultaneously. Therefore, this research significantly advances current knowledge by simultaneously calculating the technical efficiency score, calculating output elasticities, and decomposing trade openness into agricultural exports and imports. Furthermore, underdeveloped countries squander money and resources as a result of their insufficient institutional structure, which hinders their ability to efficiently capitalize on trade and specialization. Thus, our work contributes to the current literature by simultaneously combining elements of agricultural trade with government quality.

Materials and methods

The present study aims to investigate the impact of agricultural trade liberalization and governance quality on the technical efficiency of the agricultural sector in Southeast Asia. The translog SFA in a single-stage MLE was employed. The proposed methodology enables the concurrent calculation of technical efficiency scores and the determination of the fundamental determinants of technical inefficiency in the agricultural sector in Southeast Asia. The panel data concerning agricultural output, inputs, and various influencing factors were collected from eight selected ASEAN countries, namely Cambodia, Indonesia, Lao PDR, Malaysia, Myanmar, the Philippines, Thailand, and Vietnam, over the period from 2002 to 2021.

Stochastic frontier analysis (SFA)

Non-parametric data envelopment analysis (DEA) and parametric stochastic frontier analysis (SFA) are widely used in current empirical research to measure productivity and efficiency [54]. The SFA is found to be suitable for agricultural contexts, as recommended by Coelli [57]. This approach enables statistical analysis and the assessment of hypotheses related to production structure and inefficiency levels. Utilizing farm-level data can be significantly influenced by factors like measurement mistakes, missing variables, and weather variables, which can have a substantial impact on the outcomes. Another benefit of the SFA is that it allows external factors influencing the efficiency of the firms to be identified, so-called determinants of inefficiency, which are unavailable in the DEA application.

On the other hand, the assumption made by DEA that all deviations from the frontier signify inefficiency may be deemed adventurous in such cases. Consequently, DEA has found limited use in estimating agricultural frontiers. Despite its lower popularity in economics, several studies, including [[58], [59], [60], [61], [62]], have proved that DEA has gained popularity in management science literature as well as various service sectors, including finance, healthcare, telecommunications, and power distribution. Functional forms of the stochastic frontier production models

To estimate the stochastic production frontier model, the Cobb-Douglas and Transcendental-Logarithmic (Trans-Log) production functions are commonly used. The Cobb-Douglas function's assumptions, including falling marginal productivity, constant returns to scale, and competitive marketplaces for products and inputs, are sometimes considered impractical, especially in regulated sectors such as agriculture [69]. According to Christensen et al. [70], an alternative approach known as the transcendental-logarithmic (translog) production function was introduced to overcome these constraints. It does not assume perfect substitutability among inputs or ideal competition in factor markets, thereby offering greater flexibility than the Cobb-Douglas and Constant Elasticity of Substitution (CES) production functions. In addition, the adaptability of the translog model increases as the number of calculated parameters increases.

It is essential to choose either of the Cobb-Douglas and translog models, as both functional forms are extensively used in empirical studies on production, including frontier analysis [71]. Thus, assessing different alternative models and selecting the most appropriate one based on the likelihood ratio is imperative. The frontier estimation is conducted econometrically using the maximum likelihood approach, as outlined by Coelli et al. [72]. The SFA methodology accommodates both the Cobb-Douglas and translog production functions. Literally, the significance of trade openness was initially acknowledged by Adam Smith [48], who observed that it facilitates the spread of the division of labor, ultimately resulting in increased productivity. Even today, people continue to accept the validity of such a theory. Likewise, international trade exerts a notable and positive impact on productivity, both economically and statistically [84]. Furthermore, the role of international commerce in promoting technological efficiency has been and remains a topic of discussion in both theoretical and empirical arguments [41]. The existing literature generally agrees that accelerating technology transfer and innovation, facilitated by international trade, contributes to increased total factor productivity growth [[85], [86], [87], [88]]. Nevertheless, Shaik and Miljkovic [42] employed the SFA model to investigate the effects of trade liberalization on the technical efficiency of the agriculture sector in the United States. Their findings suggest that trade liberalization does not have significant impacts on technical efficiency. Furthermore, they meticulously separated trade liberalization into specific categories of agricultural imports and exports. However, unlike the previous research, which did not show statistical significance for total trade openness, the results in this particular example demonstrate that the percentage of agricultural imports in agricultural GDP and technological efficiency show contrasting patterns.

Recently, researchers have acknowledged that a country's ability to produce and utilize advanced technology may not lead to satisfactory economic performance if its governing structures are insufficient or inappropriate. Olson [43] claimed that numerous underdeveloped countries waste money and resources due to their lack of proper institutional structure, hindering their ability to maximize advantages from specialization and trade. According to Lio and Hu [50], governance may affect agricultural technical efficiency through several pathways.

Firstly, poor governance may act as a form of taxation on productive endeavors, hindering resource accumulation and reducing their efficient utilization, resulting in decreased efficiency. Secondly, poor governance, particularly corruption, may encourage involvement in transfer activities, diverting resources away from productive endeavors and resulting in decreased agricultural efficiency. Thirdly, the efficiency of agricultural production is influenced by the numbers and quality of public goods and services. Data sources and variables description

The panel data were collected from ASEAN-8 countries, namely Cambodia, Indonesia, Lao PDR, Malaysia, Myanmar, the Philippines, Thailand, and Vietnam, for the period from 2002 to 2021. This was feasible due to the availability of data and the fact that agriculture is a way of life for the people of Southeast Asia, as these eight out of ten ASEAN member states heavily rely on its production for food and income. We used the data on total crop and livestock products, covering five categories such as primary crops, processed crops, live animals, primary livestock, and processed livestock, as a representation of agricultural output to estimate the production frontier, consistent with the empirical research of [96,97]. Following numerous studies, including [1,41,74,98], we considered five agricultural inputs, including land, labor, capital, pesticides, and fertilizer, in order to measure the production frontier. Agricultural land is calculated as the land used for the cultivation of crops and animal husbandry, while labor is calculated as the total population working in agriculture. The net capital stocks of agriculture, expressed in million US dollars, were proxied by capital input. Pesticides are calculated as the total amount of pesticides for agricultural use. Moreover, fertilizer is an input that is commonly used in agricultural production and is computed by the total value of nutrient nitrogen (N), nutrient phosphate (P2O5), and nutrient potash (K2O) for agricultural use. Both agricultural output and input variables were obtained from Food and Agriculture Organization Statistics (FAOSTAT).

In addition, we considered five variables to explain their impact on technical inefficiency, in accordance with previous literature. Firstly, we decomposed agricultural trade into two components, such as agricultural exports and imports, to capture the impact of agricultural trade liberalization on technical inefficiency, consistent with the study conducted by Shaik and Miljkovic [42]. Agricultural exports and imports were calculated by the sum of agricultural exports and imports divided by GDP, which was extracted from FAOSTAT. Subsequently, we have taken three indicators of institutional quality, including voice and accountability, regulation quality, and rule of law, to establish the effects of governance quality on technical inefficiency, consistent with previous empirical research [41,50,54,55]. These three indicators were defined and obtained from the Worldwide Governance Indicators (WGI) database of the World Bank, which is evaluated using indices ranging from −2.5 to 2.5, with higher values indicating greater favorable governance performance. Generalized likelihood ratio test

Before delving into estimating the most fitting model, it is imperative to confirm the presence of technical inefficiency [72]. The likelihood ratio (LR) test was employed for this purpose. This estimation entails testing the null hypothesis (H0), which posits that “there are no effects of technical inefficiency in the countries,” expressed as γ=0 against the alternative hypothesis (H1), which states that “there is technical inefficiency in the countries,” with γ=1. Based on the results presented in Table 4, the LR test statistic exceeds the critical value established by Kodde and Palm [101], with a value of 140.24 exceeding 14.325. Therefore, the analysis findings support the rejection of the null hypothesis at a significance level of 10 percent. Moreover, the study draws the conclusion that there are substantial effects of technical inefficiency in the model, indicating that the utilization of traditional ordinary least squares (OLS) is not appropriate. Consequently, the stochastic frontier production model was adopted as the preferred functional form.

Specifically, with disaggregated agricultural trade liberalization, the results prove that both agricultural exports and imports have been shown to have a negative impact on agricultural inefficiency in Southeast Asia. The coefficients of agricultural exports and imports are −0.074 and −0.076, respectively, with a significance level of 1 %. Economically speaking, a 1 % increase in agricultural exports and imports reduces technical inefficiency by 0.074 % and 0.076 %, respectively. Based on the given dataset, variations in agricultural exports and imports significantly improved technical efficiency in the agricultural sector. Since the variations in agricultural exports and imports have been observed to be statistically significant, it is indicated that agricultural trade liberalization as a driving factor for enhancing technical efficiency does exist for the period of our analysis. Our findings align with previous studies such as Hart et al. [40] concerning the European Union's (EU) agriculture as well as Sunge and Ngepah [41] on African agriculture, which provided evidence supporting the premise that trade liberalization does have an impact on agricultural technical efficiency. Also, the present result is consistent with the recent investigation carried out by Ma et al. [111], which confirmed the premise that agricultural exports have substantial positive effects on technical efficiency. Expanding markets overseas through agricultural exports can facilitate the achievement of economies of scale. Exporters can enhance their technical innovation capabilities by gaining experience from worldwide competitiveness [112]. Moreover, in theory, developing nations have the potential to acquire sophisticated technology and managerial expertise by importing products and services from developed countries, a phenomenon known as the demonstration effect. This, in turn, can enhance efficiency levels [1]. Our findings provide support for both theoretical and practical aspects. However, our findings contradict research carried out by Shaik and Miljkovic [42] regarding US agriculture as well as Miljkovic et al. [33] on Brazilian agriculture. These studies have concluded that trade liberalization does not serve as a driving factor for enhancing agricultural technical efficiency.

When considering governance quality, the findings indicate that voice and accountability has been identified as factors that promote technical inefficiency. This factor appears to have a statistically significant and positive impact on technical inefficiency at the 1 % level. More specifically, it appears that improvements in voice and accountability seem to significantly increase technical inefficiency in agriculture. The results indicate that with a 1 % improvement in terms of voice and accountability, agricultural technical inefficiency increases by approximately 0.79 %. The observed statistical significance indicating an increase in agricultural inefficiency with respect to “voice and accountability” in Southeast Asia may be attributed to several factors. The World Bank defines “voice and accountability” as the extent to which citizens engage in government selection, and it also includes their freedom of speech, freedom of affiliation, and freedom of press. When voice and accountability are lacking, the resources in the agricultural sector may be misallocated. This could potentially be attributed to a lack of input support, modern technology adoption, and infrastructure development, which caused technical inefficiencies in the region. Another interesting viewpoint to note is that in countries where the government faces some challenges, such as a lack of transparency, low institutional quality, and corruption, the agricultural sector will encounter inefficiencies. This finding is consistent with the recent study conducted by Gelgo et al. [113], which revealed that democratic involvement and accountability in East Africa have a lesser significance in promoting the development of value-added in agriculture.

In addition, the regulatory quality demonstrates statistical significance and is positively associated with technical inefficiency. This implies that a 1 % improvement in terms of regulatory quality scores increases agricultural technical inefficiency by about 0.67 %. This finding is inconsistent with previous studies carried out by Sunge and Ngepah [41] and Bayyurt and Yılmaz [54] that established a positive relationship between regulatory quality and agricultural efficiency. The rule of law, on the other hand, was found to reduce agricultural inefficiency in the Southeast Asian region and was statistically significant at the 1 % level. This signifies that 1 % improvement in rule of law, leading to an increase in agricultural technical efficiency of 2.736 %. This suggests that the assessment of the level of trust and compliance of agents with societal norms, the effectiveness of contract enforcement, property rights, law enforcement agencies, and judicial systems, and the probability of criminal activities and acts of violence appear to reduce technical inefficiency in the agricultural sector in the Southeast Asian region. Our result is in line with the research undertaken by Lio and Hu [50], who discovered that among the six governance indicators incorporated in the model, only the rule of law demonstrated a substantial positive impact on agricultural technical efficiency for cross-country aggregate frontier analysis. However, this outcome is inconsistent with Ariabod et al. [114], asserting that rule of law negatively influences agricultural production growth in selected developing countries due to the insufficient trust in agricultural laws and regulations among agricultural producers, which may have resulted in a decrease in the efficiency and production of the agricultural sector.

Conclusions and policy implications

This study investigates the intricate relationship between agricultural trade liberalization, governance quality, and technical efficiency in the agricultural sector of Southeast Asia over the period 2002–2021. Employing panel data and utilizing the translog SFA with a single-stage MLE framework proposed by Battese and Coelli [44], this research not only calculates technical efficiency scores but also delves into the factors influencing technical inefficiency. The empirical findings reveal an overall technical efficiency score of 94 % for ASEAN-8 countries, indicating the potential to enhance agricultural production by approximately 6 % through the elimination of technical inefficiency. Remarkably, Malaysia emerges as the regional leader in efficiency with a score of 99.82 %, reflecting the focused attention of its government and policymakers on the agricultural sector. It's quite interesting to note that Cambodia's agriculture sector contributes the highest percentage to the country's GDP in Southeast Asia, accounting for 22.8 %. However, Cambodia ranks as the least proficient country in technical efficiency, with scores of 76.11 %. Despite the fact that agriculture accounts for 23.71 % of total employment, the Philippines has the second-lowest technical efficacy in the agricultural sector with a score of 90.65 %. Also, Indonesia, despite having agriculture as the second-largest sector contributing approximately 13.3 % to the GDP, ranks the third lowest in technical efficiency among the eight countries, scoring 91.64 %. The exploration suggests that Cambodia, the Philippines, and Indonesia have insufficient agricultural systems and government programs that need improvement and attention. Based on this result, the government should pass legislation that promotes the merging of small farms or cooperative agricultural systems to increase economies of scale and technical efficiency. The government should provide funding to rural infrastructure, extension services, research and development (R&D), and financial inclusion programs to ensure widespread access to modern inputs, technology, and credit.

Regarding the functions of technical inefficiency, our analysis reveals that the disaggregation of agricultural trade liberalization into agricultural exports and imports yields a statistically significant impact on agricultural technical efficiency in Southeast Asia. Based on the dataset at hand, we can infer that improvements in agricultural trade liberalization yield discernible effects on the technical efficiency of the agricultural sector. Considerable resources have been devoted in Southeast Asia over recent decades to facilitate unrestricted access for domestic producers to global agricultural markets. Noteworthy endeavors in this regard include the Uruguay Round of negotiations under the General Agreement on Tariffs and Trade (GATT) and subsequent discussions under the World Trade Organization (WTO), ASEAN Economic Community (AEC), and ASEAN Trade in Goods Agreement (ATIGA), as well as actively engaging in various free trade agreements (FTAs). These negotiations were frequently underpinned by the assertion that free trade would engender heightened productivity and technical efficiency in Southeast Asia's agriculture. Therefore, our findings support this presumed correlation during the study period. The evidence suggests that the anticipated underlying motive for free trade, as posited by these negotiations, is substantiated by the observed trends within the specified timeframe. Hence, it is imperative for governments to adopt open and liberalized trade policies in order to facilitate the exchange of technology and knowledge within the agriculture sector. Moreover, the implementation of regulations that promote competition in the local market might potentially result in improvements in efficiency and productivity.

Furthermore, the observed improvement in agricultural efficiency associated with an enhancement in the rule of law in the Southeast Asian region suggests a positive correlation between legal frameworks and effective agricultural practices. A stronger rule of law provides greater legal stability and protects property rights. This encourages producers to make long-term investments, use modern technologies, and use sustainable agricultural practices without worrying about unpredictable changes. The government is implementing legislative changes to enhance the rule of law by ensuring that legal frameworks are transparent, consistent, and conducive to agricultural development. Prioritize safeguarding property rights and resolving conflicts promptly and equitably. Furthermore, voice and accountability and regulatory quality found to contribute to technical inefficiency may be due to the limited involvement of farmers and stakeholders in the decision-making process. Therefore, the government should foster a culture of participatory decision-making in the agricultural sector by establishing platforms to facilitate communication between policymakers, farmers, and other relevant stakeholders to ensure that policies address the diverse needs and challenges encountered by the sector. In addition, it is imperative to enforce policies that foster the growth of the private sector within the farming sector. Southeast Asian nations can enhance the technical efficiency of their agricultural sector by adopting these policy suggestions to establish a conducive governance framework for effective and sustainable farming methods.

This study examines the impact of agricultural trade liberalization and governance quality on technical efficiency in the agricultural sector of ASEAN countries. It also offers valuable references for the future agricultural development of these countries. Nevertheless, this work still possesses several limitations that necessitate further comprehensive investigation. The current study employed five input variables, namely land, labor, capital, pesticides, and fertilizer, to calculate agricultural technical efficiency. The output variable utilized was agricultural production quantity. Nevertheless, we aggregated the amounts of nitrogen (N), phosphate (P2O5), and potash (K2O) nutrients as fertilizer inputs for agricultural purposes. However, we are unable to determine the specific effects of each type of fertilizer on agricultural production. Therefore, future research should take into account the computation of agricultural technical efficiency regarding each specific type of fertilizer. Second, the present study specifically examines the agricultural sector as a whole, which may restrict the applicability of policy consequences to individual crops or livestock. Future research should focus on analyzing the technical efficiency of certain key crops and animals, such as rice, maize, cassava, sugarcane, etc. The scope of the study should be narrowed down to these specific areas. This focused methodology can offer more detailed and customized analysis and suggestions for improving technical efficiency in certain agricultural subsectors within the ASEAN-8 countries. Ultimately, our analysis encompasses the liberalization of trade in the agricultural sector as a whole. Therefore, future research might focus on examining the impact of individual trade agreements on agricultural technological efficiency. By implementing this approach, the targeted policy actions can achieve more effectiveness.
