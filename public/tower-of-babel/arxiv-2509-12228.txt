Transmission Conditions for the Non-Overlapping Schwarz Coupling of Full Order and Operator Inference Models
Cameron Rodriguez, Irina Tezaur, Alejandro Mota, Anthony Gruber, Eric Parish, Christopher Wentland

Abstract
This work investigates transmission conditions for the domain decomposition-based coupling of subdomain-local models using the non-overlapping Schwarz alternating method (NO-SAM). Building on prior efforts involving overlapping SAM (O-SAM), we formulate and assess two NO-SAM variants, based on alternating Dirichlet–Neumann and Robin–Robin transmission conditions, respectively. For the subdomain-local models, we consider a mix of full order models (FOMs) and non-intrusive reduced order models (ROMs) constructed via an emerging model reduction technique known as operator inference (OpInf). Of particular novelty is the first application of NO-SAM to couple non-intrusive OpInf ROMs with each other and with FOMs. Numerical studies on a one-dimensional linear elastic wave propagation benchmark problem demonstrate that transmission condition choice and parameter tuning significantly impact convergence rate, accuracy, and stability. Robin–Robin coupling often yields faster convergence than alternating Dirichlet–Neumann, though improper parameter selection can induce spurious oscillations at subdomain interfaces. For FOM–OpInf and OpInf–OpInf couplings, higher modal content in the ROM basis improves accuracy and mitigates instabilities, in some cases outperforming coupled FOM–FOM reference solutions when it comes to accuracy. These findings highlight NO-SAM’s potential for enabling flexible, non-intrusive, and efficient multi-model coupling across independently meshed subdomains, while emphasizing the need for careful interface condition design in higher-dimensional and predictive settings.

Introduction

Despite advancements in algorithms and high-performance computing (HPC), high-fidelity modeling and simulation (mod/sim) of complex multi-scale and multi-physics systems remains a major challenge due to: (i) the time-intensive process of generating high-quality meshes, identified as “the single biggest bottleneck in [mod/sim] analyses”
[
14
]
, and (ii) the substantial computational cost and long runtimes required for performing high-fidelity simulations of complex nonlinear phenomena. While data-driven reduced order models (ROMs) have promised to mitigate the runtime burden of mod/sim, these models face their own set of challenges related to robustness, stability and accuracy, along with lengthy implementations and a lack of systematic refinement mechanisms.

The present work advances a computational technique that promises to mitigate both hurdles described above: the Schwarz alternating method (SAM)
[
39
]
. The key idea behind SAM is to decompose the physical domain on which a given partial differential equation (PDE) is posed into smaller subdomains, and then solve the governing PDE by iterating between smaller, subdomain-local problems, while exchanging boundary condition information to ensure compatibility across subdomain interfaces.
Over the past decade, our group has demonstrated SAM’s ability to achieve concurrent domain decomposition- (DD-)based coupling of subdomain-local full order models (FOMs) with each other
[
33
,
34
,
32
]
, as well as with data-driven ROMs of various flavors (e.g., intrusive projection-based ROMs
[
46
,
1
]
, physics-informed neural networks (PINNs)
[
42
]
, non-intrusive operator inference (OpInf) models
[
31
]
). In these papers, we have demonstrated that SAM has a number of advantages over competing multi-scale coupling methods. Most notably: it is concurrent and minimally-intrusive, can couple non-conformal meshes with different element topologies, and allows each subdomain to be advanced with different time integrators and time steps for dynamic problems. Importantly, SAM achieves this without introducing non-physical artifacts into the solution.

The majority of our past research has focused on the overlapping version of SAM (O-SAM), which is known to converge to the monolithic solution of a well-posed PDE when simple Dirichlet transmission boundary conditions (BCs) are prescribed on the subdomain interfaces, and the overlap region is non-empty
[
39
,
33
,
34
]
. Herein, we explore the performance of two variants of non-overlapping SAM (NO-SAM): alternating Dirichlet–Neumann NO-SAM (DN-NO-SAM)
[
47
]
and Robin–Robin NO-SAM (RR-NO-SAM)
[
29
]
. Our interest in the NO version of SAM stems from the fact that this SAM variant provides maximal meshing flexibility, as subdomains can be created and meshed completely independently of each other without having to worry about the size/shape of the overlap region. These independently-meshed subdomains can then be added to a library of archetypes, which can be glued together in arbitrary combinations using NO-SAM when creating new systems/geometries. In addition to formulating and assessing NO-SAM for FOM-FOM coupling, we also present the first (to our knowledge) formulation of NO-SAM for the DD-based coupling of non-intrusive OpInf ROMs
[
35
]
with each other and/or with FOMs. OpInf is a completely non-intrusive ROM method: since OpInf works by assuming a functional form for the ROM in terms of to-be-learned reduced operators and solving an optimization problem offline for these operators, it does not require access to the underlying FOM code like traditional (intrusive) projection-based ROM construction methods.

The work described here is an addition to a growing body of literature on DD-based OpInf-OpInf and ROM-FOM couplings. Our SAM approach is most similar to the recent work by Farcas et al.
[
13
]
, which develops a method for performing DD-based coupling of subdomain-local OpInf ROMs by learning appropriate reduced operators responsible for the coupling. The present work differs from that of Farcas et al. in two important ways: (i) in
[

[

is limited to overlapping domain decompositions (DDs). While other Schwarz-like methods for FOM-FOM and FOM-OpInf couplings exist in the literature, the majority of these methods focus on the coupling of intrusive projection-based ROMs

,

,

,

,

]

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. In Section

2
Solid mechanics problem formulation

Consider the strong form Euler-Lagrange equations for a general dynamic solid mechanics problem:

\mathrm{Div}\,\mathord{\btensor P}+\rho_{0}\mathord{\btensor B}=\rho_{0}\ddot{\boldsymbol{\Psi}}\quad\text{in }\Omega\times I

(1)

where
\Omega\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
for
d\in\{1,2,3\}
is an open bounded domain,
I\coloneqq\{t\in[t_{0},t_{f}]\}
is a closed time interval with
0\leq t_{0}<t_{f}
, and
\mathord{\btensor x}=\boldsymbol{\Psi}(\mathord{\btensor X},t):\Omega\times I\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d}
describes the mapping from the reference configuration to the current configuration with
\mathord{\btensor X}\in\Omega
. The overdot notation in
\ddot{\boldsymbol{\Psi}}
denotes a time derivative such that
\dot{\boldsymbol{\Psi}}\coloneqq\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{\Psi}}{\partial t}
and
\ddot{\boldsymbol{\Psi}}\coloneqq\frac{\partial^{2}\boldsymbol{\Psi}}{\partial t^{2}}
. The symbol
\mathord{\btensor P}
denotes the first Piola-Kirchoff stress,
\mathord{\btensor B}
is the specific body force, and
\rho_{0}
is the mass density defined in the reference configuration. It is important to note that the constitutive model used to describe
\mathord{\btensor P}
is general and can include nonlinear models.

We then have the following initial and boundary conditions for the governing equation described in (
1
):

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\Psi}(\mathord{\btensor X},t_{0})

\displaystyle=\mathord{\btensor X}_{0}

\displaystyle\text{in }\Omega

(2)

\displaystyle\dot{\boldsymbol{\Psi}}(\mathord{\btensor X},t_{0})

\displaystyle=\mathord{\btensor v}_{0}

\displaystyle\text{in }\Omega

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\Psi}(\mathord{\btensor X},t)

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{\chi}

\displaystyle\text{on }\partial_{\Psi}\Omega\times I

\displaystyle\mathord{\btensor P}\mathord{\btensor N}

\displaystyle=\mathord{\btensor T}

\displaystyle\text{on }\partial_{T}\Omega\times I

where
\partial_{\Psi}\Omega
and
\partial_{T}\Omega
denote the Dirichlet and Neumann portions, respectively, of the domain boundary
\partial\Omega
with
\partial\Omega=\partial_{\Psi}\Omega\cup\partial_{T}\Omega
and
\partial_{\Psi}\Omega\cap\partial_{T}\Omega=\emptyset
, and
\mathord{\btensor N}
denotes the unit vector normal to
\partial\Omega
.

The weak variational form of (
1
) is then given by:

\int_{I}\left[\int_{\Omega}\left(\mathrm{Div}\,\mathord{\btensor P}+\rho_{0}\mathord{\btensor B}-\rho_{0}\ddot{\boldsymbol{\Psi}}\right)\cdot\boldsymbol{\xi}\,d\Omega+\int_{\partial_{T}\Omega}\left(\mathord{\btensor P}\mathord{\btensor N}\right)\cdot\boldsymbol{\xi}\,dS\right]\,dt=0,

(3)

where
\boldsymbol{\xi}
is a test function defined so that
\mathcal{V}\coloneqq\{\boldsymbol{\xi}\in W_{2}^{1}(\Omega\times I):\boldsymbol{\xi=\bt@0}\text{ on }\partial_{\Psi}\Omega\times I\ \cup\Omega\times t_{0}\cup\Omega\times t_{f}\}
.
The variational form in (
3
) is discretized in space using the classical Galerkin finite element method (FEM)
[
22
]
. The Neumann boundary condition from (
2
) is implemented by inserting
\mathord{\btensor P}\mathord{\btensor N}=\mathord{\btensor T}
into the second integral in (
3
).

For the sake of concreteness, we consider in this work a specific linear elastic initial boundary value problem (IBVP) for the equations (
1
)–(
2
) known as the one-dimensional (1D) linear elastic wave propagation problem, described in detail in Section
2.1
.
It is straightforward to show that, in the case of a linear elastic material, the semi-discrete matrix-vector form of (
3
) can be written as:

\mathord{\btensor M}\ddot{\mathord{\btensor u}}+\mathord{\btensor K}\mathord{\btensor u}=\mathord{\btensor F}

(4)

where
\mathord{\btensor M}
is the (consistent) mass matrix,
\mathord{\btensor K}
is the stiffness matrix,
\mathord{\btensor F}
is the vector of applied external forces,
\mathord{\btensor u}=\boldsymbol{\Psi}(\mathord{\btensor X},t)-\mathord{\btensor X}
is the displacement, and
\ddot{\mathord{\btensor u}}
is the acceleration.

2.1
Model problem: wave propagation in a 1D linear elastic bar

In order to explore the various transmission conditions available when formulating NO-SAM, we consider a 1D linear elastic wave propagation problem based on the governing
equations (
1
). This problem, considered previously in
[
34
]
and
[
1
]
, is chosen due to its ability to reveal coupling artifacts, such as spurious oscillations, as the wave passes through the interface between subdomains.

Consider a simple bar geometry of length
L
with
\Omega=[0,L]\in\mathbb{R}
. Both ends of the bar are clamped such that
u(0,t)=u(L,t)=0
for all
t\geq t_{0}
. The initial conditions are that of a symmetric Gaussian wave described by the following equation:

u(x,t_{0})=\frac{a}{2}\mathrm{exp}\left[-\frac{(x-b)^{2}}{2s^{2}}\right]

(5)

for
a,b,s\in\mathbb{R}
.

To benchmark the performance of each transmission condition for NO-SAM, we developed a Python script that implements the numerical framework described above. All numerical examples in this work use the following problem setting, unless otherwise specified. A linear elastic constitutive model is employed with material constants
E=1\,GPa
and
\rho=1000\,kg\,m^{-3}
. The bar is of length
L=1\,m
and is discretized into uniform elements of length
h=0.001\,m
. The domain decomposition of the bar is defined as
\Omega_{1}=[0,0.6]
and
\Omega_{2}=[0.6,1]
. The semi-discrete equation (
4
) is advanced in time using the Newmark-
\beta
time integration scheme of constant average acceleration, for which
\beta=0.25
and
\gamma=0.5
. The simulation is run from initial time
t_{0}=0\,s
to final time
t_{f}=10^{-3}\,s
using a time step of
\Delta t=2.5\times 10^{-7}\,s
. This time step was chosen based on the wave propagation speed
c=\sqrt{\frac{E}{\rho}}=1000\,m\,s^{-1}
, for which the stable time step was calculated to be
\Delta t_{stable}=\frac{h}{c}=10^{-6}\,s

[
34
,
1
]
. Uniform, conformal meshes and identical time-step sizes are used for each subdomain, although SAM does not require this restriction. The initial displacement is defined using the symmetric Gaussian wave in (
5
) with
a=0.01
,
b=\frac{L}{2}=0.5
, and
s=0.02
.

3
NO-SAM for FOM coupling

This section outlines the Schwarz alternating method for the domain-decomposition based coupling between multiple non-overlapping subdomains. The formulation is presented for a general setting to accommodate different types of transmission conditions between the subdomains.

3.1
NO-SAM formulation

Without loss of generality, consider a partition of a given domain
\Omega
into two non-overlapping subdomains,
\Omega_{1}
and
\Omega_{2}
, as shown in Figure
1
. Here,
non-overlapping
indicates that the subdomains satisfy
\Omega_{1}\cap\Omega_{2}=\emptyset
. We are interested in solving the weak variational equation of the form (
3
) using this decomposition.

Fig. 1
:
Illustration showing an example non-overlapping domain decomposition.

As described in more detail in
[
34
]
, the dynamic SAM algorithm operates by discretizing the temporal domain into time intervals
I_{n}=\left[t_{n},t_{n+1}\right]
and solving a sequence of subdomain-local PDEs to convergence within each time interval. Specifically, for a given time interval
I_{n}
, NO-SAM solves the following sequence of subdomain-local problems:

\left\{\begin{array}[]{rcll}\mathord{\btensor M}_{1}\ddot{\mathord{\btensor u}}_{1}^{(s+1)}+\mathord{\btensor K}_{1}\mathord{\btensor u}_{1}^{(s+1)}&=&\mathord{\btensor F}_{1}&\text{in }\Omega_{1}\\[4.30554pt]
\mathord{\btensor u}_{1}^{(s+1)}&=&\mathord{\btensor u}_{D}&\text{on }\partial_{\Psi}\Omega_{1}\backslash\Gamma\\[2.15277pt]
\alpha_{12}\mathord{\btensor T}_{1}^{(s+1)}+\beta_{12}\mathord{\btensor u}_{1}^{(s+1)}&=&\bt@\lambda^{(s+1)}_{1}&\text{on }\Gamma\end{array}\right.

(6)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{rcll}\mathord{\btensor M}_{2}\ddot{\mathord{\btensor u}}_{2}^{(s+1)}+\mathord{\btensor K}_{2}\mathord{\btensor u}_{2}^{(s+1)}&=&\mathord{\btensor F}_{2}&\text{in }\Omega_{2}\\[4.30554pt]
\mathord{\btensor u}_{2}^{(s+1)}&=&\mathord{\btensor u}_{D}&\text{on }\partial_{\Psi}\Omega_{2}\backslash\Gamma\\[2.15277pt]
\alpha_{21}\mathord{\btensor T}_{2}^{(s+1)}+\beta_{21}\mathord{\btensor u}_{2}^{(s+1)}&=&\bt@\lambda^{(s+1)}_{2}&\text{on }\Gamma\end{array}\right.

(7)

\displaystyle\bt@\lambda^{(s+1)}_{1}

\displaystyle=\theta_{1}\left[\alpha_{12}\mathord{\btensor T}_{2}^{(s)}+\beta_{12}\mathord{\btensor u}_{2}^{(s)}\right]+(1-\theta_{1})\,\bt@\lambda^{(s)}_{1}

(8)

\displaystyle\bt@\lambda^{(s+1)}_{2}

\displaystyle=\theta_{2}\left[\alpha_{21}\mathord{\btensor T}_{1}^{(s+1)}+\beta_{21}\mathord{\btensor u}_{1}^{(s+1)}\right]+(1-\theta_{2})\,\bt@\lambda^{(s)}_{2}

(9)

where
s
denotes the Schwarz iteration index and
\theta_{1},\theta_{2}\in(0,1]
are relaxation parameters. For convenience, here we rewrite the Dirichlet boundary condition defined in (
2
) in terms of displacement, denoting the boundary condition on the subdomain interface as
\mathord{\btensor u}_{D}
. To allow for a general choice of transmission conditions, we introduce constants
\alpha_{ij},\beta_{ij}\in\mathbb{R}
for
i,j=1,\dots,N_{k}
, where
N_{k}
is the total number of subdomains (
N_{k}=2
in the present study). The reader can observe that, by appropriate selection of these constants, Dirichlet, Neumann, and Robin transmission conditions can all be represented in (
6
)–(
9
).

The iteration defined in (
6
)–(
9
) is repeated until convergence. We adopt the following relative convergence criterion

\frac{\big\lVert\left[\mathord{\btensor u}^{(s+1)}-\mathord{\btensor u}^{(s)}\right]+\Delta t\,\left[\mathord{\btensor v}^{(s+1)}-\mathord{\btensor v}^{(s)}\right]+\tfrac{1}{2}\,\Delta t^{2}\,\left[\mathord{\btensor a}^{(s+1)}-\mathord{\btensor a}^{(s)}\right]\big\rVert_{2}}{\big\lVert\mathord{\btensor u}^{(s)}+\Delta t\,\mathord{\btensor v}^{(s)}+\tfrac{1}{2}\,\Delta t^{2}\,\mathord{\btensor a}^{(s)}\big\rVert_{2}}<\delta

(10)

where
\delta
denotes the prescribed convergence tolerance. This criterion requires that the tolerance be satisfied across all subdomains before declaring convergence of the Schwarz iteration, thereby ensuring that all displacement, velocity, and acceleration solutions have converged prior to advancing to the next timestep. In all numerical examples presented here, we use
\delta=10^{-8}
.

This work focuses specifically on the alternating Dirichlet-Neumann and Robin-Robin transmission conditions, for which the
\alpha_{ij}
and
\beta_{ij}
constants are defined as

•

Alternating Dirichlet-Neumann:

\alpha_{12}=\beta_{21}=0
, and
\alpha_{21},\beta_{12}=1

•

Robin–Robin:

\alpha_{12},\alpha_{21},\beta_{12},\beta_{21}\neq 0

We consider formulations with these specific transmission conditions, as they have shown to lead to a convergent NO-SAM iteration in
[
47
]
and
[
29
]
, respectively. Relaxation is typically introduced only to subdomains using a Dirichlet transmission condition on the boundary
\Gamma
, as occurs in the alternating Dirichlet-Neummann variant of NO-SAM, where
\alpha_{ij}=0
and
\beta_{ij}\neq 0
.
It has been demonstrated in the literature that, for certain PDEs, setting
\theta_{i}<1
can reduce the number of Schwarz iterations required for convergence
[
47
]
. In this work,
\bt@\lambda^{i}_{0}
is initialized to zero for the first iteration, i.e.,
\bt@\lambda^{i}_{0}=\bt@0
.

For the case of the Robin-Robin NO-SAM variant, the Robin transmission condition is applied as follows. Consider without loss of generality the subdomain problem in
\Omega_{1}
. In this case, the third line of (
6
) is rearranged to solve for
\mathord{\btensor T}_{1}^{(s+1)}
, and the resulting expression is then substituted into the external force term
\mathord{\btensor F}_{1}
of the first line of (
6
), yielding

\mathord{\btensor M}_{1}\ddot{\mathord{\btensor u}}_{1}^{(s+1)}+\left[\mathord{\btensor K}_{1}+\frac{\beta_{12}}{\alpha_{12}}\mathord{\btensor S}_{1}\right]\mathord{\btensor u}_{1}^{(s+1)}=\mathord{\btensor F}_{1}+\frac{1}{\alpha_{12}}\mathord{\btensor R}_{1}

(11)

where the additional
\mathord{\btensor S}
and
\mathord{\btensor R}
terms can be viewed as Robin contributions to the stiffness matrix and the external force vector, respectively.
The derivation and resulting expression for
\Omega_{2}
is analogous, and is therefore omitted for brevity.

Remark 2.1.
There exists a wide body of literature focused on how the parameters
\alpha_{ij}
,
\beta_{ij}
and
\theta_{i}
in (
6
)–(
7
) should be selected to get the best convergence of SAM for a given IBVP. The resulting methods are termed “Optimized Schwarz methods”; for a succinct overview, the reader is referred to
[
15
]
. While it is possible to derive optimal values of the relevant parameters analytically for certain simple PDEs, this is not generally the case for generic nonlinear PDEs based on (
3
). Herein, we find the most viable values of
\alpha_{ij}
,
\beta_{ij}
and
\theta_{i}
through a numerical parameter sweep study.

3.2
Numerical results

Our first set of numerical results examines the influence of the Robin boundary condition parameters,
\alpha_{ij}
and
\beta_{ij}
, as defined in Section
3.1
, on: (i) the error between the converged Schwarz solution and the corresponding monolithic solution, and (ii) the number of Schwarz iterations required to satisfy the prescribed convergence tolerance. This is achieved by performing a parameter sweep for all four
\alpha_{ij}
and
\beta_{ij}
parameters within the 1D wave propagation problem setting described in Section
2.1
. The parameter spaces used in the sweep are:

\displaystyle\alpha_{ij}

\displaystyle\in\{10^{-3},\,10^{-1},\,1,\,3,\,5\}\cdot\tfrac{1}{\sigma_{\max}}

(12)

\displaystyle\beta_{ij}

\displaystyle\in\{10^{-3},\,10^{-1},\,1,\,3,\,5\}

where
\sigma_{\max}=\max_{t,\,x}\sigma(x,t)
denotes the maximum stress within the bar for the corresponding monolithic solution. Normalizing the
\alpha_{ij}
parameter by a reference stress value compensates for the large difference in orders of magnitude between the displacements and tractions used in the Robin transmission conditions and improves the interpretability of the results. The parameter spaces defined in (
12
) were selected to capture the full range of variation in both the error and number of iterations required for convergence.

The error between the converged Schwarz solution and the corresponding monolithic solution is calculated at each time step. For subdomain
k
at time
t_{n}
we define:

\varepsilon_{k}(t_{n})=\big\lVert\Delta\mathord{\btensor u}_{k}(t_{n})\big\rVert_{2}+\Delta t\,\big\lVert\Delta\mathord{\btensor v}_{k}(t_{n})\big\rVert_{2}+\tfrac{1}{2}\,\Delta t^{2}\,\big\lVert\Delta\mathord{\btensor a}_{k}(t_{n})\big\rVert_{2}

(13)

where

\displaystyle\Delta\mathord{\btensor u}_{k}(t_{n})

\displaystyle=\mathord{\btensor u}_{k}^{\mathrm{SCH}}(t_{n})-\mathord{\btensor u}_{k}^{\mathrm{MON}}(t_{n})

(14)

\displaystyle\Delta\mathord{\btensor v}_{k}(t_{n})

\displaystyle=\mathord{\btensor v}_{k}^{\mathrm{SCH}}(t_{n})-\mathord{\btensor v}_{k}^{\mathrm{MON}}(t_{n})

\displaystyle\Delta\mathord{\btensor a}_{k}(t_{n})

\displaystyle=\mathord{\btensor a}_{k}^{\mathrm{SCH}}(t_{n})-\mathord{\btensor a}_{k}^{\mathrm{MON}}(t_{n})

and
\lVert\cdot\rVert_{2}
denotes the Euclidean norm. Here
\mathord{\btensor u}^{\mathrm{SCH}}
,
\mathord{\btensor v}^{\mathrm{SCH}}
,
\mathord{\btensor a}^{\mathrm{SCH}}
are the converged Schwarz displacement, velocity and acceleration, and
\mathord{\btensor u}^{\mathrm{MON}}
,
\mathord{\btensor v}^{\mathrm{MON}}
,
\mathord{\btensor a}^{\mathrm{MON}}
are the corresponding monolithic quantities. Lastly, the time-averaged error is calculated as:

\varepsilon_{avg}=\frac{1}{N_{t}-1}\sum_{i=1}^{N_{k}}\sum_{n=2}^{N_{t}}\varepsilon_{k}(t_{n})

(15)

where
N_{k}
denotes the number of subdomains, and
N_{t}
is the number of time-steps.

4
NO-SAM for OpInf ROM coupling

Operator Inference
[
35
]
is a data-driven, non-intrusive, projection-based method for constructing low-dimensional surrogate models that approximate high-dimensional data. The data may be obtained from experiments or generated by numerical simulation; in this work, we focus on the latter. This section explores the use of NO-SAM to couple together subdomain-local OpInf ROMs with each other and with subdomain-local FOMs. To the best of the authors’ knowledge, this work is the first to explore the DD-based coupling of non-intrusive OpInf models using NO-SAM.

4.1
OpInf ROM overview

Similar to intrusive projection-based ROM methodologies, OpInf begins by constructing a reduced basis from a given dataset using the proper orthogonal decomposition (POD)
[
20
,
40
]
. POD is a numerical technique that extracts a variance-maximizing set of features from a sequence of field snapshots. By capturing the most significant patterns in the data with a few dominant modes, POD reduces the complexity of the system, enabling the development of efficient, low-order surrogate models.

The dataset in this work comes from the monolithic numerical solution to the problem expressed in (
4
). Once we have snapshot data for
\tau>0
separate states between
t_{0}
and
t_{f}
, we can define the following snapshot matrix:

\mathord{\btensor U}=\left[\mathord{\btensor u}(t_{1}),\,\mathord{\btensor u}(t_{2}),\,\dots\,,\mathord{\btensor u}(t_{\tau})\right]\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times\tau}

(16)

It is important to note that snapshot velocities
\dot{\mathord{\btensor u}}(t_{n})
and accelerations
\ddot{\mathord{\btensor u}}(t_{n})
can also be included in the snapshot matrix
\mathord{\btensor U}
along with the displacements, though this is not considered herein. The reduced basis is obtained by performing the singular value decomposition of
\mathord{\btensor U}=\bt@\Phi\bt@\Sigma\mathord{\btensor V}^{T}
, where
\bt@\Phi\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times N}
is an orthogonal matrix whose first
R=\operatorname{rank}(\mathord{\btensor U})>0
columns span the column space of
\mathord{\btensor U}
. The first
r\leq R
columns of
\bt@\Phi
define an optimal
r
-dimensional basis for the columns of
\mathord{\btensor U}
in the
l^{2}
sense. We denote this truncated basis by
\bt@\Phi_{r}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times r}
. The fraction of statistical energy captured by a basis of size
r
is defined as

E(r)\coloneq\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{r}\sigma_{i}^{2}}{\sum_{i=1}^{R}\sigma_{i}^{2}},

(17)

where
\sigma_{i}
denotes the
i^{th}
singular value of
\bt@\Sigma
(not to be confused with the stress notation used previously).

For standard intrusive and projection-based ROMs, reduced operators are obtained by projecting the full order operators discretizing the governing PDEs onto the span of the reduced basis using
\bt@\Phi
. The main disadvantage of this procedure is that it requires explicit access to the full order operators defining the governing problem. One selling point of OpInf ROMs is that the reduced operators are
non-intrusively
inferred directly from the snapshot data used to build
\Phi
, eliminating the need to access the full order system operators.

The first step in developing an appropriate OpInf ROM for our model problem (Section
2
) involves writing the functional form corresponding to the FOM semi-discretization.
It is straight-forward to see that, for a linear elastic material and no body force, discretizing (
3
) in space using the finite element method results in the following functional form:

\mathord{\btensor M}\ddot{\mathord{\btensor u}}+\mathord{\btensor K}\mathord{\btensor u}=\mathord{\btensor F},

(18)

where

F_{i}:=\int_{\partial_{T}\Omega}\mathord{\btensor T}\cdot\boldsymbol{\xi}_{i}\,dS

(19)

for the
i^{\mathrm{th}}
test function
\boldsymbol{\xi}_{i}
. Our goal is to derive the functional form of a projection-based ROM corresponding to (
18
).
Suppose the prescribed traction
\mathord{\btensor T}:=\mathord{\btensor t}
is piecewise constant on the element faces along the subdomain interface
\Gamma
. In this case, (
19
) can be approximated as
F_{i}=\mathord{\btensor t}\cdot\int_{\Gamma}\boldsymbol{\xi}_{i}dS
, so that
\mathord{\btensor F}
takes the form:
\mathord{\btensor F}=\mathord{\btensor H}\mathord{\btensor t}
for a given prescribed traction vector,
\mathord{\btensor t}
. The resulting discrete system
\mathord{\btensor M}\ddot{\mathord{\btensor u}}+\mathord{\btensor K}\mathord{\btensor u}=\mathord{\btensor H}\mathord{\btensor t}
incorporates the traction, but not displacement boundary conditions. Let
\mathord{\btensor i}_{d}
denote the solution
indices on which the displacement
boundary conditions are imposed, and let
\mathord{\btensor i}_{u}
denote the remaining unconstrained indices.
Suppose
\mathord{\btensor g}
denotes the vector of displacement values imposed at
\mathord{\btensor i}_{d}
.
Then, (
18
) can
be rewritten in the form

\bar{\mathord{\btensor M}}\,\ddot{\bar{\mathord{\btensor u}}}+\bar{\mathord{\btensor K}}\,\bar{\mathord{\btensor u}}=\bar{\mathord{\btensor H}}\,\mathord{\btensor t}+\bar{\mathord{\btensor B}}\,\mathord{\btensor g},

(20)

where
\bar{\mathord{\btensor M}}:=\mathord{\btensor M}(\mathord{\btensor i}_{u},\mathord{\btensor i}_{u})
,
\bar{\mathord{\btensor K}}:=\mathord{\btensor K}(\mathord{\btensor i}_{u},\mathord{\btensor i}_{u})
,
\bar{\mathord{\btensor u}}:=\mathord{\btensor u}(\mathord{\btensor i}_{u})
,
\bar{\mathord{\btensor H}}:=\mathord{\btensor H}(\mathord{\btensor i}_{u},:)
and
\bar{\mathord{\btensor B}}:=\mathord{\btensor K}(\mathord{\btensor i}_{u},\mathord{\btensor i}_{d})
.

Knowing that the structure of (
20
) is preserved for the intrusive projection-based ROM, we can assume a similar form for the semi-discretized OpInf formulation

\hat{\mathord{\btensor M}}\,\ddot{\hat{\mathord{\btensor u}}}+\hat{\mathord{\btensor K}}\,\hat{\mathord{\btensor u}}=\hat{\mathord{\btensor H}}\,\mathord{\btensor t}+\hat{\mathord{\btensor B}}\,\mathord{\btensor g},

(21)

where the “hat” notation denotes reduced order operators.
To learn the reduced order operators
\hat{\mathord{\btensor M}},\hat{\mathord{\btensor K}}\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times r}
,
\hat{\mathord{\btensor H}}\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times s}
, and
\hat{\mathord{\btensor B}}\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times m}
in the monolithic OpInf setting, we solve the following linear regression problem

\min_{\hat{\mathord{\btensor M}},\hat{\mathord{\btensor K}},\hat{\mathord{\btensor H}},\hat{\mathord{\btensor B}}}\sum_{p=1}^{j}\lVert\hat{\mathord{\btensor M}}\,\ddot{\hat{\mathord{\btensor u}}}_{p}+\hat{\mathord{\btensor K}}\,\hat{\mathord{\btensor u}}_{p}-\hat{\mathord{\btensor H}}\,\mathord{\btensor t}-\hat{\mathord{\btensor B}}\,\mathord{\btensor g},\rVert^{2}_{2}

(22)

given boundary conditions
\mathord{\btensor t}\in\mathbb{R}^{s}
and
\mathord{\btensor g}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
. Here,
\hat{\mathord{\btensor u}}_{p}=\bt@\Phi_{r}^{T}\mathord{\btensor u}(t_{p})
and
\ddot{\hat{\mathord{\btensor u}}}_{p}=\bt@\Phi_{r}^{T}\ddot{\mathord{\btensor u}}(t_{p})
are obtained from the displacement and acceleration snapshot matrices, respectively, and
j\leq\tau
denotes the number of snapshots used from the FOM dataset. If the acceleration snapshots are not readily available, they can be approximated using a finite difference scheme. To avoid the trivial solution while solving (
22
) in a fully unconstrained fashion—where each reduced operator identically equal to zero constitutes a valid minimum—we instead solve a modified linear regression problem obtained by applying the inverse of the reduced mass matrix to each term in (
21
)

\min_{\tilde{\mathord{\btensor K}},\tilde{\mathord{\btensor H}},\tilde{\mathord{\btensor B}}}\sum_{p=1}^{j}\lVert\ddot{\hat{\mathord{\btensor u}}}_{p}+\tilde{\mathord{\btensor K}}\,\hat{\mathord{\btensor u}}_{p}-\tilde{\mathord{\btensor H}}\,\mathord{\btensor t}-\tilde{\mathord{\btensor B}}\,\mathord{\btensor g},\rVert^{2}_{2}

(23)

where the tilde notation denotes operators that have been premultiplied by the inverse of the reduced mass matrix, i.e.
\tilde{\mathord{\btensor K}}=\hat{\mathord{\btensor M}}^{-1}\hat{\mathord{\btensor K}}
,
\tilde{\mathord{\btensor H}}=\hat{\mathord{\btensor M}}^{-1}\hat{\mathord{\btensor H}}
, and
\tilde{\mathord{\btensor B}}=\hat{\mathord{\btensor M}}^{-1}\hat{\mathord{\btensor B}}
. This formulation is employed in the numerical examples presented below. Once the reduced operators are determined, boundary conditions in the OpInf context are imposed directly by specifying
\mathord{\btensor g}
and
\mathord{\btensor t}
in (
21
).

Remark 4.2.
While the assumption of constant traction at the interface is trivial in the 1D setting considered here, extending this formulation to higher dimensions may introduce additional challenges, and require alternate formulations/implementations.
One possible alternate formulation assumes that the quadrature leading to
\mathord{\btensor F}
in (
19
) can be written as
\mathord{\btensor F}=\mathord{\btensor C}\mathord{\btensor t}^{\prime}
for some constant matrix
\mathord{\btensor C}
and a known (possibly nonlinear) function
\mathord{\btensor t}^{\prime}=\mathord{\btensor F}(\mathord{\btensor t})
. In this case, it is possible to learn reduced versions of
\mathord{\btensor C}
to construct the ROM. In particular,
using
\tilde{\mathord{\btensor u}}=\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{r}\hat{\mathord{\btensor u}}
leads to

\hat{\mathord{\btensor M}}\,\ddot{\hat{\mathord{\btensor u}}}+\hat{\mathord{\btensor K}}\,\hat{\mathord{\btensor u}}=\hat{\mathord{\btensor C}}\,\mathord{\btensor t}^{\prime}+\hat{\mathord{\btensor B}}\,\mathord{\btensor g},

(24)

where
\hat{\mathord{\btensor C}}=\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{r}^{T}\mathord{\btensor C}
. Learning the operators in (
24
) is expected
to lead to a viable ROM for each
\mathord{\btensor t}
. We plan to explore this research direction in future work.

An important consideration when implementing OpInf is the numerical stability of the resulting reduced order operators.
One common technique to ensure the stability of the resulting reduced order operators is regularization, which can be applied to (
23
) as

\min_{\tilde{\mathord{\btensor K}},\tilde{\mathord{\btensor H}},\tilde{\mathord{\btensor B}}}\sum_{p=1}^{j}\Big\lVert\ddot{\hat{\mathord{\btensor u}}}_{p}+\tilde{\mathord{\btensor K}}\,\hat{\mathord{\btensor u}}_{p}-\tilde{\mathord{\btensor H}}\,\mathord{\btensor t}-\tilde{\mathord{\btensor B}}\,\mathord{\btensor g}\Big\rVert_{2}^{2}+\lambda^{2}\Big(\lVert\tilde{\mathord{\btensor K}}\rVert_{F}^{2}+\lVert\tilde{\mathord{\btensor H}}\rVert_{F}^{2}+\lVert\tilde{\mathord{\btensor B}}\rVert_{F}^{2}\Big)

(25)

where the regularization term serves to penalize large entries in the reduced order operators. This is particularly important for problems with a limited number of snapshots or when a large number of modes is required to capture the target energy of the system. A parameter sweep indicated that a regularization parameter of
\lambda=10^{-4}
provides an appropriate balance between stability and accuracy. Therefore, all numerical results presented herein use
\lambda=10^{-4}
.

Another means of promoting the stability of the learned operators is to preprocess the dataset so that each variable has the same characteristic scale. This can involve variable transformations as well as scaling, shifting, and centering the data. It was found that scaling the traction data by a factor of
\frac{1}{\sigma_{\max}}
, where
\sigma_{\max}
is the maximum stress defined in Section
3.2
, showed a significant improvement on the stability and accuracy of the resulting learned operators. This approach is applied in all relevant numerical examples going forward.

4.2
NO-SAM formulation

As discussed in Section
1
, this work focuses on the domain-decomposition-based coupling between full order and OpInf reduced order models. The generic setup consists of two non-overlapping subdomains,
\Omega_{1}
and
\Omega_{2}
, with
\Omega_{1}\,\cup\,\Omega_{2}=\Omega
, as illustrated in Figure
1
. In this context, we, for example, approximate the governing PDE in (
3
) using a full order model in
\Omega_{1}
and an OpInf reduced order model in
\Omega_{2}
. This formulation can naturally be extended to various combinations of both models, as well as decompositions that include more than two subdomains.

A slightly modified version of the OpInf minimization problem in (
25
) must be solved for the case of Schwarz coupling. In this setting, the subdomain boundary conditions can no longer be assumed constant in time, as is often the case for a monolithic problem. To account for this, we modify (
25
) for subdomain
i
as

\min_{\tilde{\mathord{\btensor K}}_{i},\tilde{\mathord{\btensor H}}_{i},\tilde{\mathord{\btensor B}}_{i}}\sum_{p=1}^{j}\Big\lVert\ddot{\hat{\mathord{\btensor u}}}_{i}(t_{p})+\tilde{\mathord{\btensor K}}_{i}\,\hat{\mathord{\btensor u}}_{i}(t_{p})-\tilde{\mathord{\btensor H}}_{i}\,\mathord{\btensor t}_{i}(t_{p})-\tilde{\mathord{\btensor B}}_{i}\,\mathord{\btensor g}_{i}(t_{p})\Big\rVert_{2}^{2}+\lambda^{2}\Big(\lVert\tilde{\mathord{\btensor K}}_{i}\rVert_{F}^{2}+\lVert\tilde{\mathord{\btensor H}}_{i}\rVert_{F}^{2}+\lVert\tilde{\mathord{\btensor B}}_{i}\rVert_{F}^{2}\Big)

(26)

where
\mathord{\btensor t}_{i}(t_{p})
and
\mathord{\btensor g}_{i}(t_{p})
are altered to explicitly reflect their time dependence. For instance,
\mathord{\btensor g}_{1}(t_{p})
denotes the displacement boundary condition on the boundary
\Gamma
for subdomain
\Omega_{1}
at time
t_{p}
. Therefore, snapshot matrices containing boundary condition data on the boundary
\Gamma
for each timestep are required to infer the reduced order boundary traction and displacement operators.

When Robin transmission conditions are employed, additional reduced order operators must be introduced to account for the extra stiffness and force contributions added in (
11
). The corresponding regularized OpInf minimization problem is formulated as

\displaystyle\min_{\tilde{\mathord{\btensor K}}_{i},\tilde{\mathord{\btensor H}}_{i},\tilde{\mathord{\btensor B}}_{i}}

\displaystyle\sum_{p=1}^{j}\Big\lVert\ddot{\hat{\mathord{\btensor u}}}_{i}(t_{p})+\left[\tilde{\mathord{\btensor K}}_{i}+\frac{\beta_{ij}}{\alpha_{ij}}\tilde{\mathord{\btensor S}}_{i}\right]\hat{\mathord{\btensor u}}_{i}(t_{p})-\tilde{\mathord{\btensor H}}_{i}\,\mathord{\btensor t}_{i}(t_{p})-\tilde{\mathord{\btensor B}}_{i}\,\mathord{\btensor g}_{i}(t_{p})-\frac{1}{\alpha_{ij}}\tilde{\mathord{\btensor R}}_{i}\,\mathord{\btensor c}_{i}(t_{p})\Big\rVert_{2}^{2}

(27)

\displaystyle+\lambda^{2}\Big(\lVert\tilde{\mathord{\btensor K}}_{i}\rVert_{F}^{2}+\lVert\tilde{\mathord{\btensor S}}_{i}\rVert_{F}^{2}+\lVert\tilde{\mathord{\btensor H}}_{i}\rVert_{F}^{2}+\lVert\tilde{\mathord{\btensor B}}_{i}\rVert_{F}^{2}+\lVert\tilde{\mathord{\btensor R}}_{i}\rVert_{F}^{2}\Big)

where
\tilde{\mathord{\btensor S}}_{i}
denotes the Robin stiffness contribution,
\tilde{\mathord{\btensor R}}_{i}
is the reduced Robin boundary operator, and
\mathord{\btensor c}_{i}(t_{p})
represents the Robin boundary condition data prescribed on
\Gamma
at time
t_{p}
. The constants
\alpha_{ij}
and
\beta_{ij}
have been factored out of the operators to remove their dependence on these parameters. For all relevant numerical examples, the Robin boundary condition data
\mathord{\btensor c}_{i}(t_{p})
is scaled by a factor of
\frac{1}{\sigma_{\max}}
to enhance stability.

We emphasize that in our OpInf NO-SAM algorithm, the reduced operators appearing in (
27
) are learned once in the offline stage of the model reduction procedure, similar to the computation of the POD basis
{\bf\Phi}_{r}
. Once these operators have been solved for,
we can apply the online stage of the algorithm, in which time-stepping and subdomain synchronization via the NO-SAM algorithm is performed. Specifically,
in each time interval
I_{n}=[t_{n},t_{n+1}]
,
every Schwarz iteration involving the OpInf model is carried out according to the following steps:

1.

Gather the transmission data from neighboring subdomains in the full order space.

2.

Project the previous full order displacement, velocity, and acceleration snapshots into the reduced order basis using
\bt@\Phi_{r}
.

3.

Advance the semi-discrete system in (
21
) from
t_{n}
to
t_{n+1}
using the Newmark-
\beta
scheme and the precomputed reduced order operators, to obtain the updated reduced displacements, velocities, and accelerations.

4.

Map the updated reduced solution back to the full order space.

5.

Impose Dirichlet transmission conditions strongly (if applicable) in the full order space.

6.

Check convergence using (
10
). If satisfied, advance to the next timestep (
n\leftarrow n+1
); otherwise, move to the next subdomain and return to step 1.

Conclusions and future work

This work has presented a first investigation into transmission conditions for NO-SAM applied to the coupling of subdomain-local FOMs and non-intrusive OpInf ROMs. Through systematic studies on a 1D linear elastic wave propagation problem, we demonstrated that the choice and tuning of interface conditions play a critical role in determining convergence, accuracy, and stability. Robin–Robin transmission conditions generally outperform alternating Dirichlet–Neumann formulations in terms of efficiency, provided that parameters are carefully selected. Our results also show that OpInf ROMs, when equipped with sufficient modal content, can deliver accuracy comparable to or even exceeding FOM–FOM couplings. At the same time, we observed interface instabilities and parameter sensitivities that highlight the importance of robust transmission design and regularization strategies.
