Nurse-led care model to overcome migration-related language barriers in people with cancer (IntVeM): study protocol for a controlled before-and-after study, process and economic evaluation
Authors: Carolin Höckelmann, Johannes Bösche, Richard Dano, Stefanie Federhen, Annika Dangendorf, Wiebke Müller, Anne Adams, Kübra Annac, Yüce Yilmaz-Aslan, Patrick Brzoska, Minh Hai Vu, Sascha Köpke
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionIn 2024, 30.4% of the German population had a migration background. People with migration-related language barriers face numerous challenges within the healthcare system, which can negatively impact their health outcomes. While language barriers affect healthcare in general, oncology represents a complex field in which effective communication is crucial. For people with cancer in particular, difficulties in understanding information, treatment options, and the healthcare system itself may hinder access to services. This increases the risk of complications, prolonged hospitalisations and readmissions. At the same time, there is little information about this group, and evaluations of structured services are lacking. This study assesses a nurse-led complex intervention focusing on structured information and ongoing support for people with cancer and migration-related language barriers.Methods and analysisThe intervention is designed for people with cancer and migration-related language barriers and comprises three key elements: (a) continuous guidance provided by already existing roles of oncology nurse specialists, (b) a telephone interpreting service and (c) multilingual informational materials. We will assess the intervention’s effectiveness with a controlled before-and-after study. Assuming an effect size of d=0.5 and using a 2:1 allocation ratio with 80% power, we aim to recruit 126 people with cancer and migration-related language barriers (84 intervention; 42 control) for participation. We will assess the primary outcome psychosocial support needs assessed with the Psychosocial Risk Questionnaire (PSR) at admission, discharge, and 3-month post-discharge using paper-based or digital questionnaires. The secondary outcomes, such as knowledge, quality of life, anxiety and satisfaction, are measured alongside the primary outcomes and compare the distribution of items and scores using Student’s t-test (total scores) or the Wilcoxon rank sum test (individual items) at a one-sided significance level of 5%. We will use an embedded process and economic evaluation to examine the feasibility, acceptability and implementation of the intervention in line with the Medical Research Council guidance for process evaluations of complex interventions.Ethics and disseminationThe Ethics Committee of the Faculty of Medicine, University of Cologne (No. 23–1303 and 24–1266), approved the protocol, and the study was registered in the German Clinical Trials Register. We will disseminate the results of the study through peer-reviewed publications and at academic conferences.Trial registration numberGerman Clinical Trials Register (DRKS00034749).

Introduction

Rationale, evidence gap

In 2024, 30.4% of the German population had a migration background, of whom 63.3% were first-generation migrants. This group is at higher risk of experiencing language barriers, especially shortly after their arrival.1 2 Language barriers may arise when two people do not share the same first language.3 In recent years, migration to Germany has continued to rise, and migrant groups have become more heterogeneous in terms of origin and language proficiency.4

People with cancer and migration-related language barriers face numerous challenges within the healthcare system, which can negatively impact their health outcomes. While language barriers affect healthcare in general, oncology represents a complex field in which effective communication is crucial. For people with cancer and language barriers in particular, difficulties in understanding information, treatment options and the healthcare system itself may hinder access to services, reduce participation in preventive measures such as screenings and increase the risk of complications, prolonged hospitalisations and readmissions.5–7 People with cancer have a wide range of complex supportive care needs8 9 and the demanding nature of cancer therapies often leads to side effects that compromise quality of life. These side effects are closely linked to treatment response and survival outcomes.5 6 9 Li et al10 reported that culturally and linguistically diverse cancer survivors have unmet needs regarding general care support, health systems, general information and psychological well-being. In Germany, approximately 500 000 new cancer cases are diagnosed each year,11 with an estimated one in five newly diagnosed people with cancer having a migration background.12 The actual number of people with cancer affected by language barriers, and the extent to which these barriers influence treatment outcomes, remains unknown. This gap highlights the need for systematic strategies to support communication and understanding in cancer care.

The growing proportion of people with a migration background further emphasises the necessity for accessible information and interprofessional support along the treatment pathway.9 13 Therefore, overcoming language barriers represents a key challenge in the care of people with cancer with limited language proficiency. In Germany, systematic approaches to address language barriers in cancer care are currently lacking.

A patient-centred approach to cancer care, focusing on the complex needs and active involvement of people with cancer, relies on effective and appropriate communication as a fundamental prerequisite for establishing a strong therapeutic relationship between healthcare providers and people with cancer.14 In this context, Oncology Nurse Specialists (ONS) offer a promising approach to address the complex demands and unmet needs of people with cancer with language barriers.15

Internationally, advanced nursing roles such as nurse specialists have been successfully implemented to provide comprehensive care for vulnerable and hard-to-reach groups. These professionals act as case managers, coordinators and consultants for people with cancer and their relatives.16 Although Germany is in the early phase of developing and implementing expanded nursing roles,17 existing roles increasingly align with international criteria for advanced nursing practice.18 19 Considering Fitch’s Supportive Care Framework,9 expanded nursing roles are particularly well suited to address the specific needs of people with cancer with language barriers.

The IntVeM study

Based on this context, the IntVeM study (integrative care model for people with cancer and migration-related language barriers) was initiated. This nurse-led intervention focuses on structured information and ongoing support and comprises three key elements: (a) continuous guidance provided by ONS, (b) a telephone interpreting service and (c) multilingual informational materials. The intervention is based on the already established role of the ONS. Accordingly, the research question is: Does the implementation of a structured nurse-led information management and follow-up programme with standardised intervention time points influence the support needs, the level of informedness, the satisfaction and the quality of care among people with cancer and migration-related language barriers?

Objectives

The aim of this study is to evaluate the effectiveness and feasibility of a newly developed nurse-led care intervention for people with cancer and migration-related language barriers. Consequently, the objectives are to assess: (a) effectiveness, (b) quality of implementation, mechanisms of impact and relevant contextual factors and (c) total intervention costs.

Methods

Study design

The IntVeM study follows a controlled before-and-after design with three data collection points in both groups. We chose a controlled before-and-after design, as it enables time-based comparison with a control group and is feasible for a pragmatic single-centre setting, where randomisation is not possible. We will assess the effects (a) of the IntVeM intervention by comparing the intervention group, receiving the complex intervention, with the control group, receiving usual care. Oncology wards serve as both control and intervention wards at different time points. In addition to evaluating effectiveness, a mixed-methods process evaluation (b) will be conducted to investigate how and under which conditions the observed outcomes are achieved. In addition, (c) a comprehensive cost evaluation will be carried out to determine the total intervention costs. An overview of the study design is provided in figure 1.

Overview of the IntVeM study design.

The study follows the recommendations of the Medical Research Council (MRC) for the development and evaluation of complex interventions.20 Reporting adheres to the Standard Protocol Items: Recommendations for Interventional Trials (SPIRIT) guideline,21 complemented by items of the Guideline for Inclusion of Patient-Reported Outcomes in Clinical Trial Protocols (SPIRIT-PRO)22 where applicable (see online supplemental material - B).

Study setting

The study will be conducted at a university hospital and comprehensive cancer centre (CCC) in western Germany, which provides inpatient care to 24 000 people with cancer per year.23 In 2024, 42.7% of the city’s residents had a migration background, representing a continuous increase from 36% in 2014,24 while the proportion of those facing language barriers remains unknown. Based on our own unpublished data, around 10% (9.5%, 95% CI 8.2 to 10.7) of people with cancer (191 of 1824 people with cancer) treated at the study site between October 2023 and February 2024 experienced a language barrier related to migration. In our analysis, among the most frequently represented foreign languages were Turkish (18.3%), Russian (16.8%), Ukrainian (7.3%) and Arabic (6.8%).

The hospital employs around 12 500 staff members. Recently, nurses with expanded roles have been introduced, with 36 currently working in these positions across several specialties, including oncology, wound management, psychiatry and surgery.23

Currently, language barriers at the university hospital are not addressed in a systematic approach but rather rely on the individual efforts of healthcare providers.

Description of expanded nursing roles

In 2021, a centralised unit for ONS was implemented at the University Hospital. This unit coordinates and supports specialised nursing practice across multiple tumour-specific centres, such as the Head and Neck Cancer Centre, the Visceral Oncology Centre and the Breast Cancer Centre. The unit is affiliated with the Department of Nursing Practice Development and Nursing Science, which enables structured role development guided by experienced nursing scientists and practice development experts. This unit currently comprises seven ONS, each of whom holds at minimum a bachelor’s degree in nursing. In addition, four of the seven ONS have completed a non-academic formal specialist training in oncology nursing, which includes 720 hours of theoretical and 1800 hours of practical training. Each ONS is affiliated with a specific certified CCC ward. Within these settings, the focus is placed on nursing phenomena relevant to the respective tumour entities. ONS are integrated into routine ward services one day per week. On the remaining working days, they are allocated time to focus exclusively on the delivery of extended oncology nursing services as part of the comprehensive care provided to people with cancer within the CCC structures. According to the International Council of Nursing guidelines, these qualifications and role characteristics are considered consistent with a developmental pathway towards the clinical nurse specialist role.16 To ensure alignment with advanced nursing practice standards, role development follows the principles of advanced nursing practice as outlined in Hamric and Hanson’s Advanced Nursing Practice framework,25 including core competencies such as direct clinical practice, expert coaching, consultation and systems leadership.

Intervention development including patient and public involvement

The theoretical background of intervention development is the concept of Hamric and Hanson′s advanced nursing practice25 and the supportive care framework for people with cancer,9 ensuring a patient-centred intervention. In the initial phase of intervention development, we carried out several work packages, including two systematic reviews on interventions and care models and factors affecting interventions and care model implementation. We engaged people with cancer and migration-related language barriers, their relatives, healthcare providers and ONS in the intervention development, based on the principles of experience-based co-design.26 Therefore, we actively involved people with cancer (n=8) and their relatives (n=3) by conducting interviews to understand their healthcare needs. In addition, we distributed questionnaires (n=17) to identify challenges experienced by people with cancer and their relatives in hospitals using the Interpersonal Processes of Care instrument,27 which assesses communication and patient-centred decision-making. To provide annual and as-needed-based guidance from the target group, we established an advisory board with interest holders (n=9), which will accompany the project throughout its duration. Additionally, we conducted one focus group with interpreters (n=4) to explore health care needs from the perspective of people with cancer, since they offer unique insights into communication and cultural challenges.

Apart from people with cancer, our co-design efforts focus on healthcare providers, especially ONS. Accordingly, we conducted three focus groups with healthcare providers, including oncologists, nurses, social workers and psychologists (n=22), in order to evaluate the current care situation and factors influencing care delivery. In a further survey (n=191) we applied the German version of the Cultural Competence Assessment (CCA-G), assessing physicians’ and nurses’ cultural sensitivity and cultural competence behaviour.28 Moreover, we carried out a shadowing programme (n=4) on three oncology wards to explore existing structures and workflows (see figure 2).

Methodological steps of intervention development.

After data triangulation of all work package results, the ONS had a central role in developing the complex intervention from their clinical point of view. The ONS joined biweekly 90-min meetings to discuss the project’s progress and its adaptation to their specific context. This process involved, for example, collaboratively developing the intervention components within research-practice tandems using the template for intervention description and replication checklist29 as a framework, followed by the finalisation of the selected components for implementation through discussion.

To evaluate both relevance and feasibility of the intervention components, we conducted an expert survey with key interest holders, including representatives from clinical practice, research, advisory board and organisations representing people with migration-related language barriers (n=22). We will publish details of the intervention development phase elsewhere.

Intervention design

As this nurse-led intervention focuses on structured information and ongoing support (comprising ONS, telephone interpreting service and multilingual information materials) and draws on insights from the initial phase of the IntVeM study, we identified six components that define the responsibilities of ONS in collaboration with the interprofessional team. The complex intervention distinguishes between fixed core components (components 1–2, 6), which must be implemented in all cases while taking into account the clinical experience of ONS and the individual needs of people with cancer, and flexible components (components 3, 4, 5), which can be individually adapted (table 1, figure 3). Components can be further classified into patient-related and system-related interventions. People with cancer-related interventions involve actions directly connected to patient care. These include (1) continuous person-centred support and care coordination by ONS, (2) use of multilingual (information) material, (3) use of communication and understanding aids, (4) use of decision aids and (5) continuous information provision beyond acute inpatient care. In contrast, system-related interventions are linked to organisational structures and healthcare providers, which relates to (6) access to telephone interpreter services. table 1 outlines the six components in detail.

Programme theory and logic model

Throughout the intervention development, we continuously discussed and refined assumptions regarding how the individual intervention components were expected to influence communication, supportive care and outcomes. These assumptions constitute the underlying programme theory, summarised in the logic model. The logic model was designed to illustrate how the intervention components are expected to interact and to generate the intended outcomes (figure 4). The logic models’ development was informed by the MRC framework for complex interventions20 and additional methodological principles.30 The process evaluation of the complex intervention has been grounded on the logic model.

Implementation strategies

Based on the implementation framework - integrated Promoting Action on Research Implementation in Health Services (i-PARIHS) domains31 (facilitation, context, innovation and recipients) - we designed strategies to support implementation of the intervention components. As the ONS role had already established, we focused on preparing the ONS for new additional tasks rather than redefining their role within the team.

A key implementation strategy will be to implement a 2-day training programme for the ONS, consisting of 5 hours of instruction per day. The programme will be organised into four modules, each addressing key aspects relevant to the intervention. These will include current research on migration and healthcare, effective communication strategies and approaches to overcoming language barriers, practical guidance on the implementation of the intervention and the incorporation of experiential knowledge contributed by representatives of the target population.

By involving ONS in the intervention development through the experience-based co-design approach26, we will be able to tailor the complex intervention to the specific conditions of the ward, thereby easing implementation. In addition, we will accompany the ONS in weekly 1-hour meetings to share experiences, coordinate activities, discuss adjustments, enhance teamwork and identify further training needs during the intervention phase.

To ensure sustainable implementation of the intervention and maintain coverage during illness or vacation, we will establish a network structure of ward nurses in collaboration with the ONS. The network consists of ward nurses from the same wards who are interested in the project and are willing to support continuity when needed. Participation in one of the two preparatory training days will be required to assume this role.

Prior to the start of the intervention, we will present the project to the interprofessional team of all participating wards through a 15-min kick-off event and follow-up by e-mail. We will describe the ONS role, provide each ward with a project folder containing all relevant materials, and present the team’s tasks, such as using bilingual communication cards and question-prompt lists. Additionally, we will conduct specific interprofessional team training every 6 weeks to continuously raise awareness and support implementation of intervention components, as well as the identification and documentation of the target group. Moreover, we will use 10-min training videos on how to use the newly implemented telephone interpreting service (https://triaphon.org/) to ensure proper use and continuously reinforce its value. Watching the video will be mandatory for healthcare providers, which will be supported and supervised by ward managers.

To avoid contamination, all implementation strategies will be implemented during a 2-month transition period following completion of control group recruitment and before the start of the intervention phase (figure 1). During the control phase, healthcare providers will only be instructed to routinely document language barriers among people with cancer to identify eligible participants.

Control group

People with cancer and their relatives in the control group receive standard care. This does not include any structured interventions to address language barriers or continuous follow-up by ONS. Departments and healthcare providers manage language barriers without targeted system-level support. Consequently, language barriers are typically addressed using unstructured approaches, such as interpretation by relatives, assistance from multilingual staff or digital translation tools.

Evaluation of effects

Participants

We defined inclusion and exclusion criteria on people with cancer, relatives, healthcare providers and ward level for participating in the study.

People with cancer and relative level

Eligible people are (1) adults (≥18 years old) with (2) a primary or secondary diagnosis of cancer in line with the clinical specialities of the ONS (head and neck, gastrointestinal, gynaecological and haematologic oncology), in (3) inpatient care and who (4) experience language barriers that limit their ability to speak, understand, read and/or write the common language (German) of healthcare providers.

This study focuses on people with cancer whose native language is Arabic, Russian, Turkish or Ukrainian, as these are among the most frequently spoken languages in the study setting according to our own unpublished data, and who are (5) able to read in their native language and have (6) provided written informed consent. Considering the delivery of multiple components an (7) expected minimum hospital stay of 48 hours is required after the people with cancer have provided informed consent and completed the survey at baseline.

People with cancer receiving specialist palliative care are excluded only if they are considered to be in the terminal phase of illness, defined as the actively dying phase, based on the clinical impression of the ONS and healthcare providers. In addition, people with severely impaired general condition and/or disorientation are excluded from participation.

Relatives are identified by the people with cancer and (1) must have sufficient self-reported proficiency to speak, understand, read and write German and (2) play a key role in supporting the people with cancer during their hospital stay and throughout the course of illness.

In the control group, both people with cancer and their relatives can participate independently, whereas in the intervention group, relatives can participate only if the people with cancer do.

Healthcare provider level

Healthcare providers include ONS, ward nurses and physicians of all participating wards. In the context of this study, we excluded other healthcare providers such as therapists and non-clinical staff, for example, service and administrative personnel.

Ward level

Eligible wards are general oncology wards with an assigned ONS excluding Intensive Care Units (ICU) and high-dependency units.

Sample size calculation

The sample size is based on the results of a recent mixed-methods study investigating a structured, integrated, cross-sectoral psycho-oncological programme aimed at ensuring the provision of care in both inpatient and outpatient settings in Germany32 using the same primary outcome measure.33 Jenniches et al,32 assume a standardised effect size of Cohen’s d=0.5 for the primary outcome. Using a two-sample t-test with a 2:1 allocation ratio, one-sided alpha of 0.05 and 80% power, a total of 114 people with cancer as participants will be needed. To ensure the feasibility of this single-centre trial, a one-sided statistical testing approach was chosen to optimise statistical power without unnecessarily inflating the required sample size for the specific target group. To account for potential data loss, the sample will be increased by 10%, resulting in a final sample size of 126 people with cancer participants (n=84 intervention, n=42 control). A difference between the intervention group and the control group in the change in the PSR from T0 to T1 is expected, corresponding to a standardised effect size of 0.5 (Cohen’s d). Based on the ‘study setting’ section, we assume that 24 000 people with cancer will be treated at the University Hospital per year,23 of whom 10% will face a migration-related language barrier. Although we will focus on only four languages, we consider the projected sample size to be realistically achievable within the study period.

Recruitment

People with cancer and relative level

We will recruit potential participants starting with the enrolment of people with cancer and their relatives in the control group at the participating wards. Once we reach the target sample size for the control group, we will start implementing the intervention and subsequently initiate recruitment for the intervention group on the same wards. People with cancer previously assigned to the control group may be enrolled in the intervention group if re-admitted.

Recruitment on people with cancer level will rely on daily electronic patient record. Routine assessments of the nurses include components related to language barriers. To ensure consistent assessment and documentation, we will continuously provide short training sessions, pocket cards defining language barriers along with visual aids to nurses. Nurses record language barriers, specifying the preferred (native) langauge of people with cancer, if communication is noticeably impaired due to limited language proficiency. On Mondays, Wednesdays and Fridays, ONS and nursing researchers will screen the electronic patient record. If people with cancer are documented as having limited language proficiency, we will then verify whether they meet the eligibility criteria. The trained nursing researchers or ONS will contact eligible people with cancer, provide written study information, answer any questions and obtain their written informed consent.

Recruitment on relatives’ level will take place once eligible people with cancer have been enrolled and will follow the same procedure.

Healthcare provider level

Recruitment of healthcare providers will be based on purposeful sampling and conducted via email, information posters with QR codes and direct contact between healthcare providers and ONS or nursing researchers. To minimise selection bias, we will develop a sampling plan to ensure a balanced representation of healthcare providers across different wards. While focusing primarily on nurses, we will also include physicians in the recruitment. In addition, we will ensure broad dissemination of information through a large number of recruiters.

For each outcome, we selected the instrument that was considered most appropriate for assessing the respective outcome in the target group, based on the availability of validated versions in the respective languages and its feasibility. The impact on relatives will also be considered. Table 2 presents all primary and secondary outcomes, instruments, data sources and data collection time points.

People with cancer level

Our primary outcome, psychosocial support needs to ensure medical and social care services, will be assessed using the PSR questionnaire, and changes from baseline (t0) and follow-up (t1) will be analysed. We defined the PSR as the primary endpoint, as it captures both general psychosocial support needs and domains related to health literacy, self-efficacy and social support, which closely align with the intervention’s mechanisms and goals. The key secondary outcome, assessing support needs for coping with problems will also be measured using the PSR questionnaire.33 The secondary outcomes evaluate knowledge using a Numeric Rating Scale (NRS), therapy adherence using the Brief Adherence Assessment/Screening Tool,34 engagement in the therapeutic process using collaboRATE,35 36 preference of autonomy and informed decision-making using the Control Preferences Scale (CPS and CPS-post),37 38 quality of life using EuroQol 5-Dimensions with 3-Problem Levels (EQ5D-3),39 40 anxiety using the Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale - Anxiety Subscale (HADS-A)41–43 and satisfaction using a NRS. For some instruments only selected items will be used. The number of emergency visits will be assessed as an open-ended numeric response. In addition, we will assess several people with cancer-related variables within the electronic patient record, for example, sociodemographic variables, admission diagnosis, comorbidities, contact frequency with ONS and number of inpatient stays, including length of stay (table 2A).

Based on the recommendation of Beaton et al,44 we used a forward–backward translation procedure conducted by native speakers to ensure linguistic equivalence, followed by independent pretesting. At the beginning of the intervention phase, we further pretested the complete questionnaires with the first two people with cancer in each language group.

Relatives level

The secondary outcomes include relatives’ knowledge and satisfaction using a NRS and anxiety using HADS-A41–43 (table 2B).

Healthcare provider level

The secondary outcomes include cultural awareness using the CCA-G28 (table 2C).

Data collection and management

People with cancer and relative level

At people with cancer and relative levels, we will collect data at three time points: t0 (at enrolment), t1 (at discharge) and t2 (3 months after discharge). We will collect outcome data both paper-based and electronically via Research Electronic Data Capture (REDCap) to accommodate the preferences of people with cancer and relatives. The trained nursing researchers or the trained ONS will distribute the questionnaire at t0 and t1. At t2 data will be collected either paper-based by postal mail or electronically via REDCap by e-mail. People with cancer will receive the questionnaires in their respective first language, while relatives will receive the questionnaires in German. Participants will complete the questionnaire independently (self-assessed), with support from ONS or nursing researchers if needed. To minimise missing data, we will check questionnaires for completeness on collection, inform participants about missing items and request completion where possible. If a participant selects paper-based questionnaires at follow-up, these are sent by post together with a prepaid return envelope. To promote retention and complete follow-up data, regardless of whether questionnaires are completed electronically or on paper, we will send email and/or telephone reminders 5 and 10 days after the initial contact. Prior to final data export, we will conduct comprehensive data cleaning, and we will check the data for quality and plausibility to ensure the integrity and reliability of the dataset. The characteristics of people with cancer will be collected from the electronic patient record, whereas relative characteristics will be obtained by questionnaire. Study-related information (eg, participant flow, reasons for non-participation) will be documented electronically.

We will collect the data pseudonymously using a self-generated code (sequential coding) created in advance by the nursing researchers or ONS, allowing a linkage between sets of data and individual participants. The code will be maintained throughout the entire data collection period and documented in a pseudonymisation list. We will store paper-based data in a locker and electronic data will be password protected. The pseudonymisation list will be saved securely in a separate locker. REDCap will automatically generate a daily update.

To be included in the analysis, people with cancer must have received at least one structured admission consultation that includes the core components of the intervention (table 1, figure 3, components 1–2 and 6), thereby being considered exposed to the intervention.

Paper-based data will be entered by one nursing researcher and subsequently checked for plausibility to minimise transmission errors. Only the nursing researchers have access to all data. Prior to final data export, comprehensive data cleaning will be conducted and the data will be checked for quality and plausibility to ensure the integrity and reliability of the dataset. In addition, plausibility checks will be conducted. All data will be de-identified prior to publication.

Healthcare provider level

At healthcare provider level, we will use a paper-based and electronic questionnaire at two time points: t0 (before the intervention phase) and t1 (after the end of the intervention). We will collect data anonymously. To increase retention, we will use a face-to-face and e-mail reminder.

Randomisation and allocation

Randomisation and allocation are not applicable, as the ward selection is based on pre-existing areas of responsibility of ONS, and each participating ward will first serve as a control and subsequently as an intervention group.

Blinding

Due to the type of intervention, it is not possible to blind people with cancer and their relatives, ONS delivering the intervention, healthcare providers or those involved in data collection. Due to the study design, it is also not possible to blind the statisticians.

Statistical methods for data analysis

Statistical analysis of the intervention’s effects is performed by the Institute of Medical Statistics and Computational Biology (IMSB). The IMSB receives a clean data export from REDCap. Distribution of items and scores will be compared between the groups (intervention vs control) using Student’s t-test (total scores) or the Wilcoxon rank sum test (individual items) at a one-sided level of 5%. Primarily, the mean values of the PSR items on ‘psychosocial support need to ensure medical and social care services’ and ‘support need for coping with problems’ and the HADS are considered (a priori hypotheses). We assume less than 10% of observations will be dependent. Otherwise, we will perform subgroup analyses for dependent and independent observations. For incomplete questionnaires, validated scales and their corresponding scores will be calculated according to the respective manual. If no specific instructions for handling missing values are available, we allow up to 20% missing values and will prorate the scores if applicable. Fully missing values, for example, lost-to-follow-up, will not be replaced. Adjustment for relevant covariates is performed using analysis of covariance. Descriptive statistics will be described using mean, SD and percentiles (0, 25, 50, 75, 100) for quantitative variables and absolute and relative frequencies for qualitative variables.

Evaluation of processes

In addition to the evaluation of effects, a process evaluation is fundamental to understand the functioning of the complex intervention.45 The process evaluation, with data collection points before, during and after the intervention phase, is based on a convergent mixed-method approach.46 The process evaluation will be conducted in line with the MRC Framework,20 45 focusing on the core domains: implementation, mechanisms of impact and context. The aim of the process evaluation is to assess the extent to which the intervention was implemented as planned, to identify facilitating and hindering factors and to examine how the expanded roles of the various interest holders were perceived, in order to understand how the intervention functions in practice. Due to the complexity of the intervention in terms of the number of intervention components and the heterogeneous and hard-to-reach target group, fidelity, reach and dose of the intervention are key aspects of the process evaluation with regard to implementation. Assessing the mechanisms of impacts, we will focus on the interactions between people with cancer, relatives, ONS and the multiprofessional care team. The process evaluation will explore how these groups understand, accept and integrate the new nursing role into the care of people with cancer and migration-related language barriers. These aspects are essential in order to understand how the complex intervention works in practice.45

Participants

Besides people with cancer and their relatives, we will recruit healthcare providers of the multidisciplinary care team on those wards where the intervention was implemented. Eligibility for people with cancer and relatives is limited to those assigned to the intervention group. Healthcare providers are eligible to participate if they worked on the intervention ward during the intervention phase and therefore experienced the intervention.

Data collection and management

We will collect data at four different time points: tp0 before the start of the intervention, tp1 during the intervention phase, tp2 after people with cancer completed the intervention and tp3 after the intervention phase.

For the quantitative approach we plan three surveys with healthcare providers, either paper-based or electronic, and an analysis of process data. Initially we will conduct the Organizational Readiness for Implementing Change47 questionnaire. To assess contextual factors at the institutional level, we aim to survey all ward managers (n=10) on the participating wards, who will be invited via e-mail. The questionnaires will be distributed in person by nursing researchers. Additionally, we will carry out the Cologne Role Development in Advanced Practice Nursing Questionnaire.48 We will include all ward nurses and physicians of the multiprofessional teams in order to assess interest holders’ acceptance of advanced nursing practice. The size of the teams in the participating wards varies, ranging from 30 to 70 ward nurses and from 3 to 12 physicians per ward. We will recruit a pragmatic 30% sample of healthcare providers, resulting in an estimated sample size of n=70. To minimise selection bias, we will use e-mail distribution lists, ensuring equal opportunity for participation. Furthermore, the currently validated Cologne Role Development in Advanced Practice Nursing Questionnaire19 will be completed by the ONS (n=6). The aim is to describe the current stage of role development. To enhance overall survey response, we will use email reminders and on-ward visits by the nursing researchers.

In addition, we will analyse process data by examining the intervention logbook, which contains detailed information about the delivery of intervention components, frequency and content of counselling sessions, time requirements, unforeseen obstacles and use of telephone interpreters. The analysis focuses on fidelity, dose, adaptations and reach.

For the qualitative approach we plan different data collection strategies. Individual interviews will be conducted with people with cancer (n=7) and relatives (n=3) to gain in-depth insights into their experience. Depending on the general state and preferences of the people with cancer, the interviews will be conducted face-to-face or by telephone. Data collection with healthcare providers will focus on feasibility and perceived acceptability of the intervention. Therefore, two focus groups will be carried out with ward managers, ward nurses and physicians of the participating wards (n=10–14). In addition, individual interviews will be conducted with the ONS (n=6). Due to our close collaboration with the ONS, an external project partner will conduct the data collection.

The qualitative data collection is based on semi-structured interview guides. The guiding questions will be derived from the findings of the initial study and informed by the i-PARIHS framework,31 project meetings during the intervention phase and process data. A purposive sampling strategy will be applied for participant selection to ensure the inclusion of key interest holders. A sampling plan will ensure that both people with cancer in terms of origin, cancer type and ward and healthcare providers in terms of professional group and qualification levels are equally represented. The sample size for the qualitative approach will be determined based on the principle of data saturation. We adopted the data saturation model proposed by Saunders et al,49 defining data saturation as the point at which additional data collection no longer generates new relevant information, resulting in redundancy in the data. ONS, ward managers and senior physicians will support recruitment by facilitating contact with healthcare providers and people with cancer and their relatives. All interviews and focus groups will be audio-recorded and transcribed verbatim. Any information potentially identifying individuals or institutions will be anonymised during the transcription process. The recordings will be deleted once transcription is complete.

Data analysis

Data analysis is based on and guided by the logic model.20 The quantitative and qualitative data will be analysed individually and subsequently integrated to provide a comprehensive understanding. For quantitative data, analyses will be carried out descriptively using frequency, distribution, distributional location and dispersion (eg, SD, IQR) by using SPSS. Qualitative data will be analysed using the qualitative content approach described by Mayring,50 including both deductive and inductive coding. To strengthen the credibility, two researchers will independently code the data and subsequently discuss their findings. Discrepancies will be resolved through a consensus process within the nursing researchers.

The integration of qualitative and quantitative findings will be carried out by merging the results.46 A joint display will be used in order to visualise results.51 Reporting of the results will follow the Good Reporting of a Mixed Methods Study recommendations.52

Economic evaluation

For the economic evaluation we will focus on the intervention costs in terms of general intervention costs and staffing resources. General intervention costs will include training programme expenses and materials used. The use of the telephone interpreter service will be considered as a major cost component. The staffing resources of the ONS will be a key aspect of the economic evaluation, as integrating the new ONS role into an already established job profile may reduce capacity for existing duties.

Data collection and management

General intervention costs (eg, training programme materials) will be documented by the nursing researchers. Costs for the telephone interpreter service will be assessed using billing data, analysing both the timing and the ward of service utilisation. Staffing resources will be monitored through process data using the intervention logbook, in which the ONS records both the frequency and duration of contacts, as well as the delivery of intervention components and resources required for the continuous training of healthcare providers.

Data analysis

The total costs of the intervention will be presented, including all relevant direct and indirect cost components. The data will be analysed descriptively. Considering the staffing resources, a workforce needs assessment will be conducted to determine the required number of ONS for further implementation.

Ethics and dissemination

The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Faculty of Medicine, University of Cologne (No. 23–1303 and 24–1266) and registered in the German Clinical Trials Register (DRKS00034749). Ethical approval covering all parts of the study was granted. Approval for the surveys and interviews involving healthcare providers was also obtained from the respective staff councils.

Risk and benefits

People with cancer are considered a vulnerable group from an ethical perspective. Hospital admission with an additional language barrier may further exacerbate this vulnerability. Participation in this before-and-after study may therefore be perceived as burdensome. To minimise potential burden, data collection will be conducted by experienced ONS and nursing researchers in consultation with the interprofessional care team. Prior to participation, a brief assessment of the general condition will be undertaken to avoid undue strain, and participants will be provided with written information on potential risks and benefits. Therefore, participation is considered to involve minimal risk. Potential harms are outweighed by the anticipated benefits of reducing language barriers and improving care among people with cancer.

Dissemination

All results, including negative and neutral findings, will be disseminated. Findings from the initial study, intervention development phase, and both the effect and process evaluation will be published in international peer-reviewed journals, with the intention of providing open access where feasible. Interim and final results will also be presented at relevant scientific conferences, professional meetings and interest-holder events. Publications will adhere to current reporting guidelines (eg, SPIRIT, Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials (CONSORT), Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) and Consolidated criteria for Reporting Qualitative research (COREQ), as appropriate). Datasets generated and analysed in this study can be obtained from the author on reasonable request.

Data handling and access

Written informed consent (see online supplemental material - C) will be obtained from all participants before enrolment and any data collection. Data handling will strictly comply with the EU General Data Protection Regulation and the German Federal Data Protection Act. Participants may request access to their data, withdraw consent or request deletion or correction as long as the data remain pseudonymised. All data will be stored securely in locked cabinets at the study centre or within access-restricted IT environments and deleted after a retention period of 10 years.

Given the exploratory nature of the study, no formal data monitoring will be implemented. Continuous monitoring of recruitment and intervention adherence will be conducted during regular meetings with ONS based on recruitment data and the intervention logbook.

Discussion

The IntVeM intervention is a complex intervention designed to improve quality of care for people with cancer and migration-related language barriers in oncology by addressing communication challenges. Its basis are the already established ONS, which will provide continuous person-centred support and care coordination. The intervention focuses on advancing ONS’ role for the needs of this specific group rather than on initial role development. Supplementary tools such as a bilingual treatment care plan or bilingual decision templates have been developed to strengthen the role of the ONS. Other tools (eg, bilingual communication cards, 24/7 telephone interpreting service) are further implemented within the interprofessional team. The controlled before-and-after study will investigate the effectiveness of this intervention. We expect people with cancer in the intervention group to be better informed, feel more secure, be more actively involved in treatment decisions and less likely to discontinue treatment. In addition, the intervention is anticipated to increase satisfaction, reduce complications and improve recovery outcomes, thereby decreasing the need for psychosocial support. By addressing the lack of high-quality nurse-led interventions to overcome migration-related language barriers in oncology, we will aim to provide important generalisable evidence. The embedded process evaluation will further enrich our knowledge about factors that facilitate and hinder the implementation of this complex intervention, providing a comprehensive view of how and under what circumstances it will actually work. A cost evaluation will complement the assessment of practical feasibility for further implementation.

A key strength of our study is that we developed the intervention theory-based9 20 45 and in collaboration with the ONS and other important interest holders, following the experience-based co-design approach,26 which suggests intervention development taking into account clinical structures. Additionally, the involvement of people with cancer in the initial phase indicates that the intervention will address the needs of the target group. Monthly meetings with the interdisciplinary project partners, as well as regular consultations with the scientific board, ensure methodological integrity and quality. Another strength is that the intervention will extend beyond the inpatient stay and will be implemented throughout the entire treatment pathway. This is particularly important in cancer care, because people with cancer go through multiple levels of care, especially when treatment continues over a long period.53 The involvement of relatives is another strength of the intervention, as they provide emotional support, assist with healthcare organisations and help maintain continuity of care.54 Considering diversity in healthcare is a key strength of this project, reducing health disparities and ensuring comprehensive care.55

However, it is important to note some limitations of the study. Randomisation and allocation concealment will not be feasible as ward selection is determined by the pre-existing responsibilities of ONS. This may lead to selection bias and affect the generalisability of the findings. Furthermore, blinding of participants, researchers and statisticians will not be possible due to the nature of complex intervention and study design. Awareness of group assignment can influence participants’ responses as well as the way researchers collect data. The involvement of the telephone interpreting service as a collaborating partner entails a potential conflict of interest in the evaluation. However, the service provider is excluded from all evaluation-related activities and is solely responsible for the operational delivery of the telephone interpreting services. Another limitation is the heterogeneity of the target group, which includes different cancer types, stages and treatment regimens. However, this heterogeneity is also a strength, enhancing the generalisability of findings and providing a multi-perspective view of the target group. We are aware that factors such as the timing of the initial cancer diagnosis and the number of treatment cycles may influence the outcomes of people with cancer. Exploratory subgroup analyses will be considered depending on data availability. Possible subgroup analyses are cancer type, native language and initial diagnosis. Furthermore, the target group is considered ‘hard-to-reach’,56 57 and cultural factors may influence participation, as people with cancer are required to be able to read in their native language. This requirement may reduce the acceptability of the intervention among people with limited literacy skills, who may therefore be underrepresented in the study sample. Another barrier to participation is the length of the combined instruments, which may increase participant burden. However, we have aimed to keep the questionnaire as short as possible, and support from the ONS may facilitate questionnaire completion and reduce overall burden. Nevertheless, cross-country comparability must therefore be interpreted with caution, as cultural differences may influence responses even when the same instruments are applied.58 Regarding the development of the instruments, a forward–backward translation procedure was applied to ensure linguistic and conceptual equivalence across languages. Native speakers translated instruments that were not yet available in Arabic, Russian, Turkish or Ukrainian. While this procedure follows established methodological standards, the adaptation process may still be limited by cultural and contextual nuances that are not fully captured through translation alone. Cognitive pretests with native speakers helped to identify and revise ambiguous or culturally sensitive items, and a subsequent people with cancer-based pretest confirmed overall feasibility and acceptability. Nevertheless, further validation in larger and more diverse samples of people with cancer may be necessary to confirm the reliability and transferability of the adapted instruments. However, the intervention will not focus on the overcoming of cultural barriers, which will likely play a significant role in shaping communication and interactions with healthcare providers. To better account for this diversity, we have implemented an advisory board representing diverse cultural backgrounds. Although we focus on overcoming language barriers rather than cultural barriers, this remains an important consideration.
