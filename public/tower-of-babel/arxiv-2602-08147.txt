Lyapunov Exponents for Sparsely Coupled Linear Cocycles
Reza Rastegar

Abstract
This paper studies structured products of real matrices for which the top Lyapunov exponent can be accessed by reducing the dynamics to an amenable generalization of upper triangular matrices. Exploiting prescribed zero patterns (including block-triangularity and sparse decompositions, conveniently encoded by a directed sparsity graph), we obtain explicit, computable bounds and, in favorable cases, formulas for γ 1 \gamma_{1} by combining deterministic triangular controls with a suitable refinement of the Furstenberg–Kifer lemma [ 17 ] for block-triangular products. The estimates apply both to tempered (possibly deterministic) sequences and to stationary ergodic random cocycles under standard integrability. We also discuss applications to perturbation models for linear systems, including low-rank updates, where the reduction converts the problem to lower-dimensional or scalar cocycles.

The framework covers both stationary/ergodic matrix sequences and deterministic sequences satisfying the temperedness condition (

For an integer
d\geq 2

{\mathcal{M}}_{d}

d\times d

\displaystyle\limsup_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\log\|X_{n}\|<\infty\qquad\mbox{\rm where}\qquad X_{n}:=A_{n}\cdots A_{1}.

(1)

We associate to
{\mathcal{A}}

\displaystyle\gamma(v):=\limsup_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\log\|X_{n}v\|.

(2)

In particular, we define the

\displaystyle\gamma_{1}({\mathcal{A}}):=\limsup_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\log\|X_{n}\|=\sup_{\|v\|=1}\gamma(v).

A particularly clean structure emerges when

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\left(\log^{+}\|A_{1}\|\right)<\infty,

Proposition
2.1
makes precise a common heuristic: for an upper triangular product, the diagonal controls the exponential growth, while off-diagonal terms can only create
transient
amplification.
The quantities
\alpha_{i}^{\pm}
record the asymptotic growth rates of the diagonal cocycles along each coordinate.
When the diagonal averages converge (so
\alpha_{i}^{+}=\alpha_{i}^{-}
), Corollary
2.2
recovers the clean identity
\gamma_{1}=\max_{i}\alpha_{i}
.
When they do not, the additional correction term
\sum_{r<j}(\alpha_{r}^{+}-\alpha_{r}^{-})
measures the maximal “spread” between limsup and liminf along earlier coordinates; this is exactly the amount of slack needed to control how often off-diagonal couplings can amplify the
j
th diagonal growth.
In the stationary ergodic case the spread vanishes almost surely, so the proposition can be viewed as a deterministic envelope around the classical result.

to perturbations of linear systems. These perturbations represent an important class of matrix transformations with wide-ranging applications in science and engineering.

Our first result concerns the product of random rank-one perturbations of a specific form. This proposition can also be viewed somewhat as a generalization of Theorem 5.1 in

Proposition 3.1
.

Let
(u_{n},\eta_{n})_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}
be a random, stationary, and ergodic sequence, where
u_{n}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
and
\eta_{n}\in{\mathbb{R}}
. Suppose
v\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
is a fixed vector such that

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\bigl(\log|\eta_{1}|\bigr)\leq{\mathbb{E}}\bigl(\log|\eta_{1}+v^{T}u_{1}|\bigr)<\infty.

(17)

(a)

Let
{\mathcal{A}}:=(A_{n})_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}
with
A_{n}:=\eta_{n}I_{d}+u_{n}v^{T}
satisfying (
4
). Then

\displaystyle\gamma_{1}({\mathcal{A}})={\mathbb{E}}\bigl(\log|\eta_{1}+v^{T}u_{1}|\bigr)\quad\text{and}\quad\gamma_{r}({\mathcal{A}})={\mathbb{E}}\bigl(\log|\eta_{1}|\bigr),\quad r>1.

(18)

(b)

Suppose
A\in{\mathcal{M}}_{d}
is a non-singular matrix. Let
{\mathcal{A}}:=(A_{n})_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}
with
A_{n}:=\eta_{n}A+u_{n}v^{T}
satisfying condition (
4
), and

\displaystyle P\bigl(Au_{1}v^{T}=u_{1}v^{T}A\bigr)=1.

(19)

Then,

\displaystyle-\log\rho(A^{-1})\leq\gamma_{1}({\mathcal{A}})-{\mathbb{E}}\bigl(\log|\eta_{1}+v^{T}A^{-1}u_{1}|\bigr)\leq\log\rho(A),

where
\rho(A)
denotes the spectral radius of the matrix
A
.

We note that, under mild conditions on the sequence
(\eta_{n},u_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
, the inequality (
17
) can be relaxed, since the result may be applied directly to the inverse cocycle
{\mathcal{A}}^{-1}:=(A_{n}^{-1})_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}
, with part (a) adjusted accordingly.

Remark 3.2

(Varying right vector: two extendable regimes)
.

Consider
A_{n}=\eta_{n}I+u_{n}v_{n}^{\top}
.

(i) If
v_{n}=c_{n}v
for a fixed
v\neq 0
, then
A_{n}=\eta_{n}I+\tilde{u}_{n}v^{\top}
with

\tilde{u}_{n}:=c_{n}u_{n}
. Hence Proposition
3.1
applies verbatim (under the
corresponding integrability for
\tilde{u}_{n}
), yielding
d-1
Lyapunov exponents equal to

{\mathbb{E}}\log|\eta_{1}|
and the remaining exponent
{\mathbb{E}}\log|\eta_{1}+c_{1}v^{\top}u_{1}|
.

(ii) More generally, if
v_{n}\in V
a.s. for a fixed deterministic subspace
V\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{d}

of dimension
r
, then
H:=V^{\perp}
is a common invariant subspace and

A_{n}|_{H}=\eta_{n}\,\mathrm{Id}
. Consequently

is a Lyapunov exponent of

. The remaining

exponents are those of the induced cocycle
on the quotient

Nevertheless, the determinant identity implies

\sum_{i=1}^{d}\gamma_{i}=(d-1){\mathbb{E}}\log|\eta_{1}|+{\mathbb{E}}\log|\eta_{1}+v_{1}^{\top}u_{1}|.

Proposition 3.3
.

Fix
d,m\in{\mathbb{N}}
, and let
{\mathcal{N}}:=(N_{n})_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}
be a random, stationary, and ergodic sequence of matrices

\displaystyle N_{n}:=\begin{bmatrix}A_{n}&*\\
O&\eta_{n}I_{m}\end{bmatrix},

where
\eta_{n}\in{\mathbb{R}}
and
A_{n}\in{\mathcal{M}}_{d}
. Assume that
{\mathcal{N}}
satisfies (
4
) (i.e.
{\mathbb{E}}(\log^{+}\|N_{1}\|)<\infty
), that
A_{1}
is almost surely invertible with
{\mathbb{E}}(\log^{+}\|A_{1}^{-1}\|)<\infty
, and that
\eta_{1}\neq 0
almost surely with
{\mathbb{E}}(\log^{+}|\eta_{1}|^{-1})<\infty
. Set
{\mathcal{A}}:=(A_{n})_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}
. Then

\displaystyle\gamma_{1}({\mathcal{N}})=\max\bigl({\mathbb{E}}(\log|\eta_{1}|),\,\gamma_{1}({\mathcal{A}})\bigr),

and for any
2\leq r\leq\min(m,d)
,

\displaystyle\gamma_{r}({\mathcal{N}})=\max_{0\leq\ell\leq r}\left(\sum_{s=1}^{\ell}\gamma_{s}({\mathcal{A}})+(r-\ell){\mathbb{E}}(\log|\eta_{1}|)\right)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad-\max_{0\leq\ell\leq r-1}\left(\sum_{s=1}^{\ell}\gamma_{s}({\mathcal{A}})+(r-\ell){\mathbb{E}}(\log|\eta_{1}|)\right).

Proof.

Recall that a minor of size
r
is associated to ordered sequences of indices
J
and
L
of length
r
, defined as the determinant of the submatrix formed by selecting the rows indexed by
J
and the columns indexed by
L
.
We write
[N]:=\{1,\dots,N\}
and, for
0\leq r\leq N
, we let

[N]_{r}:=\{(i_{1},\dots,i_{r}):1\leq i_{1}<\cdots<i_{r}\leq N\}
; we freely identify such increasing
r
–tuples with the underlying
r
–element subsets.
For
B\subset[m]
we write
B+d:=\{b+d:b\in B\}\subset\{d+1,\dots,d+m\}
, and for
J_{2},L_{2}\subset\{d+1,\dots,d+m\}
we interpret

I_{m}[J_{2},L_{2}]:=I_{m}[J_{2}-d,\,L_{2}-d]
.
For any matrix
A
, let
H_{r}(A)
denote the
r
-th compound matrix of
A
, whose entries are all minors of size
r
. Set
B_{n}^{(r)}:=H_{r}(N_{n})
, and let
{\mathcal{B}}_{r}:=(B_{n}^{(r)})_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}
be the sequence of
r
-th compound matrices in
{\mathcal{N}}
.

Recall that by the discussion in p. 130 of
[
5
, Section 3.2]
, we write

\displaystyle\gamma_{1}({\mathcal{B}}_{r})=\sum_{i=1}^{r}\gamma_{i}({\mathcal{N}})\quad\text{or equivalently}\quad\gamma_{r}({\mathcal{N}})=\begin{cases}\gamma_{1}({\mathcal{B}}_{r})-\gamma_{1}({\mathcal{B}}_{r-1})&r\geq 2,\\[4.0pt]
\gamma_{1}({\mathcal{B}}_{1})&r=1.\end{cases}

(20)

Observe that
B_{n}^{(r)}
is also a block upper triangular matrix, and any “diagonal” minor of
N_{n}
can be written as the product of a minor of
\eta_{n}I_{m}
and a minor of
A_{n}
. To formalize this, we define
H_{0}(A)=I_{1}
. For any two subsets
J,L\subset[m]
of the same size, the
(J,L)
-th minor of
\eta_{n}I_{m}
is zero if
J\neq L
, and equals
\eta_{n}^{|J|}
otherwise. Let
J,L\in[m+d]_{r}
be such that
J
and
L
can be written as
J_{1}\cup J_{2}
and
L_{1}\cup L_{2}
, respectively, with
J_{1},L_{1}\in[d]_{\alpha}
and
J_{2},L_{2}\in\{B+d\mid B\in[m]_{\beta}\}
for some
\alpha+\beta=r
. Then,

\displaystyle\det N_{n}[J,L]=\eta_{n}^{\beta}\det A_{n}[J_{1},L_{1}]\det I_{m}[J_{2}-d,L_{2}-d].

Then, the observation is that for
2\leq r\leq\min(m,d)
,

\displaystyle B_{n}^{(r)}=\begin{bmatrix}H_{r}(\eta_{n}I_{m})\otimes H_{0}(A_{n})&*&*&\cdots&*\\
O&H_{r-1}(\eta_{n}I_{m})\otimes H_{1}(A_{n})&*&\cdots&*\\
O&O&\cdots&\cdots&*\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots&\vdots\\
O&O&\cdots&O&H_{0}(\eta_{n}I_{m})\otimes H_{r}(A_{n})\end{bmatrix},

where
\otimes
denotes the tensor product.
Therefore, Lemma
2.3
yields

\displaystyle\gamma_{1}({\mathcal{B}}_{r})=\max_{0\leq\ell\leq r}\gamma_{1}\bigl(\bigl(\eta_{n}^{r-\ell}H_{\ell}(A_{n})\bigr)_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}\bigr)=\max_{0\leq\ell\leq r}\Bigl(\sum_{s=1}^{\ell}\gamma_{s}({\mathcal{A}})+(r-\ell){\mathbb{E}}(\log|\eta_{1}|)\Bigr).

Hence, by (
20
), the proof follows.
∎

Proof of Proposition
3.1
.

We begin with the proof of (a). If
v=0
, then
A_{n}=\eta_{n}I_{d}
and the conclusion is immediate. Assume
v\neq 0
.
Choose
w\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
with
v^{T}w=1
and extend a basis of
H:=\ker(v^{T})
to a basis of
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
by adding
w
as the last vector.
Let

be the corresponding change-of-basis matrix, so that in the new coordinates
v^{T}x
is the last coordinate of

) and

18

\tilde{\mathcal{A}}:=(\tilde{A}_{n})_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}
with
\tilde{A}_{n}:=\eta_{n}I_{d}+A^{-1}u_{n}v^{T}
. Note that
A_{n}=A\tilde{A}_{n}

\displaystyle X_{n}=A(\eta_{n}I_{d}+A^{-1}u_{n}v^{T})\cdots A(\eta_{1}I_{d}+A^{-1}u_{1}v^{T})=A^{n}Y_{n},

(21)

where

\displaystyle\gamma_{1}({\mathcal{A}})

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\lim\frac{1}{n}\log\|A^{n}\|+\gamma_{1}(\tilde{\mathcal{A}})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\log\rho(A)+{\mathbb{E}}\bigl(\log|\eta_{1}+v^{T}A^{-1}u_{1}|\bigr),

Next, from (

\displaystyle\|Y_{n}\|\leq\|X_{n}\|\,\|A^{-n}\|.

Taking logarithms in the previous inequality, dividing by

\displaystyle\gamma_{1}({\mathcal{A}})+\log\rho(A^{-1})\geq{\mathbb{E}}\bigl(\log|\eta_{1}+v^{T}A^{-1}u_{1}|\bigr).

Proposition
3.1
illustrates a general mechanism: a low-rank perturbation often preserves a large invariant subspace, and the cocycle induced on the corresponding quotient captures the remaining Lyapunov exponent(s).
In our setting the hyperplane
H=\ker(v^{T})
is invariant and
A_{n}|_{H}=\eta_{n}I
, while the induced one-dimensional cocycle on
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}/H
is multiplication by
\eta_{n}+v^{T}u_{n}
(as witnessed by the identity
v^{T}A_{n}=(\eta_{n}+v^{T}u_{n})v^{T}
).
Equivalently, after a fixed change of basis that sends
v^{T}x
to the last coordinate, each
A_{n}
becomes block upper triangular with diagonal blocks
\eta_{n}I_{d-1}
and
\eta_{n}+v^{T}u_{n}
.

This viewpoint is useful beyond rank one: when a cocycle preserves a filtration of subspaces, the Lyapunov spectrum is controlled by the induced cocycles on the successive quotients (cf. Lemma
2.3
and Proposition
3.3
).

2\times 2

Lemma 3.4
.

For a given
m\in{\mathbb{N}}
and
V\in{\mathcal{M}}_{d\times m}
, let
(U_{n},\eta_{n})_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}
be a random, stationary, and ergodic sequence, where
U_{n}\in{\mathcal{M}}_{d\times m}
and
\eta_{n}\in{\mathbb{R}}
. Define
{\mathcal{A}}:=(A_{n})_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}
and
\tilde{\mathcal{A}}:=(\tilde{A}_{n})_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}
, with

A_{n}:=\eta_{n}I_{d}+U_{n}V^{T}\quad\text{and}\quad\tilde{A}_{n}:=\eta_{n}I_{m}+V^{T}U_{n}.

Then

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{d}\gamma_{i}({\mathcal{A}})-\sum_{i=1}^{m}\gamma_{i}(\tilde{\mathcal{A}})=(d-m){\mathbb{E}}(\log|\eta_{1}|).

(24)

Moreover, if
\gamma_{1}({\mathcal{A}})\geq{\mathbb{E}}(\log|\eta_{1}|)
, then
\gamma_{1}({\mathcal{A}})=\gamma_{1}(\tilde{\mathcal{A}})
. Similarly, if for some
2\leq r\leq\min(m,d)
we have
\gamma_{r}({\mathcal{A}})\geq{\mathbb{E}}(\log|\eta_{1}|)
, then
\gamma_{r}({\mathcal{A}})=\gamma_{r}(\tilde{\mathcal{A}})
.

Proof.

Consider the following square block matrices:

R:=\begin{bmatrix}I_{d}&O\\
V^{T}&I_{m}\end{bmatrix},\quad R^{-1}=\begin{bmatrix}I_{d}&O\\
-V^{T}&I_{m}\end{bmatrix},\qquad\text{and}\qquad N_{n}=\begin{bmatrix}A_{n}&U_{n}\\
O&\eta_{n}I_{m}\end{bmatrix}.

The proof of the lemma relies on the following identity:

\displaystyle\tilde{N}_{n}:=RN_{n}R^{-1}=\begin{bmatrix}\eta_{n}I_{d}&V\\
O&\tilde{A}_{n}\end{bmatrix}.

(25)

Recalling (
1
) and using (
25
), we may write

\prod_{i=1}^{n}N_{i}=\begin{bmatrix}X_{n}&*\\
O&\prod_{i=1}^{n}\eta_{i}I_{m}\end{bmatrix}=R^{-1}\begin{bmatrix}\prod_{i=1}^{n}\eta_{i}I_{d}&*\\
0&\tilde{X}_{n}\end{bmatrix}R=R^{-1}\bigl(\prod_{i=1}^{n}\tilde{N}_{i}\bigr)R,

where
*
denotes unspecified blocks. Let
{\mathcal{N}}:=(N_{n})_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}
and
\tilde{\mathcal{N}}:=(\tilde{N}_{n})_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}
. Observe that by the Oseledets theorem,
\gamma_{i}({\mathcal{N}})
and
\gamma_{i}(\tilde{\mathcal{N}})
s exist and

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{d}\gamma_{i}({\mathcal{A}})+m{\mathbb{E}}(\log|\eta_{1}|)

\displaystyle={\mathbb{E}}\bigl(\log|\det(A_{1})|\bigr)+m{\mathbb{E}}(\log|\eta_{1}|)

\displaystyle={\mathbb{E}}\bigl(\log|\det(N_{1})|\bigr)=\sum_{i=1}^{d+m}\gamma_{i}({\mathcal{N}})=\sum_{i=1}^{d+m}\gamma_{i}(\tilde{\mathcal{N}})

\displaystyle={\mathbb{E}}\bigl(\log|\det(\tilde{N}_{1})|\bigr)={\mathbb{E}}\bigl(\log|\det(\tilde{A}_{1})|\bigr)+d{\mathbb{E}}(\log|\eta_{1}|)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{m}\gamma_{i}(\tilde{\mathcal{A}})+d{\mathbb{E}}(\log|\eta_{1}|).

Rearranging yields (
24
).

Next,
\gamma_{1}({\mathcal{N}})
and
\gamma_{1}(\tilde{\mathcal{N}})
both exist and are equal. Hence, by Lemma
2.3
,

\displaystyle\max\bigl(\gamma_{1}({\mathcal{A}}),{\mathbb{E}}(\log|\eta_{1}|)\bigr)=\max\bigl(\gamma_{1}(\tilde{\mathcal{A}}),{\mathbb{E}}(\log|\eta_{1}|)\bigr).

(26)

If
\gamma_{1}({\mathcal{A}})\geq{\mathbb{E}}(\log|\eta_{1}|)
, then (
26
) implies
\gamma_{1}({\mathcal{A}})=\gamma_{1}(\tilde{\mathcal{A}})
.

Similarly, by Proposition
3.3
, for any
2\leq r\leq\min(m,d)
, one can show inductively that

\max_{0\leq\ell\leq r}\Bigl(\sum_{s=1}^{\ell}\gamma_{s}({\mathcal{A}})+(r-\ell){\mathbb{E}}(\log|\eta_{1}|)\Bigr)=\max_{0\leq\ell\leq r}\Bigl(\sum_{s=1}^{\ell}\gamma_{s}(\tilde{\mathcal{A}})+(r-\ell){\mathbb{E}}(\log|\eta_{1}|)\Bigr).

This yields the result under the assumption
\gamma_{1}({\mathcal{A}})\geq\cdots\geq\gamma_{r}({\mathcal{A}})\geq{\mathbb{E}}(\log|\eta_{1}|)
.
∎

Proposition 3.5
.

Fix
m,d\in\mathbb{N}
, a matrix
V\in\mathcal{M}_{d\times m}(\mathbb{R})
of rank
m
, and an invertible
matrix
A\in\mathcal{M}_{d\times d}(\mathbb{R})
.
Let
(U_{n},\eta_{n})_{n\geq 1}
be a random stationary ergodic sequence with

U_{n}\in\mathcal{M}_{d\times m}(\mathbb{R})
and
\eta_{n}\in\mathbb{R}
, and define

A_{n}:=\eta_{n}A+U_{n}V^{T},\qquad\widetilde{A}_{n}:=\eta_{n}I_{m}+V^{T}A^{-1}U_{n}.

Assume

\mathbb{P}(AU_{1}V^{T}=U_{1}V^{T}A)=1.

Then, writing
{\mathcal{A}}=(A_{n})
and
\widetilde{\mathcal{A}}=(\widetilde{A}_{n})
,

-\log\rho(A^{-1})+\max\!\Big(\mathbb{E}\log|\eta_{1}|,\,\gamma_{1}(\widetilde{\mathcal{A}})\Big)\;\leq\;\gamma_{1}({\mathcal{A}})\;\leq\;\log\rho(A)+\max\!\Big(\mathbb{E}\log|\eta_{1}|,\,\gamma_{1}(\widetilde{\mathcal{A}})\Big).

Proof.

Define

\widehat{A}_{n}:=\eta_{n}I_{d}+A^{-1}U_{n}V^{T}.

By the commutation assumption,
A
commutes with
\widehat{A}_{n}
for every
n
, and hence

X_{n}:=A_{n}\cdots A_{1}=A^{n}\widehat{X}_{n},\qquad\text{where }\widehat{X}_{n}:=\widehat{A}_{n}\cdots\widehat{A}_{1}.

Therefore, by submultiplicativity,

\|X_{n}\|\leq\|A^{n}\|\,\|\widehat{X}_{n}\|\quad\text{and}\quad\|\widehat{X}_{n}\|=\|A^{-n}X_{n}\|\leq\|A^{-n}\|\,\|X_{n}\|.

Taking
\frac{1}{n}\log
and letting
n\to\infty
, and using Gelfand’s formula

\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\log\|A^{\pm n}\|=\log\rho(A^{\pm 1})
, gives

-\log\rho(A^{-1})+\gamma_{1}(\widehat{\mathcal{A}})\leq\gamma_{1}({\mathcal{A}})\leq\log\rho(A)+\gamma_{1}(\widehat{\mathcal{A}}).

It remains to identify
\gamma_{1}(\widehat{\mathcal{A}})
.
Let
K:=\ker(V^{T})
, which has dimension
d-m
(since
\mathrm{rank}(V)=m
). For
x\in K
,

V^{T}x=0
and hence
\widehat{A}_{n}x=\eta_{n}x
, so vectors in
K
grow at rate
\mathbb{E}\log|\eta_{1}|
.

On the quotient space
\mathbb{R}^{d}/K
, the induced action is computed via the surjective map

q(x)=V^{T}x
:

q(\widehat{A}_{n}x)=V^{T}(\eta_{n}x+A^{-1}U_{n}V^{T}x)=\bigl(\eta_{n}I_{m}+V^{T}A^{-1}U_{n}\bigr)\,q(x)=\widetilde{A}_{n}\,q(x).

Thus the cocycle induced on the quotient is precisely
\widetilde{\mathcal{A}}
.
By the block/extension Furstenberg–Kifer lemma (Lemma
2.3
),

\gamma_{1}(\widehat{\mathcal{A}})=\max\big(\mathbb{E}\log|\eta_{1}|,\gamma_{1}(\widetilde{\mathcal{A}})\big).

Substituting into the earlier inequality yields the claimed bounds.
∎

We conclude these notes by presenting a generalization of Lemma

For the purpose of this section, let
\mathcal{A}:=(A_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
be a random, stationary, and ergodic sequence of matrices as in the statement of Lemma
2.3
, and write each matrix
A_{n}
as

A_{n}:=A_{n,1}+A_{n,2},

where
A_{n,1}
denotes the block diagonal part and
A_{n,2}
the remaining strictly upper triangular component of
A_{n}
.

Norm convention.
Throughout Section
4
we take
\|\cdot\|
to be the operator norm induced by the vector
\ell^{1}
-norm (maximum absolute column sum); we write
\|\cdot\|_{1}
when clarity is helpful. By equivalence of norms on the finite-dimensional space
\mathcal{M}_{d}
, this choice does not change the Lyapunov exponents and (
4
) holds for
\|\cdot\|
if and only if it holds for
\|\cdot\|_{1}
. Then

X_{n}=A_{n,1}\cdots A_{1,1}+\sum_{j=1}^{n}\sum_{1\leq i_{1}<\cdots<i_{j}\leq n}A_{n,1}\cdots A_{i_{j}+1,1}\,A_{i_{j},2}\,A_{i_{j}-1,1}\cdots A_{i_{j-1}+1,1}\,A_{i_{j-1},2}\,\cdots.

It is straightforward to verify that multiplying a block diagonal matrix by a strictly block upper triangular matrix (with the same block sizes) produces a strictly block upper triangular matrix. Moreover, multiplying two strictly upper triangular matrices yields another strictly upper triangular matrix with fewer nonzero superdiagonals—a property reminiscent of the nilpotency of strictly upper triangular matrices. Consequently, all products with
j>d
vanish, and therefore

X_{n}=A_{n,1}\cdots A_{1,1}+\sum_{j=1}^{d}\sum_{1\leq i_{1}<\cdots<i_{j}\leq n}A_{n,1}\cdots A_{i_{j}+1,1}\,A_{i_{j},2}\,A_{i_{j}-1,1}\cdots A_{i_{j-1}+1,1}\,A_{i_{j-1},2}\,\cdots.

Fix
\varepsilon>0
. We will use the following standard Borel–Cantelli estimate (applied to
Y_{n}=\log^{+}\|A_{n,2}\|_{1}
).

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
(Y_{n})_{n\geq 1}
be a stationary sequence of nonnegative random variables with
{\mathbb{E}}(Y_{1})<\infty
. Then for every
\varepsilon>0
,

\sum_{n\geq 1}{\mathbb{P}}(Y_{n}>\varepsilon n)<\infty,\qquad\text{and hence}\qquad{\mathbb{P}}(Y_{n}>\varepsilon n\ \text{\rm i.o.})=0.

Proof.

By stationarity,
{\mathbb{P}}(Y_{n}>\varepsilon n)={\mathbb{P}}(Y_{1}>\varepsilon n)
. Using the tail-integral identity

{\mathbb{E}}(Y_{1})=\int_{0}^{\infty}{\mathbb{P}}(Y_{1}>t)\,dt

and partitioning the integral into intervals of length
\varepsilon
gives

\sum_{n\geq 1}{\mathbb{P}}(Y_{1}>\varepsilon n)\leq\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\int_{0}^{\infty}{\mathbb{P}}(Y_{1}>t)\,dt=\frac{1}{\varepsilon}{\mathbb{E}}(Y_{1})<\infty.

The Borel–Cantelli lemma yields the claim.
∎

Apply Lemma
4.1
with
Y_{n}:=\log^{+}\|A_{n,2}\|_{1}
. Since
A_{1,2}
is obtained from
A_{1}
by zeroing some entries, the induced
\ell^{1}
operator norm is monotone, so
\|A_{1,2}\|_{1}\leq\|A_{1}\|_{1}
and hence
{\mathbb{E}}\log^{+}\|A_{1,2}\|_{1}<\infty
by (
4
) (equivalently for
\|\cdot\|_{1}
). Therefore,

{\mathbb{P}}\!\left(\log^{+}\|A_{n,2}\|_{1}>\varepsilon n\ \text{\rm i.o.}\right)=0.

In particular, almost surely there exists a (random)
N_{\varepsilon}<\infty
such that
\|A_{n,2}\|_{1}\leq e^{\varepsilon n}
for all
n\geq N_{\varepsilon}
. Setting

C_{\varepsilon}:=\max\Bigl\{1,\ \max_{1\leq n\leq N_{\varepsilon}}\|A_{n,2}\|_{1}e^{-\varepsilon n}\Bigr\},

we obtain the uniform bound

\|A_{n,2}\|_{1}\leq C_{\varepsilon}e^{\varepsilon n}\qquad\text{for all }n\geq 1.

Consequently, using the expansion of
X_{n}
above and the fact that any nonzero monomial contains at most
d-1
factors from the strictly upper-triangular part
(A_{k,2})
, we obtain that for all sufficiently large
n
, almost surely,

\|X_{n}\|_{1}\leq C_{\varepsilon}^{\,d-1}\,n^{d}\,e^{(d-1)\varepsilon n}\,\max_{1\leq j<d}\ \max_{1\leq i_{1}<\cdots<i_{j}\leq n}\ \prod_{r=0}^{j}\big\|A_{i_{r}+1,1}\cdots A_{i_{r+1}-1,1}\big\|_{1},

(27)

where by convention
i_{0}:=0
and
i_{j+1}:=n+1
, and an empty product is interpreted as the identity matrix. (We used the crude bound
1+\sum_{j=1}^{d-1}\binom{n}{j}\leq n^{d}
on the number of summands in the expansion.)

Let
\mathcal{A}_{1}:=(A_{n,1})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
. Note that
A_{n,1}
is diagonal (hence block diagonal), so for any
1\leq i\leq j
,

\|A_{j,1}\cdots A_{i,1}\|_{1}=\max_{p\in[d]}\ \prod_{t=i}^{j}\big|(A_{t,1})_{pp}\big|.

For each
p\in[d]
, set

\beta_{p}:=\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\log\prod_{t=1}^{n}\big|(A_{t,1})_{pp}\big|\qquad\text{a.s.},\qquad\text{so that}\qquad\gamma_{1}(\mathcal{A}_{1})=\max_{p\in[d]}\beta_{p}.

Fix
\varepsilon>0
. Since
\frac{1}{n}\sum_{t=1}^{n}\log|(A_{t,1})_{pp}|\to\beta_{p}
almost surely, the random variables

G_{\varepsilon,p}^{+}:=\sup_{n\geq 1}\exp\!\Big(\sum_{t=1}^{n}\log|(A_{t,1})_{pp}|-(\beta_{p}+\varepsilon)n\Big),\qquad G_{\varepsilon,p}^{-}:=\sup_{n\geq 1}\exp\!\Big(-\sum_{t=1}^{n}\log|(A_{t,1})_{pp}|-(-\beta_{p}+\varepsilon)n\Big)

are almost surely finite, and therefore with
G_{\varepsilon,p}:=\max(G_{\varepsilon,p}^{+},G_{\varepsilon,p}^{-})
we have for all
n\geq 1
,

\prod_{t=1}^{n}\big|(A_{t,1})_{pp}\big|\leq G_{\varepsilon,p}\,e^{(\beta_{p}+\varepsilon)n},\qquad\prod_{t=1}^{n}\big|(A_{t,1})_{pp}\big|^{-1}\leq G_{\varepsilon,p}\,e^{(-\beta_{p}+\varepsilon)n}.

Consequently, for all
1\leq i\leq j
,

\displaystyle\prod_{t=i}^{j}\big|(A_{t,1})_{pp}\big|=\Big(\prod_{t=1}^{j}\big|(A_{t,1})_{pp}\big|\Big)\Big(\prod_{t=1}^{i-1}\big|(A_{t,1})_{pp}\big|\Big)^{-1}

\displaystyle\qquad\leq G_{\varepsilon,p}^{2}\,e^{\beta_{p}(j-i+1)}\,e^{\varepsilon(j+i-1)}\leq G_{\varepsilon,p}^{2}\,e^{\beta_{p}(j-i+1)}\,e^{2\varepsilon j}.

Taking the maximum over
p\in[d]
and setting
G_{\varepsilon}:=\max_{p\in[d]}G_{\varepsilon,p}
yields the uniform estimate

\|A_{j,1}\cdots A_{i,1}\|_{1}\leq G_{\varepsilon}^{2}\,e^{\gamma_{1}(\mathcal{A}_{1})(j-i+1)}\,e^{2\varepsilon j}\qquad\text{for all }1\leq i\leq j.

(28)

Applying (
28
) to each factor in (
27
) and using that each endpoint is at most
n
and that
j\leq d-1
gives

\|X_{n}\|_{1}\leq\widetilde{C}_{\varepsilon}\,n^{d}\,\exp\!\big((\gamma_{1}(\mathcal{A}_{1})+C\,\varepsilon)n\big),

for some deterministic constant
C=C(d)
and an almost surely finite (random) constant
\widetilde{C}_{\varepsilon}
.
Since
\varepsilon>0
is arbitrary, we conclude
\gamma_{1}(\mathcal{A})\leq\gamma_{1}(\mathcal{A}_{1})
.
Finally, for a diagonal (more generally, block-diagonal) cocycle, the top Lyapunov exponent is the maximum of the top Lyapunov exponents of its diagonal blocks, completing the proof.

We now introduce several definitions to abstract and extend the preceding argument to a broader class of matrices beyond the block triangular case.

Let
\wedge
denote the entrywise “AND” operation. Fix
d,k\in\mathbb{N}
with
k\leq d^{2}
.
A
shape set of order
d
, denoted
\mathcal{L}
, is a collection of nonzero, zero–one matrices of size
d\times d
,

\mathcal{L}:=\{L_{j}\mid j\in[k]\},

such that
L_{i}\wedge L_{j}=O_{d}
for all distinct
i,j\in[k]
.
For any matrix
A\in{\mathcal{M}}_{d}
, we define its
shape
, denoted
\mbox{\it Shape}{\left(A\right)}
, as the
d\times d
zero–one matrix whose
(i,j)
-th entry is one if
A(i,j)\neq 0
, and zero otherwise.

We next introduce the notion of an
\mathcal{L}
-shape graph

(\mathcal{V},\mathcal{T})_{\mathcal{L}}
, defined by its set of vertices
\mathcal{V}
and transition map
\mathcal{T}:\mathcal{V}\times\mathcal{L}\to\mathcal{V}
.
Given the shape set
\mathcal{L}
, define the set of vertices as

\mathcal{V}:=\Bigl\{\mbox{\it Shape}{\left(L_{n}\cdots L_{1}\right)}\,\big|\,n\in\mathbb{N},\ (L_{1},\ldots,L_{n})\in\mathcal{L}^{n}\Bigr\}.

(29)

Each vertex can be regarded as a zero–one matrix when necessary.
Two vertices
v_{1},v_{2}\in\mathcal{V}
are connected, written
v_{1}\to v_{2}
, if and only if there exists some
L\in\mathcal{L}
such that

v_{2}=\mathcal{T}(v_{1},L):=\mbox{\it Shape}{\left(Lv_{1}\right)}.

Finally, given any sequence of matrices
\mathcal{A}:=(A_{n})_{n\geq 0}
, we use the shape set
\mathcal{L}
to decompose each
A_{n}
into a sum of
k
“non-overlapping” matrices
\{A_{n,1},\ldots,A_{n,k}\}
such that

A_{n}=\sum_{j=1}^{k}A_{n,j},\quad\text{where}\quad\mbox{\it Shape}{\left(A_{n,j}\right)}\vee L_{j}=L_{j}.

(30)

With the notion of the shape graph established, our strategy is to exploit its structural properties to analyze products of matrices in
{\mathcal{A}}
, thereby obtaining meaningful bounds on the Lyapunov exponents of
{\mathcal{A}}
. The main result of this section is the following theorem.

Theorem 4.4
.

Let
{\mathcal{L}}
be a shape set of order
d
with
k
elements, and let
({\mathcal{V}},{\mathcal{T}})_{{\mathcal{L}}}
denote its corresponding shape graph. Assume that the shape graph contains only loops of length one (self-loops), and that each nonzero vertex has at most one self-loop. Let
{\mathcal{A}}=(A_{n})_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}
be a random, stationary, and ergodic sequence of
d\times d
matrices, each admitting a decomposition as in (
30
) with respect to the shape set
{\mathcal{L}}
. Assume further that:

(i)

For every
1\leq s\leq k
,

{\mathbb{E}}\big(\log^{+}\|A_{1,s}\|\big)<\infty.

(ii)

Let
{\mathcal{H}}\subset{\mathcal{V}}\setminus\{O_{d}\}
be the set of vertices with a self-loop, and let
{\mathcal{L}}_{{\mathcal{H}}}
be the subset of labels corresponding to their self-loops. For every
s\in{\mathcal{L}}_{\mathcal{H}}
, the matrix
A_{1,s}
is almost surely invertible, and

{\mathbb{E}}\big(\log^{+}\|A_{1,s}^{-1}\|\big)<\infty.

Define

\beta_{s}:=\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\log\left\|A_{n,s}A_{n-1,s}\cdots A_{1,s}\right\|,\quad\text{a.s.},

and set

\beta:=\max_{s\in{\mathcal{L}}_{\mathcal{H}}}\beta_{s}.

Then:

(a)

The top Lyapunov exponent satisfies

\gamma_{1}({\mathcal{A}})\leq\beta+\log k.

Moreover, if
O_{d}\in{\mathcal{V}}
, define

k_{*}:=\max_{v\in{\mathcal{V}}\setminus\{O_{d}\}}\#\bigl\{\,s\in[k]:{\mathcal{T}}(v,L_{s})\neq O_{d}\,\bigr\},

so that
k_{*}\leq k
and
k_{*}
is the maximal number of labels that keep a nonzero vertex from transitioning to
O_{d}
in one step. Then

\gamma_{1}({\mathcal{A}})\leq\beta+\log k_{*}.

(In particular, if every nonzero vertex has at least one outgoing label that transitions to
O_{d}
, then
k_{*}\leq k-1
.)

(b)

Let
{\mathcal{W}}\subset{\mathcal{H}}\setminus\{O_{d}\}
be the set of self-loop vertices
w
such that every directed path in the shape graph that ends at
w
avoids loop vertices other than
w
(equivalently, it does not pass through any vertex in
{\mathcal{H}}\setminus\{w\}
). Assume moreover that for each
w\in{\mathcal{W}}
, if
s
denotes the label of its self-loop, then there exists
r=r(w)\geq 1
such that
\mbox{\it Shape}{\left(L_{s}^{n}\right)}=w
for all
n\geq r
. If, for every
w\in{\mathcal{W}}
and every
v\in{\mathcal{V}}\setminus\{w\}
, we have

v\wedge w=O_{d},

and assume in addition that all matrices
A_{n,s}
are entrywise nonnegative almost surely (so that monomials with the same shape do not cancel entrywise). Then

\max_{s\in{\mathcal{L}}_{\mathcal{W}}}\beta_{s}\leq\gamma_{1}({\mathcal{A}}),

where
{\mathcal{L}}_{\mathcal{W}}
denotes the set of labels corresponding to the self-loops of vertices in
{\mathcal{W}}
.

4.1
Theorem
4.4
in practice

Theorem
4.4
is intended as a “plug-in” estimate for cocycles whose random matrices admit a sparse decomposition with
non-overlapping supports
. In many situations, the verification reduces to a finite, combinatorial check on the associated shape graph.
We record a short checklist.

1.

Choose a shape set.
Pick a shape set
{\mathcal{L}}=\{L_{1},\ldots,L_{k}\}
(disjoint supports:
L_{i}\wedge L_{j}=O_{d}
for
i\neq j
, where
O_{d}
is the
d\times d
zero matrix) that matches the sparsity types present in
A_{n}
.

2.

Build the shape graph.
Compute (or reason about) the vertex set
{\mathcal{V}}
in (
29
) and the transition map
{\mathcal{T}}(v,L)=\mbox{\it Shape}{\left(vL\right)}
. In practice,
{\mathcal{V}}
is generated by iterating the transitions starting from the labels.

3.

Check the acyclicity condition.
Verify that the directed graph contains no directed cycles other than self-loops, and that each nonzero vertex has at most one self-loop label. (In many common constructions,
L_{1}=I_{d}
is the unique self-loop label.)

4.

Identify the loop labels and compute
\beta
.
Let
{\mathcal{H}}
be the loop vertices and
{\mathcal{L}}_{{\mathcal{H}}}
their self-loop labels. Then
\beta=\max_{s\in{\mathcal{L}}_{{\mathcal{H}}}}\beta_{s}
, where
\beta_{s}
is the top Lyapunov exponent of the subcocycle
(A_{n,s})_{n\geq 1}
.

5.

Apply the bound.
Theorem
4.4
(a) gives
\gamma_{1}({\mathcal{A}})\leq\beta+\log k
, and if
O_{d}\in{\mathcal{V}}
the sharper
\gamma_{1}({\mathcal{A}})\leq\beta+\log k_{*}
.

We now give two worked illustrations, the first using the non-triangular example introduced above, and the second describing a general “DAG-supported” family where the
\log k
term has a transparent combinatorial meaning.

Example 4.5

(A worked bound for the non-triangular example (
33
))
.

Assume that
(A_{n})_{n\geq 1}
is i.i.d. and takes the first matrix in (
33
) with probability
p\in(0,1)
and the second with probability
1-p
. Use the shape set
{\mathcal{L}}=\{L_{1},L_{2},L_{3}\}
from (
34
), so that
k=3
, and note from Fig.
2
(a) that
O_{4}\in{\mathcal{V}}
and the shape graph has no directed cycles other than self-loops.

The only self-loop label is
L_{1}=I_{4}
, hence
{\mathcal{L}}_{{\mathcal{H}}}=\{1\}
and
\beta=\beta_{1}
. Writing
D_{n}:=A_{n,1}
for the diagonal component in the decomposition (
30
), we have that
D_{n}
is diagonal with entries distributed as

(D_{n}(1,1),D_{n}(2,2),D_{n}(3,3),D_{n}(4,4))=\begin{cases}(2,1,-1,-2),&\text{with prob. }p,\\
(1,2,3,4),&\text{with prob. }1-p.\end{cases}

For diagonal cocycles,
\beta_{1}
is explicit:

\beta_{1}=\max_{1\leq i\leq 4}\ {\mathbb{E}}\big(\log|D_{1}(i,i)|\big)=\max\Big\{p\log 2,\ (1-p)\log 2,\ (1-p)\log 3,\ p\log 2+(1-p)\log 4\Big\}.

Therefore Theorem
4.4
(a) yields the concrete estimate

\gamma_{1}({\mathcal{A}})\leq\beta_{1}+\log k_{*}=\beta_{1}+\log 2\qquad(\text{here }k_{*}=2).

This bound is nontrivial in regimes where the diagonal growth
\beta_{1}
is modest while the off-diagonal entries induce large transient amplification; the shape-graph estimate isolates the genuine exponential growth (captured by
\beta_{1}
) from the purely combinatorial proliferation of monomials (captured by
\log 2
).

Example 4.6

(DAG-supported sparsity patterns)
.

One convenient way to view this family is as a
random transfer-matrix
(or random kinetic-network) model on a directed acyclic network.
Let
x_{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a vector of nonnegative “masses” (or partial partition functions) and evolve it by

x_{n}=A_{n}x_{n-1},\qquad n\geq 1.

Then
\gamma_{1}({\mathcal{A}})
is the almost-sure exponential growth rate of
\|x_{n}\|
and may be interpreted as a
quenched free-energy density
.
A concrete parameterization is to take (for an “inverse temperature”
\theta>0
)

D_{n}=Diag\left(e^{-\theta E_{n,1}},\ldots,e^{-\theta E_{n,d}}\right),\qquad B_{n,e}=e^{-\theta U_{n,e}}\,E_{ij}\ \ \text{for }e=(i,j)\in E(G),

where
(E_{n,i})
and
(U_{n,e})
are stationary ergodic energy/edge-weight fields.
In this case the diagonal subcocycle is explicit and

\beta_{1}=\max_{1\leq i\leq d}\ {\mathbb{E}}\big(\log D_{1}(i,i)\big)=-\theta\min_{1\leq i\leq d}\ {\mathbb{E}}(E_{1,i}),

so Theorem
4.4
(a) bounds the quenched free energy by a simple “energy–entropy” upper bound (energy from
\beta_{1}
, entropy from the
\log|E(G)|
term).

To make this interpretation more concrete, note that the expansion of
X_{n}=A_{n}\cdots A_{1}
into monomials naturally enumerates
time-ordered paths
in the state space
[d]
.
Indeed, if we work in a basis where all entries are nonnegative (or simply take absolute values entrywise), then for each coordinate
j
one can view
(X_{n}\mbox{\bf 1})_{j}
as a sum over sequences

i_{0}\to i_{1}\to\cdots\to i_{n}=j

where each step either stays put (a diagonal choice from
D_{t}
) or follows an allowed directed edge of
G
(a choice from some
B_{t,e}
).
The weight of a path is a product of the corresponding random factors along the path, so the total mass is a partition-function-like sum of random path weights.
The top Lyapunov exponent
\gamma_{1}({\mathcal{A}})
is then the quenched exponential growth rate of this partition function.

Theorem
4.4
(a) can be read as follows: in an acyclic environment, the long-run exponential growth is controlled by the “best” loop (the diagonal cocycle, contributing
\beta_{1}
), while the off-diagonal moves contribute only through the number of admissible local choices.
The crude entropy term
\log|E(G)|
comes from bounding the number of admissible label sequences by
|E(G)|^{n}
; in many applications this can be sharpened by replacing
|E(G)|
with a smaller quantity such as the maximal out-degree or the spectral radius of an associated transition matrix.

Fix
d\geq 2
and let
G
be a directed acyclic graph (DAG) on vertex set
[d]
. For each directed edge
e=(i,j)\in E(G)
, let
L_{e}:=E_{ij}
be the
d\times d
matrix with a single
1
in entry
(i,j)
and zeros elsewhere, and let
L_{1}:=I_{d}
. Then

{\mathcal{L}}:=\{L_{1}\}\cup\{L_{e}:e\in E(G)\}

is a shape set (supports are disjoint), with
k=1+|E(G)|
. Consider any stationary ergodic cocycle
(A_{n})_{n\geq 1}
of the form

A_{n}=D_{n}+\sum_{e\in E(G)}B_{n,e},\qquad\mbox{\it Shape}{\left(D_{n}\right)}\vee L_{1}=L_{1},\ \ \mbox{\it Shape}{\left(B_{n,e}\right)}\vee L_{e}=L_{e},

where
D_{n}
is diagonal and each
B_{n,e}
is supported on the single entry prescribed by
e
. If
D_{n}
is a.s. invertible and
{\mathbb{E}}\log^{+}\|D_{1}^{\pm 1}\|<\infty
, and if
{\mathbb{E}}\log^{+}\|B_{1,e}\|<\infty
for all
e
, then Theorem
4.4
applies.

The DAG assumption implies that the
{\mathcal{L}}
-shape graph has no directed cycles other than the self-loops induced by
L_{1}
(products of edge labels correspond to directed paths, which cannot return in a DAG). Hence
{\mathcal{L}}_{{\mathcal{H}}}=\{1\}
and
\beta=\beta_{1}
, where

\beta_{1}=\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\log\|D_{n}D_{n-1}\cdots D_{1}\|=\max_{1\leq i\leq d}\ \lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{t=1}^{n}\log|D_{t}(i,i)|\qquad\text{a.s.}

If moreover
O_{d}\in{\mathcal{V}}
(this holds, for instance, whenever
G
has no directed path of length
d
), then Theorem
4.4
(a) gives

\gamma_{1}({\mathcal{A}})\leq\beta_{1}+\log k_{*}=\beta_{1}+\log|E(G)|\qquad(\text{here }k_{*}=|E(G)|).

In this family the additive term
\log|E(G)|
may be interpreted as a coarse “branching entropy” that accounts for the number of allowed sparsity types at each multiplication step, while
\beta_{1}
captures the genuine exponential growth coming from the diagonal subcocycle.

Remark 4.7
.

The proof of Theorem
4.4
(a) expands
X_{n}
into monomials indexed by label sequences in
\{1,\ldots,k\}^{n}
and then applies a union bound over the
k^{n}
terms.
This is where the additive
\log k
(or
\log k_{*}
) originates.
In applications one can often sharpen this step:

•

If only a subset of labels are ever reachable after a few steps (equivalently, the shape graph quickly collapses), one can replace
k
by the effective number of labels that actually contribute.

•

In “graph-like” constructions, one can bound the number of admissible label sequences in the expansion underlying part (a) by the number of directed walks in an auxiliary transition graph (equivalently, by the number of directed paths in the shape graph).
If
M
is the adjacency matrix of that transition graph, then the number of length-
n
walks is at most
\mathbf{1}^{\top}M^{n-1}\mathbf{1}\leq C\,\rho(M)^{\,n}
, where
\rho(M)
is the spectral radius.
Thus the additive entropy term can often be improved from
\log k
to
\log\rho(M)
(or more crudely to
\log\Delta^{+}
, where
\Delta^{+}
is the maximal out-degree).
In particular, if the transition graph is acyclic, this count is subexponential and the limiting “entropy” contribution vanishes.

We do not pursue these refinements here, but the shape-graph viewpoint makes them natural.

4.2
Proof of Theorem
4.4

The acyclicity assumption implies that every time-ordered monomial either (i) stays within a self-loop vertex for long stretches (hence inherits the exponential rate
\beta
from the corresponding loop cocycle) or (ii) eventually dies by hitting
O_{d}
.
Since non-loop transitions cannot repeat indefinitely, their contribution is at most subexponential (controlled using the
{\mathbb{E}}\log^{+}\|A_{1,s}\|
hypotheses).
The only remaining exponential cost is the number of distinct nonzero monomials, which is bounded by
k_{\rm eff}^{n}
; when
O_{d}
is reachable one can take
k_{\rm eff}=k_{*}
as in Theorem
4.4
, yielding the corresponding additive
\log k_{\rm eff}
term.

We now record the key uniform estimate needed in the proof of part (a).

Lemma 4.8

(Uniform bound for nonzero monomials)
.

Assume the hypotheses of Theorem
4.4
. Then for every
\varepsilon>0
there exists an almost surely finite random constant
K_{\varepsilon}
such that for all
n\geq 1
and every multi-index
(i_{1},\ldots,i_{n})\in[k]^{n}
for which the monomial

A_{1,n}(i_{1},\ldots,i_{n}):=A_{n,i_{n}}A_{n-1,i_{n-1}}\cdots A_{1,i_{1}}

is nonzero, we have

\|A_{1,n}(i_{1},\ldots,i_{n})\|_{1}\leq K_{\varepsilon}\,\exp\big((\beta+\varepsilon)n\big).

Proof.

Fix
\varepsilon>0
and set
m:=|{\mathcal{V}}|
. Let
\delta:=\varepsilon/(4m)
.

Define the stationary sequence

Y_{n}:=\max_{1\leq j\leq k}\log^{+}\|A_{n,j}\|_{1}.

Assumption (i) implies
{\mathbb{E}}(Y_{1})<\infty
. By Lemma
4.1
(applied to
(Y_{n})

such that for all

(Indeed the above bound holds for all sufficiently large

Fix a self-loop label

(A_{n,s})_{n\geq 1}
is stationary and ergodic, and
A_{n,s}
is almost surely invertible with

{\mathbb{E}}\log^{+}\|A_{1,s}\|_{1}+{\mathbb{E}}\log^{+}\|A_{1,s}^{-1}\|_{1}<\infty

Realize this stationary process as a linear cocycle over an ergodic measure-preserving transformation

(so that
A_{n,s}(\omega)=A_{1,s}(\theta^{n-1}\omega)
).

By Oseledets’ multiplicative ergodic theorem for integrable linear cocycles (see e.g.
[

\delta>0
there exists a measurable
tempered

\widetilde{K}_{s,\delta}(\omega)<\infty
such that for all
\ell\geq 1

\big\|A_{\ell,s}(\omega)\cdots A_{1,s}(\omega)\big\|_{1}\leq\widetilde{K}_{s,\delta}(\omega)\,e^{(\beta_{s}+\delta)\ell}.

Here “tempered” means that
\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\log\widetilde{K}_{s,\delta}(\theta^{n}\omega)=0
almost surely.
In particular,

Then for all

\Big\|A_{t+\ell-1,s}A_{t+\ell-2,s}\cdots A_{t,s}\Big\|_{1}\leq K_{s,\delta}(\omega)\,e^{\delta t}\,e^{(\beta_{s}+\delta)\ell}.

(38)

Let

is finite.

Fix

The associated walk on the shape graph visits at most

m

be the lengths of the self-loop blocks (so
\sum_{q=1}^{r}\ell_{q}\leq n

Using (

5
Discussion and outlook

We close with a brief discussion highlighting how the shape-graph bounds should be interpreted and where one might seek refinements.

Energy–entropy structure and regimes.

In the acyclic settings covered by Theorem
4.4
(a), the upper bound

\gamma_{1}({\mathcal{A}})\leq\beta+\log k\qquad(\text{or }\beta+\log k_{*}\text{ when }O_{d}\in{\mathcal{V}})

splits into two conceptually distinct pieces.
The term
\beta
is an “energy” contribution: it is the top Lyapunov exponent of the dominant loop cocycle(s), and it captures genuine exponential growth along directions that can persist indefinitely.
The logarithmic term is an “entropy” contribution that accounts for the number of admissible monomials in the product expansion.
In transfer-matrix language (Example above),
\beta
corresponds to the best long-run local weight accumulation, while the entropy term corresponds to the proliferation of admissible time-ordered paths.

In many models the entropy term is deliberately crude: if the off-diagonal components are small or rare, then
\gamma_{1}({\mathcal{A}})
is expected to be close to
\beta
, and the theorem quantifies that the discrepancy cannot exceed a logarithmic combinatorial correction.
Conversely, when many off-diagonal components are simultaneously active and comparable in size to the loop components, the entropy term indicates that purely combinatorial proliferation can shift the top exponent by an
O(1)
amount.

Possible refinements of the entropy term.

As emphasized in Remark
4.7
, the
\log k
term comes from counting
all
label sequences.
A natural refinement is to replace
k
by an effective growth factor associated with the shape graph itself (e.g. a spectral radius, or a maximal branching factor after reachability constraints are enforced).
Such refinements would make the bound sensitive to the geometry of the sparsity pattern, not only to the number of labels.

Beyond acyclic shape graphs.

The self-loop-only hypothesis in Theorem
4.4
is tailored to situations where feedback through off-diagonal transitions is structurally impossible (e.g. DAG-supported sparsity).
Allowing directed cycles would lead to genuinely new behavior: repeated off-diagonal transitions could create additional loop cocycles and may change the exponent in ways not captured by a single
\beta
.
One possible direction is to analyze strongly connected components of the shape graph and to combine the present methods with subadditive/Kingman-type arguments on the induced component graph.

Overlapping supports and cancellations.

A second limitation is the disjoint-support requirement in the definition of a shape set, which eliminates cancellations between distinct components.
Relaxing this assumption is delicate: cancellations can reduce growth and can also make the expansion-based counting argument too pessimistic.
It would be interesting to identify intermediate classes (e.g. sign-definite entries, monotone cones, or nonnegative matrices) where overlaps are allowed but the combinatorics remains tractable.

Practical use.

From a user’s perspective, the shape-graph method is most useful as a
diagnostic bound
:
given a structured model, it provides an explicit, checkable upper bound on the quenched growth rate and highlights which loop components control the exponent.
Even when the bound is not tight, it can still be valuable for parameter scaling (e.g. dependence on temperature
\theta
in transfer-matrix parametrizations) and for separating structural from probabilistic sources of growth.
