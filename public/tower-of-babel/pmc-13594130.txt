Co-thermal coupling of carbonate decomposition and propane dehydrogenation via hydrogen transfer
Authors: Kaige Tian, Xianhui Wang, Pengyu Xiang, Jingyi Zhao, Yan Li, Chenghang Liu, Chengbo Wang, Xiao Liu, Donglong Fu, Xinbin Ma, Chunlei Pei, Sai Chen, Jinlong Gong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Strongly endothermic reactions are always constrained by thermodynamic equilibrium that necessitates high-temperature operation. This paper describes a co-thermal coupling strategy that integrates two thermodynamic equilibrium-limited processes into a single reaction environment via hydrogen transfer. In this system, the hydrogen generated in situ from endothermic propane dehydrogenation is continuously consumed by simultaneous carbonate decomposition, thereby achieving bidirectional reaction intensification. As a result, the decomposition temperature of calcium carbonate is reduced by approximately 75 °C compared to inert conditions, while the propane dehydrogenation conversion reaches ~120% of the thermodynamic equilibrium value with a propylene selectivity of 97.8% during the continuous regeneration cycles. In situ spectroscopic and kinetic analyses demonstrate a coupled reaction network, where hydrogen transfer promotes carbonate transformation via bicarbonate-related surface intermediates and then follows the reverse water-gas shift pathway. Techno-environmental analysis indicates that this integrated process lowers the energy consumption per ton of propylene by 9.8% and reduces net CO2 emissions by 18.1% in the base-case industrial simulation, while co-producing propylene and a CaO-containing solid. These findings establish hydrogen-mediated co-thermal coupling as a general strategy for surmounting equilibrium constraints and intensifying energy- and carbon-intensive chemical processes.

Introduction

Energy-intensive chemical transformations are often governed by thermodynamic constraints1, which impose high operating temperatures and lead to substantial carbon emissions. Carbonate decomposition (CD) exemplifies this challenge2. As a core reaction in cement production, metallurgy, and mineral processing, the dissociation of CaCO3 into CaO and CO2 is strongly endothermic and equilibrium-limited (CaCO3 = CaO + CO2, ΔH0 = + 178.3 kJ/mol), typically requiring temperatures above 800 °C3. Given its large scale and high energy demand, CD represents one of the major sources of industrial CO2 emissions4,5. To date, most mitigation strategies have focused on improving heat management or capturing emitted CO2, while the necessity of high-temperature operation for the reaction itself remains largely unchanged6.

Hydrogen-assisted reduction offers a promising route to lower the decomposition temperature of carbonates7. Giardini et al. pioneered the hydrogenation of calcite, dolomite, and siderite containing CaCO3 or MgCO3 under a high-temperature and high-pressure H2 atmosphere to produce hydrocarbons, achieving a breakthrough in converting inorganic carbonates into organic carbon8. Considerable efforts have been devoted to alleviating these thermodynamic limitations through reaction coupling and process intensification. Duan and coworkers reported the decomposition of carbonates under a CH4 atmosphere that can reduce the decomposition temperature of carbonate, offering a potential pathway for lowering energy consumption9. However, the conversion efficiency of CD under atmospheres such as symmetrical tetrahedral methane remains relatively low, often requiring external reactants or additional process units, introducing complexity, energy penalties, or selectivity limitation10. Therefore, the coupling of CD with hydrogen-rich species to lower the reaction temperature remains a grand challenge.

Herein, we propose a co-thermal coupling strategy that integrates CD with propane dehydrogenation (PDH) through hydrogen transfer (Fig. 1). By integrating hydrogen generation and consumption within a single reaction environment, the coupled system reduces the temperature required for CD while driving dehydrogenation beyond its nominal equilibrium limit. At 550 °C, propane conversion reaches approximately 120% of the equilibrium value under standalone PDH conditions, accompanied by a propylene selectivity of 97.8%. In situ spectroscopy and kinetic analysis uncover a coupled reaction network wherein hydrogen transfer facilitates carbonate transformation via bicarbonate-related surface intermediates and links CO2 conversion to the chemistry of the reverse water-gas shift (RWGS) reaction. A techno-environmental assessment demonstrates that this approach lowers the energy consumption per ton of propylene by 9.8% and reduces net CO2 emissions by 18.1% under the base-case industrial simulation, while co-producing propylene and a CaO-containing solid. Beyond PDH, this work highlights the potential to intensify other energy-intensive transformations using co-thermal coupling systems.Fig. 1Schematic illustration of PDH–CD process.The H2-mediated co-thermal process intensifies carbonate decomposition with co-production of C3H6. Results and discussion

Design of co-thermal coupling of PDH–CD

The co-thermal coupling between PDH and CD is performed in a single-bed (Fig. 1). Unlike tandem catalysis or chemical looping, where reactions occur sequentially or in separated steps, our system operates both strongly endothermic reactions simultaneously under a shared heat input, with hydrogen acting as a transient mediator that directly alters the thermodynamic equilibrium of each process. The platform uses a well-known Pt1Sn1/SiO2 catalyst for PDH and a MnZr-promoted CaO/CaCO3 (MnZr–CaO/CaCO3) as the mineral component. The coupled bed establishes a reaction environment in which the local H2 pressure produced by PDH is relaxed by mineral conversion, alleviating equilibrium constraints on PDH while enabling carbonate conversion at a lower temperature than thermal decomposition. This configuration enables unidirectional hydrogen consumption from the PDH domain to the carbonate domain, reducing the hydrogen partial pressure around the PDH catalyst.

The Pt1Sn1/SiO2 was prepared by incipient wet impregnation11. This catalyst contains highly dispersed Pt–Sn nanoparticles with an average size of 1.4 ± 0.4 nm as shown by transmission electron microscope (TEM) images (Fig. 2a). In situ CO diffuse reflectance infrared Fourier-transform spectroscopy (DRIFTS) shows predominantly linearly adsorbed CO with an absence of bridge-bonded features (Fig. 2b), consistent with Sn-modified Pt ensembles typical of PtSn interfacial structures relevant to selective PDH12–14. As a control, Pt/SiO2 without Sn exhibits both linear and bridging CO adsorption features (Supplementary Fig. 1), confirming that Sn modification effectively suppresses bridging adsorption sites. X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) supports a predominantly metallic state for Pt and Sn, with the Pt 4f7/2 feature shifted to lower binding energy (70.56 eV) relative to pure Pt (Fig. 2c), reflecting Pt–Sn electronic interaction15–18. The PDH catalyst demonstrates excellent long-term stability (Supplementary Fig. 2). MnZr–CaO was prepared by a sol-gel route and converted to MnZr–CaCO3 by CO2 pre-adsorption (ads) before reaction19. Scanning electron microscope (SEM) images show that the MnZr-modified CaCO3 particles exhibit a more open and porous microstructure with finer structural features (Fig. 2d, e), whereas the unmodified CaCO3 appears comparatively denser and less percolated (Supplementary Fig. 3). Such a microstructural difference implies improved surface accessibility and more continuous gas-transport pathways in the promoted material. Consistent with these structural characteristics, a direct comparison of CO2 uptake reveals that MnZr promotion markedly enhances the reversible CO2 capacity relative to unmodified CaO (0.76 vs. 0.17 g-CO2 g−1-ads, Fig. 2f), indicating that a substantially larger fraction of Ca-based reactive sites is available for rapid carbonation. Taken together, the structure-uptake correlation supports that MnZr incorporation improves not only the nominal CO2 capacity but also the accessibility of reactive surfaces and continuous gas pathways, which are prerequisites for the mineral domain to participate in the coupled bed by promptly engaging with gas-phase intermediates20.

Proximity-dependent carbonate reduction and propylene production

We first evaluated the intrinsic thermodynamic constraints of PDH and CD by calculating their equilibrium conversions as a function of temperature under inert conditions (Supplementary Fig. 4). At atmospheric pressure, the equilibrium conversion of C3H8 increases monotonically with temperature but remains significantly limited within the commonly used temperature range of 500–600 °C, consistent with the strongly endothermic and reversible nature of the PDH reaction. Likewise, CaCO3 decomposition exhibits only a very limited thermodynamic driving force below approximately 600 °C, reflecting the high stability of the carbonate phase when the chemical potentials of reactive species are fixed by an inert atmosphere and thermal input is the sole driving variable.

In contrast, coupling PDH with CD under a shared thermal environment introduces a reductive atmosphere that perturbs this inert reference state by continuously consuming H2 during carbonate conversion. In this co-thermal configuration, both reactions are driven by the same temperature field, while hydrogen generated endothermically during PDH is simultaneously consumed by the carbonate phase. Such H2 consumption effectively lowers the H2 partial pressure in the dehydrogenation environment, thereby altering the thermodynamic equilibrium of PDH. At the same time, carbonate conversion is transformed from a purely thermally driven process into one jointly driven by thermal input and chemical coupling to H2 consumption, leading to a substantial enhancement of the attainable equilibrium extent under isothermal conditions.

We then quantified how reductive atmospheres modify the onset of carbonate conversion under these co-thermal conditions21. Temperature-programmed reaction (TPR) of MnZr–CaCO3 under Ar shows CO2 evolution beginning at ~594 °C with no detectable CO (Fig. 3a). Under 10% H2/Ar, both CO2 and CO evolution begin at 519 °C, demonstrating that H2 enables carbonate conversion at a substantially lower temperature (ΔT = 75 °C). The concurrent emergence of CO and CO2 is compatible with a sequence in which carbonate-derived CO2 undergoes subsequent reduction in the presence of H2, consistent with the RWGS pathway. To further probe CD mediated by H2 transfer under a co-thermal reaction environment, we investigated reaction behavior in a C3H8 atmosphere. C3H8 temperature-programmed surface reaction (C3H8-TPSR) over the combined bed of Pt1Sn1/SiO2 and carbonates (Fig. 3b, Supplementary Fig. 5) shows that H2 and C3H6 signals appear at ~246 °C, marking the onset of PDH activity on PtSn. In contrast, CO, H2O, and CO2 signals emerge at ~514 °C. This temperature closely matches the onset of MnZr–CaCO3 conversion under 10% H2/Ar (519 °C), placing carbonate conversion within the temperature window where H2-assisted pathways become accessible under co-thermal coupling. Figure 3c shows the performance of PDH under different reaction environments. At 550 °C, standalone PDH over Pt1Sn1/SiO2 produces propylene at 116.9 mmol gcat−1 h−1 (WHSV = 69.3 h−1). Co-feeding CO2 (CO2–PDH) increases the propylene formation rate to 178.9 mmol gcat−1 h−1, consistent with CO2 influencing PDH under these conditions22. When MnZr–CaCO3 is introduced to form the PDH–CD coupled system, the propylene formation rate increases to 402.8 mmol gcat−1 h−1, a ~3.5-fold enhancement relative to PDH under otherwise comparable conditions, while maintaining high selectivity (98.5%). Under the optimized coupling conditions, propane conversion reaches 58.6% at 550 °C, which is about 120% of its equilibrium value under standalone PDH conditions, with a propylene selectivity of 97.8%. The gain therefore reflects changes in the local reaction environment that suppress hydrogen buildup during PDH.

When CO2 co-feed is combined with the hydrogen-scavenging bed (CO2–PDH–CD), the rate decreases to 211.1 mmol gcat−1 h−1. This occurs because higher CO2 partial pressure reduces the effectiveness of carbonate conversion in consuming H2, so that H2 is no longer efficiently removed from the PDH zone, and the system becomes equilibrium-limited again. The sensitivity to gas composition further distinguishes PDH–CD from a simple “PDH + CO2” co-feed approach and emphasizes that efficient hydrogen consumption by the carbonate phase is central to the coupled performance.

Because the coupling links hydrogen generation to hydrogen consumption within the same thermal environment, we tested how bed configuration controls the magnitude of PDH intensification. Using layered and mixed-bed modes (T1–T5; Fig. 3d), we observed a clear spatial dependence. When MnZr–CaCO3 is placed upstream (upper bed) with the Pt1Sn1/SiO2 catalyst downstream (lower bed), propane conversion (47.2%) is essentially unchanged from direct PDH (47.6%). In contrast, placing the PDH catalyst upstream with MnZr–CaCO3 downstream increases conversion to 49.2%, consistent with hydrogen produced during PDH being consumed as it encounters the carbonate layer23. Further increasing intimacy between the two solids (granule stacking to powder mixing) produces a progressive enhancement in conversion (T3–T5)24,25. These results show that CD is regulated not only by the bulk gas atmosphere but also by the local reaction environment established through coupled hydrogen activation and transfer, which can be tuned by the solid-solid contact length scale. Consistent with this picture, the efficiency of hydrogen consumption in the coupled bed depends on the effective diffusion distance between PtSn sites (hydrogen generation) and carbonate surfaces (hydrogen consumption). Reducing interparticle spacing shortens this diffusion pathway, accelerates hydrogen supply‑consumption equilibration, and alleviates the hydrogen accumulation that otherwise inhibits PDH. This picture is supported by quantitative analysis of the effluent gas: from T2 to T5, as the particles are brought into closer contact, the H2/C3H6 partial pressure ratio progressively decreases, correlating directly with the increase in propane conversion (Supplementary Fig. 6). Control experiments confirm that MnZr–CaCO3 does not catalyze PDH but promotes the RWGS reaction under CO2/H2 feeds (Supplementary Fig. 7), supporting a clear functional division between Pt1Sn1/SiO2 and the carbonate phase.

PDH is commonly operated in cyclic modes with reduction and regeneration14–16,26, so we evaluated whether PDH–CD is compatible with PDH catalyst handling. Without H2 pre-reduction, Pt1Sn1/SiO2 gives 22.7% propane conversion and 85.8% propylene selectivity at 550 °C (Supplementary Fig. 8). Increasing the reduction temperature above ~350 °C progressively improves selectivity toward the level obtained after reduction at 600 °C, consistent with the role of reduction in establishing the active PtSn surface state27. In parallel, TPR shows that CaCO3 conversion under H2 does not occur below 450 °C (Fig. 3a), indicating that a practical PDH pre-reduction step (≤450 °C) can be implemented without prematurely consuming the carbonate in the coupled bed.

Beyond compatibility, stable operation of the PDH–CD system requires the mineral phase to sustain its function during repeated carbonation-decarbonation cycling. To this end, we compared the cyclic behavior of MnZr-modified and unmodified CaO. The MnZr-promoted material maintains a high and reversible CO2 uptake over repeated cycles (Fig. 3e), whereas unmodified CaO shows a rapid loss of capacity (Supplementary Fig. 9). This difference is closely linked to their structural evolution. SEM images before and after cycling reveal that unmodified CaO undergoes pronounced grain growth and pore blockage, indicative of densification that reduces accessible reaction sites and hinders gas transport (Supplementary Fig. 10). In contrast, MnZr modification largely suppresses these changes, allowing the material to retain finer grains and a connected pore structure after cycling (Supplementary Fig. 11), thereby preserving efficient gas-solid contact. By limiting densification and maintaining reversible CO2 conversion during looping, MnZr-promoted CaO/CaCO3 provides a durable mineral phase that can continuously participate in hydrogen removal under cyclic operation.

We then operated the coupled bed under continuous regeneration cycles (Fig. 3f). In each cycle, CaO was first carbonated to CaCO3 under CO2 to simulate the formation of carbonates, followed by PDH–CD reaction and oxidative regeneration of the PDH catalyst. Across cycles, PDH–CD consistently maintained higher propane conversion than PDH while sustaining high propylene selectivity, demonstrating that coupling does not inherently compromise selectivity during chemical looping cyclic operation. In addition to metal-based PDH catalysts, oxide-based PDH catalysts such as VOx/Al2O3 (3V/Al) show stable performance when paired with MnZr–CaO in a long-term test (~5000 min over 22 cycles, Supplementary Fig. 12). These findings demonstrate that the PDH–CD strategy is universally applicable to diverse PDH catalysts rather than restricted to a specific formulation. The MnZr-functionalized carbonate used here should be regarded as a standardized model material for mechanistic investigation, because its porous structure and stable carbonation-decarbonation behavior allow the hydrogen-mediated coupling process to be resolved reproducibly.

The core coupling principle can nevertheless be extended to other carbonate materials. As illustrated in Supplementary Fig. 13, physical mixtures consisting of unmodified CaCO3 and PDH catalyst also outperformed the bare PDH catalyst in terms of propane conversion, although the enhancement was smaller because pristine CaCO3 has a denser structure and lower accessible reactive surface area. Therefore, inexpensive natural carbonates such as limestone may be adapted to this coupling concept, but practical performance will require rational material engineering, reduced promoter loading, improved carbonate lifetime, and recycling of the structured carbonate phase. From a solid-handling perspective, the PDH catalyst and carbonate can be separated after reaction by sieving, gravity separation, magnetic separation, or cyclones in fluidized-bed designs. The CaO-containing product obtained from the present MnZr–CaCO3 model material should not be described as directly applicable as a cement raw material; its cement-related use would require raw-meal dilution, formulation adjustment, and verification against relevant standards for heavy-metal content and leachable heavy-metal limits28 (Supplementary Note 1). Before such verification, more suitable utilization routes include steelmaking, flue gas desulfurization, coatings, high-value calcium-based chemicals, or recycling/regeneration of the carbonate phase.

It is noteworthy that the PDH–CD coupled system exhibited a decline in propane conversion after long-term cycling (Fig. 3f). To understand the gradual decline in propane conversion during PDH–CD cycling, we compared the cycling stability of standalone Pt1Sn1/SiO2 with that of the coupled system. Standalone Pt1Sn1/SiO2 remained stable over multiple cycles, whereas the coupled system showed progressive decay. Post‑reaction characterization of Pt1Sn1/SiO2 revealed no significant shifts in the binding energies of Pt and Sn species in XPS spectra (Supplementary Fig. 14); CO‑DRIFTS predominantly exhibited adsorbed CO species with no notable recovery of bridged CO adsorption features linearly, indicating that the Pt‑Sn ensemble structure and Pt dispersion were well preserved (Supplementary Fig. 15); STEM‑EDS mappings detected no enrichment of Ca, Mn, or Zr on the Pt–Sn particles (Supplementary Figs. 16 and 17). These results indicate that neither CO2 nor carbonate‑related species led to deactivation of the Pt–Sn active sites. In contrast, the carbonate sorbent exhibited a gradual decline in reversible CO2 uptake capacity during H2-involved cyclic tests (Fig. 3e), which is highly consistent with the downward trend of propane conversion in cyclic PDH–CD reactions. Since the consumption of H2 by carbonate sorbents is critical to shifting the PDH thermodynamic equilibrium toward propylene production, the slow performance deterioration of the PDH–CD system is primarily caused by the progressive degradation of carbonate sorbents instead of active catalyst deactivation. Accordingly, the long-term durability of the carbonate sorbent is the decisive factor for the scalable and sustainable operation of the PDH–CD coupling strategy.

Hydrogen transfer-mediated coupling mechanism

We tracked the carbonate phase during gas switching and co-thermal operation. In situ X-ray diffraction (XRD) of MnZr–CaO under a CO2 atmosphere shows the growth of CaCO3 diffraction features during CO2 adsorption (0–60 min) until saturation (Fig. 4a). When this pre-carbonated MnZr–CaCO3 is physically mixed with Pt1Sn1/SiO2 and exposed to propane at reaction temperature, CaCO3 diffraction intensities decrease markedly (Fig. 4b), demonstrating carbonate consumption under the isothermal PDH atmosphere. In situ DRIFTS further resolves the surface chemistry associated with carbonate conversion. Under CO2 exposure (2300–2400 cm−1 region are attributed to gas-phase CO2), MnZr–CaO develops characteristic carbonate bands at 1540 and 2504 cm−1, along with weaker features near 1789 and 876 cm−129, consistent with progressive carbonate formation (Fig. 4c). Upon switching the CO2-saturated MnZr–CaCO3 to 10% H2/Ar, these carbonate-associated bands decay (Supplementary Fig. 19), while features attributable to bicarbonate-related species emerge at 2343 and 2361 cm−130–32. Concurrently, a band at 1586 cm−1 assigned to formate species develops33,34, demonstrating H2 breaking C-O bonds in carbonate species to form HCOO* and -OH groups35,36, accompanied by a gaseous CO feature near 2130 cm−112, consistent with formate conversion to CO and H2O. When Pt1Sn1/SiO2 is mixed with MnZr–CaCO3 and exposed to C3H8, time-resolved DRIFTS spectra (Fig. 4d) show a progressive attenuation of carbonate-and calcite-related bands at 2504 and 1540 cm−1, together with weaker features at 1789 and 876 cm−1. Concomitantly, a new band assigned to surface formate species emerges and grows at 1586 cm−1, accompanied by the appearance of gaseous CO near 2130 cm−1 and a Pt–CO feature at ~2045 cm−123. These correlated spectral evolutions indicate that carbonate species are consumed via a formate-mediated pathway, leading to CO formation under PDH conditions. Whereas neither Pt1Sn1/SiO2 alone (Supplementary Fig. 20) nor MnZr–CaCO3 alone (Supplementary Fig. 21) produces these features under identical conditions. Together, the diffraction and spectroscopy data establish a coupled reaction network in which hydrogen generated during PDH participates in carbonate transformation through bicarbonate-related surface species, while carbonate-derived CO2 undergoes RWGS chemistry via a formate pathway. The spectroscopic evidence supports an indirect pathway for carbonate consumption. Specifically, CaCO3 first decomposes to CaO and CO2, after which CO2 reacts with H2 via the RWGS reaction to produce CO and H2O. This conclusion is further supported by TPR experiments (Fig. 3a), where CO and CO2 appear simultaneously under a 10% H2/Ar atmosphere with an onset temperature of 519 °C, and by control experiments (Supplementary Fig. 7) showing that MnZr–CaCO3 alone is active for the RWGS reaction. Bicarbonate and formate are the surface intermediates detected during this process.

Kinetic study

Reaction orders and apparent activation energies (Ea) for propylene formation were quantified for PDH and PDH–CD to assess whether the coupled bed alters intrinsic dehydrogenation kinetics or primarily perturbs the hydrogen balance37,38. The reaction order with respect to propane is approximately unity over a wide partial-pressure range (0–60 kPa) for both systems (Fig. 5a), and the Ea values are nearly identical (65.12 versus 64.80 kJ mol−1; Supplementary Fig. 22). These similarities demonstrate that the elementary dehydrogenation chemistry on PtSn is preserved upon coupling and that the enhanced performance in PDH–CD does not originate from a modification of the intrinsic PDH pathway. However, as the carbonate loading is further increased (When the mass ratio of MnZr–CaCO3 to Pt1Sn1/SiO2 > 5), the rate becomes insensitive to additional carbonate, exhibiting apparent zero-order behavior (Fig. 5b). In this regime, the system possesses sufficient hydrogen-removal capacity such that hydrogen consumption no longer limits the overall reaction rate. Instead, the propylene formation rate becomes constrained by the finite rate of hydrogen generation during PDH. C3H8 pulse-dwell experiments and thermogravimetric relaxation analysis provide convergent evidence for identifying the rate-controlling step (RDS)39. During pulse-dwell measurements (Fig. 5c), the C3H8 signal intensity remains nearly constant throughout the relaxation period, indicating that transient accumulation or depletion associated with gas-phase transport or bulk solid diffusion does not govern the observed kinetics. Correspondingly, the thermogravimetric relaxation curves exhibit a linear mass-loss regime (Supplementary Fig. 23), characteristic of surface-reaction-controlled carbonate conversion rather than diffusion-limited transport of CO2 or H2 within the carbonate phase40. These observations exclude COx transport during the hydrogen-carbonate reaction as the dominant RDS on the experimental timescale.

To rationalize this behavior and the pronounced dependence on the proximity between the PDH catalyst and the carbonate phase, we explicitly considered, in Supplementary Note 2, the cross-domain transfer of hydrogen generated on PtSn dehydrogenation sites between the catalytic domain and the carbonate domain. Under conditions where internal and external diffusion limitations are excluded, the effective coupling between PDH and CD still relies on hydrogen transport between these two functional domains. Based on this, we constructed a kinetic framework that couples a Langmuir-Hinshelwood-type PDH kinetic model incorporating competitive propylene adsorption inhibition41, a shrinking-core treatment for the geometric evolution of the carbonate phase during conversion, and an explicit cross-domain hydrogen transfer term42. In this model, hydrogen generated on PtSn sites is transported to the carbonate domain via an effective cross-domain flux and is primarily consumed there through reactions such as the RWGS. Based on the above model framework, the rate differences observed experimentally among samples with different structures (Fig. 3d) can thus be interpreted in terms of variations in \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${k}_{m}{a}_{i}$$\end{document}kmai: as the degree of structural coupling between the catalytic and carbonate domains increases, such as through greater interfacial contact or more effective cross-domain transport pathways, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${k}_{m}{a}_{i}$$\end{document}kmai increases, leading to a progressive enhancement in the propylene formation rate. Importantly, with increasing carbonate content, the model shows a gradual transition of the system from a “hydrogen-removal-limited” regime to a “PDH hydrogen-generation-limited” regime. Once sufficient carbonate is present to enable rapid hydrogen removal, the rate of hydrogen generation during PDH becomes the overall RDS of the coupled system. Under the high-carbonate-loading conditions examined in this study, additional carbonate provides excess hydrogen removal capacity but does not further enhance propylene production. The coupling effect thus manifests as a shift in the limiting step, from hydrogen removal at low carbonate availability to hydrogen generation during PDH when carbonate is abundant, while the intrinsic dehydrogenation kinetics on PtSn remain unchanged throughout this transition (Fig. 5d). A Langmuir‑Hinshelwood model for the PDH reaction (Supplementary Note 2) captures this behavior well, reproducing the experimental rates over a range of propane partial pressures with an \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${R}^{2}$$\end{document}R2 of 0.99 (Supplementary Fig. 25 and Supplementary Table 1). The model assumes surface dehydrogenation as the rate‑determining step and no transport limitations, both of which are supported by the pulse‑dwell and thermogravimetric experiments.

Heat management and TEA analysis

As discussed above, a single external heat input simultaneously drives two coupled endothermic reactions within one fixed-bed reactor. While hydrogen renders the carbonate chemistry compatible with the practical PDH operating temperature window, enabling both solids to be co-heated at a single furnace setpoint rather than being separated into different reactor units because of mismatched temperature requirements.

To link this coupling to reactor-scale heat management, we performed infrared thermography under constant furnace heating. Because infrared imaging cannot resolve the temperature of individual particles (e.g., 20–40 mesh) under our experimental conditions, we adopted a layered packing as the model configuration and tracked two fixed regions of interest (upper layer: Sp1; lower layer: Sp2). As shown in Supplementary Figs. 26–28, the quartz-quartz reference bed exhibits a negligible temperature response upon switching gas atmospheres, confirming that the external heat input and baseline heat-transfer conditions are stable. Once reactive solids are introduced, the temperature response of the PDH catalyst region cools more noticeably in the presence of propane, whereas the carbonate-containing region shows the strongest cooling response in the presence of hydrogen, consistent with reaction-driven endothermicity occurring where the corresponding solid resides. When both functional solids are present, both regions respond under reaction conditions, indicating that the coupled bed behaves as a distributed endothermic load rather than a single localized cold spot (Fig. 6a). These heat-management features translate into process-level benefits in Aspen Plus simulations comparing conventional PDH with the coupled PDH–CD route on a consistent industrial basis (Supplementary Fig. 29, Supplementary Tables 2–8). The coupled scheme achieves higher single-pass C3H8 conversion while maintaining high propylene selectivity, thereby increasing propylene production and reducing separation duty (as reflected by a lower reflux requirement in the propylene column). Consequently, the energy consumption per ton of propylene decreases from 5.90 to 5.32 MWh, with reductions in fired heating, steam, electricity, and refrigeration. Even when the energy penalty for CO2 capture is included in the coupled case, net emissions per ton of propylene still decrease from 0.94 to 0.77 ton CO2 (Fig. 6b, c).

These gains come from replacing the purely thermal decomposition of CaCO3 (above 590 °C in inert gas) with an H2-mediated pathway that operates at 550 °C. Calculations on CD alone show that the H2-assisted route cuts net CO2 emissions by ~13.5% compared with conventional calcination, even after accounting for the energy needed to separate gases (Supplementary Figs. 30 and 31 and Supplementary Tables 9–15). Thus, the coupling strategy could inform lower-temperature carbonate processing routes relevant to cement-related and other carbonate-intensive industries. Although cement-related implementation of the present Mn/Zr-containing product requires standards-based verification28 (Supplementary Note 1). The economic benefits are equally notable. The higher single-pass conversion in PDH–CD substantially reduces the recycle load of unreacted propane, lowering compression, refrigeration, and separation duties. This translates into meaningful operating cost savings per ton of propylene and a significant increase in annual profit under typical market conditions. Sensitivity analysis confirms that these advantages remain robust across a wide range of feedstock prices, product values, catalyst lifetimes, carbonate-material costs and their corresponding make-up fractions, as well as operating hours (Supplementary Figs. 32 and 33, Supplementary Tables 16–19).

In conclusion, this work develops and validates a co-thermal coupling strategy that combines CD and PDH via hydrogen transfer, achieving significant performance enhancements and environmental benefits. The coupled system not only lowers the reaction temperature for CD but also breaks the equilibrium limitation of PDH, enabling propane conversion to exceed the nominal equilibrium value by ~20% with high propylene selectivity (98.5%) at 550 °C. Mechanistic studies based on in situ spectroscopy and kinetic analyses clarify that hydrogen transfer, mediated by bicarbonate-related surface intermediates, is the key to the synergistic effect, bridging carbonate transformation with RWGS-based CO2 conversion. Techno-environmental evaluation further confirms the industrial feasibility of this strategy, which can achieve a net CO2 emission reduction of up to 18.1% while realizing the co-production of high-value propylene and a CaO-containing product. This study not only provides a novel and efficient approach for CD and PDH process intensification but also offers a generalizable co-thermal coupling paradigm for upgrading other energy-intensive reactions with reduced energy consumption and carbon footprint.

Materials

H2PtCl6·6H2O (Chemart (Tianjin) Chemical Technology Co., Ltd., 99.9%), SnCl2·2H2O (Aladdin (China) Chemical Co., Ltd., 99.0%), silica gel (high purity grade, pore size 60 Å, 70-230 mesh, Sigma-Aldrich). Ca(NO3)2·4H2O (Tianjin Yuanli Chemical Co. Ltd., AR grade), Mn(NO3)2·4H2O (Macklin, AR grade, 50 wt%) solution. Zr(NO3)4·5H2O (Tianjin Yuanli Chemical Co. Ltd., AR grade). γ-Al2O3 (Aladdin (China) Chemical Co., Ltd., 99.99%). Citric acid monohydrate (Tianjin Yuanli Chemical Co., Ltd., AR grade). Ammonia (Tianjin Yuanli Chemical Co., Ltd., AR grade). Polyethylene glycol 6000 (PEG-6000, Tianjin Yuanli Chemical Co., Ltd., AR grade). NH4VO3 (Chemart (Tianjin) Chemical Technology Co., Ltd., 99.0%). Oxalic acid (Chemart (Tianjin) Chemical Technology Co., Ltd., 99.0%). All materials were used as received without further purification.

Catalyst preparation

Pt1Sn1/SiO211

The Pt1Sn1/SiO2 was synthesized by incipient wet impregnation. H2PtCl6·6H2O and SnCl2·2H2O were added to a 0.1 M HCl solution to give a final concentration of 102 mM of Pt and Sn ions. 0.5 ml of the above solution was diluted with deionized water to obtain the volume required for primary wet impregnation onto one gram of silica gel. The obtained sample was aged overnight, then dried in an oven at 80 °C for 12 h. Unless otherwise specified, the Pt1Sn1/SiO2 catalyst was pre-treated in a stream of hydrogen at 600 °C for 1 h prior to testing.

3V/Al26

The 3V/Al (3 represents 3 wt.% of V loading per gram of γ-Al2O3) was synthesized by incipient wet impregnation. NH4VO3 and oxalic acid with a molar ratio of 0.5 were dissolved in an appropriate volume of deionized water. Dropwise added to the γ-Al2O3. The obtained sample was aged overnight, then dried in an oven at 80 °C for 12 h, and finally calcined at 550 °C for 4 h in static air using a muffle furnace.

MnZr–CaO and MnZr–CaCO319

Mn- and Zr-modified CaO was synthesized via a sol-gel-combustion method with a molar ratio of Ca/Zr/Mn = 15 : 0.5 : 0.5. Appropriate amounts of metal precursors (Ca(NO3)2·4H2O, Mn(NO3)2·4H2O, Zr(NO3)4·5H2O) and citric acid monohydrate were separately dissolved in deionized water. The metal precursor solutions were then mixed with PEG-6000, accounting for 1 wt.% of the total mixture. The solution was stirred slowly at 70 °C to ensure complete dissolution and homogeneous mixing (10 min). Ammonia solution was added dropwise to adjust the pH to ∼10. The mixed solution was subjected to rotary evaporation at 90 °C to remove excess water. Subsequently, the viscous gel was vacuum-dried at 100 °C for 24 h to obtain a porous foam-like solid. Finally, the low-density foam was calcined at 700 °C in static air for 4 h with a heating rate of 2 °C min−1. The resulting sample was MnZr–CaO; MnZr–CaCO3 was obtained by treating MnZr–CaO at 600 °C for at least 60 min in a CO2 atmosphere.

Characterizations

X-ray diffraction (XRD)

XRD was performed on a Bruker D8 diffractometer equipped with graphite-monochromate Cu Kα radiation (λ = 0.15418 nm), operated at 40 kV and 200 mA. Data were collected by step scanning from 10° to 80° (2θ) at a rate of 8° min−1.

Transmission electron microscope (TEM)

TEM images were acquired on a JEM-F200 transmission electron microscope operated at 200 kV, equipped with a liquid nitrogen-cooled energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) detector for elemental analysis. The dried Pt1Sn1/SiO2 catalyst was first reduced under 10% H2/Ar flow (50 mL min−1) at 600 °C for 1 h. The powder was ultrasonically dispersed in ethanol and deposited onto copper grids coated with ultrathin holey carbon films.

Scanning electron microscope (SEM)

The morphology of the catalysts was observed using a field emission scanning electron microscope (Hitachi S-4800) with an accelerating voltage of 5 kV.

Temperature-programmed surface reaction (TPSR)

Temperature-programmed experiments were all carried out on the Micromeritics Auto Chem II 2920 chemisorption apparatus with a Mass Spectrometry (MS) OmniStar from Pfeiffer Vacuum. For TPSR of C3H8 over the pre-reduced Pt1Sn1/SiO2, MnZr–CaCO3, or Pre-reduced Pt1Sn1/SiO2 + MnZr–CaCO3, the sample was purged in a flow rate of 30 mL min−1 of Ar at 50 °C for 10 min. After switching to C3H8/Ar (10 mL min−1 C3H8, 15 mL min−1 Ar) for 30 min, the sample was heated at a rate of 10 °C min−1 up to 800 °C. For TPSR of Ar or 10% H2/Ar, after the end of purge, the gas was switched to Ar or 10% H2/Ar. The output products (CO2, C3H6, C3H8, CO, H2O, and H2, m/z equal to 44, 41, 29, 28, 18, and 2, respectively) were monitored by mass spectrometry.

X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS)

XPS was conducted on a Thermo Fisher ESCALAB-Xi equipped with an Al Kα X-ray radiation source (hѵ = 1486.6 eV). The binding energies were calibrated using the C 1s peak at 284.8 eV. Avantage software was used for core-level spectra decomposition. The catalyst was first reduced at 600 °C for 1 h in a stream of 10% H2/Ar (30 mL min−1). The binding energies were calibrated using the C 1s peak at 284.5 eV as a reference.

Pulse-dwell experiment

Pulse-experiments were carried out on the Micromeritics Auto Chem II 2920 chemisorption apparatus, and the output products were also measured on a Hiden QIC-20 mass spectrometer (C3H8, m/z = 29). Typically, putting the pre-treated samples into the U-type reactor, after this, the temperature increased to 550 °C in Ar (30 mL min−1). And then, the analysis was carried out using a C3H8/Ar atmosphere. After 32 pulses in a C3H8/Ar atmosphere, the atmosphere was switched to Ar, purged for 20 min, and then switched to C3H8/Ar to continue pulsing.

In situ XRD

The in situ XRD pattern was recorded on a Rigaku Smartlab diffractometer operating at 40 kV and 200 mA, and the graphite filtered Cu Kα with wavelength λ of 1.5418 Å was employed as the radiation source. The data points were collected by step scanning with a rate of 10° min−1 from 20° to 70° (2θ).

Thermogravimetric (TG)

The TG relaxation experiment was performed on Themys. About 50 mg pre-treated samples were treated at 300 °C for 1 h under He (100 mL/min) to eliminate the presence of hydrated species and then reacted in pure N2, a mixture of 30% H2/He or 30% C3H8/He (50 mL/min) at 550 °C, respectively.

Cyclic stability

The Cyclic stability of CaO powder with and without a MnZr promoter was measured using a thermogravimetric analyzer (Themys). For each run, approximately 10 mg of the sample was used for the adsorption of CO2 at high temperature. The testing of the adsorption process was maintained at 600 °C for 90 min within an atmosphere of 25 vol% CO2 (N2 balance, 100 mL min−1), and a desorption process was proceeding at 700 °C for 30 min in an atmosphere of pure N2 (100 mL min−1). All heating and cooling procedures of the experiment were performed in a N2 flow of 10 mL min−1 with the heating or cooling rate of 10 °C min−1. Mass flow controllers were used to control the exact flow rates of the gases. Flow rate, temperature, and weight of the samples were obtained as a function of time during the whole test.

In situ diffuse reflectance infrared Fourier-transform spectroscopy (DRIFTS)

The in situ DRIFTS experiments were carried out on a Nicolet IS50 spectrometer, equipped with a Harrick Scientific DRIFTS cell fitted with ZnSe windows. The detailed treatments are given as follows:

CO in situ DRIFTS experiments were performed to monitor the difference in coordination of Pt1Sn1/SiO2. The fresh catalysts were heated from ambient temperature to 600 °C at a rate of 10 °C min−1 and retained at 600 °C in a flow rate of 50 mL min−1 of 40% H2/Ar. Then, the catalysts were cooled down to 30 °C, and the backgrounds (8 cm−1 resolution, 64 scans) were collected after Ar purging at a flow rate of 50 mL min−1 for at least 1 h. With the addition of a flow of 3 mL min−1 of CO, the adsorption of CO molecules on the surface of the catalysts continued for 30 min. Afterwards, after purging with Ar at a flow rate of 50 mL min−1 for 1 h, the CO adsorption spectra were collected (with a resolution of 8 cm−1 and 64 scans).

CO2, H2, or C3H8 in situ DRIFTS experiments were performed to record the surface species evolution of 50 mg Pt1Sn1/SiO2, 50 mg MnZr–CaCO3, or 20 mg Pt1Sn1/SiO2 + 30 mg MnZr–CaCO3. The pre-treated catalyst was filled into the cell. Initially, the catalysts were heated at a rate of 10 °C min−1 from ambient temperature to 300 °C. The systems were then purged with Ar (50 mL min−1) at 300 °C for 1 h to remove the adsorbates. After that, the background spectra (a resolution of 8 cm−1, 64 scans) were collected. Subsequently, the sample was treated with 10% H2/Ar, 60% C3H8/Ar, or pure Ar (25 mL min−1) for 1 h, and DRIFTS spectra were collected every 1 min.
