Ameliorative effects of Crocin as a potent antioxidant with its spectral characterization against Cadmium chloride-induced hepatotoxicity
Authors: Reham Z. Hamza, Samy M. El-Megharbel, Bander Albogami, Eman H. Al-Thubaiti, Seham S. AlZahrani, Khadeejah Alsolami, Najah M. Albaqami, Dalal Sulaiman Alshaya, Jawaher J. Albaqami, Abd El-Aziz A. Diab, Mai S. Attia
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Cadmium (Cd) is a heavy metal that adversely affects the environment, as well as the cellular and metabolic systems of organisms. Cadmium salts, such as cadmium chloride (CdCl2; CD), are one of the most common pollutants. As CD is not easily degradable, its risk to humans via food-chain contamination is constantly increasing. Crocin (CRO), a potent antioxidant, is isolated from saffron, an important spice rich in carotenoids, commonly consumed globally and used as a drug to treat numerous diseases. This study mainly examined the amelioration of hepatotoxicity and oxidative stress damage induced by CD in male rats by CRO administration. CRO was chemically characterised using Infrared (FT-IR), ultraviolet (UV–Vis), X-Ray diffraction (XRD), Scanning Electron microscope (SEM), and Transmission electron microscope (TEM); it was confirmed to contain hydroxyl, ketonic ester and glycoside groups. Twenty-four male albino rats were randomised into the following groups. Control, CD (5 mg/kg), CRO (50 mg/kg), CD plus CRO at the same dose. At the end, liver and blood samples were analysed for biochemical, molecular and histological variations, revealing that CD administration notably reduced hepatic superoxide dismutase (SOD), catalase (CAT) and glutathione (GSH) levels. Meanwhile, along with hyperlipidemia, Aspartate aminotransferase (AST), Alanine aminotransferase (ALT), Lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) and Alpha-Fetoprotein (AFP) activities, and malonyldialdehyde levels (MDA) were elevated. Histopathology of liver sections revealed inflammatory cell infiltration, hepatocellular necrosis and areas of haemorrhage. Furthermore, significant induction of mammalian target of rapamycin (mTOR) and ATK1 protein kinase, which is part of the AKT subfamily of serine/threonine kinases and is involved in many signalling pathways essential for normal cell function, was observed in comparison to the control group. Meanwhile, a marked decline in beclin, autophagy-related protein light chain 3 (LC3 II) a protein involved in autophagy and cell metabolism, and Phosphatase and Tensin homolog “PTEN”-induced kinase 1 levels was detected in the hepatocytes. Western blots revealed an enhanced release of cytochrome c from the CD group compared with the CRO-treated group. In contrast to CRO-based improvement, significant hepatic amelioration was observed, with a decline in hepatocellular congestion post-CRO administration; meanwhile, no hepatocellular haemorrhage or hepatic necrosis was observed. In summary, CRO alleviated CD-induced hepatic injury due to its potent antioxidant capacities and hepatoprotective activities.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-026-63088-9.

Introduction

Cadmium (CD) is a toxic heavy metal that adversely affects cellular and metabolic systems, with elevated levels consistently observed in rats exposed to cadmium.CD is not easily broken; the pollution of CD via the food chain is increasing constantly1.

Exposure to CD occurs essentially via the foods. CD contamination is a major health concern worldwide2. Exposure to CD for long periods is well known to induce toxic effects in numerous body organs3,4.

CD is well known to enhance lipid peroxidation by excessive production of the free radicals, reactive oxygen species (ROS), with depletion of the antioxidants5.CD toxicity is essentially due to free radicals’ production6. The capability of CD to induce oxidative stress involves the generation of reactive oxygen species, which affects mitochondrial functions, leading to a reduction in the intracellular glutathione levels7.

Organ such as the liver is highly susceptible to lipid peroxidation due to factors mainly the high oxygen utilization rates, abundance of polyunsaturated fatty acids, weak antioxidant defences, and high concentrations of transition metals like Cu and Fe8.

CD alters the functioning of oxidative stress-neutralizing enzymes, disrupting tissue metabolism and exacerbating its toxic effects. It impacts lipid structural integrity and indirectly affects membrane-bound enzymes by elevating free radical production across various organs, thereby hindering the antioxidant defense system9.

Reactive oxygen species (ROS) are produced during normal metabolism, but when their levels become excessive and exceed the capacity of antioxidants, they lead to induction of severe oxidative stress10. Recently, a comprehensive investigation is underway to evaluate the preventive effects of various natural antioxidants against metal-induced toxicities11,12.

Antioxidants are becoming increasingly important in treating diseases linked to oxidative stress and are considered potential therapeutic agents for various disorders13.

Saffron (Crocus sativus L.), a member of Iridaceae, is a spice cultivated in various countries14. There is a global interest in the isolated active compounds of saffron due to their high antioxidant and anti-inflammatory functions in humans, which act via various mechanisms: reduction in lipid peroxidation, and oxidative injury markers due to its potent scavenging activity for the excessive free radicals15. The compounds of saffron with the most bioactivity are safranal, crocetin, picroCrocin and Crocin16,17. There are four types of CRO, and three of them, except Crocin-1, form cis–trans isomers18.

Numerous studies have revealed antioxidant, anti-inflammatory19 properties of CRO. CRO is a water-soluble carotenoid and is the main pigment of saffron and produces red-coloured crystals20,21. CRO possesses higher free-radical scavenging and antioxidant capacities than gallic and ascorbic acids, mainly due to its hydroxyl and glucose moieties, which react with free radicals as potential electron donors and scavenge them22.

One of the well-studied antioxidant agents is CRO. It is one of the major components of (Crocus sativus L.) and it is the diester formed from the disaccharide gentiobiose and the dicarboxylic acid crocetin (Fig. 1). CRO, a water-soluble carotenoid containing the chemical moieties crocetin and gentiobiose23. It is characterized as a slightly soluble carotenoid, while it presents coloring and flavoring capabilities. It has also been shown to have antioxidant24,25, anti-inflammatory26,27.Fig. 1Chemical structure of crocin28.

CRO, a water-soluble carotenoid isolated from Crocus sativus (saffron). It is the main pigment of saffron and gives red colour crystals20,21. Previous studies have proved that CRO possesses higher radical scavenging activity and antioxidant capacities as compared to gallic acid and ascorbic acid. The main free radical-scavenging activity of CRO is attributed to the OH group and glucose moieties as potential electron donors, which react with free radicals and thus scavenging effectively, and then provide the essential antioxidant capacity22.

CRO is synthesized from the esterification of disaccharide gentiobiose and the dicarboxylic acid crocetin and is referred to as a group of hydrophilic carotenoids including monoglycosyl or diglycosyl polyene esters of crocetin. When CRO is orally administered, it is suddenly hydrolyzed into deglycosylated trans-crocetin (the bio-active component of saffron) in the digestive system29. In accordance with the findings of Zhang et al. oral administration of CRO afforded a higher crocetin level in the plasma, suggesting the potent pharmacological value of CRO than crocetin30. Although CRO has a lower bioavailability rate, offering different ways to elevate its bioavailability as an active compound31.

Thus, CRO can protect cells against oxidative stress injuries. By altering the activities of antioxidant enzymes such as glutathione peroxidase (GPx), superoxide dismutase (SOD), and catalase (CAT) and the cell oxidative defence system at the translation level, CRO contributes to protection against reactive oxygen species (ROS). Endoplasmic reticulum (ER) plays an axial role in protecting cells under oxidative stress conditions. However, any changes in the mRNA levels of ER stressors, have a function in many stress-related diseases, such as Alzheimer disease and cancers32.

CRO via promoting the transcription of ER enzyme-encoding genes contributes to ER-inducing and subsequent oxidative stress component reduction33. Additionally, the release of ROS and pro-inflammatory molecules, along with transportation to all the tissues, contributes to all the disorders associated with inflammation. CRO exerts an anti-inflammatory effect by scavenging the free radicals and modulating the oxidative stress-related defence system34.

Crocin is recognized for its potent antioxidant properties and is utilized in the common traditional medicine as a natural remedy for various diseases due to its hypolipidemic, anti-inflammatory, and anti-carcinogenic effects35.

The main components of saffron are crocin, crocetin and safranal36. Although saffron contains 150 volatile and aromatic compounds, its orange and golden colours are primarily attributed to α-crocin content37. Phytochemical ameliorative effects have emerged as effective solutions for addressing hepatotoxicity, primarily through their mechanisms of free radical regulation38–40.

The current study investigates hepatotoxicity induced by CD through excessive ROS production, highlighting its vital role as a potent antioxidant and free-radical scavenger, as well as an alleviator of ER oxidative damage. The primary objective was to demonstrate CRO’s ameliorative potential against CD-induced hepatic toxicity.

Sample size calculation

Sample size is the minimum number of animals required in any study to test a hypothesis in alignment with ethical approvals, to minimise any suffering or pain, to have sufficient statistical power to detect a treatment effect and acquire accurate results to minimise the error rate. We desire an equal and minimal sample size for both groups to detect a difference with a power of 80% at a 95% confidence level. Thus, we assumed that ALT in the CRO versus CD group was 18 ± 2.5 versus 25 ± 3.25 (U/L). At 80% power and a 95% confidence level, the sample size required was 24 and 6 in every group. These calculations used the OPEN EPI software package41 and were confirmed using an equation to determine ‘Sample size’42, where N = sample size, 1.962 = 95% Confidence, ᵟ2 = Standard Deviation, E2 = Error Rate and calculating the Sample standard deviation (Xi − x̄)2 = 5.25.

The equation: N = 1.962Xᵟ2/E2.

N = (1.96)2 × (5.25)2/(4)2 = 105.88/16 = 6.617.

Thus, for each group, the sample size (N) was ~ 6 male rats, which validated the mean and confirmed the changes in alignment with the suitable sample size.

Male albino rats were randomly divided into four groups (N = 6). Group I (Control): Rats were I.P administered with 0.5 mL of physiological saline/day for 30 successive days. Group II (CDCl2; CD): a 5 mg/Kg dose determined based on the study of Ojo et al.43 was I.P administered for 30 successive days. As mentioned for CD, the dose was chosen per previous studies. After determining the suitable dose based on the LD50 of CdCl₂ administration and the proportion of this dose (%), it has been demonstrated that the Cd dose chosen causes remarkable oxidative stress in various tissues with a lower rate of mortality44,45. This dose was considered sufficient to induce oxidative stress in male rats, and accordingly, Group III (Crocin; CRO; Sigma–Aldrich Co., USA; CAS Number: 42553–65-1) was I.P administered at a daily dosage of 50 mg/kg for 30 successive days46. Group IV (CD + CRO): CD was I.P administered at a dose of 5 mg/kg per day; then 20 min later, allowing for good absorption, CRO was I.P administered at a dose of 50 mg/kg for 30 successive days. CD and CRO were dissolved in physiological saline before injection.

The doses of both required for each rat were determined in a dose-dependent manner based on the rat weight, and the animals were weighed weekly to adjust the I.P dose for all the experimental period. I.P was chosen as it is ethically approved and guarantees quick absorption of all compounds to accurately assess their capacities, as it is proven to deliver the medication directly into the abdominal cavity, which offers higher bioavailability, faster onset of action and protection of agents from gastrointestinal degradation47.

Blood samples

Blood samples were collected one day after the end of the experiment. Post-experimental procedure via the retro-orbital vein, a method that facilitates multiple blood draws from the same animal with minimal stress, specifically through orbital puncture from the eye plexus using heparinized micro-hematocrit capillary tubes. The blood samples were clotted at room temperature, and serum was obtained through centrifugation at 3000 rpm for 10 min. The serum samples were collected from blood as previously mentioned without EDTA for various biochemical parameters, including liver functions and lipid profiles, and stored at − 80 °C until analyses. Liver tissues were excised, rinsed with ice-cold saline, and partitioned for different analyses, including tissue homogenate preparation and molecular studies. The remaining samples were fixed in 10% neutral buffered formalin for histopathological examination.

Evaluation of liver functions and lipid profile indices

All kits of the measured parameters were measured using SPINREACT Kits (SPAIN) for lipid profile parameters (TC and TG), and kits from (Human Gesellschaft fṺr Biochemica und Diagnostica mbH Max-Planck-Ring 21–65,205 Wiesbaden-Germany) for measurement of liver function (ALT, AST and LDH), antioxidant enzymes (SOD, CAT, and GSH), and non-enzymatic marker (MDA). The following parameters are measured using GENESYS 10S UV–Vis spectrophotometer (China).

Liver function parameters

Determination of serum aminotransferase enzymes activities

All these enzymes were determined based on the manufacturer’s brochure. Alanine aminotransferase (ALT), which is a cellular enzyme, is found in the highest concentration in liver and kidney. its better application is in the diagnosis of the diseases of the liver. ALT catalyses the reversible transfer of an amino group from alanine to α-ketoglutarate, forming glutamate and piruvate. The piruvate produced is reduced to lactate-by-lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) and NADH. The rate of decrease in concentration of NADH, is proportional to the catalytic concentration of ALT.

Aspartate aminotransferase (AST) is a cellular enzyme, is found in highest concentration in heart muscle, the cells of the liver, the cells of the skeletal muscle. AST catalyses the reversible transfer of an amino group from aspartate to α-ketoglutarate, forming glutamate and oxalacetate. The oxalacetate produced is reduced to malate by NADH. The rate of decrease in concentration of NADH is proportional to the catalytic concentration of AST.

Determination of serum lactate dehydrogenase (LDH)

Serum LDH activity was assessed by using LDH kit (GmbH Schigraben, Hannover, Germany). Lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) is an enzyme with wide tissue distribution in the body. The higher concentrations of LDH are found in the liver, heart, kidney, skeletal muscle and erythrocytes. Lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) catalyses the reduction of pyruvate by NADH; the rate of decrease in concentration of NADPH is proportional to the catalytic concentration of LDH.

Determination of lipid profile

The serum total cholesterol (TC) was determined based on the concept of hydrolysis to free cholesterol and fatty acids by cholesterol esterase; the free cholesterol is then oxidized by cholesterol oxidase to cholestene-3-one, with the production of H2O2, H2O2 together with 4-aminoantiperine and phenol, yields chromagen with maximum absorbance at 500 nm, and triglycerides (TG) were determined according to48 based on the scientific concept of reaction of Glycerol and fatty acids which are formed by lipase action on the TG, then Glycerol is phosphorylated by adenosine-5- diphosphate in a reaction which is catalyzed by glycerol kinase. High-density lipoprotein–cholesterol (HDL–c) was determined based on49. Serum low-density lipoprotein–cholesterol (LDL–c) level was determined based on50 using the following formula: LDL-c = ¼ Total cholesterol levels– (Triglyceride concentration/5) – HDL-c concentration, meanwhile VLDL-c = triglyceride/5.

Calculations of risk ratio based on lipid parameters

Risk ratio (I) = (Total Cholesterol/HDL-c) ratio. (Ideal < 3.5, good < 5, Bad > 5).

Risk ratio (II) = (LDL-c/HDl-c) ratio. (Most popular risk factor of heart diseases).

Risk ratio (III) = (TG/HDl-c) ratio. (Higher values could indicate a higher risk of metabolic syndromes).

Preparation of liver tissue homogenate for antioxidant and oxidative stress markers

The liver tissues were washed with a BPS (Buffered-phosphate saline) PH = 7.4 with heparin. The hepatic tissues were homogenized in approximately 5–10 ml of cold buffer to eliminate any fats, debris or any blood cells and then centrifuged at 5000 r.p.m for about 15 min at 4̊ C. Then, the supernatants were immediately removed for antioxidant enzymes and oxidative stress analyses and stored on cold ice cubic, then frozen at “−80̊ C” until use.

Estimation of antioxidant and oxidative stress markers

All antioxidant enzyme markers were assessed by using the supernatants of the hepatic tissue homogenates. All kits used were obtained from (Human Gesellschaft fṺr Biochemica und Diagnostica mbH Max-Planck-Ring 21–65,205 Wiesbaden-Germany). SOD was determined based on the methods of51 and expressed as (U/g), CAT was expressed as (U/g) based on the methods of52, GSH was estimated based on the method of53.

Determination of oxidative stress biomarkers (Lipid peroxidation assay)

The thiobarbituric acid reactive substances (TBARS) levels as an index of malondialdehyde (MDA) based on the method of54. As MDA is the end product of lipid peroxidation with minimum absorbance at 535 nm.

Gene expression analysis via RNA isolation, cDNA synthesis, and qRT-PCR

Total RNA was extracted from liver tissues using Qiazol Lysis Reagent, following the manufacturer’s guidelines. A NanoDrop spectrophotometer was used to estimate the total RNA concentration. The extracted RNA was then reverse-transcribed into complementary DNA (cDNA) using the cDNA Reverse transcription Kit (Applied Biosystems™, Waltham, USA). During the qRT-PCR analysis, the synthesized cDNA was amplified using specific primers, with their sequences listed in Table 1. The reaction setup and thermal cycling conditions were conducted in a real-time thermal cycler, Rotor-Gene Q (Khamis et al.,2023), with a SYBER Green master mix and a TOPreal™ (Enzynomics, Daejeon, Republic of Korea). Upon Gapdh normalizer gene with other genes were calculated as a detailed scientific percentage from the control group and then the gene expression levels were normalized, and the fold change was estimated as 2 − ΔΔCT (Schmittgen and Livak.,2008).

Immunoplot analysis of cellular cytochrome C levels

Liver tissue homogenates (50 μg/well) from various treated groups were analyzed using Biorad gel electrophoresis. Tissues were lysed with RIPA buffer containing protease and phosphatase inhibitors, then agitated and sonicated for cell lysis. The lysates were centrifuged at 16,000 × g for 20 min at 4 °C, and the supernatant was collected. Protein content was assessed using the BCA method, and minute sample of protein was mixed with Laemmli sample buffer and boiled before a final centrifugation.

Protein separation by SDS-PAGE

Equal amounts of protein (20–50 μg) were loaded into the wells of 12–15% mini SDS- PAGE gel. The gels were run for (5–10) min at 90 V for the stacking gel and at (100–150 V) for the separating gel.

Transferring the protein from the gel to the membrane

The gels were incubated in 1 × transfer buffer for 10–15 min, then assembled into a transfer sandwich while avoiding air bubbles for electro-transfer onto a PVDF membrane (0.2 μm SIGMA). The blot was positioned on the cathode and the gel on the anode, with the transfer tank containing an ice block to prevent incidence of the overheating. The transfer was conducted at 100 V for 30 min to 1 h, depending on the percentage of SDS-PAGE used.

Antibodies incubation

The membrane was blocked in 5% skimmed milk in TBST at room temperature for 1 h. The membrane was incubated with the indicated primary antibodies solution diluted in 5% BSA in TBST at 4 °C. The membrane was washed 3 times for 5 min each with TBST. The membrane was incubated with HRP-conjugated secondary antibody diluted in % skimmed milk in TBST for 1 h at room temperature. The membrane was washed again 3 times for 5 min each with TBST.

Imaging and data analysis

Bands were visualized with the enhanced chemiluminescent (ECL/ThermoScientific) substrate according to the manufacturer’s recommendation. Chemiluminescence was detected using the Biorad System. Image analysis software was used to quantify the band intensities of the target proteins.

Histopathological investigation

Histopathology was performed using small specimens of the liver tissues that were collected directly after dissection and immediately transferred to a 10% neutral buffered formalin. Then, the specimens were washed, dehydrated in ascending grades of alcohol, cleared in xylene, and embedded in paraffin. After making paraffin-embedded hepatic blocks, they were cut into 5 µm thin sections using a digital microtome according to Bancroft55. For staining, it was done by use of hematoxylin and eosin.

Blinding procedures in histopathology were carried out, involving keeping the pathologist unaware of the treatment group status during the histologic slide evaluation phase, only numbering the slides to ensure the compatibility of serum results and histological investigations. The scoring criteria are based on focusing on the low-power magnification, firstly to compare all the different group fields and to be sure of the histological features in each group separately, and based on the counting method using a mark drawing to confirm the features appeared for accurate comment for each field. Histological activity index (HAI) was estimated according to the degree of the microscopic lesions56 regarding (Central vein, no hepatocyte degeneration, Regular rows of hepatocytes, inflammation or congestion, hepatocyte necrosis, inflammatory cell infiltration, congested central vein, bile duct proliferation).

Statistical analysis

The results are provided as the means ± S.E. The data were analysed using one-way ANOVA. To determine the significance of each treatment effect, post hoc Tukey’s analysis was used for comparisons. All statistical analyses were performed utilising the statistical software package SPSS version 20 (SPSS Inc., USA). Histology data were analysed based on the sum of the scores: overall score, using the Kruskal–Wallis test in SPSS. The criterion for statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

Antioxidant enzymes and oxidative stress markers

Discussion

Metals are among the earliest known toxins that can adversely affect human health. They can enter the body via environmental routes, such as food and drinking water, or soil, air and water. Metal-induced toxicity adversely impacts public health. CD is a heavy metal that threatens the health of all living beings. It is extensively used in many industries. Cigarette smoking exposes the lungs to this metal. CD increases the production of ROS, elevating oxidative stress, damaging biomolecules, including proteins and DNA, impairing their repair, causing oxidant/antioxidant imbalance and, as a result of the disruption of lipid, carbohydrate and protein metabolism, leading to cell dysfunction or death59.

Recent research suggests that almost a third of the body’s CD accumulates within the soft tissues55. CD creates oxidative stress, which impairs cell activity44. It possibly acts through lipid peroxidation and consequently inhibits protein synthesis and disrupts the metabolism of lipids, carbohydrates and amino acids. CD can inhibit DNA repair and methylation processes, thus increasing the risks of mutations60,61.

It has a high potential to interact with ions such as Ca, Zn, Cu and Fe, which results in pathological effects55,62. CD may induce apoptosis and renal impairment by impacting transport pathways and causing mitochondrial oxidative damage55. CRO possesses remarkable clinical properties, such as antiapoptotic63,64, antitumor, anticancer65,66 and potent antioxidant63,64,66,67.

Hepatoprotective drugs are used to mitigate hepatic injury induced by toxic agents such as CD44. The current results confirmed the association between CRO administration as a hepatoprotective agent and CD-induced hepatic toxicity. CRO ameliorated hepatic functions, which may have been impaired due to reduced cell membrane integrity and may be attributed to its structure, as proved by FT-IR, UV–vis, SEM and TEM findings of the current study. It possessed highly potent antioxidant and free-radical scavenging capacities. The results of this study were consistent with those of previous studies, where AST and ALT were elevated with hepatotoxicity induced by other xenobiotics in animal models and were significantly reduced with CRO administration.

In accordance with the present results, our results revealed a remarkable rise in all hepatic enzymes (ALT, AST, AFP, LDH and MDA) levels in the CD group when compared with the control and other groups, in agreement with previous studies of44, that have demonstrated the same findings.

In this study, it was found that Crocin (CRO) administration led to a reduction in elevated AST, ALT, and LDH levels caused by Colchicine-induced damage (CD). CRO acted as a hepatoprotective agent by enhancing hepatic cellular membrane integrity via its potent free radical scavenging capabilities. This demonstrated clearly the hepatoprotective high potency of CRO. These findings were in consistent with a previous study of68 where the AST and ALT levels, which were elevated with hepatotoxicity induced by cadmium metal in animal models, were reduced significantly with CRO administration.

The high changes in the enzymatic activities such as amino transferases (ALT and AST) are considered as vital markers of hepatocellular injuries, and the elevation in ALT and AST enzyme activities indicates current hepatic injury69,70. These facts reflect those of the current findings and may explained that CRO-induced free radicals disrupt polyunsaturated fatty acids in hepatic cellular membranes, particularly phospholipids, resulting in compromised hepatic integrity and increased aminotransferases ALT and AST levels.

These results corroborate the ideas of the current results’ study which confirmed that elevated ALT, AST and LDH enzyme levels were observed in CD administered rat liver cells due to incidence of the hepatic damage. Hepatic enzymes such as ALT, AST, and LDH may be elevated in blood circulation due to oxidative injury leading to destruction of hepatocellular membranes. This observation aligns with other studies where similar enzyme elevations followed CCl4-induced hepatic damage71.

Hepatoprotective drugs are used to decline the hepatic injury induced by the hepatotoxic agents such as CD44. These current results confirmed the association between CRO administration as a hepatoprotective agent and CD-induced hepatic toxicity. Administration of CRO afforded amelioration of the hepatic functions, which may be due to the hepatic cellular membrane integrity by CRO and may be attributed to the chemical structure of CRO as proved in the current study by FT-IR, Uv–vis, SEM and TEM and possessed high potent antioxidant capacities and high capacities of free radical scavenging properties. The current results were highly consistent with the previous findings where the AST and ALT were elevated with hepatotoxicity induced by other xenobiotics in animal models, and were reduced significantly with CRO administration.

Oxidative injury to hepatic tissues is alleviated by enzymatic and non-enzymatic antioxidant systems72. The results of this study corroborate the findings of several previous ones33. It showed that CRO protects hepatic tissues by regulating the antioxidant defence system, particularly through GSH, which neutralises free radicals and reduces H2O2 levels. It supports evidence from reports73,74, which demonstrated that CRO and saffron extracts reversed ischaemia–reperfusion-induced oxidative injury in many rat tissues, consistent with Gedika et al.46.

There are similarities between the attitudes expressed by75,39, respectively, who reported that CRO alleviated acrylamide-elicited neurotoxicity in rats by inhibition of the oxidative stress injury which is similar in attitude of the current study in which CRO alleviated the hepatic toxicity induced by CD.

Additionally, the present study results are in accord with previous studies, which demonstrated that saffron extract and CRO declined the excessive production of free radicals induced by high MDA levels, which is the final marker for lipid peroxidation due to their potent antioxidant properties76. These findings broadly support our current findings in similar attitudes, SOD, CAT, and GSH, which are enzymatic antioxidants, were found at elevated levels in rats treated with acrylamide. Following CRO administration, MDA levels, indicative of lipid peroxidation, along with the other antioxidants, returned to normal values.

The current results are in keeping with previous observational studies in the same concept of77 and39 as they clarified that Saffron and CRO were found to regulate oxidative markers in the rat brain, consistent with findings that SOD and CAT levels significantly increased in rats exposed to acrylamide, while CRO administration reduced these enzymatic activities. Additionally, CRO elevated liver GSH levels and decreased MDA levels in various toxin models in rats.

Consistent with the previous literatures, the current results proved that CRO act as potent antioxidant and markedly enhanced the cellular damage near to the control group. It is encouraging to compare the current results with the previous findings of78 who mentioned that Antioxidants, like CRO, can enhance the conjugation and excretion of toxic substances with GSH from the rat’s body, particularly when paired with a diet rich in these antioxidants, improving vital signs against oxidative injury.

The findings showed that CRO improved hepatocellular membranes against CD-afforded the lipid peroxidation and reversed damage to the hepatic tissues. Toxicity in hepatic tissue caused by xenobiotics had been alleviated with various plant species, including quercetin79 or rosemary80.

The gene expression results of the current study in hepatic tissues are in consistent with previous study of81, which previously confirmed that cadmium could enhance the overexpression level of MMP-3 genes, such as genes’ categories of the current study (mTOR and ATK1), overexpression were also shown in hepatic carcinomas.

Cadmium (Cd) can replace metal atoms like Zn and Cu in metallo-enzymes such as superoxide dismutase (SOD) and in proteins like mTOR and ATK1, which are part of metal-mediated processes. It enhances the excessive production of reactive oxygen species, leading to the overexpression of mTOR and ATK1 by increasing the activity of kinases (e.g., JNK1/2 and Akt) and activating transcription factors such as NF-κB82,]83. clarified that cadmium negatively affects MMP-2 activity, while Saffron extract, rich in CRO, exhibits significant antioxidant properties through mechanisms such as free radical inhibition, SOD-like activity, and reduction of lipid peroxidation84.

Numerous in vitro studies demonstrate the potent anti-tumoral properties of CRO, primarily through its ability to modify microvascular endothelial cell ultrastructure by suppressing oxidative reactions. Additionally, CRO exhibits significant radical scavenging activity, likely due to its sugar moieties, which contribute to its antioxidant properties33.

In vitro studies show that CRO significantly decreases the expression of mitochondrial stress genes. However, the effects of CRO on endoplasmic stress genes remain unclear. Additionally, some research indicates that CRO may suppress IL-1b, leading to the downregulation of both endoplasmic and mitochondrial stress genes85.

To our knowledge, this is the first study to observe the effects of CRO on cytochrome c–based mitochondria-related apoptotic pathway against hepatotoxicity induced by CD via Western blots. The findings confirmed the enhanced release of mitochondria-related cytochrome c from the CD-treated group, which confirmed the high hepatic damage caused by CD and the ameliorative effects of CRO.

In oxidative stress, the amounts of ROS relatively exceed the antioxidant capacity of cells, adversely affecting cell structures and biomolecules, such as nucleic acids and proteins, subsequently leading to various diseases. Several pharmacological agents are being used to enhance the antioxidant defence system. Numerous studies have shown the different pharmacological activities of CRO, including antioxidant and anti-inflammatory effects86.

CRO modulates oxidative injury mostly by scavenging the free radicals and stimulating the high expression of the antioxidant enzymes, including SOD, GPx and CAT, as well as reducing MDA and peroxidised membrane lipid contents20,95. Natural antioxidants such as CRO regulate oxidative damage-induced malfunctions87. Moreover, CRO improved cell dysfunction by downregulating ROS-induced KCa3.1 via the GPx1/ROS/KCa3.1 signalling pathway, suppressing ROS production and endothelial dysfunction88.

The current study proved that CRO ameliorated the CD-induced damage to hepatocyte membranes, relieving oxidative stress and reversing the tissue injury. Tissue toxicity induced by a lot of xenobiotics can be alleviated using various active-derived compounds, such as quercetin89 and herbs, such as rosemary80. Similarly, CRO administration significantly reduced CD-induced MDA elevation. Such an effect was due to its potent antioxidant capacities and free radical scavenging properties25.

Regarding the histological results, CD administration afforded a congested central vein with irregular hepatocytic cords and severe inflammation due to hepatocyte necrosis and inflammatory cell infiltration. Meanwhile, CRO administration alleviated the effects of these injuries by enhancing the formation of normal hepatocytes and reducing inflammation, as had appeared in the histological investigations and by the histological index (HAI). These observations are in great accordance with previous findings46, confirming that CRO alleviated histological alterations induced by acrylamide. CRO-treated liver sections had a normal histological structure. Meanwhile, hepatic congestion and inflammatory cell infiltration were identified within the portal hepatic tissues along with the appearance of necrosis and haemorrhagic areas in the liver parenchyma of the acrylamide-treated group, which was alleviated by CRO administration. These findings greatly corroborate the current ameliorative effects of CRO against CD-induced hepatic structure alterations.

Lesions in the liver, as well as dilation and congestion of the central vein with haemorrhage, were observed in the CD group. These changes were attributed to the hazardous effects of CD on hepatic cords44. These toxic changes were mostly due to the direct effects of heavy metals, such as CD, on the hepatocytes, executed via the bloodstream.

Necrosis can be caused by toxins or heavy metals90; the current results investigated the role of CD in inducing necrosis that developed into hepatic damage. These observations provide substantial evidence for hepatocytic injury induced by CD based on light microscopy images and robustly confirm that CRO treatment significantly reduced hepatic necrosis due to CD administration.

Conclusion

In conclusion, CD caused oxidative injury to hepatic tissues, and CRO can serve as an ameliorative agent against hepatic damage due to its antioxidant properties. Besides normo-lipidemia, AST, ALT, SOD, CAT, LDH activities, AFP and GSH levels were elevated. However, the contents of the lipid peroxidation marker MDA declined, contrary to CD-induced hepatic damage. Closer inspection revealed that in the CD-treated group, mTOR and ATK1 levels were significantly elevated compared to the control group. Meanwhile, beclin, LC3 II and PINK-1 levels declined markedly. Meanwhile, CRO robustly alleviated oxidative stress and improved the status of damaged hepatic tissues via an insignificant change in gene expression levels compared with the control group. Western blots revealed a higher release of cytochrome c from the CD group compared with the CRO-treated group. Thus, this study demonstrated that CRO has strong antioxidant properties and can alleviate hepatotoxicity caused by CD.
