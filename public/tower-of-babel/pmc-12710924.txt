Mild microwave hyperthermia promotes mitotic catastrophe, induces time-delayed cGAS-STING activation and restores sensitivity to anti-PDL1 therapy in Pan02 pancreatic cancer model
Authors: Na Li, Jiamin He, Mengjiao Chen, Shanshan Liang, Chuanhao Zhang, Wei Pan, Qiushi Wang, Fengyuan Piao, Ruoyu Wang, Jian Yu, Guanyu Gong
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTPancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC) responds poorly to immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs), representingindicating an unmet clinical need. Hyperthermia therapy, an approved anti-cancer approach with limited adverse effects, has been suggested to be capable of modulating the tumor microenvironment (TiME). This study examined the potential of microwave hyperthermia (Ht) to restructure the TiME of Pan02 PDAC model and restore Pan02 tumor’s sensitivity to ICIs, followed by mechanistic investigation. In vitro experiments were performed using a 41 °C water bath, and in vivo studies established the Pan02 murine model treated with 41 °C microwave hyperthermia + /– αPD-L1 therapy. In Ht-treated cells, increased percentages of nucleus envelope blebbing, micronucleation, and multi-/macro-nucleation were observed, and live imaging studies suggested mitotic catastrophe. Ht induced cGAS-STING activation, γH2AX formation, dsDNA release, and genomic damage (comet assay) in a time-delayed fashion 24–48 h after Ht. In the Pan02 model, the Ht and αPD-L1 combination significantly repressed the growth of Ht-treated and abscopal tumors and prolonged mouse survival. The TiME was significantly altered after Ht, featuring upregulated infiltration and maturation of DCs, CD8 + T cells, M1 macrophages, and activated cGAS-STING-IFN signaling. The addition of a STING antagonist abolished this tumor-suppressive effect. SUMO1 expression was significantly repressed after Ht, whereas SUMO1 overexpression attenuated Ht-induced cGAS-STING activation. The effect of Ht has also been validated in certain human PDAC cell lines. Taken together, our study suggests that microwave hyperthermia could be an excellent therapeutic companion of ICIs for PDAC management, associated with a novel mechanism of Ht-induced time-delayed DNA damage and STING activation.

Introduction

Pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC) remains one of the most lethal forms of cancer, with one-year survival rate of <30%.1 The lack of response to immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs)2 is not surprising because PDAC features a notorious infertile tumor immune microenvironment (TiME).2,3 TiME lacks immunoactivating cells, such as cytotoxic CD8α + T cells and dendritic cells, to generate an antitumor immune response but contains tremendous immunosuppressive tumor-associated fibroblasts, M2 macrophages, and myeloid-derived suppressor cells.2-4 Therapeutic approaches aimed at breaking down inhibitory components or reviving excitatory components have been investigated.4 For example, anti-tumor vaccines,5,6 TCRT or CART,7-9 nanoparticles targeting repressive immune cells,10,11 or a combination of these,5-11 have been explored for their potential to restructure the TiME. However, most of these approaches are only tested in preclinical models and require individualized designation, which is costly. It is worthwhile to evaluate the potential of other approaches that are more easily assessable in clinical settings to re-sensitize PDAC to immunotherapy.

Hyperthermia (Ht) treatment is a physiotherapy approach that heats target tissues (generally using microwaves or radiofrequency for energy delivery) to levels above 39 °C. In practice, hyperthermia is between 39–45°C, and mild hyperthermia at 39–41°C is better tolerated by patients.12,13 Elevated temperature is known to cause protein degradation and activate the heat-shock response,14,15 but its biological consequences remain insufficiently explored. Ht has been suggested to boost the immune system by promoting MHC-mediated antigen presentation and enhancing the release of proinflammatory cytokines and damage-associated molecular pattern (DAMP) molecules, such as HMGB1, ATP, and calreticulin.15-18 Nonetheless, the centerpiece of Ht's multifaceted roles remains to be explored.

Early researchers reported an interesting correlation between Ht and mitotic errors. Grzanka et al heated CHO-AA8 cells at 44.5 °C for 30 min and observed the number of multi-nucleated cells being persistently increased days later.19 Pawlik et al. found that in H1299 cells, augmented multi-nucleated cells after heat shock led to decreased viability of cells with p53-deficiency.20 Nakahata et al. reported that various diploid non-cancer cells and aneuploid cancer cells, including MCF7, T24, HT180 and HeLa, displayed multi-axis division, centromere dysfunction, and micronucleation after Ht,21 which phenomena seemed more profound in cancer cells than in normal cells.21 However, the downstream effect of mitotic error (ME) after heat shock has not been explored.

With an intact Wee1/CDC25 checkpoint, cells bearing mitotic errors would undergo abnormal separation, G2/M arrest, mitotic death, and senescence, and the term “mitotic catastrophe” describes the most severe scenario.22 Meanwhile, micronucleation and multinucleation are generated and release genomic dsDNA into the cytoplasm, which could potentially activate the cyclic GMP-AMP Synthase (cGAS) and stimulator of interferon genes (STING) signaling pathway.23,24 This would increase the release of interferon-β (IFNβ), which promotes DC maturation and CD8 + priming, bridging innate immunity and adaptive immunity for anti-tumor responses.25 The importance of cGAS-STING has been demonstrated in various studies of radiotherapy or DDR inhibitors, in which STING activation converted cold TiME to warm TiME26,27 and enhanced responsiveness to ICIs.26,27 In the same way, whether Ht might also activate cGAS-STING signaling and be used with ICIs for cancer management and through what mechanism remains underexplored, but research interest in this topic is increasing.28,29

In the present study conducted during the years 2022–2025, the potential connection between Ht-induced mitotic errors and cGAS-STING activation was explored using a cell culture-based hyperthermia model. The subcutaneous Pan02 mouse model of PDAC was used to evaluate the potential of microwave hyperthermia to synergize with anti-PDL1 therapy and its impact on the TiME. A mechanistic study revealed that dysregulated protein SUMOylation could be associated with Ht-mediated STING activation. Our findings provide fundamental evidence for the integration of hyperthermia with immunotherapy for PDAC management.

Materials and methods

Chemical reagents and antibodies

RIPA buffer was purchased from Thermo Scientific (Carlsbad, CA, USA). H2B-GFP plasmids of human and murine origin were purchased from Miaoling Biotech (Wuhan, China). The transfection reagents Lipofectamine 2000TM and JetPRIMETM were purchased from Invitrogen (Carlsbad, CA, USA) and Polyplus (Illkirch, France). The following antibodies were used for western blotting and immunostaining: anti-human cGAS (Cell Signaling), anti-mouse cGAS (SantaCruz), STING (Novus Biological), phospho-IRF3 Ser-386 (Cell Signaling), phospho-IRF3 Ser-396 (Cell Signaling), IRF-3 (SantaCruz), phospho-TBK1 Ser172 (Cell Signaling), TBK1 (SantaCruz), phospho-H2A.X Ser139 (Cell Signaling), PD-L1 (Cell Signaling), CD11c (Cell Signaling), CD8α (Cell Signaling), F4/80 (Proteintech), CD163 (Proteintech), CD86 (Novus Biologicals), FoxP3 (Cell Signaling), Gr1 (Invitrogen), GranzymeB (Invitrogen), Tim3 (Invitrogen), phospho-STAT1 Tyr701 (Cell Signaling), and IFNβ, CCL5, CXCL10, CD56, MICA, HMGB1, SUMO1, SUMO2/3, UBC9, SAE1, UBA2, SENP3, Beta-Actin (all from Proteintech). Details are provided in Supplemental Table 1.

Cell lines

The murine Pan02 cell line was obtained from the Cell Resource Center, Peking Union Medical College, and cultivated in Dulbecco's modified Eagle's medium (DMEM) supplemented with 5% fetal bovine serum (FBS). The human MIA-Paca2, BxPC3, PANC1, and HPAF-II cell lines were obtained from the Shanghai National Collection of Authenticated Cell Cultures and grown in DMEM with 10% FBS under standard conditions of 37 °C, 5% CO2, and 95% humidity.

Hyperthermia treatment of cultured cells

The cells were subsequently grown in 6-well plates to reach 50%–60% subconfluency. The plate was sealed in a UV-sterilized plastic bag, heated in a water bath at 41 °C for 1 h, harvested immediately, or transferred back to an incubator for an additional 12, 24, 36, or 48 h. To ensure the accuracy of the temperature, a separate plate was loaded with an equal amount of medium, placed in the same water bath, and coupled with a K-type thermocouple detector (probe immersed in medium) to monitor the temperature in real time (Figure 1A and Figure S1A).

Live cell imaging

Live cell imaging was performed using an BioTek Cytation 5 Cell Imaging Multimode Reader (Agilent). Stably transfected H2B-GFP-expressing Pan02 or HPAF-II cells were seeded in 6-well plates with phenol red-free medium and placed in a minichamber for imaging. For each experiment, 20–25 regions of interest (ROIs) were selected, and images were captured every 3 min continuously for 24 or 48 h. Bright-field images of untransfected cells were captured.

Determination of micronucleated and multinucleated cell frequencies

Cells grown on coverslips were collected 0–48 h after Ht, fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde, and immunostained with Lamin A/C antibody (Cell Signaling Technology). Images were captured at 400× using an Olympus IX70/ON2 microscope (Tokyo, Japan) coupled with a Zeiss camera. The presence of abnormal nuclear morphology was manually determined in consensus by two experimenters under blinded conditions. A micronucleated cell was defined as any cell containing paranucleus DNA aggregates separated from the primary nucleus, excluding apoptotic cells. A multinucleated cell was defined as any cell carrying a fused multilobular nucleus. A macronucleated cell was defined as any cell carrying a nucleus >2 times that of the original cell. The percentage of micro-/multi-/macro-nucleated cells was normalized to the total number of cells analyzed using ImageJ 2.0. A total of 20–30 images per coverslip and 5–6 coverslips from three experiments were included for analysis.

Determination of cytosolic dsDNA concentration

Cells were Ht-treated and harvested 0–48 h later. Half of the cells were used to count the total number. The other half was pelleted and resuspended in 100 µl of hypotonic buffer (20 mM HEPES, 2 mM EDTA, 2 mM MgCl2, pH 7.4). The cells were incubated on ice for 30 min and centrifuged at 800 × g for 5 min at 4 °C. The supernatant was centrifuged at 17,000 × g at 4 °C for 30 min to remove the mitochondria and obtain the cytosolic fraction. The dsDNA concentration was determined using the QubitTM 1× dsDNA HS assay kit (Invitrogen), read on a QubitTM 3.0 fluorometer (Thermo Fisher) and normalized to the total cell number (n × 104 cells).

Comet assay

DNA damage was evaluated using Comet assay. Briefly, untreated and Ht-treated cells were embedded in 0.5% low-melting point agarose (LMPA) on top of 0.5% normal-melting point agarose (NMPA), incubated in lysis buffer containing 1% Triton X-100, 2.5 mM NaCl, 0.1 mM Na2EDTA, and 10 mM Tris, pH 10, for 2 h at 4 °C, electrophresized in buffer containing 1 mM EDTA and 300 mM sodium hydroxide (pH 13.0), washed in naturalization buffer containing 0.4 M Tris-HCl, pH 7.5, at 4 °C, and stained with propidium iodide. Images (200×) were analyzed using CaspLab software, and the data are expressed as the percentage of tail moment from 120 to 150 cells per sample.

Subcutaneous Pan02 tumor model and combination treatment of hyperthermia and αPD-L1

The animal experiments were approved by the Ethical Committee of the Affiliated Zhongshan Hospital of Dalian University, China, and followed the international ARRIVE guidelines. Six-week-old female immunocompetent C57BL/6J mice were purchased from Changsheng Biotechnology (Liaoning, China) and maintained under routine husbandry conditions (R.T., ad libitum feeding). A total of 5 × 105 Pan02 cells were injected into the subcutaneous areas of the bilateral hips, randomly assigning one side for Ht treatment and another for observing the abscopal effect. After the tumors reached ~100 mm3, the mice were randomly (simple randomisation method) assigned to four treatment groups: control, Ht, αPD-L1, Ht+αPD-L1 (combine), each group containing 8 animals (2 cages) (total number = 32). The number per group was determined to satisfy the requirements for statistical analysis. αPD-L1 mAb (BioXcell, 10F.9G2) was intraperitoneally injected (10 mg/kg) on days 0, 3, 6, and 9. For Ht treatment, each mouse was placed in a restrainer with hind limb access, the hairs were removed, and a microwave physiotherapy device (WB-3200A, Aorui, Jiangsu, China) with a 79 mm × ⌀15 mm probe locoregionally heated tumors to 41 °C (45 mins, on days 1, 3, 5, 7, 9) under anesthesia using 1% isoflurane inhalation (with a vaporizer). A K-type thermocouple detector was attached to the skin to monitor the temperature (Figures 1B and S1B). Anti-PD-L1 injection was given before hyperthermia because mAbs need time to redistribute to subcutaneous tumors.30 The mice were observed daily for death, excessive pain, and weight loss for humanized termination (one in the Ht group was excluded for early death for no obvious reason). The tumor volume was calculated as 0.5 * a * b2 (a: longest length, b: shortest length), measured by three students under blinded conditions every 2 d (Figure 3A). Two weeks after the completion of treatment, the mice were euthanized using isoflurane overdose (20% isoflurane with a vaporizer) followed by cervical dislocation. The primary outcome was assessed using the sizes and weights of the tumors and spleens (Figures 3B−E, 6B, S2, and S4).

Sn-011 treatment

The STING antagonist Sn-011 was used in separate experiment including the untreated control, Ht+αPD-L1, and Ht+αPD-L1 + Sn011 groups, with each group containing 6 mice (one cage) (total number = 18). Sn011 (5 mg/kg in 100 µl of corn oil (Selleck)) was administered by intraperitoneal injection before Ht treatment, and the schedule for treatment was as described above (Figure 6A).

Re-injection of Pan02 cells

The experiment included 12 mice, six (1 cage) in the control group and six in the Ht+αPD-L1 group. Subcutaneous Pan02 tumor was injected only on one side and untreated or treated with Ht+αPD-L1. Two weeks after completion of treatment, the mice received another injection on the contralateral side and were observed for two more weeks for the prevalence and growth of any tumors at the re-injection sites.

Intraperitoneal Pan02 implantation model

The mice were assigned to four groups: control, Ht, αPD-L1, and αPD-L1+Ht, each containing 5 mice (1 cage) (total 20). The mice received a subcutaneous injection of Pan02 cells on one side and an intraperitoneal injection of 1 × 105 Pan02 cells and were treated as previously described. Two weeks after treatment, the mice were euthanized, and the intraperitoneal tumors were harvested and analyzed.

Immunostaining

Immunohistochemical or immunofluorescence staining of markers of infiltrating immune cells and cGAS-STING activation was performed using those antibodies described in Supplemental Table 1. Deparaffinized tissue sections were subjected to antigen retrieval using citrate buffer (pH 6.0) or EDTA buffer (pH 9.0) at 95 °C for 20 min, blocked with 5% bovine serum albumin (BSA) and blotted with primary and secondary antibodies. Signals were acquired using the chromogenic DAB and Dylight-488 and/or Dylight-549 fluorophores. Images were captured using an IX70/ON2 microscope (Olympus, Tokyo, Japan) coupled with a Zeiss camera. For morphometric quantification, 12–20 400× images were captured per tissue, and the data are expressed as the average number of positive cells per high-power field (HPF).

Immunocytochemistry

Cells grown on coverslips were fixed using 4% paraformaldehyde at R.T. or 100% pre-cooled ethanol at −20 °C, permeabilized using 0.3% Triton X-100, blocked with 3% BSA, stained with primary antibodies (described in Supplemental Table 1), and secondary antibodies and counter-stained with DAPI (0.5 µg/ml).

Western blotting

Total protein lysates were extracted using RIPA buffer (Thermo Fisher) containing proteinase inhibitor and phosphatase inhibitor cocktails (Promega). After boiling at 95 °C for 5 min, 20 or 40 µg of lysates were electrophoresed in SDS–PAGE gel, transferred to PVDF membrane (Bio-Rad), blocked with 5% milk and 0.05% Tween-20 in TBS, and incubated with primary antibodies and HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies (Supplemental Table 1). The membranes were developed using Amersham™ ECL Select™ reagent (Cytiva, US) and imaged using the ChemiDocTM MP system (Bio-Rad).

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)

Human and murine IFNβ ELISA kits were purchased from Sangon (Shanghai, China). The culture medium of Ht-treated cells was collected 0–48 h post Ht, centrifuged at 1000 × g and 4 °C for 20 min. The ELISA plates were loaded with 100 µl of medium or recombinant IFNβ standards at 37 °C for 90 min, biotinylated IFNβ antibody at 37 °C for 60 min, HRP-conjugated streptavidin at 37 °C for 30 min, and colorimetric detection reagent at 37 °C for 15 min, and the data were read on an iMark plate-reader (Bio-Rad) at 450 nm.

Statistical analysis

All the data are expressed as mean ± standard deviation. Statistical analysis was performed using the Mann‒Whitney U test, which was performed using GraphPad Prism, version 9.4.0 (La Jolla, CA, USA). Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

Results

Hyperthermia promotes mitotic errors in PDAC cells

To test whether Ht might promote the occurrence of mitotic errors,19-21 we performed Lamin A/C staining on Ht-treated Pan02 cells 0–48 h post Ht and analyzed the percentages of cells carrying micronucleation and multi/macro-nucleation. As shown in Figure 1C‒F, the percentage of micronucleated cells increased by 2-fold from ~4% to ~8%–10%, multi-/macro-nucleated cells increased 1.5-fold from ~4% to ~6%–8%, and cells carrying nuclear envelope blebbing increased 5-fold from ~0.5% to ~2.7%, respectively, before and 0‒48 h after Ht. This increase was more profound 12–48 h later rather than immediately after treatment.

DAPI staining was performed in parallel on Pan02 and human MIA-Paca-2 cells for micronucleated and multinucleated/macronucleated cell quantification. As shown in Figure S1C−E, the results of the DAPI-stained Pan02 cells were similar to our results shown in Figure 1C–F. The percentages of micronucleated and multinucleated Paca2 cells increased from ~1.4% to ~2.7% and from ~0.3% to ~0.6%, respectively, after Ht (Figure S1F–H). Increased mitotic errors in PDAC cells appeared to persist beyond 48 h post Ht.

Live imaging of H2B-GFP transfected cells indicates occurrence of mitotic catastrophe

To clarify whether Ht-treated cells would undergo mitotic catastrophe, Pan02 and HPAF-II cells were transfected with H2B-GFP plasmids, and live imaging was used to observe the dynamic changes in cell mitosis. As shown in Figure 1G, H and Supplemental movies 1–6, compared with untreated cells, the Ht-treated Pan02 cells showed relatively normal mitotic activity during the first 0–24 h post Ht, except that micronuclei were significantly more prevalent during mitosis. During 24–48 h post Ht, the Ht-treated cells were difficult to complete division. Typically, the cells appeared to undergo nuclear material condensation but failed to complete nucleus separation, in which the nuclei either de-condensed and returned to pre-mitotic status, or shrank and dispersed, resulting in cell death. To ensure that the halt of mitosis was not associated with the cytotoxicity of the H2B-GFP protein, live imaging of untransfected cells was captured under bright-field. The results confirmed that Ht-treated cells nearly stopped separating 24–48 h post Ht (Supplemental movies 7–8).

Similarly, live imaging of H2B-GFP-transfected HPAF-II cells also revealed an increased percentage of cells carrying micronucleus 0–24 h post Ht and increased apoptosis 0–24 h and 24–48 h post Ht, indicating mitotic catastrophe (Figure 1I, J, Supplemental movie 9–14). Our results suggest that Ht induces mitotic catastrophe in PDAC cells.

Hyperthermia promotes DNA damage and cGAS-STING activation in a time-delayed fashion

The mitotic-catastrophic cells might separate under replication stress and release unseparated chromatin debris as micronuclei,31 which are expected to rupture and release cytosolic chromatin fragments to activate cGAS-STING signaling.23,24 The concentrations of cytosolic dsDNA in Pan02 and HPAF-II cells were measured and found to be significantly increased 12–48 h post Ht (Figure 2A, B). Consistently, cGAS-STING activation was observed in Ht-treated Pan02 cells via western blotting (Figure 2C) and immunofluorescence (pIRF3 translocalization, Figure 2D) 24–48 h after Ht. The protein expression levels of cGAS and STING proteins were also increased. While increased mitotic errors have been suggested to promote γH2AX accumulation,23,24 elevated γH2AX levels were observed 24–48 h post-Ht (Figure 2C and 2E). The comet assay results confirmed increased DNA damage 12–48 h post Ht (Figure 2F). The ELISA results showed increased IFNβ production in the culture medium 24–48 h post Ht (Figure 2G). Using the CCK8 assay, our study demonstrated that 41 °C Ht did not inhibit the growth of PDAC cells (Figure 2H). Taken together, the results of our in vitro study demonstrated that hyperthermia increased DNA damage and cytosolic dsDNA and activated cGAS-STING signaling in Pan02 cells in a time-delayed manner, with major changes occur 24–48 h, but not immediately, post Ht. Ht was also found not to significantly alter PD-L1 expression (Figure 2C).

Hyperthermia synergizes with αPD-L1 mAb to inhibit growth of subcutaneous Pan02 tumor

Recent studies have suggested that cGAS-STING signaling activation crucially promotes the sensitivity of tumors to immune checkpoint inhibitors.23-27 We next investigated whether Ht could synergize with αPD-L1 therapy via STING activation. Our results showed that microwave hyperthermia at 41 °C re-sensitized Pan02 subcutaneous tumor to αPD-L1 treatment (Figure 3A−D). Compared with those in the untreated or αPD-L1-treated groups, the tumors in the Ht+αPD-L1 group, on both the Ht-treated and the abscopal side, demonstrated significantly reduced tumor growth and lower tumor weights upon harvest (Figure 3B, C, Figure S2A). Ht alone did not inhibit tumor growth, possibly because 41 °C Ht has no direct tumoricidal effect (Figure 2H). Spleen weight was significantly lower in the Ht+αPD-L1 group than in the other groups, suggesting that the immunosuppressive status was ameliorated32 (Figure S2B,C). To confirm that tumor inhibition was due to anti-tumor immune response, Pan02 cells were re-inoculated to mice that received no previous treatment (control) or Ht+αPD-L1 treatment. A follow-up of 2 weeks showed that all (6/6) mice in the control group, but only 2/6 mice in the Ht+αPD-L1 group, developed recognizable tumors in the re-inoculation sites (Figure 3E). The tumors in smaller size of the 2/6 mice in Ht+αPD-L1 group may be due to immune evasion associated with tumor heterogeneity (Figure 3E).

Peritoneal carcinomatosis is a common complication of PDAC. It is important to clarify whether Ht+αPD-L1 therapy could limit the growth of intraperitoneal PDAC tumors. Mice were inoculated with subcutaneous tumors receiving Ht/αPD-L1/Ht+αPD-L1 treatment or with intraperitoneal tumors by i.p. injection of Pan02 cells. As shown in Figure S2D−E, the numbers and weights of the intraperitoneal tumors in the different groups were similar, suggesting no inhibition by Ht+αPD-L1, an unexpected result possibly related to the unique TiME of peritoneal carcinomatosis.

Hyperthermia promotes infiltration and activation of dendritic cell and CD8 + T cells

We next characterized the changes in immune cell infiltration and activation in the different treatment groups. As shown in Figure 4A–D, the numbers of CD11c + dendritic cells and CD8α + T cells were significantly higher in the Ht group (Ht-treated side) and the Ht+αPD-L1 group (Ht-treated side and abscopal side) than in the control and αPD-L1 groups. The proportions of CD11c + CD86 + mature dendritic cells and CD8 + Granzyme B + (reactive) T cells were also significantly greater in the Ht+αPD-L1 group compared with the other groups. The number of F4/80 + CD86 + M1-type macrophages was significantly upregulated in Ht-treated (Ht side) and Ht+αPD-L1-treated (Ht side and abscopal side) animals (Figure S3A), whereas the numbers of CD163 + M2-type macrophages, CD11b + Gr1 + myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs), and FoxP3 + regulatory T cells showed no difference (Figure S3B–D). The number of CD56 + NK cells and the expression level of MICA showed no difference (Figure S3E,F). These results suggest that Ht/Ht+αPD-L1 treatment modulates the TiME by re-populating and reviving the immune-activating cells, but not by inhibiting immunosuppressive cells.

(A–D) Representative images and quantification results of CD11c, CD11c + CD86, CD8α, and CD8α + GranzymeB immunofluorescent stained tissue sections of untreated, αPD-L1 mAb-treated, Ht-treated, and Ht + αPD-L1 mAb-treated tumors (treatment side and abscopal side).

Hyperthermia activates the type I interferon response

Next, the activation status of the cGAS-STING-IFN-I signaling was studied. cGAS and STING protein expression was increased in Ht-treated and Ht+αPD-L1-treated tumors (Figure 5A, B). The number of pSTAT1-positive cells and IFNβ expression were greater in the Ht+αPD-L1 group (Figure 5C, D). The expression levels of the interferon-stimulated genes CCL5 and CXCL10 (which are chemoattractant cytokines of dendritic cells and CD8 + T cells, respectively) were increased in the Ht and Ht+αPD-L1 groups (Figure 5E, F). These results suggest an enhanced interferon response in TiME after Ht. The expression level of the DAMP molecule HMGB1 showed no change in Ht and Ht+αPD-L1 groups (Figure 5G), possibly because 41 °C Ht was too mild to damage the cells. The number of γH2AX-positive cells was higher in Ht and Ht+PD-L1-treated tumors (treatment side) (Figure 5H). The expression levels of PDL1 did not differ between the untreated and Ht/αPD-L1/Ht+αPD-L1-treated groups (Figure 5I). Together, our findings confirmed that Ht activates cGAS-STING signaling in Pan02 tumor tissues.

(A–I) Representative images of cGAS, STING, pSTAT1, IFNβ, CCL5, CXCL10, HMGB1, PD-L1, and γH2AX immunofluorescent or immunohistochemical stained tissue sections, respectively, of untreated, αPD-L1 mAb-treated, Ht-treated and Ht+αPD-L1 mAb-treated tumors (treatment side and abscopal side).

Addition of Sn011 abolishes tumor-suppressive effect by Ht+αPD-L1

To prove the indispensable role of STING activation in Ht-mediated immunoregulation, an experiment was carried out in which Pan02 tumors were treated with Ht+αPD-L1 and the STING antagonist Sn011. As shown in Figure 6A−C and Figure S4, Sn011 completely abolished the tumor-inhibitory effect of Ht+αPD-L1 treatment. Immunostaining results showed that the upregulation of CD11c + DC and CD8 + T cell infiltration by Ht+αPD-L1 treatment was cancelled in the Ht+αPD-L1 + Sn011 group (Figure 6D, E).

Sn011 treatment (5 mg/kg, dissolved in coin oil) by i.p. injection was administered prior to Ht treatment; (B) Growth curve of control, Ht+αPD-L1 treated, and Ht+αPD-L1 + Sn011 treated tumors, showing both the treatment side and abscopal side; (C) Survival probability curve of mice in different treatment groups; (D) and (E) Representative images and quantification results of CD11c and CD8α immunofluorescent stained tissue sections of untreated (control), Ht+αPD-L1 treated, and Ht+αPD-L1 + Sn011 treated tumors.

Sumoylation participates in cGAS-STING activation by hyperthermia

Recent publications have reported an important correlation between dysregulated protein SUMOylation and mitotic error-induced cGAS-STING activation,33,34 revealing that SUMOylation, as a critical post-translational modification for nuclear proteins, regulates chromatin integrity.35,36 We questioned whether SUMOylation is involved in hyperthermia-induced mitotic catastrophe and STING activation. The western blot results showed that SUMO1 expression underwent a delayed but prolonged decrease 12−48 h post Ht in Pan02 cells (Figure 7A). The other SUMOylation-related proteins, including SUMO2/3, SENP3, SAE1, SAE2, and UBC9, showed no change. Immunostaining confirmed that SUMO1 expression was decreased in Pan02 and HPAF-II cells after Ht (Figure 7B and Figure S5). Our results suggest that hyperthermia may cause substrate-level reduction of protein SUMOylation in PDAC cells.

(A) Expression levels of signaling proteins involved in protein sumoylation by western blot in untreated and Ht-treated Pan02 cells (0–48 h post Ht); (B) Representative images of SUMO1 and SUMO2/3 immunofluorescent staining, respectively, of untreated and Ht-treated (24 h post Ht) Pan02 cells; (C) Kaplan-Meier analysis of overall survival in patients with PDAC showing prognostic value of SUMO1 expression (based on PAAD dataset in TCGA database); (D) Comparison of SUMO1 transcript expression in matched TCGA PAAD data and GTEx data; (E) Comparison of SUMO1 transcript expression in different stages of PAAD (based on TCGA data); (F) Correlation analysis of SUMO1 transcript expression with G2/M checkpoint, P53, DNA repair and DNA replication signaling pathway; (G) Expression levels of SUMO1 by western blot in untransfected, empty vector (pcDNA3.1 (+))-transfected and SUMO1-overexpressing plasmid-transfected Pan02 cells; (H) Expression levels of signaling proteins involved in cGAS-STING activation, as well as PD-L1 and γH2AX by western blot in untreated and Ht-treated SUMO1-overexpressing Pan02 cells (0–48 h post Ht).

Data analysis of the TCGA database suggested that SUMO1 expression significantly affects the overall survival of PDAC patients, with lower expression conferring a survival advantage (Figure 7C). SUMO1 is overexpressed in PDAC tumors compared with normal pancreatic tissue, but showed no difference in different stages (Figure 7D, E). SUMO1 expression is significantly correlated with multiple pathways, including the G2/M checkpoint, DNA repair, DNA replication, and p53 pathways (Figure 7F). To confirm the involvement of SUMO1 in Ht-induced MC and STING activation, we stably overexpressed SUMO1 in Pan02 cells and found that SUMO1-overexpressing cells showed no γH2AX accumulation or cGAS-STING activation 24–48 h post Ht (Figure 7G, H).

Hyperthermia activates cGAS-STING signaling in some but not all human PDAC cells

Next, cGAS-STING activation was examined in human PDAC cells 0–48 h post Ht. As shown in Fig. S6, Ht caused time-delayed cGAS-STING activation in PANC1 and HPAF-II cells, but not in BxPC3 and MIA-Paca2 cells. Time-delayed γH2AX accumulation was observed in HPAF-II, PANC1, and MIA-Paca2 cells, but not in BXPC3 cells. These results suggest that the described effect of Ht exists in some, if not all, human PDAC cells.

Discussion

Immunotherapy represents one of the latest anti-neoplastic strategies, and it is attractive to oncologists that once it works, it might achieve long-term tumor-suppressive effects.37 However, ICI's response rate remains low, and many combination strategies have been examined. Nonetheless, additional therapeutics might introduce extra toxicity, which has been observed in αPD-L1 + αCTLA4 trials.38 Hyperthermia can be used to treat various solid tumors and has relatively low systemic toxicity. Hyperthermia treatment using photothermal and nanomedicine strategies has been shown to improve the efficacy of ICIs in some preclinical studies.39-42 One case report demonstrated excellent outcome of a stage-IV PDAC patient following combined Ht-αPD-1-αCTLA-4-IL2 treatment.43 Some clinical trials have started to evaluate the potential of Ht-integrated immunotherapy in cancer treatment.44 Therefore, our findings could be translated to the bedside, as Ht could be a promising partner of ICIs for combinatorial immunotherapy of PDAC.

Our study observes that Ht, particularly when combined with αPD-L1, increased the infiltration and activation of dendritic cells, CD8α + T cells and M1-type macrophages and yet barely changed the number of immunosuppressive Tregs, MDSCs, and M2-type macrophages. While PDAC's “immune dessert” TiME is known to be deficient in immune-activating cells and proficient in immune-suppressive cells, Ht seems at least able to resolve a partial problem. Our study also revealed that 41 °C Ht did not alter PD-L1 expression in either cell line or tissue studies (although Pan02 cells do express PD-L1 at a moderate level,45), suggesting that Ht may sensitize subcutaneous Pan02 tumors to αPD-L1 therapy by changing the TiME but not by upregulating PD-L1 expression.

Our study demonstrated interesting phenomena that hyperthermia largely, if not exclusively, induces γH2AX formation, mitotic catastrophe, and cGAS-STING activation in a time-delayed manner. These events were parallel in time, logistically consistent with each other, and all started at ~12–24 h and lasted at least 48 h post Ht. Earlier studies reported similar findings that cells with mitotic abnormalities featured most obviously 1–3 d after heat challenge.19-21 To the best of our knowledge, the vast majority of research has focused on the immediate effect of Ht treatment occurring 0–8 h after Ht for DDR protein degradation.46,47 Our study may be the first to report this time-delayed, but long-lasting effect of Ht and its impact on immune signaling.

Sumoylation has been identified as a novel immuno-oncology target in recent years. As a type of post-translational modification for many nuclear proteins,35,48 SUMOylation widely regulates many steps associated with chromatin stability, including histone modification, centromere dynamics, and chromatin remodelling.48,49 Sumoylation is a dynamic process that responds to various external proteotoxic challenges, such as hyperthermia, and is known as the SUMOylation stress response.50 In particular, since the discovery of the SUMOylation inhibitor TAK-981 in 2021,34 decreased or aberrant SUMOylation has been reported to promote micronucleation, activate the cGAS-STING pathway, and evoke antitumor immunity in various animal models, including PDAC models.33,34 Therefore, our observation that SUMOylation dynamically changes (being downregulated) after hyperthermia and is associated with cGAS-STING activation could be an important finding and yet need further investigation.

Our study employed microwave hyperthermia for in vivo study and water-bath hyperthermia for in vitro study, which two methods have been reported to differ in several aspects according to previous studies.51,52 Hader's study suggests that microwave hyperthermia has stronger cytotoxic effect and induces stronger heat shock response than water-bath.51 However, the difference is rather small (5% difference in cytotoxicity) and/or transient (regarding to HSP70 induction and changes in immune checkpoint expression) at 41 °C, but becomes more obvious at a higher temperature like 44 °C.51 Zhao YY et al. observed stronger apoptosis induction associated with ROS production and G2/M arrest by microwave than water-bath treatment at 43 °C in non-small cell lung cancer cells.52 Such difference is thought to originate from the “non-thermal” electromagnetic effects disturbing cell organelles and “hot spots” effects due to heterogenous distribution of microwave.51,52 Similar mechanism also exist in the difference between magnetic hyperthermia and water-bath hyperthermia.53 In our situation with heating temperature at 41 °C, the difference should exist but is likely to be minimal.

Our study has some major limitations. First, although our study found that HT + ICI could suppress tumor growth, we barely observed successful tumor control (Figure 3B). Since hyperthermia is commonly used in combination therapy, the introduction of gemcitabine + /– cisplatin chemotherapy and/or radiotherapy to our treatment paradigm emerges necessary in our next-stage work hopefully to achieve better outcome with disease control. Second, it is necessary to emphasize that our heterotopic subcutaneous model resembles the tumor microenvironment of PDAC tissue and has limited value to predict the therapeutic potential of Ht + ICI in human PDAC tumors. This model of treatment combination still has a long path to be translated from preclinical to clinical setting. Third, the mechanism(s) by which Ht induces mitotic catastrophe in a time-delayed fashion remains to be elucidated. Hyperthermia is known to promote the degradation of the BRCA2 protein and may interfere with DNA repair.46,47 Many other proteins may also be degradable targets of heat shock, such as the structural proteins of the centromere54-56 and those that regulate spindle stability.57,58 Fourth, we observed that Ht only activates STING signaling in some human PDAC cells, which could be explained by the recent findings that mutant TP53 impedes cGAS-STING activation.59 Pan02 cells carry wild-type TP53, whereas PANC1, BxPC3, HPAF-II, and MIA-Paca2 cells carry p.R273H, p.Y220C, p.P151S, and p.R248W mutations, respectively. TP53 knock-down or overexpression would help to clarify the role of TP53 in Ht-mediated immunomodulation. Fifth, we used IHC/multiplex-IF with morphometric analysis to assess the changes in immune cells, and future work should compensate with flow cytometry analysis, which is more accurate and objective to assess immunological changes. Finally, several further experiments would strengthen our current mechanistic explanation and/or provide additional insights. Anti-CD8 depletion experiment confirmed the importance of CTL cytotoxicity in our model. A hemispleen model of hepatic metastasis would help to understand whether Ht + ICI would suppress metastatic tumors in parenchymal organs.60 A mouse model study using KPC cells, which constitute a murine PDAC cell line carrying intrinsic TP53 and KRAS mutations would further expand our understanding. Spontaneous PDAC models and xenografting of human PDAC cells in humanized immune system mouse models could also be used to evaluate the therapeutic potential of hyperthermia with ICIs.

In summary, our study demonstrated that mild microwave hyperthermia, which is commonly employed in clinical thermotherapy centers, synergizes with αPD-L1 therapy to repress PDAC in a preclinical model through a time-delayed cGAS-STING-activating effect. Hyperthermia treatment, a relatively less popular approach for cancer management, may be a promising choice for combinatorial immunotherapy for its low toxicity and good tolerance by patients. As time elapsed, hyperthermia may find a suitable place in immunotherapy cocktails. In addition, fever, as a cardinal pathophysiological response12 and a complication of cancer,61,62 may have unknown biological significance, warranting further investigation.
