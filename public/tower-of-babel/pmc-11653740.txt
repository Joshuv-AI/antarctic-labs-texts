Molecular and clinical insights into extended-spectrum β-lactamase genes of Klebsiella pneumoniae isolated from neonatal sepsis in Ethiopia
Authors: Daniel Geleta, Gemeda Abebe, Tsion Tilahun, Alemseged Abdissa, Adane Mihret, Raffaele Joseph Cataldo, Netsanet Workneh, Abel Abera Negash, Getenet Beyene
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundKlebsiella bacterial strains harboring Extended-Spectrum Beta-Lactamase (ESBL) enzymes are the primary culprits behind neonatal sepsis globally. These strains significantly impact clinical outcomes due to their multi-drug resistance patterns in local healthcare settings. In response to this spiraling threat, we studied the prevalence and clinical implications of ESBL-encoding genes in neonates hospitalized with confirmed sepsis.MethodsA correlational study was conducted on 51 neonates diagnosed with sepsis caused by ESBL-positive Klebsiella pneumoniae at Jimma Medical Center spanning from May 2022 to July 2023. Antimicrobial resistance profiles of the bacterial isolates were determined using the Kirby-Bauer diffusion test, while multiplex polymerase chain reaction (mPCR) techniques were employed to identify resistance genes. The correlation between resistance genes and treatment outcomes was analyzed using the phi coefficient (φ) with a significance level below 0.05. The data management was executed through the utilization of WHONET and STATA software platforms.ResultsThe sample consisted of 26 (50.9%) male and the remaining 25 (49.1%) female neonates, with diverse clinical characteristics. All 51 Klebsiella pneumoniae isolates were 100% resistant to trimethoprim/sulfamethoxazole and ceftriaxone, but showed varying resistance profiles ranging from 30.8% to meropenem to 94.2% to ceftazidime. Notably, all isolates demonstrated multidrug resistance, with 23% of cases showing resistance to seven different antimicrobial classes. The most prevalent resistance genes identified were blaCTX−M (96.1%), blaTEM (94.1%), and blaSHV (88.2%). The majority of isolates (94.1%) carried at least two resistance genes, such as blaTEM and blaCTX (94.1%), blaTEM and blaSHV (86.2%), and blaCTX and blaSHV (86.2%). Notably, 84.3% of the bacteria harbored the trio of blaTEM, blaCTX, and blaSHV resistance genes, and only the presence of blaSHV in monogenic (φ = 0.4, P = 0.01) or the trio of blaTEM, blaCTX, and blaSHV genes (φ = 0.3, P = 0.02) showed positive correlation with neonatal mortality.ConclusionWe observed a significant prevalence of multidrug-resistant Klebsiella pneumoniae strains among neonates. Moreover, ESBL-resistance genes were widespread, with the blaSHV gene showing a correlation with increased neonatal mortality. These findings emphasize the urgent need for enhanced infection prevention measures, robust antimicrobial resistance surveillance, innovative treatment strategies, antibiotic stewardship initiatives, further research into resistance transfer mechanisms as well as hierarchical predictors of neonatal mortality.Clinical trial number Not applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12879-024-10344-w.

Introduction

Neonatal sepsis caused by Klebsiella infections is a serious and often deadly condition that poses a significant burden on global health, especially in developing countries where it consistently leads to devastating outcomes [1, 2]. Among these burdens, Klebsiella infections significantly contribute to the occurrence of neonatal sepsis, a systemic infection [3], and complications in managing the condition, particularly when caused by Klebsiella strains that produce Extended-Spectrum Beta-Lactamases (ESBLs) [4, 5]. The ESBL-producing strains tend to develop and spread resistance genes, and actively remain on offense with strong defense [1], posing a threat to the field [2]. More importantly, the horizontal transfer of genetic elements between bacterial strains, often mediated by mobile elements, leads to the loss of efficacy of commonly used β-lactam antibiotics [6]. This occurs through impeding drug uptake, modifying targets, deactivating drugs, or facilitating drug efflux, resulting in increased adverse outcomes in neonatal healthcare and significant clinical and epidemiological concerns [7–9].

Indeed, the scientific literature documented the identification of virulent determinants and ESBL genes in a wide range of gram-negative rod species [4]. Around 51.1% of isolates from nearly 88% of health facility-based samples show resistance to first-line antibiotics, suggesting the presence of potential ESBL encoding genes [8, 9]. Notably, a higher prevalence of these genes among Klebsiella strains was commonly reported for those belonging to the Temoniera (TEM), Sulfhydryl Variant (SHV), and Cefotaxime-Munich (CTX-M) families [10, 11]. Furthermore, rare variants such as Verona integron-encoded metallo-beta-lactamase (VIM), Guiana extended-spectrum (GES), Imipenemase (IMP), Vietnam extended-spectrum beta-lactamase (VEB), Dutch imipenemase (DIM), and Ampicillinase C (AmpC) are continuously being reported [7].

Recent statistics reveals a significant and widespread phenotypic antimicrobial resistance and related genes in various regions among ESBL-producing Klebsiella strains [11], including Ethiopia [5]. These strains exhibit spiraled resistance to penicillin, cephalosporin, aminoglycoside, phenols, quinolones, and sulfonamides as reported from previous studies [10, 11]. For example in Shanghai 100% for Ampicillin (AMP), 86.4% for Ciprofloxacin (CIP) [12], and 0% for Meropenem (MEM) while in India it was 95.9% for AMP, 75% for Gentamicin (GEN), 64.7% for Amoxicillin-Clavulanate (AMC), 42% for Piperacillin-Tazobactam (TZP), 59.3% for Chloramphenicol (CAF), and 1% for MEM [13]. Likewise, previous studies in Ethiopia reported resistance rates of 54% for CAF and 95% for Cefotaxime (CTX) [14, 15]. The literature also indicates that multidrug resistance (MDR) and resistance patterns were high in Ethiopia (88%) [16], Madagascar (nearly all isolates) [17], and India (49.1%) [18]. In terms of resistance gene distribution, Gaza reported that approximately 59% of analyzed bacteria carried at least one of the CTX-M, TEM, or SHV genes, while individual rates reported 60% for CTX-M, 57% for TEM, and 38.3% for SHV [11]. In Tanzania, about 93.2% of bacteria carried at least one of CTX-M, TEM, or SHV genes, with 62.9% having all three [10]. Another study revealed that the SHV gene exhibited a prevalence of 96.8% in Peru [19] and 83.3% in Egypt among ESBL-producing bacteria with distinctive clinical importance [20]. Concurrently, global concerns extend to the prevalence of other rare resistance genes, including VEB, DIM, GES, VIM, and AmpC [21, 22], which are encoded by different bacterial species producing metallo-β-lactamases [23]. These genes enable the breakdown of a diverse range of β-lactam antibiotics, posing significant challenges to achieve effective treatment outcomes as noted in Iran [24] and Nigeria [25].

Generally, Klebsiella infections are widely recognized as a major cause of neonatal sepsis, which often results in excessive antibiotic use in neonates and increases the risk of antimicrobial resistance [4, 5]. This perilous cycle has significant repercussions, including longer hospital stays, delays in treatment, and higher mortality rates among affected neonates [8, 9]. In light of the worldwide concern over the dangers posed by Klebsiella infections, it is concerning to note the lack of comprehensive data on antimicrobial resistance profiles and related clinical insights, especially in developing regions like Ethiopia [9, 26, 27]. To assist in addressing this concern, our present research is dedicated to analyzing the antimicrobial resistance profiles of ESBL-producing Klebsiella strains and investigating their clinical correlations with neonatal treatment outcomes.

Methods and materials

Study design, period and setting

We conducted a correlational study at Jimma Medical Center in Ethiopia from May 2022 to July 2023. Blood samples were collected from neonates admitted to the neonatal intensive care unit (NICU) and transported to the internationally accredited microbiology unit of the center. The samples were assigned unique identifiers, evaluated for quality standards, and processed with assigned codes. Monthly summaries of individual patient data were reported to the Health Information Management System office and stored using the District Health Information System 2 software.

Participants selection, criteria and data source

The research focused on neonates within 28 days of birth who were admitted to the Neonatal Intensive Care Unit at JMC and diagnosed with sepsis. Data collection was conducted using an android-based Kobo-Collect application questionnaire (Supplementary file 1) to gather information such as socio-demographic details, obstetric and delivery parameters, neonatal factors, and laboratory results from the eligible participants. The study specifically examined ESBL-positive K. pneumoniae isolates, which were selected from a cohort of 342 non-duplicate blood samples collected separately. These blood samples underwent culture testing, with 138 samples showing positive results for bacterial growth. Among the culture-positive samples, 69 were identified as ESBL producers. Of these 69 ESBL producers, 51 were specifically identified as K. pneumoniae strains producing ESBL and were chosen for further characterization [28]. Neonates with blood cultures detected with no visible bacterial growth (N = 204), Gram-positive bacteria (n = 37), ESBL-negative bacteria (n = 32), and ESBL-positive non-Klebsiella isolates (n = 18) were identified and excluded from the analysis (Fig. 1).

Antimicrobial susceptibility and phenotypic ESBL-producing strain testing

The antimicrobial susceptibility of the isolates was assessed using the Kirby-Bauer disk diffusion method on Mueller-Hinton agar, following the standard procedure [29]. Antimicrobial susceptibility testing was performed using selected panel of antibiotic disks from Liofilchem®, Italy that included eight different classes: penicillin (PEN), beta-lactamase inhibitors (BLI), sulfonamide (SUL), phenols (PHE), fluoroquinolones (QNL), aminoglycosides (AMN), and cephalosporins (CEP), and carbapenems (CAR). These disks contained specific antibiotics such as Ampicillin (AMP: 105 µg), Sulfamethoxazole-trimethoprim (SXT: 3.75/1.25 µg), Amoxicillin-Clavulanate (AMC: 20 µg), Piperacillin /Tazobactam (TZP: 10 µg), Chloramphenicol (CHL: 30 µg), Ciprofloxacin (CIP: 5 µg), Gentamicin (GEN: 10 µg), Cefotaxime (CTX: 30 µg), Ceftazidime (CAZ: 30 µg), Ceftriaxone (CRO: 30 µg), and Meropenem (MEM: 20 µg).

The inhibition zone diameters were measured with WHONET software 2022, and results were interpreted according to the 2023 CLSI breakpoints. Isolates showing resistance to at least three different classes of antibiotics were classified as multidrug resistant (MDR) [29]. Screening for ESBL production among Klebsiella isolates was performed using the following antimicrobial disks: ceftazidime (30 µg, inhibition zone ≤ 22 mm), ceftriaxone (30 µg, inhibition zone ≤ 25 mm), and cefotaxime (30 µg, inhibition zone ≤ 27 mm). Isolates were considered candidates for confirmatory testing if they exhibited a smaller diameter of inhibition for any of these drugs.

Initial identification involved a combination disc screening test, which was confirmed through a double disc diffusion test. In the double disc diffusion test, ceftazidime 30 µg or cefotaxime 30 µg alone, as well as discs containing ceftazidime or cefotaxime 30 µg with clavulanic acid, were placed on a bacterial culture. The discs were positioned 20 mm apart on the plate and incubated at 37 °C for 24 h. As per the 2023 CLSI guidelines, the presence of an increased zone of inhibition (≥ 5 mm) around the combined disc, compared to a single ceftazidime or cefotaxime disc, indicated phenotypic evidence of ESBL production. To ensure result reliability, positive and negative controls were included in the analysis, with ESBL-producing Klebsiella pneumonia ATCC 700,603 and non-ESBL-producing Escherichia coli ATCC 25,922 serving as the positive and negative controls, respectively [30–32].

DNA extraction and PCR amplification

The DNA extraction from glycerol-stocked ESBL-producing Klebsiella strains was carried out at the Jimma University Clinical Trial Center using the TANBead Nucleic Acid Extraction Kit. The extraction process was executed on an automated Maelstrom system (Taiwan) utilizing a customized program and following manufacturer’s instruction. The quantity, quality, and concentration of the extracted DNA were measured using a NanoDrop spectrophotometer at Mycobacteriology Research Center of Jimma University. For the DNA extracts that met the quality criteria, conventional polymerase chain reaction (PCR) was conducted at Armauer Hansen Research Institute (AHRI) in Addis Ababa, Ethiopia. The PCR protocol used was referenced from previous studies [33, 34]. In the protocol, eight ESBL genes were divided into two groups based on their amplicon sizes. Group 1 multiplex PCR detected blaTEM, blaCTX−M, blaOXA, and blaVIM genes, while group 2 multiplex PCR detected blaGES, blaVEB, blaDIM, and blaAmPC genes. Similarly, a uniplex analysis was performed to detect the blaSHV gene.

For all genes, amplification reactions were performed in a final volume of 25 µL, consisting of a reaction mixture of 12.5 µL of 2x Tag plus PCR Master Mix from Thermo Scientific (Lithuania, EU), 1 µL of DNA primers, 5.5 µL of nuclease-free water, and 5 µL of the template DNA preparation. The amplification process was carried out in a DNA thermal cycler, starting with a denaturation step at 95 °C for 5 min. Subsequently, 40 cycles of amplification were performed, with each cycle consisting of denaturation at 95 °C for 20 s, annealing at 61 °C for 30 s, and extension at 72 °C for 1 min. Following the amplification cycles, a final extension step was conducted at 72 °C for 7 min. To visualize the amplified DNA, 2.5 µL of each PCR reaction was subjected to gel electrophoresis on a 1.5% agarose gel in 0.5 × TAE buffer for 50 min at 120 V. The DNA in the gel was then stained with ethidium bromide (1.5 µg/mL) and detected using a UV transilluminator from Cleaver Scientific Ltd. (Rugby, UK). The study utilized particular primers outlined in Table 1 that were specifically designed to target and amplify DNA segments of defined sizes (from 184 to 928 bp) that correspond to ESBL genes. In order to guarantee the reliability of the detection technique, the experimental configuration involved both positive controls (H79) and negative controls (H8) [34].

Data management and quality control

Data quality was ensured by translating the data tool into local languages (Afaan Oromo and Amharic) and having another individual retranslate it back into original English version to ensure unbiased translation. A trial run was conducted for the translated tool on 5% of a similar population outside the study facility. Once validated and configured the data tools were integrated into Kobocollect and provided two-day training to the data collectors. The questionnaire was loaded onto their Android devices, and the investigators regularly reviewed the collected and uploaded data to maintain accuracy and consistency. The procured laboratory kits, media, antibiotic discs, and other consumables were from a reputable supplier, ensuring quality. Before using in-house-prepared culture media plates, sterilization checks on each batch were done. The laboratory analysis followed standard operating procedures to ensure consistency and accuracy. Prior to actual data analysis, data profiling was done using frequency distributions and cross-tabulations.

Data analysis

STATA version 16.0 and WHONET software tools were employed in data analysis. WHONET, a specialized tool for analyzing antimicrobial resistance patterns in microbiology data, was used to interpret the resistance patterns exhibited by the microorganisms. STATA 16.0 used to conduct descriptive analysis on antimicrobial resistance genes and their correlation with neonatal treatment outcomes. The analysis included calculating the phi coefficient (φ) at a significance level of p < 0.05. The phi coefficient serves as a correlation measure that ranges from − 1 to 1, indicating both the strength and direction of the relationship between two binary variables. Specifically, -1 indicates a complete negative correlation, 0 signifies no correlation, and 1 denotes a perfect positive correlation. These coefficients yield important insights into the relationship between variables, such as resistance genes and neonatal mortality. The analysis results were presented through concise textual descriptions, tables, and figures.

Results

Socio-demographic and clinical profiles of study subjects

In this study, 51 subjects were included, of which 52.9% of the neonates were from urban areas, and the majority (68.6%) were born to mothers aged 20–34 years. The majority of deliveries (90.2%) occurred in healthcare facilities, with non-cesarean deliveries accounting for 74.5% of the total births. A significant portion (56.9%) of neonates was delivered at 37 weeks of gestation or later and 58.8% did not receive intrapartum antibiotic prophylaxis. Males constituted 50.9% of admissions, and 62.7% of neonates had a birth weight below normal range (< 2500 g). The study found that 72.6% of neonates were diagnosed with early-onset sepsis, and 50.9% of Klebsiella infections were acquired during hospitalization. Complications upon admission included perinatal asphyxia (21.6%) and hyperbilirubinemia (13.3%), with 25.5% of cases requiring bag and mask resuscitation. All neonates received empirical antibiotic treatment, mainly with ampicillin and gentamicin (70.6%). After hospital treatment, neonates were discharged with either improvement (52.9%) or sepsis-related death (47.1%) (Table 2).

Phenotypic antimicrobial resistance profiles of the isolates

The phenotypic testing conducted in the study revealed that the Klebsiella isolates analyzed exhibited a significant level of resistance to eleven different antibiotics. Specifically, all of the isolates were found to be 100% resistant to SXT, and CRO. However, the resistance percentages varied for other antibiotics, with 94.2% of the isolates showing resistance to CAZ, 78.8% exhibiting resistance to GEN, 71.2% displaying resistance to CIP, and 67.3% demonstrating resistance to AMC. Likewise, the isolates showed resistance percentages of 53.8% for CHL and 30.8% for TZP, while none of the isolates demonstrated resistance to MEM (Fig. 2).

In further phenotypic analysis, the Klebsiella isolates demonstrated 100% MDR rare with varying patterns across different drug classes. All isolates exhibited resistance to between three and seven classes of antibiotics. The most common resistance pattern involved seven classes—specifically PEN/BLI/SUL/PHE/QNL/AMN/CEP—accounting for 29.4% of cases. Conversely, the least frequent pattern involved four classes (PEN/BLI/SUL/CEP), with an occurrence rate of 2%. Molecular profiles of ESBLs

The PCR analysis revealed varying prevalence rates for different antimicrobial resistance genes among the fifty-one (51) examined Klebsiella isolates for nine genes. Among the isolates, the CTX gene was the most commonly detected, occurring in 49 out of 51 isolates, indicating a prevalence rate of 96.1%. The TEM gene closely followed, as it was identified in 48 out of 51 isolates, demonstrating a prevalence rate of 94.1%. The SHV gene ranked third in prevalence, being present in 45 out of 51 isolates, accounting for a prevalence rate of 88.2%. In contrast, only 4 out of 51 isolates showed the presence of the VIM gene, resulting in a prevalence rate of 8.2%. Similarly, the GES gene was found in 2 out of 51 isolates, indicating a prevalence rate of 3.9%. Both the AmpC gene and the DIM gene were each identified in 1 out of 51 isolates, resulting in a prevalence rate of 2% for each gene. However, none of the isolates exhibited the presence of either the OXA gene or the VEB gene (Fig. 3).

A significant majority 48(94.1%) of Klebsiella isolates characterized, carried multiple resistance genes. The most common combinations observed were: 48(94.1%) blaTEM+blaCTX genes, 44 (86.2%) blaTEM+blaSHV genes, and 44 (86.2%) blaCTX+blaSHV genes. Additionally, 43 (84.3%) bacteria were found to harbor the trio of blaTEM, blaCTX, and blaSHV resistance genes. Similarly, a smaller proportion of bacteria 3 (5.9%) carried blaVIM, blaCTX, blaTEM, and blaSHV genes and 2 (3.9%) bacteria carry the combination of blaCTX, blaTEM, blaSHV, and blaGES resistance genes. Additionally, a rare combination of resistance genes (2%) was observed among the isolates, comprising blaDIM, blaAMPC, and blaSHV genes.

Correlation between ESBLs genes and neonatal treatment outcome

The correlation analysis conducted in this study examined the relationship between three commonly detected antimicrobial resistance genes, namely blaTEM, blaCTX, and blaSHV, and the outcomes of neonatal sepsis treatment. The findings of the analysis revealed a significant positive correlation between the presences of the blaSHV gene, either in monogenic or in combination with other genes, and neonatal mortality resulting from sepsis. Specifically, when bacteria exhibited the blaSHV resistance gene alone, there was a notable positive correlation (φ = 0.4, P = 0.01) with neonatal mortality from sepsis. Similarly, the trio of or the concurrent existence of blaTEM, blaCTX, and blaSHV genes (φ = 0.3, P = 0.02), demonstrated a positive correlation with sepsis-related neonatal treatment outcomes. However, no significant correlations were observed between the blaTEM gene (φ = 0.08, P = 0.6), blaCTX gene (φ = 0.01, P = 0.90), the co-existence of blaCTX and blaSHV genes (φ = 0.3, P = 0.05), blaSHV and blaTEM genes (φ = 0.3, P = 0.05) or blaTEM and blaCTX genes (φ = 0.08, P = 0.6) and neonatal treatment outcomes attributed to sepsis (Table 4).

Discussion

Our study offers robust evidence on the phenotypic antimicrobial resistance profiles, molecular epidemiology, and clinical correlations of antimicrobial resistance genes in neonatal sepsis caused by Klebsiella isolates. The phenotypic resistance profiles of these isolates indicate a concerning level of resistance in the study area. Results show that all isolates were 100% resistant to sulfamethoxazole-trimethoprim, cefotxime, and ceftriaxone. Significant resistance rates were also observed for ceftazidime (94.2%), gentamicin (78.8%), and ciprofloxacin (71.2%). Importantly, none of the isolates exhibited resistance to meropenem, suggesting that carbapenems may still be effective treatment options. These findings align with previously published results from Shanghai, South India, and Ethiopia. In Shanghai, isolates were resistant to ciprofloxacin (86.4%), and none showed resistance to meropenem [12]. In South India, resistance rates included 88% to gentamicin, 92% to amoxicillin-clavulanic acid, 80% to sulfamethoxazole-trimethoprim, 81% to ceftriaxone, 44% to ciprofloxacin, and 21% to meropenem [35] and 88.9% to ciprofloxacin in Madagascar [17]. Similar studies in Ethiopia reported resistance rates of 80% to amoxicillin-clavulanic acid, 86.1% to ceftazidime, and 88.9% to cefotaxime [36]. However, the current phenotypic antimicrobial resistance (AMR) rates exceed those reported in previous studies from Shanghai, India, Madagascar and Ethiopia for several antibiotics. For instance, resistance rates for cefotaxime (63.7%), ceftriaxone (65%), and ceftazidime (64.3%) were lower in the studies conducted in India [18]. Additionally, ciprofloxacin resistance was reported at 47.2%, and trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole resistance was 72.2% in Ethiopia [36]. In contrast, this study found a lower resistance rate of 31.4% for piperacillin-tazobactam compared to a previous study in Ethiopia, which reported a resistance rate of 63.9% [36]. Likewise, the multi-drug resistance rate and resistance patterns observed in the current study were consistent with the result reported in previous study conducted in Madagascar (where all isolates were MDR) [17]. The variation in antimicrobial resistance among studies can be attributed to geographical location, healthcare settings, antibiotic usage patterns, infection prevention practices, maternal characteristics, microbial factors, and environmental influences. In neonates, this includes microbial colonization influenced by maternal health, selective pressure from antibiotic exposure, differences in healthcare practices, environmental conditions, genetic predispositions, and disparities in surveillance and reporting systems [7, 37].

We also delved into the study of molecular epidemiology and gained insights into the resistance genes responsible for phenotypic characteristics. The study identified three resistance genes—CTX (96.1%), TEM (94.1%), and SHV (88.2%)—as particularly prevalent in the Klebsiella species. The occurrence of these resistance genes exceeds previously reported prevalence rates in Peru for OXA (59.8%), CTX (57.4%), and TEM (53.5%), but is lower than the SHV prevalence of 96.8% [19]. In contrast, our study found lower prevalence rates compared to those reported in Gaza, where CTX was at 55.1%, and both TEM and SHV were at 41.3% [11].

During the molecular analysis, we detected a considerable proportion of the bacteria (94.1%) displaying evidence of polygenic resistance by carrying two or more of these genes, which is consistent with findings reported from Gaza [11]. Specifically, a striking 94.1% of the bacteria were found to harbor both the TEM and CTX genes, mirroring the results of a study conducted in Peru where 84.5% of the isolates carried at least two genes [19]. Similarly, 86.2% of the bacteria carried both TEM and SHV genes, and another 86.2% possessed both CTX and SHV genes, reflecting findings that are consistent with studies conducted in India [18]. Furthermore, approximately 84.3% of the bacteria exhibited the presence of all three resistance genes (TEM, CTX, and SHV), demonstrating similarities with results reported in China [12] and Tanzania [10]. In contrast, the presence of other resistance genes, namely VIM, GES, AmpC, and DIM, was relatively less common among the bacteria examined, when compared to the previous prevalence reported in the same study area [38].

Compared to the previous study conducted in the same facility, a smaller percentage of bacteria (5.9%) exhibited a combination of VIM, CTX, TEM, and SHV genes, indicating a more complex resistance profile. Additionally, 3.9% of the bacteria carried a combination of CTX, TEM, SHV, and GES resistance genes. Notably, only one bacterium (2%) was identified as carrying the DIM, AmpC, and SHV resistance genes, highlighting its rarity within the studied population. None of the examined bacteria were found to possess the OXA or VEB genes [39]. The overall result implicates that Klebsiella strains exhibited significant genetic diversity and house a wide range of antimicrobial resistance genes and their variants, which explains the epidemiologic variations. Detecting these genes is further complicated by their rapid evolution through mutations and gene rearrangements [2]. Some resistance genes may merely exist in an undetectable lower amounts, increasing the chance of false-negative results [19].

Finally, we also investigated the relationship between the correlations of resistance genes with neonatal clinical outcome. It specifically pointed out that the presence of the SHV gene either alone or along with other genes is correlated with neonatal sepsis adverse treatment outcomes. Multiple studies, including one conducted in China [40] and another international study, have provided compelling evidence supporting the association between antimicrobial resistance caused by Klebsiella [3] and a notable number of neonatal treatment outcomes worldwide [41]. These studies reveal the concerning existence of diverse and rapidly spreading resistance genes in Klebsiella strains that significantly contribute to the severity of neonatal mortality worldwide [40, 41]. Particularly, the SHV Beta-lactamases contain various allelic variants, including ESBL [20]. Bacteria that carry the SHV gene can develop resistance to multiple antibiotics, significantly increasing the risk of mortality compared to other resistance genes. This resistance extends not only to beta-lactams but also to other classes of antibiotics, such as aminoglycosides and fluoroquinolones, through mechanisms of co-resistance and cross-resistance [20, 41]. Further, it is important to recognize that neonatal survival is influenced by a multitude of factors and is not solely determined by individual resistance gene [42]. The variability in these associations is likely influenced by the treatment protocol for neonatal sepsis, which involves administering a combination of drugs from different classes, each with varying levels of sensitivity and resistance [43]. It also glares to recognize that neonatal mortality can have multiple causes, and the findings underscore the significance of considering other factors [43, 44]. Therefore, when evaluating the factors that contribute to neonatal mortality in sepsis, a comprehensive range of variables that include the overall health of the neonate, the prevalence of resistance genes in the local population, access to healthcare, the quality of medical interventions, socioeconomic conditions, and the effectiveness of antimicrobial treatments should be taken into account [45].

Limitation of the study

The study has several limitations that should be acknowledged. It was conducted at a single healthcare facility with a limited number of participants. Furthermore, some comparisons were drawn with similar studies due to the lack of directly comparable results. These factors highlight the need for caution when generalizing the findings to the broader community.

Conclusion and recommendation

The study findings indicate a significantly high prevalence of multidrug resistance among bacterial isolates, with nearly all exhibiting resistance to commonly prescribed antibiotics. The predominant resistance genes identified were blaCTX-M, blaTEM, and blaSHV, often found in various combinations. Statistical analysis revealed a significant positive correlation between neonatal mortality rates and the presence of blaSHV alone, as well as the combined presence of blaTEM, blaCTX-M, and blaSHV. To address the growing challenge of multidrug-resistant Klebsiella pneumoniae in neonatal intensive care units, it is essential to implement rigorous infection prevention measures, establish robust surveillance systems for ongoing monitoring, invest in the development of novel therapeutics, enhance diagnostic stewardship, optimize antibiotic stewardship, and further investigate resistance gene transfer mechanisms. Additionally, it is crucial to identify the factors contributing to mortality, focusing particularly on the sequential chain of effects.
