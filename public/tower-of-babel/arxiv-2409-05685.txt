On the role of the signature transform in nonlinear systems and data-driven control
Anna Scampicchio, Melanie N. Zeilinger

Abstract
Classic control techniques typically rely on a model of the system’s response to external inputs, which is difficult to obtain from first principles especially if the unknown dynamics are nonlinear. In this paper, we address this issue by presenting an approach based on the so-called signature transform, a tool that is still largely unexplored in data-driven control. We first show that the signature provides rigorous and practically effective features to represent and predict system trajectories. Furthermore, we propose a novel use of this tool on an output-matching problem, paving the way for signature-based, data-driven predictive control.

Introduction

Control theory and practice are increasingly challenged to perform safe regulation of dynamical systems that are complex, nonlinear – and unknown. Typically, control is designed by leveraging a model of the system’s behavior in response to exogenous inputs. Such a model can take different forms, according to the “palette” reviewed in
Schoukens and Ljung (2019)
: moving away from the (typically unavailable) first-principles,
white
-box representations, there is a wide spectrum of
data-driven
methods that can be employed, depending on the amount of physical information available. When there is none, data are used to train
black
-box models, which return a typically non-interpretable mechanism to predict system trajectories in presence of new inputs – a paradigmatic example being methods based on deep learning
(
Pillonetto et al., 2025
;
Wen et al., 2023
)
.
Due to their data-hungriness and the lack of robustness certificates, data-driven control methods have instead been privileging more interpretable models, which typically try to represent the system’s behavior by means of a linear combination of suitable features. Popular and theoretically sound approaches falling into this category leverage, e.g., Koopman operator theory
(
Mauroy et al., 2020
;
Bevanda et al., 2021
)
and Volterra series
(
Cheng et al., 2017
)
– however, practical rules to choose the finite-dimensional subspace of interest are still mostly heuristic and not mature yet.

Overall, in data-driven control there is a quest for tools to represent unknown nonlinear dynamics that are accurate without being excessively data-hungry and that can be complemented by a rigorous analysis of their performance.

In this paper, we highlight the great potential of the so-called
signature transform
(or, simply,
signature
), a tool mostly studied in time-series analysis but largely unexplored in data-driven control, in addressing these issues. Specifically, we focus on continuous-time, input-affine dynamical systems (which capture most of the systems of interest in control – see
Isidori (1995)
) and adapt to them the results on the signature transform, in particular stressing its
universal approximation property
. Next, after reviewing the use of the signature for predicting the system trajectories corresponding to a new input, we propose a novel strategy based on the signature to tackle an output-matching, open-loop control problem.

Related work

The signature transform.

This mathematical object has been studied starting from the seminal work
Chen (1958)
, and flourished in the branch of stochastic analysis known as
rough path theory

(
Lyons et al., 2007
)
. Signatures found applications mostly in time-series analysis (see
Fermanian (2021)
and references therein), and have also been deployed in
Muça Cirone et al. (2024)
to provide a theoretical analysis of deep selective state-space models.

Signatures in control theory.

The work in
Chen (1958)
has been partially extended to handle input-affine dynamical systems in
Fliess (1981)
, but in general there has been no use of the signature in the context of data-driven control so far. The only exception is
Ohnishi et al. (2024)
, a work developed independently from ours, which takes a dynamic programming viewpoint and uses the signature to parametrize the value function, not to explicitly represent system dynamics.

Signature inversion.

When applying signatures to control (for instance, in the output-matching problem tackled in this paper), retrieving the generating signal given its signature is one of the biggest challenges. Such a problem has been theoretically investigated, e.g., in
Rauscher et al. (2024)
and the references therein. The only work that presents a practical algorithm to tackle it is
Fermanian et al. (2023)
, but the approximation level thereby obtained is typically too crude. The output-matching control approach proposed in this paper contributes to this line of research: it leverages an insight on the universal approximation property that has never been exploited so far, thus giving a new perspective on signature inversion.

(Continuous-time) system identification.

We can look at the signature-based model studied in this paper under the lens of
nonlinear system identification
. In that field, most of the models for dynamical systems are expressed in discrete-time, motivated by the fact that controllers are mostly digital
(
Schoukens and Ljung, 2019
)
; therefore, the literature on continuous-time identification is not as broad – see, e.g.,
(
Billings, 2013
, Chapter 10)
for a survey of available techniques. Yet, the systems to be modeled mostly work in continuous time: sampled-time models are typically just approximations, and they usually fail in situations in which data are irregularly sampled – which could occur, e.g., in financial time-series or in biomedical applications. The signature-based model addresses all of these issues, considering a continuous-time representation of the dynamics and working with time-points that can be unevenly spaced.

We now recall the properties of the signature transform that will play a pivotal role in the proposed methodology.

Useful properties.

The first notable property of the signature is the so-called
Chen’s identity

(
Chen, 1958
)
, which allows us to compute the signature of a path, e.g., on the interval
[s,t]
knowing the signatures for the portions defined on
[s,v]
and
[v,t]
.

Theorem 2

Let
X\colon[s,v]\to\mathbb{R}^{d+1}
and
Y\colon[v,t]\to\mathbb{R}^{d+1}
be two continuous paths, and let
X*Y
be their concatenation
(
Lyons et al., 2007
, Definition 2.8)
. Then, for any multi-index
i_{1}\dots i_{k}\subset\{0,\dots,d\}^{k}
,
\mathcal{S}_{s,t}^{i_{1}\dots i_{k}}(X~*~Y)=\sum_{\ell=0}^{k}\mathcal{S}_{s,v}^{i_{1}\dots i_{\ell}}(X)\mathcal{S}_{v,t}^{i_{\ell+1}\dots i_{k}}(Y)
.

{pf}

It follows from Fubini’s Theorem – see
(
Lyons et al., 2007
, Theorem 2.9)
.

The key fact that makes the signature so powerful is its
universal approximation
property: roughly speaking, it states that the signature behaves like polynomials defined on the space of paths. Formally, following
(
Cuchiero et al., 2021
, Theorem 2.3)
:

Theorem 3

For any time

in the interval
[0,1]
, let

be the solution of (
2

Z_{0}=z_{0}
. There exists a time-homogeneous, linear operator
\mathcal{L}~\colon~\oplus_{k=0}^{M}~(\mathbb{R}^{d+1})^{\otimes k}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
, depending only on
(f,M,z_{0})
, such that

Z_{t}=\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{S}_{0,t}(X)^{M})+\mathcal{O}(t^{M+1}).

(6)

{pf}

See Appendix
A.3
.

Therefore, one can train the linear model associated with
\mathcal{L}
using the data-set given by the observed input-state trajectories of the system, denoted as
\{X_{t}(m),Z_{t}(m)\}_{t\in\mathcal{D}}
with
m=1,\dots,N_{\text{train}}
; the features are the elements of the truncated signature (
5

being time-homogeneous, the vector combining the terms of the truncated signature does not change across

We conclude with the specific case of the signature of piecewise-linear paths.

Proposition 4

Consider the continuous path
X\colon[0,T]\to\mathbb{R}^{d+1}

\mathcal{S}_{t_{j},t_{j+1}}^{i_{1}\dots i_{k}}(X)=\frac{1}{k!}\prod_{\ell=1}^{k}\Delta_{j}^{i_{\ell}}.

(7)

{pf}

It follows from the definition (

Remark 3.1

Note that the factorial decay in (

Problem

Model training.

We start by using the available data-set (sampled trajectories)

Differentiate

6
Conclusions and outlook

In this paper, we showed the practical effectiveness and theoretical soundness of signature-based features in representing trajectories of unknown, input-affine (nonlinear) dynamical systems, allowing also to handle the challenging scenario in which data are irregularly sampled. Dealing with piecewise-constant exogenous inputs, we adapted the results on the signature transform to the systems of interest, and showed the performance in forecasting the system’s behavior in presence of a new, unseen input. Next, we proposed a novel signature-based control for output matching, which also gives new insights in the issue of signature inversion. Investigating both facets of prediction and open-loop control opens the floor to deploy signatures in predictive control, both in the common,
indirect
“identify-then-control” scheme and in the
direct
set-up reviewed in
Berberich and Allgöwer (2024)
. In this regard, note that the signature operates in the space of trajectories, and thus aligns with the philosophy underpinning behavioral systems theory
(
Polderman and Willems, 1998
)
used in direct, data-driven predictive control.

Extensions and generalization

Relaxing Assumptions
1
and
2
, and dealing with noise.

The model studied in this paper assumes global Lipschitz-continuity of the unknown vector fields: such a hypothesis can be relaxed to local Lipschitz-continuity, at the price of discussing uniqueness and continuity of (
2
) for sub-intervals of
[0,T]
. Furthermore, the choice of piecewise-constant inputs in (
1
) is quite standard and also aligns with common control design practice – however, such an assumption can be relaxed, as long as integrability still holds, and bearing in mind that one could need to numerically approximate the integral yielding the input path
X
. In general, the method presented in Section
4.2
can be extended to smooth input paths, possibly at the cost of increasing the number of optimization variables.
Finally, future research will deal with extending the signature-based method to the case in which measurements are noisy, deepening the preliminary results obtained in
Monzio Compagnoni et al. (2023)
.

Implementation.

As regards the coding of the proposed control strategy, the current implementation in CasADI solves (
12
) for the example presented in Section
5.2
at an average total time of 31.4 ms on a 2.6 GHz 6-Core Intel Core i7 processor. The performance can be further enhanced, both in terms of speed and solution quality, by analytically computing the gradient and the Hessian for each component of
\text{vec}(\tilde{S}(\Delta))
with respect to
\Delta
, and including them as inputs for the optimizer. The structure of the signature matrix makes such a task feasible, and this improvement can be directly included.

Reducing computational complexity.

The results attained by the proposed approach are promising, thanks to the high expressiveness of the signature-based features. Yet, applying it to high-dimensional inputs could be very demanding: the truncated signature stores
\sum_{k=0}^{M}(d+1)^{k}
terms, leading to a
\mathcal{O}((d+1)^{M})
computational complexity that could be intractable for high values of
d
even if the truncation order is relatively small. Thus, alternative features such as the randomized signature
(
Monzio Compagnoni et al., 2023
)
or the log-signature
(
Reizenstein and Graham, 2018
)
can be considered to address this issue and enhance the applicability of signature-based models in prediction and control.

Appendix A
Review of signature properties

In this section, we give a concise overview on the main algebraic and analytic structures of the signature, and build upon them to review the proof of its universal approximation property.

A.1
Main algebraic structures

By its definition (
4
), the signature belongs to the space of infinite sums of
k
-level tensors denoted by
\bigoplus_{k=0}^{+\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d+1})^{\otimes k}
. The latter is equivalent to the space of formal series of tensors

\mathcal{T}(({\scriptstyle\mathbb{R}^{d+1}}))\doteq\{\boldsymbol{a}=(a_{0},a_{1},\dots)|a_{k}\in(\mathbb{R}^{d+1})^{\otimes k}\>\forall k\in\mathbb{N}\}.

By
(
Lyons et al., 2007
, Definition 2.4)
,
\mathcal{T}(({\scriptstyle\mathbb{R}^{d+1}}))
is a real, non-commutative algebra. The sum of two elements
\boldsymbol{a}
and
\boldsymbol{b}
in
\mathcal{T}(({\scriptstyle\mathbb{R}^{d+1}}))
is given by
(a_{0}+b_{0},a_{1}+b_{1},\dots)
, while their multiplication is defined as
\boldsymbol{a}\circledast\boldsymbol{b}\,\doteq\,(c_{0},c_{1},\dots)
, with
c_{n}
given by the tensor products
\sum_{k=0}^{n}a_{k}\otimes b_{n-k}
; the unitary element for
\circledast
is the trivial signature
\boldsymbol{1}\doteq(1,0,\dots)
.

Focusing now on the elements (
3
) of a given signature
\mathcal{S}_{s,t}(X)
, one can prove that their product can be expressed as a linear combination of other (possibly higher-order) terms obtained through the so-called
shuffle product
:

Proposition 8

A permutation
\sigma
of
\{1,\dots,n+m\}
is called
(n,m)
-shuffle if
\sigma^{-1}(1)<\dots<\sigma^{-1}(n)
and
\sigma^{-1}(n+1)<\dots<\sigma^{-1}(n+m)
– intuitively, this means that the first
n
indices are shuffled separately from the last
m
. Define
\mathfrak{S}(n,m)
the collection of all of the possible
(n,m)
-shuffles of
\{1,\dots,n+m\}
, and let
I=i_{1}\dots i_{n}
and
J=j_{1}\dots j_{m}
be two multi-indices with
i_{1},\dots,i_{n},j_{1},\dots,j_{m}
in
\{0,\dots,d\}
.
Considering
(r_{1},\dots,r_{n},r_{n+1},\dots,r_{n+m})\doteq(i_{1},\dots,i_{n},j_{1},\dots,j_{m})
, the shuffle product of
I
and
J
is defined as
I\Cup J\doteq\{(r_{\sigma(1)},\dots,r_{\sigma(n+m)})\,|\,\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}(n,m)\}
. Then, it holds that, for any interval
[s,t]\subseteq[0,T]
,

\mathcal{S}_{s,t}^{I}(X)\mathcal{S}_{s,t}^{J}(X)=\sum_{K\in I\Cup J}\mathcal{S}_{s,t}^{K}(X).

{pf}

We start by interpreting the integral as a linear operator and writing (
3
) using the dual basis
(e_{0}^{\prime},\dots,e_{d}^{\prime})
of
\mathbb{R}^{d+1}
as
\int_{s\leq\tau_{1}\leq\dots\leq\tau_{k}\leq t}dX_{\tau_{1}}^{i_{1}}\dots dX_{\tau_{k}}^{i_{k}}=\langle\int_{s}^{t}dX\otimes\dots\otimes dX,\,(e_{i_{1}}^{\prime},\dots,e_{i_{k}}^{\prime})\rangle
. The claim is then an application of Fubini’s Theorem
(
Lyons et al., 2007
, Theorem 2.15)
.

From this, we have the following

Corollary 9

The terms of the signature
\mathcal{S}_{s,t}(X)
form a sub-algebra with respect to the shuffle product.

{pf}

Proposition
8
shows that the subspace of signature elements is closed with respect to multiplication.

A.2
Analytical properties

The starting point consists in the following result:

Theorem 10

The signature
\mathcal{S}_{0,t}(X)\in\mathcal{T}(({\scriptstyle\mathbb{R}^{d+1}}))
is the solution of the differential equation

d\mathcal{S}_{0,t}(X)=\mathcal{S}_{0,t}(X)\otimes dX_{t},\quad\mathcal{S}_{0,0}(X)=\boldsymbol{1}.

(15)

{pf}

See
(
Lyons et al., 2007
, Remark 2.12)
and
(
Friz and Victoir, 2010
, Proposition 7.8)

The following key properties of the signature are its direct consequences:

Corollary 11

The signature (a) is invariant under time parametrization and (b) is shift-invariant. (c) Denoting with
\overleftarrow{X}_{v}=X_{s+t-v}
for
v\in[s,t]
the path
X\colon[s,t]\to\mathbb{R}^{d+1}
run backwards in time, the signature satisfies
\mathcal{S}_{s,t}(X)\circledast\mathcal{S}_{s,t}(\overleftarrow{X})=\boldsymbol{1}
. Finally, the signature (d) exists unique, and (e) is continuous with respect to
X
.

{pf}

See
(
Friz and Victoir, 2010
, Proposition 7.10)
for the proof of (a).
Claims (b)-(e) follow from the Picard-Lindelöf Theorem stated in
(
Friz and Victoir, 2010
, Chapter 3)
. Note that (c) can also be derived from Theorem
2
.

A.3
Universal approximation

This section presents the proof of Theorem
3
, which hinges on the Stone-Weierstrass approximation Theorem:

Theorem 12

Let
\mathscr{A}

be a compact Hausdorff space,
\mathscr{C}(\mathscr{A},\mathbb{R})
the set of continuous real-valued functions defined on
\mathscr{A}
, and let
A
be a sub-algebra of
\mathcal{C}(\mathscr{A},\mathbb{R})
that contains a non-zero constant function. Then
A
is dense in
\mathcal{C}(\mathscr{A},\mathbb{R})
if and only if it separates points.

{pf}

See, e.g.,
(
Rudin, 1976
, Theorem 7.32)
.

We now show that the signature terms satisfy Theorem
12
. The space
\mathscr{A}
is given by the compact space of continuous paths with bounded 1-variation. We showed in Corollary
9
that signature elements form a sub-algebra, and the non-zero constant therein contained is the neutral element with respect to multiplication
\circledast
, which is
\mathbf{1}
. The point-separation property is ensured by the following Lemma, which is satisfied because the input path
X
contains a time component.

Lemma 13

If
X,\,Y\colon[s,t]\to\mathbb{R}^{d+1}
have at least one strictly monotone component, then
\mathcal{S}_{s,t}(X)\neq\mathcal{S}_{s,t}(Y)
.

{pf}

This sufficient condition can be derived from the results in
Hambly and Lyons (2010)
.

We can now apply Theorem
12
to the solution map of (
2
), which by Proposition
1
is continuous in
X
, and obtain the claim of Theorem
3
.
Finally, note that the time interval
[0,1]
in the statement is taken without loss of generality thanks to Corollary
11
(a).
