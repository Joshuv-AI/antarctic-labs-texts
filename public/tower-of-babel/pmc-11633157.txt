Health, beliefs, and faith: HPV vaccine uptake intent among Catholic, Evangelical, and mainline protestant parents
Authors: Jeanine PD Guidry, Shillpa Naavaal, Linnea I Laestadius, Carrie A Miller, Gina Zurlo, Candace W Burton, Kellie E Carlyle, Julie Russo, Paul B Perrin
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThe HPV vaccine has the potential to prevent nearly 92% of HPV-related cancer cases, yet its uptake remains suboptimal. While well-documented barriers to HPV vaccine uptake include inadequate knowledge and lack of provider recommendation, religious preferences have emerged as another crucial factor influencing vaccination decisions. This study examined the interrelatedness of religion, beliefs, and HPV vaccination uptake among children among Catholic, Evangelical, and Mainline Protestant parents. A nationally representative survey was conducted among 1,068 U.S. parents from one of three major Christian denominations: Catholic, Evangelical, or Mainline Protestant. We examined the extent to which demographic factors, constructs from the Health Belief Model (perceived severity, susceptibility, benefits, barriers, self-efficacy, and cues to action), and faith-based support variables contributed to HPV vaccination decisions among parents from the specified religious denominations. Among the surveyed parents, 72.3% indicated that their child had received the HPV vaccine, revealing a substantial but incomplete uptake rate. Notably, no statistically significant differences were observed in vaccination rates across the three denominational groups. Perceived HPV vaccine benefits (p < .001), perceived barriers (p < .001) and perceived self-efficacy (p = .013) were strongly associated. Parents reporting that their child’s healthcare provider asked them about the vaccine (p < .001) and those more receptive to faith-based support for HPV vaccination (p = .049) were more likely to report child HPV vaccine uptake. To enhance HPV vaccine uptake among the examined religious denominations, strengthening provider-parent communication regarding the HPV vaccine and fostering partnerships between healthcare providers and supportive religious congregations can serve as powerful levers for promoting vaccination acceptance and compliance.KEYWORDS: HPV vaccine, parental vaccine hesitancy, health belief model, religion, vaccine uptake

Introduction

Human Papillomaviruses (HPV) represent the most common sexually transmitted infections worldwide. They are the primary cause of warts and cancers of the genital areas, as well as several other cancers. including oropharyngeal cancers.1,2 HPV is therefore a rising public health concern causing nearly 36,500 cancer cases annually. In recent years, HPV-associated oropharyngeal cancers have seen a steep increase in incidence and have become a new threat surpassing cervical cancer rates.3 Despite the availability of safe and effective HPV vaccines that reliably prevent nearly 92% of new HPV-attributable cancer cases,4 the uptake of the HPV vaccine at the national level has been low and disparate. In 2022, only 58.6% of U.S. 13–15 year-olds were up to date with HPV vaccines, and males are over four percentage points lower in vaccination rates (56.6%) than females (60.7%)).5 These numbers are far from the target 80% HPV vaccination rate goals of Healthy People 2030. Furthermore, there are large disparities in both initiation and completion of HPV vaccine series by race/ethnicity and urban/rural status.6,7

Finally, the COVID-19 pandemic has been accompanied by two deeply concerning HPV vaccination-related phenomena. First, the enduring hesitancy of certain populations related to the COVID-19 vaccines has been accompanied by an increase in vaccine hesitancy focused on other vaccines.8 Second, available evidence indicates that the COVID-19 pandemic considerably disrupted HPV vaccine delivery,9–11 leading to declining vaccination rates worldwide.11 Within already hesitant populations such as certain religious denominations, this may compound the risk to adolescents.

HPV vaccination is routinely recommended by the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention’s Advisory Committee on Immunization Practices (ACIP) for adolescents aged 11 or 12 and up. It can be started as early as age nine years and is recommended for all persons through age 26 years. Depending on initiation age, it is administered as a two or three-dose regimen.12 With the growing number of HPV-associated cancers, low vaccination rates, and disparities in vaccine uptake, there is an urgent need to improve HPV vaccination rates among U.S. adolescents to reduce HPV-associated morbidity and mortality.

Messaging and communication to improve HPV vaccine uptake and reduce vaccine hesitancy can be strengthened through the application of health behavior theory.13,14 In particular, the Health Belief Model (HBM) has long been used for understanding vaccination behaviors,15,16 including HPV vaccine uptake. Applicable HBM constructs include perceived severity of and perceived susceptibility to HPV infection and its consequences, perceived benefits of and barriers to getting (or getting your child) the HPV vaccine, self-efficacy to overcome vaccination barriers, and cues to action to obtain HPV vaccination. Several studies have found the constructs of perceived barriers to the vaccine as well as perceived severity of HPV-related infections and consequences to be predictive of vaccine acceptability.17–19 Interestingly, however, results of a large study from China suggest that even when information is clear and abundant, parental intentions to obtain the HPV vaccine for their children were low.20 This suggests that additional factors in HPV vaccine hesitancy may need to be explored.

Religion and HPV vaccination

Religious preferences may also play a role in decision-making about HPV vaccination. The world’s major religions have traditionally supported the use of vaccines – for example, Pope Francis, in 2021, urged Catholics to consider vaccination an “act of love,” describing it as a “simple yet profound way to care for one another.”21 Other faith leaders have framed vaccinations as both a moral duty and an act toward a “common good.”22,23 However, there have been concerning developments pointing toward increasing religion-driven vaccine hesitancy. There has been an increase in religious exemptions for childhood vaccines among U.S. kindergartners,23,24 and parents who reported frequent religious service attendance were more likely to have decided against HPV vaccination for their children than to be undecided.25 In addition, a 2023 study among 1,557 students at a private U.S. religious university found that 22% of participants were unwilling to vaccinate a future child against HPV.26 Reasons for vaccine hesitancy include perceptions of vaccines as interfering with divine providence, belief that vaccines are not safe, and concerns about vaccine development and manufacturing involving stem cells procured from pregnancy termination or abortion procedures.27–29 Importantly, religious leaders can positively30 and negatively31 influence decisions to vaccinate. In 2013, a church in Texas was the site of a measles outbreak after the pastor expressed hesitancy about childhood vaccines.32

In the U.S., 30% of Americans self-identify as Catholic, 22% as mainline Protestant, and 21% as Evangelical.33 These three Christian communities are distinct enough to warrant separate study. Catholics hold membership in Catholic parishes that are part of a larger global hierarchical structure with headquarters at the Vatican. Core to the Catholic intellectual tradition is an emphasis on decision-making and living that integrates both faith and reason while respecting the Catholic magisterium, scripture, and tradition.34 Several studies have found that Catholic parents are more likely to vaccinate their children against HPV than nonaffiliated parents.25

Mainline Protestants represent a wide array of denominations including many different kinds of Baptists, Methodists, Lutherans, and Anglicans. As a result, there is a huge diversity of belief and practice among Protestants along the conservative-progressive theological spectrum,35 as well as different approaches to ecclesiology, church leadership, and biblical interpretation, all of which influence individual views on the relationship between science and faith.36 Studies have shown that Mainline Protestants often are more likely to get vaccinated than Evangelicals.37

Evangelicals are Christians who self-identify as such on social scientific surveys and polls, as well as Christians who are members of denominations affiliated with a national, regional, or global Evangelical body.36 For instance, for the purposes of this study, all members of denominations affiliated with the National Association of Evangelicals are considered Evangelicals. Studying Evangelical attitudes and behaviors in the U.S. is important because the U.S. is home to one of the largest populations of Evangelicals33 and so has a tremendous amount of influence in both World Christianity and global affairs through its theological output and missionary exports. In addition, people identifying as Evangelical Christians are among those most hesitant to get the HPV vaccine for their children.38,39

Given the differences in history, beliefs, practices, and authority structures among Catholics, mainline Protestants, and Evangelicals, obtaining a better understanding of HPV vaccine hesitancy in these three populations – which encompass more than two-thirds of the U.S. population – is important and relevant for provider and public health consideration when discussing HPV vaccination. In addition, greater understanding about vaccine hesitancy in these groups can inform targeted messaging campaigns focused specifically on these denominations.

To further this understanding, this paper reports the findings of a study aimed at examining the extent to which parent demographic characteristics, HBM constructs, and faith-based support factors explain HPV vaccination uptake or lack thereof among eligible children of Catholic, Evangelical, and Mainline Protestant parents, as well as HPV vaccination uptake and intent for their children among these parents.

Methods

Survey research firm Qualtrics was engaged to enroll a sample (N = 1,068) of U.S. parents in a cross-sectional, online survey in April 2023 examining factors associated with HPV vaccination among children of Catholics, Evangelicals, and Mainline Protestants. To be eligible, adult participants had to be U.S. residents, able to read and write English, self-identify as a member of one of the three above-mentioned Christian denominations, and have at least one child between the ages of 9–17 living in their home (i.e., an HPV vaccine age-eligible child: HPV vaccination can start at age 9 and is recommended up through age 26; for this study we focus on minor children with “child” as under age 18).40 Informed consent was obtained through the Qualtrics instrument, before respondents were admitted into the study. This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Virginia Commonwealth University, a large U.S. mid-Atlantic research university (IRB HM20022296).

The survey included the following measures (full questionnaire available upon request from the corresponding author):

HPV vaccination behaviors

HPV vaccination behaviors for children ages 9 and older were measured using one item with the following four options: “My child already received one or more doses of the HPV vaccine,” “I am planning to get my child the HPV vaccine as soon as possible,” “I am undecided about whether I will get my child the HPV vaccine,” and “I will not get my child the HPV vaccine.” This measure was collapsed into a discrete “vaccinated/not vaccinated” variable, with “My child already received one or more doses of the HPV vaccine” and “I am planning to get my child the HPV vaccine” classified as “vaccinated” and “I am undecided about whether I will get my child the HPV vaccine” and “I will not get my child the HPV vaccine” as “unvaccinated.”

Parent and child demographic characteristics

Parent age, child age, parent sex, child sex, race/ethnicity, education, and religious denomination were collected.

Duke University Religion Index

Religious involvement was measured using the Duke University Religion Index (DUREL),41 a five-item validated scale assessing three major dimensions of religiosity: organizational religious activity measured by one question, “How often do you attend church or other religious meetings?,” with six response options ranging from “none” to “more than once a week;” non-organizational religious activity measured by one question, “How often do you spend time in private religious activities, such as prayer, meditation or Bible study?,” with six response options ranging from “rarely or never” to “more than once a day;” and intrinsic or subjective religiosity measured by three questions, “In my life, I experience the presence of the Divine (i.e., God),” “My religious beliefs are what really lie behind my whole approach to life,” and “I try hard to carry my religion over into all other dealings in life,” all with five options ranging from “definitely not true” to “definitely true of me.” Cronbach’s alpha for the five items was .81. The DUREL was not modified for this study. Although in the original DUREL validation, the three subscales are treated as independent variables, they were summed in the current study to create an overall index of religiosity.

Health Belief Model (HBM) constructs

The HBM was applied to develop items regarding their beliefs about HPV infection risk, benefits of HPV vaccination, and barriers/facilitators to HPV vaccination. The HBM is a widely used theory to guide different health promoting behaviors. Several main constructs make up a person’s perception of disease, which directly influences their intention to engage in preventive health-related behaviors toward it. In this study, understanding HPV vaccine related beliefs helps to understand what influences someone to receive or deny the vaccine for themself or their child.42 Participants responded to each of the items described below using a seven-point Likert scale that ranged from “strongly disagree” to “strongly agree” except for the question about ease of access to the vaccine in the self-efficacy domain, which used a six-point Likert-type scale ranging from “very difficult” to “very easy.” All were based on pandemic vaccine scales developed by Myers and Goodwin.15

Perceived severity refers to the evaluation of how serious the consequences are of contracting an illness or disease. An increase in perceived severity is typically related to uptake of the recommended health action. HPV infection and related consequences were assessed using three items (e.g., “It would be serious if my child contracted HPV-related cancer later in life”). Higher scores reflected higher perceived severity. Cronbach’s alpha for items on the scale was .91.

Perceived susceptibility is the belief of personal risk toward acquiring an illness, or the likelihood of contracting an illness. HPV infection and related consequences were measured using three items (e.g., “Without the HPV vaccine, my child would be at risk of getting genital warts later in life”). Higher scores reflected higher perceived susceptibility. Cronbach’s alpha for items on the scale was .93.

Perceived benefits to vaccine are characterized by the belief that taking the recommended health action (i.e. having your child vaccinated for HPV) would be an effective decision that leads to positive outcomes. This construct was measured using 10 items focused on the benefits of the HPV vaccine (e.g., “The HPV vaccine is effective in preventing HPV-related cancers,” “Vaccinating my child against HPV could protect my child’s future partner from HPV infection,” “The HPV vaccine will protect my child’s sexual health”). Higher scores reflected higher perceived benefits. Cronbach’s alpha for these items was .96.

Perceived barriers to HPV vaccination include a wide variety of factors that make it more difficult for someone to take the recommended health action. Perceived barriers may differ from person to person. These were measured using 7 items (e.g., “The HPV vaccine may lead to long-term health problems,” “The HPV vaccine may negatively impact my child’s fertility in the future,” and “The HPV vaccine is being pushed to make money for pharmaceutical companies”). Higher scores reflected higher perceived barriers. Cronbach’s alpha for these items was .93.

Self-efficacy refers to people’s confidence in their ability to perform the recommended health action. This was measured by four items (e.g., “I feel confident in making the decision about whether my child receives the HPV vaccine”). Higher scores reflected higher self-efficacy. Cronbach’s alpha for items on the scale was .90.

Cues to action include internal (i.e. experiencing symptoms, etc.) or external (i.e. reading an article in the news, etc.) events that trigger someone to engage in the recommended health action. In the case of HPV vaccination, a conversation with a doctor may act as a catalyst to the decision to have one’s child vaccinated. This was measured using one item, “Has a pediatrician, healthcare provider, or other clinician spoken to you about getting your child the HPV vaccine?” with response options “yes” and “no.”

Faith-based support

Faith-based support for HPV vaccination was measured by four questions, each using a five-item Likert scale: “Would each of the following change/have changed your mind about your children getting vaccinated against HPV,” with questions: “A religious leader you trust got his children the HPV vaccine,” “You could get the HPV vaccine for your children at a nearby religious congregation,” “A religious leader you trust encouraged you to get the vaccine,” and “Your religious community provided assistance in getting an appointment to get your children the HPV vaccine.” Cronbach’s alpha for the four items was .87.

Statistical analyses

Descriptive statistics and univariate analyses were conducted to explore differences in HPV vaccination status by parent and child characteristics. Although modern imputation and missing data approaches such as expectation maximization or full information maximum likelihood estimation are becoming commonplace, no data were missing in the current study, so missing data did not have to be accounted for.

Stepwise hierarchical logistic regression analyses were then used to explore how demographic characteristics (therefore controlling for potential confounders), HBM variables, and faith-based vaccination support are related to HPV vaccination among children of self-identified Catholic, Evangelical and Mainline Protestant parents. In the first model/step, only demographic variables were included in order to control for possible confounders that otherwise might account for the relationships found in successive models/steps. HBM variables were added to the second model/step. In the third and final model/step, faith-based support was added as a predictor. To address and control for potential confounders, a stepwise approach to build the models was used such that Step 1 included all demographics as predictors, step 2 included all HBM variables, and step 3 faith support. Doing so evaluated the predictive effects of HBM variables above and beyond the effects of participants’ demographic characteristics, and similarly so with faith support.

Results

Of the 1,068 survey participants, 73.0% identified as women and 27.0% as men; 51.2% answered the questions for a female child, and 48.8% for a male child. The mean age of participants was 42.4 (SD = 9.30). In addition, 78.8% reported being White, and 21.2% non-White. With regard to educational attainment, 41.5% reported completing either a bachelor’s or graduate degree, and 58.5% had less than a bachelor’s degree. Finally, 32.5% reported being Catholic, 33.5% Evangelical, and 34.0% Mainline Protestant (see Table 1).

Of the total sample, 72.3% of respondents reported their child had already received at least one dose of the HPV vaccine or they were planning to get their child the HPV vaccine (designated as “Vaccinated”), and 27.7% said they were undecided or definitely not going to get their child vaccinated (designated as “Not vaccinated”). The mean and standard deviation for HBM constructs was 5.41 (SD: 1.31) for perceived benefits of the HPV vaccine for their child, 3.09 (SD: 1.57) for perceived barriers to getting the HPV vaccine for their child, 5.92 (SD: 1.19) for perceived severity of HPV related infections and cancers for their child, 5.23 (SD: 1.52) for perceived susceptibility to HPV related infections and cancers for their child, and 5.43 (SD: 1.22) for self-efficacy to get the HPV vaccine for their child. Regarding cues to action about getting the HPV vaccine for their child, 77.2% (n = 825) reported that their child’s healthcare provider had spoken to them about the HPV vaccine. The mean faith-based support score was 3.38 on a five-point scale (SD = 1.00). The mean and standard deviation for the four items on the faith-based support score was 3.47 (SD = 1.15) for “The child of a religious leader you trust got the HPV vaccine;” 3.47 (SD = 1.15) for “Your child could get the HPV vaccine at a nearby religious congregation;” 3.44 (SD = 1.17) for “A religious leader you trust encouraged you to get your child the HPV vaccine;” and 3.46 (SD = 1.16) for “The child of a religious leader you trust got the HPV vaccine.”

T-tests indicated that older parental respondents were less likely to report their child was vaccinated or that they intended to get the child the HPV vaccination (p = .005) while parents of older children were more likely to report higher HPV vaccine uptake (p < .001). Chi-square tests indicated that women (compared to men, p < .001), white respondents (compared to minority respondents, p = .009), and those with less than a bachelor’s degree (compared to those with a bachelor’s degree or higher, p = .014) were significantly less likely to report their child was vaccinated. In addition, Evangelical parents were less likely to report their child had been vaccinated compared to Mainline Protestants (p = .003).

To investigate HPV vaccine uptake, a hierarchical logistic regression was carried out to compare the effects of demographics, HBM constructs, and faith-based support for HPV vaccination on HPV vaccine uptake decisions by parents for their children. For the first step, the demographic variables were analyzed. Step 1 for HPV vaccine uptake was statistically significant, wald X2(8) = 39.67, p < .001. Compared to men, women were less likely to report HPV vaccine uptake for their child (OR: .98 and p = .042); compared to younger respondents, those who were older were less likely to report HPV vaccine uptake for their child (OR: .56 and p = .002); parents of older children were more likely to report HPV vaccine uptake for their child (OR: 1.14 and p < .001) than those of younger children; compared to parents of color, white respondents were less likely to report HPV vaccine uptake for their child (OR: .59 and p = .006); and compared to Mainline Protestant respondents, those who reported being Evangelical were less likely to report HPV vaccine uptake for their child (OR: .66 and p = .019). This model explained 7.8% (Nagelkerke R2) of the variance in HPV vaccine uptake (see Table 2).

For the second step, HBM constructs were added as predictors to the analysis. Step 2 for HPV vaccine uptake was also statistically significant, wald X2(14) = 448.66, p < .001. Most demographic variables were no longer significant, except for race/ethnicity and child age: Compared to parents of color, white respondents were still less likely to report HPV vaccine uptake for their child (OR: .52 and p = .009) and parents of older children were still more likely to report HPV vaccine uptake for their child (OR: 1.10 and p = .017). Considering the HBM constructs, we found a significant effect of perceived HPV vaccine benefits – such that an increase in perceived benefits of HPV vaccines was associated with higher HPV vaccine uptake for their child (OR: 2.50; p < .001). In addition, there was a significant effect of perceived barriers to getting the HPV vaccine – those with higher reported perceived barriers to HPV vaccination were less likely to report HPV vaccine uptake for their child compared to those with lower perceived barriers (OR: .68; p < .001). There also was a significant effect of HBM variable self-efficacy for getting the HPV vaccine for their child – those with a higher reported perceived self-efficacy to get the HPV vaccine were significantly more likely to report HPV vaccine uptake for their child (OR: 1.74; p < .001). Finally, if a healthcare provider had addressed the HPV vaccine with the parent, the parent was more likely to report HPV vaccine uptake for their child (OR: 6.18; p < .001). The R2 value of .540 suggests that demographics and HBM constructs together account for 54.4% of the variation in HPV vaccine uptake (see Table 2).

For the third and final step, faith-based support for HPV vaccinations was added as a predictor to the analysis. Step 3 for HPV vaccine uptake was statistically significant, wald X2(2) = 11.14, p = .004. In this final step, compared to parents of color, white respondents were still less likely to report HPV vaccine uptake for their child (OR: .57 and p = .024) and parents of older children were again more likely to report HPV vaccine uptake for their child (OR: 1.10 and p = .022). None of the other variables – whether the child/adolescent was male or female, child age, education attained, religious denomination, and religiosity – were significant predictors of uptake or intent at this step. Considering the HBM constructs, there was a significant effect of perceived HPV vaccine benefits – such that an increase in perceived benefits of HPV vaccines was associated with higher HPV vaccine uptake for their child (OR: 2.45; p < .001). In addition, there was a significant effect of perceived barriers to getting the HPV vaccine – those with higher reported perceived barriers to HPV vaccination compared to lower perceived barriers were less likely to report HPV vaccine uptake for their child (OR: .68; p < .001). There also was a significant effect of HBM variable self-efficacy for getting the HPV vaccine for their child – those with a higher reported perceived self-efficacy to get the HPV vaccine were significantly more likely to report HPV vaccine uptake for their child (OR: 1.74; p = .013). If a healthcare provider had addressed the HPV vaccine with the parent, the parent was then more likely to report HPV vaccine uptake for their child (OR: 6.45; p < .001). Finally, those reporting a higher faith-based support for HPV vaccinations were more likely to report HPV vaccine uptake for their child (OR: 1.31; p = .049).The R2value of .548 suggests that among parents of children eligible for the HPV vaccine, demographics, HBM constructs, and faith-based support for HPV vaccinations account for 54.8% of the variation in child HPV vaccine uptake (see Table 2).

Discussion

Although HPV vaccination uptake for children and adolescents has been widely studied,43,44 there are still critical gaps in the literature about factors that could potentially assist providers and public health officials in reaching and communicating with potentially hesitant populations, such as certain religious groups, to consider these vaccinations for their children. This study focused on demographic characteristics, health beliefs, and faith-based support predictors of HPV vaccination uptake among Catholics, Evangelicals, and Mainline Protestants in the United States.

Results suggest that among included religious groups, race/ethnicity remains a factor of concern for HPV vaccine hesitancy. This implies a need to identify new communications strategies targeting white adults, emphasizing the health beliefs that strengthen perceived benefits of the HPV vaccine, while simultaneously addressing perceived barriers to vaccination. Findings also suggest that collaborations between key public health, healthcare, and religious stakeholders may be an effective strategy for promoting vaccine uptake.

Demographically, compared to minoritized racial/ethnic groups, white Christians who identify as part of one of the three included denominations were less likely to have gotten the HPV vaccine for their eligible children. This is consistent with other HPV vaccine studies.45,46 While these results disappeared in the final step of the logistic regression, on the univariate level women (compared to men) and older respondents (compared to younger) were less likely to report HPV vaccine uptake for their eligible child. This confirms what many other studies have shown47–50 and reinforces the importance of developing interventions that promote HPV vaccine uptake and address vaccine hesitancy with a specific focus on older parents as well as mothers. An encouraging result was that female and male children were equally likely to be vaccinated for HPV in this sample. In previous studies and in the national data, often male adolescents lag behind female adolescents in HPV vaccine uptake.51,52

Interestingly, while on the univariate level, Evangelicals were less likely to report HPV vaccine uptake for their eligible child compared to Mainline Protestants, this significant result disappeared when adding the HBM constructs to the logistic regression. In other words, in the final model there was no significant difference between the denominations in HPV vaccine uptake. This is notable given long history of Evangelical skepticism toward science in general and vaccines in particular.53,54 The study results appear to indicate that differences in vaccine hesitancy by denomination can at least partially be explained by differences in HBM constructs which may be present in different levels for different denominations. This result underscores the complexity of the issue of vaccine hesitancy within specific religious groupings and highlights opportunities for educational campaigns. Another factor explaining this finding may be the exposure to the COVID-19 pandemic which may have strengthened the influence of health beliefs.55 Our data was collected in 2022/2023 following the height of the COVID pandemic. Further work is needed to explore how specific beliefs about benefits and barriers to HPV vaccination may differ by denomination, as well as the sources of those beliefs.

Findings also offer insight into the health beliefs of the respondents in this sample. Consistent with the HBM, and when controlling for multiple demographic variables, such as education, sex (both parent and child), age (both parent and child), race, denomination, and religiosity, those reporting a high level of perceived benefits of the HPV vaccine, as well as a low level of perceived barriers to getting the HPV vaccine, were more likely to report higher HPV vaccine uptake for their eligible child. Neither perceived severity of or perceived susceptibility to HPV infection and its potential consequences were significant predictors of HPV vaccine uptake status. Therefore, communication strategies and resources aimed at both increasing perceived benefits of the vaccine, and adequately addressing perceived barriers to the vaccine, may have greater chances for success.

Variability in vaccine uptake was best explained by the final regression model including both faith-based support as well as HBM constructs. This is not unexpected, considering the HBM has been extensively used in many prevention-focused health communication campaigns and interventions, including many campaigns focused on childhood and other vaccinations.15,56 Accordingly, health professionals developing HPV vaccine uptake campaigns should consider drawing on HBM constructs to help inform their work. Specifically, our data showed that reported cues to action, increased perceived benefits, and increases in self-efficacy related to the HPV vaccine were associated with increased vaccination uptake, and perceived barriers were associated with a lesser likelihood of having eligible children vaccinated against HPV. Public health messaging targeting these constructs may be especially beneficial. These findings suggest that while providing information about the risks and severity of HPV infection and its potential consequences remains essential to public health communication, framing messages with attention to the perceived benefits of the vaccine, addressing barriers to getting the vaccine, and helping to increase a family’s self-efficacy in accessing the vaccine should be included in messaging. Those asked by a healthcare provider about getting the HPV vaccine for their child were significantly more likely to report their child was vaccinated, providing a hopeful indication of trust in healthcare providers’ advice. This finding aligns with the greater body of literature indicating that there is a relationship between trust of healthcare providers and improved health outcomes or behaviors.57 This finding also highlights the importance of developing values informed communication guidance specifically for providers who serve patients who may strongly weigh religion in their health decision-making.

Implications and strategies for public health and healthcare providers

The potential role of faith-based support on HPV vaccine uptake and intent presents a distinct opportunity for positive change.58 In this sample, increasing endorsement of faith-based vaccination promotion strategies was associated with an increased likelihood of HPV vaccine uptake. This suggests that favorable attitudes toward vaccination can be encouraged and reinforced by trusted religious leaders who themselves acknowledge getting the vaccine and encourage others to do the same. Faith-based outreach can be uniquely powerful for several reasons. First, when it comes to health issues, faith leaders are often highly trusted and therefore can be uniquely positioned to address perceived vaccination barriers.59–61 As a result, religious leaders may be able to correct or refute religious or conspiracy theory-based misinformation among their congregations.27,59 This is particularly relevant considering the dramatic increase in online vaccine misinformation since the start of the COVID-19 pandemic.62–64 Furthermore, studies suggest that faith leaders, in particular from Evangelical denominations, are often open to lead faith-based vaccine outreach initiatives, suggesting this to be a strategy that is both well-supported and feasible.65 In addition, faith-based outreach may uniquely be positioned to reach minority religious populations, such as Hispanic Catholics and Black Protestants, to address vaccine hesitancy with a basis in experiences with racism. In these communities, faith leaders have a history of engagement with minority populations on societal issues and are therefore uniquely trusted by members of these groups.66,67

As clergy can be helpful in addressing perceived vaccination barriers, public health providers should consider intentional partnerships with congregations to, for example, utilize churches as vaccine distribution sites and help parishioners get vaccination appointments in order to encourage religious communities to attain higher HPV vaccine uptake.67–69 Individual healthcare providers such as pediatricians, family physicians, and nurse practitioners should consider partnering with clergy in their communities to promote understanding, endorsement, and communication on the benefits of HPV vaccination and its alignment with Christian values.69,70

Strengths and limitations

A primary strength of this study lies in its integration of health behavior theory and faith-based constructs to gain insights into HPV vaccine uptake among distinct Christian denominations. In doing so, this study is at the intersection of religious studies and public health research, providing a unique lens on relevant public health and social phenomena. However, a key limitation pertains to the potential for selection bias, as the study sample was solicited using an online panel survey firm, and thus may not fully represent the broader population of parents within these denominational groups, particularly those with heightened vaccine hesitancy.71 In particular, the online survey methodology may have precluded sampling some of the most fundamentally religious individuals who are unlikely to participate in online scientific research.72 To mitigate this limitation, future research should endeavor to reach more diverse and representative samples, potentially through the utilization of multi-modal recruitment strategies, such as using social media recruitment as well as panel recruitment; and targeted outreach to hesitant communities, such as recruitment via individual congregations of the three denominations as well as non-governmental organizations serving specific populations. Additionally, longitudinal studies could provide valuable insights into how beliefs and attitudes toward HPV vaccination evolve over time and across generations.

Another limitation is that we treated the three denominational groups as relatively homogenous groups, despite significant diversity within each. Future studies should consider the different demographics within each of these denominations, such as majority African American congregations, Hispanic Catholic parishes, and rural vs. urban religious communities. Furthermore, qualitative studies should be conducted amongst these groups to determine the effects of more nuanced vaccine decision-making factors.73 Similar studies should be considered for other Christian denominations as well as other world religions. A final limitation is that this study did not include rurality as a demographic variable, with rural groups more likely to be more hesitant toward the HPV vaccine. Future studies should consider including this.

Finally, religiosity was measured using the DUREL (Duke University Religion Index). There may be subconstructs of religiosity such as the DUREL subscales that can be focused on in future research to allow a more nuanced conceptualization of religiosity.

Conclusions

Amidst the escalating need to optimize HPV vaccination coverage in the United States, this study delved into the intricacies of demographic factors, health beliefs, and faith-based support mechanisms that shape HPV vaccination decisions among parents belonging to three prominent Christian denominations. By uncovering nuanced differences and commonalities within these groups, our findings underscore the importance of tailored interventions and communication strategies to bolster HPV vaccine uptake and ultimately safeguard public health. Beliefs about the benefits of and barriers to vaccination, the role of healthcare providers discussing vaccination with parents and faith-based HPV vaccination support are important factors that can influence vaccination status. Intervention efforts that particularly account for racial/ethnic differences and capitalize on partnerships between health providers and supportive congregations may be able to reach undecided or hesitant parents. Future work should focus on collaboration with religious leaders and congregations and develop interventions to test various communication strategies and HBM guided health messages to support and increase HPV vaccination.
