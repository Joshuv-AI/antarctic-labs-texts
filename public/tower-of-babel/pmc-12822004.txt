Sodium butyrate modulates ocular surface microbiome and attenuates inflammation of meibomian gland dysfunction in ApoE−/− mice
Authors: Qiankun Chen, Leying Wang, Yuan Wei, Xizhan Xu, Zhenyu Wei, Yan Peng, Jinding Pang, Bo Peng, Qingquan Shi, Ahyan Ilman Qudsi, Qingfeng Liang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe ocular surface microbiome (OSM) in patients with meibomian gland dysfunction (MGD) differs from that of healthy individuals. However, the precise role of OSM in MGD remains unknown. Therefore, we aimed to investigate the mechanism of OSM in the inflammation of MGD and the effects of topical sodium butyrate (SB) treatment in ApoE−/− mice.MethodsApoE−/− (n = 36) and wild-type C57BL/6J (n = 16) mice served as MGD models and healthy controls, respectively. MGD mice were treated with safety-confirmed concentrations of SB (1, 5, and 10 mM) and PBS for 3 weeks. OSM was analyzed by 16S rRNA gene sequencing (V3–V4). The slit-lamp biomicroscopy, tear cytokines, histopathology (oil red O/PAS/TUNEL staining), and TLR4/MyD88/NF-κB signaling (RT-qPCR, immunohistochemistry, and Western blotting) were evaluated.ResultsFive-month-old ApoE−/− mice exhibited typical clinical and histological features of MGD. These mice exhibited elevated tear levels of inflammatory cytokines and activation of the TLR4/NF-κB signaling pathway in the MGs and conjunctivae. Treatment with SB improved the corneal fluorescein staining score of MGD. The ApoE−/− mice demonstrated dysbiosis of OSM, characterized by an increase in Proteobacteria and a decrease in Bacteroidota. Additionally, the relative abundance of Muribacter and Muribacter muris increased in ApoE−/− mice, while that of Staphylococcus and Staphylococcus lentus decreased, and these alterations were restored by SB treatment. SB also reduced the expression of the TLR4/NF-κB p65 signaling pathway, inflammatory cytokines, and apoptosis in MGs and conjunctival tissues.ConclusionApoE−/− mice exhibited characteristic features of MGD, accompanied by dysbiosis of OSM. Topical administration of SB modulated the OSM and reduced MGD-associated inflammation. Background

With a global prevalence of 35.8%, meibomian gland dysfunction (MGD) stands as the leading cause of evaporative dry eye disease (DED) [1, 2]. Despite extensive study, the precise etiology and pathogenesis of MGD remain elusive. Factors such as eyelid margin inflammation, bacterial colonization, aging, diminished meibum quality, and other conditions have been implicated [2–4]. Increasing attention has been directed towards the inflammatory cascade as the central mechanism underlying MGD [5, 6].

The ocular surface mucosa faces continual exposure to the external environment, fostering the colonization of diverse microbial species, such as Corynebacteria, Propionibacteria, and Staphylococci, harboring in healthy conjunctiva [7, 8]. Previous studies have demonstrated significant alterations in the ocular surface microbiome (OSM) of patients with MGD [9–15]. Dong et al. [9] reported higher relative abundances of Staphylococcus and Sphingomonas and lower Corynebacterium in the conjunctival microbiome of MGD patients than in the controls. Similarly, Liang et al. [10] found that Staphylococcus aureus and Staphylococcus capitis were associated with MGD. Notably, a recent investigation revealed differential Staphylococcus colonization patterns in MGD patients with chronic Stevens-Johnson syndrome, showing a significant reduction in keratinized lid margins while maintaining predominance in adjacent eyelid skin [11]. However, current understanding of OSM alterations in MGD remains limited to cross-sectional observations and varies widely among different studies, precluding definitive conclusions regarding their pathogenic roles. The precise mechanisms underlying OSM-associated inflammation and the therapeutic potential of targeted microbiome modulation require further elucidation through animal model investigations.

Abnormalities of the meibum may cause bacterial proliferation that increases ocular surface inflammation, leading to the inflammation and hyperkeratinization of MG [4, 16]. Notably, bacterial endotoxin lipopolysaccharide can trigger inflammatory responses in human corneal, conjunctival, and meibomian gland epithelial cells [17, 18], playing a significant role in the inflammatory processes associated with MGD and DED [18–20]. However, the precise involvement of OSM in MGD-related inflammation remains to be fully elucidated.

Butyrate, a short-chain fatty acid, plays a pivotal role in maintaining microbial balance and regulating inflammation. Sodium butyrate (SB) has been shown to modulate gut microbiota and alleviate inflammation in conditions such as ulcerative colitis, steatohepatitis, Parkinson’s disease, and cholesterol gallstones in animal models [21–24]. It has also been utilized in clinical settings to modulate gut microbiota in patients with inflammatory bowel disease and irritable bowel syndrome [25, 26]. In ocular contexts, SB has demonstrated efficacy in ameliorating diabetic retinopathy by modulating gut microbiota [27], while topical application has shown promise in suppressing corneal inflammation associated with microbial keratitis and corneal alkali burn [28, 29]. Nonetheless, the effects of SB on dysbiosis of OSM and inflammation should be further studied.

In this study, apolipoprotein E gene knockout (ApoE−/−) mice were used as MGD models, exhibiting characteristic MGD phenotypes resulting from disrupted lipid and cholesterol metabolism [30]. Using these MGD mice, we investigated the relationship between OSM and inflammation. We then evaluated the therapeutic potential of SB by administering it to MGD mice and assessing its effects. We hypothesized that SB could modulate OSM and alleviate MGD-related inflammation, thereby providing new insights into its potential as a treatment strategy for MGD and related ocular surface inflammation.

Materials and methods

Establishment of the MGD models

Forty female ApoE−/− mice and 20 wild-type (WT) C57BL/6 mice (3-month-old) were obtained from Beijing Vital River Laboratory Animal Technology Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China). The mice were kept in specific pathogen-free conditions and fed standard rodent chow for 2 months before the start of the experiments. MGD models were induced in 5-month-old ApoE−/− mice according to the previous study [30]. ApoE−/− mice demonstrating characteristic eyelid margin abnormalities and fluorescein staining scores ≥ 5 were classified as MGD models, whereas WT mice exhibiting normal eyelid margins served as healthy controls. Among the experimental cohort, 36 (72 eyes) out of 40 ApoE−/− mice developed MGD phenotypes, while 16 (32 eyes) out of 20 WT mice maintained normal ocular surfaces and subsequently were utilized for experiments. The animal procedures strictly adhered to the guidelines outlined in the Association for Research in Vision and Ophthalmology Statement.

In vitro safety of sodium butyrate and animal intervention

Immortalized human corneal epithelial cells (HCECs) were cultured in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle medium (DMEM), 10% fetal bovine serum, 100U/mL penicillin, and 0.1 mg/mL streptomycin [31]. The cells were maintained in a 5% CO2 humidified incubator at 37 °C. Upon reaching 80–90% confluency, adherent cells were split. Medium renewal occurred every 2–3 days to ensure optimal growth conditions.

The 3-(4,5-dimethylthiazol-2-yl)−2,5-diphenyl tetrazolium bromide (MTT, Beyotime, Shanghai, China) method was employed to assess cell viability according to the manufacturer’s protocols. Briefly, 5000 HCECs were cultured in a 96-well plate for 24 h and exposed to varying concentrations (1 mM, 5 mM, 10 mM, and 100 mM) of SB for 24 and 48 h. Then, MTT solution was added to each well to achieve a final concentration of 0.5 mg/mL. After 4 h of incubation, 100 μL of formazan solution was added to each well and incubated for an additional 4 h. Optical density at 560 nm was then measured using a microplate reader to quantify cell viability.

After that, the safety-confirmed concentrations (1 mM, 5 mM, and 10 mM) of SB and sterile phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) were utilized to treat 5-month-old ApoE−/− mice who exhibited classic signs of MGD 4 times per day until 3 weeks (n = 5 mice/ group, 10 eyes). Positive controls consisted of untreated ApoE−/− mice with confirmed MGD, while age-matched wild-type (WT) mice without MGD manifestations served as healthy controls. Clinical examination

Lid margin abnormalities (including irregularities and obstructed MG orifices) and corneal fluorescein staining (CFS) were assessed using a slit-lamp biomicroscope. CFS was performed by instilling 1 μL of 0.1% liquid fluorescein disodium (Yuanye, Shanghai, China) into the conjunctival sac, with staining recorded after 90 s using a cobalt blue filter [30]. CFS score was quantified with a 4-point scale in each quadrant according to a previous study [32].

Tear secretion was measured by inserting a cotton phenol red thread (Jingming, Tianjin, China) into the lateral canthus of the conjunctival sac for 30 s, recording the length of the thread soaked. Tear samples for cytokine analysis were collected by dissolving 4 cotton phenol red threads from both eyes of each mouse in 60 μL of sterile PBS.

After triple disinfection of the periocular skin with 75% ethanol, specimens were collected from both the superior and inferior conjunctival sacs using sterile, individually packaged FLOQSwabs® Flocked Swabs 553C (Copan, Brescia, Italy) from the same batch. Immediately after sampling, swabs were returned to their sterile dry tubes, transported on ice, and stored at − 80 °C until DNA extraction and 16S rRNA gene sequencing [33].

Superior and inferior MGs and conjunctivae were excised using sterile microscissors following euthanasia. Infrared images of the MGs were captured using a Keratograph 5 M (Oculus, Wetzlar, Germany). The remaining tissues were stored at − 80 ℃ or embedded in optimal cutting temperature compound or paraffin for further experiments.

Plasma lipids analysis

Blood samples were collected from the euthanized mice via eyeball extraction. Plasma lipids, including total cholesterol, triglycerides, high-density lipoprotein (HDL), and low-density lipoprotein (LDL), were quantified using an enzymatic method following the guidelines set forth by the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention in China [34].

Tear cytokine analysis

Tear cytokines associated with DED were quantified using a multiple ELISA kit (QAH-DED-1, RayBiotech, Georgia, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions [32]. Antibodies targeting inflammatory mediators were employed in cytokine arrays. Following overnight incubation with tear samples, biotin-conjugated secondary antibodies were added and incubated for 2 h. This was followed by the addition of Cy3 dye-conjugated streptavidin and a subsequent 2-h incubation period. Signal intensities were captured using GenePix 4000 B (Bio-Rad, California, USA) and analyzed using Q-Analyzer software (RayBiotech, Georgia, USA). Cytokine concentrations were determined from standard curves according to the manufacturer’s protocol.

Histological analysis

MG tissues embedded in optimal cutting temperature compound or paraffin were sectioned continuously (5 μm for frozen sections and 3.5 μm for paraffin sections). Frozen sections were subjected to oil red O staining, while paraffin sections were utilized for hematoxylin and eosin (H&E), terminal deoxynucleotidyl transferase dUTP nick end labeling (TUNEL), periodic acid-Schiff (PAS), and immunostaining.

Meibum within the MGs was visualized in eyelid tissues via oil-red O staining (Solarbio, Beijing, China). Following rinsing with distilled water, frozen sections were immersed in 60% isopropyl alcohol for 20 s before staining with freshly prepared oil red O solution for 10 min. The sections were subsequently counterstained with hematoxylin and differentiated using 1% acid alcohol. Integrated lipid density was calculated using ImageJ software (National Institutes of Health, USA). Conjunctival goblet cells were evaluated using PAS staining. Paraffin sections were treated with an oxidant for 6 min and then stained with Schiff stain for 15 min in the dark before being counterstained with hematoxylin.

TUNEL staining

Apoptosis was assessed using the CoraLite®488 TUNEL Apoptosis Kit according to the manufacturer’s instructions (Proteintech, Wuhan, China). Paraffin sections were deparaffinized, hydrated, and incubated with proteinase K for 10 min. After washing with PBS, sections were incubated with TUNEL equilibration buffer for 5 min, followed by CoraLite®488 TUNEL reaction buffer containing terminal deoxynucleotidyl transferase for 2 h. Then, sections were washed with PBS containing 0.1% Triton X-100 and bovine serum albumin and stained with DAPI.

RNA extraction and qPCR

Three samples from each group, consisting of superior and inferior MGs and conjunctival tissues from one eye, were analyzed. Total RNA was extracted using the RNA Easy Fast Tissue/Cell Kit (Tiangen, Beijing, China) and reverse transcribed into cDNA using HiScript® III All-in-one RT SuperMix (Vazyme, Nanjing, China). Real-time quantitative polymerase chain reaction (qPCR) was performed using Tag Pro Universal SYBR qPCR Master Mix (Vazyme, Nanjing, China) on an ABI 7500 system (Applied Biosystems, California, USA). The qPCR cycling conditions were as follows: 95 ℃ for 30 s, followed by 40 cycles of 95 ℃ for 10 s for denaturation and 60 ℃ for 30 s for annealing and extension. The relative mRNA expression was calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt method. All primer sequences were provided in Additional file 1.

Immunostaining

Immunohistochemistry (IHC) staining was conducted to identify cells in conjunctivae and MGs positive for CD3 (ab16669, Abcam, 1:500), CD4 (ab183685, Abcam, 1:500), toll-like receptor 4 (TLR4, 19811-1-AP, Proteintech, 1:200), toll/interleukin-1 receptor domain-containing adaptor protein (TIRAP, ab17218, Abcam, 1:200), myeloid differentiation primary response 88 (MyD88, PA5-19918, Invitrogen, 1:250), tumor necrosis factor receptor-associated factor 6 (TRAF6, ab137452, Abcam, 1:200), interleukin-1 receptor-associated kinase (IRAK1, 10478-2-AP, Proteintech, 1:200), IkappaB kinase beta (IKKβ, ab124957, Abcam, 1:200), nuclear factor-kappa B (NF-κB) p65 (ab16502, Abcam, 1:200), and interferon-gamma (IFN-γ, bs-0480R, Bioss, 1:100), IL-17A antibody (26163-1-AP, Proteintech, 1:500), and inducible nitric oxide synthase (iNOS, 80517-1-RR, Proteintech, 1:200). Sections were then treated with a secondary goat anti-rabbit antibody (ab205718, Abcam, 1:1000) and stained with 3,3′-diaminobenzidine (DAB, ZSGB-BIO, Beijing, China). Positive cells in the conjunctivae were quantified using ImageJ software (National Institutes of Health, USA).

Immunofluorescence (IF) staining was conducted on the sections to detect cells positive for TLR4 (1:1000), NF-κB p65 (1:1000), IFN-γ (1:200), and tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α, ab1793, Abcam, 1:100) in the conjunctivae. Paraffin sections were incubated with primary rabbit anti-mouse antibodies overnight at 4 ℃, followed by incubation with a secondary goat anti-rabbit antibody (Alexa Fluor® 488) (ab150077, Abcam, 1:1000). Fluorescent images were captured using a fluorescence microscope (Olympus BX-51, Tokyo, Japan).

Western blotting

Equal amounts of proteins were separated by electrophoresis on a 10% polyacrylamide gel and transferred to polyvinylidene fluoride membranes [32]. After blocking with skim milk, membranes were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against TLR4 (1:1000), TRAF6 (1:1000), TIRAP (1:1000), IRAK1 (1:1000), MyD88 (1:1000), NF-κB p65 (1:1000), IFN-γ (1:1000), iNOS (1:1000), MMP-9 (ab38898, abcam, 1:1000), and β-actin (AC026, Abconal, 1:50,000). Following incubation with a secondary goat anti-rabbit antibody (1:5000), protein bands were visualized using a Tanon 5200 chemiluminescent imaging system (Tanon, Shanghai, China).

16S rRNA gene sequencing and bioinformatics analysis

The samples from the WT, ApoE−/−, PBS, and SB-treated (1, 5, and 10 mM) groups (n = 5/group) were used for 16S rRNA gene sequencing, with each sample comprising four conjunctival swabs taken from both eyes of a mouse. The samples from the periocular skin and surrounding hairs were also included (n = 5). DNA was extracted using the OMEGA Soil DNA Kit (Omega Bio-Tek, Georgia, USA) according to the manufacturer’s protocol [35]. The V3–V4 hypervariable regions of the bacterial 16S rRNA gene were amplified with 27 PCR cycles using Q5® High-Fidelity DNA Polymerase (New England Biolabs, Massachusetts, USA). The primers used were 5′-ACTCCTACGGGAGGCAGCA-3′ (forward) and reverse primer 5′-GGACTACHVGGGTWTCTAA T-3′ (reverse). PCR products were visualized by electrophoresis on 2% agarose gels (Invitrogen, California, USA) to confirm successful amplification and assess product size and specificity. Sterile ddH₂O was included as a negative control to monitor for contamination of reagents and the experimental workflow. Sequencing was conducted on the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform (Illumina, California, USA) utilizing 2 × 250 bp paired-end chemistry following the manufacturer’s standard protocols for library preparation and cluster generation. The 16S rRNA gene amplicon sequencing data were processed and analyzed using QIIME2 (version 2019.4) and R packages (v3.2.0) [36]. Paired-end reads were demultiplexed and quality-filtered using QIIME2, followed by denoising and chimera removal with the DADA2 plugin to generate amplicon sequence variants (ASVs) [37, 38]. Then, taxonomic classification was performed with a pre-trained naïve Bayes taxonomy classifier based on the SILVA database (release 138.1) [39]. Taxonomic composition and relative abundance at the phylum and genus levels were analyzed using QIIME2 and visualized using the R package ggplot2. Alpha diversity (Chao1 richness, Shannon diversity, and Simpson indices) and beta diversity were calculated in QIIME2 using Bray–Curtis distance metrics and visualized using the R package phyloseq with box plots and principal coordinate analysis (PCoA) [37]. Principal component analysis (PCA) was performed on genus-level compositional profiles. Differential abundance analysis was performed using linear discriminant analysis (LDA) effect size (LEfSe) with a significance threshold of log10 LDA score > 2 [40, 41].

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using Prism software (version 9.0; GraphPad, California, USA). Bioinformatics analysis was performed using the QIIME2 (version 2019.4) and R packages (v3.2.0). The normality of quantitative data was assessed via the Shapiro-Wilk test. Student’s t-test or Mann-Whitney test was utilized to assess between the two groups. Differences among three or more groups were analyzed using one-way ANOVA or the Kruskal-Wallis test, as appropriate. P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

ApoE−/− mice exhibited clinical and pathological features of MGD

At 5 months of age, ApoE−/− mice demonstrated higher body weights (P < 0.0001; Fig. 2b) and elevated levels of plasma total cholesterol, triglyceride, HDL, and LDL compared to WT mice (P < 0.0001, P = 0.001, P = 0.010, P < 0.0001; Fig. 2c–f). In comparison to WT mice, ApoE−/− mice displayed typical clinical and histological indicators, including increased hyperkeratinization and orifice plugging at the eyelid margin (Fig. 2a), a higher CFS score (P = 0.016; Fig. 2a and g), and a reduced area of inferior MGs (P = 0.035, P = 0.222; Fig. 2a, i, and j). Oil red O staining demonstrated lipid accumulation in the MG ducts and acini of ApoE−/− mice, as indicated by a significant difference in integrated lipid density (P = 0.034; Fig. 2l). No difference was observed between the PBS and ApoE−/− groups during the period of treatment (all P > 0.05; Fig. 5d–e). Sodium butyrate modulated the ocular surface microbiome in ApoE−/− mice

The OSM profiles of experimental samples differed significantly from those of the periocular skin, indicating that the ocular surface hosts a distinct microbial community (Figure S3). Due to the optimal effect of 5 mM SB in improving the clinical scores of ApoE−/− mice, the 5 mM SB group was utilized for comparative analysis of OSM levels with the WT, untreated ApoE−/−, and PBS-treated groups (Fig. 6). Alpha diversity analysis showed a significant decrease in the Chao 1 index in the SB group compared to the WT group (P = 0.022; Fig. 6a). However, no significant difference was observed in the Shannon index among the four groups (P = 0.093; Fig. 6a). The PBS group exhibited a lower Simpson index than the ApoE−/− group (P = 0.03; Fig. 6a), with no variation among the other groups. PCoA illustrated that the inter-habitat diversity of OSM in the SB group differed from that of the ApoE−/− group but resembled that of the WT group (Fig. 6b). PCA demonstrated that the SB group fell within the range of the WT group (Fig. 6c). Discussion

In this study, we observed that 5-month-old ApoE−/− mice presented the clinical and histological characteristics of MGD. These MGD mice displayed elevated levels of inflammatory cytokines in their tears and an upregulated TLR4/NF-κB signaling pathway in both conjunctival and MG tissues. MGD mice also exhibited significant dysbiosis of OSM. SB treatment effectively modulated OSM and attenuated inflammatory responses in MGD.

Hyperlipidemia plays a critical role in the pathogenesis of MGD. High levels of cholesterol esters, triglycerides, ceramides, and sphingomyelin are linked to abnormal meibum and increased saturated fatty acids [42]. These lipid abnormalities contribute to hyperkeratinization of eyelid margins, orifices, and MG ducts, resulting in ductal plugging and dilation [3]. Consistent with previous research, ApoE−/− mice in this study exhibited higher body weights and plasma lipid levels, including total cholesterol, triglycerides, HDL, and LDL [30, 36]. Accumulation of cellular debris and sebaceous substances further increased ductal pressure, causing acinar deformation and atrophy [43]. Additionally, MGD cases demonstrated more pronounced corneal fluorescein staining compared to controls [44].

MGD mice displayed significant ocular surface inflammation, consistent with previous studies [30]. A recent investigation found elevated concentrations of IL-6R, IL-8, MMP-9, ICAM-1, CCL4, and CCL5 in the tears of MGD patients [45]. Other studies have reported increased expression of inflammatory cytokines, including IL-1β, IL-6, IL-10, IL-17A, CXCL8, TNF-α, MMP-9, ICAM-1, and IFN-γ, in the MGs and tears of MGD patients, correlating with clinical severity [44, 46–48]. Treatment with topical corticosteroids led to clinical improvement and decreased tear cytokine levels [49]. The TLR4/NF-κB signaling pathway is pivotal in the mechanism of MGD both in vivo and in vitro [18, 50]. Increased iNOS expression, indicative of inflammation, was observed in conjunctivae and MGs in MGD, similar to findings in the lacrimal glands of Sjögren’s syndrome mice [51]. This inflammatory status leads to apoptosis of conjunctival and meibomian gland cells [5].

The ocular surface microbiome was imbalanced in MGD mice. The significant reduction in the Chao1 index, which estimates species richness, indicates a substantial depletion of rare taxa within the OSM. Conversely, the stable Shannon index, reflecting both species richness and evenness, suggests the preservation of dominant microbial populations’ relative abundance. These findings are consistent with our observations demonstrating selective reduction of low-abundance species while maintaining stable colonization patterns of the core commensal microbiome. At the phylum level, the OSM of MGD mice demonstrated compositional similarities with that of MGD patients, characterized by the predominance of Actinobacteria, Firmicutes, and Proteobacteria [9, 12, 52]. In particular, MGD had increased Proteobacteria in both patients and mice [9]. While Firmicutes reported a significant increase in MGD patients, this trend was not statistically significant in mouse models. Notably, Bacteroidetes, a major producer of short-chain fatty acids, showed significant depletion in MGD mice, consistent with previous reports of reduced Bacteroidetes abundance in patients with MGD-associated dry eye compared to the non-MGD subtype [52, 53].

Muribacter muris, previously associated with cutaneous inflammation in oxazolone-induced contact hypersensitivity models, was significantly elevated in MGD mice and subsequently attenuated by SB treatment, suggesting its potential role in MGD-related ocular surface inflammation [54]. Experimental evidence further supports Muribacter’s pro-inflammatory role across multiple anatomical compartments: short-term inflammation in nasal and middle ear tissues following controlled microbial challenge and enhanced oral colonization correlating with oropharyngeal carcinogenesis progression [55, 56]. These observations collectively implicate Muribacter as a potential mediator of inflammation in diverse epithelial environments. Corynebacterium and coagulase-negative Staphylococcus constitute core components of the healthy conjunctival microbiota, playing crucial roles in immune modulation and microbial defense [57, 58]. Our findings demonstrate significant depletion of Staphylococcus in MGD murine models, consistent with previous reports in both MGD [11] and blepharitis [59]. While shotgun metagenomic analyses have identified Staphylococcus aureus and Staphylococcus capitis as MGD-associated species [10], we observed the predominance of Staphylococcus lentus, a species implicated in neonatal health maintenance [60].

SB alleviated dysbiosis of OSM and reduced inflammation in MGD. It restored the relative abundance of key bacterial groups, including Actinobacteriota, Firmicutes, Proteobacteria, Muribacter, Staphylococcus, and Muribacter muris in OSM. Additionally, SB demonstrated the ability to restore gut microbiota in tibial chondrodysplasia by decreasing abnormally high levels of Proteobacteria and increasing abnormally low levels of Bacteroidetes in broiler chickens [61]. However, in our study, SB did not restore the abundance of Bacteroidetes, suggesting a delayed restoration compared to Proteobacteria. SB increased Actinobacteriota and decreased the up-regulated Proteobacteria in MGD mice, but did not restore the reduced Bacteroidetes. The reduction of butyrate-producing species is crucial in microbiota dysbiosis in patients with ulcerative colitis. Oral SB inhibits inflammation and improves the intestinal barrier in ulcerative colitis [24, 62]. It increases concentrations of butyric acid and total short-chain fatty acids, which help modulate gut microbiota and enhance the early development and function of the small intestine [63]. Furthermore, SB decreases IL-6, TNF-α, IL-1β, TLR4, and NF-κB p65 in mouse models, improving acute lung and intestinal injuries, as well as LPS-induced apoptosis [64, 65]. In vitro studies have shown that SB exhibits anti-inflammatory properties by suppressing the TLR4/NF-κB p65 signaling pathway in LPS-induced bovine embryo trachea cells [66]. However, this study has some limitations. First, the sample size is relatively small, suggesting the need for a larger study to confirm our findings. Second, although samples were pooled to increase microbial yield, the inherent low microbial biomass of the conjunctiva resulted in low sequencing depth. Accurate analysis in such low-biomass contexts requires rigorous use and interpretation of negative controls to differentiate true signals from background noise. Although periocular skin and sterile ddH₂O controls were used to minimize contamination from the environment, reagents, and sequencing process, blank swabs were not included to fully exclude potential background noise from the swab material or transport. Therefore, further studies are warranted to optimize control design and develop decontamination methods specifically tailored to the unique challenges posed by the low-biomass microbiome. Third, the study was limited to female mice, and potential sex-based differences in microbial profiles or responses in males were not assessed. Fourth, the MGD models used may not capture the full etiological spectrum of human MGD, which could limit the generalizability of the observed effects of SB. Finally, interspecies differences in OSM between mice and humans underscore the need for further research to evaluate SB’s impact on OSM composition and inflammation in human MGD.

Conclusion

ApoE−/− mice exhibited clinical and inflammatory characteristics of MGD, along with dysbiosis of OSM. Topical administration of SB modulated the OSM and reduced MGD-associated inflammation. These findings suggest a potential novel therapeutic approach for managing inflammation in MGD.
