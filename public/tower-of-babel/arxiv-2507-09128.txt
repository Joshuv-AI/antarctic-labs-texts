A Generalization Theory for Zero-Shot Prediction
Ronak Mehta, Zaid Harchaoui

Abstract
A modern paradigm for generalization in machine learning and AI consists of pre-training a task-agnostic foundation model, generally obtained using self-supervised and multimodal contrastive learning. The resulting representations can be used for prediction on a downstream task for which no labeled data is available. We present a theoretical framework to better understand this approach, called zero-shot prediction. We identify the target quantities that zero-shot prediction aims to learn, or learns in passing, and the key conditional independence relationships that enable its generalization ability.

Introduction

In 2021, OpenAI shocked the world by improving the zero-shot classification accuracy on ImageNet from 11.5% to 76.2% via the CLIP series of models
(
Radford et al. 2021
)
. This event redefined the goal of zero-shot prediction from producing models that generalized to
unseen classes
to those that generalized to
unseen tasks
entirely. Two fundamental drivers of CLIP’s success were 1) the use of natural language as a medium for representing arbitrary classes (as in the previous state-of-the-art Visual N-grams
(
Li et al. 2017
)
), and 2) a massive, yet carefully designed pre-training set which significantly impacted downstream performance
Radford et al. 2021
;
Fang et al. 2023
;
Xu et al. 2024
. Despite the remarkable success of these foundation model-based pipelines
Bommasani et al. 2022
, there are unique components of zero-shot prediction that warrant investigation from a theoretical point of view.

To clarify these gaps, we contrast zero-shot prediction (ZSP) with the related setting of few-shot learning (FSL). Let
\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathscr{X}
denote an input (often an image) that accompanies a discrete value
\boldsymbol{y}\in\mathscr{Y}
(often a class label).
Common to both ZSP and FSL is a pre-training procedure in which a large unlabeled dataset
\boldsymbol{x}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{x}_{N}\in\mathscr{X}
is used to produce an
encoder

\boldsymbol{\alpha}:\mathscr{X}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d}
. The
embedding

\boldsymbol{\alpha}(\boldsymbol{x})
is thought to contain information that is relevant for predicting
\boldsymbol{y}
from
\boldsymbol{x}
. Pre-training typically occurs through the process of self-supervised learning (SSL), using a
pretext task
that can be solved with only instances of
\boldsymbol{x}
(e.g. filling in a blank image patch).
In FSL, the user may then access a labeled dataset
(\boldsymbol{x}^{\mathrm{lab}}_{1},\boldsymbol{y}^{\mathrm{lab}}_{1}),\ldots,(\boldsymbol{x}^{\mathrm{lab}}_{n},\boldsymbol{y}^{\mathrm{lab}}_{n})
from which a predictor can be trained inexpensively. This often takes the form of a linear classifier
\boldsymbol{x}\mapsto\mathbf{W}\boldsymbol{\alpha}(\boldsymbol{x})+\boldsymbol{b}
for
\mathbf{W}\in\mathbb{R}^{\left|\mathscr{Y}\right|\times d}
and
\boldsymbol{b}\in\mathbb{R}^{\left|\mathscr{Y}\right|}
. Where ZSP departs from FSL is the additional challenge of being given
no directly labeled training data
.

At first glance, ZSP seems impossible. Yet, the ingenuity of practitioners has yielded the following solution; if 1) each pre-training example
\boldsymbol{x}_{i}
is paired with another “view”
\boldsymbol{z}_{i}\in\mathscr{Z}
(e.g. a caption in natural language) and 2) if each label
\boldsymbol{y}\in\mathscr{Y}
can intelligently be embedded into
\mathscr{Z}
, then the relationship between each
\boldsymbol{x}_{i}
and
\boldsymbol{z}_{i}
could provide the means to perform prediction. Concretely, one learns a complementary encoder
\boldsymbol{\beta}:\mathscr{Z}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d}
during pre-training and designs
prompts

\boldsymbol{z}_{k}^{y}
for
\boldsymbol{y}\in\mathscr{Y}
and
k=1,\ldots,m
. Then,

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{x}\mapsto\argmax_{\boldsymbol{y}\in\mathscr{Y}}\frac{1}{m}\sum_{k=1}^{m}\langle\boldsymbol{\alpha}(\boldsymbol{x}),\boldsymbol{\beta}(\boldsymbol{z}_{k}^{y})\rangle

(1)

is employed for prediction. An example of a prompt is the template text “photo of a

.”, where the blank can be filled by the textual representation of the class (e.g. “cat” or “dog”). The ZSP pipeline, from pre-training to prompt selection, is clearly a wild departure from what is explained by statistical learning theory. Moreover, while some components of these systems have been studied in the context of FSL (such as the reasons why various pre-training objectives result in encoders that provably accelerate learning), unique aspects of ZSP, such as the role of prompting and the cost of “translating” modalities have not yet received theoretical treatment. Herein lies our question.

Through what decomposition of downstream task performance can we compare zero-shot prediction to the direct supervised learner, with a transparent dependence on the 1) pre-training distribution, 2) evaluation distribution, and 3) prompting strategy?

Contributions.

In
Section

2
, we present a learning theoretic framework for the pre-training/evaluation/prompting data and
propose two expressions for the population counterpart of (
1
).
These expressions, while equivalent at the population level, reflect two classes of learning methods which we call the “conditional mean” and the “information density” approaches.
In
Section

3
, we prove a generic decomposition of the prediction error on the downstream task, which furnishes three components:
prompt bias
measures the compatibility of the prompt strategy with the pre-training and evaluation distributions,
residual dependence
measures the information-theoretic cost of using one modality to make predictions on another, and
estimation error
quantifies the effect of the finite number of pre-training examples and prompts. The estimation error decomposes further depending on whether the conditional mean or information density approaches are taken. To provide insight and demonstrate the usefulness of the decomposition, we analyze the performance of nonparametric regression methods for each approach by way of finite-sample bounds in high probability. Our framework arms practitioners with a means to imbue existing SSL-to-ZSP pipelines with theoretical guarantees, depending on the approach with which they best align.
In
Section

4
, we illustrate our theoretical claims by empirically evaluating prompt bias and residual dependence on zero-shot prediction tasks with simulated and image data.

Related Work.

One can argue that precursors to both FSL and ZSP in
machine learning can be found in the literature
of meta-learning, or “learning to learn”
(
Thrun and Pratt 1998
;
Andrychowicz et al. 2016
;
Finn et al. 2017
)
. There, the downstream evaluation tasks are given to the user upfront, so that pre-training an encoder and training a predictor for all of the evaluation tasks can be performed in one step.
On the other hand, FSL and ZSP both involve fully task-agnostic pre-training phases.
Seminal work in computer vision on matching words and pictures is also worth mentioning
Barnard et al. 2003
;
Forsyth et al. 2009
.

Two complementary bodies of work studied phenomena common to FSL and ZSP. The first considers which properties of learned encoders can provably improve downstream performance
(
Wang and Isola 2020
;
HaoChen et al. 2021
;
Atzmon et al. 2020
;
Wang and Jordan 2024
;
Du and Xiang 2024
)
. The other is dedicated to explaining how otherwise mysterious SSL objectives achieve these properties
(
Wen and Li 2021
;
Li et al. 2021
;
Pokle et al. 2022
;
Kiani et al. 2022
;
Johnson et al. 2023
;
Shwartz-Ziv et al. 2023
)
. In particular,
Balestriero and LeCun 2022
and
Tan et al. 2024
relate
various SSL objectives to spectral clustering.
One FSL-specific line of work studies when linear mappings of pre-trained encoders can achieve optimal downstream performance
(
Saunshi et al. 2019
;
HaoChen et al. 2021
;
Tosh et al. 2021
;
Lee et al. 2021
)
.

While informative representations are essential, the core of ZSP is the remarkable ability of models to make predictions without
any
task-specific data, a challenge even for the perfect encoder. For context, we avoid the historical term “zero-shot learning”
(
Larochelle et al. 2008
;
Akata et al. 2015
)
, which refers to a setting in which pre-training data is not only
labeled
, but contains metadata-based features associated with each class. In general, this only handles unseen classes, and only if the same features are observed at inference time.
To our knowledge, the only work studying ZSP based on self-supervised pre-training is
Chen et al. 2024
. In particular,
Chen et al. 2024
provides bounds on the top-
k
accuracy of ZSP for CLIP-based encoders. However, the bound
increases
with the batch size, may not decay to zero even if the pre-training loss is fully optimized and upstream and downstream data distributions are the same, and does not seem to explicitly depend on the prompt quality.
The independent and concurrent work of
Oko et al. 2025
develops a statistical analysis based on sufficiency notions, with the aim of capturing the predictive performance in the downstream task.
Their work is complementary to ours, in that they determine the distributional parameter learned by
the CLIP objective,
but also assume that the prompting strategy and downstream data distribution are “idealized”, in that the prompt bias and residual dependence quantities alluded to in the contributions are zero.

On the applied side, we are inspired by the number of works that use diverse, class-specific prompts generated using large language models (LLMs) for enhancing ZSP performance
(
Pratt et al. 2023
;
Yang et al. 2023
;
Maniparambil et al. 2023
)
and interpretability
(
Menon and Vondrick 2023
;
Esfandiarpoor et al. 2024
)
. While these empirical methods, such as the customized prompts via language models method (CuPL,
Pratt et al. 2023
), are often designed using intuition from human understanding of natural language, we aim to offer a theoretical explanation for their success from a statistical learning theory
and probabilistic graphical modeling
perspective. Despite this particular application of LLMs, we also acknowledge that “prompting” in ZSP has a different meaning than in the growing field of prompt engineering, in which inputs are designed for large language models
(
Pryzant et al. 2023
;
Wang et al. 2024
;
Guo et al. 2024
;
Sclar et al. 2024
)
.

Prediction Paths of FSL and ZSP.

For context, we contrast our setup with previous theoretical analyses of FSL, aiming to 1) highlight the fundamental differences between SSL-for-FSL and SSL-for-ZSP, 2) describe assumptions we make (and do not make) to best align with applications.
First, we consider two common SSL tasks that precede FSL. In unimodal contrastive learning,
X
and
Z
are augmented/corrupted images, and the pretext task is to identify examples derived from the same (“
+
”) or different (“
-
”) underlying image
(
Chen et al. 2020
)
. In reconstructive SSL, the encoder is pre-trained to predict a hidden portion of the raw/embedded image
(
Assran et al. 2023
)
.
Foundational works such as
Saunshi et al. 2019
and
Wang and Isola 2020
explain the success of these SSL-for-FSL pipelines by the following mechanism: the labels
\mathscr{Y}
used in the downstream task form a latent variable mixture model for the pre-training set, i.e.
Q_{X,Z}=\sum_{\boldsymbol{y}\in\mathscr{Y}}Q_{X,Z|Y=\boldsymbol{y}}\cdot Q_{Y}(\boldsymbol{y})
. Thus, generalization guarantees hinge upon the fact that learning parameters of the pre-training distribution must inherently capture its latent variables (the downstream labels). This theory is visualized in
Figure

1
(left & center); observe that if the dotted arrows were absent, the only path to solve the pretext task is
through
the label.
This FSL “prediction path” motivates another prevalent assumption of exact/approximate conditional independence of
X
and
Z
given
Y
(e.g., as in
Lee et al. 2021

latent label model
assumes equality of the marginals
P_{X}=Q_{X}
on
\mathscr{X}
. As a concrete example, this amounts to assuming that the marginal distribution of images on the Internet (
Q_{X}
) is equal to that of CIFAR-10 images (

). We explicitly track this mismatch in our generalization bounds.

For ZSP, the prevailing SSL pretext task is multimodal contrastive learning (

Generalization Guarantees for ZSP

In this section, we prove generalization guarantees for ZSP methods by comparing
\eta_{\star}

\eta_{\rho}

\eta_{\rho}

(Y_{1},Z_{1}^{\prime}),\ldots,(Y_{M},Z_{M}^{\prime})\overset{\text{\tiny i.i.d}}{\sim}\rho_{Y,Z}

We consider specific instances of both the conditional mean and information density approaches, based on learning theory in reproducing kernel Hilbert space (RKHS); our arguments do not intend to interpret foundation modeling as a kernel method, but to use the detailed analysis of the statistical errors in kernel methods to gain insight. In particular, we aim to expose two key dependences for the random triple

For

\displaystyle 2\underbrace{\lVert\eta_{\star}-\eta_{\rho}\rVert_{\mathbf{L}^{2}(P_{X})}^{2}}_{\text{information-theoretic error}}+2\underbrace{\lVert\eta_{\rho}-\hat{\eta}_{\rho}\rVert_{\mathbf{L}^{2}(P_{X})}^{2}}_{\text{estimation error}}.

(12)

The information-theoretic error captures the prompt bias and residual dependence that differentiates indirect and direct prediction, whereas the estimation error is a familiar term in statistical analysis. We discuss in
Section

D.4
how to convert the MSE bounds to risk bounds for classification.

Prompt Bias and Residual Dependence.

Here, we control the information-theoretic error term in (
12
). We state our assumptions regarding conditional probability informally and defer the formal descriptions using the language of regular conditional distributions to
Appendix

C
. We work within the latent caption model from
Section

2
, for which we consider a joint distribution
P_{X,Y,Z}
on
\mathscr{X}\times\mathscr{Y}\times\mathscr{Z}
which equals the evaluation distribution
P_{X,Y}
when marginalized over
\mathscr{Z}
.
Similar to the information density
\mathsf{R}
from
Section

2
, we introduce the conditional information density

\displaystyle\mathsf{S}_{\boldsymbol{z}}:=\frac{\mathrm{d}P_{X,Y|\boldsymbol{z}}}{\mathrm{d}(P_{X|\boldsymbol{z}}P_{Y|\boldsymbol{z}})}:\mathscr{X}\times\mathscr{Y}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0},

(13)

where
P_{X,Y|\boldsymbol{z}}
denotes the conditional distribution of
(X,Y)
given
Z=\boldsymbol{z}
, and
P_{X|\boldsymbol{z}}P_{Y|\boldsymbol{z}}
is defined analogously.
This naturally motivates the conditional dependence measure given by

\displaystyle I(X;Y|\boldsymbol{z})={\mathbb{E}}_{P_{X|\boldsymbol{z}}P_{Y|\boldsymbol{z}}}\left[(\mathsf{S}_{\boldsymbol{z}}(X,Y)-1)^{2}\right],

(14)

called the
conditional mean square contingency
.
Finally, consider the following regularity assumption on the joint distribution
P_{X,Y,Z}
, also discussed in
Appendix

C
.

Assumption 1
.

P_{X,Y,Z}
on
\mathscr{X}\times\mathscr{Y}\times\mathscr{Z}
satisfies the following:
1) Agreement of caption distribution:

P_{X}
-almost all
\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathscr{X}
,
P_{Z|\boldsymbol{x}}
exists and
P_{Z|\boldsymbol{x}}=Q_{Z|\boldsymbol{x}}
.
2) Regularity of conditional distributions:
For
P_{Z}
-almost all
\boldsymbol{z}\in\mathscr{Z}
,
P_{X,Y|\boldsymbol{z}}
exists,
P_{X,Y|\boldsymbol{z}}\ll P_{X|\boldsymbol{z}}P_{Y|\boldsymbol{z}}
, and the conditional information density (
13
) satisfies
{\mathbb{E}}_{P_{X,Y|\boldsymbol{z}}}\left[\mathsf{S}_{\boldsymbol{z}}(X,Y)\right]<+\infty
and
{\mathbb{E}}_{P_{X,Y,Z}}\left[\mathsf{S}_{Z}(X,Y)\right]<+\infty
.

To measure the bias of the prompt distribution
\rho_{Y,Z}
, we denote the analog of (
5
) under
P_{Y,Z}
as

\displaystyle g_{P_{Y,Z}}(\boldsymbol{z})={\mathbb{E}}_{P_{Y,Z}}\left[r(Y)|Z\right](\boldsymbol{z}).

We may now state the main result, proved in
Appendix

C
.

Theorem 1
.

Under
1
,

\displaystyle\lVert\eta_{\rho}-\eta_{\star}\rVert_{\mathbf{L}^{2}(P_{X})}^{2}\lesssim\underbrace{{\mathbb{E}}_{P_{Z}}\left[I(X;Y|Z)\right]}_{\text{residual dependence}}+\underbrace{\lVert g_{\rho}-g_{P_{Y,Z}}\rVert_{\mathbf{L}^{2}(P_{Z})}^{2}}_{\text{prompt bias}}.

(15)

To give context to
Theorem

1
, conditional independence relations have previously been used to describe the performance of multimodal contrastive SSL for FSL. We are particularly inspired by the
multi-view redundancy
theory of
Tosh et al. 2021
, which states informally that the population FSL predictor can approach the performance of the idealized direct predictor that is given
both

X
and
Z
at test time, if
X\perp\!\!\!\!\perp Y|Z
and
Z\perp\!\!\!\!\perp Y|X
approximately hold. However, the theory of graphical models
(
Lauritzen 1996
, Proposition 3.1)
asserts that both conditional independence relations hold only if
(X,Z)\perp\!\!\!\!\perp Y
, that is, neither view has information predictive of the label. This can be seen intuitively from
Figure

1
by breaking the arrows
X\rightarrow Y
and
Z\rightarrow Y
. Notice that we compare only to the direct predictor (
3
) given
X
(which is reflective of practice), so that we need only that
X\perp\!\!\!\!\perp Y|Z
(i.e.
X
depends on
Y
through
Z
) to close the gap. The prompt bias term (
15
) captures the possible incompatibility of the prompt distribution
\rho_{Y,Z}
with
(P_{X,Y},Q_{X,Z})
—we call prompt strategies unbiased (see
Section

D.5
) when this term is zero.

Sample Complexity and Distribution Mismatch.

The first step in our estimation error analysis is to pass the
\mathbf{L}^{2}(P_{X})
-norm term
\lVert\eta_{\rho}-\hat{\eta}_{\rho}\rVert_{\mathbf{L}^{2}(P_{X})}^{2}
from (
12
) to the
\mathbf{L}^{2}(Q_{X})
-norm counterpart
\lVert\eta_{\rho}-\hat{\eta}_{\rho}\rVert_{\mathbf{L}^{2}(Q_{X})}^{2}
. We then establish high-probability bounds on the
\mathbf{L}^{2}(Q_{X})
-norm term, with respect to the random sampling of the pre-training and prompting data. Because this initial step follows from a standard distribution shift argument (based on either a bounded likelihood ratio assumption or an additive error in total variation distance), we defer it to
Appendix

D
(see
Lemma

14
). Conceptually, the two examples below are derived from estimating the component of either (
7
) or (
8
) that involves
Q_{X,Z}
using the pre-training set and the one that involves
\rho_{Y,Z}
using the prompt strategy. In both cases, we discuss the convergence rates of state-of-the-art RKHS-based methods. As we review
Section

B.4
, these rates are typically expressed in terms of two quantities:
source condition
constants, which measure the smoothness of the target function being learned, and
eigendecay exponents
of covariance operators, which measure the effective dimension of the data. It will serve our purposes to interpret the rates in terms of the dependence between
X
and
Z
under
Q_{X,Z}
, under the following assumption.

Assumption 2
.

The pre-training distribution satisfies
Q_{X,Z}\ll Q_{X}Q_{Z}
, and the information density
\mathsf{R}
is contained in
\mathbf{L}^{2}(Q_{X}Q_{Z})
(i.e.
I(X;Z)
is well-defined).

Due to the technical overhead of each method (especially regarding mis-specified function classes), we provide high-level descriptions below and defer detailed descriptions of the specific estimation procedures and formal assumptions to
Section

D.1
(conditional mean) and
Section

D.2
(information density).
We denote by
\delta\in(0,1]
a failure probability, and
\operatorname{plog}(\cdot)
a term that is poly-logarithmic in its input.

Example 1: Nonparametric Regression.

This approach, based on (
7
), uses the pre-training set to produce an estimate
\widehat{\mathbf{M}}_{Z|X}
of the conditional mean operator and the prompts to produce an approximation
\hat{g}_{\rho}:\mathscr{Z}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
of
g_{\rho}
. For the former, we use as an example the spectral regularization learning method of
Meunier et al. 2024
, which produces a conditional mean embedding function
\widehat{F}:\mathscr{X}\rightarrow\mathcal{G}
, for an RKHS
\mathcal{G}
of real-valued functions of
\mathscr{Z}
. For any
g\in\mathcal{G}
, we then define
[\widehat{\mathbf{M}}_{Z|X}g](\boldsymbol{x})={\langle g,\widehat{F}(\boldsymbol{x})\rangle}_{\mathcal{G}}
. Note that
\widehat{F}
predicts a target that is itself a function–such methods are therefore referred to as “vector-valued” regression. By the Reisz representation theorem, a similar function
F_{\star}
can be constructed such that
[\mathbf{M}_{Z|X}g](\boldsymbol{x})={\langle g,F_{\star}(\boldsymbol{x})\rangle}_{\mathcal{G}}
.
For
\hat{g}_{\rho}
, we consider standard kernel regularized least-squares (e.g.,
Smale and Zhou 2007
) applied to
M
i.i.d. draws from
\rho_{Y,Z}
. Assuming that
g_{\rho}\in\mathcal{G}
, one can then pass the problem to controlling
\lVert\hat{g}_{\rho}-g_{\rho}\rVert_{\mathcal{G}}^{2}
and
\lVert\widehat{F}-F_{\star}\rVert_{\mathbf{L}^{2}(Q_{X};\mathcal{G})}^{2}
, where
\mathbf{L}^{2}(Q_{X};\mathcal{G})
denotes a Bochner space (reviewed in
Section

B.4
).

To derive the convergence rates below, we show in
Section

D.1
that the source condition on
F_{\star}
can be expressed in terms of the singular decay exponent of
\mathbf{M}_{Z|X}
(i.e.
\sigma_{i}\sim i^{-\gamma_{X,Z}}
from (
11
)), and the eigendecay exponents
\gamma_{X}
and
\gamma_{Z}
of the covariance operators of
Q_{X}
and
Q_{Z}
, respectively. Additionally,
\omega_{\rho}>1/2
is a parameter controlling the convergence rate of the prompt-based estimate of
g_{\rho}
. The parametrization below is chosen so that one may interpret
\omega_{\rho}
as a similar source condition for the target function
g_{\rho}
.
In the well-specified case (when
F_{\star}
is contained in the hypothesis class), we describe the convergence rate with the aggregated exponent

\displaystyle q(t)=(2\gamma_{X,Z}+\gamma_{Z}-1)^{t}\gamma_{X}^{1-t}\geq 1,\quad t\in[0,1)

where
t
depends on
F_{\star}
. The result below corresponds to
Theorem

10
in
Section

D.1
, which relies on a basis alignment assumption to aggregate the singular/eigendecays.

Theorem 2

(Informal)
.

For
\hat{\eta}_{\rho}(\boldsymbol{x})={\langle\hat{g}_{\rho},\widehat{F}(\boldsymbol{x})\rangle}_{\mathcal{G}}
, there exist
t\in[0,1)
and
C(Q_{X,Z})\geq 0
(independent of
(N,M,\delta)
) such that

\displaystyle\lVert\hat{\eta}_{\rho}-\eta_{\rho}\rVert_{\mathbf{L}^{2}(Q_{X})}^{2}\lesssim\operatorname{plog}\left(1/\delta\right)\left[N^{-\frac{q(t)}{q(t)+1}}+C(Q_{X,Z})M^{-\frac{2\omega_{\rho}-1}{2\omega_{\rho}+1}}\right]

(16)

with probability at least
1-\delta
for
N
sufficiently large.

Let us interpret the constant
q(t)
. First, the dependence on
N
ranges between
O(N^{-1/2})
and the parametric rate
O(N)
. Convergence is faster when
\gamma_{X,Z}\gg 1
or
\gamma_{Z}\gg 1
. The first case implies near-independence of
X
and
Z
, for which learning is easy as
\widehat{F}(\boldsymbol{x})
is essentially constant over
\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathscr{X}
. The second case indicates that the
Z
variable is near-finite dimensional, or that the vector-valued nature of the problem has been reduced to standard univariate regression. Convergence is slower if
\gamma_{X}\gg 1
, or if the effective dimension of
\mathscr{X}
is small relative to the effective dimension of
Z
. The balancing constant
C(Q_{X,Z})
(shown explicitly in
Theorem

10
) decays with
\gamma_{X,Z}
and
\gamma_{Z}
, so as
(X,Z)
becomes more independent or
Z
approaches finite dimensions, the variance from prompt sampling decreases. We also discuss the mis-specified case in
Section

D.1
.

Example 2: Radon-Nikodym Derivative Estimation.

This approach, based on (
8
), considers pre-training to return a learned information density
\widehat{\mathsf{R}}:\mathscr{X}\times\mathscr{Z}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
. By approximating the prompt distribution
\rho_{Y,Z}
with
\hat{\rho}_{Y,Z}
(e.g. the empirical measure in the result below), one may define the estimator
\hat{\eta}_{\rho}(\boldsymbol{x})=\mathbb{E}_{\hat{\rho}_{Y,Z}}[r(Y)\widehat{\mathsf{R}}(\boldsymbol{x},Z)]
. Similar in spirit to the previous example, we consider the kernel Radon-Nikodym derivative estimation with the spectral regularization procedure of
Nguyen et al. 2024
. The convergence rate of
\widehat{\mathsf{R}}
to
\mathsf{R}
is governed by a source condition constant
\beta\geq 1
associated to
\mathsf{R}
(see
Section

D.2
). We interpret this constant analogously to
q(t)
, in that we prove a relationship to the singular decay exponent
\gamma_{X,Z}
, but is not directly expressible in terms of the latter.

The following result corresponds to
Theorem

11
in
Section

D.2
.

Theorem 3

(Informal)
.

For
\hat{\eta}_{\rho}(\boldsymbol{x})=\mathbb{E}_{\hat{\rho}_{Y,Z}}[r(Y)\widehat{\mathsf{R}}(\boldsymbol{x},Z)]
, and
\rho_{Z}\ll Q_{Z}
, there exists
C_{\mathsf{R},\rho}(Q_{X})\geq 0
(independent of
(N,M,\delta)
) such that

\displaystyle\lVert\hat{\eta}_{\rho}-\eta_{\rho}\rVert_{\mathbf{L}^{2}(Q_{X})}^{2}\lesssim\operatorname{plog}\left(1/\delta\right)\left[N^{-\frac{\beta}{\beta+1}}+C_{\mathsf{R},\rho}(Q_{X})M^{-1}\right]\hskip-1.0pt+\hskip-1.0ptD_{\chi^{2}}(\rho_{Z}\|Q_{Z})

with probability at least
1-\delta
for all
N
sufficiently large.

Notice that the bound of
Theorem

3

includes a divergence term between
\rho_{Z}
(the captions generated by prompting) and
Q_{Z}
(the captions of the pre-training set). This term comes precisely from the error term in (
8
). This elucidates the fact that the conditional mean approach and the information density are not equivalent representations of the pre-training distribution, as one needs
both

\mathsf{R}
and
Q_{Z}
in order to identify the conditional mean.
The parametric rate
M^{-1}
reflects that samples are used to learn a joint expectation over
\rho_{Y,Z}
, which is an easier statistical problem than estimating the regression function of
Y
on
Z
that appears in
Theorem

2
. Thus, the information density approach may enjoy faster statistical convergence, at the expense of bias from the distribution mismatch on
\mathscr{Z}
. The constant
C_{\mathsf{R},\rho}(Q_{X})
relates to the
\mathbf{L}^{2}(Q_{X})
-norm of the random function
\boldsymbol{x}\mapsto r(Y)\mathsf{R}(\boldsymbol{x},Z)
for
(Y,Z)\sim\rho_{Y,Z}
; the error from finite prompts decays when this norm is light-tailed.

In both
Theorem

2
and
Theorem

3
, we aim to highlight not particular convergence rates of the chosen methods, but the framework that leads to proving them. Similar results can also be leveraged in our framework. SSL procedures such as noise contrastive estimation have been related to the estimation of
\mathsf{R}

(
Gutmann and Hyvärinen 2012
)
. For example,
Tosh et al. 2021
upper bounds
\lVert\widehat{\mathsf{R}}-\mathsf{R}\rVert_{\mathbf{L}^{2}(Q_{X}Q_{Z})}^{2}
using the suboptimality of the population risk, allowing for empirical risk minimization-style analysis.

Experiments

In
Section

1
, we asked how the downstream task performance depends on the pre-training distribution
Q_{X,Z}
, evaluation distribution

, and prompting strategy
\rho_{Y,Z}

and

(

)

). This allows us to understand the effect of prompt bias by comparing template-based prompting strategies to the unbiased setting
\rho_{Y,Z}=P_{Y,Z}
. To understand the dependence on
\rho_{Y,Z}
at a sample level, we explore how downstream performance scales with the number of prompts
M
in both the second experiment (unbiased prompting) and third experiment (LLM-based prompting). We are particularly interested in verifying the dependence on
M
(which is the dominant error when
N\gg M
) derived in

).

Classification Accuracy and Residual Dependence.

We consider a simulated binary classification task in which all distributions are compatible (i.e.
Q_{X,Z}=P_{X,Z}
and
\rho_{Y,Z}=P_{Y,Z}
for some
P_{X,Y,Z}
) and the predictors (
3
) and (
4
) can be computed analytically.
We also include the zero-shot predictor (
1
) learned by both the CLIP and VICReg objectives. Our goals are two-fold in this simulation: 1) to empirically observe that as
{\mathbb{E}}_{P_{Z}}\left[I(X;Y|Z)\right]\rightarrow 0
, the predictive performance of the indirect predictor
\eta_{\rho}
does indeed approach that of
\eta_{\star}
, and 2) that the predictors generated by common SSL methods used in practice have similar performance trends as
\eta_{\rho}
. As for the data-generating process, we consider
\mathscr{X}=\mathscr{Z}=\mathbb{R}^{d}
and a pair of Gaussian distributions
(P_{X,Z|Y=0},P_{X,Z|Y=1})
, where given
Y=\boldsymbol{y}
,

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}X\\
Z\end{bmatrix}\sim\mathcal{N}\left(\begin{bmatrix}\boldsymbol{\mu}_{X|\boldsymbol{y}}\\
\boldsymbol{\mu}_{Z|\boldsymbol{y}}\end{bmatrix},\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{C}_{XX|\boldsymbol{y}}&\mathbf{C}_{XZ|\boldsymbol{y}}\\
\mathbf{C}_{ZX|\boldsymbol{y}}&\mathbf{C}_{ZZ|\boldsymbol{y}}\end{bmatrix}\right)

with class-conditional mean vectors
\boldsymbol{\mu}_{X|\boldsymbol{y}},\boldsymbol{\mu}_{Z|\boldsymbol{y}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and covariance matrices
\mathbf{C}_{XX|\boldsymbol{y}},\mathbf{C}_{ZX|\boldsymbol{y}},\mathbf{C}_{ZZ|\boldsymbol{y}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
. In order to control the conditional dependence between
X
and
Y
given
Z
, we fix all parameters except for
\boldsymbol{\mu}_{Z|\boldsymbol{y}}
and
\mathbf{C}_{ZX|\boldsymbol{y}}
(for
\boldsymbol{y}=0,1
), and define them using a tunable parameter
\theta\in[0,1]
in a way such that the conditional distribution of
Y
given
X=\boldsymbol{x}
stays constant. We make it so that as
\theta\rightarrow 1
,

I(X;Y|\boldsymbol{z})\rightarrow 0
. Finally, to measure classification accuracy, we directly draw samples from
P_{Y,Z}
to simulate unbiased prompting. The full mathematical details are given in
Section

F.4
. We observe both of the intended effects; the left panel of

demonstrates that as

approaches

, the indirect, CLIP, and VICReg predictors approach the performance of the direct predictor in terms of classification performance. The right panel confirms that

indeed controls

in an approximately monotonic fashion.

Prompting without Bias with Observations from
P_{Z,Y}
.

Next, we illustrate the importance of the prompt bias term in
Theorem

1
by considering an ImageNet-Captions dataset, in which we may observe the joint sample
(X,Y,Z)
.
We compare the standard prompting technique using pre-defined templates to the unbiased strategy that draws samples directly from
P_{Y,Z}
. We design three sub-tasks by randomly selecting collections of 50 classes from each of 998 classes, reserving held-out prompting examples for which we can draw from
P_{Z|Y=\boldsymbol{y}}
for each
\boldsymbol{y}\in\mathscr{Y}
(see the additional details in
Appendix

F
). The zero-shot classification accuracy on a held-out evaluation set is plotted in
Figure

3
.
Observe that the threshold at which unbiased prompting outperforms the 18 default templates is approximately
M=10
across tasks. However, the performance of the unbiased approach only saturates at

and can have enormous benefits (almost 15% absolute increase in top-1 accuracy for the ResNet50 on Sub-Task 1) in performance. Thus, for models that have not yet been saturated from pre-training, prompting can close surprisingly wide gaps in zero-shot classification accuracy.

Class-Conditional Prompting with Language Models.

As mentioned in
Section

1
, we investigate CuPL as a means to implement class-conditional prompting (sampling from
\rho_{Z|Y=\boldsymbol{y}}
for each
\boldsymbol{y}\in\mathscr{Y}
) with LLMs. Our experimental setup and scientific goals differ from those used in
Pratt et al. 2023
: 1) we use lightweight encoders that have not saturated their performance during pre-training, as opposed to the large-scale ViT-L/14 architecture, 2) we quantify the variability of classification accuracy with respect to prompting by generating up to fifty times as many prompts per experiment, and 3) we employ LlaMA 3
(
Llama Team, Meta AI 2024
)
, which is free and accessible to other, as opposed to GPT-3
(
Brown et al. 2020
)
.
The results are shown in
Figure

4
, where we order the datasets in increasing number of classes per task: 47, 100, 102, 397, and 998. Similar phenomena as in
Figure

3
are observed, although the approximate saturation threshold varies per dataset from 20 for Flowers 102 and FGVC Aircraft up to 60 for DTD. Note that the choice of defaults heavily influences the baseline performance. Surprisingly, the Flowers 102 dataset uses a single default: “a photo of a

, a type of flower”, and is often able to outperform the class-conditional LLM approach on average. On the other hand, the DTD templates of the form “a photo of a

{texture, pattern, thing, object}” are dramatically outperformed by our LLM-generated captions
, with a nearly 20% increase in top-5 accuracy on the ResNet50 and ViT-B/32 architectures.

5
Conclusion

We showed how zero-shot prediction (ZSP) can be theoretically understood
as an indirect prediction path from another modality to the label. We presented two viewpoints on categorizing ZSP methods—the conditional mean approach and the information density approach—and framed a decomposition formula for their generalization abilities.
Our theoretical results and experiments highlighted the role of residual dependence and prompt bias in defining the fundamental limits of ZSP. Interesting venues for future work include the extension of our analysis
to classes of distribution shifts between the pre-training distribution and the downstream distribution,
and to causal generative modeling
Scetbon et al. 2024
;
Zhang et al. 2024
.

B.1
Conditional Expectation and the Hilbert Space
\mathbf{L}^{2}

Consider a common probability space
(\Omega,\mathscr{F},\mathbb{P})
and a topological space
\mathscr{X}
equipped with its Borel
\sigma
-algebra
\mathcal{B}(\mathscr{X})
. Given a random variable
X:\Omega\rightarrow\mathscr{X}
representing some observable data, we consider
P_{X}
to be the law of
X
, i.e.
P_{X}(B)=\mathbb{P}(X^{-1}(B))
for every Borel set
B\in\mathcal{B}(\mathscr{X})
. Our goal is to define
\mathbf{L}^{2}(P_{X})
, a Hilbert space containing equivalence classes of functions that are square integrable under
P_{X}
. As an intermediate step, we will also construct a Hilbert space
\mathsf{L}^{2}(\mathscr{G})
for
\sigma
-algebra
\mathscr{G}\subseteq\mathscr{F}
, which contains equivalence classes of
\mathscr{G}
-measurable functions that are square integrable under
\mathbb{P}
. Having both of these constructions will be helpful in working with conditional mean operators in a rigorous manner.

Quotient Space.

As a starting point, consider the set

\displaystyle\mathsf{L}^{2}_{+}(\mathscr{F}):=\left\{\text{$\mathscr{F}$-measurable functions }u:\Omega\rightarrow\mathbb{R}\text{ satisfying }\lVert u\rVert_{\mathsf{L}^{2}_{+}(\mathscr{F})}^{2}:=\int_{\Omega}u^{2}(\omega)\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}(\omega)<\infty\right\}.

For any
u,v\in\mathsf{L}^{2}_{+}(\mathscr{F})
, consider the equivalence relation “
\sim
” defined by

\displaystyle u\sim v\iff\exists\Omega_{1}\in\mathscr{F}\text{ such that }u(\omega)=v(\omega)\ \forall\omega\in\Omega_{1}\text{ and }\mathbb{P}(\Omega_{1})=1.

(17)

For any
u_{+}\in\mathsf{L}^{2}_{+}(\mathscr{F})
, we define
[u_{+}]_{\sim}\in\mathsf{L}^{2}(\mathscr{F})
as indexing the the equivalence class containing all functions that differ from
u_{+}
only on a set of
\mathbb{P}
-measure zero.
The global Hilbert space will be defined using the quotient of
\mathsf{L}^{2}_{+}(\mathscr{F})
under this equivalence relation.

Lemma 1
.

The quotient space
\mathsf{L}^{2}(\mathscr{F})=\mathsf{L}^{2}_{+}(\mathscr{F})/\sim
is a Hilbert space with the addition and scalar multiplication rules

\displaystyle(u,v)\mapsto au+bv:=[au_{+}+bv_{+}]_{\sim}\text{ for some }u_{+}\in u\text{ and }v_{+}\in v,

for scalars
a,b\in\mathbb{R}
and the inner product

\displaystyle(u,v)\mapsto{\left\langle u,v\right\rangle}_{\mathsf{L}^{2}(\mathscr{F})}:=\int_{\Omega}u_{+}(\omega)v_{+}(\omega)\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}(\omega)\text{ for some }u_{+}\in u\text{ and }v_{+}\in v,

where the definitions are independent of the choice of
u_{+}
and
v_{+}
.

Proof.

It is easy to verify that the addition, scalar multiplication, and inner product operations are well-defined (i.e. are invariant to the choice of
u_{+}
and
v_{+}
). Define the norm
u\mapsto\lVert u\rVert_{\mathsf{L}^{2}(\mathscr{F})}:=\sqrt{{\left\langle u,u\right\rangle}_{\mathsf{L}^{2}(\mathscr{F})}}
, and consider a Cauchy sequence
(u^{\scriptscriptstyle(n)})_{n=1}^{\infty}
in
\mathsf{L}^{2}(\mathscr{F})
. To confirm completeness, we identify a limit of this sequence as an element of
\mathsf{L}^{2}(\mathscr{F})
. First, consider an arbitrary sequence
u_{+}^{\scriptscriptstyle(1)},u_{+}^{\scriptscriptstyle(2)},\ldots
where
u_{+}^{\scriptscriptstyle(n)}\in u^{\scriptscriptstyle(n)}
for all
n\geq 1
. Then, we have by the Riesz-Fischer theorem
(
Schilling 2017
, Theorem 13.7)
, there exists a limit
u_{+}\in\mathsf{L}^{2}_{+}(\mathscr{F})
such that

\displaystyle\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\lVert u_{+}^{\scriptscriptstyle(n)}-u_{+}\rVert_{\mathsf{L}^{2}_{+}(\mathscr{F})}\rightarrow 0.

(18)

We then define
\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}u^{\scriptscriptstyle(n)}:=[u_{+}]_{\sim}
, and see that

\displaystyle\lVert u^{\scriptscriptstyle(n)}-[u_{+}]_{\sim}\rVert_{\mathsf{L}^{2}(\mathscr{F})}=\lVert u^{\scriptscriptstyle(n)}_{+}-u_{+}\rVert_{\mathsf{L}^{2}_{+}(\mathscr{F})}\rightarrow 0\text{ as }n\rightarrow\infty,

where the last step follows by (
18
) and completes the proof.
∎

Next, we construct closed subspaces of
\mathsf{L}^{2}(\mathscr{F})
which contain random variables that are measurable functions of another random variable. Notice that for
u,v\in\mathsf{L}^{2}(\mathscr{F})
, the statement
u=v
indicates equality of two partitions, namely collections of random variables that differ pairwise on sets of measure zero.
Letting
\sigma(X)
denote the
\sigma
-algebra generated by
X
, define the set

\displaystyle\mathsf{L}^{2}_{+}(\sigma(X)):=\left\{u\in\mathsf{L}^{2}_{+}(\mathscr{F})\text{ s.t. }u\text{ is $\sigma(X)$-measurable}\right\}.

(19)

Then, using the equivalence relation (
17
), we define the space

\displaystyle\mathsf{L}^{2}(\sigma(X)):=\mathsf{L}^{2}_{+}(\sigma(X))/\sim.

In the upcoming
Corollary

1
, we will confirm that
\mathsf{L}^{2}(\sigma(X))
is indeed a closed subspace of
\mathsf{L}^{2}(\mathscr{F})
for any random variable
X
. Before doing so, we consider the induced probability space
(\mathscr{X},\mathcal{B}(\mathscr{X}),P_{X})
. Then, we define the related linear space

\displaystyle\mathbf{L}^{2}_{+}(P_{X}):=\left\{\text{measurable functions }f:\mathscr{X}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}\text{ satisfying }\lVert f\rVert_{\mathbf{L}^{2}_{+}(P_{X})}^{2}:=\int_{\mathscr{X}}f^{2}(\boldsymbol{x})\mathrm{d}P_{X}(\boldsymbol{x})<\infty\right\}.

We define an analogous equivalence relation “
\sim_{X}
” defined as

\displaystyle f\sim_{X}g\iff\exists\mathscr{X}_{1}\in\mathcal{B}(\mathscr{X})\text{ such that }f(\boldsymbol{x})=g(\boldsymbol{x})\ \forall\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathscr{X}_{1}\text{ and }P_{X}(\mathscr{X}_{1})=1,

(20)

and the quotient
\mathbf{L}^{2}(P_{X}):=\mathbf{L}^{2}_{+}(P_{X})/\sim_{X}
. These sets are related to one another in the following lemma.

Corollary 1
.

The set
\mathsf{L}^{2}(\sigma(X))
is a Hilbert space with respect to the inner product used in
Lemma

1
, whereas
\mathbf{L}^{2}(P_{X})
is a Hilbert space with respect to the analogous inner product for
(\mathscr{X},\mathcal{B}(\mathscr{X}),P_{X})
. Furthermore,
\mathsf{L}^{2}(\sigma(X))
is a closed subspace of
\mathsf{L}^{2}(\mathscr{F})
, and it holds that

\displaystyle\mathsf{L}^{2}(\sigma(X))=\mathbf{L}^{2}(P_{X})\circ X:=\left\{[f_{+}(X(\cdot))]_{\sim}:f_{+}\in\mathbf{L}^{2}_{+}(P_{X})\right\}.

(21)

Proof.

That
\mathsf{L}^{2}(\sigma(X))
and
\mathbf{L}^{2}(P_{X})
are Hilbert spaces follows by identical arguments to
Lemma

1
. Additionally, we may invoke
Schilling 2017
to assert that
\mathsf{L}^{2}(\sigma(X))
is a closed subspace of
\mathsf{L}^{2}(\mathscr{F})
. Finally, to show (
21
), we will show that

\displaystyle\mathsf{L}^{2}_{+}(\sigma(X))=\mathbf{L}^{2}_{+}(P_{X})\circ X:=\left\{f_{+}(X(\cdot)):f_{+}\in\mathbf{L}^{2}_{+}(P_{X})\right\}

and take the quotient with respect to “
\sim
” on either side to complete the proof. First,
\mathbf{L}^{2}_{+}(P_{X})\circ X\subseteq\mathsf{L}^{2}_{+}(\sigma(X))
holds because
f_{+}(X)
is clearly
\sigma(X)
-measurable and

\displaystyle\lVert f_{+}(X)\rVert_{\mathsf{L}^{2}_{+}(\mathscr{F})}^{2}=\int_{\Omega}(f_{+}(X(\omega)))^{2}\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}(\omega)\overset{P_{X}=X_{\#}\mathbb{P}}{=}\int_{\mathscr{X}}f_{+}^{2}(\boldsymbol{x})\mathrm{d}P_{X}(\boldsymbol{x})=\lVert f_{+}\rVert_{\mathbf{L}^{2}_{+}(P_{X})}^{2}<\infty.

(22)

To show that
\mathsf{L}^{2}_{+}(\sigma(X))\subseteq\mathbf{L}^{2}_{+}(P_{X})\circ A
, first note that for any
\sigma(X)
-measurable random variable
U
, there exists a measurable function
g_{+}:\mathscr{X}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
such that
U=g_{+}(X)

(
Durrett 2019
, Exercise 1.3.8)
. Applying (
22
) gives
\lVert g_{+}\rVert_{\mathbf{L}^{2}_{+}(P_{X})}^{2}=+\infty\implies\lVert g_{+}(X)\rVert_{\mathsf{L}^{2}_{+}(\mathscr{F})}^{2}=+\infty
, which yields a contradiction as
\lVert g_{+}(X)\rVert_{\mathsf{L}^{2}_{+}(\mathscr{F})}^{2}=\lVert U\rVert_{\mathsf{L}^{2}_{+}(\mathscr{F})}^{2}<+\infty
. Thus,
\lVert g_{+}\rVert_{\mathbf{L}^{2}_{+}(P_{X})}^{2}<+\infty
, completing the proof.
∎

Conditional Expectation.

Using
Corollary

1
, for any collection of random variables
(X,Z,Y)
, we can now construct the Hilbert subspaces
\mathbf{L}^{2}(P_{X,Y})
,
\mathbf{L}^{2}(P_{X})
,
\mathbf{L}^{2}(P_{Z})
. We can then identify them with conditional expectations, i.e. projections onto
\mathsf{L}^{2}(\sigma(X,Y))
,
\mathsf{L}^{2}(\sigma(X))
,
\mathsf{L}^{2}(\sigma(Z))
, respectively. This is done in the definition below.

Definition 3

(Conditional Expectation)
.

For any random variable
U\in\mathsf{L}^{2}(\mathscr{F})
, we define the
conditional expectation

{\mathbb{E}}\left[U|\sigma(X)\right]
as the orthogonal projection of
U
onto
\mathsf{L}^{2}(\sigma(X))
, or

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\left[U|\sigma(X)\right]:=\argmin_{u\in\mathsf{L}^{2}(\sigma(X))}\lVert u-U\rVert_{\mathsf{L}^{2}(\mathscr{F})}^{2},

which uniquely exists due to the closedness of
\mathsf{L}^{2}(\sigma(X))
and the projection theorem
(
Schilling 2017
, Theorem 26.13)
. Owing to
Corollary

1
, we will also define the
conditional expectation function

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\left[U|X\right]:\mathscr{X}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}

as any measurable function satisfying the conditions
[{\mathbb{E}}\left[U|X\right]]_{\sim}\in\mathbf{L}^{2}(P_{X})
and
{\mathbb{E}}\left[U|\sigma(X)\right](\omega)={\mathbb{E}}\left[U|X\right](X(\omega))
for
\mathbb{P}
-almost every
\omega\in\Omega
. The specific function choice will not affect any of the forthcoming arguments.

Here, we defined the conditional expectation as an element of
\mathsf{L}^{2}(\sigma(X))
and associated it with a function in
\mathbf{L}^{2}(P_{X})
. Without the squared-integrability requirement, the conditional expectation may also be defined using the familiar tower property. We include the tower property below for completeness.

Lemma 2
.

(
Schilling 2017
, Theorem 27.12)

Consider
U\in\mathsf{L}^{2}(\mathscr{F})
and
X:\Omega\rightarrow\mathscr{X}
. Then, for every measurable set
A\in\sigma(X)
, it holds that

\displaystyle\int_{A}U(\omega)\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}(\omega)=\int_{A}{\mathbb{E}}\left[U|\sigma(X)\right](\omega)\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}(\omega)=\int_{X(A)}{\mathbb{E}}\left[U|X\right](\boldsymbol{x})\mathrm{d}P_{X}(\boldsymbol{x}).

We will make use of both the projection property and tower property throughout this manuscript. While conditional expectation may be defined for specific integrable functions, we may wish to define probability measures whose integrals can produce all conditional expectations simultaneously—this ideal is captured by
regular conditional distributions (r.c.d.’s)

Shorack 2000
, which we recall below.

Definition 4
.

Consider random variables
(U,V):\Omega\rightarrow\mathscr{U}\times\mathscr{V}
. Let
\mathcal{B}(\mathscr{U})
denote the Borel
\sigma
-algebra on
\mathscr{U}
. A map:
\mu:\mathscr{V}\times\mathcal{B}(\mathscr{U}):\rightarrow[0,1]
is called a
regular conditional distribution (r.c.d.)
if the following two properties hold:

1.

For each
A\in\mathcal{B}(\mathscr{U})
and
\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathscr{V}
, it holds that

\displaystyle\mu(\boldsymbol{v},A)={\mathbb{E}}_{P_{U,V}}\left[\mathds{1}_{A}(U)|V\right](\boldsymbol{v}),

for the conditional expectation defined in
Definition

3
.

2.

For
P_{V}
-almost every
\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathscr{V}
,
\mu(\boldsymbol{v},\cdot)
is a probability measure on
\mathcal{B}(\mathscr{U})
.

This will primarily be used for the conditional dependence arguments in
Appendix

C
.

B.2
Compact Operators

We collect several generalities about Hilbert spaces and linear operators (hereafter, simply “operators”) between them. Many computations will require expanding an element of a separable Hilbert space onto an orthonormal basis.

Definition 5

(Separability, Orthonormal Basis, Complete Orthonormal System)
.

For a Hilbert space
(\mathcal{H},{\left\langle\cdot,\cdot\right\rangle}_{\mathcal{H}})
over
\mathbb{R}
, the orthonormal system
e_{1},e_{2},\ldots\in\mathcal{H}
of vectors is called an
orthonormal basis (ONB)
or
complete orthonormal system (CONS)
of
\mathcal{H}
if any of the following properties hold, which are equivalent by
Schilling 2017
.

1.

For every
h\in\mathcal{H}
,
{\left\langle h,e_{i}\right\rangle}_{\mathcal{H}}=0
for all
i\geq 1
implies that
h\equiv 0
.

2.

\bigcup_{n=1}^{\infty}\operatorname{span}\left\{e_{1},\ldots,e_{n}\right\}
is dense in
\mathcal{H}
.

3.

For every
h\in\mathcal{H}
, it holds that
h=\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}{\left\langle h,e_{i}\right\rangle}_{\mathcal{H}}e_{i}
.

4.

For every
h\in\mathcal{H}
, it holds that
\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\left|{\left\langle h,e_{i}\right\rangle}_{\mathcal{H}}\right|^{2}=\lVert h\rVert_{\mathcal{H}}^{2}
.

5.

For every
h,h^{\prime}\in\mathcal{H}
, it holds that
\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}{\left\langle h,e_{i}\right\rangle}_{\mathcal{H}}{\left\langle h^{\prime},e_{i}\right\rangle}_{\mathcal{H}}={\left\langle h,h^{\prime}\right\rangle}_{\mathcal{H}}
.

If there exists a countable orthonormal basis, then
\mathcal{H}
is called
separable

(
Schilling 2017
, Definition 26.23 & Theorem 26.24)
.

When linear operators are compact, then we may decompose them in a way that generalizes the eigendecomposition and singular value decomposition for matrices.

Definition 6

(Compact Operator)
.

A linear operator
\mathbf{M}:\mathcal{G}\rightarrow\mathcal{H}
between Hilbert spaces
\mathcal{G}
and
\mathcal{H}
is called
compact
if for every totally bounded subset
B\subseteq\mathcal{G}
, the image
\mathbf{M}(B)
is relatively compact (i.e. the closure of
\mathbf{M}(B)
is compact) in
\mathcal{H}
.

Compact operators are bounded, and every bounded linear operator
\mathbf{M}
admits a unique
adjoint operator

\mathbf{M}^{*}
satisfying
{\left\langle h,\mathbf{M}g\right\rangle}_{\mathcal{H}}={\left\langle\mathbf{M}^{*}h,g\right\rangle}_{\mathcal{G}}
for all
g\in\mathcal{G}
and
h\in\mathcal{H}
. An operator
\mathbf{T}:\mathcal{H}\rightarrow\mathcal{H}
is called
self-adjoint
if
\mathbf{T}=\mathbf{T}^{*}
. Next, we collect two operator decompositions that will be used repeatedly. We refer the reader to
Gohberg et al. 2003
and
Gohberg et al. 2003
for further discussion on these topics. Just as their analogs for matrices, we refer to them as the
eigendecomposition
and
singular value decomposition
, respectively.

Theorem 4
.

(
Gohberg et al. 2003
, Chapter IV, Theorem 5.1)

Let
\mathbf{T}:\mathcal{H}\rightarrow\mathcal{H}
be a compact, self-adjoint operator on a separable Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
on
\mathbb{R}
. Then, there exists a countable orthonormal basis
\left\{e_{j}\right\}_{j\in J}
of
\mathcal{H}
and a sequence of non-zero real numbers
\left\{\lambda_{i}\right\}_{i\in I}
with
\lambda_{i}\rightarrow 0
,
I\subseteq J
, and for all
h\in\mathcal{H}
, we have that

\displaystyle\mathbf{T}h=\sum_{i\in I}\lambda_{i}{\left\langle h,e_{i}\right\rangle}_{\mathcal{H}}e_{i}.

(23)

Furthermore, if
{\left\langle h,\mathbf{T}h\right\rangle}_{\mathcal{H}}\geq 0
for all
h\in\mathcal{H}
(i.e.
\mathbf{T}
is
positive semidefinite
), then we may take
\lambda_{i}>0
for all
i\in I
, and order them in a non-increasing sequence.. We call
\left\{\lambda_{i}\right\}_{i\in I}
the non-zero
eigenvalues
of
\mathbf{T}
.

Theorem 5
.

(
Gohberg et al. 2003
, Chapter X, Theorem 4.2)

Let
\mathbf{M}:\mathcal{G}\rightarrow\mathcal{H}
be a compact operator between separable Hilbert spaces
\mathcal{G}
and
\mathcal{H}
on
\mathbb{R}
. Then, there exists an orthonormal basis
\left\{u_{j}\right\}_{j\in J}
of
\mathcal{H}
, an orthonormal basis
\left\{v_{k}\right\}_{k\in K}
of
\mathcal{G}
, and a sequence of positive real numbers
\left\{s_{i}\right\}_{i\in I}
with
s_{i}\rightarrow 0
such that the following statements hold.

•

All collections are at most countable, i.e.
I,J,K\subseteq\mathbb{N}
, and
I\subseteq J\cap K
.

•

For all
g\in\mathcal{G}
and
h\in\mathcal{H}
, we have that

\displaystyle\mathbf{M}g=\sum_{i\in I}s_{i}{\left\langle g,v_{i}\right\rangle}_{\mathcal{G}}u_{i}\text{ and }\mathbf{M}^{*}h=\sum_{i\in I}s_{i}{\left\langle h,u_{i}\right\rangle}_{\mathcal{H}}v_{i}.

(24)

We call
\left\{s_{i}\right\}_{i\in I}
the non-zero
singular values
of
\mathbf{M}
, which can be ordered in a non-increasing sequence.

The sets
J
and
K
are used to index the bases of
\mathcal{H}
and
\mathcal{G}
, so they may be larger in cardinality than
I
, which only indexes the non-zero eigenvalue and singular values, respectively.
We will also consider more specific classes of compact operators.

Definition 7
.

A compact operator
\mathbf{M}
with singular values
\left\{s_{i}\right\}_{i\in I}
(
Theorem

5
) is called
trace class
if
\sum_{i\in I}s_{i}<+\infty
(the singular values are summable) and
Hilbert-Schmidt
if
\sum_{i\in I}s_{i}^{2}<+\infty
(the singular values are square summable).

Using the singular value decomposition, we see that if
\mathbf{M}
is Hilbert-Schmidt, then
\mathbf{M}\mathbf{M}^{*}
and
\mathbf{M}^{*}\mathbf{M}
are self-adjoint trace class operators.
The set of all Hilbert-Schmidt operators
\mathbf{M}:\mathcal{G}\rightarrow\mathcal{H}
between Hilbert spaces
(\mathcal{G},{\left\langle\cdot,\cdot\right\rangle}_{\mathcal{G}})
and
(\mathcal{H},{\left\langle\cdot,\cdot\right\rangle}_{\mathcal{H}})
will be denoted by
\mathrm{HS}(\mathcal{G},\mathcal{H})
. This is itself a Hilbert space with the inner product

\displaystyle{\left\langle\mathbf{A},\mathbf{B}\right\rangle}_{\mathrm{HS}(\mathcal{G},\mathcal{H})}=\sum_{j\in J}{\left\langle\mathbf{A}g_{j},\mathbf{B}g_{j}\right\rangle}_{\mathcal{H}}

where
\left\{g_{j}\right\}_{j\in J}
can be taken to be
any
orthonormal basis of
\mathcal{G}
. Similarly, let
\left\{h_{k}\right\}_{k\in K}
be an arbitrary orthonormal basis of
\mathcal{H}
. Then, the Hilbert-Schmidt norm
\lVert\mathbf{A}\rVert_{\mathrm{HS}(\mathcal{G},\mathcal{H})}
will be defined as

\displaystyle\lVert\mathbf{A}\rVert_{\mathrm{HS}(\mathcal{G},\mathcal{H})}^{2}

\displaystyle={\left\langle\mathbf{A},\mathbf{A}\right\rangle}_{\mathrm{HS}(\mathcal{G},\mathcal{H})}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j\in J}{\left\langle\mathbf{A}g_{j},\mathbf{A}g_{j}\right\rangle}_{\mathcal{H}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j\in J}\sum_{k\in K}\sum_{l\in K}{\left\langle\mathbf{A}g_{j},h_{k}\right\rangle}_{\mathcal{H}}{\left\langle\mathbf{A}g_{j},h_{l}\right\rangle}_{\mathcal{H}}{\left\langle h_{k},h_{l}\right\rangle}_{\mathcal{H}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j\in J}\sum_{k\in K}{\left\langle h_{k},\mathbf{A}g_{j}\right\rangle}_{\mathcal{H}}^{2}=\sum_{j\in J}\sum_{k\in K}{\left\langle\mathbf{A}^{*}h_{k},g_{j}\right\rangle}_{\mathcal{G}}^{2}.

(25)

Using the singular value decomposition, we see that (
25
) is equal to the sum of the squared singular values referenced in
Definition

7
.
For
h\in\mathcal{H}
and
g\in\mathcal{G}
, we define the rank-one operator
h\otimes g:\mathcal{G}\rightarrow\mathcal{H}
via
(h\otimes g)g^{\prime}={\left\langle g,g^{\prime}\right\rangle}_{\mathcal{G}}h
for all
g^{\prime}\in\mathcal{G}
. For an operator
\mathbf{A}\in\mathrm{HS}(\mathcal{G},\mathcal{H})
, the following identity regarding rank-one operators will be useful for norm computations:

\displaystyle{\left\langle h,\mathbf{A}g\right\rangle}_{\mathcal{H}}={\left\langle\mathbf{A}^{*}h,g\right\rangle}_{\mathcal{G}}={\left\langle\mathbf{A},h\otimes g\right\rangle}_{\mathrm{HS}(\mathcal{G},\mathcal{H})}={\left\langle\mathbf{A}^{*},g\otimes h\right\rangle}_{\mathrm{HS}(\mathcal{H},\mathcal{G})}.

Finally, we will often compute Hilbert-Schmidt norms using assumptions on the singular decays of the operator in question.

Lemma 3
.

Let
\mathbf{M}:\mathcal{G}\rightarrow\mathcal{H}
be a Hilbert-Schmidt operator with singular values
\left\{s_{i}\right\}_{i\in I}
(
Theorem

5
). Assume that
I=\mathbb{N}
and that there exist constants
c,C,\gamma>0
such that
ci^{-\gamma}\leq s_{i}\leq Ci^{-\gamma}
for all
i\in\mathbb{N}
. Then,
\gamma>1/2
, and it holds that

\displaystyle\frac{c^{2}}{2\gamma-1}\leq\lVert\mathbf{M}\rVert_{\mathrm{HS}(\mathcal{G},\mathcal{H})}^{2}\leq\frac{2\gamma C^{2}}{2\gamma-1}.

Proof.

The requirement that
\gamma>1/2
follows from the square summability of
\left\{s_{i}\right\}_{i=1}^{\infty}
and the bound
s_{i}\geq ci^{-\gamma}
.
For the upper bound, write

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}s_{i}^{2}\leq C^{2}\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}i^{-2\gamma}=C^{2}\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\int_{i-1}^{i}\left\lceil x\right\rceil^{-2\gamma}\mathrm{d}x\leq C^{2}\left(1+\int_{1}^{\infty}x^{-2\gamma}\mathrm{d}x\right)=\frac{2\gamma C^{2}}{2\gamma-1}.

For the lower bound, write

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}s_{i}^{2}\geq c^{2}\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}i^{-2\gamma}=c^{2}\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\int_{i}^{i+1}\left\lfloor x\right\rfloor^{-2\gamma}\mathrm{d}x\geq c^{2}\int_{1}^{\infty}x^{-2\gamma}\mathrm{d}x=\frac{c^{2}}{2\gamma-1},

the result as desired.
∎

B.3
The Conditional Mean Operator

This section contains key properties of the conditional mean operator
\mathbf{M}_{Z|X}
and the information density
\mathsf{R}
from
Section

2
, based on the foundations of
Section

B.1
and
Section

B.2
. As we shall show, owing to a particular
Lancaster decomposition
(
Proposition

2
), both operators enjoy convenient spectral representations and relate to a measure of dependence—the mean-squared contingency.

Recall the probability space
(\Omega,\mathscr{F},\mathbb{P})
. Consider Borel measurable spaces
(\mathscr{X},\mathcal{B}(\mathscr{X}))
and
(\mathscr{Z},\mathcal{B}(\mathscr{Z}))
, and a random variable
(X,Z):\Omega\rightarrow\mathscr{X}\times\mathscr{Z}
. We denote by
Q_{X,Z}
the law of
(X,Z)
, i.e.
Q_{X,Z}(B)=\mathbb{P}\left((X,Z)^{-1}(B)\right)
for every
B\in\mathcal{B}(\mathscr{X}\times\mathscr{Z}
). Note that by
Schilling 2017
, the Hilbert spaces
\mathbf{L}^{2}(Q_{X})
,
\mathbf{L}^{2}(Q_{Z})
,
\mathbf{L}^{2}(Q_{X,Z})
, and
\mathbf{L}^{2}(Q_{X}\otimes Q_{Z})
are separable, a fact we will maintain in this section. We use the notation
[\cdot]_{X}
and
[\cdot]_{Z}
to index equivalence classes in
\mathbf{L}^{2}(Q_{X})
and
\mathbf{L}^{2}(Q_{Z})
, respectively. In other words, for a measurable function
h:\mathscr{X}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
, we will write
[h]_{X}\in\mathbf{L}^{2}(Q_{X})
to indicate that
\int_{\mathscr{X}}h^{2}(\boldsymbol{x})\mathrm{d}Q_{X}(\boldsymbol{x})<+\infty
. Recall the conditional mean function introduced in
Definition

3
. We define the conditional mean operator

\displaystyle\mathbf{M}_{Z|X}

\displaystyle:\mathbf{L}^{2}(Q_{Z})\rightarrow\mathbf{L}^{2}(Q_{X})

\displaystyle\mathbf{M}_{Z|X}[g]_{Z}

\displaystyle=[{\mathbb{E}}_{Q_{X,Z}}\left[g(Z)|X\right](\cdot)]_{X}.

(26)

The specific function
g\in[g]_{Z}
chosen for the output conditional expectation is not relevant, as all choices will result in the same equivalence class. We define
\mathbf{M}_{X|Z}
as the analogous operator for the conditional mean of
h(X)
given
Z
for
[h]_{X}\in\mathbf{L}^{2}(Q_{X})
.

Spectral Representation.

In the case that
\mathbf{M}_{Z|X}
is compact, the conditional mean operator admits a singular value decomposition, which will be instrumental in obtaining several important properties.

Proposition 1

(Singular Value Decomposition of the Conditional Mean Operator)
.

Let
\mathbf{M}_{Z|X}:\mathbf{L}^{2}(Q_{Z})\rightarrow\mathbf{L}^{2}(Q_{X})
be compact. There exists a countable orthonormal basis
\left\{\alpha_{j}\right\}_{j\in J}
of
\mathbf{L}^{2}(Q_{X})
, a countable orthonormal basis
\left\{\beta_{k}\right\}_{k\in K}
of
\mathbf{L}^{2}(Q_{Z})
, and a countable sequence of positive real numbers
\left\{\sigma_{i}\right\}_{i\in I}
satisfying
\sigma_{i}\searrow 0
,
I\subseteq J\cap K
, and the following statements in addition:

•

We may take
\sigma_{1}=1
,
\mathbf{1}_{\mathscr{X}}\in\alpha_{1}
, and
\mathbf{1}_{\mathscr{Z}}\in\beta_{1}
, where
\mathbf{1}_{\mathscr{X}}
is identically
1
on
\mathscr{X}
and
\mathbf{1}_{\mathscr{Z}}
is identically
1
on
\mathscr{Z}
.

•

For all
[g]_{Z}\in\mathbf{L}^{2}(Q_{Z})
and
[h]_{X}\in\mathbf{L}^{2}(Q_{X})
, we have that

\displaystyle\mathbf{M}_{Z|X}[g]_{Z}=\sum_{i\in I}\sigma_{i}{\left\langle[g]_{Z},\beta_{i}\right\rangle}_{\mathbf{L}^{2}(Q_{Z})}\alpha_{i}\text{ and }\mathbf{M}_{X|Z}[h]_{X}=\sum_{i\in I}\sigma_{i}{\left\langle[h]_{X},\alpha_{i}\right\rangle}_{\mathbf{L}^{2}(Q_{X})}\beta_{i}.

(27)

Proof.

Beyond the direct application of
Theorem

5
, we must prove the statement regarding
(\sigma_{1},\alpha_{1},\beta_{1})
and that
\mathbf{M}_{Z|X}^{*}=\mathbf{M}_{X|Z}
(which relates (
24
) to (
27
)) to achieve the desired result. For the first, we appeal to the variational representation of the first singular value
\sigma_{1}

(
Gohberg et al. 1990
, Section IV.1, Eq. (2))
, which states that

\displaystyle\sigma_{1}=\sup\left\{\lVert\mathbf{M}_{Z|X}g\rVert_{\mathbf{L}^{2}(Q_{X})}:g\in\mathbf{L}^{2}(Q_{Z}),\lVert g\rVert_{\mathbf{L}^{2}(Q_{Z})}=1\right\}.

(28)

We will show that
\beta_{1}=[\mathbf{1}_{\mathscr{Z}}]_{Z}
achieves the supremum. Then, it will hold that
\sigma_{1}=1
and
\alpha_{1}=\mathbf{M}_{Z|X}\beta_{1}=[\mathbf{1}_{\mathscr{X}}]_{X}
, because any version of the conditional expectation
{\mathbb{E}}_{Q_{X,Z}}\left[1|X\right]
is
Q_{X}
-almost surely equal to
1
. Consider any
[g]_{Z}\in\mathbf{L}^{2}(Q_{Z})
satisfying
\lVert g\rVert_{\mathbf{L}^{2}(Q_{Z})}=1
. Then, we apply Jensen’s inequality and the tower property (
Lemma

2
) to achieve

\displaystyle\lVert\mathbf{M}_{Z|X}[g]_{Z}\rVert_{\mathbf{L}^{2}(Q_{X})}^{2}

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathscr{X}}\left({\mathbb{E}}_{Q_{X,Z}}\left[g(Z)|X\right](\boldsymbol{x})\right)^{2}\mathrm{d}Q_{X}(\boldsymbol{x})

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\mathscr{X}}{\mathbb{E}}_{Q_{X,Z}}\left[g^{2}(Z)|X\right](\boldsymbol{x})\mathrm{d}Q_{X}(\boldsymbol{x})

\displaystyle={\mathbb{E}}_{Q_{Z}}\left[g^{2}(Z)\right]=\lVert g\rVert_{\mathbf{L}^{2}(Q_{Z})}^{2}=1.

Setting
g(\boldsymbol{z})=\mathbf{1}_{\mathscr{Z}}(\boldsymbol{z})\equiv 1
achieves the upper bound, hence also achieving the supremum in (
28
). Next, to prove that
\mathbf{M}_{Z|X}^{*}=\mathbf{M}_{X|Z}
, we similarly consider
[h]_{X}\in\mathbf{L}^{2}(Q_{X})
and write

\displaystyle{\left\langle[h]_{X},\mathbf{M}_{Z|X}[g]_{Z}\right\rangle}_{\mathbf{L}^{2}(Q_{X})}

\displaystyle={\mathbb{E}}_{Q_{X}}\left[h(X){\mathbb{E}}_{Q_{X,Z}}\left[g(Z)|X\right]\right]

\displaystyle={\mathbb{E}}_{Q_{X,Z}}\left[h(X)g(Z)\right]

\displaystyle={\mathbb{E}}_{Q_{Z}}\left[{\mathbb{E}}_{Q_{X,Z}}\left[h(X)|Z\right]g(Z)\right]

\displaystyle={\left\langle\mathbf{M}_{X|Z}[h]_{X},[g]_{Z}\right\rangle}_{\mathbf{L}^{2}(Q_{Z})},

which satisfies the adjoint relationship and completes the proof.
∎

Lancaster Decomposition.

In the remaining proofs of this section, we do not differentiate an equivalence class in an
\mathbf{L}^{2}
-space with its component functions, as the distinction will be clear from context. First, using the orthonormal bases defined in
Proposition

1
, we may form a convenient orthonormal basis of
\mathbf{L}^{2}(Q_{X}\otimes Q_{Z})
.

Lemma 4
.

The collection
\left\{\alpha_{j}\beta_{k}\right\}_{j\in J,k\in K}
from
Proposition

1
, where
\left\{\alpha_{j}\right\}_{j\in J}
is a countable orthonormal basis of
\mathbf{L}^{2}(Q_{X})
and
\left\{\beta_{k}\right\}_{k\in K}
is a countable orthonormal basis of
\mathbf{L}^{2}(Q_{Z})
, forms an orthonormal basis of
\mathbf{L}^{2}(Q_{X}\otimes Q_{Z})
.

Proof.

We first show that
\left\{\alpha_{j}\beta_{k}\right\}_{j\in J,k\in K}
is an orthonormal system. For any indices
i,i^{\prime}\in I
and
j,j^{\prime}\in J
, it holds via independence that

\displaystyle{\left\langle\alpha_{j}\beta_{j},\alpha_{j^{\prime}}\beta_{k^{\prime}}\right\rangle}_{\mathbf{L}^{2}(Q_{X}\otimes Q_{Z})}

\displaystyle={\left\langle\alpha_{j},\alpha_{j^{\prime}}\right\rangle}_{\mathbf{L}^{2}(Q_{X})}{\left\langle\beta_{k},\beta_{k^{\prime}}\right\rangle}_{\mathbf{L}^{2}(Q_{Z})}

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}1&\text{ if }j=j^{\prime}\text{ and }k=k^{\prime}\\
0&\text{ otherwise}\end{cases}.

To establish that this orthonormal system is now complete, we use the first equivalent condition in
Definition

5
. Consider
s\in\mathbf{L}^{2}(Q_{X}\otimes Q_{Z})
such that
{\left\langle s,\alpha_{j}\beta_{k}\right\rangle}_{\mathbf{L}^{2}(Q_{X}\otimes Q_{Z})}=0
for all
j\in J
and
k\in K
. Then, via Fubini’s theorem
(
Schilling 2017
, Corollary 14.9)
, it holds that

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathscr{Z}}\underbrace{\left(\int_{\mathscr{X}}s(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{z})\alpha_{j}(\boldsymbol{x})\mathrm{d}Q_{X}(\boldsymbol{x})\right)}_{g_{j}(\boldsymbol{z})}\beta_{k}(\boldsymbol{z})\mathrm{d}Q_{Z}(\boldsymbol{z})={\left\langle g_{j},\beta_{k}\right\rangle}_{\mathbf{L}^{2}(Q_{Z})}.

Because
\left\{\beta_{k}\right\}_{k\in K}
forms an ONB, it holds that the equivalence class of
g_{j}
is the zero element in
\mathbf{L}^{2}(Q_{Z})
, or in other words,
g_{j}(\boldsymbol{z})=0
for
Q_{Z}
-almost all
\boldsymbol{z}\in\mathscr{Z}
. Due to the fact that
J
is countable, we have that

\displaystyle\mathscr{Z}_{1}:=\bigcap_{j\in J}\left\{\boldsymbol{z}\in\mathscr{Z}:g_{j}(\boldsymbol{z})=0\right\}=\left\{\boldsymbol{z}\in\mathscr{Z}:g_{j}(\boldsymbol{z})=0\ \forall j\in J\right\}

is a probability one set under
Q_{Z}
. Because
\left\{\alpha_{j}\right\}_{j\in J}
is an ONB of
\mathbf{L}^{2}(Q_{X})
, it also holds that

\displaystyle\left\{\boldsymbol{z}\in\mathscr{Z}:g_{j}(\boldsymbol{z})=0\ \forall j\in J\right\}\subseteq\mathscr{Z}_{1}^{\prime}=\left\{\boldsymbol{z}\in\mathscr{Z}:s(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{z})=0\text{ for $Q_{X}$-almost all }\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathscr{X}\right\},

indicating that the right-hand side is also a probability one set under
Q_{Z}
.
Then, applying again the iterated integral,

\displaystyle\int_{\mathscr{X}\times\mathscr{Z}}s^{2}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{z})\mathrm{d}(Q_{X}\otimes Q_{Z})(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{z})

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathscr{Z}}\left(\int_{\mathscr{X}}s^{2}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{z})\mathrm{d}Q_{X}(\boldsymbol{x})\right)\mathrm{d}Q_{Z}(\boldsymbol{z})

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathscr{Z}_{1}^{\prime}}\left(\int_{\mathscr{X}}s^{2}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{z})\mathrm{d}Q_{X}(\boldsymbol{x})\right)\mathrm{d}Q_{Z}(\boldsymbol{z})

\displaystyle=0,

indicating the
s(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{z})=0
for
(Q_{X}\otimes Q_{Z})
-almost all
(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{z})\in\mathscr{X}\times\mathscr{Z}
. This completes the proof.
∎

This basis allows us to relate the conditional mean operator to a particular Radon-Nikodym derivative. As a result, both can be used to measure the dependence between
X
and
Z

(
Lancaster 1958
)
.

Proposition 2

(Lancaster Decomposition)
.

Assume that
Q_{X,Z}\ll Q_{X}\otimes Q_{Z}
, in which case there exists a Radon-Nikodym derivative
\mathsf{R}=\frac{\mathrm{d}Q_{X,Z}}{\mathrm{d}(Q_{X}\otimes Q_{Z})}
. Then, the following identity holds:

\displaystyle\mathsf{R}=\sum_{i\in I}\sigma_{i}\alpha_{i}\beta_{i}.

(29)

In particular, the operator
\mathbf{M}_{Z|X}
is Hilbert-Schmidt if and only if
\mathsf{R}\in\mathbf{L}^{2}(Q_{X}\otimes Q_{Z})
, with the equality

\displaystyle\lVert\mathbf{M}_{Z|X}\rVert_{\mathrm{HS}(\mathbf{L}^{2}(Q_{Z}),\mathbf{L}^{2}(Q_{X}))}^{2}=\lVert\mathsf{R}\rVert_{\mathbf{L}^{2}(Q_{X}\otimes Q_{Z})}^{2}=\sum_{i\in I}\sigma_{i}^{2}.

Proof.

Using
Lemma

4
, we represent
\mathsf{R}
on the ONB
\left\{\alpha_{j}\beta_{k}\right\}_{j\in J,k\in K}
. For any
j\in J
and
k\in K
, use the definition of the Radon-Nikodym derivative
(
Schilling 2017
, Theorem 20.2)
to write

\displaystyle{\left\langle\mathsf{R},\alpha_{j}\beta_{k}\right\rangle}_{\mathbf{L}^{2}(Q_{X}\otimes Q_{Z})}

\displaystyle={\mathbb{E}}_{Q_{X}\otimes Q_{Z}}\left[\mathsf{R}(X,Z)\alpha_{j}(X)\beta_{k}(Z)\right]

\displaystyle={\mathbb{E}}_{Q_{X,Z}}\left[\alpha_{j}(X)\beta_{k}(Z)\right]

\displaystyle={\mathbb{E}}_{Q_{X}}\left[\alpha_{j}(X){\mathbb{E}}_{Q_{X,Z}}\left[\beta_{k}(Z)|X\right]\right]

\displaystyle={\left\langle\alpha_{j},\mathbf{M}_{Z|X}\beta_{k}\right\rangle}_{\mathbf{L}^{2}(Q_{X})}

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\sigma_{i}&\text{ if }j=k=i\in I\\
0&\text{ otherwise}\end{cases},

where we recall
I
as the set indexing the non-zero singular values of
\mathbf{M}_{Z|X}
(see
Proposition

1
). This proves (
29
), the first claim. For the second claim, we use the orthonormality of
\left\{\alpha_{j}\beta_{k}\right\}_{j\in J,k\in K}
in
\mathbf{L}^{2}(Q_{X}\otimes Q_{Z})
, so that

\displaystyle\lVert\mathsf{R}\rVert_{\mathbf{L}^{2}(Q_{X}\otimes Q_{Z})}^{2}=\sum_{i\in I}\sigma_{i}^{2}=\lVert\mathbf{M}_{Z|X}\rVert_{\mathrm{HS}(\mathbf{L}^{2}(Q_{Z}),\mathbf{L}^{2}(Q_{X}))},

so that square-summability of
\left\{\sigma_{i}\right\}_{i\in I}
implies finiteness and equality of the left-hand and right-hand sides above.
∎

The formulas in
Section

2
simply equated
I=\mathbb{N}=\left\{1,2,\ldots\right\}
for ease of presentation. For completeness, the
\varepsilon_{d}
term in (
9
) represents the tail of (
29
), i.e.,

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{d}=\sum_{i=d+1}^{\infty}\sigma_{i}\alpha_{i}\beta_{i},

which vanishes as
d\rightarrow\infty
because
\sigma_{i}\rightarrow 0
and
\alpha_{i}\beta_{i}
is unit-norm in
\mathbf{L}^{2}(Q_{X}\otimes Q_{Z})
.

The Radon-Nikodym derivative
\mathsf{R}=\frac{\mathrm{d}Q_{X,Z}}{\mathrm{d}(Q_{X}\otimes Q_{Z})}
is also useful for converting conditional expectation computations into marginal expectation computations. In this sense, we may say that
\mathsf{R}
acts as a kernel for an integral operator representation of
\mathbf{M}_{Z|X}
, where the integral is taken with respect to
Q_{Z}
. The following identity is referenced by
Buja 1990
and
Dytso et al. 2023
. We provide a self-contained proof below.

Lemma 5
.

Adopt the setting of
Proposition

2
. Then, for all
g\in\mathbf{L}^{2}(Q_{Z})
and
h\in\mathbf{L}^{2}(Q_{X})
, it holds that

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}_{Q_{X,Z}}\left[g(Z)|X\right](\boldsymbol{x})

\displaystyle={\mathbb{E}}_{Q_{Z}}\left[g(Z)\mathsf{R}(\boldsymbol{x},Z)\right]\text{ for $Q_{X}$-almost all }\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathscr{X},

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}_{Q_{X,Z}}\left[h(X)|Z\right](\boldsymbol{z})

\displaystyle={\mathbb{E}}_{Q_{X}}\left[h(X)\mathsf{R}(X,\boldsymbol{z})\right]\text{ for $Q_{Z}$-almost all }\boldsymbol{z}\in\mathscr{Z}.

Proof.

We prove the first identity, whereas the second follows by a symmetric argument.
To confirm that the two functions are equal almost surely, it is sufficient to prove that for any measurable set
A\in\sigma(X)
(the
\sigma
-algebra generated by
X
) the relation

\displaystyle\int_{A}{\mathbb{E}}_{Q_{X,Z}}\left[g(Z)|X\right](\boldsymbol{x})\mathrm{d}Q_{X}(\boldsymbol{x})=\int_{A}{\mathbb{E}}_{Q_{Z}}\left[g(Z)\mathsf{R}(\boldsymbol{x},Z)\right]\mathrm{d}Q_{X}(\boldsymbol{x}).

(30)

By the definition of conditional expectation, we have that

\displaystyle\int_{A}{\mathbb{E}}_{Q_{X,Z}}\left[g(Z)|X\right](\boldsymbol{x})\mathrm{d}Q_{X}(\boldsymbol{x})

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathscr{X}}{\mathbb{E}}_{Q_{X,Z}}\left[g(Z)|X\right](\boldsymbol{x})\mathds{1}_{A}(\boldsymbol{x})\mathrm{d}Q_{X}(\boldsymbol{x})

\displaystyle={\mathbb{E}}_{Q_{X,Z}}\left[g(Z)\mathds{1}_{A}(X)\right]

\displaystyle={\mathbb{E}}_{{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}Q_{X}\otimes Q_{Z}}}\left[g(Z)\mathds{1}_{A}(X){\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}\mathsf{R}(X,Z)}\right],

where the last step follows from the Radon-Nikodym theorem
(
Schilling 2017
, Theorem 20.2)
. Next, we compute the expectation, taken under the product measure, using Fubini’s theorem
(
Schilling 2017
, Corollary 14.9)
. That is,

\displaystyle\int_{A}{\mathbb{E}}_{Q_{X,Z}}\left[g(Z)|X\right](\boldsymbol{x})\mathrm{d}Q_{X}(\boldsymbol{x})

\displaystyle={\mathbb{E}}_{Q_{X}\otimes Q_{Z}}\left[g(Z)\mathds{1}_{A}(X)\mathsf{R}(X,Z)\right]

\displaystyle=\int_{A}\left(\int_{\mathscr{Z}}g(\boldsymbol{z})\mathsf{R}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{z})\mathrm{d}Q_{Z}(\boldsymbol{z})\right)\mathrm{d}Q_{X}(\boldsymbol{x})

\displaystyle=\int_{A}{\mathbb{E}}_{Q_{Z}}\left[g(Z)\mathsf{R}(\boldsymbol{x},Z)\right]\mathrm{d}Q_{X}(\boldsymbol{x}).

This achieves (
30
) and completes the proof.
∎

While
Lemma

5
applies for a general function
g
, the function
g_{\rho}
from (
5
) is itself a conditional mean. This can be leveraged to produce yet another identity, which acts as a technical lemma for the proof of
Theorem

3
.

Lemma 6
.

Recall that
g_{\rho}(\boldsymbol{z}):={\mathbb{E}}_{\rho_{Y,Z}}\left[r(Y)|Z\right](\boldsymbol{z})
for
r\in\mathbf{L}^{2}(P_{Y})
. Assume in addition that
r\in\mathbf{L}^{2}(\rho_{Y})
. Then,

\displaystyle\eta_{\rho}(\boldsymbol{x})={\mathbb{E}}_{\rho_{Y,Z}}\left[r(Y)\mathsf{R}(\boldsymbol{x},Z)\right]+\int_{\mathscr{Z}}g_{\rho}(\boldsymbol{z})\mathsf{R}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{z})\left(\mathrm{d}Q_{Z}(\boldsymbol{z})-\mathrm{d}\rho_{Z}(\boldsymbol{z})\right).

for
Q_{X}
almost all
\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathscr{X}
.

Proof.

By
Lemma

5
, we already have that for
Q_{X}
-almost all
\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathscr{X}
, the identity

\displaystyle\eta_{\rho}(\boldsymbol{x})

\displaystyle={\mathbb{E}}_{Q_{Z}}\left[g_{\rho}(Z)\mathsf{R}(\boldsymbol{x},Z)\right]

\displaystyle={\mathbb{E}}_{\rho_{Z}}\left[g_{\rho}(Z)\mathsf{R}(\boldsymbol{x},Z)\right]+{\mathbb{E}}_{Q_{Z}}\left[g_{\rho}(Z)\mathsf{R}(\boldsymbol{x},Z)\right]-{\mathbb{E}}_{\rho_{Z}}\left[g_{\rho}(Z)\mathsf{R}(\boldsymbol{x},Z)\right]

\displaystyle={\mathbb{E}}_{\rho_{Z}}\left[g_{\rho}(Z)\mathsf{R}(\boldsymbol{x},Z)\right]+\int_{\mathscr{Z}}g_{\rho}(\boldsymbol{z})\mathsf{R}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{z})\left(\mathrm{d}Q_{Z}(\boldsymbol{z})-\mathrm{d}\rho_{Z}(\boldsymbol{z})\right).

Now, unpacking the first term on the right-hand side above, we recognize that for fixed
\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathscr{X}
, the random variable
\mathsf{R}(\boldsymbol{x},Z)
is
\sigma(Z)
-measurable, so via the properties of conditional expectation
(
Schilling 2017
, Theorem 27.11 (vii))
in
\mathbf{L}^{1}(\rho_{Z})
, we may write

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}_{\rho_{Z}}\left[g_{\rho}(Z)\mathsf{R}(\boldsymbol{x},Z)\right]

\displaystyle={\mathbb{E}}_{\rho_{Z}}\left[{\mathbb{E}}_{\rho_{Y,Z}}\left[r(Y)|Z\right]\mathsf{R}(\boldsymbol{x},Z)\right]={\mathbb{E}}_{\rho_{Z}}\left[{\mathbb{E}}_{\rho_{Y,Z}}\left[r(Y)\mathsf{R}(\boldsymbol{x},Z)|Z\right]\right].

Using the expression above and the tower property of the conditional expectation (
Lemma

2
), we write

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}_{\rho_{Z}}\left[g_{\rho}(Z)\mathsf{R}(\boldsymbol{x},Z)\right]

\displaystyle={\mathbb{E}}_{\rho_{Z}}\left[{\mathbb{E}}_{\rho_{Y,Z}}\left[r(Y)\mathsf{R}(\boldsymbol{x},Z)|Z\right]\right]={\mathbb{E}}_{\rho_{Y,Z}}\left[r(Y)\mathsf{R}(\boldsymbol{x},Z)\right],

completing the proof.
∎

Mean Square Contingency.

Both singular value decomposition from
Proposition

1
and the Radon-Nikodym derivative
\mathsf{R}
from
Proposition

2
can be used to calculate a dependence measure between
X
and
Z

(
Buja 1990
)
. This dependence measure arises in nonlinear canonical correlation analysis and alternating conditional expectations
Breiman and Friedman 1985
;
Bickel et al. 1993
.

Definition 8

(Mean Square Contingency)
.

Assume that
\mathbf{M}_{Z|X}
is Hilbert-Schmidt. Assume that
I=\mathbb{N}
(
Proposition

1
), where we may append zeros if
I
is finite. Recalling that
\sigma_{1}=1
, define the
mean square contingency

I(X;Z)
as any of the expressions

\displaystyle I(X;Z):=\lVert\mathbf{M}_{Z|X}\rVert_{\mathrm{HS}(\mathbf{L}^{2}(Q_{Z}),\mathbf{L}^{2}(Q_{X}))}^{2}-1=\sum_{i=2}^{\infty}\sigma_{i}^{2}.

Our definition of the mean square contingency is in fact the square of the quantity that was originally introduced as such by
Rényi 1959
, which is shown below.

Definition 9
.

Assume that
Q_{X,Z}\ll Q_{X}\otimes Q_{Z}
, so that
\mathsf{R}
exists, and that
\mathsf{R}\in\mathbf{L}^{2}(Q_{X}\otimes Q_{Z})
. Define the
Rényi mean squared contingency

Rényi 1959
as

\displaystyle I_{\text{R\'{e}nyi}}(X;Z):=\lVert\mathsf{R}-1\rVert_{\mathbf{L}^{2}(Q_{X}\otimes Q_{Z})}=\sqrt{\int_{\mathscr{X}\times\mathscr{Z}}\left(\mathsf{R}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{z})-1\right)^{2}\mathrm{d}(Q_{X}\otimes Q_{Z})(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{z})}.

If
Q_{X,Z}
is absolutely continuous with respect to a measure
\nu
on
\mathscr{X}\times\mathscr{Z}
, with joint density
q_{X,Z}
and marginal densities
(q_{X},q_{Z})
, we have that

\displaystyle I_{\text{R\'{e}nyi}}(X;Z)=\sqrt{\int_{\mathscr{X}\times\mathscr{Z}}\left(\mathsf{R}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{z})-1\right)^{2}q_{X}(\boldsymbol{x})q_{Z}(\boldsymbol{z})\mathrm{d}\nu(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{z})}.

Written in the form above,
I_{\text{R\'{e}nyi}}(X;Z)
may also be called the
\chi^{2}
-functional

(
Buja 1990
)
.

We apply
I(X;Z)=I_{\text{R\'{e}nyi}}(X;Z)^{{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}2}}
to achieve the sequence of identities following (
10
).
Using the singular decay computations from
Lemma

3
with
c=C=1
, we have that if
\sigma_{i}=i^{-\gamma}
, then

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\gamma-1}-1\leq I(X;Z)\leq\frac{1}{2\gamma-1}\iff\frac{1}{2}\frac{I(X;Z)+2}{I(X;Z)+1}\leq\gamma\leq\frac{1}{2}\frac{I(X;Z)+1}{I(X;Z)}.

(31)

For simplicity, we will use the upper bounds to describe the order of the quantities, that is,

\displaystyle I(X;Z)\sim\frac{1}{2\gamma-1}\iff\gamma\sim\frac{I(X;Z)+1}{2I(X;Z)}.

(32)

We employ this relation in the sample complexity calculations in
Appendix

D
.

B.4
Reproducing Kernel Hilbert Spaces

In this section, we review facts about the interplay between reproducing kernel Hilbert spaces (RKHSs), the
\mathbf{L}^{2}
-spaces defined in
Section

B.1
, the Hilbert-Schmidt spaces from
Section

B.2
, and Bochner spaces (introduced below). The goal is to provide the necessary background in order to understand the results regarding kernel-based estimation methods that are used in other parts of the manuscript. The analyses in
Appendix

D
rely on being able to decompose some target function (related to the dependence between
X
and
Z
) so that it may be estimated in multiple ways. One method involves estimating the Radon-Nikodym derivative
\mathsf{R}
introduced in
Proposition

2
. The second technique relies on vector-valued regression, with a target function denoted by
F_{\star}
. Most of the setup below is in service of the vector-valued regression estimation portion.

We maintain the Borel spaces
(\mathscr{X},\mathcal{B}(\mathscr{X}))
and
(\mathscr{Z},\mathcal{B}(\mathscr{Z}))
from
Section

B.3
, with the topological assumption that
\mathscr{X}
and
\mathscr{Z}
are second countable, locally compact, and Hausdorff. In addition,
\mathcal{H}
and
\mathcal{G}
each denote a separable reproducing kernel Hilbert space (RKHS) containing real-valued functions of
\mathscr{X}
and real-valued functions of
\mathscr{Z}
, respectively. We let
\phi:\mathscr{X}\rightarrow\mathcal{H}
and
\psi:\mathscr{Z}\rightarrow\mathcal{G}
be the canonical feature maps and let
k:\mathscr{X}\times\mathscr{X}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
and
l:\mathscr{Z}\times\mathscr{Z}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
be the reproducing kernels for
\mathcal{H}
and
\mathcal{G}
. The boundedness assumptions
\sup_{\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime}\in\mathscr{X}}k(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime})\leq k_{\max{}}<\infty
and
\sup_{\boldsymbol{z},\boldsymbol{z}^{\prime}\in\mathscr{Z}}l(\boldsymbol{z},\boldsymbol{z}^{\prime})\leq l_{\max{}}<\infty
are maintained throughout the paper.

Bochner Space.

We will adopt the equivalence class notation first introduced in
Section

B.1
, with respect to probability measures
Q_{X}
and
Q_{Z}
. That is, for any two real-valued measurable functions
f:\mathscr{X}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
and
h:\mathscr{X}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
, we say that
f\sim_{X}h
if

\displaystyle Q_{X}\left(\left\{\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathscr{X}:f(\boldsymbol{x})\neq h(\boldsymbol{x})\right\}\right)=0.

The notation
[f]_{X}
denotes an equivalence class with respect to the equivalence relation
\sim_{X}
, with representative
f
. We say that
[f]_{X}\in\mathbf{L}^{2}(Q_{X})
if

\displaystyle\int_{\mathscr{X}}h^{2}(\boldsymbol{x})\mathrm{d}Q_{X}(\boldsymbol{x})<\infty\text{ for some, or equivalently all }h\in[f]_{X}.

We define
\sim_{Z}
,
[\cdot]_{Z}
, and
\mathbf{L}^{2}(Q_{Z})
similarly.
We introduce a similar construction to
\mathbf{L}^{2}(Q_{X})
for
vector-valued
functions, i.e., those whose outputs lie in a Hilbert space. For measurable functions
F:\mathscr{X}\rightarrow\mathcal{G}
and
H:\mathscr{X}\rightarrow\mathcal{G}
, we will define the equivalence relation
F\sim_{X}H
via
Q_{X}\left(\left\{\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathscr{X}:F(\boldsymbol{x})\neq H(\boldsymbol{x})\right\}\right)=0
, and corresponding equivalence classes will be denoted
[F]_{X}
. We define the
Bochner space

\mathbf{L}^{2}(Q_{X};\mathcal{G})
via
[F]_{X}\in\mathbf{L}^{2}(Q_{X};\mathcal{G})
if

\displaystyle\int_{\mathscr{X}}\lVert H(\boldsymbol{x})\rVert_{\mathcal{G}}^{2}\mathrm{d}Q_{X}(\boldsymbol{x})<\infty\text{ for some, or equivalently all }H\in[F]_{X}.

Analogous to
\mathbf{L}^{2}(Q_{X})
, this is a set of equivalence classes of vector-valued functions. Recall from
Section

B.2
and
Section

B.3
that we use
\mathrm{HS}(\mathcal{U},\mathcal{V})
to denote the space of Hilbert-Schmidt operators mapping from a Hilbert space
\mathcal{U}
to another Hilbert space
\mathcal{V}
. The following result allows us to relate elements of the Bochner space
\mathbf{L}^{2}(Q_{X};\mathcal{G})
to elements of a space of Hilbert-Schmidt operators
\mathrm{HS}(\mathbf{L}^{2}(Q_{X}),\mathcal{G})
. For
[f]_{X}\in\mathbf{L}^{2}(Q_{X})
and
g\in\mathcal{G}
, the notation
f(\cdot)g
refers to the function mapping
\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathscr{X}
to
f(\boldsymbol{x})g\in\mathcal{G}
.

Theorem 6
.

(
Aubin 2000
, Theorem 12.6.1)

There exists a function
\Phi:\mathrm{HS}(\mathbf{L}^{2}(Q_{X}),\mathcal{G})\rightarrow\mathbf{L}^{2}(Q_{X};\mathcal{G})
that is a bijective linear transformation satisfying

\displaystyle\lVert\mathbf{C}\rVert_{\mathrm{HS}(\mathbf{L}^{2}(Q_{X}),\mathcal{G})}=\lVert\Phi(\mathbf{C})\rVert_{\mathbf{L}^{2}(Q_{X};\mathcal{G})}\text{ for all }\mathbf{C}\in\mathrm{HS}(\mathbf{L}^{2}(Q_{X}),\mathcal{G}),

and for every
[f]_{X}\in\mathbf{L}^{2}(Q_{X})
and
g\in\mathcal{G}
, associates

\displaystyle\Phi(g\otimes[f]_{X})=[f(\cdot)g]_{X}\iff g\otimes[f]_{X}=\Phi^{-1}([f(\cdot)g]_{X})

(33)

for the rank-one operator
g\otimes[f]_{X}\in\mathrm{HS}(\mathbf{L}^{2}(Q_{X}),\mathcal{G})
.

Based on the definition of the Hilbert-Schmidt norm in (
25
) (
Section

B.2
,
Theorem

6
will make computation of
\mathbf{L}^{2}(Q_{X};\mathcal{G})
-norms more convenient by relating them to
\mathrm{HS}(\mathbf{L}^{2}(Q_{X}),\mathcal{G})
-norms. The following technical lemma can be used to simplify computations regarding the inverse of this isomorphism.

Lemma 7
.

Let
(g_{j})_{j\in J}
be any countable orthonormal basis of
\mathcal{G}
, and let
\mathbf{C}=\Phi^{-1}([F]_{X})
for some
F:\mathscr{X}\rightarrow\mathcal{G}
such that
[F]_{X}\in\mathbf{L}^{2}(Q_{X};\mathcal{G})
. Define the functions
(f_{j})_{j\in J}
via
f_{j}(\boldsymbol{x}):={\left\langle g_{j},F(\boldsymbol{x})\right\rangle}_{\mathcal{G}}
. Then,
[f_{j}]_{X}\in\mathbf{L}^{2}(Q_{X})
for all
j\in J
, and we have the identity

\displaystyle\mathbf{C}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j\in J}g_{j}\otimes[f_{j}]_{X},

where the convergence is interpreted in terms of
\mathrm{HS}(\mathbf{L}^{2}(Q_{X}),\mathcal{G})
.

Proof.

First, consider the case in which we can write the equivalence class of
F
in
\mathbf{L}^{2}(Q_{X},\mathcal{G})
in the form

\displaystyle[F]_{X}=\sum_{j\in J}[f_{j}(\cdot)g_{j}]_{X},

(34)

for some sequence of functions
f_{1},f_{2},\ldots\in\mathbf{L}^{2}(Q_{X})
. Then, because
\Phi^{-1}
is a linear isometry, it is a bounded (hence continuous) operator with respect to the norm on
\mathbf{L}^{2}(Q_{X},\mathcal{G})
. This implies via continuity

\displaystyle\mathbf{C}=\Phi^{-1}\left([F]_{X}\right)

\displaystyle=\Phi^{-1}\left(\textstyle\sum_{j\in J}[f_{j}(\cdot)g_{j}]_{X}\right)=\sum_{j\in J}\Phi^{-1}\left([f_{j}(\cdot)g_{j}]_{X}\right)=\sum_{j\in J}g_{j}\otimes[f_{j}]_{X},

where the last step follows because
\Phi^{-1}
satisfies the relation (
33
). To achieve the identity (
34
), we fix any
\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathscr{X}
, we expand
F(\boldsymbol{x})\in\mathcal{G}
onto the basis
(g_{j})_{j\in J}
to write

\displaystyle F(\boldsymbol{x})=\sum_{j\in J}\underbrace{{\left\langle g_{j},F(\boldsymbol{x})\right\rangle}_{\mathcal{G}}}_{f_{j}(\boldsymbol{x})}g_{j}.

To pass this pointwise equality to (
34
), consider any sequence of
\mathcal{G}
-valued functions
(H_{j})_{j\in J}
such that
H_{j}(\boldsymbol{x})=f_{j}(\boldsymbol{x})g_{j}
for all
\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathscr{X}_{j}\subseteq\mathscr{X}
, where
Q_{X}(\mathscr{X}_{j})=1
. Similarly, consider
H_{0}:\mathscr{X}\rightarrow\mathcal{G}
such that
H_{0}(\boldsymbol{x})=F(\boldsymbol{x})
for all
\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathscr{X}_{0}
with
Q_{X}(\mathscr{X}_{0})=1
. Thus, we have that

\displaystyle\textstyle H_{0}(\boldsymbol{x})=\sum_{j\in J}H_{j}(\boldsymbol{x})\text{ for all }\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathscr{X}_{0}\cap\left(\bigcap_{j\in J}\mathscr{X}_{j}\right),

and because
J
is countable, this implies that
H_{0}(\boldsymbol{x})=\sum_{j\in J}H_{j}(\boldsymbol{x})
for
Q_{X}
-almost all
\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathscr{X}
, granting (
34
).
It remains to be shown that
[f_{j}]_{X}\in\mathbf{L}^{2}(Q_{X})
. This follows by the Bochner-square integrability of
F
, as

\displaystyle\int_{\mathscr{X}}f_{j}^{2}(\boldsymbol{x})\mathrm{d}Q_{X}(\boldsymbol{x})=\int_{\mathscr{X}}{\left\langle g_{j},F(\boldsymbol{x})\right\rangle}_{\mathcal{G}}^{2}\mathrm{d}Q_{X}(\boldsymbol{x})\leq\lVert g_{j}\rVert_{\mathcal{G}}^{2}\int_{\mathscr{X}}\lVert F(\boldsymbol{x})\rVert_{\mathcal{G}}^{2}\mathrm{d}Q_{X}(\boldsymbol{x})<\infty,

which completes the proof.
∎

In the sequel, we will define a statistical learning problem in which the target function
F_{\star}
is an element of
\mathbf{L}^{2}(Q_{X};\mathcal{G})
. For the kernel-based estimation approach, the estimation function
\widehat{F}\equiv\widehat{F}_{\lambda}
(where
\lambda
is a to-be-specified regularization parameter) will live in a particular vector-valued RKHS that will be isometrically isomorphic to
\mathrm{HS}(\mathcal{H},\mathcal{G})
. Using
Theorem

6
,
F_{\star}
will be associated to en element
\mathbf{C}_{\star}\in\mathrm{HS}(\mathbf{L}^{2}(Q_{X}),\mathcal{G})
via an isometric isomorphism. We introduce the concept of embeddings and interpolation spaces to describe exactly where
\mathbf{C}_{\star}
lies in between
\mathrm{HS}(\mathcal{H},\mathcal{G})
and
\mathrm{HS}(\mathbf{L}^{2}(Q_{X}),\mathcal{G})
(via a
source condition
).

Embedding Operator.

See
Section

B.2
for a review of the terminology surrounding compact operators.
Consider the
embedding operator

\mathbf{I}_{X}:\mathcal{H}\rightarrow\mathbf{L}^{2}(Q_{X})
, which identifies a function
h\in\mathcal{H}
with its equivalence class
[h]_{X}\in\mathbf{L}^{2}(Q_{X})
. Under the bounded kernel assumption, we have that
\mathbf{I}_{X}
is compact, and moreover Hilbert-Schmidt, with norm bounded as
\lVert\mathbf{I}_{X}\rVert_{\mathrm{HS}(\mathcal{G},\mathbf{L}^{2}(Q_{X}))}\leq\sqrt{k_{\max{}}}

(
Steinwart and Scovel 2012
, Lemma 2.3)
. We denote its adjoint by
\mathbf{S}_{X}:=\mathbf{I}_{X}^{*}
, and finally, construct the self-adjoint, trace class operator

\displaystyle\mathbf{T}_{X}:=\mathbf{I}_{X}\mathbf{S}_{X}:\mathbf{L}^{2}(Q_{X})\rightarrow\mathbf{L}^{2}(Q_{X}).

(35)

Applying the eigendecomposition
Theorem

4
, there exists an orthonormal basis of
\operatorname{cl}(\operatorname{range}(\mathbf{I}_{X}))\subseteq\mathbf{L}^{2}(Q_{X})
, denoted
([e_{X,i}]_{X})_{i\in I}
, and a sequence of positive, non-increasing eigenvalues
(\mu_{X,i})_{i\in I}
such that

\displaystyle\mathbf{T}_{X}=\sum_{i\in I}\mu_{X,i}{\left\langle[e_{X,i}]_{X},\cdot\right\rangle}_{\mathbf{L}^{2}(Q_{X})}[e_{X,i}]_{X}.

(36)

Note that we have only used the index set
I
from
Theorem

4
, as opposed to the larger set
J
for which we can define an ONB for the entirety of
\mathbf{L}^{2}(Q_{X})
, not only
\operatorname{cl}(\operatorname{range}(\mathbf{I}_{X}))
. Analogous to
\mathbf{T}_{X}
, we can also define the uncentered covariance operator

\displaystyle\mathbf{C}_{X}=\mathbf{S}_{X}\mathbf{I}_{X}:\mathcal{H}\rightarrow\mathcal{H}.

Similar to (
36
),
\mathbf{C}_{X}
enjoys an eigendecomposition

\displaystyle\mathbf{C}_{X}=\sum_{i\in I}\mu_{Z,i}{\langle\cdot,\mu_{X,i}^{1/2}e_{X,i}\rangle}_{\mathcal{G}}\mu_{X,i}^{1/2}e_{X,i}.

(37)

The equation above implicitly contains another fact, which is that the equivalence classes in (
36
) all contain representatives that are in
\mathcal{H}
. This defines the collection
\left\{e_{X,i}\right\}_{i\in I}
, which forms an ONB of
\operatorname{null}(\mathbf{I}_{X})^{\perp}\subseteq\mathcal{H}
. Combining (
36
) and (
37
), the embedding can be described using a singular value decomposition

\displaystyle\mathbf{I}_{X}=\sum_{i\in I}\mu_{X,i}^{1/2}\left([e_{X,i}]_{Z}\otimes(\mu_{X,i}^{1/2}e_{X,i})\right).

(38)

Lastly, we define
(\mathbf{I}_{Z},\mathbf{S}_{Z},\mathbf{T}_{Z},\mathbf{C}_{Z})
as the analogous operators for
\mathbf{L}^{2}(Q_{Z})
and
\mathcal{G}
.

Interpolation Spaces and the Inclusion Map.

For any
\alpha\geq 0
, we define the operator

\displaystyle\mathbf{T}_{X}^{\alpha/2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i\in I}\mu_{X,i}^{\alpha/2}{\left\langle[e_{X,i}]_{X},\cdot\right\rangle}_{\mathbf{L}^{2}(Q_{X})}[e_{X,i}]_{X},

(39)

\displaystyle\operatorname{dom}(\mathbf{T}_{X}^{\alpha/2})

\displaystyle=\left\{[f]_{X}\in\mathbf{L}^{2}(Q_{X}):\sum_{i\in I}\mu_{X,i}^{\alpha/2}{\left\langle[f]_{X},[e_{X,i}]_{X}\right\rangle}_{\mathbf{L}^{2}(Q_{X})}<\infty\right\},

which is considered to be well-defined when
\operatorname{dom}(\mathbf{T}_{X}^{\alpha/2})\neq\varnothing
. Then, we define the
\alpha
-interpolation space

[\mathcal{H}]^{\alpha}
via

\displaystyle[\mathcal{H}]^{\alpha}=\left\{\sum_{i\in I}a_{i}\mu_{X,i}^{\alpha/2}[e_{X,i}]_{X}:(a_{i})_{i\in I}\in\ell_{2}(I)\right\}\subseteq\mathbf{L}^{2}(Q_{X}),

where
(a_{i})_{i\in I}\in\ell_{2}(I)
indicates that
\sum_{i\in I}a_{i}^{2}<+\infty
. When
\alpha=0
, we recover
[\mathcal{H}]^{0}=\operatorname{cl}(\operatorname{range}(\mathbf{I}_{X}))
, whereas for
\alpha=1
,
[\mathcal{H}]^{1}
is isometrically isomorphic to
\operatorname{null}(\mathbf{I}_{X})^{\perp}\subseteq\mathcal{H}

(
Fischer and Steinwart 2020
)
. Thus, for
\alpha\in(0,1)
, we interpret
[\mathcal{H}]^{\alpha}
as an “interpolation” between the well-behaved functions in the RKHS
\mathcal{H}
and the elements of
\mathbf{L}^{2}(Q_{X})
.
Associated to each
[\mathcal{H}]^{\alpha}
is the
inclusion map

\mathbf{I}_{X}^{\alpha,\infty}
, which simply views an element
[h]_{X}\in[\mathcal{H}]^{\alpha}
as an element of
\mathbf{L}^{\infty}(Q_{X})
(this requires the boundedness of the kernel). Here,
\mathbf{L}^{\infty}(Q_{X})
denotes equivalence classes of real-valued functions on
\mathscr{X}
that have a finite essential supremum under
Q_{X}
.
We write
\mathbf{I}_{X}^{\alpha,\infty}:[\mathcal{H}]^{\alpha}\hookrightarrow\mathbf{L}^{\infty}(Q_{X})
when the inclusion map is continuous (see
10
).

We use the standard generalization of these notions onto spaces of vector-valued functions
(
Li et al. 2024
;
Meunier et al. 2024
)
: for any
\beta\geq 0
, we define the
\beta
-interpolation norm
for
\mathbf{C}\in\mathrm{HS}(\mathbf{L}^{2}(Q_{X}),\mathcal{G})
via the formula

\displaystyle\lVert\mathbf{C}\rVert_{\beta}:=\lVert\mathbf{C}\mathbf{T}_{X}^{-\beta/2}\rVert_{\mathrm{HS}(\mathbf{L}^{2}(Q_{X}),\mathcal{G})}\in[0,+\infty].

(40)

This norm, when finite, will be used to define the source condition of the target function
F_{\star}
alluded to in
Section

3
, as we may compute
\lVert\mathbf{C}_{\star}\rVert_{\beta}
for
\mathbf{C}_{\star}:=\Phi^{-1}([F_{\star}]_{X})
(see
Theorem

6
). While we phrase the condition in terms of the constant
\beta
above in order to relate it to the kernel methods and inverse problem literature below, we will use the constructions of
Section

B.3
to phrase the finiteness of (
40
) for
\mathbf{C}_{\star}
in terms of the mean square contingency (
Definition

8
) in
Appendix

D
.

Vector-Valued Spectral Regularization Learning.

We may now describe estimation techniques for an
\mathbf{L}^{2}(Q_{X};\mathcal{G})
-valued target function that fall into the category of vector-valued spectral regularization learning. We give only a brief overview in order to state the statistical convergence guarantees; see
Meunier et al. 2024
for a detailed description, including computational properties of the estimator.
As we prove in
Lemma

8
, there exists a function
F_{\star}:\mathscr{X}\rightarrow\mathcal{G}
such that
[F_{\star}]_{X}\subseteq\mathbf{L}^{2}(Q_{X};\mathcal{G})
and for every
g\in\mathcal{G}
,

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}_{Q_{X,Z}}\left[g(Z)|X\right](\boldsymbol{x})={\left\langle g,F_{\star}(\boldsymbol{x})\right\rangle}_{\mathcal{G}}.

(41)

For each
\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathscr{X}
, we also refer to
F_{\star}(\boldsymbol{x})
as the conditional mean embedding of
Z
given
X=\boldsymbol{x}
. Note that for a fixed
g\in\mathcal{G}
, we do not assume that
\boldsymbol{x}\mapsto{\left\langle g,F_{\star}(\boldsymbol{x})\right\rangle}
is an element of an RKHS
\mathcal{H}
. This avoids some of the technical challenges raised, for instance, in
Klebanov et al. 2020
;
Klebanov et al. 2021
, where this requirement places strong implicit restrictions on the chosen kernel and RKHS. Instead, the mis-specified case is handled using vector-valued interpolation spaces.

Next, using
Theorem

6
, we associate to
F_{\star}
the element
\mathbf{C}_{\star}=\Phi^{-1}(F_{\star})\in\mathrm{HS}(\mathbf{L}^{2}(Q_{X}),\mathcal{G})
.
Given independent and identically distributed pre-training data
(X_{1},Z_{1}),\ldots,(X_{N},Z_{N})
drawn from
Q_{X,Z}
, define the empirical (uncentered) auto-covariance and cross-covariance operator

\displaystyle\widehat{\mathbf{C}}_{XX}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\phi(X_{i})\otimes\phi(X_{i})\text{ and }\widehat{\mathbf{C}}_{ZX}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\psi(Z_{i})\otimes\phi(X_{i}).

Let
f_{\lambda}:\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
denote the spectral cutoff function

\displaystyle f_{\lambda}(x)=\begin{cases}x^{-1}&\text{ if }x\geq\lambda\\
0&\text{ otherwise}\end{cases}.

(42)

We can interpret
f_{\lambda}(x)
as a regularized inverse that behaves in a reasonable manner near
x=0
. A similar function corresponding to the more familiar Tikhonov regularization is
f_{\lambda}(x)=(x+\lambda)^{-1}
.
While other options for
f_{\lambda}
(i.e. filter functions) exist owing to the tools of regularization theory
(
Bauer et al. 2007
)
, the spectral cutoff function will be sufficient for our purposes, as it allows for the simplest statement of the upcoming results.
For a self-adjoint positive semidefinite operator
\mathbf{C}
, we define
f_{\lambda}(\mathbf{C})
as replacing each eigenvalue
\mu_{i}\geq 0
of
\mathbf{C}
with
f_{\lambda}(\mu_{i})
in the eigendecomposition (see
Theorem

4
). For regularization parameter
\lambda>0
, we define the estimator

\displaystyle\widehat{F}_{\lambda}(\cdot):=\widehat{\mathbf{C}}_{\lambda}\phi(\cdot)\text{ for }\widehat{\mathbf{C}}_{\lambda}:=\widehat{\mathbf{C}}_{ZX}f_{\lambda}(\widehat{\mathbf{C}}_{XX}):\mathcal{H}\rightarrow\mathcal{G}.

(43)

Now, consider the following assumptions, which include the source condition.

Assumption 10
.

(
Meunier et al. 2024
, Assumptions (SRC), (MOM), (EVD), (EMB))

1.

There exist positive constants
\beta>0
and
B>0
such that
\lVert F_{\star}\rVert_{\beta}:=\lVert\mathbf{C}_{\star}\rVert_{\beta}\leq B
.

2.

For positive constants
\sigma^{2},c>0
the Bernstein moment condition

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{Q_{X,Z}}\left[\lVert\psi(Z)-F_{\star}(X)\rVert_{\mathcal{G}}^{2}|X\right](\boldsymbol{x})\leq\frac{1}{2}q!\sigma^{2}c^{q-2}

is satisfied for
Q_{X}
-almost all
\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathscr{X}
and all
q\geq 2
.

3.

There exist constants
D>0
and
p<1
such that

\displaystyle\mu_{X,i}\leq Di^{-1/p}.

4.

For
\alpha\in[p,1]
, the inclusion map
\mathbf{I}_{X}^{\alpha,\infty}:[\mathcal{H}]^{\alpha}\hookrightarrow\mathbf{L}^{\infty}(Q_{X})
is bounded, with operator norm
\lVert I^{\alpha,\infty}_{X}\rVert_{\operatorname{op}}\leq A
.

Note that the first assumption is always satisfied for
\alpha=1
, due to boundedness of the kernel (as the
[\mathcal{H}]^{1}
norm can be associated to the RKHS norm of an element of
\mathcal{H}
). We pay particular attention to the constant
\beta
which defines the aforementioned source condition. In
Section

D.1
, we translate this condition into one regarding the dependence between
X
and
Z
, using the tools from
Section

B.3
. We refer to the case when
\beta\geq 1
as the
well-specified
case. We also employ one additional assumption to state the result.

Assumption 11

(Sub-Gaussian Tail)
.

There exists a positive constant
\tau>0
such that the following holds:

\displaystyle{\mathbb{P}}_{Q_{X}}\left[\lVert F_{\star}(X)\rVert_{\mathcal{G}}>t\right]\leq 2e^{-\frac{t^{2}}{2\nu^{2}}}.

11
is only used to replace a statement of the form “for
N\geq 1
sufficiently large” from
Meunier et al. 2024
with a quantitative condition on
N
. It is used to control the probability that
\lVert F_{\star}(X_{i})\rVert_{\mathcal{G}}>t
for any
i=1,\ldots,N
for the choices of
t
specified in the proof of
Meunier et al. 2024
.

Theorem 7
.

(
Meunier et al. 2024
, Theorem 4)

Consider a failure probability
\delta\in(0,1]
, the estimate
\widehat{F}_{\lambda}
defined in (
43
), and the target function
F_{\star}
defined in (
41
). Under
10
and
11
, there exists a constant
C>0
(independent of
N
and
\delta
) such that the following statements hold.

•

Case 1:
\beta+p>\alpha
.
If
N^{\left(\frac{1}{2}\left(1+\frac{p-\alpha}{p+\beta}\right)+\frac{\alpha-\beta}{2\alpha}\right)}\geq 2\nu^{2}\operatorname{plog}(N/\delta)
and

, then

\displaystyle\lVert[\widehat{F}_{\lambda}]_{X}-F_{\star}\rVert_{\mathbf{L}^{2}(Q_{X};\mathcal{G})}^{2}\leq C\operatorname{plog}(1/\delta)N^{-\frac{\beta}{\beta+p}}

with probability at least

.

•

Case 2:
\beta+p\leq\alpha
.
If

and

, then

\displaystyle\lVert[\widehat{F}_{\lambda}]_{X}-F_{\star}\rVert_{\mathbf{L}^{2}(Q_{X};\mathcal{G})}^{2}\leq C\operatorname{plog}(1/\delta)(N/\log^{2}(N))^{-\frac{\beta}{\alpha}}

1-\delta/4
.

This result is applied in
Section

D.1
and provides an example of the “conditional mean” approach outlined in
Section

2
and
Section

3
. Regarding the setting of
\lambda
in

7
, the argument follows the typical recipe of defining an element
F_{\lambda}\in\mathbf{L}^{2}(Q_{X};\mathcal{G})
which represents the population version of the regularized predictor. Let
\lVert\cdot\rVert_{\gamma}
denote the
\gamma
-interpolation norm for
\gamma\in[0,1]
, which is equal to
\lVert\cdot\rVert_{\mathbf{L}^{2}(Q_{X};\mathcal{G})}
when

. Then, the approximation error
\lVert[F_{\lambda}]_{X}-F_{\star}\rVert^{2}_{\gamma}
decays according to

when using the spectral cutoff regularizer, which reflects the classical analyses of

\lVert[\widehat{F}_{\lambda}]_{X}-[F_{\lambda}]_{X}\rVert_{\gamma}
decomposes into multiple terms which include irreducible noise terms of order

Radon-Nikodym Derivative Estimation.

To set the stage for this technique, we describe a function class in which
\widehat{\mathsf{R}}:\mathscr{X}\times\mathscr{Z}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
will live. Let
\mathcal{S}
denote a separable reproducing kernel Hilbert space (RKHS) of real-valued functions on
\mathscr{X}\times\mathscr{Z}
, with canonical feature map
\varphi:\mathscr{X}\times\mathscr{Z}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
and reproducing kernel
\kappa:(\mathscr{X}\times\mathscr{Z})\times(\mathscr{X}\times\mathscr{Z})\rightarrow\mathbb{R}

\sup\left\{\kappa(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{z},\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime},\boldsymbol{z}^{\prime}):(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{z}),(\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime},\boldsymbol{z}^{\prime})\in\mathscr{X}\times\mathscr{Z}\right\}\leq\kappa_{\max{}}
.

Let us describe the estimation procedure, which relies on a similar spectral regularization technique as the one described for vector-valued regression. Because the Radon-Nikodym derivative being estimated is
\frac{\mathrm{d}Q_{X,Z}}{\mathrm{d}(Q_{X}\otimes Q_{Z})}
, we consider having samples from both distributions available. In particular, we observe

and

(X^{\prime}_{1},Z^{\prime}_{1}),\ldots,(X^{\prime}_{N_{\mathrm{u}}},Z^{\prime}_{N_{\mathrm{u}}})\sim Q_{X}\otimes Q_{Z}

\displaystyle\widehat{\mathbf{C}}_{\mathrm{p}}=\frac{1}{N_{\mathrm{p}}}\sum_{i=1}^{N_{\mathrm{p}}}\varphi(X_{i},Z_{i})\otimes\varphi(X_{i},Z_{i}),\quad\widehat{\mathbf{C}}_{\mathrm{u}}=\frac{1}{N_{\mathrm{u}}}\sum_{i=1}^{N_{\mathrm{u}}}\varphi(X^{\prime}_{i},Z^{\prime}_{i})\otimes\varphi(X^{\prime}_{i},Z^{\prime}_{i}),

(44)

representing the paired and unpaired examples, respectively. Then, using the spectral cutoff function

(see (
42

\displaystyle\widehat{\mathsf{R}}\equiv\widehat{\mathsf{R}}_{\lambda}=f_{\lambda}(\widehat{\mathbf{C}}_{\mathrm{u}})\widehat{\mathbf{C}}_{\mathrm{p}}\mathbf{1},

(45)

can be viewed as a regularized inverse,

can readily be interpreted as the “ratio” of the covariance operator of the paired sample over that of the unpaired sample.

To state the assumptions for the analysis, we require an analogous operator to

and

We then define the

is Hilbert-Schmidt with norm bounded as

\displaystyle I(X;Y|Z=\boldsymbol{z})

\displaystyle=1-2{\mathbb{E}}_{P_{X|Z=\boldsymbol{z}}\otimes P_{Y|Z=\boldsymbol{z}}}\left[\mathsf{S}_{\boldsymbol{z}}(Y,X)\right]+{\mathbb{E}}_{P_{X|Z=\boldsymbol{z}}\otimes P_{Y|Z=\boldsymbol{z}}}\left[\mathsf{S}_{\boldsymbol{z}}^{2}(Y,X)\right]

\displaystyle=1-2{\mathbb{E}}_{P_{X,Y|Z=\boldsymbol{z}}}\left[1\right]+{\mathbb{E}}_{P_{X,Y|Z=\boldsymbol{z}}}\left[\mathsf{S}_{\boldsymbol{z}}(Y,X)\right]

\displaystyle={\mathbb{E}}_{P_{X,Y|Z=\boldsymbol{z}}}\left[\mathsf{S}_{\boldsymbol{z}}(Y,X)\right]-1.

Thus, by integrating against
P_{Z}
, we see that

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}_{P_{Z}}\left[I(X;Y|Z)\right]={\mathbb{E}}_{P_{X,Y,Z}}\left[\mathsf{S}_{Z}(Y,X)\right]-1,

where the expectation term is finite by
13
. The proof is complete.
∎

Lemma 8
.

It holds that 1)

\displaystyle[{\mathbb{E}}_{Q_{X,Z}}\left[g(Z)|X\right](\cdot)]_{X}=[{\left\langle g,F_{\star}(\cdot)\right\rangle}_{\mathcal{G}}]_{X}\text{ for all }g\in\mathcal{G}.

Using the notation from

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}_{Q_{Z}}\left[g_{\rho}^{2}(Z)\right]={\mathbb{E}}_{Q_{Z}}\left[{\left\langle g_{\rho},\psi(Z)\right\rangle}_{\mathcal{G}}^{2}\right]\leq\lVert g_{\rho}\rVert_{\mathcal{G}}^{2}\cdot\mathbb{E}_{Q_{Z}}\lVert\psi(Z)\rVert_{\mathcal{G}}^{2}\leq l_{\max{}}\lVert g_{\rho}\rVert_{\mathcal{G}}^{2},

(62)

granting the claim that

\displaystyle g\mapsto T_{\boldsymbol{x}}(g)=[\mathbf{M}_{Z|X}g](\boldsymbol{x})={\mathbb{E}}_{Q_{X,Z}}\left[g(Z)|X\right](\boldsymbol{x}).

By the same argument as (
62

\left|T_{\boldsymbol{x}}(g)\right|\leq\sqrt{l_{\max{}}}\lVert g\rVert_{\mathcal{G}}
, indicating that

is a bounded linear functional. By the Riesz representation theorem, there exists an element of

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}_{Q_{X,Z}}\left[g(Z)|X\right](\boldsymbol{x})={\left\langle g,{\mathbb{E}}_{Q_{X,Z}}\left[\psi(Z)|X\right](\boldsymbol{x})\right\rangle}_{\mathcal{G}}\text{ for all }g\in\mathcal{G}.

\displaystyle F_{\star}:\mathscr{X}\rightarrow\mathcal{G},\text{ defined by }\boldsymbol{x}\mapsto F_{\star}(\boldsymbol{x})={\mathbb{E}}_{Q_{X,Z}}\left[\psi(Z)|X\right](\boldsymbol{x})\in\mathcal{G}.

D.1.1
Decomposing the Global Error

Returning to the original quantity we wish to control from (

Lemma 9

(Error Decomposition)
.

For any choice of

it holds that

\displaystyle\lVert\hat{\eta}_{\rho}-\eta_{\rho}\rVert_{\mathbf{L}^{2}(Q_{X})}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq 3\lVert g_{\rho}\rVert_{\mathcal{G}}^{2}\cdot\lVert\widehat{F}-F_{\star}\rVert^{2}_{\mathbf{L}^{2}(Q_{X};\mathcal{G})}+3\lVert F_{\star}\rVert^{2}_{\mathbf{L}^{2}(Q_{X};\mathcal{G})}\cdot\lVert\hat{g}_{\rho}-g_{\rho}\rVert_{\mathcal{G}}^{2}

\displaystyle\quad+3\lVert\hat{g}_{\rho}-g_{\rho}\rVert_{\mathcal{G}}^{2}\cdot\lVert\widehat{F}-F_{\star}\rVert^{2}_{\mathbf{L}^{2}(Q_{X};\mathcal{G})},

Proof.

Using the reproducing property of the RKHS
\mathcal{G}
and Young’s inequality we have that

\displaystyle\lVert\hat{\eta}_{\rho}-\eta_{\rho}\rVert_{\mathbf{L}^{2}(Q_{X})}^{2}

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathscr{X}}(\hat{\eta}_{\rho}(\boldsymbol{x})-\eta_{\rho}(\boldsymbol{x}))^{2}\mathrm{d}Q_{X}(\boldsymbol{x})

\displaystyle\leq 3\int_{\mathscr{X}}{\langle g_{\rho},\widehat{F}(\boldsymbol{x})-F_{\star}(\boldsymbol{x})\rangle}_{\mathcal{G}}^{2}\mathrm{d}Q_{X}(\boldsymbol{x})+3\int_{\mathscr{X}}{\langle\widehat{F}(\boldsymbol{x}),\hat{g}_{\rho}-g_{\rho}\rangle}_{\mathcal{G}}^{2}\mathrm{d}Q_{X}(\boldsymbol{x})

\displaystyle\quad+3\int_{\mathscr{X}}{\langle\widehat{F}(\boldsymbol{x})-F_{\star}(\boldsymbol{x}),\hat{g}_{\rho}-g_{\rho}\rangle}_{\mathcal{G}}^{2}\mathrm{d}Q_{X}(\boldsymbol{x}).

Then, applying the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality in

\displaystyle\lVert\hat{\eta}_{\rho}-\eta_{\rho}\rVert_{\mathbf{L}^{2}(Q_{X})}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq 3\lVert g_{\rho}\rVert_{\mathcal{G}}^{2}\cdot\int_{\mathscr{X}}\lVert\widehat{F}(\boldsymbol{x})-F_{\star}(\boldsymbol{x})\rVert_{\mathcal{G}}^{2}\mathrm{d}Q_{X}(\boldsymbol{x})+3\left(\int_{\mathscr{X}}\lVert F_{\star}(\boldsymbol{x})\rVert_{\mathcal{G}}^{2}\mathrm{d}Q_{X}(\boldsymbol{x})\right)\cdot\lVert\hat{g}_{\rho}-g_{\rho}\rVert_{\mathcal{G}}^{2}

\displaystyle\quad+3\lVert\hat{g}_{\rho}-g_{\rho}\rVert_{\mathcal{G}}^{2}\int_{\mathscr{X}}\lVert\widehat{F}(\boldsymbol{x})-F_{\star}(\boldsymbol{x})\rVert_{\mathcal{G}}^{2}\mathrm{d}Q_{X}(\boldsymbol{x})

\displaystyle\quad+3\lVert\hat{g}_{\rho}-g_{\rho}\rVert_{\mathcal{G}}^{2}\cdot\lVert\widehat{F}-F_{\star}\rVert^{2}_{\mathbf{L}^{2}(Q_{X};\mathcal{G})},

the result as desired.
∎

D.1.3
Controlling the Prompting Term

From the decomposition given in
Lemma

9
, the estimate
\hat{g}_{\rho}
will be designed as to control the RKHS-norm error
\lVert\hat{g}_{\rho}-g_{\rho}\rVert_{\mathcal{G}}^{2}
. We phrase the assumption generically, but in a way that is reflective of the convergence rates seen in real-valued nonparametric regression. Recall the probability space
(\Omega,\mathscr{F},\mathbb{P})
introduced in
Section

B.1
.

Assumption 14
.

For constants
\delta\in(0,1]
,
M\geq 1
, and
\omega_{\rho}\in(1/2,1]
, there is an event
\mathcal{E}(\delta,M,\omega_{\rho})
that is independent of the pre-training data
(X_{1},Z_{1}),\ldots,(X_{N},Z_{N})
, such that on
\mathcal{E}(\delta,M,\omega_{\rho})
,

\displaystyle\lVert\hat{g}_{\rho}-g_{\rho}\rVert_{\mathcal{G}}^{2}\leq CB_{r}^{2}\operatorname{plog}(1/\delta)M^{-\frac{2\omega_{\rho}-1}{2\omega_{\rho}+1}}.

(69)

for a constant
C
independent of
\delta
and
M
.
On
(\Omega,\mathscr{F},\mathbb{P})
, the event
\mathcal{E}(\delta,M,\omega_{\rho})
occurs with probability at least
1-\delta/2
.

The notation
\omega_{\rho}
is chosen for the constant that determines the convergence rate, because it can be interpreted itself as a source condition constant for a real-valued nonparametric regression framework. Indeed, consider the case in which
\hat{g}_{\rho}
is computed using kernel ridge regression with parameter
\lambda
. Via the proof of their Theorem 2,
Smale and Zhou 2007
show that with probability at least
1-\delta/2
,

\displaystyle\lVert\hat{g}_{\rho}-g_{\rho}\rVert_{\mathcal{G}}\leq C(\rho_{Y,Z})\log(4/\delta)\Big[\underbrace{B_{r}M^{-1/2}\lambda^{-1}}_{\text{estimation}}+\underbrace{\lambda^{\omega_{\rho}-1/2}}_{\text{approximation}}\Big],

(70)

where
C(\rho_{Y,Z})
is a constant that depends on the prompting measure
\rho_{Y,Z}
and the choice of kernel. Optimizing the bound yields
\lambda\equiv\lambda_{M}\sim M^{-1/\left(2\omega_{\rho}+1\right)}
, which ultimately leads to the convergence rate (notice the square) in (
69
). We comment that the choice to control the error in
\hat{g}_{\rho}
in
\mathcal{G}
-norm comes from the vector-valued regression framework, in which the output space of the target function always lies in
\mathbf{L}^{2}(Q_{X};\mathcal{G})
. In isolation, the mean squared error of
\hat{g}_{\rho}
can be controlled both in
\mathbf{L}^{2}(\rho_{Z})
-norm as well as interpolation norms in between
\mathbf{L}^{2}(\rho_{Z})
and
\mathcal{G}
(see
Fischer and Steinwart 2020
, for instance). Indeed, when applying the decomposition (
70
) in
\mathbf{L}^{2}(\rho_{Z})
-norm,
Smale and Zhou 2007
show that the approximation error decays as
\lambda^{\omega_{\rho}}
(instead of
\lambda^{\omega_{\rho}-1/2}
). In this case, the optimum is achieved at
\lambda_{M}\sim M^{-1/\left(2\omega_{\rho}+2\right)}
, so that
\lVert\hat{g}_{\rho}-g_{\rho}\rVert_{\mathbf{L}^{2}(\rho_{Z})}^{2}
enjoys a convergence rate of
M^{-\omega_{\rho}/(\omega_{\rho}+1)}
.

D.2
Information Density Approach

This approach is based on the RHS of (
59
) and yields the result of
Theorem

3
.
Here, we assume that during the pre-training phase, the user produces an estimated function
\widehat{\mathsf{R}}
, which is an element of a reproducing kernel Hilbert space (RKHS). Unlike in
Section

D.1
, where we approximated
g_{\rho}
using a function
\hat{g}_{\rho}
(which aligns with the conditional mean viewpoint), the information density viewpoint in this section warrants estimating the mean of a function under
\rho_{Y,Z}
directly, using samples
(Y_{1},Z_{1}),\ldots,(Y_{M},Z_{M})\overset{\text{\tiny i.i.d}}{\sim}\rho_{Z}
. It is also important to point out a slight difference in the sampling model for the pre-training data. In order to define the estimate (
45
) for our method of choice (and similar Radon-Nikodym derivative estimation techniques), it is typically assumed that we observe data from both distributions in the ratio. In the case of
Q_{X,Z}
and
Q_{X}\otimes Q_{Z}
, this corresponds to observing
N_{\mathrm{p}}
paired examples and
N_{\mathrm{u}}
unpaired examples such that
N=N_{\mathrm{p}}+N_{\mathrm{u}}
. For simplicity, we assume that
N_{\mathrm{p}}=N_{\mathrm{u}}=N/2
, but remark that the regime in which
N_{\mathrm{u}}\gg N_{\mathrm{p}}
is an interesting and practically relevant model for future investigations.

Setup.

Let
\mathcal{S}
denote a separable reproducing kernel Hilbert space (RKHS) of real-valued functions on
\mathscr{X}\times\mathscr{Z}
, with canonical feature map
\varphi:\mathscr{X}\times\mathscr{Z}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
and reproducing kernel
\kappa:(\mathscr{X}\times\mathscr{Z})\times(\mathscr{X}\times\mathscr{Z})\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
.
We will express the error in terms of the RKHS norm difference
\lVert\widehat{\mathsf{R}}-\mathsf{R}\rVert_{\mathcal{S}}^{2}
, among other terms that capture a notion of “distribution mismatch” between the prompting marginal
\rho_{Z}
and the pre-training marginal
Q_{Z}
. This may also be interpreted as another instance of prompt bias. This error occurs because at prompting time, the user does not necessarily have any data drawn from
Q_{Z}
. As before, we maintain
\sup\left\{\kappa(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{z},\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime},\boldsymbol{z}^{\prime}):(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{z}),(\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime},\boldsymbol{z}^{\prime})\in\mathscr{X}\times\mathscr{Z}\right\}\leq\kappa_{\max{}}
.

Recall that the true
\mathsf{R}
is a kernel for the conditional mean operator when integrated under
Q_{Z}
(see
Lemma

5
), but can also be related via
Lemma

6
to the marginal distribution
\rho_{Z}
:

\displaystyle\eta_{\rho}(\boldsymbol{x})={\mathbb{E}}_{\rho_{Y,Z}}\left[r(Y)\mathsf{R}(\boldsymbol{x},Z)\right]+\int_{\mathscr{Z}}g_{\rho}(\boldsymbol{z})\mathsf{R}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{z})\left(\mathrm{d}Q_{Z}(\boldsymbol{z})-\mathrm{d}\rho_{Z}(\boldsymbol{z})\right).

This motivates the approximation
\hat{\rho}_{Y,Z}
expressed directly in terms of the prompt distribution, and the estimator

\displaystyle\hat{\eta}_{\rho}(\boldsymbol{x})=\mathbb{E}_{\hat{\rho}_{Y,Z}}[r(Y)\widehat{\mathsf{R}}(\boldsymbol{x},Z)].

(75)

Below, we consider the empirical measure

\displaystyle\hat{\rho}_{Y,Z}=\frac{1}{M}\sum_{j=1}^{M}\delta_{(Y_{j},Z_{j})}

(76)

so that for fixed
\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathscr{X}
, (
75
) reduces to a sample mean.

D.2.1
Decomposing the Global Error

The estimation error decomposition below will take the two differences into account: between the marginal distributions
Q_{Z}
and
\rho_{Z}
and between the joint distribution
\hat{\rho}_{Y,Z}
and
\rho_{Y,Z}
. For the latter, we will define random variables that take values in a Hilbert space (specifically,
\mathbf{L}^{2}(Q_{X})
). This will allow for controlling deviations between
\hat{\rho}_{Y,Z}
and
\rho_{Y,Z}
directly for the test functions being integrated. Define the independent and identically random variables

by

\displaystyle W_{j}:=r(Y_{j})\mathsf{R}(\cdot,Z_{j}),

and the element of

(interpreted as the expectation)
{\mathbb{E}}_{\rho_{Y,Z}}\left[W_{1}\right]:\boldsymbol{x}\mapsto{\mathbb{E}}_{\rho_{Y,Z}}\left[r(Y_{1})\mathsf{R}(\boldsymbol{x},Z_{1})\right]
.

Lemma 12

(Error Decomposition)
.

Assume the following conditions.

•

\rho_{Z}\ll Q_{Z}
with

-square integrable Radon-Nikodym derivative (i.e.

•

\mathsf{R}

\mathbf{L}^{2}(Q_{X}\otimes Q_{Z})

\displaystyle\quad+3\lVert\tfrac{1}{M}\textstyle\sum_{j=1}^{M}W_{j}-{\mathbb{E}}_{\rho_{Y,Z}}\left[W_{1}\right]\rVert_{\mathbf{L}^{2}(Q_{X})}^{2}.

Proof.

Using

6

\displaystyle\hat{\eta}_{\rho}(\boldsymbol{x})-\eta_{\rho}(\boldsymbol{x})

\displaystyle\quad+\int_{\mathscr{Z}}g_{\rho}(\boldsymbol{z})\mathsf{R}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{z})\left(\mathrm{d}Q_{Z}(\boldsymbol{z})-\mathrm{d}\rho_{Z}(\boldsymbol{z})\right)

Then, we have that

\displaystyle\lVert\hat{\eta}_{\rho}-\eta_{\rho}\rVert_{\mathbf{L}^{2}(Q_{X})}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq 3{\mathbb{E}}_{Q_{X}}\left[\left(\mathbb{E}_{\hat{\rho}_{Y,Z}}[r(Y){\langle\varphi(X,Z),\widehat{\mathsf{R}}-\mathsf{R}\rangle}]^{2}\right)\right]

(78)

\displaystyle\quad+3\int_{\mathscr{X}}\left(\int_{\mathscr{Y}\times\mathscr{Z}}r(\boldsymbol{y})\mathsf{R}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{z})\left(\mathrm{d}\hat{\rho}_{Y,Z}(\boldsymbol{y},\boldsymbol{z})-\mathrm{d}\rho_{Y,Z}(\boldsymbol{y},\boldsymbol{z})\right)\right)^{2}\mathrm{d}Q_{X}(\boldsymbol{x})

(79)

\displaystyle\quad+3\int_{\mathscr{X}}\left(\int_{\mathscr{Z}}g_{\rho}(\boldsymbol{z})\mathsf{R}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{z})\left(\mathrm{d}Q_{Z}(\boldsymbol{z})-\mathrm{d}\rho_{Z}(\boldsymbol{z})\right)\right)^{2}\mathrm{d}Q_{X}(\boldsymbol{x}).

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}_{Q_{X}}\left[\left(\mathbb{E}_{\hat{\rho}_{Y,Z}}[r(Y){\langle\varphi(X,Z),\widehat{\mathsf{R}}-\mathsf{R}\rangle}]^{2}\right)\right]\leq B_{r}^{2}\kappa_{\max{}}^{2}\lVert\widehat{\mathsf{R}}-\mathsf{R}\rVert_{\mathcal{S}}^{2}.

For (

For (

and

\displaystyle\left(\int_{\mathscr{Z}}g_{\rho}(\boldsymbol{z})\mathsf{R}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{z})\left(\mathrm{d}Q_{Z}(\boldsymbol{z})-\mathrm{d}\rho_{Z}(\boldsymbol{z})\right)\right)^{2}

D.3
Distribution Shift

The results of the previous two subsections provided bounds in high probability on the term

Lemma 14

(Distribution Shift)
.

Assume that

and

•

\displaystyle\lVert\eta\rVert_{\mathbf{L}^{2}(P_{X})}^{2}\leq\lVert\eta\rVert_{\mathbf{L}^{2}(Q_{X})}^{2}+\lVert\eta\rVert_{\infty}^{2}\operatorname{TV}(P_{X},Q_{X}).

(85)

•

\displaystyle\lVert\eta\rVert_{\mathbf{L}^{2}(P_{X})}^{2}\leq B_{P,Q}\lVert\eta\rVert_{\mathbf{L}^{2}(Q_{X})}^{2}.

(86)

Proof.

In the case of (

\displaystyle\lVert\eta\rVert_{\mathbf{L}^{2}(P_{X})}^{2}={\mathbb{E}}_{P_{X}}\left[\eta^{2}(X)\right]

\displaystyle={\mathbb{E}}_{Q_{X}}\left[\eta^{2}(X)\right]+\int_{\mathscr{X}}\eta^{2}(\boldsymbol{x})\left(p_{X}(\boldsymbol{x})-q_{X}(\boldsymbol{x})\right)\mathrm{d}\nu_{X}(\boldsymbol{x})

which proves the first claim. For (

\displaystyle\lVert\eta\rVert_{\mathbf{L}^{2}(P_{X})}^{2}={\mathbb{E}}_{P_{X}}\left[\eta^{2}(X)\right]={\mathbb{E}}_{Q_{X}}\left[\eta^{2}(X)\tfrac{\mathrm{d}Q_{X}}{\mathrm{d}P_{X}}(X)\right]\leq B_{P,Q}\lVert\eta\rVert_{\mathbf{L}^{2}(Q_{X})}^{2},

∎

\displaystyle\mathscr{R}(\operatorname{dec}\circ s)-\mathscr{R}(h_{\star})

\displaystyle\lesssim\sqrt{C{\mathbb{E}}_{P_{Z}}\left[I(X;Y|Z)\right]+\textstyle\sum_{j=1}^{C}\lVert g_{\rho}^{\scriptscriptstyle(c)}-g_{P_{Y,Z}}^{\scriptscriptstyle(c)}\rVert_{\mathbf{L}^{2}(P_{Z})}^{2}+C\operatorname{TV}(P_{X},Q_{X})}

\displaystyle\quad+\begin{cases}\sqrt{C}\operatorname{plog}\left(C/\delta\right)\left(N^{-\frac{q(t)}{2(q(t)+1)}}+M^{-\frac{2\omega_{\rho}-1}{4\omega_{\rho}+2}}\right)&\text{ (conditional mean)}\\
\sqrt{C}\operatorname{plog}\left(C/\delta\right)\left(N^{-\frac{\beta}{2(\beta+1)}}+M^{-1/2}\right)+\sqrt{D_{\chi^{2}}(\rho_{Z}\|Q_{Z})}&\text{ (information density)}\end{cases},

which holds with probability at least
1-\delta
. While generalization bounds for classification and structured prediction can have sharper dependences on the number of examples and number of classes for supervised learning (e.g., via the techniques of
Cabannnes et al. 2021
and references therein), the conversion from regression to classification is a remarkably general way to account for the residual dependence, prompt bias, and multiple stages of estimation that mark our problem.

Prompting Strategies

We have stated upper bounds on the statistical error in this section that depend on the size of the pre-training set
N
and the number of prompts

. To state them more precisely, however, we must also specify the sampling schemes that lead to these examples/prompts. Sampling of the pre-training data falls into fixed and well-understood categories, boiling down to whether only paired examples or a combination of paired and unpaired examples are available. We describe these as part of the background (
Section

B.4
), alongside the method to which they apply. However, the interpretation of prompting (the empirical procedure used in (
1
)) formally as a sampling scheme from a probability measure
\rho_{Y,Z}
is itself a contribution of this paper. In the results of
Section

D.1
and
Section

D.2
, we considered simple random sampling
(Y_{1},Z_{1}),\ldots,(Y_{M},Z_{M})\sim\rho_{Y,Z}
i.i.d. to provide examples of scenarios in which

and
18
can be satisfied. However, multiple practical and idealized strategies exist for prompting (such as the ones explored in
Section

4
). Below, we represent them in our framework below, as ways to define
\rho_{Y,Z}
and approximate it with
\hat{\rho}_{Y,Z}
.

•

Template-Based:
This technique reflects the earlier iterations of representing labels in natural language. Examples include “photo of a

”, “realistic photo of a

”, “drawing of a

”, etc. Notice that the prompt templates have no relationship with the class label.

•

Class Conditional:
This technique reflects the modern LLM-based techniques, such as CuPL
(
Pratt et al. 2023
)
. We parameterize the joint distribution using the conditional distributions
\rho_{Y,Z}=\sum_{\boldsymbol{y}\in\mathscr{Y}}\rho_{Z|Y=\boldsymbol{y}}\cdot\rho_{Y}(\boldsymbol{y})
for each class
\boldsymbol{y}\in\mathscr{Y}
. Sampling from each
\rho_{Z|Y=\boldsymbol{y}}
occurs by meta-prompting the LLM (such as the one we use in
Appendix

F
), which generates samples
\boldsymbol{z}^{\boldsymbol{y}}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{z}^{\boldsymbol{y}}_{1}
and empirical measures
\hat{\rho}_{Z|Y=\boldsymbol{y}}=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{k=1}^{m}\delta_{\boldsymbol{z}_{k}^{\boldsymbol{y}}}
. Our final approximation is
\hat{\rho}_{Y,Z}=\sum_{\boldsymbol{y}\in\mathscr{Y}}\hat{\rho}_{Z|Y=\boldsymbol{y}}\cdot\rho_{Y}(\boldsymbol{y})
, with
M=m\left|\mathscr{Y}\right|
.

•

Unbiased:
This techniques reflects the setting of
Figure

3
, where the user may drawn samples from a joint distribution
P_{X,Y,Z}
, where the marginal
P_{X,Y}
is in fact the data on which the zero-shot classifier will be evaluated. Then, the prompt distribution can be constructed, as we do, by drawing samples
(\boldsymbol{y}_{1},\boldsymbol{z}_{1}),\ldots,(\boldsymbol{y}_{M},\boldsymbol{z}_{M})
directly from
P_{Y,Z}

\hat{\rho}_{Y,Z}=\frac{1}{M}\sum_{j=1}^{M}\delta_{(\boldsymbol{y}_{j},\boldsymbol{z}_{j}))}

1

can be matched by the prompt distribution, the distribution mismatch term from

3
will be zero if and only if

(or the prompt captions match the pre-training captions in distribution). In this sense,

.

Covariance Operators.

To relate our theory (which centers around the mean square contingency measure of dependence) to SSL objectives, we first draw the relationship to covariance operators of
Q_{X,Z}
on particular function spaces. Let
\mathcal{H}
be an RKHS of real-valued functions
\mathscr{X}
and
\mathcal{G}
be an RKHS of real-valued functions on
\mathscr{Z}
. Then, define the cross-covariance operator
\mathbf{C}_{XZ}:\mathcal{G}\rightarrow\mathcal{H}
by

\displaystyle{\left\langle h,\mathbf{C}_{XZ}g\right\rangle}_{\mathcal{H}}=\operatorname{\mathbb{C}ov}_{Q_{X,Z}}(h(X),g(Z)),

and the analogously defined auto-covariance operators
\mathbf{C}_{XX}:\mathcal{H}\rightarrow\mathcal{H}
and
\mathbf{C}_{ZZ}:\mathcal{G}\rightarrow\mathcal{G}
.
When
\mathbf{C}_{XX}
and
\mathbf{C}_{ZZ}
are compact, we define the powers
\mathbf{C}_{XX}^{1/2}
and
\mathbf{C}_{ZZ}^{1/2}
in the sense of (
39
). It then holds by
Baker 1973
that there exists a unique bounded linear operator
\mathbf{V}_{XZ}:\mathcal{G}\rightarrow\mathcal{H}
, so that

\displaystyle\mathbf{C}_{XZ}=\mathbf{C}_{XX}^{1/2}\mathbf{V}_{XZ}\mathbf{C}_{ZZ}^{1/2}.

(93)

The operator
\mathbf{V}_{XZ}
is called the
normalized cross-covariance operator
, or NOCCO for short
(
Fukumizu et al. 2005
)
. As an abuse of notation, the NOCCO (
93
) is sometimes communicated as
\mathbf{V}_{XZ}=\mathbf{C}_{XX}^{-1/2}\mathbf{C}_{XZ}\mathbf{C}_{ZZ}^{-1/2}
, though it is uniquely defined without necessarily constructing the square-root inverses. To rigorously use the formula
\mathbf{C}_{XX}^{-1/2}\mathbf{C}_{XZ}\mathbf{C}_{ZZ}^{-1/2}
with a well-defined adjoint, we must make assumptions on the closure of the range of
\mathbf{C}_{XZ}
and
\mathbf{C}_{ZX}
being contained within the closure of the range of
\mathbf{C}_{XX}
and
\mathbf{C}_{ZZ}
, respectively. The Hilbert-Schmidt norm of the population NOCCO, when finite, is equal to the mean square contingency

\displaystyle\lVert\mathbf{V}_{X,Z}\rVert_{\mathrm{HS}(\mathcal{G},\mathcal{H})}^{2}=I(X;Z),

(94)

as shown in
Fukumizu et al. 2007b
. The relation (
94
) requires a few additional technical conditions, such as
(\mathcal{H}\otimes\mathcal{G})+\mathbb{R}
being dense in
\mathbf{L}^{2}(Q_{X}\otimes Q_{Z})
and
Q_{X,Z}
having joint and marginal densities
6
6

6

Recall that our definition of
I(X;Z)
does not include the square root that is usually used in the definition of mean square contingency.
.

Variational Characterization of the Hilbert-Schmidt Norm.

This operator is an essential component of the kernel canonical correlations analysis (CCA) problem, which (with (
94
)) will be the common bridge that ties together SSL and the mean square contingency. From the nonparametric CCA perspective, the singular values
(\sigma_{i})_{i=1}^{\infty}
refer precisely to the canonical correlations and the singular functions
((\alpha_{i},\beta_{i}))_{i=1}^{\infty}
refer to the canonical variates
(
Lancaster 1958
;
Buja 1990
;
Michaeli et al. 2016
)
. Returning to (
94
), this operator is estimated with a regularization scheme, i.e.

\displaystyle\widehat{\mathbf{V}}_{X,Z}:=(\widehat{\mathbf{C}}_{XX}+\lambda\mathbf{I})^{-1/2}\widehat{\mathbf{C}}_{XZ}(\widehat{\mathbf{C}}_{ZZ}+\lambda\mathbf{I})^{-1/2},

where
\widehat{\mathbf{C}}_{XX}
,
\widehat{\mathbf{C}}_{XZ}
, and
\widehat{\mathbf{C}}_{ZZ}
are the standard empirical covariance estimates (see
Section

B.4
) and
\lambda>0
is a regularization parameter. Then, one solves the empirical CCA problem

\displaystyle\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}h_{1},\ldots,h_{d}\in\mathcal{H}\text{ o.n.b}\\
g_{1},\ldots,g_{d}\in\mathcal{G}\text{ o.n.b}\end{subarray}}\sum_{i=1}^{d}{\langle h_{i},\widehat{\mathbf{V}}_{X,Z}g_{i}\rangle}_{\mathcal{H}}.

(95)

where o.n.b denotes an orthonormal basis.
Setting aside matters of estimation, we consider how the norm quantity
\lVert\mathbf{V}_{X,Z}\rVert_{\mathrm{HS}(\mathcal{G},\mathcal{H})}^{2}
relates to the actual encoders returned by the CCA problem (
95
) (assuming that
\widehat{\mathbf{V}}_{X,Z}\approx\mathbf{V}_{X,Z}
).
Let
(s_{i})_{i=1}^{\infty}
be ordered singular values of the Hilbert-Schmidt operator
\mathbf{V}_{X,Z}
. In this case, denoting
h_{1},\ldots,h_{d}\in\mathcal{H}
and
g_{1},\ldots,g_{d}\in\mathcal{G}
the orthonormal bases of
\mathcal{H}
and
\mathcal{G}
resp. maximizing the criterion ((
95
)), we have

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{d}{\langle h_{i},\mathbf{V}_{X,Z}g_{i}\rangle}_{\mathcal{H}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{d}s_{i}\leq\sqrt{d\sum_{i=1}^{d}s_{i}^{2}}

(96)

\displaystyle=\sqrt{d\left(\lVert\mathbf{V}_{X,Z}\rVert_{\mathrm{HS}(\mathcal{G},\mathcal{H})}^{2}-\sum_{i=d+1}^{\infty}s_{i}^{2}\right)}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{d\left(I(X;Z)-\sum_{i=d+1}^{\infty}s_{i}^{2}\right)}

The two orthonormal bases maximizing the criterion (
95
)
are actually the left and right singular functions of
\mathbf{V}_{XY}
associated to the leading
d
singular values (see
Theorem

5
). The larger the truncation level
d
, the closer the quantity is to the mean-square contingency, up to the truncation level factor
d
.

In either the population (
96
) or empirical (
95
) problems, the functions are maximizing an objective that is a measure of covariance with a constraint on variance. The constraint on variance is imposed by the norm condition on
h_{1},\ldots,h_{d}\in\mathcal{H}
and
g_{1},\ldots,g_{d}\in\mathcal{G}
, respectively); see
Fukumizu et al. 2007a
. This norm condition is relaxed into a penalization in popular SSL objectives.

Indeed, several SSL objectives can be written in an analogous
variance-regularized covariance
form. This may offer one intuitive viewpoint as to why estimators based on these objectives might exhibit similar statistical properties to those analyzed in
Section

3
. We first describe a format for these variance-regularized covariance objectives and show that a number of popular SSL objectives can be expressed in this form.

Variance-Regularized Covariance Objectives.

Recall that
\boldsymbol{\alpha}:\mathscr{X}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
\boldsymbol{\beta}:\mathscr{Z}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d}
denote encoders for
\mathscr{X}
-valued and
\mathscr{Z}
-valued objects (often images and text, respectively). We denote the standard Euclidean inner product by
{\left\langle\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{v}\right\rangle}=\sum_{j=1}^{d}u_{j}v_{j}
in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
In either case, we consider a batch of data points
(\boldsymbol{x}_{1},\boldsymbol{z}_{1}),\ldots,(\boldsymbol{x}_{n},\boldsymbol{z}_{n})
which are thought to be
n
independent and identically distributed realizations of
(X,Z)
from the probability distribution
Q_{X,Z}
over
\mathscr{X}\times\mathscr{Z}
.
Let us then define the design matrices induced by the embeddings, written as

\displaystyle\mathbf{A}:=\begin{bmatrix}-\;\boldsymbol{\alpha}(\boldsymbol{x}_{1})\;-\\
\vdots\\
-\;\boldsymbol{\alpha}(\boldsymbol{x}_{n})\;-\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d}\text{ and }\mathbf{B}:=\begin{bmatrix}-\;\boldsymbol{\beta}(\boldsymbol{z}_{1})\;-\\
\vdots\\
-\;\boldsymbol{\beta}(\boldsymbol{z}_{n})\;-\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d}.

Let
\mathbf{J}:=\mathbf{I}-\frac{1}{n}\mathbf{1}\mathbf{1}^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
be the centering matrix and construct the empirical auto-covariance and cross-covariance matrices

\displaystyle\hat{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{AA}:=(\mathbf{J}\mathbf{A})^{\top}(\mathbf{J}\mathbf{A}),\quad\hat{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{BB}:=(\mathbf{J}\mathbf{B})^{\top}(\mathbf{J}\mathbf{B}),\quad\text{and}\quad\hat{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{AB}:=(\mathbf{J}\mathbf{A})^{\top}(\mathbf{J}\mathbf{B}).

We aim to write the upcoming objectives in the form

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta})=-\operatorname{\bf Tr}\left(\hat{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{AB}\right)+\kappa\lVert\bar{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{AB}\rVert_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}+V(\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta}),

for hyperparameter
\kappa\geq 0
, matrix
\bar{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{AB}
(which is
\hat{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{AB}
with its diagonal components set to zero), and variance-regularization term
V(\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta})
. The term
V(\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta})
may explicitly include the regularized inverses of
\hat{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{XX}
and
\hat{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{ZZ}
, or may penalize variance or non-smoothness more implicitly.

Example 1: Multimodal InfoNCE (CLIP).

Consider the empirical objective for the contrastive language-image pre-training (CLIP) model
(
Radford et al. 2021
)
with batch size
n
,

\displaystyle\hat{\mathcal{L}}_{\mathrm{CLIP}}(\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta}):=-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}{\left\langle\boldsymbol{\alpha}(\boldsymbol{x}_{i}),\boldsymbol{\beta}(\boldsymbol{z}_{i})\right\rangle}+\frac{1}{2}\log\sum_{j=1}^{n}e^{{\left\langle\boldsymbol{\alpha}(\boldsymbol{x}_{i}),\boldsymbol{\beta}(\boldsymbol{z}_{j})\right\rangle}}+\frac{1}{2}\log\sum_{j=1}^{n}e^{{\left\langle\boldsymbol{\alpha}(\boldsymbol{x}_{j}),\boldsymbol{\beta}(\boldsymbol{z}_{i})\right\rangle}}+\log n,

where the
\log n
factor is appended to normalize the sums in the logarithmic terms and does not change the minimizer. Following arguments used (e.g. by
Li et al. 2021
) for the SimCLR objective—the single-modality counterpart to CLIP—we analyze the logarithmic terms via Tayler expansion. To simplify the analysis, take the large-sample limit to define the population objective

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{CLIP}}(\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta})

\displaystyle:=-\mathbb{E}_{P}{\left\langle\boldsymbol{\alpha}(X),\boldsymbol{\beta}(Z)\right\rangle}

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}_{P_{X}}\left[\log\mathbb{E}_{P}\left[e^{\left\langle\boldsymbol{\alpha}(X),\boldsymbol{\beta}(Z)\right\rangle}\Big|X\right]\right]+\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}_{P_{Z}}\left[\log\mathbb{E}_{P}\left[e^{\left\langle\boldsymbol{\alpha}(X),\boldsymbol{\beta}(Z)\right\rangle}\Big|Z\right]\right].

(98)

Next, define the quantity
c(\boldsymbol{x}):={\mathbb{E}}_{P_{XZ}}\left[{\left\langle\boldsymbol{\alpha}(X),\boldsymbol{\beta}(Z)\right\rangle}|X\right](\boldsymbol{x})
and apply a second-order Taylor expansion for every
\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathscr{X}
, the approximation

\displaystyle e^{\left\langle\boldsymbol{\alpha}(\boldsymbol{x}),\boldsymbol{\beta}(Z)\right\rangle}

\displaystyle=e^{c(\boldsymbol{x})}e^{{\left\langle\boldsymbol{\alpha}(\boldsymbol{x}),\boldsymbol{\beta}(Z)\right\rangle}-c(\boldsymbol{x})}

\displaystyle\approx e^{c(\boldsymbol{x})}\left(1+{\left\langle\boldsymbol{\alpha}(\boldsymbol{x}),\boldsymbol{\beta}(Z)\right\rangle}-c(\boldsymbol{x})+\frac{1}{2}\left({\left\langle\boldsymbol{\alpha}(\boldsymbol{x}),\boldsymbol{\beta}(Z)\right\rangle}-c(\boldsymbol{x})\right)^{2}\right).

Plugging this approximation into the first term of (
98
) yields

\displaystyle\log\mathbb{E}_{P_{Z}}\left[e^{\left\langle\boldsymbol{\alpha}(X),\boldsymbol{\beta}(Z)\right\rangle}\Big|X\right](\boldsymbol{x})\approx c(\boldsymbol{x})+\log\left(1+\tfrac{1}{2}\operatorname{\mathbb{V}ar}\left({\left\langle\boldsymbol{\alpha}(\boldsymbol{x}),\boldsymbol{\beta}(Z)\right\rangle}|X\right)(\boldsymbol{x})\right)

Using the Taylor expansion
\log(1+y)=y+o(y)
centered at
y=0
, and evaluate the first-order approximation at
y=\tfrac{1}{2}\operatorname{\mathbb{V}ar}\left({\left\langle\boldsymbol{\alpha}(\boldsymbol{x}),\boldsymbol{\beta}(Z)\right\rangle}|X\right)(\boldsymbol{x})
, we finally have that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}{\mathbb{E}}_{P_{X}}\left[\log\mathbb{E}_{P_{Z}}\left[e^{\left\langle\boldsymbol{\alpha}(X),\boldsymbol{\beta}(Z)\right\rangle}\Big|X\right]\right]\approx\frac{1}{2}{\mathbb{E}}_{P}\left[{\left\langle\boldsymbol{\alpha}(X),\boldsymbol{\beta}(Z)\right\rangle}\right]+\frac{1}{4}{\mathbb{E}}_{P_{X}}\left[\operatorname{\mathbb{V}ar}\left({\left\langle\boldsymbol{\alpha}(X),\boldsymbol{\beta}(Z)\right\rangle}|X\right)\right].

Applying an identical argument to the second term of (
98
) gives

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{CLIP}}(\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta})

\displaystyle\approx-\left(\mathbb{E}_{P}{\left\langle\boldsymbol{\alpha}(X),\boldsymbol{\beta}(Z)\right\rangle}-{\left\langle{\mathbb{E}}_{P_{X}}\left[\boldsymbol{\alpha}(X)\right],{\mathbb{E}}_{P_{Z}}\left[\boldsymbol{\beta}(Z)\right]\right\rangle}\right)

\displaystyle+\tfrac{1}{4}{\mathbb{E}}_{P_{X}}\left[\operatorname{\mathbb{V}ar}\left({\left\langle\boldsymbol{\alpha}(X),\boldsymbol{\beta}(Z)\right\rangle}|X\right)\right]+\tfrac{1}{4}{\mathbb{E}}_{P_{Z}}\left[\operatorname{\mathbb{V}ar}\left({\left\langle\boldsymbol{\alpha}(X),\boldsymbol{\beta}(Z)\right\rangle}|Z\right)\right]

\displaystyle=-\operatorname{\bf Tr}\left(\operatorname{\mathbb{C}ov}(\boldsymbol{\alpha}(X),\boldsymbol{\beta}(Z))\right)+\tfrac{1}{4}{\mathbb{E}}_{P_{X}}\left[\operatorname{\mathbb{V}ar}\left({\left\langle\boldsymbol{\alpha}(X),\boldsymbol{\beta}(Z)\right\rangle}|X\right)\right]+\tfrac{1}{4}{\mathbb{E}}_{P_{Z}}\left[\operatorname{\mathbb{V}ar}\left({\left\langle\boldsymbol{\alpha}(X),\boldsymbol{\beta}(Z)\right\rangle}|Z\right)\right].

which is the desired form for the population. Now, to rewrite the empirical version, we have

\displaystyle\hat{\mathcal{L}}_{\mathrm{CLIP}}(\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta})=\underbrace{-\operatorname{\bf Tr}\left(\hat{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{AB}\right)}_{\text{covariance}}+\underbrace{\tfrac{1}{4N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\widehat{\operatorname{\mathbb{V}ar}}_{N}\left({\left\langle\boldsymbol{\alpha}(X),\boldsymbol{\beta}(Z)\right\rangle}|X\right)(\boldsymbol{x}_{i})+\tfrac{1}{4N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\widehat{\operatorname{\mathbb{V}ar}}_{N}\left({\left\langle\boldsymbol{\alpha}(X),\boldsymbol{\beta}(Z)\right\rangle}|Z\right)(\boldsymbol{z}_{i})}_{\text{variance regularization}},

where
\widehat{\operatorname{\mathbb{V}ar}}_{N}
denotes the variance with respect to the empirical measure
\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\delta_{(\boldsymbol{x}_{i},\boldsymbol{z}_{i})}
.

Example 2: BarlowTwins.

The BarlowTwins objective
(
Zbontar et al. 2021
)
has already been interpreted as an instance of kernel canonical correlations analysis (CCA) by previous work (e.g. by
Balestriero and LeCun 2022
). This objective is usually defined in terms of the cross-correlation and auto-correlation matrices. To be consistent with other objectives in this section, we handle this by enforcing a constraint on the variance. Let
\iota_{S}:\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}\rightarrow\left\{0,+\infty\right\}
denote the convex analytic indicator function such that
\iota(\bar{\mathbf{\Sigma}})=0
if
\bar{\mathbf{\Sigma}}\in S
and equals
+\infty
otherwise. Let
\mathbf{I}
be the identity matrix in
\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
.
Given hyperparameter
\kappa>0

\displaystyle\hat{\mathcal{L}}_{\mathrm{BT}}(\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta})

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{d}\left((\hat{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{AB})_{i,i}-1\right)^{2}+\kappa\lVert\bar{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{AB}\rVert_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}+\iota_{\left\{\mathbf{I}\right\}}(\bar{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{AA})+\iota_{\left\{\mathbf{I}\right\}}(\bar{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{BB})

Thus, this objective falls into the class as well, as the penalties enforce a particular variance structure akin to the regularizers above.

(

\bar{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}:=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\boldsymbol{\alpha}(\boldsymbol{x}_{i})

can be written as

\displaystyle\hat{\mathcal{L}}_{\mathrm{SC}}(\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta})

\displaystyle:=-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}{\left\langle\boldsymbol{\alpha}(\boldsymbol{x}_{i}),\boldsymbol{\beta}(\boldsymbol{z}_{i})\right\rangle}+\frac{1}{n(n-1)}\sum_{i\neq j}\left({\left\langle\boldsymbol{\alpha}(\boldsymbol{x}_{i}),\boldsymbol{\beta}(\boldsymbol{z}_{j})\right\rangle}\right)^{2}

Example 4: Multimodal VICReg.

We use a variant of the VICReg objective shown in
Shwartz-Ziv et al. 2023

The similarity graph simply connects paired observations, leading to the invariance term below. The multimodal VICReg objective has hyperparameters
(c_{1},c_{2},c_{3},\kappa)
. To state it, define the real-valued function
r(x):=\max\left\{0,c_{1}-\sqrt{x+c_{2}}\right\}
for
x\in\mathbb{R}
. We will also apply
r
to a matrix, which returns the matrix of element-wise applications of the function. The objective is written

\displaystyle\hat{\mathcal{L}}_{\mathrm{VICReg}}(\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta})

\displaystyle=\frac{c_{3}}{2d}\underbrace{\left[\operatorname{\bf Tr}\left(r(\hat{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{AA}\right))+\operatorname{\bf Tr}\left(r(\hat{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{BB}\right))\right]}_{\text{variance}}+\frac{1}{2n}\underbrace{\lVert\mathbf{A}-\mathbf{B}\rVert_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}}_{\text{invariance}}+\underbrace{\kappa\lVert\bar{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{AB}\rVert_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}}_{\text{covariance}}.

While usually thought of as capturing a separate property, we will incorporate the invariance term into the other two terms, which crucially relies on having an extra degree of freedom via the second encoder (as opposed to the single-modality setting). Define
\bar{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}:=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\boldsymbol{\alpha}(\boldsymbol{x}_{i})
and
\bar{\boldsymbol{\beta}}:=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\boldsymbol{\beta}(\boldsymbol{z}_{i})
, then write

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2n}\lVert\mathbf{A}-\mathbf{B}\rVert_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lVert\boldsymbol{\alpha}(\boldsymbol{x}_{i})-\boldsymbol{\beta}(\boldsymbol{z}_{i})\rVert_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lVert\boldsymbol{\alpha}(\boldsymbol{x}_{i})-\bar{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}-\boldsymbol{\beta}(\boldsymbol{z}_{i})+\bar{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\rVert_{2}^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\lVert\bar{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}-\bar{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\rVert_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\operatorname{\bf Tr}\left(\hat{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{AA}\right)+\frac{1}{2}\operatorname{\bf Tr}\left(\hat{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{BB}\right)-2\operatorname{\bf Tr}\left(\hat{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{AB}\right)+\frac{1}{2}\lVert\bar{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}-\bar{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\rVert_{2}^{2}.

The final term
\lVert\bar{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}-\bar{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\rVert_{2}^{2}
can harmlessly be dropped in the objective, as all other terms do not depend on the individual means. Thus, we can redefine our VICReg objective as

\displaystyle\hat{\mathcal{L}}_{\mathrm{VICReg}}(\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta})=\underbrace{-\operatorname{\bf Tr}\left(\hat{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{AB}\right)+\kappa\lVert\bar{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{AB}\rVert_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}}_{\text{covariance}}

\displaystyle\quad+\underbrace{\frac{1}{2}\left(\operatorname{\bf Tr}\left(\hat{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{AA}\right)+\operatorname{\bf Tr}\left(\hat{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{BB}\right)\right)+\frac{c_{3}}{2d}\left[\operatorname{\bf Tr}\left(r(\hat{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{AA}\right))+\operatorname{\bf Tr}\left(r(\hat{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{BB}\right))\right]}_{\text{variance regularization}},

as intended.

F.4
Derivation of Simulation Setting

The data-generating process for
(X,Z,Y)
in the simulation from
Section

4
is as follows. Because we isolate the effect residual dependence in this simulation, we construct a joint distribution
P_{X,Y,Z}
that satisfies
1
, and moreover, such that
Q_{X,Z}=P_{X,Z}
and
\rho_{Y,Z}=P_{Y,Z}
.
Let
\mathscr{Y}=\left\{0,1\right\}
, indicating binary classification. We consider
\mathscr{X}=\mathscr{Z}=\mathbb{R}^{d}
and a pair of Gaussian distributions
(P_{X,Z|Y=0},P_{X,Z|Y=1})
, where

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}X\\
Z\end{bmatrix}\sim\mathcal{N}\left(\begin{bmatrix}\boldsymbol{\mu}_{X|\boldsymbol{y}}\\
\boldsymbol{\mu}_{Z|\boldsymbol{y}}\end{bmatrix},\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{C}_{XX|\boldsymbol{y}}&\mathbf{C}_{XZ|\boldsymbol{y}}\\
\mathbf{C}_{ZX|\boldsymbol{y}}&\mathbf{C}_{ZZ|\boldsymbol{y}}\end{bmatrix}\right)\text{ given }Y=\boldsymbol{y}.

(99)

Then, the distribution is fully specified by mean vectors and covariance matrices along with the parameter
p={\mathbb{P}}\left[Y=1\right]
. Letting
\mathcal{N}(\cdot;\boldsymbol{\mu},\mathbf{C})
indicate the density function of the
\mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{\mu},\mathbf{C})
distribution, the
direct
predictor is equal to

\displaystyle p(\boldsymbol{x}):={\mathbb{E}}_{P_{X,Y}}\left[Y|X\right](\boldsymbol{x})=\frac{p\mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{x};\boldsymbol{\mu}_{X|1})}{p\mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{x};\boldsymbol{\mu}_{X|1})+(1-p)\mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{x};\boldsymbol{\mu}_{X|0})}.

(100)

Similarly, the
indirect predictor
is given by

\displaystyle\eta_{\rho}(\boldsymbol{x})

\displaystyle={\mathbb{E}}_{P_{X,Z}}\left[{\mathbb{E}}_{P_{Z,Y}}\left[Y|Z\right]|X\right](\boldsymbol{x})={\mathbb{E}}_{P_{X,Z}}\left[p(Z)|X\right](\boldsymbol{x}),

(101)

\displaystyle p(\boldsymbol{z})

\displaystyle=\frac{p\mathcal{N}(Z;\boldsymbol{\mu}_{Z|1})}{p\mathcal{N}(Z;\boldsymbol{\mu}_{Z|1})+(1-p)\mathcal{N}(Z;\boldsymbol{\mu}_{Z|0})}.

(102)

The expectation in (
101
) over
Z
given
X=x
can be evaluated via simulation based on the mixture model
P_{Z|X=\boldsymbol{x}}=(1-p(\boldsymbol{x}))P_{Z|X=\boldsymbol{x},Y=0}+p(\boldsymbol{x})P_{Z|X=\boldsymbol{x},Y=1}
and the exact calculation

\displaystyle Z

\displaystyle\sim\mathcal{N}\left(\boldsymbol{\mu}_{Z|\boldsymbol{y}}+\mathbf{C}_{ZX|\boldsymbol{y}}\mathbf{C}_{XX|\boldsymbol{y}}^{-1}(\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{\mu}_{X|\boldsymbol{y}}),\mathbf{C}_{ZZ|\boldsymbol{y}}-\mathbf{C}_{ZX|\boldsymbol{y}}\mathbf{C}_{XX|\boldsymbol{y}}^{-1}\mathbf{C}_{XZ|\boldsymbol{y}}\right)\text{ given }X=\boldsymbol{x},Y=\boldsymbol{y}.

Finally, the
residual dependence

{\mathbb{E}}_{P_{Z}}\left[I(X;Y|Z)\right]
can be computed by the following steps.
First, notice that the conditional distribution of
X
given
Z=\boldsymbol{z}
and
Y=\boldsymbol{y}
is given by

\displaystyle X\sim\mathcal{N}\left(\boldsymbol{\mu}_{X|\boldsymbol{y}}+\mathbf{C}_{XZ|\boldsymbol{y}}\mathbf{C}_{ZZ|\boldsymbol{y}}^{-1}(\boldsymbol{z}-\boldsymbol{\mu}_{Z|\boldsymbol{y}}),\mathbf{C}_{XX|\boldsymbol{y}}-\mathbf{C}_{XZ|\boldsymbol{y}}\mathbf{C}_{ZZ|\boldsymbol{y}}^{-1}\mathbf{C}_{ZX|\boldsymbol{y}}\right).

The likelihood ratio
\mathsf{S}_{\boldsymbol{z}}
from (
13
) can be computed (where the evaluation at
\boldsymbol{x}
refers to the density) via

\displaystyle\mathsf{S}_{\boldsymbol{z}}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})

\displaystyle=\frac{P_{X|Y=\boldsymbol{y},Z=\boldsymbol{z}}(\boldsymbol{x})[\boldsymbol{y}p(\boldsymbol{z})+(1-\boldsymbol{y})(1-p(\boldsymbol{z}))]}{P_{X|Z=\boldsymbol{z}}(\boldsymbol{x})[\boldsymbol{y}p(\boldsymbol{z})+(1-\boldsymbol{y})(1-p(\boldsymbol{z}))]}

\displaystyle=\frac{P_{X|Y=\boldsymbol{y},Z=\boldsymbol{z}}(\boldsymbol{x})}{P_{X|Z=\boldsymbol{z}}(\boldsymbol{x})}

\displaystyle=\frac{P_{X|Y=\boldsymbol{y},Z=\boldsymbol{z}}(\boldsymbol{x})}{(1-p(\boldsymbol{z}))P_{X|Y=0,Z=\boldsymbol{z}}(\boldsymbol{x})+p(\boldsymbol{z})P_{X|Y=1,Z=\boldsymbol{z}}(\boldsymbol{x})}.

To simulate from the marginal
P_{Z}
, we use the mixture
pP_{Z|Y=1}+(1-p)P_{Z|Y=0}
, after which (
14
) can be directly applied.

To interpolate between the setting in which
X\perp\!\!\!\!\perp Z|Y
(the indirect predictor performs at chance) and
X\perp\!\!\!\!\perp Y|Z
(the indirect predictor is equivalent to the direct one), we use the setting

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mu}_{X|0}=\tfrac{1}{2}\mathbf{1},\quad\boldsymbol{\mu}_{X|1}=-\tfrac{1}{2}\mathbf{1}.

Let
a,b>0
be constants and let
\theta\in[0,1]
be a parameter. Then, we define

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mu}_{Z|0}=2\theta a\boldsymbol{\mu}_{X|0},

\displaystyle\quad\boldsymbol{\mu}_{Z|1}=2\theta b\boldsymbol{\mu}_{X|1}

\displaystyle\mathbf{C}_{ZZ|0}=a\mathbf{I},\quad\mathbf{C}_{ZX|0}=\tfrac{\theta a}{2}\mathbf{I},

\displaystyle\quad\mathbf{C}_{ZZ|1}=b\mathbf{I},\quad\mathbf{C}_{ZX|1}=\tfrac{\theta b}{2}\mathbf{I}

and finally
\mathbf{C}_{XX|0}=(1+\tfrac{a}{4})\mathbf{I}
and
\mathbf{C}_{XX|1}=(1+\tfrac{b}{4})\mathbf{I}
. Due to Gaussianity, it is clear that

\displaystyle\theta=0

\displaystyle\implies\mathbf{C}_{ZX|0}=\mathbf{C}_{ZX|1}=\operatorname{\mathbf{0}}\implies X\perp\!\!\!\!\perp Z|Y=y\ \forall y.

On the other hand, using the distribution of
X
given
(Z,Y)
, that is,

\displaystyle X\sim\mathcal{N}\left(\boldsymbol{\mu}_{X|y}+\mathbf{C}_{XZ|y}\mathbf{C}_{ZZ|y}^{-1}(\boldsymbol{z}-\boldsymbol{\mu}_{Z|y}),\mathbf{C}_{XX|y}-\mathbf{C}_{XZ|y}\mathbf{C}_{ZZ|y}^{-1}\mathbf{C}_{ZX|y}\right)\text{ given }Z=\boldsymbol{z},Y=y,

we have that

\displaystyle\theta=1

\displaystyle\implies\begin{cases}\boldsymbol{\mu}_{X|0}-\mathbf{C}_{XZ|0}\mathbf{C}_{ZZ|0}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\mu}_{Z|0}=\boldsymbol{\mu}_{X|1}-\mathbf{C}_{XZ|1}\mathbf{C}_{ZZ|1}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\mu}_{Z|1}\\
\mathbf{C}_{XZ|0}\mathbf{C}_{ZZ|0}^{-1}=\mathbf{C}_{XZ|1}\mathbf{C}_{ZZ|1}^{-1}\\
\mathbf{C}_{XX|0}-\mathbf{C}_{XZ|0}\mathbf{C}_{ZZ|0}^{-1}\mathbf{C}_{ZX|0}=\mathbf{C}_{XX|1}-\mathbf{C}_{XZ|1}\mathbf{C}_{ZZ|1}^{-1}\mathbf{C}_{ZX|1}\\
\end{cases}\implies X\perp\!\!\!\!\perp Y|Z=\boldsymbol{z}\ \forall\boldsymbol{z},

(103)

as the distribution of
X
remains the same given either
Z=\boldsymbol{z},Y=0
or
Z=\boldsymbol{z},Y=1
. Thus, in the simulation, we interpolate between
0
and
1
for the value of
\theta
. We set the parameters
a=5
and
b=6
simply to be different numbers for which
{\mathbb{E}}_{P_{Z}}\left[I(X;Y|Z)\right]
can be computed in a numerically stable manner. We set
p=\frac{1}{2}
and
d=2
.
