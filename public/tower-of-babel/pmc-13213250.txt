Aligners do shed microplastics: Evidence is clear, clinical risk is not
Authors: Sung-Hoon Lim
Published: 2026

At the turn of the millennium, two innovations began to reshape orthodontics: digitally fabricated clear aligners and skeletal anchorage. Clear aligner therapy has since become a mainstream component of contemporary care. Because aligners are worn for more than 20 hours per day, often for 1 to 2 years, they should be evaluated not only by their ability to move teeth but also by their material behavior during use. Their surfaces, mechanical integrity, and chemical stability may change over time, even during the 7- to 14-day wear period of a single aligner.

The recent Korean Journal of Orthodontics (KJO) study by Orilisi et al.1 builds on the same group’s earlier demonstration that commercial clear aligners shed microplastics after 7 days of simulated wear.2 Where the earlier study answered whether aligners release microplastics, the newer study asks what influences that release. Evaluating 10 commercial systems after simulated mechanical friction in artificial saliva for 7 or 14 days, Orilisi et al.1 found that microplastic release increased with wear time, with smaller particles below 5 µm becoming more evident after 14 days. Particle release varied with material composition, whereas the broad manufacturing category, namely thermoformed versus directly three-dimensionally printed (3D-printed) aligners, was not significantly correlated with particle count. Thus, the findings should not be reduced to a product ranking. Instead, they suggest that wear time and polymer composition matter, and that manufacturing category should be interpreted cautiously in light of product-specific factors such as resin formulation, degree of conversion, surface finishing, and postprocessing.

Other recent studies reinforce the importance of mechanical loading. Hemamalini et al.3 tested a single aligner brand (Invisalign) and reported that microplastic release increased with exposure duration, reaching the highest level after 7 days, which was the longest interval tested, whereas control samples stored in artificial saliva without friction showed no detectable release, confirming mechanical stress as the primary driver of microplastic detachment. Barile et al.4 used cyclic compressive loading to simulate swallowing-related forces and reported aligner damage, crack or chip formation, and particle detachment during loading. Although these studies differ in sample preparation, loading model, and analytical workflow, they converge on a practical conclusion: aligners are not static plastic shells during use. They are mechanically stressed polymeric devices that can shed microplastics under simulated functional loading.

Microplastics are generally defined as plastic particles smaller than 5 mm, with some scientific definitions setting the lower limit at 1 µm to distinguish them from nanoplastics.5 Because smaller microplastics, particularly sub-5-µm particles, and nanoplastics may have greater potential to cross biological barriers and translocate beyond the initial exposure site, their detection and characterization are especially important.5 The KJO finding that particles below 5 µm became more evident after 14 days of simulated wear1 should be interpreted as a biologically relevant signal, not as evidence of systemic toxicity. Although microplastics have been detected in diverse human tissues and biologically plausible mechanisms of harm have been proposed, causal links to specific clinical diseases remain unestablished.5 This uncertainty explains why the American Association of Orthodontists currently holds no formal position on aligner-derived microplastics.6 The appropriate starting point is therefore caution without dismissal and concern without alarm. This distinction between particle release and clinical risk is also important when interpreting comparisons among materials and manufacturing processes.

The evidence for directly 3D-printed aligners is particularly instructive because it resists simple generalization. In the KJO study, Graphy, KeySplint, and LuxCreo were evaluated as directly 3D-printed aligners. Graphy, listed as using Tera Harz TC-85 resin, showed the lowest particle release at both 7 and 14 days, although directly printed aligners tended to release more uniform and spherical particles.1 In contrast, Zecca et al.7 compared directly printed Graphy Tera Harz TC-85 specimens with thermoformed Invisalign SmartTrack specimens and reported larger and more numerous plastic particles from the directly printed material under their experimental conditions. This discrepancy should not be reduced to a question of which study is correct. The more useful lesson is that 3D printing is not a single biological or mechanical category. Resin formulation, degree of conversion, washing, postcuring device and protocol, surface finishing, internal defects, and residual monomers may all influence particle release. Comparisons among studies are therefore limited unless these manufacturing and postprocessing variables are controlled and, ideally, standardized.

These observations expose a regulatory and testing gap. In the United States, sequential aligners are Class II devices under product code NXC and 21 CFR 872.5470.8 A publicly available 510(k) summary indicates that an aligner submission may include mechanical-property testing, and US Food and Drug Administration guidance and International Organization for Standardization 10993-1 provide a risk-management framework for biological safety assessment.9-11 However, these evaluations do not appear to include aligner-specific dynamic wear testing that simulates repeated intraoral use while quantifying microplastic release, particle characteristics, and wear-related chemical leachables. The issue, therefore, is not whether aligners meet existing specifications, but whether those specifications adequately capture clinically relevant degradation during use.

Chemical release should be considered alongside particle release. A static-immersion study of thermoformed aligners reported low but detectable bisphenol A (BPA) release, although the amounts varied by material, medium, and time.12 For directly 3D-printed aligners, Willi et al.13 reported no detectable BPA release from Graphy Tera Harz TC-85 resin after 1 week of water immersion, although quantifiable urethane dimethacrylate was detected. These findings are generally reassuring, but they have an important limitation: available data do not establish whether mechanical wear alters the release of BPA, bisphenol analogs, or residual monomers from either thermoformed or directly printed aligner polymers. Because wear can roughen surfaces, create cracks and chips, and increase surface area, static leaching results may not fully reflect clinical exposure.

In vivo data add nuance. Vacuum-formed thermoplastic retainers, which share some material characteristics with aligners but are worn without composite attachments, have been shown to increase salivary BPA after placement. In that study, the vacuum-formed retainer group showed the highest levels among the retainer types tested, with BPA levels increasing from baseline to 1 hour and further to 1 week, followed by a decrease by 1 month.14 This finding indicates that thermoplastic polymeric appliances themselves can release BPA transiently in vivo. In a short-term study of patients beginning aligner treatment with composite attachments, salivary BPA rose significantly 30 minutes after attachment bonding but returned to levels not significantly different from baseline after subsequent aligner placement and after 1 week of aligner use, without attachment removal.15 Because aligners were placed after attachment bonding, this study does not isolate the effect of aligner placement alone. In a separate study of patients treated with Invisalign aligners and composite attachments for an average of 20 months, salivary BPA levels were substantially lower after attachment removal, suggesting that composite resin attachments may be an important source of ongoing salivary BPA exposure.16

The interpretive context has also changed because the European Food Safety Authority lowered the tolerable daily intake for BPA in 2023 to 0.2 ng/kg body weight per day, far below the previous threshold.17 Dental devices are not regulated as food-contact materials, but this toxicologic reassessment makes the absence of leaching data under wear conditions harder to ignore. Whether abrasion-aged aligner polymers release more BPA than unworn materials remains unanswered, although available salivary BPA studies suggest that attachment resin may be a more important contributor than the aligner polymer itself.

For clinicians, the message should be caution rather than alarm. Current evidence does not justify describing aligner therapy as unsafe, nor does it support contraindicating aligners solely because of microplastic release, including in patients with bruxism or clenching. However, parafunctional activity remains a plausible modifier of mechanical wear. In patients with substantial bruxism, clinicians may discuss this uncertainty, monitor visible aligner wear, avoid unnecessarily prolonging wear intervals, and consider alternative appliances when excessive wear is observed.

The practical implication is modest. Patients are exposed to microplastics from food, water, air, packaging, textiles, and many other sources, and aligners are unlikely to represent the dominant source of lifetime exposure. Yet how aligners are worn is clinician-prescribed and therefore potentially modifiable. If tooth movement is tracking well and a 7-day replacement protocol is clinically appropriate, the KJO data provide a precautionary rationale for avoiding unnecessary extension to 14 days.1 When clinically feasible, using smaller planned movements per aligner may reduce the need to extend the wear period of a single appliance. Conversely, if a longer interval is needed for biological or mechanical reasons, current evidence does not prove that it creates measurable harm.

Future studies should move beyond proof-of-concept demonstrations. Standardized dynamic wear protocols should approximate clinically relevant intraoral conditions, including occlusal contact, repeated insertion and removal, temperature cycling, pH variation, salivary enzymes, cleaning agents, biofilm, and bruxism-like loading. Analytical methods should extend beyond particles retained by 1.6-µm filters and include approaches capable of detecting smaller microplastics and nanoplastics that current filter-based methods may miss. The unresolved questions are how much aligners shed in vivo, which particles are biologically relevant, which materials or protocols reduce release, how parafunction modifies release, and whether current regulatory testing captures clinically meaningful degradation. Orthodontics should neither dismiss the concern nor magnify it beyond the evidence. Aligners do shed microplastics, but the health implications remain uncertain. That uncertainty should motivate more rigorous research, greater material transparency, and more clinically realistic simulated-use testing.
