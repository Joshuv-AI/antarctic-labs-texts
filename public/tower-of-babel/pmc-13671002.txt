Changes in Midrange and End Range Adjacent Segment Motion 3 Years After One‐ Or Two‐Level Cervical Arthrodesis
Authors: Clarissa M. LeVasseur, Samuel W. Pitcairn, Xinyi Dan, Yan Ma, David O. Okonkwo, Jeremy D. Shaw, William F. Donaldson, Joon Y. Lee, William J. Anderst
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundBiomechanical cadaver studies consistently find that cervical arthrodesis leads to increased motion adjacent to the arthrodesis. However, in vivo evidence indicates adjacent segment end range motion does not reliably increase at 1 year after one‐level or two‐level cervical arthrodesis. Because changes beyond 1 year remain unknown, this study assessed the effects of one‐ and two‐level cervical arthrodesis on adjacent segment motion 3 years after arthrodesis.MethodsSixty‐two patients were tested preoperatively (PRE), 1 year postoperatively (1YR‐POST), and 3 years postoperatively (3YR‐POST) along with 22 asymptomatic controls, who were tested once. A validated CT model‐based tracking technique was used to measure three‐dimensional (3D) vertebral motion from biplane radiographs acquired during dynamic flexion‐extension and axial rotation of the cervical spine. Adjacent segment intervertebral end range of motion (ROM) and midrange ROM (i.e., head within 20° of neutral) were compared between one‐level and two‐level arthrodesis patients and controls after accounting for head ROM.ResultsEnd range adjacent segment motion tended to increase over time after arthrodesis; however, significant increases were found only after two‐level arthrodesis. In contrast, midrange adjacent segment motion increased significantly after one‐ and two‐level arthrodesis and was greater than in corresponding motion segments in controls.ConclusionsMidrange adjacent segment motion may be better than end range motion for detecting early mechanical changes after cervical arthrodesis that could contribute to adjacent segment degeneration. In vivo changes in adjacent segment motion are inconsistent across patients up to 3 years after anterior cervical arthrodesis.

Introduction

Anterior cervical discectomy and fusion (ACDF) is the most common cervical spine surgery in the United States, accounting for 61.6% of the over 1.2 million cervical spine surgeries performed between 2012 and 2022 [1]. Approximately 25% of patients receiving ACDF go on to develop adjacent segment disease (ASD) within 10 years of their first surgery [2, 3]. The etiology of ASD remains unclear, but both natural degeneration [4, 5] and excessive compensatory motion [6, 7, 8] are believed to contribute to ASD.

The theory that arthrodesis leads to excessive adjacent segment motion is supported by biomechanical studies on cadavers that indicated increased motion, and therefore stress, at the adjacent segments following ACDF [4, 8, 9, 10, 11, 12], with two‐level arthrodesis exacerbating these effects [11, 12]. However, these cadaveric “time zero” studies do not account for healing or neuromuscular adaptation after surgery, and the lab‐based loading paradigms may not replicate in vivo loading [13, 14] and postsurgical kinematics [15]. Therefore, in vivo studies are preferred for assessing the effects of arthrodesis on adjacent segment motion.

There is little in vivo evidence demonstrating adjacent segment motion reliably increases after ACDF. A prospective study of 21 patients with an average follow‐up of 1 year after surgery reported, on average, no change in adjacent segment flexion/extension range of motion (ROM); however, there was great variability among patients, with adjacent segment motion increasing or decreasing up to 10° [16].

Careful study design and data analysis are critical for accurately assessing the effects of arthrodesis on in vivo adjacent segment motion. First, due to high inter‐individual variability in spinal segmental alignment and kinematics [15, 17], within‐patient comparison of preoperative to postoperative kinematics is ideal. Within‐patient comparison (rather than comparison to a healthy control group) allows for each patient to serve as their own control [15]. Second, kinematics are unique to each motion segment [17, 18, 19, 20]. When comparing the effects of one‐ versus two‐level arthrodesis or disc arthroplasty versus arthrodesis, the same adjacent motion segment should be compared between conditions. Finally, due to variation in the contribution of individual motion segments to overall spine motion throughout the range of motion [19], the total amount of head motion needs to be accounted for when analyzing intervertebral ROM.

The present study evaluates changes in adjacent segment motion after one‐level or two‐level cervical arthrodesis at 3 years after surgery. Previous analysis of adjacent segment kinematics in this cohort at 1 year after surgery indicated small (2.3° or less), but not statistically significant, increases in adjacent segment end range ROM [21]. Additionally, no differences were detected between one‐ and two‐level arthrodesis [21]. However, end range positions are rarely achieved during activities of daily living [22], and therefore the end range position does not reflect the typical repetitive loading on discs that may exacerbate adjacent disc degeneration following ACDF. Therefore, we also previously evaluated midrange adjacent segment motion. Midrange motion was defined as the intervertebral motion occurring when the head is within 20° of the neutral position, which comprises the typical motion during many activities of daily living [22]. We found intervertebral ROM during head midrange motion increased significantly at both the superior and inferior adjacent motion segments after ACDF during both flexion/extension and axial rotation [21]. While that study provided in vivo evidence that contradicts long‐held beliefs that adjacent segment end range motion reliably increases immediately after ACDF and that two‐level arthrodesis exacerbates these effects, longer‐term changes in adjacent segment motion remain unclear.

Therefore, this study evaluated end range and midrange adjacent segment motion during flexion/extension and axial rotation at 3 years after one‐ and two‐level cervical arthrodesis. We hypothesized that (1) both ROM metrics would increase from preoperatively to 3 years postoperatively, (2) increases would be greater after two‐level than one‐level arthrodesis, and (3) ROM at 3 years would exceed that in age‐matched asymptomatic controls.

Methods

This study was approved by the local Institutional Review Board (IRB) and all participants provided informed written consent prior to participating. Patients with symptoms associated with myelopathy, radiculopathy, or myeloradiculopathy resulting from either degenerative spondylosis, cervical disc herniation, or disc degeneration who were scheduled to receive either two‐level C456 or C567 arthrodesis, or one‐level C56 or C67 arthrodesis via a standard anterior approach with semi‐constrained plate fixation were recruited from clinics of seven orthopedic surgeons and neurosurgeons. Arthrodesis was performed with either autograft harvested from the iliac crest using a standard anterior approach or allograft from tricortical iliac crest, machined allograft, or fibular allograft per the surgeon's standard practice. Patients were tested prior to surgery (PRE), 12 months after surgery (1YR‐POST), and 36 months after surgery (3YR‐POST). Healthy, asymptomatic, similar‐aged adults with no history of injury or disease that may interfere with spine function, such as previous spine surgery or trauma, were recruited from a volunteer registry to match the age and sex of the patients. These controls were tested once.

During each data collection session, participants sat upright within a custom designed biplane radiography system [23] and were directed to hold still and look straight ahead. Synchronized biplane radiographs of the cervical spine were then collected for 0.1 s for a static neutral trial. Next, participants were instructed to continuously move their head and neck through their maximum flexion‐extension range of motion to the beat of a metronome set to 44 beats per minute such that the participant completed one full ROM cycle in just under 3 s. Verbal encouragement was provided to ensure maximum participant effort. Synchronized biplane radiographs were collected at 30 images per second for 3 s to capture the full flexion‐extension motion for 3 flexion/extension movement trials for each participant. This process was repeated while the participant performed full range of motion head axial rotation for three trials (Figure 1A,B). Radiographic imaging parameters were 70 kV, 160 mA, 2.5 ms pulse duration for all dynamic movement trials, with an 1800 mm source‐to‐detector distance and an angle of approximately 55° between imaging systems. The maximum radiation exposure for each visit during biplane radiography testing was estimated to be 1.3 mSv using PCXMC software (STUK, Helsinki, Finland). Reflective markers were placed on the chin, forehead, zygomatic process, sternum, C7, and acromion processes to record head and torso motion using optical motion capture (Vicon Vantage, 12‐camera system, 60 Hz) simultaneously to the biplane radiography imaging. Reflective marker motion was used to calculate head motion relative to the torso, as described previously [17].

Data collection and processing. (A) Participants performed full ROM movements (flexion/extension is shown) while (B) synchronized biplane radiographs were collected at 30 images per second. (C) C1 to C7 CT scans were collected and (D) used to create 3D bone models. (E) 3D vertebral kinematics were determined using a validated CT model‐based tracking process. (F) 6‐DOF kinematics were calculated throughout the full ROM.

CT scans of the cervical spine (C1–C7) were acquired from each participant prior to surgery with a resolution of 0.35 × 0.35 × 1.00 mm (GE Discovery CT750 HD). The average radiation exposure from the CT scans was 2.6 ± 0.7 mSv. Bone tissue was segmented from the CT volume using automated thresholding and region growing supplemented by manual segmentation (Figure 1C) (Synopsys Simpleware, Exeter, UK). A three‐dimensional (3D) model of each vertebra was generated from the segmented bone tissue [24]. Markers were interactively placed on each 3D bone model to define bone‐specific anatomic coordinate systems directed in the anterior–posterior (AP), left–right (LR), and superior–inferior (SI) directions, with the bone origin located at the center of the superior and inferior endplates (Figure 1D) [25]. The bone‐based coordinate systems were identical for PRE, 1YR‐POST, and 3YR‐POST measurements of intervertebral kinematics.

In vivo bone motion was tracked using a volumetric model‐based tracking technique that matched digitally reconstructed radiographs, generated from the subject‐specific CT‐based bone models, to the biplane radiographs (Figure 1E). Details of the calibration, image distortion correction, and registration algorithm have been previously published [26]. This model‐based tracking technique has been validated in vivo, using beads implanted into the vertebrae as the gold standard, to have an average precision of 0.35 mm or better for intervertebral translation and 1.1° or better for intervertebral rotations [27]. The six degrees of freedom kinematics (three translations and three rotations) at each cervical motion segment, normalized to the static neutral trial PRE, were calculated for each participant following established standards for reporting spine kinematics (Figure 1G) [28]. A low‐pass, 4th order Butterworth filter was used to smooth the six degrees of freedom (6‐DOF) motion path of each bone with the cutoff frequency set at 1.7 Hz for flexion/extension and 1.0 Hz for rotation, based upon residual analysis [29] and previous work [30].

End range intervertebral ROM was calculated using the maximum intervertebral flexion and extension from all three flexion/extension trials and the maximum left and right axial rotation from all three rotation trials. Intervertebral flexion/extension ROM was also divided into flexion and extension components of ROM, with all components of motion normalized to the static neutral position on the corresponding testing date. Midrange intervertebral ROM was calculated as the maximum intervertebral flexion and extension, or left and right axial rotations, from the portions of the movement that occurred while the head was within 20° of neutral head orientation, as most activities of daily living occur within this range [22, 31].

Our primary analysis focused on the C4–C5 and C6–C7 motion segments, which were adjacent to the C56 one‐level arthrodesis and either the C456 or C567 two‐level arthrodesis (Figure 2); our secondary analysis of noncorresponding segments is reported in the Appendix. We used linear models for this analysis to test for differences between one‐ and two‐level arthrodesis, and to test for differences within patients over time in terms of intervertebral end range and midrange ROM. When analyzing only flexion or only extension ROM, models were additionally adjusted for static orientation. Analyses of intervertebral end range ROM were adjusted for head ROM which changed from pre‐ to postsurgery. Tukey post hoc comparisons and adjusted p‐values were reported to account for multiple comparisons.

Motion segments where the primary analysis occurred. The C4–C5 motion segment was the superior adjacent motion segment following a two‐level C567 arthrodesis or a one‐level C56 arthrodesis. The C6–C7 motion segment was the inferior motion segment following a one‐level C56 arthrodesis or a two‐level C456 arthrodesis. C4–C5 motion and C6–C7 motion in patients were compared to the C4–C5 and C6–C7 motion segments, respectively, in asymptomatic controls.

To compare patients with age‐matched asymptomatic controls, Generalized Estimating Equation (GEE) with independence working correlation structure models was fit with group (one‐level or two‐level arthrodesis vs. control) interaction and the motion segment by time interaction, specifying the control group as the reference. At each time point, planned contrasts of patient group versus controls were obtained from the model and adjusted for multiple comparisons using Dunnett‐type multivariate‐t correction.

For completeness, we also performed a secondary analysis of noncorresponding motion segments to further explore adjacent segment motion differences between one‐ or two‐level arthrodesis regardless of motion segment. In this secondary analysis, C5–C6 motion superior to one‐level C67 arthrodesis and C3–C4 motion superior to two‐level C456 arthrodesis were added to the data from the primary analysis and means and standard deviations were reported.

Results

Demographics

A total of 77 arthrodesis patients were enrolled prior to surgery, 65 completed testing at all three time points, and 62 were included in this analysis: 21 received one‐level C56 (9 M, 12 F), 15 received one‐level C67 (6 M, 9 F), 7 received two‐level C456 (4 M, 3 F), and 19 received two‐level C567 (8 M, 11 F) anterior arthrodesis. The average age at enrollment was 47.5 ± 8.4 years, with a range of 24–60 years. Ten patients received autograft while 52 received allograft. Of the 12 patients that did not complete all three time points, 4 failed to return for their 1YR‐POST test and 8 failed to return for their 3YR‐POST test, all due to an inability to re‐contact participants. All data from the 12 patients with missing time points were excluded from this analysis. Three patients completed testing at all time points but were excluded from this analysis due to one having a posterior fusion revision surgery between the 1YR and 3YR time points, one having poor CT images that were unable to be processed, and one patient who demonstrated morphology changes at 3YR‐POST great enough that we were unable to accurately register their presurgery bone models to their post‐surgery radiographs. Twenty‐two healthy, asymptomatic, similarly‐aged adults (12 M, 10 F, average age: 47.6 ± 8.6 years, range: 28–59) were enrolled as controls. There was no difference in age or sex between the groups (all p > 0.513; Figure 3).

Patients undergoing two‐level arthrodesis had less head flexion ROM at 1YR‐ and 3YR‐POST compared to the controls (all p < 0.002), with the 1YR‐POST being significantly less than PRE (p = 0.001; Figure 4B). Patients undergoing two‐level arthrodesis also had less head extension ROM at PRE and 1YR‐POST compared to the controls (all p < 0.007; Figure 4B).

End Range Adjacent Segment ROM

Following two‐level arthrodesis, intervertebral flexion/extension end range ROM increased 1.0° (p = 0.031) and 3.4° (p = 0.002) from PRE to 1YR‐POST at both the superior and inferior adjacent motion segments, respectively (Figure 5A). In patients who received two‐level arthrodesis, axial rotation end range increased 1.1° (p = 0.047) from PRE to 3YR‐POST at the superior adjacent segment (Figure 5B). No statistically significant differences were found in intervertebral flexion/extension or axial rotation end range ROM between any time points following one‐level arthrodesis (all p > 0.090), nor were differences found between one‐level arthrodesis, two‐level arthrodesis and the controls (all p > 0.108).

Midrange ROM

During flexion/extension following a one‐level arthrodesis, intervertebral midrange ROM increased at the inferior adjacent segment 1.7° (p = 0.012) and 1.7° (p = 0.011) from PRE to 1YR‐POST and PRE to 3YR‐POST, respectively (Figure 5C). The 3YR‐POST midrange FE ROM at the inferior adjacent segment following a one‐level arthrodesis was also 2.3° larger than in healthy controls (p = 0.047; Figure 5C). During flexion/extension, intervertebral midrange ROM increased at the inferior adjacent segment 2.0° (p < 0.001) from PRE to 1YR‐POST following a two‐level arthrodesis. No other differences were found in midrange flexion/extension ROM across time points (all p > 0.293) or between groups (all p > 0.445) for the superior adjacent segment after one‐ or two‐level arthrodesis (Figure 5C). During axial rotation, patients receiving one‐level (p = 0.001), and two‐level (p = 0.016) arthrodesis demonstrated a 0.8° and 1.1° increase, respectively, from PRE to 1YR‐POST at the superior adjacent motion segment (all p < 0.016; Figure 5D). The superior adjacent motion segment during axial rotation following a two‐level arthrodesis also increased 1.4° (p = 0.012) from PRE to 3YR‐POST, with the 3YR‐POST midrange ROM being 1.5° (p = 0.035) larger than in the controls, and 1.3° (p = 0.018) larger than in one‐level arthrodesis patients (Figure 5D). During axial rotation, patients receiving one‐level arthrodesis demonstrated a 0.5° increase from PRE to 1YR‐POST at the inferior adjacent motion segment (p = 0.012). No other statistically significant differences were found in midrange axial rotation ROM across time points (all p > 0.117) or between groups at either adjacent segment during axial rotation (all p > 0.093; Figure 5D).

Components of Flexion and Extension End Range

The end range flexion ROM increased 0.8° (p = 0.021) and 1.4° (p < 0.001) at the superior adjacent segment from PRE to 1YR‐POST and PRE to 3YR‐POST, respectively, following a one‐level arthrodesis (Figure 6A). Similarly, end range flexion ROM increased 0.8° (p = 0.021) and 1.4° (p < 0.001) at the superior adjacent segment from PRE to 1YR‐POST and PRE to 3YR‐POST, respectively, following a two‐level arthrodesis (Figure 6A). End range flexion ROM also increased 1.4° (p = 0.010) at the inferior adjacent segment from PRE to 3YR‐POST following a one‐level arthrodesis (Figure 6A). Extension end range ROM increased 3.2° from PRE to 1YR‐POST (p = 0.001) at the inferior adjacent motion segment following a two‐level arthrodesis. Extension end range ROM at the inferior adjacent segment was 2.6° greater (p = 0.007) before a one‐level arthrodesis compared to a two‐level arthrodesis PRE (Figure 6B), but no significant differences were found at the 1YR‐POST or 3YR‐POST time points (all p > 0.546; Figure 6B). No other significant differences were found in end range ROM across time points (all p > 0.078), between groups for the extension motion (all p > 0.340; Figure 6B).

Components of Flexion and Extension Midrange

Midrange flexion ROM increased 1.1° (p = 0.023) and 2.1° (p < 0.001) at the superior adjacent segment from PRE to 1YR‐POST and PRE to 3YR‐POST, respectively, following a two‐level arthrodesis (Figure 6C). Midrange flexion ROM also increased 1.6° (p = 0.008) at the inferior adjacent segment from PRE to 3YR‐POST following a one‐level arthrodesis (Figure 6C). No other differences were found in midrange ROM across time points (all p > 0.138) or between groups (all p > 0.061) for the flexion component of flexion/extension(Figure 6C). Extension midrange ROM at the inferior adjacent segment was 2.0° (p = 0.010) greater before a one‐level arthrodesis compared to a two‐level arthrodesis PRE, and the motion increased 2.1° (p = 0.025) from PRE to 1YR‐POST in patients who received two‐level arthrodesis (Figure 6D). At 1YR‐POST one‐level arthrodesis, the midrange extension ROM at the inferior adjacent motion segment was 2.3° (p = 0.007) larger than in the corresponding motion segment in controls (Figure 6D). No other significant differences were found in midrange ROM during extension across time points (all p > 0.204) or between groups for the extension component of flexion/extension (all p > 0.070; Figure 6D).

Secondary Analysis of Noncorresponding Motion Segments

A trend toward small increases in adjacent segment end range and midrange ROM from PRE to 1YR‐POST and 3YR‐POST was observed in noncorresponding motion segments adjacent to the arthrodesis during flexion/extension, axial rotation, and the midranges of motion. Adjacent segment end range and midrange motion was typically similar to or greater than corresponding motion segments in controls (Supplementary Figures 1 and 2).

Discussion

The aim of this study was to evaluate longitudinal changes in adjacent segment motion during flexion/extension and rotation after one‐ and two‐level ACDF. Our results partially supported the first hypothesis: small increases in adjacent segment end range and midrange motion were observed from preoperatively (PRE) to 3 years postoperatively (3YR‐POST), although no changes from 1YR‐POST to 3YR‐POST were found. Statistically significant increases in adjacent segment motion were more frequently detected in patients after two‐level compared to one‐level arthrodesis; however, we did not find significantly greater adjacent segment ROM after two‐level compared to one‐level arthrodesis, contrary to our second hypothesis. Finally, our third hypothesis was only partially supported during the midrange motion at 3YR‐POST when midrange axial rotation was elevated relative to controls in the two‐level group but not the one‐level group, and midrange flexion/extension was elevated only at the inferior segment in the one‐level group.

Our results showed that the mean change in adjacent segment motion after cervical arthrodesis is small and not necessarily progressive up to 3 years after surgery. In our cohort, between 46% and 79% of patients showed an increase in adjacent segment motion from PRE to 1YR‐POST, PRE to 3YR‐POST, or 1YR‐ to 3YR‐POST (Figure 7). These findings are similar to a previous study of ACDF patients that found superior adjacent segment ROM increased in 11 of the 21 patients at 13 months after surgery [16]. These in vivo results suggest, at least in the short‐term, adjacent segment motion does not reliably increase in all patients after ACDF. This observation contradicts previous in vitro biomechanical studies that report consistent and immediate increases in adjacent segment ROM after arthrodesis [4, 8, 9, 10, 12]. The substantial variability in adjacent segment motion change following ACDF could be attributed to a number of factors, such as pain (before and after surgery), preexisting adjacent segment disc health, and neuromuscular adaptation after surgery (healing) [32]. Longer term follow‐up is needed to determine the etiology of this variability in ROM change, and if these early changes in motion predict longer‐term clinical outcomes such as symptom recurrence or ASD. Furthermore, when significant increases in adjacent segment motion were observed after ACDF, those changes were more often detected from PRE to 1YR‐POST as compared to 1YR‐ to 3YR‐POST. Additionally, several of the observed differences in motion within subjects over time or between groups were small and within the precision of the measurement system. Longer‐term follow‐up is needed to determine if these small differences observed early after ACDF have long‐term clinical significance. This longitudinal data provides new insight into progressive changes (or lack thereof) in the mechanical environment of adjacent segments in patients after ACDF.

Changes in adjacent segment ROM were generally similar after one‐ and two‐level arthrodesis, although statistically significant increases were more frequently detected in patients after two‐level compared to one‐level arthrodesis. These findings do not provide strong in vivo evidence to support previous biomechanical cadaver studies that consistently found that two‐level arthrodesis increases adjacent segment ROM greater than one‐level arthrodesis [8, 11, 12]. One possible explanation for this contradiction is that biomechanical cadaver studies are typically displacement driven, wherein the motion between the most cranial and caudal bones is required to be the same before and after surgery. Therefore, to compensate for the lost motion at the fused site(s), the adjacent segments following a two‐level arthrodesis must compensate more than after one‐level arthrodesis.

Although using each patient as a control is ideal for assessing changes over time after arthrodesis, comparing patient kinematics to controls is useful for identifying motion in patients that is “abnormal” or “excessive” relative to healthy individuals. Our data generally suggest adjacent segment motion up to 3‐years post‐surgery is not “abnormal” or “excessive” when compared to healthy controls of similar age.

Midrange motion data appeared to be more suitable than end range data for detecting increases in adjacent segment motion over time after one‐level arthrodesis and for detecting differences between patients and controls. Differences between controls and patients in midrange motion suggest “excessive” or “abnormal” adjacent segment motion occurs during the midrange, which encompasses most activities of daily living [22, 31], and therefore may provide new insight into joint mechanics that may lead to ASD. It is important to remember that we defined midrange motion based upon head movement from the neutral position, so when comparing patients to controls in midrange motion, we compared all subjects over identical amounts of head motion (±20° from neutral). Therefore, the midrange comparison is more standardized compared to traditional measurements made at the end range of motion that can be affected by participant effort and/or pain that limits motion at the end range.

Similar to our previous analysis of this cohort at 1 year post‐surgery [21], the increased adjacent segment ROM during flexion/extension 3 years after arthrodesis appeared to be primarily attributable to the flexion portion of the motion, while adjacent segment extension ROM tended to remain unchanged. A cross‐sectional study of 1200 asymptomatic subjects found the decrease in overall cervical spine flexion/extension ROM with age was primarily due to loss in extension ROM [33]. These findings highlight the importance of considering our study results in the context of the natural aging process, wherein loss of motion over time may be dependent upon the direction of motion and may not be equally distributed among cervical motion segments.

The study strengths and limitations should be understood while interpreting the results. One strength was accounting for total head ROM in the statistical analysis when comparing end range intervertebral ROM. Both patient groups had less head ROM in flexion/extension and axial rotation at all three time points compared to the age‐matched controls, and head ROM changed slightly over time after arthrodesis, so our analysis approach confirmed the intervertebral kinematic changes over time were not simply due to differences in head ROM. Additionally, multiple timepoints of PRE, 1YR, and 3YR postsurgery allows for better understanding of how the adjacent segment motion changes with time after surgery. In some cases, changes seen at 1YR increased by 3YR‐POST, but there were a few instances of the ROM decreasing back to PRE‐surgical amounts by 3YR‐POST. However, 1YR‐ and 3YR‐POST surgical timepoints are still relatively short follow‐up times in the development of ASD. In this cohort, only one patient required reoperation between the 1‐ and 3‐year timepoints (due to pseudoarthrosis). Further work evaluating longer term follow‐up will help to better understand the effects of time on postsurgical adjacent segment motion and allow us to explore associations between early changes in adjacent segment kinematics and development of adjacent segment disc degeneration and potentially ASD. Another strength is that the primary analysis controlled for the distinct baseline kinematics of each motion segment by comparing corresponding motion segments after arthrodesis. Pooling of different fusion levels within each group could mask important level‐specific effects. These results are limited to one and two‐level arthrodesis at the C56, C67, C456, or C567 motion segments. This study's results are limited to analysis of adjacent segment motion after arthrodesis and should not be extrapolated to disc arthroplasty. Previous studies have generally found no differences in superior adjacent segment ROM after disc arthroplasty [34, 35, 36, 37], but mixed results at the inferior adjacent motion segment [34, 35, 36]. Our kinematics outcomes are limited to flexion/extension and axial rotation and exclude lateral bending which occurs much less frequently during activities of daily living. The data reported here is limited to kinematics outcomes. It remains unclear if the reported kinematics changes are accompanied by radiographic changes characteristic of adjacent segment degeneration or if they are associated with changes in adjacent segment disc loading. These associations between kinematics changes and radiographic or mechanical changes will be explored in future studies.

The etiology of adjacent segment disease following spinal arthrodesis is still heavily debated. This in vivo prospective study provides novel mid‐term data to inform this debate. Large and consistent increases in adjacent segment motion were not observed one or three years after arthrodesis, nor did we find consistent trends of increasing ROM over time. While increased motion was more often observed following two‐level arthrodesis than following one‐level arthrodesis, differences in ROM were rarely found between the two patient groups or between patients and similar‐aged controls.
