Physics-Inspired Binary Neural Networks: Interpretable Compression with Theoretical Guarantees
Arian Eamaz Department of Electrical and Computer EngineeringUniversity of Illinois ChicagoChicago, IL, USA, Farhang Yeganegi Department of Electrical and Computer EngineeringUniversity of Illinois ChicagoChicago, IL, USA, Mojtaba SoltanalianDepartment of Electrical and Computer EngineeringUniversity of Illinois ChicagoChicago, IL, USA

Abstract
Why rely on dense neural networks and then blindly sparsify them when prior knowledge about the problem structure is already available? Many inverse problems admit algorithm-unrolled networks that naturally encode physics and sparsity. In this work, we propose a Physics-Inspired Binary Neural Network (PIBiNN) that combines two key components: (i) data-driven one-bit quantization with a single global scale, and (ii) problem-driven sparsity predefined by physics and requiring no updates during training. This design yields compression rates below one bit per weight by exploiting structural zeros, while preserving essential operator geometry. Unlike ternary or pruning-based schemes, our approach avoids ad-hoc sparsification, reduces metadata overhead, and aligns directly with the underlying task. Experiments suggest that PIBiNN achieves advantages in both memory efficiency and generalization compared to competitive baselines such as ternary and channel-wise quantization.



1
Introduction

Large neural networks, including Large Language Models (LLMs), face challenges related to their size, such as requiring vast amounts of memory for storage and retrieval, consuming substantial power, and being inefficient in communicating over networks or storage for inference. This highlights the need for efficient compression methods
[
1
,
2
,
3
]
. Quantization involves using fewer bits to store each model parameter, while pruning involves setting some parameter values to zero
[
2
,
4
,
5
]
. One effective way to significantly compress a network through quantization is by applying coarse quantization, or one-bit quantization, which converts parameters into binary values
[
6
,
7
]
. A recent paper demonstrates that an LLM with just
1.58
-bit or ternary weights (i.e.,
\{-1,0,1\}
) in its linear layers can perform as well as a high-resolution model with
3\times
more layers
[
8
]
.

Figure 1:
General DNNs vs DUNs. DUNs appear to be an excellent tool in large-scale machine learning applications due to their inherent
physics-inspired sparsity.

Although pruning and quantization are popular sparsification techniques, in many tasks, we already have some form of a priori knowledge about the problem. Instead of blindly using a dense network and then compressing it with such methods, this knowledge can be embedded directly into the architecture. In fact, there exists a class of neural architectures in which the sparsity arises from the network topology and from the physics or mathematics of the underlying task, thereby avoiding manual zeroing of neurons
[
9
,
10
,
11
]
.

The canonical example is algorithm unrolling (also called deep unfolding): a classical iterative solver for the target problem is
unrolled
into a feed-forward network, with each iteration mapped to one layer and the forward pass emulating a fixed number of solver steps. Such Deep Unrolled Networks (DUNs) embed problem operators (e.g., sensing matrices, proximal maps, or linear transforms) and constraints directly into the architecture, yielding structured sparsity, far fewer trainable parameters, and strong sample efficiency compared with generic deep networks
[
12
]
. By leveraging domain knowledge, DUNs can solve optimization problems efficiently (see Fig.
1
) and often train faster while generalizing better
[
13
]
. For instance, in sparse signal recovery, unrolled models have been shown to surpass classical solvers in accuracy at substantially lower inference cost.

For challenging problems, increasing the number of iterations is straightforward and computationally manageable in classical methods. However, in DUNs, adding iterations equates to adding layers and parameters, such as weight matrices, which can be inefficient for inference. Pruning the weights may not be a viable solution because each weight in a DUN plays a critical role in solving the primary objective, and removing them would be counterproductive. Therefore, a more practical approach to compressing the network is to employ weight quantization, especially for linear transformation weights.

1.1
Motivations and Contributions

While one-bit quantization has shown great success in dense architectures like Transformers (e.g., BitNet proposed in
[
14
]
), applying it to DUNs presents unique challenges and opportunities. Our work is the first to explore this, demonstrating that the inherent sparsity from the underlying inverse problems generating the data allows for even greater compression than state-of-the-art methods and reduces training data requirements. One of the opportunities algorithm unrolling offers is interpretability: its algorithmic structure guides compression by assigning each weight and nonlinearity a clear task-level role rather than leaving them as opaque learned parameters. Moreover, this structure provides access to provable properties that naturally translate into theoretical discussions and explicit bounds on generalization ability. We refer to the proposed network as Physics-Inspired Binary Neural Network (
PIBiNN
).
Our main contributions in this paper are:

1) Deepening a DUN is no longer a computational issue.

We apply Quantization-Aware Training (QAT) to the unrolling algorithm, allowing the network to adapt to quantization noise during training
[
6
]
. While QAT typically requires costly pre-training
[
15
]
, this burden is greatly reduced for sparse architectures such as DUN, which need far less data and compute. Moreover, using one-bit weights makes it possible to efficiently increase the number of layers or iterations in the algorithm: our results show that a one-bit DUN with
4\times
more layers surpasses the high-resolution model in both training and inference.

2) Data-driven matrix-wise binarization across all layers.

The choice of scale strongly affects quantization performance. Unlike prior work
[
14
,
8
]
that computes layer-wise scales in closed form by minimizing squared error between full-precision and binary weights, we adopt a data-driven matrix-wise approach. Our method learns a
single
global scale shared across all layers, greatly reducing parameter overhead. Using one scale is also more efficient at inference, as it avoids per-layer rescaling and simplifies deployment. Interestingly, our numerical results show that, with careful design, this one-scale scheme achieves performance comparable to more complex channel-wise quantization.

3) Embedding sparse structure from problem physics into the network.
By embedding the sparse structure of the problem directly into the network, we exploit both data and the problem’s inherent physics, making training more effective. For example, block-structured matrices naturally induce sparse, block-diagonal weights, so sparsity emerges from the unrolled architecture itself rather than from post-hoc pruning. This predefined sparsity reduces complexity, improves scalability, and requires no updates during training. Compared to other schemes, the PIBiNN learns a single scale and automatically obtains zeros from problem-driven sparsity, while ternary requires a third state (
0
) and per-block scales. This reduces metadata overhead and achieves fewer effective bits per link. To demonstrate this numerically, we compare both approaches on synthetic and real datasets, showing that sparsity arising from the structure can lead to better performance than sparsity applied during training epochs.

4) Theoretical analysis and convergence guarantees of one-bit algorithm unrolling.

Previous work
[
16
]
analyzed the generalization ability of algorithm unrolling for quadratic programs, showing that the generalization gap depends on key algorithmic properties, such as the convergence rate. While the study examined features beyond simple gradient descent, it could not derive a convergence rate when a neural network was used for the update process. We extend this analysis to sparse quadratic programs, providing the convergence rate for our one-bit algorithm unrolling. A key distinction of our approach is that each algorithm iteration is transformed into a separate layer of the unrolled network, unlike
[
16
]
, where the same network (with at least 10 layers) is reused for every update.

From a convergence perspective, prior work on Learned Iterative Shrinkage-Thresholding Algorithm (LISTA)
[
17
]
requires the weights of the DUN to belong to a specific “good” set, which is non-empty, to guarantee convergence. Ensuring such conditions for binary weights is challenging. We address this by modifying the proof framework to establish relaxed, achievable conditions for one-bit LISTA. Moreover, whereas previous analyses assume a fixed sensing matrix
[
17
,
18
,
19
,
20
]
, our work also analyzes the robustness of the algorithm when the sensing matrix varies.

5) Numerical ablation study for a large model.

We conducted a detailed numerical analysis on a large-scale network where the data arises from an inverse problem with a block-sparse structure. Comparing a fully connected network to our PIBiNN, we show that the parameter count drops from 3 billion to just 100K one-bit parameters, achieving a
\mathbf{99.9\%}
compression rate in bits. Despite this significant reduction, the model maintains competitive performance.

2
Algorithm Unrolling

Assume we have the following optimization problem:

\mathcal{P}:~\underset{\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{K}}{\textrm{minimize}}\quad f(\mathbf{x})+\gamma\mathcal{R}(\mathbf{x}),

(1)

where the target parameter
\mathbf{x}
belongs to an arbitrary set
\mathcal{K}
and the regularizer
\mathcal{R}(\cdot)
is applied to
\mathbf{x}

with a tuning parameter
\gamma
.
If the objective is differentiable, one approach to solving the problem is to use the gradient descent method,

\mathbf{x}_{k+1}=\mathbf{x}_{k}-\alpha\nabla f(\mathbf{x}_{k})-\alpha\gamma\nabla\mathcal{R}(\mathbf{x}_{k}).

(2)

The core idea of algorithm unrolling is to substitute the gradient term, specifically the gradient of the regularizer, with a neural network. In the proximal formulation, this substitution can be expressed as an operator
\mathcal{N}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
, where the weights
\boldsymbol{\theta}
are learned from the training data:

\mathbf{x}_{k}=\mathcal{N}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\left(\mathbf{x}_{k-1}-\alpha\nabla f(\mathbf{x}_{k-1})\right).

(3)

More generally, this problem can be addressed using any applicable algorithm that can be unrolled into a DUN:

\mathbf{x}_{k}=\operatorname{Alg}^{k}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{k}}\left(\mathbf{x}_{k-1},\mathbf{b}\right),~k\in[K],

(4)

with the algorithm parameter set
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{k}

and
\mathbf{b}
represents the measurements.
After processing
K
iterations, the learning problem is then to find the best model by minimizing the empirical loss function

\underset{\boldsymbol{\theta}}{\textrm{minimize}}\quad\mathbb{P}_{N}\ell_{\boldsymbol{\theta}},

(5)

where
\boldsymbol{\theta}=\left\{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{k}\right\}^{K}_{k=1}
are learnable parameters and
\mathbb{P}_{N}\ell_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}=\frac{1}{N}\sum^{N}_{i=1}\left\|\operatorname{Alg}^{K}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\left(\mathbf{x}_{K-1},\mathbf{b}_{i}\right)-\mathbf{x}^{\mathrm{opt}}_{i}\right\|
,
with
\mathbf{x}^{\mathrm{opt}}
being the true labels. Using a neural network (typically with more than
10
layers) for each iteration can lead to training instability due to the large parameter space and challenges in providing theoretical guarantees
[
11
]
. Moreover, a network used as a proximal operator may struggle to accurately replicate the behavior of the actual operator for structured parameters, such as sparse signals or low-rank matrices.

Another approach is to design a network based on the algorithm’s iterative process, where the number of layers in the network corresponds to the number of algorithmic iterations. In this framework, the activation function within each layer mimics the proximal operator rather than utilizing a multi-layer network at each iteration. One well-known example is the Ccompressed Ssensing (CS) problem, for which ISTA is a classic solver
[
21
]
.
To unroll CS solvers, numerous DUNs have been proposed, among which the most widely known is LISTA:

\mathbf{x}_{k}=\operatorname{ST}_{\theta_{k}}\bigl(\mathbf{x}_{k-1}-\mathbf{W}_{k}^{\top}\bigl(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}_{k-1}-\mathbf{y}\bigr)\bigr),~k\in[K],

(6)

where
\bigl\{\theta_{k},\mathbf{W}_{k}\bigr\}^{K}_{k=1}
are the parameters to be trained,
\mathbf{A}
is a sensing matrix, and
\mathbf{y}
is the measurement vector.
This model plays a central role in various applications, ranging from image denoising to ultrasonic image recovery
[
17
,
9
,
22
]
.

3
One-bit Unrolling: Training Process and Solvers

Denote the weights or linear transformations within each layer by
\mathbf{W}^{(k)}=[W_{i,j}^{(k)}]\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{1}\times n_{2}}
for
k\in[K]
. Our approach in QAT consists of two stages, discussed below.

Stage I
: In this stage, we consider an scaled one-bit quantization of weights.

Specifically, we investigate two approaches: (i) training with the straight-through gradient method or lazy projection, and (ii) conducting QAT as a regularized optimization process. The straight-through gradient method is equivalent to a projected gradient method, as follows:

\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\mathbf{z}^{(t)}=\lambda_{0}\operatorname{sign}\bigl(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(t)}\bigr),\\
\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(t+1)}=\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(t)}-\eta_{t}\nabla\mathbb{P}_{N}\ell_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}|_{\boldsymbol{\theta}=\mathbf{z}^{(t)}},\end{array}\right.

(7)

where

\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(t)}
represents the network’s parameters at the
t
-th epoch.
Notably, in this work, we apply the quantization operator exclusively to the network weights
\mathbf{W}^{(k)}
as

\lambda_{0}\operatorname{sign}\bigl(W_{i,j}^{(k)}\bigr)=R_{i,j}^{(k)}
.
To help the solver escape local optima that may arise due to hard projections and to encourage progress toward better solutions, the effect of hard projections can be alleviated through regularization
[
23
]
:

\mathcal{P}^{(\text{QAT})}:~\underset{\boldsymbol{\theta}}{\operatorname{minimize}}~\mathbb{P}_{N}\ell_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}+\beta\mathcal{R}\left(\boldsymbol{\theta}\right).

(8)

For example, one may use
\ell_{1}
regularization

\mathcal{R}\left(\boldsymbol{\theta}\right)=\sum_{j}\min\left\{\left|\theta_{j}-\lambda_{0}\right|,\left|\theta_{j}+\lambda_{0}\right|\right\}
.
In this version, the update process in each epoch becomes

\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(t+1)}=\operatorname{prox}_{\eta_{t}\beta\mathcal{R}}\bigl(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(t)}-\eta_{t}\nabla\mathbb{P}_{N}\ell_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}|_{\boldsymbol{\theta}=\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(t)}}\bigr).

(9)

Stage II:
Previous research used
\frac{\sum_{i,j}|W_{i,j}|}{n_{1}n_{2}}
as the per-layer scale for one-bit quantization
[
14
]
. However, we propose a data-driven method to determine this value more effectively. Additionally, we use a single scale for all layers of the network, which can introduce more efficiency to the trained model during the inference phase.

Let
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
represent the solution obtained after
Stage I
, where the weights of the network are given by
\widehat{W}_{i,j}^{(k)}=\widehat{R}_{i,j}^{(k)},~\widehat{R}_{i,j}^{(k)}\in\{-\lambda_{0},\lambda_{0}\},(i,j)\in[n_{1}]\times[n_{2}],~k\in[K]
or more compactly as
\widehat{\mathbf{W}}^{(k)}=\widehat{\mathbf{R}}^{(k)}
for
k\in[K]
. In this stage, we change
\lambda_{0}
to
\lambda_{0}\lambda
, where the goal is to optimize the following objective with respect to
\lambda
given the training dataset:

\mathcal{P}^{(\lambda)}:~\underset{\lambda}{\operatorname{minimize}}~\mathbb{P}_{N}\ell_{\lambda},

(10)

where

\mathbb{P}_{N}\ell_{\lambda}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left\|\operatorname{Alg}^{K}_{\lambda\widehat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}\bigl(\mathbf{x}_{K-1},\mathbf{b}_{i}\bigr)-\mathbf{x}^{\mathrm{opt}}_{i}\right\|
Note that
\lambda
only affects the weights of the network and not the parameters of the activation function within
\lambda\widehat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
.

4
Towards Sparse Network with Algorithm Unrolling

Although algorithm unrolling reduces the number of parameters compared to a Fully Connected Network (FCN), it still preserves full connectivity between the input and output at each layer.
Note that even a DUN only emulates the solver without reflecting the structure of the sensing matrix, effectively remaining a dense network. The core idea of the PIBiNN is to impose the problem’s structure onto the dense DUN.
If the original inverse problem involves a sparse, block-structured matrix, we can partition the inputs, where each partition of the output is generated by only a corresponding subset of the input rather than all input elements. This structure results in a sparse, block-structured matrix that the iterative algorithms must respect, reflecting the original problem’s sparsity pattern.

For example, consider the following linear matrix equation as the original inverse problem, where the goal is to recover
\mathbf{X}=\operatorname{Ro}(\mathbf{x}_{1:u})\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times u}
from the measurement matrix
\mathbf{Y}=\operatorname{Ro}(\mathbf{y}_{1:u})\in\mathbb{R}^{v\times u}
,using the sensing matrix
\mathbf{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{v\times p}
,

\mathbf{Y}=\mathbf{A}\mathbf{X}
.This equation can be reformulated as
\operatorname{vec}\left(\mathbf{Y}\right)=\mathbf{A}^{\prime}\operatorname{vec}\left(\mathbf{X}\right)
, where
\mathbf{A}^{\prime}=\left(\mathbf{I}_{u}\otimes\mathbf{A}\right)
.Therefore, we have a block-structured matrix whose diagonal parts are
\mathbf{A}
. In this equation, each column of
\mathbf{X}
is assigned to the corresponding column in the measurement matrix
\mathbf{Y}
, and the column vectors of the input matrix do not influence the output of other columns. To solve this set of linear equations, we can either apply proximal solver to each equation individually or treat the concatenation of all equations as a single update process, as described in the following model for
k\in[K]
:

\displaystyle\operatorname{Co}(\mathbf{x}_{1:u,k})=\mathcal{H}_{\theta_{k}}\bigl(\operatorname{Co}(\mathbf{x}_{1:u,k-1})-\widehat{\mathbf{W}}_{k}^{\top}\left(\mathbf{A}^{\prime}\operatorname{Co}(\mathbf{x}_{1:u,k-1})-\operatorname{Co}(\mathbf{y}_{1:u})\right)\bigr).

(11)

where
\mathcal{H}_{\theta}
is a proximal operator. Since each input partition is independent of the others, and each corresponding output partition is influenced solely by its respective input, we can construct the following weight matrix that is sparse and blocky, mirroring the structure of the sensing matrix:

\widehat{\mathbf{W}}_{k}=\operatorname{BlockDiag}_{u}(\mathbf{W}_{k})\in\{-\lambda,0,\lambda\}^{vu\times pu},~k\in[K]
, where
\mathbf{W}_{k}\in\{-\lambda,\lambda\}^{v\times p}
for
k\in[K]
are learnable parameters. This block structure ensures that each part of the model interacts only with the relevant input, maintaining the sparsity of the system.
In some problems, the sensing matrix
\mathbf{A}
itself is sparse. In such cases, even certain elements within the partitions are independent of one another, generating their outputs based solely on these corresponding elements.

5
Generalization Ability

Although the scheme proposed in this paper can be applied to a variety of problems using algorithm unrolling solvers, the theoretical guarantees are established for a hybrid architecture where the neural module is an energy function of the form
E_{\phi}((\boldsymbol{\xi},\mathbf{b}),\mathbf{x})=\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{x}^{\top}\mathbf{Q}_{\phi}(\boldsymbol{\xi})\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{b}^{\top}\mathbf{x}
, with
\mathbf{Q}_{\phi}
a varying sensing matrix or a neural network that maps
\boldsymbol{\xi}
to a matrix, and
\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{K}_{\mathcal{S}}
where
\mathcal{K}_{\mathcal{S}}
is defined as
\mathcal{K}_{\mathcal{S}}\triangleq\{\mathbf{x}=[x_{i}]\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:x_{i}=0,\forall i\in[n]\setminus\mathcal{S},|\mathcal{S}|=s\}.
This problem is equivalent to the sparse quadratic program. Note that we consider the set
\mathcal{S}
fixed but unknown in our settings. Extending such guarantees to other problems would require different DUN architectures and, consequently, distinct theoretical analyses, which lie beyond the scope of this paper.

Each energy
E_{\phi}
can be uniquely represented by
(\mathbf{Q}_{\phi}(\boldsymbol{\xi}),\mathbf{b})
, so we can write the overall optimization problem as

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}:\quad\underset{\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{K}_{\mathcal{S}}}{\textrm{minimize}}\quad E_{\phi}((\boldsymbol{\xi},\mathbf{b}),\mathbf{x}).

(12)

Given
N
i.i.d. training samples
\{\mathbf{x}^{\mathrm{opt}}(\mathbf{Q}^{\mathrm{opt}}(\boldsymbol{\xi}_{i}),\mathbf{b}_{i})\}
, the learning task is then to find the best model by performing the optimization
\left(\underset{\boldsymbol{\theta},\phi}{\textrm{minimize}}\quad\mathbb{P}_{N}\ell_{\boldsymbol{\theta},\phi}\right)
, where

\mathbb{P}_{N}\ell_{\boldsymbol{\theta},\phi}=\frac{1}{N}\sum^{N}_{i=1}\left\|\operatorname{Alg}^{K}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\left(\mathbf{Q}_{\phi}(\boldsymbol{\xi}_{i}),\mathbf{b}_{i}\right)-\mathbf{x}^{\mathrm{opt}}(\mathbf{Q}^{\mathrm{opt}}(\boldsymbol{\xi}_{i}),\mathbf{b}_{i})\right\|.

(13)

The algorithm we consider here is the following unrolled iterative procedure for
\delta\in(0,1]
(for notational simplicity, we replace
\mathbf{Q}_{\phi}(\boldsymbol{\xi})
with
\mathbf{Q}
):

\mathbf{x}_{k}=\mathcal{H}_{\theta_{k}}\left(\delta\mathbf{x}_{k-1}-\mathbf{W}^{\top}_{k}\left(\bar{\mathbf{Q}}\mathbf{x}_{k-1}-\bar{\mathbf{b}}\right)\right),~k\in[K],

(14)

where
\mathcal{H}_{\theta}(\cdot)
can be either ST or HT operator, and
\left\{\theta_{k},\mathbf{W}_{k}\in\{-\lambda,\lambda\}^{s\times n}\right\}^{K}_{k=1}
are trainable parameters.

To present the generalization result of a given algorithm, we first define three important algorithmic properties as

Convergence:
The convergence rate of an algorithm indicates how quickly the optimization error diminishes as
k
increases,

\displaystyle\left\|\operatorname{Alg}^{k}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\left(\bar{\mathbf{Q}},\bar{\mathbf{b}}\right)-\mathbf{x}^{\mathrm{opt}}\left(\mathbf{Q},\mathbf{b}\right)\right\|\leq\operatorname{Cvg}(k)\left\|\mathbf{x}_{0}-\mathbf{x}^{\mathrm{opt}}\left(\mathbf{Q},\mathbf{b}\right)\right\|.

(15)

Stability:
The robustness of an algorithm to small perturbations in the optimization
objective, which corresponds to the perturbation of
\mathbf{Q}
and
\mathbf{b}
in the quadratic case is demonstrated as,

\displaystyle\left\|\operatorname{Alg}^{k}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\left(\bar{\mathbf{Q}},\bar{\mathbf{b}}\right)-\operatorname{Alg}^{k}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\left(\bar{\mathbf{Q}}^{\prime},\bar{\mathbf{b}}^{\prime}\right)\right\|\leq\operatorname{Stab}^{\bar{\mathbf{Q}}}(k)\left\|\tilde{\mathbf{Q}}-\tilde{\mathbf{Q}}^{\prime}\right\|+\operatorname{Stab}^{\bar{\mathbf{b}}}(k)\left\|\bar{\mathbf{b}}-\bar{\mathbf{b}}^{\prime}\right\|.

(16)

Sensitivity:
The robustness to small perturbations in the algorithm parameters
\boldsymbol{\theta}
is given by,

\left\|\operatorname{Alg}^{k}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\left(\bar{\mathbf{Q}},\bar{\mathbf{b}}\right)-\operatorname{Alg}^{k}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\prime}}\left(\bar{\mathbf{Q}},\bar{\mathbf{b}}\right)\right\|\leq\operatorname{Sens}^{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(k)\left\|\boldsymbol{\theta}-\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\prime}\right\|.

(17)

As we will show in Theorem
1
, these algorithmic properties play a critical role in determining the generalization ability of a given algorithm.
As demonstrated in
[
17
]
, to guarantee the convergence of the ST algorithm (e.g., LISTA) for the sparse quadratic program, the network weights must belong to a specific set defined as follows:

Definition 1
.

Given
\mathbf{Q}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
and
\mathcal{I}=\{-\lambda,\lambda\}^{s\times n}
, a weight matrix is “good” if it belongs to
\mathcal{X}_{\mathbf{W}}(\mathbf{Q})
defined as

\displaystyle\arg\min_{\mathbf{W}\in\mathcal{I}}\left\{\max|W_{i,j}|:\mathbf{W}_{i}^{\top}\bar{\mathbf{Q}}_{i}=1,\max_{i\neq j}|\mathbf{W}_{i}^{\top}\bar{\mathbf{Q}}_{j}|=\mu_{Q}\right\}.

(18)

Selecting the weight matrices from the set of “good” weights imposes a significant constraint within the class of binary weights. This restriction suggests that satisfying the first term of (
18
) with a binary weight may be particularly difficult. Even under the assumption that the set in Definition
1
is non-empty, identifying the minimizer of (
18
) remains a highly challenging task. It is also important to note that the set of “good” weights in Definition
1
only guarantees convergence. However, as we will see in Theorem
1
, stability and sensitivity are also key factors, alongside convergence, in determining the generalization ability of an algorithm. Therefore, our objective is to develop an alternative strategy that not only ensures uniform convergence across all training samples but also guarantees generalization for binary models.

In this paper, our strategy centers on analyzing the term
\left\|\delta\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{W}_{\mathcal{S},k}^{\top}\tilde{\mathbf{Q}}\right\|

for all
k\in[K]
, where
\mathbf{W}_{\mathcal{S},k}
denotes the submatrix of
\mathbf{W}_{k}
formed by the columns indexed by
\mathcal{S}
. As shown in Appendices
D
,
E
,
F
, and
G
, this term plays a pivotal role in determining the algorithm properties. Our analysis reveals that this spectral norm must have a certain bound for all
k\in[K]
to ensure a guaranteed generalizability.

\mathbf{W}_{k}=\lambda\mathbf{B}_{k}
, where
\mathbf{B}_{k}\in\{-1,1\}^{s\times n}
for all
k\in[K]
. Then, we can write

\left\|\delta\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{W}_{\mathcal{S},k}^{\top}\tilde{\mathbf{Q}}\right\|\leq\delta+\lambda\|\mathbf{B}_{\mathcal{S},k}\|\|\tilde{\mathbf{Q}}\|
.Define
h=\sup_{k\in[K]}\sup_{\mathbf{Q}}\|\mathbf{B}_{\mathcal{S},k}\|\|\tilde{\mathbf{Q}}\|
and
\mu_{\theta}=\sup_{k\in[K]}\sup_{\mathbf{Q}}\mu_{Q}^{W_{k}}
.By choosing
\delta
appropriately and setting
\lambda=\mathcal{O}(h^{-1})
, we can guarantee that
\delta+\lambda h+\mu_{\theta}s<1
for sufficiently small value of sparsity level
s
. It is important to note that this choice of
\delta
and
\lambda
is specifically designed for the worst-case scenario. In Appendix
H.5
, we present an ablation study on the impact of
\delta
on the convergence and generalization of the ST update process.

,

, and

, we show that the parameter
\alpha_{\theta}=\sup_{k\in[K]}\sup_{\mathbf{Q}}\|\delta\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{W}^{\top}_{\mathcal{S},k}\tilde{\mathbf{Q}}\|
plays a crucial role in all algorithmic properties of the HT update process. In terms of order complexity, the impact of
\alpha_{\theta}
on all algorithm properties in this case is equivalent to Table

. Thus,
\alpha_{\theta}\in(0,1)
provides a sufficient condition for the generalization of the HT update process. In Appendix

, we present an ablation study on the impact of

on the convergence and generalization of the HT update process.

We will now demonstrate how these algorithm properties affect the generalization gap of algorithm unrolling. It is generally known
[
16
,
24
]
that the standard Rademacher complexity provides global estimates of the complexity of the model space, which ignores the fact that the training process will likely pick models with small errors. Therefore, similar to
[
16
]
, we resort to utilizing the local Rademacher complexity, which is defined as follows:

Definition 2
.

The local Rademacher complexity of
\ell_{\mathcal{F}}
at level
r
is defined as
\mathbb{E}R_{N}\ell_{\mathcal{F}}^{\text{loc }}(r)
, where

\vskip-3.0pt\ell_{\mathcal{F}}^{\text{loc }}(r)\triangleq\left\{\ell_{\boldsymbol{\theta},\phi}:\phi\in\Phi,\boldsymbol{\theta}\in\Theta,\mathbb{P}_{N}\ell_{\boldsymbol{\theta},\phi}^{2}\leq r\right\},\vskip-3.0pt

(20)

and
R_{N}\ell_{\mathcal{F}}^{\mathrm{loc}}(r)
denotes the empirical local Rademacher complexity.

Based on Definition
2
and our assumptions (see Appendix
A
), the following theorem provides the generalization ability of an algorithm unrolling:

Theorem 1
.

The generalization ability of an algorithm unrolling utilized to solve the program
\mathcal{C}
in (
12
) for any
t>0
is obtained as

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}R_{n}\ell_{\mathcal{F}}^{\text{loc }}(r)\leq\operatorname{Sens}(k)B_{\theta}+\widehat{\operatorname{Stab}}(k)\left(\sqrt{(\operatorname{Cvg}(k)+\sqrt{r})^{2}C_{1}(N)+C_{2}(N,t)}+C_{3}(N,t)+4\right),

(21)

where
\widehat{\operatorname{Stab}}(k)=\sqrt{2}sN^{-\frac{1}{2}}\operatorname{Stab}^{\bar{\mathbf{Q}}}(k)
and
\operatorname{Cvg}(k)
are worst-case stability and convergence,
B_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}=\frac{1}{2}\sup_{\boldsymbol{\theta},\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\prime}\in\Theta}\left\|\boldsymbol{\theta}-\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\prime}\right\|
,
C_{1}(N)=\mathcal{O}(\log\Gamma(N))
,
C_{3}(N,t)=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{\log\Gamma(N)}{\sqrt{N}}+\frac{\sqrt{\log\Gamma(N)}}{e^{t}}\right)
,
C_{2}(N,t)=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{t\log\Gamma(N)}{N}+\left(C_{3}(N,t)+1\right)\frac{\log\Gamma(N)}{\sqrt{N}}\right)
,
\Gamma(N)=\mathcal{N}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}},\ell_{\mathcal{Q}},L_{\infty}\right)
.

Appendix
G
shows that the algorithm’s sensitivity is directly tied to its convergence rate. The proof of Theorem
1
can be found in Appendix
B

In our scheme, sparsity arises directly from the problem physics, yielding sparse block-structured weights for the DUN, whereas ternary quantization imposes sparsification on the dense DUN via its own operator, and channel-wise binarization ignores sparsity entirely. From Tables
5
and
6
, channel-wise quantization shows the best training accuracy, but our method generalizes better than both ternary and channel-wise binarization. The key reason is that our approach preserves all signs, maintaining the solver’s spectral geometry, while zeros emerge naturally from the architecture. In contrast, ternary sets weights in
(-0.5,0.5)
to zero at each epoch, potentially discarding

50\%
and

I
. Importantly, our approach uses a single global scale across all layers, whereas ternary requires a separate scale for each channel.

The result presented in Theorem

is established under the following assumptions:

is a pair of random variables where
\boldsymbol{\xi}\in\mathcal{Y}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
\mathbf{b}\in\mathcal{B}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}

\mathbf{b}

\boldsymbol{\xi}
and

are independent, and their joint distribution follows a probability measure
\mathbb{P}
.

In our settings, we assume that both matrices
\mathbf{Q}_{\phi}
and
\mathbf{Q}^{\mathrm{opt}}

\mathcal{S}
. Specifically,
\tilde{\mathbf{Q}}_{\phi},\tilde{\mathbf{Q}}^{\mathrm{opt}}\in\mathcal{H}^{s\times s}_{r,L}
, the space of symmetric positive definite matrices whose smallest and largest singular values are bounded by
r
and
L
, respectively, with
r,L>0
. Based on this assumption, the exact minimizer of (
12
) for each sample follows a
s
-sparse structure, where the
s

\mathbf{x}^{\mathrm{opt}}_{\mathcal{S}}(\mathbf{Q}_{i}^{\mathrm{opt}},\mathbf{b}_{i})=\left(\tilde{\mathbf{Q}}_{i}^{\mathrm{opt}}\right)^{-1}\bar{\mathbf{b}}_{i},~i\in[N]
.
A similar argument holds for
\mathbf{Q}_{\phi}
, yielding:
\mathbf{x}^{\mathrm{opt}}_{\mathcal{S}}(\mathbf{Q}_{i,\phi},\mathbf{b}_{i})=\left(\tilde{\mathbf{Q}}_{i,\phi}\right)^{-1}\bar{\mathbf{b}}_{i},~i\in[N]
.

Proof of Theorem

Inspired by Lemma 4.2 of
[

]

Lemma 2
.

For every
\phi\in\Phi
and
\mathbf{Q}^{\mathrm{opt}}
it holds that

\mathbb{E}\|\tilde{\mathbf{Q}}_{\phi}-\tilde{\mathbf{Q}}^{\mathrm{opt}}\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}\;\leq\;\sigma_{b}^{-2}L^{4}\Big(\sqrt{\mathbb{E}\ell_{\theta,\phi}^{2}}+\mathrm{Cvg}_{\theta}(k)\Big)^{2},

\mathrm{Cvg}_{\theta}(k)
denotes the convergence factor of the algorithm layer.

Proof.

Let
\varepsilon:=\mathbb{E}\ell_{\theta,\phi}^{2}

(\boldsymbol{\xi},\mathbf{b})
we have

\displaystyle\ell_{\theta,\phi}(\boldsymbol{\xi},\mathbf{b})

\displaystyle\geq\|\mathbf{x}^{\mathrm{opt}}(\mathbf{Q}_{\phi}(\boldsymbol{\xi}),\mathbf{b})-\mathbf{x}^{\mathrm{opt}}(\mathbf{Q}^{\mathrm{opt}}(\boldsymbol{\xi}),\mathbf{b})\|-\|\mathrm{Alg}_{\theta}^{k}(\mathbf{Q}_{\phi}(\boldsymbol{\xi}),\mathbf{b})-\mathbf{x}^{\mathrm{opt}}(\mathbf{Q}_{\phi}(\boldsymbol{\xi}),\mathbf{b})\|,

(22)

\displaystyle\geq\|\tilde{\mathbf{Q}}_{\phi}(\boldsymbol{\xi})^{-1}\bar{\mathbf{b}}-\tilde{\mathbf{Q}}^{\mathrm{opt}}(\boldsymbol{\xi})^{-1}\bar{\mathbf{b}}\|-\mathrm{Cvg}_{\theta}(k).

(23)

Rearranging gives

\|\tilde{\mathbf{Q}}_{\phi}(\boldsymbol{\xi})^{-1}\bar{\mathbf{b}}-\tilde{\mathbf{Q}}^{\mathrm{opt}}(\boldsymbol{\xi})^{-1}\bar{\mathbf{b}}\|\leq\ell_{\theta,\phi}(\boldsymbol{\xi},\mathbf{b})+\mathrm{Cvg}_{\theta}(k).

(24)

Since
\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{b}}\mathbf{b}\mathbf{b}^{\top}=\sigma_{b}^{2}\mathbf{I}

\mathbb{E}_{\bar{\mathbf{b}}}\bar{\mathbf{b}}\bar{\mathbf{b}}^{\top}=\sigma_{b}^{2}\mathbf{I}

\displaystyle\|\tilde{\mathbf{Q}}_{\phi}(\boldsymbol{\xi})-\tilde{\mathbf{Q}}^{\mathrm{opt}}(\boldsymbol{\xi})\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\sigma_{b}^{-2}L^{4}\;\mathbb{E}_{\bar{\mathbf{b}}}\|\tilde{\mathbf{Q}}_{\phi}(\boldsymbol{\xi})^{-1}\bar{\mathbf{b}}-\tilde{\mathbf{Q}}^{\mathrm{opt}}(\boldsymbol{\xi})^{-1}\bar{\mathbf{b}}\|^{2}.

(25)

Combine this inequality with (
24
), integrate (expectation over
(\boldsymbol{\xi},\mathbf{b})
),
and use
(\mathbb{E}\ell_{\theta,\phi})^{2}\leq\mathbb{E}\ell_{\theta,\phi}^{2}

\mathbb{E}\|\tilde{\mathbf{Q}}_{\phi}-\tilde{\mathbf{Q}}^{\mathrm{opt}}\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}\leq\sigma_{b}^{-2}L^{4}\Big(\sqrt{\mathbb{E}\ell_{\theta,\phi}^{2}}+\mathrm{Cvg}_{\theta}(k)\Big)^{2},

\mathbf{W}_{i,k}^{\top}\tilde{\mathbf{Q}}_{i}=1
, we have

\displaystyle x_{i,k-1}-\mathbf{W}_{i,k}^{\top}\tilde{\mathbf{Q}}(\mathbf{x}_{\mathcal{S},k-1}-\mathbf{x}_{\mathcal{S}}^{\mathrm{opt}}(\mathbf{Q},\mathbf{b}))

\displaystyle=x_{i,k-1}-\sum_{j\in\mathcal{S},j\neq i}\mathbf{W}_{i,k}^{\top}\tilde{\mathbf{Q}}_{j}(x_{j,k-1}-x_{j}^{\mathrm{opt}}(\mathbf{Q},\mathbf{b}))

(30)

\displaystyle-(x_{i,k-1}-x_{i}^{\mathrm{opt}}(\mathbf{Q},\mathbf{b})),

\displaystyle=x_{i}^{\mathrm{opt}}(\mathbf{Q},\mathbf{b})-\sum_{j\in\mathcal{S},j\neq i}\mathbf{W}_{i,k}^{\top}\tilde{\mathbf{Q}}_{j}(x_{j,k-1}-x_{j}^{\mathrm{opt}}(\mathbf{Q},\mathbf{b})).

Then,

x_{i,k}-x_{i}^{\mathrm{opt}}(\mathbf{Q},\mathbf{b})\in-\sum_{j\in\mathcal{S},j\neq i}\mathbf{W}_{i,k}^{\top}\tilde{\mathbf{Q}}_{j}(x_{j,k-1}-x_{j}^{\mathrm{opt}}(\mathbf{Q},\mathbf{b}))-\theta_{k}\partial\ell_{1}(z_{i,k-1}),~\forall i\in\mathcal{S}.

(31)

Note that every element in
\partial\ell_{1}(x)
has a magnitude less than or equal to
1
. Therefore, for all
i\in\mathcal{S}
,

\displaystyle\left|x_{i,k}-x^{\mathrm{opt}}_{i}\right|

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{j\in\mathcal{S},j\neq i}\left|\mathbf{W}_{i,k}^{\top}\tilde{\mathbf{Q}}_{j}\right|\left|x_{j,k-1}-x_{j}^{\mathrm{opt}}(\mathbf{Q},\mathbf{b})\right|+\theta_{k},

(32)

\displaystyle\leq\mu_{Q}\sum_{j\in\mathcal{S},j\neq i}\left|x_{j,k-1}-x_{j}^{\mathrm{opt}}(\mathbf{Q},\mathbf{b})\right|+\theta_{k}.

Based on the chosen value of
\theta_{k}
, we can write
\|\mathbf{x}_{k}-\mathbf{x}^{\mathrm{opt}}(\mathbf{Q},\mathbf{b})\|_{1}=\|\mathbf{x}_{\mathcal{S},k}-\mathbf{x}_{\mathcal{S}}^{\mathrm{opt}}(\mathbf{Q},\mathbf{b})\|_{1}
for all
k
. Then

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{x}_{k}-\mathbf{x}^{\mathrm{opt}}(\mathbf{Q},\mathbf{b})\|_{1}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i\in\mathcal{S}}\left|x_{i,k}-x_{i}^{\mathrm{opt}}(\mathbf{Q},\mathbf{b})\right|,

(33)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i\in\mathcal{S}}\left(\mu_{Q}\sum_{j\in\mathcal{S},j\neq i}\left|x_{j,k-1}-x_{j}^{\mathrm{opt}}(\mathbf{Q},\mathbf{b})\right|+\theta_{k}\right),

\displaystyle=\mu_{Q}(|\mathcal{S}|-1)\sum_{i\in\mathcal{S}}\left|x_{i,k-1}-x_{i}^{\mathrm{opt}}(\mathbf{Q},\mathbf{b})\right|+\theta_{k}|\mathcal{S}|,

\displaystyle\leq\mu_{Q}(|\mathcal{S}|-1)\|\mathbf{x}_{k-1}-\mathbf{x}^{\mathrm{opt}}(\mathbf{Q},\mathbf{b})\|_{1}+\theta_{k}|\mathcal{S}|.

To generalize (
33
) for the whole dataset, we take the supremum over
\mathbf{x}^{\mathrm{opt}}(\mathbf{Q},\mathbf{b})
, and by
|\mathcal{S}|=s
we can write

\sup_{\mathbf{x}^{\mathrm{opt}}(\mathbf{Q},\mathbf{b})}\|\mathbf{x}_{k}-\mathbf{x}^{\mathrm{opt}}(\mathbf{Q},\mathbf{b})\|_{1}\leq\mu(s-1)\sup_{\mathbf{x}^{\mathrm{opt}}(\mathbf{Q},\mathbf{b})}\|\mathbf{x}_{k-1}-\mathbf{x}^{\mathrm{opt}}(\mathbf{Q},\mathbf{b})\|_{1}+s\theta_{k},

(34)

where by our choice of
\theta_{k}
, we have

\sup_{\mathbf{x}^{\mathrm{opt}}(\mathbf{Q},\mathbf{b})}\|\mathbf{x}_{k}-\mathbf{x}^{\mathrm{opt}}(\mathbf{Q},\mathbf{b})\|_{1}\leq(2\mu s-\mu)\sup_{\mathbf{x}^{\mathrm{opt}}(\mathbf{Q},\mathbf{b})}\|\mathbf{x}_{k-1}-\mathbf{x}^{\mathrm{opt}}(\mathbf{Q},\mathbf{b})\|_{1}.

(35)

By recursively applying the bound (
35
), we can obtain

\displaystyle\sup_{\mathbf{x}^{\mathrm{opt}}(\mathbf{Q},\mathbf{b})}\|\mathbf{x}_{k}-\mathbf{x}^{\mathrm{opt}}(\mathbf{Q},\mathbf{b})\|_{1}

\displaystyle\leq(2\mu s-\mu)^{k}\sup_{\mathbf{x}^{\mathrm{opt}}(\mathbf{Q},\mathbf{b})}\|\mathbf{x}_{0}-\mathbf{x}^{\mathrm{opt}}(\mathbf{Q},\mathbf{b})\|_{1}

(36)

\displaystyle=(2\mu s-\mu)^{k}\sup_{\mathbf{x}^{\mathrm{opt}}(\mathbf{Q},\mathbf{b})}\|\mathbf{x}^{\mathrm{opt}}(\mathbf{Q},\mathbf{b})\|_{1},

\displaystyle\leq(2\mu s-\mu)^{k}B.

Since
\|\mathbf{x}\|_{2}\leq\|\mathbf{x}\|_{1}
for any
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, we can rewrite (
36
) as

\sup_{\mathbf{x}^{\mathrm{opt}}(\mathbf{Q},\mathbf{b})}\|\mathbf{x}_{k}-\mathbf{x}^{\mathrm{opt}}(\mathbf{Q},\mathbf{b})\|\leq(2\mu s-\mu)^{k}B.

(37)

As long as
s<\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{2\mu}
, the convergence bound (
37
) holds uniformly for all
\mathbf{x}^{\mathrm{opt}}(\mathbf{Q},\mathbf{b})
. Therefore, the convergence parameter is

\mathrm{Cvg}(k)=(2\mu s-\mu)^{k}B.

(38)

\bullet

Limitation:
As discussed in Section
5
, the limitation of this argument stems from the constraint
\mathbf{W}_{i}^{\top}\bar{\mathbf{Q}}_{i}=1
for all
i\in[n]
. In particular, it is not clear that binary weights can satisfy this condition, and no straightforward proof appears to exist.

Appendix D
Convergence Analysis For Soft-Thresholding Operator

In this section, we provide the convergence analysis of the ST operator for any
\delta\in(0,1]
. For each layer
k\in[K]
, we select the weight matrix
\mathbf{W}_{k}
according to the construction detailed in Section
5
. Specifically, the weight matrices
\{\mathbf{W}_{k}\}_{k=1}^{K}
are chosen to satisfy:

\mathbf{W}_{k}\in\mathcal{ST}_{\mathbf{W}}(\mathbf{Q};\delta)\triangleq\left\{\mathbf{W}\in\{-\lambda,\lambda\}^{s\times n}:\left\|\delta\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{W}_{\mathcal{S}}^{\top}\tilde{\mathbf{Q}}\right\|+\mu_{Q}^{W}s\in(0,1)\right\},~k\in[K],~\delta\in(0,1],

(39)

where similar to Definition
1
,
\mu_{Q}^{W}
is defined as

\mu_{Q}^{W}=\sup_{i,j\in[n],i\neq j}\left|\mathbf{W}_{i}^{\top}\bar{\mathbf{Q}}_{j}\right|.

(40)

Similar to Appendix
C
, in order to guarantee that
\operatorname{supp}(\mathbf{x}_{k})=\mathcal{S}
, we choose
\theta_{k}
as

\theta_{k}=\mu^{W_{k}}\sup_{\mathbf{x}^{\mathrm{opt}}(\mathbf{Q},\mathbf{b})}\|\mathbf{x}_{k-1}-\mathbf{x}^{\mathrm{opt}}(\mathbf{Q},\mathbf{b})\|_{1},

(41)

where
\mu^{W_{k}}=\sup_{\mathbf{Q}}\mu_{Q}^{W_{k}}
. We can then write the convergence result as follows:

\displaystyle\left\|\mathbf{x}_{k}-\mathbf{x}^{\mathrm{opt}}(\mathbf{Q},\mathbf{b})\right\|

\displaystyle=\left\|\operatorname{ST}_{\theta_{k}}\left(\delta\mathbf{x}_{k-1}-\mathbf{W}^{\top}_{k}\left(\bar{\mathbf{Q}}\mathbf{x}_{k-1}-\bar{\mathbf{b}}\right)\right)-\mathbf{x}^{\mathrm{opt}}(\mathbf{Q},\mathbf{b})\right\|,

(42)

\displaystyle=\left\|\delta\mathbf{x}_{\mathcal{S},k-1}-\mathbf{W}^{\top}_{\mathcal{S},k}\left(\tilde{\mathbf{Q}}\mathbf{x}_{\mathcal{S},k-1}-\bar{\mathbf{b}}\right)-\mathbf{x}^{\mathrm{opt}}_{\mathcal{S}}(\mathbf{Q},\mathbf{b})-\theta_{k}\operatorname{sign}(\mathcal{F}_{k-1})\right\|,~k\in[K],

where the second step follows from the selection of
\theta_{k}
in (
41
) and

\mathcal{F}_{k-1}=\delta\mathbf{x}_{\mathcal{S},k-1}-\mathbf{W}^{\top}_{\mathcal{S},k}\left(\tilde{\mathbf{Q}}\mathbf{x}_{\mathcal{S},k-1}-\bar{\mathbf{b}}\right),~k\in[K].

(43)

We can expand (
42
) as

\displaystyle\left\|\mathbf{x}_{k}-\mathbf{x}^{\mathrm{opt}}(\mathbf{Q},\mathbf{b})\right\|

\displaystyle=\left\|\delta\mathbf{x}_{\mathcal{S},k-1}-\mathbf{W}^{\top}_{\mathcal{S},k}\left(\tilde{\mathbf{Q}}\mathbf{x}_{\mathcal{S},k-1}-\bar{\mathbf{b}}\right)-\mathbf{x}^{\mathrm{opt}}_{\mathcal{S}}(\mathbf{Q},\mathbf{b})-\theta_{k}\operatorname{sign}(\mathcal{F}_{k-1})\right\|,

(44)

\displaystyle=\left\|\delta\mathbf{x}_{\mathcal{S},k-1}-\mathbf{W}^{\top}_{\mathcal{S},k}\tilde{\mathbf{Q}}\left(\mathbf{x}_{\mathcal{S},k-1}-\mathbf{x}_{\mathcal{S}}^{\mathrm{opt}}(\mathbf{Q},\mathbf{b})\right)-\delta\mathbf{x}_{\mathcal{S}}^{\mathrm{opt}}(\mathbf{Q},\mathbf{b})-(1-\delta)\mathbf{x}_{\mathcal{S}}^{\mathrm{opt}}(\mathbf{Q},\mathbf{b})-\theta_{k}\operatorname{sign}(\mathcal{F}_{k-1})\right\|,

\displaystyle\leq\left\|\left(\delta\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{W}^{\top}_{\mathcal{S},k}\tilde{\mathbf{Q}}\right)\left(\mathbf{x}_{\mathcal{S},k-1}-\mathbf{x}_{\mathcal{S}}^{\mathrm{opt}}(\mathbf{Q},\mathbf{b})\right)\right\|+(1-\delta)\left\|\mathbf{x}^{\mathrm{opt}}(\mathbf{Q},\mathbf{b})\right\|+\theta_{k}\sqrt{s},

\displaystyle\leq\left\|\left(\delta\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{W}^{\top}_{\mathcal{S},k}\tilde{\mathbf{Q}}\right)\left(\mathbf{x}_{\mathcal{S},k-1}-\mathbf{x}_{\mathcal{S}}^{\mathrm{opt}}(\mathbf{Q},\mathbf{b})\right)\right\|+(1-\delta)B+\theta_{k}\sqrt{s},~k\in[K].

Based on the selection of
\theta_{k}
in (
41
), we have

\displaystyle\left\|\mathbf{x}_{k}-\mathbf{x}^{\mathrm{opt}}(\mathbf{Q},\mathbf{b})\right\|

\displaystyle\leq\left\|\delta\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{W}^{\top}_{\mathcal{S},k}\tilde{\mathbf{Q}}\right\|\left\|\mathbf{x}_{k-1}-\mathbf{x}^{\mathrm{opt}}(\mathbf{Q},\mathbf{b})\right\|+(1-\delta)B

(45)

\displaystyle+\mu^{W_{k}}\sqrt{s}\sup_{\mathbf{x}^{\mathrm{opt}}(\mathbf{Q},\mathbf{b})}\|\mathbf{x}_{k-1}-\mathbf{x}^{\mathrm{opt}}(\mathbf{Q},\mathbf{b})\|_{1},

\displaystyle\leq\left\|\delta\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{W}^{\top}_{\mathcal{S},k}\tilde{\mathbf{Q}}\right\|\left\|\mathbf{x}_{k-1}-\mathbf{x}^{\mathrm{opt}}(\mathbf{Q},\mathbf{b})\right\|+(1-\delta)B

\displaystyle+\mu^{W_{k}}s\sup_{\mathbf{x}^{\mathrm{opt}}(\mathbf{Q},\mathbf{b})}\|\mathbf{x}_{k-1}-\mathbf{x}^{\mathrm{opt}}(\mathbf{Q},\mathbf{b})\|,~k\in[K].

To generalize (
45
) for the whole dataset, we take the supremum over
\mathbf{x}^{\mathrm{opt}}(\mathbf{Q},\mathbf{b})
:

\displaystyle\sup_{\mathbf{x}^{\mathrm{opt}}(\mathbf{Q},\mathbf{b})}\left\|\mathbf{x}_{k}-\mathbf{x}^{\mathrm{opt}}(\mathbf{Q},\mathbf{b})\right\|

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{\tilde{\mathbf{Q}}\in\mathcal{H}^{s\times s}_{r,L}}\left\|\delta\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{W}^{\top}_{\mathcal{S},k}\tilde{\mathbf{Q}}\right\|\sup_{\mathbf{x}^{\mathrm{opt}}(\mathbf{Q},\mathbf{b})}\left\|\mathbf{x}_{k-1}-\mathbf{x}^{\mathrm{opt}}(\mathbf{Q},\mathbf{b})\right\|+(1-\delta)B

(46)

\displaystyle+\mu^{W_{k}}s\sup_{\mathbf{x}^{\mathrm{opt}}(\mathbf{Q},\mathbf{b})}\|\mathbf{x}_{k-1}-\mathbf{x}^{\mathrm{opt}}(\mathbf{Q},\mathbf{b})\|,

\displaystyle=\left[\sup_{\tilde{\mathbf{Q}}\in\mathcal{H}^{s\times s}_{r,L}}\left\|\delta\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{W}^{\top}_{\mathcal{S},k}\tilde{\mathbf{Q}}\right\|+\mu^{W_{k}}s\right]\sup_{\mathbf{x}^{\mathrm{opt}}(\mathbf{Q},\mathbf{b})}\left\|\mathbf{x}_{k-1}-\mathbf{x}^{\mathrm{opt}}(\mathbf{Q},\mathbf{b})\right\|+(1-\delta)B,~k\in[K].

By recursively applying the bound (
46
), we can obtain

\displaystyle\sup_{\mathbf{x}^{\mathrm{opt}}(\mathbf{Q},\mathbf{b})}\left\|\mathbf{x}_{k}-\mathbf{x}^{\mathrm{opt}}(\mathbf{Q},\mathbf{b})\right\|

\displaystyle\leq\Pi_{i=1}^{k}\left[\sup_{\tilde{\mathbf{Q}}\in\mathcal{H}^{s\times s}_{r,L}}\left\|\delta\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{W}^{\top}_{\mathcal{S},i}\tilde{\mathbf{Q}}\right\|+\mu^{W_{i}}s\right]\sup_{\mathbf{x}^{\mathrm{opt}}(\mathbf{Q},\mathbf{b})}\left\|\mathbf{x}_{0}-\mathbf{x}^{\mathrm{opt}}(\mathbf{Q},\mathbf{b})\right\|

(47)

\displaystyle+(1-\delta)B+\sum_{i=1}^{k-1}(1-\delta)B\Pi_{j=1}^{i}\left[\sup_{\tilde{\mathbf{Q}}\in\mathcal{H}^{s\times s}_{r,L}}\left\|\delta\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{W}^{\top}_{\mathcal{S},k-i}\tilde{\mathbf{Q}}\right\|+\mu^{W_{i}}s\right],~k\in[K].

Define
\alpha_{\theta}\triangleq\sup_{i\in[k]}\left[\sup_{\tilde{\mathbf{Q}}\in\mathcal{H}^{s\times s}_{r,L}}\left\|\delta\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{W}^{\top}_{\mathcal{S},i}\tilde{\mathbf{Q}}\right\|+\mu^{W_{i}}s\right]
. With this definition, we can rewrite (
47
) as

\displaystyle\sup_{\mathbf{x}^{\mathrm{opt}}(\mathbf{Q},\mathbf{b})}\left\|\mathbf{x}_{k}-\mathbf{x}^{\mathrm{opt}}(\mathbf{Q},\mathbf{b})\right\|

\displaystyle\leq\alpha_{\theta}^{k}B+\sum_{i=0}^{k-1}(1-\delta)B\alpha_{\theta}^{i}

(48)

\displaystyle\leq\mathrm{Cvg}_{\theta}(k),~k\in[K],

where

\mathrm{Cvg}_{\theta}(k)=\alpha_{\theta}^{k}B+\underbrace{\frac{1-\delta}{1-\alpha_{\theta}}B}_{u(\delta)},~k\in[K],

(49)

where the worst-case convergence is
\mathrm{Cvg}(k)=\sup_{\theta}\mathrm{Cvg}_{\theta}(k)
.

\bullet

Discussion on the effect of
\delta
:

In (
49
),
u(\delta)
attains its minimum at
\delta=1
, where
u(\delta=1)=0
. Thus, we expect binary network convergence to degrade as
\delta
decreases, with the best convergence at
\delta=1
. In Section
5
, we established the non-emptiness of
\mathcal{ST}_{\mathbf{W}}(\mathbf{Q};\delta)
in (
39
) by selecting
(\delta,\lambda)
such that the upper bound of
\left[\left\|\delta\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{W}_{\mathcal{S}}^{\top}\tilde{\mathbf{Q}}\right\|+\mu_{Q}^{W}s\right]
is strictly less than
1
for sufficiently small sparsity level
s
. This proof, however, does not cover the case
\delta=1
. In Section
H.5
, we provide numerical evidence that optimization methods such as Adam generally do not produce weights lying in the set (
39
) when
\delta=1
. In contrast, when
\delta\in(0,1)
is chosen appropriately, the condition in (
39
) is satisfied, ensuring the generalization guarantees discussed in Section
5
, albeit with degraded convergence. Nonetheless, for sufficiently small sparsity
s
, we also observe that Adam can still generate weights that belong to (
39
) even in the case
\delta=1
. As part of future work, we aim to tighten this bound so that it remains valid for larger sparsity levels
s
in the case
\delta=1
.

Appendix E
Convergence Analysis For Hard-Thresholding Operator

In this section, we present the convergence analysis of the HT operator for any
\delta\in(0,1]
. For each layer
k\in[K]
, the weight matrices
\{\mathbf{W}_{k}\}_{k=1}^{K}
are chosen to satisfy:

\mathbf{W}_{k}\in\mathcal{HT}_{\mathbf{W}}(\mathbf{Q};\delta)\triangleq\left\{\mathbf{W}\in\{-\lambda,\lambda\}^{s\times n}:\left\|\delta\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{W}_{\mathcal{S}}^{\top}\tilde{\mathbf{Q}}\right\|\in(0,1)\right\},~k\in[K],~\delta\in(0,1].

(50)

Similar to Appendix
C
, in order to guarantee that
\operatorname{supp}(\mathbf{x}_{k})=\mathcal{S}
, we choose
\theta_{k}
as

\theta_{k}=\mu^{W_{k}}\sup_{\mathbf{x}^{\mathrm{opt}}(\mathbf{Q},\mathbf{b})}\|\mathbf{x}_{k-1}-\mathbf{x}^{\mathrm{opt}}(\mathbf{Q},\mathbf{b})\|_{1},

(51)

where
\mu^{W_{k}}
is defined as in Appendix
D
.
We can then write the convergence result as follows:

\displaystyle\left\|\mathbf{x}_{k}-\mathbf{x}^{\mathrm{opt}}(\mathbf{Q},\mathbf{b})\right\|

\displaystyle=\left\|\operatorname{HT}_{\theta_{k}}\left(\delta\mathbf{x}_{k-1}-\mathbf{W}^{\top}_{k}\left(\bar{\mathbf{Q}}\mathbf{x}_{k-1}-\bar{\mathbf{b}}\right)\right)-\mathbf{x}^{\mathrm{opt}}(\mathbf{Q},\mathbf{b})\right\|,

(52)

\displaystyle=\left\|\delta\mathbf{x}_{\mathcal{S},k-1}-\mathbf{W}_{\mathcal{S},k}^{\top}\left(\tilde{\mathbf{Q}}\mathbf{x}_{\mathcal{S},k-1}-\bar{\mathbf{b}}\right)-\mathbf{x}^{\mathrm{opt}}_{\mathcal{S}}(\mathbf{Q},\mathbf{b})\right\|,

where the second step follows from the selection of
\theta_{k}
in (
51
). We can expand (
52
) as

\displaystyle\left\|\mathbf{x}_{k}-\mathbf{x}^{\mathrm{opt}}(\mathbf{Q},\mathbf{b})\right\|

\displaystyle=\left\|\delta\mathbf{x}_{\mathcal{S},k-1}-\mathbf{W}_{\mathcal{S},k}^{\top}\left(\tilde{\mathbf{Q}}\mathbf{x}_{\mathcal{S},k-1}-\bar{\mathbf{b}}\right)-\mathbf{x}^{\mathrm{opt}}_{\mathcal{S}}(\mathbf{Q},\mathbf{b})\right\|,

(53)

\displaystyle=\left\|\delta\mathbf{x}_{\mathcal{S},k-1}-\mathbf{W}^{\top}_{\mathcal{S},k}\tilde{\mathbf{Q}}\left(\mathbf{x}_{\mathcal{S},k-1}-\mathbf{x}_{\mathcal{S}}^{\mathrm{opt}}(\mathbf{Q},\mathbf{b})\right)-\delta\mathbf{x}_{\mathcal{S}}^{\mathrm{opt}}(\mathbf{Q},\mathbf{b})-(1-\delta)\mathbf{x}_{\mathcal{S}}^{\mathrm{opt}}(\mathbf{Q},\mathbf{b})\right\|,

\displaystyle\leq\left\|\left(\delta\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{W}^{\top}_{\mathcal{S},k}\tilde{\mathbf{Q}}\right)\left(\mathbf{x}_{\mathcal{S},k-1}-\mathbf{x}_{\mathcal{S}}^{\mathrm{opt}}(\mathbf{Q},\mathbf{b})\right)\right\|+(1-\delta)\left\|\mathbf{x}^{\mathrm{opt}}(\mathbf{Q},\mathbf{b})\right\|,

\displaystyle\leq\left\|\left(\delta\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{W}^{\top}_{\mathcal{S},k}\tilde{\mathbf{Q}}\right)\left(\mathbf{x}_{\mathcal{S},k-1}-\mathbf{x}_{\mathcal{S}}^{\mathrm{opt}}(\mathbf{Q},\mathbf{b})\right)\right\|+(1-\delta)B,~k\in[K].

To generalize (
53
) for the whole dataset, we take the supremum over
\mathbf{x}^{\mathrm{opt}}(\mathbf{Q},\mathbf{b})
:

\sup_{\mathbf{x}^{\mathrm{opt}}(\mathbf{Q},\mathbf{b})}\left\|\mathbf{x}_{k}-\mathbf{x}^{\mathrm{opt}}(\mathbf{Q},\mathbf{b})\right\|\leq\sup_{\tilde{\mathbf{Q}}\in\mathcal{H}^{s\times s}_{r,L}}\left\|\delta\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{W}^{\top}_{\mathcal{S},k}\tilde{\mathbf{Q}}\right\|\sup_{\mathbf{x}^{\mathrm{opt}}(\mathbf{Q},\mathbf{b})}\left\|\mathbf{x}_{k-1}-\mathbf{x}^{\mathrm{opt}}(\mathbf{Q},\mathbf{b})\right\|+(1-\delta)B,~k\in[K].

(54)

By recursively applying the bound (
54
), we can obtain

\displaystyle\sup_{\mathbf{x}^{\mathrm{opt}}(\mathbf{Q},\mathbf{b})}\left\|\mathbf{x}_{k}-\mathbf{x}^{\mathrm{opt}}(\mathbf{Q},\mathbf{b})\right\|

\displaystyle\leq\Pi_{i=1}^{k}\left[\sup_{\tilde{\mathbf{Q}}\in\mathcal{H}^{s\times s}_{r,L}}\left\|\delta\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{W}^{\top}_{\mathcal{S},i}\tilde{\mathbf{Q}}\right\|\right]\sup_{\mathbf{x}^{\mathrm{opt}}(\mathbf{Q},\mathbf{b})}\left\|\mathbf{x}_{0}-\mathbf{x}^{\mathrm{opt}}(\mathbf{Q},\mathbf{b})\right\|

(55)

\displaystyle+(1-\delta)B+\sum_{i=1}^{k-1}(1-\delta)B\Pi_{j=1}^{i}\left[\sup_{\tilde{\mathbf{Q}}\in\mathcal{H}^{s\times s}_{r,L}}\left\|\delta\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{W}^{\top}_{\mathcal{S},k-i}\tilde{\mathbf{Q}}\right\|\right],~k\in[K].

Define
\alpha_{\theta}\triangleq\sup_{i\in[k]}\left[\sup_{\tilde{\mathbf{Q}}\in\mathcal{H}^{s\times s}_{r,L}}\left\|\delta\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{W}^{\top}_{\mathcal{S},i}\tilde{\mathbf{Q}}\right\|\right]
. With this definition, we can rewrite (
55
) as

\displaystyle\sup_{\mathbf{x}^{\mathrm{opt}}(\mathbf{Q},\mathbf{b})}\left\|\mathbf{x}_{k}-\mathbf{x}^{\mathrm{opt}}(\mathbf{Q},\mathbf{b})\right\|

\displaystyle\leq\alpha_{\theta}^{k}B+\sum_{i=0}^{k-1}(1-\delta)B\alpha_{\theta}^{i}

(56)

\displaystyle\leq\mathrm{Cvg}_{\theta}(k),~k\in[K],

where

\mathrm{Cvg}_{\theta}(k)=\alpha_{\theta}^{k}B+\underbrace{\frac{1-\delta}{1-\alpha_{\theta}}B}_{v(\delta)},~k\in[K],

(57)

where the worst-case convergence is
\mathrm{Cvg}(k)=\sup_{\theta}\mathrm{Cvg}_{\theta}(k)
.

\bullet

Discussion on the effect of
\delta
:

Similar to the ST update, for the HT update the function
v(\delta)
attains its minimum at
\delta=1
, where
v(\delta=1)=0
. Thus, we expect the convergence of binary networks under the HT update to degrade as
\delta
decreases. For the non-emptiness of
\mathcal{HT}_{\mathbf{W}}(\mathbf{Q};\delta)
in (
50
), see the argument in Section
5
, where suitable choices of
\delta
and
\lambda
were made; however, that argument does not cover the case
\delta=1
. To establish non-emptiness in the specific case
\delta=1
, we can impose a sparse structure on the weight matrices
\mathbf{W}_{\mathcal{S},k}
. Specifically, let
\mathbf{W}_{\mathcal{S},k}
be diagonal binary matrices with diagonal entries
\lambda
or
-\lambda
. If
\mathbf{W}_{k}=\operatorname{diag}([\lambda\cdots\lambda])
and
\lambda<\frac{1}{L}
, then
\mathcal{HT}_{\mathbf{W}}(\mathbf{Q};1)
is non-empty since
\tilde{\mathbf{Q}}\in\mathcal{H}^{s\times s}_{r,L}
. Under this construction, the learning process can provably converge with the rate
\mathrm{Cvg}_{\theta}(k)=\alpha_{\theta}^{k}B
.

Appendix F
Stability Analysis For (Soft/Hard)-Thresholding Operators

In this section, we analyze and derive the stability parameters for both ST and HT update processes.

F.1
Soft-Thresholding

By considering the parameters
\left(\mathbf{Q}^{\prime},\mathbf{b}^{\prime}\right)
, we define
\mathbf{x}^{\prime}_{k}
as

\mathbf{x}^{\prime}_{k}=\operatorname{ST}_{\theta_{k}}\left(\delta\mathbf{x}^{\prime}_{k-1}-\mathbf{W}_{k}^{\top}\left(\bar{\mathbf{Q}}^{\prime}\mathbf{x}^{\prime}_{k-1}-\bar{\mathbf{b}}^{\prime}\right)\right),~k\in[K],

(58)

where
\left(\mathbf{Q}^{\prime},\mathbf{b}^{\prime}\right)
represents a perturbation of
(\mathbf{Q},\mathbf{b})
in the dataset.
We assume that
\mathbf{W}_{k}\in\mathcal{ST}_{\mathbf{W}}(\mathbf{Q};\delta)
for all
k\in[K]
and for all
\mathbf{Q}
in the dataset.
Furthermore, we consider a perturbation pair
\left(\mathbf{Q}^{\prime},\mathbf{b}^{\prime}\right)
that satisfies the following two conditions: (i) for all
k\in[K]
, the matrices
\mathbf{W}_{k}
also belong to
\mathcal{ST}_{\mathbf{W}}(\mathbf{Q}^{\prime};\delta)
such that

\displaystyle\left\|\delta\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{W}_{\mathcal{S},k}^{\top}\tilde{\mathbf{Q}}^{\prime}\right\|+\mu_{Q^{\prime}}^{W_{k}}s\leq\sup_{\tilde{\mathbf{Q}}\in\mathcal{H}^{s\times s}_{r,L}}\left\|\delta\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{W}_{\mathcal{S},k}^{\top}\tilde{\mathbf{Q}}\right\|+\mu^{W_{k}}s\in(0,1),

(59)

and (ii) the threshold parameter
\theta_{k}
in (
41
) determines the support
\mathcal{S}
of
\mathbf{x}^{\prime}_{k}
at each iteration
k\in[K]
.
By the definition of
\mathbf{x}_{k}
in (
14
),
we can write the second norm of
\mathbf{x}_{k}
for
\delta\in(0,1]
as

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{x}_{k}\|

\displaystyle=\left\|\operatorname{ST}_{\theta_{k}}\left(\delta\mathbf{x}_{k-1}-\mathbf{W}_{k}^{\top}\left(\bar{\mathbf{Q}}\mathbf{x}_{k-1}-\bar{\mathbf{b}}\right)\right)\right\|,~k\in[K],

(60)

where by choosing
\theta_{k}
as (
41
), we can write

\left\|\mathbf{x}_{k}\right\|=\left\|\delta\mathbf{x}_{\mathcal{S},k-1}-\mathbf{W}^{\top}_{\mathcal{S},k}\left(\tilde{\mathbf{Q}}\mathbf{x}_{\mathcal{S},k-1}-\bar{\mathbf{b}}\right)-\theta_{k}\operatorname{sign}(\mathcal{F}_{k-1})\right\|,~k\in[K],

(61)

where
\mathcal{F}_{k-1}
is defined as (
43
). We can further simplify (
61
) as

\displaystyle\left\|\mathbf{x}_{k}\right\|

\displaystyle=\left\|\delta\mathbf{x}_{\mathcal{S},k-1}-\mathbf{W}^{\top}_{\mathcal{S},k}\left(\tilde{\mathbf{Q}}\mathbf{x}_{\mathcal{S},k-1}-\bar{\mathbf{b}}\right)-\theta_{k}\operatorname{sign}(\mathcal{F}_{k-1})\right\|,

Convergence Inconsistencies For DUN

In Fig.

lead to inconsistent convergence behaviors, including potential increases in the objective function.
Binarization standardizes the weight magnitudes, leading to more uniform influence of each weight on the updates.
Moreover, the discrete nature of binarized weights reduces the variance in the updates. Since the
weights can only take on two values, the updates become more predictable, and the optimization
process is less susceptible to the erratic behaviors caused by fluctuating weight values. Additionally, Fig.

Appendix I
Overlap Analysis of Sparsity Patterns

Beyond overall sparsification ratios, it is also important to assess how well different schemes align with the problem-driven sparse structure. We compute the overlap between the zero positions introduced by ternary quantization and those predefined by the physics of the problem. The results show an overlap of
48\%
on the large dataset and
63\%
on the BSD500 dataset. These values indicate that while both methods induce sparsity, a significant portion of the zeros selected by ternary do not coincide with the problem’s inherent structure. For the large model, ternary zeros overlap with PIBiNN’s physics-driven zeros by only
48\%
, indicating that ternary often removes different (and potentially essential) couplings. In contrast, for BSD500 the overlap is
63\%
, suggesting ternary partially aligns with the physics structure in this setting. These results highlight that PIBiNN achieves systematic, problem-consistent sparsity, whereas ternary’s sparsity pattern is dataset-dependent and less predictable.

Appendix J
Limitations and Future Work

A key limitation of our current study lies in latency. While the proposed physics-inspired binarization achieves strong compression and accuracy trade-offs, our implementation relies on CPU-based reference kernels for packed inference. As a result, the latency results do not yet reflect the full efficiency potential of the approach. We emphasize, however, that this is primarily a systems-level concern: the core architectural and theoretical contributions of this work remain valid, and practical speedups can be realized by integrating optimized GPU or hardware kernels, which we leave to future systems research.

More broadly, our study is intentionally scoped to inverse problems with well-defined physics, where problem-driven sparsity provides meaningful structure. Extending the framework to more general domains will require additional validation. Likewise, our choice of a single data-driven scale per model emphasizes conceptual clarity and minimal parameter overhead. Future work may explore mixed-precision strategies or hybrid quantization schemes (e.g., combining one-scale and channel-wise quantization depending on layer importance), to balance performance and flexibility.

Overall, while our focus has been on demonstrating architectural and theoretical advantages, complementary efforts in systems optimization and broader application domains present natural next steps.
