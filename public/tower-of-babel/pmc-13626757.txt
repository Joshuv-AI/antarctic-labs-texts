The use of avulsed soft tissue for immediate full-thickness skin grafting in traumatic extremity injuries: A multi-institutional 10-year retrospective review of outcomes with technical considerations
Authors: Devin J. Clegg, Stephanie M. Mueller, Amy Du, LaYow C. Yu, Kyle R. Eberlin, Matthew J. Carty, Simon G. Talbot, Dennis P. Orgill
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAvulsion or degloving injuries of the extremities are often caused by high-energy trauma and can be associated with high morbidity. Multiple techniques have been proposed for management, including immediate full-thickness skin grafting (IFTSG). Despite previous publication of our experience with this technique in 1999, awareness and implementation by surgeons remains poor. Outcomes of patients who underwent IFTSG from traumatically avulsed extremity soft tissues over a 10-year period were reviewed, with technical considerations included.MethodsA retrospective cohort study of patients with degloving/avulsion-type traumatic injuries of the extremities who underwent IFTSG from January 2015 to December 2025 at two academic institutions was performed. Patients without follow-up to complete healing or final outcome were excluded. Descriptive statistical analysis was performed, with case photos and surgical technique description included.ResultsFourteen patients were included. Upper extremity injuries occurred in 86% of patients. Associated fractures occurred in 29% of patients, and 71% had initial exposure of critical underlying structures. Mean IFTSG size was 140 cm2. Mean percentage of initial IFTSG take was 88%. Complete healing was achieved in 100% of patients, with a mean time of 87 days. Two patients required additional skin grafting. No patients underwent flap procedures, placement of skin substitutes, or proximal amputations.ConclusionsIn appropriate patients, IFTSG of avulsed soft tissue in traumatic extremity wounds can reliably achieve complete healing with low complication risk and no additional donor site morbidity. Comparative studies are needed, but these promising findings should increase awareness of this technique for surgeons treating similar wounds.

Introduction

Avulsion or degloving soft tissue injuries are often caused by high-energy trauma and can be associated with high morbidity. These injuries often lead to the delamination of skin and subcutaneous tissue from underlying muscle, connective tissues, or bone, creating a partially attached flap of soft tissue with reduced perfusion and exposure of underlying structures. While the true incidence of these injuries is unknown, they commonly occur in the extremities and are often found in combination with other injuries such as long-bone fractures and damaged blood vessels, muscle, tendons, or nerves.1, 2, 3, 4 The mechanisms of injury vary, and include motor vehicle collisions, machine-related injuries, crush injuries, bite injuries, sharp lacerations, and falls, particularly in the elderly.1, 2, 3, 4 While many of these injuries are considered external degloving or avulsions with resulting open wounds, they can also occur internally.5,6 Shear forces and hematomas can cause the overlying skin and subcutaneous tissues to separate from the underlying structures, leading to poor soft tissue perfusion or necrosis.5,6

Without appropriate treatment, these injuries can lead to complications. These include significant soft tissue loss through secondary skin necrosis, exposure of underlying structures resulting in increased risk of fracture malunion or nonunion, infection, scar contracture deformity and dysfunction, amputation, and even death.2 Many techniques have been proposed for management of these soft tissue injuries, including primary laceration repair with or without removal of subcutaneous tissues,2,7 creation of split-thickness skin grafts from the avulsed skin,2,8,9 cryopreservation of the avulsed tissue for replantation,2,10,11 dermal regeneration template or skin substitute placement,12,13 and the creation of full-thickness skin grafts from the avulsed soft tissue for immediate grafting.1,7,12,14, 15, 16, 17, 18

It has long been recognized by many plastic surgeons that avulsed but partially attached soft tissues that are reapproximated have high risk of necrosis, but if removed, thinned, and replaced as a skin graft, have an increased chance of survival. Immediate full-thickness skin grafting (IFTSG) involves division of the remaining attachments of the avulsed flap and “defatting”, which is the process of removing subcutaneous adipose tissue retained within the avulsion flap. As subcutaneous tissue and fat is poorly vascularized and acts as a physical barrier to imbibition, inosculation, and capillary growth from the recipient site, its removal has been demonstrated to decrease the risk of skin graft necrosis.19, 20, 21 The remaining dermis and epidermis from the injured tissue may then be used as a full-thickness skin graft. This converts the avulsed tissues from a poorly perfused soft tissue flap to a full-thickness skin graft with lower metabolic demand, improved exposure of the dermal vascular plexus for apposition to the vascularized wound bed, and preservation of as much viable tissue surface area as possible. In 1999 we published our report of five patients with nine avulsion injuries describing this approach, which also included the use of negative pressure wound therapy (NPWT) as an adjunct to healing.17

Despite our previous publication and active involvement in trauma care at our institutions, it appears that many surgeons are not aware of this technique, with cases of massive soft tissue defects resulting from not using this readily available donor location. The current literature on IFTSG largely consists of individual case reports or small case series, with some published several years prior to more modern improvements in traumatic injury management and wound healing techniques.1,7,12,14, 15, 16, 17, 18 As such, the purpose of this study was to review the outcomes of patients who underwent IFTSG from traumatically avulsed or degloved soft tissues at two large-volume academic centers over a 10-year period, as well as to provide technical considerations and increase awareness.

Patients and methods

A retrospective cohort study was performed including patients with degloving or avulsion-type traumatic injuries of the extremities who underwent immediate full-thickness skin grafting from January 2015 to December 2025 at two academic institutions. This study was approved by our Institutional Review Board (Protocol number 2025P003110) and performed in adherence with the STROBE guidelines. Patients were identified by ICD codes for open wounds of the upper and lower extremities (ICD 10:S41, 10:S51, 10:S61, 10:S71, 10:S81, 10:S91, including all sub-codes) and CPT codes for full-thickness skin grafts to the upper and lower extremities (CPT 15,240, 15,241, 1520,15,221, including all sub-codes) prior to undergoing individual chart review for inclusion or exclusion. Exclusion criteria consisted of ages <18 years old, delayed full-thickness skin grafting (not at time of index operation), full-thickness skin grafting from a donor site that was not the avulsed soft tissue, incomplete records, and lack of follow-up to complete healing or final outcomes.

Data were obtained by manual chart review of the electronic medical record including demographics, comorbidities, medical/surgical history, traumatic injury etiology and description, operative details, complications, and outcomes during follow-up, including time to complete healing. Body mass index (BMI) was categorized according to World Health Organization criteria as underweight (<18.5 kg/m²), normal weight (18.5–24.9 kg/m²), overweight (25.0–29.9 kg/m²), and obese (≥30.0 kg/m²). Three representative patient cases were included, with written consent obtained.

Statistical analyses included descriptive statistics (count with percentage, and mean with standard deviation and range) performed in Microsoft Excel (Version 2603).

Operative technique

Wound evaluation and preparation

Avulsion or degloving-type wounds are evaluated for concurrent injuries and exposed structures. The avulsed soft tissue flap is evaluated for irreversible ischemia or other abnormalities that may preclude its use as a skin graft. Adjuncts that can be used to assess the viability of the avulsed tissue and the wound bed include intra-operative fluorescein or indocyanine green angiography. Non-viable, overly traumatized, or heavily contaminated tissues are debrided. Concomitant procedures such as the repair of fractures, vasculature, muscle, tendon, or nerve, are performed if required. Exposed structures are covered with adjacent viable and well-vascularized tissue to create a healthy wound bed that is suitable for grafting, specifically when periosteum, paratenon, or perineurium are injured or missing, as is standard in all skin grafting procedures. Hemostasis is then obtained in the wound bed to decrease the risk of post-graft placement hematoma. Once this is achieved, immediate full-thickness skin grafting (IFTSG) utilizing the avulsed soft tissue may be performed. If the wound bed or avulsed soft tissues are not deemed suitable for IFTSG, debridement is completed and temporary wound bed coverage techniques are then applied such as negative pressure wound therapy (NPWT) or simple dressings as alternative reconstructive modalities are planned. While no longer performed routinely at our institutions due to the concern for possible increased risk of infection, the grafts obtained from the avulsed tissue could also be significantly thinned and stored at 4 °C for delayed grafting within one week.

Full-thickness skin graft creation and placement

Fig. 1 demonstrates the steps of IFTSG creation and placement. First, any remaining attachments of the soft tissue avulsed flap divided. Alternatively, the remaining attachments of the skin can be left in place if it does not limit the eventual reach or coverage of the graft. Subcutaneous tissue is then completely removed from the dermal layer of the avulsed flap. Once the full-thickness skin graft has been prepared, it is secured to the underlying wound bed in standard fashion utilizing absorbable or non-absorbable sutures or staples with minimal tension, ensuring that the graft is well-apposed and contoured to the underlying vascularized tissue for maximum coverage. Intermittent fenestrations or pie-crusting can be performed in the IFTSG to allow for fluid egress, and to decrease the risk of graft failure secondary to seroma or hematoma. However, this technique may negatively impact cosmesis if too frequent. An overlying dressing or bolster is then placed to assist with graft apposition, or alternatively NPWT can be utilized. In near-circumferential or circumferential IFTSG, the continuous negative pressure suction can be decreased to -75 mmHg for avoidance of potential distal extremity perfusion compromise.Fig. 1Surgical technique of immediate full-thickness skin grafting. Postoperative care

Postoperative care is similar to that of any full-thickness skin graft: immobilization of extremities to avoid shear forces, pressure avoidance, antibiotics if clinically indicated, and maintenance of a bolster dressing or NPWT for 5–14 days if appropriate by location. After initial dressings or bolster/NPWT removal and evaluation of graft take, daily dressing changes are performed to maintain moisture and protect the wound during continued healing.

Results

Thirteen (93%) patients identified as White. Patient comorbidities included obesity (n = 5, 36%), cardiovascular disease (n = 5, 36%), congestive heart failure (n = 3, 21%), peripheral vascular disease (n = 3, 21%), and immunosuppression (n = 2, 14%). Discussion

Overall, we found that all patients in this study achieved complete healing across a variety of traumatic mechanisms, comorbidities, extremity locations, and IFTSG sizes. The average time to complete healing was 87 days and ranged from 22 days to 170 days. The average IFTSG size was 140 cm2 and ranged from 2 cm2 to 1100 cm2. Complications included partial graft necrosis resulting in debridement occurring in two patients, and infection treated with oral antibiotics in one patient. In total, four patients underwent debridement of the IFTSG including two with partial graft necrosis, and two with incomplete graft take, with an average of one debridement performed amongst those patients. Additional skin grafts were required in two patients, and both were split-thickness skin grafts. No patients had complete IFTSG loss, required secondary reconstruction with flaps or skin substitutes, nor required a proximal amputation. As the IFTSG came from the patient’s own avulsed soft tissue, there was no donor site morbidity.

“Survive as a graft or die as a flap” is an adage that describes the pathophysiology that underlies the reasoning for utilizing this technique. Degloved or avulsed tissues are often perfused from small remaining attachments, and even with seemingly adequate arterial inflow, they can have significantly impaired venous outflow due to the traumatic disruption of the more delicate and thin-walled veins, leading to tissue compromise. Additionally, retained subcutaneous tissues are poorly vascularized, increase metabolic demands, and act as a physical barrier to imbibition, inosculation, and capillary growth from the underlying vascularized tissue bed. Thus, conversion to an IFTSG has been proposed to allow the tissue to overcome these wound healing obstacles.1,14, 15, 16, 17, 18, 19, 20, 21

While little comparative data exists, this technique relies on foundational plastic surgery principles.22 It allows for early wound coverage, avoiding the potential complications of debridement alone without immediate coverage, such as desiccation, wound breakdown, and infection.23 As this technique utilizes “spare parts” that otherwise may not survive or be discarded, there is no additional donor site morbidity.24 If the IFTSG does not completely take or has partial necrosis, this can be debrided and replaced with additional coverage including a split-thickness skin graft at a potentially smaller size than would have been required previously. As seen in our patient cohort, this technique may also obviate the need for more extensive secondary reconstructive procedures such as flaps or skin substitute placement, which can be costly and increase length of stay.25, 26, 27, 28, 29, 30 As grafting is performed during the same operation as debridement and concomitant structure repair, this technique does not require a mandatory second anesthetic or operation as with banking or storage of grafts. In certain patients with isolated injuries, IFTSG may decrease hospital length of stay, as some patients may be candidates for same day postoperative discharge and continued outpatient wound care.

NPWT has been reliably demonstrated to improve the outcomes of skin grafts with improved graft take and decreased complications.17,31,32 While only 21% of our patients received NPWT, this likely reflects the location of the IFTSG recipient sites, many of which were on the digits, an area less amenable to NPWT application. In our experience, NPWT is a safe and reliable alternative to traditional bolster dressings when the recipient site location is appropriate.17 As discussed in the operative technique section, for near-circumferential or circumferential IFTSG, the authors recommend decreasing the continuous negative pressure suction to -75 mmHg for avoidance of potential distal extremity perfusion compromise. This technique was applied in both patients with lower extremity IFTSG recipient sites, as well as two patients with hand or forearm IFTSG recipient sites.

Despite the prior literature demonstrating positive outcomes utilizing this technique, including from our institution in 1999,17 awareness and implementation of IFTSG has seemingly remained poor. In our initial published case series, five patients with nine degloving injuries of the upper and lower extremities treated with IFTSG and NPWT application were described. IFTSG sizes ranged from 40 to 1200 cm2, with a mean graft take of 81% after NPWT removal.17 Over the last 25 years, the authors have found this technique to be reliable, as reflected by the current study’s mean graft take of 88%. As shown by the relatively low number of patients included over our 10-year study period, the utilization of the IFTSG approach has not greatly improved. As initial management of these injuries is often performed by trauma or orthopedic surgeons, increased education and awareness, along with early involvement of plastic surgeons, may help reduce potential unnecessary patient morbidity.

This study has limitations including its retrospective nature, smaller sample size that precludes multivariable statistical analysis, and lack of comparator. Despite these limitations, this cohort is a modern representation of ten years of patients at two metropolitan academic institutions.

Conclusions

This study demonstrates that immediate full-thickness skin grafting of degloved or avulsed soft tissue in traumatic extremity wounds can be utilized to achieve complete healing with few complications and no additional donor site morbidity in appropriate patients. This technique can be applied to both upper and lower extremity traumatic injuries of different etiologies, can be utilized in patients with various comorbidities, and can be used in patients that require concomitant repair of underlying injuries. Early involvement of plastic surgery is recommended. Comparative studies to alternative techniques are needed, but these promising findings should increase awareness of this technique for surgeons treating similar wounds.
