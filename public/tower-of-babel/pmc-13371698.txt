Cytokine gene polymorphisms and susceptibility to chronic hepatitis B virus infection in familial clusters from Iran: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Bahman Aghcheli, Emad Behboudi, Malihe Naderi, Seyed Masoud Hosseini, Mahin Tatari, Iraj Shahramian, Masoud Khoshnia, Sima Besharat, Mehran Bakhtiari, Abdolvahab Moradi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThis study investigated the associations between specific polymorphisms in the Interleukin-10 (IL-10), Interleukin-18 (IL-18), Interleukin-1 beta (IL-1β), and Tumor Necrosis Factor-alpha (TNF-α) cytokine genes and susceptibility to chronic hepatitis B infection (CHB) in Iranian families with a history of the disease.MethodsThis cross-sectional study involved 90 CHB patients, 18 individuals with Recovered hepatitis B virus (HBV) infection, and 30 healthy intrafamilial controls matched for age and sex. Genotyping of the IL-10 (−1082 G > A), IL-18 (−137 G > C), IL-1β (−511 C > T), and TNF-α (−308 G > A) polymorphisms was conducted via polymerase chain reaction with sequence-specific primers (PCR-SSP). Associations with CHB susceptibility were assessed via multivariate logistic regression adjusted for age and sex across multiple genetic models. To address potential non-independence of observations from the same family, we conducted a sensitivity analysis using a generalized estimating equation (GEE) approach with an exchangeable correlation structure, clustering by family.ResultsA significant association was identified between the IL-10–1082 AA genotype and CHB. This genotype was present in 84.4% (76/90) of the CHB patients (p < 0.0001), suggesting an increased likelihood of chronic HBV infection. This association remained in the GEE sensitivity analysis (p < 0.001). For the IL-18–137 G > C single nucleotide polymorphism (SNP), the heterozygous GC genotype was associated with a significantly greater risk of chronicity (p = 0.001). The significance of this finding was retained in the GEE model (p = 0.001). The IL-1β-511 CC genotype was less common in CHB patients (6.7%) than in controls (16.7%), suggesting a potential protective effect. No significant association was found for the TNF-α −308 G > A polymorphism.ConclusionsOur findings indicate that genetic variations in IL-10 (−1082 G > A) and IL-18 (−137 G > C) are significant predictors of susceptibility to chronic HBV infection in these familial Iranian clusters. These observational findings suggest a possible role for cytokine gene polymorphisms in the persistence of HBV infection and underscore the need for further studies to explore their utility in genetic screening for high-risk families.

Introduction

Hepatitis B virus is a partially double-stranded DNA virus that can infect the liver. While many adults clear the infection, a significant proportion develop chronic hepatitis B, which can progress to cirrhosis and hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) [1]. In Iran, the estimated prevalence of chronic HBV infection is 2.2%, which poses a significant public health burden [2]. The outcome of HBV infection is influenced by a complex interplay between the virus and the host immune response. The cell-mediated immune response, particularly the activity of cytokines, is crucial for HBV clearance [3].

Host genetic makeup is a key determinant of the immune response specifically to HBV; additionally, HBV genetic diversity and mutations are well-documented factors influencing infection outcomes [4, 5]. Furthermore, SNPs in regulatory regions of cytokine genes can alter their expression levels, thereby influencing the intensity and balance of the host immune response to HBV [6]. To make the selection criteria accessible to all readers, we note that numerous cytokines are involved in HBV immunity; however, the selection of cytokines for this study (IL-10, IL-18, IL-1β, and TNF-α) was guided by their non-redundant and pivotal roles in coordinating the immune response to HBV infection. IL-10 functions as a potent immunosuppressive agent, modulating immunity to avert immunopathology; however, its activity may concurrently dampen essential antiviral mechanisms, thereby potentially fostering HBV persistence. Conversely, IL-18 and IL-1β are prototypical proinflammatory mediators responsible for activating innate immunity, processes indispensable for initial HBV containment. TNF-α operates as a master regulator of inflammatory signaling and also possesses intrinsic activity against HBV [7, 8]. We propose that functional polymorphisms within the promoter regions of the genes encoding these molecules can skew their expression profiles. Such dysregulation may establish a predisposition to chronic infection, wherein high-expression IL-10 genotypes could engender an immunosuppressive microenvironment, whereas low-expression genotypes of proinflammatory agents like IL-18 may result in inadequate HBV clearance.

Studies have investigated SNPs in these cytokine genes as potential biomarkers for HBV persistence [9, 10]. However, findings often vary across different populations, highlighting the importance of ethnic-specific genetic studies [2, 11].

This study aimed to investigate the associations between functional promoter polymorphisms in the IL-10, IL-18, IL-1β, and TNF-α genes and susceptibility to chronic HBV infection in multigenerational Iranian families. Studying families with clustered chronic HBV infections provides a unique opportunity to control for shared environmental and genetic backgrounds. The multigenerational nature of these families allows us to sample a wider spectrum of genetic diversity within a shared familial background and may help capture age-related effects or historical HBV exposure patterns.

Materials and Methods

Study population and design

This cross-sectional study analyzed data from participants from Golestan University of Medical Sciences Data Bank. We recruited participants from families with a history of chronic HBV infection in Golestan Province, Iran. Sample collection took place between September 2019 and January 2021, at the Research Center of Gastroenterology and Hepatology, Golestan University of Medical Sciences. Figure 1 illustrates the flow of participants from initial assessment to final inclusion. The patient group included individuals who were diagnosed with CHB. The inclusion criterion required a diagnosis of CHB, defined as hepatitis B surface antigen (HBsAg) positivity for more than six months, confirmed by serological testing. The exclusion criterion was the presence of coinfections with hepatitis C virus (HCV), hepatitis D virus (HDV), or human immunodeficiency virus (HIV). The recovered group consisted of individuals characterized by serological evidence of resolved HBV infection (antibody to hepatitis B core antigen, anti-HBc positive, HBsAg negative, antibody to hepatitis B surface antigen, anti-HBs positive) and no history of CHB. The control group comprised healthy intrafamilial subjects. The control group refers exclusively to this healthy intrafamilial group and does not include the resolved HBV group, which is treated as a separate comparator to distinguish factors associated with spontaneous clearance The cohort assembly process began with identifying multi-generational family clusters (defined as families spanning at least two generations with at least one documented HBV case) from the Golestan University of Medical Sciences Data Bank, which includes records from routine health screenings, hospital admissions, and community surveillance programs in the region. A total of 250 families were initially screened based on historical HBV records; from these, 45 families were selected where multiple members consented to participation and met preliminary eligibility (e.g., no recent vaccinations or unrelated comorbidities). Consenting family members were invited via phone or in-person outreach and assembled for on-site assessment, including phlebotomy (5 mL serum for viral markers and 5 mL whole blood for cytokine SNP study). The process for control selection was as follows: within each identified multi-generational family cluster, all available members were assessed for eligibility. This involved serological testing (commercial ELISA kits for HBsAg, anti-HBc, anti-HBs, and anti-HBe) and clinical evaluation (liver function tests and medical history) to categorize into groups: CHB patients (N = 90; subdivided into Group 1 [three generations, N = 30: 10 grandmothers, 10 mothers, 10 offspring], Group 2 [two generations, N = 40: 20 mothers, 20 children], Group 3 [interfamilial members, N = 20]), recovered (Group 4, N = 18), and healthy controls (Group 5). From this pool, we identified healthy, unvaccinated individuals who were seronegative for all HBV markers (HBsAg, anti-HBc, and anti-HBe) and had no clinical evidence of liver disease. The final control group was then formed by selecting from these eligible intrafamilial individuals to match the CHB group for age (within ±5 years) and sex (using a 1:1 ratio where feasible, prioritizing closest matches to ensure balance across the cohort; matching performed manually by the research team).Fig. 1Study flowchart and laboratory workflow. The participants in this group were required to be negative for HBsAg, anti-HBc, and antibody to hepatitis B e antigen (anti-HBe); have no history of HBV vaccination or clinical evidence of liver disease; and were matched to the CHB group for age and sex. Those diagnosed with any immune-related or chronic liver diseases were excluded. All study subjects were from multigenerational families with a history of HBV infection in Golestan Province, Iran, to control for shared genetic and environmental backgrounds. The exclusion of vaccinated individuals was a deliberate design choice to control for vaccine-induced immunity, thereby minimizing a major environmental confounder and allowing a more precise investigation of the natural host genetic determinants of HBV susceptibility within these familial clusters.

We acknowledge that this may introduce a selection bias, as unvaccinated individuals in Iran might share specific socioeconomic characteristics. Similarly, by recruiting from families with a known history of HBV, we may be oversampling families with shared risk factors or historical exposure events. These considerations should be noted when generalizing the findings.

Each participant was informed about the study’s purpose, provided written informed consent, and completed a demographic questionnaire. This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Golestan University of Medical Sciences (IR.GOUMS.REC0.1399.105). Cytokine SNPs were evaluated via PCR-SSP at positions −1082, −511, −308, and −137 in the promoter regions of the IL-10, IL-1β, TNF-α, and IL-18 genes, respectively. PCR amplification was carried out in a final volume of 20 μL, containing 10 μL of Taq 2X Master Mix (Ampliqon, Denmark), 1 μL of each primer pair (0.5 μM final concentration), 6 μL of nuclease-free water, and 100 ng of genomic DNA. To verify successful amplification, a primer pair for the glyceraldehyde 3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH) housekeeping gene was included as an internal control in each reaction mixture. For each DNA sample, two separate reaction tubes were prepared. Amplification was performed in a PeqLab thermal cycler (Peqlab Biotechnology GmbH, Germany) under cycling conditions as previously described [12–15]. Statistical analysis

Primary association analyses used unconditional logistic regression adjusted for age and sex. Because participants were clustered within families, the independence assumption is violated. We therefore performed two robustness checks: (i) GEE with exchangeable correlation structure, clustering by family ID (primary sensitivity model). (ii) Firth’s penalized-likelihood logistic regression to resolve complete separation for IL-10 -1082 AA (zero controls). Multiple testing across four SNPs was corrected by Bonferroni (α = 0.05/4 = 0.0125). Inheritance models were compared by AIC (Akaike’s Information Criterion) [16]; the model with the lowest AIC is reported. All analyses were performed in R 4.4.1 (packages geepack, logistf, SNPStats).”

Association analysis under genetic models

Logistic regression analysis using Firth’s penalized-likelihood method, adjusted for age and sex, was performed to address the issue of complete separation in the data. This analysis confirmed significant associations for IL-10 and IL-18 polymorphisms (Table 5). For the IL-10 -1082 G > A polymorphism, the dominant model revealed that carrying at least one A allele (GA or AA) was associated with 12.45-fold increased odds of susceptibility to chronicity of hepatitis B infection compared with the GG genotype (p < 0.001). In the codominant model, the GA genotype was associated with an OR of 15.34 (p = 0.002); a reliable OR for the AA genotype could not be calculated as it was absent in controls. The IL-10 -1082 G > A polymorphism demonstrated a significant association with CHB. Under the dominant model, carrying at least one A allele (GA or AA) was associated with 12.45-fold increased odds of susceptibility to chronic HBV infection compared with the GG genotype (p < 0.001). For the IL-18 -137 G > C polymorphism, the overdominant model was the best fit, indicating that the heterozygous GC genotype was associated with a 10.89-fold greater risk of CHB (p = 0.001) than the combined homozygous genotypes (GG and CC). In contrast, no statistically significant associations were found for the IL-1β −511 C > T and TNF-α −308 G > A polymorphisms and CHB susceptibility after adjustment for covariates.

Haplotype analysis

Linkage disequilibrium analysis did not reveal linkages between the four SNPs. Haplotype analyses did not yield results that were more significant than the single SNP associations.

Discussion

This familial case-control investigation offers preliminary data linking IL-10 and IL-18 promoter variants to CHB susceptibility in a cohort from northeastern Iran. The use of intrafamilial controls minimizes confounding from shared genetic and environmental factors. Study limitations include a modest sample size (90 CHB cases, 30 controls, 18 recovered) and complete separation for the IL-10 AA genotype, which complicates precise risk estimation. Despite this, Firth and GEE modeling confirm statistically significant associations (P_Bonf < 0.005), though the reported odds ratios require cautious interpretation. This study provides evidence of associations between cytokine gene polymorphisms and susceptibility to chronic HBV infection in an Iranian familial cohort. The use of intrafamilial controls is a key strength, as it minimizes confounding from unmeasured environmental and genetic factors shared within families. While a familial control group is advantageous for controlling shared backgrounds, the multi-generational aspect (spanning at least two generations, including grandparents, parents, and offspring in some clusters) adds further value by capturing intergenerational HBV transmission patterns, such as vertical spread, which is prevalent in endemic areas like Iran. This design also broadens the genetic diversity sampled within families, enhancing detection of inherited polymorphisms, and allows consideration of age-related effects or historical exposures (e.g., varying vaccination or healthcare practices across generations) that could influence cytokine expression and HBV outcomes.

The IL10-1082 AA genotype was observed in 76/90 (84.4%) CHB patients and 0/30 controls. This perfect separation is driven by the modest control sample (n = 30) rather than the biological impossibility of the genotype in healthy individuals. Firth regression yields a finite OR of 12.45 (95% CI 3.81–40.67), confirming association but not implausible 50–80-fold risks. The GEE sensitivity analysis, which accounts for within-family correlation, retained identical significance (P_GEE < 0.001 for IL-10 dominant; P_GEE = 0.001 for IL-18 overdominant), confirming that clustering does not inflate the associations. Firth penalisation resolved complete separation for IL-10 AA (zero controls) and yielded finite, biologically plausible odds ratios (12.45-fold and 10.89-fold, respectively).

IL-10 is a potent anti-inflammatory cytokine that suppresses T-cell and macrophage activity. We posit that a genetically predetermined high-output IL-10 response in A allele carriers creates an immunosuppressive hepatic microenvironment that blunts the cell-mediated immune response necessary for viral clearance, thereby facilitating HBV persistence. This hypothesis is further supported by the genotypic distribution in our recovered group, where the intermediate-producing GA heterozygote was predominant (66.7%), potentially representing an immunological “sweet spot” that allows for effective HBV clearance without excessive immunopathological damage.

Our findings on IL-10 stand in stark contrast to those of several earlier Iranian studies. Sofian et al. (2013) and Moudi et al. (2016) reported no significant correlation between IL-10–1082 promoter polymorphisms and the outcome of HBV infection in their case‒control studies [17, 18]. This discrepancy likely underscores the critical importance of study design. While those studies recruited unrelated individuals from the general population, our focus on familial clusters, enriched for genetic factors and controlling for the common environment, may have revealed a significant genetic effect that might be diluted in more heterogeneous, sporadic cases. However, the surprising strength of this association warrants further validation in larger familial populations.

Conversely, our results align with the broader mechanistic understanding of the role of IL-10 in HBV persistence and support studies from other ethnicities [19]. For example, a retrospective cohort study by Wang et al. indicated that the IL-10 -1082 A allele was associated with an increased susceptibility to HBV infection in Asian populations, and Rybicka et al. demonstrated that IL-10 genotypes influencing high production were linked to HBV progression in European populations [20, 21].

The association between the IL-18 -137 G > C polymorphism and disease chronicity demonstrated a complex, nonadditive inheritance pattern. Model selection, guided by the AIC, determined that the overdominant model was the most parsimonious. Under this model, the heterozygous GC genotype was associated with a substantially increased susceptibility to chronic HBV infection (adjusted OR = 10.89, p = 0.001) relative to the combined homozygous genotypes (GG and CC). This statistically novel observation suggests that the underlying risk mechanism is not a simple function of allele dosage but may be attributable to a unique biological phenomenon intrinsic to the heterozygous state. This heterozygous effect may lead to an imbalanced and ineffective immune response that neither adequately clears the Hepatitis B infection nor properly resolves inflammation. This finding highlights the critical importance of testing multiple genetic models beyond the standard codominant approach, which might have otherwise missed this nuanced effect. Our results partially conflict with those of some studies that reported associations with homozygous variants but underscore the population-specific complexity of cytokine gene regulation [22–24].

For IL-1β-511, we observed a trend where the CC genotype was less frequent in CHB patients (6.7%) than in controls (16.7%), suggesting a potential protective role. Although this association did not reach statistical significance in our final multivariate models, the direction of the effect is consistent with findings from other Asian populations. This aligns with findings from other Asian populations. For example, studies in Chinese and Caucasian patients reported that IL-1β was associated with a higher risk of HBV-related liver cirrhosis [25, 26]. This consistency across distinct Asian populations strengthens the evidence for the involvement of IL-1β genetics in HBV pathogenesis.

Finally, the lack of a significant association for the TNF-α −308 G > A polymorphism in these studies is consistent with other reports from Iran [27, 28]. This null finding could be attributed to the very low frequency of the minor A allele in our population, limiting statistical power, or it may indicate that this particular SNP plays a minimal role in determining HBV outcome compared with the other cytokines studied, especially within the context of familial clustering.

Genetic markers have been identified in cancers such as colorectal and breast cancer, among others. Identifying biomarkers in high-risk patients is essential for enhancing their medical management. Research into cytokine polymorphisms can offer valuable insights for predicting disease severity and clinical phenotypes [29].

This study investigates cytokine gene polymorphisms, yet it acknowledges that cytokine production is a downstream consequence of the innate immune system’s initial detection of HBV. Specific Toll-like Receptors (TLRs), namely TLR2, TLR3, and TLR4, on resident immune cells like Kupffer cells bind to HBV antigens. This binding initiates key intracellular signaling pathways, including NF-κB and IRF, which directly activate the transcription of the cytokines central to this research: IL-10, IL-18, IL-1β, and TNF-α. We therefore propose that natural genetic variants in the genes governing these TLRs or their adaptor proteins (MyD88, TRIF) could significantly alter the strength and quality of this upstream immune trigger. Such alterations in the early antiviral response may subsequently bias an individual’s clinical outcome, steering it toward either HBV clearance or the establishment of chronic infection and its associated complication, hepatocellular carcinoma. For these reasons, the genetic components of the TLR signaling pathway are a rational and high-priority target for future study in familial HBV clusters [30–32].

While our study provides insights into genetic associations in an understudied population, several limitations must be acknowledged. First, the small sample size (N = 138 total, with only 18 in the resolved group) limits statistical power and increases the risk of type II errors, particularly for less common genotypes. This may explain the lack of significant findings for TNF-α −308, despite prior literature suggesting relevance. Second, as a single-site study in Golestan Province, Iran, the findings may not generalize to other ethnic or geographic groups, potentially influenced by regional HBV genotypes or environmental factors unique to this area. Third, the cohort assembly from families with known HBV history introduces selection bias, as we may have oversampled high-risk clusters, and the exclusion of vaccinated individuals could skew toward unvaccinated socioeconomic subgroups. Fourth, this is an observational cross-sectional design, so we report associations only and cannot infer causality; functional assays (e.g., cytokine expression levels) were not performed to validate the polymorphisms’ biological impact. Fifth, data availability does not meet community standards for full validation, as raw genetic sequences are not publicly deposited due to privacy concerns in familial data; however, summary statistics are provided in tables. Finally, unmeasured confounders like viral load, HBV genotype (genotype D), or comorbidities could influence results. Future studies should address these by using larger, multi-site cohorts, longitudinal designs, and integrated functional genomics to strengthen evidence.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study provides preliminary evidence that host genetics may be involved in defining the clinical trajectory of chronic HBV infection. Our key finding within a familial Iranian population is the association of the IL-10‒1082 A allele with susceptibility to chronicity, a relationship indicated by its exclusive presence in affected individuals and a greater than 12-fold increased risk of chronic HBV infection after statistical correction. These results suggest a potential clinical application for genetic screening to enable targeted monitoring strategies within high-risk families. Importantly, the generalizability of these associations may be confined to populations with comparable ethnic and familial characteristics. Further validation in large-scale, multiethnic populations and concomitant functional analyses are needed to confirm these relationships definitively and elucidate the underlying immunologic pathways. Overall, our findings associated with to initial insights into the genetic basis of HBV persistence, particularly in understudied regions like Iran, but must be interpreted cautiously given the limitations outlined.
