Nonlinear interpolation and the flow map of quasilinear equations
Thomas Alazard, Nicolas Burq, Mihaela Ifrim, Daniel Tataru, Claude Zuily

Abstract
. We prove an interpolation theorem for nonlinear functionals defined on scales of Banach spaces that generalize Besov spaces. It applies to functionals defined only locally, requiring only some weak Lipschitz conditions, extending those introduced by Lions and Peetre. Our analysis is self-contained and independent of any previous results about interpolation theory. It depends solely on the concepts of Friedrichs’ mollifiers, seen through the formalism introduced by Hamilton, combined with the frequency envelopes introduced by Tao and used recently by two of the authors and others to study the Cauchy problem for various quasilinear evolutions in partial differential equations. Inspired by this latter work, our main application states that, for an abstract flow map of a quasilinear problem, both the continuity of the flow as a function of time and the continuity of the data-to-solution map follow automatically from the estimates that are usually proven when establishing the existence of solutions: propagation of regularity via tame a priori estimates for higher regularities and contraction for weaker norms.



1.
Introduction

The theory of interpolation originated from the study of Lebesgue spaces, with the Riesz-Thorin
[
32
]
and Marcinkiewicz
[
23
]

theorems, or Sobolev spaces with the study of traces of functions, pioneered by Lions
[
20
]
. An important generalization is the scale of Besov spaces
B^{s}_{p,q}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
,
which serves as the primary example studied in the classical books by Bergh and Löfström
[
7
]

and Peetre
[
29
]
. The extension of interpolation theory to a nonlinear setting goes back to the
’70
, with works of Lions
[
21
]
, Peetre
[
28
]
, Tartar
[
41
]
,
Saut
[
33
]
,
Bona and Scott
[
8
]
, and Maligranda
[
22
]
. However, it is fair to say that it has been
relatively underexplored,
particularly concerning applications that involve nonlinearities significant enough to be relevant for studying
solutions to quasilinear partial differential equations.
We will take advantage of this opportunity to introduce a new variation of the classical interpolation techniques,
which enables us to derive automatic continuity results for
nonlinear functions defined on generalized Besov spaces. Our analysis relies solely on the concepts of Friedrichs’ mollifiers, seen through the formalism introduced by
Hamilton
[
14
]
, combined with a simplified version of the notion of frequency envelopes introduced by Tao
[
39
]
for studying dispersive equations.
In fact, our approach is strongly inspired by a series of recent papers
[
17
,
16
,
18
,
15
,
31
,
1
]

where the concept of frequency envelopes is used to offer an alternative perspective on the study of the Cauchy problem for quasilinear
partial differential

equations of different types, and in particular to prove continuous dependence of the solution as a function of the initial data.

Our main result is given in the next section (see Theorem
18
).
We begin by stating its main corollary.
The latter is in the form of a general result which guarantees that the flow map of an abstract quasilinear equation exists
and is continuous. More precisely, the next result asserts that the continuity of an abstract
flow follows from the two estimates that are usually proven when establishing the existence of solutions to a quasilinear
equation: (i) tame
a priori
estimate in
a stronger
norm and (ii)
Lipschitz bounds
in a weaker norm.
Here, using the terminology of Hamilton
[
14
]
, we say that an estimate is

tame
provided it depends linearly on the highest norm. We believe that this abstract result could be readily
used to avoid
some
ad-hoc
proofs that are somewhat subtle and technical, particularly those concerning the continuous
dependence on initial data and the continuity in time of the solutions of quasilinear dispersive equations.

Theorem 1

(Automatic continuity of the flow map)
.

Consider
\mu\in[2,+\infty]
and four real numbers
s_{0},s,s_{1}
and
r
such that

s_{0}<s<s_{1},\quad\quad r>0.

Consider
u_{0}
in the Sobolev space
H^{s}=H^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
and a
(possibly nonlinear)
function defined on the ball of radius
r
around
u_{0}
:

\Phi\colon B_{s}(u_{0},r)\mapsto L^{\mu}((0,T);H^{s_{0}}),

where
B_{s}(u_{0},r)=\{v_{0}\in H^{s};\|v_{0}-u_{0}\|_{H^{s}}<r\}
, and satisfying the two following properties:

(H1)

Weak Lipschitz
: there exists
C_{0}>0
such that

(1.1)

\forall v_{0},w_{0}\in B_{s}(u_{0},r),\quad\left\lVert\Phi(v_{0})-\Phi(w_{0})\right\rVert_{L^{\mu}((0,T);H^{s_{0}})}\leq C_{0}\left\lVert v_{0}-w_{0}\right\rVert_{H^{s_{0}}}.

(H2)

Tame estimate
: there exists
C_{1}>0
such that (with
H^{+\infty}=\cap_{s}H^{s}
),

(1.2)

\forall v_{0}\in H^{+\infty}\cap B_{s}(u_{0},r),\quad\left\lVert\Phi(v_{0})\right\rVert_{L^{\mu}((0,T);H^{s_{1}})}\leq C_{1}\left\lVert v_{0}\right\rVert_{H^{s_{1}}}.

Then we have the three following conclusions:

i)

Continuous interpolation
: for all
v_{0}\in B_{s}(u_{0},r)
, we have

\Phi(v_{0})\in L^{\mu}((0,T);H^{s})

and the map

\Phi:v_{0}\in B_{s}(u_{0},r)\mapsto\Phi(v_{0})\in L^{\mu}((0,T);H^{s})
is continuous.

ii)

Improved regularity in time
:
for all
v_{0}\in B_{s}(u_{0},r)
,
\Phi(v_{0})
automatically belongs to a Chemin-Lerner space, which means that:

\sum_{j\geq 0}\left\lVert\Delta_{j}\Phi(v_{0})\right\rVert_{L^{\mu}((0,T);H^{s})}^{2}<+\infty,

where
u=\sum_{j\geq 0}\Delta_{j}u
denotes the Littlewood-Paley decomposition
of
u
(see Appendix
A
).

iii)

Continuity in time:
Assume that
\mu=+\infty
and
suppose in addition that
for some
v_{0}\in B_{s}(u_{0},r)
, we have

(1.3)

\Phi(v_{0})\in C^{0}([0,T];H^{-\infty}).

Then we also have

(1.4)

\Phi(v_{0})\in C^{0}([0,T];H^{s}).

Remark 2
.

To avoid possible confusion in notations, let us emphasize that a key point is that
the constants
C_{0}
and
C_{1}
may depend on the radius
r
and
u_{0}
.
In particular, the estimate (
1.1
) holds provided that there exists a non-decreasing function
\mathcal{F}_{0}\colon\mathbb{R}_{+}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
such that, for all
v_{0},w_{0}
in
H^{+\infty}\cap B_{s}(u_{0},r)
,

\left\lVert\Phi(v_{0})-\Phi(w_{0})\right\rVert_{L^{\mu}((0,T);H^{s_{0}})}\leq\mathcal{F}_{0}\big(\left\lVert v_{0}\right\rVert_{H^{s}}+\left\lVert w_{0}\right\rVert_{H^{s}}\big)\left\lVert v_{0}-w_{0}\right\rVert_{H^{s_{0}}}.

Similarly, the estimate (
1.2
) holds provided that there exists a non-decreasing function
\mathcal{F}_{1}\colon\mathbb{R}_{+}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
such that, for all
v_{0}
in
H^{+\infty}\cap B_{s}(u_{0},r)
,

\left\lVert\Phi(v_{0})\right\rVert_{L^{\mu}((0,T);H^{s_{1}})}\leq\mathcal{F}_{1}\big(\left\lVert v_{0}\right\rVert_{H^{s}}\big)\left\lVert v_{0}\right\rVert_{H^{s_{1}}}.

Remark 3
.

If one knows that
\Phi
is continuous from
B_{s}(u_{0},r)
to

L^{\mu}((0,T);H^{s_{0}})
, then it is enough to assume that the

weak Lipschitz
Assumption
1
holds for smooth initial data, namely that there exists
C_{0}>0
such that

(1.5)

\forall v_{0},w_{0}\in H^{+\infty}\cap B_{s}(u_{0},r),\quad\left\lVert\Phi(v_{0})-\Phi(w_{0})\right\rVert_{L^{\mu}((0,T);H^{s_{0}})}\leq C_{0}\left\lVert v_{0}-w_{0}\right\rVert_{H^{s_{0}}}.

Remark 4
.

We shall prove a stronger result, which allows for instance to work in Besov spaces,
and also to consider periodic functions or functions defined on manifolds, as well as other variants.

Remark 5
.

This extends previous results (see in particular Saut
[
33
]
and Bona and Scott
[
8
]
)
in several directions. The key point is that we only assume that
\Phi
is defined locally.
The idea is that this setting allows us to consider the case where
both the time of existence
T
and the constants
C_{0},C_{1}
depend on
the
H^{s}
size of the solutions. This is exactly the situation for the flow map of
a quasilinear equation. Previous results made extra assumptions
and also assumed that the flow was defined globally and that the
constants
C_{0}
and
C_{1}
were uniform or depended on norms in a way that,
in practical terms, required
\Phi
to be the flow
of a semilinear evolution equation (see Remark
19
for more details).
By contrast, the assumptions in Theorem
1
readily
apply to quasilinear systems of evolution equations
that can be solved by classical iterative schemes:
the main example is the case of quasilinear systems
of hyperbolic equations, but one can also consider quasilinear Schrödinger
flows or water-wave-type systems, for which it is not at all trivial
to prove continuous dependence with respect to the initial data.
The second part of our conclusion was classically proved
by repeating the Sobolev energy estimate (see for instance the proof of Lemma
4.14
in
[
5
]
),
and is quite cumbersome in practice. The above result asserts
that this holds automatically. To the best of our knowledge,
for instance in water-wave problems, this property
was only recently proved and only for some very specific models, see e.g.
[
1
]
.

Remark 6
.

As a corollary of our proofs, one may easily extend the conclusion of the theorem to the full range of intermediate spaces
H^{\sigma}
for
s<\sigma<s_{1}
. Precisely, under the same hypothesis, the conclusion of the theorem holds with
H^{s}
replaced by
H^{\sigma}
, for all initial data
u_{0}\in B_{s}(u_{0},r)\cap H^{\sigma}
.

The study of the well-posedness of the Cauchy problem for quasilinear equations began with the analysis of hyperbolic equations, with
the celebrated works by Hadamard
[

]

[

Many qualitative properties have been deduced from the identity of weak and strong extensions of differential operators (see

The second point we want to emphasize is that the proof is significantly influenced by the arguments used in the proof of the so-called Meyer multiplier lemma (see the original article by Meyer

In this context one should also note the option of replacing the classical, discrete Littlewod-Paley decomposition with its continuous version, see

2.
Main result

2.1.
Generalized Besov spaces

Our aim will be to extend
interpolation theory to nonlinear functions acting on scales of spaces that extend beyond Besov spaces (our interest lies more in the convenience of notation rather than strict generalization). To define such generalized Besov scales, three components are required in analogy with Besov spaces: a base space
E
, an index
s\in\mathbb{R}
giving the order of smoothness, and an index
q\in[1,+\infty]
which represents a second-order correction to this order of smoothness. For instance, the Besov space
B^{s}_{p,q}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
corresponds to
E=L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
,
s\in\mathbb{R}
, and
q\in[1,+\infty]
.

We will prove that, for our purpose, one can replace
L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
by any vector space, not necessarily a Banach space,
equipped

with a pseudo-norm defined as follows:

Definition 7
.

Consider a vector space
E
over
\mathbb{K}=\mathbb{R}
or
\mathbb{K}=\mathbb{C}
, and a
functional

N\colon E\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
.
We say that
N
is symmetric, subadditive and point-separating if it satisfies the three following properties:

(1)

for all
x\in E
,
N(-x)=N(x)
,

(2)

N(x+y)\leq N(x)+N(y)
, for all
(x,y)\in E\times E
,

(3)

N(x)=0
if and only if
x=0
.

Hereafter, we shall say that such an application
N
is a pseudo-norm and that
(E,N)
is a pseudo-normed vector space. We shall denote pseudo-norms
as usual norms by
\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert_{E}
.

Several explanations are in order concerning the above definition.

Remark 8
.

(i)
Consider a pseudo-normed vector space
(E,\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert_{E})
.
Then, the
functional
d(x,y)=\left\lVert x-y\right\rVert_{E}
on
E\times E
is a distance on
E
.
In the following, it is always implicitly assumed that

E
is endowed with the topology induced by this distance.

(ii)
Every norm is a pseudo-norm.

(iii)
One motivation to consider pseudo-norms instead of norms
comes from the study of local spaces.
As an example, consider the vector space
L^{2}_{\rm loc}(\Omega)

where
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
is an open set.
Denote by
(K_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
an exhaustion by compact sets of
\Omega
. Then

\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{L^{2}_{\rm loc}(\Omega)}=\sum_{n\in\mathbb{N}}2^{-n}\frac{\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{L^{2}(K_{n})}}{1+\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{L^{2}(K_{n})}}

is a pseudo-norm on
L^{2}_{\rm loc}(\Omega)
.

(iv)
One can generalize the previous example to include most of the natural Fréchet spaces: using the terminology introduced by Hamilton
[
14
]
, any topological vector space
E
equipped with a graded point-separating family of semi-norms
\rho_{1}\leq\rho_{2}\leq\cdots\leq\rho_{n}\leq\cdots
can be seen as a pseudo-vector space for the pseudo-norm

\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{E}=\sum_{n\in\mathbb{N}}2^{-n}\frac{\rho_{n}(f)}{1+\rho_{n}(f)}\cdot

Starting with a pseudo-normed vector space
E
,
the next step is to define the associated spaces of sequences.

Definition 9

(Spaces of exponentially decaying sequences)
.

Consider a pseudo-normed
vector space
(E,\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert_{E})
.

(i)
Given
s\in\mathbb{R}
, we denote by
\Sigma^{s}_{\infty}(E)
the vector space
of those sequences
f=(f_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
with
f_{k}\in E
, such that

(2.1)

\lim_{k\to+\infty}2^{ks}\left\lVert f_{k}\right\rVert_{E}=0.

(ii)
Given
s\in\mathbb{R}
and
q\in[1,+\infty)
,
we denote by
\Sigma^{s}_{q}(E)
the vector space
of those sequences
f=(f_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
with
f_{k}\in E
, such that

(2.2)

\sum_{k\in\mathbb{N}}2^{qks}\left\lVert f_{k}\right\rVert_{E}^{q}<+\infty.

The quantities in (
2.1
), (
2.2
) are akin to superimposing a weighted
\ell^{q}
structure on
the space of
E
valued sequences. For convenience we recall and relate the appropriate
notations.

Notation 10
.

Given
q\in[1,+\infty)
(resp.
q=+\infty
), recall that

\ell^{q}(\mathbb{N})
denotes the space of those sequences
(u_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
such that

\left\lVert u\right\rVert_{\ell^{q}(\mathbb{N})}=\big(\sum_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\left\lvert u_{k}\right\rvert^{q}\big)^{1/q}<+\infty\quad(\text{resp.}\quad\left\lVert u\right\rVert_{\ell^{\infty}(\mathbb{N})}=\sup_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\left\lvert u_{k}\right\rvert<+\infty).

Denote by
c_{0}(\mathbb{N})\subset\ell^{\infty}(\mathbb{N})
the space of
those sequences such that
{\displaystyle\lim_{k\to+\infty}\left\lvert u_{k}\right\rvert=0}
.

Then, given
q\in[1,+\infty)
(resp.
q=+\infty
),

f\in\Sigma^{s}_{q}(E)
if and only if
(2^{ks}\left\lVert f_{k}\right\rVert_{E})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
belongs to
\ell^{q}(\mathbb{N})
(resp.
c_{0}(\mathbb{N})
).

The
\Sigma^{s}_{q}(E)
scale of spaces can be completed with appropriate end-points as follows:

Notation 11
.

Set

\Sigma^{\infty}_{1}(E):=\bigcap_{s\in\mathbb{R}}\Sigma^{s}_{1}(E)\quad;\quad\Sigma^{-\infty}_{\infty}(E):=\bigcup_{s\in\mathbb{R}}\Sigma^{s}_{\infty}(E).

Notice that, for all
s\in\mathbb{R}
and all
q\in[1,+\infty]
, there holds

\Sigma^{\infty}_{1}(E)\subset\Sigma^{s}_{1}(E)\subset\Sigma^{s}_{q}(E)\subset\Sigma^{s}_{\infty}(E)\subset\Sigma^{-\infty}_{\infty}(E).

Topologically, we can think of the
\Sigma^{s}_{q}(E)
spaces as pseudo-normed spaces
as follows:

Proposition 12
.

Let
s\in\mathbb{R}
and
q\in[1,+\infty)
. Then the quantity

\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{\Sigma^{s}_{q}}:=\left(\sum_{k\in\mathbb{N}}2^{qks}\left\lVert f_{k}\right\rVert_{E}^{q}\right)^{\frac{1}{q}}

defines a pseudo-norm on the vector space
\Sigma^{s}_{q}(E)
.
Similarly, the quantity

\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{\Sigma^{s}_{\infty}}:=\sup_{k\in\mathbb{N}}2^{ks}\left\lVert f_{k}\right\rVert_{E},

defines a pseudo-norm on the vector space
\Sigma^{s}_{\infty}(E)
.

Proof.

Let
q\in[1,+\infty]
. It is obvious that
\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert_{\Sigma^{s}_{q}}
is symmetric and point-separating. Now,
consider two sequences
f,g
in
\Sigma^{s}_{q}(E)
. Since

\left\lVert f+g\right\rVert_{\Sigma^{s}_{q}}=\left\lVert 2^{ks}\left\lVert f_{k}+g_{k}\right\rVert_{E}\right\rVert_{\ell^{q}},

we see that the triangle inequality for
\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert_{\Sigma^{s}_{q}}
follows from the triangle inequality for the pseudo-norm
\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert_{E}
and for the classical
norm
\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert_{\ell^{q}(\mathbb{N})}
.
∎

Remark 13
.

If
\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert_{E}
is a norm, then
\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert_{\Sigma^{s}_{q}}
is also a norm
for all values of the parameters
s\in\mathbb{R}
and
q\in[1,+\infty]
.

The elements of the
\Sigma^{s}_{q}(E)
will play a role which is similar to the sequence of terms
in the Littlewood-Paley decomposition of functions
in Besov spaces. In the broader context here, we
define generalized Besov scales of spaces associated to the base space
E
as follows:

Definition 14

(Generalized Besov scale)
.

Let
a\in\mathbb{R}
,
q\in[1,+\infty]
and
consider a pseudo-normed vector space
E
.
Let
(X^{s},\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert_{X^{s}})_{s\geq a}
be
a decreasing family of
pseudo-normed
vector
spaces, that is a family satisfying

s^{\prime}\geq s\geq a\quad\Rightarrow\quad X^{s^{\prime}}\subset X^{s}\quad\text{with}\quad\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert_{X^{s}}\leq\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert_{X^{s^{\prime}}}.

We say that it is a scale
of pseudo-normed vector spaces of type
(q,E)

provided that there
exist two linear mappings:

L\colon X^{a}\to\Sigma^{a}_{q}(E)\quad,\quad R\colon\Sigma^{a}_{q}(E)\to X^{a}

such that the three following properties hold:

(1)

R(Lu)=u
for all
u\in X^{a}
,

(2)

for all
s\geq a
and all
f\in\Sigma^{s}_{1}(E)
, there holds

L(Rf)\in\Sigma^{s}_{1}(E)
together with the estimate

(2.3)

\left\lVert L(Rf)\right\rVert_{\Sigma^{s}_{1}}\leq C(s)\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{\Sigma^{s}_{1}},

(3)

L(X^{s})\subset\Sigma^{s}_{q}(E)
and
R(\Sigma^{s}_{q}(E))\subset X^{s}
,
for all
s\geq a
.
Moreover, there exists a constant
C(s)>0
such that

(2.4)

\displaystyle\left\lVert Lu\right\rVert_{\Sigma^{s}_{q}}\leq C(s)\left\lVert u\right\rVert_{X^{s}},\quad\forall u\in X^{s},

\displaystyle\left\lVert Rf\right\rVert_{X^{s}}\leq C(s)\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{\Sigma^{s}_{q}},\quad\forall f\in\Sigma^{s}_{q}(E).

Notation 15
.

Consider a scale
(X^{s},\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert_{X^{s}})_{s\geq a}

of type
(q,E)
. Thanks to the correspondence with the space of sequences
\Sigma^{s}_{q}(E)
, we can define other functionals
on
X^{s}
. Namely, we set

\left\lVert u\right\rVert_{X^{s}_{1}}:=\left\lVert Lu\right\rVert_{\Sigma^{s}_{1}}\in[0,+\infty],\quad\left\lVert u\right\rVert_{X^{s}_{\infty}}:=\left\lVert Lu\right\rVert_{\Sigma^{s}_{\infty}}\in[0,+\infty).

We also introduce the subspaces

X^{s}_{q}:=R(\Sigma^{s}_{q}),\quad s\in(a,+\infty],\quad q\in[1,+\infty].

Remark 16
.

i)
Notice that we assume that
R\circ L=\text{Id}
but we
do not assume that
L\circ R=\text{Id}
. We only assume a weaker version of the latter property (see (
2.3
));
this is important for applications.

ii)
Notice that
\left\lVert u\right\rVert_{X^{s}_{1}}
might be infinite for some
u\in X^{s}
. However, for any
q\in[1,+\infty]
, we have
\left\lVert u\right\rVert_{X^{s}_{1}}<+\infty
for any

u\in X^{s^{\prime}}
for some
s^{\prime}>s
. Indeed,

Lu\in\Sigma^{s^{\prime}}_{q}(E)\subset\Sigma^{s}_{1}(E)
.

iii)
Since our main theorem applies for nonlinear functionals, we could as well assume that the operators
L
and
R
are nonlinear.
However, in order to keep the exposition streamlined, we refrain from doing so.

We now describe several interesting examples
of generalized Besov scales:

Example 17
.

(1)

The Sobolev spaces
X^{s}=H^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
with
s\geq s_{0}
(where
s_{0}
is any given real number)
and more generally the
Besov spaces
B^{s}_{p,q}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
with
p\in[1,+\infty)
and
q\in[1,+\infty]
are of type
(q,L^{p}(E))
. In this case the operators
L
and
R
are given by the Littlewood-Paley
decomposition (see Appendix
A
) whose definition is recalled here. Fix a function
\varphi\in C^{\infty}_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{n})

with support in an annulus
\{1/2\leq\left\lvert\xi\right\rvert\leq 2\}
, so that

\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\varphi(2^{-j}\xi)=1-\psi(\xi),\quad\supp\psi\subset\{|\xi|\leq 1\}.

We can then decompose any tempered distribution
u
on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
as
u=\Delta_{0}u+\sum_{j\geq 1}\Delta_{j}u
with

\Delta_{0}u:=\mathcal{F}^{-1}(\psi\mathcal{F}u),\quad\Delta_{j}u:=\mathcal{F}^{-1}(\varphi(2^{-j}\cdot)\mathcal{F}u),\quad j\geq 1,

where
\mathcal{F}\colon\mathcal{S}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\to\mathcal{S}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
denotes the Fourier transform.

Then, for
(s,p,q)\in\mathbb{R}\times[1,+\infty)\times[1,+\infty]
, the Besov norm is defined by

\left\lVert u\right\rVert_{B^{s}_{p,q}}:=\bigg(\sum_{j\geq 0}2^{qjs}\left\lVert\Delta_{j}u\right\rVert_{L^{p}}^{q}\bigg)^{\frac{1}{q}}.

Then
(B^{s}_{p,q})_{s\in\mathbb{R}}
is a scale of type
(q,L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{d}))
provided that
the operator
L
and
R
are defined as follows.
Firstly, one sets
L\colon u\mapsto(f_{j})_{j\geq 0}
with
f_{j}=\Delta_{j}u
.
To define
R
, we consider an auxiliary function
\widetilde{\varphi}\in C^{\infty}_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\{0\})
such that

\widetilde{\varphi}(\xi)\varphi(\xi)=\varphi(\xi)
, and then we define the operator
R
by

R((f_{j})_{j\geq 0})=f_{0}+\sum_{j\geq 0}\widetilde{\varphi}(2^{-j}D_{x})f_{j}.

Notice that
R
is well-defined
by the convergence in norm
and that
R(Lu)=u
.
We do not have
L(Rf)=f
for a given sequence
f=(f_{j})_{j\geq 0}
. However, it is easily verified that

\left\lVert L(Rf)\right\rVert_{\Sigma^{\sigma}_{r}}\leq C(\sigma,r)\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{\Sigma^{\sigma}_{r}}

for all
\sigma\in\mathbb{R}
and all
r\in[1,+\infty]
.

(2)

Consider the scale of Zygmund spaces, which plays a key role in applications since it extends Hölder spaces. Recall that they are the Besov spaces
C^{s}_{*}(\mathbb{R}^{d})=B^{s}_{\infty,\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
where
\left\lVert u\right\rVert_{B^{s}_{\infty,\infty}}=\sup_{j\geq 0}2^{js}\left\lVert\Delta_{j}u\right\rVert_{L^{\infty}}
. This scale
does not enter into the previous framework since the condition (
2.1
) would not be satisfied. Instead, one can consider the so-called little Hölder spaces, that is the closure of
C^{\infty}_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
in
C^{s}_{*}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
.

(3)

To prove Theorem
1
, we will use
these spaces with
E=L^{\infty}((0,T);L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d}))
. In this case, we recover the Chemin-Lerner spaces.

(4)

We can consider spaces of periodic functions, and more generally spaces of functions defined on manifolds. One could also consider local spaces, functions defined on open sets with boundaries or analytic functions.

2.2.
Nonlinear interpolation

Now that we have the appropriate notion of generalized Besov scales, we are ready to state our main result.

Theorem 18
.

Let
E,F
be two pseudo-vector spaces and let

a\in\mathbb{R}
and
q\in[1,+\infty]
.
Consider two scales

(X^{s},\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert_{X^{s}})_{s\geq a}

and
(Y^{s},\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert_{Y^{s}})_{s\geq a}
of type
(q,E)
and
(q,F)
, respectively.
Let
s_{0},s,s_{1}
and
r
be real numbers such that

a\leq s_{0}<s<s_{1},\quad\quad r>0.

Consider a mapping
\Phi

defined on a ball of radius
r
around the origin of
X^{s}
with values in
Y^{s_{0}}
:

\Phi\colon B_{s}(0,r)\to Y^{s_{0}},

where
B_{s}(0,r)=\{v\in X^{s};\|v\|_{X^{s}}<r\}
, and satisfying the following two properties:

(1)

Contraction: there exists
C_{0}>0
such that,

(2.5)

\forall v,w\in B_{s}(0,r),\quad\|\Phi(v)-\Phi(w)\|_{Y^{s_{0}}_{\infty}}\leq C_{0}\|v-w\|_{X^{s_{0}}_{1}}.

(2)

Tame estimate: there exists
C_{1}>0
such that,

(2.6)

\forall v\in X^{\infty}_{1}\cap B_{s}(0,r),\quad\|\Phi(v)\|_{Y^{s_{1}}_{\infty}}\leq C_{1}\|v\|_{X^{s_{1}}_{1}}.

Then, we have the following two conclusions:

(1)

Interpolation:
\Phi(v)\in Y^{s}
for all
v\in B_{s}(0,r)
;

(2)

Continuity: the mapping
\Phi\colon B_{s}(0,r)\mapsto Y^{s}
is continuous.

Remark 19
.

Several remarks are in order.

Let us insist on the fact that the function
\Phi
is not assumed to be linear.
Compared to the nonlinear results
by Tartar
[
41
]
, Bona and Scott
[
8
]
or Maligranda
[
22
]
the main differences are the following.

(1)

The assumptions of Theorem
18
are local
(the function
\Phi
is assumed to be defined only on a neighborhood
of the origin). This is essential since many nonlinear functionals are defined only on small balls (such as the flow map for a quasilinear evolution equation: indeed, for evolution equations, the
flow map is defined on the whole space
if and only if
the Cauchy problem is well-posed globally in time). To prove such a local result necessitates a different proof
compared to a corresponding global result.
Indeed, our proof is entirely distinct. It is direct, self-contained, and, in particular, does not rely on any results from the theory of interpolation. Of course, one can deduce a global result
from our local result
.

Also, in the previous papers already mentioned, it is assumed that
\Phi
satisfies the much stronger condition:

\|\Phi(v)-\Phi(w)\|_{Y^{s_{0}}}\leq f(\left\lVert v\right\rVert_{X^{s_{0}}},\left\lVert w\right\rVert_{X^{s_{0}}})\|v-w\|_{X^{s_{0}}}.

However, for applications to quasilinear equations, this assumption is never satisfied. Instead, one proves

\|\Phi(v)-\Phi(w)\|_{Y^{s_{0}}}\leq f(\left\lVert v\right\rVert_{X^{s}},\left\lVert w\right\rVert_{X^{s}})\|v-w\|_{X^{s_{0}}},

for some
s
larger than
s_{0}
. For instance in the case of nonlinear hyperbolic systems one typically chooses
s_{0}=0
for an
L^{2}
-estimate, and
s>d/2+1
so that the
H^{s}
-norm controls some Lipschitz norms of
v
and
w
.

(2)

We
only
assume weak bounds, in the sense that we estimate
\Phi(v)-\Phi(w)
(resp.
\Phi(v)
) in
Y^{s_{0}}_{\infty}
(resp.
Y^{s_{1}}_{\infty}
). This
fact
will be used to prove that
the flow-map in Theorem
1
automatically satisfies Chemin-Lerner estimates.

(3)

We do not assume that
E
or
F
are normed vector spaces. Notice also that we do not assume that
E
or
F
are complete with respect to the natural distances mentioned in Remark
8
.

(4)

The tame estimate (
3.4
) is assumed to hold only for functions in the smallest set
X^{\infty}_{1}
. In practical terms, this implies that it suffices to establish a priori estimates for smooth functions.

\displaystyle\|\Phi_{\Sigma}(v)\|_{\Sigma^{s_{1}}_{\infty}(F)}

\displaystyle=\big\lVert L_{Y}\big(\Phi(R_{X}(f))\big)\big\rVert_{\Sigma^{s_{1}}_{\infty}(F)}

3.2.
Reduction to the case

We next prove that one can assume without loss of generality that

. Indeed, we can always reduce the general case to the latter by setting

B=E\times F\quad\text{equipped with the pseudo-norm}\quad\left\lVert(u,v)\right\rVert_{B}=\left\lVert u\right\rVert_{E}+\left\lVert v\right\rVert_{F},

and by considering the mapping
\tilde{\Phi}
, acting on sequences
f=(f_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
with
f_{k}=(x_{k},y_{k})\in B=E\times F
, by

\tilde{\Phi}(f):=(0,\Phi(x)).

We thus assume in the following that
(E,\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert_{E})=(F,\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert_{F})
and write simply
\Sigma^{s}_{q}
instead of
\Sigma^{s}_{q}(E)
.

3.3.
Friedrichs’s mollifiers

We now introduce, following Hamilton
[
14
]
, a version of Friedrichs’ mollifiers adapted to the space

\Sigma^{s}_{q}(E)
.
Namely, for each
n\in\mathbb{N}
, one introduces a
linear map

S_{n}\colon\Sigma^{-\infty}_{\infty}\to\Sigma^{\infty}_{1},

such that
S_{n}
converges to the identity (in a sense to be made precise) when
n
goes to
+\infty
.

To do so, given
n\in\mathbb{N}
and a sequence
f=(f_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
we define the sequence

S_{n}f=((S_{n}f)_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}

by

(S_{n}f)_{k}:=f_{k}\quad\text{for}\quad k\leq n\quad;\quad(S_{n}f)_{k}:=0\quad\text{for}\quad k>n.

Since
(S_{n}f)_{k}
vanishes for
k>n
, the sequence
S_{n}f
obviously belongs
to
\Sigma^{s}_{1}
for all
s\in\mathbb{R}
. The operators
S_{n}
are thus smoothing operators.
Let us recall two classical results.

Lemma 23
.

i)
For all real numbers
r\leq r^{\prime}
,
all
n\in\mathbb{N}
and all
q\in[1,+\infty]
, there holds

(3.6)

\left\lVert S_{n}f\right\rVert_{\Sigma^{r^{\prime}}_{q}}\leq 2^{n(r^{\prime}-r)}\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{\Sigma^{r}_{q}}.

ii)
For all
r\in\mathbb{R}
, all
q\in[1,+\infty]
and all
f\in\Sigma^{r}_{q}(E)
,
there holds

\lim_{n\to\infty}\left\lVert f-S_{n}f\right\rVert_{\Sigma^{r}_{q}}=0.

Proof.

i)
Directly from the definitions of
S_{n}
and
\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert_{\Sigma^{s}_{q}}
, we have

\displaystyle\left\lVert S_{n}f\right\rVert_{\Sigma^{r^{\prime}}_{q}}^{q}=\sum_{k\leq n}2^{qkr^{\prime}}\left\lVert f_{k}\right\rVert_{E}^{q}\leq 2^{qn(r^{\prime}-r)}\sum_{k\leq n}2^{qkr}\left\lVert f_{k}\right\rVert_{E}^{q}\leq 2^{qn(r^{\prime}-r)}\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{\Sigma^{r}_{q}}^{q},

which implies the desired
estimate.

ii)
When
q<+\infty
, the second statement is
proved by writing

\left\lVert f-S_{n}f\right\rVert_{\Sigma^{r}_{q}}^{q}=\sum_{k\geq n+1}2^{qkr}\left\lVert f_{k}\right\rVert_{E}^{q},

and then by observing that the right-hand side in the above inequality converges to
0
as
n
goes to
+\infty
as the remainder of a convergent series. When
q=+\infty
, the results follows from the assumption (
2.1
).
∎

Assume that
q<+\infty
and
r<r^{\prime}
. The following observation contains a key improvement of (
3.6
).
We claim that there exists a constant
K>0
such that, for all
f\in\Sigma^{r}_{q}(E)
,

(3.7)

\sum_{n\in\mathbb{N}}2^{-qn(r^{\prime}-r)}\left\lVert S_{n}f\right\rVert_{\Sigma^{r^{\prime}}_{q}}^{q}\leq K\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{\Sigma^{r}_{q}}^{q}.

To see this, write

\displaystyle\sum_{n\in\mathbb{N}}2^{-qn(r^{\prime}-r)}\left\lVert S_{n}f\right\rVert_{\Sigma^{r^{\prime}}_{q}}^{q}

\displaystyle=\sum_{n\in\mathbb{N}}2^{-qn(r^{\prime}-r)}\bigg(\sum_{k=0}^{n}2^{qkr^{\prime}}\left\lVert f_{k}\right\rVert_{E}^{q}\bigg)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\bigg(\sum_{n=k}^{+\infty}2^{-qn(r^{\prime}-r)}\bigg)2^{qkr^{\prime}}\left\lVert f_{k}\right\rVert_{E}^{q}

\displaystyle\lesssim_{q}\sum_{k\in\mathbb{N}}2^{-qk(r^{\prime}-r)}2^{qkr^{\prime}}\left\lVert f_{k}\right\rVert_{E}^{q}=\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{\Sigma^{r}_{q}}^{q}.

The next lemma further improves (
3.7
) by replacing

\left\lVert S_{n}f\right\rVert_{\Sigma^{r^{\prime}}_{q}}
with the stronger norm
\left\lVert S_{n}f\right\rVert_{\Sigma^{r^{\prime}}_{1}}
. The proof relies on the Young’s inequality for sequences, which we recall here to clarify notations. Given
two sequences
u,v\colon\mathbb{Z}\to\mathbb{R}
, we denote by
u*v
the convolution product defined by

(u*v)(n)=\sum_{p\in\mathbb{Z}}u(n-p)v(p).

Given
q\in[1,+\infty)
(resp.
q=+\infty
),
denote by
\ell^{q}(\mathbb{Z})
the space of those sequences
(u_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}
such that

\left\lVert u\right\rVert_{\ell^{q}}=\big(\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}\left\lvert u(k)\right\rvert^{q}\big)^{1/q}<+\infty
(resp.
\left\lVert u\right\rVert_{\ell^{\infty}}=\sup_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}\left\lvert u(k)\right\rvert<+\infty
).
Denote by
c_{0}(\mathbb{Z})
the sub-space of
\ell^{\infty}(\mathbb{Z})
of
those sequences
(u_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}
such that
\lim_{\left\lvert k\right\rvert\to+\infty}\left\lvert u(k)\right\rvert=0
.

With these notations we have:

Proposition 24

(Young’s inequality)
.

Let
q\in[1,+\infty]
and consider two sequences
u\in\ell^{1}(\mathbb{Z})
and

v\in\ell^{q}(\mathbb{Z})
. Then
u*v\in\ell^{q}(\mathbb{Z})
together with the estimate

\left\lVert u*v\right\rVert_{\ell^{q}}\leq\left\lVert u\right\rVert_{\ell^{1}}\left\lVert v\right\rVert_{\ell^{q}}.

In addition, if
v\in c_{0}(\mathbb{Z})
then
u*v\in c_{0}(\mathbb{Z})
.

We are now in position to prove the following

Lemma 25
.

i)
For all real numbers
r<r^{\prime}

and all

f\in\Sigma^{r}_{\infty}(E)
, there holds

\sup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}2^{-n(r^{\prime}-r)}\left\lVert S_{n}f\right\rVert_{\Sigma^{r^{\prime}}_{1}}\leq\frac{1}{1-2^{r-r^{\prime}}}\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{\Sigma^{r}_{\infty}}.

ii)
For all real numbers
r<r^{\prime}
, all
q\in[1,+\infty)
and all

f\in\Sigma^{r}_{q}(E)
, there holds

\Big(\sum_{n\in\mathbb{N}}2^{-qn(r^{\prime}-r)}\left\lVert S_{n}f\right\rVert_{\Sigma^{r^{\prime}}_{1}}^{q}\Big)^{\frac{1}{q}}\leq\frac{1}{1-2^{r-r^{\prime}}}\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{\Sigma^{r}_{q}}.

Proof.

Introduce the sequence
\gamma=(\gamma_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
defined by

\gamma_{n}:=2^{-n(r^{\prime}-r)}\left\lVert S_{n}f\right\rVert_{\Sigma^{r^{\prime}}_{1}}
. Note that

\displaystyle\gamma_{n}

\displaystyle=2^{-n(r^{\prime}-r)}\sum_{k=0}^{n}2^{kr^{\prime}}\left\lVert f_{k}\right\rVert_{E}=\sum_{k=0}^{n}2^{(k-n)(r^{\prime}-r)}2^{kr}\left\lVert f_{k}\right\rVert_{E}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}2^{-\left\lvert k-n\right\rvert(r^{\prime}-r)}2^{kr}\left\lVert f_{k}\right\rVert_{E}.

We seek to interpret the last expression as the convolution of two sequences
u,v\colon\mathbb{Z}\to\mathbb{R}
. To do so,
set

\displaystyle u(p)

\displaystyle=2^{-p(r^{\prime}-r)}\quad

\displaystyle\text{for}\quad p\in\mathbb{N},\quad

\displaystyle u(p)=0\quad

\displaystyle\text{for}\quad p\in\mathbb{Z}\setminus\mathbb{N},

\displaystyle v(p)

\displaystyle=2^{pr}\left\lVert f_{p}\right\rVert_{E}\quad

\displaystyle\text{for}\quad p\in\mathbb{N},\quad

\displaystyle v(p)=0\quad

\displaystyle\text{for}\quad p\in\mathbb{Z}\setminus\mathbb{N}.

Notice that
\left\lVert u\right\rVert_{\ell^{1}}=\sum_{p\in\mathbb{N}}2^{-p(r^{\prime}-r)}=1/(1-2^{r-r^{\prime}})
.
In addition, for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
, we have

\gamma_{n}\leq(u*v)(n)=\sum_{p\in\mathbb{Z}}u(n-p)v(p).

As a consequence, the result follows from Proposition
24
.
∎

3.4.
Interpolation

In this

Proposition 26

Let

Proof.

We begin
with
an elementary result which
asserts
that

is
the larger space
\Sigma^{s_{0}}_{\infty}(E)

Lemma 27
.

The sequence
\Phi(S_{n}f)
converges to
\Phi(f)
in

\Sigma^{s_{0}}_{\infty}
when
n
tends to
+\infty

Proof.

Since

.
∎

The next result asserts that

.

Lemma 30
.

Set

\kappa=\min(s_{1}-s,s-s_{0})\quad\text{and}\quad C=\max\{C_{0},(1+2^{s_{1}-s})C_{1}\},

where
C_{0}
and

) on
\Phi
.
Then, for all

(3.14)

Proof.

We consider the cases
m\leq n
and
m>n

To handle the case

\displaystyle 2^{ms}\|(\Phi(S_{n+1}f)-\Phi(S_{n}f))_{m}\|_{E}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad=2^{m(s-s_{0})}2^{ms_{0}}\|(\Phi(S_{n+1}f)-\Phi(S_{n}f))_{m}\|_{E}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\leq 2^{m(s-s_{0})}\sup_{k\in\mathbb{N}}2^{ks_{0}}\|(\Phi(S_{n+1}f)-\Phi(S_{n}f))_{k}\|_{E}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\leq 2^{m(s-s_{0})}\|\Phi(S_{n+1}f)-\Phi(S_{n}f)\|_{\Sigma^{s_{0}}_{\infty}}\quad

\displaystyle(\text{def. of }\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert_{\Sigma^{s_{0}}_{\infty}})

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\leq C_{0}2^{(m-n)(s-s_{0})}\gamma_{n+1}\quad

\displaystyle(\text{cf.\ }\eqref{nlow})

It remains to consider the case

For that purpose, we use the bound (
3.10

\displaystyle 2^{ms}\|(\Phi(S_{n+1}f)-\Phi(S_{n}f))_{m}\|_{E}

\displaystyle\qquad=2^{-m(s_{1}-s)}2^{ms_{1}}\|(\Phi(S_{n+1}f)-\Phi(S_{n}f))_{m}\|_{E}

∎

We are now ready to prove Proposition
26
. The argument relies on a telescoping sum argument. Namely, notice that
for each index

we have the following identity in the pseudo-normed vector space

(\Phi(f)-\Phi(S_{n}f))_{m}=\sum_{p\geq n}\big(\Phi(S_{p+1}f)-\Phi(S_{p}f)\big)_{m}.

The previous summation is rigorously justified since Lemma
27

that the sequence
(\Phi(S_{n}f))_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
converges to
\Phi(f)
in the space
\Sigma_{\infty}^{s_{0}}
and hence, for all
m\in\mathbb{N}

\lim_{N\to+\infty}\left\lVert(\Phi(f))_{m}-(\Phi(S_{N}f))_{m}\right\rVert_{E}=0
.

Introduce the sequence

c=(c_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
defined by

(3.15)

c_{n}:=\gamma_{n}+\gamma_{n+1}.

Now, it follows from Lemma
30

(3.16)

\displaystyle 2^{ms}\|(\Phi(f)-\Phi(S_{n}f))_{m}\|_{E}

\displaystyle\leq 2^{ms}\sum_{p\geq n}\|(\Phi(S_{p+1}u)-\Phi(S_{p}u))_{m}\|_{E}

\displaystyle\leq C\sum_{p\geq n}2^{-\kappa|m-p|}c_{p}.

Let us now deduce from this that

(3.17)

\lim_{n\to+\infty}\|\Phi(f)-\Phi(S_{n}f)\|_{\Sigma^{s}_{q}}=0.

First, we treat the case
q=+\infty
. Since the sequence
c=(c_{p})_{p\in\mathbb{N}}
converges to
0
when
p
goes to
+\infty
, we easily verify that

\lim_{m\to+\infty}\sum_{p\geq 0}2^{-\kappa|m-p|}c_{p}=0,

and so (
3.16
) implies
that
\Phi(f)-\Phi(S_{n}f)
belongs to
\Sigma^{s}_{\infty}
for all

. Let us prove that, in addition,
\lim_{n\to+\infty}\|\Phi(f)-\Phi(S_{n}f)\|_{\Sigma^{s}_{\infty}}=0
. To do so, consider
\epsilon>0
. Using again the fact that the sequence
c=(c_{p})_{p\in\mathbb{N}}
converges to

when

goes to
+\infty
, we obtain that there exists
n_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
such that, for all

and all

,

\sum_{p\geq n}2^{-\kappa|m-p|}c_{p}\leq\Big(\sum_{p\geq n}2^{-\kappa|m-p|}\Big)\epsilon\leq\beta\epsilon\quad\text{where}\quad\beta=\sum_{b\in\mathbb{Z}}2^{-\kappa\left\lvert b\right\rvert}=\frac{2}{1-2^{-\kappa}}.

It remains to treat the case

Using Young’s inequality

(see Proposition

), we deduce from (

(3.18)

\displaystyle\|\Phi(f)-\Phi(S_{n}f)\|_{\Sigma^{s}_{q}}\leq AC\Big(\sum_{p\geq n}c_{p}^{q}\Big)^{\frac{1}{q}}\quad\text{where}

\displaystyle A=\sup_{m\in\mathbb{N}}\sum_{p\in\mathbb{N}}2^{-\kappa\left\lvert p-m\right\rvert}\leq\sum_{p\in\mathbb{Z}}2^{-\kappa\left\lvert p\right\rvert}=\frac{2}{1-2^{-\kappa}}\cdot

This implies
the desired result since
\sum_{p\geq n}c_{p}^{q}
goes to

when

tends to
+\infty
, as

(see (
3.9
)).

This concludes the proof of Proposition

.
∎

3.5.
Continuity

So far, we have proved that
\Phi(f)
belongs to
\Sigma^{s}_{q}
for any
f\in B_{s,q}(0,r)
.
It remains to prove that the mapping
\Phi\colon B_{s,q}(0,r)\to\Sigma^{s}_{q}
is continuous.

Consider
f\in\Sigma^{s}_{q}
and a sequence
(f_{N})_{N\in\mathbb{N}}
converging to
f
in
\Sigma^{s}_{q}
. We want to prove that
\Phi(f_{N})
converges to
\Phi(f)
in
\Sigma^{s}_{q}
as
N
tends to
+\infty
. The proof is based on a
3\varepsilon
-argument. Namely, it relies on the following triangle inequality, applied with
n
large enough:

(3.19)

\displaystyle\|\Phi(f_{N})-f\|_{\Sigma^{s}_{q}}

\displaystyle\leq\|\Phi(f_{N})-\Phi(S_{n}f_{N})\|_{\Sigma^{s}_{q}}

\displaystyle+\|\Phi(S_{n}f_{N})-\Phi(S_{n}f)\|_{\Sigma^{s}_{q}}

\displaystyle+\|\Phi(S_{n}f)-f\|_{\Sigma^{s}_{q}}.

As a direct corollary of the inequality (
3.18
), we will see
that the first and the third terms go to
0
when
n
tends to
+\infty
,

uniformly in
N
. Then, the second term will be analyzed
by observing that, once
n
is fixed, the term

\Phi(S_{n}f_{N})-\Phi(S_{n}f)
goes to
0
in
\Sigma^{s}_{q}

as
N
tends to
0
by an elementary interpolation argument (since this difference converges to
0
in
\Sigma^{s_{0}}_{\infty}
and is bounded in
\Sigma^{s_{1}}_{q}
).

Let us now proceed
with
the details.
Introduce the sequences

\gamma=(\gamma_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}},\quad c=(c_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}},\quad\gamma_{N}=(\gamma_{N,n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}},\quad c_{N}=(c_{N,n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\quad\text{with}\quad N\in\mathbb{N},

defined by

\displaystyle\gamma_{n}:=2^{-n(s_{1}-s)}\left\lVert S_{n}f\right\rVert_{\Sigma^{s_{1}}_{1}}\quad

and

\displaystyle c_{n}:=\gamma_{n+1}+\gamma_{n},

\displaystyle\gamma_{N,n}:=2^{-n(s_{1}-s)}\left\lVert S_{n}f_{N}\right\rVert_{\Sigma^{s_{1}}_{1}}\quad

and

\displaystyle c_{N,n}:=\gamma_{N,n+1}+\gamma_{N,n}.

It follows from the triangle inequality applied in
\Sigma^{s_{1}}_{1}
that

\left\lvert\gamma_{N,n}-\gamma_{n}\right\rvert\leq 2^{-n(s_{1}-s)}\left\lVert S_{n}(f_{N}-f)\right\rVert_{\Sigma^{s_{1}}_{1}}.

Therefore,
Lemma
25
implies that

\left\lVert\gamma_{N}-\gamma\right\rVert_{\ell^{q}}\leq\frac{1}{1-2^{s-s_{1}}}\left\lVert f_{N}-f\right\rVert_{\Sigma^{s}_{q}},

and hence

\gamma_{N}

converges in
\ell^{q}(\mathbb{N})
to
\gamma
as
N
tends to
+\infty
. Consequently,
c_{N}

converges in
\ell^{q}(\mathbb{N})
to
c
as
N
tends to
+\infty
.

Now, notice that, since for
N
large enough we have

f_{N}\in B_{s,q}(0,r)
, we can apply the estimate (
3.18
) with
f
replaced by
f_{N}
,
to write

\|\Phi(f_{N})-\Phi(S_{n}f_{N})\|_{\Sigma^{s}_{q}}\lesssim\left\lVert 1_{\geq n}c_{N}\right\rVert_{\ell^{q}},

where
1_{\geq n}=(1_{\geq n}(p))_{p\in\mathbb{N}}
is the sequence defined by
1_{\geq n}(p)=1
provided that
p\geq n
, and
1_{\geq n}(p)=0
otherwise. Notice that this inequality holds both for
q<+\infty
and
q=+\infty
.
So, by using the triangle inequality in
\ell^{q}(\mathbb{N})
,

(3.20)

\|\Phi(f_{N})-\Phi(S_{n}f_{N})\|_{\Sigma^{s}_{q}}\lesssim\left\lVert 1_{\geq n}c_{N}\right\rVert_{\ell^{q}}\leq\left\lVert 1_{\geq n}c\right\rVert_{\ell^{q}}+\left\lVert c_{N}-c\right\rVert_{\ell^{q}}.

Let
\varepsilon>0
. Since

1_{\geq n}c
goes to

0
in
\ell^{q}(\mathbb{N})
as
n
tends to
+\infty
(as already seen) and since
c_{N}-c
goes to
0
in
\ell^{q}(\mathbb{N})
as
N
tends to
\infty
, one can find
n_{0}
and
N_{0}
such that,

\|\Phi(f_{N})-\Phi(S_{n}f_{N})\|_{\Sigma^{s}_{q}}\leq\varepsilon

for all
n\geq n_{0}
and all
N\geq N_{0}
. Moreover,
since (see Proposition
26
)

\lim_{n\to+\infty}\|\Phi(f)-\Phi(S_{n}f)\|_{\Sigma^{s}_{q}}=0,

one can further assume that

\|\Phi(f)-\Phi(S_{n}f)\|_{\Sigma^{s}_{q}}\leq\varepsilon,

for all
n\geq n_{0}
. Now, write

\displaystyle\|\Phi(f_{N})-f\|_{\Sigma^{s}_{q}}

\displaystyle\leq\|\Phi(f_{N})-\Phi(S_{n}f_{N})\|_{\Sigma^{s}_{q}}

\displaystyle\quad+\|\Phi(S_{n}f_{N})-\Phi(S_{n}f)\|_{\Sigma^{s}_{q}}

\displaystyle\quad+\|\Phi(S_{n}f)-f\|_{\Sigma^{s}_{q}}.

When
n\geq n_{0}
and
N\geq N_{0}
, the first and the last term in the right-hand side above are smaller than
\varepsilon
.
It remains only to estimate the second term. More precisely, to conclude the proof of Theorem
20
, it is sufficient to prove that, for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
, there holds

(3.21)

\lim_{N\rightarrow+\infty}\|\Phi(S_{n}f_{N})-\Phi(S_{n}f)\|_{\Sigma^{s}_{q}}=0.

To do so, we notice that, given some fixed
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
the set

\{S_{n}f_{N};N\in\mathbb{N}\}\cup\{S_{n}f\}

is bounded in
\Sigma^{s_{1}}_{q}

(see Lemma
25
).
Therefore, the assumption (
3.4
) implies that

\sup_{N\in\mathbb{N}}\|\Phi(S_{n}f_{N})-\Phi(S_{n}f)\|_{\Sigma^{s_{1}}_{\infty}}\leq\sup_{N\in\mathbb{N}}\Big(\|\Phi(S_{n}f_{N})\|_{\Sigma^{s_{1}}_{\infty}}+\|\Phi(S_{n}f)\|_{\Sigma^{s_{1}}_{\infty}}\Big)<+\infty.

On the other hand, the assumption (
3.3
) implies that

\|\Phi(S_{n}f_{N})-\Phi(S_{n}f)\|_{\Sigma^{s_{0}}_{\infty}}\leq C_{0}\left\lVert S_{n}f_{N}-S_{n}f\right\rVert_{\Sigma^{s_{0}}_{1}}\leq C_{0}\left\lVert f_{N}-f\right\rVert_{\Sigma^{s_{0}}_{1}},

where we used the trivial estimate
\left\lVert S_{n}\right\rVert_{\Sigma^{s_{0}}_{1}\to\Sigma^{s_{0}}_{1}}\leq 1
to get the last inequality.
This proves that
\|\Phi(S_{n}f_{N})-\Phi(S_{n}f)\|_{\Sigma^{s_{0}}_{\infty}}
converges to
0
as
N
tends to
+\infty
.
Now we obtain the desired inequality (
3.21
) using the
following straightforward interpolation inequality.

Lemma 31
.

For all
s\in(s_{0},s_{1})
and all
q\in[1,+\infty]
, there exists a constant
C
such that,
for all
f\in\Sigma^{s_{1}}_{\infty}
,

(3.22)

\|f\|_{\Sigma^{s}_{q}}\leq C\|f\|_{\Sigma^{s_{0}}_{\infty}}^{\theta}\|f\|_{\Sigma^{s_{1}}_{\infty}}^{1-\theta},\qquad\text{with}\quad\theta=\frac{s_{1}-s}{s_{1}-s_{0}}.

Proof.

Let
N\in\mathbb{N}
and consider the decomposition
f=S_{N}f+(I-S_{N})f
.
Notice that

\displaystyle\|S_{N}f\|_{\Sigma^{s}_{q}}^{q}

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=0}^{N}2^{nqs}\left\lVert f_{n}\right\rVert_{E}^{q}=\sum_{n=0}^{N}2^{nqs_{0}}2^{nq(s-s_{0})}\left\lVert f_{n}\right\rVert_{E}^{q}

\displaystyle\leq\left(\sum_{n=0}^{N}2^{nq(s-s_{0})}\right)\Big(\sup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}2^{nqs_{0}}\left\lVert f_{n}\right\rVert_{E}^{q}\Big)^{q}\lesssim 2^{Nq(s-s_{0})}\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{\Sigma^{s_{0}}_{\infty}}^{q}.

Similarly, we have

\|(I-S_{N})f\|_{\Sigma^{s}_{q}}^{q}\leq\left(\sum_{n=N+1}^{\infty}2^{nq(s-s_{1})}\right)\Big(\sup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}2^{nqs_{1}}\left\lVert f_{n}\right\rVert_{E}^{q}\Big)^{q}\lesssim 2^{Nq(s-s_{1})}\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{\Sigma^{s_{1}}_{\infty}}^{q}.

It then follows from the triangle inequality that

\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{\Sigma^{s}_{q}}\lesssim 2^{N(s-s_{0})}\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{\Sigma^{s_{0}}_{\infty}}+2^{-N(s_{1}-s)}\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{\Sigma^{s_{1}}_{\infty}},

from which we obtain the desired inequality (
3.22
) by optimizing in

N
.
∎

The proof of
Theorem
20
is now complete.

3.6.
Proof of Proposition
22

We now assume that
\Phi
satisfies (
3.4
) together with (
3.5
).
Recall that the latter is a weak form of assumption (
3.3
), where the estimate is assumed
to hold only for smooth elements
v,w
in
\Sigma^{\infty}_{1}(E)\cap B_{s}(0,r)
.
The only place where we used the stronger assumption (
3.3
) was in the proof
of Lemma
27
. This lemma asserts that the
sequence
\Phi(S_{n}f)
converges to
\Phi(f)
in
\Sigma^{s_{0}}_{\infty}(F)
as
n

tends to
+\infty
. Therefore, we only need to prove that

),

S_{k}f

belongs to
\Sigma^{\infty}_{1}(E)\cap B_{s}(0,r)

for all

, one apply (

\sum_{p=n}^{+\infty}\|\Phi(S_{p+1}f)-\Phi(S_{p}f)\|_{\Sigma^{s_{0}}_{\infty}}\leq C_{0}\sum_{p=n}^{+\infty}\|S_{p+1}f-S_{p}f\|_{\Sigma^{s_{0}}_{1}}.

It follows that

\displaystyle\sum_{p=n}^{+\infty}\|\Phi(S_{p+1}f)-\Phi(S_{p}f)\|_{\Sigma^{s_{0}}_{\infty}}

\displaystyle\leq C_{0}\sum_{k=n+1}^{+\infty}2^{ks_{0}}\|f_{k}\|_{E}

Now, observe that, since
\Sigma^{s_{0}}_{\infty}(F)

\sum_{p=n}^{+\infty}\Phi(S_{p+1}f)-\Phi(S_{p}f)

. Moreover, since it is assumed that the mapping
\Phi
is continuous from

\Phi(f)-\Phi(S_{n}f)=\sum_{p=n}^{+\infty}\Phi(S_{p+1}f)-\Phi(S_{p}f).

Secondly, since the right-hand side in (

) converges to

as

tends to infinity,
we conclude that the sequence
\Phi(S_{n}f)
converges to
\Phi(f)
in
\Sigma^{s_{0}}_{\infty}(F)
as

tends to
+\infty
. This completes the proof.

4.
Proof of Theorem
1

We show here
that Theorem
1
can be deduced from our abstract interpolation

result in Theorem
18
.
One interesting feature of the proof is that we will exploit the fact that we only assume
some weak bounds in Theorem
18
(by weak bounds, we refer to the fact that the mapping
\Phi
is only estimated with the norms
\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert_{Y^{s_{0}}_{\infty}}
and
\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert_{Y^{s_{1}}_{\infty}}
while the conclusion involves the strong norm
\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert_{Y^{s}}
).

Let us introduce several
scales of Banach spaces.
On the one hand the Sobolev scale
X^{s}=H^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, which is of type
(2,L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d}))
when the operators
L
and
R
are given by the Littlewood-Paley decomposition, as explained in Example
17
.
We also introduce the scale of time-dependent
functions
f=f(t)
with values in
H^{s}
, which are
L^{\mu}
in time, that is the spaces

L^{\mu}((0,T);H^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{d})).

We further introduce a third scale, which we refer to as
the Chemin-Lerner
scale
Y^{s}
, defined by:

Y^{s}=\left\{f\in L^{\mu}((0,T);H^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{d}))\,;\,\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{Y^{s}}=\Big(\sum_{j\in\mathbb{N}}\left\lVert\Delta_{j}f\right\rVert_{L^{\mu}([0,T];H^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{d}))}^{2}\Big)^{\frac{1}{2}}<+\infty\right\},

where
I=\sum_{j\in\mathbb{N}}\Delta_{j}
still denotes the Littlewood-Paley decompositon of tempered distributions
u\in\mathcal{S}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
and

\left\lVert\Delta_{j}f\right\rVert_{L^{\mu}([0,T];H^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{d}))}^{2}=\bigg(\int_{0}^{T}\left\lVert\Delta_{j}(f(t))\right\rVert_{H^{s}}^{\mu}\,dt\bigg)^{\frac{2}{\mu}}.

Now the two important observations are the following. Firstly,
Y^{s}
is a scale of Banach spaces of type
(2,F)
where
F=L^{\mu}((0,T);L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d}))
. And secondly,
since
\mu\geq 2
by assumption,
it follows from the Minkowski inequality (see Stein
[
37
]
) that

\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{L^{\mu}([0,T];H^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{d}))}\leq\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{Y^{s}}.

There is in addition a little trick:
since
\left\lVert\Delta_{j}\right\rVert_{H^{r}\to H^{r}}\leq 1
(as follows from Plancherel’s theorem) for all
j\in\mathbb{N}
and all
r\in\mathbb{R}
, we have

\left\lVert\Delta_{j}f\right\rVert_{L^{\mu}([0,T];H^{r}(\mathbb{R}^{d}))}\leq\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{L^{\mu}([0,T];H^{r}(\mathbb{R}^{d}))}

and hence

\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{Y^{r}_{\infty}}=\sup_{j\in\mathbb{N}}\left\lVert\Delta_{j}f\right\rVert_{L^{\mu}([0,T];H^{r}(\mathbb{R}^{d}))}\leq\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{L^{\mu}([0,T];H^{r}(\mathbb{R}^{d}))}.

Therefore, under the assumptions of Theorem
1
we see that

\|\Phi(v)-\Phi(w)\|_{Y^{s_{0}}_{\infty}}\leq\|\Phi(v)-\Phi(w)\|_{L^{\mu}((0,T);H^{s_{0}})}

and similarly

\|\Phi(v)\|_{Y^{s_{1}}_{\infty}}\leq\|\Phi(v)\|_{L^{\mu}((0,T);H^{s_{1}})}.

Therefore, under the assumption of Theorem
1
, the flow map
\Phi
satisfies those of Theorem
18
applied with

q=2,\quad E=L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\quad\text{and}\quad F=L^{\mu}((0,T);L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})).

And hence we deduce the first two points of Theorem
1
.

It remains to prove the last
assertion of Theorem
1
about the continuity in time. Consider a function
v_{0}\in B_{s}(u_{0},r)
such that
\Phi(v_{0})
belongs to
C^{0}([0,T];H^{-\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})))
.
We want to prove that

\Phi(v_{0})
belongs to
C^{0}([0,T];H^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{d}))
.
Given an integer
N
,
introduce the smoothing operator

S_{N}\colon H^{-\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\to H^{+\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
defined by

S_{N}u:=\sum_{j=0}^{N}\Delta_{j}u.

Now, the functions
S_{N}\Phi(v_{0})
defined by

(S_{N}(\Phi(v_{0})))(t):=S_{N}(\Phi(v_{0})(t))

belong to
C^{0}([0,T];H^{+\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d}))
.
To prove that
\Phi(v_{0})
belongs to
C^{0}([0,T];H^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{d}))
, it is thus sufficient to show that
S_{N}\Phi(v_{0})
tends to
\Phi(v_{0})
in

L^{\infty}([0,T];H^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{d}))
when
N
tends to
+\infty
. To do so, we
use that, by almost orthogonality, we have

\left\lVert\Phi(v_{0})-S_{N}\Phi(v_{0})\right\rVert_{L^{\infty}([0,T];H^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{d}))}^{2}\leq K\sum_{j\geq N-N_{0}}\left\lVert\Delta_{j}\Phi(v_{0})\right\rVert_{L^{\infty}([0,T];H^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{d}))}^{2},

for some constants
K
and
N_{0}
depending only on the choice of
functions
\psi
in the Littlewood-Paley decomposition. Since the series in the right-hand is convergent (as
\Phi(v_{0})\in Y^{s}
), we see that the right-hand side goes to
0
as
N
tends to
+\infty
as the remainder of a converging series.
This completes the proof.

Littlewood-Paley decompositions

We recall here some elementary results
about the Littlewood–Paley decomposition. This decomposition makes it possible to introduce a parameter into a problem
that has none.
There are many books that develop a systematic study
of the Littlewood–Paley decomposition: Alinhac and Gérard
[
4
]
, Bahouri, Danchin and Chemin
[
5
]
, Coifman and Meyer
[
10
,
26
]
, Métivier
[
24
]
,
Muscalu and Schlag
[
27
]
, Stein
[
37
,
38
]
, Tao
[
40
]
or Taylor
[

,

]
.

Lemma 32
.

Let
n\geq 1
.
There exist
\psi\in C_{0}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
and

\varphi\in C_{0}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
such that the following properties are satisfied:

(1)

we have
0\leq\psi\leq 1
,
0\leq\varphi\leq 1
and

\supp\psi\subset\{\left\lvert\xi\right\rvert\leq 1\},\qquad\supp\varphi\subset\Bigl\{\frac{3}{4}\leq|\xi|\leq 2\Bigr\};

(2)

for all
\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
,

(A.1)

1=\psi(\xi)+\sum_{p=0}^{\infty}\varphi(2^{-p}\xi);

(3)

(almost orthogonality) for all
\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
,

(A.2)

\frac{1}{3}\leq\psi^{2}(\xi)+\sum_{p=0}^{+\infty}\varphi^{2}(2^{-p}\xi)\leq 1.

Proof.

Consider a function

\psi\in C^{\infty}_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{d};\mathbb{R})

satisfying

\psi(\xi)=1
for
\left\lvert\xi\right\rvert\leq 3/4
, and

\psi(\xi)=0
for
\left\lvert\xi\right\rvert\geq 1
.
Next, we define

\varphi(\xi)=\psi(\xi/2)-\psi(\xi)
and we note that
\varphi
is supported in the annulus

\{3/4\leq\left\lvert\xi\right\rvert\leq 2\}
.
For all integers
N
and all
\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, we have

\psi(\xi)+\sum_{p=0}^{N}\varphi(2^{-p}\xi)=\psi(2^{-N-1}\xi),

which immediately implies (
A.1
) by letting

N
tend to
+\infty
.

It remains to prove (
A.2
). For all integers
N
, we have

\psi^{2}(\xi)+\sum_{p=0}^{N}\varphi^{2}(2^{-p}\xi)\leq\Bigl(\psi(\xi)+\sum_{p=0}^{N}\varphi(2^{-p}\xi)\Bigr)^{2}.

On the other hand, we note that, for all
\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, there are never more than three non-zero terms in
the set
\{\psi(\xi),~\varphi(\xi),\ldots,\varphi(2^{-p}\xi),\ldots\}
.
Therefore, using the elementary inequality
(a+b+c)^{2}\leq 3(a^{2}+b^{2}+c^{2})
, we obtain

\Bigl(\psi(\xi)+\sum_{p=0}^{N}\varphi(2^{-p}\xi)\Bigr)^{2}\leq 3\Bigl(\psi^{2}(\xi)+\sum_{p=0}^{N}\varphi^{2}(2^{-p}\xi)\Bigr).

We then obtain (
A.2
) by letting
N
tend to
+\infty
in the previous inequalities.
∎

Let us define, for
p\geq 0
, the Fourier multipliers
\Delta_{p}
as follows:

\Delta_{0}:=\psi(D_{x})\quad\text{and}\quad\Delta_{p}:=\varphi\bigl(2^{-(p-1)}D_{x}\bigr)\quad(p\geq 1).

Then we obtain a decomposition of the identity: we have

I=\sum_{p\geq 0}\Delta_{p},

in the sense of distributions: for all
u\in\mathcal{S}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
,
the series
\sum\Delta_{p}u

converges to
u
in
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, which means that
\sum_{p=0}^{N}\langle\Delta_{p}u,\varphi\rangle_{\mathcal{S}^{\prime}\times\mathcal{S}}
converges to
\langle u,\varphi\rangle_{\mathcal{S}^{\prime}\times\mathcal{S}}
when
N
goes to
+\infty,
for all
\varphi\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
.

We then recall how to
use the Littlewood–Paley decomposition to characterize Sobolev spaces.

Proposition 33
.

Consider
s\in\mathbb{R}
. A tempered distribution
u\in\mathcal{S}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
belongs to the Sobolev space
H^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
if and only if

(a)

for all
p\geq 0
,
\Delta_{p}u\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
;

(b)

the sequence
\delta_{p}=2^{ps}\|\Delta_{p}u\|_{L^{2}}
belongs to
\ell^{2}(\mathbb{N})
.

Moreover, there exists a constant
C
such that

(A.3)

\frac{1}{C}\|u\|_{H^{s}}\leq\Bigl(\sum_{p=0}^{+\infty}\delta_{p}^{2}\Bigr)^{\frac{1}{2}}\leq C\|u\|_{H^{s}}.

Proof.

It follows from (
A.2
) and the Plancherel identity that

(A.4)

\sum_{p\geq 0}\|\Delta_{p}u\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\leq\left\lVert u\right\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}\leq 3\sum_{p\geq 0}\|\Delta_{p}u\|_{L^{2}}^{2}.

Denote by
\langle D_{x}\rangle^{s}
the Fourier multiplier
(I-\Delta)^{s/2}
.
Since
\|u\|_{H^{s}}=\left\lVert\langle D_{x}\rangle^{s}u\right\rVert_{L^{2}}
, by applying (
A.4
) with

u
replaced by
\langle D_{x}\rangle^{s}u
, we get

\sum_{p\geq 0}\left\lVert\Delta_{p}\langle D_{x}\rangle^{s}u\right\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}\leq\left\lVert u\right\rVert_{H^{s}}^{2}\leq 3\sum_{p\geq 0}\left\lVert\Delta_{p}\langle D_{x}\rangle^{s}u\right\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}.

Consider
p\geq 1
and write

\left\lVert\Delta_{p}\langle D_{x}\rangle^{s}u\right\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}=(2\pi)^{-n}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}(1+|\xi|^{2})^{s}\varphi^{2}(2^{-p}\xi)|\hat{u}(\xi)|^{2}d\xi.

Since
(1+|\xi|^{2})^{s}\varphi^{2}(2^{-p}\xi)\sim 2^{2ps}
on the support of
\varphi^{2}(2^{-p}\xi)
, we see that

(A.5)

\frac{1}{C}\,2^{2ps}\,\|\Delta_{p}u\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\leq\left\lVert\Delta_{p}\langle D_{x}\rangle^{s}u\right\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}\leq C2^{2ps}\,\|\Delta_{p}u\|_{L^{2}}^{2},

for a certain constant
C
depending only on
s
. We have a similar estimate for
\Delta_{0}u
and the desired result follows easily.
∎
