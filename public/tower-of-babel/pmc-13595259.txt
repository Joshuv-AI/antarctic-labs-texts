Valvular Heart Disease as the Main Cause of Acute Heart Failure with Preserved Ejection Fraction: Dynamic Severe Functional Valvular Regurgitation as the Main Mechanism of Decompensation
Authors: Maria Giulia Bellicini
Published: 2026

To the Editor,

We read with great interest the comprehensive review by Adeli et al., who provide an elegant overview of the bidirectional relationship between valvular heart disease and cc (HFpEF), highlighting how valvular disease may either contribute to HFpEF or develop as its consequence.1 We particularly appreciated their discussion of functional mitral and tricuspid regurgitation, emphasising that these lesions differ fundamentally from primary valvular disease in terms of pathophysiology, clinical behaviour and management. However, in our opinion, this distinction has implications extending beyond treatment decisions and may fundamentally influence HFpEF diagnosis, phenotyping and the mechanisms responsible for acute decompensation.

Current heart failure guidelines appropriately require exclusion of haemodynamically significant valvular heart disease before establishing a diagnosis of HFpEF.2,3 Importantly, this recommendation is not restricted to primary valve disease but applies to all severe valvular lesions. For organic valve disease, this principle is relatively straightforward because lesion severity is generally stable over time. Functional mitral and tricuspid regurgitation, however, are fundamentally different. Their haemodynamic severity is dynamic, reflecting not only the underlying structural substrate but also loading conditions, atrial rhythm (and rate), ventricular afterload and the degree of congestion. Consequently, the same patient may fulfil exclusion criteria for severe valvular disease during an episode of acute heart failure but no longer do so after haemodynamic stabilisation despite persistence of the underlying atrial or ventricular substrate.

This temporal behaviour has important implications for HFpEF phenotyping. Adeli et al. report that valvular heart disease represents the primary aetiology of HFpEF in approximately 20% of patients1. While this estimate is entirely consistent with the available literature, it is also likely to depend on when valvular disease is assessed. Echocardiography performed during acute congestion captures both the structural lesion and its transient haemodynamic amplification, whereas reassessment after stabilisation better reflects the patient’s underlying disease substrate function. Consequently, the apparent contribution of functional valvular disease to HFpEF may vary according to the clinical phase in which imaging is performed.

Our centre’s observations provide a practical example of this concept. In a consecutive cohort of almost 770 patients admitted for acute heart failure and left ventricular ejection fraction >40%, comprehensive echocardiography performed before diuretic therapy demonstrated that severe valvular disease represented the predominant structural substrate underlying congestion in almost 80% of patients, whereas in absence of congestion, and after systematic exclusion of severe valvular disease and other alternative HFpEF mimics, guideline-defined HFpEF accounted for only approximately 8% of congested patients.4 Interestingly, functional severe mitral and tricuspid regurgitation tended to regress at discharge or during subsequent echocardiographic controls. These findings reinforce the importance of systematically identifying severe valvular disease during the acute phase of heart failure. More importantly, they suggest that severe functional mitral and tricuspid regurgitation may transiently become severe, thereby becoming the dominant haemodynamic mechanism responsible for acute decompensation rather than simply representing an associated echocardiographic finding in HFpEF.

This interpretation may also help reconcile two apparently different concepts of HFpEF. Contemporary pathophysiological models increasingly describe HFpEF as a syndrome characterised predominantly by exercise intolerance and abnormal filling-pressure responses during exertion rather than spontaneous fluid accumulation.5 Yet patients are frequently hospitalised because of overt pulmonary or systemic congestion. We speculate that progression of atrial (or sometimes ventricular) functional mitral and tricuspid regurgitation to a haemodynamically severe grade may represent one of the mechanisms bridging these two clinical scenarios. Rather than constituting the primary disease itself, when functional mitral or tricuspid regurgitation progresses to a severe grade, it may transiently become the dominant haemodynamic mechanism responsible for acute pulmonary or systemic congestion. Once congestion has resolved and rhythm or rate of AF have been optimised, regurgitation frequently decreases below the severe threshold substrate, allowing the patient once again to fulfil contemporary guideline HFpEF diagnostic criteria, despite persistence of the underlying disease substrate.

This concept may also provide a pathophysiological framework for interpreting the growing interest in moderate secondary mitral regurgitation.6 Functional regurgitation should probably not be regarded as a static lesion classified as either moderate or severe, but rather as a phase-dependent haemodynamic phenomenon. Lesions appearing only moderate during clinical stability may transiently progress to severe regurgitation during acute decompensation or episodes of AF especially with rapid ventricular response before regressing again following haemodynamic stabilisation. Accordingly, the severity observed during a single outpatient echocardiographic examination may not necessarily reflect the maximal haemodynamic burden imposed by the lesion during episodes of acute heart failure.

Among patients with preserved ejection fraction, atrial functional mitral regurgitation (MR) probably represents the predominant functional mitral phenotype. Its structural substrate consists of atrial cardiomyopathy with annular dilatation, usually associated with high ventricular response AF episodes. Acute decompensation, particularly when accompanied by rapid ventricular response, may markedly amplify regurgitation severity without substantially altering the underlying anatomy. By contrast, ventricular functional MR is considerably less common in preserved ejection fraction and is generally associated with localised ischaemic remodelling or marked afterload mismatch, such as hypertensive emergencies or severe aortic stenosis. Functional tricuspid regurgitation behaves similarly, frequently arising from right atrial remodelling due to AF, with device-lead interference potentially providing an additional mechanism in patients with cardiac implantable electronic devices, while exhibiting comparable haemodynamic variability.

Preliminary observations from our aforementioned cohort further support this hypothesis.4 Approximately 60% of patients presenting with severe atrial functional MR no longer fulfilled echocardiographic criteria for severe regurgitation after haemodynamic stabilisation in the absence of any direct mitral intervention, whereas primary MR demonstrated substantially greater temporal stability. Regression appeared to be favoured by restoration of a more physiological atrial haemodynamic milieu, particularly following adequate rate or rhythm control in patients with AF. Importantly, this apparent improvement should not be interpreted as resolution of the underlying disease. Rather, it reflects normalisation of the haemodynamic component superimposed upon persistent atrial cardiomyopathy and annular remodelling. Accordingly, echocardiographic valve severity and disease substrate should not be considered synonymous. While contemporary heart failure therapy (renin–angiotensin–aldosterone system blockade, β blockade) may not substantially modify the haemodynamic expression of functional regurgitation, long-term prognosis improves.

Finally, the dynamic nature of functional regurgitation may require us to reinterpret how landmark HFpEF trials have shaped the contemporary guideline definition of HFpEF, particularly its exclusion of severe valvular heart disease regardless of whether the lesion is primary or functional. Although these trials profoundly influenced the current HFpEF phenotype, they were primarily designed to establish the efficacy of pharmacological therapies rather than to comprehensively phenotype valvular disease across different haemodynamic phases.

Most importantly, the absence of severe valvular disease at enrolment, generally assessed under stable conditions and potentially remote from a previous episode of acute heart failure, does not exclude that atrial functional mitral or tricuspid regurgitation had transiently become severe during that hospitalisation and subsequently regressed after decongestion.

Furthermore, not all patients underwent echocardiographic assessment at enrolment. For example, in EMPEROR-Preserved left ventricular ejection fraction could be documented by echocardiography, radionuclide ventriculography, invasive angiography, cardiac MRI or CT, meaning that comprehensive echocardiographic assessment of valvular disease was not mandatory.7 Moreover, its exclusion criterion was limited to severe obstructive or regurgitant valvular disease expected to require surgery during the trial according to the investigator’s judgement.

Similarly, DAPA-HF specifically excluded heart failure due to uncorrected primary valvular disease rather than functional regurgitation per se.8 Thus, the populations that helped shape the contemporary HFpEF phenotype cannot be assumed to have been systematically free from severe functional regurgitation. Instead, by excluding severe valvular heart disease from the HFpEF diagnosis irrespective of whether it is primary or functional, contemporary guidelines may have obscured the role of severe functional regurgitation as a dynamic, phase-dependent mechanism of acute decompensation.

Therefore, we fully support the authors’ call for HFpEF-specific recommendations and propose that future recommendations incorporate the concept of longitudinal valvular phenotyping, integrating echocardiographic assessment during acute decompensation with reassessment after haemodynamic stabilisation, including objective documentation of congestion and of adequate decongestion.9,10 Such an approach may distinguish patients in whom severe functional regurgitation transiently becomes the dominant haemodynamic mechanism of acute decompensation from those with fixed structural valvular disease, while acknowledging that many of these patients may again fulfil contemporary HFpEF diagnostic criteria once regurgitation regresses below the threshold considered haemodynamically significant. Recognising atrial functional mitral and tricuspid regurgitation as dynamic, phase-dependent haemodynamic mechanisms of acute HFpEF decompensation, rather than viewing them solely as static exclusion criteria, may ultimately improve HFpEF phenotyping, interpretation of clinical trials and selection of patients for transcatheter or surgical intervention, as well as medical therapy.
