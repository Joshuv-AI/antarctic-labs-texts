Comment on “The Prevalence of Vitamin B12, Hemoglobin, and Ferritin Deficiency in Patients with Morbid Obesity and Changes in their Blood Levels after Bariatric Surgery”
Authors: Laiba Saher
Published: 2026

Dear Editor,

I recently reviewed the paper, “The Prevalence of Vitamin B12, Hemoglobin, and Ferritin Deficiency in Patients with Morbid Obesity and Changes in their Blood Levels after Bariatric Surgery.”[1] The author’s work is commendable as this study accurately addresses the relevant issue by evaluating the changes in ferritin, hemoglobin, and B12, following the bariatric surgery. This adds valuable insight to nutritional challenges faced by obese patients. However, the inclusion of some elements could further strengthen the study’s conclusions and clinical relevance.

Iron and hemoglobin levels are crucially influenced by the menopausal status. A total of 84.8% of the population was female; however, distinction between premenopausal and post-menopausal statuses was not considered. Premenopausal females are subjected to more iron loss, making them vulnerable to anemia. A contrasting study reported that young menstruating women had a higher incidence of postoperative iron deficiency after Roux-en-y gastric bypass (RYGB).[2]

Moreover, this study used only three markers (ferritin, B12, and hemoglobin) to evaluate the iron status and related deficiencies. However, different micronutrients like zinc, copper, folate, and albumin are commonly reduced after bariatric surgery and may lead to anemia or neurological symptoms even when iron and B12 are normal. Despite normal B12, folate deficiency can cause anemia and neurological disorders. Additionally, zinc and copper depletion can impair erythropoiesis and lead to immune disturbance. A recent study has highlighted that broad micronutrient screening beyond B12 and iron markers is beneficial following post-bariatric surgery.[3]

Persistent B12 deficiency despite supplementation could be due to genetic or intrinsic factors, such as methylenetetrahydrofolate reductase (MTHFR) and TCN2 (transcobalamin II), which can impair B12 transport, metabolism, and uptake. Without genotype analysis, the study overlooks the explanation of persistent deficiency. Therefore, it is a major limitation. In contrast, a case–control study demonstrated the association between the MTHFR gene and vitamin B12 deficiency in at-risk individuals.[4]

Furthermore, without inflammatory markers like C-reactive protein (CRP) and erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR), it is difficult to interpret the ferritin levels because ferritin is an acute-phase reactant and an iron storage as well. Additionally, methylmalonic acid (MMA) and homocysteine are more accurate markers of B12 activity. Serum B12 can be falsely normal as it measures the total B12 levels, including both active and inactive forms. B12 levels appear normal in some cases of deficiency as inflammation or supplementation. Patients can have normal serum B12 levels in macrocytic anemia or neurological symptoms. In contrast a study showed that MMA and homocysteine levels are more accurate to be analyzed than serum B12 levels, particularly in postoperative patients.[5]

In summary, the authors offer validated contributions to optimizing post-bariatric surgery analysis. However, some limitations were assessed, such as the absence of compliance monitoring, lack of stratification based on menstrual status, and narrow micronutrient scope underestimates the interpretation of findings; future studies should focus on the examination of specific dietary patterns post-surgery along with diagnostic procedure and functional biomarkers of B12, like MMA and homocysteine. Exploration of genetic polymorphism and individualized supplementation can also broaden out the scope of this study.
