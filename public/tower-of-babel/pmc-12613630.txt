Prognostic value of liver stiffness in patients with heart failure: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Irina-Maria Stoleru, Mihaela Mocan, Camil-Horea-Eusebiu Crișan, Lucia Elena Niculae, Romeo Ioan Chira
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractAimsHeart failure (HF) is a complex, multisystemic condition affecting 64 million people worldwide and often leads to cardio-hepatic syndrome due to venous congestion and ischemic liver changes. Liver stiffness (LS) measured through elastographic methods has emerged as a potential noninvasive marker for cardiac outcomes in HF patients. However, its prognostic value and the implications for LS cutoff values remain uncertain, necessitating further investigation.MethodsMultiple databases (PubMed, Embase, Scopus and the Cochrane Library Database) were searched up to 1st September 2024. Two reviewers screened records and extracted data according to the inclusion and exclusion criteria. The methodological quality was evaluated using the National Heart, Lung and Blood Institute (NHLBI) for observational cohort and cross-sectional studies. The meta-analysis was performed using RevMan 5.4 and the meta-regression and publication bias analyses (including Egger’s and Rank-correlation tests) were performed using JASP software, version 0.19.1.ResultsThirty-four studies involving 3753 individuals were included in our qualitative assessment, while 11 studies involving 1085 patients were included in the quantitative assessment. Elevated LS was significantly associated with adverse cardiac events in both acute decompensated HF (ADHF, hazard ratio (HR): 1.04, 95% CI: 1.01–1.07; I² = 61%) and chronic HF (CHF, HR: 1.09, 95% CI: 1.04–1.13; I² = 81%). Meta-regression revealed that LS cutoff values did not influence pooled estimates, and heterogeneity likely stemmed from demographic and methodological differences.ConclusionIncreased LS is associated with poor outcomes in patients hospitalized for HF, with a greater overall effect size in the CHF group than in the ADHF group. The LS is a promising noninvasive marker of liver congestion in HF and may aid both therapeutic decision-making and prognostication.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12872-025-05289-0.Keywords: Liver elastography, Liver stiffness, Liver elasticity, Transient elastography, Shear wave elastography, Heart failure

Introduction

Heart failure (HF) is a complex syndrome characterized by both increased morbidity and mortality, as well as changes in quality of life and significant costs, affecting 64 million people worldwide [1]. Pathophysiological changes in the heart also lead to negative effects on the functions of other organs, such as the kidneys and liver [2]. Prolonged stasis and ischemic changes in the liver caused by hypoperfusion result in the appearance of a distinctive syndrome called cardio-hepatic syndrome, wich is characterized by increased central venous pressure and important clinical implications, including congestive hepatic fibrosis and cardiac cirrhosis [3].

In patients with congestive heart disease, right heart catheterization is the gold standard for measuring central venous pressure. However, the procedure is invasive, not available in all centers, and not useful in the follow-up of the patients [4]. New noninvasive methods for assessing liver stiffness have replaced liver biopsy and allowed the study of thei prognostic value of liver stiffness in patients with various liver diseases in the absence of hepatic congestion caused by heart failure. However, recent studies have demonstrated that liver elasticity is directly influenced by central venous pressure [5] and the right-sided filling pressure of the heart, in the absence of chronic liver disease, and is thus increased in patients with hepatic congestion [6].

Although some observational studies indicate the potential clinical usefulness of liver stiffness for HF management, the association between liver stiffness (LS) and clinical outcomes in patients with heart failure remains uncertain and a clear cutoff value that indicates the risk of cardiovascular events has not been clearly defined.

Therefore, this review aimed to evaluate the prognostic value and performance of hepatic elastographic methods in the progression of heart failure.

Materials and methods

This systematic review and meta-analysis were reported in accordance with the recommendations from the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) checklist 2020 [7]. Prior registration of the study protocol was undertaken for this review on the PROSPERO (CRD42024595600). The PICOS strategy was used, which comprised the following:

P (population): patients hospitalized for heart failure, including acute decompensated heart failure and chronic heart failure;

P (population): patients hospitalized for heart failure, including acute decompensated heart failure and chronic heart failure;

I (intervention or exposure): liver stiffness measured using an elastographic technique (transient elastography and/or shear-wave elastography);

C (comparison): not applicable;

C (comparison): not applicable;

O (outcome): re-hospitalization/morbidity/mortality.

O (outcome): re-hospitalization/morbidity/mortality.

S (study design): observational cohort studies (prospective/retrospective), cross-sectional studies.

Information sources and search strategy

We aimed to review all the current evidence about liver elastography in heart failure patients published in the electronic databases PubMed, Embase, Scopus and the Cochrane Library Database, from inception up to 1 September 2024, without applying any search filters or restrictions on duration, country or language. The following search terms were used („liver elastography” OR „liver stiffness” OR „congestive hepatopathy” OR „shear wave elastography” OR „transient elastography” OR „acoustic radiation force impulse” OR „fibroscan 502” OR „shear wave elasticity” OR „shear wave dispersion”) AND („heart failure” OR „acute heart failure” OR „right-sided heart failure” OR „tricuspid valve regurgitation”) AND („outcome” OR „prognosis”). The steps mentioned above were performed according to the search strategy for PubMed. For other databases, every step has been adapted to their respective available filters. The complete literature search is provided in Supplementary Material S1. To minimize bias in the results, a manual search was conducted for relevant missed publications by searching the references of the included articles.

Eligibility criteria

The inclusion criteria for the original articles were as follows: (1) full-text studies of observational cohort population-based/hospital-based or cross-sectional designs that assessed liver elastography for heart failure; (2) hepatic elastography evaluated using imaging techniques such as transient elastography or shear wave elastography; (3) human studies; (4) studies published in English or Romanian languages.

The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) significant alcohol consumption; (2) confirmed hepatitis virus of any etiology; (3) primary liver disease, other than nonalcoholic fatty liver disease; (4) Fontan related liver disease studies; (5) children studies; (6) editorials, letters to the editor, case reports, book chapters, conference abstracts, literature and systematic reviews, practice guidelines, commentaries or studies with no full text available.

We defined prognostic studies as those reporting outcomes over time (time-to-event or cumulative incidence). Cross-sectional studies were not included in the prognostic meta-analysis and were used only to summarize baseline associations.

Selection process

First, independent searches were conducted by two reviewers across PubMed, Embase, Scopus, and the Cochrane Library. Records were merged and duplicates were removed in EndNote, version 21.4 [8]. Next, we performed a screening evaluation by assessing the titles and abstracts of all articles for relevance. The full texts of the selected articles that fulfilled the inclusion and exclusion criteria were reviewed. Eligible full-text articles were subsequently reviewed by two independent reviewers (S.I., C.C.) while resolving any discrepancies by mutual consensus.

Data collection process

From each article, the data were extracted separately in two spreadsheets by two independent investigators (S.I., C.C.). The following study characteristics were collected: title, authors, year of publication, country, study type, baseline characteristics of the patients, type of elastography, etiology of heart failure, composite endpoints, and median liver stiffness value.

Outcome

The primary outcome was the occurrence of one of the following: readmission for heart failure, death, worsening heart failure or a composite outcome (heart transplantation, left ventricular assist device, right ventricular assist device, major adverse events such as postoperative bleeding, cerebrovascular event and infection). Due to the heterogeneity in outcome reporting across studies, we pooled all available composite endpoints as reported by individual studies.

Study quality assessment

All studies included in the final analysis were assessed by two reviewers (S.I., C.C.). The risk of bias was evaluated using quality assessment tools from the National Heart, Lung and Blood Institute (NHLBI) for observational cohort and cross-sectional studies [9]. The scale results are tabulated in Supplementary Material S2. In cases of disagreement, a decision was reached through a discussion.

Statistical analysis

The meta-analysis was performed using the RevMan 5.4 software package [10]. For unanimity, the hazard ratio (HR) value chosen was LS per 1 kPa and if available, the adjusted value was selected. Hazard ratios were pooled using the Generic Inverse Variance method.

To explore sources of heterogeneity, we performed univariable meta-regression analyses using a random-effects model with restricted maximum likelihood (REML) estimation. We initially conducted meta-regression using liver stiffness cut-off values as the primary covariate of interest, as this was consistently reported across studies. We included only adjusted data when available, in order to account for potential confounding variables. The significance of moderator variables was assessed using the omnibus test of model coefficients, with P < 0.05 considered statistically significant. Residual heterogeneity was evaluated using the Q-statistic and I² values. We considered an I²<25% as low heterogeneity, I² of 25–75% as moderate heterogeneity and I²>75% as high heterogeneity.

The possible publication bias risk was assessed by the Egger’s test. While RevMan 5.4 was used to conduct the main meta-analyses, the meta-regression and publication bias analyses (including Egger’s and Rank-correlation tests) were performed using JASP software, version 0.19.1.

Results

General results

The initial search yielded 1540 potentially relevant papers (PubMed = 204 articles, EMBASE = 667 articles, Scopus = 636 articles, and Cochrane Library = 33 articles) as demonstrated in Fig. 1.

A total of 398 studies were detected as duplicates and removed. A total of 1089 studies were excluded during the first screening. Nineteen studies were excluded because of unavailable full text, unavailable full data or language other than English or Romanian. There were 34 articles included in the qualitative synthesis, out of which 11 articles were included in the quantitative synthesis.

This systematic review and meta-analysis included a total of 3753 individuals (382 individuals in cross-sectional studies, 3087 individuals in prospective cohort studies and 284 in a retrospective cohort study). Studies were conducted in Japan (n = 14), China (n = 6), Germany (n = 3), Turkey (n = 2), USA (n = 1), Australia (n = 1), Brazil (n = 1), South Korea (n = 1), Russia (n = 1) and other parts of Europe (n = 4), and were published between 2010 and 2024.

The sex distribution was greater for males (56.7% males and 43.3% females). The patients included in the studies had a mean age of 65 years and a mean BMI of 23,43 kg/m2.

The participants had a high incidence of comorbidities: at least 21.9% had diabetes mellitus, 42.7% had hypertension, 20.8% had dyslipidemia, 29.3% had atrial fibrillation, and 27.3% belonged to New York Heart Association (NYHA) class III/IV disease. The primary cause of heart failure was ischemia, for which the incidence was at least 22.8%.

Outcome

Acute decompensated heart failure (ADHF)

Out of the eight studies that included patients with acute decompensated heart failure (ADHF), five studies (comprising 413 patients) were included in the meta-analysis. These trials evaluated the prognostic value of liver stiffness measured by transient elastography (TE) and 2D-Shear Wave Elastography (2D-SWE), with results reported in kilopascals (kPa). Where available, adjusted hazard ratios accounting for patient characteristics such as age, sex, and cardiac dysfunction markers were included.

A forest plot was generated using the Generic Inverse Variance Method with a Random Effects Model (Fig. 2). The pooled analysis revealed a statistically significant association between elevated LS and composite endpoint, with a hazard ratio of 1.04 (95% CI: 1.01–1.07; P = 0.006). Although the effect size was modest, these findings suggest that a higher LS is associated with a slight increase in the risk of unfavorable clinical outcomes. However, there was evidence of moderate heterogeneity across the included studies (I² = 61%), indicating variability in effect estimates.

Forest plot assessing the association between liver stiffness and adverse cardiac events in patients with acute decompensated heart failure

To explore potential sources of heterogeneity, a univariable meta-regression analysis was performed using the liver stiffness cutoff value as the only covariate, as this was consistently reported across studies (Table 2). A random-effects model was applied with restricted maximum likelihood estimation. The omnibus test of model coefficients did not identify significant predictors of outcomes (Q = 0.007, P = 0.933), and the LS cutoff value showed no significant impact on the pooled estimate. Nonetheless, the residual heterogeneity test indicated significant unexplained variance between studies (Q = 10.033, P = 0.018). The I² value of 52.45% and H² index of 2.103 reflect considerable between-study heterogeneity, possibly accounting for patient demographics and various study designs.

Notably, three studies published by Omote, Yoshihisa, Ohara and their teams, comprising a large cohort of 607 patients, were excluded from our analysis because they utilized point shear wave elastography (pSWE) and supersonic shear wave detection (SWD) to measure LS in m/s or m/s/kHz rather than in kPa. Their exclusion was necessary to avoid introducing substantial heterogeneity into the analysis. Nevertheless, the large sample size of these studies underscores the importance of further studies employing alternative elastography techniques, with an emphasis on standardizing LS reporting to facilitate comparison across studies.

Chronic heart failure (CHF)

The chronic heart failure (CHF) cohort included eleven studies, but only six were considered for the meta-analysis, with a total of 672 patients. Like with the ADHF, liver stiffness was assessed using TE, with all measurements reported in kPa. The pooled analysis of CHF patients revealed a greater overall effect size than that of the ADHF group (Z = 3.77, P = 0.0002), with a pooled HR of 1.09 (95% CI: 1.04–1.13), indicating a more pronounced association between elevated liver stiffness values and composite endpoint in this cohort (Fig. 3).

Forest plot assessing the association between liver stiffness and adverse cardiac events in patients with chronic heart failure

Heterogeneity was considerably grater in the CHF meta-analysis, as reflected by an I² value of 81%, suggesting greater variability across studies compared than in the ADHF group. The meta-regression analysis for chronic heart failure patients showed that the LS cutoff did not significantly influence the heterogeneity observed among the studies (P = 0.680) (Table 3). However, similar to findings in ADHF patients, residual heterogeneity remained considerably high (τ² = 0.002, I² = 76.29%), underscoring a persistent level of unexplained variation likely stemming from other sources such as methodological or demographic differences across studies.

Certain studies were excluded from our analysis due to variations in reporting methods or potential outlier effects. For instance, van Kleef et al. reported only on high liver stiffness (LS) outcomes without comparable data for the low LS group, while Ihne-Schubert did not distinguish events by LS level. Lointier and Nakayama presented LS metrics differently from our standardized approach, utilizing hazard ratios for high LS rather than LS per 1 kPa as employed across our included studies. Additionally, the study by Urabe, with a notably small cohort of 36 patients, demonstrated a significant discrepancy from all other studies, yielding a hazard ratio three to five times greater, thereby raising potential outlier concerns.

Risk of bias assessment

The NHLBI quality assessment tool for observational cohort and cross-sectional studies was used to evaluate the methodological quality of the eligible studies included in the qualitative assessment of our review, as demonstrated in Supplementary Material S2. The risk of bias assessment revealed very high methodological quality. Six articles received an overall rating of „fair” while 28 studies were rated as „good”. The most important domains affected by bias were: (1) lack of sample size justification, (2) failure to fully account for confounding variables, and (3) the absence of blinding evaluation.

For the acute decompensated heart failure (ADHF) meta-analysis, both the Rank-Correlation and Egger’s tests suggested the presence of publication bias. The Kendall’s τ value was 1.000, with a significant p-value (P = 0.017), indicating that smaller studies may be disproportionately influence the meta-analysis. This finding was further supported by Egger’s test, which yielded a statistically significant result (z = 3.069, P = 0.002), confirming asymmetry in the funnel plot. Together, these results imply a possible publication bias, likely due to smaller studies showing greater effects that skew the pooled estimate.

In contrast, for the chronic heart failure (CHF) meta-analysis, there was no strong indication of publication bias. The Rank-Correlation test showed a nonsignificant Kendall’s τ of 0.333 (P = 0.469), suggesting little to no asymmetry in smaller studies. Egger’s test approaches significance (z = 1.882, P = 0.060), but it does not meet the conventional threshold, which indicates that any potential publication bias is less prominent than that of ADHF analysis (Fig. 4). These findings suggest that the CHF meta-analysis results are more likely to represent an unbiased estimate of the effect.

Discussion

Principal finding

This is an updated systematic review and meta-analysis in which we summarize the currently available evidence on the diagnostic accuracy of liver elastography in detecting liver congestion and the prognostic value of this noninvasive method in the evaluation of heart failure. Our findings suggest a significant association between elevated LS and composite endpoints with a stronger overall effect size in the CHF group than in the ADHF group, suggesting that LS is an independent, prognostic marker of cardiovascular outcomes in patients with heart failure without other known primary liver diseases.

The pooled hazard ratios for liver stiffness as a continuous variable (HR = 1.04 for ADHF and HR = 1.09 for CHF per 1 kPa increase) represent the incremental risk associated with each unit increase in LS. While these effect sizes appear modest for individual kPa increments, their clinical relevance becomes apparent when considering the typical range of LS values observed in heart failure patients. In clinical practice, patients often exhibit differences of 5–10 kPa between those with mild versus severe congestion, suggesting that the cumulative effect could correspond to a 20–50% increase in risk of adverse outcomes. Also, most included studies demonstrated that LS maintained prognostic significance after adjustment for established risk factors including age, ejection fraction, NYHA class, and natriuretic peptides. This suggests that LS provides additional risk stratification beyond conventional markers. Therefore, while individual unit increases in LS carry modest risk, larger differences observed across patient populations could help identify those at higher risk who may benefit from closer monitoring or more aggressive decongestion (persistently elevated LS despite optimal medical therapy might support earlier consideration of mechanical circulatory support or transplant evaluation).

Comparison with other studies

The findings of the present study build upon and extend the data presented in a recent meta-analysis investigating the prognostic significance of LS specifically within the ADHF population. This meta-analysis, by Macerola et al. [44] revealed a positive association between LS and an increased risk of cardiac events, reporting a hazard ratio of 1.07 (95% CI: 1.03–1.12), alongside significant heterogeneity (I2 = 86%). However, it categorized the studies by Taniguchi et al. [20] and Wang et al. [23] categorized the studies as focused on ADHF, despite the lack of explicit specification of this context. Notably, the meta-analysis also included the Omote et al. [15] study, which utilized point shear wave elastography. In our meta-analysis, the exclusion of this study was necessary to avoid introducing substantial heterogeneity into the analysis. We also conducted a meta-analysis of the CHF group and meta-regressions to explore whether that the LS cutoff value had a significant impact on the pooled estimate. To minimize bias, we used adjusted hazard ratios when reported, regardless of which covariates were included. However, due to inconsistent reporting of adjustment variables, we could not standardize or stratify by adjustment type. Instead, study design or patient demographics may underlie the observed high residual heterogeneity in our meta-analysis.

Explanation of results

Several studies have compared admission and discharge LS in patients admitted for ADHF [13, 19, 24, 30, 32–35, 37, 42, 44]. All but one of the eleven studies reported a significant decrease in LS after diuresis, valvuloplasty or left ventricular assist device placement. Furthermore, several studies [35, 41] have demonstrated a strong correlation between LS and NT-proBNP (r = 0.747, p < 0.001). Five studies [30, 35, 37, 42, 44] have shown that both LS and natriuretic peptides levels are significantly reduced following adequate diuresis. LS appears to provide complementary information to other noninvasive markers of congestive HF in demonstrating decongestion [45], though direct comparative studies are limited. Therefore, measuring the LS trajectory during the course of decompensated HF could provide information about the reversibility of cardiogenic liver injury. We can identify two phenotypes of the organ damage: „acute” with eventual normalization of LS and „acute on chronic” with persistent high LS observed at discharge. While most studies show a significant decrease in LS after intervention, a standard has not been established and it is unclear whether residual abnormalities in LS reflect congestion or underlying fibrosis. According to some studies [13, 20, 23], a higher LS at discharge is expected to be associated with negative outcomes, particularly a greater risk of HF readmission. Moreover, it is important to define the timing of LS. In some studies, it was obtained on admission, while in others, it was obtained at discharge. A single study (Soloveva et al.) investigated both options and revealed that only discharge LS was an independent risk factor for composite endpoints.

It is important to note the interaction between the liver and the heart: heart failure and liver disease frequently occur together, and heart failure itself can lead to irreversible liver damage [46]. Consequently, in some instances, an increase in liver stiffness may be attributed to both liver congestion and liver disease, even when primary liver disease from other causes has been ruled out [47]. Furthermore, it should be noted that liver biopsy data were unavailable in most of the referenced studies. In one study [48], where liver biopsy was performed in patients considered for ventricular assist devices or heart transplant, hepatic fibrosis was found in 79.7% of the patients, and 47% of those patients had severe fibrosis.

Strengths and limitations

The generalizability of this review is significantly constrained by the limited number of eligible studies and their small sample sizes. This restriction prevents the ability of conclusions applicable to diverse populations and clinical settings from being drawn.

We acknowledge that the substantial heterogeneity observed in our analysis, particularly in the CHF subgroup (I² = 81%), represents an important limitation. Although the meta-regression performed did not identify LS cutoff as a significant source of heterogeneity, this high variability can be explained by several potential sources. First, the studies included used different elastographic techniques (TE vs. 2D-SWE), and even among TE-based studies, devices from different manufacturers were employed, which may yield systematically different LS values. Second, patient selection varied considerably: some studies included only patients with preserved ejection fraction, others with reduced or recovered EF; baseline comorbidities (e.g., diabetes, atrial fibrillation) and NYHA class distributions also differed across cohorts. These differences in population characteristics may explain the variability in adverse event rates. Third, the definition of endpoints was not entirely uniform: some studies considered all-cause mortality and HF hospitalization, while others included additional events such as LVAD implantation or transplant. We were unable to perform separate analyses for individual outcome components (readmission alone, death alone, etc.) due to insufficient reporting of these endpoints across studies. Also, we restricted our quantitative synthesis to studies reporting continuous hazard ratios (per 1 kPa) to ensure methodological homogeneity. While this approach strengthened the validity of our pooled estimates, it resulted in the exclusion of several studies that reported only categorical analyses, potentially limiting the generalizability of our findings. Finally, differences in timing of LS measurement (admission vs. discharge) and in whether adjusted or unadjusted HRs were reported may have contributed to the observed heterogeneity. These factors highlight the need for future prospective multicenter studies to harmonize methodology and definitions to improve comparability.

For the acute decompensated heart failure group, Egger’s test for funnel plot asymmetry showed a statistically significant result (z = 3.069, P = 0.002), suggesting that the regression intercept was significantly different from zero and indicating potential small-study effects. In contrast, for the chronic heart failure group, Egger’s test did not show significant asymmetry (z = 1.882, P = 0.060), indicating a lower likelihood of publication bias. We would like to note that JASP version 0.19.1, the software used for these analyses, does not display the regression intercept or its confidence interval for Egger’s test. We acknowledge that publication bias assessments, such as Egger’s test or funnel plot interpretation, are typically considered unreliable when fewer than 10 studies are included in the analysis, due to limited statistical power and increased risk of false-positive or false-negative findings. Despite this limitation, we performed Egger’s and Rank-Correlation tests to explore potential asymmetry, given the clinical importance of the topic and to provide a transparent assessment. While we reported Egger’s test and qualitatively inspected funnel plots, we were unable to conduct DOI plots with the LFK index in this revision. However, we agree that these results should be interpreted with caution.

Although all the studies included in our meta-analysis demonstrated a strong relationship between LS values and the risk of cardiovascular events, we were unable to stratify this risk based on LS cutoff values due to the variability among the included studies, with median LS values between 4,8 and 40 kPa, obtained with equipment from different manufacturers. In our meta-analysis, we made a deliberate decision to exclude those studies using pSWE or SWD due to the incompatibility of LS measurement units (m/s or m/s/kHz vs. kPa). Although the excluded studies comprised a large sample, we judged that pooling effect estimates across incompatible measurement units would introduce considerable methodological heterogeneity and potentially bias the meta-analytic results. At present, there is no validated, standardized or universally accepted method to convert liver stiffness values from m/s to kPa across elastographic platforms, due to differing underlying physics and manufacturer algorithms. Attempting to harmonize these values through imputation or conversion would have compromised the reliability of the findings. Given the lack of unit compatibility and the small number of eligible studies, we did not perform a formal sensitivity analysis that included the excluded pSWE and SWD studies. Taniguchi et al. [20] demonstrated that a LS value >10,1 kPa yielded a sensitivity of 73% and a specificity of 90% for worse outcomes, while de Avilla et al. [22] showed that a cutoff value of 5,9 kPa had the best accuracy in predincting the primary outcome of death or hospitalization with a sensitivity of 80% and a specificity of 64,1%. These cutoffs values should be validated in a larger multicenter trial to further explore the application of LS as a therapeutic tool in treating decongestion, as well as a prognostic marker both alone and in conjunction with existing markers of disease severity.

It is important to note that all studies included in our meta-analysis were observational in nature, and although adjusted hazard ratios were used when available, the possibility of residual confounding remains. Unmeasured factors, such as concomitant subclinical liver disease, right ventricular dysfunction, or volume status at the time of measurement, may influence both LS and outcomes, potentially biasing the observed associations. Future prospective studies with standardized adjustment for key confounders and, ideally, randomized designs are needed to establish causality.

Clinical implication and future research

Our findings have several potential clinical implications. Liver stiffness assessment by elastography may represent a valuable tool to improve risk stratification in patients with both acute and chronic heart failure. In clinical practice, the identification of patients at higher risk of adverse outcomes remains challenging, as conventional markers such as natriuretic peptides and echocardiographic parameters, while informative, are not always sufficient to fully capture systemic congestion and multiorgan involvement. The liver, being a highly vascularized organ that directly reflects central venous pressure, offers a unique window into hemodynamic burden.

In this context, LS measurement could complement established risk markers and provide incremental prognostic information. Patients with elevated LS values may benefit from closer monitoring, more aggressive decongestive strategies, and tailored follow-up after discharge, particularly in the vulnerable post-hospitalization period. As a noninvasive, reproducible measurement, LS could replace or complement more invasive assessments of right heart pressures in selected patients, particularly in centers where right heart catheterization is not readily available. In addition, LS assessment could support clinical decision-making regarding the timing of advanced therapies, such as mechanical circulatory support or transplantation, by identifying individuals with more severe systemic involvement.

However, several barriers to clinical implementation remain. To reduce heterogeneity in future studies, we recommend: standardization of elastography techniques with rigorous operator training and measurement quality controls, uniform definition of clinical endpoint and clear description of evaluation timing (admission vs discharge), patient stratification based on HF severity, phenotype and comorbidities. Also, a possible future goal would be to determine a specific vendor-neutral cutoff value that helps to discriminate between patients at low or high risk for worse cardiovascular outcomes (similar to the ”rule of 5” for LS assessed by transient elastography and suggested by the Baveno VII [49] criteria to evaluate the risk of decompensation and liver-related death). The heterogeneity does not invalidate our results, but rather underscores the need for a personalized approach in using LS as a prognostic biomarker in HF.

Conclusions

Heart failure is a multisystemic disease that involves multiple organs, and the liver is particularly vulnerable to congestion. Liver stiffness provides additional information on conventional markers for future worse outcomes, extending mechanistic insight into cardiohepatic interactions. While further and ideally, randomized research is needed to fully illustrate its practical applications, liver elastography shows great promise as a novel tool for improving outcomes in the management of heart failure population.
