Protocol for condensate-based stabilization of gene circuit dynamics under growth-mediated dilution in E. coli
Authors: Rong Zhang, Xiao-Jun Tian
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryGrowth-mediated dilution can destabilize synthetic gene circuits by reducing intracellular concentrations of key regulatory proteins. Here, we present a protocol that uses liquid-liquid phase separation to buffer dilution of transcription factors and stabilize synthetic gene circuits in E. coli. We describe steps for constructing phase-separating self-activation circuits, characterizing condensate material properties by fluorescence recovery after photobleaching, and imaging of condensate-promoter colocalization. We outline single-cell microscopy and population-level plate reader assays to quantify circuit activation, growth-coupled dilution dynamics, and memory retention.For complete details on the use and execution of this protocol, please refer to Zhang et al.1Subject areas: Biotechnology and bioengineering, Cell-based Assays, Gene Expression, Microbiology, Molecular Biology, Protein Biochemistry, Systems biology

Before you begin

Growth-mediated dilution can destabilize synthetic gene circuits by reducing intracellular concentrations of key regulatory proteins.2 This protocol describes the implementation of condensate-based transcriptional circuits that use liquid-liquid phase separation (LLPS) to buffer growth-mediated dilution in Escherichia coli. The procedures outlined below were used in the associated Cell study to describe the step-by-step guide of constructing phase-separating self-activation circuits and quantifying circuit stability, dilution dynamics, and memory retention at the single-cell and population levels. Although optimized for E. coli K-12–derived strains, this workflow can be adapted to other bacterial systems for other gene circuits under dynamic growth conditions.

Innovation

This protocol establishes an integrated workflow that combines gene circuit engineering with quantitative functional assays to enable reproducible analysis of condensate-based regulation in bacterial systems. It unifies commonly used techniques, including restriction-ligation cloning, plate reader-based measurements of activation and growth-coupled dilution, and live-cell fluorescence microscopy, into a coordinated framework for evaluating circuit behavior across both population and single-cell scales. The protocol further adapts fluorescence recovery after photobleaching (FRAP) and promoter colocalization imaging in Escherichia coli, enabling direct characterization of transcriptional condensates in bacteria. In addition, it standardizes experimental procedures for systematically comparing phase-separating and non–phase-separating circuit architectures, providing a practical approach for quantifying dilution dynamics and memory retention. Together, these elements extend existing synthetic biology methods by integrating circuit design with imaging and quantitative assays in a single, reproducible workflow.

Step-by-step method details

This section describes the experimental workflow for constructing and characterizing LLPS–based synthetic gene circuits in Escherichia coli. The protocol details the assembly of phase-separating and non–phase-separating control circuits through fusion of IDRs to transcription factors, followed by induction and fluorescence-based measurement of circuit activity. It further outlines assays to quantify growth-coupled dilution and memory retention, microscopy-based visualization of condensate formation, and fluorescence recovery after photobleaching (FRAP) to measure condensate dynamics. Finally, the protocol describes imaging-based analysis of condensate–promoter colocalization.

Construction of gene circuits

Timing: 3–4 days for each restriction-ligation cycle

This section describes the construction of phase-separating and non–phase-separating control gene circuits, as well as colocalization gene circuits IC41 and CT149, using a standardized restriction-ligation cloning approach. The assembly is designed to be modular and reproducible, following the iGEM RFC 10 standard. A five-level hierarchical construction strategy is implemented:

Level 1: Insert multi-cloning sites into plasmid B0034-pSB1A2 to generate plasmid B0034-MCS1-pSB1A2 and to establish a scaffold for the open reading frames (ORFs) of fusion genes (Figure 1A).

Level 2: Insert DNA fragments encoding fusion genes into the scaffold to generate complete fusion gene ORFs. Ensure all components are flanked by RFC 10 prefix and suffix sequences to enable subsequent assembly using standard iGEM RFC 10 methods.

Level 3: Add promoters and terminators to the fusion gene and luxR ORFs to form monocistronic expression cassettes on the pSB3K3 backbone. This step completes the construction of both phase-separating and non–phase-separating control circuits (Figure 1B).

Level 4: Assemble three monocistronic operons to generate IC41 (Figure 1C).

Level 5: Incorporate a lacO array into IC41 to produce the final construct, CT149 (Figure 1C).

Limitations

The buffering capacity of condensate-based circuits depends on the saturation concentration for phase separation, which varies with temperature. As a result, circuit stabilization against growth-mediated dilution is temperature dependent. For different applications or growth conditions, IDRs may require further tuning based on their upper or lower critical solution temperature (UCST or LCST) behavior to ensure robust condensate formation.

Colocalization assays require simultaneous visualization of transcription factor condensates and promoter markers within the same cell. In some cells, only one signal is detectable, which can limit colocalization analysis. This variability likely arises from resource competition between the condensate-forming transcription factor and the LacI–RFP promoter marker, and should be considered when interpreting single-cell colocalization data.

Troubleshooting

Problem 1

Circuit induction is poor with the L-ara concentrations listed here.

Potential solution

Use the E. coli strain MG1655 ΔlacIΔaraCBAD as host cells for all phase-separation circuits to ensure consistent AraC-mediated induction and to prevent host arabinose metabolism.

Problem 2

No memory retention of the phase-separating self-activation circuits is observed after data analysis.

Potential solution

•Inducer concentration and temperature effects: Maintain the same inducer gradient (0∼50 ppm) for both initial OFF-state and initial ON-state test. Compare the initial OFF-state and initial ON-state curve produced under the same temperature.

•Nutrient level: Circuit behavior can vary substantially with medium composition and nutrient availability. Rich media (e.g., LB) promote faster growth and stronger dilution effects, often destabilizing bistable states in self-activation circuits. In contrast, nutrient-limited conditions slow dilution and can enhance memory retention. These conditions can be conveniently achieved by mixing LB with M9 medium in defined ratios to tune growth rate and resource availability.
