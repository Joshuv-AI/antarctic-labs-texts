Unique Opportunities and Challenges of Artificial Intelligence in Pediatric Imaging
Authors: Amit Gupta, Anjali Agrawal, Manisha Jana
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Artificial intelligence (AI) in pediatric imaging is evolving rapidly but lags behind and remains fundamentally distinct from adult radiology AI, due to various factors, such as developmental variability, differing disease epidemiology, unique imaging constraints, and limited data ecosystems. This narrative review examines the biological, technical, and structural factors that shape pediatric imaging AI, summarizes current and emerging applications, and highlights key barriers to safe clinical translation. Evidence indicates that the most immediate and impactful applications of pediatric AI lie in image acquisition, dose reduction, and workflow optimization, while interpretive and prognostic tools remain variably mature, with bone age assessment and chest radiograph interpretation representing the most established use cases. However, broader deployment is constrained by small, fragmented datasets, lack of standardized reference labels, limited generalizability, insufficient prospective validation, and gaps in regulatory and clinical integration frameworks. Furthermore, adult-trained models frequently underperform in children, reinforcing the need for pediatric-specific design and evaluation. The review emphasizes the importance of ethical governance, stakeholder trust, and multidisciplinary collaboration in guiding implementation. Future progress will depend on shared data infrastructures, federated learning approaches, rigorous age-stratified validation, and outcome-focused evaluation aligned with pediatric care priorities. Overall, pediatric imaging AI should be regarded as a distinct clinical, technical, and ethical domain requiring evidence-driven, child-centered development to ensure safe, effective, and equitable adoption in practice.

Introduction

Artificial intelligence (AI) has rapidly expanded across radiology, with applications now spanning image acquisition, pathology detection, quantification, reporting, workflow support, and education. 1 However, the development and validation of most radiology AI tools have been driven primarily by adult patient populations and clinical priorities. Pediatric imaging has therefore entered the AI era at a position of relative underrepresentation, despite having distinct clinical needs and a strong potential to benefit from AI solutions. 2 However, pediatric imaging must not be viewed simply as a delayed extension of adult radiology AI. Differences in anatomy, physiology, disease patterns, and imaging constraints across childhood, compared with adults, influence both image acquisition and interpretation, and also make it imperative that the pediatric AI must be specifically designed for children.

These differences create both exceptional opportunities and substantial challenges. In pediatric imaging, some of the most valuable AI applications may lie upstream of diagnosis, including dose reduction, faster scanning, workflow support, and longitudinal disease monitoring. At the same time, the field faces many challenges, including a scarce validation ecosystem, smaller fragmented datasets, and fewer commercially prioritized use cases, among others. As a result, children may be disadvantaged either by the absence of AI tools specifically developed for pediatric care or, perhaps more concerningly, by off-label use of adult-trained tools that have not been adequately validated for pediatric conditions. 3

This narrative review builds on the evolving literature and examines why pediatric imaging AI differs from adult radiology AI, summarizes current and emerging applications, discusses the unique barriers to development and deployment, and outlines priorities for safer and more evidence-driven progress in the field. The detailed discussion of the basic concepts and terminologies of medical imaging AI is beyond the scope of this review, and the readers are referred to other literature for this discussion. 4 5

This narrative review is based on literature identified through searches of PubMed, Google Scholar, and reference lists of relevant articles. Articles published from January 2005 to May 2026 were considered, with emphasis on recent peer-reviewed reviews, systematic and scoping reviews, society statements, white papers, consensus guidance, and original validation studies addressing clinically relevant pediatric imaging AI applications. Search terms included combinations of pediatric radiology, pediatric imaging, AI, machine learning, deep learning (DL), radiomics, bone age, chest radiograph, fracture detection, dose reduction, image reconstruction, workflow, triage, and foundation models. Studies were selected narratively according to relevance to the scope of this review, methodological quality, clinical importance, and ability to illustrate pediatric-specific opportunities, limitations, and implementation concerns. No formal systematic review or meta-analysis was performed.

Why Is Pediatric Imaging AI Fundamentally Different?

Pediatric imaging AI is fundamentally shaped by developmental heterogeneity, age-specific disease patterns, and unique acquisition constraints, including motion artifacts, limited patient cooperation, need for sedation, and dose-reduction protocols, all of which influence image quality and algorithm performance. As emphasized in recent multi-society guidance, pediatric AI cannot be treated as a derivative of adult models and instead requires explicitly child-centered design, validation, and governance frameworks. 6

Biological Differences

Pediatric imaging occurs across a continuum of growth and development, unlike adult imaging, where anatomy is relatively stable. Normal imaging appearances vary substantially from infancy through adolescence, reflecting changes in organ size, skeletal maturation, body composition, and brain development. Consequently, an imaging pattern that is physiological at one age may be abnormal at another. This makes pediatric datasets intrinsically heterogeneous and creates a setting in which age is a central determinant of image appearance, interpretation, and algorithm performance. 7 This developmental variability is a fundamental challenge for AI development.

Epidemiological Differences

Pediatric radiology encompasses a spectrum of congenital, developmental, inflammatory, infectious, and neoplastic disorders that differs substantially from adult practice. 8 Many pediatric conditions are individually uncommon, and even high-volume pediatric centers encounter limited case numbers. This constrains the development of large, balanced, and representative training datasets, thereby weakening the conventional data-intensive AI development pathway. The challenge is further compounded by fragmentation of pediatric cohorts across institutions, age groups, and imaging protocols, making external validation and model generalizability difficult. 9

Technical and Operational Differences

Pediatric imaging is frequently performed under conditions that differ fundamentally from adult practice, including motion, limited patient cooperation, smaller body size, lower signal-to-noise ratios, sedation or anesthesia requirements, and a stronger imperative to minimize radiation exposure. 10 These factors influence image quality, protocol selection, and examination feasibility. Consequently, in pediatric imaging, AI has particular relevance not only for image interpretation but also for upstream tasks such as acquisition optimization, image reconstruction, motion correction, dose reduction, and workflow support. 11

Structural and Economic Differences

Pediatric imaging volumes are lower than adult volumes, and the commercial incentives for developing and validating pediatric-specific AI tools are correspondingly weaker. This has resulted in a relatively sparse pediatric evidence base, fewer AI tools explicitly labeled for pediatric use, and slower clinical translation. Moreover, even among AI-enabled devices that include pediatric indications, detailed reporting of pediatric validation data is often incomplete or inconsistent. 12

Implications for Model Development and Deployment

Collectively, these factors underscore that adult-trained models cannot be assumed to generalize safely to pediatric populations. A recent scoping review demonstrated that adult-derived imaging AI models show reduced performance when applied to children, with the greatest decline observed in younger age groups. 13 Similarly, studies in pediatric chest radiography indicate that algorithms trained on adult datasets do not reliably generalize across pediatric age ranges or acquisition settings. 14 These findings reinforce the need to treat pediatric imaging AI as a distinct modeling domain, requiring age-aware data curation, pediatric-specific validation, and tailored model development to ensure safe, effective, and clinically meaningful deployment.

Image Acquisition, Dose Reduction, and Quality Control

AI is increasingly applied to optimize image acquisition and reconstruction. In computed tomography (CT), studies have demonstrated that DL-based reconstruction and denoising can reduce radiation dose by approximately one-third to two-thirds while preserving diagnostic quality. 15 In magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), AI-driven techniques enable faster acquisition, improved signal-to-noise ratio, and motion correction, which is particularly valuable in uncooperative children and may reduce the need for sedation. 16 17 18 Additional applications include protocol optimization, scan planning, and automated image quality assessment. Emerging approaches such as synthetic image generation and modality translation may further enable contrast reduction and alternative acquisition strategies. 13

Image Interpretation

Compared with adult radiology, pediatric interpretive AI includes fewer established applications and a broader range of emerging use cases.

Bone age assessment represents the most mature and widely adopted pediatric AI application. 14 It is a standardized, high-volume task with a well-defined reference framework, making it particularly amenable to algorithmic modeling. Clinical use of AI-assisted bone age tools has now extended over more than a decade ( Fig. 2 ), and large-scale initiatives such as the Radiological Society of North America Pediatric Bone Age Machine Learning Challenge have demonstrated high levels of accuracy, with mean absolute deviations of only a few months relative to expert assessment. 15 Commercially available systems have shown similarly strong performance in routine practice. 16 However, even this established use case illustrates the need for continued validation. Independent comparative studies have demonstrated variability across software platforms, particularly at the extremes of age and in atypical populations. 17

Chest radiography represents one of the most extensively studied domains for pediatric interpretive AI, but remains less mature in terms of consistent clinical deployment. The most extensively studied use case is pneumonia detection and classification ( Fig. 3 ). Systematic reviews, often based on mixed adult and pediatric datasets, report high diagnostic performance, with pooled area under the receiver operating characteristic curve typically in the range of 0.85 to 0.95. 18 However, these analyses consistently highlight important limitations, including heterogeneity in datasets, variability in reference standards, and a lack of robust external testing. Pediatric tuberculosis (TB) represents another particularly important application area, where AI may support diagnosis and triage in resource-limited settings, although robust prospective validation remains limited. 19 For example, out-of-the-box use of adult-trained computer-aided detection systems has been reported to achieve sensitivities as low as 19% for pediatric TB triage. 20 Although several commercial TB computer-aided detection systems now claim pediatric-adapted models, evidence for generalizability and prospective clinical effectiveness remains limited. 21 Few studies also explore the use of AI for line and tube position assessment on neonatal and pediatric chest radiographs. 22

Additional interpretive applications are emerging across a range of pediatric conditions, including hydrocephalus assessment through ventricular segmentation, detection of skull fractures, and identification of abdominal pathologies such as ileocolic intussusception. 24 25 Within pediatric neuroradiology, other exploratory AI applications include epilepsy imaging, particularly MRI-based detection and segmentation of focal cortical dysplasia and other subtle epileptogenic lesions, and developmental disorder assessment using brain age, morphometric, and functional connectivity-based models. 24 Additional pediatric-specific AI use cases have also been identified as development priorities by the American College of Radiology Data Science Institute Define-AI Directory. 26 These include applications such as craniosynostosis detection, identification of metaphyseal and posterior rib fractures in suspected child abuse, neonatal pneumatosis detection, automated musculoskeletal measurements including scoliosis and leg-length discrepancy assessment, pediatric hip ultrasound analysis, cerebrospinal fluid shunt detection, and automated foot radiograph measurements. Although many of these applications remain in early stages of development, they illustrate the breadth of clinically relevant pediatric problems that may benefit from future AI-enabled solutions.

Prognostication, Quantitative Imaging, and Longitudinal Assessment

Beyond detection and classification, AI in pediatric imaging is increasingly being explored for prognostication, quantitative analysis, and longitudinal disease monitoring. Pediatric oncology represents one of the most actively studied domains for these applications. Current research focuses on tumor segmentation, characterization, radiomics-based feature extraction, and prediction of treatment response and clinical outcomes. 27 28 Examples include prediction of molecular features such as MYCN amplification in neuroblastoma, as well as radiomics-based modeling of survival outcomes in Wilms tumor. 29 30 Emerging frameworks also emphasize multimodal integration, combining imaging features with clinical, genomic, and treatment data to support more comprehensive decision-making. However, despite these promising developments, most applications remain in early phases of validation, and robust prospective evidence supporting routine clinical use is still limited. 28

Chronic lung disease, particularly cystic fibrosis, exemplifies AI-enabled longitudinal imaging in pediatrics. AI-driven quantitative analysis of chest CT has been used to assess structural lung disease, including airway abnormalities, parenchymal changes, and mucus plugging, which can provide reproducible and sensitive measures of disease burden over time. 31 These approaches offer the potential to detect early structural changes, standardize longitudinal assessment, and reduce interobserver variability, all of which are critical in a disease characterized by gradual progression and repeated imaging follow-up. Emerging work across other subspecialties, such as pediatric neuroradiology and congenital heart disease imaging, highlights the potential of AI for segmentation, morphometric analysis, and functional quantification. 24 32

Reporting, Workflow, Triage, and Decision Support

AI is increasingly being integrated into radiology workflows for reporting, communication, triage, and clinical decision support. While large language models and multimodal systems show promise in report generation, summarization, structured reporting, and clinical documentation, pediatric-specific evidence remains limited. 33 Similarly, AI systems designed to prioritize urgent findings, such as intracranial hemorrhage or fractures, have demonstrated utility in adult workflows ( Fig. 6 ). However, multi-society guidance emphasizes that such systems must not be assumed to perform equivalently in children without explicit pediatric validation. 6 Inaccurate prioritization may lead to clinically significant errors, underscoring the need for rigorous evaluation and continuous monitoring in pediatric deployment.

Educational and Research Applications

AI also supports education and research in pediatric radiology, extending its impact beyond direct clinical care. 34 In education, AI can facilitate the creation and curation of teaching datasets, enable case-based learning through reverse image search and automated case retrieval, and provide structured feedback to trainees. 35 Generative models may also support simulation-based learning, question generation, and adaptive educational content tailored to learner needs. These applications are particularly valuable in pediatric radiology, where exposure to rare conditions is limited and teaching resources may be unevenly distributed across institutions. 36

In research, AI facilitates large-scale data extraction, cohort identification, and analysis from imaging and report repositories. Techniques such as natural language processing can be used for structured data extraction, report mining, and case finding, supporting retrospective studies and the development of annotated datasets. 37 In pediatric imaging, where data scarcity remains a major constraint, AI-assisted curation and harmonization may play a key role in advancing the field.

Unique Roadblocks to AI Development and Deployment in Pediatric Imaging

Despite rapid methodological advances, the development and deployment of AI in pediatric imaging remains constrained by a set of interrelated, largely structural challenges that extend beyond algorithmic performance. These limitations contribute to the relative lag of pediatric AI compared with adult radiology in both evidence maturity and clinical integration.

Limited and Fragmented Datasets

The most fundamental barrier is the scarcity and fragmentation of pediatric imaging data. Available datasets are typically small, heterogeneous, and underrepresented in public repositories and commercial pipelines, with additional fragmentation across institutions, age groups, and demographics. 38 As a result, the primary bottleneck in pediatric AI remains upstream, at the level of data infrastructure rather than algorithmic innovation. Without sufficiently large, diverse, and longitudinal datasets, model generalizability remains limited.

Lack of Standardized Reference Standards and Labels

Generating high-quality ground truth is particularly challenging in pediatric imaging, which often requires subspecialist annotation, age-specific labeling, and integration of clinical context. 10 Interobserver variability is common, and many findings exist along developmental continua rather than fixed thresholds. These factors limit label reliability and constrain achievable model performance.

Limited Generalizability

Developmental heterogeneity and data scarcity predispose models to being highly sensitive to age, acquisition conditions, and disease spectrum. Adult or mixed-dataset models often fail to capture pediatric-specific patterns, leading to reduced performance, misclassification, and unreliable triage in clinical settings. 39 Beyond developmental variability, model performance may also be affected by demographic, socioeconomic, institutional, geographic, scanner, vendor, and protocol-related differences. Equitable implementation therefore requires local validation and, where feasible, subgroup performance assessment across relevant age groups, clinical settings, disease prevalence patterns, and imaging acquisition environments.

Ethical, Social, and Governance Challenges

Pediatric AI raises distinct ethical and social considerations. Children represent a vulnerable population with limited autonomy, reliance on caregivers, and a longer time horizon over which potential harms may manifest. Ethical frameworks specific to pediatric AI emphasize principles such as beneficence, non-maleficence, justice, transparency, accountability, and trust. Issues related to consent, privacy, data sharing, and accountability are amplified in multi-institutional collaborations. 6 While approaches such as federated learning can mitigate data-sharing constraints, they do not fully resolve issues related to data heterogeneity, governance, or accountability. 40 Regulatory frameworks for AI-enabled medical devices are also evolving across jurisdictions, with shared emphasis on intended use, risk classification, clinical evidence, transparency, human oversight, and post-market monitoring. For pediatric imaging, these requirements are particularly important because performance may vary by age, disease prevalence, scanner protocol, and local workflow. Adaptive or periodically updated algorithms require additional safeguards, including predefined update plans, documentation of model changes, performance surveillance, and clear accountability among developers, institutions, clinicians, and regulators. 41 42

Limited Prospective Validation and Translational Infrastructure

Most pediatric AI studies remain retrospective, single-center, and limited in scope, with relatively few prospective, multi-institutional validation studies. 34 This gap between reported performance and real-world readiness represents a major barrier to clinical translation. In practice, this creates uncertainty regarding the applicability of available tools to pediatric populations, particularly in mixed adult-pediatric clinical environments. A recent multi-society statement emphasizes the need for transparent reporting, pediatric-specific validation, and post-deployment monitoring. 6 Furthermore, reporting standards such as CLAIM, SPIRIT-AI, CONSORT-AI, and TRIPOD-AI are increasingly recognized as essential tools for ensuring methodological rigor and reproducibility. 43

Challenges in Clinical Integration and Trust

Successful deployment of pediatric AI depends on seamless integration into clinical workflows and acceptance by clinicians, patients, and families. While pediatric radiologists and other stakeholders generally support AI for improving efficiency and accuracy, there is a strong preference for human oversight. 44 Building trust will require transparent performance reporting, clear definition of intended use, and deployment models that augment rather than replace clinical expertise. Beyond algorithmic performance, clinical deployment also depends on workflow integration, information technology infrastructure, staff training, clinician acceptance, maintenance, reimbursement or funding models, economic evaluation, and operational feasibility. Future implementation studies should therefore assess not only diagnostic accuracy, but also effects on turnaround time, workload, user behavior, safety, cost, and patient-centered outcomes.

AI in Pediatric Imaging in India and the Indian Subcontinent

AI has particular relevance in India, the Indian subcontinent, and other low- and middle-income countries, where pediatric disease burden is high, imaging volumes are large, and access to subspecialty expertise is uneven. In such settings, AI can support triage, workflow prioritization, and decision-making across high-volume public hospitals, peripheral centers, and teleradiology networks.

Differences in disease patterns, body habitus, and imaging practices limit the generalizability of models developed in high-income settings. At the same time, India's large and diverse patient population provides an opportunity to develop representative datasets through multicenter collaboration. Medical Imaging Datasets for India (MIDAS) is an India-focused initiative intended to create standardized, quality-graded imaging datasets that better represent Indian patients and support AI development for Indian health care settings. 45 46 The ICMR (Indian Council of Medical Research) National Health Research Data Repository similarly reflects the increasing national emphasis on structured health-data access for research and data-driven health innovation. 47 These developments are important because many widely used public imaging datasets have historically come from non-Indian populations, and the lack of high-quality annotated health data for South Asian and Indian populations has been identified as a major barrier to AI development and validation in India. For pediatric AI, the dataset problem is further complicated by the necessity and lack of clinical metadata such as age, gestational maturity, and growth parameters, longitudinal outcomes, scanner/protocol details, radiation dose information, and expert pediatric radiology labels. AI adoption should prioritize local validation, pediatric-specific datasets, and context-relevant applications. Without this, AI risks reinforcing rather than reducing disparities in pediatric imaging care.

Chest radiography is a key application, given the burden of pneumonia and TB. While AI may aid non-specialist interpretation and triage, adult-trained models perform poorly in children, underscoring the need for pediatric-specific, locally validated tools. An AI tool validated for adult TB screening or adult chest radiography cannot be assumed to be valid for pediatric pneumonia, pediatric TB, neonatal intensive care radiographs, or pediatric emergency radiology. The absence of locally validated pediatric chest radiograph AI tools for Indian practice is a hurdle that needs to be overcome. Radiation dose optimization in CT is another priority, where AI-based reconstruction and protocol optimization can improve safety in variable-resource settings. Ultrasound, which is relatively inexpensive, portable, and radiation-free, is widely used in pediatric imaging and offers opportunities for AI-assisted acquisition and interpretation, particularly for non-expert operators. However, deployment in India must align with the Pre-Conception and Pre-Natal Diagnostic Techniques (PC-PNDT) Act, requiring careful governance to prevent misuse while supporting legitimate pediatric applications. Any AI tool for ultrasound images in India must therefore be designed with explicit safeguards to prevent misuse for fetal sex determination, which begins at the level of clinical use case, dataset curation, training, implementation, and communication of AI outputs. 48

Teleradiology combined with AI can be transformative in the Indian setting by helping with pre-screening studies, flagging suboptimal image quality, identifying critical findings, and prioritizing cases for subspecialist review. For this to be used effectively, teleradiology must have a suitable environment supported by Digital Imaging and Communications in Medicine (DICOM)-compatible systems, RIS/PACS (Radiology Information System/Picture Archiving and Communication System) integration, robust image archives, and a reliable image transmission infrastructure. 49 Akin to AI in conventional pediatric radiology practices, teleradiologists must stay in the loop, documentation of whether AI was used is a must, and autonomous reporting must be avoided unless supported by rigorous prospective evidence and regulation.

Society-Led Coordination and Multisector Collaboration

Professional organizations are increasingly defining priorities for pediatric imaging AI, including education, data stewardship, multicenter research, and implementation guidance. These efforts highlight the need for structured collaboration among clinicians, researchers, regulators, industry, and patient communities. 3 6 50 In this context, professional societies play a critical role beyond advocacy, in the development of shared standards, training frameworks, and coordinated research agendas.

Shared Data Infrastructures and Collaborative Research Ecosystems

Given the inherent scarcity of pediatric datasets, progress will rely on federated or privacy-preserving data-sharing approaches, alongside the development of registries, benchmarking platforms, and multi-reader curated datasets with standardized annotations and metadata. 38

Pediatric-Specific Evaluation Frameworks

Current evidence highlights the limitations of aggregate performance metrics and the need for age-stratified analysis, subgroup validation, and external testing across diverse populations. Future studies should incorporate prospective designs, clinically meaningful endpoints, and post-deployment monitoring to assess real-world impact. In pediatric imaging, these endpoints may extend beyond diagnostic accuracy to include reduction in radiation exposure, decreased need for sedation, improved examination success rates, and equitable performance across age groups. 6 12 Future research should also prioritize pediatric benchmark datasets and standardized evaluation frameworks that include age-stratified performance, calibration, uncertainty estimation, and clinically meaningful outcomes. Synthetic data generation and continual learning may help address data scarcity and model adaptation, but require careful validation to avoid bias amplification, data leakage, and unsafe performance drift.

Emerging AI Models and Methodologies

Foundation models and transfer learning approaches offer promising strategies to address data scarcity in pediatric imaging. 51 Self-supervised learning enables representation learning from large volumes of unlabeled data, while radiology reports can serve as complementary supervision signals. These strategies may help improve model performance in settings where high-quality labeled pediatric datasets are limited. In addition, multimodal foundation models integrating imaging, text, and clinical data may reduce annotation burden and support more comprehensive clinical modeling, although current evidence remains fragmented and evaluation remains complex. 37 Vision-language models and large multimodal models extend this concept by linking imaging findings with reports, clinical data, and prior examinations, with potential applications in image-text retrieval, draft reporting, education, and decision support. Generative and agentic AI systems may further support synthetic data generation, report simplification, protocoling, triage, and longitudinal follow-up, but their pediatric use remains exploratory because limitations such as hallucination, privacy risk, bias, tool-use errors, and lack of age-specific validation remain unresolved. 52

Strengthening Human and Institutional Infrastructure

Education is a key component, with increasing emphasis on equipping radiologists and allied professionals with the knowledge and skills required to evaluate and use AI tools effectively. In pediatrics, this educational effort should extend to technologists, referring clinicians, administrators, and developers, reflecting the multidisciplinary nature of care. Equally important is the inclusion of patients and families in the AI ecosystem to build trust and ensure that AI systems are aligned with pediatric care priorities. 44

Conclusion

AI in pediatric imaging offers exciting opportunities, but its safe use requires recognition of pediatrics as a distinct clinical and scientific domain, rather than an extension of adult radiology. While applications such as image optimization and bone age assessment demonstrate meaningful progress, many tools remain insufficiently validated for routine clinical use in children. Future progress will require robust pediatric-specific datasets, age-stratified validation, transparent reporting, prospective evaluation, and sustained clinical oversight to ensure safe, effective, and equitable implementation.
