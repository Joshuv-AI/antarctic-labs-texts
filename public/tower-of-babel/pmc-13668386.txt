Association Between Serum Trace Elements and Obstructive Sleep Apnea in Iranian Adults: Multiple-exposure Models
Authors: Habibolah Khazaie, Samaneh Nakhaee, Zohreh Manoochehri, Omran Davarinejad, Ayoob Rezaei, Borhan Mansouri, Adele Elahi, Saba Kakaei
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectivesThe association between trace elements and obstructive sleep apnea (OSA) remains an active area of research, and further studies are needed to clarify these relationships. This study examined correlations between serum essential trace element concentrations and polysomnography-confirmed OSA.MethodsThis cross-sectional study included patients recruited from the Sleep Disorders Center of Farabi Hospital in western Iran. Participants were classified as patients with OSA (n=54) or controls without OSA (n=45).ResultsIron (Fe) levels were significantly higher in participants with OSA than in controls (p=0.04). Higher Fe levels were associated with greater odds of OSA (p for trend=0.02). Participants in the fourth Fe quartile had higher odds of OSA than those in the lowest quartile, although this association was of borderline statistical significance (odds ratio [OR], 3.00; 95% confidence interval [CI], 0.96 to 10.00; p=0.06). In contrast, participants in the second and third magnesium (Mg) quartiles had lower odds of OSA than those in the first quartile (Q2: OR, 0.31; 95% CI, 0.09 to 0.96; Q3: OR, 0.26; 95% CI, 0.07 to 0.82). In the generalized weighted quantile sum regression model, the association between the metal mixture and OSA was not statistically significant (β=–0.22, p=0.59).ConclusionsSerum concentrations of selected micronutrients may be associated with OSA. The findings suggested a positive association between higher Fe concentrations and OSA, whereas intermediate Mg concentrations were associated with lower odds of OSA.

INTRODUCTION

Sleep disorders include several conditions that disrupt normal sleep patterns, such as insomnia, episodic sleep disorders, sleep apnea, and restless legs syndrome [1]. These disorders can substantially impair daily functioning and learning ability. Chronic sleep problems are also associated with several non-communicable diseases, including obesity, hypertension, diabetes, cardiovascular disease, and psychiatric or neurological disorders [2,3]. The prevalence of sleep disorders has increased markedly in recent decades. Epidemiological studies suggest that the global prevalence of these disorders ranges from 9.0% to 36.5% [4,5]. In the United States, recent reports indicate that approximately 27.1% of adults experience sleep disorders [6]. In China, the pooled prevalence has been estimated at 35.9%, with a higher burden in rural populations than in urban populations [4]. Obstructive sleep apnea (OSA) is a common sleep-related respiratory disorder characterized by chronic intermittent hypoxia and disrupted sleep architecture [7]. This condition involves recurrent upper-airway obstruction during sleep, resulting in sleep-disordered breathing. OSA is associated with several serious health outcomes, including pulmonary and systemic hypertension, myocardial infarction, stroke, cognitive impairment, daytime sleepiness, reduced concentration, depression, irritability, and anxiety [8,9]. In some cases, OSA may also be accompanied by depressive symptoms and behavioral disturbances, including suicide attempts [10]. Numerous biological, environmental, and nutritional factors influence sleep quality. In recent years, exposure to various metals—both toxic and essential—has received attention as a potential contributor to sleep problems [11–13]. Essential trace elements are particularly important because of their roles in physiological processes, including cellular signaling, oxygen metabolism, protection against oxidative stress, and neurotransmitter synthesis [3]. Maintaining optimal concentrations of essential trace elements is necessary for growth, metabolism, and neurological and immunological function [14]. In contrast, either excess or deficiency of these elements may contribute to or worsen various health conditions. Various disorders may also disrupt trace element homeostasis [15].

Recent studies have examined associations between trace element exposure and OSA prevalence. Asker et al. [16] reported that serum levels of several elements—including cadmium (Cd), cobalt (Co), copper (Cu), Iron (Fe), magnesium (Mg), manganese (Mn), lead (Pb), and zinc (Zn)—were significantly higher in individuals with OSA. In contrast, another review reported that patients with OSA often have lower serum Mg levels than healthy individuals. Although these Mg levels may remain within the normal reference range, they appear to correlate with biomarkers related to lipid profiles, glucose metabolism, calcium (Ca), and heavy metals [17]. Previous research has improved understanding of the relationship between trace elements and sleep, but much of this work has focused on sleep duration [13,18,19]. Fewer studies have evaluated associations between essential trace elements and sleep quality or sleep disorders, and their findings have often been inconsistent. Although earlier studies have examined associations between trace elements and OSA, the present study aimed to comprehensively evaluate serum concentrations of major trace elements in a clinical sample of patients with polysomnography-confirmed OSA. Unlike studies focused on individual trace elements, this study used a broader analytical approach to examine the potential interplay among multiple elements.

Metabolic disturbances associated with OSA may directly influence essential trace element homeostasis. Intermittent hypoxia, a hallmark of OSA, may upregulate proteins involved in Fe metabolism, such as hepcidin, through activation of hypoxia-inducible factor-1α (HIF-1α) [20]. This process may contribute to abnormal Fe accumulation in various tissues. Fe overload, in turn, promotes reactive oxygen species (ROS) generation and oxidative stress, which may exacerbate systemic inflammation and endothelial dysfunction and thereby amplify OSA-related pathology [21]. Conversely, deficiencies in elements such as Mg may impair anti-inflammatory responses and disrupt upper-airway muscle relaxation, increasing airway resistance and contributing to OSA pathophysiology [22]. Altered trace element levels can therefore be interpreted within a framework of hypoxia-related and oxidative stress–related dysregulation of cellular signaling networks, which may influence disease severity and complications [23]. This study investigated the relationship between serum concentrations of essential trace elements and polysomnography-confirmed OSA among patients with sleep disorders referred to Farabi Hospital in Kermanshah province, Iran.

METHODS

The study was designed as a cross-sectional investigation including 99 participants: 54 patients diagnosed with OSA and 45 controls without OSA. Participants were recruited from the Sleep Disorders Center at Farabi Hospital, affiliated with Kermanshah University of Medical Sciences. The study methods followed relevant guidelines and regulations. Blood samples were collected from all participants at the Sleep Disorders Center of Farabi Hospital after an overnight fast of at least 8 hours to minimize the influence of diet on serum trace element concentrations. After collection, samples were centrifuged to separate serum for analysis. Participants were categorized into 2 groups: patients diagnosed with OSA and healthy controls with no history of sleep disorders or neurological or psychiatric conditions. Eligibility for the patient group required a primary diagnosis of OSA and symptoms lasting at least 3 months. For screening, participants completed 2 standardized questionnaires: STOP-BANG (Snoring, Tiredness, Observed apnea, high blood Pressure, Body mass index [BMI], Age, Neck circumference, Gender) and the Epworth sleepiness scale (ESS). Participants who screened positive in 2 or more categories on these questionnaires were classified as being at high risk for OSA. The diagnosis was then confirmed by polysomnography. Control participants were recruited from individuals visiting the Sleep Disorders Center for reasons unrelated to sleep-disordered breathing, including accompanying family members, routine health check-ups, or consultations for non-sleep-related neurological issues. All controls underwent the same screening process as the OSA group, including completion of the STOP-BANG and ESS questionnaires. Controls had no symptoms or history of sleep disorders, snoring, or daytime sleepiness. They also reported no diagnosis of neurological, psychiatric, or chronic respiratory conditions. This approach was intended to ensure that the control group represented individuals without OSA and to minimize potential referral bias.

The study used predefined exclusion criteria to improve the interpretability of the findings. Individuals receiving treatment for central or OSA and night-shift workers were excluded. Participants were also excluded if they had respiratory illnesses requiring medication or chronic health conditions such as heart failure, cancer, infectious disease, or liver or kidney disorders. Pregnant or breastfeeding women were excluded, as were patients diagnosed by a physician with narcolepsy, idiopathic hypersomnia, chronic insomnia, restless legs syndrome, or other sleep-related conditions. To reduce potential confounding from external sources of trace elements, participants who reported regular use of dietary supplements, including multivitamins and mineral blends, were excluded during initial questionnaire screening and this information was confirmed in follow-up interviews. This procedure helped ensure that measured serum trace element concentrations reflected each participant’s physiological baseline without the influence of supplementation (Supplemental Material 1).

Blood serum samples were subjected to acid digestion before trace element analysis. All sample digestions used ultrapure nitric acid and ultrapure water (≥18.2 MΩ·cm at 25°C; WSC044; Fistreem, Cambridge, UK), and all solutions were stored in acid-washed perfluoroalkoxy polymer containers to minimize trace metal contamination and support accurate elemental analysis. Initially, 1 mL of each serum sample was transferred into a 25-mL glass tube. A nitric acid–perchloric acid mixture was used for digestion, beginning with the addition of 2 mL of 60% nitric acid (HNO3; Merck, Darmstad, Germany) to each sample. Samples were then left at room temperature for 1 day to allow gradual digestion. After this incubation period, 1 mL of 70–72% perchloric acid (HClO4; Merck) was added to the mixture. Samples were then placed in a hot water bath (bain-marie, TW12; Julabo GmbH, Seelbach, Germany) at 98°C for 4 hours to complete digestion. After digestion, samples were brought to a final volume of 5 mL with ultrapure water. The prepared samples were analyzed for trace elements (Ca, Co, Cu, Fe, Mg, Mn, selenium [Se], and Zn) using inductively coupled plasma (ICP) analysis (ELAN 6000; Perkin-Elmer, Concord, ON, Canada). All sample preparation and analytical procedures, including digestion, handling, contamination control, and ICP analysis, were outsourced to a certified external laboratory. These procedures were performed by a qualified technical team accredited to ISO/IEC 17025 to ensure accuracy, impartiality, and data credibility. To improve accuracy and minimize matrix interference, enriched internal standards were added at consistent concentrations to all samples and calibration solutions. This approach helped correct instrumental drift and supported reliable measurement across samples. Recoveries for all elements ranged from 95% to 101%. Accuracy was assessed by conducting 10 analyses of both spiked fractions and control samples, with results adjusted to align with the midpoint of the working range. The entire process of sample preparation and trace element measurement was outsourced to Aria Shimi Sharif laboratory in Karaj, Iram.

Quantitative variables were summarized as means and standard deviations. Between-group differences were assessed using Student’s t-test. Categorical variables were summarized as frequencies and percentages, and group distributions were compared using the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test. Because trace element concentrations showed skewed distributions, these values were presented as medians and interquartile ranges. Spearman correlation coefficients were calculated to examine correlations between trace element concentrations, and these relationships were visualized using a heat map. Given the high dimensionality of the data, least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) regression was used to identify the most relevant clinical variables associated with OSA. To investigate the association between trace element exposure and OSA, 3 logistic regression models were constructed. Model 1 included each trace element individually. Model 2 included each trace element and potential confounders, including age, gender, BMI, smoking, tea consumption, and underlying disease [12,16]. Model 3 included each trace element and the relevant variables identified by LASSO regression.

In the logistic regression analyses, the second through fourth quartiles of each metal exposure were compared with the first quartile, which served as the reference group. Odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were reported to characterize these associations. In addition, generalized weighted quantile sum (GWQS) regression was performed to evaluate the combined association of the metal mixture with OSA. Model parameters were estimated using 100 bootstrap iterations, and internal validation was conducted by randomly partitioning the data into a training subset (60%) and a validation subset (40%). The direction of the mixture effect was not constrained, allowing both positive and inverse associations. The weight of each metal was also calculated individually to estimate its contribution to OSA. The glmnet package was used for LASSO regression and the gWQS package was used for GWQS regression in R version 4.5.2 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).

Ethics Statement

All participants provided written informed consent. This study was approved by the Institutional Research Ethics Committee of Kermanshah University of Medical Sciences, Kermanshah, Iran (IR.KUMS.REC.1403.424).

RESULTS

Characteristics of Participants

The demographic and clinical characteristics of the 99 participants were analyzed by OSA status: 45.5% did not have OSA and 54.5% had OSA. These findings are summarized in Supplemental Material 2. Participants in the OSA group were significantly older than those without OSA (mean age, 49.80±12.02 vs. 41.53±12.25 years; p=0.001). The OSA group also had significantly higher mean BMI, neck circumference, and waist circumference than the non-OSA group (BMI: 31.30±5.61 vs. 25.74±4.49 kg/m2; neck circumference: 39.91±4.07 vs. 36.29±3.19 cm; waist circumference: 103.11±13.16 vs. 87.84±10.53 cm; all p<0.001). Significant between-group differences were also observed in the apnea-hypopnea index (AHI), respiratory disturbance index (RDI), snoring index, and ESS scores (all p<0.001). Mean scores were higher in the OSA group than in the non-OSA group for AHI (28.40±25.03 vs. 1.99±1.57), RDI (30.15±25.41 vs. 2.37±1.67), snoring index (338.00±274.16 vs. 94.60±155.27), and ESS score (9.43±4.92 vs. 4.40±5.55). Tea consumption was also higher among participants with OSA than among those without OSA (4.78±2.72 vs. 3.31±3.28 cup/day; p=0.02). Most participants in the OSA group (83.3%) reported snoring during sleep, and snoring status differed significantly between groups (p<0.001). Berlin questionnaire scores also differed markedly between groups, with 75.9% of participants in the OSA group classified as high risk, indicating a high prevalence of OSA-related symptoms in this study sample.

Characteristics of Trace Elements

Table 1 presents trace element concentrations in participants with and without OSA. Fe concentrations were significantly higher in the OSA group than in the group without OSA (p=0.04). Figure 1 shows the correlation analysis for the 8 trace elements. Correlation coefficients ranged from −0.19 to 0.43, indicating weak to moderate correlations among the elements. The strongest observed correlation was a moderate positive correlation between Co and Se (r=0.43).

Associations Between Trace Elements and Obstructive Sleep Apnea

The features selected by LASSO regression as having relevant associations with OSA are shown in Figure 2. At a log(λ) value of 0.03, 5 features—the Berlin questionnaire score, snoring, ESS score, tea consumption, and neck circumference—were selected and entered into model 3. The results of the 3 logistic regression models are presented in Table 2. Model 1 examined the association between each metal and OSA individually, whereas model 2 included each metal with potential confounders. In model 1, Fe and Mg concentrations were associated with OSA. A significant positive trend was observed between Fe concentration and the odds of OSA (p for trend=0.02). Participants in the highest serum Fe quartile (Q4) had 3.00-fold higher odds of OSA than those in the lowest serum Fe quartile (Q1), although this estimate was of borderline statistical significance (OR, 3.00; 95% CI, 0.96 to 10.00; p=0.06). Mg concentration showed an inverse association with the odds of OSA. Participants in the second Mg quartile (Q2) had lower odds of OSA than those in Q1 (OR, 0.31; 95% CI, 0.09 to 0.96; p=0.04), and those in the third Mg quartile (Q3) also had lower odds of OSA than those in Q1 (OR, 0.26; 95% CI, 0.07 to 0.82; p=0.02). For Se, a borderline inverse association was observed in Q3 (OR, 0.31; 95% CI, 0.09 to 0.98; p=0.05). In model 2, which adjusted for potential confounders, only Mn in Q4 showed a borderline positive association with OSA. Specifically, participants in the fourth Mn quartile had 5.09-fold higher odds of OSA than those in Q1 (OR, 5.09; 95% CI, 1.07 to 28.90; p=0.05). In model 3, none of the trace elements was predictive of OSA after adjustment for the variables selected by LASSO regression.

The GWQS model showed an inverse but non-significant association between the metal mixture index and OSA (β=−0.22; p=0.59), suggesting that higher values of the weighted mixture index tended to correspond to a lower probability of OSA. The model weights (Figure 3) indicated that Fe (weight=0.50) and Mn (weight=0.26) contributed most to the weighted index and together accounted for approximately 76% of the total mixture weight, whereas the remaining metals contributed relatively little.

DISCUSSION

This study suggests an association between serum concentrations of selected micronutrients, particularly Fe and Mg, and OSA. Participants in the fourth Fe quartile had higher odds of OSA than those in the lowest quartile. However, given the methodological limitations of this study, these findings should be considered preliminary and should be evaluated in future longitudinal research. Further work is needed to determine whether monitoring serum Fe levels could help assess or manage OSA risk. Fe is essential in living organisms and serves as a cofactor for oxygen-binding proteins [24]. Its role is well established in several biological processes related to cognitive function and brain health [25]. One important consequence of Fe deficiency is tissue hypoxia, which can reduce physical performance, disrupt sensory perception, and impair intellectual function [26]. Low Fe levels have been linked to cognitive impairment, whereas elevated Fe levels have been associated with neurological disorders such as Parkinson’s disease, Alzheimer’s disease, and other neurodegenerative conditions [27]. Excess Fe can also promote ROS and tumor necrosis factor-alpha production, increasing endothelial cell activation and monocyte adhesion [28].

Fe is currently the most extensively studied element in relation to sleep disorders. Some studies have reported associations between elevated Fe stores and OSA or sleep quality, whereas others have not supported this hypothesis. For example, elevated brain Fe levels have been associated with OSA [27]. Consistently, 1 study reported higher plasma Fe and Cu levels and lower plasma Zn and erythrocyte Se levels in patients with OSA [29]. In another study of 61 patients with OSA and 36 healthy controls, serum Fe, Mg, Mn, Cu, Zn, Co, Cd, and Pb levels were higher in patients with OSA [16]. Ji et al. [30] reported in a systematic review that serum Fe levels were positively associated with progression through sleep stages in infants. In contrast, a study of 796 patients with OSA from the Icelandic Sleep Apnea Cohort found no significant differences in ferritin levels between patients with moderate to severe OSA and 637 general-population controls [31]. A randomized controlled trial in infants also showed that those who received Fe or Zn supplementation had longer nighttime and total sleep duration than those in the placebo group [32].

Differences across studies may reflect variation in study design, study populations, sample size, diagnostic criteria, dietary status, medication use, and control selection. The causes of trace mineral disturbances, particularly Fe-related disturbances, in patients with OSA remain unclear and require further study. Previous research has shown a significant relationship between serum ferritin levels and OSA severity. Elevated ferritin levels have been reported in individuals with severe OSA, which may indicate underlying inflammatory processes. Because ferritin is an acute-phase reactant, high ferritin levels in patients with OSA may reflect inflammation [33]. Increased brain Fe deposition has also been reported in patients with OSA, particularly in regions linked to cognitive function, suggesting that inflammation and hypoxia may contribute to Fe dysregulation [27,34]. Brain Fe accumulation has been linked to neurodegenerative processes, raising concerns about the long-term cognitive health of individuals with OSA [27].

In the present study, participants with intermediate Mg levels, corresponding to the second and third Mg quartiles, had lower odds of OSA than those in the lowest Mg quartile. Mg is an essential cofactor for enzymes involved in protein synthesis and is directly related to bone metabolism. In addition, Mg acts as a cofactor in the detoxification of superoxide free radicals [16]. The Mg findings in this cross-sectional analysis are complex. Mg was not identified as a predictor of OSA in the unadjusted model overall, but intermediate Mg quartiles were inversely associated with OSA in model 1. These findings should be interpreted cautiously. The association between Mg and OSA may be influenced by confounding factors, including other trace elements and the clinical variables included in the adjusted analyses. The biological mechanisms underlying this association remain unclear and require further investigation. Future studies should clarify the role of Mg in OSA while considering diet, metabolic function, and general health status. Contrary to the present findings, a study of 61 patients with OSA and 36 healthy controls reported higher serum Mg levels in patients with OSA than in controls [16]. A systematic review also reported that Mg supplementation may reduce age-related changes in sleep patterns among older adults [30].

This study found a weak positive correlation between serum Cu and Ca levels. Several elements can have positive or inverse biological relationships. For example, Zn and Cu show metabolic and absorption antagonism because both involve metallothionein in deposition and assimilation [35]. The roles of Ca and Cu in bone development may also partly explain the observed correlation. Although few studies have examined serum Cu and Ca levels together, 1 study of urban Colombian preschool children identified an inverse relationship between these 2 serum markers [36]. Another study reported that Cu concentration was negatively correlated with bone calcium, bone density, and collagen content in male mice [37]. Ca and Cu intake may affect their circulating levels through interactions during intestinal absorption. Some researchers have reported that Ca supplements decrease fecal Cu losses and improve Cu retention in humans [38], whereas other studies have found no significant effect of Ca supplementation on Cu balance in rats [39]. High Ca levels may also indirectly promote Cu storage through metallothionein synthesis, because metal-responsive metallothionein expression is influenced by cellular Ca signaling [40]. Despite these findings, the molecular mechanisms and regulatory pathways underlying the correlation between these metals remain unclear.

The GWQS analysis showed a non-significant inverse association between the metal mixture index and OSA (β=−0.22; p=0.59), suggesting that higher weighted mixture index values tended to correspond to lower OSA probability. Given the lack of statistical significance, however, this finding should be interpreted as an exploratory trend rather than evidence of a protective effect. Fe and Mn accounted for most of the mixture weight, indicating that these metals contributed most strongly to the overall mixture index. Nevertheless, the overall mixture association remained non-significant.

This study has several limitations that should be considered when interpreting the findings. First, the relatively small sample size (n=99) may have reduced statistical power, limiting the ability to detect complex associations and the generalizability of the results to broader populations. Second, the cross-sectional design precludes causal inference and prevents determination of the directionality of the relationship between trace element levels and OSA. Third, the absence of detailed dietary intake and nutritional status data is a notable limitation, because dietary patterns directly influence serum trace element concentrations and may act as confounders. Fourth, associations between mineral concentrations and OSA severity were not comprehensively analyzed; further investigation is therefore needed to determine whether changes in these elements are related to disease progression. Fifth, and closely related to the second limitation, reverse causality remains possible. Specifically, OSA-related chronic intermittent hypoxia and systemic inflammation could alter the absorption, distribution, or excretion of trace elements, particularly Fe and Mg. For example, HIF-1α activation in OSA may upregulate hepcidin expression, leading to Fe dysregulation as a consequence rather than a cause of disease. Longitudinal studies are needed to determine whether altered trace element levels precede or follow the development of OSA. Addressing these limitations through larger longitudinal studies with concurrent detailed dietary assessments will help clarify the role of trace elements in OSA pathophysiology.
