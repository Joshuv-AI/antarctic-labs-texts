Introduction

Let
G
be an additive group, and let
X=(x_{n})_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}\in\prod_{-\infty}^{\infty}G
be an infinite two-sided sequence such that
x_{n}\in G
for all integers
n
.
In this paper, we also write
X=(x_{n}\in G:n\in\mathbb{Z})
and call it a

two-sided stream
(or simply,
stream
) over
G
, we use
St(G)
to denote the set of all streams over
G
.
Sometimes, a stream
X
is written as
(\cdots,x_{-2},x_{-1};x_{0},x_{1},\cdots)
, where the semicolon ‘;’ separates the coordinates for negative indices (
n<0
) from those for non-negative indices (
n\geq 0
). It is can also be viewed as
as a two-sided formal power series over a variable
z
:

X=X(z)=\cdots+x_{-2}z^{-2}+x_{-1}z^{-1}+x_{0}+x_{1}z+x_{2}z^{2}+\cdots.

Note that one-sided infinite sequences over
G
have been well studied in computer science under the framework of stream calculus, see, for example,
[
16
]
.

Let
G
be a topological commutative ring with additive identity
0
and multiplicative identity
1
, then
St(G)
forms an additive group under component-wise addition and is also a topological group with respect to the product topology induced by
G
. For convenience, the additive identity of
St(G)
is denoted by
O=(0^{\infty};0^{\infty})
, where
0^{\infty}
preceding the semicolon indicates that all terms to the left are zero, and
0^{\infty}
following it indicates that all terms to the right are zero.

Motivated by the multiplication of two formal power series, we define the
convolution product
(or Cauchy product) between two steams
(x_{n}:n\in\mathbb{Z})
and
(y_{n}:n\in\mathbb{Z})\in St(G)
as follows:

(x_{n}:n\in\mathbb{Z})\times(y_{n}:n\in\mathbb{Z})=\left(\sum_{i=-\infty}^{\infty}x_{i}y_{n-i}:n\in\mathbb{Z}\right).

(1)

When the series on the right-hand side converges for all
n\in\mathbb{Z}
, we say that the convolution product exists. Note that this is distinct from the Hadamard product, which will be discussed in Section 5. With respect to this convolution product, the multiplicative identity of
St(G)
is
I=(0^{\infty};1,0^{\infty})
.

Given a stream
(x_{n}:n\in\mathbb{Z})\in St(G)
, even if
(y_{n}:n\in\mathbb{Z})\in St(G)
such that

(x_{n}:n\in\mathbb{Z})\times(y_{n}:n\in\mathbb{Z})=I

exists, it is not necessarily unique. This ambiguity is illustrated by the following example:

(0^{\infty};1,-1,0^{\infty})\times(0^{\infty};1^{\infty})=(0^{\infty};1,-1,0^{\infty})\times((-1)^{\infty};0^{\infty})=I.

Equivalently, in terms of formal power series:

(1-t)\times(1+t+t^{2}+\dots)=(1-t)\times(-\frac{1}{t}-\frac{1}{t^{2}}-\dots)=1.

Because of this example, we should impose appropriate conditions on streams to guarantee both the existence and uniqueness of their convolution product.

We call a stream
(x_{n}:n\in\mathbb{Z})\in St(G)

absolutely summable
if the series
\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}x_{n}
converges and all rearrangements of its will converge to the same sum.

Definition 1
.

A polynomial
P(z)=\sum_{n\in S}a_{n}z^{n}\in\mathbb{Z}[z]
is said to be
primitive
of degree
k

if there exists a positive integer
k
with
\{0,k\}\subset S\subset\{0,1,\cdots,k\}
such that

a_{i}\in\mathbb{Z}\setminus\{0\}
for all
i\in S
and the greatest common divisor (GCD) of its
coefficients is
\pm 1
.

We say that
P(z)
is
hyperbolic
if
P(z)
does not vanish on the unit circle
\{z\in\mathbb{C}:|z|=1\}
, that is,
P(z)\neq 0
for any
z\in\mathbb{C}
with
|z|=1
.

Let
P(z)=\sum_{n\in S}a_{n}z^{n}\in\mathbb{Z}[z]
be a primitive polynomial as in Definition 1 and let
\mathbb{T}:=\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}
be the torus.
Then,
P(z)
acts continuously on
St(\mathbb{T})
(denoted by
T_{P}
) via the multiplication of formal power series, which corresponds to
the convolution product of streams defined in (
1
). Specifically,

T_{P}:(x_{n}:n\in\mathbb{Z})\mapsto(a_{n}:n\in S)\times(x_{n}:n\in\mathbb{Z})\\
=(a_{k}x_{n-k}+\cdots+a_{1}x_{n-1}+a_{0}x_{n}:n\in\mathbb{Z})\in St(\mathbb{T}).

We study the kernel of this action, denoted by
\Omega_{P}
, which we call the
stream zeros
of
P(z)
:

\Omega_{P}:=\left\{(x_{n}:n\in\mathbb{Z})\in St(\mathbb{T}):P(z)\times(x_{n}:n\in\mathbb{Z})=(0^{\infty};0^{\infty})\right\}.

In other words,
\Omega_{P}
is the set of streams over
\mathbb{T}
that are annihilated by polynomial
P(z)
, meaning their convolution product with
P(z)
yields the zero stream
O=(0^{\infty};0^{\infty})
. Equivalently,
\Omega_{P}
consists of all sequences
(x_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}
on
\mathbb{T}
that satisfy the linear recurrence relation
\sum_{j=0}^{k}a_{j}x_{n-j}=0
for all
n\in\mathbb{Z}
.

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows.
In Section 2, we establish some basic properties
of the convolution product which are useful for subsequent sections. In Section 3,
we analyze
\Omega_{P}
from a dynamical system viewpoint. In Section 4, we introduce
the notion of lift of a steam zeros on
\mathbb{T}
alongside the lifting matrix of a polynomial
P(z)
, we prove Theorem 3 which provide a construction method for a lift whenever Condition (#) (Definition 3 in Section 4) is satisfied. Moreover, we prove that any primitive
P(z)
satisfies Condition (#), a result of independent interest that generalizes earlier work by M. Newman
[
11
]
and
[
19
]
for lifting matrices of size one.

In Section 5, we utilize polynomial resultant, generalized Vandermonde’s determinant, and Hadamard product to establish one-to-one correspondence between the factorization of
P(z)
and the semi-direct sum decomposition of the additive group
\Omega_{P}
. In Section 6, the strong automorphism group of
\Omega_{P}
is introduced and studied. We prove that the group of strong automorphisms of
\Omega_{P}
can be represented by the matrices from
Saut_{P}
(Definition 4). Using Dirichlet’s unit theorem, we prove Theorem 7, which shows that there are closely relationship between the algebraic structure of
Saut_{P}
and the group of units formed from the polynomial root of
P(z)
, this have also been investigated in
[
5
,
15
]
. We provide many examples to illustrate these results, while alternative perspectives on the automorphism groups of general symbolic spaces (such as symbolic complexity) can be found in
[
7
,
9
]
.

In Section 7, we discuss the quadratic polynomial case (
\deg P=2
), showing that
Saut_{P}
is determined by the discriminant of
P(z)
. For the degree larger than 2, we obtain partial results that indicate a close connection to the theory of
Diophantine equations, generalizing the classical Pell’s equation seen in the quadratic case. We provide examples to illustrate all theses theorem.
In the last section, we focus on stream zeros over the Galois field (or finite field)
\text{GF}(q)
of order
q
, where
q
is a prime power, under the action of a polynomial
P(z)
. We prove that if
P(z)
is an irreducible polynomial of degree
k
, then
Saut_{P}
is a cyclic group of order
q^{k}-1
.

Convolution product and its inverse

In this section, we present some basic properties of the
convolution product of
St(\mathbb{C})
. As one can see from the previous discussion, the convolution inverse may not be unique, we thus wish to find some conditions that can guarantee the uniqueness of the convolution inverse. The following statement (iv) of Theorem 1 is known (
[
2
]
) by complex analytic methods, here we give another
elementary proof.

Theorem 1
.

Let
U=(u_{n}:n\in\mathbb{Z}),~V=(v_{n}:n\in\mathbb{Z}),~W=(w_{n}:n\in\mathbb{Z})
be elements in
St(\mathbb{C})
.

(i)

U\times V=V\times U
whenever either products
U\times V
or
V\times U
exists.
If among these three streams
U,V
and
W
, two are absolutely summable and the remaining one is bounded, then the iterated convolution products exist and satisfy:

(U\times V)\times W=U\times(V\times W).

(ii)
If
U
is absolutely summable, an absolutely summable
V
satisfying

U\times V=I
or
V\times U=I
(if exists) is unique. In this case, we have

U\times V=V\times U=I
and
V
is called the convolution inverse of
U
, denoted by
U^{-1}
.

(iii)
If both
U
and
V
are absolutely summable, then so is
U\times V
.
Furthermore, if both
U^{-1}
and
V^{-1}
exist, then

(U\times V)^{-1}
exists with
(U\times V)^{-1}=V^{-1}\times U^{-1},

and
(U^{-1})^{-1}=U
.

(iv)
Any primitive polynomial
P(z)=(a_{n}:n\in\mathbb{Z})

has a unique absolutely summable inverse
P(z)^{-1}
in
St(\mathbb{R})
provided that
P(z)
is hyperbolic.

Proof.

(i) The first statement is clear. For the second statement,
let
U,V,W
satisfy the required condition.
Then for any
n\in\mathbb{Z}
, we prove that

\sum_{i,j,k\in\mathbb{Z}\atop{i+j+k=n}}|u_{i}v_{j}w_{k}|<\infty.

By symmetry, we may assume that
U,V
are absolutely summable and
W

is bounded by
L
, say. Then, we have

\sum_{i,j,k\in\mathbb{Z}\atop{i+j+k=n}}|u_{i}v_{j}w_{k}|<L\sum_{i,j\in\mathbb{Z}}|u_{i}v_{j}|=L\sum_{i\in\mathbb{Z}}|u_{i}|\sum_{j\in\mathbb{Z}}|v_{j}|<\infty.

Thus,
\sum_{i,j,k\in\mathbb{Z}\atop{i+j+k=n}}u_{i}v_{j}w_{k}
converges absolutely.
Hence, we have

\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}\left(\sum_{i,j\in\mathbb{Z}\atop{i+j=n-k}}u_{i}v_{j}\right)w_{k}=\sum_{i\in\mathbb{Z}}u_{i}\left(\sum_{j,k\in\mathbb{Z}\atop{j+k=n-i}}v_{j}w_{k}\right)=\sum_{i,j,k\in\mathbb{Z}\atop{i+j+k=n}}u_{i}v_{j}w_{k}

and
(U\times V)\times W=U\times(V\times W)
.

(ii) We prove the uniqueness since the other statements follow from the
first statement in (i). Indeed, if
U\times V_{0}=U\times V_{1}=I
, then by (i), we have

V_{1}=I\times V_{1}=(U\times V_{0})\times V_{1}=(V_{0}\times U)\times V_{1}=V_{0}\times(U\times V_{1})=V_{0}.

(iii) It is clear.

(iv) Let
P(z)=\sum_{n\in S}a_{n}z^{n}
with a finite nonempty set
S=\{n\in\mathbb{Z}:a_{n}\neq 0\}
. As we know, every polynomial with real coefficients can be factored into a product of linear factors and/or irreducible quadratic factors (of degree two) with real coefficients. These further factor into two linear factors with complex coefficients of the form
z-\omega
and
z-\overline{\omega}
, here
\overline{\omega}
is the complex conjugate of
\omega
.

By factoring polynomial
P(z)
as above and applying (ii), it suffices to show that

P(z)
of the elementary forms, either non-zero constant
C
,
z
or
z-\omega
with
\omega\in\mathbb{C}
and

0<|\omega|\neq 1
(by assumption that
P(z)
is hyperbolic), possess an absolutely summable inverse.

Case 1:
P(z)=C\neq 0
. Since
C=(0^{\infty};C,0^{\infty})
,

C^{-1}=(0^{\infty};C^{-1},0^{\infty})
becomes the absolutely summable inverse.

Case 2:
P(z)=z
. One can easily verifies that
z
and
z^{-1}
are
absolutely summable and inverses of each other. This can be seen from the fact

(0^{\infty}1;0^{\infty})=(0^{\infty};0,1,0^{\infty})^{-1}
.

Case 3:
P(z)=z-w
, where
w\in\mathbb{R}
is a real number, if
|\omega|>1
,
it is obvious that

(z-\omega)^{-1}=(-1)\omega^{-1}(1-\omega^{-1}z)^{-1}=-\omega^{-1}-\omega^{-2}z-\omega^{-3}z^{2}-\cdots,

Observe that
\sum_{n}\omega^{-n}
is absolutely summable, so
(0^{\infty};-\omega^{-1},-\omega^{-2},-\omega^{-3},\cdots)
is the
absolutely summable inverse of
z-\omega
;
When
P(z)=z-w
with
|\omega|<1
, similarly, we have

(z-\omega)^{-1}=z^{-1}(1-\omega z^{-1})^{-1}=z^{-1}+\omega z^{-2}+\omega^{2}z^{-3}+\cdots,

now
(\cdots,\omega^{2},\omega,1;0^{\infty})
becomes the absolutely summable inverse of
z-\omega
.

Case 4:
P(z)=z-w
, where
\omega\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\mathbb{R}
, we also consider the complex conjugate
\overline{\omega}
of
\omega
, since
|\overline{\omega}|=|\omega|
, there is an analogous statement for
\overline{\omega}
as Case 3, by (iii),
((z-\omega)(z-\overline{\omega}))^{-1}=(z-\omega)^{-1}(z-\overline{\omega})^{-1}\in St(\mathbb{R})
. Thus,
P(z)
has the absolutely summable inverse
P(z)^{-1}\in St(\mathbb{R})
.
∎

\mathbb{Z}

Given a primitive polynomial

k_{*}:=\sum_{n\in S,a_{n}<0}a_{n}\mbox{ and }k^{*}:=\sum_{n\in S,a_{n}>0}a_{n}.

(2)

For

(or

[0,1)

While

\{~\}
is the fractional part).
Let
K_{P}=\{-2,-1,0,1\}
, and define
\xi_{n+2}=x_{n}-3x_{n+1}+x_{n+2}
, then the symbolic representation of
(x_{n}\in[0,1):n\in\mathbb{Z})

is is given by the sequence
(\xi_{n})_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}

\xi_{n}\in K_{P}
, for each

.
Let
[x]
denote the integer part of
x
, i.e.,
[x]=x-\{x\}
, where

\xi_{n+2}=x_{n}-3x_{n+1}+x_{n+2}=-[3x_{n+1}-x_{n}]\in K_{P}.

as illustrated in Figure 2.

The topological entropy of this dynamical systems is given by

\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\log\#\Xi_{P}|_{\{1,2,\cdots,n\}},

As is well known, this entropy coincides with the sum of logarithms of the eigenvalues greater than 1 of the
companion matrix, which is also equal to the logarithms of Mahler measure of polynomial

, see

has eigenvalues

4
Lifts of stream zeros

For any integers
n\leq m
, let
[n,m]
denotes the set of all integers
j
with
n\leq j\leq m
, which is the same thing as the set
\{n,n+1,\dots,m-1,m\}
.
Let
P(z)=a_{k}z^{k}+a_{k-1}z^{k-1}+\cdots+a_{0}\in\mathbb{Z}[z]
be a primitive polynomial of degree
k
and
N
be a positive integer (denote by
\mathbb{Z}_{>0}
). Define

\tilde{\Omega}_{P}:=\{(\tilde{x}_{n}\in\mathbb{R}:n\in\mathbb{Z}):a_{k}\tilde{x}_{n-k}+a_{k-1}\tilde{x}_{n-k+1}+\cdots+a_{0}\tilde{x}_{n}=0~(\forall n\in\mathbb{Z})\}

and its restriction to finite interval
[-N,N]
as:

\tilde{\Omega}_{P}|_{N}:=\{(\tilde{x}_{n}\in\mathbb{R}:n\in[-N,N]):(\tilde{x}_{n}\in\mathbb{R}:n\in\mathbb{Z})\in\tilde{\Omega}_{P}\}.

Definition 2
.

A stream
(x_{n}:n\in\mathbb{Z})\in St(\mathbb{T})
is said to have a
lift
in
\tilde{\Omega}_{P}

if for any positive integer
N\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}
, there exists a
local lift

(\tilde{x}_{n}\in\mathbb{R}:-N\leq n\leq N)\in\tilde{\Omega}_{P}|_{N}
such that

\pi(\tilde{x}_{n})=x_{n}
for any
n\in[-N,N]
, where
\pi:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{T}
is the canonical projection.
We say that
\Omega_{P}
has a lift from
\mathbb{T}
to
\mathbb{R}
if every stream in
\Omega_{P}
has a lift in
\tilde{\Omega}_{P}
.

One may ask under what algebraic conditions on polynomial
P(z)
such that the stream zeros set
\Omega_{P}
possesses a lift. We answer it in the remainder of this section.

For any positive integer
l
, define the
l\times(k+l)
-matrix
M_{P}^{(l)}
associated with
P(z)
:

M_{P}^{(l)}=\begin{pmatrix}a_{k}&a_{k-1}&\cdots&a_{0}&0&\cdots&\cdots&\cdots&0\\
0&a_{k}&a_{k-1}&\cdots&a_{0}&0&\cdots&\cdots&0\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\vdots\\
0&\cdots&\cdots&0&a_{k}&a_{k-1}&\cdots&a_{0}&0\end{pmatrix}

(3)

Definition 3
.

We say that
P(z)
satisfies
Condition (#)
if for any positive integer
l
, there exists an
integral matrix
R
(not necessarily unique) of size
k\times(k+l)
such that the determinant of the
(k+l)\times(k+l)
square matrix
A^{l}
equals
\pm 1
, where the matrix
A^{l}
is formed by augmenting
M_{P}^{(l)}
with
R
:

A_{l}=\left(\begin{array}[]{c}M_{P}^{(l)}\\
R\end{array}\right).

Partition the inverse matrix
A_{l}^{-1}
into two block submatrices:

A_{l}^{-1}=(\tilde{L}\mid L)

where
\tilde{L},L
are

(k+l)\times l
sub-matrix and
(k+l)\times k
sub-matrix of matrix
A_{l}^{-1}
, respectively.
The
(k+l)\times(k+l)
square matrix
\Lambda:=LR
, which is exactly the matrix multiplication of two matrices
L
and
R
, is then called an
lifting matrix
of
P(z)
of size
l
.

For instance, let
P(z)=3z-2
, so the degree
k=1
. For
l=1
, the matrix
M_{P}^{(1)}=(3~~-2)
. Then, we can choose the
1\times 2
matrix
R=(-1~~1)
such that
\det A_{l}=\det\left(\begin{array}[]{c}M_{P}^{(1)}\\
R\end{array}\right)=\det\begin{pmatrix}3&-2\\
-1&1\end{pmatrix}=1.

The inverse matrix of
A_{l}
is exactly

\begin{pmatrix}1&2\\
1&3\end{pmatrix}
.
So the matrix
L=\begin{pmatrix}2\\
3\end{pmatrix}.

Then the square
2\times 2
matrix

\Lambda=LR=\begin{pmatrix}-2&2\\
-3&3\end{pmatrix}

becomes a lifting matrix of
P(z)
of size 2. In fact,
let
(1/5,4/5)
be a segment of a stream in
\Omega_{P}
satisfying

3\times(1/5)-2\times(4/5)=-1\in\mathbb{Z}
. Then, we have

\begin{pmatrix}-2&2\\
-3&3\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}1/5\\
4/5\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}6/5\\
9/5\end{pmatrix}\equiv\begin{pmatrix}1/5\\
4/5\end{pmatrix}~~(\mbox{mod}~\mathbb{Z}),

and
3\times(6/5)-2\times(9/5)=0
.

Roughly speaking, Condition (
\#
) requires that one can find at least one such matrix

that makes the resulting square matrix
A_{l}
unimodular (its determinant is
\pm 1
), ensuring that
A_{l}
has an inverse with integer entries.
For the lifting matrix is of size lager than 2, see the example 2 in this section. However, when the degree of polynomial
P(z)
is larger than 2, the existence of lifting matrix is not easy. Before to show a general statement, first we present a useful lemma due to S. Akiyama
[
1
]
.

Lemma 1
.

[
1
]

In the hyperplane
H
of
\mathbb{R}^{k+l}
spanned by the row vectors of the matrix
M_{P}^{(l)}
,
any integer point in
H
can be written as a linear combination of the row vectors
of
M_{P}^{(l)}
with integer coefficients.
Consequently, the parallelepiped spanned by the
l
row vectors of
M_{P}^{(l)}
contains
integer points only at its vertices.

Return to the example of
P(z)=3z-2
, the lemma is noting but to say that
\mathbb{Z}=\{3m+2n:m,n\in\mathbb{Z}\}
( using
GCD(-2,3)=1
), which is exactly the statement of Bézout’s Lemma.

Proof.

It is enough to prove that if
\eta=(x_{1},x_{2},\cdots,x_{l})
is a row vector of size
l
with real entries such that
\eta M_{P}^{(l)}
is an integer vector, then
\eta
itself must be an integer vector. If this holds, it implies that any integer point
(y_{1},y_{2},\cdots,y_{k+l})
in the space spanned by the row vectors of
M_{P}^{(l)}
can be expressed as an integer linear combination of those rows.

First, we prove that
\eta
has only rational entries.
Suppose to the contrary that
\eta
contains irrational elements. Among these elements we choose
x_{i}
such that the index
i\in\{1,2,\dots,l\}
is the smallest one. Now we consider the
i
-th
element of the vector
\eta M_{P}^{(l)}
, which is irrational, this contradicts the assumption that
\eta M_{P}^{(l)}
is an integer vector. Thus,
\eta
must be a rational vector.

In order to show
\eta
is an integer vector, it suffices to prove that for any prime number
p
,

\nu_{p}(x_{i})\geq 0
for all
i=1,\cdots,l
, where
\nu_{p}
is the discrete valuation at

p
on rational field
\mathbb{Q}
. Suppose to the contrary that
\nu_{p}(x_{i})<0
for some prime
p
and some
i\in\{1,2,\dots,l\}
.
Let
\nu_{p}(x_{i})
attains the minimum at
x_{i}
, let
m_{0}=\min_{i}\nu_{p}(x_{i})<0
. Since there are may many possibilities
i
such that
\nu_{p}(x_{i})
attains the minimum, we consider the smallest one, say
i_{0}
. That is,
i_{0}\in\{1,\cdots,l\}
be the (unique) one
such that

\nu_{p}(x_{i_{0}})=m_{0}<\nu_{p}(x_{i})~\mbox{for all}~1\leq i<i_{0}~\mbox{and}~\nu_{p}(x_{i_{0}})\leq\nu_{p}(x_{i})~\mbox{for all}~i_{0}<i\leq l.

Since
GCD(a_{k},\cdots,a_{1},a_{0})=1
, there exists some

j\in\{0,1,\dots,k\}
such that
\nu_{p}(a_{j})=0
. Similarly, consider the smallest index
j_{0}
such that

\nu_{p}(a_{j_{0}})=0<\nu_{p}(a_{j})~\mbox{for all}~0\leq j<j_{0}.

Notice that
k+i_{0}-j_{0}\in[1,k+l]
, and the
(k+i_{0}-j_{0})
-th column of the matrix
M_{P}^{(l)}
is
(a_{j_{0}-i_{0}+1},\dots,a_{j_{0}-i_{0}+l})^{T}
, with the convention that
~a_{j}=0~\mbox{if}~j\notin\{0,1,\cdots,k\}
, and we define
\nu_{p}(0)=\infty
. So the
(k+i_{0}-j_{0})
-th element of
\eta M_{P}^{(l)}
, denoted by
\xi
, becomes

\xi=x_{1}a_{j_{0}-i_{0}+1}+\cdots+x_{i_{0}}a_{j_{0}}+\cdots+x_{l}a_{j_{0}-i_{0}+l}.

From the definition of
i_{0}
and
j_{0}
, and using the fact that for any integer
n\neq 0
,
\nu_{p}(n)
is always a non-negative integer,
it follows that

\nu_{p}(x_{i}a_{j_{0}-i_{0}-i})>\nu_{p}(x_{i_{0}}a_{j_{0}})~\mbox{for all}~i\neq i_{0}.

Using the properties that for
x,y\in\mathbb{Q}
,
\nu_{p}(xy)=\nu_{p}(x)+\nu_{p}(y),
and
\nu_{p}(x+y)\geq\min\{\nu_{p}(x),\nu_{p}(y)\}
, the inequality becomes an equality when
\nu_{p}(x)\neq\nu_{p}(y)
.
So we have

\nu_{p}(\xi)=\nu_{p}(x_{i_{0}})+\nu_{p}(a_{j_{0}})=m_{0}+0=m_{0}<0.

Thus,
\xi
is not an integer, which contradicts the assumption again, so the theorem follows.
∎

Theorem 3
.

Every primitive polynomial
P(z)
satisfies
Condition (#)
, and consequently, the stream zero set
\Omega_{P}
always admits a lift.

More precisely, for any large
N\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}
and
l=2N-k+1
,
let
\Lambda
be a lifting matrix of size
l
constructed as in Definition
3
.
Then, for any stream
(x_{n}:n\in\mathbb{Z})\in\Omega_{P}
, the vector defined by

\Lambda\mathbf{x}

constitutes a local lift of
(x_{n}:-N\leq n\leq N)
in
\tilde{\Omega}_{P}
, where
\mathbf{x}
is the column vector of the truncated sequence
(x_{n})_{-N\leq n\leq N}
(over
[-N,N]
), i.e,
\mathbf{x}:=(x_{-N},x_{-N+1},\dots,x_{N})^{T}.

Proof.

Let
l\in\{1,2,\cdots\}
be arbitrary.
To prove Condition (#), it is sufficient to prove that there exists a

l\times(k+l)
-integer matrix
R
such that the set of row vectors
of
\left(\begin{array}[]{c}M_{P}^{(l)}\\
R\end{array}\right)
is linear independent and the
parallelepiped spanned by them contains the integer points only at vertices,
since then the volume of the parallelepiped is 1 and hence,

\det\left(\begin{array}[]{c}M_{P}^{(l)}\\
R\end{array}\right)=\pm 1
.

We construct
R
inductively starting from the first row. By
Lemma 1,
M_{P}^{(l)}
has the property that the rows are linearly independent and
the parallelepiped spanned by them, say
\Gamma_{0}
, contains the integer points
only at vertices.
Then, we add any integer vector, say
\gamma
, independent of the row vectors of
M_{P}^{(l)}
.
Let
\Gamma
be the parallelepiped spanned by
\Gamma_{0}
and
\gamma
.
Since both
\Gamma_{0}
and
\gamma+\Gamma_{0}
contain the integer points only at vertices,
all the integer points in
\Gamma
which are not the vertices are properly in between

\Gamma_{0}
and
\gamma+\Gamma_{0}
.

Let
\gamma^{\prime}
be any one of them and
\Gamma^{\prime}
be the
parallelepiped spanned by
\Gamma_{0}
and
\gamma^{\prime}
. Then the volume of
\Gamma^{\prime}

and the number of integer points which are not the vertices is strictly smaller than those
of
\Gamma
. By this process, we finally find an integer vector

\xi_{1}
independent of the row vectors of
M_{P}^{(l)}
such that the parallelepiped
\Gamma_{1}
spanned by
\Gamma_{0}

and
\xi_{1}
has the property that all the integer points in it
are only those at the vertices.

Repeating this process described above, we can find a

(k+l)

in
\mathbb{R}^{k+l}
which is spanned by

\Gamma_{0}
and

such that

\Gamma_{k}
other than the vertices.
Thus, the volume of
\Gamma_{k}
is
1
and

\det\left(\begin{array}[]{c}M_{P}^{(l)}\\

,
where
R=(\xi_{1},\xi_{2},\dots,\xi_{k})^{T}.

Now we prove the existence of the lift of streams zero

. Let

.
Our aim is to show that for any

.
Without loss of generality, assume that

Let

Remark 2
.

In

]

.

(i)
For any sufficiently large

\Omega_{P}

in

, whose projection under

precisely recovers the original stream segment

. Since every element in

Factorization of polynomials and stream zeros

For a preparation, we prove the following proposition which may be well known. Since we have not found references, we provide a proof here for completeness.

Proposition 1
.

For a primitive polynomial
P(z)=a_{k}z^{k}+\cdots+a_{1}z+a_{0}\in\mathbb{Z}[z]
with degree

k\geq 1,~a_{0}a_{k}\neq 0
and
\Delta\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 1}
,

\Omega_{P}\cap\{0,\frac{1}{\Delta},\frac{2}{\Delta},\cdots,\frac{\Delta-1}{\Delta}\}^{\mathbb{Z}}

is a finite set.

It is clear that our Proposition 1 follows from the following Lemma 3.

Lemma 3
.

If
X=(x_{n}\in\mathbb{Z}:n\in\mathbb{Z})\in St(\mathbb{Z})
satisfies that

P(z)\times X=(0^{\infty};0^{\infty})~({\rm mod}~\Delta)
, then
X~({\rm mod}~\Delta)
is
periodic with the period bounded by a function of
k
and
\Delta
.

In this section, the period of a periodic sequence means the minimal period. Due to the theory of prime decomposition, to show this Lemma 3, it suffices to prove that
\Delta
be a power of a prime, say,
\Delta=p^{l}
for some prime
p
and positive integer
l
. To achieve it, we need the following:

Lemma 4
.

Let
p
be a prime and
B=(b_{n}:n\in\mathbb{Z})\in St(\mathbb{Z})
be periodic.
If
X=(x_{n}:n\in\mathbb{Z})\in St(\mathbb{Z})
satisfies that
P(z)\times X=B~({\rm mod}~p)
.
Then,
X~({\rm mod}~p)
is periodic with the period bounded by a function of

k
and the period of
B
.

Proof of Lemma 4:
Let
K
be the period of
B
. Let
h\in\{0,1,\cdots,k\}
be the minimum such that
a_{h}\neq 0~({\rm mod}~p)
, its existence is guaranteed by the assumption that

P(z)
is primitive. Since
P(z)\times X=B~({\rm mod}~p)
, so for any
n\in\mathbb{Z}
.

a_{k}x_{n-k+h}+\cdots+a_{h}x_{n}+\cdots+a_{0}x_{n+h}=b_{n+h}~({\rm mod}~p).

From the definition of
h
,

a_{k}x_{n-k+h}+\cdots+a_{h}x_{n}=b_{n+h}~({\rm mod}~p).

It follows that for any
n\in\mathbb{Z}
,
given
(x_{n-k+h},\cdots,x_{n-1})~({\rm mod}~p)
, we can see that

x_{n}~({\rm mod}~p)
is uniquely
determined by
b_{n+h}
. Here we using the assumption that
p
is prime,
a_{h}\neq 0~({\rm mod}~p)
, and the fact that
a linear congruence equation
a_{h}x=b~({\rm mod}~p)
for any
b\in\mathbb{Z}
has a unique solution in
x({\rm mod}~p)
.
Note that the number of distinct possible choices for index
n\pmod{K}
is
K
, since
B
is periodic with period
K
.

As a consequence,
x_{n}
is determined as above by

(x_{n-k+h},\cdots,x_{n-1},~n~(\mbox{mod }K))\in\{0,1,\cdots,p-1\}^{k-h}\times\{0,1,\cdots,K-1\},

Hence, for any
n
there exists
m
with
n<m
and
m-n\leq p^{k-h}K
such that
x_{n}=x_{m}
which correspond to the same element in the above.
This further implies that
x_{n+1}=x_{m+1},~x_{n+2}=x_{m+2},~\cdots
.
Since
n\in\mathbb{Z}
is arbitrary and
p^{k-h}K<\infty
, similarly, if we consider the case that the maximal
h\in\{0,1,\cdots,k\}
such that
a_{h}\neq 0~({\rm mod}~p)
, this implies that
x_{n-1}=x_{m-1},~x_{n-2}=x_{m-2},~\cdots
.
Thus we can prove that
X
is periodic with the period at most
p^{k-h}K
, which proves Lemma 3.

Now our goal is to show this claim: If
X=(x_{n}:n\in\mathbb{Z})\in St(\mathbb{Z})
satisfies that
P(z)\times X=(0^{\infty};0^{\infty})~({\rm mod}~p^{l})
,
then
X~({\rm mod}~p^{l})
is periodic with the period bounded by a function of degree of
P
and
p^{l}
. Once the claim has been proved, this further implies Proposition 1.

Proof of Proposition 1
.

In order to show the claim, we prove it by the induction on
l=1,2,\cdots
, provided that
\Delta=p^{l}
, where
p
is a prime.
If
l=1
, then the claim is nothing but Lemma 4 with
B=(0^{\infty};0^{\infty})
.
Assume that the claim holds for
l=l_{0}\geq 1
, that is, for any stream
X=(x_{n}:n\in\mathbb{Z}),
which satisfies
P(z)\times(x_{n}:n\in\mathbb{Z})=(0^{\infty};0^{\infty})~({\rm mod}~p^{l_{0}})
, then
(x_{n}:n\in\mathbb{Z})
is periodic with the period bounded by a function of
k
and
p^{l_{0}}
.

Now assume that
X=(x_{n};n\in\mathbb{Z})\in St(\mathbb{Z})
which satisfies

P(z)\times X=(0^{\infty};0^{\infty})~({\rm mod}~p^{l_{0}+1}).

As is well known, for any
n\in\mathbb{Z}
,
x_{n}
can be written as
x_{n}=x_{n}^{\prime}+x_{n}^{\prime\prime}p^{l_{0}}
, where
x_{n}^{\prime}\in\{0,1,\cdots,p^{l_{0}}-1\}
and
x_{n}^{\prime\prime}\in\mathbb{Z}
.
By direct computation, we obtain

P(z)\times(x_{n}^{\prime}:n\in\mathbb{Z})=(0^{\infty};0^{\infty})~({\rm mod}~p^{l_{0}}).

Equivalently, for any
n\in\mathbb{Z}
,
a_{k}x^{\prime}_{n-k}+\cdots+a_{1}x^{\prime}_{n-1}+a_{0}x^{\prime}_{n}=0({\rm mod}~p^{l_{0}}),
without loss of generality, we assume that

b_{n}p^{l_{0}}=a_{k}x^{\prime}_{n-k}+\cdots+a_{1}x^{\prime}_{n-1}+a_{0}x^{\prime}_{n},

by the assumption of the induction,
(x_{n}^{\prime}:n\in\mathbb{Z})({\rm mod}~p^{l_{0}})
is
periodic with the period bounded by a function of
k
and
p^{l_{0}}
. Clearly,
(x_{n}^{\prime}:n\in\mathbb{Z})({\rm mod}~p^{l_{0}})=(x_{n}^{\prime}:n\in\mathbb{Z})
, which follows from the definition of
x_{n}^{\prime}
.

Now consider the stream
B:=(b_{n}:n\in\mathbb{Z})\in St(\mathbb{Z})
, then
B
is periodic. Observe that the period of
(x_{n}^{\prime}:n\in\mathbb{Z})
is also the one of
B
, actually the period of
B
is a factor of that of
(x^{\prime}_{n}:n\in\mathbb{Z})
.
On the other hand,

a_{k}x_{n-k}+\cdots+a_{1}x_{n-1}+a_{0}x_{n}\\
=(a_{k}x^{\prime\prime}_{n-k}+\cdots+a_{1}x^{\prime\prime}_{n-1}+a_{0}x^{\prime\prime}_{n}+b_{n})p^{l_{0}}=0~~({\rm mod}~p^{l_{0}+1}).

Hence,

a_{k}x^{\prime\prime}_{n-k}+\cdots+a_{1}x^{\prime\prime}_{n-1}+a_{0}x^{\prime\prime}_{n}=-b_{n}~~({\rm mod}~p).

Therefore, by Lemma 4,
(x_{n}^{\prime\prime}:n\in\mathbb{Z})({\rm mod}~p)
is periodic with the period bounded by a function of

k
and the period of
B
.
This implies that
X~({\rm mod}~p^{l_{0}+1})
is periodic with a period bounded by a function
of
k
and
p^{l_{0}+1}
, which is the claim for
l=l_{0}+1
. As the claim holds, we complete the proof of proposition.

Let
P(z),Q(z)\in\mathbb{Z}[z]
be two primitive polynomials of degree
k
and
h
, respectively:

\begin{array}[]{cc}P(z)=a_{k}z^{k}+\cdots+a_{1}z+a_{0}&(a_{k}\neq 0,a_{0}\neq 0)\\
Q(z)=b_{h}z^{h}+\cdots+b_{1}z+b_{0}&(b_{h}\neq 0,b_{0}\neq 0).\end{array}

(5)

We define the Sylvester resultant matrix
\mathcal{R}
associated with
P(z)
and
Q(z)
, which is a square matrix of size
(k+h)\times(k+h)
as follows:

\mathcal{R}=\begin{pmatrix}a_{k}&a_{k-1}&\cdots&a_{0}&0&\cdots\\
\vdots&\ddots&\ddots&\ddots&\ddots&\vdots\\
0&\cdots&a_{k}&a_{k-1}&\cdots&a_{0}\\
b_{h}&b_{h-1}&\cdots&b_{0}&0&\cdots\\
\vdots&\ddots&\ddots&\ddots&\ddots&\vdots\\
0&\cdots&b_{h}&b_{h-1}&\cdots&b_{0}\end{pmatrix}

It is well known
[
18
]
that
P(z),Q(z)
are over
\mathbb{Q}[z]
if and only if
\mathcal{R}
is non-singular. Furthermore, if
P(z),Q(z)
are coprime, Bézout’s identity ensures the existence of polynomials
A(z),B(z)\in\mathbb{Z}[z]
satisfying:

\deg A<h,~\deg B<k~\mbox{and}~A(z)P(z)+B(z)Q(z)=\det\mathcal{R}\in\mathbb{Z}\setminus\{0\}.

Denote
\det(\mathcal{R})
by
\operatorname{Res}(P,Q)
.

In general, for a closed
\sigma
-invariant set
\Omega\subset\mathbb{T}^{\mathbb{Z}}

that is additionally invariant under the multiplicative action of
\mathbb{Z}
,
we define the
dimension
of
\Omega
which measures the core degrees of freedom of the shift space, denoted by
\dim\Omega
, as the maximal integer
l
such that

\{(x_{0},x_{1},\cdots,x_{l-1}):(x_{n},n\in\mathbb{Z})\in\Omega\}=\mathbb{T}^{l}.

Clearly,
\dim\Omega_{P}=k
and
\dim\Omega_{Q}=h
for the shift spaces generated by the respective polynomials
P(z),Q(z)
in (
5
).

For two streams
x=(x_{n}:n\in\mathbb{Z})
and
y=(y_{n}:n\in\mathbb{Z})\in St(\mathbb{R})
, we define their
Hadamard product
by

x\odot y:=(x_{n}y_{n}:n\in\mathbb{Z}).

Let
\mathbf{n}=(\cdots,-1;0,1,2,\cdots)\in St(\mathbb{Z})
represent the fundamental arithmetic progression stream. For any integer
i\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 1}
, we define its higher-order component via iterated Hadamard multiplication:

\mathbf{n}^{\odot i}:=\underbrace{\mathbf{n}\odot\cdots\odot\mathbf{n}}_{i\mbox{ times}}=(n^{i}:n\in\mathbb{Z})\in St(\mathbb{Z}).

By convention, for
i=0
,
\mathbf{n}^{\odot i}=(1^{\infty};1^{\infty}).

We now show the main theorem establishing a close relationship between polynomial factorizations in the ring
\mathbb{Z}[z]
and decompositions of their corresponding shift-invariant subspaces.

Theorem 4
.

Let
P(z),Q(z)\in\mathbb{Z}[z]
be primitive polynomials as in
(\ref{(5')})
.

(i)

P(z)
and
Q(z)
are coprime over
\mathbb{Q}[z]
if and only if
\operatorname{Res}(P,Q)\neq 0
. In this case, we have

\Omega_{P}\cap\Omega_{Q}\subset\{\frac{0}{\Delta},\frac{1}{\Delta},\cdots,\frac{\Delta-1}{\Delta}\}^{\mathbb{Z}}

where
\Delta=Res(P,Q)
.
Therefore,
P(z)
and
Q(z)
are coprime if and only if the internection

\Omega_{P}\cap\Omega_{Q}
is a finite set.

(ii)

P(z)
is a factor of
Q(z)
if and only if

\Omega_{P}\subset\Omega_{Q}
.

(iii)
If
P(z),Q(z)
are coprime, then we have

\Omega_{P\times Q}=\Omega_{P}\oplus\Omega_{Q}
, where
\oplus
denotes a direct sum modulo finite intersections, that is, the intersection of the summands is a finite set.

(iv)
If all roots of
P(z)=0
are simple, then for any

h\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 1}
,, we have the semi-direct decomposition

\Omega_{P^{h}}=\bigoplus_{i=0}^{h-1}\mathbf{n}^{\odot i}\odot\Omega_{P}.

Proof.

(i) Let
P(z),Q(z)
be coprime. Then, the resultant matrix
\mathcal{R}
is invertible
and the entries of
\Delta\mathcal{R}^{-1}
belong to
\mathbb{Z}
, where

\Delta=Res(P,Q)\in\mathbb{Z}\setminus\{0\}
. Since for any
j\in\mathbb{Z}
and any stream

\xi=(\xi_{i}:i\in\mathbb{Z})\in\Omega_{P}\cap\Omega_{Q}
, we have

\mathcal{R}(\xi_{j+1},\dots,\xi_{j+k+h})^{T}\in\mathbb{Z}^{k+h}
, which implies

(\xi_{j+1},\dots,\xi_{j+k+h})^{T}\in\frac{1}{\Delta}\mathbb{Z}^{k+h}.

Therefore,

\Omega_{P}\cap\Omega_{Q}\subset\{\frac{0}{\Delta},\frac{1}{\Delta},\cdots,\frac{\Delta-1}{\Delta}\}^{\mathbb{Z}}
, and by Proposition 1,

\Omega_{P}\cap\Omega_{Q}
is a finite set.

Conversely, if
P(z),Q(z)
are not coprime, they must share a common factor of
degree at least 1. Hence we have

\dim(\Omega_{P}\cap\Omega_{Q})\geq 1
, proving that the intersection
\Omega_{P}\cap\Omega_{Q}

is infinite.

(ii)Assume that
P(z)
divides
Q(z)
in
\mathbb{Z}[z]
, yielding the factorization
Q(z)=P(z)\cdot R(z)
for some
R(z)\in\mathbb{Z}[z]
.
To avoid any ambiguity, the ring product ”
\cdot
” (which may be omitted) is restricted to the polynomial domain
\mathbb{Z}[z]
, whereas the convolution operator ”
\times
” is reserved exclusively for the stream domain
St(\mathbb{R})
.

Since polynomial multiplication identifies with the discrete stream convolution operator, an elements is written as
P(z)
when treated as a polynomials in
\mathbb{Z}[z]
, can be also simplified to
P
when acting as an operators within
St(\mathbb{R})
.

Under this convention, multiplication in the polynomial ring

\mathbb{Z}[z]
corresponds directly to the discrete convolution product of their respective streams in
St(\mathbb{R})
:

Q(z)=P(z)\cdot R(z)\in\mathbb{Z}[z]\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad Q=P\times R\in St(\mathbb{R}).

(6)

Consequently, for any
\xi\in\Omega_{P}
, we have

Q(z)\times\xi=(P(z)R(z))\times\xi=R(z)\times(P(z)\times\xi)=(0^{\infty};0^{\infty}).

Hence, we have
\xi\in\Omega_{Q}
, so
\Omega_{P}\subset\Omega_{Q}
.

Conversely, if
P(z)
does not divide
Q(z)
, then there exists a
nontrivial factor
R(z)
of
P(z)
such that
R(z)
and
Q(z)
are coprime.
By (i), the space
\Omega_{Q}\cap\Omega_{R}
is finite, while
\Omega_{R}
is an infinite set.
Hence,
\Omega_{R}\subset\Omega_{Q}
does not hold.
Thus,
\Omega_{P}\subset\Omega_{Q}
does not hold since

\Omega_{P}\supset\Omega_{R}
, it follows directly that
\Omega_{P}\subset\Omega_{Q}
does not hold.

(iii) By (ii) and the fact that
\Omega_{P\cdot Q}
is a additive group, we obtain

\Omega_{P\cdot Q}\supset\Omega_{P}+\Omega_{Q}.

(7)

Since
P(z),Q(z)
are coprime, there exist polynomials with integer coefficients
A(z),B(z)
with
\deg A(z)<h
and
\deg B(z)<k

such that

P(z)A(z)+Q(z)B(z)=\Delta,

(8)

where
\Delta=Res(P,Q)\in\mathbb{Z}\setminus\{0\}
.
By (
8
) and commutativity of convolution product, we have

P\times Q\times B=P\times(\Delta-P\times A),~P\times Q\times A=Q\times(\Delta-Q\times B).

(9)

Choose an arbitrary stream
X:=(x_{n}:n\in\mathbb{Z})\in\Omega_{P\cdot Q}
.
By Corollary 1,
there exists a pre-image stream
Y:=(y_{n}:n\in\mathbb{Z})\in\Omega_{P\times Q}
satisfying

\Delta Y=X
.
By (
9
),

P\times(\Delta-P\times A)\times Y=Q\times(\Delta-Q\times B)\times Y=(0^{\infty};0^{\infty}).

Set

U=(u_{n}:n\in\mathbb{Z}):=(\Delta-P\times A)\times Y,~V=(v_{n}:n\in\mathbb{Z}):=(\Delta-Q\times B)\times Y.

By construction,
U\in\Omega_{P}
and
V\in\Omega_{Q}
.
Furthermore, by (
8
),

U+V=(2\Delta-P\times A-Q\times B)\times Y=\Delta Y=X.

Thus, we have
\Omega_{P\cdot Q}\subset\Omega_{P}+\Omega_{Q}
, hence
together with (
7
) and (i), we complete the proof of (iii).

(iv) The equation

\Omega_{P^{h}}=\sum_{i=0}^{h-1}\mathbf{n}^{\odot i}\odot\Omega_{P}

follows from the equation

\tilde{\Omega}_{P^{h}}=\sum_{i=0}^{h-1}\mathbf{n}^{\odot i}\odot\tilde{\Omega}_{P}.

For any
X=(x_{n}:n\in\mathbb{Z})\in St(\mathbb{T})
, define the formal derivative
X^{\prime}\in St(\mathbb{R})
as

\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}nx_{n}z^{n-1}
as usual. That is,

X^{\prime}=\sigma(\mathbf{n}\odot X)
,
where
\sigma
is the shift operator on
St(\mathbb{T})
. Explicitly, this formal operation acts as:

(\cdots,x_{-2},x_{-1};x_{0},x_{1},x_{2},\cdots)^{\prime}=(\cdots,-2x_{-2},-x_{-1},0;x_{1},2x_{2},\cdots).

By identifying
X:=(x_{n}:n\in\mathbb{Z})\in St(\mathbb{T})
with its formal power series
X(z)=\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}x_{n}z^{n}
, then this definition of formal derivative is compatible with analytical derivative quotients.
For instance,
d(z^{-1})/dz=(z^{-1})^{\prime}=-z^{-2}
, which matches the shift expression
(\cdots,1;0^{\infty})^{\prime}=(\cdots,-1,0;0^{\infty}).

Recall that for any scalar constant
h
and any stream
X=(x_{n}:n\in\mathbb{Z})
,

hX=(0^{\infty};h,0^{\infty})\times(x_{n}:n\in\mathbb{Z})=(hx_{n}:n\in\mathbb{Z})=(\cdots,hx_{-1};hx_{0},hx_{1},\cdots),

In general, The formal derivative operator satisfies the following:

(X+Y)^{\prime}=X^{\prime}+Y^{\prime},(X\times Y)^{\prime}=X^{\prime}\times Y+X\times Y^{\prime}.

If
X\in\Omega_{P}
, then
(P\times X)^{\prime}=(0^{\infty};0^{\infty})
.
Hence, if
X\in\Omega_{P^{h-1}}
, it follows that

(0^{\infty};0^{\infty})=(P^{h}\times X)^{\prime}=hP^{h-1}\times P^{\prime}\times X+P^{h}\times X^{\prime}=P^{h}\times\sigma(\mathbf{n}\odot X).

Therefore,
\sigma(\mathbf{n}\odot X)\in\Omega_{P^{h}}
, which yields
\mathbf{n}\odot X\in\Omega_{P^{h}}
.
By iteration, we establish the nested inclusion
\mathbf{n}\odot\Omega_{P^{h-1}}\subset\Omega_{P^{h}}
. So

\Omega_{P^{h}}\supset\sum_{i=0}^{h-1}\mathbf{n}^{\odot i}\odot\Omega_{P}~~(h=1,2,\cdots).

Remark 4

The equation (

It seems that some structural results can be obtained by the theory of unit groups in quadratic field (or Dirichlet’s unit theorem). Here we present an elementary proof utilizing the machinery of continued fractions(see

Proof.

(i) To establish the equivalence of conditions (i-1) through (i-4) under the assumption

Next, we rule out finite order elements for non-squares.
