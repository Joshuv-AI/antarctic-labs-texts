Pulmonary venous baffle stenosis following atrial switch repair for transposition of the great arteries
Authors: Sri Rama Ananta Nagabhushanam Padala, Aaditya Sirohi, Prathamesh G. Kumbhar, Anjali Tiwari
Published: 2026

Dear Editor,

Transposition of the great arteries (TGA), constituting 2% of all cyanotic congenital heart diseases, is marked by ventriculo-arterial discordance.[1] Atrial switch procedures, such as the Senning and Mustard operations, have significantly improved survival in patients with late presentation, particularly in developing countries.[2] With increasing long-term survivors, delayed complications, including systemic right ventricular dysfunction and baffle-related obstruction, are increasingly encountered. Here, we report a case of pulmonary venous baffle (PVB) stenosis that presented 7 years after Senning’s operation.

A 19-year-old male (weight 45 kg, height 165 cm) presented with a history of breathlessness, and haemoptysis for 9 months. He underwent modified Senning’s surgery for dextro-TGA with an intact interventricular septum and a regressed left ventricle 7 years back. On a transthoracic echocardiographic evaluation, he was diagnosed with grossly dilated pulmonary arteries and turbulent flow across the PVB. Pre-anaesthetic evaluation and investigations were normal. He was scheduled for surgical correction of PVB stenosis. Standard anaesthesia monitors were attached in the operating room. The intravenous line and left radial artery line were established under local anaesthesia. The patient received intravenous midazolam 2 mg, fentanyl 180 µg, propofol 70 mg, vecuronium 5 mg, and lignocaine 70 mg for induction following sufficient pre-oxygenation. The trachea was intubated with a polyvinylchloride endotracheal tube with an internal diameter of 8 mm. Anaesthesia was maintained with isoflurane, vecuronium, fentanyl, and an oxygen–air combination. The right internal jugular vein was cannulated following induction. Transoesophageal echocardiography (TEE) evaluation showed PVB stenosis at the lower end with a peak gradient of 22.99 mmHg [Figure 1a, b and Supplementary video 1]. Cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB) was established through standard aortic and bicaval cannulation after adequate systemic anticoagulation. The surgeon performed an enlargement of the PVB and the plication of the systemic venous baffle. CPB was terminated with inotropic support of intravenous adrenaline 0.05 µg/kg/min and noradrenaline 0.05 µg/kg/min. TEE evaluation to check for the adequacy of repair showed laminar flow across the PVB with reduction of peak gradient to 2.60 mmHg [Figure 1c, d and Supplementary video 2]. He was shifted to the cardiac intensive care unit under stable conditions and extubated after 6 h of elective ventilation.

Although largely replaced by the arterial switch operation, atrial switch procedures remain a viable option in TGA patients with late presentation. PVB stenosis is a rare but clinically significant complication following the Senning procedure, with a reported incidence of 1.9–7.6%.[3] It may result from baffle narrowing, scar formation, calcification, or right atrial retraction and can be managed either surgically or by transcatheter balloon dilatation.[4] In view of complex baffle anatomy with close proximity to pulmonary venous ostia, the transcatheter approach was not an option in our case. While two-dimensional TEE helps identify baffle narrowing, colour Doppler detects flow turbulence, and pulsed- or continuous-wave Doppler allows quantification of stenosis by measuring transbaffle pressure gradients. A gradient exceeding 4–5 mmHg in the pulmonary venous pathway or individual pulmonary veins is considered significant and warrants correction.[4] Intraoperative TEE plays a pivotal role in confirming the diagnosis and evaluating the integrity and flow in the baffles as well as ventricular function.
