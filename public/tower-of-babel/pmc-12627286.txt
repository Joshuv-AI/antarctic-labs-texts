TKI resistance related genes in LUAD predict patient prognosis and tumor immune microenvironment with LHX2 as a hub gene
Authors: Ruohan Huang, Qi Liang, Hongya Wang, Huizhu Qian, Renhua Guo
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundNon-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC), particularly lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD), is a leading cause of cancer-related mortality. Osimertinib, a third-generation epidermal growth factor receptor-tyrosine kinase inhibitor (EGFR-TKI), improves survival, but acquired resistance remains a major clinical challenge.MethodsTranscriptomic profiles of osimertinib-sensitive and -resistant EGFR-mutant cell lines were analyzed to identify resistance mechanisms. Resistance-associated genes were used to develop a Cox proportional hazards (CoxPH) prognostic model based on The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) data. Immune context and tumor mutational burden (TMB) were compared between risk groups. Core gene expression was validated by reverse transcription quantitative polymerase chain reaction (RT‒qPCR). Functional experiments including Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK8) and methylthiazolyldiphenyl-tetrazolium bromide (MTT) assays were performed to evaluate the impact of core gene on the proliferative capacities of EGFR-TKI resistance cells.ResultsResistant cells showed gene expression alterations enriched in proliferation (e.g., Wnt) and cell cycle pathways. An 18-gene signature strongly predicted overall survival (concordance index [C-index] = 0.74). High-risk patients exhibited more immunosuppressive microenvironments and higher TMB. Nine genes (GLI2, LHX2, PITX3, MSX1, ANO1, MUC5B, BEST4, SLC46A3, ECEL1P2) were independent prognostic factors, and LHX2 occupied a central position. LHX2 was upregulated in resistant cells, and its knockdown restored osimertinib sensitivity.ConclusionThis study reveals novel genes and mechanisms in osimertinib resistance. The prognostic model stratifies LUAD patients effectively, and LHX2 represents a promising therapeutic target for reversing resistance.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s12672-025-03959-1.Keywords: NSCLC, Osimertinib, Drug resistance, LHX2, Prognosis productive model

Introduction

As one of the most widespread cancers globally, lung cancer remains a primary cause of cancer-associated mortality [1]. Approximately 85% of lung cancer cases are classified as NSCLC. Among NSCLC subtypes, LUAD is the most prevalent [2]. Notably, most LUAD patients are diagnosed at an advanced stage of disease, and despite the development of various therapeutic strategies, their clinical outcomes remain unsatisfactory. The 5-year overall survival (OS) rate for advanced-stage LUAD patients continues to be under 20%.

The discovery of EGFR mutations has profoundly reshaped the therapeutic paradigm for LUAD [3]. Precision therapy for lung adenocarcinoma was initiated with the approval of the first EGFR inhibitor in 2003 and was further propelled by the discovery of specific EGFR driver mutations in 2004 [4]. Currently, third-generation EGFR-TKIs are the standard-of-care first-line treatment for NSCLC with EGFR mutations in advanced disease [5]. Numerous large-scale clinical trials, such as the FLAURA series trial, have also fully demonstrated the effectiveness of osimertinib effectiveness [6]. Nevertheless, most patients eventually acquire resistance to osimertinib, resulting in subsequent disease progression [7, 8]. Researchers have been continuously investigating the causes of resistance to EGFR-TKIs. The more widely accepted causes include secondary EGFR mutations, bypass signaling pathways, and histological transformation [3].Fundamentally, dysregulation of gene expression serves as a major driver of therapeutic resistance [9]. Clinically, patients generally undergo rebiopsy following the development of resistance to targeted therapy. The expression of certain genes is altered or activated in this process [10]. These genes may be closely correlated with multiple processes of malignant progression and development of tumor cells, as well as resistance to therapeutic agents [11]. Thus, a thorough dissection of these genes implicated in drug resistance and an exploration of their potential value in prognostic assessment hold significant implications for refining therapeutic strategies and enhancing patient prognosis. While resistance-related genes have been extensively studied in the context of LUAD progression and therapy, the predictive potential of tyrosine kinase inhibitor resistance-associated differentially expressed genes (TRRDEGs) for patient outcomes and clinicopathological traits remains insufficiently explored. Meanwhile, significant advances have been achieved in the fields of bioinformatics and genomics in recent years. Compared with animal or cell experiments, bioinformatics is safer and has shown superiority in analyzing large-scale clinical data.

Here, we obtained data for EGFR-TKI resistant and sensitive LUAD cells by analyzing gene expression data from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database. Then we carried out cluster analysis and functional enrichment analysis. Furthermore, we developed the risk score for prognosis prediction of LUAD by Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator (LASSO) and Cox analysis. The connections of the risk score with immune tumor microenvironment (TME) compositions and clinical common drugs resistance were also investigated. By integrating the above analysis with Protein-Protein Interaction (PPI) analysis, we identified LHX2, LIM-homeobox gene 2, as a key gene among these differentially expressed genes (DEGs). We further carried out a detailed investigation of LHX2 in vitro. Consequently, we identified key genes involved in TKI resistance and developed a gene signature that may serve as a prognostic biomarker and guide personalized treatment strategies for LUAD.

Materials and methods

Data access

The initial gene expression data counts for GSE262582, GSE249721 [12], GSE254485 [13], GSE272162, GSE223006 [14], GSE222820 [15], GSE103350, GSE193258 [16–18] were retrieved from the GEO database. The PC-9 and HCC827 cell lines, which are extensively utilized in studies exploring the mechanisms of sensitivity to EGFR-targeted therapies, in the development of resistance models, and in high-throughput drug screening, were selected as the cellular models for subsequent analyses. To eliminate batch effects between different datasets, the “removeBatchEffect” function from the “limma” R package was applied. Additionally, RNA sequencing data from lung adenocarcinoma patients and normal lung tissues, together with relevant clinical and survival data, were extracted from the TCGA database for subsequent prognostic analysis.

Screening of osimertinib resistant-related differentially expressed genes (ORRDEGs)

The R package “DESeq2” (version 1.44.0) was utilized in the standard comparison mode [19]. Grouping and batch variables were incorporated into the analysis. The criteria for selecting DEGs for further exploration were established at |log2FoldChange| >1 and a padj < 0.05. The findings of the differential expression analysis were depicted through a volcano plot created with the R package “ggplot2” and a heatmap assembled with the “heatmap” package. Pairwise intersections were executed for up-regulated and down-regulated DEGs between the two cell lines.

Differential gene function and pathway enrichment analysis

The R Package “clusterProfiler” (version 4.12.2) was employed to perform Gene Ontology (GO), Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway, and Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) on the DEGs from each cell line [20]. The significance thresholds were set at a P-value < 0.05 and a false discovery rate (FDR/q-value) < 0.05 for gene selection.

Construction of a prognostic model based on TCGA data

Shared DEGs from PC-9 and HCC827 cell lines were integrated with TCGA-LUAD transcriptomic and overall survival data to construct and evaluate a prognostic model using the“survival”and“survminer” R packages. Genes that exhibited a significant correlation with OS (P < 0.05) were detected via univariate Cox regression analysis. A prognostic signature was developed through LASSO regression, retaining genes with non-zero coefficients as resistance-associated candidates. Construction of a prognostic nomogram

We performed Kaplan-Meier survival analysis to find the clinical features affected prognosis, including sex, age, and TNM stage. These variables, along with the risk groups, were incorporated into a nomogram with “rms” R package. Calibration curves were plotted based on survival time to assess the model performance.

Differential expression analysis of core drug resistance-associated genes

Differential expression analysis of drug resistance-associated genes was performed by “DESeq2” on different groups from the TCGA database. Violin plots were generated to visualize expression differences. A heatmap was plotted to show the expression of these core genes across both groups from two cell lines.

Immunoinfiltration analysis

Utilizing “CIBERSORT” package enabled us to assess the association between the RiskScore and the infiltration levels of 22 immune cell subsets. The “corheatmap” function from the “Tinyarray” R package was utilized to generate a heatmap illustrating the correlations between core drug resistance-associated genes and immune cell populations. Boxplots were generated to compare the levels of immune cell infiltration and immune checkpoint gene expression in both risk groups.

Mutations and methylation analysis

Copy number variation (CNV), single nucleotide variation (SNV), and DNA methylation analyses of the core drug resistance-associated genes were conducted using the Gene Set Cancer Analysis (GSCA) database (https://guolab.wchscu.cn/GSCA) [21]. Mutation Annotation Format (MAF) data for TCGA-LUAD were obtained via the R package “TCGAmutations”. Somatic non-synonymous point mutations were quantified for each sample using the R package “maftools” (v2.20.0) [22], and the mutation frequencies of core genes were compared in two risk groups. An oncoplot was generated to visually represent the mutational landscape of the two groups.

PPI network analysis

PPI networks among the core drug-resistance-associated genes were predicted using the STRING database(https://cn.string-db.org/).

Drug resistance analysis

Drug sensitivity analysis of the core drug resistance associated genes was performed using the GSCA platform, focusing on their correlations with drug response data from the Genomics of Drug Sensitivity in Cancer (GDSC) and Cancer Therapeutics Response Portal (CTRP) databases.

Functional enrichment analysis based on LHX2 expression

Samples were categorized into high versus low LHX2 expression clusters according to the median expression level. DEGs between the two groups were identified, followed by GO, KEGG and GSEA to explore the enriched biological pathways associated with LHX2 expression.

Cell culture and establishment of drug-resistant cell lines

PC-9 and HCC827 cell lines were sourced from the Cell Bank of the Chinese Academy of Sciences (Shanghai, China). To establish osimertinib-resistant (OR) sublines, the parental PC-9 and HCC827 cells were continuously exposed to escalating doses of osimertinib, starting at 10 nM [23]. After approximately 25 weeks of treatment, resistant clones capable of proliferating in the presence of the drug were established. PC-9 and PC-9/OR cells were cultured in DMEM, while HCC827 and HCC827/OR cells were maintained in RPMI-1640 medium. All culture media were supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum and 1% penicillin-streptomycin. Cells were incubated at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO₂.

RNA extraction and RT‒qPCR analyses

Total RNA was isolated from cells using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, USA) following the manufacturer’s protocol. Complementary DNA (cDNA) synthesis was performed with a reverse transcription kit (Takara, Japan). RT-qPCR was subsequently conducted using the TB Green RT-qPCR kit (Takara, Japan) according to the provided instructions. β-actin served as the internal reference gene. Primer sequences used are listed as follows:

LHX2: Forward:5′-TTCCAGAACGCCCGAGCCAA-3′;

Reverse:5′-GGGGCTAGTCAAGTCTGTC-3′;

β-actin: Forward: 5′-CTCCATCCTGGCCTCGCTGT-3′;

Reverse: 5′-GCTGTCACCTTCACCGTTCC-3′;

Cell transfection

Specific small interfering RNA (siRNA) and negative control siRNA (si-NC) transfection was performed using Lipofectamine 2000 Transfection Reagent (Invitrogen, USA). The target sequence of LHX2 were as follows: si-LHX2 1#, CUGCGCUAAGCUGCAACGAAA; si-LHX2 2#, AGCUUCGGACCAUGAAGUCUU.

Cell proliferation assays

Cell viability was assessed using the CCK-8 assay (New Cell & Molecular Biotech, China) in PC-9/OR and HCC827/OR cells. Following LHX2 knockdown, cells were seeded at 3 × 103 cells/well in 96-well plates (Corning, NY, USA) and treated with various concentrations of osimertinib (0–9 µM). After 72 h, 100 µL of CCK-8 reagent was added to each well, and the plates were incubated for 1 h. The absorbance at 450 nm was measured using a microplate reader. Each treatment group was assayed in three independent replicates. Dose-response curves were plotted, and the half-maximal inhibitory concentration (IC50) values were calculated. Additionally, the effect of LHX2 knockdown on tumor cell growth over time was assessed using the 3-(4,5-dimethylthiazol-2-yl)-2,5-diphenyltetrazolium bromide (MTT) assay. Transfected cells were seeded in 96-well plates, and cell viability was measured every 24 h using an MTT kit (Sigma, USA). The absorbance at 490 nm was measured using a microplate reader. Each group was analyzed in three independent replicates.

Statistical analysis

Data analysis for cell-related experiments was performed using SPSS (IBM, SPSS, USA) and GraphPad Prism software (GraphPad Software, USA). The student’s t test, chi-squared test, or Wilcoxon test was used to compare the variables. Two-sided p values of 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Gene expression counts and associated clinical details sourced from TCGA comprised 541 tumor and 59 normal specimens (Tab S2).

Acquisition of drug resistance by tumor cells through the modulation of cell cycle and other pathways.

GSEA was performed on the DEGs from both cell lines. In the osimertinib-resistant PC-9 cells, upregulated pathways included Myogenesis, Interferon Gamma Response, and KRAS Signaling DN, while downregulated pathways included E2F Targets, G2M Checkpoint, and MYC Targets V1. In the HCC827 resistant cells, upregulated pathways mainly involved Epithelial-Mesenchymal Transition (EMT), Myogenesis, and Wnt/β-catenin Signaling, whereas G2M Checkpoint, E2F Targets, and mTORC1 Signaling were significantly downregulated (Fig. 1C, D, H, I). Furthermore, functional GO and KEGG pathway enrichment assessments were performed with upregulated and downregulated DEGs from both resistant cell lines. Pathways with the most significant adjusted p-values and relevance to tumor biology were selected for visualization, as shown in Fig. 1E and J.

The prognostic model based on ORRDEGs demonstrates favorable predictive efficacy

A total of 567 intersecting DEGs were integrated with the TCGA-LUAD transcriptomic dataset. Univariate Cox regression analysis pinpointed 119 genes with a significant correlation with OS, and these genes were subsequently subjected to LASSO regression to construct a prognostic model. The ideal parameter for LASSO regression was ascertained by 10-fold cross-validation (logλ = 0.030), and the resulting model achieved a C-index of 0.745. Genes selected from the LASSO regression were further analyzed using stepwise multivariate CoxPH regression modeling, achieving a C-index of 0.740 (Fig. 2A–C). Ultimately, 18 genes were included in the final CoxPH model. Among them, GLI2, LHX2, PITX3, MSX1, ANO1, and HPCAL1 were significantly linked to a worse prognosis (hazard ratio > 1), while BEST4, SLC46A3, and ECEL1P2 were significantly related to a favorable outcome (hazard ratio < 1). We further examined the expression patterns of the osimertinib resistance related genes included in the prognostic model across sensitive and resistant samples of the two cell lines, as well as in neoplastic and corresponding non-neoplastic tissues within the TCGA dataset (Fig S2A). Among these genes, only LHX2 was consistently overexpressed in tumors and linked to a worse prognosis. Therefore, LHX2 was identified as a core prognostic gene among the osimertinib resistance related candidates (Fig. 2D, E).

The predictive model integrating the riskscore with clinical information exhibits robust performance

Kaplan-Meier survival analyses were performed based on clinical characteristics available in the TCGA dataset, including age, sex, and TNM stage. Among these variables, the T, N, and M stages were identified as significantly correlated with OS (Fig. 3A-D, Fig S2B, C). The RiskScore calculated from the CoxPH model was used to stratify patients into high- and low-risk groups based on the median value. The specific formulas are provided in the supplementary materials. Kaplan–Meier analysis demonstrated a significant difference in OS between the two risk groups. The distribution of patients in the TCGA-LUAD cohort according to risk group, as well as the expression patterns of model-included genes, was visualized accordingly (Fig. 3E). A nomogram was constructed by integrating the three significant clinical variables (T, N, and M stage) with the risk stratification from the CoxPH model to predict 1-, 3-, and 5-year OS (Fig. 3F). Calibration curves demonstrated good predictive performance of the nomogram across all time points (Fig. 3G).

LHX2 regulates EGFR-TKI resistance in NSCLC cells

To validate the acquired resistance of PC-9/OR and HCC827/OR cells to osimertinib, we compared IC50 values between resistant cells and their corresponding parental lines (PC-9 and HCC827). The elevated IC50 values observed in the resistant cells confirmed the successful establishment of osimertinib resistant cell models (Fig. 7A). We assessed LHX2 mRNA expression levels in these paired cell lines and observed a significantly upregulation of LHX2 in the resistant cells relative to the parental counterparts (Fig. 7B). To further explore the biological characteristics of LHX2 in EGFR-TKI resistant cells, siRNA-mediated knockdown was used to manipulate LHX2 expression (Fig. 7C, F). CCK8 assays revealed that LHX2 knockdown significantly decreased IC50 in TKI-resistant cells (Fig. 7D, G). The MTT assay results also revealed that LHX2 knockdown reduced resistance to EGFR-TKIs and cell proliferation in TKI-resistant cells (Fig. 7E, H).

Discussion

NSCLC often harbors genetic mutations in EGFR. The third-generation TKIs, represented by osimertinib, have become a crucial therapeutic strategy for patients harboring these mutations. As first-line management of advanced EGFR-mutated NSCLC, osimertinib has demonstrated significant prolongation of progression-free survival (PFS) with median PFS ranging from 17.8 to 20.8 months [24]. FLAURA series studies confirm osimertinib’s significant efficacy in extending median PFS [25, 26]. The ADAURA trial showed that adjuvant osimertinib after surgery in stage IB–IIIA EGFR-mutant NSCLC patients achieved a 24-month disease-free survival rate of 90% in stage II–IIIA and 89% overall [27]. Although osimertinib shows clinical effectiveness, resistance inevitably develops, usually after a median duration of 19 months when used as first-line therapy and 11 months in the second-line setting [28]. Acquired resistance remains a major barrier to long-term treatment success, with resistance mechanisms unidentified in approximately 50% of patients. Known mechanisms include secondary mutations, activation of bypass signaling pathways and histologic transformation [29, 30]. We believe that dysregulated gene expression plays a crucial role in driving resistance, and therefore, establishing a gene signature related to TKI resistance not only has the potential to effectively predict prognosis but also finding patients who may benefit from alternative therapies.

We first identified DEGs between sensitive and osimertinib resistant cells in PC-9 and HCC827 cell lines. GSEA analysis highlighted that downregulated pathways in both resistant cell lines were G2M Checkpoint, E2F Targets, and MYC Targets, while the upregulated pathways included Myogenesis, Interferon Gamma Response, and EMT. These results suggest that osimertinib resistant tumor cells may evade drug-induced cytotoxicity through dysregulation of the cell cycle, induction of EMT and immune escape [31–33]. GO and KEGG enrichment analyses further showed that upregulated DEGs were predominantly involved in proliferative signaling pathways such as Wnt, PI3K–Akt, and MAPK, as well as in endothelial and extracellular matrix (ECM) remodeling. In contrast, downregulated pathways were mainly associated with cellular metabolism and molecular transport. Our GSEA results suggest that in osimertinib resistant cells, tumor cells may reduce their susceptibility to treatment by downregulating cell cycle related pathways (e.g., E2F), while compensatory activating alternative proliferative signaling pathways to sustain survival [34–36]. In addition, these cells may attempt to remodel the ECM to create a physical barrier against drug penetration [37].

We identified 18 genes that were significantly linked to OS from drug resistance-related genes shared by both two cell lines through univariate Cox regression, LASSO regression, and multivariate CoxPH analysis in the TCGA-LUAD cohort. These genes were applied to establish a CoxPH-based prognostic model, which generates a RiskScore based on tumor transcriptomic profiles to predict OS in LUAD patients. Patients classified as high-risk exhibited markedly poorer survival than those in the low-risk category, and the model achieved robust predictive accuracy with a C-index of 0.74. Based on this resistance-related gene signature, we further integrated the RiskScore with clinical variables to construct a comprehensive nomogram. The nomogram exhibited high predictive accuracy for one-, three-, and five-year overall survival probabilities, supporting its capacity to serve as a practical means of personalized prognostic estimation prediction in LUAD patients.

It has been proven by previous studies that TKI therapy can remodel the TME, but the impact of TKI resistance on the TME remains unclear [38, 39]. Building upon these findings, we extended our investigation to the interplay between resistance-associated genes and TME. Immune infiltration analysis disclosed notable differences among patients stratified by risk (high vs. low). M0 macrophages, activated mast cells, activated memory CD4⁺ T cells, and resting NK cells were significantly elevated in the high-risk group, while resting mast cells, resting memory CD4⁺ T cells, resting dendritic cells, and monocytes were markedly decreased. We also evaluated the expression levels of various immune checkpoint genes in both groups. In the high-risk group, the immunosuppressive gene CD267 was significantly upregulated, whereas co-stimulatory molecules such as CD40LG, CD48, and TNFRSF25 were significantly downregulated, indicating a relatively immunosuppressive and T cell exhaustion immune state. Interestingly, inhibitory receptors including BTLA, CD200R1, and CD160 were also expressed at lower levels in the high-risk group. This phenomenon may be explained by the method of risk stratification, where patients were equally divided based on the median RiskScore, and by the limited number of terminal-stage patients in the TCGA cohort. Additionally, the immune infiltration analysis was based on bulk RNA-seq, which may introduce certain limitations and biases. TMB analysis revealed that mutations in key oncogenes such as TP53 and KRAS were more frequent in the high-risk group. These findings collectively demonstrate that our LUAD prognostic model, constructed based on osimertinib resistance related genes, not only stratifies patients by clinical outcome but also correlates clearly with the immune landscape The model has potential utility in pinpointing patients with a higher likelihood of benefiting from targeted and immune-based treatment approaches.

Among the 18 genes included in our prognostic model, GLI2, LHX2, PITX3, MSX1, ANO1, MUC5B, and HPCAL1 were identified as independent risk factors for OS, with their higher expression levels associated with poorer prognosis. In contrast, BEST4, SLC46A3, and ECEL1P2 were positively correlated with OS, suggesting a potential protective role. Notably, GLI2, Glioma-Associated Oncogene Family Zinc Finger 2, is a terminal transcription factor in the Hedgehog signaling cascade and has been implicated in growth and progression of various solid tumors, including cholangiocarcinoma [40], colorectal cancer [41], and renal cell carcinoma [42]. Consistent with our findings, Chen demonstrated that GLI2 overexpression significantly enhances the proliferative capacity of lung cancer cells and confers resistance to EGFR-targeted inhibitors and conventional chemotherapeutic agents [43]. In breast cancer, GLI2 has been shown to promote anoikis resistance and facilitate metastasis, further supporting its role in mediating tumor aggressiveness and therapeutic resistance [44]. LHX2, LIM-homeobox gene 2, functions as a nuclear transcriptional co-regulator and plays essential roles in diverse processes such as cell proliferation, differentiation, and neural development [45]. Accumulating evidence has shown that LHX2 exerts oncogenic functions in numerous cancers, including breast cancer, esophageal squamous cell carcinoma (ESCC), and nasopharyngeal carcinoma [46–48]. Yang reported that miR-124 suppresses NSCLC cell proliferation, invasion, and migration by targeting and silencing LHX2, indicating a functional role of LHX2 in promoting tumor progression in NSCLC [49]. Furthermore, Fan Shi et al. demonstrated that downregulation of LHX2 expression can reverse cisplatin resistance in cervical cancer cells [50], suggesting that LHX2 may also contribute to chemoresistance mechanisms. Interestingly, DNA methylation is generally known to suppress mRNA transcription [51]. However, in our analysis, LHX2 in lung adenocarcinoma patients exhibited a paradoxical state of concurrent hypermethylation and high expression. A similar co-occurrence of LHX2 hypermethylation and high expression has also been observed in breast cancer patients [46, 52]. The study by Garth H. Rauscher et al. [53] offers a plausible explanation: the methylation of LHX2 occurs primarily in intronic regions rather than the promoter, which is conversely associated with enhanced gene expression. PITX3, Paired-like homeodomain transcription factor 3, is essential for the proper development of both neuronal tissues and the ocular lens. Several clinical prognostic studies have identified PITX3 methylation as an independent risk factor for poor prognosis [54, 55]. MSX1, Msh Homeobox 1, is involved in odontogenesis. Previous studies have demonstrated its role in inhibiting tumor cell proliferation and promoting apoptosis in breast and cervical cancer models [56, 57]. ANO1, Anoctamin 1, encodes a calcium-activated chloride channel and has been shown to play a role in the progression of various cancers [58, 59]. In ESCC, upregulation of ANO1 was shown to alter cholesterol metabolism and stimulate fibroblast-mediated metastatic growth [60]. Moreover, recent evidence suggests that ANO1 may mediate resistance to immunotherapy [61], highlighting its multifaceted role in tumor biology and treatment response. MUC5B, Mucin 5B, encodes a secreted mucin involved in the formation of mucus in saliva and the respiratory tract. Elevated expression of MUC5B has been observed in colorectal cancer and non-small cell neuroendocrine carcinoma (NSCNC) [62, 63]. A study demonstrated that silencing MUC5B expression reduced the chemoresistance of MCF-7 breast cancer cells [64]. HPCAL1, Hippocalcin Like 1, belongs to the neuron-specific calcium-binding protein family, primarily expressed in the retina and brain. Chen et al. identified HPCAL1 as a selective autophagy receptor involved in ferroptosis [65]. Functionally, HPCAL1 exhibits context-dependent roles in cancers. It promotes tumor growth in NSCLC [66], but inhibits tumor progression in hepatocellular carcinoma [67]. BEST4, Bestrophin 4, primarily expressed in intestinal epithelial cells, belongs to the bestrophin family of anion channels. A recent study by Wang demonstrated that BEST4 suppresses tumor progression and EMT in colorectal cancer cells, and reduced expression levels have been linked to unfavorable clinical outcomes [68]. Moreover, He reported that silencing BEST4 expression sensitized colorectal cancer cells to gefitinib, suggesting a potential role in modulating response to EGFR-targeted therapies [69]. These findings are consistent with our study. SLC46A3, Solute Carrier Family 46 Member 3, encodes a transmembrane protein localized to the lysosomal membrane. In hepatocellular carcinoma, SLC46A3 expression was found to be downregulated [70], and unlike our findings in LUAD, high SLC46A3 expression was previously reported to enhance sensitivity to sorafenib, indicating potential tumor type-specific effects in modulating drug resistance. ECEL1P2, Endothelin Converting Enzyme Like 1 Pseudogene 2, is a pseudogene with limited functional annotation to date. Collectively, these genes have potential utility not only as predictors of prognosis but also as targets for therapeutic intervention to counteract acquired osimertinib resistance. Further functional studies and external validation are required to clarify their roles and to facilitate their translation into clinical application.

Further analysis of the above genes revealed that LHX2 is significantly linked with both poor prognosis in LUAD patients and resistance to osimertinib. In the constructed PPI network, LHX2 was located at a central hub, suggesting its potential regulatory importance. In our drug resistance analysis, a significant correlation between LHX2 and resistance to other EGFR-TKIs, including gefitinib, afatinib, and erlotinib, was observed, indicating its broader role in mediating resistance across multiple targeted therapies. Moreover, pathway enrichment analysis in patients with high LHX2 expression showed upregulation of several tumor-promoting signaling pathways, such as E2F targets. In the aforementioned comparison between osimertinib-resistant and sensitive cells, pathways such as the G2M Checkpoint and E2F Targets showed a downregulation trend, which appears inconsistent with the results presented here. However, this discrepancy is not contradictory. These pathways are involved in regulating the mitotic cell cycle. In resistant cell lines, although tumor cells develop resistance to osimertinib, they cannot fully counteract the effects of EGFR pathway blockade. The coordinated action of LHX2 with multiple genes ultimately results in diminished mitotic activity in tumor cells. As previously discussed, diminished cell division capacity represents one mechanism by which tumor cells evade EGFR-TKI-induced cytotoxicity. In contrast, when analyzing TCGA-LUAD data based on high and low LHX2 expression groups, the treatment history regarding EGFR-TKI usage was not accounted for. In this context, LHX2 primarily exerts its role in promoting tumor cell proliferation. Consequently, in the high-LHX2 expression group, enhanced tumor cell division is associated with upregulation of E2F targets and concomitant activation of the G2M checkpoint pathway.

To further validate the upregulated expression of LHX2 in EGFR-TKI–resistant conditions, we performed RT-qPCR in two osimertinib resistant cell lines, which confirmed significantly elevated LHX2 expression compared to their sensitive counterparts. Concurrently, cell-based assays showed that LHX2 knockdown enhanced the drug sensitivity and suppressed the proliferation of drug-resistant tumor cells. According to a review by He et al. [71], the primary mechanisms of EGFR-TKI resistance in NSCLC patients include: EGFR tertiary mutations and amplification, activation of downstream EGFR signaling pathways, activation of bypass signaling pathways, and EMT. Existing studies demonstrate that LHX2 can promote the expression of various growth factors such as PDGF [72], FGF1 [48], and HGF [73]. Furthermore, LHX2 has been shown to aberrantly activate key downstream pathways of EGFR, including PI3K/AKT/mTOR, STAT3, and ERK1/2 [46, 48]. In breast cancer, LHX2 also promotes EMT [72]. Taken together, these findings suggest that LHX2 may contribute to osimertinib resistance in NSCLC patients through multiple mechanisms.

Conclusion

In this study, by comparing gene expression and pathway alterations in osimertinib resistant cells, we identified several novel key genes associated with osimertinib resistance in lung adenocarcinoma. The prognostic model built upon these genes revealed strong predictive value for overall survival and may serve as a useful tool to guide clinical decision-making. Among these genes, LHX2 emerged as a critical factor, potentially acting as a therapeutic target to overcome resistance to osimertinib and improve patient outcomes.

Limitations

This study has certain limitations. Most of the analyses were conducted based on publicly available datasets, and therefore lack extensive experimental validation. Furthermore, the prognostic model has not yet been externally validated in independent clinical cohorts, which will be essential in future studies to confirm its generalizability and clinical utility.
