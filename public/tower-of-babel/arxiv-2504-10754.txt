auto-fpt : Automating Free Probability Theory Calculations for Machine Learning Theory
Arjun Subramonian, Elvis Dohmatob

Abstract
A large part of modern machine learning theory often involves computing the high-dimensional expected trace of a rational expression of large rectangular random matrices. To symbolically compute such quantities using free probability theory, we introduce auto-fpt , a lightweight Python and SymPy-based tool that can automatically produce a reduced system of fixed-point equations which can be solved for the quantities of interest, and effectively constitutes a theory. We overview the algorithmic ideas underlying auto-fpt and its applications to various interesting problems, such as the high-dimensional error of linearized feed-forward neural networks, recovering well-known results. We hope that auto-fpt streamlines the majority of calculations involved in high-dimensional analysis, while helping the machine learning community reproduce known and uncover new phenomena. Our code can be found at: https://github.com/ArjunSubramonian/auto-fpt .



1
Introduction

A common task in machine learning (ML) theory is computing the
high-dimensional
limiting value of the expected normalized trace of a rational expression
R
of rectangular random matrices, i.e.,

\displaystyle r=\lim_{\phi,\psi,\ldots}\mathbb{E}\,\bar{\operatorname{tr}}\,R(A,B,\ldots;Z,W,\ldots;\lambda,\alpha,t,\ldots),

(1)

where
\bar{\operatorname{tr}}\,
is the normalized
\operatorname{tr}
operator, which maps a square matrix to the average of its eigenvalues. Equation
1
contains the following elements:

Deterministic matrices:

A,B,\ldots
represent deterministic matrices, e.g., the population covariance matrices for multivariate Gaussian distributions.

Random matrices:

Z,W,\ldots
are rectangular Gaussian random matrices with IID entries from
{\cal N}(0,c)
, for some constant
c>0
. For example,
Z
could be the whitened version of the design matrix for a regression problem, while
W
could be the Glorot-initialized weights in the random features or NTK approximation of a neural network.

Scaling constants:

\phi,\psi,\ldots
are scalars which might describe some data structural constraints or model design choices which must be respected while taking the limit. These include the aspect ratio
d/n
(i.e., rate of features to samples) and parametrization
m/n
(i.e., rate of parameters to samples).

Practitioner interventions:

\lambda,\alpha,t
are scalars which capture the interventions of ML practitioners. This includes the choice of regularization penalty, and step size and stopping time for optimization algorithms.
This also encompasses the coefficients of Hermite polynomials corresponding to the choice of activation functions in the case of neural networks.

R
typically captures a quantity like generalization or population test error
(
Hastie et al. 2022
;
Dobriban & Wager 2018
;
Adlam & Pennington 2020a
;
Bach 2024
)
, adversarial robustness error
(
Scetbon & Dohmatob 2023
)
, out-of-distribution error
(
Tripuraneni et al. 2021
)
, or subgroup test error
(
Subramonian et al. 2025
)
. The sought-for limit
r
is often considered in a regime where the dimensions of the rectangular matrices diverge at given proportionate rates, e.g.,
d/n\to\phi
,
m/n\to\psi
, where
d
is the input dimension,
n
is the sample size, and
m
is the width of the neural network.
To an ML theorist, constants like
\phi
,
\psi
, and
\lambda
play an analogous role to the constants that appear in the standard model of physics (e.g., mass of electron, charge of electron).
A theorist can analyze
r
in terms of constants like
\phi
,
\psi
, and
\lambda
to obtain fundamental insights into ML.

In practice, the limit
r
can be precisely computed analytically using the framework of operator-valued free probability theory (FPT)
(
Mingo & Speicher 2017
)
, which is at the intersection of random matrix theory (RMT) and functional analysis. An alternative approach is via replica symmetry (RS) calculations. Though analytically powerful, RS is not rigorous and its proper use in ML theory requires some training in statistical physics. In comparison, FPT only requires knowledge of linear algebra and statistics.

Main contributions.
We introduce and document a lightweight Python-based tool called
auto-fpt
for automatically computing
r
symbolically, as a function of all the relevant constants. We further show how to use
auto-fpt
to compute the high-dimensional: (1) Stieltjes transform of the Marchenko-Pastur law, (2) generalization error of classical ridge regression, (3) sum of two sample covariance matrices (subordination), and (4) training error of a random features model.
Our tool takes as input a linear pencil for
R
and outputs a reduced system of fixed-point equations that can be solved to obtain
r
.
We introduce various techniques to make the computation of these equations more tractable, especially for large pencils, e.g., matrix scalarization, sparse block matrix inversion, and duplicate equation identification and pruning.
auto-fpt
currently assumes that the random matrices are Gaussian with IID entries and the deterministic matrices commute.

A primary goal of
auto-fpt
is to make the machinery of FPT more accessible to ML theorists and practitioners, so that they can focus on other important aspects of the theoretical analysis of ML models, rather than tedious case-by-case algebraic manipulations.
To the best of our knowledge, there do not exist any open-access tools that automatically symbolically compute the high-dimensional limiting value of the expected normalized trace of rational expressions from their pencils (see Section
2
).
The only dependencies required by
auto-fpt
are SymPy
(
Meurer et al. 2017
)
and NumPy
(
Harris et al. 2020
)
, both of which are well-established open-source Python libraries.
We hope that the ML community leverages
auto-fpt
to reproduce known and uncover new phenomena in ML that contribute to our foundational understanding of phenomena like generalization, neural scaling laws, feature learning, memorization, grokking, model collapse, and algorithmic fairness, in solvable regimes which are relevant to neural networks and large language models. This is especially important in the context of emerging practices (e.g., training on synthetic data, using attention, adapting to human feedback).

2
Related Work

High-dimensional analysis of ML with FPT.

A suite of works have used operator-valued FPT to analyze the expected behavior of ML models in appropriate asymptotic scaling limits, e.g., the rate of features
d
to samples
n
converges to a finite values as
d
and
n
respectively scale towards infinity. For example,

Adlam & Pennington 2020a
theoretically analyze the double descent phenomenon
(
Spigler et al. 2019
;
Belkin et al. 2019
)
in ridge regression with random projections in a proportionate scaling limit.

Adlam & Pennington 2020b
precisely characterize the behavior of wide neural networks (e.g., triple descent).
Tripuraneni et al. 2021
present a high-dimensional analysis of how covariate shifts affect the generalization of random feature regression in different parameterization regimes.
Lee et al. 2023
analyze the disagreement of random feature models in high dimensions.

Dohmatob et al. 2025
;
Dohmatob 2021
precisely characterize the model collapse phenomenon in the random features regime, showing that even a small fraction of synthetic training data can yield a performance degradation.

Subramonian et al. 2025
study how random feature models amplify data biases in different parameterization regimes.
auto-fpt
can be used to automatically recover the FPT components of the proofs in the aforementioned works, and expand upon their results.

Automating FPT computations.
NCAlgebra
(
Helton & de Oliveira 2017
)
offers functionality to compute minimal linear pencils (specifically, descriptor realizations) for rational expressions. However, to our knowledge,
auto-fpt
is the first open-source and open-access tool to automatically symbolically compute the high-dimensional limiting value of the expected trace of rational expressions from their pencils. Our
auto-fpt
tool aims to fill this gap using FPT, in a manner that supports ML theory research. We note that the primary contribution of
auto-fpt
is
not
pencil computation.

4
Preliminaries for Ridge Regression

For simplicity and as a specific running example of
r
with which to explain
auto-fpt
, we focus on the high-dimensional generalization error of ridge regression.

4.1
Setup

Data Distribution.

We consider the following multivariate Gaussian setup
(
Hastie et al. 2022
;
Richards et al. 2021
;
Dobriban & Wager 2018
;
Bach 2024
)
:

\displaystyle\textbf{(Features) }x\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\Sigma),

(6)

\displaystyle\textbf{(Ground-truth weights) }w^{*}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\Theta/d),

(7)

\displaystyle\textbf{(Labels) }y\mid x\sim\mathcal{N}(f^{\star}(x),\sigma^{2}),\,f^{\star}(x):=x^{\top}w^{*}.

(8)

The
d\times d
symmetric positive-definite matrix
\Sigma
is the population covariance matrix. The
d
-dimensional vector
w^{*}
is the ground-truth weight vector, and it is sampled from a zero-mean Gaussian distribution with covariance matrix
\Theta/d
. Finally,
\sigma^{2}
corresponds to the label-noise level.

Models and Metrics.

We consider the solvable setting of classical ridge regression in the ambient input space. In addition to its analytical tractability, ridge regression can be viewed as the asymptotic limit of many learning problems
(
Dobriban & Wager 2018
;
Richards et al. 2021
;
Hastie et al. 2022
)
. We take the function class
\mathcal{F}\subseteq\{\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}\}
of linear ridge regression models.
For
w\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, the model
f
is defined by
f(x)=x^{\top}w,\text{ for all }x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and is learned with
\ell_{2}
-regularization. We further define the generalization error of any model
f
as
R(f)=\mathbb{E}\,[(f(x)-f^{\star}(x))^{2}].

Learning.
A learner is given
n
IID samples
\mathcal{D}=\{(x_{1},y_{1}),\ldots,(x_{n},y_{n})\}=(X\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d},Y\in\mathbb{R}^{n})
of data from the above distribution and it learns a model for predicting the label
y
from the feature vector
x
. Thus,
X
is the design matrix with
i
th row
x_{i}
, and
y
the response vector with
i
th component
y_{i}
. We learn the classical ridge regression model
\widehat{f}
using empirical risk minimization and
\ell_{2}
-regularization with penalty
\lambda
. The parameter vector
\widehat{w}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
of the linear model
\widehat{f}:x\mapsto x^{\top}\widehat{w}
is given by:

\displaystyle\widehat{w}

\displaystyle=\arg\min_{w\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}n^{-1}\|Xw-Y\|^{2}_{2}+\lambda\|w\|_{2}^{2}

(9)

\displaystyle=(M+\lambda I_{d})^{-1}X^{\top}Y/n,

(10)

where
M=X^{\top}X/n
is the empirical covariance matrix.
This is the ridge estimator; the unregularized limit
\lambda\to 0^{+}
corresponds to ordinary least-squares (OLS). We can decompose the generalization error of
\widehat{f}
into bias and variance terms as
R(\widehat{f})=B(\widehat{f})+V(\widehat{f})
where:

\displaystyle B(\widehat{f})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\,\|(M+\lambda I_{d})^{-1}Mw^{*}-w^{*}\|_{\Sigma}^{2},

(11)

\displaystyle V(\widehat{f})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\,\|(M+\lambda I_{d})^{-1}X^{\top}E/n\|_{\Sigma}^{2}.

(12)

Here,
E:=Y-Xw^{*}
is the noise vector, a random
n
-dimensional vector with IID components from
N(0,\sigma^{2})
, and independent of
X
and
w^{*}
. Further expanding the above expressions for
B(\widehat{f})
and
V(\widehat{f})
,
we obtain:

\displaystyle B(\widehat{f})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\,\|(M+\lambda I_{d})^{-1}Mw^{*}

(13)

\displaystyle-(M+\lambda I_{d})^{-1}(M+\lambda I_{d})w^{*}\|_{\Sigma}^{2}

\displaystyle=\lambda^{2}\mathbb{E}\,\|(M+\lambda I_{d})^{-1}w^{*}\|_{\Sigma}^{2}

\displaystyle=\lambda^{2}\mathbb{E}\,\bar{\operatorname{tr}}\,(M+\lambda I_{d})^{-1}\Theta(M+\lambda I_{d})^{-1}\Sigma,

(14)

\displaystyle V(\widehat{f})

\displaystyle=\frac{\sigma^{2}}{n}\mathbb{E}\,\operatorname{tr}(M+\lambda I_{d})^{-1}M(M+\lambda I_{d})^{-1}\Sigma.

(15)

Proportionate Scaling Limit.

The analysis will be carried out in a proportionate scaling limit (standard in random matrix theory) which enables us to derive deterministic formulas for the expected test risk of models:

\displaystyle d,n\to\infty,\quad d/n\to\phi.

(16)

The constant
\phi\in(0,\infty)
then captures the rate of features to samples.

4.2
Free Probability Theory Objective

Ultimately, our objective is to compute
\lim_{\phi}R(\widehat{f})=\lim_{\phi}B(\widehat{f})+\lim_{\phi}V(\widehat{f})
, where
\lim_{\phi}
is shorthand for
\lim_{\begin{subarray}{c}n,d\to\infty\\
d/n\to\phi\end{subarray}}
.
We define
Z\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d}
to be a random matrix with IID entries sampled from
{\cal N}(0,1/\sqrt{n\lambda})
. We define
K:=\Sigma^{1/2}Z^{\top}Z\Sigma^{1/2}+I_{d}
. Then, by re-expressing the bias and variance terms in the form of Eqn.
1
, we obtain the following limits of the expected trace of rational functions:

\displaystyle\lim_{\phi}B(\widehat{f})

\displaystyle=r_{B}=\lim_{\phi}\mathbb{E}\,\bar{\operatorname{tr}}\,K^{-1}\Theta K^{-1}\Sigma,

(17)

\displaystyle\lim_{\phi}V(\widehat{f})

\displaystyle=\sigma^{2}\lambda\phi^{-1}r_{V},\text{ where }

(18)

\displaystyle r_{V}

\displaystyle=\lim_{\phi}\mathbb{E}\,\bar{\operatorname{tr}}\,K^{-1}\Sigma^{1/2}Z^{\top}Z\Sigma^{1/2}K^{-1}\Sigma.

(19)

These computations will be carried out in Section
5
.

Case Study: Classical Ridge Regression

In this section, we use our tool to obtain a full analytical picture for the generalization error for classical ridge regression. Particularly, we shall recover the results of
Dobriban & Wager 2018
;
Richards et al. 2021
;

5.1
Computing Minimal Linear Pencil

The first step to applying FPT to compute
r_{B}
and
r_{V}
is constructing minimal linear pencils for their corresponding rational matrix functions:
R_{B}=K^{-1}\Theta K^{-1}\Sigma
,
R_{V}=K^{-1}\Sigma^{1/2}Z^{\top}Z\Sigma^{1/2}K^{-1}\Sigma.

Definition 5.1

(Linear Pencil
(
Helton et al. 2006
;
Helton et al. 2018
;
Volčič 2024
)
)
.

For a
p\times p
block matrix and vectors
u,v\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
,
(u,Q,v)
is a linear pencil for a rational function
R(A,B,\ldots;Z,W,\ldots)
if each entry of
Q
is an affine function of the input matrices and
R=u^{\top}Q^{-1}v
.

With a naïve approach to constructing pencils, the resultant pencil may be overly large, yielding an intractable number of fixed-point equations. Hence, we seek minimal pencils w.r.t to the size
p
, which always exist
(
Helton et al. 2006
;
Helton et al. 2018
)
.
NCAlgebra yields the pencil in Appendix
A.1
. One can verify that
R_{B}=Q^{-1}_{3,8}
. Additional simplifications can be made programmatically, such as reverting
\Sigma_{sqrt}
to
\Sigma^{1/2}
. We recall that the mechanical advantage of
Q
over
R_{B}
is that
Q
is linear in the random matrix
Z
. We follow the same process to get a minimal linear pencil for
r_{V}
(displayed in Appendix
A.2
).

The setup of

\displaystyle u^{\top}Q^{-1}v=\sum_{i,j}u_{i}v_{j}\delta_{i}^{\top}Q^{-1}\delta_{j}=\sum_{i,j}u_{i}v_{j}Q^{-1}_{i,j},

Overview of Algorithm.

We now overview the algorithm and optimizations that
auto-fpt
uses to compute the fixed-point equations from
Q,(i,j)
, with the running example of the MP law.
At a high level, our implementation decomposes the input pencil into deterministic and random block matrices, symmetrizes the matrices, and
R
-transforms the random block matrix to obtain a system of equations that can be solved for
r
.

Step 1 (Pencil Splitting):
The block matrix
Q
is split into
Q=F-Q_{X}
, where
F
contains the deterministic blocks of
Q
while
Q_{X}
contains the random blocks. This step of the calculations is handled by the a function
free_proba_utils.decompose_Q
. For example, if
Q=\left[\begin{matrix}I_{n}&-Z\\
Z^{\top}&I_{d}\end{matrix}\right]
is the linear pencil matrix we constructed for the MP law in Section
3
, then

F=\left[\begin{matrix}I_{n}&0\\
0&I_{d}\end{matrix}\right],\,Q_{X}=\left[\begin{matrix}0&Z\\
-Z^{\top}&0\end{matrix}\right].

Step 2 (Scalarization):
The block matrices
Q,F,Q_{X}
are converted to ordinary matrices
q,f,q_{X}
with entries taking values over an abstract commutative ring, respectively, where each unique matrix symbol in
Q,F,Q_{X}
is mapped to a different scalar. This step minimizes the runtime of the rest of the program, as SymPy is more optimized for symbolic computation with scalars. Henceforth, we use lowercase to refer to the scalarized version of block matrices. This step is handled by
free_proba_utils.scalarize_block_matrix
.
For the running example, this gives

\displaystyle f=\left[\begin{matrix}1&0\\
0&1\end{matrix}\right]=I_{2},\quad q=\left[\begin{matrix}1&-x_{1}\\
x_{1}&1\end{matrix}\right],

\displaystyle q_{x}=f-q=\left[\begin{matrix}0&x_{1}\\
-x_{1}&0\end{matrix}\right],

\displaystyle\text{ with the identification }Z,Z^{\top}\to x_{1}.

Step 3 (Symmetrization):
The matrices
Q,q,F,f,Q_{X},q_{X}
are symmetrized, which is necessary to compute their
R
-transform. We symmetrize a matrix
A
as:

A^{\prime}=\begin{bmatrix}0&A^{\top}\\
A&0\end{bmatrix}.

This step is handled by
free_proba_utils.symmetrize_block_matrix
. For the running example, this gives

\displaystyle F^{\prime}=\left[\begin{matrix}0&0&I_{n}&0\\
0&0&0&I_{d}\\
I_{n}&0&0&0\\
0&I_{d}&0&0\end{matrix}\right],\,q_{x}^{\prime}=\left[\begin{matrix}0&0&0&-x_{1}\\
0&0&x_{1}&0\\
0&x_{1}&0&0\\
-x_{1}&0&0&0\end{matrix}\right].

Step 4 (
R
-transform Computation):

Using operator-valued FPT, we obain that in the proportionate scaling limit:

\displaystyle G=(I_{p}\otimes\bar{\operatorname{tr}}\,)[(F^{\prime}-\mathcal{R}_{Q_{X}^{\prime}}(G)^{\circ})^{-1}],

(23)

where
p
is the number of blocks in
Q^{\prime}
. Here,
\mathcal{R}_{Q^{\prime}_{X}}
is the
R
-transform of
Q^{\prime}_{X}
which maps
\mathbb{R}^{p\times p}
to itself, and is defined further below. The operator
I_{p}\otimes\bar{\operatorname{tr}}\,
extracts the normalized traces of the blocks of a block matrix, with the convention that the trace of a rectangular block is zero, while the operation
B\mapsto B^{\circ}
embeds a
p\times p
matrix
B
into an element of the algebra of block matrices having the same size and block shapes as
Q^{\prime}
(and therefore
Q_{X}^{\prime}
), and is defined in Appendix
E
. When
Q^{\prime}_{X}
is a Gaussian block matrix, we have

\mathcal{R}_{Q^{\prime}_{X}}(G)_{ij}=\sum_{k,\ell}\sigma(i,k;\ell,j)\alpha_{k}g_{k\ell},

(24)

where
\sigma(i,k;\ell,j)
is the covariance between the entries of block
(i,k)
and block
(\ell,j)
of
Q^{\prime}_{X}
, while
\alpha_{k}
is the dimension of the block
(k,\ell)
of
G

(
Rashidi Far et al. 2008
)
. This step is handled by
free_proba_utils.R_transform
.

For the running example, this gives

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}_{Q_{X}^{\prime}}(G)^{\circ}

\displaystyle=\left[\begin{matrix}0&0&-\frac{dG_{1,1}}{\lambda n}I_{n}&0\\
0&0&0&-\frac{G_{0,0}}{\lambda}I_{d}\\
-\frac{dG_{1,1}}{\lambda n}I_{n}&0&0&0\\
0&-\frac{G_{0,0}}{\lambda}I_{d}&0&0\end{matrix}\right].

To greatly increase the efficiency of the
R
-transform computation and the inversion in the next step, we leverage the sparse structure of
(Q^{\prime})^{-1}
to infer the structure of
G
and avoid computation for
0
-entries. 0-entries in
Q^{-1}
correspond to 0-entries in
G
since
G=(I_{p}\otimes\bar{\operatorname{tr}}\,)(Q^{\prime})^{-1}
. By construction,
(Q^{\prime})^{-1}=\begin{bmatrix}0&Q^{-1}\\
(Q^{-1})^{\top}&0\end{bmatrix}
; hence, we just need to find the structure of
Q^{-1}
. We do so by proxy by efficiently computing
q^{-1}
. We split
q
into a 2-by-2 block matrix
\begin{bmatrix}A&B\\
C&D\end{bmatrix}
(with equal-sized blocks) and apply the formula for the inverse of such a block matrix. We take advantage of the fact that in many settings, the pencil
Q
has the majority of its entries along or near its diagonal; hence,
A
and
D
are often sparse and SymPy’s
inv_quick
function can be applied to these blocks to speed-up computation. In the running example, there are no 0-entries.

Step 5 (Difference Inversion):
We now compute
(f-r)^{-1}
, where
r
is the scalarization of the desymmetrized version of
\mathcal{R}_{Q_{X}^{\prime}}(G)^{\circ}
. We use the same technique from the previous step to increase the efficiency of the inversion of
f-r
. This step is handled by
free_proba_utils.inv_heuristic
. For the running example, this gives

\displaystyle(f-r)^{-1}=\left[\begin{matrix}\frac{1}{\frac{d}{\lambda n}G_{1,1}+1}&0\\
0&\frac{1}{\frac{1}{\lambda}G_{0,0}+1}\end{matrix}\right].

Step 6 (Fixed-Point Equation Construction):
The relevant fixed-point equations are recursively extracted from
g=(f-r)^{-1}
.
In particular, the first equation in our system (corresponding to the quantity of interest
r
) is
g_{i,j}
. Then, for each equation
g_{s,t}=f(g)
in our system, if
f(g)
contains entries in
g
for which our system does not contain an equation, we extract the relevant equation from
g=(f-r)^{-1}
. We repeat this procedure until the system of equations is closed. Then, the equations are simplified, e.g.,
d/n
is replaced with
\phi
. These simplifications can be manually specified in
fpt.py
using the
subs
dictionary. This step is handled by
fpt.construct_fixed_point_equations
.

In practice, the resultant system has redundant equations, as
g
contains many equivalent entries (e.g., due to the cyclic permutation invariance of trace of matrix products). To prune such equations, we identify equivalence classes of entries in
g
by identifying equivalent entries of
Q^{-1}
(from our computation in Step 4). Then, for each equivalence class, we: (1) replace all occurrences of its members in our system with a single representative of the class, and (2) remove all the equations corresponding to members of the class except for the equation for the representative. This procedure greatly reduces the size of the system. In the running example,
Q^{-1}_{0,0}=Q^{-1}_{1,1}
. This gives

\displaystyle G_{0,0}=\frac{\lambda}{\lambda+\phi G_{1,1}},\quad G_{1,1}=\frac{\lambda}{\lambda+G_{0,0}}.

(25)

Step 7 (Matricization):
Scalars in the fixed-point equations corresponding to deterministic matrices (per the map in Step 2) are reverted. In particular,
G
is recovered from
g
as
G=(I_{p}\otimes\bar{\operatorname{tr}}\,)g
. This step is handled by
fpt.matricize_expr
.
For the case of our running example, this produces Equation
4
.

We note that the runtime of
auto-fpt
does not scale with
n,d
, as these dimensions are symbolic. Furthermore, the complexity of inverting the block matrices is dominated by the need for symbolic (rather than numerical) computation and the size of the matrices (i.e., the number of blocks).

Additional Examples

We include complete example scripts for using
auto-fpt
, including for computing the MP law and generalization error of ridge regression, in the
examples
folder of our repository. We provide additional examples below. These computations are tedious with pen and paper, thereby highlighting the benefit of our tool.

6.1
Subordination: Sum of Independent Empirical Covariance Matrices

We now turn to the computation of
\mathbb{E}\,\bar{\operatorname{tr}}\,R
, where
R=(M+\lambda I_{d})^{-1}
,
M:=M_{1}+M_{2}
, and
M_{j}=X_{j}^{\top}X_{j}/n
, where
X_{1}
and
X_{2}
are independent, with
X_{j}
being an
n_{j}\times d
random matrix having IID rows from
\mathcal{N}(0,\Sigma_{j})
. Such calculations come up in the analysis of the model collapse phenomenon
(
Dohmatob et al. 2025
)
, where a model is trained on a mixture of synthetic and real data. We work in the proportionate scaling limit:

d,n_{1},n_{2}\to\infty,\quad n_{1}/n\to p_{1},\,n_{2}/n\to p_{2},\quad d/n\to\phi,

where
n:=n_{1}+n_{2}
. Here
p_{1}\in(0,1)
and
\phi\in(0,\infty)
are fixed constants and
p_{2}=1-p_{1}
.
auto-fpt
produces the following output (see Appendix
G
):

\displaystyle{G}_{3,3}=\bar{tr}{\left(\lambda\left(\lambda I+p_{1}S_{1}^{2}{G}_{1,1}+p_{2}S_{2}^{2}{G}_{5,5}\right)^{-1}\right)},

\displaystyle{G}_{2,0}=\bar{tr}{\left(-\lambda S_{1}^{2}\left(\lambda I+p_{1}S_{1}^{2}{G}_{1,1}+p_{2}S_{2}^{2}{G}_{5,5}\right)^{-1}\right)},

\displaystyle{G}_{6,4}=\bar{tr}{\left(-\lambda S_{2}^{2}\left(\lambda I+p_{1}S_{1}^{2}{G}_{1,1}+p_{2}S_{2}^{2}{G}_{5,5}\right)^{-1}\right)},

\displaystyle{G}_{1,1}=-\frac{\lambda}{-\lambda+\phi{G}_{2,0}},\quad{G}_{5,5}=-\frac{\lambda}{-\lambda+\phi{G}_{6,4}},

where we recall that
S_{1}^{2}=\Sigma_{1}
and
S_{2}^{2}=\Sigma_{2}
. Observe that, setting
e_{1}:=G_{1,1}
and
e_{2}:=G_{5,5}
and eliminating
G_{2,0}
and
G_{6,4}
, we get:

\bar{\operatorname{tr}}\,R\simeq G_{3,3}/\lambda=\bar{\operatorname{tr}}\,K^{-1}
,
where
K:=p_{1}e_{1}\Sigma_{1}+p_{2}e_{2}\Sigma_{2}+\lambda I_{d}
, and
e_{1},e_{2}>0
uniquely solve the system of equations

\displaystyle e_{1}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{1+\phi\bar{\operatorname{tr}}\,\Sigma_{1}K^{-1}},\quad e_{2}=\frac{1}{1+\phi\bar{\operatorname{tr}}\,\Sigma_{2}K^{-1}}.

(26)

This recovers an alternative statement of a classical result on the Stieltjes transform of the sum of two independent anisotropic Wishart matrices
(
Marčenko & Pastur 1967
;
Kargin 2015
)
. The above result can be easily extended from two to any finite number of groups by defining:
n:=n_{1}+n_{2}+\ldots
and
p_{j}:=\lim n_{j}/n\in(0,1)
for all
j
, with
p_{1}+p_{2}+\ldots=1
. The
K
matrix is now given by
K=p_{1}e_{1}\Sigma_{1}+p_{2}e_{2}\Sigma_{2}+\ldots
, where
\Sigma_{j}
is the covariance matrix for the
j
th group. The fixed-point equations for the
e_{j}
’s are given by

\displaystyle e_{j}=\frac{1}{1+\phi\bar{\operatorname{tr}}\,\Sigma_{j}K^{-1}},\text{ for all }j.

This precisely recovers the main result of
Couillet et al. 2011
, namely Theorem 1 thereof.

6.2
High-Dimensional Random Features Model

We now use
auto-fpt
to carry out a computation done in
Adlam & Pennington 2020b
, which studies high-dimensional random features (RF) approximations of a two-layer neural network learned with a linear signal. The random features model offers a simplified analytic handle for understanding neural networks, e.g., effect of model size, double descent.

As before, let
X=Z\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times n}
be a random design matrix, with
n
columns drawn IID from
\mathcal{N}(0,I_{d})
, and
Y_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{1\times n}
be the labels. For each training instance
x_{i}
(i.e., column
i
of
X
), the label
y_{i}
is generated as:

\displaystyle y_{i}|x_{i},\Omega,\omega\sim\omega\sigma_{T}(\Omega x_{i}/\sqrt{d})/\sqrt{d_{T}}+\epsilon_{i},

(27)

where
\sigma_{T}
is a coordinate-wise activation function,
\epsilon_{i}\sim{\cal N}(0,\sigma_{\epsilon}^{2})
is the label noise, and
\Omega\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{T}\times d}
and
\omega\in\mathbb{R}^{1\times d_{T}}
are parameter matrices with IID entries from
{\cal N}(0,1)
that are sampled once for all the data.

We have an initial two-layer neural network
N_{0}(x)=W_{2}\sigma(W_{1}x/\sqrt{d})/\sqrt{m}
, where
W_{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times d}
is the weights of the hidden layer, with IID entries from
\mathcal{N}(0,1)
, and
W_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{1\times m}
is the weights of the last layer, with IID entries from
\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^{2}_{W_{2}})
. For our predictor
\hat{y}
, we consider the class of functions learned by random feature kernel ridge regression with the Neural Tangent Kernel (NTK)
(
Adlam & Pennington 2020b
)
. We focus on the training risk

E_{train}=\mathbb{E}\,[\frac{1}{n}\|Y-\hat{y}(X)\|_{F}^{2}]
of the network in the following proportionate scaling regime:

\displaystyle n,d,m\to\infty,\quad d/n\to\phi,\quad d/m\to\psi,

(28)

for some constants
\phi,\psi\in(0,\infty)
. Functionally, we are interested in the training risk, as the test risk
E_{test}=\mathbb{E}\,[(y-\hat{y}(x))^{2}]
can be related to
E_{train}
by
E_{test}=(\lambda\tau_{1})^{-2}E_{train}-\sigma_{\epsilon}^{2}
(where
\tau_{1}
is defined by the system of equations
32
). This relation can be derived via additional free probability theory calculations (see appendix of
Adlam & Pennington 2020b
).

We focus on the setting
\sigma^{2}_{W_{2}}\to 0
(as in Section 6.5 of
Adlam & Pennington 2020b
). We shall need the following nonnegative constants:

\displaystyle\beta:=\eta-\zeta,\quad\eta:=\mathbb{E}[\sigma(z)^{2}],\quad\zeta:=[\mathbb{E}\,\sigma^{\prime}(z)]^{2},

(29)

for
z\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)
. Observe that for
a linear activation
\sigma(z)\equiv z
. Importantly,
\beta
captures the degree of curvature/nonlinearity in the activation function
\sigma
;
\beta=0
corresponds to the linear case of random projections considered in
Bach 2024
;
Dohmatob et al. 2025
;
Subramonian et al. 2025
.

In Appendix
H
, we show that the training error of the network in the NTK regime is given by:

\displaystyle E_{train}=-\gamma^{2}(\tau_{2}^{\prime}+\sigma_{\epsilon}^{2}\tau_{1}^{\prime}),

(30)

where the derivatives of
\tau_{1},\tau_{2}
are taken with respect to
\lambda
. Using
auto-fpt
, we obtain the following fixed-point equations relating
\tau_{1},\tau_{2}
:

\displaystyle\lambda\tau_{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{\lambda{G}_{0,0}}{\beta{G}_{4,4}+\lambda{G}_{3,0}+\lambda},\quad{G}_{3,0}=\frac{\phi\zeta{G}_{4,4}}{\lambda\phi+\zeta{G}_{2,2}{G}_{4,4}},

\displaystyle\lambda\tau_{1}

\displaystyle=\frac{\lambda}{\beta{G}_{4,4}+\lambda{G}_{3,0}+\lambda},

\displaystyle{G}_{4,4}

\displaystyle=-\frac{\lambda\phi}{-\beta\psi{G}_{2,2}-\lambda\phi+\phi\psi\zeta{G}_{0,3}},

\displaystyle{G}_{0,3}

\displaystyle=-\frac{\lambda{G}_{2,2}}{\lambda\phi+\zeta{G}_{2,2}{G}_{4,4}},\quad{G}_{0,0}=\frac{\lambda\phi}{\lambda\phi+\zeta{G}_{2,2}{G}_{4,4}}.

In Appendix
H
, we show via variable elimination that this system exactly recovers the coupled polynomial equations for
\tau_{1},\tau_{2}
in
Adlam & Pennington 2020b
, namely Proposition 1 thereof with
\sigma^{2}_{W_{2}}=0
:

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=-\eta\phi\tau_{1}^{2}+\eta\phi\tau_{1}\tau_{2}-\lambda\tau_{1}^{2}\tau_{2}\zeta+\phi\tau_{1}^{2}\zeta

\displaystyle\quad\,-2\phi\tau_{1}\tau_{2}\zeta+\phi\tau_{2}^{2}\zeta+\tau_{1}\tau_{2}\zeta,

(31)

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=\lambda\psi\tau_{1}^{2}\tau_{2}\zeta-\phi^{2}\tau_{1}+\phi^{2}\tau_{2}+\phi\tau_{1}\tau_{2}\zeta-\psi\tau_{1}\tau_{2}\zeta.

(32)

This calculation requires computing a single pencil for
\tau_{1},\tau_{2}
(see Appendix
H
). While NCAlgebra does not explicitly support computing a single pencil for multiple rational expressions, the modularity of pencil construction can be exploited to do so. In particular, if we would like to compute a single minimal pencil for the rational expressions
R_{A},R_{B}
, we can instead compute a minimal pencil for
R=R_{A}+R_{B}
and may be able to use the non-zero entries of
u
and
v
to identify the relevant blocks of
Q^{-1}
.

7
Discussion and Conclusion

We present
auto-fpt
, a lightweight Python-based tool for automatically symbolically computing the high-dimensional expected normalized trace of rational functions from their pencils, which is a common procedure in ML theory.
We introduce various techniques to make this computation more tractable, especially for large pencils, e.g., matrix scalarization, sparse block matrix inversion, and duplicate equation identification and pruning.
We show how to use
auto-fpt
to recover known theoretical results.
While we do not present new theoretical results, a preliminary version of
auto-fpt
was used to guide the derivation of the theories in
Subramonian et al. 2025
;
Dohmatob et al. 2025
.

auto-fpt
offers numerous promising future directions. The runtime and tractability of the tool can be empirically stress-tested with respect to rational expression size, pencil size, and numerical stability. In addition, the code can be profiled to identify performance bottlenecks. Moreover, user studies can be run with ML theorists (both RMT experts and those who are unfamiliar with FPT) to understand the efficacy and limitations of
auto-fpt
for their research and deriving novel theoretical results (e.g., analyzing two-layer neural networks beyond initialization).
For example, by default,
auto-fpt
does not support powers of traces of rational expressions, and thus cannot currently asymptotically solve the Harish-Chandra-Itzykson-Zuber integral
(
Troiani et al. 2022
)
.

With regards to future functionality,
auto-fpt
can be developed to work with non-Gaussian random matrices and non-commuting deterministic matrices.
In addition,
auto-fpt
can be developed to provide more explicit support for converting the objectives of classification problems into rational expressions, and support for learning problems where a closed-form solution may not exist.
Furthermore, it can currently require some manual effort to rewrite interesting quantities in ML as the expected trace of a rational expression, which is required to compute a pencil and use
auto-fpt
. Therefore,
auto-fpt
can be extended to intelligently perform this rewriting.

auto-fpt
can also be extended to: (1) manipulate and automatically solve the system of equations output by the program, and (2) produce phase diagrams for quantities of interest.
In addition,
auto-fpt
may require further development to be integrated as a subroutine into proof assistants.

Broader Impacts Statement

We hope that
auto-fpt
makes the machinery of ML theory more accessible to theorists and practitioners, and can help reproduce known and uncover new phenomena in ML that contribute to our foundational understanding of sociotechnical phenomena like robustness and fairness.
auto-fpt
should be used in conjunction with real-world experiments to avoid overclaiming aspects of ML models.
auto-fpt
has potential to be used in educational settings to teach RMT.

An Algebra of Block Matrices

Let
\mathcal{A}
be the (von Neumann) non-commutative unital algebra of all block matrices with the same size and block shapes as the
p\times p
block matrix
Q^{\prime}
(and therefore
Q_{X}^{\prime}
). Thus, each block
M[i,j]
of each
M\in\mathcal{A}
has same shape
\alpha_{i}\times\beta_{j}
as the corresponding block
Q^{\prime}[i,j]
of
Q^{\prime}
. Let
1_{\mathcal{A}}
be the unit element of
\mathcal{A}
. Note that
\mathcal{A}
is fully determined by the
p^{2}
pairs of integers
\{(\alpha_{i},\beta_{j})\mid 1\leq i,j\leq p\}
representing the shapes of the blocks of
Q^{\prime}
. The operator
I_{p}\otimes\bar{\operatorname{tr}}\,
which extracts the normalized traces
1
1

1

By convention, the trace of a non-square block is zero.
of the blocks of an element
M\in\mathcal{A}
is a
tracial state
for this algebra. It maps a block matrix

into a
p\times p
matrix

.

Any

matrix

can be embedded into an element

Equations
56
to

are largely from
Adlam & Pennington 2020b
, which we include here for completeness.
The predictions
\hat{y}(X)
of the network on the training set (when trained using gradient descent and the width of the network becomes large) converges to:

\displaystyle\hat{y}(x)

\displaystyle=N_{0}(X)+(Y-N_{0}(X))K^{-1}K(X,X)

(55)

\displaystyle=Y-\gamma(Y-N_{0}(X))K^{-1}.

(56)

We make use of the following definitions:

\displaystyle K:=K(X,X)+\lambda I_{n},K(x_{1},x_{2})=J(x_{1})J(x_{2})^{\top},

where
\lambda
is the regularization penalty and
J
is the Jacobian of the network’s output with respect to its parameters. In the setting
\sigma^{2}_{W_{2}}\to 0
, because
K_{1}(X,X)=(\partial N_{0}(X)/\partial W_{1})(\partial N_{0}(X)/\partial W_{1})^{\top}=0
, we have that:

\displaystyle K(X,X)

\displaystyle=K_{2}(X,X)=(\partial N_{0}(X)/\partial W_{2})(\partial N_{0}(X)/\partial W_{2})^{\top}

(57)

\displaystyle=F^{\top}F/m,

(58)

where the feature matrix
F:=\sigma(W_{1}X/\sqrt{d})
. Then, the training risk can be decomposed as follows:

\displaystyle E_{train}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\bar{\operatorname{tr}}\,((Y-\hat{y}(X))^{\top}(Y-\hat{y}(X)))

(59)

\displaystyle=\gamma^{2}\mathbb{E}\bar{\operatorname{tr}}\,((Y-N_{0}(X))^{\top}(Y-N_{0}(X))K^{-2})

(60)

\displaystyle=T_{1}+T_{2},

(61)

\displaystyle\text{ with }T_{1}

\displaystyle=\gamma^{2}\mathbb{E}\bar{\operatorname{tr}}\,Y^{\top}YK^{-2},

(62)

\displaystyle T_{2}

\displaystyle=\gamma^{2}\mathbb{E}\bar{\operatorname{tr}}\,N_{0}(X)^{\top}N_{0}(X)K^{-2},

(63)

where we have used that the expectation of terms linear in
N_{0}(X)
is 0. We observe that because
\sigma_{W_{2}}^{2}\to 0
:

\displaystyle N_{0}(X)

\displaystyle=W_{2}F/\sqrt{m},

(64)

\displaystyle T_{2}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\bar{\operatorname{tr}}\,W_{2}^{\top}W_{2}(F/\sqrt{m})(F^{\top}F/m)^{-2}(F/\sqrt{m})^{\top}

(65)

\displaystyle=\sigma_{W_{2}}^{2}\mathbb{E}\bar{\operatorname{tr}}\,(F/\sqrt{m})(F^{\top}F/m)^{-2}(F/\sqrt{m})^{\top}=0.

(66)

Now, the
Gaussian Equivalence Theorem

(
Goldt et al. 2022
)
tells us that we can replace
F
with a linearized version
F_{lin}
, and
Y
similarly with
Y_{lin}
:

\displaystyle F_{lin}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\frac{\zeta}{d}}W_{1}X+\sqrt{\beta}\Theta_{F},\quad Y_{lin}=\sqrt{\frac{1}{d_{T}d}}\omega\Omega X+\epsilon,

(67)

without changing their limiting spectral distribution.
Here,
\Theta_{F}
is a random
m\times n
matrix independent of
X
and
W_{1}
, with IID entries from
\mathcal{N}(0,1)
. Hence, we deduce that:

\displaystyle T_{1}

\displaystyle=\gamma^{2}\mathbb{E}\bar{\operatorname{tr}}\,Y_{lin}^{\top}Y_{lin}K^{-2}

(68)

\displaystyle=\gamma^{2}\mathbb{E}\bar{\operatorname{tr}}\,(X^{\top}X/d+\sigma_{\epsilon}^{2}I_{n})K^{-2}

(69)

\displaystyle=\gamma^{2}(\mathbb{E}\bar{\operatorname{tr}}\,(X^{\top}XK^{-2})/d+\sigma_{\epsilon}^{2}\mathbb{E}\bar{\operatorname{tr}}\,K^{-2})

(70)

\displaystyle=-\gamma^{2}(\tau_{2}^{\prime}+\sigma_{\epsilon}^{2}\tau_{1}^{\prime}),

(71)

where
