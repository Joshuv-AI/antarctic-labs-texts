Heat stress enhances gut microbial arginine catabolism to amplify MyD88-dependent inflammatory responses
Authors: Xianxi Ye, Qingpeng Cai, Yangjie Pan, Minxuan Xu, Yulong Li, Minjie You, Jiayi Yang, Siyuan Chen, Huacheng He, Guangliang Hong, Hong Zheng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAs global temperatures continue to rise, heat stress (HS) has emerged as a major health threat of growing concern. HS triggers systemic inflammation and multi-organ damage, but so far its molecular mechanisms remain unclear. In this study, we explored the potential mechanism by which the gut microbial alterations amplify HS-associated inflammatory responses.ResultsWe found that the gut microbiota was disrupted in HS mice as characterized by increased LPS levels and enhanced arginine catabolism. Transplant of fecal microbiota from HS mice aggravated inflammatory responses in recipient mice after HS. Exogenous arginine pretreatment notably suppressed inflammation in the liver and cortex of HS mice. Mechanistically, arginine reduced MyD88 protein levels by activating its ubiquitination and weakened the MyD88-TLR4 interaction, thereby inhibiting the nuclear translocation of p65 and the expression of pro-inflammatory genes. Clinically, lower arginine levels were detected in the serum of HS patients and positively related with liver injury and inflammatory indicators. An arginine-enriched oral inulin hydrogel was developed to prevent inflammatory responses exacerbated by the gut microbial alterations through maintaining the gut microbiota homeostasis to reduce LPS and providing a sustained supply of arginine.ConclusionsThis study reveals a mechanism by which the gut microbial alterations exacerbates HS-associated inflammatory responses via disrupting the balance between LPS and arginine, thereby providing a novel target for the prevention of HS. Introduction

As global warming persists, heat stress (HS) has become a significant risk factor for mortality and is increasingly receiving attention worldwide [1, 2]. HS is characterized by an extreme core body temperature and often accompanied by central nervous system and multiple organ injuries [1]. Several potential pathogeneses of HS have been reported to date, including coagulation disorders, oxidative stress and cell death [3]. Additionally, systemic inflammation also plays a mediating role in coagulation and heat cytotoxicity during HS [4–6]. However, the potential mechanisms on HS-associated inflammatory responses remain far from being fully understood.

The intestinal tract possesses the largest surface in the body that exposes to the outer environment and exerts as a biological barrier to maintain human health [7]. HS-induced disruption of intestinal barrier has been reported to result in systemic inflammatory responses [8, 9]. Moreover, the intestinal tract is colonized by the gut microbiota, which has been widely elucidated to affect inflammatory responses by its metabolites [10]. Li et al. revealed that microbial metabolite butyrate alleviated mucosal inflammation in DSS-induced murine colitis by regulating neutrophil functions [11]. Mossad et al. found that the gut microbiota promoted oxidative stress and mitochondrial injury in microglia via its metabolite N6-carboxymethyllysine [12]. The microbiota-derived TMAO led to abdominal aortic aneurysm in mice through upregulating inflammatory and apoptotic processes [13]. Additionally, indole-3-acetic acid produced by the gut bacteria enhanced intestinal mucin sulfation via the AHR-Papss2-Slc35b3 pathway and then reduced intestinal inflammation and barrier damage in high-fat diet-fed mice [14]. Of note, HS has been shown to disrupt the gut microbiota and host metabolism in various animal models, such as pigs [15], mice [16], rats [17], broilers [18], and bulls [19]. Herein we hypothesized that disorders in the gut microbiota and its metabolites may aggravate systemic inflammation induced by HS. Currently, however, little information is available on the association between the gut microbial metabolites and inflammatory responses after HS.

In this study, therefore, we investigated the key role and underlying mechanisms of the gut microbiota and its metabolites in HS-associated inflammatory responses. First, we identified arginine as an important metabolite that affected inflammation in the liver and cortex of HS mice by using an untargeted metabolomics method. Then, we found that arginine deficiency might be a key factor for HS-induced inflammation, which was largely attributed to enhanced arginine catabolism by the gut microbiota rather than endogenous arginine metabolism. In addition, arginine has been verified to decrease MyD88 protein levels by activating its ubiquitin-mediated degradation and inhibit the interaction between MyD88 and TLR4, thereby reducing the nuclear translocation of p65 and inflammatory responses. Then, we reported that the level of serum arginine was significantly lower and negatively correlated with inflammatory cytokines and liver function indicators in HS patients. Finally, we innovatively developed an arginine-enriched oral inulin hydrogel to prevent inflammatory responses in HS mice via maintaining the gut microbiota homeostasis to reduce LPS levels and providing sustained arginine release. Therefore, our study not only elucidated a novel mechanism by which the gut microbial alterations exacerbates HS-associated inflammatory responses, but also provided a potential target for preventing systemic inflammation caused by HS.

Materials and methods

Animals

Male C57BL/6 mice (age = 7 weeks, body weight = 20.5 ± 1.2 g) were purchased from the Vital River Laboratory Animal Technology Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China). The mice were housed in the specific pathogen-free environment (n < 5 per cage) under a stable environment (12 h light/dark cycle, 22 ± 2 °C room temperature and 50 ± 5% humidity), and received sterile food and water ad libitum at the Laboratory Animal Center of Wenzhou Medical University (Wenzhou, China). All mice were allowed to acclimatize for 1 week before the experiments. All animal experiments were carried out according to the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals and approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Wenzhou Medical University (ID: xmsq2024-0202).

Patient samples

In this study, 10 normal individuals (NORM) and 12 heatstroke patients (HSP) were ultimately enrolled after matching for age and sex and excluding individuals with underlying illnesses. HSP were clinically diagnosed according to a series of indicators: (1) the history of heat exposure; (2) the core temperature above 40 °C; (3) Glasgow coma scale, shock index, and disturbance of consciousness. Baseline characteristics of NORM and HSP are presented in Table S1. Serum samples were collected from participants at The First Affiliated Hospital of Wenzhou Medical University. This part of the study was approved by the Ethics Committee of The First Affiliated Hospital of Wenzhou Medical University (ID: KY2025-032), with written informed consent from all participants.

Establishment of heat stress (HS) mouse model

To construct the HS model, mice were deprived of food and water and placed in the climate chamber (RDN-300B, Yang Hui Instrument Co., Ltd, Ningbo, China) maintained at 39 °C and a relative humidity of 65% for 1.5 h. The rectal temperature of the mice was measured every 30 min by using a rectal thermometer (TH-212, Beijing Hichance Technology Co., Ltd., China). In this study, the mice with the rectal temperature ≥ 40 °C and weakness were defined as HS mice. After HS, the mice were transferred to a room-temperature environment, kept fasting but free access to water, and subjected to subsequent experiments after 6 h.

Antibiotic treatment and fecal bacteria transplantation

To deplete endogenous gut bacteria, the recipient normal mice were gavaged with an antibiotic cocktail (vancomycin, 50 mg/kg; neomycin, 100 mg/kg; metronidazole, 100 mg/kg; amphotericin B, 1 mg/kg) and ampicillin (1 g/L) in drinking water once a day for 7 days. All antibiotics were obtained from the Meilun Biotech Company (Dalian, China). Intestinal contents were collected from normal and HS donor mice and dissolved in sterile PBS (1:7.5, w/v). Then, the mixture was vortexed for 1 min and centrifuged at 800 g for 2 min to prepare the gut bacterial suspension. The recipient mice were gavaged with 150 μL of the bacterial suspension from normal or HS mice once a day for 3 weeks.

Analysis of bacterial load

To assess the gut bacterial load after antibiotic treatment, fecal samples were collected from mice after 7 days of antibiotic treatment for in vitro culture. Briefly, 0.1 g of fecal material was suspended in 1 mL of 0.9% sterile saline and thoroughly homogenized to prepare the initial fecal suspension. The suspension was subjected to serial dilution, and 200 μL of each diluted sample was spread onto Columbia blood agar plates. The plates were incubated at 37 °C under either aerobic or anaerobic condition, and bacterial colonies were counted after 14–16 h of incubation.

Cell counting kit-8 (CCK-8) experiment

The cell viability was assayed by CCK-8 according to the manufacturer's instructions (GK10001, GlpBio, Shanghai, China). Briefly, RAW264.7 and BV2 cells were inoculated in 96-well plates and treated with arginine at different concentrations for 6 h. Subsequently, CCK-8 reagent was added to each well and incubated at 37 °C for 1 h. Finally, the cell viability was analyzed at 450 nm with a Microplate Reader (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Wilmington, USA).

Arginine treatment

To determine the optimal treatment concentration of arginine, mice were pretreated with different concentrations of arginine (0, 50, 100, 200 and 300 mg/kg), followed by HS induction, and then samples were collected for analysis after 6 h of rewarming. In the in vitro experiments, we used cell counting kit-8 (CCK8) assay to select the optimal treatment concentration of arginine. Briefly, the cells were inoculated in 96-well plates and pretreated with arginine solution at different concentrations for 6 h to assess the effect of arginine on the cell viability. In this study, prior to the HS modeling, the mice were intraperitoneally injected with arginine (A5006, Sigma Aldrich, USA) solution prepared in PBS (phosphate buffered saline, 0.01 M, pH = 7.4) at a dose of 200 mg/kg body weight. The control mice were injected with the same volume of PBS solution. For Arg_OS or Arg_OIH treatment, the mice were administered by oral gavage at a dose of 200 mg/kg body weight 1 h before HS modeling.

Cell experiments

BV2 cells are an immortalized murine microglial cell line and have been utilized as an in vitro model for neuroinflammation and microglial function [20]. Moreover, RAW264.7 cells are a murine monocyte macrophage leukemia cell line and have been widely used in experiments related to inflammation, immunity due to their ease of culture, stable functionality, and suitability for transfection [21]. In this study, therefore, BV2 and RAW264.7 cells were purchased from the Shanghai FUHENG Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China) and cultured in a Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (C11995500BT, Gibco, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Wilmington, USA) supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% penicillin and streptomycin in a CO2 incubator (Thermo Fisher Scientific) at 37 °C with 5% CO2. To evaluate the effects of low-dose LPS (L4391, Sigma Aldrich, USA) on inflammatory responses, the cells were treated with different concentrations of LPS including 0.001 pg/mL, 0.1 pg/mL, 0.01 ng/mL and 1 ng/mL, and then the expression levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines were analyzed after 8 h. To further assess the synergistic effects of low-dose LPS and HS on inflammatory responses, the cells were treated with an LPS concentration (0.01 ng/mL) that did not induce a pro-inflammatory effect and then subjected to HS. After HS, the cells were allowed to recover at 37 °C for 6 h and collected for further analysis. To investigate the protective effect of arginine against HS/LPS-induced inflammatory responses, the cells were treated with LPS at a concentration of 100 ng/mL, which is a commonly used concentration in inflammation-related studies. In brief, the cells were administered with arginine solution (1.0 mM) or the solvent for 1 h, and then incubated at 41 °C and treated with 100 ng/mL LPS for 2 h. Subsequently, the cells were collected to detect the expression levels of pro-inflammatory genes.

Synthesis and characterization of arginine-enriched oral inulin hydrogel

Firstly, 30 mg arginine was dissolved in 500 μL methanol, followed by the addition of 0.6 g inulin and 1 mL pure water. Next, the solution was heated in water bath at 80 °C and stirred at 800 rpm for 10 min, and then standing at room temperature for 12 h to generate arginine-enriched oral inulin hydrogel (Arg_OIH). The rheological properties of Arg_OIH were evaluated by a 25-mm plate pressure controlled rheometer (HAAKE MARS60, Thermo Fisher, Germany). The test temperature was maintained at 37 °C to simulate human physiological environment. To examine the dynamic change, the hydrogel was analyzed at an angular frequency in the range of 0.1 to 70 rad/s under fixed strain of 1%, and the storage modulus (G') and loss modulus (G’) was recorded accordingly. To evaluate the shear thinning characteristics of the hydrogel, the viscosity change of the hydrogel was measured with the shear rate in the range of 0.01 to 100 s⁻1. In the in vitro degradation experiment, 1 g of Arg_OIH was weighed and placed in the center of the Petri dish. Then 10 mL PBS or simulated gastric acid (SGA) was added, and the hydrogel morphology was observed and photographed at 1, 3, 5, and 7 h.

To examine the in vitro release of arginine, Arg_OIH (equivalent to 12 mg arginine) was placed in a dialysis bag with a molecular weight cutoff of 1,000 Da and immersed in 25 mL PBS releasing buffer. The buffer solution was stirred at a rate of 100 rpm at 37 °C. Then, 10 mL of buffer solution was collected at 2, 4, 6, 8, and 10 h and refilled with equivalent volume of fresh PBS in the releasing buffer. Afterwards, the concentration of arginine was determined by using high performance liquid chromatography (HPLC, LC-2050, Shimadzu, Japan). The mobile phase was a mixture of acetonitrile and water with a volume ratio of 8:2, and the pH of water was adjusted to 2.5 with phosphoric acid. The main parameters of HPLC were set as follows: flow rate, 0.5 mL/min; injection volume, 10 μL; column temperature, 30 °C; detection wavelength, 208 nm.

The biodistribution of arginine in the gastrointestinal tract of mice after intake of oral arginine solution (Arg_OS) or Arg_OIH was investigated by the in vivo image system (IVIS, Lumina 3, Revvity, USA). Arginine was initially labeled with FITC and fabricated into the hydrogel. Both free FITC-labeled arginine and the corresponding inulin hydrogel were orally administered to mice. The mice were sacrificed 2 h post-administration, and their gastrointestinal tracts were imaged under IVIS system. The excitation wavelength was 540 nm, the emission wavelength was 570 nm, and the exposure time was 10 s.

16S rRNA gene sequencing

Total microbial genomic DNA in the intestinal contents was extracted by using the TIANamp stool DNA kit following the manufacturer’s procedure (TianGen, China). The concentrations of total extracted DNA were measured via a NanoDrop NC-2000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) and agarose gel electrophoresis. The V3-V4 regions of 16S rRNA gene were amplified with the forward primer 338 F (5'-ACTCCTACGGGAGGCAGCA-3') and the reverse primer 806R (5'-GGACTACHVGGGTWTCTAAT-3'). PCR amplicons were purified with Vazyme VAHTSTM DNA clean beads (Vazyme, Nanjing, China) and quantified by the Quant-iT PicoGreen dsDNA Assay Kit (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA) on a microplate reader (BioTek, FLx800). Subsequently, the sequencing library was established with the NovaSeq 6000SP reagent kit (500 cycles) and sequenced on the Illumina HiSeq 2500 platform (Illlumina, San Diego, USA).

Afterward, the raw tags were subjected to bioinformatics analysis. Paired-end reads were first merged using FLASH software (v1.2.7), and low-quality sequences were removed to obtain clean tags via QIIME2 [22]. Effective tags were identified through UCHIME algorithm and clustered into operational taxonomic units (OTUs) at the similarity threshold of 97% with UPARSE pipeline [23]. Taxonomic assignment of OTUs was conducted on the basis of SILVA database and Mothur algorithm at a confidence cutoff of 80%. In addition, the ratio of Gram-negative to Gram-positive bacteria (GNB/GPB) was estimated according to phylum-level taxonomic classification, with Bacteroidota, Desulfobacterota, Proteobacteria, and Verrucomicrobiota classified as putative Gram-negative phyla as well as Firmicutes and Actinobacteriota classified as putative Gram-positive phyla.

Untargeted LC–MS-based metabolomics analysis

Metabolites were extracted from the intestinal contents, serum, liver and cortex of mice for metabolomics analysis. Approximately 20 mg of the intestinal contents was weighed into an Eppendorf tube, followed by the addition of 300 μL of pre-cooled methanol. The mixture was homogenized for 60 s using a high-throughput tissue homogenizer (Beyotime, Shanghai, China) and then centrifuged at 10,000 × g for 10 min at 4 °C. The supernatant was collected into a clean tube, evaporated under a nitrogen stream, and stored at −80 °C until analysis. For the serum samples, 50 μL was transferred to an Eppendorf tube and extracted using 200 μL of methanol and acetonitrile solution (1:1, v/v). After vortexing for 15 s and standing on ice for 15 min, the mixture was centrifuged at 10,000 × g for 10 min at 4 °C. Subsequently, the supernatant was lyophilized under a nitrogen stream and stored at −80 °C until use. For the tissue samples, approximately 30 mg of liver or cortex tissue samples were weighed into an Eppendorf tube, followed by the addition of 200 μL of pre-cooled water and homogenized for 60 s by a high-throughput tissue homogenizer (Beyotime, Shanghai, China). Next, the mixture was added with 800 μL of methanol and acetonitrile solution (1:1, v/v) and subjected to a supersonic extraction for 30 min. Finally, the mixture was centrifuged at 10,000 × g for 10 min at 4 °C, and the supernatant was freeze-dried under a nitrogen stream and stored at −80 °C until further analysis. Prior to LC–MS analysis, the lyophilized powder was redissolved into methanol containing 5 ppm of 2-chloro-L-phenylalanine as an external standard filtered via a 0.22 μm filter membrane.

The untargeted metabolomics analysis was conducted on a Vanquish UHPLC system combining with Orbitrap Exploris 120 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) under both ESI + and ESI- modes at a mass range of 80–1200 m/z. The chromatographic separation was performed with an ACQUITY UPLC® HSS T3 column (2.1 × 100 mm, 1.8 µm, Waters, MA, USA). For the positive mode, the mobile phase consisted of 0.1% formic acid in acetonitrile (B1) and 0.1% form acid in water (A1). The gradient elution procedure was set as follows: 0–1 min, 8% B1; 1–8 min, 8%−98% B1; 8–10 min, 98% B1; 10–10.1 min, 98%−8% B1; 10.1–12 min, 8% B1. For the negative mode, the mobile phase included acetonitrile (B2) and 5 mM ammonium formate in water (A2), and the gradient elution procedure was: 0–1 min, 8% B2; 1–8 min, 8%−98% B2; 8–10 min, 98% B2; 10–10.1 min, 98%−8% B2; 10.1 ~ 12 min, 8% B2. The column temperature was maintained at 40 °C, and the sample injection volume was 2 µL. The MS1 and MS/MS spectra were acquired simultaneously in this study. The main parameters were set as follows: spray voltage, 3.50 kV (ESI +) and −2.50 kV (ESI-); auxiliary gas flow, 10 arb; sheath gas pressure, 40 arb; capillary temperature, 325 °C; MS1 resolving power, 60,000 FWHM; MS/MS resolving power, 15,000 FWHM; normalized collision energy, 30%; dynamic exclusion time, automatic.

The raw data were converted to mzXML format and preprocessed for peak extraction, alignment and filtering by using XCMS software (XCMS plus, CA, USA). The main parameters included: peak width, 50; bw, 0.1; minfrac, 80%; mzwid, 0.1. Peaks with the intensity below 5000 and the coefficient of variation (CV) above 30% were filtered. Metabolites were identified on the basis of the mzCloud library (https://www.mzcloud.org/), LIPID MAPS (https://www.lipidmaps.org/), HMDB (https://hmdb.ca/), MoNA (https://mona.fiehnlab.ucdavis.edu/), NIST_2020_MSMS, the self-built library of standards (PSNGM) and AI predicted MSMS spectrum library. The main parameters were set as follows: MS1 tolerance, 0.01 Da; MS2 tolerance, 0.05 Da; smoothing level, 3; minimum peak width, 5; mass slice width, 0.05; the threshold score of identification, 70.

Targeted detection of arginine using UPLC-MS

The concentrations of arginine and citrulline were measured by using the Waters ACQUITY UPLC H-Class Plus system (Waters, Milford, MA, USA) coupled with the Xevo G2-XS QTOF (Waters, Milford, MA, USA). The LC separation was carried out on a C18 chromatographic column (2.1 × 100 mm, 1.7 µm, Waters, MA, USA). Data were acquired in negative ion (ESI-) mode at the mass range of 50–1200 m/z. The column temperature was maintained at 40 °C, and the sample injection volume was 5 µL. The mobile phase contained 0.1% formic acid in water (A) and 0.1% formic acid in acetonitrile (B). The gradient elution procedure of arginine was set as follows: 0–1 min, 90% B; 1–3 min, 90%−50% B; 3–4 min, 50% B; 4–4.5 min, 50%−90% B; 4.5–6 min, 90% B. The gradient elution procedure of citrulline was established as described below: 0–1.5 min, 90%−15%B; 1.5–2.6 min, 15% B; 2.6–2.7 min, 15%−90% B; 2.7–4 min, 90% B. The concentrations of arginine and citrulline in samples were calculated using a standard curve.

ELISA and biochemical assays

The enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) was utilized to measure the levels of interleukin-6 (IL-6), tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α), interleukin-1β (IL-1β) and lipopolysaccharide (LPS) by using commercial kits (CUSABIO, Wuhan, China). The levels of alanine aminotransferase (ALT) and aspartate aminotransferase (AST) were detected in the serum using commercial kits from Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute (Nanjing, China).

Western blot (WB) analysis

For WB analysis, the proteins were extracted from animal tissues or cell samples in the lysis buffer (AR0103-100, BOSTER, Wuhan, China) containing 1 mM of protease (p1260, APPLYGEN, Beijing, CHina) and phosphatase inhibitors (ST506-2, Beyotime, Shanghai, China) for 30 min on ice, followed by homogenization and sonication. The supernatants were collected after centrifuging at 14,000 g for 15 min at 4 °C, and the concentrations of proteins were quantified using the coomassie brilliant blue method. Equal amounts of total proteins (60 μg) were loaded on and separated by 10% SDS-PAGE and then transferred onto PVDF Membrane (ISEQ00010, Millipore, Germany). Membranes were blocked with 5% non-fat milk (1172GR500, Biofroxx, Germany) for 1 h at room temperature and then incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies, including ACTIN (1:10,000, 66,009–1-Ig, Proteintech), TLR4 (1:1000, AF7017, Affinity), MyD88 (1:1000, 67,969–1-Ig, Proteintech), IkBa (1:1000, ET1603-6, HUABIO), p65 (1:1000, 10,745–1-AP, Proteintech), and ubiquitin (1:1000, 43,124, Cell Signaling Technology). After washing 3 times with TBST, membranes were incubated with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies for 1.5 h at room temperature. The protein blots were scanned via a ChemiDoc XRS + Gel System (Bio-Rad, CA, USA) and the blots intensity was quantified using ImageJ software (v1.47, National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD, USA).

Drug affinity responsive target stability (DARTS)

The protein supernatant of the cells was incubated with 1 mM arginine or vehicle for 2 h at room temperature. Next, the proteins were treated with pronase (10,165,921,001, Roche, Basel, Switzerland) for 15 min at room temperature, and no pronase-treated group was used as a vehicle. The equal volume of loading buffer was added and completely mixed, and then the mixture was boiled for 10 min, separated by SDS-PAGE and stained with coomassie blue (PT0018, Leagene, Beijing, China). Moreover, the WB analysis was conducted to assess the binding capacity of arginine to the target proteins.

Cellular thermal shift assay (CETSA)

After discarding the culture medium, the cells were washed with PBS and lysed with ice-cold lysis buffer (AR0103-100, BOSTER, Wuhan, China) for 15 min. The lysates were transferred to microcentrifuge tubes and centrifuged at 14,000 g for 15 min at 4 °C, and the supernatants were collected into a new tube. Subsequently, 1 mM of arginine or an equal volume of PBS was added and incubated for 2 h at room temperature. The mixture was aliquoted into 100 μL portions and heated in a metal bath at 37, 40, 43, 46, 49, 52 and 55 °C for 3 min, followed by cooling on ice for 3 min. After centrifugation, 60 μL of each sample was collected for the WB analysis.

Co-Immunoprecipitation (Co-IP)

Prior to the co-IP experiments, proteins were extracted from animal tissues or cell samples with 0.3 mL of NP40 lysis buffer (P0013F, Beyotime Technology, Shanghai, China) containing 1 mM of PMSF (ST506, Beyotime Technology, Shanghai, China), and a part of the lysate was regarded as the input sample. Total protein was isolated by centrifugation and added with 1 μg anti-MyD88 antibody (67,969–1-Ig, Proteintech) or mouse IgG (B900620, Proteintech) under gentle rotation at 4 °C overnight. Next, 40 μL of protein A + G agarose beads (P2055, Beyotime Technology, Shanghai, China) were added to protein lysate and incubated at 4 °C for 4 h. The immunocomplexes were harvested by washing with PBS for 5 times, and then the mixture was treated with SDS, followed by a heat treatment at 100 °C for 10 min. Finally the samples were separated in SDS-PAGE electrophoresis.

Histopathological examination

The mice were anesthetized using isoflurane and euthanized by cervical dislocation. Liver and cortex tissues were rapidly harvested, fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (BL539A, Biosharp, China) and embedded in paraffin after gradient dehydration. Subsequently, the embedded tissue samples were cut into sections with a thickness of 5 μm, and processed for hematoxylin (ZLI-9610, ZSGB-BIO, Beijing, China) and eosin (ZLI-9613, ZSGB-BIO, Beijing, China) staining. Histological images of the tissue samples were observed and captured under the light microscope (Nikon, Tokyo, Japan). For histological evaluation, at least 6 non-overlapping microscopic fields were randomly selected and examined for each tissue section. Liver injury was semi-quantitatively scored on a scale of 0–4 for each pathological feature, including hepatocyte edema/ballooning degeneration, disruption of hepatic lobular architecture, hepatocyte necrosis, inflammatory cell infiltration, and sinusoidal congestion or hemorrhage. Similarly, cortex injury was scored from 0 to 4 according to neuronal degeneration/pyknosis, preservation of cortical architecture, interstitial brain edema, cerebral vascular congestion, and inflammatory infiltration or petechial hemorrhage. The histology scores were independently assessed by three researchers, and the average scores were calculated. The total histology score ranged from 0 to 20, and higher scores indicate more severe tissue injury.

For immunohistochemical staining, the liver and cortex sections were baked at 65 °C for 8–10 h. After dewaxing and rehydration using xylene and a graded ethanol series, the sections were washed with PBS for 15 min and subjected to antigen retrieval by boiling in 10 mM sodium citrate buffer (pH = 6.0) at 100 °C for 5 min. After washing with PBS, the sections were treated with 3% H₂O₂ (PO100A, Beyotime, Shanghai, China) for 10 min to block endogenous peroxidase activity and then blocked with 5% BSA for 1 h. The sections were incubated overnight at 4 °C with a primary antibody against p65 (1:200, 10,745–1-AP, Proteintech), washed with PBS and then incubated with an HRP-conjugated goat anti-rabbit secondary antibody (1:200, SA00001-2, Proteintech) for 1 h at 37 °C. Afterward, the signal was visualized using a DAB chromogenic kit (ZLI-9018, ZSGB-BIO, Beijing, China), followed by nuclear counterstaining with hematoxylin for 1 min. Finally, images were acquired by a light microscope (Nikon, Tokyo, Japan) and quantified with ImageJ software.

For immunofluorescent staining, the RAW264.7 and BV2 cells were seeded on cell climbing slides and fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 30 min. The tissue sections and cells were permeabilized with 0.5% Triton X-100 for 10 min at room temperature, blocked with 5% BSA for 30 min, and incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against TLR4 (1:100, AF7017, Affinity), p65 (1:100, 10,745–1-AP, Proteintech), and MyD88 (1:100, 67,969–1-Ig, Proteintech). After washing with PBS, the tissue sections and cells were incubated for 1 h at 37 °C with secondary antibodies, Alexa Fluor 488-conjugated anti-rabbit IgG (1:100, HA1121, HUABIO) or Alexa Fluor 594-conjugated anti-mouse IgG (1:100, AB150116, Abcam). Cell nuclei were counterstained with DAPI (C0065, Solarbio, Beijing, China) for 15 min. Images were acquired using a Nikon C2si confocal microscope (Nikon, Tokyo, Japan) and quantified with ImageJ software.

RNA isolation and quantitative RT-PCR

Total RNA were extracted from tissue or cell samples using Trizol reagent (Cat#9109, TaKaRa, Beijing, China) and reverse-transcribed to cDNA with HiScript III RT SuperMix for qPCR (+ gDNA wiper) (R323-01, Vazyme, Nanjing, China). Quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR) was performed using a SYBR Green PCR Master Mix (1,725,124, Bio-Rad, CA, USA) on the CFX384 Touch Real-Time PCR Detection System (Bio-Rad Laboratories, CA, USA). The relative expression level was calculated by the ΔΔCT method and normalized to β-actin. The specific primer sequences were synthesized from Tsingke Biotechnology (Beijing, China) as listed in Table S2.

Transfection with siRNA

Small interfering RNA (siRNA)-mediated gene silencing was performed to achieve the knockdown of TLR4 and MyD88 in BV2 and RAW264.7 cells. Briefly, the cells were seeded into six-well plates and cultured until their density reached 70%−80%. The cells were then transfected with siRNAs targeting TLR4 or MyD88 using Lipofectamine 3000 reagent (Invitrogen, USA) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. At 48 h after transfection, the knockdown efficiency was verified by Western blot analysis. The siRNA sequences used in this study are provided in Table S3.

Statistical analysis

In this study, all mice were randomly allocated to the experiments including group assignment, HS modeling, animal treatment, sample collection and data analysis. Omics data were subjected to log-transformation followed by Pareto-scaling. Metabolomics data were analyzed through principal component analysis (PCA) to assess the metabolic changes among different groups by using SIMCA-P + software (v12.0, Umetrics AB, Umea, Sweden). The alpha-diversity of the gut microbiota was examined via non-metric multidimensional scaling (NMDS) with Bray–Curtis distance in R software (v2.15.3). Furthermore, the PCA and NMDS were statistically assessed by the permutational multivariate analysis of variance (PERMANOVA) with 999 permutations using vegan package in R software (v2.15.3). To minimize false discoveries for omics data, the statistic difference between two groups was analyzed by two-tailed unpaired Student’s t test with Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) correction using MetaboAnalyst 6.0 [24]. The volcano plot analysis was carried out by plotting log2 (FC, fold change) versus -log10 (FDR-adjusted P). The metabolite set enrichment analysis was performed for the functional classification of metabolites based on the KEGG database in MetaboAnalyst 6.0 [24].

Except omics data, univariate data analysis was conducted by using SPSS 22.0 software (SPSS, Inc., Chicago, IL, USA). The statistic difference between two groups was assessed by two-tailed unpaired Student’s t test, whereas one-way or two-way ANOVA followed by Dunnett’s or Sidak post-hoc test was applied for comparisons among multiple groups (≥ 3 groups). A p-value of less than 0.05 was considered as a statistically significant difference. The relationships among different indicators were analyzed by partial correlation analysis to exclude the interference of treatment and time point for mice as well as age and gender for human in SPSS 22.0 software.

Results

HS induces inflammatory responses and metabolic disorders in mice

To examine the impact of HS on inflammatory responses and metabolic changes, mice were subjected to a high-temperature and high-humidity environment (39 °C/60% RH) for 1.5 h, and then intestinal contents, serum, liver and cortex samples were collected after 6 and 48 h of rewarming for untargeted LC–MS-based metabolomics (Fig. 1a). After HS treatment, the mice exhibited significant weakness and the rectal temperature reached over 40 °C (Fig. 1b). Compared with control (CTRL) mice, the levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines were elevated in the serum of HS mice at 6 h after rewarming (HS6H) and returned to normal levels by 48 h (HS48H), particularly IL-6 (Fig. 1c) and IL-1β (Fig. 1e) showing statistically significant differences. TNF-α exhibited a similar trend, but its change did not reach statistical significance (Fig. 1d). We also found that HS6H mice had significantly higher levels of IL-6, TNF-α and IL-1β in both liver (Fig. 1f-h) and cortex (Fig. 1i-k) than CTRL mice, while their levels were significantly reduced in HS48H mice, except for hepatic IL-1β. Similar results were observed in serum ALT (Fig. 1l) and AST (Fig. 1m) levels, which are two main liver function indicators in clinical practice. According to the pathological staining results, liver and cortex histology scores were significantly higher in HS6H mice than CTRL mice, indicating hepatic and cortical injuries (Fig. 1n-p). These two indices were significantly reduced in HS48H mice, suggesting that hepatic and cortical injuries in HS mice were alleviated after 48 h of rewarming (Fig. 1n-p). In this study, we found that the mice exhibited a significant loss of body weight during HS, with an average decrease of 1.24 g (Figure S1a). Since all mice were fasted during HS, we speculate that dehydration might be the primary cause. To assess the impact of dehydration on liver function and inflammatory responses in mice, we performed a water deprivation (WD) experiment, as illustrated in Figure S1b. After 12 h of WD, the mice showed an average body weight loss of 1.53 g (Figure S1c), which was comparable to the level of weight loss observed during HS. The results show that serum ALT (Figure S1d) and AST (Figure S1e) levels did not differ significantly between CTRL and WD mice. In addition, no significant differences were observed in the expression levels of IL-6, TNF-α and IL-1β in the liver (Figure S1f) and cortex (Figure S1g) between CTRL and WD mice. These findings indicate that dehydration during HS treatment in this study was insufficient to induce significant liver injury and inflammatory responses in mice.

Metabolomics results reveal that the number of identified metabolites in the feces and liver samples was higher than that in the serum and cortex samples, and the proportion of significantly altered metabolites after HS was also greater (Fig. 1q). The Venn diagram analysis identified 160 common metabolites that were notably altered in all samples after HS (Fig. 1r), and these metabolites mainly involved in arginine biosynthesis, histidine metabolism, valine, leucine and isoleucine biosynthesis, citrate cycle, as well as arginine and proline metabolism (Fig. 1s). These findings suggest that HS can result in acute systemic inflammatory responses, accompanied by systemic metabolic disorders.

HS affects host metabolism and is associated with inflammation in mice

Herein we used a LC–MS-based metabolomics method to explore the effect of HS on host metabolome in mice. PCA results show that the metabolic patterns of intestinal contents (PERMANOVA p = 0.001, Fig. 2a), serum (PERMANOVA p = 0.001, Fig. 2b), liver (PERMANOVA p = 0.001, Fig. 2c), and cortex (PERMANOVA p = 0.02, Fig. 2d) in HS6H and HS48H mice were clearly separated from those of CTRL mice, indicating that HS altered the metabolic phenotype of the mice. However, the differences in the metabolic patterns between HS6H and HS48H mice were relatively modest in the liver (Fig. 2c) and cortex (Fig. 2d). The volcano plots were used to identify the significantly altered metabolites in the intestinal contents (Figure S2), serum (Figure S3), liver (Figure S4) and cortex (Figure S5) between CTRL and HS6H mice as well as between HS6H and HS48H mice. Subsequently, we utilized the Venn diagram to select the common metabolites that were significantly altered in the HS6H vs CTRL and the HS48H vs HS6H, and found 8, 4 and 2 metabolites in the intestinal contents (Figure S2c), serum (Figure S3c) and liver (Figure S4c), respectively. Using p < 0.05 and FC > 2.0 as the criterion, however, no commonly altered metabolites were identified in the cortex metabolome (Figure S5c). Therefore, we analyzed only the metabolites with p < 0.05 and identified 3 metabolites that met the criteria in the cortex (Figure S5d-f). These selected metabolites were presented as heatmaps in Fig. 2e for the intestinal contents, Fig. 2f for the serum, Fig. 2g for the liver, and Fig. 2h for the cortex. In the intestinal contents, we found that the levels of prostaglandin E2, avermectin B1a aglycone, L-arginine, heneicosylic acid, Ala-Glu and 4-hydroxybenzaldehyde were significantly reduced in HS6H mice relative to CTRL and HS48H mice, and a contrary result was observed in lumichrome and corticosterone (Fig. 2e). HS6H mice had significantly lower levels of L-arginine and 5-AOPA in the serum as well as L-arginine in both liver and cortex than CTRL and HS48H mice (Fig. 2f-h). Besides, we observed drastically higher levels of phenol sulphate and AKOS006369514 in the serum (Fig. 2f) and NAAO in the liver (Fig. 2g) of HS6H mice. In the cortex, the levels of LysoPA(16:0) and barbituric acid were considerably decreased in HS48H mice as compared with other two groups (Fig. 2h). The results from partial correlation analysis reveal that the expression levels of proinflammatory cytokines and liver function indicators were positively correlated with corticosterone and lumichrome in the intestinal contents, AKOS006369514 and phenol sulphate in the serum as well as N-alpha-acetylornithine in the liver (Fig. 2i). Notably, the arginine levels in all samples studied in this study were negatively associated with proinflammatory cytokines and liver function indicators, suggesting that arginine may exert a protective effect against HS-associated inflammation and liver injury.

HS-induced gut microbial alterations aggravates inflammatory responses in mice

To investigate the effect of HS on the gut microbiota in mice, intestinal contents were collected and subjected to a 16S rRNA sequencing analysis. At the phylum level, relative to CTRL mice, the proportion of Firmicutes was markedly decreased in the intestinal contents of HS6H mice and tended to recover in HS48H mice (Fig. 3a). The percentage of Desulfobacterota was obviously increased in the intestinal contents of HS48H mice, while the percentage of Verrucomicrobiota was elevated in HS6H mice compared with other two groups (Fig. 3a). Moreover, the proportions of Actinobacteriota and Bacteroidota varied only slightly among the CTRL, HS6H, and HS48H groups (Fig. 3a). NMDS analysis revealed that the β-diversity of the gut microbiota in the intestinal contents of CTRL mice was clearly distinct from that in HS mice, whereas the difference between HS6H and HS48H mice was relatively modest (PERMANOVA p = 0.001, Fig. 3b). In this study, the volcano plot analysis was carried out to identify the significantly altered microbes between CTRL and HS6H mice (Figure S6a) as well as between HS6H and HS48H mice (Figure S6b). Then, we identified 9 common microbes at the genus level among them from the Venn diagram (Figure S6c) and presented as a heatmap (Fig. 3c). The results demonstrate that 7 out of the 9 identified microbes belong to the Firmicutes phylum. Relative to CTRL and HS48H mice, HS6H mice exhibited significantly lower abundances of Turicimonas, Borkfalkla, Phocea, Evtepla, Parasutterella and CAG-41, and higher abundances of UBA5905 and Eubacterium (Fig. 3c). Additionally, we observed that the ratio of Gram-negative to Gram-positive bacteria (GNB/GPB) was significantly higher in HS6H mice than that in CTRL mice (Fig. 3d). Although this ratio declined in HS48H mice compared with HS6H mice, the change was not statistically significant (Fig. 3d). We also found that the levels of LPS were significantly higher in the intestinal contents (Fig. 3e) and serum (Fig. 3f) of HS6H mice relative to CTRL mice and lower in HS48H mice, which is similar with the change in the GNB/GPB ratio. Since LPS is a component of the outer membrane in GNB, these results suggest that HS may increase the proportion of GNB, thereby elevating LPS levels.

To further determine the critical role of the gut microbiota in HS-related inflammatory responses, we transplanted fecal microbiota from CTRL or HS6H mice into healthy recipient mice (FMTC or FMTH) for 3 weeks, followed by HS modeling, as shown in Fig. 3g. At the phylum level, FMTH mice had a lower proportion of Firmicutes and a higher proportion of Verrucomicrobiota than FMTC mice in the feces (Fig. 3h), which is consistent with the phylum-level changes in the gut microbiota of HS6H mice presented in Fig. 3a. In addition, a reduced proportion of Actinobacteriota was also obtained in the feces of FMTH mice relative to FMTC mice (Fig. 3h). Compared with FMTC mice, the relative abundances of Evtepla, Turicimonas, CAG-41 and Parasutterella were significantly decreased, while Eubacterium was markedly increased in the feces of FMTH mice (Fig. 3i). A higher GNB/GPB ratio was detected in the feces of FMTH mice than that in FMTC mice (p = 0.07, Fig. 3j). These alterations were similar to the changes in the gut microbiota characteristics observed between CTRL and HS6H mice, indicating that FMT partly transferred and established the gut microbiota of HS6H mice in recipient mice. FMTH mice exhibited significantly higher LPS levels in the intestinal contents (Fig. 3k) and serum (Fig. 3l) than FMTC mice. Relative to FMTC mice, the levels of ALT (Fig. 3m, P = 0.08) and AST (Fig. 3n, p < 0.001) were higher in the serum of FMTH mice. The HE staining results reveal that the histology scores of the liver (Fig. 3o) and cortex (Fig. 3p) were significantly elevated in FMTH mice compared with FMTC mice, suggesting that FMT from HS6H mice aggravated HS-induced hepatic and cortical injuries. Since p65 plays a key role in acute inflammation by inducing transcription of proinflammatory genes [25], we examine the nuclear translocation of p65 through immumohistochemical staining. The results demonstrate that FMTH mice displayed marked increases in relative p65 positive areas in the liver (Fig. 3o) and cortex (Fig. 3p) relative to FMTC mice. In addition, we also found that FMTH mice had significantly higher levels of IL-6, TNF-α and IL-1β in both liver (Fig. 3q) and cortex (Fig. 3r) than FMTC mice. Thus it can be seen that the gut microbial alterations aggravated inflammatory responses in HS mice.

To further verify the mediating role of the gut microbiota in HS-associated inflammatory responses, the mice were treated with an antibiotic cocktail (ABX) for 7 consecutive days to establish pseudo-germ-free mice, followed by HS induction (Fig. 4a). In vitro fecal bacteria culture demonstrated that ABX treatment markedly depleted the endogenous aerobic (Fig. 4b) and anaerobic (Fig. 4c) bacteria in the feces of mice. After HS treatment, ABX-treated (ABX +) mice exhibited significantly lower LPS levels in both the intestinal contents (Fig. 4d) and serum (Fig. 4e) compared with non-ABX-treated (ABX-) mice. Relative to ABX- mice, ABX + mice exhibited improved liver injury after HS treatment, as indicated by lower serum ALT (Figure f) and AST (Figure g) levels as well as reduced liver histology score (Fig. 4h). Figure 4i shows that cortex histology score was also markedly decreased in ABX + mice than that in ABX- mice after HS treatment. After HS, ABX + mice had a significantly lower p65 positive area in both the liver (Fig. 4h) and cortex (Fig. 4i) compared with ABX- mice. Consistently, the expression levels of IL-6, TNF-α and IL-1β in the liver (Fig. 4j) and cortex (Fig. 4k) were also significantly decreased in ABX + mice. These results further confirm that HS-induced alterations in the gut microbiota are an important risk factor for aggravating host inflammatory responses.

Although circulating LPS levels were significantly increased in mice after HS, the magnitude of this increase was relatively modest. We therefore speculated that low levels of LPS may act synergistically with HS to amplify inflammatory responses. To test this hypothesis, we first stimulated RAW264.7 (Figure S7a) and BV2 (Figure S7b) cells with different concentrations of LPS and detected the mRNA levels of pro-inflammatory genes. The results show that, except for LPS at 1 ng/mL, none of the other concentrations caused increases in IL-6, TNF-α and IL-1β in both RAW264.7 (Figure S7c) and BV2 (Figure S7d) cells, of which 0.01 ng/mL LPS was selected for subsequent experiments. Next, we treated RAW264.7 and BV2 cells with low-dose LPS (0.01 ng/mL) plus HS, and then assessed p65/DAPI colocalization by immunofluorescence staining (Figure S8a and b). As illustrated in Figure S8c-f, neither HS nor LPS alone increased the colocalization levels of p65 and DAPI relative to the vehicle (Veh) group, whereas combined HS + LPS treatment markedly enhanced p65/DAPI colocalization in both cells. These findings imply that low-level LPS cooperated with HS to promote nuclear translocation of p65. Compared with Veh group, we also found that the expression levels of IL-6, TNF-α and IL-1β were significantly increased in both RAW264.7 (Figure S8g) and BV2 (Figure S8h) cells after HS + LPS treatment, whereas neither HS nor LPS alone induced significant changes. Thus, even though HS induced only a modest increase in circulating LPS levels, low-level LPS can still promote inflammatory responses through its synergistic action with HS.

Gut microbiota-driven arginine catabolism is enhanced in HS mice

We then utilized an UpSet plot analysis to show intersections of important metabolites between different samples and identify arginine as a common metabolite (Fig. 5a). The Spearman correlation analysis reveals that arginine exhibited significant positive correlations across different samples (Fig. 5b). The arginine levels in the intestinal contents and serum were significantly and positively related to Borkfalkia, Evtepia, CAG-41, Parasutterella and Turicimonas, and negatively correlated with UBA5905 and QAMM01 (Fig. 5b). Hepatic arginine levels showed significant positive associations with Borkfalkia, Evtepia, Parasutterella and Turicimonas, and negative relationships with UBA5905, QAMM01 and Eubacterium. We also found that arginine in the cortex was significantly negatively correlated with UBA5905 and QAMM01. Moreover, there was a significant positive correlation between cortex arginine and Phocea (Fig. 5b). Furthermore, PICRUSt2 was conducted to predict metabolic functions of the gut microbiota between CTRL and HS6H mice based on the KEGG database. We found that arginine and proline metabolism was notably up-regulated in the intestinal contents of HS6H mice as compared with CTRL mice (Fig. 5c). Additionally, HS6H mice had significantly higher expression levels of speA and argR/ahrC in the gut microbiota than CTRL mice (Fig. 5d). The speA gene encodes an arginine decarboxylase that catalyzes the production of agmatine from arginine [26], while the argR/ahrC gene encodes the biosynthesis repressor of arginine that negatively regulates arginine biosynthesis [27]. These findings imply that the gut microbiota may also participate in arginine metabolism.

As a semi-essential amino acid, a small amount of arginine can be synthesized in the liver and kidneys of the body, but the majority still need to be obtained from foods (Fig. 5e). We found that the levels of arginine significantly increased in the feces of mice after antibiotic treatment (Fig. 5f), indicating that the gut microbiota may play a regulatory role in arginine catabolism. Moreover, after HS treatment, the arginine levels in the intestinal contents were notably lower in mice with FMT from HS6H mice (FMTH) than those in mice with FMT from CTRL mice (FMTC), as shown in Fig. 5g. Compared with FMTC mice, serum arginine levels also decreased in FMTH mice, although the difference did not reach statistical significance (Fig. 5h). Since arginine and citrulline are two key amino acids involved in arginine metabolism (Fig. 5e), we analyzed their changes in different samples from ABX + mice after HS. Relative to ABX- mice, after HS treatment, the levels of arginine in the intestinal contents were significantly increased in ABX + mice (Fig. 5i), whereas citrulline was significantly decreased (Fig. 5n). In addition, after HS treatment, arginine and citrulline levels in the serum (Fig. 5j and o), liver (Fig. 5k and p), kidney (Fig. 5l and q), and cortex (Fig. 5m and r) were significantly higher in ABX + mice compared with ABX- mice. To further confirm whether HS enhances the gut microbial arginine catabolism, we conducted an in vitro incubation experiment by adding arginine to fecal microbial suspensions from CTRL and HS6H mice, respectively (Fig. 5s). The results demonstrate that the arginine levels in the fecal microbial suspension from HS6H mice was significantly lower than that in CTRL mice after 15 min of incubation, whereas no significant difference was observed between the two groups at 5 min. The gene expression levels of arginine synthesis- and catabolism-related enzymes were analyzed in the liver (Fig. 5t) and kidney (Fig. 5u) between CTRL and HS6H mice, but no significant differences were observed. Taken together, these findings suggest that lower arginine levels in HS mice might be largely due to enhanced arginine catabolism of the gut microbiota, rather than the altered endogenous arginine metabolism.

Pretreatment with arginine prevents HS-associated inflammatory responses in vivo and in vitro

In this study, we first evaluated the effects of arginine pretreatment at different doses on liver function and inflammatory responses in HS mice (Figure S9a). The results show that, after HS treatment, mice pretreated with arginine at doses of 50 or 100 mg/kg exhibited no significant differences in serum ALT (Figure S9b) and AST (Figure S9c) levels compared with mice without arginine pretreatment. Pretreatment with 200 mg/kg arginine significantly reduced serum ALT and AST levels in HS mice, whereas 300 mg/kg arginine also significantly decreased serum ALT levels but had no significant effect on AST (Figure S9b and c). Arginine pretreatment at all tested doses in this study significantly reduced hepatic IL-6 expression in HS mice (Figure S9d). However, hepatic TNF-α expression was markedly decreased only after pretreatment with 200 mg/kg arginine (Figure S9d). In addition, pretreatment with 50 or 100 mg/kg arginine had no significant effects on hepatic IL-1β expression in HS mice, whereas its level can be notably reduced after pretreatment with 200 or 300 mg/kg arginine (Figure S9d). In the cortex, pretreatment with 50 mg/kg arginine had no significant impacts on IL-6 and TNF-α in HS mice, but significantly reduced IL-1β expression (Figure S9e). Besides, pretreatment with 100, 200 and 300 mg/kg arginine notably decreased IL-6, TNF-α, and IL-1β expression levels in the cortex of HS mice (Figure S9e). Based on these findings, 200 mg/kg was considered as the dose that exerted a relatively stable protective effect and used for subsequent experiments.

To further validate the protective effect of arginine against hepatic and cortex inflammation, mice were pretreated with arginine of 200 mg/kg prior to HS modeling, and then samples were collected and analyzed after 6 h of rewarming (Fig. 6a). We found that pretreatment with 200 mg/kg arginine can notably reduce serum ALT (Fig. 6b) and AST (Fig. 6c) levels in HS6H mice. The HE staining results exhibit that the arginine pretreatment significantly decreased histology scores in the liver (Fig. 6d) and cortex (Fig. 6e) of HS6H mice. The results from immumohistochemical staining illustrate that pretreatment with arginine significantly reduced the translocation of p65 to the nucleus in the liver (Fig. 6d) and cortex (Fig. 6e) of HS6H mice. Moreover, the relative expression levels of proinflammatory factors including IL-6, TNF-α and IL-1β were significantly lower in the liver (Fig. 6f) and cortex (Fig. 6g) of HS6H mice after arginine pretreatment.

Subsequently, we assessed the protective role of arginine in HS-driven inflammatory responses in vitro. To determine the appropriate concentration of arginine, RAW264.7 (Figure S10a) and BV2 (Figure S11a) cells were treated with different concentrations of arginine for 6 h and subjected to the CCK8 test. The results show that treatment with 10 mM arginine significantly decreased the viability of RAW264.7 and BV2 cells, whereas arginine at concentrations below 1 mM had no significant effects on the viability of both two cell types (Figure S10b and b). Thus, 1 mM of arginine was selected for further experiments in this study. Next, RAW264.7 (Fig. 6h) and BV2 (Figure S11a) cells were treated with arginine (1 mM) for 1 h, subjected to the HS + LPS exposure for 2 h, and then collected for further analysis. The immunofluorescence results illustrate that the colocalization levels of p65 and DAPI was significantly increased in both RAW264.7 (Fig. 6i and j) and BV2 cells (Figure S11c and d) after the HS + LPS exposure, suggesting that p65 was activated and entered the nucleus. However, pretreatment with arginine can inhibit the nuclear translocation of p65. These findings were further validated by the levels of proinflammatory genes, where we found that the HS + LPS exposure significantly increased IL-6, IL-1β and TNF-α, but their levels were suppressed by pretreatment with arginine especially for IL-6 and IL-1β in both RAW264.7 (Fig. 6k) and BV2 cells (Figure S11e). These results imply that arginine has the ability to prevent HS-associated inflammatory responses.

The anti-inflammatory effect of arginine is mediated by interaction with MyD88

It has been well-known that the TLR4/MyD88 signaling pathway can promote the nuclear translocation of p65 and then induces inflammatory responses (Fig. 7a). To validate the involvement of this signaling pathway in HS-associated inflammatory responses, RAW264.7 and BV2 cells with TLR4 or MyD88 knockdown were established using siRNA transfection, followed by the HS + LPS exposure, as shown in Figure S12a and b, respectively. After siRNA transfection, the protein levels of TLR4 and MyD88 in RAW264.7 (Figure S12c and d) and BV2 (Figure S12e and f) cells were significantly reduced relative to those in the control group (si-NC). We found that the HS + LPS exposure significantly increased the expression levels of IL-6, TNF-α and IL-1β in RAW264.7 (Figure S12g and i) and BV2 (Figure S12h and j) cells in the si-NC group. Importantly, after TLR4 or MyD88 knockdown, no significant alterations in the levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines were observed in these two cell types after the HS + LPS exposure, and their levels were significantly lower than those in the si-NC group (Figure S12g-j). These findings indicate that the TLR4/MyD88 pathway plays a key role in HS-associated inflammatory responses.

To explore how arginine mediates this inflammatory pathway and potential targets, drug affinity responsive target stability (DARTS) and cell thermal shift assay (CETSA) were carried out in this study. According to coomassie brilliant blue staining, we found that arginine obviously inhibited the pronase-induced degradation of proteins with a molecular weight around 33 kDa (Fig. 7b). The DARTS results reveal that the protein levels of TLR4 (Fig. 7c and Fig. S13a), MyD88 (Fig. 7c and Fig. S13b), IkBα (Fig. 7c and Fig. S13c), and p65 (Fig. 7c and Fig. S13d) were significantly reduced after the addition of pronase. In the presence of arginine, of note, only MyD88 protein degradation was significantly inhibited (Fig. 7c and Fig. S13b). The CETSA results show that MyD88 protein was gradually degraded with increasing temperature (37–55 °C), but arginine significantly attenuated the level of MyD88 protein degradation (Fig. 7d and e). These results indicate an interaction between arginine and MyD88. Moreover, we used biotin to label arginine and a pull-down assay to further verify the interaction between arginine and MyD88, and found that MyD88 can be pulled down by biotin-labeled arginine (Fig. 7f and g), confirming that arginine has the ability to interact with MyD88. Then, both RAW264.7 and BV2 cells were treated with arginine to examine the effect of arginine on MyD88 protein levels. The results show that the HS + LPS treatment significantly increased MyD88 protein levels compared with the Veh group, whereas arginine treatment markedly inhibited this increase (Fig. 7h-j). In animal experiments, we also observed that exogenous arginine supplementation significantly reduced MyD88 levels in the liver and cortex tissues of HS mice (Fig. 7k-m). To further confirm that arginine can reduce MyD88 protein levels, we inhibited de novo protein synthesis with cycloheximide (CHX) and then treated the cells with arginine at different time intervals. The results from Western blotting reveal that MyD88 protein levels in both RAW264.7 (Figure S14a and b) and BV2 (Fig. 7n and o) cells were significantly decreased at 8 h of arginine treatment. When protein degradation was inhibited by MG132, we found that arginine treatment no longer led to decreased levels of MyD88 protein in RAW264.7 (Figure S14c and d) and BV2 (Fig. 7p and q) cells. To investigate whether arginine promotes ubiquitin (Ub)-mediated degradation of MyD88, we conducted Co-IP assays of MyD88 with Ub, and found that arginine significantly increased the ubiquitination levels of MyD88 in both RAW264.7 (Figure S14e) and BV2 (Fig. 7r) cells. Our results suggest that arginine reduced MyD88 protein levels through activating its Ub-mediated degradation.

To explore whether arginine affects the interaction between MyD88 and TLR4, co-IP experiments were performed in this study. The results demonstrate that the MyD88-TLR4 interaction was notably enhanced in both the liver (Fig. 8a) and cortex (Fig. 8b) of HS6H mice, but obviously decreased after arginine treatment. Similar results were also detected in both RAW264.7 (Figure S15a) and BV2 (Figure S15b) cells. Since arginine is able to decrease MyD88 protein levels, we inhibited protein degradation with MG132 and then performed co-IP assays. Similarly, arginine markedly attenuated the MyD88-TLR4 interaction induced by the HS + LPS treatment in both RAW264.7 (Fig. 8c) and BV2 (Fig. 8d) cells. To further confirm the inhibiting effect of arginine on the MyD88-TLR4 interaction, we used immunostaining to examine the co-localization of MyD88 and TLR4. In the presence of arginine, HS6H mice exhibited lower levels in the expression of MyD88 and the co-localization with TLR4 in both the liver (Fig. 8e) and cortex (Fig. 8f). Besides, we observed the same phenomenon in RAW264.7 and BV2 cells, as illustrated in Figure S16. To further verify that the protective effect of arginine against HS-associated inflammatory responses depends on MyD88, RAW264.7 and BV2 cells with MyD88-knockdown were pretreated with arginine and then subjected to the HS + LPS exposure, as illustrated in Figure S17a and b, respectively. In the si-NC group, arginine pretreatment markedly reduced the p65 nuclear translocation and p65 positive areas in RAW264.7 (Figure S17c and e) and BV2 (Figure S17d and f) cells after the HS + LPS exposure. However, after MyD88 knockdown, the beneficial effects of arginine were completely abolished (Figure S17c-f). In addition, similar results were observed for the expression levels of IL-6, TNF-α and IL-1β in RAW264.7 (Figure S17g) and BV2 (Figure S17h) cells, indicating that MyD88 is essential for the protective effect of arginine against HS-associated inflammatory responses. There results suggest that arginine reduced MyD88 protein levels and inhibited the MyD88-TLR4 interaction by interaction with MyD88, thereby reducing the nuclear translocation of p65 and the expression of proinflammatory genes.

The arginine level is reduced in the serum of HS patients and correlated with liver function and inflammatory indicators

To determine the clinical relevance of arginine, we recruited a cohort of volunteers and 10 normal individuals (NORM) and 12 heatstroke patients (HSP) were ultimately included in this study after matching for age and sex and excluding individuals with underlying illnesses (Table S1). HSP were selected according to a series of indicators, including the history of heat exposure, the core temperature above 40 °C, Glasgow coma scale, shock index, and disturbance of consciousness (Table S1). In addition, most HSP suffered from acute liver injury, gastrointestinal dysfunction, coagulation disorders and rhabdomyolysis (Table S1). In this study, serum samples were collected from NORM and HSP for further analysis (Fig. 9a). In this human cohort, HSP exhibited significantly higher serum levels of ALT (Fig. 9b), AST (Fig. 9c), LPS (Fig. 9d), and IL-6 (Fig. 9e) than NORM, suggesting that liver injury and inflammatory responses occurred in HSP. Additionally, a significantly lower arginine level was also detected in the serum of HSP relative to NORM (Fig. 9f). After adjustment for age and sex, partial correlation analysis showed that arginine levels were negatively associated with ALT (R = −0.29), AST (R = −0.55, p < 0.05), IL-6 (R = −0.64, p < 0.01), and LPS (R = −0.46, p < 0.05), indicating that arginine depletion may represent a risk factor for inflammatory responses and hepatic injury in HSP (Fig. 9g). Taken together, our results reveal that the gut microbiota was altered in HS mice as characterized by increased LPS levels and enhanced arginine catabolism (Fig. 9h). Then, arginine deficiency resulted in a higher level of MyD88 protein by inhibiting its ubiquitin-mediated degradation and an increased interaction of MyD88 to TLR4, thereby promoting the nuclear translocation of p65 and elevating the release of pro-inflammatory cytokines (Fig. 9h).

Arginine-enriched oral inulin hydrogel prevents HS-associated inflammatory responses exacerbated by the gut microbial alterations

In this study, we revealed that the gut microbiota was markedly altered and characterized by enhanced arginine catabolism and increased LPS levels in HS mice, and thereby aggravated inflammatory responses. Given that inulin as a prebiotic can modulate the structure and function of the gut microbiota, we formulated an arginine-enriched oral inulin hydrogel (Arg_OIH) to maintain the gut microbiota homeostasis and provide a sustained supply of arginine. Subsequently, we evaluated the protective effect of Arg_OIH against HS-associated inflammation and compared its efficacy with that of oral arginine solution (Arg_OS) used in clinical practice (Fig. 10a). The arginine incorporated inulin gel was facilely formed after heating and cooling the mixture solution of arginine and inulin (Fig. 10b). As shown in Figure S18a, Arg_OIH is white in appearance with excellent injectability and capable of passing through syringe needles with various sizes, which is crucial for oral administration. The dynamic mechanical study exhibited that the storage modulus (G') of Arg_OIH exceeded the loss modulus (G"), which is also a typical rheological property of hydrogel (Figure S18b). Besides, the shear viscosity of Arg_OIH decreased with increasing shear rate, demonstrating that it had a good shear thinning property and excellent injectability (Figure S18c). The heterogeneity of pH throughout the gastrointestinal tract poses a significant challenge to oral drug delivery. Typically, the pH range in the stomach is 1.0–3.0, and in the small intestine is 6.0–7.5, increasing to 7.0–8.0 in the rectum. Therefore, the oral stability of Arg_OIH was evaluated either in PBS (pH = 7.5) or simulated gastric acid (SGA, pH = 1.2). As displayed in Figure S18d, Arg_OIH was stable in both SGA and PBS for 7 h, maintaining its original morphology without dispersing. The high stabilities of Arg_OIH in both SGA and PBS were beneficial and could ensure the successful drug transports from stomach to intestines since gastric emptying usually occurs within 3–4 h [28]. Furthermore, the release profile of arginine from Arg_OIH at intestinal environment was assessed, and we found that the hydrogel could realize sustained release of arginine with around 50% at first 2 h and then reaching to about 88% after 10 h (Fig. 10c). The in vivo imaging results illustrate that Arg_OIH exhibited stronger fluorescence intensity in the intestine of mice compared with Arg_OS (Fig. 10d), suggesting the prolonged retention of arginine in the intestine due to the assistance of the hydrogel. The relatively long retention of arginine in the intestine would facilitate its absorption and extend therapeutic effects. After pretreatment with Arg_OS or Arg_OIH, the concentrations of arginine were increased in the intestinal contents (Fig. 10e) and serum (Fig. 10f) of mice, but Arg_OIH had a relatively higher level of arginine than Arg_OS.

Besides, NMDS analysis demonstrated significant differences in the β-diversity of the gut microbiota among HS, Arg_OS and Arg_OIH groups (Fig. 10g, PERMANOVA p = 0.001), suggesting that both Arg_OS and Arg_OIH pretreatments significantly altered the structure of the gut microbiota. At the phylum level, Arg_OIH pretreatment markedly increased the proportions of Firmicutes and Proteobacteria in the intestinal contents of HS mice, but decreased the proportions of Bacteroidetes and Verrucomicrobia (Fig. 10h). In addition, Arg_OS pretreatment had little effect on the proportions of Firmicutes and Proteobacteria in the intestinal contents of HS mice, but increased Bacteroidetes and decreased Verrucomicrobia (Fig. 10h). Next, the volcano plot analysis was used to identify the significantly altered microbes at the genus level between Arg_OS and HS groups (Figure S19a) as well as between Arg_OIH and HS groups (Figure S19b). The Venn diagram analysis identified 13 and 4 microbes that exhibited significant changes exclusively in Arg_OIH and Arg_OS pretreatments, respectively, while 6 microbes showed significant alterations under both pretreatments (Figure S19c). These microbes were illustrated as heatmaps, where we found that pretreatment with Arg_OS significantly increased the relative abundances of Parabacteroides and Prevotella, but decreased Lactococcus and Unclassified_f_Peptostreptococcaceae in the intestinal contents of HS mice (Figure S19d). The relative abundances of Lysobacter, Allobaculum, Limnohabitans and Limnohabitans were significantly higher in the intestinal contents of HS mice after Arg_OIH pretreatment (Figure S19e). However, pretreatment with Arg_OIH significantly reduced the relative abundances of Oscillospira, Unclassified_f_Ruminococcaceae, Bilophila, Dehalobacterium, Unclassified_f_Lachnospiraceae, Unclassified_f_Clostridiaceae, Gemmiger, Unclassified_f_Desulfovibrionaceae and Candidatus Arthromitus in the intestinal contents of HS mice, as shown in Figure S19e. Notably, we also observed that the relative abundances of beneficial bacteria including Akkermansia and Bifidobacterium increased significantly in the intestinal contents of HS mice pretreated with Arg_OIH, but decreased after Arg_OS pretreatment (Fig. 10i). In addition, both Arg_OS and Arg_OIH pretreatments led to a significant increase in the abundance of Odoribacter and a reduction in Exiguobacterium (Fig. 10i). Additionally, Arg_OIH pretreatment significantly reduced the GNB/GPB ratio of the gut microbiota in HS mice, whereas this ratio also tended to decrease after Arg_OS pretreatment, although there was no statistical significance (Fig. 10j). This result suggests that Arg_OIH was more effective in reducing the proportion of GNB in HS mice. Therefore, we found that both Arg_OS and Arg_OIH significantly decreased LPS levels in the intestinal contents (Fig. 10k) and serum (Fig. 10l) of HS mice, but Arg_OIH exerted a more robust effect.

Subsequently, we found that pretreatment with either Arg_OS or Arg_OIH significantly decreased serum ALT (Fig. 10m) and AST (Fig. 10n) levels in HS mice, but no significant differences were obtained between the two interventions. Histopathological staining showed that both Arg_OS and Arg_OIH pretreatment notably reduced liver (Figure S20a and b) and cortex (Figure S20d and e) histology scores in HS mice, whereas Arg_OIH exhibited a relatively better preventive effect, particularly in the liver (Figure S20b). Accordingly, the relative p65 positive areas were significantly lower in the (Figure S20a and c) and cortex (Figure S20d and f) of HS mice pretreated with either Arg_OS or Arg_OIH, although no significant differences were obtained between the two treatments. Similar results were also observed for the expression levels of IL-6, TNF-α and IL-1β in the liver (Fig. 10o) and cortex (Fig. 10p) of HS mice after Arg_OS or Arg_OIH treatment. Herein we also examined the effects of arginine-free oral inulin hydrogel (OIH) on HS-associated inflammatory responses and liver injury (Figure S21a). The results show that OIH pretreatment slightly, but not significantly, reduced LPS levels in the intestinal contents (Figure S21b) and serum (Figure S21c) as well as serum ALT (Figure S21d) and AST (Figure S21e) levels in HS mice. In addition, the expression levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines, including IL-6, TNF-α and IL-1β, in the liver (Figure S21f) and cortex (Figure S21g) of HS mice also showed no significant changes after OIH pretreatment. Our findings revealed that both Arg_OS and Arg_OIH increased arginine levels in HS mice (Fig. 10q). Arginine not only promoted the ubiquitin-mediated degradation of MyD88, but also inhibited the interaction between MyD88 and TLR4, thereby suppressing p65 nuclear translocation and the expression of pro-inflammatory cytokines (Fig. 10q). Besides, Arg_OIH exhibited better efficacy in modulating the gut microbiota, particularly in reducing the proportion of GNB and the level of LPS, but its efficacy on alleviating HS-associated inflammatory responses was comparable to that of Arg_OS.

Discussion

HS causes multiple organ failure syndromes via a variety of complex pathogenesis, of which inflammatory responses are regarded as a key contributing factor [3]. In this study, we revealed that the gut microbiota were altered in HS mice as characterized by increased LPS levels and enhanced arginine catabolism, which further exacerbated HS-associated inflammatory responses. Exogenous arginine pretreatment effectively alleviated inflammatory responses in the liver and cortex of HS mice. Clinically, HS patients exhibited a lower arginine level, which was closely associated with liver injury and inflammatory indicators. Our results clarified that arginine had the ability to reduce MyD88 protein by activating its ubiquitin-mediated degradation and inhibit the MyD88-TLR4 interaction, and then decreased the nuclear translocation of p65 and the expression of proinflammatory genes. Lastly, a novel Arg_OIH method was developed to relieve HS-associated inflammatory responses exacerbated by the gut microbial alterations via maintaining the gut microbiota homeostasis to reduce LPS levels and by providing sustained arginine supplementation.

The gut microbiota has been widely reported to affect host health and disease through its metabolites, but most studies focused on chronic diseases including metabolic disorder [29], immune disease [30], diabetes [31], cardiovascular disease [32], and cancer [33]. In recent years, the role of gut microbial metabolism has also received increasing attention in acute diseases, such as stroke [34], sepsis [35], acute pancreatitis [36], acute liver injury [37], and acute lung injury [38]. HS is a severe emergency disease that has been reported to cause the gut microbial alterations and metabolic disorders [16, 19], but mechanistic studies are still lacking. Herein we found that the gut microbiota was markedly altered in HS mice and characterized by an increase in the ratio of Gram-negative bacteria, resulting in a higher level of LPS, a major component of their outer membrane [39]. It has been well known that LPS is a key proinflammatory mediator via TLR4 [40, 41]. Beyond heat cytotoxicity, our findings suggested that LPS-induced inflammation also represents a key pathophysiological mechanism of HS [3]. This pathological feature is similar to that of sepsis, and both conditions can lead to multiple organ failure in the patients; therefore, HS has been also regarded as a sepsis-like reaction to pyrexia [42]. Currently, it is still a clinical challenge to rapidly distinguish between HS and sepsis for achieving a precise treatment. In this study, we found that transplantation of the gut microbiota from HS mice exacerbated HS-associated inflammatory responses in normal recipient mice, suggesting that the gut microbial alterations might be an important contributor to the sepsis-like features observed in HS and represents a potential target for the prevention and treatment of HS.

Herein untargeted metabolomics identified another characteristic of HS mice, namely a significant reduction in systemic arginine levels, which was also confirmed in HS patients. Moreover, arginine pretreatment and clinical data analysis imply that arginine deficiency might be a risk factor for HS-associated inflammatory responses. Arginine metabolism has been reported to interact with immune system and regulate inflammation [43, 44]. The anti-inflammatory effects of arginine can be mediated by the NF-κB pathway [45], arginase-1 signaling [46], and nitric oxide synthesis [47]. In our study, we elucidated that arginine increased MyD88 ubiquitination and suppressed the MyD88-TLR4 interaction, thereby lowering the nuclear translocation of p65 and the expression levels of proinflammatory genes. Arginine belongs to a semi-essential amino acid due to its endogenous synthesis cannot fully meet the body's demands [43]. Through both in vivo and in vitro studies, we found that HS-induced reduction in systemic arginine levels was largely attributed to the enhancement of the gut microbial arginine catabolism, rather than impaired endogenous arginine metabolism. Nüse et al. reported that arginine serves as a pivotal interface between the gut microbiota and the host [48]. Moreover, the gut microbiota-dependent arginine metabolism has been shown to regulate bone mechanical adaptation [49]. An interesting study from Xu et al. reported that the gut microbiota can affect the progression of colorectal cancer by participating in intestinal arginine catabolism [50]. In emergency and severe cases, arginine has been utilized as an immune enhancer for sepsis [51], acute neurological disorders [52], and COVID-19 [53]. Also, the protective effect of arginine on HS-induced death [54] and cerebrovascular dysfunction [55] can be achieved by suppress inflammatory responses. Thus it can be seen that the gut microbial arginine metabolism has been reported to be implicated in the development of certain diseases, but relevant studies remain limited, and its association with the pathogenesis of HS has not yet been revealed. Our study not only elucidated that HS enhanced arginine catabolism of the gut microbiota, but also reported that pretreatment with arginine alleviated HS-associated inflammation in the liver and cortex by interaction with MyD88. In clinical practice, oral arginine solution is mainly used to improve blood circulation, enhance immunity, and promote wound healing, but it has not been used for the prevention of HS. Our results suggest that oral arginine solution might be utilized as a potential preventive method to mitigate HS-associated inflammatory responses exacerbated by the gut microbial alterations, although further clinical validation is still required.

HS is a severe and life-threatening disorder, but it is preventable [56]. For example, traditional Chinese medicine (TCM) has been recognized to hold great potential in the prevention of HS [57]. Besides, scientists have also explored other possible strategies to combat HS, such as nobiletin [58], catalpol [59], lactoferrin [60], rifaximin [61], probiotics [62], and type 1 ryanodine receptor inhibitor [63]. In this study, we further developed a new Arg_OIH for the mitigation of HS-associated inflammatory responses. Arg_OIH exhibited excellent injectability, enabling rapid oral delivery to the gastrointestinal tract. This hydrogel, using the prebiotic inulin as a carrier, is safe, reliable, simple to prepare, and amenable to large-scale production. Herein pretreatment with arginine via intraperitoneal injection attenuated HS-associated inflammation, but oral arginine delivery could be more convenient and more readily acceptable for its prevention. However, compared with the clinically used Arg_OS, Arg_OIH did not exhibit superior efficacy in alleviating HS-associated inflammatory responses. Of note, Arg_OIH had more favorable effects on improving the gut microbial imbalance, particularly by reducing the proportion of Gram-negative bacteria and the level of LPS while increasing the abundance of beneficial bacteria. In the future, Arg_OIH can be further optimized and modified to achieve better regulation of the gut microbiota and more efficient arginine delivery, thereby enhancing its preventive efficacy against HS.

In summary, herein we reported that HS increased arginine catabolism and LPS levels in the gut microbiota to aggravate MyD88-dependent inflammatory responses in mice. Moreover, a novel Arg_OIH strategy was established to mitigate HS-associated inflammation by reducing LPS levels and releasing arginine. Based on the findings of the present study, we proposed several limitations that need to be further investigated in the future: (1) The gut microbial characteristics of HS patients and the specific gut microbes responsible for aggravating HS-related inflammation were not fully elucidated. (2) Treatment with amino acid-controlled diet or arginine analogs can be considered to further verify the specific role of arginine. (3) The effects of HS-driven alterations in the gut microbiota and its metabolites on intestinal barrier function and inflammation deserve further exploration. (4) The clinical trials and assessments are recommended to be conducted on arginine for the prevention of HS, aiming to provide a potential solution to reduce HS-related organ injury and mortality.
