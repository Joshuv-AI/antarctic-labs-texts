Immune Clues to Sinus Blues: Linking Biomarkers to Radiologic Score of Rhinosinusitis
Authors: Lubna Khreesha, Amer Sawalha, Mohammad Darweesh, Qusai Elfadel, Mohammad Alzoubi, Saif Alhawadi, Faris Alsawair, Fares Habboub, Bashar Alsmady, Mohammad Aljarboa, Hashim Alhammouri
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundChronic rhinosinusitis (CRS) is a prolonged inflammatory condition of the nasal and paranasal sinuses that significantly affects quality of life. This study examines the relationship between CRS severity (based on Lund‐Mackay [LM] scores), osteitis, and systemic inflammatory markers, including neutrophil‐to‐lymphocyte ratio (NLR), platelet‐to‐lymphocyte ratio (PLR), monocyte‐to‐lymphocyte ratio (MLR), basophil‐to‐lymphocyte ratio (BLR), eosinophil‐to‐lymphocyte ratio (ELR), and the systemic immune inflammation index (SII).MethodsA retrospective analysis of 208 CRS patients (with or without nasal polyps) who underwent FESS at Jordan University Hospital was performed. Patients were stratified by LM scores into three groups from lowest to highest scores. Inflammatory markers were compared using non‐parametric statistical tests.ResultsELR showed the strongest correlation with radiological CRS severity (p < 0.0001) overall. BLR (p = 0.0043) and MLR (p = 0.0199) also showed significant differences, while NLR and PLR did not. ELR was significantly higher in group 3 with the highest Lund–MacKay score for CRS and posterior ethmoid and sphenoid sinus involvement. In patients without nasal polyposis, all markers except MLR showed significant differences with p < 0.05.ConclusionsELR is a promising, non‐invasive biomarker for assessing CRS severity, especially in patients without nasal polyps. It may help evaluate disease burden when imaging is unavailable, offering a cost‐effective tool in clinical practice and a valuable addition to the current management protocol for chronic rhinosinusitis treatment guidelines in low‐ and middle‐income countries.

Introduction

Chronic rhinosinusitis (CRS) is defined by persistent inflammation of the mucosa in the nose and paranasal sinuses lasting more than 12 weeks. This condition significantly impacts patients' quality of life, as it can interfere with daily activities and cause persistent discomfort [1]. CRS is highly prevalent, with a study in Saudi Arabia reporting a rate of 22.5%, reflecting its widespread burden in the region. CRS can be classified into two main phenotypes: CRS with nasal polyps (CRSwNP) and CRS without nasal polyps (CRSsNP) [2].

CRS in adults, whether with or without nasal polyps, is diagnosed when a patient experiences nasal congestion or discharge for over 12 weeks, accompanied by other symptoms such as facial pain, pressure, or reduced sense of smell. At least two of these symptoms must be present, with one of them being nasal congestion or discharge. Given its inflammatory nature, understanding the severity of CRS requires a closer look at the role inflammation plays in disease progression [3]. Objective evidence of inflammation (whether on imaging or nasal endoscopy) is recommended in addition to 12 weeks of symptoms.

The inflammation observed in CRS is heterogeneous, especially between CRSwNP and CRSsNP. Several serum immune‐based markers have been proposed to predict the prognosis of CRS, including the neutrophil‐to‐lymphocyte ratio (NLR), eosinophil‐to‐lymphocyte ratio (ELR), and platelet‐to‐lymphocyte ratio (PLR) [4, 5, 6, 7]. These biomarkers are useful for CRS and have been evaluated in other systemic inflammatory conditions [8, 9]. Understanding how inflammation impacts clinical features, disease progression, and treatment response can offer valuable insights into the overall severity of CRS [10, 11].

Both osteitis and sinus opacifications have been used as markers for disease severity [10]. Imaging techniques such as computed tomography (CT) are often used for this purpose. Lund–Mackay Score is a widely used method for quantifying radiological findings of CRS. It is easy to use, reproducible, and has high intra‐/interobserver agreement [12]. However, the CT comes with disadvantages, including high costs, radiation exposure, and the need for specialized equipment [11]. Thus, clinicians often look for alternative and more cost effective and safer methods for assessing disease severity and one example of such method is the inflammatory markers.

Despite the availability of various biomarkers, there is still no clear consensus on how best to evaluate the severity of CRS [10]. Therefore, the purpose of this research is to investigate how Lund–Mackay (LM) scores, the intensity of osteitis, various systemic inflammatory markers (such as NLR, PLR, BLR, ELR, and the systemic immune inflammation index [SII]) are related in individuals with chronic rhinosinusitis. Through this investigation, we aim to gain a deeper insight into the role of these markers in forecasting the disease's severity and the impact on the quality of life, without the abuse of scarce resources, since imaging may not always be available in healthcare systems in 3rd world countries.

Materials and Methods

Our study is a retrospective study conducted at the Jordan University Hospital. Approvals for this research were obtained from the University of Jordan Ethics Committee and the Jordan University Hospital IRB. Our research complies with the ethical guidelines of the Helsinki Declaration. The confidentiality of the patient is ensured during the research and anonymized before analysis. The study includes 208 patients, between the ages of 18 and 75 years, diagnosed with chronic rhinosinusitis (CRS), with and without nasal polyps, and subjected to functional endoscopic sinus surgery (FESS) in Jordan University Hospital obtained through random selection from the electronic medical records of previous surgeries done on over 3000 patients in the 10‐year period from 2015 to 2025. As per usual protocol for CRS patients, preoperative labs are done with CT scan. Inclusion criteria needed in‐hospital preoperative sinus CT and immunological markers data, that is, white blood cell differential counts for the reasons of obtaining levels of neutrophil/lymphocyte ratio (NLR), platelet/lymphocyte ratio (PLR), monocyte/lymphocyte ratio (MLR), basophil lymphocyte ratio (BLR) and eosinophil lymphocyte ratio (ELR). The exclusion criteria were patients with a history of immunodeficiency diseases, rheumatological diseases, autoimmune diseases, malignancies, and odontogenic sinusitis. Participants who had received systemic corticosteroids or other immunosuppressive therapy and biological agents in the 6 months prior to the preoperative imaging were excluded. Patients with severe comorbidities such as uncontrolled diabetes, uncontrolled hypertension, severe cardiovascular disease, such as acute coronary artery disease, active connective tissue disease, vasculitis, inflammatory bowel disease, chronic kidney failure, and chronic liver failure were omitted. Preoperative information, such as peripheral blood samples, was gathered to determine several inflammatory markers. The significant markers tested are the Neutrophil‐Lymphocyte Ratio (NLR), Platelet‐Lymphocyte Ratio (PLR), Eosinophil‐Lymphocyte Ratio (ELR), and basophil lymphocyte ratio (BLR). Paranasal sinus computerized tomography (PNSCT) scans were reviewed by one experienced otorhinolaryngologist, and were graded based on the Lund‐Mackay radiological staging system.

Patients were divided into three groups based on the summation of their Lund–Mackay score for all sinuses and the osteomeatal complex on both sides. Patients with a total Lund–Mackay score of 0–8 were assigned to group 1, those with a score of 9–16 were assigned to group 2, and those with a score of 17–24 were assigned to group 3. Statistical analysis was done using R Studio. Continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation and median (25th–75th percentile), and categorical variables were expressed as percentages. The Kruskal‐Wallis test was performed for distribution normality analyses, and nonparametric tests were preferred according to the results of this analysis. Dunn's test with Bonferroni correction was used for the pairwise comparisons between groups. The Wilcoxon rank‐sum test was used to compare categorical variables. p‐values less than 0.05 were accepted as significant.

In patients with polyps, no significant differences were observed among severity groups for NLR, PLR, BLR, MLR, or ELR (p > 0.05) (Table 1). However, when comparing grouped severity levels, PLR was significantly higher in the group 1 compared to the group 2 and 3 (p = 0.0240). No other markers showed significant differences (Table 5).

For patients without polyps, the Kruskal–Wallis test identified significant differences in NLR (p = 0.0336), PLR (p = 0.0023), and BLR (p = 0.0006), while MLR did not reach statistical significance (p = 0.1332). ELR also showed significant differences in non‐polyp patients (p < 0.0001), further reinforcing its potential role as a severity marker (Table 3). Post‐hoc analysis using Dunn's test with Bonferroni correction revealed that the group 1 had significantly higher NLR, PLR, and BLR than group 2 (p = 0.0389, p = 0.0038, and p = 0.0035, respectively). Additionally, group 2 had significantly higher BLR than group 3 (p = 0.0170). For ELR, significant differences were observed across all groups, with group 1 showing lower values compared to group 2 (p = 0.0008) and group 3 (p = 0.0001) (Table 4). No significant differences were observed when comparing combined groups in patients without polyps (Table 5).

Spearman's rank correlation was used to assess the relationship between inflammatory markers and sinus disease severity due to the non‐parametric nature of the data. ELR demonstrated the strongest correlations with total sinus scores, reinforcing its potential as a severity marker. Significant positive correlations were observed between ELR and Frontal Sinus Total (p = 0.3709, p < 0.0001), Maxillary Sinus Total (p = 0.2830, p < 0.0001), Anterior Ethmoid Total (p = 0.3283, p < 0.0001), Posterior Ethmoid Total (p = 0.4012, p < 0.0001), and Sphenoid Total (p = 0.3251, p < 0.0001). Notably, the strongest correlation was with the Posterior Ethmoid Total (ρ = 0.4012, p < 0.0001), suggesting ELR is particularly relevant in more inflamed regions of the sinuses. All other correlations were not statistically significant (p > 0.05).

Overall, ELR was the most consistent inflammatory marker across all analyses. It showed the strongest statistical significance in Kruskal‐Wallis and post‐hoc tests, particularly in patients without polyps. Additionally, ELR was the only marker to show strong and consistent correlations with total sinus scores, especially in the posterior ethmoid and sphenoid sinuses. Unlike NLR, PLR, BLR, and MLR, which showed weaker or mixed correlations, ELR remained significant in multiple tests and across different severity groups. The only exception to ELR's significance was in patients with polyps, where no apparent differences were observed. These findings suggest that ELR may be a reliable biomarker for assessing sinus disease severity, particularly in non‐polyp patients.

Discussion

Chronic rhinosinusitis (CRS) is a persistent inflammatory condition of the nasal and paranasal sinuses lasting more than 12 weeks, markedly impairing patients' quality of life. Various inflammatory markers have been investigated for their role in disease severity and recurrence, including elevated levels of IL‐5, IL‐6, IL‐17A, and interferon gamma in serum and nasal tissues [13]. Mucosal eosinophilia has also been strongly linked to disease severity, particularly in CRS without nasal polyps [14]. However, no single marker has gained universal acceptance, highlighting the ongoing need for further investigation.

Inflammation is central to CRS pathogenesis [15], triggering the release of Th1‐ and Th2‐type cytokines. This leads to granulocyte/macrophage colony‐stimulating factor production, IL‐5, IL‐8, RANTES, and eotaxin, promoting eosinophil and neutrophil infiltration. Th2 cytokines dominate in CRS with nasal polyps (CRSwNP), while Th1 cytokines are more prevalent in CRS without nasal polyps (CRSsNP), though both can coexist in varying degrees [16, 17].

Markers such as the Monocyte‐to‐Lymphocyte Ratio (MLR), Basophil‐to‐Lymphocyte Ratio (BLR), Platelet‐to‐Lymphocyte Ratio (PLR), and Neutrophil‐to‐Lymphocyte Ratio (NLR) are increasingly being studied for their potential use in various inflammatory and systemic diseases. Recently, these inflammatory markers have been shown to have a prognostic role in various inflammatory and systemic diseases. These hematological ratios have demonstrated predictive utility in related chronic airway inflammatory conditions. In asthma, ELR, NLR, PLR, MLR, and SII have been evaluated as markers of disease severity and systemic inflammation [18]. Notably, ELR has also been shown to predict treatment response to omalizumab in patients with severe allergic asthma [19], while in acute exacerbations of COPD, PLR, NLR, MLR, and ELR have all been shown to serve as useful prognostic biomarkers, correlating with airflow limitation, hospital length of stay, and in‐hospital mortality [20]. The most widely available serum biomarker for chronic rhinosinusitis (CRS) is eosinophil count. Its main function is mediating allergic disease, parasitic and helminthic infections, as well as participate in type 2 (TH2) inflammation that involves cytokines such as IL‐4, IL‐5, and IL‐13. Other potential biomarkers include major basic protein (MBP), eosinophil cationic protein (ECP), eosinophil peroxidase (EPX), and eosinophil‐derived neurotoxin (EDN) [21]. Although these markers have been well studied regarding nasal polyp subtypes, chronic rhinosinusitis (CRS) recurrence, and allergic rhinitis, fewer studies have investigated their association with CRS severity especially with the non‐polyposis type.

Our study is among the few that have explored the role of systemic inflammatory markers in CRS severity. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to comprehensively compare ELR, BLR, MLR, NLR, and PLR levels between CRS patients with and without nasal polyps.

Among all markers, ELR demonstrated the strongest statistical significance, consistently differentiating between all three groups of CRS cases in the overall patient population. Furthermore, ELR was the only marker strongly correlated with total sinus scores, particularly in the posterior ethmoid and sphenoid sinuses. These findings suggest that ELR may be a reliable biomarker for assessing sinus disease severity, particularly in non‐polyp patients, where it remained significant across multiple analyses.

However, ELR was not significant in patients with polyps, which contradicts the findings of Kara et al. [22], who found that ELR was significantly associated with radiological disease severity in CRS patients with nasal polyps (p = 0.012). This can be due to the small sample size of patients with polyps, which may have limited the statistical power needed to detect a significant association. Another factor explaining this discrepancy is the distinct pathophysiology of polyp‐associated CRS, which may be driven by localized mucosal inflammation rather than systemic inflammatory responses.

Consequently, systemic markers such as ELR, BLR, and MLR may not adequately reflect the inflammatory burden in polyp patients. Patients classified in the polyp group were identified postoperatively so that they might have had isolated polyps without concurrent severe mucosal inflammation. As a result, their inflammation may be predominantly local rather than systemic, which could explain the absence of any significant finding of any of the studied markers in the polyp group.

A similar pattern was observed with BLR, which showed significant differences across severity groups in the overall population and patients without polyps, but was not substantial in polyp patients. This partially contradicts the findings of Brescia et al. [23], who reported a significant association between BLR and nasal polyp recurrence. This may also be explained by the small sample size and the localized nature of inflammation in these patients, which systemic inflammatory markers may not adequately capture.

NLR and PLR did not show a statistically significant association with disease severity in the overall CRS population, a finding consistent with Zagólski et al. [24] However, we found they were both significantly associated with disease severity in patients without polyps. This suggests these markers may not be universally applicable in CRS, but they could still have potential diagnostic value in specific subgroups, particularly non‐polyp patients.

Although our study included a large sample size of 208 patients, one limitation to acknowledge is that patients with polyps were underrepresented compared to patients without polyps. In addition, group 3 also included fewer patients than group 1 and 2. The exclusion of patients taking systemic corticosteroids within the 6 months prior to surgery likely eliminates many patients with severe CRS and this could be a limitation in sample selection. Another limitation of this study could be the absence of disease severity evaluation from a quality‐of‐life standpoint which happened due to the fact that many participants lost follow up after 2 or 3 postoperative clinic visits and inability to reach them via phone. These factors could have influenced the statistical outcomes.

Furthermore, using a single experienced researcher to perform the Lund–Mackay scoring ensured consistency across evaluations, although this uniformity may also introduce some observer bias.

Additionally, because our study population included patients who underwent sinus surgery and are relatively considered to be in a clinically advanced stage of CRS, this narrowed the scope of CRS patients as a whole by not including those who are still in the mild stages of their disease.

The retrospective nature of data collection and using post‐op notes to categorize patients into polyp and non‐polyp groups may have caused the underrepresentation of patients with polyps. Future studies should seek to confirm these results in larger, prospectively designed cohorts while further exploring the role of these markers in routine clinical practice.

Conclusion

In this study, our findings suggest that inflammatory markers, particularly ELR and BLR, offer promising potential for assessing CRS severity and guiding treatment decisions. The significant differences observed across all three groups indicate that these markers could help stratify patients and predict disease progression, thereby supporting more tailored interventions such as earlier surgical management or adjusted medical therapy.

Our study contributes valuable insights into the utility of readily available inflammatory markers for CRS, underscoring their potential to enhance clinical decision‐making and patient care.
