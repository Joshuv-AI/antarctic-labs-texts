The safety and feasibility of laparoscopic partial splenectomy: analysis of perioperative indications from different vascular subtypes and improvement of surgical approach
Authors: Yixiao Li, Yugui Tian, Lingwei Meng, He Cai, Xin Wang, Yunqiang Cai, Yongbing Li, Jin Zhou, Bing Peng, Zhong Wu
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
IntroductionWhile laparoscopic splenectomy (LS) has been widely used in benign splenic tumor, more concerns have been raised for postoperatively short-term and long-term complications. Laparoscopic partial splenectomy (LPS) is a surgical option, to preserve splenic function, and reduce postoperative complications. The aim of our study was to retrospectively identify the safety and feasibility of LPS compared with LS in patients with splenic benign tumor.Materials and methodsFrom 2014 to 2024, a total of 165 patients diagnosed with occupational splenic lesions underwent splenectomy, of whom 87 underwent LPS and 78 underwent LS. We compare the perioperative parameters and long term follow up between these two groups.ResultsThe etiology of splenic space-occupying lesions was nonparasitic splenic cysts, followed by splenic lymphangioma and splenic hemangioma. Of the patients with LPS, 54 underwent conventional surgery with blockage of the splenic arterial branch and resection along the ischemic line (RAIL), and 33 underwent with our modified total splenic blood supply blockade followed by resection alone the tumor edge (RATE). The tumor size, the operative time and estimated blood loss were comparable between the LPS and LS groups. One patient developed abnormal signs during the LPS procedure and was promptly referred for LS. The LPS group had fewer pancreatic leakage, incision infection, and pulmonary infection. As for different vascular types, patients with LS under all branches of the splenic artery had a longer time to resume postoperative feeding. As for the comparison of RAIL and RATE, estimated blood and operative time were significantly reduced in patients receiving RATE. Postoperative complications were the same in patients underwent each surgical procedures.ConclusionLPS is a viable approach for patients with splenic benign tumor. We introduce the tumor artery supply types to indicate the resection region. Our RATE technique has proven to be clinically effective and safety.

Since the first report of laparoscopic splenectomy (LS) was published in 1992 [1], it has become a common procedure worldwide for spleen removal in the treatment of splenic trauma, splenic tumors, and hematological disorders [2, 3]. However, the potential for serious complications is gaining attention in the surgical community, including 3.2% rate of severe infections [4–6], overwhelming post-splenectomy infection (OPSI) with and 0.2–1.4% mortality [6], pulmonary hypertension, portal/splenic vein thromboembolic events [4, 5], as well as splenic hypoplasia compromising the body's immune system and anti-infective function over the life of the patient [6].

To preserve the immune function of the spleen after splenectomy, especially the spleen-dependent immune response [7], laparoscopic partial splenectomy (LPS) is increasingly utilized. From early 2000's, it has widely spread and extended its indications [8, 9], including various conditions for oncological or hemostatic reasons when splenectomy was nonmandatory, such as hematological disease [10], portal hypertension [11], and focal lesions [12, 13]. Compared with LS, which has been widely used in clinical practice, LPS requires more skillful operation technique than LS. Till now there is no evidence to support wide clinical applications of LPS.

Over the past decade [14–16], we initially used LPS for splenic traumatic splenic rupture and compared the perioperative metrics of LPS and LS for emergency surgery in a sample of 22 cases. We have gradually expanded the indications for LPS to include benign splenic tumors, hematological disorders, and lymphatic disorders, which have initially improved the surgical technique for LPS. However, very few studies explored the influence of the branches of splenic artery, which could take an important role in the surgical tactic to be used in splenectomy procedures as well as post-operative recovery [17]. Those limitations above motivated this study, with the objective of further compare and validate the safety and feasibility of laparoscopic partial splenectomy. In addition, we defined a new type of splenic artery supply types for the indication of resection regions. Finally, we described a novel surgical approach of total splenic blood supply blockade followed by resection alone the tumor edge (RATE). We confirm RATE technique is more effective in reducing intraoperative bleeding.

Materials and methods

Patients

165 patients from August 2014 to January 2024, in West China Hospital of Sichuan University and West China Hospital of Sichuan University (Shangjin Campus), who were diagnosed with only benign splenic tumor (splenic cyst, splenic hemangioma and splenic lymphangioma) were collected and analyzed. 87 of the patients underwent laparoscopic partial splenectomy. Of the patients with LPS, 55 underwent conventional surgery with blockage of the splenic arterial branch and resection along the ischemic line (RAIL), and 32 underwent with our modified total splenic blood supply blockade and resection alone the tumor edge (RATE). We mainly used CT scans to observe the specific imaging features of splenic tumors (round-like hypodense shadows) and the arterial vascular supply to evaluate the benign or malignant nature of splenic tumors and decide the site of resection of the lesion. The CT manifestation of benign tumors mainly shows a low-density cystic lesion with a clear border at the upper/middle/lower pole of the spleen, no enhancement, and some of them complained low-grade fever, anemia, malaise, lethargy, and occasional abdominal discomfort; whereas malignant tumors are mostly lymphomas or metastatic tumors, which have an unclear border on the CT manifestation.

Perioperative data were respectively collected and analyzed, including patient demographics, spleen size, lesion size, operative time, estimated blood loss, postoperative hospitalization days, oral intake days, blood routine test, and vascular typing of the spleen artery (SA) from preoperative CT scans, which were all from inpatient or outpatient medical records. Follow up were performed regularly after surgery. Patients with LS are not routinely vaccinated preoperatively. All patients gave written consent before surgery. This study was approved by the Review Board of West China Hospital, Sichuan University. Written informed consent was obtained from all patients before the operation.

Inclusion criteria

Based on previous experience, the inclusion criteria for patients with splenic benign tumors undergoing LPS were generally as bellow:Patients with benign splenic space-occupying lesions diagnosed by ultrasonography or enhanced computed tomography. Surgical procedure

Under general anesthesia, the patient was positioned in a right semi-decubitus posture with the left side of their body elevated for between 20 and 30 degrees. Laparoscopy was performed under CO2 pneumoperitoneum at an insufflation pressure of 12–13 mmHg. Four ports were inserted into abdominal cavity: A 10 mm port was placed in the upper umbilicus, which was for the 30° rigid camera; A 12 mm port was placed below the lower edge of the costa in the left midclavicular line, which was for laparoscopic stapler and dissector; A 5 mm port for laparoscopic grasper was placed below the xiphoid; A 5 mm port for laparoscopic grasper or aspiration was placed at the left axillary line, below the lower pole of the spleen.

For LPS, the peritoneal cavity was carefully inspected to exclude the presence of malignant tumors. The left side of the greater omentum was incised along the greater curvature, extending to the short gastric vessels, using Ultrasonic shears. Subsequently, the lesser sac was accessed, and the peri-splenic attachments slated for resection were meticulously mobilized in the vicinity of the spleen (Fig. 1). For the resection of lower part of spleen, the upper branches of the gastroepiploic vessels and the upper portion of the peri-splenic ligament were preserved and vice versa. For RAIL partial splenic resection, the branches of the splenic artery and vein which supply the resecting part of spleen were mobilized carefully, then these branches were clipped by Hem-o-lok ligation clamps and applicator forceps which resulted in a well-defined demarcation. The safe demarcation margin is 1.5 cm. Ultrasonic shears was used to transect the splenic parenchyma 1 cm inner the demarcation line. Then hemostasis was achieved using Bipolar, the remnant spleen was not fixed or sutured as a routine. The specimen was placed in a retrieval bag and removed through the 12 mm port incision. Subsequently, the surgical site was thoroughly inspected and an abdominal drainage was placed adjacent to the remnant spleen (Fig. 2). For our new LPS procedure (RATE), it differs from the past surgical procedures in that we free the spleen and block the short gastric vessels and other blood vessels before removing the tumor, leaving only the blood vessels in the splenic pedicle, and then carefully dissect the splenic artery and the splenic vein and free and block them, to block the blood supply to the whole spleen, in order to avoid massive bleeding during the removal of the tumor. We will resect the tumor directly along the edge of the tumor rather than along the 1 cm distance demarcation line. After the resection, we will perform hemostasis before releasing the splenic hilar vascular block. For LS, the spleen was carefully mobilized utilizing ultrasonic shears. Subsequently, the gastroepiploic vessels were meticulously transected using a laparoscopic LigaSure device. The splenic artery was dissected and secured with Hem-O-Lok clips. A laparoscopic linear stapler, equipped with a 45 mm white cartridge, was employed to divide the splenic pedicle. The excised specimen was then placed into a retrieval bag and extracted through a 12 mm port incision. The integrity of the surgical site was meticulously assessed, and an abdominal drain was positioned adjacent to the remaining tissue. For LS and LPS, the criteria for managing drains postoperatively are that they can be removed if there is no pancreatic leakage, no bleeding, generally less than 50 ml of drainage, and no obvious abdominal symptoms.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed with SPSS version 16.0 (SPSS Inc, Chicago, Ill). P values < 0.05 were considered significant. Independent samples t-test was used for quantitative descriptive variables and Chi-square test or Fisher exact text for was used for categorical variables.

Post-operative outcomes

As shown in Table 3, the length of hospital stay was similar between LS and LPS groups (P > 0.05). There was no mortality during hospitalization. Although the mean count of hemoglobin for both groups stayed the same after the operation (LPS: 117.0 ± 16.7 × 109/L vs LS: 113.0 ± 16.8 × 109/L, P = 0.147), patients received LPS group had shorter postoperative days available to eat (LPS: 1.7 ± 1.1 days vs LS: 2.6 ± 1.2 days, P = 0.000), less leukocyte counts (LPS: 9.4 ± 2.7 × 109/L vs LS: 13.1 ± 5.8 × 109/L, P = 0.000), and less platelet counts (LPS: 200.9 ± 84.4 × 109/L vs LS: 255.0 ± 119.2 × 109/L, P = 0.045). The patients in LS group had a higher level of both ALT (LPS: 16.2 ± 13.5 U/L vs LS: 29.0 ± 39.6 U/L, P = 0.008) and AST (LPS: 19.0 ± 10.6 U/L vs LS: 31.4 ± 31.4 U/L, P = 0.001). Between the LPS and LS groups, 20 and 22 patients developed pleural effusion, respectively, and the difference was of no statistical significance (P > 0.05). However, patients diagnosed with pancreatic leakage (LPS: n = 6 vs LS: n = 13, P = 0.050), incision infection (LPS: n = 0 vs LS: n = 5, P = 0.017) and pulmonary infection (LPS: n = 0 vs LS: n = 12, P = 0.000) occupied a greater proportion in group LS. No patients with splenic abscess or post-operative bleeding were found with two groups. The differencr was of no statistical significance in the severity of postoperative complications between the two groups when graded using the Clavien-Dindo classification (LPS: Grade I, n = 21; Grade II, n = 3 vs. LS: Grade I, n = 26; Grade II, n = 4; P = 0.928). Additionally, antibiotic therapy and nutritional support were administered to three patients in LPS group and one patient in LS group who developed pancreatic leakage. Splenic vascular typing and its effect on perioperative indicators of LPS

Here, we introduce a novel classification of splenic tumor based on the location of the tumor and the splenic artery territory anatomy. Splenic tumors can be located in the upper, middle, or lower poles in the physical sense, but tumors in different poles do not necessarily arise from the branching supply of the splenic artery in the corresponding pole. Therefore, we used preoperative CT scans to determine the branching status of the splenic artery and the location of the tumor, and defined the location of the tumor according to the main source of vascular supply (the pole of the branch with the most blood supply). For example, if the tumor is in the upper pole in the physical sense, but its main blood supply comes from the middle pole branch, we define it as middle pole (“M”). Follow-up data

All patients were followed up with upper abdominal imaging in the outpatient clinic after surgery except three who were not followed up. The median follow-up time was 3 months in the LPS group and 0.5 months in the LS group. Our telephone follow-ups were all after more than 6 months. Only 4 patients in the LS group were readmitted for chemotherapy and targeted therapy for non-splenic metastatic benign tumors after 6 months. One patient in the LS group passed away due to myocardial infarction 2 years after the surgery, other than that all patients reported symptomatic relief, and no recurrence or metastasis of the disease was detected on imaging.

Discussion

Traditionally, the strategy of splenic surgery was to remove the organ entirely. However, over the last decades, organ preservation measures have become a hot topic. Specifically, synthesis of anti-polysaccharide antibodies by splenic B-lymphocytes and phagocytosis by splenic lymphocytes play an important role in defence against infection [4, 5, 7]. In spleenless patients, the missing splenic immune function may cause OPSI, and thrombocytosis may affect the development of postoperative pulmonary hypertension [19], which in turn may lead to lung infection and pleural effusion. Due to the above reasons, the protection of the spleen's immune functions and the potential threat of postoperative complications have led to an increasing shift in surgical strategy towards partial splenectomy [11]. Although open partial splenectomy is safe and feasible in patients with concomitant different splenic disorders (e.g., splenic occupancy and hereditary spherocytosis) [20], severe postoperative pain and cosmetic problems are still unavoidable, thus, laparoscopic splenectomy is gradually expanding its reach [21].

In the current study, we performed a retrospective analysis and included only patients with benign tumors of the spleen for both LS and LPS group, for the purpose of baseline comparability. Our data showed that there were no significant differences between the groups in terms of the operation time, intraoperative blood loss, and intraoperative infusion. However, we did observe a significant decrease in the incidence of postoperative complications in LPS group. The incidence of pleural fluid was equal in both groups, whereas for pancreatic fistula, invasive infection, pulmonary infection and splenic abscess, patients with total splenectomy had a higher incidence relative to those with partial splenectomy. However, we can still consider LPS and LS to be safe in terms of bleeding risk, as none of the patients experienced postoperative hemorrhage. Therefore, LPS is less traumatic intraoperatively and has fewer complications relative to the LS group (although some of the outcomes are statistically insignificant), resulting in earlier postoperative bed mobility as well as earlier discharge from the hospital.

The splenic artery originates from the celiac trunk and travels in the retroperitoneum along the superior and posterior margins of the body and tail of the pancreas and reaches the splenorenal ligament near the tail of the pancreas to the splenic hilum after a tortuous and zigzag course [22]. There, the splenic artery divides into several branches, each terminal branch giving off small arteries that penetrate the splenic hilum. The conventional classification of the splenic artery divides it into upper and lower poles, and the branches that migrate to the upper and lower segments of the spleen are polar [23]. The implication of the polar splenic artery is clinically considerable. First, to avoid failure of splenic preservation surgery, the polar splenic artery must be properly protected when it alone supplies the upper or lower part of the spleen. Second, the polar artery tends to rupture and cause bleeding when the vascular system near the splenic hilum is ligated. Third, during splenectomy, the surgeon may overlook the presence of the polar artery and accidentally injure it, causing severe bleeding [24]. To better understand the clinical impact, we divided the splenic artery into upper, middle and lower poles or upper and lower poles, depending on the number of vascular branches outside the splenic hilum shown in CT scans. We also marked the tumor blood supply under CT scanning and noted the branch poles with the highest number of vascular sources as the main source of blood supply.

Based on our experiences, the difficulty of surgery increases when the tumor is located in the mid-pole position of the spleen, due to the tumor located closer to the main trunk of the splenic artery, and get blood supply from various branches, making the dissection of vascular more complicated during surgery. This may result in prolonged operative time, increased intraoperative hemorrhage, and prolonged postoperative recovery time. Our study proposes to statistically confirm this empirical clinical finding. It should be noted that we have only compared the relevant metrics with different vascular supplies within LPS, because for LS, different vascular supplies are not very meaningful in terms of their impact on surgery, and comparison of LPS and LS metrics at different levels of vascular supply was done to further validate the feasibility of LPS. Our results showed that, tumors located at the lower and middle poles showed significant higher volume of intraoperative bleeding, intraoperative transfusion, and postoperative hospital stay days relative to tumors at the upper and middle poles, but this difference was not statistically significant due to small sample sizes and insufficient sample normality. Tumors with blood supply sources at the lower and middle poles had the same pattern, with the difference being that the difference in their postoperative hospital stay was statistically significant (P = 0.029). We also performed a side-by-side comparison between the LPS and LS groups and found that, regardless of the pole from which the tumor's blood supply originated, patients in the LPS group tended to have a better trend in the indicators of surgical difficulty and postoperative recovery, which is consistent with what we found in our overall comparison. This was mainly reflected in the postoperative resumption of feeding time (I major: P = 0.001; M major: P = 0.003; S major: P = 0.042). It is worth noting that, with the exception of blood supply originating from the middle pole, intraoperative infusion volumes were significantly different between the LPS and LS groups (I major: P = 0.039; S major: P = 0.011), suggesting that the differences in intraoperative infusion volumes in the overall analyses were not related to the source of blood supply.

Since the first partial splenectomy performed by Franciscus Rosetti in 1590, the identification of splenic artery anomalies has become increasingly important for surgical management. The blood supply to the spleen is segmental with little collateral circulation between segments [25]. Difficulties in controlling splenic-induced hemorrhage are mainly related to the specific vascular anatomy. The splenic artery branches in groups of 2 to 3, with the number of segments varying from 3 to 7 [19]. Thus, intraoperative ligation of the ends of the splenic vessels can result in a distinct ischemic demarcation zone on the surface of the spleen which makes it possible to perform splenic parenchyma resection with less blood loss. In addition, advances in surgical techniques, such as splenic suturing with absorbable mesh, fibrin glue hemostasis, fibrin oxide or argon beam coagulation, and anastomotic techniques, have also made partial splenectomy safe and feasible [26].

There are two mainstream surgical approaches for LPS: routine LPS and selective splenic pedicle occlusion (SSPO). Routine LPS is used in patients with simple splenic hilum vascular anatomy, where the surgeon dissects the branches of the splenic hilum without blocking the splenic artery. SSPO pre-blocks the splenic artery before dissection and is used in patients with complicated splenic hilum anatomy, difficult spleen preservation, traumatic spleen rupture and high risk of bleeding during surgery. In our previous study [16], 51 patients underwent LPS of the branches of splenic artery ligation followed by RAIL. To further control intraoperative bleeding and reduce postoperative complications, we improved our modified total splenic blood supply blockade and RATE. Patients who underwent total splenic blood supply occlusion in our study did have optimization in terms of estimated blood loss and operative time. However, there was no significant reduction in estimated blood loss compared to our previous study (75.15 ± 56.80 ml vs 71.13 ± 53.87 ml). This may be related to the sample size [27].

There are some limitations to this study (1) It was a retrospective study, and we only included benign tumors. This may cause some selection bias, but in most cases LPS is applied to patients with splenic benign tumors, and splenic trauma occasionally, according to our clinical cases. (2) The sample size was relatively small; and (3) Further exploration of the reasons for the correlations of clinical indicators is lacking. However, we increase our sample sizes in general, and also identify the importance of vascular types in clinical application. We also proposed a novel surgical technique for partial splenectomy, which further reduced the intraoperative blood loss and improve the outcomes. Further prospective studies are needed.

In conclusion, this study compares the perioperative outcomes of patients with splenic benign tumors treated with LPS and LS. LPS facilitates postoperative recovery in patients with splenic benign tumors and does not increase perioperative risk. In addition, the location and blood supply of the tumor have an impact on perioperative indications, and the RATE is effective in reducing intraoperative bleeding. Prospective trials with clearer criteria are needed to demonstrate the intrinsic link between the benefits of LPS and surgical indications.
