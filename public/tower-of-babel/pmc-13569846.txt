Development and psychometric validation of SADAR-PrEP, an Indonesian PrEP knowledge and attitudes instrument
Authors: Bustanul Arifin, Ayu Vinegia, Usmar Usmar, Sylmina Dalily Alkaff, Sri Awalia Febriana, Alessandro Alfieri, Saidah Rauf, Zulkarnain Zulkarnain, Dyah Aryani Perwitasari, Fredrick Dermawan Purba, Akhmad Ardiansyah, Firas Farisi Alkaff, Sovia Salamah, Susi Ari Kristina, Latifah Mahaya Sarifah, Irnawati Hi Amin, Fajar Wahyu Pribadi, Albert Wertheimer, Maarten J Postma, Muhammad Syahrir, M Rifqi Rokhman
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe use of pre-exposure prophylaxis (PrEP) in Indonesia remains limited despite its proven effectiveness in HIV prevention, partly due to low public awareness and knowledge and the absence of validated measurement instruments for the general population. This study developed and psychometrically validated the SADAR-PrEP (Skala pengetAhuan DAn Respon terhadap PrEP) instrument through (i) forward-backward translation and cultural adaptation; (ii) content validity through multidisciplinary expert panel review (n = 25); (iii) face validity through structured cognitive interviews across regional groups; (iv) construct validity using exploratory factor analysis (EFA); (v) internal consistency reliability; and (vi) known-groups validity with effect sizes. A total of 1,150 adults from six regions in Indonesia completed the instrument. EFA revealed a unidimensional structure (KMO = 0.928; single factor eigenvalue = 4.959, explaining 49.6% of the variance; factor loadings 0.618–0.766). Internal consistency was excellent (Cronbach’s α = 0.874; corrected item-total correlations 0.538–0.676). Known-groups comparisons confirmed significant differences in scores across education, health background, and workshop attendance groups (p ≤ 0.003; Cohen’s d = 0.24–0.63). Although 74.3% expressed willingness to use PrEP, only 16.1% knew how to access services. SADAR-PrEP is the first validated PrEP knowledge and attitude instrument for the general Indonesian population, providing a scalable tool for evidence-based HIV prevention education, programme planning, and policy development.Keywords: Indonesia, Pre-Exposure Prophylaxis, SADAR-PrEP, Instrument validation, HIV prevention, Knowledge and attitudesSubject terms: Diseases, Health care, Medical research

Introduction

HIV/AIDS remains one of the most pressing global health challenges, having claimed more than 42.3 million lives to date, with no curative therapy available. In the Asia-Pacific region, strengthening prevention strategies including pre-exposure prophylaxis (PrEP), requires not only expanding service availability but also establishing reliable population-level measurement of PrEP knowledge and attitudes to guide evidence-based education and policy1,2.

Indonesia is currently facing a complex HIV/AIDS epidemic. Although there has been a decline in the incidence of new HIV infections from 50,000 in 2010 to 24,000 in 2022, the prevalence of individuals living with HIV has increased from 440,000 to 540,000 individuals. This increase was accompanied by an increase in AIDS-related mortality from 16,000 to 26,000 during the same period2. A significant recent development was the introduction of pre-exposure prophylaxis (PrEP) in late 2021. Initially launched in seven provinces, this initiative was expanded to ten provinces by 20233.

The rapid expansion of PrEP across Indonesia’s geographically and demographically diverse archipelago necessitates urgent enhancement of measurement capacity. Systematic quantification of community level PrEP knowledge is crucial for identifying gaps that hinder adoption, designing appropriately targeted educational interventions, and generating evidence for program refinement as the national rollout progresses4. In the absence of reliable measurement instruments, program planners and policymakers lack the empirical foundation required to allocate resources efficiently or to assess whether expanded PrEP access results in meaningful improvements in public understanding and acceptance.

Assessing knowledge of PrEP in Indonesia is complicated by two interrelated challenges. First, rigorously validated PrEP assessment tools are limited globally and have been developed primarily for men who have sex with men in the United States5. Efforts to adapt these tools cross-culturally have consistently shown poor psychometric performance when applied outside their original contexts6. Second, Indonesia’s socioreligious context adds a layer of complexity to measurement that existing instruments fail to adequately address. With approximately 88% of the population adhering to Islam7, cultural and religious norms surrounding sexual behavior, same-sex relationships, and reproductive health discourse significantly influence community perceptions and responses to PrEP-related information, differing markedly from secular Western populations. A portion of the population views PrEP as endorsing sexual behavior that conflicts with religious values, presenting a culturally specific barrier that current instruments cannot capture. Consequently, without an instrument that is both psychometrically sound and contextually relevant, researchers and policymakers risk conflating genuine knowledge gaps with measurement artifacts, leading to inaccurate estimates of program needs and misguided interventions4.

Indonesia’s ongoing PrEP scale-up is constrained by the absence of a standardized, culturally adapted instrument to quantify PrEP knowledge and related attitudes in the general population, limiting the translation of socioreligious norms, stigma, and geographic inequities into measurable needs for targeted education and evidence-informed policy. Although PrEP knowledge research is growing globally, general population evidence from Muslim-majority Southeast Asian contexts remains limited despite the Asia-Pacific HIV burden. To address this gap, we developed and validated the SADAR-PrEP through (i) translation and cultural adaptation following established cross-cultural adaptation guidelines; (ii) content validity through a multidisciplinary expert panel review; (iii) face validity through structured cognitive interviews with members of the target population across six regional groups; (iv) construct validity using EFA; (v) internal consistency reliability including Cronbach’s alpha, corrected item-total correlations, and alpha-if-item-deleted statistics; and (vi) known-groups validity by comparing scores between groups hypothesised to differ in PrEP knowledge, with effect sizes reported alongside inferential statistics. The validated SADAR-PrEP instrument is intended to strengthen population-level monitoring of PrEP awareness and support evidence-informed programme design and policy decisions for PrEP implementation across Indonesia.

Methods

Research design

This study used a cross-sectional design. Data were collected using an online self-administered survey administered via Google Forms and disseminated through multiple recruitment channels, including WhatsApp, Instagram, and Facebook. Data collection was conducted between August and October 2024. The Research Ethics Committee of the Faculty of Pharmacy, Hasanuddin University (Makassar, South Sulawesi, Indonesia) approved the study (No. 359/UN4.17/KEP/2024, August 5, 2024).

Participants

Eligible participants were Indonesian residents aged ≥ 17 years who lived in Indonesia. For consistency with the residence categories used in the instrument, respondents were classified into six regional groups: Sumatera, Kalimantan, Sulawesi, Bali and Nusa Tenggara, Maluku and Papua, and Jawa. Participants were recruited using purposive sampling. Prior to completing the instrument, participants received brief study information and provided online informed consent by selecting a checkbox on the first page. The minimum sample size followed a commonly applied psychometric guideline of 5–10 participants per item4. As the instrument comprised 26 items, the minimum required sample size was 260 participants.

Instrument and scoring

We used an instrument originally developed by Nakathingo (University of Namibia [UNAM], Rundu, Namibia) to assess awareness, knowledge, and willingness to use HIV pre-exposure prophylaxis (PrEP)8. Permission to use and adapt the instrument was obtained via email from Dr Daniel Ashipala (Head of Department, Nursing Sciences, Rundu Campus, University of Namibia) on September 29, 2023. The original instrument comprised three domains: (i) awareness of HIV PrEP (four items), (ii) knowledge of HIV PrEP (six items), and (iii) willingness to use PrEP (eight items)8. During cultural adaptation for Indonesia, the knowledge domain was expanded by adding four contextually relevant items (items 7–10) based on the Indonesian Ministry of Health’s PrEP guidelines, resulting in a 10-item PrEP knowledge scale9. The Indonesian version also included demographic items and supplementary items on barriers and programme-related issues, yielding a total of 26 items with mixed response formats.

The instrument included single-choice categorical items, multiple-response (“select all that apply”) items, and short-answer items. Awareness items were recorded as dichotomous or three-category responses (yes/no/unsure), and sources of information were captured using a multiple-response format. PrEP knowledge was assessed using 10 items with three response options (true/false/don’t know or yes/no/don’t know). A total PrEP knowledge score was computed by summing correct responses across the 10 knowledge items (scored 1 for correct and 0 for incorrect or “don’t know”), yielding a possible score range of 0–10, where higher scores indicate greater PrEP knowledge. Likert-type items (5-point scales) were analysed as individual ordinal measures; where applicable, negatively worded items were reverse-coded so that higher scores consistently reflected more favourable attitudes toward PrEP. All other items related to willingness, barriers, and PrEP use were analysed descriptively according to their original response formats (dichotomous, multiple response, or open-ended).

Study procedure and data collection

Translation

We conducted a forward and backward translation10,11 to evaluate equivalence across four dimensions: (1) semantic, ensuring words conveyed the same meaning in both languages, including accurate translation of technical terms such as “pre-exposure prophylaxis” and “antiretroviral”; (2) idiomatic, adapting culturally specific expressions to fit the Indonesian linguistic context rather than translating literally; (3) experiential, ensuring items reflected health experiences and contexts relevant to Indonesian settings, including differences in PrEP service delivery and community norms; and (4) conceptual, preserving the same interpretive framework for constructs such as knowledge, awareness, and willingness across cultures. Two forward translators produced independent Indonesian translations: (i) a native Indonesian translator with a medical background (infectious diseases and HIV) and (ii) a native Indonesian translator without a medical background and blinded to the study objectives. The research team synthesised both forward translations into a reconciled version (Version 1). Two independent backward translators (native English speakers) then produced backward translations. A physician with a master’s degree in public health and expertise in infectious diseases and HIV reviewed the translated version against the original instrument. The research team performed an item-by-item comparison of the backward translations with the original instrument and refined wording for clarity and cultural appropriateness, resulting in Version 2.

Cross cultural adaptation and face validity

All Indonesian researchers evaluated Version 2 through an expert committee review to assess cultural relevance, acceptability, and appropriateness of wording for the Indonesian socio-religious context while preserving the intended meaning of each item. Minor modifications were made to ensure that sensitive concepts related to sexual behaviour and HIV prevention were framed appropriately and comprehensively, producing a pre-final Indonesian version (Version 3).

Face validity was assessed by testing Version 3 among 60 members of the general public (10 participants from each of the six regional groups: Sumatera; Kalimantan; Sulawesi; Bali and Nusa Tenggara; Maluku and Papua; and Java). Structured cognitive interviews were conducted using think-aloud and verbal probing techniques to evaluate comprehension, interpretation, and clarity. Participants also provided feedback on the estimated time required to complete the instrument. Items were refined when multiple participants (e.g., ≥ 2) reported ambiguity, misinterpretation, or difficulty selecting an appropriate response option. The research team synthesised participant feedback and re-evaluated revised wording against the translated and original versions, resulting in the final Indonesian version (Version 4).

National survey administration and dissemination strategy

The final instrument (Version 4) was administered online using Google Forms and disseminated via WhatsApp, Instagram, and Facebook. To strengthen geographic coverage across Indonesia’s six regional groups, each author supported dissemination by distributing the Google Forms link within their respective regions as follows: Sumatera (ZZ); Java, Bali, and Nusa Tenggara (SDA, SAF, AA, DAP, FDP, SS, FFA, SAK, FWP, and MRR); Sulawesi and Kalimantan (BA, AV, UU, AA, MS, and LMS); and Maluku and Papua (SR and IHA). The survey was anonymous, and no direct personal identifiers were collected. To improve completeness, items were set as required (where applicable). To minimise duplicate submissions, Google Forms was configured using the “Limit to 1 response” option, allowing one submission per participant. Figure 1 illustrates the study’s procedural flow.

Data processing and consensus procedures

For translation, the primary research team reviewed outputs from each step and resolved discrepancies through consensus to preserve conceptual equivalence. For cross-cultural adaptation and face validity, participant feedback was synthesised to identify items requiring clarification or rewording. The principal investigator documented all comments, circulated a summary to the research team, and facilitated iterative discussions until unanimous agreement was reached on the final wording of each item.

Validation procedures

Content validity

Content validity was evaluated by a multidisciplinary panel of 25 expert evaluators. The panel comprised 22 authors with diverse expertise: six pharmacists with doctoral degrees and published expertise in psychometric instrument validation; four pharmacists with extensive experience in social and community pharmacy research (including one serving as the HIV/AIDS program coordinator for Maluku Province); four physicians with public health doctoral degrees; two dermatologists; two health education researchers; one clinical psychologist with multiple publications in instrument validation; one public health specialist; one general physician (M.Sc.); and one nurse with a doctoral degree. Three external specialists were also invited: one PrEP/HIV activist from South Sulawesi with grassroots community program experience; one obstetrician-gynaecologist with expertise in HIV prevention in maternal health; and one pharmacist from a government hospital in West Java specializing in TB-HIV integrated services. Each expert reviewed all items and provided qualitative feedback on relevance, clarity, and cultural appropriateness for the Indonesian context. Experts were specifically asked to evaluate whether the translated version maintained the intended meaning of each original item and to assess the completeness of all items to measure the underlying knowledge concept. Expert feedback was collected through multiple channels to accommodate geographic distribution: synchronous online discussions via Zoom, telephone consultations, and asynchronous written feedback via WhatsApp to the principal investigator. All feedback was systematically documented, and discrepancies were resolved through iterative discussions until consensus was achieved. Although formal content validity indices (I-CVI and S-CVI) were not calculated, the experts’ comments and suggestions provided sufficient qualitative evidence to support content validity. Items were retained a priori if they (i) received unanimous expert agreement on relevance and cultural appropriateness, (ii) demonstrated clear alignment with the intended construct (PrEP knowledge), and (iii) showed no significant comprehension difficulties during expert review. All six original items met these criteria and were retained with minor wording refinements. The four Indonesian-specific items also met al.l retention criteria and received unanimous endorsements from all 25 experts, resulting in a final 10-item knowledge scale. Based on expert consensus and alignment with the Indonesian Ministry of Health PrEP Technical Guidelines (2023)9, four additional items were developed and unanimously endorsed to address contextually important aspects of PrEP use in Indonesia, expanding the knowledge domain from six to ten items: Item 7: A person who has used PrEP does not need to use a condom during sexual activity (reverse-scored item assessing misconception about condom necessity); Item 8: One of the requirements for receiving PrEP services is not showing any signs of acute HIV infection (assessing understanding of PrEP eligibility criteria); Item 9: PrEP can be performed when someone is at a high risk of HIV infection, and can be stopped if the risk becomes low or no longer exists meaning PrEP does not need to be administered continuously, unlike antiretroviral therapy (assessing understanding of flexible, risk-based PrEP use); Item 10: PrEP can still be used by individuals with certain conditions such as pregnant or breastfeeding women (assessing knowledge of PrEP safety in special populations). All 25 experts unanimously agreed that these four additional items were essential for a comprehensive PrEP knowledge assessment aligned with the Indonesian implementation guidelines.

Construct validity (structural validity)

Construct validity of the 10-item SADAR-PrEP knowledge scale was examined using exploratory factor analysis (EFA) with principal axis factoring. EFA was selected as the appropriate method because the instrument was newly developed, with four items contextually adapted for the Indonesian setting, making empirical exploration of the factorial structure more appropriate than imposing a pre-specified confirmatory model12. The number of factors to retain was determined using the Kaiser criterion (eigenvalue > 1.0)13. Item retention was based on predefined criteria including salient factor loadings (≥ 0.40)14,15 and the overall interpretability of the factor solution12. CFA will be considered in future validation studies with independent samples.

Reliability

Internal consistency reliability of the 10-item PrEP knowledge scale was assessed using Cronbach’s alpha. To provide item-level evidence of scale coherence, we additionally examined corrected item total correlations, inter-item correlations, and alpha-if-item-deleted statistics.

Known-groups validity

Known-groups validity was evaluated by comparing PrEP knowledge scores across groups hypothesised a priori to differ in knowledge (e.g., educational level, health-science educational background, and prior HIV/PrEP workshop attendance). Effect size estimates were reported alongside p-values to quantify practical significance.

Data management and quality control

Rigorous data management and quality control were implemented. Downloaded data were screened for completeness and plausibility prior to analysis. The dataset was periodically reviewed to identify potential duplicate entries and inconsistent response patterns. All datasets (raw and cleaned) were securely stored in a password-protected database accessible only to the principal investigator and the author responsible for data analysis.

Statistical analysis

Categorical variables are presented as frequencies and percentages. PrEP knowledge scores (range, 0–10) are presented as means (average knowledge score) and standard deviations. Group differences for known-groups validity were assessed using independent samples t-tests (two groups) and one-way analysis of variance (> 2 groups); parametric tests were deemed appropriate given the large sample sizes across all groups (n > 200 per group). Effect sizes were reported as Cohen’s d for all group comparisons, calculated as the difference between group means divided by the pooled standard deviation, and interpreted using Cohen’s (1988) conventions (small: 0.20–0.49; medium: 0.50–0.79; large: ≥0.80)16. Associations between sociodemographic characteristics and key outcomes (willingness and barriers to PrEP use) were examined using multiple logistic regression models. Multicollinearity was assessed using the variance inflation factor (VIF) prior to model fitting. Model fit was evaluated using the Hosmer-Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test and Nagelkerke R217,18. Results are reported as odds ratios with 95% confidence intervals. All tests were two-tailed, with α = 0.05. Construct validity was examined using exploratory factor analysis (EFA) with principal axis factoring. All analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics version 27.

Results

Based on the sociodemographic characteristics of the 1150 participants presented in Table 1, the majority of participants were female (76.7%), while males accounted for only 23.3%. The largest age group was within the 17–24 year range (79.1%), with a declining distribution as age increased. In terms of educational attainment, the majority of participants held a junior or senior high school diploma as their highest qualification (54.3%), 43.6% had completed education up to a diploma or bachelor’s degree, and only 2.2% held a postgraduate degree (master/PhD). Regarding marital status, the majority of participants were unmarried (90.9%), while only 9.1% were married. In terms of religion, the majority of participants were Muslim (82.5%), followed by Christians (15.6%) and other religions (1.9%). Participants were distributed across various regions in Indonesia, with the largest proportion coming from Sulawesi (49.7%), followed by Java (32.6%), and smaller proportions from other regions such as Kalimantan, Sumatera, Bali-Nusa Tenggara, and Maluku-Papua. In terms of monthly expenditures, the majority of participants reported spending between 1 and 2 million rupiah (46.7%), whereas only 4.3% had expenditures exceeding 5 million rupiah. Regarding health education background, of the 825 participants who provided data, 53.4% had a background in health education, while 18.3% did not. Additionally, 18.6% of the participants reported having attended workshops, seminars, or webinars related to HIV/AIDS PrEP, whereas the majority (53.1%) stated that they had not participated in such educational activities. These findings provide a comprehensive overview of the sociodemographic characteristics of the participants, which can serve as a foundation for further analysis of the factors influencing their understanding and acceptance of PrEP.

Based on the data regarding awareness of PrEP presented in Table 2, most participants (64.7%) had never heard of PrEP, whereas only 35.3% stated that they were aware of this therapy. Among the participants who had heard of PrEP, the primary sources of information were print media, such as posters (23.9%), followed by social media (20.1%), friends or family (17.8%), and healthcare professionals (16.0%). Other sources of information, such as independent internet searches (13.7%), scientific journals (5.0%), and television (3.4%), accounted for smaller proportions. When asked about access to PrEP services, only 16.1% of participants knew the location of PrEP services, while the majority (83.9%) were unaware of how to access them. Additionally, regarding the cost of services, only 26.3% of participants knew that PrEP was available for free at government healthcare facilities, 70.3% lacked information on this matter, and 3.3% mistakenly believed that PrEP was not provided free of charge. Interestingly, despite the low awareness of PrEP, 53.5% of the participants expressed an interest in learning more about this therapy. However, a significant information gap remains, particularly regarding the access to and availability of PrEP services. Most participants lacked a sufficient understanding of PrEP, underscoring the need for broader and more comprehensive education and dissemination of information to enhance awareness and accessibility of PrEP, particularly through reliable and easily accessible sources for the public.

Based on the data presented in Table 3, participants’ understanding of PrEP varied. A total of 66.2% knew that PrEP is an antiretroviral therapy for HIV prevention, 31.5% were unaware of it, and 2.3% held misconceptions. Misunderstandings were still prevalent; for example, 52.3% believed that PrEP was administered to HIV-positive individuals, whereas only 15.5% believed that PrEP was intended for HIV-negative individuals. The understanding of PrEP use varies significantly. A total of 48.4% were unsure whether PrEP must be taken for life, 34.0% were aware of its flexible usage, and 49.4% did not understand that PrEP could be discontinued when the risk of HIV infection decreased. Awareness of side effects remained low, with 55.0% unaware of them, 35.7% assuming there were no side effects, and only 9.4% recognizing the existence of side effects. Regarding access and usage requirements, 62.3% knew that an HIV test was required before obtaining PrEP, whereas 34.7% were unaware of this requirement. Regarding prevention, 52.2% of participants understood that PrEP did not replace condoms, 43.0% remained uncertain, and 4.8% believed that condoms were no longer necessary. Only 38.2% understood that PrEP is provided to individuals without clinical signs of acute HIV infection and 59.4% were unaware that PrEP is safe for use in pregnant and breastfeeding women. The reliability test results for 10 items shown in Table 3 demonstrated excellent internal reliability (Cronbach’s α = 0.849).

Based on the data in Table 4, Willingness to Use PrEP, the majority of participants (74.3%) expressed their Willingness to Use PrEP, reflecting a high level of acceptance. However, it is important to note that 25.7% declined the PrEP program, primarily due to fears of potential side effects (41.5%), concerns about dependency and access to PrEP (14.8%), and social stigma (13.2%). Other factors such as cost (12.7%) and privacy (12.2%) also played a role. The key drivers of willingness to use PrEP included awareness of the risk of HIV infection (32.2%), knowledge of and information about PrEP (19.5%), and support from healthcare facilities (15.2%). Despite the high willingness expressed by the participants, only 0.4% actually used the PrEP. This highlights the gap between participants’ intentions and actual implementation. The reasons for using PrEP included awareness of increasing HIV cases (18.4%), self-protection against HIV (14.9%), and uncertainty about a partner’s HIV status (14.9%). These findings indicate that while awareness and interest are high, significant barriers such as side effects, stigma, and limited access remain. Comprehensive education and improved accessibility of PrEP services are essential for encouraging broader utilization.

Table 5 presents the results of the known-group validity analysis to test whether our instrument can differentiate the level of knowledge related to HIV-PrEP use based on participant characteristics. The results of our analysis showed significant differences in the knowledge scores based on education level, educational background in the health sector, and experience attending HIV-related workshops. Respondents with higher educational and health backgrounds had higher PrEP knowledge scores (p < 0.001). However, this was different when associated with the experience of participants who had and had not participated in HIV/AIDS seminars; namely, the difference in PrEP knowledge scores was statistically significant (p = 0.003). These results supported the discriminant validity of the instruments used in this study.

The results of the own-group analysis indicate that the SADAR-PrEP instrument effectively discriminated between groups in all three known group comparisons. We found that participants with (i) higher levels of education, (ii) health science backgrounds, and (iii) prior experience participating in HIV/AIDS seminars reported higher PrEP knowledge scores (p < 0.001 and p < 0.003). More specifically, participants with a maximum education level of senior high school reported lower PrEP knowledge scores than those in the university degree group. Across these three groups, no significant differences were found between the diploma or undergraduate and postgraduate groups (p = 0.412; Cohen’s d = 0.02), indicating a negligible effect. Participants with a health sciences education background scored significantly higher on PrEP knowledge (M = 5.03, SD = 3.15) than those without such a background (M = 3.82, SD = 3.03; p < 0.001; Cohen’s d = 0.39), indicating a small effect size. Those who had attended an HIV-related workshop also scored significantly higher (M = 5.28, SD = 3.00) than those who had not (M = 4.53, SD = 3.20; p = 0.003; Cohen’s d = 0.24), reflecting a small effect size. Across all comparisons, effect sizes were consistently small (Cohen’s d = 0.24–0.45), indicating that while differences between groups were statistically significant, their magnitude was relatively small. This pattern is consistent with the early stage of the national PrEP rollout in Indonesia and with the sample’s diverse educational backgrounds and geographic composition. The Cronbach’s alpha value for the 10-item SADAR-PrEP knowledge scale was 0.849, indicating high internal consistency.

Discussion

The SADAR-PrEP instrument, known as Skala pengetAhuan DAn Respon terhadap PrEP, was created and validated to evaluate the understanding, sources of information, accessibility, and readiness to use PrEP among the general population in Indonesia. The acronym “SADAR,” meaning “aware” or “conscious” in Indonesian, was intentionally selected because of its cultural significance in public health discussions. This culturally relevant term boosts the instrument’s acceptance and aids its application in public health communication, functioning both as a precise psychometric assessment and as a familiar concept in Indonesian health education.

The SADAR-PrEP instrument demonstrated good validity and reliability in measuring knowledge of and attitudes toward PrEP in the general Indonesian population. Known-group validity testing revealed a positive correlation between educational attainment and the PrEP knowledge scores. However, an unexpected finding emerged: Participation in HIV/AIDS seminars or webinars did not consistently correlate with higher levels of PrEP knowledge. This finding may be explained by our demographic data, majority of participants reported no prior awareness and indicated a lack of knowledge regarding PrEP service access. Following translation, cultural adaptation, and addition of four items based on the national PrEP guidelines, we evaluated two versions of the instrument. The expanded 10-item final version demonstrated significantly improved internal consistency (Cronbach-α = 0.849). These findings suggest that the instrument effectively discriminated between groups based on both educational level and exposure to HIV/AIDS information, establishing it as a valid, reliable, and contextually appropriate measurement tool for a diverse Indonesian population.

In our study, we discovered that educational attainment significantly influenced knowledge of and attitudes toward PrEP utilization as an HIV prevention strategy. Individuals with higher education, ranging from undergraduate to postgraduate degrees, demonstrated superior PrEP knowledge scores compared to those with only elementary or secondary education. Moreover, participants from health-related faculties, both current students and graduates, not only exhibited a better understanding of PrEP but also displayed more favorable attitudes and greater acceptance toward its use. Our findings are consistent with those of several international studies that have reported similar conclusions. Calderon et al. (2020), in their research involving 474 participants from diverse racial and socioeconomic backgrounds in urban American settings, confirmed that higher education levels were positively correlated with PrEP knowledge and attitudes22. Similarly, Enebeli et al. (2022) reported consistent results in a study of 802 Canadian participants from the general population, irrespective of HIV risk factors, where education enhanced PrEP comprehension and acceptance23. In Thailand, Thongsutt et al. (2021) specifically compared 400 students from health and non-health academic backgrounds, revealing that health science students possessed greater familiarity with and willingness to use PrEP24. These collective findings affirm that quality education paves the way for stronger protection against HIV, reducing transmission risks, and mitigating stigma against people living with HIV and PrEP users. Through appropriate education, individuals acquire the essential knowledge and skills for self-protection. In Indonesia, PrEP information dissemination efforts must be bolstered by educational approaches that are equitably structured and regularly implemented to ensure that all societal segments, not just academic or health-educated circles, can comprehend PrEP’s benefits as an HIV prevention measure.

We also found that participants with experience attending HIV/AIDS workshops demonstrated statistically significantly higher PrEP knowledge scores than those who had not (M = 5.28 vs. M = 4.53; p = 0.003; Cohen’s d = 0.24). However, although this difference was statistically significant, the effect size was small, suggesting that while participating in HIV/AIDS workshops contributed to increased PrEP knowledge, the impact was limited. Furthermore, based on our experience and field observations during several community service activities conducted by lecturers, we identified that many participants were still unfamiliar with HIV PrEP. Our findings suggest that current HIV education programs in Indonesia do not optimally incorporate material on PrEP as a prevention strategy. To date, the public is more familiar with condoms as the only contraceptive method for preventing HIV. Our analysis also highlights that while HIV workshops provide exposure to general HIV-related information, specific understanding of PrEP, including access, eligibility requirements, and benefits, appears to be inadequately integrated into existing HIV education curricula. This aligns with findings from several international studies showing that exposure to general HIV information does not necessarily improve specific PrEP knowledge25–27. These findings underscore the need to develop more targeted, inclusive, and community-based PrEP education programs in Indonesia programs that explicitly integrate PrEP information into existing HIV education curricula.

The original instrument was employed in a quantitative descriptive cross-sectional study involving 232 first-year students across all three faculties at the University of Namibia, Rundu Campus8. The study population was predominantly young, with 77% of participants aged 18–24 years, which is comparable to the SADAR-PrEP study where 79.1% of participants (n = 910) fell within the 17–24 age range. The original Namibia instrument assessed three primary outcome variables: awareness of PrEP (measured through questions about having heard of PrEP and knowledge of service access points), knowledge of PrEP (evaluated through questions about PrEP’s purpose, target population, duration, side effects, and testing requirements), and willingness to use PrEP. The study validated the instrument using chi-square tests to examine associations between demographic variables (gender, faculty, age, marital status, and religious background) and the three outcome variables8. While this validation approach demonstrated that both gender and faculty were significantly associated with awareness, knowledge, and willingness to use PrEP (all p-values < 0.05), the study did not report internal reliability metrics such as Cronbach’s alpha, nor did it assess construct validity or factor structure. Furthermore, the original instrument did not incorporate country-specific adaptations related to national PrEP guidelines or considerations of service accessibility within the local healthcare context. By contrast, the SADAR-PrEP instrument underwent comprehensive psychometric validation, including face validity through expert review, known-group validity testing, and internal consistency assessment (Cronbach’s α = 0.849). Additionally, four new items-aligned with Indonesian national PrEP guidelines were developed and incorporated following expert consultation, addressing aspects of PrEP information sources, service accessibility, and readiness to use PrEP that were not systematically examined in the original Namibia study. The SADAR-PrEP validation also encompassed- a-broader geographical scope, with participants recruited from six major Indonesian islands, thereby enhancing demographic and geographic representativeness. These enhancements position SADAR-PrEP as not only a valid and reliable measurement tool but also as a contextually relevant instrument specifically designed to support HIV prevention program planning and evaluation at the general population level in Indonesia.

Jaspal et al.28 developed and validated the Attitudes toward Pre-Exposure Prophylaxis (PrEP) Scale among 222 undergraduate students at De Montfort University in the United Kingdom. The 14-item instrument focused exclusively on attitudinal dimensions rather than knowledge assessment, examining perceptions and barriers related to PrEP acceptance. Validation was conducted through principal components analysis with varimax rotation, which identified three distinct factors: “Attitudes toward the Science of PrEP” (6 items, explaining 25.69% of variance), “Perception of Sexual Health Risks of PrEP” (4 items, explaining 15.34% of variance), and “Perception of Dangerous Effects of PrEP” (3 items, explaining 8.28% of variance). Together, these three factors accounted for 49.31% of the total variance. The complete scale demonstrated good internal consistency (Cronbach’s α = 0.72), with factor loadings ranging from 0.41 to 0.79 across the three dimensions. While this validation approach successfully established the multidimensional structure of PrEP attitudes, several critical limitations constrain its applicability in diverse settings. First, the instrument was limited to attitudinal assessment and did not incorporate knowledge evaluation a fundamental component for understanding baseline awareness in populations with limited PrEP exposure. Second, it excluded assessment of information sources, service accessibility awareness, and readiness to use PrEP, which are essential factors for translating attitudes into actual PrEP uptake. Third, the scale was developed and validated exclusively within the UK healthcare context, where PrEP is available through the National Health Service and embedded in a well-established sexual health infrastructure that differs substantially from resource-limited settings. By contrast, the SADAR-PrEP instrument addresses these gaps through a more comprehensive and contextually adapted approach. SADAR-PrEP integrates both knowledge and attitudes within a single instrument, achieving superior internal consistency (Cronbach’s α = 0.849 compared to 0.72). The instrument was specifically designed to align with Indonesian national PrEP guidelines and incorporates country-specific considerations such as service delivery structures and cultural factors affecting PrEP acceptance. Critically, SADAR-PrEP assesses dimensions entirely absent from Jaspal’s scale: sources of PrEP information (health facilities, media, interpersonal networks), awareness of where to access PrEP services, knowledge of cost and eligibility criteria, and behavioral readiness to initiate PrEP use. Furthermore, SADAR-PrEP underwent more comprehensive psychometric validation, including face validity through expert review, known-group validity testing demonstrating discrimination based on educational level and health background, and internal consistency assessment. This multifaceted validation approach, combined with the instrument’s broader dimensional scope and contextual adaptation to Indonesian realities, positions SADAR-PrEP as a more suitable tool for both assessing PrEP-related psychosocial factors and guiding the design of targeted educational interventions and service delivery improvements in resource-limited settings.

Budhwani et al.29 conducted a validation study of two complementary scales among 235 youth aged 16–24 years who were active PrEP users across nine sites in the United States. The PrEP Difficulties Scale, comprising 15 items, utilized principal component analysis with varimax rotation (KMO = 0.78), resulting in four factors: Disclosure, Health Effects, Logistics, and Cost, with a total internal consistency of α = 0.84 and factor loadings ranging from 0.55 to 0.87 (total variance 63.51%). The PrEP Confidence Scale, consisting of 11 items and adapted from the HIV Medication Taking Self-Efficacy Scale, similarly employed factor analysis (KMO = 0.85), yielding three factors: Scheduling, Distraction, and Planning, with an overall α = 0.87 and loadings between 0.69 and 0.84 (total variance: 68.48%). However, the Planning subscale exhibited problematic reliability (α = 0.57, below the acceptable threshold of 0.60). Both scales demonstrated robust construct validity through correlations with PrEP adherence, stigma, and social support scores. Notably, these scales were specifically designed for active PrEP users individuals already prescribed and taking PrEP focusing on measuring adherence facilitators and barriers rather than baseline awareness or knowledge of PrEP. The highly specialized sample (91.5% male, 79.1% gay-identified, sexual, and gender minority youth already engaged in PrEP care) limits the generalizability of the findings to broader populations. In contrast, SADAR-PrEP addresses a fundamentally different measurement gap: assessing the baseline knowledge and attitudes of the general Indonesian population who may have never heard of PrEP. While Budhwani’s scales support adherence monitoring among current users, SADAR-PrEP serves the upstream function of identifying population-level awareness gaps and educational needs. Furthermore, SADAR-PrEP achieves superior overall reliability (α = 0.849 vs. 0.84), integrates knowledge and attitudes into a single efficient instrument (10 vs. 26 items), avoids problematic subscales, and incorporates an assessment of information sources and service accessibility dimensions that are absent from Budhwani’s adherence-focused scales29. These distinctions position SADAR-PrEP as being better suited for public health surveillance, program planning, and educational intervention development in populations where PrEP awareness remains low.

The PrEP instruments developed in various countries typically undergo psychometric validation processes that are aligned with international standards. However, most of these validation studies have been conducted with specific populations, such as high-risk groups, which may differ contextually from the general population, including the participants in our study. For instance, the development of the PrEP knowledge, motivation, and self-efficacy scale among MSM in Colombia utilized a systematic construct validation process, involving the division of the sample for exploratory factor analysis (EFA) and subsequent confirmation of its structure through confirmatory factor analysis (CFA). This process included item selection criteria, such as a factor loading of ≥ 0.40, reporting of model fit indices, and reliability assessment at the subscale level6. In terms of stigma, the HIV PrEP Stigma Scale (HPSS) also underwent a rigorous validation process via EFA, which included evaluation of each item’s performance, internal consistency testing, and hypothesis-based construct validity testing, such as the association between stigma scores and relevant external constructs. Notably, this study also compared response formats (Likert vs. semantic differential) as part of the measurement tool optimization30. A comprehensive approach is also evident in the Young Women’s PrEP Stigma Scale in Kenya, which integrated participatory item development, pilot testing based on EFA with clear item retention rules, model fit testing, known-groups/divergent validity testing, and longitudinal application to assess measurement consistency over time31. Conversely, in the context of medical and pharmacy students, studies on PrEP knowledge tend to employ a more pragmatic validation approach, such as item development based on previous guidelines/instruments, expert review, limited pilot testing to ensure item clarity, and reporting internal consistency without extensive factor structure testing32,33. In comparison to the aforementioned literature, our study (SADAR-PrEP) deliberately focuses on the need for surveillance and mapping of PrEP needs within the general Indonesian community through robust cross-cultural adaptation, strong reliability evidence (Cronbach’s α = 0.88), and the capacity to distinguish relevant sociodemographic groups (known-group validity), rendering it more suitable for community education needs and population-based intervention planning.

In the Indonesian context, this general population approach to PrEP knowledge assessment is particularly critical, given the nation’s unique epidemiological, cultural, and structural landscape. Indonesia faces ongoing challenges in HIV prevention despite sustained public health efforts, with certain key populations experiencing disproportionately high HIV incidence, while awareness of biomedical prevention options remains limited across the broader population. As an archipelagic nation comprising more than 17,000 islands with extraordinary geographic, cultural, and linguistic diversity, Indonesia faces substantial barriers to uniform health information dissemination and equitable service access. Cultural factors, including conservative social norms surrounding sexual health discussions, religious considerations, and persistent HIV-related stigma, create additional challenges to open dialogue about HIV prevention strategies such as PrEP. Although Indonesia has established national guidelines for PrEP implementation, translating policy into widespread public awareness and accessible services has proven difficult, particularly outside major urban centers. The majority of the Indonesian population remains unaware of PrEP’s existence, its protective efficacy, where to access services, or eligibility criteria a knowledge gap that cannot be addressed without first establishing baseline awareness levels and identifying specific educational needs across diverse communities. The SADAR-PrEP instrument directly responds to this measurement gap by providing a validated, culturally adapted tool specifically designed for the Indonesian general population. By assessing not only knowledge and attitudes but also awareness of information sources and service accessibility, SADAR-PrEP enables health authorities to identify which population segments require targeted interventions, which geographic regions face the greatest knowledge deficits, and which aspects of PrEP education demand prioritization. This contextually grounded approach is essential for developing evidence-based educational strategies that resonate with Indonesian cultural values, address locally relevant barriers, and ultimately expand PrEP awareness beyond traditional high-risk groups to the general population, where unrecognized HIV risk may exist.

The SADAR-PrEP instrument offers multiple practical applications for strengthening Indonesia’s HIV prevention infrastructure at the individual, community, and systems levels. At the population surveillance level, SADAR-PrEP enables health authorities to conduct periodic assessments of PrEP awareness, knowledge, and attitudes across diverse geographic regions and demographic groups, providing longitudinal data to track progress in public education efforts and identify emerging knowledge gaps. For educational program planning, the instrument’s multidimensional structure allows program designers to pinpoint specific knowledge deficits whether related to PrEP’s protective mechanism, eligibility criteria, side effect profiles, or service access points and develop targeted educational materials that address the precise informational needs of different population segments. In service delivery contexts, SADAR-PrEP assessment results can guide strategic decisions about where to establish new PrEP service sites, which communities require enhanced outreach efforts, and what types of information dissemination channels prove most effective in reaching underserved populations. The instrument also serves as a valuable tool for evaluating the effectiveness of PrEP awareness campaigns and educational interventions through pre-post assessments, enabling program managers to demonstrate impact, justify continued funding, and refine intervention strategies based on empirical evidence. Additionally, SADAR-PrEP data can inform policy development by providing decision-makers with population-level evidence of PrEP awareness gaps, service accessibility barriers, and educational priorities, thereby supporting evidence-based resource allocation and strategic planning for national HIV prevention programs. By offering a standardized, validated measurement approach, SADAR-PrEP facilitates comparisons across regions, time periods, and intervention contexts, ultimately contributing to the more efficient, equitable, and effective deployment of limited public health resources to expand PrEP awareness and uptake in Indonesia.

To maximize the impact of the SADAR-PrEP findings and address the substantial knowledge gaps identified in this study, we recommend several strategic interventions across multiple levels of Indonesia’s health and education systems. First, comprehensive PrEP education should be systematically integrated into health education curricula at all educational levels, from secondary schools to universities, with particular emphasis on health science programs, where future healthcare providers receive foundational training. Second, community-based interventions employing culturally sensitive messaging and delivery approaches are essential for reaching populations outside formal educational settings, including training community health workers and peer educators to serve as trusted sources of accurate PrEP information within their social networks. Third, mass media campaigns utilizing television, radio, and increasingly prevalent social media platforms should be strategically designed to raise general awareness about PrEP’s availability and efficacy while simultaneously combating misinformation and reducing HIV-related stigma that inhibits open discussion of prevention options. Fourth, healthcare provider education must be strengthened to ensure that clinicians across all levels of the health system, not just infectious disease specialists, possess accurate PrEP knowledge and can confidently counsel patients about this prevention option, thereby reducing provider-level barriers to PrEP access. Fifth, structural barriers, including the geographic inaccessibility of services, financial costs, and administrative complexities, must be systematically addressed through policy interventions that expand service availability, implement sustainable financing mechanisms, and streamline PrEP delivery processes. Finally, continued research is needed to evaluate the effectiveness of these multilevel interventions using SADAR-PrEP as a standardized outcome measure, to conduct longitudinal studies tracking knowledge changes over time, and to validate the instrument among specific populations, including key populations at heightened HIV risk, diverse age groups beyond the predominantly young sample in this study, and rural communities where health information access remains most limited. These coordinated efforts across the education, healthcare, community engagement, and policy domains are essential for translating Indonesia’s national PrEP guidelines into widespread public awareness and ultimately expanding access to this critical HIV prevention tool.

Our study possesses several notable strengths that support the validity and utility of our findings. First, the large sample size of 1,150 participants drawn from six major Indonesian islands (Java, Sumatra, Kalimantan, Sulawesi, Bali, and Papua) provides substantial geographic diversity and enhances the generalizability of results across Indonesia’s archipelagic landscape, representing populations from both urban and rural settings with varying levels of healthcare infrastructure and health information access. Second, our focus on the general population rather than exclusively targeting key populations or individuals already engaged with HIV services addresses a critical measurement gap and enables assessment of baseline PrEP awareness in communities where Preventive education is most needed. Third, the study employed comprehensive psychometric validation incorporating multiple forms of evidence: face validity established through expert panel review, known-group validity demonstrated through significant differences based on educational attainment and health-related educational background, and strong internal consistency reliability (Cronbach’s α = 0.849) exceeding conventional thresholds for instrument acceptability. Fourth, SADAR-PrEP was deliberately designed to align with Indonesian national PrEP guidelines and incorporates four locally-developed items addressing country-specific considerations of information sources, service accessibility, and behavioral readiness, thereby ensuring cultural and contextual appropriateness beyond simple translation of the existing international instruments. Fifth, the instrument’s brevity (10 items in the final version) and straightforward Likert-scale response format make it highly practical for large-scale population surveys, minimizing respondent burden while maintaining comprehensive coverage of essential PrEP knowledge and attitude domains. Finally, as the first validated PrEP knowledge and attitudes instrument specifically developed for the Indonesian general population, SADAR-PrEP fills an important gap in the HIV prevention toolkit and provides a standardized measurement approach that can facilitate comparisons across regions, time periods, and intervention contexts, ultimately supporting evidence-based public health decision-making, and program evaluation efforts.

Notwithstanding these strengths, it is important to recognize several limitations when interpreting our findings. First, the cross-sectional design precludes causal inference and does not permit the assessment of test-retest reliability or temporal stability of knowledge and attitudes. Second, the use of convenience sampling and exclusive online data collection introduced systematic selection bias; the sample was predominantly young (79.1% aged 17–24 years) and geographically concentrated in Sulawesi (49.7%), limiting generalizability to older adults, rural communities, and populations with limited internet access. Third, reliance on self-reported data introduces the possibility of social desirability bias, particularly for sensitive items concerning sexual behavior and HIV risk. Fourth, SADAR-PrEP was validated in the general population; additional validation is needed among key populations at heightened HIV risk, including men who have sex with men, female sex workers, transgender individuals, and people who inject drugs, as well as in remote rural areas underrepresented in this sample. Fifth, the instrument measures knowledge and attitudes but does not assess actual PrEP uptake or adherence, limiting the establishment of predictive validity. Sixth, although content validity was supported through a structured qualitative review by a multidisciplinary expert panel (n = 25), formal content validity indices (I-CVI and S-CVI) were not calculated; future studies should incorporate systematic CVI calculation to strengthen the quantitative evidence base. Future research should prioritize longitudinal designs, probability-based sampling, validation in key populations and rural settings, and predictive validity studies. Despite these limitations, SADAR-PrEP represents a significant methodological advancement and provides Indonesian health authorities with a validated, practical tool for population-level PrEP awareness assessment and evidence-informed program planning.

Conclusion

The SADAR-PrEP (Skala pengetAhuan DAn Respon terhadap PrEP) instrument has established its validity and reliability through multiple rigorous psychometric evaluations, demonstrating its strength as a measurement instrument. We recommend that key stakeholders, including the central and local governments, as well as higher education institutions, adopt the SADAR-PrEP instrument more widely to support HIV transmission prevention programs in Indonesia. This instrument will help map the need for targeted education and interventions while also evaluating the effectiveness of ongoing HIV prevention initiatives.
