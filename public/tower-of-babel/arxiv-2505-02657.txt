Local equilibrium Wigner function for spin- 1 / 2 \nicefrac{{1}}{{2}} particles
Samapan Bhadury, Zbigniew Drogosz, Wojciech Florkowski, Sudip Kumar Kar, Valeriya Mykhaylova

Abstract
Formal connections between the spin density matrix and the Wigner function for spin- 1 / 2 {\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}} particles forming a relativistic gas are explored to determine their general structures. They suggest that the commonly used form of the local equilibrium Wigner function should be replaced by a new expression. The latter fulfills the necessary condition for the normalization of the mean spin polarization, which the former fails to reproduce. The new definition of the Wigner function leads to generalized thermodynamic relations for perfect spin hydrodynamics, identical to those obtained earlier using the classical concept of spin. Moreover, one can prove that the perfect spin hydrodynamics based on the new equilibrium Wigner function is nonlinearly causal and stable. Finally, the selection rule for the Lagrange multipliers, which is satisfied by real systems, is discussed.

Equilibrium spinor density (current status) —
Following the original idea formulated in

50

\displaystyle X^{\pm}=\exp\left[\pm\xi(x)-\beta_{\mu}(x)p^{\mu}\right]M^{\pm},

(3)

\displaystyle M^{\pm}=\exp\left[\pm\frac{1}{2}\omega_{\mu\nu}(x)\Sigma^{\mu\nu}\right].

is the spin polarization tensor, which can be expressed in terms of the electric- and magnetic-like three-vectors,
{\bm{e}}=(e^{1},e^{2},e^{3})

\displaystyle\omega_{\mu\nu}=\begin{bmatrix}0&e^{1}&e^{2}&e^{3}\\
-e^{1}&0&-b^{3}&b^{2}\\
-e^{2}&b^{3}&0&-b^{1}\\
-e^{3}&-b^{2}&b^{1}&0\end{bmatrix},

(5)

where the sign conventions follow those of
[
61
]

It has been shown in Ref.
[

{\bm{P}}=\frac{1}{2}\frac{{\rm tr_{2}}\left(f^{\pm}{\bm{\sigma}}\right)}{{\rm tr_{2}}\left(f^{\pm}\right)}

(6)

and equals

\displaystyle\hskip-14.22636pt{\bm{P}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2}\tanh\left(\frac{\sqrt{{\bm{b}}_{\ast}\cdot{\bm{b}}_{\ast}\!-\!{\bm{e}}_{\ast}\cdot{\bm{e}}_{\ast}}}{2}\right)\frac{{\bm{b}}_{\ast}}{\sqrt{{\bm{b}}_{\ast}\cdot{\bm{b}}_{\ast}\!-\!{\bm{e}}_{\ast}\cdot{\bm{e}}_{\ast}}},

(7)

From spin to spinor density —
As the methodology outlined above leads to problems with the correct definition of the mean spin polarization, one may try to reverse the line of the arguments. We may start with the general forms of the two-by-two spin density matrices and derive, using Eqs. (
1
) and (
2
), the corresponding forms of
X^{\pm}
.

Since
f^{+}_{rs}(x,p)
and
f^{-}_{rs}(x,p)
are Hermitian matrices, we can use the decomposition
[
12
]

\displaystyle f_{rs}^{\pm}(x,p)=f^{\pm}_{0}(x,p)\left[\delta_{rs}+{\bm{\zeta}}_{\ast}^{\pm}(x,{\bm{p}})\cdot{\bm{\sigma}}_{rs}\right],

(9)

where
f^{\pm}_{0}(x,p)
is the phase-space density of (anti)particles averaged over spin.
The three-vector
{\bm{\zeta}}_{\ast}^{\pm}(x,{\bm{p}})
can be interpreted as a spatial part of the polarization four-vector
\zeta^{\pm\mu}_{\ast}(x,{\bm{p}})
with a vanishing zeroth component,

\zeta^{\pm\mu}_{\ast}=\left(0,{\bm{\zeta}}_{\ast}^{\pm}\right).

(10)

As discussed above, the magnitude of the vector
{\bm{\zeta}}_{\ast}^{\pm}
is restricted to the range
0\leq|{\bm{\zeta}}_{\ast}^{\pm}|\leq 1
. In the LAB frame, where particles move with momentum
{\bm{p}}
, the form of
\zeta^{\pm\mu}
is

\displaystyle\hskip-14.22636pt\zeta^{\mu}_{\pm}=\Lambda^{\mu}_{\,\,\,\nu}({\bm{v}}_{p})\zeta^{\nu}_{\pm\ast}=\left(\frac{{\bm{p}}\cdot{\bm{\zeta}}^{\pm}_{\ast}}{m},\ {\bm{\zeta}}^{\pm}_{\ast}+\frac{{\bm{p}}\cdot{\bm{\zeta}}^{\pm}_{\ast}}{m(E_{\rm p}+m)}{\bm{p}}\right),

(11)

where
\Lambda^{\mu}_{\,\,\,\nu}({\bm{v}}_{p})
is the canonical boost depending on the particle three-velocity
{\bm{v}}_{\bm{p}}={\bm{p}}/E_{\rm p}

[
12
]
. By referring to Lorentz covariance or by making a direct calculation, one can verify the relations:

\displaystyle{\bar{u}}_{r}(p)\gamma_{5}\zeta^{+}_{\mu}\gamma^{\mu}u_{s}(p)=2m\,{\bm{\zeta}}^{+}_{*}\cdot{\bm{\sigma}}_{rs},

(12)

and

\displaystyle{\bar{v}}_{s}(p)\gamma_{5}\zeta^{-}_{\mu}\gamma^{\mu}v_{r}(p)=-2m\,{\bm{\zeta}}^{-}_{*}\cdot{\bm{\sigma}}_{rs}.

(13)

This immediately indicates that

\displaystyle X^{\pm}_{s}(x,p)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle f_{0}^{\pm}(x,p)\left[1+\gamma_{5}\not{\zeta}^{\pm}(x,p)\right].

(14)

Here we used the Feynman slash notation,
\not{\zeta}=\zeta_{\mu}\gamma^{\mu}
, and added a subscript
s
to distinguish the last formula from the previous definition of
X^{\pm}
. In fact, we observe that Eq. (
14
) disagrees with Eq. (
3
). This difference is particularly striking since Eq. (
14
) is general and does not refer to any equilibrium properties of the spin density matrix.

However, there is the possibility that our new formula for
X^{\pm}_{s}
delivers the same results as the traditional expressions. It is so if we define
{\bm{\zeta}}^{\pm}_{\ast}(x,{\bm{p}})=-\frac{1}{2}{\bm{b}}_{\ast}(x,{\bm{p}})
. We must bear in mind that this agreement may hold only for very small values of
{\bm{b}}_{\ast}(x,{\bm{p}})
and
{\bm{e}}_{\ast}(x,{\bm{p}})
. This finding was already discussed in
[
62
]
; however, without drawing any conclusions.

To proceed further, it is convenient to make use of the identity

\exp\!\left(\gamma_{5}\not{a}_{\pm}\right)\!\!=\!\cosh\!\sqrt{-a^{2}_{\pm}}\!\left[\!1+\!\frac{\gamma_{5}\not{a}_{\pm}}{\sqrt{-a^{2}_{\pm}}}\tanh\!\sqrt{-a^{2}_{\pm}}\right]\!\!.

(15)

Here
a^{\mu}_{\pm}
are spacelike four-vectors satisfying the condition

14
) and (
15
) are equal if

\zeta^{\mu}_{\pm}=\frac{a^{\mu}_{\pm}}{\sqrt{-a^{2}_{\pm}}}\tanh\sqrt{-a^{2}_{\pm}}.

(16)

The normalization condition gives

-\zeta^{2}_{\pm}={\bm{\zeta}}_{\pm*}^{2}=\tanh^{2}\sqrt{-a^{2}_{\pm}}\leq 1,

(17)

which agrees with the normalization condition for the three-vector
{\bm{\zeta}}_{*}
discussed below Eq. (
10
). For small
a^{\mu}_{\pm}
we have
\zeta^{\mu}_{\pm}=a^{\mu}_{\pm}
. Combining (
14
) and (
15

\displaystyle X^{\pm}_{s}(x,p)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle f_{0}^{\pm}(x,p)\exp\left(\gamma_{5}\not{a}_{\pm}\right),

(18)

where
\cosh\sqrt{-a^{2}_{\pm}}
was absorbed into the function
f_{0}^{\pm}(x,p)

Revised equilibrium spinor density —
Our arguments leading to the form (
18
) are based solely on the two facts: the two-by-two spin density matrix is Hermitian, and the polarization vector
{\bm{\zeta}}_{\ast}^{\pm}
satisfies the condition
0\leq|{\bm{\zeta}}_{\ast}^{\pm}|\leq 1
. However, the exponential form of (
18
) directly suggests that it can be used as an equilibrium spin density, especially in the case of the Boltzmann statistics, in which we define

\displaystyle X^{\pm}_{s}(x,p)=\exp\left[\pm\xi(x)-\beta_{\mu}(x)p^{\mu}+\gamma_{5}\not{a}\right].

(19)

Above we have assumed that the vector
a
can be interpreted as the vector Lagrange multiplier that is the same for particles and antiparticles. Furthermore, since spin thermodynamics requires, in fact, six independent Lagrange multipliers, it is convenient to introduce the relation

\displaystyle a_{\mu}(x,p)=-\frac{1}{2m}{\tilde{\omega}}_{\mu\nu}(x)p^{\nu},

(20)

with the dual polarization tensor
{\tilde{\omega}}_{\mu\nu}
.
3
3

3

The choice of the dual spin polarization tensor in (
20
) can be checked a posteriori to be consistent with other frameworks of spin hydrodynamics, for example, based on the classical spin method.
The orthogonality condition
a_{\mu}(x,p)p^{\mu}=0
may be treated as our version of the Frenkel condition that is frequently used in spin hydrodynamics with different formulations
[
63

Wigner function —
Using our expression for the spinor density matrix, we can calculate the Wigner function. With the definitions of the Wigner functions
W^{\pm}(x,k)
introduced in
[
64
]
, we find

\displaystyle W^{\pm}(x,k)\!=\!\frac{1}{4m}\int\!\mathrm{d}P\,\delta^{(4)}(k\!\mp\!p)(\not{p}\!\pm\!m)X_{s}^{\pm}(\not{p}\!\pm\!m),

(21)

where
\mathrm{d}P=\mathrm{d}^{3}p/((2\pi)^{3}E_{\rm p})
. Since
[\gamma_{5}\not{a},\not{p}]=0
, we can also write

\displaystyle W^{\pm}(x,k)=\pm\frac{1}{2}\int\mathrm{d}P\,\delta^{(4)}(k\mp p)(\not{p}\pm m)X_{s}^{\pm}.

(22)

It is interesting to note that the expressions
(\not{p}\pm m)X_{s}^{\pm}
are consistent with the expression derived in the LL course for the spinor density matrix
[
53
]
, as shown in
[
62
]
.

Starting from the Wigner function, we can derive (see
[
64
]
) the following expressions for the baryon current, energy-momentum tensor, and the spin tensor, respectively,

\displaystyle N^{\mu}(x)=\sum_{r=1}^{2}\int\mathrm{d}P\,p^{\mu}\left[f^{+}_{rr}(x,p)-f^{-}_{rr}(x,p)\right],

(23)

\displaystyle T^{\mu\nu}(x)=\sum_{r=1}^{2}\int\mathrm{d}P\,p^{\mu}p^{\nu}\left[f^{+}_{rr}(x,p)+f^{-}_{rr}(x,p)\right],

(24)

\displaystyle\begin{split}S^{\lambda,\mu\nu}(x)&=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{r,s=1}^{2}\int\mathrm{d}P\,p^{\lambda}\left[\sigma^{+\mu\nu}_{sr}(p)f^{+}_{rs}(x,p)\right.\\
&\hskip 28.45274pt\left.+\,\sigma^{-\mu\nu}_{sr}(p)f^{-}_{rs}(x,p)\right],\end{split}

(25)

with the matrices
\sigma^{+\mu\nu}_{sr}(p)=1/(2m)\,{\bar{u}}_{s}(p)\sigma^{\mu\nu}u_{r}(p)
and
\sigma^{-\mu\nu}_{sr}(p)=1/(2m)\,{\bar{v}}_{r}(p)\sigma^{\mu\nu}v_{s}(p)

[
64
,
65
]
.
It is also convenient to define the particle current

\displaystyle{\cal N}^{\mu}(x)=\sum_{r=1}^{2}\int\mathrm{d}P\,p^{\mu}\left[f^{+}_{rr}(x,p)+f^{-}_{rr}(x,p)\right].

(26)

In the spinless case, we find
{\cal N}^{\mu}=P\beta^{\mu}
. We note that because of the pseudogauge ambiguity, different forms of conserved tensors are used in the literature. The forms introduced above are known as the GLW versions, after the surnames of the authors of
[
64
]
. Since the GLW energy-momentum tensor is symmetric, the corresponding spin tensor is conserved as well,
\partial_{\lambda}S^{\lambda,\mu\nu}=0
, which is not necessarily the case if other pseudogauge is
used
[
51
]
.

Thermodynamic relations —
To provide the thermodynamic relations, in addition to the expressions given above, we need to introduce the entropy current. The latter can be defined by the formula originally presented in
[
11
]

\displaystyle\begin{split}S^{\mu}(x)&=-\frac{1}{2}\int\mathrm{d}P\,p^{\mu}\left\{{\rm tr_{4}}\,\left[X_{s}^{+}\,\left(\ln X_{s}^{+}-1\right)\right]\right.\\
&\left.\hskip 28.45274pt+\,{\rm tr_{4}}\,\left[X_{s}^{-}\,\left(\ln X_{s}^{-}-1\right)\right]\right\}.\end{split}

(27)

This leads to the relation

\displaystyle S^{\mu}=T^{\mu\alpha}\beta_{\alpha}-\frac{1}{2}\omega_{\alpha\beta}S^{\mu,\alpha\beta}-\xi N^{\mu}+{\cal N}^{\mu},

(28)

which in the spinless case is treated as a consequence of the extensivity of the thermodynamic variables such as entropy, energy, and baryon number. We note that an alternative way to introduce the entropy current is to use Eq. (
28
) as the definition of
S^{\mu}
, since all
the expressions on the right-hand side are known. In this case, we may argue that Eqs. (
27
) and (
28
) are simply consistent.

Using Eqs. (
23
)–(
26
) we can also derive the identity

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}{\cal N}^{\mu}=N^{\mu}\mathrm{d}\xi-T^{\lambda\mu}\mathrm{d}\beta_{\lambda}+{\scriptstyle{\frac{1}{2}}}S^{\mu,\alpha\beta}\mathrm{d}\omega_{\alpha\beta}.

(29)

In the spinless case, Eq. (
29
) is reduced to the Gibbs-Duhem relation. Equations (
28
) and (
29
) lead to the first law of thermodynamics in the form

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}S^{\mu}=-\xi\mathrm{d}N^{\mu}+\beta_{\lambda}\mathrm{d}T^{\lambda\mu}-{\scriptstyle{\frac{1}{2}}}\omega_{\alpha\beta}\mathrm{d}S^{\mu,\alpha\beta}.

(30)

The last equation is of crucial importance since it indicates that the entropy is conserved owing to the conservation of the baryon number, energy, linear momentum, and the spin part of the total angular momentum. Thus, our concept of local equilibrium assumes that the spin and orbital parts of the total angular momentum
(
S
and
L
)
are conserved separately. Spin-orbit coupling leading to a transfer between
S
and
L
can be included by incorporation of dissipation, as described in more
detail in
[
56
,
57
,
66
]
.

Equations (
28
)–(
30
) were introduced for the first time in
[
56
]
as generalized thermodynamic relations for perfect spin hydrodynamics. Their structure was implied by the kinetic theory results utilizing a classical spin description. In this work, we confirm the form of such thermodynamic relations in an approach that treats spin quantum mechanically through the properly constructed spin density matrix. We emphasize that Eqs. (
28
)–(
30
) are valid for any values of the spin polarization
tensor
\omega
as long as the considered integrals exist (the point discussed below).

Classical description of spin —
The problems discussed below Eq. (
7
) initiated the approach based on the classical description of spin. In this framework, motivated by the seminal paper of Mathisson from 1937
[
67
,
68
]
, one introduces the spin four-vector
s
that satisfies the two conditions:
s\cdot p=0
and
s^{2}=-\,{\gothfamily s:}^{2}
, where
\,{\gothfamily s:}^{2}=3/4
is the eigenvalue of the Casimir operator for SU(2).
With
s
included as an additional variable, the physical phase space expands, and one introduces the phase-space distribution functions
f^{\pm}(x,p,s)
depending on
x
,
p
, and
s
. The macroscopic currents are then obtained as integrals over momentum and spin spaces with the appropriate measures (details are provided in the Supplemental Material).

The classical-spin description eliminates the problems with the normalization of the mean polarization. Moreover, in the limit where the spin polarization tensor is small, it reproduces the result for the spin tensor obtained with Eqs. (
3
) and (
4
). Consequently, for a long time, the classical-spin approach has been the main tool to develop relativistic kinetic theory for particles with spin
{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}
, especially in the approximation where the spin feedback on the baryon current and the energy-momentum tensor is neglected.

Of particular interest is the comparison of the equilibrium distribution functions. In the classical-spin approach we have

\displaystyle f^{\pm}(x,p,s)=\exp\left(\pm\xi-p\cdot\beta+\frac{1}{2}\omega_{\alpha\beta}s^{\alpha\beta}\right),

(31)

with
s^{\alpha\beta}=(1/m)\epsilon^{\alpha\beta\gamma\delta}p_{\gamma}s_{\delta}
, while in the quantum case we use Eq. (
19
). This suggests the correspondence between the classical and quantum descriptions,

\displaystyle s^{\mu}\quad\longleftrightarrow\quad\frac{1}{2}\gamma_{5}\gamma^{\mu}.

(32)

Although the form of the generalized thermodynamic relations is the same as that originally found in
[
56
]
, a question can be asked about the particular form of the spin corrections to
N^{\mu}
and
T^{\mu\nu}
. The explicit calculations shown in the Supplemental Material indicate that the quadratic corrections to
N^{\mu}
and
T^{\mu\nu}
found in the present quantum framework are identical to those obtained within the classical-spin approach
[
56
]
. This shows an intriguing equivalence of performing the integration over
s
with the calculation of the traces over the spinor space. However, differences appear for large values of
{\bm{b}}_{\ast}(x,{\bm{p}})
, as in the classical case the maximum spin polarization equals
\sqrt{\nicefrac{{3}}{{4}}}
, while in the quantum case
{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}
, see also
[
58
]
.

Nonlinear causality of spin hydrodynamics —
Following the methods outlined in
[
59
]
, see also
[
69
,
70
,
71
,
72
,
73
]
, we may show that the perfect spin hydrodynamics constructed with the new Wigner function is nonlinearly causal and its equations are symmetric hyperbolic. To prove this property we first find that the macroscopic currents can be written as derivatives of a generating
function
\chi
, namely:
N^{\lambda}=-\partial^{2}\chi/(\partial\beta_{\lambda}\partial\xi)
,
T^{\lambda\mu}=\partial^{2}\chi/(\partial\beta_{\lambda}\partial\beta_{\mu})
, and
S^{\lambda,\mu\nu}=-\partial^{2}\chi/(\partial\beta_{\lambda}\partial\omega_{\mu\nu})
, where

\chi=\frac{1}{2}{\rm tr_{4}}\left[\int\mathrm{d}P\cosh\xi\left(f_{0}^{+}+f_{0}^{-}\right)\exp\left(\gamma_{5}\not{a}_{\pm}\right)\right].

(33)

By grouping the macroscopic currents and the Lagrange multipliers into multicomponent vectors:
N^{\lambda A}=(N^{\lambda},T^{\lambda\mu},S^{\lambda,\mu\nu})
and
\zeta^{B}=(\xi,-\beta^{\mu},\omega^{\mu\nu})
, we construct a test quantity

M^{\lambda AB}(\zeta)\equiv\left(\frac{\partial N^{\lambda A}}{\partial\zeta_{B}}\right)=-\left(\frac{\partial^{3}\chi}{\partial\zeta_{B}\partial\zeta_{A}\partial\beta_{\lambda}}\right).

(34)

It turns out that the spin hydrodynamic theory based on the conservation laws

\partial_{\lambda}N^{\lambda A}=0\quad\Leftrightarrow\quad M^{\lambda AB}\partial_{\lambda}\zeta_{B}=0

(35)

is nonlinearly causal if the vector
M^{\lambda AB}(\zeta)Z_{A}Z_{B}
is timelike and future-oriented for any nonvanishing real vector
Z

[
59
]
. Starting directly from (
34
), with
\chi
defined
by (
33
)
, one can prove that

M^{\lambda AB}(\zeta)Z_{A}Z_{B}=\int\,\mathrm{d}P\,p^{\lambda}\,w(p,\zeta,Z),

(36)

where
w(p,\zeta,Z)
is a positive function (we provide details of this calculation in the Supplemental Material). Since the four-momentum
p^{\lambda}
is time-like and future-oriented, so is the quantity
M^{\lambda AB}(\zeta)Z_{A}Z_{B}
obtained as a sum of momenta with positive weights.

It is difficult to overestimate the importance of this result. The nonlinear causality and symmetric hyperbolicity of the equations of motion of spin hydrodynamics ensure well-posedness of the initial-value problem and stability of the theory. This, in turn, implies that the constructed framework can be applied in numerical simulations to study the dynamics of spin-polarized
fluids.

Selection criterion —
As long as we consider real systems with finite densities of energy, linear and orbital momentum, and spin, the macroscopic currents should be given by well-defined integrals. Since the four-vector
a_{\mu}
defined by (
20
) depends on the particle momentum, the integrals over the distributions (
19
) converge for very large values of momenta if the following condition is satisfied

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{{{\bm{b}}^{\prime}}^{2}+{{\bm{e}}^{\prime}}^{2}+2|{\bm{e}}^{\prime}\times{\bm{b}}^{\prime}|}<\frac{m}{T},

(37)

where the primes denote vectors in the fluid rest frame, i.e.
u^{\mu}=(1,0,0,0)
. For the derivation of this formula we refer to our recent paper
[
60
]
and the Supplemental Material. Here we only make two points: (i) The hydrodynamic parameters used in recent calculations addressing spin polarization, for example, see
[
74
,
75
]
,
satisfy (
37
)
. (ii) One cannot take the limit
m\to 0
in (
37
), as our formalism defines the spin polarization in the PRF that does not exist for massless particles.

Summary —
In this work, we have solved a long-standing problem of the proper formulation of the local equilibrium Wigner function, which accurately defines the mean polarization vector (accounting for its correct normalization). Our new result for the spin density matrix is consistent with the expression that can be found in the LL course. Our form of the local equilibrium Wigner function offers also the possibility of a quantum derivation of the generalized thermodynamic relations for spin-
{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}
particles. Moreover, it can be used to define spin hydrodynamics that is nonlinearly causal and stable. Thus, the
constructed framework can be successfully utilized in numerical simulations of spin-polarized fluids.

We have established a direct relation to the classical-spin approach – the results for the conserved tensors exactly agree up to the second order in
\omega
since integrals over the allowed classical spin configurations are equivalent to traces over the spinor indices in this case. This clarification is crucial to establish a consistent and uniform framework of spin hydrodynamics for spin-
{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}
particles.

IV.1
1. Classical spin variables and integration measure

In the classical approach to spin
[

68
]

\displaystyle s^{\alpha\beta}=\frac{1}{m}\epsilon^{\alpha\beta\gamma\delta}p_{\gamma}s_{\delta},

which by definition is antisymmetric and orthogonal to the particle four-momentum

\displaystyle s^{\alpha}=\frac{1}{2m}\,\epsilon^{\alpha\beta\gamma\delta}p_{\beta}s_{\gamma\delta}.

(60)

In the PRF, the time component of
s^{\alpha}
vanishes, leaving only the space component given by

s^{\alpha}=(0,{\bm{s}}_{*})
. The magnitude of the spacelike component is set to the square root of the eigenvalue of the Casimir operator
\,{\gothfamily s:}^{2}
, which equals
\nicefrac{{3}}{{4}}
for spin-
{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}
particles.

To apply the principles of kinetic theory to particles with spin, one must include the quantity
s^{\alpha}
as a parameter of the distribution function. The distribution function in the standard spinless case is written as

, which is used interchangeably with
f(x,p)
, bearing in mind that the energy is on the mass shell, i.e.,
p^{0}=E_{\rm p}=\sqrt{m^{2}+{\bm{p}}^{2}}
. While extending this approach to include spin, we consider functions of the type
f(x,p,s)
, which should be considered together with the following integration measures
[
51
]

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}P=\frac{\mathrm{d}^{3}p}{(2\pi)^{3}E_{\rm p}},\qquad\mathrm{d}S=\frac{m}{\pi\,{\gothfamily s:}}\,\mathrm{d}^{4}s\,\delta(s\cdot s+\,{\gothfamily s:}^{2})\,\delta(p\cdot s).

(61)

The spin measure includes two delta functions that restrict the domain of the integration only to values that satisfy the normalization condition and obey the orthogonality to the four-momentum. The prefactor appearing in this measure is chosen such that

\displaystyle\int\mathrm{d}S=2.

(62)

We additionally provide some useful integrals in the spin space
[
51
]
:

\displaystyle\int\mathrm{d}S\,s_{\alpha}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 0,\qquad\int\mathrm{d}S\,s_{\alpha}s_{\beta}=-\frac{2}{3}\,{\gothfamily s:}^{2}\left(g_{\alpha\beta}-\frac{p_{\alpha}p_{\beta}}{m^{2}}\right).

(63)

It is possible to show that all integrals of a product of an odd number of
s
vanish.

The presence of the spin polarization tensor

In the quantum approach, the corrections to the baryon current and the energy-momentum tensor emerge from the expansion of the function

\displaystyle\cosh\sqrt{-a^{2}}=1-\frac{1}{2}a^{2}+\mathcal{O}(a^{4}).

(65)

We arrive at the above equation by expressing

in (

, using additionally Eq. (

The spin tensor contains only odd powers of the spin polarization tensor. Therefore, at the lowest nontrivial order, we can restrict our considerations to terms linear in

is a scalar function that can be calculated in the PRF, namely

\displaystyle\begin{split}w&=\frac{\cosh\sqrt{{\bm{a}}_{*}^{\,2}}}{2}\left(4\gamma^{2}_{+}+\left({\bm{n}}\cdot{\bm{\alpha}}\right)^{2}-4\gamma_{+}{\bm{n}}\cdot{\bm{\alpha}}\tanh\sqrt{{\bm{a}}_{*}^{\,2}}\right)f_{0}^{+}(x,p)\\
&\ \hskip 28.45274pt+\frac{\cosh\sqrt{{\bm{a}}_{*}^{\,2}}}{2}\left(4\gamma^{2}_{-}+\left({\bm{n}}\cdot{\bm{\alpha}}\right)^{2}-4\gamma_{-}{\bm{n}}\cdot{\bm{\alpha}}\tanh\sqrt{{\bm{a}}_{*}^{\,2}}\right)f_{0}^{-}(x,p)\\
&\ \hskip 28.45274pt+\frac{\sinh{\sqrt{{\bm{a}}_{*}^{\,2}}}}{2\sqrt{{\bm{a}}_{*}^{\,2}}}\Big({\bm{\alpha}}\cdot{\bm{\alpha}}-\left({\bm{n}}\cdot{\bm{\alpha}}\right)^{2}\!\!\Big)\Big(f_{0}^{+}(x,p)+f_{0}^{-}(x,p)\Big).\end{split}

(69)

Here the asterisk denotes the quantities in PRF, in particular, we have
a^{2}=a^{2}_{*}=-{\bm{a}}^{2}_{*}
. We have also introduced the notation:
\gamma_{\pm}=\left(\pm Z+Z\cdot p\right)^{*}
,
{\bm{n}}={\bm{a}}_{*}/\sqrt{{\bm{a}}_{*}^{\,2}}
(with
{\bm{n}}^{2}=1
), and
\alpha^{i}=\widetilde{Z}^{i0}
. Since
\tanh\sqrt{{\bm{a}}_{*}^{\,2}}\in[-1,1]
, we can conclude that the first two lines in (
69
) are positive expressions. One can easily check that the third line of (
69
) is positive as well.

VI
VI. Selection rules for the polarization tensor

Practically, all studies of spin polarization that use a concept of the spin polarization tensor
\omega
(or a spin chemical potential
\Omega=\omega T
) assume that the components of
\omega
are small. This treatment is supported by the fact that the experimentally measured effects are in fact small, typically below 1%. Therefore, most of the time one includes only the leading (first-order) terms in
\omega
. The inclusion of higher-order terms has become only a topic of more recent works
[
56
,
57
,
60
,
59
,
58
]
, where the expansion in
\omega
is studied not only because of the practical reasons but also because of the interest in the internal consistency of the constructed formalism. In this section, we first comment on the most common approximation used in the literature, and then we switch to a discussion of more recent results that incorporate all orders in
\omega
.

VI.1
1. Traditional approach

As we discuss in the main text, in most of the analyzes performed so far, the crucial assumption made to deal with well-defined polarization was that the expression
\sqrt{{\bm{b}}_{\ast}\cdot{\bm{b}}_{\ast}\!-\!{\bm{e}}_{\ast}\cdot{\bm{e}}_{\ast}}
is small. In this case, the mean polarization is directly expressed by the vector
{\bm{b}}_{\ast}
whose explicit formula reads
[
12
]

\displaystyle{\bm{b}}_{\ast}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{m}\left(E_{\rm p}\,{\bm{b}}-{\bm{p}}\times{\bm{e}}-\frac{{\bm{p}}\cdot{\bm{b}}}{E_{\rm p}+m}{\bm{p}}\right).

(70)

It is worth emphasizing that an assumption of small
{\bm{e}}
and
{\bm{b}}
is insufficient to ensure a small
{\bm{b}}_{\ast}
. The first term in (
70
) includes the Lorentz gamma factor
E_{\rm p}/m
, which may be very large for relativistic particles and compensate for the smallness of
{\bm{b}}
. Thus, the assumption about the smallness of
{\bm{b}}_{\ast}
excludes from the analysis some portion of relativistic particles.

VI.2
2. New developments

The allowed region for the Lagrange multipliers appearing in our approach has recently been studied in more detail in
[
60
]
. The main result of that study is the formula that specifies the range of the electric and magnetic components of
\omega
,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{{{\bm{b}}^{\prime}}^{2}+{{\bm{e}}^{\prime}}^{2}+2|{\bm{e}}^{\prime}\times{\bm{b}}^{\prime}|}<\frac{m}{T}.

(71)

The primes denote vectors in the fluid rest frame, where
u^{\mu}=(1,0,0,0)
. If the cross product vanishes, Eq. (
71
) is reduced to that found in
[
59
]
. It is important to stress that (
71
) holds for all real systems that have finite densities of energy, linear and orbital momentum, and spin.

As explained in
[
60
]
, in practical calculations one may use a more restrictive formula

\omega_{\rm max}\,\sqrt{\frac{1+v}{1-v}}\,<\kappa\,\frac{m}{T}\,.

(72)

Here
\omega_{\rm max}
is the magnitude (absolute value) of the largest component of the spin polarization tensor
\omega_{\alpha\beta}
in the LAB frame, while
\kappa
is a numerical factor of the order of unity,
\kappa=2/\left(2+\sqrt{2}\right)
.
The proof of (
72
) is given below, after we make a few important comments:

i)

At the top RHIC energies, the radial flow of matter usually does not exceed half of the speed of light, while the polarization effects (quantified by the magnitude of
\omega_{\rm max}
) are at the level of a fraction of a percent. Moreover, our model is expected to work at the late stages of the collisions, where the temperatures are close to
0.15
GeV, and the commonly considered masses are
[
74
,
75
]
:
m=1.1
GeV (the
\Lambda
hyperon mass) or
m=0.5
GeV (the constituent strange quark mass). Using these numbers in Eq. (
72
), we observe that this condition is fulfilled very well. At lower energies, we deal with smaller values of flow and lower temperatures, which allow to fulfill (
72
) even with much larger values of
\omega_{\rm max}
.

ii)

The condition (
72
) is satisfactory to have all thermodynamic variables well defined. We checked this property by numerical calculations of different variables, such as the baryon or energy density. Inequality (
72
) can be relaxed (larger values of
\kappa
can be allowed), if the fields
{\bm{b}}
and
{\bm{e}}
exhibit certain symmetries. For example, if one of them is zero,
\kappa=2/\sqrt{3}
.

iii)

The factor
\sqrt{(1+v)/(1-v)}
in Eq. (
37
) can be naturally interpreted as the longitudinal relativistic Doppler effect that increases “frequency”
\omega_{\rm max}
to that “seen” in the local fluid rest frame.

iv)

Last but not least, we stress that (
72
) includes only hydrodynamic variables and is different from the previously used conditions that mix hydrodynamic variables with single-particle properties (as discussed in the subsection above).

Let us now turn to the proof of (
72
). The new definition of the Wigner function includes an exponential factor that may possibly diverge for large values of the three-momentum
|{\bm{p}}|
. It has the form

\displaystyle\exp\left(-\frac{p_{\mu}u^{\mu}}{T}+\frac{1}{2m}\sqrt{-{\tilde{\omega}}_{\mu\alpha}p^{\alpha}{\tilde{\omega}}^{\mu}_{\beta}p^{\beta}}\right)=\exp\left(-\,\frac{p_{\mu}u^{\mu}}{T}\right)\exp\left(\,\frac{1}{2m}\sqrt{-{\tilde{\omega}}_{\mu\alpha}p^{\alpha}{\tilde{\omega}}^{\mu}_{\beta}p^{\beta}}\right).

(73)

The divergence appears if the damping by the temperature part is not sufficient to suppress the second term. Since this expression is Lorentz-invariant, we can first consider the LAB frame where
u^{\mu}=\gamma(1,{\bm{v}})

and

\displaystyle{\tilde{\omega}}_{\mu\nu}=\begin{bmatrix}0&b^{1}&b^{2}&b^{3}\\
-b^{1}&0&e^{3}&-e^{2}\\
-b^{2}&-e^{3}&0&e^{1}\\
-b^{3}&e^{2}&-e^{1}&0\end{bmatrix}.

(74)

We note that to switch from
\omega_{\mu\nu}
to the dual tensor
{\tilde{\omega}}_{\mu\nu}
, one replaces
{\bm{e}}
by
{\bm{b}}
and
{\bm{b}}
by
-{\bm{e}}
.

In the next step, it is convenient to transform
u
and
p
to the local rest frame of the fluid element; hence, we write

\displaystyle u^{\mu}=\Lambda^{\mu}_{\,\,\,\alpha^{\prime}}u^{\alpha^{\prime}},\qquad p^{\mu}=\Lambda^{\mu}_{\,\,\,\alpha^{\prime}}p^{\alpha^{\prime}},\qquad p_{\mu}=\Lambda_{\mu}^{\,\,\,\alpha^{\prime}}p_{\alpha^{\prime}},

(75)

where
\Lambda^{\mu}_{\,\,\,\alpha^{\prime}}=\gamma\lambda^{\mu}_{\,\,\,\alpha^{\prime}}
, with

\displaystyle\lambda^{\mu}_{\,\,\,\alpha^{\prime}}=\begin{bmatrix}1&\quad\frac{\sqrt{\gamma^{2}-1}}{\gamma}{\hat{v}}^{1}&\quad\frac{\sqrt{\gamma^{2}-1}}{\gamma}{\hat{v}}^{2}&\quad\frac{\sqrt{\gamma^{2}-1}}{\gamma}{\hat{v}}^{3}\\[5.0pt]
\frac{\sqrt{\gamma^{2}-1}}{\gamma}{\hat{v}}^{1}&\quad\frac{1}{\gamma}+\frac{\gamma-1}{\gamma}{\hat{v}}^{1}{\hat{v}}^{1}&\quad\frac{\gamma-1}{\gamma}{\hat{v}}^{1}{\hat{v}}^{2}&\quad\frac{\gamma-1}{\gamma}{\hat{v}}^{1}{\hat{v}}^{3}\\[5.0pt]
\frac{\sqrt{\gamma^{2}-1}}{\gamma}{\hat{v}}^{2}&\quad\frac{\gamma-1}{\gamma}{\hat{v}}^{2}{\hat{v}}^{1}&\quad\frac{1}{\gamma}+\frac{\gamma-1}{\gamma}{\hat{v}}^{2}{\hat{v}}^{2}&\quad\frac{\gamma-1}{\gamma}{\hat{v}}^{2}{\hat{v}}^{3}\\[5.0pt]
\frac{\sqrt{\gamma^{2}-1}}{\gamma}{\hat{v}}^{3}&\quad\frac{\gamma-1}{\gamma}{\hat{v}}^{3}{\hat{v}}^{1}&\quad\frac{\gamma-1}{\gamma}{\hat{v}}^{3}{\hat{v}}^{2}&\quad\frac{1}{\gamma}+\frac{\gamma-1}{\gamma}{\hat{v}}^{3}{\hat{v}}^{3}\end{bmatrix}.

(76)

Here
{\hat{\bm{v}}}
is the unit three-vector parallel to
{\bm{v}}
,
{\hat{\bm{v}}}={\bm{v}}/|{\bm{v}}|\equiv{\bm{v}}/v
. Since we extracted
\gamma
from
\Lambda^{\mu}_{\,\,\,\alpha^{\prime}}
, all elements of the matrix
\lambda^{\mu}_{\,\,\,\alpha^{\prime}}
are finite and bounded (
1/\gamma<1
,
\sqrt{\gamma^{2}-1}/\gamma<1
,
(\gamma-1)/\gamma<1
,
|{\hat{v}}^{i}|<1
). We can perform a similar transformation to the matrix
{\tilde{\omega}}_{\mu\nu}
by extracting the modulus of its largest element, namely,

\displaystyle{\tilde{\omega}}_{\mu\nu}=\begin{bmatrix}0&b^{1}&b^{2}&b^{3}\\
-b^{1}&0&e^{3}&-e^{2}\\
-b^{2}&-e^{3}&0&e^{1}\\
-b^{3}&e^{2}&-e^{1}&0\end{bmatrix}=\omega_{\rm max}\begin{bmatrix}0&{\hat{b}}^{1}&{\hat{b}}^{2}&{\hat{b}}^{3}\\
-{\hat{b}}^{1}&0&{\hat{e}}^{3}&-{\hat{e}}^{2}\\
-{\hat{b}}^{2}&-{\hat{e}}^{3}&0&{\hat{e}}^{1}\\
-{\hat{b}}^{3}&{\hat{e}}^{2}&-{\hat{e}}^{1}&0\end{bmatrix}\equiv\omega_{\rm max}\,{\hat{\omega}}_{\mu\nu},

(77)

where
{\hat{\bm{b}}}={\bm{b}}/\omega_{\rm max}
and
{\hat{\bm{e}}}={\bm{e}}/\omega_{\rm max}
(so we have
|{\hat{b}}^{i}|<1
and
|{\hat{e}}^{i}|<1
). Thus, similarly to
\lambda^{\mu}_{\,\,\,\alpha^{\prime}}
, the matrix
{\hat{\omega}}_{\mu\nu}
contains bounded elements.

Using the notation introduced above, we can rewrite the argument of the exponential function as

\displaystyle-\frac{E_{\rm p^{\prime}}}{T}+\frac{\gamma\,\omega_{\rm max}}{2m}\sqrt{-{\hat{\omega}}_{\mu\alpha}\lambda^{\alpha}_{\alpha^{\prime}}p^{\alpha^{\prime}}{\hat{\omega}}^{\mu}_{\beta}\lambda^{\beta}_{\beta^{\prime}}p^{\beta^{\prime}}}.

(78)

Here
E_{\rm p^{\prime}}=\sqrt{m^{2}+{\bm{p}}^{\prime^{2}}}
and
p^{\mu^{\prime}}=(E_{\rm p^{\prime}},{\bm{p}}^{\prime})
. In the limit
|{\bm{p}}^{\prime}|\to\infty
, we can use the approximations:
E_{\rm p^{\prime}}=|{\bm{p}}^{\prime}|
and
p^{\mu^{\prime}}=|{\bm{p}}^{\prime}|(1,{\hat{\bm{p}}}^{\prime})
, where
{\hat{\bm{p}}}^{\prime}={\bm{p}}^{\prime}/|{\bm{p}}^{\prime}|
. Hence, for very large values of
|{\bm{p}}^{\prime}|
, which determine the convergence of thermodynamic integrals, we can rewrite (
78
) as

\displaystyle\left(-\frac{1}{T}+\frac{\gamma\,\omega_{\rm max}}{2m}\sqrt{-{\hat{\omega}}_{\mu\alpha}\lambda^{\alpha}_{\alpha^{\prime}}{\hat{p}}^{\alpha^{\prime}}{\hat{\omega}}^{\mu}_{\beta}\lambda^{\beta}_{\beta^{\prime}}{\hat{p}}^{\beta^{\prime}}}\right)|{\bm{p}}^{\prime}|,

(79)

where we introduced the notation
{\hat{p}}^{\,\mu^{\prime}}=(1,{\hat{\bm{p}}}^{\prime})
.

Since all the elements entering the square root
\mathcal{R}\equiv\sqrt{-{\hat{\omega}}_{\mu\alpha}\lambda^{\alpha}_{\alpha^{\prime}}{\hat{p}}^{\alpha^{\prime}}{\hat{\omega}}^{\mu}_{\beta}\lambda^{\beta}_{\beta^{\prime}}{\hat{p}}^{\beta^{\prime}}}
are bounded, the expression is bounded from above. To find its upper bound, we combined numerical and analytical methods. We performed a 10-dimensional Monte Carlo maximization (with respect to
\hat{b}^{i}
,
\hat{e}^{i}
, and four angles defining the unit vectors
\hat{\bm{v}}
and
\hat{\bm{p}}
) with iterative shrinkage of the probed regions of the parameter space that were found to contain the highest values of
\mathcal{R}
. As the regions appeared to converge toward certain well-defined points, we constructed a surmise that those points indeed correspond to the maxima, and we confirmed it by calculating the partial derivatives of
\mathcal{R}
at the given points. The details will be presented in a separate work.
We found that the maximum of
\mathcal{R}
is equal to
(2+\sqrt{2})(1+v)
. Consequently, a sufficient condition for convergence of thermodynamic integrals is

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{T}+\frac{\gamma\,\omega_{\rm max}}{2m}(2+\sqrt{2})(1+v)=-\frac{1}{T}+\frac{\omega_{\rm max}}{m}\frac{(2+\sqrt{2})}{2}\sqrt{\frac{1+v}{1-v}}<0

(80)

or

\displaystyle\omega_{\rm max}\sqrt{\frac{1+v}{1-v}}\,<\,\frac{2}{2+\sqrt{2}}\,\frac{m}{T}\,\equiv\,\kappa\frac{m}{T}.

(81)

This expression corresponds to (
72
).
