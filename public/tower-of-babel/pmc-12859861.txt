Loss-of-function mutation of two phenylpropanoid pathway genes PAL1 and PAL2, coupled with MYB46 upregulation, increases fermentable sugar yield and ethanol production
Authors: Kihwan Kim, Han-Eol Jeong, Kyung-Hwan Han, Won-Chan Kim
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe recalcitrant nature of lignocellulosic biomass restricts the accessibility of hydrolytic enzymes to cellulose and hemicellulose. Although reducing lignin content improves the conversion of biomass to fermentable sugar, excessive reduction of lignin compromises cell wall integrity, lowering biomass yield.ResultsThis study aims to develop biotechnological means to genetically engineer the composition of lignocellulosic feedstock biomass for enhanced biomass conversion efficiency while minimizing adverse impacts of the genetic manipulation on plant growth. In order to test the concept, we produced transgenic Arabidopsis plants with reduced lignin content but enhanced cellulose and hemicellulose accumulation by combining CRISPR/Cas9-mediated knockout of two key phenylpropanoid biosynthetic genes PAL1 and PAL2 with the overexpression of MYB46, a central regulator of secondary wall biosynthesis. 35S::MYB46/pal1pal2 reduced lignin content by 36.2% while increasing cellulose content by 24.1% and hemicellulose content by 52.9%, compared to the wild type. In addition, 35S::MYB46/pal1pal2 increased cellulose content by 9.7% and hemicellulose content by 16.5%, compared to the pal1pal2 mutant. Enzymatic saccharification assays revealed that the biomass from the 35S::MYB46/pal1pal2 plants exhibited increased saccharification efficiency and glucose yield, compared to the wild type or the pal1pal2 mutant. Yeast-based ethanol fermentation assays further revealed that the elevated glucose yield resulted in a significant increase in fermentable sugar availability and ethanol production.ConclusionThis pathway-specific genetic engineering strategy provides a sustainable approach to bioenergy production by enhancing secondary cell wall integrity, compensating for reduced lignin through increased cellulose and hemicellulose accumulation, and improving bioethanol yields while minimizing adverse impacts on plant growth.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13036-025-00615-8.Keywords: Bioenergy, CRISPR/Cas9, Lignocellulose, MYB46, Phenylpropanoid pathway

Introduction

Meeting the global energy demands for modern standards of living has become significant challenge. Finite fossil fuel resources have accelerated the search for renewable and sustainable energy alternatives [1, 2]. Plant biomass (i.e., lignocellulosic biomass) is a highly abundant and renewable natural resource for bioenergy generation [3–5]. However, the structural diversity and complexity of cell wall present technical and scientific challenges in the efficient utilization of plant biomass [6–8]. Genetic modification approaches to modify the lignin profile of the biomass feedstock could offer potential solutions to enhance biomass processing. However, it is crucial to carefully consider potential negative effects (e.g., compromised plant responses to stresses, growth penalty) of such alterations in lignocellulosic biomass composition [9–11].

Phenylalanine ammonia-lyase (PAL, E 4.3.1.5) catalyzes the initial metabolic step of the phenylpropanoid pathway, in which L-Phenylalanine (L-Phe) is deaminated and diverted from primary metabolism to form trans-cinnamic acid (t-CA). The resulting t-CA subsequently serves as a precursor for wide array of phenolic metabolites [12, 13]. Among the four PAL isoforms (PAL1-PAL4) in Arabidopsis thaliana, PAL1 (AT2G37040) and PAL2 (AT3G53260) have been identified as the principal isoforms contributing to monolignol biosynthesis, and loss-of-function mutations of PAL1 and PAL2 results in a decreased in lignin content, an altered lignin monomer profile characterized by an increased syringyl-to-guaiacyl (S/G) ratio, and weakened of secondary cell wall architecture, changes that are accompanied by compensatory adjustments in phenylpropanoid, carbohydrate, and amino acid metabolic networks [14]. In addition, PAL1 and PAL2 are transcriptionally regulated in response to environmental factor nitrogen and temperature [15], and loss-of-function mutations of PAL1 and PAL2 lead to enhanced sensitivity to UV-B radiation and increased susceptibility to pathogen infection [16]. These studies highlight that targeting PAL1 and PAL2 at initial step of the phenylpropanoid pathway is a promising strategy for reducing lignin content and improving biomass degradability, but the broader metabolic and physiological implications associated with the loss-of-function mutation of PAL1 and PAL2 must be carefully considered.

To compensate the metabolic and physiological implications associated with the loss-of-function mutation of PAL1 and PAL2, we focused on the plant secondary cell wall, which constitutes the majority of lignocellulosic biomass and is primarily composed of cellulose, hemicellulose, and lignin. Secondary cell wall formation is achieved through the successive deposition of cellulose, hemicellulose, and lignin [17–19]. This process is governed by transcriptional activation of the biosynthetic genes involved in cellulose, hemicellulose, and lignin, ensuring the proper assembly and mechanical reinforcement of the mature secondary cell wall [20, 21]. In our previous studies, the MYB46 (AT5G12870) was identified as a central regulator of secondary cell wall formation, as it directly upregulates cellulose biosynthetic genes (CesA4, CesA7, and CesA8) [22], hemicellulose biosynthetic genes (IRX8, IRX9, and IRX14) [23], and lignin biosynthetic genes (PAL, C4H, 4CL, HCT, C3H, CCoAOMT, F5H, CCR, and CAD) [23]. Genome-wide promoter analysis has further revealed that theses secondary cell wall biosynthetic genes contain one or more MYB46-binding cis-acting regulatory motifs (M46RE) [24], supporting the evidence that these secondary cell wall biosynthetic genes are directly regulated at the transcriptional gene level by MYB46 transcription factor. Moreover, MYB46 orthologs in poplar [25, 26], rice [27], maize [27], and switchgrass [28] have been demonstrated to function as master regulators of secondary cell wall formation. Notably, although MYB46 overexpression causes excessive lignin deposition that leads to severe growth impairment [22, 29], this growth impairment is mitigated by loss of function of 4-coumarate-CoA ligase 1 (4CL1), a gene in the phenylpropanoid pathway [30]. These findings support the evidence that the growth impairment causing by MYB46 overexpression is primarily attributable to excessive lignin deposition rather than to increased cellulose or hemicellulose deposition and further indicates that loss of function of phenylpropanoid pathway-related genes is sufficient to mitigate the growth impairment causing by MYB46 overexpression.

We hypothesize that loss-of-function mutation of PAL1 and PAL2 provide a compensatory mechanism to suppress the lignin deposition and growth impairment induced by MYB46 overexpression. Simultaneously, the increased cellulose and hemicellulose deposition induced by MYB46 overexpression is expected to restore secondary cell wall integrity and biomass yield, counteracting the adverse effects associated with lignin reduction. Furthermore, to maximize the efficiency of engineered biomass, we targeted the pal1pal2 double mutant rather than each single mutant, because simultaneous loss of function of both genes results in a more pronounced reduction of lignin content and substantially higher S/G ratio, both of which influence saccharification efficiency [14, 31]. To test the hypothesis, we produced transgenic plants with overexpression of MYB46 in the background of pal1pal2 mutant (35 S::MYB46/pal1pal2) and examined overall growth phenotype, histochemical staining of the inflorescence stems, and expression of secondary cell wall biosynthetic genes in the resulting plants. We further conducted enzymatic saccharification and yeast-based ethanol fermentation assays to evaluate the saccharification conversion efficiency and ethanol yield from the engineered lignocellulosic biomass. Taken together, genetic metabolic engineering strategy to enhance cellulose and hemicellulose deposition through MYB46 overexpression in the pal1pal2 double mutant background provides a promising strategy for improving the quality of lignocellulosic biomass for biofuel production, while simultaneously mitigating the adverse effects associated with lignin reduction.

Materials and methods

Design the common gRNA target sequence and CRISPR/Cas9 binary vector construction

The sequences of PAL1 (AT2G37040) and PAL2 (AT3G53260) were obtained from The Arabidopsis Information Resource (TAIR; https://www.arabidopsis.org). To design guide RNA (gRNA) targeting two PAL genes (PAL1 and PAL2) at once, we employed the web tools of CRISPR-P version 2.0 online software (http://crispr.hzau.edu.cn/CRISPR2/) [32]. A common gRNA target sequence was selected in the second exon of both PAL1 and PAL2 (AGGAACAAGGCGATTCACGGTGG; the PAM sequence is underlined) (Supplementary Fig. 1).

To construct sgRNA modules targeted to PAL genes for CRISPR/Cas9-mediated genome editing in A. thaliana, a synthetic gRNA was inserted into the pKSE401 binary vector, a CRISPR/Cas9 plant expression vector, adopted in a previous method [33]. The construction process involved mixing equal volume of 100 pmol/µl PALs_gRNA_F (attgAGGAACAAGGCGATTCACGG; sticky ended of BsaI sequences are underlined and italicized) and PALs_gRNA_R (aaacCCGTGAATCGCCTTGTTCCT; sticky ended of BsaI sequences are underlined and italicized), incubating the mixture at 95℃ for 5 min, and then slowly cooling it to 25℃ to produce a double-stranded insert was integrated into pKSE401 binary vector using the Golden Gate Assembly method [34] (Supplementary Fig. 2).

Preparation of plant materials and growth conditions

A. thaliana, ecotype Columbia (Col-0), served as the experimental model for this study. To generate pal1pal2 mutants, Agrobacterium tumefaciens strain GV3101 harboring pKSE401:PALs_RNA binary vector was introduced into both A. thaliana Col-0 (WT) and 35 S::MYB46/Col-0 (Overexpression line #8 [22]) by using Agrobacterium-mediated transformation [35]. Briefly, a single A. tumefaciens strain GV3101 harboring the pKSE401:PALs_gRNA binary vector was cultured to a final OD600nm of 0.8 and then suspended in floral dip inoculation medium containing 5% (w/v) sucrose and 0.05% (w/v) Silwet L-77. The floral dip inoculation medium was used for the transformation of A. thaliana Col-0 and 35 S::MYB46/Col-0.

The collected candidate transgenic seeds were grown on medium containing kanamycin (50 µg/mL) to select the T1 generation of transgenic plants (T1 plants) from the T0 generation of transgenic plants (T0 plants) [36]. Briefly, the collected T0 seeds were sterilized by 70% (v/v) ethanol for 1 min, followed by seed disinfectant (25% (v/v) bleach and 0.01% (v/v) Triton X-100) for 5 min. After that, the seeds were washed with sterile distilled water five times. The surface-sterilized seeds were sown on 0.5 × Murashige and Skoog (1/2 MS) medium containing 1% (w/v) sucrose, 0.6% (w/v) phytoagar, kanamycin (50 µg/mL), and cold-treatment in the dark at 4℃ for 3 days. After cold-treatment, seeds were transferred to a growth chamber and exposed to continuous white light (100 µmol/m2/s) for 6 h at 23℃ for germination. The plate was sown with seeds and then kept in a dark condition for 48 h at 23℃. Finally, the seed in the plate was incubated for 24 h at 23℃ in order to select T1 plants that had expanded green cotyledons.

For phenotype analysis of plants, both wild type and mutant seeds were subjected to treatment in the dark at 4℃ for 3 days before germination. Subsequently, the plants were cultivated on soil in a growth chamber under long day conditions (LD, 16 h light/8 h dark photoperiod) while maintaining a light intensity of approximately 100 µmol/m2/s at a constant temperature of 23℃.

Identification of the pal1pal2 mutant and validation of CRISPR/Cas9-mediated target mutation

To identify the pal1pal2 mutation, gDNA was extracted from the leaves of the candidate of the pal1pal2 mutants and the 35 S::MYB46/pal1pal2 plants, following the gDNA preparation procedure for the respective plants [37]. The extracted gDNA of each mutant and wild type (WT) was subjected to polymerase chain reaction (PCR) amplification of the adjacent target site using specific primers (Table S1). The PCR products were subsequently verified by T7E1 assay [38, 39]. For the T7E1 assay, each PCR product was subjected to denaturation at 95℃ for 5 min, followed by annealing with a temperature ramp of -2℃ per second to 85℃ for 5 s and − 0.1℃ per second to 25℃ for 10 min. The resulting heteroduplex DNA was then digested with T7E1 enzyme at 37℃ for 2 h and subsequently electrophoresed on a 2% (w/v) agarose gel.

To conduct a detailed analysis of the CRISPR/Cas9-mediated target mutation, the adjacent target site was amplified through PCR using the gDNA extracted from the chosen homozygous mutants. To ensure accuracy in Sanger sequencing, high-fidelity PrimeSTAR® HS DNA polymerase was utilized to minimize errors during PCR amplification that could lead to the generation of multiple PCR products. Subsequently, the PCR products were subjected to Sanger sequencing. To prevent misinterpretation of the Sanger sequencing results due to the presence of multiple PCR products, the Chromas software was used, ensuring a clear presentation of nucleotide peaks in the chromatogram.

PAL activity assay

The Phenylalanine ammonia-lyase (PAL) activity was determined by the conversion of L-phenylalanine to trans-cinnamic acid (t-CA) and the accumulation of CA was quantified at 290 nm using a Ultrospec 8000 UV/VIS spectrophotometer as previous study with minor modifications [40]. PAL activity was measured in stems from 6-week-old wild types (control), pal1pal2 mutants, and 35 S::MYB46/pal1pal2 plants. Stem samples from 6-week-old WT, pal1pal2 mutant, and 35 S::MYB46/pal1pal2 plant were immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen (LN2) and ground to a fine powder using a pre-chilled mortar and pestle. The powdered samples were homogenized in 1 mL of protein extraction buffer (200 mM Tri-HCl, 150 mM KCl, 0.8% (w/v) Triton X-100, 18mM β-mercaptoethanol, 50 mM EDTA, 1mM PMSF (phenylmethylsulfonyl fluoride), pH 8.8). The homogenate was centrifuged at 13,000 × g for 15 min at 4℃, the supernatant was collected. For buffer replacement, the supernatant was transferred to an Amicon® Ultra Centrifugal Filter, 30 kDa molecular weight cutoff (MWCO) and centrifuged at 4,000 × g for 15 min at 4℃. The retained fraction was washed three times with 100 mM sodium borate-HCl (pH 8.8). The protein concentration was determined using the Bradford assay [41]. The reaction mixture consisted of 20 µL of buffer-exchanged crude extract (10–12 µg of protein), 460 µL of 100 mM sodium borate-HCl (pH 8.8), and 15 mM L-phenylalanine. The reaction mixture was incubated at 37℃ for 3 h and terminated by adding 20% (v/v) trichloroacetic acid (TCA). After centrifugation at 13,000 × g for 15 min 4℃, the resulting supernatant was used for CA quantification.

Histochemical analysis of inflorescence stems

To investigate the engineered lignocellulosic composition, 3 cm segments from the bottom of the inflorescence stem were collected from 6-week-old wild types, pal1pal2 mutants, and 35S::MYB46/pal1pal2 plants. The segments were fixed overnight at 4℃ in the dark in FAA solution containing 50% (v/v) ethanol, 5% (v/v) glacial acetic acid, and 4% (v/v) formaldehyde. Fixed samples were dehydrated through a graded ethanol series up to 100% (v/v) ethanol, then gradually infiltrated with xylene until saturated xylene was reached. The infiltrated samples were embedded in paraffin and sectioned to a thickness of 10 μm using a microtome (E-Microtome M-2065). Specimens were mounted on glass slides, deparaffinized with xylene, and rehydrated with distilled water.

The specimens were subjected to Phloroglucinol-HCl (Wiesner) staining for lignin visualization and Calcofluor white staining for cellulose visualization [42]. Wiesner staining was conducted using a modified Phloroglucinol-HCl solution (1% (w/v) Phloroglucinol, 66% (v/v) ethanol, and 4 N HCl). Calcofluor white staining was conducted using 0.1% (w/v) Calcofluor White M2R in combination with 10% (w/v) potassium hydroxide solution. Both staining procedures were conducted for the same duration for each specimen and then rinsed three times with distilled water. Representative stained specimens were photographed using a Leica ICC50 HD light microscope for lignin visualization and an Olympus BX52F2 fluorescence microscope for cellulose visualization, with excitation at 355 nm and emission detected between 300 and 440 nm.

Cell wall crystalline cellulose content determination

Cell wall crystalline cellulose contents were determined using the modified Updegraff method [43, 44]. Segments of 15 cm from the bottom of the inflorescence stem of 6-week-old wild types, pal1pal2 mutants, and 35 S::MYB46/pal1pla2 plants were ground in liquid nitrogen (LN2), and the fine powder was sequentially washed with protein solubilization buffer (PBS) (50 mM Tris-HCl, 0.5 mM EDTA, 10% (w/v) SDS, pH 8.8), distilled water, 70% (v/v) ethanol, chloroform: methanol (1:1, v/v), and fresh saturated acetone, followed by drying at 37℃. The dried samples (15 mg) were incubated with 1.5 mL of Updegraff reagent (acetic acid: nitric acid: water, 8:1:2, v/v) at 100℃ for 30 min. After centrifugation, the pellets were washed three times with distilled water and fresh saturated acetone, followed by drying at 37℃. The washed pellets were hydrolyzed with 72% (v/v) sulfuric acid at 25℃ for 1 h. The hydrolysate was reacted with Anthrone reagent, and the released glucose was quantified by measuring absorbance at 620 nm. Cellulose content was calculated by comparing the measured absorbance values to a standard curve prepared with known glucose standards.

Lignin content determination

To evaluate the lignin composition of the lignocellulosic biomass, 15 cm segments were excised from the bottom of the inflorescence stems of 6-week-old wild types, pal1pal2 mutants, and 35 S::MYB46/pal1pla2 plants. The collected samples were oven-dried at 60℃ and then ground into fine powder. The powdered samples were washed three times with 70% (w/v) ethanol, followed by sequential incubation in water: ethanol: acetone (1:2:1, v/v/v), ethanol: acetone (1:1, v/v), and ethanol: acetone (1:3, v/v) for 30 min each. The samples were then washed three times with saturated acetone. Finally, the samples were incubated in fresh saturated acetone in an oven at 60℃ until completely dried to obtain the alcohol-insoluble residue (AIR). The AIR was extracted with an ethanol-benzene mixed solution (1:2, v/v) and then treated with a glycol ether-sulfuric acid mixed solution (1:2, w/v). Lignin content was quantified using a modified Klason lignin method [45], following the TAPPI T 222 om-15 standard protocol. Total lignin content was calculated as the sum of acid-insoluble lignin (Klason lignin) and acid-soluble lignin (ASL).

Monosaccharide content determination

To evaluate the monosaccharide composition of the lignocellulosic biomass, the AIR was hydrolyzed, and the solubilized monosaccharides were derivatized to their corresponding alditol acetates following a described method [46]. The AIR was hydrolyzed with 2 M Trifluoroacetic acid (TFA) at 121℃ for 90 min. The hydrolysate was washed with isopropanol to remove residual TFA and subsequently dried under a gentle stream of air. The released monosaccharides were reduced to their corresponding alditols using freshly prepared sodium borohydride solution in 1 M ammonium hydroxide at room temperature for 90 min, followed by neutralization with glacial acetic acid. Borate residues were removed by successive evaporation with acetic acid: methanol (1:9, v/v) and methanol washes. The resulting alditols were acetylated with acetic anhydride and pyridine at 121℃ for 20 min. The alditol acetates were extracted with ethyl acetate and water, followed by phase separation. The organic layer was analyzed using an Agilent HP-6890 Gas Chromatography equipped with a flame ionization detector (GC-FID) and a SP-2330 column (30 m × 0.32 mm × 0.20 μm).

Total RNA extraction, cDNA synthesis, and qRT-PCR

The segments located 0.5 cm from the bottom of the inflorescence stem of plants were ground into a fine powder with a pestle under liquid nitrogen (LN2) in an E-tube. Total RNA was then extracted from the collected inflorescence stem samples using TRIzol reagent. The concentration of total RNA was determined by measuring the A260/A280 ratio using a NanoDrop ND-2000 spectrophotometer. Total RNA was treated with RNase-free DNase I to eliminate genomic DNA contamination, and then 2 µg of purified total RNA was reverse transcribed into first-strand complementary DNA (first-strand cDNA) using the SuperScript III Reverse transcriptase. The synthesized first-strand cDNA was used as a template for gene expression analysis.

For qRT-PCR, 1 µL of synthesized first-strand cDNA was used as template in a reaction containing PowerUp™ SYBR™ Green Master Mix to quantify double-stranded DNA synthesis for the real-time quantitative reverse transcription PCR reaction. Expression levels were normalized to the ACT8 (AT1G49240) reference gene. The details of the primers used in this study are provided in Table S1. All experiments were conducted with three independent biological replicates.

Preparation of lignocellulosic biomass and pretreatment of acid and alkaline

The 15 cm segments were excised from the bottom of the inflorescence stems of 6-week-old wild types, pal1pal2 mutants, and 35 S::MYB46/pal1pla2 plants and then trimmed into approximately 3 mm segments, subsequently dried at 60℃ until each segment reached a constant weight for further analysis. Acid and alkaline pretreatments were conducted using a modified version of the described method [47–49]. Dried segments were subjected to distinct conditions, with the acid-treated segments incubated with 1 mL of 1 N HCl per 10 mg segments at 90℃ for 3 h, alkaline-treated segments incubated with 1 mL of 62.5 mM NaOH per 10 mg of segments at 90℃ for 3 h, and non-treated segments incubated with 1 mL of distilled water per 10 mg of segments at 90℃ for 3 h. Following pretreatment, the soluble extract was discarded, and the segments were rinsed three times with distilled water. The AIR from each section was prepared according to the method described under lignin content determination.

Enzymatic saccharification assay

The enzymatic saccharification assay was conducted according to the described method [50, 51]. The 15 mg of AIR was added to 25 mL of 0.05 M citrate buffer (pH 4.8), and then 0.15 FPU (filter paper unit) of an enzyme mixture, consisting of a 5:3 ratio of commercial cellulase from Trichoderma reesei ATCC 26,921 (Sigma, Cat. No. C8546) and commercial β-glucosidases from Aspergillus niger (Sigma, Cat. No. 49291), was added to each sample to initiate enzymatic hydrolysis. The enzymatic saccharification reaction was conducted at 50℃ with shaking at 120 rpm for 120 h. At specified time points (4, 8, 12, 24, 48, 72, 96, and 120 h), 200 µL of the reaction mixture was collected and immediately boiled at 80℃ for 10 min to inactivate the enzymes. The boiled samples were centrifuged at 13,000 rpm for 20 min, and the supernatants were used to quantify glucose content. At the final time point, the remaining reaction mixtures were boiled at 80℃ for 10 min and stored at -80℃ for subsequent ethanol fermentation experiments.

The supernatant collected at each time point during the enzymatic saccharification reaction was analyzed for glucose content using the Glucose (GO) assay kit, which employs the coupled glucose oxidase-peroxidase (GOD-POD) method [52, 53]. The absorbance of the pink-colored compound, which is proportional to the glucose content, was measured at 240 nm using a Ultrospec 8000 UV/VIS spectrophotometer. Glucose contents were calculated by comparing the measured absorbance values to a standard curve prepared with known glucose standards.

Yeast-based ethanol fermentation

The Saccharomyces cerevisiae strain D452-2, which is capable of co-utilizing glucose and xylose as a carbon source [54, 55], was employed for ethanol fermentation. For the preparation of the seed culture, S. cerevisiae strain D452-2 was cultivated in YPD medium containing 20 g/L glucose and harvested during the exponential growth phase. The harvested cells were washed three times with a saline buffer to remove residual glucose from the YPD medium and then concentrated to an OD600 nm of 5.0 per milliliter (mL).

For anaerobic ethanol fermentation, 1 mL of the concentrated seed culture was inoculated into 4 mL of YP medium supplemented with 5 µL of 0.1% Resazurin (as redox indicator), 30 µL of 25% Na2S⋅9H2O solution (as a reducing agent). Additionally, 1 mL of the mixture from the enzymatic saccharification reaction, concentrated from an initial volume of 20 mL, was added to each vial. Each vial was incubated at 30℃ with shaking at 150 rpm. At specified time points (2, 4, 8, 12, 24, and 48 h), the fermentation mixtures were collected using syringes and centrifuged to obtain the supernatants, which were then stored at -80℃ for subsequent ethanol quantification.

The supernatant collected at each time point during the yeast-based ethanol fermentation was analyzed for ethanol content using the Ethanol assay kit (Megazyme), which employs the coupled alcohol dehydrogenase-aldehyde dehydrogenase method [56]. The NADH concentration was measured at an absorbance of 340 nm using a Ultrospec 8000 UV/VIS spectrophotometer. Ethanol content was calculated by comparing the measured absorbance values to a standard curve prepared with known ethanol standards.

Statistical analysis

All experiments were conducted with three independent biological replicates and analyzed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) using SAS v9.4 (SAS Institute Inc.). Multiple comparisons were conducted using Tukey’s honestly signification difference test (p < 0.05). The data were represented as mean ± standard deviation (SD).

Results

Generation and molecular validation of pal1pal2 mutant and 35S::MYB46/pal1pal2 plants

PAL1 and PAL2, members of the phenylalanine ammonia-lyase (PAL) gene family in A. thaliana, have been identified as the principal isoforms contributing to lignin biosynthesis [57], and only the pal1pal2 mutations have exhibited a significant reduction of lignin content [14, 48]. Based on these findings, we hypothesized that the combination of loss-of-function mutation of the two key genes (PAL1 and PAL2) and MYB46 overexpression may enhances saccharification efficiency by simultaneously reducing lignin content and increasing cellulose and hemicellulose accumulation, while alleviating the negative effects of reduced lignin content. To test this hypothesis, pal1pal2 mutants were generated in both A. thaliana Col-0 (WT) and 35S::MYB46/Col-0 (overexpression line #8 [22]). Homozygous pal1pal2 mutants and 35S::MYB46/pal1pal2 plants were identified using T7 endonuclease I (T7E1) assay (Supplementary Fig. 3). Homozygous mutations in both PAL1 and PAL2 were confirmed in pal1pal2 mutant lines #3 and #4, and in 35S::MYB46/pal1pal2 plant lines #1, #3, #4, and #5. The presence or absence of MYB46 expression cassette and CRISPR/Cas9 expression cassette was verified by genomic PCR (Fig. 1A). The absence of the CRISPR/Cas9 expression cassette excluded the possibility of prolonged zCas9 and gRNA transcription, minimizing the risk of off-target genome editing. In addition, the presence of the MYB46 expression cassette indicated the insertion of the MYB46 expression cassette. Transcriptional analysis revealed that MYB46 expression was highest in the 35S::MYB46/pal1pal2 line #1 (Fig. 1B and Table S2). Transcripts levels of PAL1, PAL2, and PAL4 (a direct target of MYB46) were measured (Fig. 1C-E and Table S2). Transcript levels of PAL1 and PAL4 in the pal1pal2 mutant line #3 were not significantly different from those in the WT. However, both PAL1 and PAL4 were upregulated in 35S::MYB46/pal1pal2 line #1. To precisely identify the mutated target sequences induced by the CRISPR/Cas9-mediated genome editing system, the Sanger sequencing was conducted on the target site of the pal1pal2 mutant line #3 and 35S::MYB46/pal1pal2 line #1 (Fig. 1F, G). Both mutant lines exhibited a single-nucleotide insertion, with thymine (T) inserted at the PAL1 target site (5’-AGGAACAAGGCGATTCACGG-3’) and adenine (A) at the PAL2 target site, each located three base pairs upstream of the protospacer adjacent motif (PAM) sequences. These mutation patterns were consistent with previous studies indicating that the HNH and RuvC nuclease domains of Cas9 induce double-strand breaks (DSBs) in the target DNA strand at three base pairs upstream of PAM sequences [58–60]. These insertions cause frameshift mutations that introduced premature stop codons, leading to a loss of enzymatic activity in PAL1 and PAL2 (Supplementary Fig. 4). Off-target analysis of the predicted PAL4 target site revealed no sequence alteration, indicating target-specific mutations of the approach (Fig. 1H). Based on these results, pal1pal2 mutant line #3 and 35 S::MYB46/pal1pal2 line #1 were selected for further analysis.

MYB46 overexpression rescues growth retardation and promotes cellulose and hemicellulose accumulation in pal1pal2 mutant

To assess the impact of loss-of-function mutations in PAL1 and PAL2 and the MYB46 overexpression, morphological phenotype was analyzed in the WT, pal1pal2, and 35 S::MYB46/pal1pal2 plants (Fig. 2). The pal1pal2 mutant exhibited growth retardation compared to WT, whereas the 35 S::MYB46/pal1pal2 plants exhibited growth recovery comparable to the WT (Fig. 2A). Notably, neither the pal1pal2 mutants nor the 35 S::MYB46/pal1pal2 plants exhibited purple coloration of the leaf during leaf senescence, in contrast to the WT at the same developmental stage, suggesting impaired anthocyanin accumulation in the rosette leaves. This observation is consistent with the role of the phenylpropanoid pathway in the biosynthesis of monolignols, flavonoids, and anthocyanins (Supplementary Fig. 5A) [61–63]. We hypothesized that loss-of-function mutations of PAL1 and PAL2 reduce metabolic flux toward multiple phenylpropanoid-derived branches. As expected, the pal1pal2 mutant and 35 S::MYB46/pal1pal2 plants exhibited yellow seed (Supplementary Fig. 5B), indicating reduced proanthocyanidin accumulation required for seed coat pigmentation [64, 65]. Moreover, UV-induced fluorescence from sinapoylmalate was absent in the pal1pal2 mutant and 35 S::MYB46/pal1pal2 plants (Supplementary Fig. 5C), indicating reduced sinapate ester accumulation, which plays a key role in UV protection [66–68]. Through two independent validation approaches (i.e., seed coat color and epidermal fluorescence under UV light), we verified the presence of phenylpropanoid-derived metabolites, demonstrating that the loss-of-function mutations of PAL1 and PAL2 blocks phenylpropanoid pathway, despite overexpression of MYB46. Furthermore, the 35 S::MYB46/pal1pal2 plants exhibited significantly increased plant height and stem fresh weight compared to the pal1pal2 (Fig. 2B, C and Table S3), suggesting that overexpression of MYB46 compensates the growth penalty caused by the loss-of-function mutations in PAL1 and PAL2 [16, 69]. To further assess whether the rescue of growth retardation and increase in biomass in the 35 S::MYB46/pal1pal2 plants were associated with an alteration in secondary cell wall composition, we analyzed lignin and cellulose contents in the stem. Stem cross-sections stained with Phloroglucinol-HCl exhibited reduced lignin accumulation in both the pal1pal2 and 35 S::MYB46/pal1pal2 plants compared to WT (Fig. 2D). In contrast, stem cross-sections stained with Calcofluor white exhibited increased cellulose accumulation in both the pal1pal2 and 35 S::MYB46/pal1pal2 plants compared to WT (Fig. 2E).

To quantitatively assess the effect of loss-of-function mutation of PAL1/PAL2 and overexpression of MYB46 on secondary cell wall composition, lignin and monosaccharide contents were measured in six-week-old stems of the WT, pal1pal2 and 35 S::MYB46/pal1pal2 plants (Fig. 3 and Table S4). The total lignin content was significantly reduced in the pal1pal2 and 35 S::MYB46/pal1pal2 plants when compared to WT, primarily due to decrease in acid-insoluble lignin content (Fig. 3A, B). Interestingly, acid-soluble lignin content was significantly increased in both the pal1pal2 and 35 S::MYB46/pal1pal2 plants, compared to WT (Fig. 3C). These findings indicate increase in the proportion of lignin susceptible to acid hydrolysis, which is attributable to an increased syringyl (S) lignin that is more readily degraded under acidic condition [70, 71], consistent with previous study indicating an increased S/G lignin ratio of the pal1pal2 mutant [14]. Cellulose analysis revealed that the pal1pal2 mutant exhibited a significant increase in cellulose content compared to WT, and overexpression of MYB46 in pal1pal2 background further increased cellulose content (Fig. 3D). Monosaccharide analysis revealed that the pal1pal2 mutant exhibited increased total monosaccharide content compared to WT, with notable increase in xylose, mannose, galactose, glucose content (Fig. 3E-J). Furthermore, overexpression of MYB46 in the pal1pal2 background further increased total monosaccharide content compared to the pal1pal2 mutant. In particular, glucose content was significantly increased in the 35 S::MYB46/pal1pal2 plants. The relative proportions of cell wall components exhibited that the reduced lignin content in the pal1pal2 mutant was compensated by increased cellulose and hemicellulose contents (Fig. 3K). Moreover, overexpression of MYB46 in the pal1pal2 background further increased cellulose and hemicellulose accumulation, consistent with our previous study demonstrating the role of MYB46 in promoting secondary cell wall polysaccharide biosynthesis [22, 23].

MYB46 overexpression upregulates the expression of secondary cell wall biosynthetic genes in 35S::MYB46/pal1pal2 plants

To investigate whether the morphological phenotype of the 35 S::MYB46/pal1pal2 plants is associated with transcriptional regulation of secondary cell wall-related biosynthetic genes, we analyzed the expression levels of secondary cell wall biosynthetic genes in the WT, pal1pal2, and 35 S::MYB46/pal1pal2 plants (Fig. 4 and Table S5). The 35 S::MYB46/pal1pal2 plants exhibited significantly upregulated the expression of biosynthetic genes for cellulose (CesA4, CesA7, and CesA8) (Fig. 4A-C) and hemicellulose (IRX9, IRX9, and IRX14) compared to both WT and the pal1pal2 mutant (Fig. 4D-F). These findings corroborate with previous findings that MYB46 overexpression upregulates expression of both cellulose- and hemicellulose- biosynthetic genes, resulting in increased cellulose and hemicellulose accumulation. However, although MYB46 overexpression in the 35 S::MYB46/pal1pal2 plants upregulated the expression levels of lignin biosynthetic genes (C4H, 4CL1, C3H, COMT1, F5H, HCT, CCoAOMT1, CCR1, and CAD1) compared to both the WT and pal1pal2 mutant (Fig. 4G-O), lignin content remained reduced due to impaired phenylpropanoid metabolic flux resulting from the loss-of-function mutations in PAL1 and PAL2, which key enzymes catalyze the conversion of phenylalanine to cinnamic acid in the phenylpropanoid pathway (Supplementary Fig. 5A). Interestingly, despite the fact that similar expression levels of cellulose, hemicellulose, and lignin biosynthetic genes compared to WT, with exception of C3H, cellulose and hemicellulose contents were increased in the pal1pal2 mutant (Fig. 3), suggesting that the increased cellulose and hemicellulose contents were possibly due to compensatory accumulation in response to lignin reduction [11, 48] or epigenetic regulation of cell wall biosynthesis triggered by perturbations in secondary ell wall integrity [72]. While MYB46 functions as a gatekeeper of secondary cell wall biosynthesis [21, 22, 29], overexpression of MYB46 in the pal1pal2 background selectively increased cellulose and hemicellulose accumulation while reducing lignin content while the loss-of-function mutations in PAL1 and PAL2 effectively impairs lignin biosynthesis by blocking the phenylpropanoid pathway.

MYB46 overexpression enhances saccharification efficiency and bioethanol production through improved cellulose digestibility and fermentable sugar availability under different pretreatment conditions

To assess the impact of the genetic modification of lignocellulosic biomass composition on saccharification efficiency and bioethanol production, time-course analyses of cellulose-to-glucose conversion, glucose yield, and ethanol production were conducted using 15 mg of alcohol-insoluble residue (AIR) prepared from the WT, the pal1pal2 mutant, and the 35 S::MYB46/pal1pal2 plants under different pretreatment conditions (Fig. 5). Both the acid and alkaline pretreatment increased cellulose-to-glucose conversion efficiency compared to the non-pretreatment control (Fig. 5A), consistent with previous study indicating the acid and alkaline pretreatment was attributed to enhance enzyme accessibility to cellulose by promoting the degradation of lignin and hemicellulose [73–75]. The samples with no pre-treatment from both the pal1pal2 mutant and the 35 S::MYB46/pal1pal2 plants exhibited higher saccharification efficiency to the WT samples treated with acid pretreatment after 24 h and higher saccharification efficiency to the WT samples treated with alkaline pretreatment at 48 h (Supplementary Fig. 6A). Notably, the samples with no pre-treatment from both the pal1pal2 mutant and the 35 S::MYB46/pal1pal2 plants higher glucose yields to the WT samples treated with acid or alkali pretreatment after 24 h (Supplementary Fig. 6B). In addition, cellulose-to-glucose conversion was significantly enhanced in both the pal1pal2 mutant and 35 S::MYB46/pal1pal2 plants compared to WT across all pretreatment conditions (Fig. 5B-D and Table S6). Specifically, with no-pretreatment, cellulose-to-glucose conversion efficiency gradually increased, reaching approximately to 34% in both the pal1pal2 mutant and 35 S::MYB46/pal1pal2 plants, whereas WT remained at approximately 25% at 120 h (Fig. 5B). Acid and Alkaline pretreatments further enhanced cellulose-to-glucose conversion efficiency, with both the pal1pal2 mutant and 35 S::MYB46/pal1pal2 plants exhibiting the highest rate, reaching to 50% at 120 h (Fig. 5C-D). These results suggest that reduced lignin content in both the pal1pal2 and 35 S::MYB46/pal1pal2 plants facilitates enzyme accessibility during saccharification. It is notable that the increased cellulose content observed in the 35 S::MYB46/pal1pal2 plants did not lead to any significant difference in saccharification efficiency. However, the elevated cellulose content primarily contributes to an increase in glucose yield during enzymatic hydrolysis, suggesting that cellulose content influences the glucose yield rather than the saccharification efficiency (Fig. 5E-H and Table S7). Consistent with the saccharification efficiency, both the pal1pal2 and the 35 S::MYB46/pal1pal2 plants released significantly more glucose than did WT across all pretreatment conditions. Notably, the 35 S::MYB46/pal1pal2 plants exhibited significantly increased glucose yield across all pretreatment conditions compared to the pal1pal2 mutant, suggesting that the increased cellulose content in the 35 S::MYB46/pal1pal2 plants is closely associated with the increased glucose yield.

To assess whether the glucose released during saccharification serves as a fermentable sugar for bioethanol production, yeast-based ethanol fermentation was conducted (Fig. 5I-L and Table S8). The ethanol fermentation revealed that ethanol production was significantly enhanced in both the pal1pal2 and the 35 S::MYB46/pal1pal2 plants compared to WT across all pretreatment conditions. It is notable that the 35 S::MYB46/pal1pal2 plants exhibited a higher ethanol production than did the pal1pal2 mutant, consistent with its increased glucose yield following enzymatic hydrolysis. Interestingly, although a strong linear correlation was observed between glucose yield and ethanol production (R2 = 0.9229), minor deviations from perfect linearity were observed (Supplementary Fig. 7). This deviation may be attributable to the Saccharomyces cerevisiae strain D452-2 used in this study, which is capable of fermenting both glucose and xylose into ethanol [54, 55]. Given that xylose content was further increased in the 35 S::MYB46/pal1pal2 plants, it is plausible that the contribution of xylose-derived ethanol disrupted the linearity of the glucose-ethanol relationship. Taken together, we demonstrate that loss-of-function mutations of PAL1 and PAL2, coupled with overexpression of MYB46, enhance both enzymatic saccharification efficiency and subsequent bioethanol production, highlighting the utility of this pathway-specific genetic modification strategy for efficient biomass-to-ethanol conversion.

Discussion

The structural complexity of lignocellulosic biomass hinders its enzymatic hydrolysis to fermentable sugars through enzymatic hydrolysis [8, 48]. To overcome this challenges, multiple strategies have been explored to significantly change the lignocellulosic composition with minimum negative effects on plant growth and development [11, 76–78]. We hypothesized that increasing the content of cellulose and hemicellulose may mitigate any growth penalty resulting from reduced lignin content in lignocellulosic biomass. Based on rapid flux transformation characteristics of phenylpropanoids, PAL genes are responsible for converting phenylalanine into cinnamic acid through a non-oxidative deamination reaction in the early stage of phenylpropanoid pathway (Supplementary Fig. 5A) [79–81]. A. thaliana genome possesses four PAL genes, and functional redundancy analysis of which showed no observable changes in single mutants pal1 or pal2, while significant alterations at the organismal level were observed in the pal1pal2 mutants in terms of transcriptome and metabolism [14]. Therefore, CRISPR/Cas9 genome editing, a powerful and widely adopted tool for precise genome modification [82–85], was employed to generate loss-of-function mutations in two key phenylpropanoid pathway genes, PAL1 and PAL2. Furthermore, the exclusion of the CRISPR/Cas9 expression cassette ensured genome editing stability and reduced the risk of continuous off-target activity in subsequent generations (Fig. 1). The CRISPR/Cas9-mediated knockout of pal1pal2 mutant exhibited similar characteristics of reduced lignin and infertility phenotype as previously reported for pal1pal2 mutant generated through T-DNA insertion [14] (Fig. 2, Supplementary Fig. 5B). In addition, the pal1pal2 mutant exhibited significantly higher acid-insoluble lignin, alongside an increased S/G ratio (Fig. 3A-C), which is known to enhance enzymatic digestibility due to the less recalcitrant nature of S-lignin units [14, 70, 71]. However, the pal1pal2 mutant also resulted in growth retardation, reduced anthocyanin and proanthocyanidin biosynthesis, and impaired accumulation of UV-protective sinapate esters (Supplementary Fig. 5C), reflecting broad perturbations in phenylpropanoid metabolism [62–68]. To address the adverse effects caused by the pal1pal2 mutation, we introduced constitutive overexpression of MYB46, a key transcriptional regulator of secondary cell wall formation [21–23, 29]. The 35 S::MYB46/pal1pal2 plants exhibited significant recovery from the growth retardation, with stem fresh weight increasing by 41.6% compared to the pal1pal2 mutants (Fig. 2A-C). This recovery coincided with increased content of cellulose and hemicellulose (Fig. 3D-J), which can help maintain cell wall integrity despite a reduced lignin (Figs. 2D and E and 3K).

Interestingly, the increase in cellulose and hemicellulose contents observed in the pal1pal2 mutant occurred without a corresponding upregulation of biosynthetic genes, suggesting a compensatory response through feedback or epigenetic regulation triggered by the cell wall perturbation [11, 48, 72]. In contrast, the further enhancement in cellulose and hemicellulose contents observed in the 35 S::MYB46/pal1pal2 plants reflects MYB46-mediated transcriptional activation of cellulose and hemicellulose biosynthetic genes, as indicated by increased transcript levels of CesA and IRX genes (Fig. 4A-F). While overexpression of MYB46 also upregulated lignin biosynthetic genes in the 35 S::MYB46/pal1pal2 plants (Fig. 4G-O), lignin content remained low due to the upstream block in the phenylpropanoid flux caused by the loss-of-function mutations of PAL1 and PAL2. These findings may reinforce the context-dependent role of MYB46, which upregulates cellulose, hemicellulose, and lignin biosynthetic genes at the transcriptional level but shifts toward cellulose and hemicellulose accumulation when monolignol precursors are limited.

Functionally, reduced lignin in the lignocellulose biomass significantly enhanced saccharification efficiency and fermentable sugar yield (Fig. 5A-H). Enzymatic saccharification assay demonstrated enhanced cellulose-to-glucose conversion in both the pal1pal2 and the 35S::MYB46/pal1pal2 plants compared to WT across all pretreatment conditions, indicating that reduced lignin is sufficient to enhance enzymatic accessibility (Fig. 5A-D). However, the 35S::MYB46/pal1pal2 plants exhibited significantly higher glucose yield than did the pal1pal2 mutant (Fig. 5E-H), suggesting that increased cellulose content (Fig. 3D) directly contributed to increased glucose yield during enzymatic hydrolysis. Yeast-based ethanol fermentation assay further demonstrated that the 35S::MYB46/pal1pal2 plants produced the highest ethanol yield (Fig. 5I-L), suggesting that the released glucose serves effectively as substrate for ethanol fermentation. Interestingly, the slight deviation from a perfect linear correlation between glucose and ethanol production (Supplementary Fig. 7) was attributable to the increased xylose content resulting from overexpression of MYB46 in the 35S::MYB46/pal1pal2 plants (Fig. 3F). These results suggest that overexpression of MYB46 in the 35S::MYB46/pal1pal2 plants promotes the accumulation of multiple fermentable sugars, including glucose and xylose, thereby enhancing the overall efficiency of bioethanol production.

Taken together, our study provides compelling evidence that a pathway-specific genetic strategy of targeting reduced lignin while increasing cellulose and hemicellulose can significantly improve biomass quality for biofuel applications. While reduced lignin alone improves enzymatic digestibility, it carries penalties in growth and biomass yield. MYB46 overexpression effectively alleviates the trade-offs, which leads to enhanced bioethanol production. Furthermore, the use of A. thaliana enabled rapid and precise validation of this conceptual pathway-specific genetic manipulation strategy, given that genetic manipulation in high-biomass woody species is generally more time-consuming and labor-intensive. Consequently, the strategy established offers a transferable foundation that can be applied to both woody and herbaceous bioenergy species [86–91], in which optimized lignocellulosic composition has direct implications for industrial biofuel production.

Conclusions

We show that the loss-of-function mutation of PAL1 and PAL2, coupled with MYB46 overexpression, represents an effective pathway-specific genetic manipulation strategy to enhance lignocellulosic biomass quality for enhanced saccharification efficiency and bioethanol production. Overexpression of MYB46 in the pal1pal2 mutant background appears to compensate the growth penalty and biomass productivity, caused by the double mutation. This targeted biomass engineering led to a significant increase in the saccharification efficiency and fermentable sugars regardless of pretreatment, thereby improving ethanol production. These findings highlight the efficacy of concurrently suppressing lignin biosynthesis while up-regulating secondary cell wall formation in the optimization of biomass feedstock for enhanced biomass-to-ethanol conversion.
