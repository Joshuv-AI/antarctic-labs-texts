Real‐Time Ambulatory ECG Does Not Expedite Care
Authors: Melissa Feuerborn, Michael Torre, Ann Lyons, Thomas Jared Bunch, Benjamin A Steinberg
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAmbulatory ECG (AECG) monitoring is pivotal to the diagnosis of arrhythmias and can be performed with near “real‐time” notification of abnormalities. There are limited data on the relative benefit of real‐time monitoring compared with traditional Holter monitoring.Methods and ResultsThis is a retrospective observational analysis of University of Utah Health patients who underwent ambulatory ECG studies from 2010 to 2022. The study cohort was stratified by patients with an ambulatory ECG that provides real‐time event notification (non‐Holter) versus those who do not (Holter). The outcomes were cardiac implantable electronic device procedure, ablation procedure, emergency department/hospitalization visit, and initiation of anticoagulation out to 6 months. We identified 20 259 patients, 16 650 with non‐Holter studies and 3609 with Holter studies. Holter patients were younger (mean 52 versus 55, P<0.001), more often women (60.2% versus 57%, P<0.001), and had lower mean CHADS2‐VA2Sc scores (1.7 versus 2.1, P<0.001). The median time to ablation procedure was 74 versus 72 (P=0.5), for Holter versus non‐Holter, respectively. Median days to new cardiac implantable electronic device implantation was 54 days versus 52 (P=0.6); initiation of anticoagulation among patients not already treated was 42 versus 31 days (P=0.03). Time to first emergency department visit or hospitalization was 63 versus 57 (P=0.6). In multivariable models, there were no significant differences in time to intervention between Holter and non‐Holter for each outcome.ConclusionsReal‐time monitoring demonstrates mixed results in terms of reducing time to intervention, with the significant benefit limited to oral anticoagulation initiation. It is time to revisit clinical scenarios where real‐time ambulatory monitoring may not improve health care efficiency.Keywords: ambulatory ECG, cardiac event monitoring, Holter, mobile cardiac telemetry, outcomesSubject Categories: Arrhythmias, Health Services, Quality and Outcomes

Ambulatory ECG (AECG) is a cornerstone of diagnostic testing for cardiac rhythm abnormalities. Historically, Holter monitoring was the mainstay of such studies, with results provided only after the study was completed, downloaded, and reviewed in entirety by a health care professional. More recently, mobile cardiac telemetry and cardiac event monitors have emerged as routine alternatives to Holter monitoring and are distinguished in part by near real‐time alerts to clinicians. Thus, they can provide much more time‐sensitive diagnostics for potentially serious or emergent conditions. 1 Prior data have shown that mobile cardiac telemetry devices have higher diagnostic yield and more frequent initiation of antiarrhythmic drugs. 2 However, mobile cardiac telemetry can also have a negative impact on health care resources that are required to receive, interpret, and act upon the identified actionable arrhythmias. Whether such tests translate into more timely interventions of these actionable arrhythmias for broader cohorts of patients undergoing rhythm monitoring is unclear.

The objective of the current study is to understand the relative benefit, if any, of AECG studies with “real‐time” alerts compared with traditional Holter studies, in terms of time to intervention across a broad, tertiary care population. We hypothesize real‐time monitoring will lead to earlier detection of arrythmias and earlier interventions. Specifically, we hypothesize that patients undergoing real‐time AECG studies will have earlier interventions, such as cardiac device procedures, catheter ablation procedures, emergency department visits or hospitalization, and new initiation of oral anticoagulation.

METHODS

This is a retrospective observational analysis of University of Utah Health patients who underwent AECG studies from 2010 to 2022. Inclusion criteria were adults age >18, with at least 1 completed AECG study. For patients with multiple tests, the first one was used. Monitor studies that were shorter than 2 days' duration were excluded. The study cohort was stratified by patients with an AECG that provides real‐time event notification (cardiac event monitors and mobile cardiac telemetry; hereafter non‐Holter) versus those who do not (Holter). Testing type was determined entirely by the ordering clinician, with all tests available to all ordering clinicians in the health system. The data that support the findings of this study may be available from the corresponding author upon reasonable request and pursuant to institutional data governance.

Patient demographics, comorbidities, medical therapies, and procedures were ascertained from the electronic health record, consistent with previously described and validated approaches. 3 , 4 , 5 The primary outcomes were acute arrhythmia interventions, as recorded by administrative codes in the health record. They included cardiac device implantation procedure (among those without one at baseline), catheter ablation procedure, emergency department visit or hospitalization, or initiation of anticoagulation (among those not already treated) out to 12 months. As these data are derived directly from the medical record and granular AECG study data, dates and times reflect actual encounter dates and times not influenced by documentation, billed, or paid timing. When describing “any outcome event,” patients with either cardiac device or oral anticoagulation at baseline were excluded. We used the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology cohort checklist when writing our report. 6 Analysis of the data collected as part of routine clinical care, and subsequent reporting of anonymized, aggregate data, was approved by the University of Utah Institutional Review Board. Consent was waived by the institutional review board because the study is a retrospective analysis with minimal risk to patients. The research reported adheres to the Helsinki Declaration guidelines on human research.

Statistical Analysis

We summarized patient demographics and clinical characteristics using mean±SD, and for categorical variables we reported frequency and percentages. The outcomes of interest for this study (device procedure, catheter ablation, emergency department/hospitalization, new anticoagulation) have dates available, so we analyzed them as time‐to‐event variables using log‐rank tests. A Kaplan–Meier estimate was also developed for each outcome and used to plot complication‐free survival with log‐rank tests to compare survival curves between groups: Holter versus non‐Holter. When analyzing initiation of anticoagulation, patients who were already on anticoagulation before the time of their monitor were excluded. Associations between select demographic and baseline variables and each event outcome were assessed using a multivariable Cox proportional hazards regression model predicting time to event. Similarly, patients who already had devices before the time of the monitor were excluded from the device implant outcome.

DISCUSSION

Ambulatory cardiac monitoring strategies have evolved substantially, and this is an area of great interest to potentially improve the timeliness of the treatment of actionable arrhythmias. Prior groups have explored diagnostic yields for different studies and specific vendors, as well as downstream health care costs. 7 , 8 We believe ours is the first to examine specific, time‐to‐event outcomes reflecting clinically impactful or actionable arrhythmia findings. We found modest differences in timing of outcomes among patients monitored with real‐time results (non‐Holter), compared with those using traditional Holter test—in general and contrary to expected, real‐time ambulatory monitoring patients did not undergo cardiac procedures sooner and did not present to emergency department or hospitalization sooner, though there was a favorable trend toward earlier initiation of oral anticoagulation among patients with real‐time monitoring. These aggregate results have several implications when considering the broad use of these monitoring technologies.

First, when considering these data, it is critical to consider that the monitor is just one part of the health care system responsible for assessing and treating heart rhythms. There are also potential delays in contacting patients, scheduling procedures, etc, which can offset some of the benefits of real‐time detection. Anticoagulation prescription may have been less affected by these other potential delay sources as it requires fewer steps to complete. However, these potential systemwide delay points do not explain all of the lack of benefit of the observed results as more significant arrhythmias detected by real‐time monitoring should have prompted greater rates of urgent treatment or evaluation such as emergency department evaluation.

The lack of statistical differences for many of these outcomes between real‐time and traditional monitors suggests that the cost‐effectiveness of real‐time monitoring may be inadequate to justify use among broad patient groups in which event rates of actionable arrhythmias are less on average. Judicious implementation of this technology may reduce health care costs and burden, including the personnel costs associated with real‐time review, interpretation, and care coordination. Moving forward, it will be vital to identify higher risk patients most likely to benefit from real‐time monitoring, compared with traditional monitoring.

Real‐time monitoring systems frequently generate significant additional data, requiring management, processing, and interpretation. In an era of increasing burden of data deluge, particularly among cardiac rhythm devices both physician directed and wearable, any opportunity to reduce this data burden while maintaining quality of care is needed and welcomed. 9 , 10 Yet, the benefit of many of these systems remains unclear when applied broadly, and which data are valuable to receive in a real‐time manner and which simply add noise. For example, there is likely a clear benefit of real‐time response to paroxysmal atrioventricular block, but the benefit in considering real‐time initiation of anticoagulation based upon atrial fibrillation compared with the 2‐ to 4‐week delay when a traditional Holter monitor is read and acted upon is less clear.

Real‐time monitoring is data dense and there is inadequate comparative evidence describing the accuracy of emerging ambulatory monitoring platforms, particularly the automated algorithmic diagnostics. As more and more technologies become available, data on comparative performance across systems have emerged demonstrating that not all monitors are equal. 7 , 11 , 12 Improving the accuracy and yield for these systems is a major priority in the industry, with high hopes for artificial intelligence to improve the signal‐to‐noise ratio for ambulatory (and implantable) cardiac rhythm monitoring. This remains to be shown in a clinically meaningful capacity.

These technologies must be considered not only for benefit over traditional strategies but for incremental impact on already strained health care resources. This impact is drawn over time with use of the real‐time monitor compared with a singular event at the completion of the study with traditional systems. Analogous to these wearable real‐time monitors are data transmissions from implantable loop recorders. In a study of 1457 transmissions, of which 462 (32%) were alerts and the other full downloads, the average device clinic personnel time for adjudication of 1 transmission was 15±6 minutes and 1.5±1 minutes for the overreading electrophysiologist (total of 37 hours over 4 weeks). 13 This study identified 50% of these transmissions were false positives. An improvement in resource use with device programming was observed that augmented the likelihood of actionable arrhythmias and lower rates of false positives that is analogous to the need for a large signal‐to‐noise ratio.

As AECG technology evolves, ongoing assessments of resource burden, signal‐to‐noise ratio, and overall value of care need to be evaluated as part of adoption across populations.

Limitations

Our study limitations included being an observational, single‐center cohort. It is possible that some of the potential benefits of real time monitoring may be limited by the institutional resources that can influence device and ablation times. This may be less impactful for interventions of lower resource burden (eg, oral anticoagulation initiation), or greater clinical urgency (eg, emergent pacemaker implantation). The duration of the monitor could also be a component that influences time to intervention (shorter Holter studies may bias toward earlier intervention). Lastly, urgent interventions performed outside our health system (eg, permanent pacemaker for atrioventricular block) could not be captured and could have biased the findings.

CONCLUSIONS

There was no statistical difference in timing of cardiovascular procedures or acute care between AECG monitoring in real‐time (non‐Holter) compared with traditional Holter studies. The study did not specifically examine the health system burden of real‐time monitoring compared with Holter monitors; however, this burden is critical to consider when considering broad use. Future, larger cohorts, including those with more Holter monitors, may provide adequate power to determine if there are specific patient groups in which real‐time monitoring is appropriate.
