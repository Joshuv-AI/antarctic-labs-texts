KoreaN cohort study for Outcomes in patients With Kidney Transplantation (KNOW-KT): a prospective observational cohort study focusing on graft outcomes and posttransplant complications
Authors: Jung-Hwa Ryu, Myung-Gyu Kim, Kyu Ha Huh, Hee-Yeon Jung, Jae Berm Park, Seungyeup Han, Kyung Pyo Kang, Wookyung Chung, Jaeseok Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe KoreaN cohort study for Outcome in patients With Kidney Transplantation (KNOW-KT) is a prospective, multicenter observational cohort designed to provide detailed information on the natural course of kidney transplantation (KT) and to identify risk factors for posttransplant complications. We aimed to describe graft outcomes and long-term posttransplant complications from the KNOW-KT study.MethodsKNOW-KT enrolled 1,115 transplant recipients between 2012 and 2016 and followed them for up to 11 years. Data and biological samples were collected from both transplant recipients and donors at baseline and during annual follow-up visits. Epidemiological factors, laboratory data, allograft outcomes, and patient outcomes, including posttransplant complications, comorbidities, and mortality, were analyzed.ResultsA total of 1,080 kidney transplants were registered (86.9% from living donors) with a mean recipient age of 45.8±11.6 years. ABO- and human leukocyte antigen-incompatible transplants accounted for 17.4% and 8.1% of cases, respectively, and 26.7% of recipients underwent pretransplant desensitization. The overall 1-, 5-, and 10-year graft survival rates were 98.5%, 95.0%, and 91.9%, respectively. During a median follow-up period of 9.8 years, graft failure occurred in 7.6% of recipients, while biopsy-proven acute rejection episodes were observed in 19.4%. The cumulative incidence of new-onset malignancy and infection was 7.3 per 1,000 person-years and 41.2 per 1,000 person-years, respectively.ConclusionsThe KNOW-KT study characterizes the epidemiology of Korean kidney transplant recipients and provides robust, reliable information on long-term posttransplant outcomes.Keywords: Cohort studies, Kidney transplantation, Prospective studies, Graft outcome, Transplantation complications

INTRODUCTION

End-stage kidney disease (ESKD) is a major global cause of mortality [1,2] and is placing an increasing burden on public health systems. According to the 2017 Global Burden of Disease Study, the age-standardized mortality rate among individuals with chronic kidney disease (CKD) rose from 11.6 to 15.9 per 100,000 population between 1990 and 2017, resulting in 1.2 million deaths in 2017 [3].

Kidney transplantation (KT) is the most effective treatment for patients with ESKD [4], as it prolongs patient survival and improves quality of life [5,6]. Short-term outcomes in KT have improved substantially due to advances in organ procurement, surgical techniques, and immunosuppressive regimens targeting early acute rejection, as well as the use of prophylactic antibiotic and antiviral therapies. However, long-term outcomes remain challenging because recipients often have multiple comorbidities before transplantation, and chronic immunosuppression can lead to various posttransplant complications. KT recipients are particularly susceptible to cardiovascular disease (CVD), diabetes, malignancies, infections, mineral bone disorders, fractures, and psychological complications [7,8].

To evaluate the long-term prognosis and identify risk factors for adverse outcomes after transplantation, the KoreaN cohort study for Outcomes in patients With Kidney Transplantation (KNOW-KT) was established. Between 2012 and 2016, KNOW-KT recruited 1,115 adult KT recipients from eight university-affiliated transplant centers across Korea. The study provides high-quality evidence on epidemiologic, clinical, and immunologic factors associated with diverse outcomes, transplant-related complications, health-related quality of life (HRQOL), and lifestyle behaviors after transplantation. Because a substantial proportion of participants underwent immunologically incompatible transplants, the cohort offers valuable insights into long-term prognoses for high-risk transplant cases. To enhance data robustness and compensate for attrition during Phase I, a Phase II study was launched in 2022 to enroll an additional 300 recipient-donor pairs.

As of November 2024, the KNOW-KT study group has published 23 articles in peer-reviewed international journals. The ongoing research focuses on identifying new risk factors and biomarkers for posttransplant complications. This study aimed to evaluate graft outcomes and noncardiovascular posttransplant complications and to identify associated risk factors within the KNOW-KT cohort.

METHODS

The cohort study was approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of Yonsei University College of Medicine (IRB No. 4-2014-0290) and conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and the Declaration of Istanbul. Informed consent was obtained from all study participants.

Study Population

In Phase I of the KNOW-KT study, 1,115 KT recipients were recruited from eight university-affiliated tertiary hospitals across Korea between 2012 and 2016. Participants for the Phase II study were recruited between 2022 and 2024. As of December 2024, 300 KT recipient-donor pairs had been registered, all involving living KTs. Recipients of both living and deceased donor KTs (DDKTs) and donors of living donor KTs (LDKTs) were enrolled after obtaining informed consent. All participants (recipients and donors) were ethnic Koreans aged over 18 years. Recipients aged ≤18 years and those undergoing simultaneous multiorgan transplantation or en bloc KT were excluded. The enrollment and follow-up statuses for Phases I and II are presented as flow diagrams in Supplementary Fig. 1. During follow-up, 259 participants were lost to follow-up. The reasons for attrition were as follows: 139 participants withdrew consent, 61 were transferred to other institutions where follow-up was not feasible, 46 were discontinued at the investigator’s discretion due to the inability to obtain follow-up data, and 13 were lost for unknown reasons. In strict accordance with the study protocol and ethical standards, data collection for participants who withdrew consent was terminated immediately upon receiving their notification.

Study Design and Data Collection

KNOW-KT is a prospective observational cohort study, and the detailed study design and methodology have been described previously [8]. In brief, medical data for both recipients and donors were prospectively collected. After enrollment, recipients were followed annually or until death, graft failure, or dropout. Collected data included epidemiological factors, comorbidities, HRQOL, desensitization protocols, and laboratory results from baseline to follow-up. Additional data covered immunologic parameters, types and doses of immunosuppressants, cardiovascular function and calcification, allograft-related outcomes (including biopsy results and rejection), and patient outcomes such as posttransplant complications, CVD, infections, and malignancy. Donor data were collected at enrollment. Recently, the study expanded to include information on vaccination status and coronavirus disease 2019 infection.

Graft function was assessed using the estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR), calculated with the Chronic Kidney Disease Epidemiology Collaboration creatinine-based (CKD-EPIcr) equation. eGFR was determined 28 days after transplantation and at each annual visit. The eGFR on day 28 reflects early posttransplant recovery of renal function. Serum creatinine levels were measured using an isotope-dilution mass spectrometry-traceable method, and CKD stages were classified according to CKD-EPIcr-based eGFR values.

HRQOL was evaluated every 2 years before transplantation using the Kidney Disease Quality of Life-Short Form (KDQOL-SF), which includes the kidney disease-targeted score (CKD-targeted score) and the nonkidney disease quality of life score (SF-36 score), assessing overall health status [9].

The study team included nephrologists, transplant surgeons, epidemiologists, laboratory medicine specialists, and biostatisticians. The study protocol was registered at ClinicalTrials.gov (accession number NCT02042963).

Sample Collection

Blood samples for DNA analysis were collected from recipients and donors prior to transplantation, while samples for RNA analysis were obtained at 1, 3, and 5 years posttransplantation. Serum samples were collected before transplantation and at 1, 3, and 5 years thereafter. All blood samples were processed according to the manufacturer’s protocol and stored by an external biorepository company (LabGenomics).

Outcomes

The primary endpoints were graft loss and all-cause mortality. Graft loss was defined as the requirement for maintenance dialysis for more than 3 months, retransplantation, or death with a functioning graft. Causes of graft loss included postoperative complications, rejection, recurrent or de novo glomerulonephritis, calcineurin inhibitor toxicity, BK virus nephropathy, noncompliance, and primary graft failure.

Secondary endpoints included deterioration of kidney function, acute graft rejection, CVD events, albuminuria, new-onset diabetes after transplantation (NODAT), and HRQOL. Kidney function deterioration was defined as a doubling of serum creatinine concentration or a ≥50% reduction in eGFR from the value measured 28 days posttransplantation. These estimations were calculated using the four-variable Modification of Diet in Renal Disease formula [9] and the CKD-EPI equation [10].

Additional data were collected for other adverse outcomes. Infection sites were documented, and causative pathogens were classified. Malignancy types were categorized according to the International Classification of Diseases, 10th Revision. Fractures were also recorded, including the anatomical site of injury.

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analyses were performed annually after data cleaning. Continuous variables were expressed as means±standard deviations or medians with ranges, and categorical variables were summarized as frequencies and percentages. Differences in continuous variables were analyzed using the Student t-test or analysis of variance, while categorical variables were compared using the chi-square test or the Fisher exact test. Graft survival, rejection-free survival, kidney function deterioration, infection, malignancy, and fracture incidence were estimated using the Kaplan-Meier method with log-rank testing. Variables with P<0.05 in univariate Cox regression analyses, along with clinically relevant factors identified in previous studies, were included in the multivariate Cox proportional hazards model. Independent predictors identified in this model were considered significant risk factors for the corresponding outcomes.

RESULTS

Baseline Characteristics

A total of 1,115 KTs were registered in the Phase I cohort. After exclusions, 1,080 recipients were analyzed (Table 1, Supplementary Fig. 1). Among them, 889 were LDKTs and 195 were DDKTs. The mean recipient age was 45.8±11.6 years, and 62.4% were male. Among recipients, 838 (77.6%) had undergone dialysis prior to KT. Of those who received pretransplant renal replacement therapy, 705 (65.3%) received hemodialysis and 133 (12.3%) underwent peritoneal dialysis. Preemptive KT was performed in 221 recipients (25.0%) who underwent LDKT. Detailed donor characteristics are provided in Supplementary Table 1.

Immunologic Characteristics

Within the KNOW-KT cohort, ABO-incompatible (ABOi) KT was performed in 206 cases (19.1%), and human leukocyte antigen (HLA)-incompatible transplantation (HLAi-KT) in 8.1%. Among recipients, 6.8% were positive by flow cytometry, and 1.3% were positive by complement-dependent cytotoxicity assay. High-level sensitization, defined as ≥50% panel reactive antibody, was observed in 9.7% of cases. All incompatible or highly sensitized transplantations were performed after successful pretransplant desensitization therapy. The overall desensitization rate—including ABOi-KT, HLAi-KT, and highly sensitized cases—was 26.7% (Fig. 1A and B). The 10-year graft survival rate in these recipients was 91.4%.

Basiliximab was the most commonly used induction agent, while the predominant maintenance immunosuppressive regimen consisted of tacrolimus, mycophenolate mofetil (or mycophenolic acid), and corticosteroids (Fig. 1C and D).

Living donors accounted for 82% of all donors. Among these, 61% were living related donors—most frequently siblings (42%), followed by offspring (30%)—whereas 97% of living unrelated donors were spouses (Fig. 1E).

Kidney Function After Kidney Transplantation

Changes in eGFR, calculated using the CKD-EPI equation, were analyzed with a mixed model comparing annual posttransplant values to those at day 28 post-KT. No statistically significant annual decline in eGFR was observed up to 9 years posttransplantation. However, at year 10, the mean annual decline rate was −0.394 mL/min/1.73 m2, increasing to −0.546 mL/min/1.73 m2 by year 11 (Supplementary Fig. 2A).

Graft Survival and Kidney Function After Transplantation

Ninety-six recipients (7.6%) required new dialysis or retransplantation due to loss of allograft function (death- censored graft failure). Graft failure occurred most frequently in the early posttransplant phase and declined thereafter, although the cumulative incidence of graft dysfunction continued to rise annually (Fig. 2A). The most common cause of graft loss was acute rejection, followed by BK virus nephropathy and glomerulonephritis. Up to 9 years posttransplantation, recipients of LDKTs demonstrated superior long-term graft survival compared with those of DDKTs; however, this difference diminished thereafter (P=0.091, log-rank test; Fig. 2B). Independent risk factors for graft loss included recipient diabetes, absence of steroid use at discharge, elevated serum creatinine at discharge, male donor sex, donor hypertension, and higher donor baseline serum creatinine (Table 2, Supplementary Table 3). Graft survival according to HLAi, ABOi, recipient diabetes, and age is shown in Supplementary Fig. 3A–D. Although an HLA mismatch ≥4 was associated with reduced graft survival, it was not identified as an independent predictor (Supplementary Fig. 4A).

Multiple biomarkers were evaluated as predictors of allograft function. Elevated pretransplant osteoprotegerin (OPG) concentrations were associated with poorer graft survival (hazard ratio [HR], 2.474; 95% confidence interval [CI], 1.340–4.568; P=0.004), as were elevated OPG levels 3 years posttransplant (HR, 2.244; 95% CI, 1.049–4.797; P=0.037) (Fig. 3A and B) [11]. Systolic blood pressure (BP) <110 mmHg or ≥140 mmHg at 1 year post-KT and increasing BP trajectories during follow-up were correlated with graft dysfunction (Fig. 3C and D) [12]. Serum activin A concentrations were measured at the time of KT and 1 year thereafter; patients with higher activin A levels at 1 year had a higher incidence of graft dysfunction and CKD progression (Fig. 3E–G) [13]. Although serum fibroblast growth factor 23 (FGF23) levels decreased posttransplant, elevated pretransplant FGF23 levels were associated with lower graft survival (HR, 5.882; 95% CI, 1.443–23.976; P=0.013) (Fig. 3H and I) [14]. Moreover, statin therapy initiated within 1 year after transplantation (Fig. 3J) [15] and maintaining transferrin saturation (TSAT) >35% were associated with improved graft outcomes (Fig. 3K) [16].

Allograft Rejection

A total of 211 cases (19.5%) of allograft rejection were confirmed by graft biopsy. Acute rejection occurred in 134 patients, with the highest incidence observed during the first year after transplantation: 90 cases of acute T cell-mediated rejection and 44 cases of acute antibody-mediated rejection (ABMR). The number of biopsy-proven acute rejection episodes decreased after 6 years posttransplantation (Fig. 4A). Nevertheless, the cumulative incidence of rejection continued to increase annually (Fig. 2C), with no significant difference between the LDKT and DDKT groups (Fig. 2D).

Risk factors associated with allograft rejection included HLA mismatch transplantation (HR, 2.046; 95% CI, 1.444–2.898; P<0.001), ≥4 HLA mismatches (HR, 1.545; 95% CI, 1.107–2.157; P=0.011), statin use (HR, 1.451; 95% CI, 1.090–1.932; P=0.011), and donor age (HR, 1.016; 95% CI, 1.003–1.029; P=0.013). The use of tacrolimus was associated with a significantly lower risk of rejection compared with cyclosporine (HR, 0.442; 95% CI, 0.254–0.768; P=0.004). Similarly, treatment with mycophenolate mofetil (HR, 0.712; 95% CI, 0.535–0.949; P=0.020) or mammalian target of rapamycin inhibitors (HR, 0.228; 95% CI, 0.083–0.627; P=0.004) was linked to reduced rejection risk (Table 2, Supplementary Table 3). Acute rejection-free survival according to HLAi, ABOi, recipient diabetes, and age is shown in Supplementary Fig. 3E–H. In univariate analysis, an HLA mismatch of ≥4 was not significantly associated with the incidence of acute rejection (Supplementary Fig. 4B). Over the 11-year follow-up, donor-specific antibodies (DSAs) were detected in 224 patients, with cumulative incidence rising from 10.3% to 23.4% over 10 years posttransplantation. Among these, 54 patients (24.1%) had preexisting DSAs, and 16 of them (29.6%) developed acute rejection: 11 cases (20.4%) were diagnosed as ABMR and five cases (9.2%) as acute cell-mediated rejection (ACMR). A total of 170 patients (75.9%) developed de novo DSAs after transplantation, among whom 18 (10.5%) experienced ABMR and 23 (13.5%) had ACMR (Fig. 4B and C). Higher pretransplant titers of anti-angiotensin II type 1 receptor antibodies (AT1R-Abs) were associated with a 3.2-fold increased risk of acute rejection within 1 year after KT (P=0.006) (Fig. 4D) [17]. Allograft rejection was also more frequent in the proteinuria group than in those without proteinuria (data not shown).

Kidney Function Deterioration

Over the 10-year follow-up period, 137 patients (12.7%) exhibited worsening kidney function. Independent risk factors for kidney function deterioration included recipient diabetes (HR, 1.571; 95% CI, 1.092–2.260; P=0.015), HLAi transplantation (HR, 1.832; 95% CI, 1.184–2.833; P=0.007), and baseline donor serum creatinine concentration (HR, 1.244; 95% CI, 1.037–1.494; P=0.019) (Table 2, Supplementary Table 3).

Malignancy

Seventy-three new malignant tumors were diagnosed during follow-up, corresponding to an incidence rate of 7.3 per 1,000 person-years (Fig. 5A). Thyroid cancer was the most common malignancy (10 cases), and the incidence of skin cancer showed an increasing trend beginning 5 years posttransplantation. Five patients died of cancer during the study period. Independent risk factors for malignancy were recipient age (HR, 1.038; 95% CI, 1.015–1.062; P=0.013) and a prior history of cancer before transplantation (HR, 3.037; 95% CI, 1.445–6.380; P=0.004) (Table 3).

Infection

Posttransplant infections occurred consistently throughout the follow-up period. A total of 374 initial infection cases were recorded, corresponding to a cumulative incidence of 41.2 per 1,000 person-years. The annual incidence of infection exceeded 4% during the first 6 years posttransplantation. Bacterial infections were the most frequent each year, while nonviral infections occurred steadily. Most viral infections developed within the first year after KT, and herpesvirus infections became more prevalent with increasing follow-up duration (Fig. 5B–D). Risk factors associated with infection included female sex, HLA mismatch, and older donor age (Table 3). Pretransplant desensitization and a history of diabetes were additional predictors of nonviral infection (Table 3).

Fracture and Bone-Mineral Disease-Associated Factors

The incidence rate of fractures was 5.7 per 1,000 person-years, with no significant difference between the LDKT and DDKT groups (Fig. 6A and B). Fractures were reported beginning 4 years after transplantation. The most common fracture site was the tibia, including the ankle (n=14, 20.6%), followed by the toes (n=11, 16.1%). Risk factors associated with fractures included older age (HR, 1.033; 95% CI, 1.006–1.061; P=0.015) and diabetes (HR, 2.671; 95% CI, 1.568–4.555; P=0.001). Lower serum creatinine concentration at day 28 post-KT (HR, 0.308; 95% CI, 0.134–0.708; P=0.006) was associated with reduced fracture risk. Pretransplant osteopenia and osteoporosis were independent predictors of early posttransplant fractures (Fig. 6C).

After KT, serum 1,25-(OH)2-vitamin D levels increased, while intact parathyroid hormone (PTH) levels decreased. Consequently, posttransplant serum phosphorus concentrations remained within the normal range. The mean 25-(OH)-vitamin D concentration increased from 12.7 ng/mL before transplantation to 19.9 ng/mL at 1 year posttransplant, and this improvement was sustained at approximately 25.0 ng/mL through 11 years, which remained below the WHO threshold of 30 ng/mL. Despite this, 25-(OH)-vitamin D deficiency persisted in roughly 40% of patients annually (Fig. 6D) [14]. Stratification of pretransplant FGF23 levels into tertiles revealed that high FGF23 levels were consistently associated with lower posttransplant 25-(OH)-vitamin D concentrations (Fig. 6E and F) [14,18]. However, 25-(OH)-vitamin D level showed no significant association with fracture incidence.

Anemia

The prevalence of anemia, defined as a hemoglobin concentration <11 g/dL, declined from 61.1% before transplantation to 9.68% at 1 year posttransplantation, and further to 5.6% at 6 years. However, anemia persisted annually in more than 10% of patients over 11 years of follow-up. Serum iron levels gradually increased after transplantation and tended to be higher in LDKT recipients compared with DDKT recipients. TSAT levels remained above 30% during follow-up. A recent cross-sectional analysis of KNOW-KT data demonstrated that low TSAT at 1 year posttransplant was an independent risk factor for anemia (data not shown) [19]. Although anemia improved overall, serum ferritin levels—reflecting iron stores—remained subnormal. Interestingly, a long-term follow-up analysis revealed that recipients with TSAT >35% had significantly higher risks of allograft loss and kidney function deterioration compared with those with TSAT levels between 20% and 35% (adjusted HR, 1.67; 95% CI, 1.00–2.77; P=0.049) [16].

New-Onset Diabetes After Transplantation

During the 11-year follow-up, 199 cases of NODAT (18.4%) were identified, showing a continuous rise in cumulative incidence. Independent risk factors for NODAT included recipient body mass index (HR, 1.056; 95% CI, 1.007–1.107; P=0.024), smoking (HR, 1.450; 95% CI, 1.049–2.005; P=0.025), and elevated triglyceride levels (HR, 1.002; 95% CI, 1.000–1.004; P=0.017). Furthermore, NODAT incidence was higher in recipients with trough tacrolimus concentrations >8 ng/mL during the first year posttransplantation (data not shown).

Health-Related Quality of Life

HRQOL scores improved significantly from baseline at the 2-year follow-up, with increases observed not only in CKD-targeted domains but also across all physical and mental subdomains of the SF-36. These improvements remained stable at 4, 6, and 8 years of follow-up. Generalized estimating equation analyses revealed that the absence of diabetes, CVD, or cerebrovascular disease; higher hemoglobin levels; marital status; higher education; greater family income; current employment; and possession of health insurance were positively correlated with higher SF-36 and CKD-targeted scores (Fig. 7A). eGFR showed borderline significance for the SF-36 score. Male sex and higher eGFR were also associated with higher CKD-targeted scores. While transplantation itself did not correlate with improved CKD-targeted scores, it was associated with higher SF-36 scores during follow-up. Compared with nondialysis CKD patients, KT recipients maintained comparable HRQOL scores despite similar kidney function, even in cases of posttransplant kidney function decline (Fig. 7B and C) [20].

Healthy Habits

Smoking and alcohol consumption were evaluated as behavioral factors affecting posttransplant outcomes. Persistent alcohol intake was linked to adverse lipid profiles, with higher total cholesterol and low-density lipoprotein cholesterol levels observed after 5 years (Fig. 7D and E) [21]. Both current and former smoking were significantly associated with allograft failure, and greater pretransplant tobacco exposure predicted poorer graft survival following LDKT (Fig. 7F and G) [22].

Comparison Between Standard Criteria Donors and Expanded Criteria Donors

Among deceased donor transplantations, recipients of standard criteria donor kidneys exhibited higher annual eGFR values compared with those who received expanded criteria donor kidneys. However, there were no significant differences between groups in patient survival, graft survival, or acute rejection rates (Supplementary Fig. 5).

DISCUSSION

We conducted an in-depth investigation of KT in Korea, focusing on long-term graft outcomes, noncardiovascular complications, and their associated risk factors. The KNOW-KT cohort offers a unique and foundational platform that systematically documents baseline characteristics of both donors and recipients while collecting annual follow-up data essential for prognostic and causal analyses [8]. Since 2013, KNOW-KT has generated detailed annual reports, and the present study highlights the long-term graft outcomes and noncardiovascular complications among Korean KT recipients.

Globally, organ transplantation data are primarily collected through registry systems, including the Scientific Registry of Transplant Recipients (SRTR) in the United States [23], the Collaborative Transplant Study in Europe [24], the Australia and New Zealand Dialysis and Transplant (ANZDATA) registry [25], the Japan Renal Transplantation Registry (JARTRE) [26], and the Thai Transplant Registry (TTR) [27]. In Korea, the Korean Organ Transplantation Registry (KOTRY) was established in 2014 [28,29], providing comprehensive national data across multiple organ types. Whereas registries such as SRTR, ANZDATA, JARTRE, and TTR are designed primarily for surveillance and policy development, KNOW-KT differs by functioning as an exclusively prospective cohort study dedicated to KT. This design allows for rigorous evaluation of risk factors, systematic biospecimen collection for translational research, and comparative analyses with other Korean CKD and pediatric cohorts. The consistent and high-quality data acquisition, combined with more than 10 years of follow-up among approximately 800 Phase I participants, attests to the exceptional robustness of this dataset (Supplementary Table 4).

The burden of ESKD continues to rise in Korea, and KT remains the optimal therapeutic option despite ongoing posttransplant complications [30]. A notable trend in Korea has been the proactive expansion of immunologically incompatible transplantations, including HLAi and ABOi procedures, as a strategy to address persistent organ shortages. Within the KNOW-KT cohort, 26.7% of recipients underwent desensitization, and the 10-year graft survival rate reached 91.4%, underscoring the safety and efficacy of overcoming immunologic barriers [21–33]. The recently initiated Phase II study, centered on LDKT, is expected to provide even more robust data to guide national policies. Phase II, launched between 2022 and 2024, aims to expand the combined cohort across Phases I and II to approximately 1,000 participants, accounting for attrition in the earlier phase. This phase maintains inclusion criteria identical to Phase I but incorporates enhanced nutritional assessments and longitudinal data collection on both donors and recipients. These methodological refinements are expected to strengthen the cohort’s contribution to clinical practice and translational research.

Long-term preservation of kidney function is crucial for successful transplantation. Data from this cohort demonstrated remarkable stability in eGFR, with no significant annual decline until year 9. This stability compares favorably with the natural eGFR decline of approximately 1 mL/min/1.73 m² per year observed in the general population aged ≥45 years [34]. At 1 month posttransplant, nearly all recipients were categorized within CKD stages 1–2, with gradual shifts to stages 2–3 over time. By year 11, stage 1 CKD represented fewer than 10% of cases, and stage 3b began to appear. However, the proportion of recipients progressing to stages 4–5 remained low, even after 11 years (Supplementary Fig. 2B). These findings emphasize the sustained functional integrity of transplanted kidneys—particularly in living donor recipients—and demonstrate that advances in posttransplant management have substantially extended graft longevity (Supplementary Fig. 2). Continued research into emerging biomarkers and molecular mediators will be essential to further improve graft monitoring and prediction.

Despite this stability, acute rejection remains a major determinant of long-term graft outcomes, particularly within this cohort, which includes many highly sensitized and ABOi/HLAi recipients. DSAs are a principal cause of ABMR and graft dysfunction [35,36]. De novo DSAs are more strongly associated with acute rejection than preexisting DSAs detected before transplantation [37], a pattern that aligns with the findings of this study. These results confirm the feasibility of transplantation in sensitized patients but highlight the need for vigilant posttransplant immunologic surveillance to detect de novo DSAs. Furthermore, ACMR was more frequent among patients with de novo DSAs, whereas ABMR predominated in those with preexisting DSAs, reflecting the complex dynamics of posttransplant immune interactions. The poor prognosis associated with concurrent DSA and ACMR [38] underscores a critical area for future investigation through extended follow-up and detailed graft biopsy analyses. Additionally, preexisting AT1R-Abs were identified as independent risk factors for ACMR [17], reinforcing the emerging role of non-HLA antibodies in transplant immunopathology. The standardization of AT1R-Ab titer measurements may become a valuable tool for pretransplant immunologic risk stratification and individualized immunosuppressive strategies.

Proteinuria also emerged as an important marker of adverse outcomes. Early proteinuria following transplantation is a known risk factor for acute rejection and graft loss [39], while persistent proteinuria has been linked to chronic allograft injury [40]. Although most cases in this cohort involved non-nephrotic-range proteinuria, early-onset proteinuria tended to coincide with higher rates of rejection. Further research is warranted to validate its utility as a quantitative biomarker of graft injury and as a potential predictor of long-term graft failure.

While several risk factors for graft loss paralleled those associated with worsening kidney function, serum creatinine levels at day 28 and steroid use at discharge were not correlated with long-term functional decline. This finding suggests that early and late graft deterioration may arise from distinct mechanisms, emphasizing the need for period-specific monitoring of therapy and comorbidities. Given the increasing prevalence of immunologically incompatible transplants, close surveillance for de novo HLA-DSA formation and inflammatory mediators such as interleukin-6 remains essential [41]. Internationally, the Living Kidney Donor Profile Index (LKDPI) is increasingly applied to predict graft outcomes [42]; however, its predictive performance may vary across populations. The KNOW-KT dataset could therefore serve as a foundation for developing a Korean-specific LKDPI model.

Malignancy remains a well-recognized posttransplant complication. In this cohort, the overall cancer incidence was 1.7 to 3.9 times higher than in the general population, consistent with prior Korean reports [43,44]. Thyroid cancer was the most common malignancy, often diagnosed within 2 years after transplantation, underscoring the importance of early, tailored screening and the need to evaluate the influence of immunosuppressant exposure on cancer risk.

Infection continues to represent a critical long-term concern, with a cumulative incidence of 41.2 cases per 1,000 person-years and annual rates exceeding 4% during the first 6 years. Bacterial infections predominated, peaking within the first posttransplant year, while herpesvirus infections showed persistent long-term activity. These findings highlight the importance of pretransplant vaccination, early detection, and ongoing clinical vigilance. In this cohort, female sex, desensitization, HLA mismatch, and particularly older donor age were significant predictors of infection risk. The latter finding reflects the growing reliance on aging donor populations and underscores the need for individualized infection prevention strategies in modern transplant care. Although immunosuppressant exposure itself was not independently associated with infection in this study, further analyses are needed to elucidate its long-term role in shaping infection patterns.

The incidence of posttransplant fractures in our cohort was 1.7% over 11 years, lower than previously reported rates (3.3–99.6 per 1,000 person-years) and comparable to those observed in the general population [45–47]. This relatively low rate may reflect shorter dialysis duration and less advanced CKD-related bone disease before transplantation. Fractures typically occurred after four years, later than the early high-risk period commonly reported elsewhere. Recipient age, diabetes, and low muscle mass were significant predictors, underscoring the importance of preserving muscle mass and physical function [48]. Given the rising cumulative fracture risk, long-term skeletal surveillance remains critical. The strong association between posttransplant fractures and pretransplant osteopenia or osteoporosis, together with the aging transplant population, highlights the importance of proactive bone health management [18].

Vitamin D deficiency affected approximately 40% of recipients annually and was associated with allograft dysfunction [49], proteinuria [50], and CVD [51]. Although supplementation effectively normalized serum levels, its impact on long-term prognosis remains unclear [14]. Continued follow-up will be essential to clarify whether vitamin D repletion contributes to improved graft and cardiovascular outcomes.

Although anemia generally improves after KT, it persists as a significant clinical issue [52]. In this cohort, the prevalence of anemia (hemoglobin <11 g/dL) declined sharply from 61.1% pretransplant to 9.7% at 1 year but remained above 10% throughout the 11-year follow-up. This persistence is notable, given established associations between anemia, cognitive decline, cardiovascular morbidity, graft loss, and mortality [53–55].

Our analysis of iron metabolism offers additional insight. Although serum iron and TSAT improved following transplantation, ferritin levels—reflecting iron stores—remained below normal. This suggests that TSAT may serve as a more reliable marker of posttransplant anemia, consistent with prior evidence that low TSAT at 1 year is an independent risk factor for anemia [19]. Conversely, excessive iron status—defined as TSAT >35% and ferritin >300 ng/mL—was associated with allograft dysfunction [16], implying that iron overload may adversely affect graft outcomes. Given the strong relationship between persistent or newly developed anemia and long-term prognosis, these findings underscore the need for improved biomarkers and individualized management strategies.

NODAT is a major prognostic factor, contributing to increased risks of CVD, graft dysfunction, and mortality. In our cohort, the cumulative NODAT incidence reached 18.4% at 11 years, consistent with the global range of 2% to 50%. Independent risk factors included recipient body mass index, smoking, and hypertriglyceridemia, with tacrolimus trough concentrations >8 ng/mL during the first year strongly associated with NODAT development. These findings emphasize the importance of personalized immunosuppressant titration, meticulous metabolic monitoring, and targeted lifestyle modification to optimize long-term patient and graft outcomes.

HRQOL also emerged as a pivotal long-term outcome measure. Using the KDQOL-SF instrument, we observed substantial improvements 2 years posttransplant, with sustained stability across both physical and mental domains through 8 years of follow-up. Notably, this improvement persisted even as graft function gradually declined, underscoring that KT confers enduring benefits in patient-reported outcomes compared with dialysis [56,57]. In a longitudinal comparison with a CKD population, KT recipients maintained significantly higher HRQOL scores, even when kidney function declined to levels comparable to those of CKD patients. These findings reinforce HRQOL as an essential indicator of long-term success and patient well-being after transplantation.

Lifestyle behaviors, including smoking, alcohol consumption, and sleep quality, also exerted measurable effects on posttransplant outcomes [58–60]. Persistent alcohol use was associated with adverse lipid profiles and increased cardiovascular risk, whereas smoking cessation prior to transplantation markedly improved graft survival in living donor recipients [21,22]. These results, in conjunction with emerging evidence on the roles of sleep and other modifiable health behaviors, highlight the importance of comprehensive patient education and structured behavioral interventions to sustain long-term graft function and overall health after transplantation.

This study has several limitations. First, as an observational epidemiologic cohort study, it may be subject to unmeasured confounding and potential data incompleteness. Nonetheless, the standardized protocol, multicenter participation, and centralized data quality control of the KNOW-KT study substantially enhance the reliability of these findings. Second, the biomarker-related results summarized herein were derived from previously published independent investigations conducted by multiple research groups at different time points. Because these analyses were based on heterogeneous datasets with distinct designs and endpoints, unified statistical adjustments for multiple comparisons (e.g., Bonferroni or false discovery rate corrections) were not feasible. The present analysis was therefore intended to integrate and contextualize the 10-year outcomes of the KNOW-KT cohort, rather than perform new pooled biomarker analyses. Future studies employing harmonized data across the entire cohort will be essential to enable comprehensive biomarker evaluation using appropriate multiple-comparison corrections. Despite these limitations, KNOW-KT represents one of the most comprehensive and methodologically robust longitudinal datasets on KT in Korea. It provides invaluable insights into long-term posttransplant outcomes, particularly in living donor transplantation, while enabling the identification of clinical and biochemical risk factors. With continued follow-up and cohort expansion, KNOW-KT will further inform evidence-based clinical guidelines, support international benchmarking, and ultimately contribute to optimizing graft survival, patient prognosis, and quality of life among KT recipients.

In conclusion, the KNOW-KT study is a unique, longitudinal research platform ideally suited to elucidate the long-term prognosis of KT. By generating high-quality, integrative data with a specific emphasis on LDKT, it serves as a cornerstone for advancing both clinical practice and translational research in transplantation medicine.
