Acupuncture-Mediated Neuro–Immune Regulation in Musculoskeletal Pain: Mechanisms and Therapeutic Perspectives
Authors: Tianhao Jiang, Tianlong Wang, Haoxin Shi, Siyi Dong, Yujun Gao, Shuren Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMusculoskeletal disorders (MSDs) are a major cause of chronic pain and disability worldwide, and are increasingly recognized as conditions driven by persistent inflammation and impaired tissue repair. Acupuncture has been widely used for the management of MSDs, yet its underlying mechanisms are often described in isolation at different biological levels. In this narrative review, we synthesize current evidence on how acupuncture regulates inflammatory responses in MSDs, with a particular focus on neuro–immune interactions and downstream effects on tissue repair. Accumulating studies show that acupuncture can suppress pro-inflammatory mediators such as tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α), interleukin-1β (IL-1β), and interleukin-6 (IL-6), while enhancing anti-inflammatory signals including interleukin-10 (IL-10). It also influences immune cell behavior, especially macrophage polarization, and modulates key signaling pathways such as Toll-like receptor 4/nuclear factor kappa B (TLR4/NF-κB), NOD-like receptor family pyrin domain containing 3 (NLRP3) inflammasome, and mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) cascades. Beyond inflammation control, these regulatory effects are closely linked to the activation of repair-related cells, including mesenchymal stem cells, fibroblasts, and osteoblasts. Taken together, current evidence suggests that the therapeutic effects of acupuncture arise from coordinated interactions among neural signaling, immune regulation, and tissue repair processes, forming an integrated neuro–immune–repair network. Although the overarching conceptual framework is relatively clear, much of the supporting evidence derives from preclinical models, indirect observations, or inferences extrapolated from non-musculoskeletal systems. Therefore, this review explicitly distinguishes direct musculoskeletal evidence from broader mechanistic extrapolations, while highlighting substantial heterogeneity across existing studies and the ongoing need for robust clinical validation. Overall, this systems-level framework offers a conceptual diagrammatic architecture for hypothesis generation, serving as a heuristic guide rather than a fully validated predictive model for understanding acupuncture in musculoskeletal disorders.Keywords: acupuncture, musculoskeletal disorders, neuro-immune interaction, inflammatory regulation, tissue repair, network medicine

Introduction

Musculoskeletal disorders (MSDs)—predominantly pain- and tissue-degeneration-driven conditions such as knee osteoarthritis (KOA), chronic low back pain (CLBP), and tendinopathies—represent one of the primary sources of pain and functional impairment across the globe.1,2 These conditions involve multiple components of the musculoskeletal system, including joints, bones, muscles, and related soft tissues, imposing significant pressures on healthcare infrastructures.1 Pathophysiologically, MSDs exhibit localized tissue structural breakdown and aberrant biomechanics, coupled with persistent systemic or microenvironmental immune activation; however, disease mechanisms vary substantially across specific phenotypes and anatomical sites.3,4 Current medical approaches, including pharmacotherapy (eg, NSAIDs, opioids), local selective nerve blocks, physical therapy, and surgical interventions, focus primarily on symptom relief or mechanical realignment.5–10 While these conventional conservative and invasive therapies offer acute pain relief, they often face clinical limitations: pharmacological agents carry risks of gastrointestinal, cardiovascular, or iatrogenic complications and fail to halt structural degeneration, whereas surgical procedures bear perioperative risks and variable long-term efficacy.11,12 Consequently, therapeutic strategies targeting underlying multifaceted mechanisms remain urgently needed.

Beyond localized structural lesions, persistent neuroinflammation, immune dysregulation, aberrant sensory signaling, and impaired regenerative responses collectively drive chronic MSD progression.3,4,13,14 Recent advances in neuroimmunology support the concept that tissue repair is orchestrated through coordinated crosstalk among neural signaling, inflammatory regulation, and repair-related cellular responses.15,16 Importantly, pain modulation (a neural, functional, and sensory phenomenon) and structural tissue repair (a biological, cellular, and matrix-remodeling process) represent distinct clinical and biological outcomes that require different levels and types of supporting evidence; nevertheless, both processes are deeply intertwined within local microenvironments.17–19 Sensory and autonomic neural pathways regulate immune activity,20 while inflammatory mediators simultaneously influence stem cell behavior, fibroblast activation, angiogenesis, and extracellular matrix remodeling.17,21 Such bidirectional communication forms an integrated neuro–immune–repair axis.18,19

From a systems biology perspective, these multidimensional interactions align with network medicine.22 As a multi-target intervention, acupuncture may influence interconnected neural, inflammatory, and tissue repair pathways.23–26 Increasing experimental and clinical evidence suggests that acupuncture regulates inflammatory signaling, modulates neuroimmune communication, and promotes repair–related cellular activities. However, existing reviews either focus narrowly on central analgesic circuits or discuss immune parameters in isolated biological contexts without explicitly addressing how acupuncture coordinates local tissue structural repair with neuro-immune signaling. Furthermore, a clear distinction between primary pain relief and verifiable tissue structural regeneration is frequently lacking in the current literature.

To address these gaps, this narrative review posits a focused review question: How does peripheral acupuncture stimulation integrate neural signaling, immune-inflammatory pathways, and cell-mediated structural repair into a unified system-level network to manage chronic MSDs? Specifically, this review delineates its scope to chronic degenerative and inflammatory MSDs (focusing primarily on KOA, CLBP, tendinopathies, and osteoporosis). We encompass manual acupuncture (MA), electroacupuncture (EA), and warm needle acupuncture as primary interventions, evaluated via both preclinical mechanistic evidence (animal/cellular models) and clinical evidence (RCTs, observational studies, and clinical outcome metrics such as VAS, WOMAC, alongside structural/biomarker end-points). It should be noted that MA, EA, and Dry Needling (DN) differ substantially in physical stimulation parameters, mechanoreceptor/sensory fiber recruitment, and biological outputs; thus, mechanistic evidence derived from one modality cannot be directly interchanged with another. Unlike existing reviews, the unique value of this framework lies in explicitly separating and then conceptually linking sensory pain modulation from structural matrix repair, providing a novel multi-tiered operational framework for precision acupuncture in musculoskeletal medicine.

In this review, neuroinflammatory and neuroendocrine processes are conceptualized as functional components within the broader neuro–immune regulatory system rather than independent mechanistic axes. Accordingly, the term “neuro–immune–repair network” is used consistently throughout this review as the principal conceptual framework.

The Core Components of Acupuncture-Regulated Neuro-Immune-Repair Network

Neural Component: Initiation and Transmission of Acupuncture Signals

Acupuncture stimulation first targets mechanosensitive tissue structures, including the skin, fascia, and muscles. Its biological effects do not come only from neural activation. They also involve the conversion of mechanical signals into biochemical signals. The fascial network contains many mechanosensitive ion channels. These include members of the Piezo and transient receptor potential (TRP) channel families. These channels can convert tissue deformation caused by needle insertion into electrophysiological signals. They then activate sensory nerve endings.27

The regulatory effects of acupuncture on bone and soft tissue repair start with the activation and transmission of peripheral neural signals. Acupoint stimulation is not just a simple mechanical input. It acts as a sensory signal that the nervous system can encode and amplify. Such outcomes are influenced by multiple parameters, including the depth, strength, and frequency of the applied stimulation. They also depend on the condition of the local tissue microenvironment.

At the peripheral level, needle stimulation mainly activates Aδ and C-type sensory nerve fibers in the local acupoint region.28 Studies show that when the needle reaches the dermis and subcutaneous tissue (about 2–5 mm), it can trigger firing of these sensory nerves. It can also promote local vasodilation and improve microcirculation through axon reflex mechanisms.29,30 This neurogenic control of blood flow supplies injured tissues with oxygen and nutrients. It provides a metabolic basis for later repair processes. In animal experiments, Cheng et al, using a chronic constriction injury (CCI) model of the sciatic nerve in rats, found that EA stimulation of Zusanli (ST36) and Yanglingquan (GB34) significantly reduced the sensitization of P2X purinoceptor 3 (P2X3) receptors in the dorsal root ganglion (DRG) and markedly improved the pathological morphology of the sciatic nerve. The available evidence indicates that peripheral sensory neural circuits are fundamentally involved in the regulation of pain associated with musculoskeletal disorders under acupuncture intervention.31 Consistent clinical evidence indicates that in individuals with chronic low back pain, a course of ten acupuncture sessions led to marked reductions in pain ratings alongside significant improvements in indices reflecting autonomic nervous system function. The clinical efficacy was comparable to that of conventional pharmacotherapy combined with physical therapy, further supporting the clinical feasibility of acupuncture-mediated analgesic effects in musculoskeletal diseases through neural regulation.32

Beyond the involvement of sensory neural pathways, the autonomic nervous system likewise serves a pivotal function in the regulatory processes of tissue repair induced by acupuncture. Through precise regulatory actions, acupuncture is capable of adjusting sympathetic and vagal neural activity, thereby influencing inflammatory processes at both local and systemic levels. Moderate activation of the sympathetic nervous system helps improve local microcirculation and limits excessive accumulation of inflammatory mediators, while the vagus nerve–mediated cholinergic anti-inflammatory pathway can suppress the release of pro-inflammatory cytokines at the systemic level, thereby amplifying the anti-inflammatory and reparative effects of acupuncture.33–35

Neurotransmitters together with neuropeptides function as essential intermediaries in the tissue repair mechanisms elicited by acupuncture.24 After the application of acupuncture, there is a marked increase in endorphin release, which suppresses nociceptive signaling via both central and peripheral pathways. This modulation not only alleviates pain but also enhances functional performance, thereby establishing conditions conducive to effective tissue regeneration.36–38 More importantly, acupuncture can activate sensory nerve endings, leading to the release of calcitonin gene-related peptide (CGRP).39 CGRP, a key neuropeptide secreted by sensory neurons, facilitates angiogenic processes through modulation of vascular endothelial activity, while simultaneously promoting the proliferative and differentiation capacities of bone marrow–derived mesenchymal stem cells (BMSCs).40,41 In addition, acupuncture-induced signaling of neuropeptide Y (NPY) and nitric oxide (NO) can participate in the later stages of soft-tissue structural remodeling by regulating collagen synthesis in fibroblasts.42,43 During the early phase of acupuncture stimulation, moderate amounts of substance P (SP) released through neurogenic mechanisms exert beneficial effects by initiating early sterile inflammation and recruiting reparative cells.44

At the central nervous system level, acupuncture signals are transmitted along afferent sensory pathways to the spinal cord and brainstem, and are further integrated within the hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal (HPA) axis. The paraventricular nucleus (PVN) of the hypothalamus is considered a key central node in the acupuncture-mediated regulation of the neuro–immune–repair network. Evidence indicates that acupuncture stimulation can induce the activation of corticotropin-releasing hormone (CRH)–producing neurons within the PVN, which in turn regulates peripheral inflammatory activity and contributes to tissue repair mechanisms.45–47

In addition, acupuncture can modulate the functional activity of key limbic system regions, including the hippocampus and amygdala, thereby alleviating stress responses and emotional disturbances associated with tissue injury.48 This central regulation not only helps improve pain perception but also indirectly optimizes the injured tissue microenvironment by suppressing hyperactivation of the HPA axis, thereby providing a more stable neuroendocrine milieu for tissue repair (Figure 1 and Table 1).

Immune-Inflammatory Component: The Regulation of Inflammatory Microenvironment by Acupuncture

Inflammatory response represents the primary physiological process following bone and soft-tissue injury, and the bidirectional regulation of this process by acupuncture constitutes one of the core mechanisms underlying its reparative effects.

Acupuncture balances pro- and anti-inflammatory signals through phase-dependent regulation. During the initial phase following injury, acupuncture is hypothesized to exert a mild, transient stimulatory effect on local inflammatory signaling, potentially inducing an acute, local release of pro-inflammatory factors that supports mast cell, fibroblast, and macrophage-mediated clearance of damaged tissue29,44 (a hypothesis-generating concept requiring direct structural tissue verification). This early inflammatory peak may facilitate the clearance of necrotic tissue and microbes, creating a favorable pre-repair environment.15 As repair progresses, acupuncture gradually suppresses pro-inflammatory mediators such as TNF-α, IL-1β, and interleukin-6 (IL-6), while enhancing anti-inflammatory and reparative factors including interleukin-10 (IL-10) and TGF-β, thereby promoting the transition toward inflammation resolution.50 This dynamic regulation provides spatiotemporal specificity to acupuncture-mediated inflammation, preventing excessive inflammation–induced tissue damage while maintaining effective reparative signaling.

In a rat model of KOA, Wang et al reported that EA significantly improved articular cartilage pathology, accompanied by suppression of the Toll-like receptor 4 (TLR4)/nuclear factor-κB (NF-κB) signaling pathway, reduced oxidative stress and inflammatory biomarkers in synovial fluid, and adaptive modulation of multiple microRNAs (miRNAs). These findings indicate that EA exerts chondroprotective effects by inhibiting the TLR4/NF-κB signaling axis and remodeling miRNA expression.51 Huang et al conducted a systematic review and network meta-analysis of 56 randomized controlled trials (RCTs) involving 2394 animals with KOA. The results showed that acupuncture-related therapies significantly reduced IL-1β, TNF-α, Matrix Metalloproteinase-3 (MMP3), and Matrix Metalloproteinase-13 (MMP13) levels while improving the Lequesne index and Mankin scores. These findings provide systematic evidence that acupuncture modulates KOA pathology through multi-target immuno-inflammatory regulation.52 Ruan et al showed in rabbit models of KOA with different severities that EA significantly modulated the innate immune response in synovial tissue. Treatment reduced inflammatory cytokines and matrix metalloproteinases while improving Krenn scores. Importantly, the observed therapeutic responses were greater in cases of mild KOA compared with more advanced stages, indicating that acupuncture may exert differential regulatory effects on the inflammatory microenvironment across distinct phases of disease progression.53

At the molecular scale, acupuncture inhibits the TLR4/Myeloid differentiation primary response 88 (MyD88)/NF-κB signaling pathway, thereby reducing the transcription of pro-inflammatory mediators;54,55 Moreover, acupuncture has been shown to modulate the activity of the NOD-like receptor family pyrin domain–containing 3 (NLRP3) inflammasome, thereby inhibiting the processing and secretion of IL-1β.56 This mechanism ensures that pro-inflammatory signals rapidly subside after their early tissue-clearing function, thereby creating a controlled inflammatory environment conducive to tissue repair.

Regulation of macrophage polarization is a key target of acupuncture’s anti-inflammatory and reparative effects.57 After injury, acupuncture modulates macrophage polarization along a highly dynamic, non-linear phenotypic continuum rather than a simplistic M1-to-M2 switch, thereby regulating macrophage polarization in inflamed joints. M2 macrophages further inhibit excessive inflammation and promote reparative factor expression through cytokines such as IL-10 and TGF-β.25,58 In addition, acupuncture can maintain a dynamic balance of the cluster of differentiation 4 (CD4⁺)/cluster of differentiation 8 (CD8⁺) T cell ratio and enhance the anti-inflammatory activity of regulatory T (Treg) cells.23,26 This further stabilizes the immune microenvironment at the injury site. Such coordinated multicellular regulation enables acupuncture to clear damaged tissue during the early inflammatory phase while promoting resolution and regeneration during the repair phase.

Using data from the Acupuncture Trial for Knee Osteoarthritis, Shi et al showed that EA and MA significantly reduced serum pro-inflammatory cytokines (TNF-α, IL-1β, IL-6) and cartilage degradation biomarkers (MMP-1, MMP-3, MMP-13, COMP) compared with sham acupuncture in KOA patients, accompanied by improved VAS and WOMAC scores.59

Furthermore, acupuncture exerts a notable regulatory effect on oxidative stress processes. Acupuncture enhances the activity of antioxidant enzymes, including superoxide dismutase (SOD) and glutathione peroxidase (GPx), thereby facilitating the clearance of excessive reactive oxygen species (ROS) and reducing ROS-induced lipid peroxidation as well as cellular injury.60 This effect protects repair-related cells. It also helps maintain a stable tissue microenvironment and promotes the resolution of inflammation.

In summary, acupuncture does not simply suppress inflammation. It regulates the inflammatory microenvironment in a dynamic and phase-dependent manner. Acupuncture adjusts the timing and location of pro- and anti-inflammatory mediators. It also influences immune cell polarization and oxidative stress levels. These actions help create a stable and controlled immune microenvironment that supports bone and soft tissue repair. This regulatory pattern spans the process from inflammation initiation to resolution. These insights establish a conceptual framework for interpreting the network-oriented actions of acupuncture in tissue regeneration, while also reinforcing its characterization as a therapeutic approach that integrates multiple physiological systems (Figure 2 and Table 2).

Repair Component: The Regulation of Repair-Related Cell Behaviors by Acupuncture

The effects of acupuncture on bone and soft tissue repair are ultimately achieved through its actions on different repair-related cell types. It regulates cell proliferation and differentiation. It also maintains the balance between apoptosis and autophagy. In addition, it promotes stem cell homing. These processes involve the coordinated activity of multiple key signaling pathways.

In the process of bone regeneration, maintaining equilibrium between osteoblast-mediated formation and osteoclast-driven resorption is essential for preserving skeletal metabolic stability.62 Acupuncture can regulate the Receptor Activator of Nuclear Factor κB Ligand (RANKL)/Receptor Activator of Nuclear Factor κB (RANK)/Osteoprotegerin (OPG) system to inhibit osteoclast activity and enhance osteoblast function. Acupuncture exerts a dual modulatory effect on bone remodeling: it suppresses osteoclast differentiation and activity by downregulating RANKL and RANK expression, while simultaneously enhancing osteoblast proliferation and mineralization through activation of the Wingless-related integration site/beta-catenin (Wnt/β-catenin) signaling pathway. This process also involves upregulation of osteogenic transcription factors, including RUNX2, thereby promoting the synthesis of bone matrix.63–65 However, Wnt/β-catenin signaling is highly context-dependent; while moderate stage-specific activation supports tissue repair, unchecked overactivation may yield adverse pathological effects, such as osteophyte formation or aberrant synovial cell proliferation.66 At the animal experimental level, Zhang et al, using a KOA rat model, demonstrated that EA significantly inhibited cartilage degeneration by activating the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway. In the acupuncture-treated group, the expression of β-catenin–related signaling proteins in cartilage tissue was upregulated, and cartilage histological scores were improved, suggesting that the Wnt signaling pathway represents an important molecular target through which acupuncture protects chondrocytes.67 Liao et al similarly used a KOA rat model induced by anterior cruciate ligament transection (ACLT) and found that 12 weeks of EA intervention significantly inhibited the abnormal activation of mitogen-activated protein kinases (MAPKs), including p38, JNK, and ERK, in articular cartilage. Consequently, significant improvements were observed in cartilage morphology scores, highlighting an additional mechanism through which acupuncture confers chondroprotective effects by suppressing the MAPK signaling pathway.68 Using an osteoporosis model, Zheng et al demonstrated that EA applied to acupoints along the Governor Vessel (Du meridian) and Bladder meridian enhanced both bone mineral density and bone microarchitecture in ovariectomized rats, effects that were mediated through coordinated modulation of the OPG/RANKL/RANK and Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathways;63 Zhou et al further demonstrated that after eight weeks of EA treatment in aged osteoporotic rats—with acupoints including ST36, Sanyinjiao (SP6), GB34, and Xuehai (SP10)—the levels of serum bone formation markers were significantly increased. Moreover, significant enhancements were observed in femoral and L5 vertebral bone mineral density, along with improvements in trabecular microarchitecture, indicating that acupuncture provides a protective effect against osteoporosis associated with aging.69

BMSCs, as the seed cells for bone repair, have proliferation, differentiation, and homing capacities that directly influence the effectiveness of the repair process.70 Research indicates that the combination of acupuncture and BMSC transplantation can reduce the severity of cartilage pathology while enhancing chondrocyte viability.71

During tissue repair, the functional activation of fibroblasts, tendon stem cells, and chondrocytes is essential. Mechanical signals induced by acupuncture can activate fibroblasts, which may further differentiate into myofibroblasts, thereby promoting wound healing and scar formation.72 Simultaneously, fibroblasts produce collagen, a key component essential for preserving the structural integrity and functional capacity of tissues. Importantly, studies have demonstrated that acupuncture increases type I collagen expression in the acupoint area, which is positively associated with enhanced skeletal muscle regeneration.73 For tendon injuries, acupuncture promotes structural repair and functional recovery of tendon tissue by enhancing tenocyte proliferation and local blood circulation.74 At the preclinical level, Inoue et al examined how EA influences the initial stages of tendon healing using a rat model of Achilles tendon rupture. The findings indicated that, relative to both the MA and untreated control groups, rats receiving EA (50 Hz, 20 μA, 20 min/day) showed a marked increase in total tenocyte counts, along with elevated numbers of TGF-β1– and basic fibroblast growth factor (bFGF)–expressing cells at 7 and 10 days following surgery. Moreover, the maximum tensile strength of the tendon was significantly improved at day 10, confirming that EA can accelerate early tendon repair by promoting the expression of repair-related growth factors.75 de Almeida et al further investigated a rat partial Achilles tendon transection model and found that after six sessions of EA at ST36 and Chengshan (BL57), the organizational alignment of collagen fiber bundles in the tendon was significantly improved, while MMP-2 activity was reduced. These findings suggest that EA can enhance collagen remodeling of the extracellular matrix during the proliferative repair phase, thereby promoting the restoration of functional tendon structure.76 In addition, the protective effects of acupuncture on chondrocytes are particularly important. EA effectively safeguards joint integrity and reduces pain by suppressing chondrocyte apoptosis and slowing cartilage degeneration, thereby preserving chondrocyte homeostasis and decelerating the progression of osteoarthritis.53,71

While ferroptosis has been primarily characterized in neurological disorders,77 its potential role in musculoskeletal repair remains an indirect, hypothesis-generating concept that requires direct empirical validation in bone and soft tissue models (Table 3).

The Interaction Mechanism of Neuro-Immune-Repair Network Mediated by Acupuncture

Neuro–Immune Interactions: Bidirectional Regulation Between Neural and Immune Responses

Neuro–immune interactions constitute a core regulatory process within the neuro–immune–repair network, in which inflammatory signaling serves as a functional immune component. At the peripheral level, acupuncture-activated afferent nerves release neurotransmitters that modulate immune cell function and inflammatory mediator secretion. For example, endorphins released from afferent nerves increase after acupuncture and contribute to muscle pain relief.78,79 Meanwhile, the sympathetic nervous system plays a key role in acupuncture-mediated inflammatory regulation. Acupuncture intensity modulates sympathetic activity, triggering the release of mediators such as epinephrine, catecholamines, adenosine triphosphate (ATP), and neuropeptide Y, which contribute to precise control of inflammatory responses. Many local anti-inflammatory effects of sympathetic signaling are mediated by macrophages and T lymphocytes.80 In an animal experiment, Chen et al used a monosodium iodoacetate (MIA)-induced KOA rat model and found that EA at ST36–GB34 significantly activated sympathetic-derived norepinephrine (NE) signaling in synovial tissue. This was accompanied by reduced synovial inflammatory cytokines and immune cell infiltration, along with improved nociceptive behaviors, indicating that sympathetic NE signaling mediates the local anti-inflammatory and analgesic effects of EA.81 In addition, acetylcholine released from the vagus nerve can regulate immune activity and suppress the production of pro-inflammatory cytokines through the cholinergic anti-inflammatory pathway, enabling a swift anti-inflammatory response.82

At the central neuro–immune interaction level, acupuncture activates the HPA axis via central neural pathways, thereby regulating systemic inflammatory responses. The glucocorticoids released subsequently bind to receptors on immune cells, leading to suppression of immune activation and a decrease in inflammatory cytokine production. Specifically, they inhibit NF-κB signaling in macrophages, thereby reducing the transcription of pro-inflammatory mediators, including interleukin-1 (IL-1), TNF-α, IL-6, and interleukin-8 (IL-8);83 At the same time, glucocorticoids facilitate the expansion and activation of regulatory T cells, thereby strengthening their anti-inflammatory capacity.84,85 Astrocytes within the spinal dorsal horn are crucial mediators of central neuro–immune communication. Acupuncture has the capacity to stimulate these astrocytes, facilitating the secretion of neurotrophic factors and anti-inflammatory mediators, which in turn regulates the central propagation of inflammatory signals. Studies show that acupuncture downregulates P2X7 and PKS receptors in dorsal horn astrocytes and reduces glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP) expression in astrocytes of the visual cortex and anterior cingulate cortex (ACC), suggesting that neuron–glia interactions in the dorsal horn and ACC may underlie acupuncture-mediated pain inhibition.86

Neuro–inflammation feedback regulation is a critical mechanism for maintaining network homeostasis, whereby inflammatory mediators can retroactively modulate neuropeptide release from nerve terminals, establishing a dynamic equilibrium. For example, inflammatory factors can activate or sensitize TRPA1 channels on nociceptive nerve endings to mediate EA-induced analgesia and neurogenic responses.61,87,88 Among these, the key inflammatory mediators that drive TRPA1 channel activation and promote pro-inflammatory responses include interleukin-1 (IL-1), TNF-α, IL-6, IL-8.89–91 Acupuncture can modulate this feedback regulatory loop, thereby maintaining a dynamic balance between neural activity and inflammation. Research has demonstrated that acupuncture decreases IL-1β and TNF-α levels in the rat hippocampus, blocks NF-κB nuclear translocation, and suppresses its signaling pathway, thereby reducing the production of inflammatory mediators and mitigating neuronal injury;92 Concurrently, acupuncture can attenuate central sensitization by modulating the release of vasoactive neuropeptides and neurotransmitters, suppressing glial cell activation, and decreasing both the production of inflammatory mediators and the activity of related signaling pathways.93,94 This bidirectional feedback regulation enables the neuro–immune–repair network to dynamically adapt to different stages of tissue injury and repair.

Immune–Repair Interactions: Inflammatory Regulation of Repair-Associated Cellular Responses

The inflammatory microenvironment regulates repair-related cell behavior through paracrine actions of inflammatory mediators and immune cells. Through reshaping the local microenvironment, acupuncture indirectly influences the proliferation, differentiation, and migratory behavior of repair cells, thereby facilitating tissue regeneration. Cytokines display diverse functional roles, with anti-inflammatory factors such as IL-10 and TGF-β supporting the proliferation and differentiation of repair cells. Specifically, interleukin-10 facilitates the osteogenic differentiation of BMSCs through activation of the STAT3 signaling pathway;95 TGF-β can activate the Smad signaling pathway. This process upregulates the transcription factor Sox9, which in turn stimulates the production of collagen and proteoglycans. At the same time, this pathway enhances extracellular matrix production in chondrocytes.96,97 The activity of repair-associated cells is negatively affected by the pro-inflammatory cytokine TNF-α. It stimulates chondrocytes to produce matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs). Additionally, it downregulates type II collagen expression while simultaneously enhancing the synthesis of type I collagen. In addition, TNF-α increases NO generation. This effect inhibits collagen synthesis and accelerates cell apoptosis. These changes ultimately worsen cartilage damage;98 Acupuncture can counteract this inhibitory effect by reducing TNF-α expression. This action helps promote cartilage repair.99 Inflammatory factors also regulate the migratory capacity of repair cells. After tissue damage, stromal cell–derived factor 1α (SDF-1α) interacts with C-X-C chemokine receptor type 4 (CXCR4) on BMSCs, guiding their migration to the injury site and supplying a cellular foundation for tissue repair.100

Paracrine regulation by immune cells is another key mechanism in inflammation–repair cell interactions. Anti-inflammatory M2 macrophages release neurotrophic and growth factors, including insulin-like growth factor-1 (IGF-1), vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF), and nerve growth factor (NGF), which collectively influence the behavior of repair cells. For example, IGF-1 promotes osteoblast proliferation and differentiation, stimulates extracellular matrix synthesis, and enhances bone resorption by recruiting osteoclasts;101 VEGF stimulates the proliferation and migration of endothelial cells, thereby supporting the formation of new blood vessels; NGF can promote nerve regeneration, exert neuroprotective effects, and prevent neuronal apoptosis.102 T cells can mitigate inflammation and protect cartilage by enhancing the production of anti-inflammatory cytokines, including IL-10 and TGF-β.103 Furthermore, in the initial phase following injury, neutrophils release a range of proteases and ROS to remove damaged tissue and cellular debris; however, their excessive activation or prolonged activity may exacerbate tissue damage and impede the resolution of inflammation;104 Acupuncture can modulate the temporal pattern of neutrophil infiltration, reducing their excessive accumulation during the later stages of repair and thereby preventing interference with the tissue healing process.23

Acupuncture modulates the inflammatory microenvironment and reshapes the reparative phenotype of repair cells, primarily through the regulation of key molecular targets. Rho-associated protein kinase (ROCK) serves as a critical regulator in the acupuncture-induced cytoskeletal remodeling process. For example, cytoskeletal alterations triggered by acupuncture stimulate ROCK, which subsequently contributes to the therapy’s analgesic effects.105 In addition, acupuncture is capable of regulating both the composition and organization of the extracellular matrix (ECM) within the context of the inflammatory microenvironment. As a scaffolding microenvironment for cell survival, the ECM regulates cellular signaling and behavior through interactions with cell-surface integrin receptors. Acupuncture promotes ECM synthesis and remodeling. As a result, this process generates a favorable microenvironment that facilitates the growth and differentiation of repair-associated cells.106,107

Neuro–Repair Interactions: Neural Regulation of Repair-Associated Cellular Responses

Neural signals can directly regulate the behavior of repair-related cells through neurotransmitters and neurotrophic factors. This process enables direct neuro–cellular interactions. Acupuncture activates neural pathways and strengthens this regulatory effect. It thereby helps accelerate tissue repair. At the neurotransmitter-mediated regulatory level, various neurotransmitters interact with receptors located on the membranes of repair-associated cells. They modulate cell proliferation, differentiation, and migration. Acetylcholine is a key neurotransmitter released by the parasympathetic nervous system. It can bind to cholinergic receptors on osteoblasts. This interaction promotes osteoblast proliferation and mineralization.108 SP can interact with NK-1R receptors expressed on the surface of fibroblasts. This binding enhances fibroblast activity and promotes soft tissue repair.109 Additionally, neurotransmitters such as endorphins and NE act through their specific receptors to regulate the behavior of repair-related cells. Together, they form a direct neurotransmitter-based regulatory network controlling these cells.

Neurotrophic factors are essential regulators of stem cell behavior and constitute a key component of neuro–cellular communication. Stem cells are a key source for tissue repair, and their homing and directed differentiation directly affect repair outcomes. NGF can bind to tropomyosin receptor kinase A (TrkA) receptors on the surface of BMSCs. This binding activates downstream signaling pathways and strongly promotes their differentiation into osteoblasts and neural cells.110 Furthermore, neurotrophic factors, including glial cell line–derived neurotrophic factor (GDNF) and neurotrophin-3 (NT-3), function through interactions with their specific receptors, thereby enhancing neuronal survival and facilitating axonal regeneration. In addition, they are capable of promoting the differentiation of endogenous neural stem cells, thereby triggering intrinsic repair mechanisms within the body.111

Neurovascular–cellular coordinated repair represents an extended form of neuro–cellular interaction. Acupuncture can improve local microcirculation through neural regulation. As a result, levels of oxyhemoglobin (oxy-Hb) and total hemoglobin (t-Hb) rise significantly in the stimulated region, thereby improving local blood perfusion and tissue oxygen supply.112 Adequate microcirculation provides oxygen and nutrients to repair-related cells. It also delivers regulatory molecules, including growth factors and cytokines, which help modulate their behavior. Notably, acupuncture may also participate in neuro–cellular interactions by regulating bioelectrical signals. Needle stimulation can alter the electrophysiological state of local tissues, affecting cell membrane potentials and ion channel activity, which in turn modulates the direction of cell migration and differentiation. For instance, the neuromuscular junction (NMJ) represents a specialized synaptic interface established between motor neurons, which constitute the presynaptic component, and their corresponding muscle fibers forming the postsynaptic membrane. Its primary function is to translate neuronal electrical impulses into excitatory signals within muscle cells.113 Evidence suggests that acupuncture can upregulate acetylcholine receptor expression on the postsynaptic membrane at the neuromuscular junction, thereby improving muscle dysfunction associated with neuromuscular disorders.114,115 The mechanism by which acupuncture modulates bioelectrical signals aligns closely with the recently proposed “bioelectric regulation of regeneration” theory, this may provide a potential explanatory framework for understanding the role of acupuncture in regenerative medicine (Figure 3).

Integrated Regulation of the Neuro–Immune–Repair Network: Synergistic Promotion of Repair by Multi-Component Interaction

Numerous studies demonstrate that musculoskeletal repair is an integrated process requiring coordinated action across neural, immune, vascular, and stromal compartments rather than isolated linear signaling.23–26,116,117 Preclinically, neurovascular remodeling and immune resolution operate in concert to optimize the regenerative niche.42,118 However, most mechanistic studies remain reductionist, typically evaluating a single pathway in isolated animal models while overlooking multi-organ feedback loops and long-term joint biomechanics.51,53

With respect to immune modulation, acupuncture has been extensively reported to attenuate excessive inflammatory reactions by downregulating the expression of pro-inflammatory cytokines, including TNF-α and IL-1β.119 At the same time, it facilitates the secretion of anti-inflammatory and repair-related mediators, including IL-10 (in neuropsychiatric models).120 It is important to note that this immunomodulatory effect does not occur alone. It depends on the precise regulation of neural input, emphasizing the key role of neuro–immune crosstalk in acupuncture-driven tissue repair.

Moreover, acupuncture provides vital metabolic support for tissue repair by improving local microcirculation and stimulating angiogenesis. Studies show that it can enhance endothelial cell function and activate signaling pathways related to VEGF and NO. These effects promote new blood vessel formation and improve oxygen and nutrient delivery to injured tissues. This vascular regulation reinforces the repair signals initiated by neural and immune pathways, supporting long-term tissue remodeling and functional recovery.42,118

Overall, the tissue repair effects of acupuncture arise not from a single biological process, but from the combined interactions of neural signals, immune regulation, vascular remodeling, and repair cell activity. By integrating these networks, acupuncture coordinates the functions of multiple components across different levels, creating a systemic environment that supports tissue regeneration. From a review perspective, analyzing acupuncture mechanisms within the broader neuro–immune–repair network framework can help organize existing research more systematically and provide a theoretical foundation for its application in regenerative and systems medicine.

Experimental Evidence and Clinical Application of Acupuncture-Mediated Network Regulation

Preclinical Experimental Evidence

Extensive preclinical studies have shown that acupuncture does not target a single pathway. Instead, it produces systemic therapeutic effects by coordinating multiple layers of biological networks.121,122 Studies based on classical animal models of pain, inflammation, ischemic injury, and neurodegenerative diseases indicate that acupuncture can simultaneously engage interconnected systemic networks—including neural, immune, endocrine, and vascular systems.82,123

In experimental rodent models representing both inflammatory and neuropathic pain conditions, EA stimulation at commonly used acupoints such as ST36, SP6, and Kunlun (BL60) can induce extensive remodeling of both spinal and supraspinal central nervous system networks.124,125 Electrophysiological studies have shown that in neuropathic pain models such as spinal nerve injury, 2 Hz EA can significantly reduce the C-fiber evoked firing frequency of wide dynamic range (WDR) neurons in the dorsal horn of the spinal cord, correcting abnormal neuronal firing patterns and thus alleviating central sensitization.126 Wei et al used the chronic compression of dorsal root ganglion (CCD) rat model and found that the expression of aquaporin 1 (AQP1) and its downstream TRPV4 channels in dorsal root DRG neurons was abnormally upregulated, which was closely related to hyperalgesia. This suggests that peripheral ion channels and water channels play important roles in the regulatory network of musculoskeletal pain models, providing a molecular basis for understanding how acupuncture exerts its analgesic effects by modulating the ion channel environment in DRG.127 Functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) evidence indicates that acupuncture can markedly influence resting-state functional connectivity across the brain. In individuals with chronic low back pain, baseline connectivity profiles—particularly within pain-associated networks such as the default mode network, somatosensory network, and descending pain regulatory system—have been shown to predict therapeutic outcomes, implying that acupuncture may restore disrupted connectivity patterns.128 Furthermore, acupuncture can enhance functional connectivity between pain-modulatory network nodes, such as the periaqueductal gray and the amygdala, and these changes are associated with clinical pain relief.129 This network-level regulation forms the basis for the lasting analgesic effects of acupuncture and is highly dependent on the integrity of sensory afferent pathways, as well as the central opioid and monoaminergic regulatory systems.130,131 When these key pathways are blocked, the efficacy of acupuncture is significantly diminished, providing causal evidence of its mechanism that relies on the activation of neural networks.

Beyond central nervous system regulation, the modulatory effects of acupuncture within the neuro–immune network have also been robustly validated in preclinical models of systemic inflammation. In experimental settings of endotoxemia and polymicrobial sepsis, it has been shown that stimulation of the ST36 acupoint can engage a defined sciatic–vagus–adrenal pathway, leading to dopamine secretion from the adrenal medulla and consequent inhibition of pro-inflammatory cytokine production. Disruption of any element within this neuro–immune signaling cascade eliminates the anti-inflammatory efficacy of acupuncture, thereby offering direct causal support for the link between peripheral sensory input and systemic immune modulation.49 Subsequently, research combining genetic, optogenetic, and chemogenetic techniques further refined the understanding of this mechanism, identifying specific somatosensory neuron populations and the intensity-dependent autonomic nervous system output pathways. This study systematically clarified the neural circuits involved in acupuncture-mediated network regulation.132 Although these findings from systemic inflammatory or neurological models (eg, sepsis, stroke, or rhinitis) highlight systemic neuro-immune capacity, they represent indirect, hypothesis-generating inferences when extrapolated to localized musculoskeletal conditions.

Acupuncture’s regulation of the neuroendocrine network has also been confirmed in multiple studies. In models of stress and metabolic imbalance, it can modulate HPA axis activity and correct disruptions in glucose and lipid metabolism. These findings suggest that acupuncture coordinates neural and endocrine signaling to restore metabolic balance.133

In summary, preclinical studies using representative animal models consistently show that acupuncture is a prototypical network-based intervention. It can coordinate integrative responses across multiple physiological systems. Collectively, these results establish a robust experimental basis for the clinical translation of acupuncture’s network-based regulatory mechanisms (Table 4).

Clinical Application Progress

An expanding body of clinical data indicates that the network-modulating properties of acupuncture can yield measurable therapeutic benefits across a range of disorders, particularly those characterized by dysregulation of the neuro–immune–endocrine system. Evidence derived from RCTs, systematic reviews, and meta-analyses consistently demonstrates that acupuncture not only alleviates clinical manifestations but also regulates disease-associated biological networks in patients.134,135

Within the domain of chronic pain disorders—such as chronic low back pain, osteoarthritis, and migraine—acupuncture has shown significant clinical benefits compared with sham interventions or standard therapies, including reductions in pain severity, improvements in functional capacity, and better overall quality of life. For instance, a large-scale meta-analysis based on individual patient data demonstrated that acupuncture produced superior outcomes compared with both sham procedures and no-treatment controls in managing chronic musculoskeletal pain, with benefits that were sustained over extended follow-up periods.134 In the context of clinical management for musculoskeletal conditions, Moss et al performed a multicenter randomized controlled trial that enrolled 72 individuals diagnosed with plantar fasciopathy. The results showed that, compared with the group receiving a standard home exercise program alone, patients who received additional EA intervention (once every two weeks for a total of three sessions, with a 12-week follow-up) demonstrated significantly greater improvements in both the Defense and Veterans Pain Rating Scale (DVPRS) and the Foot Function Index–Revised (FFI-R). These findings confirm that EA offers a clear additional therapeutic benefit in treating plantar fasciopathy.136 Neuroimaging-based mechanistic investigations in individuals with chronic pain have demonstrated that acupuncture can regulate brain functional networks associated with pain processing, including the default mode network (DMN), salience network, and sensorimotor network. Changes in the functional connectivity within these networks are strongly associated with the alleviation of clinical symptoms.137

In addition to its effects on pain, acupuncture has shown considerable potential in the treatment of various neurological disorders. Within stroke rehabilitation, numerous RCTs have demonstrated that integrating acupuncture with standard rehabilitation therapy can substantially enhance motor function, daily living activities, and neurophysiological outcomes in patients.138 fMRI studies suggest that these clinical benefits may be closely associated with acupuncture-induced restoration of functional connectivity within impaired motor networks.139 Likewise, in individuals with depressive disorders, acupuncture has been found to alleviate the severity of depressive symptoms while normalizing functional network abnormalities in the limbic system and prefrontal cortex that are involved in emotional regulation.140

The network-regulatory effects of acupuncture also extend to immune-mediated and inflammatory diseases. In individuals with allergic rhinitis, clinical studies have demonstrated that acupuncture can lower symptom severity, improve IgE concentrations, and alter the expression of multiple cytokines, indicating that its therapeutic benefits may involve modulation of the immune network.141 In autoimmune conditions like rheumatoid arthritis, acupuncture has been shown to provide therapeutic advantages by alleviating pain and enhancing physical function. These effects are likely mediated, at least in part, through the modulation of anti-inflammatory signaling networks.142

Despite heterogeneity in study design and acupuncture protocols, accumulating evidence supports the therapeutic potential of acupuncture in multisystem disorders. Mechanistic studies integrating biomarkers, neuroimaging, and network analysis are beginning to link its clinical efficacy with underlying network-regulatory mechanisms. Future well-designed studies with standardized outcomes and systems biology approaches will further clarify its role in modern medicine (Table 5).

Current Challenges and Future Perspectives

Although research on the network-regulatory mechanisms of acupuncture has shown broad potential across multisystem diseases, its translational advancement still faces critical challenges.143 Existing preclinical and clinical studies exhibit substantial heterogeneity in intervention protocols and methodology.143,144 As Lu et al noted, clinical studies often face limitations in study design, intervention reporting, and cross-scale mechanistic integration, underscoring the need for standardized frameworks.143 Furthermore, designing appropriate sham controls remains difficult, as minimal or non-acupoint needling may elicit non-specific physiological responses.144 To address these challenges and move beyond static conceptual frameworks, future research should prioritize specific advances across three key areas:

Standardization of Intervention Parameters

Clinical and preclinical protocols should strictly comply with Standards for Reporting Interventions in Clinical Trials of Acupuncture (STRICTA) guidelines by standardizing physical needling parameters (eg, precise anatomical depth, needle gauge, manual manipulation frequency) and electrical specifications (eg, explicit current density, waveform, and targeted frequency settings such as 2 Hz vs 100 Hz), while specifying the targeted anatomical layer (eg, fascial plane vs periosteal stimulation) to minimize inter-study variance.143,144

Integration of Multimodal Biomarkers

Future trials must move beyond subjective functional scores (VAS, WOMAC) by coupling systemic cytokine panels with local intra-articular degradation markers (eg, synovial fluid COMP, CTX-II, MMP-13) and objective neuroimaging (eg, resting-state fMRI evaluation of descending pain pathways combined with high-resolution musculoskeletal ultrasound).59,128,137 Tracking these objective endpoints longitudinally will help establish verifiable causal links between molecular modulation and structural preservation.59

Computational and Multi-Omics Network Modeling

Rather than relying on qualitative diagrammatic illustrations, future mechanistic studies should incorporate high-throughput multi-omics profiling (eg, single-cell RNA sequencing of synovial biopsies, spatial transcriptomics, and metabolomics) combined with machine-learning network algorithms.22,121 Dynamic network modeling can quantitatively simulate multi-target dose-response relationships and identify key regulatory hub genes governing neuro–immune–stromal crosstalk in musculoskeletal repair.22,121

Implementing these concrete methodological advances will be essential to validate acupuncture-specific network mechanisms and provide an evidence-based foundation for precision musculoskeletal therapy.143,144

Conclusions

In conclusion, this review synthesizes existing literature on acupuncture for musculoskeletal disorders by outlining how neural, immune, and tissue repair processes interact. Rather than offering a definitive predictive model, the proposed “neuro–immune–repair network” provides a simple diagrammatic scheme to help guide future research into how peripheral stimulation connects with broader physiological responses.

A clear distinction among the different lines of evidence is necessary to avoid overinterpreting current data. Clinical studies reliably show that acupuncture provides meaningful pain relief and functional improvement in chronic conditions like knee osteoarthritis and low back pain. At the same time, animal and cell studies indicate that needle stimulation can alter sensory neural activity, shift inflammatory markers, and affect bone or cartilage cell behavior. However, whether these biological changes translate into actual, long-term tissue regeneration in human patients remains unproven and should be treated as an open question.

Looking ahead, ideas like patient stratification, phenotype-guided treatment (such as choosing protocols based on a patient’s inflammatory state or pain profile), and personalized care should be treated as research hypotheses to test, not established clinical methods. Well-designed prospective trials using clear biomarkers and clinical endpoints are required to determine if these concepts have practical value in real-world practice.
