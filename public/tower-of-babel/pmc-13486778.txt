Triggering Receptor Expressed on Myeloid Cells 2+ Macrophages Promote Pancreatic Regeneration Through Cholesterol–Metabolism Reprogramming and Hedgehog Signaling Activation Following Acute Pancreatitis
Authors: Pengli Song, Qi Peng, Xuerui Jin, Bin Li, Ruiyan Wang, Jing Jiang, Jie Shen, Jianbo Ni, Congying Chen, Xiaolu Ge, Xiao Han, Xingpeng Wang, Guoyong Hu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background & AimsMacrophage phenotypic transition plays a crucial role in inflammation resolution and tissue repair following acute pancreatitis. Triggering receptor expressed on myeloid cells 2, a damage-sensing immune receptor expressed in macrophages, has been associated with tissue repair in various disease contexts. Here, we aimed to investigate the role of triggering receptor expressed on myeloid cells 2+ macrophages in pancreatic regeneration following acute pancreatitis.MethodsWe used single-cell RNA sequencing and immunohistochemistry to identify triggering receptor expressed on myeloid cells 2+ macrophage subpopulation and combined lineage tracing to confirm its origin. Circulating soluble triggering receptor expressed on myeloid cells 2 levels were assessed in acute pancreatitis clinical cohorts. Functional analysis was conducted using LysMCreTrem2fl/fl mice. Transcriptomic profiling and pharmacologic assays elucidated molecular mechanisms.ResultsWe show that monocyte-derived triggering receptor expressed on myeloid cells 2+ macrophages accumulate in the peri–acinar-to-ductal metaplasia region during acute pancreatitis and are essential for pancreatic regeneration. Genetic ablation of triggering receptor expressed on myeloid cells 2 impaired pancreatic regeneration, characterized by persistent inflammation, stromal deposition, and compromised acinar redifferentiation and proliferation. Mechanistically, triggering receptor expressed on myeloid cells 2 deficiency impeded macrophage phenotype transition toward an anti-inflammatory/pro-reparative state, which is associated with cholesterol metabolic reprogramming. Furthermore, triggering receptor expressed on myeloid cells 2 deficiency reduced the paracrine expression of Desert Hedgehog, thereby attenuating Hedgehog signaling in progenitor-like epithelial cells. Pharmacologic activation of the Hedgehog signaling rescued pancreatic regeneration and stromal remodeling. Immunohistochemical analysis confirmed the presence of triggering receptor expressed on myeloid cells 2+ macrophages in pancreatic tissues from patients with acute pancreatitis, and serum-soluble triggering receptor expressed on myeloid cells 2 levels inversely correlated with disease severity, further confirming the clinical relevance of triggering receptor expressed on myeloid cells 2+ macrophages in acute pancreatitis.ConclusionsTriggering receptor expressed on myeloid cells 2+ macrophages play a protective role in pancreatic repair. Targeting the triggering receptor expressed on myeloid cells 2/Desert Hedgehog axis offers a therapeutic framework for modulating macrophage function to treat pancreatitis or pancreatic insufficiency.

What You Need to Know

Background

Triggering receptor expressed on myeloid cells 2 (TREM2) has been identified as a key immune receptor that regulates macrophage function, but its role in pancreatic repair remains largely unexplored.

Impact

Triggering receptor expressed on myeloid cells 2 modulates macrophage phenotype switching by orchestrating cholesterol–metabolism reprogramming. Triggering receptor expressed on myeloid cells 2+ macrophages promote pancreatic regeneration through Desert Hedgehog paracrine signaling. Clinically, serum-soluble triggering receptor expressed on myeloid cells 2 levels inversely correlate with acute pancreatitis severity.

Future Directions

These insights establish a therapeutic framework for pancreatic regeneration, proposing targeted modulation of the triggering receptor expressed on myeloid cells 2/Desert Hedgehog axis as a novel strategy to treat pancreatitis or pancreatic insufficiency.

Acute pancreatitis (AP) is one of the most common gastrointestinal disorders, typically presenting with self-limiting inflammation and complete tissue repair in mild cases.1 In contrast, severe acute pancreatitis (SAP) is associated with a mortality rate of approximately 20%2 and may lead to severe complications such as pancreatic exocrine/endocrine insufficiency, chronic pancreatitis, or type 3 diabetes mellitus (pancreatogenic diabetes) due to impaired pancreatic regeneration.3,4 Although approximately 80% of AP cases resolve spontaneously within a week,2 indicating the existence of an endogenous anti-inflammatory/pro-reparative mechanism, the precise mechanisms governing this resolution remain unclear. A deeper understanding of these endogenous mechanisms may facilitate pancreatic regeneration and aid in preventing progression to SAP.

The onset of AP is triggered by pancreatic acinar cell damage, which releases damage-associated molecular patterns, inflammatory factors, and chemokines that recruit immune cells.5,6 Macrophages secrete tumor necrosis factor-α, interleukin (IL)-6, and RANTES to promote the formation of acinar-to-ductal metaplasia (ADM).7 This reprogramming event induces acinar cell dedifferentiation, during which they shut down the secretory program to protect against further damage and acquire the phenotype of pancreatic progenitor cells,8 such as expressing Nestin, pancreatic and duodenal homeobox 1, and Notch.9,10 As pluripotent stem cells, they are subsequently reprogrammed into mature acinar cells through activating Hedgehog (Hh) signaling.11,12 Hh signaling is activated by ligands such as Sonic Hh (Shh), Indian Hh (Ihh), or Desert Hh (Dhh).13 Successful pancreatic regeneration relies on the coordination of acinar cell plasticity and immune regulation.9,14,15 As the most abundant immune cells in the regenerative pancreas, macrophages adapt their functional phenotypes in response to tissue injury and repair during AP.4 Activated macrophages initiate and sustain inflammation through releasing inflammatory mediators, subsequently transitioning to an anti-inflammatory/pro-reparative phenotype that secretes regenerative factors to promote tissue repair.4,16 Their dynamic interaction with epithelial cells serves as a crucial regulator of AP progression by modulating the injury-repair balance.9 However, the precise mechanisms governing macrophage phenotypic transition and their coordinated signaling with epithelial cells during regeneration remain poorly characterized, creating critical knowledge gaps in understanding AP pathophysiology.

Triggering receptor expressed on myeloid cells 2 (TREM2), a damage-sensing surface receptor modulating immune responses,17 has emerged as a key mediator of tissue repair through anti-inflammatory paracrine signaling by perilesional macrophages among multiple diseases.17, 18, 19 Previous studies have described the presence of TREM2+ macrophages in AP,20 but their spatiotemporal dynamics and regenerative functions remain undefined. In the present study, through integrated single-cell transcriptomics and lineage tracing, we demonstrate that monocyte-derived TREM2+ macrophages exhibit peri-ADM region enrichment during AP and play a key role in pancreatic repair. We further confirm their presence in pathologic tissues of patients with AP and identify an inverse correlation between serum-soluble TREM2 (sTREM2) levels and disease severity. Myeloid-specific TREM2 ablation disrupted macrophage phenotypic switch through reprogramming cholesterol metabolism and impaired pancreatic regeneration. Mechanistic studies revealed that TREM2-mediated induction of macrophage-derived Dhh activates epithelial Hh signaling to drive acinar redifferentiation. This work delineates a novel TREM2/Dhh axis orchestrating macrophage–epithelial crosstalk and highlights TREM2+ macrophages as a potential therapeutic target for enhancing pancreatic regeneration.

Results

Single-Cell Transcriptomic Profiling Reveals Dynamic Changes of Macrophage Subsets Within CD11b+ Myeloid Cells During Acute Pancreatitis

To investigate the regeneration process following AP, acute biliary pancreatitis was induced in mouse models by sodium taurocholate (NaT), and the pancreas was collected at various time points over 1 week (Figure 1A–D).21 ADM lesions emerged by day 3 post-AP induction. Remarkably, these metaplastic structures were largely resolved, reverting to normal acinar architecture around day 7, suggesting pancreatic robust endogenous regenerative capacity. To dissect the immune microenvironment underpinning this pathology, we first performed F4/80 immunohistochemistry (IHC) and observed dynamic changes of macrophages in injury regions (Figure 1E). Given that macrophages represent the major CD11b+ myeloid population in AP and their phenotypic plasticity is closely associated with disease progression, we performed single-cell RNA-sequencing (scRNA-seq) on flow-sorted CD11b+ cells to systematically profile the dynamic changes of macrophage subsets during AP (Figure 1F). Following stringent quality control and batch effect correction, a total of 66,440 cells were included in the analysis (Figure 1G). Unsupervised graph-based clustering revealed 9 distinct myeloid populations characterized by canonical markers (Figure 2A). Neutrophil populations peaked at 12 hours postinduction before rapidly declining. At the early phase of AP (12 hours and day 1), monocytes were predominant and then declined on day 3, whereas dendritic cells became dominant from day 3 and day 5. A subset of resident macrophages with antigen-presenting cell (APC) characteristics, referred to as APC macrophages, was identified in our analysis. APC macrophages exhibit conventional dendritic cell (cDC)-like characteristics, including the expression of Itgax (Cd11c) and antigen presentation genes (H2-Aa, Cd74) (Figure 2B). However, they were distinguished from “bona fide” cDCs by the absence of Flt3/zbtb46 and by a unique transcriptional profile marked by Lyz1, Retnla, and C1qa, along with minimal Adgre1 expression (Figure 2B and C). Notably, macrophages constituted 48.3% of total CD11b+ cells, expressing characteristic markers (Csf1r, Adgre1, Mertk, C1qa) (Figure 2C). Differential proportional analysis revealed a significant decrease in the proportion of Ccr2loF4/80hi macrophages following AP, which was partially recovered on day 3. F4/80int macrophages peaked at day 1, before declining during tissue repair (Figure 2D). This adjustment enabled clear separation of cDC2 from macrophages, resulting in a purified macrophage classification and improved confidence in downstream interpretations. We partitioned the cells into 20 subclusters based on cluster-defining differentially expressed genes (Figure 3A–C). Two novel Trem2hi macrophage subsets were identified during ADM progression (Figure 4B): Trem2hiGpnmb+ macrophages and Trem2hiCcl8+ macrophages. Both subsets exhibited pronounced Trem2 upregulation (Figure 4C), a key regulator of lipid metabolism and immunomodulation.23 Transcriptional profiling identified anti-inflammatory signatures (Mrc1, Folr2, Igf1), characteristic of tissue-reparative macrophages, consistent with their polarized phenotype (Figures 3C and 4A). Following rapid expansion of Trem2hi macrophages from day 1 to peak abundance at day 3, we conducted a global transcriptomic analysis of the macrophages at these timepoints (Figure 4D and E). Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes pathway analysis revealed significant alterations in cholesterol metabolism-related programs (Figure 4E). Comparative transcriptomic analysis revealed distinct cholesterol metabolic profiles between early pro-inflammatory macrophages and Trem2hi macrophages in the reparative phase. Pro-inflammatory macrophages showed elevated expression of cholesterol biosynthesis (Hmgcr, encoding the rate-limiting enzyme) and cellular uptake gene (Ldlr) (Figure 4F), with S100a8+ MonoMac cells exhibiting maximal cholesterol synthesis pathway enrichment (Figure 4G). In contrast, Trem2hi macrophages demonstrated coordinated upregulation of cholesterol efflux transporters (Abca1, Abcg1) oxysterol-converting enzyme (Cyp27a1), and the reverse cholesterol transport regulator Lxra (also known as Nr1h3) expression (Figure 4F). Gene set enrichment score confirmed their preferential engagement in cholesterol efflux and oxidative phosphorylation (Figure 4H), with gene set variation analysis highlighting associations with peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor signaling and cholesterol metabolism (Figure 4I). This metabolic divergence coincided temporally with Trem2 induction, suggesting TREM2 may orchestrate cholesterol metabolic reprogramming during macrophage phenotype switching.

Triggering Receptor Expressed on Myeloid Cells 2+ Macrophages Act as Monocyte-Derived Sentinels That Are Spatiotemporally Coupled to Acinar-to-Ductal Metaplasia

In order to trace the lineage of Trem2hi macrophages during AP, we first performed RNA velocity analysis24 on pancreatic macrophage clusters (Figure 5A). The result revealed a continuous differentiation trajectory from S100a8+monocyte-macrophage subset to Trem2hi macrophages, characterized by sequential upregulation of Trem2, anti-inflammatory genes (Mrc1), and cholesterol metabolism-related genes (Abca1, Cyp27a1) (Figure 5A–C). For in vivo lineage tracing, we employed Ms4a3creRosatdTomato mice, enabling permanent tdTomato labeling of monocyte-derived cells. Flow cytometric analysis demonstrated that 91.1% ± 0.83% of pancreatic TREM2+ macrophages were tdTomato+ at Day 1 post-AP induction (Figure 5D). Although labeling efficiency gradually decreased, the majority (>65%) of TREM2+ macrophages remained tdTomato+ through day 7 (Figure 5D), confirming persistent monocytic origin during AP progression. To validate these findings, we generated bone marrow (BM) chimeras by lethal irradiation of CD45.1+ wild-type mice followed by CD45.2+ BM transplantation (Figure 5E). Donor-derived cells constituted >97% of circulating and BM leukocytes by 3 weeks post-transplant (Figure 5E). In the NaT-AP model, TREM2+ macrophages exhibited peak abundance at day 3 (94.4% ± 2.4%) and day 5 (95.4% ± 0.98%) post-induction, temporally correlating with ADM development (Figures 1B and 5F). Flow cytometric analysis paralleled subcluster analysis of scRNA-seq data findings, showing progressive elevation of TREM2 mean fluorescence intensity in F4/80+ macrophages that peaked at day 3 (Figure 5F). Spatial correlation with ADM was confirmed through multiplex immunofluorescence and IHC analyses of pancreatic sections. TREM2+ macrophages showed peri-ADM localization, codistributing with cytokeratin 19 (Ck19)+/Amylase+ transitional structures (Figure 5G). This spatial association was further evident in caerulein-induced pancreatic regeneration AP (RAP) models, where TREM2+ macrophages were observed infiltrating the margins of ADM. The close spatial proximity between these macrophages and ADM regions was confirmed by immunofluorescence staining (Figure 5H and I). Notably, ADM structures appeared at day 1 (Figure 5J), and Trem2 messenger RNA (mRNA) also peaked at day 1 (Figure 5K). Taken together, spatiotemporal changes in TREM2+ macrophages were closely associated with ADM progression in situ.

Soluble Triggering Receptor Expressed on Myeloid Cells 2 Inversely Correlates With the Severity and Inflammatory Response of Patients With Acute Pancreatitis

To evaluate the clinical relevance of TREM2+ macrophages in human pancreatitis, we performed IHC analysis of TREM2 and CD68 protein in perilesional pancreatic tissues from patients with AP secondary to pancreatic cancer and observed that TREM2 and CD68 expression localized to almost identical areas in these tissues (Figure 6A). TREM2 exists in membrane-bound and soluble forms, with the latter generated by ectodomain shedding mediated by a disintegrin and metalloproteinase (ADAM) 10 or ADAM17.17,25 Previous studies have shown that serum levels of soluble triggering receptor expressed on myeloid cells 2 (sTREM2) correlate positively with TREM2 expression in inflammatory organs.26,27 Given the established correlation between sTREM2 levels and tissue TREM2+ macrophages, we measured serum sTREM2 (ssTREM) concentrations in 97 patients with AP and 26 healthy controls using enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay within 2 weeks post-admission, to evaluate its potential as a biomarker for disease severity (Table 1). Patients with AP exhibited markedly elevated sTREM2 vs healthy controls (median, 23.26; interquartile range [IQR], 17.28–34.37 ng/mL vs 13.34; IQR, 11.11–15.30 ng/mL; P < .0001) (Figure 6B). Stratification by revised Atlanta classification28 revealed an inverse severity gradient, with sTREM2 levels significantly decreased in both moderately severe AP and SAP compared with mild AP (P < .005 for trend) (Figure 6C and Table 1). Moreover, we observed that significant negative correlations between serum sTREM2 and canonical inflammatory markers, including C-reactive protein (r = −0.33; P = .0059), IL-6 (r = −0.32; P = .0004), and leukocyte percentage (r = −0.25; P = .002) (Figure 6D). These results confirmed the presence of TREM2+ macrophages, suggesting a potential link between TREM2 and local immune modulation. Myeloid-Specific Triggering Receptor Expressed on Myeloid Cells 2 Deficiency Delays Pancreatic Regeneration Following Acute Pancreatitis

Given the conserved spatiotemporal association between TREM2+ macrophages and ADM across NaT-AP and caerulein-RAP models, we established myeloid-specific Trem2 knockout mice (Trem2ΔMye) to explore the role of TREM2 in pancreatic regeneration using RAP model. This model achieves near-complete acinar depletion by day 1 postinduction, enabling precise tracking of regenerative dynamics (Figure 7A). The efficiency of TREM2 knockout in macrophages was confirmed by Western blot (Figure 7B). Comparative histopathology revealed disrupted pancreatic regeneration in Trem2ΔMye mice, characterized by increased ADM, disturbed lobular architecture with increased fibrosis, and slightly enlarged and irregular luminal space in acinar-derived ductal-like structures compared with the controls (Figure 7C and D). Quantitative analysis of exocrine cell loss and restoration demonstrated persistently impaired acinar restoration in Trem2ΔMye mice, with 23.80% ± 2.36% acinar dedifferentiation by day 7 vs 12.76% ± 0.93% in littermates (Figure 7E). Notably, pancreatic regeneration was significantly impaired in Trem2ΔMye mice, with a marked reduction of acinar redifferentiation that persisted even at day 7, whereas littermate controls exhibited near-complete regeneration by this time (Figure 7C). Immunofluorescence costaining for Ck19 and amylase demonstrated that acinar cells lost their identity and failed to recover in Trem2ΔMye mice (Figure 7F). Proliferation analysis further revealed defective acinar cell renewal in Trem2ΔMye mice (Figure 7G), whereas Sirius red staining showed increased fibrosis with persistent collagen deposition at day 7 compared with controls (Figure 7H). Next, we sought to investigate whether TREM2 regulates macrophage phenotype post-RAP induction. To this end, we performed immunofluorescence staining for inducible nitric oxide synthase (iNOS) and CD206 in pancreatic sections. In Trem2ΔMye mice, we found an increase in iNOS+ macrophages and a decrease in CD206+ macrophages compared with controls (Figure 8D). Consistently, flow cytometry showed similar results (Figure 8E). To further confirm the above results, we isolated bone marrow–derived macrophages (BMDMs) from Trem2ΔMye and control mice. In Trem2ΔMye BMDMs, quantitative polymerase chain reaction (qPCR) analysis revealed increased expression of pro-inflammatory genes (Nos2, Tnf, Il1b) and impaired induction of anti-inflammatory genes (Il10, Chil3, Arg1) compared with controls (Figure 8F), confirming that TREM2 deficiency shifted macrophage polarization toward a pro-inflammatory state.

Furthermore, we explored the exact mechanism by which TREM2 modulates macrophage function and phenotype. Pancreatic macrophages from Trem2ΔMye and control mice were isolated and subjected to RNA sequencing analysis (Figure 9A). As expected, we found that TREM2 deficiency upregulated genes involved in chemotaxis and pro-inflammatory responses, whereas genes related to growth factors and anti-inflammatory pathways were mostly downregulated (Figure 9B). Gene Ontology analysis further revealed suppression of phagocytosis and protein secretion pathways alongside an upregulation of inflammation-related pathways (Figure 9C). TREM2+ Macrophages Promote Pancreatic Regeneration Through Desert Hedgehog Paracrine Signaling

The spatial and phenotypic characteristics of TREM2+ macrophages prompted us to investigate whether TREM2+ macrophages facilitate pancreatic regeneration via paracrine signaling. The activation of Hh signaling pathway has been shown to be required for effective pancreatic regeneration.11 Although RNA sequencing revealed reduced Dhh expression trends in TREM2-deficient macrophages (Figure 9E), subsequent validation by Western blot analysis showed selective downregulation of Dhh protein in Trem2ΔMye mice compared with controls (Figure 10A). In vitro experiments further confirmed that lipopolysaccharide/interferon-γ stimulation induced Dhh in wild-type macrophages, whereas TREM2 deficiency significantly suppressed this induction (Figure 10B and C). Conversely, adenovirus-mediated Trem2 overexpression markedly enhanced Dhh expression (Figure 10B). Collectively, these findings indicate that TREM2 exerts a positive regulatory effect on Dhh expression in macrophages. Furthermore, TREM2 deletion resulted in reduced endogenous Hh activity, as indicated by decreased expression of 2 Hh downstream targets, smoothened (Smo) and glioma-associated oncogene homolog 1 (Gli1), in Trem2ΔMye mice compared with controls (Figure 10D). To further corroborate our findings, we utilized a pharmacologic approach to determine whether Hh activation could restore regeneration in Trem2ΔMye mice. Following caerulein-induced injury, smoothened agonist (SAG) was administered intraperitoneally to activate the Hh pathway (Figure 10E and F). Histopathologic scoring revealed that SAG treatment partially rescued acinar redifferentiation (Figure 10G), as shown by increased amylase expression and reduced colocalization of amylase and Ck19 (Figure 10H). Sirius Red staining showed a moderate reduction of collagen deposition in SAG-treated Trem2ΔMye mice compared with vehicle-treated controls (Figure 10I), suggesting improved stromal remodeling. IHC analysis of Ki67 and SRY-Box transcription factor 9 (Sox9) further revealed enhanced acinar cell proliferation and decreased ductal marker expression, with quantitative image analysis corroborating these phenotypic improvements (Figure 10J). Taken together, these findings delineate a TREM2/Dhh axis, whereby macrophage-derived Dhh drives acinar redifferentiation, proliferation, and stromal remodeling during tissue repair.

Discussion

Macrophages play a central role in tissue injury and repair through phenotypic transition,4 producing context-specific signals that coordinate tissue homeostasis.15,30 However, the precise factors governing these transitions in vivo have yet to be fully elucidated. Here we performed scRNA-seq to determine the dynamic phenotypic transition of pancreatic macrophages following AP injury. During AP, pro-inflammatory macrophages undergo cholesterol metabolic reprogramming concomitant with upregulated Trem2 expression, which sequencing analyses suggest may facilitate their transition into anti-inflammatory and reparative TREM2+ macrophages to aid tissue repair. Although macrophage–epithelial crosstalk has been proposed,14,15,31 the mechanisms by which these interaction signals are released into the lesional microenvironment and their effects on neighboring cells remain poorly characterized.8,15 In this study, we establish a macrophage-driven regulatory network essential for pancreatic repair. We found that TREM2+ macrophages become spatiotemporally coupled to ADM, secreting Dhh in a paracrine manner. This paracrine activation of Hh signaling in progenitor-like epithelial cells drives acinar redifferentiation and proliferation, thereby promoting pancreatic regeneration. Together, these findings establish TREM2 as an in vivo regulator of pro-reparative macrophage function and highlight its therapeutic potential as an immunomodulatory target in AP.

Pancreatic macrophages are derived from resident tissue macrophages and monocytes, yet their respective contributions during pancreatitis remain controversial. Previous studies have established that resident macrophages primarily exert their functions in pancreatic cancer,32 whereas AP is predominantly mediated by monocyte-derived macrophages.4 Our findings demonstrate that TREM2+ macrophages are principally derived from monocytes, consistent with prior reports.26 Therefore, therapeutic strategies targeting TREM2 activation in circulating infiltrating monocytes may represent a promising approach for AP treatment. Furthermore, we identified 2 subsets within the Trem2hi macrophage population: Trem2hiGpnmb+ and Trem2hiCcl8+ macrophages. Despite sharing pro-reparative gene signatures, these subsets diverged in their pathway enrichment profiles (Figure 11A). Trem2hiGpnmb+ macrophages were enriched in extracellular matrix (ECM)-receptor interaction (Fn1, Thbs1, Spp1, Cd44), suggesting their roles in tissue remodeling, whereas Trem2hiCcl8+ macrophages were associated with endocytosis (Mrc1, Stab1, Cd163, Lrp6), consistent with an immune-regulatory phenotype. TREM2+ macrophages have been reported to exert pro-reparative functions in various disease contexts, primarily through debris clearance,33,34 secretion of anti-inflammatory mediators,18 and regulation of metabolic reprogramming.18 In this study, we explored the mechanisms underlying the pro-reparative functions of TREM2+ macrophages in AP. Previous studies in Alzheimer’s disease have shown that TREM2 senses lipids and mediates myelin phagocytosis, highlighting its role in regulating microglial cholesterol transport and metabolism.35 Consistent with these findings, our transcriptomic data indicate that TREM2 deficiency impairs the phagocytic clearance of cellular debris, leading to intracellular cholesterol accumulation and metabolic reprogramming. Such changes were associated with amplified inflammatory responses and diminished pro-reparative capacity. Notably, TREM2-deficient macrophages exhibited marked upregulation of liver X receptor alpha (LXRα) mRNA (Figure 11B), accompanied by disrupted cholesterol homeostasis. Given that TREM2 expression is regulated by LXRα36 and that scRNA-seq revealed selective enrichment of LXRα in the TREM2+ subpopulation, we hypothesize that TREM2 functions as a downstream effector in LXRα-mediated cholesterol homeostasis. Specifically, after AP injury, pro-inflammatory monocyte-derived macrophages engulf cellular debris, leading to cholesterol overload that activates LXRα and subsequently upregulates TREM2. In turn, TREM2 promotes expression of cholesterol efflux genes such as Abca1 and Abcg1, which are highly enriched in TREM2+ macrophages, thereby restoring intracellular cholesterol homeostasis, mitigating lipotoxicity, and driving polarization toward an anti-inflammatory/prorepair phenotype. These metabolic alterations extend beyond inflammation regulation. Our results showed that TREM2 knockout downregulated macrophage phagocytosis, proliferation, and secretory activities. The transcriptional changes we observed, particularly impaired cholesterol efflux, likely contribute to broader metabolic rewiring that directly compromises phagocytic capacity, proliferative expansion, and the secretory output required for tissue remodeling.

TREM2 function is highly context- and ligand-dependent.17,37 It recognizes a diverse array of ligands, including anionic phospholipids, glycolipids, lipoproteins (such as apolipoprotein E), and damage-associated molecular patterns released during tissue injury. Different ligands preferentially trigger distinct downstream signaling cascades. For example, phosphatidylserine tends to drive spleen tyrosine kinase–mediated phagocytosis, whereas apolipoprotein E more strongly promotes anti-inflammatory responses. In the AP model, substantial lipid-rich debris from damaged acinar cells is generated. We speculate that lipid-derived ligands, particularly anionic phospholipids and lipoproteins, likely predominate in activating TREM2 on infiltrating monocyte-derived macrophages. However, the precise ligands operating in this context remain to be identified through targeted biochemical approaches.

The role of TREM2+ macrophages in fibrosis remains controversial. TREM2+ macrophages have been reported to promote the expression of fibrotic factors and ECM deposition in pulmonary fibrosis,38,39 whereas in wound healing40 and steatohepatitis,26,41 they appear to limit fibrotic responses. In AP model, however, TREM2 deficiency led to impaired regeneration and exacerbated fibrosis. This may be attributed to prolonged persistence of pro-inflammatory macrophages and neutrophils in the absence of TREM2, which may, in turn, activate pancreatic stellate cells and enhance ECM production.42,43 Collectively, these findings highlight that TREM2+ macrophages exhibit context-dependent roles in fibrotic processes, exerting either profibrotic or antifibrotic effects depending on the specific disease microenvironment.

The establishment of an early proregenerative niche is crucial for tissue regeneration.15 Although ADM is a reversible process that allows pancreatic acinar regeneration, persistent inflammation or injury can lead to the development of pancreatic intraepithelial neoplasia.44 Transient activation of the Hh signaling pathway following AP injury has been shown to support pancreatic regeneration,11 whereas inactivation of Hh signaling prevents the restoration of tissue homeostasis.45 Given the pivotal role of Hh signaling in pancreatic repair, we investigated its regulation by TREM2+ macrophages. TREM2 deficiency in macrophages resulted in downregulation of protein secretion pathways, consistent with both in vitro and in vivo findings showing reduced Dhh expression. Furthermore, we found negligible expression of Shh and Ihh in macrophages, suggesting that Dhh is the predominant Hh ligand produced by this population. Our findings indicate that TREM2 deficiency suppressed activation of Hh signaling and significantly delayed pancreatic regeneration. Finally, we show that timely and moderate reactivation of the Hh pathway after AP injury can rescue pancreatic regeneration and improve disease outcomes. These findings define a critical immune–regenerative signaling axis between macrophages and progenitor-like epithelial cell–driven tissue repair. However, sustained or excessive Hh activation may contribute to fibrosis and the development of pancreatic intraepithelial neoplasia.46 When developing Hh pathway modulators, the regenerative benefits must be weighed against the risks of fibrosis or tumorigenesis.

Furthermore, our study is the first to confirm the presence of TREM2+ macrophages within pathologic tissue of patients with AP and establish an inverse correlation between ssTREM2 levels and severity of AP. The modest inverse relationship is consistent with the proposed anti-inflammatory role of TREM2+ macrophages. sTREM2 is primarily generated through ADAM10/17-mediated ectodomain shedding of membrane-bound TREM2. Circulating sTREM2 often serves as a proxy for the abundance or activation state of TREM2-expressing macrophages, as evidenced in various diseases.26,47 The reduced sTREM2 in SAP may therefore reflect insufficient recruitment, polarization, or reparative activity of TREM2+ macrophages—consistent with the impaired inflammation resolution and regeneration observed in our Trem2ΔMye models. Therefore, ssTREM2 holds promise as a noninvasive biomarker for monitoring inflammatory resolution, pancreatic repair progression, and potentially insufficient TREM2+ macrophage activity during AP. The lack of a significant difference between moderately severe and severe AP cases may be attributable to the limited sample size in our cohort. Future studies with larger, longitudinal patient cohorts are warranted to validate the generalizability of sTREM2 as a prognostic tool for disease progression, severity stratification, and therapeutic response in AP.

In summary, our findings demonstrate that TREM2+ macrophages play a central role during inflammation resolution and tissue repair. They promote pancreatic regeneration by coordinating cholesterol metabolism and activating Hh signaling in crosstalk with epithelial cells. By coupling immunometabolic reprogramming with developmental Hh signaling, this study delineates a mechanistic paradigm for pancreatic injury and repair, further suggesting the outstanding roles of macrophages in tissue regeneration. These insights establish a therapeutic framework for pancreatic regeneration, proposing targeted modulation of TREM2+ macrophage functions as novel strategies to treat pancreatitis or pancreatic insufficiency.

Materials and Methods

Materials

The primary antibodies employed in the study include anti-amylase (sc-46657) from Santa Cruz, anti-Ki67 (ab16667) from Abcam, anti-CK19 (ab52625) from Abcam, anti-TREM2 (ab305103) from Abcam, anti-F4/80 (#70076S) from Cell Signaling Technology, anti-iNOS (18985-1-AP) from Proteintech, anti-CD206 (ab64693) from Abcam, anti-F4/80 (28463-1-AP) from Proteintech, anti-CD45 (#70257) from Cell Signaling Technology, anti-CD68 (#76437) from Cell Signaling Technology, anti-Sox9 (#82630) from Cell Signaling Technology, anti-Gli1 (66905-1-Ig) from Proteintech, anti-Dhh monoclonal antibody (mAb; ab270453) from Abcam, anti-Smo mAb (ER64985) from HUABIO, and anti β-actin mAb (T0022) from Affinity. Citrate antigen retrieval solution (E673002) was purchased from Sangon. Vectastain Elite ABC kit (PK-6101) was purchased from Vector Laboratories. Caerulein (cat#HY-A0190) was purchased from MedChemExpress. NaT (86,339-5G) was purchased from Sigma-Aldrich. SAG (HY-12848) was purchased from MedChemExpress.

Human Samples

Analysis of samples from patients with AP was approved by the Ethics Committee of Shanghai General Hospital, China (ethics no. 2020-224). Patients diagnosed with AP based on the revised Atlanta classification28 and admitted to the Department of Gastroenterology or Emergency between February 2023 and October 2024 were enrolled. Individuals with coexisting inflammatory diseases and tumors were excluded. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants. Paraffin-embedded sections of pancreatic cancer tissue and adjacent acute pancreatitis regions were provided by Dr Jiang Long (ethics no. 2021-130, Shanghai General Hospital) for histologic and IHC analysis.

Collection of Peripheral Blood Samples From Patients With Acute Pancreatitis

Peripheral blood samples (5 mL) were collected in EDTA-coated tubes at designated timepoints from 48 hours to 14 days following AP onset. Demographic characteristics are shown in Table 1. After centrifugation, the serum supernatant was isolated for analysis.

Mice

C57BL/6 J mice were purchased from Shanghai SLAC Laboratory Animal Co, Ltd. CD45.1+ mice were provided by Professor Honglin Wang (Shanghai General Hospital), Ms4a3CreRosa26tdTomato mice by Professor Bing Su48 (Shanghai Jiao Tong University School of Medicine), and LysMCreTrem2fl/fl (Trem2ΔMye) mice by Professor Deping Kong (Shanghai General Hospital). All mice were age-matched (6–8 weeks) and sacrificed at the indicated timepoints following AP induction. Animal experiments were approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of Shanghai General Hospital (2019-A053-01).

Animal Care and Monitoring

All mice were maintained under specific pathogen-free conditions in individually ventilated cages (5 mice per cage) with wood shavings as bedding, under a 12-hour light/dark cycle at 22 °C, with ad libitum access to water and standard rodent chow. Mice were earmarked and randomly assigned to experimental groups (n = 3–8 per group) using a randomization table. Pathologists assessing the histologic outcomes were blinded to group allocation, although blinding was not applied during experimental procedures. All animal experiments adhered to the principles of replacement, refinement, and reduction (the 3Rs) and were conducted in accordance with relevant animal welfare legislation. This study is reported in compliance with the Animal Research: Reporting of In Vivo Experiments (ARRIVE) guidelines.49

Animal Experiments

Acute biliary pancreatitis was induced by retrograde infusion of 2.5% NaT into the biliopancreatic duct at 7.5 μL/min using an infusion pump.21 Pancreatic RAP model was induced by caerulein administration as previously described.50 The final caerulein injection was defined as day 0. For Hh pathway activation, SAG (MedChemExpress, HY-12848) was prepared in dimethyl sulfoxide, diluted in sterile saline, and administered intraperitoneally at 10 mg/kg on day 0. Control mice received vehicle only. Pancreatic tissues were collected on day 3 post-AP induction.

Serum Amylase and Lipase Assay

Serum was isolated via centrifugation at 400 g for 15 minutes at 4 °C. Amylase activity in the supernatant was quantified using a Roche/Hitachi Modular Analytics System (Roche Diagnostics), following the manufacturer’s calibration protocols.

Flow Cytometry and Cell Sorting

Pancreatic leukocytes were isolated for flow cytometry analysis using a collagenase digestion technique as previously described.4,51 Briefly, the pancreas was minced into small pieces and incubated in fluorescence-activated cell sorting buffer (phosphate-buffered saline + 10% fetal bovine serum) containing 2 mg/mL collagenase type IV. The tissue was then shaken at 37 °C for 10 to 15 minutes, followed by vortexing at low speed for 20 seconds. The digested mixture was filtered through a 70-μm mesh, and the cell pellet was dipped in red blood cell lysing buffer for 5 minutes. The cells were centrifuged and collected for cell surface or intracellular staining. Murine pancreatic myeloid cells were sorted according to CD11b expression.

Single-Cell RNA-Sequencing

CD11b+ cells from 5 mice per time point were pooled to ensure sufficient cell numbers for sequencing, and key findings were validated using alternative verification methods (eg, flow cytometry). OE Biotech completed the single-cell sequencing and data analysis. According to the manufacturer’s protocol, scRNA-seq libraries were prepared with Chromium Single Cell 3’ Reagent v2 Kits. Single-cell suspensions were loaded on a Chromium Single Cell Controller Instrument (10x Genomics) to generate single-cell gel beads in emulsions. Briefly, a total of 500,000 single cells, derived from an average of 5 mice, were suspended in phosphate-buffered saline without calcium and magnesium. Approximately 20,000 cells were added to each channel, with a targeted cell recovery estimate of 15,000 cells. Data quality statistics were performed on the raw data by using the 10x Genomics official software, Cell Ranger (version 9.0.0). Based on the Cell Ranger preliminary quality control, the Seurat (version 4.0.0) software package is used to further quality-control process the data. Detected gene numbers less than 200, unique molecular identifier less than 1000, and log10 genes per unique molecular identifier less than 0.7 were ruled out. Low-quality cells where >10% of the counts belonged to mitochondrial genes and >5% of the counts belonged to hemoglobin genes were discarded. A total of 66,440 single cells were included in downstream analysis after removal of the double cells using DoubletFinder software.

Reclustering

To further resolve heterogeneity within monocyte, macrophage, and dendritic cell populations, these cells were subsetted from the CD11b+ myeloid compartment and reclustered using Seurat. Secondary clustering was performed with the Louvain algorithm (via FindClusters function) at a resolution of 1.0, which effectively separated cDC2 cells from macrophages and improved the purity of the macrophage population. Cluster-specific marker genes were then identified using FindAllMarkers function in Seurat with min.pct = 0 and logfc.threshold = 1.5. This analysis yielded 20 subclusters, which were annotated based on their differentially expressed genes. Details of the subclustering parameters, their correspondence to the original CD11b+ myeloid clusters, and dot plots showing representative markers (eg, Ly6c2, Zbtb46, C1qa, and Mrc1) are provided in Figure 3C.

Scoring of Biological Processes

The AddModuleScore function in the Seurat R package was used to calculate the scores for the cholesterol synthesis and cholesterol efflux gene sets. Specifically, the function computed the average expression levels of each program (cluster) at the single-cell level, subtracting the aggregated expression of control feature sets. All analyzed features were binned based on averaged expression, and the control features were randomly selected from each bin.

Gene Set Variation Analysis

The background gene set file was downloaded and compiled from the Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes database using the GSEABase package (version 1.44.0). Pathway activity scores for individual cells were calculated using the GSVA package (version 1.30.0). Finally, the LIMMA package (version 3.38.3) was employed to assess the differences in signaling pathway activity between different groups.

RNA Velocity Analysis

RNA velocity analysis of cells was conducted using the Velocyto program.24 This method utilizes the relative abundance of unspliced and spliced mRNA to predict the future state of cells. The calculated RNA velocity represents a vector that forecasts cell transitions, with the direction and speed of each transition determined by the amplitude and direction of the velocity arrows on the t-SNE plot. Thus, the hierarchical relationships between cell populations can be inferred based on the directional flow within the RNA velocity vector field. Initially, annotation of spliced and unspliced reads was performed using the velocyto.py command-line tools. Subsequent analysis was carried out using Velocyto.R pipeline. Genes expressed in at least 1 cell population were retained. RNA velocities for each cell were estimated using the gene.relative.velocity.estimates function. Lastly, the velocity field was mapped onto the existing t-SNE space.

Mixed Bone Marrow Chimeras

To establish BM chimeras, the recipient CD45.1+ mice were exposed to 9 Gy γ radiation in 2 doses, ∼3 hours apart. Subsequently, 5 ×106 BM cells isolated from CD45.2+ donors were infused into recipients via the tail vein. Before inducing AP, the chimeric mice were given approximately 3 weeks to recover, ensuring stable engraftment.

Histology

Pancreas samples were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde, embedded in paraffin, and cut into 4-μm transverse sections at different levels. Paraffin sections were used for hematoxylin and eosin staining. For immunofluorescence staining, antigen retrieval of immunofluorescence staining was achieved with heat in citrate antigen retrieval solution. After antigen blocking, primary antibodies were incubated overnight at 4 °C. The primary antibodies used were anti-amylase at a dilution of 1:100, anti-Ki67 at 1:100, anti-CK19 at 1:100, anti-F4/80 at 1:400, anti-iNOS at 1:500, and anti-CD206 at 1:1000. After secondary antibody incubation, 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole was used for nuclear staining. The sections were imaged using a confocal microscope (LEICA, SP8). For IHC staining, the sections were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies: anti-TREM2 at a dilution of 1:100, anti-F4/80 at 1:400, anti-CD45 at 1:100, anti-CD68 at 1:400, anti-Ki67 at 1:100, anti-Sox9 at 1:200, and anti-Gli1 at 1:200 after blocking endogenous peroxidase activity. Following incubation, a Vectastain Elite ABC kit was applied according to the manufacturer’s instructions. After visualization with 3,3'-diaminobenzidine, the sections were counterstained with hematoxylin. For Sirius Red staining, sections were deparaffinized, and the slides were incubated for 30 minutes in a solution of saturated picric acid containing 0.1% Sirius red and 0.1% fast green.

Isolation and Intervention of Bone Marrow–Derived Macrophages

BM cells were prepared as previously described.52 To induce macrophage polarization, BMDMs were stimulated with lipopolysaccharide (100 ng/mL) and interferon-γ (10 ng/mL) for pro-inflammatory activation, or with IL-4 (50 ng/mL) for anti-inflammatory activation. For Trem2 overexpression, BMDMs were collected on day 4 of differentiation, seeded, and transduced with adenoviral vectors at a multiplicity of infection of 500. Transduction efficiency was assessed by fluorescence microscopy at 24 and 48 hours postinfection, followed by cytokine-induced polarization.

Western Blotting

Pancreatic tissue and BMDM protein extracts were prepared as previously described.51,53 Equal protein amounts (40 μg/lane) were separated by 7.5% or 12.5% sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (Epizyme Biotech) and transferred to nitrocellulose membranes (Millipore). Membranes were incubated with primary antibodies (TREM2/Dhh/Smo/Gli1, 1:1000. β-actin, 1:20,000) overnight at 4 °C, followed by probing with secondary antibodies for 1 hour at 37 °C. Protein bands were visualized using Odyssey CLx (LI-COR) and quantified via ImageStudio, normalized to β-actin. Results are presented as fold changes relative to the control.

RNA Extraction and Quantitative Reverse Transcription Polymerase Chain Reaction

Total RNA from pancreatic tissue or cells were extracted by TRIzol or by RNeasy Micro Kit (QIAGEN) following manufacturer’s instructions. Then, complementary DNA was synthesized by reverse transcription using PrimeScript RT Master Mix (TAKARA, RR036A). Gene expression was quantified using qPCR TB Green Master Mix (TAKARA, RR420A). The expression levels of target genes were normalized to Rplp0 and calculated using 2−ΔΔCt method. Statistical Analysis

The Mann–Whitney test was used to compare 2 unpaired data sets that were not normally distributed. For normally distributed, unpaired data, the t-test was applied. One-way analysis of variance was used for comparisons among multiple groups. A P value of < .05 was regarded as statistically significant. The results are shown as the mean ± standard error of the mean (Prism 8; GraphPad Software).
