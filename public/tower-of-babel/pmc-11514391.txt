Psychological predictors of incident subjective cognitive complaints in community dwelling older adults
Authors: Amber Khan, Emmeline I. Ayers, Joe Verghese, Mirnova E. Ceïde
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSubjective cognitive complaint (SCC) is associated with future cognitive decline and may be a marker for clinical intervention in the progression to dementia. Among the viable predictors of SCC, psychological factors are clinically relevant, non-invasive early indicators of older adults at elevated risk. This aim of this study is to determine whether psychological symptoms: dysphoria and apathy precede incident SCC in the dementia pathway.MethodsParticipants (n = 592) enrolled in the Central Control of Mobility in Aging Study were includes in the analyses, with prevalent cases excluded. Apathy and dysphoria scale scores were derived using confirmatory factor analysis of the Geriatric Depressive Scale. Cox regression analyses was used to determine the association between apathy and dysphoria scores and incident SCC.ResultsOver a mean follow up of 1.90 years, 44 individuals (9.26%) developed incident SCC. Baseline apathy scale score was significantly associated with 4-fold increased risk of SCC (HR 4.39, 95%CI: 1.32–14.67), adjusted for cognition but not age and dysphoria scale score. Baseline dysphoria scale score was not associated with increased risk of SCC in adjusted analyses.ConclusionIn this longitudinal analysis of community dwelling older adults, apathy was associated with an increased risk of SCC, when adjusting for cognition but not dysphoria. Finally, this study highlights apathy as an early risk factor, which may precede SCC in the progression to dementia and consequently, may identify a high risk group for clinical screening and intervention.

Introduction

In the new era of disease modifying therapies for Alzheimer’s disease (AD), it is critical to identify older adults are at highest risk for AD, in order to evaluate for AD biomarkers and intervene early in the disease progression [1]. Elucidating pre-dementia syndromes can help to identify high-risk populations and inform potential disease pathways and establish treatment targets [2] Older adults with subjective cognitive complaints (SCC) may be at greater risk of developing dementia. SCC are reports of cognitive decline irrespective of findings on objective neuropsychological tests [3]. SCC may be a clinical marker of the progression from pre-dementia to dementia [4]. The association between SCC and future cognitive decline has been established in longitudinal, population-based studies [5–8]. Yet, there is limited data for predictors of incident SCC, which is critical for understanding the progression from normal aging to dementia and opportunities for early intervention. Similarly, depression and apathy are also known to be associated with later cognitive decline and dementia [9–12]. However, whether psychological factors, such as depression or apathy precede future SCC or contribute to dementia progression in an alternate pathway is unclear. Among the viable predictors of SCC, psychological factors are clinically relevant, non-invasive early indicators of older adults at elevated risk.

Cross sectional community-based studies have found an association between depressive symptoms and SCC [8,13–18]. However, these studies do not address the directionality of this association, and there are only a few longitudinal studies examining the association between affective symptoms and incident SCC. One longitudinal study showed that depressive symptoms were not associated with future SCC [19]. In contrast to this negative study, a multicenter study of motoric cognitive risk syndrome (MCR), a predementia syndrome characterized by SCC and slow gait, found that depressive symptoms predicted MCR [20]. Thus, the existing longitudinal data on the association between depressive symptoms and incident SCC is mixed.

Similarly, apathy has been associated with incident MCR, non-amnestic MCI, and dementia [10,11,21]. Lugtenburg and colleagues showed that the cognitive/motivational domain of depression predicted the onset of dementia but not when adjusted for subjective cognitive complaints [12]. The relationship of apathy to SCC is unclear from this study because the cognitive/motivational domain included both SCC and apathy symptoms. It remains unknown from this study is the direction of association between apathy and SCC.

When discussing depression as a risk factor for cognitive decline, it is important to clarify that the clinical diagnosis of depression includes dysphoria and apathy symptoms. Dysphoria is characterized by an unpleasant mood with uneasiness, irritability, and hopelessness [22] whereas apathy has been characterized by a blunted or lack of affect and lack of initiation and motivation [23]. Many studies that have investigated the association between depression and SCC do not distinguish between dysphoria and apathy domains. Prior evidence suggests that apathy and dysphoria components of the construct of depression may have independent effects on future cognition [9,24]. Also, apathy may mediate the association between the construct of depression and poor cognitive and motor outcomes [25,26].

To our knowledge, there are no longitudinal studies specifically looking at the association between apathy or dysphoria and incident SCC. The aim of this longitudinal, community-based study is to determine whether dysphoria and apathy in older adults independently predict incident SCC. We specifically use the term dysphoria to highlight the positive symptoms of depression. In this study, we use ‘subjective cognitive complaint’ to broadly encompass all areas of cognition, recognizing that a cognitive complaint may not be restricted only to memory. In addition, we restrict our definition to ‘complaint’ rather than ‘decline’ given the measures of assessment used in this analysis most specifically capture cognitive ‘complaints’ rather than total decline. This study contributes to the important clinical and public health challenge of identifying older adults at risk of dementia early in the natural history of the disease.

Methods

Study population

The Central Control of Mobility in Aging Study (CCMA) is a prospective cohort study that enrolled community dwelling older adults (>65 years and older) without dementia. The purpose of this study is to identify cognitive and neural predictors of mobility in later life. Participants residing in Westchester, New York were identified through population lists and were recruited by mail and telephone. A telephone screening interview was used to assess eligibility. Participants were excluded from the study if they had psychiatric disorders, hearing or vision impairment, inability to ambulate independently, baseline dementia, recent or upcoming medical procedure that would affect mobility, were receiving hemodialysis, or spoke a primary language other than English. Participants received comprehensive neuropsychological, psychological, and cognitive and mobility testing and a neurologic exam. Participants were enrolled between 2011 and 2017 and were re-evaluated annually. The enrollment and cohort design are described in Figure 1. Participants provided written informed consent and the study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of the Albert Einstein College of Medicine (#2010-224) [27]. This study adheres to the Declaration of Helsinki.

Flowchart for enrollment of participants.

Assessment of apathy

In this analysis, apathy was identified using two distinct measures. For the first measure, we employed confirmatory factor analysis of the Geriatric Depression Scale [28] (GDS) to yield a 2-factor measurement model, utilizing 15 of the items: Dysphoria (8 items; Cronbach’s α = 0.75) and Apathy (7 items; Cronbach’s α = 0.63), with good model fit (Table 1). Using this measurement model, we computed scale scores using the mean of the items in each factor. This provides a robust measure to minimize type II error.

The second measure characterizes apathy as a dichotomous variable (GDS3A), which is derived from an apathy subscale using three items from the GDS short form [29,30]. The three items in the apathy subscale include these questions: Have you dropped many of your activities and interests? Do you prefer to stay at home, rather than going out and doing new things? Do you feel full of energy? An affirmative response to the first two questions and negative response to the third question were considered positive responses. A score of ≥ 2 indicates the presence of apathy. Several studies, including the CCMA cohort of this study, have previously used this apathy subscale to explore inflammatory and biological correlates in community dwelling populations [11,31,32]. This subscale has varied sensitivity (29–69%) and specificity (85–92%) when compared to the Apathy Scale (an abbreviated form of the Apathy Evaluation Scale) in community dwelling populations [31,33].

Assessment of dysphoria

Dysphoria was also assessed using two distinct measures. In the first measure, dysphoria was identified using the same confirmatory factor analysis by our group. Dysphoria was derived as a continuous variable from the averaged responses to eight questions from the 30 item GDS, which are shown in Table 1.

In the second measure, dysphoria was measured using the 12 non-apathy related items from the GDS Short Form (GDS12D). A score of ≥ 2 indicates presence of dysphoria. Other longitudinal studies of apathy in community dwelling populations have also used this measurement of dysphoria [11,32].

Assessment of subjective cognitive complaint

While SCC can describe cognitive changes in varied domains, the Subjective Cognitive Decline Initiative (SCD-I) Working Group highlights that the majority of assessments have centered on memory questions [3]. Similarly, we defined SCC using the memory item on the GDS: Do you feel that you have more problems with memory than most? An affirmative response to this question indicated the presence of SCC [34].

Covariates

Covariates were included in the regression model based on bivariate analysis, previous literature, and biological plausibility. Covariates included were medical comorbidities, ethnicity, gender, years of education, age, and cognitive summary score on the Repeatable Battery for the Assessment of Neuropsychological Status (RBANS) [35]. Race and ethnicity were self-identified by the participant in a questionnaire. Cognitive score in this analysis is derived from an age and education adjusted score for global cognition derived from the Repeatable Battery for the Assessment of Neuropsychological Status (RBANS). Medical comorbidities were diagnosed by physicians and characterized categorically by the presence of zero, 1–2, or 3 or more medical comorbidities [36].

Statistical analysis

Bivariate analyses were conducted of baseline characteristics of participants based on SCC status. Differences between participants with and without incident SCC were compared using independent sample t-tests for comparison of continuous variables and Pearson’s chi-square tests for comparison of categorical variables. Cox regression models were used to evaluate the association between apathy, dysphoria, and incident SCC. Models were checked for proportional hazards assumptions graphically and using statistical tests. Covariates were added in a forward stepwise approach based on significance of in bivariate analyses. All statistical analysis was done using SPSS version 29.

Results

The CCMA Cohort consists of 592 community dwelling older adults. Within this sample, 54 people were excluded due to missing data and an additional 63 people were excluded due to baseline SCC. Table 2 includes demographic data for the remaining 475 older adults included in the regression analysis. Participants were followed for a maximum of 6.44 years and a mean of 1.90 (±1.58) years. Overall, this cohort comprised of 55.8% women and 44.2% men, with a mean age of 76.1 (±6.46) years and mean 14.8 (±2.89) years of education. The prevalence of baseline dysphoria was 18.5% and the prevalence of baseline apathy was 27.4%. Within this cohort, 44 individuals (9.26%) developed incident SCC. There were statistically significant differences in baseline age and RBANS cognitive summary score between those who developed incident SCC and those who did not, but there were no significant differences in gender, ethnicity, medical comorbidity, or years of education.

Given the lack of association with the dependent variable of interest and the limited number of cases of SCC, Cox regression models were not adjusted for gender, ethnicity, medical comorbidity, or years of education. Baseline apathy scale score was significantly associated with 4-fold increased risk of SCC (HR 4.39, 95%CI: 1.32–14.67), adjusted for cognition but not age and dysphoria scale score (Table 3). Baseline dysphoria scale score was only associated with a 5-fold increased risk of SCC in crude analysis (HR 5.15, 95%CI: 1.19–22.41), but not adjusted for overall cognition, age, or apathy scale score (Table 4). Cox regression models of categorical apathy and dysphoria similarly showed no associations with incident SCC.

Discussion

The objective of our study was to understand the relationship between psychologic symptoms and SCC, as they are all early risk factors for dementia [5–9,24]. In this study, we found that apathy was associated with a 4-fold risk of incident SCC, adjusted for cognition but not age or dysphoria. Conversely, there was no association between dysphoria and incident SCC, in adjusted analyses. These findings contribute to the limited literature on predictors of subjective cognitive complaints. Furthermore, in our study, age and cognitive performance are important confounders in the relationship between psychological factors: dysphoria and apathy and cognitive complaints.

It is notable that although both apathy and dysphoria are components of ‘depression’, the risk of SCC was increased with higher levels of apathy but not dysphoria; even when adjusting for cognitive performance. This is consistent with the extant literature that suggests that apathy mediates the previously described association between depression and cognitive/functional decline [25,26,37,38]. Chilovi and colleagues have also described a differential rate of progression from MCI to dementia, in which people with apathy had higher rates of progression to dementia than those with depression (dysphoria) [39]. Our findings and the extant literature suggest that apathy in addition to subjective cognitive complaints should be prioritized for risk stratification and a novel target for Alzheimer’s disease prevention screening.

Strengths of this study include use of a longitudinal design in a well described community dwelling cohort. Longitudinal study designs can help elucidate the pathway from normal aging to dementia and identify early points of intervention [40,41]. Additionally, a community dwelling cohort is particularly suited for identifying risk factors for dementia compared to a medically ill population.

Our study has some limitations. While the baseline apathy rates were consistent with those reported in other community dwelling studies [9,11,32], we utilized items from the Geriatric Depression Scale rather than using an apathy specific measure like the Apathy Evaluation Scale [42]. There is no well-established gold standard instrument for assessing apathy [43]. Larger cohort studies including the Baltimore Epidemiologic Catchment Area study [9] and the Prevention of Dementia by Intensive Vascular Care trial [11] assessed apathy using subscales of depression assessment tools. We used confirmatory factor analysis of the GDS to measure apathy. Confirmatory factor analysis showed good reliability and validity, which was practically beneficial to this study given the extended time that would have been needed to supply additional scales.

Similarly, we measured SCC using a single item response in the GDS, which focused on memory, even though the concept of SCC refers to subjective changes in all domains. Consequently, the sensitivity of this measure may be lower than desired. The Subjective Cognitive Decline Initiative Working Group has noted that 59% of measures of SCC are memory focused but this international consortium was established to address this challenge [44].

Other minor limitations were noted. As this study was conducted in a community dwelling cohort, the population was relatively healthier, and these findings may not be generalizable to clinical settings such as: primary care clinics or long term care settings. The incident rate of SCC in this study was significantly lower than the only known incident rate reported in another community-based study, which limited the analyses and inclusion of potential confounders. In the community-based study by Sakurai et al. which was based in Tokyo and included a cohort of approximately 400 adults aged 65–85, the incidence of SCC was reported as 21.5%. SCC in this cohort was determined by a single response to the question ‘do you have problems with your memory?’ [19]. The reason for discrepancy in incidence rates of SCC are not known, however, it is possible that the type of clinical question matters. Thus, more longitudinal community-based studies of incident SCC are needed. Finally, we evaluate dysphoria and apathy at a single point in time rather than over time, and thus whether apathy and dysphoria persisted over time in this cohort is not known.

Further investigations of the pathway between apathy, SCC, and incident dementia are warranted, especially in cohorts with higher rates of SCC. Future directions should include psychometric evaluation of the reliability of different assessments of SCC in populations with varying levels of apathy. Further, mediation analysis could be used to elucidate these relationships further.

Another inquiry may be whether persistent apathy over time impacts the incidence of SCC. Finally, in situations in which people with SCC go on to develop apathy, whether psychological factors moderate the relationship between SCC and dementia thus indicating an alternate pathway.

Conclusion

This study adds to the literature on SCC and psychological risk factors for dementia. This is one of the few longitudinal analyses of incident SCC and the first to our knowledge looking at the relationship between psychological factors and incident SCC. We found no association between dysphoria and incident SCC in a population of community dwelling older adults, in adjusted analyses. Apathy was associated with a 4-fold increased risk of SCC, when adjusting for cognition but not the other important confounders. Finally, this study highlights apathy as an early risk factor, which may precede SCC in the progression to dementia and consequently, may identify a high risk group for clinical screening and intervention.
