Alcohol policy modelling studies require realistic assumptions—authors’ reply
Authors: Daniela Correia, Maria Neufeld, Carina Ferreira-Borges, Beatrice Lauby-Secretan, Jürgen Rehm
Published: 2026

We thank Manthey and Kilian for their thoughtful comments1 on our study,2 which models the potential cancer-preventive impact of alcohol control policies in the EU. Given alcohol's substantial contribution to the EU's cancer burden, understanding how marketing bans and other control measures can influence public health is crucial.

Our modelling builds directly on IARC Handbook 20B (3; see also4), a multi-year undertaking involving more than 25 international experts. The primary aim of the Handbook was to establish causality between alcohol policies and changes in consumption based on rigorous and transparent criteria, rather than to identify the most realistic real-world effect size for each policy. In estimating the impact on cancer incidence we did not seek to revisit the Handbook's deliberations, but relied on its conclusions.

One of the main policies that was evaluated and found to have sufficient evidence of a causal effect on reducing alcohol consumption was a strong ban on alcohol marketing, supported by four studies.3 We selected Rossow's Norwegian advertising ban study5 because alcohol marketing, and therefore the impact of marketing restrictions, is strongly shaped by social and cultural factors. Norway's similarity to the EU in this regard makes it the most relevant study for modelling the policy's impact in the EU.

Regarding Nelson's analyses on ban on marketing,6 also mentioned by Manthey and Kilian, many variants of the statistical analyses are presented, with mixed results. This inconsistency illustrates the instability of the marketing-ban evidence base, reinforcing our decision not to rely on this study. Accordingly, we agree that the evidence for marketing bans is less robust than for taxation and availability restrictions, both in number and age of studies; this difference is already explicit in the Handbook. Marketing bans require further research, as the volume of existing reviews and commentaries far outpaces rigorous empirical studies.

On the adolescent-specific mechanism, we agree this is plausible and complementary, rather than contradictory, to our findings. A comprehensive marketing ban in Lithuania was associated with a 35% reduction in the frequency of adolescent intoxication (IRR = 0.65, 95% CI: 0.56–0.77), plus smaller reductions in alcohol consumption (12%) and binge drinking (18%), within one year of implementation.7 Conversely, Rossow's estimate reflects a longer window immediately following implementation (1960–2006) alongside an average effect over 1975–2006 for a general-population outcome.5 These findings suggest marketing bans may operate through different mechanisms and timescales. In this sense, we welcome the correspondents' call for more nuanced, age- and time-stratified modelling of the impact of strong marketing bans on alcohol consumption and associated harm.
