Electrospun Flexible P(VDF-HFP) Composite Films Incorporated with Gd/Zr Codoped BiFeO3 Nanoparticles for Synergistic Piezophotocatalytic Degradation of Ofloxacin
Authors: Xuan Liu, Liangliang Chang, Aiguo He, Zengzhe Xi, Chunsheng Zhou, Baoyue Cao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The persistence of fluoroquinolone antibiotics like OFL in aquatic environments poses a significant ecological and public health risk due to their poor biodegradability and potential to induce antibiotic resistance. This study reports the fabrication of free-standing, flexible nanofibrous films via electrospinning, incorporating optimal BGFZ9 nanoparticles into a P­(VDF-HFP) matrix (denoted as P­(VDF-HFP)@BGFZ9). Comprehensive characterization reveals that the BGFZ9 nanoparticles act as effective heterogeneous nucleating agents, significantly promoting the formation of piezoelectric β-phase in P­(VDF-HFP) and enhancing the ferroelectric polarization of the composite through interfacial coupling. Under ultrasonic excitation, the composite film exhibits remarkable piezocatalytic performance for OFL degradation, following pseudo-first-order kinetics. The film with an optimal 10% BGFZ9 loading achieves a reaction rate constant of 0.684 min–1·g–1 under piezocatalysis, which is approximately 1.22 times higher than that of the pure polymer film. A synergistic piezophotocatalytic effect is observed under combined ultrasonic and light irradiation, leading to an OFL removal efficiency of 85.71% within 3 h and a further increased rate constant of 1.043 min–1·g–1. Radical scavenger experiments and EPR spectroscopy identify superoxide radicals •O2 – and h + as the primary reactive species responsible for the degradation. The composite film demonstrates excellent cycling stability, retaining 94.4% of its initial activity after five consecutive runs, alongside robust structural integrity. This work provides a novel strategy for developing efficient, stable, and easily recyclable flexible piezocatalytic materials for antibiotic wastewater remediation.

Introduction

The widespread detection of fluoroquinolone antibiotics, particularly ofloxacin (OFL), in aquatic environments poses a significant threat to ecosystem integrity and public health due to their persistence, poor biodegradability, and potential to induce antibiotic resistance. − Conventional water treatment processes often exhibit limited efficacy against such recalcitrant micropollutants, necessitating the development of advanced oxidation technologies. Piezocatalysis, which harnesses mechanical energy to induce polarization in piezoelectric materials for the generation of reactive oxygen species (ROS), has emerged as a promising and sustainable remediation strategy. , Recent comprehensive reviews have systematically summarized the significant advancements in piezocatalytic materials, mechanisms, and their applications for environmental remediation, providing valuable context for understanding the current state of the field. , Mechanistic investigations elucidate that ROS, including piezoelectrically generated holes (h +), superoxide radicals (•O2 –), and hydroxyl radicals (•OH), play a decisive role in the degradation process. These species selectively attack the piperazine ring of OFL through pathways such as decarboxylation, demethylation, and C–N bond cleavage, ultimately leading to its complete mineralization.

Among various piezoelectric materials, multiferroic bismuth ferrite (BiFeO3, BFO) nanoparticles are particularly attractive due to their room-temperature ferroelectricity, narrow bandgap (∼2.2 eV), and visible-light responsiveness. − However, the practical application of pristine BFO is hindered by intrinsic limitations, including rapid charge carrier recombination and a relatively low piezoelectric coefficient (d 33), which constrain its catalytic efficiency. − Strategic ion doping has been demonstrated as an effective approach to modulate these properties. For instance, Gd3+ substitution at the Bisite can enhance ferroelectric polarization by suppressing spiral spin structures and oxygen vacancy (OV), while Zr4+ doping at the Fe-site can reduce the bandgap energy and improve charge mobility, thereby synergistically boosting the piezocatalytic performance. , Recent studies on other semiconductor systems, such as Mo-doped BiVO4 for photoelectrocatalytic OFL degradation, have also highlighted the critical role of enhancing the built-in electric field through crystal dipole engineering for superior charge separation and catalytic activity. While such strategies significantly improve performance, they often require an external electrical bias for operation.

Despite these advancements, powdered catalysts face practical challenges in real-world water treatment, such as agglomeration, difficult separation, and potential secondary pollution, which impede their scalable application. To address these issues, the integration of active inorganic nanoparticles into a flexible, processable, and inherently piezoelectric polymer matrix to form self-supporting composite films presents a viable solution. Poly­(vinylidene fluoride-co-hexafluoropropylene) (P­(VDF-HFP)) stands out as an ideal polymeric host due to its excellent chemical stability, flexibility, and significant piezoelectric response. The electrospinning process can further induce a high content of the electroactive β-phase in P­(VDF-HFP), enhancing its intrinsic piezoelectricity. , To address the separation and recovery challenge, various strategies have been explored. These include immobilizing catalysts on supports, developing magnetic catalysts for easy magnetic retrieval, and incorporating active components into polymer matrices to form membranes or films. For instance, magnetic catalysts like Fe3O4@S-doped ZnO offer convenient separation but may suffer from metal ion leaching and reduced active surface area. Similarly, polymer-supported catalytic membranes enhance recoverability but often rely on additional chemical oxidants or complex multistep fabrication processes. Therefore, developing a self-supported, flexible catalytic film that combines high intrinsic activity, robust stability, and exceptional ease of recovery without requiring external bias or chemical additives represents a highly desirable yet challenging goal. The incorporation of active inorganic fillers like doped BFO can act as a nucleating agent to promote β-phase formation and establish a local built-in electric field at the filler–polymer interface, facilitating charge separation and yielding a synergistic enhancement in the overall piezoelectric and catalytic response. , This approach uniquely leverages mechanical energy as the sole driving force, eliminating the need for external electrical power or chemical oxidants, while the monolithic film format guarantees effortless recovery and reuse.

Therefore, this work focuses on the fabrication of flexible P­(VDF-HFP)@BGFZ9 composite nanofiber films via electrospinning, where BGFZ9 represents the optimally Gd/Zr codoped BiFeO3 nanoparticles. The study systematically investigates the effect of BGFZ9 incorporation on the composite’s phase composition, morphology, and surface chemistry. The piezocatalytic performance for OFL degradation under mechanical agitation is evaluated in detail, along with an assessment of the film’s cycling stability. This study aims to elucidate the synergistic catalytic mechanism between the polymeric piezoelectric matrix and the inorganic active component and to demonstrate the superior practical advantages of this free-standing flexible film system compared to other recyclable catalyst forms. The findings are expected to pave the way for developing efficient, stable, and easily recyclable flexible piezocatalytic films, thereby offering a novel and sustainable approach for environmental remediation applications.

Experimental Section

Materials and Reagents

All substances and chemicals were utilized as received, without any further purification. Bi­(NO3)3·5H2O (99.9%), Gd­(NO3)3·5H2O (99.9%), Fe­(NO3)3·9H2O (99.9%), and Zr­(NO3)2·2H2O (99.5%) were bought from Rhawn. Tartaric acid (>99.5%) was bought from Aladdin. Poly­(vinylidene fluoride-co-hexafluoropropylene) (P­(VDF-HFP), Melt index: 3∼8 g/10 min; Tm: 138∼142 °C), Dimethylacetamide (DMAC, 99.8%, Water ≤ 50 ppm), and Ofloxacin (98.0%) were purchased from Macklin.

Synthesis and Characterization of P­(VDF-HFP)@BGFZ9 Composite Films

BGFZ9 Nanoparticles

The BGFZ9 nanoparticles were synthesized via a tartaric acid-modified sol–gel method. Stoichiometric amounts of Bi­(NO3)3·5H2O, Fe­(NO3)3·9H2O, Gd­(NO3)3·5H2O, and Zr­(NO3)2·2H2O were dissolved in deionized water under continuous magnetic stirring at ambient temperature. Dilute HNO3 was added dropwise during stirring to inhibit the hydrolysis of Bi3+ and to obtain a homogeneous, transparent solution. Tartaric acid was then introduced as a chelating agent (at a 1:1 molar ratio to the total metal ions). The solution was heated at 120 °C under reflux until complete solvent evaporation, yielding a porous brown precursor gel. The dried gel was subsequently calcined in air at 550 °C for 1 h to remove organic residues and crystallize the perovskite phase.

P­(VDF-HFP)@BGFZ9 Composite Films

The P­(VDF-HFP)@BGFZ9 composite films were fabricated via a standard electrospinning technique, as schematically illustrated in Figure . The fabrication process comprised three main steps. First, predetermined amounts of the as-synthesized BGFZ9 nanoparticles (0, 0.2, and 0.4 g, corresponding to weight percentages of 0, 10, and 20% relative to the polymer) were dispersed in 7 mL of N,N-dimethylformamide (DMF) solvent. The mixture was subjected to ultrasonication using a cell disruptor for 30 min to obtain a homogeneous dispersion. Second, 2 g of (P­(VDF-HFP)) pellets was added to the nanoparticle dispersion. The mixture was then heated in an oil bath at 88 °C for 2 h under continuous magnetic stirring until the polymer was completely dissolved, resulting in a viscous and uniform electrospinning precursor solution. Finally, after cooling to room temperature, the precursor solution was loaded into a syringe equipped with a 21-gauge blunt needle. The syringe was mounted onto a programmable electrospinning apparatus. A flat aluminum foil was used as the collector. The electrospinning parameters were optimized as follows: a positive voltage of +13 kV applied to the needle, a negative voltage of −2 kV applied to the collector, a solution feed rate of 0.1 mm/s, a tip-to-collector distance of 15 cm, and a needle translation distance of 40 cm to ensure the formation of a uniform film. The as-spun fibrous mat was carefully peeled off from the Al foil and dried in an oven at 80 °C for 12 h to remove residual solvent, yielding the final free-standing P­(VDF-HFP)@BGFZ9 composite films.

Flow diagram of the preparation of P­(VDF-HFP)@BGFZ9 composite films.

The materials were, respectively, characterized by X-ray diffraction (XRD, X’ Pert Powder PRO, PANalytical, Almelo, The Netherlands) with Cu Kα1 radiation (λ = 1.5418 Å) and operated at 40 kV and 40 mA, which could be used for analyzing the crystal structure of the powder samples. X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS, K-α, Thermo Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA), transmission electron microscopy (TEM, Talos F200x, FEI, Hillsboro, USA), UV–vis spectrophotometer (Cary-5000, Agilent, Santa Clara, CA, USA), an Electron Spin Resonance Spectrometer (EPR, EMXplus-6/1, Bruker, Karlsruhe, Germany), and Electrochemical Workstation (CHI760E, Chenhua, Shanghai, China) were used.

Investigation of Piezophotocatalytic Degradation Performance

The piezophotocatalytic performance of the as-prepared P­(VDF-HFP)@BGFZ9 composite films was evaluated by monitoring the degradation of OFL under different excitation conditions: ultrasonic vibration, simulated solar light irradiation, and their synergistic combination. A standard experimental procedure was followed.

The piezophotocatalytic performance of the as-prepared P­(VDF-HFP)@BGFZ9 composite films was evaluated by monitoring the degradation of OFL under different excitation conditions: ultrasonic vibration alone, simulated solar light irradiation alone, and their synergistic combination. A standard experimental procedure was followed. Briefly, a 50 mg portion of the composite film was immersed in 50 mL of an OFL aqueous solution with an initial concentration of 5 mg L–1 (pH ∼ 6.3) under continuous magnetic stirring. Prior to catalytic testing, the suspension was magnetically stirred in the dark for 30 min to establish an adsorption–desorption equilibrium. Subsequently, the degradation experiments were initiated under the specified conditions. For the experiments involving light, a 300 W xenon lamp (CEL-HXF300, Beijing China Education Au-light Co., Ltd.) equipped with an AM 1.5G filter was used as the simulated solar light source, providing a continuous spectrum with a primary wavelength range of 300–2500 nm. For ultrasonic excitation, a cleaning bath (200 W, 45 kHz) was employed. In the synergistic piezophotocatalysis test, both ultrasonic and light irradiation were applied simultaneously. At predetermined time intervals, approximately 1.0 mL of the reaction mixture was withdrawn. The concentration of OFL in the solution was determined by measuring its absorbance at a characteristic wavelength of ∼287 nm using a UV–vis spectrophotometer. The degradation efficiency (%) was calculated according to eq based on the Lambert–Beer law: 1 degradationrate%=C/C0×100% respectively. C 0 and C denote the initial and postreaction concentrations of the OFL, respectively. To identify the primary reactive oxygen species (ROS) involved in the catalytic process, EPR spectroscopy coupled with spin trapping was employed. For the detection of •OH, 10 mg of the catalyst was dispersed in 1 mL of methanol. For the detection of •O2 –, the catalyst was dispersed in 1 mL of ultrapure water. To each dispersion, 10 μL of a 100 mM aqueous solution of 5,5-dimethyl-1-pyrroline N-oxide (DMPO) was added as a spin-trapping agent. The mixtures were then subjected to ultrasonication (40 kHz) for 30 min under the respective catalytic conditions to allow for the generation and trapping of radicals, forming stable DMPO-radical adducts. The EPR spectra of these adducts were recorded at room temperature using an X-band EPR spectrometer (Bruker A300) with the following standardized parameters: microwave power, 6.32 mW; modulation amplitude, 1 G; sweep width, 100 G. The characteristic signal patterns (a 1:2:2:1 quartet for DMPO-•OH and a 1:1:1:1 quartet for DMPO-•O2 –) were used to unambiguously confirm the generation of specific radicals during the catalytic process

Results and Discussion

Characterization Results

To investigate the influence of BGFZ9 nanoparticles on the microstructure of the composite films from a crystallographic perspective, XRD analysis was performed at room temperature. The results are presented in Figure a. The XRD pattern of the pristine P­(VDF-HFP) film exhibits two distinct diffraction peaks near 18 and 20°. According to the established literature, the peak at approximately 18.1° is assigned to the nonpolar α-phase of P­(VDF-HFP), while the peak at about 20.3° corresponds to its piezoelectrically active β-phase. The coexistence of characteristic peaks for both α- and β-phases in the pure film indicates that the electrospun film is a mixture of these phases. This observation aligns with the well-documented understanding that the high electric field stretching experienced by the polymer jet during electrospinning promotes the formation of the β-phase. The diffraction patterns of the composite films incorporated with BGFZ9 nanoparticles show significant alterations: (1) The relative intensity of the β-phase characteristic peak is enhanced, and its shape becomes sharper upon doping. This suggests that the BGFZ9 nanoparticles act as heterogeneous nucleating agents, promoting the crystallization and preferred orientation of the piezoelectric β-phase within the P­(VDF-HFP) matrix, which is consistent with previous studies on β-phase induction by nanofillers in PVDF-based polymers. (2) Distinct diffraction peaks attributable to the BGFZ9 nanoparticles are observed in the patterns for the 10 and 20% composite films. The intensity of these peaks increases with higher BGFZ9 loading, confirming the successful incorporation and uniform dispersion of the nanoparticles within the P­(VDF-HFP) fibrous matrix, while their crystal structure remains intact after composite formation. (3) The characteristic peaks of the P­(VDF-HFP) β-phase and BGFZ9 coexist in the composite films without noticeable peak shifting, indicating a primarily physical compounding process without chemical reactions that would alter the respective crystal structures. These results collectively demonstrate that BGFZ9 nanoparticles can effectively optimize the crystallization behavior of P­(VDF-HFP) through interfacial effects and spatial confinement.

XRD patterns (a), ATR-FTIR spectra (b), and XPS survey spectrum (c) along with the O 1s high-resolution spectrum (d) of the P­(VDF-HFP)@BGFZ9 composite films.

To further investigate the influence of BGFZ9 nanoparticles on the crystalline phase of the polymer matrix, ATR-FTIR spectroscopy was employed to characterize the P­(VDF-HFP)@BGFZ9 composite film loaded with 10% BGFZ9. This analysis probes the regulatory effect of the nanofiller at the molecular vibration level, with the results presented in Figure b. The spectrum of the neat P­(VDF-HFP) film exhibits distinct absorption bands near 764 and 975 cm–1, which are attributed to the CF2 bending vibration and the CH2 rocking vibration of the nonpolar α-phase, respectively. Concurrently, the characteristic absorption bands observed at 840 and 1276 cm–1 correspond to the coupled mode of CH2 bending and CF2 stretching vibrations and the asymmetric stretching vibration of the CF2 group in the polar β-phase, respectively. This aligns with the XRD results, indicating the coexistence of α- and β-phases, confirming that the electrospun neat polymer film is a mixture of both phases. The FTIR spectrum of the composite film underwent significant changes upon the incorporation of BGFZ9 nanoparticles. Compared to the neat polymer film, the intensities of the characteristic peaks for both the α-phase (∼764, 975 cm–1) and the β-phase (∼840, 1276 cm–1) were notably enhanced. This indicates that the addition of BGFZ9 nanoparticles improved the overall crystallinity of the polymer. The underlying mechanism is that the nanoparticles act as effective heterogeneous nucleation sites, not only inducing the formation of the piezoelectrically active β-phase but also potentially promoting the growth of some α-phase crystals. Specifically, the charges carried on the surface of the BGFZ9 nanoparticles can generate strong ion–dipole interactions with the partially positive CH2 or partially negative CF2 groups in the P­(VDF-HFP) chains. This interfacial interaction provides additional nucleation sites at the nanoparticle/polymer interface, promoting the alignment of polymer chains during crystallization and thereby stabilizing the higher-energy all-trans conformation, i.e., the β-phase. It is important to note that no new characteristic absorption bands or significant shifts of existing bands were observed in the FTIR spectra. This suggests that the interaction between the BGFZ9 nanoparticles and the P­(VDF-HFP) matrix is primarily physical, without chemical reactions that alter their respective structures.

To further investigate the chemical composition and interfacial interactions of the composite films, XPS analysis was conducted (Figure c,d). Compared to the pristine P­(VDF-HFP) film, distinct peaks corresponding to O 1s, Bi 4f, and Fe 2p are clearly observed in the wide-scan spectrum of the composite film, directly confirming the successful incorporation of BGFZ9 nanoparticles into the polymer matrix. Deconvolution of the O 1s high-resolution spectrum (Figure d) reveals three components: peaks at lower binding energies (∼529.0 and ∼530.5 eV) are attributed to lattice oxygen, while a broad peak at a higher binding energy (∼531.5 eV) corresponds to oxygen vacancies. Compared to the pure polymer, the significant increase in the OV peak in the composite film strongly demonstrates that the Gd/Zr codoping strategy successfully introduces abundant oxygen vacancies. During the catalytic process, these OVs play a dual role: first, they act as electron trapping centers, effectively suppressing the recombination of photogenerated carriers and prolonging their lifetime; second, they modify the local electronic structure of the material’s surface, enhancing the adsorption and activation of O2. Under the synergy of the piezoelectric field and OV, the charge separation efficiency is significantly promoted, thereby facilitating the massive generation of ROS such as •O2 – and •OH, which ultimately endows the composite material with excellent OFL degradation performance.

The influence of BGFZ9 nanoparticle doping on the microstructure of the P­(VDF-HFP) composite films was comparatively analyzed using SEM and EDS, with the results presented in Figure . Figure a shows that the pristine P­(VDF-HFP) film exhibits a smooth, continuous, and relatively dense three-dimensional porous network structure composed of interwoven fibers, which is characteristic of the electrospinning process. This structure provides a high specific surface area and porosity, serving as an ideal scaffold for the construction of a piezoelectric-responsive substrate. As shown in Figure b, the incorporation of 10 wt % BGFZ9 nanoparticles leads to a significant morphological change in the composite film. The fiber surfaces become notably rougher with nanoparticles uniformly attached and partially embedded within the fibers. A slight increase in the fiber diameter and a significant enhancement in network porosity are also observed. This structural evolution is attributed to the BGFZ9 nanoparticles acting as heterogeneous nucleation sites during electrospinning, which alter the stability of the polymer jet and the phase separation dynamics, thereby inducing the formation of a more porous and looser fibrous network. The corresponding EDS mapping clearly reveals the uniform distribution of characteristic elements, such as Fe and Bi, throughout the fibrous network, confirming the good dispersion of BGFZ9 nanoparticles within the P­(VDF-HFP) matrix without severe agglomeration. However, when the BGFZ9 doping content is increased to 20 wt % (Figure c), the excessive nanoparticle loading induces adverse structural changes. Local aggregation of nanoparticles occurs on the fiber surfaces and at junctions, compromising the morphological continuity and integrity of some of the fibers. This may introduce defects or stress concentration points within the film, potentially hindering the formation of a uniform built-in electric field. The corresponding EDS map (Figure f) shows higher signal intensities for the elements, but the enhanced signals in localized areas suggest particle enrichment phenomena. In summary, the microstructural analysis indicates that a 10 wt % doping ratio achieves an optimal morphological balance in the P­(VDF-HFP) composite films. This specific loading significantly enhances the film’s surface roughness and porosity, which is beneficial for improving the adsorption capacity and mass transfer, while simultaneously maintaining the continuity of the fibrous network and the homogeneous dispersion of the nanoparticles.

SEM and EDS images of the P­(VDF-HFP)@BGFZ9 composite films: (a, d) 0%, (b, e) 10%, and (c, f) 20%.

To elucidate the effect of BGFZ9 incorporation on the ferroelectric properties of the P­(VDF-HFP) matrix, the local piezoelectric response and ferroelectric domain structure of the composite films were characterized using PFM, a powerful technique for nanoscale visualization of ferroelectric domains. The results are presented in Figure . The pure P­(VDF-HFP) film exhibits relatively weak amplitude signals and low phase contrast in the PFM images, indicating limited ferroelectric polarization response and poorly defined domain structures. In contrast, the composite film with 10% BGFZ9 loading demonstrates significantly enhanced PFM responses. The amplitude signal intensity is markedly increased, and the phase image displays high-contrast features with clear boundaries between bright and dark domains, confirming that the introduction of BGFZ9 nanoparticles effectively enhances the ferroelectric ordering within the film matrix. The local hysteresis loops shown in Figure c,f exhibit characteristic ferroelectric signatures: the amplitude–voltage loop displays a distinct “butterfly” shape, and the phase–voltage curve shows a well-defined hysteresis loop with an ∼180° phase-switching, which is a hallmark of reversible spontaneous polarization. Notably, the maximum amplitude value of the BGFZ9-incorporated composite film is substantially higher than that of the pure P­(VDF-HFP) film. Furthermore, the phase-switching angle reaches the ideal 180°, whereas the pure film shows incomplete polarization reversal, likely due to the presence of nonswitchable polarization regions or domain-pinning effects. The enhancement in ferroelectric performance is primarily attributed to the multifaceted roles of the BGFZ9 nanoparticles within the composite film. (1) As a strong ferroelectric material, BGFZ9 introduces an additional source of ferroelectric order into the composite system. (2) Acting as a heterogeneous nucleating agent, the nanoparticles effectively promote the formation of the piezoelectric β-phase within the polymer chains during the electrospinning process, a phenomenon well-documented for piezoelectric filler/PVDF composites. (3) The interfacial effects between the nanoparticles and the polymer matrix may generate local electric fields, which assist in the alignment of dipoles and reduce the pinning of domain walls by defects, thereby leading to clearer domain structures and more complete polarization switching.

Effect of External Mechanical Force on the Piezophotocatalytic Performance

To evaluate the catalytic performance of the P­(VDF-HFP)@BGFZ9 composite films, 50 mg of each film sample was immersed in 50 mL of a 5 mg/L OFL aqueous solution. Degradation experiments were conducted under two distinct conditions: ultrasonic excitation for piezocatalysis and simultaneous visible-light irradiation and ultrasonic treatment for piezophotocatalysis. The corresponding results are presented in Figure .

As shown in Figure a,b, the degradation kinetics of OFL by all samples under piezocatalytic conditions conformed to a pseudo-first-order reaction model. The degradation efficiency initially increased and then decreased with higher BGFZ9 nanoparticle loading. The film with 10% BGFZ9 loading exhibited the highest first-order reaction rate constant of 0.684 min–1·g–1, which was approximately 1.22 times that of the pure P­(VDF-HFP) film. This confirms that the incorporation of BGFZ9 nanoparticles is a key factor in enhancing the catalytic performance of the composite film. The performance enhancement mechanism can be attributed to a dual synergistic effect: (1) The Gd and Zr codoping introduces oxygen vacancies in the BGFZ9 lattice, which act as electron traps to suppress the recombination of e––h + pairs. Furthermore, the doping optimizes the ferroelectric domain structure and piezoelectric coefficient of the material, enabling the generation of a stronger piezoelectric potential under mechanical stress for more efficient charge separation and migration. (2) The interfacial coupling formed between the BGFZ9 nanoparticles and the P­(VDF-HFP) polymer matrix not only promotes the formation of the piezoelectric β-phase in P­(VDF-HFP) via heterogeneous nucleation but also establishes a strong local piezoelectric field and dielectric environment within the composite. This collectively enhances the conversion of mechanical energy into chemical energy, driving the generation of more ROS like •OH and thereby accelerating the catalytic degradation of OFL. The decline in catalytic performance beyond 10% BGFZ9 loading is attributed to nanoparticle aggregation within the polymer matrix at high loadings. This aggregation creates a stress-shielding effect, hindering efficient mechanical energy transfer, and may also reduce the overall flexibility and effective polarized area of the film. To clearly elucidate the synergistic contributions of piezoelectric and photocatalytic processes, a direct comparison between piezocatalysis and piezophotocatalysis was performed. Figure c,d reveals that the composite films demonstrated superior degradation efficiency under the coupled piezophotocatalytic condition compared to the sole piezocatalytic mode. For the film with 10% BGFZ9, the reaction rate constant increased to 1.043 min–1·g–1, achieving an OFL removal rate of 85.71% within 3 h. This value is 1.53 times the efficiency of pure piezocatalysis (0.684 min–1·g–1), indicating a robust synergistic effect rather than a simple additive effect. This observation aligns with recent studies on BiFeO3-based systems, where the synergy between piezoelectric polarization and photoexcitation often leads to a “1 + 1 > 2” enhancement by dynamically modulating band bending and facilitating charge separation. The underlying mechanism is attributed to the piezoelectric potential induced by mechanical stress, which effectively facilitates the separation of photogenerated e––h + pairs, significantly inhibiting charge recombination and thus enhancing the overall quantum efficiency and degradation rate. Similar synergistic enhancements, where piezophotocatalytic rate constants are several times higher than those of individual processes, have been widely reported in high-performance composite systems for antibiotic degradation. In summary, the P­(VDF-HFP)@BGFZ9 composite film, successfully fabricated via electrospinning, not only addresses the recovery challenge of nanocatalysts but also achieves efficient and stable degradation of OFL through the intrinsic synergy between piezoelectric and photocatalytic effects, as quantitatively demonstrated by the comparative kinetic analysis.

As illustrated in Figure , the film with 10% BGFZ9 loading maintained a high OFL removal rate, decreasing only slightly from 85.71 to 79.27% over five consecutive piezophotocatalytic cycles, corresponding to a performance retention of 94.4%. This indicates excellent cycling stability and structural durability. The slight decrease in activity is typically attributed to the temporary blocking of active sites by the adsorption and accumulation of intermediate products or pollutant molecules during the reaction, rather than irreversible chemical deactivation or structural collapse of the catalyst itself. Specifically, based on the degradation pathway of fluoroquinolone antibiotics, the adsorbed species likely include hydroxylated intermediates, piperazine ring-opened products, and small molecular organic acids, which may occupy surface active sites and OV. This observation is consistent with reports, such as that by Zeitoun and Yehia, on the slight activity decline of TiO2/PVDF electrospun nanofilms after multiple cycles due to the accumulation of surface adsorbates. Notably, similar performance retention (e.g., ∼90% after 5 cycles) has been reported for other piezoelectric polymer-based composites like MoS2@PVDF-HFP, highlighting the robust structural integrity of such flexible film systems under cyclic catalytic conditions. A comparison of the XRD patterns of the composite film before and after the cycling tests (Figure b) shows that the positions and intensities of the diffraction peaks remain essentially unchanged. This confirms the high structural stability of the composite film during prolonged catalytic operation, with no significant lattice distortion, phase transformation, or change in crystallite size occurring. In conclusion, the BGFZ9 nanoparticles and the P­(VDF-HFP) matrix form a robust composite structure during the electrospinning process, effectively preventing the leaching or detachment of nanoparticles from the fibrous network under vigorous hydrodynamic and mechanical agitation.

Analysis of the Catalytic Mechanism

To elucidate the reaction mechanism of the P­(VDF-HFP)@BGFZ9 composite film in piezophotocatalysis, radical scavenger experiments were conducted to investigate the role of ROS. Specifically, 1,4-benzoquinone (BQ), tert-butanol (TBA), and ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA) were employed as scavengers for •O2 –, •OH, and h + , respectively, during piezophotocatalytic degradation tests. Concurrently, EPR spectroscopy was utilized to analyze the characteristic signals of •O2 –, •OH, and OV. The experimental results are presented in Figure .

Piezophotocatalytic catalysis performance after captures added of 10% of P­(VDF-HFP)@BGFZ9 composite films. Degradation curve and first-order reaction rate constant (a), •O2 – (b), •OH (c), and OV (d) EPR spectra under ultrasonic conditions of 30 min.

As shown in Figure a, the addition of BQ and EDTA to the piezophotocatalytic reaction system containing the 10% BGFZ9-loaded composite film led to a significant decrease in the degradation rate constant. This confirms that •O2 – and h + are the key ROS for OFL degradation during the piezocatalytic process. In contrast, the inhibitory effect observed after adding TBA was relatively weaker, indicating that •OH is not the primary direct oxidation pathway in this catalytic system. This observation aligns with the typical piezocatalytic mechanism: the piezoelectric potential induced by mechanical stress primarily drives the separation of e––h + pairs. The e– reacts with adsorbed O2 on the composite film surface to generate •O2 –, while the h + may directly oxidize OFL molecules. The EPR test results shown in Figure b,c corroborate the generation of these radicals. After 30 min of ultrasonic treatment, characteristic signals for the DMPO-•O2 – and DMPO-•OH adducts were clearly detected in both the pure P­(VDF-HFP) film and the composite films with BGFZ9 nanoparticles. However, the intensity of the characteristic 1:1:1:1 quartet signal for the DMPO-•O2 – adduct was significantly stronger for the composite film with 10% BGFZ9 loading compared to the pure P­(VDF-HFP) film. This demonstrates that the piezoelectric effect of the BGFZ9 nanoparticles, under applied stress, induces a more efficient release of e–, which is the critical factor for enhanced •O2 – generation. The signal intensity of the characteristic quartet for the DMPO-•OH adduct was also slightly higher for the 10% composite film, confirming that the incorporation of BGFZ9 promotes •OH formation. Combined with the TBA scavenging results, this suggests that the •OH present in the system is likely generated indirectly via the disproportionation of •O2 – (2•O2 – + 2H+ → H2O2 + O2), rather than through the direct oxidation of H2O by h + . Furthermore, as shown in Figure d, the composite film containing BGFZ9 nanoparticles exhibited a significantly stronger characteristic signal for the OV adduct compared to the film without doping, with a g-value of 2.003, which is typical for paramagnetic resonance signals from single electrons trapped at OV sites. The formation of OV is primarily attributed to the Gd/Zr codoping strategy: the partial substitution of Bi3+ by smaller Gd3+ ions and Fe3+ by Zr4+ ions introduces lattice distortion and disrupts the original charge balance. To maintain electroneutrality, positively charged OVs are spontaneously formed in the lattice as charge-compensating defects. These OVs play a dual role: they act as charge traps to prolong the lifetime of photogenerated carriers and provide active sites for O2 adsorption and subsequent •O2 – generation. Comprehensive analysis indicates that the composite film with 10% BGFZ9 loading exhibits a significantly enhanced capacity for generating active species, which is highly consistent with its optimal OFL catalytic degradation performance. This “optimal loading” can be attributed to the following synergistic effects: at a 10% loading, BGFZ9 nanoparticles achieve a highly uniform dispersion within the P­(VDF-HFP) fibrous matrix, maximizing the effective interfacial contact area between the nanoparticles and the polymer. This optimized dispersion state not only promotes the formation of the piezoelectric β-phase but also ensures that the piezoelectric polarization fields generated by both the nanoparticles and the polymer under ultrasonic stress can be most effectively superimposed, driving more efficient charge separation. Simultaneously, the corresponding OV concentration at this loading is at an optimal level, effectively trapping e– without becoming recombination centers due to excessive defects. The Gd and Zr codoping optimizes the piezoelectric properties of BFO and introduces OV, thereby enhancing the charge carrier separation efficiency within the composite film. Meanwhile, the BGFZ9 nanoparticles, uniformly dispersed within the P­(VDF-HFP) matrix, synergize with the polymer. Under ultrasonic stress, they generate a stronger piezoelectric potential, driving more efficient O2 reduction and h + oxidation processes. This establishes a catalytic pathway of “piezoelectric charge separation → •O2 – generation and h + oxidation → pollutant oxidative degradation.”

The enhanced piezophotocatalytic activity of the P­(VDF-HFP)@BGFZ9 composite film primarily stems from three interconnected processes: efficient charge separation induced by external fields, optimized interfacial charge transfer, and the synergistic oxidation involving multiple ROS. (1) Enhanced built-in electric field and interfacial coupling: the piezoelectric active components within the composite film are excited by external fields to generate polarized charges. Ultrasonic stress causes deformation of the P­(VDF-HFP) matrix, leading to the rearrangement of its internal dipole moments and the generation of a piezoelectric polarization field. Concurrently, the same ultrasonic stress induces lattice distortion in the uniformly dispersed, ferroelectric and piezoelectric BGFZ9 nanoparticles embedded within the composite film, triggering their own piezoelectric polarization. Critically, the electrospinning process and subsequent corona poling align the molecular dipoles of P­(VDF-HFP) and the ferroelectric domains of BGFZ9 nanoparticles. More importantly, the surface properties of BGFZ9, particularly the OV introduced by Gd/Zr codoping and the associated localized dipoles, create a strong interfacial coupling with the aligned dipoles of the P­(VDF-HFP) β-phase. This coupling is not merely physical mixing; it establishes a directional and intensified macroscopic built-in electric field throughout the composite, as analyzed in similar heterojunction interface engineering studies. This built-in electric field provides a powerful driving force for the rapid separation and directional migration of charge carriers generated by all external field stimuli, enabling them to overcome Coulombic attraction. (2) Regulation of charge migration path and band bending: the optimized charge separation and migration pathways significantly suppress the recombination of e––h + pairs, thereby promoting interfacial redox reactions. The built-in electric field, reinforced by the interfacial coupling, induces significant band bending at the BGFZ9/P­(VDF-HFP) interface. This bending creates a favorable energy gradient that directs photogenerated electrons in BGFZ9 toward the interface and potentially into the polymer matrix, while holes are driven in the opposite direction, effectively minimizing bulk recombination. The separated e– accumulates on the surface of the composite film and is efficiently captured by the OV introduced by BGFZ9. These OVs act not only as electron traps but also, due to their gradient distribution possibly induced by the composite interface and poling, contribute to an additional OV gradient field that synergizes with Ebuilt-in to further facilitate charge separation. The OV serves as active adsorption and activation sites for O2 molecules, lowering the energy barrier for its reduction and thereby facilitating the efficient generation of •O2 –. This synergistic effect between OV and the built-in electric field creates a highly reactive interface. (3) Synergistic piezophotocatalytic pathway and performance under combined conditions: the piezophotocatalytic synergy in the P­(VDF-HFP)@BGFZ9 composite film constitutes a complex system involving multifield coupling and multiprocess cascading. The core of its catalytic degradation lies in the material design that constructs a powerful, directional built-in electric field. This field simultaneously optimizes the separation and migration processes of charge carriers generated by both the piezoelectric effect and photon energy, achieving a synergistic effect where the combined outcome is greater than the sum of its parts. Critically, the OV within the BGFZ9 nanoparticles is integral to this synergy, enhancing light absorption, improving charge dynamics, and increasing surface reactivity. Ultimately, the high concentrations of e– and h + at the composite film surface drive the reduction of O2 to form •O2 – and the direct oxidation of OFL by h +, respectively, establishing a complete catalytic pathway: “External field excitation → e––h+ pair separation → interfacial ROS generation → OFL catalytic degradation.” In this pathway, ROS, primarily •O2 – and h+, with •OH playing a secondary role, attack the piperazine ring of OFL, initiating decarboxylation, demethylation, and hydroxylation reactions, leading to the efficient decomposition and mineralization of OFL. These reactions ultimately lead to the cleavage of the piperazine and quinolone rings of the OFL molecule, generating small molecular acids, CO2, H2O, etc., achieving efficient decomposition and mineralization.

Conclusions and Outlook

In summary, a flexible P­(VDF-HFP)@BGFZ9 composite film with outstanding piezocatalytic activity has been successfully fabricated via electrospinning. The Gd/Zr codoping effectively modulates the band structure and ferroelectric properties of BiFeO3. The interfacial synergy between the BGFZ9 nanoparticles and the P­(VDF-HFP) matrix not only induces the formation of the piezoelectric β-phase in the polymer but also establishes a local built-in electric field, collectively facilitating the efficient conversion of mechanical energy into chemical energy. The composite film demonstrates high efficiency in degrading ofloxacin under ultrasonic vibration, with a significant synergistic enhancement observed between the piezoelectric and photocatalytic processes. Mechanistic investigations reveal that superoxide radicals and holes are the key reactive species governing the degradation. Moreover, the film exhibits excellent cycling stability and structural integrity, addressing the practical challenges of catalyst recovery and agglomeration faced by powder catalysts. This work validates the strategy of integrating multiferroic nanoparticles with flexible piezoelectric polymers for constructing high-performance, practical environmental remediation materials, offering a new material platform for the application of advanced oxidation technologies in water treatment. Looking forward, the potential of this flexible composite film extends beyond the current piezophotocatalytic system under static or ultrasonicated conditions. Future research could explore its application in dynamic, flow-through water treatment systems. As suggested by recent studies, hydrodynamic forces in flowing water can induce piezoelectric polarization in suitable materials, offering a sustainable energy source for catalysis without external ultrasonic generators. The flexible and robust nature of the electrospun P­(VDF-HFP)@BGFZ9 film makes it an ideal candidate for integration into such flow reactors, where water pressure and turbulence could directly drive the piezoelectric response, enabling continuous and energy-efficient pollutant degradation. Furthermore, inspired by the forward-looking concept of “multi-physics field synergy engineering,” the material design principle demonstrated here can be extended to more complex multifield coupling scenarios. These advanced oxidation processes based on multifield synergy represent a promising frontier for next-generation water purification technologies.
