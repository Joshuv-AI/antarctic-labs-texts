A Visual Dataset for Anomaly Detection in Self-Driving Laboratories
Authors: Shiwei Lin, Zesen Chen, Xiaobin Jia, Yi Wang, Chenxu Wang, Shuyuan Zhang, Xiaozhen Ding, Boyuan Du, Weili Ding, Huaping Liu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Self-driving laboratories accelerate the application of the scientific method and the discovery process through high-throughput experimentation, intelligent perception and planning, and effective human-robot collaboration. However, detecting anomalies in events, object states, and environmental conditions remains challenging due to process uncertainty and environmental complexity. To support research in this area, we construct a dataset for process anomaly detection in scientific experiments. The dataset is collected from a fully automated Polydimethylsiloxane synthesis workflow involving collaborative robots. Images were captured from first-person views using end-effector cameras mounted on mobile and fixed robotic arms, covering 11 checkpoints and 14 distinct viewpoints. In total, the dataset includes 1,671 images and 2,788 image-text pairs. Each sample contains step-specific descriptions, anomaly labels, and region-level annotations. This dataset intended to support a range of visual anomaly detection tasks, including image-text classification, anomaly type recognition, anomaly localization, grounded captioning, and anomaly attribution analysis. It offers a practical resource for developing intelligent anomaly monitoring and automated decision-making systems in self-driving laboratory environments driven by visual perception.

Background & Summary

Self-Driving Laboratories and Operational Safety

Self-driving laboratories (SDLs) are intelligent systems that integrate experimental automation with data-driven decision-making, aiming to accelerate the application of the scientific method and the discovery process1,2. SDLs significantly reduce the researcher’s involvement in repetitive or hazardous operations and can tackle complex tasks3. Recent progress in chemical synthesis4,5 and material discovery6,7 has demonstrated the powerful potential of SDLs. With the advancement of robotics8,9, large language models10, multi-robot systems11, and multi-agent coordination technologies12, SDLs are entering a period of rapid evolution toward more autonomous, intelligent, and generalizable AI-driven paradigms13.

Despite these advances, the inherent complexity, diversity, and uncertainty of scientific experiments impose substantial challenges on robotic perception and planning. In particular, anomaly detection during experimental procedures is essential for maintaining laboratory safety and operational stability1,14. In self-driving scientific laboratories, robots can handle repetitive and hazardous operations, supporting researchers and reducing their workload. However, execution failures are often inevitable due to the complexity and variability of laboratory environments. When human-robot collaboration is involved, these workflows become even more dynamic and unpredictable, introducing additional uncertainties into the execution process15,16. If a robot executes a task when the target object is not at the expected location, or its state does not meet the operational requirement, subsequent operations may fail or be invalid, as illustrated in Fig. 1(a). Furthermore, faults during execution, such as spillage or object drop, can lead to operational failures or pose environmental safety risks. In SDLs, safety is a fundamental concern, and it is essential to bring safety to the forefront to ensure that the necessary safeguards are in place to mitigate against potential anomalies17,18. While control-level safety can be addressed through tighter planning constraints19, unexpected events and faults during the experimental process must be efficiently detected. Visual information can help track experimental progress, support decision-making, and detect anomalies, thereby improving laboratory safety practices. To our knowledge, few datasets are specifically tailored for process anomaly detection in self-driving scientific laboratories. Existing research has focused mainly on specific scenarios, such as industrial defect detection20,21, abnormal behavior recognition22–24, computer vision-based material recognition25, and fixed-view experimental workflow monitoring14. The datasets involve in these studies, such as C-VAD, MVTec AD 2, COCO caption, VisA and PCB Bank are not originally designed to support process anomaly detection tasks and may lack the semantic context or scenario diversity required for such applications.

Anomaly Information Acquisition and Detection

Building a comprehensive, continuous, and real-time intelligent monitoring systems for self-driving scientific laboratories often demands substantial financial and human resources. In contrast, basic global monitoring setups often face challenges such as limited resolution, occlusion, and interference from multiple targets, which make it difficult to effectively capture anomalies during experimental procedures. These limitations also hinder the development of robust models and affect their generalization capabilities. Therefore, instead of relying on global surveillance, we explore alternative approaches for process anomaly observation. In this context, end-effector cameras mounted on robotic arms provide a practical and cost-effective solution to capture visual information related to anomalies. This approach enables budget-constrained laboratories to implement efficient anomaly detection with minimal overhead, while integrating seamlessly into existing workflows without disrupting routine operations, as illustrated in Fig. 1(b). This setup requires only the addition of a vision sensor to the robot end-effector, which is commonly found in many scientific laboratories. This configuration reduces the deployment cost and can be readily extended to diverse experimental scenarios.

While first-person perspectives captured by robotic systems provide rich visual information, image data alone may be insufficient for reliable anomaly detection. Effective detection requires appropriate context support. Recent advances demonstrate that constructing multi-scale semantic cues, which align region features at different scales, improves both detection and localization accuracy26–28. The creation of pseudo-spoof negative samples by combining semantic prompts with spatial masks directs models to focus on abnormal regions, enabling them to learn anomaly concepts from normal-only data and generalize across diverse scenes29. Studies based on vision-language models show that clear and precise textual descriptions effectively mitigate hallucination while strengthening visual grounding and causal reasoning30–33. Motivated by these developments, we construct a multimodal dataset that pairs first-person visual observations with contextual information describing experimental stages, to support the study of process anomalies in self-driving scientific laboratories. Specifically, in scientific laboratory environments, the same image may be associated with multiple free-form text descriptions, which potentially lead to different detection outcomes. Although previous studies have highlighted the potential of vision-based anomaly detection in scientific workflows32, their datasets were limited in viewpoint diversity and scene coverage, which may not reflect the full complexity of real-world experimental environments.

Methods

Experimental Workflow

We implemented a fully automated PDMS synthesis workflow and validated its feasibility through robotic execution trials. Figure 3(a) illustrates the basic sequence of robotic operations, including the equipment involved and operational checkpoints at each stage. Although the diagram depicts the main pathway of the process, some optional branches were not included, such as allowing silicone to air-dry instead of using the vacuum drying oven, or using a syringe for direct dosage rather than weighing on the electronic balance. These variants were excluded as they did not affect the data collection strategy. To enable image acquisition and eye-in-hand calibration, we installed end-effector cameras on both the mobile Wooshrobot and the fixed Panda robot. Devices such as the capping device, electronic balance, vortex mixer, and vacuum drying oven can receive control signals from the robot via communication protocols, allowing for automated operation. As shown in Fig. 3(b), we present the framework for data collection and processing. Drawing on our experience with automated experimentation, we decomposed the overall workflow into meta-steps and identified critical checkpoints. We then finalized the definitions of anomalies and their corresponding scenarios through extensive discussion. Guided by these predefined scenarios and steps, images were collected in the real-world environment using a camera mounted on the robotic arm. Finally, we annotated the image-text pairs using a hybrid approach that combines manual annotation with automated assistance.

Checkpoints Selection and Meta-step Decomposition

While anomalies could potentially arise at any position in repetitive robotic tasks, monitoring and analyzing every individual action would make the workflow excessively complex and inefficient. We observed that motion planners often rely on specific transfer or intermediate positions during task execution. These positions serve as critical junctions for detecting anomalies, as they represent necessary transitions in the flow of materials, indicated as “position” in Fig. 3(a). Consequently, the workflow can be segmented into a limited number of checkpoints, each corresponding to critical positions that are essential for task transitions and anomaly monitoring. While anomalies at each checkpoint may result from either robotic or human actions, monitoring these limited yet critical points enables effective detection and risk assessment across the entire experimental workflow without the need to distinguish the executor.

In total, we identified 11 checkpoints throughout the PDMS synthesis process. These are mainly located near essential devices such as the capping device and the vortex mixer. Based on these 11 checkpoints and the optional workflow branches, we decomposed the preparation process into 27 meta-steps. Each meta-step consists of one or more atomic robotic actions, depending on the complexity of the task segment.

Anomaly Categorization

We systematically analyzed the types of anomalies that could occur at each checkpoint. These anomalies are categorized based on whether they occur before or after the execution of a subtask. Anomalies before execution include Missing and Inoperable object. Anomalies after execution include Transfer failure and Unfulfilled object. Additionally, we define Environmental disturbance, which refers to anomalies in external environmental factors that interfere with the progress of the experiment. Image Acquisition Strategy

We used the end-effector cameras of both the mobile and fixed robots to capture images from a first-person perspective. In real-world laboratory environments, the viewing angle for image acquisition can vary depending on laboratory scale, workspace layout, or task stages. To enhance the practical applicability of the dataset and accommodate the variability in robotic navigation paths, we adopted a multi-view image acquisition strategy. Specifically, we defined 14 distinct viewpoints for capturing the target at each checkpoint, as illustrated in Fig. 4. Not all of these 14 predefined viewpoints are physically reachable at every checkpoint. Considering that a scientific laboratory may contain either or both mobile and fixed robots, we designed the acquisition coverage accordingly. This setup provided diverse coverage from both robot perspectives.

Text Annotation and Box Labeling

We implemented a standardized automatic labeling process that links each image to its corresponding semantic description based on predefined meta-step information, in order to promote consistency and reduce bias. Based on 27 well-defined meta-steps and the overall experimental setup, GPT-4o38 was first used to generate possible anomaly scenarios that may occur before and after each meta-step. These preliminary outputs were then reviewed and refined by seven domain experts through collective discussion grounded in actual laboratory experience. All identified cases were categorized into five predefined anomaly types, as summarized in Table 1. The resulting structured metadata record includes detailed semantic fields such as stage description, operator, start and destination location, detection content, anomaly label description, and anomaly label. This structured metadata record served as both a guideline during image acquisition and a critical reference for automatic annotation stage.

During the image acquisition process, two experimenters followed the predefined sequence of meta-steps to set up both normal and abnormal scenarios systematically. Images were captured from a first-person view using cameras mounted on the end-effector of the robotic arms. After image capture was completed, an automatic data processing and annotation program was executed to associate each image with its corresponding semantic description based on the structured metadata record defined prior to the experiment.

To further enhance the structural completeness of the dataset, we manually annotated the region of interest (ROI) in each image using rectangular bounding boxes. These boxes covered both normal and abnormal areas, enabling the development of anomaly detection models with spatial localization capability. The annotation task for the bounding box was carried out by four annotators using the LabelImg tool, following a unified labeling standard, to ensure spatial consistency and semantic alignment. Specifically, we conducted three rounds of annotation. In the first round, three annotators independently labeled 100 randomly selected images, and their annotations were cross-validated using the Intersection over Union (IoU) metric to establish a consistent labeling standard. In the second round, the same three annotators collaboratively labeled the full dataset. In the third round, four annotators jointly performed refinement and correction to ensure final quality.

As an additional step, we also labeled key operational objects such as test tubes, silicone containers, and molds, which serve as auxiliary information for downstream visual tasks. Finally, we used the anomaly label description field to generate captions describing the region of interest (ROI) for each image-text pair.

To further ensure data integrity, two researchers conducted cross-validation after automatic alignment of image-text pairs. This process verified the correctness of image-text mapping, label consistency, and completeness. For the small number of mismatches or ambiguous annotations identified during validation, we conducted image re-capture or data correction to ensure accurate alignment between each image and its corresponding textual descriptions.

Context-Aware Visual Anomaly Detection

The anomaly annotations in this dataset are accompanied by contextual textual descriptions, providing strong support for context-aware visual anomaly detection. In real-world experimental settings, it is often difficult to determine whether an anomaly is present based on visual input solely. Many visual comprehension are semantically dependent32,46, requiring a comprehensive understanding of the experimental information, subtask and anomaly description. To support context-aware anomaly detection, each image is paired with structured contextual information, enabling more accurate and semantically informed anomaly detection research. Each image sample is accompanied by a set of structured textual fields. The stage description and the anomaly label description are expressed in natural language, enabling the dataset to be applied to large multimodal models such as GPT-4V47, GPT-4o38, MiniGPT-430 and Qwen-VL48 for visual anomaly detection. Our previous work has demonstrated the feasibility of this approach32. To demonstrate the practical utility of the dataset, we implemented a context-aware visual anomaly detection method as a simple illustrative baseline. In this setting, each image and its corresponding textual information are provided as input to a vision-language model, which is then prompted to determine whether an anomaly is present in the image and to generate a corresponding reasoning process. Additionally, we employed a CLIP-based approach to perform anomaly detection on the dataset under a zero-shot setting27.

As shown in Fig. 9, the overall success rates for anomaly detection are higher under top-down and horizontal viewpoints compared to other perspectives. In contrast, the success rate of anomaly detection decreases noticeably under 90-degree lateral viewpoints, while the false positive and false negative rates increase. This is primarily due to the heavy occlusion in these lateral views, a result that is in line with the expected challenges in real-world laboratory environments. Notably, in Fig. 9(a), the fixed arm’s right-up viewpoint contains only four samples, making the results less representative. Bounding Box Annotation and Applications

To support anomaly localization tasks such as visual grounding49 and grounded captioninging50, we provide bounding box annotations for all images in the dataset. With these annotations, the dataset can be used not only to determine whether an anomaly is present, but also to localize where the anomaly is and to describe what the anomaly is.

Each image in the dataset is accompanied by a rectangular bounding box that explicitly marks the spatial region of the anomaly. Each bounding box was manually drawn to semantically align with the corresponding natural language anomaly description, covering the region of interest (ROI) in the image rather than the entire object. The annotations cover five categories of anomalies, and for normal cases, the bounding box is simply labeled “Normal”.

In the first annotation round, three annotators independently labeled 100 randomly selected images. Cross-validation of their bounding boxes showed an average Intersection over Union (IoU) exceeding 0.85, indicating high consistency and agreement in annotation standards. In the third round, a fourth annotator joined the previous three to collaboratively review all annotations. During this process, common issues such as boundary offsets, size inconsistencies, incorrect anomaly labels, and missing boxes were identified and corrected. Samples with substantial deviations were re-annotated.

As shown in Fig. 10, the visualizations display the bounding box annotations and corresponding captions. The bounding boxes exhibit clear boundaries and alignment with the corresponding text descriptions, offering support for downstream tasks such as grounded captioning, spatial understanding, and multimodal anomaly reasoning. In addition, we annotated the primary object to provide spatial cues, which may serve as a supplementary reference for localization tasks and also serve as supervision for object detection tasks51 and visual question answering (VQA)52 tasks.Fig. 10Example of bounding box annotations and caption.

Usage Notes

The dataset is organized in a standardized format. The annotations.json file contains the mapping between images and their corresponding textual descriptions, and the dataset can be accessed using all major programming languages such as Python, MATLAB, and R. Both the data and annotations are publicly available online.

This dataset can be used for a variety of computer vision and multimodal research tasks. With paired images and structured textual metadata, it allows for context-aware visual anomaly sanalysis. Based on information such as experimental stage, detection objective, and anomaly category, tasks may include anomaly classification, anomaly detection, anomaly type identification, and anomaly description generation. The provided bounding box annotations further enable spatial reasoning tasks, such as anomaly localization, visual grounding, and grounded captioning.

The dataset includes experimental planning information, subtask descriptions, and operator roles, which provide contextual data useful for studies on anomaly attribution and response strategy modeling. Both tasks are especially important in the context of human-robot collaboration safety, enabling intelligent systems not only to detect anomalies, but also to understand their causes and formulate appropriate responses. The meta-step structure of the dataset allows for the composition of various experimental workflows. Each meta-step can be associated with different executors (e.g., human or robot), making the dataset applicable to studies involving anomaly attribution across task executions. When used with vision-language models, such configurations may support research into explainable anomaly reasoning in experimental workflows.

The extreme viewpoint samples within the dataset can be utilized as an out-of-distribution (OOD) test set for cross-viewpoint generalization, enabling systematic evaluation of model robustness under conditions of rare viewpoints, occlusion, or scale variations. These samples can also be incorporated as rare instances into training sets for algorithmic data augmentation studies, mixed with conventional viewpoint samples to mitigate viewpoint bias.
