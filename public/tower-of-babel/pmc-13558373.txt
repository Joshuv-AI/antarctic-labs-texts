PKPD-Based Translational Modeling of Calaspargase Pegol Preclinical Activity in Hepatocellular Carcinoma
Authors: Eef Hoeben, Vincent Madelain, Chayan Acharya, Pierre Barbier Saint Hilaire, Ethika Tyagi, Malek Okour, Sylvain Fouliard
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground and ObjectiveCalaspargase pegol (CalPEG) is a PEGylated conjugate of L-Asparaginase (L-Asp) and an asparagine specific enzyme approved by the Food and Drug Administration (FDA) as a component of a multi-agent chemotherapeutic regimen for the treatment of acute lymphoblastic leukemia (ALL) in children and young adults. Although CalPEG is an established and effective treatment for childhood ALL, its potential in solid tumors is still underexplored. Recent reports illustrated that L-Asp may also have potential for the treatment of certain aggressive solid tumors, including hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC)[1–5]. In order to investigate this potential, clinical and preclinical data were integrated using model-informed drug development (MIDD) approaches, which are among the most adequate tools to facilitate the translation of preclinical into clinical data [6–9]. This work explores the potential of pharmacokinetic/pharmacodynamic tumor growth inhibition (PKPD TGI) modeling to maximize the use of in vivo preclinical data and PK data in patients with ALL for translation of preclinical observed antitumor activity to clinical efficacy.MethodsA PKPD TGI model was developed that described tumor volumes (TVs) versus time in a preclinical hypermethylated xenograft mouse model relevant for HCC. Translation of the PKPD TGI model to human was performed by linking the structural TGI model developed in mice with human PK model of CalPEG. This model was developed on PK data, i.e., plasma Asp activity (PAA), obtained in pediatric and adult patients with ALL and was used to predict TGI profiles of CalPEG in adult patients with HCC.ResultsThe developed PKPD TGI model adequately described the TV versus time profiles in tumor bearing (TB) mice and quantified the PKPD relationship between PAA and TGI in TB mice. The structural mouse TGI model was linked to predicted human PK parameters and this human PKPD TGI model was used to perform simulations and to predict antitumor activity of CalPEG at different doses/schedule in patients with HCC.ConclusionsThe results indicate that PKPD TGI modeling can be used to build a preclinical to clinical translational framework to predict the exposure–response relationship in patients with HCC and to inform dosing strategies or trial designs for solid tumors.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1007/s13318-026-01010-4.

Key Points

CalPEG is a well-established ALL treatment with unexplored potential in solid tumors.A translational PK/PD modeling framework is described, aiming at leveraging preclinical information to predict the potential activity of CalPEG in patients with solid tumors.The findings can help inform dosing strategies or trial designs for solid tumors.

Introduction

L-Asparaginase (L-ASNase) is an enzyme that hydrolyses the nonessential amino acid asparagine (Asn) into aspartic acid and ammonia and is currently a cornerstone drug in the treatment of childhood and adult acute lymphoblastic leukemia (ALL). ALL lymphoblasts, in contrast to healthy cells, largely lack the enzyme asparagine synthetase (ASNS) expression, and thus depend on extracellular sources of Asn for their survival. Depletion of circulating Asn by L-ASNase results in starvation and selective apoptosis of the leukemic blasts. More recently, accumulating evidence suggests that L-ASNase could also have clinical potential for the treatment of other subtypes of leukemia or certain aggressive solid tumors, including hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) [10].

However, one of the main reasons preventing the expansion of today’s FDA-approved L-ASNases to solid cancers is their high toxicity, especially in adults, which includes severe immunological side effects (hypersensitivity) as well as nonimmune related toxicities, such as pancreatitis, liver toxicities, coagulopathy, and neurotoxicity [11, 12].

The design of optimized and novel L-ASNases molecules/formulations provided opportunities to address both the short half-life and the unwanted toxicities of L-ASNases. Calaspargase pegol (S095015, SC-PEG Escherichia. coli L-asparaginase, CalPEG) is a stabilized formulation of L-ASNase and results from the covalent conjugation of monomethoxypolyethylene glycol (PEG) to lysine residues on the enzyme via a succinimidyl carbonate linker [13, 14]. The addition of the PEG-tag delays elimination from the body and increases the half-life of PEG-L-ASNase in humans towards 16.1 days. CalPEG received USA marketing authorization in 2018 as a component of a multi-agent chemotherapeutic regimen for the treatment of ALL in children and young adults up to 21 years of age.

Although CalPEG is an established and effective treatment for childhood ALL, its potential in solid tumors is still underexplored. Therefore, preclinical in vitro and in vivo experiments were performed to evaluate whether CalPEG could also have clinical potential for the treatment of solid tumors, including HCC [15, 16]. It has been shown that ASNS promoter-hypermethylated HCC cell lines with the lowest ASNS mRNA expression are the most responsive to CalPEG treatment in vitro [17]. An in vivo efficacy study in female BALB/c nude mice bearing human ASNS promoter-hypermethylated cell line derived xenograft (CDx) model relevant for HCC (JHH5) showed that CalPEG treatment inhibited tumor growth (TG) in a dose-dependent manner and resulted in tumor rejections at the highest doses.

Cancer remains an unmet medical need, not only to develop new drugs, but also to improve the speed and efficiency of the development of oncology drugs. PKPD modelling has become a key tool in early understanding and quantification of the exposure–response relationship and associated variability in order to inform dosing strategies and designs for clinical trials. Therefore, an integrated quantitative translational PKPD modeling approach was used to facilitate the translation of preclinically observed antitumor activity into clinical efficacy. Active CalPEG exposure in patients with HCC was predicted on the basis of exposure–response data generated in this JHH5 xenograft mouse model and available PK information in pediatric and adult patients with ALL integrated into a population PK (popPK) model [18]. popPK models have been developed for PEG-conjugated asparaginase in children with ALL [19, 20] and are used to predict PAA levels in childhood patients with ALL in case those data are lacking due to clinical practice (missing values, monitoring gaps) or noncompliance and to adapt treatment schedules. In this PKPD analysis, owing to the lack of published popPK data in solid tumors, PAA in HCC patients was predicted using a popPK model, developed on PAA derived from pediatric and adult patients with ALL, under the assumption of similar PAA in patients with ALL and HCC.

Although preclinical xenograft models represent a simplification of a human malignancy and their predictive value have been challenged, this translational approach was found to be predictive for clinical efficacy for many small molecule oncology compounds [21–23] and was therefore considered to be useful to understand the degree of antitumor activity associated with CalPEG and explore its potential clinical activity in the HCC indication. The PKPD TGI model flow, as presented in the paper, can be applied to optimize early clinical trial designs in HCC, including dosage optimization, for a more efficient selection of doses for later phases and for potential combination strategies.

Methods

Data Collection

The in vivo PK and therapeutic efficacy of CalPEG was evaluated in the treatment of a subcutaneous (s.c.) JHH-5 HCC xenograft model in 40 female BALB/c mice. Each mouse was inoculated s.c. at the right flank region with JHH-5 tumor cells (1 × 107) in 0.2 mL of phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) mixed with Matrigel (1:1) for tumor development. On the day of treatment, the mean tumor size was 175 mm3. Vehicle and CalPEG were administered i.v. every 4 days (Q4D) for 28 days at 0 IU/kg (vehicle) 20, 40, and 100 IU/kg (CalPEG). Plasma samples for PK, i.e., plasma asparaginase activity (PAA), were collected 5 min before the 3rd administration on D8 (N = 10 mice/group) and 5 min before the 5th administration on D16 (N = 10 mice/group). At termination, plasma PK samples were collected before the 8th administration on D28 for the first N = 5 mice/treated group and 2 h after the 8th administration on D28 for the remaining N = 5 mice/treated group (Supplementary Table S1). The mice from the vehicle group were prematurely killed (before day 28) as their tumor volume exceeded the ethical limit of 2000 mm3. Asparaginase activity in mouse plasma was measured indirectly using a coupled enzymatic assay in which asparaginase activity was the rate-limiting step [17].

TVs were measured twice per week before randomization (D0) and were measured every 2 days after randomization (D0) up to the last dosing day on D28. TVs were measured in two dimensions using a caliper and the volume was expressed in mm3 using the formula V = (L × W × W)/2, where V is tumor volume, L is tumor length (the longest tumor dimension) and W is tumor width (the longest tumor dimension perpendicular to L). Nonmeasurable TVs were reported as < 1mm3.

A total of 40 mice (N = 10/dose group, including vehicle group) were finally included in the population PKPD TGI analysis for the JHH5 model. The final dataset included 527 TV observations and 87 PAA measurements for PK.

PK/PD Model Development

A stepwise PKPD TGI modeling approach was considered to translate preclinically observed antitumor activity into clinical efficacy. Step 1 consisted of the description of the plasma asparaginase activity (PAA, expressed in IU/L) versus time profiles in female nongrafted and grafted BALB/c nude mice using a PK model. Step 2 consisted of the development of a PKPD TGI model to describe the TV versus time profiles in mice xenografted with JHH5 cells. Step 3 consisted of the prediction of human PK of PAA using an existing population PK model of CalPEG developed in pediatric and adult patients with ALL. Step 4 consisted of the development of a human PKPD TGI model, linking the structural mouse TGI and human population PK model, and use the integrated model for prediction of clinical antitumor activity in patients with HCC.

PK Model and Prediction of PAA in Nongrafted and Grafted Female BALB/c Nude Mice

A PK model describing the kinetics of PAA after i.v. administration of CalPEG in female nongrafted and grafted BALB/c nude mice had been developed earlier on the basis of a rich dataset (344 PK samples in 90 mice administered with doses ranging from 6 to 300 IU/kg) and was considered for the description of the PAA–time profiles in these experiments (Table 1, Supplementary Table S2, Supplementary Figs. S1 and S2). Briefly, the final PK model was a one-compartment model with nonlinear Michaelis–Menten elimination. To account for a time-dependency after multiple administration, an indirect response model was used. In this model, it was assumed that the elimination of asparaginase is through proteolysis and that the elimination rate of CalPEG was dependent on a (relative) amount of enzyme (Ae) in the system (Ae = 1: elimination at baseline, Ae = 0: no elimination). CalPEG was assumed to decrease Rin of the enzyme in a concentration-dependent manner, i.e., in the presence of CalPEG, Ae will decrease and as a consequence, the elimination rate of CalPEG will decrease as well. With this parameterization, Ae cannot go above 1 and only a decrease of CalPEG elimination rate was observed. Random effects were identified in the model parameters for V, Vmax, and Km, describing the inter-individual variability (IIV) of these parameters. Residual variability was modeled using a proportional error model. This PK model was initially assessed for its ability to describe PAA in the current experiment through an external visual predictive check (VPC). The design of the PK part of the study was simulated using this model, then median and 90% prediction interval (PI) were superimposed with measured data. As the VPCs showed that the existing PK model was able to correctly predict the new PAA observations in the xenograft efficacy JHH5 model, no further model development was deemed needed; the estimated population PK parameters, including IIV, was fixed in the PKPD TGI model for a sequential PKPD approach [24].

Model selection was based on different criteria. PK Model in Pediatric and Adult Patients with ALL

A population PK model of CalPEG (legacy model) was initially developed in pediatric and young patients with ALL (age range 1–26 years) after administration of calaspargase pegol [18]. A description of the studies included for population PK model development is provided in Supplementary Tables S3A, S3B, and S3C. The structural model is a two-compartmental disposition model with saturable transport to the peripheral compartment and combined linear and nonlinear elimination from the central compartment. A model representation is given in Sect Human Efficacious Dose Prediction. The model demonstrated a close to directly proportional relationship between body surface area (BSA) and the linear clearance (CL), the maximum capacity of the nonlinear elimination (Vmax) and the volume of the central compartment (Vc). In addition, a statistically significant difference in Vc between male and female subjects was identified. IIV terms were supported on Vc, CL, and k21 (rate constant from the peripheral to the central compartment). The residual variability for CalPEG was described by a combined error model on log-transformed data. The legacy model was later updated with additional data from adult patients with ALL (age range 24–73 years). The final model (age range 1–73 years) is a two-compartment model characterized by a saturable distribution and a combined saturable and linear elimination, similar to the legacy model. Previously identified covariate–parameter relationships were estimated with similar effect sizes. An additional covariate–parameter relationship of Vmax decreasing with increasing age was identified. The residual variability in the final PK model was simplified to a proportional error model (additive on the log scale). A summary of the PK model parameters is given in Table 1. The VPCs for external qualification of the PK model are given in Supplementary Figs. S3A–S3C.

The analysis dataset used to build the final model included data after administration of CalPEG from doses of 1000 IU/m2 up to 2500 IU/m2 and contained, in total, 144 subjects with observations, i.e., 1680 observations and 11.7 observations per subject.

Human Efficacious Dose Prediction

A “human” PKPD TGI model was developed by linking the structural mouse TGI model with the human population PK model. The assumption was made that the PKPD relationship remained similar in mice and patients, and that the PK (PAA) in adult patients with HCC would be similar to the PK in adult patients with ALL. This model allowed us to perform simulations of TV versus time profiles and to project active doses in adult patients with HCC. The population PK model was used to perform simulations (N = 1000) in adult patients with HCC after a 2-h infusion administered every 3 weeks (Q3W) at doses of 500, 750, 1000, 1250, 1500, 2000, and 2500 IU/m2 and to predict exposure (Ctrough, Cinf, and Cavg) after the 1st, 3rd, and 5th administration. The typical population values of the model parameters with their corresponding IIV (CL, Vc and k21) were used for the simulations. Covariate values were set on the basis of typical values in patients with HCC. BSA was set to the values reported by the National Institute of Health for the corresponding age group(s) (i.e., 2.05 m2 (males) and 1.81 m2 (females) for age range of 50–69 years); simulations were performed separately for males and females and as the majority of HCC patients are > 55 years of age, simulations were performed for a 55-year-old patient.

A surrogate of clinical response in these simulations was defined as a 90% reduction in TV versus baseline, i.e., complete tumor regression [26].

Data Analysis and Software

Nonlinear mixed-effects modeling (NONMEM®) [27, 28] was performed using NONMEM version 7.4.4 software (ICON Development Solutions, Ellicott City, Maryland, USA) [29]. The first-order conditional estimation with interaction option (FOCE-I) algorithm was used to estimate population model parameters in NONMEM and the $COVARIANCE routine was used to provide precision of parameter estimates. Dataset exploration and visualization, as well as graphical analysis were performed using R statistical software for Windows version 4.3.1 (R Development Core Team, Vienna, Austria).

The imputation method was used for TV measurements that were reported as < 1 mm3 and were equated to the value of the limit of quantification (LoQ)/measurability set to 1 mm3.

Inter-individual variability (IIV) was tested on model parameters by a backward approach. In this backward step, all IIV included in the model were removed separately and kept in the model if this removal was significant (p = 0.01, chi-squared distribution, one degree of freedom (df)). For residual unexplained variability, different error models were tested, including a proportional error model and a combined additive and proportional error model.

The ability of the model to describe and/or predict data were assessed by standard GOF plots, normalized prediction distribution error (NPDE) [30], and visual predictive check (VPC).

Results

PK Analysis in Nongrafted and Grafted Female BALB/c Nude Mice

The PK model built on extensive PK data of PAA in female BALB/c nude mice (Supplementary Table S2) was used to predict the PAA observations in the JHH5 mouse model. The population PK parameters, including IIV, estimated by this PK model were fixed in the PKPD TGI model (Table 1). An external VPC showed that this PK model was able to correctly predict the new PAA observations in the JHH5 model as the observed PAA observations were adequately predicted and were equally distributed around the predicted median without over or underestimation of the variability (Fig. 1). PKPD TGI Modeling with In Vivo Efficacy Data

A semi-mechanistic TGI model was used for PKPD TGI modeling [25]. Individual TV were fitted simultaneously for vehicle and treated groups. In the absence of treatment, the model assumed that the TG occurred in two phases, i.e., initially, an exponential growth phase characterized by kg (exponential rate constant of TG) followed by a linear growth phase kgl (zero-order rate constant of TG). A linear concentration effect relationship was selected for the final model and a slope (slp) estimated, as this model resulted in the lowest BIC value and provided the best fit to the data. A delay model with three transit compartments, i.e., tumor cells pass through progressive stages of damage because of the drug’s action, allowed to model the process of cell death, which occurred at later times and was characterized by the model parameter tau. Tau was estimated to be 41.1 h and resulted in an average time for a cell to die of about 5–6 days.

Exponential random effects were added to TV at baseline (w0), slp, and kg. Exclusion of IIV on slp and w0 resulted in an increase in the OFV of > 6.63 (p < 0.01, chi-squared distribution, df = 1) and were kept in the final model. Removal of IIV on kg resulted in model instability and was therefore kept in the model. A combined additive and proportional error model best described the residual error. Final CalPEG population PKPD TGI parameters are given in Table 1. The uncertainty on estimates for fixed effects was small, with RSE < 20%, and the uncertainty on IIV was moderate.

GOF plots showed a good agreement between observations and individual predictions of TV in untreated groups and groups treated with CalPEG. Scatter plots did not show any substantial and systematic deviations from the identity line, except for the low population predicted TV values as indicated by the light grey circles in Fig. 2a, corresponding to time points where 4 mice out of 10 had data below LoQ, whereas 6 mice had measurable TV up to the end of the study (day 28). As only measurable TV values (and not data below LoQ) are represented on the GOF plots, this deviation is not unexpected. Overall, the GOF plots presented in Fig. 2b show a good model fit, although a slight misspecification can be observed. GOF plots were scattered evenly around the zero line when represented for CWRES and NDPE versus PRED, respectively (Fig. 2b). The IWRES, CWRES, or NDPE plots were scattered evenly around the horizontal zero line on the y-axis, when represented versus time with most of the points within −2 to 2 (Fig. 2c). No outlier data, i.e., observations with CWRES greater than 5, were observed on CWRES plots. The value of epsilon-shrinkage for the CalPEG population PK/PD model was low (4.2%), indicating no risk of perfect-fit.

Discussion

It has been shown that a relatively high fraction of HCC is hypermethylated at the asparagine (Asn) synthetase (ASNS) promoter [17]. Testing CalPEG across a panel of HCC cell lines demonstrated that ASNS promoter-hypermethylated cell lines with the lowest ASNS mRNA expression are the most responsive to CalPEG treatment in vitro. An in vivo efficacy study in mice bearing the human ASNS promoter-hypermethylated xenograft model of HCC (JHH5 model) showed that CalPEG treatment inhibited TG in a dose-dependent manner and resulted in tumor rejections at the highest doses. However, it has to be taken into account that this hypermethylated JHH5 model is not necessarily representative for the whole HCC population, but might represent a subpopulation that is more prone to responding to CalPEG. The inhibition of TG in xenografted female BALB/c nude mice receiving CalPEG has been studied through PKPD TGI modeling using a population approach and TV data from this hypermethylated CD× JHH5 model. In this model, several dose levels of CalPEG were evaluated allowing for a better description and quantification of the dose–PAA–efficacy relationship. A mathematical framework was built to translate this relationship from animals to humans and to predict clinical efficacy of CalPEG in a (sub)population of patients with HCC.

An existing population PK model of PAA in female BALB/c mice was used to predict the PAA in the JHH5 xenograft efficacy study. An external VPC showed that all treatment groups were adequately described (Fig. 1), and no difference in PK between experiments was identified, thus the estimated PK model parameters were used to simulate PAA of CalPEG for the regimen in the xenograft efficacy study during the PKPD TGI modeling process.

A semi-mechanistic TGI model [25] was used to fit the TV versus time profiles from the xenograft efficacy study. From two primary model parameters i.e., kg (related to the growth characteristics of the tumor) and the slope (slp) of the linear PKPD relationship (measure of the potency of the compound), a secondary parameter, CT (i.e., minimal threshold PAA for tumor regression; CT = kg/slp), was derived [32, 33]. The estimated minimal PAA of CalPEG needed to reach tumor regression in mice was 220 IU/L. On day 12, the predicted median predose PAA values were 71.3, 167, and 596 IU/L at 20, 40, and 100 IU/kg, respectively. PAA values were around or above CT for the entire treatment duration and resulted in complete tumor regression in 6 out of 10 mice dosed at 40 IU/kg (day 14) and in all mice at 100 IU/kg (day 12). The reason for the tumor regrowth observed after CalPEG treatment at 40 IU/kg in the remaining four mice could be owing to development of resistance under CalPEG treatment, which was not accounted for by the model. Integration of a time-varying effect in the model due to resistance or adding a resistance term to the model structure may further have improved the model performance [34]. Genotyping of the xenograft tumors in mice, to assess alterations that could lead to drug resistance, was not performed;therefore, the reason for resistance development in those four mice is unknown.

To account for a delay in the death of the tumor cells upon treatment, a model with three transit compartments was used. The number of transit compartments between cell proliferation and cell death is empirical and was selected on the basis of a model that provided the best fit to the data.

The final TGI model fitted the observed TV data well in every experimental condition and at all tested doses, although some model misspecifications were observed in the GOF plot (Fig. 2a) for the low population predicted TV values, which could be explained by the values below LoQ, and represented 36% of the total number of observations. The imputation method was used to handle TV measurements below LoQ (set to 1 mm3) in order to decrease the time it took for the model to converge and might (partly) explain the model misspecification. Therefore, the final model was rerun using the Laplacian conditional estimation with an interaction method for final parameter estimation and the M3 method for handling of the LoQ values [35]. TV measurements that were reported as 0 were flagged as censored values (with a limit of quantification (LoQ)/measurability set to 1 mm3). A summary of the modeling results of this rerun is given in Supplementary Table S5 and Supplementary Figs. S7A–S7C. Additional bias could also have been introduced by the technique used to measure the TV [36]. It is standard practice to measure subcutaneous TV by manual measurement of the length and width of the tumor using calipers. These measurements introduce biases that are primarily owing to observer subjectivity, skin thickness, subcutaneous fat, and variations in tumor shape that are often irregular instead of an assumed spheroid.

The concentration effect relationship of CalPEG was found to be linear. Additional relationships (power model, Emax, sigmoid Emax) were evaluated as well, but did not improve the fit through the data and resulted in an increase of BIC value.

For translation to patients, the estimated PD parameters from the JHH5 model in tumor bearing mice were linked to the predicted PAA in patients with HCC. As currently no PK data are available in patients with HCC, an existing PK model [18] that was developed on PAA data obtained in young and adult patients with ALL was used to predict PAA in patients with HCC under the assumption that there is no difference in PK between patients with ALL and HCC.

The results of the simulations must be interpreted in light of the model assumptions made and take into account the limitations of the preclinical xenograft model. The human model assumed that the growth characteristics, potency, and PKPD relationship (linear) of CalPEG were similar between mice and patients with HCC. However, tumor volume doubling times (TVDT) in patients with HCC (~120–150 days) are higher than the TVDT in tumor-bearing mice (~2.17 days) [37]. In addition, it has been shown that HCC tumors are very heterogeneous, with a wide variation in tumor growth patterns depending on viral liver diseases and tumor size. The TV in patients with HCC is approximately 40.2 cc which is much larger than the TV at inoculation (~0.165 cc) in mice. Both factors could have been used for the prediction of TV in patients with HCC and would have improved prediction of active doses in patients with HCC. It should also be noted that xenograft mouse models are a simplification of a human cancer and does not take into account tumor metastasis, host immunity, tumor heterogeneity, and development of tumor resistance. Nevertheless, the relationship between drug exposure and response from xenograft models are still useful to understand the degree of TGI of the drug and for translation of antitumor activity from mice to humans. In addition, it should be recognized that the knowledge of current mathematical modeling in oncology is mainly built on experience with small molecules [21, 22] and that for immune-oncology agents, differences between mouse and human immune systems would need to be taken into account.

The predicted TV versus time curves (Fig. 6; Males, BSA 2.05 m2 and age, 55 years old) illustrated that complete tumor regression (i.e., 90% TV reduction) was observed at doses > 750 IU/m2. Visual inspection of the data in Fig. 6 illustrates that the steady-state of CalPEG is reached after approximately 9–12 weeks, which is consistent with the half-life of CalPEG (t1/2 of 16.1 days), indicating that after 12 weeks of treatment, no further increase in PK is expected. Based on all preclinical data, the predicted clinical doses associated with complete tumor regression were 750 IU/m2 Q3W (after dose 5), 1000 IU/m2 (after dose 3), and 1250 IU/m2 (dose 2–3). This means that it will take approximately 15 weeks, 9 weeks, or 6 weeks before complete tumor regression will be observed with a Q3W regimen of CalPEG, provided via a 2-h infusion, at 750, 1000, or 1250 IU/m2, respectively. The time to reach complete tumor regression could be shortened by further increasing the dose (> 1250 IU/m2); however, this might not be feasible due to dose-limited toxicities. Alternatively, before reaching the target dose level and to reach steady-state earlier, the dose frequency could be increased and a dose escalation scheme could be proposed. In females (Supplementary Fig. S6; BSA 1.81 m2 and age, 55 years old), similar clinical doses associated with complete tumor regression were identified, but since exposure in females was slightly higher than in males, complete tumor regression was achieved earlier. These model-based dose predictions were in line with human equivalent doses derived via the PK matching approach, i.e., exposures at 20 IU/kg (mice) ~750 IU/m2 Q3W (patients), 40 IU/kg ~1000 IU/m2 Q3W and 100 IU/kg ~2000 IU/m2 Q3W. The above findings show that population PKPD TGI models can help inform on dosing strategies and in designing successful clinical trials.

Conclusions

A PKPD TGI model that was developed adequately described the TV versus time profiles in a preclinical hypermethylated xenograft model relevant for HCC. This TGI model was used to quantify the PKPD relationship between PAA and TGI in mice. Then, a human PKPD TGI model was developed by linking the structural mouse TGI model with the human population PK model. This human PKPD TGI model was used to perform simulations and to predict antitumor activity of CalPEG at different doses/schedule in patients with HCC. This work adequately illustrates how legacy clinical data together with newly generated preclinical data can be integrated into a PKPD TGI model to build a preclinical to clinical translational framework in order to be able to predict the exposure–response relationship in newly considered indications. In addition, the translational modeling work as presented in the paper can also be used to predict clinical response in cases of combination therapies and for quantitative prediction of the exposure–safety relationship.
