Exploiting the Notch pathway in HTLV-1–driven adult T-cell leukemia/lymphoma: a mini-review
Authors: Mohammad Mehdi Akbarin, Zahra Farjami, Hugo Ramírez Álvarez
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Human T-cell leukemia virus type 1 (HTLV-1) is the etiological agent of adult T-cell leukemia/lymphoma (ATLL) and several inflammatory disorders. Despite extensive research, the molecular mechanisms underlying HTLV-1 pathogenesis remain incompletely defined. Among host pathways hijacked by the virus, Notch signaling has emerged as a critical regulator of T-cell biology, immune evasion, and leukemogenesis. The Notch family, comprising four receptors (NOTCH1-4) and five ligands (Jagged1/2, DLL1/3/4), regulates key cellular processes, including proliferation, differentiation, and survival. Evidence suggests that HTLV-1 proteins, such as Tax and HBZ, directly or indirectly modulate Notch activity, leading to the constitutive activation of the Notch pathway and oncogenic transformation. Mutations in NOTCH1 and FBXW7 further stabilize the Notch intracellular domain (NICD), sustaining the transcription of proliferative and anti-apoptotic genes. Recent studies also highlight the involvement of JAG1 overexpression, H19/miR-675-mediated regulation, and crosstalk with PI3K and STAT3 signaling in maintaining Notch-driven leukemic phenotypes. Preclinical findings demonstrate that inhibition of Notch through γ-secretase inhibitors, restoration of FBXW7 function, or blockade of ligand-receptor interactions reduces ATLL cell growth and leukemia-initiating cell populations. However, clinical trials with selective Notch inhibitors show limited efficacy, underscoring the complexity of targeting this pathway. This mini-review provides an overview of Notch signaling in HTLV-1 infection, its contribution to ATLL pathogenesis, and therapeutic opportunities. Understanding the receptor-specific roles and molecular interactions within the Notch cascade may offer novel avenues for targeted interventions in HTLV-1-associated malignancies.

Introduction

Human T-cell leukemia virus (HTLV) is a retrovirus that infects CD4 + T lymphocytes and is the causative agent of adult T-cell leukemia/lymphoma (ATLL) and HTLV-associated myelopathy/tropical spastic paraparesis (HAM/TSP) [1, 2]. Despite decades of research, the molecular mechanisms underlying HTLV pathogenesis remain only partially understood [3, 4]. Viral proteins, such as Tax and HBZ, play critical roles in cell proliferation, transformation, and immune evasion; however, the host cellular pathways that facilitate or restrict HTLV infection are equally important [5, 6] Among these, signaling pathways that control cell fate decisions and immune regulation have gained attention as potential contributors to HTLV-driven oncogenesis and persistence [7, 8].

Tax acts as a potent transactivator that promotes cell proliferation and survival by constitutively activating pathways such as NF-κB, PI3K/AKT, and Notch, while also interfering with DNA repair mechanisms [5, 6]. In contrast, HBZ, which is consistently expressed in ATLL cells, contributes to immune evasion, maintenance of proliferation, and modulation of host transcriptional programs.

One such pathway is the Notch signaling cascade, which plays a central role in regulating cell proliferation, differentiation, and survival [9]. Notch signaling is highly conserved across species and exerts context-dependent effects that are crucial for development, hematopoiesis, and immune function [10, 11].

The Notch family comprises four receptors (NOTCH1–4) and five ligands, which regulate transcriptional programs controlling proliferation, differentiation, and survival [12]. While NOTCH1 has been extensively studied in HTLV-1–associated ATLL, the contribution of other receptors remains poorly defined. Importantly, accumulating evidence suggests that HTLV-1 proteins interact with Notch signaling components, leading to constitutive activation of the pathway and promoting leukemic transformation [13].

Although evidence suggests that Notch signaling is aberrantly activated in ATLL cells, the precise mechanisms linking HTLV infection to Notch dysregulation remain elusive [13]. Current studies suggest crosstalk between viral proteins (e.g., Tax and HBZ) and Notch-related pathways; however, the extent to which each Notch receptor family member contributes to disease onset, progression, or persistence has not been comprehensively addressed {. Furthermore, little is known about whether therapeutic targeting of Notch could be effective in HTLV-related diseases, as has been explored in other hematological malignancies. This knowledge gap limits the development of targeted therapeutic interventions that could complement existing treatment strategies for HTLV-associated disorders.

This mini-review aims to provide a comprehensive overview of the role of Notch family members in HTLV infection and ATLL pathogenesis. We summarize the current understanding of Notch biology, highlight evidence linking Notch signaling to HTLV-driven transformation, and discuss the potential therapeutic implications of modulating this pathway. By clarifying the contributions of individual Notch receptors and their downstream targets, this review seeks to identify key areas for future investigation and opportunities for translational research in HTLV-related malignancies.

General aspects of the NOTCH family

The Notch family represents an evolutionarily conserved signaling system that plays a central role in cell-cell communication [14]. Initially discovered in Drosophila melanogaster, Notch signaling is now known to be essential in mammals for controlling a wide variety of biological processes, including embryonic development, hematopoiesis, vascular remodeling, and immune regulation [14–16]. Its importance lies in its ability to govern binary cell fate decisions and maintain tissue homeostasis. At the same time, aberrant activation or suppression of Notch signaling has been associated with multiple pathological conditions, ranging from developmental syndromes to cancer [14, 17].

In mammals, the Notch family consists of four receptors, NOTCH1, NOTCH2, NOTCH3, and NOTCH4, and five canonical ligands that belong to two structurally distinct families: Jagged (Jagged1 and Jagged2) and Delta-like (DLL1, DLL3, and DLL4) [18, 19] (Table 1) (Fig. 1). These ligands are single-pass transmembrane proteins expressed on neighboring cells, making Notch signaling a strictly juxtacrine mechanism that requires direct cell-cell contact [14, 20]. This feature distinguishes the pathway from many other signaling cascades mediated by soluble factors, emphasizing its importance in local microenvironments, such as the thymus, lymphoid organs, and bone marrow niche [14, 16, 21]. The structural organization of Notch receptors is critical for their function. The extracellular portion contains multiple epidermal growth factor (EGF)-like repeats, which are responsible for ligand binding, followed by a negative regulatory region (NRR) that prevents ligand-independent activation [18, 24] (Fig. 1). The intracellular portion harbors several key domains, including a RAM domain, ankyrin repeats, nuclear localization sequences, and a PEST sequence that controls receptor turnover [18, 24]. Upon ligand binding, conformational changes expose cleavage sites in the receptor, initiating a cascade of proteolytic events (Fig. 1).

The Notch signaling pathway proceeds through a unique activation mechanism involving two successive proteolytic cleavages [25]. ADAM-family metalloproteases and the second catalyze the first cleavage by the γ-secretase complex [26] (Fig. 1). This sequential processing releases the Notch intracellular domain (NICD) into the cytoplasm. The NICD rapidly translocates into the nucleus, where it interacts with the DNA-binding protein RBP-Jκ (also known as CSL or CBF1) and co-activators of the Mastermind-like (MAML) family [26, 27]. In the absence of NICD, RBP-Jκ is bound to transcriptional repressors and silences target gene expression [26, 27]. NICD binding converts this repression into transcriptional activation, thereby initiating the expression of downstream target genes, most prominently the HES (Hairy and Enhancer of Split) and HEY (HES-related with YRPW motif) gene families [28, 29] (Fig. 1). These transcription factors then regulate diverse processes, including cell cycle progression, apoptosis, and lineage specification.

A distinctive feature of Notch signaling is its context dependency. While NOTCH1 activation is crucial for T-cell lineage commitment in the thymus, aberrant NOTCH1 signaling has been identified as a primary oncogenic driver in T-cell acute lymphoblastic leukemia (T-ALL) [17, 30]. Similarly, NOTCH2 and NOTCH3 play essential roles in B-cell differentiation, vascular development, and smooth muscle biology; however, dysregulation has been linked to marginal zone lymphomas, vascular tumors, and smooth muscle disorders [31, 32]. NOTCH4, although less extensively studied, contributes to angiogenesis and regulates the function of endothelial cell [33, 34]. The ligands also display functional diversity: DLL4 is indispensable for vascular branching, while Jagged1 mutations underlie Alagille syndrome, a congenital disorder affecting the liver, heart, and skeleton [33, 34]. DLL3 is an atypical ligand with inhibitory properties and has been implicated in small cell lung cancer, where it serves as a potential therapeutic target [35, 36].

While NOTCH1 represents the most extensively studied receptor in T-cell malignancies, accumulating evidence indicates that NOTCH3 also plays a significant oncogenic role. NOTCH3 is overexpressed in a substantial proportion of T-ALL cases, including more than 60% of those harboring NOTCH1 activating mutations, and activating mutations in NOTCH3 have also been reported [17, 37]. These findings suggest that NOTCH3 may cooperate with or complement NOTCH1 signaling in driving T-cell leukemogenesis. In contrast, the role of NOTCH3 in ATLL remains largely unexplored. Given its established contribution to T-cell malignancies, further investigation into NOTCH3 signaling in the context of HTLV-1 infection may provide new insights into disease mechanisms and reveal additional therapeutic opportunities.

Crosstalk with other signaling pathways further complicates the biology of the Notch pathway. For example, interactions with the NF-κB, Wnt/β-catenin, PI3K/AKT, and TGF-β pathways fine-tune the outcome of Notch activation in immune cells and cancer [38–40]. The balance of these signals determines whether Notch functions as an oncogene, tumor suppressor, or regulator of immune tolerance. This plasticity underscores why the pathway can be considered analogous to a “molecular switchboard,” integrating multiple cues to direct cellular responses.

Given its wide-ranging functions, dysregulation of Notch signaling contributes to the pathogenesis of numerous diseases [21, 41]. In addition to hematological malignancies, Notch mutations or abnormal activities have been implicated in solid tumors (e.g., breast, lung, and colorectal cancers), neurodegenerative disorders, and vascular abnormalities [28, 41, 42]. Significantly, γ-secretase inhibitors and monoclonal antibodies targeting Notch receptors or ligands have been developed as therapeutic strategies; however, their clinical application is limited by toxicity due to the essential role of Notch in normal tissue homeostasis [43, 44].

Overall, the Notch family constitutes a critical regulatory system with broad physiological and pathological implications. Understanding the specific contributions of each receptor, ligand, and signaling partner remains essential for delineating their roles in viral infections, such as HTLV, where the hijacking of host pathways drives leukemogenesis and immune evasion. This foundational knowledge of Notch biology provides the basis for exploring its potential as both a biomarker and a therapeutic target in HTLV-associated diseases.

The role of NOTCH family in HTLV-1 infection

Human T-cell leukemia virus type 1 (HTLV-1) hijacks host cellular pathways to sustain viral persistence and promote disease progression [3, 45]. Among these, the Notch signaling pathway has emerged as a central regulatory network due to its essential roles in T-cell development, immune homeostasis, and oncogenic transformation [46, 47]. Given that HTLV-1 primarily infects CD4 + T lymphocytes, the dysregulation of pathways controlling proliferation, differentiation, and survival such as Notch is particularly relevant to both viral persistence and leukemogenesis.

Viral exploitation of NOTCH signaling: global and mechanistic insights

The interaction between HTLV-1 and Notch signaling reflects a broader strategy whereby viral proteins reprogram host cellular networks. Systems-level analyses have demonstrated that HTLV-1 proteins interact with multiple host signaling pathways, including NOTCH, thereby shaping infection outcomes and promoting transformation [48]. These interactions can alter receptor activation, transcriptional outputs, and immune regulatory processes, suggesting that NOTCH signaling functions as both a mediator of viral persistence and a contributor to disease progression [48].

Consistent with this, broader oncogenic models indicate that viruses including HTLV-1 exploit NOTCH signaling through both direct and indirect mechanisms [49]. These include promoting NICD accumulation, modulating transcriptional complexes, and sustaining the expression of downstream target genes involved in proliferation and survival (Fig. 2). Genetic and post-translational mechanisms sustaining NOTCH activation

A major mechanism underlying persistent NOTCH activation in adult T-cell leukemia/lymphoma (ATLL) involves genetic and post-translational dysregulation of NICD turnover. Mutations in the PEST domain of NOTCH1 impair its degradation by the ubiquitin ligase F-box and WD repeat domain-containing seven (FBXW7), leading to stabilization of NICD and sustained signaling [50]. Similarly, alterations in FBXW7 itself disrupt NICD degradation, resulting in prolonged transcriptional activation of NOTCH target genes that promote proliferation and survival [51].

Importantly, viral proteins further amplify this effect. HTLV-1 Tax has been shown to directly interfere with FBXW7 function, preventing NICD turnover and enhancing NOTCH signaling activity [52]. This viral-host interaction represents a critical convergence point where genetic lesions and viral oncogenes cooperate to sustain pathway activation. Supporting this, genomic analyses have identified recurrent alterations in NOTCH1 and FBXW7 in ATLL patients, reinforcing the importance of this axis in disease pathogenesis (Fig. 2) [53].

Viral-driven activation of NOTCH signaling

Beyond stabilizing NICD, HTLV-1 actively promotes NOTCH signaling through direct activation mechanisms. Experimental studies demonstrate that HTLV-1 infection leads to increased NICD expression in ATL cells, and that NOTCH1 knockdown significantly reduces cellular proliferation and tumorigenicity [13]. These findings support a model in which the viral Tax protein enhances NOTCH1 signaling, contributing to the expansion and survival of infected T cells.

HBZ, the constitutively expressed HTLV-1 bZIP factor, plays a central role in maintaining ATLL but has a less clearly defined relationship with Notch signaling compared to Tax. Current evidence suggests that HBZ may indirectly influence Notch-related transcription through interactions with host transcription factors and pathways such as STAT3 and Wnt/β-catenin. Given its persistent expression and potential involvement in leukemia-initiating cell biology [54, 55], elucidating the role of HBZ in Notch pathway modulation remains an important unmet need.

Pharmacological inhibition further supports the functional importance of this pathway. Blocking NOTCH activation using γ-secretase inhibitors reduces ATLL cell proliferation and tumor formation, highlighting NOTCH signaling as a key driver of HTLV-1–associated leukemogenesis [50].

Ligand-dependent and transcriptional regulation of NOTCH signaling

In addition to receptor-level activation, HTLV-1 infection modulates NOTCH signaling through ligand-dependent and transcriptional mechanisms. The NOTCH ligand Jagged1 (JAG1) is significantly overexpressed in ATLL patient samples, and this upregulation is driven by Tax via STAT3 and NFATc1 transcription factors [56]. Functional studies demonstrate that inhibition of JAG1 reduces downstream NOTCH1 signaling and impairs cell migration, suggesting a role in tumor progression and dissemination [56].

MicroRNA-mediated regulation further refines this process. For example, miR-124a targets STAT3 and NFATc1, thereby modulating JAG1 expression and indirectly influencing NOTCH pathway activity [56]. These findings highlight the importance of ligand-driven and transcriptional control in sustaining NOTCH signaling in HTLV-1–infected cells.

Epigenetic and non-coding RNA regulation of NOTCH signaling

Recent evidence underscores the role of non-coding RNAs in regulating NOTCH signaling in ATLL. The long non-coding RNA H19 and its associated microRNA miR-675 are overexpressed in ATLL cells and contribute to NOTCH pathway activation by regulating NOTCH1 and JAG1 expression [57]. Notably, this activation can occur independently of NOTCH1 or FBXW7 mutations, suggesting alternative mechanisms for sustaining NOTCH signaling in leukemic cells [57].

This highlights a layered regulatory network in which epigenetic and post-transcriptional mechanisms complement genetic and viral factors to maintain pathway activity.

Functional consequences: proliferation, survival, and leukemia-initiating cells

NOTCH signaling exerts complex and context-dependent effects in ATLL. While it promotes proliferation and survival of leukemic cells, its role in leukemia-initiating cells (LICs) appears more nuanced. Side population (SP) cells, which are enriched for LICs, exhibit low NOTCH1 activity and reduced expression of stemness-associated markers such as STAT3, β-catenin/Wnt3, and HBZ [58]. Activation of NOTCH1 leads to depletion of these SP cells, suggesting that NOTCH signaling may negatively regulate LIC maintenance [58].

Additionally, an inverse relationship between NOTCH1 and PI3K signaling has been observed, indicating that cross-talk between pathways may influence stemness and therapeutic responses [58]. These findings emphasize the dual and context-dependent role of NOTCH signaling in ATL biology.

Cooperative oncogenic networks and disease evolution

Large-scale genomic and transcriptomic studies further demonstrate that NOTCH signaling operates within a broader oncogenic network. Integrated sequencing analyses reveal that ATLL progression is driven by the accumulation of mutations affecting NOTCH, TCR, and STAT3 pathways, along with clonal evolution over time [59]. Similarly, studies in related T-cell malignancies identify frequent alterations in NOTCH1, FBXW7, and other oncogenic drivers, highlighting the clinical relevance of NOTCH pathway dysregulation [60].

These findings underscore the cooperative nature of HTLV-1–driven oncogenesis, in which NOTCH signaling acts as a central node within a complex signaling landscape.

Collectively, these studies demonstrate that HTLV-1 exploits the NOTCH signaling pathway through multi-layered mechanisms, including viral protein activity, genetic alterations, ligand-dependent activation, and epigenetic regulation. This convergence leads to constitutive NOTCH activation, promoting T-cell proliferation, survival, and disease progression.

At the same time, the pathway exhibits context-dependent effects, particularly in leukemia-initiating cells, highlighting the complexity of its role in ATLL (Fig. 2). These insights position NOTCH signaling as a critical therapeutic target, with strategies such as γ-secretase inhibition and restoration of FBXW7 function representing promising avenues for intervention (Fig. 2).

The human T-cell leukemia virus type 1 (HTLV-1) encodes the oncoprotein Tax, which modulates multiple host signaling pathways to promote leukemogenesis. In monocytes, Tax enhances the expression of the NOTCH ligand Jagged1, while deregulated microRNAs (miR-124a and H19/miR-675) contribute to altered signaling through modulation of transcription factors such as signal transducer and activator of transcription 3 (STAT3) and nuclear factor of activated T-cells cytoplasmic 1 (NFATc1). Jagged1 interacts with NOTCH receptors via the Delta/Serrate/LAG-2 (DSL) domain, epidermal growth factor (EGF) repeats, and cysteine-rich (CR) regions, initiating NOTCH signaling through the transmembrane domain (TMD) and extracellular domain (NECD).

In T cells, Tax interferes with the tumor suppressor F-box and WD repeat domain-containing 7 (FBXW7), an E3 ubiquitin ligase responsible for targeting NOTCH intracellular domain (NICD) for degradation, thereby preventing NICD turnover and leading to its accumulation in the cytoplasm. Accumulated NICD translocates into the nucleus, where it binds recombination signal-binding protein for immunoglobulin kappa J region (RBP-Jκ) and recruits mastermind-like protein (MAML) to activate transcription of downstream genes. This induces the expression of the hairy and enhancer of split (HES) and hairy/enhancer-of-split-related with YRPW motif (HEY) family members, which regulate immune responses, cell survival, and proliferation. Additionally, NICD activates the phosphoinositide 3-kinase (PI3K)/AKT pathway, targeting downstream effectors such as glycogen synthase kinase 3 (GSK3), Bcl-2-associated death promoter (BAD), and caspase-9 (CAS9), thereby further promoting oncogenic survival signaling. Tax also suppresses the PEST domain-mediated degradation of NICD, reinforcing its stability and sustained transcriptional activity. Collectively, these interactions between Tax and NOTCH signaling components highlight a critical mechanism by which HTLV-1 promotes T-cell transformation and the development of adult T-cell leukemia/lymphoma. Therapeutic approach for NOTCH inhibition in T-cell acute malignancies

Targeting the Notch signaling pathway represents a promising therapeutic strategy in HTLV-1-associated diseases, particularly ATLL, where constitutive activation of Notch drives proliferation, survival, and malignant transformation of infected T cells. Viral proteins, such as Tax, interfere with negative regulators like FBXW7, leading to sustained Notch activity. Additionally, modulators like JAG1 and the H19/miR-675 axis further enhance signaling. Therapeutic approaches aimed at inhibiting Notch, ranging from γ-secretase inhibitors that block NICD release to agents that restore FBXW7 function or disrupt ligand-receptor interactions, hold potential to reduce leukemic cell growth, impair the maintenance of leukemia-initiating cells, and limit disease progression. This section examines current strategies for inhibiting Notch in HTLV-1 infection and their potential clinical implications.

Rakowski, L. A. et al. (2011) investigated the therapeutic effects of inhibiting Notch signaling and TLX1 (also known as HOX11) in T-ALL [61]. Using a conditional mouse model, they demonstrated that suppression of TLX1 expression slowed the growth of TLX1-driven tumor cell lines and transiently delayed leukemia progression in vivo [61]. Similarly, inhibition of Notch signaling abrogated the growth of these cell lines and delayed the progression of leukemia. However, these effects were transient, as the tumors eventually “escaped” inhibition, suggesting that the biological pathways impacted by TLX1 and Notch lose their importance in fully established tumors, which are supplanted by stronger oncogenic pathways [61]. The study concluded that while Notch and TLX1 inhibitors may not be effective as single agents, they could contribute to combination therapy for TLX1-driven acute leukemia.

Yu, P. et al. (2015) explored the combined therapeutic effects of Notch-1 pathway blockade, bortezomib, and romidepsin in a murine MT-1 adult T-cell leukemia model [62]. By combining Notch-1 inhibition with bortezomib and romidepsin, a synergistic effect was achieved, resulting in enhanced antitumor responses [62]. This combination therapy not only reduced tumor growth but also targeted leukemia-initiating cells, which are often resistant to conventional treatments. Therefore, this combination therapy led to enhanced antitumor efficacy, significantly reducing tumor burden and prolonging survival in the treated mice [62]. Notably, the combination therapy also decreased the number of leukemia-initiating cells, suggesting a potential strategy for targeting the root of the malignancy [62].

Moreover, the inhibitor of NOTCH in T-ALL is evaluated. Borthakur, G. et al. in 2021, by a phase 1 clinical trial evaluated the safety and preliminary efficacy of crenigacestat (LY3039478), a selective Notch1 inhibitor, in combination with dexamethasone in adult patients with relapsed or refractory T-ALL and T-cell lymphoblastic lymphoma (T-LBL) [63]. This multicenter, open-label, dose-escalation study enrolled 36 patients, primarily with T-ALL (86.1%). The treatment regimen consisted of oral crenigacestat administered three times per week, in conjunction with dexamethasone at 24 mg twice daily on days 1–5 of each 28-day cycle. The study aimed to determine the maximum tolerated dose (MTD) and assess the clinical activity of this combination therapy [63].

The results indicated that the MTD of crenigacestat was 75 mg, with dose-limiting toxicities observed at higher doses, including gastrointestinal hemorrhage and severe nausea, vomiting, and diarrhea [63]. Treatment-emergent adverse events were reported in 77.8% of patients, with the most common being gastrointestinal in nature. Regarding clinical efficacy, only one patient (2.8%) achieved a confirmed partial response, with a duration of 10.5 months [63]. Additionally, six patients (16.7%) had stable disease, while 12 (33.3%) experienced disease progression. The remaining 17 patients (47.2%) were not evaluable for response. These findings suggest that while the combination of crenigacestat and dexamethasone is tolerable at the MTD, it demonstrates limited clinical activity in this patient population [63]. Further studies are needed to explore alternative dosing strategies or combination therapies to enhance therapeutic outcomes in T-ALL and T-LBL.

Finally, Sergio, I. et al. (2024) highlighted the therapeutic potential of combining Notch inhibitors with BH3 mimetics in T-ALL, where aberrant Notch1 signaling drives uncontrolled T-cell proliferation and survival [64]. In preclinical models, γ-secretase inhibitors (GSIs) effectively reduced Notch1 activity, leading to decreased leukemic cell growth and induction of apoptosis. BH3 mimetics, which target anti-apoptotic BCL-2 family proteins, enhanced these effects by promoting mitochondrial-mediated cell death, demonstrating a synergistic impact when used in combination [64].

As a summary, targeting Notch signaling represents a compelling therapeutic avenue in HTLV-1-associated T-cell malignancies, including ATLL and T-ALL, given its central role in driving proliferation, survival, and malignant transformation. Preclinical studies demonstrate that Notch inhibition, either alone or in combination with agents such as TLX1 suppression, proteasome inhibitors, histone deacetylase inhibitors, or BH3 mimetics, can reduce tumor growth, impair leukemia-initiating cell populations, and enhance antitumor efficacy. However, clinical translation remains challenging, as evidenced by the limited activity observed with selective Notch inhibitors, such as crenigacestat, in patients with relapsed or refractory disease, highlighting the need for optimized combination strategies and alternative dosing regimens. Overall, these findings highlight the therapeutic potential of Notch-targeted interventions in HTLV-1-infected T cells, underscoring the need for continued research to enhance efficacy and overcome resistance mechanisms.

Conclusion and future perspective

HTLV-1 exploits the Notch signaling pathway to drive viral persistence, deregulate T-cell homeostasis, and promote oncogenic transformation in ATLL. Viral proteins, such as Tax and HBZ, in conjunction with genetic alterations in NOTCH1 and FBXW7, establish a state of constitutive Notch activation that sustains proliferation, survival, and resistance to apoptosis. Additional regulators, including JAG1 overexpression and non-coding RNAs like H19/miR-675, further reinforce this malignant program. Therapeutic inhibition of Notch signaling has demonstrated promise in preclinical studies, particularly when combined with proteasome or BCL-2 family inhibitors, yet clinical translation remains hindered by toxicity and limited efficacy. Moving forward, precise dissection of receptor-specific contributions, integration with other signaling pathways, and optimized combination strategies will be essential to unlock the therapeutic potential of Notch-targeted interventions. Ultimately, clarifying the role of Notch in HTLV-1 infection not only advances our understanding of viral oncogenesis but also offers opportunities for innovative, mechanism-based treatments for ATLL.

Future studies should unravel the receptor-specific contributions of the Notch family in HTLV-1 infection and ATLL progression, since most current data focus predominantly on NOTCH1, leaving the roles of NOTCH2-4 largely undefined. High-resolution approaches such as single-cell transcriptomics, spatial proteomics, and longitudinal genomic profiling will be valuable for dissecting clonal evolution and heterogeneity within ATLL, particularly in relation to Notch activity and its interplay with viral proteins. Furthermore, the functional relevance of non-coding RNAs, including long non-coding RNAs (lncRNAs) and microRNAs, which modulate Notch signaling, warrants deeper investigation as potential biomarkers and therapeutic targets. Preclinical studies should expand beyond γ-secretase inhibitors to explore novel strategies, such as monoclonal antibodies, small molecules that restore FBXW7 function and rational drug combinations with BCL-2 or PI3K/AKT inhibitors, to overcome resistance and toxicity issues. Finally, translating these findings into clinical practice will require well-designed trials incorporating biomarker-driven patient stratification, enabling the development of precision therapies that exploit the vulnerabilities of Notch-dependent HTLV-1-infected cells.
