Lung microbiome predictors of epigenetic aging and potential associations with smoking and electronic cigarette use
Authors: Ajmal Khan, Boseung Choi, Seungbae Kang, Daniel Y Weng, Kevin Ying, Joseph P McElroy, Sahar Kamel, Sarah A Reisinger, Mark D Wewers, Peter G Shields, Min-Ae Song
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe lungs harbor diverse microbial communities that may influence pulmonary health, potentially through lung aging. While accelerated lung aging can increase susceptibility to pulmonary diseases, no studies have yet linked the lung microbiome to biological aging in disease-free individuals.Materials and methodsWe assessed well-studied methylation-based biological aging (mAge) markers (Horvath, GrimAge, PhenoAge, and telomere-length) in the lungs of healthy smokers (SM), electronic cigarette (EC) users, and never-smokers (NS) (n = 26, 21–30 years). We used metatranscriptome profiling to detect live bacteria. Using XGBoost, we performed feature selection on 1016 bacterial species to predict faster or slower lung mAge, and the selected bacterial species were used as explanatory variables in a logistic regression model. Linear regression analyses examined the associations between identified bacterial species and urinary metabolites of exposure to smoking and EC use, including volatile organic compounds (VOCs) and polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons (PAHs).ResultsThe logistic regression models identified bacterial species that classified individuals with faster or slower lung aging based on each mAge estimate (accuracy 77%–85%; AUC 0.78–0.91). Two species strongly predictive of GrimAge, Alistipes finegoldii and Arachidicoccus sp.BS20 were significantly less present in SM compared to NS. Arachidicoccus sp.BS20 was significantly associated with nicotine-intake-adjusted metabolites of several VOCs and PAHs in SM and EC users.ConclusionFor the first time, our study suggests potential associations of the microbiome with biological aging in the lungs of healthy individuals. In addition, the findings indicate that exposure to smoking and EC may be linked to shifts in particular microbial profiles associated with biological aging of the lungs. These results support the need for larger studies to better understand the direction and possible mechanisms of these relationships, and to further explore the lung microbiome as a potential target for interventions aimed at mitigating pulmonary aging and disease risk.Graphical abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13148-026-02126-9.Keywords: Lung microbiome, Epigenetic aging, Cigarette, Electronic cigarette, VOCs, PAHs

Introduction

The microbiome refers to the collective genome of microorganisms in a particular environment, including their interactions with each other and with their host [1]. While the gut microbiome is the most extensively studied [2], the lung microbiome has historically been overlooked due to the outdated belief that the lungs are sterile [3]. However, recent studies, including ours, have shown that the lungs harbor diverse microbial communities [4–7].

In the healthy lung, the normal beneficial microbiome participates and cooperates in host functions such as metabolism [8] and defense [9]. In contrast, dysbiosis, microbial imbalance, has been shown to promote and develop lung diseases such as chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) [10], asthma [11], and cancer [12].

Aging is an inevitable biological process characterized by declining physiological function and increased vulnerability to environmental stressors, morbidity, and mortality [13, 14]. The aging process involves notable changes at the nuclear, cellular, and subcellular levels [15, 16], including epigenetic alterations, such as DNA methylation, a key hallmark of aging [17]. DNA methylation occurs predominantly on cytosines followed by guanine residues (CpGs) and is a well-established epigenetic marker regulating gene expression, including genes involved in lung disease development and progression [18, 19]. This process is dynamic, changing with the human lifetime and environmental factors [20].

Recently, DNA methylation-based aging (mAge) markers, namely epigenetic clocks, have drawn significant attention in lung diseases to capture biological aging and understand their roles in disease risk and outcomes [21, 22]. Several epigenetic clocks have been developed to incorporate epigenetic aging markers in multiple or specific tissues, thereby improving predictive performance for specific aging measures, morbidity, or mortality [23]. These epigenetic clocks include Horvath’s pan-tissue clock, which was developed to estimate for chronological age across multiple tissues [24, 25]; PhenoAge, which incorporates clinical biomarkers and is intended to indicate phenotypic aging, lifespan, and healthspan [26]; Grim-mAge, which integrates DNA methylation surrogates of plasma proteins and smoking exposure to predict lifespan and mortality [27]; and TL-mAge for DNA methylation-based telomere length [28], which are found to be associated with tobacco smoking [23, 29, 30].

Smoking is known to impair lung function [31], accelerate mAge [29, 32], and alter both epigenetic mechanisms [33, 34] and the lung microbiota [7, 35]. Our prior study showed that cigarette smoking and electronic cigarette (EC) use were associated with distinct lung microbial species in healthy individuals [7]. Cigarette smoke is a mixture of toxic chemical compounds, including volatile organic compounds (VOCs) and polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons (PAHs), with several known to be probable human carcinogens [36–38]. These toxicants are also found in EC, while their levels are substantially lower compared to cigarette smoke [39].

Despite the extensive research on the impact of tobacco on lung health [31], the interplay between smoking-related toxicants, the lung microbiome, and lung biological aging remains unknown. In this study, we hypothesize that the lung microbiome may impact lung biological aging, measured by epigenetic clocks. Using machine learning (ML), we identified lung bacterial species predictive of accelerated (faster) or decelerated (slower) mAge in the lungs of healthy individuals. Furthermore, we examined how exposure to smoking- and EC-related toxicants (i.e., Volatile Organic Compounds and Polycyclic Aromatic Hydrocarbons) relates to these microbial predictors of lung aging in smokers and EC users.

Materials and methods

Study participants and exclusion criteria

Recruitment and study design were documented in detail previously [7, 34, 40]. Briefly, between 2015 and 2019, local print and TV media were utilized for the recruitment of study participants, all of whom were healthy adults aged 21–30 years and were willing to undergo bronchoscopy. The participants who were regular users of marijuana and/or those who had underlying medical conditions such as COPD, cancer, or autoimmune disorders, were excluded to avoid any potential confounders related to pre-existing diseases.

In this study, we included 26 participants with microbiome and mAge data available, consisting of never-smokers (NS, n = 10), smokers (SM, n = 7), and electronic cigarette (EC) users (n = 9). Sex distribution differed across groups: 60% of NS were male, 66.7% of EC users were male, and all SM were male (Table 1). As per CDC guidelines, NS were classified as adults who have never smoked, or who have smoked < 100 cigarettes in their lifetime, while SM were defined as adults who have smoked ≥ 100 cigarettes in their lifetime. The EC users were defined as individuals who have not smoked a single cigarette and have been using EC daily for at least 6 months. Detailed information on prior smoking history among EC users, including former cigarette exposure, is provided in Supplementary Table 1.

The Institution Review Board (IRB) of The Ohio State University (OSU) approved the study (the IRB approval number: 2015C0088, ClinicalTrials.gov: NCT02596685; 2015–11-04). Written informed consent was obtained from each participant. The participants attended an orientation session under a trained clinical research assistant prior to undergoing bronchoscopy.

Bronchoalveolar lavage (BAL) and bronchial epithelial brushing samples

BAL for microbiome and bronchial epithelial brushing samples for epigenetic aging from the main bronchus were collected during the bronchoscopy at The OSU James Comprehensive Cancer Center [7, 34, 40]. Light intravenous sedation was used for the bronchoscopy. Randomization protocol was used for lavage sampling from the left or right lungs of the subjects. For BAL, the bronchoscope was inserted into the subsegmental bronchus, and 20 ml saline was injected at least 5–7 times to recover the samples at each lavage. Promptly, six 1 ml aliquots were made of BAL samples and centrifuged at 10,000 g to remove the supernatant. The cell pellets were resuspended in 1 ml QIAGEN RNA protect cell reagent and stored at − 80 °C. For bronchial epithelium, the brush heads were immersed in RNAprotect reagents (QIAGEN) and stored at − 80 °C until further analyses.

Microbiome analysis

RNA extraction and RNA sequencing for microbiome analysis

miRNeasy kit (Qiagen, Germantown, MD) was used for the extraction of RNA from BAL cell pellets. A Nanodrop spectrophotometer (Thermo Scientific, Waltham, MA) was used for the measurement of RNA concentration, which ranged from 4 to 267.8 (ng/μl). The samples were then prepared for library generation. Bioanalyzer (Agilent, Santa Clara, CA) was used for the determination of adequate RNA integrity. To remove the rRNA, the total RNA was treated with TruSeq Stranded Total RNA Gold (Illumina, San Diego, CA), and the total RNA was then used for the synthesis of cDNA, which was then used for library generation. The final concentration range was 0.27–10.8 (ng/μl), which was measured through Qubit. RNA sequencing was performed using Illumina Hi-seq 4000 at 2 × 150 bps. To demultiplex reads, the Base Space was used, which generated read 1 and 2 FASTQ files for each subject.

Data processing

To evaluate the quality of the reads FASTQ files were then subjected to FASTQC. Those reads were retained and had quality scores of at least 30. To trim adapter sequence the Cutadapt was utilized. Sequencing quality metrics, including sequencing depth per sample, are provided in Supplementary Table 2, demonstrating adequate read coverage across all study groups. The rest of the reads were then aligned to the human genome (Hg19) by TopHat v.2.1.0, [41], via the below parameters: -p 8 -no-coverage-search-library-type fr-firststrand hg19.genome Read1.fastq.gz Read2.fastq.gz. Reads that were not mapped to the human genome were aligned to complete genomes of microbiomes downloaded from the NCBI RefSeq database (https://ftp.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/genomes/refseq/) (dated March 2018). Kraken version 1 [42] was utilized for alignment with the below parameters: kraken --preload –Kraken_Database --fastq-input --gzip-compressed --paired Microbiome_Read1.fastq.gz Microbiome_Read2.fastq.gz --output Microbial_alignment. The count table was then made from alignments files results: kraken-report -Kraken_Database Microbial_alignment. The Kraken aligned reads were then fed to Bracken v.1 to align to bacterial sp. utilizing the below parameters: python est_abundance.py -i BAL_Samples_Full_Kraken_subanalysis -k KMER_DISTR.txt. The reason for using the Bracken was that it can categorize some microbial reads into genera or higher levels. Bracken reclassified the entire non-species classifications from Kraken to species-level classification. To account for differences in sequencing depth across samples, bacterial read counts were normalized prior to downstream analyses by converting counts to relative abundances based on the total number of bacterial reads per sample [43]. Bacterial species were taken if they fulfilled the given two conditions: (1) the presence of species in at least two subjects and (2) aligned reads of the species of at least 0.005% of total aligned microbial reads in a subject. Detailed data processing was performed as reported in our previous work [6, 7].

Epigenetic aging (mAge) markers

Using an AllPrep DNA/RNA kit (Qiagen), genomic DNA was extracted from bronchial brushings, treated with bisulfite (EZ DNA Methylation kit, Zymo Research, Irvine, CA), and then analyzed for genome-wide methylation using the Infinium MethylationEPIC BeadChip (Illumina, San Diego, CA). As described in our previous paper [29], mAge estimates were calculated by using Horvath’s New Methylation Age Calculator (https://dnamage.genetics.ucla.edu/new) with advanced analysis [27, 44]. Epigenetic age acceleration (mAge acceleration, mAA) for Horvath [24, 25], GrimAge [27], PhenoAge [26], and Telomere-length [DNAmTL] [28] was calculated as the residual from regressing mAge on chronological age. For Horvath, GrimAge, and PhenoAge, individuals with positive or negative mAA values were defined as having accelerated (faster) or decelerated (slower) mAge than expected. For DNAmTL, positive or negative acceleration values indicate longer or shorter telomere length than expected. Because these epigenetic clocks reflect different aspects of biological aging, we examined them to assess which epigenetic aging-related processes in the lungs are most affected by the microbiome, smoking, and EC exposure examined in this study.

Urinary metabolites of smoking and EC use

Liquid chromatography–tandem mass spectrometry (LC–MS/MS) was employed to detect the level of 10 mercapturic acid metabolites of VOCs and 8 mono-hydroxylated metabolites of PAHs (OH-PAHs) in the urine samples as described by [45]. Briefly, LC–MS/MS analyses were performed with an Agilent (Palo Alto, CA) 1200 HPLC coupled with a Thermo-Finnigan (San Jose, CA) TSQ Quantum Ultra triple-stage quadrupole mass spectrometer, with an atmospheric pressure chemical ionization (APCI) source. The chromatographic separation was performed using gradient elution on an analytical column (Phenomenex Synergi Polar-RP column, 150 mm × 4.6 mm, 4 μm, Phenomenex Inc. Torrance, CA). Ten kinds of mercapturic acid metabolites measured were phenyl mercapturic acid (PMA,) (a metabolite of benzene), 4-hydroxy-2-buten-1-yl-mercapturic acid (MHBMA3) (a metabolite of 1,3-butadiene), 2-hydroxy-3-buten-1-yl-mercapturic acid (MHBMA1&2) (a metabolite of 1,3-butadiene), 2-hydroxy-3-buten-1-yl-mercapturic acid (HEMA) (a metabolite of ethylene oxide), methyl mercapturic acid (MMA) (a metabolite of methylating agent), 2-cyanoethyl mercapturic acid (CNEMA) (a metabolite of acrylonitrile), 3-hydroxypropyl mercapturic acid (3-HPMA) (a metabolite of acrolein), 2-hydroxypropyl mercapturic acid (2-HPMA) (a metabolite of propylene oxide), 2-carbamoyl ethyl mercapturic acid (AAMA) (a metabolite of acrylamide), and 3-hydroxy-1-methylpropylmercapturic acid (HPMMA) (a metabolite of crotonaldehyde). Eight types of OH-PAHs measured were 2-naphthol (a metabolite of naphthalene), 1-hydroxyfluorene (a metabolite of fluorene), 2-hydroxyfluorene (a metabolite of fluorene), 3-hydroxyfluorene (a metabolite of fluorene), 1-hydroxyphenanthrene (a metabolite of phenanthrene), 2-hydroxyphenanthrene (a metabolite of phenanthrene), 3,4-hydroxyphenanthrene (a metabolite of phenanthrene) and 1-hydroxypyrene (a metabolite of pyrene). To assess the nicotine intake-adjusted urinary biomarkers in smokers and EC users to control for differences in nicotine exposure, Creatinine-adjusted metabolites were further normalized using the molar sum of two key nicotine metabolites (Trans-3-hydroxy cotinine and cotinine) as the total nicotine equivalent (TNE).

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using R V 4.5.1 software [46]. Descriptive statistics for continuous variables are presented as mean and standard deviation, while categorical variables are presented as frequency and percentage.

K-means clustering analysis was used to divide the data into two groups, including faster and slower mAge groups.

We primarily used XGBoost as the primary exploratory feature selection method to identify bacterial species associated with mAge-based older and younger groups in a high-dimensional setting [47]. To prevent information leakage and artificially inflated performance during feature selection, we utilized the nested leave-one-out cross-validation (LOOCV) framework. In each LOOCV iteration, one sample was held out as the test set, while the remaining samples formed the training set. Feature selection was performed exclusively within the training set by fitting the XGBoost model and extracting the top-ranked microbial species based on the Gain index [48]. A classifier was then re-fit using only the selected features within the same training fold, and an out-of-sample probability for the held-out test sample was generated. This procedure was repeated across all folds, and all selected bacterial species were saved, and the area under the curve (AUC) was computed from the concatenated out-of-sample predictions. No information from the test subject, including feature importance or selected features, was used during training. To assess the robustness of feature selection, we performed a multi-seed stability analysis by repeating the entire nested LOOCV procedure across 100 random seeds. Feature stability was quantified as the frequency with which each microbial species was selected across seeds and LOOCV folds, allowing us to distinguish consistently selected bacterial species from those sensitive to random initialization. This approach provides an empirical measure of selection robustness under extremely small sample sizes.

To evaluate whether the bacterial species identified by XGBoost were robust to modeling assumptions, we benchmarked XGBoost against several simpler, alternative classifiers. Specifically, we applied (i) logistic regression with L1 regularization (LASSO), (ii) random forest, and (iii) support vector machines (SVM), each implemented within the same nested LOOCV and multi-seed repetition framework. For each method, feature selection was performed exclusively within the training folds, and species importance was summarized across folds and random seeds using frequency-based stability metrics: non-zero coefficients (LASSO), high variable importance (random forest), or linear classifier weights (SVM) [49].

The selected bacterial species were associated with the mAge acceleration group using the logistic regression model. One-way ANOVA (analysis of variance) was used to associate the identified bacterial species with the smoking group.

The relationships between TNE-adjusted VOCs and PAHs and the identified bacteria were performed using Pearson’s correlation model and further using a multiple regression model after adjusting for chronological age, gender, and race. Values of FDR < 0.1 were considered statistically significant. The flow scheme of the modeling and analysis process is provided in Fig. 1.

Results

Characteristics of study participants

Table 1 summarizes the characteristics of the 26 study participants. The median age was 26.8 years, and the median body mass index (BMI) was 25.9 kg/m2. Overall, 27% were women, and 85% identified as white. The cohort included seven smokers (SM), nine electronic cigarette (EC) users, and ten never-smokers (NS). The median duration of smoking among SM was 9.3 years, while EC users reported a median of 3 years of EC use.

Across all participants, the median mAge estimates were 25.9 years for Horvath-mAge, 40.6 years for Grim-mAge, 21.1 years for Pheno-mAge, and 6.0 for TL-mAge (Table 1). For mAge acceleration (mAA, indicating slower or faster mAge), both EC users and SM exhibited positive median values for Grim-mAA (0.6 and 1.8, respectively) and Pheno-mAA (0.7 and 1.7), suggesting faster epigenetic aging than expected. In contrast, NS showed negative median values for both Grim-mAA (–1.5) and Pheno-mAA (–1.9), indicating decelerated biological aging. TL-mAA values showed a similar group-wise pattern, while the direction of mAA was reversed, with NS having positive values and EC users and smokers showing negative values.

Clustering faster or slower lung mAge groups

Using the simple k-means clustering, individuals were clustered into faster or slower mAge groups for each of four mAA markers (Horvath-mAA, Grim-mAA, Pheno-mAA, TL-mAA. Overall, two clustered groups by each mAA marker clearly differ between those with faster or slower mAge groups, indicating a well-performed clustering (Fig. 2A–D, Table 2). An enrichment analysis of smoking status by two clusters revealed enriched SM in the faster mAA groups (Fig. 2E–H), with a significantly different enrichment pattern for Grim-mAA (P = 0.004).

Identification of lung bacterial species that predict faster or slower lung mAge groups

Supplementary Tables 3–6 summarize feature selection results using XGBoost with a nested LOOCV procedure across 100 random seeds, resulting in the selection of the top two bacterial species. The identified bacterial species (n = 9–20) from each mAA marker are largely unique to each mAA marker, with little overlap (Supplementary Fig. 1). Of them, Alistipes finegoldii was shared between TL-mAA and Grim-AA, while Candidatus Walczuchella monophlebidarum was identified from both Pheno-mAA and Horvath-mAA. We further confirmed the findings from XGBoost through alternative and similar classifications, using LASSO, random forest, and SVM, each implemented within the same nested LOOCV and multi-seed repetition framework (Supplementary Tables 3–6). Further, logistic regression models with two selected bacterial species on each of mAA showed significant associations (Table 3). Further, each pair of bacterial species selected from the ML algorithm to predict each mAA was also found to be statistically significant with each of the mAA (P = 0.016–0.049), except for Bdellovibrio bacteriovorus (P = 0.062) for Horvath that had borderline significance. The bacterial species predicted for Grim-mAA (Alistipes finegoldii and Arachidicoccus sp. BS20) and DNAmTL-mAA (Burkholderia lata and Bacteroides zoogleoformans) showed negative associations, whereas those predicted for Horvath-mAA (Chamaesiphon minutus and Bdellovibrio bacteriovorus) and Pheno-mAA (Aeromonas sp. ASNIH7 and Candidatus Walczuchella monophlebidarum) exhibited positive associations.

Discussion

This study, for the first time, reports the potential associations of lung microbiome with biological aging, as assessed by epigenetic clocks, using machine learning approaches. We further reveal the associations between host exposure, specifically smoking/EC-related toxicants, and epigenetic age acceleration (mAA)-related bacterial species.

The microbiome is increasingly recognized as a key modulator of human health and diseases [50–52]. Although the lungs were historically considered sterile [53], advanced sequencing technologies have identified diverse microbial communities within the lower respiratory tract, which play important roles in maintaining pulmonary homeostasis and preventing diseases [5–7, 54]. While an earlier study hypothesized potential contamination of bacteria from the passage of the bronchoscope through the oral cavity to the lungs, accumulating evidence, including our previous publication, supports the presence of a distinct microbial community [7, 55]. Furthermore, epigenetic changes can alter the balance of lung microbiomes, potentially driving dysbiosis and contributing to the development and progression of pulmonary diseases [56, 57]. This indicates the complex and bidirectional interactions between the epigenome and the microbiome [58].

To identify bacterial species linked to lung biological aging, we first estimated four epigenetic clocks, including Horvath (for chronological age), Grim (for lifespan and mortality), Pheno (for healthspan), and DNAmTL (for telomere lengths), rather than relying on a single epigenetic clock. It is because each clock was developed using a different set of methylation sites and captures unique aspects of epigenetic aging.

Among the bacterial species identified as predictors of mAA markers, several are reported to be involved in human health, not limited to the lungs. Bdellovibrio bacteriovorus (Horvath-mAA predictor) is known for its antimicrobial properties and protective effects in systemic infections and cystic fibrosis [59–61]. For Grim-mAA, Alistipes finegoldii belongs to the phylum Bacteroidetes, commonly found in healthy individuals [62]. While Alistipes finegoldii has been linked to both protective (e.g., cardiovascular and liver diseases) [63–65] and pathogenic roles (e.g., colorectal cancer) [66, 67], it has not been reported in the lungs. Aeromonas sp. ASNIH7 (Pheno-mAA predictor) is a known pathogen linked to infections of the skin, muscles, and gastrointestinal tract [68, 69]. For the DNAmTL, Burkholderia lata has been associated with various infections, including in cystic fibrosis patients [70], while Bacteroides zoogleoformans has been linked to gut dysbiosis, abscess formation, and bacteremia [71]. While further studies are required to understand definitive biological mechanisms of epigenetic aging-related bacterial species in lung diseases, the identified bacterial species may have potential promise for future translational applications, including lung aging-related risk stratification, early disease detection, or targeted interventions of pulmonary diseases.

Smoking is considered a significant modifier of microbiome compositions, reducing beneficial bacteria and promoting pathogenic taxa, potentially increasing disease risk [53]. Our earlier work in the lungs and oral cavities showed an overall lower abundance of smoking-related bacterial species in SM compared to NS or EC users of healthy individuals [7]. In line with those results, in the current study, bacterial species predictive of Grim-mAA, Alistipes finegoldii, and Arachidicoccus sp. BS20 was identified to have a significantly lower abundance in SM compared to NS. EC users had an intermediate level of Alistipes finegoldii between SM and NS, with borderline significance compared to NS. Since most of the EC users are former smokers, this bacterium may reflect long-lasting smoking-specific effects or be altered by both smoking and EC exposure, with less impact from EC use.

Importantly, cigarette smoke contains numerous toxicants harmful to lung health [72]. While EC liquids and aerosols have lower levels, they still include these compounds, including VOCs and PAH [73]. Our recent study revealed several VOCs to be significantly associated with lung epigenetic aging [74], but underlying biological mediators remain unknown. Given the emerging role of the microbiome as a key modulator of biological aging [75, 76], for the first time, the current study assessed smoking- and EC-related, toxicant-specific bacterial species associated with epigenetic aging in the lower respiratory tract. Using nicotine-intake adjusted urinary metabolites of VOCs in SM and EC, even after adjusting for demographic covariates, we found significantly negative associations between Grim-mAA related Arachidicoccus sp. BS20 and four metabolites of VOCs or PAH (acrylonitrile [CNEMA] [77, 78], 1,3-butadiene [MHBMA1&2] [79, 80], benzene [PMA] [81, 82], and fluorene [1-hydroxyfluorene] [12, 83, 84]) that are known to be related to reduced pulmonary function and cause airway irritation. Notably, the International Agency for Research on Cancer (IARC) classified acrylonitrile [85], 1,3-butadiene [86], and benzene [87] as Group 1 carcinogens. Consistent with previous studies [73, 88–91], all forementioned metabolites are significantly higher in SM compared to NS (Supplementary Table 7). Also, EC users showed significantly higher levels of metabolites of acrylonitrile, 1,3-butadiene, and fluorene [83, 84] than NS. These findings suggest that Grim-mAA-associated Arachidicoccus sp. BS20 may be altered by smoking- and EC-related VOCs and PAH, potentially contributing to lung aging. The use of metatranscriptomics in our study, which identifies transcriptionally active (live) bacterial species, further supports the relevance of these microbial markers for personalized risk stratification in smokers and EC users.

It is important to note the limitations of our study. The cross-sectional design limits the ability to assess the dynamic nature of lung microbial communities and biological aging over time, which restricts our ability to draw causal conclusions from the findings. Also, there may be unmeasured covariates (e.g., occupation, ambient and indoor air pollution, social and health-related factors, other respiratory exposure, and smoking- or EC–related factors such as device type or prior smoking) that could influence the observed associations. Additionally, the small sample size and subgroup imbalance may constrain the generalizability of our findings, underscoring the need for larger, longitudinal studies that include a wide range of ages, diverse races/ethnicities, and balanced sexes. Although the machine learning approach used in this study is novel, it may have a risk of overfitting in this approach with high-dimensional features. Nevertheless, through benchmarking and stability analyses, we found that the main results were generally consistent, supporting the robustness of our findings. Because microbes from the oral cavity may contribute to the lung microbiome, future studies that include both oral and lower airway samples may help our understanding of how microbial transfer or shared communities are associated with biological aging.

Despite these limitations, a key strength of this study is its focus on the lungs, which are the target organ of pulmonary diseases, to first address the relationships between lung microbiome and lung aging. Using the metatranscriptomics approach, we identified live bacterial species, providing species-level resolution beyond that of conventional DNA sequencing (i.e., 16S rRNA gene sequencing), which only offers genus-level resolution. Importantly, we also further assessed smoking- and EC-related exposure biomarkers with epigenetic aging-related bacterial species. In this study, the analytic framework intentionally combines nonlinear and linear approaches because they serve different purposes. The ML methods allow for the detection of potentially nonlinear, high-dimensional microbial signatures without imposing linear assumptions. In contrast, logistic regression provides an interpretable linear estimate of association for follow-up inference. PCoA was used for visualization and did not contribute to model-based inference. Although these methods rely on different statistical assumptions, their roles in the data analytical framework are distinct and complementary, and together they provide a coherent strategy for identifying, validating, and interpreting microbial markers of lung epigenetic aging.

Conclusion

In conclusion, our study provides the first evidence on an association between transcriptionally active lung bacterial species and epigenetic aging in the lungs of healthy individuals. The findings further indicate that exposure to smoking- and EC-related VOCs and PAHs may alter specific lung microbial profiles associated with lung aging. Based on the findings of this study, we support longitudinal studies that follow individuals over time, which could help determine whether changes in lung microbiome composition can lead to faster or slower epigenetic aging. Experimental studies, including in vitro or animal models, may also help examine how selected bacteria or microbial communities can impact pathways related to respiratory diseases, such as inflammation, oxidative stress, or other biological processes relevant to lung aging and potentially lung disease. In addition, mechanistic studies are needed to understand the causal relationships between smoking- and EC-related bacterial species and epigenetic aging, and to evaluate whether the potential microbiome-targeted interventions could help mitigate pulmonary aging and their roles in pulmonary diseases.
