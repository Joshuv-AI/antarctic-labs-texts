Impact of ball weight on elbow kinetics and kinematics in team handball overhead throws
Authors: Yasin Yuzbasioglu, Nihat Özgören, M. Zeki Ozkol
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
PurposeOverhead throwing is a dynamic motion critical for team handball performance. The purpose of this study was to compare elbow joint kinetics and kinematics during the acceleration phase (AP) via different weighted balls.MethodsEleven active male handball players (aged 21.36 ± 4.65 years), free from musculoskeletal pain and without any injuries, performed three trials with three different weighted balls in random order: a regular ball (RB: 427 g), a light ball (LB: 360 g) and a heavy ball (HB: 1000 g). The kinematic data were collected in an indoor laboratory with an 8-camera infrared motion capture system. A ten-segment mechanical model of the upper extremity was built via Simscape libraries in MATLAB/Simulink to calculate joint torques via inverse dynamics simulations. Kinematic and kinetic parameters were compared via one-way repeated-measures ANOVA.ResultsOur results revealed that the ball release velocity decreases as the weight increases (p < 0.001). Increasing the ball weight resulted in a 12.5% decrease in the release velocity of HB compared with that of LB. The change in the ball release velocity was probably a result of the significant differences in elbow extension velocity and torque.ConclusionThe findings of this study indicate that using lighter balls may reduce stress on the elbow during overhead throwing activities, while heavier balls significantly increase elbow extension torque. These findings have direct implications for designing training programs aimed at performance enhancement and injury prevention.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13102-025-01500-z.

Introduction

Overhead throwing is a dynamic and high-velocity motion that is a crucial performance parameter in team handball. This complex motion requires an understanding of elbow kinematics and kinetics, particularly the stresses that occur during the acceleration and deceleration phases of the throw [1, 2].

The elbow joint plays a critical role in force generation and transfer, which is essential for achieving optimal throwing performance [3, 4]. Studies have shown that overhead-throwing athletes, including those in sports such as team handball, baseball, and javelin, experience significant forces on the shoulder and elbow joints, increasing their susceptibility to injury due to the repetitive nature of the movement [5, 6]. Considering the repetitive nature of throwing in team handball, understanding the biomechanical effects of overhead throwing on the elbow joint is critical for optimising performance and minimising injury risk [7, 8].

As a key training principle for improving throwing performance, athletes must adapt to specific forms of overload (e.g., general strength training, resistance band training, and throwing with overweight and underweight balls).

One common training approach involves weighted balls, which are believed to enhance strength, velocity, and neuromuscular coordination [9–11]. The effects of varying ball weights on elbow joint kinetics and kinematics during overhead throwing remain a topic of ongoing research. Although several studies have investigated weighted ball training, mainly in baseball [2, 9, 12–14], a specific research gap exists regarding detailed kinetic analyses, such as elbow torque profiles throughout the throwing motion, in team handball players. The first principle involves using overweighted balls during training to create an overload force. Using a weighted ball in training improves the ball’s release velocity without any throwing-related injuries [13, 15, 16]. However, research indicates that heavier balls may lead to increased joint stress, potentially increasing the risk of injuries such as medial collateral ligament tears, which are often the result of repetitive microtrauma during throwing activities [14, 17–19]. The second training principle involves underweighted balls, focusing on achieving an overload of velocity [12]. Conversely, lighter balls have been suggested to improve throwing velocity without the risk of joint injury, as they allow for better kinematic patterns and reduce the overall load on the elbow [20–22]. While using weighted balls in overhead throwing training presents opportunities for performance enhancement, it also poses risks that must be carefully managed. Van den Tillaar and Ettema reported that changing ball weights, such as being 20% lighter and 20% heavier than a regular ball, influenced the kinematics of throwing in experienced female handball players [23]. Consequently, due to the kinematic changes, differences in the ball release velocity occurred.

The current literature highlights the need for further investigation into the effects of different ball weights on elbow joint mechanics, particularly in team handball [6, 23–25]. Understanding these dynamics is essential for developing evidence-based training practices that optimise performance while minimising injury risk. It was hypothesised that increased ball weight would result in a decreased ball release velocity, driven by alterations in elbow joint angular velocities and torques, and an increase in elbow torques. From this perspective, this study aimed to investigate elbow joint kinetics and kinematics during the acceleration phase (AP) using different weighted balls.

Methods

Participants

Eleven male active national league handball players (aged 21.36 ± 4.65 years, body weight: 77.92 ± 10.75 kg, height: 180.19 ± 8.98 m), free of musculoskeletal pain and without injuries, participated in the study. The participants were informed about the study and signed an informed consent form before the experiments. The study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the university ethics committee (20-2.1T/27).

Experimental setup

A motion capture system (Vicon Ltd., Bilston, England) consisting of 8 near-infrared optical cameras (model: Bonito) was used to capture the overhead throwing motions of the participants. The optical cameras were connected to the laptop computer via two 8-port gigabit PoE network switches (TrendNet, TPE-80WS, USA). The cameras were placed at proper heights via tripods around the motion field. A rectangular Table 0.80 m from the floor was placed in the centre of the motion capture area (Fig. 1). A seat removed from a chair was mounted on one of the shorter sides of the table, using screws and nuts, on its four corners. A 1.5-meter-long strap was passed under the seat, with both ends remaining on the sides to ensure that the participants were in place during the throwing motion.

Data collection

The cameras and floor plane (table surface) were calibrated sequentially via the Blade software’s (version 2.6.1) calibration tool. The calibration wand was placed in the middle of the table, and the origin of the motion capture area was set. A total of 24 markers were placed at the anatomical landmarks of each participant prior to the motion capture session. One additional marker was placed on the ball to track the ball’s trajectory during motion. The participants sat on the seat with their thighs strapped to prevent lower-extremity movement during overhead throwing and standardise the motion. Since the throws were made in a sitting position, force production from the lower extremity was restricted, allowing the upper extremity to play a more dominant role, with a focus on its mechanics. This experimental design was intentionally chosen to isolate the mechanics of the upper extremity and restrict force variables from the lower extremity, allowing for a more controlled analysis of the direct effect of the ball weight on the elbow joint. The range of motion data for each participant was captured before the session. After a 15-minute general warm-up that included jogging and throwing drills for the throwing arm, throwing performance was assessed in a sitting throw situation, where an overarm throw was made towards a 1.80 m-tall catcher positioned 7 m away. The participants performed three trials with each ball in a random order: the regular ball (RB: 427 g), the beach handball ball (LB: 360 g) and the weighted pilates ball (HB: 1000 g). Participants were asked to perform overhead throws with their dominant arm at maximum voluntary speed toward the target. Between each throw, approximately 1 min of rest was allowed to avoid fatigue. The overhead throwing motion of the participants was recorded via Blade software at a frame rate of 240 Hz. Finally, the participants’ weight and height were measured using a scale (Tanita BC 418) and a stadiometer (Seca). The circumference of the limbs was measured for further use in the computer simulation model.

Data processing

The full-body skeleton labelling template of the Blade software was customised, and a 24-marker, 10-segment upper-extremity labelling template was created. The template was saved as a .vst (Vicon skeleton template) file (Fig. 2). Four additional markers were used for the calibration of the skeleton template using the range of motion data, which were then removed during the throwing motions. The template comprised the pelvis, abdomen, thorax, head, clavicles (left and right), upper arms (left and right), and forearms (left and right). The motions were solved via the inverse kinematics solver in the Blade software to obtain upper-body kinematics. The 3D trajectories of the labelled markers and Euler angles of the joints were exported as .trc (Track Row Column) and .bvh (Biovision Hierarchy) files, respectively.

The raw marker trajectories and joint angles were filtered using a zero-phase, 2nd-order Butterworth low-pass digital filter with a cut-off frequency of 16 Hz to eliminate fluctuations. The joint angular velocity and angular accelerations were calculated from filtered angular displacement data.

Simulation model

A ten-segment mechanical model of the upper extremity was built (Fig. 4) via Simscape libraries in Simulink (version 10.3). The table was visualised using an Infinite Plane block measuring 1.4 m long and 0.8 m wide. The model’s root is the pelvis bone, which is connected to the world frame with a bushing joint with six degrees of freedom (three rotational and three translational). Other segments within the body were connected with a Gimbal joint with three rotational degrees of freedom. The ball was a spherical solid connected to the wrist via a Cartesian joint with three translational degrees of freedom. The ball’s mass varied over time and was 0 kg after the ball was released. Therefore, the inverse dynamics calculations do not consider the ball’s mass after the release frame. The segments were visualised as rigid rectangular prisms. The segment length values were extracted from the subject-specific scaled skeleton .vsk files. The mass, mass centre location, and segment inertia were calculated via the Chandler parameters [26]. The mean total mass of the model was 551.60 ± 71.65 N, corresponding to 56.28 ± 7.31 kg. The geometry of the pelvis was visualised as an extruded solid, a four-cornered rigid body, defined via the 3D trajectories of the four markers attached to the posterior and anterior superior iliac spines.

Prediction of ground reaction forces and inverse dynamics simulation

Since no force platform was used on the seating surface, the ground reaction forces (Fgr) acting on the pelvis had to be known to calculate joint moments via the inverse dynamics method. Fgr was predicted using the top-down method [27, 28], which involves calculating the linear acceleration vectors (a) of the segment centre of mass. In the first simulation, the model was run with the kinematic data, and the linear acceleration vectors of the centre of mass of the segments were sensed via a transform sensor. Then, following the top-down method (Eq. 2) and knowing the segment masses (m), Fgr was calculated. After calculating Fgr, the inverse dynamic simulation was performed. It was assumed that Fgr acts at the centre of mass of the pelvis, and the three components (Fx, Fy, Fz) of Fgr were given as inputs via the external force and torque block in Simulink. A total of 99 inverse dynamics simulations were performed via the ode1be (backwards Euler) solver of Simulink with a fixed step size of 0.0042 s (same as the sampling frequency of motion capture). The outputs of the inverse dynamic simulations were joint moments and the ball velocity. A flowchart of the aforementioned simulation procedures is shown in Fig. 4.

Statistical analysis

All the statistical analyses were performed via the Statistics and Machine Learning Toolbox (version 23.2) in MATLAB. The computed joint angles and angular velocities, joint moments, and ball release velocity were time-normalised by resampling the data to compare the results. The time-normalised variables contained 101 samples (0% – 100% time in increments of one). The joint moments were then normalised by the participant’s weight. The normality of all the data was checked via the Shapiro‒Wilk test. If normality was achieved, a one-way repeated-measures ANOVA test was performed. Then, pairwise comparisons were made via Tukey’s HSD test to detect significant differences. If normality was not achieved, the Friedman and Wilcoxon signed-rank tests with a Bonferroni adjustment were used to examine the differences at p ≤ 0.05.

Since we did not measure the Fgr experimentally, we cannot compare it to the real data. So instead of validating against force plate data, we validated against physical consistency with whole-body dynamics, following Newtonian mechanics. For the whole human body considered as a single system, the actual acceleration of the human’s centre of mass (aCoM) times the mass of the system (m) should be equal to the calculated Fgr at every time step. However, the estimated ground reaction forces via the top-down method will never perfectly satisfy the equation (Eq. 3), a residual force (rF) will be obtained. Results

The mean values and standard deviations of the selected parameters are given in the Supplementary Material File Table 1. The average duration of the AP was 0.23 ± 0.06 s for the 360 g ball, 0.24 ± 0.09 s for the 427 g ball, and 0.25 ± 0.08 s for the 1000 g ball. There was no statistically significant difference in the duration of the AP among the 360, 427, and 1000 g throws (χ²(2) = 4.23, p = 0.12, Kendall’s W = 0.06).

The change in ball velocity throughout the AP is presented in Fig. 5 and Supplementary Material File Fig. 1. Discussion

The current study investigated the impact of varying ball weights on elbow kinematics and kinetics during the overhand throwing motion of team handball players. The results demonstrated that ball weight influenced throwing mechanics, particularly elbow joint kinetics and kinematics. Additionally, increasing the ball weight significantly reduced the ball release velocity. These findings are consistent with previous studies investigating the effects of ball weight on hand throwing [21, 23, 29, 30].

The mean ball release velocity obtained with the RB method was lower (13.49 ± 1.89 m/s) than the velocities obtained in earlier studies (22–26 m/s) on experienced male handball players during competitions [31–33]. Higher throwing velocities are achieved in throws made while standing and running. This is due to the increased acceleration of the pelvis and torso during leg lifting [34, 35]. Overhead throwing from a seated position eliminates the kinetic chain contribution from the legs and trunk, turning the movement into an isolated upper-limb task. In our study, the ball release velocity may have been lower because participants sat at the table and used only their upper bodies to generate force. This eliminates the significant contribution of lower-extremity power to ball speed in normal throwing technique; therefore, the generalizability of the findings to overhead throws performed from a standing position should be evaluated in this context. Our results indicate that using LB results in a 4.2% increase in ball release velocity compared with throwing with RB. Conversely, compared with RB, HB ball release decreased by 8.7%. This observation aligns with previously published research on weighted balls [23]. The decrease in the ball release velocity can be explained by changes in the kinematics and kinetics of the upper limb, particularly at the elbow joint. Van den Tillaar and Ettema reported that elbow and shoulder joint moments were the main contributors to the ball release velocity [36].

Our findings indicate that ball weight significantly affects throwing kinematics, particularly in terms of joint angular moments. Previous studies reported that elbow extension velocity decreased as ball weight increased, highlighting the relationship between load and throwing mechanics [20–22, 34, 37, 38]. Our results supported this, with the HB exhibiting significantly lower elbow extension velocities than the LB. The reduced elbow extension velocity for a heavier ball was likely due to the increased muscular demand required to accelerate the ball. Although the effect of the ball weight on the joint angles was minimal, the joint angular velocities demonstrated a marked relationship with the ball weight. The peak elbow flexion velocity occurred earlier with LB than with RB and HB, whereas the maximal elbow extension velocity was consistently achieved at ball release. This timing shift is due to mechanical and neural constraints from the load-velocity relationship. Mechanically, lighter loads with lower inertia enable rapid initial acceleration, reaching peak velocity early in the concentric phase [39]. Neurally, it reflects modulation of the triphasic muscle activation pattern. The earlier peak elbow flexion velocity with the LB may allow for faster arm acceleration and quicker neuromuscular activation, thus increasing throwing velocity. This timing difference could be considered in the design of drills designed to develop speed and explosiveness in training programs. Our study demonstrated that the LB, which models this faster, earlier flexion velocity, also produced significantly lower elbow extension torque than the HB. Considering these two findings together, LB training may be an ideal method for developing neuromuscular adaptations to increase throwing speed in athletes without increasing joint torque and stress. Several studies highlight that the timing and nature of joint velocities contribute to overall throwing effectiveness and injury risk [40, 41]. The elbow extension velocity during the acceleration phase directly affects the ball’s release velocity [21, 23, 38]. The reduction in elbow extension velocity as the ball weight increases is likely due to the greater elbow extension torque required to accelerate the ball and overcome the heavier ball’s greater inertia. This relationship is consistent with the biomechanics discussed in the relevant studies, emphasising the importance of analysing kinematics concerning interthrow variability and torque application [40, 42].

We also found that the peak elbow extension torque was significantly greater with the HB throw than with the LB throw. Previous studies have reported increased joint moments with increasing ball weight during overhead throwing [2, 21]. Van Muijen and colleagues noted that torque-time parameters improved after 8 weeks of training with a heavy ball [43]. These findings suggest that the effect of heavy ball throwing on throwing muscle improvement. On the other hand, light ball training improved the ball’s release velocity [21, 22, 43]. Elevated elbow extension torque may increase the risk of elbow injuries, particularly among young athletes still developing their throwing mechanics [44]. In contrast to previous research, there were no significant differences in elbow flexion torque based on ball weight [2], indicating that elbow flexors contribute similarly regardless of ball mass. The differences between the baseball and handball throwing styles, as well as the model used for inverse dynamics analysis, may influence the estimated torque magnitudes.

Finally, varus torque at the elbow enhances elbow strength and ensures safety [45]. Our findings revealed that the mean varus torque was 44% greater with the heavy ball (HB: 0.11 ± 0.22 Nm/kg) than with the regular ball (RB: 0.07 ± 0.09 Nm/kg; p > 0.05). Although no statistically significant difference was found, a significant trend toward increased elbow varus torque was observed with heavy ball use. This phenomenon should be investigated in larger-sample studies. Slowik et al. (2019) reported a positive correlation between higher ball velocity and elbow varus torque [46]. However, they also indicated that thrower-specific characteristics limit the generalisability of the results. Therefore, it is conceivable that differences in team handball and baseball pitching may also limit generalisability. It has also been shown that training with weighted balls enhances throwing speed; however, it also increases elbow varus torque, raising concerns about injury risk [14]. Anz et al. noted a significantly higher rate of elbow injury in professional throwers with increased elbow varus torque over 3 seasons [47]. Using different weighted balls in training may effectively increase throwing velocity and prevent injury by allowing adaptation to increased varus torque. In this study, the difference in varus torque exhibited the same trend between the throws during AP. Our findings align with the conclusion of Moore et al. that as the weight of the baseball increases, the elbow varus torque increases, directly influencing the mechanics of the throw [38]. We believe that ball weight, ball release velocity, and elbow varus torque are interrelated. Accordingly, this modifiable factor may be a reasonable target for preventing elbow injuries. Optimised training with weighted balls may adapt to higher elbow varus torque without the risk of injury. Consequently, throwing with the RB may affect ball velocity due to this adaptation. In summary, the relationship between the ball weight and throwing kinematics is versatile. Specifically, joint velocities and torques are influenced by ball weight, which affects performance metrics like ball release velocity and potentially increases the risk of injury during throwing activities.

The present study has several limitations. No control group was included in the study because the main question was whether ball weight affected the elbow. First, the sample excluded youth athletes and left-handed players, which limits its applicability to all team handball players. Additionally, only male athletes were included in the study. Due to differences in strength and anthropometric parameters between male and female athletes, the generalizability of these findings to female handball players is limited. Second, the ball weight conditions were not highly varied. Finally, kinematic and kinetic measurements were based on markers placed on the skin, creating a soft tissue artefact commonly found in marker-based motion capture systems, which is considered the gold standard for accurate sports biomechanics measurements.

In top-down or kinematic-driven inverse dynamics, estimating ground reaction forces inherently introduces residual forces due to discrepancies between whole-body kinematics and the assumed external force environment. Previous studies have shown that such residuals commonly range between 10 and 20% of body weight [48], even in well-controlled gait and functional-task analyses, primarily due to uncertainties in segment inertial parameters, errors in centre of mass accelerations, and imperfect kinematic data [49, 50]. These residuals arise from several sources, including segment mass distribution errors, marker noise, joint-centre mislocation, and filtering choices, all of which directly influence the dynamic consistency of the Fgr. In this study, we further assumed that the total reaction forces acted as a single resultant vector applied at the pelvis centre of mass. This simplification neglects the true spatial distribution of contact forces and may propagate inaccuracies into joint-torque calculations. The interpretation of our findings should therefore consider these modelling assumptions and their associated residual dynamics. Future work could integrate pressure-mapping systems or adopt a multicontact approach, both of which have been shown to improve the physiological realism of force estimation and reduce residual forces in kinematic-driven simulations.

In conclusion, the results of the present study suggest that throwing with underweighted balls increases throwing velocity, consistent with earlier findings. Throwing with weighted balls overhead can affect the kinetics and kinematics of training, potentially affecting results. Although using weighted balls during training may improve athletes’ physical attributes, especially strength and velocity, a cautious approach to their use is necessary to reduce the risk of joint overuse injuries. Combining training routines that utilise both heavy and light balls has been thought to improve performance and maintain elbow joint health. This method is considered to contribute to the long-term sustainability of an athletic career. If the goal is to increase the ball velocity, it may be recommended to use 15–20% lighter balls in training programs; if the goal is to increase the functional strength of the shooting muscles, it may be recommended to use heavy balls periodically while monitoring the stress on the elbow and not overdoing it. As a practical suggestion, coaches can incorporate weighted ball training into their programs by using low-volume, high-quality repetitions, such as 1–2 sessions per week with 10–15 throws per session. The athlete’s fatigue level and deterioration in technique should be closely monitored, and weight increases should be made gradually.
