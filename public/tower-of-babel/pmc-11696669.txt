Cultural adaptation, validation and standardization of the developmental screening tools ASQ:SE-2 in Iranian children
Authors: Ghazal Shariatpanahi, Simin Khayatzadeh Kakhki, Paniz Pourpashang, Parisa Ashournia, Malihe Rahimi, Masoumeh Ghasempour, Niloufar Ghanbari
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
The Ages and Stages Questionnaires: Social-Emotional, Second Edition (ASQ) is a parental-report tool designed to assess the social-emotional development of young children. Its adaptation and validation in diverse cultural contexts are vital for its effective application. This study aimed to culturally adapt, validate, and standardize the ASQ for Iranian children. A cross-sectional study involving a random sample of Iranian children aged 1–66 months was conducted. Validity and cultural adaptation were assessed through expert panel evaluations, while nationwide sampling ensured reliability and validity. Statistical analyses included Cronbach's alpha for internal consistency and the determination of score ranges defining monitoring zones and cut-off points. Face and content validity, along with cultural relevance, were confirmed by 51 experts. Cronbach's alpha ranged from 0.655 to 0.789, indicating acceptable reliability across age groups. The prevalence of social-emotional developmental delays (scores above the cut-off) was 22.8 %, with 7 % of children falling within the monitoring zone. The highest rate of delays was in the 12-month age group (28.39 %), while the lowest was in the 2-month group (17.12 %). The culturally adapted Persian ASQ provides a valuable tool for assessing social-emotional development in Iranian children.

Introduction

Screening for developmental disorders is a process through which children suspected of experiencing developmental delays or disabilities, particularly in their early and subtle stages, are identified for further specialized diagnostic tests [1]. Developmental screening provides specialists and families with valuable insights into a child's developmental stages and future expectations [2]. The primary benefit of developmental screening lies in its ability to detect developmental disorders early, enabling the implementation of intervention programs that support the child's development [3].

In recent years, there has been a growing global interest in developing and utilizing questionnaires based solely on parental reports to assess the developmental status of children [4,5]. These tools enhance the accuracy of developmental evaluations by capturing a broader range of behaviors within the child's home environment. An additional advantage of parental-report-based tests is their flexibility and ease of implementation [[6], [7], [8]].

The significance of social-emotional competence in child development has been widely recognized. Social-emotional competence refers to a child's ability to build secure relationships with adults and peers, manage and express emotions in socially and culturally appropriate ways, and engage with their environment for learning and exploration [[9], [10], [11]]. Social-emotional competence encompasses various facets, such as emotional regulation, empathy, perspective-taking, attachment, and inhibitory control. Social competence focuses on behaviors that facilitate positive interactions, while emotional competence involves the effective management of emotions to achieve personal goals [[12], [13], [14], [15]].

Children's mental health, which is closely tied to their social-emotional well-being, has become a major public health concern in many countries, including Iran. Early childhood mental health refers to the social, emotional, and behavioral well-being of children from birth to six years old and is essential for lifelong development [16,17]. The failure to identify mental health issues during early childhood can lead to long-term consequences, such as anxiety, depression, and behavioral disorders, which may persist into adolescence and adulthood. In Iran, the growing concern for children's mental health is influenced by factors such as family dynamics, socio-economic challenges, and cultural pressures [18].

The integration of developmental and social-emotional health services into primary healthcare systems highlights the importance of identifying children at risk for mental health issues and providing timely interventions.

Research findings suggest that social-emotional competence plays a critical role in developing developmental skills and school readiness in young children [19,20]. It is a foundational skill for academic performance and lifelong well-being [21]. Conversely, a lack of social-emotional competence or challenges in this area during early childhood can lead to significant and lasting adverse effects, such as increased aggression, anxiety, social withdrawal, and disruptive behaviors [22]. Early identification of developmental delays allows for timely interventions, which can improve the developmental trajectories of affected children [23,24]. These proactive measures also reduce the need for costly remedial services in the future, promoting long-term success and well-being [25,26].

In Iran, the second version of ASQ and also the third version has been translated into Persian, localized, and standardized for more than 15 years ago by the Department of Child Health affiliated to the Ministry of Health, Treatment and Medical Education, University of Social Welfare and Rehabilitation Sciences and the Exceptional Children Education Organization [27,28] and has been used for children developmental screening at PHC level and ASQ-SE is a supplementary tool for evaluating social and emotional development.

Utilizing scientifically validated screening tools to detect social-emotional challenges in their early stages is crucial for facilitating timely interventions. This approach ensures that appropriate support and resources are promptly provided, allowing these issues to be addressed proactively and mitigating potential long-term impacts on children's social and emotional well-being [29]. Numerous assessment tools with exceptional psychometric characteristics have demonstrated efficacy in screening social-emotional development across diverse cultural contexts. Among these, the ASQ consists of a series of parent-completed questionnaires designed for children aged 1–72 months. Its primary goal is to identify potential delays in social-emotional development at the earliest possible stage [30,31]. Renowned for its robust psychometric properties, the ASQ has been extensively utilized in early childhood programs in the United States, including initiatives such as Head Start and Early Head Start [32].

The researchers in this study aimed to localize, validate, and standardize the Persian version of the ASQ for use with Iranian children.

Material and methods

Study population

This study utilized a cross-sectional design to validate and standardize the ASQ questionnaire. The study population consisted of a random sample of Iranian children aged 1–66 months. Both parents were required to have at least an elementary level of education and to provide informed consent for their child's participation. Children with clear developmental delays previously diagnosed by medical professionals or therapists, or those with a history of any type of rehabilitation intervention, were excluded from the study.

Study sample

The study employed two sampling methods. Initially, for the formal and content validity as well as the cultural adaptation of the tool, 51 experts in child health and development—including pediatric specialists, neurologists, developmentalists, psychiatrists, psychologists, occupational therapists, and speech therapists—were selected. These experts assessed the tool's validity, content, and cultural appropriateness.

In the second stage, to evaluate the reliability and validity of the tool, 11,760 Iranian children were selected nationwide. A flowchart illustrating participant recruitment and exclusion is presented in Fig. 1. To facilitate sampling, the provinces were stratified into seven regions. One provincial capital and one additional city from other areas within each region were randomly selected, yielding 14 cities (7 provincial capitals and 7 other cities).Fig. 1Flowchart of participant recruitment and exclusion.Fig. 1

Flowchart of participant recruitment and exclusion.

Within each city, 10 urban and 10 rural clusters were randomly chosen in collaboration with the Iran Statistics Center to meet the desired sample size. In each cluster, a household was randomly designated as the starting point. Sampling then proceeded house-to-house, following detailed instructions provided to interviewers who received prior training. From each cluster, the target was to sample one girl and one boy from each age group, resulting in 21 girls and 21 boys per cluster, or 420 girls and 420 boys per city. This method ensured the required distribution across age and gender groups.

About the instrument: ASQ:SE-2

The ASQ:SE-2 is a standardized tool designed to evaluate the social and emotional development of children aged 1–72 months, facilitating the early identification of social and emotional challenges. The original version of the ASQ:SE-2 was introduced by Squires et al., in 2002, published by Brookes Publishing Company [30]. It was developed as a complementary tool to the Ages and Stages Questionnaire-Third Edition (ASQ-3), which comprehensively screens developmental areas such as communication, gross and fine motor skills, problem-solving abilities, and personal-social skills [33].

In contrast, the ASQ focuses exclusively on assessing social and emotional behaviors. This second edition incorporates significant revisions, including updates to the normative sample, improved psychometric properties, and enhancements to existing items. Additionally, it addresses limitations of the original version by improving its ability to detect autism and other social-emotional delays. The ASQ evaluates seven dimensions of social-emotional development: self-regulation, compliance, communication, adaptive behaviors, autonomy, affect, and interpersonal interaction.

The ASQ comprises nine questionnaires tailored for specific age groups: 2, 6, 12, 18, 24, 30, 36, 48, and 60 months. Each questionnaire can be used for children within its respective age range. The number of questions varies across questionnaires, ranging from 19 items in the 6-month version to 33 items in the 60-month version. Each questionnaire concludes with open-ended questions addressing eating habits, sleep patterns, toileting routines, and parental concerns. Closed-ended questions serve as scoring criteria and offer four response options: Often or always, Sometimes, Rarely, or Never. These items are categorized into seven domains: self-regulation, compliance, adaptive behaviors, autonomy, affect, and interpersonal interaction. Completing a questionnaire typically takes 10–15 min [30].

Translation and re-translation of the tool

Initially, the items of the ASQ:SE-2 were translated from English to Persian by two translators proficient in both Persian and English, as well as knowledgeable in the specialized field of children's development. Subsequently, the translators and the researcher reviewed and compared the translations. Following editing and correction of the translations, they collaboratively reached a consensus to determine the optimal translation for the ASQ:SE-2 items. Subsequently, the final translated text of both instruments underwent re-translation from Persian to English by a proficient translator fluent in both languages, who was independent of the initial two translators and the research team. Following this, the moderator and the research team compared the re-translations with the original English questions in the original version of both tools. Inconsistencies were identified and extracted, and through editing and correcting the translations, a consensus was reached on the final determination of the best translation.

Determination of face and content validity and cultural adaptation of the tool

In the subsequent stage, specialists from various fields (totaling 51 individuals) were purposefully selected to evaluate the tool's face validity, content validity, and cultural adaptation. Face validity focused on ensuring the clarity, comprehensibility, and applicability of the questions. Content validity assessed the thematic and contextual relevance of each item to the tool's purpose, while cultural adaptation verified the absence of cultural discrepancies in the questions' text and content relative to Iranian popular culture.

Feedback and correction suggestions from the experts were thoroughly reviewed, and the research team, led by the moderator, incorporated the recommended revisions into the questionnaire. Following this process, the finalized version of the ASQ questionnaires was prepared for field assessment.

Field assessment

Recognizing the importance of training executive colleagues—including managers of children's groups, experts, and interviewers—at the universities where the program was implemented, clear and comprehensive instructions were developed for implementing and scoring the ASQ questionnaire, as well as for conducting cluster-based community sampling (door-to-door).

In this stage, one provincial center and one city from each of the seven regions of the country were randomly selected for field implementation. The director of the children's department and a children's affairs expert from each selected city were invited to participate in a one-day training workshop held in Tehran. This workshop, conducted with the assistance of the project manager, provided participants with detailed instructions on all steps of the project. After the workshop, group managers and children's experts at the respective universities were tasked with transferring this training to suitable, interested, and experienced interviewers in their cities.

To select the clusters, the moderator and research team, with assistance from the Iranian Statistics Center, randomly identified cluster heads (households) in each city—10 urban and 10 rural clusters per city—across the 14 selected cities. The ASQ questionnaires, which had been translated, were formatted and printed as computerized versions. Educational brochures containing developmental intervention recommendations from the ASQ authors were also translated and printed. Furthermore, software for data collection and recording within the Children's Health Department portal was developed and prepared.

Field implementation then commenced, starting with the 10 urban and 10 rural clusters in each city, and continued until the required sample size was achieved. During the initial household visits, the study's objectives were explained to families, and parental cooperation and consent were obtained. Detailed instructions on completing the questionnaires were provided, and the questionnaires were distributed. Follow-up appointments were scheduled to collect the completed questionnaires and ensure their quality.

Upon completing sampling and data collection in the 14 selected cities, the filled computerized questionnaires were sent to the designated company in Tehran. The data were then reviewed for completeness and entered into an Excel file for further analysis.

Monitoring and quality control of data

To ensure data quality and effective monitoring, the following measures were implemented: 1) Selection of Interviewers: Interviewers were preferably female, in consideration of cultural norms and families' receptiveness during home visits. Candidates were chosen from individuals with prior field interview experience through medical science universities and demonstrated both interest and responsibility in their work. 2) Daily Communication with Surveyors:Trained experts from the respective medical science universities maintained daily contact with active surveyors in the designated cities. This communication clarified the correct starting point for sampling (clusters), guided the continuation of sampling, and reminded surveyors about the proper direction of movement between households. 3) Monitoring Sampling Progress: Once questionnaire administration began, experts at the medical science universities communicated daily with each active interviewer at the city level. This process monitored sampling progress and addressed any issues encountered during implementation. 4) Review of Questionnaires: Academic supervisors conducted thorough checks on all completed questionnaires submitted by interviewers. These checks ensured accurate calculation of the child's age, alignment of the child's age with the correct questionnaire, completeness of responses, absence of scribbles, and precision in scoring. 5)Verification with Families: Experts randomly selected a subset of families whose children were enrolled in the study to verify data accuracy. Verification involved contacting families using the contact details provided in the questionnaire to confirm dates of questionnaire distribution and completion, as well as adherence to implementation instructions by interviewers. 6) Virtual Communication and Problem Resolution: Ongoing virtual communication between pediatric experts and pediatric group managers at the relevant universities allowed for prompt resolution of questions, ambiguities, and challenges faced by field experts. Issues were regularly reported to the research team and project executives, enabling timely responses and solutions. 7) Final Data Review: After submission to the data collection company, the computerized questionnaires underwent further scrutiny by the company's experts. This step ensured completeness and identified any glaring contradictions or issues in the submitted data.

Statistical analysis

The ASQ:SE-2 score range was established by calculating the mean ± standard deviation for each subscale across all ASQ:SE-2 items. Since the total score distribution was not normal in any of the age groups, it was not feasible to utilize the mean and standard deviation to determine the monitoring area and cut-off point. Consequently, the monitoring area was established by identifying the distance from the 65th percentile point (65th percentile) to the cut-off point.

The cut-off point was also calculated based on the median plus half the difference between the first and third quartiles (median + (3rd quartile – 1st quartile)/2). For each item within each subscale, and for all statistical analyses, STATA 14.2 (StataCorp LP, College Station, TX, USA) was employed.

Ethical considerations

This project was reviewed and approved by the Ethics Committee of Tehran University of Medical Sciences (Faculty of Medicine) under the ethics code IR.TUMS.MEDICINE.REC.1398.028. Parents were assured that all information obtained would remain confidential with the project's executives. They were informed about the research objectives and methodology, and written consent was subsequently obtained from them. During the investigation, if a child was identified as having a developmental delay or disorder, they were referred to appropriate treatment and rehabilitation centers. Furthermore, if parents chose not to consent to participate in the research, their decision was respected. Although their child was not included in the study, they were still provided with other necessary services.

Results

Validity, cultural adaptation

In a targeted manner and after conducting a focus group with experts, the questionnaire was assessed for face validity (confirmation that the question text was clear, comprehensible, and applicable), content validity (confirmation of the thematic and content alignment of each item with the tool's purpose), and cultural adaptation (confirmation that the text and content of the questions did not conflict with the popular culture of the Iranian people). After receiving correction comments from all relevant experts, the moderator and research team incorporated the proposed revisions into the questionnaire's text. Thus, the final version of the ASQ:SE-2 questionnaires was prepared for field implementation.

Discussion

According to the viewpoints of 51 experts, the face validity, content validity, and cultural relevance of the Persian versions of both tools were confirmed. Regarding the reliability of the ASQ questionnaires, Cronbach's alpha values ranged from 0.655 to 0.789 across all age groups, indicating acceptable reliability. The overall prevalence of social-emotional developmental delays (scores higher than the cutoff) was 22.8 %, with 7 % of all children scoring in the monitoring zone. The highest rate of social-emotional delays was observed in the 12-month age group (28.39 %), while the lowest rate was observed in the 2-month age group (17.12 %). In terms of internal consistency, Cronbach's alpha values for the ASQ:SE-2 questionnaire ranged from 0.544 to 0.789 across different age groups. While these values indicate acceptable reliability for most age intervals, the alpha for the 18-month age group was notably lower (0.544), suggesting relatively poor internal consistency for that specific age range. This was acknowledged by the expert panel, with the understanding that field implementation would provide further insights into the tool's reliability and that potential adjustments could be made as needed.

The results of the present study indicate that the Persian, culturally adapted versions of the ASQ:SE-2 developmental screening tool exhibit suitable face and content validity for Iranian children. Furthermore, they demonstrate acceptable reliability across most age groups and developmental areas. Numerous studies conducted in various populations of children have consistently confirmed the validity and reliability of this screening test, including its translated and localized versions. In a study conducted across 18 countries in Asia, Africa, Europe, North America, and South America, and reported in 2007, the sensitivity and specificity of this test were reported as 88.0 % and 82.5 %, respectively [34]. It should be noted that good developmental screening tests typically have reliability and specificity ranging from 70 % to 80 % [35].

The implementation of this tool has been approved in various at-risk populations, including premature children, children with a history of open-heart surgery, and children born through in-vitro fertilization [36,37]. Studies have shown a significant correlation between the scores of this test and IQ scores [38]. This assessment employs straightforward language, a comprehensive set of questions, and supplementary visuals, enhancing its clarity and usability. Moreover, it can be completed quickly in just 10 min, is cost-effective, and requires no specialized training [39]. Given the limited availability of reliable developmental screening tools for Iranian children, this study aimed to standardize and validate the ASQ questionnaire for this population. The previous validation of the ASQ by Sajedi et al. [40] affirmed its suitability for screening developmental disorders among children in Tehran. The current investigation confirms that the localized Farsi version of the ASQ developmental screening questionnaire exhibits adequate reliability and validity for use with Iranian children. The utilization of standardized cut-off points derived from this study is recommended for accurate assessment. In another study conducted by our team, researchers evaluated the validity and reliability of the ASQ-3 instrument in the Iranian child population, and the results showed that the Iranian version of the ASQ-3 is valid and reliable [41].

The current investigation encountered several limitations. The primary limitation of this study is the use of a modified cut-off score tailored to our specific population, rather than the standard cut-off scores outlined in the ASQ:SE-2 user guide. This modification was necessary due to factors such as cultural, demographic, and population-specific differences, which we believe better reflect the unique characteristics of our sample. While this approach ensures that our findings are accurately aligned with the needs of our population, it may limit direct comparability with studies that use the standard ASQ:SE-2 cut-off scores.

Additionally, to facilitate the completion of computerized questionnaires, parents were instructed to indicate their preferred options by either marking blank spaces with a cross or filling them in, to ensure accurate interpretation by the computer. Interviewers were also tasked with verifying the accuracy of parental actions upon receiving completed questionnaires. If discrepancies were noted, interviewers either manually entered responses using a new questionnaire or requested parents to rectify any errors. This process aimed to mitigate the potential for human errors in transferring data between questionnaires.

Moreover, during the test-retest conducted in Tehran, interviewers were scheduled to revisit participants' homes with prior appointments to collect completed questionnaires and ensure their quality. Subsequently, similar questionnaires were distributed to families for completion a second time in the presence of the interviewer. However, due to the possibility of significant developmental changes in children—particularly in younger age groups—within a week, this approach might have influenced parental responses during the second stage and, consequently, the test-retest reliability outcomes. Similarly, it's conceivable that completing the questionnaires provided parents with indirect insight into their child's developmental abilities and potential disabilities compared to peers of the same age. This newfound awareness might have led to improved interactions between parents and their child during the one-week interval between the initial test and retest, potentially influencing the child's acquisition of new skills and consequently impacting the retest results, leading to greater disparities and inconsistencies compared to the initial testing phase. Furthermore, the reported prevalence of developmental delays across various domains and age groups in our study was not derived from a dedicated sampling process for this purpose. Instead, sampling was focused on instrument validation and establishing Iranian cut-off points.

One limitation of our study was the requirement for parents to have at least an elementary school education to complete the ASQ questionnaire, which may have led to the exclusion of less-educated individuals. This exclusion could potentially underestimate the prevalence of social-emotional issues, as lower educational levels have been linked to higher risks of developmental challenges in children. To mitigate this limitation, future studies could consider involving healthcare professionals, such as nurses or trained health practitioners, to assist parents with limited literacy in completing the questionnaire. This approach could enhance the inclusivity of data collection, ensuring that the developmental status of children from diverse educational backgrounds is accurately represented and assessed.

Despite its limitations, this study boasts several strengths that set it apart from many others in the field of child development research, both nationally and internationally. It stands as one of the few endeavors in Iran and beyond to validate and standardize a screening tool for children's development across such diverse demographic dimensions, employing meticulous and representative sampling techniques that reflect Iranian society. Moreover, the adoption of a community-based (door-to-door) approach further enhances its methodological robustness. The involvement of a team of top-tier experts in the field for activities such as face validity assessment, content validity evaluation, and cultural adaptation underscores the study's rigor. The breadth and depth of expertise enlisted for these stages are unparalleled in comparable studies.

Conclusion

In summary, the Iranian cut-off points established for the Persian version of the ASQ among Iranian children are considered applicable and relevant at the national level. However, it is important to note that the reported prevalence of developmental delay across various domains and age groups in this study was not derived from dedicated sampling for this purpose; rather, sampling was conducted to validate the instrument and determine Iranian cut-off points. Consequently, this may not provide a precise estimation of the disorder's frequency within Iranian society. Therefore, it is recommended that standardized questionnaires and Iranian cut-off points obtained from this study, with an adequate sample size and proportional sampling, be used in future assessments. This would enable the measurement of developmental status among different age groups across various regions of the country, facilitating comparisons with the findings of this study.
