Development and internal validation of an ICU mortality prediction model for patients with concurrent sepsis and heart failure: a retrospective cohort study based on eICU-CRD
Authors: Mao Ye, He Huang, Suqi Lv, Dingchao Lv, Xiaolan Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSepsis and heart failure are common critical conditions within the ICU, and their coexistence significantly increases the difficulty of patient management and mortality risk. This study aims to develop and validate a practical nomogram for predicting ICU mortality, thereby facilitating risk stratification and clinical decision-making for patients coded as having both sepsis and heart failure.MethodsThis study constitutes a retrospective cohort investigation. Patients meeting inclusion criteria for both sepsis and heart failure were identified via the eICU-CRD database, subsequently randomised in a 7:3 ratio to form training and validation cohorts. Variable selection employed the Least Absolute and Selective Operator (LASSO) regression, with nomogram analysis constructed. Model discriminatory ability was assessed via area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC). Calibration was evaluated using calibration curves and the Hosmer-Lemeshow test. Decision curves and clinical impact curves were plotted to evaluate the model’s net benefit and clinical applicability.ResultsA total of 1,394 patients were included and divided into a training set (975 cases) and a validation set (419 cases) at a ratio of 7:3. The final model integrated eight independent predictors: mechanical ventilation, lactate, respiratory rate, white blood cell count, age, platelet count, systolic blood pressure, and oxygen saturation. The model demonstrated acceptable to good discriminatory performance in both training and validation cohorts (AUC 0.826 [95% CI 0.789–0.863] and 0.798 [95% CI 0.732–0.864], respectively), with good calibration (Brier scores: training cohort 0.090, validation cohort 0.091; Hosmer-Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test P-values > 0.05), and demonstrated clear clinical net benefit.ConclusionsThis study developed and internally validated a predictive nomogram based on eight routine clinical variables for ICU mortality in patients coded for both sepsis and heart failure. The model demonstrated acceptable discrimination and calibration. However, given the broad, code-based case definition and the lack of heart failure-specific variables, this tool should be reframed as an internally validated ICU mortality prediction model for this high-risk population, rather than as a cardiology-specific or clinically actionable nomogram. External validation is required before any clinical application.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40842-026-00323-2.

Introduction

Sepsis and heart failure represent two of the most challenging problems in modern critical care, with persistently high rates of incidence and mortality [1, 2]. When these conditions co-occur to form septic heart failure, patients exhibit a highly vulnerable clinical phenotype: a complex interplay of dysregulated inflammatory responses and impaired cardiac pump function frequently escalates rapidly into refractory shock and multiple organ dysfunction, resulting in mortality rates far exceeding those of either condition alone [3–5].

Current prognostic assessment in intensive care units (ICUs) primarily relies on generalised critical illness scoring systems such as APACHE and SOFA [6]. Whilst these scoring systems have been validated across broad cohorts of critically ill patients, they lack specificity for particular conditions, such as sepsis complicated by acute heart failure [7, 8]. For this patient cohort, the predictive accuracy of these generalised tools may be limited, as they may not be able to optimally integrate and weigh the specific contributions of concurrent sepsis and cardiogenic injury [9]. This creates a significant gap in the availability of precise predictive tools for this high-risk group.

The development of prognostic tools in intensive care medicine increasingly relies upon large-scale clinical databases and machine learning techniques. Predictive maps have become central drivers of bedside risk stratification by translating complex statistics into easily comprehensible charts. Such tools consolidate multiple variables into a single, intuitive probabilistic assessment. Multiple studies have attempted to independently predict the prognosis of sepsis or heart failure using biomarkers, haemodynamic parameters, and organ failure scores [10–12]. However, there remains a need to develop a tool integrating routine clinical and laboratory data to predict the early in-ICU prognosis of patients with concurrent sepsis and heart failure. It should be noted that the term “sepsis-associated heart failure”, as used in the literature and in this study, may encompass two distinct entities: sepsis-induced cardiomyopathy (new-onset heart failure caused directly by sepsis) and the development of sepsis in patients with pre-existing chronic heart failure (of any phenotype). Our retrospective, coding-based definition is unable to reliably distinguish between these two scenarios.

This study aimed to develop and internally validate a pragmatic nomogram based on eICU-CRD data, enabling the prediction of in-hospital mortality in ICU patients coded for both sepsis and heart failure using readily accessible parameters.

Materials and methods

Data source

This retrospective study utilised the eICU Collaborative Research Database. This database comprises a multicentre intensive care unit (ICU) dataset encompassing over 200,000 hospital admissions across 335 ICUs in 208 US hospitals between 2014 and 2015 [13]. Access to this database requires validation and certification through the PhysioNet Review Board’s data usage protocol. The database is released under the Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act (HIPAA) Safe Harbour provisions. Access is granted solely to users who have completed the Collaborative Institution Training Initiative (CITI) course on data or sample research. Compliance with Safe Harbour standards is certified (HIPAA certification number: 73425064). This study adheres to the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki.

Population selection criteria

This study identified patients with both sepsis and heart failure through International Classification of Diseases (ICD) codes within the eICU-CRD database. Relevant ICD-9 codes include 0,389, 99,590, 42,820, 42,821, 42,822, 42,823, 42,840, 42,841, 42,842, 42,843, 42,830, 42,831, 42,832, 42,833, 4,280, 4,281, 40,491, 40,492, 40,493, 4,169, 4,150, 900,969, ICD-10 codes: I509. Among the study subjects, patients with suspected infection and a Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (SOFA) score of ≥ 2 were also categorised as having sepsis according to the Sepsis 3.0 criteria [14]. Heart failure was defined as a syndrome characterised by inadequate cardiac output due to structural or functional cardiac abnormalities, leading to systemic or pulmonary congestion, encompassing all types of heart failure with varying ejection fractions [15]. We acknowledge that the eICU-CRD database does not consistently provide echocardiography data, natriuretic peptide test results, or invasive haemodynamic monitoring (such as PiCCO) data. Consequently, we are unable to validate the diagnosis of heart failure using objective indicators of cardiac structure or function. This diagnosis is limited to the ICD codes entered by the attending physician during the initial hospital admission. This code-based retrospective definition cannot reliably distinguish between subtypes of heart failure (such as heart failure with reduced ejection fraction, heart failure with preserved ejection fraction, acute decompensated heart failure, and chronic stable heart failure), nor can it definitively identify sepsis-induced cardiomyopathy. Ultimately, 1,394 patients meeting the combined diagnostic codes for both sepsis and heart failure were included; detailed information is presented in Fig. 1.

Data extraction and management

This study extracted relevant variables from the eICU-CRD: (1) Patient general information; (2) Past medical history; (3) Severity scores, including the Acute Physiology and Chronic Health Evaluation (APACHE), Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (SOFA), and Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS); (4) Vital signs, electrolytes, and biochemical parameters within 24 h of ICU admission; (5) Outcome: ICU mortality.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables with a normal distribution were expressed as mean ± SD, with intergroup differences assessed via t-tests. Non-normally distributed continuous variables were reported as median (interquartile range, 25th to 75th percentile) and compared between groups using the non-parametric Mann-Whitney U test. Qualitative variables were presented as n (%), analysed via Fisher’s exact test or chi-square test. Variables with a missing rate exceeding 30% were excluded from the analysis. For the remaining variables, the proportion of missing values in the eight predictor variables retained in the final model were as follows: Mechanical ventilation: 0 (0%); Lactate: 51 (3.6%); Respiratory rate: 39 (2.7%); White blood cell count: 1 (0.07%); Age: 0 (0%); Platelet count: 2 (0.14%); Systolic blood pressure: 63 (4.51%); Oxygen saturation: 56 (4.00%) (Other missing variables Supplementary Table 1). Multiple Imputation by Chained Equations (MICE) was employed under the Missing At Random (MAR) assumption. Five imputed datasets were generated, incorporating all candidate predictor variables and the outcome variable into the imputation model. Rubin’s rules were applied to integrate estimates across imputed datasets. Sensitivity analysis results from the full case analysis were consistent, confirming the robustness of the imputation method. Following data imputation, univariate logistic regression analysis was employed to assess the association between these variables and ICU mortality. Subsequently, a multivariate logistic regression model was utilised to adjust for confounding factors and identify independent predictors [16]. Subsequently, a nomogram was created by assigning scores to each factor based on its impact on the outcome. Construct receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves [17], calibration curves, decision curve analysis (DCA), and clinical impact curves to further evaluate the model’s applicability. All statistical tests were conducted using two-tailed methods. Analyses were performed using R software (version 4.2.2), with p-values ≤ 0.05 considered statistically significant.

Results

Baseline characteristics from the eICU-CRD

This study involved 1,394 patients coded for both sepsis and heart failure, of whom 183 (13.3%) died during their ICU stay. Non-surviving patients, in comparison to survivors, exhibited advanced age, elevated heart and respiratory rates, increased white blood cell count, creatinine, blood urea nitrogen, and lactate, decreased platelet count, lymphocyte percentage, albumin, serum calcium levels and oxygen saturation. The non-surviving cohort had a higher prevalence of mechanical ventilation and diabetes. Additionally, they presented higher APACHE, and SOFA scores, along with a lower Glasgow Coma Scale score (Table 1). Table 2 compares the distribution patterns of the training dataset (975 patients) and the test dataset (419 patients), divided in a 7:3 ratio. No significant differences were observed in baseline characteristics between the two datasets (p > 0.05), indicating that the two groups are sufficiently comparable.

LASSO regression selection of predictor variables

To optimize the model structure and mitigate the risk of overfitting, this study employed LASSO regression for preliminary variable screening. Following the determination of the optimal penalty parameter (λ 1se = -3.5845) via cross-validation, 13 clinical indicators were selected for further analysis (Fig. 2A and B). Subsequently, multivariable logistic regression was applied to identify independent predictors associated with 28-day ICU mortality. The final predictive model incorporated the following 8 variables: Mechanical ventilation (mv), lactic acid (lac), respiratory rate, white blood cell count (wbc), age, platelets (plt), systolic blood pressure (sbp), arterial oxygen satura-tion (spo2). The results of the multivariable logistic regression analysis indicated that mv (OR 4.343, 95% CI 2.71–7.04, p < 0.001), lactic acid (OR 1.152, 95% CI 1.049–1.269, p = 0.003), respiratory rate (OR 1.082, 95% CI 1.035–1.132, p = 0.001), white blood cell count (OR 1.045, 95% CI 1.019–1.071, p = 0.001), age (OR 1.025, 95% CI 1.008–1.043, p = 0.005) were independent risk factors for patient prognosis; whereas platelets (OR 0.996, 95% CI 0.993–0.998, p < 0.001), systolic blood pressure (OR 0.970, 95% CI 0.955–0.985, p < 0.001), arterial oxygen satura-tion (OR 0.849, 95% CI 0.774–0.931, p = 0.001) were identified as protective factors (Fig. 3A and B).

Production of nomograms

The nomogram incorporates eight independent predictors identified through regression analysis. Each variable contributes specific points, which are summed to predict survival probability. For each point on the nomogram, individual scores for each variable are calculated based on the characteristics of a given patient. The total score is derived from the sum of these individual scores, which corresponds to the probability of mortality (Fig. 4).

Discrimination, calibration and evaluation

The predictive performance of this model was comprehensively evaluated across three dimensions: discrimination, calibration, and clinical utility. In terms of discriminatory ability, the model achieved an area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) of 0.826 (95% CI 0.789–0.863) in the training cohort and 0.798 (95% CI 0.732–0.864) in the validation cohort, indicating acceptable to good discriminatory performance (Fig. 5A and B). According to standard AUC classification criteria (0.5–0.7: poor, 0.7–0.8: acceptable, 0.8–0.9: good, > 0.9: excellent), the AUC value for the training set was 0.826, falling within the ‘good’ range; whereas the AUC value for the validation set was 0.798, situated at the threshold between ‘acceptable’ and ‘good’. The wide confidence intervals, particularly in the validation set (95% CI 0.732–0.864), suggest that its true performance may be subject to variation. The calibration curve, adjusted using the guided method, approached the ideal 45-degree diagonal line in both datasets, indicating a high degree of agreement between predicted probabilities and actual outcomes (Fig. 6A and B). This conclusion was further supported by the Brill score (training set: 0.090, validation set: 0.091) and non-significant results from the Hosmer-Lemeshow test (training set p = 0.957, validation set p = 0.934). Moreover, clinical decision curve and clinical impact curve analyses demonstrate that our model yields considerable net gains across both training and testing datasets, as illustrated in the decision curve analysis (Fig. 7A and B) and clinical impact curve analysis (Fig. 7C and D).

Comparison with existing severity scores

To assess the incremental value of this nomogram over traditional severity scores in prognostic prediction, we calculated the net reclassification improvement (NRI) and integrated discrimination improvement (IDI) relative to APACHE and SOFA scores. Compared with APACHE, this nomogram demonstrated an NRI of 0.868 (95% CI: 0.698–1.038; IDI = 0.172, 95% CI: 0.133–0.212) and the validation set (NRI = 0.833, 95% CI: 0.578–1.088; IDI = 0.139, 95% CI: 0.07–0.209). Similarly, compared with the SOFA score, this nomogram demonstrated superior performance in both the training set (NRI = 0.914, 95% CI: 0.746–1.081; IDI = 0.173, 95% CI: 0.133–0.213) and validation set (NRI = 0.896, 95% CI: 0.647–1.146; IDI = 0.191, 95% CI: 0.124–0.258) (Supplementary Table 2).

Sensitivity analysis

First of all, to assess model robustness, we conducted a sensitivity analysis using in-hospital mortality as a surrogate endpoint. Among 1,394 patients, 189 patients (20.73%) died during hospitalisation. The model retained good discriminatory ability, achieving an AUC value of 0.784 (95% CI: 0.749–0.820) on the training set and 0.769 (95% CI: 0.714–0.825) on the validation set (Supplementary Fig. 1). These results were consistent with the primary analysis using ICU mortality. Secondly, in our sensitivity analysis excluding mechanical ventilation, the AUC values for the training and validation sets were 0.761 (95% CI: 0.718–0.805) and 0.730 (95%CI: 0.655–0.805) respectively (Supplementary Fig. 4). Thirdly, the results from the full data sensitivity analysis were consistent, validating the robustness of the interpolation method. For further details, please refer to Supplementary Fig. 2 and Fig. 3.

Discussion

We developed and validated a novel nomogram for the prediction of mortality in the ICU in patients retrospectively coded as having both sepsis and heart failure using a large multicentre intensive care database. The final model incorporated eight readily available clinical variables (mechanical ventilation, lactate, respiratory rate, white blood cell count, age, platelet count, systolic blood pressure and oxygen saturation) and performed well. The model is moderately discriminative ability (AUC of 0.826 and 0.798 for training and validation cohorts respectively), calibration and clinically useful (as validated by decision curve analysis) and provides a practical bedside risk stratification tool for this high prevalence population. Nevertheless, given the limitations discussed below, this paper should be repositioned as an ICU mortality model (internal validation) for combined sepsis and heart failure diagnosis codes, rather than a prediction model specifically tailored to cardiology or intended for clinical guidance. Its predictive factors primarily reflect the overall severity of the disease, rather than pathophysiological characteristics specific to heart failure.

The choice of predictive factors in our model is based on some pathophysiology principles. The strong association between mechanical ventilation and mortality may reflect not only respiratory failure severity but also the complex cardiopulmonary interactions of septic shock [18, 19]. Positive pressure ventilation may influence preload and right ventricular function [20]. Lactate is a key variable that is used as a substitute marker for systemic tissue hypoxia and impaired oxygen use (a consequence of microcirculation failure in sepsis and low cardiac output due to heart failure) [21]. Higher respiratory rate and white blood cell count are classic symptom of systemic inflammation and physiological stress [22]. Inclusion age is related to the influence of immunosenescence and decreased physiological reserve on sepsis [22]. It is noteworthy that elevated platelet counts and systolic blood pressure represent protective factors. This may reflect the unique context of our cohort: in this patient population, relatively preserved platelet counts may indicate less severe disseminated intravascular coagulation or bone marrow suppression, while elevated systolic blood pressure typically signifies a more responsive circulatory state to initial resuscitation rather than the presence of chronic hypertension [23, 24]. Consequently, these variables may serve as markers of less severe physiological disturbance. Regarding the inclusion of mechanical ventilation, its strong correlation with disease severity must be interpreted with caution. We acknowledge that mechanical ventilation may largely reflect an increase in overall disease severity or treatment intensity, rather than representing an independent prognostic biological factor specific to heart failure. Although mechanical ventilation undoubtedly reflects the severity of respiratory failure, in the specific context of sepsis complicated by heart failure, the prognostic information it provides may extend beyond serving merely as an indicator of severity [25]. Positive pressure ventilation introduces complex cardiopulmonary interactions - reducing venous return (preload) while increasing right ventricular afterload - which may directly exacerbate haemodynamic impairment in patients with pre-existing cardiac dysfunction [26, 27]. Consequently, its use as a predictor holds both clinical and pathophysiological rationale. To empirically address concerns regarding circulatory prediction, we conducted a sensitivity analysis by refitting the model after excluding mechanical ventilation. Results demonstrated a slight decline in model discriminative capacity, with training set AUC decreasing from 0.826 to 0.761 and validation set AUC from 0.798 to 0.730. These findings indicate that whilst mechanical ventilation provides meaningful incremental prognostic value, the model maintains reasonable performance even after excluding this variable. This supports the model’s robustness and mitigates concerns regarding circulatory prediction.

To assess the robustness of the primary findings, we conducted a sensitivity analysis using in-hospital mortality as a surrogate endpoint. This endpoint was selected to capture deaths occurring throughout the entire hospitalisation period, thereby complementing the ICU-specific perspective of the primary outcome. The model demonstrated consistent predictive performance, with an area under the curve (AUC) of 0.784 (95% CI: 0.749–0.820) in the training set and 0.769 (95% CI: 0.714–0.825) in the validation set. These results were highly consistent with those obtained using ICU mortality (0.826 and 0.798, respectively), indicating robust discriminative capability of our model across different mortality definitions. The slight reduction in AUC for in-hospital mortality may reflect the influence of post-ICU events, which exhibit weaker direct associations with acute critical illness. Nevertheless, the consistency in both the direction and magnitude of effects across the two endpoints reinforces the reliability and universality of our prognostic model.

Our model has advantages in terms of current evidence of sepsis and heart failure. While APACHE and SOFA are effective for general ICU cohorts, they may not capture the unique risk characteristics of patients with concomitant cardiac disease. Recently, studies have begun to study specific biomarkers or echocardiography parameters for sepsis-induced cardiomyopathy [28, 29]. Our nomogram is different in that it is based on readily available, electronically available variables and not rely on special investigations that may not be performed or readily available in all centres. This enables it to be scaleable and immediate clinical application. Our results align partially with previous models that identified lactate, vasopressor use and organ failure scores as predictors [30–32]. However, integrating these readily available variables into a dedicated model specifically tailored for the subgroup of patients with both sepsis and heart failure represents a novel approach aimed at bedside utility. The model maintains robust performance without integrating complex echocardiographic data and suggests that routine clinical data contain important prognostic signals. This reflects the development of “electronic scores” from electronic health records [32, 33]. Concurrently, our prediction chart offers significant incremental prognostic value compared to traditional severity scoring systems. NRI and IDI analyses indicate that, relative to APACHE IV and SOFA, this chart correctly reclassified a substantial proportion of patients (NRI ranging from 0.833 to 0.914; IDI ranging from 0.139 to 0.191). These findings suggest that integrating eight readily accessible variables captures prognostic information inadequately addressed by universal critical illness scores in this specific patient population. This highlights the model’s potential for enhancing risk stratification and guiding clinical decision-making in this vulnerable population.

The key contribution of this work is that it allows for a detailed risk stratification of sepsis subtypes rather than “one size fits all” scoring systems [34]. Clinically, this nomogram could serve as a decision support tool. By displaying visualised, quantified mortality risk estimates, the nomogram may assist clinicians in risk stratification and in discussions regarding resource allocation. Consistent with the appropriate use of prognostic scores in intensive care, it should not be used as the sole basis for individual treatment decisions nor for directly communicating raw risk scores to families without proper contextualization. However, we emphasize that this prognostic tool should not be used as the primary basis for initiating specific personalized treatments (such as inotropic support or advanced hemodynamic monitoring). Those decisions require direct diagnostic evaluation, including echocardiography, invasive hemodynamic monitoring, and serial clinical assessment [13]. The model is clinically useful under different threshold probabilities, which can show net benefit can be achieved under different clinical risk tolerance levels.

Our study retains potential limitations. Firstly, as a retrospective study utilising the eICU-CRD, the identification of sepsis and heart failure relied upon ICD-9/10 coding and clinical criteria rather than prospective determination. Despite employing standardised definitions, misclassification bias cannot be entirely excluded. Variations in coding practices across institutions, alongside potential under-or over-diagnosis of heart failure, may have influenced case selection. Secondly, the diagnosis of heart failure relied entirely on ICD codes, without confirmatory echocardiography, natriuretic peptides or invasive haemodynamic assessment. This is a significant limitation, as code-based diagnoses may be subject to misclassification, coding bias and a lack of standardised criteria. Thirdly, whilst internal validation achieved through randomised grouping demonstrates the study’s internal validity, the absence of external validation from independent cohorts across different healthcare systems or geographical regions limits the generalisability of the findings. Fourthly, we must acknowledge a key limitation of this set of predictors. Most of the variables in our final model (e.g. lactate, respiratory rate, white blood cell count) are merely non-specific markers of acute disease severity, rather than pathophysiological indicators specific to heart failure. This nomogram does not incorporate key heart failure-specific variables, such as echocardiographic parameters, natriuretic peptides, cardiac troponin, the use of vasopressors or inotropic agents, volume status, fluid balance, renal replacement therapy, and formal classification of heart failure subtypes. Consequently, this nomogram may be more suitable as a general ICU severity model rather than a disease-specific tool for patients with sepsis-associated heart failure. This limitation should be carefully considered when interpreting the results of this study, and future research should incorporate these missing variables to develop more phenotype-specific predictive tools. We emphasise that external validation is an absolute prerequisite for clinical application. Without such validation, our nomogram remains merely a tool for generating hypotheses, rather than a clinical tool ready for immediate use.

Conclusion

This study developed and internally validated a nomogram to predict the risk of mortality in ICU patients with concurrent sepsis and heart failure, based on eight routine clinical variables. The model demonstrated acceptable discriminatory power (AUC 0.798–0.826) and calibration. However, given that the broad, code-based case definition cannot distinguish between subtypes of heart failure or sepsis-induced cardiomyopathy, and given the lack of heart failure-specific variables, this tool should be regarded as a general prognostic aid for high-risk ICU patients. It is not a cardiology-specific or clinically actionable nomogram. External validation is required prior to clinical application. Whilst such prognostic tools may aid in risk stratification and risk adjustment within research, they do not directly improve patient outcomes; their potential clinical benefit depends on how risk assessment is integrated with clinical judgement and evidence-based interventions.
