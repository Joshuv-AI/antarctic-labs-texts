iPSC-derived skeletal muscle spheroids for Duchenne Muscular Dystrophy modeling
Authors: Joyce Esposito, Felipe de Souza Leite, Igor Neves Barbosa, Thaís Maria da Mata Martins, Giovanna Gonçalves de Oliveira Olberg, Ziad Al Tanoury, Kayque Alves Telles-Silva, Mayana Cristina da Silva Pardo, Tatiana Jazedje, Raul Hernandes Bortolin, Mario Hiroyuki Hirata, Olivier Pourquié, Mayana Zatz
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe progressive skeletal muscle degeneration observed in Duchenne Muscular Dystrophy (DMD) patients requires multiple cycles of satellite cells (SCs) activation to promote tissue regeneration. Dystrophic SCs present intrinsic defects, and the disrupting fibrotic niche hinders appropriate muscle recovery. Traditional 2D culture systems face challenges in modeling the DMD muscle niche and SCs behavior. Our aim was to validate a 3D culture of skeletal muscle spheroids (iSMS) for DMD modeling, as compared to the traditional 2D culture, while investigating the pathophysiological mechanisms of dystrophin deficiency in vitro.MethodsTo compare iSMS with traditional 2D myogenic differentiation, we differentiated wild-type (WT), dystrophic (DMD) isogenic induced pluripotent stem cells (iPSCs), as well as iPSCs derived from DMD patients, characterized myogenic markers levels and assessed differences in proliferation and differentiation using RT-qPCR, immunofluorescence, and flow cytometry.ResultsOur data showed that iSMS improved PAX7 expression in vitro, while MYOD1, MYOG, MYF5, and MYH3 expression were significantly reduced. These findings suggest that, at three weeks of myogenic differentiation, iSMS cultures retained satellite-like cells in a less activated, progenitor-like state. Accordingly, we identified higher expression of canonical Notch signaling genes such as JAG1 and NOTCH1 in iSMS compared to 2D. We also characterized the response of 2D and iSMS to terminal differentiation medium, providing a valuable comparison with muscle fibers derived from human adult myoblasts. Additionally, we showed that DMD iSMS-derived progenitors proliferated at reduced levels compared with WT, a characteristic not observed in progenitors derived from 2D cultures. Finally, we performed iSMS and 2D myogenic differentiation of iPSC lines from three patients with DMD.ConclusionOur results highlight important advantages of using the iSMS differentiation platform over 2D for DMD in vitro modeling. Exploring these 3D systems may help to gain a deeper understanding of SCs behavior to advance in novel treatments for DMD, which might be applicable to other forms of muscular disorders.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13395-026-00428-3.Keywords: Muscle spheroids, Induced pluripotent stem cells, Duchenne Muscular Dystrophy, Disease Modeling, Satellite Cells

Introduction

Duchenne Muscular Dystrophy (DMD) is an X-linked genetic disease caused primarily by disruptive mutations in the DMD gene causing dystrophin deficiency [21, 35]. In skeletal muscles, the lack of dystrophin leads to progressive degeneration and necrosis, extracellular matrix deposition, delayed regeneration, and consequent loss of muscular function over time [16]. Previously considered a myofiber-only disorder, DMD has also been characterized by satellite cells (SCs) dysfunction and consequent impaired regenerative capacity [28].

SCs are formed in the late fetal stage, and they are characterized by Pax7 expression in adult muscles [43, 49]. In homeostasis, SCs are maintained in a quiescent state closely associated with myofibers, between the sarcolemma and basal lamina [66]. After muscle injury, SCs are activated and enter the cell cycle, which is accompanied by the temporal and spatial expression of the myogenic regulatory factors Myf5, MyoD, MyoG, and MRF4, a family of basic helix-loop-helix transcription factors responsible for regulation of prenatal and postnatal myogenesis [64]. The balance between SCs activation and quiescence and between asymmetric and symmetric cell divisions determines the maintenance of the stem cell pool throughout life [4]. However, this balance is severely compromised in dystrophin-deficient muscles due to constant regeneration and degeneration cycles accompanied by SCs intrinsic defects and the disrupting niche [28].

Studying skeletal muscles and the dynamics of SCs formation and maintenance in DMD has been challenging because of difficulties in extrapolating animal model results to human disease contexts [59]. The in vitro culture of human cells can circumvent these challenges, but conventional bidimensional (2D) cultures fail to reproduce critical aspects of muscle physiology, stem cell fate, and niche, hindering the maintenance of SCs for long periods [36, 42]. Furthermore, ethical and technical issues limit isolation and expansion of SCs from the skeletal muscle of DMD patients [67].

Numerous studies have focused on the development of advanced 3D skeletal muscle models that better replicate skeletal muscle interactions, mechanical loading, stiffness, and extracellular matrix composition [23]. These models may also improve our knowledge of the pathological mechanisms of neuromuscular diseases, contributing to drug development and precision medicine [27]. However, only a limited number of studies have reported the generation and maintenance of human SCs in these culture systems, which is a current challenge in the field, especially in the context of DMD.

The advent of induced pluripotent stem cells (iPSCs) has enabled the investigation of patient-specific cells and the molecular mechanisms governing disease progression [12, 54]. Transgene-free protocols for the myogenic differentiation of human iPSCs can recapitulate key aspects of early skeletal muscle development, ultimately giving rise to PAX7+ cells with SCs properties [2, 9]. Despite being a valuable model for studying patient-specific SCs and myogenic progenitors, there are challenges in establishing appropriate phenotypic in vitro parameters to compare healthy and dystrophic cells [11, 52]. However, the poor complexity of traditional 2D cultures can be improved using more physiological 3D models [44]. For instance, artificial 3D hydrogel-based muscles from iPSCs of patients with skeletal muscle laminopathies highlighted nuclear abnormalities and deformities that were not apparent in conventional 2D cultures [32].

In this study, we have performed differentiation of iPSCs into skeletal muscle spheroids (iSMS). Our aim was to produce myogenic progenitors and satellite-like cells from iPSCs, comparing iSMS with conventional 2D culture using a DMD lineage (DMD) and its parental isogenic WT lineage. Our results showed a marked increase in PAX7 mRNA expression in iSMS and a concomitant decrease in myogenic differentiation markers compared to 2D cultures. Furthermore, we characterized Notch pathway regulation and proliferative behavior in 2D and iSMS from WT and DMD isogenic lineages. We also provided a valuable comparison between skeletal muscle fibers derived from iPSCs and primary human adult myoblasts. Finally, we characterized iSMS from patient-derived iPSCs with different pathogenic variants of DMD.

Results

Differentiation into myogenic progenitors in 2D versus iSMS culture systems

First, we tested whether primary myogenic differentiation could be performed to generate iSMS using the protocol described by Chal et al. [8] for adherent cultures, and potential outcome differences between WT and DMD isogenic lines. Therefore, 2D and iSMS differentiation of iPSCs into myogenic progenitors was performed side by side (Fig. 1a). No visible morphological changes were observed in the 2D cultures comparing DMD and WT (Fig. 1b), and DMD and WT iSMS displayed similar dimensions (Fig. 1c). Gene expression analysis revealed that the myogenic markers MYOD1, MYOG, MYF5, and MYH3 were significantly downregulated in iSMS relative to 2D across all comparisons, indicating that the iSMS condition consistently induces lower myogenic marker expression (Fig. 1d). Within the same culture condition, we did not detect any differences between WT and DMD. Immunocytochemistry of iSMS revealed less than 10% MYOD+ and approximately 17% MYF5+ cells, with comparable levels between WT and DMD (Fig. 1e and f).

Based on the reduction in myogenic markers, we sought to further characterize the iSMS model by evaluating the presence of different cellular populations (Supplementary Figure S1). We assessed the expression of markers for mesenchymal cells and/or fibroadipogenic progenitors (CD90, PDGFRa, and CD73), endothelial cells (CD31), neuromesodermal progenitors (SOX2), and myogenic progenitors (CD56 and CD82). WT and DMD iSMS contained approximately 5.72 ± 0.8 and 7.8% ± 1.1 SOX2+ cells, respectively, and 10% ± 3.4 and 5% ± 1.9 CD90+ cells, respectively, whereas less than 1% were CD73+, PDFGFRa+ or CD31+ (Supplementary Figure S1a and S1b). The proportion of these cell types did not differ significantly between WT and DMD iSMS. Supporting a predominant myogenic identity, more than 60% of iSMS-derived cells expressed at least one of the two myogenic markers CD56 or CD82 (Supplementary Figure S1c, S1d). Despite slight differences in the distribution of CD56/CD82 subpopulations, no significant changes were observed between WT and DMD iSMS (Supplementary Figure S1d). Overall, these data confirm the successful generation of myogenic progenitors utilizing the iSMS protocol.

Derivation of PAX7+ satellite-like cells in differentiated WT and DMD in 2D and iSMS

We hypothesized that iSMS provide a more suitable environment for the generation and maintenance of PAX7+ cells during differentiation. Also, we investigated whether DMD would differ from WT in terms of SCs formation and maintenance. On differentiation day 21, nuclear Venus YFP signal was detected in iSMS (Fig. 2a). Immunocytochemistry further confirmed the presence of PAX7+ cells (Fig. 2a). On average, iSMS contained approximately 41% PAX7+ cells in both WT and DMD (Fig. 2b).

To verify the feasibility of selecting the PAX7+ cells for future cell sorting applications, we successfully performed flow cytometric analysis of Venus-YFP reporter cells (Fig. 2c). Importantly, we verified that PAX7 mRNA levels were increased in WT iSMS relative to WT 2D and DMD 2D, and in DMD iSMS relative to DMD 2D and WT 2D (Fig. 2d). Interestingly, while PAX7 expression was significantly reduced in DMD iSMS versus WT iSMS, there were no significant differences between DMD and WT in 2D cultures. Taken together, our data show that iSMS support the derivation and maintenance of satellite-like cells with elevated PAX7 expression at this stage of myogenic differentiation.

Notch pathway regulation in iSMS and 2D cultures

Because Notch regulates SC quiescence and fate through cell–cell interactions, we hypothesized that its activity would differ in the 3D iSMS environment compared to 2D cultures, particularly when comparing WT and DMD. As an exploratory analysis, we evaluated Notch pathway gene expression in WT versus DMD samples cultures in 2D and iSMS using an RT2 profiler array. Of the 84 genes assessed, 13 were excluded due to lack of detectable expression (Supplementary Table S3). Compared to WT, DMD samples displayed 16 downregulated and 19 upregulated genes in 2D. In contrast, 41 genes were downregulated and 5 upregulated in DMD iSMS versus WT iSMS, suggesting a more pronounced reduction in Notch transcripts (Supplementary Figure S2). Comparing WT and DMD in both culture systems, 24 genes were commonly differentially expressed.

To validate gene expression alterations, we analyzed key canonical Notch pathway components using RT-qPCR. Compared to WT 2D, WT iSMS exhibited upregulation of JAG1, NOTCH1, HES1, HEYL and NRARP, but not NOTCH2 (Fig. 3). Similarly, DMD iSMS showed increased expression of JAG1, NOTCH1, HES1 and NRARP, but not NOTCH2 and HEYL, as compared to DMD 2D. Notably, when comparing DMD and WT, HEYL was the only gene significantly downregulated in the DMD group under both 2D and iSMS conditions. Our findings highlight that the type of cell culture may directly impact Notch signaling in iPSC-derived myogenic progenitors, potentially affecting the detection of cell-intrinsic phenotypic readouts associated with this pathway.

Secondary differentiation of 2D and iSMS cultures

Next, we investigated how myogenic progenitors in iSMS would respond to a subsequent differentiation step following primary differentiation, in which cells are exposed to terminal differentiation medium (hereafter referred to as “secondary differentiation”). In 2D cultures, this process usually requires cell dissociation followed by expansion in myoblast growth medium and differentiation into skeletal muscle fibers [8]. In contrast, to evaluate how the 3D environment of iSMS influences the maintenance of the progenitor state, iSMS were exposed to both growth and terminal differentiation media without prior dissociation (Fig. 4a). The 2D and iSMS cultures were evaluated after 8 days (SD8) of secondary differentiation, allowing adequate response to differentiation cues. Importantly, the terminal differentiation medium contains SB-431542, a potent TGF-B receptor type I kinase inhibitor that stimulate myotube fusion [20].

PAX7 mRNA was detected in all groups after 8 days of treatment with terminal differentiation medium, and expression levels were similar between WT 2D and WT iSMS, and between DMD 2D and DMD iSMS (Fig. 4b). Contrary to what we observed in primary differentiation, PAX7 was reduced in DMD 2D versus WT 2D, but this difference was not found in WT iSMS versus DMD iSMS. To better investigate PAX7 kinetics, we compared PAX7 expression from undifferentiated iPSCs (D0) with cells after primary differentiation day 21 (D21) and after secondary differentiation day 8 (SD8). This analysis revealed marked PAX7 upregulation in 2D after secondary differentiation compared to D21, while in iSMS PAX7 expression remained relatively stable (Supplementary Figure S3). Interestingly, equivalent analysis of MYOD1, MYF5, MYOG and MYH3 mRNA expression revealed that iSMS have distinct expression kinetics compared to 2D cultures, with a slower upregulation of MYOD1, MYF5, MYOG and MYH3, while 2D cultures exhibit faster upregulation of myogenic markers throughout differentiation. Overall, these data show that, although iSMS are responsive to terminal differentiation medium, they retain a more progenitor satellite-like cell state than 2D cultures.

Additionally, we compared myogenic progenitors from iPSCs after secondary differentiation with CD56+/CD82+ human primary myoblasts, a highly myogenic population, purified as previously described [53] (Supplementary Methods and Supplementary Figure S4a). The purified myoblasts are predominantly PAX7+ and MYOD+ cells (Supplementary Figure S4b) and were differentiated for 5 days using the same protocol as for myogenic progenitors from iPSC. The myoblasts differentiation generated elongated multinucleated MyHC+ myotubes, herein referred as pMYOT (Supplementary Figure S4c).

PAX7 expression was markedly reduced in pMYOT compared to iPSC-derived skeletal muscles (Fig. 4b). MYF5 and MYH3 levels did not differ between WT and DMD in either 2D or iSMS cultures, but both were upregulated in pMYOT (Fig. 4c). MYOD1 was downregulated in iSMS relative to correspondent WT and DMD 2D cultures, and upregulated in pMYOT. Consistently, the expression of MYOG, a terminal differentiation marker, was reduced in DMD compared to WT in both 2D and iSMS cultures. Overall, the expression levels of MYF5, MYOD1, MYOG and MYH3 were higher in pMYOT, confirming a more mature myogenic identity compared to iPSC-derived myotubes.

Expansion, proliferation and differentiation of myogenic progenitors from iSMS

We next tested whether myogenic cells generated in iSMS could be dissociated and expanded in 2D without losing myogenic potential. After SD8, iSMS were replated onto Matrigel and expanded in myoblast growth medium, allowing cells to adhere and migrate out (Fig. 4d). The iSMS-derived and 2D-derived myogenic progenitors were subcultured and frozen for long-term storage. After thawing and expanding, we compared cell proliferation and maintenance of the differentiation capacity of both 2D and iSMS-derived myoblasts from WT and DMD. We found a higher percentage of EdU-positive cells in the myogenic progenitors derived from WT iSMS than all other groups, especially comparing with myogenic progenitors from DMD iSMS (Fig. 4e). In contrast, the percentage of proliferative myogenic cells did not differ between the WT 2D and DMD 2D. Proliferation curves using xCELLigence real-time analysis for 18 days confirmed the higher differences between WT and DMD in cell proliferation of myogenic progenitors derived from iSMS (Fig. 4f). These data suggest that iSMS are a suitable model for evaluating the consequences of DMD pathogenic mutations on the proliferative behavior of iPSC-derived myogenic progenitors.

After secondary differentiation and freezing/thawing cycle, the iSMS-derived myogenic progenitors retained their myogenic potential, similar to what was reported for 2D cultures [9]. Immunocytochemistry for TITIN confirmed that WT and DMD iSMS myogenic progenitors were fusion-competent (Fig. 4g and h). In line with cell proliferation assays, DMD iSMS-derived myotubes contained fewer activated PAX7−MYOD+ cells and more PAX7+MYOD− than WT, indicating a potential defect in MYOD upregulation in response to terminal differentiation medium (Fig. 4i and j).

3D iSMS versus 2D differentiation from DMD patient derived iPSCs

We also aimed to assess how iSMS differentiation occurs in iPSCs from unrelated DMD patients. The iPSCs were produced and characterized accordingly and expressed pluripotent stem cell markers, as confirmed by RT-qPCR of SOX2, NANOG, and OCT4 (Supplementary Figure S5a), and immunofluorescence for OCT4 and SSEA4 (Supplementary Figure S5b). We also confirmed that iPSCs were free of chromosomal imbalances (Supplementary Figure S5c and S5d).

We performed 2D and iSMS differentiation of three iPSC lineages from DMD patients (DMD-A, DMD-B and DMD-C; Supplementary Table S2), and one control (CTRL-1) (Fig. 5a) and evaluated the expression of myogenic markers. Compared to its correspondent 2D culture, DMD-C iSMS was shown to upregulate MYF5, MYOD1, MYOG and MYH3. The iSMS from DMD-A upregulated MYF5, MYOG, and MYH3, whereas DMD-B did not show significant changes in its gene expression profile relative to its corresponding 2D culture, with the exception of MYOD1 downregulation (Fig. 5b). CTRL-1 iSMS demonstrated an upregulation of MYF5, downregulation of MYOD1, and a similar level of other myogenic markers comparing to CTRL-1 2D.

To determine lineage-specific differences, we also compared the CTRL-1 and DMD lineages within each culture condition. In 2D cultures, compared to CTRL-1, PAX7 was downregulated in all three DMD lineages, MYF5 was not different among groups, MYOD1 was downregulated in DMD-B and DMD-C, and MYOG and MYH3 were downregulated only in DMD-B. In iSMS, compared to CTRL-1, MYF5, MYOG and MYH3 were significantly downregulated in DMD-B, but this pattern was the opposite for the DMD-A and DMD-C lineages, that have showed an upregulation of MYOD1, MYOG and MYH3. These findings reinforce that patient-specific iPSCs display heterogeneous behavior during in vitro myogenic differentiation, consistent with previous observations [11]. Notably, the iSMS culture accentuated these differences, revealing that some DMD lines (e.g. DMD-A and DMD-C) were more prone to differentiation, whereas DMD-B exhibited delayed myogenesis. Importantly, despite this variability, successful myogenic induction was achieved for all DMD patient-derived lines.

To further validate the feasibility of this 3D model for the derivation of PAX7+ satellite-like cells from iPSCs, we generated a PAX7 reporter iPSC from patient DMD-C. We showed that progenitors derived from the iSMS reporter DMD-C line were able to differentiate after subculturing (Fig. 5c) and generated a population of YFP+ cells (Fig. 5d). This emphasizes the relevance of 3D culture in generating satellite-like cells from iPSCs from dystrophic patients. Overall, this could be particularly important in the study of DMD, since dystrophin deficiency directly affects SCs fate [15].

Discussion

Modeling genetic diseases with iPSCs has been an exciting approach to uncover the molecular and cellular mechanisms controlling patients’ phenotype [31, 54]. The iPSCs-derived progenitors can successfully recapitulate key aspects of diseases as previously shown for amyotrophic lateral sclerosis [41], Parkinson’s disease [40], Down syndrome [26], Alzheimer’s disease [63], DMD [12], among many others. However, the use of iPSC technology to model muscle diseases is challenging due to poor reproducibility and efficiency, distinct genetic backgrounds, lack of appropriate natural cell–cell and cell–matrix interactions, and of mechanical and physical properties [10, 13]. To overcome some of these limitations, an increasing number of studies have aimed to develop in vitro 3D skeletal muscle models using pluripotent stem cells [22, 32, 33, 45, 48].

DMD, the most severe form of muscular dystrophy, is characterized by progressive skeletal muscle degeneration and SC dysfunction over time [14]. Understanding SC behavior provide new perspectives for the treatment of this devastating disease [17]. Our main question was: Is iSMS a more suitable culture system than 2D for DMD in vitro modeling? To address this question, we generated iPSC-derived muscle spheroids (iSMS) adapted from previously described transgene-free [8, 9] and free-floating culture [22] protocols.

Three weeks were sufficient to derive PAX7+ satellite-like cells, as evidenced by the presence of YFP+ cells and the identification of PAX7 protein and mRNA. This time point was chosen based on previous results indicating that PAX7 peaked approximately at day 21 of primary differentiation [2]. Additionally, floating cultures for long periods (over six weeks) may result in reduced myogenic activity [48]. Our data revealed that primary differentiation of iSMS drastically reduced the expression of myogenic markers MYOD1, MYF5, MYOG and MYH3 while increasing PAX7 expression compared to 2D cultures. Importantly, we detected higher PAX7 mRNA expression in WT compared with DMD iSMS, while this difference was absent in 2D cultures. This finding suggests that the 3D culture system may enhance the detection of intrinsic defects in dystrophic lineages.

Although we observed this difference in PAX7 expression, the proportion of SCs in iSMS was similar between WT and DMD. Correspondingly, a previous study employing 2D culture of this DMD iPSC lineage showed no significant differences between WT and DMD in the number of PAX7+ cells [3]. Considering our gene expression data, one hypothesis is that, individually, WT satellite-like cells express higher levels of PAX7 (possibly forming more PAX7High cells) than DMD satellite-like cells. Overall, despite being distinct models, this is in agreement with previous studies showing that dystrophin deficient SCs lose quiescence precociously, whereas the number of SCs is maintained or elevated in dystrophic muscles [29, 46].

When secondary differentiation was performed, a more distinct gene expression pattern was observed: PAX7 was downregulated in DMD 2D as compared to WT 2D, but not in WT iSMS versus DMD iSMS. Expression kinetic analysis revealed that, although the iSMS upregulated some myogenic markers in SD8 compared to primary differentiation D21, PAX7 levels were still stable, while in 2D a faster upregulation of most myogenic markers occurred. In contrast, PAX7 downregulation is expected after long-term differentiation of iPSCs [22, 25], since terminal differentiation medium promotes the activation of satellite-like cells to fuse with existing fibers. Furthermore, it is known that SB-431542 treatment accelerates cell fusion [20]. However, it appears that this kinetics was delayed in iSMS, maintaining cells in a more progenitor-like state. Accordingly, mouse muscle stem cell myospheres can be sustained in a pre-myogenic state and later induced to differentiate into multinucleated myotubes [61].

Our data suggest that D21 of primary differentiation is a more appropriate time point for the analysis or isolation of PAX7+ satellite-like cells produced in the iSMS. Accordingly, a previous study showed that the percentage of quiescent PAX7+/Ki67− cells in skeletal muscle organoids was higher on day 30 than in subsequent time points day 70 and 130 [51].

Improving the production of PAX7+ cells is particularly important for isolating this population for downstream applications. This is relevant given the intrinsic heterogeneity of myogenic differentiation from pluripotent stem cells, which generates additional lineages such as neuroprogenitors and mesenchymal cells, as shown in our flow cytometric characterization and previously reported [62]. Consistent with this variability, our cultures also contained CD56−CD82− fraction representing 26–37% of the cells in iSMS. While this reflects a limitation of this study, similar levers of heterogeneity are commonly reported in iPSC-derived myogenic differentiation protocols. In addition, there is no consensus regarding the most appropriate surface markers to define myogenic identity. Although the CD56+CD82+ subset represents a population strongly enriched for myogenic cells, the single-positive populations (CD56⁺CD82⁻ and CD56⁻CD82⁺) may also include myogenic cells, and the absence of these markers does not necessarily exclude additional myogenic cells within the culture. Moreover, SC are intrinsically heterogeneous [56], and future studies dissecting this diversity are crucial for advancing DMD therapies.

Notch signaling is a highly conserved pathway essential for SCs self-renewal, differentiation, and quiescence, being essential for determining SCs homing in their niche [5, 7, 38]. Through cell–cell interactions, the transmembrane Notch receptors Notch1, Notch2, Notch3 or Notch4 interact with Notch ligands Dll1, Dll3, Dll4, Jag1 or Jag2, thereby activating intracellular molecular events culminating with the expression of the transcription factors of the Hes and Hey family [65]. As Notch signaling depends on cell–cell interaction and directly dictate PAX7 expression [60], we investigated how the type of culture would modulate this pathway. In an exploratory analysis, we found common differentially expressed genes between WT and DMD in iSMS and 2D. Most importantly, our data showed that a greater number of Notch related genes were downregulated in DMD iSMS as compared to WT iSMS than the same comparison in 2D. It is well established that diminished Notch signaling contributes to DMD pathogenic mechanisms [19]. For example, Notch signaling blockade in mouse SCs led to muscular dystrophy phenotype and impaired muscle regeneration [30]. A missense variant in POGLUT1 gene causes autosomal recessive limb-girdle muscular dystrophy with decreased levels of Notch activation and Notch-dependent loss of SCs [50]. When looking for specific canonical Notch components, we noted an upregulation as a consequence of the iSMS culture, in both WT and DMD, suggesting that the Notch pathway may be strongly activated. Our results reveal that iSMS are a good platform for studying DMD and Notch pathway, paving the way for future studies modulating Notch for therapeutic purposes.

In the present study, we also identified that myogenic progenitors from isogenic DMD iSMS displayed reduced cell proliferation, as evidenced by EdU and real-time xCELLigence experiments, whereas no differences were observed in 2D DMD. Several studies have reported proliferative defects in dystrophic primary myoblasts and replicative senescence due to telomere shortening [6, 47, 58]. Escaper GRMD dogs overexpressing Jagged1 displayed greater proliferative capacity [57]. Conversely, another study showed that dystrophic myoblasts exhibited increased proliferation [18]. Even following a freeze–thaw cycle, iSMS-derived myogenic progenitors retained fusion competence. However, compared with WT, DMD iSMS exhibited impaired PAX7 cells activation and MYOD+ cells formation at this stage, suggesting defects in progenitor activation and reinforcing their proliferative defects.

Here, we also validated the generation of iSMS using iPSCs from DMD patients. Resembling what we observed for WT versus DMD iSMS, DMD-B exhibited PAX7 mRNA reduction compared to CTRL-1, but this difference was not observed for DMD-A, and DMD-C. A high degree of heterogeneity was observed, with DMD-A and DMD-C exhibiting a more accelerated differentiation, while DMD-B differentiation was delayed. Concordantly, a study using a similar sphere-based myogenic differentiation protocol described different efficacies of four iPSC lines from different donors [48]. Despite the heterogeneity observed, we showed the feasibility of producing iSMS from patients iPSCs independent of the DMD mutation.

Improving the reproducibility of patient-derived iSMS remains an important challenge, considering the clinical and genetic heterogeneity of DMD. Several strategies could help address this variability in future studies. First, the prospective enrichment of iPSC-derived myogenic progenitors through cell sorting may reduce heterogeneity and improve myogenesis [55]. Also, single-cell RNA-seq could be performed to identify cellular composition and transcriptional profile, enabling identification of subpopulations relevant for the differentiation outcome [39]. Furthermore, integrating data from iSMS with clinical features, such as ancestry, DMD mutation, and genetic modifiers, could clarify sources of biological heterogeneity among DMD lineages. The generation of isogenic controls through CRISPR/Cas9-mediated correction of DMD mutations would be another powerful approach, as it enables the direct assessment of dystrophin deficiency while minimizing confounding genetic variability [11]. Finally, incorporating more robust control groups, such as unrelated individuals from diverse ancestries or genetically matched family members lacking pathogenic DMD variants could facilitate the interpretation of patient-specific phenotypes.

Typically, most protocols generate cells by using 2D cultures before transitioning them into a 3D context [23]. Here, we produced myogenic progenitors directly in a 3D environment and demonstrated that they maintained their proliferation and differentiation potential. Our primary differentiation did not involve Matrigel or any type of xenogenic extracellular matrix component, favoring their therapeutic application, since Matrigel exhibits batch-to-batch variability and may contain xenogeneic contaminants [1]. In addition, the originally described sphere-based protocol require spheres dissociation weekly [22], and we speculate that this could disturb the quiescence/activation dynamics of satellite-like cells during differentiation. Our adapted iSMS protocol has the potential to generate myogenic progenitors for use in cell therapy and in the development of 3D muscle constructs for disease modeling. Overall, our data suggests that iSMS is a good platform for studying human DMD, opening new perspectives to understand SCs disease-related dysfunction and to develop novel therapeutic strategies for this devastating muscle disease.

Methods

iPSC lines and patients

CRO wild type (WT) and CRO DMD (DMD) isogenic cell lines have been produced and characterized elsewhere [2, 3]. We introduced a knock-in in exon 1 of PAX7 promoter in the WT and DMD lineages to create PAX7-Venus reporter lineages, as previously described [2].

The iPSCs lines DMD-A and DMD-B were derived from the patient’s skin biopsies, and DMD-C from a blood sample. CTRL-1 was produced from skin biopsies of a healthy male individual and has been characterized previously [34, 37]. Primary myoblasts were kindly provided by Professor Mariz Vainzof from the cell repository of the Human Genome and Stem Cells Research Center (HUG-CELL).

Briefly, fragments from skin biopsies were treated with dispase (1 mg/ml; Sigma-Aldrich) for 24h and migrating fibroblasts were expanded in DMEM High glucose supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 100 µg/ml Normocin. Peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMC) were obtained through whole blood isolation with FicolPaque (Sigma-Aldrich), expanded in StemSpan medium (StemCell Technologies) supplemented with 50 ng/ml of stem cell factor (SCF), 1 µM dexamethasone, 2U/ml erythropoietin (EPO), 10 ng/ml interleukin 3 (IL-3), 40 ng/ml insulin growth factor-1 (IGF-1), and 100 µg/ml normocin in low attachment plates until ready for reprogramming.

Reprogramming and characterization of iPSCs

Fibroblasts or PBMCs were reprogrammed using the Human Dermal Fibroblast Nucleofector™ Kit (Lonza) or Human CD34+ Cell Nucleofector™ Kit (Lonza), according to the manufacturer’s instructions, with the episomal vectors pCXLE-hOCT3/4-shp53-F, pCXLE-hSK, and pCXLE-hUL (Addgene) using NucleofectorTM 2b Device.

After nucleofection, PBMCs were plated on top of a murine embryonic fibroblast layer in hESC medium (DMEM/F12 supplemented with 2mM GlutaMAX-I, 20% knockout serum replacement (KSR), 1% non-essential amino acids (NEAA), 100 µM 2-mercaptoetanol, 100 µg/ml normocin, 2 µM SB431542, 0.5 µM PD0325901, 2 µM thiazovivin, 0.5 mM valproic acid, 0.25 mM sodium butyrate (NaB), 10 ng/ml fibroblast growth factor 2 (FGF2). For fibroblasts, the same medium was used, but thiazovivin and NaB were omited. The emerging iPSCs colonies were isolated and transferred to Matrigel-coated plates in Essential 8™ medium (Thermo Fisher Scientific) supplemented with 5 µM Y-27632. Colonies were expanded using Essential 8 medium with 100 µg/ml normocin. Characterization and differentiation were performed after at least eight passages.

The expression of pluripotency markers NANOG, SOX2, and OCT4 was evaluated using RT-qPCR, and the presence of OCT4 and SSEA4 positive cells was confirmed using immunofluorescence (Supplementary Methods). Multiplex ligation-dependent probe amplification of subtelomeric regions (P036 and P070; MRC-Holland) was performed in iPSC lines as previously described [24] to check for chromosomal imbalances.

Generation of PAX7 reporter lineages

Using CRISPR-Cas9, we performed a knock-in in exon 1 of PAX7 promoter in the WT, DMD and DMD-C lineages, as described [2]. Briefly, iPSCs were transfected with plasmids carrying the 2A-NLS-Venus sequence in frame with exon 1 of PAX7. Edited cells were selected and genotyped.

Differentiation into myogenic progenitors: 2D and iSMS culture

Skeletal muscle differentiation was carried out using a transgene-free protocol [8] that recapitulates the key molecular events of paraxial mesoderm specification, followed by differentiation into skeletal muscle fibers and progenitors.

Briefly, iPSCs were plated in 6 well Matrigel coated plates (20 µg/cm2) at low confluence (2.9 × 105 cells per well) for 2D differentiation. For iSMS, iPSCs were detached using a cell lifter, transferred to 6 well low-attachment plates, and cultivated on an orbital shaker at 65 rpm. This protocol was adapted from a previously described free-floating protocol for deriving EZ spheres from iPSCs [22].

In the first two days, the medium was changed to DMEM F12 supplemented with 2 mM GlutaMAX-I, 1% ITS, 1% NEAA, 0.003 mM CHIR-99021, and 0.0005 mM LDN-193189 (Fig. 1A, CL medium). On day 3, 20 ng/ml FGF2 was added to the CL medium (CLF). From day 6 to 8, the medium was changed to DMEM High glucose, 2 mM GlutaMAX-I, 15% KSR, 1% NEAA, 0.2% PenStrep, 0.1 mM 2-mercaptoetanol, 0.01 µg/ml of hepatocyte growth factor (HGF), 0.002 µg/ml IGF-1, 0.020 µg/ml FGF2, and 0.0005 mM LDN-193189 (KHIFL medium). The factors HGF, FGF2, and LDN were removed from days 8 to 12 (KI), and the cells were treated with medium supplemented with HGF and IGF from days 12 to 21.

On day 21 of differentiation, cells in the 2D experiment were treated with 0.07% Collagenase IV (ColIV) and TrypLE™ Express Enzyme (1X), filtered through a 70 µM cell strainer and resuspended in myoblast growth medium (SKGM-2, Lonza CC-3245) supplemented with 10 µM Y-27632. The skeletal muscle spheroids (iSMS) generated were maintained in low-attachment plates and the medium was changed to SKGM-2 with 10 µM Y-27632. The cells were maintained in SKGM-2 without Y-27632 for approximately 72 h. To perform secondary differentiation, the medium was changed to DMEM/F12 supplemented with 2 mM GlutaMAX-I, 20% KSR, 1% ITS, 0.2% penicillin streptomycin and 10 µM SB431542 and changed every other day for 8 days.

To check the freezing/thawing capacity, after secondary differentiation, cells from 2D and iSMS were cultivated in SKGM-2 medium. The iSMS were allowed to attach to Matrigel-coated plates and migrate out of the iSMS explants. Single cells were frozen in FBS and 10% DMSO. Myogenic progenitors were thawed, seeded on Matrigel-coated plates in SGKM-2 medium, and differentiated into multinucleated fibers using the same protocol as in the secondary differentiation step.

Quantitative reverse transcriptase PCR (RT-qPCR)

Total RNA was extracted on day 21 of primary differentiation using QIAzol Lysis Reagent followed by isolation with RNeasy Mini kit (Qiagen), according to the manufacturer’s protocols. Myogenic progenitors from 2D and iSMS were treated with terminal differentiation medium (secondary differentiation), and samples were collected on day 8.

About 0.5 µg of RNA was utilized for cDNA synthesis using High-Capacity cDNA Reverse Transcription Kit (ThermoFisher). The cDNA was diluted at a 1/20 ratio and the PCR reaction was set up with primers (3 µM) for MYOD1, MYOG, MYF5, MYH3, PAX7, JAG1, NOTCH1, NOTCH2, HES1, HEYL, and NRARP (Supplementary Table S1) and Fast SYBR™ Green Master Mix. The reactions were run on a QuantStudio™ 12K Flex Real-Time PCR System. Data were processed using Expression Suite Software, fold change was calculated using the ∆∆Ct method with WT 2D, CTRL-1 or iPSCs (D0) for normalization, and RPLP0 as endogenous control.

RT array – Notch pathway

RNA samples (0.5 µg) from day 21 of differentiation of WT and DMD iPSC lines from 2D and iSMS cultures were used for cDNA synthesis with RT2 First Strand Kit. Then, master mix with corresponding cDNA was prepared and samples were dispensed in RT2 Profiler PCR Array Human Notch Signaling Pathway Plus (PAHS-059YC, Qiagen), according to the manufacturer’s protocol using Applied Biosystems 7500 Fast Real-Time PCR System. Analysis was performed in RT2 Profiler PCR Data Analysis platform (https://dataanalysis2.qiagen.com/pcr).

Immunofluorescence

iSMS on day 21 of differentiation were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) for 1 h. For some stainings, we embedded iSMS in Optimal Cutting Temperature (O.C.T.) compound, immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen and sectioned using a cryostat. For other staining procedures, we performed the protocol using the whole iSMS.

Samples were permeabilized with 0.5% Triton X-100 in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) for 15 min. Blocking was performed with 30 min of incubation with 0.1% Triton X-100 and 10% FBS in PBS. Samples were incubated with primary antibodies against TITIN (DSHB, 1/300), MYOD (ab64159, 1/300 and ab133627, 1/100), MYF5 (sc302, 1/100), and PAX7 (DSHB, 1/100) overnight at 4 °C. Then, incubation for 2–3h was performed with anti-rabbit AlexaFluor 546 or anti-mouse AlexaFluor 488 (1/1000) secondary antibodies and NucBlue™ Fixed Cell Stain ReadyProbes™ reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Immunostained iSMS were analyzed in glass-bottom dishes or mounted slides using a confocal microscope (LSM 800). Confocal Z-stacks were collected and reconstructed into 3D images using ZEN Blue software (Zeiss) or FIJI (ImageJ 1.54p). FIJI was used for quantitative analysis.

Flow cytometry

On day 21, PAX7-Venus reporter WT, DMD or DMD-C myogenic cells (from 2D and 3D experiments) were treated with ColIV and TryPLE Express, filtered through 70 µm cell strainer and analyzed on LSRFortessa X-20 or BD FACSymphony™ A1. Non-reporter cells were used as the negative controls. A total of 50.000 events were acquired. The flow cytometry data were analyzed using FlowJo X software.

For iSMS characterization, myogenic cells were stained with the following antibodies: PE Mouse Anti-Human CD90 (BD555596); PE Anti-PDGFR alpha antibody [16A1]; Mouse monoclonal IgG1 (ab99921); BD Pharmingen™ APC Mouse anti-Human CD73 (Cat. 560847); BD Pharmingen™ PerCP-Cy™5.5 Mouse anti-Sox2 (Cat. 561506); BD Pharmingen™ PE Mouse Anti-Human CD31 (Cat. 555446); APC anti-CD56 antibody, (BioLegend, Cat. 318310); and PE anti-CD82 antibody (BioLegend, Cat. 342103). Cells were treated with Fix & Perm (Life Technologies), according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Cells stained with the corresponding isotype controls were used as negative controls. Positive controls consisted of cells known to express the corresponding marker. A total of 50.000 events were acquired on BD FACSymphony™ A1 and analyzed using FlowJo X.

Cell proliferation assay

Myogenic progenitors (single cells) derived from the 2D and iSMS protocols were thawed, expanded in SKGM-2 medium, and plated at a density of 2400 cells/cm2. After 4 days, cells were treated with EdU 1 µM for 1 h and detection was performed using the Click-iT® Plus EdU Flow Cytometry Assay Kit, following the manufacturer’s instructions. Cells incubated with EdU only, but not with the reaction cocktail, were used as negative controls. Samples were analyzed using a BD FACSymphony™ A1 Cell Analyzer and at least 20000 events were acquired. FlowJo X software was used for analysis.

Differentiation of myoblasts from 2D and iSMS cultures

After thawing, the cells were expanded in SKGM-2 medium. About 4000 cells were plated per well in Nunc® Lab-Tek® II Chamber Slides™ (Sigma). When confluence was reached, terminal differentiation medium was added and changed every other day, for eight days. Cells were fixed and immunostained with primary antibodies against TITIN, MYOD, or PAX7 followed by secondary antibodies and nuclei staining, as described in the immunostaining section.

Statistical analysis

All experiments were performed at least in triplicate. For the RT-qPCR analysis of WT and DMD isogenic reporter cells, and of patients-derived iSMS, we employed one-way or two-way ANOVA and Tukey’s or Sidak’s posttest. For comparisons between WT and DMD iSMS only, we performed unpaired t-tests. Graphs and statistical analysis were performed using GraphPad Prism version 6.00 for Windows (GraphPad Software, La Jolla, California, USA, www.graphpad.com).
