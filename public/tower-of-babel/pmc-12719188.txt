Effects of ultrasonic treatment and pH shift on the structure, functional properties and in vitro digestion of peanut protein
Authors: Chunyu Li, Chun Hu, Weinong Zhang, Junbo He, Yanpeng Zhang, Lijuan Han
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe growing demand for plant proteins has driven research into optimizing peanut protein extraction and functionality. This study investigated the influence of ultrasonic treatment and pH shift on peanut protein structure, functional properties and in vitro digestion. Results indicated that 30-min ultrasound improved peanut protein extraction rate and purity, while 15–30 min ultrasonic treatment at pH away from 4 reduced its mean particle size and improved its solubility, emulsifying and foaming properties. Additionally, 15–45 min ultrasonic treatment at pH 2–10 decreased peanut protein elastic modulus (G′) and viscous modulus (G″) and increased its apparent viscosity. Moreover, 30-min ultrasound increased peanut protein digestibility and free amino content. Furthermore, ultrasonic treatment changed the peanut protein secondary and tertiary structure and decreased its relative crystallinity. By combining ultrasound and pH shifts, this study provides novel insights into optimizing peanut protein extraction and functionality, facilitating its broader application in the food industry.Keywords: Peanut protein, Ultrasonic treatment, Structure, Functional properties, Rheological properties, Digestibility

Introduction

Peanuts are a crop widely cultivated around the world and account for roughly 11 % of the total global protein consumption (Cui et al., 2023). Peanuts are rich in unsaturated fats and linolenic acid and provide various health-promoting nutrients, including proanthocyanidins, flavonoids and resveratrol (Zhao, Liu, Zhang, Zhu, & Ao, 2019). In terms of dry weight, peanuts contain approximately 30 % crude protein, with a well-rounded amino acid composition, an ideal variety of volatile substances, and a low level of anti-nutritional factors (Zhao et al., 2019).

In recent years, food protein has received extensive attention as a nutrient and stabilizer for colloids systems (Wu, Liu, Li, & Feng, 2024; Feng et al., 2025). Among the various food protein sources, peanut protein stands out as a high-quality option, boasting a digestibility of over 90 %, a biological value of 59 %, and a protein utilization rate of 51 %, making it more readily absorbed and nutritionally superior compared to soy protein (Amelia et al., 2020). Moreover, prior studies suggest that peanut protein offers nutritional benefits comparable to those of animal protein (Hariharan, Patti, & Arora, 2023; He et al., 2025). For instance, peanut protein powder contained 55 % protein, which was 2.7-fold higher than beef and 2.6-fold higher than chicken as well as 3.8-fold higher than eggs (Zhao, Chen, & Du, 2012). Meanwhile, peanut protein lacked the strong and pungent flavor often associated with soy proteins, making it more versatile and easier to integrate into a broader range of food products (Cui et al., 2023). Furthermore, peanut protein consisted of 10 % water-soluble protein and 90 % salt-soluble protein, with the latter portion comprising approximately 73 % arachidin (14S), 6 % arachidin I (2S), 21 % arachidin II (8S), and other minor components (Sun, Zhang, Zhang, Tian, & Chen, 2020), which enabled it to form gels and foams. Nevertheless, its limited solubility and poor emulsifying properties restrict its widespread use in the food field, suggesting the necessity to solve this problem by improving its physicochemical and functional properties.

As a non-thermal food processing technique, ultrasonic treatment is extensively utilized in the food field to modify protein structures through mechanisms such as cavitation and shear forces (Chen et al., 2024). For instance, high-intensity ultrasound can enhance solubility as well as foaming and emulsifying characteristics of giant squid protein and mussel sarcoplasmic protein (Arredondo-Parada, Torres-Arreola, Suarez-Jimenez, Ramirez-Suarez, & Juarez-Onofre, 2020). Additionally, ultrasonic treatment has been shown to improve the functional characteristics and digestibility of chickpea protein isolate (Kang, Zhang, Guo, Lei, & Yang, 2022). Moreover, an appropriate ultrasound intensity has been reported to markedly increase protein solubility and surface hydrophobicity, decrease total sulfydryl (T-SH) and free sulfydryl (free-SH) content, and enhance both emulsification characteristics and digestion rate (Li, Qi, Zhang, & Li, 2023). Furthermore, ultrasound has been used to alter the structure of soybean isolate protein-hyaluronic acid complexes and enhance the stability of their loaded astaxanthin emulsions in a suitable range (Huang et al., 2024; Feng et al., 2025). However, systematic research remains limited regarding the influence of ultrasonic treatment on the extraction rate, structure, functionality, rheological properties and in vitro digestion of peanut protein.

Given that peanut protein is an amphiphilic biological macromolecule, its functional properties are significantly influenced by the solution pH. Therefore, the main objective of the present study is to investigate the combined effects of ultrasound and pH treatment on the peanut protein structure, functional properties, rheological properties and in vitro digestion. To achieve this goal, the impacts of ultrasound and pH treatment on peanut protein structure were analyzed through surface hydrophobicity, tertiary structure, functional group change and crystalline structure. Additionally, the influence of ultrasound and pH treatment on peanut protein surface and functional characteristics were investigated in terms of particle size, zeta-potential, solubility, emulsifying and foaming properties. Moreover, the rheological and digestive characteristics of ultrasound-treated peanut protein were also evaluated. This study offers a scientific basis for the use of ultrasonic and pH-modified peanut protein in the food field.

Materials and methods

Materials

Skimmed peanut meal was obtained from Dongzhuang Oil Mill (Ningyang County, Shandong, China). Hexyl hydride, sulfuric acid, boric acid, ethanol, potassium bromide, glycine, sodium dodecyl sulfonate, 8-aniline-1-naphthalenesulfonic acid, sodium hydroxide (NaOH), hydrochloric acid (HCl) and methanol were of analytical grade and purchased from Sinopharm Group Chemical Reagent Co., LTD (Shanghai, China). Sodium dodecyl sulfate polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) gel rapid preparation kit and bicinchoninic acid (BCA) protein assay kit were bought from Biyuntian Biotechnology Co., LTD (Shanghai, China). Artificial gastric juice (10 g/L pepsin) and artificial intestinal juice (10 g/L trypsin) were provided by Dongwan Chuangfeng Automation Technology Co., LTD (Guangdong, China). Analytical-grade phthalaldehyde and Coomassie brilliant blue were bought from Aladdin Reagent Co., LTD (Shanghai, China). Analytical-grade β-mercaptoethanol was provided by MacLean Biochemical Technology Co., LTD (Shanghai, China).

Ultrasonic assisted extraction of peanut protein

Briefly, the mold-free peanut meal was ground and passed through an 80-mesh sieve, followed by degreasing using n-hexane at a solid-to-liquid ratio of 1:15 (w/w). Next, peanut protein was isolated from the defatted peanut meal using the alkali solution-acid precipitation method, with ultrasonic treatment (L-USC-6L, Beijing Lanjieke Technology Co., LTD) applied at 120 W and 40 kHz for 0, 15, 30 and 45 min, respectively. Subsequently, the extracted peanut protein samples were washed, dialyzed for 24 h, lyophilized and stored at 4 °C for subsequent use. Finally, the peanut protein extraction rate was measured by dividing the weight of lyophilized protein by the weight of peanut meal.

Purity determination of peanut protein

The peanut protein purity was determined as described by AOAC. The protein content was calculated by nitrogen content (N) × 5.3 (nitrogen conversion factor) (Hu, Xiong, Xiong, Chen, & Walayat, 2020). Measurements for each sample were performed in triplicate.

Rheological behaviors determination of peanut protein

The influences of ultrasonic treatment (0, 15, 30 and 45 min; 120 W; 40 kHz) on peanut protein rheological behaviors were determined using a multifunctional rheometer (TA Instruments, UK) as reported by He et al. (2024). Different peanut protein solutions at pH 2, 4, 6, 8 and 10 were prepared separately using 0.1 mol/L phosphoric acid buffer solution. Subsequently, the changes in the elastic modulus (G′) and viscous modulus (G″) of peanut protein samples over time were determined through frequency scanning in a range of 0.1 to 100 Hz and shear strain of 1 %. Additionally, the peanut protein apparent viscosity was determined as a function of shear rate in the range from 0.1 to 1000 s−1.

Digestive properties measurement of peanut protein

Protein digestibility

In vitro simulated digestion of ultrasound-treated peanut protein was analyzed using simulated gastric fluid (SGF) and simulated intestinal fluid (SIF) as described by Choi and Chin (2024). Each 10 mL of the SGF solution was transferred into a centrifuge tube and preheated in a 37 °C water bath, followed by adding the liquid sample (5 mg/mL, 10 mL) and adjusting the final pH to 3.0 using 1 mol/L hydrochloric acid. Then, the mixture was transferred to a constant-temperature shaker at 37 °C and oscillated continuously at 110 rpm to simulate gastric digestion. At 0, 30, 60, 90 and 120 min, 1 mL of the sample was collected for analysis. The reaction was terminated by adding 100 μL of 1 mol/L NaOH.

Next, each 10 mL of gastric digestive fluid was added to 10 mL of simulated intestinal fluid (SIF), followed by adjusting the pH to 7.0 using 1 mol/L NaOH. After transferring the mixture to a shaker at 37 °C and oscillating it continuously at 110 rpm for 2 h, each 1 mL of the sample was collected separately at 30, 60, 90 and 120 min, followed by boiling the solution for 10 min to terminate the digestion process. All digested samples were centrifuged at 8000 rpm and 4 °C for 15 min. The concentration of soluble protein in the supernatant of digested samples was measured by bicinchoninic acid (BCA) protein assay.

Free amino group of peanut protein

The peanut protein free amino group was measured as reported by Yang et al. (2022). Briefly, the o-phthalaldehyde (OPA) reagent was prepared by dissolving 80 mg of phthalaldehyde in 2 mL of methanol, followed by mixing it with sodium tetraborate (100 mM, 50 mL) and sodium dodecyl sulfate (20 % w/w, 5 mL), neutralizing with 100 μL of β-mercaptoethanol, and diluting to 100 mL with distilled water. For the assay, the digested sample was diluted 10-fold and combined with the OPA reagent at a 1:20 ratio (v/v). After incubation at 37 °C for 2 min, the absorbance of the mixture was determined at 340 nm with an ultraviolet (UV) spectrophotometer (UV-6100, China). The content of free amino groups was computed using a standard curve generated with l-serine.

Protein molecular weight

Briefly, the digested sample solution was mixed with the protein loading buffer solution in a 1:4 ratio (v/v) and then incubated in boiling water for 5 min to denature the proteins. Next, the samples were loaded onto a polyacrylamide gel, and electrophoresis was run at 70 mV in the stacking gel and 120 mV in the separating gel until full migration of the dye front to the gel bottom. Once the electrophoresis concluded, the gel was immersed in Coomassie Brilliant Blue solution for 30 min to reveal the protein bands, followed by rinsing with a destaining solution to remove excess stain and improve band clarity. Finally, the protein subunit bands were observed and documented under appropriate lighting conditions for analysis (Yang et al., 2022).

Surface hydrophobicity determination of peanut protein

The influence of ultrasonic treatment (0, 15, 30 and 45 min; 120 W; 40 kHz) on peanut protein surface hydrophobicity at pH 2, 4, 6, 8 and 10 was determined by the fluorescence spectrophotometer (F-4600, Japan) with the methodology of Hu and Xiong (2022). Each sample solution was diluted to 0.05, 0.10, 0.15, 0.20 and 0.25 mg/mL, followed by transferring 4 mL of diluted sample into separate test tubes, and adding 20 μL of 8-aniline-1-naphthalenesulfonic acid to each tube for 15 min of reaction. The fluorescence measurements were conducted at an excitation wavelength of 390 nm and an emission wavelength of 470 nm, with both slit widths adjusted to 5 nm. Finally, the surface hydrophobicity was measured by the curve slope of fluorescence intensity versus sample concentration.

Ultraviolet spectrum measurement of peanut protein

The impacts of ultrasonic treatment (0, 15, 30 and 45 min; 120 W; 40 kHz) on peanut protein ultraviolet spectrum at pH 2, 4, 6, 8 and 10 were determined by the ultraviolet spectrophotometer (UV-1800, Japan) as outlined by Hu and Xiong (2022) with a small modification. Briefly, the sample was diluted to 0.25 mg/mL using a phosphoric acid buffer solution (0.01 mol/L, pH 7.2), and the adsorption spectra of the mixture was recorded at a sampling interval of 1 nm with a scanning range of 200–800 nm.

Fluorescence spectrum determination of peanut protein

The impacts of ultrasonic treatment (0, 15, 30 and 45 min; 120 W; 40 kHz) on peanut protein fluorescence spectrum at pH 2, 4, 6, 8 and 10 were determined at 400 V by the fluorescence spectrophotometer (F-4600, Japan) as outlined by Hu and Xiong (2022) with a small adjustment. Specifically, the sample was diluted to 0.25 mg/mL using a phosphoric acid buffer solution (0.01 mol/L, pH 7.2), and each sample was measured at the excitation wavelength of 290 nm, and a scanning speed of 1200 nm/min, with slit widths adjusted to 5 nm.

Infrared spectrum measurement of peanut protein

The impacts of ultrasonic treatment (0, 15, 30 and 45 min; 120 W; 40 kHz) on peanut protein Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spectrum at pH 2, 4, 6, 8 and 10 were measured by the infrared spectrometer (NEXUS-470, Nicolet) as described by He et al. (2024). Specifically, each 2 mg of freeze-dried peanut protein sample was combined with potassium bromide and ground in an agate mortar to obtain uniform powder. After compressing an appropriate amount of powder into tablets using a tablet press mold, the resulting thin slices were scanned at 4000–400 cm−1 with a resolution of 4 cm−1 and 32 scans.

X-ray diffraction determination of peanut protein

The impact of ultrasonic treatment (0, 15, 30 and 45 min; 120 W; 40 kHz) on peanut protein X-ray diffraction pattern at pH 2, 4, 6, 8 and 10 was measured by the X-ray diffraction instrument (D/Max-IIIA, Japan) with the methodology of He et al. (2024). The lyophilized samples were ground into powder, and the experiment was conducted in the range of 5–60° at a scanning rate of 10°/min.

Microstructure observation of peanut protein

The impact of ultrasonic treatment (0, 15, 30 and 45 min; 120 W; 40 kHz) on peanut protein surface microscopic morphology at pH 2, 4, 6, 8 and 10 was determined by a scanning electron microscope (SEM) at 2 kV (ZEISS Company, Germany). Briefly, a trace amount of powder was fixed onto the copper conductive plate using double-sided tape, followed by gold sputtering for 45 s. Each sample was scanned and photographed at a magnification of 1000 (Hu et al., 2020).

Statistical analysis

The experimental data analysis was performed by mainly using SPSS software 17.0 for Duncan's multiple comparisons. All experiments were performed in triplicate and the experimental data were presented as: average value ± standard deviation. A statistically significant difference between the groups was identified at a significance level of p < 0.05.

Results and discussion

Analysis of peanut protein surface properties

Peanut protein extraction rate and purity under ultrasonic treatment

Ultrasonic treatment can improve protein extraction rate through cavitation effect and mechanical vibration. Fig. 1A presents the influence of ultrasonic treatment on the peanut protein extraction rate and purity. The protein extraction rate was shown to be 18.91 % by conventional alkali-soluble acid-precipitated method and was significantly increased to 20.30 % under ultrasound treatment from 0 to 45 min. This result could be attributed to the propagation of ultrasonic waves through liquids, generating high-frequency pressure fluctuations, inducing cavitation effects, and enabling the formation, expansion and collapse of microscopic bubbles, ultimately disrupting cell walls and accelerating peanut protein release (Vilkhu, Mawson, Simons, & Bates, 2008).

Effects of ultrasonic treatment on extraction rate and purity (A) of peanut protein, as well as its zeta-potential (B), particle size (C), solubility (D), emulsifying activity (EAI) and emulsifying stability (ESI) (E), and foaming activity (FC) and foaming stability (FS) (F) as a function of pH with 2, 4, 6, 8 and 10. Different small letters indicate significant difference between different samples at p < 0.05.

Additionally, the purity of peanut protein extracted by conventional alkali-soluble acid-precipitated method was 91.78 % in contrast to 95.32 % under ultrasound treatment from 0 to 30 min. This was probably attributed to disruption of peanut cell walls under ultrasonic treatment through cavitation effects and mechanical vibrations, promoting the efficient release of proteins while minimizing the co-extraction of impurities, thereby enhancing protein separation efficiency and significantly improving the peanut protein purity (Vilkhu et al., 2008). As ultrasonic treatment was prolonged to 45 min, the peanut protein purity significantly decreased to 89.06 %. A possible explanation is that prolonged ultrasonic treatment may rupture cell structures completely, leading to the release of more non-target components (such as lipids, polysaccharides or impurities) and their co-extraction with proteins, thereby reducing the peanut protein purity (Das, Panesar, & Saini, 2022).

Peanut protein zeta-potential and particle size under ultrasonic and pH treatment

Zeta-potential is very important for analyzing the dispersion and aggregation behavior of colloidal particles (Chen & Zhang, 2023). With pH increasing from 2 to 10, the peanut protein zeta-potential decreased from 13.59 to −15.33 mV with a net charge of zero at pH 4.5. This might be attributed to the protonation or deprotonation of carboxyl and amino groups on the peanut protein surface at different pH levels, causing the net charge to change from positive to negative (Li et al., 2025). When treated by ultrasound from 0 to 45 min, the zeta-potential at pH 2 showed a gradual decrease from 13.59 to 12.32 mV, probably because ultrasonic treatment caused the redistribution of surface charges and changes in the aggregation state of peanut protein molecules (Chen & Zhang, 2023). Meanwhile, at 45 min of ultrasound and with pH increasing from 2 to 10, the peanut protein zeta-potential decreased from 12.32 to −12.1 mV with a net charge of zero at pH 4.5. The ultrasound-treated peanut protein exhibited a trend consistent with that of the untreated peanut protein. This observation indicated that ultrasonic treatment did not significantly alter the peanut protein surface charge and the noted changes in peanut protein zeta-potential were largely attributed to pH-induced ionization of surface groups.

Particle size is the key parameter that affects protein functions such as solubility, emulsification and foamability. The impacts of ultrasound treatment on peanut protein mean grain size are presented in Fig. 3C. With pH increasing from 2 to 10, the peanut protein mean particle size first increased from 480.83 to 5027.67 nm and then decreased to 456.43 nm. The observed increase in peanut protein mean grain size at pH 4 was likely due to protein aggregation at the isoelectric point. When treated by ultrasound from 0 to 30 min, the peanut protein mean particle size increased from 480.83 to 1001.83 nm, probably due to protein aggregation (Jiao et al., 2025). Meanwhile, with pH increasing from 2 to 10, the peanut protein average grain size reached maximum at pH 6 and then decreased. This might be attributed to alterations in the surface charge distribution or conformation of peanut proteins induced by ultrasonic treatment, making proteins aggregation behavior more significant at pH 6 (Jiao et al., 2025). With ultrasound time further increased to 45 min, the peanut protein mean grain size dropped from 1001.83 to 422.57 nm. With pH increasing from 2 to 10, the peanut protein average grain size reached the maximum (3898.37 nm) at pH 4 and then decreased to 555.83 nm. A possible explanation is that the prolonged exposure to ultrasound generated sustained mechanical shear forces, leading to progressive disruption of peanut protein into smaller particles (Justino et al., 2024).

Analysis of peanut protein functional properties

Peanut protein solubility under ultrasonic and pH treatment

Solubility can affect protein emulsibility and foamability, so it is a very significant functional property for proteins (Wang et al., 2019). Fig. 2A presents the impact of ultrasonic and pH treatment on peanut protein solubility. The peanut protein solubility showed a decrease with pH increasing from 2 to 4, likely owing to the proximity of pH 4 to the isoelectric point of peanut protein, which induced protein self-aggregation. With pH increased from 4 to 10, the peanut protein solution showed an increase. This was probably due to the increase of negative charge on protein molecules as pH rose, which enhanced the electrostatic repulsion between them, reduced the molecular aggregation, and consequently improved solubility (Gao, Shen, Lan, Cui, & Rao, 2020).

Effects of ultrasonic treatment on storage modulus (G′), loss modulus (G″) (A (0 min), B (15 min), C (30 min) and D (45 min)) and apparent viscosity (E (0 min), F (15 min), G (30 min) and H (45 min)) of peanut protein as a function of pH with 2, 4, 6, 8 and 10.

After ultrasonic treatment from 0 to 45 min, the peanut protein solution showed a slow increase. One possible explanation is that ultrasonic treatment caused the protein molecules to expand moderately, exposing more hydrophilic residues that were originally wrapped inside, thereby enhancing the binding ability of protein to water molecules (Jiao et al., 2025). Additionally, the ultrasonic treatment might loosen protein conformation by altering secondary or tertiary structures, thereby enhancing their dispersion and solubility in water (Jiao et al., 2025). With the increase of pH from 2 to 10, the solubility showed the same trend between peanut proteins with and without ultrasound-treatment. This observation indicated that ultrasonic treatment enhanced the solubility of proteins by modifying their aggregation state or conformation but did not significantly affect their pH sensitivity.

Peanut protein emulsifying properties under ultrasonic and pH treatment

Emulsibility is the protein capacity to promote the formation and stabilization of emulsion (Othmeni, Blecker, & Karoui, 2025). Fig. 1E presents the impacts of ultrasonic and pH treatment on peanut protein emulsifying properties. With pH increasing from 2 to 4, the peanut protein emulsifying activity showed a decrease, probably ascribed to peanut protein aggregation at near its isoelectric point (pI). With pH increasing from 4 to 10, the peanut protein emulsifying activity showed an increase. This was probably ascribed to enhanced electrostatic repulsion, promoting protein dispersion and uniform adsorption onto oil droplets, thereby fostering the development of a more stable emulsified layer (Wu, Li, Wu, Wang, & Xie, 2021).

Additionally, with pH increasing from 2 to 4, the peanut protein emulsifying stability showed a decrease from 74.00 min to 24.82 min, probably ascribed to peanut protein aggregation at near the isoelectric point. With pH increasing from 4 to 10, the peanut protein emulsifying stability increased from 24.82 min to 48.01 min. A possible explanation is that when the pH deviated significantly from the isoelectric point, the enhanced protein solubility allowed protein molecules to adsorb more efficiently at the oil-water interface, forming a stable interfacial film, thus enhancing emulsifying stability (Othmeni et al., 2025).

When treated by ultrasound for 15 min, the peanut protein emulsifying activity decreased from 2050.63 to 817.83 m2/g, and with ultrasonic time further increased to 45 min, the emulsifying activity increased to 2442.33 m2/g. This result was probably attributed to a reduction in peanut protein emulsifying activity induced by protein aggregation during the initial stage of ultrasonic treatment (15 min). However, prolonged ultrasonic treatment induced protein depolymerization, reducing the molecular size and enabling rapid adsorption onto the oil-water interface, thereby restoring and enhancing the emulsifying activity (Sun, Zhang, Zhang, Tian, & Chen, 2021).

When treated by ultrasound from 0 to 45 min, the peanut protein emulsifying stability decreased from 74.00 min to 26.45 min. This decrease was probably attributed to the intense mechanical shear forces, cavitation effects, and local high temperatures induced by ultrasonic treatment. Under these conditions, protein molecules can be partially or completely denatured, generating a loose structure and low mechanical strength in the interfacial film, making it difficult to resist oil droplet aggregation and thus reducing emulsifying stability (Sun et al., 2021). With pH increasing from 2 to 10, the peanut protein emulsifying stability decreased first and then increased, agreeing with the trend of the protein untreated with ultrasound. This result suggested that pH played a key role in protein emulsification stability, while ultrasonic treatment may have a relatively small impact on its emulsification stability.

Peanut protein foaming properties under ultrasonic and pH treatment

Proteins foamability plays an important role in the development of foods with stirring and aeration properties (Das et al., 2022). Fig. 1F presents the effects of ultrasonic and pH treatment on peanut protein foaming activity and stability. With pH increasing from 2 to 4, the protein foaming activity and stability exhibited a decrease. A possible explanation is that pH 4 was close to the protein isoelectric point, leading to protein aggregation and a reduction in protein adsorption at the interface (Yuan, Ren, Zhao, Luo, & Gu, 2012). When pH further increased from 4 to 10, the protein foaming capacity and stability slowly increased, suggesting that the peanut protein exhibited high emulsifying activity and stability at pH far from the isoelectric point. This was probably because the increased solubility in peanut protein at this environment can reduce its mean grain size, enabling its quick adsorption in the air/water interface to form an interface film (Yuan et al., 2012).

Under ultrasonic treatment from 0 to 45 min, the peanut protein foaming capacity showed a slow decrease, but the foaming stability exhibited no significant change. A possible explanation is that in the early stage of ultrasonic treatment, gas saturation was relatively high in the liquid, making it easy to form bubbles. However, as the processing time extended, the gas in the liquid was gradually consumed, reducing gas saturation and bubble formation and thus a corresponding decline in foaming activity (Yuan et al., 2012). With pH increasing from 2 to 10, the peanut protein foaming activity and stability declined first and then increased, agreeing with the result without ultrasound treatment, indicating that ultrasound did not change the essential structure of peanut protein.

Peanut protein rheological properties under ultrasonic and pH treatment

The conformational changes and intermolecular interactions of proteins can be gained by analyzing rheological properties, thereby elucidating the intricate relationship between structure and function (Hu & Xiong, 2022). Fig. 2 presents the effects of ultrasonic and pH treatment on peanut protein elastic modulus (G′) and viscous modulus (G″). G′ was greater than G″ for all samples, suggesting their gel-like properties. When pH increased from 2 to 10, both G′ and G″ decreased in all samples, probably because hydration and changes in ionic strength further weakened interactions between protein molecules (He et al., 2024).

When treated by ultrasound from 0 to 45 min, both G′ and G″ exhibited a decrease in all samples. This result was possibly attributed to ultrasound-induced cavitation effect and mechanical shear forces, which disrupted the protein three-dimensional network structures (such as secondary, tertiary or quaternary structures), leading to depolymerization or partial denaturation of peanut protein molecules and ultimately a reduction in elastic modulus (G′) and viscous modulus (G″) (Lin et al., 2020). With pH increasing from 2 to 10, both G′ and G″ exhibited the same trend between ultrasonic treated and untreated proteins. This result indicated that protein conformational changes were mainly influenced by pH rather than ultrasonic treatment.

The influence of ultrasound and pH treatment on peanut protein apparent viscosity is presented in Fig. 2, where the apparent viscosity of all samples is seen to decline with increasing shear rate, indicating their pseudoplastic fluid characteristics. Additionally, the peanut protein apparent viscosity exhibited pH-dependent variations, with a decrease as pH increased from 2 to 4, followed by an increase at pH 6, and then a gradual decrease from pH 6 to 10. This might be ascribed to changes in protein charge state, molecular interactions and structural conformations in different pH ranges (Lin et al., 2020).

Under ultrasound treatment from 0 to 45 min, peanut protein exhibited an increase in apparent viscosity. This phenomenon could be ascribed to the combined effects of ultrasonic treatment, such as inducing the expansion and aggregation of protein molecules, enhancing their hydration capacity, and promoting homogenization of the solution, thereby strengthening intermolecular interactions, increasing flow resistance, and ultimately causing a rise in apparent viscosity (Hu & Xiong, 2022). When pH increased from 2 to 10, the apparent viscosity showed a similar trend between ultrasonic treated and untreated peanut proteins, indicating that protein conformational changes were mainly influenced by pH.

Peanut protein digestive properties under ultrasonic treatment

Ultrasonic treatment can modify protein structure and functionality, improve its digestibility and bioavailability, and offer a scientific foundation for advancing efficient and nutritious food processing technologies (Dai et al., 2024). Fig. 3A shows the impact of ultrasonic treatment on peanut protein digestive properties. During the gastric digestion stage (0–120 min), peanut protein digestibility showed a slow increase, which can be attributed to the breakdown of peanut protein into smaller polypeptide fragments under the action of pepsin (Dai et al., 2024). During the intestinal digestion stage (120–240 min), peanut protein digestibility reached its maximum and gradually stabilized. A possible explanation is that in artificial simulated intestinal fluid, digestive enzymes such as trypsin and chymotrypsin acted on proteins, breaking them down into polypeptides and amino acids. As the reaction progressed, the binding sites of enzymes and proteins gradually became occupied, causing the reaction rate to reach its maximum and the digestibility to increase rapidly (Jiang, Zhao, Nian, Wu, & Li, 2021). With the progress of digestion, peanut proteins were gradually hydrolyzed into polypeptides and amino acids, leading to a reduction in both substrate concentration and enzyme-substrate binding opportunities, and ultimately causing the digestion rate to slow down until reaching reaction equilibrium and digestibility stabilization (Jiang et al., 2021).

Fig. 3B presents the impact of ultrasonic treatment on peanut protein free amino groups. During the gastric digestion stage (0–120 min), the peanut protein free amino acids showed a slow increase, indicating that protein hydrolysis was relatively slow in the gastric juice. This was likely due to the acidic environment and limited pepsin activity during the simulated gastric digestion stage, where the relatively short digestion time resulted in the generation of fewer free amino groups during protein hydrolysis (Choi & Chin, 2024). During the intestinal digestion stage (120–240 min), the peanut protein free amino acids showed a significant increase. This increase was likely ascribed to the neutral or weakly alkaline conditions, coupled with the synergistic effects of trypsin and bile salts, as well as the prolonged digestion period, which collectively led to a substantial increase in free amino groups through protein hydrolysis (Choi & Chin, 2024). During ultrasonic treatment from 0 to 30 min, the peanut protein free amino acids increased during the digestion process, but decreased when the ultrasonic treatment was extended to 45 min. This might be attributed to that in the initial ultrasonic treatment stage (0 to 30 min), ultrasonic waves enhanced the enzymatic hydrolysis efficiency by disrupting the protein structure and thus promoted the release of free amino acids. However, with the time further extended from 30 to 45 min, excessive ultrasonic treatment may lead to protein aggregation and enzyme activity inhibition, thereby reducing the content of free amino acids (Liu, Dong, Shen, Shi, & Ou, 2021).

Fig. 3 displays the molecular weight changes of peanut proteins in different samples after gastrointestinal digestion. Before digestion, the protein was dispersed across various bands, each corresponding to different molecular weights. After digestion with gastric juice (0–120 min), peanut protein was partially degraded, and the resulting protein fragments were observed as bands at 10–20 kDa. After digestion in intestinal fluid, only a small portion of protein retained a faint band at 10 kDa, probably due to the breakdown of proteins into small-molecular-weight polypeptides and amino acids under the action of pancreatic enzymes (Liu et al., 2021). This was consistent with the result of free amino groups (Fig. 3B). After ultrasonic treatment (0–45 min), peanut protein was partially degraded, with most remaining proteins concentrated at 10–15 kDa. After digestion with gastric juice (0–120 min), only a small portion of the proteins retained a faint band at 10 kDa, probably due to degradation of peanut protein under the action of pepsin. Additionally, after digestion in intestinal fluid, the protein bands almost completely disappeared, indicating that ultrasound accelerated peanut protein hydrolysis. A possible explanation is that ultrasonic treatment could significantly improve protein hydrolysis efficiency by destroying the peanut protein structure, increasing the cleavage sites, promoting the enzymatic hydrolysis reaction, and thus increasing the yield of free amino groups (Liu et al., 2021). This conclusion can be supported by the results of free amino groups (Fig. 3B).

Analysis of peanut protein structure changes

Peanut protein surface hydrophobicity under ultrasonic and pH treatment

Surface hydrophobicity refers to the ability of hydrophobic amino acid residues exposed on the surface of protein molecules to interact with water molecules (Xue et al., 2018). Fig. 4A presents the effects of ultrasonic and pH treatment on peanut protein surface hydrophobicity. With pH increasing from 2 to 6, the surface hydrophobicity showed a significant decrease, probably due to the change of peanut protein from unfolding to folding and the encapsulation of hydrophobic groups (Xue et al., 2018). With pH further increased from 6 to 10, the surface hydrophobicity showed no great change, most likely owing to the stable protein conformational structure, its saturated charge distribution, the predominant influence of polar groups, and its stable aggregation behavior (Xue et al., 2018).

Effects of ultrasonic treatment on surface hydrophobicity (A), ultraviolet spectra (B (0 min), C (15 min), D (30 min) and E (45 min)) and fluorescence spectra (F (0 min), G (15 min), H (30 min) and I (45 min)) of peanut protein as a function of pH with 2, 4, 6, 8 and 10. Different small letters indicate significant difference between different samples at p < 0.05.

With ultrasonic time extended from 0 to 30 min, the peanut protein surface hydrophobicity showed a decrease from 7593.1 to 7096.6. This decrease can be attributed to that ultrasound promoted the interaction between protein molecules and water molecules and enhanced solvation, thus increasing surface hydrophilicity and decreasing hydrophobicity (Jiang et al., 2014). As ultrasound time further increased to 45 min, the surface hydrophobicity exhibited an increase. A possible explanation is that.

in the later ultrasound stage, the enhancement of hydrophobic interactions and hydrogen bonding between protein molecules promoted protein aggregation, thereby exposing more hydrophobic groups on the surface (Jiang et al., 2014). With pH increasing from 2 to 10, the surface hydrophobicity showed the same change trend between ultrasonic-treated and untreated peanut proteins, suggesting that ultrasonic treatment did not destroy protein essential structure.

Peanut protein ultraviolet spectrum under ultrasonic and pH treatment

Changes in protein ultraviolet absorption spectra can reflect the microenvironment of aromatic amino acids, so they are usually used to evaluate whether ultrasonic treatment induces unfolding, folding or aggregation, thereby revealing conformational changes in the protein (He et al., 2024). Fig. 4 shows the effects of ultrasonic and pH treatment on peanut protein ultraviolet absorption spectra. The peanut protein exhibited an increase in ultraviolet absorption spectral intensity in the pH range of 2–4, probably ascribed to the exposure of aromatic amino acids. However, in the pH range of 4 to 10, the peanut protein exhibited a decrease in the maximum ultraviolet absorption intensity. A possible explanation is that pH increase may cause protein molecules to change gradually from a partially folded state to a more extended or loose conformation, thereby reducing the exposure of tryptophan amino acids and weakening the ultraviolet absorption intensity (He et al., 2024). Meanwhile, with pH increasing from 2 to 10, a red shift occurred in the maximum ultraviolet absorption intensity, indicating the exposure of aromatic amino acids to a more hydrophilic environment.

When treated by ultrasound from 0 to 30 min (Fig. 4D), the peanut protein exhibited a decrease in the maximum ultraviolet absorption intensity. This was likely attributed to ultrasonic-induced cavitation and shear forces, which may partially unfold the peanut protein tertiary and quaternary structures, exposing internal hydrophobic groups and aromatic amino acids to favor ultraviolet absorption near 280 nm, however, this structural unfolding altered the microenvironment, leading to a reduction in absorption capacity and ultimately lowering the maximum ultraviolet absorption intensity (Li et al., 2025). With ultrasound time further prolonged to 45 min (Fig. 4E), the peanut protein exhibited a rise in the maximum ultraviolet absorption peak intensity. This was likely ascribed to prolonged ultrasonic treatment, which could further expand the peanut protein secondary and tertiary structures, enabling the exposure of more aromatic amino acids and a strong ultraviolet absorption capacity, thereby increasing the light absorption intensity (Li et al., 2025). Additionally, under ultrasound treatment from 0 to 45 min, no great change was observed in the position of the peanut protein maximum absorption peak. Moreover, with pH increasing from 2 to 10, the ultraviolet absorption intensity remained consistent between ultrasonic-treated and untreated peanut proteins, indicating that ultrasound treatment did not alter the essential structure of peanut protein.

Peanut protein fluorescence spectrum under ultrasonic and pH treatment

Fluorescence spectroscopy, particularly endogenous fluorescence spectroscopy, can sensitively reflect microenvironment changes of aromatic amino acids in proteins (Jiang et al., 2014). The influence of ultrasonic and pH treatment on fluorescence spectrum of peanut protein is shown in Fig. 4. With pH increasing from 2 to 4, the peanut protein exhibited a reduction in maximum fluorescence emission intensity, probably due to the aggregation of a large amount of peanut protein at pH 4, leading to the exposure of tryptophan residues (Jiang et al., 2014). With pH further increasing from 4 to 10, the peanut protein exhibited an increase in maximum fluorescence emission intensity. This was likely induced by the conformation unwinding of proteins with increasing pH, exposing aromatic amino acids to a more hydrophilic environment and resulting in a rise in maximum fluorescence emission intensity (Wang et al., 2024). Additionally, with pH increasing from 2 to 10, the position of maximum fluorescence emission peak transitioned from 340 to 330 nm. This transition was likely attributed to the increase of pH, which may induce the unfolding of protein conformations, thereby exposing aromatic amino acids (tryptophan) to a more hydrophobic environment (Wang et al., 2024).

With the extension of ultrasonic time from 0 to 15 min, the maximum fluorescence emission peak of peanut protein exhibited a gradual rise (Fig. 4G). This was likely due to ultrasonic treatment, which exposed tryptophan to a hydrophilic surrounding, reducing energy loss, and enhancing the fluorescence emission intensity (Jiang et al., 2014). With ultrasonic time further increased to 45 min (Fig. 4I), the peanut protein exhibited a decrease in maximum fluorescence emission peak. This was likely attributed to changes in protein conformation after long-term ultrasonic treatment, decreasing tryptophan exposure and lowering fluorescence emission intensity (Li, Wu, Wang, Li, & Bai, 2020). Meanwhile, with the increase of pH from 2 to 10, the fluorescence emission peak showed a similar change trend between ultrasonic-treated and untreated peanut proteins. A possible explanation is that pH changes primarily drove protein unfolding and exposed aromatic amino acids to a hydrophilic surrounding, reducing energy loss during fluorescence, while ultrasonic treatment, although able to alter protein structure, did not change this fundamental mechanism (Li et al., 2020).

Peanut protein FTIR spectrum under ultrasonic and pH treatment

FTIR spectrum can be used to characterize functional group changes of proteins. Specifically, the characteristic absorption peak of amide acid (3500–3000 cm−1) corresponds to the bending vibration for —NH and —OH groups. Amide I bands with a peak at 1600 cm−1 predominantly stem from C—O stretching vibration, and amide II region at 1500 cm−1 is primarily ascribed to —NH bending vibration (Hu & Xiong, 2022). The influences of ultrasonic and pH treatment on FTIR spectra of peanut protein are presented in Fig. 5. When untreated with ultrasound and at pH 2 (Fig. 5A), the amino stretching vibration peak of peanut protein was 3433.7 cm−1, mainly associated with the stretching vibration of amino groups. A possible explanation is that the increased charge on the protein surface at a pH away from the isoelectric point increased the interaction between protein and water, leading to a rise in the hydrogen bond interaction (Li et al., 2025). Under ultrasound treatment from 0 to 45 min, the peaks corresponding to amino stretching vibration and amide I, II, and III bands shifted to 3412.8 cm−1, 1654.2 cm−1, 1543.2 cm−1 and 1227.7 cm−1, respectively. These shifts could be ascribed to the ultrasound-induced disruption of hydrogen bond network in protein molecules, which altered α-helices and β-sheets of proteins and modified the local chemical environment of amino acids, thereby causing the shifts in absorption peak positions (He et al., 2024). As pH rose from 2 to 10, the FTIR spectra of ultrasound-treated and untreated peanut proteins exhibited similar trends, suggesting that ultrasonic treatment did not destroy the essential structure of peanut protein.

Peanut protein X-ray diffraction under ultrasonic and pH treatment

X-ray diffraction patterns can be used to determine the crystal structure and morphology of protein molecules (Li et al., 2025). Fig. 5 shows the effects of ultrasound and pH treatment on X-ray diffraction patterns of peanut protein. In Fig. 5E, it is shown that at pH 2, peanut protein had a relative crystallinity of 20.6 % with four distinct crystalline peaks at 27.3°, 31.7°, 45.5° and 56.5°, respectively. With the increase of pH from 2 to 6, the relative crystallinity of peanut protein exhibited a decrease from 20.6 % to 7.3 % with three distinct crystalline peaks at 31.7°, 45.5°and 56.5°. A possible explanation is that as pH increased, the orderliness of proteins decreased, and the crystalline peaks in the X-ray diffraction signal may reduce or disappear, manifested as a decline in relative crystallinity (Li et al., 2025). With pH further increasing to 10, the relative crystallinity of peanut protein decreased to 5.4 % without obvious crystalline peaks. This result might be due to the difficulty of protein molecules in assembling into an ordered crystal structure at a high pH value, leading to a decrease in the clarity of crystalline peaks in the X-ray diffraction pattern (Li et al., 2025).

When treated by ultrasound from 0 to 45 min, the relative crystallinity of peanut protein at pH 2 dropped from 20.6 % to 9.0 % (Fig. 5E and H), probably ascribed to the disordered structure of protein molecules caused by ultrasonic treatment, leading to the weakening of crystalline peaks in X-ray diffraction signals (Das et al., 2022). With pH increasing from 2 to 10 (Fig. 5H), the relative crystallinity of peanut protein decreased to 4.7 % and the crystalline peak had almost disappeared. This was likely attributed to the gradual transition of the protein conformation from an ordered to a disordered state, leading to the progressive weakening and eventual disappearance of the crystallization peak (Das et al., 2022). This was in line with the result of peanut proteins untreated by ultrasound (Fig. 5E).

Peanut protein microstructure under ultrasonic and pH treatment

The impact of ultrasonic and pH treatment on peanut protein surface morphology is presented in Fig. 6. As shown in Fig. 6A1–B1, at pH 2 and 4, the surface of peanut protein had a loose and porous structure. This might be attributed to the formation of a large number of positive charges in protein molecules under low pH conditions, enabling a relatively strong electrostatic repulsion between molecules, thus forming a loose porous structure (Das et al., 2022). With pH increasing from 4 to 10 (Fig. 6C1–E1), the peanut protein surface exhibited a flat structure. This result was likely due to protein unfolding induced by the high alkaline pH, which increased the exposure of hydrophobic and sulfhydryl groups, ultimately resulting in extensive protein aggregation and the formation of smooth structural characteristics (Li et al., 2025).

Effects of ultrasonic treatment on microstructure of peanut protein as a function of pH (2 (A1–A4), 4 (B1–B4), 6 (C1–C4), 8 (D1–D4) and 10 (E1–E4)).

When treated with ultrasound from 0 to 45 min, the surface of peanut protein at pH 2 changed from a rough and porous structure to a flat structure (Fig. 6A1–A4). This structure change could be induced by the unfolding of protein molecules under ultrasonic treatment, which exposed hydrophobic groups on peanut protein molecules, allowing them to interact and form larger aggregates during freeze-drying (Xue et al., 2018). With pH increasing from 2 to 6 (Fig. 6A4–C4), the surface of peanut protein changed from a flat structure to a porous structure, probably due to the decrease of protein solubility, making molecules more likely to aggregate or precipitate and resulting in a porous surface structure (Li et al., 2025). With pH increasing from 6 to 10 (Fig. 6D4–E4), the surface of peanut protein formed a smooth structure, consistent with the structure of untreated peanut protein, suggesting that ultrasound did not destroy the essential structure of peanut protein.

Conclusion

This study revealed the impact mechanism of ultrasound and pH treatment on the structure, functionality, rheological behavior and in vitro digestion of peanut protein. 30-min ultrasound was shown to improve the peanut protein extraction rate and purity. Meanwhile, ultrasonic treatment (15–30 min) at a pH value away from 4 (near the isoelectric point) could increase the peanut protein solubility, foaming and emulsifying properties by changing hydrophilicity, tertiary structures and crystallinity. Additionally, ultrasonic treatment (15–45 min) at pH 2–10 could decrease the peanut protein elastic modulus (G′) and viscous modulus (G″) and improve its apparent viscosity. Moreover, 30-min ultrasound could improve the digestibility of peanut protein and the content of free amino groups. This study has laid a theoretical basis for the use of ultrasonic and pH-modified peanut protein in the food industry, particularly in high-protein beverages, dairy alternatives and functional food products.
