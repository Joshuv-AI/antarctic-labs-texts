Quasi-immanants


Abstract
For an integer partition λ \lambda of n n and an n × n n\times n matrix A A , consider the expansion of the immanant Imm λ ​ ( A ) \text{Imm}^{\lambda}(A) as a sum indexed by permutations σ \sigma of order n n , with coefficients given by the irreducible characters χ λ ​ ( ctype ​ ( σ ) ) \chi^{\lambda}(\text{ctype}(\sigma)) of the symmetric group S n S_{n} , for the cycle type ctype ​ ( σ ) ⊢ n \text{ctype}(\sigma)\vdash n of σ \sigma . Skandera et al. have introduced combinatorial interpretations of a generalization of immanants given by replacing the coefficient χ λ ​ ( ctype ​ ( σ ) ) \chi^{\lambda}(\text{ctype}(\sigma)) with preimages with respect to the Frobenius morphism of elements among the distinguished bases of the algebra Sym of symmetric functions. Since Sym is contained in the algebra QSym of quasisymmetric functions, this leads us to further generalize immanants with the use of quasisymmetric functions. Since bases of QSym are indexed by integer compositions, we make use of cycle compositions in place of cycle types to define the family of quasi-immanants introduced in this paper. This is achieved through the use of the quasisymmetric power sum bases due to Ballantine et al., and we prove a combinatorial formula for the coefficients arising in an analogue, given by a special case of quasi-immanants associated with quasisymmetric Schur functions, of second immanants.

1
Introduction

Matrix functions such as determinants and permanents form a fundamentally important part of advanced linear algebra. Matrix
functions known as
immanants
were introduced in 1934
[
18
]
and generalize both determinants
and permanents in a way that may be seen as forming a bridge between linear algebra and algebraic combinatorics. This generalization
due to Littlewood and Richardson is defined with the use of the irreducible characters of symmetric groups, in such a way so that every
integer partition of
n
gives rise to an immanant for a given
n\times n
matrix. Immanants have important applications in many
different areas such as algebraic graph theory, and this motivates research endeavors based on the introduction of and application of
generalizations of immanants. Previously, we introduced a generalization of immanants using the characters of diagram algebras
containing symmetric group algebras
[
5
]
. In contrast, we consider, in our current paper, generalizing immanants
using an extension of symmetric
functions
, as opposed to symmetric
groups
.

An integer partition is a finite tuple of weakly decreasing positive integers. For an integer partition
\lambda
, if the sum of the entries
of
\lambda
equals
n
, we write
\lambda\vdash n
, and we let the order
|\lambda|
of
\lambda
be equal to
n
. We let the
length
\ell(\lambda)
of
\lambda
refer to the number of its entries, writing
\lambda=(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},\ldots,\lambda_{\ell(\lambda)})
. For a permutation
\sigma
in the symmetric group
S_{n}
, we let
\text{ctype}(\sigma)
denote the
partition of
n
obtained by sorting the cycle lengths of
\sigma
. For
\lambda\vdash n
, we also let
\chi^{\lambda}(\mu)
denote
the value of the irreducible character
\chi^{\lambda}
of
S_{n}
evaluated at any permutation
\sigma\in S_{n}
such that

\text{ctype}(\sigma)=\mu
. For an
n\times n
matrix
A=[a_{i,j}]_{1\leq i,j\leq n}
and for
\lambda\vdash n
, the

immanant

\text{Imm}^{\lambda}
may then be defined so that

\text{Imm}^{\lambda}(A)=\sum_{\sigma\in S_{n}}\chi^{\lambda}(\text{ctype}(\sigma))\prod_{i=1}^{n}a_{i,\sigma_{i}},

(1)

again with reference to Littlewood and Richardson’s paper
[
18
]
. Skandera et al.
[
7
,
28
]
have explored combinatorial applications of extensions of (
1
)
based on the transition matrices between the usual bases of the algebra of symmetric functions. We further extend such immanant
functions, with the use of quasisymmetric functions, as opposed to symmetric functions.

We let power sum generators be given by formal sums of the form
p_{n}=\sum_{i\geq 1}x_{i}^{n}
for indeterminates
x_{i}
for

i\geq 1
, referring to MacDonald’s text on symmetric functions for details
[
19
]
. We then write

p_{\mu}=p_{\mu_{1}}p_{\mu_{2}}\cdots p_{\mu_{\ell(\mu)}}

(2)

for an integer partition
\mu
. We then let expressions of the form
s_{\lambda}
for
\lambda\vdash n
be defined by taking formal
linear combinations so that

s_{\lambda}=\frac{1}{n!}\sum_{\sigma\in S_{n}}\chi^{\lambda}(\text{ctype}(\sigma))p_{\text{ctype}(\sigma)}.

(3)

Expressions of the form
s_{\lambda}
are referred to as
Schur functions
and are of vital importance within algebraic combinatorics.
The work of Skandera et al.
[
7
,
28
]
concerns generalizations of (
1
)
using power sum expansions for the usual bases of symmetric functions, and, in contrast, we generalize (
1
) with
the use of the
quasisymmetric
power sums introduced by Ballantine et al.
[
1
]
.

For a positive integer
n
, adapting notation from the work of Skandera
[
28
]
, we define the Frobenius map
\text{Frob}=\text{Frob}_{n}
(cf.
[
25
, §4.7]
) so that it maps an element
\theta
in a space of symmetric group traces to
the symmetric function

\theta\mapsto\frac{1}{n!}\sum_{\sigma\in S_{n}}\theta(\sigma)p_{\text{ctype}(\sigma)},

(4)

observing how this can be thought of as generalizing (
3
), in the sense that
\chi^{\lambda}\mapsto s_{\lambda}
.
The Frobenius map in (
4
) provides both an isometry and an algebra isomorphism between the space of class
functions on symmetric groups and symmetric functions, referring to Sagan’s text for details
[
25
, §4.7]
. Since immanants
can be defined in a natural way according to (
3
), this leads to combinatorial topics and problems on the
generalization of immanants using the Froebnius mapping in (
4
), and this was considered in an equivalent way in the
work of Skandera et al.
[
7
,
28
]
. Since the Frobenius map is of fundamental importance in
terms of how it forms connections between symmetric functions and the representation theory of the symmetric group, the foregoing
points lead us to generalize immanants using a generalization of symmetric functions.

Informally, since the algebra
QSym
of quasisymmetric functions is such that its bases are indexed by integer compositions,
whereas the bases of
Sym
are indexed by integer partitions, it can be thought of as being advantageous to employ a

cycle composition
analogue of partitions of the form
\text{ctype}(\sigma)\vdash n
involved in the above definition of

\text{Imm}^{\lambda}
, in the following sense. Since an integer composition is not necessarily equal to the tuple obtained by
permuting its entries, cycle compositions, as defined in Section
3
, can be thought of as “containing more
information” about permutations relative to partitions of the form
\text{ctype}(\sigma)
.

2
Background

For power sum generators as defined above in the set
\mathbb{Q}[\![x_{1},x_{2},\ldots]\!]
of formal power series over
\mathbb{Q}

with the given indeterminates, we proceed to define the algebra
Sym
of symmetric functions by writing
\textsf{Sym}=\mathbb{Q}[p_{1},p_{2},\ldots]
, i.e., so that
Sym
is the commutative subalgebra of
\mathbb{Q}[\![x_{1},x_{2},\ldots]\!]

generated by
\{p_{1},p_{2},\ldots\}
. By setting the degree of
p_{n}
as
n
for positive integers
n
, we thus have that
Sym
may equivalently be defined as the free commutative
\mathbb{Q}
-algebra with one generator in each degree. In
view of the product rule in (
2
), we find that bases of
Sym
are indexed by the set
\mathcal{P}
of all
integer partitions, writing
p_{()}=1
, giving us the
power sum basis

\{p_{\lambda}\}_{\lambda\in\mathcal{P}}
of
Sym
. The transition matrices associated with the expansion rule in (
3
) then allow us to define the

Schur basis

\{s_{\lambda}\}_{\lambda\in\mathcal{P}}
of
Sym
.

An integer composition refers to a finite tuple
\alpha
of positive integers, and we let
\ell(\alpha)
denote the number of entries of
\alpha
and write
\alpha=(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\ldots,\alpha_{\ell(\alpha)})
. If the sum of the entries of
\alpha
is equal to a
given integer
n
, we let this property be denoted by writing
\alpha\vDash n
, and we let the order
|\alpha|
of
\alpha
be equal
to
n
. Also, we let
\mathcal{C}
denote the set of all integer compositions, including the empty composition
()
, writing
()\vDash 0
. Integer compositions are of basic importance in the combinatorial and algebraic study of quasisymmetric functions,
which we define as below.

For a nonempty element
\alpha
in
\mathcal{C}
, we write
M_{\alpha}
to denote the element in
\mathbb{Q}[\![x_{1},x_{2},\ldots]\!]
such that

M_{\alpha}=\sum_{i_{1}<i_{2}<\cdots<i_{\ell(\alpha)}}x_{i_{1}}^{\alpha_{1}}x_{i_{2}}^{\alpha_{2}}\cdots x_{i_{\ell(\alpha)}}^{\alpha_{\ell(\alpha)}},

and we write
M_{()}=M_{0}=1
. This leads us to define
QSym
as the
subalgebra of
\mathbb{Q}[\![x_{1},x_{2},\ldots]\!]
spanned by
\{M_{\alpha}\}_{\alpha\in\mathcal{C}}
, which provides a
basis of
QSym
that we refer to as the
monomial basis
. For an integer composition
\alpha
, we let
\text{sort}(\alpha)
denote the integer partition obtained by sorting the entries of
\alpha
, i.e., in weakly decreasing order. For a nonempty integer
partition
\lambda
, we set

m_{\lambda}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\alpha\in\mathcal{C}\\
\text{sort}(\alpha)=\lambda\end{subarray}}M_{\alpha}

(5)

and we write
m_{()}=m_{0}=1
. We have that
m_{\lambda}\in\textsf{Sym}
for all
\lambda\in\mathcal{P}
, and that
\{m_{\lambda}\}_{\lambda\in\mathcal{P}}
is a basis of
Sym
, i.e., the
monomial basis
of
Sym
.

Since a main purpose of this paper is to generalize the definition in (
1
) using elements of
QSym
, this
leads us to consider how Skandera et al.
[
7
,
28
]
have previously applied generalizations of
(
1
) using the standard bases of
Sym
, as in with the elementary immanants defined below.

The elementary symmetric generator
e_{n}\in\textsf{Sym}
for
n\geq 0
is such that
e_{0}=0
and such that

e_{n}=\sum_{1\leq i_{1}<i_{2}<\cdots<i_{n}}x_{i_{1}}x_{i_{2}}\cdots x_{i_{n}}

for
n>0
. By then writing
e_{\lambda}=e_{\lambda_{1}}e_{\lambda_{2}}\cdots e_{\lambda_{\ell(\lambda)}}
, the
elementary basis
of
Sym
is
\{e_{\lambda}\}_{\lambda\in\mathcal{P}}
. Following
[
7
,
28
]
, we may define coefficients of the form

\epsilon^{\lambda}(\sigma)
, for
\sigma\in S_{n}
and for
\lambda\vdash n
, according to the expansion such that

e_{\lambda}=\frac{1}{n!}\sum_{\sigma\in S_{n}}\epsilon^{\lambda}(\sigma)p_{\text{ctype}(\sigma)}.

(6)

The
elementary immanant

\text{Imm}^{\epsilon^{\lambda}}(A)

may then, according to
[
7
]
, be defined so that

\text{Imm}^{\epsilon^{\lambda}}(A)=\sum_{\sigma\in S_{n}}\epsilon^{\lambda}(\sigma)\prod_{i=1}^{n}a_{i,\sigma_{i}}.

(7)

Again with reference to
[
7
,
28
]
, we let, again for
\lambda\vdash n
and for
\sigma\in S_{n}
, scalars of the form
\phi^{\lambda}(\sigma)
be such that

m_{\lambda}=\frac{1}{n!}\sum_{\sigma\in S_{n}}\phi^{\lambda}(\sigma)p_{\text{ctype}(\sigma)},

(8)

i.e., so that
\phi^{\lambda}
is the preimage of
m_{\lambda}
with respect to
Frob
.
Following
[
7
]
,
we then define the
monomial immanant

\text{Imm}^{\phi^{\lambda}}(A)
so that

\text{Imm}^{\phi^{\lambda}}(A)=\sum_{\sigma\in S_{n}}\phi^{\lambda}(\sigma)\prod_{i=1}^{n}a_{i,\sigma_{i}}.

(9)

We write

h_{n}=\sum_{1\leq i_{1}\leq i_{2}\leq\cdots\leq i_{n}}x_{i_{1}}x_{i_{2}}\cdots x_{i_{n}}

to denote the
n^{\text{th}}

complete homogeneous generator of
Sym
for integers
n>0
, writing
h_{()}=h_{0}=1
and writing
h_{\lambda}=h_{\lambda_{1}}h_{\lambda_{2}}\cdots h_{\lambda_{\ell(\lambda)}}
. The scalars in the expansions among (

(
6
), and (

\langle h_{\lambda},m_{\mu}\rangle=\delta_{\lambda,\mu}=\begin{cases}1&\text{if $\lambda=\mu$,}\\
0&\text{if $\lambda\neq\mu$.}\end{cases}

(10)

In the work of Skandera
[
28
]
, the classical immanant function in (
1
) was implicitly generalized to matrix functions given
by replacing the coefficient
\chi^{\lambda}(\text{ctype}(\sigma))
in (
1
) with the Hall inner product with
p_{\text{ctype}(\sigma)}

as an argument and a fixed element of
Sym
as an argument. Since

is not self-dual, as reviewed below, it is not clear how an

As a noncommutative analogue of the generating set such that

\textsf{Sym}=\mathbb{Q}[h_{1},h_{2},\ldots]
, we set

\textsf{NSym}:=\mathbb{Q}\langle\text{{\bf h}}_{1},\text{{\bf h}}_{2},\ldots\rangle

(11)

for indeterminates
\text{{\bf h}}_{n}
that we endow with a degrees so that
\text{deg}(\text{{\bf h}}_{n})=n
, i.e., so that the structure
in (

) may be understood as the free
\mathbb{Q}
-algebra with one generator in each degree. This algebra was
introduced by Gelfand et al.
[
8
]
and is referred to as the

noncommutative symmetric functions
. For a nonempty integer composition
\alpha
, we write
\text{{\bf h}}_{\alpha}=\text{{\bf h}}_{\alpha_{1}}\text{{\bf h}}_{\alpha_{2}}\cdots\text{{\bf h}}_{\alpha_{\ell(\alpha)}}
, and we set
\text{{\bf h}}_{()}=\text{{\bf h}}_{0}=1
, so that the family
\{\text{{\bf h}}_{\alpha}\}_{\alpha\in\mathcal{C}}
is a basis of
NSym
, i.e., the

complete homogeneous basis
of
NSym

For a vector space
V
over a field
\mathbbm{k}
, the
dual

V^{\ast}
of

consists of linear morphisms from
V
to

\mathbbm{k}
, giving rise to a bilinear pairing
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle\colon V\otimes V^{\ast}\to\mathbbm{k}

NSym
and

are dual, with respect to the graded dual Hopf algebra structures on the specified algebras,
according to the pairing

\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle\colon\textsf{NSym}\otimes\textsf{QSym}\to\mathbb{Q}

(12)

such that

\langle\text{{\bf h}}_{\alpha},M_{\beta}\rangle=\delta_{\alpha,\beta},

(13)

by direct analogy with (

The bilinear mapping in (

is of key importance in relation to the quasisymmetric power sums
[
1
]

required for our construction.

3
Quasi-immanants

Determinants naturally arise in terms of how the primary bases of the algebra of symmetric functions relate to one another, as in the
classical Jacobi–Trudi rules, and this gives rise to problems and research investigations on immanants of Jacobi–Trudi matrices

[
10
,
29
,
30
]
. Further and notable research contributions that concern
both immanants and symmetric functions
[
9
,
16
,
17
,
23
,
24
]

motivate our work and lead to questions as to the use of quasisymmetric functions in place of
symmetric functions and in relation to immanants.
To define the quasi-immanant function introduced in this paper,
we first require the notion of a
cycle composition
, as defined below.

Definition 1
.

For a permutation
\sigma
, the
cycle composition

\text{ccomp}(\sigma)
of
\sigma
is the integer composition obtained by
taking the cycle decomposition of
\sigma
, and by then writing each cycle as a sequence of integers in increasing order and sorting the
resultant cycles in increasing order, lexicographically, and by then taking the consecutive lengths of the cycles in the resultant list.

Example 1
.

Inputting

print(permu);
print(permu.to_cycles());
print([len(t) for t in permu.to_cycles()]);

into
SageMath
, we obtain the output

[2, 1, 5, 4, 6, 7, 3]
[(1, 2), (3, 5, 6, 7), (4,)]
[2, 4, 1]

and this illustrates that

\text{ccomp}\left(\begin{smallmatrix}1&2&3&4&5&6&7\\
2&1&5&4&6&7&3\end{smallmatrix}\right)=(2,4,1)
.

To define the quasi-immanant matrix functions introduced in this paper, we are to require one of the families of quasisymmetric power
sums introduced by Ballantine et al.
[
1
]
. In this direction, we begin by defining the

descent set
of an integer composition
\alpha
so that

\text{Set}(\alpha)=\{\alpha_{1},\alpha_{1}+\alpha_{2},\ldots,\alpha_{1}+\alpha_{2}+\cdots+\alpha_{\ell(\alpha)-1}\}.

We also write
\beta\preceq\alpha
to denote the relation such
that
\text{Set}(\alpha)\subseteq\text{Set}(\beta)
. For
\beta
and
\alpha
such that
\beta\preceq\alpha
, we write

\alpha=(\beta_{1}+\cdots+\beta_{i_{1}},\beta_{i_{1}+1}+\cdots+\beta_{i_{1}+i_{2}},\ldots,\beta_{i_{1}+\cdots+i_{k-1}+1}+\cdots+\beta_{i_{1}+\cdots+i_{k}}).

We may then define
\beta^{(j)}
as the composition given by the consecutive entries
in
\beta
given by the terms in the
j^{\text{th}}
entry on the right-hand side of the above formula for
\alpha\succeq\beta
.

We then set

\ell(\beta,\alpha)=\prod_{j=1}^{\ell(\alpha)}\ell\left(\beta^{(j)}\right).

Similarly, writing
\text{lp}(\beta)=\beta_{\ell(\beta)}
, we set

\text{lp}(\beta,\alpha)=\prod_{i=1}^{\ell(\alpha)}\text{lp}\left(\beta^{(i)}\right).

Also, for
\lambda\vdash n
, letting
m_{i}
denote the number of parts of
\lambda
of size
i
, and letting the maximal such size be

z_{\lambda}=1^{m_{1}}m_{1}!2^{m_{2}}m_{2}!\cdots k^{m_{k}}m_{k}!,

(14)

so that (
14

\lambda
,
with respect to the action of the order-
n
symmetric group on itself given by conjugation.
For an integer composition
\alpha
, following the work of
Ballantine et al.
[
1
]
,
we set
z_{\alpha}=z_{\text{sort}(\alpha)}
.

Type 1 and 2

may be defined via the inverse relations such that

M_{\beta}=\sum_{\alpha\succeq\beta}(-1)^{\ell(\beta)-\ell(\alpha)}\frac{\text{lp}(\beta,\alpha)}{z_{\alpha}}\Psi_{\alpha}

(15)

and such that

M_{\beta}=\sum_{\alpha\succeq\beta}(-1)^{\ell(\beta)-\ell(\alpha)}\frac{\prod_{i}\alpha_{i}}{z_{\alpha}\ell(\beta,\alpha)}\Phi_{\alpha}.

(16)

The inverse relations in (

can be shown to provide a refinement of the power sum

For an

We find that Definition

Quasi-Schur second immanants

The cells of

\left(\hat{T}(j,k)\neq 0\ \text{and}\ \hat{T}(j,k)\geq\hat{T}(i,k)\right)\Longrightarrow\hat{T}(j,k)>\hat{T}(i,k-1).

\{\mathfrak{S}_{\alpha}^{\ast}\}_{\alpha\in\mathcal{C}}

\{{\text{\large{\textcjheb{/s\char 33\relax}}}}_{\alpha}^{\ast}\}_{\alpha\in\mathcal{C}}

One might think that the determinant identities in (

\text{QImm}_{\ast}^{\mathfrak{S}^{\ast}_{(1^{n})}}\ \ \ \text{and}\ \ \ \text{QImm}_{\ast}^{\mathcal{S}_{(1^{n})}}

Lemma 1
.

For positive integers

\mathcal{S}_{(2,1^{n})}\not\in\textsf{\emph{Sym}}

Proof.

For compositions
\alpha,\beta\vDash n

\alpha\blacktriangleright\beta

if
\text{sort}(\alpha)
is lexicographically strictly greater than

\text{sort}(\beta)

\text{sort}(\alpha)=\text{sort}(\beta)

and
\alpha
is lexicographically strictly greater than
\beta

For the matrix with rows and columns indexed by compositions
\alpha\vDash n

and ordered by
\blacktriangleright

so that the
(\alpha,\beta)

M_{\beta}
in
\mathcal{S}_{\alpha}

this transition matrix is upper unitriangular, as shown by

[

This gives us that
\mathcal{S}_{(2,1^{n})}

equals the sum of
M_{(2,1^{n})}
and a scalar multiple of

gives us the desired result.
∎

Theorem 4
.

For a natural number
n\geq 3
and for an
n\times n
matrix

, the quasi-immanant

\text{\emph{QImm}}^{\mathcal{S}_{(2,1^{n-2})}}_{\Psi}(A)

satisfies

\text{\emph{QImm}}^{\mathcal{S}_{(2,1^{n-2})}}_{\Psi}(A)=\sum_{\sigma\in S_{n}}c_{\sigma}\prod_{i=1}^{n}a_{i,\sigma_{i}},

where the coefficient
c_{\sigma}
satisfies the following:

1.

If
\text{\emph{ccomp}}(\sigma)_{1}=1
, then

c_{\sigma}
equals
(-1)^{n-\ell(\text{\emph{ccomp}}(\sigma))}
times the number of
n
-permutations
of same cycle type as
\sigma

2.

If
\text{\emph{ccomp}}(\sigma)_{1}=2
, then

c_{\sigma}
equals
(-1)^{n-1-\ell(\text{\emph{ccomp}}(\sigma))}
times the number of
n
-permutations
of the same cycle type as
\sigma

3.

If
\text{\emph{ccomp}}(\sigma)_{1}>2

c_{\sigma}=0

Proof.

From (

n!M_{(2,1^{n-2})}=\sum_{{\begin{subarray}{c}\alpha\vDash n\\
\alpha_{1}\geq 2\end{subarray}}}(-1)^{n-1-\ell(\alpha)}\frac{n!}{z_{\alpha}}\text{lp}((2,1^{n-2}),\alpha)\Psi_{\alpha}

and that

n!M_{(1^{n})}=\sum_{\alpha\vDash n}(-1)^{n-\ell(\alpha)}\frac{n!}{z_{\alpha}}\text{lp}((1^{n}),\alpha)\Psi_{\alpha}.

(21)

We see that (

\alpha_{1}>2\end{subarray}}}(-1)^{n-1-\ell(\alpha)}\frac{n!}{z_{\alpha}}\Psi_{\alpha}.

Example 2
.

We find that the analogue of second immanants given in
Theorem
4
is not equivalent to second immanants.
For example, consider the
3\times 3
case, with

\text{QImm}_{\Psi}^{\mathcal{S}_{(2,1)}}\left(\begin{array}[]{ccc}a_{1,1}&a_{1,2}&a_{1,3}\\
a_{2,1}&a_{2,2}&a_{2,3}\\
a_{3,1}&a_{3,2}&a_{3,3}\\
\end{array}\right)=\\
a_{1,1}a_{2,2}a_{3,3}-3a_{1,1}a_{2,3}a_{3,2}+3a_{1,2}a_{2,1}a_{3,3}+3a_{1,3}a_{2,2}a_{3,1}.

Observe that

\text{ccomp}\left(\begin{smallmatrix}1&2&3\\
1&3&2\end{smallmatrix}\right)=(1,2)
, and that
the coefficient of
a_{1,1}a_{2,3}a_{3,2}
is equal to

(-1)^{3-2}
times the number of

3
-permutations of cycle type
(2,1)
, i.e.,
the number of permutations among

\text{ccomp}\left(\begin{smallmatrix}1&2&3\\
1&3&2\end{smallmatrix}\right)

and

\text{ccomp}\left(\begin{smallmatrix}1&2&3\\
3&2&1\end{smallmatrix}\right)

and

\text{ccomp}\left(\begin{smallmatrix}1&2&3\\
2&1&3\end{smallmatrix}\right)
.
For the second immanant of a
3\times 3
matrix, we obtain

d_{2}\left(\begin{array}[]{ccc}a_{1,1}&a_{1,2}&a_{1,3}\\
a_{2,1}&a_{2,2}&a_{2,3}\\
a_{3,1}&a_{3,2}&a_{3,3}\\
\end{array}\right)=2a_{1,1}a_{2,2}a_{3,3}-a_{1,2}a_{2,3}a_{3,1}-a_{1,3}a_{2,1}a_{3,2}.

The inequivalence illustrated in Example
2

leads us to consider how the combinatorial rule in Theorem
4

could be used to obtain identities for quasi-immanants of infinite families of matrices
and how such identities relate to second immanants.

Example 3
.

Immanants of Toeplitz matrices are often studied in relation to recursive properties of such immanants. This leads us to consider
quasi-immanants of Toeplitz matrices, in relation to second immanants. For example, we find that quasi-immanants of the form

\text{QImm}_{\Psi}^{\mathcal{S}_{(2,1)}}
provide a natural companion to the second immanant relation such that

d_{2}\left(\begin{array}[]{ccccccc}0&1&0&\cdots&0&0&0\\
1&0&1&\ddots&0&0&0\\
0&1&0&\ddots&0&0&0\\
\vdots&\ddots&\ddots&\ddots&\ddots&\ddots&\vdots\\
0&0&0&\ddots&0&1&0\\
0&0&0&\ddots&1&0&1\\
0&0&0&\cdots&0&1&0\\
\end{array}\right)_{n\times n}=\begin{cases}(-1)^{\frac{n}{2}+1}&\text{if $n$ is even,}\\
0&\text{otherwise,}\end{cases}

with

\text{QImm}_{\Psi}^{\mathcal{S}_{(2,1^{n-2})}}\left(\begin{array}[]{ccccccc}0&1&0&\cdots&0&0&0\\
1&0&1&\ddots&0&0&0\\
0&1&0&\ddots&0&0&0\\
\vdots&\ddots&\ddots&\ddots&\ddots&\ddots&\vdots\\
0&0&0&\ddots&0&1&0\\
0&0&0&\ddots&1&0&1\\
0&0&0&\cdots&0&1&0\\
\end{array}\right)_{n\times n}=\\
\begin{cases}-\left(-\frac{1}{2}\right)^{\frac{n}{2}}\frac{n!}{\left(\frac{n}{2}\right)!}&\text{if $n$ is even,}\\
0&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

With regard to the combinatorial rule in Theorem
4
, similar results can be obtained for

\text{QImm}_{\Phi}^{\mathcal{S}_{(2,1^{n-2})}}
and for quasi-immanants more generally, and we encourage explorations of this.
