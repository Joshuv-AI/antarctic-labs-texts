Asymmetric life-history trade-offs shape sex-biased longevity patterns
Authors: Ella Rees-Baylis, Daiping Wang, Xiang-Yi Li Richter, Charlotte de Vries
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Sex differences in ageing and lifespan are widespread across taxa, yet their evolutionary causes remain debated. A leading hypothesis proposes that such differences arise adaptively from sex-specific life-history trade-offs, but formal theoretical support is lacking. We therefore develop a mathematical model to examine how these trade-offs shape lifespan evolution across ecological and mating system contexts. Under monogamy, individuals optimise a survival-reproduction trade-off, mediated by mating opportunities in males and offspring production in females. Varying trade-off strengths shows that male-biased longevity emerges across a wider parameter range, consistent with patterns in monogamous species. This asymmetry arises because increased female longevity reduces offspring production, lowering population size and mating rates and thereby constraining selection on female lifespan, whereas longer male lifespan evolves more readily unless mating costs are high. Incorporating density dependence shows that ecological feedbacks disproportionately reduce survival in the intrinsically longer-lived sex, potentially masking evolved differences. Beyond monogamy, we show that mating system variation shapes longevity through competition intensity, determined by the limiting sex, and demographic scaling, where female number sets reproductive output and the selective context for longevity. Overall, we highlight that life-history trade-offs and demographic feedbacks jointly determine the direction and magnitude of sex differences in lifespan.

Introduction

Patterns of sex differences in ageing and lifespan vary widely across and within taxa. For instance, in most mammals, males tend to experience higher mortality rates than females, whereas in birds, sex differences in mortality tend to be smaller but are often female-biased1–8. Several non-adaptive, mechanistic explanations have been proposed, including the influence of sex hormones, matrilineal mitochondrial inheritance, and sex chromosome asymmetries1,9. For example, the “unguarded sex chromosome" hypothesis suggests that the heterogametic sex (e.g., male mammals, female birds) may suffer higher mortality due to increased expression of recessive deleterious alleles given only a single X or Z chromosome copy10,11. Furthermore, there is evidence in some species of a “toxic Y (or W)" effect, whereby the higher concentration of repetitive elements on these chromosomes can contribute to reduced longevity as they become misregulated with age9,12. Although the sex determination system correlates with adult sex ratio bias—used as a rough proxy for sex-biased mortality—across multiple taxa13, empirical support for theories of maladaptive asymmetric inheritance remains mixed11,14,15. Accordingly, adaptive evolutionary hypotheses have also been proposed to play a major role in shaping sex differences in ageing and longevity.

A predominant idea is that sex differences in lifespan are adaptive, and are shaped by sex-specific selection for different optima in life-history traits6,16. Central to life-history theory is that investment in current reproduction may come at the cost of survival and future reproductive opportunities17. Since the sexes often differ in reproductive roles, this trade-off can lead natural selection to favour different optimal strategies in males and females, resulting in sex-specific mortality and longevity patterns16,18–20. Various aspects of reproduction can incur survival costs, including mate acquisition, gamete production, and parental care21, and as such, the nature of this trade-off can differ between the sexes.

Females directly produce offspring and often provide more care, both of which can incur survival costs, leading to an evolutionary trade-off between reproduction and survival. Since reproduction is typically limited by female fecundity, female life histories may also be more directly linked to population-level dynamics, although empirical evidence largely focuses on direct survival costs of reproduction. For example, avian species with higher annual egg productivity exhibit stronger female-biased mortalities22, and incubation has been shown to incur physiological and long-term survival costs23,24. Gestation and lactation are particularly energetically costly for mammalian females25, and more generally across taxa, sex biases in parental care are positively associated with sex biases in adult mortality5,26. In contrast, the primary survival costs of reproduction in males are often linked to competition for mates and the traits arising from this, leading to a trade-off between survival and mating opportunities. Polygynous mating systems—common among mammals—intensify sexual selection in males and are associated with male-biased mortality2,27,28. Accordingly, the social monogamy typical of most bird species is thought to reduce male-male competition and contribute to higher male survival. This idea is further supported by exceptions across taxa: polygynous bird species tend to show male-biased mortality patterns5,22, while the few monogamous mammals often have female-biased mortality27.

Still, sex-biased mortality patterns in the wild are not only driven by mating systems but also emerge from complex interactions between environmental conditions and the costs of sexual selection29. Moreover, sexual selection and competition for mating opportunities still persist under monogamy—whether to secure a high-quality partner, obtain and defend a territory, or maintain pair bonds30,31. Thus, whilst mating systems help to explain broad patterns—such as male-biased mortality in predominantly polygynous mammals and female-biased mortality in socially monogamous birds—there remains substantial variation in both the magnitude and direction of sex biases in mortality and lifespan within each mating system that is not yet fully understood. Current insights have been drawn primarily from empirical studies and meta-analyses, and there remains a lack of formal theoretical predictions to explain how sex-specific life-history trade-offs can shape lifespan evolution, particularly when accounting for feedbacks between individual life histories and population-level processes such as population size, density-dependent regulation, and mating structure.

To address this, we construct a mathematical model in which individuals face a trade-off between survival and reproduction: they can evolve to live longer at the cost of reduced annual reproduction, or vice versa (Fig. 1a). The trade-offs here operate at the level of evolved life-history strategies (i.e., genetic allocation), rather than representing within-individual plastic changes in reproductive effort over the life course. We begin by examining monogamy—a mating system common in birds and also present in mammals—as a baseline case in which sexual selection operates but reproductive skew is limited. The sexes differ only in the mechanism underpinning the life-history trade-off: in males, it arises from mating competition; in females, from investment in offspring production (Fig. 1b, c). We then extend the model to examine how variation in mating group composition, a key axis of mating system variation, shapes the evolution of sex-biased longevity. By systematically varying the strength of these sex-specific trade-offs, we derive predictions for sex differences in life expectancy under different life-history scenarios. Results

For all model results, we examine the evolved equilibrium values of the sex-specific ages of onset of senescence—that is, the age at which senescence significantly reduces survival (Fig. 1b). Initially, we assume the male age of onset (Mm) evolves to optimise the trade-off between survival and annual mating probability, while the female age of onset (Mf) evolves to balance survival with the annual number of offspring produced per monogamous union (Fig. 1b, c). These traits evolve toward a stable evolutionary equilibrium that is robust to initial trait values (Fig. 1d, Supplementary Fig. 1). We investigated how the strength of the male and female trade-offs (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${s}_{\,{{\rm{m}}}}^{{{\rm{M}}}}$$\end{document}smM and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${s}_{\,{{\rm{f}}}}^{{{\rm{O}}}}$$\end{document}sfO, respectively, where M is for Mating and O is for Offspring production) influence the evolved equilibrium ages of onset of senescence. For easier interpretation and comparability with empirical data, we present the results in terms of male and female life expectancies at adulthood (Lm and Lf, respectively), since this is a standard metric to report (sex-specific) longevity (Fig. 1d; see “Methods” for details). This is defined as the average age at death, conditioned on survival to adulthood (where adulthood in our model is age two, the first age at which reproduction can become non-zero). For sex differences in longevity, we present the relative adult life expectancy difference ((Lf − Lm)/max(Lf, Lm)) as described in the large comparative dataset in ref. 8. This metric is bound between −1 and 1, with 0 representing no sex differences in longevity. A positive value of 0.5, for example, is a 50% female lifespan advantage, whereas a negative value of −0.5 is a 50% male lifespan advantage.

Males outlive females under a wider range of trade-off strengths

To assess the robustness of this result, we varied two key reproductive parameters: maximum annual fecundity (the number of offspring a union can produce per year, diminished by the female trade-off) and mating efficiency (the proportion of the mating pool that mates under balanced operational sex ratios;32). The main pattern—where males outlive females across a wider range of trade-off strengths—remains robust across all parameter combinations tested. Higher maximum annual fecundity shortens life expectancy in both sexes, consistent with pace-of-life theory, and also reduces both the magnitude of sex differences and the parameter space favouring male-biased longevity (Fig. 3). Higher mating efficiency likewise reduces life expectancy in both sexes (Supplementary Fig. 2), suggesting that when mating is inefficient, there is a stronger selective advantage to prolonged lifespan—allowing individuals to reproduce over more years—rather than having a higher annual reproductive output over a shorter life. Female determination of union reproduction constrains female longevity

Why do males outlive females across a wider range of scenarios in our model, even when intrinsic trade-off strengths are equal? The key asymmetry does not arise directly from the intrinsic trade-offs themselves, which are symmetric in form, but indirectly from how they act on different components of reproduction and thereby feed back differentially into population-level dynamics. Female longevity simultaneously affects offspring production per union and, through this, total population size and union formation. Male longevity, in contrast, affects only the proportion of unions formed within this female-determined demographic baseline.

Offspring per union is a fixed parameter influenced solely by female longevity via the trade-off, whereas the number of unions depends on the abundance of each sex and is constrained by the operational sex ratio (OSR): the number of unions cannot exceed the number of individuals of the limiting sex. Because survival operates identically in both sexes, equivalent perturbations to male and female longevity generate symmetrical deviations in OSR away from parity (Fig. 4c) – yet these do not translate into equal number of unions being formed (Fig. 4d). Reducing female longevity increases offspring per union via the trade-off, increasing population size and thus increasing the number of individuals of the limiting sex which allows more unions to form than under an equivalent reduction in male longevity. Conversely, increasing female longevity reduces offspring production, decreasing population size and number of individuals of the limiting sex, amplifying the loss of unions relative to an equivalent increase in male longevity. Consequently, changes in female longevity exert a stronger influence on the absolute number of unions possible in the population, and male longevity trade-offs then act on this female-driven demographic baseline (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$U\cdot {e}^{-{s}_{{{\rm{m}}}}^{{{\rm{M}}}}({M}_{{{\rm{m}}}}-1)}$$\end{document}U⋅e−smM(Mm−1)). The marginal invasion fitness cost of increased longevity is thus higher for mutant females than for mutant males, even when intrinsic trade-off strengths are equal. Female longevity is therefore more tightly constrained by its reproductive costs, biasing evolutionary outcomes toward longer male lifespans across a wider parameter range. This interpretation is further supported by a model scenario in which we assume bi-parental care of offspring. In this extension, we apply the survival-offspring production trade-off to both sexes, so that population size and subsequent union formation are jointly determined by male and female life histories. This restores demographic symmetry between the sexes; the model is entirely symmetric with respect to sex when survival-offspring production trade-off strengths are equal, such that the sex with the stronger trade-off evolves a shorter life expectancy (Supplementary Fig. 4). Any remaining bias towards shorter male life expectancy arises due to males incurring additional costs of mating effort.

Density regulation can conceal intrinsic sex differences in survival

The results so far have shown sex-biases in longevity as a result of evolved differences in senescence—that is, differences in intrinsic survival with age. However, in natural populations, individuals are also subject to extrinsic sources of mortality. Density-dependent mortality, for example, due to competition for limited resources, is particularly common since few populations grow exponentially. To account for this, we compare intrinsic life expectancies (based solely on senescence) with realised life expectancies, which incorporate both intrinsic and density-dependent survival.

As a simple illustrative case, we assume that density-dependent mortality acts equally across all age classes and both sexes—for instance, under uniform resource limitation where all individuals require the same amount of resources (but see Supplementary Fig. 5 for density regulation instead affecting fecundity). Under this assumption, our main result still holds: males outlive females under a wider range of trade-off conditions. This is because equal density dependence does not alter the intrinsic ages of onset of senescence that evolve33–35. However, the magnitude of sex differences in realised life expectancy is substantially reduced compared to the intrinsic differences alone (Fig. 5a). Although both sexes experience the same additional density-dependent mortality per time step, the longer-lived sex—alive for more time steps—experiences a stronger compound effect of this mortality. This compounding effect diminishes the observed sex difference in life expectancy. Importantly, this implies that sex asymmetries in survival-reproduction trade-offs can result in large intrinsic survival differences between the sexes, but that empirically these differences may become only marginally detectable due to the effects of density regulation. Second, demographic scaling alters the selective context of longevity. Even when intrinsic life-history trade-offs are fully symmetric (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${s}_{\,{{\rm{f}}}}^{{{\rm{O}}}}=0$$\end{document}sfO=0), mirror-image group compositions do not produce equivalent outcomes. For instance, three males and one female result in a 49% male life expectancy advantage, while three females and one male yield only a 47% female advantage, and female-biased groups produce shorter absolute life expectancies in both sexes than their male-biased mirrors (Fig. 6b). This asymmetry arises because increasing the number of females per group linearly increases offspring production (Fig. 6c), generating total greater reproductive output and sustaining larger mating pools than equivalently sized male-biased groups (Fig. 6d)—mechanistically analogous to increasing maximum annual fecundity per female, which likewise shortens life expectancy in both sexes and compresses evolved sex differences (Fig. 3).

Group composition thus shapes sex differences in longevity through two mechanisms. First, competition intensity varies with which sex is limiting: the minority sex in the mating pool (majority in mating groups) faces weaker competition and lower mortality costs from mating effort. Second, the number of females scales total reproductive output, reshaping the demographic context in which selection on longevity acts—demonstrating that sex differences need not stem solely from asymmetries in how life-history trade-offs operate in each sex.

Discussion

We here presented a mathematical model providing a theoretical framework for understanding how sex-specific trade-offs between survival and reproductive success can drive the evolution of sex differences in lifespan. By systematically varying the strength of these trade-offs across different scenarios, we demonstrate that either sex may evolve to live longer depending on assumptions about the relative costs each sex pays for reproduction. Our results provide new theoretical insights into the adaptive basis of sex-biased longevity and highlight the central role of demographic feedbacks alongside life-history trade-offs in shaping lifespan evolution.

In our baseline model, we assume monogamous mating and conventional sex roles: males trade off survival with mating opportunities, and females trade off survival with offspring production. Here, either sex may evolve to live longer, generally depending on which faces the weaker trade-off. This offers a potential explanation for the observed variability in sex-biased longevity among (socially) monogamous species: while slight female-biased mortality is common, male-biased patterns also occur (e.g.,2,5). Our model suggests this variation could stem, at least in part, from differences in the strength of sex-specific life-history trade-offs. For example, in the monogamous least sandpiper (Calidris minutilla), females tend to outlive males (Fig. 5c). Males provide extensive parental care, including incubation and often sole responsibility for chick rearing after females depart early38. In our model, this could reflect a very weak trade-off between survival and offspring production in females—or a strong one in males—parameter regions where female longevity is expected to exceed male longevity. Conversely, in the monogamous black turnstone (Arenaria melanocephala), males tend to live longer than females (Fig. 5c). These birds exhibit strong mate fidelity and return annually to the same territory39, which in our model may correspond to a weak trade-off between survival and mating opportunities in males, supporting greater male longevity.

These examples are, however, illustrative. While empirical support for early-late trade-offs exists across many species40, many other factors beyond intrinsic trade-offs—such as ecological constraints and environmental variability—also affect sex- and species-specific ageing and lifespan patterns18,41,42. For instance, although strong sexual selection in males (akin to a strong male trade-off strength in our baseline model) is predicted to result in shorter lifespans in males, sexual selection proxies such as sexual size dimorphism or testes size often show inconsistent relationships with sex-biased longevity and ageing rates16,43,44. This challenges the centrality of life-history trade-offs in shaping sex differences in longevity and highlights the need for targeted empirical studies—such as manipulations of reproductive investment or survival costs, which can directly reveal such trade-offs (e.g.,45)—as well as further theoretical work incorporating different assumptions.

Importantly, males tend to outlive females across a wider range of trade-off scenarios in our baseline monogamous model, even when intrinsic trade-off strengths are equal between the sexes. This asymmetry arises because female longevity feeds back into population size through its effect on offspring production, whereas male longevity affects only the proportion of potential unions that form within this female-driven demographic baseline. This feedback imposes tighter evolutionary constraints on female lifespan, whereas selection can more readily favour longer male lifespans, especially when the survival-mating trade-off is weak. Our results thus suggest male-biased longevity as a plausible baseline outcome under strong female determination of pair reproduction, with this bias reversible depending on the strength of male reproductive costs.

This demographic asymmetry relates to the concept of female demographic dominance in population dynamics, whereby it is assumed that population growth is primarily determined by female survival and fecundity. Although female demographic dominance may appear evolutionarily irrelevant in diploid organisms, given equal average genetic contribution of the sexes to the next generation, theoretical work has shown otherwise46–48. Our model highlights another consequence: demographic scaling of reproduction through females creates evolutionary constraints that may bias longevity evolution toward males even under symmetric intrinsic trade-offs. Factors that reduce female demographic dominance may shift these outcomes. When both sexes trade-off longevity with offspring production in our model – as under bi-parental care – symmetry is restored, and sex differences in longevity depend only on relative trade-off strengths (Supplementary Fig. 4). Empirically, species with more extensive male parental care tend to show greater male-biased mortality5, consistent with males incurring additional survival costs that shift the balance toward female-biased longevity. Sex-role reversed species provide valuable test cases for these ideas. In the sequentially polyandrous barred buttonquail (Turnix suscitator), males assume primary care-giving roles, yet females still produce each clutch. Males live on average 1.7 times longer than females49, indicating that the survival costs of producing offspring in females can outweigh those of raising them in males. These examples highlight that while our baseline monogamy model captures key mechanisms through specific trade-off assumptions – survival costs of sexual selection in males and of offspring production or care in females – sex-specific life-histories are diverse, and the extent of female demographic dominance likely varies across species and mating systems.

To explore how mating system variation more broadly shapes these dynamics, we extended the model to vary group composition—the number of males and females per mating group—revealing how this major axis of mating system variation shapes sex-biased longevity through two mechanisms. First, consistent with existing theory2,27,28, competition intensity varies with which sex is limiting: the sex in the minority within the mating group (and thus majority in the mating pool) experiences weaker competition and lower assumed mortality costs from mating effort, favouring longer lifespan. This predicts that males should generally live longer in male-biased (polyandrous) groups and females in female-biased (polygynous) groups—patterns broadly consistent with empirical observations across mating systems2,8. Second, and less widely recognised, the number of females per group scales total reproductive output, reshaping the demographic context in which selection on longevity acts. Female-biased groups sustain larger populations and more mating opportunities than equivalently sized male-biased groups, selecting for shorter lifespans in both sexes—an effect mechanistically similar to increasing maximum annual fecundity per female (Fig. 3). This demographic scaling means that mirror-image group compositions (e.g., three males with one female versus three females with one male) do not produce equivalent longevity outcomes, even when intrinsic trade-offs are fully symmetric between the sexes. Group composition thus shapes sex-biased longevity not only through competition intensity differences between the sexes, but also through population-level feedbacks that alter the selective landscape for both sexes. Together, these findings underscore that sex differences in longevity emerge from the interplay between individual-level trade-offs and population-level demographic processes. Models focusing solely on individual life-history optimisation without accounting for demographic feedback may therefore miss key drivers of sex-biased lifespan evolution.

Beyond these feedbacks operating through reproduction, our model also demonstrates that density regulation can greatly diminish the observed magnitude of evolved intrinsic sex differences in lifespan: the longer-lived sex accrues a greater exposure to density-dependent mortality, offsetting some of its survival advantage from the weaker intrinsic trade-off. Our results thus suggest that large intrinsic survival differences between the sexes may exist but become only marginally detectable due to ecological constraints such as density regulation. A recent comparative analysis found that sex differences in life expectancy tend to be larger in wild populations than in captive ones8, which could reflect stronger density regulation in managed zoo populations, obscuring intrinsic dimorphism, though other factors, such as abundant resources and veterinary care may also play a role. Similarly, sex differences in lifespan can vary across populations of the same species29, which our results suggest could arise in part from variation in the strength or form of density regulation across populations, in addition to environmental differences.

Nonetheless, in nature, density dependence is unlikely to act uniformly across individuals as we have modelled here (though our main result holds when density regulation acts on fecundity instead; Supplementary Fig. 5). Extrinsic mortality risks—such as predation, disease, or competition—may vary by sex, age, or condition, and the sex roles that generate different intrinsic survival-reproduction trade-offs between the sexes will likely also result in differential exposures to extrinsic hazards. For example, competition for mating opportunities in males may not only incur intrinsic survival costs but also increase exposure to predation50 and increase parasite risk51. Our model also assumes that all individuals are of equal quality, but individual heterogeneity can obscure trade-offs52,53. If quality differences reflect environmental variation—for example, whether individuals have access to abundant or scarce resources—then high-quality individuals may achieve both higher fecundity and longer lifespans without facing the same survival-reproduction trade-off as lower-quality individuals, masking the trade-off at the population level (e.g.,54,55). Such heterogeneity could complicate empirical detection of the sex-specific trade-offs and longevity patterns predicted by our model. Exploring how such sex-, age-, and condition-dependent mortality influences the evolution of sexual dimorphism in ageing and lifespan thus remains an important direction for future work.

Another important consideration is sexual conflict6,21,56. Our model assumes no intra-locus sexual conflict—that is, no genetic constraint arising from shared longevity-affecting loci under sexually antagonistic selection—allowing the sexes to genetically evolve independently towards their respective optima and potentially resulting in strong lifespan dimorphism. Whilst there is some empirical evidence that traits affecting longevity may have some capacity to evolve semi-independently in each sex57–59, shared genetic architecture likely limits the extent to which each sex can independently optimise its strategy6,60. For instance, artificial selection experiments in Callosobruchus maculatus beetles have shown that males live longer and females live shorter than their sex-specific optima as a result of genetic correlations for lifespan between the sexes61. Such correlations would likely reduce the magnitude of sex differences in lifespan as predicted by our model. Nonetheless, even in the absence of intra-locus constraints, the sexes remain demographically interdependent: lower male mating effort can reduce female fecundity if males become limiting, for instance. Our model thus underscores that sexual dimorphism in ageing and longevity does not emerge solely from independent sex-specific optimisation, but also from the interactive consequences of reproductive strategies.

Finally, we modelled the evolution of sex-specific life expectancy assuming fixed, non-dimorphic ageing rates. Life expectancy integrates multiple components of mortality, including the age of onset of senescence and rate of actuarial ageing. We focused here on this composite outcome because sex differences in longevity are well documented and show consistent associations with factors such as the intensity of sexual selection8,29, whereas empirical patterns in sex-specific ageing rates are less consistent28,29. Extending the model to allow sex-specific evolution of ageing rates remains a valuable next step as clearer empirical patterns emerge, particularly given that sex differences in longevity do not necessarily imply sex differences in ageing rates4,16,62. More longitudinal studies in wild populations are needed to better understand the evolution of sex-specific ageing. Moreover, much of our understanding of early-late trade-offs comes from laboratory studies, often measured only in females—presumably since female reproductive effort is easier to quantify40. Future empirical work should test for survival-reproduction trade-offs in both sexes, ideally within the same wild population.

In conclusion, our model provides a general first theoretical framework for how sex-specific trade-offs between survival and reproduction can shape sex-biased lifespan evolution under several life-history scenarios. It identifies key mechanisms—asymmetric trade-off strengths, demographic feedbacks, and sexual interactions—that may help explain variation in the magnitude and direction of sex-biased longevity across taxa. Critically, our results highlight that demographic processes and population-level feedbacks play a central role in shaping life-history evolution, even when trade-offs operate at the individual level. Male-biased longevity may evolve as a result of female demographic dominance in reproduction, unless males have strong reproductive costs that can shift the bias towards longer female longevity. These insights provide testable predictions for empirical studies and a foundation for more complex theoretical investigations of sex-specific ageing and lifespan evolution.

Methods

Baseline model overview

We constructed a discrete-time, two-sex, age-structured matrix population model63. The population is divided into single males, single females, and monogamous unions formed by one male and one female (Fig. 1a). Senescence is modelled using the standard Gompertz hazard function, where individuals experience an exponentially increasing risk of death with age. We assume that this mortality trades off with reproduction, such that each sex can evolve to live longer at the cost of reduced reproductive output, and vice versa. In males, this trade-off operates through their annual probability of joining a union (i.e., mating opportunities); in females, it acts through the annual number of offspring produced per union (Fig. 1b, c). This is the only sex difference in the model, so the labels “male" and “female" are arbitrary: for example, the trade-offs could be reversed in systems with female-female competition or male-biased parental care. We used an adaptive dynamics framework to allow the age of onset of senescence—the age at which senescence substantially decreases survival—to evolve in each sex, with evolution proceeding until they reach a stable evolutionary equilibrium (Fig. 1d; Supplementary Fig. 1). We varied the strength of the sex-specific trade-offs between survival and reproduction to observe the effects this has on the equilibrium longevities that evolve in each sex. Survival probability is initially close to 1, declining gradually and then markedly at age Mi. Mi thus represents the age at which senescence (i.e., intrinsic mortality) begins to strongly limit survival, and corresponds to the modal age at death in the absence of extrinsic mortality. The term \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$b{e}^{b(x-{M}_{i})}$$\end{document}beb(x−Mi) is the Gompertz hazard rate, a widely used model of senescence that assumes an exponentially increasing risk of mortality with age64. Taking the exponential of the negative integral of this function over the interval [x, x + 1] yields the discrete survival probability for that time step65. All individuals die upon reaching the maximum age class, such that ui(ω) = 0. The age of onset of senescence for males and females (Mm and Mf, respectively) is the evolving trait in the model. Their equilibrium values represent how sex-specific life histories evolve to optimise the trade-off between survival and reproduction.

To reproduce, single males and females must first form monogamous mating unions. Union formation is modelled using a flexible, frequency-dependent minharmonic mating function32.

Although not explicitly implemented, one can interpret individuals in the model as haploid, carrying two unlinked loci with sex-specifically expressed genes: one affecting the male trait (Mm) and one affecting the female trait (Mf). Offspring inherit one allele at each locus, randomly contributed by the two parents. In the resident population, this inheritance has no phenotypic consequence, as all individuals share the same genotype (Mf, Mm).

Evolution via adaptive dynamics

For both models, we used an adaptive dynamics framework to model the evolution of the male and female ages of onset of senescence (Mm and Mf, respectively), simulating these dynamics to obtain numerical solutions. At each time step, we assessed whether a rare mutant—with a slightly altered age on onset of senescence (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${M}_{{{{\rm{m}}}}^{*}}$$\end{document}Mm* or \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${M}_{{{{\rm{f}}}}^{*}}$$\end{document}Mf*)—could invade a resident population at equilibrium. This process was repeated until the trait values converged to a numerical equilibrium, representing uninvadable singular strategies (Supplementary Fig. 1).

Calculation of life expectancies

In the model, the evolving traits of interest are the male and female ages of onset of senescence (Mm and Mf, respectively). However, to aid interpretation of model results and to allow for comparison with empirical data, we instead report the male and female life expectancies at adulthood (Lm and Lf, respectively): the average age at death, conditioned on survival to adulthood (age 2 in our model). To achieve this, we use the age-specific survival matrices for males and females (Um and Uf, respectively), which contain age-specific survival probabilities along the sub-diagonal (Eq. (1); see Supplementary Methods for details). These survival matrices are used to calculate the fundamental matrix Ni for each sex i, where each element nxy represents the average time an individual of age y can expect to spend in age class x 66: 27\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${{{\bf{N}}}}_{i}={({{{\bf{I}}}}_{\omega }-{{{\bf{U}}}}_{i}{e}^{-v{N}_{r}})}^{-1},$$\end{document}Ni=(Iω−Uie−vNr)−1, where Iω is an ω × ω identity matrix with 1’s along the diagonal and 0’s elsewhere. Summing each column of Ni yields an ω × 1 vector of life expectancies: the average time until death for a sex i individual of each age class. We define life expectancy from adulthood as the second element of this vector—corresponding to age class 2—the first age class with non-zero reproduction in our model.

We distinguish between two types of life expectancy: intrinsic and realised. Intrinsic life expectancy reflects how long an individual is expected to live based on senescence only, excluding any extrinsic mortality. For this, we set v = 0 in equation (27) to remove the added density-dependent mortality from the life expectancy calculation. Realised life expectancy (shown only in Fig. 5) reflects how long individuals can be expected to live on average given their intrinsic survival and the additional mortality as a result of density regulation. For this, we set v = 0.0001 in equation (27), the default parameter value used in simulations (Table 1).

Empirical data

The empirical data presented in Fig. 5b, c was sourced from11 (bird and mammal data), the “Birds of the World" database36 (bird data), and29 (mammal data). For each source, we used all species that are classified as strictly (socially) monogamous and for which average life expectancies from adulthood of both sexes was available. Data in ref. 11 and 36 contain sex-specific life expectancies, but not a mating system classification. For species from these datasets, we classified bird mating systems using the definitions in ref. 68 and for mammals using the data from the “Animal Diversity Web" database69 if available for the species, and appended this to the life expectancy data. These criteria resulted in sex-specific life expectancies for n = 44 (socially) monogamous bird species and n = 11 monogamous mammal species.
