Marine heatwaves have emerged as a major disturbance to seagrasses and cause large-scale die-offs, yet the physiological mechanisms underlying seagrass responses to thermal stress remain unclear. Vivanco-Bercovich et al. (2026) used a simulated marine heatwave experiment to show that in surfgrasses—seagrasses that colonize rocky substrates in the turbulent surf zone—warming above a threshold triggered severe physiological consequences that persisted beyond the exposure to warming. These results provide an important contribution to the expanding body of work on seagrass thermal tolerance thresholds, suggesting that intensifying marine heatwaves will exceed the stress limits of natural seagrass populations across the globe, precipitating cascading and far-reaching ecological impacts.

Seagrass vulnerability to warming

Increased sea surface temperatures and more frequent and intense marine heatwaves are major threats to stability and function of seagrass ecosystems (Smale et al., 2019). The largest seagrass meadow on Earth in Shark Bay, Australia, was devastated by a major marine heatwave in 2010–2011. The event was linked to loss of 1000 km2 of meadow area, estimated emission of 2–9 Tg CO2-eq from stored sediment carbon, decline of seagrass-associated species, and closure of scallop and crab fisheries (Kendrick et al., 2019). Warming has also contributed to substantial declines in seagrass meadows globally, such as in the Mediterranean, Chesapeake Bay, and Florida Bay (Marbà and Duarte, 2010; Moore et al., 2012; Carlson et al., 2018). Furthermore, warm temperatures that do not meet the climatological definition of a marine heatwave (sensu Hobday et al., 2016) can still cause physiological stress and decline in seagrasses (e.g. Beca-Carretero et al., 2018).

Perhaps unsurprisingly, the impacts of warming on seagrasses are not linear, due to simultaneous impacts on multiple aspects of plant health and functioning (Box 1; Berger and Berg, 2024). Research to date suggests an overarching conceptual model in which thermal stress compounds dramatically at temperatures above a physiological threshold, due to differential impacts of warming on rates of respiration and photosynthesis in seagrass. That is, the rate of respiration increases steadily as temperatures rise, while the rate of gross photosynthesis increases up to a critical threshold temperature, after which it decreases rapidly (Marsh et al., 1986; Moore et al., 2021). Because of this differential impact of increasing temperatures, seagrasses will experience a carbon imbalance above a critical temperature threshold, as the respiratory demands of the plants exceed the rate of carbon assimilation from photosynthesis. Additional environmental conditions, including water clarity and pH, can further impact daily photosynthesis available to meet carbon demands, potentially exacerbating the impact of warming on seagrass carbon balance (Zimmerman et al., 2015). In their study, Vivanco-Bercovich et al. (2026) provide further empirical support for this model and extend its applicability to surfgrasses.

The combined effect of these changes to plant metabolism and physiology can be substantial. Importantly, differences in thermal sensitivity between key metabolic processes will lead to a physiological stress threshold. In seagrasses, rates of both respiration and photosynthesis increase in response to increasing temperatures, but photosynthesis exhibits a temperature optimum; at temperatures above some critical threshold, rates of photosynthesis decline sharply. In contrast, respiration rates can increase more quickly than photosynthesis as temperatures rise, and respiration rates continue increasing at temperatures above the optimum temperature for photosynthesis. These differential patterns in thermal sensitivity lead to a reduction in net photosynthesis and a carbon imbalance at high temperatures (Marsh et al., 1986; Lee et al., 2007; Fig. 1). Thermal stress responses are most commonly studied at the morphological level in seagrasses (e.g. measuring growth rates in response to warming), but studies of seagrass transcriptomics indicate responses in molecular mechanisms involved in maintaining photosynthetic stability, including oxidative-stress defence, protein folding, and cell-wall fortification (Marín-Guirao et al., 2017). Biochemical studies have also observed changes in seagrass photosynthetic capacity, chlorophyll degradation, and increased sulfide stress, as well as shifts in carbohydrate allocation and biomass between aboveground and belowground tissues (reviewed in Nguyen et al., 2020). The physiological thresholds derived from measurements of seagrass metabolisms thus represent combined effects of thermal stress across multiple biological levels.

A recent study by Berger and Berg (2024) illustrated the magnitude of these effects in situ for eelgrass (Zostera marina) habitats. Using the non-invasive aquatic eddy covariance technique, they showed that temperatures above a thermal threshold of 28.6 °C decreased daytime O2 fluxes of eelgrass habitats by 50%, shifting the ecosystem metabolism from metabolic balance into a net heterotrophic state. The underlying cause of this effect is likely multifaceted, but rooted in the high sensitivity of photosynthesis to elevated temperatures (Wahid et al., 2007), whereby heat stress damages the cellular structures responsible for photosynthesis and reduces the plant’s photosynthetic capacity (Nguyen et al., 2021). Limited growth and loss of biomass may then ensue, and sustained exposure to thermal stress may cause secondary effects on processes influencing the resilience of seagrass populations (e.g. depletion of carbon reserves in rhizomes, changes to reproductive phenology), key ecological interactions (e.g. herbivory, disease susceptibility, microbial interactions), and valuable ecosystem services (e.g. carbon sequestration, habitat structure, sediment accretion).

Although in situ studies are limited, thermal stress thresholds have been identified in laboratory studies of other seagrass species (see reviews by Lee et al., 2007; Nguyen et al., 2021). There are important trait differences between seagrass species that likely influence stress thresholds, such as growth rate and the ratio of aboveground to belowground biomass, and recent evidence suggests that sensitivity to thermal stress likely varies between populations habituated to different temperature regimes (Beca-Carretero et al., 2018). However, much of the conceptual framework of thermal stress impacts on seagrass metabolism and carbon balance transfers between species. Additional studies of other species, including this study of surfgrasses (Vivanco-Bercovich et al., 2026), will broaden understanding of the physiological thresholds governing seagrass responses to thermal stress.

Physiological breakdown with extreme thermal stress

Vivanco-Bercovich et al. (2026) exposed surfgrass (Phyllospadix scouleri) to thermal stress in a simulated marine heatwave and measured a suite of ecophysiological responses. This study built on the existing literature by examining an understudied species of seagrass and used experimental temperature treatments that reflect both current levels of warming and predicted more intense end-of-century warming as climate change intensifies. The authors collected surfgrass from a population in the Baja California peninsula, relatively close to the warm distribution limit for the species, and held the plants in recirculating seawater mesocosms. After an acclimation period of 1 week, the plants were exposed to the warming treatments for 7 d, then returned to ambient temperature over an additional 7 d to assess post-warming performance. During each phase, the authors quantified photosynthesis and respiration, nitrogen metabolism, oxidative stress, and growth.

A key finding from this study is that exposure to temperatures 5 °C above ambient (comparable to a contemporary ‘severe’ marine heatwave; Hobday et al., 2018) did not negatively impact the plants, whereas exposure to temperatures 8 °C above ambient (comparable to a future ‘extreme’ marine heatwave; Hobday et al., 2018) caused a widespread negative physiological response with significant declines in daily productivity. A substantial increase in respiration demand for plants in the extreme marine heatwave treatment likely pushed them across a metabolic threshold, leading to a ‘tipping point’ and a negative carbon balance throughout the heatwave. Based on the two temperature treatments in the experiment, the thermal threshold for this population of surfgrass was approximated as 26.5 °C. Marine heatwaves in the region already reach 25 °C, suggesting a limited thermal safety margin before local seagrass populations experience detrimental levels of thermal stress.

Furthermore, the results in Vivanco-Bercovich et al. (2026) emphasize the importance of temporal dynamics in understanding thermal stress effects on seagrass. The negative carbon balance triggered by the extreme heatwave treatment persisted throughout the post-warming phase, indicating that thermal stress responses were not restricted to the duration of an acute event. In addition, for certain response variables, the impact of thermal stress was most evident after warming ceased, indicating a lagged and complex physiological response to warming events. Notably, the variables that showed a significant timing effect tended to be linked to photosynthesis (e.g. effective quantum yield, electron transport rate, maximum fluorescence), whereas impacts on respiration rates were immediate and remained similarly elevated during and after warming. Recent field studies suggest that periods of relief from high temperatures are likely key to the ability of seagrasses to withstand exposure to thermal stress (Berger et al., 2024). Vivanco-Bercovich et al. (2026) show that the magnitude of metabolic responses depends on not only temperature thresholds but also the duration and temporal pattern of exposure to thermal stress, highlighting the importance of studying recovery phases as well as periods of stress.

Translating the plant-level physiological results of this mesocosm experiment to natural populations has important and concerning ecological implications. In surfgrass meadows, like other seagrass habitats, both the physical habitat structure and many ecosystem functions derive directly from seagrass biomass, density, and morphology. The suppressed growth observed here under the extreme heatwave coupled with potential loss of biomass may alter nutrient cycling, carbon sequestration, shoreline stabilization, water purification, and other important functions that rely on shoot density and length. Additionally, stressors that negatively impact plant metabolism can reduce resilience to subsequent disturbances by reducing the accumulation of carbon reserves needed to fuel re-growth and recovery. By identifying how specific physiological responses to thermal stress impact plant growth and productivity, this study provides insight into the mechanisms that underpin seagrass performance in a warming climate. Vivanco-Bercovich et al. (2026) included daily fluctuations of ±1.5 °C to mimic the diel cycle in the field and maintained clonal integrity of the plants to allow for translocation of resources; these experimental design elements improved the generalizability of insights from the mesocosm experiments. Additional research is needed to build on these results and link plant-level metabolic responses to the secondary impacts of thermal stress on ecological interactions and ecosystem functions.

Scaling up from plants to ecosystems

As exemplified by Vivanco-Bercovich et al. (2026), simulated marine heatwaves are a key experimental approach for identifying physiological responses in seagrass that can support the development and testing of mechanistic hypotheses. Other approaches are also needed to fully understand the physiological and ecological implications of climate warming. Common gardens, reciprocal transplants, and long-term field observations across seasons and thermal gradients are all needed to detail how seagrasses will respond to increasing thermal stress. Cross-scale synthesis that integrates plant-level responses and ecosystem dynamics is also critical (Noble et al., 2026), particularly as temperatures continue to warm and marine heatwaves become more frequent and severe.

Seagrasses provide a unique context to deepen understanding of plant responses to the warming climate. Key physiological differences between these marine plants and their terrestrial counterparts affect carbon metabolism pathways, and the dynamic environmental conditions in the coastal zone can both amplify and moderate the impacts of thermal stress. In turn, synthesis of seagrass responses to thermal stress across scales will inform ecological theory of plant and ecosystem resilience, particularly the role of foundation species. Extreme heating events threaten foundational plant communities globally (Smale et al., 2019), and a mechanistic understanding of plant resilience to thermal stress is needed to enable the development of predictive and adaptive frameworks to address ecosystem vulnerability.
