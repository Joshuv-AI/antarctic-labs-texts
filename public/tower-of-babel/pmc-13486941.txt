Beyond treatment response: Injection‐site reactions as part of the extended‐release buprenorphine experience
Authors: Julien Cabé, Georges Brousse
Published: 2026

In their recent mixed‐methods study, Lowry et al. highlighted important variation in patient responses to and experiences of extended‐release buprenorphine [1]. We agree that long‐acting injectable buprenorphine should not be viewed as a uniform treatment experience and would argue that one practical component of this experience deserves greater clinical attention: injection‐site reactions.

Long‐acting injectable buprenorphine has become an important treatment option for opioid use disorder, with demonstrated benefits for treatment retention, clinical stability and patient‐reported outcomes [2, 3, 4]. As it expands in routine care, injection‐site reactions are likely to become an increasingly visible concern for clinicians and patients [5, 6, 7], potentially acting as a barrier to treatment uptake and limiting access to this therapeutic option. Yet the available evidence suggests that these events are usually mild to moderate, transient and manageable, rather than treatment‐limiting [3, 8]. The most commonly reported reactions are pain, pruritus, erythema, swelling and induration [3, 8]. Palpable nodules may also occur, and often reflect the expected local tissue response to depot formation rather than an infectious complication. Importantly, the incidence and severity of these reactions appear to decrease over time in many patients [5, 7].

This distinction between expected local reactogenicity and true complications is clinically important. More severe events, including cellulitis, abscesses, ulceration and skin necrosis, have been reported, particularly in the context of suboptimal injection technique or inadvertent intradermal administration [8, 9, 10]. However, these events appear uncommon and do not necessarily preclude continuation of treatment if they are recognised early and managed appropriately [10, 11]. The practical implication is that clinicians need a framework not only for identifying complications, but also for normalising and anticipating benign reactions.

This patient‐centred framing is consistent with findings from Neale et al., who showed that the first days after long‐acting injectable buprenorphine initiation may involve a mixture of distressing and beneficial bodily experiences [12]. In that context, proactive explanation of possible local reactions may reduce anxiety, improve preparedness and support adherence. Prevention should therefore be considered part of routine care: strict subcutaneous administration, correct angle and depth of injection, site rotation and avoidance of local friction after injection are all simple but important measures [13, 14]. Early conservative management of mild reactions with cold compresses and standard analgesics, and of more moderate reactions with topical hydrocortisone and oral antihistamines, may help support treatment acceptability and maintain patient confidence [11]. Some published reports further suggest applying ice for 10–15 minutes before injection to reduce pain, and considering local analgesia when pain is more substantial [11, 13, 14].

If long‐acting injectable buprenorphine is to achieve its full potential, injection‐site reactions should not be framed primarily as a barrier to treatment uptake. Rather, they should be understood as predictable and usually manageable events that require good technique, patient education and early clinical attention. A clearer and more standardized approach to anticipating and managing these reactions may help preserve adherence and strengthen confidence in a treatment with substantial clinical benefit.
