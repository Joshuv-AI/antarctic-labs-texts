Retained foreign bodies in spine surgery: Never events, near never events, but not just adverse events
Authors: Nancy E. Epstein, Marc A. Agulnick
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background: Retained foreign bodies (RFB), or those left behind following spine surgery, are considered “Never Events (NE < 1/1000: they should never happen),” or “Near Never Events (NNE < 1/100; they should nearly never happen)”, but are not just “Adverse Events (AE >/= 1/100).” The vast majority of NE/NNE are due to cotton sponges, cottonoids, or residual cotton strands (i.e., collectively called Textilomas or Gossypibomas). However, RFB additionally included; fractured needles, guidewires, fractured screws/implants/drains, and/or broken instruments (i.e., scalpels). Notably, the spine surgeon of record, as captain of the ship, is primarily liable for RFB and is central to ensuing medicolegal suits. However, secondarily liable are the adjunctive surgical/medical personnel, (i.e., physicians, Physician Assistants, Nurses, Nurse Practitioners, Physical Therapists, Occupational Therapists), and others who are independent or work full-time for hospitals.Methods: Patients with RFB may present with acute, subacute, or chronic/delayed pain and suffering. Additional complaints include; lost wages, sustained physical disability and/or injury attributed to these objects. Most RFB are diagnosed on plain X-rays, followed by MR and/or CT studies.Results: RBS’s may include; retained drain fragments, broken needles, fractured guidewires, broken scalpel blades, fractured screws, and/or instruments. Retrieval procedures warrant a wide variety of different techniques, some of which fail. Notably, RFB’s largely occur due to the performance of; emergent procedures, doing an unfamiliar operation, encountering anatomical variants, or operating on patients with elevated body mass indexes (BMI). Additionally these include; surgeons’ failure to order and/or radiologists’ failure to correctly read intraoperative X-rays/fluoroscopic images, and/or nurses’ failures to correctly perform end of surgery counts.Conclusion: RFBs, or foreign bodies left behind following spine surgery, are considered “Never Events (< 1/1000)” or “Near Never Events (< 1/100),” and are not just “Adverse Events (> 1/100)”. When they do occur, the operating surgeon bears primary responsibility, but the nursing/adjunctive staff and hospital are also liable.

INTRODUCTION

Following spine surgery, retained foreign bodies (RFB) (i.e., those left behind) are “Never Events (NE < 1/1000: should not happen),” or “Near Never Events (NNE < 1/100: should nearly never happen), but are not just “Adverse Events (AE >/= 1/100: adverse events that normally occur). The vast majority of RFB are due to cotton sponges, cottonoids, residual cotton strands (i.e., termed Textilomas or Gossypibomas). The remainder typically include; retained fractured/broken needles, drains, screws, guidewires, broken instruments/fragments, and/or dangerously migrated implants. RFB may cause acute, subacute, or chronic/delayed symptoms; occasionally, they remain asymptomatic [Table 1]. They are mostly diagnosed on X-rays, but may also be seen on MR and/or CT studies. Varied explanations as to why RFB occur include; emergency procedures, unfamiliar operations, anatomical variants, elevated body mass index (BMI), failure to perform or failure to correctly read X-rays/fluoroscopy, and failed nursing counts at the end of surgery/procedures. Notably, spine surgeons, as “captains of the ship,” are ultimately responsible for most RFB, but the nursing/adjunctive staff, including physicians, Physician Assistants, Physical Therapists, Occupational Therapists and others, whether working for or outside the hospitals are also liable.

Incidence of (i.e. Never Events (< 1/1000), Near Never Events (< 1/100) vs. Adverse Events (>/= 1/100) During Lumbar Disc Surgery (28,395 Patient Sample)

Ramirez et al. (1989) focused on the incidence of major events (i.e., “Never Events” (NE < 1/1000)”, “Near Neer Events (NNE < 1/100)”, vs. “Adverse Events (AE > 1/100), when evaluating 28,395 patients undergoing lumbar disc surgery [Table 1].[14] The mortality rate of 5.9/10,000 (0.59/1000) qualified as a “Never Event.” Of the 157/10,000 other major events, 3 of 4 qualified as NNE (i.e., infection (30.7/10,000 = 0.3/100), new neurological deficits (29.8/10,000 = 0.298/100), pulmonary embolism (10.7/10,000 = 0.1/100), while only 1 event, myocardial infarction met criteria for a NE (5.6/10,000 = 0.56/1000). Reoperations per 10,000 addressed 3 NE (i.e., postoperative hematomas (8.7/10,000 = 0.87/1000), ventral perforation (1.6/10,000 = 0.16/1000), or retained foreign body (0.7/10,000 = 0.07/1000); however, cerebrospinal fluid leaks constituted a NNE (10.5/10,000 = 0.105/100).

X-rays, MR and/or CT Diagnosis of Retained Foreign Bodies (RFB) Most Frequently Consisting of Sponges, Cottonoids, Cotton Strands (i.e., Textilomas or Gossypibomas)

Etiology of Textilomas or Gossypibomas

Seven studies cited different types of RFB diagnosed following lumbar surgery [Table 1].[6,11-13,16] Van Goethem et al. (1991) utilized MR and CT to diagnose paraspinal Textiloma/Gossypibomas (GOS) attributed to leaving behind Cottonoids (Codman, USA) following lumbar diskectomies [Table 1].[16] Okten et al. (2006) further defined textilomas as RFB predominantly consisting of retained cotton gauze pads following prior lumbar disc procedures [Table 1].[13] Other authors similarly reviewed cases in which surgical sponges/cotton or gauze pads, or cotton swabs/patties were left behind in lumbar surgical spinal wounds yielding Textilomas or Gossypibomas [Table 1].[6,11,12]

X-ray, MR, or CT Findings of Textilomas or Gossypibomas

Multiple studies documented classical X-ray, MR, or CT diagnostic findings for Textilomas or Gossypibomas [Table 1].[6,8,11,12,16] In Van Goethem et al. (1991), X-rays clearly showed the central filament marking the cottonoids; however, these were less frequently diagnosed on CT or MR studies.[16] For Kim et al. 7 cases (2007), on T2 WI MR, the center of the Textilomas/Gossypibomas was typically hyperintense, while the rim was hypointense, but markedly enhanced with contrast.[6] Naama et al. (2010) on MR and CT studies usually found these lesions in posterior paravertebral locations where they presented as paraspinal soft tissue masses.[12] In 2015, Luo et al. 6 clinicians readily identified neuropatties measuring 3 x 1 inches or 2 x 1/2 inches on lateral X-rays in cadavers (i.e., placed in the anterior/posterior cranial fossa or thoracolumbar spine), but routinely missed smaller neuropatties measuring only 1/2 x 1/2 inches or 1/4 x 1/4 inches.[8] Meshkini et al. (2017) diagnosed a round, sacral lesion on MR that at surgery proved to be a Textiloma, not a tumor.[11]

Preoperative Differential Diagnoses for Textilomas or Gossypibomas

In most cases, preoperative differential diagnoses for Textilomas or Gossypibomas included paraspinal tumors, scar, and/or foreign body reactions often consistent with infections and/or abscesses [Table 1].[11-13,16] Okten et al., (2006) differential diagnoses included infection or abscess, while for Meshkini et al. (2017), they originally considered these lesions to be tumors.[11,13]

Short vs. Long-Term Delays in Diagnosing Textilomas or Gossypibomas

Some patients with Textilomas or Gossypibomas remained asymptomatic, while others developed symptoms early (i.e., 2 months) or late (i.e., 3, 4, 6, and 12 years later) [Table 1].[5-7,11-13,16] Classically, as in Kim et al., 7 cases, patients presented with just low back pain, often without evidence of infection, and typically not requiring emergency surgery.[6] Lotfinia et al. observed a 53-year-old with an original L2-L3 far lateral disc who presented with recurrent symptoms of low back pain 2 months after the original surgery [Table 1].[7] Alternatively, four studies cited patients as presenting with recurrent symptoms due to Textilomas at 3, 4, 6, and 12 years following their index procedures (i.e., lumbar diskectomy or lumbar surgery).[5,11-13]

Surgical Findings for Textilomas or Gossypibomas

Intraoperatively, surgeons typically found that Textilomas or Gossypibomas were comprised of central cotton matrices surrounded by a peripheral retained foreign body reaction localized to the original surgical site [Table 1].[5-7,11-13,16] Is et al. observed (2007) that retained sponges/Gossypibomas mimicked paraspinal abscesses, but the pathology actually revealed retained sponges between paraspinal muscles.[5] Naama et al. (2010) also confirmed the classical paravertebral locations of these lesions.[12]

Long-Term Consequences of Retained Drains

Gausden et al. (2015) summarized the adverse events observed for 7 patients exhibiting long-term retained drains following spinal surgery (1990-2008) [Table 1].[4] The study included 7 cases; of these, 5 had surgery, while 2 without surgery experienced no untoward events. Two years later, none of the 5 patients having drains removed required additional surgery, while those treated nonsurgically experienced no further adverse events. Based on these findings, the authors concluded that there was likely no significant morbidity for leaving at least some asymptomatic drains alone (i.e., without removal/reoperation) in the future.

Cauda Equina Adhesions Caused by Retained Foreign Bodies Following Laminectomy: An Experimental Rat Study

Creating an experimental model to mimic epidural retained foreign body reactions, Yamagami et al. (1993) evaluated the histology of epidural cauda equina adhesions (CEA) following laminectomies performed in rats [Table 1].[18] The study design included 4 groups: I Sham procedures (wound exposure only), 2 Laminectomy, 3 Laminectomy and leaving behind an extradural thread to mimic an RFB, and 4 Laminectomy placing extradural Kaolin (i.e., a ceramic). Groups 2 and 3 showed postoperative adhesions that largely resolved within 6 weeks, while for group 4, the adhesions were maintained for 12 weeks. Notably, groups 2-4 also showed destruction of the myelin sheath and axonal loss, along with adhesive arachnoiditis and other neural degenerative/inflammatory findings.

Retained/Entrapped Fractured Fragments of Kerrison Rongeurs, Scalpel Blades, Guidewires, and Pedicle Screws, Broken Catheters, or Migrated Cages Requiring Various Retrieval Techniques

Multiple studies documented various retained fractured fragments from Kerrison Rongers, broken scalpel blades, fractured guidewires, broken catheters, or migration of implants [Table 1].[1-3,9,10,17,19]

Fractured Scalpel Blades In Disc Spaces After Lumbar Diskectomy/Laminectomy

Two studies discussed 5 total cases of retained, fractured, broken scalpel blades deep in lumbar disc spaces following diskectomy/laminectomy [Table 1].[1,2] In the first study, Amirjamshidi et al. (1994) presented 4 cases in which retained broken scalpel blades could not be easily retrieved deep in disc spaces during lumbar diskectomies; notably, 2 of the 4 migrated once patients were ambulatory.[1] Due to the inherent risk of major abdominal visceral/vascular injuries from these retained fragments, the authors recommended stringent further attempts at retrieval. In a second study by Aras et al. (2014), a scalpel blade/part also fractured deep into the disc space during a lumbar diskectomy.[2] One day postoperatively, the fragment could be seen on the lateral X-ray anterior to the sacrum. He underwent a laparotomy to remove the fragment, but the fragement had already entered the iliac vein. Emergent angiography further revealed that it had rapidly embolized into the inferior vena cava. Subsequently, the patient required an emergent right lateral thoracotomy to retrieve the fractured/broken scalpel fragment/part.

Failed Attempt to Capture Broken Distal Fragment of Thoracolumbar Pedicle Screw Using “ Interference Fit Technique”

In 1997, Vanichkachorn et al. discussed risks of fracture/breaking of pedicle screws during implantation that could risk nerve root injury, a dural sheath violation, and/or vascular trauma [Table 1].[17] They then presented a case involving a broken distal fragment of a thoracolumbar pedicle screw. Their attempt to capture the screw fragment utilizing the “Interference Fit Technique” failed, causing the broken fragment to migrate ventrally into the retroperitoneal space. Subsequently, utilizing both X-ray and CT, they localized the fragment immediately adjacent to the aorta, and then succeeded in its’ atraumatic removal.

Previous Lumbar Drain Catheter Fracture Resulted in Retained Guidewire Requiring Removal

In 2015, Manix et al. performed the percutaneous retrieval of a guidewire that had fractured intrathecally during an attempt to remove a previously placed lumbar drain catheter; utilizing a Loop Snare Device, the broken guidewire was retrieved without incident [Table 1].[10]

3 Cases of Retained Posterior Lumbar Interbody Fusion Cage (PLIFC) Due to Intraoperative Antepulsion into Retroperitoneal Space

In Ceylan et al. (2015), three patients exhibited intraoperative antepulsion of PLIFC cages into the retroperitoneal compartment; they were subsequently removed [Table 1].[3]

Retained Fractured Pedicle Cannulation Probe

Yuk et al. (2017) described a patient with a postoperative persistent right L5 radiculopathy [Table 1].[19] This was diagnosed as due to a Retained Fractured Pedicle Cannulation Probe that had become embedded into the vertebral body; the authors subsequently used a mallet and osteotome to loosen and remove the fragment.

Retained Metal Tip of Kerrison Rongeur

Lv et al. (2018) described a 50-year-old male where the metal tip of a Kerrison Rongeur fractured, and was left behind following a C5 Anterior Corpectomy/Fusion (ACF) [Table 1].[9] Intraoperatively, surgeons were aware that the fractured fragment (i.e., cutting edge of the Kerrison punch) had moved behind the C4 body; they then extended the surgery to include a C4 corpectomy, but still could not retrieve the fragment. Two years later, it had spontaneously migrated behind the C2 body; at this point it was left in place since the patient was asymptomatic.

Need for Improvement in Standardized Postoperative Surgical Counting Processes to Eliminate Retained Foreign Objects

Van Anderson et al. (2025) assessed the standard surgical count processes used following spine operations [Table 1].[15] In a quality improvement initiative, they assessed errors resulting in retained foreign bodies, and additionally looked at Count Breakaway Components (i.e., including counts of pedicle screw stems and nut tops). They determined that counting errors occurred when they relied on vendors for counting, where their own documentation was inaccurate, and/or when they failed to consult/review the count with a circulating nurse. Their Root Cause Analysis revealed that the main causes of retained foreign bodies included; human errors, a lack of communication in the operating room, and the failure to utilize a formal counting process. Their introduction of new procedures led to no new “Breakaway Incidents”; these procedures included standard counting times, use of visual tools, and more effective documentation techniques for the medical record.

Conclusion

Foreign bodies left behind following spine surgery are typically “Never Events (< 1/1000)” or “Near Never Events (< 1/100)” and only rarely meet the criteria for “Adverse Events (>/= 1/100)”. When they do occur, the operating surgeon bears primary responsibility, but the adjunctive surgical/medical personnel, including nursing, Physical Therapy, Occupational Therapy, and other hospital staff members are also liable.
