Epigenetic Regulation of Imprinted Genes in the Neurodevelopment of Offspring Born to Women with Preeclampsia: A Review
Authors: Xingyi Qi, Ying Zhang, Xinying Liu, Yanhong Xu, Chengcheng Jin, Zewei Liang, Xia Xu, Jianying Yan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPreeclampsia (PE) is a severe hypertensive disorder of pregnancy that poses significant risks to both maternal health and offspring development. Increasing epidemiological evidence suggests that children born to women with PE have a higher risk of neurodevelopmental disorders (NDDs), including autism spectrum disorder (ASD), cognitive impairment, and attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD). However, the underlying biological mechanisms remain incompletely understood. This review aims to summarize current evidence on the association between PE and offspring neurodevelopmental outcomes, with a particular focus on the potential role of imprinted genes in epigenetic regulation. A literature search was conducted using databases including PubMed, Web of Science, and Scopus, covering studies published up to 2025. Relevant articles were identified using keywords such as “preeclampsia”, “imprinted genes”, “epigenetics”, “DNA methylation”, and “neurodevelopment”. Current evidence suggests that epigenetic alterations, particularly aberrant DNA methylation of imprinted genes, may be associated with disrupted placental function and potentially influence fetal brain development through proposed mechanisms. These changes are thought to be involved in the placenta–brain axis, although most findings remain associative and are derived from a combination of human observational studies and animal models. In conclusion, imprinted genes may play an important role in the epigenetic mechanisms linking PE and offspring neurodevelopmental abnormalities. However, findings across studies remain heterogeneous and sometimes inconsistent. Therefore, further studies are required to clarify causal relationships and to translate these findings into clinical applications.

Introduction

Preeclampsia (PE) is a pregnancy-specific hypertensive disorder that typically occurs during the mid to late stages of gestation,1 affecting approximately 2%-8% of pregnant women worldwide2 and posing serious risks to both maternal and neonatal health.3,4 The hallmark features of PE are new-onset hypertension and significant proteinuria after 20 weeks of gestation.5,6 Although its precise pathogenesis remains incompletely understood, both clinical and pathological studies indicate that placental dysfunction is central to disease development.6–8 Placental insufficiency can result in reduced oxygen and nutrient supply to the fetus, thereby potentially impairing brain development9–12 and increasing the risk of neurodevelopmental disorders (NDDs), such as autism spectrum disorder (ASD), cognitive impairment, and attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD), among the offspring of affected mothers.13–19

In recent years, increasing evidence has highlighted the role of epigenetic regulation as a pivotal mechanism in this process.20–22 Among various epigenetic factors, particularly due to their enriched expression in placental tissues and established roles in coordinating fetal growth and brain development, imprinted genes have attracted particular attention. These genes influence neuronal differentiation, synapse formation, and neural circuit establishment through parent-of-origin–specific expression mechanisms,23–25 suggesting that they may serve as important regulators of offspring neurodevelopment in pregnancies complicated by PE. This review aims to synthesize current evidence on the role of imprinted genes and their epigenetic regulation in the neurodevelopment of offspring born to women with preeclampsia, highlighting imprinted genes as potential molecular mediators linking placental dysfunction to neurodevelopmental outcomes.

Literature Search Strategy

A comprehensive literature search was conducted using PubMed, Web of Science, and Scopus databases for studies published up to 2025. The following keywords were used: “preeclampsia”, “imprinted genes”, “epigenetics”, “DNA methylation”, and “neurodevelopment”.

Relevant original studies, reviews, and experimental studies were included. Studies not related to neurodevelopmental outcomes or epigenetic mechanisms were excluded.

Epidemiological Evidence Linking PE to NDDs in Offspring

A growing body of epidemiological research suggests that PE is associated with an increased risk of NDDs in offspring,13,26,27 indicating a potential link to long-term neurodevelopmental challenges.

Large population-based registry studies and birth cohort investigations have consistently reported that children born to mothers with PE have a higher incidence of ASD.14,28,29 Furthermore, the severity and early onset of PE appear to be positively associated with the likelihood of ASD diagnosis in offspring.30 Evidence also supports an association between PE and ADHD, as demonstrated by several case-control and prospective cohort studies.29,31–33

In addition, some studies have suggested an increased risk of intellectual disability and other neurodevelopmental impairments in children exposed to PE.28,34 However, much of this evidence is derived from regional or small- to medium-scale studies, and findings remain inconsistent.

Notably, not all studies have identified a statistically significant association between PE and specific NDDs, particularly ASD.35 These inconsistencies may be attributed not only to differences in study design and sample size, but also to residual confounding factors such as prematurity, intrauterine growth restriction, and shared genetic susceptibility between mothers and offspring. Importantly, most available evidence is based on observational studies, which limits the ability to infer causality.

Overall, current evidence supports a potential association between PE and an increased risk of ASD and ADHD, particularly in large-scale registry studies from Nordic and North American populations. However, evidence linking PE to other NDDs remains limited and requires further validation through well-designed longitudinal and multicenter studies.

Potential Role of Imprinted Genes in Offspring NDDs Induced by PE

Imprinted Genes

Imprinted genes are a unique class of genes that exhibit monoallelic expression, whereby only the maternal or paternal allele is expressed during embryonic development, while the other allele is epigenetically silenced, primarily through DNA methylation. This parent-of-origin–specific expression is essential for normal mammalian growth and development and represents a highly specialized form of epigenetic regulation.36 The proper function of imprinted genes depends on tightly regulated DNA methylation patterns and histone modification states.37 Disruptions in these regulatory mechanisms may lead to aberrant gene expression, which has been associated with developmental abnormalities and increased susceptibility to disease.

Aberrant Expression of Imprinted Genes

Imprinted genes play essential roles in normal neurodevelopment, including the regulation of neuronal proliferation, differentiation, axon guidance, and synaptic formation.24 Many of these genes38–41 are highly expressed in key brain regions such as the cortex and hippocampus and exhibit dosage-sensitive effects, making their precise regulation critical for normal neural circuit development.36

Dysregulation of imprinted gene expression may disturb intrinsic growth factor signaling pathways and neurodevelopmental processes, potentially affecting both prenatal brain development and postnatal neural function.39,42,43 Such alterations may influence neuronal differentiation, synaptogenesis, and cortical organization.44

Abnormal expression of certain imprinted or imprinting-related genes, such as UBE3A45–47 and DLK1,48 as well as downstream neurodevelopmental genes including members of the DLX family,49–51 has been associated with neurodevelopmental abnormalities. Epigenetic alterations related to PE may affect the expression balance of these genes, thereby potentially contributing to altered neurodevelopmental outcomes in offspring.

Abnormal DNA Methylation

Aberrant DNA methylation observed in PE may alter the transcriptional activity of imprinted genes and affect chromatin structure, thereby potentially influencing cellular proliferation and differentiation in the developing fetal brain.52–54

Evidence from animal models suggests that maternal hypertension and placental hypoxia—key features of PE—may be associated with altered methylation patterns of imprinted genes in fetal brain tissues.55,56 These changes may alter the expression of neurodevelopment-related genes, thereby affecting neuronal survival, axon growth, and synaptic function.

For instance, methylation-related alterations in genes such as BDNF have been linked to changes in hippocampal neurogenesis, which may influence learning and memory.57,58 However, the molecular pathways linking PE to these epigenetic modifications remain incompletely understood and require further investigation.

Indirect Regulation via Neuro-Immune-Metabolic Pathways

PE is characterized by systemic inflammation, oxidative stress, and altered energy metabolism, which may indirectly influence fetal neurodevelopment by affecting immune balance and nutrient transfer at the maternal–fetal interface.59

Imprinted genes have been reported to participate in immune and metabolic regulation and may therefore indirectly contribute to neurodevelopmental processes. For example, genes such as MEG3 and PEG3 have been implicated in immune cell differentiation and cytokine regulation. Dysregulation of these genes may be associated with inflammatory responses and altered metabolic efficiency, which could affect neural progenitor proliferation and synapse formation.60–63

Additionally, imprinted genes such as DLK1 and GNAS are involved in metabolic signaling pathways, and their altered expression may influence neuronal energy availability and development.64,65

Maternal-Fetal Interface and Placental-Brain Axis Signaling

The placenta serves as a critical interface for maternal–fetal exchange, regulating oxygen, nutrient, and waste transport. Imprinted genes are widely expressed in placental tissues and are involved in trophoblast proliferation, differentiation, apoptosis, and vascular remodeling.66

These genes contribute to placental development and function. For example, IGF2 is involved in promoting placental growth,22 whereas PHLDA2 has been associated with restricting placental expansion.22,66 Other genes, such as MEST21 and PEG10,21,22,67 have been implicated in trophoblast differentiation and angiogenesis.

Placental-derived signals may influence fetal brain development through the placenta–brain axis,59 suggesting that imprinted genes may act as molecular mediators within the placenta–brain axis. Dysregulation of this axis in PE may affect oxygen and nutrient delivery to the fetal brain, potentially influencing neuronal differentiation and synaptic development.68 However, the extent to which this pathway directly contributes to neurodevelopmental outcomes remains to be fully clarified.

Regulation of Neurotransmission and Synaptic Plasticity

Certain imprinted or imprinting-related genes may be involved in the regulation of neurotransmitter systems and synaptic plasticity. For instance, the DLX gene family plays a central role in the differentiation of GABAergic interneurons within the central nervous system, and its dysregulation can disrupt the balance between excitatory and inhibitory signaling.69–72

Disruption of these processes may affect synaptic density and neurotransmission efficiency, potentially influencing neural network function.39 Such changes may be associated with altered neurotransmitter synthesis, release, and synaptic modulation, which could contribute to the development of NDDs such as ASD and ADHD.63

Overall, these mechanisms are likely to be interconnected rather than independent. However, much of the current evidence is derived from experimental models and observational studies. As such, direct causal relationships between PE, imprinted gene dysregulation, and offspring neurodevelopmental outcomes remain to be fully established. Further integrative and longitudinal studies are required to validate these proposed mechanisms.

Interactions Between Imprinted Genes and Other NDD Risk Genes

Certain imprinted genes may act synergistically with high-risk NDD-related genes such as ADNP, KDM6B, and CHD2.73 For example, ADNP activation in microglia triggers the release of pro-inflammatory cytokines via the NLRP3 inflammasome pathway.74,75 Co-occurrence of such inflammation with aberrant imprinted gene expression may exacerbate neurodevelopmental damage. Similarly, CHD2 and KDM6B, which are involved in chromatin remodeling and histone demethylation, may interact epigenetically with imprinted genes to influence neuronal maturation and synaptic formation, thereby increasing susceptibility to NDDs.76,77

Evidence for these interactions is primarily derived from experimental and molecular studies, with limited direct validation in human cohorts. Collectively, these findings suggest that imprinted genes may act as potential mediators of PE- associated neurodevelopmental alterations in offspring. Their interactions with other risk genes represent an important direction for understanding how maternal–fetal disorders shape neurobehavioral phenotypes.

Perspectives and Conclusions

Current evidence suggests that PE is associated with an increased risk of NDDs in offspring, potentially involving complex mechanisms related to epigenetic regulation and imprinted gene expression. Imprinted genes may serve as important links between placental function, maternal–fetal signaling, and neurodevelopment; however, the extent and specificity of these relationships remain to be fully elucidated.

A number of studies have identified altered expression and methylation patterns of imprinted genes such as DLX5, MEG3, and MEST in PE placentas. These molecular alterations have also been detected in maternal plasma21,78 and umbilical cord blood,79 suggesting their potential relevance as biomarkers. Nevertheless, current evidence is largely derived from observational and experimental studies, and their clinical applicability for early detection remains to be validated in large-scale prospective cohorts.

Future research should aim to integrate multi-omics approaches, including single-cell transcriptomics, DNA methylation profiling, and chromatin accessibility analyses, to better characterize the regulatory roles of imprinted genes. In addition, longitudinal cohort studies and experimental validation using animal models and brain organoid systems will be important for clarifying potential mechanisms and improving translational relevance.

This review has several limitations. The evidence summarized is largely derived from observational studies and experimental models, which limits causal inference. In addition, heterogeneity in study design, population characteristics, and epigenetic measurement methods may affect the consistency of findings.

In conclusion, imprinted genes represent a promising area of research in understanding the potential link between PE and offspring neurodevelopment. However, further well-designed studies are required to validate current findings and to determine their clinical significance.
