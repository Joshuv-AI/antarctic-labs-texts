Local-Order-Invariant Logic on Classes of Bounded Degree
Derek R. Aoki

Abstract
Local-order-invariant (first-order) logic is an extension of first-order logic where formulae have access to a ternary local order relation on the Gaifman graph, provided that the truth value does not depend on the specific order relation chosen. Weinstein asked a number of questions about the expressive power of order-invariant and local-order-invariant logics on classes of finite structures of bounded degree, classes of finite structures in general, and classes of locally finite structures. We answer four of his five questions, including showing that local-order-invariant logic collapses to first-order logic on classes of bounded degree. We also investigate epsilon-invariant logic. We show that epsilon-invariant logic collapses to first-order logic on classes of bounded degree by containing it in local-order-invariant logic in this setting, and we give an upper bound for epsilon-invariant logic in terms of local-order-invariant logic on general finite structures. Finally, in the process of proving these theorems, we demonstrate some principles which suggest further directions for showing upper bounds on invariant logics, including an upper bound on epsilon-invariant logic in general.

Introduction

Finite model theory is the study of the descriptions of finite structures, or databases, in formal languages. Generally, questions of concern are about the expressivity or computational complexity of these languages. These languages are known to capture various aspects of computation, such as the seminal result of Fagin
[
11
]
equating the queries in
\mathrm{NP}
to those expressible in existential second-order logic.

In standard logics like first-order logic, fixed-point and transitive closure logics, or the aforementioned existential second-order logic, one can only refer to the relations of the model itself—the semantics. One might wish, though, to be able to reference predicates not in the vocabulary of the input databases, such as the order in which the elements of the database are stored, or the successor relation corresponding to that order. A logic must treat isomorphic structures in the same way; to make this use of orderings or successors meaningful, we must say that the query must return the same answer regardless of ordering. The queries which reference orderings but for which changing the ordering does not change the truth value are called order-invariant.

From here, when we discussion invariant logics, we will specifically refer to invariant first-order logic (as opposed to, for instance, fixed-point logic). By the result of classical model theory known as the Craig Interpolation Theorem, any query defined over arbitrary (possibly infinite) structures which is invariant in this way must be first-order definable. However, the Interpolation Theorem fails in the finite, and this collapse does not occur in the finite either. Thus, there has been a research program investigating the expressivity and computational complexity of such invariant logics on finite structures, of which we will describe the most relevant strands. One strand of investigation has been into finding examples which separate various invariant logics from first-order logic
[
23
,
24
]
. A second has been into computational and descriptive complexity of invariant logics
[
6
,
9
,
25
]
. A third has been into demonstrating collapse in specific instances, such as for successor-invariant logic on classes of bounded degree
[
14
]
or for order-invariant logic on trees, strings, and hollow trees
[
5
,
13
]
. A final one has been into attempting to apply more general techniques of finite and classical model theory to these logics, such as methods using types
[
4
]
and locality
[
16
,
18
,
22
]
.

A specific logic of interest for databases is epsilon-invariant logic, based on Hilbert’s epsilon calculus
[
20
]
of classical model theory, as discussed in
[
23
]
. This is where, for each first-order formula
\varphi(\vec{x},y)
and tuple
\vec{a}
, we choose a specific element
b
of the universe such that
\varphi(\vec{a},b)
holds and fix that, referring to this chosen element in formulae by a term
\epsilon_{y}(\vec{x},y)
. This is of interest in databases because it is in fact equal in expressiveness to the frozen-witness-invariant logic
\mathrm{FO}[witness]^{+}
discussed in
[
2
,
3
]
. Another logic of interest, whose relationship with prior work is instead primarily through locality, is local-order-invariant logic. A local order on a simple graph is a ternary relation
\preceq(x,y,z)
such that when
x
is fixed,
\preceq(x,y,z)
orders the neighbors of
x
. Such local orders have been mentioned in finite model theory before, such as in
[
5
,
7
]
, but as a basis for an invariant logic were first introduced in
[
22
]
. This notion can be easily generalized to arbitrary finite structures by considering instead the Gaifman graph of the structure.

Our contribution
:

1.

In Section 3, we bound local-order-invariant logic on classes of bounded degree, following in the direction of
[
14
]
and answering a question of
[
22
]
. In Section 4, answering all but one of the remaining questions of Weinstein in
[
8
]
, we show that both order- and local-order-invariant logics do not collapse to first-order logic on arbitrary finite or arbitrary locally finite structures. We also show that in many cases of concern, classes with Gaifman graphs of bounded diameter, local-order-invariant logic is just as expressive as order-invariant logic. In Section 5, we bound epsilon-invariant logic by local-order-invariant logic on classes of bounded degree, demonstrating that it collapses to first-order logic on such classes.

2.

We believe that this is the first use of Hanf threshold locality to upper bound invariant logics. Previous upper bound results have followed from actually constructing the invariant structure, such as the explicit construction of a successor in
[
14
]
or of orderings in
[
5
,
15
]
. Ours, however, make use of locality theorems in
[
22
]
which have not been applied concretely until now. We also introduce a method to decompose an invariant logic into simpler ones, applying this to epsilon-invariant logic and indicating that similar decompositions could hold for other invariant logics.

The Gaifman graph is an important object of study because it lets us define notions of locality. Locality theorems tell us that it is enough to look at the neighborhoods of the points of a structure to determine first-order properties of the points and the structure. This is partially encapsulated in the following definition and theorem:

Definition 2
.

Let
r,t\in\omega
. We say that
\mathcal{A}
and
\mathcal{B}
in
\mathfrak{F}_{\sigma}
are Hanf threshold
r,t
-equivalent, denoted
\mathcal{A}\approx_{r,t}\mathcal{B}
, if
\min(|\{a\in\mathcal{A}:\tp_{r}^{\mathcal{A}}(a)=\tau\}|,t)=\min(|\{b\in\mathcal{B}:\tp_{r}^{\mathcal{B}}(b)=\tau\}|,t)
for every isomorphism type
\tau
of
r
neighborhoods in
\sigma
. We say that
\mathfrak{J}\subseteq\mathfrak{F}_{\sigma}
is Hanf
r,t
-threshold local if
\mathcal{A}\approx_{r,t}\mathcal{B}
implies that
\mathcal{A}\in\mathfrak{J}\Leftrightarrow\mathcal{B}\in\mathfrak{J}
.

We say that a query
\mathfrak{J}\subseteq\mathfrak{F}_{\sigma}
is elementary if it is exactly the models of some first-order formula
\varphi
in the language of
\sigma
. We have the following theorem, originally proved by Hanf for infinite models in
[
17
]
and later for finite models by Fagin, Stockmeyer, and Vardi in

]
:

Theorem 3

(Fagin-Stockmeyer-Vardi)
.

Every elementary query on

is Hanf

-threshold local for some

.

Locality results tend not to extend to other logics, as sufficiently powerful ones including monadic second-order logic (

) or first-order logic augmented with a transitive closure operator (

) can see past the neighborhood of a point. However, the following several definitions will be used to introduce a class of logics which do satisfy the Hanf threshold locality theorem above. Specifically, we will use the framework of

Definition 4
.

Let

be a subset of

,

a subset of

.

1.

For

and

\mathcal{A}^{\prime}\upharpoonright_{\sigma}=A
(where

denotes the reduct of
\mathcal{A}^{\prime}
to
\sigma
).

2.

We say that

is an
R
-presentation scheme for

\mathcal{A}
in
\mathfrak{K}

\mathcal{A}^{\prime}\in\mathbb{P}

.

3.

We say that

The first example of presentation schemes to be studied, albeit not in those terms, was order-invariant logic, in which

is binary and a linear order on

. This is, notably, an elementary presentation scheme. Other prevalent examples are traversal-invariant logic, which is when

is binary and a successor relation on a structure corresponding to some linear ordering. The former is elementary, while the latter is not. Each of these have been shown to be strictly stronger in terms of expressive power than first-order logic. The following will be most central to us in this article:

Definition 5
.

A ternary relation
\preceq(x,y,z)
is a local order on a simple graph

if for fixed

,
\preceq_{a}(y,z)\equiv\preceq(a,y,z)
orders the
G
-neighbors of
A
.

As we will see later, this is an elementary presentation scheme. Local orders have some other properties which will be necessary for us to apply theorems of

]

Definition 6
.

Let
\mathbb{P}
be an

3
Collapse of Local-Order-Invariant Logic

In this section, we introduce the notion of a local order on an arbitrary structure of vocabulary
\sigma
, show that the presentation scheme for local orders is local and neighborhood-bounded, prove via a theorem of
[
22
]
that local-order-invariant elementary queries are Hanf
r,t
-threshold local for some
r,t\in\omega
, and conclude by demonstrating that Hanf
r,t
-threshold local Boolean queries on classes of bounded degree are elementary.

We begin with said definition of arbitrary local orders.

Definition 9
.

A ternary relation
\preceq(x,y,z)
is said to be a local order on
\mathcal{A}\in\mathfrak{F}_{\sigma}
if when
x
is fixed,
\preceq
linearly orders the neighbors of
x
in
\mathcal{G}(\mathcal{A})
. We define the
\preceq
-presentation scheme
\mathbb{O}
, where
\preceq
is ternary, as the class of structures

where
\mathcal{A}
is in
\mathfrak{F}_{\sigma}
and
\preceq
is a local order on
\mathcal{A}
. We define the
\leq
-presentation scheme
\mathbb{L}
as the class of structures of the form
(\mathcal{A},\leq)
where

is a linear order on
\mathcal{A}
.

It is well-known and clear that
\mathbb{L}
is elementary, as was remarked earlier. We mention the existence of the following family of formulae
\varphi_{LO,\psi}(R,y)
, where
\psi(\vec{x},y)
is first-order and

is binary, which says that

is a linear order on the substructure
\mathcal{A}\upharpoonright\{a\in\mathcal{A}:\psi(\vec{x},a)\text{ in }\mathcal{A}\}
for every

. The full first-order definition is left for

With that out of the way, we introduce the theorem of

]

Theorem 10

(Lindell-Towsner-Weinstein)

Let

denote the class of elements of

with Gaifman graphs of degree at most

. Let
\mathbb{P}
be a local, neighborhood-bounded elementary

-presentation scheme for
\mathfrak{F}_{\sigma}^{d}
. If
\mathfrak{J}\subseteq\mathfrak{F}^{d}_{\sigma}
is an elementary

-invariant Boolean query on
\mathfrak{F}_{\sigma}^{d}
, then there exist

such that

is Hanf

-threshold local.

Our approach now is simply to show that
\mathbb{O}
is elementary, local, and neighborhood-bounded, after which showing that Hanf

-threshold local queries on

are elementary is sufficient to prove our main theorem. The proofs for these lemmas are below.

We show elementarity, locality, and neighborhood-boundedness for
\mathfrak{F}_{\sigma}
instead of

as this requires no extra effort, is stronger, and will be used in Section 5.

Lemma 11
.

The class
\mathbb{O}\subseteq\mathfrak{F}_{\sigma\cup\{\preceq\}}
of pairs

such that
\preceq
is a local order on
\mathcal{A}
is an elementary
\preceq
-presentation scheme for
\mathfrak{F}_{\sigma}
.

Proof.

The approach is fairly clear: we construct a formula of the form

for every

holds if and only if

and defines a linear ordering otherwise. The technical details are done in

in a formula

\beta_{r}(x,y)

which says that

1.

\preceq(x,y,z)
is restricted to elements satisfying

, and

2.

\preceq(x,y,z)
restricted to

is a linear ordering, using the

mentioned above.

∎

Lemma 12
.

\mathbb{O}
, as defined previously, is a local and neighborhood-bounded
\preceq
-presentation scheme on

.

Proof.

We first address Condition (1), locality. For any
\mathcal{A}
,
\mathcal{A}^{\prime}
such that
\mathcal{A}^{\prime}\mathbb{O}\mathcal{A}
, and

, the

,

,
\mathcal{B}^{\prime}
, and
\mathcal{C}^{\prime}
, we can construct the amalgamation

as follows: Take any local order

on
\mathcal{A}
(of course, at least one exists), and then for any

if and only if

\mathcal{B}^{\prime}
or

holding implies that no two of

,

have distance greater than

in
\mathcal{G}(\mathcal{A})
.
∎

Lemma 13
.

Let
\mathfrak{J}
be a boolean query on
\mathfrak{F}_{\sigma}^{d}
. Then
\mathfrak{J}
is Hanf
r,t
-threshold local if and only if it is elementary.

Proof.

Informally, we wish to exploit the fact that each Hanf
r,t
-threshold local equivalence class is definable, and then use the fact that there are finitely many of these. Let
\mathfrak{J}\subseteq\mathfrak{F}_{\sigma}^{d}
be
r,t
-threshold local. It is known that for any structure
\mathcal{A}\in\mathfrak{F}_{\sigma}
, there is a formula
\varphi_{\mathcal{A}}
such that for
\mathcal{B}\in\mathfrak{F}_{\sigma}
,
\mathcal{B}\models\varphi_{\mathcal{A}}
if and only if
\mathcal{B}\cong\mathcal{A}

[
21
]
. Because the relation
x\in B_{r}^{\mathcal{A}}(y)
is definable in
\mathcal{A}
, there is similarly a formula
\varphi_{\tau}
for every
r
-neighborhood type
\tau
such that
(\mathcal{A},x)\models\varphi_{\tau}
if and only if
\tp_{r}^{\mathcal{A}}=\tau
. Because of the degree-boundedness of elements of
\mathfrak{F}_{\sigma}^{d}
, there is
g:\omega^{2}\to\omega
such that

has cardinality at most
g(d,r)
. There are finitely many pointed
\sigma

up to isomorphism, so there is
n:\omega^{2}\to\omega
such that the isomorphism types of
r

\mathfrak{F}_{\sigma}^{d}
can be enumerated
\tau_{1},\dots\tau_{n(d,r)}
.

For every
\mathcal{A}\in\mathfrak{F}_{\sigma}^{d}
, we define the tuple
c_{r,t}(\mathcal{A})\in\{0,\dots t\}^{n(d,r)}
as
c_{r,t}(\mathcal{A})_{i}=\min(|\{a\in\mathcal{A}:\tp_{r}^{\mathcal{A}}(a)=\tau\}|,t)
. By Hanf
r,t
-threshold locality, for
\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B}\in\mathfrak{F}_{\sigma}^{d}
,

implies
\mathcal{A}\in\mathfrak{J}\Leftrightarrow\mathcal{B}\in\mathfrak{J}
. Denote by
T_{\vec{m},r,t}
the set
\{\mathcal{A}\in\mathfrak{F}_{\sigma}^{d}:c_{r,t}(\mathcal{A})=\vec{m}\}
. We know that for every
\vec{m}
,
T_{\vec{m}}\subseteq\mathfrak{J}
or

Theorem 14

Example 17

Our first application is one that has been documented several times. Define a circular successor relation on a structure

We now come to an example which has not been stated before, but which has been mentioned. Let

Definition 20

We say that a class

Lemma 21

If

is a

-invariant elementary Boolean query if and only if it is an

-invariant elementary Boolean query.

Proof.

We proceed so as to use

(\Rightarrow)

This direction is simpler. Consider the following formula in the language

This says, roughly, that

in

(\Leftarrow)

We will define inductively, using a local ordering, a linear ordering on

for every

The full first-order definitions of these are in

Remark 26
.

As it turns out, order- and local-order-invariant logic are not stronger than first-order logic in this unary case. We sketch the argument below.

Let
\sigma=\langle U_{1},\dots U_{n}\rangle
be a vocabulary with only unary relations. We show that for every
k
, there exists a
k^{\prime}
such that if
\mathcal{A}

\mathcal{B}
are equivalent with respect to
\mathrm{FO}[k^{\prime}]

. This suffices because it means that quantifier rank
k

r

\mathcal{A}\in\mathfrak{F}_{\sigma}
has
m_{i}

. For any
\sigma

\mathcal{A}

such that if

[

5
Epsilon-Invariant Logic

In this section, we explain epsilon-invariant logic, in which formulae are allowed to make use of a choice function on the powerset of the universe as long as the truth value is independent of the specific choice function. We will give upper bounds on epsilon-invariant logic both in general and on classes of bounded degree, using the results of the previous sections for the former. Finally, we give theorems which suggest further possibilities for upper bounds both on epsilon-invariant logic and more traditional first-order invariant logics.

5.1
Collapse on Classes of Bounded Degree

We will give some background on what the epsilon in epsilon-invariant logic is. We define an
\epsilon
term in the language of
\sigma
to be an expression of the form
\epsilon_{y}(\varphi)
, where
\varphi(\vec{x},y)
is a first-order formula in the language of
\sigma

\mathrm{FO}+\epsilon

\sigma
to be the atomic formulae in the language of
\sigma
whose arguments may include
\epsilon

\epsilon_{k}
term as an
\epsilon
term where
\varphi
has quantifier rank at most
k
and
\vec{y}
has length at most
K
, and
FO+\epsilon_{k}
formulae are defined accordingly.

FO+\epsilon
formulae are evaluated over structures of the form
(\mathcal{A},\epsilon)
, where
\mathcal{A}
is an element of
\mathfrak{F}_{\sigma}
and
\epsilon
is a function
\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{A})
to
\mathcal{A}
such that
\epsilon(X)\in X
for

and

is the powerset of the domain of
\mathcal{A}
. In other words,
\epsilon
is a choice function on the powerset of
\mathcal{A}
. An
\epsilon

is evaluated as
\epsilon(\{a\in\mathcal{A}:\varphi(a,\vec{b})\text{ holds in }\mathcal{A}\})
for
\vec{b}
a tuple of elements of
\mathcal{A}
of the same length as
\vec{y}
.

Now, finally, we come to the invariant half of the phrase epsilon-invariant logic:

Definition 27

Let
\varphi
be an

-invariant sentences do not depend on choice of

.

-invariance is defined analogously.

Just as we used

Let

Remark 29

6
Conclusions

We have established the collapses of local-order-invariant and epsilon-invariant logic on classes of bounded degree, as well as answered several questions of Weinstein. We have also introduced a way to decompose presentation schemes into simpler ones. Interesting avenues for further investigation include the following.

1.

Does local-order-invariant logic collapse on other tame graph classes? One way to study this might be decompositions as above, but another might be to extend
Theorem 10
to said tame graph classes instead.

2.

Can upper bounds such as these on epsilon-invariant logic provide us with separation results? It has long been open whether or not epsilon-invariant logic is equal in expressibility to order invariant logic. Could we, for instance, answer this question by demonstrating that order-invariant logic does not collapse to first-order logic on classes of bounded degree?

3.

Can we find decompositions of order- or traversal-invariant logic which might help us prove upper bounds on those?
