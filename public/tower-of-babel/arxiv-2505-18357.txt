Flex: Grid-Responsive Provisioning and Scheduling for Elastic Cloud Clusters
Walid A. Hanafy, Li Wu, David Irwin, Prashant Shenoy

Abstract
. Computing energy demand and grid capacity growth are at a crossroads. Data centers are becoming large, concentrated electricity consumers just as power grids face capacity shortages while also integrating more variable renewable generation, causing grid conditions to change significantly across hours and seasons. These variations create an opportunity for grid-responsive operations, in which workloads are shifted to align energy use with time-varying grid conditions, prioritizing clean, low-cost periods of surplus generation and low congestion. Fortunately, a significant fraction of computing demand comes from batch jobs that are delay-tolerant and elastic, enabling clusters to shift or scale jobs, or adjust the provisioned capacity in response to grid conditions. However, provisioning and scheduling decisions are closely coupled. Effective cluster capacity depends on how jobs are scaled, while job-scaling decisions depend on the available capacity. At the same time, exogenous grid signals ( e.g. , carbon intensity and electricity prices) determine the cost of both scheduling and provisioning decisions. To address this problem, we present Flex , a grid-responsive resource manager for elastic cloud clusters that coordinates provisioning of cluster capacity and scheduling of elastic batch jobs. Flex takes a data-driven approach, periodically learning optimal scheduling and provisioning decisions based on recent history and reusing them to guide coherent runtime provisioning and scheduling. We implement Flex on top of AWS ParallelCluster and evaluate it using real-world workload traces and grid signals, including carbon intensity and electricity prices. Our results show that Flex reduces carbon emissions by up to ∼ \sim 57%, within 2.1% of the oracle, and electricity costs by up to 60% relative to signal-agnostic baselines.

Introduction

Data centers’ energy demand is growing at unprecedented rates
(
Shehabi et al., 2024
)
, with estimates that the global data center energy consumption will reach 1000 terawatt-hours (TWh) by 2026
(
International Energy Agency, 2024
)
, and 6–12% of the total U.S. electricity demand within the next 3–5 years
(
Shehabi et al., 2024
)
. This surging demand is causing severe energy shortages in the grid
(
Lin et al., 2024
)
, and raising concerns about energy prices and the environmental and health impacts of data centers
(
Han et al., 2024
;
Maji et al., 2025
)
.
To address this issue, cloud providers have relentlessly focused on improving the energy efficiency of their data centers through infrastructure- and operational-level optimizations
(
Orgerie et al., 2014
;
Barroso and Hölzle, 2007
)
.
Unfortunately, energy efficiency improvements alone are insufficient to offset the significant growth in energy consumption
(
Koomey and Naffziger, 2015
)
. Efficiency improvements reduce how much energy a data center uses, but do not change
when it is drawn from an increasingly variable grid.
This variability has motivated the design of
grid-responsive
data centers, which align energy consumption with time-varying grid conditions, prioritizing clean, low-cost periods of surplus generation and low congestion
(
Lin and Chien, 2023
;
Colangelo et al., 2026
;
Wierman et al., 2014
)
.

Grid-responsive data center operations react to changes in grid conditions to enhance a data center’s operational efficiency while also helping the grid
(
Qureshi et al., 2009
;
Sukprasert et al., 2024
)
.
To do so, data centers typically respond to
exogenous grid signals
to adjust their energy consumption. For instance, data centers respond to
binary
demand response (DR) signals
(
Zhang et al., 2019
;
Acun et al., 2026
)
, which force data centers to curtail their on-grid energy consumption, as well as
continuous
cost signals, such as carbon intensity, electricity prices, and renewable availability, that determine the cost or emissions associated with that energy consumption.
Given the potential above, there has been significant recent work on
signal-aware
optimizations to reduce costs and emissions of cloud systems by workload scheduling, scaling, and resource provisioning decisions to align the energy consumption with the availability of low-cost or green energy sources
(
Hanafy et al., 2023b
;
Wiesner et al., 2021
;
Souza et al., 2023
;
Acun et al., 2023
;
Dodge et al., 2022
;
Gsteiger et al., 2024
;
Rao et al., 2010
;
Goiri et al., 2012
;
Qureshi et al., 2009
;
Liu et al., 2011
)
.
These efforts focus on batch jobs, such as machine learning training
(
Paszke et al., 2019
)
and HPC workloads
(
Fox et al., 2017
;
Martín-Álvarez et al., 2024
;
Tarraf et al., 2024
)
, which are often delay-tolerant and elastic, enabling data centers to shift and scale this work toward the hours when electricity is cleaner and cheaper, trading modest delays for lower costs and emissions
(
Acun et al., 2023
;
Sukprasert et al., 2024
;
Radovanović et al., 2023
;
Hanafy et al., 2023b
;
Xu et al., 2025
;
Lechowicz et al., 2026
)
.

Existing grid-responsive resource management approaches focus either on
workload scheduling
by deciding when and how to execute batch jobs, whether for individual or multiple jobs, or on
capacity provisioning
by adjusting the number of cloud-provisioned CPU or GPU servers according to the grid signal.
Despite these two decisions being tightly coupled, existing systems do not coordinate their decision-making for these two tasks, treating them as separate and independent problems.
For instance, multiple approaches focus on
scheduling
a single job by adapting the demand against grid signals
(
Wiesner et al., 2021
;
Hanafy et al., 2023b
;
Lechowicz et al., 2023
)
. However, such approaches often require
a priori
knowledge of job length and treat the cloud as an unlimited pool from which resources can always be drawn, which can lead to the “thundering herd” problem
(
Ruane, 1990
;
Hanafy et al., 2026
)
. Cluster-level schedulers apply the same ideas across many jobs by considering a fixed cluster capacity and shifting and scaling jobs to align demand with the hours of cheap and low-carbon energy
(
Hanafy et al., 2024
;
Xu et al., 2025
;
Goiri et al., 2012
)
.
In contrast to scheduling approaches, a second line of work focuses on resource
provisioning
, exploiting the low average utilization of data centers
(
Tirmazi et al., 2020
;
Shehabi et al., 2016
;
Shehabi et al., 2024
)
to scale the resource pool up and down according to the grid signal
(
Lin and Chien, 2023
;
Zhang and Chien, 2021
;
Zheng et al., 2020
)
. For example, Google’s virtual capacity curve (VCC) forecasts future demand to compute a time-varying capacity limit, implicitly pushing flexible work toward low-carbon periods and scheduling jobs within the varied capacity using signal-agnostic policies
(
Radovanović et al., 2023
)
.
However, provisioning and scheduling decisions are highly co-dependent and tightly coupled with the exogenous grid conditions.

Designing grid-responsive capacity provisioning and scheduling approaches must address three key challenges:

Challenge 1:
Coupling between capacity and job scalability.

Expanding capacity is beneficial only if the available jobs can efficiently convert that capacity into useful progress. At the same time, how jobs scale depends on the capacity available to them, while current and future grid signals determine the cost of both provisioning and scheduling decisions.

Challenge 2:
Intertemporal coupling across the scheduling horizon.

This coupling extends across the entire planning horizon. Every deferral or scale-down shifts demand into later time slots. So, the future demand that a cluster must provision depends not only on exogenous workload arrivals and grid conditions, but also on the demand induced by its own earlier scheduling decisions. As a result, grid-responsive operation is inherently sequential, where each decision changes the workload state and reshapes the feasible and cost-effective options available later.

Challenge 3:
Multiple decision timescales.

Scheduling and provisioning decisions operate on different timescales. Capacity adjustments typically require minutes to hours, whereas scheduling decisions may be made at the second granularity. A grid-responsive controller must therefore coordinate fast workload-level decisions with slower infrastructure-level actions without overreacting to short-term fluctuations or missing longer-term grid opportunities.

This coupling across decisions and time makes wrong decisions costly on both fronts. Shrinking capacity at the wrong moment strangles running jobs or forces delays, while growing it prematurely leaves servers idle or assigns them to jobs that scale poorly, hurting completion times and increasing costs.
More importantly, layering a signal-aware capacity curve over a signal-agnostic scheduler will lead to suboptimal and even counterproductive decisions, since whether acquiring or releasing a server is worthwhile depends jointly on the grid signal and on the elasticity and slack of the jobs that would use it, a result we confirm empirically in
Section 6
(See
Figure 15
).
As a result, the resource provisioning and scheduling decisions must simultaneously consider (i) the cluster’s current and expected demand and its elasticity, (ii) the exogenous grid signal and its variations, and (iii) the trade-off between delaying work and the savings that waiting may yield.

To address these challenges, we present
Flex
, a grid-responsive resource manager that jointly provisions cluster capacity and schedules elastic batch jobs.
Flex
builds on a key observation: while these dependencies make it hard to decide any dimension in isolation, there is one place where all of them are fully visible at once,
the realized past
. Over the past time windows, the grid signal, job arrivals, their scaling behavior, and all interactions among them are known exactly, so the jointly optimal provisioning and scheduling decisions can be computed with certainty. And because these inputs recur with diurnal and weekly patterns, decisions that were optimal in the recent past, or in similar situations, remain effective in the near future. Our hypothesis is that
recomputing the optimal provisioning and scheduling decisions over the recent past, and reusing them at runtime, is an effective approach for grid-responsive management of elastic batch workloads
.

Flex
operationalizes this intuition with a
data-driven
approach inspired by
hindsight learning

(
Sinclair et al., 2023
)
, replaying the recent past via an offline algorithm that knows exactly when jobs arrived, their elasticity and delay tolerance, and how the signal evolved.
However, computing this optimal schedule is nontrivial, and we address this complexity by deriving a fast, polynomial-time algorithm that computes the optimal joint provisioning and scheduling decisions.

Flex
then distills these executions into simple provisioning and scheduling runtime parameters, a resource provisioning capacity, and a threshold for marginal progress, enabling jobs to scale up until their added progress no longer justifies the signal’s cost. Then, at runtime,
Flex
matches the cluster’s current state against the recomputed executions and reuses their parameters, keeping provisioning and scheduling coherent without explicit coordination, and falling back to conservative decisions whenever the present no longer resembles the past.

Importantly, by following the offline optimal decisions,
Flex
encodes the dependencies among provisioning and scheduling decisions, the exogenous grid signal and its variations, and the sequential nature of the decisions. As a result,
Flex
addresses the key limitations of prior work and operates without (i)
a priori
knowledge of job lengths, since the threshold prioritizes jobs by their observed marginal progress rather than their declared duration, and (ii) forecast-based planning over future demand and grid signals, since it reuses decisions from the realized past, which embeds how demand responds to provisioning and scheduling decisions.

In designing, implementing, and evaluating
Flex
, our paper makes the following contributions.

(1)

We present the design of
Flex
, a grid-responsive resource manager that coordinates cluster capacity provisioning and scheduling of elastic batch jobs using hindsight learning—continuously relearning optimal decisions based on recent history and reusing them at runtime.

(2)

We implement
Flex
on top of AWS ParallelCluster
(
Amazon Web Services, 2024
)
and evaluate it on CPU and GPU clusters running elastic MPI and machine learning workloads, complemented by year-long trace-driven simulations.

(3)

Our evaluation spans real-world workload traces and grid signals across eight grid regions, and compares
Flex
against signal-agnostic, per-job scaling, fixed-capacity, and provisioning-only baselines, as well as an oracle with perfect knowledge of future signals and job lengths.
Using carbon intensity as the grid signal,
Flex
reduces energy-related emissions by up to
\sim
57% compared to signal-agnostic baselines, coming within 2.1% of an oracle with perfect future knowledge, and cuts energy costs by up to 60% under the electricity-price grid signal.

2.1.
Grid-Responsive Data Centers

Data centers are major contributors to load in most electricity grids
(
Colangelo et al., 2026
)
.
To maintain safe and reliable grid operation, utilities and system operators have traditionally relied on
binary
demand-response (DR) signals, where large consumers are asked or incentivized to adjust their electricity consumption in response to grid needs
(
Acun et al., 2026
;
Faruqui et al., 2009
;
Liu et al., 2013
;
Wierman et al., 2014
;
Colangelo et al., 2026
)
.
Depending on the objective, DR may require data centers to curtail power during peak-demand or emergency periods, or encourage them to reduce consumption through economic signals such as Coincident Peak Pricing (CPP)
(
Liu et al., 2013
;
Wierman et al., 2014
;
Acun et al., 2026
)
.

In addition to DR signals, grid operators have developed a range of
continuous
grid signals that capture variations in energy supply and demand, such as carbon intensity, electricity prices, and renewable availability.
These signals are often used as proxies for the energy supply mix and for the availability of surplus generation and low congestion.
For instance, energy’s carbon intensity (CI), measured in grams of CO
2
equivalent per kWh of electricity (g
\cdot
CO
2
eq/kWh), captures the greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions per unit of electricity generated.
The emissions from a unit of electricity generated depend on the mixture of generation sources, where fossil-based sources (
e.g.
, natural gas and coal) have a high carbon intensity, while renewable sources (
e.g.
, wind, solar, and hydro) have a low or zero carbon intensity.

The presence of such signals has motivated a broader design goal of
grid-responsive
data centers. Rather than reacting only to discrete DR events, a grid-responsive data center continuously aligns its workload execution, capacity provisioning, and scheduling decisions with time-varying grid conditions. By shifting flexible demand toward periods with lower carbon intensity, lower electricity prices, higher renewable availability, or lower grid congestion, data centers can reduce emissions and operating costs while also providing more predictable and flexible demand to the grid
(
Lin and Chien, 2023
;
Colangelo et al., 2026
;
Wierman et al., 2014
;
Chien, 2021
)
.

2.3.
Signal-aware Scheduling and Provisioning

Grid-responsive scheduling has focused on approaches that schedule jobs against the grid signal (
e.g.
, carbon intensity and electricity prices), and on provisioning that adjusts the cluster size accordingly.

Temporal Shifting.

The temporal variations in carbon intensity have motivated researchers to utilize the inherent temporal flexibility of batch jobs by running them during low-carbon periods and suspending them during high-carbon periods
(
Wiesner et al., 2021
;
Souza et al., 2023
;
Hanafy et al., 2024
;
Acun et al., 2023
;
Lechowicz et al., 2023
;
Sukprasert et al., 2024
)
.
The same idea extends to electricity price, where schedulers shift batch jobs toward cheaper hours
(
Goiri et al., 2011
)
.
In addition, researchers have proposed elastic scheduling methods in which jobs are typically scaled down during low-carbon periods and suspended during high-carbon periods, eliminating the need to extend the expected completion time and increasing savings compared to typical suspend-resume approaches
(
Hanafy et al., 2023b
;
Souza et al., 2023
)
.
The key issue is that these approaches schedule individual jobs, relying on hand-tuned thresholds
(
Wiesner et al., 2021
;
Souza et al., 2023
)
that require manual effort to balance savings against performance, or on full knowledge of job lengths
(
Hanafy et al., 2023b
;
Lechowicz et al., 2023
)
, which is error-prone in practice
(
Kuchnik et al., 2019
;
Ambati et al., 2021
)
.
They also ignore cluster-wide capacity constraints, causing demand bursts during low-carbon periods
(
Hanafy et al., 2026
)
, a phenomenon known as the stampede or thundering-herd problem.

Capacity Provisioning.
At the cluster level, grid-responsive optimizations utilize the ability to vary the cluster capacity as well as the low average utilization of data centers
(
Tirmazi et al., 2020
;
Shehabi et al., 2016
;
Shehabi et al., 2024
)
, changing the cluster capacity based on temporal variations in the grid signal, predominantly carbon intensity
(
Radovanović et al., 2023
;
Lin and Chien, 2023
;
Perotin et al., 2023
;
Zheng et al., 2020
;
Zhang and Chien, 2021
)
.
The key idea behind these approaches is to vary cluster size based on electricity carbon intensity, opportunistically using larger cluster capacities during low-carbon periods.
For example, Google’s virtual capacity curve (VCC)
(
Radovanović et al., 2023
)
computes a time-varying capacity limit and schedules jobs within it, while
(
Lin and Chien, 2023
)
scales capacity based on electricity prices.
However, these approaches vary capacity without leveraging application elasticity or workload characteristics, leading to higher costs, emissions, or delays.

2.4.
Elastic Batch Jobs and Scaling Profiles

Elastic scaling, also referred to as malleability
(
Tarraf et al., 2024
;
Martín-Álvarez et al., 2024
)
, is the ability to change the allocated resources seamlessly and has been shown to be applicable to a broad class of distributed batch jobs.
For instance, machine learning frameworks (
e.g.
, PyTorch
(
Paszke et al., 2019
)
), Data Processing frameworks (
e.g.
, Spark
(
Zaharia et al., 2012
)
), Parallel Programming Frameworks (
e.g.
, MPI
(
Comprés et al., 2016
)
and Charm++
(
Kale and Krishnan, 1993
)
) allow applications to adapt resources dynamically.
Elastic scaling capabilities have enabled cluster operators to
avoid head-of-line blocking, improve fault tolerance, and reduce energy consumption
(
Tarraf et al., 2024
;
Gupta et al., 2014
;
Prabhakaran et al., 2015
;
Xiao et al., 2020
;
D’Amico et al., 2019
;
Peng et al., 2018
;
Jayaram Subramanya et al., 2023
;
Qiao et al., 2021
)
. In contrast, we exploit elastic scaling to make clusters grid-responsive, reducing their carbon emissions or electricity cost.

Elastic scaling of a batch job must consider its scaling characteristics, since batch jobs rarely scale linearly with the number of allocated servers. Typically, the scaling behavior of a distributed job depends on its compute and communication characteristics
(
Jayaram Subramanya et al., 2023
;
Hanafy et al., 2023b
;
Li et al., 2024
)
. The greater the communication per unit of compute, the less the job’s throughput scales, as communication bottlenecks grow with added resources, resulting in diminishing increases in performance.

3.
Grid-Responsive Elastic Cloud Clusters

This section describes the grid-responsive provisioning and scheduling problem in elastic cloud clusters that this paper addresses.
Our work targets a slotted horizon
t\in\mathcal{T}\triangleq\{1,\dots,T\}
and a set of preemptive jobs
\mathcal{J}
, with
N=|\mathcal{J}|
, that run concurrently on multiple servers and communicate across components during execution.
The cloud cluster is homogeneous, consisting of either CPU or GPU servers, and its capacity can be varied over time (using cloud interfaces to acquire or release servers) up to a configurable maximum
M
.
Users submit jobs to one of several queues (
e.g.
, by priority), each with a pre-configured maximum delay, or “slack,” chosen according to their willingness to delay in exchange for higher savings.
Each job
j
has an arrival time
a_{j}\in\mathcal{T}
, a length
l_{j}
, and a best-effort delay target
d_{j}
of its selected queue
i
.
In the offline setting, however, job lengths are known, so we map this delay budget to a completion deadline
e_{j}=a_{j}+l_{j}+d_{j}
that the ILP enforces.
At each time slot, each job
j
can use a number of non-shared resources
k
allocated to the job that can be varied at run-time between an upper and lower bound:

k\in[k_{j}^{\min},k_{j}^{\max}]
where
k_{j}^{\min}
and
k_{j}^{\max}
denote the minimum and maximum numbers of servers that can be allocated to that job.
Our model also supports non-elastic workloads (i.e.,
k_{j}^{\min}=k_{j}^{\max}
), in which only the cluster capacity is scaled (see
Section 6.4
).

Each job
j
has a
normalized
elastic scaling throughput profile
\Phi_{j}:k\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
with
\Phi_{j}(0)=0
and non-increasing marginals over
k\geq 1
,
\Delta_{j}(k)\triangleq\Phi_{j}(k)-\Phi_{j}(k-1)
, normalized so that
\Delta_{j}(k_{j}^{\min})=1\geq\Delta_{j}(k_{j}^{\min}+1)\geq\cdots\geq 0
.
This assumption is realistic for elastic jobs, as adding more resources will not introduce superlinear behavior but rather diminishing returns
(
Hanafy et al., 2023b
;
Jayaram Subramanya et al., 2023
)
.
In practice, the profiles can be learned from profiling or performance
(
Qi et al., 2017
;
Oyama et al., 2016
;
Pei et al., 2019
;
Justus et al., 2018
;
Cai et al., 2017
;
Peng et al., 2018
;
Shi et al., 2018
)
.
Using a resource at time slot
t
incurs a cost
c_{t}
set by an exogenous
grid signal
, where
c=\{c_{1},c_{2},\dots,c_{T}\}
is the sequence of signal values over the
T
-length window.
We formalize the offline problem as an integer linear program (ILP).

Decision variables.

x_{j,t,k}\in\{0,1\}
: whether the
k
-th resource unit for job
j
is active at time
t
,

y_{j,t}\in\{0,1\}
: whether job
j
is running in slot
t
.

At runtime, however, the future grid signal, arrivals, and job lengths are unknown. Each slot,

), directing capacity to the jobs that turn it into the most progress while respecting each job’s slack. As highlighted in

4.
Flex
Design

This section presents
Flex
’s design and its elastic scaling-driven provisioning and scheduling algorithms.

4.1.
Flex
Overview

Flex
is a grid-responsive resource manager for batch-oriented cloud clusters. The design of
Flex
is based on three key principles:

(1)

Elastic Scaling:

Flex
applies elastic scaling to both its provisioning and scheduling policies, with a grid-responsive focus.
Flex
dynamically adjusts the cluster capacity and scale of batch jobs in response to variations in the grid signal, workload demand, and workload scalability.

(2)

Separate Provisioning from Scheduling:

Flex
views cluster resource management as two distinct tasks: provisioning and scheduling. The provisioning policy
\phi(\cdot)
determines how many servers to acquire from the cloud for the entire cluster, while the scheduling policy
\psi(\cdot)
determines which batch jobs to run on the available servers and how many servers to allocate to each. This separation of provisioning and scheduling is similar to that in other frameworks, such as Mesos
(
Hindman et al., 2011
)
. Importantly, this separation is at the level of
execution
, and
Flex
still learns the two policies
jointly
from the oracle, so provisioning accounts for how jobs actually scale.

(3)

Data-Driven Learning:

Lastly, inspired by
hindsight learning

(
Sinclair et al., 2023
)
,
Flex
takes a
data-driven
approach that simulates an offline oracle policy over past job arrivals to determine how an optimal algorithm (with full knowledge of job characteristics, the grid signal, and future arrivals) would schedule jobs cost-efficiently, and distills those decisions into the provisioning and scheduling parameters it uses at runtime.
This works because the grid signal and arrivals
recur
with diurnal and weekly regularity, so a situation
Flex
faces at runtime usually resembles one in the recent past, and the oracle’s decision for it remains effective. In addition, we handle workload changes by relearning these parameters over a rolling window, adapting to distribution shift. Nonetheless,
Flex
includes a basic fallback policy that serves as a safeguard when it cannot mimic the optimal policy’s behavior, ensuring reasonable performance during sudden changes.

Together, these principles address the three challenges from
Section 1
.
Flex
’s data-driven learning replays an offline oracle that jointly optimizes capacity and scheduling over a past window, thereby provisioning capacity in light of how jobs actually scale (Challenge 1) and how earlier decisions reshape the future queue (Challenge 2). Elastic scaling provides the knobs to implement these decisions at both the cluster and individual job levels, while separating provisioning from scheduling allows the two to operate at their natural timescales (Challenge 3).

Figure 3
depicts
Flex
’s architecture and shows how the design principles above are instantiated through the learning and execution phases. In doing so,
Flex
employs a two-step approach: 1) a Learning Phase, where
Flex
employs data-driven learning over the most recent cluster execution traces and captures the key decisions at different runtime states, and 2) an Execution phase, where
Flex
utilizes such knowledge to enhance its provisioning and scheduling, as we detail below.

4.2.
Hindsight Learning Phase

Flex
’s hindsight learning phase (see
Figure 3
) employs data-driven learning on recent cluster execution logs by replaying them to an offline oracle algorithm and learning from its provisioning and scheduling decisions. The process involves periodically (
e.g.
, daily) tracking the job arrival logs and grid signal traces over a window of length
T
and replaying those traces to a simulated oracle algorithm. Note that the oracle algorithm cannot be employed in practice, as it requires full knowledge of the job arrival sequence, job characteristics, and grid signal variations. However, since the learning phase operates on historical traces, the entire arrival trace, job characteristics, and grid signal traces are known over the window
T

These oracle decisions can be viewed as mappings from the overall system state at each time
t
to the cluster capacity used for that state and the scheduling behavior in that case.
As an example, consider a simplified system state described by two parameters: a grid signal value at time

The tuple mappings from the oracle’s simulated decisions at each time step

Algorithm 1
uses a greedy approach to elastically scale and schedule jobs.
The key insight is that for any given signal value, doing more work per unit of energy (i.e., greedily choosing jobs with lower

\rho_{j,t}^{(k)}=\frac{c_{t}}{\Delta_{j}(k)}

j,t\in[a_{j},e_{j}]
and
k\in[k_{j}^{\min},k_{j}^{\max}]
.
Then, it sorts the list (
Algorithm 1

The algorithm then iterates over the list, greedily assigning resources to jobs while respecting the maximum capacity, using the

Baselines.
We compare

4

While these baselines were proposed for carbon intensity, we utilize them to optimize whichever grid signal is active.

For fairness, we adapt these baselines to schedule workloads within a variable capacity cluster, and we assume that all baselines have access to historical traces and can use the mean job length for computing the schedule:

(1)

Agnostic
: This policy represents the status quo, where jobs are scheduled FCFS without elastic scheduling, which we use as a baseline to compute the savings for all policies.

(2)

(

)
: We utilize GAIA’s Lowest-Window Policy, which schedules jobs in a non-elastic manner by selecting the best start time based on the mean job length within a time window
w

(3)

WaitAWhile

(
Wiesner et al., 2021
)
:
We implement the threshold version of the

WaitAWhile
policy, which operates the job in a suspend-resume fashion according to the grid signal. The threshold is determined by the 30th percentile of grid signal predictions for the next 24 hours. To respect the delay target, the job runs to completion once the permitted delay is exhausted, and we use FCFS to resolve contentions.

(4)

CarbonScaler

(
Hanafy et al., 2023b
)
: We adapted the
CarbonScaler
algorithm to run on a multi-job cluster, where the schedule is computed based on historical job lengths. In addition, to respect the cluster-wide capacity, we prioritize scaling jobs with higher marginal throughput. Lastly, when the job surpasses its delay target, it runs until completion.

(5)

GREEN+

(
Xu et al., 2025
)
:
We implement
GREEN+
, a cluster-level extension of GREEN that uses elastic scheduling and job reordering to reduce total cost and makespan, setting the allowed degradation
\gamma

To make it comparable to our delay-aware policies,
GREEN+
implements a per-job delay budget, in which jobs may wait while slack remains but are prioritized for execution once their target delay is exhausted.

(6)

Flex (Oracle)
: Finally, we added the offline oracle as a baseline that implements
Algorithm 1
and assumes full knowledge of the grid signal and job length.

Related Work

Signal-aware scheduling and provisioning.

Prior carbon- and price-aware schedulers temporally shift work to favorable hours, for individual jobs
(
Hanafy et al., 2023b
;
Wiesner et al., 2021
;
Lechowicz et al., 2023
;
Souza et al., 2023
)
or across a fixed cluster
(
Hanafy et al., 2024
;
Xu et al., 2025
)
. A complementary line adjusts
capacity
, growing and shrinking the server pool with the grid signal
(
Radovanović et al., 2023
;
Zhang and Chien, 2021
;
Lin and Chien, 2023
;
Wijayawardana and Chien, 2026
)
or participating in demand response
(
Xing et al., 2023
;
Lin and Chien, 2025
;
Zhang et al., 2019
;
Acun et al., 2026
)
. These treat scheduling and provisioning separately or plan against demand forecasts. Closest to our work, SPADE
(
Lechowicz et al., 2026
)
is signal-aware and provisions dynamically, but targets DAG data-processing with threshold-based deferral.
Flex
instead
co-designs
capacity provisioning with elastic job scheduling, resolving their circular dependence by replaying oracle decisions rather than forecasting demand.

Elastic scheduling.

Elastic and malleable schedulers
(
Tarraf et al., 2024
)
scale jobs to optimize makespan
(
Peng et al., 2018
;
D’Amico et al., 2019
)
, throughput
(
Jayaram Subramanya et al., 2023
;
Xiao et al., 2020
;
Qiao et al., 2021
)
, energy
(
You et al., 2023
)
, and carbon
(
Xu et al., 2025
;
Hanafy et al., 2023b
)
.
Flex
differs by making the cluster grid-responsive and jointly sizing its capacity.

Data-Driven Resource Management.

Flex
’s data-driven policy relates to case-based reasoning
(
Watson and Marir, 1994
)
and hindsight learning
(
Sinclair et al., 2023
)
, which reuse solutions from similar past cases.
Related data-driven resource management approaches match incoming jobs to profiled ones (Paragon
(
Delimitrou and Kozyrakis, 2013
)
), infer SLOs from past runs (Morpheus
(
Jyothi et al., 2016
)
), or predict workloads from telemetry (Resource Central
(
Cortez et al., 2017
)
).

Flex
differs by distilling the oracle into simple run-time parameters, not a case store or learned policy.

8.
Conclusion

This paper presents
Flex
, a grid-responsive resource manager for cloud clusters.
Flex
employs a data-driven approach to guide near-optimal scheduling and provisioning decisions for elastic batch jobs across CPU and GPU clusters. Our evaluation showed that
Flex
reduces energy-related emissions by up to 57%, is within 2.1% of an oracle, and generalizes to a real-time electricity-price signal, cutting costs by up to 60%. In the future, we plan to extend our grid-responsive provisioning and scheduling approaches to interactive workloads and heterogeneous cluster settings.

while keeping it feasible, we rebalance along an alternating trail in the bipartite job-slot graph, adding
m_{\ell}
and alternately removing and adding units at the visited jobs and slots so that every job’s total benefit (its work requirement) and every slot’s total usage (its capacity
M
) are preserved. Every unit removed along the trail is feasible in the residual instance at step
\ell
, so by greedy minimality (
12
) and
1
it has ratio at least
\rho(m_{\ell})
. Because moves are ordered by non-decreasing cost-per-benefit, this exchange does not increase the total cost
(
Federgruen and Groenevelt, 1986
)
.

Repeating the exchange transforms
\mathsf{OPT}
into
\mathsf{G}
without ever increasing cost, so
\mathrm{cost}(\mathsf{G})\leq\mathrm{cost}(\mathsf{OPT})
, contradicting
\mathrm{cost}(\mathsf{OPT})<\mathrm{cost}(\mathsf{G})
. Therefore
Algorithm 1
returns a minimum-cost schedule.
∎
