Mindfulness in nursing education: an integrative review
Authors: Ashley Fenton, Rika Win, Maureen Ndzi, Kathryne Neiling
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesTo assess the impact of mindfulness-based interventions (MBIs) on nursing students and identify best practices for integration into nursing education.DesignIntegrative literature review guided by PRISMA standards.Data sourcesPubMed, CINAHL, PsycINFO, and Embase were searched for studies published between 2019 and 2024.MethodsStudies focused on MBIs for undergraduate or graduate nursing students were evaluated using the Johns Hopkins Evidence-Based Guidelines. Data were synthesized to identify outcomes and implications for education.ResultsFifteen studies met inclusion criteria. MBIs were consistently associated with reductions in stress, anxiety, and depression, and improvements in emotional regulation, self-care, and spirituality. Common strategies included meditation, breathing exercises, guided imagery, and structured training. Benefits consistently short-term, with limited evidence on long-term effects or comparative effectiveness.ConclusionsMBIs show promise in supporting nursing student well-being and resilience but evidence is insufficient to confirm long-term impact on professional burnout. Findings should be interpreted cautiously given variability in study design and outcome measures Future studies should include more diverse populations, evaluate sustained outcomes, and compare delivery methods to guide curricular integration.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Keywords: Mindfulness; Nursing education; Stress, psychological; Burnout, professional; Students, nursing

Introduction

Burnout is a widespread concern in healthcare and nursing education, characterized by emotional exhaustion and reduced personal accomplishment [1, 2]. Nursing students, facing intense academic and clinical demands, are particularly vulnerable. Mindfulness is defined as “the awareness that arises through paying attention, on purpose, in the present moment, nonjudgmentally” [3]. Mindfulness-based interventions (MBIs) offer evidence-based strategies to mitigate stress, anxiety, and burnout in this population. Despite growing interest, there remains a gap in the literature regarding standardized best practices for implementing mindfulness in nursing curricula. This integrative review is necessary to identify effective elements of MBIs and inform future educational interventions.

Background

Nursing students experience heightened stress levels due to the academic and clinical demands placed upon them throughout their education. This stress often leads to burnout, well-documented as a notable concern within the nursing profession. Burnout in nursing students mirrors the professional phenomenon, characterized by emotional exhaustion, depersonalization, and a reduced sense of personal accomplishment [4]. The World Health Organization identifies burnout as an occupational phenomenon stemming from chronic stress that is not adequately managed [2]. Nursing students, facing increasing pressures from coursework and clinical responsibilities, are particularly vulnerable to developing burnout early in their careers, which can negatively affect their academic performance and future professional readiness [5].

Successfully managing stress is crucial for registered nurses (RNs) and advanced practice registered nurses (APRNs) as they navigate the demanding healthcare environment [1]. Nurses who cannot manage their stress effectively are at higher risk for burnout, which can compromise their ability to provide quality care [6]. In the long term, effective stress management is vital for maintaining personal well-being and professional resilience. This is particularly important for APRNs, who often take on leadership roles in patient care and decision-making [1]. Managing stress helps foster clinical decision-making, enhances the nurse’s capacity to engage empathetically with patients, and reduces the risk of burnout that might otherwise lead to early departure from the profession [7]. Nurses who develop stress management skills early in their careers are better equipped to thrive in high-pressure situations and maintain long-term career satisfaction.

The growing body of research highlights the potential benefits of integrating mindfulness into nursing education to address this stress. Mindfulness-based interventions (MBIs) have been shown to reduce anxiety, stress, and symptoms of burnout in nursing students. For example, mindfulness practices that promote self-awareness and emotional regulation can help students manage stress, thereby preventing burnout and improving overall well-being [8]. Studies suggest that incorporating mindfulness programs into nursing curricula supports students’ mental health and fosters resilience and emotional stability, which are crucial for their future roles as healthcare providers [9, 10]. Given the persistent stress levels experienced by nursing students, integrating mindfulness is an essential strategy for academic success and developing long-term professional coping mechanisms [7]. Understanding the role of mindfulness interventions within the varied nursing curricula may help find common themes and patterns that were beneficial or needed improvement to guide new research.

Studies have reported an increase in rates of stress and psychological distress among graduate students, which is primarily attributed to the educational rigor and demands of the program [11]. While mindfulness-based interventions have been shown to decrease symptoms including stress, burnout, anxiety, and depression, particularly in undergraduate nursing students, strategies to implement mindfulness into academia and graduate-level programs have not been well defined in the literature. While findings are promising, variability in intervention content, delivery methods, and measurement tools complicates conclusions regarding effectiveness. Therefore, a synthesis focused on methodological quality and outcome measurement is needed to inform curricular integration.

Purpose

The purpose of this integrative review is to assess the impact of mindfulness on nursing students and determine best practices for integrating into nursing education. Specifically, this review examined outcomes (both positive and negative) of MBIs for nursing students. There are three aims of this review:

Examine elements of mindfulness-based interventions for nursing students.

Examine elements of mindfulness-based interventions for nursing students.

Explore outcomes of mindfulness interventions for nursing students.

Explore outcomes of mindfulness interventions for nursing students.

Assess implications for the development of a mindfulness-based intervention for nursing students.

Methods

This integrative review followed Whittemore & Knafl’s (2005) framework for integrative reviews and sought to examine the literature related to mindfulness interventions in nursing education [12]. This review was also conducted in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines and Johns Hopkins evidence-based guidelines [13, 14]. The comprehensive screening process is reported in the PRISMA flow chart (Fig. 1).

PRISMA flow diagram

Search Strategy: This review followed these steps: (1) database identification, (2) eligibility criteria definition, (3) title and abstract screening, (4) full-text review, (5) data extraction via a standardized form, (6) quality assessment using the Johns Hopkins Evidence Summary Tool, and (7) synthesis of findings.

The review process was a collaborative effort between all four authors. All authors independently screened titles, abstracts, and full texts. Disagreements were resolved via consensus. Data extraction included intervention type, outcome measures, and sample characteristics. Themes were synthesized through iterative coding and cross-comparison. Inclusion and exclusion criteria were determined prior to beginning the literature search. Inclusion criteria included English, full-text journals, and online sources. To ensure the most recent findings, only articles from the past five years were included (2019–2024). The population in the literature must have included nursing students in either undergraduate or graduate programs. Studies reporting neutral or negative outcomes were not excluded, and all results were synthesized to reflect the full range of findings. Exclusion criteria included non-English and non-full-text articles. Literature published prior to 2019 was also excluded from the search. Articles that included professional working nurses only were not included for review. Articles that did not focus on mindfulness as a core component of the intervention were excluded. Studies without interventions (observational) were also excluded. Studies were excluded if they lacked an intervention component, focused solely on practicing nurses, or did not explicitly identify mindfulness as the core of the intervention. In addition, articles were excluded if interventions were not delivered as structured group or curricular program. Peer-led interventions were excluded because they did not align with faculty-led curricular approach. Four major databases, PubMed, CINAHL, PsycINFO, and Embase, were searched for literature. Key search terms included “nursing students,” “nursing education,” “student nurse,” and “mindfulness,” (“nursing students“[MeSH Terms] OR “student nurse”) AND (“mindfulness“[MeSH Terms] OR “mindfulness-based interventions”) AND (“education, nursing“[MeSH Terms]) Filters: English, 2019–2024, Full text. The combination of key search terms for each database was collected to allow for the reproducibility of the search.

Results

A comprehensive search of all databases identified 275 records. After removing 31 duplicates and screening abstracts and full texts, 15 studies met inclusion criteria for this review. Evidence level & quality

Across the fifteen studies included in this review, the majority utilized quasi-experimental designs consistent with Level II evidence according to the Johns Hopkins Nursing Evidence-Based Practice model. Only two studies were classified as randomized controlled trials, representing Level I evidence, the highest level of research strength within this framework [15, 16].

Thirteen studies were classified as Level II quasi-experimental, indicating intervention-based research without randomization [5, 8–10, 17–25]. These studies reflect strong methodological consistency in design, but limited control related to randomization and group assignment.

When rated for quality of evidence, most studies were rated as either A (high) or B (good) quality based on criteria including sample size adequacy, methodological rigor, and use of validated measures. Four studies met A-level quality standards due to sufficient sample sizes, clearly defined interventions, and consistent outcomes [8, 10, 18, 22]. Eight studies were rated as B-level quality, demonstrating reasonably consistent findings and sound methodology but limited by smaller samples or lack of control groups [5, 16, 19–21, 23–25]. One study was classified as C-level (low) quality due to small sample size and methodological limitations [9].

Setting and sample description

The setting for the studies was variable based on location. Of the 15 studies reviewed, 14 involved undergraduate nursing students, with one study focusing on advanced practice registered nurses (APRNs) participating in a family nurse practitioner mindfulness program [21]. Five studies were completed in the United States [9, 17, 19, 21, 25], five in Asia [10, 16, 20, 23, 24], three in Turkey [15, 18, 22], one in Italy [8], and one in Canada [5]. All of the studies reported most participants as female. Fourteen of the studies included undergraduate nursing students while one included advanced practice family nurse practitioner students [21].

Timeframe

All interventions found in the literature included mindfulness-based content; however, the total timeframe of interventions varied. The shortest number of group sessions was three [1], and the longest was 14 weeks [18, 21]. Five studies were between 4 and 5 weeks in length [5, 17, 19, 23, 24]. Seven studies were between 6 and 8 weeks in timeframe [8–10, 16, 20, 22, 25].

The frequency of the group sessions was also found to vary among the studies. Eight of the studies were found to have weekly interventions that included brief instruction through groups or web-based videos and lasted under an hour [5, 10, 17, 19–21, 24, 25]. Four studies’ weekly interventions were between 60 and 90 min [9, 15, 18, 23] and three were two hours or greater weekly [8, 16, 22].

Delivery methods

Most of the interventions took place in person and included education on mindfulness and demonstration of mindfulness practices [8, 16, 18, 19, 21–24]. One intervention was designed to be in person, however due to the COVID pandemic moved to virtual after 75% of the content was delivered in person [21]. Of the interventions delivered remotely, this included live interactive sessions [15, 20, 25] and pre-recorded mindfulness practice videos that could be utilized outside of the live interactive sessions [20, 25]. Two studies were also found to use learning management systems to integrate mindfulness-based content directly into a course [5, 17]. Several interventions were also found to develop or utilize existing phone applications for students to practice outside of the sessions [5, 20].

Content & theory

While interventions all included elements of mindfulness practices, there was variability in the specific content and skills included in the literature. The most found mindfulness content found in the studies included meditation [5, 9, 16–18, 23–25], breathing exercises [9, 15, 16, 18, 19, 21, 22], body awareness [10, 17, 19, 22], mindful eating, [9, 16, 19] yoga [18, 21] and guided imagery [15, 21].

Other unique content found in this review included mandala drawing [15] as well as the inclusion of clinical learning attributes (knowledge and understanding, willingness, professionalism, communication and interaction, personal attributes, and skills preparation) [20]. Theoretical frameworks for the interventions were also noted to have similarities among the studies. Watson’s human caring theory was utilized to guide the development of two interventions [15, 19]. Cognitive behavioral-based therapies were also found to be a guiding framework for the interventions [8, 16]. In addition, one intervention used the transactional theory to guide implementation [10].

Another important element within the interventions was using reinforcement activities outside of the group to encourage applying knowledge. These assignments included practicing mindfulness skills and homework assignments between group sessions [16, 18, 20]. One intervention also used blogging and weekly reflection between group sessions to reinforce learned content [5]. Another study included peer support sessions to encourage and reinforce mindfulness practices between the group sessions [20].

Outcomes

In addition to understanding the content and delivery methods of mindfulness-based interventions for nursing students, this review also examined how outcomes were measured and the overall impact on students’ well-being. A summary of outcome measures and findings (including statistical significance) is displayed in Table 2 Summary of Mindfulness Interventions and Outcomes in Nursing Education. The most frequently reported outcomes across the fifteen included studies were reductions in perceived stress and increases in mindfulness. Stress was commonly assessed using the Perceived Stress Scale (PSS-10) [5, 10, 17, 21, 22, 25] and the Depression Anxiety Stress Scale, (DASS-42) [22, 24] while DASS-21 was also used to capture stress symptoms in one study [20]. Similarly, anxiety was measured primarily with the State-Trait Anxiety Inventory (STAI) and DASS subscales, showing consistent post-intervention decreases [9, 15, 17]. Anthony and Prasad [24] also used the DASS and found reductions in depressive symptoms.

Mindfulness was one of the most frequently measured constructs, typically evaluated using the Mindful Attention Awareness Scale (MAAS) [5, 9, 10, 17, 21, 22] or the Five Facet Mindfulness Questionnaire (FFMQ) [18, 23]. These measures consistently demonstrated increases in mindful awareness, attention regulation, and present-moment focus following participation in yoga, meditation, or structured mindfulness programs.

Several studies incorporated holistic well-being and self-regulatory frameworks such as self-care, self-compassion, and resilience. Improvements in self-care and self-efficacy were observed in four studies [16, 18–20], often measured with instruments such as the Self-Compassion Scale (SCS), Self-Care Inventory (SCI), or Self-Care Assessment Worksheet (SCAW). Emotional regulation and emotional intelligence also improved in two studies [15, 16] awhich used the Scale of Positive and Negative Experience (SPANE) and the Wong and Law Emotional Intelligence Scale (WLEIS) respectively. Both reported significant post-intervention improvements, with [16] finding increased emotional intelligence across all domains and [15] demonstrating enhanced emotional regulation reflected by higher positive and lower negative affect scores on the SPANE.

Given the academic and clinical demands of nursing education, burnout has become an increasingly important outcome. Two studies reported reduced burnout following mindfulness-based interventions, measured with the Maslach Burnout Inventory (MBI) and the Copenhagen Burnout Inventory (CBI) [8, 20]. Other notable outcomes included enhanced spiritual well-being scores as measured by the Spiritual Well Being scale [15], improved communication and interpersonal skills measured using the Supportive Communication scale for nursing students and the Caring Behaviors Inventory [16].

Collectively, these studies indicate that mindfulness-based programs positively influence multiple dimensions of nursing students’ well-being, reducing stress and anxiety, fostering mindfulness, and supporting holistic domains of professional and personal growth. Two studies also incorporated qualitative analyses to complement quantitative outcomes. Lam et al. [20] identified themes of nurturing self-care through mindfulness, empowering essential skills for clinical practice, and maintaining momentum alongside challenges and enablers. Similarly, Yagi et al. [23] highlighted themes of initial challenges to mindfulness practice, becoming more mindful, intrapersonal benefits, and improved interpersonal awareness. Notably, Lam et al. [20] also found that while mindfulness techniques effectively decreased burnout, they did not significantly alleviate depression, anxiety, or stress over time, underscoring the nuanced and multifaceted nature of mindfulness outcomes in nursing education research.

Discussion

This review examined elements and outcomes of mindfulness-based interventions for nursing students and evaluated their potential for integration into nursing curricula. Most studies demonstrated that participation in mindfulness-based interventions was associated with decreases in perceived burnout, stress, anxiety, and depression, as well as improvements in spirituality and self-care among nursing students. These findings suggest potential value in integrating mindfulness approaches within nursing education. However, the available evidence primarily reflects short-term outcomes. For example, one study [20] reported that mindfulness techniques reduced burnout among nursing students but did not significantly alleviate depression, anxiety, or stress over time, underscoring the need for longitudinal research to evaluate the durability of these effects.

The results of this literature are consistent with previous findings in the literature and growing body of evidence, including two studies, a meta-analysis, and systematic review, which revealed mindfulness-based interventions as a positive coping mechanism, showing benefits in increasing self-awareness, better physiological health, improved academic and clinical performance, stress and burnout reduction, and decreased levels of anxiety and depression among nursing students [26]. Differences in reported outcomes may be related to the focus of each intervention, as well as duration and modality. For example, longer programs (e.g., 14-week yoga courses) examined outcomes such as self-compassion, whereas shorter interventions more often measured stress relief. Thus, the distinction may reflect differences in what was studied rather than program length alone. One study [16] found that participation in an eight-week mindfulness program was associated with significantly higher emotional intelligence scores, particularly in self-awareness, emotional regulation, and empathy, suggesting this may be an area for further exploration.

Furthermore, a United States-based qualitative study exploring the pros and cons of mindfulness techniques among first-generation ethnic minority undergraduate nursing students supported some of the findings of this study [27]. In this US-based study, mindfulness techniques were associated with positive coping outcomes for the rigors and stressors of the educational program [27]. There was an overarching theme of the benefits of incorporating mindfulness education into the curriculum and the need to continue research in health promotion for nursing students [26, 27]. A recent systematic review [28] synthesized randomized controlled trials evaluating interventions for academic burnout among nursing students, including approaches such as Qigong, progressive muscle relaxation, laughter therapy, and coping skills training. When comparing this review to another systematic review, similar observations were made with the positive effects of stress management interventions in nursing education but also highlighted the need for larger-scale studies in a variety of settings and cultures [28].

Implications for practice

While the reviewed studies demonstrate improvement in outcomes such as stress reduction, self-compassion, and emotional intelligence, the current level of evidence is not strong enough to recommend specific mindfulness strategies as a means of mitigating burnout among new nurses. None of the included studies demonstrated a direct impact on long-term burnout outcomes after students entered the workforce, highlighting a critical gap in the literature. Furthermore, it remains unclear whether certain mindfulness activities are more effective than others, or what the essential components of such programs should be.

Despite these limitations, supporting the mental health of nurses entering the workforce is essential to mitigating burnout rates and fostering self-care skills that extend into professional practice. Nursing curricula should address mental health proactively as a preventative measure. Key considerations for integration include timing of delivery (e.g., prior to clinical rotations or during transition-to-practice courses), use of pre/post assessments, and tailoring strategies to realistic nursing schedules [16, 18, 20]. Several studies found benefits when mindfulness education was placed before high-stress transitions such as clinical rotations or entry into practice [8, 15, 16, 20, 21, 23].

Overall, mindfulness-based practices appear to help alleviate stress and can be effective when embedded in nursing education, but additional large-scale, longitudinal studies are needed to determine which approaches are most effective and how best to sustain their benefits [9, 17–20, 22–24].

Strengths

This review focuses on the effectiveness of mindfulness interventions in stress reduction and burnout among nursing students. The included studies provide consistent evidence of positive outcomes, such as reduced anxiety and stress, improved mindfulness, and enhanced emotional regulation. These findings align with the overall goal of developing coping strategies for nursing students, regardless of gender or academic level. While a wide range of mindfulness practices were examined across diverse settings, this variability underscores the need to determine which practices are most effective and how best to deliver them within nursing curricula.

Limitations

One limitation of this review is that most studies included a predominantly undergraduate female population. While this reflects the demographics of many nursing programs, it limits the generalizability of the findings to other populations, such as male nursing students, graduate nursing students, or those in other healthcare professions. Heterogeneity among the studies outcomes, timeframe and interventions should also be considered as a limitation. Additionally, many studies were conducted in specific geographic regions, such as the United States, Asia, and Europe, which may not fully capture the global diversity of nursing education. Despite these limitations, the results are still relevant to understanding the role of mindfulness in nursing education.

Conclusions

With academic rigor and clinical demands, nursing students frequently experience stress, depression, and anxiety. While current evidence does not establish a direct link between stress during nursing school and later career burnout, it does suggest that early exposure to self-care and coping strategies may provide students with tools that could translate into healthier professional practice. The findings of this review indicate that mindfulness-based interventions can support stress reduction, enhance self-care habits, improve self-efficacy, and strengthen emotion management during training.

Future research should address gaps identified, including the need for studies with more diverse nursing student populations, particularly male students and those in graduate or advanced practice programs, to enhance generalizability. Longitudinal studies are also needed to explore whether benefits persist beyond the intervention period and how they may influence resilience and well-being throughout professional practice. Comparative research examining delivery methods (e.g., in-person vs. virtual) and implementation strategies (e.g., integration into formal curricula vs. extracurricular activities) will help identify the most effective approaches.

Overall, integrating mindfulness-based interventions into nursing curricula shows promise in reducing stress and anxiety and in promoting resilience and well-being among students, thereby better preparing them for the demands of the healthcare profession.
