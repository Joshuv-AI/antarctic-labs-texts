Reoperation after short-segment lumbar spinal fusion surgery in dialysis patients: A retrospective cohort study
Authors: Tomohisa Inoue, Keiji Wada, Jun Sugaya, Ken Okazaki, Toru Doi
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Patients with dialysis are at high risk of reoperation after lumbar spinal fusion surgery. However, the risk of reoperation after short-segment (≤2 fusion levels) lumbar spinal fusion surgery in this patient group has not been completely investigated. This study aimed to compare the risk of reoperation after short-segment lumbar spinal fusion surgery between patients with dialysis and matched controls without dialysis. We retrospectively evaluated 85 patients who underwent short-segment lumbar spinal fusion surgery at a single institution. Based on the current dialysis treatment, patients were divided into the non-dialysis and dialysis groups. We compared the patients’ characteristics, surgical data, pre- and postoperative radiographic measurements, locomotive functional levels in activities of daily living (ADL), rate of whole reoperation, and cause-specific incidence of reoperation such as adjacent segmental disease (ASD) and implant failure between the non-dialysis and dialysis groups. Then, these variables were compared after adjusting for age, sex, and body mass index (BMI) via a propensity-score-matched analysis. In total, 68 patients in the non-dialysis group and 17 patients in the dialysis group were included in this study. The dialysis group was significantly younger and had a lower postoperative ADL status than the non-dialysis group. Moreover, the dialysis group had a significantly higher rate of whole reoperation (8.8% vs 41.2%, P = .003), reoperation due to ASD (4.4% vs 23.5%, P = .027), and reoperation due to implant failure (0.8% vs 11.8%, P = .038) than in the non-dialysis group. Based on a propensity-score-matched analysis, the dialysis group had a significantly higher rate reoperation rate after short-segment lumbar spinal fusion surgery than the non-dialysis group (0.0% vs 58.3%, P = .005). The current study firstly clarified that patients with dialysis are at significantly high risk of reoperation even after short-segment lumbar spinal fusion surgery.

1. Introduction

The number of patients with dialysis is continuously increasing annually worldwide, and recent advancements in the field of dialysis have resulted in a longer life expectancy and a better quality of life in such patients.[1,2] In particular, Japan has the second highest number of patients receiving dialysis worldwide.[3] Moreover, the mean age of patients with dialysis has also increased, with an overall rise of roughly 18 to 20 years by 2016 since 1983 in Japan.[2] By contrast, long-term dialysis can negatively affect bone mineral status, which leads to conditions such as osteoporosis and osteopenia, and can result in several spinal disorders such as destructive spondyloarthropathy (DSA) or spinal canal stenosis.[4–6]

Patients receiving dialysis commonly have multiple general comorbidities such as cardiovascular disease, diabetes mellitus (DM), anemia, malnutrition, and systemic immunodeficiency.[1,7,8] Hence, patients with dialysis undergoing spine surgery are more likely to have a high mortality rate and an increased risk of major perioperative adverse events, including readmission, reoperation, and postoperative complications.[9–15] Therefore, spine surgeons should cautiously plan surgical strategies for spinal disorders in patients on dialysis who present with severe pain or neurological deficits. Previous studies have reported postoperative clinical improvement or increased activity levels after lumbar spine surgery in patients with dialysis.[11,16] A comparison study after matching for background factors showed that the visual analog pain scale and general outcomes after posterior instrumented lumbar spinal surgery did not significantly differ between patients receiving dialysis and controls.[15] However, another study has shown that patients with dialysis had significantly worse clinical outcomes after lumbar spinal surgery compared with controls.[17] Thus, considering whether patients receiving dialysis have better clinical outcomes after lumbar spinal surgery remains controversial; spine surgeons should evaluate perioperative adverse events after lumbar spinal surgery and determine appropriate surgical procedures.

Among major perioperative adverse events after spinal surgery, the prevalence of reoperation after spinal surgery in patients with dialysis dependence has been relatively high based on large-scale databases and retrospective cohort studies.[11,13–15,17–19] In particular, the reoperation rate after lumbar spinal fusion surgery in patients with dialysis was approximately 10% to 21.4%.[14,15,18,19] Postoperative surgical site infection, epidural hematoma, spinal stenosis worsening, implant loosening, and adjacent segment disease (ASD) are the major causes of reoperation after lumbar spinal fusion surgery in patients with dialysis dependence.[11,15,17–19] Especially, patients with dialysis had a higher prevalence of symptomatic ASD after lumbar spinal fusion surgery (43%) and a higher reoperation rate for symptomatic ASD (10%).[18] Patients with dialysis dependence undergoing multilevel fusion surgery are at higher risk of ASD than those with single-level fusion surgery (59% vs 23%).[18] Thus, some studies have shown that patients receiving dialysis who underwent lumbar spinal surgery are at risk of reoperation. However, the risk of reoperation after short-segment lumbar spinal fusion surgery, which is expected to have a lower risk of reoperation due to ASD, in this patient group has not been completely investigated after adjusting for background factors.

This study aims to compare the rate of reoperation after short-segment (≤2 fusion levels) lumbar spinal fusion surgery between patients on dialysis and matched controls after adjusting for age, sex, and BMI.

2. Methods

2.1. Patient selection

This single-center retrospective cohort study was approved by the ethics committee of Tokyo Women’s Medical University. The medical records of patients who underwent spine surgeries for clinical purposes were retrospectively reviewed. This study had minimal risk to patients; thus, the need for informed consent has been waived by our institutional review board. Further, it was conducted in accordance with the relevant guidelines and regulations/ethical principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. The sample size in the present study was determined by including as many consecutive dialysis patients as possible during the study period. Consecutive patient selection was employed to minimize selection bias.

This study enrolled consecutive patients ≥ 20 years old who underwent lumbar spinal fusion surgery from September 2008 to December 2021. The inclusion criteria were as follows: patients who were followed up ≥6 months after surgery; and those who underwent lumbar spinal fusion surgery at 1 or 2 vertebrae levels. The exclusion criteria were as follows. Patients with rheumatoid arthritis were excluded because a higher rate of reoperation after lumbar spinal fusion surgery has been previously reported.[20] Patients who received renal transplantation were excluded because most of them had a history of dialysis. Patients with lumbar spinal disorders caused by tumor, infection, or trauma were excluded because the causes of the disease themselves resulted in excessive spinal destructive changes. Patients who previously underwent lumbar surgery were also excluded because this study aimed to examine the reoperation rate after an initial spinal surgery. Based on the current dialysis treatment, eligible patients were divided into the non-dialysis and dialysis groups.

2.2. Surgical techniques

All surgeries were performed by a single board-certified spine surgeon. Lumbar spinal fusion surgery was indicated for lumbar spinal disorders (including lumbar spinal stenosis with spondylolisthesis, lumbar spinal instability, and severe spinal deformity), lumbar foraminal stenosis, and lumbar DSA. Lumbar spinal fusion surgery was performed using both the oblique lumbar interbody fusion (OLIF) and the posterior lumbar fusion (posterolateral fusion [PLF] or posterior lumbar interbody fusion [PLIF]) techniques. Each surgical technique was detailed as follows: OLIF was performed using the pre-psoas approach from the left side of the abdomen. The lateral aspect of the vertebral body was exposed while avoiding the psoas major muscle posteriorly. After removing the vertebral endplate and the intervertebral disc, bone grafting and intervertebral cage insertion were conducted. Finally, percutaneous pedicle screws were inserted; PLF was performed using traditional open exposure of the posterior lumbosacral spine component. After pedicle screw insertion at the lumbosacral fusion levels, unilateral or bilateral decompression of the spinal canal was conducted. Then, bone grafting was conducted along both sides of the relevant spinal lamina; PLIF was performed using traditional open exposure, decompression of the spinal canal, and pedicle screw insertion posteriorly, as well as the PLF technique. After removing the vertebral endplate and the intervertebral disc, bone grafting and intervertebral cage insertion were conducted at 1 or both sides of the relevant spinal levels.

2.3. Data collection

We assessed the patients’ characteristics (including age, sex, BMI, follow-up period, DM, hypertension [HT], and ischemic heart disease [IHD] such as angina pectoris or myocardial infarction) and surgical data (such as the number of fusion levels, operation time, and the volume of intraoperative bleeding). The type and duration of dialysis treatment were examined in patients with dialysis. To assess clinical conditions, the preoperative and postoperative (timing of final follow-up) locomotive functional levels in activities of daily living (ADL) were evaluated using the scoring system established by the Ministry of Health, Welfare, and Labor in Japan. This ADL scoring system is well recognized and is approved by the Long-Term Care Insurance System to assess the locomotive function in elderly people in Japan.[21,22] This ADL rating scale, expressed as the ADL score, has been previously utilized to assess patients’ clinical status after spine surgery[23] and in dialysis patients who underwent spine surgery.[11] The ADL score was scored on an 8-point scale from 0 (worst) to 7 (best), according to the patients’ ADL status. The detailed scoring system is described as follows: 0, unable to roll over in bed independently; 1, able to roll over in bed independently; 2, unable to transfer to the wheelchair independently; 3, able to transfer to the wheelchair independently; 4, goes out rarely with assistance; 5, goes out frequently with assistance; 6, goes around the neighborhood; and 7, goes out using public transport and goes a long way away from home. For the radiographic assessment of lumbar spine alignment, lumbar lordosis (LL) was evaluated on preoperative and postoperative (timing of final follow-up) lateral X-ray images in the sitting position. LL was determined by measuring the angle between a line drawn parallel to the upper edge of 1st lumbar vertebrae and a line drawn parallel to the upper edge of the 1st sacral vertebrae. The change in LL was defined as the difference between preoperative and postoperative LL. We evaluated the interrater reliability for the measurement of LL by calculating the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC). Two experienced spine surgeons (TI and TD) investigated the interrater reliability in a sample of 10 randomly selected subjects (a total of 20 measurements of LL), with values of 0.60 to 0.80 indicating good reliability and >0.80 indicating excellent reliability.[24] The ICC of the LL measurement for the interrater reliability was 0.986, indicating excellent reliability.

2.4. Assessment of reoperation

We assessed the reoperation after lumbar spinal fusion surgery at ≤ 2 vertebrae levels. Reoperation was defined as a second surgery including the same or adjacent spinal levels to the initial surgical site. The causes of reoperation were also investigated. Among the causes of reoperation, symptomatic ASD was defined as symptomatic spinal stenosis and severe back pain with radiographic change in the adjacent segment levels, such as worsening of spondylolisthesis, segmental kyphosis, and adjacent disc collapse.[25] Cohen’s kappa coefficient (κ) values were calculated to assess the interrater reliability for the assessment of reoperation causes. Two experienced spine surgeons (TI and TD) assessed the reoperation causes in patients who underwent reoperation after short-segment lumbar spinal fusion. Interrater reliability was classified as poor when the κ value was ≤0.20, fair when 0.20 to 0.40, moderate when 0.41 to 0.60, good when 0.61 to 0.80, and excellent when >0.80.[26] The κ value of interrater reliability for assessing reoperation causes was 1.00, indicating excellent reliability.

2.5. Statistical analysis

Data were expressed as mean (standard deviation [SD]). Demographic and surgical data, radiographic measurements, and ADL scores were compared between the non-dialysis and dialysis groups using Fisher exact test for categorical variables and the unpaired t test for continuous variables. The proportion of whole reoperation and each cause of reoperation were also compared between the 2 groups. Moreover, a propensity-score-matched analysis was performed to compare variables after adjusting for background factors. Clinical factors such as age, sex, and BMI were included in calculating the propensity score. The optimal caliper width was determined by calculating 0.2 of the SD of the logit of the propensity score.[27] One-to-one matching between the 2 groups based on the estimated propensity score was conducted using the logistic regression model. A post hoc power analysis was performed for the propensity-score-matched analysis data. All data were analyzed using IBM SPSS Statistics, version 23 (IBM Corp, Armonk). A P value of <.05 was considered statistically significant.

3. Results

We retrospectively reviewed 298 patients ≥ 20 years old who underwent lumbar spinal fusion surgery from September 2008 to December 2021. Of these, we selected 237 patients who were followed up for ≥6 months after surgery and underwent lumbar spinal fusion surgery at 1 or 2 vertebrae levels. Furthermore, 152 patients were excluded from our analyses for the following reasons: patients with rheumatoid arthritis (n = 37); those who received renal transplantation (n = 14); those with lumbar spinal disorder caused by tumor (n = 1), infection (n = 5), or trauma (n = 5); and those who previously underwent lumbar surgery (n = 90). Consequently, 85 patients were included in our study (Fig. 1).

In total, 68 patients (36 men and 32 women) were included in the non-dialysis group and 17 (9 men and 8 women) in the dialysis group. All dialysis patients in our study underwent hemodialysis. The mean duration of dialysis treatment was 183.7 (SD 135.7) months in the dialysis group. Table 2 shows the comparison results of demographic and surgical data, radiographic measurements, ADL score, and reoperation rates between the non-dialysis and dialysis groups. The dialysis group was significantly younger (P = .001) and had a lower postoperative ADL score (P = .025) than the non-dialysis group. There were no significant differences in the proportion of male patients, BMI, follow-up period, DM, HT, IHD, the number of fusion levels, surgical factors, radiographic measurements, and preoperative ADL score between the 2 groups. The dialysis group had a significantly higher reoperation rate than the non-dialysis group (41.2% vs 8.8%, P = .003). ASD was the most common cause of reoperation in the dialysis group. Further, the dialysis group had a significantly higher proportion of ASD or implant failure requiring reoperation than the non-dialysis group (ASD: 23.5% vs 4.4%, P = .027; implant failure: 11.8% vs 0.0%, P = .038).

4. Discussion

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study showing that compared with matched patients not receiving dialysis, those with dialysis had a significantly higher risk of reoperation after short-segment lumbar spine fusion surgery. This study obtained the following findings: First, patients with dialysis undergoing short-segment lumbar spinal fusion surgery had a significantly worse postoperative ADL status and a higher rate of reoperation, particularly due to ASD or implant failure, compared with patients without dialysis. Second, patients with dialysis dependence exhibited a significantly higher rate of reoperation after short-segment lumbar spinal fusion surgery than those without dialysis even after adjusting for age, sex, and BMI. The study results suggest that spine surgeons should make a cautious decision to perform lumbar spinal fusion surgery, even short-segment spinal fusion, in patients with dialysis. Moreover, even after short-segment lumbar spinal fusion surgery without a significant change in lumbar spinal sagittal alignment, which is expected to have a lower risk of reoperation due to ASD, patients with dialysis should be carefully followed up for the risk of reoperation.

Previous studies have also reported that patients with dialysis are at a higher risk of reoperation after lumbar spinal fusion surgery.[11,13–15,17–19] However, these studies had the following issues to evaluate the risk of reoperation after lumbar spinal fusion surgery in patients with dialysis. Ottesen et al reported that patients with dialysis undergoing elective spinal surgery have a significantly higher risk of aggregated adverse outcomes and adverse events, including reoperation.[13] However, this study did not evaluate detailed clinical information or radiographic findings due to a large national database study. Some retrospective cohort studies did not compare the reoperation risk due to lack of controls.[11,14,18,19] Another research has compared the risk of reoperation between patients with dialysis and those without.[17] However, lumbar spinal surgery included decompression alone and decompression with fusion surgery. Yu et al have compared functional outcomes, laboratory data, complications, and solid fusion rate after posterior instrumented lumbar spinal surgery between uremic patients under maintenance hemodialysis and matched control patients. Results showed that patients with hemodialysis had a significantly higher rate of postoperative complications, including reoperation.[15] This is the only comparative study on reoperation after lumbar spinal fusion surgery between matched groups. However, this study did not consider pre- and postoperative lumbar spinal alignment or the number of fusion vertebrae levels. Considering the abovementioned issues, the current study selected the patients who underwent lumbar spinal fusion at 1 or 2 vertebrae levels and examined the perioperative change in lumbar spinal sagittal alignment. Moreover, we performed a propensity-score-matched analysis to adjust for age, sex, and BMI. As a result, this study had a novelty to more reliably clarify a higher reoperation risk in patients with dialysis by limiting the study subjects to those with short-segment lumbar spinal fusion and performing the comparison study after adjusting background factors, although further investigation is still necessary.

Dialysis patients are reported to have an increased risk of low bone mineral density due to hyperparathyroidism and 1,25-dihydroxyvitamin D deficiency,[28] which results in a higher risk of fracture, pseudoarthrosis, and implant failure after lumbar spinal fusion surgery.[12,15,18] The current study revealed that patients with dialysis dependence had a higher reoperation rate, especially due to ASD or implant failure, after short-segment lumbar spinal fusion surgery. Considering previous studies and our findings, reduced bone quality in dialysis patients may contribute to a higher reoperation rate due to ASD or implant failure after short-segment fusion surgery. Recent study has demonstrated the importance of perioperative assessment and management of osteoporosis in adult patients undergoing elective spinal reconstructive surgery.[29] Therefore, for dialysis patients with reduced bone mineral density, perioperative assessment and treatment of poor bone health should be considered even in short-segment lumbar spinal fusion surgery to reduce the risk of reoperation due to ASD or implant failure. Moreover, postoperative clinical and radiographic follow-up should be performed more carefully and frequently in dialysis patients than non-dialysis patients, such as every 3 months after surgery. Further prospective studies with a longer follow-up are necessary to validate the impact of osteoporosis management on reducing reoperation rate after short-segment lumbar spinal fusion surgery in patients with dialysis.

A previous study found that the visual analog pain scale and the general outcome assessment, established as a 5-grade patient-centered questionnaire (feel excellent, feel better, no change, feel worse, and feel terrible), did not significantly differ between uremic patients and matched controls.[15] However, this study did not evaluate the locomotive functional levels. The current study is the first report to assess the perioperative locomotive functional levels and compare the ADL status between patients with dialysis and those without dialysis who underwent lumbar spinal fusion surgery. The study results indicated that patients with dialysis had a significantly lower ADL level after lumbar spinal fusion surgery than those without dialysis. Furthermore, patients with dialysis had a relatively lower postoperative ADL status than matched patients without dialysis via a propensity-score-matched analysis, though not a significant statistical difference. One possible explanation for the difference in postoperative ADL levels between the dialysis and non-dialysis groups in our study might be attributed to variations in reoperation rates between the 2 groups. However, considering that there were multiple factors associated with postoperative ADL status, further prospective studies should be performed.

The current study had a relatively high rate of reoperation after lumbar spinal fusion surgery (7/17, 41.2%) compared with previous studies (10% to 21.4%).[14,15,18,19] One possible explanation for this high incidence of reoperation might be attributed to the relatively longer dialysis duration in our study cohort. A previous report indicated that severe complications after spinal surgery were more likely to occur in patients undergoing hemodialysis for > 15 years.[14] In this study, the mean dialysis duration was 183.7 months (15.3 years). This longer dialysis duration > 15 years may contribute to a relatively higher reoperation rate in our study. Another possible explanation for the high reoperation rate in our study might be caused by the difference in surgical indications for reoperation after lumbar spinal fusion surgery. Considering that our institution is accustomed to the postoperative management of dialysis, surgical intervention for postoperative spinal disorders in dialysis patients was more likely to be easily adopted.

This study has several limitations. First, the sample size was small as the study was conducted at a single-center. Thus, further investigation with a large cohort is required. By contrast, considering that all surgeries in this study were performed by a single spine surgeon, bias regarding surgical technique or indication could have been reduced to conduct a comparison study. Second, lumbar spinal fusion surgery included various approaches, which might have affected the study results. Third, global sagittal alignment was not assessed in our study. Given that we selected patients who underwent short-segment lumbar spinal fusion surgery, which is expected to have fewer changes in spinal alignment, the change in global sagittal alignment might have little influence on study results. In practice, the mean change in LL was only 3 degrees in our study cohort. Fourth, this was a single-center retrospective cohort study with restrictions on the study population levied by the inclusion and exclusion criteria, which potentially bias the generalizability of the findings. The inclusion and exclusion criteria may also lead to the possibility of selection bias. Fifth, this retrospective study had information bias due to unblinded outcome assessment. Sixth, we did not preregister the statistical analysis plan because this is a retrospective cohort study, which may impair the rigor of the study. Seventh, this study did not examine other potential confounders such as bone mineral status, and the mean follow-up period was relatively short to assess reoperation after lumbar spinal fusion surgery, which may have affected our findings. Finally, our study did not assess pain level and clinical outcomes. Therefore, future prospective studies on the risk of reoperation in patients with dialysis with a longer follow-up should be performed to address these limitations.

In conclusion, patients with dialysis are at significantly higher risk of reoperation after short-segment lumbar spinal fusion surgery. The study results may be a resource for patient counseling and efforts to improve treatment safety in dialysis patients undergoing lumbar spinal fusion surgery.
