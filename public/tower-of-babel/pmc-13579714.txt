Genomic surveillance of SARS-CoV-2 in Tunisia during the omicron era: insights from the national influenza & other respiratory viruses laboratory
Authors: Zaineb Hamzaoui, Sana Ferjani, Takoua Bouchouicha, Latifa Charaa, Ichrak Landolsi, Ines Medini, Roua Ben Ali, Sarra Chammam, Salma Abid, Lamia Kanzari, Mouna Ben Sassi, Sameh Trabelsi, Ilhem Boutiba Ben Boubaker
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundOmicron sublineages have dominated global SARS-CoV-2 circulation since late 2021, but there aren’t enough multi-year datasets that detail lineage dynamics and genomic surveillance performance in North Africa. The National Influenza and Other Respiratory Viruses Laboratory in Tunisia has been conducting integrated SARS-CoV-2 genomic surveillance for four years.MethodsBetween 1 November 2021 and 30 September 2025, 8,635 nasopharyngeal swabs (NPS) received through the national sentinel network were tested for SARS-CoV-2 by real-time RT-PCR, of which 1,020 (11.8%) were positive. A total of 669 (65.6%) positive samples were successfully sequenced using Illumina RNA Prep with Enrichment and a respiratory virus panel. Pango lineages and GISAID clades were assigned with standard tools, and sequencing coverage, geographic representativeness, turnaround times, temporal trends, lineage diversity, mutational profiles, and phylogenetic placement were summarized descriptively.ResultsThe overall SARS-CoV-2 positivity rate was 11.8%. Median governorate-level sequencing coverage among RT-PCR–positive samples was 60.0% (IQR 28.6–88.9), and the share of sequences closely matched the share of positives (r = 0.992), indicating near-proportional sampling.While SARS-CoV-2-positive NPS displayed several waves, including substantial peaks in mid-2022 and mid-2023 and subsequent winter peaks, each linked to the establishment or replacement of dominant Omicron lineages, testing volumes indicated recurrent winter rises. Among the 669 sequenced SARS-CoV-2-positive samples, 92 (13.8%) showed co-detection with at least one other respiratory virus, including 16 (2.4%) involving influenza viruses.XBB* lineages accounted for 25.0% of genomes, followed by BA.4/BA.5* (22.1%), JN.1* and descendants (13.6%), BA.2* (12.6%), and BA.1* (9.0%), while Delta represented 8.7% of genomes; the remaining 9.1% belonged to other lineages, including pre-Omicron variants and non-XBB recombinants. Almost all genomes belonged to Omicron-associated GISAID clades GK and GRA. Overall, sequencing quality was high (mapped reads typically ≥0.9), while Tunisian SARS-CoV-2 genomes showed a progressive increase in amino acid changes—from ~30–50 substitutions in early Delta/BA.1/BA.2 to >70–80 in later XBB and JN.1* sublineages, with some recombinants approaching 100 substitutions. Phylogenetic analyses showed that Tunisian genomes clustered within globally circulating Delta and Omicron clades.ConclusionsRapid Omicron sublineage turnover and co-detections with other respiratory viruses, including influenza, were captured by genomic monitoring integrated into Tunisia’s influenza-based sentinel network, which achieved broad sequencing coverage and overall near-proportional sampling despite subnational heterogeneity.This integrated, multi-pathogen approach provides a scalable model for sustained genomic surveillance and future respiratory pandemic preparedness in the region.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12985-026-03262-7.

Introduction

Since late 2019, severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 (SARS-CoV-2) has evolved under heterogeneous immune, epidemiological, and geographic pressures, generating successive variants and sublineages.

In November 2021, the World Health Organization (WHO) classified B.1.1.529 (Omicron) as a Variant of Concern, indicating the onset of a phase marked by increased lineage turnover and substantial antigenic diversity within the Omicron clade [1].

Sustained genomic surveillance has therefore remained essential for situational awareness and for informing public health decision-making.

Omicron rapidly diversified into multiple sublineages that displaced earlier variants. Early waves were dominated by BA.1 and BA.2, followed by BA.4/BA.5 and their descendants, which showed enhanced immune escape compared with pre-Omicron variants [2, 3].

Recombinant Omicron lineages like XBB, which are descended from BA.2 and have convergent spike substitutions linked to widespread antibody escape, emerged as a following evolutionary stage [4].

In parallel, the highly divergent BA.2.86 lineage, and later its JN.1 descendant, attracted global attention because of their extensive spike changes and rapid expansion; JN.1 was classified by WHO as a Variant of Interest in December 2023 [1, 4]. Consistent with these global observations, our prior report from Charles Nicolle Hospital documented early Tunis cases of JN.1 and its defining features [5].

Following the emergence of JN.1, a succession of JN.1-descendant lineages drove transmission through 2024–2025.

Recombinants like XEC and FLiRT lineages like KP.2 and KP.3 spread throughout several locations, demonstrating the ongoing influence of convergent evolution and recombination on the Omicron lineage landscape [6–8]. Instead of a single globally dominating lineage, surveillance by late 2025 revealed co-circulation of several JN.1 descendants with temporal and regional variations in prevalence [8].

In this study, we describe the genomic epidemiology of SARS-CoV-2 in Tunisia from November 2021 through September 2025, quantifying the turnover of Omicron sublineages and summarizing the scale and cadence of national sequencing within an integrated respiratory-virus surveillance pathway. Specifically, we aimed to (i) quantify sequencing coverage and geographic representativeness, (ii) describe temporal patterns of Omicron sublineage replacement, and (iii) assess co-detection of SARS-CoV-2 with other respiratory viruses within a multiplex surveillance framework. This work provides a consolidated reference for Tunisia’s Omicron era and a practical framework for integrated national genomic surveillance going forward.

Methods

Study context and setting

This study was conducted at the Laboratory of Microbiology, National Influenza and Other Respiratory Viruses Center, Charles Nicolle Hospital (Tunis, Tunisia). As part of the national influenza surveillance program, the laboratory functions as a central hub, receiving and analyzing specimens from multiple regions across Tunisia.

Nasopharyngeal swabs (NPS) were routinely screened for SARS-CoV-2 during the acute pandemic phase at the National Reference Laboratory of Influenza and Other Respiratory Viruses national reference laboratory for surveillance of flu and other respiratory viruses. Positive specimens were automatically sequenced to facilitate quick lineage and clade designation. Using shared molecular platforms and overlapping clinical symptoms, SARS-CoV-2 detection and sequencing were included in the influenza-based sentinel approach in the post-pandemic phase to guarantee operational sustainability and efficiency.

During the acute pandemic phase, routine respiratory testing within the surveillance network focused on SARS-CoV-2 alone. From the 2021–2022 influenza season onward, broader multiplex respiratory virus testing was reintroduced, allowing influenza and other respiratory viruses to be documented again within the same surveillance network.

Sample collection and demographics

Between November 1, 2021, and September 30, 2025, a total of 8,635 NPS samples were received at the National Reference Laboratory of Influenza and Other Respiratory Viruses, Charles Nicolle Hospital, of which 1,020 tested positive for SARS-CoV-2.

These 8,635 NPS were collected through Tunisia’s national influenza and other respiratory viruses influenza-like illness (ILI)/ severe acute respiratory infection (SARI) sentinel surveillance network and processed at the National Reference Laboratory; they do not represent all COVID-19 tests performed nationally. The sentinel network includes two categories of sites distributed across all 24 governorates: community-based ILI sites (85 primary health care centers) and hospital-based SARI sites (11 university hospital departments), using WHO 2014 case definitions. The sentinel network covers all age groups presenting with ILI/SARI at participating sites; catchment population is not formally defined at national level. Sampling was standardized: ILI sites forwarded a predefined representative subset (target one NPS per ILI site per week), while SARI sites forwarded NPS from eligible hospitalized SARI cases; no prioritization by region or time was applied. Sentinel sampling was seasonal prior to 2024 (week 40 to week 17) and was expanded to year-round sampling from 2024 onward; consequently, testing volumes fluctuated during epidemic waves and programmatic sampling periods.

This study includes patient demographic and clinical data. Demographic and clinical variables were extracted when available, and missingness was documented. Age and sex were taken from the GISAID submission metadata, where missing values were coded as “unknown”. Clinical metadata were available for 311/669 sequenced cases (46.5%), while age data were missing for 59/669 cases (8.8%). Missingness was not formally modeled, but preliminary inspection suggested variation by year and governorate.

The number of SARS-CoV-2-positive samples (Pg) that were sequenced (Sg) for each governorate g was used to calculate sequencing coverage (Coverage_g = Sg/Pg).

By comparing each governorate’s share of positives (Pg/ΣP) to its share of sequences (Sg/ΣS), geographic representativeness was evaluated. We report Pearson’s correlation (r) between governorates. Pearson’s correlation (r) was used to quantify linear agreement between paired governorate-level shares (share of positives vs. share of sequences) and was computed across the 24 governorates (excluding the “Unknown” stratum) using unweighted governorate-level shares. A two-sided p-value was reported and the 95% confidence interval for r was computed using Fisher’s z transformation. We computed an equity index EI_g = (Sg/ΣS)/(Pg/ΣP) to assess proportionality, and we identified under-sampling when EI_g < 0.80 and over-sampling when EI_g > 1.20. These thresholds (0.8 and 1.2) were used as pragmatic cut-offs corresponding to ± 20% deviation from proportional sampling (EI = 1). The Wilson score method yielded 95% confidence intervals for coverage (z = 1.96). Wilson 95% confidence intervals were computed per governorate and displayed as error bars in Fig. 2B.

Dispersion summaries include the median and interquartile range (IQR = Q3–Q1; 25th–75th percentile). Specimens with uncertain origin were grouped under an “Unknown” stratum; this stratum was included in national totals but excluded from subnational comparisons because it does not correspond to a geographic governorate. Analyses were performed in Python (pandas, numpy, matplotlib).

Molecular diagnostics and multiplex respiratory virus testing

Viral RNA was extracted from nasopharyngeal swab viral transport medium using the Chemagic 360 instrument (PerkinElmer, USA), according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The US CDC Influenza SARS-CoV-2 (Flu SC2) Multiplex Assay was used for the simultaneous detection of influenza A, influenza B, and SARS-CoV-2 by real-time RT-PCR. Influenza-positive specimens were further characterized using CDC real-time RT-PCR assays for influenza A subtyping (A(H1N1)pdm09 and A(H3N2)) and influenza B lineage determination (Yamagata and Victoria) [9]. Additionally, broader respiratory virus screening was performed using the FTD™ Respiratory Pathogens 21 assay according to the manufacturer’s instructions [10]. This assay allows the simultaneous qualitative detection of multiple respiratory viruses, including influenza A and B viruses, respiratory syncytial virus, rhinovirus, seasonal coronaviruses, parainfluenza viruses, human metapneumovirus, adenovirus, and other respiratory pathogens. Co-detections of SARS-CoV-2 with other respiratory viruses identified by multiplex testing were summarized descriptively among sequenced SARS-CoV-2-positive samples. Non-SARS-CoV-2 respiratory viruses detected by multiplex testing were not sequenced systematically, as the sequencing workflow used in this study was specifically designed for SARS-CoV-2.

Next generation sequencing and bioinformatic analysis

Whole-genome sequencing was performed for all SARS-CoV-2–positive NPS with cycle threshold (Ct) values below 30. All Ct < 30 specimens were attempted for sequencing, without additional prioritization by governorate or time. Ct ≥ 30 specimens were not sequenced because low viral RNA input at higher Ct values is more likely to yield incomplete genomes and insufficient coverage for reliable consensus generation and lineage assignment.

Illumina RNA Prep with Enrichment and the Respiratory Virus Oligo Panel (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA) were used to build the libraries, which were then sequenced on an Illumina iSeq 100. After demultiplexing and trimming the reads, consensus genomes were built against Wuhan-Hu-1, assessed using Nextclade (QC and clade assignment), and cross-checked using Genome Detective.

Lineages were assigned within the Pango/Nextstrain frameworks. A mapping between nomenclatures (Pango lineages, GISAID clades, and Nextstrain/Nextclade clades) is provided in Supplementary Table S1. Only genomes that passed quality control (QC) were retained for downstream analyses and submitted to GISAID. QC was first assessed in Nextclade (overall QC status and key metrics such as genome completeness and ambiguity content), and consensus sequences were additionally cross-checked using Genome Detective to confirm lineage plausibility. Genomes failing QC (incomplete consensus with high Ns or Nextclade QC failure) were excluded from downstream analyses and were not deposited. Final consensus sequences and minimal metadata were submitted to GISAID with accession identifiers recorded for traceability. Turnaround time was defined as the number of days between specimen collection and genome submission to GISAID, and was summarized overall and stratified by collection year and governorate using the median and IQR.

For brevity, we adopt the convention that an asterisk appended to a lineage name indicates that the term encompasses that lineage and all its direct descendant sublineages.

Using Nextclade outputs, we compiled sequencing quality and mutation load for each Pango lineage, including the percentage of mapped reads and the total number of amino acid changes and deletions in comparison to the Wuhan-Hu-1 reference (NC_045512.2).

Phylogenetic analysis

Phylogenetic placement of Tunisian genomes was performed using Nextclade for SARS-CoV-2 (https://clades.nextstrain.org).

Consensus sequences were placed into the SARS-CoV-2 reference phylogeny, assigned to Nextstrain clades, and matched to the Wuhan-Hu-1 reference genome (NC_045512.2).

The resulting mutation-annotated tree, including all 669 Tunisian genomes and the global background, was exported from Nextclade and used for graphical visualization. For phylogenetic context, we used Nextclade to place Tunisian consensus genomes onto the global SARS-CoV-2 reference phylogeny and to visualize their distribution among internationally circulating clades. No de novo Tunisia-only phylogeny was reconstructed, no formal clustering or introduction analyses were performed, and branch support metrics were not evaluated.

Results

Sequencing overview and geographic coverage

During the study period (November 1, 2021 to September 30, 2025), the national reference laboratory for surveillance of influenza and other respiratory viruses confirmed 1,020 SARS-CoV-2 infections by RT-PCR. Per protocol, samples with Ct < 30 (n = 820; 80.4%) were attempted for whole-genome sequencing, whereas samples with Ct ≥ 30 (n = 200; 19.6%) were not sequenced. Among the 820 sequencing attempts, 669 genomes (81.6%) passed quality control and were deposited in GISAID; these 669 genomes constituted the analytic cohort for this study and represented 65.6% (669/1,020) of all RT-PCR–confirmed infections detected at the reference laboratory during the study period.

Year-specific estimates are provided in Supplementary Table S2. The proportion of RT-PCR–positive samples with Ct ≥ 30 (and therefore not sequenced) varied over time, from 14.6% in 2022 to 26.6% in 2024. Likewise, the QC pass rate among Ct < 30 sequencing attempts ranged from 90.8% in 2023 to 43.8% in 2025. Governorate-level estimates are shown in Supplementary Table S3. Across governorates, the proportion of Ct ≥ 30 positives ranged from 0.0% to 100.0%, and QC success among Ct < 30 samples ranged from 0.0% to 100.0% (excluding governorates with no Ct < 30 samples available for sequencing). In the highest-volume governorates (Tunis, Sfax, Nabeul, Ariana), QC pass rates were consistently high (93.8–96.8%).

SARS-CoV-2 genome sequencing turnaround times, which are measured in days between sample collection and genome submission to GISAID, varied greatly (Fig. 1). When grouped into 10-day intervals, only 43.5% of genomes were submitted within 90 days of collection. Delays between 150 and 200 days were less frequent (≈ 9.0% of genomes), whereas a substantial fraction of sequences (14.9%) showed very prolonged delays of more than 200 days.

Governorate-level sequencing coverage and representativeness

At the governorate level, sequencing coverage among RT-PCR–positive samples ranged from 0.0% in some interior governorates to 100.0% in one northern governorate, with a median of 60.0% (IQR 28.6–88.9; Fig. 2A, B). Coverage was generally higher in densely populated coastal regions (often ≥ 80.0%) and lower in several central and southern areas, where < 40.0% of positives were sequenced, and some governorates contributed no genomes. National representativeness remained high overall: the share of sequences closely tracked the share of positives (Pearson’s r = 0.992; n = 24 governorates; 95% CI : 0.981–0.997; p = 2.9 × 10⁻²¹; unweighted), indicating near-proportional sampling (Fig. 2C). However, an equity index revealed relative under-sampling in 12 of 24 governorates (index < 0.8) and over-sampling in one governorate (index > 1.2), mostly in settings with low case counts.

Demographic and clinical characteristics

Among the 669 sequenced SARS-CoV-2–positive patients, females were slightly more represented than males (53.4% vs. 45.7%), with sex unknown for < 1.0% of cases; the sex ratio (M/F) was 0.86. The age distribution spanned all life stages, but adults and older adults predominated. Patients ≥ 70 years accounted for the largest proportion (15.2%). Age information was missing for 8.8% of patients (Table 2). We did not formally test the missingness mechanism; however, missing age was more frequent in some years and governorates than others. Clinical information was available for 311/669 (46.5%) sequenced samples. Accordingly, symptom and comorbidity frequencies are reported among cases with available clinical metadata only. Among cases with available clinical metadata, fever and cough were the most frequently reported symptoms, present in about one-third of cases (33.7% and 31.5%, respectively). Among cases with available clinical metadata, comorbidities were reported in a minority of patients: chronic lung disease (9.6%) and cardiovascular disease (7.7%) were the most common conditions, followed by diabetes (5.1%) and chronic renal disease (2.9%). Other comorbidities, including hematological, neurological, immune system, and hepatic diseases, were each reported in < 2.0% of patients (Table 2).

Seasonal patterns of testing activity and SARS-CoV-2 positivity

Over the study period, testing activity showed a recurrent winter increase. The total number of NPS processed each month was generally higher between November and March, with pronounced peaks in late 2021–early 2022 and a maximum in January 2023, and smaller rises in late 2023–early 2024 and late 2024–early 2025 (Fig. 3). SARS-CoV-2–positive NPS displayed a series of epidemic waves, with large peaks in mid-2022 and mid-2023 and additional peaks during winter months (including early 2022 and January 2024). These peaks in case detection coincided with the emergence or replacement of predominant Omicron lineages, including early BA.1.1/BA.2 waves, the BA.5.2 wave in mid-2022, subsequent BQ.1, EG.13, and XBB.2.3.11 waves, and later successive JN.1 sublineages (Fig. 3).

Among the 669 sequenced SARS-CoV-2-positive samples, 92 (13.8%) showed co-detection with at least one additional respiratory virus identified by multiplex respiratory testing. The most frequent co-detections involved rhinovirus (19/669, 2.8%), adenovirus (10/669, 1.5%), influenza A/H3N2 (9/669, 1.3%), and RSV (8/669, 1.2%). Overall, 16/669 samples (2.4%) showed co-detection involving influenza viruses, including influenza A/H3N2 and influenza A/H1N1pdm09, either alone or in combination with other respiratory viruses (Table 3). At the surveillance-network level, influenza detections resumed after broader multiplex respiratory virus testing was reintroduced from the 2021–2022 season onward (Supplementary Table S6).

Lineage diversity of SARS-CoV-2 during the omicron period

We classified different Pango lineages into seven major categories and showed the contribution of each constituent lineage within its group to give a simplified picture of the genomic landscape (Fig. 4).

Genomic characteristics and mutational profiles of circulating lineages

Supplementary Table S7 summarizes the genomic features of the major Pango lineages, including the number of amino acid changes and deletions and the median mapped read coverage.

Across all major lineages, mapped read proportions were consistently high, typically ≥ 0.9, indicating good sequencing quality.

The genomes of the early Delta and BA.1/BA.2 sublineages had about 30–50 amino acid alterations, but the later XBB* and JN.1* sublineages often had more than 70–80 substitutions, with some recombinants having up to ~ 100 substitutions. Consistent with the slow accumulation of mutations along the Omicron backbone, the number of amino acid deletions likewise gradually rose over time, reaching up to 18–19 deletions in some JN.1-related genomes (Supplementary Table S7).

Discussion

During the study period, a total of 669 SARS-CoV-2 genomes, representing 65.6% of all RT-PCR–confirmed infections diagnosed, were sequenced. This proportion is unusually high for a middle-income setting and approaches proposed benchmarks for adequate genomic surveillance [6, 11].

Despite this large sequencing volume, turnaround time for data sharing was heterogeneous. Fewer than half of genomes were submitted to GISAID within 90 days of collection, and a non-negligible fraction experienced delays of more than 150–200 days. Similar delays have been reported in other genomic surveillance systems as pandemic-era resources contract and laboratory and bioinformatics workflows become fragmented [7]. International guidance recommends that sequence data be shared as rapidly as possible, ideally within ~ 21 days of specimen collection. Meeting this timeline accelerates variant detection and risk assessment.

At the subnational level, sequencing coverage varied widely, with coastal governorates generally achieving higher coverage than several central and southern regions. The equity index highlighted relative under-sampling in about half of governorates and slight over-sampling in one; however, most of these imbalances occurred in governorates with very few confirmed cases, suggesting limited impact on the overall robustness of the national picture. Nevertheless, reinforcing access to sampling and specimen transport from underserved regions would further strengthen representativeness. Geographically representative sequencing is critical for the early detection of novel variants: when most genomes originate from a few urban centers, variants emerging in peripheral areas may be detected late or missed altogether [8].

We could not estimate the proportion of all nationally confirmed COVID-19 cases represented by this dataset because national confirmation denominators across all testing pathways were not available for the full study period. Therefore, these data reflect the ILI/SARI sentinel surveillance sampling frame processed at the National Reference Laboratory rather than the entirety of COVID-19 testing performed nationally.

Taken together, these findings highlight Tunisia-specific operational insights for integrated respiratory surveillance: strengths include high sequencing output within the sentinel sampling frame, broad geographic participation, and consistently high sequencing quality, while key weaknesses include heterogeneous turnaround times, residual geographic imbalances in coverage, and incomplete clinical metadata. Maintaining SARS-CoV-2 sequencing within the existing influenza ILI/SARI platform provides a pragmatic route to sustainability beyond the pandemic; further improvements could be achieved by streamlining specimen transport and bioinformatics workflows, and by strengthening routine metadata capture at site level.

At the surveillance-network level, influenza detections were absent during the 2020–2021 season and resumed after broader multiplex respiratory virus testing was reintroduced from the 2021–2022 season onward. This pattern indicates that influenza circulation was again documented within the sentinel network once testing broadened beyond SARS-CoV-2 during the post-acute pandemic phase. However, this comparison is descriptive and should not be interpreted as a formal causal assessment of the effect of SARS-CoV-2 PCR implementation on influenza surveillance performance.

In addition, non-SARS-CoV-2 respiratory viruses identified through multiplex testing were not subjected to systematic sequencing, as the sequencing approach used in this study was SARS-CoV-2-specific.

Overall, the co-detection pattern observed in this study is broadly in line with published reports indicating that respiratory viral co-infections in COVID-19 patients were heterogeneous across settings, with influenza viruses and rhinovirus/enterovirus among the commonly reported viral co-detections [12]. The predominance of rhinovirus in our dataset is also consistent with broader pandemic-era literature showing that rhinovirus/enterovirus often persisted or re-emerged earlier than other respiratory viruses, while enveloped viruses such as influenza, RSV, human metapneumovirus, parainfluenza viruses, and seasonal coronaviruses were markedly suppressed during the first phase of the COVID-19 pandemic [13]. In this context, the absence of other respiratory virus detections during the earliest pandemic phase in our setting is not unexpected and likely reflects both the global suppression of non-SARS-CoV-2 respiratory viruses under non-pharmaceutical interventions and the temporary prioritization of SARS-CoV-2 within respiratory surveillance systems [13, 14]. This interpretation is supported by global analyses showing a sharp worldwide decline in influenza circulation during the pandemic and by international surveys indicating that influenza surveillance activities were temporarily disrupted in many settings while priority was given to SARS-CoV-2 [14, 15]. Taken together, our findings fit the broader global pattern of early pandemic suppression of non-SARS-CoV-2 respiratory viruses, followed by their re-emergence and renewed co-circulation once broader multiplex respiratory testing and integrated surveillance were re-established [13, 15].

The sequenced SARS-CoV-2 cases showed a slight female predominance (53.4% female, M/F sex ratio ~ 0.86). This near-parity in sex distribution is consistent with many COVID-19 cohorts globally, where infection rates have been comparable between males and females despite higher severity in males [16]. All age groups were represented, but adults and older adults predominated, with 15.2% ≥70 years, in line with the greater testing and hospitalization of older individuals [17]. Clinical data were available for only ~ 47.0% of sequenced patients, underscoring metadata limitations.

Fever and cough were recorded in approximately 33.0% of cases among those with symptom data, which is consistent with typical COVID-19 presentations but also suggests mild, unusual symptoms or inadequate reporting, comparable with the common mild or asymptomatic Omicron infections reported previously [17].

Chronic comorbidities were documented in a minority of patients, suggesting that many sequenced cases were outpatients or mild infections rather than exclusively hospitalized patients. These proportions are broadly comparable to literature reports, although diabetes appears slightly less frequent than the 10–30% often observed in severe hospitalized cohorts, possibly reflecting under-reporting or a younger demographic [18, 19].

Overall, the clinical and demographic characteristics of Tunisian cases are in line with published COVID-19 series, suggesting that—within the sentinel surveillance sampling frame and among cases with available metadata—the sequenced infections encompassed a broad clinical spectrum.

Clinical metadata were incomplete, with symptoms and comorbidities available for only 311/669 (46.5%) sequenced cases, which may introduce reporting bias if documentation completeness differed by site, workload periods, or region. We did not formally test the missingness mechanism; however, missing age was more frequent in some years and governorates than others, indicating that metadata completeness was not uniform. Therefore, symptom and comorbidity frequencies should be interpreted descriptively among cases with available metadata and should not be extrapolated to the full sequenced cohort.

Nevertheless, our sequencing inclusion criterion may have introduced a degree of selection bias. Our sequencing strategy prioritized RT-PCR–positive samples with Ct < 30 to maximize genome recovery and reduce the likelihood of incomplete consensus sequences at low viral RNA input. However, excluding higher-Ct samples (Ct ≥ 30) may introduce selection bias toward infections with higher viral loads and/or earlier sampling timepoints, potentially underrepresenting late presentations or low-level positive cases. In addition, Ct values are influenced by sampling quality, time since symptom onset, and assay/platform differences, and may therefore vary across settings and over time. We therefore interpret lineage proportions and geographic comparisons in the context of this Ct-based inclusion criterion and report temporal and governorate-level patterns of Ct eligibility and QC success to improve transparency. Despite this limitation, the high QC pass rate among attempted samples and the broad geographic coverage support the robustness of the main surveillance trends described.

Tunisian SARS-CoV-2 surveillance showed a clear seasonal pattern in testing, with higher NPS volumes each winter, likely reflecting overlap with influenza season, increased care-seeking for respiratory symptoms, and greater indoor crowding in cooler months. In contrast, SARS-CoV-2 positivity followed a series of distinct epidemic waves, each temporally associated with the emergence of a new Omicron sublineage. These temporal associations are based on visual inspection of surveillance curves rather than formal time-series modeling. Omicron was introduced in late 2021, with an initial BA.1/BA.2 wave in early 2022, followed by a major BA.5.2 wave in May–July 2022. Recombinant XBB.2.3.11 was associated with another positive surge in July–August 2023. A following JN.1 wave in winter 2023–2024 was followed by extensive circulation of JN.1 offspring into 2024–2025. These waves occurring in both winter and summer underscore that, unlike influenza, SARS-CoV-2 has not yet become a strictly seasonal virus and can still generate off-season outbreaks whenever novel immune-evasive variants emerge.

These dynamics broadly mirror trends across North Africa, the Eastern Mediterranean, and Southern Europe. Countries with similar Mediterranean climates reported comparable Omicron waves, including BA.4/BA.5 surges around mid-2022 and later introductions of XBB and its descendants. By mid-2024, JN.1 descendants, notably KP.3.1.1, predominated in the WHO European Region and were also detected in multiple Mediterranean countries, indicating that Tunisia and its neighbors were confronting highly evolved Omicron lineages in parallel.

Across all analyzed SARS-CoV-2 lineages, sequencing quality was high, with about 90% of reads mapping to the reference genome, which supports confidence in the mutation counts. Early variants such as Delta, Alpha, and early Omicron (BA.1/BA.2) carried roughly 30–50 aminoacid substitutions and only a few deletions per genome.

Later Omicron sublineages and recombinants, on the other hand, displayed significantly higher mutational loads, frequently reaching 70–90 substitutions in addition to 12–19 deletions. Overall, these patterns show that Omicron-derived lineages are gradually accumulating substitutions and indels, especially in recombinant variants like XBB and JN.1, which suggest continuous diversification on the Omicron backbone.

At the clade level, our dataset followed the well-described global transition from Delta (clade GK/21J) to Omicron (clades GRA and related Nextstrain clades), with successive turnover among Omicron clades over 2021–2025.

Phylogenetic analysis showed that Tunisian genomes did not form country-specific outlier clusters but were fully embedded within the global SARS-CoV-2 tree, with early sequences falling in Delta clade 21 J and all subsequent genomes distributed across the major Omicron clades 21 K (BA.1), 21 L (BA.2), 22B/22E (BA.5 and related sublineages), and later 23D/23E and 24 A–24 H, corresponding to XBB* and JN.1* lineages.

A limitation of our phylogenetic component is that it relied on Nextclade reference-tree placement rather than de novo phylogenetic reconstruction. Consequently, the tree provides global context for lineage turnover and illustrates how Tunisian genomes relate to internationally circulating clades, but it does not support formal inference of transmission clusters, the number of introductions, or within-country spread patterns. In addition, branch support metrics were not assessed.

This pattern is in line with reports from other North African and Mediterranean settings, where national datasets similarly revealed Delta and subsequent Omicron genomes scattered among international sequences instead of forming long, geographically restricted clades. Together with the high lineage diversity documented in our dataset, these findings are consistent with repeated introductions and turnover of globally circulating variants, rather than the persistence of long, Tunisia-specific phylogenetic clusters; however, formal introduction/cluster inference was not performed.

Conclusion

This four-year analysis shows that an influenza-based sentinel network in a middle-income North African country can support sustained SARS-CoV-2 genomic surveillance within the sentinel sampling frame and track rapid Omicron lineage turnover. Tunisia’s surveillance captured successive Delta and Omicron waves, documented the progressive accumulation of amino acid substitutions in later XBB and JN.1-related sublineages, and showed that Tunisian genomes are embedded within globally circulating clades rather than forming isolated country-specific clusters. By combining near-proportional sampling across governorates with multiplex testing for other respiratory viruses, this platform provides a scalable model for integrated genomic surveillance that can be leveraged for future SARS-CoV-2 variants and other emerging respiratory pathogens.
