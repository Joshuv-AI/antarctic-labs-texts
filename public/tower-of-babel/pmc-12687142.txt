Introduction

Multiple-valvular heart disease poses significant diagnostic and therapeutic challenges. The presence of multiple valvular lesions exerts interdependent haemodynamic effects that may obscure the severity of individual valve dysfunction, complicating clinical assessment. While surgical intervention remains the definitive treatment for multivalvular disease, it is often contraindicated in elderly or high-risk patients.

Balloon valvuloplasty may serve as a rescue intervention for patients with symptomatic or haemodynamic deterioration. We present a rare case of a dialysis-dependent patient with pan-valvular disease who underwent palliative balloon valvuloplasty on three stenotic valves. This case underscores the role of individualized, multidisciplinary care and the potential utility of balloon valvuloplasty when definitive treatment is not feasible.

Case presentation

A 74-year-old woman with end-stage renal disease on maintenance haemodialysis and a history of surgical tricuspid (St. Jude Medicine Epic 29 mm porcine valve) and pulmonary bioprosthetic valve (St. Jude Medicine Epic 23 mm porcine valve) replacement for infective endocarditis 3 years prior was admitted to the hospital with complaints of diffuse abdominal pain, watery diarrhoea occurring four to five times per day, and progressive dyspnoea on exertion. On the initial evaluation, her vital signs were stable. Physical examination revealed diffuse abdominal tenderness. Laboratory testing showed a rising total bilirubin of 2.14 mg/dL. Abdominal computed tomography revealed acute cholecystitis and cholangitis with common bile duct dilatation, and she underwent endoscopic retrograde cholangiopancreatography (ERCP) with successful stone extraction. Although she initially stabilized following the ERCP, her total bilirubin began to rise steadily, reaching 25 mg/dL. A repeat abdominal computed tomography (CT) showed heterogenous enhancement of the liver without biliary obstruction, which was compatible with congestive liver (Figure 1). Given her history of valvular disease, an echocardiogram was performed to evaluate cardiogenic hepatic congestion. Echocardiography after valve replacement 3 years ago showed a tricuspid prosthesis mean gradient of 2.42 mmHg and a pulmonary prosthesis mean gradient of 4.36 mmHg. However, the prosthetic tricuspid valve exhibited significant stenosis, with a mean pressure gradient of 9 mmHg this time (Figure 2A–C) The pulmonary valve prosthesis also showed stenosis, with a mean gradient of 36 mmHg (Figure 2D and E). Additionally, there was severe native aortic stenosis (AS) with a mean pressure gradient of 55 mmHg (Figure 2F and G) and moderate mitral regurgitation (MR) (Figure 3). The left ventricular systolic function was normal with a left ventricular end-diastolic volume (LVEDV) of 48.4 mL, end-systolic volume (LVESV) of 15.8 mL, and ejection fraction (LVEF) of 67.4%, while the right ventricular systolic function was mildly impaired with an end-diastolic area (RVEDA) of 8.8 cm², an end-systolic area (RVESA) of 5.5 cm², and a fractional area change (FAC) of 37%. The right heart catheterization revealed a trans-pulmonary valve pressure gradient of 43 mmHg and a trans-tricuspid valve pressure gradient of 46 mmHg. The cardiac index was markedly reduced at 1.54 L/min/m², consistent with low-output heart failure and pan-valvular heart disease. In view of the patient’s critical condition and multivalvular stenosis, the heart team elected to discuss the further management. Due to financial constraints and the high surgical risk, transcatheter valve-in-valve (VIV) procedures and surgical treatment were not favoured. We proceed with palliative balloon valvuloplasty for symptomatic relief. Using 18 × 40 mm Tyshak II and Nucleus balloons, percutaneous balloon valvuloplasty was performed sequentially on the tricuspid, pulmonary, and aortic valves (Figure 4). Post-procedural echocardiography showed significant improvement: the mean gradient across the tricuspid valve increased slightly to 14 mmHg (Figure 5A and B), which was considered related to the increase of blood flow. The pulmonary valve gradient decreased to 25 mmHg (Figure 5C and D), and the aortic valve gradient improved to 31 mmHg (Figure 5E and F).

Following the intervention, the patient’s dyspnoea improved, and her total bilirubin dropped from 25 to 5 mg/dL over several days. Given the residual prosthetic valve dysfunction, a transcatheter VIV procedure was considered for both the tricuspid and pulmonary positions, along with transcatheter aortic valve implantation (TAVI) for the native aortic valve. However, the family declined the dual VIV procedure due to financial issue. The patient was managed with medical therapy. Despite initial improvement, her overall condition remained tenuous due to underlying cardiac dysfunction and end-stage renal disease (ESRD). She succumbed to recurrent infection one month later (Supplementary material).

Discussion

This case illustrates an uncommon and complex presentation of severe pan-valvular dysfunction, including prosthetic tricuspid and pulmonary stenosis, native severe AS, and moderate MR. The patient underwent palliative balloon valvuloplasty of the tricuspid, pulmonary, and aortic valves, leading to short-term clinical and haemodynamic improvement. This case highlights the challenges of managing advanced valvular disease in frail, high-risk patients.

Multiple valvular heart disease (MVD) imposes a complex haemodynamic burden, as lesions in different valves interact and complicate assessment. Tricuspid stenosis can contribute to a low-flow state, leading to underestimation of the pressure gradient across the pulmonary valve. Similarly, pulmonary stenosis may reduce left ventricular preload, resulting in underestimation of the severity of aortic stenosis. Despite these flow limitations, the measured pressure gradients of our case remained markedly elevated, consistent with severe dysfunction of all affected valves. In addition, MVD has been associated with worse clinical outcomes. In TAVI subjects, the presence of mitral or tricuspid regurgitation was identified as an independent predictor of mortality.1,2

The treatment of MVD is complex and requires thorough discussion by a multidisciplinary heart team, along with shared decision-making with the patient. While surgical intervention can address all affected valves simultaneously, it was deemed unsuitable in this case due to the patient’s advanced age, redo surgery, and dialysis-dependent status, all of which significantly increased surgical risk. Transcatheter valve replacement, combined with balloon valvuloplasty, may also be a treatment option. Zhang et al. reported a case of severe aortic stenosis and native pulmonary stenosis, in which simultaneous TAVI and balloon pulmonary valvuloplasty were performed. The intervention resulted in significant haemodynamic improvement without complications.3 Durability for the valve should also be considered. Transcatheter heart valves (THVs) used in TAVI procedures have demonstrated acceptable durability, with a THV failure rate of 9.7% at 10 years reported in the NOTION trial.4 In the Valve-in-Valve International Database (VIVID) Registry, tricuspid VIV procedures showed a THV reintervention rate of up to 10% at 3 years.5 Shorter durability of right-sided bioprosthetic valves has also been observed in previous surgical studies, highlighting the challenges associated with long-term outcomes in right-sided valve replacements.6 In our case, the patient’s family declined tricuspid and pulmonary VIV procedures due to concerns about early bioprosthetic valve failure and financial limitations. As a result, we proceeded with palliative balloon valvuloplasty of the aortic valve and both bioprosthetic tricuspid and pulmonary valves.

To prevent suicide left ventricle, the valvuloplasty was performed sequentially starting with the tricuspid valve, followed by the pulmonary valve, and finally the aortic valve. Waldman et al. and Rana et al. showed in the case series that balloon sizes approximating but not exceeding the internal diameter of the prosthesis are generally effective and safe, especially in bioprosthetic valves.7,8 We choose 18 × 40 mm balloon for the valvuloplasty. This strategy prioritized safety to avoid inducing significant regurgitation, with plans to escalate balloon size only if pressure gradient reduction proved insufficient. The procedure was successful and resulted in immediate haemodynamic improvement and significant relief of hepatic congestion. This highlights the safety and feasibility of balloon valvuloplasty as a palliative or bridging option for MVD when surgical or transcatheter valve replacement is not feasible or available.

Conclusion

This case highlights the diagnostic and therapeutic challenges of managing pan-valvular heart disease involving both native and prosthetic valves in a dialysis patient. When surgical or transcatheter valve replacement is not feasible, balloon valvuloplasty can serve as a bridge or palliative option. Careful clinical judgement and multidisciplinary coordination are essential in tailoring treatment to patient-specific circumstances and limitations.
