When the TEE Probe Will Not Move
Authors: Jo-Nan Liao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Corresponding AuthorKey words: complication, echocardiography, imaging

Transesophageal echocardiography (TEE) is among the most widely performed cardiac imaging procedures. As interventional cardiology has expanded, particularly in transcatheter valvular intervention and atrial fibrillation ablation, the role of TEE has moved beyond diagnostic imaging into procedural practice. Although TEE is generally safe,1,2 catastrophic complications such as esophageal perforation can occur. Because the probe is advanced without direct visualization of the esophageal lumen, buckling may develop unrecognized. In this situation, the distal probe doubles back on itself, becomes fixed in flexion, and forms a U-shaped configuration within the esophagus, with the potential to injure the esophageal wall.

Only scattered case reports of buckled TEE probes have been published, and the true incidence is probably underestimated. The case series by Ho et al3 is therefore valuable because it systematically describes this rare but potentially serious complication and summarizes a stepwise approach to its diagnosis and management. In the 3 cases reported by Ho et al3 in this issue of JACC: Case Reports, the first clues to TEE buckling were inability to advance or withdraw the probe without resistance and failure to obtain the expected TEE images. This is the moment when operators should raise the suspicion of probe buckling. The instinct is usually to withdraw the probe, but withdrawal may tighten the buckle and cause further damage. When a probe is fixed in a U-shaped configuration, traction can concentrate force at the apex of the loop and convert a positional problem into mucosal trauma. The key response is therefore not to use force or simply pull back, but to stop and use appropriate imaging to recognize buckling of the TEE probe, as well as its location and configuration. In this case series, fluoroscopy or chest radiography was used to confirm the diagnosis. When fluoroscopy is not available, such as in intubated patients in the intensive care unit, or when the precise anatomical location cannot be determined by fluoroscopy or chest radiography, as in the third case, fiberoptic endoscopy performed by the otolaryngology service can be considered.

Once TEE buckling is confirmed, instead of withdrawing it forcefully, advancing the probe into the gastric fundus under fluoroscopic visualization may straighten the loop and allow safe retrieval. In the gastric fundus, the wider and more distensible space permits anteflexion and retroflexion maneuvers to unfold the loop without placing additional stress on the esophagus. This maneuver is counterintuitive but has been shown to be useful in several case reports.4 Continuous visualization during probe manipulation and use of the capacious gastric fundus as a space for unbuckling are key components to safely and successfully execute this maneuver (Figure 1).

Fluoroscopy-Guided Rescue of a Buckled Transesophageal Echocardiography (TEE) Probe

Buckling occurs when the distal TEE probe doubles back on itself into a fixed U-shaped configuration, risking esophageal injury. (Top) Recognizing the anatomical, procedural, and device-related factors that predispose to buckling is key to prevention. Suspicion should arise with resistance to probe movement, failure to obtain the expected images, and a probe fixed in maximum anteflexion. The diagnosis can be confirmed with bedside chest radiography (CXR), fluoroscopy, or fiberoptic endoscopy. (Bottom) A step-by-step, fluoroscopy-guided maneuver allows safe resolution: the buckled probe is (A) confirmed in the mid-esophagus, (B) advanced distally into the stomach rather than withdrawn against the buckle, and (C) straightened within the capacious gastric fundus before removal.

The feasibility of this rescue strategy also depends on where the complication is recognized. In the electrophysiology or structural-heart laboratory, fluoroscopy and operators familiar with catheter manipulation are often immediately available, allowing prompt confirmation and unbuckling. Outside this setting, particularly in the operating room or intensive care unit, where immediate fluoroscopy may not be available, endoscopic or other imaging confirmation of probe location should be secured before any further manipulation is attempted.

Factors Related to TEE Buckling

Based on this case series and the prior literature, potential contributors to TEE probe buckling can be broadly considered as device-related, anatomical, or procedure-related factors. The 3 cases reported by Ho et al3 involved pediatric or mini-multiplane probes used in adult patients, whereas standard adult TEE probes have been described in other reports. A probe that becomes rigidly fixed in maximum flexion should also raise concern for buckling once resistance is encountered.

Among anatomy-related factors, structural esophageal abnormalities are particularly relevant. In the second case reported by Ho et al,3 a corkscrew esophagus was identified after probe retrieval. Other potential anatomical barriers include esophageal stricture and diverticula. In addition, the probe may be diverted away from the esophageal lumen if it is not centered along the midline during intubation, particularly in patients with an endotracheal tube or suboptimal positioning during the TEE examination.

Sedation is another important consideration. At least 2 of the 3 cases in this series occurred under conscious sedation, and most previously reported cases occurred under either conscious sedation or general anesthesia.5,6 Greater depth of sedation may mask patient responses to difficult TEE intubation that would otherwise alert the operator to a potential problem. These factors deserve special attention during TEE intubation and may also provide important clues when resistance occurs during probe advancement or withdrawal.

In summary, this is a small case series and cannot determine the true incidence of TEE buckling, but it highlights an underrecognized and clinically consequential problem. TEE buckling can occur under specific circumstances, and prompt recognition, accurate diagnosis with appropriate imaging, and stepwise management may help prevent catastrophic complications.
