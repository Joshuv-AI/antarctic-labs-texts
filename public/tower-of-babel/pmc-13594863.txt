Multi-omics and experimental validation identify RAPGEF2 as a protective prognostic biomarker in clear cell renal cell carcinoma
Authors: Yanfei Chen, Xiaoxue Yu, Chao Cai, Jianming Lu, Xiaowen Lin, Zhimei Wen, Shu Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Highlights•Single-cell, spatial transcriptomic and multi-omics analyses reveal endothelial-enriched RAPGEF2 in KIRC.•RAPGEF2 independently predicts favorable survival across multicenter KIRC cohorts.•Experimental validation links RAPGEF2 to tumor suppression and immunotherapy benefit.

Introduction

Kidney Renal Clear Cell Carcinoma (KIRC) is the most common histological subtype of renal cell carcinoma (RCC), accounting for approximately 70%–80% of all RCC cases, and represents the major contributor to RCC-related mortality [1]. In recent years, the incidence of kidney cancer has continued to rise [2]. Owing to the insidious nature of early clinical symptoms, a subset of patients is already diagnosed at the stage of locally advanced disease or distant metastasis [3]. Moreover, even after radical surgical resection, a considerable proportion of patients subsequently develop recurrence or metastasis, thereby limiting long-term survival benefits [4]. Although therapeutic strategies such as VEGF-targeted therapy, tyrosine kinase inhibitors, and immune checkpoint inhibitors have substantially improved the clinical outcomes of patients with advanced KIRC, primary or acquired resistance, heterogeneous therapeutic responses, and insufficient rates of durable response remain central challenges in current clinical management [[5], [6], [7]].

Previous studies have demonstrated that KIRC is characterized by pronounced molecular heterogeneity [8] and metabolic reprogramming [9], both of which collectively influence tumor evolution, metastatic potential, the status of the immune microenvironment, and patient prognosis. Although traditional TNM staging and pathological grading are widely used for risk assessment, they fail to fully capture the complex molecular biological differences underlying KIRC [10], thereby limiting their utility in precise prognostic stratification and individualized therapeutic decision-making. Therefore, the identification and validation of novel biomarkers that reflect KIRC initiation and progression, metastatic risk, therapeutic response, and survival outcomes are of great significance for refining existing prognostic evaluation systems, identifying high-risk patients, uncovering potential therapeutic targets, and advancing precision diagnosis and treatment strategies [11].

RAPGEF2, also known as Rap guanine nucleotide exchange factor 2 or PDZ-GEF1, is a member of the RAPGEF family. It primarily functions as a guanine nucleotide exchange factor (GEF) by promoting the conversion of Rap small GTPases from the GDP-bound inactive state to the GTP-bound active state. Structurally, RAPGEF2 contains multiple conserved functional domains, including a CDC25 homology domain, a Ras-associating domain, a Ras exchange motif, a PDZ domain, and a cyclic nucleotide-binding domain, which collectively support its regulatory effects on Rap1/Rap2 [12]. RAPGEF2 is broadly expressed across multiple tissues and has particularly important biological roles in the nervous system. RAPGEF2-mediated Rap signaling participates in key cellular processes, including cell adhesion, migration, proliferation, and differentiation [13]. However, its role in renal cancer remains unexplored.

With the development of high-throughput sequencing technologies and the reduction in sequencing costs, multi-omics data mining has become an important approach for identifying novel tumor biomarkers [14,15]. As shown in Fig. 1, this study aims to comprehensively elucidate the value of RAPGEF2 as a novel biomarker in KIRC by integrating single-cell omics, the TCGA pan-cancer cohort, multicenter renal cancer cohorts, mutation analysis, local clinical cohorts, and in vitro experimental validation.Fig. 1Graphical overview of the study design and major findings.

Materials and methods

Data collection and processing

The datasets and corresponding clinical information used in this study were obtained from publicly available resources. The TCGA cohort was downloaded from the UCSC Xena platform (https://xenabrowser.net/datapages/). Data for the ICGC-RECA-EU cohort were retrieved from the International Cancer Genome Consortium database (https://docs.icgc-argo.org/), whereas the E-MTAB-1980 cohort was obtained from the ArrayExpress database (https://www.ebi.ac.uk/biostudies/ArrayExpress/). For survival analyses, only tumor samples with available RNA-seq profiles and complete survival information were retained. Samples with a follow-up duration of <30 days were excluded. The baseline characteristics of the datasets analyzed in this study are summarized in Supplementary Table S1.

Single-cell pan-cancer analysis

Pan-cancer single-cell transcriptomic profiles were integrated from the TISCH database to characterize the expression pattern of RAPGEF2 across different tumor types [16]. A pan-cancer single-cell expression heatmap of RAPGEF2 was generated using the R package pheatmap (version 1.0.12) (https://cran.r-project.org/package=pheatmap). After Z-score normalization, sample similarity was estimated based on Euclidean distance, followed by hierarchical clustering using Ward’s linkage method. Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP) was applied to visualize the cellular distribution of RAPGEF2 expression in the KIRC_GSE159115 and KIRC_GSE171306 datasets [17]. In addition, the Nebulosa package was used to perform weighted kernel density estimation, thereby improving the visualization of gene expression patterns in single-cell transcriptomic data.

Spatial transcriptomic analysis

Processed spatial transcriptomic data were obtained from the Sparkle database (https://www.grswsci.top) for spatial transcriptomic analysis of KIRC tissue sections. Following previous studies [18,19], the integrated 10x Visium sequencing data were visualized for KIRC1 and KIRC2(GSE175540) using the SpatialFeaturePlot and SpatialPlot functions in the Seurat package [20].

Prognostic analysis

The optimal cutoff value for RAPGEF2 expression was determined using the R package survminer (version 0.5.0). Kaplan–Meier(KM) survival analysis and Cox proportional hazards regression were performed with the survival package in R (version 3.5–5) [21] to evaluate the prognostic relevance of RAPGEF2. Variables with *P* ≤ 0.05 in univariate Cox regression analysis were subsequently incorporated into the multivariate Cox regression model to identify independent prognostic factors.

Immunohistochemistry (IHC)

The KIRC tissue microarray used in this study, comprising 43 KIRC cases with available follow-up information, was purchased from Shanghai Zhuoli Biotech Co., Ltd. Before paraffin embedding, tissue specimens were fixed in 4% formaldehyde. Paraffin-embedded tissue blocks were cut into 4-μm-thick sections. The sections were treated with 1% H₂O₂ to block endogenous peroxidase activity and subsequently incubated with non-immune goat serum to reduce nonspecific binding. Thereafter, the slides were incubated overnight at 4 °C with a primary antibody against RAPGEF2 (Bioss, bs-11581R), followed by incubation with a biotinylated secondary antibody for 30 min at room temperature. Immunohistochemical staining was evaluated based on both the proportion of positively stained cells and staining intensity. The percentage of positive cells was scored as follows: 0, <10%; 1, 11%–50%; 2, 51%–80%; and 3, >80%. Staining intensity was graded as 0 for negative staining, 1 for weak staining, 2 for moderate staining, and 3 for strong staining. The final IHC score for each sample was calculated by multiplying the percentage score by the intensity score.

Gene mutations and CNV alteration

To characterize the mutational landscape of the TCGA cohort, somatic mutation data and copy number alterations (CNAs) were analyzed using the R package maftools (version 2.22.10) [22]. Differences in genomic mutations and CNAs between RAPGEF2 expression subgroups were further visualized with the ComplexHeatmap package in R (version 2.18.0) [23]. The chi-square test was applied to compare the frequencies of somatic mutations and CNAs across RAPGEF2 expression groups. In addition, the Wilcoxon rank-sum test was used to assess the association between RAPGEF2 expression levels and specific genetic alterations.

Cell culture and transient transfection

KIRC cell lines Caki-1 and 786-O were obtained from Beina Chuanglian Biotechnology Institute (Beijing, China). Caki-1 cells were maintained in McCoy’s 5A medium (16,600,082, Gibco), whereas 786-O cells were cultured in RPMI-1640 medium (11,875,093, Gibco). Both media were supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBSST-01,033-500, OriCell) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin (15,140,122, Gibco). Cells were incubated at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO2. Cells were transiently transfected with negative control or RAPGEF2-targeting shRNAs (Tsingke) using shRNA-mate reagent (G04003– 1, GenePharm) following the manufacturer’s protocol. Knockdown efficiency was verified by qPCR, and the shRNA and primer sequences are provided in Supplementary Table S2.

Cell proliferation assays

For the CCK-8 assay, cells were seeded into 96-well plates at a density of 2000 cells per well and allowed to adhere overnight, which was defined as day 1. At each indicated time point, the culture medium was replaced with 100 μL of fresh complete medium containing 10 μL of CCK-8 reagent (MA0218, Meilunbio), followed by incubation at 37 °C for 2 h. Cell viability was assessed by measuring the absorbance at 450 nm using a microplate reader. Measurements were repeated every 24 h until day 5, and proliferation curves were generated accordingly.

EdU assay was performed using the BeyoClick™ EdU-594 Cell Proliferation Assay Kit (C0078S, Beyotime). Briefly, cells were seeded into 6-well plates and incubated with 10 μM EdU for 2 h. After fixation with 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min, cells were washed, permeabilized with 0.3% Triton X-100 for 10 min, and incubated with the Click reaction solution for 30 min at room temperature in the dark. Nuclei were counterstained with Hoechst 33,342 (P0133, Beyotime) for 10 min. After washing, EdU-positive cells were visualized and quantified.

Transwell migration assay

Cell migration after RAPGEF2 knockdown was assessed using Transwell chambers. Briefly, 4 × 10⁴ transfected cells suspended in serum-free medium were seeded into the upper chamber, while complete medium was added to the lower chamber as a chemoattractant. After incubation for 48 h at 37 °C with 5% CO₂, migrated cells were washed with PBS, fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 30 min, stained with 0.1% crystal violet (C0121, Beyotime), and quantified under a microscope.

Functional enrichment analysis

Using the TCGA dataset, Spearman correlation analysis was performed to assess the association between RAPGEF2 expression and the expression levels of all other mRNAs. Genes ranked by their correlation coefficients were subjected to Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) for KEGG pathway enrichment using the clusterProfiler R package (version 4.10.1) [24], and enriched pathways were ordered according to the normalized enrichment score (NES). Visualization of GSEA results was generated via the R package GseaVis [25].

To further characterize metabolic pathway activity, Gene Set Variation Analysis (GSVA; version 1.50.5) [26] was conducted based on metabolic gene sets obtained from the KEGG database. Pathways significantly associated with RAPGEF2 expression were identified using the limma R package (version 3.58.1) [27].

Assessment of immunotherapy response

Immunotherapy cohort data involving anti-PD-L1 therapy, CAR-T therapy, and anti-CTLA-4 therapy were obtained from the BEST database [28]. KM survival analysis was performed to evaluate the prognostic relevance of RAPGEF2 expression in patients receiving these immunotherapeutic strategies.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses, data processing, and visualization were performed in R software (version 4.3.0). Differences between two groups were assessed using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test. All P values were two-sided, and P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Result

Pan-cancer exploration of the expression significance of RAPGEF2

We first analyzed the expression pattern of RAPGEF2 across different cancer types, with a particular focus on KIRC, using pan-cancer single-cell transcriptomic data. The results showed that RAPGEF2 exhibited heterogeneous expression patterns across multiple tumor types and displayed a relatively prominent expression signal in KIRC samples (Fig. 2A). Two KIRC single-cell datasets, GSE159115 and GSE171306, were further selected for cell annotation and expression localization analyses. UMAP dimensionality reduction revealed that major cell populations within tumor tissues could be identified, including malignant cells, monocytes/macrophages, endothelial cells, fibroblasts, T cells, NK cells, neutrophils, and mast cells (Fig. 2B). The expression distribution map of RAPGEF2 indicated that its expression signal was predominantly enriched in endothelial cells, while relatively lower expression was observed in malignant cells. This study further performed spatial transcriptomic analysis on two independent KIRC sections to resolve the spatial cellular distribution of the RAPGEF2 within KIRC tissues, and likewise found that RAPGEF2 was predominantly enriched in endothelial cells (Fig. 2C–D).Fig. 2Single-cell and Spatial transcriptomic expression landscape of RAPGEF2. Subsequently, we evaluated the association between RAPGEF2 expression and patient prognosis in the TCGA pan-cancer cohort. Univariate Cox regression analysis demonstrated that RAPGEF2 expression was significantly associated with OS, DSS and PFI in patients with multiple tumor types (Fig. S1A-C), and acted as a prognostic protective factor in most cancers. In KIRC, high RAPGEF2 expression was significantly associated with a reduced risk of mortality. Specifically, the OS analysis showed an HR of 0.627 (p < 0.001), the DSS analysis showed an HR of 0.531 (p < 0.001), and the PFI analysis showed an HR of 0.589 (p < 0.001). Collectively, these findings suggest that RAPGEF2 not only exhibits a distinct expression profile in KIRC but is also closely associated with patient survival outcomes, indicating its potential value as a prognostic biomarker in KIRC.

Multicenter cohorts identify RAPGEF2 as an independent protective prognostic factor in KIRC patients

To further clarify the prognostic significance of RAPGEF2 in KIRC, we analyzed the relationship between RAPGEF2 expression levels and patient survival outcomes using multicenter cohorts. In the TCGA-KIRC cohort, Kaplan–Meier survival analysis showed that patients in the high-RAPGEF2 expression group had significantly better OS, PFI, and DSS than those in the low-expression group, with statistically significant differences observed for all endpoints (all log-rank p < 0.001; Fig. 3A). To validate the robustness of these findings, we further performed survival analyses in two independent external cohorts, ICGC_RECA-EU and E-MTAB-1980. Consistently, high RAPGEF2 expression was significantly associated with prolonged OS in both validation cohorts, with a log-rank p value of 0.004 in the ICGC_RECA-EU cohort and < 0.001 in the E-MTAB-1980 cohort (Fig. 3B, C).Fig. 3Multicenter validation of RAPGEF2 as an independent protective prognostic factor in KIRC. We next used univariate Cox regression models to evaluate the associations of RAPGEF2 and conventional clinicopathological factors with patient prognosis. In the TCGA cohort, RAPGEF2 expression was significantly associated with OS. In addition, RAPGEF2 expression was also significantly associated with PFI and DSS (Fig. 3D). Specifically, higher RAPGEF2 expression was associated with a lower risk of adverse outcomes, with hazard ratios of 0.540, 0.480, and 0.400 for OS, PFI, and DSS, respectively, all reaching statistical significance. Similarly, in the ICGC_RECA-EU and E-MTAB-1980 external validation cohorts, high RAPGEF2 expression was significantly associated with a reduced risk of death, further supporting its prognostic relevance (Fig. 3D). To exclude the potential confounding effects of clinicopathological variables, we further constructed multivariate Cox regression models. After adjustment for variables including age, sex, TNM stage, clinical stage, and pathological grade, RAPGEF2 remained an independent prognostic factor for patients with KIRC. In the TCGA cohort, high RAPGEF2 expression was associated with lower risks of OS events (HR = 0.710, p = 0.010), PFI events (HR = 0.740, p = 0.033), and DSS events (HR = 0.530, p < 0.001) (Fig. 3E). In the ICGC_RECA-EU and E-MTAB-1980 validation cohorts, RAPGEF2 likewise retained its independent prognostic value and was associated with reduced OS risk (ICGC_RECA-EU: HR = 0.620, p = 0.042; E-MTAB-1980: HR = 0.420, p = 0.024; Fig. 3E). Taken together, these findings indicate that RAPGEF2 serves as an independent protective prognostic factor across multicenter KIRC cohorts.

In addition, we explored the relationship between RAPGEF2 expression and clinical parameters. In the TCGA-KIRC, patients with high T stage (T3+T4) showed significantly lower RAPGEF2 expression than those with low T stage (T1+T2) (P < 0.001); patients with lymph node metastasis (N1+N2) showed lower expression than those without lymph node metastasis (N0+NX) (P = 0.004); patients with distant metastasis (M1) showed lower expression than those without distant metastasis (P < 0.001); patients with high pathological stage (stage III+IV) showed lower expression than those with low stage (stage I+II) (P < 0.001); and patients with high grade (G3+G4) showed lower expression than those with low grade (G1+G2+GX) (P < 0.001). These trends were validated in the E-MTAB-1980 and ICGC_RECA-EU, although some parameters did not reach statistical significance, this may be attributable to differences in sample collection methods and detection techniques across centers (Table S1). Taken together, the low-RAPGEF2 subgroup generally exhibited a higher clinical grade.

TMA cohort IHC validation shows the prognostic value of RAPGEF2

Validation based on public cohorts was mainly conducted at the RNA level. To determine whether RAPGEF2 exhibits similar prognostic value at the protein level, we further evaluated RAPGEF2 expression using IHC staining in a TMA cohort. Kaplan–Meier survival analysis showed that patients with higher RAPGEF2 IHC scores had significantly better overall survival than those with lower scores, with a statistically significant difference between the two groups (log-rank p = 0.03; Fig. 4A). Representative IHC images further revealed marked differences in RAPGEF2 protein expression in tumor tissues from patients with different prognostic outcomes. In cases with poor prognosis, RAPGEF2 staining signals were relatively weak, with only sparse light brown-yellow positive staining observed in the cytoplasmic and membranous regions of tumor cells. In contrast, cases with favorable prognosis exhibited stronger brown-yellow positive RAPGEF2 staining, indicating relatively elevated protein expression levels (Fig. 4B). Low RAPGEF2 expression is associated with a higher mutational burden in KIRC

To explore the genomic features underlying differential RAPGEF2 expression, we evaluated somatic mutation profiles and copy number alteration patterns between the high- and low-RAPGEF2 expression groups in the KIRC cohort. First, the mutation landscape revealed that the top five frequently mutated genes in KIRC were VHL, PBRM1, TTN, SETD2, and BAP1, with VHL and PBRM1 showing the highest mutation frequencies, consistent with the typical molecular genetic characteristics of KIRC [29] (Fig. 5A). Further comparison of mutation frequencies between the high- and low-RAPGEF2 expression groups showed that several gene mutations differed notably between the two groups. Compared with the low-RAPGEF2 expression group, the high-RAPGEF2 expression group exhibited a lower mutation frequency of BAP1, while the mutation frequency of SYNE1 also showed a decreasing trend (Fig. 5B). At the copy number alteration level, distinct alteration patterns were also observed between the high- and low-RAPGEF2 expression groups. Specifically, the frequencies of 7q21.12 amplification and 14q31.1 deletion were markedly lower in the high-RAPGEF2 expression group than in the low-expression group (Fig. 5B).

We next analyzed the relationship between the mutation status of key genes and RAPGEF2 expression levels. The results showed that RAPGEF2 expression differed significantly between mutant and wild-type samples for SETD2, BAP1, MTOR, ATM, and SYNE1 (Fig. 5C). Among these, samples harboring BAP1, MTOR, or SYNE1 mutations generally exhibited lower RAPGEF2 expression levels, whereas RAPGEF2 expression was relatively higher in ATM-mutant samples. Finally, to assess the association between RAPGEF2 expression and chromosomal instability, we compared the fraction of genome altered and copy number alteration burden between the high- and low-RAPGEF2 expression groups. The results showed that the high-RAPGEF2 expression group had significantly lower levels of fraction genome altered (FGA), fraction genome gained (FGG), and fraction genome lost (FGL) than the low-expression group (Fig. 5D). Moreover, the burdens of arm-level gains, arm-level losses, focal gains, and focal losses were also significantly reduced in the high-RAPGEF2 expression group.

Taken together, RAPGEF2 expression is closely associated with the somatic mutation landscape, specific copy number alteration events, and overall genomic instability in KIRC. Tumors with low RAPGEF2 expression exhibit a higher genomic alteration burden and more frequent chromosomal abnormalities, suggesting that they may represent a KIRC subtype characterized by greater molecular heterogeneity.

RAPGEF2 knockdown promotes the progression of KIRC cells

We further validated the effect of RAPGEF2 on the biological behavior of KIRC cells through in vitro experiments. RAPGEF2 knockdown models were established in the Caki-1 and 786-O cell lines, and knockdown efficiency was assessed by qRT-PCR. Compared with the negative control (NC) group, two independent shRNAs significantly reduced the relative expression level of RAPGEF2 in both Caki-1 and 786-O cells, indicating the successful establishment of RAPGEF2-knockdown cell models (Fig. 6A). Next, CCK-8 assays showed that, in both Caki-1 and 786-O cells, the OD values of the RAPGEF2-knockdown groups increased more markedly over time and were significantly higher than those of the control group at later time points (Fig. 6B). EdU incorporation assays showed a clear increase in the number of EdU-positive cells after RAPGEF2 knockdown in both Caki-1 and 786-O cells. Quantitative analysis further demonstrated that the EdU/DAPI-positive ratios in the sh-1 and sh-2 groups were significantly higher than those in the NC group (Fig. 6C). In transwell assays, compared with the NC group, the number of cells migrating through the membrane was markedly increased in the RAPGEF2-knockdown groups (Fig. 6D). RAPGEF2 may be involved in metabolic reprogramming in KIRC

To investigate the potential molecular mechanisms of RAPGEF2 in KIRC, we first performed GSEA based on RAPGEF2 expression stratification. As shown in Fig. S2, RAPGEF2 was significantly associated with the enrichment of multiple metabolic pathways, including glycerolipid metabolism, inositol phosphate metabolism, propanoate metabolism, oxidative phosphorylation, and pyrimidine metabolism. These findings suggest that differential RAPGEF2 expression may be accompanied by the remodeling of multiple metabolic and cellular biological pathways in KIRC.

To further validate the relationship between RAPGEF2 and metabolic pathways, we applied GSVA to evaluate differences in metabolic pathway activity between the high- and low-RAPGEF2 expression groups. The GSVA heatmap revealed broad differences in the activity of various metabolic pathways between the two groups, involving multiple metabolic categories, including amino acid metabolism, carbohydrate metabolism, energy metabolism, lipid metabolism, nucleotide metabolism, glycan biosynthesis and metabolism, and xenobiotic biodegradation and metabolism (Fig. 7A and Table S3). Among the significantly altered metabolic pathways, the high-RAPGEF2 expression group exhibited higher activities of inositol phosphate metabolism and lysine degradation, whereas the activities of valine, leucine, and isoleucine biosynthesis and riboflavin metabolism were significantly reduced in the high-RAPGEF2 expression group (Fig. 7B).Fig. 7Association between RAPGEF2 and metabolic pathway remodeling in KIRC.(A) GSVA heatmap showing differential metabolic pathway activities between high- and low-RAPGEF2 expression groups in the TCGA-KIRC. (B) Violin plots comparing representative metabolism-related pathways between RAPGEF2 expression groups. (C) Correlation analyses between RAPGEF2 expression and selected metabolic pathway scores.

Association between RAPGEF2 and metabolic pathway remodeling in KIRC.

Correlation analysis further confirmed that RAPGEF2 expression was positively correlated with the inositol phosphate metabolism score (r = 0.72, p < 1e-04) and lysine degradation score (r = 0.55, p < 1e-04). In contrast, RAPGEF2 expression was negatively correlated with the valine, leucine, and isoleucine biosynthesis score (r = −0.41, p < 1e-04) and the riboflavin metabolism score (r = −0.51, p < 1e-04) (Fig. 7C). Collectively, these results indicate that RAPGEF2 expression is closely associated with metabolic pathway remodeling in KIRC, suggesting that RAPGEF2 may participate in KIRC metabolic reprogramming.

RAPGEF2 expression is associated with clinical benefit in immunotherapy cohorts

In addition to surgical treatment, immunotherapy has become an important component of therapeutic strategies for KIRC [30]. Therefore, we further explored the association between RAPGEF2 expression and patient survival outcomes across multiple immunotherapy cohorts. First, in the Hugo cohort 2016 and IMvigor210 cohort 2018, which included patients receiving anti-PD-1/PD-L1 therapy, Kaplan–Meier survival analysis showed that patients with high RAPGEF2 expression had significantly better overall survival than those with low RAPGEF2 expression. In the Lauss cohort 2017, which involved CAR-T therapy, the high-RAPGEF2 expression group showed a trend toward a lower risk of disease progression. However, the difference did not reach statistical significance (log-rank p = 0.20; Fig. 8B). In addition, in the VanAllen cohort 2015, comprising patients treated with anti-CTLA-4 therapy, RAPGEF2 expression was significantly associated with progression-free survival (Fig. 8C). Discussion

RAPGEF2 is a guanine nucleotide exchange factor that activates Rap small GTPases by facilitating the transition from the GDP-bound inactive state to the GTP-bound active state [31]. RAPGEF2-mediated Rap signaling has been implicated in several fundamental cellular processes, including cell adhesion, migration, proliferation, and differentiation [32]; however, its role in KIRC has remained largely unexplored. Our pan-cancer single-cell analysis demonstrated that RAPGEF2 exhibited heterogeneous expression across tumor types and showed a relatively prominent expression signal in KIRC. Notably, in the single-cell dataset and spatial transcriptomic dataset of KIRC, RAPGEF2 expression was mainly enriched in endothelial cells, whereas relatively lower expression was observed in malignant cells. This cellular distribution suggests that RAPGEF2 may not only reflect tumor-intrinsic biological properties but may also be associated with the vascular or stromal components of the KIRC microenvironment[33,34]. Given the highly vascular nature of KIRC [35], the endothelial enrichment of RAPGEF2 may have particular relevance to tumor angiogenesis, vascular integrity, and microenvironmental organization, although this hypothesis requires further experimental validation.

A major finding of this study is the robust prognostic value of RAPGEF2 in KIRC. In KIRC, high RAPGEF2 expression was associated with reduced mortality risk for OS, DSS, and PFI. These findings were further confirmed in multicenter KIRC cohorts. KM analysis showed that patients with high RAPGEF2 expression had significantly better survival outcomes in TCGA-KIRC, ICGC_RECA-EU, and E-MTAB-1980 cohorts. Importantly, multivariate Cox regression analysis demonstrated that RAPGEF2 remained an independent prognostic factor after adjustment for conventional clinicopathological variables. These observations indicate that RAPGEF2 may help improve risk stratification in KIRC.

The prognostic value of RAPGEF2 was further supported at the protein level using IHC in a tissue microarray cohort. Patients with higher RAPGEF2 IHC scores exhibited significantly better overall survival than those with lower scores, and representative IHC images showed stronger RAPGEF2 staining in tumors from patients with favorable outcomes. Consistent with previous studies indicating that RAPGEF2, as a cytoplasmic guanine nucleotide exchange factor, is predominantly localized to the cytoplasm [36].This protein-level validation is particularly important because transcriptomic biomarkers may not always translate into clinically applicable protein markers [37]. The consistency between mRNA-based survival analysis and IHC-based validation strengthens the potential of RAPGEF2 as a clinically relevant prognostic biomarker for KIRC.

Genomic analyses further revealed that RAPGEF2 expression was closely associated with the molecular heterogeneity of KIRC. Low-RAPGEF2 tumors exhibited higher genomic alteration burden. In particular, the co-occurrence of BAP1 mutations with low RAPGEF2 expression deserves attention. BAP1 encodes a deubiquitinase involved in DNA damage repair [38]; its mutation can give rise to multiple tumor types [39] and BAP1-mutant KIRC has been characterized as a molecularly distinct, aggressive subtype associated with poor prognosis[40,41] In this study, BAP1 mutations were more frequent and significantly enriched in low-RAPGEF2 tumors, providing a potential genomic explanation for the poor prognosis of this subgroup, and consistent with the significantly elevated FGA, FGG, FGL, and local copy-number variation burden in the low-RAPGEF2 group. These findings suggest that low RAPGEF2 expression may characterize a genomically unstable and molecularly heterogeneous KIRC subtype.

Functional experiments provided biological evidence supporting the tumor-suppressive role of RAPGEF2 in KIRC cells. Knockdown of RAPGEF2 in Caki-1 and 786-O cells significantly increased cell proliferation and migration. These in vitro results are consistent with the clinical observation that low RAPGEF2 expression predicts worse outcomes, suggesting that RAPGEF2 may restrain malignant progression in KIRC. Notably, metabolic reprogramming is a hallmark of KIRC, a process driven primarily by VHL loss and the subsequent stabilization of HIF transcription factors[42,43]. In this study, functional enrichment analyses indicated that RAPGEF2 expression was closely associated with metabolic remodeling. We noted that RAPGEF2 was significantly negatively correlated with the biosynthesis of branched-chain amino acids. Leucine, a member of branched-chain amino acids, is an important activating signal for mTORC1 activity: as a direct ligand of the intracellular sensor Sestrin2, leucine binding activates mTORC1 and promotes anabolism [44]. The upregulation of branched-chain amino acid biosynthetic pathways in the low-RAPGEF2 KIRC subgroup suggests that enhanced leucine supply may sustain mTORC1 activation, thereby driving protein translation and lipid synthesis while suppressing autophagy [45], conferring an anabolic advantage to tumor cells to meet their biomass demands. This pathway association provides a potential explanation, from the perspective of amino acid metabolism, for the aggressive phenotype of low-RAPGEF2 tumors.

Finally, exploratory immunotherapy analyses suggested that RAPGEF2 may be associated with treatment response in specific immune therapeutic contexts. High RAPGEF2 expression was associated with better overall survival in anti-PD-1/PD-L1 cohorts and showed a potential favorable trend in a CAR-T cohort, whereas its association with anti-CTLA-4 therapy appeared distinct. These findings suggest that although patients with low RAPGEF2 expression generally exhibit poorer overall prognosis, they may derive greater benefit from anti-CTLA-4 therapy and could therefore be considered as a potential subgroup for this therapeutic strategy.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, although multiple public datasets and an internal IHC cohort were used, most clinical analyses were retrospective. In particular, the internal IHC cohort comprised only 43 patients; owing to the relatively small number of endpoint events, we used only KM survival analysis to explore its prognostic significance. Second, although knockdown experiments support a tumor-suppressive role, the downstream molecular mechanisms of RAPGEF2 require further investigation through rescue experiments, in vivo models, and mechanistic studies of metabolic regulation. In addition, the association between RAPGEF2 and metabolic reprogramming was derived solely from transcriptomic enrichment analysis and remains at the level of correlation.

In conclusion, our study identifies RAPGEF2 as an independent protective prognostic biomarker in KIRC and suggests that its low expression is linked to genomic instability, metabolic reprogramming, enhanced malignant phenotypes. These findings provide a rationale for further investigation of RAPGEF2 as a biomarker and potential functional regulator in KIRC.
