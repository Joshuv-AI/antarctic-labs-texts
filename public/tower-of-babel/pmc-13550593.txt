High-performance near-infrared circularly polarized electroluminescence with an emission peak beyond 900 nm
Authors: Jiaqi Wang, Xiang Guan, Xiuling Li, Jinli Liu, Chunli Zhao, Junnan Wang, Yourong Wu, Chengjie Wang, Zhuojian Li, Renjing Chen, Peng Wu, Xueting Liu, Zixun Zhao, Zemin Wang, Yingjie Zhao, Zongwen Liu, Jiao Liu, Xin Tong, Zhiming Wang, Zhanhua Wei, Kebin Lin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Near-infrared spin-light-emitting diodes (NIR-Spin-LEDs), which generate circularly polarized light, offer opportunities for applications such as biomedicine. However, the longest reported emission wavelength of NIR-Spin-LEDs remains limited to 782 nm, making spin-polarized electroluminescence beyond 800 nm challenging. Here we show NIR-Spin-LEDs based on a mixed-dimensional tin-based perovskite heterostructure incorporating chiral R/S-α-methylbenzylammonium (R/S-MBA+) spacer cations. The low-dimensional phases induced by R/S-MBA+ provide chiroptical activity, while three-dimensional FA0.9Cs0.1SnI3 domains serve as the near-infrared emissive centers. Controlled crystallization produces a cypress-leaf-like morphology that promotes carrier confinement and improves charge-injection balance. The resulting devices exhibit electroluminescence peaking at 905 nm, with an external quantum efficiency of 7.8% and a maximum electroluminescence dissymmetry factor (gEL) of 5.5 × 10−2. This work extends spin-polarized electroluminescence into the deep near-infrared region and provides a strategy for developing lead-free NIR-Spin-LEDs.

Introduction

Circularly polarized near-infrared (CP-NIR) light is pivotal for biomedical imaging, encrypted communication, and spintronics1–5. Current generation methods relying on external optical waveplates suffer from energy loss and bulky footprints, making materials capable of direct CP-NIR electroluminescence (EL) highly desirable for integrated applications6–8. While significant progress has been made in circularly polarized light (CPL) materials, achieving long-wavelength EL remains a challenge9–12. Many research efforts have attempted to apply these new materials to the fabrication of long-wavelength circularly polarized electroluminescent devices. As shown in Table 1, the emission of existing devices is limited to ~782 nm13–16. Although inorganic quantum dots can reach wavelengths beyond 1000 nm, they have failed to yield efficient EL devices due to poor carrier transport17. Chiral perovskite materials have emerged as a leading research frontier in spin optoelectronics due to their unique optical activity and spin-manipulation capabilities18–21. The synthesis of chiral perovskites typically leverages the intrinsic defect tolerance of perovskite frameworks and the structural diversity of chiral organic molecules22. Incorporating chiral components into the perovskite lattice effectively induces structural asymmetry and thereby produces CPL23. At present, most of the reported circularly polarized electroluminescent perovskite light-emitting diodes (PeLEDs) are based on lead-halide perovskites, with the emission wavelength mainly confined to the visible to short near-infrared region13,24,25. Tin (Sn) is known as an ideal lead alternative element in perovskite optoelectronic devices, and Sn-based perovskites offer a narrower bandgap, strong spin-orbit coupling and low toxicity, making them promising candidates for achieving circularly polarized electroluminescence (CPEL) in the longer-wavelength NIR region26–28.

Achieving CPEL-NIR from chiral Sn-based perovskites remains an unresolved challenge due to several key constraints. The poorly controlled crystallization of Sn-based perovskites often yields heterogeneous polycrystalline films rich in defects, which severely suppresses luminescence efficiency29. The facile oxidation of Sn2+ to Sn4+ during processing and operation, which may create deep-level traps and nonradiative recombination centers, undermines both device performance and stability30,31. While the soft lattice nature of Sn-based perovskites allows the incorporation of a robust and efficient chiral framework into its defect-sensitive active layer, this process is inherently challenging, as it requires a finely balanced interplay among chiral additives, crystallization thermodynamics and optoelectronic properties. These coupled issues constitute major bottlenecks that must be addressed to translate chiral Sn-based perovskites into practical CP-NIR devices.

In this work, bulky chiral R/S-α-methylbenzylammonium (R/S-MBA+) spacer cations were introduced into the Sn-based perovskite to regulate crystallization and construct a mixed-dimensional (2D/3D) perovskite heterostructure. The R/S-MBA+ cations promote the formation of chiral low-dimensional 2D phases, which are mainly responsible for the chiroptical response, while the 3D FA0.9Cs0.1SnI3 domains serve as the NIR emissive centers. Meanwhile, the steric and coordination effects of R/S-MBA+ modulate the evolution of precursor clusters, suppress premature aggregation and uncontrolled coalescence, and thereby yield high-quality mixed-dimensional Sn-based perovskite films with improved crystallinity. The resulting cypress-leaf-like morphology further promotes carrier confinement and increases the effective contact area between the perovskite and the electron-transport layer, facilitating electron injection and improving carrier-injection balance in intrinsically p-type Sn-based perovskites. As a result, the optimized devices achieve NIR circularly polarized electroluminescence at 905 nm, with a peak EQE of 7.8% and a maximum electroluminescence dissymmetry factor gEL of 5.5 × 10−2. This work provides a viable route toward lead-free, compact CP-NIR emitters.

Results and discussions

Fabrication of R/S-MBA-doped Sn-based perovskite films

One of the most promising strategies for obtaining high-quality Sn-based perovskite films is to reduce their dimensionality by introducing low-dimensional phases into the 3D matrix. This configuration enables the formation of 2D/3D energy landscapes, which effectively confine charge carriers and significantly suppress non-radiative recombination. An effective method for constructing such mixed-dimensional perovskites is incorporating large organic spacer cations. To simultaneously generate 2D perovskite phases and introduce a chirality source, we selected R/S-methylbenzylamine (R/S-MBA+) as the spacer cation (Supplementary Fig. 1). The R/S-MBAI precursors were chemically synthesized (Supplementary Fig. 2), and the detailed synthesis procedure is described in the “Methods” section. The concentration of R/S-MBA+ at the A-site has a decisive influence on the strength of the chiral signal and the optical properties of the perovskite film. Accordingly, four perovskite films were fabricated from precursor solutions containing nominal R-MBA precursor ratios (R/S-MBA+:FA+) of 0%, 30%, 50%, and 70% relative to the small A-site cations. Hereafter, the samples are denoted as 0%, 30%, 50%, and 70% R-MBAI, respectively. Unless otherwise specified, these labels refer to the nominal precursor compositions rather than the actual cation compositions in the deposited films. Bulk 1H NMR analysis of the dissolved perovskite films is provided in Supplementary Fig. 3. The effect of different concentrations of R-MBAI on tuning the optical and morphological properties of the R-MBA-doped Sn-based perovskites was investigated.

Figure 1a illustrates the mixed-dimensional Sn-based perovskite heterostructure composed of chiral low-dimensional phases and 3D emissive domains. After introducing R-MBAI, a new low-angle diffraction peak appears at 6.7°, which can be assigned to the characteristic reflection of the 2D perovskite phase (R-MBA)2SnI432, indicating the formation of R-MBA+-induced chiral low-dimensional layered phases. With increasing R-MBAI concentration, the intensity of this low-dimensional-phase-related peak gradually increases, suggesting the progressive growth of the 2D layered component. Meanwhile, the diffraction peaks at 14.1° and 28.3° are retained, confirming the coexistence of the 3D FA0.9Cs0.1SnI3 phase, which serves as the NIR emissive domain. Notably, the 50% R-MBAI sample exhibits the strongest diffraction intensity associated with the 3D phase, indicating improved crystallinity of the emissive matrix. In contrast, the 70% R-MBAI sample shows weakened 3D-phase diffraction together with enhanced low-dimensional-phase signals, suggesting that excessive R-MBA+ cations disrupt the formation of the 3D network and promote the growth of lower-dimensional phases. These results confirm the formation of a mixed-dimensional structure consisting of R-MBA+-induced chiral low-dimensional phases and 3D FA0.9Cs0.1SnI3 emissive domains. The successful incorporation and configuration of the R-MBA+ cations within the perovskite lattice were further corroborated by Fourier-transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy (Supplementary Fig. 4). Characteristic vibrations of the phenyl ring are evident in all composite films. The in-plane ring stretching vibration, sensitive to the electronic structure of the aromatic system, was observed at approximately 1497 cm−1. Significantly, the band exhibited a slight but consistent shift to higher wavenumbers compared to that of the pure R-MBAI powder (1488 cm−1), indicating a modified electronic environment of the phenyl ring upon its confinement within the inorganic framework. The sharpened feature in the 70% sample suggests a more ordered or distinct packing arrangement of the organic cation, consistent with the enhanced formation of a low-dimensional phase as identified by XRD.

The scanning electron microscopy (SEM) results clearly demonstrate that introducing the chiral molecule R-MBAI significantly affects the crystallization and morphology of the film (Supplementary Fig. 5). The 0% R-MBAI film shows markedly rough and irregularly perovskite crystals compared to the 30% R-MBAI and 50% R-MBAI films. The 30% R-MBAI film exhibits sharp dendritic features with substantial thickness variations. Specifically, the 50% R-MBAI film exhibits unique cypress-leaf-like radial perovskite grains, where the cypress-leaf-like grew from the center towards the periphery, and the perovskite crystals between the cypress-leaf-like are not completely continuous. While the 70% R-MBAI film exhibits an amorphous morphology, it shows suppressed 3D perovskite formation, possibly due to excessive organic bulky cations, which is consistent with XRD results where the relative content of the 3D perovskite phase decreased. Based on this comprehensive analysis and the results of tests on the performance of PeLED devices (Supplementary Fig. 6), the 50% R-MBAI sample was identified as optimal and selected for further investigation.

The steady-state photoluminescence spectra of the samples are shown in Fig. 1c. The R-MBA-doped Sn-based perovskite film shows a significantly higher PL intensity than that of the FA0.9Cs0.1SnI3, indicating that the introduction of an appropriate R-MBAI could effectively suppress non-radiative recombination in perovskite films. To further investigate the PL enhancement mechanism, we conducted time-resolved photoluminescence (TRPL) measurement (Supplementary Fig. 7). The R-MBA-doped Sn-based perovskite film showed longer lifetimes compared to the FA0.9Cs0.1SnI3 film. The UV-vis spectra show that, as the amount of R-MBAI increases, the absorption rate exhibits a downward trend (Supplementary Fig. 8). The main reason is the insertion of the large organic spacers, which promotes the formation of low-dimensional phases in the film. Specifically, the emergence of new absorption peaks in Fig. 1c aligns with the mirror-imaged Cotton effects observed in the CD spectra (Fig. 1d), confirming their origin from the pure (R/S-MBA)2SnI4 phase32. These CD signals, which were absent in the rac-MBAI film (Supplementary Fig. 9), confirm that the chiral R/S-MBA+ cations effectively induce chirality in the low-dimensional 2D layered phase. Together with the retained 3D FA0.9Cs0.1SnI3 absorption and emission features, these results confirm the coexistence of chiral low-dimensional phases and 3D emissive domains in the mixed-dimensional heterostructure. These results indicate that the ground-state chiroptical response is mainly associated with the R/S-MBA+-induced chiral low-dimensional phase, while the 3D FA0.9Cs0.1SnI3 domains remain responsible for the NIR emission. The pseudo-color TA map and delay-dependent TA spectra show that the transient feature associated with the low-dimensional phase decays rapidly at early delay times, while the NIR feature associated with the 3D FA0.9Cs0.1SnI3 phase gradually builds up. The ultrafast low-dimensional to 3D energy transfer may compete with spin relaxation, allowing partial spin polarization to be preserved before radiative recombination in the 3D FA0.9Cs0.1SnI3 emissive domains. Therefore, the observed CPL emission at ~905 nm is attributed to radiative recombination of partially spin-polarized carriers in the 3D emissive domains after energy funneling from the chiral 2D phase.

Crystallization control of perovskites enabled by R/S-MBA+ addition

Previous studies have shown that the major challenges of Sn-based perovskites arise from their susceptibility to Sn2+ oxidation and their difficult-to-control crystallization process34–37. We found that the addition of R-MBAI had a significant effect on the crystallization behavior of Sn-based perovskites.

We systematically investigated the influence of R-MBAI introduction on the crystallization kinetics of Sn-based perovskites to elucidate the underlying mechanism governing the device performance of R-MBA-doped Sn-based perovskite films. Initially, we analyzed the defect formation mechanism in the FA0.9Cs0.1SnI3 film, where Cs+ doping contributes to improved structural stability, while the addition of SnF2 serves to effectively suppress the oxidation of Sn2+38,39. The inferior performance of FA0.9Cs0.1SnI3 is primarily attributed to the rapid and uncontrolled aggregation of perovskite nanoclusters during crystallization40. As shown in Fig. 2a, the FA0.9Cs0.1SnI3 precursor solution is dominated by solvent-coordinated SnI2 complexes, which can adopt chain-like structural motifs in the coordinated state41. During the spin-coating and annealing processes, the rapid evaporation of the solvent promoted the swift formation of perovskite clusters, resulting in an excessive number of nucleation sites. The intrinsically fast crystallization kinetics of FA0.9Cs0.1SnI3 led to disordered crystal stacking and a significantly elevated defect density, thereby causing severe fluorescence quenching. Correspondingly, incorporating 50% R-MBA+ cation bearing bulky phenyl groups introduced pronounced steric hindrance. During subsequent annealing, this steric crowding generated intercluster repulsion, which suppressed rapid aggregation and uncontrolled coalescence of perovskite clusters. Such kinetic regulation promoted more controlled crystal growth, leading to improved lattice ordering and preferred orientation. Meanwhile, the outward diffusion of the chiral species from the film interior toward the periphery facilitated chiral perovskite crystallization and self-assembly, and the organic cations simultaneously passivated surface defects. In-situ PL measurements during the annealing process were performed to validate our hypothesis and directly compare the crystallization pathways (Fig. 2c). In this process, the PL intensity showed a trend of initial increasing and then decreasing. The in-situ PL kinetics reveal that the crystallization of the R-MBA-doped Sn-based perovskite film was significantly retarded, with its characteristic perovskite emission signal appearing only after 16 s of annealing. In contrast, the FA0.9Cs0.1SnI3 film crystallized almost immediately upon heating. After reaching their respective maximum intensities, both films exhibited varying degrees of PL quenching (inset curve in Fig. 2c). The R-MBA-doped Sn-based perovskite film not only demonstrated higher PL intensity but also exhibited markedly improved stability compared with the FA0.9Cs0.1SnI3 film. This performance enhancement is attributed to the effective defect passivation by the chiral cations, which significantly suppresses non-radiative recombination.

Figure 2d shows the more specific PL evolution during the nucleation and crystallization processes (PL intensity rise, FA0.9Cs0.1SnI3 within 2–8 s and R-MBA-doped Sn-based perovskites within 16–24 s). The distinct red shift in the FA0.9Cs0.1SnI3 film, which might originate from burst nucleation at the early crystallization stage, where small grains dominated the system, and as the solvent evaporated, the grains grew progressively, leading to a gradual reduction in the quantum confinement effect and consequently the observed red shift. By comparison, the R-MBA-doped Sn-based perovskite film maintained a stable emission wavelength throughout the crystallization process. The stability can be attributed to the binding of R-MBA+ cations to one-dimensional perovskite chains, where steric repulsion from the phenyl groups suppressed cluster coalescence, thereby reducing the effective cluster size and improving dispersion homogeneity. The resulting more stable precursor system enabled slow and ordered crystal growth from the very beginning, thereby avoiding significant spectral shifts. Further 1H NMR analysis revealed an upfield shift of the -NH2 proton signal in R-MBAI (Fig. 2e), confirming hydrogen-bond formation between R-MBA+ and SnI2. This interaction facilitates the transmission of steric effects to the perovskite clusters. To verify the influence of these interactions at the solution state, dynamic light scattering (DLS) measurements were performed (Fig. 2f). In the absence of steric regulation, the FA0.9Cs0.1SnI3 precursor contained relatively large and polydisperse clusters, which tended to promote excessive nucleation and irregular crystal growth. By contrast, the R-MBA-doped Sn-based perovskite precursor featured markedly smaller and more uniform clusters. A consistent trend was observed for the precursor containing 30% R-MBAI, which exhibits an intermediate cluster size (Supplementary Fig. 12), further highlighting the role of R-MBA+ in modulating cluster formation.

The ordered assembly in solution directly influenced the surface chemical states of the resulting perovskite films. X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS, Fig. 2g) shows that the N1s binding energy of the –NH3+ group in the R-MBA-doped Sn-based perovskites film is 400.4 eV, which is distinct from that of the pristine perovskite (402.2 eV) and pure R-MBAI (402.7 eV). This shift indicates that R-MBAI interacted with the perovskite film through the –NH3+ moiety, consistent with effective defect passivation.

The improved film quality is directly reflected in device performance. The trap-state density was evaluated using the space-charge-limited current (SCLC) method with a device of structure of ITO/SnO2/perovskite/TPBi/LiF/Al. The R-MBA-doped Sn-based perovskites device exhibited a significantly lower trap-filled limit voltage (VTFL) than the FA0.9Cs0.1SnI3 (Supplementary Fig. 13), indicating a reduced defect density. In addition, atomic force microscopy (AFM) shows that the R-MBA-doped Sn-based perovskites film has a lower surface roughness (Ra) than that of the FA0.9Cs0.1SnI3 film (Supplementary Fig. 14).

Influence of thermal annealing on the grain structure of perovskite

During our investigation, we found that precise control over the crystallization of R-MBA-doped Sn-based perovskites plays a decisive role in the performance of CPEL-NIR devices. The SEM images reveal pronounced grain-size inhomogeneity. The dendritic edge regions exhibit large and continuously connected grains (white-circled areas in Supplementary Fig. 4c), whereas the central region is dominated by relatively small and isolated crystallites. The edge morphology, characterized by gradual coalescence into larger and irregular grains, is indicative of excessive Ostwald ripening, which may modify carrier transport pathways within the perovskite film.

To obtain high-quality perovskite crystals with suppressed over-ripening and systematically elucidate the microstructural evolution, we performed controlled thermal annealing by precisely tuning the film crystallization conditions. By regulating the extent of grain ripening, we reproducibly accessed three representative states, named as Low-crystallinity (I), Target (II), and Over-ripened (III), and the detailed protocol were provided in Supporting Information. The low-crystallinity films were formed under vacuum-assisted negative pressure, which retained residual solvent at grain boundaries and thereby hindered complete lattice formation and continuous crystal growth. In the absence of thermal assistance, chiral-molecule-driven ordering was largely suppressed. The SEM images show densely packed and poorly crystallized grains with noticeable residual unreacted precursors. In the Target state, a high-quality mixed-dimensional film with a nominal composition of R-MBA-doped Sn-based perovskites was obtained. The subsequent appropriate annealing facilitated the regular arrangement of chiral cations, which helped suppress nonradiative recombination. The SEM images reveal refined surface features together with enlarged mesoscopic growth domains, giving rise to a cypress-leaf-like radial morphology. In the Over-ripened state, prolonged annealing promoted the thermally driven loss of chiral cations and partial degradation of low-dimensional phases. The initial isolated grains coarsened and merged via Ostwald ripening into elongated and stripe-like features, accompanied by a pronounced reduction in film coverage. As the R-MBA+ content decreased, steric regulation became insufficient, and the crystallization behavior increasingly resembled that of the R-MBA-doped Sn-based perovskites film. To clarify the origin of the cypress-leaf-like morphology, we further prepared perovskite films based on S-MBA and rac-MBA under identical fabrication conditions. Comparative SEM images (Supplementary Fig. 16) show that the R-MBA-, S-MBA-, and rac-MBA-based films all exhibit highly similar leaf-like radial morphologies. This result indicates that the cypress-leaf-like morphology is not uniquely determined by molecular handedness, but mainly arises from the steric effect of the bulky MBA-based organic cations and the crystallization pathway during film formation.

Meanwhile, as shown in Supplementary Fig. 17, when the films were treated at different temperatures (40 °C, 80 °C, and 100 °C), excessively high temperatures were found to have transformed the morphology into long and radially extending stripes originating from the center. Corresponding electroluminescent devices fabricated based on these films were evaluated (Supplementary Fig. 18). The performance results further confirm that films exhibiting a small-sized and isolated cypress-leaf-like radial morphology deliver superior electroluminescent performance.

The decisive role of morphological evolution in governing carrier diffusion and recombination dynamics is illustrated in Supplementary Fig. 19 and Supplementary Fig. 20. In the Over-ripened film with elongated and connected stripe-like features, coalesced grains provide extended transport pathways, which promote long-range and nondirectional carrier diffusion. Such long-range diffusion increases both trap-assisted nonradiative losses and the probability of Auger recombination. In contrast, the Target film consists of spatially separated cypress-leaf-like emissive domains. This domain isolation localizes electron-hole pairs within individual emissive domains, thereby suppressing trap-assisted quenching associated with long-range migration and promoting radiative recombination. This interpretation is supported by the carrier-injection balance analysis shown in Supplementary Fig. 21, which reveals more severe hole-dominated injection and carrier imbalance in the non-optimized devices.

To systematically elucidate the properties of the films at different representative crystallization states and validate the related mechanisms, comprehensive characterizations were performed. The XRD patterns (Fig. 3b) show that the low-crystallinity perovskite film exhibited a weakened diffraction intensity and a broadened full width at half maximum (FWHM) which were possibly due to incomplete removal of strong coordinating solvents and the persistence of solvent-SnI2 intermediate species (e.g., SnI2·DMSO) that retarded ion rearrangement and suppressed crystallization42,43. XRD peak-broadening analysis was used to estimate the coherent crystallite size of the films (Supplementary Table 1). Together with the XRD, SEM, and defect-characterization results, the improved performance of the Target film can be attributed to improved crystallinity, appropriate mixed-dimensional phase distribution, reduced defect density, and optimized film morphology.

Meanwhile, insufficient thermal activation might trigger early-stage chemical decomposition and halide loss in Sn iodide perovskites, generating iodide vacancies and compressive lattice contraction, as reflected by the shift of diffraction peaks toward higher 2θ44. The characteristic diffraction peaks of the chiral low‑dimensional phase (R‑MBA)2SnI4 disappeared in Over-ripened samples, confirming the thermal decomposition of the chiral low-dimensional perovskite phase. The CD spectra (Fig. 3c) show that the signal intensity of the Over-ripened film decreased significantly compared with that of the Target R-MBA-doped Sn-based perovskites film, indicating a weakened chiroptical activity. Besides, the CD spectrum of the Low-crystallinity film resembles that of free R/S-MBA+ (Supplementary Fig. 22), suggesting that insufficient crystallization prevents the effective formation of the chiral low-dimensional 2D layered phase, thereby weakening the chiroptical response of the mixed-dimensional heterostructure.

The degradation of the low‑dimensional organic layers also compromised their barrier function against moisture and oxygen. As shown in Fig. 3d, the XPS analysis indicates an increased Sn4+/Sn2+ ratio after decomposition of the low‑dimensional phase, pointing to enhanced oxidation of Sn2+. The insufficiently crystallized film also exhibited oxidation due to lattice attack by residual solvents. The PL spectra (Fig. 3e) further reveal that the Low-crystallinity film showed weak emission and a slight blue shift owing to small grains and extensive non‑radiative recombination at grain boundaries, while the Over-ripened film exhibited both quenched and red‑shifted emission resulting from defect formation, reduction of low-dimensional components and large‑grain growth. The TRPL measurements (Fig. 3f) demonstrate that the carrier lifetime in the optimally crystallized Target film was significantly longer than those in the other states. These findings were corroborated by the SCLC results (Fig. 3g), extracting VTFL values of 2.21 V, 1.68 V, and 2.01 V for the three stages, respectively. The lowest VTFL value of the Target film confirms its low defect density.

In summary, we have achieved efficient NIR CPEL by introducing R/S-MBA+ spacer cations to construct a mixed-dimensional Sn-based perovskite heterostructure composed of chiral low-dimensional phases and 3D emissive domains, combined with regulation of crystallization behavior. Effective is the controlled cypress-leaf-like radial morphology improves carrier confinement and carrier injection balance, thereby enhancing the radiative efficiency of the device. Using the optimized Target crystallization state, we realized unprecedented CPEL with an emission peak at 905 nm, a competitive EQE of 7.8% and a maximum of gEL = 5.5 × 10−2. This work provides a general material and device design strategy toward lead-free, long-wavelength and integrated spin-polarized CP-NIR emitters.

Methods

Materials

Cesium iodide (CsI, 99.999%), tin (II) iodide (SnI2, 99.999%), Formamidinium iodide (FAI, 99.99%), lithium fluoride (LiF, 99.99%) and tin powder (Sn, 99.8%) were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich. R/S-methylbenzylamine (R/S-MBA, 99%) and rac-methylbenzylamine (rac-MBA, 98%) were acquired from TCI. Ethanol (EtOH, >99.5%) and tin (II) fluoride (SnF2, 98 %) were obtained from Adamas-beta. 1,3,5Tris(1-phenyl-1H-benzimidazol-2-yl)benzene (TPBi, 99%) were purchased from Xi’an Yuri Solar Co., Ltd. Dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO, 99.9%) and N,N-dimethylformamide (DMF) were purchased from Thermo Fisher Scientific Co., Ltd. PEDOT:PSS (AI 4083) was purchased from Ossila. All the chemicals listed above are commercially available and can be used without further purification.

Synthesis of R/S/rac-MBAI

R/S/rac-MBAI was synthesized by reacting R/S/rac-MBA with a slight excess of hydroiodic acid (molar ratio 1:1.05) in an ice bath under continuous stirring for 4 h. The solvent was subsequently removed by rotary evaporation at ambient temperature to obtain the crude product. Purification was achieved through double recrystallization from absolute ethanol using diethyl ether as an anti-solvent. The final white crystalline product was collected and vacuum-dried at 70 °C for 12 h.

Preparation ofchiral Sn-based perovskite films

The precursor solution (0.25 M) was prepared in a mixed solvent of DMSO and DMF (4:1) by dissolving R/S/rac-MBAI, FAI, CsI, SnI2, SnF2 and Sn powder (5 mg mL−1) with molar ratios of 1.2x: 1.2(1-x): 0.1: 1: 0.1, where x represents the nominal R/S-MBAI precursor ratio relative to FAI (0%, 30%, 50%, and 70%). Unless otherwise specified, all composition labels (0%, 30%, 50%, and 70% R-MBAI) used throughout this manuscript and the Supporting Information denote the nominal molar ratios in the precursor solutions and are employed solely to identify samples prepared from different precursor formulations, rather than the actual cation compositions in the deposited perovskite films. The solution was stirred overnight in a N2-filled glovebox at room temperature and filtered through a 0.1 μm PTFE filter before use. The perovskite films were fabricated via a one-step spin-coating method. A 40 μL precursor solution was first dispensed onto the substrates and spun at 4000 rpm for 60 s, then followed by annealing under controlled conditions.

Device fabrication

The ITO substrates were ultrasonically cleaned in a detergent solution for 15 min, followed by sequential rinsing with deionized water and ethanol, and finally dried under N2 flow. The substrates were further treated with oxygen plasma (LEBO instrument) for 5 min. A diluted PEDOT:PSS solution was spin-coated onto the treated ITO substrates at 4000 rpm for 60 s, followed by annealing at 150 °C for 15 min in air. Subsequently, the substrates were transferred into a N2-filled glovebox for deposition of the perovskite layer as described above. Finally, the samples were loaded into a thermal evaporation system where 45 nm TPBi, 1.2 nm LiF and 100 nm Al were sequentially deposited under high vacuum (<5 × 10−4 Pa). The active area of the resulting PeLEDs was 0.04 cm2.

Film and device characterization measurements

The XRD patterns were acquired using a Bruker D8 advance diffractometer. The FTIR spectra were collected from Bruker Tensor spectrometer in attenuated total reflection (ATR) mode. The 1H NMR spectra were recorded using a Bruker AV-600 spectrometer. The XPS measurements were conducted using a Thermo Scientific K-Alpha spectrometer. The CD spectra were measured from Applied Photophysics Chirascan in the range of 300–1000 nm. The CPL measurements on thin films were excited by a 365 nm laser, and the CPEL measurements on PeLEDs were driven by a Keithley 2400 source meter. The devices were encapsulated before testing to improve operational stability. The CPEL measurement setup was further upgraded from a single-path sequential configuration to a dual-path simultaneous configuration. The EL emitted from the same operating PeLED was divided into two equivalent optical paths using a 50/50 beam splitter, and the two opposite circularly polarized EL components were analyzed simultaneously by two equivalent detection channels consisting of quarter-wave plates, linear polarizers, and spectrometers. The UV-vis absorption spectra were recorded using a Shimadzu UV-2600 spectrophotometer. The PL and TRPL measurements were conducted using a HORIBA Jobin Yvon Fluorolog-3 system. SEM images were obtained from Thermo scientific Apreo 2S. The AFM results were acquired using the Bruker Dimension Icon. The TA spectra were measured by Harpia-TA (Light Conversion) transient absorption spectrometer. The pump and probe beams were derived from the 1030 nm PHAROS femtosecond lasers (200 uJ pulse-150 KHz, ~200 fs, Light Conversion). The pump pulse was generated by output pulse 1030 nm and goes into the optical parametric amplifier (OPA, Orpheus-HP, Light Conversion) to get 405 nm as a pump pulse to excite samples. The super continuous white light probe was generated by focusing 10% of the 1030 nm onto sapphire.

Device characterization

The current density-voltage-radiance (J–V–R), external quantum efficiency (EQE), and electroluminescence (EL) spectra of the PeLEDs were measured using a calibrated integrating-sphere system consisting of a QEPro spectrometer (Ocean Optics, Insight), a Keithley 2400 source meter, and a LabVIEW-based automated acquisition program. During the measurements, the devices were mounted inside the integrating sphere to collect the emitted photons over nearly the entire 4π solid angle. The EQE was calculated from the measured total emitted optical power together with the injected current, while the EL spectra were simultaneously recorded by the QEPro spectrometer. All measurements were carried out under ambient conditions unless otherwise specified.
