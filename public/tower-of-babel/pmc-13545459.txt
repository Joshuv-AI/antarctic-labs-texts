Low statistical power creates two symmetrical problems, not one
Authors: Kenneth R Foster
Published: 2026

To the Editor,

In their simulation study on the magnification of errors in small studies that select for statistical significance (M errors), Jaksic et al. recommend that readers should “treat results from analyses with low statistical power … with caution, as they are more likely to produce exaggerated effect estimates” [1]. They recommend that readers “give greater weight to estimates with narrow confidence intervals” than to significance status alone. However, their analysis characterizes only results that reach significance.

Low power creates two symmetrical problems: it overstates the effect size in results that are selected for statistical significance (Type M error, as defined by Gelman and Carlin [2], though the underlying phenomenon has long been recognized in epidemiology as sparse data bias [3] and was noted as early as the 1950s [4]), and leads to results, whether significant or not, that are frequently too imprecise to be useful: the confidence interval may be compatible with effect sizes several-fold different from, or opposite in sign to, the point estimate, leaving little basis to discriminate among competing explanations [5]. This can lead to a different problem: a wide CI can lead to fragile results such that small changes in the underlying data could push the results into or out of significance [6]. In this letter I extend Jaksic et al.'s approach to this second half of the picture, which their recommendation acknowledges but is not quantified in their analysis.

Both effects are correlated and become worse with small-n studies. To explore this, I conducted a Monte Carlo simulation that assumes two groups, an informative group and a much larger comparison group, with a true effect size (Cohen's d) of 0.5, i.e., 0.5 population standard deviations. The informative group varied in size while the comparison group was fixed at 1000. This design could describe an observational study comparing health in an exposed group and a much larger unexposed reference population, or an experimental study comparing outcomes in exposed vs. control groups with appropriate adjustment of study size. Details are provided in the Appendix.

For each group, the sampling mean was drawn from N(μ, σ2/n), where σ2 is the population variance, while the resulting sample variance, s2, follows σ2 · χ2₍ₙ₋₁₎ / (n − 1). This propagates the extra uncertainty that comes from estimating the standard deviation from a small sample. The mean difference and its 95% confidence interval were computed using Welch's t-test (unequal-variance, unequal-n formula), which is appropriate whenever the two group sizes differ substantially. Each simulated scenario continued until at least 25,000 significant results had accumulated, requiring between about 30,000 and 280,000 total replicates depending on group size. Details of the simulation are in the Appendix, and its results are summarized in Table 1.

With only three observations in the informative group, the CI on the effect size is compatible with a harmful effect (group mean differences <0) more than three times the size of the true effect size, to a beneficial effect more than five times its size. When results are selected for statistical significance (third column), the mean effect size is twice the true value. These two effects, of sampling on the width of confidence intervals and of selection bias in choosing results that pass the significance filter, become much worse as the size of a study is decreased. Confidence intervals that are wide relative to the point estimate can give rise to a further problem when a bound lies near the null: excessive fragility of the study's significance verdict.

These two measures answer two different questions: “what parameter values are compatible with these data?” and “assuming a plausible true effect, how much should I expect a statistically significant estimate to exaggerate it?” While the precise statistical mechanisms differ, both effects are largely determined by study size. This suggests a simple guidance that covers both problems at once: be skeptical of any result, significant or not, whose reported interval is wide relative to its own point estimate. Either effect could lead to a substantial misinterpretation of the results of an underpowered study.

Misinterpretation of confidence intervals has been documented for decades and remains rampant [7], and Type M error is misused in a parallel way: although Gelman and Carlin never proposed the use of the exaggeration ratio to adjust individual effect size estimates, researchers have used it for exactly that purpose [8]. But careful consideration of these two complementary effects, taken together, could provide a more complete assessment of a study than consideration of either effect alone. Both failures come from reading the p-value and stopping there.

Simulation methodology

A Python program was developed to perform a Monte Carlo simulation of experimental outcomes in which a continuous measurement (e.g. a biomarker level) was compared between an “informative” group I and a comparison group C. The size of group C was fixed at 1000 individuals, while the size of group I was varied (3, 5, 10, 20, 50, and 100 individuals) to examine how group size affects the precision and reliability of the comparison. The true effect size (μI − μC, the difference between true group means) was assumed to be 0.5 population units (in Cohen's d, a “medium” effect), with a common population SD of 1.

For each group, the sampling mean was drawn from N(μ, σ2/n), where σ2 is the population variance, while the resulting sample variance, s2, follows σ2 · χ2₍ₙ₋₁₎ / (n − 1). For each simulated run, the difference between group means was tested using Welch's t-test (unequal-variance formula, using the Python package scipy.stats), which provides a p-value and a confidence interval on the difference between group means. For runs that reached statistical significance, the effect size was retained. For each size of group I, the simulation continued until at least 25,000 significant runs had accumulated, requiring between about 30,000 and 280,000 total runs depending on group size. For each size of group I, the median of the upper bounds and the median of the lower bounds of the confidence interval, taken across all runs, were used to display a representative interval in the table.

This simulation approximates a population of studies of this design, whether experimental or observational: for every run, a t-test and confidence interval are computed exactly as they would be for one real dataset, and runs that happen to reach statistical significance are examined separately to compare their effect size with the true effect.
