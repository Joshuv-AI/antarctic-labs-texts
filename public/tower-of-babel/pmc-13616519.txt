Early Origin of the Human Seminal Plasma Proteome During Vertebrate Evolution
Authors: Jose M Ranz, Alberto Civetta
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSeminal fluid proteins (SFPs)-encoding genes are rapidly evolving at the sequence level and often considered prone to frequent phylogenetic turnover. One mechanism underlying such rapid turnover is the lineage-restricted origin of new SFP genes, which results in a complement of phylogenetically young genes. Recent advances in genome resources and comparative genomics now enable more comprehensive cross-taxa analyses, providing improved estimates of the timing of gene origin during evolution. Using these resources alongside proteomic data from human seminal plasma, we traced the evolutionary origins of 646 putative human SFP genes. We find that most SFPs trace back to the bony vertebrate (Euteleostomi) radiation. These ancient SFP genes are more pleiotropic than younger ones, which are enriched in immunity-related functions and regulate proteolytic activity, particularly peptidase inhibitors. Together, these results underscore that the human proteome components of the seminal plasma originated early during vertebrate evolution, providing insights into the history of reproductive biology in deuterostomes.

Seminal fluid proteins (SFPs) influence a wide range of traits affecting male and female post-mating fitness, contributing as well to the reproductive isolation between populations. SFP-encoding genes typically exhibit rapid sequence evolution, and their origination patterns through the phylogeny drive ejaculate protein composition divergence across species. Here, we present a comprehensive analysis of human seminal plasma proteins, tracing their evolutionary origins across vertebrates. We find that the human SFP gene complement is overall evolutionarily ancient, with many genes present already in the genome of the common ancestor of bony fishes and humans. Notably, the evolutionary age of SFP genes is tightly associated with their functional breadth: ancient SFP genes tend to display a wider range of functions, while younger SFP genes are enriched primarily for immune and proteolytic functions. Our results highlight the contribution of an evolutionarily ancient gene repertoire to the contemporary reproductive functions of human SFPs.

Introduction

Seminal fluid proteins (SFPs) are transferred to females during copulation where they exert a number of important post-mating physiological and behavioral effects. Although these responses have been primarily studied in insects (Avila et al. 2011), mammalian SFPs have also been shown to facilitate fertilization by protecting the sperm against immune challenges, mediate the formation of a sperm storage reservoir, and trigger female responses that can induce ovulation, among others (McGraw et al. 2015; Schjenken and Robertson 2020). Notably, despite their central role in fertilization, SFP genes show, on average, rapid sequence evolution that is phylogenetically widespread (Swanson and Vacquier 2002; Clark and Swanson 2005; Ramm et al. 2008). In addition to this overall pattern of rapid sequence evolution, there is evidence of a major overhaul of the secretory function of tissues and anatomy of the glands that produce the seminal fluid (McGraw et al. 2015). However, our understanding of the evolution of SFP genes is largely incomplete, particularly in vertebrates, taxa in which we lack a systematic analysis of how the protein complement of the seminal fluid arose across key evolutionary transitions. Recently, Drosophila melanogaster SFPs have been shown to have 62% of their encoding genes originating before the radiation of the genus Drosophila (Ranz et al. 2025). Whether this pattern of ancient origin holds beyond protostomes remains untested.

Unlike previous work, which has largely emphasized the divergence and turnover of these genes, our focus here is to identify potentially phylogenetically old members of the SFP gene complement and to evaluate their prevalence. Specifically, by examining human SFPs’ phylogenetic origin, we address whether the previously reported pattern of a phylogenetically old SFP gene complement found for D. melanogaster is an oddity restricted to protostomes or a more universal pattern that extends to deuterostomes. First, we capitalize on proteomic characterizations of human seminal plasma from semen samples from two different studies (Pilch and Mann 2006; Batruch et al. 2011) to delineate an updated list of 646 human SFPs common to both studies. Then, using upgraded genomic resources from 23 vertebrate species (Shao et al. 2019; Chen et al. 2025), we establish the branch of origin of human SFP genes. We find that the majority of human SFPs are phylogenetically ancient and that the proportion of ancient genes among SFPs is overrepresented relative to the rest of the genome. The potential repurposing of the molecular machinery underlying male reproductive functions has important implications for the evolution of reproductive systems across the animal kingdom.

Materials and Methods

Sfp Gene Data

We retrieved data from two independent studies that characterized the human seminal plasma proteome from one (Pilch and Mann 2006) and three healthy individuals (Batruch et al. 2011), respectively. Both studies employed mass spectrometry and rigorous filtering strategies to ensure high-confidence protein identification, primarily relying on score-based validation derived from database search engines such as Mascot (Perkins et al. 1999). Additionally, both workflows incorporate layers of validation, such as replicate analyses and manual spectrum inspection, to improve the reliability of their results. Further, a reinforced list was obtained by excluding proteins with less than two peptide counts in either study. Additionally, this reinforced list was filtered by using coverage information provided by Batruch et al. 2011 (Batruch et al. 2011) to require a minimum sequence coverage of 10%, thus retaining more robust protein identifications for downstream analyses. Average protein abundance was calculated using the data from Batruch et al. 2011.

Tau values for human genes were taken as calculated (Palmer et al. 2021), which were based on the GTEx (version 8, https://www.gtexportal.org/home/) expression values for genes and transcripts. Tau values range from 0 to 1, from broader to more restricted expression, respectively (Yanai et al. 2005). Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment was assessed using DAVID (Sherman et al. 2022) and redundant terms were clustered under parental terms using the rrvgo R package (Sayols 2023), using a similarity threshold of 0.7 to reduce redundancy among GO terms. As the choice of the background gene set can substantially influence the enrichment results and their interpretation, the analysis was conducted using all protein-coding genes in the genome, and only the protein-coding genes present in the analyzed age category, as the background reference gene set. P-values were corrected for multiple tests following Benjamini-Hochberg (Benjamini and Hochberg 1995). Only functional terms with an associated adjusted P-value below 0.01 were considered. For each parental term, the enrichment score (−log10(Padj)) and the fold enrichment (log2(FE)) were calculated.

Phylogenetic Gene Age Data

The gene age information for 19,665 protein-coding human genes across seven evolutionary transitions or age classes along the vertebrate phylogeny was taken from Chen et al. 2025 (Chen et al. 2025). The authors used the Ensembl hg38_ver95 release and an upgraded version of GenTree (http://gentree.ioz.ac.cn/) (Shao et al. 2019), which relies on multiple pairwise sequence alignments between humans and 22 additional species: chimp, orangutan, gibbon, rhesus monkey, marmoset, rabbit, guinea pig, rat, mouse, horse, dog, cow, elephant, opossum, platypus, chicken, zebra finch, lizard, western clawed frog, stickleback, pufferfish, and zebrafish. Briefly, GenTree uses microsynteny information and a parsimony-based framework assigning gene origin at the deepest phylogenetic node. This approach is robust across genome releases and insensitive to the quality of gene annotation (Shao et al. 2019).

Statistical Analyses

Tests for chi-square goodness-of-fit, analysis of residuals, Benjamini–Hochberg correction for multiple testing, two-sample test for equality of proportions with continuity correction, two-proportion z-test, exact binomial test, and Kruskal–Wallis rank-sum test were conducted using built-in functions in R (R Development Core Team 2016).

Results and Discussion

We retrieved information from two different proteomic studies of seminal plasma. The first identified 879 nonredundant seminal plasma proteins obtained from three ejaculates of a single healthy donor, while the second documented 1,923 nonredundant human SFPs obtained from pooling ejaculates from different healthy individuals (Pilch and Mann 2006; Batruch et al. 2011). Among the 2,156 nonredundant lists of SFPs derived from combining both initial lists, we found a common set of 646 proteins to both studies (Fig. 1a). Due to methodological differences in the mass spectrometry experiments performed in the two studies, and the potential presence of broadly expressed housekeeping proteins that are not necessarily SFPs, for example, carry-over proteins shared among a variety of secretory pathways, we examined both the nonredundant list and the common set of proteins for characteristics expected among reputed human SFPs. Specifically, we found that the common set of 646 proteins exhibits a significant increase in the proportion of proteins associated with the functional terms extracellular region (GO:0005576) and extracellular space (GO:0005615) compared to the total 2,156 proteins, consistent with the possibility of an enrichment in the common set of soluble proteins (746/2156 or 34.6% vs 353/646 or 54.7%; two-sample test for equality of proportions with continuity correction (2TEP): χ2 = 83.52, d.f. = 1, P < 2.2 × 10−16; Fig. 1b). In addition, we delineated a reinforced subset by considering peptide sequence coverage and number of peptides identified for each protein. Specifically, we identified a list of 340 SFPs that had at least two peptides per protein in both studies (reinforced list; Fig. 1a). This reinforced list of SFPs featured an even tighter association with the mentioned cellular compartment GO terms (353/645 or 54.7% vs 219/340 or 64.4%; %; 2TEP: χ2 = 8.2, d.f. = 1, P = 4.2 × 10−03) (Fig. 1b).

We reasoned that any properly curated list of human SFPs should account for most of the protein content of the samples analyzed. Using the protein level estimates across the three seminal samples assayed by Batruch et al. 2011 (Batruch et al. 2011), we calculated the average relative abundance of each putative SFP in our common set. The proteins in the common set accounted for 86.6% of the total protein content of the samples analyzed by Batruch et al. 2011. Examining the interplay between protein abundance and the common list of SFPs revealed the presence of 367 of the 506 proteins (ie 56.8%) in the top quartile most abundant proteins. The top 10% of the most abundant proteins present in our common list, ie 64, accounting for 68.4% of the total protein content (Fig. 1c), did not differ from the content represented by the top 10% in the reinforced list (34, accounting for 63.4%) (two-proportion z-test, z = 1.58, P = 0.113). The top 10 most abundant putative human SFPs were identical across both the common and the reinforced lists. These included proteins known to be relevant for semen coagulation after ejaculation (SEMG1 and SEMG2) (Anamthathmakula and Winuthayanon 2020), structural components of the seminal coagulum (FN1) (Lilja et al. 1987), bacterial protection (LTF and PIP) (Gopalkrishnan et al. 1994; Nowicka-Bauer et al. 2023), seminal coagulum liquefaction (KLK3) (Anamthathmakula and Winuthayanon 2020), and sperm capacitation roles (ALB and NPC2) (Ozkocer and Konac 2021; Zhao et al. 2021) (Fig. 1d). Importantly, neither the common set nor the reinforced set excluded other much less abundant, but well-known, SFPs, such as prostasin (PRSS8) and the prostate stem cell antigen (PSCA) (Reiter et al. 1998; Takahashi et al. 2003). Collectively, we concluded that our filtering strategy effectively controlled for false positives while retaining reputed SFPs.

Subsequently, to date the branch of origin during the vertebrate phylogeny of our list of SFPs, we leveraged a comprehensive human gene age dating, which combines microsynteny information across 23 vertebrates within a framework of maximum parsimony, to date the origin of 19,665 protein-coding human genes across six evolutionary transitions following the Euteleostomi: Tetrapoda, Amniota, Mammalia, Theria, Eutheria, and Primate (Shao et al. 2019; Chen et al. 2025). The combination of the proteomics and gene dating datasets resulted in a catalog of 615 human SFP-encoding genes dated across seven age classes (Fig. 2a). For example, relative to the examples of known SFPs mentioned above, their respective encoding genes were originated during the evolutionary transition of the Euteleostomi (FN1, NPC2, and PRSS8), during that of the Tetrapoda (ALB), during that of the Mammalia (PIP), or during that of the Eutheria (KLK3, LTF, PSCA, SEMG1 and SEMG2). To determine whether this diversity in time of origin is representative of SFP genes more broadly, we evaluated whether they are proportionally represented across evolutionary transitions relative to the abundance of the different gene age classes in the genome, finding that this was not the case (chi-square goodness-of-fit, χ2 = 48.23, d.f. = 6, P = 1.06 × 10−08). Importantly, post hoc tests further uncovered an excess of human SFP genes that were present already in the ancestral genome of the Euteloestomi, ie branch 0, prior to 429 mya (Kumar et al. 2022), and a deficit in the three youngest evolutionary transitions (Fig. 2a). This pattern of branch of origin can be further simplified as a contrast between the branch corresponding to the Euteloestomi and older vs the rest (ie br0 vs br1-6; 2-tailed exact binomial test, observed proportion of success = 410/615 = 0.667 > expected = 0.566, with a 95% CI of 0.628 to 0.704, P = 4.16 × 10−7) (Fig. 2b).

Further, we focused on the reinforced subset of 328 human SFPs. We also found a departure from the random expectation (chi-square goodness-of-fit, χ2 = 168.52, d.f. = 6, P < 2.2 × 10−16) (Fig. 2c). Subsequent post hoc tests confirmed the enrichment for human SFPs originated in branch 0, which was further supported by the two-categories test (br0 vs br1-6; 2-tailed exact binomial test, observed proportion of success = 216/328 = 0.659 > expected = 0.566, with a 95% CI of 0.604 to 0.71, P = 6.7 × 10−4) (Fig. 2d). Post hoc tests also confirmed an underrepresentation of SFPs originated in recent evolutionary transitions, but unlike for the common list, this signal was only significant for the Primate radiation, possibly as a result of the most limited statistical power associated with the reinforced list of SFPs. Overall, our findings suggest that many of the human SFPs were already present in the ancestor to all vertebrate lineages.

One potential confounding factor to consider is a nonrandom association between SFP abundance, and therefore probability of detection in proteomic analysis, and gene age. If the most ancient SFPs were characterized by higher levels of expression compared to younger SFPs, then ancient SFPs would be more likely to be detected, thus explaining the statistical enrichment detected. To ascertain this possibility, we tested for differences in relative abundance (Batruch et al. 2011) among SFPs across different age classes. The analysis of the common set did not reveal any significant difference in relative abundance across the seven age classes (Kruskal–Wallis rank-sum test, χ2 = 5.92, d.f. = 6, P = 0.433) nor when considering only two broadly defined categories, ie br0 vs br1-6, (Kruskal–Wallis rank-sum test, χ2 = 1.33, d.f. = 1, P = 0.248). The equivalent analyses with the reinforced set did not offer a substantially different picture, with the comparison across seven age classes not entailing any significant difference either (Kruskal–Wallis rank-sum test, χ2 = 10.67, d.f. = 6, P = 0.099) and the test between the two broadly defined categories revealing significant differences in abundance but not in the direction of the proposed confounding scenario as genes in the age category br1-6 were more highly expressed than those in the br0 category (Kruskal–Wallis rank-sum test, χ2 = 4.12, d.f. = 1, P = 0.042). Collectively, these results do not support that the SFPs originated in the ancestor to all vertebrates are more abundant, being therefore more easily detectable, thus explaining their excess in relation to SFPs from younger age classes.

As gene age is associated with gene properties that reflect different functional constraints, we hypothesized that older human SFP genes should be more pleiotropic than younger SFP genes (Hao et al. 2010). We examined two different proxies of pleiotropy, breadth of tissue expression and gene ontology, thus reducing dependence on any single measure of pleiotropy. We found that ancient genes originating during the Euteleostomi radiation have a lower tissue-specificity (τ—tau index; Fig. 3) and a more diverse array of gene ontologies (Fig. 4a; Table S1), particularly in relation to biological processes and molecular functions, compared to those originated subsequently within other evolutionary transitions. These results were consistent across the list of SFPs considered. Importantly, as older genes are known to have a more diverse array of gene ontologies (Hao et al. 2010), we replicated our analyses by searching SFP enrichment patterns relative to the rest of the genes within the same age category instead of using all the genes in the genome as a background reference set (Fig. 4b; Table S2). In this second set of analyses, we found the same result, ie a broader collection of ontologies associated with ancient SFPs compared to younger SFPs (Fig. 4b). The biological process, molecular function, and cellular component enrichment patterns of the evolutionarily younger SFP gene complement are unique, showing a distinctive enrichment of proteolytic activity (eg proteasome complex and peptidase activity) and immune terms such as antibacterial humoral response (Fig. 4a and 4b). Moreover, the specific association of “specific granule lumen,” a compartment characteristic of neutrophils containing antimicrobial proteins (Okuda et al. 1981), with younger SFPs further supports an immune-related profile. The enrichment for immune-related and protease terms is likely related to inflammatory responses in mammals that may aid in fertilization (McGraw et al. 2015). Protease inhibitors, in particular, may protect sperm from proteolysis, thereby promoting immune tolerance and supporting pregnancy (Laflamme and Wolfner 2013). The younger components of the human seminal plasma proteome reflect a more recent evolutionary acquisition of genes specialized in post-mating functions, which may bias reproductive outcomes in contexts such as male-male competition.

Conclusions

Our results in humans, and previously in the protostome D. melanogaster (Ranz et al. 2025), suggest that a substantial fraction of SFP genes are not evolutionary young. Instead, our results reveal a deep evolutionary origin that spans major transitions during the evolution of the vertebrates. These findings open new avenues for exploring how ancient molecular frameworks have been used across the diversity of reproductive and mating systems seen across the vertebrate phylogeny. Particularly, it remains to be clarified whether the molecular mechanisms whereby SFPs impact reproductive functions are ancient, and therefore potentially widespread across multiple contemporary species, or have been more recently coopted, therefore being associated with specific lineages. Distinguishing between both scenarios will require extensive proteomic characterization of seminal plasma across the vertebrate phylogeny.
