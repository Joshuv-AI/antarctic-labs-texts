Improving medical students’ responses to emergencies with a simulated cross-cover paging curriculum
Authors: Lauren A. Heidemann, Samantha Kempner, Emily Kobernik, Elizabeth Jones, William J. Peterson, Brittany B. Allen, Matthew Wixson, Helen K. Morgan
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTNew residents are often unprepared to respond to medical emergencies. To address this gap, we implemented a simulated cross-cover paging curriculum. All senior medical students enrolled in a required specialty-specific (internal medicine, procedures, emergency medicine [EM], obstetrics and gynecology [OBGYN], family medicine and pediatrics) residency preparation course (RPC) in 2020–2021 participated. Students received 3–6 specialty-specific pages that represented an urgent change in clinical status about a simulated patient. For each page, students first called a standardized registered nurse (SRN) to ask additional questions, then recommended next steps in evaluation and management. The SRNs delivered immediate verbal feedback, delayed written feedback, and graded clinical performance using a weighted rubric. Some items were categorized as ‘must do,’ which represented the most clinically important actions. Trends in clinical performance over time were analyzed using the Jonckheere–Terpstra test. Of the 315 eligible students, 265 (84.1%) consented for their data to be included in the analysis. Clinical performance improved from a median (interquartile range) of 59.4% (46.9%, 75.0%) on case 1 to 80.0% (68.0%, 86.7%) on case 6 (p < .001). The percentage of ‘must do’ items improved significantly, from 69.2% (53.8, 81.8%) to 80.0% (66.7%, 88,9%) (p < .001). Scores improved over time for all specialty courses except for EM and OB/GYN. Surveyed students largely found this to be a valuable addition to the RPC curriculum with a 4.4 overall rating (1 = poor to 5 = excellent). This novel curriculum fills important gaps in the educational transition between medical school and residency. The simulated paging platform is adaptable and generalizable to learners entering different residency specialties.

Introduction

First-year residents are often the first responders for hospitalized patients with medical emergencies, yet many are unprepared to care for such patients [1]. Students may be shielded from handling these high acuity responsibilities in medical school or may simply lack experience due to the rarity of certain emergent events. Nonetheless, it is the responsibility of medical schools to ensure that graduating students are ready for day one of residency, which includes being competent and capable in the initial evaluation and management of urgent and emergent medical scenarios [2].

In the United States, the Coalition for Physician Accountability’s Undergraduate Medical Education-Graduate Medical Education Review Committee recently advised that specialty-specific ‘just-in-time’ training is necessary for all incoming first-year residents [3]. Many U.S. medical schools have implemented residency preparation courses (RPCs), also known as transition courses, to address this need [4], and our prior work has demonstrated that residents felt more prepared for residency if they participated in such courses [5]. It is critical that these transition courses teach and assess students’ ability to handle the medical emergencies they will be expected to encounter in their future practice.

We designed a simulated cross-cover paging curriculum to address this need. Cross-cover is defined as caring for hospitalized patients when the primary team is not present and is a common responsibility of residents. In this curriculum, students are the first responders to specialty-specific urgent and emergent scenarios paged by standardized registered nurses (SRNs) and receive immediate feedback from SRNs about their performance. It is ideal that SRNs are included in such scenarios given their integral role in recognizing patient decompensation and aiding with initial resuscitation measures at the bedside. A simulated paging curriculum has been shown to be effective for improving clinical decision-making in surgeons [6] but has not been studied across all specialties. We have previously shown that such a curriculum improves interprofessional communication amongst students enrolled in internal medicine [7] and pediatrics [8] RPCs.

In this study, we aimed to evaluate whether repeated exposure to simulated pages regarding significant changes in patient medical status improved learner ability to manage these scenarios. We examined two outcomes: overall clinical performance and clinical performance on essential ‘must do’ aspects of patient care. We also examined learners’ satisfaction and acceptability with this novel curriculum and assessments.

Materials and methods

Setting and participants

Participants in our study included all senior medical students enrolled in six specialty-specific required RPCs in the spring of 2020 and 2021. Courses range from four weeks (internal medicine, family medicine, pediatrics) to eight weeks (procedures, obstetrics and gynecology, emergency medicine) in duration. The University of Michigan Institutional Review Board deemed this study to be exempt (HUM00141289). Written informed consent was obtained from students for their assessment data to be used for research purposes.

Simulated paging curriculum

Two SRNs administered the simulated cross-cover paging curriculum [7]. During cross-cover scenarios, residents rely on a handoff of information about patients whom they have often never met. Our paging curriculum used simulation to introduce relatively novice learners to the critical skill of appropriate cross-coverage during urgent and emergent medical scenarios. Simulation-based medical education is a valuable tool in improving the safe delivery of medical care, particularly because it provides uniform training and allows for trainees to learn from errors without any actual patient harm [9].

Students received a one-hour orientation to the curriculum, which included expectations and review of an example case. They then received a standardized handoff of information describing hospitalized patients and were ‘on call’ to receive 3–6 specialty-specific cross-cover pages about urgent scenarios involving these patients. The number of cross-cover pages varied by specialty as certain specialties (e.g., family medicine) incorporated other cases (not included in this study) that emphasized responding to outpatient phone calls. Upon receiving the page, students called the SRN, who provided more information about the patient using a standardized script. Students could ask for additional history and physical examination findings, as well as order diagnostic testing (some results are available immediately) and treatments. Students were instructed to close the communication loop and indicate they would notify their senior resident if warranted.

Clinical performance evaluation

SRNs graded clinical performance using a pre-defined weighted electronic rubric for each case consisting of ‘must do’ (+2 points); ‘should do’ (+1 point); ‘could do’ (0 points); ‘should not do’ (−1 point); or ‘must not do’ (−2 points) diagnostic, therapeutic, and communication items. SRNs received training in both clinical content and the provision of formative feedback, thereby helping to assure uniformity in their communication and assessment across cases. The rubric format has been previously described [6]. Each rubric was based on clinical guidelines and reviewed by at least 2–3 experts to establish consensus. ‘must do’ items represent the most critical items that all new interns must perform and were determined by the course directors. Feedback

Immediately after the phone call, the SRNs delivered verbal feedback to the students about the rubric items. Providing real-time feedback to our learners is based on Ericsson’s conceptual framework of deliberate practice for learning [10]. Active engagement in deliberate practice allowed for immediate incorporation of certain critical tasks prior to subsequent pages, which provided opportunity for repeated performance to refine these skills. SRNs also emailed written feedback electronically within several days, describing strengths and weaknesses of the student’s clinical management and communication. For two cases, the students did not receive immediate verbal feedback and were instead expected to document a note. At regular intervals, a faculty member reviewed the cases with the class in depth, illustrated the gold standard approach, and answered questions.

Validity

The rubrics were based on current guidelines and reviewed by several content experts, representing content validity. Additionally, the structure of this curriculum was realistic and authentic, representing what participants will encounter as interns – being interrupted in the middle of the day or evening via page to discuss an urgent matter with a registered nurse about a hospitalized patient for whom they have little information. The similarity of this construct to the responsibilities during intern year represents response process validity.

Satisfaction and acceptability evaluation

In 2021, we asked 164 students to rate the overall quality of the paging curriculum on a 5-point scale (1 = poor, 2 = fair, 3 = good, 4 = very good, 5 = excellent) and if they recommend that the paging curriculum should remain part of the RPC in the future (1 = strongly disagree, 2 = disagree, 3 = neutral, 4 = agree, 5 = strongly agree).

Analysis

We analyzed all variables using descriptive statistics such as median and proportions, where appropriate. After inspecting skewness and kurtosis, and the Shapiro–Wilk test, clinical performance scores were determined to be non-normally distributed and thus expressed using median and interquartile range (IQR). We performed a test for linear trends (Jonckheere–Terpstra test), a non-parametric test to examine the relationship between clinical performance score and case order (case number) for all RPC specialties. We performed all data management and statistical analyses using SAS version 9.4 (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, N.C.) and interpreted statistical significance using 95% confidence intervals.

In terms of satisfaction, 87 of 164 students (53.0%) evaluated the paging curriculum, rating its quality as 4.38 (SD 0.78) and recommending it remain part of the RPCs as 4.63 (SD 0.65).

Discussion

In this single-center study, we designed a novel simulated paging curriculum that aimed to prepare senior medical students to handle urgent medical emergencies on cross-cover patients. Our data is consistent with prior data from curricula in the surgical literature that show simulated paging can help prepare students for internship [6]. Our study is novel in that it focused specifically on urgent and emergent inpatient cross-cover conditions and encompassed multiple specialties.

It is interesting that the ability to handle medical emergencies improves despite the variability of case content. Some items are common to most grading rubrics, such as asking for vital signs, closing the communication loop, and indicating they would notify their senior resident. It is possible that students improved due to improvement in these specific items. It is also possible that students felt more comfortable making medical decisions about urgent scenarios as they received more opportunities to do so along with regular feedback. While it is impossible to know the exact impact on clinical practice, we believe that completion of this curriculum leads to meaningful differences in patient care given that the rubrics are intended to capture essential immediate actions, especially those ‘must do’ items.

It is unclear why all courses except obstetrics and gynecology and emergency medicine showed improvement. It is possible that the case difficulty increased over time. It is also possible that both courses were underpowered given the small number of participants. Additionally, students in the obstetrics and gynecology course had a relatively high median score on the first case, but in 2021, they only received three inpatient pages, which may have limited their opportunities to improve, especially given that they did not receive immediate feedback from SRNs on cases that required documentation. It is also possible that there are decision-making processes inherent to these specialties that are not fully captured on the rubrics.

The role of the registered nurse as assessor and provider of feedback during the crucial time prior to a student’s transition to residency is a novel and noteworthy aspect of our curriculum. Prior research has shown miscommunication with nurses to be a root cause for patient safety events [11], yet nurses are rarely formally integrated into medical student education. Their central role in this curriculum that improved medical students’ clinical performance over time is important evidence that should inform medical educators as they strive to create meaningful interprofessional curricula, especially in the transition to residency space. Having consistently well-trained SRNs has been an important lesson learned over the years of iterating this curriculum. In prior years, we tried to administer this with volunteer RNs, but the curriculum was non-uniformly distributed and the assessment data was less robust. Although financial resources are needed for this investment, we have found that having a smaller cohort of trained RNs improves the efficiency and reliability of the assessment data collection.

Limitations include that this was a single-center study, which may limit the generalizability of these findings to other student populations or institutions. Additionally, the cases were different throughout the RPCs and case order varied between years, limiting our ability to interpret the impact of individual cases. We did not account for case difficulty due to challenges determining how to objectively define a ‘more difficult’ or ‘less difficult’ case – especially given that this varies by students’ prior experience in clinical rotations. Lastly, while simulation has demonstrated efficacy in enhancing performance during these emergency situations, it is imperative to juxtapose this strategy with alternative strategies, such as immersive clinical rotations, to ascertain the most effective preparing model for students.

Future directions include expanding this curriculum to other institutions and gathering additional validity evidence such as comparisons with other performance data (e.g., board scores or residency performance evaluations) as well as intern performance data and patient safety indicators. In the future, this data may be valuable to feed forward to residency programs [12] to serve as a baseline performance metric and inform intern goal-setting. Given the nature of this simulated curriculum, it is feasible to expand it to other institutions and areas of health professions education, while keeping in mind that heterogeneity in RPCS may influence consistent implementation. This curriculum can easily be performed in the virtual setting given that the phone is the primary mode of communication and that all rubrics are submitted electronically, improving potential transferability, however does involve significant financial resources. Other institutions could consider the use of nursing students to administer this curriculum, as this would more truly provide an interprofessional education experience and could help offset some of the financial considerations.

In conclusion, simulated paging is an effective means of assessing and improving clinical decision-making in the context of medical emergencies. This curriculum allows senior medical students to practice making high-stakes decisions prior to doing so in the clinical setting and is ideally implemented during transition to residency training.
