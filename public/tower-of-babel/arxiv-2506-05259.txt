Learning long range dependencies through time reversal symmetry breaking
Guillaume Pourcel, Maxence Ernoult

Abstract
Deep State Space Models (SSMs) reignite physics-grounded compute paradigms, as RNNs could natively be embodied into dynamical systems. This calls for dedicated learning algorithms obeying to core physical principles, with efficient techniques to simulate these systems and guide their design. We propose Recurrent Hamiltonian Echo Learning (RHEL), an algorithm which provably computes loss gradients as finite differences of physical trajectories of non-dissipative, Hamiltonian systems . In ML terms, RHEL only requires three “forward passes” irrespective of model size, without explicit Jacobian computation, nor incurring any variance in the gradient estimation. Motivated by the physical realization of our algorithm, we first introduce RHEL in continuous time and demonstrate its formal equivalence with the continuous adjoint state method. To facilitate the simulation of Hamiltonian systems trained by RHEL, we propose a discrete-time version of RHEL which is equivalent to Backpropagation Through Time (BPTT) when applied to a class of recurrent modules which we call Hamiltonian Recurrent Units (HRUs). This setting allows us to demonstrate the scalability of RHEL by generalizing these results to hierarchies of HRUs, which we call Hamiltonian SSMs (HSSMs). We apply RHEL to train HSSMs with linear and nonlinear dynamics on a variety of time-series tasks ranging from mid-range to long-range classification and regression with sequence length reaching ∼ 50 ​ k \sim 50k . We show that RHEL consistently matches the performance of BPTT across all models and tasks. This work opens new doors for the design of scalable, energy-efficient physical systems endowed with self-learning capabilities for sequence modelling.

in

Taking inspiration from HEB and using standard ML tools, we propose

With the primary goal in mind to inform the design of self-learning physical systems and to facilitate its comparison to HEB, we first introduce RHEL in

Problem statement

2.1
Notations & model description.

Notations.

Given a differentiable mapping
H:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
, we denote
\nabla_{\bm{v}_{1}}H\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\bm{v}_{1}}\times 1}
and
\nabla_{\bm{v}_{1},\bm{v}_{2}}^{2}H\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\bm{v}_{1}}\times d_{\bm{v}_{2}}}
the gradient and Hessian of
H
respectively. When necessary, we use the Leibniz notation to denote as
\nabla_{i}H
the gradient of
H
with respect to the
i^{\rm th}
variable. When considering a differentiable mapping
\mathcal{M}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
, we denote its Jacobian matrix as
\bm{\partial}\mathcal{M}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d}
. We also use the notation
d_{\bm{v}_{1}}
to denote a
total
derivative with respect to some variable
\bm{v}_{1}
which accounts for both direct and
indirect
effects of the variable
\bm{v}_{1}
on the function being differentiated.

Continuous Hamiltonian model.

Following
[
10
]
, we model neurons as a vector
\bm{\Phi}\in\mathcal{S}\equiv\mathbb{R}^{d_{\bm{\Phi}}}
comprising a position vector
\bm{\phi}\in\mathbb{R}^{\frac{d_{\bm{\Phi}}}{2}}
and a momentum vector
\bm{\pi}\in\mathbb{R}^{\frac{d_{\bm{\Phi}}}{2}}
such that
\bm{\Phi}:=(\bm{\phi}^{\top},\bm{\pi}^{\top})^{\top}
. We define
\bm{\Sigma}_{z}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\bm{\Phi}}\times d_{\bm{\Phi}}}
such that
\bm{\Sigma}_{z}\cdot\bm{\Phi}=(\bm{\phi},-\bm{\pi})
and
\bm{\Sigma}_{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\bm{\Phi}}\times d_{\bm{\Phi}}}
such that
\bm{\Sigma}_{x}\cdot\bm{\Phi}=(\bm{\pi},\bm{\phi})
. Denoting
\bm{\theta}\in\Theta\equiv\mathbb{R}^{d_{\bm{\theta}}}
and
\bm{u}\in\mathcal{U}\equiv\mathbb{R}^{d_{\bm{u}}}
the model parameters and inputs, we define the
Hamiltonian
associated to the model as a mapping
H:\mathcal{S}\times\Theta\times\mathcal{U}\to\mathbb{R}
. Taking
-T
as the origin of time and starting from
\bm{\Phi}(-T)=\bm{x}
, we say that
\bm{\Phi}
follows
Hamiltonian dynamics
under a sequence of inputs
t\to\bm{u}(t)
and with some parameters
\bm{\theta}
if it satifies the ordinary differential equation (ODE):

\forall t\in[-T,0]:\ \partial_{t}\bm{\Phi}(t)=\bm{J}\cdot\nabla_{\bm{\Phi}}H[\bm{\Phi}(t),\bm{\theta},\bm{u}(t)],\quad\bm{J}:=\begin{bmatrix}\bm{0}&\bm{I}\\
-\bm{I}&\bm{0}\end{bmatrix}.

(1)

Assumptions.

The most fundamental requirement of the whole proposed approach for the Hamiltonian models at use is
time-reversal symmetry
. Heuristically, if we were recording the dynamics of a conversative system described by Eq. (
1
) and playing the resulting recording forward and backward, we could not distinguish them as both are
physically feasible
(Lemma
A.3
). A direct consequence of this fact is that if neurons are let to evolve for some time and then bounced back,
i.e.
their momenta are reversed, they will
exactly
travel back to their initial state (Corollary
A.3
). Namely (see Fig.
1
):

\left(\bm{\Phi}(-T)\underset{\mbox{Eq.~(\ref{def:hamiltonian-flow})}}{\longrightarrow}\bm{\Phi}(0)\right)\ \Rightarrow\ \left(\bm{\Phi}^{\star}(0):=\bm{\Sigma}_{z}\cdot\bm{\Phi}(0)\underset{\mbox{Eq.~(\ref{def:hamiltonian-flow})}}{\longrightarrow}\bm{\Phi}^{\star}(-T)\right)

(2)

2.2
Learning as constrained optimization

Problem formulation.

Our goal can be framed as a constrained optimization problem where we aim to find a set of model parameters
\bm{\theta}
such that, under some input sequence
t\to u(t)
, the model trajectory approaches as much as possible some target trajectory, as measured by a
loss function

L
, under the constraint that the model trajectory is
physically feasible
in the sense of satifying Eq. (
1
):

\min_{\bm{\theta}}L:=\int_{-T}^{0}dt\ell[\bm{\Phi}(t),t]\quad\mbox{s.t.}\quad\forall t\in[-T,0]:\ \partial_{t}\bm{\Phi}(t)=\bm{J}\cdot\nabla_{\bm{\Phi}}H[\bm{\Phi}(t),\bm{\theta},\bm{u}(t)],

(3)

where
L
reads as the sum of
cost functions

t\to\ell(\cdot,t)
. The most common approach to solve Eq. (
3
) is by gradient descent or variants thereof such that the problem boils down to computing
d_{\bm{\theta}}L
. Note that the problem defined by Eq. (
3
) is more general than the one solved by the seminal HEB work
[
10
]
as: i) the loss function
L
is defined over the
whole
trajectory, ii) the Hamiltonian is
time-dependent
(through
t\to\bm{u}(t)
) and parametrized by
\bm{\theta}
which is shared across the whole computational graph.

Algorithmic baseline.

One standard approach to compute
d_{\bm{\theta}}L
is the
continuous-adjoint state method
(ASM)
[
28
]
, which can be simply regarded as the continuous counterpart of backward-mode AD. Given some
forward
trajectory
\{\bm{\Phi}(t)\}_{t\in[-T,0]}
spanned by Eq. (
1
), this method prescribes solving the following
backward
ODE:

\bm{\lambda}(0)=0,\quad\partial_{t}\bm{\lambda}(t)=\nabla^{2}_{1}H[\bm{\Phi}(-t),\bm{\theta},\bm{u}(-t)]\cdot\bm{J}^{\top}\cdot\bm{\lambda}(t)+\nabla_{1}\ell[\bm{\Phi}(-t),-t],

(4)

with the gradient of the loss with respect to the initial state of the neurons
\bm{x}
and the model parameters
\bm{\theta}
given by (Theorem
A.1
):

d_{\bm{x}}L=\bm{\lambda}(T),\quad d_{\bm{\theta}}L=\int_{0}^{T}g_{\bm{\theta}}(t)dt,\quad\mbox{with}\quad g_{\bm{\theta}}(t):=\nabla^{2}_{1,2}H[\bm{\Phi}(-t),\bm{\theta},\bm{u}(-t)]\cdot A^{\top}\cdot\bm{\lambda}(t)

(5)

Note that in theory, the forward trajectory
\{\bm{\Phi}(t)\}_{t\in[-T,0]}
can either be stored or recomputed backwards alongside
\bm{\lambda}
yielding
\mathcal{O}(1)
memory cost – a property which holds beyond Eq. (
1
) specifically
[
31
]
. However in practice, recomputing variables backward through a
discrete
computational graph is generally inexact and induces bias in the gradient estimation
[
32
]
, unless a
reversible
discretization scheme is used. Fortunately,
symplectic
integrators associated with Hamiltonian flows are reversible
[
33
]
, a property which has been leveraged to yield memory savings in neural networks
[
30
]
. Therefore, the continuous ASM
as well as
our proposed algorithm (Alg.
1
) also naturally inherit this memory efficiency as a
model feature
rather than a feature of the training algorithms themselves.

3
Recurrent Hamiltonian Echo Learning (RHEL)

3.1
Definition in continuous time & equivalence with continuous ASM

We are now equipped to introduce
Recurrent Hamiltonian Echo Learning
(RHEL). In comparison to the continuous ASM, RHEL does not require solving a separate adjoint ODE akin to Eq. (
4
). Instead, RHEL simply prescribes running multiple additional times the forward trajectory Eq. (
1
) with three modifications: i) the neurons are first
conjugated
,
i.e.

\bm{\Phi}\to\bm{\Phi}^{\star}
; ii) the inputs
t\to\bm{u}(-t)
are processed
backwards
; iii) the trajectory of the neurons is slightly nudged, with a strength
\epsilon
, towards direction of decreasing loss values with cost functions
t\to\ell[\cdot,-t]
processed
backwards
too. More precisely, let us assume a “free” forward trajectory Eq. (
1
) has been executed between
-T
and
0
yielding some neuron state
\bm{\Phi}(0)
. Then, we define the
echo
dynamics of the neurons
\bm{\Phi}^{e}
through:

\bm{\Phi}^{e}(0)=\bm{\Phi}^{\star}(0),\quad\partial_{t}\bm{\Phi}^{e}(t,\epsilon)=\bm{J}\nabla_{\bm{\Phi}^{e}}H[\bm{\Phi}^{e}(t,\epsilon),\bm{\theta},\bm{u}(-t)]-\epsilon\bm{J}\nabla_{\bm{\Phi}^{e}}\ell[\bm{\Phi}^{e}(t,\epsilon),-t]\quad\forall t\in[0,T]

(6)

A crucial observation is that when
\epsilon=0
, the echo trajectory simply matches the forward trajectory in reverse:
\bm{\Phi}^{e}(t,\epsilon=0)=\bm{\Phi}^{\star}(-t)
for
t\in[0,T]
(Lemma
A.3
). When
\epsilon\neq 0
, the resulting trajectory difference
\bm{\Phi}^{e}(t,\epsilon)-\bm{\Phi}^{e}(t,\epsilon=0)
implicitly encodes for the error signals carried by the continuous ASM. We introduce this result more formally in Theorem
3.1
with the help of the following quantities capturing the essence of RHEL as a
forward-only
, difference-based gradient estimator:

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\Delta^{\rm RHEL}_{\bm{\theta}}(t,\epsilon)&:=-\frac{1}{2\epsilon}\left(\nabla_{2}H[\bm{\Phi}^{e}(t,\epsilon),\bm{\theta},\bm{u}(-t)]-\nabla_{2}H[\bm{\Phi}^{e}(t,-\epsilon),\bm{\theta},\bm{u}(-t)]\right),\\
\Delta^{\rm RHEL}_{\bm{\Phi}}(t,\epsilon)&:=\frac{1}{2\epsilon}\bm{\Sigma}_{x}\cdot\left(\bm{\Phi}^{e}(t,\epsilon)-\bm{\Phi}^{e}(t,-\epsilon)\right),\end{array}\right.

Theorem 3.1

(Informal)
.

Under mild assumptions on the Hamiltonian function and with
t\to\lambda(t)
and
t\to g_{\bm{\theta}}(t)
defined by Eq. (
4
)–(
5
), the continuous ASM and RHEL are equivalent at all times in the sense that:

\forall t\in[0,T],\quad\bm{\lambda}(t)=\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}\Delta^{\rm RHEL}_{\bm{\Phi}}(t,\epsilon),\quad g_{\bm{\theta}}(t)=\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}\Delta^{\rm RHEL}_{\bm{\theta}}(t,\epsilon)

Proof sketch.

Defining
\Delta_{\bm{\Phi}}^{\rm RHEL}(t):=\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}\Delta_{\bm{\Phi}}^{\rm RHEL}(t,\epsilon)
and noticing that
\Delta_{\bm{\Phi}}^{\rm RHEL}(t)=\bm{\Sigma}_{x}\cdot\partial_{\epsilon}(\bm{\Phi}^{e}(t,\epsilon))|_{\epsilon=0}
, we can show by differentiating Eq. (
6
) around
\epsilon=0
and with some algebra that
t\to\Delta_{\bm{\Phi}}^{\rm RHEL}(t)
satisfies the same ODE as
t\to\bm{\lambda}(t)
(Eq. (
4
) and have same initial conditions, therefore are equal at all times. The other two equalities of Theorem
3.1
can then be easily deduced.
∎

Example.

We numerically Theorem
3.1
on a toy model of six coupled harmonic oscillators
i
(Fig.
2
) with mass
m_{i}
and spring parameters
k_{i},k_{ij}
. This system is described by the Hamiltonian
H[\bm{\Phi},\bm{\theta},\bm{u}]=\sum_{i}\frac{\pi_{i}^{2}}{2m_{i}}+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i}k_{i}\bm{\phi}_{i}^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i}\sum_{j,j>i}k_{ij}(\bm{\phi}_{j}-\bm{\phi}_{i})^{2}
with
\bm{\theta}=\{m_{i},k_{i},k_{ij}\}_{i,j}
that leads to the dynamic and associated gradient estimators (see App.
A.3.1
):

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\forall i\in\llbracket 1,d_{\bm{\Phi}}\rrbracket\qquad\partial_{t}\phi_{i}=\pi_{i}/m_{i},\quad\partial_{t}\pi_{i}=-k_{i}\phi_{i}+\sum_{j,j\neq i}k_{ij}(\phi_{j}-\phi_{i})\\
g_{k_{ij}}(t)=\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}\frac{-1}{2\epsilon}\left(\left(\phi_{j}^{e}(t,\epsilon)-\phi_{i}^{e}(t,\epsilon)\right)^{2}-\left(\phi_{j}^{e}(t,-\epsilon)-\phi_{i}^{e}(t,-\epsilon)\right)^{2}\right)\end{array}\right.

3.2
Extension to discrete time & equivalence with BPTT

In this section, we propose a
discrete-time
version of RHEL by first defining a family of discrete models as integrators of the Hamiltonian flow which
exactly
preserve time-reversal symmetry, defining an associated surrogate problem and
then
solving it. This “discretize-then-optimize” approach ensures a better gradient estimation than directly discretizing RHEL theory in continuous time
[
32
]
.

Hamiltonian Recurrent Units (HRUs).

Figure 3:
Computational graph of
\mathcal{M}_{H,\delta}
.

We now assume that the neurons
\bm{\Phi}
obey the following discrete-time dynamics, starting from
\bm{\Phi}_{-K}=\bm{x}
and:

\bm{\Phi}_{k+1}=\mathcal{M}_{H,\delta}[\bm{\Phi}_{k},\bm{\theta},\bm{u}_{k}]\quad\forall k=-K\cdots-1,

(11)

where
\mathcal{M}_{H,\delta}
denotes the
integrator
associated with the Hamiltonian function
H
with time discretization
\delta
. We classically pick
\mathcal{M}_{H,\delta}
as the
Leapfrog
integrator
[
33
]
and restrict ourselves to
separable
Hamiltonians of the form
H[\bm{\Phi}]=T[\bm{\pi}]+V[\bm{\phi}]
to yield a reversible
and
explicit integration scheme. One possible parametrization of the Leapfrog integrator in this case is as the composition of
three explicit Euler integrators
, which alternate between updates of the position
\bm{\phi}
(first and third steps) and of the momentum (second step) – see Def.
A.3
for a detailed description and Fig.
3
for the associated computational graph. As these two intermediate integrator time steps are needed to accurately define the RHEL learning rule in discrete time, we explicitly denote them as fractional time units:

\mathcal{M}_{H,\delta}:\quad\bm{\Phi}_{k}\longrightarrow\bm{\Phi}_{k+1/3}\longrightarrow\bm{\Phi}_{k+2/3}\longrightarrow\bm{\Phi}_{k+1}

(12)

We call such models
Hamiltonian Recurrent Units
(HRUs). Therefore by design, the time-reversal symmetry property defined in Eq. (
2
) extends in discrete time to HRUs (Corollary
A.6
).

Surrogate learning problem.

Given this modelling choice, the continuous-time problem introduced in Eq. (
3
) naturally translates here in discrete time as:

\min_{\bm{\theta}}L:=\sum_{k=-K+1}^{0}\ell[\bm{\Phi}_{k},k]\quad\mbox{s.t.}\quad\bm{\Phi}_{k+1}=\mathcal{M}_{H,\delta}[\bm{\Phi}_{k},\bm{\theta},\bm{u}_{k}]\quad\forall k=-K\cdots-1

(13)

Algorithmic baseline.

Eq. (
13
) defines a classical RNN learning problem where
Backpropagation Through Time
(BPTT),
i.e.
the instantiation of backward-mode AD in this context, is a natural algorithmic baseline. BPTT simply amounts to apply the “chain rule” backward through the computational graph spanned by the forward pass of a HRU (Eq. (
11
)). Alternatively, it can also be regarded as the discrete counterpart of the continuous ASM previously introduced (Theorem
A.3
). For this reason, we re-use the same notations as above and define the following quantities associated to BPTT:

d_{\bm{\theta}}L=\sum_{k=0}^{K-1}g_{\bm{\theta}}(k),\quad g_{\bm{\theta}}(k):=d_{\bm{\theta}_{k}}L,\quad g_{\bm{u}}(k):=d_{\bm{u}_{-k}}L,\quad\bm{\lambda}_{k}:=d_{\bm{\Phi}_{-k}}L,

(14)

where
g_{\bm{\theta}}(k)
,
g_{\bm{u}}(k)
and
\bm{\lambda}_{k}
denote the “sensitivity” of the loss
L
to
\bm{\theta}
at time step
k
,
\bm{u}_{-k}
and
\bm{\Phi}_{-k}
respectively – see App.
A.2.2
for a more detailed definition and derivation of BPTT.

RHEL in discrete time.

Finally, we extend RHEL in discrete-time by defining the echo dynamics on HRUs as:

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\bm{\Phi}^{e}_{0}(\epsilon)=\bm{\Phi}^{\star}_{0}+\epsilon\bm{\Sigma}_{x}\cdot\nabla_{\bm{\Phi}}\ell[\bm{\Phi}_{0},0],\\
\bm{\Phi}^{e}_{k+1}(\epsilon)=\mathcal{M}_{H,\delta}[\bm{\Phi}^{e}_{k}(\epsilon),\bm{\theta},\bm{u}_{-(k+1)}]-\epsilon\bm{J}\cdot\nabla_{\bm{\Phi}^{e}}\ell[\bm{\Phi}^{e}_{k+1},-(k+1)]\quad\forall k=0,\cdots,K-1\end{array}\right.

Denoting:

H^{1/2}[\bm{\Phi}^{e}_{k}(\epsilon),\bm{\theta},\bm{u}_{-(k+1)}]:=\frac{1}{2}\left(H[\bm{\Phi}^{e}_{k+1/3}(\epsilon),\bm{\theta},\bm{u}_{-(k+1)}]+H[\bm{\Phi}^{e}_{k+2/3}(\epsilon),\bm{\theta},\bm{u}_{-(k+1)}]\right),

(17)

we can define the discrete-time counterpart of Eq. (
3.1
) as:

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\Delta_{\bm{\theta}}^{\rm RHEL}(k,\epsilon)&:=-\frac{\delta}{2\epsilon}\left(\nabla_{2}H^{1/2}[\bm{\Phi}^{e}_{k}(\epsilon),\bm{\theta},\bm{u}_{-(k+1)}]-\nabla_{2}H^{1/2}[\bm{\Phi}^{e}_{k}(-\epsilon),\bm{\theta},\bm{u}_{-(k+1)}]\right)\\
\Delta^{\rm RHEL}_{\bm{u}}(k,\epsilon)&:=-\frac{\delta}{2\epsilon}\left(\nabla_{3}H^{1/2}[\bm{\Phi}^{e}_{k}(\epsilon),\bm{\theta},\bm{u}_{-(k+1)}]-\nabla_{3}H^{1/2}[\bm{\Phi}^{e}_{k}(-\epsilon),\bm{\theta},\bm{u}_{-(k+1)}]\right)\\

Experiments

Foreword.

We first introduce the two types HRUs at use, empirically assess the validity of Theorem
3.3
by statically comparing gradients computed by RHEL and BPTT on HSSMs made up of these two types of HRUs, then perform training experiments across classification and regression sequence tasks. Importantly, the goal of the proposed experiments is
not
to improve the SOTA performance on these tasks. Rather we want to show, on a
given
model satisfying the requirements of RHEL (
i.e.
being a HRU or a stack thereof), that RHEL maintains training performance
with respect to BPTT
.

Linear HRU block.

Following recent work
[
29
]
, we introduce the
linear
HRU block with the parametrization

\bm{\theta}^{(\ell)}=\{\bm{A}^{(\ell)},\bm{B}^{(\ell)},\bm{W}^{(\ell)}\}
and Hamiltonian:

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}H^{(\ell)}[\bm{\Phi}^{(\ell)},\bm{\theta}^{(\ell)},\bm{\Phi}^{(\ell-1)}]=\frac{1}{2}\|\bm{\pi}^{(\ell)}\|^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\bm{\phi}^{(\ell)^{\top}}\cdot\bm{A}^{(\ell)}\cdot\bm{\phi}^{(\ell)}-\bm{\phi}^{(\ell)^{\top}}\cdot\bm{B}^{(\ell)}\cdot\bm{u}^{(\ell)},\\
\bm{u}^{(\ell)}=F[\bm{\phi}^{(\ell-1)},\bm{W}^{(\ell)}],\end{array}\right.

where
\bm{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{(d_{\bm{\Phi}}/2)\times(d_{\bm{\Phi}}/2)}
is assumed to be
diagonal
and to have positive entries,
\bm{B}^{(\ell)}\in\mathbb{R}^{(d_{\bm{\Phi}}/2)\times d_{u}}
,
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
, and
F
is the nonlinear spatial building block parametrized by
\bm{W}^{(\ell)}
(see App.
A.3.3
). Under these assumptions, it can be shown that such HRUs have a bounded spectrum and yield HSSMs which are universal approximators of continuous and causal operators between time-series

To demonstrate the validity of our approach beyond linear HRUs, we also introduce a

\quad+\left(\bm{A}^{(\ell)}\right)^{-\top}\cdot\mathbf{log}\left(\mathbf{cosh}\left(\bm{A}^{(\ell)}\cdot\bm{\phi}^{(\ell)}+\bm{B}^{(\ell)}\cdot\bm{u}^{(\ell)}+\bm{b}^{(\ell)}\right)\right),\\

Limitations.

We observe on Tables
1
–
2
that RHEL slightly underperforms BPTT, though this gap is statistically significant only for nonlinear HSSMs on regression tasks. This performance difference can be attributed to: i) the approximation bias introduced by finite nudging in our method, a known issue in related works
[
37
]
, and ii) numerical precision that requires some careful selection of the nudging strength (see App.
A.4.4
).

Related work.

On top of its direct connection with HEB
[
10
]
, RHEL belongs to a large body Hamiltonian-based ML algorithms dedicated to physics-informed neural networks
[
38
,
39
,
40
]
, memory-efficient models
[
30
]
, generative models
[
41
]
, sampling
[
42
,
43
,
44
]
and optimization
[
45
]
algorithms. RHEL also extends the design of scalable self-learning machines beyond
dissipative
systems
emulating
implicit differentiation

[
46
,
47
,
48
,
49
,
50
]
. In comparison to these algorithms, RHEL crucially avoids prohibitively long simulation times due to the use of lengthy root-finding algorithms, extends their core mechanics to the broader domain of sequence modelling and possibly larger-scale tasks.

Future work.

Yet, the restriction of RHEL to non-dissipative systems limits model expressivity
[
29
]
. While inducing artificial dissipation through the use dissipative integrators
[
29
]
or by embedding a dissipative system within a larger conservative one
[
10
]
are convenient workarounds, there is ultimately a need for a temporal credit assignment algorithm tailored for
truly
dissipative systems. Additionally and in the spirit of HEB, an idealized version of RHEL should be entirely “black-box” as it still requires explicit Hamiltonian parameter gradients to implement its learning rule (Alg.
1
). This could possibly be achieved for instance using homeostatic control
via
control knobs acting on the model parameters
[
51
]
. Another exciting direction of work is to have RHEL operate
online
– it currently requires one forward pass and subsequent ones revisiting the inputs in reverse order. Finally, while HRU hidden units can be recomputed from their final state
[
30
]
, it still requires storing the input sequence of the HRU so that memory gains are not evident within a stack of HRUs. Looking beyond analog physical systems, endowing RHEL with better memory efficiency and online mechanics could potentially yield a compelling alternative to BPTT on
digital
accelerators like GPUs
[
14
]
.

Conclusion.

While we
simulated
Hamiltonian dynamics alongside RHEL on GPUs, the greatest potential of RHEL lies within
real
Hamiltonian systems, namely on-chip photonic circuits
[
52
]
, superconducting circuits
[
53
]
, or spintronic systems
[
54
]
.
We hope this work will incentivize the search for alternative analog hardware capable of training sequence models with much higher energy efficiency, as well as alternative algorithms for digital hardware.

\displaystyle+\int_{-T}^{0}dt\left[\partial_{\bm{\theta}}\bm{\Phi}(t,\bm{\theta})^{\top}\cdot\nabla^{2}_{\bm{\Phi}}H[\bm{\Phi}(t,\bm{\theta}),\bm{\theta},\bm{u}(t)]\cdot\bm{J}^{\top}+\nabla^{2}_{\bm{\theta},\bm{\Phi}}H[\bm{\Phi}(t,\bm{\theta}),\bm{\theta},\bm{u}(t)]^{\top}\cdot\bm{J}^{\top}-\partial^{2}_{\bm{\theta},t}\bm{\Phi}(t,\bm{\theta})^{\top}\right]\cdot\bm{\lambda}(t).

We can transform the last term of the integrand with
\partial_{\bm{\theta},t}\bm{\Phi}(t,\bm{\theta})
by applying Schwartz’s theorem and and integration by parts as:

\displaystyle-\int_{-T}^{0}dt\partial_{\bm{\theta},t}\bm{\Phi}(t,\bm{\theta})^{\top}\cdot\bm{\lambda}(t)

\displaystyle=-\int_{-T}^{0}dt\partial_{t,\bm{\theta}}\bm{\Phi}(t,\bm{\theta})^{\top}\cdot\bm{\lambda}(t)

\displaystyle=\left[-\partial_{\bm{\theta}}\bm{\Phi}(t,\bm{\theta})^{\top}\cdot\bm{\lambda}(t)\right]_{-T}^{0}+\int_{-T}^{0}dt\partial_{\bm{\theta}}\bm{\Phi}(t,\bm{\theta})^{\top}\cdot\partial_{t}\bm{\lambda}(t)

\displaystyle=-\partial_{\bm{\theta}}\bm{\Phi}(0,\bm{\theta})^{\top}\cdot\bm{\lambda}(0)+\int_{-T}^{0}dt\partial_{\bm{\theta}}\bm{\Phi}(t,\bm{\theta})^{\top}\cdot\partial_{t}\bm{\lambda}(t)

where the contribution of the first term at
t=-T
vanishes because:

\bm{\Phi}(-T,\bm{\theta})=\bm{x}\Rightarrow\partial_{\bm{\theta}}\bm{\Phi}(0,\bm{\theta})=0

Plugging this back into
d_{\bm{\theta}}\mathcal{L}
yields:

\displaystyle d_{\bm{\theta}}\mathcal{L}

\displaystyle=-\partial_{\bm{\theta}}\bm{\Phi}(0,\bm{\theta})^{\top}\cdot\bm{\lambda}(0)

\displaystyle+\int_{-T}^{0}dt\partial_{\bm{\theta}}\bm{\Phi}(t,\bm{x},\bm{\theta})^{\top}\cdot\left[\nabla_{\bm{\Phi}}\ell[t,\bm{\Phi}(t,\bm{\theta}),\bm{\theta}]+\nabla^{2}_{\bm{\Phi}}H[\bm{\Phi}(t,\bm{x},\bm{\theta}),\bm{\theta}]\cdot\bm{J}^{\top}\cdot\bm{\lambda}(t)+\partial_{t}\bm{\lambda}(t)\right]

\displaystyle+\int_{-T}^{0}dt\left(\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}\ell[t,\bm{\Phi}(t,\bm{\theta}),\bm{\theta}]+\nabla^{2}_{\bm{\Phi},\bm{\theta}}H[\bm{\Phi}(t,\bm{x},\bm{\theta}),\bm{\theta}]\cdot\bm{J}^{\top}\cdot\bm{\lambda}(t)\right)

Denoting
\bm{\Phi}_{*}
the solution of the (primal) ODE and by defining
\bm{\lambda}_{*}
as the solution of the adjoint ODE:

\partial_{t}\bm{\lambda}_{*}(t)=-\nabla^{2}_{\bm{\Phi}}H[\bm{\Phi}_{*}(t,\bm{\theta}),\bm{\theta},\bm{u}(t)]\cdot\bm{J}^{\top}\cdot\bm{\lambda}_{*}(t)-\nabla_{\bm{\Phi}}\ell[t,\bm{\Phi}_{*}(t,\bm{\theta}),\bm{\theta}],\quad\bm{\lambda}_{*}(0)=0

we have:

\displaystyle d_{\bm{\theta}}L

\displaystyle:=d_{\bm{\theta}}\int_{-T}^{0}dt\ell[t,\bm{\Phi}(t,\bm{\theta}),\bm{\theta}]

\displaystyle=d_{\bm{\theta}}\mathcal{L}(\bm{\Phi}_{*},\bm{\lambda}_{*},\bm{\theta})

\displaystyle=\int_{-T}^{0}dt\left(\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}\ell[t,\bm{\Phi}_{*}(t,\bm{\theta}),\bm{\theta}]+\nabla^{2}_{\bm{\Phi},\bm{\theta}}H[\bm{\Phi}_{*}(t,\bm{x},\bm{\theta}),\bm{\theta}]\cdot\bm{J}^{\top}\cdot\bm{\lambda}_{*}(t)\right)

Derivation of
d_{\bm{x}}L
.

Similarly, we can prove that:

\displaystyle d_{\bm{x}}\mathcal{L}

\displaystyle=\partial_{\bm{x}}\bm{\Phi}(0,\bm{x})^{\top}\cdot\left[\nabla\ell[\bm{\Phi}(0,\bm{x})]-\bm{\lambda}(0)\right]

\displaystyle+\int_{-T}^{0}dt\partial_{\bm{x}}\bm{\Phi}(t,\bm{x})^{\top}\cdot\left[\nabla_{\bm{\Phi}}\ell[t,\Phi(t,\bm{\theta}),\bm{\theta}]+\nabla^{2}_{\bm{\Phi}}H[\bm{\Phi}(t,\bm{x}),\bm{\theta},\bm{u}(t)]\cdot\bm{J}^{\top}\cdot\bm{\lambda}(t)+\partial_{t}\bm{\lambda}(t)\right]

\displaystyle+\bm{\lambda}(0)

Using the same
\bm{\Phi}_{*}
and
\bm{\lambda}_{*}
, we get
d_{\bm{x}}L=\bm{\lambda}(0)
.
∎

Proof of Theorem

Technical Lemmas.

We first introduce two technical Lemmas which will be needed for the derivation of our main result.

Lemma A.1

(Block-wise Pauli matrices and associated properties)
.

Defining
\bm{\Sigma}_{x},\bm{\Sigma}_{y},\bm{\Sigma}_{z}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\bm{\Phi}}\times d_{\bm{\Phi}}}
as:

\bm{\Sigma}_{x}:=\begin{bmatrix}\bm{0}&\bm{I}_{d_{\bm{\Phi}}/2}\\
\bm{I}_{d_{\bm{\Phi}}/2}&\bm{0}\end{bmatrix},\quad\bm{\Sigma}_{y}:=\begin{bmatrix}\bm{0}&-i\bm{I}_{d_{\bm{\Phi}}/2}\\
i\bm{I}_{d_{\bm{\Phi}}/2}&\bm{0}\end{bmatrix},\quad\bm{\Sigma}_{z}:=\begin{bmatrix}\bm{I}_{d_{\bm{\Phi}}/2}&\bm{0}\\
\bm{0}&-\bm{I}_{d_{\bm{\Phi}}/2}\end{bmatrix}

where
i
denotes the imaginary unit, the following equalities hold:

1.

\bm{J}=i\bm{\Sigma}_{y}

2.

\bm{\Sigma}_{x}^{2}=\bm{\Sigma}_{y}^{2}=\bm{\Sigma}_{z}^{2}=\bm{I}_{d_{\bm{\Phi}}/2}
,

3.

\bm{\Sigma}_{x}\cdot\bm{\Sigma}_{y}=i\bm{\Sigma}_{z}
,
\bm{\Sigma}_{y}\cdot\bm{\Sigma}_{z}=i\bm{\Sigma}_{x}
,
\bm{\Sigma}_{z}\cdot\bm{\Sigma}_{x}=i\bm{\Sigma}_{y}
,

4.

\bm{\Sigma}_{i}\cdot\bm{\Sigma}_{j}=-\bm{\Sigma}_{j}\cdot\bm{\Sigma}_{i}
for any
i\neq j\in\{x,y,z\}
.

Proof of Lemma
A.1
.

Because of the block-wise structure of
\bm{\Sigma}_{x},\bm{\Sigma}_{y},\bm{\Sigma}_{z}
, these equalities can be easily checked.
∎

Lemma A.2
.

Under the assumptions of Def.
A.1
, the following equalities hold for all
\bm{\Phi},\bm{\theta},\bm{u}
:

\displaystyle\nabla_{\bm{\Phi}}H[\bm{\Phi},\bm{\theta},\bm{u}]

\displaystyle=\bm{\Sigma}_{z}\cdot\nabla_{\bm{\Phi}^{\star}}H[\bm{\Phi}^{\star},\bm{\theta},\bm{u}],

\displaystyle\nabla^{2}_{\bm{\Phi}}H[\bm{\Phi},\bm{\theta},\bm{u}]

\displaystyle=\bm{\Sigma}_{z}\cdot\nabla^{2}_{\bm{\Phi}^{\star}}H[\bm{\Phi}^{\star},\bm{\theta},\bm{u}]\cdot\bm{\Sigma}_{z},

\displaystyle\nabla_{\bm{\Phi},\bm{\theta}}H[\bm{\Phi},\bm{\theta},\bm{u}]

\displaystyle=\nabla_{\bm{\Phi}^{\star},\bm{\theta}}H[\bm{\Phi}^{\star},\bm{\theta},\bm{u}]\cdot\bm{\Sigma}_{z}

Proof of Lemma
A.2
.

The above equalities can be simply obtained by differentiating through the time-reversal invariance hypothesis – which is possible because of the differentiability of
H
with respect to
\bm{\Phi}
and
\bm{\theta}
– and using the chain rule.
Namely, given some
\bm{\Phi},\bm{\theta},\bm{u}
:

\displaystyle\nabla_{\bm{\Phi}}H[\bm{\Phi},\bm{\theta},\bm{u}]

\displaystyle=\nabla_{\bm{\Phi}}\left(H[\bm{\Phi}^{\star},\bm{\theta},\bm{u}]\right)

\displaystyle=\left(\partial_{\bm{\Phi}}\bm{\Phi}^{\star}\right)^{\top}\cdot\nabla_{\bm{\Phi}^{\star}}H[\bm{\Phi}^{\star},\bm{\theta},\bm{u}]

\displaystyle=\bm{\Sigma}_{z}^{\top}\cdot\nabla_{\bm{\Phi}^{\star}}H[\bm{\Phi}^{\star},\bm{\theta},\bm{u}]=\bm{\Sigma}_{z}\cdot\nabla_{\bm{\Phi}^{\star}}H[\bm{\Phi}^{\star},\bm{\theta},\bm{u}],

since
\bm{\Phi}^{\star}:=\bm{\Sigma}_{z}\cdot\bm{\Phi}
. The other equalities are derived in the same way.
∎

Time-reversal invariance.

We highlight here how the assumption
H[\bm{\Phi},\cdot,\cdot]=H[\bm{\Sigma}_{z}\cdot\bm{\Phi},\cdot,\cdot]
given inside Def.
A.1
entails time-reversal invariance of the dynamics – up to time-reversal the input sequence.

Lemma A.3

(Time-reversal invariance of the dynamics)
.

Under the assumptions given in Def.
A.1
, if
\bm{\Phi}
the solution of the ODE:

\partial_{t}\bm{\Phi}(t)=\bm{J}\cdot\nabla_{\bm{\Phi}}H[\bm{\Phi}(t),\bm{\theta},\bm{u}(t)],

the function
\widetilde{\bm{\Phi}}^{\star}:t\to\bm{\Sigma}_{z}\cdot\bm{\Phi}(-t)=[\bm{\phi}^{\top}(-t),-\bm{\pi}^{\top}(-t)]^{\top}
is solution of the same ODE with the time-reversed input sequence
t\to\widetilde{\bm{u}}(t):=\bm{u}(-t)
.

Proof.

Let
t\in[-T,0]
. We have:

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\widetilde{\bm{\Phi}}^{\star}(t)

\displaystyle=-\bm{\Sigma}_{z}\cdot\partial_{t}\bm{\Phi}(-t)\qquad

(Def. of
\widetilde{\bm{\Phi}}^{\star}
and chain rule)

\displaystyle=-\bm{\Sigma}_{z}\cdot\bm{J}\cdot\nabla_{\bm{\Phi}}H[\bm{\Phi}(-t),\bm{\theta},\bm{u}(-t)]\qquad

(by assumption)

\displaystyle=+\bm{J}\cdot\bm{\Sigma}_{z}\cdot\nabla_{\bm{\Phi}}H[\bm{\Phi}(-t),\bm{\theta},\bm{u}(-t)]\qquad

(Lemma
A.1
)

\displaystyle=\bm{J}\cdot\bm{\Sigma}_{z}^{2}\cdot\nabla_{\bm{\Phi}^{\star}}H[\bm{\Phi}^{\star}(-t),\bm{\theta},\bm{u}(-t)]\qquad

(Lemma
A.2
)

\displaystyle=\bm{J}\cdot\nabla_{\bm{\Phi}^{\star}}H[\widetilde{\bm{\Phi}}^{\star}(t),\bm{\theta},\bm{u}(-t)]\qquad

(Lemma
A.1
)

∎

Time reversibility.

A direct consequence of the time-reversal of the dynamics under consideration (Lemma
A.3
) is
time reversibility
: upon flipping the momentum of
\bm{\Phi}
at time
t=0
(
\bm{\pi}(0)\leftarrow-\bm{\pi}(0)
) and presenting the input sequence in reversed order, the system evolves backward to its initial state.

Corollary A.3

(Reversibility of the dynamics)
.

Under the same assumptions as Lemma
A.3
, we define
\bm{\Phi}^{e}
as the solution of the ODE:

\bm{\Phi}^{e}(0)=\bm{\Phi}^{\star}(0),\quad\partial_{t}\bm{\Phi}^{e}(t)=\bm{J}\cdot\nabla_{\bm{\Phi}^{e}}H[\bm{\Phi}^{e}(t),\bm{\theta},\bm{u}(-t)]\quad\forall t\in[0,T].

Then:

\forall t\in[0,T]:\quad\bm{\Phi}^{e}(t)=\widetilde{\bm{\Phi}}^{\star}(t)

Proof.

\bm{\Phi}^{e}
and
\widetilde{\bm{\Phi}}^{\star}
satisfy: i) the same initial conditions (
\widetilde{\bm{\Phi}}^{\star}(0)=\bm{\Phi}^{\star}(0)=\bm{\Phi}^{e}(0)
), ii) the same ODE (Lemma
A.3
), therefore by unicity of the solution of the ODE, they are equal at all time over the domain of definition of
\bm{\Phi}
.
∎

Main result.

We are now ready to state and demonstrate our main result in continuous time.

Theorem A.2

(Equivalence between RHEL and the continuous ASM)
.

Under the assumptions of Def.
A.1
and Def.
A.2
, let
\bm{\Phi}
be the solution of the ODE for
t\in[-T,0]
:

\bm{\Phi}(-T)=\bm{x},\quad\partial_{t}\bm{\Phi}(t)=\bm{J}\cdot\nabla_{\bm{\Phi}}H[\bm{\Phi}(t),\bm{\theta},\bm{u}(t)].

Given
\bm{\Phi}
, let
\bm{\Phi}^{e}
be defined as the solution of the other ODE for
t\in[0,T]
:

\bm{\Phi}^{e}(0)=\bm{\Phi}^{\star}(0),\quad\partial_{t}\bm{\Phi}^{e}(t,\epsilon)=\bm{J}\nabla_{\bm{\Phi}^{e}}H[\bm{\Phi}^{e}(t,\epsilon),\bm{\theta},\bm{u}(-t)]-\epsilon\bm{J}\nabla_{\bm{\Phi}^{e}}\ell[-t,\bm{\Phi}^{e}(t,\epsilon),\bm{\theta}].

Defining:

\displaystyle\Delta^{\rm RHEL}_{\bm{\theta}}(t,\epsilon)

\displaystyle:=\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}\ell[-t,\bm{\Phi}^{e}(t,\epsilon),\bm{\theta}]+\frac{1}{2\epsilon}\left(\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}H[\bm{\Phi}^{e}(t,\epsilon),\bm{\theta},\bm{u}(-t)]-\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}H[\bm{\Phi}^{e}(t,-\epsilon),\bm{\theta},\bm{u}(-t)]\right),

\displaystyle\Delta^{\rm RHEL}_{\bm{\Phi}}(t,\epsilon)

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{2\epsilon}\bm{\Sigma}_{x}\cdot\left(\bm{\Phi}^{e}(t,\epsilon)-\bm{\Phi}^{e}(t,-\epsilon)\right),

we have:

\forall t\in[0,T],\quad\bm{\lambda}(t)=\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}\Delta^{\rm RHEL}_{\bm{\Phi}}(t,\epsilon),\quad g_{\bm{\theta}}(t)=\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}\Delta^{\rm RHEL}_{\bm{\theta}}(t,\epsilon)

where
\bm{\lambda}
and
g_{\bm{\theta}}
are defined in Corollary
A.1
.

Proof.

Defining
\Delta^{\rm RHEL}_{\bm{\Phi}}(t):=\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}\Delta^{\rm RHEL}_{\bm{\Phi}}(t,\epsilon)
and
\Delta^{\rm RHEL}_{\bm{\theta}}(t):=\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}\Delta^{\rm RHEL}_{\bm{\theta}}(t,\epsilon)
, note that:

\displaystyle\Delta^{\rm RHEL}_{\bm{\Phi}}(t)

\displaystyle=\bm{\Sigma}_{x}\cdot\partial_{\epsilon}\bm{\Phi}^{e}(t,\epsilon)|_{\epsilon=0}

\displaystyle\Delta^{\rm RHEL}_{\bm{\theta}}(t)

\displaystyle=\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}\ell[-t,\bm{\Phi}^{e}(t,0),\bm{\theta}]+\partial_{\epsilon}\left(\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}H[\bm{\Phi}^{e}(t,\epsilon),\bm{\theta},\bm{u}(-t)]\right)|_{\epsilon=0}

Derivation of
\bm{\lambda}(t)=\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}\Delta^{\rm RHEL}_{\bm{\Phi}}(t,\epsilon)
.

Given
t\in[0,T]
, differentiating the ODE satisfied by
\bm{\Phi}^{e}
with respect to
\epsilon
at
\epsilon=0
yields:

\displaystyle\partial_{t}(\partial_{\epsilon}\bm{\Phi}^{e}(t,\epsilon)|_{\epsilon=0})

\displaystyle=\partial_{\epsilon}(\partial_{t}\bm{\Phi}^{e}(t,\epsilon))|_{\epsilon=0}\qquad

(Schwartz Theorem)

\displaystyle=\partial_{\epsilon}(\bm{J}\cdot\nabla_{\bm{\Phi}^{e}}H[\bm{\Phi}^{e}(t,\epsilon),\bm{\theta},\bm{u}(t)]-\epsilon\bm{J}\nabla_{\bm{\Phi}^{e}}\ell[t,\bm{\Phi}^{e}(t,\epsilon),\bm{\theta}])|_{\epsilon=0}

\displaystyle=\bm{J}\cdot\nabla^{2}_{\bm{\Phi}^{e}}H[\bm{\Phi}^{e}(t,0)]\cdot\partial_{\epsilon}\bm{\Phi}^{e}(t,\epsilon)|_{\epsilon=0}-\bm{J}\nabla_{\bm{\Phi}^{e}}\ell[t,\bm{\Phi}^{e}(t,0),\bm{\theta}].

By Lemma
A.3
:

\bm{\Phi}^{e}(t,0)=\widetilde{\bm{\Phi}}^{\star}(t)\quad\forall t\in[0,T],

therefore:

\displaystyle\partial_{t}(\partial_{\epsilon}\bm{\Phi}^{e}(t,\epsilon)|_{\epsilon=0})

\displaystyle=\bm{J}\cdot\nabla^{2}_{\bm{\Phi}^{\star}}H[\bm{\Phi}^{\star}(-t)]\cdot\partial_{\epsilon}\bm{\Phi}^{e}(t,\epsilon)|_{\epsilon=0}-\bm{J}\nabla_{\bm{\Phi}^{\star}}\ell[t,\bm{\Phi}^{\star}(-t),\bm{\theta}]

\displaystyle=\bm{J}\cdot\bm{\Sigma}_{z}\cdot\nabla_{\bm{\Phi}}^{2}H[\bm{\Phi}(-t,0)]\cdot\bm{\Sigma}_{z}\cdot\partial_{\epsilon}\bm{\Phi}^{e}(t,\epsilon)|_{\epsilon=0}-\bm{J}\cdot\bm{\Sigma}_{z}\cdot\nabla_{\bm{\Phi}}\ell[t,\bm{\Phi}(-t),\bm{\theta}]\qquad

(Lemma

)

Additionally, note that we have by Lemma

(25)

and

(as defined in Corollary

) satisfy the same initial conditions and the same ODE, therefore they are equal at all times.

Derivation of
g_{\bm{\theta}}(t)=\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}\Delta^{\rm RHEL}_{\bm{\theta}}(t,\epsilon)
.

Note that by Lemma

and time-reversal invariance of

:

\displaystyle\Delta^{\rm RHEL}_{\bm{\theta}}(t)

\displaystyle=\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}\ell[-t,\bm{\Phi}^{\star}(-t,0),\bm{\theta}]+\partial_{\epsilon}\left(\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}H[\bm{\Phi}^{e}(t,\epsilon),\bm{\theta},\bm{u}(-t)]\right)|_{\epsilon=0}

coincide, the remainder of the derivation focuses on the second term of

\displaystyle=\nabla^{2}_{\bm{\Phi}^{e},\bm{\theta}}H[\bm{\Phi}^{e}(t,0),\bm{\theta},\bm{u}(t)]\cdot\bm{\Sigma}_{z}\cdot\bm{J}^{\top}\cdot\bm{\lambda}(t)\qquad

(Lemma

)

\displaystyle=-\nabla^{2}_{\bm{\Phi}^{e},\bm{\theta}}H[\bm{\Phi}^{e}(t,0),\bm{\theta},\bm{u}(t)]\cdot\bm{\Sigma}_{z}\cdot i\bm{\Sigma}_{y}\cdot\bm{\lambda}(t)\qquad

\displaystyle=-\nabla^{2}_{\bm{\Phi}^{e},\bm{\theta}}H[\bm{\Phi}^{e}(t,0),\bm{\theta},\bm{u}(t)]\cdot\partial_{\epsilon}\bm{\Phi}^{e}(t,\epsilon)|_{\epsilon=0}\qquad

(Lemma

A.1.4
Connection to Hamiltonian Echo Backpropagation (HEB)

Remark 3
.

The above setup and implementation of RHEL is not exactly that of Hamiltonian Echo Backprop (HEB,
[
10
]
). In particular:

•

the loss function in HEB is only defined at the
final
time step,

•

the interaction with
\ell
does not happen
simultaneously
with
H
,

•

finally, we would like to recover the HEB formula giving the gradient estimate of the loss with respect to the initial state of the neurons.

In the following corollary, we make slight algorithmic adjustments to match the seminal HEB implementation as much as possible
while preserving the generality of the sequence modelling setting
,
i.e.
dependence of
H
with
\bm{\theta}
and
\bm{u}
. Note that in the seminal HEB work,
H

A.2
Theoretical results in discrete time

Summary.

In this section, we introduce all the results derived in
discrete
time. More precisely:

•

We first formally define
Hamiltonian Recurrent Units
(HRUs, Definition
A.3
). HRUs can be regarded as the discrete-time counterpart of the continuous model introduced in the previous section (Definition
A.1
), namely as an
explicit
and
symplectic
integrator of the continuous Hamiltonian model which preserves the time-reversal invariance and time-reversibility properties in discrete time. We also introduce the constrained optimization problem naturally associated with HRUs (Definition
A.4
), which is the discrete time counterpart of the constrained continuous optimization problem introduced in the previous section (Definition
A.2
).

•

We then formally define
Hamiltonian State Space Models
(HSSMs) as stacks of HRUs (Definition
A.5
) and the
multilevel
constrained optimization problem which is naturally associated to these models (Definition
A.6
).

•

We state and prove our algorithmic baseline,
Backpropagation Through Time
(BPTT), through the lens of the
Lagrangian
formalism to establish a clear connection with the continuous ASM. We first introduce and derive BPTT in its general form (Theorem
A.3
) and then apply it more specifically to a HRU as defined in Definition
A.3
(Corollary
A.5
).

•

As we did in continuous time, we introduce a series of technical Lemmas needed to extend RHEL in discrete time. We first demonstrate the time-reversibility of HRUs on a single time step (Lemma
A.4
), which then enables us to extend the time reversibility property derived in continuous time (Corollary
A.3
) to
discrete
time (Corollary
A.6
). After introducing one last technical result (Lemma
A.5
), we then state and prove RHEL in discrete time when applied to HRUs (Corollary
A.7
). As the algorithm prescribed by Corollary
A.7
includes solving an
implicit equation
, we finally introduce a slight practical (
i.e.
fully explicit) variant of RHEL in discrete time (Corollary
A.8
).

•

Lastly, we show how to estimate gradients end-to-end in HSSMs by using
RHEL–chaining
(Theorem
A.4
). We also highlight that in practice, when using feedforward transformations across HRUs, the algorithm prescribed by Theorem
A.4
implicitly requires to chain RHEL through HRUs and
automatic differentiation
through these feedforward transformations (Remark
8
). This remark fundamentally underpins the actual algorithmic implementation of RHEL which was used throughout our experiments.

A.2.1
Definitions & assumptions

Definition A.3

(Hamiltonian Recurrent Unit)
.

Given
\bm{\theta}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\bm{\theta}}}
,
K\in\mathbb{N}^{\star}
and an input sequence
\left(\bm{u}_{k}\right)_{k\in[-K,0]}\in\left(\mathbb{R}^{d_{\bm{u}}}\right)^{K}
, the Hamiltonian Recurrent Unit (HRU) prediction is given by:

\bm{\Phi}_{k+1}=\mathcal{M}_{H,\delta}[\bm{\Phi}_{k},\bm{\theta},\bm{u}_{k}]\quad\forall k=-K\cdots-1,

with
H:=T+V
and:

\mathcal{M}_{H,\delta}:=\mathcal{M}_{T,\delta/2}\circ\mathcal{M}_{V,\delta}\circ\mathcal{M}_{T,\delta/2},\quad\mathcal{M}_{T,\delta}:=\bm{\Phi}+\delta\bm{J}\cdot\nabla_{\bm{\Phi}}T,\quad\mathcal{M}_{V,\delta}:=\bm{\Phi}+\delta\bm{J}\cdot\nabla_{\bm{\Phi}}V

We assume that:

1.

H
is separable,
i.e.

V
and
T
only depend on
\bm{\phi}
and
\bm{\pi}
respectively:

V[\bm{\Phi},\bm{\theta},\bm{u}]=V[\bm{\phi},\bm{\theta},\bm{u}],\quad T[\bm{\Phi},\bm{\theta},\bm{u}]=T[\bm{\pi},\bm{\theta},\bm{u}]

2.

T
and
V
are time-reversal invariant:

\displaystyle\forall\bm{\Phi}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\bm{\Phi}}},\ \forall\bm{\theta}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\bm{\theta}}},\ \forall\bm{u}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\bm{u}}}:\quad\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}T[\bm{\Phi},\bm{\theta},\bm{u}]=T[\bm{\Sigma}_{z}\cdot\bm{\Phi},\bm{\theta},\bm{u}],\\
V[\bm{\Phi},\bm{\theta},\bm{u}]=V[\bm{\Sigma}_{z}\cdot\bm{\Phi},\bm{\theta},\bm{u}]\end{array}\right.

3.

T
and
V
are twice differentiable with respect to
\bm{\Phi}
,
\bm{\theta}
and
\bm{u}
.

Remark 4
.

Note that
\mathcal{M}_{H,\delta}
is simply a Leapfrog integrator associated with
H
. We justify each of our design choices below:

•

3 steps-parametrization.
We write the Leapfrog integrator in a three-steps fashion to yield a
reversible
integrator.

•

Separability of the Hamiltonian.
In the case where
\bm{\phi}
and
\bm{\phi}
can be separated out in the Hamiltonian function, the Leapfrog integrator becomes explicit
[
33
]
.

•

T
and
V
as functions of
\bm{\Phi}
.
Although
T
and
V
only depend on
\bm{\pi}
and
\bm{\phi}
respectively, we choose to define them as functions of
\bm{\Phi}
so that the proof of RHEL in the continuous case seamlessly translates to the discrete case.

(Constrained optimization optimization problem in discrete time)
.

Given a continuous Hamiltonian model (Def.
A.1
), we consider the following constrained optimization problem:

\min_{\bm{\theta}}L:=\sum_{k=-K+1}^{0}\ell[\bm{\Phi}_{k},k]\quad\mbox{s.t.}\quad\bm{\Phi}_{k+1}=\mathcal{M}_{H,\delta}[\bm{\Phi}_{k},\bm{\theta},\bm{u}_{k}]\quad\forall k=-K\cdots-1

where we assume that:

1.

\ell
is time-reversal invariant:

\forall\bm{\Phi}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\bm{\Phi}}},\ \forall\bm{\theta}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\bm{\theta}}},\ \forall k=-K,\cdots,0:\quad\ell_{k}[\bm{\Phi},\bm{\theta}]=\ell_{k}[\bm{\Sigma}_{z}\cdot\bm{\Phi},\bm{\theta}]

2.

\ell
is twice differentiable with respect to
\bm{\Phi}
and
\bm{\theta}
.

Definition A.5

(Hamiltonian State Space Models)
.

Given
(\bm{\theta}^{(1)},\cdots,\bm{\theta}^{(N)})\in\left(\mathbb{R}^{d_{\bm{\theta}}}\right)^{N}
,

and an input sequence

\bm{\Phi}^{(0)}:=\overline{\bm{u}},\quad\forall\ell\in\llbracket 0,N-1\rrbracket:\ \overline{\bm{\Phi}}^{(\ell+1)}=\bm{\mathcal{M}}^{(\ell)}_{H^{(\ell)},\delta}[\overline{\bm{\Phi}}^{(\ell+1)},\bm{\theta}^{(\ell)},\overline{\bm{\Phi}}^{(\ell)}],

(Multilevel optimization problem in discrete time)
.

Given a HSSM (Def.
A.5
), we consider the following constrained optimization problem:

\displaystyle\min_{\bm{\theta}}L:=\sum_{k=-K+1}^{0}\ell[\bm{\Phi}_{k},k]\quad\mbox{s.t.}\quad

\displaystyle\bm{\Phi}^{(0)}:=\overline{\bm{u}},

A.2.2

General form.

We first state and prove Backpropagation Through Time (BPTT) for any integrator
\mathcal{M}_{H,\delta}

Theorem A.3

(Backpropagation Through Time (BPTT))
.

Given assumptions in Def.
A.3
–
A.4
, the gradients of the loss with respect to the parameters
\bm{\theta}
and the inputs
\bm{u}_{-k}
are given by:

d_{\bm{\theta}}L=\sum_{k=0}^{K-1}g_{\bm{\theta}}(k),\quad d_{\bm{u}_{-(k+1)}}L=g_{\bm{u}}(k)\quad\forall k\in\llbracket 0,K-1\rrbracket,

with:

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}g_{\bm{\theta}}(k)&=\nabla_{2}\ell[\bm{\Phi}_{-k},\bm{\theta},-k]+\partial_{2}\mathcal{M}_{H,\delta}[\bm{\Phi}_{-(k+1)},\bm{\theta},\bm{u}_{-(k+1)}]^{\top}\cdot\bm{\lambda}_{k}\\
g_{\bm{u}}(k)&=\partial_{3}\mathcal{M}_{H,\delta}[\bm{\Phi}_{-(k+1)},\bm{\theta},\bm{u}_{-(k+1)}]^{\top}\cdot\bm{\lambda}_{k},\end{array}\right.

and where
(\bm{\lambda}_{k})
satisfy the following recursion relationship:

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\bm{\lambda}_{0}=\nabla_{1}\ell[\bm{\Phi}_{0},0],\\
\bm{\lambda}_{k+1}=\partial_{1}\mathcal{M}_{H,\delta}(\bm{\Phi}_{-(k+1)},\bm{\theta},\bm{u}_{-(k+1)})^{\top}\cdot\bm{\lambda}_{k}+\nabla_{1}\ell[\bm{\Phi}_{-(k+1)},-(k+1)]\quad\forall k=0,\cdots,K-1\end{array}\right.

Proof of Theorem
A.3
.

BPTT can classically be derived through the application of the “chain rule” backward through the inference computational graph defined in Def.
A.3
. Another useful viewpoint though, which directly connects BPTT as the discrete counterpart of the continuous ASM and will be useful later in the appendix, is to derive it through
the method of Lagrangian multipliers
. Namely, the Lagrangian associated to the constrained optimization problem in Def.
A.4
reads as:

\mathcal{L}(\bm{\Phi},\bm{\lambda},\bm{\theta},\bm{u})=\sum_{k=0}^{K-1}\ell[\bm{\Phi}_{-k},\bm{\theta},-k]+\bm{\lambda}_{k}^{\top}\cdot\left(\mathcal{M}_{H,\delta}\left[\bm{\Phi}_{-(k+1)},\bm{\theta},\bm{u}_{-(k+1)}\right]-\bm{\Phi}_{-k}\right)

Extremizing
\mathcal{L}
with respect to
\bm{\Phi}
and
\bm{\lambda}
yield
\bm{\Phi}_{k,*}
and
\bm{\lambda}_{k,*}
:

\displaystyle\forall k=0,\cdots,K-1:\quad\partial_{\bm{\lambda}_{k}}\mathcal{L}(\bm{\Phi}_{*},\bm{\lambda}_{*},\bm{\theta},\bm{u})

\displaystyle=\mathcal{M}_{H,\delta}\left[\bm{\Phi}_{-(k+1),*},\bm{\theta},\bm{u}_{-k}\right]-\bm{\Phi}_{-k,*}=0,

\displaystyle\partial_{\bm{\Phi}_{0}}\mathcal{L}(\bm{\Phi}_{*},\bm{\lambda}_{*},\bm{\theta},\bm{u})

\displaystyle=\nabla_{1}\ell[\bm{\Phi}_{0,*},\bm{\theta},0]-\bm{\lambda}_{0,*}=0

\displaystyle\forall k=1,\cdots,K-1:\quad\partial_{\bm{\Phi}_{-k}}\mathcal{L}(\bm{\Phi}_{*},\bm{\lambda}_{*},\bm{\theta},\bm{u})

\displaystyle=\nabla_{1}\ell[\bm{\Phi}_{-k},\bm{\theta},-k]+\partial_{1}\mathcal{M}_{H,\delta}[\bm{\Phi}_{-k},\bm{\theta},\bm{u}_{-k}]^{\top}\cdot\bm{\lambda}_{k-1}-\bm{\lambda}_{k}=0,

Finally, the total derivative of
L
with respect to
\bm{\theta}
reads as:

\displaystyle d_{\bm{\theta}}L

\displaystyle=d_{\bm{\theta}}\mathcal{L}(\bm{\Phi}_{*},\bm{\lambda}_{*},\bm{\theta},\bm{u})

\displaystyle=\partial_{\bm{\theta}}\mathcal{L}(\bm{\Phi}_{*},\bm{\lambda}_{*},\bm{\theta},\bm{u})+\partial_{\bm{\theta}}\bm{\Phi}_{*}^{\top}\cdot\underbrace{\partial_{\bm{\Phi}}\mathcal{L}(\bm{\Phi}_{*},\bm{\lambda}_{*},\bm{\theta},\bm{u})}_{=0}+\partial_{\bm{\theta}}\bm{\lambda}_{*}^{\top}\cdot\underbrace{\partial_{\bm{\lambda}}\mathcal{L}(\bm{\Phi}_{*},\bm{\lambda}_{*},\bm{\theta},\bm{u})}_{=0}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{K-1}\nabla_{2}\ell[\bm{\Phi}_{-k},\bm{\theta},-k]+\partial_{2}\mathcal{M}_{H,\delta}[\bm{\Phi}_{-(k+1)},\bm{\theta},\bm{u}_{-(k+1)}]^{\top}\cdot\bm{\lambda}_{k}

The total derivative of
L
with respect to
\bm{u}_{-k}
is derived in the exact same fashion.

and therefore:

\bm{\Phi}_{k}=\mathcal{M}^{-1}_{H,\delta}[\bm{\Phi}_{k+1},\bm{\theta},\bm{u}]=\mathcal{M}_{H,-\delta}[\bm{\Phi}_{k+1},\bm{\theta},\bm{u}],

\bm{\phi}_{k+1/3}&=\bm{\phi}_{k+2/3}\\
\bm{\pi}^{\star}_{k+1/3}&=\bm{\pi}^{\star}_{k+2/3}-\delta\nabla_{\bm{\phi}}V[\bm{\phi}_{k+2/3},\bm{\theta},\bm{u}]\\

where equations bearing on
\bm{\pi}

\quad\bm{\lambda}_{k+1}^{(N)}=\partial_{1}\mathcal{M}_{H^{(N-1)},\delta}\left[\bm{\Phi}^{(N)}_{-(k+1)},\bm{\theta}^{(N-1)},\bm{\Phi}^{(N-1)}_{-(k+1)}\right]^{\top}\cdot\bm{\lambda}^{(N)}_{k}+\nabla_{1}\ell\left[\bm{\Phi}^{(N)}_{-(k+1)},\bm{\theta},-(k+1)\right]\end{array}\right.

Given the definition of the dynamics of
\bm{\Phi}^{e,(N)}

by hypothesis
, we can directly apply Corollary
A.7
to obtain:

\displaystyle d_{\bm{\theta}^{(N-1)}}L

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{K-1}\nabla_{2}\ell\left[\bm{\Phi}^{(N)}_{-k},\bm{\theta}^{(N-1)},-k\right]-\delta\left.\partial_{\epsilon}\left(\nabla_{2}H^{(N-1),1/2}\left[\bm{\Phi}^{e,(N)}_{k}(\epsilon),\bm{\theta}^{(L-1)},\bm{\Phi}^{(N-1)}_{-(k+1)}\right]\right)\right|_{\epsilon=0},

\displaystyle d_{\bm{\Phi}^{(N-1)}_{k-1}}L

\displaystyle=\partial_{3}\mathcal{M}_{H^{(N-1)},\delta}[\bm{\Phi}^{(N)}_{-(k+1)},\bm{\theta}^{(N-1)},\bm{\Phi}^{(N-1)}_{-(k+1)}]^{\top}\cdot\bm{\lambda}_{k}^{(N)}

\displaystyle=-\delta\left.\partial_{\epsilon}\left(\nabla_{3}H^{(N-1),1/2}\left[\bm{\Phi}^{e,(N)}_{k}(\epsilon),\bm{\theta}^{(L-1)},\bm{\Phi}^{(N-1)}_{-(k+1)}\right]\right)\right|_{\epsilon=0}

Induction (
\ell+1\to\ell
).

Let us assume that the desired property is satisfied at layer
\ell+1
. We denote again
\bm{\Phi}^{(\ell)}_{k}
and
\bm{\lambda}^{(\ell)}_{k}
for
k\in\llbracket 0,K-1\rrbracket
the critical point of
\mathcal{L}
.
We have, for
k\in\llbracket 0,K-1\rrbracket
:

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\bm{\lambda}^{(\ell)}_{0}=\partial_{3}\mathcal{M}_{H^{(\ell)},\delta}\left[\bm{\Phi}^{(\ell+1)}_{0},\bm{\theta}^{(\ell)},\bm{\Phi}^{(\ell)}_{0}\right]^{\top}\cdot\bm{\lambda}^{(\ell+1)}_{0}\\
\forall k=0,\cdots,K-1:\\
\quad\bm{\lambda}_{k+1}^{(\ell)}=\partial_{1}\mathcal{M}_{H^{(\ell-1)},\delta}\left[\bm{\Phi}^{(\ell)}_{-(k+1)},\bm{\theta}^{(\ell-1)},\bm{\Phi}^{(\ell-1)}_{-(k+1)}\right]^{\top}\cdot\bm{\lambda}^{(\ell)}_{k}+\partial_{3}\mathcal{M}_{H^{(\ell)},\delta}\left[\bm{\Phi}^{(\ell+1)}_{-(k+1)},\bm{\theta}^{(\ell)},\bm{\Phi}^{(\ell)}_{-(k+1)}\right]^{\top}\cdot\bm{\lambda}^{(\ell+1)}_{k}\end{array}\right.

Using the induction hypothesis at layer
(\ell+1)
:

\displaystyle\partial_{3}\mathcal{M}_{H^{(\ell)},\delta}\left[\bm{\Phi}^{(\ell+1)}_{-(k+1)},\bm{\theta}^{(\ell)},\bm{\Phi}^{(\ell)}_{-(k+1)}\right]^{\top}\cdot\bm{\lambda}^{(\ell+1)}_{k}

\displaystyle=-\delta\left.\partial_{\epsilon}\left(\nabla_{3}H^{(\ell),1/2}\left[\bm{\Phi}^{e,(\ell+1)}_{k}(\epsilon),\bm{\theta}^{(\ell)},\bm{\Phi}^{(\ell)}_{-(k+1)}\right]\right)\right|_{\epsilon=0}

\displaystyle=\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}\Delta_{\bm{\Phi}^{(\ell)}_{(k+1)}}(\epsilon)

Therefore on the one hand, denoting
\Delta_{\bm{\Phi}^{(\ell)}}:=\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}\Delta_{\bm{\Phi}^{(\ell)}}(\epsilon)\in\mathbb{R}^{K\times d_{\bm{\Phi}}}
, the dynamics on
\bm{\lambda}
rewrite:

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\bm{\lambda}^{(\ell)}_{0}=\Delta_{\bm{\Phi}^{(\ell)}_{0}}\\
\forall k=0,\cdots,K-1:\\
\quad\bm{\lambda}_{k+1}^{(\ell)}=\partial_{1}\mathcal{M}_{H^{(\ell-1)},\delta}\left[\bm{\Phi}^{(\ell)}_{-(k+1)},\bm{\theta}^{(\ell-1)},\bm{\Phi}^{(\ell-1)}_{-(k+1)}\right]^{\top}\cdot\bm{\lambda}^{(\ell)}_{k}+\Delta_{\bm{\Phi}^{(\ell)}_{(k+1)}}\end{array}\right.

On the other hand, the dynamics of
\bm{\Phi}^{e,(\ell)}
read
by hypothesis
:

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\bm{\Phi}^{(\ell)}_{0}=\left(\bm{\Phi}^{(\ell)}_{0}\right)^{\star}+\epsilon\bm{\Sigma}_{x}\cdot\Delta_{\bm{\Phi}^{(\ell)}_{0}}(\epsilon)\\
\forall k=0,\cdots,K-1:\\
\quad\bm{\Phi}_{k+1}^{e,(\ell)}=\mathcal{M}_{H^{(\ell-1)},\delta}\left[\bm{\Phi}_{k+1}^{e,(\ell)},\bm{\theta}^{(\ell-1)},\bm{\Phi}^{(\ell-1)}_{-(k+1)}\right]+\epsilon\bm{\Sigma}_{x}\cdot\Delta_{\bm{\Phi}^{(\ell)}_{(k+1)}}\end{array}\right.

using Corollary
A.8
with
y=\Delta_{\bm{\Phi}^{(\ell)}}
, we conclude that:

d_{\bm{\theta}^{(\ell)}}L=\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}\Delta\bm{\theta}^{(\ell)}(\epsilon)

∎

Remark 7
.

Theorem
A.4
, and more generally our definition of HSSMs (Def.
A.5
) assume that the connectivity pattern of the HRU units is a
linear chain
. Note that while we chose this hypothesis for the sake of clarity of our results and their derivations, Theorem
A.4
could be seamlessly extended to any
Directed Acyclic Graph
(DAG) of HRUs. This allows, for instance as a simple and realistic case, to use skip connections across HRUs within HSSMs.

Remark 8
.

Note that RHEL chaining as prescribed by Theorem
A.4

implicitly chains RHEL and automatic differentiation
. Indeed, if
H
explicitly parametrizes feedforward mappings across HRUs as:

H^{(\ell)}\left[\bm{\Phi}^{e,(\ell+1)}_{k}(\epsilon),\bm{\theta}^{(\ell)},\bm{\Phi}^{(\ell)}_{-(k+1)}\right]=H^{(\ell)}\left[\bm{\Phi}^{e,(\ell+1)}_{k}(\epsilon),\bm{\theta}^{(\ell)}_{\alpha},F\left[\bm{\Phi}^{(\ell)}_{-(k+1)},\bm{\theta}^{(\ell)}_{\beta}\right]\right],

(44)

then, denoting
\bm{u}^{(\ell)}:=F\left[\bm{\Phi}^{(\ell)}_{-(k+1)},\bm{\theta}^{(\ell)}_{\beta}\right]
, we have:

\displaystyle\left.\partial_{\epsilon}\left(\nabla_{3}H^{(\ell),1/2}\left[\bm{\Phi}^{e,(\ell+1)}_{k}(\epsilon),\bm{\theta}^{(\ell)},\bm{\Phi}^{(\ell)}_{-(k+1)}\right]\right)\right|_{\epsilon=0}

\displaystyle=\partial_{1}F\left[\bm{\Phi}^{(\ell)}_{-(k+1)},\bm{\theta}^{(\ell)}_{\beta}\right]^{\top}\cdot\left.\partial_{\epsilon}\left(\nabla_{3}H^{(\ell),1/2}\left[\bm{\Phi}^{e,(\ell+1)}_{k}(\epsilon),\bm{\theta}^{(\ell)}_{\alpha},\bm{u}^{\ell}\right]\right)\right|_{\epsilon=0}

\displaystyle\approx{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\partial_{1}F\left[\bm{\Phi}^{(\ell)}_{-(k+1)},\bm{\theta}^{(\ell)}_{\beta}\right]^{\top}}\cdot{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\frac{1}{2\epsilon}\left(\nabla_{3}H^{(\ell),1/2}\left[\bm{\Phi}^{e,(\ell+1)}_{k}(\epsilon),\bm{\theta}^{(\ell)}_{\alpha},\bm{u}^{\ell}\right]-\nabla_{3}H^{(\ell),1/2}\left[\bm{\Phi}^{e,(\ell+1)}_{k}(-\epsilon),\bm{\theta}^{(\ell)}_{\alpha},\bm{u}^{\ell}\right]\right)}

The red part is done by automatic differentiation and the blue part by RHEL. This underpins the implementation of RHEL chaining we used in our own code.

A.3.1
Toy model

In this section, we provide the gradient estimators for the parameters of the toy model. The toy model is a simple network of six mechanically coupled oscillators. Each oscillator is described by a state
\bm{\Phi^{i}}=({\phi^{i}},{\pi^{i}})
where
{\phi^{i}}\in\mathbb{R}
is the position of the oscillator and
{\pi^{i}}\in\mathbb{R}
is its momentum. It has a mass parameter
m_{i}
and spring parameter
k_{i}
. Any pair of oscillators
(i,j)
is coupled via the spring parameter
k_{ij}
. The input to the models is a time-varying external force
\bm{u}(t)\in\mathbb{R}
coupled to oscillator
1
. During the echo passes, the nudging force is modelled by a spring coupling with parameter
\epsilon\in\mathbb{R}
to an external force
y(t)\in\mathbb{R}
. The Hamiltonian of the system is given by:

\displaystyle H[\bm{\Phi},\bm{\theta},\mathbf{u}]=\sum_{i}\frac{(\pi^{i})^{2}}{2m_{i}}+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i}k_{i}(\phi^{i})^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i}\sum_{j>i}k_{ij}(\phi^{j}-\phi^{i})^{2}+\mathbf{u}\,\phi^{1}

(45)

Which gives the following equations of motion:

\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\dot{\phi}^{i}=\frac{\pi^{i}}{m_{i}}\quad\text{for all }i\in\{1,6\}\\[5.0pt]
\dot{\pi}^{i}=-k_{i}\phi^{i}+\sum_{j,j\neq i}k_{ij}(\phi^{j}-\phi^{i}),\quad i\in\{2,3,5,6\}\\[5.0pt]
\dot{\pi}^{1}=-k_{1}\phi^{1}+\sum_{j,j\neq 1}k_{1j}(\phi^{j}-\phi^{1})+\bm{u}\\[5.0pt]
\dot{\pi}^{4}=-k_{4}\phi^{4}+\sum_{j,j\neq 4}k_{4j}(\phi^{j}-\phi^{4})-\delta_{e}\epsilon(\bm{\phi}^{4}-y)\end{array}\right.

where
\delta_{e}
is the indicator function of the echo pass, it’s equal to
1
during the echo pass and
0
otherwise.

RHEL gradient estimators of the model parameters.

For the mass
m_{i}
, we have:

\displaystyle\Delta^{RHEL}_{m_{i}}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2\epsilon}\left(\nabla_{m_{i}}H\left[\bm{\Phi}^{e}(t,\epsilon),\bm{\theta},\bm{u}\right]-\nabla_{m_{i}}H\left[\bm{\Phi}^{e}(t,-\epsilon),\bm{\theta},\bm{u}\right]\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\epsilon}\left(\frac{\left(\pi^{i}(t,\epsilon)\right)^{2}}{2m_{i}^{2}}-\frac{\left(\pi^{i}(t,-\epsilon)\right)^{2}}{2m_{i}^{2}}\right)

(46)

For the spring parameters
k_{i}
, we haves:

\displaystyle\Delta^{RHEL}_{k_{i}}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2\epsilon}\left(\nabla_{k_{i}}H[\bm{\Phi}^{e}(t,\epsilon),\bm{\theta},\bm{u}]-\nabla_{k_{i}}H[\bm{\Phi}^{e}(t,-\epsilon),\bm{\theta},\bm{u}]\right)

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2\epsilon}(\left(\phi^{i}(t,\epsilon)\right)^{2}-\left(\phi^{i}(t,-\epsilon)\right)^{2})

(47)

For the coupling parameters
k_{ij}
, we have:

\displaystyle\Delta^{RHEL}_{k_{ij}}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2\epsilon}\left(\nabla_{k_{ij}}H[\bm{\Phi}^{e}(t,\epsilon),\bm{\theta},\bm{u}]-\nabla_{k_{ij}}H[\bm{\Phi}^{e}(t,-\epsilon),\bm{\theta},\bm{u}]\right)

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2\epsilon}(\left(\phi^{j}(t,\epsilon)-\phi^{i}(t,\epsilon)\right)^{2}-\left(\phi^{j}(t,-\epsilon)-\phi^{i}(t,-\epsilon)\right)^{2})

(49)

RHEL gradient estimators of the state sensitivities

For the state position sensitivities, we have:

\displaystyle\Delta^{RHEL}_{\phi^{i}}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2\epsilon}\bm{\Sigma}_{x}\left(\bm{\Phi}^{e}(t,\epsilon)-\bm{\Phi}^{e}(t,-\epsilon)\right)

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2\epsilon}\left(\pi^{i}(t,\epsilon)-\pi^{i}(t,-\epsilon)\right)

(50)

For the state momentum sensitivities, we have:

\displaystyle\Delta^{RHEL}_{\pi^{i}}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2\epsilon}\bm{\Sigma}_{x}\left(\bm{\Phi}^{e}(t,\epsilon)-\bm{\Phi}^{e}(t,-\epsilon)\right)

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2\epsilon}\left(\phi^{i}(t,\epsilon)-\phi^{i}(t,-\epsilon)\right)

(51)

A.3.2
HSSM architecture

In this section, we outline the detailed architecture of a full multi-layer HSSM architecture. For the inference, we re-use the same stacking architecture of recurrent blocks and feedforward elements as the LinOSS model
[
29
]
. The model starts by encoding an input sequence
\overline{\bm{u}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\bm{u}}\times K}
via an affine transformation. The transformed sequence then progresses through multiple HSSM blocks, linear (see Appendix
A.3.3
), or nonlinear (see Appendix
A.3.4
), directly followed by nonlinear transformations. These transformations include
the Gaussian error linear unit (GELU)
[
55
]
and the Gated Linear Unit (GLU)
[
56
]
, defined as

\text{GLU}(\mathbf{x})=\text{sigmoid}(\mathbf{W}_{1}\mathbf{x})\circ\mathbf{W}_{2}\mathbf{x}

where
\mathbf{W}_{1,2}
represent trainable weight matrices, accompanied by a residual
connection. The sequence output from the final block undergoes a second affine transformation
to produce the model output.

The full linear and nonlinear HSSM is further presented in Algorithm
2
. For clarity, when applying operations to sequence elements denoted with an overline (e.g.,
\overline{\bm{u}}
), these operations are implicitly broadcast across the time dimension. Specifically, for any function
f
applied to
\overline{\bm{u}}
, we have
f(\overline{\bm{u}})_{t}=f(\bm{u}_{t})
for all time steps
t\in\{1,2,...,K\}
.

For the inference of the linear HSSMs, we keep the same recurrent block as LinOSS with a slight change in the integrator (see
A.3.3
). For the nonlinear HSSM, we replace the recurrent block by a UniCORRN recurrent block
[
30
]
for which we use the same integrator as for the linear HSSM (see
A.3.4
).

A.3.3
Linear HRU Block

Hamiltonian of the recurrence.

The linear HRU block is the composition of a nonlinear spatial transformation and a linear recurrent transformation (see Eq.
4
). Here we provide more details about the linear recurrence that is computed with the RHEL gradient estimator.
The linear recurrence is defined by the following Hamiltonian:

\displaystyle H[\bm{\Phi},\bm{\theta},\bm{u}]

\displaystyle=T[\bm{\pi},\bm{\theta},\bm{u}]+V[\bm{\phi},\bm{\theta},\bm{u}]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\|\bm{\pi}\|^{2}+\left(\frac{1}{2}\bm{\phi}^{\top}\bm{A}\bm{\phi}-\bm{\phi}^{\top}\bm{B}\bm{u}\right)

(52)

Dynamics.

The dynamics of the linear HRU block are defined by the following equations:

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\dot{\bm{\phi}}=\bm{\pi}\\[5.0pt]
\dot{\bm{\pi}}=-\bm{A}\bm{\phi}+\bm{B}\bm{u}\end{array}\right.

Which, after time-discretization with the integrator defined in
A.2.1
, gives the following equations:

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{aligned} \bm{\phi}_{k+1/3}&=\bm{\phi}_{k}+\frac{\delta}{2}\nabla_{\bm{\pi}}T[\bm{\pi}_{k},\bm{\theta},\bm{u}_{k}]\\
&=\bm{\phi}_{k}+\frac{\delta}{2}\bm{\pi}_{k}\\
\bm{\pi}_{k+1/3}&=\bm{\pi}_{k}\\
\bm{\phi}_{k+2/3}&=\bm{\phi}_{k+1/3}\\
\bm{\pi}_{k+2/3}&=\bm{\pi}_{k+1/3}+\delta\nabla_{\bm{\phi}}V[\bm{\phi}_{k+1/3},\bm{\theta},\bm{u}_{k}]\\
&=\bm{\pi}_{k+1/3}-\delta\bm{A}\bm{\phi}_{k+1/3}+\delta\bm{B}\bm{u}_{k}\\
\bm{\phi}_{k+1}&=\bm{\phi}_{k+2/3}+\frac{\delta}{2}\nabla_{\bm{\pi}}T[\bm{\pi}_{k+2/3},\bm{\theta},\bm{u}_{k}]\\
&=\bm{\phi}_{k+2/3}+\frac{\delta}{2}\bm{\pi}_{k+2/3}\\
\bm{\pi}_{k+1}&=\bm{\pi}_{k+2/3}\end{aligned}\right.

(55)

with the initial condition
\bm{\Phi}_{-K}=(\bm{\phi}_{-K}^{\top},\bm{\pi}_{-K}^{\top})^{\top}=\bm{x}
.

For the echo passes, the initial condition are
\bm{\Phi}^{e}_{0}=\bm{\Phi}_{0}^{\star}\pm\epsilon\bm{\Sigma}_{x}\cdot\Delta_{\bm{\Phi}}\ell[\bm{\Phi}_{0},0]
. The dynamics equations follow below, with modifications from equation
55
highlighted in blue::

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{aligned} \bm{\phi}^{e}_{k+1/3}&=\bm{\phi}^{e}_{k}+\frac{\delta}{2}\bm{\pi}^{e}_{k}\\
\bm{\pi}^{e}_{k+1/3}&=\bm{\pi}^{e}_{k}\\
\bm{\phi}^{e}_{k+2/3}&=\bm{\phi}^{e}_{k+1/3}\\
\bm{\pi}^{e}_{k+2/3}&=\bm{\pi}^{e}_{k+1/3}-\delta\bm{A}\bm{\phi}^{e}_{k+1/3}+\delta\bm{B}{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\bm{u}_{-(k+1)}}\\
\bm{\phi}^{e}_{k+1}&=\bm{\phi}^{e}_{k+2/3}+\frac{\delta}{2}\bm{\pi}^{e}_{k+2/3}{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}+\epsilon\nabla_{\bm{\pi}}\ell[\bm{\Phi}_{-(k+1)}]}\\
\bm{\pi}^{e}_{k+1}&=\bm{\pi}^{e}_{k+2/3}{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}+\epsilon\nabla_{\bm{\phi}}\ell[\bm{\Phi}_{-(k+1)}]}\end{aligned}\right.

(56)

RHEL gradient estimators.

The gradient estimators of the parameters of the linear HRU are:

\displaystyle\Delta^{RHEL}_{\bm{A}}(k,\epsilon)

\displaystyle=-\frac{\delta}{2\epsilon}\left(\nabla_{\bm{A}}H^{1/2}[\bm{\Phi}^{e}_{k}(\epsilon),\bm{\theta},\bm{u}_{-(k+1)}]-\nabla_{\bm{A}}H^{1/2}[\bm{\Phi}^{e}_{k}(-\epsilon),\bm{\theta},\bm{u}_{-(k+1)}]\right)

\displaystyle=-\frac{\delta}{4\epsilon}\left[\left(\bm{\phi}^{e}_{k+1/3}(\epsilon)^{\top}\bm{\phi}^{e}_{k+1/3}(\epsilon)+\bm{\phi}^{e}_{k+2/3}(\epsilon)^{\top}\bm{\phi}^{e}_{k+2/3}(\epsilon)\right)\right.

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\left.-\left(\bm{\phi}^{e}_{k+1/3}(-\epsilon)^{\top}\bm{\phi}^{e}_{k+1/3}(-\epsilon)+\bm{\phi}^{e}_{k+2/3}(-\epsilon)^{\top}\bm{\phi}^{e}_{k+2/3}(-\epsilon)\right)\right]

\displaystyle=-\frac{\delta}{2\epsilon}\left[\left(\bm{\phi}^{e}_{k+1/3}(\epsilon)\right)^{\top}\left(\bm{\phi}^{e}_{k+1/3}(\epsilon)\right)-\left(\bm{\phi}^{e}_{k+1/3}(-\epsilon)\right)^{\top}\left(\bm{\phi}^{e}_{k+1/3}(-\epsilon)\right)\right]

(57)

\displaystyle\Delta^{RHEL}_{\bm{B}}(k,\epsilon)

\displaystyle=-\frac{\delta}{2\epsilon}\left(\nabla_{\bm{B}}H^{1/2}[\bm{\Phi}^{e}_{k}(\epsilon),\bm{\theta},\bm{u}_{-(k+1)}]-\nabla_{\bm{B}}H^{1/2}[\bm{\Phi}^{e}_{k}(-\epsilon),\bm{\theta},\bm{u}_{-(k+1)}]\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{\delta}{4\epsilon}\left[\left(\bm{\phi}^{e}_{k+1/3}(\epsilon)+\bm{\phi}^{e}_{k+2/3}(\epsilon)\right)\bm{u}_{-(k+1)}^{\top}\right.

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\left.-\left(\bm{\phi}^{e}_{k+1/3}(-\epsilon)+\bm{\phi}^{e}_{k+2/3}(-\epsilon)\right)\bm{u}_{-(k+1)}^{\top}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{\delta}{2\epsilon}\left[\bm{\phi}^{e}_{k+1/3}(\epsilon)\bm{u}_{-(k+1)}^{\top}-\bm{\phi}^{e}_{k+1/3}(-\epsilon)\bm{u}_{-(k+1)}^{\top}\right]

(58)

The gradient estimator with respect to the input of the recurrent transformation is:

\displaystyle\Delta^{RHEL}_{\bm{u}}(k,\epsilon)

\displaystyle=-\frac{\delta}{2\epsilon}\left(\nabla_{\bm{u}}H^{1/2}[\bm{\Phi}^{e}_{k}(\epsilon),\bm{\theta},\bm{u}_{-(k+1)}]-\nabla_{\bm{u}}H^{1/2}[\bm{\Phi}^{e}_{k}(-\epsilon),\bm{\theta},\bm{u}_{-(k+1)}]\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{\delta}{4\epsilon}\left[\bm{B}^{\top}\left(\bm{\phi}^{e}_{k+1/3}(\epsilon)+\bm{\phi}^{e}_{k+2/3}(\epsilon)\right)\right.

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\left.-\bm{B}^{\top}\left(\bm{\phi}^{e}_{k+1/3}(-\epsilon)+\bm{\phi}^{e}_{k+2/3}(-\epsilon)\right)\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{\delta}{2\epsilon}\left[\bm{B}^{\top}\bm{\phi}^{e}_{k+1/3}(\epsilon)-\bm{B}^{\top}\bm{\phi}^{e}_{k+1/3}(-\epsilon)\right]

(59)

Parallel Scan.

Similarly to the LinOSS model
[
29
]
, we can compute the recurrence of Linear HRU with a parallel scan
[
5
]
to reduce the computational time. To implement the parallel scan, we need to put the discretized dynamics in a form that can be computed in parallel. For this we vectorize the Equation
55
:

\displaystyle\bm{\Phi}_{k+1}

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}\bm{I}&\frac{\delta}{2}\bm{I}\\
\bm{0}&\bm{I}\end{bmatrix}\cdot\bm{\Phi}_{k+2/3}

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}\bm{I}&\frac{\delta}{2}\bm{I}\\
\bm{0}&\bm{I}\end{bmatrix}\cdot\left(\begin{bmatrix}\bm{I}&\bm{0}\\
-\delta\bm{A}&\bm{I}\end{bmatrix}\cdot\bm{\Phi}_{k+1/3}+\begin{bmatrix}\bm{0}\\
\delta\bm{B}\cdot\bm{u}_{k}\end{bmatrix}\right)

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}\bm{I}&\frac{\delta}{2}\bm{I}\\
\bm{0}&\bm{I}\end{bmatrix}\cdot\begin{bmatrix}\bm{I}&\bm{0}\\
-\delta\bm{A}&\bm{I}\end{bmatrix}\cdot\begin{bmatrix}\bm{I}&\frac{\delta}{2}\bm{I}\\
\bm{0}&\bm{I}\end{bmatrix}\cdot\bm{\Phi}_{k}+\begin{bmatrix}\frac{\delta^{2}}{2}\bm{B}\cdot\bm{u}_{k}\\
\delta\bm{B}\cdot\bm{u}_{k}\end{bmatrix}

which gives us the discrete dynamics in matrix form:

\displaystyle\bm{\Phi}_{k+1}

\displaystyle=M\bm{\Phi}_{k}+F_{k}

(60)

with:

\displaystyle M=\begin{bmatrix}I-\frac{\delta^{2}}{2}\bm{A}&\delta[I-\frac{\delta^{2}}{4}\bm{A}]\\[5.0pt]
-\delta\bm{A}&I-\frac{\delta^{2}}{2}\bm{A}\end{bmatrix},\quad F_{k}=\begin{bmatrix}\frac{\delta^{2}}{2}\bm{B}\cdot\bm{u}_{k}\\[5.0pt]
\delta\bm{B}\cdot\bm{u}_{k}\end{bmatrix}

(61)

To compute the echo passes
\bm{\Phi}^{e}_{k},k\in\{1,\cdots,K\}
, we only need to adapt
F_{k}
to get for the positive nudging
+\epsilon
:

\displaystyle F_{k}^{e}=\begin{bmatrix}\frac{\delta^{2}}{2}\bm{B}\cdot\bm{u}_{-(k+1)}+\epsilon\nabla_{\bm{\pi}}\ell[\bm{\Phi}_{-(k+1)}]\\[5.0pt]
\delta\bm{B}\cdot\bm{u}_{-(k+1)}+\epsilon\nabla_{\bm{\phi}}\ell[\bm{\Phi}_{-(k+1)}]\end{bmatrix}

(62)

A.3.4
Nonlinear HRU

Hamiltonian of the recurrence.

The nonlinear HRU block is the composition of a nonlinear spatial transformation and a nonlinear recurrent transformation (see Eq.
4
). The Hamiltonian of the nonlinear HRU block is defined by the following equations:

\displaystyle H[\bm{\Phi},\bm{\theta},\bm{u}]

\displaystyle=T[\bm{\pi},\bm{\theta},\bm{u}]+V[\bm{\phi},\bm{\theta},\bm{u}]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\|\bm{\pi}\|^{2}+\frac{\alpha}{2}\|\bm{\phi}\|^{2}+\left(\bm{A}^{-\top}\cdot\mathbf{log}\left(\mathbf{cosh}\left(\bm{A}\cdot\bm{\phi}+\bm{B}\cdot\bm{u}+\bm{b}\right)\right)\right)

(63)

Dynamics.

The dynamics of the nonlinear HRU block are defined by the following equations:

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\dot{\bm{\phi}}=\bm{\pi}\\[5.0pt]
\dot{\bm{\pi}}=-\left(\bm{\tanh}\left(\bm{A}\bm{\phi}+\bm{B}\bm{u}+b\right)+\alpha\bm{\phi}\right)\\
\end{array}\right.

Which, after time-discretization with the integrator defined
in
A.2.1
, gives the following equations:

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{aligned} \bm{\phi}_{k+1/3}&=\bm{\phi}_{k}+\frac{\delta}{2}\nabla_{\bm{\pi}}T[\bm{\pi}_{k},\bm{\theta},\bm{u}_{k}]\\

A.3.5
Differentiating the time-discretization

In training HRUs, one can also train the multidimensional time-discretization
\delta\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\bm{\Phi}}\times d_{\bm{\Phi}}}
that is assumed to be diagonal. For this, it suffices to reparametrize the integrator and the Hamiltonian, so that the time discretization becomes a parameter of the Hamiltonian. Hence the HRU equation
11
becomes:

\displaystyle\bm{\Phi}_{k+1}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{M}_{\hat{H},1}[\bm{\Phi}_{k},\bm{\theta},\bm{u}_{k},\delta]\quad\forall k=-K\cdots-1,

(67)

with
\hat{H}
is a reparametrization of the Hamiltonian
H
such that
\mathcal{M}_{\hat{H},1}[\bm{\Phi}_{k},\bm{\theta},\bm{u}_{k},\delta]=\mathcal{M}_{H,\delta}[\bm{\Phi}_{k},\bm{\theta},\bm{u}_{k}]
.

For instance, for the linear HRU, we can reparametrize the Hamiltonian as:

\displaystyle\hat{H}[\bm{\Phi},\bm{\theta},\bm{u},\delta]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\bm{\pi}^{\top}\delta\bm{\pi}+\left(\frac{1}{2}\bm{\phi}^{\top}(\delta\bm{A})\bm{\phi}-\bm{\phi}^{\top}(\delta\bm{B})\bm{u}\right)

(68)

And for the nonlinear HRU, we can reparametrize the Hamiltonian as:

\displaystyle\hat{H}[\bm{\Phi},\bm{\theta},\bm{u},\delta]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\bm{\pi}^{\top}\delta\bm{\pi}+\frac{\alpha}{2}\bm{\phi}^{\top}\delta\bm{\phi}+\frac{1}{2}\left(\delta\bm{A}^{-\top}\cdot\mathbf{log}\left(\mathbf{cosh}\left(\bm{A}\cdot\bm{\phi}+\bm{B}\cdot\bm{u}+\bm{b}\right)\right)\right)

(69)

Note that to match with previous implementations of the nonlinear HRU
[
30
]
, we used the following parametrization for the discretization step:

\displaystyle\hat{H}[\bm{\Phi},\bm{\theta},\bm{u},\delta]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\bm{\pi}^{\top}\sigma(\delta)\bm{\pi}+\frac{\alpha}{2}\bm{\phi}^{\top}\sigma(\delta)\bm{\phi}+\frac{1}{2}\left(\sigma(\delta)\bm{A}^{-\top}\cdot\mathbf{log}\left(\mathbf{cosh}\left(\bm{A}\cdot\bm{\phi}+\bm{B}\cdot\bm{u}+\bm{b}\right)\right)\right)

(71)

where
\sigma(\delta)
is a diagonal matrix with
\sigma(\delta)_{ii}=0.5+0.5\tanh(\delta_{ii}/2)
.

As mentioned in Remark
8
, learning in HSSMs with RHEL involves chaining RHEL gradient estimators with Automatic Differentiation (AD). This design makes RHEL implementation readily compatible with modern automatic differentiation frameworks such as PyTorch or JAX. These libraries provide the capability to create custom functions that, when invoked within a computational graph, are automatically differentiated. Algorithm
3
demonstrates how to implement RHEL/AD chaining for the recurrent component of an HRU block.

To implement a custom autodiff function
HRU
, we must define two additional functions required by AD:
ForwardHRU
and
BackwardHRU
. The
ForwardHRU
function is invoked when the
HRU
function is called within a computational graph to be differentiated. The
ForwardHRU
function returns two elements: its output for computing the remainder of the computational graph
\bar{\bm{\Phi}}
, and a
residual
to be stored for the backward pass. For the HRU, the residual consists only of the final state
\bm{\Phi}_{K}
from the forward pass. The
BackwardHRU
function is called during graph backpropagation. It receives as input the loss gradient from the upstream portion of the computational graph
\bar{g}
, the input from the forward pass
\bar{u}
, and the residual
\bm{\Phi}_{K}
. The
BackwardHRU
function computes the gradient of the loss with respect to the HRU block parameters, which is subsequently used to update the HRU parameters. It also computes the gradient of the loss with respect to the forward pass input
\bar{u}
, which is then used to propagate the loss gradient backward through the computational graph.

These three functions are then registered with the autodiff library using the
HRU.define_custom_autodiff
function, enabling the library to automatically differentiate the
HRU
function when it is called within a computational graph.

The three functions utilize two helper functions:
HRU-HELPER
and
LearningHRU
. The
HRU-HELPER
function implements the HRU dynamics and is employed in both the forward and backward passes. The
LearningHRU
function implements the RHEL gradient estimator and is used in the backward pass to compute the gradient of the loss with respect to the HRU block parameters.s

Simulation details

.The code to run the experiments is implemented using the JAX auto-differentiation framework

A.4.4
Gradient re-scaling for dealing with numerical instabilities

Analysis

From the theoretical results on RHEL, the nudging strength
\epsilon
should be
chosen as small as possible to accurately estimate the gradient of the loss function.
However, naive numerical implementation can encounter underflow issues. In RHEL,
gradient information is encoded in small perturbations to the state
\bm{\Phi}
that
generate the echo trajectory
\bm{\Phi}^{e}
. This presents numerical challenges when
the perturbations and state values differ by several orders of magnitude.

In practice, when using finite precision representations (such as floating-point
numbers), the perturbations may be lost due to discretization error, where small
values cannot be accurately represented or distinguished from zero in finite
precision arithmetic.

To address this numerical challenge, we employ a simple gradient rescaling method.
We multiplicatively scale the output loss
\ell[\cdot,\cdot]
by a constant

\gamma>1
during the echo dynamics computation. This amplification ensures that
the perturbation-induced changes in the loss remain within the representable range
of floating-point arithmetic. Subsequently, we divide the RHEL parameter gradient
estimate
\left(\Delta_{\bm{\theta}}^{\rm RHEL}(k,\epsilon)\right)
by the same
scaling factor
\gamma
to recover the unbiased gradient.

We now demonstrate the effectiveness of this gradient rescaling method on both
Linear and Nonlinear HSSM. To evaluate the effect of gradient scaling, we compare
the gradients of RHEL and BPTT. Given a HSSM model, we randomly sample a tuple

(\bm{u}_{i},\bm{y}_{i})\sim\mathcal{D}
from the SCP1 dataset (see
App.
A.4.1
) and compute the gradients of the loss function
with respect to the parameters of the recurrence of the HRUs in
Fig.
5
A for the linear HSSM and
Fig.
6
A for the nonlinear HSSM.

To gain a more fine-grained understanding, we also compute the gradients with
respect to the inputs of the third layer of the HSSM (see
Eq.
4
and
4
) in
Fig.
5
B and
Fig.
6
B. Compared to the parameter gradients,
these input gradients are not time-averaged and hence reveal more detail about
the underflow issues.

Gradient rescaling recovers the underflow issues.

As a general pattern across both architectures, we observe that the unscaled
(
\gamma=10^{0}
) parameter and input gradients tend to be biased: they have a
norm ratio above
1.0
and cosine similarity below
1.0
. We also note that
for both the linear and nonlinear cases, there exists a scaling factor
(
10^{6}
for linear and
10^{4}
for nonlinear) that recovers a nearly perfect
match between RHEL and BPTT.

Additionally, for the input gradients that are not time-averaged (first column
of Fig.
5
B and
Fig.
6
B), there is a large amount of noise
in the unscaled gradients, which is eliminated for the best-performing scaling
factor (
10^{6}
for linear and
10^{4}
for nonlinear). We conjecture that this
noise is due to the underflow effects described above.

Linear HSSM: scaling improves gradient matching

For the linear analysis, we observe a general pattern: the more we scale the
RHEL gradient, the better it matches BPTT, both for input gradients and
parameter gradients (Fig.
5
A and B).

Nonlinear HSSM: optimal scaling balances underflow and linearization errors

For the nonlinear analysis, we also observe that scaling the RHEL gradient
improves the match between RHEL and BPTT gradients. However, for higher values
of the scaling factor (
\gamma=10^{6}
), we observe that both input and parameter
gradients show a drop in matching quality. We conjecture that this is due to
linearization errors. If the loss gradient is too large, the nudging it drives
will be large, and the finite difference method used for the learning rule
will no longer be valid.

Solution Implemented.

For the experiments, we conducted a grid search over the scaling parameter
\gamma
. In our initial implementation, we applied gradient scaling without the corresponding downscaling step, effectively amplifying the gradient magnitude throughout training. This provided valuable insights into the sensitivity of RHEL dynamics to gradient scaling and improved performance. The complete rescaling procedure (including both upscaling and downscaling) will be implemented in the camera-ready version to provide a more comprehensive analysis of the numerical stabilization approach.
