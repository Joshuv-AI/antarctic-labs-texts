Modular Prompt Optimization: Optimizing Structured Prompts with Section-Local Textual Gradients
Prith Sharma, Austin Z. Henley

Abstract




1
Introduction

Large language models (LLMs) are increasingly used as general-purpose problem solvers, where behavior is controlled primarily through prompting rather than task-specific training
[
21
,
4
]
. In practice, prompt quality often becomes the main limiting factor. Small changes in wording, order, or formatting can lead to large differences in model performance, reliability, and even safety. This sensitivity is especially challenging for smaller, open-source instruction-tuned models, which typically require clearer instructions and benefit more from explicit structure
[
3
]
. As a result, developing principled and repeatable methods for improving prompts has become an important part of building robust LLM-based systems.

Real-world prompts are rarely simple or well-structured. They are often written as long blocks of text that mix several different purposes, such as defining the model’s role, providing background information, describing the task, listing constraints, and specifying the desired output format
[
26
]
. When these elements are combined without clear separation, a range of problems can occur. Constraints may be incomplete or contradicted later in the prompt, formatting instructions may be ignored, and changes made to fix one issue can unintentionally introduce new ones. Over repeated rounds of refinement, prompts may also grow longer and harder to manage. These problems are difficult to diagnose because it is often unclear which part of the prompt is responsible for a given error, making prompt improvement slow and largely ad hoc.

Many prior works have studied automatic prompt optimization, but existing methods do not fully resolve these challenges. Continuous prompt tuning approaches optimize prompts in a latent embedding space while keeping the language model fixed
[
24
,
13
,
14
]
. Although this makes them parameter-efficient, the resulting prompts are not directly interpretable. More recent methods optimize prompts directly in natural language by treating improvement as a search or self-refinement process
[
29
,
20
,
27
]
. These approaches preserve readability and work in black-box settings, but they typically treat the prompt as a single piece of text and apply updates to it as a whole. As a result, it is hard to target specific parts of the prompt, interactions between unrelated instructions can increase, and important information may be altered during rewriting.

At the same time, practical prompting guidelines have increasingly adopted structured prompt formats that separate system role, context, task description, constraints, and output format
[
19
,
2
]
. This practice reflects a simple but often overlooked idea that different parts of a prompt play different roles. When prompts are structured in this way, errors and improvements can be traced back to specific sections, and optimization can focus on refining individual components rather than rewriting the entire prompt at once.

In this paper, we introduce Modular Prompt Optimization (MPO), a schema-based prompt optimization framework that treats prompts as structured objects composed of fixed semantic sections. MPO applies section-local textual gradients at each iteration using a critic model which generates targeted natural-language improvements for each section independently while keeping the overall schema fixed. Updates are then consolidated with de-duplication to prevent prompt bloat and reduce interference between components. This design makes optimization localized, interpretable, and robust to uncontrolled rewriting.

We evaluate MPO on two reasoning benchmarks, ARC-Challenge
[
6
]
and MMLU
[
9
,
8
]
, using LLaMA-3 8B-Instruct and Mistral-7B Instruct as solver models. Across both benchmarks and models, MPO consistently outperforms an untuned structured prompt and TextGrad
[
27
]
. These improvements are achieved without modifying model parameters or altering prompt structure. The results suggest that maintaining a fixed prompt schema while optimizing semantic sections independently is an effective and practical approach for improving reasoning performance in small open-source LMs.

2.4
Prompt Structure, Task Decomposition and positioning of our work

Concurrently, practitioner-oriented prompting guides have converged on a consistent structured prompt format, typically decomposing prompts into semantic sections
[
19
]
,
[
2
]
. While these documents are not framed as optimization research, they implicitly encode a theory of prompt structure, suggesting that different components of a prompt serve distinct functional roles and should be reasoned about independently.

Recent research has begun to formalize this intuition. Task Facet Learning
[
12
]
proposes decomposing prompts into facets and learning new task-specific components from labeled errors. While effective, this approach modifies the prompt structure itself and focuses on discovering new task knowledge from downstream performance signals.

In contrast, our work is inspired by structured prompting practices rather than task-facet discovery. We assume a fixed, documentation-driven prompt schema and study how optimizing each semantic section independently using textual-gradient style feedback can improve performance. Rather than discovering new task facets or altering prompt topology, we focus on refining how existing task information is organized, expressed, and constrained within a stable prompt structure. This design is particularly motivated by small open-source LMs, which benefit disproportionately from explicit structure and modular instruction decomposition
[
3

.

3
Approach

We propose Modular Prompt Optimization (MPO), a schema-based prompt optimization framework that treats prompts as structured objects composed of semantically distinct components. MPO refines prompts by iteratively improving the content of each component independently, while keeping the overall prompt schema fixed. An overview of the MPO optimization process is shown in Figure
1
.

Prompt schema.

MPO decomposes each prompt into a fixed set of semantic sections:
System Role
,
Relevant Context
,
Task Details
,
Constraints
, and
Output Format
. This decomposition is inspired by widely adopted practitioner prompting guidelines and reflects the observation that different parts of a prompt play distinct functional roles in shaping model behavior. Throughout optimization, the schema itself is held constant. MPO does not search over prompt structures or reorder sections. If a section is missing in the original prompt, it is initialized by extracting or inferring relevant content directly from the original prompt, without introducing new task information.

Prompt state and optimization objective.

MPO maintains an explicit notion of prompt state. Let

p_{0}=\{s_{0}^{(1)},s_{0}^{(2)},\dots,s_{0}^{(K)}\}

denote the initial structured prompt, where each
s_{0}^{(k)}
corresponds to one semantic section. The goal of MPO is to produce a sequence of refined prompt states

p_{0}\rightarrow p_{1}\rightarrow\dots\rightarrow p_{n},

where each successive prompt improves task performance while preserving interpretability and structural consistency.

Section-wise textual gradients.

At each iteration
t
, MPO evaluates each prompt section independently using a critic language model. Rather than rewriting the entire prompt, the critic produces textual gradients for each section. Formally, for section
k
, the critic generates

\Delta s_{t}^{(k)}=\mathcal{C}\!\left(s_{t}^{(k)},\,p_{t}\setminus s_{t}^{(k)}\right),

where
\mathcal{C}
denotes the critic model and
p_{t}\setminus s_{t}^{(k)}
provides the surrounding prompt context without modifying other sections. Each
\Delta s_{t}^{(k)}
is expressed in natural language and represents a proposed improvement in alignment with the intended task.

Textual Gradients application and consolidation.

Section updates are applied additively, yielding an intermediate section:

\tilde{s}_{t+1}^{(k)}=s_{t}^{(k)}\oplus\Delta s_{t}^{(k)},

where
\oplus
denotes textual aggregation. As repeated iterations may introduce redundancy or semantically overlapping instructions, MPO applies an LLM-based de-duplication and consolidation step:

s_{t+1}^{(k)}=\mathcal{D}\!\left(\tilde{s}_{t+1}^{(k)}\right),

where
\mathcal{D}
removes redundant content while preserving everything else. The updated prompt state is then reassembled as

p_{t+1}=\{s_{t+1}^{(1)},s_{t+1}^{(2)},\dots,s_{t+1}^{(K)}\}.

Iterative refinement.

This process can be repeated for a number of iterations depending on the prompt and task as shown in Figure
1
.. By restricting updates to localized, section-specific deltas and consolidating them after each iteration, MPO reduces interference between unrelated prompt components and prevents uncontrolled prompt growth.

Experimental setup.

We evaluate MPO on LLaMA-3 8B-Instruct and Mistral-7B-Instruct as solver models. For each model, we compare MPO-optimized prompts against Textgrad
[
27
]
and a fixed baseline prompt that follows the same schema but does not undergo iterative refinement.

Evaluation is conducted on two standard reasoning benchmarks: ARC Challenge
[
6
]
and the full MMLU benchmark
[
9
]
,
[
8
]
. For ARC, performance is measured using exact-match accuracy on multiple-choice questions. For MMLU, we report average accuracy across all subject domains.

MPO is applied independently for each model–dataset pair, producing a task-specific and model-specific optimized prompt. This setup allows us to assess both absolute performance gains and the consistency of MPO improvements across different models.

Experiments are conducted using two open-source instruction-tuned solver models: LLaMA-3 8B-Instruct and Mistral-7B-Instruct. 5
Conclusion and Future Work

We presented Modular Prompt Optimization (MPO), a schema-constrained prompt optimization framework that refines prompts by independently optimizing their semantic components using section-local textual feedback followed by aggregation and de-duplication. By treating prompt structure as a fixed inductive bias rather than a free-form text object, MPO enables targeted refinement of system role, context, task specification, constraints, and output format without altering prompt topology or model parameters.

Across ARC-Challenge and MMLU, and over multiple open-source instruction-tuned models, MPO consistently outperforms both untuned structured prompts and global textual gradient-based methods such as TextGrad. These gains are achieved through localized updates that reduce interference between prompt components, leading to more stable and generalizable improvements. The results suggest that explicit prompt structure, coupled with section-wise optimization, is an effective mechanism for improving reasoning performance in open-source language models without additional training or task-specific data.

Future work can extend MPO along several directions. First, it would be interesting to study whether using stronger open-source models as critics for generating section-wise feedback leads to further improvements and how the choice of critic model affects optimization stability and transferability. Second, MPO offers a natural framework for studying prompt safety and robustness, including whether prompts that induce unsafe or jailbreak behaviors are amplified or mitigated through structured optimization, and how section-local updates interact with safety-aligned system roles and constraints. Finally, MPO may be evaluated on a broader range of reasoning benchmarks and coupled with automated validation or stopping mechanisms, and further combined with programmatic prompting or agentic pipelines to support more complex structured optimization workflows.
