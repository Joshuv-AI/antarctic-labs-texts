Analyzing total rewards perceptions of marketing professionals through linkedin discourse: a natural language processing approach
Authors: Sezai Tunca, Yavuz Selim Balcioglu, Ceren Cubukcu Cerasi, Ayse Bilgen, Ayse Ilgun-Kamanli
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study examines how marketing professionals publicly articulate total rewards perceptions through LinkedIn discourse, extending natural language processing methodologies via domain-specific model development. Analyzing 35,691 comments from 2023, we identify significant shifts toward digital skill-based compensation discourse (+ 31.2%) and strong positive sentiment associations with flexible work arrangements (r = .48, p < .001), particularly among technology sector professionals. Hierarchical regression analysis confirms that digital skill discourse orientation and industry sector interact to predict sentiment variation (ΔR² = 0.04, p < .001), with digital competency emphasis demonstrating differential effects across technology, retail, and consumer goods contexts. Drawing on Skill-Based Total Rewards Theory, we provide preliminary empirical support for three propositions: Digital Skill-Reward Congruence, Market Value of Skills, and Skills-Based Flexibility principles. The sentiment analysis model achieved an F1-score of 0.89, while industry-specific analysis revealed significant variations (F(2,35688) = 27.32, p < .001) across sectors. Recognizing that LinkedIn discourse reflects publicly constructed professional identity positions rather than unfiltered latent attitudes, we interpret these patterns as expressions of signaled reward preferences within professional networking contexts. Methodologically, the study demonstrates NLP applications for large-scale professional sentiment analysis. Practically, findings inform industry-specific compensation strategies recognizing digital competencies and workplace flexibility as critical retention factors. This research contributes to compensation theory through quantitative operationalization of SBTRT propositions while establishing methodological frameworks for discourse-based organizational research.Keywords: Marketing Compensation, Digital Skills Recognition, Total Rewards Analytics, Social Media Discourse Analysis, Professional Benefits EvolutionSubject terms: Psychology, Environmental social sciences

Introduction

LinkedIn discourse reveals critical gaps between stated compensation preferences and authentic professional sentiment about total rewards packages. Marketing professionals discuss rewards differently in organic peer conversations than in formal survey contexts, creating opportunities for understanding genuine preference evolution that traditional methodologies miss. This distinction matters for three reasons. First, authentic discourse captures emerging compensation trends before they appear in formal organizational policies or structured survey instruments. Second, sector-specific discourse patterns enable targeted compensation strategy development aligned with industry characteristics. Third, temporal discourse analysis tracks real-time preference evolution during periods of rapid workplace transformation.

Existing research relies predominantly on traditional survey methodologies with significant limitations. Terera and Ngirande (2014) surveyed 200 marketing professionals about rewards and retention, while McIlveen and colleagues (2021) interviewed 450 marketing professionals about workplace satisfaction. WorldatWork (2022) conducted the largest survey-based examination with 2,847 marketing professionals across multiple industries. Despite methodological rigor, these approaches share common constraints. Structured questionnaires limit respondents to predetermined categories, potentially missing emerging trends. Social desirability bias influences formal survey responses, particularly regarding sensitive compensation topics1,2. Martinez and colleagues3 demonstrated that professionals express 34% more negative sentiment about workplace policies through social media compared to formal surveys, suggesting traditional methods underestimate authentic dissatisfaction.

This research addresses three critical gaps through LinkedIn discourse analysis of 35,691 marketing professional comments collected throughout 2023, providing empirical validation of Skill-Based Total Rewards Theory while establishing methodological frameworks for understanding authentic professional sentiment about evolving compensation structures.

Definitional framework: total rewards in organizational context

This study examines total rewards as comprehensive compensation packages provided by employing organizations to their marketing professional workforce, encompassing both monetary compensation elements including salary, bonuses, and performance incentives and non-monetary benefits such as flexible work arrangements, professional development opportunities, digital tool access, and career advancement pathways. The research specifically focuses on rewards offered by current employers rather than external recognition systems or inter-organizational reward mechanisms. Accordingly, the scope of analysis includes employer-provided benefits, workplace flexibility arrangements, skills development programs, recognition systems, and career progression opportunities, while excluding external rewards such as industry recognition awards, professional association honors, or compensation offered by potential alternative employers during recruitment processes.

Rationale for marketing professional focus

The study’s specific focus on marketing professionals represents a strategic methodological choice based on three empirical and theoretical considerations that distinguish this occupational group from other professional categories in their relationship with total rewards structures. Marketing professionals operate at the intersection of traditional business functions and emerging digital technologies, creating unique compensation challenges that reflect broader workplace transformation trends. The marketing profession has experienced particularly rapid digital transformation, with traditional advertising and brand management roles evolving into data-driven, technology-enabled positions requiring new skill sets and competencies. This transformation creates distinctive total rewards considerations that may not be present in other professional contexts with more stable technological requirements. The marketing profession demonstrates exceptional heterogeneity in skill requirements across industry sectors, with technology sector marketing professionals requiring different competencies than those in retail or consumer goods contexts. This sectoral variation enables systematic examination of how industry characteristics influence total rewards preferences, providing insights that extend beyond occupation-specific findings to broader questions about industry-compensation relationships. Marketing professionals maintain particularly active LinkedIn presence and engagement patterns, creating rich data sources for social media discourse analysis. The professional networking requirements inherent in marketing roles generate authentic professional discourse about career development, compensation expectations, and workplace satisfaction that may be less accessible in other occupational contexts with different professional communication patterns.

The academic literature examining marketing professionals’ perceptions of total rewards through digital platforms remains underdeveloped, with existing research predominantly relying on traditional survey methodologies6. A systematic review of compensation research in marketing reveals that 78% of studies published between 2020 and 2023 employed cross-sectional surveys or structured interviews, limiting the capture of authentic, unfiltered professional discourse7. Traditional survey approaches, while methodologically rigorous, present inherent limitations when examining sensitive topics such as compensation satisfaction. The social desirability bias phenomenon, extensively documented in organizational research, suggests that respondents may provide socially acceptable responses rather than authentic perspectives when directly questioned about workplace rewards1,8. Furthermore, structured survey instruments may constrain respondents’ ability to express nuanced viewpoints about emerging compensation trends, particularly those related to digital transformation and flexible work arrangements. LinkedIn discourse analysis offers methodological advantages by capturing organic, peer-to-peer professional conversations that occur without researcher intervention. This approach provides access to authentic expressions of professional sentiment that may differ significantly from responses provided in formal survey contexts9. The platform’s professional networking environment encourages candid discussions about career advancement, compensation expectations, and workplace satisfaction, creating a rich data source for understanding contemporary professional perspectives on total rewards. Recent methodological studies have demonstrated significant differences between social media-expressed sentiments and survey-reported attitudes in professional contexts. Research by Martinez and colleagues3 found that professionals expressed 34% more negative sentiment about workplace policies in social media contexts compared to formal survey responses, suggesting that traditional methods may underestimate employee dissatisfaction levels.

Survey methodology represents a cornerstone of organizational research, with well-established protocols for validity, reliability, and generalizability that have advanced our understanding of workplace phenomena for decades. However, specific empirical evidence suggests that social desirability bias presents particular challenges when examining sensitive topics such as compensation satisfaction and workplace reward preferences. Social desirability bias, defined as the tendency of respondents to provide answers they perceive as socially acceptable rather than authentic, has been documented extensively in compensation research contexts. Krumpal’s (2013) comprehensive meta-analysis of 72 studies examining sensitive survey topics found that compensation-related questions exhibited significantly higher social desirability response rates compared to general workplace satisfaction measures, with effect sizes ranging from 0.34 to 0.67 across different organizational contexts.

Specifically relevant to compensation research, Podsakoff and colleagues2 demonstrated that employees systematically underreport compensation dissatisfaction in formal survey contexts when questions are administered by organizational representatives or when respondent anonymity cannot be guaranteed. Their experimental study with 1,247 employees across multiple organizations revealed that compensation satisfaction ratings were 23% higher in employer-administered surveys compared to independent third-party administered surveys, suggesting significant response bias effects. More directly applicable to marketing professional contexts, Van de Mortel’s (2008) research on response bias in workplace surveys found that professionals in client-facing roles, including marketing positions, exhibited heightened social desirability tendencies when discussing workplace satisfaction topics. This pattern reflected concerns about professional reputation and career advancement implications of expressing dissatisfaction with compensation packages.

Contemporary research by Martinez and colleagues3 provides compelling evidence for the specific context of compensation research. Their comparative study examined 892 marketing professionals’ responses to identical compensation satisfaction questions administered through both formal surveys and analyzed through social media discourse analysis. Results revealed that professionals expressed 34% more negative sentiment about compensation packages in social media contexts compared to formal survey responses, with particularly pronounced differences in discussions about work-life balance and flexible work arrangements. The theoretical foundation for these bias effects stems from impression management theory, which suggests that individuals modify their responses in formal evaluation contexts to maintain positive professional images. In compensation research, this manifests as reluctance to express dissatisfaction with rewards packages, particularly when such responses might be perceived as ungrateful or professionally damaging. These empirical findings do not diminish the value of survey methodology but rather highlight the importance of methodological triangulation in compensation research. Social media discourse analysis provides complementary insights that can validate and extend survey-based findings by capturing authentic professional expressions that may differ from formal response contexts.

The literature review reveals three critical research gaps that necessitate empirical investigation through innovative methodological approaches. First, the predominant reliance on survey-based methodologies has limited our understanding of how marketing professionals authentically express and discuss their total rewards preferences in organic professional contexts. The documented social desirability bias effects in compensation research suggest that traditional approaches may not capture the full spectrum of professional sentiment about evolving reward structures, particularly those related to digital transformation trends. Second, existing research has insufficiently examined how industry-specific contexts influence marketing professionals’ reward preferences and sentiment patterns. While studies by Terera and Ngirande (2014) and McIlveen et al. (2021) provide valuable insights into general marketing professional satisfaction, they lack the granular industry-specific analysis necessary to understand how sectoral characteristics shape reward valuations. The technology, retail, and consumer goods sectors present distinct operational environments that likely influence how marketing professionals perceive and prioritize different components of total rewards packages. Third, the temporal evolution of marketing professionals’ reward preferences remains underexplored in the context of accelerated digital transformation. The COVID-19 pandemic and subsequent digital acceleration created unprecedented conditions where traditional reward structures evolved rapidly, yet existing research has not systematically examined how these changes manifested in professional discourse over time. The Skill-Based Total Rewards Theory provides a theoretical framework for understanding these changes, but empirical validation through real-time professional discourse analysis remains limited. These research gaps collectively indicate the need for methodological innovation that can capture authentic professional sentiment across industry sectors and temporal periods. LinkedIn discourse analysis offers the methodological precision necessary to address these gaps by providing access to organic professional conversations that reveal unfiltered perspectives on total rewards evolution. Furthermore, the integration of natural language processing techniques with social media analytics presents opportunities to scale qualitative insights about professional discourse while maintaining analytical rigor. This methodological approach can generate sector-specific insights that inform both theoretical understanding and practical compensation strategy development.

Total rewards and employee satisfaction

Contemporary research demonstrates that organizational total rewards significantly influence employee satisfaction, retention, and organizational commitment across diverse industry contexts and generational cohorts. Total rewards represent the comprehensive value proposition that employing organizations provide to marketing professionals, encompassing both tangible compensation elements and intangible workplace benefits that contribute to overall employment satisfaction. The organizational total rewards framework distinguishes between employer-provided compensation and external recognition systems, focusing specifically on the employment relationship between marketing professionals and their current organizations. This approach enables systematic examination of how employing organizations structure their total rewards packages to attract, retain, and motivate marketing talent within specific industry contexts. Recent evidence from Malaysia’s food and beverage industry reveals that job satisfaction, organizational commitment, work-life balance, and competitive pay structures collectively reduce employee turnover intentions, with organizations that cultivate positive work environments and provide comprehensive development opportunities achieving superior talent retention outcomes (Yook Pei et al., 2025). The relationship between total rewards and employee satisfaction exhibits distinct generational patterns, with Generation Z employees demonstrating complex preference structures that prioritize both extrinsic rewards such as compensation and recognition alongside intrinsic motivators including personal growth opportunities and meaningful work purpose (Mabaso, 2025). Similarly, Millennials in post-COVID workplace environments emphasize five critical reward categories encompassing remuneration, well-being, development, recognition, and benefits, with non-monetary rewards such as flexible and remote work arrangements becoming essential components for achieving work-life balance satisfaction (Mabaso & Mathebula, 2025). The effectiveness of total rewards systems depends significantly on mediating factors, with research on Generation Z employees revealing that perceived supervisor support and comprehensive total rewards packages mediate the relationship between flexible work arrangements and turnover intentions, suggesting that reward system success requires integrated management approaches rather than isolated benefit provision (Protsiuk, 2025). Cross-cultural validation from Central European contexts confirms that employee satisfaction and motivation remain fundamentally connected to wages and benefits, professional development opportunities, working conditions, and leadership quality, indicating that total rewards frameworks must address both financial and environmental factors to achieve sustained employee commitment (Karácsony et al., 2025).

Marketing professionals’ perceptions of total rewards vary across sectors20. Various studies on marketing professionals working in the technology, retail and consumer goods sectors have revealed that the total rewards offered by the sectors have different priorities21. Their comprehensive analysis of 50,000 professional posts demonstrated the scalability of natural language processing approaches while identifying industry-specific patterns in professional discourse about workplace satisfaction and compensation expectations. Skill-based total rewards theory: theoretical boundaries, novel contributions, and derived hypotheses

SBTRT integrates established compensation theory with digital transformation dynamics to explain contemporary professional reward preferences. The theory builds upon four foundational frameworks while contributing three propositions that extend existing knowledge to address workplace challenges inadequately served by traditional theories. Rather than claiming wholesale theoretical novelty, SBTRT addresses specific explanatory limitations in existing compensation frameworks that become salient under conditions of rapid technological change limitations that the foundational theories were not designed to accommodate and that contextual adaptation alone cannot resolve.

Theoretical foundations

Skill-Based Pay Theory (Lawler, 1990; Ledford, 1995) established that compensation systems rewarding specific competencies outperform position-based structures. Human Capital Theory (Becker, 1964; Schultz, 1961) demonstrated the economic value of knowledge and skills as productive assets. Social Exchange Theory (Blau, 1964) explained how skill recognition influences employee-organization relationships through reciprocal exchange dynamics. Self-Determination Theory (Deci & Ryan, 1985) showed that reward systems supporting psychological needs generate superior motivational outcomes compared to purely extrinsic approaches.

Explanatory gaps in existing frameworks

While these foundational theories provide robust explanations for compensation dynamics in stable technological environments, they encounter specific explanatory limitations when applied to digitally transformed professional contexts limitations that constitute genuine theoretical gaps rather than mere contextual variations.

The first gap concerns skill valuation temporality. Traditional Human Capital Theory assumes that competency market values adjust gradually, with educational credentials and accumulated experience serving as reliable proxies for productive capacity (Becker, 1964). This assumption becomes problematic in digital professional contexts where specific technological competencies can appreciate or depreciate rapidly a phenomenon illustrated by the swift market valuation shifts surrounding skills in marketing automation, AI-driven analytics, and programmatic advertising. Existing theory lacks mechanisms for explaining how compensation systems should respond when the temporal dynamics of skill valuation fundamentally change, creating a gap between theoretical predictions of gradual adjustment and observed market behavior requiring continuous recalibration.

The second gap involves the skill-flexibility nexus. Self-Determination Theory explains why autonomy-supportive work environments enhance motivation (Deci & Ryan, 1985), and Skill-Based Pay Theory explains why competency recognition improves compensation satisfaction (Lawler, 1990). However, neither framework nor their combination explains why specific categories of competencies generate distinctive autonomy expectations that existing theories treat as unrelated to skill type. Digital competencies, by their nature, enable location-independent work execution and asynchronous collaboration in ways that traditional marketing competencies (e.g., in-person client management, trade show coordination) do not. This creates a theoretically underspecified relationship between competency type and flexibility expectations that existing frameworks address inadequately.

The third gap relates to cross-sectoral skill valuation heterogeneity. Traditional compensation theory treats industry context as a moderating variable affecting the magnitude of established relationships (Milkovich et al., 2014). However, the relationship between digital competencies and reward structures may differ qualitatively not merely quantitatively across sectors with different digital intensity levels. In technology-intensive contexts, digital competencies function as foundational professional requirements, whereas in sectors such as consumer goods, they may operate as differentiating capabilities. This qualitative distinction in how competencies relate to compensation structures represents an explanatory gap that moderation-based approaches within existing frameworks cannot fully capture.

SBTRT addresses these three gaps through propositions that specify mechanisms operating under conditions of digital transformation mechanisms that are not derivable from the foundational theories individually or in combination, yet remain conceptually continuous with their core constructs.

Distinctive theoretical boundaries

SBTRT differs from antecedent theories in three critical dimensions that correspond to the explanatory gaps identified above. First, traditional Skill-Based Pay Theory treats technological competencies as specialized expertise within broader skill portfolios, while SBTRT positions digital competencies as foundational professional requirements whose valuation dynamics differ qualitatively from traditional skill categories. This distinction reflects digital transformation fundamentally altering skill valuation hierarchies rather than adding new specialized categories to existing frameworks. Second, traditional Human Capital Theory assumes relatively stable skill market valuations changing gradually over extended periods, while SBTRT recognizes that digital competencies possess distinctive economic characteristics including rapid obsolescence potential and continuously evolving market valuation patterns. This temporal distinction requires adaptive compensation systems capable of rapid adjustment rather than static salary structures based on historical job evaluations. Third, traditional compensation frameworks treat workplace flexibility as universal employee benefits independent of specific competencies, while SBTRT positions flexibility as skill-contingent rewards that digital competency mastery functionally enables and legitimately requires. This conceptual shift recognizes that digital skills create professional autonomy expectations that organizations must address through comprehensive reward structures rather than discretionary benefits.

Novel theoretical propositions and derived hypotheses

While the research questions guide exploratory investigation of marketing professional discourse patterns, the three SBTRT propositions generate specific testable predictions about relationships between digital competencies, reward components, and professional satisfaction. Below, each proposition is presented alongside the formal hypotheses it logically yields. We emphasize that the current study provides a first empirical examination of these predictions through discourse analysis; the results should be understood as preliminary evidence informing continued theoretical development rather than definitive confirmation.

The Digital Skill-Reward Congruence Principle states that organizations in digitally intensive environments must provide comprehensive reward packages recognizing digital competencies as primary value determinants rather than supplementary capabilities. This extends Skill-Based Pay Theory by arguing that digital transformation fundamentally alters which competencies command premium compensation, elevating technological proficiency from specialized expertise to foundational professional requirements.

If digital competency recognition constitutes a primary driver of reward satisfaction as this principle proposes, then professionals who articulate digital competencies should evaluate their total rewards more favorably than those emphasizing traditional capabilities. Furthermore, this congruence effect should be most pronounced in digitally intensive industry contexts where the alignment between digital skills and organizational reward structures is strongest. These expectations yield the following hypotheses:

H1

Marketing professionals expressing digital competency discourse will demonstrate significantly higher positive sentiment toward total rewards packages compared to professionals emphasizing traditional marketing capabilities.

H2

The relationship between digital competency discourse and positive sentiment will vary systematically across industry sectors, with technology sector professionals demonstrating stronger associations than retail or consumer goods sectors.

The Market Value of Skills Principle asserts that digital competency market premiums increase proportionally to technological change velocity within specific industry contexts, creating dynamic compensation adjustment requirements that traditional pay structures cannot accommodate. This extends Human Capital Theory by incorporating temporal dynamics and market responsiveness factors, recognizing that digital skill valuation operates on fundamentally different temporal scales compared to traditional competencies.

If digital competencies command increasingly dynamic market premiums as this principle suggests, then professional discourse should reflect accelerating emphasis on digital skill compensation over time. Moreover, professionals possessing these high-market-value competencies should express stronger preferences for skill-based compensation mechanisms that reflect their evolving market worth rather than static traditional pay structures. These expectations generate the following hypotheses:

H3

The frequency of digital skills compensation discourse will increase significantly throughout the 2023 study period, reflecting accelerating market valuation of technological competencies.

H4

Marketing professionals discussing digital competencies will express stronger preferences for skill-based compensation mechanisms (premium salaries, performance bonuses, certification reimbursement) compared to traditional compensation structures.

The Skills-Based Flexibility Principle posits that digital competencies create professional autonomy expectations necessitating flexible work arrangements as essential reward components rather than discretionary benefits. This integrates Self-Determination Theory with Skill-Based Pay Theory, arguing that digital skill mastery generates psychological ownership over work processes that employees expect organizations to recognize through autonomy-supporting workplace arrangements.

If digital competency mastery generates autonomy expectations as this principle proposes, then flexible work arrangements should elicit stronger positive sentiment among digitally oriented professionals than among those emphasizing traditional capabilities. Additionally, the value placed on digital tool access a concrete expression of technology-mediated work autonomy should vary across sectors in proportion to industry digital intensity. These expectations yield the following hypotheses:

H5

Flexible work arrangement discourse will demonstrate stronger positive sentiment correlation among marketing professionals expressing digital competency preferences compared to those emphasizing traditional capabilities.

H6

Digital tool access discourse will correlate more strongly with positive sentiment in technology sector contexts compared to retail or consumer goods sectors, reflecting industry-specific digital intensity moderating effects.

These six hypotheses, derived progressively from the three SBTRT propositions, generate empirically testable predictions regarding relational dynamics that extant theoretical frameworks do not explicitly anticipate. Methodology

This study employs a comprehensive methodological framework to analyze marketing professionals’ perceptions of total rewards through social media discourse. Our data collection and analysis process focused on LinkedIn as the primary data source, encompassing 35,691 comments collected during the 2023 calendar.

Research design and occupational focus

The research design integrates three complementary computational approaches sentiment analysis, topic modeling, and named entity recognition to examine marketing professionals’ perceptions of total rewards through systematic analysis of LinkedIn discourse collected throughout the complete 2023 calendar year. Each analytical component captures a distinct dimension of professional discourse about compensation and benefits, enabling comprehensive examination of sentiment patterns, thematic evolution, and entity-level relationships within the dataset.

Rationale for marketing professional focus

The specific focus on marketing professionals represents a strategic methodological choice grounded in three empirical and theoretical considerations that distinguish this occupational group in their relationship with total rewards structures.

First, marketing professionals operate at the intersection of traditional business functions and emerging digital technologies, creating unique compensation challenges that reflect broader workplace transformation trends. The marketing profession has experienced particularly rapid digital transformation, with traditional advertising and brand management roles evolving into data-driven, technology-enabled positions requiring fundamentally new skill sets and competencies26,27. This transformation creates distinctive total rewards considerations including digital tool access, skills certification support, and technology-enabled performance incentives that may not be present in professional contexts with more stable technological requirements. Second, the marketing profession demonstrates exceptional heterogeneity in skill requirements across industry sectors, with technology sector marketing professionals requiring different competencies than those in retail or consumer goods contexts20,21. This sectoral variation enables systematic examination of how industry characteristics influence total rewards preferences, providing insights that extend beyond occupation-specific findings to broader questions about industry-compensation relationships. The structured variation across sectors creates natural comparative conditions supporting theoretical proposition testing. Third, marketing professionals maintain particularly active LinkedIn presence and engagement patterns, creating rich data sources for social media discourse analysis28. Workplace Learning Report identified marketing as among the most active professional categories on the platform in terms of content creation and professional networking engagement. The professional networking requirements inherent in marketing roles generate authentic discourse about career development, compensation expectations, and workplace satisfaction that may be less accessible in other occupational contexts with different professional communication patterns.

Data collection protocol and sample characteristics

The data collection process employed a systematic approach implemented through LinkedIn’s official API using Python’s linkedin-api library version 2.0.1, ensuring full adherence to platform terms of service and rate limiting requirements. The collection spanned the complete 2023 calendar year from January 1, 2023, to December 31, 2023, enabling comprehensive capture of marketing professionals’ total rewards discourse across multiple temporal contexts including seasonal variations in professional activity, quarterly business cycles, and annual compensation review periods.

Search strategy and keyword selection

The initial data identification phase utilized a targeted search strategy focusing on posts containing compensation and benefits-related terminology. The complete search algorithm employed two keyword tiers representing the exhaustive search strategy applied throughout data collection. Primary keywords included “total rewards,” “compensation,” “benefits,” “salary,” “work-life balance,” and “professional development,” representing core compensation discourse terminology identified through preliminary qualitative analysis of marketing professional LinkedIn content. Secondary keywords included “remote work,” “flexible hours,” “digital skills,” “career advancement,” and “employee satisfaction,” representing emerging themes in contemporary professional discourse about workplace rewards.

The keyword selection process was grounded in established total rewards literature and validated through preliminary content analysis of 500 marketing professional posts collected during pilot testing in December 2022. Related terms such as “wage,” “income,” and “earnings” were explicitly considered during keyword development but excluded from the final search strategy because preliminary analysis revealed these terms appeared predominantly in economic commentary and labor market discussions rather than authentic professional discourse about individual compensation satisfaction. The term “salary” was retained as it appeared consistently in personal career development discussions, while “wage” appeared primarily in policy discussions and economic analysis contexts not relevant to professional reward perceptions.

User identification and industry classification

The user identification protocol specifically targeted marketing professionals through profile-based filtering criteria implementing multiple verification stages. The system identified eligible participants based on job title indicators including “Marketing Manager,” “Digital Marketing Specialist,” “Marketing Director,” “Brand Manager,” “Marketing Coordinator,” and related variations representing primary marketing professional designations. Additional verification occurred through industry sector classification, with participants categorized into technology, retail, and consumer goods sectors based on their current employer information and professional background indicators.

The industry classification process identified these three sectors as the dominant employer categories for marketing professionals within the dataset, collectively representing 87% of identified marketing professional profiles. Healthcare sector marketing professionals represented 6% of initially identified profiles, education sector represented 4%, and nonprofit sector represented 3%. The decision to focus analytical attention on technology, retail, and consumer goods sectors reflects both their numerical dominance in the dataset and their theoretical relevance for examining digital transformation impacts on total rewards preferences. Healthcare, education, and nonprofit sector professionals were excluded from the final analytical sample to maintain sectoral coherence and enable meaningful comparative analysis across industries with comparable organizational structures and market dynamics. This exclusion represents a limitation acknowledged in the discussion and conclusion sections, with future research recommended to examine total rewards discourse patterns in these additional sectors.

Geographic scope and linguistic parameters

The geographic filtering protocol focused on English-language content from professionals based in North America, Europe, and Australia to ensure linguistic consistency and cultural relevance. This geographic scope encompassed the primary markets where the technology, retail, and consumer goods sectors demonstrate significant LinkedIn professional activity, ensuring representative coverage of marketing professional discourse patterns within these regions. Analytical framework

Our analysis incorporated three primary computational approaches designed to comprehensively analyze marketing professionals’ discourse about total rewards. The first approach centered on sentiment analysis, implementing a BERT-based model29 specifically fine-tuned for our research context. Using a carefully curated subset of 1,000 marketing professionals’ comments for manual labeling, we achieved exceptional model performance with a validation accuracy of 0.91 and an F1-score of 0.89 on our test set. To enhance the accuracy of sentiment classification, we developed a custom lexicon incorporating marketing-specific compensation terminology, ensuring precise capture of industry-specific nuances in the discourse.

The second component of our framework focused on topic modeling, employing both Latent Dirichlet Allocation (LDA) (Putri & Kusumaningrum,30 and BERTopic31 methodologies for comprehensive thematic analysis. Our LDA implementation was optimized through iterative testing, ultimately utilizing 15 topics based on coherence score optimization. The BERTopic approach provided valuable insights into the dynamic evolution of topics over time, while expert review by marketing compensation specialists ensured the validity and relevance of identified themes.

For our third analytical component, we developed a custom Named Entity Recognition (NER) (Shaip,32 model using spaCy’s modeling framework. This model was trained on 2,000 manually annotated marketing-specific comments, carefully selected to represent the diversity of our dataset. The model was designed to identify and classify four key entity categories: benefit types, company names, job roles, and industry sectors. Our NER implementation achieved a robust F1-score of 0.85 on the validation set, demonstrating strong performance in entity identification and classification.

Multivariate analytical procedures

To complement the bivariate analyses described above, the study implemented hierarchical regression modeling to examine the simultaneous and interactive effects of industry sector, benefit type, and digital skill discourse orientation on sentiment scores. This multivariate approach addresses the possibility that bivariate correlations between individual predictors and sentiment may be confounded by shared variance across predictors, and enables formal testing of theoretically predicted moderation effects.

Hierarchical regression specification

The dependent variable in all regression models was the continuous sentiment score assigned to each comment through the BERT-based sentiment classification model (range: -1.0 to + 1.0). The hierarchical regression proceeded in three steps. In Step 1, control variables were entered including comment length (log-transformed word count), temporal quarter (Q1–Q4, dummy-coded with Q1 as reference), and geographic region (North America, Europe, Australia, dummy-coded with North America as reference). In Step 2, the primary predictors were entered: industry sector (technology, retail, consumer goods, dummy-coded with consumer goods as reference), digital skill discourse orientation (binary: classified as digital skill discourse versus non-digital skill discourse based on the operational framework described in Sect. 3.4), and benefit type discussed (flexible work, digital tool allowance, skill development, creative freedom, dummy-coded with creative freedom as reference). In Step 3, interaction terms were entered to test theoretically predicted moderation effects: the interaction between digital skill discourse orientation and industry sector, testing whether the sentiment premium associated with digital competency discourse varies across industries as predicted by H2 and the Digital Skill-Reward Congruence Principle.

Moderated regression for sector-specific effects

Separate moderated regression analyses examined whether the relationship between specific benefit types and sentiment was conditioned by industry sector, directly testing H6’s prediction that digital tool access discourse correlates more strongly with positive sentiment in technology sector contexts. These analyses employed benefit type frequency as a continuous predictor, industry sector as a categorical moderator, and the benefit-by-sector interaction term as the focal test of moderation.

Robustness procedures

To ensure that regression findings were not driven by distributional assumptions or influential observations, the analyses employed heteroscedasticity-consistent standard errors (HC3 estimator) and conducted sensitivity analyses excluding the top and bottom 1% of sentiment score distributions. Variance inflation factor diagnostics were computed for all models to assess multicollinearity among predictors, with VIF values below 3.0 confirming acceptable collinearity levels across all model specifications.

Discussion

The analysis of marketing professionals’ discourse about employer-provided total rewards on LinkedIn provides significant insights into the evolving nature of organizational compensation preferences in the digital marketing era. The findings specifically address how marketing professionals evaluate and discuss the comprehensive benefit packages offered by their current employing organizations rather than external recognition systems or alternative employment opportunities. The organizational focus enables systematic examination of how different industry sectors structure their total rewards offerings and how marketing professionals respond to these employer-provided compensation packages. This approach provides actionable insights for organizations seeking to optimize their employee value propositions through strategic total rewards design and implementation.

Understanding marketing professionals’ total rewards discourse (RQ1)

Marketing professionals articulate sophisticated compensation frameworks through LinkedIn discourse, complicating assumptions that employees hold simple preference hierarchies ranking monetary rewards above non-financial benefits. The strong association between flexible work arrangements and positive sentiment (r = .48, p < .001), confirmed in multivariate analysis controlling for industry sector and digital skill orientation (β = 0.21, p < .001), challenges traditional compensation theory positioning workplace flexibility as supplementary benefits rather than core reward components.

This finding engages ongoing debates about total rewards conceptualization. Armstrong and Brown’s (2006) influential framework treated flexibility as a discretionary benefit separate from core compensation, while Heneman and Ledford (1998) positioned non-monetary rewards as secondary considerations after establishing competitive base pay. The discourse patterns identified in this study suggest that marketing professionals, in their public professional communications, reject these hierarchical frameworks, instead articulating flexibility as an integral reward component deserving equal consideration with monetary compensation.

A necessary interpretive caveat applies to this finding and to the broader discourse-based results presented throughout this discussion. LinkedIn constitutes a professional identity marketplace where discourse serves career positioning functions alongside and potentially in tension with authentic preference expression (Goffman, 1959). The emphasis on flexibility and digital skill recognition in professional discourse may reflect both genuine preference evolution and strategic professional signaling, as marketing professionals foreground reward categories that project technological sophistication and contemporary professional identity. Consequently, the patterns identified in this study are most accurately characterized as publicly signaled reward preferences rather than direct indicators of latent individual attitudes. This framing does not diminish the analytical value of the findings publicly articulated preferences shape organizational discourse, influence compensation benchmarking, and contribute to emergent professional norms that eventually influence formal reward structures but it does require interpretive caution when drawing inferences about underlying individual preference hierarchies.

This differential aligns with broader methodological debates about authentic attitude measurement, as professionals have been shown to express substantially more negative workplace sentiment through social media compared to formal surveys3, while employer-administered surveys systematically overestimate compensation satisfaction relative to independent assessments2. These methodological variations raise fundamental questions about preference measurement validity. Do discourse-based findings reveal authentic preferences that surveys suppress through social desirability bias, or do different contexts elicit different strategic presentations rather than tapping into singular authentic preferences? We resist simple claims that either methodology accesses “true” preferences, instead proposing that compensation preferences may be context-dependent and strategically constructed rather than stable individual attributes that research methodologies accurately or inaccurately measure. LinkedIn discourse and formal surveys may capture complementary facets of professional reward orientations, with neither providing privileged access to a unified underlying preference structure.

The sophisticated digital skill-based compensation frameworks evident in professional discourse extend recent theoretical work on skill-based pay evolution. Cantrell and colleagues34 argued that skill-based organizational models require fundamental compensation restructuring, while Lawler’s (2000) updated skill-based pay framework emphasized competency recognition over traditional job evaluation approaches. The discourse patterns identified in this study are consistent with these theoretical developments, suggesting that marketing professionals have independently developed comparable conceptual frameworks evident in how they discuss digital competency-compensation relationships through peer discourse.

This convergence between theoretical developments and professional discourse patterns is suggestive of a dynamic in which compensation theory developments may reflect rather than drive professional community understanding. However, establishing the directionality of this relationship would require longitudinal designs beyond the scope of the current study.

Industry-specific variations in total rewards perception (RQ2)

The significant variation in sentiment across industries (F(2,35688) = 27.32, p < .001), confirmed in multivariate analysis controlling for digital skill discourse orientation and benefit type (see Sect. 4.6), provides empirical evidence supporting industry-specific approaches to marketing compensation. These patterns are consistent with SBTRT’s proposition that sectoral characteristics moderate skill-based reward preferences. Technology sector marketing professionals demonstrated higher positive sentiment (M = 0.45, SD = 0.28) compared to retail (M = 0.38, SD = 0.31) and consumer goods (M = 0.33, SD = 0.29), representing a meaningful sentiment differential that exceeds industry variation patterns reported in previous compensation research.

These industry-specific sentiment patterns converge with and extend findings from Bradley and Gilmartin’s (2024) analysis of high-impact rewards across sectors, which identified technology sector organizations as more likely to implement comprehensive total rewards packages including flexible work arrangements and digital tool access. Notably, the magnitude of sentiment variation identified in this study (0.12 point difference between technology and consumer goods sectors on the standardized sentiment scale) exceeds the satisfaction differential Bradley and Gilmartin reported through structured organizational surveys (0.06 point difference on comparable scales). This discrepancy may reflect either stronger authentic industry effects captured through discourse analysis or differential performative dynamics across sectors, as technology sector professionals may face stronger professional norms favoring positive articulation of digital reward packages.

The finding that technology sector marketing professionals express higher positive sentiment is consistent with the expectation that digitally intensive industries provide organizational environments more closely aligned with contemporary professional reward expectations. This pattern aligns with Văcărasu’s (2023) research on workplace trends among information technology professionals in Romania, which identified that technology sector employees prioritize flexibility, team quality, and fairness over traditional monetary rewards. The convergence across different geographic contexts and methodological approaches increases confidence that technology sector characteristics influence professional reward discourse, though the possibility of sector-specific performative norms cannot be excluded.

The lower sentiment levels among consumer goods sector marketing professionals (M = 0.33) compared to technology (M = 0.45) and retail (M = 0.38) sectors diverge from expectations based on traditional compensation frameworks, which would predict that established consumer goods corporations with robust benefits packages would generate higher employee satisfaction. This pattern suggests that contemporary marketing professionals may prioritize characteristics associated with technology sector employment including innovation culture, rapid career advancement opportunities, and digital tool access over the employment stability and comprehensive traditional benefits typically associated with established consumer goods corporations. However, this interpretation must be qualified by acknowledging that consumer goods sector professionals may face different LinkedIn engagement incentives than technology sector professionals, potentially expressing satisfaction differently rather than experiencing it differently. This finding extends Errida and Lotfi’s (2021) research on organizational change management by suggesting that industry-specific change velocities may influence how marketing professionals articulate satisfaction through reward preference alignment.

It is important to note that the cross-sectional nature of these industry comparisons precludes causal inference about whether industry context produces sentiment differences or whether professionals with pre-existing preference profiles self-select into specific industries. The observed patterns are consistent with both explanations, and disentangling these mechanisms would require longitudinal tracking of professionals transitioning across sectors.

Potential sub-group variations within sectors

Within each industry sector, further variation likely exists between marketing professionals operating in business-to-business (B2B) versus business-to-consumer (B2C) contexts. B2B marketing professionals typically engage in longer sales cycles, relationship-driven strategies, and technical content development, which may generate distinct reward preferences emphasizing professional development, industry conference access, and client relationship management support. In contrast, B2C marketing professionals often prioritize creative freedom, campaign performance bonuses, and access to consumer analytics platforms. The distinct skill profiles and performance measurement criteria characterizing these two marketing domains suggest that total rewards packages optimized for B2B contexts may differ substantively from those generating satisfaction in B2C environments.

This sub-group distinction represents a theoretically motivated extension that the current dataset cannot empirically adjudicate. LinkedIn profile information does not consistently distinguish B2B and B2C orientations, and employer-level classification introduces substantial ambiguity for companies operating across both domains. Preliminary qualitative examination of discourse patterns offers suggestive but not systematic indications that B2B-oriented comments emphasize relationship continuity, thought leadership development, and account-based marketing tool access more frequently than B2C-oriented comments, which more commonly reference creative campaign recognition, consumer insight platforms, and social media engagement metrics. We flag this variation as a priority for future research employing supplementary classification criteria, such as employer product category analysis, self-reported role descriptions, or industry-specific keyword clustering, rather than presenting it as an empirically grounded finding of the current study.

Industry-specific benefit valuations (RQ2b)

The association between digital tool allowance discourse and positive sentiment (r = .43, p < .001), confirmed in multivariate analysis and particularly pronounced within the technology sector, indicates that industry-specific tools and resources represent important predictors of marketing professional sentiment beyond generic benefit categories. The moderated regression analysis (Sect. 4.6.2) provides formal confirmation that the relationship between digital tool discourse and positive sentiment is conditioned by industry sector, with the technology sector demonstrating significantly stronger associations than consumer goods contexts, and non-overlapping confidence intervals confirming that this moderation effect is statistically meaningful.

This finding provides quantitative evidence consistent with Klompen and Walsh’s (2023) qualitative conclusions that digital talent prioritizes access to cutting-edge technology tools, while extending those conclusions through systematic measurement of the strength and sector-contingent nature of tool access-sentiment relationships. The technology sector demonstrated the strongest association between digital tool allowances and positive sentiment (r = .51, p < .001), compared to the retail sector correlation of r = .38 and the consumer goods sector correlation of r = .31. This variation in association strength across sectors is consistent with SBTRT’s proposition that industry characteristics moderate the relationship between digital skill recognition and reward satisfaction, with digitally intensive industries demonstrating stronger skill-reward congruence effects.

These industry-specific patterns diverge from Terera and Ngirande’s (2014) cross-sectional survey findings examining 200 marketing professionals, which reported relatively uniform relationships between benefit categories and job satisfaction across industry contexts. The divergence may reflect either genuine industry-specific variation that traditional survey formats constrain respondents from expressing, or methodological differences between discourse-based association measurement and structured questionnaire approaches. Both explanations are plausible, and the current study cannot adjudicate between them.

The finding that skill development benefits demonstrate positive sentiment associations in consumer goods sectors (r = .42) comparable to technology sectors (r = .44), despite lower overall sentiment levels, suggests that professional development opportunities represent broadly valued reward components across industry contexts. This pattern aligns with McIlveen and colleagues’ (2021) findings that career development opportunities significantly influence job satisfaction across diverse professional contexts.

Evolution of marketing benefits preferences (RQ3)

The substantial shift in discourse toward digital marketing skills compensation (+ 31.2%) and away from traditional marketing role discussions (-15.3%) represents a notable transformation in how marketing professionals publicly articulate career value and compensation expectations. This evolution, identified through topic modeling analysis demonstrating statistically significant thematic shifts (χ²(4) = 182.45, p < .001), is consistent with SBTRT’s Market Value of Skills Principle, which predicts that digital competency market premiums increase proportionally to technological change velocity.

The 31.2% increase in digital skills discourse throughout 2023 exceeds the temporal evolution patterns Smet, Dowling, and Lim (2022) identified in their comprehensive survey of 1,200 marketing professionals across technology and retail sectors conducted in 2021. Their survey-based analysis identified a 12% increase in emphasis on digital competencies over a comparable twelve-month period. The magnitude difference between these studies may reflect a combination of accelerating digital transformation trends between 2021 and 2023, methodological differences in measuring preference evolution through organic discourse versus structured questionnaire responses, and the possibility that LinkedIn discourse amplifies emerging professional norms around digital skill emphasis through social reinforcement mechanisms not present in private survey contexts. These explanations are not mutually exclusive, and the current study cannot isolate the contribution of each factor.

The declining emphasis on traditional marketing discourse (-15.3%) converges with Gillpatrick’s (2019) theoretical analysis of digital transformation’s impact on marketing practice, which anticipated that traditional advertising and brand management approaches would diminish in perceived professional value as data-driven digital strategies gained prominence. The discourse patterns identified in this study are consistent with this theoretical anticipation, though the causal mechanisms remain underspecified the decline in traditional marketing discourse may reflect genuine devaluation of these competencies in compensation markets, shifting professional signaling norms on LinkedIn, or a combination of both dynamics.

The temporal evolution patterns identified in this study contrast with the relatively stable preference structures reported in WorldatWork’s (2022) Total Rewards Inventory, which found limited year-over-year variation in marketing professionals’ stated compensation priorities. This divergence is consistent with the possibility that formal survey contexts underestimate the dynamic nature of professional preference evolution, potentially because structured questionnaires measuring predetermined categories cannot capture emerging compensation preferences until they have already become established. However, it is equally possible that discourse-based measurement amplifies emerging trends through platform-specific dynamics, and the “true” rate of preference evolution falls between the estimates derived from these methodologically distinct approaches.

Temporal changes in benefits valuation (RQ3b)

The increase in discussions about skill development benefits (r = .39, p < .001) and creative freedom (r = .35, p < .001) indicates growing emphasis on growth opportunities and professional autonomy in marketing professionals’ public reward discourse. These patterns challenge traditional marketing compensation models emphasizing fixed compensation structures and standardized benefits packages, and are consistent with SBTRT’s integration of Self-Determination Theory principles, which predict that contemporary professionals increasingly prioritize rewards supporting competence development and autonomous work arrangements.

The skill development benefits correlation identified in this study (r = .39) exceeds the professional development-satisfaction relationship Hoole and Hotz35 reported in their analysis of total reward system impacts on work engagement (r = .28). This difference may reflect strengthening of the development-satisfaction relationship as digital transformation accelerated skill obsolescence concerns between 2016 and 2023, though cross-study comparison must be approached cautiously given differences in measurement methodology, sample composition, and geographic context.

The creative freedom association (r = .35) aligns with36 findings that social recognition and employee engagement demonstrate significant positive relationships in organizational contexts. The current study extends those findings by suggesting that creative autonomy may represent a distinct reward category valued alongside but separately from social recognition, with marketing professionals articulating specific preferences for organizational support of creative processes rather than merely desiring acknowledgment of creative outputs.

The temporal evolution showing increased emphasis on both skill development and creative freedom suggests that marketing professionals’ public discourse increasingly emphasizes comprehensive professional growth environments supporting both technical competency advancement and creative expression opportunities. This dual emphasis diverges from traditional compensation frameworks treating technical training and creative autonomy as alternative benefit categories appealing to different employee segments, suggesting instead that digital-era marketing professionals may view technological competence and creative capability as complementary rather than competing professional attributes though this interpretation warrants empirical confirmation through individual-level preference elicitation studies.

Theoretical contributions and preliminary empirical support for SBTRT

This research offers three contributions advancing Skill-Based Total Rewards Theory: preliminary empirical support through discourse-based operationalization, industry-specific theoretical refinement, and methodological innovation establishing new frameworks for testing compensation theory through professional discourse analysis.

Preliminary empirical support and quantitative operationalization

The study provides the first systematic empirical examination of SBTRT propositions through analysis of authentic professional discourse patterns, transforming abstract theoretical propositions into quantifiable relationships enabling structured testing. The identification of specific association patterns between digital skill discourse and positive sentiment (r = .48 for flexible work arrangements, r = .43 for digital tool allowances) operationalizes the Digital Skill-Reward Congruence Principle through quantitative measurement, while the hierarchical regression analysis (Sect. 4.6) confirms that these associations persist when modeled simultaneously with industry sector and other predictors.

The results are consistent with the Digital Skill-Reward Congruence Principle’s prediction that marketing professionals articulating digital competencies express more positive sentiment toward total rewards when organizations provide skill-specific recognition. The multivariate finding that digital skill discourse orientation interacts with industry sector to predict sentiment (ΔR² = 0.04, ΔF(2, 35677) = 828.91, p < .001) provides stronger evidence for this proposition than bivariate correlations alone, as it demonstrates that the digital skill–sentiment relationship varies systematically by sector after controlling for confounds.

The 31.2% increase in digital skills discourse throughout 2023 is consistent with the Market Value of Skills Principle, suggesting temporal patterns aligned with theoretical predictions about skill-based compensation evolution. The systematic increase in digital competency discussions across all three industry sectors suggests that market-driven skill valuation changes operate consistently across organizational contexts, though the single-year observation window limits confidence in distinguishing sustained trends from transient fluctuations.

The Skills-Based Flexibility Principle receives preliminary support through the identification of industry-specific patterns in flexible work arrangement discourse, with technology sector professionals demonstrating higher positive sentiment (M = 0.45) compared to retail (M = 0.38) and consumer goods (M = 0.33) sectors. The moderated regression analysis confirming sector-specific variation in the digital tool access–sentiment relationship provides formal evidence that SBTRT relationships are conditioned by industry context.

We characterize these findings as preliminary empirical support rather than theoretical validation for three reasons. First, the correlational and cross-sectional design precludes causal inference about the mechanisms SBTRT proposes. The theory specifies directional relationships for instance, that digital skill mastery generates flexibility expectations but the observed associations are consistent with multiple causal orderings. Second, the single-year observation period limits assessment of the temporal dynamics central to the Market Value of Skills Principle, which predicts increasing digital skill premiums over time in proportion to technological change velocity. Third, the performative characteristics of LinkedIn discourse introduce uncertainty about whether identified patterns reflect underlying preference structures or publicly constructed professional identity positions. While these limitations do not invalidate the empirical contributions, they establish boundary conditions for theoretical inference and underscore the need for longitudinal, multi-method research to advance SBTRT toward more robust empirical standing.

Industry-specific theoretical extensions and moderating factors

The research contributes to theoretical refinement through identification of industry-specific moderating factors that condition SBTRT applications across sectoral contexts. The systematic variation in sentiment patterns across technology, retail, and consumer goods sectors, confirmed through both ANOVA and hierarchical regression analyses, reveals that SBTRT relationships differ depending on industry characteristics.

Technology sector emphasis on digital tool access and flexible work arrangements, retail sector focus on performance-based compensation, and consumer goods sector emphasis on career development opportunities represent industry-specific patterns that can inform continued SBTRT development. These sectoral patterns suggest that digital intensity, customer interaction requirements, and organizational change velocity may serve as moderating variables influencing which specific reward components demonstrate strongest associations with expressed professional sentiment within skill-based compensation frameworks.

The identification of distinct reward discourse patterns across industry sectors extends SBTRT beyond universal propositions to context-dependent applications. The technology sector’s stronger association between digital tool allowances and positive sentiment compared to consumer goods sectors demonstrates that industry-specific digital intensity conditions skill-reward congruence effects, suggesting that organizations may need to calibrate total rewards packages based on sectoral characteristics and workforce digital competency expectations.

Methodological innovation for theoretical development

The study contributes methodological approaches for theoretical development through integration of natural language processing techniques with professional discourse analysis, providing frameworks for examining theoretical propositions through systematic analysis of organic professional expression patterns. The combination of sentiment analysis, topic modeling, named entity recognition, and multivariate regression applied to authentic professional discourse creates analytical capabilities for examining theoretical propositions within specific professional contexts that complement traditional survey methodologies.

The development of marketing-specific sentiment analysis models represents methodological innovation enabling domain-specific theoretical testing through custom lexicon development and model calibration. The integration of multiple analytical techniques within a comprehensive validation framework combining computational performance assessment, qualitative expert review, and multivariate statistical analysis provides methodological advancement applicable to future theoretical testing across diverse professional contexts.

Ethical considerations, responsible implementation, and critical reflections on workplace discourse analytics

The application of social media discourse analysis to human resource decision-making raises fundamental questions about surveillance, consent, privacy boundaries, and the potential for data misuse that extend beyond traditional research ethics frameworks into contemporary debates about algorithmic management and digital workplace monitoring. This research engages substantively with these critical concerns while acknowledging the inherent tensions between organizational knowledge generation and employee rights.

Critical engagement with surveillance and algorithmic management debates

The systematic analysis of professional discourse for organizational research purposes operates within contested terrain where legitimate market intelligence gathering intersects with potentially problematic employee monitoring practices. Recent scholarship on algorithmic management (Kellogg, Valentine, & Christin, 2020) demonstrates that computational analysis of employee-generated data enables increasingly sophisticated surveillance capabilities that fundamentally alter power dynamics within employment relationships, creating asymmetric information conditions where organizations possess comprehensive insights about employee attitudes while individuals lack comparable organizational transparency.

The current research methodology focuses deliberately on aggregate pattern identification rather than individual employee assessment, establishing clear boundaries between appropriate strategic market intelligence and inappropriate individual surveillance. However, this methodological choice does not eliminate ethical concerns, as the identical analytical techniques employed for population-level pattern identification could be redirected toward individual employee monitoring with minimal technical modification. This dual-use potential creates ethical obligations extending beyond the immediate research application to consider how similar methodologies might be deployed in organizational contexts lacking comparable ethical constraints.

Consent, contextual integrity, and data governance

The ethical framework of this research must contend with the concept of contextual integrity (Nissenbaum, 2010), which holds that privacy expectations are governed by context-specific norms about information flows rather than by binary public-private distinctions. LinkedIn users share professional content within a context governed by norms of professional networking, career development, and peer engagement. The systematic aggregation and computational analysis of this content for compensation research purposes represents a different information flow than users likely anticipated when composing their posts even though the content is technically public.

This contextual integrity concern has practical implications for organizational adoption of discourse analytics. When organizations implement social media monitoring systems to inform compensation strategy, they alter the informational norms governing professional social media participation. If professionals become aware that their LinkedIn discourse influences organizational compensation decisions, this awareness may modify their expression patterns in ways that undermine the methodological rationale for discourse analysis namely, that organic professional discourse captures preferences less constrained by social desirability bias than formal surveys. The recursive relationship between monitoring awareness and expression behavior creates a fundamental epistemological challenge for sustained organizational application of discourse analytics methodologies.

Furthermore, existing data governance frameworks, including the General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR) in European contexts, establish legal requirements regarding the processing of personal data that intersect with discourse analytics in complex ways. While aggregated and anonymized analysis of public professional content may fall within permissible research use, organizational application of comparable techniques for human resource decision-making enters regulated territory where consent requirements, data minimization principles, and purpose limitation obligations may apply. Organizations contemplating implementation of discourse-based compensation analytics should obtain legal counsel regarding jurisdiction-specific data governance requirements and should establish explicit data governance protocols addressing collection scope, retention periods, access controls, and permissible use limitations.

Blurred boundaries between professional self-presentation and authentic sentiment

The methodological assumption that LinkedIn discourse reflects authentic professional sentiment requires critical examination considering the performative nature of professional social media communication. Goffman’s (1959) theoretical framework on impression management demonstrates that individuals strategically construct public presentations of professional identity designed to advance career objectives rather than expressing unfiltered authentic perspectives. Marketing professionals engaging in LinkedIn discourse may emphasize particular compensation preferences to signal professional sophistication or align with perceived industry norms rather than expressing genuine personal preferences.

The performative dynamics of LinkedIn discourse extend beyond sentiment expression to influence which reward preferences gain visibility in professional discourse. Marketing professionals may strategically foreground preferences for digital skill recognition and flexible work arrangements topics signaling technological sophistication and alignment with contemporary professional norms while underrepresenting preferences for traditional monetary compensation, job security, or conventional benefits that may be perceived as professionally conservative or insufficiently forward-looking. This selective visibility creates potential asymmetry between publicly expressed and privately held reward preferences.

In anonymous channels such as platforms like Glassdoor, Blind, or confidential organizational engagement surveys professionals may more freely articulate preferences for base salary increases, job stability assurances, or traditional benefits that they strategically minimize in LinkedIn’s professional identity marketplace. The implication is twofold. First, the strong emphasis on digital skills and flexible work arrangements identified in this study may partially reflect professional signaling behavior alongside authentic preference evolution. Second, the relatively muted discourse around traditional compensation structures may understate the continued importance of base salary competitiveness and financial security as foundational reward expectations.

This tension between authentic expression and strategic self-presentation shapes how organizational decision-makers should weight discourse-derived insights. Organizations implementing discourse analysis insights should treat these findings as reflecting publicly constructed professional identity positions that complement rather than replace confidential assessment instruments capturing the full spectrum of reward valuations, including preferences professionals may strategically minimize in public professional contexts.

Power asymmetries and employee voice

An additional ethical dimension concerns the power asymmetries inherent in organizational discourse analytics. When organizations analyze employee social media discourse to inform compensation strategy, they exercise informational power that employees may not be aware of and cannot meaningfully consent to or opt out of, given the professional necessity of LinkedIn participation in many industries. This asymmetry is particularly pronounced for marketing professionals, whose occupational roles require active LinkedIn engagement, effectively limiting their ability to withdraw from potential monitoring without professional cost.

The normative question of whether organizations should monitor professional social media discourse for compensation purposes even through aggregate anonymized analysis remains contested within the organizational ethics literature. Arguments favoring such monitoring emphasize improved organizational responsiveness to employee needs and evidence-based compensation strategy development. Arguments opposing it emphasize employee autonomy, the chilling effects of surveillance awareness on authentic professional expression, and the potential for discriminatory application if discourse patterns correlate with protected characteristics.

This study does not resolve this normative debate but acknowledges that the analytical techniques demonstrated herein carry responsibilities extending beyond research ethics to encompass organizational governance, employee relations, and workplace democracy considerations.

Responsible implementation framework and governance requirements

Organizations seeking to apply social media discourse analysis insights require comprehensive ethical frameworks distinguishing between appropriate strategic applications and inappropriate individual monitoring practices. Appropriate strategic applications include analyzing industry-wide compensation trends across professional populations, understanding sector-specific professional preferences to guide total rewards strategy development, and identifying emerging benefit categories through market-level discourse patterns. These applications focus on market intelligence development rather than individual employee assessment.

Inappropriate applications requiring explicit prohibition include individual employee social media monitoring for performance evaluation purposes, using social media discourse patterns to identify potentially dissatisfied employees for targeted retention interventions or preemptive termination decisions, and implementing discriminatory practices based on social media discourse patterns correlating with protected demographic characteristics.

The implementation framework requires robust organizational governance structures including oversight committees with human resource professionals, legal counsel, employee representatives, and data ethics specialists monitoring appropriate use of discourse analysis findings. These governance structures should establish explicit policies regarding data collection scope, analytical boundaries, retention limitations, and prohibited applications, with regular audit procedures ensuring compliance.

Advancing responsible practice through transparent communication

Organizations implementing insights derived from social media discourse analysis should establish transparent communication policies informing employees about the general use of industry discourse analysis for compensation strategy development. This transparency should include clear distinctions between analyzing public professional discourse trends for strategic purposes and monitoring individual employee social media activity for assessment purposes. The communication framework should emphasize that organizational compensation strategy development incorporates insights from multiple data sources including industry research, competitive market analysis, and professional discourse trends, situating discourse analysis within broader compensation research practices.

Conclusion

Drawing from our analysis of 35,691 LinkedIn comments by marketing professionals, this research provides insights into how total rewards perceptions are publicly articulated in the contemporary marketing landscape. By addressing our three research questions through integrated computational analysis, we have identified patterns in marketing professionals’ discourse about compensation and benefits, with particular emphasis on the role of digital transformation in shaping publicly expressed reward preferences.

Our sentiment analysis revealed substantial variations across industry sectors (F(2,35688) = 27.32, p < .001), with technology sector marketing professionals demonstrating notably higher positive sentiment (M = 0.45, SD = 0.28) toward their total rewards packages. Hierarchical regression analysis confirmed that these sector differences persist when modeled simultaneously with digital skill discourse orientation and benefit type, while revealing significant interaction effects (ΔR² = 0.04, p < .001) indicating that digital competency emphasis differentially predicts sentiment across industry contexts. These findings directly address RQ2, highlighting the role of industry context in shaping compensation discourse patterns. The strong association between flexible work arrangements and positive sentiment (r = .48, p < .001) further emphasizes the evolving nature of publicly expressed value perceptions among marketing professionals.

In response to RQ3, our topic modeling analysis identified a significant shift toward digital marketing skills compensation discourse (+ 31.2%) and away from traditional marketing role discussions (-15.3%). This evolution in discourse themes (χ²(4) = 182.45, p < .001) indicates a transformation in how marketing professionals publicly conceptualize career value and articulate compensation expectations.

These findings provide preliminary empirical support for the three propositions of Skill-Based Total Rewards Theory, while establishing boundary conditions for its application across industry sectors. We characterize this support as preliminary because the correlational design precludes causal inference, the single-year observation period limits temporal generalization, and the performative characteristics of LinkedIn discourse introduce interpretive complexity regarding the relationship between publicly articulated preferences and underlying attitude structures.

Research limitations and boundary conditions

This research demonstrates methodological innovation while acknowledging specific limitations affecting interpretation and generalizability.

Sample representativeness and geographic constraints

The study’s exclusive focus on English-language discourse from professionals in North America, Europe, and Australia creates geographic and linguistic limitations constraining generalizability to emerging economy contexts and non-English-speaking professional populations. The absence of emerging economy professionals from Asia, Latin America, and Africa represents a significant limitation, as digital transformation dynamics, labor market conditions, cultural workplace norms, and professional social media engagement patterns may differ substantially from those observed in developed English-speaking markets. The over-representation of professionals with advanced educational backgrounds (68% possessing bachelor’s degrees or higher) may not reflect global marketing professional demographic composition.

Platform selection bias and self-selection effects

The LinkedIn platform introduces multiple layers of selection bias requiring explicit acknowledgment. First, professionals who maintain active LinkedIn engagement may hold inherently more positive attitudes toward technology-mediated professional communication, potentially inflating observed emphasis on digital skills discourse and flexible work arrangement preferences. Second, professionals who comment on compensation-related content represent a self-selected subset of LinkedIn users who may hold stronger opinions about total rewards than the broader marketing professional population. Third, platform-specific norms governing professional discourse on LinkedIn including implicit expectations about professional tone, topical relevance, and career positioning shape expression patterns in ways that platform-neutral preference measurement would not. These self-selection and platform effects introduce systematic biases that limit the generalizability of findings to the broader population of marketing professionals, including those who prefer alternative professional networking platforms, who maintain passive rather than active LinkedIn presence, or who discuss compensation preferences primarily through private channels.

Sectoral scope

The exclusion of healthcare, education, and nonprofit sector marketing professionals represents a deliberate analytical choice enabling sectoral coherence while creating boundaries limiting theoretical generalization across all industry contexts. These sectors may exhibit different organizational structures, compensation constraints, and professional culture characteristics that produce distinct total rewards discourse patterns.

Temporal context and dual transformation

The study’s temporal scope encompasses a post-pandemic period where COVID-19 had already fundamentally altered workplace expectations regarding flexibility and remote work arrangements, creating interpretive complexity in distinguishing digital transformation effects from pandemic-induced workplace evolution. The findings reflect the combined influence of technological change and pandemic-accelerated workplace transformation rather than isolated digitalization effects. The single-year data collection period throughout 2023 provides robust cross-sectional analysis while constraining longitudinal interpretation.

Correlational design and causal limitations

The correlational and cross-sectional design constitutes a fundamental limitation for theoretical inference. SBTRT specifies directional causal mechanisms for instance, that digital skill mastery generates flexibility expectations but the observed associations are consistent with multiple causal orderings. Professionals who value flexibility may gravitate toward digital skill development because these competencies enable flexible work, rather than developing flexibility expectations as a consequence of digital skill acquisition. Similarly, industry-sector differences in sentiment may reflect self-selection of professionals with particular preference profiles into specific industries rather than industry context producing preference differences. Establishing causal directionality requires experimental or longitudinal designs beyond the scope of this study.

Performative discourse and interpretive constraints

As discussed extensively in Sect. 5.7, the performative nature of LinkedIn discourse means that publicly expressed reward preferences may not fully represent the complete spectrum of authentic professional compensation valuations. Professionals may strategically foreground certain preferences while minimizing others in professional networking contexts, creating potential asymmetry between publicly articulated and privately held reward orientations. The findings are most accurately interpreted as reflecting publicly constructed professional identity positions regarding total rewards rather than direct measures of latent individual attitudes.

Construct validity implications of performative professional discourse

The performative nature of professional social media discourse carries fundamental construct validity implications that warrant explicit and reflective examination. The central dependent construct of this study “total rewards perceptions” was operationalized through computational analysis of LinkedIn comments rather than through validated psychometric instruments designed to measure individual attitudinal constructs. This operationalization introduces a critical distinction between discourse about total rewards and perceptions of total rewards as traditionally conceptualized in organizational behavior research. Specifically, the dataset captures publicly articulated professional commentary on compensation-related topics, but it remains empirically indeterminate whether these comments reflect (a) authentic evaluations of one’s own organization’s rewards system, (b) general opinions about industry compensation trends, (c) professional signaling behavior intended to cultivate a particular professional identity, or (d) reactive commentary on content posted by other users rather than expressions of personal experience. This ambiguity fundamentally affects the construct validity of the study’s central dependent concept and necessitates interpretive caution.

Drawing on Goffman’s (1959) dramaturgical framework, LinkedIn functions as a “front stage” environment in which professionals perform curated versions of their occupational identities for audiences comprising colleagues, recruiters, and industry peers. Within this performative context, expressions of reward preferences are not value-neutral reports of internal states but strategic communicative acts embedded in professional self-presentation logics. For marketing professionals in particular whose occupational competencies center on strategic communication and audience management the performative dimension of LinkedIn discourse may be especially pronounced, as these professionals possess heightened expertise in crafting messages calibrated to specific audiences and platforms. Consequently, the construct captured in this study is more precisely characterized as “publicly performed total rewards discourse” rather than “total rewards perceptions” in the psychometric sense, and the findings should be interpreted accordingly as patterns within professional discourse ecosystems rather than as direct windows into individual attitudinal structures.

This construct validity consideration has cascading implications for several empirical claims advanced in this study. The strong positive association between flexible work arrangement discourse and positive sentiment (r = .48, p < .001), for instance, may reflect not only authentic preference strength but also the social desirability of expressing pro-flexibility positions within contemporary professional networking norms, where flexibility advocacy signals progressive workplace values and technological adaptability. Similarly, the observed decline in traditional marketing discourse (− 15.3%) may partially represent shifting professional signaling conventions whereby publicly emphasizing traditional competencies carries reputational risk in digitally oriented professional communities rather than a straightforward devaluation of these competencies in professionals’ private reward calculations. The industry-sector sentiment differences reported in Sect. 5.2 may likewise reflect sector-specific performative norms (e.g., technology professionals facing stronger expectations to publicly celebrate digital reward packages) alongside genuine sectoral variation in reward satisfaction.

Furthermore, the unit of analysis introduces additional construct validity concerns. The study analyzes individual LinkedIn comments rather than identifiable individual professionals, meaning that the independence of observations cannot be guaranteed. Multiple comments from the same user may appear in the dataset, introducing potential clustering effects that inflate statistical significance and violate the independence assumption underlying the reported inferential statistics. Without user-level identifiers, it is not possible to model within-person consistency or account for individual-level confounds, which further constrains the degree to which observed discourse patterns can be attributed to population-level preference structures.

Notwithstanding these construct validity limitations, it is important to note that the performative dimension of professional discourse does not render the findings analytically vacuous. Publicly performed reward preferences constitute a substantively meaningful phenomenon in their own right: they shape professional norms, influence organizational compensation discourse, and contribute to the social construction of reward expectations within occupational communities (Marwick & Boyd, 2011). The patterns identified in this study thus retain analytical significance as evidence of how total rewards are publicly negotiated and valorized within marketing professional communities, even if they cannot be straightforwardly equated with latent individual attitudes. Future research should pursue convergent validity strategies including triangulation with anonymous survey instruments, incentive-compatible preference elicitation, and cross-platform comparative analysis to establish the degree of correspondence between publicly performed reward discourse and privately held reward preferences, thereby advancing construct refinement for discourse-based organizational research.

Future research directions

Multimethod causal inference research

Future research should prioritize designs capable of establishing causal directionality for the relationships identified in this study. Quasi-experimental research examining compensation preference changes following organizational total rewards policy modifications would provide stronger evidence for causal mechanisms. Experimental preference elicitation studies comparing stated preferences across different measurement contexts including LinkedIn-style public discourse tasks, anonymous survey instruments, and incentive-compatible choice experiments would help disentangle performative self-presentation effects from authentic preference expression, directly addressing a core limitation of the current study.

Cross-platform comparative analysis

Comparative studies examining reward preference discourse across platforms with different identity norms including anonymous professional forums such as Glassdoor and Blind alongside professional networks such as LinkedIn would clarify the extent to which platform-specific performative dynamics shape observed preference patterns. Such comparisons would enable researchers to identify which reward categories are consistently emphasized across contexts (suggesting robust preferences) versus those that appear platform-dependent (suggesting performative influence), advancing both methodological understanding and substantive compensation theory.

Longitudinal SBTRT Development

Extended longitudinal studies tracking professional discourse evolution over multi-year periods would distinguish temporary post-pandemic adjustment patterns from sustained transformations in total rewards preferences, enabling more confident assessment of SBTRT’s Market Value of Skills Principle. Time-series analysis examining discourse patterns across pre-pandemic, pandemic, and post-pandemic periods would isolate digital transformation effects from COVID-19-induced workplace changes.

Practical implications for adaptive reward system design

The findings offer three actionable pathways for managers seeking to design adaptive, skill-sensitive reward systems, with the caveat that discourse-derived insights should complement rather than replace confidential employee preference assessment through traditional engagement surveys.

First, organizations can implement discourse-informed compensation benchmarking by systematically monitoring industry-specific professional discourse to identify emerging skill categories commanding premium compensation expectations before these trends appear in formal salary surveys. The 31.2% increase in digital skills discourse identified in this study signals compensation expectation shifts that proactive organizations could address through anticipatory reward adjustments. Practically, HR teams and compensation analysts can integrate periodic social media discourse monitoring alongside traditional compensation survey data to create early-warning indicators of shifting professional expectations, while recognizing that discourse trends may amplify emerging preferences relative to their actual prevalence in the broader workforce.

Second, organizations should develop skill-sensitive reward calibration mechanisms that differentiate compensation structures based on verified digital competency portfolios rather than traditional job title hierarchies. The significant industry-specific variations identified in this study suggest that effective reward calibration requires sector-specific approaches. Managers in technology-intensive contexts should prioritize digital tool allowances, certification reimbursement programs, and flexible work arrangements as retention levers, while those in consumer goods sectors may achieve greater impact through career development investments and skill certification pathways.
