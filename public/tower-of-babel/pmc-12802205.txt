Efficiency of health systems in developing countries: the case of West African countries
Authors: Kossivi Akoetey, Anne Viallefont
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Developing countries are faced with numerous health challenges such as lack of funding, increasing frequency and magnitude of epidemic risks, organizational and socio-cultural difficulties. In this context, we developed this study to assess for the first time the efficiency of health systems in the countries of the West African sub-region, firstly, to identify the systems that best adapt to these challenges and secondly, to highlight the factors that influence the health production process. To achieve this, we used the World Bank’s, Worldwide Development Indicators and Worldwide Governance Indicators databases, from 2000 to 2022. We applied the stochastic fixed-effect frontier method of Kumbhakar et al. (2014) to account for unobservable heterogeneity in the estimates. We used a novel multiple imputation approach to deal with missing data, while determining the fractions of missing information in the estimates. The results show that the average relative efficiency for all countries in the sub-region is 89.7%. Countries in the West African sub-region could theoretically increase life expectancy at birth by an average of 10.3%, which represents 6.5 years, with the same level of resources used. The results also show that health systems in these countries have higher permanent inefficiency than temporal inefficiency, suggesting that they mainly face structural challenges. Per capita health expenditure, gross domestic product per capita, out-of-pocket expenditure per capita, literacy rate, poverty gap and voice and accountability were positively associated with the efficiency of their health systems.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13561-025-00695-8.

Introduction

Health systems play a vital role in improving population health and saving lives. However, they face significant challenges, including the risk of epidemics, persistent concerns about the quality and equity of care, and limited resources, which are further strained by rising healthcare expenditures. The strategies and resources used to respond to these challenges vary from country to country. Notably, countries with similar levels of healthcare spending often achieve different health outcomes. In some of the poorest-performing nations, it is estimated that 20 to 40% of global health expenditures are wasted each year [1], resulting in insufficient healthcare provision. This situation leads to high death and disability rates, and hopelessness among populations [2].

Our study aims to assess the efficiency of health systems in developing countries, focusing specifically on those in the West African sub-region. The goal is to identify the systems best adapted to current health challenges and to highlight the key factors that influence the production of health outcomes.

The countries of the West African sub-region are members of the Economic Community of West African States (ECOWAS), established in 1975. It comprises 15 countries: Benin, Burkina Faso, Cabo Verde, Côte d’Ivoire, The Gambia, Ghana, Guinea, Guinea-Bissau, Liberia, Mali, Niger, Nigeria, Senegal, Sierra Leone, and Togo. Its health agency, the West African Health Organization (WAHO), aims to harmonize health policies, pool resources, and promote research across the region. Despite these efforts, the sub-region continues to face major health challenges. In 2020, West African countries had the highest maternal mortality rate, with an average of 754 deaths per 100,000 live births, compared to just 4 per 100,000 in Australia and New Zealand [3]. Health spending remains below 6% of GDP, compared to nearly 9% in Europe [4]. Health insurance systems are underdeveloped, with approximately 70% of health expenditure paid directly by households. Health infrastructure is often inadequate, and inequalities in access to healthcare persist. Moreover, the 2013 Ebola outbreak and the COVID-19 pandemic further weakened health systems [5], while political instability and terrorism continue to destabilize the region [6].

Despite these pressing issues, few studies have focused specifically on the efficiency of health systems in West African countries. Some broader studies, such as the WHO’s 2000 report on global health system performance [2] and the work by Evans et al. (2000) [7], placed most Sub-Saharan African (SSA) countries among the least efficient countries studied. Grigoli et al. also showed that compared to emerging and developing Western Hemisphere and Asian countries, African countries were relatively more inefficient in public spending on health [8]. In a study specifically on African countries, Novignon and Lawanson, showed that the health system of Cabo Verde was among the efficient countries while two other countries in the sub-region namely, Sierra Leone and Mali, were reported more inefficient than other countries [9]. Other research argued that health spending in Africa is too low to ensure efficiency [10], suggesting that increased investment is a necessary but not sufficient condition, efficiency in spending being equally critical [9]. Specifically for West African countries, only Koku investigated the efficiency of public health spending [11]. However, that study focused on public expenditure in general, not on health systems specifically. Additionally, it did not account for unobservable heterogeneity between countries, which can potentially bias efficiency estimates [12]. Furthermore, missing data limited the number of observations to between 107 and 150, introducing potential bias if the missingness was not completely random [13]. To our knowledge, no study has explicitly assessed the efficiency of health systems in West African countries. Our study addresses that gap by analyzing health system efficiency across the sub-region countries for the first time. The findings aim to enrich the literature on health system performance and guide policymakers in taking more effective health decisions.

To conduct this analysis, we used the most recent data from the World Development Indicators (WDI) and the Worldwide Governance Indicators (WGI) for the period 2000 to 2022. We applied the stochastic frontier analysis (SFA) model developed by Kumbhakar et al. [12], which accounts for unobserved heterogeneity in the estimation process, details of which are provided in the methodology section. To address the challenge of missing data, we implemented a multiple imputation approach [14, 15], which minimizes bias due to missing values. Further details are presented in the data section.

Data and methodology

Data

We used World Bank (WB) data, in particular the Worldwide Governance Indicators (WGI) and World Development Indicators (WDI), accessible online. The study covered a panel of 23 years from 2000 to 2022 and the 15 countries of the West African sub-region.

Result variable

In the literature, health status is identified as the main outcome of health systems. However, it is not easy to determine, and it depends on other factors that are independent of health systems [2, 16]. For these reasons, life expectancy at birth or disability-adjusted life expectancy, the universal health coverage index, avoidable mortalities or vaccination coverage are the indicators most commonly used to represent the production of health systems [17]. In our framework, the disability-adjusted life expectancy variable was not available over the period considered. We therefore opted for life expectancy at birth as the outcome variable. It captures the effort of health systems in the production of health care while taking into account all age groups of the population.

However, this variable has a natural lower bound, and its use as a direct output of the health system is therefore controversial. Life expectancy in a theoretical setting without any modern health system would not be zero [18]. As a robustness check, we used several adjusted versions of the life expectancy variable by subtracting 20, or 35 years to approximate the portion attributable to the health system. We also followed the proposal by Nolte and McKee [19] by using under-5 mortality as an alternative indicator, as well as a composite index combining life expectancy, maternal mortality ratio (per 100,000 live births), and under-5 mortality, to better capture the performance of the health system.

Input and environmental variables

Health is a multidimensional concept, and several factors contribute to its production. However, its financing remains the main driving force. Health expenditure, measured as a percentage of GDP or per capita is commonly used in the literature as the primary input in the production of health systems [17]. In this study, we used health expenditure per capita, expressed in purchasing power parity (PPP) in current international dollars, as the main input variable. Additionally, we included other financing-related variables, such as out-of-pocket expenditure per capita, PPP in current international dollars. This variable reflects the share of healthcare costs directly borne by patients, often due to absent or incomplete health insurance coverage. We also used GDP per capita, based on PPP in constant 2021 in current international dollars. Further explanatory variables included the number of physicians (general practitioners and specialists) per 1,000 inhabitants, the poverty gap at $3/day (expressed as a percentage of the poverty line, as defined by the World Bank), and the literacy rate, calculated as the percentage of the population aged 15 and over who can read and write. In the particular case of West African countries, political instability and terrorism have become increasingly significant and affect the lives of the population [20]. To account for this, we included the “Political Stability and Absence of Violence/Terrorism” index, which measures perceptions of the likelihood of political instability and/or politically-motivated violence, including terrorism, as constructed by the World Bank. We also included the “Voice and Accountability” variable, which captures perceptions of the extent to which a country’s citizens are able to participate in selecting their government, as well as enjoy freedom of expression, freedom of association, and free media. Since the last two governance indicators often contain negative values, we added the absolute value of their minimum plus one before applying logarithmic transformations. Managing missing data

Some variables introduced in the model had a high number of missing values. Including these values in the estimation without completing them was not possible, as the data set would then be reduced to a mere 13 full observations out of 345 lines. Besides, the missing process cannot be considered completely at random. We thus chose to use a multiple imputation method to get unbiased estimates of the parameters of the model and of their variances [14]. According to Stead & Wheat, multiple imputation of the dependent variable in the SFA analysis would affect the error term, whereas its use for explanatory variables improves the global information taken into account in the model, without introducing any bias [15]. This approach involves generating M sets of completed data (M≥5) on which parameter values will be estimated, then pooling the results of the M estimations [21]. Here we imputed M = 10 datasets, using the approach of Honaker et al.,(2011) and package Amelia 2 in R [22]. This approach, specifically designed for macroeconomic panel data, allowed us to take into account the high temporal auto-correlation of the variable values within countries, as the previous or next value of the variable in the country is taken into account in the imputation process. Methodology and estimation strategy

Efficiency in economics is a relative measure between observed production and the maximum achievable production (isoquante) for a given number of inputs. The technical efficiency of health systems is defined as the maximum possible level of health production (output) obtained from a given set of health-related resources (input).

Two types of methods are used in the literature to estimate the efficiency of health systems, that is, parametric and non-parametric methods. Parametric methods impose a predefined functional form on the efficiency frontier, whereas nonparametric methods determine the efficiency frontier empirically from the raw observations of the sample, without imposing a functional form on it a priori [16]. However, the latter does not take into account measurement error and heterogeneity in the sample, which are considered as inefficiency [24]. Parametric methods distinguish the residual error due to inefficiency, which can be improved, from the random error [16]. In our framework, given the importance of exogenous factors (climatic hazards, insecurity and external dependence) and heterogeneity (differences in size and economic areas) between countries, we opted for the parametric panel SFA method with a country fixed effect, introduced by Kumbhakar et al. [12].

Stochastic Frontier Analysis (SFA) modelling

In SFA approach, an outcome variable (output) is regressed on explanatory variables including all inputs and environmental variables. The part not explained by the model, the residuals, is used to estimate the inefficiency.

The SFA country fixed effect method allows to distinguish between heterogeneity in countries, permanent inefficiency, and temporal inefficiency, in the presence of random shocks. This is important for policymakers, as relatively high temporal inefficiency indicates that inefficiency is related to random factors that could disappear over time, whereas a more important permanent inefficiency implies the existence of permanent factors like culture, geography, colonial history, etc., that impact inefficiency over multiple periods, advocating for a change in long term policy. Robustness of the results

Exclusion of Cabo Verde

Over the studied period, the health expenditures per capita in Cabo Verde were much higher than in any other country of the sub-region (average = 289.8 vs. all countries average = 116.7) and increased from 129.49 to 663.91 $ per capita over the study period (see graph 2 in appendix). This might be explained by the relatively good economic health of this country, with reasonable inflation rates, and also by a strong political choice: by the end of the studied period, Cabo Verde was spending more than 4% of its GDP on domestic health expenditures, whereas the average in the other countries was around 1% (WDI data).

We tried to exclude Cabo Verde from the set of studied countries, and the results are very similar to those with Cabo Verde. The efficiency scores are highly correlated at 99.6%, indicating the robustness of efficiency scores.

Modifying the output measurement

To take into account the fact that the minimum life expectancy in a theoretical area without any health system would not be zero, we conducted the analysis by subtracting a number of years that could be considered as the minimum life expectancy at birth without healthcare. We subtracted 20 or 35 years from life expectancy at birth, mechanically yielding larger estimates of effects and smaller efficiency scores, as one year of increase in life expectancy represents a larger percentage of this more realistic output variable. However, the ranking of the countries in terms of efficiency remained similar to the initial ranking (see Table 6 in appendix).

We also used an indicator of good health based on under-5 mortality. This indicator is computed as 0.25 – X5, where X5 is the under-5 mortality rate. The value 0.25 was chosen as a maximum child mortality threshold. In 2000, the countries with the worst child mortality experienced a mortality rate slightly smaller than this value. The constructed variable can thus be interpreted as “avoided under-five mortlity”. The results of these analyses appear in Table 6. The estimated efficiencies on this indicator are lower than for life expectancy, and Niger appears to be more fragile in terms of capacity to control child mortality, whereas Benin is relatively more efficient on this criterion. The rest of the ranking does not change much.

The explanatory variables multicollinearity check

Multicollinearity arises when independent variables are highly correlated, which can distort the estimation of individual effects, inflate standard errors, and subsequently affect the efficiency scores. To address this potential issue, we examined both the correlation matrix and the variance inflation factors (VIF) for all explanatory variables included in the model (see Table 7 in appendix). All VIF values were found to be below the commonly accepted threshold of 5, suggesting that multicollinearity is not a major concern in our specification [27]. However, some variables exhibited strong pairwise correlations: both out-of-pocket expenditure per capita and GDP per capita were correlated with health expenditure per capita at over 60%, while political stability was correlated with voice and accountability at a similar level. We thus chose to exclude the variables out-of-pocket expenditure per capita and political stability, to check for their masking effects. The results show no change in the statistical significance of the remaining variables, and the efficiency scores obtained after removing these variables were highly correlated (98,1%) with those from model 3, confirming the robustness of our findings.

Discussion

The average relative efficiency estimated for countries in the West African sub-region was 89.7%. This suggests that, on average, these countries could theoretically increase life expectancy at birth by 10.3% points, or 6.5 years, using the same level of resources. This highlights the significant inefficiencies and resource wastage within the healthcare systems of some of these countries. These findings are consistent with those of Novignon and Lawanson who estimated the relative efficiency of healthcare systems in Sub-Saharan Africa at around 80% [9]. The slight improvement in the average relative efficiency in our results may be attributed to the use of more recent data. However, we observe a large gap of 26.3% points between the most efficient country, Niger (with an average efficiency of 98.1%), and the least efficient, Nigeria (average efficiency of 71.8%). Nigeria could increase life expectancy at birth by 20 years, while Niger could only improve it by about 1 year using their actual levels of healthcare resources. There is also a large difference in the sources of inefficiency, which could partly explain the rankings observed. On average, permanent inefficiency accounts for 8.8%, whereas residual (temporal) inefficiency is only 1.6% (graph 4 in appendix). This means that structural inefficiencies are more significant than short-term or managerial inefficiencies across all countries and years studied. Looking at individual countries, Liberia and Niger which ranked highest, face greater temporal inefficiencies than structural ones (Fig. 1). According to Kumbhakar et al. (2015), structural inefficiencies are generally more difficult to address and require long-term reforms [28]. The efficiency of West African countries could thus be improved by prioritizing long-term policy interventions over short-term adjustments. Countries such as Niger or Liberia could improve their life expectancy quickly through an increase in fundings. However, the majority of countries face deeper, structural issues that significantly impact healthcare efficiency. Similar findings were reported by Nassar et al. in middle-income countries. According to them, this requires a change in direction in health policy [1].

Determinants of efficiency

Our results also reveal that key explanatory variables, health expenditure per capita, GDP per capita, out-of-pocket expenditure per capita, literacy rate, poverty gap, and voice and accountability, significantly affect the efficiency of healthcare systems in West African countries. These results are in line with earlier studies [17]. The positive effects of health expenditure and GDP per capita on life expectancy demonstrate a wealth effect. Higher income levels lead to more investments in health, thus improving health system outcomes. Conversely, out-of-pocket expenditure negatively affects efficiency, which is in accordance to the literature [29]. High direct payments do not fully cover the financing of the system. As the poverty gap, they tend to reduce health investments, which negatively impacts health outcomes. Furthermore, the literacy rate has a positive effect, as education improves individuals’ understanding of medical advice and enhances their health-seeking behavior.

In the context of West African countries, political instability, dictatorship, and lack of civil liberties often disrupt healthcare systems. This explains the fact that, the voice and accountability index, which reflects democratic governance, freedom of speech, and media freedom, is positively associated with healthcare efficiency.

However, our results do not show a significant effect of the number of physicians per 1,000 inhabitants, which contradicts findings in the literature [17]. This may be due to the low overall physician density in the region, which likely falls below the threshold required to have a meaningful impact.

Structural and country specific issues

The inefficiencies observed point out both shared and country-specific issues. Most countries in the sub-region follow a pyramidal healthcare structure and face similar challenges, poor coverage, underfunding, workforce shortages, and minimal health insurance coverage. For instance, in average for all countries, 50% of total health expenditures in 2000 and 2022 are direct out-of-pocket expenses and the average number of physicians is just 0.14 per 1,000 people. These issues call for broad policy reforms such as increasing coverage, strengthening governance and regulation, and enhancing public financing, per the Abuja declaration recommendations [30].

It is noteworthy that both life expectancy at birth and its main drivers, GDP per capita and Health expenses per capita, have increased in every country over the studied 23-year period. Our study distinguishes a group of countries that perform well and have a high efficiency in every ranking (Niger, Liberia, Gambia, etc.). In Fig. 1, time-varying efficiencies are increasing over the years for many countries, reflecting mainly an increase in life expectancy relatively more rapid than the increase in health spending.

However, some other countries seem to have been unable to follow a route of efficiency over the years. In Table 5, we note that the efficiency was initially very similar in every country, but across time, countries such as Nigeria have increased their GDP per capita, and their health expenses, without being able to achieve a large increase of life expectancy for their populations.

The case of Cabo Verde is very particular, as it is the richest country in the West African subregion, and also has the longest life expectancy (almost 76 years at the end of the period), but at the cost of a very high investment in health. This simply shows that there is a non-linear cost of improving life expectancy: it costs less to improve it by one year for countries that have initially a short life expectancy than for countries that already have a long one.

Concerning country-specific issues, in Niger (the most efficient country), the widespread use of traditional medicine which remains often the only healthcare option available to the majority of the population, fills the gap left by the formal system [31]. In Nigeria (the least efficient), the system is dominated by the private sector, with high costs and widespread counterfeit drugs [32]. These problems are behavioral and structural and require long-term reforms focused on regulation, not just funding. Côte d’Ivoire, Ghana, and Sierra Leone, which also show low permanent efficiency (Fig. 1), face similar structural issues and should prioritize regulatory and policy reforms.

Limitations of the study

First, it is important to emphasize that these results are relative, and dependent on the selection of countries and the variables considered. Including additional countries or modifying the output variable could alter the efficiency scores. However, although we used life expectancy at birth as a proxy for health system performance, we tested alternative proxy variables and obtained similar results, thus confirming the robustness of our findings.

Additionally, as shown by the calculated rates of missing information in our models, some loss of information due to missing data is inevitable, regardless of the imputation method applied. Nonetheless, since the imputation is based on random sampling methods, the estimation of standard errors takes this uncertainty into account. Moreover, variables with too many missing observations could not be reliably imputed. This constraint explains the exclusion of certain variables, such as UHC service coverage, and the use of alternative proxies like the number of physicians per 1,000 inhabitants.

One of the main methodological challenges in this analysis is the potential for endogeneity, which may arise from omitted unobservable variables and simultaneity bias. For example, health expenditures may be influenced by the performance of the health system itself, as a country with higher life expectancy might also invest more in healthcare to take care of the ageing population. Furthermore, factors such as the use of traditional medicine or self-medication, for which no consistent data is available, could also impact health outcomes and be somehow linked to the amount of health spending. These sources of endogeneity could lead to biased and inconsistent estimates. We attempted to avoid most of this pitfall, with a panel fixed-effects approach that fully controls for time-invariant unobservable heterogeneity. Nevertheless, caution is advised when interpreting the causal relationships suggested by the model.

Conclusion

The aim of this study was to determine the efficiency of healthcare systems in countries in the West African sub-region and identify factors that explain this efficiency, in order to minimize resource wastage while learning from those with best practices. To our knowledge, this is the first study specifically determining the efficiency of healthcare systems in these countries. We used a fixed-effect stochastic frontier method to account for unobservable heterogeneity in the estimates. We also employed a novel approach of multiple imputation to address the issue of missing data. This approach would enable other researchers to more easily conduct studies on developing countries while eliminating biases associated with missing observations.

Healthcare expenditure per capita and GDP per capita had a strong positive effect on life expectancy, whereas out-of-pocket expenditure per capita seemed to have a negative effect. Literacy rate and the poverty gap could be taken into account in this study despite large proportions of missing values, thanks to the use of multiple imputations, and showed significant relationships with life expectancy. Interestingly, the WGI indicator voice and accountability was significantly associated with life expectancy at birth, denoting a need for sound governance.

The ranking of the countries in terms of efficiency is not the same as their ranking in life expectancy, because some countries do not achieve very long life expectancies with large health spending, and others have already a long life expectancy, that comes with a high cost.

The results have shown that countries in the West African sub-region could theoretically increase their average life expectancy at birth by 6.5 years on average, with the same level of resources utilized. Liberia, Togo, Gambia and Niger have high permanent efficiencies, which suggests that these two countries, among the poorest, are in fact efficient in achieving the best possible life expectancy for the money they spend, and could probably increase the health of the population by increasing spending. On the contrary, the other countries have a permanent inefficiency that is larger than the temporal inefficiency, which indicates a need for a shift in policies, with a focus on funding, control, and governance.
