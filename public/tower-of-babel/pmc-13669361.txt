Curvature-driven passive mixing in three-dimensional conical helix micromixers
Authors: İsmail Bütün, Tunahan Parlayıcı, Süleyman Çelik, Erchan Aptoula, Ali Koşar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPassive microfluidic mixing is fundamentally constrained by the dominance of laminar flow, a limitation commonly addressed using embedded obstacles or surface modifications, often at the cost of increased fabrication complexity and hydraulic resistance. Here, we introduce a conical helix micromixer (CHM) that combines circular and helical microchannel topologies into a monolithic geometry capable of passively generating progressively intensified Dean vortices through continuously varying curvature. This curvature-driven mechanism induces strong transverse transport and achieves a mixing index (MI) > 95% in the first turn of the CHM across varying flow rates (1–2500 µL min−1) without requiring any internal roughness structures or external energy inputs, as validated by field-based mixing simulations. A central methodological contribution is a reduced-order planar representation of the full three-dimensional (3D) CHM geometry for flow-field analysis and optimization. Direct geometrical validation against the original 3D conical-helix centreline demonstrated projection-induced deviations below 2% across all investigated configurations, with most cases remaining below 1%, confirming that the planar representation preserves the dominant geometric characteristics of the true 3D flow path with negligible loss of fidelity. This reduced-order strategy lowered computational costs by approximately an order of magnitude, while preserving a strong correlation with full 3D field-based calculations across the investigated ranges of Reynolds numbers. Geometric and flow parameters were systematically explored using a Taguchi framework, while gradient-boosted machine-learning models were employed as mechanistic analytical tools to quantify parameter sensitivity. Both analyses consistently identified the inlet curvature radius as the dominant design variable governing Dean vortex onset and mixing-length compression, with tighter curvature significantly accelerating the development of transverse mixing. The results prove that controlled curvature modulation alone can function as an efficient passive mixing engine, providing a compact, fabrication-ready platform for microreactor synthesis, point-of-care diagnostics, and scalable lab-on-a-chip applications.Subject terms: Engineering, Physics

Introduction

Micromixers, a subclass of microfluidic devices and microreactors, have emerged as indispensable tools for efficient microscale fluid mixing. Their utility spans a wide spectrum of applications, ranging from drug discovery and high-throughput screening1,2 to chemical synthesis3,4, clinical diagnostics5, and bio-analysis6. Broadly, micromixers can be categorized into two principal classes: passive and active micromixers7. Passive micromixers rely on channel geometry or embedded structures, leveraging mechanisms such as inertial effects, secondary flows, velocity gradients, and molecular diffusion to induce mixing8, whereas active micromixers exploit external actuation to enhance the mixing performance. In particular, active micromixers integrate external physical fields, including acoustic9–11, electric12–14, magnetic15,16, thermal17,18, or centrifugal (high-gravity) forces19,20, into microfluidic systems, thereby achieving a significantly improved mixing efficiency compared with their purely passive counterparts. Consequently, the elimination of external energy requirements renders passive micromixers more advantageous for certain mixing applications. However, achieving an optimal mixing performance necessitates the careful optimization of geometric parameters in passive micromixers, which has typically been conducted through computational modeling and simulation-based design approaches.

In geometry-induced micromixers, structural design, velocity, and fluid properties are among the most influential parameters affecting the mixing performance under fully developed laminar flow conditions8,21 and chaotic flows22,23. Following the nanolitre-size mixing performance24–26, various types of geometric structures at the microscale level were developed to assess the effect of geometrical parameters on the mixing performance of micromixers27–31. Therefore, geometric modifications within the microchannel, such as the incorporation of baffles, obstacles, or curved pathways, can significantly enhance mixing by inducing secondary flows, promoting chaotic advection, and enhancing the fluid–fluid interactions31–34. Design-related morphological features can induce complex flow patterns, improving the passive mixing efficiency. Yet, their practical implementation at the microscale often proves to be challenging, as fine structural dimensions and complex geometric features impose strict constraints on device manufacturing processes. Conversely, microchannels with simpler, unobstructed geometries are considerably easier to fabricate but tend to exhibit a lower mixing efficiency (~60%)35. In this regard, it is necessary to optimize geometric parameters to balance ease of modeling and fabrication with functional mixing performance.

Curved microchannel designs result in secondary flow phenomena such as Dean vortices, with a reduced radius of curvature (RC) giving rise to stronger secondary flow effects36. In such a design, the flow structures play a critical role in enhancing chaotic advection, thereby improving the mixing performance36,37. Consequently, increasing the number of curvatures or loops in the microfluidic chip design extends the residence time between fluid streams, thereby enhancing mixing performance and promoting more complete homogenization. In addition, the aspect ratio of the microchannel is a key design parameter, influencing the velocity profiles, residence time, and interfacial contact area between fluid streams38. Accordingly, the interplay of governing factors determines the flow behavior and critically affects the mixing efficiency, while embedded features such as obstacles, cavities, grooves, or surface roughness amplify this effect by generating localized vortices and facilitating flow splitting and recombination, the hallmark mechanisms of passive chaotic mixing35,39.

In addition to geometric considerations, the velocity of the fluid inside the microfluidic channel stands out as one of the most influential parameters affecting the overall mixing performance40. It interacts with the channel design to determine the final mixing outcome. For instance, increasing the flow rate in linear microchannels, where laminar flow confines mixing to molecular diffusion, reduces the residence time and lateral mass transfer, thereby significantly impairing the mixing efficiency under high-velocity conditions41. Unlike straight microchannels, the curved structures in serpentine microchannels generate centrifugal forces, inducing secondary flows, particularly at moderate Reynolds numbers42. Thus, as the flow rate increases, these vortices intensify, enhancing transverse mixing. However, when the velocity exceeds an optimal threshold in smooth serpentine micromixers without any baffles and grooves, the associated decrease in the residence time might reduce the overall mixing efficiency35. A further passive design principle involves the use of circular and helical microchannels, such as spiral or coil geometries, which are particularly effective in maintaining a high mixing performance even at higher flow rates, due to their continuous curvature and extended path length that enhance Dean flows and facilitate chaotic advection35,43. At low flow rates, mixing in helical and circular microchannels is predominantly governed by molecular diffusion44. However, as the flow rate increases, these geometries become increasingly effective due to the development of strong Dean vortices, which generate secondary flows that enhance fluid interfacial interaction and mixing45. Unlike straight microchannels, which typically suffer from reduced mixing efficiency at elevated flow rates due to shortened residence time and limited cross-stream diffusion, curved channels can maintain or even improve mixing performance up to a critical threshold46. Beyond this optimal point, excessive inertial forces might induce Dean flow instability, leading to distorted flow patterns and a decline in the mixing uniformity37. Existing helical47 and spiral micromixer designs36,48, however, operate at a fixed curvature radius, which constrains the Dean number to a geometry-invariant value and limits the capacity to sustain progressive intensification of secondary flow along the channel length. The conical helix architecture introduced here resolves this constraint by coupling a monotonically varying curvature with the helical topology, generating a spatially evolving Dean vortex structure that continuously reinforces transverse mixing without requiring internal structural elements. Therefore, while higher flow rates generally hinder diffusion-limited mixing in straight microchannels, they can substantially enhance the performance in curved geometries, provided that the design is carefully optimized to exploit inertial and secondary flow mechanisms44.

Several strategies have been developed to realize three-dimensional helical microfluidic architectures, each carrying inherent trade-offs among structural complexity, fabrication accessibility, and functional versatility (Supporting Information S1). Sacrificial-template approaches using low-melting-point metal wires embedded in polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS) yield channels with near-circular cross-sections, yet the multi-step wire removal, lead contamination risk, and dependence on vacuum-assisted heating impose significant process constraints49. While flow-induced buckling of biopolymer jets enables elegant one-step generation of helical microfibers with tunable architectures50,51, such approaches remain confined to gelation-dependent chemistries incompatible with the reactive, solvent-rich environments demanded by continuous-flow synthesis. Among existing approaches, the rope-coiling extrusion of polyvinyl alcohol (PVA) filaments via desktop 3D printers offers the closest conceptual parallel to the present study. Building upon this PVA-based templating strategy, we extend its utility by overcoming the dimensional limitations imposed by viscous-jet instabilities and nozzle resolution52.

In inertial microfluidics, the mixing performance is commonly characterized by the mixing index (MI), mixing ratio (MR), mixing length (ML), and mixing time (MT), all of which are well established in the micromixing literature. These performance measures are primarily governed by the microchannel geometry, where parameters such as the curvature, loop number, and aspect ratio determine the residence length and time required to achieve the desired mixing level, thereby influencing the device cost and throughput by reducing the fabrication complexity and enabling more compact system designs44. Conventional evaluation approaches often struggle to analyze the complex three-dimensional flow fields, motivating more tractable yet geometry-rich designs53. Therefore, in this study, we introduce a conical helix micromixer (CHM) that preserves analytical simplicity while harnessing the physics of curved channels (Fig. 1): centrifugal forcing and Dean-type secondary flows at finite Reynolds number, augmented by inertial asymmetries arising from the helical layout44. The resulting intensification of chaotic advection shortens the required mixing length and time, yielding rapid, high-efficiency mixing. The CHMs integrate the functional advantages of both helical and curved microchannel architecture while leveraging geometric asymmetry. This configuration gives rise to multiple dominant forces: centrifugal effects intrinsic to helical geometries, inertial forces that emerge at higher Reynolds numbers, and additional imbalances induced by the asymmetric design. Together, these dynamics intensify vorticity fields and substantially enhance the mixing performance, enabling both high efficiency and rapid response.

The CHM is distinguished from existing helical and spiral passive micromixers along three axes that, to our knowledge, have not been simultaneously addressed in the literature. At the geometric level, while conventional helical microchannels maintain a fixed curvature radius, the conical helix geometry imposes a continuously varying curvature along the channel axis, which progressively modulates the local Dean number and sustains vortex intensification across the entire mixing length, rather than allowing Dean flow to stabilize into a periodic, efficiency-limiting regime, enabling near-complete mixing within 10 mm and under 1 s, without embedded obstacles, surface roughness, or external energy inputs. This geometry-driven performance is supported analytically by a 2D representation of the full 3D CHM flow field, which reduces the computational cost by an order of magnitude with negligible loss of metric accuracy, validated against 3D field-based calculations across the complete Reynolds number range, making high-throughput parametric exploration tractable without sacrificing physical fidelity. Building on this reduced-order analytical foundation, ensemble ML models serve not as decoupled surrogate predictors applied independently of the design loop, but as mechanistic interrogation tools embedded within a coupled CFD–Taguchi–ML framework, converging on the inlet curvature radius as the dominant parameter governing Dean vortex onset and mixing-length compression. Together, these contributions offer a geometry-first design framework, whose mechanistic insights into curvature-driven flow dynamics extend the applicability of passive microfluidic platforms across chemical synthesis, clinical diagnostics, next-generation microphysiological modeling, and biomedical research.

Results and discussion

The combination of DoE outcomes (25) and multiple flow rates (16) yields distinct optimal mixing conditions, as illustrated in Supporting Information S2. Depending on the results, the CHMs with DP=200μm, DC=8mm, and HT=20mm (Fig. 1) have the maximum mixing ratio for all flow rates (Supporting Information S2). At 10 μL min−1, the flow operates in an intermediate inertial regime, in which residence time is insufficient for diffusion-dominated mixing, yet the Dean number remains below the threshold required for fully developed secondary vortex structures44, a condition that yields the longest mixing length (ML) among all tested configurations (Supporting Data 1). At lower flow rates, diffusion dominates, while stronger secondary flows enhance transverse mixing and shorten the mixing length at higher rates (Supporting Data 1). Under extremely low flow rates, ~0.1–1 µL min−1, mixing is dominated by diffusion54, resulting in relatively high mixing times, MT (mixing time) >500 s, (Supporting Information S3 and Supporting Data 1). In this regime, increasing FR (flow rate) slightly reduces MT, but the system remains inefficient due to limited convective transport22. Also, the velocity and vorticity fields in the optimized CHM (Supporting Information S4) enhance mixing performance (to mix fluids homogeneously, which reduces ML and MT) (Supporting Data 1).

The ML decreases significantly from ~70 to ~10 mm (Supporting Information S3 and Supporting Data 1). At the same time, MT is reduced by more than an order of magnitude (4000 s to <1 s) at intermediate flow rates of 10–100 µL min−1. This demonstrates the transition from diffusion-dominated mixing to Dean flow-assisted convective mixing, in which secondary vortices accelerate cross-sectional fluid transport (Supporting Information S3–S5). At very high flow rates (≥1000 µL min−1), ML remains short (~11 mm), and MT reaches its minimum of ~0.002 s (Supporting Information S3 and Supporting Data 1). However, further increases in FR beyond 1000 µL min−1 yield diminishing returns in MR (mixing ratio), suggesting that a saturation effect is reached, where greater inertia no longer improves mixing efficiency (Supporting Information S3 and S4, and Supporting Data 1). From a geometrical point of view, the DP (pipe diameter) strongly influences MR and ML. For a smaller DP (200 µm), MR is stable ( ~ 100%), while ML remains compact (Supporting Data 1 and Supporting Information S5). For larger DP (300 µm, particularly at 200–500 µL min−1), MR increases (up to 300%), but ML is extended. This suggests that a larger DP enhances vortex strength (Supporting Information S6 and Supporting Data 1), thereby promoting mixing, but requires additional channel length to achieve homogenization.

DC directly influences the Dean number (De) (Supporting Information S5 and S6) due to changes in the radius of curvature. Smaller DC values (4 mm) consistently produce stronger vortical motion, reflected as shorter ML and reduced MT (Supporting Information S5 and S6, and Supporting Data 1). Conversely, larger DC (7–8 mm) at high flow rates yields stable MR but requires slightly longer ML, since weaker curvature reduces secondary flow intensity (Supporting Information S5 and S6, and Supporting Data 1). This aligns with the classical Dean flow theory, where a reduced curvature lowers centrifugal instabilities and delays vortex formation55. HT also has a secondary but noticeable impact. Larger HT (30 mm) tends to increase MT at low flow rates, as the larger cross-sectional area reduces shear-induced mixing56. Under optimized conditions (20 mm), MR remains stable, and ML is minimized, indicating a balance between hydrodynamic resistance and Dean vortex formation.

To strengthen the experimental validation of the proposed CHM, direct flow-visualization experiments (Fig. 2) were performed using CHM (in Fig. 8). Mixing evolution was systematically investigated over a broad flow-rate range spanning 1–2500 μL min−1 through simultaneous experimental observation and CFD-based concentration-field analysis (Fig. 2). The inlet and outlet flow structures were monitored using a CCD camera coupled to a desktop microscope, enabling direct visualization of the interface deformation, transverse transport, and downstream homogenization under varying hydrodynamic conditions (Supporting Data 2 and 3). The experimentally observed flow patterns were then qualitatively compared against the numerically predicted concentration distributions to assess the physical consistency of the reduced-order CFD framework.

The results reveal a clear flow-regime-dependent transition in the mixing behavior that remains consistent with the underlying Dean-flow dynamics predicted numerically. At very low flow rates (<10 μL min−1), mixing is predominantly governed by molecular diffusion, resulting in substantial homogenization near the channel entrance due to extended residence times. As the flow rate increases, inertial effects progressively strengthen curvature-induced secondary Dean vortices, enhancing cross-stream advection and accelerating interfacial stretching within the spiral channel. This transition from diffusion-dominated to inertia-enhanced mixing becomes increasingly pronounced at flow rates of approximately 2000–2500 μL min−1, where enhanced mixing performance is experimentally observed near the inlet region, indicating strong vortex-assisted transverse transport. Importantly, the experimental observations show a good qualitative agreement with the CFD-predicted concentration fields across the investigated operating range, thereby supporting both the physical validity of the proposed CHM design and the reliability of the reduced-order numerical representation.

Overall, MR is primarily enhanced by increasing DP and FR, since both promote stronger Dean vortices. In contrast, ML is minimized at intermediate DC and moderate HT values and decreases substantially as FR enters the inertia-dominated regime. Consequently, MT offers the largest improvement, decreasing from hundreds of seconds under diffusion-dominated conditions to sub-second values within the laminar inertial regime. These numerical trends were further supported qualitatively by direct flow-visualization experiments, which revealed progressively intensified transverse mixing and near-complete homogenization at elevated flow rates, consistent with the CFD-predicted Dean-vortex-enhanced transport behavior.

Flow profiles

Dean flow refers to the characteristic pair of counter-rotating secondary vortices that arise when a fluid passes through a curved or spiral (helical) channel44. The channel curvature generates a centrifugal pressure gradient, displacing the axial velocity maximum toward the outer wall. In response, viscous stresses drive a compensating cross-sectional circulation that manifests as two coherent vortical structures, known as Dean cells. The intensity of this secondary motion is quantified by the Dean number, a dimensionless parameter that links flow inertia with channel curvature37. The flow mechanism observed in the Supporting Information S4 and S6 reflects the classical development of Dean vortices in curved microchannels. At the inlet, the axial flow remains predominantly laminar with only minor perturbations, resulting in weak secondary circulation. As the fluid moves downstream along the spiral channel, centrifugal forces induced by curvature generate cross-sectional pressure gradients, which in turn drive the formation of counter-rotating vortices (Supporting Information S5 and S6). Upon increasing the flow rate, these vortices intensify and expand, redistributing momentum across the channel cross-section and enhancing transverse mixing (Supporting Information S5 and S6). This progression from near-parabolic inlet profile to strongly vortex-dominated outlet structures demonstrates the pivotal role of curvature-induced instabilities in transitioning the flow from diffusion-limited to convection-enhanced mixing.

At low flow rates, the cross-sectional velocity fields remain nearly symmetric with weak or poorly organized vortices (Fig. 3), suggesting that molecular diffusion is the primary mixing mechanism. As the flow rate increases to 100 µL min−1, distinct counter-rotating Dean vortices begin to emerge, accompanied by sharper velocity gradients across the cross-section. This indicates the onset of curvature-induced secondary circulation, which enhances convective transport beyond purely diffusive processes. At even higher flow rates (1000 µL min−1), the vortical structures become fully developed and span the entire channel cross-section, reflecting the strong influence of inertial and centrifugal effects. The observed transition from diffusion-dominated transport to vortex-dominated mixing highlights the critical role of flow rate in tuning the mixing efficiency.

Predictive modeling

The regression analyses were conducted using three distinct regression methods, based on the database in Supporting Data 1. The primary metrics assessed in our analyses were the R-squared (R2) value and the mean absolute error (MAE) (Supporting Information S7). The R2 value, commonly known as the coefficient of determination, is a quantitative measure of how well our regression model predicts or explains the variability in the data.

Upon thorough examination of the regression analyses, it is evident that the R² value for MT is exceptionally high, at 0.99. This indicates an excellent fit for our model in explaining the variability associated with MT. The MAE for MT is approximately 7.113, which can be considered a reasonable deviation, given that the mixing time spans a considerable range from 0.002 to 4000 s, reflecting the inherent variability of the mixing process. This suggests a strong justification for the combined use of regression analysis and classification methods in future investigations.

There are similar yet slightly lower R2 values for ML and MR. Specifically, ML has an R2 of approximately 0.94, accompanied by a MAE of 1.547, while MR has an R2 of approximately 0.93, with a remarkably small MAE of just 0.007 (Supporting Information S8). These metrics suggest that both the mixing length and the mixing ratio can be well represented by our regression approach, albeit with slightly lower accuracy than mixing time.

Consistent with our findings from the classification analysis, Reynolds number emerges as the dominant parameter influencing outcomes across all three mixing parameters (ML, MT, MR) in our regression studies. The regression analyses provide reliable estimates close to the true values, which could significantly enhance our understanding of mixing behavior in relevant applications.

Classification of mixing performance metrics

Approximately 400 mixing ratio data points, ranging from about 91% to 62%, were divided into four separate classes (Supporting Information S8). Many models were used to determine reasonable boundaries for these classifications. Due to the imbalanced distribution of data across the four classes, the “Synthetic Minority Oversampling Technique” (SMOTE) was employed to balance the classes using synthetic data57. As a result, the number of training data was increased to 456. To avoid overfitting, cross-validation was applied, yielding successful results. In this context, test accuracy and F1-score can both reach 86.6% (Supporting Information S8). Detailed analysis of the models revealed that, based on “Feature Importances,” the Reynolds number is the parameter most influential in the classification.

In this approach, which involves classification based on the mixing length, the data distribution across classes is even more scattered than in the previous methods (Supporting Information S8). Nevertheless, the results are still satisfactory in terms of performance. The mixing distances (ML) ranged from 10.9 mm to approximately 71 mm. Including synthetic data, a total of 612 data points were used for model training. The test accuracy reaches as high as 88.3%, with no signs of overfitting or underfitting. Unlike the previous methods, the inlet diameter of the liquids entering the helix is the most influential parameter in this classification. For mixing times, a logarithmic approach was utilized to establish class boundaries due to the significant differences in values. Mixtures taking up to 0.1 s were classified as the fastest and labeled “Gonzales,” while those taking 100 s or longer were categorized as “Oxcart.” Although the class imbalance was not as pronounced as in the mixing ratio-based classification, SMOTE was again employed to generate a specific number of synthetic data points, resulting in a total of 396 data points for model training. The minimal difference between validation and test scores indicates that the model was well trained, with neither overfitting nor underfitting. Test accuracy reached up to 94%, highlighting good performance in making future predictions after model training (Supporting Information S8). As observed in the mixing ratio-based classification, the Reynolds number significantly influences the classification outcomes.

Feature importance analysis in Fig. 4 reveals that the pipe diameter (DP) consistently emerges as the dominant parameter on the mixing performance across all three output metrics in low-sample regimes (Group A, n = 70), yet its relative influence diminishes as the dataset size increases, with the channel diameter (DC) becoming the primary driver of Mixing Length (importance = 0.48) in the intermediate regime (Group B, n = 215) and all three geometric parameters converging toward more equitable contributions in the high-adherent group (Group C, n = 115, R2 up to 0.98), which collectively suggests that the predictive hierarchy among DP, DC, and channel height (HT) is sensitive to both the sample size and the specific mixing metric.

Mixing efficiency comparison

To clarify, the numerical MI, MR, ML, and MT were obtained from calibrated top-view analysis of CFD-derived concentration maps, whereas the experimental analysis provided only fluorescence-based outlet MI. Experimental MR, ML, and MT were not determined. Two-dimensional microchannel geometries are well-suited for experimental analysis with an aqueous Rhodamine-B solution, enabling straightforward, concentration-dependent evaluation of mixing performance. In contrast, three-dimensional nonlinear helical microchannels pose significant challenges for experimental imaging, primarily because the microscope stage’s limited movement range restricts full layer-by-layer scanning of the entire 3D structure. While top-view images obtained from simulation studies facilitated effective two-dimensional analysis, direct experimental observation of mixing behavior relied on the Rhodamine B intensity profiles collected from the inlet and outlet regions, schematized in Fig. 6. Although the mixing index (MI) values could be determined from these profiles, experimentally estimating the mixing length and mixing time was not feasible. Nonetheless, the simulation results demonstrated complete (100%) mixing efficiency and showed a good agreement with the experimentally derived mixing index values, confirming the reliability of the approach.

The experimental results obtained using the confocal imaging demonstrate that the 3D spiral micromixer sustains a consistently high mixing index (MI ≈ 1.0) across a broad range of flow rates, from diffusion-dominated regimes at sub-microlitre scales to convection-enhanced transport at mL/min throughputs. Even at very low flow rates (Re < 1), where molecular diffusion is typically insufficient, the channel geometry induces secondary flows that enable near-complete mixing, while the Dean vortices stabilize the mixing performance with minimal fluctuations at intermediate flow rates. At high flow rates (Re > 1000), only a slight decrease in MI is observed, reflecting the reduced residence time relative to convective transport; however, the overall efficiency remains above 0.95, underscoring the robustness and versatility of the micromixer for applications ranging from precise low-volume assays to high-throughput biochemical processing.

Discussion

This study focuses on the parametric optimization and validation of flow parameters, specifically the flow rate and geometric properties DP, DC, and HT, on the mixing performance of CHMs. The optimization process employs artificial intelligence-based algorithms to model the system through intelligent computational frameworks. The effects of the mixing ratio (MR), mixing length (ML), mixing time (MT), and mixing index (MI) on the micromixing performance within the fabricated 3D microchannels were thoroughly investigated. Consequently, the optimal microchannel configuration for enhanced mixing performance was identified. While the mixing index reaches 100% in all channels (Supporting Information S9), the most favorable parameter set, based on the mixing ratio analysis, was determined to be DP=200μm, DP=200μm, and DC=8mm.

In Supporting Information S9, concentration distributions within a spiral microchannel are presented to evaluate the micromixing efficiency. The plots were superimposed with colored streamlines representing the concentration gradients ranging from 0 mol m−3 (blue) to 1 mol m−3 (red), according to the concentration fields. The uniformly green circular contours around the spiral geometry indicate a near-homogeneous concentration field at the channel outlet. The outermost contour values (~0.50004 mol m−3) reflect a consistent average concentration across multiple radial positions. This suggests that the micromixer achieves complete mixing from the end of the spiral path. The RGB color distribution, converging towards green (0, 255, 0), supports the interpretation of a balanced 1:1 mixing ratio between two fluids of initially distinct concentrations (e.g., red = 1 mol m−3, blue = 0 mol m−3). The intermediate green implies successful superposition and diffusion-dominated mixing. In contrast (Supporting Information S9), a detailed insight into spatial concentration heterogeneity can be obtained via discrete RGB color-coded sampling points extracted along the spiral. Each labeled point (129, 265, 126) represents the RGB value at a specific location, demonstrating the gradual evolution of concentration along the mixing path. Near the channel inlet, colors closer to red or blue dominate (135, 265, 120), reflecting unmixed or partially mixed streams. As moving towards the outlet, the RGB values transition towards green (128, 255, 127), and the inner spiral region has a clear blue-to-green transition, while the red-to-green shift occurs in the outer stream, indicating dual inlet convergence and efficient rotational mixing. This RGB-based concentration mapping illustrates the mixing dynamics in a spiral microchannel and highlights how diffusion and convective streaming lead to full homogenization. The final average outlet concentration of 0.5 mol m−3 confirms that equal-volume streams of 0 mol m−3 and 1 mol m−3 successfully merge.

The high mixing efficiency in the results (Supporting Information S9) can be attributed to the unique flow characteristics within the asymmetric 3D helical geometry. As the fluid moves through each curved segment, centrifugal forces induce secondary flow structures known as Dean vortices46. These counter-rotating vortices generate strong transverse mixing by changing the radius of curvature and continuously redistributing fluid layers across the cross-section. In an asymmetric design, subtle differences in curvature and pitch between segments disturb the flow symmetry, which enhances these secondary flows and leads to more chaotic mixing behavior46. Unlike symmetric mixers, where flow paths are more predictable and stratified, the asymmetry here introduces controlled irregularities that disrupt laminar streamlines, which promotes chaotic advection46. The spiral configuration also provides a long channel length within a compact area, increasing the residence time and allowing mixing to occur over a sustained period. Moreover, the alternating curvature along the channel length causes periodic compression and expansion of the flow, generating localized shear gradients that further accelerate inter-stream diffusion. The RGB values sampled along the spiral (in Supporting Information S9) capture this transition. Initially, distinct red and blue regions are visible near the entrance. However, as the fluid advances through the spiral region, the RGB values progressively shift towards green, indicating the gradual homogenization of concentration.

Here, in three-dimensional microchannel systems, incorporating additional features such as surface roughness or baffles is generally impractical. Instead, flow behavior is primarily governed by the channel geometry and flow velocity. This inherent simplicity reduces the number of design parameters, thereby enabling flow modeling to be more straightforward and computationally efficient. Nevertheless, fully resolving three-dimensional flow fields remains highly time-consuming and computationally demanding. To address this challenge, it is often more rational to adopt a design strategy that enables comprehensive two-dimensional visualization of the flow field while preserving the essential characteristics of three-dimensional flow.

Helical microchannel geometries could be considered as candidates for this purpose; however, they suffer from significant limitations. When viewed from above, only the flow profile of the outermost coil is visible, which limits meaningful assessment of key parameters such as the mixing ratio, mixing length, and mixing time. To overcome these constraints, a CHM structure is proposed. This configuration allows the flow profile of each circular segment to be clearly captured from the top view, effectively yielding a two-dimensional projection of the overall flow field. Such visualization not only simplifies experimental observation but also improves the accuracy and efficiency of numerical modeling, offering a practical and versatile alternative for evaluating mixing behavior and performance.

Beyond visualization, the novel CHM structure achieves near-complete mixing efficiency approaching (~100%) without conventional structural enhancements such as surface roughness or internal obstacles. This represents a significant advancement in the micromixer design compared to conventional micromixers (Supporting Information S10). Furthermore, an original parametric study was conducted to investigate the interplay between the geometry and flow velocity, employing RGB-based image analysis as a methodological innovation. The quantitative results highlight the superior performance of the CHM (DP = 100 μm, DC = 4 mm, HT = 20 mm) with the smallest mixing length of 10.96 mm, mixing time of 0.01721 s at FR = 300 μL min−1, mixing ratios exceeding 90%, and mixing index approaching 100%. Collectively, these findings demonstrate that the CHM not only overcomes the practical limitations of traditional 2D and 3D helical micromixers but also sets a new benchmark for the efficiency and design simplicity in microfluidic mixing systems.

Another important issue is the pressure drop and deformation of the microfluidic chip, due to the deformable material, which directly affects the mixing performance58. To validate the operational robustness of the CHM, comprehensive 3D Fluid-Structure Interaction simulations were performed using the COMSOL Multiphysics software, revealing that structural deformation of the PDMS substrate remains negligible, with a peak wall displacement of just 7.69 × 10−3 mm, even at a maximum inlet gauge pressure of 92.7 kPa, which confirms the mechanical integrity of the device across its full operating range (Supporting Information S10). Pressure drop analysis over four orders of magnitude (0.1–2500 µL min−1) demonstrated a strictly linear ΔP–Q relationship on a log-log scale, consistent with Hagen-Poiseuille-governed laminar flow, with ΔP remaining as low as 0.11 kPa at 1 µL min−1 and not exceeding 87 kPa even at 500 µL min−1, well within the tolerances of standard PDMS assemblies. Crucially, this low hydraulic cost does not compromise the mixing performance. Continuously varying the radius of curvature of the conical helix sustains Dean vortex reorganization along the entire channel length, thereby driving chaotic advection and yielding high mixing indices without relying on sharp bends, abrupt transitions, or embedded obstacles. These results propose the CHM as a geometrically efficient, pressure-tolerant, and mechanically stable platform, particularly well-suited for biological and pressure-sensitive microfluidic applications.

Another important aspect of the present study is the systematic comparison between the full three-dimensional (3D) conical-helix geometry and its reduced-order two-dimensional (2D) planar representation (Supporting Information S11). To evaluate the geometric fidelity of the simplified model, the true 3D centreline length of the conical helix was directly compared with the effective planar projected flow-path length extracted from the CAD geometry. The planar flow-path was approximated by the arithmetic mean of the projected inner and outer spiral arc lengths, thereby representing the effective channel centreline trajectory. The projection-induced deviation was subsequently quantified by normalizing the difference between the true 3D centreline length and the effective planar centreline length to the length of the original 3D centreline. Remarkably, the resulting discrepancies remained consistently minimal across all investigated configurations, generally below 1% and, in all cases, below 2%. These findings demonstrate that the reduced-order planar representation preserves the dominant geometric characteristics of the original 3D conical-helix flow path with negligible loss of geometric fidelity, while simultaneously enabling substantially lower computational cost and more tractable high-throughput parametric exploration.

To summarize, the interplay among Dean vortex-induced convection, asymmetry-driven chaotic advection, extended path length, and diffusion results in an exceptional mixing performance. The data and images confirm that the proposed 3D micromixer achieves complete homogenization, making it a robust and efficient solution for microfluidic applications that require precise, rapid mixing. Thus, the designed 3D spiral micromixer ensures effective, symmetric mixing with low deviation, making it suitable for applications requiring rapid homogenization in compact geometries.

Conclusion

This study investigated the mixing performance of a new-generation three-dimensional (3D) CHM specifically designed for microfluidic applications. Extensive CFD simulations were conducted to characterize the flow and concentration fields over a broad parametric space, and the resulting datasets were subsequently analyzed using Python-based statistical and machine-learning frameworks. The key hydrodynamic and mixing metrics, including mixing ratio (MR), mixing length (ML), and mixing time (MT), were systematically evaluated. To assess predictive capability and parameter sensitivity, multiple regression and classification approaches were implemented using ensemble and nonlinear machine learning algorithms, including Voting Ensemble, LightGBM, XGBoost, Random Forest, and CatBoost. The results demonstrated that nonlinear ensemble-based approaches consistently outperformed linear models, achieving a high predictive accuracy and strong R2 performance across all target variables. These findings confirm the robustness of the proposed CFD-ML-assisted framework and highlight its potential for high-throughput optimization and intelligent design of passive microfluidic systems for drug synthesis, particle focusing, cell separation, and point-of-care diagnostic technologies.

From a fluid-mechanics perspective, CHMs introduce fundamentally different transport physics compared with conventional planar spiral and standard helical micromixers by combining continuously varying curvature and torsion along the channel trajectory. This spatially evolving geometry continuously modulates the Dean vortex structure, enhances cross-sectional advection, suppresses concentration stratification, and promotes strong transverse transport even in low-Re regimes where conventional 2D spiral micromixers often exhibit limited performance. Consequently, the proposed CHM configuration achieved shorter mixing lengths, substantially reduced mixing times, and near-complete homogenization over compact channel distances without requiring embedded obstacles, roughness elements, or external energy input. Experimental flow-visualization studies further demonstrate a qualitative agreement with CFD-predicted concentration fields across diffusion-dominated and inertia-enhanced operating regimes, supporting the physical validity of the proposed design framework.

Another key contribution of this study is the systematic comparison between the full 3D conical-helix geometry and its reduced-order planar representation. To evaluate geometric fidelity, the true 3D centreline length was directly compared with the effective planar projected flow-path length extracted from the CAD geometry. The planar flow path was approximated using the arithmetic mean of the projected inner and outer spiral trajectories to represent the effective channel centreline. The resulting projection-induced deviations remained consistently low across all investigated geometries, generally below 1% and below 2% in all cases, confirming that the reduced-order planar representation preserves the dominant geometric characteristics of the original 3D conical-helix flow path with negligible geometric distortion, which can be considered to have an ignorable effect on mixing indices. This finding is particularly significant because it validates the use of computationally efficient reduced-order analysis while maintaining a close physical consistency with the original 3D geometry, thereby enabling substantially lower computational cost and more tractable large-scale parametric optimization. Thus, the results propose the CHM as a compact, fabrication-ready, and physically robust passive mixing platform for next-generation lab-on-a-chip and microreactor technologies.

Materials and methods

The CHM geometries with specific parameters, namely DP (µm) denoting the cross-sectional diameter of the microchannel, DC (mm) representing the diameter of the first helical loop in an overhead (top-down) view and corresponding to a complete circular turn, HT (mm) indicating the total height of the microchannel, are segmented into five equal sections with a uniform vertical spacing between each loop, depending on the geometric parameters in Supporting Information S12, and were designed in the SolidWorks 2022 software (Fig. 1) and imported into the COMSOL Multiphysics 6.4 software for numerical modeling (Fig. 5).

The geometry of the CHM is adjusted by the local radius of curvature, RC (Supporting Information S13), which governs the strength of Dean vortices. The micromixing simulations combine laminar fluid flow, mass transport between two streams, and coupled reactive flow and species transport. This coupled framework provides the concentration distributions used to evaluate micromixing performance at high spatial resolution (Supporting Information S14).

The primary quantitative workflow was based on calibrated two-dimensional top-view analysis of CFD-derived concentration maps. For quantitative image analysis, top-view concentration maps were exported from the CFD simulations at 300 DPI and a resolution of 4096 × 4096 pixels using a fixed viewpoint and concentration color scale. Therefore, the analyzed images represented color-mapped CFD concentration fields rather than experimental images. The RGB values were calibrated against the normalized concentration scale, where 0 mol m−3 and 1 mol m−3 represented the two inlet concentrations, and 0.5 mol m−3 represented the fully mixed target concentration. The resulting pixel distributions were used to calculate the numerical mixing index (MI ≥ 0.95) and mixing ratio (MR) to identify the position along the projected channel path at which the target mixing condition was first achieved. MR was calculated from the proportion of these pixels within the channel region derived from the concentration fields, and ML was defined as the first downstream position where the selected MR threshold (MR ≥ 0.95) was reached.

The mixing length (ML) was calculated from this position, whereas the mixing time (MT) was determined from the corresponding simulation-derived transport time. Accordingly, MI, MR, ML, and MT obtained through this procedure are simulation-derived metrics calculated from calibrated two-dimensional top-view representations of CFD concentration fields. They should not be interpreted as direct volumetric integrations of the native three-dimensional CFD field or as quantities calculated from experimental images.

In the final step, the initially defined parameters, FR (and indirectly Re), DP, DC, and HT, together with the resulting MR, ML, and MT values, were evaluated using Voting Ensemble, LightGBM, XGBoost, CatBoost, and Random Forest algorithms for classification and regression analyses (Supporting Information S15–S17 and Fig. 7).

Experimental studies

Device fabrication

Initial flow experiments were performed using a representative design with the following dimensions: DP=200μm,DC=8mm,and HT=20mm. To form the 3D microchannel, PVA fiber (DP=200μm) was helically wound around conical mandrels shaped to replicate the desired geometry (Fig. 8), followed by thermal treatment to fix the structure.

Subsequently, a base polymer mixture of polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS) and its curing agent at a 10:1 weight ratio was added to the casting chamber to encapsulate the PVA-template assembly. After completing the thermal curing process, the solidified PDMS construct was demoulded and immersed in deionized water to selectively dissolve the sacrificial PVA. This process yielded monolithic PDMS-based CHMs with smooth internal surfaces and precise structural integrity for subsequent microfluidic tests. Recent advances in manufacturing techniques that require stepwise solution processes, including design optimization, mask preparation, and microfabrication, now enable microfluidic chips to be fabricated in almost any geometry. What ultimately governs this process is not technological limitations but rather the architecture of the microfluidic chip and sacrificial structures used in its formation. Once these conditions are fulfilled, fabrication becomes straightforward, opening the door to versatile and accessible microfluidic platforms.

Experimental procedure and mixing index evaluation

Rhodamine-B (RhB, λexciation ~ 545 nm, λemission ~ 566 nm, Merck KGaA, Darmstadt, Germany) was dissolved in double-deionized water (dd-H₂O) under magnetic stirring to prepare a homogeneous 0.5 mM solution. Fluids were delivered using a programmable dual-syringe pump (KD Scientific, Legato 200; syringe capacity: 0.5 µL–140 mL). In typical experiments, 40 mL of dd-H₂O and 40 mL of the 0.5 mM Rhodamine B solution were infused at identical flow rates.

Due to stage motion constraints, a laser scanning confocal microscopy (Carl-Zeiss LSM 710 Confocal Microscope (with UV laser and 458, 488, 514, 561, 633 nm laser units)) could not produce or analyze a complete three-dimensional image of the entire structure due to motion constraints. Although two-dimensional reconstructions could be generated, a 5-mm microchannel height (HT) sampled at 1-µm resolution would yield ≥5000 images, rendering comprehensive analysis impractical. Instead, we quantified mixing by extracting fluorescence intensity profiles at the channel outlet, using ZEN Blue 3.5 edition, and determining the mixing index. To evaluate the mixing performance of CHMs, straight microchannels were added to each inlet and outlet (Fig. 9), and intensity profiles were measured only along the linear profile lines. These intensity profiles served as the basis for experimental analysis of the mixing performance (Supporting Information S18).

Throughout the entire set of investigated microchannel geometries, a concentration distribution approaching (C = 0.5 mol m−3) was consistently observed, indicating effective homogenization between the inlet streams and confirming the reliability of the mixing index as a representative metric for quantifying the mixing performance. The results further demonstrate that near-complete mixing can be achieved within the first loop of the CHM geometry. To quantify the experimental mixing behavior, fluorescence micrographs were analyzed by extracting transverse intensity profiles from a predefined strip located at the channel outlet, and the mixing performance was subsequently evaluated using the relative mixing index (MI) definition59 (Supporting Information S18).
