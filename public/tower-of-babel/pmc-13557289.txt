Trichoscopy of Scalp Folliculotropic Mycosis Fungoides: A Review of the Literature
Authors: Francesca Pampaloni, Stephano Cedirian, Daniel Fernandez Melo, Aurora Maria Alessandrini, Alessandro Pileri, Bianca Maria Piraccini, Michela Starace
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMycosis fungoides (MF) is the most common type of T-cell lymphoma and is characterized by the infiltration of the epidermis by small to medium-sized atypical T lymphocytes. Folliculotropic MF (FMF) is a rare clinicopathological variant of MF characterized by preferential infiltration of the follicular epithelium. Clinical presentation of FMF can vary from follicle-based patches to plaques, nodules, and tumors, with cicatricial or not alopecia. The most involved site is the head, especially the face.ObjectiveThe aim of this review was to assess the main trichoscopic findings reported in scalp lesions.MethodsA comprehensive literature search of PubMed was performed.ResultsTen studies were included; the trichoscopic features reported were decreased number of pilosebaceous units, dotted dilated vessels, white/yellow scales, and perifollicular halos. The main clinical presentation reported were patchy alopecia or generalized alopecia. Most of the studies included were case reports, so studies with larger sample sizes are strongly needed to make trichoscopy a complementary diagnostic tool to improve the differential diagnoses between FMF and other scalp disorders.ConclusionTrichoscopy plays a key role in diagnosing FMF of the scalp, revealing characteristic features such as milky-red globules, orange-yellow patches, comedones, and distinct vascular patterns.

Introduction

Mycosis fungoides (MF) is the classic and most common type of T-cell lymphoma. It is characterized by epidermic infiltration of atypical small to medium-sized T lymphocytes. MF typically affects patients with an average age of 60 years, with a male-to-female ratio of approximately 2:1 [1]. MF has an indolent clinical course and demonstrates a wide range of manifestations, from erythematous patches and plaques to nodular lesions. Besides the conventional form, several subtypes have been described, such as pagetoid reticulosis, poikilodermatous mycosis fungoides, granulomatous slack skin, hypopigmented or hyperpigmented variants, and folliculotropic mycosis fungoides (FMF) [1]. FMF accounts for 17.8% of the MF types and 5% of all primary cutaneous lymphomas. It is, therefore, the least common variant of MF [1,2]. The majority of studies have reported that the condition occurs more frequently in males, with male-to-female ratios ranging between 2:1 and 5:1 [3]. In FMF, atypical T lymphocytes invade the follicular epithelium, with or without follicular mucinosis or infiltration of the epidermis [4]. Follicular mucinosis refers to mucin deposits within the follicular epithelium, and can be classified into primary (idiopathic, without underlying disease) and secondary follicular mucinosis, which usually occurs in association with systemic disorders like MF, and to a lesser extent, hematological disorders, non-lymphoid skin conditions, or drug-induced conditions [4,5]. In MF the head and face are the main affected sites (up to 70% of the time). It manifests as follicle-based lesion, including patches, plaques, nodules, acne-like eruptions, and papules that mimic folliculitis [6,7]. Up to 65% of patients have some form of alopecia, often associated with severe pruritus [3,7]. The clinical spectrum of FMF-related alopecia includes cicatricial and non-cicatricial forms such as alopecia areata, a combination of both, and generalized hair loss [7–11].

Objective

Unlike its clinical and histopathological features, there are limited data available on trichoscopy of FMF. Our review aimed to highlight the current understanding trichoscopic findings of scalp FMF.

Methods

A comprehensive literature search of PubMed was performed from the database’s inception to 01 December 2025. The search terms were “mycosis fungoides”, “folliculotropic”, “follicular”, “dermoscopy”, and “trichoscopy”. Titles and abstracts of all retrieved articles were preliminarily evaluated for eligibility. In cases where these sections lacked adequate information to apply the predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria, the complete manuscripts were subsequently reviewed in full.

Eligibility Criteria

All articles reporting data about FMF trichoscopic findings were selected. We restricted our search to publications in English and excluded abstracts, article reviews, and studies on non-human subjects.

Search Strategies

The final search terms were “folliculotropic mycosis fungoides” OR “follicular mycosis fungoides” AND “dermoscopy” OR “trichoscopy”, resulting in 767 articles. After screening titles and abstracts, articles were deleted due to being unrelated to trichoscopic findings in folliculotropic mycosis fungoides, and the full text of the remaining 26 articles was evaluated. This process excluded 16 of these articles and left 10 for analysis. Among these ten articles, there were seven case reports, two case series, and one cross-sectional study.

Results

We found seven publications about patchy alopecia [10,12–17], one reporting two cases of alopecia universalis [11], and another of both patchy-plaque and generalized alopecia [8]. One paper did not mention the clinical presentation [18]. Most of the included articles involved adult patients, with only two studies reporting cases in pediatric patients aged 10 and 12 years [16,17]. Discussion

This narrative review summarizes the main trichoscopic features of FMF. Rudnicka et al. detailed several distinctive features in their ‘Atlas of Trichoscopy’ [12]: orange-yellow patchy areas, milky-red globules, comedonal lesions within alopecic patches, and a vascular granular well-margined pattern, which likely reflect perifollicular inflammation due to lymphocytic infiltrates.

Miteva et al. [11] presented two cases of alopecia universalis, where dermoscopy revealed features such as diffuse erythema, empty follicular openings containing keratotic plugs or filiform spicules, mild scaling, black dots, and scattered broken hairs. Slawinska et al. [13] reported various findings, including decreased pilosebaceous units, an increase in single-hair follicular units, white and yellow scales, milky-white globules, yellow dots (with or without black dots or broken hairs), short hairs with split ends, short hairs with triangular-shaped ends, short broken hairs, and pigtail hair. They additionally noted regions containing white dots and linear structures, which corresponded to hair follicles replaced by fibrosis, providing a direct trichoscopic–histopathological correlation with scarring evolution. In a series of three cases, Geller et al. [14] identified perifollicular halos and broken hairs as notable trichoscopy findings in a case of FMF involving the hairy scalp, suggesting perifollicular inflammatory infiltrates with secondary hair shaft damage. Souissi et al. [15] reported a case of FMF with early scalp manifestations, where trichoscopy revealed keratinaceous debris surrounding follicle openings and multiple keratotic cone-shaped spicules on the neck and upper back resembling small spiky keratotic lesions, likely corresponding to early folliculotropism and follicular hyperkeratosis. Kreutzer KM et al. [10] documented a case of scarring alopecia characterized by the complete loss of adnexal structures and the presence of comedonal lesions, reflecting advanced follicular destruction with fibrotic replacement. Trichoscopic examination in 18 patients reported by Gallo et al. [9] revealed six features referable to nonscarring alopecia. More than half of the patients had decreased number of pilosebaceous units (single hair) (83.3%), dotted dilated vessels (77.8%), broken-dystrophic hairs (66.7%), vellus hairs (61.1%), spermatozoa-like pattern vessels (55.6%), and yellow-dots (55.6%) (Figure 1), findings compatible with superficial perifollicular infiltration without permanent follicular destruction. A minority of patients (16.6%–44.4%) presented features referable to both nonscarring and scarring alopecia. Among those typical of nonscarring, they identified dilation of follicular openings, scales-crusts, purpuric dots, short hairs with split ends, pigtail hairs, perifollicular hyperkeratosis, milky-white globules, and black dots. The ones found that are more typical of scarring alopecia were white dots/lines and the absence of follicles-replaced fibrosis, indicating irreversible adnexal loss. The authors also assessed the trichoscopic findings from the two clinical subgroups reported: generalized alopecia and patchy-plaque alopecia. More than half of the people in each group presented the same six features: dotted dilated vessels, decreased number of pilosebaceous units/single hair, vellus hair, broken/dystrophic hairs, spermatozoa-like pattern vessels, yellow-dots. No differences were identified in the less frequently observed abnormalities, apart from the presence of short hairs with split ends and the lack of follicular dots. The first finding was not seen in patients with generalized alopecia but was identified in 38.5% (5/13) of those with patchy alopecia. Conversely, follicular dots were absent 40% of the time in generalized and 15.4% of the time in patchy alopecia, suggesting variable degrees of follicular preservation across clinical phenotypes. In a sample of 26 cases of FMF, Errichetti E et al. [18] reported the following in decreasing order of prevalence: lack of hairs (92.3%), dilated follicles (80.8%), follicular plugs (61.5%), white scales (42.3%), dotted vessels, perifollicular white color, and follicular red dots (30.8% each); linear curved vessels (15.4%), linear vessels with branches and focal white structureless areas (11.5% each), and yellow scales/crusts and white circles (7.7% each) (Figure 1), reinforcing the concept that follicular plugging and vessel involvement are among the most consistent trichoscopic hallmarks of FMF.

It remains unclear whether the described trichoscopic findings can be related to the two different patterns of clinicopathological features reported, early stage and advanced stage, each with different prognostic implications [19]. Patients with plaques with histologically dense perifollicular infiltrates, tumors, or erythroderma developed indolent and aggressive FMF, while patients with patches or follicular papules and patients with plaques histologically characterized by sparse intrafollicular or perifollicular infiltrates containing few and small neoplastic T cells showed a better prognosis [19,20].

Based on the available data, it can be hypothesized that most cases of FMF initially manifest as nonscarring alopecia, reflecting early-stage disease with predominantly superficial follicular involvement identifiable by trichoscopy and confirmed histologically. Conversely, FMF cases presenting as scarring alopecia appear to represent more advanced disease stages, clinically characterized by plaques and histopathologically by deeper perifollicular infiltration and fibrosis.

Trichoscopy represent a useful tool to differentiate FMF from other more common dermatoses. The main differential diagnoses of FMF of the scalp include alopecia areata, lichen planopilaris, discoid lupus erythematosus, seborrheic dermatitis, psoriasis, tinea capitis, and follicular mucinosis. These conditions may closely resemble FMF from both a clinical and a trichoscopic perspective, particularly when they manifest as patchy or diffuse alopecia. Several trichoscopic features are helpful in differentiating FMF from its main mimickers. Alopecia areata is typically characterized by yellow dots, black dots, exclamation mark hairs, and short vellus hairs [21], lichen planopilaris and discoid lupus erythematosus frequently show perifollicular scaling, blue-grey dots, and loss of follicular openings [21], and seborrheic dermatitis and psoriasis predominantly display diffuse scaling and disease-specific vascular patterns [22]. Tinea capitis can be similar from a clinical point of view causing a patchy alopecia, but trichoscopic signs are extremely different (comma hairs, corkscrew hairs, zigzag hairs, Morse code-like hairs) except for black dots, broken hairs, perifollicular scaling, and interfollicular scaling [23], and typical trichoscopic findings in follicular mucinosis are brownish-yellow dots and dilated follicular opening that represent dilated follicular ostia filled with keratin and mucin, follicular red dots that represent dilated blood vessels surrounding the affected hair follicles, and hair shaft abnormalities which include broken or fragmented hairs, twisted hairs, and coiled hairs, reflecting the disruption of normal hair growth. The dermoscopic appearance of mucin accumulation along the hair shaft is known as the “toothpaste signs”. Perifollicular scaling is also described but is not typical of follicular mucinosis [24–26]. The absence of follicular ostia allows distinguishing between scarring and nonscarring alopecia.

Differentiating between FMF and follicular mucinosis can be challenging. Factors such as age at onset, clinical progression, lesion distribution, and ancillary tests (e.g., T-cell receptor gene rearrangement) should be considered during long-term follow-up. Trichoscopic signs provide a rapid and effective method for guiding diagnosis [5].

Although a recent systematic review on dermoscopic and trichoscopic features of folliculotropic mycosis fungoides has been published [27], our review differs substantially in scope and inclusion criteria. Specifically, we focused exclusively on scalp involvement, which led to the exclusion of several previously reported studies lacking precise localization or involving non-scalp sites while also allowing the inclusion of additional scalp-specific and trichoscopic references that were not considered in the earlier review.

Conclusions

In conclusion, the diagnosis of FMF relies on a combination of clinical and histopathological findings. This comprehensive review endeavored to consolidate our current knowledge about the trichoscopic manifestations of FMF affecting the scalp. FMF often manifests as either patchy or generalized/universalis alopecia, with a range of pertinent trichoscopic findings emerging in the literature. These encompass milky-red globules, orange-yellow patchy areas, comedonal lesions, diffuse erythema, and distinctive vascular patterns like dotted, dilated, and spermatozoa-like vessels. Additionally, trichoscopic observations include empty follicular openings filled with keratotic plugs or filiform spicules, white/yellow scales, black/yellow dots, and the presence of vellus and broken hairs.

While there has been a growing interest in this field in recent years, it is worth noting that most studies included in our literature review relied primarily on findings from case reports and limited case series. Thus, future research efforts should prioritize larger sample sizes to enhance the diagnostic utility of trichoscopy, allowing for improved differentiation between FMF and other scalp-related disorders.
