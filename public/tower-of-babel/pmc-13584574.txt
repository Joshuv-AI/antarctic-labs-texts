Synthesis and Fundamental Evaluation of [67Ga]Ga-NOTA‑D‑KV6 as a Potential Cationic Amphiphilic Peptide Probe for Tumor Imaging
Authors: Takeshi Chiga, Takeshi Fuchigami, Akari Matsuura, Masayuki Munekane, Kenji Mishiro, Sakura Yoshida, Makoto Oba, Hiroshi Wakabayashi, Seigo Kinuya, Kazuma Ogawa
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SVS-1, a cationic amphiphilic peptide (CAP), adopts a β-hairpin conformation only upon interacting with the surface of cancer cells, thereby disrupting their membrane. Previously, we developed [67Ga]­Ga-NOTA-KV6, an SVS-1 derivative for tumor imaging, which demonstrated favorable early tumor uptake but poor retention. To address this limitation, we designed and evaluated a novel d-amino acid–based SVS-1 analogue, [67Ga]­Ga-NOTA-d-KV6, to enhance metabolic stability and improve pharmacokinetic properties. Fluorescein isothiocyanate (FITC)-labeled d-KV6 exhibited high accumulation in cancer cells, similar to that of FITC-KV6. In addition, uptake of [67Ga]­Ga-NOTA-d-KV6 was significantly greater in cancer cells than in normal cells. Similar to [67Ga]­Ga-NOTA-KV6, cellular uptake of [67Ga]­Ga-NOTA-d-KV6 in cancer cells was significantly reduced at low temperature (4 °C) and in the presence of several different types of endocytic inhibitors, indicating internalization via multiple endocytosis pathways. Metabolic stability studies revealed that [67Ga]­Ga-NOTA-d-KV6 was significantly more stable than [67Ga]­Ga-NOTA-KV6. An in vivo biodistribution study of [67Ga]­Ga-NOTA-d-KV6 revealed low blood retention, high accumulation in the liver and kidneys, and minimal uptake by other organs. Tumor accumulation of [67Ga]­Ga-NOTA-d-KV6 was moderate, but tumor retention was improved compared with that of [67Ga]­Ga-NOTA-KV6, leading to a tumor-to-blood ratio of 17.3 at 240 min postinjection. These findings demonstrate that d-amino acid substitution improved the plasma stability and tumor retention of the CAP derivative while maintaining preferential uptake in cancer cells. Although further optimization is required to reduce liver and kidney accumulation and improve tumor-to-background contrast, mirror peptide formation represents a promising strategy for the development of CAP-based cancer theranostic agents.

Introduction

Certain cationic amphiphilic peptides (CAPs) exhibit antitumor activity owing to their unique combination of cationic and hydrophobic amphiphilic properties. Among these, peptides such as SVS-1 (KVKVKVKVdPPTKVKVKVK-NH2; hereafter referred to as KV6) have demonstrated the ability to selectively target and kill cancer cells. − CAPs exert anticancer effects primarily through electrostatic interactions with the negatively charged cancer cell membranes. Importantly, these peptides exhibit minimal toxicity toward normal healthy cells, as the surface membranes of such cells typically carry a neutral charge. KV6 undergoes a conformational change into a β-hairpin structure exclusively on contact with cancer cell surfaces, enabling it to disrupt their membranes preferentially. − It has also been reported to serve as a carrier system for the targeted intracellular delivery of anticancer agents or nanoparticles to cancer cells. Positron emission tomography (PET) and single-photon emission computed tomography (SPECT) provide ultrasensitive and noninvasive deep-tissue visualization of tumors. Moreover, theranostic ligands integrating diagnostic and therapeutic radionuclides into a single compound have proven effective for precise cancer diagnosis and treatment. CAPs have been reported to improve the in vivo behavior and target specificity of cancer-targeting molecules, including radiopharmaceuticals. We previously developed several [67Ga]­Ga-labeled KV6 analogs by modifying the peptide chain length or replacing lysine residues with alternative amino acids such as arginine or histidine. The macrocyclic chelator 1,4,7-triazacyclononane-1,4,7-triacetic acid (NOTA), along with its analogs, has been recognized as one of the most stable chelators for radiogallium. Consequently, we developed several NOTA-CAPs as radiogallium-labeled radioligands for cancer imaging. The results of this study confirmed the promising potential of [67Ga]­Ga-NOTA-KV6 as a tumor-targeted radioligand. However, substantial tracer localization in the liver and kidneys was evident at early time points, whereas blood retention and tumor accumulation decreased over time. Since [67Ga]­Ga-NOTA-KV6 is primarily composed of l-amino acids, it could be degraded by endogenous enzymes in the blood or other compartments and eliminated before reaching the tumor tissue. Indeed, several d-peptides have been reported to serve as effective in vivo imaging agents, demonstrating superior proteolytic resistance and prolonged biological half-lives. Therefore, improving the metabolic stability and pharmacokinetic properties of [67Ga]­Ga-NOTA-CAPs remains an important objective. It has been reported that substituting l-amino acid residues in anticancer and antibacterial peptides with their corresponding d-amino acid residues enhances their stability against proteases such as trypsin, largely retaining their biological activity. , Because CAPs are not internalized through receptor-specific binding mechanisms, it has been reported that the mirror peptide d-KV6, mainly composed of d-amino acids, exhibits a similar anticancer mechanism with minimal changes in antitumor efficacy.

In this study, we synthesized d-KV6 by inverting the d- and l-configurations of the amino acids in KV6. After fluorescent or 67Ga labeling, we performed fundamental in vitro characterization and in vivo biodistribution evaluations of [67Ga]­Ga-NOTA-d-KV6 as a cancer-imaging probe (Figure ).

Materials and Methods

General

Commercially available reagents were used as received unless otherwise noted. Fluorescein isothiocyanate (FITC) was obtained from Sigma-Aldrich (St. Louis, MO, USA). Fluorenylmethyloxycarbonyl (Fmoc)-ε-aminohexanoic acid (Ahx)-OH was purchased from Merck Millipore (Billerica, MA, USA). The other Fmoc-protected amino acids together with 2-(1H-benzotriazole-1-yl)-1,1,3,3-tetramethyluronium hexafluorophosphate (HBTU), hydroxybenzotriazole (HOBt), and O-(6-Chloro-1H-benzotriazol-1-yl)-N,N,N′,N′-tetramethyluronium hexafluorophosphate (HCTU) were obtained from Watanabe Chemical Industries Co., Ltd. (Hiroshima, Japan). N,N-Diisopropylethylamine (DIPEA) was purchased from Nacalai Tesque Co., Ltd. (Kyoto, Japan). Rink amide AM resin was obtained from Merck Millipore. Mass spectrometry (MS) data were acquired using either an Ultraflex matrix-assisted laser desorption/ionization time-of-flight/time-of-flight mass spectrometer (MALDI-TOF/TOF; Bruker Daltonics, Bremen, Germany) or a JMS-T100TD electrospray ionization mass spectrometer (ESI-MS; JEOL Ltd., Tokyo, Japan). Peptides were purified and characterized using a reverse-phase high-performance liquid chromatography (RP-HPLC) system equipped with an LC-20AD pump, an SPD-20A UV detector monitoring absorbance at 220 nm, and a CTO-20A column oven set to 40 °C (Shimadzu, Kyoto, Japan). Radioactivity counts of the radioligands were measured with an auto well gamma counter (ARC-7010, Aloka Co., Ltd., Tokyo, Japan). Commercially available [67Ga]­Ga-citrate (Nihon Medi-Physics Co., Ltd., Tokyo, Japan) was converted to [67Ga]­GaCl3 using 0.1 M HCl and a Sep-Pak Silica Plus Light Cartridge (Waters Co., Ltd., Milford, MA, USA) as previously reported. Absorbance and fluorescence were measured using a Cytation 3 multimode reader (Biotek, Winooski, VT, USA).

Peptide Synthesis

All peptides were prepared by a stepwise solid-phase method using Fmoc chemistry. Approximately 100–150 mg of Rink amide AM resin (loading capacity, 0.55 mmol/g) was employed for peptide synthesis using Fmoc-protected amino acids. After swelling in N,N-dimethylformamide (DMF) for 18 h, the resin underwent Fmoc deprotection with 20% (v/v) piperidine in DMF. Following DMF washes to eliminate piperidine, the next amino acid was coupled using Fmoc-amino acid (3 equiv), HCTU (3 equiv), and DIPEA (6 equiv) dissolved in DMF (1.2 mL). The sequence of deprotection and coupling was repeated until the target peptide was completely elongated. To prepare fluorescently labeled peptides, FITC (3 equiv) was coupled to the peptides in DMF using DIPEA (6 equiv) as the base at room temperature over 18 h. The N-terminal NOTA conjugate was then prepared according to a reported procedure. Briefly, the resin-bound peptide was treated with bromoacetic acid (5 equiv) and 1-ethyl-3-(3-(dimethylamino)­propyl) carbodiimide (EDC) (2.5 equiv) in dichloromethane (DCM) to introduce a bromoacetyl group at the N-terminus. Subsequently, a solution of 1,4,7-triazacyclononane (5 equiv) in DCM was added to the resin, and the mixture was stirred for 3 h. The remaining secondary amines of the NOTA ring were then alkylated with tert-butyl 2-bromoacetate (3 equiv) in the presence of DIPEA (3 equiv) in 1-methyl-2-pyrrolidone (2 mL) for 2 h. Following completion of the labeling procedure, the peptides were cleaved from the resin using a mixture of trifluoroacetic acid (TFA)/H2O/triisopropylsilane/1,2-ethanedithiol (94:2.5:1:2.5, v/v/v/v) for 90 min with gentle agitation. The resulting filtrate was separated from the resin and added to cold diethyl ether to precipitate the peptides. The peptide precipitate was collected by centrifugation at 2,500g for 5 min, washed twice with diethyl ether, and pelleted again by centrifugation after each wash. Purification of the crude products was performed by HPLC using a Cosmosil C18 column (5C18-AR-II, 4.6 × 150 mm; Nacalai Tesque) with a water/acetonitrile gradient containing 0.1% TFA at a flow rate of 1.0 mL/min. After HPLC purification, FITC-d-KV6 and FITC-KV6 were obtained at yields of 7% and 24%, respectively. Each eluted peptide was analyzed using ESI-MS (Table S1).

Synthesis of Ga-NOTA-KV6 Derivatives

Ga-NOTA-KV6 was synthesized as previously reported. For the synthesis of Ga-NOTA-d-KV6, NOTA-d-KV6 (0.7 mg, 0.29 μmol) and Ga­(NO3)3·xH2O (0.74 mg) were dissolved in 800 μL of 0.4 M 4-(2-hydroxyethyl)-1-piperazineethanesulfonic acid (HEPES) (pH 4.0), and heated at 95 °C for 30 min (Scheme ). The reaction mixture was purified by RP-HPLC using a Cosmosil 5C18-AR-II column (4.6 × 150 mm). Elution was performed at 1.0 mL/min with a linear gradient from 10% to 45% acetonitrile in 0.1% aqueous TFA over 30 min, affording Ga-NOTA-d-KV6 as a white solid (0.44 mg, 0.18 μmol, 63%). The peptides were analyzed using ESI-MS (Table S1).

Synthesis of [67Ga]­Ga-NOTA-D-KV6

A 15 μL aliquot of [67Ga]­GaCl3 (74 MBq/mL, 1.1 MBq) was added to 15 μL of a 1.0 mM NOTA-d-KV6 solution prepared in 0.4 M HEPES (pH 4.0), and the mixture was heated at 95 °C for 30 min. The product was then purified by RP-HPLC on a Cosmosil 5C18-AR-II column (4.6 × 150 mm) using a linear gradient of 13–19% acetonitrile in 0.1% aqueous TFA over 40 min at a flow rate of 1.0 mL/min to afford [67Ga]­Ga-NOTA-d-KV6. The product was identified as [67Ga]­Ga-NOTA-d-KV6 using HPLC analysis after the coinjection of nonradioactive Ga-NOTA-d-KV6. [67Ga]­Ga-NOTA-d-KV6 was obtained in a decay-corrected radiochemical yield of 78%. Radiochemical purity exceeded 95% as confirmed by HPLC analysis under the same conditions as described above.

Synthesis of [67Ga]­Ga-NOTA-KV6

A 15 μL aliquot of [67Ga]­GaCl3 (74 MBq/mL, 1.1 MBq) was added to 15 μL of a 1.0 mM NOTA-KV6 solution prepared in 0.4 M HEPES (pH 4.0), and the mixture was heated at 95 °C for 30 min. The product was then purified using RP-HPLC on a Cosmosil 5C18-AR-II column (4.6 × 150 mm) using a linear gradient of 13–19% acetonitrile in 0.1% aqueous TFA over 40 min at a flow rate of 1.0 mL/min to afford [67Ga]­Ga-NOTA-KV6. The product was identified as [67Ga]­Ga-NOTA-KV6 using HPLC analysis after the coinjection of nonradioactive Ga-NOTA-KV6. The decay-corrected radiochemical yield of [67Ga]­Ga-NOTA-KV6 was 80%. Radiochemical purity exceeded 95% as confirmed by HPLC analysis under the same conditions as described above.

Determination of the Partition Coefficients

The octanol-to-water partition coefficients of [67Ga]­Ga-NOTA-d-KV6 and [67Ga]­Ga-NOTA-KV6 were determined as described previously. Radiolabeled peptides were allowed to equilibrate between 1-octanol and 0.1 M phosphate buffer (PB, pH 7.4), after which the radioactivity in each phase was quantified using an auto well gamma counter. The partition coefficients were calculated from the ratio of radioactivity in the 1-octanol phase to that in the aqueous phase and expressed as the common logarithm (log P).

Preparation of Liposomes

Ten mM 1,2-dimyristoyl-sn-glycero-3-phosphocholine (DMPC) and 10 mM 1,2-dioleoyl-sn-glycero-3-phospho-l-serine (DOPS) sodium salt were combined in the specified molar ratios (100:0, 95:5, 90:10, 50:50) in a round-bottom flask and subsequently dissolved in CHCl3. After complete dissolution of the phospholipids, the organic solvent was removed by gradual evaporation under reduced pressure, resulting in the formation of a thin lipid film on the flask wall. The resulting lipid film was hydrated with either 500 μL of 10 mM HEPES buffer (pH 7.4) for dynamic light scattering (DLS) and zeta potential measurements or 500 μL of buffer containing 50 mM Bis-Tris propane (BTP) and 150 mM NaF (pH 7.4) for circular dichroism (CD) spectroscopy. The suspension was heated at 60 °C until the lipid film detached from the flask wall, vortexed until completely dispersed, and extruded through a 100 nm membrane filter at 60 °C. For DLS and CD measurements, the liposome suspension was purified by gel filtration using a PD-10 column (Cytiva, Marlborough, MA, USA). DLS measurements were performed within 2 h of liposome preparation.

Zeta Potential Analysis of NOTA-D-KV6-Liposome Mixtures

Liposomal suspensions were incubated with NOTA-d-KV6 at final concentrations of 10 or 20 μM for 1 h at 37 °C. Liposome solutions without NOTA-d-KV6 were incubated under the same conditions. The particle size and zeta (ζ) potential were determined using a Zetasizer Nano-ZS system (Malvern Panalytical, Malvern, United Kingdom) equipped with a 633 nm He–Ne laser. Zeta potential measurements were performed within 4 h of liposome preparation.

Circular Dichroism Spectroscopy

Each liposomal suspension was incubated with 0.15 mM NOTA-d-KV6 prepared in pH 7.4 buffer containing 50 mM Bis-Tris propane (BTP) and 150 mM NaF for 1 h. Circular dichroism spectra were acquired over the wavelength range of 200–250 nm using a JASCO J-820 spectropolarimeter (JASCO) equipped with a 1.0 mm path length cell. The spectra were converted to residue molar ellipticity, [θ]R (deg·cm2·dmol–1). CD measurements were performed within 4 h of liposome preparation.

In Vitro Stability of [67Ga]­Ga-NOTA-D-KV6 in PB

An aliquot (100 μL) of [67Ga]­Ga-NOTA-d-KV6 (37 kBq) prepared in 0.1 M PB (pH 7.4) was incubated at 37 °C for 60, 120, or 240 min. At each time point, samples were withdrawn and analyzed by RP-HPLC with radioactivity detection.

In Vitro Stability of [67Ga]­Ga-NOTA-KV6 Derivatives in Mouse Plasma

The stability of [67Ga]­Ga-NOTA-KV6 derivatives in mouse plasma was evaluated according to a previously described method. Each radiotracer (10 μL, 2.0–3.0 MBq) was combined with 90 μL of freshly isolated mouse plasma and incubated at 37 °C for 60, 120, or 240 min. After incubation, proteins were precipitated by adding 100 μL of ice-cold 2-propanol, followed by centrifugation at 1,700g for 15 min at 4 °C. The resulting supernatant was passed through a 0.45 μm membrane filter prior to RP-HPLC analysis.

Cell Cultures

Mouse fibroblast-like 3T3-L1 and human diploid fibroblast IMR-90 cells were obtained from the JCRB Cell Bank (Tokyo, Japan). Epidermoid carcinoma KB cells were obtained from DS Pharma Biomedical Co. Ltd. (Osaka, Japan). IMR-90 and KB cells were cultured in Eagle’s minimum essential medium containing 10% fetal bovine serum, and 3T3-L1 cells were cultured in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium containing 10% bovine calf serum. All culture media were supplemented with 100 IU/mL penicillin and 100 μg/mL streptomycin. Cell cultures were maintained at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO2.

Measurement of Zeta Potential of IMR-90 Cells

IMR-90 cells were harvested using trypsin and resuspended in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS; pH 7.4), and diluted to 1.0 × 105 cells/mL. The zeta potential was then determined with an ELSZ-2000ZS instrument (Otsuka Electronics, Tokyo, Japan).

Cellular Uptake Study

Each cell line was grown to confluence in a 12-well plate. Following washing with PBS, FITC-KV6 derivatives (1 μM) or [67Ga]­Ga-NOTA-KV6 derivatives (1.0 kBq) in growth medium (0.3 mL) were applied to the wells, followed by incubation at 37 °C. For the blocking study, KB cells were coincubated with [67Ga]­Ga-NOTA-d-KV6 or [67Ga]­Ga-NOTA-KV6 with or without increasing amounts of the corresponding nonradioactive peptide (NOTA-d-KV6 or NOTA-KV6) for 1 h at 37 °C. After incubation, the cells were rinsed twice with 0.3 mL of heparin-containing PBS (20 units/mL) and lysed for 20 min at 37 °C by cell lysis buffer M (Fujifilm Wako, Osaka, Japan). Fluorescence intensity of the lysates was quantified using a Cytation 3 multimode microplate reader in top-read mode with an excitation wavelength of 483 nm and fluorescence detection at 510–600 nm. The detector gain was set to 100, and fluorescence values were corrected by subtracting the background signal obtained from lysates of untreated cells. Radioactivity in the cell lysates was quantified using a gamma counter. Protein concentrations were quantified using a Protein Assay BCA Kit (Nacalai Tesque). Cellular uptake was expressed as the percentage of applied dose (AD) per milligram of protein (% AD/mg protein).

Confocal Fluorescence Imaging of FITC-D-KV6-Treated Cells

KB cells were seeded onto 8-well coverslips (Iwaki, Tokyo, Japan). FITC-d-KV6 (10 μM) was added and incubated for 1 h protected from light. After incubation, the medium was removed, and the cells were rinsed twice with ice-cold PBS followed by two washes with ice-cold heparin/PBS (20 units/mL). For nuclear staining, cells were treated with Hoechst 33342 (2.5 μg/mL) for 30 min, followed by two washes with ice-cold PBS. Fluorescence images were acquired using a confocal laser scanning microscope (LSM710; Carl Zeiss Microscopy GmbH, Jena, Germany) with excitation wavelengths of 405 nm for Hoechst 33342 and 488 nm for FITC-d-KV6.

Assessment of the Internalization Pathway of [67Ga]­Ga-NOTA-D-KV6

Each cell line was cultured in 12-well plates. For experiments with endocytosis inhibitors, cells were incubated with the respective inhibitors for 30 min before the addition of 1.0 kBq of [67Ga]­Ga-NOTA-d-KV6 (0.3 mL), followed by incubation at 37 °C for 1 h. The control group was defined as the uptake (% AD/mg protein) following incubation without the inhibitor at 37 °C for 1 h. EIPA (100 μM) was used as a macropinocytosis inhibitor, nystatin (50 μM) as a caveolae-dependent endocytosis inhibitor, and sucrose (450 mM) diluted in serum-free medium as a clathrin-dependent endocytosis inhibitor to investigate the mechanisms of internalization. In the 4 °C incubation group, cells were maintained at 4 °C for 30 min before peptide addition. The remaining steps were the same as those in the cellular uptake study.

Tumor Xenograft Model

Animal experiments were carried out in accordance with the Guidelines for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals of Kanazawa University. The study protocol was approved by the Committee on Animal Experimentation of Kanazawa University. Female BALB/c nu/nu mice (4 weeks old) were obtained from Japan SLC Inc. (Shizuoka, Japan). Animals were housed under controlled environmental conditions with a 12 h light/dark cycle and provided with unrestricted access to food and water. Each mouse was inoculated subcutaneously in the right shoulder with approximately 1.0 × 107 KB cells. Biodistribution studies were performed after tumors had reached 300–500 mm3, typically 1–2 weeks after inoculation.

Biodistribution of [67Ga]­Ga-NOTA-D-KV6 in Tumor-Bearing Mice

Mice were administered [67Ga]­Ga-NOTA-d-KV6 (10 kBq/100 μL) via intravenous injection through the tail vein. At the designated time points, animals were euthanized, after which the organs were excised and weighed. Radioactivity associated with each tissue was quantified using an auto well gamma counter (n = 4–6), and the results were expressed as the percentage of the injected dose per gram of tissue (% ID/g).

Results and Discussion

Binding of FITC-D-KV6 and FITC-KV6 to KB Cells In Vitro

FITC-labeled d-KV6 (FITC-d-KV6) and the lead compound FITC-KV6 were synthesized as shown in Scheme S1. Their cancer cell-binding properties were evaluated to determine the potential of d-KV6 as a scaffold for cancer diagnostic agents. Cellular uptake experiments of FITC-d-KV6 and FITC-KV6 were conducted using epidermoid carcinoma KB cells and expressed as % AD/mg protein. After 120 min of incubation, FITC-d-KV6 uptake in KB cells was 54.28 ± 7.48% AD/mg protein (Figure ), which was comparable to that of FITC-KV6 (vs 42.49 ± 7.19% AD/mg protein) (P = 0.3511). Confocal fluorescence imaging was performed to investigate the cellular localization of FITC-d-KV6. In our previous study, FITC-KV6 was primarily observed on and near the plasma membrane of KB cells. Similarly, FITC-d-KV6 exhibited a comparable fluorescence distribution pattern (Figure ). The FITC fluorescence was predominantly observed near the plasma membrane, with punctate intracellular fluorescence signals also detected within the cells.

Zeta Potential and Circular Dichroism Spectrum of NOTA-D-KV6 upon Addition of Liposomes

NOTA-d-KV6 was synthesized using a method analogous to that previously reported (Scheme S2). Previous studies have shown that NOTA-CAPs can bind to cancer cells, causing the folding of peptides and integration into lipid bilayers with surfaces enriched with anionic phospholipids. , The binding of NOTA-d-KV6 to liposomes is presumed to occur via electrostatic interactions, making quantitative evaluation challenging. In previous studies, the binding of functional peptides to liposomes has often been evaluated using zeta potential measurements. We compared the zeta potentials to investigate the binding interactions between NOTA-d-KV6 and three types of liposomes with varying phosphatidylcholine (PC) to phosphatidylserine (PS) ratios as biomimetic membrane models. The measurement of the zeta potential of the liposomes revealed negative values for all three types. The zeta potential became more negative as the PS content of the liposomes increased (Figure ). In our previous study, the zeta potential of liposomes shifted toward more positive values in a dose-dependent manner following the addition of NOTA-KV6, and liposomes with a higher PS content showed a greater positive shift in zeta potential. Similarly, for NOTA-d-KV6, the zeta potential of the liposomes shifted toward more positive values with increasing peptide concentration. This suggested that the peptide recognizes anionic phospholipids in the same manner as NOTA-KV6.

Zeta potential of PC/PS liposomes at different PC/PS ratios following incubation with 0, 10, or 20 μM NOTA-d-KV6 in 10 mM HEPES buffer (pH 7.4) for 1 h. Data are presented as the mean ± SD (n = 3).

The peptide structure during the interaction with the cell membrane is a critical factor for cancer-targeting CAPs because it significantly influences their tumor accumulation ability. KV6 is believed to be internalized into cancer cells following its binding to the lipid bilayer and subsequent folding into a β-sheet structure. Indeed, previous studies indicated that both KV6 and NOTA-KV6 exhibited CD spectra characteristic of random-coil structures in neutral phospholipids but displayed spectra indicative of β-sheet conformation in liposomes containing anionic PS. , We considered that NOTA-d-KV6 adopts a β-sheet structure upon binding to negatively charged phospholipids. Because the peptide moiety of NOTA-d-KV6 is the enantiomer of that of NOTA-KV6, its CD spectrum was expected to be the mirror image of that of NOTA-KV6. Therefore, we obtained the CD spectrum of NOTA-d-KV6 under conditions identical to those used in our previous study. The CD spectrum of NOTA-d-KV6 was evaluated in a pH 7.4 buffer to assess changes in the secondary structure of the peptide upon the addition of the prepared liposomes. As shown in Figure , the CD spectrum of NOTA-d-KV6 in buffer exhibited minimal changes upon the addition of neutral DMPC liposomes, indicating that the peptide predominantly adopted a random-coil conformation. In contrast, a substantial spectral shift was observed for the negatively charged liposomes composed of a 1:1 mixture of DMPC and DOPS. The spectrum of NOTA-d-KV6 exhibited maximum ellipticity at approximately 200 nm in the presence of buffer or neutral liposomes. In contrast, upon the addition of anionic liposomes, the spectrum displayed a maximum at approximately 210–220 nm and a negative ellipticity near 200 nm (Figure ). These spectral features were consistent with the mirror-image CD spectrum expected for the d-peptide and indicated a conformational transition comparable to that previously observed for SVS-1 and NOTA-KV6. , This suggested that NOTA-d-KV6 also adopts a β-sheet conformation upon interaction with negatively charged vesicles, indicative of β-hairpin formation. Specifically, the spectra suggest that both the l- and d-forms of KV6 derivatives adopt β-sheet conformations instead of random-coil structures upon interaction with negatively charged regions, such as those found on cancer cell membranes. This result aligns with previous reports on interactions with negatively charged phospholipids, , further supporting the expectation that NOTA-d-KV6 could exhibit similar uptake mechanisms and efficacy in targeting cancer cells.

Radiosynthesis of [67Ga]­Ga-NOTA-CAPs

NOTA-d-KV6 and NOTA-KV6, precursors for 67Ga-radiolabeling, were reacted with [67Ga]­GaCl3 at 95 °C for 30 min to give [67Ga]­Ga-NOTA-d-KV6 and [67Ga]­Ga-NOTA-KV6, respectively (Scheme ). Following HPLC purification of the crude products, the initial portion of the radioactive peak corresponding to the target product was isolated after separation from the unlabeled precursor detected by UV (Figure S1A,B). Product identity was verified by coinjection with the corresponding nonradioactive Ga-NOTA-d-KV6 and Ga-NOTA-KV6 standards (Figure S2). The products achieved radiochemical yields of 78% and 80%, with radiochemical purities exceeding 95%. The radiosynthesis was performed using carrier-free [67Ga]­GaCl3. Analytical HPLC confirmed the absence of the unlabeled precursor after HPLC purification (Figure S1C,D). Therefore, both [67Ga]­Ga-NOTA-d-KV6 and [67Ga]­Ga-NOTA-KV6 were regarded as carrier-free after purification, and the calculated molar activity was 61.7 GBq/μmol for each radiotracer. Experimental log P values of [67Ga]­Ga-NOTA-d-KV6 and [67Ga]­Ga-NOTA-KV6 were −2.42 ± 0.07 and −2.62 ± 0.20, respectively, indicating that the lipophilicities of these peptides were similar, as expected given that [67Ga]­Ga-NOTA-d-KV6 is the mirror-image peptide of [67Ga]­Ga-NOTA-KV6.

Stability of [67Ga]­Ga-NOTA-D-KV6 in PB and Mouse Plasma

First, the stability of [67Ga]­Ga-NOTA-d-KV6 was evaluated in 0.1 M PB at 37 °C (pH 7.4) for 60 min, showing a high stability with 95.3 ± 1.59% of the compound remaining intact. Enzymes often cleave or degrade peptides. Endogenous proteases (e.g., trypsin, chymotrypsin, and endopeptidases) recognize the three-dimensional conformation of l-amino acids, enabling them to bind and cleave peptide substrates. For example, trypsin and elastase preferentially hydrolyze peptides at lysine and valine residues, respectively, and KV6 is susceptible to enzymatic cleavage in the plasma. In contrast, d-amino acids have distinct conformations that poorly align with the active sites of most proteolytic enzymes, rendering them more resistant to enzymatic degradation. Thus, mirror peptide formation was expected to improve plasma stability. We evaluated the stability of [67Ga]­Ga-NOTA-d-KV6 and [67Ga]­Ga-NOTA-KV6 in mouse plasma at 37 °C for 60, 120, and 240 min to assess their suitability for in vivo applications. After 60 min of incubation, [67Ga]­Ga-NOTA-d-KV6 exhibited high metabolic stability comparable to [67Ga]­Ga-NOTA-KV6 (89.0 ± 1.15% vs 86.9 ± 0.34%). However, with prolonged incubation, a clear difference was observed: the stability of [67Ga]­Ga-NOTA-d-KV6 declined more gradually, retaining 57.1 ± 6.43% at 240 min, whereas that of [67Ga]­Ga-NOTA-KV6 markedly decreased to 23.7 ± 7.50%. These results indicate that although both peptides exhibit similar initial stability, [67Ga]­Ga-NOTA-d-KV6 is significantly more resistant to plasma-mediated degradation over time (Figure ). However, because NOTA-d-KV6 still contains l-amino acids, it may remain partially susceptible to enzymatic degradation. Additionally, the potential disruption of coordination between 67Ga and NOTA in [67Ga]­Ga-NOTA-d-KV6 could affect its stability. In Figure S3, multiple degradation products were detected in small amounts, making the analysis challenging. The peaks from the initial fraction may represent metabolized peptide fragments or free 67Ga dissociated from NOTA. Although the precise mechanism remains unclear, the amino acid sequence of KV6 may confer reduced susceptibility to peptidase-mediated degradation, further narrowing the difference in stability between the two peptides.

In Vitro Cellular Uptake of [67Ga]­Ga-NOTA-D-KV6 and [67Ga]­Ga-NOTA-KV6

Cellular uptake of [67Ga]­Ga-NOTA-d-KV6 and [67Ga]­Ga-NOTA-KV6 was evaluated in cancerous KB, noncancerous 3T3-L1, and IMR-90 cells, as shown in Figure . Many studies on peptide-based nuclear imaging agents use acidic glycine buffer to wash the cell surface. However, in this study and our previous report, we evaluated the cellular uptake of FITC-labeled peptides, which are susceptible to degradation under strongly acidic conditions. Therefore, to maintain identical experimental conditions for both FITC-labeled and radiolabeled peptides, ice-cold heparin/PBS was used as a milder and established method for removing cell surface-bound peptides. The cellular uptake of both [67Ga]­Ga-NOTA-d-KV6 and [67Ga]­Ga-NOTA-KV6 was highest in KB cells (110 ± 4.08% and 76.4 ± 10.6% AD/mg protein, respectively) and was significantly higher than that in the normal cell lines, 3T3-L1 and IMR-90. Furthermore, the uptake of [67Ga]­Ga-NOTA-d-KV6 by the KB cells was significantly higher than that of [67Ga]­Ga-NOTA-KV6. The zeta potential on the cell membrane surface of IMR-90 cells was −16.4 ± 2.29 mV, which was less negative than that of KB cells (−26.6 ± 2.19 mV) and more negative than that of 3T3-L1 (−11.2 ± 3.13 mV) in our previous study. A plot of the correlation between cellular uptake and zeta potential revealed a strong negative relationship, indicating that uptake increased as the zeta potential became more negative (Figure S4). These results suggested that [67Ga]­Ga-NOTA-KV6 derivatives are preferentially recognized and accumulate in cancer cells because of their membrane charge properties. The significantly higher uptake of [67Ga]­Ga-NOTA-d-KV6 than that of [67Ga]­Ga-NOTA-KV6 in KB cells (Figure ) may be partly attributable to the improved stability conferred by d-amino acid substitution (Figure ), which could increase the amount of intact peptide available for interaction with the cancer cell membrane during incubation. To evaluate the potential influence of residual nonradioactive precursor on cellular uptake, competitive inhibition studies were performed using increasing concentrations of the corresponding nonradioactive peptides (Figure S5). Cellular uptake of both [67Ga]­Ga-NOTA-d-KV6 and [67Ga]­Ga-NOTA-KV6 was significantly inhibited by the corresponding nonradioactive peptides in a concentration-dependent manner. Assuming a simplified radiosynthesis for future 68Ga applications without HPLC purification, the estimated concentration of residual nonradioactive precursor in the cellular uptake assay would be approximately 0.045 μM, which is lower than the lowest concentration (0.1 μM) examined in the present study. Therefore, although residual precursor may affect cellular uptake at sufficiently high concentrations, its influence under the expected 68Ga labeling conditions is likely to be limited.

Evaluation of the Internalization Pathway for [67Ga]­Ga-NOTA-D-KV6

Previous studies reported that the uptake of nanoparticles conjugated with KV6-based peptides or their derivatives by cancer cells occurs via endocytosis. To examine the internalization pathway of [67Ga]­Ga-NOTA-d-KV6, we evaluated the effects of endocytosis inhibitors on its cellular uptake. To suppress endocytosis, KB cells were incubated with each peptide at 4 °C. Additionally, we examined how inhibition of major endocytic pathways influences peptide uptake by KB cells. 5-(N-Ethyl-N-isopropyl) amiloride (EIPA), nystatin, and sucrose were applied to block macropinocytosis, caveolae-mediated, and clathrin-mediated endocytosis, respectively. The uptake of [67Ga]­Ga-NOTA-d-KV6 was significantly reduced to 19%, 31%, 65%, and 60% of the control following incubation at 4 °C and treatment with EIPA, nystatin, and sucrose, respectively (Figure ). Similarly, our previous study demonstrated that the uptake of [67Ga]­Ga-NOTA-KV6 was reduced to 35%, 58%, and 58% of control following incubation at 4 °C and treatment with EIPA and nystatin, respectively, whereas sucrose treatment caused only a slight, nonsignificant decrease. [67Ga]­Ga-NOTA-d-KV6 and [67Ga]­Ga-NOTA-KV6 were internalized via multiple endocytic pathways; however, their uptake mechanisms exhibited subtle differences. Together with the improved stability of [67Ga]­Ga-NOTA-d-KV6 (Figure ), these differences in endocytic pathway utilization may contribute to its higher cellular uptake in KB cells compared with [67Ga]­Ga-NOTA-KV6 (Figure ).

In Vivo Biodistribution of [67Ga]­Ga-NOTA-D-KV6

Finally, the in vivo distribution of [67Ga]­Ga-NOTA-d-KV6 was assessed at 30, 60, 120, and 240 min after injection in KB tumor-bearing mice and expressed as %ID/g (Figure ). The detailed accumulation data are summarized in Table S2. [67Ga]­Ga-NOTA-d-KV6 showed high accumulation in the liver and kidneys, with rapid clearance from the bloodstream (Figure A,B). At 240 min postinjection, [67Ga]­Ga-NOTA-d-KV6 demonstrated lower kidney accumulation than [67Ga]­Ga-NOTA-KV6 (46.4 ± 19.8% ID/g vs 71.3 ± 23.2% ID/g) but slightly higher liver accumulation (16.0 ± 3.58% ID/g vs 14.8 ± 3.08% ID/g) as shown in Figures A and S6A. Uptake in other nontarget organs remained low throughout the study and was comparable between the two radioligands. Tumor uptake of [67Ga]­Ga-NOTA-d-KV6 was moderate but sustained over time (Figure B), indicating improved retention compared with [67Ga]­Ga-NOTA-KV6 across time points (Figure S6 and our previous report). As a result, tumor-to-blood ratios increased progressively, reaching 17.3 ± 9.19 at 240 min postinjection (Figure C). At this time point, [67Ga]­Ga-NOTA-d-KV6 exhibited significantly higher tumor uptake as well as tumor-to-blood, tumor-to-liver, and tumor-to-kidney ratios than [67Ga]­Ga-NOTA-KV6 (Figure ). On the other hand, blood activity was lower for [67Ga]­Ga-NOTA-d-KV6, potentially reflecting differences in in vivo behavior between the two radioligands. The enhanced tumor retention of [67Ga]­Ga-NOTA-d-KV6 is likely multifactorial and may be associated with its improved plasma stability (Figure ) and enhanced cellular uptake (Figure ). In addition, d-amino acid substitution induced membrane-associated conformational changes (Figure ), and subtle differences in cellular internalization behavior were observed (Figure ), although their contribution to tumor retention remains unclear.

Rapid clearance of [67Ga]­Ga-NOTA-d-KV6 from the blood was observed, with levels decreasing from 0.5% ID/g at 30 min to 0.05% ID/g at 240 min. Although [67Ga]­Ga-NOTA-d-KV6 reached its maximum tumor uptake of 2.21 ± 0.87% ID/g at 30 min postinjection (Table S2), the peak tumor uptake remained relatively low. Moreover, tumor radioactivity gradually decreased over time, suggesting that improving tumor retention may further enhance overall tumor accumulation. Although [67Ga]­Ga-NOTA-d-KV6 showed significantly greater plasma stability than [67Ga]­Ga-NOTA-KV6 after 120 and 240 min of incubation (Figures and S3), this improved stability did not result in prolonged blood circulation or enhanced initial tumor uptake in vivo. At 30 min after injection, when tumor uptake reached its maximum, the blood radioactivity of both radiotracers had already decreased to similarly low levels (0.57 and 0.5412 % ID/g for [67Ga]­Ga-NOTA-d-KV6 and [67Ga]­Ga-NOTA-KV6, respectively), whereas the difference in plasma stability became significant only after 120 min of incubation. Furthermore, despite the improved plasma stability, the peak tumor uptake of [67Ga]­Ga-NOTA-d-KV6 remained lower than that previously reported for [67Ga]­Ga-NOTA-KV6 (2.21 vs 4.4612 % ID/g at 30 min). In contrast, [67Ga]­Ga-NOTA-d-KV6 showed greater tumor retention at 240 min (0.70 vs 0.32% ID/g, corresponding to approximately 32% and 7% of the respective peak tumor uptake). These findings suggest that the improved plasma stability of [67Ga]­Ga-NOTA-d-KV6 may have contributed to its prolonged tumor retention, but was not sufficient to prolong blood circulation or enhance the initial tumor uptake. Therefore, further structural optimization will be required not only to prolong blood circulation, for example through PEGylation, but also to improve tumor targeting and thereby enhance overall tumor accumulation.

Although further refinement is required to optimize tumor accumulation and clearance from normal tissues in tumor-bearing mice, these findings offer valuable insights into the advancement of cancer theranostic agents that use CAPs as their core structure. The tumor-to-liver and tumor-to-kidney ratios were markedly low (0.091 and 0.024, respectively) at 30 min, and these values decreased further over time, with even lower ratios observed at 60, 120, and 240 min. These findings clearly indicate the need for improvement in tumor selectivity to improve imaging performance. We recently developed a fusion peptide combining KV6 and the RGD peptide, which targets integrins highly expressed in cancer cells, and demonstrated enhanced tumor accumulation together with prolonged retention compared with the RGD derivative. Future research will focus on further reducing liver and kidney uptake by modifying the physicochemical properties of KV6 derivatives, such as hydrophobicity and charge distribution, as well as on additional structural optimization to maximize tumor-to-background contrast.

Conclusion

We developed [67Ga]­Ga-NOTA-d-KV6 in this study, aiming to enhance the tumor accumulation and metabolic stability of [67Ga]­Ga-NOTA-KV6. Confocal microscopy revealed that FITC-d-KV6 fluorescence was mainly observed near the cell membrane. [67Ga]­Ga-NOTA-d-KV6 showed higher cellular uptake in cancer cells than noncancerous cells, which may reflect preferential interaction with negatively charged cancer cell membranes. In addition, plasma metabolic stability of [67Ga]­Ga-NOTA-d-KV6 was significantly improved compared with that of [67Ga]­Ga-NOTA-KV6. In vivo biodistribution studies indicated that [67Ga]­Ga-NOTA-d-KV6 exhibited prolonged tumor retention, as well as high tumor-to-blood and tumor-to-muscle ratios. Collectively, d-KV6, derived by replacing l-amino acids in KV6 with d-isomers, maintained favorable tumor-to-blood ratios while retaining the characteristic membrane-responsive secondary structure. However, further optimization is required to reduce the high accumulation in the kidneys and liver and to improve the tumor-to-kidney and tumor-to-liver ratios. These findings suggest that mirror peptide formation represents a promising strategy for improving the performance of CAPs. These findings provide a basis for the further development of CAP derivatives for cancer theranostic applications, although additional optimization and in vivo imaging studies will be required before practical imaging applications can be considered.
