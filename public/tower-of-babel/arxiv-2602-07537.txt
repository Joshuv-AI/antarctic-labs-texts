Propagation of Chaos for Nonlinear Markov Chains
James Vuckovic ( james@jamesvuckovic.com )

Abstract
We study 1-Wasserstein propagation of chaos for “McKean-type” nonlinear Markov chains and their associated interacting particle systems. This paper is organized into two parts: the first part combines arguments from various areas of nonlinear Markov theory into a systematic treatment of quantitative, nonasymptotic empirical measure estimates and propagation of chaos, with Lipschitz regularity as the primary tool. We also study extensions to uniform-in-time propagation of chaos and improved convergence rates under stronger assumptions such as transportation inequalities, modified metrics, or geometric ergodicity. The second part of this work consists of two detailed applications of our results to specific systems of interest: an Euler-Maruyama scheme for the standard McKean-Vlasov diffusion, and particle filtering via Feynman-Kac distribution flows.

Introduction

In this work, we study a general class of discrete-time “McKean-type” nonlinear Markov chains, by which we mean stochastic processes
\{X_{n}\}_{n=0}^{\infty}
such that

\mathbf{P}(X_{n+1}\in\bullet|X_{n},\dots,X_{0})=K_{\eta_{n}}(X_{n},\bullet);~~~\eta_{n}:=\mathrm{Law}(X_{n})

for some family of Markov kernels
\{K_{\eta}~|~\eta\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbf{R}^{d})\}
. These processes generalize the notion of a Markov chain to the case where the evolution depends not just on
X_{n}
but also the
law
of
X_{n}
. We also consider the related system of particles
\overline{X}^{N}_{n}:=(X^{1}_{n},\dots,X^{N}_{n})
, initially i.i.d., such that

\mathbf{P}(X^{i}_{n+1}\in\bullet|\overline{X}^{N}_{n},\dots,\overline{X}^{N}_{0})=K_{m(\overline{X}^{N}_{n})}(X^{i}_{n},\bullet);~~~m(\overline{X}^{N}_{n}):=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\delta_{X^{i}_{n}}.

In this setup, each particle
X^{i}_{n}
has the same one-step transition as
X_{n}
except that the law of
X_{n}
is replaced with the empirical measure above. This models the popular family of interacting particle systems where the particles interact via their common empirical measure. Such systems arise in physics, such as in the modelling of granular media
[
9
]
or polymers
[
31
]
, models of complex systems such as mean field games
[
49
,
8
]
, and probabilistic algorithms such as Bayesian filtering
[
40
]
or advanced Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) techniques
[
2
,

,
14
]
.

Intuitively, if
N
is large and
K_{\bullet}
is reasonably well-behaved, we should hope for a law of large numbers, i.e. that
m(\overline{X}^{N}_{n})\approx\mathrm{Law}(X_{n})
. To formulate this precisely, one possibility is to study subsets of (dependent) particles
X^{N,q}_{n}:=(X^{1}_{n},\dots,X^{q}_{n})
and measure the closeness of
\eta^{N,q}_{n}:=\mathrm{Law}(X^{N,q}_{n})
to the law of
q
i.i.d. particles according to
\eta_{n}
, denoted
\eta_{n}^{\otimes q}
. In other words, one can attempt to study the convergence, if any, for
\eta^{N,q}_{n}
to
\eta_{n}^{\otimes q}
as a function of
N,q
for some appropriate notion of convergence on
\mathcal{P}(\mathbf{R}^{d})
. If
\eta^{N,q}_{n}\to\eta^{\otimes q}_{n}
, this is called
propagation of chaos

[
44
]
and any convergence as strong or stronger than weak convergence implies, among other things, the convergence of
m(\overline{X}^{N}_{n})\to\mathrm{Law}(X_{n})
in law
[
60
, Proposition 2.2]
.

While an intuitive property, obtaining rigorous propagation of chaos estimates can be challenging. In particular, the particles
\overline{X}^{N}_{n}
are
a priori
statistically dependent, so the usual techniques for laws of large numbers do not automatically apply, and furthermore the dependence can evolve over time. We aim to address these difficulties by providing a systematic treatment of propagation of chaos for the above class of nonlinear Markov chains through the use of Lipschitz regularity of the associated nonlinear Markov kernels.

Main Result

In the first half of this work, we show that if
\mu\mapsto\int\mu(\mathrm{d}x)K_{\mu}(x,\mathrm{d}y)
is an
L
-Lipschitz
mapping of
\mathcal{P}_{1}(\mathbf{R}^{d})\to\mathcal{P}_{1}(\mathbf{R}^{d})
and if the
p
-th moments
\mathbf{E}[\|X^{1}_{k}\|^{p}]\leq M_{k}^{(p)}
are bounded for some
p>1
and
k=0,1,\dots,n
then

\mathbf{W}_{1}(\eta^{N,q}_{n},\eta^{\otimes q}_{n})\lesssim\frac{q}{N^{1/d}}\sum_{k=0}^{n}\left(M_{k}^{(p)}\right)^{1/p}L^{n-k}+\frac{q^{3}}{N}.

We also show that the second term can be dropped under a slightly stronger type of Lipschitzness. The full statement is in Theorem
5
, where we show how these two ingredients — regularity and moment control — can be used to derive quantitative propagation of chaos rates, including uniform-in-time estimates under appropriate conditions, thereby sidestepping the issues described above for a broad class of systems.

Applications

In the second half of this work, we show how the general result described above may be applied to specific, yet representative, nonlinear Markov chains of interest. The first is an Euler-Maruyama discretization of the McKean-Vlasov SDE, see e.g.,
[
6
]
, for which we obtain a propagation of chaos result in the uniformly convex setting using mainly elementary arguments. The second application is propagation of chaos for nonlinear filtering in the context of Feynman-Kac distribution flows
[
23

where we show that, in particular, the update kernel for the popular SIS-R nonlinear filter
[
32

is locally Lipschitz and derive propagation of chaos therefrom. The background for both of these applications is in Section

Discrete Time

Whereas theoretical considerations led to enormous progress in the continuous-time theory of nonlinear Markov processes, applications and the analysis of certain classes of algorithm have been key sources of inspiration for the development of the discrete-time theory. Among different types of algorithms, nonlinear (or Bayesian) filtering theory has been, by far, the most fecund problem for the advancement of the theory of nonlinear Markov chains. Interacting particle approximations of nonlinear filtering algorithms, typically called “particle filters” or “Monte Carlo filters”

2.1
Preliminaries

Notation

We deal with the Borel measurable space
(\mathbf{R}^{d},\mathcal{B}(\mathbf{R}^{d}))
, and the set of probability measures
\mathcal{P}(\mathbf{R}^{d})
on this space. For
\mu\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbf{R}^{d})
, we denote the
p
-th moment of
\mu
is

\mathbf{M}_{p}(\mu):=\int\|x\|^{p}\mu(\mathrm{d}x)

and we will use the notation
\mathbf{V}_{p}(x):=\|x\|^{p}
so in particular
\mathbf{M}_{p}(\mu)=\int\mathbf{V}_{p}\mathrm{d}\mu=\mu(\mathbf{V}_{p})
. We will also write
\mathcal{P}_{p}(\mathbf{R}^{d}):=\{\mu\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbf{R}^{d})~|~\mathbf{M}_{p}(\mu)<\infty\}
. The
p
-Wasserstein distance for
p\geq 1
between
\mu,\nu\in\mathcal{P}_{p}(\mathbf{R}^{d})
is defined as

\mathbf{W}_{p}(\mu,\nu):=\inf_{\gamma\in\mathcal{C}(\mu,\nu)}\left(\int\|x-y\|^{p}\gamma(\mathrm{d}x,\mathrm{d}y)\right)^{1/p}

with
\mathcal{C}(\mu,\nu)
the set of couplings of
\mu,\nu
and
\|\bullet\|
the standard Euclidean metric.
On the
q
-fold tensor product space
(\mathbf{R}^{d})^{\otimes q}
, we use the
\ell_{2}
norm, i.e., which is the natural extension of the Euclidean norm

\|(x^{1},\dots,x^{q})-(y^{1},\dots,y^{q})\|:=\left(\sum_{i=1}^{q}\|x^{i}-y^{i}\|^{2}\right)^{1/2}.

Let
K:\mathbf{R}^{d}\times\mathcal{B}(\mathbf{R}^{d})\to[0,1]
be a Markov kernel and
f:\mathbf{R}^{d}\to\mathbf{R}
be measurable; then we write
Kf(x):=\int K(x,\mathrm{d}y)f(y)
. Let
\mu\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbf{R}^{d})
; then we write
\mu K(\mathrm{d}y):=\int\mu(\mathrm{d}x)K(x,\mathrm{d}y)
. Finally let
K,M
be Markov kernels; then
KM(x,\mathrm{d}y):=\int K(x,\mathrm{d}z)M(z,\mathrm{d}y)
. For a collection of points
x^{1},\dots,x^{n}\in\mathbf{R}^{d}
, we denote by
\overline{x}:=(x^{1},\dots,x^{n})
and
m(\overline{x})(\mathrm{d}x):=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\delta_{x^{i}}(\mathrm{d}x)\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbf{R}^{d})
.

Basic Constructions

We will consider a family of Markov kernels indexed by probability measures, not necessarily time-homogeneous, denoted by
K^{(n)}_{\bullet}:=\{K^{(n)}_{\eta}~|~\eta\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbf{R}^{d})\}
where each
K^{(n)}_{\eta}
is a Markov kernel and
n\in\{1,2,3,\dots\}
. When this family is time-homogeneous, we will drop the superscript notation and just write
K_{\bullet}
. The
p
-Wasserstein contraction coefficient
\tau_{p}(K^{(n)}_{\bullet})
is defined as

\tau_{p}(K^{(n)}_{\bullet}):=\sup_{\mu,\nu\in\mathcal{P}_{p}(\mathbf{R}^{d}),~\mu\neq\nu}\frac{\mathbf{W}_{p}(\mu K^{(n)}_{\mu},\nu K^{(n)}_{\nu})}{\mathbf{W}_{p}(\mu,\nu)}

i.e., it is the best constant such that

\mathbf{W}_{p}(\mu K^{(n)}_{\mu},\nu K^{(n)}_{\nu})\leq\tau_{p}(K^{(n)}_{\bullet})\mathbf{W}_{p}(\mu,\nu).

These “nonlinear” Markov kernels define nonlinear mappings
\Phi_{n}:\mathcal{P}(\mathbf{R}^{d})\to\mathcal{P}(\mathbf{R}^{d})
by
\mu\mapsto\Phi_{n}[\mu]:=\mu K^{(n)}_{\mu}
. These nonlinear mappings form a nonlinear semigroup
\Phi_{m,n}
defined for
n\geq m
as

\Phi_{m,n}:=\left\{\begin{array}[]{l l}\Phi_{n}\circ\cdots\circ\Phi_{m+1}&n>m\\
\mathrm{Identity}&n=m.\end{array}\right.

Much of the semigroup-related notation in this work is derived from
[
23
]
. The kernels
K^{(n)}_{\bullet}
can be used to define a time-inhomogeneous, nonlinear Markov chain: given an initial measure
\eta_{0}\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbf{R}^{d})
and
X_{0}\sim\eta_{0}
, we can define

X_{n+1}\sim K^{(n+1)}_{\eta_{n}}(X_{n},\bullet);~~~\eta_{n}:=\mathrm{Law}(X_{n}).

(4)

It is clear that that
\eta_{n}
satisfies
\eta_{n+1}=\eta_{n}K^{(n+1)}_{\eta_{n}}=\Phi_{n+1}[\eta_{n}]
, and also that
\eta_{n}=\Phi_{0,n}[\eta_{0}]
. We will refer to
X_{n}
or
\eta_{n}
as the “mean-field” system. We will also use
\tau_{1}(\Phi_{n})
interchangeably with
\tau_{1}(K^{(n)}_{\bullet})
.

Given an initial measure
\eta_{0}\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbf{R}^{d})
and
X^{1}_{0},\dots,X^{N}_{0}\overset{iid}{\sim}\eta_{0}
with
N\geq 1
, we can define the interacting particle system
\overline{X}^{N}_{n}:=(X^{1}_{n},\dots,X^{N}_{n})
associated with the mean field dynamics as

X^{i}_{n+1}\sim K^{(n+1)}_{\eta^{N}_{n}}(X^{i}_{n},\bullet);~~~\eta^{N}_{n}:=m(\overline{X}^{N}_{n}).

(5)

The following important property is an obvious consequence of the symmetry and transition mechanisms for the system above, and will be used throughout this work.

Fact
.

When
X^{1}_{0},\dots,X^{N}_{0}
are exchangeable, the system
(X^{1}_{n},\dots,X^{N}_{n})
is exchangeable, i.e., its joint law is invariant under permutation. However, unlike the mean-field case, it is
not
true that
\eta^{N}_{n+1}=\Phi_{n+1}[\eta^{N}_{n}]
, but rather
(X^{1}_{n+1},\dots,X^{N}_{n+1})
are
conditionally i.i.d.
given
\mathcal{F}^{N}_{n}:=\sigma(X^{1}_{n},\dots,X^{N}_{n})
with common law
\Phi_{n+1}[\eta^{N}_{n}]
.

We are interested in the joint law of
q
-subsets of
(X^{1}_{n},\dots,X^{N}_{n})
; by excheageability it is sufficient to define
\eta^{N,q}_{n}:=\mathrm{Law}(X^{1}_{n},\dots,X^{q}_{n})
for
1\leq q\leq N
. In particular, we are interested in the extent to which
\eta^{N,q}_{n}\approx\eta_{n}^{\otimes q}
.

2.2
Main Result

The results below establish quantitative propagation using three basic ingredients: Lipschitzness of the map

\mathbf{E}[\|X^{1}_{n}\|^{p}]

and

Assumption 2.2

(Lipschitz Regularity)

Let

A1.

There are constants

\mathbf{W}_{1}(\mu K^{(n)}_{\mu},\nu K^{(n)}_{\nu})\leq\tau_{1}(K^{(n)}_{\bullet})[\nu]\cdot\mathbf{W}_{1}(\mu,\nu)

\mathbf{E}[\|X^{1}_{n}\|^{p}]=\mathbf{E}[\mathbf{M}_{p}(\eta^{N}_{n})]=\mathbf{E}[\mathbf{M}_{p}(\Phi_{n}[\eta^{N}_{n-1}])]

[0,\infty]

Proof.

On one hand by exchangeability

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}[\mathbf{M}_{p}(\eta^{N}_{n})]=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathbf{E}[\|X^{i}_{n}\|^{p}]=\mathbf{E}[\|X^{1}_{n}\|^{p}].

On the other, using
\mathcal{F}^{N}_{n}:=\sigma(X^{1}_{n},\dots,X^{N}_{n})
and
X^{i}_{n}\sim K^{(n)}_{\eta^{N}_{n-1}}(X^{i}_{n-1},\bullet)
, we get

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}[\|X^{1}_{n}\|^{p}]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbf{E}[\|X^{i}_{n}\|^{p}]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbf{E}[\mathbf{E}[\|X^{i}_{n}\|^{p}|\mathcal{F}^{N}_{n-1}]]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathbf{E}[K^{(n)}_{\eta^{N}_{n-1}}\mathbf{V}_{p}(X^{i}_{n-1})]

\displaystyle=\mathbf{E}\left[\eta^{N}_{n-1}K^{(n)}_{\eta^{N}_{n-1}}(\mathbf{V}_{p})\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbf{E}[\Phi_{n}[\eta^{N}_{n-1}](\mathbf{V}_{p})].

∎

Proposition 2

Suppose that Assumption

\displaystyle\eta^{N}_{n}(f)-\eta_{n}(f)=

These ingredients are by now fairly standard for propagation of chaos; various forms of regularity and moment control appear in many papers studying propagation of chaos in discrete and continuous time. Proposition 3

Suppose that Assumption

2.3
Sharpness and Rates

Let us first note that Proposition
2
is sharp at the current level of generality, i.e., it is not hard to construct nontrivial, contractive nonlinear Markov kernels that attain the
\mathbf{E}[\mathbf{W}_{1}(\eta^{N}_{n},\eta_{n})]\gtrsim N^{-1/d}
bound using arguments from
[
29
]
. Indeed, the following simple nonlinear Markov kernel is instructive.

Proposition 8
.

Let
\mathcal{U}_{d}
be the uniform distribution on
[-1,1]^{d}
and define

K_{\eta}(x,\mathrm{d}y)=\frac{1}{3}\delta_{x}(\mathrm{d}y)+\frac{1}{3}\eta(\mathrm{d}y)+\frac{1}{3}\mathcal{U}_{d}.

Then
K_{\bullet}
is a strict contraction, and there is
\eta_{0}
such that
\mathbf{E}[\|X^{1}_{n}\|^{p}]\leq M^{(p)}_{\star}<\infty~\forall p>1,~n\geq 0
and

\mathbf{E}[\mathbf{W}_{1}(\eta^{N}_{n},\eta_{n})]\gtrsim N^{-1/d}

uniformly in
n
.

Proof.

For the same of simplicity, take
\eta_{0}
to be bounded and supported on the unit cube; in particular, it has finite moments of all orders. The same is true of
X^{1}_{n}
under these conditions. Moreover,
K_{\bullet}
is a strict contraction such that

\mathbf{W}_{1}(K_{\mu}(x,\bullet),K_{\nu}(y,\bullet))\leq\frac{1}{3}\|x-y\|+\frac{1}{3}\mathbf{W}_{1}(\mu,\nu).

Note that
\eta_{n}
has a strictly positive density on the unit cube for
n\geq 1
for any initial measure. Indeed,

\eta_{n}=\left(\frac{2}{3}\right)^{n}\eta_{0}+\left(1-\left(\frac{2}{3}\right)^{n}\right)\mathcal{U}_{d}

so that the density of
\eta_{n}
, also denoted
\eta_{n}
, satisfies
\eta_{n}(x)\geq(1-(2/3)^{n})2^{-d}\geq(1-2/3)2^{-d}=3^{-1}2^{-d}
. Thus, classical results form quantization theory, e.g.,
[
35
, Theorem 6.2]
, imply that

\mathbf{W}_{1}(\eta^{N}_{n},\eta_{n})\gtrsim N^{-1/d}~~~\eta_{n}-a.s

regardless of the dependence structure of
\eta^{N}_{n}
, or even if
\eta^{N}_{n}
is not supported on
\mathcal{U}_{d}
. Furthermore, since the cited quantization result only depends on the volume of the unit cube, the lower bound on the density, and the dimension, the above result holds
uniformly
in
n
.
∎

This example reveals an important limitation of our technique: by leveraging empirical measure theory to establish propagation of chaos in the ways above, we cannot hope to overcome the curse of dimensionality, i.e., the
1/d
in the exponent of
N
, at least in general. Indeed, the overall argument above holds for any measure having a density w.r.t. the Lebesgue measure on
\mathbf{R}^{d}
and satisfying mild moment conditions (although perhaps not uniformly), and this result is a geometric fact of approximating continuous measures by discrete ones, not a probabilistic result. Furthermore,
\mathbf{W}_{1}(\eta^{N,q}_{n},\eta_{n}^{\otimes q})
may be
much
better that
\mathcal{O}(N^{-1/d})
. In fact, in the example above, it is easy to see that
\mathcal{U}_{d}
is invariant for
K_{\bullet}
, and if
\eta_{0}=\mathcal{U}_{d}
in Proposition
8
, then
\eta^{N,1}_{n}=\mathcal{U}_{d}~\forall n\geq 0
. This implies
\mathbf{W}_{1}(\eta^{N,1}_{n},\eta_{n})=\mathbf{W}_{1}(\eta^{N,1}_{n},\mathcal{U}_{d})=0
, even though the same is not necessarily true for
q>1
due to correlations between particles.

On the other hand, the results of Section
2.2
apply in very broad settings. Lipschitzness is a mild assumption that is likely satisfied by many systems of interest as we will show in later sections. Therefore we see that propagation of chaos is a relatively common phenomenon for McKean-type nonlinear Markov chains, and Lipschitzness is a convenient setting for the study of many applications derived from this theory. A slower rate of
\mathcal{O}(N^{-1/d})
may well be an acceptable trade-off, at least as a starting point for further analysis.

Nevertheless, it is interesting to ask how one can improve these bounds and, based on the remarks above, the most obvious solution is to bypass the use of the empirical measure entirely. Transportation inequalities and their favourable tensorization properties provide one such option, and we discuss this in some detail below.

Transportation Inequalities

First, let us investigate the case where
\eta_{n}
satisfies a
T_{2}(\lambda_{n})
transportation inequality for each
n\geq 0
, i.e. that
\mathbf{W}_{2}(\nu,\eta_{n})\leq\sqrt{2\lambda_{n}\mathbf{KL}(\nu\|\eta_{n})}~\forall n\geq 0
and every
\nu\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbf{R}^{d})
with
\nu\ll\eta_{n}
. This can be used to obtain dimension-free quantitative propagation of chaos as follows: proceeding formally and neglecting issues of absolute continuity, if
\eta_{n}
satisfies a
T_{2}(\lambda_{n})
inequality then its
q

T_{1}(\lambda_{n})
inequality on the product space
(\mathbf{R}^{d})^{\otimes q}
, i.e.
\mathbf{W}_{1}(\nu,\eta_{n}^{\otimes q})\leq\sqrt{2\lambda_{n}\mathbf{KL}(\nu\|\eta_{n}^{\otimes q})}

[
34
]
. But then we can apply an inequality of Csizar for exchangeable measures
[

to obtain

\mathbf{W}_{1}(\eta^{N,q}_{n},\eta_{n}^{\otimes q})\leq\sqrt{2\lambda_{n}\mathbf{KL}(\eta^{N,q}_{n}\|\eta_{n}^{\otimes q})}\lesssim\frac{\sqrt{q}}{\sqrt{N}}\sqrt{\lambda_{n}\mathbf{KL}(\eta^{N,N}_{n}\|\eta_{n}^{\otimes N})}.

This is a substantial improvement over Theorem
5
because it does not explicitly depend on
d
(although
\lambda_{n}\mathbf{KL}(\eta^{N,N}_{n},\|\eta_{n}^{\otimes N})

\eta_{n}

On the other hand,

\mathbf{W}_{1}(\eta_{n}^{N,q},\eta^{\otimes q}_{n})\leq\sqrt{2\lambda_{n}q\mathbf{KL}(\eta^{N,q}_{n},\eta^{\otimes q}_{n})}\lesssim\frac{q}{\sqrt{N}}\sqrt{\lambda_{n}\mathbf{KL}(\eta^{N,N}_{n}\|\eta_{n}^{\otimes N})}.

This is still a net improvement over our current results as the scaling in
N
is no longer dependent on
d
, and the worst-case scaling in
q
is
\mathcal{O}(q)
versus
\mathcal{O}(q^{3})
previously.

In both cases, the issue of
\mathbf{KL}(\eta^{N,N}_{n}\|\eta^{\otimes N}_{n})
remains. However this may not to be too much of an issue for many interesting applications. Showing

\frac{\mathrm{d}\eta^{N,N}_{n}}{\mathrm{d}{\eta^{\otimes N}_{n}}}(x)\propto\exp H_{N}(x)

with
N\mapsto H_{N}(x)
is
\mathcal{O}(1)
will suffice, where
H_{N}
is an “interaction Hamiltonian”. For example, considering the system in Section
5
, in
[
23
, Theorem 8.2.3]
it is shown that
\eta^{N,N}_{n}\ll\eta_{n}^{\otimes N}
and furthermore they derive
\mathcal{O}(1)
bounds w.r.t
N
in-terms of regularity of
K_{\bullet}
, which become uniform-in-time as soon as
K_{\bullet}
satisfies sufficient regularity conditions
[
23
, Corollary 8.5.1]
. This essentially shows that our
\mathcal{O}(q/N^{1/d}\vee q^{3}/N))
results in Section
5
can be be strengthened to
\mathcal{O}(q/\sqrt{N})
, at the cost of using deep knowledge of the system of interest versus general tools such as Lipschitz regularity.

2.4
Moments

We finish this section with a discussion on establishing moment bounds of the form
\mathbf{E}[\|X^{1}_{n}\|^{p}]\leq M^{(p)}_{n}
as required by Theorem
5
. Many popular examples of nonlinear Markov kernels
K_{\eta}
have a structure which consists of a “linear” part and a nonlocal “interaction part” which depends ultimately on a integral w.r.t.
\eta
. Notably,
both
applications from Sections
4
&
5
, which have very different origins, satisfy this structure. Below, we show how popular “drift criterion” or “Lyapunov function” techniques from linear Markov theory can be used to establish moment control when
K_{\eta}
satisfies this form.

Assumption 2.3

(Lyapunov Moment Condition)
.

Let
V:\mathbf{R}^{d}\to[0,\infty)
be a measurable function. Assume that there are
a_{n},b_{n},c\in[0,\infty)
with
a_{n}+b_{n},c>0
, such that

K^{(n)}_{\eta}V(x)\leq a_{n}V(x)+b_{n}\eta(V)+c.

Proposition 9
.

Assume Assumption
2.3
holds and that
\eta_{0}(V)<\infty
. Then for
n\geq 0
,
\eta_{n}
from (
4
) satisfies

\eta_{n}(V)\leq A(n)\eta_{0}(V)+c\sum_{k=1}^{n}\prod_{j=k+1}^{n}(a_{j}+b_{j})

where

A(k):=\prod_{i=1}^{k}(a_{i}+b_{i}),~~~k=1,2,\dots.

Proof.

For
n=1
, we have

\eta_{1}(V)\leq(a_{1}+b_{1})\eta_{0}(V)+c=(a_{1}+b_{1})\eta_{0}+c\sum_{k=1}^{1}\prod_{j=2}^{1}(a_{j}+b_{j}).

Hence assuming true for
n
, for
n+1
we have

\displaystyle\eta_{n+1}(V)

\displaystyle=\eta_{n}(K^{(n)}_{\eta_{n}}V)

\displaystyle\leq(a_{n+1}+b_{n+1})\eta_{n}(V)+c

\displaystyle=(a_{n+1}+b_{n+1})\left[A(n)\eta_{0}(V)+c\sum_{k=1}^{n}\prod_{j=k+1}^{n}(a_{j}+b_{j})\right]+c

\displaystyle=A(n+1)\eta_{0}(V)+c\sum_{k=1}^{n}\prod_{j=k+1}^{n+1}(a_{j}+b_{j})+c

\displaystyle=A(n+1)\eta_{0}(V)+c\sum_{k=1}^{n+1}\prod_{j=k+1}^{n+1}(a_{j}+b_{j})

since the
k=n+1
-th term is 1:

\prod_{j=(n+1)+1}^{n+1}(a_{j}+b_{j}):=1.

∎

Now we extend this approach to the moments of the particle system (
5
), which is actually what is required for Theorem
5
. We leverage the useful fact that, while in general
\eta^{N}_{n}\neq\Phi_{n}[\eta^{N}_{n-1}]
, it is true in the
weak sense
, i.e.,
\mathbf{E}[\eta^{N}_{n}(f)]=\mathbf{E}[\Phi_{n}[\eta^{N}_{n-1}](f)]
for the same reason as Lemma
1
. Note that this is only a one-step equality, i.e., we do
not
have
E[\eta^{N}_{n}(f)]=\mathbf{E}[\Phi_{n}[\Phi_{n-1}[\eta^{N}_{n-2}]](f)]
. However, we can use the fact that the inequality in Assumption
2.3
is
recursive
to say something about the particle dynamics, in expectation. Therefore, a one-step equality
\mathbf{E}[\eta^{N}_{n}(f)]=\mathbf{E}[\Phi_{n}[\eta^{N}_{n-1}](f)]
plus a recursive inequality on
K^{(n)}_{\eta}
allows us to treat the moments of the particle system like those of the (simpler) mean-field system in Proposition
9
.

Proposition 10
.

Suppose that Assumption
2.3
holds, and that
\mathbf{E}[\eta^{N}_{0}(V)]<\infty
. Then

\mathbf{E}[\Phi_{n+1}[\eta^{N}_{n}](V)]\leq A(n+1)\mathbf{E}[\eta^{N}_{0}(V)]+c\sum_{k=1}^{n+1}\prod_{j=k+1}^{n+1}(a_{j}+b_{j}).

Proof.

Firstly, for
\mathcal{F}^{N}_{n}:=\sigma(X^{1}_{n},\dots,X^{N}_{n})
, we have almost surely

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}[\Phi_{n+1}[\eta^{N}_{n}](V)|\mathcal{F}^{N}_{n}]

\displaystyle=\eta^{N}_{n}\left(K^{(n+1)}_{\eta^{N}_{n}}V\right)

\displaystyle\leq a_{n+1}\eta^{N}_{n}(V)+b_{n+1}\eta^{N}_{n}(V)+c

\displaystyle=(a_{n+1}+b_{n+1})\eta^{N}_{n}(V)+c

so that by the Law of Total Expectation,

\mathbf{E}[\Phi_{n+1}[\eta^{N}_{n}](V)]\leq(a_{n+1}+b_{n+1})\mathbf{E}[\eta^{N}_{n}(V)]+c.

Using exchangeability, the Law of Total Expectation again, and the fact that
X^{i}_{n}\sim K^{(n)}_{\eta^{N}_{n-1}}(X^{i}_{n-1},\bullet)
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}[\eta^{N}_{n}(V)]

\displaystyle=\mathbf{E}\left[\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}V(X^{i}_{n})\right]=\mathbf{E}[V(X^{1}_{n})]=\mathbf{E}[K^{(n)}_{\eta^{N}_{n-1}}V(X^{1}_{n-1})]

\displaystyle\leq a_{n}\mathbf{E}[V(X^{1}_{n-1})]+b_{n}\mathbf{E}[\eta^{N}_{n-1}(V)]+c

\displaystyle=(a_{n}+b_{n})\mathbf{E}[\eta^{N}_{n-1}(V)]+c.

Hence, proceeding as in Proposition
9
, we get

\mathbf{E}[\eta^{N}_{n}(V)]\leq A(n)\mathbf{E}[\eta^{N}_{0}(V)]+c\sum_{k=1}^{n}\prod_{j=k+1}^{n}(a_{j}+b_{j})

so that

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}[\Phi_{n+1}[\eta^{N}_{n}](V)]

\displaystyle\leq(a_{n+1}+b_{n+1})\mathbf{E}[\eta^{N}_{n}(V)]+c

\displaystyle\leq A(n+1)\mathbf{E}[\eta^{N}_{0}(V)]+c\sum_{k=1}^{n+1}\prod_{j=k+1}^{n+1}(a_{j}+b_{j}).

∎

The following is an immediate corollary of the above results.

Corollary 11
.

Suppose that Assumption
2.3
is satisfied, and that
\mathbf{E}[\eta^{N}_{0}(V)]<\infty
. Let
p\geq 1
; if there exists constants
\theta>0
and
R\geq 0
such that

\|x\|^{p}\leq\theta V(x)~\forall\|x\|>R

then there are constants
M_{n}^{(p)}\in(0,\infty)
such that

\mathbf{E}[\|X^{1}_{n}\|^{p}]\leq M^{(p)}_{n}~~~\forall n=0,1,2,\dots

Furthermore, if
a_{n}+b_{n}<1~\forall n\geq 1
, then the above bound can be replaced by a uniform constant
M^{(p)}_{\star}
.

Remark 2.3
.

Note that the same corollary applies instead with
V(x)=\exp(\alpha\|x\|^{2});~\alpha>0
. In this case, we obtain control over square-exponential moments, which in turn implies finiteness of
all
moments, and also a
T_{1}
transportation inequality
[
5
]
, as discussed in Section
2.3
.

3
Uniform Propagation of Chaos

Below is an immediate consequences of Theorem
5
when
K^{(n)}_{\bullet}
is a contraction for every
n\geq 1
.

Corollary 12

(Uniform Propagation of Chaos)
.

Suppose
\tau_{1}(K^{(n)}_{\bullet})\leq\tau_{1}^{\star}<1~\forall n\geq 1
and
M^{(p)}_{k}\leq M^{(p)}_{\star}<\infty~\forall k\geq 0
. Then

\mathbf{W}_{p}(\eta^{N,q}_{n},\eta^{\otimes q}_{n})\leq C_{p,d}\cdot\frac{\left(M^{(p)}_{\star}\right)^{1/p}}{1-\tau_{1}^{\star}}\frac{q}{N^{1/d}}.

3.1
Modified Wasserstein Distance

Definition 13

(Modified 1-Wasserstein Distance)
.

Suppose there is a concave, increasing function
\rho:[0,\infty)\to[0,\infty)
satisfying
\rho(0)=0
and

\underline{c}r\leq\rho(r)\leq\overline{C}r

for constants
\underline{c},\overline{C}\in(0,\infty)
. Then

d_{\rho}(x,y):=\rho(\|x-y\|)

defines a metric on
\mathbf{R}^{d}
and we may define the associated 1-Wasserstein distance on
\mathcal{P}_{\rho}(\mathbf{R}^{d})

\mathbf{W}_{\rho}(\mu,\nu):=\inf_{\gamma\in\mathcal{C}(\mu,\nu)}\int d_{\rho}(x,y)\gamma(\mathrm{d}x,\mathrm{d}y)=\inf_{\gamma\in\mathcal{C}(\mu,\nu)}\int\rho(\|x-y\|)\gamma(\mathrm{d}x,\mathrm{d}y)

where we have defined

\mathbf{M}_{\rho}(\mu):=\int\rho(\|x\|)\mu(\mathrm{d}x)

and

\mathcal{P}_{\rho}(\mathbf{R}^{d}):=\{\mu\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbf{R}^{d})~|~\mathbf{M}_{\rho}(\mu)<\infty\}.

It is easy to see that
\mathbf{W}_{\rho}
and
\mathbf{W}_{1}
are equivalent and that
\mathbf{M}_{1}(\mu)<\infty
iff
\mathbf{M}_{\rho}(\mu)<\infty
. However
\mathbf{W}_{\rho}
offers more flexibility to tailor the metric
d_{\rho}(x,y)
to the kernel of interest and therefore obtain a contraction. This would lead straightforwardly to a uniform propagation of chaos in the case of a time-homogeneous kernel or a uniform-in-
n
bound on the contraction coefficients. This approach is now a standard technique in the study of Markov chains
[
39
]
and nonlinear diffusions
[
27
,
26
]
.

Corollary 14
.

Suppose that the assumptions of Theorem
5
are satisfied, and that
K^{(n)}_{\bullet}
is a contraction in
\mathbf{W}_{\rho}
, i.e.

\mathbf{W}_{\rho}(\mu K^{(n)}_{\mu},\nu K^{(n)}_{\nu})\leq\tau_{\rho}(K^{(n)}_{\bullet})\mathbf{W}_{\rho}(\mu,\nu)

where
\tau_{\rho}(K^{(n)}_{\bullet})\leq\tau_{\rho}^{\star}<1
and
M^{(p)}_{n}\leq M^{(p)}_{\star}<\infty
for all
n\geq 1
. Then

\mathbf{E}[\mathbf{W}_{1}(\eta^{N}_{n},\eta_{n})]\leq C_{p,d}\frac{\overline{C}}{\underline{c}}\cdot\frac{1}{N^{1/d}}\frac{(M^{(p)}_{\star})^{1/p}}{1-\tau_{\rho}^{\star}}.

Proof.

Firstly, we note that
\mathcal{P}_{1}(\mathbf{R}^{d})=\mathcal{P}_{\rho}(\mathbf{R}^{d})
so that

\displaystyle\tau_{1}(K^{(n)}_{\bullet})

\displaystyle=\sup_{\mu,\nu\in\mathcal{P}_{1}(\mathbf{R}^{d}),~\mu\neq\nu}\frac{\mathbf{W}_{1}(\mu K^{(n)}_{\mu},\nu K^{(n)}_{\nu})}{\mathbf{W}_{1}(\mu,\nu)}

\displaystyle=\sup_{\mu,\nu\in\mathcal{P}_{\rho}(\mathbf{R}^{d}),~\mu\neq\nu}\frac{\mathbf{W}_{1}(\mu K^{(n)}_{\mu},\nu K^{(n)}_{\nu})}{\mathbf{W}_{1}(\mu,\nu)}

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{\mu,\nu\in\mathcal{P}_{\rho}(\mathbf{R}^{d}),~\mu\neq\nu}\frac{\overline{C}}{\underline{c}}\cdot\frac{\mathbf{W}_{\rho}(\mu K^{(n)}_{\mu},\nu K^{(n)}_{\nu})}{\mathbf{W}_{\rho}(\mu,\nu)}

\displaystyle=\frac{\overline{C}}{\underline{c}}\tau_{\rho}(K^{(n)}_{\bullet}).

Hence, we can use this to improve the estimate from Theorem
5
:

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}[\mathbf{W}_{1}(\eta^{N}_{n},\eta_{n})]

\displaystyle\leq C_{r,d}\cdot\frac{1}{N^{1/d}}\sum_{k=0}^{n}\left(M^{(p)}_{k}\right)^{1/p}\tau_{1}(\Phi_{n,k})

\displaystyle\leq C_{p,d}\frac{\overline{C}}{\underline{c}}\cdot\frac{1}{N^{1/d}}\sum_{k=0}^{n}\left(M^{(p)}_{k}\right)^{1/p}\tau_{\rho}(\Phi_{n,k})

\displaystyle\leq C_{p,d}\frac{\overline{C}}{\underline{c}}\cdot\frac{1}{N^{1/d}}\left(M^{(p)}_{\star}\right)^{1/p}\sum_{k=0}^{n}(\tau_{\rho}^{\star})^{n-k}

\displaystyle\leq C_{p,d}\frac{\overline{C}}{\underline{c}}\cdot\frac{1}{N^{1/d}}\frac{\left(M^{(p)}_{\star}\right)^{1/p}}{1-\tau_{\rho}^{\star}}.

The other results from Section
2
apply
mutatis mutandis
.
∎

3.2
Geometric Ergodicity

Finally, we explore the possibility of converting a non-uniform propagation of chaos result, e.g. from Theorem
7
, into a uniform one. Specifically, we show that
geometric ergodicity
of the mean field and interacting particle systems, plus non-uniform propagation of chaos, can result in uniform propagation of chaos. This approach was explored in
[
38
]
in continuous time for the 2-Wasserstein distance.

Assumption 3.1
.

Let
K_{\bullet}
be time-homogeneous.

A1.

Assume that
K_{\bullet}
satisfies Assumption
A1
, that
M_{k}^{(r)}\leq M_{\star}^{(p)}<\infty
, and that
\tau:=\tau_{1}(K_{\bullet})>1
so that by Proposition
2

\mathbf{E}[\mathbf{W}_{1}(\eta^{N}_{n},\eta_{n})]\leq\widetilde{C}_{p,d}N^{-1/d}\tau^{n}

for some
\widetilde{C}_{p,d}>0
independent of
N
.

A2.

There is
\alpha<1,~C<\infty
, an invariant measure
\eta_{\infty}\in\mathcal{P}_{1}(\mathbf{R}^{d})
for (
4
) and for every
N\geq 1
, there is an invariant measure
\eta^{N,N}_{\infty}\in\mathcal{P}_{1}((\mathbf{R}^{d})^{\otimes N})
for (
5
) such that we have

\displaystyle\mathbf{W}_{1}(\eta_{n},\eta_{\infty})

\displaystyle\leq C\alpha^{n}\mathbf{W}_{1}(\eta_{0},\eta_{\infty})

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}[\mathbf{W}_{1}(\eta^{N}_{n},\eta^{N}_{\infty})]

\displaystyle\leq C\alpha^{n}\mathbf{E}[\mathbf{W}_{1}(\eta^{N}_{0},\eta^{N}_{\infty})]

where
\eta^{N}_{\infty}:=m(\overline{X}^{N}_{\infty})
with
\overline{X}^{N}_{\infty}\sim\eta^{N,N}_{\infty}
.

A3.

\eta_{\infty}
has finite
p
-th moment for some
p>1
.

Proposition 15
.

Suppose Assumption
3.1
holds. Then there exists a constant
C^{\prime}>0
independent of
N
such that

\mathbf{E}[\mathbf{W}_{1}(\eta^{N}_{\infty},\eta_{\infty})]\leq C^{\prime}N^{-1/d}.

Proof.

For any trajectories
\eta_{n},\eta^{N,N}_{n}
following dynamics (
4
), (
5
) respectively, with initial conditions
\eta_{0}=\eta_{\infty}
,
X^{i}_{0}\overset{iid}{\sim}\eta_{0}
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}[\mathbf{W}_{1}(\eta^{N}_{\infty},\eta_{\infty})]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbf{E}[\mathbf{W}_{1}(\eta^{N}_{\infty},\eta^{N}_{n})]+\mathbf{E}[\mathbf{W}_{1}(\eta^{N}_{n},\eta_{n})]+\mathbf{W}_{1}(\eta_{n},\eta_{\infty})

\displaystyle\leq C\alpha^{n}\mathbf{E}[\mathbf{W}_{1}(\eta^{N}_{\infty},\eta^{N}_{0})]+\widetilde{C}_{p,d}N^{-1/d}\tau^{n}+\underbrace{C\alpha^{n}\mathbf{W}_{1}(\eta_{0},\eta_{\infty})}_{=0}

\displaystyle=C\alpha^{n}\mathbf{E}[\mathbf{W}_{1}(\eta^{N}_{\infty},\eta^{N}_{0})]+\widetilde{C}_{p,d}N^{-1/d}\tau^{n}

\displaystyle\leq C\alpha^{n}\mathbf{E}[\mathbf{W}_{1}(\eta^{N}_{\infty},\eta_{0})]+C\alpha^{n}\mathbf{E}[\mathbf{W}_{1}(\eta^{N}_{0},\eta_{0})]+\widetilde{C}_{p,d}N^{-1/d}\tau^{n}

\displaystyle=C\alpha^{n}\mathbf{E}[\mathbf{W}_{1}(\eta^{N}_{\infty},\eta_{\infty})]+C\alpha^{n}\mathbf{E}[\mathbf{W}_{1}(\eta^{N}_{0},\eta_{\infty})]+\widetilde{C}_{p,d}N^{-1/d}\tau^{n}.

Note that
\eta^{N}_{0}\neq\eta^{N}_{\infty}
, but rather
\eta^{N}_{0}=\widetilde{\eta}^{N}_{\infty}=:m(\widetilde{X}^{1}_{\infty},\dots,\widetilde{X}^{N}_{\infty})
where each
\widetilde{X}^{i}_{\infty}\overset{iid}{\sim}\eta_{\infty}
. However, we do have from
[
29
]
that

\mathbf{E}[\mathbf{W}_{1}(\widetilde{\eta}^{N}_{\infty},\eta_{\infty})]\leq C_{p,d}\mathbf{M}_{p}(\eta_{\infty})N^{-1/d}

so that

\mathbf{E}[\mathbf{W}_{1}(\eta^{N}_{\infty},\eta_{\infty})]\leq C\alpha^{n}\mathbf{E}[\mathbf{W}_{1}(\eta^{N}_{\infty},\eta_{\infty})]+\widetilde{C}_{p,d}^{\prime}N^{-1/d}(\tau^{n}+1).

Then picking
n=\lceil\log(2C)/\log(1/\alpha)\rceil
we get

\mathbf{E}[\mathbf{W}_{1}(\eta^{N}_{\infty},\eta_{\infty})]\leq 2\widetilde{C}N^{-1/d}(2C)^{\log(\tau)/\log(1/\alpha)}.

∎

Theorem 16
.

Suppose Assumption
3.1
holds, and that
\eta^{N,N}_{0}=\eta_{0}^{\otimes N}
. Then there is
\kappa\in(0,1/d)
and
C^{\prime}
independent of
n,N
s.t.
\forall n\geq 0
and
N\geq 1
we have

\mathbf{E}[\mathbf{W}_{1}(\eta^{N}_{n},\eta_{n})]\leq\frac{C^{\prime}}{N^{\kappa}}.

Proof.

Using the triangle inequality, plus Proposition
15
, we get

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}[\mathbf{W}_{1}(\eta^{N}_{n},\eta_{n})]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbf{E}[\mathbf{W}_{1}(\eta^{N}_{n},\eta^{N}_{\infty})]+\mathbf{E}[\mathbf{W}_{1}(\eta^{N}_{\infty},\eta_{\infty})]+\mathbf{W}_{1}(\eta_{\infty},\eta_{n})

\displaystyle\leq C\alpha^{n}\mathbf{E}[\mathbf{W}_{1}(\eta^{N}_{0},\eta^{N}_{\infty})]+\widetilde{C}N^{-1/d}+C\alpha^{n}\mathbf{W}_{1}(\eta_{0},\eta_{\infty}).

Using the fact that
X^{i}_{0}\overset{iid}{\sim}\eta_{0}
,

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}[\mathbf{W}_{1}(\eta^{N}_{0},\eta^{N}_{\infty})]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbf{E}[\mathbf{W}_{1}(\eta^{N}_{0},\eta_{0})]+\mathbf{W}_{1}(\eta_{0},\eta_{\infty})+\mathbf{E}[\mathbf{W}_{1}(\eta_{\infty},\eta^{N}_{\infty})]

and using
[
29
, Theorem 1]
again on the first term and Proposition
15
on the last term we get

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}[\mathbf{W}_{1}(\eta^{N}_{0},\eta^{N}_{\infty})]

\displaystyle\leq C^{\prime\prime}N^{-1/d}+\mathbf{W}_{1}(\eta_{0},\eta_{\infty})+\widetilde{C}N^{-1/d}

\displaystyle=C^{\prime\prime\prime}N^{-1/d}+\mathbf{W}_{1}(\eta_{0},\eta_{\infty}).

Therefore inserting this above, we are left with

\mathbf{E}[\mathbf{W}_{1}(\eta^{N}_{n},\eta_{n})]\leq C_{0}\alpha^{n}+C_{1}N^{-1/d}

for some constants
C_{0},C_{1}
independent of
n,N
.

To conclude, we balance this estimate, which is good for large
n
but has a term independent of
N
, against the estimate from Theorem
7
, which behaves well in
N
for all fixed
n
but explodes as
n\to\infty
. In precise terms, we have

\mathbf{E}[\mathbf{W}_{1}(\eta^{N}_{n},\eta_{n})]\leq C_{2}\left((\alpha^{n}+N^{-1/d})\wedge\tau^{n}N^{-1/d}\right)

hence for
n_{0}:=(1/d)\log(N)/\log(\tau/\alpha)
, distinguishing between
n<n_{0}
and
n>n_{0}
establishes the proof, with

\kappa:=\frac{1}{d}\frac{\log(1/\alpha)}{\log(\tau/\alpha)}.

∎

Note that
\mathbf{E}[\mathbf{W}_{1}(\eta^{N}_{n},\eta_{n})]
being geometrically ergodic, independent of
N
no less, is a strong assumption. It is stronger than asking the same of
\eta^{N,N}_{n}
. It would be much more desirable to study
\eta^{N,N}_{n}
as this is a
bona fide
linear Markov chain, and hence its ergodicity is very well understood (although dependence on
N
is another matter). However, all we know at this level of generality is that

\mathbf{E}[\mathbf{W}_{1}(\eta^{N}_{n},\eta^{N}_{\infty})]\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\mathbf{W}_{1}(\eta^{N,N}_{n},\eta^{N,N}_{\infty})

using the
1/\sqrt{N}
-Lipschitzness of the map
x:=(x^{1},\dots,x^{N})\mapsto m(x)
. Assuming instead that
\eta^{N,N}_{n}
is uniformly-in-
N
ergodic, we would then get

\mathbf{E}[\mathbf{W}_{1}(\eta^{N}_{n},\eta^{N}_{\infty})]\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}C\alpha^{n}\mathbf{W}_{1}(\eta^{N,N}_{0},\eta^{N,N}_{\infty}).

However, this is incompatible with the strategy of Proposition
15
as we need to relate the right side back to the left side in order to obtain a self-contained bound.

Corollary 17
.

Under Assumption
3.1
, we have the uniform propagation of chaos

\mathbf{W}_{1}(\eta^{N,q}_{n},\eta_{n}^{\otimes q})\leq C^{\prime}\frac{q}{N^{\kappa}}+2\frac{q^{3}}{N}.

This is
\mathcal{O}(q/N^{\kappa}\vee q^{3}/N))
which is worse than the coupling bound in Theorem
7
. However, note that above we explicitly assumed that the Lipschitz constant was greater than one so we cannot plug this back into Theorem
7
and we must use the more general, but worse, approach in Theorem
5
that doesn’t use additional regularity. One exception is that if Assumption
A2
is satisfied with
c<1
but
c+C>1
, this would result in
\mathcal{O}(q/N^{\kappa})
bounds.

4
Euler-Maruyama Scheme for McKean-Vlasov SDE

In this section, we use the tools developed thus far to study propagation of chaos for a family of nonlinear Markov kernels associated to Euler-Maruyama discretizations of the following McKean-Vlasov nonlinear stochastic differential equation, recalled from (
2
)

\mathrm{d}X_{t}=-\nabla V(X_{t})\mathrm{d}t-\nabla W*\eta_{t}(X_{t})\mathrm{d}t+\sqrt{2}\mathrm{d}B_{t}.

(9)

This equation, and continuous-time propagation of chaos techniques for its associated particle system, are discussed in Section
1.1
. In particular, the numerical analysis of these nonlinear diffusions was originally studied in
[
6
]
, and we use the same setup, although our analysis is different. Hence consider the simple first-order, explicit Euler-Maruyama scheme with discretization size
\delta>0
associated to (
9
)

X_{n+1}=X_{n}-\delta\nabla V(X_{n})-\delta\nabla W*\eta_{n}(X_{n})+\sqrt{2\delta}Z_{n};~~~\eta_{n}:=\mathrm{Law}(X_{n}),~Z_{n}\overset{iid}{\sim}\mathcal{N}(0,I_{d})

(10)

which defines a nonlinear Markov chain having the nonlinear Markov kernel

K^{\delta}_{\eta}(x,\mathrm{d}y):=\mathcal{N}(x-\delta\nabla V(x)-\delta\nabla W*\eta(x),2\delta I_{d})(\mathrm{d}y)

(11)

where
\mathcal{N}(m,\Sigma)
is the
d
-dimensional multivariate normal with mean vector
m
and covariance matrix
\Sigma
. Below, we show how the techniques developed in Section
2
can be readily applied to prove propagation of chaos in the case that
V+W
is uniformly convex. Note that
\delta
will be fixed for this analysis, so we will suppress it in the notation when there is no chance of confusion.

Assumption 4.1
.

Let
V,W:\mathbf{R}^{d}\to\mathbf{R}
.

A1.

Assume that
\mathrm{Hess}(V)\geq\lambda_{V}I_{d}
and
\mathrm{Hess}(W)\geq\lambda_{W}I_{d}
for
\lambda_{V},\lambda_{W}\in\mathbf{R}
.

A2.

Assume that
\nabla V
is
C_{V}
-Lipschitz and that
\nabla W
is
C_{W}
-Lipschitz.

A3.

W
is an even function.

Now we prove a basic lemma on the properties of the interaction term.

Lemma 18
.

Let
\mu,\nu\in\mathcal{P}_{1}(\mathbf{R}^{d})
.

1.

Suppose Assumption
A1
, i.e.
W:\mathbf{R}^{d}\to\mathbf{R}
is s.t.
\mathrm{Hess}(W)\geq\lambda_{W}I_{d}
for some
\lambda_{W}\in\mathbf{R}
. Then for
\mu\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbf{R}^{d})

\left\langle x-y,\nabla W*\mu(x)-\nabla W*\mu(y)\right\rangle\geq\lambda_{W}\|x-y\|^{2}

Suppose Assumption

\nabla W

Suppose Assumptions

,
A3
, i.e.
\nabla W
is Lipschitz-continuous with constant

\mathbf{M}_{2}(\eta)<\infty

\|\nabla W*\eta(x)\|^{2}\leq 2C_{W}^{2}\|x\|^{2}+2C_{W}^{2}\mathbf{M}_{2}(\eta).

Proof.

1.

We have

\displaystyle\left\langle x-y,\nabla W*\mu(x)-\nabla W*\mu(y)\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\mathbf{E}_{\mu}[\left\langle x-y,\nabla W(x-Z)-\nabla W(y-Z)\right\rangle]

\displaystyle=\mathbf{E}_{\mu}[\left\langle(x-Z)-(y-Z),\nabla W(x-Z)-\nabla W(y-Z)\right\rangle]

\displaystyle\geq\lambda_{W}\mathbf{E}_{\mu}[\|(x-Z)-(y-Z)\|^{2}]=\lambda_{W}\|x-y\|^{2}

2.

Consider

\displaystyle\|\nabla W*\mu(x)-\nabla W*\nu(y)\|

\displaystyle\leq\|\nabla W*\mu(x)-\nabla W*\mu(y)\|+\|\nabla W*\mu(y)-\nabla W*\nu(y)\|

\displaystyle=\left\|\int\nabla W(x-z)\mu(\mathrm{d}z)-\int\nabla W(y-z^{\prime})\mu(\mathrm{d}z^{\prime})\right\|\cdots

\displaystyle+\left\|\int\nabla W(y-z)\mu(\mathrm{d}z)-\int\nabla W(y-z^{\prime})\nu(\mathrm{d}z^{\prime})\right\|

\displaystyle=\left\|\int[\nabla W(x-z)-\nabla W(y-z)]\mu(\mathrm{d}z)\right\|+\left\|\int\nabla W(y-z)\mu(\mathrm{d}z)-\int\nabla W(y-z^{\prime})\nu(\mathrm{d}z^{\prime})\right\|

The first term can be bounded using Lipschitzness of
W

\left\|\int[\nabla W(x-z)-\nabla W(y-z)]\mu(\mathrm{d}z)\right\|\leq\int\|\nabla W(x-z)-\nabla W(y-z)\|\mu(\mathrm{d}z)\leq C_{W}\|x-y\|.

For the second term, let
\gamma\in\mathcal{C}(\mu,\nu)
be any coupling of
\mu,\nu

\displaystyle\left\|\int\nabla W(y-z)\mu(\mathrm{d}z)-\int\nabla W(y-z^{\prime})\nu(\mathrm{d}z^{\prime})\right\|

\displaystyle=\left\|\int\nabla W(y-z)\gamma(\mathrm{d}z,\mathrm{d}z^{\prime})-\int\nabla W(y-z^{\prime})\gamma(\mathrm{d}z,\mathrm{d}z^{\prime})\right\|

\displaystyle=\left\|\int[\nabla W(y-z)-\nabla W(y-z^{\prime})]\gamma(\mathrm{d}z,\mathrm{d}z^{\prime})\right\|

\displaystyle\leq\int\|\nabla W(y-z)-\nabla W(y-z^{\prime})\|\gamma(\mathrm{d}z,\mathrm{d}z^{\prime})

\displaystyle\leq C_{W}\int\|z-z^{\prime}\|\gamma(\mathrm{d}z,\mathrm{d}z^{\prime}).

Taking the infimum over all such couplings yields

\|\nabla W*\mu(y)-\nabla W*\nu(y)\|\leq C_{W}\mathbf{W}_{1}(\mu,\nu)

3.

Using Lipzchitzness of
\nabla W
and evenness of
W

\nabla W(0)=0

\displaystyle\|\nabla W*\eta(x)\|^{2}

\displaystyle=\left\|\int\nabla W(x-y)\eta(\mathrm{d}y)\right\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\int\|\nabla W(x-y)\|^{2}\eta(\mathrm{d}y)

\displaystyle\leq C_{W}^{2}\int\|x-y\|^{2}\eta(\mathrm{d}y)

\displaystyle\leq C_{W}^{2}2\|x\|^{2}+2C_{W}^{2}\mathbf{M}_{2}(\eta).

Now we can prove a central result establishing the regularity of

Proposition 19
.

Suppose that Assumption
4.1
holds, and let
\mu,\nu\in\mathcal{P}_{1}(\mathbf{R}^{d})

\mathbf{W}_{2}(K_{\mu}(x,\bullet),K_{\nu}(y,\bullet))\leq\sqrt{1-2\delta(\lambda_{V}+\lambda_{W})+\delta^{2}(C_{V}+C_{W})^{2}}\|x-y\|+\delta C_{W}\mathbf{W}_{1}(\mu,\nu).

Proof.

Since
K_{\eta}(x,\bullet)
is Gaussian, we can use the formula for the optimal

\mathbf{W}_{2}(K_{\mu}(x,\bullet),K_{\nu}(y,\bullet))=\|x-\delta\nabla V(x)-\delta\nabla W*\mu(x)-(y-\delta\nabla V(y)-\delta\nabla W*\nu(y))\|.

We will use the triangle inequality to separately estimate

\mathbf{W}_{2}(K_{\mu}(x,\bullet),K_{\nu}(y,\bullet))\leq\underbrace{\mathbf{W}_{2}(K_{\mu}(x,\bullet),K_{\mu}(y,\bullet))}_{(1)}+\underbrace{\mathbf{W}_{2}(K_{\mu}(y,\bullet),K_{\nu}(y,\bullet))}_{(2)}.

For
(1)
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbf{W}^{2}_{2}(K_{\mu}(x,\bullet),K_{\mu}(y,\bullet))

\displaystyle=\|x-y-\delta(\nabla V(x)+\nabla W*\mu(x)-(\nabla V(y)+\nabla W*\mu(y))\|^{2}

\displaystyle=\|x-y\|^{2}-2\delta\left\langle x-y,\nabla V(x)+\nabla W*\mu(x)-(\nabla V(y)+\nabla W*\mu(y))\right\rangle+\cdots

\displaystyle\cdots+\delta^{2}\|\nabla V(x)+\nabla W*\mu(x)-(\nabla V(y)+\nabla W*\mu(y))\|^{2}.

The last term can be straightforwardly estimated by
\delta^{2}(C_{V}+C_{W})^{2}\|x-y\|^{2}
from the Lipschitz assumptions and Lemma

. For the middle term, we have

\displaystyle 2\delta\left\langle x-y,\nabla V(x)+\nabla W*\mu(x)-(\nabla V(y)+\nabla W*\mu(y))\right\rangle

\displaystyle=2\delta\left\langle x-y,\nabla V(x)-\nabla V(y)\right\rangle+2\delta\left\langle x-y,\nabla W*\mu(x)-\nabla W*\mu(y))\right\rangle

\displaystyle\geq 2\delta(\lambda_{V}+\lambda_{W})\|x-y\|^{2}

using Lemma
18
. Hence for
(1)

\mathbf{W}_{2}(K_{\mu}(x,\bullet),K_{\mu}(y,\bullet)\leq\sqrt{1-2\delta(\lambda_{V}+\lambda_{W})+\delta^{2}(C_{V}+C_{W})^{2}}\|x-y\|.

For
(2)

18
to obtain

\displaystyle\mathbf{W}_{2}(K_{\mu}(y,\bullet),K_{\nu}(y,\bullet))=\delta\|\nabla W*\mu(y)-\nabla W*\nu(y)\|\leq\delta C_{W}\mathbf{W}_{1}(\mu,\nu).

Hence putting these together yields the result.

Moments

The second ingredient necessary for the application of Theorem

Proposition 22
.

Let
K^{\delta}_{\eta}
be the nonlinear Markov kernel from (
11
) and suppose Assumption
4.1
holds. Then

K^{\delta}_{\eta}\mathbf{V}_{2}(x)\leq a\mathbf{V}_{2}(x)+b\eta(\mathbf{V}_{2})+c

where

\displaystyle a

\displaystyle:=1+\delta(2-2(\lambda_{V}+\lambda_{W}))+4\delta^{2}(C_{V}^{2}+C_{W}^{2})

\displaystyle b

\displaystyle:=\delta(2+4\delta)C_{W}^{2}

\displaystyle c

\displaystyle:=(1+4\delta)\delta\|\nabla V(0)\|^{2}+2d\delta.

Proof.

Recall first that if
Y\sim K^{\delta}_{\eta}(x,\bullet)

Y=x-\delta\nabla V(x)-\delta\nabla W*\eta(x)+\sqrt{2\delta}Z;~~~Z\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I_{d}).

We will use the fact

K^{\delta}_{\eta}\mathbf{V}_{2}(x)=\mathbf{E}_{Y\sim K^{\delta}_{\eta}(x,\bullet)}[\|Y\|^{2}]

\mathbf{E}[\|u+vZ\|^{2}]=\mathbf{E}[\|u\|^{2}]+2\mathbf{E}[\left\langle u,vZ\right\rangle]+v^{2}\mathbf{E}[\|Z\|^{2}]=\|u\|^{2}+v^{2}\mathbf{E}[\|Z\|^{2}],

which follows from linearity of expectations and that
\mathbf{E}[Z]=0

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}[\|Y\|^{2}]=

\displaystyle\|x\|^{2}-2\delta\left\langle x,\nabla V(x)\right\rangle-2\delta\left\langle x,\nabla W*\eta(x)\right\rangle+\cdots

\displaystyle+\delta^{2}\|\nabla V(x)\|^{2}+\delta^{2}\|\nabla W*\eta(x)\|^{2}+2\delta^{2}\left\langle\nabla V(x),\nabla W*\eta(x)\right\rangle+2\delta\mathbf{E}[\|Z\|^{2}].

Now recall that
\nabla V

\|\nabla V(x)\|\leq C_{V}\|x\|+\|\nabla V(0)\|

\displaystyle\left\langle x,\nabla V(x)\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\left\langle x-0,\nabla V(x)-\nabla V(0)+\nabla V(0)\right\rangle

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}[\|Y\|^{2}]\leq

\displaystyle\|x\|^{2}-2\delta\left\langle x,\nabla V(x)\right\rangle-2\delta\left\langle x,\nabla W*\eta(x)\right\rangle+\delta^{2}\|\nabla V(x)\|^{2}+\delta^{2}\|\nabla W*\eta(x)\|^{2}+\cdots

\displaystyle\|x\|^{2}-2\delta(\lambda_{V}+\lambda_{W})\|x\|^{2}-2\delta\left\langle x,\nabla V(0)\right\rangle-2\delta\left\langle x,\nabla W*\eta(0)\right\rangle+\cdots

5
Particle Filtering & Feynman-Kac Distribution Flows

Finally, we study an important class of nonlinear Markov chains connected to many areas of applied mathematics including, but not limited to, nonlinear filtering problems
[
21
]
, sequential Monte Carlo
[
16
]
, genetic algorithms
[
15
]
, and Markov chain Monte Carlo
[
2
,
61
]
. This is the so-called Feynman-Kac model framework studied at length in
[
23
]
and related works. We do not go into full depth here as the topic is extensive, but focus on the nonlinear Markov chain interpretation and its connections to nonlinear filtering.

5.1
Problem Setup

Consider a family of nonlinear measure maps
\Phi_{n}:\mathcal{P}(\mathbf{R}^{d})\to\mathcal{P}(\mathbf{R}^{d})
defined as

\Phi_{n}[\eta]:=\Psi_{G_{n-1}}(\eta)M^{(n)}=\int\Psi_{G_{n-1}}(\eta)(\mathrm{d}x)M^{(n)}(x,\bullet)

(13)

where
M^{(n)}
is a family of time-inhomoegeous
linear
Markov kernels,
G_{n}:\mathbf{R}^{d}\to[0,\infty)
is a family of potential functions, and
\Psi_{G_{n}}(\eta)
is the
Boltzmann-Gibbs transformation
associated to
G_{n}
, defined for measurable
f:\mathbf{R}^{d}\to\mathbf{R}
as

\Psi_{G_{n}}(\eta)(f):=\frac{1}{\eta(G_{n})}\int f(x)G_{n}(x)\eta(\mathrm{d}x)

(14)

whenever
\eta(G_{n})>0
.

It is possible to obtain a (nonunique) family of nonlinear Markov kernels
K^{(n)}_{\bullet}
that satisfy
\Phi_{n}[\eta]=\eta K^{(n)}_{\eta}
; we will study the choice

\displaystyle K^{(n)}_{\eta}(x,\mathrm{d}y)

\displaystyle:=S^{(n-1)}_{\eta}M^{(n)}(x,\mathrm{d}y);

(15)

\displaystyle S^{(n)}_{\eta}(x,\mathrm{d}y)

\displaystyle:=\lambda_{n}G_{n}(x)\delta_{x}(\mathrm{d}y)+(1-\lambda_{n}G_{n}(x))\Psi_{G_{n}}(\eta)(\mathrm{d}y)

(16)

where
\lambda_{n}\in(0,1)
is chosen such that
\lambda_{n}G_{n}(x)\leq 1~\forall x\in\mathbf{R}^{d}
. In particular,
\Psi_{G_{n}}(\eta)=\eta S^{(n)}_{\eta}
for any
\eta\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbf{R}^{d})
for which all terms are defined. This corresponds to “selection-mutation” dynamics wherein the potential functions
G_{n}
determine the “fitness” of
x
,
S^{(n-1)}_{\eta}(x,\mathrm{d}y)
samples according to this fitness with rejection, and then
M^{(n)}
mutates the sample
x
independently.

Connection to Bayesian Filtering

The system (
13
) has a deep connection to Bayesian (aka, nonlinear) filtering. To be specific, consider a sequence of pairs of random variables
(X_{n},Y_{n})
where
X_{n}
is the
state
variable which is unobserved, and
Y_{n}
is the observation variable, which is observed. We are interested in computing the
smoothed
distribution
\mathbf{P}(X_{n}\in\mathrm{d}x_{n}|Y_{\leq n}=y_{\leq n})
and the
prediction
distribution
\mathbf{P}(X_{n+1}\in\mathrm{d}x_{n+1}|Y_{\leq n}=y_{\leq n})
. To see how this relates to (
13
), first note the connection between the Boltzmann-Gibbs transformation (
14
) and Bayes’ formula: for a likelihood density with a fixed
y
written as
G(x):=\mathbf{P}(Y=y|X=x)
and prior
\mathbf{P}(X\in\mathrm{d}x)
we have

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}(X\in\mathrm{d}x|Y=y)

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathbf{P}(Y=y|X=x)}{\mathbf{P}(Y=y)}\mathbf{P}(X\in\mathrm{d}x)

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathbf{P}(Y=y|X=x)}{\int\mathbf{P}(Y=y|X=x^{\prime})\mathbf{P}(x\in\mathrm{d}x^{\prime})}\mathbf{P}(X\in\mathrm{d}x)

\displaystyle=\frac{G(x)}{\int G(x^{\prime})\mathbf{P}(X\in\mathrm{d}x^{\prime})}\mathbf{P}(X\in\mathrm{d}x)

\displaystyle=\Psi_{G}(\mathbf{P}(X\in\bullet))(\mathrm{d}x).

Now, for a fixed realization
\{Y_{n}=y_{n}\}_{n=0}^{\infty}
, recall the Bayesian filtering equations: first, we have the smoothing equation

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}(X_{n}\in\mathrm{d}x|Y_{\leq n}=y_{\leq n})

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathbf{P}(Y_{n}=y_{n}|X_{n}=x)}{\mathbf{P}(Y_{n}=y_{n}|Y_{<n}=y_{<n})}\mathbf{P}(X_{n}\in\mathrm{d}x|Y_{<n}=y_{<n})

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathbf{P}(Y_{n}=y_{n}|X_{n}=x)}{\int\mathbf{P}(Y_{n}=y_{n}|X_{n}=x^{\prime})\mathbf{P}(X_{n}\in\mathrm{d}x^{\prime}|Y_{<n}=y_{<n})}\mathbf{P}(X_{n}\in\mathrm{d}x|Y_{<n}=y_{<n})

which by the above remark can be written as

\mathbf{P}(X_{n}\in\mathrm{d}x|Y_{\leq n}=y_{\leq n})=\Psi_{G_{n}}(\mathbf{P}(X_{n}\in\bullet|Y_{<n}=y_{<n}))(\mathrm{d}x)

for the observation likelihood
G_{n}(x):=\mathbf{P}(Y_{n}=y_{n}|X_{n}=x)
. Then we have the prediction equation

\mathbf{P}(X_{n+1}\in\mathrm{d}x|Y_{\leq n}=y_{\leq n})=\int\mathbf{P}(X_{n+1}\in\mathrm{d}x|X_{n}=x^{\prime})\mathbf{P}(X_{n}\in\mathrm{d}x^{\prime}|Y_{\leq n}=y_{\leq n})

which can be written

\mathbf{P}(X_{n+1}\in\mathrm{d}x|Y_{\leq n}=y_{\leq n})=\int\mathbf{P}(X_{n}\in\mathrm{d}x^{\prime}|Y_{\leq n}=y_{\leq n})M^{(n+1)}(x^{\prime},\mathrm{d}x)

for

M^{(n+1)}(x,\mathrm{d}x^{\prime}):=\mathbf{P}(X_{n+1}\in\mathrm{d}x^{\prime}|X_{n}=x).

Hence, by identifying
\eta_{n}(\mathrm{d}x):=\mathbf{P}(X_{n}\in\mathrm{d}x|Y_{<n}=y_{<n})
, we have reformulated the Bayesian filtering equations as a Feyman-Kac distribution flow which is precisely (
13
).

Simulation and Particle Filtering

Solving the nonlinear filtering problem is plainly valuable for many applications. However, except for special cases, the Markov chain
X_{n+1}\sim K^{(n+1)}_{\eta_{n}}(X_{n},\bullet)
cannot be simulated directly due to the dependence on
\eta_{n}
. On the other hand, one may approximate
\eta_{n}\approx\eta^{N}_{n}=:m(\overline{X}_{n})
with an empirical measure, and obtain a family of algorithms for filtering generally called “particle filtering” algorithms that implement a time-inhomoheneous Markov chain

X^{i}_{n}\sim K^{(n+1)}_{\eta^{N}_{n}}(X^{i}_{n},\bullet)

which
can
be simulated. In particular, this kernel models the popular “SIS-R filter” as a self-interacting Markov chain. Clearly, understanding the accuracy of the resulting particle system as a function of
N
has practical implications on the accuracy of real-world decisions based on filtered estimates.

This is simple algebra.

\displaystyle a_{n}+b_{n}

\displaystyle=\widetilde{a}_{n}\lambda_{n-1}\overline{G}_{n-1}+\widetilde{a}_{n}\overline{G}_{n-1}(1-\lambda_{n-1}\varepsilon)\varepsilon^{-1}

\displaystyle=\widetilde{a}_{n}\left(\frac{\varepsilon\lambda_{n-1}\overline{G}_{n-1}}{\varepsilon}+\frac{\overline{G}_{n-1}(1-\lambda_{n}\epsilon)}{\epsilon}\right)

\displaystyle=\widetilde{a}_{n}\frac{\overline{G}_{n-1}}{\varepsilon}.

∎

Remark 5.2
.

Let us comment on the intuition of
\widetilde{a}_{n}\overline{G}_{n-1}/\varepsilon<1
. Another way of describing
\overline{G}_{n-1}/\varepsilon
is

\frac{\overline{G}_{n-1}}{\varepsilon}=\frac{\max_{x}G_{n-1}(x)}{\min_{y}G_{n-1}(y)}=\max_{x,y}\frac{G_{n-1}(x)}{G_{n-1}(y)}.

This is equivalent to the existence of a number
\epsilon(G_{n-1})>0
such that
G_{n-1}(y)\geq\epsilon(G_{n-1})G_{n-1}(x)
which is in fact the ubiquitous “condition
(G)
” from
[
23
, Section 3.5.2]
and measures the “mixing” of
G
. Therefore, one can interpret
\widetilde{a}_{n}\overline{G}_{n-1}/\varepsilon<1
as balancing the moment containment of
M^{(n)}
against the mixing of
G_{n-1}
— in particular, the less mixing
G_{n-1}
is, the more
M^{(n)}
must compensate to maintain bounded moments.

Lemma 27
.

Assume that
\overline{G}:=\|G\|_{\infty}<\infty
and Assumption
5.3
holds. Then for any
\mu,\nu\in\mathcal{P}_{1}(\mathbf{R}^{d})
for which
\mu(G),\nu(G)>0
,

\mathbf{W}_{1}(\Psi_{G}(\mu),\Psi_{G}(\nu))\leq\left(\frac{\|G\|_{\mathrm{Lip}}+\widetilde{G}+\overline{G}}{\mu(G)}+\frac{\|G\|_{\mathrm{Lip}}\overline{G}}{\mu(G)\nu(G)}\mathbf{M}_{1}(\nu)\right)\mathbf{W}_{1}(\mu,\nu).

Proof.

Let
f\in\mathrm{Lip}(1)
. Then

\displaystyle|\Psi_{G}(\mu)(f)-\Psi_{G}(\nu)(f)|

\displaystyle=\left|\frac{\mu(Gf)}{\mu(G)}-\frac{\nu(Gf)}{\nu(G)}\right|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{\mu(G)\nu(G)}|\mu(Gf)\nu(G)-\nu(Gf)\mu(G)|

\displaystyle\leq\underbrace{\frac{\nu(G)}{\mu(G)\nu(G)}|\mu(Gf)-\nu(Gf)|}_{(1)}+\underbrace{\frac{|\nu(Gf)|}{\mu(G)\nu(G)}|\nu(G)-\mu(G)|}_{(2)}.

For the second term

\displaystyle(2)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{|\nu(Gf)|}{\mu(G)\nu(G)}\|G\|_{\mathrm{Lip}}\mathbf{W}_{1}(\mu,\nu)

and without loss of generality we may assume
f(0)=0
hence

|\nu(Gf)|=\left|\int G(x)f(x)\nu(\mathrm{d}x)\right|\leq\int G(x)\|x\|\nu(\mathrm{d}x)\leq\overline{G}M_{1}(\nu).

Thus

(2)\leq\frac{\overline{G}M_{1}(\nu)}{\mu(G)\nu(G)}\|G\|_{\mathrm{Lip}}\mathbf{W}_{1}(\mu,\nu).

For the first term, we have

(1)\leq\frac{1}{\mu(G)}\|Gf\|_{\mathrm{Lip}}\mathbf{W}_{1}(\mu,\nu)

so now we must estimate
\|Gf\|_{\mathrm{Lip}}
. But

\displaystyle\|Gf\|_{\mathrm{Lip}}=\sup_{x\neq y}\frac{|G(x)f(x)-G(y)f(y)|}{\|x-y\|}

and without loss of generality,
\|x-y\|\leq 1
therefore

\displaystyle|G(x)f(x)-G(y)f(y)|

\displaystyle\leq|f(x)||G(x)-G(y)|+|G(y)||f(x)-f(y)|

\displaystyle\leq|f(x)-f(0)||G(x)-G(y)|+|G(y)||f(x)-f(y)|

\displaystyle\leq|f(x)-f(0)||G(x)-G(y)|+\overline{G}\|x-y\|.

For the first term, using the Mean Value Theorem we get

\displaystyle|f(x)||G(x)-G(y)|

\displaystyle\leq|f(x)||\|\nabla G(tx+(1-t)y)\|\|x-y\|

for
t\in[0,1]
so that

\displaystyle|f(x)|\|\nabla G(tx+(1-t)y)\|

\displaystyle\leq|f(x)-f(0)|\|\nabla G((1-t)x+ty)\|

\displaystyle\leq\|x\|\|\nabla G((1-t)x+ty)\|

\displaystyle\leq(\|(1-t)x+ty\|+t\|x-y\|)\|\nabla G((1-t)x+ty)\|

\displaystyle\leq(1+\|(1-t)x+ty\|)\|\nabla G((1-t)x+ty)\|

\displaystyle\leq\|\nabla G\|_{\infty}+\widetilde{G}.

Recalling that
\|\nabla G\|_{\infty}\leq\|G\|_{\mathrm{Lip}}
we have

\|Gf\|_{\mathrm{Lip}}\leq\|G\|_{\mathrm{Lip}}+\widetilde{G}+\overline{G}

and therefore

(1)\leq\frac{\|G\|_{\mathrm{Lip}}+\widetilde{G}+\overline{G}}{\mu(G)}\mathbf{W}_{1}(\mu,\nu).

Putting everything together yields the result.
∎

Proposition 31

(Basic Properties)
.

1.

, and
\mu,\nu\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbf{R}^{d})

\mathbf{W}_{p}^{p}(\mu K,\nu K)\leq\inf_{\gamma\in\mathcal{C}(\mu,\nu)}\int\mathbf{W}_{p}^{p}(K(x,\bullet),K(y,\bullet))\gamma(\mathrm{d}x,\mathrm{d}y)

\displaystyle\mathbf{W}_{1}(\mu K,\mu Q)

\displaystyle=\sup_{f\in\mathrm{Lip}(1)}|\mu Kf-\mu Qf|

Following the argument from
[
13
]
, let
\pi_{x,y}
be a coupling of
K(x,\bullet),K(y,\bullet)

x,y\in\mathbf{R}^{d}

\gamma

\mu,\nu

\pi(\mathrm{d}x,\mathrm{d}y):=\int\pi_{x,y}\gamma(\mathrm{d}x,\mathrm{d}y)

Proposition 32

(Contractions)
.

Let
K

hence dividing and raising both sides to the power

Lemma 33
