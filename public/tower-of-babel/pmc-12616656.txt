Gut microbiota-derived TMAO drives the kidney-bone-vascular axis in chronic kidney disease complications
Authors: Yuyan Lei, Guiling Xiong, Dongyu Lei, Hao Wu, Xueqiong Peng, Lulu Chen, Qing Fang, Yusi Wu, Yu Wu, Xiaohui Li, Ying Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractChronic kidney disease (CKD) is often accompanied by comorbidities such as skeletal disorders and vascular calcification, which significantly elevate the mortality risk among affected patients. In recent years, dysbiosis of the gut microbiota has emerged as a key pathological factor in the progression of CKD, particularly through the accumulation of its metabolic product, trimethylamine-N-oxide (TMAO). TMAO is produced in the liver via the gut microbiota’s metabolism of dietary precursors such as choline, betaine, and L-carnitine. It is primarily excreted via the kidneys, and its levels are markedly elevated in CKD patients due to impaired renal function. Studies have demonstrated that TMAO is not only closely associated with an increased risk of vascular calcification but may also exacerbate skeletal disorders in CKD patients by disrupting bone metabolism. This review aims to elucidate the mechanisms underlying TMAO’s role in CKD, particularly focusing on its critical involvement in the kidney-bone-vascular axis. Based on existing evidence, we propose potential therapeutic strategies for modulating gut microbiota and TMAO levels, including dietary modifications, probiotic interventions, TMA cleavage enzyme inhibitors, FMO3 inhibitors, and pharmacological regulation, to improve skeletal and cardiovascular health in CKD patients.Keywords: Gut microbiota metabolites, TMAO, chronic kidney disease, bone disease, vascular calcification

Introduction

Chronic kidney disease (CKD) is a persistent abnormality in kidney structure or function lasting over three months, with implications for overall health [1]. CKD affects approximately 700 million individuals globally, and its prevalence has steadily increased in recent years [2]. Beyond progressive renal dysfunction, CKD is associated with a spectrum of severe complications, among which skeletal disorders and vascular calcification are particularly prominent. Skeletal complications in CKD patients are characterized by a significantly increased risk of osteoporosis and fractures [3], with dialysis patients exhibiting a fracture risk 1.5- to 8-fold higher than that of the general population [4]. Concurrently, vascular calcification represents a key pathological basis for the heightened incidence of cardiovascular disease in this population, markedly elevating the risk of cardiovascular events and mortality [5,6]. These comorbidities not only impair patients’ quality of life but also significantly elevate mortality risk. Evidence demonstrates a high prevalence of concurrent bone disease and vascular calcification among CKD patients [7–9]. In mouse models of CKD induced by high-phosphate and adenine-rich diets, vascular calcification is frequently accompanied by skeletal abnormalities [10]. This strong correlation suggests the potential involvement of shared pathophysiological mechanisms underlying vascular and skeletal complications in CKD.

Traditional drivers, such as disturbances in calcium-phosphate metabolism and secondary hyperparathyroidism (SHPT), play crucial roles in the onset of vascular calcification and skeletal disorders in patients with CKD. As renal function deteriorates, impaired phosphate excretion leads to hyperphosphatemia [11], which not only induces the transformation of vascular smooth muscle cells (VSMCs) into an osteoblast-like or chondrocyte-like phenotype [12] but also stimulates the secretion of parathyroid hormone (PTH), thereby triggering SHPT [13]. This, in turn, results in the excessive stimulation of osteocytes, leading to either osteoporotic or osteosclerotic changes [14]. Furthermore, oxidative stress and chronic inflammation, commonly observed in CKD, contribute to endothelial dysfunction and promote vascular calcification [15]. These factors also suppress osteocyte function, thereby fostering the development of skeletal disorders [16,17]. The deficiency of calcification inhibitors, such as osteoprotegerin and osteopontin [18], further exacerbates the progression of both vascular calcification and skeletal pathology.

In recent years, gut microbiota dysbiosis has emerged as a novel research focus on CKD-related complications. The gut microbiota, often called the invisible endocrine organ of the human body, is increasingly recognized as a pivotal pathological factor in CKD progression [19]. Due to the decline in renal function, CKD patients experience the accumulation of metabolic waste products, such as urea and creatinine, which the kidneys cannot effectively clear. These substances enter the gastrointestinal tract via the bloodstream, profoundly altering the gut microenvironment [19,20]. For instance, urea is metabolized by gut microbes into ammonia, increasing intestinal pH and disrupting the microbial ecological balance [21]. Additionally, dietary and pharmacological management of CKD patients further affects their gut microbiota balance. Patients are often recommended to adhere to low-protein, low-potassium, and low-phosphorus diets to alleviate renal burden and slow disease progression. However, the necessity of restricting fruits and vegetables, which are rich in potassium and phosphorus, leads to a reduction in beneficial microbial species and an overgrowth of pathogenic ones, accompanied by alterations in metabolic byproducts [22]. Moreover, long-term medication use, including antibiotics, phosphate binders, and iron supplements, can directly or indirectly disrupt the gut microbiome [23–25]. This dysbiosis facilitates the accumulation of uremic toxins such as indoxyl sulfate, para-cresyl sulfate, and trimethylamine-N-oxide (TMAO) [26,27]. TMAO, a key gut microbiota metabolite, has garnered significant attention. Studies have shown that the plasma TMAO concentration in healthy individuals is within the reference range of 5.72 µM, with a median value of 1.70 µM [28]; at the same time, levels in patients with end-stage CKD can rise dramatically, reaching up to 99.9 µM [29]. Increasing evidence suggests that TMAO correlates strongly with CKD progression, promotes skeletal disorders [30], and increases the risk of vascular calcification [31]. Furthermore, TMAO is implicated in various pathological mechanisms, including inflammation, oxidative stress, and apoptosis [32–35]. These processes may interact via the gut-kidney-bone-vascular axis, collectively influencing CKD patients’ skeletal and cardiovascular health.

Targeting TMAO holds promise as a novel therapeutic approach to improve skeletal and cardiovascular health in patients with CKD. Although several reviews have explored the association between TMAO and CKD or vascular calcification [36,37], no study has systematically integrated the complex interactions of TMAO with the kidney, skeletal, and vascular systems in CKD, nor the underlying potential mechanisms. Unlike Rodrigues et al. who focused on PCS- and IS-mediated mechanisms of vascular and bone complications, this review systematically examines the independent role of TMAO in this axis, thereby addressing a current gap in the literature [38]. This paper aims to elucidate the intricate relationships between TMAO, the skeletal system, and the vascular system in CKD, and to explore the potential pathophysiological mechanisms and intervention strategies. The goal is to provide a theoretical foundation and novel insights for managing of skeletal and cardiovascular diseases in CKD patients.

Generation and metabolism of TMAO

TMAO is a small organic molecule with a molecular weight of 75.11 g/mol and a chemical formula of (CH3)3NO. It exhibits diverse physiological functions in biological systems: (1) it regulates protein stability in aqueous environments by modulating hydrogen bond strength; (2) it serves as an electron acceptor in the anaerobic metabolism of bacteria; and (3) it functions as an osmolyte, maintaining cellular volume [39,40]. As a gut-derived metabolite, TMAO predominantly originates from foods rich in choline, phosphatidylcholine, and L-carnitine, such as red meat, eggs, and fish. Upon ingestion, these foods are metabolized by specific gut microbiota (e.g., Clostridium hathewayi, Anaerococcus hydrogenalis, Clostridium asparagiforme spp.) to produce trimethylamine (TMA) [41]. Following intestinal absorption, TMA is transported via the portal vein to the liver, where it is oxidized by flavin-containing monooxygenase 3 (FMO3) to generate TMAO [42,43]. In healthy individuals, the metabolism of TMAO is primarily dependent on renal function. Studies have shown that after administering radiolabeled TMA or TMAO, 94.5% of the labeled dose is excreted in the urine within 24 h, with only 4% eliminated in the feces and less than 1% exhaled via the breath [43]. However, in patients with renal disease, as renal function declines, there is a marked elevation in the levels of TMAO in the bloodstream [44]. Notably, this aberrant increase in TMAO concentrations is normalized following renal transplantation [45].

The role of TMAO in the kidney-bone-vascular axis

Common pathophysiological pathways activated by TMAO

The pathogenic effects of TMAO across various organ systems are primarily mediated through a set of common interconnected pathways, chiefly chronic inflammation and oxidative stress. A central mechanism is the activation of the NF-κB signaling pathway and the NLRP3 inflammasome, leading to the upregulated secretion of pro-inflammatory cytokines such as IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α [31,46–53]. Concurrently, TMAO enhances the activation of NADPH oxidases (NOX), resulting in the accumulation of reactive oxygen species (ROS), which exacerbates oxidative damage and further fuels inflammatory responses [49,54–58]. These core mechanisms—NF-κB/NLRP3-driven inflammation and ROS generation—interconnect to form a mutually reinforcing cycle that underlies TMAO’s detrimental effects.

The bidirectional relationship and mechanisms between TMAO and CKD

The relationship between TMAO and CKD is not merely a simple unidirectional causal association but involves a complex bidirectional interplay. On the one hand, impaired renal function and the associated dysbiosis in CKD patients lead to an elevation in TMAO levels. On the other hand, elevated TMAO levels further exacerbate the progression of CKD.

The mechanisms underlying the increased TMAO concentration in CKD patients remain a topic of ongoing debate. Some studies suggest that the elevation in TMAO levels may be attributed to its reduced clearance rate. The kidneys serve as the primary route for TMAO excretion, and as CKD progresses, a decline in estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) leads to the accumulation of TMAO in the body [40]. For instance, Pelletier et al. found that the elevated TMAO levels in CKD patients were primarily associated with a decrease in eGFR rather than the intake of dietary precursors such as choline and betaine [59]. Additionally, Wang et al. analyzed data from 979 CKD patients and demonstrated a dose-dependent relationship between TMAO levels and eGFR [60]. A meta-analysis involving 32 clinical studies further supported the significant correlation between elevated TMAO concentrations and reduced eGFR [61]. However, an increasing body of evidence suggests that the synthesis of TMAO may also be enhanced in CKD patients. Several studies have shown a concurrent increase in TMAO and its precursor, TMA, in CKD patients [62–64], indicating that the accumulation of TMAO may be linked to an elevated production of TMA in the gut. Furthermore, animal experiments have revealed increased expression of FMO3 in the liver of CKD mice, which may contribute to elevated TMAO levels [65]. Moreover, dysbiosis in the gut microbiota of CKD patients has been recognized as a critical factor in promoting TMAO synthesis. Microbial analysis has shown that end-stage renal disease (ESRD) patients exhibit enhanced potential for TMAO biosynthesis in their gut microbiota [66]. Xu et al. employed PICRUSt (a functional prediction tool based on 16S rRNA sequencing) and found that dysbiosis in the gut microbiome of CKD patients led to increased TMAO levels, with a significant upregulation of three genes involved in TMA production [67]. Furthermore, fecal transplantation from CKD patients into mice resulted in a significant increase in TMAO levels in these animals [67], directly confirming the role of the CKD gut microbiome in TMAO synthesis. Additionally, based on the concept of microbiota regulation, studies have shown that TMAO participates in the diarrhea of kidney Yang deficiency syndrome by mediating the gut-kidney axis [68,69]. Targeted regulation of the cecal microbiota can effectively reduce TMAO and inflammatory factor levels, significantly alleviating the symptoms of diarrhea in kidney Yang deficiency syndrome [70,71]. In addition to microbial imbalances, CKD patients often exhibit compromised intestinal epithelial integrity. This ‘leaky gut’ phenomenon allows microbial products and metabolites to more readily enter the bloodstream [72], thereby exacerbating the accumulation of TMAO. In summary, elevated TMAO levels in CKD patients may arise from combined reduced eGFR and enhanced TMAO synthesis. Impaired renal function leads to decreased TMAO clearance, while gut dysbiosis and impaired intestinal barrier function further promote TMAO synthesis and accumulation. These intricate mechanisms contribute to the significant elevation of TMAO levels in CKD patients and may increase the risk of bone disease and vascular calcification.

The role of TMAO in promoting the progression of CKD is primarily mediated through mechanisms of chronic inflammation and oxidative stress, as discussed in Section 3.1. These processes collectively exacerbate renal inflammatory responses and drive the development of fibrosis. On one hand, TMAO activates innate immune signaling pathways and downstream pro-inflammatory mediators, thereby amplifying inflammatory signaling; on the other hand, it upregulates NOX and inhibits superoxide dismutase (SOD), leading to elevated oxidative stress levels, which further promote the onset of renal fibrosis [46,49,52,57,58]. Beyond these common mechanisms, TMAO also directly drives renal pathology through various specific pathways. For instance, it activates signaling pathways such as PERK/Akt/mTOR and PI3K/AKT, promoting renal fibroblast proliferation, collagen synthesis, and fibrogenesis [48,73]. Concurrently, TMAO can induce endoplasmic reticulum stress and macrophage M1 polarization, indirectly reinforcing a fibrotic microenvironment [46,53]. Collectively, these mechanisms deepen our understanding of the pathological role of TMAO in CKD and provide a theoretical basis for targeted therapeutic strategies.

The role and mechanistic insights of TMAO in skeletal disorders: Current research advances

Skeletal disorders are highly prevalent among patients with CKD, manifesting predominantly as an increased risk of osteoporosis and bone fractures. Osteoporosis is a metabolic bone disorder characterized by reduced bone mineral density (BMD) and the deterioration of bone microarchitecture [74]. Central to its pathogenesis is the disruption of the dynamic balance in bone remodeling, whereby bone resorption (mediated by osteoclasts) outpaces bone formation (primarily driven by osteoblasts) [75]. This imbalance leads to decreased bone mass and compromised bone quality, substantially elevating the risk of fragility fractures [76]. In recent years, increasing attention has been directed toward the gut microbiota and its metabolites as critical regulators of bone health [77–79]. Among these microbial-derived compounds, TMAO has emerged as a molecule of interest in the context of skeletal disorders.

Clinically, elevated serum TMAO levels have been observed in patients with skeletal disorders, with several studies demonstrating a significant inverse correlation between TMAO concentrations and femoral neck BMD [54]. This phenomenon is particularly pronounced in high-risk fracture populations. Postmenopausal women with hip fractures exhibit elevated TMAO levels. Specifically, their TMAO concentrations correlate negatively with bone mineral density and positively with inflammatory markers (such as hs-CRP) and serum calcium levels. Further logistic regression analysis confirms elevated TMAO levels are independent predictors of hip fractures [80]. However, the complexity of this association is reflected in contradictory evidence observed in specific populations. A randomized controlled trial conducted in overweight or obese individuals (n = 264) revealed that a reduction in TMAO levels was associated with a decrease in both spinal and total BMD, and these individuals also exhibited a higher risk of type 2 diabetes [81]. It is worth noting that this seemingly paradoxical finding may be attributed to the complex interplay between TMAO, metabolic disorders, and bone metabolism. For instance, individuals with type 2 diabetes often exhibit higher BMD but also increased fracture risk due to impaired bone quality and microarchitecture, which may not be fully captured by BMD measurements alone [82,83]. Therefore, the relationship between TMAO and bone health may be context-dependent, influenced by factors such as metabolic status, inflammatory burden, and body composition. Future studies should incorporate more comprehensive assessments of bone quality and fracture risk to clarify the role of TMAO in different subpopulations. A longitudinal study of community-dwelling elderly individuals found that, while no significant association between TMAO levels and hip fracture risk or total hip BMD was observed in the overall population, high TMAO levels still significantly increased the risk of hip fractures in the overweight and obese subgroups. This observation may be attributed to the oxidative stress and inflammatory status induced by obesity, which could make individuals more susceptible to the effects of pro-inflammatory compounds such as TMAO [84]. Currently, there is a lack of large-scale clinical studies directly examining the relationship between TMAO and bone metabolism markers—such as PTH, fibroblast growth factor 23 (FGF23), and alkaline phosphatase (ALP)—in patients with CKD. This gap warrants further investigation in future studies. Importantly, emerging interventional studies have begun to shed light on a potential causal relationship between TMAO and bone metabolism. In obese individuals, adherence to a vegetarian diet significantly reduced TMAO levels while concurrently improving BMD [85]. Similarly, in patients with coronary artery disease, probiotic supplementation reduced circulating TMAO levels and improved bone density [86]. These findings suggest that targeting the TMAO metabolic pathway may represent a novel therapeutic strategy for improving skeletal health.

The impact of TMAO on skeletal health has been extensively studied in animal models. TMAO primarily promotes bone loss through mechanisms of chronic inflammation and oxidative stress. On the one hand, TMAO enhances osteoclast differentiation and activity, thereby intensifying bone resorption; on the other hand, it induces oxidative damage and downregulates osteoprotective factors, collectively contributing to the degradation of trabecular bone architecture [50,51]. In addition to the aforementioned mechanisms, emerging evidence suggests that TMAO can influence skeletal health via multiple pathways, including gut microbiota modulation. In aged mice, dysbiosis of the gut microbiota leads to increased hepatic FMO3 expression, thereby elevating circulating TMAO levels and promoting bone loss. Similarly, in ovariectomized (OVX) mice and obesity models induced by high-fat diets, elevated serum TMAO levels were associated with marked bone loss. Experimental interventions have shown that modulation of the gut microbiota using gold nanospheres (GNS) can reduce TMAO levels and alleviate skeletal disorders symptoms in OVX mice [87]. Additionally, Zhan et al. reported that TMAO exposure in Sprague-Dawley (SD) rats leads to a reduction in trabecular number, an increase in trabecular spacing, and an elevated marrow space-to-trabecular ratio [88]. Furthermore, fecal microbiota transplantation (FMT) has proven effective in lowering elevated TMAO levels, promoting bone formation, and improving overall skeletal health [30]. Taken together, these findings indicate that TMAO also contributes to the pathogenesis of skeletal disorders through mechanisms involving the gut-bone axis.

At the cellular level, the effects of TMAO on osteoblasts and osteoclasts have been extensively studied. TMAO promotes bone loss by inducing chronic inflammation and oxidative stress. On one hand, it enhances the release of pro-inflammatory factors and disrupts redox balance, inhibiting osteoblast differentiation; on the other hand, it exacerbates the adipogenic differentiation tendency of bone marrow mesenchymal stem cells (BMSCs), thereby disrupting the homeostasis between bone formation and resorption [54]. In addition to these mechanisms, TMAO directly influences osteocyte function through various pathways. It inhibits the mineralization capacity and expression of osteogenic genes (e.g., OCN, Runx2, and ALP) in human primary BMSCs (hBMSCs), while exosome-like nanoparticles derived from Pueraria lobata-derived exosome-like nanovesicles (PELNs) can reduce TMAO levels and enhance autophagy, thus improving the differentiation and function of hBMSCs [88]. Furthermore, Wang et al. demonstrated that TMAO promotes osteoclast differentiation in a dose-dependent manner, as evidenced by an increase in TRAP-positive osteoclasts, enhanced F-actin ring formation, and expanded bone resorption areas, alongside upregulation of osteoclast-associated genes (such as Calcr, Traf6, Dcstamp, Acp5, c-Fos, and Nfatc1) [50]. Moreover, TMAO activates PERK-dependent endoplasmic reticulum stress and disrupts the mitochondrial unfolded protein response (UPRmt), thereby impairing osteoblast function in mouse calvarial MC3T3-E1 cells. Inhibition of PERK or activation of UPRmt effectively reverses this effect, restoring mitochondrial function and mineralization capacity [30]. These findings suggest that TMAO exerts a synergistic influence on osteocyte function through multiple pathways, and targeting these pathways may provide novel strategies for the treatment of skeletal disorders.

In conclusion, TMAO plays a pivotal role in the pathogenesis of skeletal disorders through various mechanisms (Table 1), including activation of the NF-κB signaling pathway, induction of oxidative and ER stress, suppression of osteoblast differentiation, and promotion of osteoclast activity. Despite some discrepancies in clinical findings, TMAO’s osteoporotic effects have been substantiated in specific populations, such as postmenopausal women and individuals with overweight or obesity. However, it is important to acknowledge that many of the studies are based on animal models or small-scale clinical observations, which limits their direct translational applicability. For instance, while animal studies provide mechanistic insights, they often do not fully recapitulate the complexity of human skeletal disorders, particularly in the context of CKD. Similarly, clinical studies are frequently limited by sample size, cross-sectional design, and potential confounding factors. Therefore, future large-scale, longitudinal human studies are warranted to validate these findings and assess the clinical utility of TMAO as a biomarker or therapeutic target for skeletal disorders in CKD patients.

The role and mechanisms of TMAO in vascular calcification: mechanisms and research advances

Vascular calcification refers to the abnormal deposition of calcium and phosphate minerals in the intima or media of the arteries [5], leading to arterial stiffening and reduced elasticity. In its early stages, this process may manifest as atherosclerosis. This phenomenon is highly prevalent in CKD and significantly increases the risk of cardiovascular events. In recent years, the role of TMAO in vascular calcification has garnered increasing attention.

Numerous studies have demonstrated a close association between TMAO levels and vascular calcification. For instance, research by He et al. found that serum TMAO levels in hemodialysis patients were significantly higher compared to healthy controls, with the serum TMAO levels in patients with high abdominal aortic calcification (AAC) scores being notably elevated relative to those with low AAC scores. Further logistic regression analysis revealed that elevated serum TMAO levels were an independent risk factor for the development of vascular calcification in hemodialysis patients [89]. Another study in CKD patients showed that those with aortic arch calcification had significantly higher plasma TMAO levels than those without calcification, and Spearman correlation analysis revealed a significant positive correlation between serum TMAO levels and aortic arch calcification scores, suggesting that TMAO may play a potential role in the pathogenesis of vascular calcification in CKD patients [31]. Additionally, a study on living donor kidney transplant (LD-KTx) recipients with renal failure found that even after adjusting for CKD-related risk factors, TMAO levels were independently correlated with coronary artery calcification (CAC) scores [55]. A study by Li et al. further supported this finding, involving 127 patients with ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction (STEMI). They discovered that patients with calcification had significantly higher plasma TMAO levels than those without calcification. TMAO concentrations were significantly positively correlated with vascular calcification burden (including maximum calcified arc, thickness, and length) [90]. Furthermore, a study involving participants without cardiovascular disease found that individuals with a CAC score >100 had significantly elevated plasma TMAO levels, and TMAO levels were positively correlated with the extent of calcification (R = 0.54; p = 0.017) [91]. Given that CAC represents a late-stage pathological hallmark of atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease (ASCVD), studies investigating the association between TMAO and ASCVD provide deeper mechanistic insights. A prospective cohort study involving 6,767 initially healthy U.S. adults demonstrated that elevated TMAO levels were associated with an increased risk of ASCVD events, dose-dependent over a median follow-up of 11.3 years, with the association remaining significant after multivariable adjustment [92]. Notably, this relationship was particularly pronounced in individuals with impaired renal function. Data from the Cardiovascular Health Study revealed that elevated TMAO levels conferred a 56% higher risk of incident ASCVD (HR = 1.56; P for trend = 0.007) exclusively in the subgroup with eGFR <60 mL/min/1.73 m2, whereas no significant association was observed among those with preserved renal function [93]. These findings underscore renal function as a critical modulatory factor in the pro-atherogenic effects of TMAO.

In experimental studies, the mechanistic role of TMAO has been further elucidated. In a high-phosphorus environment, TMAO exacerbated vascular calcification in CKD rats, whereas antibiotic intervention to reduce TMAO levels effectively attenuated the degree of calcification [31]. At the cellular level, TMAO has been shown to promote the calcification of VSMCs through the induction of chronic inflammation and oxidative stress [31,55].

The role of TMAO in vascular calcification has been preliminarily clarified (Table 2), especially in CKD patients, where significantly elevated TMAO levels substantially increase the risk of vascular calcification. However, current research predominantly focuses on clinical correlation analyses, and the precise molecular mechanisms through which TMAO promotes vascular calcification remain incompletely understood, warranting further investigation. Future studies should aim to uncover the precise signaling pathways through which TMAO regulates vascular calcification and identify potential intervention targets to provide new theoretical foundations and strategies for preventing and treating vascular calcification.

Integrated pathological network analysis of the ‘kidney-bone-vascular axis’ mediated by TMAO

The pathophysiology of CKD involves a complex interplay among renal dysfunction, skeletal disorders, and vascular calcification, collectively termed the ‘kidney-bone-vascular axis’. Impaired renal function disrupts mineral metabolism and exacerbates systemic inflammation and oxidative stress, which in turn promote bone loss and vascular pathology. Vascular calcification is increasingly recognized as a process closely linked to bone metabolism. Molecules such as osteocalcin, osteoprotegerin (OPG), and receptor activator of nuclear factor-κB ligand (RANKL) play dual roles in both tissues [94–97], suggesting a shared regulatory network between vascular and skeletal systems. For instance, osteocalcin not only regulates bone mineralization but also influences VSMC calcification; OPG, a decoy receptor for RANKL, modulates both osteoclastogenesis and vascular calcification; and the RANKL/RANK/OPG axis is pivotal in bone remodeling and also implicated in vascular pathology. This bidirectional crosstalk underscores the existence of a bone-vascular axis, as highlighted by Rodrigues et al. who noted: ‘Cells with both osteoblastic and osteoclastic potential have been described in vascular tissues’ [38].

Within this intricate network of bone-vascular crosstalk, a comprehensive synthesis of current evidence establishes TMAO as a central mediator linking gut-derived metabolites to renal, skeletal, and cardiovascular pathologies in CKD. Elevated circulating TMAO levels in CKD patients exacerbate skeletal disorders and vascular calcification primarily through promoting chronic inflammation and oxidative stress (Figure 1). Specifically, TMAO activates inflammatory signaling pathways and upregulates osteoclast differentiation factors such as NFATc1 and TRAP in bone tissue, thereby accelerating bone resorption [50]. Concurrently, in VSMCs, TMAO promotes transdifferentiation into osteoblast-like cells via inflammatory pathway activation [31]. This shared inflammatory mechanism synergistically amplifies both skeletal disorders and vascular calcification. Furthermore, TMAO enhances NOX activation, leading to increased oxidative stress [56]. Oxidative stress impairs osteoblast differentiation and disrupts mitochondrial unfolded protein response in bone, further hindering bone formation [54]. It also directly stimulates calcium phosphate deposition within the vascular wall, facilitating vascular calcification [55]. Notably, TMAO may further disrupt the bone-vascular axis by dysregulating key signaling molecules. For example, by activating NF-κB [50], TMAO may increase RANKL while suppressing OPG expression, thus facilitating osteoclastogenesis and contributing to vascular calcification—a mechanism suggestive of bone-vascular imbalance.

The pathological relationship of the kidney-bone-vascular axis. In CKD patients, the level of TMAO, a metabolite of the gut microbiota, is significantly elevated. TMAO may promote the pathological occurrence and progression of the kidney-bone-vascular axis through the synergistic mechanism of chronic inflammation and oxidative stress. Abbreviations: CKD, chronic kidney disease; TMA, trimethylamine; FMO3, flavin-containing monooxygenase 3; TMAO, trimethylamine-N-oxide.

The clinical significance of this pathological network lies in its elucidation of the bidirectional reinforcement mechanism between bone and vascular pathology: vascular calcification increases arterial stiffness, reduces bone blood flow, and accelerates skeletal disorders [98], while bone-derived hormones (such as osteocalcin) further promote the progression of vascular calcification [99]. Notably, conventional treatment targets for CKD-related skeletal disorder, such as vitamin D [100], have been shown to lower TMAO levels effectively [101]. This provides a novel explanation for the multi-organ protective effects of vitamin D and strongly suggests that TMAO serves as a ‘hidden mediator’ linking traditional calcium-phosphate dysregulation to bone-vascular comorbidities. Therefore, targeting TMAO holds the potential to overcome the current treatment bottleneck of addressing kidney-bone diseases and vascular calcification as separate entities, while offering a fresh perspective on the integrated regulation of the ‘multi-organ axis’. This presents a solid theoretical foundation and a promising intervention target for developing revolutionary strategies to improve bone and cardiovascular health in CKD patients.

However, it is important to acknowledge that while substantial evidence supports the role of TMAO in individual organ systems (kidney, bone, vasculature), direct experimental evidence demonstrating its simultaneous impact on all three systems within the same model is currently limited. Most existing studies are derived from separate clinical observations or single-organ experimental models, which may not fully capture the integrative pathophysiology of the kidney-bone-vascular axis. Therefore, future investigations employing multi-organ imaging, systemic biomarker profiling, and integrated animal models are urgently needed to validate the proposed network and elucidate the temporal and mechanistic priorities within this axis.

Targeting TMAO: potential therapeutic strategies

Elevated levels of TMAO are closely associated with the progression of CKD, decreased bone mineral density, and exacerbated vascular calcification. Targeting the regulation of TMAO presents a promising therapeutic approach for managing the complications of bone disorders and vascular calcification in CKD patients (Figure 2).

Dietary modifications

Dietary modulation to reduce circulating levels of TMAO represents a promising and mechanistically well-defined intervention strategy, primarily achieved through limiting the intake of TMAO precursors and regulating gut microbiota metabolism. Preclinical studies have demonstrated that increased dietary fiber intake can remodel gut microbial composition, inhibit microbial TMA production, and subsequently reduce systemic TMAO levels [102]. In addition, oolong tea has been shown to significantly attenuate plasma TMAO concentrations in carnitine-supplemented C57BL/6 mice by inhibiting hepatic FMO3 activity [103]. Clinical investigations have further confirmed that the major dietary precursors of TMAO—namely choline, phosphatidylcholine, and L-carnitine—are abundantly present in animal-derived foods such as red meat, seafood, dairy, and eggs. Reducing the intake of these foods has been associated with a marked decline in circulating TMAO concentrations [104–108]. Moreover, adherence to a strict vegan diet has been shown to effectively lower plasma TMAO levels in individuals with metabolic dysregulation and obesity [85]. Certain foods, such as garlic and pistachios, have also demonstrated potential in reducing TMAO levels [109,110] (Table 3), although robust clinical validation remains lacking and further human trials are warranted. While dietary interventions are generally regarded as safe, prolonged restriction of animal-based foods may increase the risk of nutrient deficiencies—particularly in vitamin B12, iron, and essential fatty acids. Additionally, interindividual variability in gut microbiota composition underscores the importance of personalized dietary strategies to ensure both efficacy and safety.

Taken together, a balanced and health-conscious dietary pattern remains the most cost-effective and sustainable approach to lowering TMAO levels. Importantly, individual differences in gut microbial ecology may lead to heterogeneous responses to dietary interventions, highlighting the need for future research to develop tailored, microbiome-informed nutritional strategies.

Probiotic and prebiotic interventions

Probiotics and prebiotics are widely regarded as natural and safe components that can modulate gut microbiota to reduce TMAO levels, primarily through enhancing beneficial bacterial growth, inhibiting TMA generation, or direct enzymatic degradation of TMA. Their efficacy and safety have been evaluated in both preclinical models and clinical trials.

Animal studies have provided further mechanistic insight and expanded the list of potential probiotic and prebiotic candidates. For instance, Lactobacillus casei, inulin prebiotics, Lactobacillus reuteri CCFM8631, Bifidobacterium breve, and Bifidobacterium longum were all shown to significantly reduce TMAO levels in rodents fed high-fat or high-choline diets by modulating gut microbial composition [111–113]. Moreover, Bifidobacterium animalis subsp. Lactis F1-3-2 was found to colonize the cecum and directly degrade TMA, thereby attenuating TMAO-induced atherosclerosis in C57BL/6 mice [114]. A consortium consisting of Lactobacillus amylovorus LAM1345, Lactobacillus plantarum LP1145, and Lactobacillus fermentum LP33 also significantly reduced plasma TMAO in choline-fed mice through gut microbiota restructuring [115]. Additionally, Methanogenic archaea were reported to lower plasma TMAO in both C57BL/6J and Apoe−/− mice [116]. Notably, Lactobacillus plantarum ATCC 8014 was shown to inhibit TMA secretion and reduce hepatic TMAO synthesis, thereby alleviating cognitive decline and neuropathology in a mouse model of Alzheimer’s disease [117]. Several human studies have demonstrated the beneficial effects of specific probiotic strains in reducing TMAO levels. For example, supplementation with Lactobacillus rhamnosus GG significantly decreased serum TMAO levels in patients with myocardial infarction [118]. Similarly, Bifidobacterium lactis Probio-M8, as an adjunct to conventional treatment, notably reduced serum TMAO in individuals with coronary artery disease [86]. Importantly, a 4–24 week probiotic intervention in patients with CKD not only significantly improved renal function biomarkers but also reported no adverse events, supporting the clinical safety of microbiota-targeted TMAO-lowering strategies for slowing CKD progression [119] (Table 4).

TMA lyase inhibitors

CutC/D enzymes are key bacterial enzymes responsible for the conversion of choline to TMA, making them important therapeutic targets for reducing TMAO levels. Current research in this area remains predominantly preclinical. Wang et al. were the first to identify the TMA lyase inhibitor DMB, which significantly reduces TMAO levels and alleviates atherosclerosis in mice fed high-choline or L-carnitine diets by inhibiting CutC/D activity in microorganisms [120]. Subsequently, DMB has been widely applied in animal studies, demonstrating TMAO-lowering effects in models such as pulmonary arterial hypertension in rats [121] and heart failure in mice [122]. However, the inhibitory potency and specificity of DMB still require improvement. On this basis, Hazen’s team developed two more efficient CutC/D inhibitors: FMC and IMC. These inhibitors significantly lower plasma TMAO levels in C57BL/6J mice and permanently inactivate CutC/D, achieving long-lasting suppression without affecting the vitality of gut symbiotic bacteria [123]. Among them, IMC demonstrated superior efficacy over DMB, significantly reducing TMAO levels and mitigating renal injury in CKD mouse models [124]. Furthermore, Tain et al. found that after treatment with IMC in maternal CKD rats, alterations in the gut microbiota and a reduction in TMAO levels were observed, alleviating hypertension and renal hypertrophy in their offspring [125] (Table 5).

Potential safety concerns—including off-target effects, long-term impacts on microbial ecology, and metabolic consequences of chronic enzyme inhibition—remain largely unexplored. Although these inhibitors show promise as research tools, their translational applicability requires rigorous evaluation in human trials to establish safety and biological activity in patients.

FMO3 inhibitors

Inhibiting hepatic FMO3 activity represents a promising strategy to reduce circulating TMAO levels, as FMO3 is the key enzyme catalyzing the oxidation of TMA to TMAO. Several natural compounds have demonstrated FMO3 inhibitory effects in preclinical studies. Ginkgolide B (GB), an extract from Ginkgo biloba leaves, was shown by Lv et al. to significantly suppress both the expression and activity of FMO3, resulting in reduced TMAO levels [126]. Similarly, guggulsterone (GS), a farnesoid X receptor (FXR) antagonist, downregulated hepatic FMO3 expression and reduced TMAO production, exhibiting protective effects against atherosclerosis in animal models [127]. Additionally, 3,3′-diindolylmethane (DIM), a phytochemical derived from cruciferous vegetables, was reported by Chen et al. to inhibit FMO3 activity, thereby lowering TMAO levels and improving metabolic dysfunction [128]. To date, indole-3-carbinol (I3C), another compound derived from cruciferous vegetables, is among the few FMO3 inhibitors that have been evaluated in human studies. It has been shown to significantly inhibit FMO3 activity and reduce the TMAO/TMA ratio in clinical settings [129] (Table 6). Although these inhibitors show efficacy in reducing TMAO, the long-term safety of systemic FMO3 inhibition remains uncertain. Potential risks—including altered drug metabolism, nutrient interactions, and unforeseen off-target effects—warrant careful evaluation. While I3C has been investigated in humans, most FMO3 inhibitors currently lack clinical safety data. Further translational and clinical studies are essential to establish both safety and efficacy for chronic use in human populations.

Pharmacological and natural interventions

Pharmaceutical drugs, natural extracts, and other interventions have demonstrated significant potential in reducing TMAO levels through diverse mechanisms (Table 7). Melatonin significantly reduced plasma TMAO in a CKD rat model [130], while linagliptin decreased TMAO levels in an adenine-induced renal insufficiency model in C57BL/6 mice [131]. Other drugs such as ranitidine, finasteride, and metformin also reduced TMAO in various disease models [132,133]. Several drugs have shown TMAO-lowering effects in human studies. Statins directly reduce plasma TMAO in patients with cardiovascular diseases or diabetes, partly through modulation of gut microbiota [134,135]. Micarbazine lowers plasma TMAO concentrations in humans by enhancing its urinary excretion [136]. Additionally, a combination of broad-spectrum antibiotics (metronidazole and ciprofloxacin) inhibits the rise in TMAO following dietary choline or carnitine intake in clinical settings [137].

Pharmacological and natural interventions to reduce TMAO.

Multiple natural extracts have shown efficacy in preclinical studies. These include pterostilbene, wild Lonicera caerulea berry polyphenol extract, baicalin, berberine, fructus Ligustri lucidi, Lycium barbarum polysaccharide, Ganoderma lucidum spore extract, purple sweet potato anthocyanins (which also inhibited myocardial TMAO accumulation), gypenoside, resveratrol, quercetin, astaxanthin, and Curcuma aromatica oil [51,138–149]. Jiaogulan saponins reduced plasma but not brain TMAO, suggesting tissue-specific effects [145]. Non-pharmacological approaches such as medicinal charcoal improved cardiac function and reduced TMAO in CKD mice on a high-choline diet [150], and vitamin D supplementation lowered plasma TMAO in C57BL/6J mice [101].

While clinical agents such as statins and antibiotics have established safety profiles, their use specifically for TMAO reduction requires further long-term assessment. Natural extracts and novel compounds, though promising in animal studies, generally lack clinical safety data. Potential risks include off-target effects, microbial resistance (in the case of antibiotics), and altered nutrient or drug metabolism. Translational studies are needed to evaluate the efficacy and safety of these interventions in humans.

Conclusion and future perspective

This study transcends the traditional research limitations focused solely on individual complications, proposing an innovative hypothesis that TMAO may simultaneously drive bone disease and vascular calcification in CKD through shared pathophysiological mechanisms, including chronic inflammation, oxidative stress, and dysregulated calcium-phosphate metabolism. While preliminary investigations have explored the interactions between TMAO, CKD, skeletal health, and vascular calcification, most existing studies are confined to analyzing the associations among two or three factors, lacking a comprehensive and systematic analysis of all relevant variables. This fragmented research landscape hinders a deeper understanding of the complex pathophysiological mechanisms underlying multiple complications in CKD and impedes the development of more effective integrated therapeutic strategies.

In light of these limitations, this paper adopts a holistic and integrative research perspective to systematically investigate the mechanistic role of TMAO in CKD-related bone disease and vascular calcification. This approach provides a novel direction for future fundamental research. It lays a solid theoretical foundation for developing precision therapeutic strategies targeting multiple complications in CKD in clinical practice, thus advancing research in this field toward a more in-depth and comprehensive understanding. Moreover, early warning of CKD-associated bone disease and vascular calcification is critical; however, single biomarkers often fall short of meeting the complex predictive requirements. TMAO’s clinical detection methods are recognized biomarkers for chronic diseases, offering rapid and accurate results [151–153]. Therefore, TMAO holds potential as a novel auxiliary biomarker, which, when used in conjunction with other biomarkers, could improve the accuracy of predicting CKD-related bone disease and vascular calcification.

Furthermore, targeted interventions that reduce TMAO levels—such as dietary modifications, probiotics and prebiotics, FMO3 inhibitors, and pharmaceutical treatments—offer new therapeutic avenues for CKD patients, effectively improving bone and cardiovascular health. However, challenges remain in the clinical application of these strategies. For instance, in certain patients with FMO3 gene mutations, TMA cannot be metabolized to TMAO, leading to the accumulation of TMA in the body, which is excreted through urine, sweat, and breath, causing a characteristic fishy odor and significantly impairing quality of life [154]. Additionally, while some drugs (e.g., antibiotics) may lower TMAO levels, they may promote bacterial resistance and suppress beneficial gut microbiota [155]. While prebiotics and probiotics may positively influence gut microbiota composition, their therapeutic efficacy remains highly individualized, largely due to the complex interplay of multiple factors that shape the gut microbial ecosystem [156]. Moreover, a major bottleneck in translating these strategies—including probiotics/prebiotics, TMA lyase inhibitors, and FMO3 inhibitors—into clinical applications for CKD is the lack of long-term, large-scale, prospective cohort studies specifically targeting the CKD population. Such studies are urgently needed to rigorously evaluate the effectiveness of these interventions in reducing TMAO levels, as well as their long-term safety profiles, including potential impacts on gut microbial ecology, nutritional status, drug–drug interactions, and organ toxicity. Critically, these studies should also assess their influence on clinically meaningful endpoints, such as fracture incidence, progression of vascular calcification, cardiovascular events, and all-cause mortality. These efforts are essential for advancing TMAO-targeted therapies toward clinical implementation.

TMAO plays a pivotal role in the progression of CKD and the development of skeletal disorders and vascular calcification. A variety of TMAO-targeted intervention strategies have demonstrated promising effects, offering novel therapeutic avenues for managing bone disorders and vascular complications in CKD patients. However, it is essential to recognize that most current evidence derives from preclinical or small-scale clinical studies, which may not directly translate to broader CKD populations. Therefore, future research should prioritize well-designed, large-scale prospective cohorts and randomized controlled trials to establish causal relationships and evaluate the efficacy of TMAO-lowering interventions on hard clinical endpoints such as fracture rates and cardiovascular events. Beyond these clinical studies, further mechanistic investigation is imperative to unravel the complex roles of TMAO in CKD and its related comorbidities. This requires moving beyond correlative evidence by employing rigorously designed interventional studies that dissect the causal molecular mechanisms underlying the kidney–bone–vascular axis. Such efforts should include the application of gene-editing technologies in cellular and animal models to selectively modulate key downstream signaling pathways of TMAO, such as NF-κB, NLRP3, PERK/Akt/mTOR, and ROS-associated cascades. This approach would allow for systematic elucidation of TMAO’s direct regulatory effects on bone metabolic imbalance (i.e., osteoblast/osteoclast dysfunction) and the osteogenic transdifferentiation and calcification of vascular smooth muscle cells. Building on these mechanistic insights, prospective interventional clinical studies—such as those involving pharmacological inhibitors of TMAO synthesis or probiotic/prebiotic-based interventions—should be designed not only to monitor dynamic changes in circulating TMAO levels, but more critically, to assess their cross-organ protective effects on bone mineral density and microarchitecture, vascular calcification progression, and related biomarkers. Such a paradigm shift—from mechanistic exploration to therapeutic validation—represents a crucial step toward transitioning from associative findings to causal inference and precision intervention. It will also lay a solid scientific foundation for developing multi-organ protective strategies targeting TMAO. In summary, while TMAO-targeted therapies hold significant promise for managing CKD-related bone disease and vascular calcification, their clinical translation remains a long-term endeavor requiring sustained research.
