Prevalence, genotype diversity, and zoonotic potential of bovine rotavirus A in Amhara National Regional State, Ethiopia: A multicenter cross-sectional study
Authors: Debasu Damtie, Aschalew Gelaw, Yitayih Wondimeneh, Yetemwork Aleka, Zewdu Siyoum Tarekegn, Phillip Davis, Belay Tessema, Anastasia N Vlasova
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Highlights•RVA prevalence among diarrheic calves was higher (15.4 %) than suggested previously.•Circulating RVA strains were dominated by G6, G10, P[1], P[11], and P[14] genotypes.•Uncommon human-like P[4] and P[8] genotypes were detected in calves.•The common porcine G8P[13] genotype was detected in cattle for the first time.•High sequence identity between bovine and human RVAs suggests (reverse) zoonosis.Keywords: Rotavirus A, Bovine, Prevalence, Genotype, Zoonosis, Diarrhea

Background

Rotaviruses (RVs) are associated with gastroenteritis in animals, and can infect a broad range of animal species including dogs, cats, cattle, horses, and birds (Estes and Kapikian, 2007). Rotavirus A (RVA) is one of the viral causes of acute gastroenteritis affecting the young of many animal species. It represents the main cause of neonatal diarrhea in calves globally (Garaicoechea et al., 2006, Garaicoechea et al., 2011). A systematic review study involving published survey articles in 24 countries from five continents found that RVA was detected in 33.7 % of the bovine samples tested, signifying worldwide distribution of the infection in cattle population (Papp et al., 2013). A study conducted in 1992 in Ethiopia reported that 16.7 % of neonatal calf diarrhea was attributable to RVA (Abraham et al., 1992). Neonatal calf diarrhea represents an important sanitary problem in livestock production. It is a complex syndrome affecting calves within the first weeks of life, causing important economic losses related to death, treatment costs and reduction in weight gain of affected animals (Badaracco et al., 2014, Adah et al., 2001, Esona et al., 2009).

Out of the different species of RVs described in bovines, RVA accounts for most infections in cattle. Studies reported a prevalence of 6.2 % and 13.7 % RVA infection among diarrheic calves in Bangladesh and Nigeria, respectively (Hossain et al., 2020, Aliyu et al., 2018). Genotyping analysis study of RVA in Australia reported that G6 and G10 G-types and P[5] and P[11] P-types as a dominantly circulating RVA genotypes in calves (Swiatek et al., 2010). Comparable finding has been reported in Bangladesh where among 17 sequenced bovine RVA strains, G8 (17.9 %) was reported to be the most prevalent G-genotype followed by G10 (8 %) and G6 (1.6 %). This same Bangladesh study reported P[1] (11.3 %) as the most frequently detected P-genotype followed by P[11] (3.2 %) and P[15] (1.6 %) (Hossain et al., 2020). Likewise, a whole genome sequence analyses of three African bovine RVAs in South Africa were reported to be G8P[1] and G6P[5] (Jere et al., 2012). Assessment of the spatial and temporal distribution of dominantly circulating RVA genotypes is important in the prevention and control of RVA induced acute gastroenteritis and subsequent death among children and calves (Badaracco et al., 2014, Khan and Khan, 1991, Tate et al., 2016, Rondelli et al., 2018).

Certain animal RVA strains had antigenic similarities to some human strains (Albert et al., 1987), that increased speculation about the role of animals as a source of RVA in humans. Based on the identification of unusual RVA types, with properties of strains more commonly found in animals, which were isolated from various cases of human infection, it is believed that RVA can be transmitted zoonotically (Adah et al., 2001, Ramachandran et al., 1996, Armah et al., 2001, Holmes et al., 1999). These unusual human RVA types may have arisen either as zoonotic transfer of whole virions in some rare events where a novel G5P[7] strain was identified in a Cameroonian child (Esona et al., 2009) or as genetic reassortants between human and animal strains during coinfection of a single cell (Doan et al., 2013, Ruggeri et al., 2011). Genetically, RVAs are diverse, and different gene segments are peculiarly distributed across various animal species, suggesting existing host species barriers and host range restriction. However, a number of gene segments, mostly those encoding the neutralizing antigens (defining G and P types), have been identified repeatedly in humans in different parts of the world during surveillance studies (Gentsch et al., 2005, Santos and Hoshino, 2005).

Bovine RVAs have also contributed to the generation of novel human strains on several occasions. Strains with bovine-like VP7 and VP4 specificities such as G6, G8 and G10 in conjunction with a variety of P types, P[1], P[2], P[4], P[6], P[8], P[9], P[11] and P[14] have been detected worldwide (Santos and Hoshino, 2005). The unusual African G8P[6] and G8P[8] RVA strains may have arisen through several reassortment events involving human G2P[4] strains, DS-1-like, and strains carrying the G8, P[6] and P[8] genotypes (Matthijnssens et al., 2006). The bovine-like strains, G10P[11], are regarded as common human enteric pathogen in the Indian subcontinent (Ward et al., 2008). In our previous systematic review and meta-analysis study, we have demonstrated that unusual RVA strains of genotypes G8 and G12 are circulating among children in Ethiopia (Damtie et al., 2020). Knowledge on RVA burden, prevalent circulating RVA genotypes, and zoonotic transmission potential of bovine RVA is very limited in Ethiopia in general and it is even scarcer in the study area. Thus, this study aimed at assessing the magnitude, genotype diversity, and zoonotic transmission potential of the circulating RVA strains in cattle population/calves in Amhara National Regional State, Ethiopia.

Materials and methods

Study design and settings

This study had a multi-center farm-based cross-sectional design involving 94 dairy farms in and around Bahir Dar, Gondar, and Debre Markos cities of the Amhara National Regional State, Ethiopia (Fig. 1). A total of 266 fecal samples were collected from calves between January 2022 and December 2022.

Study site specific RVA infection prevalence among calves in Amhara National Regional State, Ethiopia, 2022.

Sample collection, transport, and storage

Approximately 4 g of fecal samples were collected by hand from the rectum of each diarrheic calf aged ≤6 months using sterile gloves. The samples were immediately stored at –20 °C at the participating veterinary clinics. They were then transported to the Molecular Biology Laboratory at the School of Biomedical and Laboratory Sciences, University of Gondar in a cold box. Upon arrival, the samples were aliquoted into two cryovials and stored at –80 °C until testing.

RNA extraction, RVA detection and genotyping

RNA isolation and qRT-PCR screening were done as previously (Damtie et al., 2024). RVA positive RNA samples were preserved by drying them using GENTegra RNA tubes (Gentegra LLC, Pleasanton, California, United States) and shipped to The Ohio State University, USA for RVA genotyping. Briefly, according to the RNArchive protocol, 40 µl of RNA was added to individual GENTegra tubes that were incubated for 5 min at room temperature (21–25 °C) which was followed by solubilizing and mixing the RNA samples with the RNArchive matrix. Then, the RNA was air-dried in a biosafety cabinet for 24 h. At the OSU, the dried RNA samples were reconstituted with 40 µl of molecular biology grade water to recover the RNA and tested by qRT-PCR to ensure RVA RNA stability and integrity. Most RNA samples remained positive with acceptable Ct values (≤ 27).

To determine RVA genotypes, one-step conventional RT-PCR was performed using genotyping primers targeting the outer capsid VP7 and VP4 genes as described previously (Gelaw et al., 2018, Gouvea et al., 1990, Gault et al., 1999, Gentsch et al., 1992). One primer pair for each VP7 and VP4 genes were used that were adopted from previous studies. These primers yielded some partial sequence data for approximately 2/3 of the RVA-positive samples. Additionally, we designed two additional primer pairs (for VP7 and VP4) based on the partial sequence data in order to obtain sequence data for the samples that we failed to genotype using the first primer pairs (Table 1). SuperScript IV One-Step RT-PCR kit (Invitorgen, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) was used to amplify the target genes. To generate sufficient amounts of the target gene amplicons (≤75 µl), 37.5 µl of 2x Platinum™ SuperFi™ RT-PCR Master Mix, 4.5 µl of the forward and reverse primers each, 0.75 µl of SuperScript™ IV RT Mix, 20.25 µl of nuclease-free water, and 6 µl of template RNA (pre-treated with 1.5 µl of DMSO) were used. Reaction conditions were as follows: 50 °C for 10 min for reverse transcription, 98 °C for 2 min of initial denaturation followed by 40 cycles of denaturation (98 °C) for 10 s, annealing (48 °C for VP7 and 50 °C for VP4) for 10 s and extension (72 °C) for 30 s.

Genotype assignment and phylogenetic analysis

First, the VP7 and VP4 sequences were analyzed by Basic Local Alignment Search Tool for Nucleotides (BLASTn), the National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI) database. This was followed by the analysis using a web-based Rotavirus A Genotyping Tool Version 0.1 (available at: https://www.rivm.nl/mpf/typingtool/rotavirusa/job/462661131/ (accessed on May 30th, 2024)) to confirm the G- and P-types of the RVA VP7 and VP4 genes, respectively. Multiple alignments of our and reference (from the GenBank) sequence data were performed using CLUSTAL Omega multiple sequence alignment (MSA) tool integrated into Molecular Evolutionary Genetics Analysis (MEGA) version 11 software. Phylogenetic trees were constructed using maximum likelihood method and generalized time reversal model validated by 1000 replicates bootstraps as previously described (Tamura et al., 2011).

Data entry and analysis

The animal husbandry practice, clinical and laboratory data were analyzed using SPSS version 29 statistical software. The frequency and cross-tabulation were done to summarize descriptive data. Pearson's chi-squared test was used to determine association between specific outcomes and explanatory variables. P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Animal husbandry practice and clinical characteristics

A total of 266 calves, originating from 94 dairy farms (Gondar 70, Bahir Dar 8, and Debre Markos 16) in the three major cities of the Amhara National Regional State (Bahir Dar, Gondar, and Debre Markos) were included. The mean herd size per farm was 18.77 cattle ranging from 2–90 animals. The mean age of the calves was 3.26 ± 1.7 months. All calves had diarrhea, and the mean diarrhea duration was 2.6 ± 2.1 days (Table 2).

Bovine RVA genotype diversity

A total of 30 RVA isolates were genotyped successfully for G and P-types. Three G-types (G6, G8, and G10) (Fig. 3A) and six P-types (P[1] P[4], P[8], P[11], P[13] and P[14]) (Fig. 3B) were circulating in calves in Ethiopia. The G10 (41 %) and P[11] (38 %) genotypes were the most dominant G and P-types, respectively.

Phylogenetic analysis of RVA

The phylogenetic analysis of the circulating G-types revealed close relatedness to bovine and human strains previously reported globally. The circulating G6 strains were found to be closely related (92.74–100 % nt identity) and cluster with the G6 strain isolated from Roe deer in Slovenia and human strains reported from Italy, Ghana, South Africa, and Belgium with nt identity >89 % (Fig. 5A and Table 5).

Discussion

RVA is the most common cause of diarrhea in calves resulting in morbidity and mortality in calves. In this study, the prevalence of RVA among calves was 41/266 (15.4 %, 95 % CI = 11.3 %–19.5 %). This figure is much higher than in previous reports from central Ethiopia (1.41 % and 3.34 %) (Garoma, 2023, Debelo et al., 2021), Turkey (8.92 %) and Brazil (9.90 %) (Caruzo et al., 2010, Yilmaz, 2016). Consistent with our findings, studies among diarrheic calves in India reported 13.11 % and 15.68 % prevalences (Makwana et al., 2020a, Rai et al., 2011). On the other hand, the prevalence we report here is lower than study findings reported from Bangladesh (22.5 %) (Uddin Ahmed et al., 2022). The variability in prevalence might be related to spatio-temporal differences (Louge Uriarte et al., 2023), availability of interventions such as bovine RVA vaccination or methodological differences among the different studies. The herd-level RVA prevalence was 24/94 (25.53 %). Implicating that a quarter of the farms had at least one RVA positive calf with the potential of infecting vulnerable groups of cattle in the same farm or with a potential of zoonotic spread depending on the circulating RVA strain in the farm. In this study, the prevalence of RVA was higher among younger (0–8 weeks) age calves 20.4 % compared to older age (>8 weeks) calves 12 %. This observation might be associated with immature immune system and developing gastrointestinal tissue in young calves that makes them more susceptible to RVA infection compared to their older counterparts (Chase et al., 2008, Cho and Yoon, 2014). This finding is supported by a study in Bangladesh which reported that young calves were more affected by RVA compared to their older counter parts (Uddin Ahmed et al., 2022).

In this study, the circulating G-types were predominantly G10, followed by G6, and G8. Similar studies in Japan and Bangladesh reported G10, G6 and G8 as the major genotypes circulating in calves (Uddin Ahmed et al., 2022, Hasan et al., 2022). Diverse P-genotypes such as P[1], P[8], P[4], P[11], P[13] and P[14] were identified. The P[11] genotype was found to be the dominant circulating strain. Similarly, P[11] was reported as a dominant strain genotype in India (Malik et al., 2012) and Bangladesh (Uddin Ahmed et al., 2022). Human-like bovine P[4] and P[8] genotypes were detected in the cattle population. These P[4] and P[8] genotypes were detected among children in the same study area and time period as we reported recently (Damtie et al., 2024). This may pose a public health threat as these circulating strains may have cross-species transmission potential to cause disease in humans. A similar finding of the uncommon G8P[8] and G8P[4] genotypes were reported from Brazil in 2002 (Gómez et al., 2010). Alternatively, it may be suggestive of ongoing reverse zoonotic spillover events. Of importance, our study is the first one to identify the common porcine P[13] genotype (Chandler-Bostock et al., 2014, Naseer et al., 2017, Wu et al., 2017) in cattle. Interestingly, a closely related G9P[13] strain has been reported to co-circulate in humans and pigs in Taiwan (Wu et al., 2017), suggesting this genotype can be associated with an increased risk of or result from interspecies transmission.

G10P[11] (37.5 %) was the dominant G/P combination followed by G6P[14] (18.8 %) and G6P[1] (12.5 %) circulating among calves. Likewise G10P[11] was reported as the most dominant RVA genotype circulating in calves in Bangladesh (Uddin Ahmed et al., 2022). Moreover, G10P[11] genotype has been repeatedly reported as a major cause of severe gastroenteritis in neonates and infants in India (Iturriza Gómara et al., 2004, Babji et al., 2021). These observations underscore the zoonotic importance of the circulating bovine RVA strains in Ethiopia.

The phylogenetic analysis of the G-genotypes revealed that the circulating G6 strains cluster together with the G6 strain isolated from Roe deer in Slovenia and wild type human strains reported from Italy, Ghana, and Belgium. The G8 strains cluster with a wild type Indian bovine G8 strain, India and Bangladesh caprine G8 strains, and human strains from Taiwan, Vietnam, Czech Republic, Thailand, Singapore and USA. The circulating G10 strains were closely related to bovine G10 strains from China, Turkey, Brazil, and Iran and human strains from Vietnam and Ghana. These observations underscore the high genetic diversity and the associated zoonotic transmission potential of the circulating bovine RVA strains in Ethiopia. The multiple circulating P-genotypes, P[1], P[4], P[8], P[11], P[13], and P[14], have demonstrated high nucleotide identity and clustering together with wild type human and bovine RVA strains. These observations emphasize their potential for interspecies and zoonotic transmission. Alternatively, these strains could have emerged into the bovine population due to reverse zoonosis. These findings are consistent with previous studies reporting the emergence of novel RVA strains which usually originate from animals including bovines (Gentsch et al., 2005, Matthijnssens et al., 2006, Tatsi et al., 2023).

Conclusions and recommendations

The prevalence of RVA among calves was significant. Diverse RVA genotypes were circulating in the cattle population with very high sequence identity to previously isolated bovine and human RVA strains reported globally. Hence, the circulating bovine RVA strains can possess significant zoonotic potential and or result from reverse zoonotic spillover. Therefore, continuous surveillance of the circulating RVA strains is crucial to curb RVA associated morbidity and mortality in human and cattle populations through integrated one health approaches and strategies.
