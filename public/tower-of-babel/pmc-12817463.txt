Effectiveness of antimicrobial photodynamic therapy in endodontic infection: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Upma Das, Jamal Giri, Ashok Ayer
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe key element for a successful root canal treatment is proper cleaning and debridement of the canal. In recent years, antimicrobial photodynamic therapy (aPDT) has been used as an adjunct to conventional root canal treatment to reduce postoperative pain and microbial load. This systematic review investigates the effectiveness of aPDT in the reduction of postoperative pain and microbial load during root canal treatment in endodontic infection.MethodsFive electronic databases: Medline, EMBASE, Scopus, Web of Science and Cochrane Library were searched for randomized controlled trials using aPDT as a supplement during conventional root canal treatment. Two authors independently performed study selection, data extraction and risk of bias assessment in duplicate and a third author resolved conflicts. The Risk of Bias (ROB2) tool was used to assess the quality of the studies included in the systematic review, and the certainty of evidence was evaluated using the Grading of Recommendations, Assessment, Development and Evaluation (GRADE) criteria.ResultsEleven studies were included in the systematic review, of which five studies reporting postoperative pain after the use of aPDT during endodontic treatment were subjected to meta-analysis. Random-effects meta-analyses found that aPDT significantly reduced postoperative pain measured using visual analogue scale at both 24 h (Standardized mean difference: -1.21) and 72 h (Standardized mean difference: -0.63). While meta-analysis could not be conducted on the remaining six studies due to heterogeneity, the findings suggested a reduction in microbial load. Only three of the eleven studies had a low risk of bias.ConclusionIt can be concluded that aPDT, as an adjunct to chemo-mechanical irrigation, is effective in decreasing postoperative pain and reducing microbial load in endodontic infection.Trial registrationPROSPERO (CRD42024567223).Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12903-025-07501-1.

Introduction

The overall aim of a root canal treatment is to eliminate the endodontic infection within the root canal system. In endodontic infection, microorganisms play a crucial role in forming the pulpal inflammation, leading to subsequent pulpal necrosis and periapical lesion. The entire removal or a notable reduction of the microbial load during root canal treatment is a major factor in determining the final prognosis of root canal therapy [1]. The critical component of root canal therapy is instrumentation paired with an effective chemico-mechanical debridement technique to ensure rigorous disinfection.

Due to the complex root canal anatomy, such as accessory canals and anastomoses, the thorough cleaning of the root canal is difficult. To enhance the disinfection process, apical foramen enlargement and greater concentration of sodium hypochlorite solutions have been used. However, these procedures may lead to over-instrumentation and extrusion of the irrigants, depending on operator’s skill and technique, which can cause periapical tissue irritation and may induce a biological inflammatory response [2]. Various hydraulic, laser, sonic, ultrasonic and ozone therapy techniques have been developed to obtain better disinfection of the infected root canal to improve the endodontic treatment outcome [3].

In recent years, antimicrobial photodynamic therapy (aPDT) has emerged as an aid to conventional root canal treatment for managing endodontic infections. It uses a photosensitizing agent, which is activated by a specific wavelength of light. The activation produces reactive oxygen species that effectively kill bacteria and disrupt biofilm within the root canal [4]. The commonly used photosensitizers in treating endodontic infections are hematoporpyrin derivatives, toluidine blue O, methylene blue, cyanine, phtahlocyanine and phototherapeutic agents. Its effectiveness differs as per the dye, in addition to the irradiation parameter used with laser or other light sources [5, 6]. It has been reported to have anti-inflammatory, analgesic and antimicrobial effects [7].

Several studies have shown that aPDT is remarkably efficient when combined with conventional endodontic therapy in relieving postoperative pain, minimizing microbial activity, and promoting periapical healing in cases of endodontic infections [8–12]. However, some studies have found no significant improvement in pain alleviation or antimicrobial effects when using aPDT as an adjunct with conventional root canal treatment compared to chemo-mechanical preparation alone [2, 13]. Existing systematic reviews on aPDT for endodontic infections report inconsistent conclusions, likely because they combine in-vitro, in-vivo, and clinical studies. To resolve this uncertainty, we conducted a systematic review focusing exclusively on randomized controlled trials, the highest standard of clinical evidence, to evaluate whether aPDT reduces postoperative pain and microbial load.

Materials and methods

Protocol registration

The study protocol was registered in PROSPERO (CRD42024567223), and the review was conducted and reported according to the PRISMA guidelines [14].

Information sources

The electronic databases searched included Medline, EMBASE, Scopus, Web of Science and Cochrane Library up to August 1, 2024. The search was later updated on December 31, 2024. No restrictions regarding language and the date of publication were imposed.

Search strategy

For Medline the preliminary search protocol was developed using the MeSH terms and free texts and for other database the search method was later developed based on syntax (supplementary document 1). The search terms for all five databases were((Photodynamic Inactivation OR Photosensitization OR Photosensitizer OR PDT) AND (Endodontic infection or symptomatic or asymptomatic or apical periodontitis or pulpal necrosis or Root canal therapy or endodontic treatment)). All identified articles were imported into reference management software (EndNote Version X9, Clarivate Analytics, Philadelphia, PA) and duplicates were removed. The remaining articles were then imported into Covidence systematic software for screening.

Selections process

At first, the articles were screened based on the titles and abstracts independently and in duplicate by two reviewers (UD and JG); articles that did not address the research question were excluded. The same reviewers reviewed the full texts of the articles that passed the initial screening using defined eligibility criteria independently and in duplicate. The third reviewer (AA) was consulted to resolve any disagreements when necessary. The process is illustrated in the PRISMA study flow diagram (Fig. 1).Fig. 1PRISMA study flow diagram

PRISMA study flow diagram

Data collection process and data items

A custom-made data extraction table was used by two reviewers (UD and JG) for the extraction of relevant data from the selected full-text articles independently. The following information was collected from the selected studies: author’s name, year of publication, country of origin, study purpose, design, and setting, eligibility criteria, sample size and characteristics, types of teeth, endodontic diagnosis, instruments used for root canal preparation, irrigation methods during biomechanical preparation, parameters for antimicrobial photodynamic therapy, methods for measuring microbial load and pain, main outcomes, funding sources, ethical approval, and conflicts of interest. Additional information was not sought from the authors.

Risk of bias assessment

The selected studies were independently reviewed by two reviewers (UD and JG) according to the ROB2 tool [15]. Further, a comparison of the results was done, and disagreements were resolved by mutual consensus.

Effect measures and synthesis methods

The studies measured the level of postoperative pain using the Visual Analogue Scale (VAS). From the eligible studies, the sample size, mean and standard deviation (SD) of pain score for each comparison group were extracted. Further, the mean and standard deviation (SD) on the pain score were calculated from the studies reporting median value and range by Wan’s method [16]. Eventually, the mean difference, sample size and standard error were calculated, and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were used to determine the absolute difference in pain scores between the control and aPDT groups.

The random effect model meta-analysis was conducted with the ‘metaphor’ package in R due to the heterogeneity among the studies. Heterogeneity of individual studies was calculated by using Cochrane’s Q statistic and I2 index [17].

Certainty assessment

To assess the certainty of the evidence, “Grading of Recommendations, Assessment, Development, and Evaluation” (GRADE) was used using the Grade pro GDT Tool [18, 19].

Results

Study selection

Electronic databases search yielded 1780 articles: Medline [207], Embase [201], Scopus [307], Web of Science [1005], and Cochrane [60]. De-duplication in EndNote resulted in 1311 unique articles that were subjected to title and abstract screening. Eventually, 1266 articles were excluded during the screening, and 45 articles were deemed suitable for full-text review. During the full text review 32 articles were excluded and reasons for exclusion were provided (Supplementary document 2). Finally, 11 articles were included in the qualitative synthesis, out of which 5 were subjected to meta-analysis.

Study characteristics

The studies included in the review are shown in Table 1. Of the 11 studies selected for the review, 6 studies evaluated the reduction of microbial load after the use of aPDT during root canal treatment [10, 20–24] and 5 studies assessed postoperative pain after the use of aPDT during root canal treatment [2, 8, 25–27]. Two studies evaluated the periapical healing and microbial load reduction after using aPDT during root canal treatment [10, 20]. The reported age of the participants in the included studies ranged from 15 to 77 years [10, 20–22, 24, 25]. Five studies did not report the age of the participants [2, 8, 23, 26, 27]. The total number of participants in the included studies ranged from 10 to 70, with a total sample size of 332. The studies originated from 3 countries, with the highest number of studies from Brazil [2, 8, 20–23, 25–27], one study from India [24] and another from the USA [10]. The studies were published between 2015 and 2024. Eight studies included patients presenting with pulpal necrosis and apical periodontitis in single-rooted teeth [10, 21–27]. Only 3 studies included patients presenting with pulpal necrosis and apical periodontitis in multi-rooted teeth [2, 8, 20]. Four studies determined aPDT's effect on postoperative pain during root canal treatment in patients presenting with endodontic infections [8, 25–27]. One study evaluated the influence of the apical limit of instrumentation and aPDT on postoperative pain during root canal treatment [2]. Postoperative pain was recorded using VAS at 24 h, 48 h, 72 h, and 1-week intervals. Microbiological analyses

Studies used different approaches to microbial analyses (cultural and molecular analyses). Out of 6 studies, 4 studies quantified microbial load thorough cultural analysis by counting colony-forming units [10, 22–24]. Some studies additionally quantified endotoxins, specifically lipopolysaccharides, to assess the inflammatory potential within the root canal using biochemical analyses [22, 23]. Despite procedural differences, the studies aimed to assess microbial load reduction as an outcome of aPDT in endodontic treatment. While others used molecular analyses such as polymerase chain reaction (PCR), DNA extraction, amplification and hybridization techniques to assess the presence of specific microorganisms [20]. Additionally, one of the studies effectively integrated culture analysis with molecular analysis, such as DNA extraction, amplification and quantification techniques to identify and quantify specific targeted micro-organisms, such as E.faecalis and Candida species [21]. However, due to large heterogeneity in the microbial analyses for organism detection meta-analysis could not be conducted for the reduction in the microbial load.

Pain measurement

The Visual Analogue Scale was used to measure pain. The VAS consisted of a 100 mm line with no marks except for the number “0” at the beginning and “10” at the end [8]. The time intervals at which pain was recorded on the VAS scale varied: two studies recorded pain at 24 h, 72 h, and 1 week [8, 26]; one study recorded pain at 24 h, 48 h, 72 h, and 1 week [2]; and another study recorded pain at 6 h, 12 h, 24 h, 48 h, 72 h, and 1 week [25, 27]. As the time intervals for the pain measurement were different among the studies, VAS scores at 24 h and 72 h were used for the measurement of postoperative pain and were subsequently included in the meta-analysis.

Synthesis of results

The studies implemented different approaches for evaluating the effect of aPDT in the reduction of microbial load. Due to considerable heterogeneity among the studies, evaluating the reduction in microbial load, only a qualitative synthesis was possible [10, 20–24]. However, studies used the VAS score to determine post-operative pain after the use aPDT in endodontic treatment. Five studies reporting the VAS scores were included in the meta-analysis [2, 8, 25–27]. The meta-analysis comparing VAS pain scores between the control and aPDT groups was conducted using the mean, standard error and sample size of each group.

Effectiveness of aPDT in the reduction of microbial load

This systematic review evaluated six studies for the effect of photodynamic therapy in the reduction of microbial load during root canal treatment. Four studies found a significant reduction in microbial load with aPDT when used in conjunction with chemo-mechanical preparation [10, 22–24].Whereas two studies found a decrease in the number of specific microorganisms such as E. faecalis and C. albicans, following the use of aPDT [20, 21]. These studies demonstrated high heterogeneity were thus not included in the meta-analysis.

Effectiveness of aPDT on postoperative pain level

The effectiveness of aPDT in reducing postoperative pain, relative to a control group, was quantified using the standardized mean difference as the effect size, and analyzed through a random-effects meta-analysis. At 24 h the overall effect size was −1.21 (95% CI: −2.08 to −0.34) (Fig. 2), whereas at 72 h effect size was −0.63(95% CI: −1.13 to −0.12) (Fig. 3), indicating a significant reduction in postoperative pain scores with the use of aPDT in endodontic treatment. Discussion

This systematic review and meta-analysis evaluated the effectiveness of aPDT in the reduction of postoperative pain and microbial load during root canal treatment in endodontic infections based on randomized controlled trials. Five studies describing the reduction of postoperative pain using aPDT with moderate heterogeneity were included in the meta-analysis [2, 8, 22, 25, 26]. Overall, the meta-analyses supported the effectiveness of photodynamic therapy in reducing pain at 24 h and 72 h post-treatment.

While the effect sizes varied across studies, all reported a reduction in pain (negative standardized mean difference values), and their confidence intervals overlapped the pooled effect estimates. Despite study variabilities, the findings strongly supported the effectiveness of aPDT in reducing pain at 24 h and 72 h post-treatment, with a consistent overall benefit across diverse study settings. Although there were some variabilities among studies related to patient age, sample size, and methodology, the reduction in postoperative pain was statistically significant (p < 0.05). However, two studies reported no statistical difference in postoperative pain reduction between the control and intervention groups, which may have contributed to the observed heterogeneity [2, 25]. In one study, differences in the concentration of the photosensitizing agent and the operator’s experience could have influenced outcomes [25]. While in another study, chlorhexidine was used as an auxiliary chemical, and its anti-inflammatory effects in the periapical area may have influenced the results [2]. Overall, the observed heterogeneity may be due to the lack of a standardized aPDT protocol, variations in irrigation protocols, and differences in operator skill.

Alonaizan and AlFawaz [28] reported a narrative outcome in which two out of five trials showed a statistically significant improvement in post-endodontic pain in the aPDT group, leading to a debatable conclusion that aPDT reduces postoperative pain. In contrast, this review found that the included studies overall showed a significant reduction in postoperative pain in the aPDT group at both 24 h and 72 h. The differences in findings may be attributed to the inclusion of retreatment and endodontic surgery cases, as well as variations in sample size and laser parameters in the previous review [28].

The rationale is that aPDT eliminates pain through two pathways [7]. First, by generating reactive oxygen species through activation of the photosensitizer with a specific light wavelength. It targets and eliminates microorganisms while suppressing the immune response, thereby reducing inflammation. Second, aPDT modulates nociception and relieves pain by inducing electrochemical changes in cells [7].

In this systematic review, aPDT effectively reduced postoperative pain within the first 24 h and 72 h. However, Luo et al. [29], who evaluated the effectiveness of various laser therapy modalities in comparison to conventional root canal treatment, found that laser adjuvant therapies such as low-level laser therapy, diode laser and photodynamic therapy demonstrated only marginal benefits on reducing postoperative pain intensity.

Further, this systematic review evaluated six studies to assess the effect of photodynamic therapy in the reduction of microbial load during root canal treatment. But due to high heterogeneity in microbial count data, the studies were not suitable for meta-analysis. However, the qualitative synthesis indicated a significant reduction in microbial count, particularly of E. faecalis and Candida species, along with improved periapical healing in cases of primary endodontic infection associated with periradicular lesions [10, 20–24].

Conventional root canal treatment removes debris, infected dentin and microorganisms. However, it often fails to eliminate microorganisms deep in dentinal tubules, which can lead to secondary infection and treatment failure. Additionally, the effectiveness of the conventional root canal treatment is limited in complex anatomies of teeth such as isthmus, curved canals, fins and anastomoses [3, 30–35]. However, the use of aPDT can assess all the complex anatomy of the root canal through photosensitizers [11]. The passage of low laser light can easily kill the bacteria, as photosensitizers such as Methylene blue and toluidine blue are more sensitive to gram-positive and gram-negative bacteria [36]. The photosensitizers have a high degree of selectivity for killing micro-organisms without affecting the host cell variability [37] and they have been used effectively for the eradication of multidrug-resistant microorganisms [38]. They also reduce the chances of secondary infection by disinfecting canals to a greater depth of dentinal tubules and eliminating bacteria such as E.faecalis, which may lead to secondary infection [32].

The result of our study is in agreement with earlier studies, which demonstrated a positive effect of aPDT in the reduction of microbial load in the root canal treatment [39, 40]. In contrast, one study found that the efficacy of aPDT was similar to NaOCl used in conventional chemo-mechanical preparation in the removal of bacteria from the root canal [41]. Our finding was supported by a review conducted in primary teeth, which demonstrated that aPDT significantly reduced the microbial load when used in combination with conventional endodontic treatment [42].

The risk of bias assessment found that five studies [10, 21, 24–26] were at high risk, three [2, 20, 23] at moderate risk and another three [8, 22, 27] at low risk. Several limitations were found in the included studies. The reporting standards among the included clinical trials were inconsistent, as several studies failed to follow the CONSORT (Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials) guidelines. A few studies did not clearly report the randomization process, allocation concealment and blinding procedure. Blinding of the patients was present in all included studies, but blinding of the operator was only implemented in a few studies. However, blinding the operator is difficult due to the nature of the intervention involved, as the procedure requires direct operator involvement with a specific expertise.

Moreover, there was a lack of standardization in tooth length, canal preparation size, and aPDT application parameters in the studies. Additionally, differences in bacterial sampling methods, culture media, incubation times, and detection techniques led to inconsistent outcomes and increased heterogeneity. Regardless of these limitations, all included studies were clinical trials, offering high-level evidence. Most of the studies in the review used the VAS scale, ensuring reliable pain assessment. Unlike previous reviews with mixed study types and laser approaches, this review focuses specifically on aPDT's effectiveness in reducing postoperative pain and microbial load in root canal treatment.

Future perspectives of aPDT

Overall, this review found that aPDT can effectively reduce the postoperative pain and microbial load when used as an adjunct to conventional root canal treatment in endodontic infections. Additionally, by providing disinfection at the greater depth of the dentinal tubules, it prevents the chances of secondary infection and also leads to minimal damage to the tooth structure. However, the heterogeneity in study designs and the lack of standardized protocol in the use of aPDT limits the generalizability of our findings. Future studies should focus on optimizing photodynamic therapy protocols in endodontics by improving photosensitizer penetration and reducing irradiation time, while using standardized treatment parameters to enhance comparability across studies. Such optimization may lead to more pronounced clinical benefits, including better control of postoperative pain, inflammation, and edema.

Conclusions

Overall, this systematic review and meta-analysis revealed that aPDT can significantly reduce postoperative pain both at 24 h and 72 h. There was a reduction in microbial load count after the use of aPDT in conventional root canal treatment. However, more randomized controlled trials with low risk of bias and standardized aPDT protocols are necessary to strengthen the evidence.
