Maximum volume coordinates for Grassmann interpolation: Lagrange, Hermite, and errors
Rasmus Jensen, Ralf Zimmermann

Abstract
We present a novel approach to Riemannian interpolation on the Grassmann manifold. Instead of relying on the Riemannian normal coordinates, i.e. the Riemannian exponential and logarithm maps, we approach the interpolation problem with an alternative set of local coordinates and corresponding parameterizations. A special property of these coordinates is that their calculation does not require any matrix decompositions. This is a numerical advantage over Riemann normal coordinates and many other retractions on the Grassmann manifold, especially when derivative data are to be treated. To estimate the interpolation error, we examine the conditioning of these mappings and state explicit bounds. It turns out that the parameterizations are well-conditioned, but the coordinate mappings are generally not. As a remedy, we introduce maximum-volume coordinates that are based on a search for subblocks of column-orthogonal matrices of large absolute determinant. We show that the order of magnitude of the asymptotic interpolation error on Gr ⁡ ( n , p ) \operatorname{Gr}(n,p) is the same as in the Euclidean space. Two numerical experiments are conducted. The first is an academic one, where we interpolate a parametric orthogonal projector Q ​ Q T QQ^{T} , where the Q Q –factor stems from a parametric compact QR–decomposition. The second experiment is in the context of parametric model reduction of dynamical systems, where we interpolate reduced subspaces that are obtained by proper orthogonal decomposition.

Introduction

Numerical operations on nonlinear Riemannian manifolds are commonly referred to as ‘Riemannian computing’.
Riemannian computing methods have established themselves as important tools in a large variety of applications, including computer vision, machine learning, and optimization, see

Absil et al. 2004
;

;

;
Gallivan et al. 2003
;

;

, and the anthologies

;
Srivastava and Turaga 2015
.
They also have applications in statistics and data science

and in numerical methods for differential equations
Benner et al. 2015
;
Celledoni et al. 2020
;
Hairer et al. 2006
;
Iserles et al. 2000
.

A special subclass of Riemannian computing problems is the interpolation of manifold-valued functions.
Suppose that

f:D\to\mathcal{M},\quad x\mapsto f(x)

is a differentiable function on a Euclidean domain
D\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
with outputs on a Riemannian manifold
(\mathcal{M},\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle)
.
Given a sample data set
\{(x_{i},f(x_{i}))|i=1,\ldots,k\}
,
manifold interpolation is concerned with computing an
interpolant

\hat{f}:D\to\mathcal{M},\quad x\mapsto\hat{f}(x)\quad\text{ with }\hat{f}(x_{i})=f(x_{i}),\quad i=1,\ldots,k.

The basic interpolation task may be enhanced by including samples of derivative data of
f
, which then goes by the name of Hermite interpolation.
Classical Euclidean interpolation algorithms (Lagrange Newton, Hermite, radial basis functions, …) rely heavily on the underlying vector space structure; a feature which manifolds are lacking. Therefore, the challenge in manifold interpolation is to ensure that the interpolant
\hat{f}
respects the manifold structure.

The standard approach to manifold interpolation can be subdivided in three key steps:

1

1

This approach works, if the sampled data can be covered by a single coordinate chart. Otherwise, extra measures have to be taken to ensure a smooth transition of the interpolant between overlapping coordinate domains.

Step 1:

Preprocessing.
Map the sample data from the manifold
\mathcal{M}
to a (Euclidean) coordinate domain. This requires one to select a suitable coordinate chart.

Step 2:

Interpolation.

Employ a Euclidean interpolation method that is linear in the sample data to interpolate the data images in the coordinate domain.

Step 3:

Postprocessing.
Map the interpolated data from the Euclidean coordinate domain to the manifold.
For consistency, this has to be done with the inverse of the coordinate chart selected in step 1.

In this work, we investigate interpolation methods on the Grassmann manifold
\operatorname{Gr}(n,p)
of
p
-dimensional linear subspaces of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
Interpolation of subspaces can be used for
parametric model reduction
,
where Grassmann data points appear as subspaces of dominant modes, usually associated with a complex dynamical system,
Amsallem and Farhat 2008
;
Zimmermann 2014
;
Benner et al. 2015
;
Ciaramella et al. 2024
;
Hess et al. 2023
;
Zimmermann 2021
;
El Omari et al. 2025
;
Pfaller et al. 2020
.

This is also our main motivation to investigate Grassmann interpolation problems. The natural habitat of model reduction problems is high-dimensional spaces. Therefore, computational efficiency is of special concern and we work under the informal ‘low-rank’ assumption that
n\gg p
, i.e., the dimension of the subspaces is much lower than that of the ambient space
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

Original contribution

Two issues are of special interest in the context of manifold interpolation. On the one hand, the numerical feasibility and efficiency, on the other hand estimates and control of the interpolation error. With the work at hand, we aim to contribute to both of them.

With regard to the first point, we examine local Grassmann coordinates that do not require the computation of matrix decompositions.
Although known for at least three decades, see
(
Helmke and Moore 1994
, Appendix C.4)
,
to the best of our knowledge, these coordinates have not yet been used for interpolation applications, nor have they been studied theoretically.
We show that this set of coordinates gives rise to a retraction on the Grassmann manifold. Moreover, we quantify the conditioning of the coordinates and their inverses, which are the associated parameterizations. Further, we discuss
how to optimize the coordinates with respect to their condition number, and why this is important.
For reasons that will become clear later, we refer to the optimized coordinates as
maximum–volume coordinates
(MV coordinates).

The property of being ‘matrix decomposition–free’ should be seen in contrast to the Riemannian normal coordinates. In a certain geometric sense, Riemannian normal coordinates (see
Section

6
) are the best possible option for interpolation problems. However, their numerical realizations on the Grassmann manifold rely on the singular value decomposition and matrix exponentials and/or matrix sine and cosine functions. The advantage of matrix decomposition–free coordinates is most prominent when Hermite interpolation is performed, because processing derivative data requires differentiating matrix decompositions when Riemannian normal coordinates are employed
Zimmermann 2020
.

With regard to the second point, we investigate sources of error in all of the aforementioned three steps ‘preprocessing’, ‘interpolation’, ‘postprocessing’. In particular, we examine how interpolation errors that stem from the Euclidean interpolation scheme are forwarded from the coordinate domain to the manifold.

Finally, we demonstrate the findings by means of numerical experiments, where Lagrange- and Hermite interpolation on the Grassmann manifold are considered. We observe that interpolation in MV coordinates leads to interpolants that exhibit an accuracy comparable to their Riemannian normal coordinate counterparts.

State of the art and related work

Univariate Hermite manifold interpolation has been considered explicitly in
Jakubiak et al. 2006
;
Zimmermann 2020
;
Séguin and Kressner 2024
.
A related line of research is the generalization of Bézier curves and the De Casteljau-algorithm
to Riemannian manifolds, see
Bergmann and Gousenbourger 2018
;
Gousenbourger et al. 2018
;
Nava-Yazdani and Polthier 2013
;
Popiel and Noakes 2007
;
Samir and Adouani 2019
.
Subdivision schemes based on geodesic averages have been investigated in
Dyn and Sharon 2017
;
Wallner and Dyn 2005
;
Hofit et al. 2023
.
A survey on manifold interpolation methods for applications in model reduction is given in
Zimmermann 2021
.
Recently, a very accessable tutorial focusing on Grassmann interpolation has appeared
Ciaramella et al. 2024
.

We are aware of four results on manifold interpolation errors:

1.

The appendix of
Attali et al. 2007
contains sandwich bounds ‘Euclidean distance vs. manifold distance’ for embedded submanifolds where the Riemannian metric is the one obtained from the ambient Euclidean space.
While not of concern in
Attali et al. 2007
, these bounds can be used to estimate manifold interpolation errors. The recent preprint
Mataigne et al. 2024
gives specific comparative bounds for the Stiefel manifold.

2.

The work
Zimmermann 2020
includes a result on error propagation ‘tangent space to manifold’ that relies on Riemannian normal coordinates and the local sectional curvature.
A consequence of this result is that if the tangent space interpolation scheme features an asymptotic error of
\mathcal{O}(h^{k})
for interpolation steps
h\to 0
, then so does the manifold interpolation scheme.

3.

By covariant calculus, it is shown in
Séguin and Kressner 2024
that Hermite interpolation using retractions and a Riemannian version of the De Casteljau algorithm
exhibits an asymptotic error of
\mathcal{O}(h^{4})
,
h
being the interpolation step size, just like with the Euclidean counterpart.

4.

Recently in
Jacobsson et al. 2024
, an error bound associated to approximating functions on Riemannian manifolds is established by applying Riemannian comparison theory.
The error bound obtained is similar to the one from
Zimmermann 2020
, but relies on the Toponogov comparison theorem from Riemannian geometry and thus requires global curvature bounds rather than local ones. This result also shows that the order of the asymptotic interpolation error is preserved when transiting from the tangent space to the manifold.

To the best of our knowledge, the numerical errors associated with the preprocessing step of Riemannian interpolation, i.e, with the mapping of the sample data onto a suitable coordinate domain have not yet been considered in the literature.

2.1
The Grassmann manifold

The Grassmann manifold (or Grassmannian)
\operatorname{Gr}(n,p)
is the set of all
p
dimensional subspaces of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
,

\operatorname{Gr}(n,p)=\{\mathcal{U}\leq\mathbb{R}^{n}\mid\dim\mathcal{U}=p\}.

A subspace
\mathcal{U}
may be uniquely represented by

1.

\mathcal{U}=\operatorname{span}(U)
, where
U\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times p}
is a matrix whose columns form a basis of
\mathcal{U}
.
Mind that
\operatorname{span}(U)
is unique, but the spanning matrix
U
is not.

2.

The orthogonal projector
P_{\mathcal{U}}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
onto
\mathcal{U}
.
This is the unique linear, idempotent map (
P_{\mathcal{U}}\circ P_{\mathcal{U}}=P_{\mathcal{U}}
) with
\operatorname{ran}(P_{\mathcal{U}})=\mathcal{U}
,
\ker(P_{\mathcal{U}})=\mathcal{U}^{\bot}
.
The orthogonal complement is with respect to the Euclidean inner product.
Orthogonal projectors are equivalently characterized by being self-adjoint,
P_{\mathcal{U}}^{T}=P_{\mathcal{U}}
, or by featuring unit norm
\|P_{\mathcal{U}}\|_{2}=1
, which is the smallest possible value a projector can exhibit, see
(
Saad 1992
, Section 3.1)
.

For the numerical representation of subspaces, we will use column-orthogonal matrices exclusively.
The set of all such matrices, i.e., the set of orthonormal
p
–frames is called the Stiefel manifold

\operatorname{St}(n,p)=\{U\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times p}\mid U^{T}U=I_{p}\}.

Each subspace
\mathcal{U}\in\operatorname{Gr}(n,p)
corresponds to an equivalence class of Stiefel matrices
[U]=\{UR\mid R\in O(p)\}\subset\operatorname{St}(n,p)
.
Any
U\in[U]
is called a matrix representative of the subspace
[U]
.
When formalized, this construction shows that the Grassmann manifold is a quotient manifold of the Stiefel manifold

\operatorname{Gr}(n,p)=\operatorname{St}(n,p)/O(p),

see

Edelman et al. 1998
;
Absil et al. 2008
;
Bendokat et al. 2024
;
Zimmermann 2021
, and
(
Lee 2012
, Chapter 21)
for the full theoretical background.
Given any not necessarily column-orthonormal
U\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times p}
with
\operatorname{span}(U)=\mathcal{U}
,
the orhogonal projector onto
\mathcal{U}
is

P_{\mathcal{U}}=U(U^{T}U)^{-1}U^{T}.

For

U\in[U]\subset\operatorname{St}(n,p)
, the formula reduces to
P_{\mathcal{U}}=UU^{T}
.
This matrix representation is unique and independent from the chosen matrix representative
U\in[U]\subset\operatorname{St}(n,p)
.

Both the Stiefel and the Grassmann manifold are smooth manifolds; yet the former is embedded in the Euclidean matrix space
\mathbb{R}^{n\times p}
, while the latter is abstract when viewed as a quotient space of equivalence classes.
However, by considering the Grassmann manifold as the set of orthogonal projectors

\operatorname{Gr}(n,p)=\{P\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}\mid P^{2}=P,P^{T}=P,\rank(P)=p\}\subset\operatorname{sym}(n),

it is an embedded submanifold of
\operatorname{sym}(n)\subset\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
.

The archetype projector onto the first
p
Cartesian coordinates is

P_{0}=\begin{pmatrix}I_{p}&0\\
0&0\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}I_{p}\\
0\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}I_{p}&0\end{pmatrix}=:I_{n,p}I_{n,p}^{T}.

(1)

An eigenvalue decomposition shows that any other projector is obtained from an orthogonal similarity transformation on
P_{0}
,

P\in\operatorname{Gr}(n,p)\Leftrightarrow\exists Q\in O(n):P=QP_{0}Q^{T}.

Note that if we split

p

P=QP_{0}Q^{T}=UU^{T}

The similarity transformation

(2)

and the Grassmann set of orthogonal projectors is the

The dimensions of the manifolds

3.2
Numerical processing of Grassmann data

Formally, the local coordinates for the Grassmannian
\operatorname{Gr}(n,p)
reviewed in
Section

3.1
work with projectors, i.e. matrices in
\operatorname{sym}(n)\subset\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
.
Given a subspace
\mathcal{U}\in\operatorname{Gr}(n,p)
with orthonormal basis
U\in\operatorname{St}(n,p)
, the orthogonal projector is
UU^{T}\in\operatorname{sym}(n)
.
Yet, the most important practical applications that feature Grassmann data are those that emphasize and exploit the low-rank structure of
UU^{T}
. Especially, if
n\gg p
, an
(n\times n)
matrix must never be formed. This means that in an algorithmic implementation, the coordinate chart
\Psi
receives a matrix representative
U=\begin{pmatrix}U_{1}\\
U_{2}\end{pmatrix}\in\operatorname{St}(n,p)
as an input, where the subblocks are of dimension
U_{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times p}
,
U_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{(n-p)\times p}
.
The output is evaluated as

\Psi(UU^{T})=\Psi\begin{pmatrix}U_{1}U_{1}^{T}&U_{1}U_{2}^{T}\\
U_{2}U_{1}^{T}&U_{2}U_{2}^{T}\end{pmatrix}=U_{2}U_{1}^{T}(U_{1}U_{1}^{T})^{-1}=U_{2}U_{1}^{-1}\in\mathbb{R}^{(n-p)\times p}.

(11)

Thus, the largest dimension of matrices formed in code is
(n\times p)
.
The upper
(p\times p)
–subblock
U_{1}
may be replaced with any subselection of
p
rows
U
that are linearly independent, with the subblock
U_{2}
then being chosen as the complementary subset of rows of
U
.

The associated parameterization
\varphi
can be evaluated as follows:

(A)

Using matrix square roots:
Given
B\in\mathbb{R}^{(n-p)\times p}
,
compute a matrix square root
T
such that

(I_{p}+B^{T}B)=T^{2}.

The associated
(n\times p)
Stiefel representation of the projector
\varphi(B)

is
U:=\begin{pmatrix}I_{p}\\
B\end{pmatrix}T^{-1}
.
Obviously,
U^{T}U=I_{p}
.

(B)

Using the Cholesky decomposition:
Compute
L\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times p}
, lower triangular, such that

(I_{p}+B^{T}B)=LL^{T}

The associated
(n\times p)
Stiefel representation of the projector
\varphi(B)

is
U:=\begin{pmatrix}I_{p}\\
B\end{pmatrix}L^{-T}
.
Again,
U^{T}U=I_{p}
.

When processing derivative data in Stiefel representation, it is important to note that if
U(t)
is a Stiefel representation of a curve
U(t)U(t)^{T}=P(t)\in\operatorname{Gr}(n,p)
, then
\dot{U}(t)
is not necessarily a
horizontal
Stiefel representation of
\dot{P}(t)
. To obtain a horizontal Stiefel representation of
\dot{P}(t)
, compute
\Delta(t)=\quantity(\dot U(t)U(t)^T-U(t)\dot U(t)^T)U(t)
, which is the horizontal lift of
\dot{P}(t)
.

3.3
Lagrange- and Hermite interpolation

We are now ready to discuss interpolation. We assume throughout the section that for a given set of Lagrange– or Hermite data
\mathcal{S}=\quantity{t_i,f_i,f_i'}_{i=1}^{d}
, all the points
f_{i}\in\operatorname{Gr}(n,p)
are in the domain of a single chart
\Psi:\operatorname{Gr}(n,p)\to\mathbb{R}^{(n-p)\times p}
. To map the Grassmann data to the local coordinate domain, we use that the local chart
\Psi
is a local diffeomorphism, with differential
d\Psi_{P}:\T_{P}\operatorname{Gr}(n,p)\to\T_{\Psi(P)}\mathbb{R}^{(n-p)\times p}\simeq\mathbb{R}^{(n-p)\times p}

being a vector space isomorphism. Hence points and tangent vectors can be mapped bijectively to the local coordinate domain, and we obtain
\bar{\mathcal{S}}=\quantity{t_i,\bar f_i,\bar f_i'}_{i=1}^{d}\subset\mathbb{R}^{(n-p)\times p}
. Similar to interpolation in normal coordinates, the vector space structure of
\mathbb{R}^{(n-p)\times p}
allows us to apply any interpolation scheme from classical interpolation theory. The well–known Lagrange interpolation polynomial is stated in
Section

7
. For Hermite interpolation, we use the formulation as in
(
Quarteroni et al. 2007
, Section 8.5)
. Considering the special case of two data samples
\quantity{t_i,\bar f_i,\bar f_i'}_{i=1}^{2}
, we construct the cubic Hermite polynomial

\gamma(t)=L_{00}(t)\bar{f}_{0}+L_{10}(t)\bar{f}_{1}+L_{01}(t)\bar{f}_{0}^{\prime}+L_{11}(t)\bar{f}_{1}^{\prime}.

The coefficient functions
L_{00},\dots,L_{11}
are listed in
Section

7
. The resulting Hermite interpolant of
f
is
\hat{f}(t^{*})=\varphi(\gamma(t^{*}))
. Replacing
\gamma(t)
with the Lagrange polynomial based on the data in local coordinates gives similarly the Lagrange interpolant of
f
.

To map derivative data from
\T_{f_{i}}\operatorname{Gr}(n,p)
to the local coordinates, we compute the directional derivative of
\Psi
.
With the quotient map
\Pi:\operatorname{St}(n,p)\to\operatorname{Gr}(n,p),U\mapsto UU^{T}
we have the Stiefel counterpart of
\Psi
,

\tilde{\Psi}:\operatorname{St}(n,p)\to\mathbb{R}^{(n-p)\times p},\quad U\mapsto\tilde{\Psi}(U)=(\Psi\circ\Pi)(U).

It holds
d\tilde{\Psi}_{U}:\T_{U}\operatorname{St}(n,p)\to\mathbb{R}^{(n-p)\times p}
and

d\Psi_{\Pi(U)}\quantity(d\Pi_U(T)))=d\tilde{\Psi}_{U}(T).

Let
U(t)=\begin{pmatrix}U_{1}(t)\\
U_{2}(t)\end{pmatrix}
be a Stiefel curve with
U(0)=U
,
\dot{U}(0)=T=\begin{pmatrix}T_{1}\\
T_{2}\end{pmatrix}
and consider the corresponding Grassmann curve
P(t)=\Pi(U(t))=U(t)U(t)^{T}
with
P(0)=P,\dot{P}(0)=X=D\Pi_{U}(T)=TU^{T}+UT^{T}
.
Then

d\Psi_{P}(X)=d\tilde{\Psi}_{U}(T)=\evaluated{\frac{\dd}{\dd t}}_{t=0}\tilde{\Psi}(U(t))=T_{2}U_{1}^{-1}-U_{2}U_{1}^{-1}T_{1}U_{1}^{-1}\in\mathbb{R}^{(n-p)\times p}.

(12)

This formula allows one to compute the Grassmann derivative
d\Psi_{P}(X)
by using Stiefel representatives with matrix blocks of size
(n-p)\times p
and
p\times p
.
Since
d\Psi_{P}
has the inverse map
d\varphi_{\Psi(P)}
, we have that Hermite interpolating
f:I\to\operatorname{Gr}(n,p)
on the sample data
\mathcal{S}=\quantity{t_i,f_i,f_i'}_{i=1}^{d}
is equivalent to Hermite interpolating
\bar{f}=\Psi\circ f
on the sample data
\bar{\mathcal{S}}=\quantity{t_i, \bar f_i, \bar f_i'}_{i=1}^{d}=\quantity{t_i, \Psi(f_i), d\Psi_{f_i}(f_i')}_{i=1}^{d}
, and it is by this construction that the Hermite polynomial
\gamma(t)
stated above is the Hermite interpolant in local coordinates of
\bar{f}
.

Comparing to interpolation in Riemannian normal coordinates, mapping derivative data to the local coordinate domain does not require a finite differences–approximation.

To motivate the following investigations, consider the Stiefel matrix

. Exactly the same challenge face CUR-matrix decompositions (also called pseudo-skeleton matrix approximations)
Goreinov et al. 1997

and the discrete empericial interpolation method (DEIM),
Chaturantabut and Sorensen 2010
;
Drmač and Gugercin 2016
, and it is closely related to rank-revealing matrix facorizations

Chandrasekaran and Ipsen 1994
;
Gu and Eisenstat 1996
;
Damle et al. 2024
.
In fact, a minor modification of
(
Goreinov et al. 1997
, Lemma 2.1)
shows that if the block
U_{1}
is formed by
p
rows that maximize the ‘volume’
|\det(U_1)|
, then

\norm{U_1^{-1}}_{\textnormal{F}}\leq\sqrt{p(n-p)+p}.

For a coordinate chart
\Psi
that is based on the optimal choice of the subblock
U_{1}
, we thus obtain

\norm{\Psi(P_U)}_{\textnormal{F}}\leq\sqrt{p(n-p)+p-p}=\sqrt{p(n-p)}.

These observations suggest to optimize the subblock selection.
This is equivalent to computing a permutation matrix
\mathcal{P}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
such that

\mathcal{P}\begin{pmatrix}U_{1}\\
U_{2}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}\tilde{U}_{1}\\
\tilde{U}_{2}\end{pmatrix},\quad\norm{\tilde U_1^{-1}}_{\textnormal{F}}<\norm{U_1^{-1}}_{\textnormal{F}}.

(13)

Computing the local coordinates of the permuted blocks is the same as composing the local coordinate chart with a map
\Upsilon_{\mathcal{P}}:\operatorname{Gr}(n,p)\to\operatorname{Gr}(n,p),P\mapsto\mathcal{P}P\mathcal{P}^{T}
to obtain the modified chart
\Psi\circ\Upsilon:\operatorname{Gr}(n,p)\to\mathbb{R}^{(n-p)\times p}
.

By
(
Goreinov et al. 1997
, Lemma 2.1)
, a practical approach to optimize the chart is to use a volume maximizer for selecting the subblock
U_{1}
.
We will call the resulting chart a
maximum–volume local coordinate chart
or
MV-chart
for short.

The literature knows a large variety of methods for this task, see
Damle et al. 2024
and we have no intention (and no need) to decide which method works best.
In the numerical experiments, we apply the maxvol method of
(
Goreinov et al. 2010
, Algorithm 1)

to compute the permutation matrix
\mathcal{P}
.

2:

to

do

on

among
\quantity{\mcP_1,\dots,\mcP_d}

.

and prepare for preprocessing

Given a set of Grassmann data
\quantity{f_i}_{i=1}^{d}
in Stiefel representation, for consistency in the interpolation procedure, one and the same permutation matrix
\mathcal{P}

must be chosen when preparing the data for preprocessing.
Therefore, the actual task is to find a single permutation matrix such that the upper
p\times p
blocks of each of the Grassmann samples

are well–conditioned.

For example in model reduction applications, where the data

often stems from reduced bases for parametric dynamical systems, it is to be expected that the rows that contain essential independent information are located in similar positions throughout the data set.

3.5
The conditioning of the local parameterizations

The absolute condition number of a continuously differentiable map
f:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{m}
is
(
Higham 2008
, Theorem 3.1)

\operatorname{cond}(f,X)=\max_{\norm{D}=1}\norm{df_X(D)}.

The next lemma provides a bound for the condition number of the local parameterization
\varphi
from (
8
). This result will be used to quantify how the interpolation errors in local coordinates are amplified when forwarded to the manifold. Mind that
d\varphi_{B}:\mathbb{R}^{(n-p)\times p}\to\T_{\varphi(B)}\operatorname{Gr}(n,p)\subset\operatorname{sym}(n)\subset\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
. Since we consider the Grassmannian of rank-
p
projectors as an embedded submanifold of
(\operatorname{sym}(n),\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{0})
with metric inherited from this space, we are required to compute
\|d\varphi_{B}(\Delta)\|_{0}
in the canonical metric, i.e., the Euclidean metric up to a factor of
\frac{1}{2}
, if we want the results to be compatible with the Riemannian measures.
The appropriate norm on the input domain
\mathbb{R}^{(n-p)\times p}
is the Frobenius norm
\norm{\cdot}_{\textnormal{F}}
. For the linear maps
d\varphi_{B}
, we use the operator norm

\|d\varphi_{B}\|_{\textnormal{F},0}=\sup_{V\neq 0}\frac{\|d\varphi_{B}(V)\|_{0}}{\norm{V}_{\textnormal{F}}}=\max_{\norm{V}_{\textnormal{F}}=1}\|d\varphi_{B}(V)\|_{0}
.

Lemma 4
.

Let
B\in\mathbb{R}^{(n-p)\times p}
with a compact SVD of
B=U\Sigma V^{T}
with
\Sigma=\diag(\sigma_{1},\ldots,\sigma_{p})
.
The condition number of
\varphi
at
B
is bounded by

\operatorname{cond}_{0}(\varphi,B)\leq\sqrt{2}\sqrt{\left(\frac{1}{(1+\sigma_{p}^{2})^{2}}+\max_{i=1,\ldots p}\quantity{ \frac{\sigma_i^2}{(1+\sigma_{i}^2)^2}}\right)}+1.

(14)

The global worst-case bound is

M:=\sup_{B\in\mathbb{R}^{(n-p)\times p}}\{\operatorname{cond}_{0}(\varphi,B)\}\leq\sqrt{\frac{5}{2}}+1\approx 2.5811.

(15)

Proof.

For
B\in\mathbb{R}^{(n-p)\times p}
write
P=\varphi(B)
and
S=S(B)=(I_{p}+B^{T}B)^{-1}
. The differential in direction
\Delta\in\mathbb{R}^{(n-p)\times p}
, is

\displaystyle d\varphi_{B}(\Delta)=

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}0\\
\Delta\end{pmatrix}S\begin{pmatrix}I_{p}&B^{T}\end{pmatrix}-\begin{pmatrix}I_{p}\\
B\end{pmatrix}S(\Delta^{T}B+B^{T}\Delta)S\begin{pmatrix}I_{p}&B^{T}\end{pmatrix}+\begin{pmatrix}I_{p}\\
B\end{pmatrix}S\begin{pmatrix}0&\Delta^{T}\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}0&0\\
\Delta S&\Delta SB^{T}\end{pmatrix}-\begin{pmatrix}I_{p}\\
B\end{pmatrix}S\begin{pmatrix}I_{p}&B^{T}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}0&\Delta^{T}\\
\Delta&0\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}I_{p}\\
B\end{pmatrix}S\begin{pmatrix}I_{p}&B\end{pmatrix}+\begin{pmatrix}0&S\Delta^{T}\\
0&BS\Delta^{T}\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}0&0\\
\Delta S&\Delta SB^{T}\end{pmatrix}-P\begin{pmatrix}0&\Delta^{T}\\
\Delta&0\end{pmatrix}P+\begin{pmatrix}0&S\Delta^{T}\\
0&BS\Delta^{T}\end{pmatrix}.

(16)

To compute a bound for
\operatorname{cond}_{0}(\varphi,B)=\norm{d\varphi_B}_{\textnormal{F},0}
, let
\Delta\in\mathbb{R}^{(n-p)\times p}
with
\norm{\Delta}_{\textnormal{F}}=1
.
Because
P=\varphi(B)
is an orthogonal projector, the middle term in (
16
) is bounded by

\norm{P\begin{pmatrix}
0&\Delta^T\\
\Delta&0
\end{pmatrix}P}_{0}^{2}\leq\norm{\begin{pmatrix}
0&\Delta^T\\
\Delta&0
\end{pmatrix}}^{2}_{0}=\frac{1}{2}\tr(\begin{pmatrix}
\Delta^T\Delta& 0\\
0 & \Delta\Delta^T
\end{pmatrix})=\tr(\Delta^T\Delta)=1.

The first and last term of
d\varphi_{B}(\Delta)
in (
16
) have the same norm.

\displaystyle\norm{\begin{pmatrix}
0&S\Delta^T\\
0&BS\Delta^T
\end{pmatrix}}_{0}^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\tr\begin{pmatrix}0&0\\
\Delta S&\Delta SB^{T}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}0&S\Delta^{T}\\
0&BS\Delta^{T}\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\tr\begin{pmatrix}0&0\\
0&\Delta SS\Delta^{T}+\Delta SB^{T}BS\Delta^{T}\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}(\norm{S\Delta^T}_{\textnormal{F}}^{2}+\norm{BS\Delta^T}_{\textnormal{F}}^{2})

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2}\left(\norm{S}_{2}^{2}\norm{\Delta}_{\textnormal{F}}^{2}+\norm{BS}_{2}^{2}\norm{\Delta}_{\textnormal{F}}^{2}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{1}{(1+\sigma_{\min}^{2})^{2}}+\max_{i=1,\ldots p}\quantity{ \frac{\sigma_i^2}{(1+\sigma_{i}^2)^2}}\right),

where we have used that
\|XY\|_{F}\leq\|X\|_{2}\|Y\|_{F}
, see
(
Zimmermann and Stoye 2025
, Lemma 3)
.
Since
g(t)=\frac{1}{(1+x^{2})^{2}}\leq 1
and
f(t)=\frac{t^{2}}{(1+t^{2})^{2}}\leq\frac{1}{4}
, we have the global bound.
∎

3.6
From Euclidean distances to manifold distances

The Grassmann manifold
(\operatorname{Gr}(n,p),\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{0})
of orthogonal projectors is an embedded submanifold of
(\operatorname{sym}(n),\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{0})
. Hence,
Lemma

1

applies and yields comparative bounds between the intrinsic Riemannian distance and the extrinsic Euclidean distance.
To make the bounds concrete, we need the maximum Euclidean curvature of a unit-speed Grassmann geodesic when considered as a space curve in
(\operatorname{sym}(n),\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{0})
.

Lemma 5
.

Let
t\to y(t)
be a unit–speed geodesic on
\left(\operatorname{Gr}(n,p),\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{0}\right)
.
Let
\kappa(t)=\|\ddot{y}(t)\|
denote the Euclidean curvature of
y
, when considered as a space curve in the ambient space
(\operatorname{sym}(n),\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{0})
.
Then
\kappa(t)\leq 2
.

Proof.

Consider a Stiefel representative
\gamma(t)=Q_{0}\exp_{m}\quantity(t\begin{pmatrix}
0&-\Delta^T\\
\Delta&0
\end{pmatrix})\begin{pmatrix}I_{p}\\
0\end{pmatrix}

of a unit–speed geodesic
y(t)=\gamma(t)\gamma(t)^{T}
on
\operatorname{Gr}(n,p)
. Without loss of generality,
Q_{0}=I_{n}
. With
X:=\begin{pmatrix}0&-\Delta^{T}\\
\Delta&0\end{pmatrix}
it holds

\dot{y}(t)=\dot{\gamma}(t)\gamma(t)^{T}+\gamma(t)\dot{\gamma}(t)^{T}=\exp_{m}\quantity(tX)\quantity(XP_0+P_0X)\exp_{m}\quantity(-tX).

Unit–speed implies

1=\norm{\dot y(t)}_{0}^{2}=\norm{XP_0+P_0X}_{0}^{2}=\frac{1}{2}\quantity(2\tr(\Delta^T\Delta))=\norm{\Delta}_{\textnormal{F}}^{2}.

This yields that
\ddot{y}(t)=\exp_{m}\quantity(tX)\begin{pmatrix}2\Delta^{T}\Delta&0\\
0&2\Delta\Delta^{T}\end{pmatrix}\exp_{m}\quantity(-tX)

has norm

\displaystyle\kappa(t)^{2}=\norm{\ddot y(t)}_{0}^{2}=\frac{1}{2}\tr(\begin{pmatrix}
4\Delta^T\Delta\Delta^T\Delta&0\\
0&4\Delta\Delta^T\Delta\Delta^T
\end{pmatrix})=4\|\Delta^{T}\Delta\|_{F}^{2}\leq 4.

∎

Any two points
P,\tilde{P}\in\operatorname{Gr}(n,p)
with
\operatorname{dist}(P,\tilde{P})<\frac{\pi}{2}
can be connected by a unique minimizing
geodesic
(
Bendokat et al. 2024
, Proposition 5.2)
.

Lemma

1
combined with
Lemma

5
gives

\operatorname{dist}(P,\tilde{P})\geq\|P-\tilde{P}\|_{0}\geq\sin(\dist(P,\tilde P)).

(17)

Applying the mean–value inequality
Hall and Newell 1979
, we obtain for
B_{1},B_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{(n-p)\times p}

\norm{\varphi(B_1)-\varphi(B_2)}_{0}\leq\max_{t\in[0,1]}\norm{d\varphi_{(B_2+t(B_1-B_2))}}_{\textnormal{F},0}\norm{B_1-B_2}_{\textnormal{F}}=M\norm{B_1-B_2}_{\textnormal{F}},

with
M=\max_{t\in[0,1]}\norm{d\varphi_{(B_2+t(B_1-B_2))}}_{\textnormal{F},0}
.

With this bound at hand, we can estimate the manifold interpolation error in terms of the interpolation error in local coordinates by combining
Lemma

1
and
Lemma

4

Theorem 2
.

Let
B,\tilde{B}\in\mathbb{R}^{(n-p)\times p}
two points in the coordinate space, which we interprete as true and interpolated data, respectively.
Let
P=\varphi(B),\tilde{P}=\varphi(\tilde{B})
be the corresponding manifold images.
Then

\operatorname{dist}(P,\tilde{P})\leq\arcsin(M\normF{B-\tilde B})=M\norm{B-\tilde B}_{\textnormal{F}}+\mathcal{O}\quantity(\normF{B-\tilde B}^3),

provided
\norm{B-\tilde B}_{\textnormal{F}}<\frac{1}{M}
and
\operatorname{dist}(P,\tilde{P})<\frac{\pi}{2}
. The amplification factor
M

is addressed in
Lemma

4
and is at most
\sqrt{\frac{5}{2}}+1
.

Proof.

The first inequality is a direct consequence of
Lemma

1
combined with the curvature result of
Lemma

5
. A Taylor expansion of
\arcsin
yields the second equality.
∎

Remark 2
.

In particular,

4
Experimental results

We will now present numerical evidence that interpolation in local coordinates can be competitive in terms of the interpolation accuracy when compared to interpolation in Riemannian normal coordinates. As indicated in the previous section we will focus on Lagrange– and Hermite interpolation.

In the experiments, we will interpolate data pairs exclusively, eg. two points and their derivative information. In this special case, Lagrange interpolation in local coordinates becomes linear interpolation and the normal coordinate variant becomes geodesic interpolation.

4.2
Parametric model reduction of a dynamical system

In computational neuroscience, one is interested in modeling the electronic dynamics of neurons, which is applicable to, for example, brain science and prediction of experiments. The FitzHugh–Nagumo model (FN–model)

5
Summary and outlook

We have presented a method for interpolation in local coordinates on the Grassmann manifold, which, as opposed to working in Riemannian normal coordinates and many other common retractions, does not require any matrix decompositions. For the Grassmann manifold, we have devised a strategy for obtaining maximum–volume coordinates. Employing MV coordinates has proven to be highly beneficial for the interpolation accuracy or even strictly necessary, in order to avoid algorithmic breakdowns.

We have conducted a thorough error analysis and quanitfied how errors associated with (Euclidean) interpolation in the local coordinate domain are forward to interpolation errors on the Grassmann manifold. In particular, the error analysis shows that the asymptotic interpolation error is preserved. This means that if the interpolation method in coordinate domain has an asymptotic error
\mathcal{O}(h^{k})
, for step-size
h\to 0
, then same holds for the interpolation error on the manifold.

The focus of this paper is on a comparison of the performance of Riemannian interpolation on
\operatorname{Gr}(n,p)
under different coordinates, not on the interpolation as such.
For simplicity, Lagrange– and Hermite interpolation between only two points was considered. However, it is straightforward to construct higher–order interpolants using data from more than two points, provided that all data can be mapped to the same coordinate domain.
For applications in parametric model-order reduction, it seems reasonable to expect that the dominant information is contained in similar locations across a data set of POD basis matrices, so that maximum–volume coordinates that provide a well-conditioned sub-block for a selected sample matrix, are unlikely to lead to ill-conditioned sub-blocks at the other sample matrices in a given data set.
How to truly generalize the maximum–volume sub-block search to a data set of multiple matrices is an open question.

In the numerical experiments, we have observed that the error that is introduced at the preprocessing stage due to the conditioning of the coordinate charts has a considerable impact on the final interpolation accuracy.
When utilizing the optimized maximum-volume coordinates, the interpolation errors in the numerical experiments were on par with those associated with the Riemannian normal coordinates.

A task for future research is to quantify the conditioning (and associated preprocessing errors) for other Grassmann coordinate charts such as the inverses of common Grassmann retractions.

Riemannian normal coordinates

The Riemannian exponential map with domain on
\T_{P}\operatorname{Gr}(n,p)
sends a tangent vector
X
to the endpoint of the geodesic
c_{P,X}:[0,1]\to\operatorname{Gr}(n,p)
that starts from
P
with velocity
X
. Hence, the exponential provides manifold images for tangent vectors

\operatorname{Exp}_{P}:\T_{P}\operatorname{Gr}(n,p)\to\operatorname{Gr}(n,p),\quad X\mapsto\tilde{P}=c_{P,X}(1).

In general, the Riemannian exponential is not globally defined on the asssociated tangent spaces, but on the Grassmann and Stiefel manifolds it is. These manifolds are goedesicallly complete, thus complete, see
(
do Carmo 1992
, Hopf-Rinow’s Theorem, Chap. 7, Thm 2.8)
.
The Riemannian exponential is locally a diffeomorphism. The inverse map is called the Riemannian logarithm

\operatorname{Log}_{P}:\operatorname{Gr}(n,p)\supset\mathcal{D}\to\T_{P}\operatorname{Gr}(n,p),\quad\tilde{P}\mapsto X=(\operatorname{Exp}_{P})^{-1}(\tilde{P}).

Matrix formulae for both maps are in
Edelman et al. 1998
;
Absil et al. 2004
;
Bendokat et al. 2024
. The collection of Riemannian logarithm maps provides an atlas for the Grassmann manifold, i.e., a collection of coordinate charts. The Riemannian exponential maps are the associated parameterizations. Together, they form the
Riemannian normal coordinates
.
The Riemannian normal coordinates are radially isometric in the sense that the
Riemannian distance between
P
and
\tilde{P}=\operatorname{Exp}_{P}(X)
is exactly
the same as the length of the tangent vector
\|X\|_{0}
,
(
Lee 1997
, Lem. 5.10 & Cor. 6.11)
.
For interpolation purposes,
isometric
coordinates would be ideal, because then the coordinate maps would be free of geometric distortions. However, there can be no full isometry between a flat tangent space and a curved manifold. Therefore, the Riemannian normal coordinates, which are at least radially isometric, can be considered the gold standard for data interpolation on manifolds.

Interpolation in Riemannian normal coordinates

In the following, when considering Lagrange interpolation of a dataset
\quantity{t_i,f_i}_{i=1}^{d}
, we build the
d-1
Lagrange polynomial, and compute the interpolant
\hat{f}(t^{*})
for
t^{*}\in[t_{1},t_{d}]
. For Hermite interpolation, given
t^{*}\in[t_{i},t_{i+1}]
, compute the Hermite interpolant based on the data
(t_{i},f_{i},f_{i}^{\prime}),(t_{i+1},f_{i+1},f_{i+1}^{\prime})
.

7.1
Lagrange interpolation in normal coordinates

Recall that the Lagrange polynomial of order
d-1
passing through data in a vector space
\quantity{(t_i,g_i)}_{i=1}^{d}\subset\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}
is given by

L(t)=\sum_{i=1}^{d}g_{i}\prod_{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle j=1}}{{j\neq i}}}^{d}\frac{t-t_{i}}{t_{i}-t_{j}}.

(19)

Given a dataset on the Grassmann manifold and some
t^{*}\in[t_{1},t_{d}]

\quantity{t_i,f_i}_{i=1}^{d}
, fix
f_{i}
and map the data to the tangent space
\T_{f_{i}}\operatorname{Gr}(n,p)
using the Riemannian logarithm
\Delta_{j}=\operatorname{Log}_{f_{i}}(f_{j})
for each
j
. Letting
\Delta_{j}
play the role of
g_{j}
in (
19
), one can compute the Lagrange interpolant in the tangent space to obtain
L(t^{*})
. The Lagrange interpolant on
\operatorname{Gr}(n,p)
is obtained as
\hat{f}(t^{*})=\operatorname{Exp}_{f_{i}}(L(t^{*}))
.

7.2
Hermite interpolation in normal coordinates

Given a dataset containing point and derivative information
(t_{0},f_{0},f_{0}^{\prime}),(t_{1},f_{1},f_{1}^{\prime})
, we conduct Hermite interpolation in Riemannian normal coordinates, as it was formulated in general and for the Stiefel manifold in
Zimmermann 2020
. The task is to construct a curve in the tangent space with base at either
f_{0}
or
f_{1}
which, when composed with the Riemannian exponential map, is the Hermite interpolant. We select the second data point
f_{1}
as the anchor. It follows from
(
Zimmermann 2020
, Theorem 1)
that the Hermite interpolation curve in the tangent space
\T_{f_{1}}\operatorname{Gr}(n,p)
is given by

\mu(t)=L_{00}(t)\xi_{f_{0}}+L_{10}(t)\cdot 0+L_{01}(t)v_{f_{0}}+L_{11}(t)v_{f_{1}},

where the Hermite coefficient functions are
(
Quarteroni et al. 2007
, Section 8.5)

\displaystyle L_{00}(t)

\displaystyle=\quantity(1-\frac{2}{t_0-t_1}(t-t_0))\quantity(\frac{t-t_1}{t_0-t_1}^2),

\displaystyle L_{10}(t)

\displaystyle=\quantity(1-\frac{2}{t_1-t_0}(t-t_1))\quantity(\frac{t-t_0}{t_1-t_0})^{2},

\displaystyle L_{01}(t)

\displaystyle=(t-t_{0})\quantity(\frac{t-t_1}{t_0-t_1})^{2},

\displaystyle L_{11}(t)

\displaystyle=(t-t_{1})\quantity(\frac{t-t_0}{t_0-t_1})^{2}.

Note that
\mu(t_{1})=0
ensures that
\operatorname{Exp}_{f_{1}}(\mu(t_{1}))=f_{1}
,

\xi_{f_{0}}=\operatorname{Log}_{f_{1}}(f_{0})
,
v_{f_{1}}=f_{1}^{\prime}
and
v_{f_{0}}
is given by

\displaystyle v_{f_{0}}

\displaystyle=\left.\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}s}\right|_{s=0}(\operatorname{Log}_{f_{1}}\circ\operatorname{Exp}_{f_{0}})(sf_{0}^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\frac{(\operatorname{Log}_{f_{1}}\circ\operatorname{Exp}_{f_{0}})(hf_{0}^{\prime})-(\operatorname{Log}_{f_{1}}\circ\operatorname{Exp}_{f_{0}})(-hf_{0}^{\prime})}{2h}+\mathcal{O}(h^{2}).

Finally, the Hermite interpolant is given by
\hat{f}(t^{*})=\operatorname{Exp}_{f_{1}}(\mu(t^{*}))
.

Grassmann subspace distance

Every two subspaces
\mathcal{U},\tilde{\mathcal{U}}\in\operatorname{Gr}(n,p)
can be connected by a shortest (but not necessarily unique) geodesic,
Wong 1967
,
(
Bendokat et al. 2024
, Section 5)
. The Riemannian distance between two subspaces is the length of such a shortest connecting geodesic and
can be computed via the principal angles between
\mathcal{U}
and
\tilde{\mathcal{U}}
.

If
U,\tilde{U}\in\operatorname{St}(n,p)
are column-orthogonal matrices with

\operatorname{span}(U)=\mathcal{U},\operatorname{span}(\tilde{U})=\tilde{\mathcal{U}}
, then the principal angles are

\theta_{k}:=\arccos(\sigma_k)\in[0,\frac{\pi}{2}]
,
where
\sigma_{k}
is the
k
-th singular value of
U^{T}\tilde{U}\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times p}
.
Let
\Theta=(\theta_{1},\ldots,\theta_{p})^{T}\in[0,\frac{\pi}{2}]^{p}
be the vector of principal angles.
Then, the Riemannian subspace distance is

\operatorname{dist}(\mathcal{U},\tilde{\mathcal{U}})=\|\Theta\|_{2}=\sqrt{\sum_{k}\theta_{k}^{2}}\leq\sqrt{p}\frac{\pi}{2}.

(20)
