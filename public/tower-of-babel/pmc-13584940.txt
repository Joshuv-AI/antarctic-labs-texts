Motivating Medical Innovators: A Qualitative COM-B Analysis of Physician-Scientists Engagement in Biomedical Advancement in Chinese Academic Medical Centers
Authors: Wenshuo Zhao, Mingyuan Liu, Ping Sun, Weiyi Ye, Mian Wu, Xin Tian, Youqing Xin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPhysician-scientists connect clinical care with knowledge generation, yet sustained engagement in medical innovation after entering clinical practice remains insufficiently understood. This study explored how motivation, capability, and contextual opportunity shape innovation engagement and how physician-scientists navigate dual clinical and research roles in Chinese academic medical centers.MethodsWe conducted a constructivist exploratory qualitative study involving 27 MD-PhD physician-scientists recruited through purposive sampling from six academic medical centers in Beijing, China, between March 2024 and February 2025. Data were collected through individual semi-structured interviews and analyzed using inductive thematic analysis informed by Braun and Clarke’s framework. The COM-B model was applied as an interpretive framework to examine how capability, opportunity, and motivation shaped innovation behaviors. Conceptual saturation was achieved after analysis of the 27 interviews.ResultsFour interconnected themes emerged: (1) dual professional roles, characterized by dynamic movement between role accumulation and role conflict as physicians negotiated clinical and research responsibilities; (2) evolving motivational mechanisms, including prosocial goals, achievement orientation, status-related aspirations, and impression management; (3) innovation ecosystems, including institutional policies, organizational support, and collaborative networks that enabled or constrained engagement; and (4) capability development, including self-efficacy, resilience, knowledge, and research experience. These findings indicate that physician-scientists’ innovation engagement was shaped by continuous interactions among personal motivation, professional capability, and environmental opportunity rather than by any single determinant.ConclusionThese context-specific findings support coordinated educational and institutional strategies rather than reliance on individual motivation alone. Priorities include formally protected research time, career and evaluation pathways that recognize dual clinical-research contributions, longitudinal mentorship and continuing research training, and transparent interdisciplinary collaboration mechanisms.

Introduction

Medical innovation is central to improving healthcare services, treatment outcomes, and patient health. Physicians who bridge clinical practice and scientific inquiry play a critical role in advancing medical science and innovation.1,2 These professionals, often holding both MD and PhD degrees, integrate clinical duties with research responsibilities.3,4 Although titles vary by country, “physician-scientist” is widely used to describe clinicians with scientific training who contribute to patient care and medical knowledge advancement.3,5 This definition, applied to the growing yet underexplored cohort in China, draws from global standards while reflecting the local healthcare and research context.6,7

Contemporary medical education increasingly recognizes the importance of developing physicians’ scholarly competencies, including the ability to critically appraise evidence, integrate knowledge into clinical practice, and contribute to the advancement of healthcare.8 Within internationally recognized competency frameworks such as CanMEDS, the “Scholar” role highlights physicians’ responsibilities in lifelong learning, evidence-informed practice, knowledge translation, teaching, and contribution to knowledge creation and dissemination.9 Within this broader scholarly framework, physician-scientists represent a specialized group of clinicians whose professional activities extend beyond applying existing knowledge to generating new medical knowledge through scientific inquiry.9 By integrating clinical insights with research expertise, physician-scientists play a critical role in advancing biomedical innovation and translating discoveries into improved patient care.9–11 Despite their importance, the number of physician in the research workforce has declined, and many MD-PhD graduates exit research roles after entering clinical practice.12–14 Similar challenges are observed in China, where expansion of physician-scientist training programs has not fully prevented attrition from research careers, which has been associated with clinical workload, limited funding, financial pressures, and inadequate mentorship.15–17 In response to these challenges, international task forces and professional organizations have proposed strategies to recruit, train, and retain physician-scientists, emphasizing the importance of facilitating transitions from doctoral training to independent research careers and supporting the integration of clinical and research responsibilities.18–25 Despite these efforts, longstanding challenges have become increasingly complex in contemporary healthcare environments, where escalating clinical demands and competing professional responsibilities may constrain sustained engagement in medical innovation.19,26 More recently, changes in healthcare environments have highlighted additional challenges for physician-scientists, including potential constraints on protected research time and reported differences in academic productivity among physician-scientists.27

Prior literature focuses largely on external factors that negatively affect physician-scientists’ engagement in innovation, such as clinical workload, reduced funding, limited role models, and salary gaps.12,28 They provide limited understanding of how physician-scientists experience, navigate, and sustain innovation engagement within their professional contexts. How physician-scientists reconcile the competing demands of clinical practice and research, sustain their commitment to innovation, and draw upon available personal and institutional resources over time remains insufficiently understood.29 Exploring these experiences is essential for revealing the processes that enable physicians to remain engaged in medical innovation despite the practical challenges associated with dual professional roles.

Physician-scientists’ innovation engagement involves the integration of clinical expertise, research capability, and knowledge translation activities.30,31 Sustaining such engagement requires not only individual competence and motivation but also supportive institutional environments, collaborative networks, and opportunities for research development.29 Therefore, innovation engagement represents a complex professional behavior shaped by the dynamic interaction of individual, contextual, and motivational factors.

To better understand the multifaceted determinants of physician-scientists’ innovation engagement, the COM-B model provides a behavioral framework that conceptualizes behavior (B) as the interaction among capability (C), opportunity (O), and motivation (M) (Figure 1).32 This framework is particularly relevant to physician-scientists’ innovation engagement, as such activities require not only research motivation but also adequate scientific capability and supportive professional environments.29 Moreover, COM-B can guide the identification of potential intervention targets, including educational initiatives, organizational support, and policy strategies.32 Previous studies have highlighted the importance of motivation in sustaining research engagement and suggested that motivational processes may evolve throughout career development.33–35 Within the COM-B framework, motivation represents a key component influencing behavior.32 Self-Determination Theory (SDT) provides a complementary perspective by explaining how motivation may develop and be sustained within professional contexts.34 For physician-scientists, motivational processes may be influenced by opportunities to pursue personally meaningful research questions, the development of research-related skills and a sense of competence, and supportive relationships fostered through mentorship and collaborative networks.34,36 These processes occur within broader institutional environments, highlighting the importance of considering motivation alongside capability and opportunity when examining innovation-related behaviors.32 Figure 1COM-B Framework. This study aimed to explore how physician-scientists sustain engagement in medical innovation through the interplay of motivation, capability, and contextual opportunity within Chinese academic medical centers. The study examined how motivational processes, individual capabilities, and institutional contexts shape innovation engagement, and how physician-scientists navigate the intersection of clinical practice and research. By examining these processes, this study aims to provide insights that inform medical education, faculty development, and institutional approaches to fostering sustainable engagement in physician-scientist careers.

Methods

Study Design and Theoretical Orientation

This study adopted a constructivist epistemological orientation and an exploratory qualitative design to investigate how physician-scientists perceive, experience, and sustain engagement in medical innovation within their clinical and academic contexts. Constructivism posits that knowledge and meaning emerge from individual experiences and social interactions, making it suitable for understanding physician-scientists’ subjective realities.37 Data were analyzed inductively to generate themes grounded in participants’ accounts.38 The COM-B framework was subsequently used as a sensitizing framework, while allowing emergent concepts beyond the framework to be identified.32 The reporting of this qualitative study was guided by the Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research (COREQ) guidelines.39

Setting and Participants

The study was conducted in six academic medical centers in Beijing, China, between March 2024 and February 2025. These institutions represent major academic healthcare settings with established clinical research programs and physician-scientist development pathways.

A purposive criterion-based sampling strategy was employed to recruit physician-scientists with diverse professional backgrounds and career stages. Participants were categorized as early-career, mid-career, or established physician-scientists. Career stage was determined by years of clinical and research experience. Invitations were distributed through institutional communication platforms, followed by systematic screening against predefined criteria. Eligible participants met the following criteria: (1) holding a medical degree and currently practicing clinical medicine; (2) having formal research training or research experience; (3) actively participating in biomedical research or medical innovation activities; and (4) providing informed consent. Participants were excluded if they were not physicians, were not engaged in research activities, or were unable to complete the interview.

A total of 30 eligible physician-scientists were invited, and 27 participants completed interviews and were included in the final analysis.

Data Collection

Data were collected through individual semi-structured interviews, a method well suited to exploring participants’ experiences, interpretations, and perspectives on complex phenomena.40 All interviews were conducted by WS, an researcher with training and experience in qualitative interviewing. The interviewer had no direct supervisory or evaluative relationship with participants. To minimize potential power asymmetry, participants were informed that their responses would not influence professional evaluation, and interviews were conducted in private rooms at participants’ institutions to facilitate open discussion and protect confidentiality. Each interview lasted approximately 60–90 minutes.

An interview guide was developed according to the study objectives and informed by the COM-B framework as a sensitizing conceptual lens.32 The questions were designed to elicit participants’ experiences, perceptions, and decision-making processes related to engagement in medical innovation. The interview guide is available in the Supplementary Material. With participants’ consent, all interviews were audio-recorded and transcribed verbatim. Data were collected between March 2024 and February 2025.

Data adequacy was evaluated iteratively during recruitment and analysis.41 Recruitment continued until subsequent interviews contributed limited additional conceptual insights and the research team considered the dataset sufficient to address the study objectives.42 After 27 interviews, the research team determined that sufficient conceptual depth had been achieved because subsequent interviews generated limited additional insights relevant to the research questions.42 Decisions regarding data adequacy were discussed during regular analytic meetings and documented in reflexive notes. Sample adequacy was additionally considered using the concept of information power, which emphasizes that the required sample size depends on the relevance, specificity, and richness of the collected data.41

Data Analysis

Interview transcripts were analyzed using an inductive thematic analysis approach informed by Braun and Clarke’s framework.38 The research team first familiarized themselves with the transcripts through repeated readings and generated initial codes capturing participants’ experiences and perspectives.38 During this phase, coding remained data-driven, with attention to participants’ narratives rather than predetermined theoretical constructs.38

Data were analyzed inductively, with themes developed from participants’ accounts.38 The COM-B framework was subsequently used as a sensitizing framework to interpret the emerging patterns, while allowing additional concepts beyond the framework to emerge.32 COM-B was not applied as an a priori coding framework; instead, it was used during interpretation to examine how capability, opportunity, and motivation influenced physician-scientists’ engagement in medical innovation.

NVivo software was used to facilitate data organization and management. Two researchers independently familiarized themselves with the transcripts and generated initial codes based on participants’ accounts. They subsequently engaged in reflexive discussions to critically examine interpretations, refine conceptual categories, and develop coherent themes. Throughout the analytic process, the team repeatedly returned to the original transcripts to ensure that interpretations remained closely connected to participants’ accounts while generating theoretically informed insights.43 Participant validation of preliminary interpretations was not conducted. Given the constructivist orientation of this study, rigor was addressed through reflexive engagement among researchers, investigator triangulation, and transparent documentation of analytic decisions rather than through seeking confirmation of a single interpretation from participants.

Reflexivity and Rigor

The research team comprised investigators with expertise in medical education, clinical practice, and qualitative research. Recognizing that researchers’ professional experiences and perspectives may shape data interpretation, the team engaged in ongoing reflexive discussions throughout data collection and analysis to critically examine potential assumptions and maintain interpretive transparency.43

The credibility of the findings was supported through investigator triangulation, iterative discussions among team members, reflexive documentation, and systematic recording of analytic decisions throughout the research process.44

Ethics Approval

This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of Beijing Friendship Hospital, Capital Medical University in Beijing, China (Approval Reference Number: 2024-P2-353-01). All participants provided informed consent before participating in the study. They were informed about their right to withdraw at any time without consequences. To protect participant confidentiality, interview transcripts were systematically de-identified prior to analysis. Direct identifiers, including names, workplace details, and contact information, were removed during the transcription process. Potentially identifying contextual information, such as specific specialties, departmental positions, gender, career stage, years of experience, and other unique personal or professional characteristics, was carefully reviewed and modified when necessary to minimize the risk of re-identification. Relevant details were generalized or omitted while preserving the meaning and contextual richness of participants’ accounts.

Results

Twenty-seven physician-scientists participated (12 females, 15 males). All participants had clinical medicine backgrounds and held PhD degrees, with experience in clinical or translational research. Participants’ ages ranged from 30 to 60 (mean: 38.2 years). They were affiliated with six university hospitals in Beijing. They represented various medical specialties and integrative disciplines, spanning both dominant and less common clinical fields within each hospital.34 Fourteen were from internal medicine, seven from surgery, and six from other specialties (eg, dermatology, dentistry, radiology, emergency medicine). Theme 1. Dual Roles of Physician-Scientists

Role Identity

While all participants recognized themselves as physician-scientists, clinical identity remained dominant in most cases. However, this dominance was not simply a personal preference; it was shaped by institutional expectations, professional recognition systems, and the economic structure of clinical practice.

Participants described clinical work as the most visible and rewarded component of their professional role. Several noted that clinical productivity directly influenced departmental evaluation and personal income, whereas research outcomes often required long-term investment with uncertain returns. When the department evaluates my contribution, clinical volume is still the most visible indicator. Publications and innovation projects are important, but they do not immediately translate into departmental benefits.(P08, Male, Mid-career physician-scientist)

When the department evaluates my contribution, clinical volume is still the most visible indicator. Publications and innovation projects are important, but they do not immediately translate into departmental benefits.(P08, Male, Mid-career physician-scientist)

A deviant case was observed among two senior physician-scientists who temporarily transitioned to full-time research positions but later returned to clinical practice because they perceived patient care as central to their professional identity.

Role Accumulation

Clinical practice informed relevant research questions, and research findings improved patient care. Many of my research questions came directly from patients I treated. Without clinical exposure, I would not know which problems truly mattered. (P15, Female, Established physician-scientist)

Many of my research questions came directly from patients I treated. Without clinical exposure, I would not know which problems truly mattered. (P15, Female, Established physician-scientist)

This finding suggests that the dual role can generate a positive feedback loop: clinical experience enhances problem identification, while research participation improves diagnostic reasoning and patient communication.

Role Conflict

Balancing clinical and research roles was challenging, with time constraints and financial factors often pushing physicians toward clinical duties. Role conflict reflected a structural tension between immediate clinical obligations and long-term innovation activities. Participants frequently described a “clinical-first” environment in which revenue-generating clinical activities received greater institutional priority than exploratory research. Some relied on personal time to conduct research, causing fatigue and limiting their capacity to meet performance targets. Collaborations were complicated by authorship and promotion requirements, hindering smooth innovation processes. After completing outpatient clinics and ward responsibilities, research becomes something I do after work. Sometimes I question whether I can maintain this path long term. (P03, Female, Early-career physician-scientist)

After completing outpatient clinics and ward responsibilities, research becomes something I do after work. Sometimes I question whether I can maintain this path long term. (P03, Female, Early-career physician-scientist)

However, negative cases indicated that strong departmental research cultures could partially offset these pressures. Physicians embedded in supportive teams reported that protected research time and mentorship transformed dual roles from competing demands into mutually reinforcing activities. Although my clinical workload is high, our department provides protected research time every week. (P21, Female, Mid-career physician-scientist)

Although my clinical workload is high, our department provides protected research time every week. (P21, Female, Mid-career physician-scientist)

When reflecting on various stages of their careers, all physicians reported experiencing both role accumulation and conflict, with each state shifting depending on external circumstances and personal situations.

Theme 2. Motivational Mechanisms Underlying Innovation Engagement

Motivation was not a fixed personal attribute but an evolving process shaped by professional values, career experiences, and environmental feedback.

Prosocial Motivation

Many physician-scientists were driven by the desire to improve patient outcomes and societal well-being. They leveraged clinical experiences to guide research directions and translate scientific findings into better care. Several physicians also aimed to advance their specialties. Theme 3. Innovation Ecosystems Influencing Opportunities and Constraints

Supportive Policies

Policy guidance, especially related to funding priorities, steered research directions. Government and institutional frameworks influenced which topics physicians pursued, often emphasizing meeting assigned research tasks over translating findings into practice. National funding programs provided opportunities that I would not have had otherwise. (P08, Male, Mid-career physician-scientist)

National funding programs provided opportunities that I would not have had otherwise. (P08, Male, Mid-career physician-scientist)

Organizational Support

Institutional environments determined whether individual motivation could be converted into sustained innovation behavior. Participants highlighted that access to protected research time, cross-disciplinary collaboration, and mentoring support played an important role in fostering environments that encouraged curiosity and resilience. Some centers offered training for grant proposals and “research clinics” for consultation. However, resource imbalances persisted, with limited access to laboratories or equipment in clinical departments. Some participants noted inconsistent implementation of research leave policies. The encouragement from my department director was critical. Without that support, I might have abandoned research. Even the cleverest housewife cannot cook a meal without rice. (P16, Male, Mid-career physician-scientist)

The encouragement from my department director was critical. Without that support, I might have abandoned research. Even the cleverest housewife cannot cook a meal without rice. (P16, Male, Mid-career physician-scientist)

These findings suggest that supportive micro-environments, rather than institutional policies alone, shaped whether physicians could sustain curiosity-driven innovation. Departments that normalized research time, facilitated mentorship, and encouraged interdisciplinary exchange created conditions in which innovation became compatible with clinical responsibilities.

Interpersonal Networks

All participants reported resource shortages, leading them to collaborate with other physicians, researchers, and academics. Early-career physician-scientists often joined projects led by mentors or department heads, while mid-career individuals sought external collaborations for manpower and equipment. Collaborative networks served as compensatory structures that reduced the limitations of individual resources. I secured funding for the project, but I lacked sufficient time and adequate equipment to complete it on my own. Through collaboration, I was able to utilize the personnel and equipment from other institutions within the same team. (P19, Female, Mid-career physician-scientist)

I secured funding for the project, but I lacked sufficient time and adequate equipment to complete it on my own. Through collaboration, I was able to utilize the personnel and equipment from other institutions within the same team. (P19, Female, Mid-career physician-scientist)

Knowledge and Experience

Physician-scientists rely on explicit knowledge from their education and tacit knowledge acquired through mentorship and practice. Some sought more interaction with basic researchers. Choosing research topics and securing funding after PhD training were major challenges. Experienced physician-scientists bridged clinical and basic research, developing a cohesive perspective integrating both fields. Integrative Analysis: Dynamic Interactions Among Motivation, Capability, and Opportunity

Our analysis, aligned with the COM-B framework, revealed how motivation, capacity, and opportunity interact to shape physicians’ innovation behaviors.

Motivation was strongly influenced by personal characteristics and environmental factors. Physicians with high self-efficacy were better able to manage role conflicts and persist in research.

Those in resource-limited environments often experienced diminished motivation due to a lack of support. This highlights how motivation can be constrained by external factors. The lack of support made it harder to stay motivated. (P03, Female, Early-career physician-scientist)

The lack of support made it harder to stay motivated. (P03, Female, Early-career physician-scientist)

Participants described motivation and capability as mutually reinforcing processes. Physicians who remained strongly committed to addressing clinical problems often actively acquired new research skills, whereas successful research experiences further strengthened their confidence and willingness to continue innovation. Motivated physicians were more likely to enhance their self-efficacy and invest in skill development, facilitating better management of their dual roles. Conversely, low motivation was associated with reduced self-efficacy, making it harder to overcome role conflicts. Because I wanted to address clinical problems, I learned new research methods step by step. (P11, Female, Early-career physician-scientist)

Because I wanted to address clinical problems, I learned new research methods step by step. (P11, Female, Early-career physician-scientist)

Opportunity, particularly organizational support and interpersonal networks, played a key role in fostering innovation resilience. Physicians in supportive environments reported greater innovation success. Collaborative networks allowed me to continue my work despite clinical demands. (P20, Female, Mid-career physician-scientist)

Collaborative networks allowed me to continue my work despite clinical demands. (P20, Female, Mid-career physician-scientist)

In contrast, those in environments with fewer resources struggled to maintain motivation and were less resilient to innovation challenges.

The interactions between motivation, capacity, and opportunity were evident, with motivation enhancing capacity in supportive environments, while resource constraints and weak support networks reduced motivation, limiting innovation capacity (Figure 2). Discussion

This study examined the motivations and influencing factors that shape physician-scientists’ engagement in technological innovation. Multiple motivations coexist and can shift throughout their careers and in different contexts, aligning with previous research on motivational changes over time.33,45 Previous research on physician-scientist development has highlighted persistent challenges in recruiting, training, and retaining physicians pursuing research-intensive careers.46,47 Although prior studies have underscored the roles of scientific interest, early research exposure, and structured training pathways in fostering physician-scientist trajectories,47–49 our findings further demonstrate that sustained engagement in innovation is shaped by the dynamic interplay between individual motivations, professional experiences, and institutional contexts. These results extend existing perspectives on physician-scientist workforce development by suggesting that successful participation in research training alone may not translate into long-term innovation engagement after physicians enter clinical practice. While interventions such as extracurricular research programs and structured MD-PhD training pathways have been effective in promoting early research involvement,48,50 maintaining physician-scientist engagement requires continuous support throughout career development.47

Prosocial motivation emerged as a primary driver, consistent with studies showing that many Chinese students initially choose medicine out of a desire to help patients.51 Our findings also highlighted competition, achievement, and impression management as significant motivators, echoing previous literature on factors influencing the decision to pursue a PhD and research careers.33,52,53 These motivations are neither mutually exclusive nor fixed over time. Participants highlighted that their motivations evolved across different career stages and professional contexts, suggesting that physician-scientist development may involve dynamic rather than linear trajectories. This interpretation is consistent with previous studies demonstrating that physician-scientist career development is shaped by evolving professional identities, institutional contexts, and career-stage-specific factors.47,54,55 Sustained engagement in research may further depend on the availability of institutional support, recognition mechanisms, and opportunities to integrate clinical and research responsibilities.47 Impression management emerged as a distinct motivator, shaping behaviors among early-career physicians who seek to establish a positive professional image.

This research reaffirmed that knowledge and skills are crucial for establishing a research career, particularly during the challenging transition from medical to research training.56,57 Physician-scientists who trained under experienced mentors navigated this transition more easily, emphasizing the importance of committed mentorship in guiding early-career researchers.58 However, our findings suggest that maintaining research capability after entering clinical practice remains challenging. As clinical duties increasingly occupy physicians’ time and attention, opportunities for continued learning, methodological refinement, and exposure to emerging scientific developments may be constrained.59 Thus, long-term engagement in research requires not only foundational training but also continuous support and opportunities to preserve and enhance scientific competencies throughout professional development.28

Organizational support was an important external factor shaping innovation engagement. Unlike previous studies that primarily emphasized funding shortages, participants in this research did not identify funding as their predominant barrier. Instead, they described institutional talent programs, seed funding opportunities, and access to competitive grants as important facilitators.60 This difference may reflect the characteristics of our study setting, as participants were recruited from highly resourced academic medical centers in Beijing. In such environments, the availability of research infrastructure may alleviate some financial constraints, whereas organizational factors, including protected research time, mentorship support, and evaluation systems, may become more prominent determinants of research engagement.61,62 Departments may also play an important role through resource allocation and leadership support.63

Interpersonal networks were also critical for sustaining innovation. Innovation communities enabled physicians to access expertise, equipment, and collaborative resources beyond their individual capacity. This finding is consistent with existing literature on strengthening physician-scientist ecosystems, which emphasizes the importance of structured mentorship, interdisciplinary collaboration, and institutional mechanisms that bridge clinical needs and scientific expertise.12,17 However, participants also highlighted intellectual property concerns and unclear benefit allocation as barriers to collaboration. Future institutional strategies should therefore focus not only on creating collaborative opportunities but also on developing transparent mechanisms that support sustainable partnerships.

The findings can be interpreted through the COM-B framework, suggesting that physicians’ innovation behaviors emerge from the interplay between individual capabilities, motivation, and the opportunities embedded within their professional environment.32 Although physicians may have the necessary research expertise and willingness to engage in innovation, these individual factors may not translate into sustained innovative practices without adequate institutional support.64,65 Thus, fostering physician innovation requires not only cultivating individual capacity and motivation but also creating an environment that enables these resources to be effectively translated into practice. While COM-B highlights how institutional constraints restrict the opportunity and capability for innovation,32 self-determination theory provides an additional perspective on their motivational implications.66 Such constraints may influence physicians’ experiences of autonomy, competence, and relatedness, potentially affecting their willingness and ability to sustain innovation engagement.34,67 Because these psychological needs were not directly examined, this interpretation remains theoretical and requires future empirical validation.

Our findings also suggest a potential tension between the formal emphasis on scientific inquiry and the implicit messages conveyed through everyday clinical environments. Although academic medical institutions increasingly recognize the importance of research and innovation, participants described that immediate clinical responsibilities and visible performance expectations could compete with the sustained effort required for innovation.68,69 From a medical education perspective, this may reflect a gap between the values explicitly promoted through research training and the priorities experienced in routine clinical practice.70

Balancing clinical and research roles is challenging. Our study extends the literature by identifying dual dynamics of role accumulation and role conflict.71,72 While role synergy can enhance both clinical practice and research, time constraints, resource gaps, and differences in objectives often lead to conflicts.72 Clinical workloads, regulatory requirements, and electronic health records reduce time for research.73,74 Physician-scientists frequently sacrifice personal time to conduct research and face systemic issues related to compensation, promotion, and authorship.26,75,76

This study’s strengths include a qualitative approach that provides rich, in-depth insights into physician-scientists’ motivations and technology innovation behaviors, moving beyond quantitative measures. Applying the COM-B framework highlighted how personal attributes, organizational resources, and professional networks interact to shape innovation engagement.32 The identification of shifting patterns between role accumulation and role conflict provides additional insight into how physician-scientists manage competing professional responsibilities across different career stages.

Several limitations warrant consideration when interpreting these findings. First, although stratified sampling was employed to capture diversity in career stage and professional background, participants were recruited from six academic medical centers in Beijing. These institutions are characterized by relatively strong research capacity, established academic infrastructure, and promotion systems that emphasize scholarly achievement. Consequently, the perspectives captured in this study may not fully represent physicians working in rural hospitals, non-academic institutions, or healthcare settings with limited research resources. Therefore, the applicability of these findings to other organizational and healthcare contexts requires further evaluation. Second, the institutional environment in which participants practiced may have shaped their experiences and interpretations. As physicians were situated within academic medical centers, their views may have been influenced by institution-specific promotion requirements, expectations for research productivity, and prevailing professional norms within Chinese academic medicine.77 These contextual features should be taken into account when interpreting the reported barriers and facilitators associated with physician-scientist development. Third, the cross-sectional nature of this study limited exploration of how physicians’ motivations, professional identities, and engagement in research may change throughout their careers. Longitudinal investigations are needed to better understand the dynamic process of physician-scientist development. Finally, as with other qualitative studies, interview findings may be subject to recall and social desirability biases. Moreover, although the research team employed reflexive practices, investigator triangulation, and iterative analytic discussions to strengthen rigor, their insider perspectives as clinicians and medical education researchers may have shaped the interpretation and development of themes.

To support physician-scientists, institutional and educational strategies should address both individual development and structural conditions. Future initiatives should establish clearer career pathways that recognize the dual contributions of clinical care and research, rather than relying primarily on individual physicians’ ability to negotiate competing demands. Protected research time should be incorporated into institutional planning and employment expectations to prevent research activities from being consistently displaced by clinical responsibilities. Strengthening mentorship programs, providing continuous research training, and developing collaborative innovation networks may further support long-term engagement. Institutional evaluation systems may need to recognize a broader range of physician-scientist contributions beyond traditional clinical productivity measures, including research advancement, collaborative activities, and innovation-related outcomes. These strategies may help create more sustainable environments for physician-scientists within academic medical settings.

Conclusions

This qualitative study highlights how physician-scientists’ engagement in medical innovation is shaped by the interplay of motivation, capability, and opportunity. Their dual clinical and research roles require a dynamic balance between role accumulation and role conflict, reflecting persistent challenges in integrating clinical responsibilities with innovation activities. To create more sustainable environments, institutions should establish structural supports, including formally protected research time embedded within workload allocation and employment expectations, as well as clearer career pathways that recognize both clinical and research contributions. These findings provide context-specific implications for medical education, faculty development, and institutional policy reform.
