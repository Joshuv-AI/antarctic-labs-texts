The hidden risk: inadvertent pregnancies in random start ovarian stimulation protocols and ovarian hyperstimulation syndrome
Authors: Ayse Seyhan, Bulent Urman, Baris Ata
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo examine the risk of severe ovarian hyperstimulation syndrome (OHSS) when random-start ovarian stimulation (RSOS) is performed during an undetected, spontaneously conceived pregnancy.DesignA case-reportSubjectsA 24-year-old woman with polycystic ovary syndrome, a history of two early pregnancy losses, and 25 months of infertility, who underwent in vitro fertilization (IVF) with RSOS at an external fertility clinic.ExposureThe RSOS begun while an early intrauterine pregnancy was already present but unrecognized.Main Outcome MeasuresOnset and severity of OHSS, assessed by clinical symptoms, serum human chorionic gonadotropin levels, and the need for hospitalization or intervention.ResultsFollowing oocyte retrieval, the patient suffered severe abdominal pain and significant ascites consistent with OHSS and was found to have a concurrent intrauterine pregnancy. Outpatient management failed to control her symptoms, and she required hospitalization and termination of the pregnancy. She conceived naturally six months later and delivered a healthy infant.ConclusionThis case underscores the significant risk of undetected pregnancies during RSOS, which can lead to severe OHSS. It emphasizes the critical need for vigilant monitoring of progesterone and human chorionic gonadotropin levels and the implementation of strategies to detect potential pregnancies before trigger of oocyte maturation. Clinicians must weigh carefully the benefits of flexible stimulation protocols against the risks of unintended pregnancies, especially in patients with a reasonable chance of achieving spontaneous conception.Key Words: Random-start ovarian stimulation, luteal phase stimulation, inadvertent pregnancy, ovarian hyperstimulation syndrome

Traditionally, ovarian stimulation was started in the early follicular phase of the cycle. The recognition of multiple waves of follicle development throughout the menstrual cycle and increasing use of vitrification revolutionized ovarian stimulation for assisted reproductive technology (1). In the absence of an intent for fresh embryo transfer, ovarian stimulation can be started anytime in the menstrual cycle, targeting the active follicular wave at that time. This practice of random start ovarian stimulation (RSOS) has opened a new era, especially for oncofertility patients, because of the urgency in starting cancer treatment following gamete or embryo cryopreservation.

Random start ovarian stimulation was extended gradually to oocyte donors, patients undergoing elective cryopreservation of oocytes or embryos for fertility preservation, and patients using freeze-all protocols, primarily due to planned preimplantation genetic testing for aneuploidy or the risk of ovarian hyperstimulation syndrome (OHSS) (2, 3, 4, 5). Numerous studies have validated that late luteal phase stimulation is as effective as follicular phase stimulation in terms of the number of oocytes retrieved, euploid embryos, and live birth rates (6, 7, 8).

Ovarian hyperstimulation syndrome is a potentially fatal complication of ovarian stimulation and is characterized by increased vascular permeability, leading to fluid extravasation into the pelvic cavity and ascites formation, as well as hemoconcentration and reduced vascular volume. This condition is mediated by vascular endothelial growth factor, which is stimulated by human chorionic gonadotropin (hCG) and correlates with disease severity (9, 10). Physical examination findings can range from abdominal distention and discomfort to severe ascites and massive hydrothorax, while laboratory results may vary in severity, indicating mild hematocrit elevations, severe electrolyte imbalances, hypoproteinemia, and elevated liver enzymes and creatinine levels (11). Ovarian torsion, acute respiratory distress syndrome, renal failure, multiorgan failure, and thromboembolism are specific complications that can occur in the setting of OHSS (12, 13, 14).

Today, we know that the core pathophysiological process of OHSS is mediated either by the luteotropic effect of exogenous hCG used to trigger final oocyte maturation or by endogenous hCG during pregnancy (15). While RSOS provides flexibility, there may be some risk of an unnoticed early pregnancy, which could produce hCG during the stimulation, potentially triggering the pathophysiological cascade leading to OHSS. Recently, two case reports have transformed this theoretical concern into a reality (16). Case report

A 24-year-old female patient sought in vitro fertilization (IVF) treatment at an external fertility clinic. The patient provided consent for this case report to be published. She had a history of two spontaneously conceived early pregnancy losses. She had been trying to conceive for 25 months following the last one. Her medical and surgical history is otherwise unremarkable.

She fulfilled Rotterdam criteria for polycystic ovary syndrome, with oligomenorrhea (menstrual cycles ranging from 35–90 days) and polycystic-appearing ovaries, characterized by an antral follicle count of approximately 30 follicles per ovary, in the absence of clinical or biochemical hyperandrogenism. Additionally, anti-Müllerian hormone (AMH) was measured at 3.8 ng/mL. A three-dimensional ultrasound examination revealed a normal uterine cavity. The semen analysis was normal according to World Health Organization criteria (17).

On day 28 of her cycle, ultrasound showed an endometrial thickness of 5 mm and all follicles <10 mm in size. Baseline serum follicle-stimulating hormone, luteinizing hormone, and estradiol levels at the start of stimulation were not measured at the referring clinic and, therefore, were unavailable for this report. She was stimulated with a fixed gonadotropin-releasing hormone (GnRH) antagonist protocol with 225 IU/day recombinant follicle-stimulating hormone. On day 12 of stimulation, there were 20 follicles >10 mm; of these, 8 follicles measured ≥18 mm, 6 measured 14–17 mm, and 6 measured 10–13 mm. The endometrium was hyperechogenic with a thickness of 15 mm. Serum estradiol and progesterone levels were 4,683 pg/mL and 49.01 n g/mL. A GnRH agonist trigger (triptorelin 0.2 mg, gonapeptyl, Ferring) was given and a total of 11 oocytes were collected 36 hours later. There were 9 mature oocytes, all of which were fertilized successfully, and 2 blastocysts were frozen. Given her high-risk profile for OHSS (polycystic ovarian syndrome [PCOS], high antral follicle count, AMH 3.8 ng/mL, and estradiol 4,683 pg/mL on trigger day), cabergoline 0.5 mg/day was started prophylactically for seven days beginning on the day of trigger, and daily 0.25 mg GnRH antagonist (cetrorelix 0.25 mg/day; Cetrotide, Merck Group) injections were given for three days from the day of oocyte retrieval, before the onset of clinical symptoms or confirmation of pregnancy, reflecting the treating physician's concern about the potential development of OHSS. In this case report, we define postoperative day (POD) as the day or days following oocyte collection.

On POD #4, the patient was evaluated in the fertility clinic with abdominal pain, nausea, and vomiting. Her blood pressure was 110/70 mmHg, heart rate 60 beats per minute, oxygen saturation 99%. A transabdominal ultrasound showed markedly enlarged ovaries and ascites extending to the perihepatic and splenic regions, and a transvaginal scan demonstrated an endometrial thickness of 17 mm. A culdocentesis was performed on POD #4. She was hemodynamically stable, maintained adequate oral intake, and had no respiratory compromise, so a decision was made to manage her as an outpatient. She returned the next day (POD #5) for persistent symptoms and underwent a second culdocentesis. On POD #6 because of progressive abdominal distention, dyspnea, and nausea, she was hospitalized for clinical deterioration and managed for severe OHSS. Laboratory results at admission were as follows: total leucocyte count 21,500 mL, hematocrit 46.9% , creatinine 1.83 mg/dL, creatinine clearance 77 mL/min, sodium 124 mmol/L, potassium 5.4 mmol/L, bilirubin 1.4 mg/dL, aspartate transaminase 43 U/L, alanine transaminase 24 U/L, albumin 2.0g/dL , lactate dehydrogenase 326 U/L (266–500), and serum hCG level was 3,263 mIU/mL. Her serum hCG levels were 3,559 mIU/mL, 3,392 mIU/mL, 2,818 mIU/mL, 3,553 mIU/mL on On PODs #7, #8, #9, and #10, respectively. Follow-up ultrasounds did not show either an intrauterine or extrauterine pregnancy. During her clinical course through POD #9, the patient underwent a total of five culdocenteses — two performed on an outpatient basis (POD #4 and POD# 5) and three during her subsequent hospitalization (PODs #6, 7, 9).Her symptoms did not improve and she was given a methotrexate (MTX) injection with a diagnosis of pregnancy of unknown location. By POD # 11, she had no signs of resolution of OHSS and was transferred to our unit at the American Hospital of Istanbul. The initial ovarian stimulation, oocyte retrieval, and early management of OHSS (including culdocenteses) and treatment of pregnancy of unknown location with MTX administration were performed at another fertility clinic. Our team first became involved in the patient’s care when she was transferred to the American Hospital on POD #11 because of persistent severe OHSS.

On admission, transvaginal ultrasound demonstrated an intrauterine gestational sac measuring 2.8 mm. Both ovaries appeared enlarged with multiple corpora lutea, the largest of which measured 60 × 70 × 55 mm. There was a notable amount of free fluid present in the pelvis, as well as collections in the subphrenic (80 mm) and the subhepatic (120 mm) areas. She was started intravenous crystalloid solution and human albumin. Doses were adjusted on the basis of the patient's intake and output. A prophylactic daily dose 40 mg of enoxaparin also was started.

Given the severe OHSS and exposure to MTX, a joint decision was taken to take additional steps to terminate the pregnancy, which was now confirmed to be an intrauterine gestation. Dilation and curettage was done on the second day of hospitalization which corresponds to POD #12, and three culdocenteses were performed on consecutive days (PODs #12–14) draining a total of 9 L of ascitic fluid to relieve abdominal distension and precipitate diuresis. Histopathologic examination of the curettage specimen subsequently confirmed the diagnosis by demonstrating products of conception composed of chorionic villi and decidual tissue. On POD #14, hCG was measured at 332 mIU/mLL. The patient's clinical and laboratory findings started to improve by the day 6 of hospitalization which coincides with POD #16, and she was discharged to be monitored on an outpatient basis. During the outpatient follow-up, she did not require any further drainage. Six months later she conceived naturally and gave birth to a healthy infant weighing 3,120 grams at 39 weeks. Discussion

To the best of our knowledge, this is the third documented case of an unrecognized pregnancy that occurred during RSOS and subsequently led to severe OHSS. In this case, although no hCG trigger was administered, the patient experienced clinically severe and challenging OHSS. While we cannot definitively establish a causal relationship between OHSS and the incidental pregnancy, it is crucial to emphasize that the onset and severity of OHSS in this patient are associated closely with her pregnancy status.

The patient exhibited several baseline and ovarian stimulation characteristics associated with an increased risk of OHSS, including a diagnosis of PCOS, an antral follicle count >24, AMH >3.4 ng/mL, relatively aggressive stimulation, >17 follicles measuring >10 mm, and an estradiol level >3500 pg/mL on the day of trigger (11). She began to exhibit OHSS symptoms on day 6 after the GnRH agonist trigger; although serum hCG was not measured that day, it was estimated to be approximately 1,600 mIU/mL based on a subsequent value of 3,263 mIU/mL obtained on day 8 after trigger. Her OHSS was unusually severe and prolonged, with symptoms lasting approximately 13 days and requiring drainage of nearly 16,500 mL of ascitic fluid, suggesting that sustained endogenous hCG production may have exacerbated granulosa cell stimulation and intensified the clinical course (18). Unsurprisingly, the patient's condition improved significantly following termination of the pregnancy.

The administration of MTX was a critical decision for this patient, particularly as her symptoms did not improve despite decreasing hCG levels (3,559 mIU/mL on POD #7, 3,392 mIU/mL on POD #8, and 2,818 mIU/mL on POD #9). Although the hCG levels were beyond the discriminatory zone for an intrauterine pregnancy, the ultrasound findings revealed only a homogeneous hyperechogenic endometrium with a thickness of 19 mm, which did not provide definitive evidence of a viable pregnancy. The subsequent decrease in follow-up hCG levels further suggested the likelihood of a nonviable intrauterine pregnancy. However, it is important to note that ruling out ectopic pregnancy was challenging because of the highly stimulated state of the ovaries, each approaching 10 cm in size, which can complicate ultrasound detection of ectopic pregnancies. While mifepristone/misoprostol protocols are commonly preferred for medical abortion, the presence of the potential for an abnormal intrauterine pregnancy and the risk of ectopic pregnancy necessitated the use of MTX in this case. Methotrexate serves as a treatment for ectopic pregnancy and a medication for medical abortion, making it an appropriate choice given the clinical scenario.

Inadvertent pregnancy appears to negatively affect IVF outcomes, particularly regarding oocyte quantity and the development of high-quality embryos. In this case, despite having 20 follicles >10 mm and an estradiol level of 4,683 pg/mL, only 11 oocytes were retrieved, resulting in the development of just 2 blastocysts. The observed quality and quantity of the oocytes suggest suboptimal performance, which may be linked to elevated serum hCG levels during the stimulation process, a phenomenon that has been documented in inadvertent pregnancies during GnRH agonist downregulated IVF cycles (19). An additional theoretical explanation is that not all visible follicles were aspirated successfully at retrieval, which, if present, also could have contributed to the persistence of a high granulosa cell mass and, thus, to the severity of OHSS.

Ovarian stimulation has become more patient-friendly because of the introduction of the RSOS protocols, that allow the patients greater flexibility in scheduling their stimulation cycles. Starting stimulation in the early follicular phase typically is limited to cycle days 2 or 3 of menstruation. In contrast, initiating ovarian stimulation during the late follicular or luteal phase allows for a more flexible start date. Moreover, numerous studies have indicated that ovarian stimulation in the luteal phase is associated with similar if not better clinical outcomes to that of the early follicular phase. A comparable number of oocytes, blastocysts, the euploid blastocyst rate per injected metaphase II (MII) oocyte, ongoing implantation/delivery rate were observed in luteal phase start stimulation when compared with the early follicular phase (20, 21, 22, 23).

One significant consideration for RSOS is the potential for an undetected pregnancy to occur during stimulation, so it may be prudent to avoid luteal stimulation in couples with a likelihood of pregnancy unless there is another justification. This is true especially for women with high ovarian reserve because of the risk of OHSS. On the other hand, in cases of poor ovarian reserve, gonadotropin administration is not expected to cause any harm during pregnancy, aside from the unnecessary stimulation (16, 19).

With any RSOS, it is crucial to have a reliable method or strategy for detecting an unnoticed pregnancy, as inadvertent pregnancy is not an uncommon occurrence. Lawrenz et al. (24) reported an incidence of undetected pregnancy during luteal phase stimulation at 2.1%, on the basis of a comprehensive analysis of 488 stimulation cycles.

Screening for pregnancy before starting gonadotropin treatment in random start protocols may be difficult, as hCG levels may be too low to be detected with standard assays. In the present case, the cycle phase at the time of stimulation could not be reliably assigned on sonographic grounds alone: the baseline ultrasound demonstrated a 5 mm endometrium without a visible corpus luteum, findings that in isolation would be more compatible with the early follicular phase of a prolonged cycle or an anovulatory cycle in the setting of PCOS than with a classical secretory luteal phase. However, retrospective back-calculation from the hCG value of 3,263 mIU/mL on POD 6, corresponding to a gestational age of approximately 5 weeks (LMP-equivalent), or roughly 21 days post-conception, places the estimated date of conception approximately 2–3 days before the start of ovarian stimulation. Therefore, stimulation most likely began in the periovulatory to early luteal window of the conception cycle.

In oligoanovulatory women with PCOS and irregular menses, ovulation is unpredictable and the absolute cycle day is an unreliable surrogate for the true endocrine cycle phase. Therefore, excluding pregnancy before initiating stimulation, regardless of apparent cycle stage, is prudent. This is consistent with the international PCOS guideline recommendation that pregnancy be excluded before ovulation induction therapy; the same principle extends to any pharmacologic ovarian stimulation, for which a prestimulation serum hCG measurement is a simple safeguard (25). In this particular instance, it is important to note that serum hCG levels were not measured. On the other hand, even in clinics that initiate luteal phase stimulation only after confirming negative serum hCG at the start of treatment cycle, 10 patients were reported with an existing, yet undetected natural conception pregnancy during this phase (24). Although hCG screening at the start of ovarian stimulation is not always sufficient or precise, it should still be part of routine assessments. As seen in our case, hypothetical calculations based on the date of the first hCG measurement suggest that the patient's hCG level at the start of ovarian stimulation likely would have been approximately 3–4 mIU/mL. While this result is negative, it is not <0.1 mIU/mL, and such a value could have heightened awareness of a possible unnoticed growing pregnancy during ovarian stimulation.

The hallmark of early luteal regression in a nonfertile cycle is a decline in progesterone production. In contrast, a rise in progesterone during the luteal phase, or the presence of extremely high levels of progesterone on the day of the trigger, raises the possibility of endogenous hCG rescuing the corpus luteum. Although specific progesterone thresholds for follow-up hCG testing are challenging to define, patterns of rising or persistently elevated progesterone can be significant. In instances of undetected natural conception, the median progesterone level at the onset of stimulation is reported to be 12.12 ng/mL (with a range of 7.7–17.5), suggesting that elevated levels might require additional consideration in clinical decision-making (24).

Semrl et al. (16) reported two cases of severe OHSS that occurred because of inadvertent pregnancies during luteal phase stimulation. The two cases involved either high (>60 ng/mL) or rising progesterone levels (0.26ng/mL on start day,2.11 ng/mL day 7, 4.07 ng/mL on day 9, and 4.59 ng/mL on day 10). In our current case, the progesterone level was measured at 49.01 ng/mL on the day of the trigger. In such cases, reflex hCG testing should be performed to confirm or rule out an inadvertent pregnancy. If the patient had been monitored closely with regular progesterone measurements throughout the cycle, rather than relying on a single measurement on trigger day (49.01 ng/mL), it would have been evident that progesterone levels increased progressively. The very high levels of progesterone on the trigger day should have prompted consideration of a potential unnoticed pregnancy; the cycle could have been cancelled during stimulation, and most likely catastrophic severe OHSS could have been prevented. However, the hCG test was scheduled for POD #7, at which point the patient was already exhibiting symptoms of OHSS.

In conclusion, individuals who have a reasonable chance of achieving spontaneous conception, such as younger women, couples with a shorter duration of infertility, or those who have conceived previously without assistance, may be advised to initiate stimulation during the follicular phase (26). Clinics should assess carefully which patients are likely to benefit from RSOS and develop a clear strategy for detecting any unnoticed pregnancies during stimulation. Furthermore, precycle patient counseling is vital; it should explicitly address the need for formal abstinence or contraception is recommended in the weeks leading up to random-start initiation. This discussion is essential, considering the profound emotional and financial ramifications associated with prolonged infertility as well as psychological and physical burden that may arise from the potential need for pregnancy termination because of critically severe OHSS.
