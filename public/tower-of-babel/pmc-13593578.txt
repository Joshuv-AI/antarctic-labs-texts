Medical Student Perspectives on Female Anatomical Representation in CPR Training
Authors: Rhiannon Craig, Alicia Falconer, Hope Stevens, Enam Haque
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundFemales who experience an out-of-hospital cardiac arrest (OHCA) are more likely to die than males in the same scenario, with bystanders often reluctant to perform CPR due to worries or uncertainty around touching/exposing breasts. This study aims to explore the perspectives of UK medical students on female anatomy representation in CPR training, as medical students generally attend multiple CPR training sessions, and this training shapes their future clinical practice.MethodsA Qualtrics questionnaire was formulated, featuring quantitative and qualitative questions. This questionnaire was distributed, via Email and WhatsApp groups, to medical students who are currently studying at UK-based medical schools. Participants were primarily recruited from volunteers of the widening participation charity “Medics and Me”. The questionnaire was closed after two months. Due to the small sample size (N=32), quantitative data was analysed via appropriate methods such as percentage values and measures of central tendency, as well as being statistically analysed. Qualitative data was coded and analysed, independently, then via NVivo. All data was cross-checked by each researcher to enhance analytical robustness.ResultsThe majority of respondents felt, in their experience, that CPR training failed to consider female casualties. Common themes included the use of only flat-chested CPR models and a lack of discussion around providing CPR to female casualties. There was a statistically significant difference between mean confidence performing CPR on a male member of the public (M = 4.5, SD = 0.8) compared with a female member of the public (M = 3.4, SD = 1.3), conditions: Z = 3.862 and p < 0.000002.ConclusionThe findings highlight a perception among medical students that CPR training fails to consider female casualties. This supports a need for further research in this area and ultimately interventions such as incorporation of female-representative CPR models.Keywords: CPR, cardiac arrest, female representation, gender disparity, education

Background

Out of hospital cardiac arrests (OHCA) remain a significant cause of mortality within the UK, with an incidence of more than 40,000 per annum1 and an accompanying survival rate of just under 10%.2 The importance of early cardiopulmonary resuscitation (CPR) and automated external defibrillation (AED) for improving survival rates has been described in the literature since the 1990s, with the introduction of the “chain of survival” concept.3,4 Thus, bystander training plays a fundamental role in OHCA management, with CPR and AED training demonstrated to be linked with increased survival rates.5,6

There is, however, a potential disparity within bystander training when considering male and female casualties. Multiple studies have demonstrated that female casualties are less likely to receive bystander CPR and AED.7–9 This could be due to fear by men of being accused of inappropriately touching or injuring female casualties, socio-cultural fears of removing clothing from female casualties, and bystanders lacking awareness of differences in hand placement and AED placement between males and females.10,11 Acknowledgement of gender-specific differences is key to high quality CPR technique, as metal-containing bras and binders may interfere with AED shock delivery and, similarly, reluctance to expose or touch the breasts may impede AED pad placement or compression depth.12 This failure to consider female casualties may also be explained by a lack of anatomically representative female training mannequins.13

Globally, there is also some evidence to suggest that females experience a higher mortality rate than males following cardiac arrest between the stages of hospital admission and discharge, despite a greater likelihood of experiencing return of spontaneous circulation (ROSC).14,15 The reasons for the increased likelihood of ROSC remain unclear; this finding appears to be most significant in pre-menopausal females, thus one theory commonly discussed in the literature is that oestrogen provides a protective effect, contributing to an initial survival advantage up to the point of hospital admission.16–18 In the context of our research into the perspective of medical students on female anatomical representation in CPR, the gender difference in survival following admission emphasises the importance of addressing gender biases early in medical education, as – whether conscious or unconscious – this could skew future clinical decision making. Furthermore, the aforementioned studies found that females were less likely to be offered life-enhancing interventional procedures, following cardiac arrest, compared to male inpatients. Though further research in this area is indicated to clarify conflicting findings, and the extent to which gender biases contribute to differences in mortality, this highlights that gender disparity in CPR training and provision is part of a wider issue extending into various elements of patient care.

Medical students are a cohort of the population who are likely to receive regular CPR training as part of their course, with an estimated 80% of students completing basic life support training as part of their degree.19 As members of the general public, as well as future healthcare professionals, their views are crucial in assessing whether the quality of CPR and AED training is adequate. The quality of training they receive at this stage will ultimately shape their future clinical practice. Furthermore, this exposure to a variety of CPR training sessions places medical students in a prime position to identify any gaps and to subsequently advocate for change. A thorough literature review was conducted and identified a research gap in the assessment of medical students’ CPR training, and their understanding and perspectives, in terms of female anatomy.

Thus, this study aimed to explore the perspectives of UK medical students, regarding female anatomy representation in their CPR training. The authors hoped the findings would identify any major gaps in knowledge and confidence issues with current training.

Methods

Design

The authors undertook an initial scoping review of the literature, which identified a need for further research in this area. This informed the design of a mixed methods study, comprising an online password-protected Qualtrics questionnaire. The questionnaire mainly consisted of closed-ended questions, with space for free text comments.

Ethics

The study was approved by the University of Manchester Proportionate Review Ethics Committee. (Reference number 2025–22532-41129)

This study complies with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Participants & Recruitment

The inclusion criteria for the study were medical students currently studying at UK-based medical schools, and volunteering for the charity, Medics and Me. This is a national charity that aims to widen participation in Medicine, with focused initiatives to diversify student access and success. It was created in 2020 and comprises over 400 medical student volunteers in 36 UK medical schools. This questionnaire was distributed three times, via Email and WhatsApp groups, to all medical students who volunteered for Medics and Me. The questionnaire was open for respondents between April and July 2025. The authors then widened the sample size through Medics and Me Leads in each of the different medical schools, who invited other medical students in their medical school WhatsApp groups. This opportunistic sampling aimed to yield a sample size of at least 50 respondents.

Data Collection & Storage

Respondents accessed a link in the invitation Email to the questionnaire, reading the participant information sheet and consent form, before pressing a button to give implied consent to undertake the questionnaire. The informed consent included publication of anonymised responses and direct quotations. The anonymous questionnaire responses were collected through Qualtrics, which was password-protected and could only be accessed by members of the research team. The same platform was used to generate the initial results, and the authors then performed quantitative and qualitative analysis of the data. The data was stored on a secure server, managed by the University of Manchester, which only the principal investigator could access. Any analysed data was shared between the authors via a secure Microsoft OneDrive system.

Data Analysis

The questionnaire collected demographic data on gender, ethnicity, and information relating to medical school (year of training, whether undergraduate or postgraduate, whether intercalation offered in degree).

Statistical analysis was conducted using a Wilcoxon signed-rank test. Due to the exploratory nature of the study and relatively small sample size, and to avoid the generation of false or exaggerated outcomes, there was additional analysis using measures of central tendency for the Likert scale questions and percentages of the total cohort were calculated for the true/false‑style closed questions. This allowed for a degree of comparison to be made both between questions and between options within a question, therefore assisting analysis of the data set. Modal values were calculated for each of the five Likert‑scale statements by representing each possible answer with a numerical value (strongly disagree = 1, strongly agree = 5 etc). This same numerical system was then utilised to calculate a mean score for each statement to allow a more direct comparison between statements.

Thematic analysis was used to analyse the free text comments. Thematic analysis can be broadly defined as “a research method used to identify and interpret patterns or themes in a data set” and can “often lead to new insights and understanding”.20 This inductive approach to analysis allowed the themes to develop naturally based on the raw qualitative data.21,22 Inductive analysis was particularly helpful in addressing this area of research, as the medical student perspective had not been previously investigated in this way, and we held no preconceptions regarding the data which may be produced from this questionnaire prior to completion of data collection and analysis. The qualitative free text comments were independently analysed by two of the authors. One author analysed the data without the use of analytic software, and the second author used the qualitative analysis software tool NVivo. NVivo enabled categorisation, labelling and visualisation of qualitative data, facilitating a more accurate analytical process and meaningful data interpretation.23,24 Both authors then reviewed the collected codes and themes, to triangulate the findings, to confirm whether similar codes/themes had been identified. The remaining members of the research team were also consulted to confirm that all were in agreement with the final themes.

Qualitative Analysis

The following themes were generated by qualitative analysis and triangulation of the data:

1. Differences in CPR technique for anatomical females versus males

2. Confidence to provide CPR as a medical student

3. Addressing gender disparity, in CPR training, in undergraduate medical education

Differences in CPR Technique for Anatomical Females versus Males

Question 10

Respondents generally reported a lack (or sometimes absence) of gender-specific information, including those who engaged with multiple CPR teaching sessions throughout their time at medical school.

We have never been told about CPR on a woman’s anatomy. Especially about larger breasts and AED pad placement and need to cut bras! (Participant 50)

We learnt cpr and no additives for if the patient is female. (Participant 44)

Gender differences were discussed primarily through external organisations outside of medical school.

The only significant mentions about the differences for females in medical school have been regarding AED use. The majority of information I have received regarding CPR was in a first aid course outwith the medical school - it was a group of mostly females who asked lots of questions about the differences in CPR for males and females (Participant 33)

I have done a lot of CPR training outside of med school due to volunteering with the ambulance service and even that has not covered the differences. We barely do any CPR training as it is in medical school and making sure that women get the best care should be mandatory. (Participant 46)

Information provided was perceived to be somewhat vague and that attempts at broaching discussion around gender-based differences in CPR training may be met with ambiguous responses, leaving students feeling uncertain.

Removing anything wired discussed. Discussion of doing “anything necessary” in order to deliver appropriate CPR. (Participant 21)

I had the same question during my BSL training and I was told there may not be a huge difference, but the answer wasn’t further explained and I do believe there can be differences based on each individual’s breast tissue? (Participant 37)

I feel I could deliver adequate assistance but it would be better if I were certain. (Participant 20)

I have never practiced on a female manikin. I have been taught on adult male and paediatric manikins, but not on a female. If I was required to do so I would feel able to complete CPR on a female member of the public, but I would be less confident than if I was completing it on a male member of the public. Addressing Gender Disparity in CPR Training in Undergraduate Medical Education

Question 15

Students frequently attributed a lack of understanding and confidence around female CPR technique to having never practiced on a representative female CPR model. It is therefore unsurprising that several students suggested the incorporation of female mannequins as a means of improving the current standard of CPR training and addressing gender disparity in the undergraduate medical curriculum.

Male and female mannequins, as well as going over defib placements, bra [removal/difficulties], and hand placement for effective compressions in patients with larger breasts (Participant 20)

As I said, they should absolutely address anatomical differences. None of the mannequins have breasts and they should address the fact that a lot of women have very large breasts and practice so that people do not feel uncomfortable. (Participant 46)

Many of the students expressed that more teaching on female-specific CPR techniques would be a helpful way to improve the quality of CPR training in undergraduate medical education.

Female manikins, education surrounding female CPR given the same weighting as the information about male CPR - rather than having the female information being an intentional add-on to the predominantly male assumption. (Participant 1)

Acknowledging the fact that there is differences and teaching us about them. Having different mannequins of varying sizes available for teaching would also make a huge difference. (Participant 19)

The results suggest that medical students would welcome more discussion around amending the CPR approach for female casualties, as 81% of respondents reported that gender-specific differences were never discussed during their CPR training session(s) at medical school.

Less afraid to openly discuss the differences surrounding breasts and defibrillator placements and CPR administration (Participant 6)

Acknowledging the fact that there is disparities instead of just brushing over the topic. (Participant 19)

Question 17

Several students expressed concern that this lack of female representation in their education could contribute to a reduced quality of care for female patients. Gender disparity in the broader curriculum would need to be explored separately, and on a larger scale, in order to obtain a clearer insight into this issue.

I feel that for some examinations, we are not taught what to do if the subject is a woman, regardless of the examination. For example, in DRE training, we are taught with a male manikin, which is due to females not having prostates, but we do not experience how this difference in anatomy feels to the person examining. In abdominal exams, the manikins are male, and I feel this causes embarrassment for male medical students, in particular when they get on wards with elderly or large breasted women, as they do not know how to respectfully ask a patient to move their breast, or how to do so themselves in an appropriate manner - or feel awkward doing so. With a chest exam, not much attention is given to whether they may be differences in auscultation or percussive ability in large breasted women, and again, the manikins are male. (Participant 1)

It’s been recognised in a lot of clinical data that classical symptom variation between males and females (eg heart attack), and that the female presentation isn’t massively addressed, as well as gender disparity as a whole between symptomology and most effective managements (Participant 12)

Discussion

This study aimed to explore medical student perceptions of the quality of their CPR training, particularly in relation to performing CPR on female casualties. This was achieved through a questionnaire exploring their knowledge and confidence in performing CPR on female casualties.

It was concerning that only 44% of students were able to correctly identify how CPR differed between male and female casualties. In the free text comments, the respondents attributed this to a lack of acknowledgement of female anatomy and key gender-specific considerations (such as undergarment removal and hand positioning) in CPR training sessions. Students incorrectly stated that hand placement makes no difference to chest compression depth when more breast tissue is present. Though the compression depth itself remains the same at 5–6 cm, the literature suggests that larger breasts make it more challenging to achieve a hand placement that procures this depth.10,25 This concern regarding breast tissue was again reiterated throughout the open‑response questions, as some participants expressed that, although they would be comfortable to perform CPR on a female casualty by utilising their training, they would be concerned that large breasts would contribute to reduced effectiveness of chest compressions. This was commonly correlated to learning on only flat-chested CPR models.

63% of students failed to recognise the reluctance of the general public to perform CPR on female casualties, due to the risk of inappropriate exposure or touching.26 A recent study suggested this was due to a lack of confidence and knowledge regarding CPR on female casualties, with a third of males saying they would be concerned about being accused of “inappropriate touching” if they were to provide CPR to a female.27 Medical students in this study did not share this reluctance, but lacked confidence in performing CPR on female casualties when compared to male casualties (mean scores of 3.4 vs 4.5 out of 5). This corroborates a study looking at gender‑based differences in the outcomes of CPR. It found that females were less likely to receive bystander resuscitation than their male counterparts (49.1% vs 54.9%).14 Therefore, females who experience OHCA inherently face a greater risk of poor outcomes than males, including mortality, due to hesitance or absolute reluctance to commence life-saving CPR.

OHCA is unfortunately just one of several examples of gender discrimination and bias in health care. For example, females face a greater risk of poor outcome from cardiovascular disease, which is commonly portrayed as male-predominant despite the risk of developing cardiovascular disease being similar between males and females.28 Recent research has also documented the effects of menopause on cardiovascular disease.29,30 This highlights a need to acknowledge gender-specific contributions to clinical presentation and management. Furthermore, female patients presenting with health concerns (such as chronic pain) are more likely to be dismissed under the assumption that the primary issue is psychological, contrary to males who are perceived as more stoic and thus more credible.31–34 Gender is an independent determinant of health and must be acknowledged in order to establish rapport with female patients and improve health outcomes.

One way of addressing gender inequality in CPR provision, and outcome, is by openly discussing gender-specific differences during CPR training sessions. Our study explored the perspective of medical students who have participated in one or more CPR/BLS training sessions. The qualitative and quantitative data produced by this study illustrate a discrepancy in what students expect from their teaching compared with what they are receiving, with respondents frequently expressing a need for improvement. This raises questions around the perspective of CPR educators regarding gender-specific differences, and whether they are aware that omitting to discuss these differences appears to be negatively impacting students. However, there does not appear to be recent research exploring these issues. We also acknowledge that some medical schools may outsource their CPR training and thus have little control over the content of sessions, or equipment used (including representative female CPR models), but again this appears to be an under-researched topic. We therefore propose that further research – such as qualitative interviews with CPR instructors or interventional studies assessing the impact of introducing representative female CPR models, through affordable adaptations – could identify potential barriers restricting the supply of high-quality, inclusive CPR training of medical students.

This discussion comes at a time when NHS England has recently removed advanced life support training from the Foundation programme list of competencies, making high quality BLS and ALS training at medical school more important than ever.35 A 2019 study found that BLS knowledge amongst medical students was poor, with only 10% of medical students who responded (from 21 UK medical schools) able to answer all of the MCQ’s correctly.19 Furthermore, 81% of the students would like more BLS training in the curriculum. A separate study assessing BLS/ALS awareness amongst final year medical students reiterated a need for mandatory, high quality BLS/ALS training at regular intervals throughout medical education.36 Seven years on from this European study - featuring 99 universities from 14 different countries - it appears that this remains an area of significant concern, as the respondents of our questionnaire expressed a similar desire for more BLS training and quality improvement.

The lack of anatomically female mannequins in CPR training was a common theme in the free text responses and correlated directly with the lack of confidence expressed by a number of the medical students. Male mannequins account for between 55–75% of those used in CPR training across a number of organisations.37,38 This lack of representation acts as a direct barrier to building confidence providing CPR to individuals with female anatomy, raising awareness of differences in techniques, and could correlate with the lower survival rates observed amongst female casualties who require CPR.7 Within this critique it is important to acknowledge that there are factors that influence medical schools’ ability to provide their students with diverse mannequins. The most obvious of these is the cost impact of buying new equipment to include mannequins with female anatomy.39 However, there are more affordable ways of achieving this goal, with numerous options available to modify existing equipment, or the possibility for alterations to be made by the medical schools themselves.37,40,41 There is also the possibility of utilising other forms of training, such as case-based discussions centred around gender differences, or use of other supplementary training materials to bridge the gap between the current inadequate equipment and successful gender‑specific CPR training.

Several of the students expressed that the lack of acknowledgement of females in CPR training was just one example of a larger, systemic problem, as described by Arsever et al.42 This study found that clinical vignettes featuring females typically portrayed outdated gender stereotypes despite there being equal representation of males and females. This further highlights the importance of accurate CPR training for female anatomy, as it is a symptom of a greater systemic issue. This gender bias within the medical curriculum could negatively impact the care of females by depicting a very one-dimensional view of female health, not considering the varied ways they may present when experiencing a health concern, such as OHCA, and instead placing emphasis on stereotypes and assumed behaviours.

Strengths and Limitations

One particular strength of this study was the inclusion of mixed methodology. Mixed methodology enables researchers to compare the quantitative and qualitative data in complement, as opposed to separately.43 It also enables complex issues to be explored from different angles,44 as well as provide methodological triangulation.45 Crucially, this can minimise the risk of bias and facilitate a more in-depth exploration of the proposed research question.

Another research strategy implemented as part of a dynamic analytical process was investigator triangulation. This approach was utilised in both the qualitative and quantitative analyses for this study to reduce bias and improve the reliability and validity of the findings.46

However, the authors acknowledge the small sample size, with only 32 participants completing the questionnaire in full, making it difficult to generalise findings. The demographics of participants also did not reflect national data, with 81% of participants being female, compared to the 60% cohort in UK medical schools.47 This presents a risk of non-response bias, as female respondents likely possess a greater awareness of gender discrimination in medicine and may be more inclined to express these opinions to drive change for female patients. In order to truly reflect the extent of this issue, future research should include a representative sample of medical students featuring an equal gender distribution. However, as this study was exploratory in nature, its aim was not to draw conclusions that could be directly applied to all medical students, but rather to highlight a gap in teaching that could be identified even with a smaller sample.

We also acknowledge that the recruitment methods carried a risk of selection bias, as the majority of respondents were volunteers of the charity “Medics and Me”. As this charity advocates for broadening access to medicine, and with many of the volunteers being medical students from widening participation backgrounds, this cohort is likely to have a better awareness of topics associated with exclusivity, which may include pre-existing knowledge of gender discrimination in CPR training. Although we did attempt to recruit from a broader population of medical students (via university WhatsApp groups and societies), the majority of respondents were active members of Medics and Me, and it would be challenging to determine the number of non-member respondents from the data obtained.

Finally, a further minor limitation was that, as this was an exploratory pilot study, the Qualtrics questionnaire was not externally validated prior to distribution. The questionnaire was developed by three of the researchers, and the face and content validity was checked by the final researcher who is an expert in medical education. Ideally, this assessment of validity would have been conducted by an external expert in the field of CPR training specifically.

Conclusion

This study highlighted a potential lack of adequate training and resources to perform CPR on female casualties in UK medical schools. Students suggested that representative female mannequins could improve understanding and confidence, alongside open discussion around relative gender-specific differences in technique. Changes may be challenging to implement for financial or practical reasons. However, change can begin with CPR trainers acknowledging gender differences and utilising gender‑sensitive resources. Future studies should explore how low-cost interventions such as mannequin modifications and targeted discussion impact student confidence when performing CPR on anatomical females.
