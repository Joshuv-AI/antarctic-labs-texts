Tuning porosity of macroporous hydrogels enables rapid rates of stress relaxation and promotes cell expansion and migration
Authors: Bryan A. Nerger, Kirti Kashyap, Brendan T. Deveney, Junzhe Lou, Blake F. Hanan, Qi Liu, Andrew Khalil, Tenzin Lungjangwa, Maria Cheriyan, Anupam Gupta, Rudolf Jaenisch, David A. Weitz, L. Mahadevan, David J. Mooney
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
SignificanceHydrogels that exhibit rapid rates of stress relaxation typically have limited long-term mechanical stability in aqueous environments. We demonstrate that tuning the porosity of high molecular weight macroporous alginate hydrogels enables rapid rates of stress relaxation and mechanical stability in cell culture medium. Macroporous alginate hydrogels may be broadly relevant for in vitro culture models of developing tissues or for materials-based cell therapies.

Hydrogels offer a promising biomaterial platform for enabling tissue engineering strategies to repair or replace damaged tissues. In addition to stiffness, the time-dependent mechanical properties of hydrogels, denoted by viscoelasticity, broadly regulate cell behaviors such as migration (1), differentiation (2), and proliferation (3). Alginate (1, 4), hyaluronic acid-collagen (5), and dynamic poly(ethylene glycol) (PEG) (6), among others (7), have emerged as versatile hydrogels that can approximate the time-dependent mechanical behavior of tissues. However, the rate of stress relaxation in these hydrogels can be significantly slower than developing tissues such as the embryonic kidney (4) and mature tissues such as the brain (8), liver (9), and adipose (10). While approaches such as conjugated PEG spacers (2), dynamic covalent crosslinks (11), and molecular weight (MW) reduction (2, 12) can increase the rate of stress relaxation, hydrogels with rapid rates of stress relaxation are typically unstable when used to culture cells for long periods of time due to the limited stability of low MW polymers selected (4), limitations of the crosslinking chemistry (13), or degradability of the material via cell-secreted proteases. We hypothesized that the rate of stress relaxation of hydrogels could be accelerated without sacrificing mechanical stability by changing the hydrogel architecture of high MW alginate hydrogels.

A variety of hydrogel scaffold architectures have been investigated in order to balance the demands of mechanical stability and mass transport. Porous scaffolds generated using freezing (14, 15), foaming (16, 17), microfluidics (18), porogen leaching (19, 20), or phase separation (21), among many other techniques, have shown the ability to provide sufficient mechanical stability while minimizing restrictions on mass transport and encouraging cell migration and tissue growth. While the stiffness of porous scaffolds can be readily tuned to mimic physiological stiffness in vivo, the time-dependent viscoelastic properties of macroporous hydrogel scaffolds remain less studied. Macroporous hydrogels have been shown to mimic the viscoelastic properties of brain tissue, but the impact of hydrogel architecture on mechanical properties was not systematically studied (22). Understanding the relationship between porosity and viscoelasticity would enable more accurate mechanical matching between scaffolds and target biological tissues and inform the design of hydrogel architectures.

Here, we generate ionically crosslinked macroporous alginate hydrogels in order to investigate the relationship between hydrogel architecture and viscoelasticity. We demonstrate that tuning porosity in macroporous hydrogels can accelerate the rate of stress relaxation by an order of magnitude as compared to bulk alginate hydrogels. Consistent with computational predictions, encapsulation of single cells and multicellular spheroids reveals enhanced cell expansion and migration in the macroporous alginate hydrogels relative to cells encapsulated in bulk hydrogels. Together, these data reveal the connection between the bulk architecture and time-dependent mechanical properties of macroporous alginate hydrogels and inform design principles for engineering porous scaffolds for in vitro culture models and materials-based cell therapies.

Results

Rapid Generation of Macroporous Alginate Hydrogels Using Syringe Mixing.

We first generated a protocol for the rapid fabrication of macroporous alginate hydrogel scaffolds. Using syringe mixing, air pockets were introduced into alginate hydrogels during the early stages of ionic (Ca2+) crosslinking (Fig. 1A). Bovine serum albumin (BSA) was added to stabilize the resulting macroporous alginate hydrogel structure during mixing and gelation. After gelation, air was removed from the hydrogel pores and replaced with cell culture medium using vacuum degassing (Fig. 1A). The resulting macroporous alginate hydrogels contain a uniform distribution of liquid-filled pores (Fig. 1 B–D and SI Appendix, Fig. S1) with an average pore diameter of ~75 μm (Fig. 1E) and a broad distribution of pore geometries (SI Appendix, Fig. S1). To ensure mechanical integrity as well as a consistent distribution of pore size and geometry, scaffolds were typically generated with a 30% volume fraction of porosity and 1% BSA (SI Appendix, Fig. S2). Further increasing the porosity or reducing the BSA concentration resulted in reduced stability of the macroporous structure before the alginate hydrogel gelation was complete (SI Appendix, Fig. S2).

Porosity Enhances the Rate of Stress Relaxation in Macroporous Alginate Hydrogels.

We next investigated the impact of porosity on the mechanical properties of macroporous alginate hydrogels with liquid-filled or air-filled pores (Fig. 2A). Hydrogel stiffness was evaluated with measurements of Young’s modulus. Viscoelasticity was evaluated by measuring the half-time of relaxation, which represents the time required to relax 50% of the stress in a stress relaxation test. As compared to bulk alginate hydrogels, macroporous hydrogels with air- or liquid-filled pores had a significantly lower Young’s modulus (Fig. 2B). Stress relaxation tests revealed that macroporous hydrogels were more viscoelastic with relaxation half times significantly smaller than bulk hydrogels (Fig. 2 C and D). Consistent with these results, rheological analysis revealed that macroporous hydrogels have a significantly higher loss tangent as compared to bulk hydrogels (SI Appendix, Fig. S3). In addition, the increased rate of stress relaxation observed in macroporous hydrogels was observed for hydrogels fabricated with both low and high MW alginate (Fig. 2C). Moreover, the Young’s modulus and rapid rate of stress relaxation observed in macroporous hydrogels were maintained with only small changes after a 1-wk immersion in culture medium (SI Appendix, Fig. S4) despite rapid loss of BSA from the hydrogels (SI Appendix, Fig. S5). By comparison, the Young’s modulus of bulk low MW alginate hydrogels containing conjugated PEG spacers decreased by ~50% after immersion in culture medium for 1 wk (SI Appendix, Fig. S4). Together, these data demonstrate that macroporous hydrogels with 30% porosity are significantly softer and relax stress significantly faster as compared to bulk hydrogels.

Analysis of the half-time of relaxation revealed that macroporous hydrogels with liquid-filled pores have a faster rate of relaxation relative to hydrogels with air-filled pores (Fig. 2D). This effect was more pronounced for hydrogels with high MW alginate and suggests that poroelasticity, which describes energy dissipation as a result of fluid flow through the hydrogel network (23), may contribute to the mechanical properties observed in macroporous hydrogels. To decouple the role of hydrogel architecture and poroelasticity on the rate of stress relaxation, we next generated interstitial alginate hydrogels that consisted of bulk hydrogels with embedded microparticles instead of porosity. Similar to macroporous hydrogels, interstitial hydrogels had a significant increase in the rate of stress relaxation relative to bulk hydrogels (SI Appendix, Fig. S6). The half-time of interstitial hydrogels was comparable to macroporous hydrogels with empty pores and an order of magnitude slower as compared to macroporous hydrogels with filled pores. These data suggest that the rapid rates of stress relaxation observed in macroporous alginate hydrogels with filled pores arise from both modified hydrogel architecture and poroelasticity.

Hydrogel Porosity and BSA Concentration Affect Stiffness and Viscoelasticity.

We next sought to understand how porosity and BSA concentration impact the stiffness and viscoelasticity of alginate hydrogels. To evaluate the role of porosity, hydrogels were fabricated with varying volume fractions of porosity up to 30%. Higher volume fractions of porosity resulted in a decreased Young’s modulus (Fig. 3A) and an increased rate of stress relaxation (Fig. 3 B and C). We also found that BSA had a similar effect on hydrogel mechanics in the absence of porosity, wherein increasing concentrations of BSA resulted in a decreased Young’s modulus and an increased rate of stress relaxation (Fig. 3 D–F). Reducing the alginate concentration in bulk hydrogels that contain BSA attenuated the effect of BSA on hydrogel stress relaxation, which suggests that interactions between alginate chains and BSA mediate the effect of BSA on the rate of stress relaxation (SI Appendix, Fig. S7). Together, these data demonstrate that the increased rates of stress relaxation observed in the macroporous alginate hydrogels are determined by the volume fraction of porosity and the BSA concentration.

Macroporous Hydrogels Promote Cell Expansion and Migration in 3D Culture.

To evaluate the impact of the macroporous hydrogel architecture on cell behavior in 3D culture, we next incorporated cells into bulk and macroporous alginate hydrogels. MDA-MB-231 human breast cancer cells and HEK293T cells were chosen for encapsulation experiments based on the ability of these cells to rapidly migrate and form spheroids (24, 25).

To begin, a single-cell suspension of MDA-MB-231 human breast cancer cells was encapsulated within bulk and macroporous alginate hydrogels. All hydrogels used for cell encapsulation were functionalized with arginine–glycine–aspartic acid (RGD) ligands to promote integrin-mediated adhesion. After 7 d in culture, the viability and circularity of cells encapsulated in bulk or macroporous hydrogels were similar (SI Appendix, Fig. S8). We observed a significantly higher cell density in macroporous hydrogels as compared to bulk hydrogels (SI Appendix, Fig. S8). These data demonstrate that macroporous hydrogel architecture supports 3D cell culture and improves cell expansion relative to that in bulk hydrogels.

We next encapsulated spheroids, consisting of HEK293T cells with stable expression of the fluorescent FastFUCCI cell cycle reporter, into bulk and macroporous alginate hydrogels. After 7 d in culture, we observed a significant increase in the area and decrease in the circularity for spheroids cultured in macroporous hydrogels as compared to those in bulk hydrogels (Fig. 4 A and B). We observed similar differences in area and circularity of MDA-MB-231 human breast cancer spheroids encapsulated in bulk and macroporous hydrogels (SI Appendix, Fig. S9). In addition to changes in spheroid morphology, we also observed a higher fraction of cells in the G1 phase of the cell cycle in spheroids encapsulated in macroporous hydrogels as compared to those in bulk hydrogels (Fig. 4 C and D).

Computational Model of Tissue Morphogenesis in Macroporous Alginate Hydrogels.

To comprehensively investigate the interplay between hydrogel architecture, viscoelasticity, and cell behavior, we developed a particle-based computational model (Materials and methods) adapted from a previously published model (1). The cells of the tissue and beads of the nonporous matrix consist of soft elastic spheres of radius “a.” The cells are active (26) and proliferate with time in favorable conditions, whereas the matrix beads are passive and have an interaction that takes viscoelasticity into account. To account for the porous nature of the matrix, beads of size a were introduced. These porous beads, which are randomly placed within the matrix, have only steric repulsion from the nonporous beads of the matrix and the cells of the tissue. To better mimic the structural complexity of the hydrogels under consideration, we introduced porosity into the viscoelastic matrix by randomly placing the beads and varying levels of porosity volume fractions (Φ) (Fig. 5A). We assume that the inertial effects are negligible and they show overdamped dynamics. By controlling the friction coefficients μt (spheroid viscosity) and μm (hydrogel matrix viscosity), we control the effective viscosity of the tissue and the matrix.

Consistent with experimental results, simulations predicted increased spheroid volume and reduced sphericity in porous matrices as compared to bulk viscoelastic matrices (Fig. 5 B and C). As expected, the fraction of migratory cells (Fig. 5D) and the distance that cells migrate increase with increasing volume fractions of porosity (Fig. 5E). Moreover, a phase diagram, color coded based on the sphericity of encapsulated cell spheroids, predicted the combinations of matrix viscoelasticity and porosity required to modulate cell migration (Fig. 5F). These data are generally consistent with experimental data for HEK293T spheroids encapsulated in bulk or macroporous alginate hydrogels with varying levels of porosity (Fig. 5 G–J). Together, these results predict that small volume fractions of porosity (≤30%) can considerably impact the behavior of encapsulated cell spheroids. The impact of small volume fractions of porosity over short timescales suggests that porosity does not only impact cell behavior by providing open spaces for growth and migration but also alters the mechanical properties of the matrix in a way that impacts the behavior of cells that are not directly in contact with pores. This prediction is consistent with experimental observations of spheroids in bulk hydrogels with sparsely distributed pores, wherein cell migration and growth are observed in the direction of nearby pores (SI Appendix, Fig. S10).

Discussion

In this study, we assessed the mechanical properties of macroporous alginate hydrogels with tunable porosity. Our data revealed that macroporous hydrogels have a significantly faster rate of stress relaxation as compared to that of bulk hydrogels. The mechanical properties of the macroporous hydrogels depend on the volume fraction of pores, the concentration of BSA used to stabilize the macroporous structure during hydrogel gelation, and the MW of the alginate polymer. Encapsulation of cells revealed increased cell expansion and migration in macroporous hydrogels as compared to cells in bulk hydrogels. Finally, a computational model generated a first principle–based framework for predicting the impact of hydrogel porosity and viscoelasticity on the morphogenesis of encapsulated cell spheroids.

Macroporous alginate hydrogels were fabricated using a syringe mixing process that enabled the total porosity to be precisely tuned. Consistent with prior studies of porous hydrogels generated using syringe mixing (27), the average pore diameter in macroporous alginate hydrogels was less than 100 μm. Moreover, the exponential decay of pore size frequency observed for larger pores is similar to the pore size distributions reported previously (28) for foams generated using syringe mixing. While the total porosity achieved was limited to 30% in this study, increasing the weight percent of the alginate polymer can enable the fabrication of hydrogels with higher levels of porosity (16).

Bulk compression testing of macroporous hydrogels revealed a significant decrease in the Young’s modulus and an increase in the rate of stress relaxation relative to bulk hydrogels. These findings are consistent with prior studies that show the incorporation of porosity can significantly decrease the Young’s modulus of the hydrogel (29). The rapid stress relaxation rate was observed for both low and high MW alginate, which is advantageous due to the increased stability of high MW alginate under culture conditions (4). Rapid rates of stress relaxation were obtained at very small strains (10%) with the macroporous hydrogels described in this study. The rates of stress relaxation reported here are similar to fast-relaxing colloidal gels (30), and they can be achieved at comparatively lower strains that are still within the range of tissue-applied strain (31). In terms of gel stability, the gel mechanical properties were stable for 7 d in culture, which represents a significant improvement as compared to hydrogels that use PEG incorporation to enhance relaxation rates (32).

Both porosity and BSA concentration impacted the rate of stress relaxation of alginate hydrogels. Consistent with our experiments conducted with interstitial alginate hydrogels, modifying the architecture of the hydrogel by incorporating porosity increased the rate of stress relaxation when porosity reached 30% of the total volume. While BSA was added to stabilize the macroporous hydrogel architecture during gelation, it unexpectedly increased the rate of stress relaxation. The role of BSA in accelerating stress relaxation remains unclear, but it might sterically interfere with the crosslinking of alginate polymer chains and therefore enhance stress relaxation in a manner that is similar to the role of conjugated PEG spacers in alginate hydrogels (2). The different mechanisms by which BSA and porosity impact stress relaxation provide multiple avenues for designing hydrogels with fast rates of stress relaxation.

Despite brief exposure to a vacuum during hydrogel degassing, cell viability remained high and encapsulated cells expanded and migrated within a 7-d culture period. These results are consistent with prior findings that show that temporary exposure to a weak vacuum does not have a critical impact on cell viability or proliferation (33). However, exposure to a high-vacuum chamber has been shown to impact membrane permeability and cell adhesion (34). Based on these findings, we anticipate that macroporous hydrogels could be broadly compatible with cell types beyond those chosen in this study provided that the shear forces from mixing and brief vacuum exposure in the protocol are not detrimental to the cell viability or behavior.

Both single cells and spheroids were successfully encapsulated into macroporous alginate hydrogels. While single cells could be directly encapsulated by addition to the liquid alginate before syringe mixing, spheroids were added in between layers of cast macroporous hydrogel in order to prevent damage to the spheroid during syringe mixing. For single-cell encapsulations, the higher cell number observed in porous hydrogels as compared to bulk hydrogels is consistent with previous reports showing the dependence of cell number on scaffold porosity (35). For spheroid encapsulations, the increased fraction of cells in the G1 phase of the cell cycle in macroporous alginate hydrogels is consistent with the increased cell migration observed in macroporous hydrogels as previous studies have shown cells are more migratory in the G1 phase of the cell cycle (36). Our findings are also consistent with prior studies that show spheroid expansion is greater in porous hydrogels (37).

In this study, cells were initially encapsulated in the continuous phase of the hydrogel in order to maximize the cellular response to hydrogel viscoelasticity. However, encapsulated cells also grew into the porous spaces of the hydrogel, which suggests that cell behavior in macroporous hydrogels is driven by cellular responses to both the mechanical properties and the porous architecture of the hydrogel. Given that pore size is known to broadly impact cell behavior in porous materials (35, 38–40), further investigation into the relative roles of hydrogel viscoelasticity and architecture on cell behavior will be required to expand the use of macroporous hydrogels to new biological applications. To that end, the computational model presented in this study provides a first principle–based framework for decoupling and systematically studying the impact of matrix viscoelasticity and porosity on cell growth and migration.

In conclusion, this study reveals the link between hydrogel architecture and stress relaxation in the context of macroporous alginate hydrogels. These findings demonstrate that hydrogel architecture can impact stress relaxation over a broad range of alginate MWs and therefore overcome the limitations of mechanical stability associated with previous approaches for enhancing stress relaxation. Looking forward, macroporous alginate hydrogels with rapid rates of stress relaxation may enable studies about the role of matrix viscoelasticity in tissue development or in materials-based cell therapies. Moreover, it is likely that engineered porosity can be used to tune the stress relaxation of hydrogel systems beyond ionically crosslinked alginate, provided that the gelation rate is rapid enough to stabilize the macroporous structure generated during syringe mixing.

Materials and Methods

Alginate Preparation.

Sodium alginate (Protanal LF10/60; FMC) with an average MW of ~135 kDa was used for experiments. Low MW alginate (~44 kDa) was obtained via irradiation of LF10/60 alginate with a 3mrad cobalt source. Very low MW alginate (~35 kDa) consisted of LF20/40 alginate (FMC Biopolymer) irradiated with a 8mrad cobalt source and conjugated with RGD and PEG-amine (5 kDa, Laysan Bio) as described and characterized in a prior study (2). Unless stated otherwise, all experiments were conducted using alginate with a MW of 135 kDa. High and low MW alginates consisted of 65 to 75% α-L-guluronic acid and 25 to 35% β-D-mannuronic acid monomers. Prior to use in experiments, alginate was treated with 0.5 g of activated charcoal (C9157, Sigma-Aldrich)/g alginate for 30 min with constant stirring. After allowing the charcoal to gravity settle at room temperature, alginate was sterile filtered (0.22 μm), frozen at −20 °C, lyophilized, and stored at −20 °C. Experimental details for the functionalization of alginate with RGD (theoretical degree of substitution = 20) using carbodiimide chemistry, which was required for single-cell and spheroid encapsulation experiments, have been described in detail in prior publications (4, 41).

Alginate Characterization with NMR.

The degree of RGD modification on alginate was measured by proton NMR (1H NMR, D2O, 400 MHz) spectroscopy (SI Appendix, Fig. S11) following a previously reported method with minor modifications (42). Briefly, potassium hydrogen phthalate (KHP) was used as the internal standard (δ at 7.50 ppm) at a concentration of 0.05 mM in D2O. Alginate-RGD of different MWs were dissolved respectively in 1 mL D2O containing 0.05 mM KHP for NMR characterization. The concentration of RGD in the sample was quantified by correlating the integration of the peak at 1.6 ppm (arginine structure, 2H) to the integration of the peak at 7.5 ppm (KHP, 2H for each peak). The degree of RGD modification on alginate was calculated according to their fraction concentration and MW. On average, the number of RGD peptides on each alginate polymer is 18 for alginate of MW 135 kDa and 5.2 for alginate of MW 44 kDa.

Macroporous Alginate Hydrogels.

Lyophilized alginate was dissolved overnight (>12 h) at room temperature (23 °C) in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM)/F-12 culture medium (11320033, Thermo Fisher Scientific) at a concentration of 25 mg/mL. To encourage rapid dissolution, samples were stored on a test tube shaker (Multi Reax; Heidolph). Hydrogels were generated using a syringe mixing process with 1 mL Luer-Lok syringes (309628, BD) and a female Luer thread-style coupler. Dissolved alginate and air were introduced into one syringe while calcium sulfate dihydrate (C3771, Sigma-Aldrich), DMEM/F-12 culture medium, and BSA (10735078001; Roche) were added to the other syringe. The syringes were coupled and then mixed rapidly until a stable macroporous hydrogel structure was generated (~15 s) (Movie S1). The resulting hydrogel was rapidly cast onto glass plates [described in prior protocol (43)] and then incubated at 37 °C for 1 h to complete gelation (Movie S1). After gelation, hydrogels were immersed in 1 mL of DMEM/F-12 culture medium and exposed to a vacuum (−0.7 bar) for ~2 min using a benchtop vacuum desiccator in order to replace the air in the pores with culture medium (SI Appendix, Fig. S12). Unless stated otherwise, all macroporous hydrogels were 2 mm thick and were generated with 30% by volume porosity and 1% BSA. Alginate hydrogel formulations are reported in SI Appendix, Table S1.

Interstitial Alginate Hydrogels.

Poly(methyl methacrylate) microparticles (27 to 32 μm diameter; Cospheric) or core-shell microparticles (described below) were incorporated at a volume fraction of ~63% into alginate dissolved in culture medium prior to the syringe mixing process and crosslinking.

Core-Shell Microparticle Fabrication.

Oil-in-water core-shell polymeric microparticles were generated using a polydimethylsiloxane microfluidic drop-maker (44). The channels of the device were plasma-treated and flushed with a polyvinyl alcohol (PVA) solution to render the channel hydrophilic (45). In forming oil-in-water droplets, an outer aqueous phase containing 0.5 wt% PVA with a MW of 13 to 23 kDa and an inner oil phase of HFE-7500 fluorinated oil with a fluorinated polymer dispersed within it were used. The flow rate of the aqueous and oil phases in the device were adjusted to generate stable droplets. When the PVA and fluorinated polymer meet at the interface of the droplet they form covalent crosslinks. The interaction between the polymers at the interface generates an elastic interfacial film on the surface of the droplet.

Parallel Plate Compression of Alginate Hydrogels.

Data were acquired using a parallel plate mechanical tester (model 3220-ES Series III, Bose). All alginate hydrogels were cast to a thickness of 2 mm and incubated for 1 h at 37 °C before mechanical analysis. To prevent dehydration during testing, 100 μL of DMEM/F-12 culture medium were added on top of the hydrogel after it was placed on the bottom plate of the mechanical tester. Prior to the acquisition of data, ~1 g of preloading was applied to the hydrogel. Next, the sample was compressed to 10% strain over 2 s. The applied strain was then held constant for 300 s in order to measure the stress relaxation of the sample. All mechanical testing was performed at room temperature.

Rheological Measurements.

Data were acquired using a stress-controlled rheometer (Discovery HR-3, TA Instruments) and a 20-mm parallel plate. Alginate hydrogels were mixed with the calcium sulfate crosslinker to initiate gelation, immediately cast onto glass plates, and transferred to an incubator at 37 °C for 1 h for gelation. Samples were next transferred to the Peltier plate, and the 20-mm parallel plate was lowered to achieve a 2 mm gap. Excess sample was trimmed, and 5 cSt silicone oil (317667, Sigma-Aldrich) was added around the perimeter of the sample to prevent water evaporation during the test. Shear loss and storage moduli were acquired at 1% strain and 1 Hz. All measurements were conducted at 25 °C.

BSA Release from Alginate Hydrogels.

BSA release was assessed in macroporous alginate hydrogels cast to a thickness of 2 mm, incubated for 1 h at 37 °C, and immersed in 1 mL of DMEM/F-12 for degassing as above. Samples were incubated in the degassing medium at 37 °C for 5 d. On day 5, medium was collected and replaced with 1 mL of DMEM/F-12. On day 7, medium was collected, and the remaining hydrogels were dissociated with 9.09 mM ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA) (E6758, Sigma-Aldrich). All samples were immediately frozen at −80 °C after collection. Samples were thawed on ice and assayed using a Pierce Micro BCA Kit (23235, Thermo Fisher Scientific) to determine protein concentration. Samples were serially diluted in PBS or PBS with 0.4545 mM EDTA for released or dissociated samples. Protein concentration was determined across all dilutions, and the recommended amount of working reagent was added and incubated at 60 °C for 60 min in a thermocycler and transferred to black-walled, clear-bottom plates (3881, Corning). Absorbance was read at 562 nm on a plate reader (Synergy H1, Biotek Instruments). The final concentration was calculated using a standard curve with known concentrations of BSA from each plate, respectively. Total protein was calculated from the concentration and the measured sample volumes before assaying.

Fluorescence Microscopy.

Images of fluorescent hydrogels and cells were obtained using a Zeiss LSM 710 laser scanning confocal microscope equipped with a 5× air objective or 10× water-immersion objective. Whole-hydrogel imaging was accomplished using the built-in tile acquisition feature with 15% overlap between adjacent tiles.

Cell Culture.

MDA-MB-231 (American Type Culture Collection) and HEK293T cell cycle reporter (see below) (46) cells were cultured in DMEM/F-12 medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (26140079, Thermo Fisher Scientific). Spheroids were generated by seeding 5,000 cells per well of ultralow attachment round bottom 96-well microplate (7007, Corning). 9.5 μg/mL of rat tail type I collagen (354249, Corning) was added to the cell suspension prior to seeding in order to promote cell aggregation. After seeding cells, the plate was centrifuged at 200 × g for 2 min, rotated 180°, and centrifuged again for 2 min. After centrifugation, cells were incubated for a minimum of 48 h to allow spheroids to form. Medium changes were performed every 3 to 4 d, and the incubator was maintained at 37 °C and 5% CO2.

Cell Cycle Reporter Cell Line.

To generate stable expression of the FastFUCCI reporter, DNA fragments encoding the monomeric Kusabira Orange 2-human CDT1 fusion and monomeric Azami Green-human Geminin fusion were synthesized (IDT). The hCDT1 represented amino acids 30 to 120, the hGEM represented amino acids 1 to 110, and the fusion proteins were separated via a T2A chysel sequence to allow for expression from a single CAGGS promoter. The synthesized construct was cloned into an AAVS1-homology donor vector previously published (47) following restriction with XbaI and MluI digest of the targeting vector and inclusion of homology arms on the synthesized reporter construct using HiFi DNA Assembly (New England Biolabs). The AAVS1-CAGGs-FastFUCCI donor template vector was introduced in HEK293T human embryonic kidney cells along with an AAVS1-targeting Cas9 PX458 (#48138-Addgene) containing the sgRNA sequence: GGGGCCACTAGGGACAGGAT plasmid using nucleofection at ratio of 4:1 according to the manufacturer’s specifications (Lonza 4D Nucleofector). The cells were selected using puromycin driven by the endogenous AAVS1 transcription via a splice acceptor and 2A chysel sequence upstream of the puromycin cassette in the AAVS1-homology donor selector. Cells were then expanded and further enriched for reporter expression using fluorescence-activated cell sorting (FACS). Cells were frozen immediately after FACS enrichment, banked, and thawed for use in experiments.

Cell Encapsulation.

Single-cell suspensions were encapsulated using a Luer-Lok syringe mixing approach as described above in the macroporous alginate hydrogels section of the methods. To encapsulate single cells, cell suspensions were added to the syringe with alginate prior to mixing (SI Appendix, Fig. S8). To ensure consistent seeding densities in both bulk and macroporous hydrogels, the ratio of the number of cells to the volume of liquid alginate before gelation (~146 cells/µL) was maintained constant between conditions. For spheroids, a modified protocol was used to avoid breaking apart the spheroids during encapsulation. First, a layer of macroporous alginate was cast onto the bottom of a culture dish. Spheroids were then immediately placed on top of the alginate hydrogel and a second layer of macroporous alginate was cast on top of the spheroids to complete the encapsulation (SI Appendix, Fig. S9).

Cell Viability Analysis.

The viability of encapsulated cells was determined using a live/dead cell viability kit (L3224; Invitrogen). After 7 d of encapsulated culture, cell-laden hydrogels were immersed in a staining solution consisting of 2 µM calcein AM and 4 µM ethidium homodimer-1 for 30 min before fluorescence images were acquired.

Quantification of Hydrogel Porosity.

Porosity was assessed in degassed hydrogels by incorporating 2 MDa fluorescein isothiocyanate–dextran (FD2000S; Sigma-Aldrich) into the culture medium that hydrogels were immersed in prior to imaging. The average pore size was measured using the Analyze Particles feature in ImageJ.

Quantification of Force/Stress Relaxation.

The relaxation time of alginate hydrogels was evaluated using the half-time of relaxation. The half-time represents the time required to dissipate 50% of the initial force or stress observed during a stress relaxation mechanical test. The half-time was quantified as the time at which the normalized force curve first reached a value less than 0.5.

Quantification of Spheroid Area and Circularity.

The area and circularity [Eq. 1; area (A) and perimeter (P)] of spheroids were quantified using ImageJ. The area per field of view is reported for spheroids due to the extent of cell migration observed in macroporous hydrogels.[1]C=4πAP2.

Computational Model.

To model a cell spheroid embedded in a viscoelastic porous extracellular matrix (ECM), soft interacting spherical particles of radius a subjected to forces with appropriate Langevin dynamics were used. Similar to prior work, the spherical particles representing the cells are motile and proliferating, while those forming the ECM are nonmotile and nonproliferating (1). To make the matrix porous, two types of beads were used; the first type of bead represents the nonporous part of the matrix, and the second type of bead represents the porous part of the matrix. These beads are classified based on their interaction forces. Henceforth, the beads forming the nonporous part of the matrix will be referred to as nonporous beads and beads forming the porous part of the matrix as porous beads.

1. Properties of cells and their interaction forces:

1.1. Cell activity: Cells are considered active and move randomly, similar to the Brownian particles in a thermal bath, except that movement is caused by the cell’s active nature rather than the environment’s temperature (48, 49).

1.2. Cell division: A cell can divide if it satisfies the following constraints: First, if it is older than a free growth-rate time scale τg, and second, if it is energetically favorable (50, 51). For cell division in the model, the Markov chain Monte Carlo approach and the Metropolis–Hastings algorithm are used to determine the energetically favorable divisions (52). At each time step, a cell is randomly selected and its age is checked. If the cell is older than τg, it can divide. The new cell will take up space adjacent to the old cell and have an angle selected at random from a uniform distribution over [0 to 2π]. 3.3. Initial setup for the simulation: For the initial setup, particles were put inside a sphere with a radius of 4a (initially 79 particles). These particles are randomly distributed and tightly packed. Now, beads are arranged surrounding the sphere in a closed-packed structure until a radius of 8a. Therefore, the beads form a closed annular sphere with an inner radius of 5a and an outer radius of 8a. This annular region consists of 1,613 tightly packed beads. For making a few beads as porous beads, based on porosity level, some beads are randomly selected as porous beads.

The tissue properties remain fixed for all simulations. These properties include the tissue viscosity μt=10, motility parameter M=0.8, and the timescale to add one new cell in the absence of stress, τg=1. For ECM, the elastic coefficient is fixed, G′ = 50. The ratio of viscous friction for porous and nonporous beads is fixed to 1:30. The porosity level, ϕ, is varied from 0 to 30% and the matrix viscosity, μnm, is varied such that the viscosity ratio, μ=μt/μnm varies from 0.0067 to 0.04.

4. Simulations for generating a phase diagram:

To explore the effect of porosity on the tissue morphology, simulations were started from the no porosity case and with high matrix viscosity, μ = 0.0067, after time t=300τg, at which point the tissue is spherically symmetric. Keeping all other parameters constant, the porosity (ϕ) was varied from 0 to 30%. As porosity increases, there is a breaking of spherical symmetry in the tissue, leading to the formation of protrusions. Since the porous part of the matrix does not have restoring forces, they can be easily displaced due to the pressure applied by the growing tissue, and hence, there is a nonuniformity in the growth direction of the tissue.

Further, the matrix viscosity is decreased, thereby increasing the viscosity ratio and varying the matrix relaxation time τm=μnm/G′. The tissue growth is observed for the same set of porosity. Decreasing matrix viscosity increases the cell proliferation rate, and hence cell flux j increases, j=τg/τt (where τt is the time it takes to add one cell to the tissue). The tissue maintains the spherical structure for the no porosity case but leads to symmetry breaking at lower porosity levels with decreasing μnm. Hence, a phase space plot characterized by the viscosity ratio and porosity level is developed.
