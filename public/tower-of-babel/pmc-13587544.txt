The forest as a health-promoting place—the meaning for seniors of playing padel in the forest: a qualitative study
Authors: Susanne Knutsson, Johan Bergh, Ulrica Hörberg
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeThere is evidence of a clear connection between health and exposure to nature; however, many older people today miss out on the opportunities offered by the forest. The purpose of this study is therefore to describe and understand the meaning for seniors (aged 65 years or older) of playing padel at a court located in the forest.MethodInterviews with eight seniors who had played padel in the forest. The methodological and interpretation framework was based on Gadamer’s hermeneutic philosophy.ResultsThe seniors described that they experienced joy, calmness, connection with nature, meaningful social interaction, and enhanced wellbeing when playing padel in the forest. Four themes with subthemes were identified: Playing padel in the forest brings out the ability to see and feel the underlying beauty in hitting a ball; brings out the ability to actually see and appreciate the existence of the forest; gives a sense of an existing concealed natural healing power in the forest; and gives a sense of belonging.DiscussionThere is a mutual dependence between activity and place, and playing padel in the forest reveals an awareness of one’s own basic values, the values that give meaning to life, which can constitute motives for one's own, others and the forest’s health and wellbeing, as nature and humans are seen as a wholeness. These values and motives form one's energy source for health-promoting actions.Keywords: Seniors, padel, caring science, caritas, forest, health, wellbeing

Introduction

Of the approximately 10.5 million people who live in Sweden today, 13.8 per cent are 65 years of age or older, and this proportion is expected to increase significantly in the coming years. Eighty-five per cent of Sweden’s population lives in cities (Ekonomifakta, 2025), where toxic environmental exposure is a major public health issue (Lelieveld et al., 2015). We know that there are clear connections between health and exposure to nature (Baek et al., 2022; Bowler et al., 2010; Gascon et al., 2015; Haluza et al., 2014; James et al., 2015; Oh et al., 2017; Park et al., 2024; Seymour, 2016). However, many older people today have lost contact with the forest and thus also miss out on the opportunities it offers. Access to everyday nature is important and provides opportunities for physical activity, recreation, rest and recovery, as well as an increased understanding of nature. Increased exposure to nature leads to improved mental health, increased physical activity and reduced mortality. Being able to move outdoors and experiencing major social changes often affects the most vulnerable in the population, such as the elderly (Vos et al., 2015). The positive impact of nature-based interventions on the health of the elderly has also been researched and highlighted by Irvine et al. (2024), Pasanen et al. (2024), and Tong et al. (2025a), who critically examine existing NBIs and their associated health outcomes, with a focus on identifying the environmental qualities and pathways that are either facilitating or impeding. For users, Catissi et al. (2024) have developed an evidence map showcasing nature-based interventions targeting older individuals' health and wellbeing, and Spiegel et al. (2025) provides user-centred insights into how inclusive forest design can support the physical, emotional, and social wellbeing of older adults.

Modern urban living is associated with a sedentary lifestyle and chronic stress, which contribute to an increase in non-communicable diseases, such as diabetes, obesity and depression (Vos et al., 2015). Many of these diseases can be prevented by investing in environments for physical activity and recreation (World Health Organisation (WHO), 2025). The positive health benefits of spending time in the forest include improvements to immune system function (increased natural killer cells/cancer prevention), cardiovascular system function (decreased hypertension/coronary artery disease, reduced heart rate and blood pressure), respiratory system function (lowered allergies and respiratory diseases), depression and anxiety (decreased mood disorders and stress), mental relaxation (decreased Attention-Deficit/Hyperactivity Disorder) and human feelings of “reverence” (increased gratefulness and selflessness). The body’s calm and quiet system is activated when one spends time in the forest, and research has shown that an attractive forest (Hannerz et al., 2016) and VOCs (volatile organic compounds) in the forest (Antonelli et al., 2020) have an impact on wellbeing and health. Bengtsson and Grahn (2014) argue that there is a connection (Supportive Environment Theory, SET) between stress and nature, and describe supportive factors for wellbeing, such as environmental and social aspects. Grahn et al. (2021) describe that the experience of calm and connection is associated with a reduction in stress levels and with being able to develop health and coping skills. They stress that this experience provides a possible role for the oxytocinergic system in acting as a physiological mediator for health-promoting effects in humans caused by nature (Grahn et al., 2021). Different values ​​that forest environments can offer people such as virginity and mystery, forest feeling, freedom and space, species richness and nature education, cultural history, activity and challenge, service and togetherness (Swedish Forestry Board & [Skogsstyrelsen], 2015) have been described.

A focus on the elderly is important owing to the high prevalence later in life of chronic disorders, including multimorbidity (Boyd et al., 2005), as well as experiences of loneliness, anxiety and depression, and limited mobility (The Public Health Agency of Sweden, 2026). In Sweden, the Social Services Act aims to make social services more preventive, accessible and knowledge-based (National Board of Health & Welfare, 2025), and scientific research focusing on older people in these areas is therefore important. Some research has been conducted on older people in relation to the forest and activities (Baek et al., 2022; Kabisch et al., 2017; Park et al., 2024; Ramanpong et al., 2025) and to Shinrin-Yoku, “forest bathing” or healing forest walks; however, most of these studies take a quantitative perspective. Playing padel, tennis, or squash are activities that appeal to all generations. Padel, however, is a dynamic mix of tennis and squash and is especially suitable for seniors since it is less physically demanding and can increase physical fitness, such as improving cardiovascular health, strengthening muscles (legs, core) and maintaining coordination and balance, which are essential for preventing falls. Playing padel can also lead to mental stimulation, social well-being and stress reduction (Racket-trip, 2026). Scientific research has shown that regular padel practice can improve physical fitness, promote health (Sánchez-Alcaraz & Courel-Ibáñez, 2022) and potentially develop motor and cognitive skills (Pradas et al., 2021).

Beginning padel practice requires no high technical skills and can be played outdoors, which makes it a powerful tool for health promotion and since padel is played massively in recreational environments, it might play an important role in promoting physical habits among children and adults (Sánchez-Alcaraz & Courel-Ibáñez, 2022). Quantitative studies about the effect of physical activity for seniors have been carried out; however, qualitative studies in this subject are rare, and no scientific studies have been found that describe seniors’ own experiences of outdoor padel and how they experience playing in the forest. There is also a lack of knowledge about older people's experiences of being in the forest doing a physical activity, such as padel, integrated with a caring science perspective. Such knowledge could lead to new insights regarding the value for seniors of being in the forest performing a physical activity such as padel, and for their health and wellbeing as a wholeness.

Theoretical framework

This study takes a humanistic perspective and is grounded in caring science, in which the human being is seen as a wholeness, with physical (body), mental (soul), spiritual (spirit), social (meaningful context, interdependence in relation to others) and cultural (environment) dimensions and in which each person is unique and lives in an inescapable situation of mutual dependence on others (Bergbom et al., 2021; Eriksson, 2018; Løgstrup, 1994, 1997). The human is seen as free, with an ability to make decisions regarding different choices, but also with the responsibility to do good (Kierkegaard, 1843). Health is defined as wholeness and holiness, and an ontological aspect of health is emphasised, with the human seen as an inseparable being comprising a body, soul and spirit. To be healthy is to be whole and to feel whole. Health is seen as relative, a movement, individual and multidimensional: soundness, freshness and wellbeing. Soundness is related to one’s pre-understanding, rules of life and previous experiences, while freshness is related to one’s physical characteristics, absence of disease and objective. Wellbeing is related to the person’s life world and expresses a feeling, something that is valuable; it is about the person’s inner experiences, which are therefore unique and individual. A person’s wellbeing involves what a certain individual experiences as good or less good, based on that person’s self-interest, private life, work life, environment and leisure and other contexts, as well as physically, mentally, sexually, financially or otherwise (Bergbom et al., 2021; Eriksson, 2018). Deeper knowledge and understanding of the humanistic triad—human, nature and spiritual forces—are based on the idea that ethics precedes ontology, forming the foundation for an ethos, an ethical stance, and a value base (Eriksson & Lindström, 2003).

Aim

The aim of this study is to describe and understand the meaning for seniors (aged 65 years or older) of playing padel at a court located in the forest.

Method

Design

A qualitative, inductive, descriptive and interpretive design with a hermeneutic approach guided by Gadamer (2013) was used, as we wanted to understand the meaning of playing padel in the forest. The significance of Gadamer’s hermeneutics is the ontological focus (being) and the capacity to interpret human understanding. The SRQR (O'Brien et al., 2014) reporting guideline was used to enhance the quality and transparency of the data.

Participants

In the autumn of 2024, all eight seniors (4 women and 4 men), aged 65–80 years, with an average age of 72 years, and who had played padel outside in the forest from April to September 2024, once a week, were invited to participate in the study. It can be seen as a total sample size, and in qualitative research, the richness and variation in the narratives have more focus than the number of participants (Gadamer, 2013). All of them agreed to participate. Two of the participants had done regular weekly physical activities before, and all eight had prior experience of forests or outdoor walks. All eight had one year’s experience of playing padel inside, and three had also played outside. Prior to this, they had not played padel at all. Two live in the countryside, and some had seen each other before, but none of them moved in the same social circles.

Data collection

All eight seniors who had played padel outside in the forest during the summer of 2024 were informed by the first author, while they were playing padel inside at the beginning of September, about the study, the aim and the voluntariness to participate. The seniors received the information about the outdoor padel court through friends and an information sheet that was at the indoor padel court, and a bulletin board at the city library. The first author has been the new half of them since before and had seen them playing in the inside padel hall and outside at the padel court in the forest. The seniors received oral and written information at the time and were thereafter given consideration time and agreed to participate by mail or phone within a few days. The first author decided the time and place of the interview, which was conducted in a private space in connection with the padel court outside in the forest. At the time of the interview, the seniors provided signed informed consent, after which the last author conducted the interview in accordance with Gadamer (2013) conversation approach. The last author had never met the participants before, had never been at the padel court and had never played padel. The conversation started with the open question “Tell me about what it means for you to play padel in the forest?” Questions such as “Tell me more?” and “How do you mean?” were asked to clarify and to understand the seniors’ experiences. The conversation contained both questions and answers, with the senior talking more freely about their experiences. All the interviews were conducted within the space of 6 h, each lasting approximately 30–45 min, and the seniors played padel both before and after the interviews. The interviews gave rich descriptions of the seniors' experiences of playing outdoor padel in the forest. The interviews were audio-recorded and then transcribed verbatim.

Data analysis

The methodological and interpretation framework is based on Gadamer (2013) hermeneutic philosophy: what another human being says means understanding something and not re-experiencing the other human’s experiences. However, what is said must be understood based on its time context, meaning that it can be understood in another way later. The ability to understand does not require knowledge of rules or methods since we are all part of history, historical awareness and tradition, which is a positive condition for our knowledge and understanding and is important for our interpretation. However, our existing knowledge and understanding may also blind us if we do not recognize our own prejudices (Gadamer, 2013). The authors of this study are nurses, foresters, forest owners and padel players with experience in healthcare and of being in the forest, and the interviews, the analysis, and the final interpretation are understood in light of these prejudices and the theoretical framework or horizon. The interpretation/understanding in this paper is existential (Ödman, 1992), as we focus on what it meant to participants to play padel in the forest. Gadamer (2013) argues that a text needs to have absolute priority and must be understood on its own terms, which calls for sincerity and openness; its meaning is what it says about the “thing” (Gadamer, 2013). The text from all the interviews/conversations was read through separately several times by the first and last authors to gain an initial understanding. A dialogue was then conducted with the text to search for meaning, which involves searching for basic patterns in the text, with questions being posed to it as well as its answers and telling. Examples of such questions are: “What does it mean for the senior to play padel in the forest?” “What is the meaning?​​​​​” “How does it feel?” In a meaningful dialogue, humans’ understanding horizons are conveyed through language and questions generated both by the text and by the researchers (Gadamer, 2000). Movement back and forth between parts of the text and the whole text was ongoing until the questions were answered. These answers were then understood in light of the theoretical framework or horizon. Patterns emerged, and units of meaning, which described what it meant for the seniors to play padel in the forest, were identified. Units of meaning with similar content were then grouped into subthemes and thereafter into themes and the main theme (Table I).

The framework and the authors’ previous experience of playing padel and being in the forest are used in the hermeneutic spiral from pre-understanding to understanding to new pre-understanding throughout the analytical process. Something new and something old merge, and a fusion of horizons occurs (Gadamer, 2013). Reflexive discussions between all the authors were carried out throughout the whole analytic process. Some small differences in interpretations were seen and discussed; assumptions were thoroughly challenged throughout the coding process, and alternative explanations in all coding steps were considered before reaching consensus. All three authors analysed and read the final understanding, and a consensus among the authors was reached.

Ethical considerations

The seniors received both written and verbal information about the study, confidentiality and its voluntary nature and were given time to ask questions and reflect before providing informed consent. To assure confidentiality, the taped and transcribed interviews are kept in a safety deposit box, and no list of names or other identification exists. The result is anonymous, and the quotes are put as numbers. Ethical issues were considered based on the principles of the World Medical Association (WMA) (2024). Ethical approval was obtained from the Swedish Research Council (Dnr: 2023-06455-01).

Playing padel in the forest gives awareness about valuable things in life that the seniors really haven't seen or felt before. The outdoor padel together with the forest illuminates these values, which are natural and unique, within each senior. The values exist deep inside but are hidden if they are not actually confronted, experienced or touch the heart. Playing padel in the forest is felt as unique, a blessing and a fortune to be in a context that brings meaning, meaningful relations and a sense of feeling well in the whole body. Through this, the main theme “Feeling privileged and touched by the heart of the innermost you” is formed.

Brings out the ability to see and feel the underlying beauty in hitting a ball

Being able to play padel in the forest gives an awareness of love, simplicity and good and joyfulness in life. This, as a wholeness, brings out an ability to see and feel the underlying beauty and meaning in hitting a ball.

Being in a place that brings joy

The seniors describe an overwhelming feeling of joy and happiness when playing padel in the forest: “It’s fun, a fun time… leave with a feeling of joy…” (1). It is a joy felt in the body. They express the feeling that it is beautiful and lovely and say it gives them a positive feeling in their body—strength and energy that continues even when they come home: “…a joy that removes worries and is lasting…” (3). They know that they can get injured, but they are not afraid, and they have the perception that they can get hurt no matter what they do. The joy overcomes fear. They enjoy being outside and playing; they think it is fun and nice and that it is a different feeling compared to playing inside even though they also feel that this brings joy, it’s another type of joy. It gives satisfaction and deep wonder and amazement at the fact that hitting a ball can be so much fun: “…it’s amazing to run after a ball...laugh…” (3). This feeling of joy leads to an awareness of one’s own values in life. The place enhances the experience. The forest acts as a liberator, a kind of releaser of one’s own hidden values, and this awareness leads to thoughts about what is good and beautiful in life.

Existing in beloved movements

The seniors describe an inner feeling of love concerning the fact of being able to move, quickly and with coordination “…it’s a good feeling…has an added value…touches me…and makes me feel alive…” (3) when playing padel in the forest. They feel that their legs get stronger and that it enhances their balance, and they express a feeling of being able to find more power in their body, soul and spirit. It gives them a kick. They also feel that playing padel can prevent falls in different kinds of situations in life when one’s body needs to adapt and bear things. To be in this padel movement is like being in a moment of love, in which this “love” feeling leads to feelings of power and strength.

Being in the simple

The seniors describe a feeling of “being in the simple”: “…you don’t have to dress up, you can...come as you are…we put it on our level…” (1). They can just come as they are; no big plans or sacrifices are needed. No equipment is required. They experience that it is easy to come and play: “…. it’s a simple and an easy way to get active…” (8). The seniors describe a kind of relief at going outside to play. There are no expectations on them to be at a certain skill level; adjustments are sometimes needed, but they tend to solve this problem themselves. The seniors feel that they can be as they are, a “being in the simple”, meaning being in something genuine and true, something natural without any artificial influences.

Brings out the ability to actually see and appreciate the existence of the forest

Playing padel in the forest generates an awareness of the forest’s presence, beauty and vastness that it is impossible to see if one is not there. An appreciation that it really exists has emerged.

Being in the beautiful

The seniors describe that playing padel in the forest makes them aware; they value the fact of the obviousness of being able to be in nature, in the beauty there. They feel it is a boon, a thing that helps and is beneficial: “…I’ve told many people this, that we’re here playing padel and how nice and beautiful it is, with the forest and everything, but they think it’s unbelievable ...that there’s a padel court here.…it must be unique…and a boon…” (4). The forest feels undemanding and protective, ever-changing and not static. The forest feels alive. The seniors become aware of the nuance, tint and variegation in the forest; there is lightness, darkness and different kinds of colours. They become aware of the beauty of the forest and that it is so much more than “just” trees. They see different kinds of trees, clearings, different types of soil and moss around the trees, old-growth forests and young forests. They become aware of the forest’s existence and the forest as an asset, and the fact that the nuances visualise its beauty. They realise that they are in the middle of this beauty.

Existing in an overwhelming feeling of the vastness of the forest

The seniors describe that playing padel in the forest makes them aware of the forest’s immensity and width. The forest is seen as infinite: “it isn’t boxed in” (1). There are no walls or limits: “it doesn’t feel cramped” (7); “there’s another aesthetic harmony” (1). The wideness and a lack of limitedness lead to feelings of feeling free and of freedom. This awareness is described as appearing suddenly and giving an overwhelming feeling of awakening. They are touched.

A sense of an existing concealed natural healing power in the forest

Being touched by the peacefulness in the forest instils peace in the mind, and the silence and otherness give an awareness of naturalness and uniqueness. This natural harmony cherishes the soul and touches the spirit and reveals a sense of healing power which was previously not seen and felt; it was hidden.

Being in a state of peace of mind

The seniors describe that playing padel in the forest makes them satisfied and content. They experience feelings such as elation and harmony; they experience that it is a kiss for their soul. They describe it as an enjoyment for all one’s senses, which the body perceives as an external stimulus “…I open my mind…so to say…” (2). The senses of sight and smell are heightened. They described that sight impressions, such as colours, light/darkness, form and shape, affect them. The sight impressions are experienced and expressed as beautiful, peaceful and relaxing. When driving through the forest to the padel court, they experience a mixed forest, sometimes with clearings, which they perceive as the most beautiful and peaceful since the light is more prominent, while a dense pine forest with a carpet of lingonberries or a fir forest with healthy green moss, where the light shines down through the mighty treetops, is felt to be very magical and full of mystery. Mist and fog are also experienced as beautiful, and the forest as having charm. The seniors also describe that there are different kinds of smells in the forest, both good and bad, but they experience that it is the variation and others’ talking about it that makes them aware of the smell. They also describe that it is nice when it rains in the forest, when the sun shines, and when the wind blows a bit lightly. They perceive the forest as protective and valuable and feel that just being there playing padel is peaceful and relaxing. There is a calmness involved, and the seniors describe this calmness as something dominant and vital: “It’s so peaceful…and the peace follows me home…” (4). They feel that the calmness is incorporated into their body and preserved.

Being heartly amazed by the woodland silence

The seniors describe that playing padel in the forest makes them aware of their sense of hearing. They hear birds singing beautifully, the wind blowing, and the leaves trembling. The sounds of, and simply being in, the forest are experienced as soothing rather than disturbing, compared to the city with all its sounds and noise. All the sensory impressions, combined with listening to the forest, are experienced as a kind of communication with the world around them “…it’s a kind of communication…a communication with the forest” (5) and they realise that there is something there even though they cannot see it: “…it’s another dimension” (5). The seniors experience that they learn and understand things by being in the middle of the forest. They describe that even if they hear woodland sounds, there is a silence that strikes them. This silence means a great deal to them; it amazes them and touches their heart, and they say it is indescribable. The silence allows them to think and to calm down.

To pace into an otherness

The seniors describe that playing padel in the forest is a unique phenomenon: “…it’s indescribable…does it exist anywhere else at all?” (4). They feel that they are privileged. They experience the forest as undemanding, placing no demands on them: “…I don’t need to talk to it…” (8). The forest does not ask for anything back. It is described as something else, another dimension; one needs to experience it to understand. This otherness is something entirely different and brings the seniors into a state in which they become aware of their own individuality by looking at it from a different perspective.

A sense of belonging

The gratefulness for being in the forest, playing padel in a social community constituting unconditional relationships and meaningfulness, creates a mutual dependence in a trinity and hallmarks of a sense of belonging.

Being in a different kind of friendship

The seniors say that it feels nice to be in the forest playing padel with people you would not otherwise meet. Meeting new people who are outside your usual social sphere gives feelings of just being able to be: “…with these padel friends you get…you have new questions and you get to know a lot; you talk to them and…everyone’s open and honest and you learn a lot…” (4). The seniors say that they met some of the others at their former work many years ago, or went to school with them, but that they do not belong to the same community today. They tell stories and share memories from the past, which they experience as wonderful and fun. They experience the conversations as different and put them in a different way than usual; there is no prestige, and there are never any problems. They perceive the friendships that develop as being founded on the same conditions, with no seriousness, but just a relaxing community. At the same time, however, they appreciate their usual social community and acknowledge its vital importance. The new and different relationships that develop while playing padel in the forest can be seen as something unconditional, with no strings attached, a kind of freedom and automatic reaction, a naturalness that the relationship is dependent on.

Being in a meaningful context

The seniors describe that playing padel in the forest with others gives a great deal back: “…I’m incredibly grateful to be here and to be able to play…in this special community…drink coffee and talk…” (4). The coffee breaks are very important, as they can sit and just talk; it is a peaceful, highly appreciated moment. They appreciate that they are all nearly the same age, everyone being older. They express that they do not usually attend groups such as this but realise that it means a great deal for their wellbeing. It is something to look forward to, and having company, being part of a group, leads to one actually attending, not wanting to skip it or slip away. Not being alone is experienced as added value. A relationship is created when one gets to know others, feels accepted and can laugh together. The seniors experience that when they are in this context, they listen and learn. This social community gives them strength and a feeling of longing for the next time: “…I just want it to be Wednesday again…” (3). It gives them satisfaction and contentment at being healthy and being able to be part of this, and it generates a feeling and an awareness of not taking for granted what can seem to be a self-evident fact, but that is actually not available to everyone. The context—the forest, the padel and the social community in togetherness—gives them meaning. It provides a balanced self-perception and an awareness of one’s limitations. The seniors described being in a state of humbleness, connected to dignity and respect, when in this context. A positive feeling towards something precious and meaningful develops, and a sense of gratefulness appears.

Discussion

A mutual dependence between activity and place

The seniors described that playing padel in the forest gives them feelings of awareness of the good and the joyfulness in life; they feel love and simplicity in the moment. Playing outdoor padel in the forest felt like it gave something extra compared to playing inside. The padel and the forest, in togetherness, bring meaning and a feeling of wholeness, and strengthen each other. In other words, practising an activity you like, in a place you like, where your heart is, gives meaning and wholeness. This, according to Eriksson, based on Aristotle (1995) and, Gadamer (2003, 2013) who considers the human being as well as health a natural part of a greater whole (Eriksson, 2002, 2007), is a prerequisite for health and wellbeing. Thus, a mutual dependence between an activity you like and a place you like when it comes to health and wellbeing may arise. Eriksson (2003) emphasizes that humans yearn for their own place, a personal space, where they are respected and affirmed as they are. Nevertheless, the physical activity that padel entails, along with being in the forest, can be seen as a preventor of older people’s immobility and prerequisites for contracting a disease, such as mobility (The Public Health Agency of Sweden, 2026; National Board of Health & Welfare, 2025; World Health Organisation (WHO), 2025), and spending time in the forest (Hannerz et al., 2016; Siah et al., 2023) have been proven to have positive health benefits. The seniors also experienced a kind of naturalness, in many ways, when playing padel in the forest. It has been shown that greater perceived naturalness generates more activities, higher aesthetic values and self-reported wellbeing (Sang et al., 2016), and observed reductions in mortality in the age group of 65 or older (Kabisch et al., 2017). The seniors also experienced calmness when playing in the forest. Previous research shows that being in the forest may reduce cortisol levels (Park et al., 2010), decrease heart rate (Lee et al., 2011; Ramanpong et al., 2025), increase the activity in the parasympathetic nervous system and decrease that in the sympathetic nervous system, leading to calmness and thereby increased health and wellbeing (Lee et al., 2011; Park et al., 2010). Additionally, inhaling forest VOCs such as limonene and pinene also has beneficial psychological and physiological effects (Antonelli et al., 2020). This highlights the oxytocinergic system and its role in mediating anti-stress and health-promoting effects induced by nature. According to Grahn et al. (2021), the body’s calming and quieting system is activated when spending time in the forest, which enhances one’s overall wellbeing and promotes health and psychological development.

The seniors in this study described that they feel happy, strong and active when playing padel in the forest. Eriksson (1994) is talking about this as vitality, a source of energy in life, of joy, desire, and a will to live. Vitality is bound to awareness, strength and freedom, and is released when one experiences that one is doing something valuable and when finding meaning in life (Eriksson, 1994). Vitality is promoted when one wants and dares to choose one’s own path to health, but is inhibited if external factors are allowed to steer one’s life (Nyström, 2014). Respect for a person’s absolute dignity and autonomous choice is vital (Eriksson, 1994, 2018).

The seniors also described an inner feeling of love at the fact of being able to move and to be in the forest. Love (Eriksson, 2018) is a basic value in life; a prerequisite for life and togetherness, a source of power, strength and motivation, and can be a signpost for which path one wants to take or what kind of action one is willing to take. Love is inherent in us and manifests itself in human encounters and in creation and nature; it gives strength and energy. Love has power, can imply a will to act in a good way, for both oneself and others, and is the source of all true caring and the motive and desire for one’s actions (Alvsvåg, 2017; Eriksson, 1994; Hemberg et al., 2017; Hemberg, 2015). Eriksson’s caritative caring theory comprises thoughts of love, mercy and compassion, and the fundamental idea and motive entails alleviating suffering and promoting and protecting health and life. When feeling love, one is touched in one’s heart and can therefore also experience compassion, mercy and brief (Eriksson, 1987, 2018; Eriksson & Lindström, 2007; Eriksson, 2007).

The seniors also expressed that playing padel in the forest brings them joy. Joy can be understood as a power and motivation for oneself and others, and can be likened to what is good in humans and to the experience of growing closer to the goal of becoming the innermost you. Joy is a basic value in life: ethos. It gives strength that can lead to independence and the feeling of being significant. It can also provide a feeling of desire and love for life itself. Joy is also contact making, has a social value, and can dampen irritation and anger (Eriksson, 2018; Selander, 2014; Selander, 2022; Wärnå et al., 2007; Wallinvirta, 2011). However, there is a possibility that the seniors in this study held particularly positive views due to self-selection into the activity and their continued engagement with forest padel.

The senior experience of “simple” can be seen as something genuine, true and natural. This “simple” feeling can lead to feelings of relief and of being able to be oneself, be the innermost you, with no expectations and no title to live up to, but rather just to be. Gadamer (2013) emphasizes that there is an unbreakable relationship between human existence and the world around us and that seeing and realizing the real and unveiling the truth constitute the original meaning of evidence. The “simple” feeling can lead to feelings of relief and an easing of stressful environmental influences (Eriksson, 2018). Nevertheless, performing an activity that you love, in a place that you love, brings joy and a feeling of naturalness, which gives meaning and wholeness, and can constitute motivational factors in seniors’ efforts to achieve increased health and wellbeing. Mueller and Thomas (2025) explored, in a scoping review, health outcomes for older adults engaging in outdoor-based interventions. The result showed components of successful ageing, such as prevention of disease/disability, retaining good physical/cognitive functioning, and active engagement. This is in line with Oshanthi Liyanage et al. (2023) findings in their narrative review. Health outcomes such as mental, physical and physiological, and social and behavioural were found, and mental health outcomes were found as the main health and wellbeing outcome. Tong et al. (2025b) study combined validated measures of personal projects and health outcomes with data on environmental factors and found complex interactions between personal goals, environmental factors, and wellbeing. The study showed that both objective and subjective environmental barriers shaped mobility and activity patterns. No, differences in engagement could be seen in the demographic data between the seniors in our study; however, Carter et al. (2026) found that engagement for taking part in led walks was greatest for women in the group of people aged 55+. Forest-based group activities for older adults, such as padel, could be implemented by the community, private owners or the health service as a prescription activity. However, consideration and actions need to be taken to assess costs, transportation and safety. Participants should easily get information about when to play and the weather, for instance, by mail. “Come and try padel” hours could be provided to give the seniors a chance to see if their physical ability is enough. Padel is, however, often considered as especially suitable for seniors since it is less physically demanding and maintains coordination and balance (Pradas et al., 2021), which are essential for preventing falls (Racket-trip, 2026) and promoting health (Sánchez-Alcaraz & Courel-Ibáñez, 2022).

A way to discover the innermost you—one’s ethos

The seniors described that playing padel in the forest helped them visualize and gain awareness about life and the beauty and art in the forest, its aesthetics. Historically, the search for beauty has always been central to humans, as beauty includes the creation of a harmonious whole in which the inherent feelings of love, as well as basic values, constitute the driving force. The seniors also described the forest’s vastness, the forest as something infinite, not square, not limited, an aesthetic harmony, which can instil feelings of freedom and feeling free. Freedom enlightens bad habits, and responsibility can change and even break bad habits (Wärnå et al., 2007; Wallinvirta, 2011, 2022). According to Franco et al. (2017), the lack of straight lines in natural environments makes them restorative, and according to Mårtensson (1995), infinity is a possibility and has, according to Bornemark (2022), been shown to give people a feeling of peace and quiet.

The seniors described that when they played padel in the forest, they became aware of the forest’s vastness, which revealed a sudden effusive understanding, an overwhelming awakening, which can be explained in terms of being “touched in the heart” as it in itself leads to feelings of love (Eriksson, 2018) and a new understanding (Gadamer, 2013). Being out in the forest gives the seniors the ability to actually see the forest and appreciate it. According to Eriksson (2009) and Eriksson (2010), seeing, realising, experiencing and feeling are related to knowing. To see and be aware of something, one needs to be open-minded and challenge one’s own preunderstanding in order to reach new knowledge and awareness (Gadamer, 2013). So, it is necessary to physically be at a place and see that place (here, the forest) in order to reach an awareness and understanding of its complexity and one’s dependence on it. Eriksson emphasises that seeing and perceiving, being touched by/sensing something, can lead to a discovery of the truth (knowledge), the beautiful (aesthetics) and the good (ethics) (Eriksson, 2009, 2010).

The seniors express a kind of gratefulness for being able to play padel in the forest. Gratefulness is a profound spiritual act that brings us closer to the truth and opens the door to the reality of love. It is a bridge between hearts and a key to understanding the eternal presence of love in every moment. By being grateful, we open the door to our own wholeness, as a reminder that everything is interconnected; everything is a whole in existence. Being grateful strengthens both mind and body, and improves our wellbeing by reducing stress, strengthening relationships and promoting joy (Emmons & McCullough, 2004; Eriksson, 2006, 2007).

The results of this study show that playing padel in the forest can lead to an awareness and to finding your basic values, the innermost you—ethos, a synthesis between personal human values and the culture and history you are part of. Ethos is made concrete through actions and is the basic motive that makes the action meaningful (Eriksson, 2003, 2018). It is a natural, innate characteristic, but is also referred to as habits (Eriksson, 2003). To recognize ethos is to be sensitive to the voice of the heart and to have the courage to connect to one’s innermost human being. All humans have an obligation to care for nature: it is an ethical obligation and a responsibility towards our fellow human beings (e.g., Cyrus the Great, the Bible). In ancient Greece, as well as in the Aristotelian worldview, a human being was regarded as a natural part of nature and the surrounding world, which means that human health is seen in relation to this entity, a human being is seen as a natural part of a greater whole. These ethical views are sprung from history, from a tradition, where one can feel at home and have a sense of belonging (Eriksson, 2018). A sense of athomeness occurs, giving courage, joy, and warmth, and there arises an inner force and willingness to take responsibility for the realization of what is good (Eriksson, 2003; Hilli & Eriksson, 2019). Gadamer (2013) notes that something that strongly affects us emotionally affects our ethos and understanding, and emphasises (Gadamer, 2000) that ethics and ethos belong together and that ethics precedes ethos.

A way to an awareness of one’s senses

The seniors describe playing padel in the forest as an enjoyment for all the senses. They felt uniqueness and peace in their mind and were heartly amazed by the silence and the otherness. A kind of healing power was hidden in this natural element; a calmness, another dimension, was described. According to Firth et al. (2015), healing is “a holistic, transformative process of repair and recovery in mind, body, and spirit resulting in positive change, finding meaning, and movement towards self-realization of wholeness, regardless of the presence or absence of disease”, which is also in line with Eriksson's (1994) thoughts. The woodland silence touches the heart. According to Eriksson (2018), being touched can instil a feeling of love and compassion as well as a willingness to help both oneself and others (Eriksson, 2018). Silence is the relative or total absence of sound, and environments where the sound level does not exceed 20–30 decibels are considered quiet. Silence increases concentration and cognitive abilities, for example, and can show power; offer relaxation and recovery; provide space for reflection, self-insight and personal development; promote creativity; and give strength and presence. Silence can also decrease the level of stress hormones and the risk of high blood pressure and heart attack, as well as relieve stress (Donelli et al., 2023).

The seniors express that playing padel in the forest is a unique phenomenon, experienced as being undemanding, with no claims placed on them, another dimension, anotherness. The art of understanding is essential for understanding both oneself and others. It involves recognising the otherness that is present in life and remaining open to whatever reveals itself in one’s life situation (Dahlberg, 2018).

Gives feelings of belonging

The seniors describe that meeting new people who are outside their usual social sphere creates feelings of being able to simply be; there is no prestige, and there are never any problems. This can be interpreted as there being no strings attached, a kind of freedom, a naturalness that the friendships one makes are dependent on. This friendship can have a caritative foundation, built on a natural mutuality that entails caring for someone/something, feeling love for someone or something, a relationship that does not require something back on either an ontological or a natural level (Eriksson, 1987, 2018). Spending time in a meaningful context, the forest, the padel and the social community in togetherness can be seen as giving meaning and being health-promotive. This provides a balanced self-perception and an awareness of one’s limitations. Being in this context can be seen as being in a state of humbleness, connected to the emergence of dignity and respect. Dignity is connected to love and mercy if a human being has an ethos based on caritas and in honouring the absolute dignity of others, and according to Eriksson (2018), may lead to feelings and actions.

The seniors also describe a longing to play in the forest. Longing for (Eriksson, 2018; Ueland, 2013, 2022), something can be a driving force for health, dignity and wellbeing. A longing can manifest itself, for example, a yearning for social contact and nature. According to Eriksson (2003), humans wish to be unique but at the same time yearn for fellowship with others; they yearn for their own place, a personal space, where they are respected and affirmed as they are. They create their homes and their own “being”, a place where they feel they belong. Not belonging to something or not having relationships may lead to feelings of loneliness, which can be devastating, especially for older people, and can lead to poor health and wellbeing (Eriksson, 2003). Actions to prevent loneliness in older people are ongoing (National Board of Health & Welfare, 2025).

So, the seniors feel whole—a wholeness in all dimensions, soundness, freshness and wellbeing—when playing padel in the forest. They experience health and wellbeing. However, if one constituent of these dimensions, for example, tradition, ethical obligations to nature, ethos, absence of disease, lifeworld or values in life, is not cared for, one suffers. If one’s foundation is built on caritas, love and mercy are inborn, and there is an ethical foundation built on tradition, respect, dignity and holiness as well as an obligation to care for oneself, others and the forest, as humans are a natural part of a greater whole (Eriksson, 2003; 1994).

Methodological considerations

This study’s results are based on interview data from eight participants, limiting their generalisability. However, data collection contexts similar to the one in this study are very rare, and we were able to obtain diverse and rich data by including participants of different ages who lived both in the city and in the countryside. The richness and variation of the data in connection with the study's aim are felt to justify the number of participants. There is a risk that the positive result in this study may be a potential characteristic of the study sample rather than a phenomenon that is universally positive. An existential level of understanding, i.e., the meaning that seniors experience from playing padel in the forest, is considered to have been reached. The theoretical framework has influenced both the results and the discussion, which is in line with Gadamer's (2013) thoughts and beliefs. It is also important to acknowledge that the concepts and themes are one possible interpretive lens, not the only explanation of participants' experiences.

Conclusion

This study shows that the seniors experience joy, calmness, beloved movements, simplicity, beautifulness, vastness, peace of mind, silence, otherness, friendship and being in a meaningful context when playing padel in the forest. Wellbeing, vitality, meaningful social connections and positive engagement with nature are seen. The physical, psychological (soul), social, and spiritual and cultural benefits of performing an activity in the forest that you love and enjoy are reflecting the result and may lead to feelings of health and to feel whole. Being there, playing padel, seeing it, gives them an awareness and brings out feelings of naturalness, love and gratefulness and seems to illuminate the seniors' own basic values in life—ethos—which may help them to find their inner willingness and motivation to health. Ontologically, it can be said that when ethos is found, and the heart is touched, feelings of love arise; and if one’s ethical foundation is built on caritas, then love, mercy, and compassion will be manifested for oneself, others and the forest, since we believe that being and nature form a whole. On these grounds, it may be possible to establish a caritative caring relationship with the forest, and the love and compassion for it may lead to a feeling of wanting to care for it and to spend time in it. This can lead not only to increased health and wellbeing but also to suffering, if one’s ability to be out in the forest and play in any way has vanished. One’s energy source, which is one's needs and desires, is formed by one's ontological aspects (one's own value base—ethos) of health and one's will, willingness, motivation, motivating factors to find meaning, life and love. If one's willingness to find meaning, life and love is naturally present, it has a close relationship to practice, to actions, since the desire for life is a necessary condition for survival. With consideration to a small, geographically limited sample size and the interpretive nature of hermeneutic analysis, more and varied qualitative research is needed related to seniors, forests, physical activity, such as padel and health and wellbeing. The study shows, however, that opportunities for the surrounding world, such as activity, place, relationships, and historical and cultural context, to contribute may lead to having meaning in life and thereby a will to be health-promoting and to live, which may lead to increased health and wellbeing.
