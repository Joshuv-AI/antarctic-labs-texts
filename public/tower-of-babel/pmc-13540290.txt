Rationale and design of a PROBE trial comparing continuous enteral nutrition to time-restricted enteral nutrition in severe stroke
Authors: Tao Yu, Guangzhi Shi, Zi Ye, Xiaochun Jiang, Wang Jia, Yilong Wang, Yongjun Wang, Jie Xu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground and purposeContinuous enteral nutrition treatment is routinely provided in the intensive care unit (ICU) for patients with critically ill stroke and contributes to circadian rhythm disruption because a time-limited diet is part of the normal biological rhythm of diurnal species. Our study aims to evaluate the safety as well as the effectiveness of time-restricted enteral nutrition in ICU patients who have had a severe stroke.Methods and designThe Time-restricted Enteral Nutrition vs continuous Enteral nutriTion (TENET) research is a multicentre, prospective, randomised, open-blinded, endpoint trial that compares continuous enteral nutrition to time-restricted enteral nutrition in persons who have had a severe stroke. People with severe stroke who score ≥11 points on the National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale (NIHSS) or ≤12 points on the Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) will be included and randomly assigned to restrict the duration of daytime enteral nutrition to 8–12 hours (time-limited enteral nutrition group) or a 24-hour cycle of continuous enteral nutrition (control group). Until the patient stops receiving enteral nutritional support, passes away, is moved out of the ICU or enteral nutrition is used for 28 days, whichever occurs first, enteral nutritional support will continue. Every patient will have a 90-day follow-up.Study outcomesA poor 90-day functional prognosis (which is indicated by a ≥3 points modified Rankin scale (mRS) score) is the primary efficacy endpoint. The secondary efficacy endpoints include the GCS, NIHSS, mRS, the Barthel index (all recorded at discharge), the 90-day Barthel index and the duration of ICU stay. The safety outcomes include adverse events and serious adverse events during hospitalisation, along with the 28- and 90-day mortality.DiscussionClinical evidence for the safety as well as effectiveness of time-restricted enteral nutrition in persons with severe stroke will be provided by this TENET study.Trial registration numberNCT06161948.Keywords: Stroke

Introduction

Severe stroke is often accompanied by symptoms such as disturbance of consciousness and difficulty swallowing, which affect normal eating habits and eventually lead to malnutrition.1–4 Under these circumstances, nutritional guidelines recommend enteral nutrition as the first-line treatment in the intensive care unit (ICU) for persons with severe stroke,5 6 and the current typical method involves incessant enteral nutrition through a gastric tube.7 8 However, long-term enteral nutrition is also recognised as a risk factor for complications such as hyperglycaemia, lipid metabolism disorders, intestinal flora circadian rhythm disorders and gastric retention,9 which are related to a poor prognosis of severe stroke.10 Therefore, optimising the enteral nutrition strategy is crucial for enhancing patient outcomes, minimising complications and refining the general prognosis of persons who are critically ill.11 12 There is an ongoing debate on the best enteral feeding strategy for critically ill persons.

The practice of restricting dietary consumption to a particular window of time, typically 6–12 hours per day, is known as time-restricted feeding (TRF), in accordance with the body’s circadian rhythms.13 TRF has been proven to be beneficial in animal models as well as in humans. Animal models have shown their effectiveness in promoting weight loss, preventing obesity, reducing liver fat content, improving insulin sensitivity and protecting against hyperlipidaemia, hepatic steatosis, liver ischaemia‒reperfusion injury and inflammation.14 15 Among obese individuals, time-restricted eating contributes to a reduction in fat as well as body weight, apart from enhancements in blood pressure and emotional well-being.16–18 In non-obese individuals, it can improve fasting blood glucose levels, increase intestinal microbial diversity and reduce oxidative stress.19 20 Although TRF has various benefits in the general population, evidence-based clinical research supporting its efficacy and safety in improving outcomes among severe stroke patients in the ICU is lacking.

Therefore, to explore the effectiveness as well as safety of early time-limited enteral nutrition use for persons with severe stroke, along with ≥11 National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale (NIHSS) scores or ≤12 Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) scores, we intend to carry out the Time-restricted Enteral Nutrition vs continuous Enteral nutriTion (TENET) trial.

Methods

Research design

The TENET study is a multicentre, prospective, randomised, open-blinded, endpoint (PROBE) clinical trial. At a 1:1 ratio, all recruited patients will be randomised to either the control group or the time-limited enteral nutrition group. Enteral nutrition will be administered to those in the time-limited enteral nutrition group for 8–12 hours every day. Start and end times for enteral feeding are 06:00 to 08:00 and 16:00 to 18:00, respectively. The control group will comprise patients who will be administered continuous enteral nutrition for 24 hours daily. All eligible participants will be followed up with for 90 days. 35 centres with an ICU in China will take part in the TENET trial.

Critically ill persons will be included, and all procedures will be carried out in compliance with the Declaration of Helsinki’s tenets and the institutional research committee’s ethical guidelines. The institutional review board of Wannan Medical College's Yijishan Hospital granted ethics approval under number 2023–66. Prior to their registration, all participants or their designated representatives will be asked for their informed consent.

Patient population

Critically ill patients with severe stroke in the ICU will be consecutively recruited. The inclusion criteria will be: (1) age <80 years but ≥18 years; (2) cerebral infarction or parenchymal haemorrhage within 72 hours of onset; (3) ≤12 points of GCS scores or ≥11 points of NIHSS scores on admission;10 (4) planned enteral nutrition with an estimated treatment duration of ≥7 days and (5) informed consent obtained. Patients meeting the following exclusion criteria will be excluded: (1) because of enteral nutrition contraindications, requiring complete parenteral nutrition; (2) postintestinal resection or postgastrectomy; (3) enteral nutrition therapy had already commenced and exceeded 12 hours; (4) any of the following associated illnesses: (1) severe cardiac insufficiency (≤50% ejection fraction); (2) advanced cancer; (3) severe renal failure (≥4 mg/dL serum creatinine or ≤30 mL/min glomerular filtration rate) and (4) severe liver failure (≥7 Child–Pugh scores); (5) prior mental disorder or ≥3 modified Rankin Scale (mRS) scores; (6) according to the patient’s nutritional therapist or doctor, the current study plan is not optimal; (7) death is inevitable during admission; the attending physician, the patients or their authorised representatives do not commit to active treatment; (8) patients who have underlying diseases other than severe stroke, which leads to a prognosis for survival of <90 days and (9) current participation in another clinical trial. figure 1 shows the selection criteria of this clinical trial.

Baseline characteristics

Age, height, sex, weight and ethnicity are among the basic demographic data that will be gathered. Additionally, data on symptoms, disease course, lesion location, lesion type (haemorrhagic or ischaemic stroke), medical history, smoking history and alcohol use and patient classification (non-surgical, emergency surgical or elective surgical) will be recorded. The NIHSS and GCS scores during screening, as well as the Nutritional Risk Screening (2002) and the Acute Physiology and Chronic Health Evaluation II (APACHE II) scores, will also be evaluated by well-trained physicians.

Randomisation and blinding

With a 1:1 allocation ratio, patients will be randomised to either a time-limited enteral nutrition regimen or a continuous enteral nutrition regimen. Block randomisation without stratification will be used to centrally produce the list of randomisation codes. The local investigator will obtain the random codes, and participants will be assigned to the corresponding trial protocol according to the randomisation. At each centre, the study endpoint assessors are well-trained physicians who will remain blinded to the treatment group details.

Treatment programmes

Time-restricted enteral nutrition

The patient’s ideal body weight will be used to determine how much protein and calories they need each day. Protein needs range from 1.2 to 1.5 g/kg/day, whereas energy needs are about 25 kcal/kg/day. Enteral nutrition will continue until the patient discontinues it, dies, is transferred out of the ICU or has been used for 28 days, whichever comes first. Across the 35 centres, both time-limited enteral nutrition and 24-hour continuous enteral nutrition treatment will use identical enteral nutrition formulas, which are made of enteral nutritional emulsion (TP-HE, 1.5 kcal per millilitre; FRESENIUS KABI SSPC) and Revilife Whey Protein Powder (protein content 80%). The enteral nutritional emulsion and Revilife Whey protein powders were obtained from the same manufacturer and are composed of the same ingredients.

Continuous enteral nutrition

The treatment plan for this group is otherwise identical to that of the time-restricted enteral nutrition group, including the same strategies for calculating protein and energy needs, as well as the same nutrient solution administration. Trail enteral nutrition will continue until the patient is moved out of the ICU, stops receiving it, passes away or has been receiving enteral nutrition for 28 days, whichever comes first. Table 1 lists the specifics of the treatment strategies applied to the control group.

We recommend that the two groups of patients use a uniform continuous infusion of trial enteral nutrition solution, either through a gastric tube or a gastrointestinal tube. The target infusion rates were set for the two groups of patients according to the daily feeding duration of enteral nutrition. The daily enteral nutrition duration in the time-limited enteral nutrition group is 8–12 hours per day, with the start time between 06:00 and 08:00 and the end time between 16:00 and 18:00. Patients in the 24-hour continuous enteral nutrition group will receive continuous enteral nutrition 24 hours a day.

All patients in both the 24-hour continuous enteral nutrition control and time-restricted enteral nutrition therapy groups requiring enteral nutrition treatment after cessation of the TENET trial intervention will be converted to non-trial enteral nutrition formulations according to the local ICU wards’ and non-ICU wards’ usual enteral nutrition regimens. All other treatments will be performed on the basis of the local investigator’s judgement, including surgery or other invasive procedures, frequency and methods of measurement of blood sugar and gastric residual volumes, monitoring and solutions for gastrointestinal intolerance and so forth.

Concomitant medications and treatments

In addition to the research protocol, all other treatments will follow the Chinese Stroke Society’s ‘Consensus on the Management of Severe Cerebrovascular Disease in China’ and the ‘Chinese Stroke Association Guidelines for Clinical Management of Ischaemic Cerebrovascular Diseases: Executive Summary and 2023 update’.5 If surgery or invasive procedures are deemed absolutely necessary after enrolment, enteral nutrition will be continued or suspended according to the local investigator’s judgement. Enteral nutrition should be resumed as soon as the patient’s condition permits. Based on a previous study,8 investigators should suspend or terminate enteral nutrition interventions when they encounter any of the following situations. In this trial, the factors warranting suspension of enteral nutrition include the following: (1) poor patient tolerance to the enteral nutrition solution, presenting as significant gastrointestinal symptoms such as diarrhoea, vomiting and gastric retention; (2) technical issues such as feeding tube discontinuation or enteral nutrition solution shortages due to various causes; (3) patients undergoing imaging examinations or surgical procedures outside the ICU; other kinds of intensive care procedures requiring temporary suspension of the trial’s enteral nutrition intervention or invasive operations within the ICU, such as removing endotracheal tubes or performing bedside tracheostomies. The factors warranting permanent termination of enteral nutrition include the following: (1) patients who have achieved oral feeding capability; (2) patients whose enteral nutrition intervention has already lasted 28 days; (3) patients who have been transferred out of the ICU; (4) patients who do not meet the inclusion criteria and should be excluded according to the exclusion criteria; (5) the researchers consider continued enteral nutrition treatment to increase patients’ risk of adverse events; (6) patients or their agents who withdraw signed informed consent forms; (7) patients who die; (8) patients who are in the palliative care stage; (9) the researchers believe that the enteral nutrition intervention in this trial may lead to overfeeding; (10) the researchers identify insufficient feeding during the trial enteral nutrition intervention; (11) the researchers consider the trial’s enteral nutrition treatment to be unsuitable for the patient; (12) patients who require special nutritional therapy interventions; (13) patients with severe electrolyte imbalances and (14) patients with strict limitations of fluid resuscitation volume.

Outcomes and follow-up

Visits to the study will take place on days 90, discharge and randomisation. Information will be collected via face-to-face consultations or telephone interviews. On the enrolment day (day 1), the baseline information of the participants will be recorded, and venous blood samples will be collected. Blood samples will be collected to detect blood lipid metabolism and glucose metabolism at the time of enrolment and on days 7, 14 and 28 of the trial enteral nutrition intervention. Metabolomics was used to detect blood lipid metabolism and glucose metabolism. Laboratory tests, including nutritional status (albumin, prealbumin, haemoglobin, transferrin levels) and electrolytes21 (phosphorus, sodium, magnesium and potassium levels in the serum), will be conducted at baseline and on days 7, 14 and 28 of the trial enteral nutrition intervention to monitor the clinical status of patients. We recruited subcentres that were willing to participate in patient stool collection, and 10 subcentres demonstrated strong collaboration capabilities and possessed appropriate equipment. Therefore, participants enrolled in these 10 subcentres were included in the stool analysis. Study organisation

The steering committee will be solely in charge of the trial’s planning, implementation and publishing in addition to providing strategic and scientific guidance. It will ensure the quality, execution and management of the study. The steering committee approved the study protocol and operational guidelines before the study began. It will go over and report on the study’s progress at regular telephone conferences or in-person meetings. The executive committee will gather available blinded data, assess the trial’s progress and offer pertinent study direction. Major decisions will be made at face-to-face meetings organised by the executive committee.

Data Security Monitoring Board

To ensure the study satisfies the highest ethical and patient safety requirements, a Data Safety Monitoring Board (DSMB) will periodically assess its progress. It will comprise academics who are not directly participating in the experiments, such as independent statisticians. Before the trial begins, the Executive Committee, as well as DSMB, will certify DSMB qualifications, such as roles, responsibilities and membership. A report will be submitted to the committee chair immediately after each DSMB meeting.

Sample size estimate

The primary efficacy endpoint was used to determine the sample size. The Optimising Early Enteral Nutrition in Severe Stroke trial data10 suggests that the 90-day adverse prognostic event rate for patients receiving full enteral nutrition is assumed to be 80%. Compared with continuous feeding, a time-limited diet is expected to reduce the 90-day adverse prognostic event rate by 18%. The relative risk reduction was set according to previous trials involving similar study populations,10 in combination with clinical considerations (modest clinical benefits from time-restricted enteral nutrition for severe stroke with a high disability rate) and practical feasibility (achievable sample size). Based on prior neurocritical care trials, the research groups in the design phase deemed a relatively lowered risk of no less than 15%–20% clinically relevant;10 23 24 thus, a slightly more conservative value of 18% was chosen. After the 90-day follow-up has been finished by 60% of the patients, an interim analysis will be carried out. The overall significance level for the two-sided test is set at 0.048, with a power of 90% and a dropout rate of 5%. Each group is expected to require 210 cases, for an overall 420 patients.

Statistical analysis

The intention-to-treat principle will be followed when analysing the outcomes (both primary and secondary). The trial centre will be regarded as a random effect, and log-binomial regression will help analyse variances between groups for the primary outcome. The 95% CIs and relative rates will be shared. A generalised linear mixed model or ordinal logistic regression will help analyse the secondary outcomes, and a log-binomial regression model will be applied for the safety outcomes. An interim analysis is planned when 60% of the subjects undergo randomisation and complete 90 days of follow-up. The O’Brien–Fleming design will help adjust the alpha level during the interim analysis. During the interim analysis and at the conclusion of the trial, p<0.005 and p<0.048 will be deemed significant, respectively. R programme 4.3.3 will be used for all statistical tests.

Complete data will be used in our primary analysis, and sensitivity analysis with multiple imputation for missing outcome data will be performed. These additional analyses will confirm the robustness of our findings. The primary efficacy outcome will be further analysed according to the following subgroups: diabetic/non-diabetic status, sex, stroke type, age, admission GCS score, time to random assignment and admission APACHE score.

Version modifications

We modified the protocol in two ways, and the current version is 3.0. In V.2.0, the name of the project was changed from ‘The effect of a time-restricted diet on the prognosis of patients with severe stroke’ to ‘The effect of time-restricted enteral nutrition on the prognosis of severe stroke patients: PROBE trial.’ The upper age limit for patient inclusion was increased to no more than 80 years. The expected duration of enteral nutrition for patients ranged from more than 14 days to greater than or equal to 7 days. The inclusion criteria were updated by adding ‘cerebral infarction or parenchymal haemorrhage within 72 hours of onset’ and removing ‘drinking test score ≥3 points or consciousness impairment’. The exclusion criteria were changed to the current exclusion criteria, as described in the previous section on exclusion criteria. Enteral nutrition’s endpoint was modified so that it would continue until the patient passed away, stopped receiving it, had received enteral nutrition for 28 days or was moved from the ICU, whichever came first. The first modifications were made before the trial’s kick-off meeting. In V.3.0, we adjusted the collection of faeces to be carried out at ten sub-centres.

Discussion

To our knowledge, the TENET trial is the first multicentre PROBE study investigating how time-restricted enteral nutrition affects severe stroke prognosis. To optimise enteral feeding practices in the ICU, this trial investigates whether time-restricted enteral nutrition lowers the risk of a poor 90-day prognosis in severe stroke cases.

In animal models, studies on time-restricted eating have shown many benefits, including weight loss, prevention of hyperlipidaemia and hepatic steatosis and alleviation of inflammation as well as liver ischaemia‒reperfusion injury.14 15 Abnormal liver function is a common complication of enteral nutrition. Abnormal liver function often leads to lipid metabolism disorders, and hyperlipidaemia is a common manifestation of lipid metabolism disorders. During hyperlipidaemia, the environment in which microorganisms in the gut survive changes, and changes in their physical and chemical properties and material structures affect the metabolism of normal intestinal flora, such as Bifidobacteria, Lactobacilli and Enterococci. The number of these organisms significantly decreases, and the relative number of Enterobacteriaceae increases, resulting in bacterial imbalance. Permanent alterations in the large intestine’s microecological system can result from a high-fat diet. Intestinal dysbiosis promotes the secretion of proinflammatory factors by increasing intestinal levels of intestinal bacteria, leading to chronic low-level inflammation. A time-restricted diet reversed the immunological dysregulation as well as the ileal transcriptome rhythm abnormality brought on by a high-fat diet by reprogramming particular chromatin and nucleosome cascades, as well as acting on immune-related pathways. By controlling the bile acid signalling, time-restricted eating shields the body from the negative effects of a high-fat diet.25 Therefore, a time-restricted diet may be an effective means to maintain the stability of the intestinal flora, improve insulin resistance and reduce blood lipid and cholesterol levels.

Currently, evidence of a time-restricted eating pattern in persons who are critically ill is scarce. Clinical guidelines on the timing of nutritional support are uncertain, and the evidence for continuous nutritional support is weak.6 26 As a diurnal species, healthy individuals usually adopt a time-restricted eating pattern, with meals during the day and fasting at night. Nevertheless, persons who are critically ill in the ICU have disrupted circadian rhythms and usually receive a time-unrestricted, 24-hour cycle of continuous infusion of nutrient solutions, during the day and at night.8 26 27 For critically ill patients, the 24-hour continuous feeding method has certain advantages; patients with poor gastrointestinal tolerance when receiving enteral nutrition can obtain sufficient calories and reduce aspiration risk and pneumonia cases caused by enteral nutrition treatment.28 Nutritional support therapy must be administered throughout the day in a TRF schedule.29 A clinical trial was carried out by Liu et al30 to assess the safety as well as the effectiveness of time-restricted eating in obesity cases. Nevertheless, their findings showed that a time-restricted eating pattern did not further help patients lose body fat or weight in comparison to a calorie-restricted eating pattern. Thus, clinical investigations on the safety and effectiveness of time-restricted enteral nutrition assistance are required, even though a TRF pattern is a physiological compliance therapeutic practice. There is also a dearth of evidence regarding the safety and effectiveness of daytime feeds for persons who are critically ill.

Here, the treatment plan for the time-restricted enteral nutrition therapy group is otherwise identical to that of the continuous enteral nutrition control group, including the same strategies for calculating calorie and protein needs, alongside the same nutrient solution administration. However, since the enteral nutrition solution in the time-restricted enteral nutrition therapy group will be infused for 8–12 hours, approximately half of the infusion time in the control group (24-hour cycle), whether patients with severe stroke can tolerate time-limited nutrition is a safety indicator that we focused on. Thus, the daily delivery of enteral nutrition and gastrointestinal tolerance of enteral nutrition, such as residual gastric volume, regurgitation and vomiting, use of promotility agents, highest daily blood glucose concentration, daily insulin intake and lowest daily blood glucose concentration, will be documented and compared.

In conclusion, time-restricted enteral nutrition’s impact on the 90-day prognosis of severe stroke in persons who are critically ill will be examined by the TENET research. The present trial will provide valuable data for choosing between a continuous 24 hours enteral nutrition model and a time-restricted enteral nutrition model.
