Postoperative stimulated thyroglobulin and the ps-tg/TSH ratio enhance the 2025 ATA risk stratification for predicting radioiodine response in papillary thyroid carcinoma
Authors: Bingyu Ran, Huiting Zhang, Ying Li, Jingjie Shang, Yong Chen, Yingxin Li, Qijun Cai, Guowei Li, Bin Guo, Jian Gong, Hao Xu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPostoperative stimulated thyroglobulin (ps-Tg) is an established biomarker for assessing treatment response in papillary thyroid carcinoma (PTC), but optimal integration of biochemical parameters into the 2025 American Thyroid Association (ATA) recurrence risk stratification system remains undefined.Materials and methodsWe retrospectively analyzed 1,117 PTC patients who underwent first radioactive iodine (RAI) therapy at the First Affiliated Hospital of Jinan University between 2015 and 2021. Two models were built to estimate the probability of achieving an excellent response (ER) 1 year after RAI: Model 1 included all variables selected by LASSO (pathological N stage, 2025 ATA recurrence risk stratification, ps-Tg, ps-Tg/thyroid-stimulating hormone [TSH] ratio, and thyroglobulin antibody [TgAb]); Model 2 served as a control, including only pathological N stage and recurrence risk stratification. Model performance was evaluated using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis, LASSO regression, decision curve analysis (DCA), and net reclassification improvement (NRI).ResultsROC analysis identified optimal cut-off values of 4.10 ng/mL for ps-Tg and 0.07 for the ps-Tg/TSH ratio (area under the curve [AUC] 0.856 and 0.843, respectively). In the training cohort, Model 1 outperformed Model 2 (AUC 0.862 vs. 0.614; R2 0.531 vs. 0.056) and significantly improved reclassification (IDI 0.389; overall NRI 0.297; event NRI 0.163). DCA demonstrated greater clinical net benefit for Model 1 across risk thresholds of 0.02–0.80, and findings in the validation cohort were consistent.ConclusionBased on ps-Tg, the ps-Tg/TSH ratio, TgAb, pathological N stage, and the ATA 2025 recurrence risk stratification, the combined model showed good predictive value for 1-year ER after RAI therapy in patients with PTC. This model may serve as a complementary tool to existing postoperative risk assessment and provide additional support for follow-up stratification and individualized management.

Introduction

Papillary thyroid carcinoma (PTC) is the most common subtype of differentiated thyroid carcinoma, accounting for approximately 80–85% of all thyroid cancers [1]. In recent years, the global incidence of PTC has continued to rise, a trend primarily attributed to advances in imaging techniques and the expansion of screening programs [2]. This has led to improved early detection but may also contribute to overdiagnosis in some cases, underscoring the importance of individualized treatment and follow-up strategies for patients with PTC. Although the overall prognosis of PTC is excellent and disease-specific mortality remains extremely low [3,4], a subset of patients still experience suboptimal therapeutic responses, recurrence, or distant metastasis [5,6]. Consequently, clinical focus has shifted from merely extending survival to achieving personalized treatment and high-quality follow-up, aiming to balance disease control with quality of life.

Radioactive iodine (RAI) therapy is a cornerstone of postoperative management for PTC, effectively ablating remnant thyroid tissue and reducing the risk of recurrence [7]. However, individual responses to RAI therapy vary considerably. Some patients achieve an excellent response (ER), as defined by the American Thyroid Association (ATA), whereas others fail to attain ER and remain at risk of persistent or recurrent disease, necessitating closer surveillance and additional therapeutic interventions [8,9]. Therefore, early identification of patients who are unlikely to achieve ER at 1 year after RAI therapy – preferably before or shortly after treatment – is essential for risk stratification, follow-up intensity planning, and early identification of patients who may need closer surveillance or additional therapeutic consideration.

The ATA guidelines provide a recurrence risk stratification system that is closely associated with the likelihood of an incomplete response to initial therapy across different patient subgroups and serves as a key framework for prognostic assessment in patients with PTC [10–12]. A retrospective study reported that the proportion of patients achieving an excellent response after postoperative RAI was 88% in the low-risk group, significantly higher than in the intermediate-risk (63%) and high-risk (47%) groups [13]. Nevertheless, accumulating evidence indicates that when radioactive iodine therapy is guided solely by recurrence risk categories, treatment intensity may become misaligned with individual disease biology, leaving some patients undertreated while subjecting others to unnecessary overtreatment [14–18].

Accordingly, the updated 2025 ATA guidelines further refine recurrence risk stratification compared with the 2015 system, expanding the original three-tier scheme (low, intermediate, and high risk) into a four-tier framework (low, low–intermediate, high–intermediate, and high risk) and more systematically integrating key clinicopathologic determinants such as histologic subtype, lymph node burden, extranodal extension, and AJCC stage [19]. These refinements allow a more granular assessment of recurrence risk based on pathological disease burden. Nevertheless, thyroid function–related biochemical markers closely linked to RAI response – particularly postoperative stimulated thyroglobulin (ps-Tg) and thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH)-dependent metrics such as the ps-Tg/TSH ratio – have still not been explicitly incorporated into this stratification framework with defined parameters or cut-off values. Although anatomical and pathological information provides reasonable stratification accuracy, it cannot fully capture the biological activity of residual disease or its responsiveness to TSH, underscoring the need to integrate postoperative biochemical indicators that reflect the functional status of tumor remnants to further enhance predictive performance for RAI efficacy [13,18, 20,21].

Among postoperative biochemical indicators that may capture residual disease activity more directly than anatomical or pathological variables, ps-Tg and the ps-Tg/TSH have attracted particular attention. Post-stimulated Tg, a TSH-induced biomarker measured after thyroidectomy, reflects the functional burden of remnant thyroid tissue and microscopic disease and is widely used for pre-RAI risk assessment and long-term surveillance [22,23]. However, because Tg secretion is strongly dependent on TSH levels, inter-individual differences in the degree of TSH stimulation can lead to substantial variability in ps-Tg concentrations under comparable tumor burdens, thereby increasing the risk of misclassification [24]. To attenuate this confounding effect, the ps-Tg/TSH ratio has been proposed as a normalized index that adjusts ps-Tg for the intensity of TSH stimulation and provides a more stable representation of residual disease activity [25–30].

Several studies have shown that ps-Tg/TSH is comparable or even superior to ps-Tg alone in predicting RAI response – including the likelihood of achieving an excellent response – as well as recurrence risk [28,31–33], particularly among thyroglobulin antibody (TgAb)–negative patients, in whom it retains independent prognostic value [34]. Because ps-Tg/TSH explicitly accounts for TSH fluctuations, it is considered more robust and accurate in reflecting tumor activity than ps-Tg alone. Nonetheless, other reports have suggested that Tg itself may exhibit stronger independent prognostic power [29,35], highlighting ongoing uncertainty regarding the relative predictive performance of ps-Tg versus the ps-Tg/TSH ratio in predicting treatment response and prognosis.

Within the context of the 2025 ATA risk stratification framework, there is still a lack of large-scale, methodologically robust evidence on how thyroid-related biochemical parameters should be incorporated into predictive models to improve the prediction of an excellent response at 1 year after adjuvant RAI therapy. Moreover, although both ps-Tg and the ps-Tg/TSH ratio have shown promising prognostic performance, their optimal cutoff values remain heterogeneous across studies and have not been standardized for clinical application.

Therefore, we conducted a large retrospective study involving 1,117 PTC patients who underwent initial RAI therapy at the First Affiliated Hospital of Jinan University between 2015 and 2021.

We systematically evaluated the associations of postoperative biochemical indicators (ps-Tg, ps-Tg/TSH, and TgAb status) with the achievement of ER at 1 year after RAI and explored reference threshold values based on this cohort. Building upon the 2025 ATA recurrence risk categories and pathological N stage, we then developed a combined model for predicting the probability of achieving ER at 1 year after RAI and internally validated its performance. The model’s discrimination, calibration, and clinical utility were assessed using receiver operating characteristic analysis, reclassification metrics (including the integrated discrimination improvement and net reclassification improvement), and decision curve analysis. This study aims to provide additional information to complement existing postoperative risk assessment and to offer a reference for follow-up stratification and individualized management.

Materials and methods

Study design and participants

This was a single-center retrospective cohort study. Clinical data of 1,875 patients with papillary thyroid carcinoma (PTC) who underwent radioactive iodine (RAI) therapy between January 2015 and December 2021 were retrieved from the inpatient electronic medical record system of the Department of Nuclear Medicine, the First Affiliated Hospital of Jinan University.

Inclusion criteria were as follows: ① Histopathological confirmation of PTC; ② Total or near-total thyroidectomy; ③ Underwent initial RAI therapy at our institution; ④ Availability of both diagnostic whole-body iodine scan (Dx-WBS) before RAI and post-therapeutic whole-body iodine scan (Rx-WBS); ⑤ Negative serum human chorionic gonadotropin (HCG) test in female patients; ⑥ Complete clinical, pathological, and follow-up data; ⑦ Blood samples collected 3–5 days before RAI therapy after withdrawal of levothyroxine for four weeks.

Exclusion criteria were as follows: ① Absence of Dx-WBS or other essential imaging data; ② Incomplete clinical history, surgical records, or pathological reports; ③ Non–initial RAI treatment; ④ Coexistence of other histological types of thyroid carcinoma (e.g. follicular, medullary, or anaplastic carcinoma); ⑤ Positive thyroglobulin antibody (TgAb ≥ 40 IU/mL) before RAI; ⑥ Absence of exact values for post-stimulated thyroglobulin (ps-Tg) or thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH), precluding calculation of the ps-Tg/TSH ratio; ⑦ Severe hepatic, renal, or cardiac dysfunction; ⑧ Missing or incomplete follow-up information.

After rigorous screening, a total of 1,117 patients who met the above criteria were included in the final analysis. Following a standard one-year follow-up period, the clinical observation phase for the cohort was completed by January 2023. The case selection process is shown in Figure 1.

Data collection

Demographic and pathological data

Age, sex, body mass index (BMI), maximum tumor diameter, laterality, extrathyroidal extension (ETE), vascular invasion, tumor T stage, pathological N stage, number of metastatic lymph nodes, distant metastasis, and recurrence risk stratification.

Hormonal indicators

ps-Tg, TgAb, TSH, free thyroxine (FT4), and the ps-Tg/TSH ratio (using the Beckman Coulter DxI 800 analyzer, employing enzymatic chemiluminescence immunoassay for determination).

Imaging examinations

Dx-WBS, Rx-WBS, neck ultrasonography, chest CT, and 18F-fluorodeoxyglucose positron emission tomography/computed tomography (18F-FDG PET/CT). Lymph node metastases identified on Dx-WBS were recorded as Dx-LNs.

All data were obtained from the hospital’s electronic medical record system, laboratory testing reports, and the nuclear medicine imaging archiving system. Tumor staging was determined according to the 8th edition of the American Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC) STNM classification (Table S1). Recurrence risk stratification was defined according to the 2025 American Thyroid Association (ATA) Guidelines (Table S2).

Outcome definition

The primary outcome of this study was the achievement of an Excellent Response (ER) at a fixed time point of 12 months (±1 month) after the initial RAI therapy. The evaluation included serum Tg and TgAb levels as well as imaging examinations (neck ultrasonography, chest CT, diagnostic radioiodine scintigraphy, and 18F-FDG PET/CT). According to the 2025 ATA Guidelines’ response-to-therapy criteria (Table S3), patients were classified into four groups: ① ER; ② Indeterminate response (IDR); ③ Biochemical incomplete response (BIR); ④ Structural incomplete response (SIR).

All enrolled patients in this study received only standard TSH suppression therapy during the follow-up period, and no other tumor-specific interventions were implemented.

Data management and analysis timeline

The retrospective data retrieval and standardization process was conducted from November 2025 to January 2026. Subsequently, statistical modeling and data analysis, including the integration of the 2025 ATA risk stratification framework, were performed between January 2026 and February 2026. This ensures the study utilizes the most contemporary clinical guidelines and analytical methods.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.5.1; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). Continuous variables were tested for normality using the Shapiro–Wilk test and presented as median (interquartile range, IQR). Group comparisons were conducted using the Mann–Whitney U test. Categorical variables were expressed as frequencies and percentages and compared using the χ2 test.

Spearman’s rank correlation analysis was used to assess the relationships among ps-Tg, ps-Tg/TSH, TgAb, and achievement of ER. The predictive performance of ps-Tg and ps-Tg/TSH for ER was evaluated using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis, calculating the area under the curve (AUC), sensitivity, specificity, Youden index, and 95% confidence interval (95% CI) to determine optimal cutoff values.

The dataset was randomly divided into a training set and a validation set at a 7:3 ratio, with a fixed random seed (set.seed = 2025) to ensure reproducibility. None of the included variables contained missing data; therefore, no imputation or deletion was required. Achievement of ER was defined as the dependent variable.

First, univariate logistic regression was performed to identify variables significantly associated with ER (p < 0.05 or of clinical relevance). Second, variance inflation factors (VIFs) were calculated for all included variables; variables with VIF > 5 were excluded to avoid multicollinearity. Variables with significant effects and no multicollinearity were then entered into a least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) regression model. Tenfold cross-validation was applied to optimize the penalty parameter (λ), and λ_min was selected for subsequent variable selection.

As the primary goal of this model was to utilize routinely available pre-RAI patient-related data, administered RAI activity was intentionally excluded as a core variable, as including it would make the model partially dependent on treatment allocation itself. To address potential dose-related confounding, we performed a sensitivity analysis using multivariable logistic regression, adjusting for RAI dose category. This analysis was intended solely as a robustness check and was not used to alter the final model structure.

A predictive model was then developed using the key variables selected by LASSO regression. Model fit and discriminative ability were assessed using the AUC, Nagelkerke R2, Akaike Information Criterion (AIC), and Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC).

Decision curve analysis (DCA) was conducted to evaluate the clinical net benefit of the model across a threshold probability range of 0.0–1.0, covering the full spectrum of potential clinical intervention thresholds and assessing changes in net benefit across different risk levels.

Model reclassification improvement was assessed using the Net Reclassification Improvement (NRI) and Integrated Discrimination Improvement (IDI) indices. NRI quantified the percentage improvement in correct reclassification of ER and non-ER patients, whereas IDI measured the average improvement in model discrimination between ER and non-ER patients. A 2% change in predicted probability was used as the threshold for both NRI and IDI evaluations.

All statistical tests were two-sided, and a p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

All patients received standardized I-131 therapy. Specifically, 89 patients (7.97%) were administered an initial activity of 100 mCi, while 460 (41.18%) and 507 (45.39%) received 125 mCi and 150 mCi, respectively. Only 61 patients (5.46%) received doses exceeding 150 mCi.

Association between thyroid-related hormonal levels and ER

The adequacy of thyroid stimulation was assessed across the entire cohort. The median stimulated TSH level was 64.2 mIU/L (interquartile range, 41.3–93.9 mIU/L), with a mean of 71.6 ± 35.6 mIU/L. Overall, 93.5% (1,044/1,117) of patients achieved the guideline-recommended threshold of TSH >30 mIU/L before RAI, supporting generally adequate stimulation in the study population. The remaining 6.5% (73/1,117) had slightly lower levels, with a mean TSH of 27.4 ± 3.1 mIU/L (range, 26.2–29.9 mIU/L), suggesting that their stimulation status was close to the recommended threshold.

Group comparisons were conducted using the Mann–Whitney U test. Compared with the non-ER group, patients in the ER group exhibited significantly lower ps-Tg and ps-Tg/TSH levels (both p < 0.001), whereas differences in TSH and FT4 levels were not statistically significant (both p > 0.05). Notably, TgAb levels in the ER group were slightly higher than those in the non-ER group (p < 0.001). Since this analysis was limited to patients with TgAb < 40 IU/mL (negative range), these findings suggest that even low-titer variations in TgAb may still be associated with treatment response. (A–E) Raincloud plots showing the distributions of ps-Tg, TSH, ps-Tg/TSH, TgAb, and FT4 between the ER and non-ER groups. Asterisks indicate statistical significance based on the Mann–Whitney U test (p < 0.05). (F) ROC curves for the five thyroid-related hormonal parameters.

Spearman’s correlation analysis demonstrated that ps-Tg (rs = −0.607, p < 0.001) and ps-Tg/TSH (rs = −0.586, p < 0.001) were significantly and negatively correlated with ER, whereas TgAb showed a weak positive correlation (rs = 0.204, p < 0.001). These results indicate that lower ps-Tg and ps-Tg/TSH levels are closely associated with a higher likelihood of achieving ER.

ROC curve analysis further confirmed the above findings. Both ps-Tg and ps-Tg/TSH demonstrated good predictive performance, with optimal cutoff values of 4.10 ng/mL and 0.07, respectively. The corresponding areas under the curve (AUCs) were 0.856 and 0.843 (Figure 2F), with Youden indices of 0.588 and 0.560, sensitivities of 83.8% and 81.2%, and specificities of 75.0% and 74.8%, respectively. These results indicate that both parameters can serve as effective biochemical indicators for predicting the achievement of an excellent response (ER) after RAI therapy.

Univariate analysis and variable selection

Univariate logistic regression revealed that T4 stage, N1b stage, distant metastasis, maximum tumor diameter > 2 cm, number of metastatic lymph nodes, high recurrence risk stratification, and thyroid-related biochemical indicators (ps-Tg, ps-Tg/TSH, TgAb, and FT4) were all significantly associated with achieving ER (all p < 0.05). In contrast, age, sex, BMI, ETE, and vascular invasion showed no significant associations with ER (all p > 0.05) (Table S4). Although FT4 did not differ significantly between groups in the simple comparison, it demonstrated an independent association in univariate logistic regression, suggesting that its effect may be influenced by interactions with other variables.

VIF assessment was performed for candidate variables intended for model inclusion. The results indicated substantial multicollinearity for T stage (VIF = 57.73), maximum tumor diameter (VIF = 9.81), ETE (VIF = 6.87), vascular invasion (VIF = 8.23), number of metastatic lymph nodes (VIF = 11.58), and recurrence risk stratification (VIF = 19.52). Given that recurrence risk stratification comprehensively reflects tumor invasiveness and metastatic characteristics, it was retained, whereas the other collinear variables were excluded. Upon re-evaluation, the VIF of recurrence risk stratification decreased to 2.49, indicating that multicollinearity had been resolved.

Using tenfold cross-validation, the optimal penalty parameter was determined as λ_min = 0.013. The LASSO model ultimately identified five key variables significantly associated with ER: pathological N stage, recurrence risk stratification, ps-Tg, ps-Tg/TSH, and TgAb (Figure 3A–B). These variables were used to construct Model 1, whereas Model 2 served as the control model, including only pathological N stage and recurrence risk stratification.

To assess the potential impact of dose-related factors on the main findings, we performed a sensitivity analysis. Univariate logistic regression revealed that RAI dose was significantly associated with 1-year ER (p < 0.001), but this association was no longer significant in the multivariate regression model (p = 0.426). For both ps-Tg and ps-Tg/TSH, univariate logistic regression showed significant associations with 1-year ER (p < 0.001). Specifically, for ps-Tg, the odds ratio (OR) was 0.85 (95% CI: 0.83–0.87) in univariate regression, and 0.86 (95% CI: 0.84–0.89) in multivariate regression (p < 0.001 for both). Similarly, for ps-Tg/TSH, the univariate OR was 0.05 (95% CI: 0.03–0.10), and the multivariate OR was 0.78 (95% CI: 0.58–1.06), with p = 0.043.

Model performance and validation

In the training set, Model 1 (Figure 3C) demonstrated superior discrimination and goodness of fit compared with Model 2 (Figure 3D) (Model 1: AUC = 0.862, R2 = 0.531, AIC = 709.73, BIC = 752.80; Model 2: AUC = 0.614, R2 = 0.056, AIC = 1043.05, BIC = 1071.02). The calibration curve showed excellent agreement between the predicted and observed probabilities of achieving an ER for Model 1 (Figure 3E). The calibration curve for Model 2 is shown in Fig. S1B.

DCA further demonstrated that Model 1 provided a markedly higher net benefit in predicting an ER than the treat-all or treat-none strategies (Figure 3F). Across the full threshold probability range of 0.0–1.0, the net benefit curve of Model 1 consistently outperformed that of Model 2 (Fig. S1E), with the greatest difference observed between 0.1 and 0.8. This finding suggests that Model 1 offers greater clinical decision-making value. Moreover, while Model 2’s benefit was primarily limited to the 0.35–0.74 range, Model 1 extended this advantage to a broader 0.02–0.80 range, indicating superior flexibility for risk-based management.

Compared with Model 2, Model 1 showed significantly improved overall reclassification, as reflected by an IDI of 0.389 (95% CI: 0.16–0.62) and an overall NRI of 0.297 (95% CI: 0.182–0.412). Specifically, Model 1 demonstrated a superior ability to correctly reclassify patients with an Excellent Response, with an event NRI of 0.163 (95% CI: 0.052–0.264).

In the validation set, Model 1 consistently outperformed Model 2 in discrimination and goodness of fit, with similar trends observed for AUC, R2, AIC, and BIC values, demonstrating strong concordance with the training set results (Table S5). Calibration curves also confirmed superior model fitting for ER prediction (Fig. S1A, C). DCA further indicated that Model 1 achieved higher net benefits across most threshold probabilities, underscoring its robust clinical applicability and generalizability (Fig. S1D, F).

Discussion

In this large retrospective cohort of 1,117 PTC patients undergoing initial RAI therapy, we developed and validated a multiparametric predictive model integrating ps-Tg, ps-Tg/TSH ratio, TgAb, pathological N stage, and the 2025 ATA recurrence risk stratification to predict the probability of achieving ER within one year after RAI therapy. With the inclusion of the three hormonal variables, the combined model demonstrated improved predictive performance in discrimination, reclassification, and decision curve analysis. Its results were generally consistent across the training and validation sets, suggesting a relatively stable performance within the present study population. Moreover, the optimal cutoff values for predicting ER were 4.10 ng/mL for ps-Tg and 0.07 for the ps-Tg/TSH ratio; however, their applicability in other populations requires further validation.

Tg is a specific molecular biomarker reflecting both the mass and functional activity of thyroid tissue. As early as 2012, Rosario et al. confirmed that postoperative stimulated Tg effectively predicted RAI ablation success [36]. Since then, ps-Tg has become a core indicator for evaluating RAI efficacy and recurrence risk in differentiated thyroid carcinoma (DTC). Most studies have reported practical ranges of ps-Tg ≈ 3–10 ng/mL [28–30,34, 37–39] and ps-Tg/TSH ≈ 0.06–0.13 to distinguish ER from non-ER. Our optimal thresholds were slightly lower, likely reflecting the younger age, smaller tumor size, and strict diagnostic and imaging inclusion criteria of our cohort. Variations in assay platforms and measurement units may also shift absolute values; therefore, local calibration should be performed before cross-center application.

Because Tg synthesis and release are influenced by TSH stimulation, Tg alone may be confounded by endogenous TSH variability. To address this, Prpić et al. systematically evaluated and first identified ps-Tg/TSH = 0.126 as an independent predictor of RAI failure [40]. Subsequent studies across different regions and populations have validated the robust predictive value of this ratio [30,34,38]. Moreover, ps-Tg/TSH has been associated not only with ablation response but also with lymph node and distant metastases. When the ratio exceeds 1.0, it almost invariably indicates occult metastasis [41]. Livhits et al. further found that ps-Tg > 154 μg/L or ps-Tg/TSH > 1.13 markedly increased the risk of pulmonary metastasis in pediatric DTC [42]. These findings indicate that ps-Tg/TSH reflects continuous tumor burden and retains predictive value across disease stages. In this study, although ps-Tg/TSH independently predicted ER after RAI in PTC patients, its predictive ability was slightly inferior to ps-Tg.

Using LASSO regression, we established an integrated predictive model comprising pathological N stage, recurrence risk stratification, ps-Tg, ps-Tg/TSH, and TgAb. Compared with the conventional pathological and risk stratification model, this model demonstrated better predictive performance in discrimination (AUC 0.862 vs. 0.614), goodness-of-fit, calibration, and decision curve analysis, indicating a higher potential net benefit. Evaluation using NRI and IDI demonstrated that the inclusion of hormonal variables improved the model’s reclassification ability. These results are consistent with recent large-scale studies demonstrating that adding biochemical markers such as stimulated Tg to traditional structural risk factors (e.g. number or ratio of metastatic lymph nodes, N stage)enhances risk discrimination [30,34,38]. Such integrated models centered on biochemical indicators may serve as a complement to existing risk assessment approaches and provide a reference for postoperative follow-up stratification and individualized management. Our model is primarily focused on predicting short-term response to initial RAI therapy within one year, rather than long-term oncologic outcomes such as recurrence, progression, or disease-specific survival.

Notably, RAI dose may influence treatment outcomes. In our sensitivity analysis, after adjusting for RAI dose category in a multivariable logistic regression model, the association between ps-Tg and 1-year ER remained significant, with minimal change in effect size before and after adjustment (OR = 1.137, 95% CI: 1.029–1.256, p = 0.012). Similarly, the association between ps-Tg/TSH and 1-year ER remained significant after adjustment for RAI dose category, although the strength of the association weakened (OR = 0.78, 95% CI: 0.58–1.06, p = 0.043). This suggests that the main findings of this study were relatively stable, despite adjusting for dose-related factors. However, as this study is retrospective in nature, the potential for residual confounding related to RAI dose cannot be entirely excluded.

We also observed that TgAb levels, although within the negative range, were slightly higher in the ER group and showed a weak positive correlation with ER. This finding suggests that low-titer TgAb may reflect an underlying immunologic background rather than analytical interference. Its potential association with autoimmune thyroid conditions such as Hashimoto thyroiditis warrants further investigation [34]. Notably, FT4 levels showed no significant intergroup differences but were statistically associated with ER in univariate analysis. This likely reflects physiological interactions during levothyroxine withdrawal, wherein elevated TSH is accompanied by decreased FT4, indicating a strong inverse relationship [30,38,43]. Thus, FT4 pri­marily represents the adequacy of thyroid hormone withdrawal and metabolic responsiveness rather than residual disease or RAI outcome. Several studies have also reported collinearity between FT4 and Tg fluctuations; once Tg or Tg/TSH is included in multivariable models, the independent effect of FT4 diminishes, explaining the loss of statistical significance [28,34]. These findings are consistent with our results, suggesting that FT4 has limited influence on RAI efficacy and that its apparent statistical significance arises mainly from interaction effects with TSH and Tg, rather than a direct pathological association.

The strengths of this study include a relatively large sample size and the inclusion of PTC patients across different risk categories, which to some extent provides a relatively rich data basis for model construction. Internal validation suggested that the model had a certain degree of stability within this cohort. Nevertheless, several limitations should be acknowledged. This was a single-center retrospective study and may, therefore, be subject to selection bias. A small proportion of ATA low-risk patients who received RAI were included in this cohort, and the external applicability of the model may be affected given that treatment strategies for these patients may vary across centers. Adherence to levothyroxine withdrawal was not objectively monitored, and the number of patients treated with recombinant human TSH (rhTSH) was small, limiting the generalizability of the findings to that subgroup. Finally, the model has not yet undergone independent external validation, and its applicability across different regions, assay platforms, and treatment settings should therefore be interpreted with caution.

Conclusion

This study suggests that a combined model incorporating ps-Tg, the ps-Tg/TSH ratio, TgAb, pathological N stage, and the 2025 ATA risk stratification may offer incremental value in predicting 1-year excellent response after RAI therapy in patients with papillary thyroid carcinoma. The model may serve as a complement to existing postoperative risk assessment and provide additional reference for follow-up stratification and individualized management.
