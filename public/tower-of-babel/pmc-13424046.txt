Strain-Dependent Differences in Baseline Retinal Immune Homeostasis: A Comparative Study of Albino and Pigmented Mice
Authors: Miguel A Martínez-López, Víctor Paleo-García, Sara Rubio-Casado, José A Fernández-Albarral, José A Matamoros, Juan J Salazar, Ana I Ramírez, Eva M Marco, Meritxell López-Gallardo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMouse models are essential for glaucoma research; however, strain-dependent biological differences may complicate the interpretation and translational value of experimental findings. Because baseline retinal immune tone can shape the response to ocular hypertension (OHT), defining constitutive inflammatory profiles across commonly used mouse strains is critical. Basal intraocular pressure (IOP), retinal interleukin (IL)-1β, interferon gamma (IFN-γ), IL-6, IL-4, IL-10, and vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) levels by Luminex xMAP, and the cellular localization of inflammatory mediators by immunofluorescence using Iba-1, GFAP, Brn3a, NF-200, and VEGFR1 were compared between male albino Swiss CD1 and pigmented C57BL/6J mice. Basal IOP did not differ between strains, whereas the basal retinal immune environment did. CD1 mice showed higher IL-1β and IL-4 levels and detectable IFN-γ, whereas C57BL/6J mice showed higher IL-6, IL-10, and VEGF levels. Immunofluorescence revealed strain-specific localization: in CD1 retinas, IL-1β was detected in microglia and macroglia, IFN-γ in microglia, and IL-6 mainly in microglia; in C57BL/6J retinas, IL-1β and IL-6 were mainly associated with macroglia (Müller cells and astrocytes), and IL-4 with microglia. In both strains, IL-10 localized to NF-200-positive axons and VEGFR1 to glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP)-positive macroglia. Despite similar basal IOP, albino Swiss CD1 and pigmented C57BL/6J mice differ markedly in their basal retinal immune profiles. Albino mice show a more reactive baseline state, whereas pigmented mice display tighter immunoregulation and stronger immune-vascular support. Strain selection is therefore critical for experimental design, data interpretation, and translational relevance to ensure preclinical findings translate into human outcomes.Graphical Abstract Pigmented C57BL/6J and albino CD1 mice strains differ in retinal immune homeostasis. In general, CD1 mice show higher pro-inflammatory cytokines, whereas C57BL/6J mice exhibit increased anti-inflammatory and vascular-support factors (expressed as color intensity). These baseline cytokine strain-differences reflect a different location profile within different retinal cell populations, i.e. macroglial, microglial and retinal ganglion cells (RGCs) axons. Keywords: Retina, Albino CD1 mice, Pigmented C57BL/6J mice, Retinal ganglion cells (RGCs), Macroglial cells, Microglial cells

Introduction

Albino Swiss mice have been proposed as particularly useful in experimental glaucoma models because they exhibit higher susceptibility to Retinal Ganglion Cell (RGC) damage across different injury paradigms, including optic nerve crush, NMDA-induced excitotoxicity, and laser-induced OHT (Nadal-Nicolás et al. 2009; De Groef et al. 2016; Di Pierdomenico et al. 2022). However, it is important to also consider that albino rodents exhibit several neurodevelopmental and visual abnormalities, including reduced visual acuity, nystagmus, and partial or complete lack of melanin (Jeffery 1997; Chotard et al. 2024). Melanin is essential for proper retinal pigment epithelium (RPE) development and function. Its deficiency impairs critical processes—such as phototransduction support and photoreceptor outer segment phagocytosis—and disrupts embryonic RGC neurogenesis, ultimately altering RGC projections and axon decussation in central visual nuclei (Roffler-Tarlov et al. 2013; Iwai-Takekoshi et al. 2016). Moreover, inherent strain-dependent neuronal differences have also been described at the level of the RGC population. Salinas-Navarro et al. (2009) reported that adult Swiss albino mice contain approximately 49,500 RGCs per retina, whereas adult C57 pigmented mice contain approximately 42,700 RGCs, representing an approximately 17% difference between strains. This baseline difference in RGC number should be considered when interpreting RGC axon-related markers, axon projections and decussation. In addition, pigment-related alterations may also affect retinal function and photoreceptor organization. Chotard et al. (2024) demonstrated that B6 albino Tyrc/c mice show altered retinal functional responses compared with pigmented controls, supporting the idea that pigment mutations can influence retinal physiology. Similarly, differences in cone number and distribution have been described between albino and pigmented mice, indicating that pigmentation status may also affect the cellular architecture of the outer retina (Ortín-Martínez et al. 2014). Therefore, strain-dependent differences between albino and pigmented mice should be interpreted not only in terms of inflammatory or immune parameters, but also considering baseline neuronal, photoreceptor, and functional retinal features. Recently, in our facilities, we have detected an unusual rate of animals - Swiss CD1 mice from different suppliers - with a phenotype compatible with partial or complete photoreceptor loss, leading to visual impairment or even blindness (rates higher than 60%; unpublished observations). Consequently, these mice were not included in our research studies, prompting the selection of the inbred C57BL/6J strain as a genetically stable and reliable alternative.

C57BL/6J mice features a fully pigmented retina and have been widely used in neuroinflammation, neurodegeneration and ophthalmic research due to its phenotypic stability and robust visual function. C57BL/6J is the standard background for most transgenic models. In the context of ocular hydrodynamics, pigmented mouse strains most accurately approximate human intraocular pressure (IOP) profiles (Cameron Millar et al. 2015). Moreover, C57BL/6 mice have been reported to be more resistant in three experimental models related to glaucomatous optic neuropathy—optic nerve crush, NMDA-induced excitotoxicity, and laser-induced OHT (De Groef et al. 2016). However, the neurobiological mechanisms underlying these strain differences in the OHT responses, between Swiss CD-1 and pigmented C57BL/6J mice, are still to be fully described.

Given the fact that differences in the inflammatory responses to a variety of stimuli have been reported between these two mice strains (Nikodemova and Watters 2011; Carreras et al. 2019; Thiriot et al. 2022; Abhilash et al. 2025), similar differences in the immune response to OHT in the retina might be suggested. Therefore, it is crucial to first characterize the baseline retinal immune homeostasis in these distinct mouse strains.

While animal models are essential for understanding glaucoma, maximizing their translational value requires investigating diverse genetic backgrounds. Defining strain-dependent baseline variability is crucial for improving reproducibility, interpreting neuroinflammatory endpoints, and selecting appropriate preclinical models. This concept applies broadly to neurodegenerative settings where the basal cytokine milieu influences vulnerability to injury. Therefore, we aimed to characterize basal IOP and baseline retinal cytokine levels (interleukin IL-1β, IFN-γ, IL-6, IL-4, IL-10, VEGF) and cell-type expression patterns of key inflammatory mediators in adult male CD1 vs. C57BL/6J mice. These baseline profiles are essential context for future ocular hypertension or injury studies.

Materials and Methods

All procedures involving animals were carried out in compliance with the European Directive 2010/63/EU, the Spanish Royal Decree 53/2013, and the ARVO Statement for the Use of Animals in Ophthalmic and Vision Research. Experiments in albino Swiss CD1 mice (University of Murcia breeding colony) were approved by the Ethics Committee for Animal Research of the University of Murcia and by the Animal Health Service of the Regional Ministry of Agriculture and Water (January 11, 2017; protocol ID: A13170110). Experiments in male C57BL/6J mice (Charles River, Les Oncins, France) were approved by the Local Ethics Committee on Animal Experimentation of the Complutense University of Madrid and authorized by the Community of Madrid (CEEA-UCM: July 17, 2023; PROEX: 158.1/23). The study was reported in accordance with the ARRIVE (Animal Research: Reporting of In Vivo Experiments) guidelines (du Sert et al. 2020).

Animals

A total of 102 outbred albino Swiss CD1 male mice and 97 inbred C57BL/6J male mice were used in this study. It is worth mentioning that these animals have been included in other complementary studies (unpublished data) that investigate additional molecular, cellular and structural effects of the increment of IOP in the retina. In the present study, a certain number of animals were randomly selected for the multiplex analysis and the immunofluorescence study. Animals arrived at 12–16 weeks of age at the animal facility of the School of Medicine, Complutense University of Madrid (ANUC ES28079000086). CD1 mice were obtained from the University of Murcia breeding colony (Murcia, Spain), whereas C57BL/6J mice were purchased from Charles River Laboratories (Les Oncins, France). Both strains were housed under identical environmental conditions in the same facility. Due to housing limitations, experiments were conducted in two consecutive cohorts (Swiss CD1 first, followed by C57BL/6J). At arrival, mean body weight was 42 ± 2 g for CD1 mice and 26.36 ± 1.7 g for C57BL/6J mice. Animals were maintained in pairs in polycarbonate cages (55 × 32 × 18 cm) with environmental enrichment (cotton and cardboard tubes), under controlled conditions (21 ± 1 °C; 60 ± 10% humidity; 12 h light/dark cycle, lights on at 07:00), with ad libitum access to water and standard chow (Altromin®). A one-week acclimatization period was allowed prior to any experimental manipulation.

Intraocular Pressure (IOP) Measurements

The IOP (mmHg) was measured on all the animals. IOP was assessed in anesthetized mice using a rebound tonometer (Tono-Lab, Tiolat OY, Helsinki, Finland), as previously described (Fernández-Albarral et al. 2021; Martínez-López et al. 2024). Anesthesia was induced with 2% isoflurane (ISOFLO®, Zoetis SL, Spain) delivered in oxygen (1.5 L/min). For each independent eye, three consecutive readings were recorded, each consisting of the average of six automated measurements, and the final IOP value was calculated as the mean of these three readings. All measurements were performed at the same time of day to minimize circadian variation.

Multiplex Immunoassay

Mice were euthanized by intraperitoneal overdose of sodium pentobarbital (Dolethal Vetoquinol®, Especialidades Veterinarias, Alcobendas, Madrid, Spain). Eyes were enucleated and retinas were rapidly dissected. The RPE was gently removed from all retinas by brushing to minimize pigment-related interference. Retinas were immediately frozen at -80 °C.

Animals were randomly selected from each experimental group (strain) to obtain a sample size of 6 retinal pools coming from 3 retinas from different animals. Since the amount of proteins within each retina was not enough to measure all the indicated biomarkers; based on previous studies (Fernández-Albarral et al. 2021) 3 retinas had to be pooled to generate an appropriate biological sample for our purposes. The pooled retinal sample was considered the statistical unit for multiplex analysis; and given the nature of the study, the multiplex findings should be interpreted as exploratory biological trends. For each retinal pool, we took three retinas from three different randomly selected animals, and in each pool, we alternated right and left eyes obtaining two possible pools : 1 left and 2 right eyes, or 1 right and 2 left eyes, each eye belonging to different animals. However, because these animals also belong to a wider unpublished study, in the case of albino CD1 mice we only had access to a limited sample size. Therefore, a total of 6 CD1 mice were employed, providing 12 retinas organized in 4 pooled retinal samples from different animals (n = 4), and 9 C57BL/6J mice were employed, that provided 18 retinas organized in 6 pooled retinal samples from different animals (n = 6). Retinal tissue was homogenized in lysis buffer, followed by freezing and centrifugation, and supernatants were used for protein quantification as previously described (Fernández-Albarral et al. 2021). Samples were randomly coded prior to analysis to minimize bias.

Cytokine and chemokine concentrations were determined using a multiplex magnetic bead-based immunoassay (MILLIPLEX® MAP Mouse Cytokine/Chemokine Magnetic Bead Panel, Merck KGaA, Darmstadt, Germany) based on Luminex xMAP® technology. Quantification was performed using two platforms: Bio-Plex 200 for CD1 samples and xMAP INTELLIFLEX® for C57BL/6J samples. Both platforms rely on the same bead-based Luminex xMAP principle, and all samples were processed using the same commercial assay panel, the same manufacturer-recommended protocol, the same standard-curve strategy, and platform-specific calibration according to the manufacturers’ instructions. Moreover, previous technical comparisons between Bio-Plex 200 and xMAP INTELLIFLEX systems have shown comparable performance and correlated output when appropriate platform settings are used. Therefore, this strategy was considered appropriate for exploratory comparison of strain-dependent cytokine profiles. The analytes included pro-inflammatory cytokines (IFN-γ, IL-1β, IL-6), anti-inflammatory cytokines (IL-4, IL-10), and VEGF.

Immunofluorescence

Mice were deeply anesthetized with medetomidine (Medetor®, Virbac España S.A., Barcelona, Spain) and ketamine (Anesketin®, Dechra Veterinary Products SLU, Barcelona, Spain), and transcardially perfused with cold saline (0.9% NaCl), and then with cold 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) solution in 0.1 M phosphate buffer using a volume of 15 mL/g of bodyweight. For the immunofluorescence study a total of eight randomly selected animals (4 CD1 and 4 C57BL/6J) were employed.

The upper eyelid was sutured as an anatomical reference prior to enucleation. Eyes were then extracted and immersed in 4% PFA at 4 °C for 24 h. After removal of cornea and lens, eyecups containing the retina were post-fixed in the same fixative for an additional 24 h. Samples were washed three times (30 min each) in 0.1 M phosphate-buffered saline (PBS; Sigma-Aldrich, USA, Ref. P4417; pH 7.4).Tissues were cryoprotected in graded sucrose solutions (10%, 20%, and 30%) and embedded in tissue-freezing medium (Tissue-Tek® O.C.T. Compound, Sakura Finetek, Barcelona, Spain). Eyecups were stored at -30 °C until sectioning. Sagittal Sect. (16 μm thickness) were obtained using a Leica CM3050 cryostat (Leica Biosystems, Heidelberg, Germany). Sections were mounted on gelatin-coated slides (three sections per slide), air-dried, and stored at -30 °C until use.

Immunofluorescence staining was performed on four random slides per experimental group (n = 4) randomly selected for each tested antibody, based on previously published protocols (Fernández-Albarral et al. 2021) for selected pro- and anti- inflammatory mediators (IL-1β, IL-6, IL-4, IFN-γ and IL-10) and for VEGF Receptor 1 (VEGFR1), together with cell-type markers (Iba-1 for microglia, GFAP for astrocytes/Müller cell activation, and Brn3a for RGCs), and RGCs axon marker NF-200, as detailed in Table 1. For IL-4 and IL-6 immunostaining, antigen retrieval was performed before primary antibody incubation using a citrate-based antigen-unmasking solution (Vector Laboratories, Inc., Newark, CA, USA; Ref. H-3300) for 1 min at 100 °C, followed by rinsing in 0.1 M PBS. Because antibody reactivity differed between mouse strains, working dilutions were separately optimized for CD1 and C57BL/6J samples to achieve an appropriate signal-to-noise ratio. Immunofluorescence images from co-localization studies were employed only for descriptive purposes, and no quantitative analysis was performed; thus, no direct comparisons between the intensity of the staining were done.

During image acquisition and analysis, two independent investigators, blinded to the experimental groups, performed the evaluation. All samples were analyzed using an Axio Imager M.2 fluorescence microscope (Carl Zeiss AG, Oberkochen, Germany) equipped with an Apotome-2 module (Carl Zeiss AG, Oberkochen, Germany) and an AxioCam 503 Mono high-resolution camera (Carl Zeiss AG, Oberkochen, Germany). The microscope was fitted with a Zeiss filter set 64 for Alexa Fluor 594, a Zeiss filter set 49 for Alexa Fluor 405 and Zeiss filter set 10 for Alexa Fluor 488. Images were taken at room temperature using a 20× objective lens. Image acquisition and analysis were performed using the ZEN2 software version 3.6 (Carl Zeiss, Oberkochen, Germany). All lighting conditions, brightness and contrast, were kept constant during the capture process, and no non-linear transformations were performed. Finally, the Figs were designed using Adobe Photoshop 2024 version 25.3.1 (Adobe Systems, San Jose, CA, USA).

Statistical Analysis

The pooled retinal sample was considered the statistical unit for multiplex analysis. Data were analyzed by using the GraphPad Prism software (version 9.0; GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA); each value was assigned to the corresponding experimental group and the analysis was automatically performed. A two-tailed unpaired Student t-test was performed once normality and homoscedasticity were verified by using the Shapiro–Wilk and Levene tests, respectively. Sample size was determined based on availability and prior experience with multiplex analyses (Fernández-Albarral et al. 2021); therefore, the study should be considered exploratory. Two experimental groups (mouse strains: CD1 vs. C57BL/6J) were compared, with statistical significance set at p < 0.05. Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). All statistical tests were two-tailed.

Results

Intraocular Pressure (IOP) Measurements

When comparing IOP between C57BL/6J and CD1 mice no significant differences were observed: 14.70 ± 1.74 mmHg in C57BL/6J male mice and 14.80 ± 1.91 mmHg in CD1 male mice. These basal IOP levels are considered as normotensive.

Retinal Cytokines and VEGF

IL-1β

The retinal content of IL-1β in baseline conditions (Fig. 1) was significantly higher in CD1 mice compared to C57BL/6J animals [t(8) = 12.04, p < 0.0001, η2 = 0.95]. In the immunofluorescence study IL-1β was found in GFAP+ cells in both strains (Fig. 2), mainly in the GCL, that is, most probably associated to the astrocytic feet of Müller cells. In addition, in CD1 mice IL-1β was also observed in Iba-1 + cells, in deeper retinal layers.

IFN-γ

Retinal IFN-γ levels were only found in CD1 mice (7.59 ± 1.96 pg/mg), whereas in C57BL/6J mice, the content of IFN-γ was undetectable in the retina (Fig. 1.). Similarly, in the immunofluorescence study, IFN-γ only co-localized with Iba1 + cells, that is, microglial cells, in the INL (Fig. 3.), despite no IFN-γ expression could be detected in C57BL/6J mice.

Basal IL-6 levels were lower in CD1 than in C57BL/6J mice [t(8) = 3.47, p = 0.0084, η2 = 0.6] (Fig. 1). IL-6 immunoreactivity co-localized with GFAP+ cells in C57BL/6J retinas, whereas in CD1 retinas it co-localized predominantly with Iba-1 + microglia (Fig. 4).

CD1 mice showed higher basal levels of IL-4 compared to C57BL/6J mice [t(8) = 11.02, p < 0.0001, η2 = 0.94] ( Fig. 1). In CD1 no clear co-localization was found between IL-4 and Iba-1 nor GFAP, although in C57BL/6J mice retinas, the immunoreactivity study achieved a co-localization of both IL-4 and Iba-1 + cells, that is, microglia cells (Fig. 5).

Basal IL-10 levels in the retina of CD1 mice were significantly lower than those found in C57BL/6J mice retinas [t(8) = 4.54, p = 0.0019, η2 = 0.72] (Fig. 1). In this case, IL-10 did not co-localize with any of the cellular markers employed (i.e., Brn3a, GFAP, nor Iba-1) in any of the mice strains used, except for co-localization with NF-200, that is, suggesting IL-10 to be expressed along RGC axons (Fig. 6).

Discussion

Herein we have demonstrated a differential profile of baseline cytokine and neurotrophic/vasculoprotective mediators in the retinal of two mouse strains widely used in biomedical research: outbred albino Swiss CD1 and inbred pigmented C57BL/6J. By combining multiplex quantification with qualitative cell-type colocalization, we demonstrate that baseline retinal immune homeostasis varies significantly across these strains. Thereafter, these baseline differences likely influence how each model responds to neurodegenerative and inflammatory insults, as OHT in our glaucoma experimental model. Accounting for these baseline strain differences is crucial for interpreting responses to neurodegenerative diseases like glaucoma. Furthermore, this comparative approach provides a framework to explore similar variability in human albinism and highlights how baseline disparities might confound the evaluation of new therapies Furthermore, understanding these baseline differences across mouse strains significantly improves the translational potential of preclinical drug testing.

No differences in basal IOP levels were observed between C57BL/6J and CD1 male mice. However, previous studies, have reported inter-strain differences, being IOPs higher in the pigmented versus the albino strains (Savinova et al. 2001; Cameron Millar et al. 2015). In the present study, we reported IOP values similar to those previously reported for pigmented (C57BL/6J) mice, although the IOP values achieved for our albino strain, CD1 mice, seemed to be slightly higher to those reported in other albino (A/J and BALB/cJ) strains (Cameron Millar et al. 2015), but similar to IOP values previously found (Cuenca et al. 2010; Vidal-Sanz et al. 2012). These differences in IOP were related to differences in aqueous humor dynamics, and thus, from this perspective, of all strains considered, the pigmented strains were those proposed to resemble the human eye more closely in this regard (Cameron Millar et al. 2015).

Regarding pro-inflammatory cytokines, CD1 mice showed markedly higher basal IL-1β levels than C57BL/6J mice. IL-1β has been directly implicated in retinal pathology, including diabetic retinopathy, where it accelerates apoptosis of retinal capillary cells through NF-κB activation, particularly under high-glucose conditions (Kowluru and Odenbach 2004). Therefore, the higher basal IL-1β levels observed in CD1 mice may reflect a retinal environment with greater intrinsic vulnerability to inflammatory and vascular stress. Consistent with this interpretation, CD1 mice displayed a higher abundance of microglial cells in the retinal photoreceptor outer segment layer, whereas these cells were scarce in C57BL/6J retinas. Our colocalization analyses further support this view: in Swiss CD1 mice, IL-1β expression was detected in both microglial and macroglial cells, whereas in C57BL/6J mice IL-1β colocalized with macroglia. The involvement of two glial populations in IL-1β production may therefore account for the substantially higher basal levels detected in the retina of CD1 mice. In the same direction, IFN-γ was only detected in albino Swiss CD1 animals. IFN‑γ is one of the most potent natural inducers of microglial activation, promoting both their activation state and their production of inflammatory mediators (Langmann 2007; Nakagawa and Chiba 2014; Ivashkiv 2018). Actually, in Swiss CD1 mice, our colocalization data confirm the expression of IFN‑γ exclusively in microglial cells. In contrast, IFN-γ could not be detected in C57BL/6J animals, suggesting that its retinal expression under basal conditions may be transient, minimal, or below the detection limits of the employed techniques. This result is also consistent with previous evidence of strain-dependent immune responses outside the retina, since Carreras et al. (2019) reported lower IFN-γ levels in C57BL/6J mice than in CD1 mice following an experimental inflammatory challenge. Although obtained in a systemic fungal sepsis model, these findings support the concept that C57BL/6J and CD1 mice differ intrinsically in their IFN-γ-related inflammatory responses. Furthermore, this discrepancy may arise from strain-specific immune regulation, as the functional RPE in C57BL/6J mice secretes immunosuppressive factors (TGFβ and PEDF) to maintain immune privilege and dampen proinflammatory pathways such as IFN-γ (Strauss 2005; De Groef et al. 2016). This interpretation is further supported by the cellular landscape: microglial cells are highly abundant in the photoreceptor outer-segment layer of Swiss CD1 mice, yet notably scarce in C57BL/6J mice (Rojas et al. 2014), as indicated above.

Conversely, IL-6 exhibited the opposite pattern, with pigmented C57BL/6J mice displaying higher basal levels than albino Swiss CD1 mice. This reduction in the albino strain likely reflects decreased IL-6 secretion from the RPE, a primary physiological source. In contrast, among pigmented mice, this baseline IL-6 can drive a feed-forward loop, activating both microglial and macroglial cells to further amplify its own production (Kettenmann et al. 2013; Nakagawa and Chiba 2014). Although IL-6 is generally considered as a pro-inflammatory cytokine (Wang et al. 2011; Zhou et al. 2025), several studies have demonstrated clear anti-inflammatory and neuroprotective properties, including the ability to counteract inflammatory mediators and prevent RGC apoptosis (Sappington et al. 2006; Scheller et al. 2011; Echevarria et al. 2017; Willis et al. 2020). In a retinal ischemia/reperfusion model, exogenous IL-6 prevented approximately 50–70% of RGC loss, supporting a protective role for IL-6 in the inner retina after injury (Sanchez et al. 2003). Similarly, IL-6 has been identified as a marker of axonal transport disruption during experimental glaucoma and as a mediator that stimulates neuritogenesis in cultured RGCs (Chidlow et al. 2012). Our colocalization data reveal a striking strain-specific pattern: IL-6 is found primarily in microglia in CD1 retinas, but predominantly in GFAP+ Müller/astrocytes in C57BL/6J (Fig. 4). This suggests that in retina astrocytes and Müller cells produce IL-6 constitutivelyand implies that macroglia-derived IL-6 helps maintain baseline retinal homeostasis in pigmented strains.

Regarding basal anti-inflammatory cytokines, Swiss CD1 mice exhibited higher IL-4 levels than C57BL/6J mice. Intriguingly, while IL-4 expression was restricted to microglia in pigmented mice, it did not colocalize with any assessed glial populations in Swiss CD1 retinas. This suggests two possibilities: IL-4 in albino mice may originate from non-glial sources, such as infiltrating or peripheral immune cells (Portales-Cervantes et al. 2020; Plum et al. 2024)., or its elevation reflects a compensatory response to a more reactive basal retinal milieu. Ultimately, these findings indicate that IL-4 plays distinct, strain-specific roles in retinal immune homeostasis (Colton 2009). Functionally, IL-4 exerts potent anti-inflammatory and anti-apoptotic effects, protecting against RGC axonal degeneration and promoting a neuroprotective microglial phenotype (Zuo et al. 2024). This shift is especially vital in tissues with high basal inflammation, such as the albino retina. Given the developmental alterations and elevated oxidative stress inherent to albino retinas, their higher basal IL-4 levels are likely to function as an adaptive response to counterbalance these intrinsic vulnerabilities.

Similarly, basal IL-10 and VEGF levels were higher in C57BL/6J than in Swiss CD1 mice, reflecting stronger baseline protection against inflammatory damage in the pigmented strain. IL-10 constrains pro-inflammatory signaling, while VEGF—constitutively produced by cells such as Müller glia and the RPE—provides essential vascular and trophic support (Boyd et al. 2003; Wang et al. 2010; Porro et al. 2020). Although the RPE was removed during dissection, its in vivo influence likely still contributes to this neural retina homeostasis. In the retina, IL-10 signaling has been shown to promote RGC survival through STAT3-dependent mechanisms and to regulate stress-induced apoptosis (Boyd et al. 2003; Porro et al. 2020). VEGF, although frequently associated with pathological vascular permeability, also has important retinal cell-derived functions; Müller cell-derived VEGF is a key mediator of retinal vascular responses and glial-vascular communication (Wang et al. 2010). Therefore, the higher basal IL-10/VEGF profile in C57BL/6J mice may indicate a more active regulatory, trophic, and vascular-supportive retinal environment. Consistent with these distinct regulatory roles, our immunofluorescence data showed that basal IL-10 was restricted exclusively to RGC axons, lacking glial colocalization. In contrast, VEGFR1 localized to GFAP-positive macroglia in both strains, underscoring the role of macroglia in physiological VEGF signaling and vasculoprotection.

Taken together, these findings have practical implications for model selection and experimental design. Although basal IOP was comparable under the present conditions, the distinct retinal cytokine profiles indicate that Swiss CD1 and C57BL/6J mice should not be considered interchangeable. The relatively reactive baseline milieu observed in Swiss CD1 mice, characterized by higher IL-1β levels and detectable IFN-γ, may be advantageous for studies focused on susceptibility to retinal ganglion cell damage, inflammatory amplification, or neuroprotective interventions in optic nerve injury, excitotoxic damage, or ocular hypertension models, in which albino/CD1 backgrounds have been reported to show greater vulnerability. Conversely, the higher basal IL-10 and VEGF levels found in C57BL/6J mice, together with their pigmented retina, stable visual phenotype, and widespread use as the background for genetically modified lines, may make this strain particularly useful for mechanistic studies requiring controlled immunoregulation, preserved visual function, or evaluation of glial and vascular support. Accordingly, strain-dependent baseline differences should be incorporated into strain selection, sample-size estimation, endpoint definition, and the interpretation of cytokine changes after injury, rather than being regarded as a secondary experimental variable. Beyond glaucoma, the constitutive immune and vasculotrophic differences described here may also be relevant for experimental models of retinal inflammation, neurodegeneration, excitotoxicity, ischemic or vascular stress, and immune-mediated ocular diseases, in which the pre-existing balance between pro-inflammatory and regulatory/trophic signals can influence disease onset, progression, and therapeutic response (Nikodemova and Watters 2011; Cameron Millar et al. 2015; De Groef et al. 2016; Di Pierdomenico et al. 2022).

In conclusion, our findings demonstrate that albino Swiss CD1 and pigmented C57BL/6J mice exhibit markedly different basal retinal immune landscapes despite similar basal IOP levels. Swiss CD1 mice showed a more reactive baseline inflammatory profile, whereas C57BL/6J mice displayed stronger immunoregulatory and vasculotrophic features. These findings provide a critical framework for reinterpreting discrepant, strain-specific outcomes in experimental models of neurodegenerative diseases like glaucoma. They powerfully underscore that mouse strains are not interchangeable when investigating retinal neuroinflammation, neuroprotection, or vascular homeostasis. Ultimately, because this baseline dictates how the retina reacts to disease, it heavily impacts the validity of animal models in drug discovery. Therefore, careful strain selection is essential for reliable drug testing and ensuring preclinical results actually translate to human patients.

Study Limitations

Several limitations should be considered. First, animals were studied in two consecutive cohorts (Swiss CD1 first, then C57BL/6J) due to housing-capacity constraints. Although both cohorts were processed in the same facility using identical protocols, Swiss CD1 and C57BL/6J animals originated from different providers; therefore, minor cohort-related variability (e.g., vendor-associated microbiota differences or other time-dependent factors) cannot be entirely excluded. Second, only male mice were included, and given known sex-dependent differences in immune and glial signaling, these basal strain-specific profiles may not fully generalize to females. Third, cytokine quantification was performed on two xMAP detection platforms (Bio-Plex 200 and INTELLIFLEX). Both use Luminex xMAP technology, standardized calibration workflows and previous technical comparisons support cross-platform concordance between Bio-Plex 200 and xMAP INTELLIFLEX systems; but a direct bridge validation using the same retinal samples on both platforms was not performed in the present study. Therefore, small platform-dependent differences in sensitivity or fluorescence acquisition cannot be fully excluded. Fourth, the multiplex analysis included a small number of biological replicates per strain. Although Shapiro–Wilk testing was applied, its power is limited with small sample sizes; therefore, p-values were interpreted cautiously and complemented with effect-size estimates.
