Multiple polylogarithms, a regularisation process and an admissible open domain of convergence
Pawan Singh Mehta and Biswajyoti Saha

Abstract
. In this article, we study the analytic properties of the multiple polylogarithms in the s s -aspect. Although the domain of absolute convergence of the series defining the multiple polylogarithms is well-known, the study towards a larger open domain of (conditional) convergence has been limited, particularly when the depth is ≥ 2 \geq 2 . Here, we exhibit a larger open domain of (conditional) convergence for this series by writing certain translation formulas satisfied by them. The series moreover defines a holomorphic function in this open set. We then introduce a regularisation process for the multiple polylogarithms, extending an earlier work of the second author. This regularisation process requires a generalisation of the Euler-Boole summation formula that we derive in the appendix of this article. The regularisation process leads to a larger open domain, where the series (conditionally) converges at integer points. The holomorphicity at such points is a more delicate question and this regularisation process is to be used to study the local behaviour of the multiple polylogarithms around such points.

1.
Introduction

Throughout this article, we denote the set of all non-negative integers by
{\mathbb{N}}
.
Let
a
be a positive integer and
z
be a complex number with
\lvert z\rvert<1
. The classical
polylogarithm function is defined by the convergent power series

\mathrm{Li}(a;z):=\sum_{n>0}\frac{z^{n}}{n^{a}}.

Convergence of such a series depends on
z
and
a
both. For example, we can take
\lvert z\rvert\leq 1
,
if
a\geq 2
. However, for
\lvert z\rvert>1
, the series is not convergent.

For a fixed value of
z
with
\lvert z\rvert\leq 1
, the polylogarithm functions can be considered as the
Dirichlet series with the integer
a
replaced by a complex variable
s
such that
\Re(s)>1
, i.e.

\mathrm{Li}_{z}(s):=\sum_{n>0}\frac{z^{n}}{n^{s}}.

Note that the above Dirichlet series converges (absolutely) for every complex number
s
if
|z|<1
.
Now if
|z|=1
, the domain of convergence of this Dirichlet series depends on the value of
z
.
For
z=1
, we get back the ubiquitous Riemann zeta function, which converges (absolutely) for
\Re(s)>1
.
However, for
|z|=1
with
z\neq 1
, the Dirichlet series
\mathrm{Li}_{z}(s)
converges for
\Re(s)>0
.
This is immediate as the partial sum
\sum_{N>n>0}z^{n}=\frac{z-z^{N}}{1-z}
is bounded. This also shows
that in this case, the Dirichlet series
\mathrm{Li}_{z}(s)
does not converge for any
s
with
\Re(s)<0
.

The multiple polylogarithms are defined by considering several variable analogue of the classical polylogarithm functions.
For positive integers
r
,
a_{1},\ldots,a_{r}
and complex number
z_{1},\ldots,z_{r}
with
|z_{i}|<1
for all
1\leq i\leq r
,
the multiple polylogarithm (of depth
r
) is defined by the convergent power series

\mathrm{Li}(a_{1},\ldots,a_{r};z_{1},\ldots,z_{r}):=\sum_{n_{1}>\cdots>n_{r}>0}\frac{z_{1}^{n_{1}}\cdots z_{r}^{n_{r}}}{n_{1}^{a_{1}}\cdots n_{r}^{a_{r}}}.

Again, this definition can be extended for
|z_{i}|\leq 1
for all
1\leq i\leq r
, if
a_{1}\geq 2
.
Further, this series can be considered as a multiple Dirichlet series by fixing complex numbers

z_{1},\ldots,z_{r}
and replacing
a_{1},\ldots,a_{r}
by complex variables
s_{1},\ldots,s_{r}

with
\Re(s_{1}+\cdots+s_{i})>i
for
1\leq i\leq r
, i.e.,

(1)

\mathrm{Li}_{(z_{1},\ldots,z_{r})}(s_{1},\ldots,s_{r}):=\sum_{n_{1}>\cdots>n_{r}>0}\frac{z_{1}^{n_{1}}\cdots z_{r}^{n_{r}}}{n_{1}^{s_{1}}\cdots n_{r}^{s_{r}}},

where
(s_{1},\ldots,s_{r})\in U_{r}
with

U_{r}:=\{(s_{1},\ldots,s_{r})\in{\mathbb{C}}^{r}~:~\Re(s_{1}+\cdots+s_{i})>i~\text{ for all }~1\leq i\leq r\}.

The above series converges normally on compact subsets of
U_{r}
and
therefore defines a holomorphic function there (see
[
7
, Proposition 2]
).
The second author further showed in
[
7
, Theorem 9]
(albeit using different notations)
that the multiple Dirichlet series (
1
) can be extended to a meromorphic function on the
whole of
{\mathbb{C}}^{r}
and the set of singularities depends on
the values of the product
z_{[1,i]}:=z_{1}\cdots z_{i}
for
1\leq i\leq r
.

Theorem 1

(Saha)
.

The function
\mathrm{Li}_{(z_{1},\ldots,z_{r})}(s_{1},\ldots,s_{r})

extends to a meromorphic function on the whole of
{\mathbb{C}}^{r}
.
If
z_{[1,i]}\neq 1
for all
1\leq i\leq r
, then

\mathrm{Li}_{(z_{1},\ldots,z_{r})}(s_{1},\ldots,s_{r})
is holomorphic on
{\mathbb{C}}^{r}
.
Otherwise, if
i_{1}<\cdots<i_{m}
denote all the indices such that
z_{[1,i_{j}]}=1

for all
1\leq j\leq m
, then the set of all possible singularities of

\mathrm{Li}_{(z_{1},\ldots,z_{r})}(s_{1},\ldots,s_{r})
can be given as follows:

(a)
If
i_{1}=1
, then
\mathrm{Li}_{(z_{1},\ldots,z_{r})}(s_{1},\ldots,s_{r})
is
holomorphic outside the union of the hyperplanes given by the equations

s_{1}=1;~s_{1}+\cdots+s_{i_{j}}=n\ \text{ for all }n\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{\leq j}\ \text{and }2\leq j\leq m.

(b)
If
i_{1}\not=1
, then
\mathrm{Li}_{(z_{1},\ldots,z_{r})}(s_{1},\ldots,s_{r})
is
holomorphic outside the union of the hyperplanes given by the equations

s_{1}+\cdots+s_{i_{j}}=n\text{ for all }n\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{\leq j}\text{ and }1\leq j\leq m.

This indicates that there could be an open domain of convergence bigger than
U_{r}

for the series in (
1
), while treating it as the limit

(2)

\lim_{N\to\infty}\sum_{N>n_{1}>\cdots>n_{r}>0}\frac{z_{1}^{n_{1}}\cdots z_{r}^{n_{r}}}{n_{1}^{s_{1}}\cdots n_{r}^{s_{r}}},

depending on the values of
z_{i}
, exactly as in the case of depth
r=1
.
This question does not seem to have been addressed before.
If all the
z_{i}
’s are of unit modulus, the convergence of the series in (
1
) outside
U_{r}
will be conditional
convergence and hence we follow the above convention for the convergence going forward.
It is easy to see that for
s\in{\mathbb{C}}
with
\Re(s)>1
, the series

\sum_{n_{1}>n_{2}>0}\frac{(-1)^{n_{2}}}{n_{1}^{s}}
converges and the sum is
-2^{-s}\zeta(s)
.
One can also see that the series
\sum_{n_{1}>n_{2}>0}\frac{(-1)^{n_{1}}}{n_{1}}
does not converge,
although the multiple Dirichlet series
\mathrm{Li}_{(-1,1)}(s_{1},s_{2})
is holomorphic everywhere in

{\mathbb{C}}^{2}
. Hence there are some inherent intricacies. This brings us to our first theorem. We need some notations.

For
r\geq 1
an integer and
{\bf z}:=(z_{1},\ldots,z_{r})\in{\mathbb{C}}^{r}
where
|z_{i}|\leq 1
for all
1\leq i\leq r
,
let
q({\bf z})
be the smallest positive integer such that
1\leq q({\bf z})\leq r
and the product
z_{[1,q({\bf z})]}\neq 1
.
If no such
q({\bf z})
exists, then we set
q({\bf z})=r+1
. We define an open set
U_{r}(\mathbf{z})
of
\mathbb{C}^{r}
as follows:

(3)

\begin{split}U_{r}(\mathbf{z}):=\{&(s_{1},\ldots,s_{r})\in\mathbb{C}^{r}:\Re(s_{1}+\cdots+s_{i})>i\text{ if }1\leq i<q({\bf z})\\
&\text{ and }\Re(s_{1}+\cdots+s_{i})>i-1\text{ if }q({\bf z})\leq i\leq r\}.\end{split}

Note that in general, we have
U_{r}\subseteq U_{r}(\mathbf{z})
. Further,
U_{r}(\mathbf{z})=U_{r}

if and only if
z_{i}=1
for all
1\leq i\leq r
(i.e.,
q({\bf z})=r+1
).
In this context, we prove the following theorem.

Theorem 2
.

With the notations and convention as above, the multiple Dirichlet series in (
1
)
converges on
U_{r}(\mathbf{z})
. Moreover, it defines a holomorphic function on
U_{r}(\mathbf{z})
.

This theorem therefore extends the known result for the depth
1
Dirichlet series
\mathrm{Li}_{z}(s)
.
Also, in the last example, note that the point
(1,0)
lies on the boundary of
U_{2}(-1,1)
.
However, this open domain of convergence is not optimal in general and we can do better.
For example, as
N
tends to
\infty
, one has

(4)

\sum_{N>n_{1}>n_{2}>0}\frac{(-1)^{n_{2}}}{n_{1}^{2}n_{2}^{-1}}=\frac{\log 2}{2}-\frac{\pi^{2}}{16}+o(1).

Clearly,
(2,-1)\notin U_{2}(1,-1)
. In fact, as we can see, for
s\in{\mathbb{C}}
with
\Re(s)>1
, the limit

\lim_{N\to\infty}\sum_{N>n_{1}>n_{2}>0}\frac{(-1)^{n_{2}}}{n_{1}^{s}n_{2}^{-1}}

exists. Our next aim in this article is therefore to establish a more accurate and larger open domain
where the series in (
1
) (viewed as the limit in (
2
)) converges at such interesting integer points.
To do this we need to set up a regularisation process for the multiple polylogarithms (at roots of unity), following the work
of the second author
[
8
]
. This is done in the next section. Using this regularisation process, for roots of unity
z_{1},\ldots,z_{r}
,
we can give a more accurate and larger open domain where the multiple Dirichlet series
\mathrm{Li}_{(z_{1},\ldots,z_{r})}(s_{1},\ldots,s_{r})

converges at integer points. The optimality of this admissible open domain of convergence can be explained
in light of the local behaviour of the multiple polylogarithms at integral points, which is going to appear in a forthcoming article
[
6
]
.

To state our theorem in this context, we will need a few more notations. Let
r\geq 1
be an integer and
\mathbf{z}=(z_{1},\ldots,z_{r})\in{\mathbb{C}}^{r}

be such that
z_{1},\ldots,z_{r}
are roots of unity. For
1\leq i\leq j\leq r
, consider the products
z_{[i,j]}:=z_{i}\cdots z_{j}
and
for each
1\leq j\leq r
, define

(5)

I_{j}(\mathbf{z}):=\{i:1\leq i\leq j\text{ and }z_{[i,j]}=1\}\ \text{ and }\ Q_{j}(\mathbf{z}):=|I_{j}(\mathbf{z})|,

the number of elements in
I_{j}(\mathbf{z})
.
Note that in general there is no comparing relations among various
Q_{j}(\mathbf{z})
for
1\leq j\leq r
.
Consider the open set

(6)

V_{r}(\mathbf{z})=\{(s_{1},\ldots,s_{r})\in{\mathbb{C}}^{r}:\Re(s_{1}+\cdots+s_{i})>Q_{i}(\mathbf{z})\text{ for all }1\leq i\leq r\}.

It is easy to see that
V_{r}(\mathbf{z})=U_{r}
if and only if
\mathbf{z}=(1,\ldots,1)
. Moreover,

U_{r}(\mathbf{z})\subseteq V_{r}(\mathbf{z})
. This follows from the fact that

Q_{i}(\mathbf{z})\leq i
for all
1\leq i\leq r
and further
Q_{i}(\mathbf{z})\leq i-1
for all
q(\mathbf{z})\leq i\leq r
.
Note that
U_{2}(-1,1)=V_{2}(-1,1)
and
U_{2}(-1,-1)=V_{2}(-1,-1)
, but
U_{2}(1,-1)\subsetneq V_{2}(1,-1)
,
as
(2,-1)\in V_{2}(1,-1)\setminus U_{2}(1,-1)
. Moreover, the special value of the series
ar
(2,-1)
is
\frac{\log 2}{2}-\frac{\pi^{2}}{16}
(see (
4
)).
In this regard, we prove the following theorem.

Theorem 3
.

Let
r\geq 1
and
z_{1},\ldots,z_{r}\in{\mathbb{C}}
be roots of unity. The multiple Dirichlet series
converges at integral points of
V_{r}(\mathbf{z})
. Moreover, for an integral point
(s_{1},\ldots,s_{r})\in V_{r}(\mathbf{z})

and
k_{1},\ldots,k_{r}\in\mathbb{N}
, the series

(7)

\sum_{n_{1}>\cdots>n_{r}>0}\frac{z_{1}^{n_{1}}(\log n_{1})^{k_{1}}\cdots z_{r}^{n_{r}}(\log n_{r})^{k_{r}}}{n_{1}^{s_{1}}\cdots n_{r}^{s_{r}}}

converges when we consider it as the limit

(8)

\lim_{N\to\infty}\sum_{N>n_{1}>\cdots>n_{r}>0}\frac{z_{1}^{n_{1}}(\log n_{1})^{k_{1}}\cdots z_{r}^{n_{r}}(\log n_{r})^{k_{r}}}{n_{1}^{s_{1}}\cdots n_{r}^{s_{r}}}.

Our proof of Theorem
2
uses translation formulas satisfied by the partial tails of the
series in (
1
). A prototype of such translation formulas can be found in
[
7
, Theorems 6, 7]
(which was
derived for the whole sum). The proof of Theorem
2
is given in Section 3.

But this method does not seem to yield Theorem
3
. For a proof of Theorem
3
, we need to set up
a regularisation process for the multiple polylogarithms (at the roots of unity). The regularisation process allows us
to assign a (regularised) value of the series in (
7
), even when it does not converge. But more importantly,
this regularised value would be equal to the sum of the series in (
7
) whenever it converges.
To give an example, consider the series
\sum_{n>0}(-1)^{n}
. This series does not converge,
but we can write, for any positive integer
N
,

\sum_{N>n>0}{(-1)^{n}}=-\frac{(-1)^{N}}{2}-\frac{1}{2}.

Hence, we set the regularised value of this series to be
-1/2
. The details of this regularisation process is
discussed in the next section (Section 2), which is of independent interest and its other important implications
are explored in our forthcoming article
[
6
]
. Although, we need to build on the work of the second author

[
8
]
, there are key differences that we encounter here.
For example, together with the Euler-Maclaurin summation formula, we need an extension of the Euler-Boole summation formula.
A structural proof of the Euler-Boole summation formula along with a proof of the Euler-Maclaurin summation formula
was presented in
[
2
]
by Borwein, Calkin and Manna.
We follow their exposition to get a generalised Euler-Boole summation formula for the roots of unity.
This does not seem to have been done before. We have added it as an appendix to this article to keep our exposition
independent to the extent possible.
Finally, we give the proof of
Theorem
3
in Section 4. We remark here that even though we have convergence of the series
(
7
) in
V_{r}({\bf z})\cap{\mathbb{Z}}^{r}
, the holomorphicity of
\mathrm{Li}_{(z_{1},\ldots,z_{r})}(s_{1},\ldots,s_{r})

is a more delicate question as one may not have uniform convergence of (
1
)
in the domain
V_{r}({\bf z})
. The question of holomorphicity of
\mathrm{Li}_{(z_{1},\ldots,z_{r})}(s_{1},\ldots,s_{r})

is also explored in
[
6
]
, by means
of understanding the local behaviour of the multiple polylogarithms at integral points.

It is important to mention that a structured study of the multiple polylogarithms was initiated by
Goncharov in 1990’s. A detailed account of that can be found in
[
5
]
and the
references therein. As we develop the regularisation process of the multiple polylogarithms
at the roots of unity, we refer the reader to
[
4
,
1
,
10
]
for some existing literature on the
special values of the multiple polylogarithms at the roots of unity. Various analytic properties of the multiple
polylogarithms, including the analytic continuation as functions of
z_{1},\ldots,z_{r}
, have also been
studied (see
[
5
, §2]
,
[
9
]
).

2.
Regularisation process for the special values of the multiple polylogarithms and the higher order derivatives

To define a regularisation process for the special values of the multiple zeta functions, in
[
8
]
, the second author
needed the concept of a comparison scale and asymptotic expansion of a sequence of complex numbers
relative to the given comparison scale. Here we need the concept of asymptotic expansion of a sequence
of complex numbers relative to the given comparison scale
with variable coefficients
, as given in

[
3
, Chap. V, §2.5]
.

In what follows,
{\mathbb{N}}
denotes the set of non-negative integers. Let
\mathcal{E}
be the comparison scale,
on the set
{\mathbb{N}}
, filtered by the Fréchet filter, formed by the sequences

\left((\log n)^{l}n^{-m}\right)_{n\geq 1},

where
l\in{\mathbb{N}}
and
m\in{\mathbb{Z}}
. Also, let
r
be a positive integer and
z_{1},\ldots,z_{r}\in{\mathbb{C}}
be roots of unity.
Let
\mathcal{C}
be the
{\mathbb{C}}
-algebra generated by the constant sequence
(1)_{n\geq 1}
and the sequences
of the form
(z_{i}^{n})_{n\geq 1}
for
1\leq i\leq r
.
Note that as a
{\mathbb{C}}
-vector space,
\mathcal{C}
is finite dimensional since the set

\mathcal{S}:=\{z_{1}^{k_{1}}\cdots z_{r}^{k_{r}}:k_{i}\in{\mathbb{N}}\text{ for }1\leq i\leq r\}

is finite. For every element
\xi\in\mathcal{S}
, there are infinitely many
(k_{1},\ldots,k_{r})\in{\mathbb{N}}^{r}

such that
\xi=z_{1}^{k_{1}}\cdots z_{r}^{k_{r}}
. By the exponent of
\xi
, we mean the smallest such
element in
{\mathbb{N}}^{r}
, as per the dictionary ordering. Let
S
denote the set of exponents of
\mathcal{S}
.
Then
\mathcal{C}
has a basis of the form

(9)

\mathcal{B}=\{(z_{1}^{k_{1}n}\cdots z_{r}^{k_{r}n})_{n\geq 1}:(k_{1},\ldots,k_{r})\in S\},

As
(1)_{n\geq 1}\in\mathcal{B}
, we have
(0,\ldots,0)\in S
. It is immediate that
\mathcal{B}

is a spanning set for
\mathcal{C}
. Moreover,
as we are only taking
(k_{1},\ldots,k_{r})\in S
, we get the linear independence
using a Vandermonde determinant trick. The
{\mathbb{C}}
-algebra
\mathcal{C}
satisfies the following conditions:

(a)

for every sequence
(a_{n})_{n\geq 1}\in\mathcal{C}
,
(a_{n})_{n\geq 1}
is a bounded sequence;

(b)

if
(a_{n})_{n\geq 1}\in\mathcal{C}
with
a_{n}=o(1)
for sufficiently large
n
,
then we must have
a_{n}=0
for all but finitely many
n
.

This allows us to define the concept of asymptotic expansion of a sequence
of complex numbers relative to
\mathcal{E}
with coefficients in
\mathcal{C}
(see
[
3
, Chap. V, §2.5]
).

Definition 1
.

We say that a complex sequence
(u_{n})_{n\geq 1}
has an asymptotic expansion relative
to the comparison scale
\mathcal{E}
with coefficients in
\mathcal{C}
to arbitrary precision if
there exists a family of sequences
\left((a_{n}^{(l,m)})_{n\geq 1}\right)_{l\in{\mathbb{N}},m\in{\mathbb{Z}}}
in
\mathcal{C}

such that we can write

u_{n}=\sum_{l\geq 0,\hskip 1.42262ptm\leq A}a_{n}^{(l,m)}(\log n)^{l}n^{-m}+o(n^{-A})\text{ as }n\to\infty,

for any integer
A
, where for
m
sufficiently small, these sequences are the constant sequence
(0)_{n\geq 1}
,
and for any
m
, these sequences are the constant sequence
(0)_{n\geq 1}
for all but finitely many
l\in{\mathbb{N}}
.
The smallest
m
such that a sequence of the form
\left(a_{n}^{(l,m)}\right)_{n\geq 1}
is non-zero for some
l\in{\mathbb{N}}
, is called
the order of the asymptotic expansion.

If a complex sequence
(u_{n})_{n\geq 1}
has an asymptotic expansion relative to
\mathcal{E}

with coefficients in
\mathcal{C}
to arbitrary precision, then this expansion is unique
(see
[
3
]
, Chap
V
, §2.5). Now we consider the sequence
\big(a_{n}^{(0,0)}\big)_{n\geq 1}
and we can
write it as a linear combination of elements of
\mathcal{B}
as follows:

\left(a_{n}^{(0,0)}\right)_{n\geq 1}=\sum_{(k_{1},\ldots,k_{r})\in S}\lambda_{(k_{1},\ldots,k_{r})}(z_{1}^{k_{1}n}\cdots z_{r}^{k_{r}n})_{n\geq 1},

where
\lambda_{(k_{1},\ldots,k_{r})}\in{\mathbb{C}}
.

Definition 2
.

Let
(u_{n})_{n\geq 1}
be a complex sequence having an asymptotic expansion relative to
\mathcal{E}

with coefficients in
\mathcal{C}
to arbitrary precision. The complex number
\lambda_{(0,\ldots,0)}

is called the regularised value of the sequence
(u_{n})_{n\geq 1}
(relative to
\mathcal{E}
and
\mathcal{C}
).

Note that if two sequences differ by only finitely many terms and one of them has
an asymptotic expansion relative to
\mathcal{E}
with coefficients in
\mathcal{C}
to arbitrary precision,
then the other one also has such an expansion and their asymptotic expansions are the same. This
observation allows us to extend Definition
2
to sequences
(u_{n})
which are only
defined for
n
large enough.

Example 1
.

Let
\mathcal{C}_{1}
be the
{\mathbb{C}}
-algebra generated by the constant sequence
(1)_{n\geq 1}
and sequence
((-1)^{n})_{n\geq 1}
.
For the sequences,
(u_{n})_{n\geq 1}=\sum_{n>m>0}{(-1)^{m}}
and
(v_{n})_{n\geq 1}=\sum_{n>m>0}{(-1)^{m}\log m}
, we have

u_{n}=-\frac{(-1)^{n}}{2}-\frac{1}{2}\ \text{ and }\ v_{n}=-\frac{(-1)^{n}\log n}{2}+\frac{\log(\pi/2)}{2}+o(1),\text{ as }n\to\infty.

Hence the regularised value of the sequences
(u_{n})_{n\geq 1}
and

(v_{n})_{n\geq 1}
(relative to
\mathcal{E}
and
\mathcal{C}_{1}
) are
-1/2
and
\frac{\log(\pi/2)}{2}
, respectively.

These examples are in the spirit of the following proposition that we need in the context of the regularisation of the
special values of the multiple polylogarithms. The proposition below can be seen as an extension of
[
8
, Proposition 1]
.

Proposition 1
.

Let
(u_{n})_{n\geq 1}
be a sequence of complex numbers having an asymptotic expansion
to arbitrary precision relative to
\mathcal{E}
with coefficients in
\mathcal{C}
.
Then the sequence
(v_{n})_{n\geq 1}
defined by
v_{n}:=\sum_{n>m>0}u_{m}
also has
an asymptotic expansion to arbitrary precision relative to
\mathcal{E}
with coefficients in
\mathcal{C}
.

Proof.

It is enough to show that the sequence
(v_{n})_{n\geq 1}
has an asymptotic expansion
to precision
n^{-A}
relative to
\mathcal{E}
with coefficients in
\mathcal{C}
, for any integer
A\geq 1
.
For
(u_{n})_{n\geq 1}
, we have a family of sequences
\left((a_{n}^{(l,m)})_{n\geq 1}\right)_{l\in{\mathbb{N}},m\in{\mathbb{Z}}}

in
\mathcal{C}
such that

u_{n}=\sum_{l\geq 0,\hskip 1.42262ptm\leq A+1}a_{n}^{(l,m)}(\log n)^{l}n^{-m}+o(n^{-A-1}),

as
n
tends to
\infty
, for every
A\in{\mathbb{Z}}
. It is therefore enough to establish a
suitable asymptotic expansion for
(v_{n})_{n\geq 1}
in the following three cases:

(1)

u_{n}=(\log n)^{l}n^{-m}
for
l\in{\mathbb{N}},m\in{\mathbb{Z}}
;

(2)

u_{n}=o(n^{-A-1})
as
n\rightarrow\infty
;

(3)

u_{n}=z^{n}(\log n)^{l}n^{-m}
for
l\in{\mathbb{N}},m\in{\mathbb{Z}}
and
z=z_{1}^{k_{1}}\cdots z_{r}^{k_{r}}
for some
k_{1},\ldots,k_{r}\in{\mathbb{N}}
.

Cases (1) and (2) follow from
[
8
, Proposition 1]
.
In case (3), we apply the generalised Euler-Boole summation formula (see (
20
) in Appendix A).
Hence, we just need to note that the derivatives of the functions on
(1,\infty)
of the form

f_{(l,m)}(t)=(\log t)^{l}t^{-m}
, for
l\in{\mathbb{N}},m\in{\mathbb{Z}}
, has an asymptotic expansion to the precision

&\ll\frac{1}{M^{\epsilon}}+\frac{1}{M^{\epsilon}}\sum_{k\geq 1-\delta_{2}}\sum_{M>n_{2}\geq 2}\left\|\frac{(|s_{2}|+\delta_{2}+1)_{k+1}}{(k+1)!}\frac{1}{n_{2}^{\Re(s_{1}+s_{2})+\delta_{1}+\delta_{2}-1-\epsilon+k}}\right\|_{\overline{D}}\ll\frac{1}{M^{\epsilon}}.\end{split}

In this case we note that
t_{(z_{2})}(s_{2})_{M}-t_{(z_{2})}(s_{2})_{M,2}=z_{2}^{2}
. Hence

\left\|\frac{z_{1}^{M}}{(M-1)^{s_{1}+\delta_{1}-1}}t_{z_{2}}(s_{2})_{M}\right\|_{\overline{D}}\ll\frac{1}{M^{\epsilon}}.

Now we suppose
r>2
. Note that

t_{(z_{2},\ldots,z_{r})}(s_{2},\ldots,s_{r})_{M}=t_{(z_{2},\ldots,z_{r})}(s_{2},\ldots,s_{r})_{M,2}
.
Here we consider the identity (
15
) with
N=2
, for the variable
s_{2},\ldots,s_{r}
,
and then multiply
\frac{z_{1}^{M}}{(M-1)^{s_{1}+\delta_{1}-1}}
on both the sides to get

(16)

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\frac{z_{1}^{M}z_{2}}{(M-1)^{s_{1}+\delta_{1}-1}}t_{(z_{[2,3]},z_{4},\ldots,z_{r})}(s_{2}+s_{3}+\delta_{2}-1,s_{4},\ldots,s_{r})_{M-1,2}\\
&+\frac{z_{1}^{M}(z_{2}-1)}{(M-1)^{s_{1}+\delta_{1}-1}}t_{(z_{2},\ldots,z_{r})}(s_{2}+\delta_{2}-1,s_{3},\ldots,s_{r})_{M,2}\\
&+\frac{z_{1}^{M}z_{2}^{2}}{(M-1)^{s_{1}+\delta_{1}-1}}t_{(z_{3},\ldots,z_{r})}(s_{3},\ldots,s_{r})_{2}-\frac{z_{1}^{M}z_{2}^{M}}{(M-1)^{s_{1}+s_{2}+\delta_{1}+\delta_{2}-2}}t_{(z_{3},\ldots,z_{r})}(s_{3},\ldots,s_{r})_{M-1}\\
&=\sum_{k\geq 0}\frac{(s_{2}+\delta_{2}-1)_{k+1}}{(k+1)!}\frac{z_{1}^{M}}{(M-1)^{s_{1}+\delta_{1}-1}}t_{(z_{2},\ldots,z_{r})}(s_{2}+\delta_{2}+k,s_{3},\ldots,s_{r})_{M,2}.\end{split}

Simple computations show that if
(a_{1},\ldots,a_{r})\in U_{r}({\bf z})
, then
(a_{1},a_{2}+a_{3}+\delta_{2}-1,a_{4},\ldots,a_{r})\in U_{r-1}(z_{1},z_{[2,3]},z_{4},\ldots,z_{r})

and we also have
(a_{1}+a_{2}+\delta_{1}+\delta_{2}-1,a_{3},\ldots,a_{r})\in U_{r-1}(z_{[1,2]},z_{3},\ldots,z_{r})
. This by the induction hypothesis
allows us to find polydiscs
D_{1},D_{2}
around the last two points, and
\epsilon_{1},\epsilon_{2}>0
, respectively, such that

\left\|\frac{z_{1}^{M}z_{2}}{(M-1)^{s_{1}+\delta_{1}-1}}t_{(z_{[2,3]},z_{4},\ldots,z_{r})}(s_{2}+s_{3}+\delta_{2}-1,s_{4},\ldots,s_{r})_{M-1,2}\right\|_{\overline{D}_{1}}\ll\frac{1}{M^{\epsilon_{1}}},

and

\left\|\frac{z_{1}^{M}z_{2}^{M}}{(M-1)^{s_{1}+s_{2}+\delta_{1}+\delta_{2}-2}}t_{(z_{3},\ldots,z_{r})}(s_{3},\ldots,s_{r})_{M-1}\right\|_{\overline{D}_{2}}\ll\frac{1}{M^{\epsilon_{2}}}.

Now we choose an
\epsilon>0
such that
\epsilon<\epsilon_{1},\epsilon_{2}
and a polydisc
D
around
(a_{1},\ldots,a_{r})
such that for all

(s_{1},\ldots,s_{r})\in D
, we have
\Re(s_{1})+\delta_{1}>1+\epsilon
,
\Re(s_{1}+s_{2})+\delta_{1}+\delta_{2}>2+\epsilon
and moreover

(s_{1},s_{2}+s_{3}+\delta_{2}-1,s_{4},\ldots,s_{r})\in D_{1}
and
(s_{1}+s_{2}+\delta_{1}+\delta_{2}-1,s_{3},\ldots,s_{r})\in D_{2}
. For this
\epsilon>0
,
we note that

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\sum_{k\geq 1-\delta_{2}}\left\|\frac{(s_{2}+\delta_{2}-1)_{k+1}}{(k+1)!}\frac{z_{1}^{M}}{(M-1)^{s_{1}+\delta_{1}-1}}t_{z_{2}}(s_{2}+\delta_{2}+k,s_{3},\ldots,s_{r})_{M,2}\right\|_{\overline{D}}\\
&\ll\frac{1}{M^{\epsilon}}\sum_{k\geq 1-\delta_{2}}\sum_{M>n_{2}>n_{3}>\cdots>n_{r}>0}\left\|\frac{(|s_{2}|+\delta_{2}+1)_{k+1}}{(k+1)!}\frac{1}{(M-1)^{\Re(s_{1})+\delta_{1}-1-\epsilon}}\frac{1}{n_{2}^{\Re(s_{2})+\delta_{2}+k}n_{3}^{\Re(s_{3})}\cdots n_{r}^{\Re(s_{r})}}\right\|_{\overline{D}}\\
&\ll\frac{1}{M^{\epsilon}}\sum_{k\geq 1-\delta_{2}}\sum_{M>n_{2}>n_{3}>\cdots>n_{r}>0}\left\|\frac{(|s_{2}|+\delta_{2}+1)_{k+1}}{(k+1)!}\frac{1}{n_{2}^{\Re(s_{1}+s_{2})+\delta_{1}+\delta_{2}-1-\epsilon+k}n_{3}^{\Re(s_{3})}\cdots n_{r}^{\Re(s_{r})}}\right\|_{\overline{D}}\ll\frac{1}{M^{\epsilon}},\end{split}

by Lemma
1
. This, together with (
16
), implies that

\left\|\frac{z_{1}^{M}}{(M-1)^{s_{1}+\delta_{1}-1}}t_{(z_{2},\ldots,z_{r})}(s_{2},\ldots,s_{r})_{M}\right\|_{\overline{D}}=O(M^{-\epsilon}).

∎

We are now ready to prove Proposition
2
.

Proof of Proposition
2
.

We will prove the statement by induction on
r
. Let
r=1
. For this, we write the
identity (
10
) as

\displaystyle(z_{1}-1)t_{z_{1}}(s_{1}+\delta_{1}-1)_{M,N}+\frac{z_{1}^{N}}{(N-1)^{s_{1}+\delta_{1}-1}}-\frac{z_{1}^{M}}{(M-1)^{s_{1}+\delta-1}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k\geq 0}\frac{(s_{1}-1)_{k+1}}{(k+1)!}t_{z_{1}}(s_{1}+\delta_{1}+k)_{M,N}.

Since
a_{1}\in U_{1}(z_{1})
, we have
a_{1}+\delta_{1}>1
. Hence by Lemma
2
, there exists a disc
D
around
a_{1}
and
\epsilon>0

such that as
N\to\infty
, the sum

\sum_{k\geq 1-\delta_{1}}\left\|\frac{(s_{1}+\delta_{1}-1)_{k+1}}{(k+1)!}t_{z_{1}}(s_{1}+\delta_{1}+k)_{M,N}\right\|_{\overline{D}}\ll\frac{1}{N^{\epsilon}}.

Note that choosing
D
suitably small, we can also ensure that, as
N\to\infty
,

\left\|\frac{z_{1}^{N}}{(N-1)^{s_{1}+\delta_{1}-1}}\right\|_{\overline{D}},\left\|\frac{z_{1}^{M}}{(M-1)^{s_{1}+\delta_{1}-1}}\right\|_{\overline{D}}\ll\frac{1}{N^{\epsilon}}.

This therefore implies that
\|t_{z_{1}}(s_{1})_{M,N}\|_{\overline{D}}=O(N^{-\epsilon})
as
N\to\infty
.
Now for
r\geq 2
, we use (
15
). We first note that if
(a_{1},\ldots,a_{r})\in U_{r}({\bf z})
, then

(a_{1}+a_{2}+\delta_{1}-1,a_{3},\ldots,a_{r})\in U_{r-1}(z_{[1,2]},z_{3},\ldots,z_{r})
. Using the induction hypothesis, we can find
a polydisc
D_{1}
around the point
(a_{1}+a_{2}+\delta_{1}-1,a_{3},\ldots,a_{r})
and
\epsilon_{1}>0
such that

\left\|t_{(z_{[1,2]},z_{3},\ldots,z_{r})}(s_{1}+s_{2}+\delta_{1}-1,s_{3},\ldots,s_{r})_{M-1,N}\right\|_{\overline{D}_{1}}\ll\frac{1}{N^{\epsilon_{1}}}.

Now we find a polydisc
D
around
(a_{1},\ldots,a_{r})
such that for all the points
(s_{1},\ldots,s_{r})\in D
, we have

(s_{1}+s_{2}+\delta_{1}-1,s_{3},\ldots,s_{r})\in D_{1}
. Choosing
D
small enough, by Corollary
1
, we can further ensure that
for some
0<\epsilon<\epsilon_{1}
, we have as
N\to\infty
,

\left\|\frac{z_{1}^{N}}{(N-1)^{s_{1}+\delta_{1}-1}}t_{(z_{2},\ldots,z_{r})}(s_{2},\ldots,s_{r})_{N}\right\|_{\overline{D}},\left\|\frac{z_{1}^{M}}{(M-1)^{s_{1}+\delta_{1}-1}}t_{(z_{2},\ldots,z_{r})}(s_{2},\ldots,s_{r})_{M-1}\right\|_{\overline{D}}\ll\frac{1}{N^{\epsilon}}.

Moreover, by Lemma
2
, we have as
N\to\infty
,

\sum_{k\geq 1-\delta_{1}}\left\|\frac{(s_{1}+\delta_{1}-1)_{k+1}}{(k+1)!}t_{(z_{1},\ldots,z_{r})}(s_{1}+\delta_{1}+k,s_{2},\ldots,s_{r})_{M,N}\right\|_{\overline{D}}\ll\frac{1}{N^{\epsilon}}.

Hence using (
15
), we finally deduce that as
N\to\infty
,

\|t_{(z_{1},\ldots,z_{r})}(s_{1},\ldots,s_{r})_{M,N}\|_{\overline{D}}=O(N^{-\epsilon}).

This completes the proof of Proposition
2
.
∎

With this the proof of Theorem
2
is complete. In the next section, we prove Theorem
3
.

4.
Proof of Theorem
3

For
u_{n}
as in Theorem
4
, we prove Theorem
3
by estimating the order of the asymptotic expansion of the sequence

(u_{n})_{n\geq 1}
relative to
\mathcal{E}
with coefficients in
\mathcal{C}
.
We first prove the following lemma where
z=z_{1}^{k_{1}}\cdots z_{r}^{k_{r}}\neq 1
for some
k_{1},\ldots,k_{r}\in{\mathbb{N}}
.

Lemma 4
.

Let
a\in{\mathbb{Z}}
and
P(X)\in{\mathbb{C}}[X]
. Then the order of the asymptotic expansion of the sequence
(v_{n})_{n\geq 1}

relative to
\mathcal{E}
with coefficients in
\mathcal{C}
, where the sequence
(v_{n})_{n\geq 1}
defined by

(17)

v_{n}:=\sum_{m=1}^{n-1}\frac{z^{m}P(\log m)}{m^{a}},

is at least
\min(0,a)
.

Proof.

Note that it is enough to prove the statement when
P(X)
is a monomial.
For
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
, the order of the asymptotic expansion of the sequence
\left(\frac{(\log n)^{k}}{n^{a}}\right)_{n\geq 1}
(relative to

\mathcal{E}
with coefficients in
\mathcal{C}
) is
a
and for the sequence
\frac{d}{dx}\frac{(\log x)^{k}}{x^{a}}|_{x=n}

is
a+1
. Hence, by the generalised Euler-Boole summation formula (
20
) in Appedinx
A
,
the order of the asymptotic expansion of the sequence
\left(v_{n}\right)_{n\geq 1}
is at least
\min(0,a).

∎

Remark 1
.

For
v_{n}
as in (
17
), by applying Lemma
4
and Theorem
4
, we see that for any integer
A\geq a
and
0<\epsilon<1
, we have,
as
n\rightarrow\infty
,

\displaystyle v_{n}=c+\frac{z^{n}P_{0}(\log n)}{n^{a}}+\frac{z^{n}P_{1}(\log n)}{n^{a+1}}+\cdots+\frac{z^{n}P_{A-a}(\log n)}{n^{A}}+o\left(\frac{1}{n^{A+\epsilon}}\right),

where
c\in{\mathbb{C}}
and
P_{i}(X)\in{\mathbb{C}}[X]
for all
0\leq i\leq A-a.

Remark 2
.

It is worthwhile to recall
[
8
, Remark 6]
that for
a\in{\mathbb{Z}}
and
Q(X)\in{\mathbb{C}}[X]
,
the order of the asymptotic expansion of the sequence
(v_{n})_{n\geq 1}
relative to
\mathcal{E}
with coefficients in
\mathcal{C}
, where

v_{n}:=\sum_{m=1}^{n-1}\frac{Q(\log m)}{m^{a}},

is at least min
(0,a-1)
and
for any integer
A\geq a-1
and
0<\epsilon<1
, we have, as
n\rightarrow\infty
,

\displaystyle v_{n}=d+\frac{Q_{0}(\log n)}{n^{a-1}}+\frac{Q_{1}(\log n)}{n^{a}}+\cdots+\frac{Q_{A-a+1}(\log n)}{n^{A}}+o\left(\frac{1}{n^{A+\epsilon}}\right),

where
d\in{\mathbb{C}}
and
Q_{i}(X)\in{\mathbb{C}}[X]
for all
0\leq i\leq A-a+1
.

To write the proof inductively, we need some notations similar to (
5
). For
1\leq i\leq j\leq r
, define

q_{[i,j]}=q_{[i,j]}({\bf z}):=\begin{cases}1&\text{ if }z_{[i,j]}=1,\\
0&\text{ if }z_{[i,j]}\neq 1\end{cases}\ \text{ and }\ Q_{[i,j]}=Q_{[i,j]}({\bf z}):=q_{[i,j]}+\cdots+q_{[j,j]}.

Note that taking
i=1
, we get
Q_{[1,j]}=Q_{j}({\bf z})
, as in (
5
).
We set
Q_{[i,j]}=0
if
1\leq j<i\leq r
.
Further, for
(a_{1},\ldots,a_{r})\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{r}
, set
A_{[i,j]}:=a_{i}+\cdots+a_{j}
for
1\leq i\leq j\leq r
.
In this context, we prove the following proposition which enables us to complete the proof of Theorem
3
.

Proposition 3
.

Let
r\geq 1
be an integer. Let
(u_{n})_{n\geq 1}
be the sequence defined as in Theorem
4
. Then the order of the asymptotic
expansion of the sequence
(u_{n})_{n\geq 1}
, relative to
\mathcal{E}
with coefficients in
\mathcal{C}
, has order at least

\min\left(0,A_{[1,1]}-Q_{[1,1]},A_{[1,2]}-Q_{[1,2]},\ldots,A_{[1,r]}-Q_{[1,r]}\right)
.
More precisely, for sufficiently large integer
A
and
0<\epsilon<1
, we have, as
n\rightarrow\infty
,

(18)

u_{n}=c+\sum_{i=1}^{r}\sum_{j=0}^{A-A_{[1,i]}+Q_{[1,i]}}\frac{F_{(i,j)}(\log n)}{n^{A_{[1,i]}-Q_{[1,i]}+j}}z_{[1,i]}^{n}+o\left(\frac{1}{n^{A+\epsilon}}\right),

where
c\in{\mathbb{C}}
and
F_{i,j}(X)\in{\mathbb{C}}[X]
for all
i,j
as above.

Note that Proposition
3
extends
[
8
, Remark 7]
for the series defining the multiple polylogarithms.

Proof of Proposition
3
.

We prove this by induction on
r
. When
r=1
, the statement follows from Remarks
1
and
2
.
Now we assume that
r\geq 2
. Let
A\geq 1
be a given positive integer. Consider the sequence
(v_{n_{1}})_{n_{1}\geq 1}
defined by

v_{n_{1}}:=\sum_{n_{1}>n_{2}>\cdots>n_{r}>0}\frac{z_{2}^{n_{2}}\log^{k_{2}}n_{2}\cdots z_{r}^{n_{r}}\log^{k_{r}}n_{r}}{n_{2}^{a_{2}}\cdots n_{r}^{a_{r}}}.

Now by the induction hypothesis, corresponding to
(z_{2},\ldots,z_{r})
, we get that for
A^{\prime}=A+a_{1}-1
and
0<\epsilon<1
, we have,
as
n_{1}\rightarrow\infty
,

\displaystyle v_{n_{1}}=c_{1}+\sum_{i=2}^{r}\sum_{j=0}^{A^{\prime}+Q_{[2,i]}-A_{[2,i]}}\frac{G_{(i,j)}(\log n_{1})}{n_{1}^{A_{[2,i]}-Q_{[2,i]}+j}}z_{[2,i]}^{n_{1}}+o\left(\frac{1}{n_{1}^{A^{\prime}+\epsilon}}\right),

where
c_{1}\in{\mathbb{C}}
and
G_{(i,j)}(X)\in{\mathbb{C}}[X]
for all
i,j
. Let
(w_{n_{1}})_{n_{1}\geq 1}
be the sequence

w_{n_{1}}=\frac{z_{1}^{n_{1}}(\log n_{1})^{k_{1}}}{n_{1}^{a_{1}}}
. Then

u_{n}=\sum_{n_{1}=1}^{n-1}(v_{n_{1}}\cdot w_{n_{1}})
where

v_{n_{1}}\cdot w_{n_{1}}=c_{1}\frac{z_{1}^{n_{1}}(\log n_{1})^{k_{1}}}{n_{1}^{a_{1}}}+\sum_{i=2}^{r}\sum_{j=0}^{A^{\prime}+Q_{[2,i]}-A_{[2,i]}}\frac{(\log n_{1})^{k_{1}}G_{(i,j)}(\log n_{1})}{n_{1}^{A_{[2,i]}-Q_{[2,i]}+j+a_{1}}}z_{[1,i]}^{n_{1}}+o(n_{1}^{-A^{\prime}+a_{1}-\epsilon}).

Note that
A_{[2,i]}+a_{1}=A_{[1,i]}
. Now if we write
X^{k_{1}}G_{(i,j)}(X)
as
H_{(i,j)}(X)
, then
to get the desired asymptotic expansion of
u_{n}
(relative to
\mathcal{E}
with coefficients in
\mathcal{C}
),
we need to look at the asymptotic expansion of the sequence of the form,

\sum_{n_{1}=1}^{n-1}z_{[1,i]}^{n_{1}}\frac{H_{(i,j)}(\log n_{1})}{n_{1}^{A_{[1,i]}-Q_{[2,i]}+j}}

for each
i,j
as above, together with for
(i,j)=(1,0)
where
H_{(1,0)}(\log n_{1}):=c_{1}(\log n_{1})^{k_{1}}
,
as
\sum_{n_{1}=1}^{n-1}o(n_{1}^{-A^{\prime}+a_{1}-\epsilon})=c_{0}+o(n^{-A^{\prime}+a_{1}-1-\epsilon})=c_{0}+o(n^{-A-\epsilon})
,
for some
c_{0}\in{\mathbb{C}}
.

Now for each
1\leq i\leq r
,
q_{[1,i]}
is a value that depends on the value of the product
z_{[1,i]}
,
namely,
q_{[1,i]}=0
if
z_{[1,i]}\neq 1
and
q_{[1,i]}=1
if
z_{[1,i]}=1
. So using
Remarks
1
and
2
,
we see that, as
n\to\infty
, each of the above sums is of the form

c_{i,j}+z_{[1,i]}^{n}\sum_{k=0}^{A-A_{[1,i]}+Q_{[1,i]}-j}\frac{H_{(i,j,k)}(\log n)}{n^{A_{[1,i]}-Q_{[2,i]}-q_{[1,i]}+j+k}}+o(n^{-A-\epsilon}),

for

and
H_{(i,j,k)}(X)\in{\mathbb{C}}[X]

and collecting the terms for

where

and

Proof of Theorem
3
.

As an immediate corollary, from (

) we see that if

for all

, then the sequence

in Theorem

.

Example 2
.

If
(z_{1},z_{2})=(1,-1)
, then
Q_{[1,1]}=1
and
Q_{[1,2]}=q_{[1,2]}+q_{[2,2]}=0
. We have

V_{2}((1,-1))=\{(s_{1},s_{2})\in{\mathbb{C}}^{2}:\Re(s_{1})>1,\Re(s_{1}+s_{2})>0\}.

The two variable multiple polylogarithm

\mathrm{Li}(s_{1},s_{r};1,-1)=\sum_{n_{1}>n_{2}>0}\frac{(-1)^{n_{2}}}{n_{1}^{s_{1}}n_{2}^{s_{2}}}

is convergent for
(s_{1},s_{2})\in V_{2}((1,-1))\cap{\mathbb{Z}}^{2}
. The set
V_{2}((1,-1))
is somewhat optimal
for the series
\sum_{n_{1}>n_{2}>0}\frac{(-1)^{n_{2}}}{n_{1}^{s_{1}}n_{2}^{s_{2}}}
to converge, as for
N\to\infty
, we have

\sum_{N>n_{1}>n_{2}>0}\frac{(-1)^{n_{2}}}{n_{1}^{2}n_{2}^{-2}}=-\frac{\log 2}{2}+\frac{1}{4}+\frac{(-1)^{N}}{4}+o(1).

Appendix A
Generalised Euler-Boole summation formula

Let
k\geq 1
be a positive integer and
\zeta
be a primitive
k^{th}
root of unity. For
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
, large enough, the aim
of this appendix is to establish a formula that can be used to estimate a sum of the form
\sum_{a=1}^{n-1}\zeta^{a}f(a)

for a given ‘well behaved’ function
f
defined on
[1,n]
, in terms of derivatives and integrals related to
f
.

When
\zeta=1
, we have the well-known
Euler-Maclaurin summation formula

[
2
, 2.10]
,

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}f(i)=

\displaystyle\int_{1}^{n}f(t)dt+\sum_{j=1}^{m}\frac{B_{j}}{j!}\left(f^{(j-1)}(n)-f^{(j-1)}(1)\right)+\frac{(-1)^{m+1}}{m!}\int_{1}^{n}B_{m}(\{t\})f^{(m)}(t)dt,

where
B_{j}(x)
is the
j^{th}
-
Bernoulli polynomial
given by the generating series

\frac{te^{xt}}{e^{t}-1}=\sum_{n\geq 0}B_{n}(x)\frac{t^{n}}{n!},

with
B_{j}
denoting the
j^{th}

Bernoulli number
given by
B_{j}=B_{j}(0)
and
f(x)
is an
m
-times continuously differentiable function on
[1,n]
.

Similarly, for
\zeta=-1
, one has the
Euler-Boole summation formula

[
2
, 24.17]
,

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}(-1)^{i}f(i)=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j=0}^{m-1}\frac{E_{j}(0)}{j!}\left(-f^{(j)}(1)-(-1)^{n}f^{(j)}(n)\right)+\frac{1}{2(m-1)!}\int_{1}^{n}f^{(m)}(t)\mathaccent 869{E}_{m-1}(-t)dt,

where
E_{j}(0)
is the value at
0
of the
Euler polynomial

E_{j}(x)
, given by the generating series

\displaystyle\frac{2e^{xt}}{1+e^{t}}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}E_{n}(x)\frac{t^{n}}{n!},

and
\mathaccent 869{E}_{n}(x)
is the
periodic Euler polynomial
defined by
\mathaccent 869{E}_{n}(x+1)=-\mathaccent 869{E}_{n}(x)
and
\mathaccent 869{E}_{n}(x)=E_{n}(x)
for
0\leq x<1
(see
[
2
, 24.2]
).

To prove such a formula for
\sum_{a=1}^{n-1}\zeta^{a}f(a)
where
\zeta
is a primitive
k^{th}

root of unity
\zeta
for
k\geq 2
, we follow the exposition by Borwein, Calkin and Manna
[
1
]
.
We first extend the notion of Euler polynomials. For an integer
k\geq 2
,
we first consider the generating series

(19)

\displaystyle\frac{ke^{xt}}{1+e^{t}+\cdots+e^{(k-1)t}}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}E_{k,n}(x)\frac{t^{n}}{n!},

where
E_{k,n}(x)
are polynomials in
x
. We call these polynomials as the
generalised Euler polynomials
.
Note that when
k=2
, we get back the Euler polynomials
E_{n}(x)
defined above.
Now for a given primitive
k^{th}
root of unity
\zeta
, define the corresponding
periodic generalised Euler polynomial

by setting
\mathaccent 869{E}_{k,n}(x):=E_{k,n}(x)
for all
0\leq x<1
and

\mathaccent 869{E}_{k,n}(x+m):=\zeta^{-m}\mathaccent 869{E}_{k,n}(x)
for all
x\in{\mathbb{R}}
and
m\in{\mathbb{Z}}
.
This is analogous to the definition of periodic Euler polynomial, when
k=2
and
\zeta=-1.

To state our theorem, we need some notations. For
k\geq 2
an integer and integers

1\leq i\leq j\leq k-1
, consider the vectors

\displaystyle\mathbf{v}_{i,j}:=\left(\zeta^{j},\zeta^{j-1},\ldots,\zeta^{i}\right)\text{ and }\mathbf{w}_{i,j}:=\left(\frac{i}{k}-1,\frac{i+1}{k}-1,\ldots,\frac{j}{k}-1\right)

in the inner product space
{\mathbb{C}}^{j-i+1}
with the standard inner product
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
. So

\langle\mathbf{v}_{i,j},\mathbf{w}_{i,j}\rangle=\zeta^{j}\left(\frac{i}{k}-1\right)+\zeta^{j-1}\left(\frac{i+1}{k}-1\right)+\cdots+\zeta^{i}\left(\frac{j}{k}-1\right).

Main Theorem
.

Let
k\geq 2
be an integer and
\zeta
be a primitive
k^{th}
root of unity. For integers
m\geq 1,n\geq k
, let
f
be a
complex valued function on
[1,n]
whose first
m
many derivatives are absolutely integrable on
[1,n]
.
Then we have the following summation formula:

(20)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\sum_{a=1}^{n-1}\zeta^{a}f(a)&=\frac{1}{k}\left(\sum_{t=1}^{k-1}f(t)\sum_{a=1}^{t}\zeta^{a}+\zeta^{n}\sum_{t=0}^{k-2}f(n+t)\sum_{a=t+1}^{k-1}\zeta^{a}\right)\\
&+\sum_{i=1}^{k-2}\langle\mathbf{v}_{1,i},\mathbf{w}_{1,i}\rangle\ \Delta[f](i)+\zeta^{n}\sum_{i=2}^{k-1}\langle\mathbf{v}_{i,k-1},\mathbf{w}_{i,k-1}\rangle\ \Delta[f](i+n-2)\\
&+\langle\mathbf{v}_{1,k-1},\mathbf{w}_{1,k-1}\rangle\sum_{i=1}^{m-1}\frac{E_{k,i}(0)}{i!}\left\{\zeta^{k-1}f^{(i)}(k-1)-\zeta^{n}f^{(i)}(n)\right\}\\
&+\langle\mathbf{v}_{1,k-1},\mathbf{w}_{1,k-1}\rangle\frac{1}{(m-1)!}\int_{k-1}^{n}\mathaccent 869{E}_{k,m-1}\left(k-x\right)f^{(m)}(x)dx,\end{split}

where
\Delta[f](x)=f(x+1)-f(x)
is the difference operator.

Taking
k=2
and
\zeta=-1
in (
20
), we get

\langle\mathbf{v}_{1,1},\mathbf{w}_{1,1}\rangle=\frac{1}{2}
and hence we get back the
Euler-Boole summation formula, as stated above. Hence, we refer to (
20
) as a

generalised Euler-Boole summation formula
.

Proof.

To prove our theorem, we begin by recalling the general setup from
[
1
]
. Let
g
be an absolutely
continuous probability density function with finite moments. In particular,

\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}g(u)du=1\ \ \ \text{ and }\ \ \ \int_{-\infty}^{\infty}|u|^{k}g(u)du<\infty\text{ for all }k\in{\mathbb{N}}.

From now onward, we will denote
\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}
by
\int
.
Consider the Strodt operator
S_{g}
associated with the probability density function
g
on the
space of complex-valued functions given by

S_{g}(f)(x):=\int f(x+u)g(u)du.

For an integer
n\geq 0
, let
P_{n}(x)
be the corresponding Strodt polynomial for
S_{g}
, i.e.,

S_{g}(P_{n})(x)=x^{n}
for all
x\in{\mathbb{R}}
.
For
1\leq a<n
, a sufficiently smooth function
f
and a fixed integer
m\geq 0
, define the remainder

R_{m}(a):=f(a)-\sum_{n=0}^{m}\frac{S_{g}(f^{(n)})(a)}{k!}P_{n}(0).

As remarked in
[
1
]
, the process of deriving a summation formula essentially reduces
to finding an expression for
R_{m}(a)
as an integral involving the Strodt polynomials
P_{n}(x)
,
corresponding to the Strodt operator
S_{g}
.
As
P_{0}(x)=1
and
\int g(u)du=1
, we have

\displaystyle R_{0}(a)

\displaystyle=f(a)-\int f(a+u)g(u)du=\int(f(a)-f(a+u))g(u)du

\displaystyle=\int\int_{u}^{0}f^{\prime}(a+s)g(u)dsdu=\int V(s)f^{\prime}(a+s)ds,

where

V(s):=\left\{\begin{array}[]{lr}\int_{-\infty}^{s}g(u)du&\text{ for }s<0,\\
\int_{-\infty}^{s}g(u)du-1&\text{ for }s\geq 0.\end{array}\right.

Hence we have

(21)

f(a)=S_{g}(f)(a)+\int V(s)f^{\prime}(a+s)ds.

For our proof, we consider the Strodt operator
S_{g}
associated to the probability weight function

g(u):=\frac{\delta_{0}(u)+\cdots+\delta_{k-1}(u)}{k}.

So, here the Strodt operator is

(22)

\displaystyle S_{g}(f)(x)=\frac{f(x)+f(x+1)+\cdots+f(x+k-1)}{k},

and the generalised Euler polynomials
E_{k,n}(x)
are the corresponding Strodt polynomials.
Now we have

(23)

\displaystyle\begin{split}V(s)&=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\frac{1}{k}-1&\text{ for }0\leq s<1,\\
\frac{2}{k}-1&\text{ for }1\leq s<2,\\
\vdots\\
\frac{k-1}{k}-1&\text{ for }k-2\leq s<k-1,\\
0&\text{otherwise}.\end{array}\right.\\
\end{split}

Then from (
21
), we get,

\displaystyle f(a)=\frac{1}{k}\sum_{i=0}^{k-1}f(a+i)+\int V(s)f^{\prime}(a+s)ds

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{k}\sum_{i=0}^{k-1}f(a+i)+\int_{0}^{k-1}V(s)f^{\prime}(a+s)ds

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{k}\sum_{i=0}^{k-1}f(a+i)+\int_{a}^{a+k-1}V(x-a)f^{\prime}(x)dx.

We multiply the above expression by
\zeta^{a}
and sum for
1\leq a\leq n-1
to get

(24)

\sum_{a=1}^{n-1}\zeta^{a}f(a)=\frac{1}{k}\sum_{a=1}^{n-1}\sum_{i=0}^{k-1}\zeta^{a}f(a+i)+\sum_{a=1}^{n-1}\zeta^{a}\int_{a}^{a+k-1}V(x-a)f^{\prime}(x)dx.

We begin with the sum,

\displaystyle\sum_{a=1}^{n-1}\sum_{i=0}^{k-1}\zeta^{a}f(a+i)

\displaystyle=\sum_{t=1}^{n+k-2}f(t)\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}a+i=t,\\
1\leq a\leq n-1,\\
0\leq i\leq k-1\end{subarray}}\zeta^{a}

\displaystyle=\sum_{t=1}^{k-1}f(t)\sum_{a=1}^{t}\zeta^{a}+\sum_{t=k}^{n-1}f(t)\sum_{a=t-k+1}^{t}\zeta^{a}+\sum_{t=n}^{n+k-2}f(t)\sum_{a=t-k+1}^{n-1}\zeta^{a}.

Note that,
\sum_{a=t-k+1}^{t}\zeta^{a}=\zeta^{t-k+1}(1+\zeta+\cdots+\zeta^{k-1})=0
,
as
\zeta
is a
k^{th}
root of unity. Hence the second sum in the right-hand side of the above equation vanishes. Moreover,
in the last sum, replacing
t
by
n+t
, we have

\displaystyle\sum_{a=1}^{n-1}\sum_{i=0}^{k-1}\zeta^{a}f(a+i)

\displaystyle=\sum_{t=1}^{k-1}f(t)\sum_{a=1}^{t}\zeta^{a}+\sum_{t=0}^{k-2}f(n+t)\sum_{a=t+n-k+1}^{n-1}\zeta^{a}

(25)

\displaystyle=\sum_{t=1}^{k-1}f(t)\sum_{a=1}^{t}\zeta^{a}+\zeta^{n}\sum_{t=0}^{k-2}f(n+t)\sum_{a=t+1}^{k-1}\zeta^{a}.

Now we consider the last term on the right-hand side of (
24
). For
0\leq i\leq k-2
, if
x\in[a+i,a+i+1)
, we have

V(x-a)=\frac{i+1}{k}-1
. So,

(26)

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\sum_{a=1}^{n-1}\zeta^{a}\int_{a}^{a+k-1}V(x-a)f^{\prime}(x)dx\\
&=\sum_{a=1}^{n-1}\zeta^{a}\left(\int_{a}^{a+1}\left(\frac{1}{k}-1\right)f^{\prime}(x)dx+\cdots+\int_{a+k-2}^{a+k-1}\left(\frac{k-1}{k}-1\right)f^{\prime}(x)dx\right).\end{split}

Note that in (
26
), we have
\Delta[f](i)=\int_{i}^{i+1}f^{\prime}(x)dx
for
1\leq i\leq n+k-3
.
We will compute the coefficient of each of these integrals.
We make three cases: (a)
1\leq i\leq k-2
, (b)
k-1\leq i\leq n-1
and (c)
n\leq i\leq n+k-3
.

Case (a).
In this case, the coefficient of
\Delta[f](i)
in (
26
) is

\zeta^{i}\left(\frac{1}{k}-1\right)+\zeta^{i-1}\left(\frac{2}{k}-1\right)+\cdots+\zeta\left(\frac{i}{k}-1\right)=\langle\mathbf{v}_{1,i},\mathbf{w}_{1,i}\rangle.

Case (b).
In this case, the coefficient of the integral
\int_{i}^{i+1}f^{\prime}(x)dx
in (
26
) is

\displaystyle\zeta^{i}\left(\frac{1}{k}-1\right)+\zeta^{i-1}\left(\frac{2}{k}-1\right)+\cdots+\zeta^{i-(k-2)}\left(\frac{k-1}{k}-1\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\zeta^{i-(k-1)}\left\{\zeta^{k-1}\left(\frac{1}{k}-1\right)+\zeta^{k-2}\left(\frac{2}{k}-1\right)+\cdots+\zeta\left(\frac{k-1}{k}-1\right)\right\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\zeta^{i+1}\langle\mathbf{v}_{1,k-1},\mathbf{w}_{1,k-1}\rangle.

Case (c).
In this case, the coefficient of
\Delta[f](i)
in (
26
) is

\displaystyle\zeta^{n-1}\left(\frac{i-n+2}{k}-1\right)+\zeta^{n-2}\left(\frac{i-n+3}{k}-1\right)+\cdots+\zeta^{i-k+2}\left(\frac{k-1}{k}-1\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\zeta^{n}\left\{\zeta^{k-1}\left(\frac{i-n+2}{k}-1\right)+\zeta^{k-2}\left(\frac{i-n+3}{k}-1\right)+\cdots+\zeta^{i-n+2}\left(\frac{k-1}{k}-1\right)\right\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\zeta^{n}\langle\mathbf{v}_{i-n+2,k-1},\mathbf{w}_{i-n+2,k-1}\rangle.

Hence (
26
) becomes

(27)

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\sum_{a=1}^{n-1}\zeta^{a}\int_{a}^{a+k-1}V(x-a)f^{\prime}(x)dx\\
&=\sum_{i=1}^{k-2}\langle\mathbf{v}_{1,i},\mathbf{w}_{1,i}\rangle\ \Delta[f](i)+\langle\mathbf{v}_{1,k-1},\mathbf{w}_{1,k-1}\rangle\sum_{i=k-1}^{n-1}\int_{i}^{i+1}\zeta^{i+1}f^{\prime}(x)dx\\
&\ \ +\zeta^{n}\sum_{i=n}^{n+k-3}\langle\mathbf{v}_{i-n+2,k-1},\mathbf{w}_{i-n+2,k-1}\rangle\ \Delta[f](i).\end{split}

Clearly,

(28)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\zeta^{n}\sum_{i=n}^{n+k-3}\langle\mathbf{v}_{i-n+2,k-1},\mathbf{w}_{i-n+2,k-1}\rangle\ \Delta[f](i)=\zeta^{n}\sum_{i=2}^{k-1}\langle\mathbf{v}_{i,k-1},\mathbf{w}_{i,k-1}\rangle\ \ \Delta[f](i+n-2).\end{split}

Now we will deal with the term
\sum_{i=k-1}^{n-1}\int_{i}^{i+1}\zeta^{i+1}f^{\prime}(x)dx
in (
27
),
using the general theory of finite Strodt operator and Strodt polynomials as in
[
1
]
.
We compute the periodic generalised Euler polynomial
\mathaccent 869{E}_{k,0}(x)
. For
i\in{\mathbb{Z}}
, we have

\displaystyle\mathaccent 869{E}_{k,0}\left(k-x\right)=\mathaccent 869{E}_{k,0}\left(k-(1+i)+1+i-x\right)=\zeta^{1+i-k}\mathaccent 869{E}_{k,0}\left(1+i-x\right)=\zeta^{i+1}\mathaccent 869{E}_{k,0}\left(1+i-x\right).

If
x\in(i,i+1)
, we have
1+i-x\in(0,1)
. Therefore
\mathaccent 869{E}_{k,0}\left(1+i-x\right)=E_{k,0}\left(1+i-x\right)=1

for
x\in(i,i+1)
. Hence for
x\in(i,i+1)
,

\displaystyle\mathaccent 869{E}_{k,0}\left(k-x\right)=\zeta^{i+1}.

Hence we have

(29)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\sum_{i=k-1}^{n-1}\int_{i}^{i+1}\zeta^{i+1}f^{\prime}(x)dx&=\sum_{i=k-1}^{n-1}\int_{i}^{i+1}\mathaccent 869{E}_{k,0}\left(k-x\right)f^{\prime}(x)dx=\int_{k-1}^{n}\mathaccent 869{E}_{k,0}\left(k-x\right)f^{\prime}(x)dx.\end{split}

Now we recall
[
1
, Theorem 2.3]
that the Strodt polynomials satisfy the differential equation

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dx}E_{k,n}(x)=nE_{k,n-1}(x),

for
n\geq 1
. So this implies that,

(30)

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dx}E_{k,n}(k-x)=-nE_{k,n-1}(k-x).

Hence for an integer
m\geq 1
, using (
30
) for the periodic generalised Euler polynomials
and integrating by parts, we get

(31)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\int_{k-1}^{n}\mathaccent 869{E}_{k,m-1}\left(k-x\right)f^{(m)}(x)dx=&\frac{1}{m}\left\{\mathaccent 869{E}_{k,m}\left(1\right)f^{(m)}(k-1)-\mathaccent 869{E}_{k,m}\left(k-n\right)f^{(m)}(n)\right\}\\
&+\frac{1}{m}\int_{k-1}^{n}\mathaccent 869{E}_{k,m}\left(k-x\right)f^{(m+1)}(x)dx.\end{split}

Since
\mathaccent 869{E}_{k,m}\left(k-n\right)=\zeta^{n-k}E_{k,m}\left(0\right)=\zeta^{n}E_{k,m}\left(0\right)
and
\mathaccent 869{E}_{k,m}\left(1\right)=\zeta^{-1}E_{k,m}\left(0\right)
, (
31
) becomes

(32)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\int_{k-1}^{n}\mathaccent 869{E}_{k,m-1}\left(k-x\right)f^{(m)}(x)dx=&\frac{E_{k,m}(0)}{m}\left\{\zeta^{k-1}f^{(m)}(k-1)-\zeta^{n}f^{(m)}(n)\right\}\\
&+\frac{1}{m}\int_{k-1}^{n}\mathaccent 869{E}_{k,m}\left(k-x\right)f^{(m+1)}(x)dx.\end{split}

Applying (
32
) repeatedly, (
29
) becomes

(33)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\sum_{i=k-1}^{n-1}\int_{i}^{i+1}\zeta^{i+1}f^{\prime}(x)dx&=\sum_{i=1}^{m-1}\frac{E_{k,i}(0)}{i!}\left\{\zeta^{k-1}f^{(i)}(k-1)-\zeta^{n}f^{(i)}(n)\right\}\\
&+\frac{1}{(m-1)!}\int_{k-1}^{n}\mathaccent 869{E}_{k,m-1}\left(k-x\right)f^{(m)}(x)dx.\end{split}

Now in (
24
), we apply (
A
) and (
27
), and use (
28
) and (
33
), to get the desired
formula (
20
). This completes the proof of the theorem.
∎
