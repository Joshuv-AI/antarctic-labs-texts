Rank-Accuracy Trade-off for LoRA: A Gradient-Flow Analysis
Michael Rushka, Diego Klabjan

Abstract
Previous empirical studies have shown that LoRA achieves accuracy comparable to full-parameter methods on downstream fine-tuning tasks, even for rank-1 updates. By contrast, the theoretical underpinnings of the dependence of LoRA’s accuracy on update rank remain relatively unexplored. In this work, we compare the accuracy of rank- r r LoRA updates against full-parameter updates for fine-tuning tasks from a dynamical systems perspective. We perform gradient flow analysis in both full-rank and low-rank regimes to establish explicit relationships between rank and accuracy for two loss functions under LoRA. While gradient flow equations for LoRA are presented in prior work, we rigorously derive their form and show that they are identical for simultaneous and sequential LoRA parameter updates. We then use the resulting dynamical system equations to obtain closed-form relationships between LoRA rank and accuracy for trace-squared and Frobenius-norm low-rank approximation loss functions.

Introduction

Parameter-efficient finetuning (PEFT) has become the norm for training deep learning models on downstream tasks. Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA)
(
Hu et al. 2022
)
in particular has gained wide popularity among such methods. Consider
f:\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}\to\mathbb{R}
which has been optimized by parameters
W_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}
. Keeping
W_{0}
frozen, finetuning consists of finding an update
\Delta W\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}
which approximates
\min\limits_{\Delta W}f(W_{0}+\Delta W)
for smaller downstream datasets introduced post-training. While full-parameter finetuning (FPFT) methods search all of
\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}
for optimal
\Delta W
, LoRA splits the classical gradient descent (GD) algorithm into separate updates which solve
\min_{A,B}\limits f(W_{0}+BA)
, where
B\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times r}
and
A\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times m}
for
r<<\min(n,m)
. By lowering the number of finetuning parameters from
nm
to
r(n+m)
, LoRA enables parameter efficiency crucial for models with hundreds of billions of trainable parameters.

While several empirical studies have verified the effectiveness of LoRA for finetuning models in both natural language processing
(
Li et al. 2024
;
Lazauskas 2025
;
Hanindhito et al. 2025
;
Mao et al. 2025
)
and computer vision tasks
(
Zanella & Ayed 2024
;
Agiza et al. 2024
)
, the breadth of theoretical literature on LoRA remains sparse. Existing work in this area focuses on convergence
(
Malinovsky et al. 2024
;
Mu & Klabjan 2025
)
, expressiveness bounds
(
Zeng & Lee 2024
)
, and initialization
(
Hayou et al. 2024a
)
. Moreover, several such studies
(
Malladi et al. 2023
;
Jang et al. 2024
)
are conducted entirely within the neural-network setting, relying on specific architectural assumptions which do not directly generalize to the loss functions studied in our analysis. In particular, we know of only one prior
work
(
Xu et al. 2025
)
which studies the learning dynamics of LoRA using gradient flow (GF), an area we seek to build upon. While prior studies
(
Xu et al. 2025
;
Zeng & Lee 2024
)
have derived theoretical
bounds
on the loss and approximation error attainable by LoRA for certain
rank thresholds
, to our knowledge none have studied explicit, closed-form relationships between either loss or approximation error and rank parameter
r
. By
approximation error
, we mean the Frobenius-norm discrepancy between the full-rank fine-tuning update and its rank-
r
approximation. We establish here these explicit rank-accuracy tradeoffs for LoRA via a gradient-flow analysis.

1.1
Related Work

1.1.1
Expressive Power of LoRA

Previous work by Zeng and Lee
(
Zeng & Lee 2024
)
shows that, in the context of Fully Connected Neural Networks (FNNs) and Transformer Networks (TFNs), there exists a low-rank update which perfectly adapts a prefrozen network to a smaller target network. Let
W_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}
represent a prefrozen model and
\bar{W}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}
a smaller target model. Zeng and Lee demonstrate, under mild assumptions on network architecture, that
there exists
a rank-
R

(
where
R<\min(n,m)
)
adapter
\Delta W
such that
W_{0}+\Delta W
exactly represents the target model, i.e.
W_{0}+\Delta W=\bar{W}
. They note that
R
must exceed an explicit threshold which they derive. For FNNs where
\rank(\Delta W)
is lower than the required threshold for perfect adaptation, they provide an upper bound on the resulting approximation error. However, they only demonstrate a rank-independent error bound for any
\rank(\Delta W)
below the exact-adaptation threshold without analyzing any particular finetuning algorithm. In contrast, our analysis operates outside any specific neural network architecture and provides a rank-dependent final loss and approximation error from an optimization perspective, demonstrating the accuracy achieved by LoRA-adapted GD for any given rank
r<\min(n,m)
.

1.1.2
LoRA Gradient Flow

To our knowledge, only Xu et al. have previously analyzed LoRA from a GF perspective
(
Xu et al. 2025
)
. While their work studies the convergence of LoRA within a matrix factorization (MF) setting, they focus on how convergence
rates
and
behavior
depend on
initialization schema
. In particular, Xu et al. consider fine-tuning a perfectly factorized matrix
Y_{\text{pre}}=W_{2}W_{1}
toward an updated target matrix
Y_{\text{ft}}
via low-rank updates
B_{2}A_{2}
and
B_{1}A_{1}
. They find that the low-rank updates converge to the target matrix with arbitrary precision, i.e.

\displaystyle\lim_{t\to\infty}\frac{1}{2}\left\lVert Y_{\text{ft}}-\Big(W_{2}+B_{2}(t)A_{2}(t)\Big)\Big(W_{1}+B_{1}(t)A_{1}(t)\Big)\right\rVert^{2}=0.

By contrast, our work focuses on the distinct classical low-rank approximation problem. In particular, we analyze how well LoRA can approximate any given matrix
W_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}

as a function of rank
. In this sense, our work connects more closely to foundational MF work by Eckhart, Young, and Mirsky
(
Eckart & Young 1936
;
Mirsky 1960
)
, providing a continuous-time extension of their theoretical findings from the perspective of LoRA optimization dynamics.

Finally, while Xu et al. state the gradient-flow equations governing LoRA, we provide their rigorous derivation and show that these equations are invariant to whether LoRA updates are performed sequentially or simultaneously, resolving an ambiguity between theoretical formulations and common implementation practice.

1.1.3
Other Related Theoretical Work

Further theoretical work on LoRA focuses on convergence rates
(
Malinovsky et al. 2024
;
Mu & Klabjan 2025
)
, initialization
(
Hayou et al. 2024a
)
, and learning-rate selection
(
Hayou et al. 2024b
)
. Related work on MF
(
Ward & Kolda 2023
)
studies convergence behavior of factorized optimization problems but does not consider low-rank approximation. Other studies propose spectral initialization methods for LoRA
(
Bałazy et al. 2024
;
Meng et al. 2024
;
Wang et al. 2025
;
Mu et al. 2025
)
which we adopt but do not novelly contribute to in our analysis.

1.2
Contributions

We build on prior theoretical studies of LoRA by applying GF to analyze the loss and approximation error attained by LoRA as a
function of rank
. Our contributions are:

1.

While the ODEs governing LoRA are presented in previous work
(
Xu et al. 2025
)
, we provide the first (to our knowledge) rigorous derivation of these GF dynamics as the continuous-time limit of deterministic gradient descent with fixed step size under a low-rank parameterization. In particular, for updates of the form
BA
, we show that the resulting GF equations are identical under simultaneous updates of
A
and
B
(using data from the previous iterate) and sequential updates (with
A
updated after
B
using data from the current iterate).

2.

We use the resulting GF equations to analyze the learning dynamics for the trace-squared objective under LoRA:

\displaystyle\underset{\begin{subarray}{c}B\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times r}\\
A\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times n}\end{subarray}}{\min}\,\frac{1}{2}\Tr^{2}(W_{0}-BA).

(1)

where
r<\min(n,m)
. The trace-squared loss acts as a smooth spectral regularizer closely related to nuclear-norm minimization, arising in applications such as kernel alignment
(
Cristianini et al. 2006
)
and covariance shrinkage
(
Lancewicki 2019
)
. We show that LoRA GF dynamics admit a closed-form solution to (
1
) for near-arbitrary initial conditions. We solve for this solution and compute the resulting asymptotic loss and approximation error relative to the full-rank optimum as explicit functions of
r
.

3.

We use GF to analyze the learning dynamics of the low-rank approximation problem under LoRA, namely

\displaystyle\underset{\begin{subarray}{c}B\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times r}\\
A\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times m}\end{subarray}}{\min}\,\frac{1}{2}\left\lVert W_{0}-BA\right\rVert^{2}.

(2)

where
r<\min(n,m)
. Low-rank approximation is ubiquitous in applied mathematics and ML, with applications in areas such as recommender systems
(
Chen et al. 2020
)
and image processing
(
Liu et al. 2019
)
. We show that LoRA GF dynamics for (
2
) admit a closed-form solution under a spectral initialization scheme described in previous works
(
Meng et al. 2024
;
Bałazy et al. 2024
;
Xu et al. 2025
;
Wang et al. 2025
;
Mu et al. 2025
)
. Using spectral initialization, we show that the nonzero singular values of the rank-
r
finetuning matrix
BA
converge to the top
r
singular values of
W_{0}
, demonstrating that LoRA achieves the optimal low-rank solution characterized by the Eckart–Young–Mirsky (EYM) theorem
(
Eckart & Young 1936
)
.

Notation

A
, we write
A^{T}
for its transpose and
a_{ij}
for its
(i,j)
-th entry. We use
\left\lVert A\right\rVert
to denote the Frobenius norm of
A
and
\left\lVert\mathbf{a}\right\rVert_{2}
to denote the Euclidean norm of a vector
\mathbf{a}
. We use
\lfloor\cdot\rfloor
to denote the integer floor function. We denote
\Tr^{2}(W):=\Big(\Tr(W)\Big)^{2}
.

For a time-dependent function
f(t)
we write

f^{\prime}(t):=\frac{d}{dt}f(t)
for its time derivative. Uppercase
A(t)
refers to a matrix-valued function of time. When emphasizing time dependence, we write
a_{ij}(t)
for the
(i,j)
-th entry of
A(t)
, with

denoting its value at initialization.

We refer to the set of all natural numbers using
\mathbb{N}
and to the set of non-negative real numbers using
\mathbb{R}_{0}^{+}
.

Let
B\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times r}
. For a scalar-valued function

g(B,A)
of
two matrices
, we define the partial gradient

\nabla_{B}g(B,A):=\frac{\partial g}{\partial B}
to be the matrix in

\mathbb{R}^{n\times r}
whose
(i,j)
-th entry is

\frac{\partial g}{\partial b_{ij}}
.

Similarly, let
W\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}
. For a scalar-valued
function
f(W)
of a
single matrix
, we define the gradient

{\nabla f(W):=\frac{\partial f}{\partial W}}
to be the matrix in

\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}
whose
(i,j)
-th entry is

\frac{\partial f}{\partial w_{ij}}
.

The
n\times n
identity matrix is denoted by
I_{n}
. The
n\times m
matrix of all zeroes is denoted by
\mathbf{0}_{n\times m}
.

2
Results

2.1
LoRA Gradient Flow Equations

Denote the Cartesian product space
\Theta:=\mathbb{R}^{n\times r}\times\mathbb{R}^{r\times m}
. Consider a function
g:\Theta\to\mathbb{R}
which we seek to minimize over
\Theta
:

\displaystyle\underset{\begin{subarray}{c}B\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times r}\\
A\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times m}\end{subarray}}{\min}\,g\left(B,A\right)

(3)

In the context of LoRA, we will have
{g(B,A)=f(W_{0}+BA)}
for
f:\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}\to\mathbb{R}
, where
W_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}
gives the weights from a pre-trained task. We solve (
3
) via LoRA-adapted GD given in Algorithm
1
of Appendix
A
. Algorithm
1
applies alternating updates to
A
and
B
and is agnostic to how these updates are performed. For
\lambda=1
, the updates of
A
and
B
are performed simultaneously, with both parameters evaluated using data from the previous iteration. In contrast,
\lambda=0
corresponds to sequential updates, where
B
is first updated using its prior iterate, after which
A
is updated using the newly computed value of
B
from the current iteration. For
0<\lambda<1
, the update of
A
uses a convex combination of the values of
B
from the current and previous iterations. Algorithm
1
further generalizes the update scheme by allowing
A
and
B
to be updated a total of
k
times within each iteration. While
\lambda=1
is most efficient in practice for parallelism, we allow arbitrary
\lambda\in[0,1]
and
k\in\mathbb{N}
to show that the resulting GF equations for LoRA are invariant to the choice of update scheme.

The update equations at any given iteration
i
of Algorithm
1
are given by

\displaystyle B_{ik+j+1}^{(\alpha)}

\displaystyle=B_{ik+j}^{(\alpha)}-\alpha\nabla_{B}g\left(B_{ik+j}^{(\alpha)},A_{ik}^{(\alpha)}\right)

(4)

\displaystyle A_{ik+j+1}^{(\alpha)}

\displaystyle=A_{ik+j}^{(\alpha)}-\alpha\nabla_{A}g\left(\left[\lambda B_{ik}^{(\alpha)}+(1-\lambda)B_{(i+1)k}^{(\alpha)}\right],A_{ik+j}^{(\alpha)}\right).

(5)

The discrete sets of updates for
A
and
B
motivate us to define their continuous affine interpolations:

\displaystyle Y_{\alpha}(t)

\displaystyle=B_{\lfloor\frac{t}{\alpha}\rfloor}^{(\alpha)}-\left(t-\left\lfloor\frac{t}{\alpha}\right\rfloor\alpha\right)\nabla_{B}g\left(B_{\lfloor\frac{t}{\alpha}\rfloor}^{(\alpha)},A_{h(t)}^{(\alpha)}\right)

(6)

\displaystyle X_{\alpha}(t)

\displaystyle=A_{\lfloor\frac{t}{\alpha}\rfloor}^{(\alpha)}-\left(t-\left\lfloor\frac{t}{\alpha}\right\rfloor\alpha\right)\nabla_{A}g\left(\left[\lambda B_{h(t)}^{(\alpha)}+(1-\lambda)B_{h(t)+k}^{(\alpha)}\right],A_{\lfloor\frac{t}{\alpha}\rfloor}^{(\alpha)}\right),

(7)

where

\displaystyle h(t)

\displaystyle=\left\lfloor\frac{t}{\alpha}\right\rfloor-\left(\left\lfloor\frac{t}{\alpha}\right\rfloor\mod k\right).

(8)

We proceed by defining the candidate GF dynamics
a priori
.
Specifically, let
X:\mathbb{R}_{0}^{+}\to\mathbb{R}^{r\times m}
and

Y:\mathbb{R}_{0}^{+}\to\mathbb{R}^{n\times r}
be matrix-valued functions
satisfying the initial-value problem (IVP)

\displaystyle\frac{dY(t)}{dt}

\displaystyle=-\nabla_{Y}g\Big(Y(t),X(t)\Big)

(9)

\displaystyle\frac{dX(t)}{dt}

\displaystyle=-\nabla_{X}g\Big(Y(t),X(t)\Big)

(10)

\displaystyle Y(0)

\displaystyle=B_{0}

(11)

\displaystyle X(0)

\displaystyle=A_{0}.

(12)

We justify the existence of solutions
Y(t)
and
X(t)
under the following assumptions:

Assumption 2.1

(Uniform Boundedness of Iterates)

We assume that all iterates
\left\lVert B_{ik+j}^{(\alpha)}\right\rVert
and
\left\lVert A_{ik+j}^{(\alpha)}\right\rVert
remain uniformly bounded during finetuning.

Assumption 2.2

(Uniform Boundedness of Gradient)

We assume that the gradient
\left\lVert\nabla g(B,A)\right\rVert
is uniformly bounded on bounded subsets of
\Theta
.
That is, for every bounded set
S\subset\Theta
, there exists a constant
M_{S}<\infty
such that

\|\nabla g(B,A)\|\leq M_{S}\quad\text{for all }(B,A)\in S.

Definitions of norms and gradients on
\Theta
are provided in Remark
A.1
of Appendix
A
.

In subsequent portions of our derivation, we assume that
g
is Lipschitz smooth:

Assumption 2.3

(Lipschitz Smoothness of
g
)

The function
g
is Lipschitz smooth on bounded subsets of
\Theta
. That is, for any

(B_{1},A_{1}),(B_{2},A_{2})\in\Theta
satisfying

\displaystyle\max\!\Big(\left\lVert B_{1}\right\rVert,\left\lVert A_{1}\right\rVert,\left\lVert B_{2}\right\rVert,\left\lVert A_{2}\right\rVert\Big)\leq R,

(13)

there exists a constant
L_{R}>0
such that

\displaystyle\left\lVert\nabla g(B_{2},A_{2})-\nabla g(B_{1},A_{1})\right\rVert\leq L_{R}\left\lVert(B_{2},A_{2})-(B_{1},A_{1})\right\rVert.

(14)

Under these assumptions, we show that

\displaystyle\lim_{\alpha\to 0}||Y_{\alpha}(t)-Y(t)||

\displaystyle=0

(15)

\displaystyle\lim_{\alpha\to 0}||X_{\alpha}(t)-X(t)||

\displaystyle=0

(16)

for all
t\geq 0
. The uniform convergence in
(
15
–
16
) implies that the
continuous affine interpolations defined in
(
6
–
7
), and thus the
underlying discrete iterates themselves, evolve according to the
dynamical system in
(
9
–
10
) in the limit where

\alpha\to 0
. This brings us to our final result:

Theorem 2.4

Consider an objective function
g:\Theta\to\mathbb{R}
satisfying Assumptions
2.1
–
2.3
which is minimized via Algorithm
1
. During finetuning, the iterates produced by Algorithm
1

evolve according to the dynamical system in
(
9
–
10
) for arbitrary

\lambda\in[0,1]
and
k\in\mathbb{N}
.

The full proof of Theorem
2.4
is provided in
Appendix
A
. We now present two problems to demonstrate use of this result to analyze how the accuracy of LoRA depends on update rank.

2.2
Trace-Squared Objective

Consider the finetuning problem

\displaystyle\underset{\begin{subarray}{c}W\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}\end{subarray}}{\min}\,\frac{1}{2}\Tr^{2}(W_{0}-W),

(17)

as well as its rank-
r
constrained equivalent:

\displaystyle\underset{\begin{subarray}{c}B\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times r}\\
A\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times n}\end{subarray}}{\min}\,\frac{1}{2}\Tr^{2}(W_{0}-BA),

(18)

where
r<n
. We seek to compare the final losses obtained by full-rank GD on
(
17
) and by
LoRA-adapted GD on
(
18
) as a function of

r
. Additionally, we quantify the rank-dependent approximation error between the
respective minimizers of
(
17
) and
(
18
).

From the GF equation for full-rank GD, we know that the learning dynamics of (
17
) during finetuning are governed by

\displaystyle\frac{dU(t)}{dt}

\displaystyle=-\nabla\left(\frac{1}{2}\Tr^{2}\Big(W_{0}-U(t)\Big)\right)=\Tr\big(W_{0}-U(t)\big)\,I_{n}.

(19)

Here,
U:\mathbb{R}_{0}^{+}\to\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
denotes the full-rank
iterate produced by full-rank GF applied to (
17
) at time
t\geq 0
. We show that the unique closed-form solution to (
19
) is given by

\displaystyle U(t)

\displaystyle=\frac{1-e^{-nt}}{n}\Tr\left(W_{0}-Y_{0}X_{0}\right)I_{n}+Y_{0}X_{0},

(20)

where we initialize
U(0)=Y_{0}X_{0}
. The full-rank iterates converge to the minimizer of (
17
):

\displaystyle\lim_{t\to\infty}U(t)=\frac{1}{n}\Tr\!\left(W_{0}-Y_{0}X_{0}\right)I_{n}+Y_{0}X_{0}.

(21)

For consistency in our comparison of the full-rank and low-rank minimizers,
we employ the same initialization
Y_{0}X_{0}
in the LoRA setting. Following
LoRA initialization
(
Hu et al. 2022
)
, we initialize

Y_{0}=\mathbf{0}_{n\times r}
and draw the entries of

X_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times n}
i.i.d. from a centered Gaussian distribution:

\displaystyle x_{ij}(0)\sim\mathcal{N}\!\left(0,\sigma^{2}\right).

(22)

where
\sigma^{2}>0
. With this initialization
scheme in mind, the full-rank iterates converge to

\displaystyle\lim_{t\to\infty}U(t)=\frac{1}{n}\Tr\!\left(W_{0}\right)I_{n},

(23)

which yields zero final loss in
(
17
). Full details for our calculation of (
23
) are provided in Appendix
F
.

For the rank-
r
problem in (
18
), we assume uniform boundedness of the iterates as stated in
Assumption
2.1
. Under this
assumption, we show that the gradient of the trace-squared objective in (
18
) is uniformly bounded and Lipschitz continuous on bounded subsets of
\Theta
. Furthermore, we assume

Assumption 2.5

(Nonzero Initialization for
\left\lVert X_{0}\right\rVert
)

We assume
\left\lVert X_{0}\right\rVert\neq 0
, which holds almost surely for the Gaussian initialization in (
22
).

Assumption 2.6

(Nonzero Trace for Prefrozen Weights)

We assume
\Tr(W_{0})\neq 0
.

Observe the ODEs in (
26
–
27
), which govern the learning dynamics of LoRA GD for (
18
). In the case where either of Assumptions
2.5
or
2.6
is violated, the learning dynamics of (
18
) are trivially given by

\displaystyle Y(t)

\displaystyle=\mathbf{0}_{n\times r}

(24)

\displaystyle X(t)

\displaystyle=\mathbf{0}_{r\times n}

(25)

for all
t\geq 0
due to stationary point initialization.

With all assumptions from Section
2.1
satisfied, we know that the learning dynamics of (
18
) during finetuning are governed by

\displaystyle\frac{dY(t)}{dt}

\displaystyle=-\nabla_{Y}\left(\frac{1}{2}\Tr^{2}\Big(W_{0}-Y(t)X(t)\Big)\right)

\displaystyle=\Tr(W_{0}-YX)X^{T}

(26)

\displaystyle\frac{dX(t)}{dt}

\displaystyle=-\nabla_{X}\left(\frac{1}{2}\Tr^{2}\Big(W_{0}-Y(t)X(t)\Big)\right)

\displaystyle=\Tr(W_{0}-YX)Y^{T}

(27)

Here,
Y:\mathbb{R}_{0}^{+}\to\mathbb{R}^{n\times r}
and
X:\mathbb{R}_{0}^{+}\to\mathbb{R}^{r\times n}
denote the low-rank iterates produced by LoRA-adapted GF applied to (

) at time

. We find the following closed-form solution to (

):

\displaystyle Y(t)

\displaystyle=q(t)\Tr\!\left(W_{0}\right)X_{0}^{T}

(28)

\displaystyle X(t)

\displaystyle=p(t)X_{0}

(29)

where

and

given in Appendix

) holds for arbitrary

Right multiply (
28
) by (

) to find the rank-

) at time

:

\displaystyle Y(t)X(t)

\displaystyle=p(t)q(t)\Tr(W_{0})X_{0}^{T}X_{0}.

\displaystyle\lim_{t\to\infty}Y(t)X(t)

\displaystyle=\lim_{t\to\infty}p(t)q(t)\Tr\left(W_{0}\right)X_{0}^{T}X_{0}

\displaystyle=\frac{\Tr(W_{0})}{\left\lVert X_{0}\right\rVert^{2}}X_{0}^{T}X_{0},

(32)

which yields zero final loss in (

). Full details for our calculation of (

) are provided in Appendix

.

Our analysis thus shows that LoRA-adapted GF converges to a global minimizer of (
18
) for arbitrary

, bringing us to our next result:

Theorem 2.7

Consider the trace-squared optimization problems in
(
17
) and
(
18
). For (
18
), assume
uniform boundedness of the iterates as in
Assumption
2.1
as well as the nonzero initialization in Assumptions
2.5
–
2.6
.
Under GF, both the full-rank and LoRA rank-
r
dynamics
converge to global minimizers attaining zero final loss. In particular, for all

r<n
, the final losses obtained in the low-rank and full-rank cases are
zero.

In Appendix
G
, we show that the relative approximation error between
U(t)
and
Y(t)X(t)
converges to

\displaystyle\lim_{t\to\infty}\frac{\left\lVert Y(t)X(t)-U(t)\right\rVert}{\left\lVert U(t)\right\rVert}

\displaystyle=\frac{\sqrt{n\left\lVert X_{0}^{T}X_{0}\right\rVert^{2}-\left\lVert X_{0}\right\rVert^{4}}}{\left\lVert X_{0}\right\rVert^{2}},

(33)

for arbitrary
X_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times n}
with nonzero norm. If we draw the independent entries of
X_{0}
from (
22
), we can calculate the expectation of the
square
of our relative approximation error with respect to the initial conditons as a function of
n
and
r
:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\lim_{t\to\infty}\frac{\left\lVert Y(t)X(t)-U(t)\right\rVert^{2}}{\left\lVert U(t)\right\rVert^{2}}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{n^{2}+n-2}{nr+2}.

(34)

By Jensen’s Inequality
(

Theorem 2.8

Low-Rank Approximation

Consider the low-rank approximation problem

\displaystyle\underset{\begin{subarray}{c}B\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times r}\\

, we show that LoRA-adapted GD converges to the optimal rank-

solution to (

(

We again assume uniform boundedness for the iterates as stated in Assumption
2.1
. Under this
assumption, we show that the gradient of the objective in (

\Theta

Assumption 2.9

(Rank of

)

Let

. We assume that

\displaystyle r<k\leq\min(n,m).

(37)

With all assumptions from Section

satisfied, the learning dynamics for (

\displaystyle\frac{dY(t)}{dt}

\displaystyle=-\nabla_{Y}\left(\frac{1}{2}\left\lVert W_{0}-Y(t)X(t)\right\rVert^{2}\right)

\displaystyle\frac{dX(t)}{dt}

to the top
r

We begin by calculating the singular value decomposition (SVD) of

:

\displaystyle W_{0}

\displaystyle=U\Sigma_{0}V^{T}.

(40)

where the singular values in
\Sigma_{0}
are in non-increasing order along the diagonal. Using the matrices of left and right singular vectors for

, we define the following transformations of

and

\displaystyle\tilde{Y}

\displaystyle=U^{T}Y

\displaystyle\tilde{X}

\displaystyle=XV.

Under this change of variables,
(

–

\displaystyle\frac{d\tilde{Y}(t)}{dt}

\displaystyle=\left(\Sigma_{0}-\tilde{Y}\tilde{X}\right)\tilde{X}^{T}

\displaystyle\frac{d\tilde{X}(t)}{dt}

We initialize

\tilde{X}_{0}

\displaystyle\tilde{x}_{ij}(0)

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}0&i\neq j,\\
\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^{2})&i=j.\end{cases}

To prevent stationary point initialization in (

(Nonzero Diagonal Initialization)

We assume

This initialization scheme preserves diagonality during training, which

39

\displaystyle\frac{d{y}}{dt}

Here,

and

For

\displaystyle y(t)

\displaystyle=q_{s}(t)s_{0}x_{0}

\displaystyle=p_{s}(t)x_{0}

(49)

where

in Appendix

as
{t\to\infty}

{\rank\!\left(\tilde{Y}(t)\tilde{X}(t)\right)=r}
. Hence, after convergence,

and

extra
k-r

\displaystyle YX

\displaystyle=U\tilde{Y}\tilde{X}V^{T}.

\displaystyle\lim_{t\to\infty}\left\lVert W_{0}-Y(t)X(t)\right\rVert^{2}

where

. The result in (

and its optimal rank-

minimizer
(
Eckart & Young 1936

are zero after convergence, we can write the final rank-

minimizer for (

\displaystyle\lim_{t\to\infty}Y(t)X(t)

\displaystyle=U_{r}\Sigma_{0,r}V_{r}^{T}

where

and

, and

singular values of
W_{0}
, with all remaining diagonal entries equal to zero. The minimization problem equivalent to
(
36

unconstrained rank is trivially solved by

itself. Consequently, the
converged approximation error between the full-rank and low-rank solutions
coincides with the final loss in this setting. Our GF analysis
therefore shows that, for low-rank approximation, the accuracy of LoRA scales with

at a rate given by the tail of the singular-value spectrum of
W_{0}
, with residual error determined by the
(r+1)
th singular value. We thus arrive at our final result:

Theorem 2.11

Consider the optimization problem in
(

). Assume
uniform boundedness of the iterates as in
Assumption

and nonzero initialization as in Assumption

Under LoRA GF, the converged rank-

A detailed calculation for this section is provided in Appendix
I
.

3
Conclusion

In this work, we presented a generalized GD update scheme for
LoRA-adapted finetuning which is agnostic to whether the low-rank factors

A
and
B
are updated simultaneously or sequentially. We provided a rigorous derivation of the GF equations governing this
algorithm under standard boundedness and Lipschitz-smoothness assumptions and showed that the resulting LoRA GF is invariant to choice of update scheme.

Using these dynamical systems governing the behavior of LoRA, we calculated the accuracy of LoRA as a function of rank
r
. For the trace-squared objective, we found that LoRA attains zero final loss for
arbitrary rank. Moreover, under standard LoRA initialization
(
Hu et al. 2022
)
, the expected relative approximation error between the converged low-rank and full-rank
solutions decreases at least as fast as
r^{-1/2}
. For low-rank approximation, we found that the improvement in approximation
accuracy achieved by LoRA depends on the tail values of
the singular-value spectrum of
W_{0}
as the rank
r
increases, consistent with the optimal rank–accuracy
trade-off characterized by the EYM
theorem
(
Eckart & Young 1936
)
.

Several limitations of our study point to future work. First,
our spectral initialization scheme may prove unfeasible in settings where
computing the full SVD of the prefrozen weights
W_{0}
is impractical. Second, the rank–accuracy dependence derived here is specific to our chosen optimization objectives. Extension of this analysis involves repeating our calculation for each new loss function, while only two elementary objectives are studied in this work.

Future work could address these limitations by developing a generalized
update scheme for SGD adapted via LoRA and deriving its
associated GF equations. Additional directions include
application of our analysis to more complex or task-specific loss functions as well
as design of LoRA-based approaches to low-rank approximation which do not rely
on access to the full SVD of
W_{0}
.

Lemma D.1

(Lipschitz Smoothness for Trace Squared Objective)

Define
g:\mathbb{R}^{n\times r}\times\mathbb{R}^{r\times n}\to\mathbb{R}
via

\displaystyle g(B,A)

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{2}\Tr^{2}(W_{0}-BA)

(407)

for constant
W_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
. Then the objective gradient
\nabla g
is Lipschitz smooth in our training domain. Namely, there exists
L_{R^{\prime}}>0
such that, for any
(B_{1},A_{1}),(B_{2},A_{2})\in\mathcal{D}_{R^{\prime}}
, we have

\displaystyle\left\lVert\nabla g(B_{1},A_{1})-\nabla g(B_{2},A_{2})\right\rVert\leq L_{R^{\prime}}\left\lVert\Big(B_{1},A_{1}\Big)-\Big(B_{2},A_{2}\Big)\right\rVert

(408)

Proof:

Let
(B_{1},A_{1}),(B_{2},A_{2})\in\mathcal{D}_{R^{\prime}}
be given. Refer to the partial gradients for
g(B,A)
in (
265
–
266
) of Appendix
C
. We have

\displaystyle\left\lVert\nabla g(B_{1},A_{1})-\nabla g(B_{2},A_{2})\right\rVert

\displaystyle=\left\lVert\Bigg(\nabla_{B}g(B_{1},A_{1})-\nabla_{B}g(B_{2},A_{2})\,,\,\nabla_{A}g(B_{1},A_{1})-\nabla_{A}g(B_{2},A_{2})\Bigg)\right\rVert

(409)

The partial gradients with respect to
B
simplify to

\displaystyle\nabla_{B}g(B_{1},A_{1})-\nabla_{B}g(B_{2},A_{2})

\displaystyle=-\Tr(W_{0}-B_{1}A_{1})A_{1}^{T}+\Tr(W_{0}-B_{2}A_{2})A_{2}^{T}

(410)

\displaystyle=\Tr(W_{0}-B_{2}A_{2})A_{2}^{T}-\Tr(W_{0}-B_{1}A_{1})A_{2}^{T}+\Tr(W_{0}-B_{1}A_{1})A_{2}^{T}-\Tr(W_{0}-B_{1}A_{1})A_{1}^{T}

(411)

\displaystyle=\Big[\Tr(W_{0}-B_{2}A_{2})-\Tr(W_{0}-B_{1}A_{1})\Big]A_{2}^{T}+\Tr(W_{0}-B_{1}A_{1})\left(A_{2}^{T}-A_{1}^{T}\right)

(412)

\displaystyle=\Big[\Tr(B_{1}A_{1}-B_{2}A_{2})\Big]A_{2}^{T}+\Tr(W_{0}-B_{1}A_{1})\left(A_{2}^{T}-A_{1}^{T}\right)

(413)

Recall that
|\Tr(BA)|\leq\left\lVert B\right\rVert\left\lVert A\right\rVert
(Cauchy-Schwarz for the Frobenius inner product). Taking the norm, we find

\displaystyle\left\lVert\nabla_{B}g(B_{1},A_{1})-\nabla_{B}g(B_{2},A_{2})\right\rVert

\displaystyle=\left\lVert\Big[\Tr(B_{1}A_{1}-B_{2}A_{2})\Big]A_{2}^{T}+\Tr(W_{0}-B_{1}A_{1})\left(A_{2}^{T}-A_{1}^{T}\right)\right\rVert

(414)

\displaystyle\leq\left\lVert\Big[\Tr(B_{1}A_{1}-B_{2}A_{2})\Big]A_{2}^{T}\right\rVert+\left\lVert\Tr(W_{0}-B_{1}A_{1})\left(A_{2}^{T}-A_{1}^{T}\right)\right\rVert

(415)

\displaystyle=|\Tr(B_{1}A_{1}-B_{2}A_{2})|\left\lVert A_{2}^{T}\right\rVert+|\Tr(W_{0}-B_{1}A_{1})|\left\lVert A_{2}^{T}-A_{1}^{T}\right\rVert

(416)

\displaystyle=|\Tr(B_{1}A_{1}-B_{1}A_{2}+B_{1}A_{2}-B_{2}A_{2})|\left\lVert A_{2}^{T}\right\rVert+|\Tr(W_{0}-B_{1}A_{1})|\left\lVert A_{2}^{T}-A_{1}^{T}\right\rVert

(417)

\displaystyle=\left|\Tr\Big(B_{1}(A_{1}-A_{2})+(B_{1}-B_{2})A_{2}\Big)\right|\left\lVert A_{2}^{T}\right\rVert+|\Tr(W_{0}-B_{1}A_{1})|\left\lVert A_{2}^{T}-A_{1}^{T}\right\rVert

(418)

\displaystyle=\left|\Tr\Big(B_{1}(A_{1}-A_{2})\Big)+\Tr\Big((B_{1}-B_{2})A_{2}\Big)\right|\left\lVert A_{2}^{T}\right\rVert+|\Tr(W_{0}-B_{1}A_{1})|\left\lVert A_{2}^{T}-A_{1}^{T}\right\rVert

(419)

\displaystyle\leq\Big(\left\lVert B_{1}\right\rVert\left\lVert A_{1}-A_{2}\right\rVert+\left\lVert B_{1}-B_{2}\right\rVert\left\lVert A_{2}\right\rVert\Big)\left\lVert A_{2}^{T}\right\rVert+|\Tr(W_{0}-B_{1}A_{1})|\left\lVert A_{2}^{T}-A_{1}^{T}\right\rVert

(420)

\displaystyle\leq\Big(R^{\prime}\left\lVert A_{1}-A_{2}\right\rVert+\left\lVert B_{1}-B_{2}\right\rVert R^{\prime}\Big)R^{\prime}+|\Tr(W_{0}-B_{1}A_{1})|\left\lVert A_{2}^{T}-A_{1}^{T}\right\rVert

(421)

\displaystyle\leq\Big(\left\lVert A_{1}-A_{2}\right\rVert+\left\lVert B_{1}-B_{2}\right\rVert\Big)R^{\prime 2}+\Big(|\Tr(W_{0})|+|\Tr(B_{1}A_{1})|\Big)\left\lVert A_{2}^{T}-A_{1}^{T}\right\rVert

(422)

\displaystyle\leq\Big(\left\lVert A_{1}-A_{2}\right\rVert+\left\lVert B_{1}-B_{2}\right\rVert\Big)R^{\prime 2}+\Big(|\Tr(W_{0})|+\left\lVert B_{1}\right\rVert\left\lVert A_{1}\right\rVert\Big)\left\lVert A_{2}^{T}-A_{1}^{T}\right\rVert

(423)

\displaystyle\leq\Big(\left\lVert A_{1}-A_{2}\right\rVert+\left\lVert B_{1}-B_{2}\right\rVert\Big)R^{\prime 2}+\Big(|\Tr(W_{0})|+R^{\prime 2}\Big)\left\lVert A_{2}^{T}-A_{1}^{T}\right\rVert

(424)

\displaystyle=\left\lVert B_{1}-B_{2}\right\rVert R^{\prime 2}+\Big(|\Tr(W_{0})|+2R^{\prime 2}\Big)\left\lVert A_{2}-A_{1}\right\rVert

(425)

\displaystyle\leq\left\lVert B_{1}-B_{2}\right\rVert\Big(|\Tr(W_{0})|+2R^{\prime 2}\Big)+\Big(|\Tr(W_{0})|+2R^{\prime 2}\Big)\left\lVert A_{2}-A_{1}\right\rVert

\displaystyle=\Big(|\Tr(W_{0})|+2R^{\prime 2}\Big)\Big(\left\lVert B_{1}-B_{2}\right\rVert+\left\lVert A_{1}-A_{2}\right\rVert\Big)

\displaystyle\left\lVert\nabla_{B}g(B_{1},A_{1})-\nabla_{B}g(B_{2},A_{2})\right\rVert

{\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}L_{R^{\prime}}}:=\sqrt{2}\Big(|\Tr(W_{0})|+2R^{\prime 2}\Big)
. Squaring (

\displaystyle\left\lVert\nabla_{B}g(B_{1},A_{1})-\nabla_{B}g(B_{2},A_{2})\right\rVert^{2}

An identical calculation for
\left\lVert\nabla_{A}g(B_{1},A_{1})-\nabla_{A}g(B_{2},A_{2})\right\rVert

\displaystyle\left\lVert\nabla_{A}g(B_{1},A_{1})-\nabla_{A}g(B_{2},A_{2})\right\rVert^{2}

\displaystyle\leq{\frac{1}{2}L_{R^{\prime}}^{2}}\bigg(\left\lVert B_{1}-B_{2}\right\rVert^{2}+\left\lVert A_{1}-A_{2}\right\rVert^{2}\bigg)

(431)

Together, (

431

\displaystyle\left\lVert\nabla g(B_{1},A_{1})-\nabla g(B_{2},A_{2})\right\rVert^{2}

\displaystyle=\left\lVert\nabla_{B}g(B_{1},A_{1})-\nabla_{B}g(B_{2},A_{2})\right\rVert^{2}+\left\lVert\nabla_{A}g(B_{1},A_{1})-\nabla_{A}g(B_{2},A_{2})\right\rVert^{2}

(432)

\displaystyle\leq{L_{R^{\prime}}^{2}}\bigg(\left\lVert B_{1}-B_{2}\right\rVert^{2}+\left\lVert A_{1}-A_{2}\right\rVert^{2}\bigg)

(433)

\displaystyle=L_{R^{\prime}}^{2}\left\lVert\Big(B_{1},A_{1}\Big)-\Big(B_{2},A_{2}\Big)\right\rVert^{2}

(434)

or simply

\displaystyle\left\lVert\nabla g(B_{1},A_{1})-\nabla g(B_{2},A_{2})\right\rVert\leq L_{R^{\prime}}\left\lVert\Big(B_{1},A_{1}\Big)-\Big(B_{2},A_{2}\Big)\right\rVert

for all
(B_{1},A_{1}),(B_{2},A_{2})\in\mathcal{D}_{R^{\prime}}
Thus,

Lipschitz smooth on

Appendix F
Learning Dynamics for Trace-Squared Loss (Full-rank)

Let
W_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
be a matrix of frozen pretraining weights. We wish to analyze the learning dynamics of the full-rank optimizer
W\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
produced by applying full-rank GD to the problem

\displaystyle\underset{\begin{subarray}{c}W\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}\end{subarray}}{\min}\,\frac{1}{2}\Tr^{2}(W_{0}-W)

(517)

The gradient for the loss function
f(W)
above is given by

\displaystyle\nabla f(W)=-\Tr\left(W_{0}-W\right)I_{n}

(518)

where
I_{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
denotes the identity matrix. The gradient flow ODE describing the learning dynamics of the trace-squared objective in
(
517
) under full-rank gradient descent is given by

\displaystyle\frac{dU(t)}{dt}

\displaystyle=-\nabla f\big(U(t)\big)=\Tr\big(W_{0}-U(t)\big)\,I_{n},

(519)

\displaystyle U(0)

\displaystyle=Y_{0}X_{0},

(520)

where the solution
U:\mathbb{R}_{0}^{+}\to\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
denotes the continuous-time analogue of the full-rank GD iterates.
Throughout the calculation below, we allow a general initialization
Y_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times r}
for completeness. However,
to facilitate direct comparison with the low-rank learning dynamics for the same objective
(see Appendix
C
), we specialize to the choice

Y_{0}=\mathbf{0}_{n\times r}
at the conclusion of our analysis.

Taking the trace of both sides of (
519
–
520
), we have

\displaystyle\frac{d\Tr\Big(U(t)\Big)}{dt}

\displaystyle=\Tr\Big(W_{0}-U(t)\Big)\Tr\left(I_{n}\right)

(521)

\displaystyle\frac{d\Tr\Big(U(t)\Big)}{dt}

\displaystyle=n\Tr\Big(W_{0}-U(t)\Big)

(522)

\displaystyle\frac{d\Tr\Big(U(t)\Big)}{dt}

\displaystyle=n\Tr\left(W_{0}\right)-n\Tr\Big(U(t)\Big)

(523)

\displaystyle\Tr\Big(U(0)\Big)

\displaystyle=\Tr(Y_{0}X_{0})

(524)

which is solved by

\displaystyle\Tr\Big(U(t)\Big)

\displaystyle=\Tr(W_{0})-e^{-nt}\Tr\left(W_{0}-Y_{0}X_{0}\right)

(525)

Plugging our closed-form expression for
\Tr\Big(U(t)\Big)
into (
519
), we arrive at

\displaystyle\frac{dU(t)}{dt}

\displaystyle=\Tr\Big(W_{0}-U(t)\Big)I_{n}

(526)

\displaystyle\frac{dU(t)}{dt}

\displaystyle=\left[\Tr\left(W_{0}\right)-\Tr\Big(U(t)\Big)\right]I_{n}

(527)

\displaystyle\frac{dU(t)}{dt}

\displaystyle=\left[\Tr\left(W_{0}\right)-\Tr(W_{0})+e^{-nt}\Tr\left(W_{0}-Y_{0}X_{0}\right)\right]I_{n}

(528)

\displaystyle\frac{dU(t)}{dt}

\displaystyle=e^{-nt}\Tr\left(W_{0}-Y_{0}X_{0}\right)I_{n}

(529)

which, for our initial condition in (
520
), is solved by

\displaystyle U(t)

\displaystyle=\frac{1-e^{-nt}}{n}\Tr\left(W_{0}-Y_{0}X_{0}\right)I_{n}+Y_{0}X_{0}

(530)

For a quick sanity check, note that

\displaystyle U(0)

\displaystyle=Y_{0}X_{0}

(531)

\displaystyle\lim\limits_{t\to\infty}\Tr\Big(U(t)\Big)

\displaystyle=\Tr\left(\frac{1}{n}\Tr\left(W_{0}-Y_{0}X_{0}\right)I_{n}+Y_{0}X_{0}\right)

(532)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\Tr\left(W_{0}-Y_{0}X_{0}\right)\Tr\left(I_{n}\right)+\Tr(Y_{0}X_{0})

(533)

\displaystyle=\Tr\left(W_{0}-Y_{0}X_{0}\right)+\Tr(Y_{0}X_{0})

(534)

\displaystyle=\Tr(W_{0})

(535)

which gives the result we would expect for the minimization problem in (
517
).

Taking the limit of
U(t)
as
t\to\infty
, the gradient flow in (
519
–
520
)
converges to the following global minimizer of (
517
):

\displaystyle\lim_{t\to\infty}U(t)=\frac{1}{n}\Tr\!\left(W_{0}-Y_{0}X_{0}\right)I_{n}+Y_{0}X_{0}

(536)

In particular, for the same initialization
Y_{0}=\mathbf{0}_{n\times r}
used in our low-rank analysis
(Appendix
C
),
the full-rank flow converges to

\displaystyle\lim_{t\to\infty}U(t)=\frac{1}{n}\Tr(W_{0})\,I_{n}.

(537)

To demonstrate uniqueness of our solution
U(t)
to the IVP in (
519
–
520
), suppose there exists an additional solution
U^{*}(t)
which satisfies (
519
–
520
). We then have

\displaystyle\frac{dU^{*}(t)}{dt}

\displaystyle=\Tr\Big(W_{0}-U^{*}(t)\Big)I_{n}

(538)

\displaystyle U^{*}(0)

\displaystyle=Y_{0}X_{0}

(539)

We can calculate

\displaystyle\frac{dU(t)}{dt}-\frac{dU^{*}(t)}{dt}=\frac{d}{dt}\Big(U(t)-U^{*}(t)\Big)

\displaystyle=\Tr\Big(W_{0}-U(t)\Big)I_{n}-\Tr\Big(W_{0}-U^{*}(t)\Big)I_{n}

(540)

\displaystyle=\Tr\Big(U^{*}(t)-U(t)\Big)I_{n}

(541)

\displaystyle=-\Tr\Big(U(t)-U^{*}(t)\Big)I_{n}

(542)

or simply

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\Big(U(t)-U^{*}(t)\Big)

\displaystyle=-\Tr\Big(U(t)-U^{*}(t)\Big)I_{n}

(543)

Note that
U(0)-U^{*}(0)=Y_{0}X_{0}-Y_{0}X_{0}=\mathbf{0}_{n\times n}
.
Now define
S:\mathbb{R}_{0}^{+}\to\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
via

\displaystyle S(t)

\displaystyle=U(t)-U^{*}(t)

(544)

\displaystyle S(0)

\displaystyle=Y_{0}X_{0}-Y_{0}X_{0}=\mathbf{0}_{n\times n}

(545)

Then (
543
) gives

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\Big(S(t)\Big)

\displaystyle=-\Tr\Big(S(t)\Big)I_{n}

(546)

Take the trace of both sides of (
546
) to find

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\Tr\Big(S(t)\Big)

\displaystyle=-\Tr\Big(S(t)\Big)\Tr\left(I_{n}\right)

(547)

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\Tr\Big(S(t)\Big)

\displaystyle=-n\Tr\Big(S(t)\Big)

(548)

which is solved with the initial condition
\Tr\Big(S(0)\Big)=0
by

\displaystyle\Tr\Big(S(t)\Big)

\displaystyle=0

(549)

Since the trace of
S(t)
has only the trivial solution, (
546
) becomes

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\Big(S(t)\Big)

\displaystyle=\mathbf{0}_{n\times n}

(550)

This implies that
S(t)
is a constant equal to its initial condition. Thus,

\displaystyle S(t)

\displaystyle=\mathbf{0}_{n\times n}

(551)

for all
t\in[0,\infty)
. This implies that

\displaystyle U(t)=U^{*}(t)

(552)

and our solution
U(t)
to the IVP in (
519
–
520
) must be unique.

Appendix G
Low-Rank Approximation Error for the Trace-Squared Objective Relative to the Full-Rank Minimizer

In this work, we analyze both the full-rank and LoRA parameterizations of the trace-squared fine-tuning problem.
Using gradient flow dynamics, we characterize the convergence behavior of classical full-rank gradient descent
as well as low-rank–adapted gradient descent toward minimizers of their respective objectives:

\displaystyle\underset{W\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}}{\min}\;

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\Tr^{2}(W_{0}-W)

(553)

\displaystyle\underset{\begin{subarray}{c}B\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times r}\\
A\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times n}\end{subarray}}{\min}\;

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\Tr^{2}(W_{0}-BA).

(554)

We find that application of classical GD to the full-rank problem converges to the following minimizer of (
553
):

\displaystyle\lim_{t\to\infty}U(t)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\Tr(W_{0})\,I_{n}\in\underset{W\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}}{\arg\min}\;\frac{1}{2}\Tr^{2}(W_{0}-W)

(555)

while application of low-rank adapted GD to the low-rank problem converges to the following minimizer of (
554
):

\displaystyle\lim_{t\to\infty}Y(t)X(t)

\displaystyle=\frac{\Tr(W_{0})}{\left\lVert X_{0}\right\rVert^{2}}X_{0}^{T}X_{0}\in\underset{\begin{subarray}{c}B\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times r}\\
A\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times n}\end{subarray}}{\arg\min}\;\frac{1}{2}\Tr^{2}(W_{0}-BA)

(556)

Recall that, for both the low-rank and full-rank variants of our problem, we adopt the standard LoRA initialization scheme
(
Hu et al. 2022
)
, namely

U(0)=Y(0)X(0)=Y_{0}X_{0}
, where
Y_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times r}
is initialized as the zero matrix and

X_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times n}
has entries drawn i.i.d. from a centered Gaussian distribution with variance
\sigma^{2}
. Namely,

\displaystyle x_{ij}(0)\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^{2}).

(557)

where
x_{ij}
are the individual elements of
X
.

Recall from Appendices
C

and
F
that both
the full-rank and low-rank gradient flows converge to global minimizers of the
trace-squared objective, achieving zero final loss. We thus examine only the approximation error between the resulting full-rank and low-rank minimizers. In particular, we calculate the final relative error:

\displaystyle\lim_{t\to\infty}\frac{\left\lVert Y(t)X(t)-U(t)\right\rVert^{2}}{\left\lVert U(t)\right\rVert^{2}}

(558)

The norm in the denominator is simply

\displaystyle\lim_{t\to\infty}\left\lVert U(t)\right\rVert^{2}

\displaystyle=\left\lVert\frac{1}{n}\Tr(W_{0})\,I_{n}\right\rVert^{2}

(559)

\displaystyle=\frac{|\Tr(W_{0})|^{2}}{n^{2}}\left\lVert I_{n}\right\rVert^{2}

(560)

\displaystyle=\frac{|\Tr(W_{0})|^{2}}{n^{2}}\cdot n

(561)

\displaystyle=\frac{|\Tr(W_{0})|^{2}}{n}

(562)

For the numerator, we find

\displaystyle\lim_{t\to\infty}\left\lVert Y(t)X(t)-U(t)\right\rVert^{2}

\displaystyle=\left\lVert\frac{\Tr(W_{0})}{\left\lVert X_{0}\right\rVert^{2}}X_{0}^{T}X_{0}-\frac{1}{n}\Tr(W_{0})\,I_{n}\right\rVert^{2}

(563)

\displaystyle=\left|\Tr(W_{0})\right|^{2}\left\lVert\frac{X_{0}^{T}X_{0}}{\left\lVert X_{0}\right\rVert^{2}}-\frac{1}{n}\,I_{n}\right\rVert^{2}

(564)

Calculate the Frobenius norm above:

\displaystyle\left\lVert\frac{X_{0}^{T}X_{0}}{\left\lVert X_{0}\right\rVert^{2}}-\frac{1}{n}\,I_{n}\right\rVert^{2}

\displaystyle=\Tr\left(\left[\frac{X_{0}^{T}X_{0}}{\left\lVert X_{0}\right\rVert^{2}}-\frac{1}{n}\,I_{n}\right]^{T}\left[\frac{X_{0}^{T}X_{0}}{\left\lVert X_{0}\right\rVert^{2}}-\frac{1}{n}\,I_{n}\right]\right)

(565)

\displaystyle=\Tr\left(\left[\frac{X_{0}^{T}X_{0}}{\left\lVert X_{0}\right\rVert^{2}}-\frac{1}{n}\,I_{n}\right]\left[\frac{X_{0}^{T}X_{0}}{\left\lVert X_{0}\right\rVert^{2}}-\frac{1}{n}\,I_{n}\right]\right)

(566)

\displaystyle=\Tr\left(\frac{X_{0}^{T}X_{0}X_{0}^{T}X_{0}}{\left\lVert X_{0}\right\rVert^{4}}-\frac{2}{n}\frac{X_{0}^{T}X_{0}}{\left\lVert X_{0}\right\rVert^{2}}+\frac{1}{n^{2}}I_{n}\right)

(567)

\displaystyle=\Tr\left(\frac{X_{0}^{T}X_{0}X_{0}^{T}X_{0}}{\left\lVert X_{0}\right\rVert^{4}}\right)-\frac{2}{n}\Tr\left(\frac{X_{0}^{T}X_{0}}{\left\lVert X_{0}\right\rVert^{2}}\right)+\frac{1}{n^{2}}\Tr\left(I_{n}\right)

(568)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\left\lVert X_{0}\right\rVert^{4}}\Tr\left({X_{0}^{T}X_{0}X_{0}^{T}X_{0}}\right)-\frac{2}{n\left\lVert X_{0}\right\rVert^{2}}\Tr\left({X_{0}^{T}X_{0}}\right)+\frac{1}{n^{2}}\cdot n

(569)

\displaystyle=\frac{\left\lVert X_{0}^{T}X_{0}\right\rVert^{2}}{\left\lVert X_{0}\right\rVert^{4}}-\frac{2\left\lVert X_{0}\right\rVert^{2}}{n\left\lVert X_{0}\right\rVert^{2}}+\frac{1}{n}

(570)

\displaystyle=\frac{\left\lVert X_{0}^{T}X_{0}\right\rVert^{2}}{\left\lVert X_{0}\right\rVert^{4}}-\frac{1}{n}

(571)

We then have

\displaystyle\lim_{t\to\infty}\left\lVert Y(t)X(t)-U(t)\right\rVert^{2}

\displaystyle=\left|\Tr(W_{0})\right|^{2}\left(\frac{\left\lVert X_{0}^{T}X_{0}\right\rVert^{2}}{\left\lVert X_{0}\right\rVert^{4}}-\frac{1}{n}\right)

(572)

The square of the relative error between the low-rank and full-rank minimizers for arbitrary
X_{0}\neq\mathbf{0}_{r\times n}
is then

\displaystyle\lim_{t\to\infty}\frac{\left\lVert Y(t)X(t)-U(t)\right\rVert^{2}}{\left\lVert U(t)\right\rVert^{2}}

\displaystyle=\left|\Tr(W_{0})\right|^{2}\left(\frac{\left\lVert X_{0}^{T}X_{0}\right\rVert^{2}}{\left\lVert X_{0}\right\rVert^{4}}-\frac{1}{n}\right)\cdot\frac{n}{|\Tr(W_{0})|^{2}}

(573)

\displaystyle=\frac{n\left\lVert X_{0}^{T}X_{0}\right\rVert^{2}}{\left\lVert X_{0}\right\rVert^{4}}-1

(574)

Note that the expression above is non-negative since
\sqrt{n}\left\lVert X_{0}^{T}X_{0}\right\rVert\geq\left\lVert X_{0}\right\rVert^{2}
by Cauchy-Schwarz applied to the eigenvalues of
X_{0}^{T}X_{0}
.
We then have the final relative error

\displaystyle\lim_{t\to\infty}\frac{\left\lVert Y(t)X(t)-U(t)\right\rVert}{\left\lVert U(t)\right\rVert}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\frac{n\left\lVert X_{0}^{T}X_{0}\right\rVert^{2}}{\left\lVert X_{0}\right\rVert^{4}}-1}

(575)

\displaystyle=\frac{\sqrt{n\left\lVert X_{0}^{T}X_{0}\right\rVert^{2}-\left\lVert X_{0}\right\rVert^{4}}}{\left\lVert X_{0}\right\rVert^{2}}

(576)

To reiterate, the expression above for the relative error between the low-rank and full-rank gradient flow minimizers is valid for arbitrary
X_{0}\neq\mathbf{0}_{r\times n}
. However, we can study the
expected
approximation error as a function of
r
and
n
in the context of the standard LoRA initialization scheme in (
557
).

Initializing
X_{0}\in\mathbbm{R}^{n\times r}
as in (
557
), begin by calculating the expected value of the ratio

\displaystyle\frac{\left\lVert X_{0}^{T}X_{0}\right\rVert^{2}}{\left\lVert X_{0}\right\rVert^{4}}

(577)

Write

\displaystyle X_{0}=rZ

(578)

where
r=\left\lVert X_{0}\right\rVert
and
Z=\dfrac{X_{0}}{\left\lVert X_{0}\right\rVert}
. Then (
577
) becomes

\displaystyle\frac{\left\lVert X_{0}^{T}X_{0}\right\rVert^{2}}{\left\lVert X_{0}\right\rVert^{4}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\left\lVert r^{2}Z^{T}Z\right\rVert^{2}}{\left\lVert r\right\rVert^{4}}=\frac{r^{4}\left\lVert Z^{T}Z\right\rVert^{2}}{r^{4}}=\left\lVert Z^{T}Z\right\rVert^{2}=\sum_{a=1}^{r}\sum_{b=1}^{r}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}z_{ia}\,z_{ib}\,z_{ja}\,z_{jb}

(579)

To calculate the expectation of the expression above, we then need the expectation of the individual scalar entries of
Z
.
Let
\mathbf{z}\in\mathbb{R}^{nr}
denote the vector of stacked entries of
Z
, and let
\mathbf{x_{0}}\in\mathbbm{R}^{nr}
denote the vector of stacked entries of
X_{0}
. Use
\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert_{2}
to represent the vector Euclidean norm. Then

\displaystyle\mathbf{z}

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathbf{x_{0}}}{\left\lVert\mathbf{x_{0}}\right\rVert_{2}}

(580)

We have that

\displaystyle\mathbf{x_{0}}\sim\mathcal{N}\left(0,\sigma^{2}I_{nr}\right)

(581)

By the properties of any scalar random variable
\xi
that

\displaystyle\Var(\sigma\xi)

\displaystyle=\sigma^{2}\Var(\xi)

(582)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\sigma\xi\right]

\displaystyle=\sigma\mathbb{E}\left[\xi\right]

(583)

we can write

\displaystyle\mathbf{x_{0}}

\displaystyle=\sigma\mathbf{g}

(584)

for
\mathbf{g}\in\mathbb{R}^{nr}
, where

\displaystyle\mathbf{g}\sim\mathcal{N}\left(0,I_{nr}\right)

(585)

It is known that
\dfrac{\mathbf{g}}{\left\lVert\mathbf{g}\right\rVert_{2}}
is uniformly distributed on the unit sphere in
\mathbb{R}^{nr}

(
Vershynin 2018
)
. We then immediately have

\displaystyle\mathbf{z}

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathbf{x_{0}}}{\left\lVert\mathbf{x_{0}}\right\rVert_{2}}=\frac{\sigma\mathbf{g}}{\sigma\left\lVert\mathbf{g}\right\rVert_{2}}=\frac{\mathbf{g}}{\left\lVert\mathbf{g}\right\rVert_{2}}

(586)

and therefore
\mathbf{z}
is uniformly distributed on the unit sphere. Now denote
\rho:=\left\lVert\mathbf{g}\right\rVert_{2}
. We know that
\mathbf{z}
and
\rho
are independent
(
Vershynin 2018
)
. Choose arbitrary indices
i,j\in\{1,\dots,nr\}
. For the corresponding scalar components of
\mathbf{z}
and
\mathbf{g}
, we have

\displaystyle z_{i}^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{g_{i}^{2}}{\rho^{2}}\qquad\text{ and }\qquad z_{i}^{2}z_{j}^{2}=\frac{g_{i}^{2}g_{j}^{2}}{\rho^{4}}

(587)

or

\displaystyle\rho^{2}z_{i}^{2}

\displaystyle=g_{i}^{2}\qquad\text{ and }\qquad\rho^{4}z_{i}^{2}z_{j}^{2}=g_{i}^{2}g_{j}^{2}

(588)

Taking advantage of the independence of
\mathbf{z}
and
\rho
, we find

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\rho^{2}\right]\mathbb{E}\left[z_{i}^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[g_{i}^{2}\right]\qquad\text{ and }\qquad\mathbb{E}\left[\rho^{4}\right]\mathbb{E}\left[z_{i}^{2}z_{j}^{2}\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[g_{i}^{2}g_{j}^{2}\right]

(589)

We can thus calculate

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[z_{i}^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathbb{E}\left[g_{i}^{2}\right]}{\mathbb{E}\left[\rho^{2}\right]}\qquad\text{ and }\qquad\mathbb{E}\left[z_{i}^{2}z_{j}^{2}\right]=\frac{\mathbb{E}\left[g_{i}^{2}g_{j}^{2}\right]}{\mathbb{E}\left[\rho^{4}\right]}

(590)

By standard results for Gaussian normal distributions, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[g_{i}^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=1

(591)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[g_{i}^{2}g_{j}^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}1&i\neq j\\
3&i=j\end{cases}

(592)

Since
\mathbf{g}\in\mathbb{R}^{nr}
and
\rho=\left\lVert\mathbf{g}\right\rVert_{2}
, we know that

\displaystyle\rho^{2}

\displaystyle=\left\lVert\mathbf{g}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}=\sum\limits_{i=1}^{nr}g_{i}^{2}

(593)

Therefore,
\rho^{2}
follows a chi-squared distribution with
nr
degrees of freedom, or
\rho^{2}\sim\chi^{2}_{nr}
.
By standard results for first and second moments of chi-squared distributions, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\rho^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=nr

(594)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\rho^{4}\right]

\displaystyle=nr(nr+2)

(595)

Putting these results together, (
590
) becomes

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[z_{i}^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{nr}\qquad\text{ and }\qquad\mathbb{E}\left[z_{i}^{2}z_{j}^{2}\right]=\begin{cases}\dfrac{1}{nr(nr+2)}&i\neq j\\
\dfrac{3}{nr(nr+2)}&i=j\end{cases}

(596)

Thus we have the second and fourth moments of the scalar components of
\mathbf{z}
, or, equivalently, the scalar compenents of
Z
.

We are now ready to compute the expectation of
\dfrac{\left\lVert X_{0}^{T}X_{0}\right\rVert^{2}}{\left\lVert X_{0}\right\rVert^{4}}
. Taking the expectation of (
579
) gives

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{\left\lVert X_{0}^{T}X_{0}\right\rVert^{2}}{\left\lVert X_{0}\right\rVert^{4}}\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{a=1}^{r}\sum_{b=1}^{r}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}\left[z_{ia}\,z_{ib}\,z_{ja}\,z_{jb}\right]

(597)

The expectation

\mathbb{E}\!\left[z_{ia}\,z_{ib}\,z_{ja}\,z_{jb}\right]

depends only on whether the row indices
a,b
and the column indices
i,j
coincide, and hence takes one of four possible values corresponding to the cases where
i=j
or
i\neq j
and
a=b
or
a\neq b
:

A.

a=b
and
i=j
. In this case, all four factors coincide, and by
(
596
),

\mathbb{E}\!\left[z_{ia}\,z_{ib}\,z_{ja}\,z_{jb}\right]=\mathbb{E}\!\left[z_{ia}^{4}\right]=\frac{3}{nr(nr+2)}.

B.

a=b
and
i\neq j
. In this case,

\mathbb{E}\!\left[z_{ia}\,z_{ib}\,z_{ja}\,z_{jb}\right]=\mathbb{E}\!\left[z_{ia}^{2}z_{ja}^{2}\right]=\frac{1}{nr(nr+2)},

which is again given by (
596
).

C.

a\neq b
and
i=j
. In this case,

\mathbb{E}\!\left[z_{ia}\,z_{ib}\,z_{ja}\,z_{jb}\right]=\mathbb{E}\!\left[z_{ia}^{2}z_{ib}^{2}\right]=\frac{1}{nr(nr+2)}.

D.

a\neq b
and
i\neq j
. In this case, the four indices are distinct, and, by symmetry on the unit sphere,

\mathbb{E}\!\left[z_{ia}\,z_{ib}\,z_{ja}\,z_{jb}\right]=0.

See Appendix
H
for an explanation of this case.

Note that, for the sum in (
597
), we will have
nr
terms fall under case A (
r
choices for
a
and
n
choices for
i
). We will have
nr(n-1)
terms satisfy case B (
r
choices for
a
and
n(n-1)
choices for
i
). For case C, we will have
nr(r-1)
terms (
n
choices for
i
and
r(r-1)
choices for
a
). Terms for case D contribute nothing to the expectation value and do not need to be counted. We finally calculate our sum:

\displaystyle\sum_{a=1}^{r}\sum_{b=1}^{r}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}\left[z_{ia}\,z_{ib}\,z_{ja}\,z_{jb}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{3nr}{nr(nr+2)}+\frac{nr(n-1)}{nr(nr+2)}+\frac{nr(r-1)}{nr(nr+2)}

(598)

\displaystyle=\frac{3}{(nr+2)}+\frac{(n-1)}{(nr+2)}+\frac{(r-1)}{(nr+2)}

(599)

\displaystyle=\frac{n+r+1}{nr+2}

(600)

So we arrive at

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{\left\lVert X_{0}^{T}X_{0}\right\rVert^{2}}{\left\lVert X_{0}\right\rVert^{4}}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{n+r+1}{nr+2}

(601)

The expectation of the
square
of the final relative error between the rank-
r
and full-rank solutions is then

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\lim_{t\to\infty}\frac{\left\lVert Y(t)X(t)-U(t)\right\rVert^{2}}{\left\lVert U(t)\right\rVert^{2}}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{n\left\lVert X_{0}^{T}X_{0}\right\rVert^{2}}{\left\lVert X_{0}\right\rVert^{4}}-1\right]

(602)

\displaystyle=n\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{\left\lVert X_{0}^{T}X_{0}\right\rVert^{2}}{\left\lVert X_{0}\right\rVert^{4}}\right]-1

(603)

\displaystyle=n\cdot\frac{n+r+1}{nr+2}-1

(604)

\displaystyle=\frac{n^{2}+n-2}{nr+2}

(605)

From our expectation for the square of the final relative error, we can use Jensen’s inequality to find an upper bound on the expectation of the final relative error itself. Define

\displaystyle R^{2}:=\lim_{t\to\infty}\frac{\left\lVert Y(t)X(t)-U(t)\right\rVert^{2}}{\left\lVert U(t)\right\rVert^{2}}.

(606)

Note that
R^{2}\geq 0
and
\mathbb{E}[R^{2}]
is finite by (
605
). Recall that the square root function
\phi(x)=\sqrt{x}
is concave on
[0,\infty)
. By Jensen’s inequality
(
Dekking et al. 2005
)
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\sqrt{R^{2}}\right]\leq\sqrt{\mathbb{E}\left[R^{2}\right]}

(607)

Combining this with (
605
), we arrive at

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\lim_{t\to\infty}\frac{\left\lVert Y(t)X(t)-U(t)\right\rVert}{\left\lVert U(t)\right\rVert}\right]\leq\sqrt{\frac{n^{2}+n-2}{nr+2}}

(608)

Appendix H
Vanishing Fourth-Order Moment in Appendix
G

Consider the random vector
\mathbf{z}\in\mathbb{R}^{nr}
which is uniformly distributed on the unit sphere in
\mathbb{R}^{nr}
. Let

S^{nr-1}=\{\mathbf{v}\in\mathbb{R}^{nr}:\|\mathbf{v}\|_{2}=1\}

denote the unit sphere, and let
|S^{nr-1}|
denote its surface area. Throughout this section, integrals over
S^{nr-1}
are taken with respect to its surface measure
dS
.

The uniform distribution on
S^{nr-1}
is then given by the probability measure

d\mathbb{P}(\mathbf{z})=\frac{1}{|S^{nr-1}|}\,dS(\mathbf{z}),

which is normalized since

\frac{1}{|S^{nr-1}|}\int_{S^{nr-1}}dS(\mathbf{z})=1.

Let
z_{i},z_{j},z_{k},z_{l}
denote any four distinct scalar entries of the random vector
\mathbf{z}
. We wish to show that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[z_{i}z_{j}z_{k}z_{l}\right]

\displaystyle=0

(609)

We can calculate (
609
) directly by integrating over
S^{nr-1}
. We have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[z_{i}z_{j}z_{k}z_{l}\right]

\displaystyle=\int_{S^{nr-1}}z_{i}z_{j}z_{k}z_{l}\,d\mathbb{P}(\mathbf{z})

(610)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{|S^{nr-1}|}\int_{S^{nr-1}}z_{i}z_{j}z_{k}z_{l}\,dS(\mathbf{z})

(611)

Note that the unit sphere
S^{nr-1}
is invariant under coordinate sign flips. In particular, let
T:\mathbb{R}^{nr}\to\mathbb{R}^{nr}
denote the orthogonal transformation that flips the sign of the
i
th coordinate and leaves all others unchanged. Then
T(S^{nr-1})=S^{nr-1}
, and the surface measure
dS(\mathbf{z})
is invariant under
T
. Under this transformation,

z_{i}z_{j}z_{k}z_{l}\;\mapsto\;(-z_{i})z_{j}z_{k}z_{l}=-\,z_{i}z_{j}z_{k}z_{l}.

Therefore,

\int_{S^{nr-1}}z_{i}z_{j}z_{k}z_{l}\,dS(\mathbf{z})=-\int_{S^{nr-1}}z_{i}z_{j}z_{k}z_{l}\,dS(\mathbf{z}),

which implies that the integral vanishes.

Appendix I
Learning Dynamics for Low-rank Matrix Approximation

Let
W_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}
be a matrix of frozen pretraining weights. We wish to analyze the learning dynamics of the low-rank optimizer
BA\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}
produced by applying LoRA to the finetuning problem

\displaystyle\underset{\begin{subarray}{c}B\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times r}\\
A\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times m}\end{subarray}}{\min}\,\frac{1}{2}\left\lVert W_{0}-BA\right\rVert^{2}

(612)

where
r<<\min(n,m)
. The partial gradients for our objective
h(B,A)=\frac{1}{2}\left\lVert W_{0}-BA\right\rVert^{2}
are given by

\displaystyle\nabla_{A}h(B,A)

\displaystyle=-B^{T}(W_{0}-BA)

(613)

\displaystyle\nabla_{B}h(B,A)

\displaystyle=-(W_{0}-BA)A^{T}

(614)

Assumption I.1

(Bounded Domain)

We optimize (
612
) over the subspace
\mathcal{D}_{R^{\prime}}\subseteq\Theta
, where
R^{\prime}>0
is some finite number. In other words, we assume that there exists some
R^{\prime}>0
such that the norms on both
B
and
A
remain bounded above by
R^{\prime}
during training.

In practical settings, Assumption 1.1 is automatically enforced by computational memory constraints (e.g., finite-precision arithmetic and fixed-parameter storage). The assumption above ensures that the objective gradient remains bounded during training:

Remark I.2

(Boundedness of Gradient for Squared Frobenius Loss)

The objective gradient
\nabla h(B,A)
remains bounded above during training. That is, for all
B,A\in\mathcal{D}_{R^{\prime}}
, we have

\displaystyle\left\lVert\nabla h(B,A)\right\rVert^{2}

\displaystyle=\left\lVert\nabla_{B}h(B,A)\right\rVert^{2}+\left\lVert\nabla_{A}h(B,A)\right\rVert^{2}

(615)

\displaystyle=\left\lVert(W_{0}-BA)A^{T}\right\rVert^{2}+\left\lVert B^{T}(W_{0}-BA)\right\rVert^{2}

(616)

\displaystyle\leq\left\lVert W_{0}-BA\right\rVert^{2}\left\lVert A\right\rVert^{2}+\left\lVert W_{0}-BA\right\rVert^{2}\left\lVert B\right\rVert^{2}

(617)

\displaystyle=\left\lVert W_{0}-BA\right\rVert^{2}\Big(\left\lVert A\right\rVert^{2}+\left\lVert B\right\rVert^{2}\Big)

(618)

\displaystyle\leq 2R^{\prime 2}\left\lVert W_{0}-BA\right\rVert^{2}

(619)

\displaystyle\leq 2R^{\prime 2}\left(\left\lVert W_{0}\right\rVert+\left\lVert BA\right\rVert\right)^{2}

(620)

\displaystyle\leq 2R^{\prime 2}\left(\left\lVert W_{0}\right\rVert+\left\lVert B\right\rVert\left\lVert A\right\rVert\right)^{2}

(621)

\displaystyle\leq 2R^{\prime 2}\left(\left\lVert W_{0}\right\rVert+R^{\prime 2}\right)^{2}

(622)

So
\left\lVert\nabla h(B,A)\right\rVert\leq\sqrt{2}R^{\prime}\left(\left\lVert W_{0}\right\rVert+R^{\prime 2}\right)
throughout training.

We then have Lipschitz smoothness during training:

Lemma I.3

(Lipschitz Smoothness for Squared Frobenius Objective)

Our objective gradient
\nabla h
is Lipschitz smooth in our training domain. Namely, there exists
L_{R^{\prime}}>0
such that, for any
(B_{1},A_{1}),(B_{2},A_{2})\in\mathcal{D}_{R^{\prime}}
, we have

\displaystyle\left\lVert\nabla h(B_{1},A_{1})-\nabla h(B_{2},A_{2})\right\rVert\leq L_{R^{\prime}}\left\lVert\Big(B_{1},A_{1}\Big)-\Big(B_{2},A_{2}\Big)\right\rVert

(623)

Proof of this lemma can be found in Appendix
J
.

Having shown that the squared Frobenius objective in (
612
) satisfies Assumptions
A.2
,
A.4
, and
A.7
from Appendix
A
, the ODEs describing the learning dynamics of
h(B,A)
under LoRA are given by (see appendix
A
)

\displaystyle\frac{dY(t)}{dt}

\displaystyle=\left(W_{0}-YX\right)X^{T}

(624)

\displaystyle\frac{dX(t)}{dt}

\displaystyle=Y^{T}\left(W_{0}-YX\right)

(625)

for any
t\in[0,T]
, where
T>0
is arbitrary.

Unlike the trace-squared objective in Appendix
C
, we are not aware of a closed-form solution to the ODE above under conventional element-wise initialization, i.e., when all
rm
entries of
X_{0}
are drawn independently from a normal distribution. We will show, however, that the solution to (
624
–
625
) converges to the theoretical rank-
r
minimizer
(
Eckart & Young 1936
)
of (
612
) when utilizing the spectral initialization schema used in previous work
(
Xu et al. 2025
)
.

Recall that
W_{0}
is a known matrix of prefrozen weights. We begin by assuming that the pretrained weight matrix
W_{0}
has rank strictly greater than
r
:

Assumption I.4

(Rank of
W_{0}
)

Let
k=\rank(W_{0})
. We assume that

\displaystyle r<k\leq\min(n,m).

(626)

Note that violation of Assumption
I.4
renders (
612
) outside the scope of LoRA.

Denote the singular value decomposition (SVD) of
W_{0}
by

\displaystyle W_{0}=U\Sigma_{0}V^{T},

(627)

where
\Sigma_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}
is the rectangular diagonal matrix whose main diagonal entries are the singular values of
W_{0}
(in non-increasing order). Here,
U\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
and

are the left and right singular vector matrices of

, respectively. Recall that
U
and

are orthogonal.

For spectral initialization of
X_{0}
and
Y_{0}
, define the rotated variables

Appendix J
Proof of Lemma
I.3

Lemma J.1

(Lipschitz Smoothness Squared Frobenius Objective)

Define
h:\mathbb{R}^{n\times r}\times\mathbb{R}^{r\times m}\to\mathbb{R}
via

\displaystyle h(B,A)

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{2}\left\lVert W_{0}-BA\right\rVert^{2}

(702)

for constant
W_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}
. Then the objective gradient
\nabla h
is Lipschitz smooth in our training domain. Namely, there exists
L_{R^{\prime}}>0
such that, for any
(B_{1},A_{1}),(B_{2},A_{2})\in\mathcal{D}_{R^{\prime}}
, we have

\displaystyle\left\lVert\nabla h(B_{1},A_{1})-\nabla h(B_{2},A_{2})\right\rVert\leq L_{R^{\prime}}\left\lVert\Big(B_{1},A_{1}\Big)-\Big(B_{2},A_{2}\Big)\right\rVert

(703)

Proof:

Let
(B_{1},A_{1}),(B_{2},A_{2})\in\mathcal{D}_{R^{\prime}}
be given. Refer to the partial gradients for
h(B,A)
in (
613
–
614
) of Appendix
I
. We have

\displaystyle\left\lVert\nabla h(B_{1},A_{1})-\nabla h(B_{2},A_{2})\right\rVert

\displaystyle=\left\lVert\Bigg(\nabla_{B}h(B_{1},A_{1})-\nabla_{B}h(B_{2},A_{2})\,,\,\nabla_{A}h(B_{1},A_{1})-\nabla_{A}h(B_{2},A_{2})\Bigg)\right\rVert

(704)

The partial gradients with respect to
B
simplify to

\displaystyle\nabla_{B}h(B_{1},A_{1})-\nabla_{B}h(B_{2},A_{2})

\displaystyle=-(W_{0}-B_{1}A_{1})A_{1}^{T}+(W_{0}-B_{2}A_{2})A_{2}^{T}

(705)

\displaystyle=(W_{0}-B_{2}A_{2})A_{2}^{T}-(W_{0}-B_{1}A_{1})A_{2}^{T}+(W_{0}-B_{1}A_{1})A_{2}^{T}-(W_{0}-B_{1}A_{1})A_{1}^{T}

(706)

\displaystyle=\Big((W_{0}-B_{2}A_{2})-(W_{0}-B_{1}A_{1})\Big)A_{2}^{T}+\left(W_{0}-B_{1}A_{1}\right)\left(A_{2}^{T}-A_{1}^{T}\right)

(707)

\displaystyle=\left(B_{1}A_{1}-B_{2}A_{2}\right)A_{2}^{T}+\left(W_{0}-B_{1}A_{1}\right)\left(A_{2}^{T}-A_{1}^{T}\right)

(708)

Taking the norm, we find

\displaystyle\left\lVert\nabla_{B}h(B_{1},A_{1})-\nabla_{B}h(B_{2},A_{2})\right\rVert

\displaystyle=\left\lVert\left(B_{1}A_{1}-B_{2}A_{2}\right)A_{2}^{T}+\left(W_{0}-B_{1}A_{1}\right)\left(A_{2}^{T}-A_{1}^{T}\right)\right\rVert

(709)

\displaystyle\leq\left\lVert\left(B_{1}A_{1}-B_{2}A_{2}\right)A_{2}^{T}\right\rVert+\left\lVert\left(W_{0}-B_{1}A_{1}\right)\left(A_{2}^{T}-A_{1}^{T}\right)\right\rVert

(710)

\displaystyle\leq\left\lVert\left(B_{1}A_{1}-B_{2}A_{2}\right)\right\rVert\left\lVert A_{2}^{T}\right\rVert+\left\lVert\left(W_{0}-B_{1}A_{1}\right)\right\rVert\left\lVert\left(A_{2}^{T}-A_{1}^{T}\right)\right\rVert

(711)

\displaystyle\leq\left\lVert\left(B_{1}A_{1}-B_{2}A_{2}\right)\right\rVert R^{\prime}+\Big(\left\lVert W_{0}\right\rVert+\left\lVert B_{1}A_{1}\right\rVert\Big)\left\lVert\left(A_{2}^{T}-A_{1}^{T}\right)\right\rVert

(712)

\displaystyle\leq\left\lVert\left(B_{1}A_{1}-B_{2}A_{2}\right)\right\rVert R^{\prime}+\Big(\left\lVert W_{0}\right\rVert+\left\lVert B_{1}\right\rVert\left\lVert A_{1}\right\rVert\Big)\left\lVert\left(A_{2}^{T}-A_{1}^{T}\right)\right\rVert

(713)

\displaystyle\leq\left\lVert\left(B_{1}A_{1}-B_{2}A_{2}\right)\right\rVert R^{\prime}+\Big(\left\lVert W_{0}\right\rVert+R^{\prime 2}\Big)\left\lVert\left(A_{2}^{T}-A_{1}^{T}\right)\right\rVert

(714)

\displaystyle\leq\left\lVert\left(B_{1}A_{1}-B_{1}A_{2}+B_{1}A_{2}-B_{2}A_{2}\right)\right\rVert R^{\prime}+\Big(\left\lVert W_{0}\right\rVert+R^{\prime 2}\Big)\left\lVert\left(A_{2}^{T}-A_{1}^{T}\right)\right\rVert

(715)

\displaystyle\leq\left\lVert B_{1}(A_{1}-A_{2})+(B_{1}-B_{2})A_{2}\right\rVert R^{\prime}+\Big(\left\lVert W_{0}\right\rVert+R^{\prime 2}\Big)\left\lVert\left(A_{2}^{T}-A_{1}^{T}\right)\right\rVert

(716)

\displaystyle\leq\Big(\left\lVert B_{1}(A_{1}-A_{2})\right\rVert+\left\lVert(B_{1}-B_{2})A_{2}\right\rVert\Big)R^{\prime}+\Big(\left\lVert W_{0}\right\rVert+R^{\prime 2}\Big)\left\lVert\left(A_{2}^{T}-A_{1}^{T}\right)\right\rVert

(717)
