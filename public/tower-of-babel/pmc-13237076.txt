CFD investigation of PCM/PMMA enhanced CMUs with dual hollow sections for improved thermal performance in cold and freezing climates
Authors: Naif Albelwi, Mounir Ltifi, Omar J. Alkhatib, Pradeep Kumar Singh, Ibrahim Mahariq, Raouf Hassan, Yahya Rahmani, Mahidzal Dahari
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This study investigates the thermal performance of a new thermally smart functional brick (TSFB) filled with three paraffin-based phase change materials (PCMs)—pentadecane, hexadecane, and heptadecane— encapsulated Polymethyl methacrylate (PMMA) shells under cold and freezing climate conditions. A clay masonry unit (CMU) containing two hollow sections was numerically analyzed using a computational fluid dynamics (CFD) framework based on the finite volume (FVM) method, incorporating fully dynamic boundary conditions to capture realistic thermal performance. Four PCM volume fractions (0%, 3%, 6%, and 9%) were examined to evaluate their effects on indoor temperature (Tis), heat flux (qis), melting fraction (λ), and daily power consumption (DPC). The results show that incorporating different PCM types reduces the fluctuation of Tis compared with the no-PCM wall. For example, adding pentadecane at a 9% volume fraction decreases the Tis fluctuation from 9.03% to 6.58%. Heat-flux fluctuations decrease by up to 11%, and the specimens filled with pentadecane and hexadecane at ϕ = 9% exhibit the strongest heat-flux moderation. Additionally, incorporating pentadecane at ϕ = 9% yields the greatest energy benefit, reducing DPC by approximately 12–15%. Overall, low-melting PCMs demonstrate superior suitability for cold-climate building envelopes.

Introduction

Literature review

Energy use in buildings, mainly for heating and cooling, is a major source of greenhouse gas emissions because it relies heavily on fossil fuel, based electricity and direct combustion of natural gas. This continuous demand contributes to air pollution, climate change, and the depletion of non-renewable resources1–5. At the same time, inefficient buildings translate directly into higher operating costs, as more energy is wasted through poor insulation, outdated equipment, and uncontrolled ventilation. These higher energy bills place a financial burden on households, businesses, and public institutions, and can increase energy price fluctuations6–9. Together, the environmental damage and economic losses highlight the urgent need to improve building energy efficiency and accelerate the transition to cleaner energy sources. One practical way to reduce reliance on HVAC systems for active cooling and heating is to incorporate advanced materials and products that improve energy management in urban buildings10–15. Among these approaches, TSFB have recently attracted significant attention as a promising tool for enhancing energy efficiency. TSFB incorporating PCMs are designed to act as passive thermal buffers within building envelopes, cutting the need for active heating and cooling13,16–20. In these systems, PCMs embedded in cavities or porous structures of the brick absorb excess heat during the day by melting at a targeted transition temperature, and then release the stored heat when the ambient temperature drops and the material solidifies21–23. This melt–freeze cycle smooths out indoor temperature fluctuations, reduces peak thermal loads on HVAC systems, and can shift energy demand away from critical periods24–26. By carefully selecting the PCM type, melting temperature, and encapsulation method, these smart bricks can be tailored to specific climate conditions and building uses, offering a practical, scalable strategy to enhance energy efficiency and thermal comfort in urban buildings. Therefore, many studies have been conducted to investigate the performance of TSFB integrated with different types of PCMs under various environmental conditions, such as cold and hot climates27–33. Xi Meng34 proposed the dynamic rotating latent-energy-storage envelope (DRLESE), a novel PCM-wall concept that enables active indoor heating by rotating the PCM layer to capture solar gains and release heat indoors. Using an experiment-validated numerical heat-transfer model, DRLESE showed direct indoor heat release with inner heat flow around − 22 to − 80 W/m² compared with a static PCM wall. A follow-up study35 found that performance depends strongly on rotation timing, with optimal rotations tied to sol-air temperature conditions. Under optimized timings, indoor effective heat release reached 3182.9 kJ/day and inner-surface heat flow increased by 5.86–12.26%. Mahdaoui et al.36 proposed smart hollow bricks filled with PCM as a passive solution to improve the thermal inertia of lightweight Moroccan walls exposed to extreme summer swings (about 45 °C day and 25 °C night). Results show that incorporating PCM in the brick cavities markedly damps heat flow through the envelope, reducing and delaying indoor temperature peaks compared with conventional hollow bricks. The study37 numerically evaluated hollow brick walls with integrated PCMs across eight Mediterranean cities and three building types (single-family, multi-family, hotel) using an apparent heat capacity model. Its novelty lies in mapping PCM performance over a broad melting range (22–32 °C). Results show that, with a PCM melting at about 26 °C, cooling energy demand can be cut by up to 56% in North-East Mediterranean locations, while buildings in South-East cities gain essentially no savings. Ghafoorian et al.38 used CFD framework to compare a standard brick, a brick with one encapsulated PCM layer, and a brick with three PCM layers, explicitly targeting year-round thermal comfort and energy management. Simulations show that the three-layer PCM brick cuts peak indoor temperature swings by up to 4 °C in summer, while a single-layer design lets the wall hit peak temperatures about 2 h earlier and up to 5 °C hotter. The study39 on seven hollow brick wall setups showed that Thermal Insulation Material (TIM) mainly boosts thermal resistance, while PCM in inner cavities strengthens thermal inertia, cutting the decrement factor from 12 to 17% to 2%, increasing time lag from 1.5 to 2.0 h to 6.2–6.5 h, and lowering peak heat flux from 35 to 39 W/m² to 19–26 W/m². Nassr et al.40 numerically analyzed bricks containing three aluminum PCM capsules and finds they perform optimally when indoor temperatures are 20–24 °C and outdoor temperatures 25–31 °C, where they most effectively damp temperature swings over a 12 h cycle. Jellaj et al.41 numerically tested four wall setups for Marrakech’s semi-arid climate and shows that a hybrid wall with two PCMs (melting at 16 °C and 29 °C) cuts summer indoor temperature peaks by about 25%, boosts winter night comfort by 15%.

Although most research has been conducted in hot and warm climate regions, few studies have focused on cold and freezing climates1,42–45. For instance, Congxiang et al.46 used simulation approaches for a residential building in Jingzhou’s hot-summer/cold-winter climate and shows that embedding PCMs in the middle or inner wall layers, with phase change temperatures between 15 and 35 °C, can strongly enhance envelope thermal mass. With optimal settings, cooling demand drops by up to 46% at 20 °C, heating demand by up to 79% at 15 °C, and total HVAC energy use by up to 47%, reaching a minimum of about 15.12 kWh/m². Huang et al.47 experimentally compared PCM-filled and conventional hollow bricks in cold-climate conditions and shows that PCM bricks keep the inner surface on average 0.89 °C warmer, raise the internal temperature by about 4.14 °C with fluctuations under 1.5 °C, and cut inner-surface heat flux to 7.37 W/m²—7.71 W/m² lower than unfilled bricks. Y Huang et al.1 experimentally studied the thermal performance of PCM-filled and conventional hollow bricks in cold climates and finds that PCM bricks raise inner surface and midsection temperatures by about 0.99 °C and 3.85 °C, cut the interior–exterior surface temperature difference by 5.62 °C, and lower average inner-surface heat flow by 8.57 W/m².

However, further studies are still required to evaluate the performance of TSFBs embedded with different PCMs in cold and freezing climate regions where energy use can increase dramatically. In such contexts, maintaining indoor temperatures around 15 °C can be considered an acceptable and comfortable level, in contrast to most previous studies that typically target higher set-point temperatures. In this work, we analyze the performance of a CMU with two hollow sections filled with three different PCMs—pentadecane, hexadecane, and heptadecane—each encapsulated within a PMMA shell and possessing melting temperatures of 10, 18, and 22 °C, respectively, under cold and freezing conditions. To this end, we use a CFD framework based on the FVM to assess the effects of PCM type and volume fraction (0%, 3%, 6%, and 9%) on key parameters such as indoor temperature (Tis), indoor heat flux (qis), and their temporal variations. Unlike most earlier studies, we impose fully dynamic boundary conditions that closely mimic real outdoor–indoor fluctuations. Problem description and material characterization

Maintaining indoor temperatures in buildings located in cold and freezing climates is challenging (−15 °C \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\le$$\end{document}Tenv \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\le$$\end{document}15 °C). Peak energy demand during certain hours can strain the electrical grid in urban areas and significantly increase energy costs. Therefore, even a 2–3 °C increase in indoor temperature can be highly beneficial. In some regions, maintaining indoor temperatures around 15–18 °C can be an effective strategy for managing energy consumption. In such cases, pursuing higher comfort temperatures is unnecessary and only leads to increased costs and additional challenges. Hence, we introduced a CMU featuring two hollow sections along its thickness. As illustrated in Fig. 1, the height, width, and thickness of the CMU are denoted by H, W, and L, respectively, while the dimensions of the hollow sections are represented by w and h. The values of these geometric parameters are provided in Table 1. As mentioned in the previous section, this CMU was filled with three different PCMs—pentadecane, hexadecane, and heptadecane—encapsulated within PMMA shell at various volume fractions (0%, 3%, 6%, and 9%). Pentadecane, hexadecane, and heptadecane are paraffin-based PCMs with melting temperatures of approximately 9–11 °C, 17–19 °C, and 20–22 °C, respectively. These materials exhibit sharp and repeatable phase transitions, chemical stability, and minimal supercooling, making them reliable for cyclic thermal storage. Although their inherently low thermal conductivity limits heat-transfer rates, they provide effective temperature regulation within their respective operating ranges, and their non-corrosive, non-toxic nature makes them suitable for long-term integration into building envelopes and other thermal-management systems. Moreover, these materials are encapsulated within a PMMA shell. PMMA shells are used because they prevent liquid leakage during melting, provide strong chemical and mechanical stability, and enable long-term thermal cycling without degradation. PMMA also offers good compatibility with building materials, protects the PCMs from oxidation and contamination, and preserves structural integrity while still allowing effective heat transfer despite its relatively low thermal conductivity. Boundary conditions and fundamental equations

The accurate formulation of governing equations and boundary conditions is fundamental to any CFD simulation because it determines whether the numerical model truly represents the physical system. While the conservation equations of mass, momentum, and energy describe the underlying physics, the solution remains physically meaningless unless boundary conditions, such as inlet/outlet specifications, thermal loads, and wall interactions, are defined correctly. Proper boundary definition anchors the computation to real operating conditions, ensures a well-posed problem, and improves numerical stability and convergence, allowing CFD to provide reliable predictions of fluid flow, heat transfer, and phase-change behavior in engineering applications.

To improve the reliability of the numerical simulations, this study applies dynamic boundary conditions that more realistically capture the thermal interactions occurring between indoor and outdoor environments, a strategy largely overlooked in previous investigations. Equations and effective properties

To develop a numerical framework that is both computationally efficient and thermally representative, this study adopts a set of well-justified assumptions that preserve the dominant heat-transfer behavior of PCM/PMMA-enhanced CMUs. The principal thermophysical properties of the CMU body and cementitious matrix (ρ, k, and Cₚ) are assumed to be temperature-independent over the investigated range, a standard and widely used simplification that reduces model complexity with negligible impact on accuracy for building-scale analyses. In addition, the PCM–PMMA phase is considered to be uniformly dispersed within the CMU, which allows the composite response to be captured using effective-medium relations without explicitly resolving the microscale capsule geometry. Finally, since the PCM is microencapsulated and finely distributed, the difference in thermal conductivity between solid and liquid phases is less influential at the CMU scale than in bulk paraffin systems; accordingly, we represent the PCM/PMMA region using averaged solid–liquid thermophysical properties. Changes in volume during PCM phase transition are omitted because such variations are typically small and largely constrained by the encapsulating polymer shell, and the material is not designed to carry structural loads. Likewise, thermal deformation of the CMU is disregarded, because the analysis of mechanical behavior. Nevertheless, Under the quasi-static loading conditions typical of building envelopes, this local expansion is negligible relative to the overall CMU structure. These modeling choices align with established practices in thermal energy storage research and support a clear formulation of the transient heat-conduction problem through partial differential Eqs48,51–57.

Simulation approach

In CFD problems, computational modeling provides a powerful tool for examining heat-transfer mechanisms in advanced building materials and structures, especially when experimental testing is constrained by cost, duration, or environmental variability. Within this study, the FVM is employed to enforce energy conservation across the geometrically complex PCM/PMMA-enhanced TSFB, enabling detailed simulation of transient thermal behavior in a system that includes both material nonuniformity and phase-change effects. The governing fundamental energy equation is discretized and evaluated at every control volume to resolve the time-evolving temperature field throughout the structure. Temporal integration is performed using a first-order explicit Euler scheme, selected for its simplicity and stability at sufficiently small-time increments, while spatial terms in the Laplacian operator are treated with a second-order central difference approximation to maintain adequate numerical accuracy. This numerical formulation provides an effective compromise between computational cost and predictive fidelity, making it suitable for capturing the dynamic heat-storage and heat-release processes of PCM/PMMA under realistic operating conditions.

Mesh independency analysis

A mesh-independence study is a critical step in any CFD analysis because it verifies that the numerical results are not biased by the chosen grid resolution. As the mesh becomes finer, the solution should approach a stable value that reflects the underlying physics rather than artifacts of discretization. Without this verification, predicted temperatures, velocities, or heat fluxes may change simply because of mesh size, leading to unreliable or misleading conclusions. By systematically refining the grid and comparing key outputs, the analyst ensures that the simulation results are both accurate and robust, providing confidence that the numerical solution represents the true behavior of the system rather than numerical error. In this study, the optimal mesh size was determined by analyzing the maximum of Tis of the CMU filled with pentadecane PCM encapsulated in PMMA at a \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\phi$$\end{document} of 9%. As shown in Fig. 2, the change in Tis becomes negligible once the mesh exceeds approximately 120,000 elements. Therefore, a mesh size of 120,000 elements is sufficient to provide accurate results while minimizing computational cost.

Validation process

Validation analysis is a crucial step in any CFD study because it confirms whether the numerical model actually reflects real physical behavior rather than just producing mathematically convenient results. Without validation, CFD outcomes are nothing more than predictions with unknown reliability, regardless of how refined the mesh or advanced the turbulence model may be. By comparing simulated results against experimental measurements or well-established benchmark data, researchers can identify modeling errors, assess the accuracy of governing assumptions, and ensure that the computational setup captures the dominant physical mechanisms of the problem. A validated model provides confidence that subsequent parametric studies, optimizations, or design evaluations are grounded in reality and not driven by numerical artifacts. To assess the reliability of our numerical predictions, we compared the simulated indoor heat flux qis​ for the case with φ = 10% and t = 8 (hr) against the results reported in Ref48., ensuring that both studies used the same geometry and external conditions, including outdoor temperature and PCM volume fraction. Although the cited study used a modified embedded-PCM configuration and considered somewhat warmer ambient conditions, the comparison is still meaningful because the underlying heat-transfer behavior and boundary-condition framework are essentially comparable. The outdoor temperatures considered in this evaluation were 5, 10, 15, 20, and 25 °C. As shown in Fig. 3, our results exhibit good agreement with the referenced data, indicating that the numerical model provides a reliable representation of the heat-transfer behavior.

Results and discussions

Maintaining stable indoor temperatures and reducing energy consumption in cold and freezing climates are essential for improving thermal comfort in buildings. Contrary to the conventional belief that comfortable indoor temperatures must fall between 19 and 25 °C, many regions consider 15–18 °C acceptable, which can substantially reduce energy demand and associated costs. In this study, we developed a type of TSFB containing two hollow sections and filled it with three different PCMs at various volume fractions.

Based on Fig. 4a–c, Tis exhibits a sinusoidal trend, which reflects the realistic dynamic boundary conditions used in the simulation. For all cases, Tis initially decreases with a similar slope until it reaches its minimum value. For structures containing the same PCM type, increasing the PCM volume fraction raises this minimum temperature. This occurs because specimens with a higher PCM content can absorb and release more thermal energy, helping to maintain higher indoor temperatures during early-morning hours (5 A.M., t = 0 h) through approximately mid-afternoon (2–5 P.M., t = 9–12 h). After reaching the minimum, Tis increases as the outdoor temperature rises due to solar radiation. In all cases, the maximum Tis ranges from 16.8 °C to 17.2 °C and occurs between t = 16–21 h. As illustrated in Fig. 4, increasing the PCM volume fraction slightly reduces this maximum value, thereby limiting the overall temperature fluctuation. Following the peak, Tis decreases again with a similar slope across all cases. By comparing the Tis results for cases using the same PCM, it can be observed that before the inflection point (t = 10.5 h for pentadecane, t = 11.5 h for hexadecane, and t = 12 h for heptadecane), specimens with higher volume fractions (ϕ) consistently exhibit higher Tis at any given time. After the inflection point, however, increasing ϕ leads to lower Tis. For pentadecane- and hexadecane-filled structures, increasing the PCM volume fraction shifts the Tis curves toward later times. This behavior is not observed in the TSFB filled with heptadecane; increasing ϕ produces negligible change. In this case, the presence of PCM after t = 12 h has virtually no influence due to its relatively high melting temperature. Furthermore, when comparing different PCMs, the pentadecane-filled cases show higher Tis values before the inflection point and lower values afterward. Hexadecane produces the greatest rightward shift of the Tis curves, which can be advantageous for managing energy demand during peak consumption periods. To provide a clearer comparison, the daily variation of Tis is illustrated in Fig. 5, showing the difference between its maximum and minimum values over a 24-hour cycle. According to this figure, using pentadecane as the PCM yields the smallest Tis variation compared with the other PCMs at equivalent volume fractions. For example, at ϕ = 9%, the Tis variation for TSFB filled with pentadecane, hexadecane, and heptadecane is 6.58%, 7.53%, and 8.17%, respectively. Moreover, for each PCM type, increasing ϕ reduces the fluctuation of Tis, with the most pronounced effect observed for pentadecane and hexadecane. For instance, in the case of pentadecane, increasing ϕ from 0% to 9% decreases the Tis variation from 9.03% to 6.58%.

In addition to analysis of Tis results, investigating heat flux in buildings is especially important when evaluating PCM-enhanced structural units such as TSFBs, because heat flux determines how effectively these materials moderate indoor temperature under dynamic outdoor conditions. In PCM-filled systems, the rate of heat transfer controls when and how the material absorbs or releases latent heat, which directly affects the timing of indoor temperature peaks and valleys. By analyzing heat flux, it becomes possible to assess whether the PCM actually reduces thermal loads, delays heat transfer during peak hours, and stabilizes indoor temperatures more effectively than conventional masonry units. This information is essential for quantifying the real contribution of PCMs to energy savings, validating the thermal performance of TSFBs, and optimizing PCM type and volume fraction for buildings in cold or freezing climates.

According to Fig. 6a–c, which presents the qis curves over a 24-hour period, the heat-flux profiles exhibit a trend similar to that of Tis. Following a sinusoidal pattern, qis increases until it reaches its maximum value, occurring at t = 4–8 h for the pentadecane-filled cases and at approximately t = 6 h for the hexadecane- and heptadecane-filled cases. After reaching the peak, the curves decrease toward their minimum values, which are negative for all specimens except the pentadecane case at ϕ = 9%. The absence of negative heat flux in this particular case indicates that it absorbs sufficient energy to release heat at around t = 19 h. In the final stage of the cycle, qis gradually returns to its initial value. Another notable feature is the inflection point of the heat-flux curves, which occurs at t = 10.5 h, t = 11.15 h, and t = 12 h for specimens incorporating pentadecane, hexadecane, and heptadecane, respectively. By examining the qis results for specimens containing the same PCM, it becomes evident that before the inflection point, increasing the PCM volume fraction decreases the magnitude of qis, while after the inflection point, higher volume fractions lead to increased qis values. However, the influence of heptadecane volume fraction on qis is substantially smaller than that of the other PCMs. Its relatively high melting temperature limits its participation in the latent heat absorption and release cycle. Additionally, consistent with the behavior observed in the Tis curves, increasing ϕ shifts the qis curves toward later times, particularly in specimens containing pentadecane and hexadecane. For example, in the hexadecane-filled cases, the minimum qis values occur at t = 19 h for ϕ = 3%, t = 19.5 h for ϕ = 6%, and t = 20.5 h for ϕ = 9%. The variation of qis is presented in Fig. 7. As previously discussed, the smallest qis variation occurs in the specimen containing pentadecane at ϕ = 9%. Furthermore, for specimens incorporating pentadecane or hexadecane, increasing the PCM volume fraction consistently reduces the qis variation. For example, in the pentadecane-filled structures, the qis variation decreases from 110.3% at ϕ = 0% to 107.3% at ϕ = 3%, 103.4% at ϕ = 6%, and 98.56% at ϕ = 9%. In contrast, adding heptadecane has no meaningful effect on this parameter for any of the tested volume fractions.

Evaluating the PCM melting fraction is crucial because it reveals whether the material is genuinely activated for latent-heat storage or is largely inactive and contributing only marginally to thermal control. The melting fraction indicates the portion of PCM undergoing phase change at a given time, making it possible to identify when the PCM is engaged, how long it remains active, and the extent of heat absorption and release. Without this metric, the performance of a PCM-enhanced TSFB cannot be assessed reliably, different PCMs cannot be compared objectively, and the PCM volume fraction cannot be optimized. Figure 8a–c presents the λ of the three PCMs over a 24-hour cycle, illustrating their distinctly different thermal behaviors. For specimens containing pentadecane, λ initially decreases as Tis drops (Fig. 4a), indicating that the PCM undergoes solidification. The melting fraction then remains constant until approximately t = 8 h, during which no phase change occurs. As Tis increases, pentadecane, owing to its low melting temperature, rapidly transitions from solid to liquid, and λ reaches 100%. Once the PCM is fully melted, the curves show that the available PCM volume is insufficient to absorb additional heat, despite this plateau occurring over a relatively short time interval; therefore, a larger pentadecane volume would be required for sustained thermal buffering. After t = 18 h, the PCM begins to solidify again, releasing heat back into the indoor space. In contrast, the hexadecane- and heptadecane-filled specimens exhibit markedly different behavior. At the beginning of the cycle, λ remains nearly constant at approximately 5% for hexadecane and 0% for heptadecane. Between t = 8–12 h, the melting fractions increase sharply and reach maximum values of approximately 61% for hexadecane and only 19% for heptadecane. This large difference is attributed to the higher melting temperature of heptadecane, which prevents it from actively participating in the heat-absorption and release process. Nevertheless, unlike pentadecane, the volumes of hexadecane and heptadecane used in this study are sufficient and do not reach a fully melted state.

Overall, the results confirm that selecting a PCM with a melting temperature compatible with the building’s thermal cycle is critical for maximizing energy savings in TSFB systems.

Conclusion

This study presented a comprehensive numerical investigation of a TSFB incorporating three paraffin-based PCMs—pentadecane, hexadecane, and heptadecane—encapsulated in PMMA shells and embedded in a CMU with two hollow sections. The novelty of this work lies in evaluating PCM-enhanced CMU specifically under cold and freezing climatic conditions using fully dynamic boundary conditions, a feature rarely considered in previous studies focused primarily on hot climates. A CFD framework based on the finite volume method was developed to resolve transient heat conduction and phase-change phenomena while examining ϕ = 0%, 3%, 6%, and 9%. The data demonstrate that low-melting PCMs substantially enhance the thermal stability of building envelopes in cold climates. To be more specific, the results indicate that using different PCM types lowers Tis fluctuations relative to the wall without PCM. For instance, introducing pentadecane at a 9% volume fraction reduces the Tis fluctuation from 9.03% to 6.58%. Heat-flux analyses revealed sinusoidal transfer behavior synchronized with outdoor fluctuations, and heat-flux variation decreased by up to 11%, with pentadecane and hexadecane at ϕ = 9% showing the strongest moderation. Melting-fraction results highlighted distinct thermal responses: pentadecane fully melted and solidified each cycle, hexadecane reached a maximum λ of 61%, and heptadecane only 19%, confirming limited activation due to its higher melting temperature. On the other hand, DPC further reinforced these observations, showing reductions of approximately 12–15% for pentadecane and moderate improvements for hexadecane, while heptadecane yielded minimal gains. Overall, the findings confirm that selecting PCMs with melting temperatures aligned to the thermal regime of cold-climate buildings is critical for maximizing latent-heat benefits. Pentadecane at higher volume fractions provides the most effective strategy for enhancing indoor thermal stability and reducing heating energy demand, demonstrating the strong potential of PCM-integrated TSFBs as passive energy-saving components in cold-region building envelopes. Moreover, a comprehensive techno-economic assessment, including manufacturing cost, energy savings, and payback period under region-specific conditions, should be conducted in future work to further evaluate the practical feasibility of the proposed PCM/PMMA-enhanced CMU concept.
