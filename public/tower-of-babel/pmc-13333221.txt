Evaluating Multimodal Large Language Models in Neuroradiology: Methodological Considerations
Authors: Li Chen, Peipei Zhang
Published: 2026

We read with interest the article by Suh et al. [1], “Evaluating the Accuracy and Diagnostic Reasoning of Multimodal Large Language Models in Interpreting Neuroradiology Cases From RadioGraphics.” The authors are to be commended for addressing a highly relevant and rapidly evolving topic through a thoughtfully designed study. In particular, the use of 401 neuroradiology and head-and-neck quiz cases derived from RadioGraphics, together with the evaluation of not only diagnostic accuracy but also lesion localization, imaging findings, hallucinations, and overall acceptability, provides a more clinically meaningful framework than simple answer matching alone.

We would like to raise several points that may help to further contextualize the findings. First, although the authors appropriately highlight the educational value of RadioGraphics-derived cases, the claim that these cases ensure independence from model training data may warrant more cautious phrasing. Because the source figures and legends were drawn from published educational material that had been accessible online for varying periods before model testing, the possibility of partial exposure cannot be fully excluded. In this context, the benchmark may be better regarded as a lower-leakage dataset rather than one definitively free of contamination. Future work would be strengthened by prospectively assembled, institution-specific cases or by post-cutoff datasets with documented access control [2].

Second, the analysis of the presence or absence of clinical history is particularly interesting; however, the current design may not fully isolate the effect of textual information. Cases without clinical history were not experimentally generated by removing history from all cases; rather, they were cases in which no usable history remained after editing the original legends. Consequently, these cases may differ systematically from those in which clinical history was retained in terms of intrinsic difficulty, ambiguity, or reliance on imaging alone. A paired, within-case design, in which the same case is assessed both with and without clinical history, would more directly quantify the incremental value of text input and provide stronger support for the conclusion that current multimodal large language models may overrely on textual information [3].

Third, the head-to-head comparison across models should be interpreted with caution, as the systems were tested during different time windows. Given the rapid evolution of proprietary multimodal models, asynchronous evaluation may introduce variability that is difficult to distinguish from true architectural differences. This issue is particularly relevant because repeatability was not assessed a priori and, therefore, could not be examined retrospectively. Repeated testing with locked model versions, synchronized evaluation periods, and explicit reporting of run-to-run variability would improve reproducibility and strengthen future benchmarking studies.

Fourth, rationale analysis was performed only for GPT-4V at a temperature of 1 because it achieved the highest top-3 accuracy. However, GPT-4o demonstrated very similar top-3 performance and the highest top-1 accuracy. Because first-rank correctness may be more clinically relevant than inclusion of the correct diagnosis anywhere within the top three, it would be informative to determine whether the reasoning profile of GPT-4o differs from that of GPT-4V with respect to lesion localization, description of imaging findings, or hallucination patterns. More broadly, comparisons with radiology-specific multimodal systems, as well as with readers at different levels of experience beyond neuroradiology subspecialists, may provide a more balanced view of where general-purpose multimodal models currently stand in clinically realistic settings [4].

Despite these considerations, the study makes an important contribution. The authors move the discussion beyond whether a model can generate a plausible differential diagnosis and instead examine whether that diagnosis is grounded in faithful image interpretation. This distinction is important. The findings remind readers that apparently correct outputs may still reflect incomplete or unreliable visual reasoning, particularly when clinical context is limited. This message is highly relevant to both clinical governance and future model development.

We thank the authors for this timely and carefully conducted study and hope that these comments help further advance the framework for evaluating multimodal artificial intelligence in radiology.
