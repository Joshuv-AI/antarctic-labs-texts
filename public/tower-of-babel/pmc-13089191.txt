A Nationwide Study of Pyruvate Dehydrogenase Complex Deficiency in Sweden
Authors: Antri Savvidou, Kalliopi Sofou, Sofia Thunström, Sofia Ygberg, Erik A. Eklund, Karin Naess, Gittan Kollberg, Niklas Darin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background and ObjectivesPyruvate dehydrogenase complex (PDHc) deficiency is a potentially treatable neurodegenerative genetic disorder. It represents a common cause of mitochondrial disease. Most published reports are limited to single cases, and population-based data are lacking. The objective of this study was to investigate the prevalence, incidence, and life expectancy and to explore genotype–phenotype correlations, clinical onset, and disease course.MethodsWe conducted a nationwide, population-based epidemiologic cohort study with retrospective, longitudinal, and cross-sectional components. The cohort included all individuals residing in Sweden who were diagnosed between 2003 and 2022.ResultsA total of 54 patients (35 female patients) were identified, corresponding to a birth prevalence of 2.43 per 100,000 live births (95% CI 1.86–3.17) and a point prevalence of 0.44 per 100,000 population (95% CI 0.40–0.65). Eight patients (15%) died during the study period. The primary causes of death were congenital lactic acidosis (n = 4), stroke (n = 2), and infection (n = 2). We identified 35 pathogenic variants, including 11 not previously reported. X-linked PDHA1-related disease was the most common subtype (n = 44; 30 female patients), accounting for 81% of patients. Prenatal onset occurred in 20 female and 2 male patients. All but 1 affected female survived (97%), whereas more than 40% of affected male patients died (log-rank p < 0.001). Severe frameshift variants were detected in 23% of female patients but were absent in male patients. The clinical presentation was heterogeneous. Facial dysmorphism occurred in 76% of patients, polyneuropathy in 54%, stroke-like episodes or lesions in 15%, and perinatal leukoencephalopathy in 11%. CSF lactate was elevated in all 23 patients who underwent lumbar puncture. Mitochondrial functional studies using polarography or ATP production rate assessment in 34 individuals revealed a reduced pyruvate + malate/glutamate + malate oxidation ratio in all but 1 patient.DiscussionThis nationwide, population-based study reports on the occurrence and survival of PDHc deficiency. We demonstrate genotype–sex–phenotype differences in PDHA1-related disease and describe a wider spectrum of clinical features than previously recognized. The broader detection likely reflects the study's population-based and cross-sectional design. Furthermore, we show that the discrepancy between pyruvate and glutamate oxidation in muscle mitochondrial investigations may serve as a diagnostic clue.

Introduction

The pyruvate dehydrogenase complex (PDHc) is a multi-subunit enzyme complex composed of 3 core catalytic components (E1–E3) that requires 5 essential cofactors (thiamine pyrophosphate, lipoate, coenzyme A, FAD, and NAD+). It catalyzes the oxidative decarboxylation of pyruvate, the end product of glycolysis, to acetyl-CoA which subsequently enters the Krebs cycle and enables aerobic energy production.1,2 PDHc deficiency is among the most common causes of mitochondrial disease and a frequent cause of primary lactic acidosis in children.3

The deficiency results in a primary neurodegenerative disorder where the clinical spectrum is broad, ranging from prenatal onset characterized by neurodevelopmental and/or encephaloclastic brain lesions, intrauterine growth restriction, congenital microcephaly, and facial dysmorphism to a neonatal presentation with muscular hypotonia and encephalopathy accompanied by lactic acidosis or acidemia, to early childhood onset with Leigh syndrome, or later manifestations including episodic ataxia or dystonia, stroke-like episodes, and polyneuropathy.4-8 Facial dysmorphism has also been reported in some cases.8,9 Early detection is crucial to enable timely initiation of treatments, including ketogenic diet and thiamine supplementation.10-13

The genetic basis of primary PDHc deficiency is heterogeneous, with approximately 80% of cases caused by pathogenic variants in the X-linked PDHA1 gene, which encodes the E1α subunit of the first component of the complex.4,5,14 Other known genetic causes include pathogenic variants in PDHB (encoding the E1b subunit), PDHX (encoding the E3 binding protein), DLAT (encoding the E2 subunit), and PDP1 (encoding the catalytic subunit of pyruvate dehydrogenase phosphatase). Pathogenic variants in DLD, which encodes dihydrolipoamide dehydrogenase, affect not only PDHc but also other mitochondrial keto-acid dehydrogenases, resulting in a broader clinical phenotype that may include hepatic and/or myopathic features.15-17

Most published reports of PDHc deficiency are limited to single cases or exploratory case series, and very few studies have attempted to characterize the full clinical phenotype in larger patient cohorts or to assess the disease's clinical course. Population-based data are lacking, and the true birth and point prevalence's, and long-term outcomes of the disease remain uncertain. Based on registry data for mitochondrial diseases in North America, the estimated birth prevalence of PDHc deficiency ranges from 1 in 50,000 to 1 in 75,000 live births.18 In addition, a newborn screening study in Ohio, using amino acid ratio analysis, identified 3 patients with PDHc deficiency among 123,414 live births.19

The aim of this study was to (1) investigate the prevalence, incidence, and life expectancy of PDHc deficiency in Sweden; (2) identify potential genotype–phenotype correlations; and (3) explore the clinical onset and disease course.

Methods

Study Population

This was a retrospective, cross-sectional, nationwide study including all patients residing in Sweden who were diagnosed with primary PDHc deficiency between January 1, 2003, and December 31, 2022. Inclusion criteria were:Phenotypic features consistent with PDHc deficiency, such as prenatal developmental or clastic brain lesions, neonatal lactic acidosis, Leigh syndrome, episodic dystonia or ataxia, stroke-like episodes, or polyneuropathy; andThe presence of either (1) known pathogenic variant(s) in a gene known to cause primary PDHc deficiency (PDHA1, PDHB, DLAT, DLD, PDP1 or PDHX) or (2) variant(s) not previously reported in the literature in the same genes, predicted to be deleterious, in combination with biochemical features suggestive of PDHc deficiency, that is, elevated lactate and/or pyruvate in blood or CSF, a low lactate-to-pyruvate ratio in blood and/or CSF, or reduced PDHc activity in blood thrombocytes.

Patients with secondary PDHc deficiency because of other underlying conditions were excluded from the study.

Patients were identified through multiple national resources, including registries at the 3 national clinical centers for inherited metabolic diseases, the 2 national laboratories responsible for the biochemical diagnostics of inherited metabolic diseases, and 6 national genetic laboratories. In addition, the “National Cause of Death Register” was reviewed for the period 1987–2022. Letters were also sent to pediatric and neurology departments across the country yielding a response rate of more than 90%. The search was conducted using the International Classification of Diseases (ICD) codes, ICD-9 code 2718 and ICD-10 code E74.4. For the cross-sectional component of the study, patients and/or their caregivers were contacted by their treating physician with information about the study and were included after informed consent.

Study Design

The study used a population-based, epidemiologic cohort design, with retrospective, longitudinal, and cross-sectional components (Figure 1).

Data Collection

Retrospective data were collected between December 2019 and December 2022 using a standardized clinical report form (CRF) that captured demographic information (including sex and growth data), dysmorphic features, age at symptom onset and diagnosis, the symptom and signs progression, results from genetic, biochemical tests at diagnosis, as well as data on postnatal neuroradiology, treatment, social factors, education, and occupation.

Functional muscle mitochondrial studies were performed, comprising polarography or ATP-production measurements, spectrophotometric analyses of respiratory chain enzymes, and determination of citrate synthase activity, as previously described.20,21

Data collection for the cross-sectional study was conducted between March and December 2022.

Population statistics were obtained from Statistics Sweden.22 On the prevalence date December 31, 2022, the total Swedish population was 10,549,347 with 2,222,000 live births recorded between 2003 and 2022.

A standardized CRF was also used in the cross-sectional study to gather historical data and clinical examination findings, including neurologic assessments, somatic features, growth parameters, dysmorphic features, and congenital malformations. Photographs of patients were taken and subsequently reviewed in consultation with a clinical geneticist specializing in dysmorphology. Dysmorphic features were classified according to the “Elements of Morphology Guidelines.”23

Terminology

Prenatal disease onset was defined based on the presence of developmental or clastic lesions on neuroimaging indicative of prenatal onset7 or by the presence of congenital microcephaly. Postnatal onset was defined as the appearance of clinical symptoms after birth, in the absence of radiologic or clinical features suggestive of prenatal onset.

Age period was defined as neonatal period (younger than 1 month), early infancy (1–12 months), childhood (13 months–12 years), adolescence (13–18 years), and adulthood (older than 18 years).

A stroke-like episode was defined as a paroxysmal neurologic event with abrupt onset and lasting more than 24 hours, characterized by altered consciousness (encephalopathy) and/or focal neurologic impairment, for example, hemiparesis, ataxia, visual impairment, or dysphasia. Stroke-like lesion is used when neuroradiology findings suggest stroke while stroke is used when the individual had a stroke-like episode confirmed by stroke-like lesions on neuroradiology.

Perinatal leukoencephalopathy was considered when MRI during the first month of life showed hyperintensities of the cerebral white matter in T2-weighted images with swelling.7

Peripheral neuropathy was considered in patients exhibiting suggestive symptoms and signs, for example, pes cavus, distal limb weakness, decreased tendon reflexes, or hypoesthesia with impaired vibration sense. It was delineated further based on neurophysiologic investigations. Microcephaly was defined as head circumference more than 2 SDs below the mean for age and sex.

Small for gestational age (SGA) was defined as birth weight below the 10th percentile for the gestational age.24

Major clinical manifestations were derived from a combination of the patient's medical history and the most recent clinical evaluation, based on either cross-sectional study assessments or the treating physician's latest examination.

Analyses

Statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS statistics version 29.0.2.0 and R version 4.5.2 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). Given the rarity of the disease and the limited number of events, all statistical analyses should be interpreted as exploratory.

Categorical variables are presented with numbers and percentages. 95% CIs for birth prevalence and point prevalence were calculated using Wilson scores intervals.

Birth prevalence (lifetime risk at birth) was defined as the number of individuals diagnosed with PDHc deficiency in the study population between 2003 and 2022, divided by the total number of live births during the same period, under the assumption that the rate of postnatal diagnosis is equal to the birth rate.25-28

Point prevalence was defined as the number of affected individuals alive and residing in Sweden on December 31, 2022, relative to the population at risk at that time, that is, the entire population.

Kaplan–Meier survival analysis was used to estimate age-specific survival probabilities and cumulative mortality, with follow-up from birth until death or censoring at the end of data capture on December 31, 2022. Because most diagnosed individuals were born in 1980 or later, when genetic diagnostics became available, cumulative mortality was evaluated over a 40-year age horizon. Survival curves were compared between male and female patients with PDHA1-related disease using the log-rank test.

Standard Protocol Approvals, Registrations, and Patient Consents

The study was approved by the Swedish Ethical Review Authority (#289-17, 2020–06408, 106-04, 2019-05101). Informed consent was obtained from the patients and/or their legal guardians before undertaking any study-related procedures.

Data Availability

The data supporting the findings of this study are available, anonymized, from the corresponding author to any qualified investigator on reasonable request.

Results

Birth Prevalence and Point Prevalence

A total of 54 patients (19 male and 35 female patients) were diagnosed between 2003 and 2022 (eFigure 1). The overall birth prevalence was 2.43 per 100,000 live births (95% CI 1.86–3.17). As of December 31, 2022, 46 patients were alive, corresponding to an overall point prevalence of 0.44 per 100,000 population (95% CI 0.40–0.65).

PDHA1-related disease (n = 44; 30 female patients) was the most common, with a birth prevalence of 1.98 per 100,000 live births (95% CI 1.48–2.66) and a point prevalence of 0.35 per 100,000 population (95% CI 0.32–0.55) accounting for 81% of all identified patients. The second most common genetic subtype was PDHX-related disease, identified in 5 individuals. DLAT-related and DLD-related diseases were each observed in 2 patients, whereas 1 patient had PDHB-related disease.

Clinical Features

Clinical presentation occurred in the neonatal period in 33 individuals, early infancy in 12 individuals, childhood in 7 individuals, and adolescence or adulthood in 1 individual each.

The predominant clinical features included congenital microcephaly (n = 7), congenital lactic acidosis (n = 13), global developmental delay/intellectual disability (n = 46), muscular hypotonia (n = 21), epilepsy (n = 22), spasticity (n = 25), ataxia (n = 13), dystonia (n = 13), Leigh syndrome (n = 13), episodic movement disorders (n = 16), polyneuropathy (n = 29), stroke or stroke-like episodes (n = 8), and perinatal leukoencephalopathy (n = 6).

Additional details of the major genetic and clinical findings for each individual are provided in eTables 1 and 2.

Craniofacial Dysmorphic Features

Facial photographs from 38 patients were analyzed for dysmorphic features. Assessment of the philtrum was possible in 30 individuals but limited in others because of the lack of suitable images, as a neutral facial expression is required for accurate evaluation. Craniofacial dysmorphic features were observed in 29 individuals (Table 1), with the most common findings being a bulbous nose (n = 25), thick eyebrows (n = 23), large ear lobes (n = 21), microcephaly (n = 21), smooth philtrum (n = 18), malpositioned teeth (n = 15), thin upper vermillion (n = 10), wide mouth (n = 10), and hypertelorism (n = 10). Short palpebral fissures were noted in 7 individuals (Figure 2).

Clinical and Radiologic Features in Individuals With Prenatal Disease Onset

PDHA1-related disease was the underlying cause in 22 of 26 individuals with prenatal onset, most of whom were female individuals (n = 20). Prenatal developmental brain lesions were identified in 18 individuals, including corpus callosum agenesis or dysgenesis (n = 17) and neuronal migration disorder (polymicrogyria n = 1, heterotopias n = 1). All but one of these individuals also exhibited lesions considered to be clastic, manifesting as ventriculomegaly (n = 15), intraventricular membranes (n = 11), periventricular cysts (n = 4), leukoencephalopathy (n = 3), and high signal intensity in the periventricular white matter (n = 4). In addition, 1 individual experienced a postnatal stroke during the first year of life, which on CT manifested as hypoattenuating abnormalities involving most of the right temporal lobe.

The predominant clinical features over the disease course in these female patients included global developmental delay/intellectual disability (n = 20), microcephaly (n = 14, congenital in 3), spasticity (n = 11), cavus feet (n = 10), epilepsy (n = 9), hypotonia (n = 9), dystonia (n = 7), and ataxia (n = 3). A stroke-like episode and exercise-induced dystonia were each observed in 1 individual. None of these participants exhibited Leigh-like episodes or lesions on clinical follow-up or brain MRI. The median age for the most recent examination was 1 year and 3 months (range 0.0–17.7 years; 95% CI 0.6–3.1 years).

One male patient with PDHA1-related disease presented with congenital microcephaly and lactic acidosis and died in early infancy, whereas another male patient with early infantile clinical presentation, exhibited motor developmental delay and intermittent ataxia, with signs of spasticity and dystonia at age 6 years.

One individual with PDHX-related disease had corpus callosum agenesis and another had abnormal gyration of the frontal lobes. Both were born SGA and later developed intellectual disability. Both siblings with DLAT-related disease had congenital microcephaly, and later developed intellectual disability and exercise-induced dystonia.

Clinical and Radiologic Features in Individuals With Postnatal Disease Onset

PDHA1-related disease was identified in 22 of 28 individuals with postnatal onset (female individuals, n = 10). Radiologic findings among these individuals included Leigh-like lesions (n = 11, globi pallidi; n = 11, putamina; n = 6, nuclei caudati; n = 8, thalami; n = 4, brainstem; n = 4, and female individuals, n = 4), stroke-like lesions (n = 5; female individuals, n = 3), leukoencephalopathy (n = 2; both male individuals), and normal MRI findings (n = 2).

Among the 10 female patients with PDHA1-related disease, the predominant clinical features over the disease course included global developmental delay and/or intellectual disability (n = 6), followed by intermittent movement disorder episodes (muscle weakness, ataxia, or polyneuropathy) (n = 5), stroke-like episodes (n = 4), spasticity (n = 4), and persistent ataxia (n = 3). Pes cavus was observed in 9 individuals, and acquired microcephaly was noted in 2 patients.

All male patients with neonatal onset, all of whom presented with congenital lactic acidosis, died during infancy or early childhood (n = 4). Among those with onset during infancy or early childhood (n = 8), the most common manifestations were intermittent movement disorders, including muscle weakness, ataxia, or polyneuropathy (n = 6), followed by intellectual disability (n = 5) and epilepsy (n = 2). Pes cavus or peripheral neuropathy was present in 6 individuals, and acquired microcephaly was documented in 2 patients.

Among patients with PDHX-related disease (n = 3), the predominant clinical features included global developmental delay and/or intellectual disability (n = 3), epilepsy (n = 3), spasticity (n = 3), and hypotonia (n = 2). Two brothers with DLD-related disease (n = 2), both presented with congenital lactic acidosis, global developmental delay and/or intellectual disability, epilepsy, spasticity, dystonia, and acquired microcephaly. The individual with PDHB-related disease exhibited intermittent muscle weakness, ataxia, and pes cavus.

Genetic Findings

Of the 54 individuals, 18 were members of 8 families. In total, 35 gene variants were identified in our cohort, of which 24 were previously reported as pathogenic. The remaining variants that were not previously reported (n = 11) were all predicted by in silico programs to be deleterious (Mutation Taster).29 One individual with a VUS was diagnosed prenatally based on MRI features, characteristic of PDHc deficiency, and subsequently developed congenital lactic acidosis. The diagnoses for the remaining 10 individuals were confirmed by further biochemical investigations, including muscle mitochondrial studies (eTable 3).

Genotype–Phenotype Correlations

In PDHA1-related disease, prenatal onset occurred predominantly in female patients (20 female vs 2 male patients), whereas postnatal onset was nearly evenly distributed between sexes (10 female vs 12 male patients). The identified pathogenic PDHA1 variants (n = 44) (Figure 3) were missense (n = 31, female patients = 22), frameshift (n = 7, female patients = 7), insertion/in-frame (n = 4, female patients = 1), or synonymous (n = 2, male patients = 2). Categorization of the gene variants revealed a different pattern between female and male patients, with frameshift variants present in 23% of female patients, all with prenatal onset, and absent in male patients (Figure 4).

Biochemical Investigations

Hyperlactatemia was present at clinical onset in 36 of 44 patients, with a median lactate concentration of 5.7 mmol/L (interquartile range [IQR] 3.3–10, normal p-lactate ≤2.0). Plasma pyruvate levels were measured in 15 individuals, showing a median of 253 mmol/L (IQR 176–555). Among 12 individuals with elevated lactate levels, the median plasma lactate/pyruvate ratio was 10.6 (IQR 8.7–12). In all 24 individuals examined, CSF lactate level was elevated with a median value of 4.4 mmol/L (IQR 2.9–5.4 mmol/L) (normal CSF-lactate ≤1.7).

PDHc enzyme activity was assessed in blood thrombocytes in 18 individuals (PDHA1: n = 15—female patients, n = 11; male patients, n = 4; PDHX: n = 3).33 In female patients with PDHA1-related disease, activity ranged from 34 to 814 pmol/min × mg protein (ref value 479–992 ± 2 SD), with a median of 374 (IQR 242–560); in 7 patients, values were below 60% of the mean value for healthy controls. In the 4 male patients with PDHA1-related disease, PDHc activity ranged from 4 to 72 pmol/min × mg protein, with a median level of 6.7 (IQR 5–39) (ref value 479–992 ± 2 SD), corresponding to 0.8%–9.8% of the mean activity in healthy controls. In the 3 individuals with PDHX-related disease, enzyme activity ranged from 128 to 345 pmol/min × mg protein.

Muscle mitochondrial analyses were performed in 28 individuals, including polarography20 (n = 12) or measurement of mitochondrial ATP production rate31 (n = 16) (eTable 4). Findings were normal in 1 patient, a female with PDHA1-related disease, whereas 27 patients showed reduced pyruvate + malate oxidation or ATP production compared with glutamate + malate (data not shown). On average, the oxidation or ATP production rate with pyruvate + malate decreased to a median of 33% (IQR 19.5%–52.0%), while the corresponding pyruvate + malate/glutamate + malate oxidation ratio was reduced to a median of 43.3% (IQR 26.0%–70.5%).

Eight patients died during the study period, including 7 with PDHA1-related disease (6 males) and 1 with DLD-related disease, as shown in Table 2. Four patients had been treated with KD, one of whom also received thiamine. The primary causes of death were congenital lactic acidosis with metabolic decompensation (n = 4), stroke (n = 2), and infection (n = 2). Discussion

We identified a birth prevalence (lifetime risk at birth) of 2.43 per 100,000 live births which was considerably higher than the point prevalence 0.44 per 100,000 population, probably reflecting the mortality and historical underdiagnosis associated with these disorders. Notably, our birth prevalence estimate aligns with that reported in an exploratory neonatal screening study conducted in Ohio.3 In our cohort, cumulative mortality by age 40 years was 15%, which is substantially lower than the 32%–39% reported in previous studies.4,5,9 This difference may reflect the identification of a greater proportion of patients with attenuated disease forms, who typically have longer survival. All patients who died, did so in early childhood. Among patients who died within the first weeks of life, lactic acidosis with metabolic decompensation was the most frequent cause of death, whereas stroke was the predominant cause among those who died later during infancy. We still believe our estimates likely represent an underestimation of the true occurrence in the population because some individuals may have died before receiving a diagnosis, may have been misclassified under alternative diagnoses such as atypical cerebral palsy, or may present with milder phenotypes that are less likely to be recognized and diagnosed.8 Given that PDHc deficiency is potentially treatable with thiamine supplementation and/or a ketogenic diet,10,11 improved recognition of the clinical features is critical to facilitate earlier and more accurate diagnosis.

In concordance with previous studies, we found PDHA1-related disease in 81% of the cohort. It is worth noting that 68% of these individuals were female individuals, a proportion considerably higher than the 30%–50% reported in earlier studies.4,5,9,14,34 The clinical phenotype also differed, with two-thirds of the female patients presenting with the prenatal-onset form, which was extremely rare in male patients, 86% of whom had postnatal presentation. The clinical course varied as well: All but 1 female patient survived, whereas more than 40% of the male patients had died. Leigh syndrome, the most common postnatal phenotype, was not observed among female patients with prenatal onset during the disease course.

The observed sex difference may be explained by the X-linked mode of inheritance. It is believed that only male fetuses carrying milder variants with substantial residual enzyme activity survive to birth, and these individuals typically do not present with severe structural brain anomalies.7,35-37 In female patients, random X-chromosome inactivation38,39 results in neuronal expression of either the mutant or the normal allele. While cells expressing the mutant allele are considered nonviable, and their loss has been suggested to contribute to developmental brain abnormalities, cells expressing the normal allele ensure fetal survival and may contribute to a more stable postnatal course. In support of this, we observed distinct patterns of gene variants between female and male patients. Frameshift variants, which are expected to cause more severe phenotypes, were identified exclusively in female patients with prenatal-onset disease. This observation suggests that truncating variants in hemizygous male patients may result in early embryonic lethality, potentially occurring before routine fetal imaging and consequently reducing the likelihood of prenatal detection of affected male fetuses.

Comparisons of the prevalence of clinical features with previous larger patient cohorts are challenging because of methodologic differences. We believe that the discrepancies observed may, at least in part, be explained by our population-based design and the active assessment of associated features in a cross-sectional evaluation.

Facial dysmorphism with FAS-like features was observed in 76% of patients in our cohort, a prevalence markedly higher than the 10%–33% reported in previous studies.9,40,41 This difference is likely attributable to our study design, in which we actively assessed for these features in a cross-sectional evaluation. We also noted a wider spectrum of dysmorphic features than previously described which we classified as mild with straight thick eyebrows, bulbous noses, smooth philtrum, and large ear lobe or severe with severe microcephaly, thick eyebrows, hypertelorism, bulbous nose, wide mouth, and tooth malposition. The milder phenotype would be an extension of the suggested FAS-like features in PDHc deficiency.5,40 Of note, 71% of individuals with postnatal clinical onset exhibited craniofacial dysmorphism, suggesting that a prenatal disease onset may also have occurred in these cases.

Spasticity was observed in 46% of individuals in our cohort, substantially higher than the 10%–30% previously reported. Similarly, signs of polyneuropathy were identified in more than 50% of participants, exceeding the 3%–40% reported in earlier studies. Leigh syndrome was characterized by involvement of the globi pallidi in all affected individuals, which represents a characteristic feature of PDHc deficiency, also reported in previous studies.7,42 In addition, we identified stroke-like episodes in 15% and perinatal leukoencephalopathy in 11% of patients, clinical features not described in the previous patient cohorts.4,5,9,14 Although these manifestations are well recognized in other mitochondrial disorders, they have only rarely been reported in association with PDHc deficiency outside of this cohort.7,8,43

While clinical suspicion across a broad spectrum of features, combined with NGS analysis, represents the 2 most important steps in establishing the diagnosis, the process remains challenging. Hyperlactatemia, although generally regarded as a biomarker of the disease, was absent in one-fifth of the individuals in our cohort. By contrast, CSF lactate analysis showed much higher sensitivity, with elevated levels detected in all investigated individuals. Moreover, tissue-based measurements of PDHc activity in female patients with PDHA1-related disease, the most common subgroup, may yield inconclusive results because of random X-inactivation.44 This results in a mosaic distribution of cells expressing either the mutant or the normal allele, leading to variable enzyme activity across tissues.6,45 Owing to the clinical overlap with mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation disorders, many patients underwent muscle mitochondrial diagnostic workup, including polarography or ATP production measurements. A valuable diagnostic clue in these analyses, rarely reported in the literature,33,46 was the detection of reduced oxidation or ATP production rates with pyruvate + malate compared with glutamate + malate, suggestive of PDHc deficiency. This pattern was observed in all investigated individuals in our study, except for 1 female individual with PDHA1-related disease.

This study presents a national, epidemiologic investigation of PDHc deficiency. Its primary strength lies in the comprehensive population-based design, including systematic patient identification through national registries and collaboration with clinic centers nationwide to ensure complete case ascertainment. To enhance diagnostic accuracy, data completeness, and consistency in phenotypic characterization, a cross-sectional component was also conducted. The limitation of this study was the relatively small sample size, which may limit the generalizability of the findings. Consequently, some of the observed correlations may be attributable to random variation rather than true underlying associations. Furthermore, treatment data were only described for deceased individuals.
