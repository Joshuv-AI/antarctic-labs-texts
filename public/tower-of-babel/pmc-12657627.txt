Joint Association of Depression and Sleep Duration With All‐Cause and Cardiovascular Mortality: A Cross‐Sectional Study With Inflammation Mediation Analysis
Authors: Yingjie Li, Xirui Wu, Jing Dong, Xiubo Jiang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground and AimsThe relationship between depression, sleep duration, and mortality outcomes remains incompletely elucidated. This study aimed to investigate the combined effects of depression and sleep duration on mortality risk, while also examining the potential mediating role of inflammatory markers in these associations.MethodsThe analysis included 41,346 adult participants from the US National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES). Mortality outcomes were determined through linkage with the National Death Index (NDI) records up to December 31, 2019. Multivariate Cox proportional hazards regression models were employed to assess the associations between depression, sleep duration, and both all‐cause and cardiovascular mortality. Additionally, the mediating effect of C‐reactive protein (CRP) was evaluated.ResultsDepression was independently associated with elevated risks of both all‐cause mortality (HR: 1.40, 95% CI: 1.12–1.74) and cardiovascular mortality (HR: 1.79, 95% CI: 1.21–2.65). Similarly, short sleep duration was independently linked to increased all‐cause mortality risk (HR: 1.19, 95% CI: 1.03–1.37). The combination of depression and abnormal sleep duration (either short or long) significantly amplified mortality risks, with long sleep duration showing particularly strong associations (all‐cause mortality: HR: 1.84, 95% CI: 1.19–2.85; cardiovascular mortality: HR: 2.28, 95% CI: 1.05–4.95). Mediation analysis suggested that CRP may partially explain the joint associations of depression and sleep duration with both all‐cause and cardiovascular mortality.ConclusionThe coexistence of depression and abnormal sleep duration is associated with significantly increased mortality risk, with CRP potentially playing a measurable mediating role in these associations.

Introduction

Depression is linked to a notable decrease in life expectancy, primarily because it significantly heightens susceptibility to serious health conditions such as cardiovascular disease [1], diabetes [2], metabolic syndrome [3] and cancer [4]. Most importantly, numerous studies indicate that depression, particularly major depressive disorder, is associated with increased mortality risks in patient samples [5, 6, 7], or among specific patient populations [8, 9]. However, limited research has been conducted on random population samples within community settings, creating a gap in knowledge regarding how these relationships present in broader community‐based populations.

Abnormal sleep duration, including insufficient sleep and excessive sleep, is not only a comorbidity of depression, but also a prodromal symptom that can predict the occurrence and outcome of depression [10]. Several meta‐analyses have explored the relationship between sleep duration and all‐cause or cause‐specific mortality [11, 12, 13]. Some studies suggest that both insufficient and excessive sleep are associated with a higher risk of death [11, 12]. However, other research reveals that specific insomnia symptoms, such as difficulty initiating or maintaining sleep and early morning awakenings, may not necessarily correlate with all‐cause or cardiovascular mortality [14]. These inconsistencies highlight the need for further investigation into the connection between sleep duration and mortality risk.

The inflammation hypothesis has been suggested as a potential pathway connecting sleep disturbances and depression [10, 15, 16]. Longitudinal studies and experimental sleep deprivation research have demonstrated that sleep disruptions lead to elevated inflammation levels [17]. Furthermore, inflammation is widely recognized as a critical factor in the development of depression. Antidepressant treatments have been shown to significantly lower peripheral inflammatory markers and enhance anti‐inflammatory responses [18]. Emerging evidence indicates that increased inflammatory markers play a partial mediating role in the link between sleep disturbances and depressive symptoms [19]. Furthermore, systemic inflammation is intricately connected to the pathogenesis of various conditions, including obesity, aging, and chronic diseases. For instance, C‐reactive protein (CRP), a well‐established, stable, and readily measurable indicator of systemic inflammation [20], has been observed to be elevated in obese individuals [21], older adults [22], and patients with chronic illnesses such as cardiovascular diseases [23]. Increased CRP levels have also been correlated with elevated mortality risks [23, 24]. However, the specific contribution of depression and sleep duration to all‐cause and cardiovascular mortality through inflammatory mechanisms in the general population remains poorly understood.

Although depression and sleep duration are both associated with mortality, it is unclear whether their effects on mortality interact or if inflammation mediates these relationships. To address this, we analyzed data from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) to assess the individual and joint impacts of depression and sleep duration on all‐cause and cardiovascular mortality, while also exploring CRP's potential mediating role.

Materials and Methods

Database and Study Population

The data were obtained from the 2005–2010 and 2015–2018 waves of the NHANES, a stratified, multistage probability sample representing the noninstitutionalized civilian population in the United States. This study included individuals aged 20 years or older who provided complete data on depression, self‐reported sleep duration, CRP levels, demographic details, and survival status. Potential outliers were systematically evaluated using interquartile range (IQR) criteria (values below Q1 ‐ 1.5×IQR or above Q3 + 1.5×IQR) for continuous variables, but no biologically implausible values were identified for sleep duration or depression scores. Since inflammatory markers exhibit significant and unstable fluctuations during acute inflammation, individuals with a baseline CRP concentration exceeding 10 mg/dL or a white blood cell (WBC) count above 11 × 1000 cells/μL were also excluded to eliminate those in the acute inflammatory phase [25, 26]. The final analytical sample consisted of 41,346 participants (Figure 1).

Flow chart of participants selection.

Primary Measures

To evaluate depression, we employed the Patient Health Questionnaire (PHQ‐9) [27]. This instrument captures the frequency of nine depressive symptoms over the past 2 weeks. Each symptom is rated on a 0–3 scale, and the ratings are aggregated to yield a total score spanning 0 to 27. This total score operates on a continuum, where higher values are indicative of more severe depressive states. To capture a broader spectrum of clinically relevant depressive symptoms, including subclinical cases which constitute a significant public health concern, we employed a cutoff score of ≥ 5 to define depression in our primary analysis [27]. A sensitivity analysis using a more stringent cutoff (PHQ‐9 ≥ 10) was conducted to confirm the robustness of our findings [27].

Sleep duration was measured by asking participants about their typical nightly sleep on weekdays or workdays. In our primary analysis, responses were categorized as short (< 7 h), normal (7–8 h), or long (> 8 h) to align with common classifications and facilitate comparisons with the extensive existing literature [28]. Furthermore, a sensitivity analysis employing more extreme cutoffs (< 6 and > 9 h) was conducted to test the robustness of the observed associations.

During the 2005–2010 NHANES surveys, serum CRP measurements were conducted using latex‐enhanced nephelometry technology on Behring equipment, which had a detection threshold of 0.02 mg/dL. In subsequent surveys from 2015 to 2018, serum CRP analysis was performed using Beckman UniCel systems, with detection limits of 0.011 mg/dL (2015–2016) and 0.015 mg/dL (2017–2018). When CRP levels fell below the detection threshold, they were estimated by dividing the threshold value by the square root of two. Detailed laboratory protocols for both methods are available in the NHANES Laboratory Procedures Manual (2005–2006 and 2015–2016 cycles, accessible at: [https://wwwn.cdc.gov/nchs/nhanes/continuousnhanes/labmethods.aspx?Cycle=2015-2016]).

Outcome and Follow‐up

The primary endpoints of this investigation included both overall mortality and deaths specifically related to cardiovascular events. Mortality information was acquired through linkage with the National Death Index (NDI), with follow‐up extending through the end of 2019. Participants were tracked from their enrollment during 2005–2018 until either their death or the study's cutoff date of December 31, 2019, depending on which event came first. Total mortality was defined as death from any origin, whereas cardiovascular‐related fatalities were categorized according to specific ICD‐10 classifications, including cardiac conditions (I00–I09, I11, I13, I20–I51) and cerebrovascular disorders (I60–I69).

Assessment of Potential Covariates

The NHANES interviews collected data on sociodemographic and behavioral factors, such as age, sex, race/ethnicity, education, poverty‐to‐income ratio (PIR), smoking habits, and physical activity levels. Smoking status was divided into never smoked, former smoker (smoked more than 100 cigarettes but not currently smoking), and current smoker. Physical activity was assessed based on the 2018 Physical Activity Guidelines, with participants classified as “physically inactive” (not meeting the guidelines) or “physically active” (meeting the guidelines of ≥ 150 min of moderate‐intensity or ≥ 75 min of vigorous‐intensity activity per week). Weight and height were measured to calculate body mass index (BMI) as weight (kg) divided by height (m²). Obesity was defined as a BMI ≥ 30, and extreme obesity as a BMI ≥ 35. Hypertension was identified as systolic blood pressure (SBP) ≥ 140 mmHg, diastolic blood pressure (DBP) ≥ 80 mmHg, or current use of antihypertensive medication. Diabetes was identified through self‐reported physician diagnosis, while cancer history was determined based on a diagnosis of cancer or malignancy.

Statistical Analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using methods appropriate for the respective variable types. Continuous variables were presented as mean ± standard error, whereas categorical variables were summarized as frequencies with weighted proportions. Group comparisons were performed using t‐tests for continuous variables between depression groups, analysis of variance (ANOVA) for continuous variables across sleep duration categories, and χ² tests for categorical variables.

The study utilized multivariate Cox regression (R package “survival”) to calculate hazard ratio (HR) and 95% Confidence interval (CI), to evaluate independent and combined effects of depression and sleep duration on mortality outcomes, with participants having normal sleep duration and no depression serving as the reference group. Three hierarchical models were constructed: Model 1 adjusted for demographic factors (age, sex, race); Model 2 incorporated socioeconomic and lifestyle variables (education, marital status, PIR, smoking, physical activity, BMI); Model 3 further included clinical comorbidities (hypertension, diabetes, cancer). To assess the interaction between depressive symptoms and sleep duration on mortality risk, we constructed Cox proportional hazards models with interaction terms. Besides, to assess the robustness of our findings, we employed multiple imputation to handle missing covariate data while maintaining complete‐case analysis for the primary exposure variables (depression and sleep duration) and outcome (mortality). Specifically, we used Multiple Imputation by Chained Equations (MICE) in R to create 5 imputed datasets. The imputation model incorporated all variables involved in the subsequent analysis, including mortality status, depressive symptoms, sleep duration, and all covariates. The analysis results from these 5 datasets were then pooled according to Rubin's rules to obtain final parameter estimates and standard errors that account for the uncertainty due to missing data. The proportional hazards assumption was verified through Schoenfeld residual tests (all p > 0.05), with linearity of continuous predictors confirmed using martingale residual plots. The presence of multicollinearity was evaluated by calculating the variance inflation factor (VIF), where a value greater than 10 was considered indicative of substantial multicollinearity. The results demonstrated no evident multicollinearity, with all computed VIF values falling below 5.

To examine the potential mediating role of CRP in the relationship of depression and sleep duration with mortality, we conducted simple mediation analyses. The model estimated the following pathways (illustrated in Figure 2): the total effect of the depression and sleep duration (exposure) on mortality (outcome) (Path C), the effect of the depression and sleep duration on CRP (mediator) (Path A), and the effect of the CAP on mortality (Path B). The proportion mediated was quantified as (indirect effect/total effect) × 100%.

Path diagram of the mediation analysis models.

All analyses were performed using R software (version 4.2.2; R Core Team, 2022) and accounted for the complex survey design of NHANES by applying appropriate survey weights. Statistical significance was defined by two‐tailed p values of less than 0.05.

Ethics Approval and Consent to Participate

The data for this study were sourced from NHANES. The survey protocol received approval from the NCHS Research Ethics Review Board, and all participants provided informed consent before their inclusion. The ethical approval for this secondary analysis was waived by the School of Public Health, Qingdao University.

In terms of sleep duration, 54.0% of the participants reported sleeping for 7–8 h per day, 29.2% slept for less than 7 h, and 16.7% slept for more than 8 h. Participants who were non‐Hispanic Black, unmarried or widowed, divorced, or separated, had a PIR below 1.30, were below high school graduates, were current smokers, and had inactive physical activity were more likely to have abnormal self‐reported sleep duration.

Associations of Depression, Sleep Duration and Mortality

Table 2 presents Cox proportional hazards model results on depression, sleep duration, and mortality risk among 41,326 participants. Over a median follow‐up of 6.51 years, 6,799 deaths occurred, including 1,784 cardiovascular deaths. After adjusting for age, sex, and race, depression significantly increased all‐cause (HR = 1.68, 95% CI = 1.45–1.96) and cardiovascular mortality risk (HR = 2.01, 95% CI = 1.52–2.67), with significance maintained in further adjusted models. We have performed a sensitivity analysis using a PHQ‐9 cutoff of ≥ 10 to represent more severe depression. The results of the Cox models using this definition (presented in Supplementary Table 1) remained consistent with our primary findings. Short sleep duration was also associated with higher all‐cause mortality risk (HR = 1.33, 95% CI = 1.16–1.53), persisting in subsequent models, while long sleep duration showed no significant association. We have also performed a sensitivity analysis using alternative cut‐points (< 6 and > 9 h), and the results affirm that the association between sleep duration and our primary outcome remains robust and consistent with our main findings (presented in Supplementary Table 1). In addition, sensitivity analyses using multiple imputation yielded consistent effect estimates (Supplementary Table 2), reinforcing the robustness of these findings. Age‐stratified analysis (Supplementary Table 3) revealed depression significantly increased all‐cause (HR = 1.35, 95% CI = 1.15–1.59) and cardiovascular mortality risk (HR = 2.01, 95% CI = 1.52–2.67) in participants over 45. Short sleep duration elevated all‐cause mortality risk across all ages and cardiovascular mortality risk in those over 45. Gender‐stratified analysis showed consistent depression‐mortality associations in both sexes, while abnormal sleep duration increased all‐cause mortality risk only in women (Supplementary Table 3).

As shown in Table 3, after full adjustment for covariates, the Cox regression analysis indicated no statistically significant multiplicative interaction between depressive symptoms and either short sleep duration or long sleep duration on all‐cause mortality (all p > 0.05). Regarding additive interaction for all‐cause mortality, the results for depressive symptoms and short sleep duration indicated no statistically significant additive interaction (p > 0.05). In contrast, a statistically significant additive interaction was observed between depressive symptoms and long sleep duration: RERI = 0.30 (95% CI: 0.07, 0.54), Ap = 0.16 (95% CI: 0.05, 0.27), and SI = 1.48 (95% CI: 1.09, 2.01) (all p < 0.05). This suggests a synergistic additive interaction, meaning the co‐occurrence of depressive symptoms and long sleep duration is associated with an increased risk of all‐cause mortality. However, for cardiovascular mortality, after full adjustment, the Cox regression analysis revealed no statistically significant multiplicative or additive interactions between depressive symptoms and either short or long sleep duration (all p > 0.05) (Table 4).

Joint Associations of Depression, Sleep Duration and Mortality

Table 5 highlights the combined effects of depression and abnormal sleep duration on mortality risk. Adults with both depression and abnormal sleep duration faced increased risks of all‐cause and cardiovascular mortality. After adjusting for age, sex, and race, depression with short sleep duration significantly elevated all‐cause (HR = 1.95, 95% CI = 1.58–2.41) and cardiovascular mortality risk (HR = 2.16, 95% CI = 1.42–3.29). Similarly, depression with long sleep duration increased all‐cause (HR = 2.58, 95% CI = 1.73–3.84) and cardiovascular mortality risk (HR = 2.66, 95% CI = 1.26–5.62), with significance maintained in further adjusted models. In contrast, neither depression nor abnormal sleep duration alone significantly affected mortality risks. Sensitivity analyses using multiple imputation yielded consistent effect estimates (Supplementary Table 4), reinforcing the robustness of these findings.

Mediation Analysis

Causal mediation analysis was performed to assess CRP's role in mediating the relationships between depression, sleep duration, and mortality risks. Using the group without depressive symptoms and with normal sleep duration as the reference, the mediating effects of CRP accounted for 5.30% and 6.63% of the total effects in the associations of depressive symptoms combined with long sleep duration with all‐cause and cardiovascular mortality, and in the associations of depressive symptoms combined with short sleep duration with all‐cause and cardiovascular mortality, respectively (all p < 0.05) (Table 6). These findings suggest that CRP partially mediates the joint effects of depressive symptoms and sleep duration on the risks of all‐cause and cardiovascular mortality.

Discussion

In the present study, we conducted an initial examination of the combined impact of depressive symptoms and sleep patterns on mortality outcomes. Over an average observation period of 6.51 years, our findings revealed that depressive disorders were significantly correlated with elevated risks of both all‐cause and cardiovascular‐related deaths. Concurrently, insufficient sleep duration showed a notable association with heightened all‐cause mortality risk. The integrated analysis revealed that the simultaneous presence of depressive symptoms and sleep disturbances exerted a synergistic effect, substantially amplifying mortality risk. These findings collectively emphasize the necessity for enhanced clinical surveillance of individuals presenting with comorbid depression and sleep abnormalities to optimize health outcomes. Furthermore, our research identified CRP as a partial mediator in the relationship between depression, sleep disturbances, and mortality outcomes.

In line with earlier research, our findings indicate that depressive symptoms are linked to both all‐cause and cardiovascular mortality. A meta‐analysis conducted in 2002 pooled data from previous studies and identified an elevated risk of mortality associated with depression [29]. Another meta‐analysis conducted in 2014 reported a significant association between depression and excess mortality in both community populations and specific patient groups [30]. Depression contributes to mortality at both individual and population levels, with the elevated death rates associated with it being attributed to various factors. These include higher suicide rates, accidental deaths from risky behaviors, and unhealthy lifestyles that hinder recovery or adherence to treatment plans [31]. Additionally, depression may trigger physiological changes that heighten disease vulnerability, ultimately leading to death by disrupting immune, endocrine, and neurological processes [29].

Meanwhile, our results indicate that insufficient sleep increases the risk of all‐cause mortality, aligning with numerous previous studies that have highlighted the detrimental effects of short sleep duration on overall health [11, 12]. This correlation underscores the importance of achieving adequate sleep for maintaining long‐term health and well‐being. However, our study also observed that excessive sleep did not significantly influence the risk of mortality. A meta‐analysis showed shorter sleep duration was associated with a higher risk for all‐cause and cardiovascular mortality, and longer sleep and all‐cause and cardiovascular mortality should be interpreted cautiously because of high heterogeneity [32], which was similar to our findings. Several factors may explain this lack of statistical significance and the heterogeneity noted across studies. First, measurement error in self‐reported sleep duration may obscure true associations, particularly at the extremes of the distribution. Second, the potential for reverse causality cannot be ruled out, whereby underlying undiagnosed diseases or poor health status may lead to prolonged sleep duration. Third, residual confounding by socioeconomic factors, mental health conditions, or other unmeasured variables might further contribute to the observed null association. Finally, population‐specific characteristics and variations in the definition of “excessive sleep” across studies may also account for the inconsistent findings in the literature.

Moreover, the current investigation revealed a significant interaction between sleep patterns and depressive disorders, suggesting that patients with depressive symptoms exhibiting either insufficient or excessive sleep duration are particularly vulnerable to elevated risks of both overall mortality and cardiovascular‐related deaths. Existing research has predominantly examined depressive disorders and sleep duration in isolation, yet their synergistic impact on health outcomes remains largely unexplored, with only one prior study linking them to increased overall mortality risk [33]. And another study indicated that sleep duration partly explained the association between depressive symptoms and cardiovascular mortality [34]. While depressive disorders can manifest independently, they frequently co‐occur with sleep‐related disturbances [35]. Evidence from systematic reviews has established that both insufficient and excessive sleep duration are significantly correlated with elevated depression risk in adult populations [36], with longitudinal studies further confirming substantial associations between insomnia occurring independently or concurrently with depressive disorders [37]. Through comprehensive analysis, this study elucidates the individual and synergistic contributions of these factors to mortality risk, offering valuable insights for population‐level health management.

Our analysis revealed that CRP might mediate the combined effect of depression and abnormal sleep duration on mortality risk. Depression is increasingly recognized not just as a psychological disorder but also as a state of systemic inflammation, with numerous studies documenting heightened CRP levels among depressed individuals [15, 38, 39]. This finding suggests that inflammation, as reflected by elevated CRP levels, may be a crucial intermediary process through which depression exerts its detrimental effects on health outcomes. This inflammatory state may originate from multiple biological pathways: (1) chronic stress activation of the hypothalamic‐pituitary‐adrenal (HPA) axis leading to glucocorticoid resistance and subsequent pro‐inflammatory cytokine release [40, 41]; (2) depression‐associated alterations in gut microbiota composition that promote intestinal permeability and endotoxin translocation [42, 43]; (3) depression‐related behavioral changes (e.g., physical inactivity, poor diet) that independently promote inflammation [44]; and (4) metabolic dysregulation, where metabolomic aging clocks reveal profound interconnectivity between inflammatory and metabolic pathways [45, 46]. Previous study indicated that the combined of depression and inflammation is associated with all‐cause mortality in specific patients [47, 48]. Our mediation analysis provides further evidence for this inflammatory pathway, indicating that the increased risk of mortality observed in depressed populations may be partially explained by their heightened inflammatory state. Abnormal sleep duration, whether it be too short or too long, has been independently associated with increased inflammation [17, 49], through several biological mechanisms like sleep deprivation‐induced activation of nuclear factor kappa‐B (NF‐κB) signaling and subsequent pro‐inflammatory gene expression [50]; and disruption of circadian rhythms leading to altered immune cell trafficking and function [51]. A study reported participants who reported < 6 or > 7 h of sleep had higher risk of cardiovascular death attributable to inflammation [52]. When combined with depression, the inflammatory burden imposed by both conditions may be additive, further elevating CRP levels and thereby intensifying the risk of mortality.

This study benefits from several notable methodological strengths, including access to a substantial participant cohort obtained through a nationally representative, well‐established survey in the United States. The study employs a population‐level observational framework, incorporates comprehensive adjustments for various lifestyle parameters and comorbid health conditions, and conducts sophisticated interaction analyses with adequate statistical precision to ensure reliable findings. Additionally, this is the first study to investigate the mediating role of CRP in the relationship between depression, sleep duration, and mortality risk. However, several limitations in our study should be acknowledged. First, and most importantly, the cross‐sectional nature of our data precludes any definitive causal inference. Although mediation analysis was employed to explore potential pathways, the absence of a temporal sequence between the measurements of CRP, depression, and sleep duration means that the observed associations may not reflect causal relationships and should be interpreted as suggestive of a hypothesized mediating role. Second, sleep duration was assessed via self‐report, which is subject to recall bias and does not capture objective sleep characteristics. Third, depression was identified using the PHQ‐9 questionnaire rather than a clinical diagnostic interview, which may introduce information bias. Nonetheless, the PHQ‐9 is a well‐validated and widely accepted instrument for depression screening in large epidemiological studies like NHANES [53].

In terms of public health implications, our findings demonstrate a joint association between sleep duration and depression, supporting the integration of routine screening for depressive symptoms (e.g., using the PHQ‐9) and sleep patterns into primary care workflows. Comprehensive strategies that concurrently address mental health and sleep hygiene should be developed, especially for vulnerable populations, as they may be crucial for improving health outcomes and reducing mortality. Furthermore, the partial mediation by CRP in the association between depression, abnormal sleep duration, and mortality suggests that addressing systemic inflammation could represent a potential therapeutic avenue to mitigate adverse health outcomes linked to co‐occurring depression and disordered sleep.

Conclusion

Our study demonstrated a joint association between sleep duration and depression, highlighting that comprehensive interventions targeting both sleep and mental health may be crucial for improving outcomes and reducing mortality among vulnerable populations. Furthermore, our findings revealed that CRP partially mediated the association between depression, abnormal sleep duration, and mortality risk. This suggests that addressing inflammation could potentially help mitigate adverse health outcomes linked to depression and disordered sleep.
