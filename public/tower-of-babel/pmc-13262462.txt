Lichenoid granulomatous stomatitis: a series of 12 cases and a literature review
Authors: Furong Yang, Tingning Xiao, Haiyan Luo, Chunlei Li, Yan Gao, Hong Hua, Pan Wei
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundLichenoid granulomatous stomatitis (LGS), characterized by a combined histopathological presentation of lichenoid reactions and granulomatous inflammation, is a rare oral disease. Although it has been reported previously, it has never been assessed specifically.MethodsWe retrieved cases presenting with both lichenoid reactions and granulomatous inflammation between January 1, 2013, and May 1, 2024, at the Peking University Hospital of Stomatology. LGS diagnosis was based on the histopathological presence of both lichenoid reactions and granulomatous inflammation. We retrospectively assessed the clinicopathology of all the included patients. We also conducted a literature review of relevant literature highlighting the demographic and clinicopathological characteristics of LGS.ResultsIn total, 12 patients with tissue from 13 biopsy sites were included. Most patients (83.33%) were females, and the mean age was 46.58 ± 14.37 years (range = 22–68 years). All patients were of Chinese descent, and the most common LGS site was the dorsum of the tongue (n = 7), followed by labial mucosa (n = 6), buccal mucosa and vestibule (n = 5), gingiva (n = 2), and most multifocal involvement (n = 7). The clinical manifestations of LGS ranged from swelling to reticulated plaques and erythematous or erythroleukoplakic appearance. Histopathologically, the lesions were characterized by lichenoid reactions concomitant with granulomatous inflammation. Based on Magro and Crawson’s classification system for lichenoid granulomatous dermatitis, six, six, and one biopsy samples were classified into type I, II, and IV LGS, respectively. In particular, the biopsy sites for all type II LGS samples were the dorsum of the tongue, whereas those for most type I LGS samples were the labial and buccal mucosa. Most patients were treated with Chinese patent medicine or tacrolimus ointment, which led to symptom relief but not complete resolution.ConclusionLGS is a distinct entity, often multifocal (notably the tongue), with mixed lichenoid-granulomatous features and site-histotype correlations. Treatments alleviate symptoms but not disease, highlighting the need for further study.

Introduction

Oral lichenoid lesions (OLLs) represent a large spectrum of disorders related to varied inciting factors, such as medications, localized reactions to dental restorations, and graft-versus-host disorders [1]. In particular, based on the World Health Organization’s 2007 classification, an oral lesion that does not fulfill all criteria for an oral lichen planus (OLP) is considered an OLL. Histologically, a lichenoid reaction is characterized by interface reactions with a band-like lymphocytic infiltration neat the epithelium and liquefaction degeneration of the basal cell layer. Granulomatous inflammation in the oral cavity may be due to foreign materials, infective microorganisms, or allergic conditions. Histologically, granulomatous reaction patterns include necrotizing granulomas, nonnecrotizing granulomas, suppurative granulomas, diffuse granulomatous inflammation, and foreign body giant cell reaction [2]. This tissue reaction pattern limits the range of diagnostic possibilities.

Lichenoid reactions and granulomatous inflammation are nonspecific immune response patterns that typically enable differential diagnoses. However, the concurrent presence of a lichenoid reaction and granulomatous inflammation in the same biopsy from skin or oral mucosa is rare. This uncommon condition has been described as a lichenoid granulomatous reaction; it is subcategorized according to the lesion location into lichenoid granulomatous dermatitis (LGD) and lichenoid granulomatous stomatitis (LGS).

Gonzalez et al. reported the first case of inflammatory dermatosis with combined features of lichenoid reactions and granulomatous inflammation in 1986 [3]. In 1990, Daley and Wysocki described lichenoid reactions and granulomatous inflammation in the oral mucosa, specifically in cases of foreign body gingivitis, for the first time [4]. In a case series, lichenoid reactions and granulomatous inflammation cooccurred in 16 of 61 (26%) biopsies from patients with foreign body gingivitis [5]. In 2000, Magro and Crowson reported 40 LGD cases [6]. In 2006, Robinson first proposed the concept of Lichenoid Granulomatous Stomatitis (LGS) after reporting six cases demonstrating concurrent lichenoid reactions and granulomatous inflammation in oral mucosa, with a definitive diagnosis established after excluding foreign body or infectious etiologies [7]. In 2019, Hakeem et al. retrospectively analyzed the clinical and histological features of LGS in 47 cases [8]. To our knowledge, only three studies [7–9] and three abstracts [10–12] have reported cases of LGS, whereas only one case of concurrent OLP and granulomatous cheilitis has been reported [13]. In total, 65 cases of cooccurrence of lichenoid reactions and granulomatous inflammation have been reported. This disorder has never been assessed specifically thus far.

Herein, we present 12 cases of LGS, with clinical presentations of lichenoid papules on the tongue, labial and buccal mucosa, vestibule, and gingiva, histopathologically accompanied by lichenoid reactions and granulomatous inflammation. Moreover, a review of LGS cases in previous literature was also condocted.

Methods

We retrospectively retrieved the demographic, clinical, and histopathological data of patients diagnosed as having cooccurrence of lichenoid reactions and granulomatous inflammation through biopsy at the Peking University Hospital of Stomatology between January 1, 2013, and May 1, 2024. The inclusion criterion was the presence of both a lichenoid pattern of inflammation and histiocytic infiltration or granulomatous reaction in the oral cavity biopsy. The exclusion criteria included the presence of foreign materials or microorganisms (when necessary, polarized light microscopy and corresponding special staining methods were applied for identification and exclusion), insufficient tissue in the sample, or excessively fragmented specimens. Finally, all patients were classified according to the histological classification system for LGD reported by Magro and Crawson [6].

A comprehensive literature search was conducted in PubMed and Web of Science up to February 16, 2026, using the query (“lichenoid granulomatous stomatitis” OR “lichenoid granulomatous reaction” OR (“lichenoid” AND “granulomatous”)) in title/abstract fields. After removing duplicates from the initial 273 records, 141 unique articles were identified and supplemented with 3 additional records from reference lists. Screening excluded non-human studies (n = 6), reviews/basic research (n = 33), and articles not specifically describing co-localized oral lichenoid-granulomatous pathology (n = 87). Full-text review of the remaining 18 articles further excluded those without oral mucosal involvement, resulting in 7 articles for final inclusion. Results

Clinical characteristics

In total, 12 patients were included in this case series; their clinical characteristics are listed in Table 1. All cases were negative for refractile and nonrefractile visible foreign bodies under a light microscope with and without polarization, and were also negative for fungal infections, tuberculosis, sarcoidosis, and other diseases with similar clinical presentations. All patients were of Chinese descent, and the number of female patients was higher than that of male patients (n = 10 vs. 2; female-to-male ratio = 2.8:1). The mean age was 46.58 ± 14.37 years (range = 22–68 years); most patients were younger (age = 20–44 years; n = 6, 50%), followed by those middle-aged (age = 45–59 years; n = 3, 25%) and older (age = 60–74 years; n = 3, 25%). Regarding anatomical sites of the lesions, two-thirds of the patients presented with multifocal lesions (n = 8), whereas the remaining patients had solitary lesions (n = 4/12). Moreover, one-third of the patients (n = 4) presented with a localized erythematous patch on the labial mucosa with or without attendant swelling or with or without buccal mucosa lesion (Fig. 2A–D). Most patients (n = 7, 58.33%) presented with white papules on the dorsum of the tongue (Fig. 2E–H), and two patients demonstrated nodular hyperplasia (Fig. 2 I and J) with or without stripes or red lesions on the buccal mucosa, gingiva, or other sites. One patient presented with white patches on the buccal mucosa or gingiva but without other lesions (Fig. 2K–L).

LGS occurred on the tongue (n = 7, 58.33%), labial mucosa (n = 6, 50.00%), buccal mucosa and vestibule (n = 5, 41.67%), and gingiva (n = 2, 16.67%). Two patients (16.67%) demonstrated proliferative lesions on the dorsum of the tongue. Moreover, lesions occurred on the nonkeratinized mucosa in four (33%) patients, the keratinized mucosa in three (25%) patients, and both in five (41.67%). Only seven (58.33%) patients reported pain in the oral mucosa, but the other five (41.67%) did not.

Regarding their medical history, one patient had a 20-year history of hypertension and of taking Betaloc (a beta-blocker) and Irbesartan (an angiotensin receptor inhibitor); this patient developed oral symptoms after wearing a removable partial denture. Another patient had porcelain crown restoration material present intraorally. Finally, two other patients had a history of pulmonary tuberculosis and rheumatoid arthritis, respectively.

Histopathological features

All patients demonstrated liquefaction degeneration of the basal cell layer, a band-like lymphohistiocytic infiltration at the epitheliomesenchymal interface, and superficial small histiocytic aggregates in the middle of a band-like lymphocytic infiltrate or well-formed granulomas in the lamina propria. The histological features of specimens from all 12 patients were analyzed according to Magro and Crowson’s classification system, which classified LGD into five types on the basis of the presence of histiocytes [6].

Figure 3 Illustrates the histopathology characteristics of the included patients, and Table 2 presents the biopsy appearance and histologic assessment results. Among 13 biopsy sites, the most prominent feature was the presence of extensive aggregates of histiocytes (n = 6, 46.15%) or more defined granulomas (n = 6, 46.15%), followed by multinucleated giant cells (n = 1, 8.33%). All patient specimens demonstrated a band-like lymphocyte infiltration. Finally, the histological sections from six (46.15%), six (46.15%), and one (8.33%) were categorized into Magro and Crowson’s classification types I, II, and IV, respectively.

Treatment and follow-up

Of the 12 patients, 11 (91.67%) were available for follow-up after biopsy, with a median follow-up duration of 5 years (range: 3 months to 11 years). At the last clinical visit or telephonic follow-up, two patients (18.18%) achieved complete resolution of lesions, nine (81.82%) showed partial improvement with controlled symptoms. Treatment regimens included Chinese patent drug therapy (n = 5), topical tacrolimus ointment (n = 4), and local corticosteroids (n = 1), while one patient received no specific therapy. Among the two patients with complete resolution, one was managed with traditional Chinese herbal therapy and the other received no treatment. Notably, one patient demonstrated marked clinical improvement after replacing metallic denture materials with non-metallic alternatives, suggesting a potential environmental antigen trigger. However, the therapeutic role of medications in LGS remains unclear due to incompletely documented pharmacotherapy (e.g., uncharacterized components of Chinese patent drug) and the concomitant use of multiple agents.

Discussion

At the World Workshop on Oral Medicine IV in 2006, a working group proposed categorizing OLL into three types: oral moss-like contact lesions, oral moss-like drug reactions, and graft-versus-host disease [14]. However, in 2019, Carrozzo et al. [15] indicated that the aforementioned criteria are unclear and do not cover other diseases with moss-like features and expanded the OLL classification to more than ten types, one of which was LGS. LGS, first described by Robinson et al. in 2006, is a clinically rare or underrecognized disease. To our knowledge, only a few articles thus far have reported LGS cases, all of which were summarized by Hakeem et al. [8]. Lichenoid reactions are primarily driven by CD8⁺ T-cell-mediated cytotoxicity targeting basal keratinocytes, largely mediated through cytokines such as IFN-γ and TNF-α, resulting in basal cell liquefactive degeneration. In contrast, granulomatous inflammation is associated with Th1/Th17 polarization, macrophage activation, and multinucleated giant cell formation, supported by IL-12/IL-23-related pathways.

The coexistence of lichenoid and granulomatous inflammatory processes in LGS may reflect a unified immunopathogenic mechanism rather than the coincidental overlap of two independent reactions. Shared antigenic triggers, including dental materials or microbial factors, may initiate immune responses driven by self-derived or environmental antigens or superantigens. These stimuli can recruit cytotoxic CD8⁺ T cells and histiocytes through interconnected immune pathways. Cytokine crosstalk further amplifies tissue injury. IFN-γ, for example, not only promotes keratinocyte apoptosis characteristic of lichenoid reactions but also plays a pivotal role in macrophage activation and granuloma maturation [16].

Epithelial–mesenchymal interactions represent another important component of LGS pathobiology. CD8⁺ T-cell-mediated destruction of basal keratinocytes may alter the epithelial microenvironment, potentially influencing the recruitment and activation of macrophages and other immune cells involved in granuloma formation.

Genetic susceptibility may further modulate disease expression. Certain HLA-DRB1 polymorphisms, previously associated with autoimmune disorders, may predispose individuals to dysregulated immune responses by influencing antigen presentation and T-cell activation [17]. Such host factors may contribute to the distinctive dual histopathological features observed in LGS. Unlike OLP, the consistent coexistence of lichenoid and granulomatous components in LGS supports its recognition as a unique clinicopathological entity shaped by specific antigenic burden and host immune context.

We consider LGS to represent a distinct clinicopathological entity rather than merely a reaction pattern. Hakeem A suggested that this condition may broaden the conceptual boundaries of OLP [8], highlighting the ongoing debate regarding its classification. In our cohort, the dual histopathologic architecture—characterized by basal cell liquefaction degeneration, band-like lymphohistiocytic infiltration, and the presence of granulomas or histiocytic aggregates—was consistently observed. Furthermore, the systematic exclusion of foreign body reactions, infections, and other idiopathic granulomatous diseases further argues against a purely exogenous reactive process. The clinical presentation also differed from that typically seen in classical OLP and established granulomatous disorders. The repeated identification of this combined clinicopathological pattern across cases, together with its recognizable clinical correlates, supports the interpretation of LGS as a distinct entity. Nevertheless, further mechanistic and multicenter studies are warranted to clarify its nosological status.

Currently, an established diagnostic standard for LGS remains unavailable; in most cases, the diagnoses are based on medical history and biopsy results. Most studies have described LGS cases based on well-defined granulomas accompanied by lichenoid infiltration of the band-like layer of lymphocytes in the superficial lamina propria. The current case series included 12 patients whopresented with similar mucosal lesions on the labial mucosa or the dorsum of the tongue, and their demographic and clinical characteristics were similar to those reported previously. However, in the current series, the average age was 46 years (range = 22–68 years) with a female-to-male ratio of 5:1, younger than that reported previously: an average age of 59 (range = 30–88 years) years with a female-to-male ratio of 2.4:1. Moreover, the clinical presentations of our patients with LGS varied and included swelling, as well as white, red, and mixed lesions. All 12 of our cases presented with lichenoid white papules and stripes—which may lead to misdiagnosis as OLP.

All 12 patients in this case series demonstrated granulomatous with lichenoid infiltration despite the presence of various clinical manifestations. Nevertheless, this was the first report of two cases demonstrating nodular hyperplasia on the dorsum of the tongue. Moreover, in contrast to previous case reports demonstrating a predominance of isolated lesions, approximately two-thirds of our 12 cases had multifocal lesions. In addition, half of the patients had symptoms similar to those of OLP; this differential diagnosis required the dental clinician to be aware of the differences between LGS and OLP.

Of all 13 biopsy sites in our case series, 6 biopsy samples from the dorsum of the tongue were of Magro and Crowson’s classification type II LGS, whereas 6 from the labial and buccal mucosa were of type I LGS. This regional disparity aligns with prior observations that oral sites with heightened mechanical stress (e.g., tongue dorsum) or microbial exposure (e.g., gingiva) may predispose to granuloma formation via sustained antigenic stimulation. Tongue-specific factors, such as keratinocyte turnover rates or lymphatic drainage patterns, might amplify Th1/Th17 polarization, fostering both cytotoxic T-cell activity and granulomatous responses. In contrast, nonkeratinized mucosa (e.g., labial/buccal) may favor isolated lichenoid reactions due to reduced antigen persistence. Hakeem et al. similarly reported type II predominance in tongue lesions, suggesting site-specific immunologic microenvironments drive histopathological divergence in LGS [8]. These findings highlight the need to contextualize biopsy findings within anatomical locations for refined diagnostic and therapeutic strategies.

In addition, we did not find an interstitial array between collagen fibers similar to that in interstitial GA, singly disposed Langhans giant cells, or granulomatous vasculitis in any of our 12 cases—consistent with the study of Hakeem et al. [8]. Thus, these three histopathological manifestations may be unique to LGD and do not appear in other oral mucosal lesions, possibly due to structural differences between the oral mucosa and skin.

LGD and LGS are rare diseases, and their clinical correlation remains unclear and difficult to determine. In 2000, Magro and Crowson studied LGD in 40 cases, and their results showed that LGD may be associated with drug eruptions, hepatobiliary disorders, Crohn’s disease, diabetes mellitus, thyroiditis, cutaneous T-cell lymphoma (CTCL), infections (Epstein–Barr virus or herpes zoster), rheumatoid arthritis, and active infections (Mycobacterium leprae, fungus, Mycobacterium tuberculosis, and syphilis), and idiopathic lichenoid eruptions (OLP, lichen striatus, lichen planopilaris, and lichen nitidus) [6]. In 2019, Braswell et al. studied 56 LGD cases and concluded that LGD may be associated with drug allergy, lichenoid keratosis, tattoo reactions, postherpetic dermatitis, and postscabietic dermatitis. Moreover, LGD may be associated with a low probability of pigmented purpuric dermatosis, lichen striatus (or acquired blaschkoid dermatitis), actinic granulomas, contact dermatitis, or Flegel disease [18]. In 2018, Diaz-Perez et al. presented a case of LGD induced by nivolumab during advanced glioblastoma treatment [19].

In most cases, LGS, which has histopathologic patterns similar to LGD, is associated with hypersensitivity due to various clinical triggers, such as medication use, foreign bodies, and infection. The most commonly implicated medications have been antihypertensive agents, proton-pump inhibitors, statins, tumor necrosis factor α inhibitors, selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors, nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs, hydrochlorothiazide, and hypoglycemic drugs [18]. The foreign bodies may be derived from toothpaste and dental material components [4, 5]. The implicated active infections include M. tuberculosis infection, M. leprae infection, fungal infection, and syphilis [18]. Gouveris et al. reported a case of nivolumab-induced LGS in a patient with advanced melanoma in 2022; this was the first report of LGS as a side effect of immune checkpoint inhibitor treatment [9]. In contrast to the previously described cases of LGD, few cases were identified as induced by medicine use or by foreign bodies; nevertheless, an analysis of 77 previously reported cases demonstrated that the etiology of most LGS cases remains unclear. Similarly, in the current case series, we could not identify the underlying associations or causes in most cases. Nevertheless, one of our cases had a 20-year history of hypertension along with simultaneous beta-blocker and angiotensin receptor inhibitor use, and the symptoms developed after wearing a removable partial denture. In another case, the patient had porcelain crown restoration material intraorally. The lesions in both these cases were related to foreign bodies or medicine use. In addition, one case had a history of pulmonary tuberculosis, whereas another case had that of rheumatoid arthritis; both of these are clinical factors for LGD.Further studies are needed to clarify whether this represents a distinct disease or a reactive spectrum driven by microbial/host immune dynamics.

The most common treatment for LGS has involved the use of topical corticosteroids. However, the effectiveness of topical corticosteroids in LGS remains debated: it has been effective, leading to complete resolution of the lesions in some cases [9, 10, 12], whereas in the remaining cases, it has been ineffective, with the lesions remaining persistent but localized [7, 11].

No globally accepted established diagnostic standard for LGS is available currently; the diagnosis is generally based on medical history and biopsy results. The clinical presentation of white papules, stripes, or red lesions leads to diagnostic confusion or overt misdiagnosis as OLP, lichenoid reaction, allergic stomatitis, or other conditions. Of our 12 cases, 7, 3, 1, and 4 were initially suspected of having OLP, OLLs, oral leukoplakia, and LGS, respectively. Thus, the misdiagnosis rate for LGS is extremely high. The Magro and Crowson classification provides a useful conceptual reference but has limitations when applied to oral mucosal lesions. The framework was developed for cutaneous pathology, whereas the oral mucosa differs in epithelial structure, keratinization, immune microenvironment, microbiome exposure, and mechanical stress [26, 27]. These differences may alter inflammatory morphology and the diagnostic significance of histologic patterns. Moreover, clinical descriptors used in dermatology may not translate directly to mucosal disease. Development of mucosa-specific clinicopathological criteria may improve characterization of LGS.

To facilitate clinical recognition, we propose a set of major and minor criteria based on the recurring features observed in this study (Table 5). The major criteria highlight core histopathological findings, while the minor criteria emphasize supportive clinical manifestations. In particular, the presence of both lichenoid reactions and granulomatous inflammation in the biopsy is considered to indicate LGS.

LGS may represent an underrecognized condition in clinical practice. Its variable presentation, including white papules, reticular/striated changes, or erythematous lesions, may contribute to diagnostic confusion and misdiagnosis as oral lichen planus, lichenoid reactions, or allergic stomatitis. These challenges underscore the importance of mucosal biopsy for definitive diagnosis. Increased awareness of LGS among dental clinicians is therefore essential to reduce diagnostic delays. Clinically, patients exhibit heterogeneous symptomatology, with pain present in only a subset of cases. Management is primarily directed toward symptomatic control. Conventional therapeutic approaches, including topical corticosteroids and anti-inflammatory agents, generally result in partial improvement, whereas complete lesion resolution is uncommon. Persistent or recurrent lesions observed during follow-up suggest that LGS may follow a chronic or relapsing course, highlighting the need for ongoing clinical monitoring and optimization of treatment strategies.

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. Although the maximum follow-up duration reached 11 years, the mean follow-up period was relatively limited (approximately 5.9 years), and one patient was lost to follow-up after the initial visit. Consequently, the long-term clinical behavior and outcomes of LGS should be interpreted with caution, and studies with extended follow-up are necessary to better define its prognosis. Additionally, the study was constrained by a modest sample size and an ethnically homogeneous cohort. Future multicenter investigations involving larger and ethnically diverse populations, along with comprehensive medical history assessment, are warranted to validate and generalize our findings.

Conclusion

This study characterizes lichenoid granulomatous stomatitis (LGS) as a distinct clinicopathologic entity with frequent multifocal involvement, most commonly affecting the tongue. LGS demonstrates variable clinical presentations and a consistent coexistence of lichenoid and granulomatous histopathological features, with site-related histologic patterns. Clinically, LGS may present diagnostic challenges and exhibit limited responsiveness to conventional therapies. The observed persistence and recurrence emphasize the importance of long-term monitoring and highlight the need for further research into its pathogenesis and optimized management strategies.
