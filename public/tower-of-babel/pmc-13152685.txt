Occipital Bone Erosion Following C1–2 Posterior Fixation: A Comparative Per-Screw Analysis of Tan versus Harms-Goel Techniques
Authors: Shigeo Ueda, Nobuaki Naito, Hokuto Yamashita, Jiro Ohara, Atsuhiko Toyoshima, Miyuki Fukuda, Nobuhiro Sasaki, Masayuki Kuroda, Minoru Hoshimaru
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveTo investigate the relationship between C1 screw trajectory and occipital bone erosion in C1–2 posterior fixation. MethodsThis retrospective cohort study analyzed 27 patients (54 screws) who underwent C1–2 posterior fixation between March 2018 and March 2023 at a single institution by multiple surgeons. Screws were classified by trajectory: Tan technique (n=39) or Harms-Goel (HG) technique (n=15). Primary outcome was occipital bone erosion; the secondary outcome was breach of inner cortical layer. Per-screw analysis was performed using generalized estimating equations to account for bilateral screw clustering. ResultsMean follow-up was 48.3 (range, 24–84) months. Occipital bone erosion occurred in 51.3% (20 of 39) of Tan screws versus 6.7% (1 of 15) of HG screws (p<0.001). Breach of inner cortical layer occurred exclusively with the Tan technique (10.3% vs. 0%, p=0.302). In mixed Tan+HG cases (n=3), erosion occurred only on the Tan side (2 of 3 screws) with no erosion on HG side (0 of 3 screws). C1 upper line transgression was a critical risk factor; no erosion occurred in screws that did not exceed this anatomical landmark (adjusted relative risk [RR], 6.82; 95% confidence interval [CI], 2.41–19.31). Additional risk factors included height O–C1 ≤4.5 mm (RR, 3.81; 95% CI, 1.51–6.28) and height O–C1 extension ≤1 mm (RR, 4.86; 95% CI, 2.05–11.53). No patients required reoperation for erosion-related symptoms during follow-up. ConclusionScrew trajectory is the primary determinant of occipital bone erosion following C1–2 fixation. The HG technique demonstrated significantly lower erosion rates (6.7% vs. 51.3%). When anatomically feasible, HG technique may be considered to reduce erosion risk.

INTRODUCTION

C1–2 posterior fixation has become the standard surgical treatment for atlantoaxial instability caused by various pathologies including rheumatoid arthritis, trauma, congenital anomalies, and degenerative conditions [1]. The widespread adoption of 3-dimensional navigation systems has enhanced the safety and accuracy of screw placement, with reported accuracy rates exceeding 95% in recent series [2]. Despite these technological advances, long-term complications associated with different screw insertion techniques remain insufficiently characterized in long-term postoperative follow-up.

Two primary techniques for C1 screw insertion have been established: the Tan technique, first described in 2003, which involves screw placement through the posterior arch of C1 [3], and the Harms-Goel (HG) technique, introduced by Goel in 1994 and modified by Harms in 2001, which utilizes the lateral mass entry point [4,5]. While both techniques have demonstrated favorable fusion rates exceeding 90% in most series [6], their differential impact on adjacent anatomical structures during long-term follow-up has not been systematically evaluated.

Ikuta et al. [7] reviewed cases from the literature and reported that rod or screw interference with the occipital bone could lead to progressive erosion, potentially resulting in breach of the inner cortical layer. However, systematic comparisons between different screw trajectories and identification of specific anatomical risk factors remain lacking. The primary objective of this study was to investigate the relationship between C1 screw trajectory and the incidence of occipital bone erosion following C1–2 posterior fixation.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

1. Study Design and Participants

This single-center, retrospective cohort study was approved by the institutional review board of Shin-ai kai Spine Center, Katano Hospital (IRB No. 20201220-1). We reviewed consecutive patients who underwent C1–2 posterior fixation between March 2018 and March 2023, with final follow-up completed on March 30, 2025. The inclusion criteria were: (1) atlantoaxial instability requiring C1–2 posterior fixation, (2) minimum follow-up of 24 months, and (3) availability of postoperative computed tomography (CT) or lateral radiographs. Exclusion criteria included previous occipitocervical fusion, concurrent C1–2 anterior fusion, and revision surgery.

Sample size calculation was based on preliminary review of the first 12 patients in our database, which showed erosion rates of approximately 50% with the Tan technique and 10% with the HG technique. Using these estimates, we calculated that 48 screws (approximately 24 per group) would provide 80% power to detect a 40% difference in erosion rates between techniques (α=0.05, 2-sided, Fisher exact test). Given the observed group size imbalance (39 Tan vs. 15 HG), our study was adequately powered for the primary comparison. Twenty-seven patients (54 C1 screws) met the inclusion criteria.

2. Surgical Technique

All procedures were performed by multiple surgeons using 3-dimensional navigation (StealthStation S8, Medtronic, Inc., USA) with intraoperative cone-beam CT (O-arm, Medtronic, Inc.). Various implant systems were used based on surgeon preference and availability. The choice between Tan and HG techniques was determined by surgeon preference and anatomical considerations, particularly the course of the vertebral artery as assessed on preoperative CT angiography [8].

For the Tan technique, the entry point was at the junction of the C1 posterior arch and lateral mass, with trajectory directed approximately 10°–15° medially and 5°–10° cephalad, as described in the original publication [3]. For the HG technique, the entry point was positioned at the center of the C1 lateral mass after careful dissection of the C2 nerve root and surrounding venous plexus, following the modification by Harms and Melcher [5].

Iliac crest autograft was harvested in all cases for bone grafting. In cases without C1 laminectomy, morselized bone graft was placed between the C1 posterior arch and the C2 lamina/spinous process after decortication. In cases with prior C1 laminectomy, the C1–2 facet joints were opened, decorticated, and packed with autograft to promote fusion.

3. Radiographic Assessment

All patients underwent postoperative CT scans for evaluation of occipital bone erosion. Lateral radiographs were also obtained at follow-up visits as clinically indicated. The primary outcome of occipital bone erosion was assessed by CT in 100% of cases (54 of 54 screws). The following parameters were assessed:

(1) C1 upper line: A line connecting the superior borders of the C1 anterior and posterior arches on lateral radiograph (Fig. 1A). Screw heads or rods exceeding this line cranially were classified as C1 upper line transgression.

(1) C1 upper line: A line connecting the superior borders of the C1 anterior and posterior arches on lateral radiograph (Fig. 1A). Screw heads or rods exceeding this line cranially were classified as C1 upper line transgression.

(2) Height O–C1: The minimum distance between the superior border of the C1 posterior arch and the occipital bone, measured on lateral radiographs in neutral position.

(2) Height O–C1: The minimum distance between the superior border of the C1 posterior arch and the occipital bone, measured on lateral radiographs in neutral position.

(3) Height O–C1 extension: The same measurement obtained on lateral radiographs in maximum extension.

(3) Height O–C1 extension: The same measurement obtained on lateral radiographs in maximum extension.

(4) Occipital bone erosion: Defined as visible cortical discontinuity or scalloping of the occipital bone adjacent to the C1 screw or rod.

(5) Breach of inner layer: Complete penetration of the inner cortical table of the occipital bone visible on CT.

4. Statistical Analysis

Primary analysis used per-screw generalized estimating equations (GEE) with an exchangeable working correlation structure and cluster-robust standard errors to account for within-patient clustering. Relative risks (RR) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated.

Continuous variables were expressed as mean±standard deviation and compared using Student t-test with Welch correction. Categorical variables were compared using Fisher exact test. Receiver operating characteristic curves were constructed to determine optimal cutoff values using the Youden index.

Statistical significance was set at p<0.05. All analyses were performed using R ver. 4.3.0 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Austria) with the geepack package.

5. Data Availability Statement

The data that support the findings of this study are available from the corresponding author upon reasonable request.

RESULTS

1. Patient Demographics and Baseline Characteristics

Twenty-seven patients (15 males, 12 females) with a mean age of 71.2±10.8 years underwent C1–2 posterior fixation. The distribution was: Tan technique bilaterally in 18 patients (36 screws), HG technique bilaterally in 6 patients (12 screws), and mixed Tan+HG technique in 3 patients (3 Tan screws+3 HG screws). Mean follow-up was 48.3±18.2 (range, 24–84) months.

Table 1 summarizes baseline characteristics. No significant differences existed in age (70.3±11.2 years vs. 71.9±9.9 years, p=0.642) or follow-up duration (47.8±19.1 months vs. 49.5±16.3 months, p=0.754) between groups.

2. Primary Outcome: Occipital Bone Erosion

Occipital bone erosion occurred in 21 of 54 screws overall (38.8%). The erosion rate was significantly higher with Tan technique: 20 of 39 screws (51.3%) compared to 1 of 15 HG screws (6.7%, p<0.001, Fisher exact test) (Fig. 2A).

In the 3 mixed Tan+HG patients, erosion was observed exclusively on the Tan side (2 of 3 screws) with no erosion on the HG side (0 of 3 screws), providing within-patient evidence for technique-specific risk.

3. Secondary Outcome: Breach of Inner Cortical Layer

Breach of inner cortical layer occurred in 4 of 39 Tan screws (10.3%) and 0 of 15 HG screws (0%, p=0.302) (Fig. 3D). All 4 patients with breach remained asymptomatic during follow-up, and no patient required reoperation for erosion-related complications.

4. C1–2 Fusion Assessment

Radiographic fusion was assessed based on the presence of continuous trabecular bone bridging between C1 and C2 on CT imaging. Overall fusion rate was 92.6% (25 of 27 patients). Two patients showed possible pseudarthrosis with lucency around screws on CT; however, both patients remained clinically stable without symptoms, and no reoperation for pseudarthrosis was required during the follow-up period. Notably, the case shown in Fig. 3B demonstrated solid fusion with no evidence of C2 pedicle screw loosening on CT evaluation.

5. C1 Upper Line as a Critical Risk Factor

C1 upper line transgression was observed in 30 of 39 Tan screws (76.9%) compared to only 1 of 15 HG screws (6.7%) (Fig. 1A–C). Critically, no erosion occurred in any screw (0 of 23) that did not exceed the C1 upper line. Among screws exceeding the C1 upper line, the erosion rate was 67.7% (21 of 31) (Table 2). Multivariate analysis confirmed C1 upper line transgression as an independent risk factor (adjusted RR, 6.82; 95% CI, 2.41–19.31; p<0.001) (Table 3). Given the strong association between technique and C1 upper line transgression (76.9% vs. 6.7%), the adjusted estimates from multivariate GEE analysis should be interpreted with caution. The wide CI for the adjusted RR of Tan technique (1.18–60.43) reflects model instability due to collinearity between these variables.

6. Additional Anatomical Risk Factors

Receiver operating characteristic curve analysis identified optimal cutoff values for anatomical parameters: height O–C1 ≤4.5 mm (sensitivity 72%, specificity 81%, p<0.001) and height O–C1 extension ≤1.0 mm (sensitivity 77%, specificity 84%, p<0.001) (Fig. 2B). These were significant risk factors in multivariate analysis: height O–C1 ≤4.5 mm (RR, 3.81; 95% CI, 1.51–6.28; p=0.01) and height O-C1 extension ≤1 mm (RR, 4.86; 95% CI, 2.05–11.53; p=0.001) (Table 4).

DISCUSSION

This study demonstrates that screw trajectory is the primary determinant of occipital bone erosion following C1–2 posterior fixation. The substantial difference in erosion rates between Tan (51.3%) and HG (6.7%) techniques suggests a technique-associated risk pattern. However, given the retrospective, non-randomized study design, this association should be interpreted with caution. Technique selection was influenced by anatomical considerations and surgeon preference, and several factors closely linked to the technique—such as screw head position relative to the C1 upper line and construct profile—may act as mediators or confounders.

The most significant finding is the identification of the C1 upper line as a critical anatomical landmark. No erosion occurred in any of the 23 screws that remained below this line, regardless of technique. This observation provides a mechanistic explanation for the technique-dependent erosion rates: the Tan technique positions the screw head through the posterior arch, which inherently increases the likelihood of C1 upper line transgression (76.9% of Tan screws vs. 6.7% of HG screws). In contrast, the HG technique’s lateral mass entry point typically results in screw head positioning below the C1 upper line.

While the effect size observed in this study is substantial, we acknowledge that anatomical selection factors and surgeon preference in technique choice limit our ability to draw definitive causal conclusions. The within-patient comparison in mixed Tan+HG cases provides supportive evidence, but the small number of such cases (n=3) precludes firm conclusions.

It is important to note that HG technique did not completely eliminate erosion risk, with 1 of 15 screws (6.7%) developing erosion. This case involved C1 upper line transgression, consistent with our proposed mechanism. The 95% CI for erosion risk with HG technique ranges from approximately 0.2% to 31.9% (exact binomial), indicating that larger studies are needed to precisely quantify the risk.

1. Center-Specific and Construct-Related Factors

The erosion rate of 51.3% observed with the Tan technique in this study is notably higher than what might be expected based on prior case reports, which have described occipital bone erosion primarily as a rare late complication. Several factors may have contributed to this finding.

First, regarding surgical technique: rod protrusion above the C1 posterior arch was consistently minimized to less than 5 mm in all cases, regardless of the technique used (Fig. 3A). Despite this meticulous attention to construct profile, erosion still developed in a substantial proportion of cases. This observation suggests that even well-positioned implants can cause erosion over time when the screw head exceeds the C1 upper line.

Second, our systematic CT surveillance protocol with a minimum 24-month follow-up likely detected subclinical erosions that would be missed with routine radiographic follow-up alone. Importantly, erosion can develop insidiously even when the intraoperative appearance is satisfactory and subsequent plain radiographs show no abnormalities; this is illustrated by the representative case (Fig. 3A–C) where erosion was identified only on CT at 2.5 years postoperatively. This finding has significant clinical implications for postoperative surveillance.

Additionally, the high rate of C1 upper line transgression in our Tan technique cases (76.9%) may reflect anatomical characteristics of our patient population (predominantly elderly patients with age-related anatomical and morphological variations of the craniovertebral junction, such as reduced atlantooccipital interval). These considerations limit the generalizability of our absolute erosion rates, which should be interpreted in the context of our surveillance protocol and patient population. Multicenter studies with standardized measurement protocols are needed to better characterize the true incidence of this complication.

The clinical significance of occipital bone erosion in this cohort was limited, as all patients remained asymptomatic and no reoperation was required. Ikuta et al. [7] reported symptomatic cases presenting with headache that required implant removal. Longer follow-up studies are needed to determine whether progressive erosion eventually leads to clinical symptoms or complications.

2. Study Limitations

Several limitations merit consideration. First, this is a retrospective single-center study with inherent selection bias. The choice of technique was based on surgeon preference and anatomical considerations, not randomization. Second, the sample size is limited, particularly for the HG group (n=15), which affects the precision of risk estimates. The 95% CI for HG erosion rate is wide (0.2%–31.9%), and larger prospective studies are needed. Third, procedures were performed by multiple surgeons using various implant systems, introducing heterogeneity. While this reflects real-world practice, it limits our ability to assess surgeon-specific or implant-specific effects. Fourth, bone quality was not systematically assessed with dual-energy x-ray absorptiometry scanning, and patient-reported outcomes were not collected. Fifth, the follow-up intervals were not standardized, potentially leading to detection bias.

3. Statistical Limitations of the Multivariate Analysis

The interpretation of our multivariate GEE model results (Table 3) requires careful consideration of several statistical and conceptual issues. First, there is strong collinearity between surgical technique and C1 upper line transgression: the majority of Tan screws (76.9%) exceeded the C1 upper line, whereas almost all HG screws (93.3%) did not. Second, no cases of occipital bone erosion were observed among screws that remained below the C1 upper line, resulting in near-complete separation of outcomes. Under these conditions, adjusted RR estimates become unstable, as reflected by the wide CIs (e.g., RR for Tan technique: 1.18–60.43).

Moreover, C1 upper line transgression likely functions as a key mediating variable linking screw trajectory to occipital erosion, rather than an independent confounder. Adjusting simultaneously for both technique and this anatomical variable complicates interpretation and may obscure the true pathway of effect. Therefore, our multivariate results should not be interpreted as evidence of an independent, technique-specific effect, but rather as descriptive associations that require validation in larger datasets. The univariate finding that technique is strongly associated with erosion risk remains clinically meaningful, even if the precise mechanistic pathway cannot be definitively established from this analysis.

Despite these limitations, several strengths support our conclusions: the large effect size (51.3% vs. 6.7%), the within-patient comparison in mixed cases eliminating patient-level confounders, and the clinically adequate follow-up period (mean, 48.3 months).

CONCLUSION

Screw trajectory is the dominant risk factor for occipital bone erosion following C1–2 posterior fixation. The C1 upper line serves as a critical anatomical landmark; no erosion occurred in screws that did not exceed this line. The Tan technique was associated with significantly higher erosion rates (51.3%) compared to the HG technique (6.7%), which may be related in part to the higher rate of C1 upper line transgression. When anatomically feasible, the HG technique may be considered to reduce erosion risk. Further prospective studies with randomized technique allocation are warranted to establish causal relationships.
