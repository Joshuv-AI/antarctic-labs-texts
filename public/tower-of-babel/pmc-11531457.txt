Preparation of transferrin-modified IR820-loaded liposomes and its effect on photodynamic therapy of breast cancer
Authors: Zhang Di, Zhang Shuhe, Shan Baoding, Zhao Yihan, Jin Guangming
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveTo prepare and characterize transferrin (Tf)-modified liposomes (Lipo) encapsulating the photosensitizing agent neoindocyanine green (IR820), and to investigate their effects on breast cancer 4T1 cells as well as in a breast cancer mouse model.MethodsPhotosensitive liposomes, IR820@Lipo and Tf-IR820@Lipo, were synthesized using thin film dispersion, with encapsulation efficiency assessed via UV detection. The physicochemical properties were analyzed using transmission electron microscopy (TEM) and particle size analysis. Uptake by breast cancer 4T1 cells was evaluated through confocal laser scanning microscopy and flow cytometry, while cell proliferation inhibition was measured using the CCK8 assay. Differences in intracellular fluorescence intensity were utilized to assess drug aggregation in vivo through small animal imaging techniques. The anticancer efficacy and potential side effects of the formulations were examined through pharmacodynamic studies conducted in vivo.ResultsThe mean particle sizes of IR820@Lipo and Tf-IR820@Lipo were found to be 84.30 ± 15.66 nm and 116.20 ± 14.68 nm respectively, with zeta potentials of −8.21 ± 2.06 mV for IR820@Lipo and −5.23 ± 1.19 mV for Tf-IR820@Lipo; TEM revealed that the liposomes exhibited a spheroid morphology with uniform distribution across samples; encapsulation efficiencies reached 86.38 ± 0.99% for IR820@Lipo and an impressive 93.81 ± 1.06% for Tf-IR820@Lipo; notably, Tf-IR820@Lipo significantly inhibited proliferation while promoting apoptosis of the 4T1 cells upon laser irradiation; reactive oxygen species (ROS) detection indicated enhanced fluorescence intensity within the treated cells under light exposure when utilizing both formulations; in vivo experiments demonstrated tumor accumulation of both IR820@Lipo and Tf-IR820@Lipo, indicating effective tumor targeting within a breast cancer mouse model; pharmacodynamic assessments revealed that Tf-IR820@Lipo exhibited superior inhibitory effects against breast cancer without causing liver or kidney dysfunctions in mice nor presenting significant toxic side effects overall.ConclusionGiven its high targeting capability, potent efficacy, and low toxicity profile, Tf-IR820@Lipo holds promise as a novel therapeutic agent for breast cancer treatment when combined with photodynamic therapy (PDT), potentially offering new avenues for patient management.

Introduction

According to the latest survey conducted by the National Cancer Center, breast cancer remains the most prevalent cancer among women in China [1]. In China, due to its large population base, both the incidence and mortality rates of breast cancer are among the highest globally [2]. Currently, breast cancer treatment in China has developed into a comprehensive system that includes traditional chemoradiotherapy, targeted therapy, endocrine immunotherapy, and emerging "precision medicine" approaches [3–5]. However, despite these advancements, breast cancer mortality rates remain high due to variations in patient awareness and disparities in medical standards across different regions. Presently, conventional intravenous systemic chemotherapy not only targets cancer cells but also adversely affects normal cells, resulting in significant trauma and side effects for patients. Furthermore, this method does not optimize drug absorption rates effectively. Therefore, there is an urgent need for new therapeutic strategies that offer enhanced targeting capabilities, improved absorption rates, reduced adverse effects, and simplified administration.

Studies have demonstrated that the expression of Transferrin receptor (TfR) on leukemia, lung cancer, liver cancer, and breast cancer cells is significantly elevated compared to normal cells [6–8]. In light of this phenomenon, some researchers have explored the modification of Transferrin (Tf) for use as a carrier for anti-cancer agents. Through the specific binding between Tf and TfR, these agents can be delivered directly to tumor cells, thereby enhancing their targeting efficacy [9]. Our research group’s previous studies indicate that TF-modified docetaxel nanoparticles exhibit markedly improved targeting capabilities against non-small cell lung cancer [10, 11].

Photodynamic therapy (PDT) employs the interaction among photosensitizers, light sources, and oxygen molecules to treat tumors as well as viral and bacterial infections [12–14]. The origins of PDT date back to 1900 when Danish physician Finsen first introduced its concept. Subsequently, in 1913, German biophysicist Raab made significant advancements by discovering rapid photochemical reactions involving dyes. Building upon this foundation, Raab conducted pioneering experiments related to PDT in 1924 [15]. The fundamental principle underlying PDT involves a photosensitizing reaction triggered by stimulation from a photosensitizer; this process generates substantial amounts of reactive oxygen species (ROS), leading to apoptosis or cell death [16, 17]. In contrast, traditional chemotherapy primarily inhibits DNA replication, RNA transcription, and protein synthesis within cancer cells through drug-induced mechanisms [18]. Currently, the combination of PDT with chemotherapy has emerged as a widely adopted minimally invasive treatment modality for various conditions including melanoma and port wine stains as well as senile melasma degeneration [19, 20]. IR820 shares structural similarities with indocyanine green (ICG), which is clinically approved; however IR820 demonstrates greater stability both in vitro and in vivo. When combined with PDT under laser irradiation conditions, IR820 induces oxidative reactions within cells resulting in an increase in intracellular ROS levels beyond cytotoxic thresholds leading ultimately to cell death. In this study we initially prepared IR820@Lipo and Tf-IR820@Lipo photosensitive liposomes followed by characterization. We then investigated their application in conjunction with PDT on breast cancer 4T1 cells using mouse models of localized breast cancer—providing novel insights into potential clinical strategies for treating breast cancer.

The conjugation of transferrin to the phospholipid moiety

A total of 11.915 g of Hydroxyethyl piperazine ethane sulfonic acid (HEPES) was dissolved in a final volume of 50 mL distilled water, and the pH was adjusted to 8 using 0.1 mol/L NaOH. Subsequently, 8.7 mg of DSPE-PEG2000-Mal was dissolved in 5 mL HEPES buffer, followed by the addition of 23 mg transferrin (Tf). The mixture was stirred at room temperature for 24 h under a nitrogen atmosphere. Unreacted components were removed via dialysis, and the purified DSPE-PEG2000-Tf lyophilized cake was obtained through freeze-drying of the dialysate.

Preparation of Tf-modified liposomes

Liposomes were prepared using the thin film dispersion method. A total of 8 mg of DPPC, 7 mg of CHOL, 4 mg of DSPE-PEG2000, and 1 mg of IR820 were accurately weighed and dissolved in 3 mL of trichloromethane. Following ultrasonic dissolution, the trichloromethane was removed via rotary evaporation at 40 °C for 30 min in a water bath, resulting in the formation of a uniform film on the walls of the apparatus while completely evaporating all organic solvents. Subsequently, PBS (pH = 7.4; 2 mL) was added to hydrate the film for one hour before sonication with a probe in an ice-water bath for ten minutes (power: 65 W; ultrasound duration: two seconds; intermittency: two seconds). The mixture was then transferred into a centrifuge tube and centrifuged at 15,000 rpm for thirty minutes to remove unencapsulated drugs, yielding IR820@Lipo.

For another formulation, a total of 8 mg of DPPC, 7 mg of CHOL, 2 mg each of DSPE-PEG2000 and DSPE-PEG2000-Tf along with an additional milligram (1 mg) of IR820 were weighed and dissolved in three milliliters (3 mL) trichloromethane. After ultrasonic dissolution and subsequent removal by rotary evaporation under similar conditions as previously described—resulting again in a uniform film—the hydration process involved adding PBS (2 mL), followed by sonication under identical parameters as outlined earlier to ensure consistency. This mixture was also subjected to centrifugation at fifteen thousand revolutions per minute for thirty minutes to eliminate any unencapsulated drugs ultimately producing Tf-IR820@Lipo.

The TU-1810 ultraviolet spectrophotometer (Beijing Puzi General Instrument Co., Ltd., China) was employed for measurements. Calibration was performed using standard solutions prior to measurement within the wavelength range from 200 to 400 nm ensuring that readings occurred within the maximum absorption spectrum specific to IR820. The sample containing IR820 was placed into a clean sample cell devoid of scratches or stains before initiating spectrophotometric analysis; absorbance readings were recorded accordingly. The concentration of IR820 was subsequently calculated based on its absorbance values alongside known optical path lengths according to Lambert–Beer’s law.

The encapsulation efficiency of IR820 was assessed through ultrafiltration. Stability of Tf-IR820@Lipo

The color and transparency of the Tf-IR820@Lipo solution, along with its particle size, zeta potential, and polydispersity index (PDI), were measured daily over a storage period of seven days at 4 °C.

In vitro drug release from Tf-IR820@Lipo

Tf-IR820@Lipo was dissolved in phosphate buffer and placed in a dialysis bag (MWCO = 10 kDa). The two ends of the bag were securely tied with cotton thread and immersed in 50 mL of phosphate buffer containing 0.1% Tween 80, oscillating at 100 rpm at a temperature of 37 °C for a duration of 72 h. At specified time points (0.5, 1, 2, 4, 8, 12, 24, 36, and 48 h), medium samples (0.5 mL) were withdrawn and replaced with an equal volume of fresh medium. Following the final sampling point, the dialysis bag was cut open and shaken for an additional two hours to facilitate dose calculation. The extracted medium was then centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for ten minutes; drug release was quantified using ultraviolet spectrophotometry to construct a cumulative release curve.

Cell uptake

Coumarin 6 (C6) is a highly efficient, fat-soluble fluorescent dye. An appropriate amount of C6 powder was dissolved in absolute ethanol to prepare a stock solution at a concentration of 1 mg/mL. DPPC (1.6 mg), CHOL (1.4 mg), and DSPE-PEG2000 (0.8 mg) were dissolved in trichloromethane, followed by the addition of 200 µL of the C6 solution (1 mg/mL in anhydrous ethanol) with ultrasonic treatment for 2 min. The resulting film was hydrated with 2 mL of deionized water for 1 h, after which ultrasound was applied in an ice-water bath for 10 min (power: 65 W; ultrasonic duration: 2 s; intermittency: 2 s). The mixture was then transferred into a centrifuge tube and centrifuged at 15,000 rpm for 30 min to remove unencapsulated C6, yielding C6@Lipo. In the subsequent preparation of Tf-C6@Lipo, DSPE-PEG2000-Tf (0.4 mg) replaced DSPE-PEG2000 (0.8 mg), following the same experimental protocol.

Logarithmically growing 4T1 cells were inoculated into a twelve-well plate containing sterile cover slips at a density of 1 × 105 cells per well and cultured at 37 °C with 5% CO₂ for twenty-four hours to promote adherence. After removing the medium, free C6 at a concentration of 10 µg/mL in serum-free RPMI-1640 medium along with C6@Lipo and Tf-C6@Lipo were added for four hours of incubation. Following this period, the medium was discarded; cells were washed three times with cold PBS and fixed using paraformaldehyde solution (4%) for fifteen minutes. Nuclei were stained with DAPI dye solution (500 µL/well; concentration: 1 µg/mL) for fifteen minutes before washing three times with PBS again. Finally, slides were inverted onto anti-fade sealant coverslips, and green fluorescence intensity was measured using confocal laser scanning microscopy (CLSM), capturing images as needed.

Flow cytometry measurements of cell uptake

Logarithmically growing 4T1 cells were inoculated into 12-well plates at a density of 1.2 × 105 cells per well and cultured in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO₂ at 37 °C for 24 h to promote adherence. Following this incubation, the medium was removed, and solutions of C6, C6@Lipo, and Tf-C6@Lipo in serum-free RPMI-1640 medium were added for an additional four hours of incubation. Afterward, the medium was discarded; the cells were washed three times with cold PBS before adding 0.5 mL of pancreatic trypsin in EDTA and incubating for 2–3 min to facilitate trypsinization. The digestion process was terminated by adding 1.5 mL of fresh medium. Subsequently, the cells were centrifuged for 5 min, re-suspended in 0.5 mL PBS, and uptake was measured using flow cytometry.

Proliferation of 4T1 cells (CCK8)

Logarithmically growing 4T1 cells were inoculated into 96-well plates at a density of 8 × 103 cells per well and cultured for 24 h. Free IR820, IR820@Lipo, and Tf-IR820@Lipo were subsequently added to achieve final drug concentrations of 5, 1, 0.5, 0.1, or 0.05 µg/mL with an additional 4 h of incubation. The cells were then exposed to an 808 nm laser (0.5 W/cm2) for 10 min in continuous mode from the top of the plates before being further cultured for 24, 48, and 72 h. Following this period, the old medium was removed; then, 10 µL of CCK-8 solution was added and incubated for an additional half hour to three hours prior to measuring absorbance at a wavelength of 450 nm.

Detection of in vitro reactive oxygen species (ROS)

Logarithmically growing 4T1 cells were inoculated into a 12-well plate containing a sterile 20 mm cover slip at a density of 1 × 105 cells per well and cultured in a humidified atmosphere with 5% CO₂ at 37 °C for 24 h to promote adherence. After this incubation period, the medium was removed, and 0.5 µg/mL IR820 was added to the culture medium, excluding the control group. The cells were then divided into five experimental groups: Control, IR820@Lipo(−)Laser, Tf-IR820@Lipo(−) Laser, IR820@Lipo( +)Laser, and Tf-IR820@Lipo( +)Laser for an additional 4 h of culture. Following this incubation, the medium was discarded; the cells were washed three times with cold PBS before adding a ROS detection probe (20 μmol/L). After 4 h of incubation with the probe, the cells were irradiated with an 808 nm laser (0.5 W/cm2) for 10 min before being observed using confocal laser scanning microscopy (CLSM).

Breast cancer transplanted tumor model and drug distribution in vivo

A stock solution of 0.5 mg/mL was prepared by dissolving DiR in acetonitrile. The powders of DPPC (0.4 mg), CHOL (0.35 mg), and DSPE-PEG2000 (0.2 mg) were accurately weighed and dissolved in chloroform, followed by the addition of 50 µL of the DiR solution, which was sonicated for 2 min to ensure complete dissolution. Trichloromethane was removed via rotary evaporation in a water bath at 40 °C for 30 min, resulting in the formation of a uniform film on the walls of the apparatus while completely evaporating all organic solvents. Subsequently, 2 mL of PBS was added to hydrate the film for 1 h; then, ultrasonic treatment with an ice-water bath was applied for 10 min (power: 65 W; ultrasound duration: 2 s; intermittency: 2 s) to obtain DiR@Lipo. To prepare Tf-DiR@Lipo, DSPE-PEG2000-Tf (0.1 mg) was incorporated into the aforementioned raw materials using the same method.

4T1 cells were trypsinized, and PBS was added to terminate digestion before resuspending and centrifuging them at 1500 r/min for five minutes. Fifteen Balb/c mice were divided into three groups (n = 5 each) and injected subcutaneously with 0.3 mL of a cell suspension containing 1 × 107 cells/mL (approximately 3 × 106 cells) under their right hind leg medial region to establish a subcutaneous tumor model. Once tumor volume reached approximately 120 mm3, free DiR, DiR-carrying liposomes (DiR@Lipo), and TF-modified liposomes carrying DiR (Tf-DiR@Lipo) were administered via tail vein injection while monitoring drug distribution through fluorescence probe detection at a dosage of 50 µg/kg body weight. In vivo drug distribution assessments utilized fluorescent probes within this dosing regimen as per ethical guidelines established by Yanbian University Affiliated Hospital's Ethics Committee; it is noted that maximum allowable tumor size should not exceed 600 mm3. At study conclusion, maximum observed tumor volume measured 568 mm3, remaining compliant with institutional standards.

In vivo pharmacodynamic study of Tf-IR820@Lipo

An additional fifteen Balb/c mice were divided into five groups (n = 3 per group), and a 0.3 mL suspension (~ 3 × 106 cells) was injected into the medial side of the right hind leg of each mouse to establish an orthotopic breast cancer model. When the subcutaneous tumor volume reached approximately 150 mm3, saline, blank liposomes, free IR820, IR820@Lipo, and Tf-IR820@Lipo were administered via tail vein injection at a dosage of 20 mg/kg every seven days for a total of four injections. Following each injection, tumors were immediately exposed to an 808 nm laser (0.5 W/cm2) for 10 min. On the third day after the final dose, mice were euthanized by dislocation under anesthesia in accordance with ARRIVE guidelines published in 2010; sodium pentobarbital was used as anesthetic at a dosage of 20–30 mg/kg body weight. Subsequently, tumor sizes from each group were collected for evaluation and stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E). Additionally, to assess potential toxicity and side effects associated with each treatment preparation on the mice, body weight changes were monitored every three days throughout the dosing period. Finally, serum levels of alanine transaminase (ALT), aspartate transaminase (AST), creatinine (Cr), and blood urea nitrogen (BUN) were measured to evaluate liver and kidney function.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS version 26.0 software. Measurement data are presented as mean ± standard deviation, and comparisons among multiple groups were performed using the t-test. A p-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Detection of reactive oxygen species (ROS)

The ROS detection kit employed in this experiment utilizes a quantitative method to assess the levels of reactive oxygen species in cells, based on fluorescence intensity changes of 2,7-dichlorodihydrofluorescein diacetate (DCFH-DA). DCFH-DA is non-fluorescent and can freely permeate the cell membrane, entering the cytoplasm where it is hydrolyzed by intracellular esterases to yield DCFH. Upon oxidation, DCFH produces 2',7'-dichlorofluorescein (DCF), with green fluorescence intensity being directly proportional to the level of reactive oxygen species within the cell. Consequently, measuring DCF fluorescence allows for quantification of ROS levels in cells. As shown in Fig. 8, no fluorescence was observed in the control group; however, there was a significant enhancement of intracellular green fluorescence intensity in the Tf-IR820@Lipo group. This indicates that following liposome treatment, 4T1 cells exhibited robust ROS production during laser exposure.Fig. 8Detection of ROS. (× 100). In vivo pharmacodynamic study of Tf-IR820@Lipo

The in vivo therapeutic effects of various agents were evaluated based on tumor growth inhibition in orthotopic breast cancer-bearing mice. As illustrated in Fig. 10A, B, no significant difference in tumor growth was observed between the saline group (Control) and the Blank Lipo group, whereas Tf-IR820@Lipo demonstrated a significantly stronger tumor inhibitory effect compared to the other groups. H&E staining of tumor tissues (Fig. 10C) revealed almost no apoptotic cells in both the control and blank Lipo groups, with only a small number of apoptotic cells present in the free IR820 and IR820@Lipo groups; conversely, a substantial number of apoptotic tumor cells were identified in the Tf-IR820@Lipo group. These findings further corroborate that Tf-IR820@Lipo effectively inhibits breast cancer cell growth and induces apoptosis. Discussion

In the process of PDT, a photosensitizer must be introduced into the target tissue, followed by a photosensitivity reaction induced by laser irradiation at specific wavelengths and energy densities, which leads to cell apoptosis and death through the generation of reactive oxygen species (ROS) [21]. However, most photosensitizers have excitation wavelengths in the ultraviolet or visible range; these shorter wavelengths exhibit limited penetration and suboptimal therapeutic effects on lesions located deep within the body. To address this limitation, researchers have developed upconverting nanoparticles with excitation wavelengths situated within the 'optical transparent window' (600–1100 nm), thereby enhancing tissue penetration [22]. The Tf-IR820@Lipo formulation prepared in this study falls within this wavelength range, demonstrating effective therapeutic outcomes for deeply located breast tumors. Furthermore, advancements in light source technology and photosensitizers have been documented in previous studies [23, 24], facilitating ongoing improvements in PDT technology for broader applications. Consequently, PDT is anticipated to become an important modality for treating tumors and other diseases in the future.

The IR820@Lipo and Tf-IR820@Lipo formulations developed in this study exhibited appropriate size, uniform distribution, stable dispersion, and a pronounced sustained release effect, with minimal clearance by the circulatory system. The preparation process incorporates DPPC and DSPE-PEG2000, which impart a negative charge to the liposomes; this negatively charged nature reduces the pH of the aqueous phase within the liposome, creating a pH gradient. Consequently, negatively charged liposomes enter the inner aqueous phase in a molecular state due to this gradient, preventing them from crossing the phospholipid bilayer and returning to the outer aqueous phase. The ammonium sulfate gradient increases between both inner and outer aqueous phases, indirectly enhancing encapsulation efficiency. Furthermore, when PBS solution is utilized as the release medium to assess the slow-release characteristics of negatively charged liposomes, their release behavior aligns with leaky tank conditions since both PBS solution and pH are consistent; thus demonstrating significant slow-release effects. Conversely, the slower release profile of Tf-IR820@Lipo can be attributed to its electrical properties that exhibit greater adsorption compared to target cells possessing opposite charges. This characteristic facilitates gradual drug release while concurrently mitigating toxicity. Additionally, both formulations demonstrated favorable drug loading capacity and encapsulation efficiency alongside superior performance in drug loading capabilities. Coumarin-6 was employed for observing drug entry via laser confocal microscopy; results indicated that Tf-IR820@Lipo achieved high cellular uptake efficiency—resulting in enhanced cytotoxicity against 4T1 cells when delivering anti-tumor agents [25]. This efficacy arises from both IR820@Lipo and Tf-IR820@Lipo featuring an aqueous core enveloped by a phospholipid bilayer capable of simultaneously carrying hydrophilic and lipophilic drugs—thereby facilitating easier entry into 4T1 cells compared to other carriers for effective anticancer action [26].

Currently, several studies have employed IR820 in photodynamic therapy (PDT) for anti-tumor treatment [27, 28]. Under laser irradiation, Tf-IR820@Lipo can enhance intracellular reactive oxygen species (ROS) levels in 4T1 cells, leading to cell death by disrupting the balance of intracellular ROS and exceeding the tolerance threshold of these cells [29]. The CCK-8 assay was utilized to evaluate the inhibitory effects of IR820@Lipo and Tf-IR820@Lipo on 4T1 cell proliferation. Results indicated that all three drug + laser combinations exhibited a certain degree of inhibition on 4T1 cell proliferation, with Tf-IR820@Lipo + laser demonstrating the most potent effect. In vitro ROS detection results revealed that Tf-IR820@Lipo produced high levels of ROS in 4T1 cells under laser irradiation, thereby confirming the efficacy of PDT using photosensitive liposomes and indicating its potential for effectively targeting 4T1 breast cancer cells.

In this study, a mouse model of orthotopic breast cancer was successfully established using 4T1 cells. Tumor growth exhibited a time-dependent relationship consistent with tumor quantity and demonstrated a favorable correlation with drug efficacy. Consequently, the metabolism, distribution, and action of liposomes in mice could be objectively assessed. Figure 9 illustrates varying levels of drug accumulation indicated by color: blue represents lower drug accumulation, red indicates higher accumulation, while negligible fluorescence signifies minimal drug presence. In the Free DiR group, rapid metabolism and limited storage resulted in low drug accumulation in both the intestine and tumors; thus, almost no fluorescence was detected. Conversely, Tf-IR820@Lipo displayed the highest aggregation within tumors, indicating superior targeting capability. The bar chart further confirms that Tf-IR820@Lipo achieved maximal drug accumulation at the tumor site. The in vivo pharmacodynamic study revealed that Tf-IR820@Lipo exerted a significantly stronger inhibitory effect on tumors compared to other groups without evident toxicity or side effects. This enhanced efficacy is attributed to the active targeting properties of Tf-loaded liposomes which preferentially accumulate at tumor sites. When combined with laser irradiation, they can effectively induce an anticancer response while minimizing drug retention in normal tissues and consequently reducing side effects significantly. PDT has emerged as an important alternative treatment for various advanced cancers due to its ability to improve survival rates without compromising patients' quality of life. However, challenges remain such as insufficient light penetration depth limiting therapeutic effectiveness against deep-seated tumors, difficulties in controlling laser parameters, and suboptimal synergy among various chemotherapeutic agents during combination therapies [30, 31]. Accumulating more clinical data will be essential for addressing these challenges moving forward.

Tf-IR820@Lipo is anticipated to emerge as a novel therapeutic agent for breast cancer, owing to its high targeting capability, efficacy, and low toxicity. When combined with PDT, it offers a promising treatment option for patients with advanced breast cancer who are not suitable candidates for surgical intervention, thereby providing new insights into the clinical management of this disease.
