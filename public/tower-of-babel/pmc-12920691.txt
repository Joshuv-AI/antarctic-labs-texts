Prognostic nutritional index and systemic inflammation index as predictors of cirrhosis severity: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Behzad Hatami, Omid Ghanbarpour, Leila Pasharavesh, Amir Hassan Rabani, Toktam Alirezaei, Zahra Geshani, Sahar kalami, Hosein Jafary
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractAim:We assessed whether the Prognostic Nutritional Index (PNI) and the Systemic Inflammation Index (SII) are associated with cirrhosis severity and decompensation.Background:Cirrhosis severity is routinely staged with MELD and Child-Pugh scores, which only partially capture nutritional and inflammatory status. Methods: We conducted a cross-sectional study of 202 cirrhotic adults at Shahid Beheshti University hospitals (2018–2022). Demographic, clinical, and lab data were obtained from records. PNI (10 × albumin + 0.005 × lymphocytes) and SII (platelets × neutrophils ÷ lymphocytes) were calculated. Associations with MELD, Child–Pugh, and complications were analyzed (α = 0.05; SPSS).Results:Higher SII was associated with greater disease severity, including higher MELD (P = 0.040) and higher Child-Pugh class (overall P = 0.010), with SII levels higher in Child-Pugh class C than in class B (P = 0.001). PNI showed an inverse relationship with MELD, consistent with better nutritional status at lower severity. PNI was higher in patients without ascites (P = 0.020). Both indices differed in patients with hepatic encephalopathy versus those without (P = 0.010). Moreover, SII did not differ across variceal grades (P = 0.891), whereas PNI was lower in higher grades (overall P = 0.005).Conclusion:PNI and SII exhibit significant associations with established severity measures in cirrhosis. These indices may complement MELD and Child-Pugh by integrating nutritional and inflammatory information into routine assessment. Prospective, externally validated studies are warranted to determine predictive utility and clinical impact.Key Words: Liver cirrhosis, Nutritional status, Inflammation, Prognosis, Severity of illness index, Predictive value of tests, Ascites, Esophageal varices

Introduction

Cirrhosis is the end stage of chronic liver injury characterized by architectural distortion with diffuse fibrosis and regenerative nodules, leading to portal hypertension, organ dysfunction, and clinically significant decompensation. In routine practice, the Child-Pugh and Model for End-Stage Liver Disease (MELD) scores remain anchors for risk stratification and transplant prioritization; however, these tools only partially capture domains such as malnutrition, frailty, and systemic inflammation that materially influence outcomes in cirrhosis (1-3). Contemporary guidance encourages structured assessment of nutrition and frailty alongside traditional staging and symptom-based management in decompensated disease (1, 4).

Composite indices derived from routine laboratories have therefore garnered interest as pragmatic complements. The Prognostic Nutritional Index (PNI), calculated as 10 × albumin (g/dL) + 0.005 × lymphocyte count (/µL), summarizes nutrition-linked information that reflects hepatic synthetic function and immune competence. The Systemic Immune-Inflammation Index (SII), defined as platelets × neutrophils ÷ lymphocytes (all per µL), intends to capture leukocyte- and platelet-based inflammatory tone. Recent clinical studies associate lower PNI with worse liver function and adverse outcomes in chronic liver disease, including cirrhosis, supporting its face validity as a concise nutrition-linked severity signal (5-7).

By contrast, evidence for SII in cirrhosis is mixed: cohorts enriched for acute-on-chronic liver failure (ACLF) or organ-support settings often report positive associations with short-term prognosis, whereas unselected clinic populations show weak or null relationships (8-10).

Heterogeneity likely reflects both biological and methodological factors. Systemic inflammatory activity fluctuates with intercurrent infection, bleeding, or therapeutic interventions, making a single cross-sectional SII snapshot vulnerable to short-term noise. Studies also differ in sampling frames, settings, assay harmonization, treatment of right-skewed distributions, and confounder adjustment, and relatively few investigations have aligned PNI and SII directly against MELD and Child-Pugh in unselected cohorts using harmonized measurements and prespecified models (1, 2, 9).

In addition, related inflammatory surrogates, such as the neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR) and C-reactive protein (CRP), show variable prognostic value across the spectrum of chronic liver disease, underscoring context-dependent effects and the need for careful validation (11, 12).

To address these gaps, we evaluated whether PNI and SII are associated with established severity anchors (MELD, Child-Pugh) and key decompensation features (ascites, hepatic encephalopathy) in adults with cirrhosis. We hypothesized a priori that lower PNI, indicating poorer nutritional status, and higher SII, indicating greater inflammatory burden, would align with greater disease severity and more advanced decompensation, providing incremental and clinically interpretable context alongside standard staging (1, 2).

Methods

We conducted an observational, analytical cross-sectional study from 2018 to 2022 at Taleghani and Shohada-ye Tajrish tertiary teaching hospitals, affiliated with Shahid Beheshti University of Medical Sciences, Iran. Data from the index clinical evaluation were abstracted from the electronic health record and standardized paper charts.

Adults aged 18 years or older with a clinically ascertained diagnosis of cirrhosis) Based on routine clinical, laboratory, imaging, and/or endoscopic findings, those who presented to inpatient or outpatient services during the accrual period were screened using a convenience sampling approach. Exclusion criteria were prior partial hepatectomy, known hematologic disorders, severe active infection at the index evaluation, and refusal to participate; patients returning solely for short-interval follow-up (≤30 days) of a prior index visit were not eligible for a new index entry. In total, 202 participants met eligibility criteria and were included in the analysis.

Collected variables included demographics (age, sex), cirrhosis etiology, and routine laboratory values (complete blood count with differential, ALT, AST, ALP, prothrombin time/INR, total bilirubin, creatinine, sodium, albumin). Disease severity was assessed using the Model for End-Stage Liver Disease (standard formula) and the Child-Pugh classification (ascites, encephalopathy, bilirubin, albumin, INR).

Decompensation complications at the index evaluation were recorded as present or absent and included ascites (clinical or imaging criteria), spontaneous bacterial peritonitis (defined as ≥250 polymorphonuclear cells per µL in ascitic fluid [equivalent to per mm³]), gastrointestinal variceal bleeding, and hepatic encephalopathy according to West Haven criteria. Esophageal varices were graded F1–F3 by endoscopy according to standard guidelines.

The Prognostic Nutritional Index was calculated as 10 × albumin (g/dL) + 0.005 × lymphocyte count (per µL), and the Systemic Inflammation Index as platelets × neutrophils ÷ lymphocytes, with all counts expressed per µL. Laboratory tests were taken from results obtained on the same day as the index evaluation or, if unavailable, the nearest value within 7 days. Units were harmonized a priori (e.g., albumin in g/dL, transaminases in IU/L, cell counts per µL), and data underwent range checks, cross-field validation, and source-record verification of implausible values.

Counts and laboratory units were harmonized a priori; implausible or internally inconsistent items triggered verification workflows, and unresolved entries were temporarily flagged as “NA” until re-abstracted from source records.

All eligible patients accrued during the study period were and included in the analysis (N = 202). We acknowledge potential selection bias from convenience sampling and discuss its implications for generalizability. To mitigate information bias, standardized variable definitions were used, endoscopic grades were abstracted from specialist reports, and double-check procedures were applied for key outcomes. Given the cross-sectional design, reverse causation—where disease severity may influence the indices—remains possible.

Continuous variables are presented as mean (SD) or median (IQR), as appropriate, and categorical variables as counts (%). Group comparisons used the t-test or Mann–Whitney U for two groups and ANOVA or Kruskal–Wallis for multiple groups; for ordered severity classes, linear trend was assessed across Child-Pugh (A–C) and predefined MELD categories using linear contrasts after ANOVA or the Jonckheere–Terpstra test when assumptions were not met. Correlations used Pearson or Spearman, depending on the distributional assumptions.

Right-skewed variables, including the Systemic Inflammation Index, were inspected and, when appropriate, log-transformed; primary analyses treated predictors continuously, and prespecified categorical cut-offs were explored in sensitivity analyses. Multivariable models included linear regression for MELD (continuous), ordinal logistic regression for Child-Pugh class, and binary logistic regression for complications (ascites, hepatic encephalopathy, variceal bleeding). Candidate covariates comprised age, sex, cirrhosis etiology, body mass index, sodium, bilirubin, creatinine, INR, albumin, and use of diuretics or non-selective beta-blockers; linearity, multicollinearity (variance inflation factors), goodness-of-fit, and residual diagnostics were assessed.

Missingness was quantified; primary analyses used a complete-case approach. Sensitivity analyses included repeating models with the log-transformed Systemic Inflammation Index and excluding cases with high covariate missingness, with results compared with the primary estimates; where applicable, multiple imputation by chained equations was performed. Effect sizes are reported with 95% confidence intervals and two-sided α = 0.05. Multiplicity in secondary analyses was controlled using the Benjamini–Hochberg procedure. Analyses were performed with IBM SPSS Statistics v26. The protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of Shahid Beheshti University of Medical Sciences (Written informed consent was obtained from all participants; the study was observational and not a clinical trial).

Correlations of PNI and SII with MELD

In contrast, PNI showed a downward shift with increasing MELD; one-way ANOVA indicated a significant global difference (P = 0.046), driven primarily by the <10 vs >15 comparison (unadjusted P = 0.041; Table 5). Overall, PNI (rather than SII) aligned more closely with MELD-defined severity in this cohort; pairwise findings are exploratory and should be interpreted with appropriate caution.

Across Child-Pugh classes (A–C), SII did not differ at the global level (P = 0.130). By contrast, PNI varied significantly across classes (P = 0.001), with lower values in more advanced classes. In unadjusted post-hoc comparisons, PNI was lower in class C than in class A and also differed between classes A and B, whereas the contrast between classes B and C was not significant. No pairwise differences were observed for SII.

Global comparisons showed that SII did not differ significantly across Child-Pugh classes (Appendix Table 1), with all pairwise comparisons yielding non-significant results (Appendix Table 2). In contrast, PNI varied significantly among classes, with unadjusted analyses showing differences between class A and both classes B and C, while no significant difference was observed between classes B and C (Appendix Table 2). This pattern is illustrated in Appendix Figure 1, where PNI distributions shift with advancing disease severity, whereas SII values remain comparatively stable.

Correlation analyses further indicated that Child-Pugh scores correlated only weakly with SII (r = 0.185, r² = 3.4%) but showed a stronger, inverse association with PNI (r = −0.346, r² = 12.0%), suggesting that PNI aligns more closely with Child-Pugh–defined severity in this cohort (Appendix Table 3).

When stratified by ascites status, SII did not differ significantly between patients with and without ascites (P = 0.400; Appendix Table 4). By contrast, PNI was markedly lower in patients with ascites (P < 0.001; Appendix Table 4), consistent with impaired nutritional status in decompensated disease. Further analysis by ascites grade showed no global differences in SII (P = 0.592; Appendix Table 5), whereas PNI values declined significantly with increasing severity (P = 0.044), with the strongest separation observed between severe and mild ascites. These findings reinforce the interpretation that nutritional compromise, as reflected by PNI, rather than systemic inflammatory burden, more closely tracks with the presence and severity of ascites in cirrhotic patients (Appendix Tables 4–5).

Box-and-whisker visualizations demonstrated distinct patterns for ascites status and severity. For ascites status (present vs absent), systemic inflammatory index (SII) did not differ significantly between groups (P = 0.400; Appendix Figure 2, Appendix Table 4), whereas prognostic nutritional index (PNI) was markedly lower in patients with ascites (P < 0.001; Appendix Figure 2, Appendix Table 4).

When stratified by ascites severity (mild, moderate–severe, severe), global comparisons again revealed no significant variation in SII (P = 0.592; Appendix Figure 3, Appendix Table 5). By contrast, PNI values declined significantly with increasing severity (global P = 0.044), with the biggest unadjusted difference observed between mild and severe ascites (Appendix Table 6). Other pairwise contrasts did not reach significance (Appendix Table 6).

Taken together, these findings indicate that nutritional status, as captured by PNI, rather than systemic inflammatory burden (SII), more closely reflects both the presence and the clinical severity of ascites in this cohort. Analyses involving the very small SBP-positive subgroup (n = 3) were exploratory and limited to descriptive comparisons.

In analyses of spontaneous bacterial peritonitis (SBP), neither SII nor PNI differed significantly between SBP-positive and SBP-negative patients (P = 0.078 and P = 0.165, respectively; Appendix Table 7, Appendix Figure 4). The very small size of the SBP-positive subgroup (n = 3) limits the interpretability of these results, and estimates should therefore be regarded as exploratory.

By contrast, significant differences were observed according to variceal status. Patients with esophageal varices exhibited lower SII values compared with those without varices (P = 0.035) and markedly reduced PNI values (P < 0.001), indicating greater nutritional compromise in the variceal group (Appendix Table 8).

SII values did not differ significantly across variceal grades (P = 0.891; Appendix Table 9). By contrast, PNI varied significantly by grade (P = 0.005), with patients in grade F3 showing lower mean values than those in grades F1 and F2, consistent with greater nutritional compromise in more advanced varices (Appendix Table 9).

Pairwise comparisons further supported this pattern: no significant contrasts were observed for SII, whereas only the comparison between F1 and F3 reached unadjusted significance for PNI (Appendix Table 10). These findings suggest that nutritional status, as reflected by PNI, but not systemic inflammatory burden (SII), declines with increasing variceal grade.

Discussion

In this cross-sectional cohort of 202 adults with cirrhosis, PNI aligned more consistently measures. Across MELD categories, PNI differed level and was lowest in the highest MELD stratum, whereas SII did not show significant global differences. Correlation analyses were directionally concordant—PNI correlated inversely with MELD, while SII showed a weak, non-significant positive correlation. Appendix results further indicated that PNI, rather than SII, correlated with Child-Pugh class and the presence and severity of ascites, and that PNI was lower among patients with esophageal varices. Although effect sizes were modest and not uniform across subgroups, the overall pattern supports PNI as a more stable cross-sectional marker of disease burden in unselected cirrhosis.

These findings are consistent with recent reports associating lower PNI with worse liver function and outcomes in chronic liver disease, including cirrhosis (5-7). In contrast, the performance of SII appears context dependent. Studies in highly inflamed settings such as ACLF or artificial liver support report associations between higher SII and short-term prognosis, while general ambulatory cohorts often show attenuated or null relationships, mirroring our observations and reinforcing the biological plausibility of case-mix–dependent inflammatory signal (8-10).

Broader work on systemic inflammation in acutely decompensated cirrhosis suggests dynamic “waves” of inflammatory activity that single biomarkers may capture incompletely, especially outside high-inflammation contexts (13, 14).

Within current practice frameworks, Child-Pugh and MELD remain essential anchors for severity assessment and triage; nevertheless, guidance emphasizes that malnutrition, frailty, and systemic inflammation materially shape trajectories from compensation to decompensation (1, 2). In this context, PNI can pragmatically enrich the interpretation of MELD and Child-Pugh by highlighting nutrition-linked risk already embedded in routine testing, particularly when MELD is modest or when clinical concern for frailty is high. Our results align with recent evidence that sarcopenia and frailty independently predict adverse outcomes in cirrhosis, supporting the clinical logic of adding a nutrition-weighted composite alongside standard scores (15, 16).

SII also may still carry value in high-inflammatory case mixes, but our data and the broader literature suggest limited robustness as a general-purpose correlate in routine outpatient care (8, 9).

Several considerations qualify inference. Cross-sectional sampling precludes temporal ordering between changes in PNI or SII and subsequent decompensation; longitudinal designs are needed to test whether intra-individual changes in PNI or SII track risk. Right-skew, particularly for SII, and potential measurement variability can attenuate associations despite transformation and sensitivity analyses. Residual confounding may persist despite prespecified covariates, and small subgroups (for example, spontaneous bacterial peritonitis) limit precision. Finally, heterogeneity by etiology and medication exposure may contribute to between-study differences, as seen in the variable performance of other inflammatory surrogates such as NLR and CRP (11, 12).

Clinically, our results argue for a complementary role of PNI, not a replacement for MELD or Child-Pugh. A low PNI in patients with borderline MELD or ambiguous trajectories may prompt earlier nutritional assessment, dietetic referral, or frailty-oriented interventions, consistent with guidance for comprehensive care in decompensated cirrhosis (1, 4). SII may be most informative in acutely inflamed contexts such as ACLF, where inflammatory burden is a dominant short-term driver of outcomes (8, 10).

Future work should move from association to clinical utility. Prospective, adequately powered, multicenter cohorts spanning compensated and decompensated states should test whether PNI and SII improve discrimination and calibration over MELD and Child-Pugh, with prespecified confounder adjustment, non-linear modeling as needed, principled handling of missing data, and internal as well as external validation. Decision-analytic approaches (including decision-curve analysis and net reclassification) can quantify whether adding PNI or SII changes management in ways that improve outcomes. Head-to-head comparisons with readily available markers, such as albumin alone, the neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio, and C-reactive protein, will define the incremental value. Longitudinal designs should evaluate whether within-patient changes in PNI or SII track decompensation risk, respond to nutritional or anti-inflammatory interventions, and support reproducible thresholds across laboratories and patient subgroups (1, 2, 9, 14).

Conclusion

PNI showed a more consistent cross-sectional relationship with MELD-defined severity and complementary decompensation markers in our cohort, whereas SII showed weaker, less consistent associations. These findings support PNI as a pragmatic, nutrition-linked complement to established scores in routine assessment. Whether integrating PNI or SII into care pathways improves decisions and outcomes will require rigorous prospective validation and decision-focused evaluation across etiologies and settings (1-3, 5-10, 16).
