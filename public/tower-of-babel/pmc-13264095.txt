Translation-driven design: development of an antibiotic-loaded hydrogel for the management of orthopaedic device-related infection
Authors: Claudia Siverino, Lena Gens, Pamela Nylund, Andrew L. Foster, Willemijn Boot, Dominic Gehweiler, Mats Bue, Stephan Zeiter, Andrea Montali, R. Geoff Richards, Matteo D'Este, T. Fintan Moriarty
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Orthopedic device‐related infection (ODRI) is a devastating complication, often requiring repeated revision surgeries and prolonged antibiotic treatment. Injectable biodegradable biomaterials that deliver and maintain high local concentrations of antibiotics to the surgical field may eliminate the need for additional surgeries while improving treatment outcomes. In this study, we introduce a Gel for Delivery of Antibiotics (GEDAI) for local administration of tobramycin (T), gentamicin (G) or gentamicin-vancomycin (GV). GEDAI was developed with translatability and ease of intraoperative handling as design goals. GEDAI displayed storage and loss moduli, shear thinning, and elastic recovery properties appropriate for intraoperative applications, with good adhesion to stainless steel and tissues but reduced adhesion to surgical gloves. In vitro assays showed no adverse effects of the GEDAI on viability of fibroblasts, macrophages and endothelial cells, or osteogenic differentiation of human bone marrow stromal cells (hBMSCs). Methicillin susceptible and resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MSSA and MRSA), growing in planktonic culture or biofilm, were significantly reduced after exposure to the antibiotic loaded-GEDAI compared to GEDAI without antibiotics (p < 0.05). In three distinct in vivo studies in sheep, GEDAI-T reached a peak local concentration above 2000 μg/mL within 2 h and substantially reduced bacterial counts in two models with implant retention (GEDAI-T or GEDAI-G), and completely eradicated infection in an implant exchange model (GEDA-GV). GEDAI directly targets two unmet needs in ODRI: achieving bactericidal local concentrations without systemic toxicity and providing a ready-to-use formulation. The antibiotic-loaded GEDAI formulations promise to improve treatment outcomes in challenging infection cases, potentially reducing healthcare costs and patient burden in postoperative infection management.

Introduction

Musculoskeletal trauma is a major contributor to global morbidity. Although injuries such as bone fractures are successfully managed and heal over time, complications such as fracture-related infections (FRI) are common, affecting an estimated 1.8 million people each year [1]. Infections can lead to prolonged recovery, multiple surgeries, and significant long-term impairment impacting quality of life [[2], [3], [4]]. The overall infection rate following internal fixation can reach up to 43% for open fractures with extensive soft tissue injury [1], with Staphylococcus aureus being the most common causative pathogen. Infections with antibiotic resistant bacteria, such as methicillin-resistant S. aureus (MRSA), have limited treatment options and reduced treatment efficacy [[5], [6], [7]]. The WHO reports a global median antimicrobial resistance (AMR) level of 35% [8]. The challenge of AMR infection will continue to complicate the management of orthopedic device related infections (ODRI) in the coming years.

The management and outcome of ODRI are largely dictated by the antibiotic tolerant biofilms that form on the implants, since systemic antibiotic treatment alone is insufficient to eradicate them [[9], [10], [11], [12], [13]]. The principles behind ODRI management generally include removal and excision of dead tissue and non-viable material, ensuring stable bone fixation, dead-space management and sufficient soft tissue coverage, as well as antimicrobial therapy [14,15]. A major variable, impacting both patients and healthcare costs, is the number of surgical procedures required to manage the infection. Debridement, antimicrobial therapy, and implant retention (DAIR) is often the preferred treatment approach whenever possible, as it is associated with fewer invasive procedures and shorter hospital stays [[16], [17], [18], [19]]. However, the incidence of treatment failure associated with implant retention (DAIR) is higher than that observed with implant removal, particularly when patient selection criteria are not carefully considered. Implant exchange may be performed in a single step (direct exchange of implants) or in a 2-stage exchange (removal, followed by a temporary treatment period prior to placement of a new implant). Reducing the number of surgical procedures whilst maintaining or improving eradication rates would represent a significant advancement in alleviating the challenges posed by ODRI, especially in AMR cases.

Local antibiotic therapy has become increasingly popular due to its benefits of providing higher concentrations directly to the surgical field, without significant systemic exposure [20]. Polymethyl methacrylate (PMMA) bone cement has traditionally been the most widely used biomaterial for this purpose in orthopedic surgery [21], although as a non-degradable material, it requires subsequent surgical removal. Alternatives to PMMA bone cements include bone void fillers made from calcium sulfate or calcium phosphate (±hydroxyapatite) that are also loaded with antibiotics, which show comparable efficacy to PMMA in terms of host tissue response, and managing ODRI [[22], [23], [24], [25], [26], [27]]. Antibiotic-loaded bone void fillers are primarily intended for bone regeneration rather than for antibiotic delivery, and there is therefore an important gap in the clinical portfolio for a custom-designed, dedicated antibiotic delivery vehicle that does not have a primary component (i.e., bone substitute) that is not required in the management of the infection.

Hydrogels have several potential benefits as purpose-built vehicles for local antibiotic delivery for the treatment of ODRI. Handling of an appropriate viscosity gel allows widespread distribution within the surgical site (e.g., intramedullary canal and soft tissues) facilitating antibiotic delivery and application in any wound regardless of implant or tissue damage. Furthermore, the release kinetics of the antibiotics from hydrogel can be adjusted based on the hydrogel properties. Avoiding the use of bone substitutes eliminates unnecessary components from the formulation, leaving only a rapidly biodegradable hydrogel after antibiotic release. In the context of ODRI, an easy-to-apply hydrogel delivering high doses of antibiotics to the surgical field after a thorough debridement would promise improved treatment outcomes beyond what is achievable with systemic antibiotics alone. A number of antibiotic loaded hydrogels have been described in the literature, using natural or synthetic polymers [28,29], loaded with different antibiotics [[30], [31], [32], [33], [34]] and used to treat bone infections in small and large animal models [[35], [36], [37], [38]]. However, none of these have been specifically designed with translation in mind or thoroughly tested in large animal models of ODRI, which is crucial for achieving robust preclinical efficacy and ensuring successful clinical translation.

The Gel for Delivery of Antibiotics (GEDAI) is an injectable hydrogel-based platform designed to specifically address the challenging scenario of ODRI, based on clinician input and antibiotic stewardship principles. The antibiotic formulations investigated in this study were selected to reflect clinically relevant concentrations commonly used in local antibiotic delivery systems for orthopaedic infections, particularly for antibiotics targeting Staphylococcus species such as gentamicin, tobramycin, and vancomycin [[39], [40], [41]]. The GEDAI was designed to: (i) deliver an immediate high-peak local antibiotic dose; (ii) resorb without a second surgery; (iii) offer ease of handling and application, ensuring it adheres to tissue and stainless steel, but not to surgical gloves; (iv) suit any anatomical site or implant type; (v) avoid fluid accumulation and wound healing delays; (vi) minimize the total antibiotic load, while maximizing short-term local concentrations.

This manuscript presents a translation-driven approach to the development of GEDAI, from formulation and comprehensive in vitro characterization to evaluation of its antimicrobial efficacy in clinically relevant large-animal models. The in vitro work included assessment of the hydrogel's physicochemical properties, rheological behavior, antibiotic release kinetics, antimicrobial activity, and biocompatibility across multiple relevant cell types. These studies were designed to ensure both functional performance and biological compatibility prior to in vivo evaluation. The subsequent testing in large-animal models incorporating human implants and clinically relevant surgical procedures enables assessment of the hydrogel under conditions that closely reflect clinical practice, supporting its potential for translation.

Results

Physicochemical properties, viscoelastic profile and dispersed phase stability of GEDAI

GEDAI is a stable oil in water emulsion-gel, in which the continuous phase is constituted by an isotonic water-based solution and the dispersed phase is made by a medical grade oil. Stabilizers and surfactants are also based on excipients with a consolidated track history of use in products approved for parenteral use in humans. Given the high water solubility and low affinity to hydrophobic solvents of all antibiotics investigated in this study [[40], [41], [42]], tobramycin sulfate (T), gentamicin sulfate (G) and vancomycin (V) are expected to partition in the aqueous phase of the emulsion rather than interact with the dispersed oil phase.

The unique adhesion and handling properties of GEDAI arise from its emulsion formulation. Although all components of GEDAI individually have a long history of use in medicinal products and medical devices, their incorporation into an emulsion requires a full characterization of the dispersed phase, which is the main determinant for its safety, stability, and the elution properties of the contained antibiotic.

The stability of the GEDAI emulsions prepared through sonication was evaluated by observing phase separation. The emulsions demonstrated phase stability, showing no signs of creaming or sedimentation. Microscopy did not reveal any increase in coalescence or Ostwald ripening of smaller droplet sizes (Fig. S1 A and B). Zeta potential, hydrodynamic diameter (HD) and its polydispersity were analyzed by measuring two independent samples in duplicate. The measured zeta potential was well below −30 mV for all batches, indicative of a stable dispersion (Table S1). The hydrodynamic diameter was 557 ± 76 nm, with a polydispersity index of 0.084 ± 0.054 (Table S2).

The rheological characterization of GEDAI and its various antibiotic-loaded formulations (GEDAI-T, GEDAI-G, and GEDAI-GV) is presented in Fig. 1. All the formulations display an initial linear viscoelastic region (LVE) as shown by the amplitude sweep indicating storage (G′) and loss modulus (G″) as functions of shear strain (γ) (Fig. 1A). The LVE designates the range of deformations in which the internal structure of the soft material is not disrupted, responses are reversible, and therefore the stress-strain relationship is linear [43]. Due to these characteristics, this small deformation range is typically investigated in oscillatory rheology to consistently compare the values of the viscoelastic shear moduli. The LVEs values are illustrated in Fig. 1B. GEDAI exhibits a storage modulus and loss modulus of approximately 340 Pa and 210 Pa, respectively (Table S3), with a damping factor (G"/G′) of 0.62 and a LVE up to about 12.7% strain (Table S3). The addition of 1% G and 2% T results in increased storage and loss moduli, with values of G' = 410 ± 1 Pa and G" = 235 ± 1 Pa for GEDAI-G, and G' = 447 ± 7 Pa and G" = 234 ± 3 Pa for GEDAI-T. The damping factors are approximately 0.57 and 0.52, respectively. When 1% G and 4% V are added, the viscoelastic behavior of the gel is significantly affected, leading to granule formation and an increase in storage modulus by more than one order of magnitude compared to the other formulations (G' = 11417 ± 2744 Pa). The loss modulus also increased, albeit less dramatically (G" = 2741 ± 753 Pa), resulting in a damping factor of around 0.24 for GEDAI-GV. The variability in measurements for this formulation reflects its granular texture, which was observed only in the GEDAI-GV formulation and not in the GEDAI-G and GEDAI-T, both of which remained macroscopically homogeneous. The granular appearance likely reflects the presence of solid-like domains within the hydrogel matrix, resulting from the higher loading level of this antibiotic. The strain step test, where the material is subjected to alternating low (within the LVE) and high (beyond the LVE) strains, show that all formulation exhibit a predominance of G″ at high strain, while demonstrating good recovery of properties previously observed at low strain (Fig. 1C). This trend was also noted in the formulations containing V and G. The viscosity curves indicate that all formulations are shear thinning (Fig. 1D), with viscosities of approximately 550, 760, 900, and 7125 Pas at a shear rate of 0.1 s−1 for GEDAI, GEDAI-G, GEDAI-T, and GEDAI-GV, respectively. At a higher shear rate of 100 s−1, viscosities drop to around 2 Pas for GEDAI, GEDAI-G, and GEDAI-T, and approximately 30 Pas for GEDAI-GV. It is important to note that the viscosity results for V-containing formulations are influenced by their granular texture, so these values should not be considered absolute.

Fig. 1E and Fig. S2 show the rheological tack test curves for all the formulations with metal top and bottom plate and the tack curve for GEDAI-T, GEDAI-G and GEDAI-GV with latex-free nitrile glove or leather on the top plate (Fig. S3). When the metal or nitrile surface is used on the top plate, all formulations exhibit cohesive failure at around 1 mm (Fig. 1E). The measured work when metal/metal is used is around 1 mJ (Fig. 1F–Table S2). However, when covering the top plate with a nitrile glove the measured work decreased to 0. 578 ± 0.045, 0.940 ± 0.099 and 0.305 ± 0.251 mJ for GEDAI-G, GEDAI-T and GEDAI-GV respectively, indicating reduced tackiness (Table S2). In contrast, when utilizing leather to simulate human tissues, the cohesiveness increases, resulting in improved tack, as indicated by the larger area under the curve (Fig. 1E) and the higher work values of 7.21 ± 2.76, 2.02 ± 0.46 4.29 ± 2.89 mJ for GEDAI-G, GEDAI-T and GEDAI-GV respectively (Table S4). These findings confirm that GEDAI meets the intra-operative handling design goal of preferentially adhering to tissues and metal rather than surgical gloves. To further investigate the reduced adhesion observed on glove materials, ATR-FTIR analysis of the glove surface before and after contact with GEDAI was performed (Fig. S4). ATR-FTIR spectra revealed clear differences in the fingerprint region after contact with GEDAI, suggesting partial coverage of the glove surface by gel components.

GEDAI had no effect on any of the analyzed genes involved in osteogenic differentiation (Fig. 2B). In general, the gene expression of the GEDAI were comparable to those of the Osteo control.

Additionally, the effect of the antibiotic-loaded GEDAI formulations was tested on the hTERT-BJ1 over 7 days (Fig. S5A) and compared to GEDAI without antibiotic. The GEDAI-T did not affect cell viability from day 1 to day 7. In contrast, GEDAI-G had no impact on cell viability on day 1, but a significant decrease was observed by days 3 and 7 compared to GEDAI (p < 0.0001). The decrease in cell viability was more pronounced for the GEDAI-GV compared to the GEDAI, with less than 50% of cells being viable on day 1 (p < 0.0001) but also from the visible signs of cells' distress and death (Fig. S5A, GEDAI-GV). The microscopic images of cells after 3 days of culture (Fig. S5B) show comparable cell viability and morphology of GEDAI, GEDAI-T and GEDAI-G compared to control with residues of released GEDAI products appearing as lipid droplets. The morphology of cells exposed to GEDAI-GV showed visible signs of cells' distress and death (Fig. S5B). The antibiotic-loaded GEDAI formulation (GEDAI-G and GEDAI-GV) led to a decrease in osteogenic differentiation (Fig. S5C). This decline is primarily associated with the high concentrations of antibiotics released into the wells, rather than the effect of the GEDAI itself. In vitro studies showed that the osteogenic differentiation of hBMSCs in the presence of different antibiotic concentrations (1, 100, and 1000 μg/mL of T, G and G/V) resulted in compromised mineralization, as measured by ARS staining (Fig. S5D).

Sustained release of antibiotics from GEDAI while preserving antimicrobial activity

The in vitro release of antibiotics from the GEDAI was evaluated over an 8-day period (Fig. 3). The antibiotics T, G, and V were completely released from the GEDAI within the first 48 h (Fig. 3A). In contrast, G and T mixed with phosphate buffer saline (PBS) were fully released within the first 6 h (Fig. 3B). For V, a slower release was noted in both the GEDAI-GV and PBS-GV conditions compared to the other antibiotic-loaded formulations.

The antimicrobial activity of the released antibiotics was tested against methicillin-susceptible S. aureus (MSSA) (Fig. 3C) and methicillin-resistant S. aureus (MRSA) (Fig. 3D). All antibiotics released from the GEDAI formulations exhibited antimicrobial activity against both strains for up to 48 h. Both GEDAI-T and GEDAI-G maintained a consistent zone of inhibition (ZOI) for the first 48 h. In the control groups, PBS-T and PBS-G, the rapid release of antibiotics within the first 6 h resulted in no detectable ZOI beyond that time. For V, the antimicrobial activity of GEDAI-GV and PBS-GV against MRSA showed a prolonged ZOI overtime (Fig. 3D). The ZOI for GEDAI-GV decreased by 168 h, while the ZOI for PBS-GV, in contrast to the other PBS controls, remained prolonged until 48 h.

The antibiotic-loaded GEDAI demonstrates a significant reduction in bacterial viability in both planktonic cultures and biofilms

We also investigated the antimicrobial activity of antibiotic-loaded GEDAI in vitro. Planktonic cultures of MSSA and MRSA were exposed to either the antibiotic-loaded GEDAI or their corresponding antibiotics in PBS. The use of GEDAI-T and GEDAI-G against MSSA (Fig. 4A) and GEDAI-GV against MRSA (Fig. 4B) resulted in a significant reduction in bacterial counts after 24 h of exposure (p ≤ 0.05). In addition, we tested the antimicrobial activity of the antibiotic-loaded GEDAI against a 48 h biofilm grown on a titanium disc (Fig. 4C and D). The biofilms of MSSA and MRSA were exposed for 24 h to different formulations of the antibiotic-loaded GEDAI, GEDAI alone, or were left untreated. Local application of GEDAI-T in an in vivo model of plating osteosynthesis FRI managed with classical DAIR reduces infection burden

The FRI plating osteosynthesis sheep model was established by inoculating 106 colony forming unit (CFU)/animal of MSSA over the 2 mm defect area (Fig. 5A and B). After 2 weeks the wound was debrided and irrigated with saline (Fig. 5C) and samples were collected for bacteriological quantification. During revision surgery, microdialysis probes were used to measure local antibiotic tobramycin concentration after GEDAI-T injection (Fig. 5D). The tobramycin levels reached their peak concentration (2875 ± 2406 μg/mL) over the plate within the first 2 h after application. In contrast, the highest concentration in the subcutaneous tissue (Sub. T.) (1992 ± 526 μg/mL) was observed at approximately 4 h post-application (Fig. 5E). Subsequently, tobramycin levels gradually declined, reaching levels below the Minimum Inhibitory Concentration (MIC, - EUCAST reported MIC breakpoint for S. aureus of 2 mg/L) at 48 h. The levels of tobramycin found in the bone's intramedullary canal (IMC) remained detectable, although minimal. It is important to note that the osteotomy is refreshed during revision surgery for the animals receiving IML and IML + GEDAI-G to facilitate proper irrigation of the IMC and injection of the gel, hence those animals started with less callus than the controls. Bone density gradually increases over time in all groups without any significant difference between the groups (Fig. 6H). Taken together, the data indicates that GEDAI-G does not hinder fracture healing and that once the infection is cleared, callus formation is accelerated.

Radiographic analysis revealed qualitative differences between groups at the end of the study (Fig. S10). SOC animals showed radiographic features suggestive of persistent infection, including osteolysis around screws adjacent to the osteotomy site. In contrast, such features were not observed in the IML or IML + GEDAI-G groups, which exhibited preserved bone morphology and stable implant positioning. No quantitative radiographic assessment was performed, and the findings are descriptive. Histological analysis showed more advanced trabecular bone formation in SOC animals compared with the IML and IML + GEDAI-G groups (Fig. S11). This difference is consistent with defect refreshing during revision surgery in the IML-based groups. In both IML and IML + GEDAI-G groups, a visible defect remained and was partially filled with dark azure-blue stained cartilage-like tissue, indicative of ongoing endochondral repair, with no qualitative differences observed between the two treatment groups.

Discussion

Local antibiotic delivery is an increasingly recognized option in the management of ODRI. The use of local antibiotics is recommended in a prophylactic setting, particularly in high-risk patients [45], however, equivalent clinical evidence is lacking for their role in the treatment of infection. To provide a solution for local antibiotic delivery, custom-designed for management of ODRI, we have developed an emulsion-based hydrogel platform. Early in the development phase, we conducted a survey among orthopedic trauma surgeons and infection management specialists to understand their preferences for local antibiotic applications. The feedback (unpublished) revealed a strong preference for hydrogel-based delivery systems over alternatives such as implant coatings or antibiotic powders. Surgeons emphasized the need for formulations that are easy to handle, do not stick to gloves in wet conditions, and can be effortlessly applied in the operating room in any anatomical location. These insights directly shaped the design criteria of GEDAI, which prioritizes safety, stability, usability, pharmacokinetics, manufacturability, and handling properties.

Most hydrogels for antibiotic delivery described in the literature rely on chemically modified polymers which are neither commercially available nor included in the list of pharmaceutically recognized ingredients. Biopolymer chemical modification introduces additional preparation steps, increasing complexity, the need for industrial validation, and the burden of quality control. Importantly, the cost of specialized raw materials can also pose a significant barrier to clinical translation. Moreover, the sterilization of chemically conjugated systems must be validated to ensure that the conjugates withstand the process. In the vast majority of preclinical studies in the literature, the sterilization methods used are not industrially validated. In contrast, GEDAI is fully compatible with steam sterilization, a well-established and regulator-trusted process. All GEDAI components are already included in medicinal products or medical devices currently used in humans in both the EU and the USA, substantially reducing regulatory hurdles. Additionally, emulsion processing is ubiquitous in the pharmaceutical and medical device industries, being routinely employed in the manufacture of creams, ointments, and injectable or parenteral formulations (e.g., parenteral nutrition products). Consequently, all processing steps required for GEDAI preparation are already standard in industry, further lowering barriers to clinical translation.

GEDAI adheres to implant materials (stainless steel and titanium) and a skin surrogate (leather) whilst displaying only moderate adhesion to surgical gloves. Surface analysis indicated that contact with the hydrogel alters the chemical composition of the glove surface, likely due to partial coverage by gel components that mask exposed functional groups and modify interfacial interactions. Differences in substrate chemistry likely also contribute to this behavior: surgical gloves are predominantly hydrophobic and contain few polar functional groups, whereas tissue-like substrates present a broader range of chemical functionalities capable of forming intermolecular interactions with the hydrogel. These differences in surface chemistry likely account for the higher adhesion forces observed on tissue-like materials and support the intended intra-operative handling characteristics of the material. Another feature of the hydrogel is its injectability. For injectable hydrogels, low internal resistance is essential for smooth injection, while the gel must retain its shape after injection. GEDAI demonstrates a favorable balance between flowability and shape retention, with G′ values close to G". GEDAI demonstrated shear-thinning properties, with lower viscosity at high shear rates indicating good injectability and higher viscosity at low shear rates preventing diffusion after application. Emulsion stability is also important because, over time, oil droplets may coalesce, leading to breakdown of the formulation and separation of the oil and water phases. All emulsion preparations showed a zeta potential below −30 mV, indicating emulsion stability [46]. The addition of sodium oleate in GEDAI compensates for any reduction in zeta potential due to the zwitterionic egg-derived phosphatidylcholine, thus enhancing stability. Importantly, the final physical texture of the combination was antibiotic dependent. For antibiotics with high water solubility and lower molecular mass such as tobramycin and gentamicin, the combination with GEDAI led to minimal changes in the viscoelastic profile. The combination with vancomycin, which displays a lower charge/molecular mass ratio, led to a GEDAI-GV formulation with formation of solid-like precipitates that resulted in lower adhesion energy in tack tests, and a more prolonged antibiotic release. The more extended release can be attributed to the solid-like precipitates, which must first solubilize before vancomycin can diffuse out of the gel. This behavior is reflected in the release profile: when gentamycin and vancomycin are present in the same formulation, vancomycin's release reaches plateau 24 h after gentamicin. These results indicate that the physicochemical properties of GEDAI meet predefined design criteria for clinical translatability and ease of use, although antibiotic choice can impact on its properties such as injectability and release. Taken together, the adhesion, rheology and stability data show that GEDAI meets its predefined handling targets. Further testing may be appropriate prior to including alternative antibiotics as material properties may be altered. This highlights that, while the platform is adaptable, the selection of antibiotics may require formulation-specific optimization to ensure consistent handling and performance for clinical use.

By incorporating the antibiotics within the GEDAI formulation, the duration of antibiotic release in vitro is extended compared to antibiotics in PBS. In vivo, we also measured the local concentrations of a human-scale dose in sheep. The primary finding indicated a mean peak tobramycin concentration of approximately 3000 μg/mL adjacent to the plate within the first 3 h of application and a slightly delayed peak in the surrounding subcutaneous tissues of approximately 2000 μg/mL at 4 h. At both sites, the concentrations remained above a target MIC value of 2 μg/mL for approximately 24 h. This represents an approximate 400-fold increase in peak concentration compared to what is achievable through systemic delivery of a standard dose measured in cancellous bone [47]. This is consistent with previous reports demonstrating that high local antibiotic concentrations achieved through local delivery systems can exceed systemic levels and contribute to improved infection control in orthopedic settings [48]. As a concentration-dependent antibiotic, achieving this elevated peak concentration aligns with the antibiotic's pharmacokinetic/pharmacodynamic index [27,[49], [50], [51]]. By utilizing a sheep model to estimate local tissue concentrations, the data provides a realistic estimate of local tissue concentrations in the extremities of humans receiving plate fixation. However, it is important to note that these local tissue concentrations can vary significantly in less vascularized areas or within different anatomical locations. Previously, application of a hyaluronic acid-based gentamicin loaded hydrogel inside the IMC resulted in a similar peak concentration, but extended duration of approximately 9 days above MIC [35]. The differences in local tissue concentrations highlight the impact of anatomical location, tissue vascularity and perfusion on antibiotic release from relatively similar hydrogel compositions. Antibiotic release from GEDAI is primarily governed by diffusion from the aqueous phase of the emulsion hydrogel rather than by bulk degradation of the material, indicating that release kinetics and material degradation are not necessarily directly coupled. Geometric factors such as diffusion path length may also influence release behavior and were not systematically investigated in this study. While quantitative assessment of in vivo hydrogel degradation was not performed, qualitative observations during the study indicated progressive disappearance of the material over time.

The high peak concentration is ideal for antibacterial activity but should also be considered in the context of local cytotoxicity. Indeed, the combination of GEDAI with different antibiotic agents produced antibiotic-specific alterations in biological characteristics. Gentamicin and vancomycin at high concentrations affected cell viability in the short term (within 7 days) and osteogenic differentiation in the long term (over 28 days). In contrast, tobramycin had no effect on either cell viability or osteogenic differentiation. This aligns with our findings and with previous evidence of antibiotic toxicity at high concentrations [[52], [53], [54], [55], [56]]. However, in vivo measurements revealed a concentration peak on the first day, which quickly decreased to levels below the MIC, but covering a much larger surface area than the cell culture well. This suggests that the in vitro system (with prolonged high levels and limited surface contact area) does not completely mimic the conditions found in a living organism, and the negative effects observed in cell culture may be exaggerated relative to their in vivo impact. From a clinical perspective, the primary concern with the use of aminoglycosides is ototoxicity, which can be monitored by measuring systemic exposure. Since the systemic antibiotic levels released from the antibiotic-loaded GEDAI were not detectable after 24 h, the primary clinical safety concerns are within limits for GEDAI.

In terms of efficacy of GEDAI in treating infection, this study proved that local application of antibiotic loaded hydrogel GEDAI-GV successfully treated MRSA infection in a single stage revision more successfully than the current SOC with systemic antibiotic treatment approach. The combination of G and V was chosen for in vivo testing because it is aligned with the antibiotic susceptibility of the infecting pathogen and is commonly used in local applications in human patients with MRSA infection. Recent data have demonstrated the effectiveness of single-stage revision compared to two-stage revision in terms of infection eradication, re-infection rates and cost effectiveness [17,57,58]. However, only certain subgroups of patients are eligible for a single stage exchange [19]. The numerous advantages of the single stage procedure, including single surgical intervention, a shorter duration of antibiotic treatment and reduced costs, could potentially expand eligibility if combined with the use of a locally applied hydrogel. This approach could benefit patients with high antibiotic resistance, those who have previously undergone single-stage procedures, and patients with comorbidities [19,58]. Management of infection through the DAIR technique also offers an appealing option for both patients and surgeons. Nevertheless, the current demographic of patients suitable for DAIR remains quite restricted, as outcomes tend to be unfavorable for individuals who do not fulfill the specified criteria. Expanding the range of patients who can benefit from DAIR would greatly enhance patient care. The sole application of antibiotic-loaded GEDAI (GEDAI-T) in a DAIR approach proved to effectively reduce the bacterial load with 1-log reduction of bacterial load compared to SOC group receiving only systemic antibiotic treatment. This result, although not perfect, highlights the main limitation of the DAIR approach, addressing the bacteria within the IMC that are not accessible with classical implant retention. In our previous study, we developed a novel method to irrigate the infected IMC and eliminate the bacteria present within it [44]. The irrigational method, the IML, we applied was essential for significantly removing the deeper, stubborn nidus of infection. The application of this method resulted in a significant reduction of the bacterial load and complete eradication in the soft tissue and in the IMC. However, a strong improvement is observed when the GEDAI-G is injected within the IMC and over the plate. All samples collected at euthanasia were culture negative, with only one out of five animals testing culture positive at the implant site. Therefore, the addition of local antibiotic therapy to the IML was essential for eradicating the infection with this DAIR approach. In parallel to the antimicrobial efficacy, micro-CT confirmed complete cortical bridging in all animals and a significant bone-volume gain only in the GEDAI-G group, suggesting that the burst release of antibiotics does not compromise fracture union. These findings align with our earlier studies in rabbits and sheep and strengthen the case for clinical translation [35,59].

The proven efficacy of the antibiotic-loaded GEDAI formulations in several large animal models of ODRI represents a significant advance toward novel clinical treatment possibilities in ODRI. The innovative potential of the antibiotic-loaded GEDAI lies in fulfilling the design goals for its physicochemical properties, thereby addressing the unmet clinical needs in infection management for orthopedic trauma. Its composition reflects these design goals and offers several unique advantages over existing solutions. Testing GEDAI in large animal models that closely mimic human clinical scenarios, along with the promising results from this research, supports its high translational potential and lays the groundwork for future clinical evaluation in ODRI clinical settings. As part of this translational pathway, future studies will include dedicated evaluation of local tissue responses to the hydrogel and its degradation products in non-infected models to further characterize material-specific effects.

Materials and methods

GEDAI preparation and characterization

GEDAI is an emulsion-based hydrogel prepared by adding antibiotic solution to a precursor designated as “pre-GEDAI”. All percentages are expressed as w/w % unless stated otherwise.

Pre-GEDAI preparation. The pre-GEDAI was prepared by mixing phospholipid E80 (1.49%), sodium oleate (0.04%) and soybean oil (12.4%) (all pharmaceutical grade and supplied by Lipoid GmbH, Ludwigshafen, Germany) in a glass beaker at 70 °C under magnetic stirring for 4 h. A glycerin solution was prepared by dissolving glycerin (2.82%) (Carl Roth GmbH, Karlsruhe, Germany) in mQ water (79.53%) under magnetic stirring. The lipids (phospholipid E80, sodium oleate and soybean oil, total of 13.93%) were then combined with the glycerin solution and homogenized using a Silverson Laboratory Mixer L5M at max power for 10 min. The mixture was allowed to rest for 1 h. Subsequently carboxymethylcellulose sodium salt (CMC) (3.72%) (Blanose, Ashland Industries Europe GmbH, Schaffhausen, Switzerland) was added and mixed thoroughly using a kitchen blender. The resulting mixture was left for passive hydration at room temperature overnight and then sterilized with steam for 20 min at 121 °C using an autoclave (Varioklav, Sterico AG, Wangen-Brüttisellen, Switzerland). This formulation was utilized for all experiments except for the GEDAI-GV formulation used in vivo, which was sonicated with an ultrasound homogenizer for 7 min at 60 W.

Antibiotic-loaded GEDAI. The pre-GEDAI was mixed with milliQ water to yield GEDAI. The antibiotics (T (Sigma-Aldrich, Buchs, Switzerland), GS (Carl Roth GmbH, Karlsruhe, GermanyRoth) and/or with V hydrochloride (Labatec Pharma SA, Geneva, Switzerland)) are first dissolved in milliQ water and then mixed with the pre-GEDAI formulation, resulting in the final antibiotic-loaded hydrogel. The mixing was performed by connecting two syringes, one filled with gel and one with milliQ water/antibiotic solution in the ratio of 4:1, and then repeatedly extruding the resulting mixture back and forth about 60 times to reach a final antibiotic concentration of 2% for GEDAI-T, 1% GEDAI-G, and 1% and 4% of G and V respectively for GEDAI-GV. For the in vivo study where the GEDAI-GV was applied, the final formulation was diluted prior to application due to the formation of granules in the gel, preventing a smooth application. After the addition of milliQ water the GEDAI-GV could be easily extruded. The final antibiotic concentrations were 0.85 ± 0.05% and 3.4 ± 0.1% for G and V respectively (Table S5).

Microscopy. The stability of the emulsions was observed microscopically under an Axiovert 200M Inverted Fluorescence Microscope equipped with an Axiocam HRc Rev. 2 camera. (Carl Zeiss Microscopy, Jena, Germany). Approximately 0.01 mL emulsion samples after ultrasound treatment or high-shear homogenization (same day and 4 days after preparation) were placed on a glass slide and covered with a coverslip. After 10 min of equilibration, samples were examined at room temperature at 40× magnification.

Zeta Potential and Dynamic Light Scattering (DLS). Hydrodynamic Radius, Polydispersity index and Zeta-Potential of oil in water (o/w) emulsions (before addition of CMC) were determined using the dynamic or quasi-elastic light scattering (DLS) technique and electrophoretic mobility. Solutions for DLS measurements were prepared by diluting 1:200 the original sample with mQ water and filtering through a 0.20 μm porosity membrane filter. The mQ water was degassed by sonication for 20 min. The measurements were performed by means of a Brookhaven 90 Plus DLS size analyzer. The apparatus is equipped with He-Ne laser (wavelength λ = 632.8 nm) and a detector recording light intensity at fixed scattering angle θ = 90°. Ten runs for each experiment were performed at room temperature. The viscosity of water solvent at 25 °C was 0.890 cP. The refractive index of Water and Soybean oil were 1.330 and 1.468 respectively. The DLS instrument was first tested with a polystyrene (PS) latex particle standard (DH = 100 nm) after dilution with degassed mQ water (1:1000). In order to avoid multiple scattering, all samples were analyzed after dilution with degassed mQ water using a cuvette (1 × 1 cm) to obtain a homogeneous almost transparent solution. To determine the mean diameter and the polydispersity and to estimate the reliability of data, samples were analyzed in duplicates.

Rheology. To evaluate the viscoelastic properties rheological experiments were performed on the GEDAI, GEDAI-T, GEDAI-G and GEDAI-GV using an Anton Paar MCR 302 Rheometer (Anton Paar GmbH, Austria) equipped with a 25 mm cone-plate (CP25-1 SN31077) with 1° angle and gap of 0.049 mm, or parallel plate (PP25-1 SN31938) geometry for tack tests. Samples were loaded on the rheometer by a spatula or syringe. The excess amount of sample that protruded beyond the geometry area was carefully removed. To prevent the drying of the sample, a layer of silicon oil was applied to the edge interfacing with air; this was not done for the tack tests. The measurement temperature was kept at 25 °C using a Peltier effect-based control temperature system. Amplitude sweep tests (Pa) were performed with shear strain (γ) ranging from 0.1 to 1000% with a constant frequency of 10 rad/s. The viscosity curves (Pas) were measured in the shear rate ranging from 0.1 to 100 s–1. Elastic recovery tests (Pa) were performed with low and high shear strain (γ) of 1 and 300% respectively with a constant frequency of 10 rad/s. Tack tests were performed starting with a working gap of 0.25 mm increased at the speed of 0.1 mms–1. The adhesive strength [mJ] was derived from the area under the force-time curve. For tack tests with gloves and leather, powder free nitrile exam gloves (SensiCare Ice, Medline Geneva, Switzerland) or chamois leather (soaked in water for 3 h, squeezed by hand prior to testing to remove water) were attached with a zip-tie to a PP25 disposable geometry D-CP/PP 7. Special care was taken to achieve a flat surface at the bottom. For each measurement a new piece of glove or leather was used.

Attenuated Total Reflection Fourier Transform Infrared (ATR-FTIR). Surgical gloves (latex; Medline, Lot GUIL21W37, manufacturing date 2021-09-01) were cut into flat sections for analysis. For measurements of the glove alone, the material was placed directly onto the ATR crystal and gently pressed into full contact using the instrument's pressure arm. For measurements involving GEDAI, glove and leather samples were evenly smeared with the gel and then positioned on the ATR crystal. Samples were again pressed with the pressure arm to ensure consistent contact during acquisition. Spectra were collected using a Jasco FT/IR-4X instrument over the range of 400–4000 cm−1. Processing steps included noise elimination, automatic baseline correction, reduction of atmospheric H2O and CO2 contributions, and spectral smoothing.

In vitro biological analyses of the GEDAI formulations

Cell culture. Telomerase-immortalized human foreskin fibroblasts (hTERT-BJ1) were purchased from Clontech (USA) and cultured in Dulbecco's modified Eagle's Medium (DMEM, Gibco, Thermo Fisher, Zürich, Switzerland) with 1 g/l glucose and 10% Fetal Bovine Serum (FBS, Corning®, Amsterdam, The Netherlands) [60]. Human umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVECs) were cultured in EGM®-2 Endothelial Cell Growth Medium-2 BulletKit® (Lonza, Switzerland) according to the manufacturer's instructions. Human peripheral blood mononuclear cells (hPBMCs) were isolated from healthy human donor (n = 1, male). The buffy coat was obtained from the Regionaler Blutspendedienst SRK Graubünden, and hPBMCs were isolated via density gradient centrifugation using Histopaque‐1077 (Sigma‐Aldrich, Merck KGaA, Darmstadt, Germany) at 800g for 20 min at room temperature. Cells were seeded in Minimum Essential Medium Eagle – Alpha Modification (α-MEM, Gibco, Thermo Fisher, Zürich, Switzerland) with the addition of 100 μg/mL streptomycin (Gibco, Thermo Fisher, Zürich, Switzerland), 100 U/mL penicillin (Gibco, Thermo Fisher, Zürich, Switzerland) and 10% FBS (Corning®) and 50 ng/mL human macrophage colony-stimulating factor (M-CSF, Peprotech, Zürich, Switzerland). Bone marrow aspirates were obtained from patients undergoing spine surgery at the Inselspital Bern, Switzerland. The Swiss Human Research Act does not apply to research which involves anonymized biological material and/or anonymously collected or anonymized health related data [61]. General consent, which also covers anonymization of health-related data and biological material, was obtained from all cell donors. hBMSCs were isolated using a standardized procedure as previously described [62]. In total 5 donors were used for experiments with hBMSCs (3 M/2 F; mean age of 46 years; age range: 29-75). After isolation, an initial cell density of 3x103 cells/cm2 was maintained for hBMSCs subculture and were grown until passage 2 (p2) in α-MEM with the addition of 100 μg/mL streptomycin, 100 U/mL penicillin, 5 ng/mL basic Fibroblast Growth Factor (bFGF, Fitzgerald Industries International, Acton, MA, USA) and 10% FBS (Corning®). Maintenance of cultures was performed at 37 °C/5% CO2, and the medium was refreshed on alternate days.

Cell viability assay. CellTiter-Blue® (Promega, Madison, Wisconsin, USA) assay was performed on hTERT-BJ1, hPBMCs-derived macrophages and HUVECs over 7 days, with time point at day 1, 3 and 7. All cell types were seeded in a 6-well plate at a density of 1.5x104 cells/cm2 and grown in their appropriate cell culture media. The assay was performed with n = 2 or 3 (technical replicates). After overnight cell attachment, a 40 μm pore size cell strainer (Corning®, Amsterdam, The Netherlands) was inserted in each well of the 6-well plate and 1 mL of GEDAI, GEDAI-T, GEDAI-G or GEDAI-GV was pipetted on the cell strainer. Control cultures were carried out in the appropriate cell culture media without GEDAI. The medium was refreshed on day 3. On day 1, 3 and 7, cells were first washed with PBS and then imaged using a Axiovert A1 inverted microscope, LD A-Plan 5x/0.15 Ph1 objective, equipped with an Axiocam 105 color camera (Carl Zeiss Microscopy GmbH, Jena, Germany). Cells for negative control were incubated for 5 min with Dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO, Thermo Fisher, Switzerland). All the conditions were incubated with fresh media and Cell Titer Blue reagent according to manufacturer instructions for 2 h at 37 °C/5% CO2. Absorbance was measured at 570 nm using 600 nm as a reference wavelength with a plate reader (TECAN, Männedorf, Switzerland.).

Induction of osteogenic differentiation of human mesenchymal stromal cells. For osteogenic differentiation, hBMSCs at p2 (n = 3 donors (1M/2F), n = 2 technical replicates) were seeded in a 6-well plate at a density of 1.5x104 cells/cm2 on Thermanox coverslips (Nunc, Milian AG, Geneve, Switzerland). After overnight cell attachment, medium was changed to DMEM +10% FBS with addition of 5 mM β-glycerol phosphate, 50 μg/mL ascorbic acid 2-phosphate and 10 nM of dexamethasone–cyclodextrin complex (water-soluble formulation) (all from Sigma-Aldrich, Buchs, Switzerland). Control cells were cultured in DMEM +10% FBS (basal condition). A cell strainer (40 μm pore size) with 1 mL of GEDAI, GEDAI-G or GEDAI-GV was inserted in each well with osteogenic media. Control cells were maintained in osteogenic or basal medium for 28 days. To maintain stable levels of differentiation factors, the medium was refreshed on alternate days. For RNA isolation, the collection of samples was performed on day 7, 14 and day 28. Furthermore, ARS staining was performed on day 28 to compare calcium deposition as a final differentiation outcome.

Additionally, hBMSCs at p2 (n = 3 donors (3M), n = 2 technical replicates) were cultured for 28 days in osteogenic media including 1, 100, 1000 μg/mL antibiotic solution (T, G or GV). Control hBMSCs were culture in osteogenic or basal media. ARS staining was performed on day 28 to compare calcium deposition as a final differentiation outcome.

Isolation of RNA. Standard TRIreagent extraction (Molecular Research Center Inc., Cincinnati, OH, USA) with 1-Bromo-3-chloropropane (Sigma-Aldrich) was used for the isolation of total RNA. After phase separation, the precipitation of RNA was carried out from the aqueous phase by adding 2-propanol (Sigma-Aldrich, Buchs, Switzerland). RNA pellet was washed with 75% EtOH and finally reconstituted with DEPC-H2O. NanoDrop 1000 (Thermo Fisher, Switzerland) was used to measure the total RNA concentration and purity was evaluated by assessment of the A260/280 and A260/230 ratios.

Gene expression analysis. For total gene expression, TaqMan Reverse Transcription reagents (Applied Biosystems, Foster City, CA, USA) were used to synthesize cDNA from 1000 ng of total RNA, as per recommendations of the manufacturer. Markers of osteogenic differentiation (see Table S6) were analyzed using qPCR. TaqMan Gene Expression Master Mix (Applied Biosystems, Thermo Fisher, Switzerland) in a QuantStudio 7 Flex Real-Time PCR system (Applied Biosystems, Thermo Fisher, Switzerland) was used to achieve the amplification of target genes by applying the following protocol: 2 min at 50 °C; 10 min at 95 °C; 40 cycles of 15 s at 95 °C, 1 min at 60 °C. TaqMan gene expression assay (Thermo Fisher, Switzerland) or custom-designed primers and probes (Microsynth AG, Balgach, Switzerland) were used to test the genes of interest (Table S6). Results are expressed as 2 −ΔCt, with RPLP0 used as a reference gene.

Alizarin Red staining and quantification. At day 28 samples were fixed in 4% neutral buffered formalin and stained with a 40 mM, pH 4.2 solution of Alizarin Red (Sigma-Aldrich, Buchs, Switzerland). After extensive washing to remove unbound staining, pictures were taken with a Axiovert A1 inverted microscope, LD A-Plan 5x/0.15 Ph1 objective, equipped with an Axiocam 105 color camera (Carl Zeiss Microscopy GmbH, Jena, Germany). Subsequently, Alizarin Red was solubilized from cultures with the acetic acid method as previously described [63] and quantified by measuring absorbance at 540 nm using a plate reader (TECAN, Männedorf, Switzerland).

In vitro release of antibiotics from GEDAI-T, GEDAI-G and GEDAI-GV

The in vitro release of T, G, and V from three formulations, GEDAI-T, GEDAI-G and GEDAI-GV, was conducted with n = 3 technical replicates for each formulation. Control samples were prepared in PBS (Gibco, Thermo Fisher, Zürich, Switzerland) at the corresponding concentrations, with one technical replicate. Five hundred μL of each formulation was placed into dialysis tubes (10 mm in width, 10 cm in length, MWCO 12-14 kDa) that had been rinsed four times and pre-hydrated overnight with milliQ water. The tubes were sealed at both ends with zip ties, and an aluminum washer was added to ensure the samples always remained submerged. A blank control sample containing only 500 μL GEDAI was also prepared. Each sample was immersed in 45 mL pre-heated PBS in 50 mL Falcon tubes and gently shaken at 50 rpm at 37 °C in a shaking incubator. At predetermined time points (2 h, 6 h, 12 h, 24 h, 48 h, 72 h, and 1 week), the dialysis tubes were removed from the Falcon tubes which were subsequently stored at −70 °C until analysis (LC-MS/MS and ZOI). The dialysis tubes were then transferred to new Falcon tubes containing 45 mL of fresh PBS. All samples were stored at −70 °C. After the final time point, the hydrogels and antibiotic solutions were dispersed in 45 mL PBS for 24 h by cutting the dialysis tubes in half to recover any unreleased antibiotics on day 8.

Quantification of antibiotic concentrations by LC-MS/MS

Chromatography was performed on a 1290 Infinity II Ultra-High Performance Liquid Chromatography (UHPLC) system (Agilent Technologies, California, USA). Separation was achieved on a Zorbax Eclipse XDB-C18 column (150 × 3.0 mm, 1.8 μm, Agilent Technologies, California, USA). Formic acid (0.1 %, v/v) in water and acetonitrile (supplied with 0.1 % formic acid, v/v) were employed as mobile phases A and B respectively. The elution profiles were: 0.0-1.0 min, 3 % B; 1.0-3.00 min, 3-98 % B; 3.0-4.9 min, 98 % B; 4.9-5.0 min, 98-3 % B and 5.00-6.00 min 3 % B. The mobile phase flow rate was 400 μL/min. The column temperature was maintained at 40 °C. The liquid chromatography was coupled to an Ultivo Triplequadrupole mass spectrometer (Agilent Technologies, California, USA) equipped with a Jetstream electrospray ion source (ESI) operated in positive ion mode. The instrument parameters were optimized by infusion experiments with pure standards. The ion spray voltage was set to +3000 V in positive ion mode. Dry gas temperature was set to 325 °C and dry gas flow to 10 L/min. Sheath gas temperature was set to 400 °C and sheath gas flow to 12 L/min. Nebulizing gas was set to 40 psi. Nitrogen was used as dry gas, nebulizing gas and collision gas. Multiple reaction monitoring (MRM) was used to monitor precursor ion fragment ion transitions. MRM transitions and other parameters such as fragmentor voltage and collision energies were optimized using reference standards. Both Q1 and Q3 quadrupoles were maintained at unit resolution. Mass Hunter Quantitation Analysis for QQQ software (Version 10, Agilent Technologies, California, USA) was used for data processing. Linearity in ionization efficiency was verified by analyzing dilution series that were also used for quantification.

Dilution series was prepared from 0.01 ng/mL to 10000 ng/mL and separate dilution series were run for each target antibiotic. Leucine-Enkephalin was used as internal standards for injection control and correction of inter-injection variation. For gentamicin, the lower limit of quantification upon dilution was 10 ng/mL, with imprecision values of 8.4% at 100 ng/mL and 9.2% at 5000 ng/ml. For tobramycin, these values were 8 ng/mL, 5.7% and 2.2 %, respectively, while for vancomycin, they were 44 ng/mL, and 7.4% and 7.9%.

Sample preparation for LC-MS/MS. The samples were first mixed with milliQ grade water containing 0.1 % formic acid (v/v) and 1000 ng/mL leucine-enkephalin internal standard (5 μL sample were added to 95 μL water with formic acid and internal standard). To keep the antibiotic within the range of the standard dilution, samples with high antibiotic concentrations were further diluted with deionized water containing 0.1 % formic acid (v/v) to final dilutions of 200-fold prior to analysis by liquid chromatography coupled to tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS). Samples for analyzing the release of T, GS, V from GEDAI were filtered through 0.22 μm PVDF membrane (Multiscreen® 96 well Plate, hydrophilic PVDF membrane, Millipore, Massachusetts, USA) to remove any particulate polymer residue. The PBS samples from this experiment were treated in the same way. Plasma samples for T quantification were cleaned by using 30 kDa cutoff filters (AcroPrep Advance 96-well filter plates for Ultrafiltration - 350 μL, Omega 30K MWCO, Cytiva Europe) according to the manufacturer's manual. Prior to application to the filters, the samples were centrifuged at 21000×g for 10 min at 10 °C. 200 μL of the supernatant was applied to the filter plates. Filter plates were centrifuged for 60 min at 1500×g. An aliquot of the flow through was mixed with milliQ grade water containing 0.1 % formic acid (v/v) and 1000 ng/mL leucine-enkephalin as internal standard (10 μL sample was added to 190 μL). Diluted samples were directly subjected to analysis by LC-MS/MS.

In vitro analyses of antimicrobial activity of the GEDAI formulations

S. aureus ATCC 25923 (MSSA) and S. aureus EDCC 5443 (MRSA) were used for the in vitro assays. S. aureus EDCC 5443 (MRSA, DSM No. 29134) is a clinical isolate resistant to methicillin but sensitive to gentamicin (MIC 0.038 μg/ml), vancomycin (MIC 0.5 μg/ml).

Planktonic experiments. MSSA or MRSA cultures were grown overnight in TSB at 37 °C with agitation at 100 rpm. The following day, the overnight cultures were diluted 1:20 in TSB and cultured for 1 h under the same conditions. Those cultures were then centrifuged at 3220 rcf for 5 min, washed once with PBS, and then resuspended in TSB. After 3 min sonication, the OD600 was adjusted to 0.01. Six mL of this planktonic bacterial suspension were placed in a 6-well plate and a 40 μm pore size cell strainer was inserted into the well containing one of the different GEDAI formulations (0.5 mL/well). GEDAI-T or GEDAI-G were applied to the MSAA, while GEDAI-GV was applied to the MRSA. After 24 h of incubation at 37 °C with gentle agitation at 50 rpm, the plates were sonicated for 3 min and solutions were diluted to spot inoculate 10 μL of each dilution on tryptic soy agar (TSA) plates to quantify the surviving population.

Biofilm experiments. Biofilms of MSAA or MRSA were grown on metal discs for 48 h and then exposed to the different formulation of GEDAI. Specifically, MSAA biofilms were exposed to GEDAI-T or GEDAI-G, while MRSA biofilms were exposed to GEDAI-GV. Briefly, MSSA or MRSA were grown overnight in TSB at 37 °C with agitation at 100 rpm. The following day, the bacterial culture was centrifuged at 3220 rcf for 5 min, washed once with PBS, and then finally resuspended in TSB. After 3 min sonication, the OD600 was adjusted to 0.01, and 1 mL of this culture was added to each metal disc. The biofilm was allowed to grow at 37 °C for 48 h on a shaking incubator and then the different conditions were applied: GEDAI, GEDAI-T, GEDAI-G, T (2%), G (1%) or left untreated (control). The different GEDAI formulations (0.5 mL/well) were applied using a 40 μm pore size cell strainer, which was placed in the well containing the disc with the biofilm. After 24 h, the metal discs were sonicated in PBS and solutions were diluted to spot inoculate 10 μL of each dilution on TSA plates to quantify the surviving population.

Zone of inhibition (ZOI). The ZOI experiment was performed using the solution released from the different formulation of antibiotic-loaded GEDAI (described above in “in vitro release of antibiotics from GEDAI-T, GEDAI-G and GEDAI-GV”). A total 10 μL from the released antibiotic solution was pipetted on blank paper discs (BD BBL™ Sensi-Disc™). The strains: S. aureus ATCC 25923 (MSSA) or EDCC 5443 (MRSA) were used for the inhibition zone experiment. An overnight culture from MSSA or MRSA were grown in TSB, which was subsequently diluted to an OD600 = 0.5. The bacteria were spread on a TSA plate using a cotton swab and the discs with the samples collected overtime were applied on top. The control samples, T, G, and GV were also obtained from the same release experiment. The plates were incubated for 24 h and afterwards the inhibition zone was measured using the Scan® 1200 - HD automatic colony counter and ZOI (Interscience, Frankfurt, Germany).

Animal models and ethical approval

Reporting of all animal studies presented in this work is done according to ARRIVE guidelines. Procedures were performed in an Association for Assessment and Accreditation of Laboratory Animal Care (AAALAC) International approved facility, according to Swiss animal protection law and regulations. Study approval was granted by the ethical committee of the canton of Grisons in Switzerland (approval numbers GR/38/2018, GR/07/2020, GR/22/2021, GR/08E/2024).

Clinically healthy, skeletally mature, female Swiss Alpine sheep were enrolled in this study. Sheep were selected for this study because they permit the use of human-sized fixation implants and surgical instruments, along with an antibiotic dosing regimen comparable to the standard of care for humans. Before being included in the study, the sheep underwent a clinical examination and blood analysis conducted by a veterinarian to ensure they were suitable for participation. Prior to the start of the study, the sheep were acclimatized to the animal holding facility for at least two weeks prior to the surgical procedure. During this time, they were kept in groups and fed with hay and mineral supplements with free access to drinking water. Only female sheep were included due to the limited availability of rams of this age. Study design, including groups, animal age, weight and timeline for infection duration, revision surgery, and treatment, as well as outcome measures are shown in Fig. 5, Fig. 6, Fig. 7. Within each in vivo study (DAIR, DAIR + IML and single stage), sheep were randomly assigned to either the SOC group or the treatment group (GEDAI-T, GEDAI-G and GEDAI-GV). Animals were allocated to their group only after completion of debridement at revision surgery and surgeons were blinded to the treatment group until revision surgery. Technical support for bacteriology and histology was blinded through all study. The investigator analyzing the data (C.S.) was blinded for results evaluation and unblinded for group allocation.

Animal model of plating osteosynthesis including DAIR

In total fifteen skeletally mature Swill alpine sheep were operated, but ten animals were included in the study and five animals were excluded due to different criteria: one animal due to lung adenocarcinoma signs before surgery, one animal died in recovery from revision surgery, most likely due to cardiac arrest, one animal was excluded 3 days post operative due to the fractured tibia, in one animal there was not enough fascia to close the wound during revision, and another had an open wound with fistula leading to plate and was euthanized before revision.

Surgery, revision surgery, GEDAI-T application and post-operative care

Surgery. The sheep were sedated with 0.04 mg/kg detomidine (Equisedan, Graeub, Switzerland) injected intramuscularly 30 min (min) before being transported to the surgical preparation room. Induction was done by intravenous (IV) injection of 0.2 mg/kg midazolam (Midazolam Sintetica, Sintetica, Switzerland) and 4 mg/kg ketamine (Ketasol 100, Graeub, Switzerland). Anesthesia was maintained using approximately 3% sevoflurane (Sevofluran Baxter, Baxter, Germany) in oxygen and air. Preemptive analgesia was conducted using lumbosacral epidural analgesia with 1 mL buprenorphine (Bupaq, Streuli Pharma AG, Switzerland) mixed with 5 mL lidocaine (Lidocain 2%, Streuli Pharma AG, Switzerland), as well as non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drug with 1.4 mg/kg carprofen (Carprodolor, Virbac, Switzerland) injected IV. Each animal received 2.2 mg/kg ceftiofur (Excenel, Zoetis, Zürich, Switzerland) IV as a peri-operative antibiotic at least 30 min before the surgical incision of the first surgery. For revision surgery, antibiotics were only given after all bacteriological samples had been collected. The sheep were placed in right lateral recumbency, and the right hind leg was aseptically prepared. A skin incision on the medial aspect of the right tibia was done followed by blunt subcutaneous preparation to expose the medial aspect of the tibial bone. The plate (10-hole 5.5 mm steel plate, DePuySynthes Inc.) was placed on the medial aspect of the tibia, its distal end approximately 2 cm proximal to the medial malleolus. For each surgery, the plate was manually pre-bent (distally between screw holes #8/9 and #9/10 to ∼10°) for better anatomical fit. Plate holes were drilled with a 4.3 mm drill bit, and the plate was fixed with 5.0 mm locking screws. A standardized transverse osteotomy was created by cutting the bone with an oscillating saw and custom-made jig, resulting in a gap width of 2 mm.

Inoculation. A total of 2.5 mL of saline solution containing 3 × 106 CFU of MSSA was pipetted over the plate prior to closure. The observation period of two weeks until revision aims to replicate early infection treatment cases. Two animals included in the SOC group of the DAIR model (Fig. 5) were included in the previous manuscript [44], which described in detail the establishment of the FRI and DAIR model in sheep.

DAIR Revision surgery. Anesthesia and aseptic preparation were performed for all the animals at revision in the same manner as for the primary surgery. A skin incision was made on the medial aspect of the tibia, followed by blunt subcutaneous preparation to expose the plate and the osteotomy. Visibly infected or necrotic tissue, abscess formations as well as pus were removed with blunt and sharp debridement and sampled for bacterial culture. The implant was cleaned with gauze swabs. All surfaces were then flushed with sterile saline from a bulb syringe. In total, 1 L of sterile saline was used per sheep. The samples (debridement tissue and irrigation fluid) were used for bacterial quantification (methods described below).

Microdialysis

Microdialysis was employed to dynamically evaluate the in vivo unbound fractions of tobramycin concentrations released from GEDAI-T concurrently from the target tissues [64]. The principle underlying microdialysis is that diffusion across the semipermeable membrane occurs quantitatively in both directions, resulting in a fraction of molecules within the target tissue permeating the membrane and being collected in vials. All microdialysis catheters were individually calibrated utilizing the retrodialysis by drug method prior to the commencement of the sampling period [65], employing a known concentration of tobramycin (5 mg/L).

Microdialysis equipment from M Dialysis AB (Stockholm, Sweden) was utilized and included 63 MD catheters (membrane lengths: 10 mm and 30 mm; molecular cutoff: 20 kDa) and 107 microdialysis pumps producing a flow rate of 1 μL/min. All catheters were perfused with saline (9 mg/mL, B. Braun) during the sampling period.

Placement of microdialysis catheters. At the end of revision surgery, three microdialysis catheters were placed in the IMC, cranial to the plate at the height of the osteotomy, and in the subcutaneous tissue (Sub. T) (30 mm catheter next to the plate and in Sub. T, 10 mm catheter in the IMC). To allow placement in the IMC, a 2 mm monocortical hole was drilled cranial to the plate on the height of the 4th screw, angulated distally. All three catheters were tunneled through skin and soft tissue and placed at their dedicated sampling location.

Application of GEDAI-T. Before the wound closure, a sterile insemination catheter (inner diameter 2.6 mm, KRUUSE, Denmark) was placed along the plate with its opening at the distal end of the plate. The surgical incision was closed in three layers (fascia, subcutis, skin) leaving an opening proximally for the catheter. After calibration of the microdialysis probes (see above), 10 mL of GEDAI-T was applied via the catheter while slowly pulling it out to spread the gel evenly along the plate. The wound opening was then closed with a purse string suture.

Post-operative care. Sheep were kept in supportive slings during the whole study to prevent excessive load on the operated leg. A bandage was placed after surgery to protect the wound and was removed after 5–7 days. To alleviate post-operative pain, the animals received 0.05 mg/kg buprenorphine (Bupaq, Streuli Tiergesundheit, Switzerland) intramuscular postoperatively and 6 h later, transdermal Fentanyl (fentanyl patch (Fentanyl-Mepha, Mepha, Switzerland); 2 μg/kg/h) for 72 h and 1.4 mg/kg carprofen (Carprodolor, Virbac, Switzerland) subcutaneously for 5 days. Additional analgesia was administered if deemed necessary due to signs of pain. During the study, animal welfare was evaluated by the animal caretakers and veterinarians using a scoresheet including the following parameters: behavior, respiration, food uptake/rumination, excretion, bodyweight, weight bearing of the operated leg, and wound healing. After each surgery (index surgery and revision surgery) the animals were closely monitored for any sign of discomfort and scored twice per day until day 5 post op, daily until day 7 post op, then twice weekly until the end of the study. All sheep were weighed weekly or biweekly.

Microdialysis sampling over 7 days. After revision surgery, microdialysis samples were taken every 30 min in the first hour after application of GEDAI-T, hourly until 6 h after application, every 2 h until 24 h, every 4 h until 96 h (4 days), every 12 h until 5 days and every 24 h until 7 days. Depending on the time of the day during application of GEDAI-T, specific time points were skipped in case of limited availability of personnel during the night.

Collection vials were connected to the catheters for 1 h of sampling for each timepoint, apart from the first two timepoints in which the vials were connected for 30 min each.

Antibiotics and euthanasia. After revision surgery, the sheep were treated systemically with flucloxacillin (2 g/sheep) by IV infusion over 30 min four times a day for 2 weeks. Thereafter, sheep were treated with rifampicin (600 mg/sheep) as an IV infusion over 1 h followed by injection of 12 mg/kg sulfamethoxazone + 2.4 mg/kg trimethoprim IV infusion two times a day for four weeks. After 2 weeks of antibiotic wash-out, the animals were euthanized by means of IV administration of 75-100 mg/kg pentobarbital (Eskonarcon, Streuli Pharma AG, Switzerland).

Blood analysis. Blood samples were taken preoperatively and weekly afterwards using EDTA tubes (Sarstedt, Germany). The white blood cell count was analyzed in the EDTA sample using the vet-abc (Medical solution GmbH, Switzerland) (Fig. S12).

Radiography

Radiographs (antero-posterior (AP) and mediolateral (ML) view) were taken post-operatively, before and after revision, and every second week from the beginning of the study.

Tibia computed tomography (CT)

CT scans (Revolution EVO, GE Medical Systems (Schweiz) AG) of the operated tibia were performed post-revision surgery at 2 weeks, afterwards at 8, and at euthanasia. For the CT scans, the sheep were sedated similarly as described for surgery above. Induction was done by IV injection of 0.15 mg/kg midazolam (Midazolam Sintetica, Sintetica, Switzerland) and 2 mg/kg propofol (Propofol Lipuro 1%, Streuli Tiergesundheit, Switzerland). Anesthesia was maintained using 2-3% sevoflurane (Sevofluran Baxter, Baxter, Germany) in oxygen and air. CT settings were 120 kVp energy, 200 mAs tube current without dose modulation, and a slice thickness of 0.625 mm. The images were reconstructed with the bone kernel. A bone mineral density (BMD) calibration phantom (QRM-BDC/6, QRMGmbH, Möhrendorf, Germany) was present in all scans to be able to calibrate the image data from HU to BMD values. Data was analyzed using a current version of Amira (Amira 2021.1, FEI SAS a part of Thermo Fisher Scientific, Hillsboro, Oregon, USA) to monitor changes to the osteotomy. The osteotomy gap (Region of Interest, RoI) was segmented via interpolation between both osteotomy sides for the half-tube opposite the bone plate to minimize metal artifact influence on the evaluation. Subsequent scans were registered to the baseline scans using rigid registration. With the resulting transformation matrix, the RoI was transformed to the respective scans to allow evaluation of the same regions. Bone was segmented automatically (threshold = 400 mg CaHA/ml) and bone volume and BMD were computed within the RoI using direct voxel counting methods. All image processing and analysis were performed blindly with custom TCL scripts in Amira.

Sample processing for bacteriological analyses and histology

At revision, the debridement tissue was weighed and then homogenized using 20 mL PBS. The irrigation fluid was sonicated for 10 min using Bandelin Ultrasonic water bath (Model RK 510 H). At euthanasia, the subcutaneous tissue, bone cores (including one bone core from the osteotomy side and 2 bone cores from the proximal part, and 2 from the distal part), bone marrow, plate and screws were collected. The bone cores (8 mm diameter) were obtained using a specially designed and custom-made stainless-steel drill (made at the AO Research Institute Davos). The subcutaneous tissue was collected by dissecting all tissue above the plate, while the complete bone marrow was collected using a curette after separating the proximal and distal bone parts at the osteotomy. All the tissue samples, aside from the plate and screws, were weighed, and afterwards, 20 mL of PBS was added to all the tissues. Subcutaneous tissue and bone marrow samples were first homogenized and then sonicated for 10 min as described above. For the plate and screws, 60 mL of PBS was added and then sonicated. Bacteria were diluted to spot inoculate 10 μL of each dilution on BA plates and incubated overnight at 37 °C to quantify the surviving population (n = 3 per sample). Undiluted samples (200 μL) were also plated on a BA plate. In case of a negative result in the undiluted BA plate, the samples were filtered through a membrane, and the membrane was cultured on the BA plate overnight, allowing the total number of bacteria/samples to be enumerated.

In the GEDAI-T group, one animal exhibited an unusually high bacterial count at the time of euthanasia. To determine whether this animal might have developed antibiotic resistance, a ZOI test was performed as described above using tobramycin-loaded disc on bacteria colonies collected from the animal after euthanasia compared to the original MSSA.

Identification of S. aureus in culture-positive samples was performed for at least one colony from each culture-positive animal, using Staphaurex™ Latex Agglutination Test (Thermo Fisher, Switzerland). Additional bone cores were collected from the osteotomy site for histological analyses. One sample was fixed and processed for Giemsa&Eosin (G&E) staining, while a second one was fixed, decalcified, and processed for Brenn and Brown (BB) staining [66] and tartrate-resistant acid phosphatase (TRAP) staining, which allows the visualization of osteoclasts.

Torque measurements

After euthanasia and soft tissue removal, measurement of removal torque force was performed on all the screws from each plate using an instrumented screwdriver (Advanced Force Gauge, Mecmesin, Slinfold, West Sussex, UK). Tissue adhering to the screw head was removed to enable adequate screwdriver contact [67].

Animal model of plating osteosynthesis including DAIR with IML

Twenty-two skeletally mature Swiss alpine sheep were operated in this study. Fifteen skeletally mature Swiss alpine sheep were included. Seven animals had to be excluded, one due to no weight bearing after revision, three due to open wound before revision, and three because at revision surgery the fascia could not sutured.

Surgery, revision surgery, GEDAI-G application and post-operative care

The surgery details of the DAIR + IML are the same described above for the DAIR approach and with full description of the methodology in Siverino et al. 2025 [44].

Inoculation. Differently from the DAIR model, the inoculation protocol was modified, and two collagen sponges (1/8 of TissuFleece E 3.6 × 1.8 cm, Baxter AG, Illinois, United States), each with 1 × 106 CFU MSSA were inserted through the osteotomy, one towards the proximal and one towards the distal part of the IMC. Additionally, 1 mL of 1 × 106 CFU MSSA was pipetted over the plate prior to closure (250 μl on screw #3, 250 μL on screw #7, 2 x 250 μL on top of the defect). The total inoculum was calculated as ∼2.5 × 106 ± 1.8 × 106 CFU/animal.

Revision surgery with IML and GEDAI-G application. IML. After flushing the surfaces with sterile saline (0.5 L), the osteotomy was refreshed using a scalpel blade, and the lavage was repeated (0.5 L). A unicortical hole was drilled in screw hole #4 (not used for plate fixation). Screws #3, 7, and 9 were removed (and sent for bacteriological culture), allowing access to the IMC, where the infected issue was retrieved as far as possible using curettes. Tissue plasminogen activator (tPA; Actilyse 20 mg, Boehringer, Switzerland/Activase rt-PA, Roche, Switzerland; 1 mg/ml) was injected into each of the four holes (2 mL per hole) and left for 10 min to degrade fibrin clot in the IMC and support the irrigation of the IMC. After this, the proximal (between holes #3 and #4) and distal (between holes #7 and #9) segments were flushed with sterile saline using a push-and-pull system with two 50 mL syringes. This procedure was repeated between holes #3 and #9. New screws were placed in the screw holes. In total, 1 L of saline was used for the IML per sheep. All the collected samples (debridement tissue, irrigation fluid, debridement tissue from IML, irrigation fluid from IML, screws #3, #7, and #9) were used for bacterial quantification as described above.

Animals in the DAIR + IML study underwent thoracic computed tomography (CT) and blood culture tests to identify potential risks associated with the IML approach, such as emboli formation in the lungs and the dissemination of bacteria within the circulation. However, this part of the study is part of the previous published work focused on model establishment [44]. For the thoracic CT and blood culture methodology refer to Siverino et al. 2025 [44].

Postoperative care and systemic antibiotic therapy were performed as described above for the DAIR model study.

Sample processing for bacteriological analyses and histology as described above. To note that in this group debridement tissue for the IMC was weighed and then homogenized using 20 mL or 10 mL PBS, respectively. The irrigation fluids and the screws (in 12 mL of PBS) were sonicated for 10 min.

Animal model of single stage revision after infection

Seven skeletally mature Swiss alpine sheep were included in the study. The animal welfare protocols; surgical procedures and postoperative management were performed as previously described in Foster et al. 2020 [38]. The animals were divided into two groups: systemic antibiotic (n = 3) vs GEDAI-GV injection at revision surgery (n= 4) + systemic antibiotic.

Surgical procedure. Anesthesia was induced as described above for the DAIR approach. This project was part of an analgesia project investigating the efficacy of a peripheral nerve block compared with epidural anesthesia. Half of the animals received ropivacaine around the femoral and sciatic nerve under ultrasound guidance and the other half received this epidurally [68]. Next to this, they received the same analgesic and prophylactic antibiotic regimen as described above for the DAIR approach.

The intramedullary (IM) nail (190 mm long 7.5 mm humerus, UHN; Depuy Synthes, Switzerland) and a 3.9 mm proximal locking bolt were inserted through the left proximal tibia via a medial parapatellar approach. A 4.3 mm unicortical hole was drilled into the proximal tibial diaphysis (12 cm distal to the tibial plateau) to prepare the site for the inoculum (described below). All wounds were closed in layers with absorbable suture material.

Inoculation. The bacterial inoculum of MRSA was prepared as previously described [69]. A 20 μl sample of the bacterial suspension was added to a collagen sponge (TissuFleece E; Baxter AG, Illinois, United States) to result in a target final inoculum of 6 × 106 CFU/animal. The quantitative culture was performed to confirm the inoculum was within an acceptable range (5 × 106 - 1 × 107 CFU/animal).

Revision surgery. Eight weeks following the initial surgery, sheep underwent a single‐stage revision surgery. Anesthesia protocol and surgical approaches are the same reported in Fosters et al. 2020 [38]. A simple debridement of the IM canal was performed using a femoral canal brush (Smith and Nephew AG, Zug, Switzerland). This tissue was collected for microbiological analyses as bone marrow. The IM nail and removed hardware (bolt) were all collected for microbiological analysis.

GEDAI-GV injection during revision surgery. The GEDAI-GV was prepared as described above. Before insertion of a new UHN nail, 20 mL of GEDAI-GV was inserted into the IM canal using a syringe attached to a soft plastic cannula (Rauclair‐E 4/1,5; Rehau Vertriebs AG, Switzerland).

Postoperative care and systemic antibiotic therapy. Postoperative analgesia protocol and postoperative care were consistent with the study of Foster et al. 2020 [38]. Systemic antibiotic treatment consists of: 1g Vancomycin as an IV infusion for 2 weeks (10 mg/kg) and 2 weeks of 600 mg Rifampicin IV infusion and 0.6 ml Trimethazol per 10 kg bodyweight for 4 weeks.

Radiography. Radiographic imaging was performed on the anterior‐posterior and lateral side of the left tibia pre and post both initial and revision surgeries. A high‐resolution contact radiograph was taken following euthanasia using a technical film (D4 Structurix DW ETE; Agfa, Mortsel, Belgium) and a cabinet X‐ray system (Model No. 43855 A, Faxitron X‐ray Corporation, Arizona, USA) at 42 kV for 5 min, with a 0.5 mm aluminum filter.

Sample processing for bacteriological analyses. After euthanasia, the left leg was dissected under sterile conditions. The soft tissue, the interlocking bolt, the IM nail, the bone marrow, and the bone samples (taken from the inoculation and both screw holes) were placed in sterile PBS. Soft‐tissue, bone, and bone marrow samples were homogenized (Omni‐TH handheld homogenizer; LabForce AG, Muttenz, Switzerland). Tissue samples, as well as the interlocking bolt and the IM nail were then sonicated for 3 min.

Two hundred microliters from each sample were placed on a blood agar plate (BA, Oxoid Massachusetts, USA) and incubated at 37 °C for 24 h before counting colonies. The lower limit of detection (LLOD) for soft tissue, bone, and bone marrow samples was 50 CFU. The PBS of the explanted interlocking bolt and IM nail was filtered through a membrane (EZ‐Fit Filtration Unit; Merck, Darmstadt, Germany), which was cultured, yielding an LLOD of 1 CFU. From each animal with a culture‐ positive result, an S. aureus‐specific latex agglutination test (Staphaurex Plus; Remel, California, USA) was performed to confirm the isolate was S. aureus.

Statistical analysis

The cell culture experiments were performed with n = 3 technical replicates for the hTERT-BJ1, hPBMCs-derived macrophages and HUVECs and n = 2 (technical replicates) of n = 5 (biological replicates, donors) for the hBMSCs and 2-way ANOVA Tukey's mixed-effects analysis multiple comparison tests were performed to analyze statistical differences between conditions in the cell viability test. For the gene expression analyses (RUNX2, SOX9, COL1A1, IBSP, PDPN), 2-way ANOVA with the Tukey's multiple comparisons test was performed while for RUNX2/SOX9 ratio and ARS quantification Kruskal-Wallis one-way ANOVA was performed. The ZOI experiment was performed with n = 9 technical replicates and data analyzed using 2-way ANOVA with Šídák's multiple comparisons test. In vitro analyses of GEDAI-GV antimicrobial activity were performed using Kruskal-Wallis one-way ANOVA. In vivo bacteriology analysis of GEDAI-T and GEDAI-GV groups compared to SOC were performed using the Mann–Whitney test. Statistical comparisons of GEDAI-G, with and without IML approach compared to SOC were performed using the Kruskal–Wallis test followed by Dunn's multiple comparisons test. A sample size calculation was performed to define group sizes. The power calculation indicated a group size of six to detect a statistically significant difference greater than 70% in the total number of culture-positive samples between treatment groups, which was based on previous in vivo studies assessing infection eradication treatment strategies. However, during the course of the study a clear treatment effect of GEDAI-GV became evident, while animals in the control SOC group receiving only systemic antibiotics remained heavily infected. Considering the severity of the infection model and the high burden for animals in the control group, the study was adjusted to reduce the number of animals in the SOC group, in accordance with ethical principles for animal experimentation and the 3Rs (Reduction).
