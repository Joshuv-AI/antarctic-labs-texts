TNNT2 Mutation in Pediatric Dilated Cardiomyopathy
Authors: Bi Huang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Corresponding AuthorKey words: cardiomyopathy, children, genetic mutations, heart transplantation, whole-exome sequencing

Pediatric cardiomyopathy encompasses a clinically and genetically heterogeneous group of myocardial diseases that carry substantial morbidity and mortality in children. Dilated cardiomyopathy (DCM) is the most common form requiring heart transplantation (HTx) in the paediatric age group,1 yet the precise factors that govern how quickly an individual child progresses from diagnosis to end-stage heart failure (HF) have remained poorly defined. In this issue of JACC: Asia, Liu et al2 present a carefully conducted retrospective cohort study that sheds important light on this question by linking whole-exome sequencing (WES) data to transplant outcomes in a large, single-center experience from Fuwai Hospital, Beijing, China.

The investigators enrolled 67 children (<18 years) and 166 adults who underwent HTx for primary cardiomyopathy between October 2004 and July 2023. All participants underwent WES testing, standard echocardiographic evaluation, and histopathological examination of the explanted hearts. Pathogenic or likely pathogenic variants were identified using American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics criteria, with allele frequencies cross-referenced against the 1000 Genomes Project and gnomAD databases.

The authors found that nearly half of the pediatric patients (49.3%) carried a mutation, and the proportion varied significantly across cardiomyopathy subtypes (P = 0.003). Across all subtypes, children progressed to transplantation far more rapidly than adults, most strikingly in DCM, where the median time from diagnosis to HTx was only 12 months in children compared with 103 months in adults (P < 0.001). In the DCM cohort, TNNT2 mutations were substantially more prevalent among children than adults (P = 0.001), and children harboring a TNNT2 variant reached HTx in a median of just 19 months versus 189 months for those without the variant (P = 0.002). The most frequently observed variant was an in-frame deletion p.K220del, found in 4 pediatric DCM patients. Quantitative histopathology of the explanted hearts confirmed greater fibrosis in TNNT2-mutation carriers, providing a plausible mechanistic basis for accelerated disease progression.

Several features make this study noteworthy. First, the cohort is large relative to the rarity of pediatric HTx, in which 67 transplanted children with WES data in a single center is an uncommon resource. Second, the simultaneous inclusion of an adult comparison cohort permits direct age-stratified comparisons that isolate pediatric-specific genetic patterns rather than simply reporting mutation prevalence in isolation. Third, the integration of histopathological data, specifically quantified collagen deposition, moves the analysis beyond association toward mechanistic plausibility. Fourth, the authors appropriately applied Benjamini-Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) correction and Firth's penalized logistic regression to address the multiple-comparison and sparse-data problems inherent in rare-variant analyses, demonstrating commendable statistical rigor.

Despite these strengths, several limitations merit comment. The retrospective single-center design introduces selection bias: All children represent end-stage disease requiring HTx, so the findings cannot be directly extrapolated to the broader population of children with DCM managed medically. The absolute number of pediatric TNNT2-mutation carriers is small (n = 7), and the confidence intervals around the 19-month median time to HTx are correspondingly wide. Although the authors performed FDR correction, replication in an independent cohort will be essential before clinical risk-stratification algorithms are modified.

The study also raises questions that future research should address. Are there modifier variants or environmental triggers that determine which TNNT2-mutation carriers progress rapidly versus those who remain compensated for longer? The p.K220del in-frame deletion deserves dedicated functional characterization: Does it disrupt troponin-actin interactions in a uniquely deleterious way during the high cardiac-output demands of early childhood? Additionally, whether early initiation of guideline-directed medical therapy or mechanical circulatory support can attenuate the accelerated course in TNNT2-positive children is a clinically pressing question not addressed by the present data.

The translational message is compelling The scientific statement from the American Heart Association recommends genetic testing for pediatric cardiomyopathy,3 and this study reveals that a positive TNNT2 result could then trigger more frequent echocardiographic surveillance, earlier listing for transplantation, and timely referral to a center with pediatric mechanical circulatory support capabilities. More broadly, the study reinforces the concept that pediatric and adult cardiomyopathy, despite sharing phenotypic labels, are not biologically equivalent.4 The differential mutation spectrum, with TNNT2 predominating in children and LMNA, MYH7, and TTN truncating variants more common in adults, suggests that age of onset itself shapes the genetic architecture of HF-requiring cardiomyopathy. This has implications for registry design, trial enrollment criteria, and the interpretation of adult HF genetics data in pediatric clinical practice.

TNNT2 encodes cardiac troponin T, an essential component of the thin filament regulatory complex. Mutations in TNNT2 have long been recognized as causes of both DCM and hypertrophic cardiomyopathy,5 and certain variants have been linked to early onset and rapid progression.6,7 Functional studies have demonstrated that DCM-associated TNNT2 variants decreased cardiac microtissue contraction.8 The histopathological finding of increased fibrosis in TNNT2 mutation carriers observed by Liu et al2 is consistent with prior mouse models showing accelerated ventricular remodeling in TNNT2-mutant backgrounds.9 The mechanisms and clinical implications regarding the association between TNNT2 mutation and pediatric DCM are summarized in Figure 1.

This figure illustrates the molecular and cellular consequences of a TNNT2 mutation leading to pediatric dilated cardiomyopathy. The mutation affects the troponin complex, reducing calcium sensitivity and impairing myosin-actin activation. This results in reduced force generation and sarcomere disarray, ultimately causing myocardial fibrosis and the development of dilated cardiomyopathy. In conclusion, Liu and colleagues have made a substantive contribution to the genetics of pediatric cardiomyopathy by demonstrating that TNNT2 mutations confer a dramatically accelerated trajectory to HTx in children with DCM. If validated prospectively, these findings support integrating rapid TNNT2 genotyping into the standard diagnostic work-up of every child presenting with newly diagnosed DCM. The challenge now is to translate this genetic signal into actionable clinical protocols and to design the prospective trials that will determine whether genotype-guided intensification of therapy can alter the natural history for these children and their families.
