Design and initial evaluation of a certified committee model for review of in vivo gene therapy and related technologies, together with ex vivo gene therapy, under the act on the safety of regenerative medicine
Authors: Hisako Katano, Kenji Konomi, Masaya Nakamura, Ichiro Sekiya
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionThe 2024 amendment to the Act on the Safety of Regenerative Medicine expanded the Act's regulatory scope to include in vivo gene therapy and related technologies. This created a need for certified committees for regenerative medicine to address associated scientific and regulatory issues. This study aimed to design and provide an initial evaluation of a practical and implementable committee model for reviewing newly covered technologies and ex vivo gene therapy under the amended Act.MethodsWe conducted an institutional implementation study to develop a dual-committee model consisting of an independent second committee for gene therapy review, supported by selected shared foundational functions from an existing cell-based therapy committee. The success of the initial implementation was prospectively evaluated at the first review meeting using five predefined items: committee establishment, organizational structure, membership composition, workflow establishment, and operational feasibility.ResultsA newly established specific certified committee for regenerative medicine for gene therapy review was certified by the Ministry of Health, Labour and Welfare on November 28, 2025. The predefined organizational model was implemented as an independent second committee with a shared institutional foundation. The required membership categories were fulfilled, including those specific to gene therapy review. A standardized review workflow incorporating prior administrative checks and a preliminary document review was established and successfully applied at the first review meeting, without major procedural disruption.ConclusionsA dual-committee model combining an independent second committee with a shared institutional foundation provided a practical and operationally feasible approach for implementing gene therapy review capacity within an existing regenerative medicine review infrastructure.

Introduction

The Act on the Safety of Regenerative Medicine, which came into effect in Japan in 2014, established a regulatory framework for the provision of regenerative medicine that included a risk-based review of provision plans by certified committees for regenerative medicine [1,2]. Under this framework, Class III regenerative medicine is reviewed by a certified committee for regenerative medicine, whereas Class I and Class II regenerative medicine are reviewed by a specific certified committee for regenerative medicine [1]. In the 2024 legal amendment, in vivo gene therapy and related technologies were newly brought within the scope of the Act, in addition to the previous framework centered on cell-processed products [1,3,4]. As a result, certified committees intended to review gene therapy are now required to possess expertise that extends beyond that traditionally required for review of cell-based regenerative medicine [4].

Review of in vivo gene therapy and related technologies that have been newly brought within the scope of the Act requires consideration of issues that differ in important respects from those encountered when reviewing conventional cell-based therapy. In addition to the general assessment of scientific validity, ethics, and participant protection, the committees must now be able to evaluate other factors, including characteristics specific to nucleic acids and related compounds; methods of delivery, manufacturing and quality control; possible effects on the human body; and environmental considerations, including handling of genetically modified organisms under the Cartagena Act [4,5]. Because many existing certified committees were originally developed to review cell-based regenerative medicine, it cannot be assumed that they already possess sufficient expertise or operational readiness for gene therapy review. In particular, after the legal amendment, the practical question was not merely whether a small number of specialists could be added to an existing committee; rather, it became a question of how to design a committee that could be convened and operated in a stable manner with personnel qualified for gene therapy review.

To address this challenge, we designed and implemented a certified committee model for review of in vivo gene therapy and related technologies newly brought within the scope of the Act, together with ex vivo gene therapy, under the amended Act by establishing an independent second committee for gene therapy review while sharing selected foundational functions with an existing committee for cell-based therapy. In this study, successful initial implementation was defined as achievement of regulatory certification, fulfillment of required member categories including those specific to gene therapy review, establishment of a standardized operational workflow from application receipt to issuance of the opinion letter, and confirmation that this workflow could be applied in the first review meeting. By presenting both the organizational design and its initial operational feasibility, this study aims to provide a practical reference model for institutions seeking to add gene therapy review capacity to an existing regenerative medicine review infrastructure.

Methods

Study design

This study was designed as an institutional implementation study to develop and conduct an initial evaluation of a newly established specific certified committee for regenerative medicine. The intention was to review in vivo gene therapy and related technologies newly brought within the scope of the Act, together with ex vivo gene therapy, after the 2024 legal amendment to the Act on the Safety of Regenerative Medicine. In response to the expanded regulatory scope of the Act, we designed a committee model consisting of an independent second committee for review of those technologies together with ex vivo gene therapy, with selected shared foundational functions from an existing committee for cell-based therapy.

Successful initial implementation was prospectively defined using five evaluation measures: (1) formal establishment of the committee through completion of the required regulatory procedures and preparation of publicly available documents; (2) implementing an organizational structure combining an independent second committee with a shared foundation; (3) fulfillment of the required membership categories, including Category 4 (expertise in the manufacturing of specified cell-processed products or specified nucleic acids used in the regenerative medicine under review), Category 9 (scientific knowledge and insight into the effects of gene therapy on the human body), and Category 10 (scientific knowledge and insight into the handling of genetically modified organisms involving nucleic acids); (4) establishing a standardized review workflow incorporating prior administrative checks by the secretariat and preliminary document review by committee members; and (5) operational application of this workflow in the first review meeting.

Committee design policy

To accommodate gene therapy review under the amended Act, we adopted a policy of designing an independent second committee rather than simply expanding the existing committee primarily intended for cell-processed products. Specifically, we established a dual-committee structure consisting of Committee I, which mainly reviews cell-based therapy, and Committee II, which mainly reviews in vivo/ex vivo gene therapy. We then positioned them as independent review bodies, but with selected foundational functions common to the protection of research participants shared between the two committees (Fig. 1). In this design, committee-specific expertise was separated according to the main scope of review, whereas the legal, bioethical, biostatistical, and lay perspectives were shared. In addition, the same secretariat supported both committees in maintaining administrative consistency and continuity of operation. Based on these requirements, we organized Committee I and Committee II to ensure that both committees satisfied the basic member composition, while Committee II also included members who met the requirements of Categories 9 and 10. Category 4, which concerns expertise in the manufacture of specified cell-processed products or specified nucleic acids used in the regenerative medicine under review, was also considered an essential element for both committees, especially regarding the practical review of gene therapy-related products and materials. Therefore, the compositions of Committee I and Committee II were structured so that all required categories were fulfilled, including the additional categories required for gene therapy review (Table 1).

Operational workflow

An operational workflow was developed to standardize the review process from application submission to issuance of the opinion letter. The review process was organized along a fixed timeline extending from advance administrative preparation to the formal review meeting and issuance of the opinion letter (Fig. 2). The process began with the application submission, with optional pre-submission consultation available when needed. After receipt of the application, the secretariat conducted initial eligibility screening and acceptance procedures, followed by contract or agreement execution, if required. The submitted documents were then checked by the secretariat for completeness and conformity with submission instructions before the documents were circulated to committee members for preliminary review. After this preparatory process, the case was discussed in an online committee review meeting, and the committee's decision was documented in an opinion letter. The applicant then submitted the required materials to the Ministry of Health, Labour and Welfare and other relevant authorities.Fig. 2Standardized workflow and timeline for review by Committee II. In principle, the application-related administrative preparation started several weeks before the review meeting, with secretariat-based screening and member-based preliminary review performed before the meeting date. The intent of this process was to improve procedural completeness, reduce delays caused by deficiencies in submission materials, and allow committee discussion to focus on substantive scientific, ethical, and regulatory issues during the formal review meeting.

Initial evaluation procedure

The initial implementation of the committee model underwent a descriptive evaluation according to the five predefined evaluation measures. For committee establishment, we confirmed whether the committee could be formally launched by completion of registration-related procedures and preparation of the publicly disclosed documents required for operation. For organizational structure, we confirmed whether the dual-committee model, with its shared foundation, could be implemented as designed. For membership composition, we verified whether each required category was fulfilled, with particular attention to Categories 4, 9, and 10, which were relevant to gene therapy review (Table 1, Table 2). For workflow establishment, we confirmed whether the standardized process could be instituted from submission intake to opinion issuance (Fig. 2). Finally, for operational feasibility, we examined whether this workflow could be applied at the first review meeting of Committee II without causing any major procedural disruption.

Because the purpose of this study was to report the design and initial implementation of an institutional review system under a new regulatory framework, the resulting evaluation was descriptive rather than comparative, and no hypothesis testing was planned. This evaluation framework was predefined to determine whether the committee model was operationally implementable under the amended regulatory requirements.

Results

Committee establishment

Committee II, a newly established specific certified committee for regenerative medicine intended to review in vivo gene therapy and related technologies newly brought within the scope of the Act, together with ex vivo gene therapy, was certified by the Ministry of Health, Labour and Welfare on November 28, 2025 (certification no. NA8250004). The committee regulations, internal rules, membership list, and minute templates were also prepared and made publicly available. Completion of these regulatory and administrative steps fulfilled the predefined criteria for formal committee establishment and enabled Committee II to enter an operational state capable of accepting applications and issuing opinion letters.

Organizational structure

The predefined organizational model was implemented as a dual-committee structure consisting of Committee I for cell-based therapy and Committee II for in vivo gene therapy and related technologies newly brought within the scope of the Act, together with ex vivo gene therapy, with both committees functioning as independent review bodies while sharing selected foundational functions related to research participant protection (Fig. 1). Legal, bioethical, biostatistical, clinical research, and lay perspectives were shared between the two committees, whereas gene therapy–specific expertise was concentrated in Committee II. This structure fulfilled the predefined criteria for implementation of an independent second committee supported by a shared institutional foundation.

Membership composition

The predefined criterion for membership composition was met by securing all required member categories for Committee II, including Categories 9 and 10, which are specifically required for review of in vivo gene therapy and related technologies newly brought within the scope of the Act, in addition to the general categories required for certified committees for regenerative medicine (Table 1, Table 2). Committee II also fulfilled Category 4, thereby ensuring expertise relevant to the manufacture of specified cell-processed products or nucleic acids used in the regenerative medicine under review. Committee I did not include members in Categories 9 or 10, whereas Committee II included two members in Category 9 and three members in Category 10. The Category 9 members were Drs. Toru Uchiyama and Hitoshi Osaka, and the Category 10 members were Drs. Eriko Uchida, Masafumi Onodera, and Takamasa Takeuchi. These names are also listed in the publicly available committee roster. This composition established review capacity for both the effects of gene therapy on the human body and the handling of genetically modified organisms involving nucleic acids, as required under the amended regulatory framework.

Operational workflow

The predefined criteria for establishing the workflow were met by implementing a standardized review process that incorporated prior administrative checks by the secretariat and preliminary document review by committee members before formal committee deliberation. The review process was organized along a fixed timeline that extended from advance administrative preparation to the formal review meeting and issuance of the opinion letter (Fig. 2). This workflow enabled the identification of formal deficiencies in submission documents and addressed before committee discussion and allowed a clearer organization of scientific, ethical, and regulatory issues in advance of the review meeting. In addition, an operational framework was prepared to maintain a quorum and the required expertise in the event of absences or recusals, with access to external advice when necessary during the initial phase of committee operation.

Operational feasibility at the first review meeting

The final predefined criteria for operational application of the workflow in practice were met at the first review meeting of Committee II, held on February 10, 2026. At this meeting, the review proceeded according to the predefined process, which included confirmation of conflicts of interest, prior administrative checks by the secretariat, preliminary document review by committee members, and formal committee deliberation and determinations.

The first reviewed case was an in vivo gene therapy study that had been conducted under the Guidelines for Gene Therapy Clinical Research. After the amended Act came into force, this case became subject to review under the Act on the Safety of Regenerative Medicine because new patient administration was to continue after the amendment. The case was operationally complex because it required careful regulatory and institutional consideration as an ongoing study that was transitioned into the amended legal framework. Before the formal review, the secretariat received preliminary inquiries as to whether the committee was accepting in vivo gene therapy cases for review. These inquiries were administrative in nature and were not part of the formal deliberation. During the first review meeting, the main issues discussed included regulatory and operational considerations arising from the transition of an ongoing study into the amended legal framework. A written opinion was obtained from one external technical expert. The case was reviewed over two meetings, each lasting approximately 2 h. Although some members were absent, a quorum was present and the required expertise was satisfied at both meetings. Attendance was 22 of 26 members at both meetings. Among Category 9 members, 2 of 2 attended both meetings, and among Category 10 members, 1 of 3 attended the first meeting and 2 of 3 attended the second meeting. This ensured that the required expertise was represented at both meetings. No committee member with a conflict of interest was identified for this case.

No major procedural disruption occurred during this first operation. Taken together, these findings showed that the committee model structure was successfully established and was operationally implementable in practice under the amended regulatory requirements.

Discussion

This study described the design and initial implementation of a specific certified committee for regenerative medicine intended to review in vivo gene therapy and related technologies newly brought within the scope of the Act, together with ex vivo gene therapy, under the 2024 amendment to the Act on the Safety of Regenerative Medicine. Using a predefined evaluation framework, we demonstrated that this type of committee could be formally established, organized as an independent second committee with a shared institutional foundation, fulfilled the additional required member categories for review of in vivo gene therapy and related technologies newly brought within the scope of the Act, and operated through a standardized workflow that was applicable at the first review meeting. This study was intended to assess the feasibility of implementation rather than the quality of the review itself. These findings support the practical implementation of a gene therapy–oriented review committee within an existing institutional framework for regenerative medicine review.

The importance of this finding lies in the regulatory and scientific implications of the amended Act. The expansion of the Act to include in vivo gene therapy and related technologies means that certified committees must now address issues that extend beyond those typically encountered during a review of cell-based regenerative medicine [1,4]. In addition to general considerations of scientific validity, ethics, and participant protection, a review of in vivo gene therapy and related technologies newly brought within the scope of the Act requires expertise related to specified nucleic acids and related compounds, their manufacture and delivery, their effects on the human body, and the handling of genetically modified organisms [4,5]. Therefore, the present study addressed a practical question arising directly from the legal amendment, namely how an institution can build a review system that is formally compliant while also remaining substantively capable of evaluating these additional issues.

A central feature of the present model was the combination of independence and continuity. Alternative organizational approaches could include integration into an existing committee, complete separation, or a regional/shared review model. Each of these has distinct advantages and challenges (Table 3). In our institutional context, the dual model was considered the most practical option for balancing independence and continuity while preserving a shared institutional foundation. We judged that visible independence of gene therapy–specific expertise was necessary while still preserving administrative continuity and reviewer availability. Complete separation would have required that the new committee develop its deliberative foundation entirely de novo, which would incur a greater operational burden and impart less stability during the early phase. By contrast, the dual model allowed the new committee to build on the mature deliberative culture already established in the existing committee, including shared perspectives in law, bioethics, biostatistics/clinical trials, and lay representation.

At the same time, simple integration into an existing committee was considered less appropriate because issues specific to gene therapy and related technologies, as well as the expertise additionally required for their review, needed a clearer and more independent representation in the deliberative structure. This included the roles of Category 9 and Category 10 members, along with Category 4 expertise specifically relevant to specified nucleic acids, rather than only cell-processed products. This design enabled the concentration of gene therapy-specific expertise where it was needed, while preserving continuity in the related legal, ethical, methodological, and lay perspectives.

The membership findings are also important. Committee II fulfilled the required categories specific to review of in vivo gene therapy and related technologies newly brought within the scope of the Act, including Categories 9 and 10, in addition to the general categories required for certified committees for regenerative medicine. This point is not merely procedural. The additional categories introduced under the amended framework reflect the need for review capacity that is scientifically aligned with the distinct characteristics of gene therapy. In that sense, the present study's findings suggest that institutional preparedness for gene therapy review should not be judged solely by formal committee certification but should also consider whether the committee can secure and sustain the expertise needed for substantive deliberation [7,8].

Another important implication concerns the workflow design. Formal certification and member appointment alone are unlikely to ensure effective committee operation under a newly expanded regulatory framework. In the present model, the prior administrative checks by the secretariat and preliminary review by committee members incorporated before the formal meeting allowed clarification in advance regarding deficiencies in submission materials and key scientific or procedural issues. The successful application of this workflow at the first review meeting suggests that early operational standardization is an important component of implementation. This may be particularly relevant during the initial phase of gene therapy review, when the greatest uncertainty is likely to arise regarding procedures, documentation, and role allocation.

This study has several limitations. First, because it reflects the experience of a single institution, the present organizational model may not be directly transferable to all settings. Second, because the evaluation was limited to initial implementation and the first review meeting, it does not address long-term operation, reproducibility across multiple cases, objective evaluation of review quality, or consistency of decision-making over time. Third, this was a descriptive implementation study and did not compare alternative committee structures, such as full integration of gene therapy review into an existing committee or complete separation without any shared foundation. Future work should examine how different institutional models affect review efficiency, deliberative quality, sustainability of expert participation, and consistency of regulatory interpretation.

Despite these limitations, the present study provides a practical reference model for institutions preparing to review in vivo gene therapy and related technologies newly brought within the scope of the Act, together with ex vivo gene therapy, under the amended Act on the Safety of Regenerative Medicine. The key message is that the implementation of gene therapy review capacity requires integrated institutional design across organizational structure, member composition, and operational workflow. Within that context, a dual-committee model with a shared foundation appears to offer a feasible approach for expanding review capacity while maintaining continuity in participant protection and committee operation. This interpretation directly supports the conclusion that a gene therapy–oriented review committee can be designed and initially implemented in a practical and operable manner within an existing review infrastructure for regenerative medicine.

Conclusions

We designed and initially implemented a specific certified committee for regenerative medicine intended to review in vivo gene therapy and related technologies newly brought within the scope of the Act, together with ex vivo gene therapy, under the amended Act on the Safety of Regenerative Medicine. The results suggest that a dual-committee model with a shared institutional foundation can provide a practical and operationally feasible approach for institutions seeking to expand review capacity for gene therapy within an existing regenerative medicine review infrastructure.
