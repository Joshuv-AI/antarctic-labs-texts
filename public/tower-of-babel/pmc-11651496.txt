Whether perceived limb heaviness affects return to work among patients with ischemic stroke
Authors: Yuanyuan Chen, Hongyan Yang, Yanqin Chen, Hui Wei, Meijuan Lan
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
This study aims to explore the relationship between perceived limb heaviness and return to work (RTW) among ischemic stroke patients. We retrospectively reviewed the data of consecutive participants recruited in the CIRCLE study. Perceived limb heaviness was gathered from self-reported by 1-year follow-up. The chi-square test and logistic regression analysis were used to analyze the independent risk factors of RTW. A total of 209 participants completed the study. The participants were predominately 81.3% male, with a mean age of 51.24 ± 9.09 years. In total, 79 participants (37.8%) had returned to work 12 months after the stroke, 36 (17.2%) felt limb heaviness, and 67.9% had a son or 2 sons. The participants who had not RTW were on contemplation status, and those who had RTW were on proactive maintenance status. Logistic regression analysis showed that participants with 2 sons were independently associated with an increased chance of RTW (P = .016, odds ratio [OR] = 3.990). Participants who had perceived limb heaviness were independently associated with a low rate of RTW compared with no perceived limb heaviness (P = .009, OR = 0.235). This research finds that participants experiencing limb heaviness might lower the rate of RTW after 12 months. The other interesting finding is that participants with 2 sons might have a higher rate of RTW.

1. Introduction

Stroke, a global health problem, is a leading cause of disability.[1] Whether it is physical or psychological dysfunction would have a significant impact on return to work (RTW).[2] RTW has been identified as a critical priority in the National Institute of Health and Care Excellence guidelines on stroke rehabilitation.[3] RTW is vital for life satisfaction after a stroke. Furthermore, it is economically essential for the family and society that the RTW is optimized because not RTW means a loss of family income and a loss of productivity for society.[4] Optimizing RTW is a significant social issue.

The frequency of RTW in previous studies has varied from geographical and period time, with RTW rates in working-age adults from 11% to 85%.[5] The surveys show that only 16.4% of participants had RTW in the United States,[6] 39.1% in the Netherlands,[7] and 48.3% in Sweden,[8] 17% in China.[9] So, some European countries have high rates of RTW, and others have lower rates. For RTW analyses in Sweden, almost 50% of participants RTW within 3 months, 70% within 1 year, 80% within 2 years, and 85% for several years post-stroke.[10] In order to improve the rate of RTW, barriers to RTW should be found.

Different factors have been identified as barriers to RTW. The demographic factors, like older age, female, and educational qualifications, are negatively associated with RTW.[11,12] Severe stroke and dysfunction are the critical factors that affect RTW.[13,14] Post-stroke fatigue, depression, and post-stroke cognitive impairment also will lead to a low rate of RTW.[15,16] The social factors are the national policy of rehabilitation strategy, employment, and public subsidies for disabilities.[17] In summary, the previous research on factors of RTW is mainly focused on the dimension of demographic characteristics, which are disease-related, psychological, and social. The relationship between sensorimotor and RTW has yet to be discussed.

Perceived limb heaviness in stroke is commonly thought of as a manifestation of muscle weakness.[18] However, Kuppuswamy et al[19] study discovered that limb heaviness was not because of muscle weakness, but maybe a centrally arising sensorimotor disorder. Previous research also indicated that changes in muscle strength would not lead to perceived limb heaviness, perhaps a sensorimotor disorder of central origin.[20] This implies that sensorimotor dysfunction would exacerbate perceived limb heaviness, and hinder work sustainability, potentially impacting RTW.[7,15] Given the low rate of RTW after stroke, it is imperative to explore the contributing factors. Perceived limb heaviness, as a form of sensorimotor dysfunction, maybe one such factor affecting RTW. To our knowledge, whether perceived limb heaviness after stroke alters RTW remains unclear. So, exploring the further relationship between them was crucial. In this study, we aimed to screen participants with perceived limb heaviness and evaluate the status of RTW by the readiness for return-to-work scale (RRTW), and subsequently examine the correlation between these variables.

2. Methods

2.1. Participants

In this study, we retrospectively reviewed the data of consecutive participants recruited in the CIRCLE study[21] (Clinical Trials. gov ID: NCT03702452) between November 21, 2018, and November 19, 2019. The CIRCLE study was to verify that nursing-directed rehabilitation in patients with ischemic stroke can compensate for the shortage of professional rehabilitation therapists.[22] The inclusion criteria are between 18 and 64 years old; diagnosed as having an ischemic stroke by computed tomography or magnetic resonance imaging and meeting the diagnostic criteria of the World Health Organization; had a 5-day workweek for the past 3 months before stroke; have the ability of communication; and having signed an informed consent form. The exclusion criteria are death within the research cycle; recurrence of stroke in a 1-year follow-up; cardiopulmonary dysfunction; a history of craniocerebral trauma, fracture trauma, or rheumatoid arthritis; or already had a physical disability or other diseases that had an impact on the affected work; cognitive impairment or other mental illness that prevents cooperation with researchers. All the participants were provided with rehabilitation therapy after 24 hours of hospital admission and had a 1-year follow-up. A total of 209 participants were included in this study (Fig. 1).

2.2. Measures

2.2.1. Demographic and clinical variables

Demographic and clinical variables were abstracted from medical records. The variables included age, gender, educational background, work, and first stroke. Given that the parents support sons more in traditional families in China, tremendous pressure would appear on families with sons, and we collected the number of sons. Muscle strength of the affected limbs was assessed by the Medical Research Council scale through telephone interviews.

2.2.2. Perceived limb heaviness

Perceived limb heaviness[19] was measured by 2 levels of a question, “My limbs can become very heavy (compared with the other side or before).” The question had 2 answers asking participants to agree or disagree with the statement. If the participants agreed with these statements, they had perceived limb heaviness. If the participants disagreed with either statement, they did not perceive limb heaviness. Participants were instructed to answer the question based on how they felt in the past 1 week through telephone by 1-year follow-up. In this study, perceived limb heaviness was independently administered to all participants.

2.2.3. Independence function assessment

The modified Barthel index (MBI)[23,24] consists of 10 items, 100 total points, and 5 different weights of rating scales. It has been widely used in China and has reliability (intraclass correlation coefficient = 0.866–0.997), and high criterion-related validity (R = 0.816–1.000) with the BI, a score of above 80 was considered functional recovery. The modified Rankin Scale (MRS) has 7 levels used to measure neurological recovery in patients after stroke.[25] Interobserver variability, using pooled reliability, yielded a weighted κ of 0.90.[25] A score below 2 was considered functional recovery. All 2 scales were gathered from self-reported through telephone by 1-year follow-up.

2.2.4. Readiness for return-to-work scale

The RRTW was developed by Franche et al[26] and used in musculoskeletal disorders, and then widely used in cancer and mental or behavioral disorders. It reflects the stage of behavioral intention when the patient returns to work. The RRTW consists of 2 parts, 6 dimensions, and 22 items. The first part is aimed at non-returning workers, including 4 dimensions and 13 items: pre-contemplation (RTW has not been considered because of injury or illness), contemplation (start thinking about RTW), prepared for action—self-evaluative (3 items), and prepared for action—behavioral (start preparing for RTW, such as seeking information about RTW actively or making specific plans); the second part focuses on returned workers, including 2 dimensions and 9 items: uncertain maintenance (implement a program to RTW) and proactive maintenance (maintain the current work). The scale was scored at the Likert 5-level and assigned 1 to 5 points from “strongly disagree” to “strongly agree.” Item average score was calculated for each dimension, and the highest item average score indicated the participants’ stage. The information was gathered from self-reported through telephone by 1-year follow-up.

2.2.5. Statistical analysis

Data analysis was conducted using IBM SPSS version 24. All statistical tests were 2-tailed and took an α of 0.05 to indicate significance. Used descriptive statistics to report the demographic and clinical characteristics of the participants, including age, gender, educational background, number of sons, muscle grade of the limbs, MBI, MRS, perceived limb heaviness, and RTW. The age, MRS, and MBI were converted to categorical data, and person Chi-squared tests examined all the data. Logistic regression analysis with backward stepwise selection (P > .10 for exclusion) was used to explore the association between age, number of sons, muscle of limbs, MRS, MBI, perceived limb heaviness, and RTW.

2.3. Patient and public involvement

Patients and the public were not involved in this research’s design, conduct, reporting, or dissemination plans.

3. Results

Seventy-nine participants (37.8%) had RTW 12 months after the stroke. Participants were predominately male (81.3%), living alone (7.7%), working full-time before stroke (59.8%), and having suffered a first ischemic stroke (89.0%). The mean age was 51.24 ± 9.09 years, the mean muscle grade of the upper (lower) limb was 4.32 ± 1.50 (4.75 ± 0.76), and most of the participants had a son (67.9%), they had a difference between RTW and not RTW. Gender, education level, work, living alone, and the first stroke were not significantly associated with RTW. See Table 1 for patient demographics.

4. Discussion

In this study, the main findings were perceived limb heaviness would result in a lower rate of RTW after stroke; in China, having 2 sons may lead to a high rate of RTW after a stroke; 1 year later, the effect of demographic data and ADL status on RTW after stroke may be minimal; the patients with stroke who did not have RTW were on contemplation status, and those who had RTW were on proactive maintenance status.

The study results revealed an interesting conclusion that the perceived limb heaviness after stroke is related to RTW (Table 3). The possible explanation was that perceived limb heaviness would significantly impact physical strength, energy levels, and concentration. When a patient perceives their limb as heavy, it may impede their belief in a full recovery from the stroke, potentially hindering their ability to RTW. Perceived limb heaviness was a common phenomenon, but was easy to ignore.[20] Kuppuswamy et al[19] found that limb heaviness was perhaps a sensorimotor disorder of central nervous system. When patients suffered a stroke, the higher motor cortical activity would be affected, related to a sensation of heaviness in the limbs.[27] Consequently, stroke patients with sensorimotor motor disorders would experience increased limb heaviness,[18,19] which greatly impeded their reintegration into community. RTW after a stroke required a lot of limb activity, and some tasks even required speed or weight, which would lead to physical fatigue and reduce energy levels.[28] Patients experienced limb heaviness after stroke would perceive fatigue more intensely, acted as a barrier to engaging in vigorous exercise and altered RTW.[29] Previous research has indicated that perceived limb heaviness can exacerbate fatigue, which in turn affects RTW.[18,19,30] Therefore, it is plausible to assert that perceived limb heaviness, as a sensorimotor issue, can diminish the motivation for RTW after stroke.

China is a developing country, and the burden of rehabilitation, care, and economic costs after stroke is very high, especially for families, rehabilitation.[31] The pressure of raising a son could be handled. When the family had 2 sons, the burden could become more serious. In the traditional culture of China, the son was the primary provider of support for the older. Also, the parents supported them more.[32] Participants in this study were predominately male (81.3%) and were the mainstay of the economy. The more sons they have, the heavier the burden. This encouraged them to RTW sooner. So, 2 sons would relate to a higher rate of RTW.

In this research, we found that the effect of ADL status on RTW after stroke may be minimal 1 year later. Almost 80% of participants were independent of ADL at 1-year follow-up. The degree of neurologic impairment recovery was better than in the previous study.[1] That was because the severe patients with ischemic stroke were not recruited in the CIRCLE study. This indicated that mild or moderate patients with stroke who received treatment and rehabilitation regularly would be independent of ADL and could not become the main factor of RTW. Age was the factor of RTW in the previous research.[7,12] The older the participants, the lower the rate of RTW. However, in this study, the rate of RTW was also low among young participants. A possible explanation was that young people have high expectations of work in Chinese. The patient had not quite recovered in the 1-year follow-up and was not prepared for RTW. Therefore, the rate of RTW was similar between different ages.

Further analysis of the status of RTW revealed that patients with stroke who had not RTW were on contemplation status, and those who had RTW were on proactive maintenance status. After 1 year, 36.2% of participants without RTW had yet to prepare for work. This reflected the perceived limb heaviness, making them not confident in RTW.

5. Limitation

This study had some limitations. First, this was single-center research; the results could only represent one region and might bias the representation of all hospitalized individuals, and the study findings might not be generalized to all patients with stroke. Second, the RRTW was a self-report questionnaire, which may not reflect the actual situation of the patients with stroke. Finally, the sample size may be more significant, and the period may be extended to get more stable results.

6. Conclusion

In conclusion, the perceived limb heaviness of patients with stroke was the factor in a lower rate of RTW. This was an exciting result and would provide new information for research on RTW. If we identified the perceived limb heaviness in a timely and gave effective intervention, a low rate of RTW would be potentially preventable.
