Stochastic variance reduced extragradient methods for solving hierarchical variational inequalities
Pavel Dvurechensky, Andrea Ebner, Johannes Carl Schnebel, Shimrit Shtern, Mathias Staudigl

Abstract
We are concerned with optimization in a broad sense through the lens of solving variational inequalities (VIs) – a class of problems that are so general that they cover as particular cases minimization of functions, saddle-point (minimax) problems, Nash equilibrium problems, and many others. The key challenges in our problem formulation are the two-level hierarchical structure and finite-sum representation of the smooth operators in each level. For this setting, we are the first to prove convergence rates and complexity statements for variance-reduced stochastic algorithms approaching the solution of hierarchical VIs in Euclidean and Bregman setups.

Introduction

Hierarchical optimization is an increasingly active research area with many applications in machine learning, see, e.g.,
[
30
,
37
,
62
,
8
,
20
]
. At the same time, the algorithmic solution of hierarchical variational inequalities (VIs) is a much less studied field, despite its applications going far beyond ML, including optimal control and mechanics
[
9
]
, network/traffic and economic equilibrium modeling
[
28
,
51
]
, and noncooperative game-theoretic equilibrium selection
[
56
,
46
]
.
Our main goal is to advance algorithmic and complexity understanding of hierarchical VIs in the big data setting when the involved operators admit a finite sum representation. Particularly, we show that variance reduction techniques also work in this complex context and lead to an algorithm that has improved sample complexity compared to the state-of-the-art.

For the mathematical statement of the problem, let
\mathcal{X}
be a finite dimensional real vector space with inner product
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
and corresponding norm
\norm{\cdot}
. We solve the following hierarchical VI

(P)

\text{ Find }x^{\ast}\in\mathcal{S}_{2}\text{ s.t. }\langle{\mathsf{F}}_{1}(x^{\ast}),x-x^{\ast}\rangle+g_{1}(x)-g_{1}(x^{\ast})\geq 0\quad\forall x\in\mathcal{S}_{2}\triangleq\zer({\mathsf{F}}_{2}+\partial g_{2}).

The data of this problem consist of monotone operators
F_{i}:\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{X},i=1,2
and proper convex lower semi-continuous functions
g_{i}:\mathcal{X}\to(-\infty,\infty]
. Before we proceed, we give several motivating examples. We also refer to Section
4
where we provide numerical tests of our algorithms.

Example 1.1

(equilibrium selection)
.

An important class of problems falling in the domain of (
P
) is the equilibrium selection problem

\min_{x}f_{1}(x)+g_{1}(x)\quad\text{s.t. }x\in\textnormal{SOL}({\mathsf{F}}_{2},\mathcal{K})

where
f_{1}:\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}
is Lipschitz smooth and
g_{1}:\mathcal{X}\to(-\infty,\infty]
is a proper closed convex function, and
\mathcal{K}\subseteq\mathcal{X}
a nonempty closed convex set. In this formulation, the composite convex function in the upper level defines a design criterion, used to select among the solution set of a variational inequality problem

\text{ Find }y\in\mathcal{K}\text{ s.t. : }\langle{\mathsf{F}}_{2}(y),x-y\rangle\geq 0\qquad\forall x\in\mathcal{K}.

In this case, finite-sum representation on the lower level naturally appears when this VI comes from matrix games or constrained optimization reformulated as a saddle-point problem
[
1
,
49
]
.
Stochastic versions of the equilibrium selection problem have only been studied so far in
[
36
]
. They develop a stochastic approximation algorithm based on the extragradient method, and show an
O(1/\varepsilon^{4})
iteration complexity at both levels. This rate is inferior to our scheme, which can achieve
O(1/\varepsilon^{3})
iteration complexity.
If
{\mathsf{F}}_{2}=\nabla f_{2}
, for a convex smooth and real-valued function
f_{2}
, we recover the simple bilevel optimization problem
[
59
,
23
]
. As far as we know, the only analysis of stochastic versions of the simple bilevel optimization problem is reported in
[
18
]
, where a stochastic Frank-Wolfe method is shown to display an
O(1/\varepsilon)
iteration complexity.

Example 1.2

(Hierarchical games)
.

Hierarchical games are a useful model template for computational approaches to competitive mechanism design and certain dynamic games. The key goal of mechanism design is to implement a desired Nash equilibrium in a population of agents. Hence, the designers need to anticipate the equilibrium conditions of the lower level problem. For concreteness, we consider the situation in which two upper level players manage the strategies of
N
lower-level players. The lower level players engage in a Nash game, characterized by local minimisation problems

\min_{y^{\nu}\in\mathcal{Y}^{\nu}}\{h^{\ell}_{\nu}(y^{\nu},y^{-\nu})+\varphi^{\ell}_{\nu}(y^{\nu})\}.

Under standard differentiability and monotonicity assumptions on the data of the game, we can characterize equilibria of this lower level game in terms the variational inequality
\mathcal{S}_{2}=\zer({\mathsf{F}}_{2}+\partial g_{2})
, where
{\mathsf{F}}_{2}(y)=(\nabla_{y^{1}}h^{\ell}_{1}(y),\ldots,\nabla_{y^{N}}h^{\ell}_{N}(y))
. The problem of the upper level players is to select a Nash equilibrium in the lower level game by itself being engaged in a strategic optimisation problem of the form

\min_{x^{\mu}\in\mathcal{X}^{mu}}\{h^{u}_{\mu}(x^{\mu},x^{-\mu})+\varphi^{u}_{\mu}(x^{\mu})\}\quad\text{s.t.: }(x^{\mu},x^{-\mu})\in\mathcal{S}_{2},\mu\in\{1,2\}.

The strategy
x^{\mu}=(y^{\nu})_{\nu\in\mathcal{N}_{\mu}}
consists of the actions of the adjunct lower level players. Writing the equilibrium conditions for the resulting hierarchical games leads to the problem formulation (
P
).

1.1
Related works

Variational inequalities

VIs provide a versatile mathematical model for numerically approaching important classes of equilibrium problems in game theory, control, and learning theory. A very popular choice for solving VIs has been the extragradient method (EG)
[
40
]
. For single-level VIs with Lipschitz operator, the standard convergence results for these algorithms display complexity
O(\varepsilon^{-1})
for monotone problems, and linear rates of convergence for strongly monotone problems. Complexity statements are usually made in terms of ergodic averages and well-defined gap functions. Generalizations for stochastic problems with infinite expectations were proposed in
[
38
,
41
]
. The complexity of such problems contains an extra term
O(\varepsilon^{-2})
in the monotone setting and
O(\varepsilon^{-1})
in the strongly monotone setting due to the variance of stochastic realizations of the operator, and this can not be improved without changing the problem class. For the setting of finite expectation, i.e., when the operator is given as a sum of finitely many elements, variance reduction methods allow to improve the rates back to
O(\varepsilon^{-1})
complexity for monotone problems and linear rate for strongly monotone settings
[
7
,
34
,
22
,
1
,
31
]
. For further references, we refer to a recent survey
[
12
]
.

Hierarchical VIs

Despite bilevel optimization and equilibrium selection being well-understood (see, e.g., recent survey
[
11
]
), we are aware of only a few papers on numerical methods for solving hierarchical VIs
[
61
,
60
,
42
]
. Among those, only
[
42
]
contains complexity statements. Recently, the papers
[
57
,
2
,
25
]
have addressed deterministic hierarchical VIs and proposed generalizations of EG for this setting with the rates
O(1/k^{\delta})
(lower level) and
O(1/k^{1-\delta})
(upper level), where
\delta\in(0,1)
and
k
is the iteration counter.
[
39
]
addresses the full stochastic setting where the operators are given in terms of mathematical expectations. They obtains worse complexities due to the variance in the stochastic oracle.

1.2
Outline of results and comparison

To the best of our knowledge, we propose the first set of variance reduction algorithms for hierarchical VIs in the finite expectation setting. Namely, we propose a simple algorithm for the Euclidean setup and another algorithm for the general Bregman setting, both achieving the same convergence rates
O(1/k^{\delta})
(lower level) and
O(1/k^{1-2\delta})
(upper level), i.e., we nearly achieve the deterministic rates showing that in this challenging context of hierarchical VIs variance reduction is a powerful technique. An important ingredient of our analysis is showing that, thanks to a special geometric Attouch-Czarnecki condition
[
4
]
on the lower-level solution set, the iterates of the algorithm are almost surely bounded. This allows us to drop the assumption of a compact domain of
g_{1}
and/or
g_{2}
used in previous works on hierarchical VIs.

We derive the main structural results for resolving the hierarchical equilibrium problem, picking the extragradient method, a popular algorithmic template, with numerous applications in AI and ML, particularly in the context of GAN training

2.1
Preliminaries

Let
\mathcal{X}
be a finite dimensional vector space with Euclidean inner produce
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
and induced norm
\norm{\cdot}
. The proximal operator is defined as

\prox_{g}(x)=\argmin\{g(y)+\frac{1}{2}\norm{y-x}^{2}\}
. We have

(2.1)

\bar{z}=\prox_{g}(z)\Leftrightarrow\langle\bar{z}-z,u-\bar{z}\rangle\geq g(z)-g(\bar{z})\qquad\forall u\in\mathcal{X}.

Assumption 1
.

The following hypothesis are assumed to hold throughout the paper:

(i)

The solution set
\mathcal{S}_{1}
of (
P
) is nonempty. In particular,
\mathcal{S}_{2}\triangleq\zer({\mathsf{F}}_{2}+\partial g_{2})\neq\varnothing
.

(ii)

The functions
g_{i}:\mathcal{X}\to(-\infty,\infty],i=1,2
are proper convex and lower semi-continuous.

(iii)

The operators
{\mathsf{F}}_{i},i=1,2,
are monotone and
L_{{\mathsf{F}}_{i}}
-Lipschtiz.

(iv)

The operators
{\mathsf{F}}_{i}
admit a finite sum representation
{\mathsf{F}}_{i}=\sum_{\mathtt{a}\in\mathcal{A}}{\mathsf{F}}_{i}^{\mathtt{a}}
, where
\mathcal{A}
is a finite index set, and
{\mathsf{F}}_{i}^{\mathtt{a}}:\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{X}
are given mappings.

We remark that Assumption
1
implies that
\mathcal{S}_{2}
is a non-empty, closed, and convex set
[
10
]
.

Remark 2.1
.

The assumption that both operators
{\mathsf{F}}_{1},{\mathsf{F}}_{2}
can be represented as a finite sum with indices coming from a common index set
\mathcal{A}
is without loss of generality. Indeed, if we would have the representation
{\mathsf{F}}_{i}=\sum_{\alpha\in\mathcal{A}_{i}}{\mathsf{F}}_{i}^{\mathtt{a}}
, then define
\mathcal{A}=\mathcal{A}_{1}\cup\mathcal{A}_{2}
, and declare for example
\mathtt{a}\in\mathcal{A}_{i}\setminus\mathcal{A}_{j}
the operator
{\mathsf{F}}_{j}^{\mathtt{a}}=0
. Alternatively, we could label the mappings with a double index
\mathtt{a}=(\mathtt{a}_{1},\mathtt{a}_{2})\in\mathcal{A}_{1}\times\mathcal{A}_{2}\equiv\mathcal{A}
.

We also assume the non-smooth part
g_{1}
of the upper-level VI to satisfy a finite variation property, where we define the variation over the set
\mathcal{B}\times\mathcal{C}\subseteq\mathcal{Z}\times\mathcal{Z}
as

\Var(g_{1}|\mathcal{B}\times\mathcal{C})\triangleq\sup_{(x,y)\in\mathcal{B}\times\mathcal{C}}\absolutevalue{g_{1}(x)-g_{1}(y)}.

Assumption 2
.

The set
\dom(g_{1})\cap\dom(g_{2})
is closed, and for any compact sets
\mathcal{B},\mathcal{C}\subset\mathcal{Z}
satisfying
\mathcal{B},\mathcal{C}\subset\dom(g_{1})\cap\dom(g_{2})
, we have
\Var(g_{1}|\mathcal{B}\times\mathcal{C})<\infty
.

Assumption 3
.

The solution set
\mathcal{S}_{1}
of (
P
) satisfies

\mathcal{S}_{1}=\zer({\mathsf{F}}_{1}+\partial g_{1}+\NC_{\mathcal{S}_{2}}).

Similar assumptions have been made in
[
15
]
for solving constrained variational inequalities. It is essentially a constraint qualification assumption, and a rather mild condition.

Define the mappings
{\mathsf{V}}_{k}(x)\triangleq\beta_{k}{\mathsf{F}}_{1}(x)+{\mathsf{F}}_{2}(x)
and
G_{k}(x)\triangleq\beta_{k}g_{1}(x)+g_{2}(x)
. The combined operator has again a finite sum structure
{\mathsf{V}}_{k}=\sum_{\mathtt{a}\in\mathcal{A}}{\mathsf{V}}^{\mathtt{a}}_{k}
. Moreover, each
{\mathsf{V}}_{k}
is monotone and Lipschitz. Let
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P})
be a probability space and
\xi:\Omega\to\Xi
a random variable. In order to realize our stochastic algorithm, we assume to have access to a stochastic oracle
{\mathsf{V}}_{k}^{\xi}
, returning noisy feedback information on the true mapping
{\mathsf{V}}_{k}
.

Assumption 4
.

For all
k\in\mathbb{N}
, the operator
{\mathsf{V}}_{k}
admits a stochastic oracle
{\mathsf{V}}^{\xi}_{k}
such that
\mathbb{E}[{\mathsf{V}}_{k}^{\xi}(x)]={\mathsf{V}}_{k}(x)
for all
x\in\mathcal{X}
. There exists
\mathcal{L}_{k}>0
such that
\sup_{k\geq 0}\mathcal{L}_{k}\leq L_{1}
and

(2.2)

\mathbb{E}\left[\,\norm{\opV^{\xi}_k(x) - \opV_k^{\xi}(y)}\right]\leq\mathcal{L}_{k}\norm{x-y}\qquad\forall x,y\in\mathcal{X}.

Example 2.1
.

Working in a finite sum setting, we can construct different stochastic oracles. Since
{\mathsf{V}}_{k}=\sum_{\mathtt{a}\in\mathcal{A}}{\mathsf{V}}^{\mathtt{a}}_{k}
, if each
{\mathsf{V}}^{\mathtt{a}}_{k}
is
L_{k,\mathtt{a}}
-Lipschitz, then the triangle inequality gives
L_{{\mathsf{V}}_{k}}\leq\sum_{\mathtt{a}\in\mathcal{A}}L_{k,\mathtt{a}}
. The two simplest stochastic oracles can be defined as follows:

1.

{\mathsf{V}}^{\mathtt{a}}_{k}(x)=\absolutevalue{\scrA}{\mathsf{V}}^{\mathtt{a}}_{k}(x)
and
\xi:\Omega\to\mathcal{A}
is the uniformly distributed random variable with law
Q_{\mathtt{a}}\triangleq\mathbb{P}(\xi=\mathtt{a})=\frac{1}{\absolutevalue{\scrA}}
. In this case
\mathcal{L}_{k}=\sqrt{\absolutevalue{\scrA}\sum_{\mathtt{a}\in\mathcal{A}}L_{k,a}^{2}}
. Since
\beta_{k}\leq\beta_{1}
, we can take as upper bound on the Lipschitz modulus
L_{1}=\mathcal{L}_{1}
.

2.

Let
\xi:\Omega\to\mathcal{A}
be the random variable with law
Q_{\mathtt{a}}\triangleq\mathbb{P}(\xi=\mathtt{a})=\frac{L_{1,\mathtt{a}}}{\sum_{\mathtt{a}\in\mathcal{A}}L_{1,\mathtt{a}}}
. Set
{\mathsf{V}}^{\xi}_{k}(x)=\frac{1}{q_{a}}{\mathsf{V}}^{\mathtt{a}}_{k}
on the event
\{\xi=\mathtt{a}\}
. In this case, we have
\mathcal{L}_{k}=\sum_{\mathtt{a}\in\mathcal{A}}L_{1,\mathtt{a}}=L_{1}.

Gap functions for hierarchical VI’s.

Gap functions are a common tool to measure the suboptimality of a given test point when solving a variational inequality. For single-level VIs, a very common formulation of a gap function is given by

(2.3)

\Theta(x|{\mathsf{F}},g,\mathcal{U})=\sup_{y\in\mathcal{U}}\left\{H^{({\mathsf{F}},g)}(x,y)\triangleq\langle{\mathsf{F}}(y),x-y\rangle+g(x)-g(y)\right\},

where
\mathcal{U}
is a compact subset of
\mathcal{X}
, used to deal with the possibility of unboundedness of
\dom(g)

[
58
,
50
]
. Since our aim is to solve hierarchical variational systems, we have to define merit functions for the upper and lower level.

Definition 2.1
.

The feasibility gap and optimality gap for (
P
) are defined as

(2.4)

\displaystyle\Theta_{\rm Feas}(x|\mathcal{C})=\sup_{y\in\mathcal{C}}H^{({\mathsf{F}}_{2},g_{2})}(x,y)\qquad\text{ and }\qquad\Theta_{\rm Opt}(x|\mathcal{C}\cap\mathcal{S}_{2})=\sup_{y\in\mathcal{C}\cap\mathcal{S}_{2}}H^{({\mathsf{F}}_{1},g_{1})}(x,y).

To obtain two-sided bounds on gap functions, we invoke a sharpness condition of the lower-level solution set of (
P
).

Definition 2.2

(Weak Sharpness)
.

Let
\mathcal{S}
be the solution set of
\HVI({\mathsf{F}},g)
. We say
\mathcal{S}
is
(\kappa,\rho)
-weak sharp with
\kappa>0
and
\rho\in(1,2)
if

(2.5)

(\forall z^{\ast}\in\mathcal{S})(\forall z\in\dom(g)):\quad H^{({\mathsf{F}},g)}(z,z^{\ast})\geq\kappa\rho^{-1}\dist(z,\mathcal{S})^{\rho}.

Weak sharpness is a common assumption in hierarchical optimization
[
17
,
21
,
14
]
, and hierarchical equilibrium problems alike
[
57
]
. Similar conditions have been used in an SVRG analysis of extragradient methods for single-level problems in
[
47
]
. Combining this geometric condition, with the optimality measures
\Theta_{\rm Opt}
and
\Theta_{\rm Feas}
, we are able to establish a-priori bounds on the gap functions involved in measuring the quality of test points
x
. The proof is given in Appendix
B
.

Lemma 2.3
.

Consider problem (
P
). Let Assumption
1
and
3
hold. Let
\mathcal{U}_{1}\subseteq\dom(g_{1})
be a nonempty compact set with
\mathcal{U}_{1}\cap\mathcal{S}_{1}\neq\varnothing
. Then, there exists a constant
B_{\mathcal{U}_{1}}>0
such that

(2.6)

\Theta_{\rm Opt}(x|\mathcal{U}_{1}\cap\mathcal{S}_{1})\geq-B_{\mathcal{U}_{1}}\dist(x,\mathcal{S}_{2})\quad\forall x\in\mathcal{X}.

Suppose
\mathcal{S}_{2}
is
(\kappa,\rho)
-weakly sharp. Then for all nonempty and compact subsets
\mathcal{U}_{2}\subseteq\dom(g_{2})
with
\mathcal{U}_{2}\cap\mathcal{S}_{2}\neq\varnothing
, and all
x\in\dom(g_{2})
, we have

(2.7)

\dist(x,\mathcal{S}_{2})\leq\left[\rho\kappa^{-1}\Theta_{\rm Feas}(x|\mathcal{U}_{2}\cap\mathcal{S}_{2})\right]^{1/\rho}.

\theta\in(0,1]
, probability distribution
Q
, step size
(\tau_{k})_{k},\alpha\in(0,1)

(\beta_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}

for

\xi_{k}\sim Q

\displaystyle z_{k}=\alpha x_{k}+(1-\alpha)w_{k}

\displaystyle w_{k+1}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}x_{k+1}&\text{ with probability }\theta\\

Algorithm 1

Hierarchical extragradient with variance reduction

The main result of this paper are two complexity statements for the averaged iterates generated by Algorithm
1
in terms of the gap functions (
2.4
). We work in a specific geometric setting in which we assume some structure on the solution set of the lower level problem. Associated to the bifunction
H^{({\mathsf{F}}_{2},g_{2})}
, Appendix
B
introduces the mapping

(2.8)

\varphi^{({\mathsf{F}}_{2},g_{2})}(x,u)\triangleq\sup_{y\in\dom(g_{2})}\{H^{({\mathsf{F}}_{2},g_{2})}(x,y)+\langle y,u\rangle\}.

This function encodes dual properties of the variational inequality, since by (
B.1
)

(2.9)

\varphi^{({\mathsf{F}}_{2},g_{2})}(x,u)\leq\sup_{y\in\dom(g_{2})}\{\langle y,u\rangle-H^{({\mathsf{F}}_{2},g_{2})}(y,x)\}=\left(H^{({\mathsf{F}}_{2},g_{2})}(\bullet,x)\right)^{\ast}(u).

The following summability condition is essentially due to
[
4
]
:

Assumption 5
.

[Attouch-Czarnecki condition]
The step size sequence
(\tau_{k})_{k\geq 0}
and the regularization sequence
(\beta_{k})_{k\geq 0}
satisfy

(2.10)

(\forall p\in\textnormal{Range}(\NC_{\mathcal{S}_{2}})):\;\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\tau_{k}\left[\sup_{z\in\mathcal{S}_{2}}\varphi^{({\mathsf{F}}_{2},g_{2})}(z,\beta_{k}p)-\supp(\beta_{k}p|\mathcal{S}_{2})\right]<\infty.

To understand the meaning of Assumption
5
, it is instructive to specialize our setting to the simple bilevel optimization case. In that situation, the data of the lower level problem are identified with
{\mathsf{F}}_{2}=\nabla f_{2}
and
\mathcal{S}_{2}=\argmin_{z}(f_{2}+g_{2})(z)
, and thus

\displaystyle H^{({\mathsf{F}}_{2},g_{2})}(y,z)

\displaystyle\leq f_{2}(y)-f_{2}(z)+g_{2}(y)-g_{2}(z)\triangleq\hat{f}_{2}(y)-\hat{f}_{2}(z)

\displaystyle=(\hat{f}_{2}-\min\hat{f}_{2})(y)-(\hat{f}_{2}-\min\hat{f}_{2})(z).

Hence,
\varphi^{({\mathsf{F}}_{2},g_{2})}(z,p)\leq(\hat{f}_{2}-\min\hat{f}_{2})(z)+(\hat{f}_{2}-\min\hat{f}_{2})^{*}(p)
. Since for
z\in\mathcal{S}_{2}
it holds
(\hat{f}_{2}-\min\hat{f}_{2})(z)=0
, this further implies

\sup_{z\in\mathcal{S}_{2}}\varphi^{({\mathsf{F}}_{2},g_{2})}(z,\beta_{k}p)-\supp(\beta_{k}p|\mathcal{S}_{2})\leq(\hat{f}_{2}-\min\hat{f}_{2})^{*}(\beta_{k}p)-\supp(\beta_{k}p|\mathcal{S}_{2}).

In
[
3
,
54
,
14
]
the summability condition

\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\tau_{k}\left[(\hat{f}_{2}-\min\hat{f}_{2})^{*}(\beta_{k}p)-\supp(\beta_{k}p|\mathcal{S}_{2})\right]<\infty

is imposed. Clearly, this condition is more restrictive than our condition (
2.10
).

Remark 2.2
.

Assumption
5
looks quite daunting to verify, but as already observed in
[
14
]
, it fits very natural to the geometric setting of this paper. Indeed, let us assume that
\mathcal{S}_{2}
is
(\kappa,\rho)
-weakly sharp, with
\kappa>0
and
\rho\in(1,2)
. According to Definition
2.2
, for all
x\in\dom(g_{2})
and for all

. Hence, eq. (

) yields for all
x\in\mathcal{S}_{2}

\displaystyle\varphi^{({\mathsf{F}}_{2},g_{2})}(x,\beta_{k}p^{\ast})-\supp(\beta_{k}p^{\ast}|\mathcal{S}_{2})

\displaystyle\leq\left(H^{({\mathsf{F}}_{2},g_{2})}(\bullet,x)\right)^{\ast}(\beta_{k}p^{\ast})-\supp(\beta_{k}p^{\ast}|\mathcal{S}_{2})\leq\kappa^{-\frac{1}{\rho-1}}\left(\frac{\rho-1}{\rho}\right)\beta_{k}^{\frac{\rho}{\rho-1}}\norm{p^{\ast}}^{\frac{\rho}{\rho-1}}.

This shows that under the
(\kappa,\rho)
-weak sharpness condition, the summability condition (
2.10
) is satisfied whenever
\sum_{k\geq 1}\tau_{k}\beta^{\frac{\rho}{\rho-1}}_{k}<\infty.

2.3
Analysis

To proceed with the analysis, we define the filtration
{\mathbb{H}}\triangleq(\mathcal{H}_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
and
{\mathbb{F}}\triangleq(\mathcal{F}_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}_{k}\triangleq\sigma(x^{0},\xi_{0},\ldots,\xi_{k-1},w_{k}),

Note that
\mathcal{H}_{k}\subset\mathcal{F}_{k}
for all

\mathcal{H}_{k}

\mathcal{F}_{k}

\mathsf{A}_{k+1}(\omega)\triangleq{\mathsf{V}}_{k}(w_{k}(\omega))+{\mathsf{V}}_{k}^{\xi_{k}}(y_{k+1}(\omega))-{\mathsf{V}}^{\xi_{k}}_{k}(w_{k}(\omega)).

By Assumption

\mathbb{E}[\mathsf{A}_{k+1}|\mathcal{H}_{k}]={\mathsf{V}}_{k}(y_{k+1})\;\mathbb{P}-\text{a.s.}

For the iterates

(2.13)

\displaystyle\Psi_{k}(x)\triangleq\langle{\mathsf{V}}_{k}(y_{k+1}),y_{k+1}-x\rangle+G_{k}(y_{k+1})-G_{k}(x)

Lemma 2.4
.

Let Assumptions
1

4
hold. Given
\alpha\in(0,1],\theta\in(0,1]
and
\tau_{k}\leq\frac{\sqrt{1-\alpha}}{\mathcal{L}_{k}}\gamma

\gamma\in(0,1)
. For any

Also we have for the update steps in the algorithm the following prox-like inequality:

B.1
Fitzpatrick and Gap functions

Let
\mathsf{M}:\mathcal{Z}\to 2^{\mathcal{Z}}
be a maximally monotone operator. The
Fitzpatrick function

[
29
,
10
]

\mathcal{F}_{\mathsf{M}}:\mathcal{Z}\to(-\infty,+\infty]
, associated with the operator
\mathsf{M}
, is defined as

\mathcal{F}_{\mathsf{M}}(x,u)\triangleq\sup_{(y,v)\in\gr(\mathsf{M})}\{\langle x,v\rangle+\langle y,u\rangle-\langle y,v\rangle\}.

Following
[
13
]
, we define the gap function
\gap_{\mathsf{M}}(x)\triangleq\mathcal{F}_{\mathsf{M}}(x,0)
.
\gap_{\mathsf{M}}
is convex, and in fact the smallest translation invariant gap function associated with the monotone operator
\mathsf{M}

[
13
, Theorem 3.1]
. Importantly, this gives the properties
\gap_{\mathsf{M}}(x)\geq 0
and
\gap_{\mathsf{M}}(x)=0
if and only if
x\in\zer(\mathsf{M})
. To make this concept concrete, observe that if
\mathsf{M}={\mathsf{F}}+\NC_{\mathcal{C}}
, then the above definition of the gap function reduces to the well-known Auslender dual gap function
[
27
,
5
]

\gap_{{\mathsf{F}}+\NC_{\mathcal{C}}}(x)=\sup_{y\in\mathcal{C}}\langle{\mathsf{F}}(y),x-y\rangle.

If
\mathsf{M}={\mathsf{F}}+\partial g
for a function
g\in\Gamma_{0}(\mathcal{Z})
, we easily obtain

\gap_{{\mathsf{F}}+\partial g}(x)\leq\sup_{y\in\dom(g)}\langle{\mathsf{F}}(y),x-y\rangle+g(x)-g(y).

To the data
({\mathsf{F}},g)
, we thus associate the bifunction
H^{({\mathsf{F}},g)}:\mathcal{Z}\times\mathcal{Z}\to[-\infty,\infty]
defined as

H^{({\mathsf{F}},g)}(x,y)\triangleq\langle{\mathsf{F}}(y),x-y\rangle+g(x)-g(y).

If
{\mathsf{F}}:\mathcal{Z}\to\mathcal{Z}
is monotone and continuous on
\dom(g)
, it is easy to verify that
H^{({\mathsf{F}},g)}(x,y)\leq-H^{({\mathsf{F}},g)}(y,x)
for all
(x,y)\in\dom(g)\times\dom(g)
(i.e.
H^{({\mathsf{F}},g)}
is a monotone bifunction
[
35
]
). In the structured setting
\HVI({\mathsf{F}},g)
, we obtain the following bounds on the Fitzpatrick function, showing its close connection to the bifunction
H^{({\mathsf{F}},g)}
and the Auslender dual gap function. First, the convex subgradient inequality yields the relation

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{{\mathsf{F}}+\partial g}(x,u)

\displaystyle=\sup_{y\in\dom(\partial g),\xi\in\partial g(y)}\{\langle x-y,{\mathsf{F}}(y)\rangle+\langle\xi,x-y\rangle+\langle y,u\rangle\}

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{y\in\dom(g)}\{\langle{\mathsf{F}}(y),x-y\rangle+g(x)-g(y)+\langle y,u\rangle\}

\displaystyle=\sup_{y\in\dom(g)}\{H^{({\mathsf{F}},g)}(x,y)+\langle y,u\rangle\}\triangleq\varphi^{({\mathsf{F}},g)}(x,u).

The function
\varphi^{({\mathsf{F}},g)}(x,u)
is thus seen as the Fitzpatrick transform of
H^{({\mathsf{F}},g)}
. Second, since the function
x\mapsto H^{({\mathsf{F}},g)}(x,y)
is convex, we see

(B.1)

\varphi^{({\mathsf{F}},g)}(x,u)\leq\sup_{y\in\dom(g)}\{\langle y,u\rangle-H^{({\mathsf{F}},g)}(y,x)\}=\left(H^{({\mathsf{F}},g)}(\bullet,x)\right)^{\ast}(u).

In particular,

(B.2)

\displaystyle\varphi^{({\mathsf{F}},g)}(x,u)\geq\langle x,u\rangle\qquad\forall x\in\dom(g),

(B.3)

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{{\mathsf{F}}+\partial g}(x,0)\leq\sup_{y\in\dom(g)}H^{({\mathsf{F}},g)}(x,y)=\varphi^{({\mathsf{F}},g)}(x,0).

Following this notation, we define for a given a subset
\mathcal{C}\subseteq\mathcal{Z}
, the restricted dual gap function as

(B.4)

\Theta(x|{\mathsf{F}},g,\mathcal{C})\triangleq\sup_{y\in\mathcal{C}}H^{({\mathsf{F}},g)}(x,y).

The role of such localized gap functions is made clear in the next lemma, whose proof can be found in
[
25
]
.

Lemma B.1
.

Let
\mathcal{C}\subset\dom(g)
be a nonempty compact convex set. Consider problem
\HVI({\mathsf{F}},g)
with
{\mathsf{F}}:\mathcal{Z}\to\mathcal{Z}
monotone and Lipschitz continuous. The function
x\mapsto\Theta(x|{\mathsf{F}},g,\mathcal{C})
is well-defined and convex on
\mathcal{Z}
. For any
x\in\mathcal{C}
we have
\Theta(x|{\mathsf{F}},g,\mathcal{C})\geq 0.
Moreover, if
x\in\mathcal{C}
is a solution to
\HVI({\mathsf{F}},g)
, then
\Theta(x|{\mathsf{F}},g,\mathcal{C})=0
. Conversely, if
\Theta(x|{\mathsf{F}},g,\mathcal{C})=0
for some
x\in\mathcal{C}
for which there exists an
\varepsilon>0
such that
\mathbb{B}(x,\varepsilon)\cap\mathcal{C}=\mathbb{B}(x,\varepsilon)\cap\dom(g)
, then
x
is a solution of
\HVI({\mathsf{F}},g)
.

Our geometric framework is phrased in terms of weak-sharpness of the lower-level solution set

. Originally formulated for optimization problems in
[

]
, weak-sharpness in the context of variational inequalities has been defined in

]
. Subsequently, implications in terms of error bounds of primal and dual gap functions have been stated in

. The following definition of weak sharpness is from

]
.

Definition B.2
.

Let

be continuous and monotone over

. Assume
\dom(g)
is closed. The solution set
\mathcal{S}
of

is

such that

(\forall z^{\ast}\in\mathcal{S}):\quad\tau\mathbb{B}(0,1)\subseteq{\mathsf{F}}(z^{\ast})+\partial g(z^{\ast})+[\TC_{\dom(g)}(z^{\ast})\cap\NC_{\mathcal{S}}(z^{\ast})]^{\circ}

Based on
[

]
, we can give the following characterization of weak sharpness in terms of an error bound involving the dual gap function of

. A proof can be found in

]
.

.

Consider problem

with

a closed, convex, and nonempty subset of

This Proposition shows that weak sharpness implies that the gap function satisfies an error bound property

]
. Motivated by this fact, we propose the following definition:

(Weak Sharpness)

Let

be the nonempty solution set of

.

An important class of examples arises when

,

]
. In the case where the

in the context of hierarchical minimization. Specifically, let us assume that

), and telescoping this expression, we immediately arrive at the estimate (
2.15
).

C.3
Full proof of Theorem
2.6

The lengthy and rather technical proof of Theorem
2.6
is organised in various steps. First, we establish the upper bound on the feasibility gap
\Theta_{\rm Feas}
. In the next step use insights obtained from that part in order to prove upper bounds on the optimality gap
\Theta_{\rm Opt}
. Since the latter gap function is a-priori signless, we need to establish an informative lower bound as well. This is the last step in the proof in order to deduce (
2.17
). Exploiting then the geometric setting provided by weak sharpness of the lower level solution set, allows us to prove two-sided bounds on the optimality gap, which in the end allow us to sandwich this optimality measure.

C.3.1
Establishing the rates of the feasibility gap

To obtain rates in terms of these restricted merit functions, we depart from (
C.2
):

\displaystyle\norm{x_{k+1}-x}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\alpha\norm{x_{k}-x}^{2}+(1-\alpha)\norm{w_{k}-x}^{2}-\alpha\norm{y_{k+1}-x_k}^{2}-(1-\alpha)\norm{y_{k+1}-w_{k}}^{2}

\displaystyle+2\tau_{k}\langle\mathsf{A}_{k+1}-{\mathsf{V}}_{k}(y_{k+1}),x-y_{k+1}\rangle+2\tau_{k}\langle{\mathsf{V}}^{\xi_{k}}_{k}(w_{k})-{\mathsf{V}}^{\xi_{k}}_{k}(y_{k+1}),x_{k+1}-y_{k+1}\rangle.

Calling

\displaystyle-\norm{x_{k+1}-y_{k+1}}^{2}-2\tau_{k}\Psi_{k}(x)+2\tau_{k}(M_{1}^{k}(x)+M_{2}^{k}).

a.s.

With the specific choice

\displaystyle-(1-\theta)\norm{y_{k+1}-x_k}^{2}+2\tau_{k}(M_{1}^{k}(x)+M_{2}^{k})-\norm{x_{k+1}-y_{k+1}}^{2}-\theta\norm{y_{k+1}-w_{k}}^{2}

Calling

(C.7)

\displaystyle R^{k}_{1}(x)

\displaystyle\triangleq\norm{w_{k+1}-x}^{2}-(1-\theta)\norm{w_{k}-x}^{2}-\theta\norm{x_{k+1}-x}^{2}

\displaystyle R_{2}^{k}

\displaystyle\triangleq\norm{x_{k+1}-y_{k+1}}^{2}+\theta\norm{y_{k+1}-w_{k}}^{2},

the above simplifies to

\mathbb{E}[R_{1}^{k}(x)|\mathcal{F}_{k}]=0
, and the tower property gives

We define the variation of a function

over bounded sets

If Assumption
5
holds, thanks to Lemma
2.5
, there exists a deterministic constant

such that
x_{k},y_{k}\in\mathbb{B}(x_{0},C_{r})
for all

,
\mathbb{P}
-a.s. If

\dom(g_{1})\cap\dom(g_{2})
by construction. Hence, there exists a measurable set

\displaystyle\langle{\mathsf{F}}_{1}(y_{k+1}(\omega)),x^{*}-y_{k+1}(\omega)\rangle

\displaystyle\leq\norm{\opF_{1}(y_{k+1}(\omega))}\cdot\left(\norm{x^{*}}+\norm{x_{0}}+\norm{x_{0}-y_{k+1}(\omega)}\right)+g_{1}(x^{*})-g_{1}(y_{k+1}(\omega))

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{x\in\mathcal{U}_{2}}D(x,x_{0}^{0})+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{s=0}^{S-1}\sum_{k=0}^{K-1}\mathbb{E}[\norm{Z_{k+1}^{s}}^{2}_{*}]\leq\sup_{x\in\mathcal{U}_{2}}D(x,x_{0}^{0})+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{s=0}^{S-1}\sum_{k=0}^{K-1}\tau_{s}^{2}L_{s}^{2}\norm{y^{s}_{k+1}-w^{s}}^{2},

where we have used in the second equality that
y_{k+1}^{s}
is
\mathcal{H}_{s,k}
-measurable and
\mathbb{E}[Z_{k+1}^{s}|\mathcal{H}_{s,k}]=0
. For the last inequality we have used the fact that
\mathbb{E}[\norm{X-\Ex(X)}^{2}]\leq\mathbb{E}[\norm{X}^{2}]

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\norm{Z_{k+1}^s}^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=\tau_{s}^{2}\mathbb{E}\left[\norm{(\opV_s^{\xi_k^s}(y_{k+1}^s)-\opV^{\xi_k^s}_s(w^s)) - (\opV_s(y_{k+1}^s)+ \opV_s(w^s))}^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\tau_{s}^{2}\mathbb{E}\left[\norm{\opV_s^{\xi_k^s}(y_{k+1}^s)-\opV^{\xi_k^s}_s(w^s)}^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\tau_{s}^{2}L_{s}^{2}\mathbb{E}\left[\norm{y_{k+1}^s-w^s}^{2}\right].

We further use the stepsize assumption
\tau_{s}^{2}L_{s}^{2}\leq(1-\alpha)/4
and Lemma
3.1
(iii)
to obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{x\in\mathcal{U}_{2}}\sum_{s=0}^{S-1}\sum_{k=0}^{K-1}\tau_{s}M_{1}(x,s,k)\right]\leq\sup_{x\in\mathcal{U}_{2}}D(x,x_{0}^{0})+\frac{1}{8}\left(4\mathcal{E}^{0}(x^{*})+4K\sum_{s=0}^{S-1}\tau_{s}h(\beta_{s}p^{*})\right)

\displaystyle=\sup_{x\in\mathcal{U}_{2}}D(x,x_{0}^{0})+\frac{1}{2}\left(\mathcal{E}^{0}(x^{*})+K\sum_{s=0}^{S-1}\tau_{s}h(\beta_{s}p^{*})\right),

which gives us the final bound

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\Theta_{\rm Feas}(\hat{x}^{S}|\mathcal{U}_{2})]\leq

\displaystyle\frac{\sup_{x\in\mathcal{U}_{2}}\mathcal{E}^{0}(x)+\sup_{x\in\mathcal{U}_{2}}D(x,x_{0}^{0})+\mathcal{E}^{0}(x^{*})/2}{KT_{S}}+\frac{\sum_{s=0}^{S-1}\beta_{s}\tau_{s}C_{\mathcal{U}_{2}}}{T_{S}}

\displaystyle+\frac{\sum_{s=0}^{S-1}\tau_{s}h(\beta_{s}p^{*})}{2T_{S}}.

To estimate the optimality gap, we divide (
D.17
) by
\beta_{s}
and take
x=x_{2}^{*}\in\mathcal{S}_{2}
, which implies that
0\leq-H^{({\mathsf{F}}_{2},g_{2})}(x_{2}^{*},y^{s}_{k+1})
for any
k,s
. By monotonicity of
{\mathsf{F}}_{1}

-H^{({\mathsf{F}}_{1},g_{1})}(x,y^{s}_{k+1})\geq H^{({\mathsf{F}}_{1},g_{1})}(y^{s}_{k+1},x)

(D.24)

\tau_{s}\sum_{k=1}^{K-1}H^{({\mathsf{F}}_{1},g_{1})}(y^{s}_{k+1},x_{2}^{*})\leq\frac{1}{\beta_{s}}\mathcal{E}^{s}(x_{2}^{*})-\frac{1}{\beta_{s}}\mathcal{E}^{s+1}(x_{2}^{*})+\frac{\tau_{s}}{\beta_{s}}\sum_{k=0}^{K-1}M_{1}(x,s,k)+\frac{1}{\beta_{s}}\sum_{k=0}^{K-1}M_{2}(s,k).

For any fixed
x

\mathcal{E}^{s}(x)
is a.s. bounded by some constant
\tilde{C}_{x}
. Using this, summing these inequalities, and using the convexity of

(D.25)

H^{({\mathsf{F}}_{1},g_{1})}(\hat{x}^{s},x_{2}^{*})\leq\frac{\tilde{C}_{x_{2}^{*}}}{\beta_{S}KT_{S}}+\frac{1}{KT_{S}}\left(\sum_{s=0}^{S-1}\frac{\tau_{s}}{\beta_{s}}\sum_{k=0}^{K-1}M_{1}(x,s,k)+\sum_{s=0}^{S-1}\frac{1}{\beta_{s}}\sum_{k=0}^{K-1}M_{2}(s,k)\right).

Since for any compact

\mathbb{E}[\Theta_{\rm Opt}(\hat{x}^{S}|\mathcal{U}_{1}\cap\mathcal{S}_{2})]\leq\frac{C_{\mathcal{U}_{1}}}{\beta_{S}KT_{S}}+\frac{1}{KT_{S}}\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{x\in\mathcal{U}_{1}\cap\mathcal{S}_{2}}\sum_{s=0}^{S-1}\frac{\tau_{s}}{\beta_{s}}\left(\sum_{k=0}^{K-1}M_{1}(x,s,k)\right)\right].

The last term can be estimated in the same way as above, by using Lemma
A.3

Z_{k+1}^{s}=\frac{\tau_{s}}{\beta_{s}}(\mathsf{A}^{s}_{k+1}-{\mathsf{V}}_{\kappa}(y^{s}_{k+1}))

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{x\in\mathcal{U}_{1}\cap\mathcal{S}_{2}}\sum_{s=0}^{S-1}\sum_{k=0}^{K-1}\frac{\tau_{s}}{\beta_{s}}M_{1}(x,s,k)\right]\leq\sup_{x\in\mathcal{U}_{1}\cap\mathcal{S}_{2}}D(x,x_{0}^{0})+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{s=0}^{S-1}\sum_{k=0}^{K-1}\frac{\tau_{s}^{2}L_{s}^{2}}{\beta_{s}^{2}}\norm{y^{s}_{k+1}-w^{s}}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{x\in\mathcal{U}_{1}\cap\mathcal{S}_{2}}D(x,x_{0}^{0})+\frac{1}{2\beta_{S}^{2}}\left(\mathcal{E}^{0}(x^{*})+K\sum_{s=0}^{S-1}\tau_{s}h(\beta_{s}p^{*})\right),

where we used that
\beta_{s}
is decreasing, the stepsize assumption
\tau_{s}^{2}L_{s}^{2}\leq(1-\alpha)/4
and Lemma
3.1
(iii)
. This gives us finally

(D.27)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\Theta_{\rm Opt}(\hat{x}^{S}|\mathcal{U}_{1}\cap\mathcal{S}_{2})]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{C_{\mathcal{U}_{1}}}{\beta_{S}KT_{S}}+\frac{\sup_{x\in\mathcal{U}_{1}\cap\mathcal{S}_{2}}D(x,x_{0}^{0})}{KT_{S}}

\displaystyle+\frac{\mathcal{E}^{0}(x^{*})}{2K\beta_{S}^{2}T_{S}}+\frac{\sum_{s=0}^{S-1}\tau_{s}h(\beta_{s}p^{*})}{2\beta_{S}^{2}T_{S}}.

Let us choose
\tau_{s}=\bar{\tau}\leq\frac{\sqrt{1-\alpha}}{2L_{1}}
and
\beta_{s}=\frac{1}{(K(s+1))^{\delta}}
with
\delta\in(0,1/2)
. Then, we have (cf. the derivation after (
2.19
))
h(\beta_{s}p^{*})\leq\frac{\rho-1}{\rho}\alpha^{-\frac{1}{\rho-1}}\beta_{s}^{\frac{\rho}{\rho-1}}\norm{p^{*}}^{\frac{\rho}{\rho-1}}=C_{\rho}K^{-\delta\rho^{*}}(s+1)^{-\delta\rho^{*}}\leq C_{\rho}K^{-1}(s+1)^{-\delta\rho^{*}}

\delta\rho^{*}>1

\sum_{s=0}^{S-1}h(\beta_{s}p^{*})\leq\tilde{h}_{\rho}/K
, where
\tilde{h}_{\rho}

\bar{h}_{\rho}
defined in (
2.20

\sum_{s=0}^{S-1}\beta_{s}=\sum_{s=0}^{S-1}(Ks)^{-\delta}\leq\frac{K^{-\delta}}{1-\delta}\left(S^{1-\delta}+\delta\right)
.

Denoting
R^{2}(x^{*},x_{0}^{0})\triangleq\sup_{x\in\mathcal{U}_{2}}\mathcal{E}^{0}(x)+\sup_{x\in\mathcal{U}_{2}}D(x,x_{0}^{0})+\mathcal{E}^{0}(x^{*})
, we obtain from (
3.3
) the following bound on the feasibility gap

(D.28)

\displaystyle

\displaystyle{\mathbb\displaystyle E}[\Theta_{\rm Feas}(\hat{x}^{S}|\mathcal{U}_{2})]\leq\frac{R^{2}}{KT_{S}}+\frac{\sum_{s=0}^{S-1}\beta_{s}\tau_{s}C_{\mathcal{U}_{2}}}{T_{S}}+\frac{\sum_{s=0}^{S-1}\tau_{s}h(\beta_{s}p^{*})}{2T_{S}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{R^{2}}{\bar{\tau}KS}+\frac{\frac{K^{-\delta}}{1-\delta}\left(S^{1-\delta}+\delta\right)}{S}C_{\mathcal{U}_{2}}+\frac{\tilde{h}_{\rho}/K}{2S}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{R^{2}}{\bar{\tau}KS}+\frac{4C_{\mathcal{U}_{2}}}{(KS)^{\delta}}+\frac{\tilde{h}_{\rho}}{2KS}.

For the refined version of the optimality gap, we use (
D.27
)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\Theta_{\rm Opt}(\hat{x}^{S}|\mathcal{U}_{1}\cap\mathcal{S}_{2})]\leq\frac{C_{\mathcal{U}_{1}}}{\beta_{S}KT_{S}}+\frac{\sup_{x\in\mathcal{U}_{1}\cap\mathcal{S}_{2}}D(x,x_{0}^{0})}{KT_{S}}+\frac{\mathcal{E}^{0}(x^{*})}{2K\beta_{S}^{2}T_{S}}+\frac{\sum_{s=0}^{S-1}\tau_{s}h(\beta_{s}p^{*})}{2\beta_{S}^{2}T_{S}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{C_{\mathcal{U}_{1}}}{\bar{\tau}KS\cdot(KS)^{-\delta}}+\frac{\sup_{x\in\mathcal{U}_{1}\cap\mathcal{S}_{2}}D(x,x_{0}^{0})}{\bar{\tau}KS}+\frac{\mathcal{E}^{0}(x^{*})}{2\bar{\tau}KS\cdot(KS)^{-2\delta}}+\frac{\tilde{h}_{\rho}/K}{2S\cdot(KS)^{-2\delta}}

\displaystyle=\frac{C_{\mathcal{U}_{1}}}{\bar{\tau}(KS)^{1-\delta}}+\frac{\sup_{x\in\mathcal{U}_{1}\cap\mathcal{S}_{2}}D(x,x_{0}^{0})}{\bar{\tau}KS}+\frac{\mathcal{E}^{0}(x^{*})}{2\bar{\tau}(KS)^{1-2\delta}}+\frac{\tilde{h}_{\rho}}{2(KS)^{1-2\delta}}.

Appendix E
Detailed Numerical Experiments

In this section, we will explore two numerical examples to illustrate our theoretical results.
In both examples, we use the a matrix game, with matrix
M\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}

\max_{y\in\Delta_{m}}\min_{x\in\Delta_{n}}x^{\top}My,

where for any
l\in\mathbb{Z}
,
\Delta_{l}
is the unit simplex in dimension
l
.
For some, predefined
\nu\in\mathbb{Z}
we take

(E.1)

\displaystyle M=I_{\nu}\otimes U,\quad U=\begin{pmatrix}1&-1\\
-1&1\end{pmatrix},

where
\otimes
denotes the Kronecker product, and
U
is the matrix associated with classical ’matching pennies’ game.
The set of optimal strategies is
x^{\ast}=\frac{1}{2}\mu\otimes(1,1)^{T}
, and
y^{\ast}=\frac{1}{2}\eta\otimes(1,1)^{T}

where
(\mu_{s})_{s\in[\nu]},(\eta_{s})_{s\in[\nu]}\in\Delta_{\nu}
.
For
z=(x,y)
the optimal solution of this matrix game is given by a solution to the variational inequality with data

\tilde{g}(z)=\indicator_{\Delta_{n}}(x)+\indicator_{\Delta_{m}}(y)
and

\tilde{F}(z)=\begin{bmatrix}My\\
-M^{T}x\end{bmatrix}=\sum_{j\in[m]}\begin{bmatrix}M^{j}y_{j}\\
\boldsymbol{0}_{n}\\
\end{bmatrix}+\sum_{i\in[n]}\begin{bmatrix}\boldsymbol{0}_{n}\\
M_{i}x_{i}\end{bmatrix}.

M^{j}
is the
j
th column of
M
, and
M_{i}
is the
i
th row of
M
as a column vector.

E.1
Equilibrium Selection

The equilibrium selection problem finds an equilibrium of a game, among all possible equilibrium points, which minimizes a certain objective
f(z)
. Considering the objective function
f(z)=\frac{1}{2}\norm{z}^{2}
, the equilibrium selection problem is given by

(E.2)

\displaystyle\min_{z\in\mathbb{R}^{n+m}}

\displaystyle\norm{z}^{2}

s.t.

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}x\\
y\end{pmatrix}\in\begin{pmatrix}\argmin_{\tilde{x}\in\Delta_{n}}\tilde{x}^{\top}My\\
\argmax_{\tilde{y}\in\Delta_{m}}x^{\top}M\tilde{y}\end{pmatrix}.

This problem can be cast as a hierarchical VI of form (
P
) with data
g_{1}(z)=0,\;g_{2}(z)=\indicator_{\Delta_{n}}(x)+\indicator_{\Delta_{m}}(y)
, and

\displaystyle F_{1}(z)=\sum_{j\in[m]}\begin{bmatrix}\boldsymbol{0}_{n}\\
e_{j}^{m}y_{j}\end{bmatrix}+\sum_{i\in[n]}\begin{bmatrix}e_{i}^{n}x_{i}\\
\boldsymbol{0}_{m}\end{bmatrix},\quad F_{2}=\tilde{F},

where
e^{n}_{i}
is the
i
th vector in the standard basis of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
Therefore, at each iteration
k
of the algorithms
G_{k}(z)=g_{2}(z)=\indicator_{\Delta^{n}}(x)+\indicator_{\Delta^{m}}(y)
.

Since both
{\mathsf{F}}_{1}
and
{\mathsf{F}}_{2}
can be written as a finite sum of
n+m
components, we set the random variable
\xi
to be the set of components chosen at each iteration.
Specifically,
\xi=(i,j)
with probability
Q((i,j))=c_{i}r_{j}
, where
(c_{i})_{i\in[n]}\in\Delta_{n}
and
(r_{j})_{j\in[m]}\in\Delta_{m}
, and we sample the random operators
via so called "importance-sampling’ as in
[
1
]
:

\displaystyle{\mathsf{F}}_{1}^{\xi}(z)=\begin{pmatrix}\frac{1}{c_{i}}e_{i}^{n}x_{i}\\
\frac{1}{r_{j}}e_{j}^{m}y_{j}\end{pmatrix},\ {\mathsf{F}}_{2}^{\xi}(z)=\begin{pmatrix}\frac{1}{r_{j}}M^{j}y_{j}\\
-\frac{1}{c_{i}}M_{i}x_{i}.\end{pmatrix},

where
D_{i}
and
D^{j}
denote the
i
-th row respective
j
-th column of a matrix
D
. One evaluation of
V_{k}(z)=F_{2}(z)+\beta_{k}F_{1}(z)
has therefore complexity
\mathcal{O}(2nm)
and of
V_{k}^{\xi}(z)=F_{2}^{\xi}(z)+\beta_{k}F_{1}^{\xi}(z)
is
\mathcal{O}(n+m)
.

In the following, we present the operators and definition of
Q
for each of the algorithms, as well as the setup and results of the computational study for this example.

E.1.1
Setting for Algorithm
1

In each iteration of Algorithm
1
, we are required to compute

(E.3)

\displaystyle\prox_{G_{k}}(z)=\left(\Pi_{\Delta_{n}}(x),\Pi_{\Delta_{m}}(y)\right),

where
\Pi_{\mathcal{C}}
denotes the euclidean orthogonal projection onto set
\mathcal{C}

\prox_{G_{k}}
in
\tilde{\mathcal{O}}(n+m)
operations and therefore ignore it in computing the overall complexity of the Algorithm. The total computational cost of one iteration of Algorithm
1
is therefore on average
\mathcal{O}(2\theta nm+n+m)
.

We define the sampling probabilities to be

\displaystyle r_{j}=\frac{\norm{M^j}^{2}}{\norm{M}_{F}^{2}},\quad c_{i}=\frac{\norm{M_i^2}}{\norm{M}_{F}^{2}}.

We can obtain a Lipschitz constant
\mathcal{L}_{k}
satisfying Assumption
4
via

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\xi\sim Q}\left[\norm{V_k^\xi(z)}^{2}_{2}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\norm{ \beta_k \begin{pmatrix}
\frac{1}{c_i} e_i^n x_i\\
\frac{1}{r_j} e_j^m y_j
\end{pmatrix}+\begin{pmatrix}
\frac{1}{r_j} M^jy_j\\
-\frac{1}{c_i} M_ix_i
\end{pmatrix} }_{2}^{2}\leq 2\beta_{k}^{2}\mathbb{E}\norm{ \begin{pmatrix}
\frac{1}{c_i} e_i^n x_i\\
\frac{1}{r_j} e_j^m y_j
\end{pmatrix} }_{2}^{2}+2\mathbb{E}\norm{ \begin{pmatrix}
\frac{1}{r_j} M^jy_j\\
-\frac{1}{c_i} M_ix_i
\end{pmatrix} }_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle=2\beta_{k}^{2}\left(\underset{i\sim c}{\mathbb{E}}\norm{\frac{1}{c_i}e^n_ix_i}_{2}^{2}+\underset{j\sim r}{\mathbb{E}}\norm{\frac{1}{r_j}e^m_jy_j}_{2}^{2}\right)+2\norm{M}_{F}^{2}\norm{z}_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle=2\beta_{k}^{2}\left(\sum_{i\in[n]}c_{i}\norm{\frac{1}{c_i}e^n_ix_i}_{2}^{2}+\sum_{j\in[m]}\norm{\frac{1}{r_j}e^m_jy_j}_{2}^{2}\right)+2\norm{M}_{F}^{2}\norm{z}_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle=2\beta_{k}^{2}\left(\sum_{i\in[n]}\frac{1}{c_{i}}(x_{i})^{2}+\sum_{j\in[m]}\frac{1}{r_{j}}(y_{j})^{2}\right)+\norm{M}_{F}^{2}\norm{z}_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq 2\left(\beta_{k}^{2}\max_{i,j}\left\{\frac{1}{c_{i}},\frac{1}{r_{j}}\right\}+\norm{M}_{F}^{2}\right)\norm{z}_{2}^{2}

where we have bounded
\mathbb{E}\norm{F_2^\xi(z)}_{2}^{2}\leq\norm{M}_{F}^{2}\norm{z}_{2}^{2}
as in
[
1
]
. Thus, we can conclude,
by the linearity of
V_{k}^{\xi}
and
Jensen’s inequality and

(E.4)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\norm{V_k^{\xi}(u)-V_k^{\xi}(v)}\right]\leq

\displaystyle\sqrt{\mathbb{E}\left[\norm{V_k^{\xi}(u)-V_k^{\xi}(v)}^{2}_{2}\right]}=\sqrt{\mathbb{E}\left[\norm{V_k^{\xi}(u-v)}^{2}_{2}\right]}\leq\mathcal{L}_{k}\norm{u-v}

with

\mathcal{L}_{k}=\sqrt{2(\beta_{k}^{2}{\max_{i,j}\left\{\frac{1}{c_{i}},\frac{1}{r_{j}}\right\}}^{2}+\norm{M}_{F}^{2})}.

E.1.2
Setting for Algorithm
2
.

For this setting we look at two possible geometries.

\ell_{1}
-norm.

For
z=(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{n+m}

(E.5)

\norm{z}=\sqrt{\norm{x}_{1}^{2}+\norm{y}_{1}^{2}},

with the corresponding dual norm
\norm{z^\ast}_{\ast}=\sqrt{\norm{x}_{\infty}^{2}+\norm{y}_{\infty}^{2}}
. In this geometry, we use the distance generating function as the sum of negative entropy functions over
x

\mathsf{d}(z)=\sum_{i=1}^{n+m}z_{i}\log(z_i)=\sum_{i\in[n]}x_{i}\log(x_i)+\sum_{j\in[m]}y_{j}\log(y_j),

Thus, at each outer iteration

(E.6)

\displaystyle T(u,v,w)

\displaystyle=\argmin\{\langle u,z\rangle+G_{s}(z)+\alpha D(z,v)+(1-\alpha)D(z,w)\}

\displaystyle=\argmin_{z\in\Delta_{n}\times\Delta_{m}}\langle u,z\rangle+\alpha\sum_{i=1}^{n+m}z_{i}\log\frac{z_{i}}{v_{i}}+(1-\alpha)\sum_{i=1}^{n+m}z_{i}\log\frac{z_{i}}{w_{i}}.

Thus, due to the separability of the operator in each index, for

where

(E.7)

c_{i}=\frac{\left|u_{i}^{x}-v^{x}_{i}\right|}{\norm{u^x - v^x}_{1}},\quad r_{j}=\frac{\left|u_{j}^{y}-v_{j}^{y}\right|}{\norm{u^y-v^y}_{1}},

and

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\xi\sim Q_{u,v}}\left[\norm{\opV_s^{\xi}(u) - \opV_s^{\xi}(v)}^{2}_{\ast}\right]

\displaystyle\leq 2\beta_{s}^{2}\mathbb{E}_{\xi\sim Q{u,v}}\left[\norm{\opF_1^{\xi}(u) - \opF_1^{\xi}(v)}^{2}_{\ast}\right]+2\mathbb{E}_{\xi\sim Q_{u,v}}\left[\norm{F_2^{\xi}(u) - F_2^{\xi}(v)}_{\ast}^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=2\beta_{s}^{2}\left(\sum_{i\in[n]}\norm{u^x-v^x}_{1}\absolutevalue{u^x_i - v^x_i}+\sum_{j}^{m}\norm{u^y-v^y}_{1}\absolutevalue{u^y_i - v^y_i}\right)+2\norm{M}_{\max}^{2}\norm{u-v}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq{2}\beta_{s}^{2}\norm{u-v}^{2}+2\norm{M}_{\max}^{2}\norm{u-v}^{2}

\displaystyle=({2}\beta_{s}^{2}+2\norm{M}_{\max}^{2})\norm{u-v}^{2},

so
L_{s}=\sqrt{2(\beta_{s}^{2}+\norm{M}^{2}_{\max})}.

\ell_{2}
-norm.

We also look at the standard Euclidean geometry, i.e., with

In this case,

(E.8)

Thus, under this geometry we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\xi\sim Q_{u,v}}\left[\norm{\opV_s^{\xi}(u) - \opV_s^{\xi}(v)}^{2}_{2}\right]

\displaystyle\leq 2\beta_{s}^{2}\mathbb{E}_{\xi\sim Q{u,v}}\left[\norm{\opF_1^{\xi}(u) - \opF_1^{\xi}(v)}^{2}_{2}\right]+2\mathbb{E}_{\xi\sim Q_{u,v}}\left[\norm{\opF_2^{\xi}(u) - \opF_2^{\xi}(v)}_{2}^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\leq 2\beta_{s}^{2}\left(\underset{i\sim c}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\norm{\frac{1}{c_i}e^n_i(u^x_i - v^x_i)}_{2}^{2}\right]+\underset{j\sim r}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\norm{\frac{1}{r_j}e^m_j(u^y_j-v^y_j)}_{2}^{2}\right]\right)

\displaystyle+2\underset{i\sim c}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\norm{\frac{1}{c_i}M_i(u^x_i - v^x_i)}_{2}^{2}\right]+\underset{j\sim r}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\norm{\frac{1}{r_j}M^j(u^y_j-v^y_j)}_{2}^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=2\beta_{s}^{2}\left(\sum_{i\in[n]}\frac{1}{c_{i}}(u^{x}_{i}-v^{x}_{i})^{2}+\sum_{j}^{m}\frac{1}{r_{j}}(u^{y}_{j}-v^{y}_{j})^{2}\right)

where the last inequality is due to the definition of

Similarly to the previous setting, we have

satisfies Assumption

E.2
Linearly Constrained Equilibrium

This problem addresses finding an equilibrium, which additionally satisfies a set of linear constraints, that is, we are interesting in finding a vector
z=(x,y)
such that

(E.11)

\begin{pmatrix}x\\
y\end{pmatrix}\in\begin{pmatrix}\argmin_{\tilde{x}\in\Delta_{n}}\tilde{x}^{\top}My\\
\argmax_{\tilde{y}\in\Delta_{m}}x^{\top}M\tilde{y}\end{pmatrix},\quad Bx+Cy=d,

for some given matrices
B\in\mathbb{R}^{\nu\times n},\ C\in\mathbb{R}^{\nu\times m}
and vector
d\in\mathbb{R}^{\nu}
and matrix
M
as defined in
E.1
.
The problem’s linear constraints can therefore be cast as the following optimization problem

\min_{x\in\Delta_{n},y\in\Delta_{m}}\frac{1}{2}\norm{Bx+Cy-d}^{2}.

Thus, using standard optimality conditions, problem (
E.11
) can be formulated as hierarchical VI of form (
P
), with
g_{1}=0
,
g_{2}=\tilde{g}

F_{1}=\tilde{F},\quad F_{2}(z)=P(Bx+Cy-d)=P\left(\sum_{i\in[n]}B^{i}x_{i}+\sum_{j\in[m]}C^{j}y_{j}-d\right),\quad P=\begin{pmatrix}B^{\top}\\
C^{\top}\end{pmatrix}.

This leads to
G_{k}
and
\prox_{G_{K}}
being as in the previous example.

Using the definition of random variable
\xi
as in the last example, taking values as pairs
(i,j)
, and distributed according to
Q
, where
Q((i,j))=c_{i}r_{j}
where
c\in\Delta_{n}
and
r\in\Delta_{m}
. Specifically, we define the random operators:

\displaystyle{\mathsf{F}}_{1}^{\xi}(z)=\begin{pmatrix}\frac{1}{r_{j}}M^{j}y_{j}\\
-\frac{1}{c_{i}}M_{i}x_{i}\end{pmatrix},\;{\mathsf{F}}_{2}^{\xi}(z)=P(\frac{1}{c_{i}}B^{i}x_{i}+\frac{1}{r_{j}}C^{j}y_{j}-d)

Thus, while computing
