Efficacy of perineal massage in second stage of labour for prevention of perineal trauma: a randomized controlled trial
Authors: Kritika Gupta, Shikha Chadha, Sandhya Jain, Hima C. Kille, Baba Saheb Ambedkar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundHigh-quality intra-partum care is crucial for all expectant mothers to ensure safe delivery and favourable postpartum recovery. Vaginal delivery when associated with perineal trauma makes postpartum period painful and increases risk of long-term morbidity. Perineal trauma has been reported to be more common among Asian women. Interventions which minimise the perineal trauma will enhance the experience of motherhood. Various strategies have been explored but there is no firm consensus on their usage. We conducted this study to evaluate the effectiveness of perineal massage during the second stage of labor in reducing perineal trauma.MethodsA randomised controlled study was conducted at a referral centre. Four hundred and fifty women were randomised into two groups of 225 women. Second stage of labor was managed with perineal massage along with fetal head flexion and perineal support in group A and only fetal head flexion and perineal support was given in group B. The rate of intact perineum, episiotomy, perineum tears, duration of second stage and acceptability of perineal massage were compared between the 2 groups.ResultsGroup A showed a significantly higher rate of intact perineum (p = 0.03) and a lower rate of episiotomies (p = 0.04). Overall rates of perineal tears between groups were not statistically different (p = 0.95). The duration of the second stage of labor was significantly shorter in Group A (p < 0.001). Women experienced significant perineal discomfort but 81.8% said they would opt for it in the subsequent delivery.ConclusionPerineal massage is a simple, easily applicable and cost-effective technique. It reduced episiotomy rates and duration of second stage of labor significantly. Its routine use can be offered with appropriate counselling to all labouring women.

Background

Perineal injury is common at the time of vaginal birth, and its occurrence is influenced by several maternal and intrapartum factors. Factors associated with perineal trauma include nulliparity, certain ethnic backgrounds, macrosomia, malposition, prolonged or assisted second stage, shoulder dystocia, induction of labour, epidural analgesia and the use of episiotomy. Since, most of these factors are not modifiable, establishing practical strategies to minimise perineal tears is of great concern.

Perineal trauma in form of episiotomy as well as spontaneous tears can make routine daily activities uncomfortable impeding early postpartum recovery, breastfeeding, and mother–infant bonding due to pain. They also increase the risk of postpartum haemorrhage, wound complications, dyspareunia and persistent perineal pain. Literature reviews indicate that postpartum anal and urinary incontinence represent a significant postpartum problem across populations [1, 2]. These consequences may persist well beyond the puerperium affecting long-term quality of life. This makes prevention of perineal trauma particularly important.

The second stage of labour is a crucial period when simple intrapartum measures by the care provider may influence perineal outcomes. Numerous practices have been described, including perineal support, controlled delivery of the head, hands-off techniques, warm compresses, perineal massage, lumbosacral massage, varied pushing styles, hydrotherapy and selective episiotomy.

The traditional hands-on method has long been used with the aim of reducing trauma, with several studies reporting potential benefit [3–6]. More recently, the hands-off method has gained attention as it may reduce the elective use of episiotomy, although its effect on other types of perineal injury remains uncertain [2].

Episiotomy is being used globally with the intention of preventing severe perineal trauma despite current research indicating that it offers no protection against perineal tears and does not improve long term outcomes. It is associated with increased pain, blood loss, healing issues and longer recovery [7, 8]. Evidence-based practice today is to use restrictive episiotomy in order to reduce the risk of obstetric anal sphincter injuries in specific clinical scenarios like instrumental deliveries, fetal distress, shoulder dystocia, macrosomia and difficult vaginal deliveries.

Antenatal perineal massage starting from approximately 34 weeks period of gestation has been found to increase perineal tissue stretchability thus reducing severe degree of tears and need for episiotomy when used regularly in nulliparous women. However, this is not evident in parous women and also it does not decrease minor degree of tears. It is beneficial in reducing postpartum pain significantly at 3 months [9–12]. The problems with its performance are acceptability, training and compliance.

Intrapartum perineal massage during the second stage of labour enhances tissue flexibility and also improves blood flow which reduces severe perineal trauma and shortens second stage duration in nulliparous women. WHO Labour Care Guide includes intrapartum perineal massage as supportive measures to help reduce perineal injury. Cochrane review inferred that perineal massage in the second stage may reduce the risk of third- and fourth-degree tears, but not that of first- or second-degree tears or episiotomy. Though, the evidence was of low quality for intactness of perineum and lower degrees of tears [2].

Perineal massage, whether antenatal or intrapartum contribute to improved postpartum pelvic-floor outcomes, including reduced perineal pain and lower risk of fecal or flatus incontinence at 3 months postpartum [13].

Perineal trauma is more pronounced in nulliparous women whose pelvic tissues are generally firmer and less stretchable. Asian women have a greater likelihood of spontaneous tears and also episiotomy rates are higher reaching up to 60% in nulliparous women. The present study was designed to evaluate whether perineal massage during the second stage of labour can reduce perineal trauma among nulliparous women and to evaluate its acceptability.

Method

Aim and objectives

The aim of our study was to determine the efficacy of perineal massage in second stage of labour in prevention of perineal trauma as an added intervention to fetal head flexion and perineal support.

The primary objective was to determine the efficacy of perineal massage in second stage of labour in reducing perineal trauma. Outcome measured were Rate of intact perineum, Rate of perineal tear and Rate of episiotomy. The secondary objectives were (a) To study the effect of perineal massage on duration of second stage of labour, (b) To study the patient perception of pain due to perineal massage and acceptability of the procedure and (c) To estimate the impact of clinical risk factors on perineal trauma.

Design and setting

The randomised controlled study was carried out in a tertiary care hospital over a period of eighteen months. The hospital scientific and ethical committee approval was granted before the study vide no. F. No.5(18)/2022/BSAH/DNB/Scientific Committee and vide no. F. No.5(32)/2020/BSAH/DNB/PF 22554-55 respectively. We use ICMR Ethical Guidelines for Biomedical Research on Human Participants. Clinical trial registration is not applicable.

Sample size

Sample size calculation was based on the study by Rodrigues et al. [14] The minimum required sample size with 80% power of study and 5% level of significance was calculated as 225 in each group.

Randomisation

Block randomisation was done in blocks of 10. A box with 5 chits each of group A and group B was made. Every patient entering the study picked one slip and thus became part of that group. In group A women were managed with perineal massage along with fetal head flexion and perineal support at the time of delivery. In group B women were managed with fetal head flexion and perineal support at the time of delivery.

Eligibility criteria

Nulliparous women with vertex presentation, with gestational age > 37 weeks admitted in labour room, at or before the start of active labour (≥ 6 cm) were included in the study. Exclusion criteria were women with non-vertex presentation, prolonged active stage of labour (> 12 h), instrumental/ operative delivery or with some contraindication to vaginal delivery.

Data collection

Detailed history including name, age, occupation, socioeconomic status, number of antenatal visits, obstetric history, menstrual history, personal history, [smoking tobacco chewing] any surgical and medical history was recorded in each patient. General physical and systematic examination was done.

Gestational was confirmed by Last menstrual period (LMP) and early trimester USG or available USG. Per abdomen examination was done to ascertain period of gestation, lie, presentation and fetal heart rate was auscultated. Cervical assessment was done to assess the bishops score. ANC antenatal investigations were reviewed.

Informed consent

was taken from the women for inclusion in the study after counselling about the modalities for managing second stage of labour. Women were randomized according to block randomization into two groups.

First stage of labour was monitored with WHO partograph. There was no use of epidural anesthesia. Time of full dilatation of cervix was noted.

During the expulsive phase of second stage patient was made to lie in dorsal position of delivery. It was ensured that the urinary bladder was empty. The management of the second stage was done according to the group allocated to the woman.

Second stage management in two groups

All deliveries were conducted by trained residents of the department. It was ensured that the urinary bladder was empty. In women included in group A, perineal massage was done by a doctor using gloved index and middle fingers with lubricant inside vagina for sweeping motion from 3’O clock to 9’O clock position and gently stretching the perineum during the patient pushing in the expulsive phase of second stage of labour. Perineal massage was given intermittent and gently during uterine contraction, to reduce the discomfort to the women. At crowning, fetal head flexion and perineal support during delivery was done (modified Ritgen manoeuvre). In group B fetal head flexion and perineum support was provided. There was restrictive use of episiotomy. The need for episiotomy was assessed by the resident conducting the delivery. Right lateral episiotomy was given if required as per hospital protocol.

Time of delivery of the baby and baby weight was noted. Active management of third stage was done followed by inspection of the perineum and vagina. Presence of intact perineum, genital tract tears and or episiotomy was recorded. If the perineal trauma was observed, its location was noted and degree of perineal tear assessed and recorded as per RCOG guideline [15]. Any extension or tear associated with episiotomy was also documented.

Perineal discomfort due to perineal massage was analyzed on visual analog scale an hour after transfer to postnatal room. Patient was also asked if she would like to opt for the perineal massage technique in her subsequent delivery as an indicator of acceptability. All information was noted on a predesigned proforma.

Statistical analysis

Categorical variables are presented in numbers and percentage. Continuous variables are presented as mean ± SD and median. Quantitative variables were compared using unpaired t-test between the two groups and Paired t-test/ Wilcoxan test within the groups across follow-ups. Qualitative variable was compared using Chi-Square test/Fisher’s exact test. The data was analyzed with Statistical Package for Social Sciences version 23.0.

Discussion

A number of interventions have been evaluated to reduce perineal injury during vaginal birth; however, the evidence does not clearly favour any single preventive approach. Intrapartum perineal massage during the second stage of labor is one of the modalities in which only a short-term clinical intervention requiring minimal logistics can reduce perineal trauma. Current literature is divided over short-term benefits, including reduced episiotomy rates, lower incidences of severe lacerations, and decreased immediate postpartum pain. Its ability to ensure long-term pelvic floor preservation and its comparative effectiveness is also debateable. While some studies found it to significantly reduces episiotomy rates and OASI, others report no significant protective effect against specific laceration degrees. Other limitations include heterogenous intervention protocols and a lack of robust evidence comparing multiple simultaneous interventions, like perineal support, warm compresses etc. While perineal massage alone is beneficial, nevertheless evidence suggests that “care bundles” may provide superior protection [16].

The present study assessed the efficiency of perineal massage in second stage of labour along with perineal muscle support and modified ritgen manoeuvre in preventing trauma. Delivery with an intact perineum helps in early puerperal recovery and maternal bonding but it may not translate into better long-term outcomes as first and second-degree tears heal very well. Women in perineal massage group in the study had a significantly higher rate of intact perineum (15.1%). Several studies have reported better perineal integrity following massage [2, 17–21] A systematic review and meta-analysis by Marco-Rodriguez et al. also found that massage during the second stage significantly increased the likelihood of an intact perineum [22, 23] Some earlier studies had not shown any such benefit [24–26].

The overall rates of perineal tears were comparable between two groups (34.6% vs. 33%) in the study, with no statistically significant difference. Studies varies in their results regarding overall tears. Many trials reported that the total frequency of tears often remains similar between groups, though the severity is typically reduced [19, 22, 24, 27]. Some researchers reported higher frequency of spontaneous tears in the massage group as a direct trade-off for the lower episiotomy rate [19, 28, 29]. Meta-analysis by Aquino, Venugopal and Silva et al. noted fewer tears following massage [17, 30, 31].

First-degree tears occurred more often in massage group (p = 0.035) consistent with findings from several other trials [18–20, 27, 28, 32, 33]. Second-degree tears were slightly less common, but the difference was not significant. Previous authors have reported a possible decrease in second-degree tears [18–20, 23, 27, 30, 32, 34], A recent meta-analysis published that there’s a significant reduction in second-degree lacerations, with a Relative Risk (RR) of 0.56 [35]. Cochrane Review did not find a clear difference in second-degree tears stating it was not consistently seen across all trial subgroups [36].

The need for episiotomy was 10% lesser with the use of perineal massage in the study which was statistically significant. Comparative trials report rates as low as 31% to 42% in massage groups versus 70% to 93% in routine care groups [21, 22]. The lower episiotomy rate may reflect greater perineal pliability and improved assessment by the birth attendant, which led to more selective use of the procedure. The decrease in episiotomy was not as much in our study probably because threshold for episiotomy is much lower at the institute. Few authors observed no difference in episiotomy rates in their study [25, 26]. A recent systematic review and meta-analysis found no significant evidence that perineal massage during the second stage of labor reduces the need for episiotomy or spontaneous lacerations in primiparous women [29].

The most critical outcome is the incidence of third- and fourth-degree tears. There was no reduction in severe perineal trauma in this study. This needs to be interpreted carefully due to small number of OASI in the study group. Literature indicates a protective effect showing a significant reduction in severe perineal trauma with RR 0.52 to 0.56. [17, 22, 30, 31, 37, 38]. Rodriguez et al. found no meaningful effect [22]. A comprehensive Cochrane review noted that evidence for perineal massage alone in reducing severe tears during labour remains uncertain [36].

A consistent finding in literature is the shortened duration of second stage with perineal massage by a median of 10 to 15 min especially in primigravida and with no use of epidural analgesia. This is attributed to decreased muscular resistance and improved tissue distensibility [19–21, 26–28, 30]. The meta-analyses done also support the possibility that massage may shorten the expulsive phase [17, 22].

Pain assessment revealed significantly higher pain scores in the massage group, particularly during the first hour postpartum. Evidence on pain reduction with massage is inconsistent. Some studies found a reduction in pain 24 h after delivery [27, 29]. Dwan et al. suggests that massage of the perineum may result in little to no difference in immediate perineal pain [36]. It has been reported, that prenatal and intrapartum massage significantly reduced the risk of perineal pain at 3 months postpartum (RR = 0.64) [23, 37]. Karacam et al. did not find any long-term difference [39]. Postpartum perineal pain is common among nulliparous women and may interfere with sleep, maternal comfort, breastfeeding, and bonding. Venugopal et al. noted that the reduction in pain was not merely a result of having fewer tears, but also a result of the massage’s effect on the nerve endings in the perineum. By desensitizing the area and improving local circulation, the inflammatory response postpartum is moderated [30].

On analysis of factors associated with tears several maternal and fetal variables, including age, infant birth weight, haemoglobin levels, and episiotomy, showed associations with tear severity, though no single factor emerged as a dominant predictor. This suggests that perineal outcomes are influenced by a combination of maternal, fetal, and intrapartum variables.

These findings are consistent with evidence showing that induction does not independently increase OASI risk and that factors such as fetal sex and BMI have limited predictive value. The strong association between higher birthweight and severe tears aligns with well-established data identifying macrosomia as a major contributor to perineal trauma [40]. Maternal age also emerged as a relevant factor, supported by studies linking increasing age with reduced perineal elasticity [41]. The significant relationship between episiotomy and severe tears mirrors global recommendations advocating restrictive use to avoid extensions [8].

The current study is a prospective randomised control study. We have included only nulliparous women and have taken a comparable sample. The study included feedback from participants on their willingness to opt for perineal massage in future deliveries, providing insight into the acceptability of the intervention. The limitation of our study is that it is a single centre study which may limit the generalisability of the findings to other settings or populations. Blinding of healthcare providers and analyst was not done which could introduce some bias. The study focused on immediate perineal outcomes and did not include long-term follow-up to assess postpartum recovery and maternal satisfaction. Future research should focus on long-term outcomes, multi-centre trials using more of restrictive episiotomy and use of substances which would enhance the acceptability and effectiveness of perineal massage.

Healthcare providers should educate pregnant woman about the risks and benefits of perineal massage and make an informed decision prior to labour begins. There remains a gap to establish standardised guidelines for perineal massage during labour, including the frequency, duration and techniques to be used and long-term maternal outcomes.

Both ACOG [42] and WHO [4] recognise perineal massage as a reasonable, low-cost measure that may help reduce trauma when used appropriately. Wider implementation should be guided by patient comfort and clinician training. Based on our findings, intrapartum perineal massage appears to promote a higher rate of intact perineum and fewer episiotomies and thus may have a role in everyday clinical practice.

Conclusion

The key findings of the study are increased the incidence of an intact perineum and decreased episiotomy rates and shortened duration of second stage. Although the overall frequency of tears did not differ, the fewer episiotomies and higher proportion of intact perineum leading to reduced suturing requirements was a potential advantage. This in turn could facilitate faster return to daily activities and improved maternal-infant bonding. The procedure itself was uncomfortable to the patient, but the patients were not averse to the idea of its repeat application. This technique is practicable, inexpensive, and reproducible, making it a viable option for reducing trauma and suitable for training midwives, nurses and doctors. One should provide perineal massage after an informed decision is made allowing women to participate in this crucial aspect of childbirth.
