Mechanism-informed identification of FDA-approved topoisomerase inhibitors disrupting SARS-CoV-2 nucleocapsid–RNA interactions
Authors: Da-Wei Zhang, Xiao-Shuang Xu, Lei Xu, Liangxu Xie, Yimin Li, Shan Chang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractSevere acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 (SARS-CoV-2) nucleocapsid protein (NP) interaction with viral RNA is vital for viral replication and immune evasion, making it an attractive target for antiviral development. Inspired by adenosine triphosphate (ATP)’s competitive inhibition of NP–RNA binding, we screened 121 FDA-approved ATP-competitive kinase inhibitors, identifying mitoxantrone (IC₅₀ = 1.22 µM) as a potent inhibitor. Considering its known topoisomerase inhibitory activity, we further screened 23 additional topoisomerase inhibitors, uncovering four active compounds—pixantrone (IC₅₀ = 5.67 µM), doxorubicin (IC₅₀ = 20.19 µM), epirubicin (IC₅₀ = 7.23 µM), and suramin (IC₅₀ = 0.44 µM). Biolayer interferometry (BLI) revealed distinct inhibition mechanisms: suramin bound directly to the NP C-terminal domain (CTD) with a KD of 0.26 µM, whereas the other compounds primarily targeted RNA, with pixantrone showing the highest RNA affinity (KD = 0.63 µM). Complementary molecular docking analyses supported these observations, indicating suramin’s preference for NP binding and anthracycline derivatives engaging RNA. Our findings demonstrate the feasibility of a mechanism-informed repurposing strategy and identify FDA-approved topoisomerase inhibitors and suramin as valuable chemical starting points. Although these compounds are not directly suitable as antivirals due to toxicity concerns, they provide promising scaffolds for further optimization aimed at selective disruption of SARS-CoV-2 NP–RNA interactions.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13065-025-01689-7.Keywords: SARS-CoV-2, Nucleocapsid protein, Topoisomerase inhibitors, RNA–protein interaction inhibitors, Drug repurposing, Biolayer interferometry

Introduction

The Coronavirus Disease 2019 (COVID-19) pandemic, caused by the SARS-CoV-2, continues to exert profound health and socioeconomic impacts globally [1, 2]. Although widespread vaccination has significantly reduced severe disease outcomes, emerging viral variants—particularly those harboring spike (S) protein mutations—present ongoing challenges due to enhanced transmissibility and partial immune evasion [3–5]. While the S protein remains central to vaccine design and therapeutic antibody development, clinically approved antiviral agents—such as remdesivir, which targets RNA-dependent RNA polymerase (RdRp), and paxlovid, which targets the viral main protease (Mpro)—primarily act on non-S viral or host components [6–8]. The emergence of resistant variants underscores the need to explore additional, highly conserved viral targets.

The NP is one of the most conserved structural proteins of SARS-CoV-2 and is integral to viral replication, genome packaging, and modulation of host immune responses [9–12]. Structurally, the SARS-CoV-2 NP consists of two well-defined domains—the N-terminal domain (NTD) and the CTD—connected by a central intrinsically disordered region (IDR). The NTD primarily mediates RNA binding, whereas the CTD is involved in both RNA binding and protein dimerization. Meanwhile, the IDR modulates the RNA-binding affinity and promotes higher-order oligomerization [13]. These complementary functions suggest that the NP is a valuable antiviral target [14–18]. Recent studies have supported this concept, showing that small molecules such as (-)-gallocatechin gallate (GCG), suramin, and K31 can effectively interfere with NP–RNA binding and significantly reduce viral replication [19–21].

Previous studies have demonstrated that ATP competitively displaces RNA from NP, suggesting a potential mechanism for small-molecule inhibitors [22, 23]. During the COVID-19 pandemic, the kinase inhibitor imatinib, an ATP mimetic originally developed for cancer therapy, entered clinical trials against SARS-CoV-2, further supporting this mechanistic hypothesis [24]. Inspired by these findings, we reasoned that ATP-competitive kinase inhibitors could similarly disrupt the NP–RNA interaction. We therefore screened 121 FDA-approved ATP-competitive kinase inhibitors and identified mitoxantrone as a potent inhibitor. Given that mitoxantrone is also a known topoisomerase inhibitor, we subsequently evaluated an additional 23 FDA-approved topoisomerase inhibitors, identifying four additional compounds—doxorubicin, pixantrone, epirubicin, and suramin—that efficiently disrupt the NP–RNA interaction.

Our results highlight the power of rational, mechanism-informed drug screening strategies. Rather than relying on large-scale blind screens, a focused selection based on structural or mechanistic hypotheses (such as ATP mimicry or RNA binding) can yield active antiviral compounds efficiently. Among the five identified inhibitors, suramin uniquely targets NP through its CTD, whereas mitoxantrone, pixantrone, doxorubicin, and epirubicin act mainly through direct RNA binding. These mechanistic insights highlight repurposed topoisomerase inhibitors and suramin as promising starting points for the development of agents targeting SARS-CoV-2 NP–RNA interactions.

Materials and methods

Agents and inhibitor library

Anti-His6-Eu cryptate and streptavidin (SA)-conjugated XL665 were purchased from CisBio (Bedford, MA, USA). An FDA-approved drug library was obtained from MedChem Express (Shanghai, China). The protease inhibitor cocktail was acquired from Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc. (Waltham, MA, USA). NTA biosensors were sourced from Sartorius (Shanghai, China), and black 96-well microplates were obtained from Greiner Bio-One (Darmstadt, Germany). White 384-well shallow microplates were purchased from PerkinElmer (Boston, MA, USA). HisCap 6FF prepacked columns were obtained from Smart Lifesciences (Changzhou, China), while a Superdex 200 Increase 10/300 GL column was acquired from Cytiva (Shanghai, China). A 5′-biotinylated RNA oligonucleotide (UCUAAACGAAC) was synthesized by GENEWIZ (Suzhou, China).

Protein expression and purification

The full-length gene encoding the SARS-CoV-2 NP (residues 1–419) was cloned into pET-28a plasmid via EcoRI and XhoI sites (designated as pET28-N FL), provided by the Guangdong Medical Laboratory Animal Center. The N sequence used for cloning and in vitro assays corresponds to Wuhan-Hu-1 (NC_045512.2); the full coding sequence is provided in Supplementary Data. Constructs for the SARS-CoV-2 NP NTD (residues 44–174) and NP CTD (residues 247–364) were amplified by PCR from the pET28-N FL plasmid and inserted into pET-28a via NdeI and XhoI. All proteins were expressed in E. coli BL21 (DE3) cells in lysogeny broth (LB) medium containing kanamycin as previously described [25]. Upon reaching an OD₆₀₀ of 0.8, expression was induced with 0.5 mM IPTG for 16 h at 18 °C. The cells were then harvested by centrifugation. For purification, cells were resuspended and sonicated in buffer A (25 mM HEPES, pH 7.5, 1 M NaCl, 5 mM MgCl2, 10% glycerol, 5 mM β-mercaptoethanol, 5 mM imidazole, with a 1:100 dilution of protease inhibitor cocktail). The lysate was clarified by centrifugation at 45,000 × g for 30 min at 4 °C. The supernatant was loaded onto a HisCap 6FF metal affinity chromatography column, washed with buffer B (25 mM HEPES, pH 7.5, 300 mM NaCl, 5 mM MgCl2, 10% glycerol, 5 mM β-mercaptoethanol, 20 mM imidazole), and eluted with buffer C (25 mM HEPES, pH 7.5, 300 mM NaCl, 5 mM MgCl2, 10% glycerol, 5 mM β-mercaptoethanol, 300 mM imidazole). Proteins were concentrated using centrifugal concentrators and further purified by size-exclusion chromatography on a Superdex 200 Increase 10/300 GL column, using gel filtration buffer (25 mM HEPES, pH 7.5, 300 mM NaCl, 5 mM MgCl2, 10% glycerol, 1 mM dithiothreitol). Purified proteins were quantified by spectrophotometry and stored in aliquots, flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen, and stored at − 80 °C.

In vitro SARS-CoV-2 NP-RNA interaction assay

A homogeneous time-resolved fluorescence (HTRF) assay was used to measure the interaction between SARS-CoV-2 NP and RNA as previously described [25]. The assay was performed in white 384-well microplates using HTRF reaction buffer (phosphate-buffered saline, PBS, supplemented with 0.05% Tween-20). Each well received 1 µl of reaction buffer, 2 µl of biotinylated RNA (biotin-UCUAAACGAAC), and 2 µl of His6-tagged SARS-CoV-2 NP (His-NP). Following a 1-hour incubation at room temperature, 5 µl of a mixture containing anti-His6-Eu cryptate (as the fluorescent donor) and XL665-conjugated SA (as the fluorescent acceptor) in assay buffer, with 100 mM potassium fluoride, was added. After another 1-hour incubation at room temperature, the plate was read using an Envision multilabel reader. Raw counts per second (cps) were measured at 665 nm and 620 nm, and the signal was expressed as the 665/620 nm ratio × 1000. Dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) was used as a negative control, while GCG served as a positive control. Prior to the addition of the assay mixture, compounds were dispensed into each well of the 384-well plate. Dose-response curves and IC50 values were calculated and visualized using GraphPad Prism software.

Binding affinities of compounds to SARS-CoV-2 NP or RNA

Binding kinetics were analyzed using the Octet RED96 system, with assays conducted in black 96-well plates and a kinetic buffer (PBS containing 0.01% Tween-20). The total working volume for each well was 200 µl, and all binding studies were performed at 30 °C with continuous shaking at 1000 rpm. Before each assay, NTA biosensor tips were pre-immersed in 200 µl of PBS for 10 min at room temperature. Data were analyzed using ForteBio software, globally fitted using a 1:1 Langmuir binding model to determine the dissociation constant (KD). Binding affinities of compounds to SARS-CoV-2 NP, NP NTD, and NP CTD were assessed using BLI. For each protein (50 µg/ml), NTA biosensors were loaded for 300 s at room temperature. Following a baseline period of 60 s, sensors were exposed to increasing concentrations of compounds (0, 6.25, 25, and 100 µM) for 60 s of association, followed by a 60-s dissociation period in the kinetic buffer. Double-reference subtraction was applied to calculate binding constants.

To determine the binding affinities of compounds to RNA, biotinylated ssRNA (50 nM) was coated onto SA biosensors for 800 s. After a baseline step of 60 s in kinetic buffer, the sensors were exposed to solutions containing varying concentrations of compounds (0, 6.25, 25, and 100 µM) for 60 s of association, followed by a 60-s dissociation period in the kinetic buffer. Control sets, including interference patterns from the biotinylated RNA with buffer and uncoated biosensors with a mixture of compounds, were analyzed. Double-reference subtracted data were used to calculate binding constants.

Molecular docking analysis

Molecular docking was performed using the Neurosnap online platform (https://www.neurosnap.ai/) with the Boltz-1 (AlphaFold3) model. This AI-driven approach integrates state-of-the-art protein/RNA structure prediction with ligand–target docking in a single workflow, eliminating the need for separate target structure preparation, ligand optimization, or manual conformational sampling.

For RNA–ligand docking, the nucleotide sequence of the SARS-CoV-2 NP–binding RNA fragment was used as input, together with the SMILES strings of doxorubicin, epirubicin, mitoxantrone, and pixantrone. For protein–ligand docking, the full-length SARS-CoV-2 NP amino acid sequence was uploaded along with the SMILES string of suramin. Neurosnap’s Boltz-1 (AlphaFold3) engine automatically predicted the 3D structure of the target RNA or protein and performed docking to the ligand in a single computational pipeline. Default scoring functions were applied, and the top-scoring poses were selected for analysis. Predicted binding sites and key interacting residues or nucleotides were identified and recorded.

Results

Mechanism-informed screening strategy

Our experimental rationale is summarized in Fig. 1. Previous biochemical studies have shown that ATP competitively inhibits viral RNA binding to the SARS-CoV-2 NP by occupying its nucleic acid–binding domains. Building on this finding, we hypothesized that ATP-competitive kinase inhibitors—designed to bind ATP-binding pockets with high affinity—might similarly interfere with the NP–RNA interaction.

Mechanism-informed screening strategy. Schematic illustration of the hypothesis-driven screening approach employed in this study to identify inhibitors of the SARS-CoV-2 NP–RNA interaction. Traditional antiviral drug discovery often relies on random large-scale screening of vast chemical libraries without prior mechanistic insights, resulting in high costs and low hit rates. In contrast, our mechanism-informed strategy leverages the established observation that ATP competitively disrupts the binding of RNA to the NP. Guided by this mechanistic understanding, we hypothesized that ATP-competitive kinase inhibitors might similarly interfere with N–RNA interactions

In contrast to conventional large-scale screening, which often relies on random testing of large chemical libraries that yield low hit rates, our approach is hypothesis-driven and mechanism-informed. As illustrated in Fig. 1, the workflow begins with the established observation that ATP disrupts NP–RNA binding, forming the conceptual mechanistic anchor for the screen. Guided by this rationale, we assembled a focused set of 121 FDA-approved ATP-competitive kinase inhibitors, selected for their structural compatibility with ATP-binding sites and established pharmacological profiles.

The screening proceeded in two stages. In the primary screen, compounds were tested at a uniform high concentration to quickly capture potential inhibitors of the NP–RNA interaction. Positive candidates were then subjected to secondary assays, including quantitative determination of binding affinities and inhibition constants, to validate activity and assess potency. This stepwise design reduced false positives and established a direct mechanistic link to ATP-site competition.

By integrating prior mechanistic insights with a rational compound selection and validation pipeline, this strategy improves screening efficiency, reduces resource requirements, and increases the likelihood of identifying biologically relevant NP–RNA interaction inhibitors. In doing so, it reflects a targeted antiviral discovery framework, visually contrasted in Fig. 1 with the less efficient traditional screening paradigm.

Identification of candidate inhibitors

To test this hypothesis, 121 ATP-competitive kinase inhibitors were selected from an FDA-approved drug library (details in Table S1). These compounds were screened for their ability to inhibit the NP–RNA interaction using a previously established HTRF assay. The screening workflow is shown in Fig. 2A, with all compounds tested at 50 µM (raw data shown in Table S2). GCG was used as a positive control. As shown in Fig. 2B, mitoxantrone exhibited the strongest inhibition rate, reducing NP–RNA binding by 85.4%. Notably, mitoxantrone functions as both a protein kinase C inhibitor and a topoisomerase II inhibitor. This dual activity prompted further screening of 23 topoisomerase inhibitors (details in Table S3) from the same library. As shown in Fig. 2C, four topoisomerase inhibitors—doxorubicin, pixantrone, epirubicin, and suramin—exhibited strong inhibition, with rates of 86.4%, 90.6%, 91.8%, and 98.5%, respectively (raw data shown in Table S2). The chemical structures of these five active compounds are illustrated in Fig. 2D. These results demonstrate that both ATP-competitive kinase inhibitors and topoisomerase inhibitors can provide active compounds that disrupt the NP–RNA interaction, thereby validating our hypothesis and screening strategy.

Dose-dependent Inhibition of NP–RNA binding

To characterize the inhibitory potency of the five hit compounds, dose–response experiments were performed using serial dilutions (0.09–50 µM) in the HTRF assay (raw data shown in Table S2). The half-maximal inhibitory concentration (IC₅₀) values were determined as follows: mitoxantrone, 1.22 µM (Fig. 3A); doxorubicin, 20.19 µM (Fig. 3B); pixantrone, 5.67 µM (Fig. 3C); suramin, 0.44 µM (Fig. 3D); and epirubicin, 7.23 µM (Fig. 3E). For reference, under identical assay conditions, we previously reported an IC₅₀ of 2.80 µM for the positive control GCG [25]. These data confirm that all five compounds exhibited dose-dependent inhibition of the NP–RNA interaction, with suramin showing the greatest potency. For translational context, we summarize literature-reported antiviral activities (cell types and EC₅₀/IC₅₀) and cytotoxicity (CC₅₀) for each retrieved scaffold in Supplementary Table S5.

Binding affinities between compounds and NP

To assess whether the observed inhibitory effects were due to direct binding to NP, BLI assays were performed. A working model and experimental setup are shown in Fig. 4A and B. An initial single-concentration screen (100 µM) showed that only suramin exhibited detectable binding to NP (Fig. 4C). Subsequently, five-point dilution assays were performed to determine the dissociation constants (KD) for suramin binding to full-length NP and its domains (Fig. 4D). Suramin displayed the highest affinity for NP CTD (KD = 0.26 µM, Fig. 4E), followed by full-length NP (KD = 1.67 µM, Fig. 4F), and the lowest affinity for the NP NTD (KD = 8.01 µM, Fig. 4G). These findings indicate that suramin specifically targets NP, particularly the CTD, whereas the other compounds do not directly interact with the protein. All raw data were shown in Table S4.

RNA-binding activities of the remaining compounds

Since mitoxantrone, doxorubicin, pixantrone, and epirubicin did not bind to NP, we hypothesized that their inhibitory effects might result from binding to viral RNA and preventing its interaction with NP (Fig. 5A). To evaluate this, BLI was used to measure their binding affinities to 5’-biotinylated viral RNA immobilized on a SA sensor (Fig. 5B). In a single-concentration screen (100 µM), all four compounds exhibited measurable RNA binding, whereas suramin did not (Fig. 5C). Subsequent five-point dilution assays (Fig. 5D) revealed the following KD values: pixantrone, 0.63 µM (Fig. 5E); epirubicin, 13.2 µM (Fig. 5F); doxorubicin, 23.7 µM (Fig. 5G); and mitoxantrone, 63.3 µM (Fig. 5H). These results support a distinct mechanism whereby the four anthracycline-related compounds inhibit the NP–RNA interaction through direct binding to viral RNA. All raw data were shown in Table S4.

Molecular Docking analysis

Molecular docking was performed using the Neurosnap platform with the Boltz-1 (AlphaFold3) model to further explore the molecular basis of compound activity. The results revealed distinct binding modes for the anthracycline/anthracenedione derivatives compared with suramin. For RNA-binding compounds, doxorubicin interacted strongly with nucleotides G8 and C2 through hydrogen bonding and π–π stacking (Fig. 6A), whereas epirubicin formed multiple contacts with C7, U3, and G8 (Fig. 6B). Mitoxantrone (Fig. 6C) and pixantrone (Fig. 6D) both exhibited preferential binding to C2 and G8, suggesting these nucleotides represent recurrent hotspots for small-molecule engagement. In contrast, suramin displayed a protein-directed binding mode, engaging multiple positively charged residues (R385, R388, R260, R259, and R338) within or near to the NP CTD (Fig. 6E). These docking simulations are consistent with our biochemical data, highlighting two complementary inhibitory mechanisms: anthracycline/anthracenedione derivatives disrupt RNA availability by direct RNA binding, whereas suramin targets the NP, particularly the CTD, to block RNA association. Discussion

Most antiviral drug development efforts against SARS-CoV-2 have primarily focused on well-established viral targets, including S protein, envelope (E) protein, RdRp, and Mpro (also known as 3CLpro) [10]. However, the NP, which is essential at multiple stages of viral replication, has received comparatively less attention as a direct antiviral target. In our previous work, we established a robust high-throughput screening method to identify inhibitors of the NP–RNA interaction [25]. Building on this platform, the present study explored strategies for selecting candidate compounds for further evaluation.

Our biochemical assays confirmed that ATP-mimicking small molecules can interfere with NP–RNA binding, in line with previous reports that ATP itself can displace RNA from NP. [22, 23]. This mechanistic link provides a rationale for evaluating ATP-competitive kinase inhibitors in this context. Indeed, imatinib—an ATP-competitive kinase inhibitor originally developed for cancer therapy—was even explored in clinical trials during the COVID-19 pandemic as a potential therapeutic agent against SARS-CoV-2. [24] Within our focused screen of 121 FDA-approved ATP-competitive kinase inhibitors, mitoxantrone emerged as a potent NP–RNA inhibitor. Notably, mitoxantrone also functions as a topoisomerase inhibitor, prompting us to expand our search to 23 additional topoisomerase inhibitors. This strategy successfully identified four further active compounds—pixantrone, doxorubicin, epirubicin, and suramin—that robustly disrupted NP–RNA binding.

Subsequent biophysical analyses revealed distinct binding modes for these compounds. Suramin specifically targets the SARS-CoV-2 NP, whereas mitoxantrone, pixantrone, doxorubicin, and epirubicin primarily bind to RNA. These findings validate our strategic approach, demonstrating that focused, hypothesis-driven compound selection can efficiently yield active inhibitors without relying on large-scale indiscriminate screening efforts.

Mitoxantrone is a well-characterized inhibitor of protein kinase C [26] and a potent topoisomerase II inhibitor [27]. It exhibits well-documented anticancer activity [28, 29] and is clinically used in the treatment of multiple sclerosis [30]. Previous studies have also reported antiviral properties of mitoxantrone against viruses such as orthopoxvirus, where it blocks virion assembly possibly by targeting viral DNA ligase and preventing recruitment of host topoisomerase II; and against respiratory syncytial virus, where it inhibits replication in vitro through a mechanism independent of classical topoisomerase inhibition [31, 32]. Recent research further indicates that mitoxantrone effectively inhibits SARS-CoV-2 replication in cell-based models, primarily through targeting the viral Mpro, binding to human cell-surface heparan sulfate to block viral attachment, and interacting with the CTD of non-structural protein 1 to disrupt its association with the 40 S ribosomal subunit and subsequent suppression of host mRNA translation [33–35]. However, further development is hampered by its cytotoxicity and a narrow therapeutic window (EC50 ≈ 10 µM; CC50 ≈ 8 µM; selectivity index < 1). [36] In the current study, we found that mitoxantrone also effectively inhibits the SARS-CoV-2 NP–RNA interaction. Interestingly, previous high-throughput screening studies demonstrated that mitoxantrone could inhibit interactions between RNA and RNA-binding proteins, such as the HuR [37]. Those authors suggested that mitoxantrone, a well-known nucleic acid ligand, [38] might inhibit RNA-binding interactions through non-specific precipitation of mRNA [39]. Additional research indicates that mitoxantrone can stabilize RNA secondary structures, such as the stem-loop structure of tau pre-mRNA, implying that RNA binding could be fundamental to its diverse pharmacological effects [40].

Pixantrone, a structural analogue of mitoxantrone and a known topoisomerase II inhibitor and DNA intercalator, exhibits promising anticancer and immunomodulatory activities [41–43]. Recent studies indicated that pixantrone inhibits SARS-CoV-2 replication by targeting cellular heparan sulfate and impairing viral inclusion body formation [43]. Consistent with prior reports, pixantrone inhibited SARS-CoV-2 infection in HEK293T cells (EC₅₀ ≈ 0.3 µM; CC₅₀ >50 µM) and reduced pulmonary viral loads in K18-hACE2 transgenic mice when administered at 100 µg/kg. [36] Our findings demonstrate that pixantrone, like mitoxantrone, effectively disrupts the NP–RNA interaction through direct RNA binding.

Doxorubicin and its derivative epirubicin, two widely used anthracycline chemotherapeutics known to inhibit human topoisomerases, [44–46] also emerged as active inhibitors of the NP–RNA interaction. Prior studies in Calu-3 human lung epithelial cells reported that doxorubicin (EC₅₀ ≈ 1.169 µM; CC₅₀ >10 µM) and epirubicin (EC₅₀ ≈ 1.078 µM; CC₅₀ >10 µM) inhibit SARS-CoV-2 infection [47, 48]. These effects may be mediated through activation of interferon-stimulated gene pathways [43] and inhibition of viral enzymes such as TMPRSS2 (transmembrane protease serine 2) and PLpro (papain-like protease) [48]. In the present study, we show that both anthracyclines disrupt the NP–RNA interaction primarily by binding directly to RNA. Given their well-characterized nucleic acid binding properties, [49–51] it is likely that their antiviral effects are at least partially mediated by RNA binding, providing another mechanism underlying their antiviral efficacy.

Suramin, an established antiparasitic and antiviral compound, [52] was identified in our screen as directly targeting the SARS-CoV-2 NP. Using BLI, we measured the binding affinities of suramin to the full-length NP, the NP NTD, and the NP CTD separately. The results showed that suramin binds to all three constructs, with the strongest interaction observed for the NP CTD (KD = 0.26 µM), followed by the full-length protein (KD = 1.67 µM) and the NP NTD (KD = 8.01 µM). This finding is consistent with previous work reporting predominant binding of suramin to the NP CTD and its ability to impair NP–RNA binding at low micromolar concentrations [20]. In a separate study, Guo et al. demonstrated that suramin can block the interaction between NP NTD and RNA, but they did not examine its binding to the NP CTD or the full-length protein [53]. Taken together, these results are not contradictory; rather, they are complementary. Both studies support the conclusion that suramin can disrupt NP–RNA binding, while our work extends these findings by showing that suramin engages multiple domains of NP, with the highest affinity for the NP CTD. In Vero E6 cells, suramin inhibited SARS-CoV-2 with an EC₅₀ of 20 ± 2.7 µM (CC₅₀ ≈ 5 mM); subsequent work showed variant-dependent potency with EC₅₀ values of 134 ± 32 µM (wild type), 80 ± 19 µM (Delta), and 3.0 ± 1.5 µM (Omicron), while cytotoxicity remained low (CC₅₀ >10 mM) [54, 55]. Collectively, these findings highlight that the identified compounds engage the NP or its RNA with distinct mechanisms, and for suramin, its antiviral potential is supported not only by its direct NP binding but also by its reported cellular activity against SARS-CoV-2.

Although several of the compounds identified in this study—such as mitoxantrone, pixantrone, doxorubicin, and epirubicin—are primarily approved for cancer therapy and are associated with notable toxicity profiles, it is important to emphasize that our intent is not to propose their direct clinical use against SARS-CoV-2. Instead, these molecules should be viewed as chemical starting points (hits or lead scaffolds) for subsequent structure optimization aimed at improving selectivity, reducing toxicity, and enhancing antiviral potency. Even suramin, the only antiparasitic agent identified here, is known to have limited selectivity and potential adverse effects, yet it remains a valuable mechanistic probe and a promising scaffold for further medicinal chemistry optimization. Moreover, potential off-target interactions with human proteins, while a legitimate concern for clinical translation, do not diminish the utility of these compounds in the early stages of drug discovery. We have therefore discussed their reported mechanisms, therapeutic indications, and possible limitations to provide a balanced perspective on their role in antiviral development.

In conclusion, we applied a mechanism-informed screening strategy and identified several FDA-approved topoisomerase inhibitors, along with suramin, that disrupt SARS-CoV-2 NP–RNA interactions. Biophysical assays and molecular docking analyses revealed distinct binding preferences, with suramin binding directly to the NP, whereas anthracycline derivatives primarily interact with RNA. Although these compounds are not immediately suitable for clinical application due to their known toxicity profiles, they serve as valuable chemical scaffolds and offer mechanistic insights for future drug discovery efforts.
