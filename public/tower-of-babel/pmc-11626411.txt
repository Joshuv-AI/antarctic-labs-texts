Teneurin C‐Terminal Associated Peptide (TCAP)‐1 Attenuates Restraint Stress‐Induced Corticosterone Increases in Male Mice and Rats
Authors: Lauren E Mueller, Roseanne S Wexler, David A Lovejoy, Robert B Stein, Andrew M Slee
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTHyperactivation of the hypothalamic‐pituitary‐adrenal (HPA) axis response can result in anxiety and other neuropsychiatric disorders and effective therapeutics are needed to mitigate this maladaptive response. Here we examined the effects of Teneurin C‐terminal Associated Peptide (TCAP)‐1, a peptide known to inhibit corticotropin releasing factor (CRF)‐mediated stress, on the physiological expression of stress, and whether the effects of TCAP‐1 were dependent on the route of administration. We first examined whether subcutaneous administration of TCAP‐1 influenced tube restraint stress‐induced corticosterone (CORT) increases in both male mice and rats. Using a similar model, we further examined the efficacy and time course of intranasal TCAP‐1. Results showed that subcutaneous TCAP‐1 administration attenuated the expression of the physiological manifestation of stress in male mice and rats, and that intranasal TCAP‐1 delivered prophylactically is effective at attenuating stress‐induced CORT increases in male rats. These data indicate that TCAP‐1 delivered though non‐invasive routes of administration could have potential as a clinically relevant anxiolytic.

Introduction

While exposure to a noxious stimulus elicits a multitude of effects in an organism, even the threat of one can induce neuroendocrine, autonomic, and behavioral responses and precipitate an anticipatory state of stress known as anxiety [1]. Anxiety, which is modulated in particular by the hypothalamic‐pituitary‐adrenal (HPA) axis and sympatho‐medullary system [2], can occur regardless of whether the stimulus (i.e., stressor) is consequential and is worsened by continued exposure to the perceived stressor [3]. Following exposure to a stressor, an increase in Corticotropin‐Releasing Factor (CRF), secreted from the paraventricular nucleus of the hypothalamus, initiates the HPA axis response, a signaling cascade that ultimately results in the release of glucocorticoids, such as corticosterone in rodents or cortisol in humans, from the adrenal cortex [4]. Hyperactivation of this response has been suggested to play a vital role in anxiety and other neuropsychiatric disorders [5, 6].

CRF belongs to an evolutionarily conserved family of peptides that are integral to the regulation of homeostatic‐related physiological functions in metazoans [7, 8]. The persistence of this family of peptides indicates that CRF‐like responses are advantageous to survival. Essential genes throughout evolution often give rise to sister lineages through various genetic duplication events and become further associated with other systems [7, 8]. The fact that CRF is an early‐evolved system, together with the nature in which evolving systems associate, suggests that CRF may in turn be modulated by an even earlier existing system.

One such CRF predecessor may be Teneurins, Teneurin C‐terminal Associated Peptide (TCAP)‐1, a member of the TCAP family [9] has been shown to play an important role in the regulation of stress [10]. Previous in situ hybridization studies found that TCAP‐1 mRNA and CRF receptors are highly co‐expressed in the same brain regions that are critical for stress regulation such as the limbic system, hypothalamus, and cerebellum [10, 11]. Additionally, i.c.v. administration of TCAP‐1 prior to CRF attenuated c‐Fos expression in these regions and reduced anxiety‐like behaviors [12], suggesting that TCAP‐1 inhibits CRF‐induced stress‐related activity. Furthermore, i.c.v. TCAP‐1 exhibited anxiolytic effects in two behavioral paradigms frequently employed to study anxiolytic drugs, increasing the amount of time spent in the open arms on an elevated plus‐maze and decreasing the CRF‐associated acoustic startle response [11, 13]. However, findings also suggest that TCAP‐1 likely exerts its neuromodulatory effects through an HPA‐independent CRF‐related mechanism [10, 11, 12, 13, 14], indicating that additional signaling pathways may have evolved in parallel with the HPA axis‐mediated pathway and that other CRF system modulators such as TCAP‐1 should be further evaluated as a target for the treatment of stress.

Here we examined the effects of TCAP‐1 on the physiological expression of stress using two different systemic routes of administration. Using tube restraint as a model of stress [15], we first examined whether subcutaneous administration of TCAP‐1 following the onset of stress influenced CORT increases in both mice and rats. Using a similar model of stress, we examined the efficacy and time course of prophylactic intranasal TCAP‐1 at attenuating CORT level increases elicited by stress in rats.

Methods

Drugs

Synthetic mouse TCAP‐1 (TCAP‐1) was synthesized and purchased from Chinese Peptide Company, Incorporated (Hangzhou, China). TCAP‐1 is 41 amino acids long with the sequence: QQLLGTGRVQGYDGYEVLSVEQYLELS DSANNIHEMROSEI [11]. TCAP‐1 was first suspended in distilled water with ammonium hydroxide and vortexed and further diluted using saline. In Figures 1, 2, 3 TCAP‐1 (50–500 nmol/kg) was administered subcutaneously (s.c.) while in Figures 3 and 4 TCAP‐1 (3–250 nmol/kg) was administered intranasally (i.n.). I.N. drug delivery was achieved by hand restraining rats at approximately a 45° angle and administering drug into the nose via pipette.

Animals

Male 8‐week‐old C57BL/6 mice (Charles River Laboratories, Kingston, NY) and male 8‐week‐old Sprague Dawley rats (Charles River Laboratories, Kingston, NY) were used for these experiments and randomly assigned to treatment groups. Subjects were group housed on a 12/12‐h light/dark cycle with behavior conducted during the light period. Mice and rats had free access to water and food except during periods of stress and blood sampling. All housing and experimental conditions complied with national guidelines for the care and use of laboratory animals and by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee guidelines of NeoSome Life Sciences LLC (Lexington, MA). The number of animals used for each experiment are reported in the corresponding statistics and figure legends. Power analyses were not performed, however group sizes were chosen based on similar studies using these assays which generated statistically interrogable data. At least six animals were initially assigned to each treatment group.

Experimental Design

Four studies were conducted to evaluate the effects of TCAP‐1 administered via two different routes with experimenters blinded to the treatments on the tube restraint stress‐induced response in mice and rats. First, a proof‐of‐concept study was conducted in mice to evaluate the ability of TCAP‐1 (250 nmol/kg; s.c.) to attenuate stress‐elevated plasma corticosterone (CORT). Second, a similar study was carried out using rats and multiple doses of TCAP‐1 (50–500 nmol/kg; s.c.) to examine whether the effects translated across species and to establish an optimal s.c. dose. Third, a dose‐range finding study was conducted in rats to establish optimal doses of TCAP‐1 (30–250 nmol/kg) administered i.n. prior to stress to attenuate the stress‐induced CORT increase. Fourth, a similar study was conducted in rats at a lower dose‐range with an additional timepoint to more thoroughly establish optimal doses of TCAP‐1 (3–18.5 nmol/kg; i.n.) as well as the time course of its effects.

Tube Restraint Stress

Physical restraint was used to induce stress by removing subjects from their home cage and gently placing them in tubes that restrain major head and limb movement for 15 min (Figures 1 and 2) or 40 min (Figures 3 and 4; [16, 17, 18]). Specifically, tubes used for mice were polycarbonate cylinders (20 cm long, 3.175 cm in diameter), while those used for rats were polycarbonate cylinders (20 cm long, 6.5 cm in diameter). Immediately following restraint stress, subjects were either placed back into their home cage or a blood sample was collected and then subjects were returned to their cage.

Plasma Corticosterone Assessment

A total of 100–200 μL of blood was sampled to detect plasma CORT levels both at the beginning of the experiment before subjects were stressed to establish baselines and then at multiple timepoints after some instances of tube restraint‐stress. In mice, a 5 mm lancet was used to prick the submandibular vein, blood was collected into heparin separator mini collect tubes (Greiner Bio‐One, Monroe, North Carolina) and placed on ice. A similar process was carried out for the collection of blood from rats except samples were taken from either the saphenous vein using a 7 mm lancet, or by cardiac puncture in anesthetized rats using a 23‐gauge needle attached to a 5 mL syringe. Samples were then spun in a refrigerated microcentrifuge at 2000 × g for 8 min, after which plasma was extracted. Finally, plasma CORT levels were quantified using a rodent CORT enzyme‐linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA, Cayman Chemical Company, Ann Arbor, MI). The process was executed as per the manufacturer's instruction, with absorbance measured at 420 nm using a microplate reader (Molecular Devices, San Jose, CA). Values registering outside of the standard curve were excluded from subsequent analyses.

Data Analysis

All statistical analyses and graphs were performed using Prism 9 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA). ROUT outlier analysis was performed within each treatment group for each test; outliers were excluded from subsequent analysis. Values were calculated as percent increases from group baseline averages and expressed as mean ± SD. Statistics were performed on ELISA data using one‐ or two‐way analysis of variance (ANOVA) with follow‐up Bonferroni's or Dunnett's (when there were multiple treatment groups) tests for multiple comparisons to determine significance. An a priori level of significance of p < 0.05 was considered significant in these studies.

Results

Subcutaneous TCAP‐1 Administration Attenuates the Physiological Expression of Stress in Mice

Since our previous findings indicated that s.c. TCAP‐1 was effective in attenuating naloxone‐precipitated jumping in mice [19], we first conducted a small experiment to determine whether TCAP‐1 administration attenuates physiological responses to a stressful stimulus in mice. To do so, we measured plasma corticosterone (CORT) levels before, during, and after repeated stressors in mice treated with either TCAP‐1 (250 nmol/kg, s.c.) or vehicle (Figure 1a). For this experiment, mice received a daily 15‐min tube restraint stressor for 6 days. Blood samples were collected prior to the first stressor on Day 1, as well as after the stressors on Days 4 and 6. Meanwhile, TCAP‐1 or vehicle was administered prior to the stress sessions on Days 2 and 3. Plasma was isolated from blood samples and assayed for CORT concentration, and values were calculated as percent increases with respect to baseline levels. Across plasma samples, there was an effect of treatment (day × treatment two‐way ANOVA; treatment: F1,8 = 53.63, p < 0.0001; vehicle (s.c.), n = 4; 250 TCAP‐1 (nmol/kg; s.c.), n = 6; Figure 1b) but no day × treatment interaction. While plasma CORT levels increased by nearly 200% from baseline levels on Day 4 in the vehicle control group indicating the success of the procedure, the percent increase from baseline was lower in the TCAP‐1‐treated mice. Interestingly, while CORT levels remained elevated from baseline on Day 6 in the vehicle‐treated control group, CORT levels in mice treated with TCAP‐1 were lower, returning to near baseline concentrations.

Subcutaneous TCAP‐1 Administration Attenuates the Physiological Expression of Stress in Rats

We next sought to determine the effects of subcutaneous TCAP‐1 treatment on the physiological stress response in rats using a broader dose range. Using an identical design as in the previous experiment (i.e., six daily 15‐min stressors), we measured plasma CORT concentrations before, during, and after repeated stressors in rats treated across a dose range of TCAP‐1 (50–500 nmol/kg; s.c.) or vehicle (Figure 2a). There were effects of day and treatment with a day × treatment interaction (two‐way ANOVA; day: F1,21 = 19.6, p = 0.0002; treatment: F4,25 = 6.811, p = 0.0007; int: F4,21 = 6.161, p = 0.0019; Vehicle (s.c.), n = 6; 50 TCAP‐1 (nmol/kg; s.c.), n = 6; 100 TCAP‐1 (nmol/kg; s.c.), n = 6; 250 TCAP‐1 (nmol/kg; s.c.), n = 5; 500 TCAP‐1 (nmol/kg; s.c.), n = 6; Figure 2b). While CORT levels increased from baseline on Day 4 in the vehicle‐treated control group, those treated with TCAP‐1 (50–500 nmol/kg; s.c.) demonstrated concentrations closer to baseline with several treatment groups being comparatively lower than controls (Day 4: ‘vehicle’ vs. ‘50 TCAP‐1′, p = 0.0002; ‘vehicle’ vs. ‘100 TCAP‐1′, p < 0.001; ‘vehicle’ vs. ‘250 TCAP‐1′, p = 0.0021; ‘vehicle’ vs. ‘500 TCAP‐1′, p < 0.0001; Dunnett's test). Although rats were retested on Day 6, CORT levels had returned to baseline in the vehicle‐treated control rats.

Pretreatment With Intranasal TCAP‐1 Attenuates the Physiological Expression of Stress in Rats

Intranasal (i.n.) drug administration is a non‐invasive method of drug delivery that often achieves faster onset with reduced side effects compared to more traditional systemic routes of administration such as subcutaneous, intravenous, and intraperitoneal [20, 21, 22, 23, 24]. Therefore, we next wanted to test whether pretreatment with TCAP‐1 i.n. attenuated the physiological expression of stress in rats. To do so, we measured CORT levels before and after stress in rats pretreated with a dose range of TCAP‐1 i.n. (30–250 nmol/kg) or vehicle i.n. (Figure 3a). Rats treated with TCAP‐1 s.c. (250 nmol/kg) were also included as a positive treatment control‐comparison group (Figure 3a). Since the previous experiments showed that TCAP‐1 was more effective in rats as compared to mice in attenuating repeated tube restraint stress, for this experiment, we administered the rats a single yet more profound 40‐min session of tube restraint stress to increase the overall CORT response. Blood was collected pre‐stress and prior to the first TCAP‐1 treatment, as well as at three different timepoints post‐stress (Day 8). Meanwhile, TCAP‐1 or vehicle was administered for two consecutive days prior to tube restraint stress. There were effects of day, treatment, as well as day × treatment interaction (two‐way ANOVA; day: F1.516,91.71 = 24.40, p < 0.0001; treatment: F5,61 = 18.43, p < 0.0001; int: F10,121 = 3.405, p = 0.0006; vehicle (i.n.), n = 12; 30 TCAP‐1 (nmol/kg; i.n.), n = 12; 60 TCAP‐1 (nmol/kg; i.n.), n = 12; 125 TCAP‐1 (nmol/kg; i.n.), n = 11; 250 TCAP‐1 (nmol/kg; i.n.), n = 11; 250 TCAP‐1 (nmol/kg; s.c.), n = 6; Figure 3b). Plasma CORT was generally increased from baseline levels in samples collected from the vehicle‐treated control group across all time points following stress, indicating the success of the assay. The two lower doses of TCAP‐1 i.n. produced a dose‐dependent attenuating effect on stress‐induced CORT increases at 0 and 30 min post‐stress, with statistical effects apparent for the 60 nmol/kg group (0 min: ‘vehicle i.n.’ vs. ‘60 TCAP‐1 i.n.’, p = 0.0137; 30 min: ‘vehicle i.n.’ vs. ‘60 TCAP‐1 i.n.’, p = 0.0012; Dunnett's test). In contrast, the two higher doses of TCAP‐1 i.n. did not attenuate stress‐induced plasma CORT increases. Inspection of the data indicated that these doses of TCAP‐1 i.n. increased CORT responses beyond the vehicle‐treated control group, which was supported statistically for 125 nmol/kg group (0 min: ‘vehicle i.n.’ vs. ‘125 TCAP‐1 i.n.’, p < 0.0001; Dunnett's test). Importantly, in agreement with previous findings, 250 nmol/kg TCAP‐1 s.c. attenuated stress‐induced CORT increases at 30 min post‐stress compared to the vehicle‐treated controls (30 min: ‘vehicle i.n.’ vs. ‘250 TCAP‐1 s.c.’, p = 0.0052; Dunnett's test), indicating the appropriateness of our dosing schedule and stress paradigm. A similar pattern of effects was visually apparent at 60 min.

Pretreatment With Low‐Dose Intranasal TCAP‐1 Attenuates the Physiological Expression of Stress in Rats

Because pretreatment with TCAP‐1 i.n. (30–60 nmol/kg) attenuated stress‐induced CORT increases in rats, we further decreased the dose range to find lower effective doses and characterize the persistence of their effects. We measured CORT levels before and after stress in rats pretreated with a lower dose range of TCAP‐1 i.n. (3–18.5 nmol/kg) or vehicle (Figure 4a). For this experiment, rats received two 40‐min sessions of tube restraint stress. Blood was collected pre‐stress and prior to the first TCAP‐1 treatment, three timepoints after the initial stress session on Day 6, and then two timepoints after the final stress session on Day 11. Meanwhile, TCAP‐1 or vehicle was administered for two consecutive days prior to tube restraint stress on Day 6. There were effects of day and treatment with a day × treatment interaction across groups on the percent of stress‐induced CORT increases from baseline (two‐way ANOVA; day: F2,120 = 32.40, p < 0.0001; treatment: F5,64 = 19.83, p < 0.0001; int.: F10,120 = 3.651, p = 0.0003; vehicle (i.n.), n = 8; 3 TCAP‐1 (nmol/kg; i.n.), n = 8; 9.5 TCAP‐1 (nmol/kg; i.n.), n = 8; 18.5 TCAP‐1 (nmol/kg; i.n.), n = 8; Figure 4b). Again, consistent with the effect of restraint stress, CORT plasma concentrations were elevated from baseline levels in samples collected from the vehicle‐treated control group on both Days 6 and 11 at all‐time intervals post‐stress, indicating success of our paradigm. Across testing intervals, inspection of the data indicated a generally dose‐dependent effect of TCAP‐1 i.n. on decreasing CORT plasma levels compared to baseline. Statistical testing supported these observations, with CORT increases attenuated across the TCAP‐1 i.n. dose range compared to vehicle both immediately (Day 6, 0 min.: ‘vehicle’ vs. ‘3 TCAP‐1′, p < 0.0001; ‘vehicle’ vs. ‘9.5 TCAP‐1′, p < 0.0001; ‘vehicle’ vs. ‘18.5 TCAP‐1′, p < 0.0001; Dunnett's test), 30 min (Day 6, 30 min.: ‘vehicle’ vs. ‘3 TCAP‐1′, p = 0.0042; ‘vehicle’ vs. ‘9.5 TCAP‐1′, p = 0.0035; ‘vehicle’ vs. ‘18.5 TCAP‐1′, p = 0.0002; Dunnett's test), and 60 min following stress (Day 6, 60 min.: ‘vehicle’ vs. ‘3 TCAP‐1′, p = 0.0027; ‘vehicle’ vs. ‘9.5 TCAP‐1′, p < 0.0001; ‘vehicle’ vs. ‘18.5 TCAP‐1′, p < 0.0001; Dunnett's test). Similar trends in the percent increase in CORT concentration from baseline were noted on Day 11, with statistical testing supporting these observations across the TCAP‐1 i.n. dose range compared to vehicle at 30 min following Stress 3 (Day 11, 30 min.: ‘vehicle’ vs. ‘3 TCAP‐1′, p = 0.0062; ‘vehicle’ vs. ‘9.5 TCAP‐1′, p = 0.0004; ‘vehicle’ vs. ‘18.5 TCAP‐1′, p = 0.0001; Dunnett's test).

Discussion

For the first time, we investigated the effects of TCAP‐1 on the physiological expression of stress induced by tube restraint using two different routes of administration. As demonstrated in previous literature, restraint stress increased plasma CORT levels, a readout of physiological stress, in both mice and rats [25, 26]. Initiation of subcutaneous TCAP‐1 treatment following the onset of stress sessions attenuated plasma CORT responses to subsequent stressors, indicating the suppression of physiological stress by TCAP‐1 in mice and rats. Further, our studies revealed that pretreatment with intranasal TCAP‐1 before the first tube restraint session suppressed future stress‐induced plasma CORT increases with comparable efficacy to that of subcutaneous administration, broadening the potential therapeutic application of TCAP‐1 due to the lower invasiveness of this route of administration. We also found the effect of intranasal TCAP‐1 to be more durable than previously observed, with stress‐attenuating effects apparent at 6 days post‐administration. Together, the data from this study provide evidence for the neuromodulatory role of TCAP‐1 in the stress response and warrant further studies to characterize its therapeutic utility.

This study adds to the literature base implicating TCAP‐1 as an important regulator of the stress response [10, 12, 14]. Previous studies showed that TCAP‐1 administration increased the duration of time spent in the open arm on an elevated plus‐maze and suppressed the CRF‐associated acoustic startle response [11, 13], both of which are assays commonly used to study anxiogenic and anxiolytic activity. Further, this study compliments recent mouse work that examined the effect of TCAP‐1 on naloxone‐precipitated opioid withdrawal, an assay known to elicit anxiety‐like behavioral and physiological responses [19, 27]. In the study, mice were first treated with TCAP‐1 either before or during morphine conditioning and then administered naloxone to precipitate withdrawal. TCAP‐1 dose‐dependently inhibited both the behavioral (i.e., jumping) and physiological (i.e., plasma CORT increase) consequences of withdrawal in mice. Therefore, these recent findings combined with the previous literature make a strong argument for a role of TCAP‐1 in stress‐related behavioral and physiological responses. Though the sample size of the control group in the mouse experiment was small, n = 4, we believe the demonstration of efficacy in rats in subsequent experiments shows that the effect is real and reproducible.

The anxiolytic effect of TCAP‐1 likely functions through an HPA‐independent CRF‐adjacent mechanism. Previous studies using in situ hybridization demonstrated that TCAP‐1 mRNA is highly expressed in brain regions where CRF receptor expression is found, and which also are central for the regulation of stress, like the cerebellum, limbic system, and hypothalamus [10, 11]. I.C.V. pretreatment with TCAP‐1 prior to CRF decreased CRF‐induced c‐Fos expression in these regions and suppressed anxiety‐like behaviors [12], suggesting that TCAP‐1 blocks stress‐related CRF activity. However, the timing and route of TCAP‐1 and CRF administration seem to be important determinants [14]. One study comparing the acute effects of i.c.v. and intravenous (i.v.) CRF in rats pretreated with i.v. TCAP‐1 in an open field arena found that while TCAP‐1 had an anxiolytic effect (greater time spent in center and greater distance traveled in center) on subjects receiving i.c.v. CRF, it had a mild anxiogenic effect (fewer center entries and less total distance traveled) on those that received i.v. CRF. The study further showed that acute i.v. co‐administration of CRF and TCAP‐1 elicited plasma CORT increases comparable to those measured in assay control subjects that received i.v. CRF alone. Cumulatively, the authors concluded that the ability of TCAP‐1 to regulate stress is not via direct action on peripheral glucocorticoids but rather through a central action in the brain [14]. As such, ensuring sufficient delivery and exposure of TCAP‐1 within the brain at the appropriate timepoints relative to stress onset will be an important milestone from a therapeutic development perspective. Accordingly, building on the present data, one future important addition will be elucidating the kinetics of TCAP‐1 pretreatment on modulating subsequent stress responses. 1‐ to 2‐day pretreatment with TCAP‐1 can decrease the CORT response to subsequent stressful events, but it is currently unclear whether the CORT response to stressors encountered at time points further removed from TCAP‐1 treatment, such as 1 week or 1 month, may also be dampened. While this study did demonstrate the ability of TCAP‐1 to attenuate the CORT responses to stressors 6 days following its administration, the same subjects had already been tested 1 day post‐administration. Thus, it is difficult to disentangle the purely stress‐attenuating effects of TCAP‐1 from potentially separate effects which may have facilitated faster habituation to the test–retest design in the current study. Therefore, giving a uniform pretreatment of TCAP‐1 and testing the physiological response to a single stressful event delivered at different timepoints post‐TCAP‐1 for each test group may offer a clearer picture of how long TCAP‐1 remains effective to blunt stress responses. Furthermore, examining the ability of TCAP‐1 to attenuate the development or expression of physiological and behavioral responses in chronic stress models such as chronic unpredictable stress will be of great importance.

Another important addition of the present study is the demonstration that the anxiolytic effects of TCAP‐1 are achieved following its delivery via the non‐invasive intranasal route of administration. Previously, both central (i.e., i.c.v.) and systemic (i.e., s.c. or i.v.) routes of administration had been employed to deliver TCAP‐1 to test subjects, with different effect profiles and time courses as discussed above. However, optimizing routes of administration can greatly benefit therapeutic utility, resulting from improved potency and kinetics, improved patient compliance, and lower invasiveness and dosing complications. Here, we tested intranasal delivery of TCAP‐1 across a broad dose range and compared it directly to s.c. administration. From this data we highlight two major points. First, the attenuation of stress‐induced plasma CORT increases by intranasal TCAP‐1 follows a classical inverted U‐shaped dose response curve. TCAP‐1 i.n. administration across a broad dose range over two experiments revealed that while a generally dose‐dependent CORT attenuation was apparent from doses of 3–60 nmol/kg, at the higher doses tested of 125 and 250 nmol/kg, the effect of TCAP‐1 on stress‐induced CORT diminished, potentially reflecting an off‐target mechanism. Future studies should seek to further characterize the dose–response of i.n. TCAP‐1 to determine the minimum effective dose and maximal efficacy in various models of stress. Second, i.n. delivery of a relatively small dose of TCAP‐1 (e.g., 60 nmol/kg) was able to achieve similar attenuation of plasma CORT as much larger dose given subcutaneously (e.g., 250 nmol/kg). The reasons for this may be several‐fold and range from decreased metabolism of TCAP‐1 prior to brain exposure to a more rapid and higher peak brain concentration following delivery. Regardless, the demonstration that i.n. delivery of TCAP‐1 is effective in reducing the stress response may help TCAP‐1 to be employed clinically due to decreased invasiveness and less total drug needed per administration. Future work that seeks to characterize the pharmacokinetics of TCAP‐1 following different routes of administration may help determine the optimal delivery route and timing relative to stress onset.

Thus, the present study addressed two main gaps in knowledge. First, in the existing literature, the effects of TCAP‐1 had generally only been examined in acute assays that screen for anxiolytic activity, but do not depend on the animal being stressed. Therefore, we used a stressor to precipitate increases in CORT and investigated whether this physiological response could be blunted with TCAP‐1. Second, much of this previous TCAP‐1 literature used central (i.e., i.c.v.) administration to deliver TCAP‐1 prior to assessing anxiolytic activity. While we and others had published prior work showing that s.c. administration of TCAP‐1 was effective in certain models, it had not been performed in the context of stress. Therefore, we investigated whether systemic routes of administration were effective against induced stress, and investigated whether an intranasal formulation, which would be even easier to administer clinically, was effective.

In summary, this study found that subcutaneous and intranasal administration of TCAP‐1 significantly attenuated the development and expression of the physiological manifestation of stress in mice and rats. Future work characterizing the pretreatment timing dependencies of TCAP‐1 and other non‐invasive routes of delivery will be useful in further advancing TCAP‐1 as a clinically relevant anxiolytic.
