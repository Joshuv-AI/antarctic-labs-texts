Affinity improvement of specific scFvs interacting with the Neisseria meningitidis factor H-binding protein: a molecular dynamics simulation approach and experimental study
Authors: Bijan Ghazanfari, Zahra Ebrahimi, Fatemeh Yarian, Arezu Lari, Ali Ghanbariasad, Bahram Kazemi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundIn silico approaches are becoming increasingly important for enhancing the affinity of antibodies and antibody fragments, including single-chain variable fragments (scFvs). In this study, we present an in silico affinity-maturation workflow designed to improve the affinity of an scFv directed against Neisseria meningitidis factor H-binding protein (fHbp) by integrating in silico maturation, molecular dynamics (MD) simulations, and experimental validation. Approximately 300 single-point substitutions were designed at scFv residues predicted to interact with fHbp, and the resulting V3 (R99D) scFv–fHbp complexes were analyzed through all-atom MD simulations. Guided by bioinformatic analyses, the top V3 (R99D) variant was cloned and expressed, and its binding activity was validated by ELISA.ResultsMD analyses identified five variants, Y35H, L31R, R99D, R99H, and A100K, that exhibited markedly enhanced complex stability. In all variants, the scFv secondary structure remained intact, whereas fHbp showed shrinkage relative to the scFv in the V3 (R99D)–fHbp complexes during the simulations. Hydrogen-bonding patterns and inter-residue distances further supported the improved affinity of the variants relative to the native form. The R99D variant showed the highest binding affinity, with an experimentally measured value of 67.93 × 10⁹ M⁻¹.ConclusionCollectively, these findings underscore affinity maturation as a powerful strategy for engineering high-affinity antibody fragments.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12896-026-01204-3.Keywords: N. meningitidis, Affinity improvement, Factor H binding protein, Single-chain variable fragment, Molecular dynamics simulation

Background

Rational improvement of the binding affinity of protein–protein interactions (PPIs), including antigen–antibody interactions, remains a major goal in protein engineering and in the development of high-affinity antibodies for the diagnosis and treatment of various diseases. Accordingly, many studies have focused on enhancing PPIs through random or site-directed mutagenesis [1–3]. Rational improvement of the binding affinity of protein–protein interactions (PPIs), including antigen–antibody interactions, remains a major goal in protein engineering and in the development of high-affinity antibodies for the diagnosis and treatment of various diseases. Accordingly, many studies have focused on enhancing PPIs through random or site-directed mutagenesis [4].

Advances in antibody technology, together with growing knowledge of antibody–target interactions, have made it possible to design and optimize engineered antibodies more effectively. The physicochemical properties of specific residues, along with energy scores, are important criteria in antibody engineering. Over the past decade, several computer-based design strategies have been developed to improve antibody affinity with far greater speed and accuracy [5]. These approaches may provide novel routes for designing therapeutic antibodies against numerous pathogens that have resisted traditional vaccine development, including bacteria and highly antigenically variable viruses such as HIV, HCV, influenza, and Ebola virus [6].

Factor H-binding protein (fHbp), also known as GNA1870, plays an important role in the pathogenesis of Neisseria meningitidis and in vaccine design [7]. Because it mediates primary interaction with human factor H (fH), thereby downregulating the alternative complement pathway and facilitating innate immune evasion, fHbp is essential for the survival of N. meningitidis in the human host [8]. It is therefore a valuable therapeutic target for bactericidal antibodies. Since the interaction with human factor H occurs on an extracellular and structurally accessible surface, fHbp represents an attractive target for antibody-based recognition and engineering. Consequently, a detailed characterization of the physicochemical determinants of this protein–protein recognition, including interface residues, hydrogen bonding, steric complementarity, and electrostatic interactions, is essential for the rational design of higher-affinity binders.

In our previous study, a novel single-chain variable fragment (scFv) antibody against N. meningitidis fHbp was selected and developed by ribosome display [9–11]. In the present study, we employed a structure-based computational design strategy combined with a ligand-based affinity-improvement approach to enhance the affinity of the anti-fHbp scFv. To this end, molecular docking was used to identify key scFv residues involved in antigen–scFv interactions. To design and subsequently select the best mutants, single-point substitutions and descriptor-based screening were applied. Using all-atom MD simulation and the umbrella sampling method, the binding free energy of five variants and the extent of affinity improvement were calculated. The selected antibody was recombinantly produced, and its binding affinity was confirmed in vitro.

Methods

Model generation and docking

Given the pivotal role of complementarity-determining regions (CDRs) in mediating antigen recognition and binding in antibody structures, their precise identification and characterization formed the foundation of the present investigation. Defining these hypervariable loops in the antibody framework, together with their specific contributions to the initial antigen–antibody complex, represented the first essential step in this study. The parental scFv sequence used here is publicly available in GenBank under accession number LC191294. Accordingly, the PIGS server (https://bio.tools/pigs) was used to generate a three-dimensional wild-type scFv model [10, 12]. PIGSpro is a robust, biophysically grounded antibody-structure prediction platform that uses canonical conformations to model CDR loops with high precision. By integrating an updated template library, refined predictions of VH–VL domain orientation, and improved handling of non-canonical conformations, it remains a reliable benchmark in the era of rapidly advancing deep-learning methodologies. Its transparency and reproducibility make it valuable for therapeutic antibody design, humanization, and paratope optimization, while its open-access format supports broad use in academic research. For the antigen model, the fHbp crystal structure (PDB code: 2KC0, 2.86 Å) was used. Critical residues involved in the interaction between scFv and fHbp were identified by docking with the ClusPro server (https://cluspro.bu.edu) [13].

Following this, a structure-guided mutation-design strategy was applied to select candidate mutations. Approximately 300 single-point mutants were evaluated according to several criteria: (i) spatial proximity to the interface, (ii) potential for hydrogen-bond formation, (iii) improved electrostatic complementarity, and (iv) steric compatibility. The resulting three-dimensional models of the variants were docked with fHbp. Altogether, a comprehensive set of 300 molecular docking runs was conducted to systematically explore the binding interactions. The docking energy scores were used for primary screening. This process led to the prioritization of five variants, Y35H, L31R, R99D, R99H, and A100K, for detailed MD simulation and experimental validation. A concise summary of the mutation-design workflow, screening criteria, and progression from approximately 300 single-point mutants to the five selected variants is provided in Supplementary Table S1.

The selection of these variants was driven by computational analysis of the scFv–fHbp interface. In particular, R99D was identified as a promising candidate because it could introduce a beneficial charge reversal at the binding site, thereby enhancing electrostatic complementarity with the antigen. The selected variants were subsequently evaluated in terms of root mean square deviation (RMSD), radius of gyration (Rg), and root mean square fluctuation (RMSF). All visualizations were generated using Chimera. In addition, the LIGPLOT program was used to identify residues involved in ligand interactions [14].

MD simulations

All-atom molecular dynamics (MD) simulations were performed using the GROMACS software package (version 4.5.3) and the GROMOS96 43a1 force field [15] to systematically investigate the structural dynamics, conformational stability, and key intermolecular interactions between the engineered single-chain variable fragment (scFv) mutants and factor H-binding protein (fHbp) within the resulting antigen–antibody complexes. The GROMOS96 43a1 force field was selected because of its well-documented reliability in simulating protein–protein interfaces, particularly in systems involving flexible loops, side-chain reorientation, and long-range electrostatic contributions that are critical for binding [15].

In addition to geometric complementarity, the evaluation of these variants explicitly considered electrostatic compatibility, because charged substitutions at the binding interface were expected to exert a substantial influence on association affinity. To realistically represent the solvent environment, the simple point-charge (SPC) water model was used to explicitly model water molecules [16]. This three-site rigid water model offers a favorable balance between computational efficiency and accuracy in reproducing the key thermodynamic and structural properties of bulk water under ambient and physiological conditions. The initial configurations were obtained directly from the Protein Data Bank (PDB) structures of the native scFv–fHbp complex, as well as from each mutant scFv complexed with fHbp. Prior to simulation, these structures underwent standard preparation steps, including the addition of missing hydrogen atoms, assignment of appropriate protonation states at neutral pH, and verification of bond lengths and stereochemistry. Each complex was then centered in a rhombic dodecahedral periodic simulation box with a minimum distance of 2.0 nm between any protein atom and the box boundary, thereby ensuring sufficient solvent padding to minimize periodic boundary artifacts and permit full conformational sampling over the course of the trajectory. The box was subsequently filled with pre-equilibrated SPC water molecules [16], with any waters overlapping protein atoms removed. The net charge of each solvated system was neutralized by adding the appropriate number of Na⁺ and Cl⁻ counterions. To further mimic physiological conditions, additional Na⁺ and Cl⁻ ions were introduced to achieve a final ionic strength of 150 mM, which is representative of the ionic environment typically encountered in serum or extracellular fluid, where antibody–antigen recognition occurs in vivo. Proper ionic strength is essential for accurately capturing electrostatic screening, solvation effects, and the overall stability of the protein complex during the MD trajectory.

Expression of the V3 (R99D) scFv in E. coli

The mutant scFv gene (V3) was synthesized into the pET28a plasmid (Bioneer, Korea), and the expression vector was subsequently transformed into Escherichia coli BL21 (DE3). The amino acid sequence of the V3 (R99D) scFv is provided in Supplementary File S1.

Single colonies were cultured in 50 mL of LB broth containing 30 µg/mL kanamycin at 37 °C and 200 rpm until the OD600 reached 0.8. Recombinant protein expression was then induced with 1 mM IPTG (Merck, Germany). The His-tagged protein was analyzed by SDS–PAGE on 12% polyacrylamide gels, followed by Coomassie Brilliant Blue R-250 staining. For Western blotting, the protein was transferred to nitrocellulose membranes (Whatman, UK) and probed with an HRP-labeled anti-His-tag monoclonal antibody (Abcam, UK) at a 1:1000 dilution. Immunoreactivity was visualized using DAB/H₂O₂ substrate solution (Roche, Germany).

Purification of the V3 (R99D) scFv

The recombinant V3 (R99D) scFv was purified using Ni²⁺-NTA agarose resin according to the kit protocol (Novagen, USA). First, the cell pellets were suspended in 5 mL of denaturing buffer (6 M urea, 20 mM NaH₂PO₄, and 500 mM NaCl, pH 8.0), lysed by sonication on ice, and centrifuged at 12,000 × g for 20 min. The resulting supernatant was then loaded onto the resin, washed three times with 15 mL of wash buffer containing an imidazole gradient (10, 20, and 30 mM), and eluted with 1 mL of elution buffer containing 500 mM imidazole. Finally, the eluted protein was dialyzed against PBS at room temperature for five hours.

Affinity analysis of selected scFv

The affinity constant (Kaff) of the selected scFv for the recombinant antigen fHbp was determined as described by Beatty et al. [17]. An ELISA microplate was coated overnight at 4 °C with S-tagged fHbp antigen at concentrations of 2, 1, 0.5, and 0.25 µg/mL in PBS (pH 7.2). Purified scFv antibodies at concentrations of 5, 2.5, 1.25, and 0.625 µg/mL were applied to the corresponding antigen concentrations. All ELISA experiments were performed with three independent biological replicates, defined as separate protein expression and purification batches followed by independent ELISA runs, with each condition tested in technical triplicate. Next, a horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated anti-His-tag antibody (Abcam, UK) at a 1:10,000 dilution was used as the secondary antibody. The mixture was incubated for 1 h at room temperature. The HRP substrate 3,3′,5,5′-tetramethylbenzidine (TMB) was then added, and the reaction was allowed to proceed for 10 min. The reaction was terminated by adding 50 µL of 2 N H₂SO₄, and the absorbance was measured at 450 nm. Data are presented as the mean ± standard deviation (SD) of the biological replicates. The apparent affinity constant (Kaff) was calculated using the Beatty equation: Kaff = (n − 1)/2(n [Ab′] − [Ab]), where n = [Ag]/[Ag′]. No additional statistical tests for significance between the native and V3 (R99D) scFv were performed in this study, because the primary aim was to quantify the affinity improvement of the R99D variant relative to the previously reported native scFv value [9].

Results

The initial molecular docking analysis of the interaction between the native antibody and the antigen identified the key amino acid residues of the antibody that contribute to antigen binding. Table 1 lists the residues involved in scFv CDR binding to fHbp through hydrogen-bonding and hydrophobic interactions. Based on this interaction profile, single-point mutations were introduced at selected positions to evaluate their individual contributions to binding affinity. The mutations were designed either to strengthen or to disrupt specific interface features, including hydrogen bonding, electrostatic interactions, and steric fit. After evaluating approximately 300 docking outcomes, five scFv variants were selected for detailed analysis: Variant 0, native (wild-type) scFv, used as the reference control; Variant 1 (Y35H), a tyrosine-to-histidine substitution designed to assess changes in aromaticity and hydrogen-bonding potential; Variant 2 (L31R), a leucine-to-arginine substitution used to assess the effect of introducing a positive charge at the interface in CDR1-VH; Variant 3 (R99D), an arginine-to-aspartate substitution in FR3-VH intended to reverse the charge and probe the role of ionic interactions; Variant 4 (R99H), an arginine-to-histidine substitution at the same position to preserve charge potential while introducing imidazole functionality; and Variant 5 (A100K), an alanine-to-lysine substitution in CDR3-VH designed to introduce a flexible positive side chain and explore extended electrostatic reach. All variants, together with the native control, underwent all-atom molecular dynamics simulations under identical conditions to compare conformational behavior, interface stability, and persistent interaction patterns relative to the wild-type complex. Notably, all selected variants were located in the antibody VH chain.

Energy computation of the model

Table 2 presents the ClusPro energy scores for the native form and the best-designed mutants. The ClusPro energy score represents the interaction energy between two proteins and is expressed as E = Erep + Eattr + Eelec + EDARS, where these terms correspond to the repulsive and attractive components of van der Waals interactions, electrostatic energy, and DARS energy, respectively. The DARS term is a pairwise, structure-based potential constructed from decoys as the reference state.

For clarity, V0 denotes the native scFv, whereas V1 through V5 denote Y35H, L31R, R99D, R99H, and A100K, respectively. To assess stability during the simulations, RMSD, Rg, and RMSF were calculated for fHbp, the scFv, the mutant variants, and their complexes. Figure 1 shows the RMSD values of the variants over time; faster stabilization and lower oscillation indicate more rapid attainment of structural stability. Stable, low RMSD profiles across all components indicate preserved structural integrity and limited unfolding or dissociation over the simulation timescale for all variants. V3 exhibited the lowest RMSD fluctuation, suggesting superior structural consistency within the fHbp–scFv complex, and reached a plateau after 9 ns. In contrast, V1 and V4 showed the largest RMSD fluctuations, indicating that their mutations (Y35H and R99H, respectively) were less stable and required more time to equilibrate.

Secondary structure changes

DSSP analysis was performed on all protein entries in the PDB to identify secondary structural changes in the variants, because secondary structure is critical for protein function and its disruption can lead to structural irregularities. Such variations often involve interconversion between turns and random coils. Owing to its high mobility, the N-terminus typically shows the greatest secondary structure variability. Figure 3 demonstrates that the designed variants exhibited no significant secondary structural changes, further supporting their stability.

Discussion

The findings of the present study demonstrate the effectiveness of an integrated computational-experimental strategy for substantially enhancing the antigen-binding capacity of a single-chain variable fragment (scFv) directed against factor H-binding protein (fHbp), a critical surface-exposed virulence determinant that enables Neisseria meningitidis to evade host complement-mediated immunity. This approach is consistent with prior reports in which in silico-guided affinity maturation has proved instrumental in optimizing antibody-like binders. For example, Ebrahimi et al. used homology modeling and molecular dynamics to redesign nanobodies targeting the PLGF receptor; they identified key interfacial residues, generated mutants, docked them to select low-energy poses, and ultimately validated four high-affinity candidates through simulation [18]. Similarly, Ghaffari et al. rationally engineered affibody variants against HER2 by exploiting electrostatic complementarity, with MD simulations confirming superior receptor engagement for one charge-introduced mutant over another [19]. Payandeh et al. applied docking and dynamics to mutate ofatumumab residues involved in CD20 binding, yielding variants with markedly improved predicted affinity [20]. Comparable workflows have also been used to refine bevacizumab for VEGF binding in ovarian cancer models [21] and to generate stabilized scFv libraries against dengue envelope epitopes [22]. More recently, such principles have been extended to scFv mutants against small molecules such as amantadine, producing up to a 3.9-fold increase in sensitivity through targeted Gly-to-Phe substitution at a key site [23].

In the present investigation, the parental scFv model was reliably constructed using PIGSpro (http://biocomputing.it/pigspro), which leverages an expanded template repository together with refined loop and orientation optimization to produce biophysically interpretable structures that remain competitive with modern deep-learning approaches [10, 12]. Subsequent ClusPro docking [13] delineated critical interface contacts, thereby enabling the semi-rational design of approximately 300 point mutants.

Binding-energy evaluation provided additional insight into affinity improvement. The observed reduction in binding energy for the optimized variant reflects strengthening of key intermolecular interactions and is consistent with the enhanced complex stability observed in our MD simulations. Notably, recent computational frameworks, such as those discussed by Biswas et al. [24], underscore the value of accurate binding-energy estimation as a reliable predictor of protein-protein interaction stability and functional integrity. By aligning our analyses with such methodologies, we confirmed that the selected point substitution effectively anchored the scFv within the fHbp binding pocket, thereby accounting for the observed increase in affinity.

Rigorous filtering through all-atom MD simulations ultimately identified the R99D substitution in VH framework region 3 as the optimal variant. This arginine-to-aspartate exchange, positioned adjacent to CDR-H3, introduced a localized charge reversal that conferred exceptional conformational stability, as evidenced by minimal RMSD and Rg fluctuations, reduced per-residue flexibility (RMSF) at interface residues, and sustained intramolecular hydrogen-bonding throughout the extended trajectories. Comparative LIGPLOT analyses revealed reorganized polar and hydrophobic interaction networks that enhanced electrostatic steering and interfacial complementarity without perturbing the global fold architecture [25, 26].

Experimental validation further corroborated the computational findings. The R99D variant yielded an ELISA-determined affinity constant (K_aff) of 67.93 × 10⁹ M⁻¹, representing a marked improvement over the native scFv (approximately 8.65 × 10⁹ M⁻¹) [9]. It should be emphasized that docking scores, MD-derived stability descriptors, and experimentally measured affinity are related but not identical metrics. In the present study, ClusPro docking was used primarily as an initial screening tool to identify favorable interface geometry and electrostatic complementarity among candidate variants. Accordingly, docking scores were interpreted as qualitative ranking criteria rather than direct quantitative predictors of binding affinity. Likewise, MD-derived parameters such as RMSD, Rg, RMSF, DSSP, and hydrogen-bond profiles were used to assess structural stability, compactness, and preservation of the binding interface during simulation, but these metrics do not directly measure binding free energy or equilibrium affinity constants. Within this framework, the R99D variant showed consistent behavior across computational and experimental analyses: it ranked favorably in docking-based screening, exhibited lower structural fluctuations and a more stable interface during MD simulations, and ultimately demonstrated improved apparent affinity in the ELISA assay. Nevertheless, this agreement should be interpreted qualitatively rather than as a strict quantitative correlation between docking score, MD stability metrics, and experimental affinity. Docking is limited by simplified scoring functions and incomplete treatment of conformational flexibility, solvation, and entropy, whereas MD stability descriptors reflect dynamic behavior rather than thermodynamic binding strength. In addition, the ELISA-based value reported here represents an apparent affinity measurement for the selected variant rather than a full thermodynamic comparison of all designed mutants.

Although the GROMOS96 43a1 force field, combined with the SPC water model, adequately described short- to intermediate-timescale interface behavior in this system, future studies may benefit from more contemporary parameter sets, such as CHARMM36m or AMBER ff19SB, coupled with enhanced-sampling protocols, such as Gaussian accelerated MD, to better capture infrequent conformational states and entropic contributions.

Likewise, although ClusPro enabled efficient rigid-body sampling, integration with state-of-the-art AI-augmented docking pipelines or AlphaFold3-generated conformational ensembles could further improve pose discrimination and epitope specificity in subsequent optimization rounds. Overall, this work delineates a streamlined and mechanistically coherent pathway for scFv affinity enhancement, one that seamlessly bridges homology-based modeling, physics-based virtual screening, and empirical validation, and thus offers a compelling framework for accelerating the development of potent antibody fragments against high-priority bacterial pathogens.

Although the present study primarily relied on molecular dynamics-based stability descriptors to assess model robustness, additional model-quality validation using Ramachandran plot analysis, ERRAT, ProSA, or Verify3D would provide further confidence in the structural reliability of the designed variants and should be considered in future work. Future studies may also benefit from complementary biophysical validation, such as isothermal titration calorimetry or related calorimetric approaches, to quantify the thermodynamic parameters underlying the scFv–fHbp interaction.

Conclusion

This study combined in silico and experimental approaches to improve the affinity of an scFv antibody for fHbp. Residues Tyr35, Leu31, Arg99, and Ala100 were identified as key contributors to the interaction. The Arg99Asp mutation produced the greatest improvement in binding score, with no significant changes observed in the MD analysis. These results suggest that ligand-based affinity enhancement is a promising strategy for designing high-affinity antibody fragments.
