Research on geological hazard susceptibility evaluation using an information value-modified entropy weight combined model
Authors: Maogang Qin, Bo Chen, Jun Zhu, Jianquan Lv, Mingzhi Lin, Mingkun Lin, Yong Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Research on geological hazard susceptibility evaluation can effectively prevent threats to people’s lives and property from geological disasters. This paper selects eight influencing factors, namely, slope, altitude, distance to river, distance to road, precipitation, distance to fault, engineering geology, and the normalized difference vegetation index (NDVI), taking Qionghai city as the study area. By employing the modified entropy weight method, an information value-modified entropy weight combined model for geological hazard susceptibility evaluation is proposed. Susceptibility evaluations were conducted via the entropy weight method, modified entropy weight method, information value method, and combined model. The study area in Qionghai city was divided into four susceptibility zones: high, moderate, low, and very low. A comparative analysis of the results from the four methods revealed that the combined model yielded results that were most consistent with the actual situation. ROC curve analysis indicated that the combined model (AUC = 0.888) outperformed the entropy weight method (0.350), modified entropy weight method (0.830), and information value method (0.797) models, with significantly improved accuracy. Research has demonstrated that the modified entropy weight method overcomes the limitation of insufficient coverage of influencing factors during calculation. By constructing the information value-modified entropy weight combined zonation model, the problem of unsatisfactory evaluation performance when a single model is used is resolved. Simultaneously, key factors promoting and inhibiting the formation of geological hazard susceptibility zonation in the study region were identified. The results can provide a basis for government efforts in geological hazard prevention and control, as well as site selection and planning for major engineering projects.

Introduction

Geological hazards are natural geological phenomena. The fundamental reason they become disasters is our inability to accurately predict their occurrence time, location, and characteristics. According to data from the National Bureau of Statistics, China experienced 54,280 geological hazards from 2015 to 2022, resulting in 2488 casualties and direct economic losses of 23.61 billion yuan. Over the past 20 years, global geological hazard events accounted for only 9% of all disasters but caused 59% of all disaster-related deaths, making them the most lethal type of disaster by far1. Consequently, it is necessary to conduct preliminary assessments and early warnings for geological hazards and rationally plan development areas to prevent their occurrence at the source.

Geological hazard susceptibility evaluation is based on fundamental geological environmental conditions and uses static factors of geological hazards as references to predict the probability of geological hazard occurrence within a specific area2. This approach is particularly important for the early proactive identification of geological hazard risks and for disaster prevention and mitigation. It has always been a research focus for experts and scholars. Over the past few decades, numerous prediction models have been established to map susceptibility zonation. The main predictive models include empirical models, such as the fuzzy logic model3 and the analytic hierarchy process4,5; statistical analysis models, such as weights of evidence6, the information value method7, and the certainty factor method8; and machine learning models, such as artificial neural networks9, support vector machines10, random forests11,12, logistic regression13, and the XGBoost model14. The above are all single evaluation methods. Owing to the various shortcomings of single evaluation methods, it is difficult to meet accuracy requirements. For example, empirical models are highly subjective and struggle to reflect objective geological environmental conditions; statistical analysis models suffer from incomplete consideration of influencing factors; and machine learning models have unexplainable “black box” issues. Previous researchers have conducted related studies on model combinations. Li et al. used an information value logistic regression model for landslide susceptibility evaluation15. The results from Zheng et al. show that the entropy index–extreme learning machine hybrid model outperforms single statistical models16, while Xiao et al. proposed integrating Optuna optimization with a random forest model to assess landslide susceptibility in Yunnan17. These previous studies provide a solid foundation for the current research.

The information value method compares the frequency of geological hazard occurrence under specific factors within a particular evaluation unit with the regional frequency of geological hazard occurrence18,19, but it fails to consider the impact of coupling relationships between influencing factors on geological hazard susceptibility. The entropy weight method is an objective evaluation method that considers the relationships among influencing factors20,21, but it does not consider the influence of geological hazard site occurrence. This study improves the entropy weight method by optimizing the defect of insufficient consideration of influencing factors during calculation. Then, an information value-entropy weight combined geological hazard susceptibility zonation model is established. This model effectively compensates for the defects of both methods. It is an evaluation model that relies entirely on natural environmental conditions for calculation and division, is not affected by subjective factors, and is interpretable. When this model is applied to the susceptibility zonation of geological hazards in Qionghai city, the results show that this model has higher accuracy than the single information value method or entropy weight method does, indicating the reasonable construction of this model. Its results can better prevent threats to people’s lives and property from geological hazards.

Study area and data sources

Study area

Qionghai city is located in the eastern part of Hainan Island, China, Asia (Fig. 1a–b). It is the permanent site of the Boao Forum for Asia and the location of the Lecheng International Medical Tourism Pilot Zone, with a total area of 1710 km2. The terrain slopes step-like from west to east, with an altitude ranging from 1 to 1264 m and an average altitude of 66 m (Fig. 1c). It borders the South China Sea to the east. Surface water resources are abundant, with an annual total water resource of 3.96 billion m3, including 8 medium-sized reservoirs. The city’s total forest area is 1231.23 km2, with a coverage rate of 72%. It has a tropical monsoon and humid marine climate, with an average annual temperature of 25.08 °C and abundant rainfall. The average annual precipitation is 2077.62 mm, which is distributed mainly in the southwestern part of the study area. Rainfall is concentrated from May to October, often with heavy rain and rainstorms, accounting for approximately 77% of the annual total.

Qionghai city is situated in the eastern part of the South China Fold System and features relatively well-developed fault structures. The main distributions are the Changjiang-Qionghai Fault Zone (F1), Wenchang Fault Zone (F2), Longbo-Hanlin Fault Zone (F3), Jiapen-Longjiang Fault Zone (F4), and Dongling Fault Zone (F5) (Fig. 1d). The surface is covered mainly by Quaternary loose sedimentary soils and bedrock weathered residual soils. The Quaternary loose sedimentary soils are mainly distributed in coastal sandy areas, first terraces, and floodplains of rivers such as the Wanquan River, Jiujiang River, and Tayang River, showing a zonal distribution. Bedrock weathered residual soil is the main soil type in the study area and is widely distributed, primarily consisting of sandy clay and gray‒brown to earthy yellow, with a general thickness of 2–25 m. The particle composition is mainly feldspar and quartz, the soil is hard-plastic, and the structure is slightly dense.

According to the results of the 1:50,000 detailed geological hazard survey, 54 geological hazard sites were identified in Qionghai city (Fig. 1d), all of which are potential collapse sites and are distributed mainly in the central and southwestern parts of the study area. The slopes are classified by type: 16 are highway slopes, 22 are cut slopes for construction, 15 are riverbank slopes, and 1 is coastal slope. In terms of scale, 53 are small, and 1 is large, cumulatively threatening 729 people and property worth 36.47 million yuan. The rainy season from May to October is the high-incidence period for geological hazards, with 50 sites (90.9%), especially during extreme weather events such as typhoons and continuous heavy rainfall, which are prone to triggering geological hazards. Human activities are the main cause of geological hazards, which are manifested mainly as cut slopes for construction, mountain road construction, and coastal development and construction, resulting in 42 sites (76%).

Research methods

The research method used in this study includes 4 key steps (Fig. 2): (1) Establishing a database: Determining geological hazard sites through field surveys and collecting data on factors that may affect geological hazard occurrence. (2) Selecting appropriate influencing factors: Pearson correlation analysis of the influencing factors in the database was used, highly correlated factors were removed, and 8 suitable influencing factors were screened out. (3) Establishing evaluation models: This study first modifies the entropy weight method and then combines it with the information value method to establish the information value-modified entropy weight combined model. Susceptibility zonation maps are subsequently calculated for the entropy weight method, modified entropy weight method, information value method, and combined model. (4) The prediction accuracies of the four models (entropy weight method, modified entropy weight method, information value method, and combined model) are compared via ROC curves and performance indicators.

Selection of influencing factors

Screening key factors influencing geological hazard susceptibility and constructing a geological hazard evaluation index system are crucial steps in conducting geological hazard susceptibility assessments25. Various factors influence and control the degree of geological hazard susceptibility, including topography, stratigraphic lithology, hydrogeology, engineering geology, rainfall, and human activities. Zhao et al. used 14 influencing factors, such as elevation, slope, landform, and aspect, to conduct landslide susceptibility evaluations via information values and machine learning ensemble models26. Liang et al. used 12 influencing factors, such as precipitation, maximum height difference, and altitude, for shallow landslide susceptibility evaluation research27. This shows that the influencing factors in various studies are selected on the basis of the geological and environmental characteristics of the study area28. On the basis of a comprehensive analysis of the geological environmental characteristics of the study area and considering evaluation requirements, economic feasibility, and application reliability as prerequisites, this paper selects eight factors—slope, altitude, distance to river, distance to road, precipitation, distance to fault, engineering geology, and the NDVI—to establish the evaluation index system. The Pearson correlation coefficient (r) was calculated. When | r | < 0.3, the correlation between two variables is very weak and can be considered uncorrelated29. The Pearson coefficients for the selected influencing factors in this study range from − 0.26 to 0.18 (Fig. 3), all below 0.3, indicating a very weak correlation and confirming the reasonableness of the selected influencing factors.

Analysis and classification of influencing factors

Slope

Slope is a key topographic factor influencing the occurrence of geological hazards, as its variation directly controls the stress distribution and stability conditions of the slope body. Generally, as the slope angle increases, the gravitational component acting along the slope on the surficial rock and soil mass increases. Under external forces such as rainfall, earthquakes, or human disturbance, the potential risk of geological hazards such as landslides and collapses rises significantly30. In this study, the slope within the study area was classified into five grades using the natural breaks method: 0–4.2988°, 4.2988–9.5529°, 9.5529–16.9565°, 16.9565–26.5094°, and 26.5094–60.9°. Statistical results indicate that geological hazard sites are predominantly distributed within the 9.5529–16.9565° interval, totaling 18 sites (33% of the total). This is followed by the 4.2988–9.5529° interval with 15 sites (27% of the total). The high and steep slope segment of 26.5094–60.9° contains the fewest hazard sites, only 5 (Fig. 4a). It can be observed that the slope range of 4.2988–16.9565° is a high-incidence area for geological hazards, which also coincides with the most concentrated area of human engineering activities. Residents often excavate slope toes and construct buildings on slopes, disrupting the original equilibrium state of the slope. This leads to a redistribution of slope stress, concentrating it at the toe or weak structural planes, thereby significantly reducing slope stability and triggering hazards such as collapses31. In contrast, high and steep slopes, experiencing less human disturbance, have relatively lower trigger frequencies for hazards despite their inherently lower natural stability32.

Altitude

Altitude, as a key indicator characterizing landform morphology and topographic patterns, profoundly influences regional geological structure, climatic features, and human activity patterns. Consequently, it leads to significant differences in the spatiotemporal distribution and developmental mechanisms of geological hazards33. Based on the natural breaks method, this study classified the altitude of the study area into five grades: 1–58 m, 58–143 m, 143–276 m, 276–543 m, and 543–1254 m. Statistical results show that geological hazard sites are highly concentrated in low-altitude areas. The 1–58 m interval contains the highest number of hazard sites, reaching 36 (66% of the total). No hazard sites were recorded in areas above 276 m. Areas below 143 m collectively contain 49 hazard sites, representing a high proportion of 90% (Fig. 4b). This sufficiently demonstrates that the development of geological hazards in the study area exhibits a distinct characteristic of clustering at low altitudes.

Low altitude areas typically feature relatively gentle terrain and concentrated resources, making them zones of the most frequent human engineering activities. Frequent engineering activities such as slope cutting for construction, road excavation, and coastal zone development strongly alter the original slope morphology and stress state, disrupting the natural equilibrium of rock and soil masses. This significantly increases the risk of geological hazards such as landslides and collapses34. Therefore, the high incidence of geological hazards in low altitude areas is essentially the result of the combined effects of natural topographic conditions and intense human activities.

Distance to river

The hydrodynamic actions of rivers (such as scouring, erosion, and undercutting) are among the key external forces affecting bank slope stability. Continuous lateral erosion gradually creates free faces at the slope toe, leading to a reduction in supporting force, stress redistribution within the slope body, and disruption of its natural equilibrium, thereby inducing geological hazards like collapses or landslides. This process is particularly pronounced during high-flow periods due to increased discharge and enhanced water flow impact35,36. The study area is rich in surface water resources with numerous rivers, primarily including the Wanquan River, Jialang River, Tayang River, and Jiuqu River. Among them, the Wanquan River, as the main river in the study area, has a total length of approximately 156.6 km, a drainage area of 3693 km2, a total drop of 523 m, and an average annual discharge of 172 m3/s. Its upstream divides into north and south branches. The south branch is mainly the Chengpo River, originating from the Nanling range near Linbei Village in Wuzhi Mountain. The north branch is mainly the Dabian River, originating from Fengmenling in the southeastern part of the Limuling range. The two tributaries converge at Hekouzui and flow from west to east into the sea37. Influenced by the Coriolis effect, the right bank of the upper Wanquan River experiences stronger erosion with well-developed gullies, while the left bank is dominated by deposition, accumulating substantial medium to coarse sand materials. Along the bank section near Longjiang Town, the slope height ranges from 3 to 15 m with gradients of 40–55°, and the soil structure is relatively loose. This section hosts 12 geological hazard sites (21.8% of the total).

To further quantify the influence of river distance on hazard distribution, this study employed an equidistant buffer analysis, establishing buffer zones outward from the riverbank: 0–60 m, 60–120 m, 120–180 m, 180–240 m, 240–300 m, and > 300 m (Fig. 4c). Field investigations indicate that areas > 300 m are essentially no longer directly affected by fluvial processes. The spatial distribution of hazard sites shows the highest density within the 0–60 m buffer, containing 15 sites (27% of the total). This is primarily attributed to direct and frequent scouring and undercutting by river flow within this range. The next highest is the 240–300 m buffer with 10 hazard sites (18% of the total). This zone often corresponds to the slope crest or rear edge of the bank, where slope gradients change significantly and human activities (such as building construction, cultivation) further disturb the slope body, leading to a relatively concentrated occurrence of hazards. The 60–240 m interval represents the middle section of the bank slope, where the slope body is relatively intact with less human disturbance, resulting in a correspondingly sparse distribution of hazard sites.

Distance to road

Road construction is a significant indicator of regional socio-economic development. However, the construction process often involves large-scale slope excavation, which disrupts the primary structure and stress equilibrium of rock and soil masses, significantly reducing slope stability and subsequently inducing geological hazards such as landslides and collapses38. The study area is located in the economically developed eastern region of Hainan Province, characterized by a dense road network encompassing multi-level systems including expressways, national highways, provincial highways, and rural roads. Among these, newly constructed roads generally have relatively comprehensive slope engineering protection measures, commonly including slope protection, drainage systems, and retaining walls. Consequently, the overall stability of slopes along these roads is high, with only sporadic minor collapses occurring locally. In contrast, slopes along rural roads, especially in low mountainous and hilly areas, are often formed by excavation into planar slopes with irregular morphology and a lack of effective protection, resulting in poor stability and prominent hazard potential.

According to statistics, geological hazard sites directly triggered by road construction within the study area total 16 (29% of the total). To quantify the spatial extent of road influence, this study employed an equidistant buffer analysis, dividing the area surrounding roads into the following five distance zones: 0–50 m, 50–100 m, 100–150 m, 150–200 m, and > 200 m (Fig. 4d). Field surveys indicate that areas > 200 m are essentially not directly affected by road excavation activities. The spatial distribution of hazard sites is highly concentrated. The 0–50 m buffer zone contains the densest concentration, with 28 hazard sites (52% of the total). This is primarily attributed to the high intensity of slope excavation and the most severe disturbance to rock and soil masses within this range. The number of hazard sites decreases significantly within the 50–200 m range, reflecting the attenuation of engineering disturbance effects with increasing distance, coupled with reduced human activity intensity, allowing slopes to gradually approach a more natural stable state.

Precipitation

Precipitation is a critical factor triggering geological hazards, particularly during the rainy season. Persistent heavy rainfall and typhoon rainstorm events show a significant correlation with the frequency and scale of disasters39. The mechanisms primarily involve two aspects: firstly, rainwater infiltration softens rock and soil masses, increases water content and unit weight, thereby reducing their shear strength; secondly, water accumulation in residual and slope deposits leads to slope saturation, generating pore water pressure and lubricating potential failure surfaces (such as weathering interfaces), which substantially diminishes anti-sliding capacity and ultimately triggers hazards such as landslides and collapses40. The spatiotemporal distribution of geological hazards within the study area closely corresponds with precipitation patterns. Statistics indicate that approximately 90% of hazard events are directly or indirectly induced by rainfall. The occurrence period of hazards often coincides with or slightly lags behind heavy rainfall events, and both the number and scale of hazards show a significant positive correlation with rainfall duration and intensity.

To further quantify the relationship between the spatial distribution of precipitation and hazard occurrence, this study employed the equal interval method to classify the regional average annual precipitation into five categories: 1706–1800 mm, 1800–1900 mm, 1900–2000 mm, 2000–2100 mm, and 2100–2200 mm (Fig. 4e). The results reveal that the precipitation interval of 1800–1900 mm contains the highest concentration of hazard sites, totaling 25 (46% of the total). This interval is primarily located in the hilly terrain of the upper reaches of the Wanquan River, an area with intensive human engineering activities. Under the coupled effects of rainfall, topography, and anthropogenic disturbance, this region has become a zone of high susceptibility to geological hazards.

Distance to fault

Fault structures are one of the key geological factors controlling the development of geological hazards. Their influence is two aspects: on one hand, fault zones themselves provide potential sliding surfaces or cut boundaries for hazards like landslides and collapses, directly affecting their scale and morphology41. On the other hand, faulting activity leads to intense fracturing and crack development in slope rock and soil masses, exacerbating weathering and groundwater infiltration, thereby significantly reducing overall slope stability. In areas with active faults, the fractured rock structure not only weakens the static stability of slopes but may also trigger earthquakes due to fault activity, further increasing geological hazard risk42.

To quantify the impact of fault proximity on hazard distribution, this study used the equal interval method to establish buffer zones outward from fault lines, delineating six distance intervals: 0–200 m, 200–400 m, 400–600 m, 600–800 m, 800–1000 m, and > 1000 m (Fig. 4f). The results show that 18 hazard sites (33% of the total), are located within 1000 m of faults. These sites exhibit a scattered distribution across the distance intervals without a clear, distance-dependent concentration pattern. This spatial distribution suggests that, within the current study period, the controlling effect of fault structures on geological hazard development is relatively limited. However, this does not preclude potential fault activity in the past or future.

Engineering geology

Lithology, soil types, and structure are essential intrinsic conditions for geological hazard formation. Their properties directly influence slope stability and determine the type, scale, and spatial distribution pattern of hazards43,44. Generally, rock masses with hard lithology and intact structure possess high shear strength and exhibit relatively weaker hazard development. Conversely, rock masses with weak lithology and fragmented structure are more susceptible to weathering and groundwater influence, significantly reducing their resistance to erosion and sliding, making them more prone to hazards like landslides an45,46.

The engineering geological rock groups within the study area can be classified into eight types: harbor lagoon silt area, estuarine loose soft rock area, basalt weathered red soil area, marine terrace sandy area, alluvial diluvial wavy plain area, metamorphic rock hilly plain area, magmatic rock hilly area, and clastic rock hilly area (Fig. 4g). Among these, the clastic rock hilly area exhibits the most developed geological hazards, with 23 recorded sites accounting for 42% of the total. In contrast, various plain geomorphological units have very few or no hazard sites. This significant spatial heterogeneity clearly indicates that the spatial variation in rock/soil types plays an important controlling role in hazard distribution. Clastic rocks, often characterized by developed bedding, susceptibility to weathering, and strength anisotropy, are more likely to form a disaster-prone geological background within hilly terrain.

NDVI

Vegetation plays a vital role in maintaining slope stability and inhibiting soil erosion. Typically, areas with higher vegetation coverage exhibit a relatively lower degree of geological hazard development47. The mechanisms by which vegetation influences slope stability mainly include two aspects: firstly, hydrological regulation, where vegetation canopies intercept precipitation, reducing direct raindrop impact on slopes, and root water uptake combined with leaf transpiration lowers soil moisture content and groundwater levels; secondly, mechanical reinforcement, where plant roots bind soil particles, enhancing shear strength and erosion resistance. Roots penetrating into bedrock can also provide an anchoring effect similar to bolts, thereby improving overall slope stability48.

This study used the Normalized Difference Vegetation Index (NDVI) to quantify vegetation coverage. Based on the natural breaks method, the NDVI within the study area was classified into five intervals: 0.06–0.4919, 0.4919–0.6681, 0.6681–0.7926, 0.7926–0.8708, and 0.8708–0.9999 (Fig. 4f). However, the study area is located in a tropical monsoon climate zone with abundant rainfall, leading to rapid and dense vegetation growth. Notably, statistical results show that the highest NDVI interval (0.8708–0.9999), representing the densest vegetation cover, contains the largest number of hazard sites, with 16 hazard sites (29% of the total). This finding appears counterintuitive to conventional understanding. It suggests that in hot and humid tropical monsoon regions, dense vegetation may interact with factors such as topography, rainfall, soil saturation, and human activities, creating a more complex environment for geological hazard development. This reflects the uniqueness of hazard formation mechanisms in such regions.

The entropy weight method relies entirely on the interrelationships between influencing factors, forming a judgment matrix to calculate the weight of each evaluation factor. It is a common objective weight calculation method20,50. This study improves the entropy weight method by optimizing the first step of range standardization and the second step of calculating the proportion via formulas (2), (3), and (4). Using range standardization to process original values can make the minimum value equal to zero, preventing it from participating in the next calculation and evaluation process and thus ignoring the influence of that evaluation factor, which clearly does not meet evaluation needs. In this study, positive factors were improved by directly calculating the weight of each evaluation factor, whereas the weights of negative factors were arranged in reverse order. This modification ensures that every evaluation factor contributes to the calculation, leading to more reasonable results.

Evaluation results

The respective weight values were assigned to the corresponding raster maps. Using the ArcGIS Raster Calculator, the evaluation factors were overlaid51,52. The natural breaks method was subsequently used to divide the study area into four zones: the very low susceptibility zone, the low susceptibility zone, the moderate susceptibility zone, and the high susceptibility zone. For the entropy weight method model, the high susceptibility zone area is 445 km2, accounting for 26.6% of the total area, and is mainly distributed in the relatively flat plain area (Fig. 6a), which is inconsistent with the characteristics of geological hazard development in the study area. The high susceptibility zone of the modified entropy weight method model is distributed mainly around roads, rivers, and faults, but the southwestern hilly area prone to cut slopes for construction is classified as a low susceptibility zone, with an area of 544 km2, accounting for 32.6% (Fig. 6b), which differs significantly from the survey results. The high susceptibility zone of the information value method model is distributed mainly around roads and rivers and similarly classifies the southwestern hilly area prone to cut slope for construction as a low susceptibility zone (Fig. 6c). Moreover, the interactions between the influencing factors are not reflected. The high susceptibility zone of the combined model is mainly distributed in roads, rivers, faults, and the southwestern area, with an area of 217 km2, accounting for 13% (Fig. 6d), essentially covering the areas prone to geological hazards in the study area. The moderate susceptibility zone is distributed mainly around the high susceptibility zone and acts as a buffer. The low susceptibility zone is distributed mainly in the northwestern and central-eastern areas and is strongly affected by topography and vegetation. The very low susceptibility zone is distributed mainly in the northeast and central-south regions. This area has a gentle terrain, developed vegetation, and a good geological environment and lacks the necessary preconditions for geological hazard occurrence. The susceptibility zonation divided by the combined model conforms to the actual survey situation in the study area, indicating that the combined model proposed in this study has good application.

The entropy weight method model has the largest area in the moderate- and high-susceptibility zones. The modified entropy weight method model and the information value method model have the lowest areas. The combined model’s area increases slightly. The entropy weight method model has the smallest area in the very low- and low-susceptibility zones. The modified entropy weight method model and the information value method model have greater areas. The combined model area decreases somewhat (Fig. 7). This is because the combined model classifies more of the southwestern hilly area into moderate- and high-susceptibility zones, which conforms to the development pattern of geological hazards in the study area.

Model validation is a very important standard for measuring the accuracy of various statistical techniques in evaluating models53. This study uses ROC curves to validate the geological hazard susceptibility zones generated by the four models. The results are shown in Fig. 8 The area under the ROC curve (AUC) reflects the model’s performance. The closer the curve is to the left peak, the closer the AUC value is to 1, and the more accurate the model is24. The AUC value of the entropy weight method model is 0.350, that of the modified entropy weight method model is 0.830, that of the information value method model is 0.797, and that of the combined model is 0.888. This shows that the information value-modified entropy weight combined model has the highest accuracy, followed by the modified entropy weight method model and the information value method model, with the entropy weight method model having the lowest accuracy (Fig. 8). This finding indicates that, compared with single models, the performance of the information value-modified entropy weight combined model is significantly improved.

Discussion

The selection of influencing factors greatly affects the division of geological hazard susceptibility zones, which vary from place to place. The geological hazards in the study area are mainly small collapses, which are spatially distributed as points in residential areas, occur linearly along river channels, and occur mostly in the rainy season. Therefore, this study selected slope, altitude, distance to river, distance to road, precipitation, distance to fault, engineering geology, and the NDVI as influencing factors, considering the geological environmental characteristics of the study area and the factors that may affect the occurrence of geological hazards. In subsequent similar studies or project developments, suitable influencing factors can be selected on the basis of actual local conditions.The methods used to subdivide the influencing factors into evaluation factors differ. The natural breaks method was used for slope, altitude, and the NDVI because these three types of influencing factors have clustering characteristics. The natural breaks method can classify data with small interval changes, which is conducive to analysing the impact of similar characteristics on geological hazard occurrence. The equal interval method was used for distance to river, distance to road, and distance to fault because these three variables have linear characteristics; the closer they are to the influencing factor, the greater the probability of geological hazard occurrence. When the distance exceeds a certain value, the factor has no influence. Precipitation is the statistical average from 2000 to 2019 and is divided according to customary precipitation classification methods, i.e., equal value division. Engineering geology is divided according to the engineering properties of each area. The classification of these influencing factors considers their own attributes and possible influence ranges, fully reflecting that the classification range can finely reflect the development characteristics of geological hazards.This study improved the entropy weight method, ensuring that all evaluation factors participate in the calculation and overcoming the defect of item omission in the standardization of the entropy weight method. Additionally, this study proposes a calculation method for the combination of the information value method and the modified entropy weight method. This method combines the advantages of both methods, resulting in higher evaluation accuracy. The evaluation results of geological hazard susceptibility in Qionghai city indicate that the information value-modified entropy weight combined model has the highest accuracy, followed by the modified entropy weight method model and the information value method model, with the entropy weight method model having the lowest accuracy. This demonstrates that the information value-modified entropy weight combined model proposed in this study has the best effect and the highest accuracy and can be applied in practice.Figure 9 shows the differences between each evaluation factor and the mean value in the information value-modified entropy weight combined model. It can be observed that the following ranges act as positive factors promoting the formation of geological hazard susceptibility zonation: slope (9.5529–60.9°), elevation (58–276 m and 543–1254 m), distance to river (0–120 m and 240–300 m), distance to road (0–100 m), precipitation (1900–2217 mm), distance to fault (0–200 m and 600–800 m), the alluvial diluvial wavy plain area and the clastic rock hilly area, and NDVI (0.06–0.7926). Among these, the slope (26.5094–60.9°), elevation (143–276 m), distance to river (0–60 m), distance to road (0–50 m), precipitation (2000–2100 mm), and distance to fault (0–200 m) are the primary controlling factors for the formation of geological hazard susceptibility zonation. The remaining ranges act as negative factors inhibiting their formation, with the slope (0–4.2988°), distance to river (> 300 m), distance to road (> 200 m), distance to fault (> 800 m), and the marine terrace sandy area being the main inhibitory factors. This study effectively identifies the key factors influencing geological hazard occurrence, providing crucial support for geological hazard prevention and development planning in the study area.Figure 10 shows the standard error scatter plot for information value, weights determined by the modified entropy weight method, and the combined weights. The factors slope, elevation, distance to fault, precipitation, and engineering geology generally show minor differences. Twenty four data points (53.3% of the total) have values below the average. These smaller differences indicate consistent trends and high credibility of the evaluation results. Figure 11 presents the sum of the standard errors of the evaluation factors for the three models. Based on the mean value, these sums are divided into two parts: the blue area represents regions where the value is below the mean, indicating relatively minor differences, covering an area of 880 km2 (51.5% of the total area), primarily distributed in the central and northern parts of the study area. The three models show relatively consistent performance in these regions, indicating high credibility. In contrast, distance to river, distance to road, and NDVI generally exhibit larger differences. Twenty one data points (46.7% of the total) have values above the average. These larger differences mainly stem from the varying calculation results of the three models. the red area represents regions where the value is above the mean, indicating relatively large differences, covering an area of 830 km2 (48.5% of the total area), mainly distributed in the eastern part of the study area. These areas of divergent model performance precisely reflect aspects where the accuracy of the evaluation results can be further examined.

Conclusions and suggestions

This paper selects eight influencing factors: slope, altitude, distance to river, distance to road, precipitation, distance to fault, engineering geology, and NDVI. The weights (values) of the evaluation factors were calculated via the entropy weight method, modified entropy weight method, information value method, and information value-modified entropy weight combined model. Through factor overlay, the natural breaks method was finally used to divide the area into four zones: very low, low, moderate, and high susceptibility zones. The characteristics of the susceptibility zones divided by the four models were analysed.By improving the entropy weight method, this study overcomes the limitation of insufficient coverage of influencing factors during calculation and proposes an information value-modified entropy weight combined with a geological hazard susceptibility zonation model. This model effectively integrates the advantages of both and compensates for their respective defects. It is an evaluation model based entirely on natural environmental conditions for zonation calculations, eliminates the influence of human judgment, and possesses interpretability.The analysis of the four models for geological hazard susceptibility in Qionghai city reveals that the susceptibility zones divided by the information value-modified entropy weight combined model are highly consistent with the actual survey results. According to the ROC curve analysis, the AUC value of the combined model is 0.888, which is higher than those of the other three single models, indicating that the information value-modified entropy weight combined model proposed in this study has the best effect and significantly improved accuracy.Through this study, the slope (26.5094–60.9°), elevation (143–276 m), distance to river (0–60 m), distance to road (0–50 m), precipitation (2000–2100 mm), and distance to fault (0–200 m) were identified as the key controlling factors for the formation of geological hazard susceptibility zonation. Conversely, the slope (0–4.2988°), distance to river (> 300 m), distance to road (> 200 m), distance to fault (> 800 m), and the marine terrace sandy area were identified as the key inhibitory factors.The eight influencing factors and accuracy selected in this study, the information value-modified entropy weight combined model for dividing geological hazard susceptibility zones, can be used for regional geological hazard prevention, planning, and major engineering project site selection. It is suggested to strengthen slope monitoring, construction land management, and engineering control measures in high-susceptibility areas.Additionally, this study only considered static influencing factors and did not incorporate dynamic triggering factors such as earthquakes and typhoons. Future research could introduce dynamic factors and integrate machine learning models to further enhance predictive accuracy, thereby improving the comprehensiveness of the study.The developmental conditions and influencing factors of geological hazards (e.g., land use, vegetation cover, engineering construction) exhibit dynamic changes; therefore, geological hazard evaluations should be updated periodically.In the study area, geological hazard sites are most developed in regions with high vegetation cover, a phenomenon that deviates from conventional understanding. This presents an intriguing finding, suggesting the need for further research on the non-linear relationship between geological hazards and vegetation in tropical monsoon regions, in order to deeply reveal the important role of vegetation in the mechanisms of geological hazard development.
