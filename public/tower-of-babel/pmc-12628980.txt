Molecular epidemiology, antimicrobial resistance, and virulence determinants of Enterococcus faecium bloodstream isolates: a 7-year study in Shandong, China
Authors: Huanxin Sun, Yujie Fang, Shengnan Hu, Dongyue Lyu, Xiutao Dong, Jiazheng Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundEnterococcus faecium is a major cause of hospital-acquired infections, particularly bloodstream infections, which are associated with high mortality rates. This study aimed to characterize the molecular epidemiology, antimicrobial resistance profiles, and virulence determinants of E. faecium isolates from bloodstream infections in a tertiary hospital in Shandong, China, over a seven-year period (2017–2023).ResultsAmong 91 E. faecium isolates, 4 were vancomycin-resistant (VREfm), and 87 were vancomycin-susceptible (VSEfm). High resistance rates (> 87.9%) were observed for ciprofloxacin, levofloxacin, penicillin, ampicillin, and erythromycin. Multilocus sequence typing (MLST) identified 16 sequence types (STs), dominated by ST78 (41.8%) and ST555 (22.0%), with a novel ST designated ST2760. Notably, the prevalence of ST555 increased significantly from 18.2% in 2021 to 43.8% in 2023. Thirteen of the 16 STs (95.6% of isolates) were assigned to Clonal Complex 17 (CC17), while ST775, ST922, and the novel ST2760 were phylogenetically distinct from this predominant lineage. Virulence gene analysis revealed high detection rates (> 73%) of adhesion-associated genes (pilA, pilB, acm, sgrA, scm, esp, and ecbA). The pilA gene was significantly more prevalent in ST78 (92.1%) than in ST555 (40.0%), conferring higher biofilm-forming capacity. The absence of pilA in ST555 strains prior to 2021 correlated with lower detection rates, while its emergence post-2021 coincided with enhanced environmental fitness and increased clinical prevalence.ConclusionThis study highlights the predominance of ST78 and ST555 in E. faecium bloodstream infections, with their increasing prevalence linked to acquisition of the pilA gene and enhanced environmental survival through biofilm formation. The identification of a novel ST2760 underscores the ongoing genetic diversity of E. faecium. These findings provide critical insights for optimizing infection control strategies and guiding empirical therapy for E. faecium bloodstream infections.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12866-025-04510-6.

Background

Enterococci, typically considered innocuous inhabitants of the human gastrointestinal microbiota, have undergone a significant shift in their status, emerging as pivotal hospital-acquired pathogens on a global scale [1]. These pathogens are capable of causing a wide range of infections, encompassing urinary tract infections, skin and soft tissue infections, intra-abdominal infections, endocarditis, and bloodstream infections [2]. Among these, enterococcal bloodstream infections present a substantial clinical challenge owing to their high mortality rates, ranging from 21.4% to 64.2% [3]. Worldwide, enterococcal bloodstream infections account for approximately 10% of all bloodstream infections [4]. In the United States, Enterococcus spp. is the third most common pathogen responsible for bloodstream infections [5], and it ranks fifth in China [6] and Europe [4]. Among the Enterococcus species responsible for bloodstream infections, Enterococcus faecium is associated with more severe comorbidities and higher mortality rates [7–9]. Furthermore, Enterococcus species have developed resistance to multiple classes of antimicrobial agents, including vancomycin, quinolones, macrolides, and tetracyclines, posing significant challenges to effective clinical anti-infective treatment strategies [10].

E. faecium generates a wide spectrum of virulence factors. Among these, pili [11], enterococcal surface protein (Esp) [12], collagen-binding proteins (Acm) [13], collagen adhesins from E. faecium (Scm) [14], serine-glutamate repeat-containing protein A (SgrA) [15], E. faecium collagen-binding protein A (EcbA) [15], and aggregation substance (Asa1) are adhesion factors [16]. These components facilitate effective bacterial adhesion to host cells and/or biofilm formation. Such adhesion is deemed a pivotal trigger event in the development of numerous infectious diseases. Additionally, other virulence factors such as hyaluronidase (Hyl) [17], cytolysin (Cyl) [18], and gelatinase (Gel) [19] contribute to the disruption of host tissue barriers by extracellularly producing enzymes and toxins. This mechanism promotes bacterial dissemination and significantly enhances their invasive capabilities. Hence, uncovering the virulence gene profile of E. faecium and enhancing our comprehension of its epidemiological characteristics is of significant importance.

Multilocus sequence typing (MLST) is a valuable technique for assessing genetic relationships and conducting molecular epidemiological studies of E. faecium [20]. Previous molecular epidemiological studies have shown that on a global scale, E. faecium can be primarily classified into two distinct types: one type, represented by Clonal Complex 94 (CC94), is associated with community and commensal environments, while the other type, designated as Clonal Complex 17 (CC17), is adapted to hospital settings [21]. The CC17 lineage of E. faecium is recognized as a genetic lineage that poses a significant health risk [22–24].

In this seven-year study (2017–2023) conducted at a tertiary hospital in Shandong, China, we systematically identified and characterized 91 E. faecium strains isolated from bloodstream infections. This investigation enhances our understanding of the epidemiological patterns and specific characteristics of bloodstream-derived E. faecium, providing critical data that can inform and refine future clinical practices and infection control strategies.

Results

Clinical and demographic characteristics of E. faecium bloodstream isolates

A total of 91 non-duplicate E. faecium strains were isolated from bloodstream samples between 2017 and 2023. The annual distribution of these isolates was as follows: 10 in 2017, 7 in 2018, 15 in 2019, 9 in 2020, 11 in 2021, 23 in 2022, and 16 in 2023. The departmental distribution of E. faecium bloodstream infections revealed a distinct pattern, with Intensive Care Unit (ICU) cases predominating at 52.7% (48/91) of isolates, followed by Gastroenterology and Hematology departments at 7.7% (7/91) each. The remaining cases were distributed across various clinical specialties, none of which provided more than five samples: Neurosurgery and Oncology (5.5% each, 5/91); Cardiac Surgery, Hepatology and Urology (4.4% each, 4/91); with minimal representation from Cardiology (2.2%, 2/91); and Emergency Medicine, Neonatology, Nephrology, Thoracic Surgery and Traditional Chinese Medicine (1.1% each, 1/91).

The demographic profile of the 91 E. faecium-infected patients is presented in Table 1. Of the total, 63.7% (58 patients) were male, while 36.3% (33 patients) were female. Age distribution reveals that the majority of patients were older adults (65.9%, 60 patients), followed by middle-aged adults (27.5%, 25 patients) aged 41–60 years. Phenotypic and genotypic characterization of antibiotic resistance

The antimicrobial susceptibility profiles of 91 E. faecium strains are detailed in Table 2 and Table S1. Generally speaking, over 87.9% of the isolates showed resistance to ciprofloxacin, levofloxacin, penicillin, ampicillin, and erythromycin. Resistance rate for erythromycin was 40.7%. Conversely, the resistance rates to tigecycline and vancomycin were only at 6.6% and 4.4%, respectively. Additionally, none of the isolates exhibited resistance to linezolid, quinupristin/dalfopristin, or daptomycin. Four isolates exhibited vancomycin-resistant phenotypes and were classified as VREfm (vancomycin-resistant E. faecium) isolates. Molecular characterization revealed that all four VREfm strains (100%) harbored the vanA resistance determinant. No other vancomycin resistance genotypes (vanB, vanC, vanD, vanE, or vanG) were detected. Multidrug-resistant E. faecium (MDR-Efm) is defined as strains resistant to at least one agent from three distinct classes of antimicrobial agents [25]. In this study, 81 isolates of E. faecium satisfied the criteria for MDR-Efm, constituting 89.0% of the total sample. These isolates comprised 4 VREfm and 77 VSEfm strains. Notably, while all VREfm isolates (100%) displayed MDR phenotypes, 88.5% (77/87) of VSEfm strains were also multidrug-resistant, with no significant difference between groups (p = 1.000) (Table 2). Further analysis revealed that the most common resistance pattern among these MDR-Efm isolates was co-resistance to penicillin, ampicillin, levofloxacin, ciprofloxacin, and erythromycin (45 isolates, 49.5%), followed by resistance to the aforementioned five antibiotics plus tetracycline (26 isolates, 28.6%).

Distribution and properties of virulence factors

Among these, the pilB gene was the most prevalent, detected in 98.9% (90 out of 91 isolates). The acm, sgrA, and scm genes followed closely, with a prevalence rate of 96.7% each (88/91 isolates). The esp and ecbA genes were also highly prevalent, found in 85.7% (78/91) and 84.6% (77/91) of the isolates, respectively. In contrast, the hyl gene demonstrated limited prevalence (22.0%, 20/91), while seven virulence determinants (asa1, cpd, cylL-L, cylL-S, cylL-A, efaA, and gel) were undetectable across all isolates. All isolates carried at least three virulence genes, with a substantial majority (89.0%, 81 out of 91) harboring six or more determinants. Multilocus sequence typing (MLST) profiles

The MLST analysis uncovered a total of 16 distinct sequence types (STs), comprising 15 previously documented STs and one novel ST identified in this study (Fig. 1 and Table S2). This newly discovered ST has been submitted for designation and received the identifier ST2760. Detailed profiles of the newly identified ST with a novel variant within the ddl gene, designated ddl-205, are provided in Table S2. Moreover, 76 of the isolates (representing 83.5% of the total) belonged to five predominant STs (each represented by ≥ 5 isolates). The most frequently observed type was ST78, comprising 41.8% (38 out of 91) of all isolates, followed by ST555, which accounted for 22.0% (20 out of 91) of the examined strains (Fig. 1 and Table S2). According to the PubMLST classification scheme, 13 of the 16 STs belonged to the Clonal Complex 17 (CC17), representing 95.6% (87/91) of analyzed isolates. The remaining three STs (ST775, ST922, and novel ST2760) were phylogenetically distinct from this epidemic lineage, which suggests the presence of genetically divergent strains in our clinical collection.Fig. 1Multilocus sequence typing (MLST) of 91 E. faecium isolates. In terms of annual distribution, ST78 remained the predominant ST between 2017 and 2022. However, in 2023, the dominant ST shifted from ST78 to ST555. Notably, from 2017 to 2020, the proportion of ST555 remained relatively stable, ranging between 10.0% and 14.3%. Starting in 2021, the frequency of ST555 began to increase significantly, rising from 18.2% in 2021 to 26.1% in 2022, and further increasing to 43.8% in 2023. The prevalence of other minor STs exhibited year-to-year variations, wherein some STs either decreased in frequency or transitioned to different minor STs (Fig. 1). For instance, the ST192 strain was detected once annually in both 2017 and 2018, after which it disappeared from our observations between 2019 and 2022. Interestingly, a single isolate of ST192 re-emerged in 2023.

Furthermore, MLST profiling identified 11 distinct STs among ICU isolates and 10 STs in non-ICU isolates (Table S3). Epidemiological analysis revealed that ST78 and ST555 were the predominant lineages in both clinical settings. These two STs demonstrated comparable distribution patterns between ICU and non-ICU wards without statistically significant differences (Table S3): ST78 accounted for 45.8% (22/48) of ICU isolates versus 37.2% (16/43) in non-ICU isolates (p = 0.405), while ST555 represented 18.8% (9/48) and 25.6% (11/43) in ICU and non-ICU environments, respectively (p = 0.432). Although certain distribution patterns were observed among less prevalent STs (Table S3), these differences also failed to reach statistical significance (all p > 0.05). For example, ST17 was detected exclusively in ICU isolates (10.4%, 5/48; p = 1.000), while ST363 showed a higher detection rate in non-ICU environments (9.3%, 4/43) compared to ICU (2.1%, 1/48; p = 0.295), among others.

Discussion

Bloodstream infections are systemic infections caused by pathogens invading the blood, ranking as the seventh leading cause of death in Europe and North America [26]. Bloodstream infections caused by E. faecium exhibit significant mortality rates, particularly among hospitalized patients and individuals with multiple comorbidities [27]. This study provides an in-depth analysis of the clonal characteristics, virulence gene profiles, and antibiotic resistance of E. faecium in the bloodstream, aiming to offer scientific insights for optimizing clinical treatment strategies and infection control measures.

Antimicrobial agents are crucial for the treatment of E. faecium infections. However, this study revealed that among 91 bloodstream infection isolates, as many as 89.0% exhibited a multidrug-resistant phenotype, with resistance rates to penicillins, fluoroquinolones, and macrolides all exceeding 87.9%. This high level of resistance aligns with the overall trend reflected by relevant surveillance data in China [28, 29]. It is noteworthy that these antimicrobials are widely used in livestock farming for disease treatment, prevention, and even growth promotion [30, 31], and their excessive use undoubtedly accelerates the development and dissemination of resistant strains. Within the One Health framework, animals, the environment, and humans form a closed-loop system that continuously exchanges resistant bacteria and resistance genes [32]. Long-term and systematic antimicrobial resistance (AMR) surveillance of clinical strains can provide early warning indicators and guide clinical medication, thereby significantly improving the efficacy of infection treatment [33]. From a One Health perspective, this study holds significant public health importance.

Furthermore, our study uncovered a vancomycin resistance rate of 4.4% among the E. faecium bloodstream isolates. This rate is notably lower than those reported in European (19.0%) [34], American (66%) [35], Australian (32.6%) [36], and African (e.g., Egypt, 46.1%) [37] regions. In contrast, it aligns with data from the China Antimicrobial Surveillance Network (CHINET, 2017–2024), which reported rates of 1.0%−3.5% (www.chinets.com), and is consistent with other Chinese regional studies where rates did not exceed 2% [28, 29]. These findings collectively underscore the significant geographic heterogeneity in the global prevalence of vancomycin-resistant E. faecium (VREfm). What’s more, none of the isolates exhibited resistance to linezolid, quinupristin/dalfopristin, or daptomycin. Our findings are in line with those reported by CHINET and other studies conducted in various regions of China [28, 29], suggesting that these drugs could be effective treatment options for controlling infections.

The presence of virulence genes is pivotal for the pathogenic potential of E. faecium. However, the distribution patterns of these genes can vary significantly among different strains. Therefore, understanding the prevalence of such virulence genes is essential for effective epidemiological management of E. faecium infections. In this study, we observed a high detection rate (exceeding 73%) of virulence factors, including pilA, pilB, acm, sgrA, scm, esp, and ecbA. These virulence factors are all associated with adhesion and biofilm formation [38, 39] with some belonging to the MSCRAMM (microbial surface component recognizing adhesive matrix molecules) or pilus-encoding gene families [40]. During the initial phase of bloodstream infections, bacteria must withstand shear forces generated by blood flow and identify suitable host cells or tissues for adhesion [41, 42]. The aforementioned virulence factors facilitate specific recognition and attachment to the extracellular matrix or vascular endothelial cells, which is crucial for successful bacterial bloodstream infections [38, 39]. Consequently, the high prevalence of these adhesion-associated genes among bloodstream infection isolates underscores their essential role in enabling E. faecium to adapt to the host environment and establish infections.

MLST, a well-defined and versatile molecular typing technique, involves the identification of precise and portable nucleotide sequences from internal fragments of multiple housekeeping genes. This technique serves as a valuable tool for assessing the genetic background of bacterial populations [43]. Based on PubMLST classification, we observed that Clonal Complex 17 (CC17) was prevalent in our hospital, encompassing 13 STs and 95.6% of strains. Our findings are consistent with previous studies that have identified CC17 as a major clone complex of E. faecium associated with nosocomial infections worldwide [21, 22, 44]. Multiple previous studies have demonstrated that E. faecium strains within the CC17 frequently exhibit enhanced metabolic adaptability, increased acquisition of antimicrobial resistance determinants, and the presence of virulence genes that promote host colonization and disease progression [21, 22, 45–49]. These characteristics likely confer a survival and transmission advantage to CC17 E. faecium strains in hospital environments. This may be the primary factor contributing to the current epidemiological situation of CC17 in our study and on a global scale. Intriguingly, ST775, ST922, and the novel ST2760 were phylogenetically distinct from CC17, demonstrating genetic diversity and suggesting potential expansion of emerging lineages in this region. Additionally, in this study, a total of 16 STs were identified, with ST78 being the most prevalent, accounting for 41.8% of all isolates, followed by ST555 at 22.0%. ST78 is already known to be prevalent not only in some Asian countries, such as China and South Korea [50, 51], but also in Australia and numerous European countries, including Spain and Italy [52–54]. In contrast, although ST555 has appeared in some molecular epidemiological studies, it is rarely reported as a dominant type.

Pili, also referred to as fimbriae, have been identified in Gram-positive bacteria [55, 56]. These surface structures play a critical role in bacterial adhesion, biofilm formation [57, 58], and resistance to macrophage-mediated clearance [59]. Such functional attributes enhance bacterial survival in hospital environments and may contribute to the increased prevalence of bloodstream infections. In E. faecium, two distinct types of pili, PilA and PilB, have been identified, encoded by the pilA and pilB genes, respectively. Both pili types exhibit these functional properties, which likely promote their survival and persistence in the bloodstream. Notably, the majority of ST555 strains in this study lack the pilA gene, potentially resulting in diminished capabilities in adhesion, biofilm formation, and bloodstream survival compared to ST78 strains. Temporal distribution analysis further revealed a striking absence of pilA-positive ST555 strains between 2017 and 2020. However, all eight pilA-positive ST555 strains were first detected after 2021. This trend underscores substantial temporal changes in the prevalence and characteristics of the ST555 lineage. Considering the pivotal role of PilA pili, the emergence of pilA-positive ST555 strains after 2021 has enabled many of these strains to co-express both PilA and PilB pili, potentially enhancing their adhesion, biofilm-forming capacity, and bloodstream survival. This genetic adaptation, coupled with improved biofilm-forming ability, is likely a key driver of the increasing prevalence of ST555 strains from 2021 to 2023. Further mechanistic studies using pilA knockout mutants in ST555 strains are needed to determine the specific role of this gene in biofilm formation and environmental fitness.

In this study, we identified a novel sequence type, ST2760, harbored virulence genes, including pilA, pilB, sgrA, and scm, which are associated with adhesion and biofilm formation. Although ST2760 currently represents a minor proportion of the isolates, its presence, coupled with its virulence gene repertoire, raises concerns about its potential to drive future outbreaks. Enhanced surveillance and targeted infection control measures are critical to mitigate its transmission and prevent potential public health threats.

However, this study has some limitations that should be considered. Firstly, due to its retrospective nature and incomplete clinical records, we could not establish precise associations between bacterial genotypes and clinical outcomes. Second, as a single-center study with retrospective design and relatively small sample size (n = 91), the generalizability of our findings may be limited. Finally, pilA knockout mutants will be essential to mechanistically characterize how this gene contributes to the environmental adaptation of clinical E. faecium isolates. Therefore, future multicenter studies should combine comprehensive clinical data with mechanistic investigations to better define genotype-phenotype relationships.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study provides a comprehensive analysis of the antimicrobial resistance profiles, virulence determinants, and molecular epidemiology of E. faecium isolates from bloodstream infections in a tertiary hospital in Shandong, China. ST78 and ST555 emerged as the predominant STs, with their increased prevalence strongly associated with acquisition of the pilA gene and its enhanced biofilm-forming capacity. Additionally, a novel sequence type, ST2760, was identified as an emergent lineage distinct from the prevalent CC17, further underscoring the genetic diversity of E. faecium. These findings provide critical insights into the evolving epidemiology of E. faecium bloodstream isolates, highlighting the necessity of continuous surveillance to inform infection control strategies and optimize therapeutic interventions.

Methods

Bacterial isolates

A total of 91 unique E. faecium isolates were collected from blood samples of patients across diverse departments, including Intensive Care Unit (ICU), Gastroenterology and Hematology divisions, and other wards at the First Affiliated Hospital of Shandong First Medical University located in Shandong, China. These samples were collected over a period spanning from 2017 to 2023. Blood specimens were gathered from patients presenting with suspected bacterial bloodstream infections in adherence to the criteria established by the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) [60]. All isolates were identified using the matrix-assisted laser desorption ionization-time of flight mass spectrometry (MALDI-TOF MS) (Bruker MALDI Biotyper system, Germany).

Antimicrobial susceptibility testing (AST)

AST was conducted using the Vitek 2 Compact system (BioMérieux, France) with AST-GP-67 cards. All E. faecium isolates were tested against ten antibiotics: penicillin, ampicillin, ciprofloxacin, levofloxacin, erythromycin, quinupristin/dalfopristin, linezolid, vancomycin, tetracycline, and tigecycline. Daptomycin susceptibility testing was additionally performed using the broth microdilution method with reagents purchased from Wenzhou Kangtai Biological Technology Co., Ltd. (Zhejiang, China). The results were interpreted according to Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI) criteria, except for tigecycline, where breakpoints were determined based on the adapted guidelines provided by the European Committee on Antimicrobial Susceptibility Testing (EUCAST; http://www.eucast.org). E. faecalis ATCC 29212 and S. aureus ATCC 25923 were used as quality control strains.

Detection of virulence and vancomycin resistance genes

The genomic DNA of the 91 E. faecium isolates was extracted using the Tiangen DNA nucleic acid extraction kit (Tiangen, China). To identify virulence-associated genes in these isolates, conventional PCR assays were performed according to standard PCR amplification protocols [61–63]. The targeted virulence genes included: adhesion-related genes (pilA, pilB, esp, acm, scm, sgrA, ecbA, and asa1); secreted virulence factors (hyl, cylL-L, cylL-S, cylL-A, and gelE); conjugation-related gene (cpd) and endocarditis antigen gene (efaA). To investigate the molecular mechanisms of vancomycin resistance, PCR screening was also performed to detect the presence of vanA, vanB, vanC, vanD, vanE, and vanG resistance genes [64, 65]. Randomly selected positive amplicons were sequenced to confirm the identity of the amplified products.

Biofilm formation assay

Biofilm formation by E. faecium clinical isolates was assessed as previously described [66] with minor modifications. Briefly, single colonies were cultured in Brain Heart Infusion (BHI) broth overnight at 37 °C. Bacterial suspensions were diluted 1:100 in BHI broth containing 0.5% glucose, and 200 µL aliquots were added to microtiter plates. Each isolate was tested in triplicate, with uninoculated medium as negative control. After 24 h static incubation at 37 °C, plates were washed three times with PBS, fixed with anhydrous ethanol for 15 min, and stained with 0.1% crystal violet for 15 min. Excess stain was removed by rinsing with distilled water, and bound dye was solubilized in DMSO for 15 min. Optical density (OD) was measured at 570 nm, with final values corrected by subtracting the negative control average.

Multilocus sequence typing (MLST)

MLST analyses were performed for all E. faecium isolates using the scheme available in the PubMLST database (https://pubmlst.org/organisms/enterococcus-faecium). Sequence types (STs) were determined by amplifying and sequencing seven housekeeping genes (adk, atpA, ddl, gyd, gdh, purK, and pstS). The obtained sequences were compared to those in the PubMLST database to assign allele numbers and define STs. Alleles or STs that did not match any existing entries in the database were designated as novel. These novel alleles and STs were subsequently submitted to the PubMLST curators for verification and official designation. A minimum-spanning tree, based on allelic differences among the seven housekeeping genes, was constructed using BioNumerics software. Clonal Complex 17 (CC17) conformed to the classification scheme of PubMLST.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS Statistics 21.0 for Windows. Comparisons of resistance rates and virulence gene distributions between VRE and VSE, as well as between ST78 and ST555 isolates, were performed using the chi-square test. The unpaired t-test was used to compare biofilm-forming capacity between two groups. A two-sided p-value below 0.05 was considered indicative of statistical significance.
