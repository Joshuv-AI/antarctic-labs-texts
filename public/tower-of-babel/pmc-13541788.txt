Salience Network Dynamics Across Spontaneous Attacks, Interictal Rest and Interictal Pain Imagery in Menstrually Related Migraine
Authors: Inês Esteves, Alexandre Perdigão, Ana R Fouto, Amparo Ruiz-Tagle, Gina Caetano, Joana Cabral, Isabel Pavão Martins, Raquel Gil-Gouveia, César Caballero-Gaudes, Patrícia Figueiredo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe persistent sensitivity to sensory and painful stimuli of people with migraine has been associated with disruption of the salience network (SN). Although migraine is characterized by severe head pain attacks, brain changes have been observed in people with migraine in between attacks (i.e., during the interictal phase). We hypothesized that SN dynamics may be altered in people with migraine during spontaneous attacks as well as an attack imagery task engaging sensory, emotional, and cognitive aspects of pain. To test this, we analysed the SN dynamics in patients with episodic menstrually related migraine without aura across three conditions: at rest during a spontaneous attack; during the interictal phase while performing an imagery task recalling a previous attack and at interictal rest. Healthy controls were studied in matching conditions, including rest and a non-migraine head-pain imagery. We collected functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) data, using a dynamic functional connectivity (dFC) analysis to examine SN temporal features from a total of 26 participants. A significant between-group difference was observed during the pain imagery task, with people with migraine exhibiting a shorter SN dwell time than controls. Such differences were not detected during spontaneous attacks or interictal rest. The between-group difference in SN dynamics observed during the pain imagery condition suggests that this paradigm may be useful for engaging the cognitive and emotional dimensions of pain. Introduction

Migraine is a cyclic neurovascular disorder marked by recurrent episodes of pulsating headaches and an increased sensitivity to sensory stimuli, leading to symptoms such as photophobia, phonophobia, and nausea (Goadsby et al. 2017). The active headache period, known as the ictal phase, typically lasts between 4 and 72 h (Peng and May 2020), while the interictal phase refers to the intervals between attacks. Functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) studies measuring responses to visual, olfactory, and painful stimuli have consistently shown that people with migraine exhibit abnormal sensory processing, lack typical habituation between attacks, and display atypical connectivity in sensory networks (de Tommaso et al. 2014; Russo et al. 2018; Schwedt et al. 2015). This persistent sensitivity to sensory and painful stimuli is closely linked to the function of the salience network (SN), which is crucial for detecting, filtering, and prioritizing relevant information (Seeley 2019). Anchored in the anterior cingulate cortex (ACC) and the anterior insula, it acts as a switch between self-focused and externally-focused brain networks, regulating attention, and integrating sensory, emotional, and cognitive information (Schimmelpfennig et al. 2023). Thus, SN disruptions may contribute to the increased sensitivity to sensory stimuli and the deficits in sensory processing seen in people with migraine both during the attacks and between them. Consistent with this, neuroimaging studies have frequently reported alterations in the SN of people with migraine (Xue et al. 2012; D. Yu et al. 2017a, b), as well as particularly within the ACC (Li et al. 2016; Liu et al. 2023; Ou et al. 2024; Wang et al. 2022; D. Yu et al. 2017a, b) and the insula (Araújo et al. 2023; Coppola et al. 2018; Huang et al. 2024; Lim et al. 2021; Tian et al. 2021; Wei et al. 2020; Yu et al. 2017a, b).

Pathological changes in the SN may be indicative of impaired cortical excitability (Coppola et al. 2019). These changes could reflect disproportionate responses in hyperexcitable regions when exposed to external stimuli, which aligns with the characteristic difficulties of people with migraine in adapting to sensory input and a disrupted balance between excitatory and inhibitory neurotransmitters (Aurora and Wilkinson 2007; O’Hare et al. 2023; Vecchia and Pietrobon 2012; Younis et al. 2017). To assess this, Veréb et al. (2020) studied the SN dynamic functional connectivity (dFC) in people with migraine, both with and without aura, during the interictal phase. Although the study only observed changes in people with migraine with aura, increased cortical excitability is also a feature of migraine without aura (Cortese et al. 2017). Consequently, we propose that this phenomenon is present and may be elicited during pain imagery experiences in people with migraine without aura.

Previous studies have mainly examined the SN’s functional connectivity in the interictal phase, either during rest or in response to painful stimulation. To address this scarcity, we study the SN during spontaneous migraine attacks. Moreover, to isolate the emotional and cognitive aspects of pain processing from the sensory and physiological changes associated with the attack, we study the SN during an imagery task designed to elicit the experience of a migraine attack in the absence of pain, i.e., the interictal phase. We consider that such an imagery task should allow us to probe the network’s engagement in pain-related cognitive and emotional processes, while disentangling the nociceptive processing from the other components that take part in the experience of a migraine attack. Several studies have shown that tasks involving the imagination of pain or its suggestion through hypnosis lead to functional changes in the insula and ACC, as well as other regions associated with pain processing, in healthy adults (Derbyshire et al. 2004; Gu and Han 2007; Ogino et al. 2006; Raij et al. 2005, 2009; Richter et al. 2010). In fact, Eck et al. (2011) showed that during the processing of pain-related words, people with migraine exhibited increased activation of the insula. Furthermore, our previous research indicates that pain imagery can significantly deactivate the ACC, and other pain-processing regions, in people with migraine during the interictal phase, relative to healthy controls (Perdigão et al. 2024).

Given that the SN likely exhibits dynamic and task-sensitive temporal patterns, probing its response through a pain imagery task could yield insights into how cognitive and emotional processing of pain engages this network. Research has mostly focused on the interictal phase or induced attacks due to the unpredictability of migraine and the difficulty of capturing ictal-phase fMRI data (Schramm et al. 2023; Tolner et al. 2019). Moreover, traditional static resting-state analyses characterize average connectivity patterns, but may miss dynamic fluctuations central to understanding migraine.

Therefore, here we examined the dFC of the SN in a group of people with migraine without aura during the ictal phase at rest, as well as during the interictal phase, along with a group of matching healthy controls, both at rest and while performing a pain imagery task, using a longitudinal design. This approach allows us to assess the temporal variability of SN connectivity during intrinsic, resting conditions as well as during active, pain-related cognitive engagement. We hypothesize that the pain imagery task selectively engages cognitive and emotional representations of pain, which may amplify alterations in SN temporal dynamics in people with migraine relative to controls. Moreover, these patterns may differ from those observed in the ictal phase, as ongoing nociceptive input and physiological changes dominate network activity during attacks. By exploring these dynamic connectivity patterns, we aim to characterize temporal changes in the SN across resting-state and pain imagery conditions in menstrually related migraine without aura, and to investigate whether group differences are more apparent during engagement in pain-related cognitive and emotional processing.

Materials and Methods

This study is part of a broader research project on brain imaging in migraine (MIG_N2Treat). It followed a prospective, longitudinal, within-subject design, and consisted of a comprehensive neuroimaging protocol encompassing arterial spin labelling MRI, diffusion MRI and task-fMRI as well as resting-state fMRI. In this report we focus specifically on the pain imagery task and resting-state fMRI data, while the methods and results of other parts of the protocol are described elsewhere (Domingos et al. 2023; Esteves et al. 2025; Fouto et al. 2024a, b; Guadilla et al. 2025; Matoso et al. 2024; Ruiz-Tagle et al. 2025). The research protocol and statistical analysis were not preregistered. The study was approved by the Hospital da Luz Ethics Committee and all participants provided written informed consent in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki 7th revision.

Participants and Experimental Design

People with migraine were recruited by a neurologist during routine consultations at the headache outpatient clinics of Hospital da Luz, Lisbon, Portugal. Healthy female controls were recruited through social media advertisements targeting the general population and university campuses, ensuring they matched the patient group in gender, age, contraceptive use, and menstrual phase at the time of scanning. We did not conduct a statistical power analysis prior to starting the study. Notwithstanding, we aimed to have a sample size of 15 people with migraine, aligning with previous MRI cohort studies that detected group differences during spontaneous migraine attacks with sample sizes of N = 13 (Coppola et al. 2016, 2018) or N = 17 (Amin et al. 2018). The inclusion criteria were: being between 18 and 55 years; having at least 9 years of education; speaking Portuguese as their first language; being generally healthy (excluding migraine, for the patients), with no diagnosed condition significantly impacting active and productive life or causing life expectancy to be below 5 years. Additionally, patients were required to suffer from episodic menstrually related migraine without aura, diagnosed according to the criteria of the 3rd edition of the International Classification of Headache Disorders (ICHD-3), Appendix A1.1.2 (International Headache Society 2018). The menstrual relationship of the migraine attacks was established by a clinical interview conducted by the neurologist responsible for the recruitment of people with migraine. The exclusion criteria were: history or presence of a neurologic condition (excluding migraine, for the patients); history or presence of a psychiatric disorder and/or severe anxiety and/or depressive symptoms as assessed through questionnaires; history or presence of vascular disease; receiving treatment with psychoactive drugs (including anxiolytics, antidepressants, anti-epileptics, and any migraine prophylactics); being pregnant or trying to get pregnant, breastfeeding, being post-menopausal, or using contraception precluding cyclic menses; contraindications for MRI.

We aimed to assess patients during the ictal (M-ict) and interictal (M-inter) phases. For the interictal session, participants were required to be free from pain for at least 48 h before, with confirmation of the absence of a migraine attack obtained 72 h post-scan. Healthy controls were assessed in two phases of the menstrual cycle to match the peri-ictal and interictal phases of the patients, yielding respectively the perimenstrual phase (HC-pm) and the post-ovulation phase (HC-postov).

Resting-state (RS) fMRI data were collected (7 min, eyes-open) both for M-inter/HC-postov and M-ict/HC-pm. Participants were instructed to keep their eyes open, looking at a black screen, and to not think about anything in particular. For the pain imagery (PI) task, patients (M-inter) performed attack imagery, recollecting the experience of their most severe migraine attack episode, while controls (HC-postov) had to recall their most severe episode of internal head pain (e.g. tooth pain). The task consisted of eight 20-s periods of pain imagery alternated with eight 20-s pain relief imagery (~ 5min30s), with eyes-closed following verbal instructions. Additionally, we collected detailed clinical history information from people with migraine, including disease duration, typical attack characteristics (e.g., pain intensity, frequency, and duration), and related clinical features. Demographic variables (e.g., age and years of education) were obtained for both groups. All participants completed the State–Trait Anxiety Inventory (STAI; State and Trait subscales)(Silva and Campos 1998; Spielberger et al. 1983) and the Positive and Negative Affect Schedule (PANAS)(Watson et al. 1988) to assess anxiety and affective state. The STAI-Trait was administered once to capture stable anxiety disposition, whereas the STAI-State and PANAS were administered at the beginning of each experimental session to assess session-specific anxiety and affective states prior to task performance. People with migraine additionally completed the Headache Impact Test (HIT-6)(Kosinski et al., 2003), a measure of headache-related functional impact, and the Mig-SCog questionnaire (Gil-Gouveia et al. 2011), which assesses migraine-related subjective cognitive complaints. These measures were used to characterize the sample and to explore potential associations between clinical features and neuroimaging metrics.

MRI Data Acquisition

The MRI data was collected using a 3T Siemens Vida system, with a 64-channel radio frequency (RF) coil. Functional images were acquired with a T2*-weighted gradient-echo Echo Planar Imaging (EPI) with the following parameters: TR/TE = 1260/30ms, flip angle = 70º, in-plane generalized autocalibrating partially parallel acquisition (GRAPPA) acceleration factor = 2, simultaneous multi-slice (SMS) factor = 3, 60 slices, 2.2 mm isotropic voxel resolution. T1-weighted structural images were obtained with a magnetization-prepared rapid gradient echo (MPRAGE) sequence, with TR = 2300 ms, TE = 2.98 ms, inversion time (TI) = 900 ms, and 1 mm isotropic voxel resolution. Fieldmap magnitude and phase images were obtained using a double-echo gradient echo sequence (TR = 400.0 ms, TE = 4.92/7.38 ms, voxel size: 3.4 × 3.4 × 3.0 mm3, flip angle = 60º). We acquired 262 and 333 fMRI volumes for the pain imagery task and resting state, respectively. Earplugs were provided to reduce acoustic noise exposure, and small cushions were placed around the head to minimize movement.

MRI Data Preprocessing

The FMRIB Software Library (FSL)’s tools (Jenkinson et al. 2012) were used to preprocess the MRI data. For the structural images this included non-brain tissue removal using Brain Extraction Tool (BET) and bias field correction using FMRIB’s Automated Segmentation Tool (FAST). Each structural image was registered to the subject’s functional space (reference volume) as well as the standard MNI space using FMRIB’s Linear Image Registration Tool (FLIRT) and FMRIB’s Non-Linear Image Registration Tool (FNIRT), respectively. fMRI data preprocessing consisted of: EPI distortion correction using FMRIB’s Utility for Geometrically Unwarping EPIs (FUGUE) based on the acquired fieldmap; volume realignment with respect to the middle volume (reference) using FLIRT, yielding 6 rigid-body motion parameters; and high-pass temporal filtering with a cut-off frequency of 0.01 Hz to remove slow drift fluctuations. Finally, spatial smoothing was performed using SUSAN tool (FSL’s SUSAN), employing a Gaussian kernel with a full-width half maximum of 3.3 mm. The preprocessing pipeline can be found here: https://github.com/martaxavier/fMRI-Preprocessing.

Dynamic Functional Connectivity (dFC)

For dFC computation and characterization of the temporal dynamics of the SN, we employed the leading eigenvector dynamic analysis (LEiDA) framework (Cabral et al. 2017), previously used to study conditions such as major depressive disorder (Figueroa et al. 2019) and schizophrenia (Farinha et al. 2022). Extraction of Dynamic Functional Connectivity (dFC) Eigenvectors

The dynamic functional connectivity eigenvectors were extracted from preprocessed parcellated fMRI data (Fig. 2a). fMRI data was parcellated by computing region average time courses using Schaefer atlas (Schaefer et al. 2018) with 100 parcels, directly corresponding to the 7 canonical intrinsic functional networks defined by Yeo et al. (2011): fronto-parietal network (FPN), default mode network (DMN), dorsal attention network (DAN), limbic network (LN), salience network (SN), somatomotor network (SMN) and visual network (VN). The time series of each region were demeaned and bandpass-filtered from 0.01 to 0.1 Hz using a 5th order Butterworth filter.

We estimated the individual instantaneous phase of each region () for each timepoint by applying the Hilbert Transform to each region time-course. The phase coherence ( PhCoh) between regions n and p for each timepoint was then estimated as:

PhCoh values vary between − 1 and 1, where − 1 indicates that areas n and are anti-phase at time t and 1 means they are synchronized. For all subjects, we computed it for all pairwise combinations of brain areas ( n,p), with at each timepoint t, with for the PI task or for the RS. The first and last timepoints for each scan were excluded since they could suffer distortions induced by the Hilbert transform.

Following the LEiDA approach, which concentrates on the principal patterns of PhCoh matrices by analysing their leading eigenvector, we computed the leading eigenvector for each time point, , a vector (reflecting the parcellation into 100 regions) that indicates the main direction of phase alignment across all brain regions, dividing brain regions into two communities based on the sign of its elements. If all elements of share the same sign, it reflects a global phase coherence, meaning all regions are grouped into one community. However, if contains both positive and negative elements, brain regions are divided into two communities according to their phase coherence. The absolute value of each element of indicates how strongly each brain region contributes to its assigned community. By convention, we set up so that most of its elements are negative (multiplying by -1 in case this is not true), noting that both and are valid leading eigenvectors. This approach allows us to select a distinct functional network comprising brain regions with positive elements, representing regions that deviate from the global coherence mode. By employing the LEiDA methodology, we achieve significant dimensionality reduction while preserving over 50% of the variance in phase coherence across all scans and time points.

Identification of Recurrent dFC States

After calculating from the PhCoh matrix for each time point, we identified recurring dFC states observed over time (Fig. 2b). To do this, we combined the leading eigenvectors from all scans, including data from both groups and all three conditions, into a single dataset. We then used k-means clustering to group the leading eigenvectors into clusters, exploring a range of 3 to 10 clusters ( K=3:10). Clustering was conducted by minimizing the cosine distance between eigenvectors within each cluster. The algorithm was run 1,000 times, with a maximum of 500 iterations per run, to minimize the chance of settling on a local rather than a global solution. For each value of K, the process yielded K clusters. Each cluster was represented by a centroid (), a vector of dimension , corresponding to a recurrent dFC state. The centroid was calculated as the average of the leading eigenvectors assigned to that cluster.

dFC State Assignment to SN

After determining the possible clustering solutions, we identified the centroid that most closely resembled the SN for each number of clusters K, i.e., the one with the largest spatial overlap (Fig. 2c). To achieve this, we created a vector, , representing the SN, where each element was calculated as the proportion of voxels in each region of Schaefer’s functional parcellation (with direct matching to Yeo’s intrinsic functional networks) corresponding to the SN, relative to the total number of voxels in that region. We then computed the Pearson correlation between and each cluster centroid vector, . Following the standard LEiDA convention, all negative values of were first set to zero, such that the correlation was computed using only the positive centroid elements, which define the regions participating in the functional network of interest, whereas negative values represent regions with an opposite phase relative to the dominant pattern. For each number of clusters, K, the centroid with the highest significant correlation with was designated as representing the SN, .

SN dFC State Temporal Features

For each clustering solution, we derived a binary time course for the salience network (SN) state for each subject (Fig. 2d). This binary time course indicated whether the SN was in the background (when the assigned centroid did not correspond to the SN) or dominant (for time points assigned to the SN centroid). To characterize the SN dynamics, we calculated two parameters: fractional occupancy and dwell time. Fractional occupancy represents the probability of the SN state occurring, defined as the percentage of time points assigned to the SN state. Dwell time refers to the average duration of the SN state, calculated as the mean number of consecutive time points assigned to the SN state across all occurrences. This calculation excludes instances where the state occurs at the very beginning or end of the scan, since it may have already started or may have not finished yet and the duration would not be correctly estimated.

Statistical Analysis

Between-group differences in age were assessed using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test. Statistically significant differences between groups during pain imagery and resting state for both sessions were evaluated using a permutation-based t-test as implemented in the LEiDA toolbox This non-parametric test evaluates group differences by permuting group labels to generate the null distribution, rather than relying on standard parametric distributions. The null distribution was computed separately for each population, and a t-test was performed for each of the 5,000 permutations to compare between-group (two-sample) and within-group (one-sample, performed only for the resting state). For each parameter, we then applied multiple comparisons False Discovery Rate (FDR) correction with the Benjamini-Hochberg method (Benjamini and Hochberg 1995; Martínez-Cagigal 2021). FDR correction was applied separately for the between-group and within-group analyses, accounting for the number of clustering solutions (K = 3–10) and the number of comparisons in each analysis (between-group: M-inter vs. HC-postov RS; M-inter vs. HC-postov PI; M-ict vs. HC-pm RS; within-group: HC-postov vs. HC-pm RS; M-inter vs. M-ict RS). The effect size was evaluated using Hedges’ g, to accommodate the small sample size (Hedges and Olkin 1985). To assess the potential influence of age on the primary findings, post hoc robustness analyses were conducted using linear regression models including group and age as predictors.

To examine the potential clinical relevance of salience network (SN) dynamic functional connectivity (dFC) metrics, we performed correlation analyses using Spearman correlation between temporal features showing significant differences between M and HC and a subset of clinically relevant variables. These included three measures linked to usual attack severity and impact (attack pain intensity, HIT-6, and Mig-SCog), as well as three measures reflecting session-specific affect and anxiety (PANAS positive affect, PANAS negative affect, and STAI-State). Associations were evaluated using two-tailed Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient, given the sample size. These analyses were considered exploratory and were not included in the multiple-comparisons correction applied to the primary group-level permutation tests.

Moreover, no significant differences were found within-group for RS, as shown in Figure S2 (Supplementary Material). As a robustness analysis to assess the potential influence of age, linear regression models were fitted for the significant dwell time comparisons (K = 7-9), with dwell time as the dependent variable and group and age as predictors. The results were consistent with the primary permutation analysis, with the group effect remaining statistically significant across all three clustering solutions after adjustment for age, whereas age was not a significant predictor in any model (Table S4, Supplementary Material). As a further robustness analysis, the primary LEiDA permutation analysis was repeated after excluding the participant with the truncated pain imagery scan. Although the between-group comparisons no longer survived FDR correction, the pattern of results remained consistent, with similar effect sizes and uncorrected permutation p-values across the clustering solutions that were significant in the primary analysis (K = 7-9), suggesting that the findings were not solely driven by the truncated acquisition (Table S5, Supplementary Material).

Regarding the association between the significant results and the clinical variables, there were no significant correlations, as shown in Figure S3 (Supplementary Material).

Discussion

We found a significant between-group difference in SN dynamics during the pain imagery task, in which people with migraine recalled a previous migraine attack and healthy controls recalled non-migraine head pain, with people with migraine exhibiting shorter SN dwell time. In contrast, no between-group differences were detected in the present sample during resting state either in the interictal phase or during spontaneous migraine attacks (ictal phase). dFC analysis revealed that the SN state in patients remained dominant for shorter periods than in controls, indicating reduced temporal persistence of the SN state (see Supplementary Material for complementary analyses of fractional occupancy and entry count). Importantly, this between-group difference was observed during the pain imagery task, suggesting that this paradigm may be particularly sensitive to group differences in SN dynamics related to the cognitive and emotional processing of pain-related representations rather than reflecting a generalized disruption. However, because the recalled experiences differed between groups, it remains to be determined whether these differences are migraine-specific or are influenced by characteristics of the recalled experiences.

Salience Network Dynamics in Migraine

Our finding of shorter mean duration of SN occurrences indicates increased temporal instability of SN dynamics during pain imagery, which is not fully aligned with the work by Veréb et al. (2020) reporting greater SN temporal instability in people with migraine with aura during the interictal phase. In contrast to Veréb et al. (2020), we found SN instability in patients without aura and only while they were engaged in the imagery of an attack. It is possible that, during the resting state, only people with migraine with aura, who likely exhibit higher cortical excitability, show altered sensory processing that leads to changes in SN dynamics, whereas our use of a pain imagery task enabled us to detect these effects in patients without aura. This interpretation is also consistent with the heterogeneous resting-state fMRI literature in migraine with aura, in which findings have often been difficult to reproduce across studies (Hougaard et al. 2015, 2023). One possible explanation is that resting-state activity may be inherently more variable than task-related brain activity, whereas a standardized pain imagery paradigm may provide a more controlled context for detecting group differences in network dynamics. In line with Veréb et al. (2020), a resting-state electroencephalography (EEG) study reporting that a microstate linked to the SN exhibited a shorter mean duration for patients during the interictal phase compared to healthy controls (Li et al. 2022). Indeed, EEG microstates are thought to reflect the spatiotemporal dynamics of brain networks showing quasi-stable spatial configurations, akin to the fMRI dFC states in our analysis (Abreu et al. 2021; Allen et al. 2018; Bréchet et al. 2019; Qiu et al. 2023). In contrast with these previous studies, we did not detect alterations in SN dynamics during rest but only during the pain imagery task. This may reflect the fact that the task explicitly engages cognitive and emotional processes related to pain, such as memory retrieval, anticipation, and evaluative appraisal, which strongly recruit the SN. Under these conditions, subtle alterations in salience processing may become more detectable than during unconstrained resting-state activity.

Pain Imagery and the Salience Network

This is the first study to employ a pain imagery fMRI paradigm to assess SN connectivity in people with migraine. We have previously investigated brain activation using our proposed pain imagery fMRI paradigm by employing a whole-brain voxel-wise approach (Perdigão et al. 2024). We found that, compared to healthy controls, people with migraine exhibited significantly greater deactivation of the ACC, among other pain-processing regions, indicating a dysregulation of pain inhibition. The ACC is involved in the emotional aspect of pain processing, becoming active when we observe others in pain and when we experience our own pain-related distress. It also helps regulate perceived pain intensity, contributing to the ability to adapt to discomfort and manage suffering in pain responses (Fuchs et al. 2014; Xiao and Zhang 2018). These findings suggest that alterations in these regions may emerge even in the absence of direct nociceptive input, when patients engage with pain-related mental representations. The current study further supports it through its involvement in the abnormal dynamics of the SN.

A few fMRI studies have previously analysed brain activation during pain imagery or similar protocols (Derbyshire et al. 2004; Gu and Han 2007; Ogino et al. 2006; Raij et al. 2005, 2009), reporting the involvement of areas belonging to the SN, such as the ACC, insula, cerebellum, dorsolateral prefrontal cortex and secondary somatosensory cortex. To our knowledge, only Eck et al. (2011) employed a pain imagery paradigm in people with migraine, whereby participants had to create mental images (compared to counting the number of vowels) based on pain-related and non-pain related adjectives. During imagination of pain-related words, patients showed stronger activation in affective pain-related brain regions compared to controls. Interestingly, although the words used in the paradigm were not migraine-specific, five people with migraine and one control referred having imagined a headache as an example of a pain-related word. Despite some similarity with our imagery conditions, the SN connectivity and temporal dynamics were not analysed and therefore the results cannot be directly compared with our current study.

Areas of the Salience Network

The ACC and the insula are two crucial regions within the SN that contributed to the identification of the SN dFC state in our study. Since pain is a multidimensional experience (Lee et al. 2020), the pain imagery task is expected to preferentially engage the affective and cognitive components of pain rather than its sensory-discriminative aspects, which aligns with the known roles of the ACC and insula. In fact, observing a noxious stimulus being administered to someone else, or viewing a cue that signals its impending delivery, does not directly activate nociceptors, but it carries a high degree of salience (Iannetti and Mouraux 2010).

Furthermore, the insula has been linked to autonomic and somatosensory alterations in migraine, as it is involved in autonomic regulation, interoception and emotion (Menon and Uddin 2010; Moayedi 2014). The anterior insula is connected to the middle and inferior temporal gyri, the anterior cingulate cortex, and various limbic regions, all of which contribute to its role in emotional regulation. In contrast, the posterior insula is linked to premotor, sensorimotor, supplementary motor areas, and the middle-posterior cingulate cortex, supporting its involvement in sensorimotor integration. The insula has been associated not only with nociception but also with the affective dimension of pain, particularly the unpleasantness of a painful experience (Lee et al. 2020). It is also involved in empathizing with others’ pain (Jackson et al. 2006) and remains active during noxious stimulation even in individuals with congenital insensitivity to pain (Salomons et al. 2016). This aligns with our findings that alterations occur even without painful stimuli; simply imagining a painful and unpleasant experience can lead to observable differences in the SN of people with migraine.

Attack Imagery vs. Attack Experience

Since we did not detect between-group differences in SN dynamics during spontaneous migraine attacks (ictal phase), either comparing to healthy controls or to the interictal phase of people with migraine, our findings suggest that different processes may dominate large-scale brain dynamics during the ongoing experience of an attack compared to the cognitive reconstruction of an attack through imagery. These mechanisms might either obscure the contribution of the SN or fundamentally alter its behaviour, by affecting oxygenation, metabolism, and neuronal excitability. For instance, during the ictal phase, heightened cortical excitability or other network-level adaptations (Messina et al. 2023; Peng and May 2019) could dominate sensory processing, potentially obscuring subtler alterations in SN dynamics that may be more easily observed during engagement in a pain-related cognitive task. This shift in network dynamics indicates that the migraine attack involves complex overlapping processes that differ from those present during periods without an attack.

Another possible explanation for not finding SN disruption in the ictal phase is that sensory processing abnormalities fluctuate throughout the migraine cycle, intensifying in the preictal phase but generally diminishing during the attack (de Tommaso et al. 2014). This suggests that an adaptive or protective mechanism may be active during the interictal phase, leading to an amplification of certain sensory processing components during this period.

Importantly, pain imagery may engage predictive and memory-based aspects of pain processing that are not fully active during resting-state ictal scans, when the experience of an attack is actually occurring and not being retrieved. In people with migraine, repeated headache experiences could strengthen associations between pain cues and anticipatory or evaluative processes, transiently destabilizing SN dynamics during imagery. This mechanism highlights how cognitive and emotional representations of pain can selectively recruit the SN, revealing alterations that may be masked when ongoing nociceptive input dominates network activity.

Although the proposed attack imagery task does not exactly mimic the experience of a migraine attack, it successfully elicited SN dynamics that were distinct from healthy controls undergoing a pain imagery task. However, because the recalled experiences differed between groups, it remains unclear whether these differences reflect migraine-specific mechanisms or characteristics of the retrieved memories. Nevertheless, the findings suggest that this paradigm may provide a valuable approach for studying the cognitive and emotional aspects of recalled pain experiences in people with migraine. When applied during the interictal phase, this attack imagery paradigm may help disentangle these processes from the sensory and physiological changes accompanying an ongoing migraine attack, although future studies using more closely matched imagery conditions and behavioural assessments will be important to clarify the underlying mechanisms.

Limitations

Regarding the pain imagery task, all participants were informed about it before entering the MR scanner and were given the opportunity to select their pain experiences and practice beforehand. However, it was only performed during the interictal session for people with migraine and the post-ovulation for healthy controls, not allowing for a within-group comparison. Moreover, due to its subjective nature, the content of their imagery may have varied significantly, possibly leading to less consistent or less specific mental representations among healthy controls. Our protocol did not include post-task ratings of the imagery experience, which could have provided insight into these variations by assessing variables such as imagery vividness, recalled pain intensity, emotional valence, and cognitive effort. These measures would also have enabled a quantitative comparison of the recalled experiences between groups. Future studies could incorporate established imagery questionnaires, such as the Vividness of Visual Imagery Questionnaire (Marks 1973) or the Plymouth Sensory Imagery Questionnaire (Andrade et al. 2014), together with visual analogue scales for task-specific ratings. Moreover, because people with migraine recalled previous migraine attacks whereas healthy controls recalled non-migraine head pain, the observed SN differences may reflect characteristics of the recalled experiences (e.g., emotional salience, self-relevance, vividness, or affective content) rather than migraine-specific neural mechanisms. Future studies using more closely matched imagery conditions are needed to disentangle these possibilities.

Furthermore, we did not observe significant associations between the neuroimaging findings and clinical measures indexing typical attack severity (pain intensity), headache-related impact (HIT-6), migraine-related cognitive complaints (Mig-SCog), or session-level affective and anxiety states assessed prior to scanning. However, these measures were collected outside the task context and may not adequately capture momentary subjective experience during imagery. Additionally, the potential influence of having eyes open during resting state versus closed during the pain imagery task cannot be entirely ruled out, and further data is needed to clarify these effects.

The relatively small sample size of the study is an obvious limitation of our study, as it limits the statistical power to detect more subtle between-group differences and may not fully capture the diversity of migraine attack experiences and FC patterns. The sample size was constrained by two main factors: our choice of a very specific cohort of people with migraine (menstrually related episodic migraine without aura), and the longitudinal design requiring each patient to be studied over multiple sessions. Although the choice of a specific cohort reduces variability between patients, thus eliminating potential confounds, it not only limits the sample size but also restricts the generalizability of our findings to other migraine subtypes. On the other hand, the relatively homogeneous cohort also reduced potential sources of biological and clinical variability that are often present in broader migraine cohorts.

Moreover, the data were not always collected within the same migraine cycle for each participant. This may have introduced additional variability due to differences between attacks in the same patient, including pain intensity during the ictal phase.

Regarding the LEiDA analysis, clustering was performed using all fMRI time points across participants, and the k-means algorithm was run with multiple replicates to reduce the influence of convergence to local minima. Importantly, the reported differences in the LEiDA temporal metrics were consistently observed across adjacent clustering solutions (K = 7-9), supporting the stability of these metrics and of the identified salience network state across different levels of clustering granularity. Nevertheless, replication in larger, independent cohorts is needed to establish the reproducibility and generalizability of these LEiDA temporal metrics and the associated findings.

Another limitation is the use of hormonal contraception by some participants, introducing a potential confound, since hormones influence FC (Dubol et al. 2021) and migraine pathophysiology (Ahmad and Rosendale 2022; Krause et al. 2021). Unfortunately, restricting the use of hormonal contraception would have further reduced the sample size. Additionally, we did not measure hormonal levels, which renders uncertainty in the definition of the menstrual cycle in women with natural menstrual cycles.

Conclusions

We employed a case-control longitudinal design to study patients with menstrually related migraine without aura during ictal and interictal phases, along with healthy controls matched for menstrual cycle phase. Resting-state and interictal pain imagery fMRI data were analyzed using time-varying functional connectivity to characterize SN dynamics. Compared to healthy controls, people with migraine exhibited shorter SN dwell times specifically during the pain imagery task, whereas no between-group differences were detected during interictal or ictal resting states. These findings suggest that pain imagery may provide a valuable paradigm for investigating SN dynamics in people with migraine by engaging the cognitive and emotional processing of recalled pain. Future studies using more closely matched imagery conditions are needed to determine whether the observed between-group differences reflect migraine-specific mechanisms or differences in the recalled experiences between groups.
