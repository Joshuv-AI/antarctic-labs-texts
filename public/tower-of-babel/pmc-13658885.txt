Evaluation of Different Methods of Haemoglobin Estimation Among Malaria‐Infected Patients
Authors: Mina Ofosu, Jessica Ntiamoah, Patricia Sam Barko, Prince Elikem Torli
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundHaemoglobin (Hb) estimation is essential for assessing anaemia, particularly in malaria‐endemic settings. Automated Full Blood Count (FBC) analysers, cyanmethemoglobin spectrophotometry, and portable Hb meters are commonly used, but differences in analytical performance may affect measurements.MethodsThis cross‐sectional study compared Hb measurements obtained using an automated FBC analyser, the cyanmethemoglobin method, and a portable Hb meter among 100 malaria‐infected patients aged 16–65 years at Kumasi South Hospital, Ghana. Measurements were compared using Pearson's correlation coefficient, intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC), Lin's concordance correlation coefficient (CCC), Bland–Altman analysis, percentage error, and Passing–Bablok regression.ResultsThe three methods showed very strong correlations (r = 0.955–0.992, p < 0.001), excellent ICC (single measures = 0.969; average measures = 0.990), and high concordance (CCC = 0.95–0.99). Mean biases were −0.06 g/dL for FBC analyser versus portable Hb meter, −0.25 g/dL for FBC analyser versus cyanmethemoglobin, and −0.20 g/dL for portable Hb meter versus cyanmethemoglobin. The corresponding 95% limits of agreement were −0.59 to 0.47, −1.48 to 0.98, and −1.35 to 0.95 g/dL, respectively. Passing–Bablok slopes were 1.00 for all comparisons. The FBC analyser and portable Hb meter showed the closest agreement.ConclusionThe three methods demonstrated good agreement and broadly comparable Hb measurements. The findings support the practical use of portable Hb meters where point‐of‐care testing is required, while recognising that agreement does not establish diagnostic accuracy or analytical validation.Keywords: agreement, cyanmethemoglobin, full blood count analyser, haemoglobin estimation, haemoglobin meter, malaria

Introduction

Haemoglobin (Hb) is an iron‐containing porphyrin protein responsible for transporting oxygen from the lungs to body tissues, thereby supporting cellular energy metabolism [1]. In addition to oxygen transport, haemoglobin contributes to carbon dioxide transport, hydrogen‐ion buffering, and nitric oxide metabolism, highlighting its physiological importance and the central role of haemoglobin estimation in the diagnosis and management of anaemia and other haematological disorders [2]. Anaemia, defined as a haemoglobin concentration below the level required to meet an individual's physiological needs, remains the most common haematological disorder worldwide, affecting approximately one‐third of the global population [3]. According to the World Health Organization (WHO), haemoglobin concentration remains the primary laboratory indicator for diagnosing and classifying anaemia and for monitoring treatment response and disease severity [4, 5]. Although clinical features such as fatigue, pallor, and tachycardia may suggest anaemia, laboratory confirmation is essential for accurate diagnosis and appropriate clinical management [6]. Anaemia remains an important public health concern in Ghana, particularly in malaria‐endemic settings where malaria‐associated reductions in haemoglobin make reliable haemoglobin estimation important for patient assessment and management [7]. Following transmission by infected Anopheles mosquitoes, Plasmodium parasites undergo hepatic and erythrocytic stages of development, ultimately invading and destroying red blood cells while impairing erythropoiesis [8, 9]. These pathophysiological processes result in haemolysis, splenic sequestration of infected and uninfected erythrocytes, and reduced haemoglobin concentrations, making accurate haemoglobin estimation an integral component of malaria diagnosis, clinical assessment, and therapeutic monitoring [10, 11]. Furthermore, malaria‐associated alterations in blood composition may influence haemoglobin measurement and contribute to variation between analytical methods [12]. Several methods are currently available for haemoglobin estimation, including automated Full Blood Count (FBC) analysers, the cyanmethemoglobin method, and portable haemoglobin (Hb) meters. The cyanmethemoglobin (Drabkin's) method remains the internationally recognised established reference method for haemoglobin determination and is recommended by both the World Health Organization and the International Council for Standardization in Haematology because of its standardized spectrophotometric principle and established analytical performance [13, 14]. Automated FBC analysers provide rapid, precise, and reproducible measurement of haemoglobin together with comprehensive haematological parameters using technologies such as electrical impedance, flow cytometry, and light scattering [15, 16]. However, their widespread use in resource‐limited settings may be constrained by equipment costs, infrastructure requirements, maintenance demands, and the need for trained personnel [17, 18]. Conversely, portable Hb meters have become increasingly popular because they provide rapid, convenient, and point‐of‐care haemoglobin estimation with minimal infrastructure requirements, although concerns remain regarding their comparability with laboratory‐based methods [19, 20]. Previous studies have compared different haemoglobin estimation methods in clinical and field settings, demonstrating varying levels of correlation and agreement depending on the analytical method, sample type, and study population [21, 22]. However, evidence comparing these methods specifically among malaria‐infected patients remains limited, particularly in malaria‐endemic and resource‐limited settings where accurate, affordable, and practical haemoglobin estimation is essential for patient care. Given that malaria‐associated physiological changes may influence haemoglobin measurement, further evaluation of method agreement in this population is warranted. Therefore, this study compared haemoglobin concentrations obtained using an automated FBC analyser, the cyanmethemoglobin method, and a portable Hb meter among malaria‐infected patients to evaluate their agreement, comparability, and practical applicability.

Materials and Methods

Study Design and Setting

A hospital‐based cross‐sectional study was conducted among malaria‐infected patients attending Kumasi South Hospital, Kumasi, Ghana, between April 2025 and June 2025. Kumasi South Hospital is a secondary‐level public healthcare facility that serves as a referral centre for surrounding communities and routinely provides malaria diagnosis, treatment, and comprehensive laboratory services. The study was designed to compare haemoglobin (Hb) concentrations obtained using an automated Full Blood Count (FBC) analyser, the cyanmethemoglobin‐based established reference method, and a portable Hb meter.

Study Population

The study included laboratory‐confirmed malaria‐infected patients aged 16–65 years who attended Kumasi South Hospital during the study period and provided written informed consent. Patients who had received blood transfusions within the preceding 3 months, those with known haematological disorders unrelated to malaria, or individuals with incomplete clinical or laboratory data were excluded from the study.

The calculated minimum sample size was 106 participants. A total of 100 eligible participants were ultimately recruited, representing approximately 94% of the calculated sample size. This slight shortfall was due to participant availability during the predefined study period.

Participants were recruited using a consecutive convenience sampling approach, whereby all eligible malaria‐infected patients who presented to the hospital during the study period and met the inclusion criteria were invited to participate until the target sample size was achieved. Although probability sampling may reduce selection bias, this approach was considered appropriate because recruitment was restricted to patients with confirmed malaria presenting during the study period.

Blood Sample Collection

Approximately 5 mL of venous blood was collected aseptically from each participant by trained laboratory personnel using standard phlebotomy procedures. Blood samples were dispensed into ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA) anticoagulant tubes, labelled with unique identification numbers, and transported promptly to the laboratory for analysis. All samples were analysed as soon as possible after collection to minimise pre‐analytical variation.

Laboratory Analysis

Haemoglobin Estimation Using the Automated Full Blood Count (FBC) Analyser

Approximately 2 mL of EDTA‐anticoagulated whole blood was gently mixed by repeated inversion to ensure sample homogeneity before analysis. Haemoglobin concentration was measured using the Sysmex XN‐550 automated Full Blood Count analyser (Sysmex Corporation, Kobe, Japan) according to the manufacturer's operating instructions. The analyser determines haemoglobin concentration using the sodium lauryl sulfate (SLS)‐haemoglobin method while simultaneously providing complete blood count parameters through impedance and flow cytometric technologies. Following sample aspiration through the analyser probe, haemoglobin values were automatically generated and recorded in g/dL for statistical analysis.

Haemoglobin Estimation Using the Cyanmethemoglobin Method

Haemoglobin concentration was determined using the cyanmethemoglobin (Drabkin's) reference method in accordance with the recommendations of the International Council for Standardization in Haematology. Three test tubes labelled Blank (B), Standard (S), and Test (T) were prepared, and 5 mL of Drabkin's reagent was dispensed into each tube. Twenty microlitres (20 μL) of whole blood was added to the tube labelled Test and mixed thoroughly. The reaction mixture was allowed to stand at room temperature for 15 min to ensure complete conversion of haemoglobin derivatives (except sulfhaemoglobin) to cyanmethemoglobin.

The spectrophotometer was calibrated at 540 nm using the reagent blank before measuring the absorbance of the standard and test samples. Haemoglobin concentration was calculated using the equation: Hb (g/dL) = Absorbance of testAbsorbance of standard× Concentration of standard.

The procedure was repeated for all study samples.

Haemoglobin Estimation Using the Portable Hb Meter

Haemoglobin concentration was measured using a portable Hb meter (HemoCue Hb 301, HemoCue AB, Ängelholm, Sweden) according to the manufacturer's instructions. Fresh EDTA‐anticoagulated whole blood was applied to a compatible disposable test strip inserted into the analyser, and the haemoglobin concentration was displayed electronically within seconds and recorded in g/dL. The device was checked using the manufacturer's quality‐control procedures before routine sample analysis and maintained according to the manufacturer's recommendations throughout the study.

Anaemia Classification

Anaemia severity was classified according to the World Health Organization (WHO) haemoglobin cut‐offs for adults aged 15–65 years. The WHO thresholds were applied directly to haemoglobin values obtained from all three methods (Sysmex‐FBC analyser, portable Hb meter, and cyanmethemoglobin method) to grade anaemia severity [4].

Quality Assurance

All laboratory analyses were performed by qualified laboratory personnel following standard operating procedures. The automated FBC analyser underwent routine maintenance, calibration, and internal quality‐control verification according to the manufacturer's recommendations before participant sample analysis. For the cyanmethemoglobin method, freshly prepared Drabkin's reagent, reagent blanks, and haemoglobin standards were used to ensure assay accuracy, and the spectrophotometer was calibrated and blanked before sample analysis. The portable Hb meter was operated according to the manufacturer's instructions, and quality‐control procedures were conducted before each testing session to ensure consistent instrument performance. Duplicate measurements were performed as part of routine quality assurance, and their mean values were used for analysis; formal repeatability and coefficient‐of‐variation analyses were not undertaken.

Data Entry and Statistical Analysis

Data were entered into a computerized database, cleaned, and validated before analysis. Statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics version 26.0 and R software version 4.2. Continuous variables were assessed for normality and summarized as mean ± standard deviation, while categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages. Pearson's correlation coefficient (r) was used to assess the strength of the linear relationship between haemoglobin measurements obtained by the different methods. Agreement among the methods was assessed using the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) based on a two‐way mixed‐effects model with absolute agreement and Lin's concordance correlation coefficient (CCC). Pairwise differences between methods were evaluated using one‐way analysis of variance (ANOVA), followed by appropriate post hoc pairwise comparisons. Bland–Altman analysis was performed for each pair of haemoglobin estimation methods to assess systematic bias and agreement at the individual measurement level. Mean bias and the corresponding 95% limits of agreement (LoA) were calculated and presented graphically using Bland–Altman plots. Percentage error was also calculated, and Passing–Bablok regression analysis was performed for each pairwise comparison. The regression slope and intercept were reported for each comparison. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05. Results were presented in tables and figures, as appropriate.

Discussion

Principal Findings

This study compared haemoglobin concentrations obtained using an automated FBC analyser, a portable Hb meter, and the cyanmethemoglobin method among malaria‐infected patients. The three methods demonstrated strong linear relationships and high overall agreement. The closest agreement was observed between the automated FBC analyser and the portable Hb meter, while the cyanmethemoglobin method produced slightly higher haemoglobin concentrations on average than the other two methods. Bland–Altman analysis demonstrated small mean biases across the three pairwise comparisons, although the width of the limits of agreement differed between method pairs. These findings indicate that the three methods produced broadly comparable haemoglobin measurements in the study population, while also demonstrating that correlation alone is insufficient to establish agreement between analytical methods or to infer interchangeability of measurements.

Age, Gender, and Anaemia Severity Specific Performance

Haemoglobin measurements obtained using the three methods were generally comparable across the age groups examined. The statistically significant differences observed in the 30–39‐year group and among males aged ≥ 60 years should, however, be interpreted cautiously because some age‐ and sex‐specific subgroups contained relatively few participants, reducing the precision of subgroup estimates and increasing the possibility of chance findings. Among females aged 30–39 years, the cyanmethemoglobin method produced slightly higher haemoglobin values than the automated FBC analyser and portable Hb meter. This age group includes many women of reproductive age, among whom anaemia and iron deficiency remain important public health concerns, partly because of menstrual iron loss and increased iron requirements [2]. The observed differences in this subgroup should not, however, be interpreted as evidence of a systematic age‐ or sex‐specific measurement effect because the study was not primarily powered for stratified comparisons. Moreover, differences between haemoglobin measurement methods have been reported in previous method‐comparison studies, supporting the need to interpret subgroup variations within the broader analytical agreement between methods [23]. Anaemia severity classification showed that the distribution of anaemia categories was consistent across the three methods, with the majority of participants having no anaemia (61.0% across all methods) and severe anaemia being uncommon (0–2 participants per method). The consistency of classification across methods supports the overall agreement observed in the study. The small number of participants with severe anaemia prevents a formal subgroup‐specific agreement analysis for this category, as such analyses would be underpowered and at increased risk of Type I error. Among males aged ≥ 60 years, the automated FBC analyser produced slightly higher haemoglobin values than the cyanmethemoglobin method. Age‐related changes in haematopoiesis and haemoglobin concentrations have been documented in older adults and may contribute to biological variation in haemoglobin measurements [24, 25]. However, given the relatively small number of participants in this subgroup, this finding should be regarded as exploratory rather than evidence of a consistent age‐related analytical effect. Overall, the broadly comparable measurements across most age and sex groups and the consistent distribution of anaemia severity categories support the comparability of the three methods across the demographic and clinical spectrum of the study population, while the subgroup differences highlight the importance of cautious interpretation when sample sizes are limited.

Agreement, Correlation, and Concordance Among Methods

The overall pattern of agreement indicates that the three haemoglobin estimation methods produced broadly comparable measurements, with the closest agreement observed between the automated FBC analyser and portable Hb meter. The greater variability observed in comparisons involving the cyanmethemoglobin method may reflect differences in analytical principles, calibration, and measurement platforms, as reported in previous haemoglobin method‐comparison studies [12]. The small mean biases and percentage errors observed across the comparisons further indicate limited systematic differences between the methods, although the wider limits of agreement involving cyanmethemoglobin suggest greater variability at the individual measurement level. The clinical relevance of these differences should therefore be considered in relation to the intended application and relevant allowable‐error criteria [26, 27]. The Passing–Bablok regression results provide additional support for the absence of proportional differences between the methods, as the slope was 1.00 for all three pairwise comparisons. The observed intercepts, particularly in comparisons involving cyanmethemoglobin, suggest the presence of some fixed differences between methods. Together with the Bland–Altman findings, these results indicate that the differences between methods were largely systematic rather than progressively increasing across the haemoglobin measurement range. The strong Pearson correlations, ICC, and Lin's concordance correlation coefficients are consistent with previous evaluations of laboratory‐based and point‐of‐care haemoglobin systems [2, 13]. However, correlation measures the strength of association rather than the closeness of individual measurements and therefore does not establish agreement or interchangeability [26]. Likewise, concordance and reliability do not establish diagnostic accuracy against an independent reference standard. This distinction is important because analytical agreement and clinical accuracy represent different concepts. Although the three methods demonstrated good agreement and strong correlations, the small systematic differences observed between methods—particularly involving the cyanmethemoglobin method—mean that the methods should not automatically be considered interchangeable. Interchangeability would require that the methods produce results sufficiently close that replacing one method with another would not affect clinical decisions. The observed mean differences of 0.2–0.25 g/dL, while small, could influence anaemia classification when haemoglobin values are close to diagnostic thresholds. Taken together, the complementary analyses support good agreement among the three methods within the study population, with the FBC analyser and portable Hb meter showing the closest comparability. These findings support their potential utility under the conditions evaluated but should not be interpreted as formal diagnostic validation or evidence of universal interchangeability.

Implications for Clinical Practice

The findings have practical relevance for haemoglobin testing in malaria‐endemic and resource‐limited settings, where the choice of measurement method may be influenced by laboratory infrastructure, turnaround time, and availability of trained personnel. The close agreement between the portable Hb meter and automated FBC analyser suggests that point‐of‐care haemoglobin measurement may provide a practical option where access to automated laboratory analysers is limited, consistent with previous evidence on point‐of‐care haemoglobin systems [2, 13]. However, the observed differences between methods indicate that results should not automatically be considered interchangeable across all clinical circumstances. The choice of method should therefore be guided by the intended application and available resources. Automated FBC analysers provide broader haematological information but require laboratory infrastructure, maintenance, and trained personnel, whereas portable Hb meters offer greater portability and operational simplicity. In situations where haemoglobin values are close to clinically important decision thresholds, awareness of method‐specific differences is particularly important, and confirmation using an established laboratory method may be appropriate. Thus, the present findings support the practical use of these methods within their respective testing environments but do not establish universal interchangeability or diagnostic equivalence.

Limitations

The study was conducted at a single healthcare facility using a consecutive convenience sampling approach, which may limit the generalisability of the findings to other populations and healthcare settings. The sample size was determined using a prevalence‐based formula rather than an agreement‐specific approach, and the final sample was slightly below the calculated target, which may have limited statistical power for some analyses. Although the overall sample size was adequate for the primary analyses, the relatively small number of participants in some age‐ and sex‐specific subgroups limited the precision of subgroup comparisons and increased the risk of Type I error (false positive findings) when interpreting subgroup‐specific differences. Important factors that may influence haemoglobin concentrations, including malaria parasite density, nutritional status, hydration status, menstrual status, and coexisting medical conditions, were not assessed. Although participants were classified according to WHO anaemia severity categories, the small number of participants with severe anaemia (≤ 2 participants per method) prevents a meaningful subgroup‐specific agreement analysis. Conducting such analyses with very small sample sizes would increase the risk of Type I error and produce unstable estimates that could not be reliably interpreted. In addition, analytical precision, including repeatability and coefficient of variation, was not formally evaluated, and long‐term instrument performance and inter‐laboratory reproducibility were not assessed. These limitations should be considered when interpreting the findings and when designing larger multicentre studies of haemoglobin method comparability.

Conclusion

The automated FBC analyser, portable Hb meter, and cyanmethemoglobin method demonstrated strong concordance and generally small mean differences for haemoglobin estimation among malaria‐infected patients. The closest agreement was observed between the automated FBC analyser and the portable Hb meter, while the cyanmethemoglobin method produced slightly higher haemoglobin concentrations on average. Bland–Altman analysis confirmed relatively small mean biases, although the limits of agreement indicate that differences between individual measurements may be greater than suggested by mean comparisons alone. These findings support the comparability of the three haemoglobin estimation methods within the study population but should not be interpreted as evidence of diagnostic accuracy or full analytical validation. Selection of a haemoglobin estimation method should consider the intended clinical or screening application, analytical characteristics, cost, and available infrastructure. Larger multicentre studies with broader populations and formal assessment of analytical precision and clinical performance are warranted to further evaluate the interchangeability of these methods.
