Agonal time in donation after circulatory death lung donors and associations with early recipient pulmonary allograft function and long-term post-transplant outcomes
Authors: Justin T Wang, Katherine G Phillips, Kelly Drozdowicz, Nader Moazami, Luis F Angel, Travis Geraci, Stephanie Chang, Justin Chan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundUptake of donation after circulatory death (DCD) in lung transplantation is limited by concerns surrounding ischemic injury. This study aimed to assess the effects of prolonged donor agonal time during DCD procurement on recipient outcomes.MethodsOne thousand three hundred five DCD lung recipients from January 2016 to June 2024 from the Scientific Registry of Transplant Recipients were analyzed. Donor agonal time was measured from the start of the agonal phase (SpO2<80% or systolic blood pressure<80 mmHg) to the declaration of death. A nonlinear 3-knot spline was fit for agonal time against composite outcomes of impaired allograft oxygenation (PaO2/FiO2≤200 or ECMO support at 72 hours post-transplant) and prolonged respiratory recovery (intubation or iNO requirement at 72 hours post-transplant, intubation for ≥5 days, or reintubation). Cohorts of recipients from “long-agonal” (n = 837) and “short-agonal” (n = 468) donors were defined by an agonal time threshold of 15 minutes.ResultsSplines of agonal time against either composite outcome were statistically insignificant after multivariate correction. A trend towards impaired allograft oxygenation was observed with increased agonal time until an inflection point at 13.9 minutes. In the cohort analysis, long-agonal recipients displayed higher rates of PaO2/FiO2≤200 (24% vs 17%, p = 0.017) at 72 h post-transplant and higher rates of long-term graft failure (p = 0.026) and retransplantation (p = 0.01), but equivalent overall survival (p = 0.087).ConclusionsProlonged DCD donor agonal time is not significantly associated with impaired post-transplant recovery following DCD lung transplantation. Although prolonged agonal time was associated with increased graft failure, the exact contribution of donor agonal time to this outcome remains unclear.Keywords: Lung transplantation, Donation after circulatory death, Donor agonal time, Primary graft dysfunction, Long-term lung transplant outcomes

Background

For patients with end-stage lung disease, lung transplantation (LTx) remains the only curative treatment. In 2023, the OPTN observed the highest number of deceased-donor lung procurements and lung transplants overall. Nevertheless, a discrepancy remains between the supply of donor lungs and the demand of transplant recipients, with the number of candidates on the lung transplant waiting list remaining nearly constant since 2011.1

Donation after circulatory death (DCD) is a procurement method that widens the pool of available lung donors; however, concerns in DCD LTx have been raised surrounding warm ischemia time (WIT) during the progression of the donor to circulatory death (i.e., donor agonal time) and subsequent reperfusion injury.2 Negative organ procurement runs resulting in rejected lungs after donation are also more common for DCD donors compared to DBD donors.3, 4

We extended the scope and follow-up period of prior studies on donor agonal time by assessing donor agonal time in the U.S. national transplant registry for all DCD lung transplants.5, 6 We evaluated pre-transplant donor and recipient characteristics, measures of short-term post-transplant allograft function, and long-term recipient outcomes.

Materials and methods

Data source, study population, and agonal time definition

The dataset examined in this study was sourced from the Scientific Registry of Transplant Recipients (SRTR). Parameters of this dataset are described in the OPTN/SRTR Annual Data Report.1 The study was deemed exempt from institutional review board approval. The study population included all adult (age ≥ 18 years) DCD lung donors in the SRTR database, restricted to single-organ lung transplants from January 2016 to June 2024 (n = 1536) (Figure S1). Donor agonal time was calculated beginning at the start of the agonal phase (SpO2 < 80% or systolic blood pressure < 80 mmHg) and concluding with the declaration of death consistent with the OPTN definition. Recorded agonal times greater than 200 or less than 0 minute were assumed to be erroneous (n = 231) and were excluded, leaving a final cohort of 1,305 patients for analysis.

Natural cubic spline analysis

To quantify the relationship between agonal time and short-term post-transplant outcomes, a natural cubic spline analysis was performed of agonal time against 2 composite outcomes. The first composite outcome was a proxy for impaired allograft oxygenation, defined as a PaO2/FiO2 (P/F) ratio less than or equal to 200 and/or a requirement of extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (ECMO) support. For the first composite outcome, 473 cases were excluded for missingness of either recorded P/F ratio or ECMO support at 72 hours post-transplant. The second composite outcome was defined as a proxy for prolonged respiratory recovery, defined as any of the following post-transplant: persistent intubation or inhaled nitric oxide (iNO) support at 72 hours post-transplant, intubation for ≥5 days, or reintubation. For the second composite outcome, 44 cases were excluded for missingness of at least one of the composite outcome criteria. All splines were fit for 3 knots assigned to the 10th, 50th, and 90th percentiles of agonal time. Splines were adjusted for donor covariates of age, sex, a smoking history of more than 20 pack-years, cause of death, pre-procurement donor P/F ratio, unilateral vs bilateral transplant, recipient lung allocation score (LAS) at match time, and use of EVLP. For adjustment, continuous covariates were held constant at the mean and binary covariates at the mode. Inflection points were identified at agonal times corresponding to changes in the sign of the first derivative of the spline. Predictive significance of covariates and the spline fit for donor agonal time was assessed with analysis of variance.

Long-agonal and short-agonal cohort analysis

Based on the cubic spline analysis for the composite outcome of impaired oxygenation, an exploratory, data-driven threshold for defining 2 agonal time cohorts was identified. Cases were categorized as either “long-agonal donor” cases with donor agonal time of greater than or equal to 15 minutes or “short-agonal donor” cases with an agonal time of less than 15 minutes.

Short-term recipient lung allograft function was assessed at 72 hours post-transplant via endpoints of intubation status, iNO support, ECMO support, ventilator FiO2, P/F ratio, incidence of PaO2/FiO2≤200, and both impaired oxygenation and prolonged respiratory recovery composite outcomes.7 Other short-term outcomes included total length of hospital stay and incidence of pre-discharge dialysis or airway dehiscence. Long-term outcomes included all-cause recipient survival (excluding retransplantation), allograft failure, and retransplantation events. Although allograft failure is reported by each transplant center individually, the definition by the OPTN includes removal of the allograft or if the recipient is placed on a chronic allograft support system.

Statistical analysis

Donor and recipient characteristics were compared across agonal time cohorts through the Wilcoxon rank-sum test for continuous variables and either Pearson’s χ2 test without continuity correction or Fisher exact test, as appropriate. Numerical characteristics were reported as median and IQR, while categorical characteristics were reported as proportions and raw fractions. Short-term recipient endpoints were compared using equivalent statistical methods.

Long-term recipient outcomes were assessed through survival analysis via Kaplan-Meier curves. Recipients lost to follow-up were excluded; remaining recipients were followed from transplant date until death, allograft failure, retransplantation, or censorship on July 3, 2024. Survival curve confidence intervals were calculated via log-transformation of Greenwood’s method, and survival curves were compared between cohorts using the log-rank test.8 All statistical analyses were conducted in RStudio version 2026.08.1.

Results

Donor and recipient characteristics

After applying exclusion criteria, 1,305 DCD lung transplants in the SRTR database from 2016 to 2024 were analyzed. Based on the set agonal time threshold, 468 cases were stratified as “short-agonal donors” (35.9%), and 837 cases were stratified as “long-agonal” donors (64.1%). The median donor agonal time was 17 minutes (IQR:[12, 23]) (Figure S2).

Characteristics of DCD lung donors are documented in Table 1, and characteristics of DCD lung recipients are documented in Table 2. Short-agonal donors were more likely to be male (63% vs 57%, p = 0.029) and younger (39 vs 40 years, p = 0.048). Otherwise, both agonal time cohorts displayed equivalent demographic characteristics, pre-transplant disease burden, and cold ischemia time (CIT) (7.5 vs 7.3 hours, p = 0.77) (Table 1, Table 2).

Natural cubic spline analysis

The overall prevalence of the composite outcome for impaired oxygenation was 39.7% (331/832), and for prolonged respiratory recovery was 56.9% (717/1,261). For both the impaired oxygenation (p = 0.356) and the prolonged respiratory recovery (p = 0.266) composite outcomes, splines for donor agonal time did not achieve statistical significance (Table S5). Qualitatively, spline analysis revealed a nonlinear relationship between agonal time and the composite outcome for impaired oxygenation. Before an inflection point at 13.9 minutes, prolonged agonal time was associated with impaired oxygenation. This association plateaued until a second inflection point at 40.3 minutes, beyond which the effect of agonal time appeared to increase. Increased agonal time was associated with reduced recipient P/F ratio at 72 hours until 21.1 minutes and plateauing until 59.3 minutes, beyond which the effect of agonal time became more prominent. Increasing agonal time was associated with increased ECMO support at 72 hours post-transplant up to 10.9 minutes and from 27.4 to 81.3 minutes. However, for agonal times from 10.9 to 27.4 minutes and beyond 81.3 minutes, a negative association with increased agonal time was observed (Figure 1). Increased agonal time had a modestly positive association with the prolonged respiratory recovery composite outcome until 48 minutes, beyond which prolonged agonal times were less significant. However, prolonged agonal time was not associated with any components of the prolonged respiratory recovery composite outcome (Figure 2).

Agonal cohort analysis

Short-term outcomes

Short-term outcomes are shown in Table 3. Similar rates of ventilator dependence, ECMO support, and iNO requirements were seen. The long-agonal cohort demonstrated increased rates of P/F≤200 at 72 hours post-transplant (24% vs 17%, p = 0.017), but there was no difference in the incidence of impaired oxygenation (37% vs 41%, p = 0.23) or prolonged respiratory recovery (55% vs 58%, p = 0.25) by composite outcome criteria.

Long-term outcomes

No difference in recipient survival was observed between agonal time cohorts (p = 0.09) (Figure 3). However, there was a statistically significant increase in the incidence of post-transplant graft failure (p = 0.026) and retransplantation (p = 0.01) in the long-agonal cohort (Figures 4 and 5).

At an aggregated median follow-up time of 14.2 months, recipient survival was 84.8% (95% CI:[81.3%, 88.5%]) in the short-agonal cohort and 81.7% (95% CI:[78.8%, 84.9%]) in the long-agonal cohort. At the same timepoint, freedom from graft failure was 93.6% (95% CI:[91.0%, 96.3%]) for the short-agonal cohort and 91.7% (95% CI:[89.5%, 93.4%]) for the long-agonal cohort, while no retransplantation events were reported in the short-agonal cohort and 5 events were reported in the long-agonal cohort.

Discussion

Natural cubic spline analysis

Extended agonal time had a weakly positive association with prolonged respiratory recovery but was not correlated with any of the constituent endpoints of this composite outcome. A possible inflection point was identified at approximately 15 minutes for the impact of agonal time on impaired short-term allograft oxygenation. Increasing agonal times up to 15 minutes may also be associated with increased ECMO support. However, this effect plateaued after 15 minutes, with prolonged agonal times associated with a decreased ECMO requirement. The proposed inflection point should be interpreted conservatively as an exploratory stratification due to the limitations of data missingness for the spline analysis. Moreover, the few donors with extremely long agonal times may magnify incidental differences in recipient post-transplant management, including ECMO use. These inconsistent findings suggest that prolonged donor agonal time is not significantly associated with impaired post-transplant outcomes.

Agonal cohort analysis

After stratifying by the hypothesized agonal time cutoff, the long-agonal recipient cohort had an increased rate of P/F≤200 in the short term and increased rates of graft failure and retransplantation in the long term, but overall survival was equivalent across agonal time cohorts. The outcome of graft failure is individually reported by each transplant center but conceivably encompasses retransplantation events. As there was no significant difference in the prevalence of either the impaired oxygenation outcome or prolonged respiratory recovery outcome between agonal cohorts, it is likely that there is no statistically significant association of prolonged agonal time with short-term post-transplant outcomes of DCD lung transplant recipients. The impaired short-term P/F ratio in the long-agonal cohort should be interpreted conservatively and warrants further study to clarify contributing mechanisms and recipient risk factors. There was no difference in the use of EVLP between short- and long-agonal cohorts, suggesting that within the analyzable registry population, the clinical approach to DCD lung procurement and organ preservation is not affected by donor agonal time.

This study aligns with the published literature, which shows no association of donor agonal time with recipient mortality.5, 6, 9 A recent study using the ISHLT definition of functional WIT (fWIT), beginning when systolic blood pressure falls below 50 mmHg, examined the effect of prolonged DCD donor progression to asystole on short- and long-term outcomes and reported an increased rate of PGD-3 in recipients of lungs from DCD donors with fWIT beyond 15 minutes. Although this finding preliminarily aligns with the impaired P/F ratio found in the long-agonal cohort of this analysis, it is not possible to make a direct comparison between the findings of this study and those described in this recent report due to the differential definitions of fWIT.10, 11

Concerns have been raised surrounding the negative effects of warm ischemia during DCD procurement. Studies in DCD liver, heart, and kidney transplantation suggest that prolonged WIT impairs long-term transplant outcomes, with WIT carrying a larger effect than agonal time alone.12, 13, 14 Guidelines suggest that impaired viability of DCD hearts or livers becomes especially significant as WIT approaches 30 minutes.15, 16 Lungs may exhibit unique compensatory mechanisms for prolonged donor agonal times and WIT, with preservation of lung graft function reported at WIT of up to one hour; passive diffusion of ventilated oxygen to alveolar cells could attenuate ischemic injury.17 These mechanisms contribute to the success of cadaveric LTx and could explain the equivalent post-transplant outcomes observed in this analysis.18, 19

This study also identified equivalent overall survival but marginally increased graft failure in recipients of long-agonal donors compared to short-agonal donors. The findings of equivalent long-term recipient survival between agonal time cohorts extends prior studies reporting equal post-transplant survival for DCD and DBD recipients.6, 20, 21, 22, 23 Recent single- and multi-center studies have likewise reported equivalent rates of long-term graft failure and incidence of chronic lung allograft dysfunction between DCD and DBD recipients.24, 25 Prolonged WIT during DCD procurement leads to a generalized inflammatory response, which could contribute to long-term graft dysfunction.26, 27, 28 Alternatively, a proportion of short-agonal DCD donors could display physiology similar to DBD donors but are classified as DCD donors due to equivocal results of brain-death testing (e.g., uncertain cerebral perfusion) or donor family preference. Lungs procured from this subset of DCD donors could exhibit short- and long-term graft function comparable to DBD lungs. Nevertheless, it is difficult to isolate the contribution of extended agonal time alone when evaluating long-term outcomes, limiting the ability to establish a true causal pathway from agonal time to long-term recipient endpoints—the long-term findings of this analysis should be interpreted as hypothesis-generating. Variations in pre-procurement donor ECMO use or protocols for the withdrawal of life-sustaining treatment could further affect donor lung physiology. Such considerations are particularly relevant considering database limitations in capturing specific etiologies of graft failure, configurations of pre-procurement donor support, and parameters of the DCD procurement process. Further research is warranted to better define the mechanisms driving long-term outcomes among DBD, short-agonal DCD, and long-agonal DCD donors.

Emerging data have also suggested that lung preservation at 10°C is noninferior to conventional storage on ice.29 Early studies of static 10°C storage or hypothermic oxygenated perfusion indicate that these methods could also attenuate ischemia–reperfusion injury by augmenting native mitochondrial activity.30, 31 The majority of transplant cases in this analysis predated widespread use of static 10°C storage, and it is unclear if widespread use of these preservation techniques would significantly affect our findings. Further study is required to assess if methods of 10°C preservation may diminish the effects of prolonged agonal time identified in this analysis.

Donor agonal time cannot be practically controlled, and accurately predicting the course of donor progression to asystole remains challenging. Machine learning models have been developed to prognosticate donor progression during the agonal phase, with factors such as brainstem reflexes, serum laboratory values, and arterial oxygen saturation demonstrating the most predictive impact.32 Transplant teams should emphasize these factors during donor evaluation, rather than the duration of agonal time alone, and utilize such tools to risk-stratify the viability of lungs from DCD donors.

Strengths, weaknesses, and conclusions

Strengths of this study include the analysis of a large multi-center cohort, standardized data collection, and a long follow-up timeframe inherent to the SRTR database. A robust analysis was also conducted of the effect of prolonged donor agonal time as a continuous variable.

This study has several limitations. The aggregate nature of the SRTR database obscures inter-institutional differences in the aggressiveness of donor lung usage. Limitations in registry data prevented assessment of PGD due to the lack of data regarding radiological confirmation of pulmonary infiltrates as a diagnostic criterion. The composite outcome of impaired allograft oxygenation was assessed as a surrogate endpoint, but more granular studies are necessary to fully describe the relationship between extended donor agonal time and PGD.

The agonal time definition used in this study aligns with the OPTN definition documented in the HRSA Glossary and encoded in the SRTR database. As the SRTR database does not capture specific timepoints in the DCD procurement process, fWIT or ISHLT-defined intervals could not be assessed (Figure 6). This constraint limits comparability to other studies using different agonal time definitions. Moreover, the registry does not capture the contribution of EVLP time to overall CIT. As a result, this analysis could not assess relationships between changes in the use of EVLP and post-transplant outcomes. A separate analysis using the ISHLT definition of agonal phase identified a similar short-term outcome of reduced P/F ratio with prolonged agonal time; however, differing agonal time definitions preclude direct comparison of such findings.10 This limitation highlights the need for international standardization of the definition of fWIT and agonal phase to improve the quality of future research, similar to current efforts in refining definitions of primary graft dysfunction.33

Graphical representation of ISHLT-defined intervals during DCD procurement. “Agonal time,” as defined in this study, corresponds to the interval between SpO2 < 80% (or SBP < 80 mmHg, not pictured) and declaration of death as identified on this figure. Total functional warm ischemia time, in most studies, corresponds to the sum of Interval 1 and Interval 4.

Another limitation is the high missingness of P/F data at 72 hours post-transplant in the cohort, which limited the sample size eligible for spline-based analyses for the composite outcome of impaired oxygenation. To quantify the effect of missingness, recipient and donor characteristics and post-transplant outcomes were compared between included and excluded cases for the composite outcome of impaired oxygenation. A higher average CIT was identified in included donors, but agonal times were equivalent between included and omitted donors. Compared to excluded cases, included recipients were more likely to require life support at transplant and have a pre-transplant diagnosis of restrictive lung disease but displayed only marginally higher LAS. Included recipients also displayed poorer short-term post-transplant outcomes and long-term survival (Table S1, S2, Figure S3). A full analysis is included in the data supplement. The secondary outcome of prolonged respiratory recovery was also assessed to evaluate a composite outcome with less missingness. The statistical insignificance of spline fits to both composite outcomes suggests no significant association of prolonged agonal time with post-transplant outcomes. The inability to confirm the effect of such missing data remains a significant limitation, and the study findings are otherwise suggestive of a negative result.

In conclusion, this study serves as a large-scale analysis of the associations of prolonged donor agonal time on short- and long-term recipient outcomes in DCD LTx. In general, prolonged donor agonal time is not associated with significant changes in short-term post-transplant recipient outcomes. Although an association of prolonged agonal time with increased long-term graft failure was observed, the isolated effect of agonal time on long-term outcomes could not be directly studied, warranting further analysis. Standardized definitions of agonal time and further research in DCD LTx is indicated to fully characterize the relationship between pre-procurement donor physiology and post-transplant outcomes, with the goal of informing interventions to improve allograft function, especially for high-risk recipients.
