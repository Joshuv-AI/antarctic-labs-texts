Factors influencing residents’ continued use intention of smart community platforms: an integrated theoretical perspective
Authors: Juan Wang, Xianghui Kong, Xiaojing Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAs smart community platforms (SCPs) become integral to digital governance, understanding the factors influencing user persistence is increasingly important. This study develops an integrated framework by synthesizing the Unified Theory of Acceptance and Use of Technology (UTAUT) and Innovation Diffusion Theory (IDT). Data from 515 valid questionnaires collected in China were analyzed using a dual-stage PLS-SEM and Artificial Neural Network (ANN) approach. The results indicate that while Performance Expectancy and Relative Advantage form the cognitive basis of utility, Satisfaction serves as the pivotal affective driver for continued use. Notably, the ANN analysis reveals a non-linear importance hierarchy, identifying Social Influence as a more potent factor than traditional linear estimates suggest. This study contributes to the literature by moving beyond simple linear assumptions to provide a deeper understanding of user retention, offering practical strategies for the long-term sustainability of smart community engagement.Keywords: Smart community platforms, Continued use intention, Trust, UTAUT, IDT

Introduction

A smart community, which leverages information technology, intelligent management, and enhanced services, represents an innovative approach to community governance. By integrating community service resources and applying advanced technologies, including big data and artificial intelligence, smart communities improve operational efficiency and enhance residents’ quality of life [1]. Internet technology has been increasingly integrated into community governance, supporting residents through various innovations and contributing to notable economic and social benefits in modern communities [2]. Technologically based communication in smart communities helps reshape urban environments, drives economic and social innovation, and generates significant benefits at the community level. It also highlights social, ethical, and environmental aspects, promoting community development and encouraging residents to learn, adapt, and participate in innovation [3]. Furthermore, smart communities emphasize social inclusion and active participation by redefining collective decision-making processes to support both social and environmental goals. However, the future development of smart cities and global environments will face numerous challenges, especially due to the rapid growth of Internet of Things (IoT) devices providing similar services. The quality of services delivered by many smart community platforms (SCPs) often relies on service rating systems. However, these rating mechanisms face limitations in accurately identifying the best service providers within smart community applications. To address this issue, a supervisor trust-based approach has been proposed. This method enhances the flexibility of service evaluation, reduces reliance on manual feedback to gather information about providers, and improves the scalability of the ranking system, allowing it to support a large number of feedback-enabled devices [4].

From a communication network perspective, a smart community integrates people, objects, businesses, communication, and energy through advanced information technologies, creating a comprehensive, seamless, and human-centered governance system. Its architecture is closely linked to big data, which acts as the engine driving the governance framework. The process of governance here is both fluid and persistent, generating substantial amounts of big data that form the foundation for refined management and insightful decision-making [5].

From this perspective, smart communities have attracted significant attention from both government and academic circles. Qi and Guo [6] developed a community-integrated service platform that connects with all systems within the smart community, aiming to enhance governance capacity and the efficiency of community services. Liu et al. [7] proposed a unified intelligent community system based on a four-dimensional tensor model that encodes information about objects, time, location, and human activities to examine the impact of human behavior and its relationships with other components. Subsequent studies demonstrated that the proposed intelligent community model, grounded in the unified fourth-order tensor, exhibited high flexibility and scalability. To address traffic congestion, environmental pollution, and resource imbalance, the LocalFocus big data service platform was proposed to optimize urban governance and promote residents’ well-being by integrating and analyzing multi-source, heterogeneous big data [8]. However, most of these proposals are limited because they do not fully capture the implications of smart communities and lack comprehensive research on their development. Additionally, these studies often overlook the user perspective. Previous studies have shown that the performance of SCPs is closely linked to the users' willingness and acceptance of continuous use [9]. The low adoption rate of community services offered by SCPs further limits service quality and sustainable development, hindering the growth of smart communities and the fulfillment of residents’ interests [10].

This study focused on identifying the key factors that shape users’ continued use of smart community platforms. By integrating the unified theory of acceptance and use of technology (UTAUT) and innovation diffusion theory (IDT), it examined how various factors interact to support long-term user engagement. Although the technology acceptance model (TAM) provides a parsimonious and robust framework explaining individuals’ acceptance of information systems [11], its explanatory scope has been questioned, particularly in the context of electronic or emerging technologies. One major limitation is its limited consideration of contextual and social factors—such as social influence (SI)—that may significantly shape technology adoption decisions in real-world settings. To address these limitations, Venkatesh et al. [12] proposed UTAUT, which synthesizes key constructs from several prominent technology acceptance models and demonstrates stronger predictive power. Despite its integrative strength, UTAUT has been criticized for omitting potential variables, which limits its flexibility in accommodating emerging predictors, as well as for its constrained applicability to consumer-oriented innovations due to its original development in organizational contexts [13, 14].

IDT provides a foundational framework for understanding technology dissemination by emphasizing attributes such as relative advantage (RA), compatibility (CO), and complexity [15]. However, while IDT effectively explains how innovation characteristics drive initial adoption, it offers limited insight into the cognitive and relational mechanisms that sustain long-term usage behavior [16]. Furthermore, the flexibility of IDT has occasionally led to fragmented theoretical interpretations across different domains. Consequently, relying solely on IDT may provide an incomplete understanding of continued use intention, necessitating the integration of complementary perspectives that capture users’ expectancy-based evaluations and psychological responses.

From a theoretical perspective, both IDT and UTAUT aim to explain and evaluate user adoption of SCPs. Based on attitude/behavior theory, UTAUT emphasizes that users’ beliefs and expectations are the primary factors determining whether they choose to use SCPs. It focuses on the psychological reaction to the technology rather than the technology itself, assuming that users adopt SCPs because of the benefits provided, such as efficiency and convenience. However, UTAUT lacks a dimension to consider how the inherent innovation attributes of a system match the specific environment, which can lead to inconsistent results in complex systems. It does not explicitly distinguish between the nature of the innovation and the psychological response of the user. Incorporating specific innovation characteristics can enhance the model’s ability to explain technology adoption in real-world contexts. The IDT framework complements this by focusing on the innovation itself, emphasizing attributes such as relative advantage and compatibility. In this integration, “Complementarity” arises from the alignment between the objective attributes of the innovation (IDT) and the subjective cognitive processes of the user (UTAUT). The emphasis on IDT addresses the limitations of UTAUT in considering the innovation-technology pairing process and whether the system’s attributes meet specific needs. By synthesizing these two perspectives, this study developed a more holistic framework that bridges the gap between technological attributes and psychosocial evaluations. This integration ensured that both the features of the innovation and the psychological processes were represented, revealing how the innovative nature of the platform aligned with the residents’ functional needs.

Furthermore, sustained engagement with SCPs is driven by a dual psychological mechanism integrating cognitive evaluation and emotional assurance. Unlike general e-commerce, SCPs represent a digitalized social space where residents’ interactions are often obligatory—driven by the necessity of accessing essential community services rather than purely voluntary consumption. This inherent asymmetry in power and the involvement of sensitive household data heighten users’ perceptions of vulnerability and psychological risk. In this context, trust operates as a critical psychological safeguard and a risk-mitigation tool that reduces perceived threats to privacy and enhances confidence in the platform’s integrity. Simultaneously, as residents continuously assess whether the platform effectively supports their daily interactions, the confirmation of these expectations fosters satisfaction. This is not merely a measure of utility, but a cognitive–affective state that reinforces a sense of community belonging and fulfills the informal psychological contract between residents and the digital governance system. Ultimately, while trust alleviates the “cognitive friction” of privacy concerns, satisfaction translates service quality into emotional attachment, jointly sustaining long-term engagement.

In addition, most existing studies on user behavior in smart communities rely on linear additive assumptions. These models presume that various psychological and technological drivers can offset each other in the user decision-making process. This perspective may overlook complex user psychology, specifically non-compensatory anchors that represent indispensable requirements that cannot be substituted by other strengths of the platform. By positioning technological attributes and psychosocial evaluations as parallel drivers, this study addresses the need to clarify the hierarchical importance and potential non-linear dependencies among these variables.

Literature review

Research context: smart community platform

Smart community development aims to integrate digital technologies into community governance and service delivery [17]. A smart community encompasses the development of an intelligent infrastructure, the strengthening of data-driven management, the promotion of active community engagement, and the integration of intelligence, informatization, and ecological sustainability [18]. As a result of integrating information technology into community governance, smart communities have begun to assume multifaceted roles. During the COVID-19 pandemic, the availability of data platforms enabled relevant stakeholders explored diverse solutions in innovative and timely ways [19], effectively curbed the further deterioration of the situation. SCPs can also encourage residents to take part in urban resilience planning by offering participatory mapping tools. These tools help provide useful information for planning community-scale resilience efforts [20]. SCPs can play unique roles in different geographical settings. One notable example was a digital ecosystem developed for coastal fishing communities, which aimed to address challenges such as limited digital access at sea, low digital literacy, and lack of timely market information [21]. A similar solution was implemented in island communities through a cloud-based IT platform designed to support energy asset management for integrated variable renewable energy systems [22].

In the domain of community energy, modular system platforms were proposed to monitor residents’ energy usage and promote more mindful and efficient consumption [23]. Building on the need for privacy-preserving and high-performance systems, some studies have incorporated personalized federated deep reinforcement learning into SCPs to optimize computation offloading and resource allocation [24]. To further improve the efficiency and flexibility of energy management, behavior-driven IoT systems with adaptive architectures have also been introduced in renewable energy communities [25].

Most existing studies focus on system design and technical optimization, with relatively little attention given to the behavioral mechanisms that drive users’ continued engagement with these platforms. To address this gap, this study examines the key factors that influence continued use intention in the context of SCPs.

Theoretical application and model integration

To examine users’ willingness to continue using digital platforms, scholars employed various theoretical perspectives, including the S–O-R framework, TAM, and ECT, across diverse contexts such as AI assistants and social media influencers [26–28]. Additionally, the service-dominant logic was applied to analyze how value co-creation drives participation in online communities [29]. While these studies offer valuable foundations, few have considered the synergistic effects of usage environment, platform characteristics, and user psychology. To address this gap, a comprehensive model is constructed primarily grounded in UTAUT and IDT, with trust incorporated as a critical psychological determinant.

In this framework, UTAUT serves as the structural foundation to capture socio-technical drivers. Previous research has demonstrated its robustness in analyzing continued use behavior across various domains, including MOOCs, mobile learning apps, and generative AI in higher education [30–32]. However, as UTAUT often overlooks the specific innovative attributes of the technology itself, IDT is integrated to bridge this gap. By accounting for innovation-specific attributes such as RA and CO, the model can better distinguish SCPs from traditional methods. This logic aligns with prior studies that emphasize IDT’s explanatory power in the adoption of tourism apps, electronic currency, and medical internet services [33–35]. Furthermore, acknowledging that technological features alone cannot sustain engagement, trust is introduced as an essential psychological bridge. As a key factor affecting users’ willingness to disclose information and shape continued behavior [36], trust plays a pivotal role in chatbot services, drone delivery, and healthcare applications [37–39]. However, in the unique context of SCPs, trust must coexist with user satisfaction to ensure behavioral persistence. Beyond functional utility, satisfaction represents the confirmation of initial expectations and the fulfillment of psychological contracts [40]. Unlike general e-commerce, SCPs involve involuntary interactions, sensitive household data, and social reciprocity. While trust mitigates the risks associated with these unique tensions, satisfaction reinforces emotional attachment by validating the platform’s reliability in meeting daily community needs. Together, these theories provide a holistic framework for understanding the multi-dimensional mechanisms driving sustained user engagement in SCPs.

The integration of UTAUT and IDT offers a comprehensive lens that simultaneously addresses the utilitarian structure of technology adoption and the innovation-specific context of smart community governance. Specifically, while UTAUT provides the fundamental path logic for evaluating cognitive expectations, IDT extends this by characterizing the unique attributes of SCPs such as their superiority over traditional methods and their alignment with community life. By paralleling these innovation-centric attributes with traditional expectancy factors, the integrated model ensures that the evaluation of user behavior is technically grounded and contextually relevant to the innovative nature of digital governance. This synergy allows the research to capture the transition from simple technology acceptance to the long-term adoption of an institutional innovation, providing a more granular assessment of how residents process the digital transformation of their living environment.

To rigorously validate this multi-dimensional framework, a two-phase methodological approach combining partial least squares structural equation modeling (PLS-SEM) and artificial neural networks (ANN) is employed. This integrated method is designed to enhance both the predictive accuracy and explanatory power of the model. While PLS-SEM is utilized to identify the hypothesized linear relationships among the constructs, the ANN component employs a multi-layer perceptron architecture to capture potential non-linear interactions that traditional linear models may overlook. Furthermore, this dual-stage analysis quantifies the relative importance of each predictor through sensitivity analysis and clarifies the causal paths within the model. The integration of structural modeling and neural networks ensures a robust identification of the key determinants driving sustained user engagement in smart communities.

Theoretical basis and assumptions

Trust and continued use intention

Trust is defined as a psychological state representing the confidence that individuals have in expected interactions, reflecting the expectation that a service provider will behave in accordance with established commitments [41]. In digital environments, trust serves as a critical mechanism for mitigating perceived risks and uncertainty, functioning as the psychological foundation for sustained user-provider relationships.

Within the ecosystem of SCPs, trust acts as a critical psychological mechanism for mitigating the perceived risks and uncertainties inherent in digital governance. Unlike general e-commerce, SCPs involve the handling of highly sensitive personal data (e.g., household records, property payments, and security access), which places residents in a vulnerable position. From a theoretical perspective, trust functions as a complexity-reduction tool; when residents believe in the platform provider's integrity and reliability, their cognitive burden of monitoring for potential service failures or data breaches is significantly reduced [42]. Prior research supports that such trust is a fundamental driver of sustained participation in digital systems by offsetting technological uncertainty [43, 44]. In the community context, this psychological security fosters a stable service relationship, transforming initial usage into a long-term behavioral commitment [45]. Consequently, higher levels of trust are expected to strengthen users’ continued use intention by reinforcing the perceived reliability of the platform's social and technical functions. Based on these considerations, we formulate the following hypothesis:

UTAUT-based behavioral factors

To systematically capture the multi-dimensional drivers of residents’ engagement with SCPs, this study adopts the full suite of core constructs from UTAUT: Performance Expectancy (PE), Effort Expectancy (EE), Social Influence (SI), and Facilitating Conditions (FC) [12]. The decision to employ the complete UTAUT framework is based on its holistic capacity to account for both individual-functional motivations (PE and EE) and environmental-social determinants (SI and FC), which are uniquely intertwined in a residential governance context. Specifically, while PE and EE evaluate the utilitarian efficiency of digitalized community services, SI and FC reflect the social dynamics and institutional support provided by neighborhood committees and property management. This integrated perspective ensures a comprehensive assessment of how technical attributes are perceived before being processed into psychological satisfaction. The robust predictive power of these constructs was validated across various technology adoption domains, including e-learning, mobile services, and open-source systems [46–48].

Performance expectancy: utilitarian value and trust

PE reflects the perceived benefits and efficiency gains residents derive from using SCPs. From a theoretical perspective, when a platform effectively fulfills utilitarian needs such as streamlining administrative tasks or improving service accessibility, it validates the functional competence of the system. This consistent utility reduces psychological uncertainty and therefore fosters user trust in the provider’s capability [49, 50]. Furthermore, the realization of these expected gains creates a state of satisfaction by confirming the practical value of the platform [51]. Consequently, high levels of PE serve as a primary driver of the rational intention to continue using the service. Based on these considerations, the following hypotheses are formulated:

H2: PE exerts a significant positive influence on users’ continued use intention.

Effort expectancy: cognitive ease and satisfaction

EE reflects the perceived ease of interacting with SCPs. Based on the principle of cognitive parsimony, users prefer systems that minimize the mental effort required for task completion. Within an SCP, intuitive interfaces and simplified processes reduce the cognitive burden on residents, thereby preventing frustration and fostering a positive state of satisfaction. Recent research confirms that such ease of use significantly shapes the utilitarian perspective and subsequent satisfaction of technology users [52]. This emotional contentment, once established, acts as a psychological catalyst that may subsequently strengthen the long-term intention to persist with the service. Consequently, the reduction in effort provides the necessary evaluative foundation for sustained platform engagement through the mediation of user satisfaction. Based on these considerations, the following hypothesis is formulated:

H5: EE has a positive impact on user satisfaction.

H5: EE has a positive impact on user satisfaction.

Social influence: normative pressure and community trust

SI refers to the extent to which residents perceive that important others, such as family, neighbors, or community leaders, believe they should use SCPs. From a theoretical perspective, when individuals face technological uncertainty, they often rely on the collective cues of their social network to reduce perceived risk and validate their own choices. Positive feedback from early adopters or official community endorsements provides an external validation of the platform’s reliability, which subsequently fosters user trust in the system [53]. Furthermore, the desire for social alignment and the internalization of community norms create a psychological incentive for individuals to conform to collective behavior. Empirical evidence suggests that such favorable social cues are a critical driver of technology usage across diverse service environments, reinforcing the behavioral commitment of users to modern digital systems [54]. Consequently, the integration of SCPs into the social fabric of the community serves to strengthen the long-term intention to persist with the service. Based on these considerations, the following hypotheses are formulated:

H6: SI has a positive impact on users’ continued use intention.

H6: SI has a positive impact on users’ continued use intention.

H7: SI has a positive impact on user trust.

H7: SI has a positive impact on user trust.

Facilitating conditions: resource support and contentment

FC refers to the degree to which residents perceive that the necessary technical infrastructure and support are available to enable the use of SCPs. From a theoretical perspective, the availability of these resources reduces functional barriers and mitigates the frustration associated with technical difficulties. When residents feel supported by adequate guidance, their perceived self-efficacy increases, which directly fosters a positive emotional evaluation of the service experience. Previous research confirms that such facilitating factors significantly shape user satisfaction and subsequent usage intentions [30]. Furthermore, robust external support has been validated as a key determinant of technology adoption across diverse urban populations [55]. Consequently, the presence of strong FC is expected to enhance overall user contentment by ensuring a smooth service interaction. Based on these considerations, the following hypothesis is formulated:

H8: FC has a positive impact on user satisfaction.

H8: FC has a positive impact on user satisfaction.

IDT-based innovation attributes

In addition to the behavioral factors from UTAUT, this study incorporates Relative Advantage (RA) and Compatibility (CO) from IDT to capture the unique innovative characteristics of SCPs. These two attributes are specifically selected because they represent the critical evaluation criteria for residents transitioning from traditional manual community services to digital solutions. RA is employed to assess how residents perceive the superiority of SCPs over conventional governance models in terms of efficiency and convenience. CO is included to examine how well the digital platform aligns with the existing values, daily routines, and lifestyle needs of community members. By focusing on these two attributes, the model provides a more nuanced understanding of how the inherent innovativeness of SCPs influences the psychological shift toward continued use. While IDT originally proposes five attributes, variables such as complexity, trialability, and observability are excluded here to avoid redundancy with UTAUT constructs (e.g., EE) and to maintain focus on the most impactful drivers identified in mature digital service contexts [56].

Relative advantage: comparative utility and user satisfaction

RA represents the degree to which an innovation is perceived as superior to the traditional practices it replaces. Within the context of SCPs, RA reflects the comparative utility residents experience when transitioning from manual administrative processes to digitalized management systems. From a theoretical perspective, when individuals recognize that a new technology offers distinct benefits such as enhanced efficiency or convenience over existing solutions, this perceived superiority leads to a positive cognitive appraisal of the innovation. Such an evaluative process confirms the value of the platform, thereby fostering a state of user satisfaction. Empirical research indicates that RA is a critical determinant of user attitudes and evaluative responses across various technological domains [57, 58]. Furthermore, the realization of these comparative benefits has been shown to be a fundamental driver of positive psychological outcomes among technology adopters [59]. Consequently, the distinct advantages provided by SCPs are expected to serve as a primary source of resident contentment. Based on the above literature, the following hypothesis is proposed regarding RA:

H9: RA has a positive impact on user satisfaction.

H9: RA has a positive impact on user satisfaction.

Compatibility: lifestyle alignment and behavioral persistence

CO refers to the degree to which an innovation aligns with the existing values, past experiences, and needs of potential adopters. In the context of SCPs, high CO ensures that platform functions integrate seamlessly into residents’ daily routines. Theoretically, this alignment reduces the psychological resistance and cognitive effort typically required for new technology, thereby fostering a sense of “fit” that directly enhances user satisfaction [60]. Furthermore, when a technology complements established habits, it is more easily internalized, which reinforces the intention for continued use [61]. Consequently, the harmony between SCPs and resident lifestyles serves as a critical determinant of both immediate contentment and long-term engagement. Based on these considerations, the following hypotheses are formulated:

H10: CO exerts a positive influence on user satisfaction.

H10: CO exerts a positive influence on user satisfaction.

H11: CO positively influences users’ continued use intention.

Satisfaction: the emotional bridge to continued engagement

Satisfaction represents the comprehensive emotional evaluation formed by users after interacting with a product or service. Within the conceptual framework of this study, satisfaction serves as a critical mediating variable that translates technical and innovative characteristics from the UTAUT and IDT models into long-term behavioral outcomes. Theoretically, when the perceived performance of SCPs meets or exceeds resident expectations, the resulting positive affect reinforces the user's psychological attachment to the platform. Empirical research confirms that this evaluative state is a primary driver of continued use intention, often exerting a more significant influence than functional fluency alone [62]. Furthermore, high levels of satisfaction is validated as a fundamental catalyst for sustained consumer involvement in various service-oriented domains [63]. Consequently, the accumulation of positive service experiences is expected to solidify the resident's commitment to persist with platform usage. Based on the above literature, the following hypothesis is proposed:

H12: User satisfaction positively influences the intention to continue platform usage.

Research methodology

Questionnaire design and measures

The measurement instruments for this study were adapted from well-established scales to ensure content validity. A five-point Likert scale was employed to measure the constructs, with responses ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) to 5 (strongly agree). All items were tailored to the specific context of SCPs.

To ensure conceptual clarity within the digital governance context, key constructs were operationally defined as follows. SI is defined as the degree to which residents perceive social pressure from significant others such as neighbors, family, or community staff to utilize the SCPs. Trust is operationalized as the residents’ confidence in the platform’s technical reliability and the data privacy protection measures implemented by the authorities. Satisfaction refers to the cumulative affective evaluation of the residents’ experience, representing the extent to which the SCPs meet their functional needs and governance expectations. CO is defined as the degree to which using the SCPs is perceived as being consistent with the residents’ existing values, past experiences, and daily living habits. These definitions provided the theoretical basis for subsequent scale adaptation and item refinement.

To ensure the reliability and clarity of the instrument, a preliminary pilot survey was conducted from July 1 to July 20, 2025. This phase involved 30 participants with extensive experience using various SCPs. Based on the feedback from the pilot study and initial reliability testing, several items were refined or removed where the original wording exhibited conceptual drift or redundancy.

For instance, an item regarding user Satisfaction was changed from “using the services provided by SCP is appropriate” to “The SCPs consistently meet my needs”, shifting the focus from moral judgment to subjective psychological fulfillment. Regarding SI, items emphasizing macro-level factors such as media reports and government policies were replaced with those focusing on the influence of neighbors and family members, which better captures the immediate social environment of the user. Additionally, for Continued Use Intention, the wording was revised from “I plan to continue using SCPs” to “I intend to continue using SCPs” to emphasize voluntary behavioral motivation rather than a mere scheduled arrangement.

Furthermore, two measurement items were removed to enhance the parsimony and conceptual clarity of the scale. Specifically, an item from user satisfaction (“Using the services provided by the SCPs make me feel happy”) was deleted due to high conceptual redundancy with other satisfaction indicators. In general psychological research, “Happy” (affective) and “Satisfaction” (evaluative) are conceptually distinct. However, within the specific context of utilitarian SCPs, user emotions are primarily driven by the efficient fulfillment of daily functional needs. In this setting, “Satisfaction” functions as an affective-cognitive synthesis, which represents an emotional state of gratification resulting from a cognitive appraisal of service performance. Therefore, the removal of the specific “Happy” item does not imply a loss of affective data. Instead, the remaining satisfaction indicators were deemed sufficient to capture the emotional response required to drive long-term behavioral persistence, while avoiding semantic redundancy that could compromise the stability of the structural model. An item for Continued Use Intention (“I expect to continue using the SCPs for a long time”) was also excluded, as participants perceived it as a technological expectation or a long-term forecast rather than a direct personal intention. Following these adjustments, a final set of 27 items was retained to test the hypotheses, as detailed in Table 1. The formal data collection commenced thereafter in August 2025.

Construction and measurement items

Sampling and data collection

Formal data collection was conducted from August to September 2025 via the Wenjuanxing platform, following the pilot study. An inclusion criterion was set requiring all participants to be community residents with actual experience using the SCPs. A snowball sampling technique was utilized to recruit participants. Initial survey links were distributed within official resident WeChat groups in collaboration with community management committees and property offices. These initial respondents were then encouraged to refer other eligible residents in their respective communities. A screening question was used at the beginning of the questionnaire to verify usage experience, and respondents without such experience were excluded. This approach ensured that the collected data reflected the perceptions of actual users.

To mitigate common method bias (CMB) prior to data collection, several procedural remedies were implemented. The survey introduction explicitly stated that all responses were collected anonymously and voluntarily. It was also emphasized that there were no right or wrong answers and that the data would be used for academic purposes only, which helped to reduce social desirability bias. Furthermore, the sequence of the measurement items was randomized to prevent respondents from developing cognitive patterns or consistency motifs during the survey.

Data cleaning was performed through a rigorous multi-stage screening process to ensure high data quality. First, responses completed in under 60 s were excluded as “speeding”. Second, logical consistency was verified by cross-examining responses between highly correlated constructs. For instance, questionnaires exhibiting extreme logical contradictions, such as reporting very low satisfaction while expressing a very high intention to continue using the platform, were identified and removed. Third, the dataset was screened for straight-lining patterns, where responses with zero or near-zero variance across all 27 items were discarded. Additionally, the online survey was designed to require a response for every item, thereby eliminating the issue of missing values.

After this screening process, 515 valid responses were retained from the 600 distributed questionnaires, resulting in an effective response rate of 85.8%. To analyze common method variance (CMV) after collection, Harman’s single-factor test was conducted. The results indicated that the first principal factor accounted for 27.5% of the total variance, which is well below the threshold of 40%. This confirms that common method bias is not a significant concern in this study. The outcomes of the descriptive statistical analysis from the formal survey are summarized in Table 2.

Instrument reliability and validity assessment

Prior to conducting subsequent data analyses, the measurement model was assessed to verify its reliability and confirm its adequacy in representing the theoretical constructs. Accordingly, to guarantee the measurement model’s reliability, assessments of internal consistency, convergent validity, and discriminant validity were conducted.

Assessment of the measurement model

The findings indicated that both Cronbach’s alpha(CA) and Composite Reliability(CR) values surpass the recommended threshold of 0.70, demonstrating high internal reliability of the constructs. Moreover, all factor loadings exceeded the standard threshold of 0.708, and the Average Variance Extracted (AVE) values were above 0.50, indicating satisfactory convergent validity.

Discriminant validity

Empirical results and hypothesis testing

Two-stage analytical strategy

This study employed a combined two-stage methodology that integrates PLS-SEM with ANN. PLS-SEM is a flexible method well-suited to assess complex model relationships; however, it primarily captures linear relationships based on predefined models and paths. It is unable to detect non-linear patterns. Therefore, an ANN was utilized to validate and supplement the findings, thereby offering deeper insights into the phenomenon under investigation. The ANN also enables the ranking of key factors within the model, offering practical recommendations for the development and optimization of the smart community platforms [65].

Artificial neural network evaluation

Model predictive accuracy

While PLS-SEM is well-suited for analyzing linear relationships and structural pathways, it possesses limited capability in identifying complex non-linear interactions among variables. To further elucidate the factors influencing users’ continued use intentions toward the SCPs, the ANN method was employed to examine both linear and non-linear relationships. Unlike traditional linear models, the ANN approach captures non-compensatory decision-making processes, providing a more nuanced understanding of user behavior in complex digital environments. Employing PLS-SEM alongside ANN helps to mitigate the inherent ‘black box’ nature of ANN models [67].

Since the structural model includes three endogenous constructs, separate ANN analyses were conducted for each. Using the multi-layer perceptron (MLP) algorithm in SPSS, the models for CUI, TR, and SA were individually examined. Each ANN model used two-hidden-layers to capture deeper non-linear relationships. To ensure numerical stability and a smooth mapping of non-linear effects, the sigmoid activation function was applied to both the hidden and output layers. The selection of these hyperparameters (two hidden layers and sigmoid activation) was optimized to balance the model’s learning capacity with its ability to generalize to unseen data. The ANN models for TR, SA, and CUI are illustrated in Figs. 3, 4 and 5, respectively.

A primary discrepancy between the linear and non-linear results emerged regarding EE. In the PLS-SEM analysis (Table 6), EE was identified as the strongest linear predictor of satisfaction (β = 0.216, p < 0.001). However, the ANN sensitivity analysis (Table 9) elevates EE to a definitive 100% normalized importance, while other functional predictors like PE (38.58%) and RA (29.40%) show significantly lower relative impact. This dramatic polarization suggests that in the smart community context, EE functions as a “non-compensatory threshold”. Unlike general consumer applications where high functional benefits (PE) could offset a complex interface, for digital urban governance platforms, technical simplicity is a mandatory prerequisite. Without meeting the threshold of low cognitive effort, the platform’s innovative features fail to translate into user satisfaction, shifting the theoretical understanding from a compensatory linear logic to a hierarchical threshold logic.

Furthermore, a significant “hierarchical displacement” of variable importance occured between the two models regarding SI. In the linear path analysis (Table 6), SI exerted a relatively modest direct effect on CUI (β = 0.154), ranking below satisfaction and trust. Conversely, the ANN results (Table 10) ranked SI as a dominant driver with a high normalized importance of 54.00%, substantially outperforming trust (34.44%) and PE (47.11%). This importance leap indicates that the impact of community norms and peer behavior is substantially underestimated by linear estimations. It suggests a “social contagion” mechanism unique to residential ecosystems, where the influence of neighbors triggers a disproportionate increase in retention once a critical mass of adoption is reached. By revealing this non-linear “importance reversal”, the study demonstrates that the integrated model is not merely additive but generative, capturing the “tipping point” dynamics that govern the sustainability of smart community engagement.

Discussion

Discussion of results

This study employed a two-stage analytical approach that combines PLS-SEM and ANN to examine the key factors influencing users’ continued use of SCPs. All twelve proposed hypotheses were supported by the results. By integrating UTAUT and IDT, the framework allowed for a comprehensive examination of various determinants of user behavior. Overall, these findings highlight the value of combining multiple theoretical models with advanced analytical methods to gain a deeper understanding of users’ continued use behavior.

The results confirmed that trust and PE were the bedrock of user persistence (H1: β = 0.181, t = 3.682, p < 0.001; H2: β = 0.112, t = 2.647, p < 0.01; H3: β = 0.324, t = 7.549, p < 0.001). Consistent with Hamidi [43], trust acts as a fundamental risk-mitigating mechanism; in the absence of perceived security and reliability, even high-performing platforms fail to retain users. Interestingly, the ANN analysis (model 1)identified PE as the most vital precursor to building trust with 100% normalized importance. This suggests a cognitive-to-relational transition: users do not grant trust blindly but base it on the platform’s demonstrated functional utility. This aligns with Gallin et al. [50], reinforcing that for digital public services, “perceived competence” (performance) is the primary driver of institutional trust. Furthermore, the prominence of the privacy dimension (TR1) underscores the existence of a “psychological contract” regarding sensitive data handling. In smart community ecosystems, residents exchange personal household information for service convenience. Consequently, any perceived ambiguity in data privacy (such as the opaque processing of payment records) will trigger a “risk-avoidance” mechanism. This implies that while functional utility initiates the trust-building process, sustained engagement is contingent upon a continuous sense of digital safety that serves to mitigate the inherent vulnerability of institutional digitalization.

A key theoretical contribution of this study is the confirmation of satisfaction as the pivotal affective bridge between cognitive evaluation and long-term intention(H4:β = 0.118, t = 2.421, p < 0.05; H5:β = 0.216, t = 5.326, p < 0.001; H12:β = 0.238, t = 5.490, p < 0.001). While Alam [52] noted the impact of ease of use on attitudes, the ANN results (Table 8) revealed a more profound non-compensatory logic: EE achieved a 100% importance ranking, dramatically overshadowing PE (38.58%) and RA (29.40%). This importance polarization suggests that in the smart community context, EE functions as a “structural gatekeeper” rather than a secondary predictor. Unlike general consumer applications, high functional utility (PE) cannot compensate for complex interfaces in digital urban governance platforms. Since these systems serve a diverse demographic that includes a 23.3% elderly population, technical simplicity becomes a mandatory prerequisite. Theoretically, this implies a hierarchical threshold logic: if an interface exceeds the user's cognitive effort limit, superior functional performance becomes “cognitively inaccessible”. In such cases, the resulting frustration cannot be offset by high performance, which leads to a breakdown of the satisfaction-retention link. In terms of practical management, this necessitates a shift from a “feature-rich” to a “friction-free” development strategy, prioritizing “Cognitive Offloading” through minimalist User Interface (UI) designs (e.g., high-contrast “Elderly Modes”) to reduce the mental budget required for navigation. Ultimately, while PE acts as a “pull factor” for rational adoption, EE dictates the emotional contentment required for staying, establishing technical simplicity as the mandatory baseline for securing the psychological contract between residents and the smart community system.

The findings regarding SI (H6:β = 0.154, t = 3.271, p < 0.01; H7:β = 0.269, t = 6.504, p < 0.001) and RA (H10:β = 0.116, t = 2.418, p < 0.05; H11:β = 0.117, t = 2.871, p < 0.01) highlighted the unique socio-technical nature of smart communities. The results resonate with Cai et al. [53] but add a crucial layer: the ANN analysis identified a significant upward shift in the importance of SI (54.00%) compared to its linear path coefficient. This suggests a “social contagion” effect. Specifically, within the closed ecosystem of a residential community, peer recommendations and neighbor behavior exert a disproportionate influence that traditional linear models often underestimate. Beyond a simple “bandwagon effect”, this non-linearity suggests a psychological transition from compliance to norm internalization. Under this mechanism, platform utility is amplified by informational SI, as observing neighbors’ success reduces individual perceived risk. Furthermore, as participation reaches a potential “tipping point”, the use of SCPs may shift from an individual choice to a collective community norm, where the desire for social alignment and collective efficacy triggers a more rapid, self-sustaining adoption trajectory.

Furthermore, the significant roles of RA, CO, and FC aligned with the broader innovation diffusion literature (H8:β = 0.162, t = 3.419, p < 0.01; H9:β = 0.128, t = 2.335, p < 0.05; H10:β = 0.116, t = 2.418, p < 0.05; H11:β = 0.117, t = 2.871, p < 0.01). As suggested by Yang and Kwon [61], users evaluate SCPs not in a vacuum, but in comparison to traditional offline services. The platform must not only be “good” but “better and more fitting” than old routines. Finally, the confirmation of FC as a driver of satisfaction (consistent with Li and Zhao [30] underscores that technical support and infrastructure are essential hygiene factors that sustain the user experience. Importantly, the sensitivity analysis from the ANN model (Table 10) identified satisfaction as the ultimate determinant of long-term retention (100% importance), suggesting that while cognitive and social factors initiate the use, the post-adoption affective experience is what ensures sustainability.

Theoretical and practical implications

Theoretical implications

Theoretically, this study contributes to the technology adoption literature by successfully integrating UTAUT and IDT, offering a comprehensive explanation for SCPs continuance that accounts for both user psychological perceptions and technology innovation attributes. The unique contribution of this integration lies in the revelation of a dual-track evaluative mechanism. While UTAUT establishes the structural paths of expectancy-based logic, the inclusion of RA and CO from IDT provides the necessary contextual depth. This combination helps explain how residents internalize specific innovation characteristics. This synergy addresses the limitations of using a single theoretical lens, demonstrating that long-term user engagement is not merely a product of functional utility, but is fundamentally driven by the alignment between technological innovation and established community routines. By bridging these two frameworks, the study advances the understanding of user behavior from a general technology acceptance process to a specialized assessment of “institutional innovation fit”, highlighting that the RA and CO of a platform are essential prerequisites for translating technical performance into sustained behavioral persistence. Furthermore, the methodological combination of PLS-SEM and ANN uncovers complex non-linear and non-compensatory relationships that traditional linear models often fail to detect. For instance, the ANN results showed that SI possessed a high normalized importance of 54.00%, which actually surpassed trust at 34.44%. This finding suggests that social dynamics in community environments may exert a disproportionately large impact, providing a more robust theoretical foundation for exploring complex human-technology interactions in localized social contexts.

Beyond the empirical validation of the integrated framework, this study offers deeper theoretical insight by identifying a non-compensatory hierarchy among the key predictors. Traditional models often assume a compensatory relationship where a weakness in one area can be offset by strength in another. However, the findings suggest that in the smart community context, factors such as satisfaction and EE act as primary anchors. This indicates that their influence is fundamental and non-substitutable, which refines the boundary conditions of both UTAUT and IDT. By uncovering these non-linear interdependencies and the disproportionate impact of social dynamics, this research moves from a simple additive integration toward a more sophisticated explanatory mechanism. It successfully bridges the gap between technological attributes and psychosocial evaluations.

Practical implications

From a practical perspective, these empirical findings provide a data-driven roadmap for platform developers and community managers to enhance resident retention. The high normalized importance of satisfaction in the ANN analysis indicated that management efforts must prioritize the cumulative user experience as a primary non-compensatory driver. Specifically, the strong links between EE, FC, and satisfaction suggest that platform interfaces should be simplified through “one-click” service modules for high-frequency tasks. To further optimize FC, especially for the elderly demographic (23.3%, see Table 2), developers should implement age-appropriate design features, such as high-contrast visual interfaces, voice-activated navigation, and simplified “Elderly Mode” dashboards. For the tech-savvy majority (74% aged 18–45), management should focus on streamlining high-frequency “Payment” and “Community Business Processing” (e.g., utility management and service booking) to maximize efficiency. By integrating automated service modules for 24/7 basic inquiries and providing real-time progress tracking, managers can significantly reduce the cognitive effort required by residents, thereby establishing essential prerequisites for long-term engagement.

Regarding the critical role of trust, particularly the privacy dimension (TR1), platform providers must move beyond passive compliance to active psychological reassurance. Given that over 76% of users utilize the platform for sensitive community business and payments, the implementation of “Visible Security” features is recommended. This includes a simplified privacy dashboard that allows users to toggle data permissions with one click. Additionally, a “Data Usage Log” should clearly show when and why sensitive information (such as home addresses or payment records) was accessed. Instead of complex legal jargon, using short “Pop-up Privacy Snapshots” before users submit service requests can provide the immediate transparency needed to mitigate privacy concerns.

Furthermore, the significant impact of RA implies that the sustainability of SCPs depends on their ability to offer superior efficiency compared to traditional offline channels. Managers can optimize resource allocation by ensuring that core services, such as digital voting and security alerts, demonstrate a clear advantage in speed and transparency. Finally, the non-linear importance of SI suggests that practitioners should leverage social proof. Instead of generic promotion, managers can implement “Digital Resident Ambassador” programs. This initiative involves engaging respected senior residents to lead peer-to-peer digital literacy workshops in community centers. By introducing community-based incentive schemes, such as digital credits redeemable for local offline services, practitioners can trigger a positive social contagion effect. This approach facilitates the achievement of a critical mass of adoption, leading to a disproportionate increase in user loyalty as residents feel both technically supported and socially encouraged to retain platform usage.

Conclusion

Summary of the study

This study integrates UTAUT and IDT frameworks using a PLS-SEM and ANN approach to examine user persistence in smart community platforms. The results reveal a “Cognitive-Affective-Behavioral” logic, where PE and RA form the cognitive base, while satisfaction acts as the core affective driver. Crucially, the ANN analysis identifies EE as a non-compensatory prerequisite, indicating that platform simplicity is essential and cannot be offset by functional utility. Furthermore, the findings highlight a “social contagion” effect, where SI exerts a disproportionate impact on retention. In conclusion, long-term sustainability requires a strategic focus on minimizing cognitive friction, ensuring emotional satisfaction, and leveraging community social networks.

Limitations and future research

Although this study provides a comprehensive understanding of the factors influencing continued use intentions toward SCPs, several limitations warrant acknowledgment. Firstly, while the current model identifies core structural drivers, it does not fully account for the potential moderating roles of demographic variables such as age, education levels, and digital literacy. These factors may significantly shape how residents perceive EE and SI; for instance, digital literacy might mitigate the impact of technical barriers on satisfaction. Future research should incorporate these variables as moderators to develop a more granular understanding of user behavior across diverse social strata.

Secondly, the cross-sectional nature of the data limited the ability to draw definitive causal inferences over time. Since user perceptions evolve as platforms mature, future studies should employ longitudinal designs to track the dynamic shifts in user loyalty. Additionally, while the ANN model captured non-linear interactions, its “black box” nature may overlook the nuanced motivations behind user behavior. Integrating qualitative methodologies (such as in-depth interviews) would provide richer contextual insights into the subjective experiences of residents, particularly regarding the social and emotional dimensions of community digitalization.

Thirdly, the reliance on self-reported measures and the regional disparities in smart community development may introduce subjectivity and limit the generalizability of the findings. Future research should adopt stratified sampling strategies that classify communities based on their digital maturity and economic tiers, allowing for a more comparative analysis that accounts for regional disparities in platform operation and maintenance capabilities.

Finally, although the sample included a significant proportion of middle-aged and elderly residents (23.3% aged over 46), the reliance on snowball sampling via WeChat may introduce selection bias. The respondents might represent a more technologically proficient segment of the community. Consequently, the findings should be interpreted with caution when generalizing to the broader elderly population who may face digital barriers. Future research could employ offline stratified sampling to enhance the representativeness of diverse demographic groups.
