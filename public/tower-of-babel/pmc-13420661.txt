Left Subclavian Artery Thrombosis Causing Myocardial Ischemia in Patients With Prior Coronary Artery Bypass Grafting
Authors: Omar Toubat, Ryan A. Watson, Russell D. Rosenberg
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAcute subclavian artery thrombosis is a rare cause of myocardial ischemia in patients with prior coronary artery bypass grafting.Case SummaryWe present 2 cases of patients with left internal mammary artery (LIMA) to left anterior descending bypass grafts who developed cardiac ischemia secondary to acute thrombotic occlusion of the left subclavian artery. The first patient developed ventricular fibrillatory arrest after esophagectomy and the second patient had non–ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction in the setting of a recent colonoscopy. In both cases, left subclavian artery patency was reestablished following deployment of a covered stent. Antegrade flow to the LIMA to left anterior descending was restored with resolution of ischemic symptoms in both patients.DiscussionAcute thrombotic occlusion of the left subclavian artery is a rare cause of myocardial ischemia in patients with LIMA-dependent coronary circulation.Take-Home MessageEndovascular management with covered stent deployment is a feasible alternative to thrombectomy in this setting.

Case 1

History of presentation

A 62-year-old man presented with a history of hypertension, hyperlipidemia, diabetes mellitus, peripheral arterial disease status post right carotid and right femoral endarterectomy, prior right lower extremity critical limb threatening ischemia on rivaroxaban (Xarelto), coronary artery disease status post robotic left internal mammary artery (LIMA) to left anterior descending (LAD) bypass grafting in 2013, and locally advanced gastroesophageal junction adenocarcinoma. The patient completed induction chemotherapy and was admitted for therapeutic resection with Ivor-Lewis esophagectomy. Preoperatively, Xarelto was held 4 days before admission.

The patient's operation was uncomplicated and he was transferred from the intensive care unit to the step-down unit on postoperative day 1. On the morning of postoperative day 5, the patient experienced a ventricular fibrillatory cardiac arrest. Return of spontaneous circulation was transiently achieved after defibrillation, and the patient re-arrested.

Differential diagnosis

The differential diagnosis for cardiac arrest in a postesophagectomy patient is varied and includes multiple etiologies: cardiac (acute myocardial infarction, myocardial ischemia, left ventricular outflow tract obstruction, valvular heart disease, coronary artery thromboembolism), pulmonary (pulmonary embolism, pneumonia, atelectasis, mucus plugging, tension pneumothorax), and metabolic (electrolyte derangements, severe acidosis). In addition, postsurgical complications such as anastomotic leak, significant bleeding, or iatrogenic injury to cardiovascular or pulmonary structures should be considered.

Intervention

Rhythm analysis during the second arrest initially demonstrated ventricular fibrillation followed by pulseless electrical activity. It was suspected that the patient may be experiencing a pulmonary embolism and the decision was made to empirically give tissue plasminogen activator. Return of spontaneous circulation was achieved with a total arrest time of ∼45 minutes and postarrest electrocardiogram (ECG) was consistent with atrial fibrillation with rapid ventricular response and no acute ischemic changes. The patient was reintubated, stabilized on vasoactive medications, and transferred to the intensive care unit. Computed tomography (CT) imaging was obtained and ruled out the presence of pulmonary embolism. Severe ostial calcification of the left subclavian artery was noted on CT imaging, concerning for LIMA-LAD ischemia secondary to subclavian stenosis. Postarrest transthoracic echocardiography demonstrated left ventricular ejection fraction of 55% with hypokinesis of the mid-apical segments and no significant valvular disease, consistent with the potential for LAD territory ischemia.

On postoperative day 6, the patient was taken to the catheterization lab for coronary and subclavian angiography. The left subclavian artery was found to be completely occluded at the ostium, with a 50 mm thrombotic occlusion extending from the ostium into the proximal segment just before the origin of the LIMA and vertebral arteries (Figure 1). The lesion was easily crossed with a guidewire and glidecatheter. Intravascular ultrasound was performed and confirmed severe calcific stenosis of the ostial subclavian artery, along with thrombus throughout the proximal vessel. A 9 × 59-mm GORE VIABAHN VBX covered stent was delivered with the distal landing zone just proximal to the LIMA and vertebral artery origin (Figure 2). Outcome and follow-up

The remainder of the patient's postoperative course was uncomplicated. The patient was extubated the day after placement of his subclavian stent (postoperative day 7) demonstrating no residual neurologic or cardiovascular deficits. He transitioned from the intensive care unit to the step-down unit on postoperative day 10 and was discharged home on postoperative day 17 on dual antiplatelet therapy. On most recent follow-up 14.5 months after subclavian stent placement, the patient continues to do well with no recurrent chest pain or residual deficits from his arrest.

Case 2

History of presentation

A 72-year-old man presented with a history of hypertension, hyperlipidemia, peripheral arterial disease status post right carotid endarterectomy, coronary artery disease status post coronary artery bypass grafting with an LIMA-LAD and saphenous vein graft to obtuse marginal artery (OM1) in 2019. In 2022, the patient underwent left main coronary artery drug eluting stent placement for progressively worsening angina and progressive obstructive disease, with improvement in his symptoms.

In 2024, the patient underwent routine colonoscopy with polypectomy where he developed significant hematochezia. He was hospitalized for management of his gastrointestinal bleeding with blood product transfusion. His course was complicated by the development of acute chest pain, nausea, and vomiting. ECG demonstrated acute ischemic changes and high-sensitivity troponin was found to be elevated at 1,400 ng/L, consistent with a diagnosis of non–ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction (NSTEMI).

Differential diagnosis

The differential diagnosis for NSTEMI in a patient with history of coronary artery disease and hematochezia includes the following: coronary artery atherosclerotic plaque rupture and thrombosis from progressive or newly developed disease (type I), myocardial oxygen supply-demand mismatch (type II), in-stent thrombosis or restenosis in patients with prior coronary stenting (type IV), coronary artery vasospasm, or coronary artery thromboembolism.

Intervention

The patient was transferred to our medical center for further management of his NSTEMI. The patient's chest pain continued to worsen. Transthoracic echocardiogram demonstrated left ventricular ejection fraction 35% with inferoseptal hypokinesis and no significant valvular abnormalities. Repeat ECG demonstrated a new left bundle branch pattern, widening QRS complex, and associated troponin elevation to 1,632.

On hospital day 2, the patient was taken to the catheterization laboratory where angiography demonstrated significant calcification of the left subclavian artery ostium, acute thrombus in the proximal left subclavian artery, and a dissection flap just proximal to the LIMA origin (Figure 3). An intraaortic balloon pump was placed for mechanical circulatory support, and the subclavian lesion was crossed with a guidewire and catheter. Intravascular lithotripsy (7 × 60 mm) of the left subclavian artery was performed. This was followed by deployment of a 7 × 39-mm GORE VIABAHN VBX covered stent into the subclavian artery, with the distal edge landing just proximal to the vertebral and LIMA origins. Outcome and follow-up

The patient's postprocedural recovery was uneventful. The patient's angina resolved and the intraaortic balloon pump was removed on hospital day 4. He was transferred from the intensive care unit to the step-down unit on hospital day 5 and discharged home in stable condition with dual antiplatelet therapy on hospital day 8. Over a follow-up period of 17 months, the patient continues to do well and has been stable from a cardiovascular perspective.

Discussion

Coronary subclavian steal syndrome is a well-recognized cause of myocardial ischemia in patients with LIMA-dependent coronary flow.1,2 Most often this is due to progressive stenosis of the proximal left subclavian artery causing a hemodynamically significant obstruction proximal to the LIMA take-off.1,2 In settings of increased demand, reduced antegrade flow to the LIMA conduit can precipitate myocardial ischemia. In contrast to this more chronic atherosclerotic disease process, the present case series highlights a distinct and less well described mechanism of coronary subclavian steal due to acute thrombotic occlusion of the proximal left subclavian artery. In both cases presented here, subclavian artery thrombosis is believed to have resulted in sudden compromise of LIMA-dependent coronary flow, manifesting as acute coronary syndrome.

The mechanism of subclavian artery thrombosis in the present study remains unclear. Previous studies have described thrombotic occlusion of the subclavian artery secondary to traumatic injury,3 hypercoagulable states,4,5 and external compression due to anatomic anomalies.6,7 Both patients in this report had established histories of peripheral arterial disease with significant calcification noted in the proximal left subclavian artery. As such, plaque rupture from this atherosclerotic substrate is the most likely presumed cause of acute thrombus formation. Additional risk factors for thrombus formation in the first patient include hypercoagulability due to malignancy, pro-inflammatory state secondary to recent neoadjuvant chemotherapy, and labile hemodynamics in the setting of esophagectomy. In the second case, additional risk factors include hypercoagulability due to transfusion of blood products, or that a low-flow state in the subclavian artery may have been exacerbated by hypovolemia in the setting of gastrointestinal bleeding and the presence of a focal arterial dissection, which was noted proximal to the LIMA take-off on diagnostic angiography. In both cases, prothrombotic rebound after discontinuation of direct oral anticoagulant (case 1) and antiplatelet agents (case 2) may have contributed. In either case, diagnostic confirmation of acute thrombus superimposed on chronic calcific stenosis of the subclavian artery is critical as it has important implications for therapeutic management.

In contrast to subclavian steal caused by progressive calcific stenosis, acute thrombus formation has important diagnostic considerations. As demonstrated in these 2 cases, patients may not have a history of recurrent angina or vertebral insufficiency typical of chronic subclavian stenosis or coronary steal physiology. Physical exam findings such as differential pressure or signs of ischemia in the upper extremities may not manifest until after thrombus formation. Acute thrombus formation can suddenly precipitate myocardial ischemia with potentially catastrophic manifestations, including acute coronary syndrome and cardiac arrest. Therefore, a high index of suspicion for acute coronary subclavian steal physiology due to subclavian thrombosis should be considered in LIMA-LAD patients with sudden-onset angina or evidence of myocardial ischemia and known peripheral arterial disease. Although noninvasive imaging (CT imaging, Doppler ultrasound) may be helpful to identify flow-limiting obstructions in the proximal subclavian artery, given the potential for rapid hemodynamic instability and catastrophic cardiovascular collapse, selective subclavian angiography should be promptly considered to evaluate the subclavian system in these patients.

The therapeutic management of subclavian artery thrombus causing steal physiology requires careful consideration. Although standard endovascular techniques in balloon angioplasty, intravascular lithotripsy, and stenting are well described for calcific subclavian stenosis, the optimal management of acute thrombotic occlusions is less clear. As demonstrated in our first patient, systemic thrombolysis may transiently improve flow through the occluded vessel but does not appear to provide a durable solution. Two previous reports describe successful percutaneous thrombectomy without stent implantation for subclavian thrombus.4,8 Although these reports suggest that percutaneous thrombectomy alone may be a successful strategy, it carries the potential for incomplete thrombus removal, distal embolization into the LIMA or vertebral arteries, and propagation of dissection when present (as in case 2). In addition, the long-term durability of thrombectomy without stent reinforcement remains unclear. For these reasons, we elected to deploy a balloon expandable covered stent rather than perform primary thrombectomy. This approach allowed for the simultaneous exclusion of intraluminal thrombus, restoration of vessel patency, stabilization of any intimal disruptions, and the potential for durable treatment of the underlying calcific ostial stenosis. In both cases, completion angiography demonstrated widely patent stents with improved antegrade flow through the LIMA take-off. In addition to this angiographic result, there was rapid clinical improvement in both patients. Importantly, both patients remain stable from a cardiovascular perspective over an intermediate term follow-up. In addition to endovascular therapies, previous reports have described surgical left carotid to subclavian bypass as a viable option in this setting.9 Although such therapies may also produce a durable solution, they are more invasive and may not be optimal in unstable patients.

Conclusions

The present cases demonstrate that acute thrombotic occlusion of the subclavian artery is a rare cause of acute myocardial ischemia in patients with LIMA-dependent coronary circulation (Video 1). Selective subclavian angiography is required to confirm the diagnosis. Endovascular management with covered stent deployment is a reasonable approach to exclude intraluminal thrombus, establish vessel patency, and restore antegrade flow to the LIMA with durable results to the intermediate term.Take-Home Messages•Acute thrombotic occlusion of the left subclavian artery is an underrecognized but potentially catastrophic cause of myocardial ischemia in patients with prior LIMA-LAD bypass grafting.•Endovascular management with covered stent placement is an alternative to thrombectomy and can achieve durable results to the intermediate term.
