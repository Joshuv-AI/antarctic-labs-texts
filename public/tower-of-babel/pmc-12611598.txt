Removal of Extraradicular Separated Instrument by Targeted Endodontic Microsurgery Using the 3D‐Printed Guide and Trephine: A Case Report
Authors: Lin Yang, Liang Chen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThis case report aimed to present an accurate and minimally invasive approach to retrieve a separated instrument beyond the apical foramen, using a 3‐dimensional (3D) printing guide and trephine bur. A 31‐year‐old female patient was referred for treatment of the left maxillary central incisor, complaining that a fractured file had been pushed out of the root canal by her previous dentist approximately 2 weeks prior. On clinical examination, tooth #9 was temporarily sealed and slightly tender on percussion. Periodontal probing was within normal limits. Radiographic and cone‐beam computed tomographic imaging revealed a periapical radiolucent lesion at tooth #9, with a separated instrument approximately 3 mm long extruded approximately 2 mm beyond the apical foramen and 4 mm deep in the bone. The tooth was diagnosed with symptomatic apical periodontitis. After root canal treatment, endodontic surgery was performed to retrieve the extraradicular separated instrument with the aid of a 3D printing guide and trephine bur. The root end was ultrasonically prepared and retrograde filled. The patient was asymptomatic when recalled 3 months and 2 years after the surgery, and the follow‐up periapical radiograph showed successful healing of the periapical lesion. This method enables a single‐step operation, completing the removal of the extraradicular separated instrument while performing osteotomy.Keywords: 3D printed template, cone‐beam computed tomography, extruded instrument, guided endodontics, separated instrument retrieval

Summary

Targeted endodontic microsurgery using the 3D‐printed guide and trephine enables accurate and minimally invasive retrieval of separated instruments beyond the apical foramen, achieving simultaneous instrument retrieval and osteotomy in a single step.

Introduction

Instrument separation refers to the fracture of endodontic instruments during root canal therapy, which may obstruct disinfection of the root canal system. A recent review of the literature revealed that the fracture rate could range between 0.25% and 6% for stainless steel instruments, while for nickel‐titanium (NiTi) rotary instruments, the rate varies from 1.3% to 10.0% [1]. A number of treatment techniques have been described to manage instrument separation, including ultrasonic technique [2], trephine bur technique [3], micro‐tube and loop technique [4] and laser technique [5]. Nevertheless, the separated instrument (SI) may extrude beyond the apical foramen if excessive pressure is applied to the instrument [6, 7]. The location of the SI within the root canal, and the duration of the attempt to loosen it, are both significant factors in determining the potential for extrusion [6]. Few studies have evaluated the influence of a retained SI on the prognosis of endodontic treatment, but generally, a SI retained beyond the apical foramen is considered a failure in terms of instrument separation management [6, 8].

Recently, 3D‐printed guides used in endodontic surgery offer advantages like accurate root apex localization, soft tissue preservation, protection of critical anatomical structures, and time savings [11, 12, 13, 14, 15]. In addition, a 3D‐printed guide combined with a trephine bur has been used in cases of endodontic surgery to complete osteotomy, root‐end resection, and biopsy in a single step [16]. This technique was introduced as targeted EMS.

To address the challenges described above, this case report presents an accurate and safe method for retrieving an extraradicular SI using the combination of a 3D‐printed guide and trephine bur via a targeted EMS technique.

Case History and Examination

A 31‐year‐old female patient was referred for treatment of the left maxillary central incisor. She complained that there was a fragment of a fractured file that had been pushed out of the root canal by her previous dentist approximately 2 weeks prior, and she felt intermittent discomfort during mastication. Her medical history was unremarkable. On clinical examination, tooth #9 was temporarily sealed and slightly tender on percussion. Periodontal probing was within normal limits. Radiography and CBCT revealed a periapical radiolucent lesion at tooth #9, with a file fragment approximately 3 mm long extruded approximately 2 mm beyond the apical foramen and 4 mm deep in the bone (Figure 1A,B).

A separated instrument beyond the apex of tooth #9. (A, B) Preoperative periapical radiograph and CBCT image. (C) Periapical radiograph after initial root canal treatment.

Differential Diagnosis, Investigations and Treatment

The tooth was diagnosed with symptomatic apical periodontitis. Both non‐surgical and surgical methods for managing the SI were fully discussed with the patient. The patient agreed to non‐surgical treatment. In brief, Mtwo NiTi rotary instrumentation (VDW, Munich, Germany) was used to clean and shape the root canal in the following sequence: 10/0.04, 15/0.05, 20/0.06, and 25/0.06. Irrigation between each instrument was done using 6% sodium hypochlorite solution and 17% ethylene diamine tetraacetic acid (EDTA). An ultrasound system (P5 Newtron XS, SATELEC Inc., Viry‐Chatillon, France) was introduced to activate the irrigants. Subsequently, the root canal was dried with paper points, followed by calcium hydroxide medication placement in the root canal. Two weeks later, the root canal was obturated using the single‐cone technique with gutta‐percha (Dentsply Maillefer, Ballaigues, Switzerland) and a bioceramic sealer, iRoot SP (Innovative BioCreamix Inc., Vancouver, Canada). The tooth was then restored using flowable composite resin (Beautifil Flow Plus, Shofu Inc., Kyoto, Japan) and packable composite resin (Filtek Supreme Ultra, 3 M EPSE, St. Paul, MN, USA). So, the SI was retained in the apical area when the root canal treatment was completed over the course of two appointments (Figure 1C).

Two months later, the patient returned to the hospital, complaining that she still felt discomfort around the treated tooth during mastication and was worried about the SI. She agreed to have it completely removed by surgery. Therefore, surgical treatment with a 3D‐printed guide and trephine bur was conducted as follows.

A 3D reconstruction of the surgical site was obtained from the CBCT data using Materialize Mimics (Mimics Innovation Suite 18.0, Materialize Dental, Leuven, Belgium). The location of the SI was marked on the 3D model. The surgical guide was designed based on the location of the SI, nasopalatine duct, the trephine bur size, the surgical approach, and the flap design (Figure 2A,B). The surgical guide was fabricated using polylactic acid (PLA, Wiiboox, China) and 3D printing (WEEDO F260, Wiiboox, China) (Figure 2C).

Surgical guide design and fabrication. (A) The surgical guide was designed based on the separated instrument and related structures. (B) A trephine bur with a 4‐mm inner diameter was selected to correspond to the size of the separated instrument. (C) The surgical guide was fabricated by 3D printing.

Anesthesia was performed with the buccal and palatal infiltration of 2% mepivacaine hydrochloride with 1:100,000 epinephrine (Scandonest, Septodont, France) over the apex of tooth #9 and adjacent teeth to involve the entire surgical site. A full‐thickness triangular flap was created and elevated with a #15 scalpel blade and a #9 Molt periosteal elevator (Hu‐Friedy, USA). After retraction of the flap, the buccal bone was assessed, and there were no defects in the cortical plate. The surgical guide was fitted onto tooth #9 to verify the accuracy (Figure 3A).

Guided endodontic surgery. (A) The 3D‐printed guide was positioned on tooth #9. (The black arrow showed the guide). (B) The trephine bur was placed through the target ring and drilled into the bone under running saline. (The black arrow showed the trephine bur). (C) The bone core was created and extracted. (The black arrow showed bone core). (D, E) The bone core was submitted for radiographic examination. (F) Periapical radiograph immediately after the surgery. (G, H) Periapical radiograph 3 months and 2 years after the surgery. (I) Intraoral photograph 3 months after the surgery.

All surgical procedures were performed using a surgical operating microscope (OMS2350, Zumax, China). A trephine bur with a 4‐mm inner diameter was selected to correspond to the size of the SI (Figure 2B). The trephine bur was placed through the target ring and drilled into the bone under running saline (Figure 3B) until a depth of 5 mm was reached. The bone core created by the trephine bur (Figure 3C) was pulled out with the #9 Molt periosteal elevator and immediately submitted for radiographic examination, which revealed that the entire SI was simultaneously removed with the bone core (Figure 3D,E).

The root end was prepared using ultrasonically powered tips (Pure Newtron AS3D, Satelec Acteon, France) to a depth of 3 mm without root‐end resection and retrograde‐filled with Iroot BP (Innovative BioCreamix Inc., Vancouver, Canada). A periapical radiograph was taken to verify both correct root‐end filling and the absence of the fractured file fragment (Figure 3F). The surgical site was closed with 5/0 sutures, and the sutures were removed 7 days later.

The bone core with the SI inside was scanned by micro‐CT (VivaCT40, SCANCO, Switzerland) to further assess the accuracy of the guided surgery. The micro‐CT results showed that the SI was not cut by the trephine bur during the surgery (Figure 4A) and that the periapical lesion opened around the SI (Figure 4B).

Micro‐CT analysis. (A) 3D reconstruction showing that the entire separated instrument was located in the bone core. (B) Cross‐sectional images showing that the periapical lesion opened around the separated instrument.

Outcome and Follow‐Up

The patient was asymptomatic when recalled 3 months (Figure 3G,I) and 2 years (Figure 3H) after the surgery, and the follow‐up periapical radiograph showed a normal periodontal ligament space with no periapical lesion present.

Discussion

Study reported that computer‐guided techniques enabled more precise and consistent apical positioning compared with freehand techniques [17]. A study evaluated the accuracy of apical access assisted by 3D‐printed guides. The average deviation for freehand operations by dentists was 2.63 mm, while the average deviation when using 3D‐printed guides was 1.74 mm [18]. Targeted EMS combining 3D‐printed guide technology with trephine burs can protect surrounding soft tissues, neurovascular structures, and mucosa from unnecessary damage, while reducing postoperative pain and swelling [19, 20]. In vitro studies have shown that the length and angle deviations of apical resection during targeted EMS are significantly lower than those without guide application [21]. Meanwhile, targeted EMS demonstrates low technical sensitivity [22]. In the present case, the micro‐CT results showed that the bone core cut by the trephine bur with a 4‐mm inner diameter contained the entire 3‐mm SI, indicating that the use of a 3D‐printed guide may allow for accurate localization of the periapical region within 0.5 mm of deviation. These results are similar to those of previous studies.

With the improvement of 3D‐printed technology in precision and therapeutic effect, it has become a major breakthrough in the field of endodontics. 3D‐printed technology is applied in endodontics for the management of calcified root canals, apical surgery, non‐surgical root canal retreatment, autogenous tooth transplantation, and removal of separated instruments, etc. [23, 24]. However, there are limited studies and case reports on the retrieval of SI. The 3D‐printed guide is securely fixed in the oral cavity, enabling precise positioning of the separated site in the root canal, presetting the angle and depth required for instrument retrieval. The trephine is then utilized to expose the head of the SI, after which the SI is removed [25, 26]. In some case reports, the SI was retrieved through free‐hand microscopic endodontic surgery [27, 28]. Sudha et al. [29] and Kaddoura et al. [12] respectively used 3D‐printed guides to assist in the precise positioning of osteotomy for the extraction of separated instruments in the lower segment of the root canal. For extraradicular SI, Angaliya et al. used 3D‐printed guides to locate osteotomy and removed the extraradicular SI through a modified suction device with a delivery tip attached to the high vacuum suction dental unit [20]. To our knowledge, the present case report is the first to use 3D‐printed guides and trephines for the removal of SI completely located outside the apical foramen. This innovative method enables a single‐step operation, completing the removal of the separated instrument while performing osteotomy.

While the accuracy and precision of 3D‐printed guides are crucial for improving the efficiency and success rate of complex endodontic treatments, potential errors may accumulate throughout the digital workflow [30]. The design of 3D‐printed guides is technically challenging, often time‐consuming, and requires considerable expertise in digital software [31, 32]. Although intraoral scanning enhances the accuracy of 3D reconstructions, discrepancies may still occur [33]. The printing process itself is another critical factor. Although printers based on digital light processing (DLP) or stereolithography (SLA) are known to produce guides with higher accuracy [34], the guide in this case was fabricated using fused deposition modeling (FDM) due to equipment availability. FDM‐printed PLA guides, an FDA‐approved biocompatible material, have been widely used in surgical instruments and guides, such as in leg osteotomy and arthrodesis procedures [35]. While it is biocompatible and cost‐effective, it has lower rigidity and is more susceptible to deformation under mechanical stress. To mitigate this, the sleeve of the guide was thickened, a sharp trephine bur was used to reduce lateral forces, and the guide was fully supported on the occlusal surfaces to enhance stability. Furthermore, a guided pilot drill was first used to create a predefined path, through which the trephine was subsequently introduced, significantly improving alignment and reducing the risk of deviation. Meanwhile, it is critical to ensure that the guide remains stable and does not tilt or shift during the procedure. Inadequate stabilization or design inaccuracies of the guide can lead to iatrogenic accidents. A case report described an iatrogenic root perforation that occurred due to insufficient fixation of the endodontic guide [36]. To minimize such potential errors, it is advised to remove the guide to check whether the proper path will be followed during template‐guided surgery [37]. Additionally inspection windows can also be designed on the guide to check its accuracy [38].

Root‐end resection is one of the biological principles applied in EMS. Resection of 3 mm of the root end could remove the majority of anatomical variations, such as apical ramifications and lateral canals [39]. On the other hand, root‐end resection can reduce the crown‐to‐root ratio, which may lead to additional stress being placed on or rotational displacement of the treated tooth. Therefore, to ensure the long‐term success of endodontic surgery, it is important to take into account the biomechanical conditions of the apically resected tooth [40, 41]. In this case, the SI was located completely beyond the apical foramen.

Despite the endodontic surgery is performed without root‐end resection, the patient remains asymptomatic when recalled 2 years after the surgery. This was confirmed by periapical radiographic imaging, which demonstrated successful healing of the periapical lesion.

During targeted EMS, the corticocancellous bone core harvested with the trephine bur can be placed back into the operative site as an autogenous graft [16]. In this case, the bone core was submitted for radiographic examination and discarded, but for further consideration, if the fractured instrument fragment could be removed from the bone core within a limited time, the bone core can be used as an autogenous graft to benefit the healing of the periapical lesion.

Within the limitations of this case report, targeted endodontic microsurgery may be particularly useful in cases of SI extruded beyond the root foramen with no defects in the cortical plate. This technique could help the endodontist find the exact location of the SI, avoid displacement of the SI during the surgery, achieve the preservation of important structures and simplify the surgical procedure. Nevertheless, more clinical and laboratory studies should be performed to confirm the reliability and viability of this method in the future.
