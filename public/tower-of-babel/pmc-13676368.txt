Association between Perioperative Blood Glucose Levels and Wound Complications in Diabetic Patients Undergoing Adult Spinal Deformity Surgery: A Retrospective Cohort Study
Authors: Tomohiro Yamada, Yu Yamato, Tomohiko Hasegawa, Go Yoshida, Tomohiro Banno, Hideyuki Arima, Shin Oe, Hiroki Ushirozako, Koihiro Ide, Yusuke Murakami, Yukihiro Matsuyama
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Introduction Adult spinal deformity (ASD) surgery is highly invasive and carries a substantial risk of postoperative wound complications, particularly in patients with type 2 diabetes mellitus (DM). Perioperative hyperglycemia is a known risk factor for surgical-site infection, but glucose thresholds specific to patients with diabetes and ASD remain unclear. Methods We retrospectively reviewed 437 adults who underwent ASD correction between 2010 and 2020, of whom 50 (11.4%) had type 2 DM. Capillary blood glucose was measured at standardized intervals from the day of surgery through postoperative day 3 and at least once daily thereafter until discharge. For each diabetic patient, we calculated an overall mean perioperative glucose value (mean of all in-hospital measurements from postoperative day 0 to discharge) and maximum/mean glucose during postoperative days 0-3. These patient-level indices were compared between patients with and without wound complications, and receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis based on overall mean glucose was used to explore thresholds associated with wound morbidity. Results Wound complications occurred more often in patients with DM than those without DM (18.0% vs 3.1%, p<0.001). Among the patients with diabetes, overall mean perioperative glucose was higher in the wound-complication group than in the non-wound group (184.8±35.1 vs 156.1±26.6 mg/dL; p=0.020). ROC analysis of overall mean glucose yielded an area under the curve of 0.75 and suggested an exploratory threshold around 185 mg/dL. Patients with diabetes also had higher rates of systemic complications. Conclusions Type 2 DM was associated with substantially increased wound morbidity and systemic complications after ASD surgery. Higher overall mean perioperative glucose in patients with diabetes was linked to wound complications, and an exploratory threshold near 185 mg/dL showed moderate discriminatory ability. These findings support the concept that tighter perioperative glycemic control may reduce wound morbidity, although the proposed threshold should be regarded as hypothesis-generating and should be confirmed in larger prospective studies.

Introduction

Adult spinal deformity (ASD) surgery has become increasingly common as the aging population grows and surgical techniques advance, enabling more extensive corrections of spinopelvic malalignment and improved quality of life in older patients1,2). However, these procedures are among the most invasive in spinal surgery, often involving long operative times, high intraoperative blood loss, and multilevel fixation, thereby predisposing patients to wound-related complications such as surgical-site infection (SSI), dehiscence, and delayed healing3-5). Despite substantial improvements in perioperative management, wound complications remain a major cause of morbidity and healthcare costs, frequently leading to prolonged hospitalization, unplanned reoperation, and diminished postoperative satisfaction6,7). Identifying modifiable perioperative risk factors—particularly those related to metabolic and vascular status—is therefore essential for improving outcomes and for preventing wound-related failures in ASD surgery8,9).

Among numerous modifiable risk factors, perioperative hyperglycemia has consistently emerged as a critical determinant of postoperative wound complications. Elevated blood glucose impairs neutrophil chemotaxis and phagocytic activity, compromises collagen synthesis, and promotes oxidative stress and endothelial dysfunction, all of which impede tissue repair and host defense mechanisms10,11). In general and orthopedic surgery, postoperative glucose levels above 140-180 mg/dL have been independently associated with increased risk of SSI and wound dehiscence12-15). Current clinical guidelines from the American Diabetes Association and the Centre for Perioperative Care therefore recommend maintaining glucose between 140 and 180 mg/dL during the perioperative period16).

Given these considerations, it is clinically important to clarify how perioperative blood glucose relates to wound complications, specifically in patients with diabetes undergoing ASD correction, and to explore whether certain glycemic thresholds might be useful for risk stratification. However, these targets were derived primarily from cardiac or general surgery populations and their applicability to spine deformity surgery, which involves longer operative times, greater tissue dissection, and extensive instrumentation, remains uncertain17). Therefore, the aim of this study was to examine the association between perioperative blood glucose levels and postoperative wound complications in patients with ASD and type 2 diabetes, and to perform an exploratory analysis of patient-level glucose indices and potential threshold values that may inform future perioperative glycemic management strategies.

Materials and Methods

This retrospective cohort study included adult patients aged older than 40 years who underwent corrective fusion surgery for ASD at a single tertiary spine center between January 2010 and December 2020. A total of 437 consecutive patients were identified from the institutional surgical records. Patients with incomplete perioperative glucose monitoring data or missing complication records were excluded from further analysis. This study was approved by our local institutional review board, which waived the requirement for informed consent due to its retrospective nature.

Perioperative blood sugar control management

We defined diabetes mellitus (DM) as a new diagnosis of hemoglobin A1c (HbA1c) ≥6.5%, fasting blood glucose level ≥126 mg/dL, or a 2-h blood glucose level ≥200 mg/dL assessed with an oral glucose tolerance test18). In patients with DM, perioperative glycemic management was individualized according to the level of preoperative glycemic control. Patients with HbA1c level greater than 7.0% were routinely referred to an endocrinologist for specialized management. In such cases, insulin administration followed either a fixed-dose protocol or a sliding-scale regimen. The specific approach was selected on a case-by-case basis, taking into account the patient's chronic glycemic control, pre-existing antidiabetic therapy, and dynamic glucose fluctuations in the perioperative period. For patients whose HbA1c was ≤ 7.0%, perioperative glycemic control was managed directly by the surgical team using the same sliding-scale insulin protocol, applied at identical time intervals (before breakfast, lunch, dinner, and at bedtime).

In patients with type 2 diabetes, capillary blood glucose was measured four times on the day of surgery (early morning, immediately before transferring to the operating room, immediately after returning to the ward, and at bedtime). On postoperative days (PODs) 1-3, glucose was measured before breakfast, lunch, dinner, and at bedtime. Thus, most measurements were obtained in a fasting or semi-fasting state.

For each patient, we calculated (1) the maximum and mean glucose values during POD0-3 and (2) the overall mean perioperative glucose across all measurements during hospitalization (from POD0 to discharge). These patient-level indices were compared between patients with and without wound complications using the Mann–Whitney U test.

Complication assessment

All perioperative complications were identified through electronic medical records and postoperative follow-up documentations within 30 days of surgery. Complications were categorized into the following domains: (1) Wound-related: including superficial wound dehiscence and deep SSI; (2) Cardiovascular: myocardial infarction, arrhythmia, or cardiac arrest; (3) Respiratory: pneumonia or pulmonary complications requiring intervention; (4) Neurocognitive: postoperative delirium; (5) Renal and urological: urinary tract infection (UTI); (6) Hematologic: symptomatic postoperative hematoma requiring intervention; and (7) Gastrointestinal: acute abdomen, ileus, or significant gastrointestinal dysfunction.

Statistical analysis

Complication incidence was calculated for the diabetic subgroup and was stratified according to each complication type. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis based on these patient-level indices (primarily the overall mean perioperative glucose) was performed to explore glucose thresholds associated with the development of wound complications. The area under the curve (AUC) was used to evaluate predictive accuracy. Sensitivity, specificity, and corresponding confidence intervals were reported. In addition to univariable comparisons, we performed a patient-level multivariable logistic regression analysis to explore independent risk factors for postoperative wound complications. The dependent variable was the occurrence of any wound complication (yes/no). Candidate predictors were selected a priori based on clinical relevance and included DM, age, body mass index (BMI), American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) class, operative time, intraoperative blood loss, three-column osteotomy use, pelvic fixation, staged surgery, smoking status, and chronic steroid use. All the variables were entered simultaneously using the enter method, and results are presented as odds ratios with 95% confidence intervals.

In the subgroup of patients with type 2 diabetes, patient-level summary indices of glycemic exposure (overall mean perioperative glucose and maximum/mean glucose during POD0-3) were compared between patients with and without wound complications using the Mann–Whitney U test. All statistical computations were performed using Statistical Package for SPSS (version 26.0; IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Values of p<0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Results

Patient cohort and baseline characteristics

A total of 437 patients who underwent ASD surgery were included, of whom 50 (11.4%) had type 2 DM and 387 (88.6%) did not have it. Baseline characteristics are summarized in Table 1. Patients in the DM group tended to be slightly older than those without DM (70.6±7.1 vs 68.2±6.8 years, p=0.068), with no significant difference in sex distribution (men: 80.0% vs 82.4%, p=0.673). Mean BMI was significantly higher in patients with DM (24.5±3.7 vs 22.8±3.8 kg/m2, p=0.002). The underlying spinal pathology (vertebral fracture, Parkinson disease, kyphoscoliosis, adolescent scoliosis, kyphosis, and iatrogenic fracture) was comparable between the groups (all p>0.05). Bone mineral density T-scores were similar in patients with and without DM (−1.4 vs −1.6, p=0.36). With respect to preoperative physical status, most patients in both the groups were classified as ASA grade 2; however, the proportion of ASA grade 3 was higher in the DM group (20.0% vs 7.0%), whereas no patient with DM was classified as ASA grade 1, reflecting a greater burden of systemic comorbidity among patients with DM.

Surgical characteristics

Surgical information is shown in Table 2. The majority of procedures in both the groups were performed via a posterior-only approach (68.0% in DM vs 51.4% in non-DM), with similar proportions undergoing staged anterior–posterior or posterior–anterior reconstructions. Perioperative complications according to diabetes status

Perioperative complications are summarized in Table 3. The incidence of wound complications was significantly higher in patients with DM than in those without DM (9/50, 18.0% vs 12/387, 3.1%; p<0.001). Diabetes was associated with an approximately sevenfold increase in the odds of wound complications (odds ratio [OR] 6.9, 95% confidence interval [CI] 2.7-17.3). In addition to wound events, several other perioperative complications were more frequent in the DM group. The incidence of delirium was significantly higher in the patients with DM (24.0% vs 12.9%, p=0.035). Cardiac disorders occurred in 8.0% of the patients with DM compared with 1.8% of the patients without DM (p=0.044). Acute abdominal complications were also more common in the DM group (12.0% vs 3.6%, p<0.001). Postoperative hematoma was observed in 4.0% of the patients with DM versus 0.8% of patients without DM (p=0.049). In contrast, rates of thrombotic events, pulmonary complications, and UTIs did not differ significantly between the groups (all p>0.05).

Perioperative blood glucose levels and wound complications in patients with type 2 diabetes

Among the patients with diabetes, those who developed wound complications had significantly higher overall mean perioperative glucose levels (mean of all in-hospital measurements from POD0 to discharge) than those without wound complications (184.8±35.1 vs 156.1±26.6 mg/dL; p=0.020). In contrast, neither the maximum nor the mean glucose values limited to POD0-3 differed significantly between the groups.

When wound complications were stratified into no complication (n=41), superficial dehiscence (n=6), and deep SSI (n=3), the overall mean perioperative glucose showed a stepwise increase across the categories (156.1±26.6, 173.5±37.3, and 207.3±17.7 mg/dL, respectively; p=0.024). Pairwise testing demonstrated significantly higher glucose levels in patients with deep infection compared with those without wound complications (p=0.011), whereas differences involving superficial dehiscence did not reach statistical significance, likely due to the small sample size.

To address concerns regarding potential reverse causality, we conducted a supplementary analysis using only glucose values measured prior to the diagnosis of wound complications. ROC analysis of these pre-event glucose values demonstrated moderate discriminatory ability for predicting wound complications (AUC 0.63). The optimal cutoff based on Youden index was 163 mg/dL, indicating that patients with pre-complication glucose levels above this threshold were more likely to develop postoperative wound events. This analysis supports a temporal association between hyperglycemia and wound complications and reinforces the importance of proactive glycemic control in the early perioperative period.

The ROC analysis indicated that the overall mean perioperative glucose had the best discriminatory ability for wound complications among patients with diabetes (AUC 0.75). The optimal Youden index identified a threshold of approximately 185 mg/dL, yielding a sensitivity of 0.67 and specificity of 0.85 (Figure 1). By contrast, indices restricted to POD0-3 showed only modest discrimination (AUC 0.59-0.63).

Discussion

In this study, we evaluated perioperative glycemic patterns and postoperative complications in ASD patients with type 2 DM and explored patient-level blood glucose indices associated with wound complications, identifying an exploratory threshold that may be useful for risk stratification rather than as a definitive treatment target. Our analysis demonstrated that perioperative glucose levels were significantly higher in patients who developed wound-related events compared with those who did not, and ROC analysis revealed an exploratory threshold around 185 mg/dL with only moderate discriminative ability (AUC 0.75). Importantly, patients with diabetes exhibited a sevenfold increase in wound complications compared with the patients without diabetes, reinforcing the substantial vulnerability of this subgroup. Together, these findings highlight the importance of considering diabetes-specific perioperative glucose thresholds for risk stratification in ASD surgery, and provide preliminary quantitative data to inform the design of future interventional studies, rather than to immediately define fixed perioperative management targets.

In the present cohort, the association between diabetes and wound complications remained evident even after accounting for key demographic and surgical covariates. In our patient-level multivariable logistic regression model, DM was independently associated with an increased odd of postoperative wound complications, whereas other factors such as age, BMI, ASA class, operative time, blood loss, three-column osteotomy, and pelvic fixation did not reach statistical significance. Although the limited number of events restricts the complexity and precision of the model, these adjusted findings support the notion that diabetes per se, and not only the greater surgical invasiveness often seen in patients with diabetes, contributes to heightened wound vulnerability. This independent effect is consistent with previous reports linking diabetes to impaired tissue perfusion, immune dysfunction, and delayed wound healing, and provides additional context for our exploratory analyses of perioperative glycemic control.

Our findings are consistent with previous studies demonstrating that DM is a significant risk factor for postoperative wound morbidity in spine surgery19,20). However, this study extends the literature by providing an exploratory perioperative glucose threshold specific to diabetic patients undergoing ASD correction. Prior investigations in general or orthopedic surgery have reported that postoperative glucose levels exceeding 140-180 mg/dL10,21) are associated with increased SSI rates, but these thresholds were derived from heterogeneous surgical populations with substantially lower operative complexity. In the spine field, Hikata et al.22) reported that elevated preoperative HbA1c (>6.5%) increased SSI risk following posterior lumbar and thoracic fusion, whereas several studies identified postoperative hyperglycemia as an independent predictor of infection in orthopedic procedures9,19,23). Nevertheless, none of these studies evaluated real-time perioperative glucose patterns or established a cutoff that reflects the physiological stress and prolonged invasiveness characteristic of ASD surgery. By demonstrating that wound complications were significantly more common at perioperative glucose levels above approximately 185 mg/dL, our study suggests that patients with ASD and type 2 diabetes may be at an increased risk above this range and that more stringent glycemic targets could be explored in future studies for risk stratification, rather than being immediately adopted as routine clinical practice. These findings underscore the potential value of diabetes-specific perioperative risk stratification strategies in complex deformity correction and support further investigation into proactive perioperative glycemic management, rather than providing definitive treatment thresholds.

The association between perioperative hyperglycemia and wound complications observed in our cohort is biologically plausible and supported by extensive mechanistic evidence. Hyperglycemia induces a broad spectrum of immune, microvascular, and cellular dysfunctions that collectively impair wound healing. Elevated glucose levels inhibit neutrophil chemotaxis, phagocytosis, and oxidative burst activity, thereby compromising early innate immune responses that are essential for bacterial clearance and prevention of SSI24). Experimental studies have shown that short-term hyperglycemia can suppress leukocyte mobilization and impair reactive oxygen species generation, reducing the capacity to control microbial contamination introduced during surgery25). Furthermore, hyperglycemia promotes endothelial dysfunction and increases microvascular permeability26), resulting in tissue edema and impaired oxygen diffusion—conditions that are particularly detrimental within the extensive posterior exposure and the dead space created during ASD surgery. The significant increase in wound events at glucose levels above an exploratory threshold around 185 mg/dL observed in this study is therefore consistent with the underlying biology and supports further investigation into proactive perioperative glycemic management strategies in this population.

Several limitations should be acknowledged when interpreting the findings of this study. First, perioperative glucose management was not standardized across all patients with type 2 diabetes; instead, insulin therapy—whether fixed-dose, basal–bolus, or sliding scale—was selected individually based on each patient's metabolic status and on the discretion of the treating endocrinologist or surgical team. Such variability may have introduced treatment-related heterogeneity that could influence perioperative glucose trajectories and consequently, wound outcomes. Second, although the study included a substantial number of perioperative glucose measurements, we relied on intermittently sampled capillary glucose values to construct patient-level summary indices rather than using continuous glucose monitoring. As a result, our metrics may not fully capture rapid fluctuations or nocturnal glycemic variability that could also influence wound healing. Third, this was a single-center retrospective study, and institutional protocols, surgical techniques, and postoperative care pathways may limit the generalizability of our findings to other settings. These limitations highlight the need for future prospective studies with standardized perioperative glycemic protocols and comprehensive monitoring to verify and refine the optimal glucose target for patients with high-risk diabetes undergoing complex deformity correction.

In conclusion, among patients with type 2 diabetes undergoing ASD surgery, higher overall mean perioperative blood glucose levels are associated with an increased incidence of postoperative wound complications, particularly deep SSI. ROC analysis indicated that an average perioperative glucose in the range of approximately 185 mg/dL provides only moderate discrimination for predicting wound problems. Accordingly, this value should not be interpreted as a definitive management target but rather as an exploratory threshold that may help to inform the design of future interventional studies on perioperative glycemic control. Given the retrospective, single-center nature of our cohort, the limited number of events, and the non-standardized diabetes management, our findings should be considered hypothesis-generating, and they should be validated in larger, prospective, multicenter cohorts before being translated into firm clinical recommendations.
