Corticosteroid-associated osteonecrosis in paediatric compared with adult patients: are they the same disease?
Authors: Takahiro Igei, Gabriella B. Smith, Simon Kwoon-Ho Chow, Amishi Jobanputra, Bryan Wen Khoo, Kevin G. Shea, Stuart B. Goodman
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Osteonecrosis of the femoral head (ONFH) is a debilitating condition with a poorly characterized pathogenesis. Among the reported aetiologies, excessive alcohol consumption and corticosteroid administration are leading causes. Corticosteroids are often administered as a life-saving treatment for primary oncological or autoimmune conditions, but the subsequent development of ONFH can significantly impair the quality of life of both paediatric and adult patients. This review provides a comprehensive summary and critical analysis of recent advances in the understanding of corticosteroid-associated ONFH, with a focus on the distinctions between paediatric and adults patients. By elucidating age-dependent biological mechanisms and clinical characteristics, this work aims to enhance understanding of the disease’s pathogenesis. These insights may facilitate the development of improved strategies for early diagnosis and timely intervention, with the goal of preserving the functional and biomechanical integrity of the femoral head and improving patient outcomes across different ages.Cite this article: Bone Jt Open 2026;7(5):682–691.

Introduction

Osteonecrosis (ON) is the death of bone marrow due to inadequate blood supply, and a debilitating condition with a pathogenesis that remains poorly characterized.1 Osteonecrosis of the femoral head (ONFH) affects patients’ quality of life (QoL), leading to constant pain, difficulty walking, and limitations in completing activities of daily living as the condition progresses, though some patients may be asymptomatic or minimally symptompatic, especially in the earlier stages.

ONFH accounts for 20,000 new cases per year in the USA, and 8% to 10% of all hip arthroplasties performed in the USA.2-5 ON is associated with corticosteroid use, excessive alcohol intake, radiation, chemotherapy, and trauma.1,6-8 Across ages, corticosteroids are often administered as a life-saving treatment for primary autoimmune or oncological conditions. In adults, corticosteroids are also widely used in the treatment of autoimmune diseases such as systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE), inflammatory bowel disease (IBD), rheumatoid arthritis (RA), and other diseases such as asthma and kidney transplantation6,9 (Table I).

In paediatric populations, primary diseases commonly associated with corticosteroid use include acute lymphoblastic leukaemia (ALL), Hodgkin lymphoma, and SLE.10-13 A recent multicentre review of large health systems database records showed that corticosteroid use is associated with ON development for paediatric patients with acute lymphoblastic leukaemia, systemic lupus erythematous, Crohn’s disease, juvenile idiopathic arthritis, and solid organ transplants, especially renal disease and post renal transplantation.13,14

Due to improvements in the treatment of ALL, the survival rate in paediatric patients is now approximately 90% ten years after diagnosis.15 However, this improvement in survival rates is closely linked to high-dose corticosteroid treatment, which in turn contributes to increased rates of ON.16 Corticosteroids are reported to increase the expression of nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide phosphate (NADP)H oxidase (NOX) subunits, suppress the phosphoinositide 3-kinase-protein kinase B (PI3K-AKT) signalling pathway, and induce mitochondrial dysfunction, all of which collectively contribute to an increase in reactive oxygen species (ROS) generation (Figure 1).

In addition, corticosteroids down-regulate key antioxidant enzymes, including catalase, superoxide dismutase (SOD), γ-glutamylcysteine synthetase (γ-GCSc), hemeoxygenase1 (HO-1). There are presumed physiological differences between paediatric and adult patients, including the presence of epiphyseal growth plates,11 blood supply to the femoral head,17 and metabolic factors, such as bone turnover, lipid metabolism,18 and glucose metabolism.12,19 However, no previous studies have comprehensively examined corticosteroid-associated ONFH stratified by paediatric and adult populations. This review summarizes and analyzes the latest findings on corticosteroid-associated ONFH with a focus on the differences between paediatric and adult populations. In this review, paediatric ON is defined as patients who developed ON after receiving corticosteroid treatment under the age of 18 years in terms of bone maturity, though several studies include adolescents up to the age of 20 years in combined paediatric and adolescent analyses. By examining age-specific mechanisms and clinical features in the existing literature, this review aims to contribute to a better understanding of the disease’s pathogenesis and to the identification of strategies for early detection and timely intervention.

Corticosteroid-associated ONFH

Pathogenesis

The pathogenesis of corticosteroid-associated ONFH is likely multifactorial with genetic, environmental, and other components contributing to the development and progression of the disease. Corticosteroids have a direct toxic effect that leads to cellular apoptosis, impairment of bone turnover,20-22 and a decrease in arterial blood flow to the femoral head due to thrombus or embolus.23-25 Some studies liken ONFH to an intraosseous compartment syndrome.26 On the cellular level, corticosteroids have the effect of promoting the differentiation of mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs) into adipocytes,27 increasing intracellular lipid synthesis, and inducing hypertrophy of adipocytes. This process can lead to intraosseous hypertension, decreased blood flow, and intravascular coagulation that eventually causes ischaemia around the femoral head.27-30 If sufficient fibrinolysis and angiogenesis are not achieved, the persistence of ischaemia can lead to bone marrow necrosis and osteocyte death, resulting in femoral head collapse and subchondral fractures.26,31 Therefore, genetic factors such as vascular thrombosis, decreased fibrinolysis, decreased angiogenesis, and conditions that lead to vascular compromise can impact both the blood flow and recovery in the femoral head. Genetic factors predisposing to ON include Protein C and S deficiencies, mutations in factor V Leiden or the prothrombin 20210 A gene, and polymorphisms of the plasminogen activator inhibitor-1 gene.26,32 In addition, genes related to corticosteroid metabolism, such as CYP3A4, CYP450, and ABCB1, are associated with the onset of ONFH.33,34

Incidence of osteonecrosis ALL and SLE patients

In ALL patients, reported rates of ON are 0.9% to 4% in those under ten years of age, 8.9% to 29% in patients aged ten to 20 years of age, and 8% in those over 20 years, indicating a higher risk in adolescents and young adults.35-38 A recent systematic review of ON in patients between one and 21 years of age, demonstrated rates of ON development in up to 39% of patients on steroids.13 In patients with SLE, the incidence of ON also increases with age.39,40 Nakamura et al reported that patients over 15 years of age had a tenfold higher incidence of ON than those aged under 15 years.39 Moreover, corticosteroid-associated ON is also common in SLE patients, with reported rates of 3% to 6% in patients aged under 15 years, 49% to 51% in those aged 15 to 20 years, and 39% to 41% in patients aged over 20 years40,41 (Table II). These findings indicate that both age and SLE are important risk factors for the development of ON.

Dosage of corticosteroid as an important risk factor

In adult patients, corticosteroid associated ONFH is defined as a patient who has a history of corticosteroid use of > 2,000 mg of prednisolone or its equivalent within three months according to the Association Research Circulation Osseous (ARCO) classification26,42 (Table III). The mean cumulative dose of corticosteroid administered to SLE patients who developed ON was 11,000 mg, and the mean maximum daily dose was 33 mg.43 A prospective study of SLE patients also reported that corticosteroid administration of 60 mg/day was associated with a ten times higher risk of ONFH compared with patients taking less than 20 mg/day.44

In children, the dose-dependent relationship between corticosteroids and ONFH has also been characterized. Moriya et al46 reported that the cumulative dose of dexamethasone is associated with risk of ON. Liu et al9 investigated the risk factors of ON in children with immune kidney disease, and reported that total cumulative dose of prednisone and methylprednisolone were independent risk factors. In patients with ALL, some articles reported that the cumulative dose of glucocorticoid correlated with the risk of ON.11,47 Girard et al48 reported the cumulative incidence of ON in childhood with ALL was 23.8% after transplantation for patients with the dose higher than 2,055 mg/m2.

The use of alternate week dexamethasone reduces the risk of osteonecrosis in paediatric patients with ALL, compared with continuous use.37 Dexamethasone has approximately six- to seven-times the potency of prednisolone, a longer half-life, and has less propensity for central nervous system (CNS) recurrence, thereby increasing event-free survival.49 However, a large multicentre database study demonstrated high rates of ON development in older paediatric patients and those receiving dexamethasone, compared with prednisone or prednisolone.13

Age-related changes

Skeletal maturity: One of the major differences between younger patients and adults is the degree of skeletal maturity.11 In addition to differences in height and weight, the epiphyseal growth plate of the femur closes at approximately 14 years of age in females and 16 years in males (Table IV).50 Before physeal closure, the primary blood to the femoral head is provided by the lateral epiphyseal vessels bypassing the physeal barrier; the skeletally immature femoral head is more vulnerable compared with adults.51 After physeal closure, the blood supply to the femoral head becomes more stable, having a source through the physeal scar region and the lateral epiphyseal vessels.52 While most clinical and epidemiological studies rely on age-based definitions, future investigation into differences in ON stratified by skeletal maturity are warranted.

Lipid and glucose metabolism over time: Sex hormones decline with age in adults, cholesterol levels rise, and lipid disorders and arteriosclerosis becomes more likely (Table IV).53 Patients with corticosteroid-associated ONFH have significantly higher levels of total cholesterol, triglycerides, compared with control patients, suggesting a relationship with the development of ONFH.54 Lipid metabolism has been reported to contribute to the development of ONFH; peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor γ(PPARγ) is activated by corticosteroid administration, promoting adipogenesis while inhibiting osteogenesis (Figure 1).55

In a systematic review, the risk of ON in sites other than the jaw in patients with diabetes was elevated, though not statistically significant.56 Although the association between diabetes and ON remains inconclusive, increased BMI has been consistently identified as an independent risk factor for ON in paediatric patients with ALL.57

Oxidative stress: Oxidative stress can induce defects in cellular differentiation and apoptosis, affecting the skeletal system.55 Peroxidation after corticosteroid administration is considered an important factor in ON.58 Long-term corticosteroid administration affects physiological balance between oxidative and glycolytic metabolism and leads to oxidative stress.59 Moreover, antioxidant enzymes (catalase, superoxide dismutase (SOD), γ-glutamylcysteine synthetase (γ-GCSc), and hemeoxygenase-1 (HO-1)) are down-regulated by corticosteroids.60,61 The down-regulation of antioxidant enzymes is related to osteoclast markers (cathepsin K and receptor activator of nuclear factor kappa-Β ligand (RANKL)) and increased osteoclast formation and activity.59 These reactions cause an increase in ROS levels, osteoclasts, and empty osteocyte lacunae, eventually leading to altered bone structure and ONFH.

ROS are highly reactive oxygen-derived molecules that are generated as by-products of normal cellular metabolism in mitochondria.55,62 ROS increase vascular permeability and cause oxidative damage to DNA, leading to apoptosis and necrosis of cells.58

Ageing is significantly related to the generation of ROS and the response to oxidative stress.63 Mitochondrial function declines and oxidative stress increases with age (Table IV).64 Oxidative stress is lower in paediatric patients due to the activation of the antioxidant systems associated with increased growth hormone during adolescence.65

Diagnosis

MRI is the gold standard to diagnose ONFH in both children and adults.66 Early detection of ONFH is critical to promote timely intervention to increase the ability to alter the progression of the process. Common imaging features across diagnostic methods in patients with ONFH include:

Like adults, diagnostic criteria for children with corticosteroid-associated ONFH rely on clinical presentation and imaging with MRI as the gold standard for early detection.11 Currently, screening MRI has been employed in research settings67 but is not the standard of care.68 Corticosteroid-associated ONFH can be detected through careful follow-up including periodic history and physical examination, and selective MRI studies.9 A high index of suspicion should be maintained in patients treated with corticosteroids.

In adults, the differential diagnoses include osteoarthritis of the hip, transient femoral head necrosis, subchondral fragility fractures of the femoral head, and rapidly destructive arthritis of the hip.69 In paediatric patients, differential diagnoses include Perthes' disease and slipped capital femoral epiphysis (SCEF).70 Perthes' disease is an idiopathic condition in which transient interruption of blood flow to the proximal femur leads to ischaemia of the femoral head.71 Of note, SCFE does not necessarily cause ONFH, but OFNH development is more likely with unstable SCFE, and in some cases, the treatment of this condition may contribute to the development of ONFH secondarily.72,73

Treatment options

Several treatment options exist for ONFH, including core decompression and cell therapy, femoral osteotomy, total hip arthroplasty (THA), with THA as the definitive treatment. Investigation into strategies to prevent joint collapse and degeneration in patients at risk for ON is an ongoing area of study.

Core decompression and cell therapy: Arlet et al77 reported core decompression (CD) as a treatment of ONFH in adults that prevents the collapse of the femoral head.78 CD involves drilling a hole from the lateral side of the femur to the necrotic area of the femoral head to reduce the intraosseous pressure and promote angiogenesis, thereby promoting new bone formation and preventing the progression of the condition.79 Recently, combination therapy involving the administration of autologous bone marrow aspirate concentrate (BMAC) or mesenchymal stromal cells (MSCs) following CD has been reported.80 In adults, combination therapy with CD and BMAC has been reported to reduce the risk of femoral head collapse and THA compared with CD alone81-83 (Table VI).

Zhao et al84 performed a randomized study in adults with pre-collapse ONFH and demonstrated that CD with MSCs improved Harris Hip Scores and reduced the necrotic volume compared with CD alone. Although, CD with MSC therapy can delay the progression of ONFH in early stage patients, whether regeneration of the necrotic area takes place is still unresolved.85 Furthermore, ageing is associated with a decrease in the number and proliferative capacity of MSCs in the bone marrow.86

In paediatric ALL, there are concerns about the risk of seeding leukaemic cells to other sites and about the effects of chemotherapy on the differentiation of MSC.87 MSCs can differentiate into various types of cells, such as chondrocytes and osteoblasts, and are also involved in macrophage recruitment, making them an essential factor in bone regeneration.88

Femoral osteotomy: One of the surgeries to address ONFH is transtrochanteric rotational osteotomy. Transtrochanteric rotational osteotomy was first reported by Sugioka.89,90 Kawano et al91 reported the ten-year survival rate of rotation osteotomy in adult patients with ONFH was 85.4%. Under 20 years of age, the hip joint preservation rate at a mean follow-up period of 14 years was 92%.92 Another study showed rotational osteotomy had excellent success rates (82% to 100%, three to 15 years)69 (Table VI). This surgery may be indicated for paediatric ONFH.

THA: In paediatric patients, the reported implant survival rate of THA ranges 86% to 95% (five to ten years of follow-up).93,94 However, Swarup et al95 reported the 20-year implant survival rate was 48% in patients aged under 25 years. In adult patients, the survival rate ranges from 89% to 94% (15 to 16 years)96,97 (Table VI). THA is generally considered a last resort option only after other treatment methods have been evaluated and considered non-tenable. Additionally, paediatric patients face a higher risk of implant wear and subsequent revision surgery due to their longer life expectancy.

Other: Bisphosphonates are treatments for osteoporosis to suppress osteoclast activity. Although this drug has been also used wisely for ONFH, evidence of the clinical outcomes is limited. For paediatric patients with ALL, Agarwala et al98 reported that the combination of intravenous zolendronic acid and oral alendronate provided a satisfactory outcome, 91% had not required any surgical treatment. However, Yuan et al99 reported that bisphosphonate could not delay the progression of ONFH. As a treatment option, using bisphosphonate for ONFH is still controversial.

Conclusion

The pathogenesis of corticosteroid-associated ONFH is currently considered to be multifactorial and incompletely characterized. Predisposing diseases and risk factors vary with age. Understanding the differences in incidence of ONFH based on primary diseases, as well as the relationship between corticosteroid dosages across different ages, is crucial for estimating the risk of developing ON following corticosteroid administration.

In the existing literature, it is difficult to dissect the individual contribution of underlying diagnosis, corticosteroid exposure, and age in the development of ON. Given that for many of the common autoimmune and oncological conditions in which patients develop ON, few do not receive corticosteroids, there are limited data to allow us to differentiate between the condition compared with corticosteroids; though in vivo studies support that corticosteroid administration alone induces ON, inducing osteonecrosis solely through SLE or renal transplantation remains challenging. Additionally, while some studies compare paediatric with adult ON,6 others focus on one population limiting our ability to isolate the role of age. We posit that corticosteroids are the primary driver with factors relating to age, skeletal maturity, and cellular differences likely modifying this relationship. The data in paediatric and adults patients remain limited and may be insufficient in many cases. Longitudinal studies investigating ONFH in paediatric patients as well as comparing paediatric and adults patients are warranted.

While there are substantial reports on the combined use of CD and cell therapy in adults, evidence in paediatric patients remains limited. Transtrochanteric rotational osteotomy has been reported to provide favourable prognosis in some patient populations, but THA remains the definitive treatment option for degenerative change associated with ONFH. In summary, this review characterizes the cellular aetiology, epidemiology, risk factors, and treatment options for ONFH in both adults and children. Improved understanding of ONFH, its pathogenesis, and effective interventions may not only allow for improved understanding regarding the similarities and differences between the condition in adults and children but also clinical and research initiatives to promote preservation of the hip joint across all ages.

Take home message

- Corticosteroid-associated osteonecrosis differs between paediatric and adult patients in terms of primary disease, steroid exposure, bone, lipid, and angiogenetic metabolism.

- Recognizing these age-dependent differences may facilitate earlier diagnosis and timely intervention to preserve joint function.
