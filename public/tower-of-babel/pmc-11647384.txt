Comprehensive spinal correction rehabilitation (CSCR) study: a randomised controlled trial to investigate the effectiveness of CSCR in children with early-onset idiopathic scoliosis on spinal deformity, somatic appearance, functional status and quality of life in Shanghai, China
Authors: Yingxian Li, Xin Li, Qimeng Fan, Juping Liang, Tongtong Zhang, Zhengquan Chen, Haibin Guo, Lili Cai, Xuan Zhou, Qing Du
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionPresently, the physiotherapy scoliosis-specific exercise recommended by the International Scientific Society on Scoliosis Orthopaedic and Rehabilitation Treatment guidelines is primarily advised for adolescents with scoliosis, and there is a lack of individualised and precise exercise therapy for children with early-onset idiopathic scoliosis (EOIS), whose bodies are in the nascent stages of development. Therefore, this study aims to investigate the efficacy of comprehensive spinal correction rehabilitation in treating EOIS.Methods and analysisThis study will be designed as a randomised controlled trial, and the subjects participating in the study will consist of 122 EOIS patients between the ages of 7–10 years. Subjects will be randomly assigned to an experimental group (n=61) and a control group (n=61). Both groups will be treated for 6 months, with the experimental group receiving comprehensive spinal correction rehabilitation (including specific exercise therapy, balance and stability training, breathing training, strength training, home-based exercise and bracing) and the control group receiving core stability training. Enrolled subjects will be evaluated at the initial visit and after 6 months of treatment, and data will be processed and analysed after collecting data from both evaluations. The primary outcome index is the major curvature Cobb angle, and the secondary outcome indices are the angle of trunk rotation, plumb line distance, the Trunk Aesthetic Clinical Evaluation index, forced vital capacity and the Scoliosis Research Society-22 questionnaire.Ethics and disseminationThe current study received ethical approval from the Xinhua Hospital Ethics Committee Affiliated to Shanghai Jiao Tong University School of Medicine (XHEC-C-2023-108-3). Written informed consent will be obtained from all participants and their guardians. Any interim analyses and full results will be published in an international peer-reviewed journal.Trial registration numberThe study protocol was registered at www. Clinical trials. gov (number identifier: ChiCTR2300078359).

Introduction

Early-onset idiopathic scoliosis (EOIS) is defined as idiopathic scoliosis (IS) that presents before 10 years of age.1 It can be further categorised into infantile idiopathic scoliosis (IIS), from birth to 3 years and 11 months, and juvenile idiopathic scoliosis (JIS), from 4 to less than 10 years.2 Although a small number of studies have shown the prevalence of EOIS to be less than 1%, its high rate of progression makes the disease still worthy of attention.3 4 An increase in the Cobb angle of 5° or more is considered progression, and a meta-analysis showed the progression rate of EOIS is 49%.5 As children with EOIS age, the progressive spinal deformity affects the normal alignment of the spine, resulting in uneven strength of the paravertebral muscles. This uneven strength of the paravertebral muscles of the spine can cause sagittal imbalance and even make the child appear to have postural asymmetry.68 In addition, the progressive deformity gradually alters the standard thoracic structure, thereby limiting the adequate expansion of the thoracic cavity during respiration. Alterations in thoracic structure also affect alveolar proliferation, leading to endogenous alveolar hypoplasia. Incomplete alveolar development causes irreversible changes in the lung structure. This alteration ultimately results in pulmonary dysfunction and affects the quality of life of the child.912 Therefore, it is essential to slow or stop the progression of spinal deformity as much as possible in the early stages of EOIS.

The clinical management of EOIS is complicated by the fact that it involves a complex balancing act between thoracic and pulmonary maturation and spinal development.13 Current treatments for EOIS include surgical treatment, serial casting, bracing, halo-gravity traction and exercise therapy. Among them, surgical treatment has been regarded as salvage treatment for failed conservative treatment. This is because surgical treatment is traumatic for children and has many postoperative complications, which are not conducive to their normal growth and development. Serial casting (for cases with less than 2 years old) and bracing (for cases with an age range of 1–10 years) are the recommended non-operative treatment of EOIS. To preserve the growth potential of the spine, EOIS at younger ages, especially before age two, could achieve a non-negligible benefit from serial casting for delaying the first surgery.1416 A previous study reported a 69% (94 IIS) complete correction ratio for serial casting in 136 IIS children.17 A recent cohort study reported a success rate of 49% for serial casting in IIS patients whose age range at the time of the first cast was 9 to 63 months.18 Nevertheless, the serial casting procedure involves multiple exposures to anaesthesia for the child, which might result in different levels of harm to the child’s neurological growth. The Food and Drug Administration issued a ‘Drug Safety Communication’ in December 2016, advising that general anaesthesia and sedatives administered to children younger than 3 years old ‘could affect the development of children’s brains’.19 This statement highlights the importance of bracing and exercise therapy in treating patients with EOIS. Moreover, bracing is not merely a conservative approach but also a complementary strategy alongside serial casting or perioperatively.4 20 A long-term study tracking the efficacy of bracing in EOIS patients aged 0–3 years until they reached skeletal maturity or underwent spinal fusion demonstrated that out of 29 patients, 9 (which is 31%) benefited from the treatment over a period of 7 to 15 years.21 Halo gravity traction is mainly used preoperatively to improve scoliosis deformity and enhance surgical outcomes. The first four treatments are mainly for patients with moderate or severe EOIS, while exercise therapy can be recommended as a treatment for mild cases.1314 2224 In recent years, there have been several conventional exercise therapies used in the conservative management of IS, such as core stability exercises (CSEs), Pilates and yoga, of which CSEs are the most widely used.2527 CSE is designed to stimulate the deep core muscles and neuromuscular control systems to improve spinal stability. Several studies have found that those patients who have undergone CSE interventions exhibit greater activation of spinal muscles, more co-ordinated contractions across segmental muscles, and better postural control compared with those who did not undergo CSE interventions.26 28 Since the occurrence of IS may be related to muscle imbalance in the core muscles, several researchers have applied CSE to the treatment of scoliosis (the therapeutic goal of CSE is to improve muscle imbalances in the core muscles in patients with IS). Some randomised controlled trials (RCTs) have shown that CSE improves the balance of electromyographic signals in the paraspinal muscles of the spine between segments on either side of the convexity and concavity; spinal flexibility and strength of the lumbar flexor and extensor muscles have also been improved in those children with IS who perform CSE.29 30 However, the precision with which patients train while performing CSE is not easy to grasp and depends on continuous supervision by the training provider.31 In addition, the target muscle groups for CSE are still unknown. Some studies have only focused on strengthening the transversus abdominis and multifidus, but the internal and external abdominal obliques and erector spinae also need to be trained to maintain spinal stability and mobility.32 Given the difficulties in assessing the individual differences between patients and the limitations of CSE, more individualised and precise combination therapies will be needed in the treatment of patients with EOIS and are subject to further research.

In addition to the conventional exercise therapies mentioned above, physiotherapy scoliosis-specific exercise (PSSE) is recommended in the Scoliosis Orthopaedic and Rehabilitation Treatment guidelines as the preferred method for early and effective improvement of IS scoliosis angle and mitigation of deformity progression, of which exercise therapies for EOIS mainly include Schroth, Scientific Exercise Approach to Scoliosis, Functional Individual Therapy of Scoliosis approach (FITS), Barcelona Scoliosis Physical Therapy School approach (BSPTS) and Lyon.13337 However, there is still a lack of high-level evidence indicating that PSSEs are effective in improving spinal deformity in patients with EOIS, as well as a lack of certainty about the efficacy of the therapy in improving lung function, increasing bone strength and improving joint laxity in patients.1 38 39 Therefore, there is a need to further explore more targeted exercise therapies to provide more precise rehabilitation programmes for children with EOIS. Previous studies have found that aerobic exercise, resistance exercise and strength training can help improve lung function, bone strength, and joint laxity in children, and these factors may be added to PSSE to increase interventions for bone and joint development.4042

Comprehensive spinal correction rehabilitation (CSCR), which is an innovative therapy that incorporates the characteristics of developing children and adolescents, will be introduced in this study. CSCR is based on specific exercise therapy to correct spinal deformities while improving the physical fitness of the child and consists of specific exercise therapy, balance and stability training, respiratory training, strength training, home self-corrective exercises and bracing. However, the efficacy of this therapy for EOIS is unclear; therefore, we designed this study protocol to compare two treatment modalities, CSCR and CSE, to investigate the therapeutic efficacy of CSCR in treating EOIS using a prospective, single-blind, randomised controlled clinical study design. We hypothesised that CSCR would be more effective than CSE in improving spinal deformity, somatic appearance, functional status and quality of life in patients with EOIS. It is crucial to highlight that the interventions employed in our study necessitate active engagement and a certain level of comprehension and co-operation from the patients. Therefore, our investigation was primarily focused on JIS, who are more likely to meet these requirements.

Methods and analysis

Aims and objectives

The purpose of this study will be to investigate the efficacy of CSCR in the treatment of EOIS.

Study design

This was a prospective, single-blind, randomised controlled clinical study comparing the 6-month effects of CSCR (experimental group) to CSE (control group) for EOIS. This study was approved by the Ethics Committee, and clinical registration was completed. The trial was registered prospectively at www. Clinical trials. gov (ChiCTR2300078359). After Ethics Committee approval, the study execution started on 1 December 2023 and is expected to end on 1 December 2027. The flow of this study is shown in figure 1.

Randomisation and masking

There will be a 1:1 allocation of subjects to experimental and control groups. Random grouping information will be generated directly using the randomization website (www.randomization.com), and the generated grouping information will be placed in an opaque envelope with the code written on the outside of the envelope and sealed and given to the researcher. Subjects will be numbered on enrolment in the study and will complete all baseline assessments before opening the corresponding numbered envelope. Interventions will be conducted according to the grouping information in the envelope. Coders will not be involved in the recruitment, intervention or assessment of trial subjects. Due to the open-ended nature of this study, therapists and patients must not be blinded when offering or receiving treatment. However, patients will be asked not to disclose their subgroups to ensure that all assessors are blinded. To reduce bias, a proprietary site will be used for all treatment programmes throughout the intervention, and each subject will be assessed individually in a uniform assessment room to avoid breaking blindness. A trained evaluator will complete all subjects’ assessments before initial treatment and immediately after 6 months of intervention. Thereafter, patients in both groups will continue their daily activities, but they will not be able to participate in any other formal exercise programme. Intervention

Experimental group programme

Patients in the experimental group will be evaluated after enrolment, and based on the evaluation results, they will be treated with CSCR, which includes specific exercise therapy, balance and stability training, breathing training, strength training, home-based exercise and bracing.

1. Specific exercise therapy includes three-dimensional self-correction, manual therapy and distraction therapy.36 Among them, three-dimensional self-correction involves self-correction exercises in different positions according to the scoliosis condition to correct the abnormal posture and at the same time correct the spinal deformity in three planes: sagittal, coronal and horizontal planes; manipulative therapy can adjust the small spinal joints, relax the tense muscles and improve the vertebral flexibility; and distraction therapy can achieve the effect of reversal of correction through axial stretching of spinal paravertebral muscles.

2. Balance and stability training will be performed using a static balancing instrument (Tetrax°) (Sunlight Medical Ltd, Israel). Tetrax° would adjust the height and tilt of the display according to the height and weight of patients, using visual feedback for the centre of gravity control. During training, the patient needs to move the light point representing the centre of gravity on the display to the indicated light point. The system records the speed and accuracy of the movement, and the patient’s centre of gravity movement and bipedal weight bearing while standing are accurately captured by the force platform.43

3. Breathing training requires patients to take deep breaths. The therapist guides patients to inhale forcefully with the concave side at the end of inhalation with words and manoeuvres and to exhale forcefully with the convex side at the end of exhalation. Patients wearing braces can perform breathing training with them.

4. Strength training mainly includes training of the spinal muscles as well as the core muscles. It can be incorporated into home-based exercises to train core strength, with specific movements including abdominal breathing and plank support, in addition to diagonal support and unilateral bridge movements. When performing strength training, the difficulty can be increased by reducing the support area.

5. Home-based exercise requires one-on-one teaching by a therapist to ensure the accuracy of the movements. Once the movements are learnt, the patients will conduct self-training at home, which is a series of gymnastic movements to correct the three-dimensional deformity of the spine, and parents will assist in the correction and supervision of daily sitting and standing postures.

The intensity of the above treatments will be set according to the tolerance level of the individual, and the treatment will last for 6 months.

6. In this study, bracing will be added when the patient’s Cobb angle is 25°~45°. Due to the Chêneau brace being widely used worldwide and its effectiveness in combination with exercise therapy for the treatment of scoliosis having been demonstrated, this type of brace will be used in our study.16 20 44 The brace should be worn for 20 or more hours per day. When a patient initially receives a brace, there can be a 1- to 2-week adaptation period, and the daily wearing time can be gradually increased from 2 to 3 hours to 20 hours or more. The brace should be adjusted promptly if the patient experiences any pain or discomfort during the bracing.

The frequency of treatment is 60 min once a week for outpatient treatment and 30 min once a day for home rehabilitation. The CSCR protocol is presented in table 2.

Control group program

After the assessment, patients in the control group will undergo CSE under the supervision and guidance of a physiotherapist, which is designed based on previous studies and consists of three phases: warm-up phase (10 min), training phase (40 min) and relaxation phase (10 min).29 The warm-up phase focuses on stretching exercises to activate the core muscles; the training phase includes bridge exercises, plank support training and supine leg raise training to strengthen the lumbar, abdominal and paravertebral muscles, which in turn improves the stability of the spine; and the relaxation phase also focuses on stretching exercises to relieve muscle tension. The intensity of the treatment will be set according to the individual’s fatigue level during the training, and the treatment will last for 6 months. Patients will also be instructed to perform 30 min of core training at home every day. The same as the CSCR group, the Chêneau brace will be needed when the Cobb angle of the spinal deformity is 25°~45°, and the daily wearing time of the brace is 20 or more hours. The frequency of treatment is 60 min once a week for outpatient treatment and 30 min once a day for home rehabilitation. The CSE protocol is presented in table 3.

In addition to the rehabilitation treatment for both groups, regular follow-up and health education will be included to inform the patients and their parents of the risks and dangers of scoliosis progression, teach the patients to pay attention to daily postural management and instruct the parents to recognise the progression of the deformity in time, emphasising the importance of regular follow-up.

Compliance supervision

All patients will be added to the spine health digital management platform (WeChat applet), and the researchers will set the patients into a trial group and a control group according to the grouping. In the experimental group, a home training diary will be used to monitor patient compliance during home rehabilitation. Parents will create a training diary on the Spine Health digital management platform and record the time and frequency of the patient’s daily self-corrective exercises. Daily wearing time of the brace will also be recorded by patients or parents via this platform. Then the quality control personnel will check the patient’s daily training and bracing time regularly every week through the spine health digital management platform. During outpatient treatment, the quality of self-corrective gymnastics movements will be controlled by the therapist through on-site auditing every week. Researchers can answer patients' questions online on the management platform, share knowledge about spine health science and remind patients to reassess after 6 months of intervention. Attendance will be expressed as a percentage of actual attendance to the needed attendance, home-based exercise adherence will be expressed as a percentage of actual self-corrective gymnastics completed to the minimum needed number of sessions, outpatient rehabilitation adherence will be expressed as a percentage of total outpatient treatments completed to the minimum needed number of sessions, and if adherence is less than 80%, the intervention will be considered to be poorly adhered to. For patients wearing a brace, insufficient brace-wearing hours (less than 20 hours) will also be considered poor adherence. If their brace is adjusted, the specific reason and clear portion of the adjustment will be recorded. Patients with poor adherence and their parents will be contacted by the researcher to document the reasons for the decline in adherence.

Adverse events

All adverse events occurring during the trial will be recorded by the physiotherapist in an adverse event form. Potential adverse events included a sustained worsening of the scoliosis angle (increasing by 10° or more), exercise leading to injury or falls and back pain. The investigators will assess the association between the adverse events and the intervention and decide whether to discontinue the intervention, as well as give immediate targeted treatment for the adverse events and refer for further treatment if necessary to maximise the protection of the subjects.

Comprehensive assessment

Subjects participating in the trial will be assessed twice at the initial visit and after 6 months of intervention. Specific assessments will include the basic information of subjects and physical developmental indicators (gender, age, height, weight, body mass index and Risser sign), trunk appearance (angle of trunk rotation (ATR), plumb line distance, the index of Trunk Aesthetic Clinical Evaluation (TRACE)), radiographic measurements (the major curvature Cobb angle), forced vital capacity (FVC) and quality of life surveys (The Scoliosis Research Society-22 questionnaire scores (SRS-22)).

Primary outcome measurement

Change in the major curve Cobb angle

Enrolled subjects will have full spine standing orthopantomograms taken and the major curve Cobb angle measured.45 Measurements will be made on full-length orthopantomograms of the spine by a trained rehabilitation physician, either by software or manually, in four steps that can be broken down into four steps: (1) selection of the upper and lower end vertebrae; (2) drawing of an extension line; (3) making a plumb line for the extension line; and (4) angular measurements. The patient’s Cobb angle value was recorded, and the maximum Cobb angle was recorded if there were multiple curved areas.

Secondary outcome measurements

Angle of trunk rotation

Trunk rotation measurement is a useful tool for assessing spinal deformities, and its measurements correlate well with those of X-ray measurements (r=0.7); the sensitivity of this measurement is 87%, and the specificity is 73%.1 46 During the measurement, the examinee will need to bend forward gradually with both feet together in an upright position, and the examiner will check whether the two sides of the spine are flush during the whole process of bending and use a trunk rotation metre (Bunnell Scoliometer, USA) to measure ATR of the main thoracic segment (T1–T4), the thoracolumbar segment (T5–T12) and the lumbar segment (T12–L4). Then, the maximum ATR value of each part will be read and recorded. The trunk rotation metre has a unit scale of 1° and a measurement range of 0° to 30°.47 The final classification will be based on the ATR value, with ATR<5° considered no scoliosis, 5°≤ATR<7° considered degree I, 7°≤ATR<10° considered degree II and ATR≥10° considered degree III.48

Plumb line distance

One of the current non-imaging measurements of the sagittal plane of the spine uses a plumb line. The plumb line is a simple, fast and reliable tool for evaluating the effectiveness of rehabilitation treatments.49 Grunstein et al50 found that the retest reliability of the sagittal plane of the spine measured by the plumb line was 0.98, the intertester reliability was 0.99, and the results measured using this method correlated well with the results measured using X-rays (r=0.79–0.83). Plumb line distance can be measured by measuring the vertical distance from seven markers on the subject’s back to the plumb line, which are the posterior occipital convexity of the head, the anterior cervical convexity, C7, the last convexity of the thoracic vertebrae (T5–T6), T12, L3 and S1, and the values of the measurements are expressed in millimetres. Finally, the distance of the last convex point of the thoracic spine (mm) was subtracted from the distance measured at each point (mm) to obtain the sagittal distance, and the spinal sagittal imbalance was determined by the sum of the distances between C7 and L3. Finally, the spinal condition of the patient’s spine will be categorised as flat back (<60 mm), normal (60–90 mm) and kyphosis (>90 mm) based on the sum of the distances between C7 and L3.

Trunk aesthetic clinical evaluation

Scoliosis is mainly characterised by the spine curving to one side in an ‘S’ or ‘C’ shape, and its abnormal appearance is manifested by unequal shoulders, protruding scapulae, uneven back and asymmetrical thorax. Correcting posture and improving back appearance are the main reasons for most scoliosis patients to seek treatment.7 TRACE score is a scale developed by Italian scholar Zain et al51 for the assessment of scoliosis appearance, with a total score of 12 points, which needs to be assessed in four parts, namely, shoulder asymmetry score (0–3), scapular asymmetry score (0–2), lumbar asymmetry score (0–4) and hemithoracic asymmetry score (0–2). TRACE has sufficient reproducibility and sensitivity for routine TRACE and is sufficiently reproducible and sensitive for routine clinical practice, and the scoring is convenient and easy to learn, so it has been widely used in clinical scoliosis assessment.51

Forced vital capacity

Abnormal curvature and rotation of the spine and distortion of thoracic structures can lead to compromised respiratory function in patients with scoliosis, and in severe cases, restrictive or obstructive lung disease may develop, affecting lung function. Especially for patients with EOIS, the thorax and lungs develop in childhood, and the spinal deformity that occurs at this time may result in reduced thoracic volume, decreased thoracic mobility and decreased lung function. It has been shown that the greater the degree of scoliosis, the higher the risk of pulmonary dysfunction.52 The most commonly used index for the evaluation of lung function is FVC. Lung function tests will be performed using the QuarkPFT4 Spirometer (COSMED, Italy).53

SRS-22 dimension scores

The SRS-22 questionnaire is a simple, practical, child-specific health-related scale for children with IS that is recommended globally by the Scoliosis Research Society and has been widely used to assess the effects and outcomes of scoliosis and the SRS-22 questionnaire has been applied to assess the health-related quality of life of children with early-onset scoliosis.54 The SRS-22 questionnaire consists of 22 items covering five dimensions, each with a score range of 1–5, including functional activity, pain, self-image, mental health and satisfaction with treatment, with higher scores indicating better quality of life for patients. The intragroup correlation coefficients for these five dimensions were 0.74, 0.78, 0.86, 0.81 and 0.84, respectively, showing good retest reliability.55

Sample size calculation

G*Power 3.1.9.7 (Heinrich-Heine-Universität Düsseldorf, Germany) software was used for sample size calculation. The main efficacy evaluation index in this study will be the amount of change in the Cobb angle of the lordosis before and after treatment. According to Kim et al,56 who studied the effect of exercise therapy on the change in the major Cobb angle of scoliosis, the change in the Cobb angle in the experimental group was (−11.60±4.16)°, and the change in the Cobb angle in the control group was (−8.00±6.04)°. The effect size was calculated to be 0.694, with α set to be 0.05 and β set to be 0.05, which means that 55 patients were needed to be enrolled in each group. Considering the possibility of a 10% loss to follow-up, 61 patients are needed for each group, and a total of 122 patients are planned to be enrolled.

Statistical analysis

The data analysis set followed the intention-to-treat analysis (ITT) principle, and all individuals participating in randomisation will be included in the study analysis set for the main secondary indicators. Missing data from the study were filled in according to the method of substitution by the last measurement.57 The primary and secondary outcome metrics in this study using the modified ITT set (mITT) will include all subjects participating in randomised subgroups and at least one intervention. In addition, the per-protocol set (PP) will be used as a supplement to the ITT analysis set. The PP set will include subjects who perform the trial procedure in strict compliance with the protocol. The safety analysis set (SS) will include all subjects who receive at least one intervention. The mITT set will be used for both primary outcome indicators and secondary outcome indicators, with the PP set as a supplement.

The S‒W test will be used to determine whether the data conform to a normal distribution; normal distribution will be expressed as the mean±standard deviation (x̅ ± SD) for measures, median and quartiles (M (P25, P75)) for non-normal distribution and enumeration data (constitutive ratios) for counts; the primary outcome metrics (the major curvature Cobb angle) will be compared between groups using an independent samples t test or a Mann‒Whitney rank-sum test differences between preintervention and postintervention will be compared between groups using independent samples t-test or Mann‒Whitney rank-sum test for secondary outcome indicators ATR, plumb line distance, TRACE, FVC and SRS-22, and the χ2 test or Fisher exact probability method for comparing differences between groups before and after the intervention; safety indicators will be quantitatively described using the SS set to describe safety indicators, including the number of reports of back pain, falls or injuries related to the intervention, and their timing in children who report back pain during and at the end of the intervention. All data analyses will be performed using SPSS (V.25.0; Armonk, NY, USA, IBM Corporation) statistical software, and a two-sided p<0.05 will indicate statistical significance.

Quality control and quality assurance

The investigators have appropriate clinical research qualifications, and the clinical trial project leader or principal investigator is responsible for the training before the start of the clinical trial. One researcher will be scheduled for spinal radiographs. All personnel involved in the assessment will be trained in the operation of various assessment instruments, such as the assessment process, pulmonary function instrument, static balance instrument, plumb line and trunk rotation measuring instrument, and will be assessed periodically. An inspector will be available in both the trial and control groups to supervise the monthly assessments. A data and safety monitoring committee will be established to ensure the quality of each process. The Clinical Research Unit will be responsible for controlling the quality and safety of this study to ensure the safety of the subjects and the validity of the data. Based on the results, data quality and evidence of adverse events, the trial may be terminated if the intervention is not useful or effective. If an adverse event occurs, the Principal Investigator will decide whether to suspend the intervention in this study based on the adverse event.

Discussion

EOIS patients are characterised by young age, weak self-control, large individual differences, high risk of deformity progression and susceptibility to cardiopulmonary function, which makes any treatment very challenging for children with EOIS. As the spinal deformity continues to worsen, patients with EOIS may experience restricted thoracic growth and development, reduced thoracic volume, and in severe cases, even restrictive lung disease, and respiratory failure.5861 The reasons for this series of phenomena are related to the developmental characteristics of the lungs, thorax and spine; early studies have shown that the alveoli develop rapidly by the age of 8 years, while the most rapid spinal growth occurs in early childhood, and the development of the thoracic spine determines the vertical diameter of the thoracic cage, which in turn affects thoracic volume.91162 63 BSPTS therapy in PSSEs improves respiratory function and the patient’s ability to perform activities of daily living through specific respiratory training techniques.64 Lyon therapy slows the progression of spinal deformity in patients with EOIS by promoting spinal extension and enhancing thoracic kyphosis and lumbar lordosis correction, as well as stabilisation, balance, proprioception and functional activity training.37 Polish scholar Marianna Białek followed 41 EOIS patients treated with FITS for an average of 4.8 years, and the results confirmed that FITS therapy reduces the Cobb angle and effectively delays the progression of spinal deformity in EOIS patients.35 Although PSSEs have been shown to improve spinal deformity in EOIS, efficient interventions combining exercise therapy, home rehabilitation and online supervision have not been used. The aim of integrated spinal correction therapy proposed in this study is to identify abnormalities in trunk appearance, imaging changes, function and quality of life issues that occur in patients with EOIS based on assessment results. The therapy is further optimised based on specific exercise therapy by adding appropriate balance and stability training, breathing training, strength training, home-based exercise and daily postural management for each type of problem identified after patient assessment as a way to enhance the effectiveness of early rehabilitation treatment for EOIS, stop or delay the progression of scoliosis, improve the quality of life of patients and alleviate the financial burden of patients and their families. Therefore, we designed this RCT to compare the therapeutic effects of two interventions for EOIS patients undergoing CSCR versus CSE for 6 months. We investigate the effect of CSCR on the improvement of spinal deformity, somatic appearance, functional status and quality of life in patients with EOIS and provide high-quality intervention programmes and evidence for the early rehabilitation of patients with EOIS to guide future clinical practice.
