Association between headache and hearing impairment in middle-aged and older adults in China health and retirement longitudinal study
Authors: Youran Dai, Chenyang Li, Liang Wang, Ruiqing Bo, Zerun Cheng, Wenhui Yang, Guofeng Pan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Global population aging presents remarkable socioeconomic and public health challenges worldwide. Headache rank as the second leading global cause of disability and have been implicated in the pathogenesis of hearing impairment (HI). Nevertheless, the precise relationship between these conditions remains poorly researched, particularly in aging populations. This study utilized data from the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS) among adults aged 45 years and older. Participants with pre-existing HI or missing data were excluded, resulting in a final sample of 3,872 individuals. Headache was assessed via questionnaire, and HI was evaluated through self-reports. The study adjusted for potential confounders, including sociodemographic characteristics, health-related factors, and chronic conditions, and employed multivariable logistic regression models to investigate the association between headache and HI. A total of 3,872 participants were included in the final analysis. A significant positive association was found between headache and HI, which persisted after adjusting for confounders (OR = 1.67; 95% CI: 1.34–2.08; P < 0.001). Gender-stratified analyses revealed a stronger association among males (OR = 2.18; 95% CI: 1.43–3.31; P < 0.001) compared to females (OR = 1.50; 95% CI: 1.15–1.96; P = 0.003). Subgroup analyses did not demonstrate significant interaction effect. This study demonstrates a significant association between headache and HI, suggesting that headache may contribute to HI through vascular mechanisms. Early intervention for headache may help prevent HI. Further research is needed to elucidate the underlying pathophysiological mechanisms and validate these findings across diverse populations.

Introduction

Global population aging has become an irreversible trend. According to United Nations projections1, one in six people worldwide will be aged 60 years or older in 2030. This figure is expected to increase twofold by 2050, reaching a total of 2.1 billion. China is one of the countries with the most severe population aging and the largest elderly population. The proportion of individuals aged 65 and above is projected to rise from 14.9% in 2022 to 26.1% by 20502. These substantial demographic changes pose significant challenges to socioeconomic development and public health systems worldwide. Among the myriad health challenges associated with aging, headache disorders and hearing impairment (HI) are two highly prevalent conditions that significantly impact functional ability and quality of life.

Headache is characterized by discomfort in the head, neck, and facial regions3, represents the second leading cause of disability worldwide, accounting for 5.6% of the global disease burden4. According to the International Classification of Headache Disorders (ICHD-3), headaches can be categorized into primary headaches (migraine, tension-type headache etc.) and secondary headaches (typically caused by head/neck trauma, infections, or cerebrovascular disorders)5. Secondary headaches are more prevalent among older adults6. Similarly, HI is regarded as the top three most prevalent health conditions in the elderly population7. It affects approximately 11% of individuals aged 60 years or older in China8, and about 20% of the global population9. With accelerating population aging, nearly 2.5 billion people worldwide are projected to develop varying degrees of hearing loss by 2050, including at least 700 million requiring hearing rehabilitation services10. Several cross-sectional studies have reported an association between headache and tinnitus11,12, providing preliminary evidence for a potential link between headache and auditory symptoms. Longitudinal studies have extended these findings. Research based on the Taiwan Longitudinal Health Insurance Database (NHIRD) and the Korean National Health Insurance Service-National Sample Cohort has shown that migraine is associated with various ear disorders and an increased risk of sudden sensorineural hearing loss13–15. Furthermore, a case-control study found that individuals with migraine performed significantly worse on central auditory tests than controls, suggesting that the impact of headache on the auditory system may also involve central auditory processing pathways16.

Despite accumulating evidence, the long-term association between headache and HI in older adults remains understood. Furthermore, although research conducted in Taiwanese and Korean populations provides valuable insights for East Asian cohorts, evidence from middle-aged and older populations in mainland China is still lacking. To address this research gap, the present study utilized data from the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS) to investigate the association between headache and HI in a nationally representative sample of Chinese adults aged 45 and older. This finding may inform public health strategies and identify potential intervention targets for HI prevention, thereby facilitating clinical diagnosis and management of this condition.

Methods

The CHARLS study protocol was approved by the Ethical Review Committee of Peking University. All participants provided written informed consent prior to enrollment. The approval number for the main household survey, including anthropometrics, is IRB00001052-11015; the approval number for biomarker collection was IRB00001052‐11,014. Details information about the CHARLS data are available on website (http://charls.pku.edu.cn/en).

Study design and population

This study utilized data from the CHARLS database. The CHARLS is a nationwide ongoing cohort study with follow-up assessments every 2 to 3 years since its 2011 baseline. At baseline, a multistage probability sampling method (probability proportional to size, PPS) was adopted, recruiting over 17,000 individuals from about 10,000 households across 150 counties in 28 provinces. Information was collected through face-to-face interviews using computer-assisted personal interviewing (CAPI) technology17,18. Sampling weights are provided to account for the complex sampling design and non-response. However, as our primary aim was to explore the association between baseline self-reported headache and subsequent HI, rather than to estimate national prevalence, these weights were not applied in this analysis. This approach is consistent with the CHARLS user guideline, which indicates that weights are primarily intended for population-level inference.

We conducted a 7-year longitudinal study (2011–2018) using the 2011 population data as baseline. After excluding participants with baseline HI or missing follow-up HI information, we longitudinally monitored eligible subjects until 2018. From the initial 17,705 participants, we first excluded those aged < 45 years (n = 404). These younger participants originated from the CHARLS sampling design, which includes spouses of eligible respondents aged ≥ 45 years regardless of their own age. We then excluded participants with missing baseline headache data (n = 3,422), missing 2018 HI outcome data (n = 3,292), or prevalent HI at baseline (n = 5,487), leaving 5,100 eligible participants. After further excluding 1,228 participants with missing covariate data (gender, education, hypertension, diabetes, sleep duration, and obesity), 3,872 participants were included in the final analysis. The final analytical sample comprised 3,872 participants (Fig. 1).

Assessment of headache

Headache was assessed using the health status module in the CHARLS questionnaire. Participants were asked: “Do you often experience discomfort due to physical pain?” and required to answer “yes” or “no”. Participants who answered “yes” were asked to indicate all painful areas. Those who reported head pain were classified as having a headache19,20. In this study, only participants who reported head pain at baseline were constituted the exposure group for the longitudinal analysis.

Assessment of HI

HI was assessed through self-reported measures. The accuracy of the self-reported question has been demonstrated to be comparable to that of objective hearing measurements21–23. Participants were asked: “How is your hearing? (If you frequently wear hearing aids, how is your hearing with the aids on? If you do not frequently wear hearing aids, how is your hearing without them? )”. Responses were categorized into five levels: excellent, very good, good, fair, and poor. Participants reporting “fair” or “poor” hearing were classified as having HI24. We excluded participants who reported HI at baseline and identified self-reported HI that emerged during the follow-up period based solely on the self-rated hearing measure from the 2018 wave.

Covariates assessments

Potential confounding factors were adjusted for in this study based on established evidence from previous research, data availability and clinical experience25,26. Socio-demographic characteristics included age, gender (male, female), education level (illiterate, primary school, middle school, high school or above), residential location (urban, rural), and marital status (married with spouse present, others). Health-related factors comprised smoking status (current, former, never smoker), drinking status in the past year (never, less than once per month, more than once per month), body mass index (BMI), and daily sleep time (< 7 h, ≥ 7 h). Chronic conditions considered were hypertension and diabetes. BMI was calculated as weight in kilograms divided by height in meters squared and categorized according to WHO criteria: underweight (< 18.5 kg/m²), normal weight (18.5–25.0 kg/m²), overweight (25.0–30.0 kg/m²), and obese (≥ 30.0 kg/m²)27. Hypertension was defined as either self-reported physician diagnosis or measured blood pressure ≥ 140/90 mmHg28. Diabetes mellitus was determined by self-reported physician diagnosis or meeting any of the following criteria: fasting plasma glucose ≥ 126 mg/dL, non-fasting glucose ≥ 200 mg/dL, or hemoglobin A1c (HbA1c) ≥ 6.5%29.

Statistical analyses

For descriptive analysis, categorical variables were shown as percentages, while continuous variables were provided as means with standard deviation (SD). All numerical variables were tested for normality using the Shapiro-Wilk test, with all consigned to the normal distribution. Participants were stratified into two groups according to HI statue. Demographic and clinical characteristics were compared between the two subgroups by student’s-t test for continuous variables and χ2 test for categorical variables. The variance inflation factor (VIF) was computed for all continuous variables in the model to assess multicollinearity, with all values below 5. As the exact timing of HI onset could not be determined from the discrete follow-up waves, logistic regression was used to estimate the association between baseline headache and the binary outcome of HI occurrence during the follow-up period. The results were displayed as odds ratios (OR) and 95% confidence intervals (CI). The results were displayed as odds ratios (OR) and 95% confidence intervals (CI). The original model is a single regression model without adjusting any confounding factors. To minimize potential confounding effects, multivariable logistic regression models were also utilized. Four logistic regression models were constructed: Crude model was unadjusted; Model 1 adjusted for age, education level, marital status, and residence location; Model 2 additionally adjusted for drinking status, smoking status, sleep time and BMI; Model 3 further adjusted for hypertension status, and diabetes status. Model 3 serves as the primary model. Subgroup analyses and interaction tests were performed to explore heterogeneity across subgroups. Statistical analyses were conducted using R version 4.5.0 (https://www.r-project.org/). A P value < 0.05 was regarded as statistically significant.

The baseline characteristics of the participants stratified by headache statue are shown in Table 1. A total of 3,872 participants were included in the final analysis, with a mean age of 56.70 ± 8.36 years and 51.60% being female. The number of people over 60 years old was 1,297 (33.50%). The study found that 415 (10.72%) of the participants reported having headache at baseline, 2,076 (53.62%) reported HI over the 7-year follow-up. Compared with those without headache, individuals with headache were more likely to be female, have a lower education level, living in rural area, and have a shorter daily sleep time (all P < 0.05).

Association between headache and HI

Four models were constructed to explore the relationship between headache and HI (Table 2). The results demonstrated that HI was positively associated with headache in the non-adjusted model (OR = 1.76; 95% CI: 1.42–2.18; P < 0.001), the partially adjusted model (OR = 1.70; 95% CI: 1.37–2.11; P < 0.001), and the fully adjusted model (OR = 1.67; 95% CI: 1.34–2.08; P < 0.001). Stratified analyses revealed gender differences in the magnitude of association. Males exhibited stronger associations (OR = 2.18; 95% CI:1.43–3.31, P < 0.001) compared to females (OR = 1.50; 95% CI:1.15–1.96, P = 0.003).

Subgroup analyses

Subgroup analyses and interaction tests were conducted by gender, residence location, smoke, drink, hypertension, diabetes, marital status, educational level, age, sleep time and BMI in order to identify any potential population differences (Fig. 2). The results revealed no statistically significant interaction effects (all P for interaction > 0.05) between headache and HI across various demographic and clinical characteristics. These findings indicate that the positive relationship between headache and HI is robust across diverse population subgroups without evidence of significant effect modification by the examined covariates.

Discussion

This study demonstrated that headache was significantly associated with an increased risk of HI, with this association persisting after comprehensive adjustment for potential confounders. Subsequent subgroup analyses did not identify statistically significant effect modification by various demographic or clinical characteristics (all P for interaction > 0.05), suggesting the robustness of the results.

Both headache and hearing impairment represent significant public health burdens in the aging population. The prevalence of headache among older adults has been reported to range from 12% to 50%30. Although the incidence of new-onset migraine and tension-type headache declines with advancing age, the proportion of chronic migraine among affected individuals increases31–34. Concurrently, research indicate that 1.57 billion people experienced hearing loss, with individuals aged 50 years and older accounting for 62.1%35. Untreated hearing loss may adversely affects the quality of life of the elderly36. Collectively, these epidemiological findings underscore the clinical relevance of investigating the association between headache and hearing impairment in middle-aged and older populations. A population-based cohort study in Taiwan revealed that migraine was associated with an increased risk of sudden sensorineural hearing loss15. Analysis of the NHIRD database indicated that patients with a history of non-migraineous headache exhibit significantly increased risks of developing tinnitus, sensorineural hearing loss, and sudden deafness37. Research has also revealed a higher prevalence of migraine in patients with sudden sensorineural hearing loss (39.34%) compared to the general population38. A Russian epidemiological study further supported this correlation, reporting an elevated incidence of headache among hearing-impaired individuals39. Our findings support previous research indicating an association between headache and HI. The subgroup analysis results were robust, which suggests that the findings of the present study may be applicable to the general population.

The association between headache and HI may involve multiple pathophysiological mechanisms. The mechanisms linking migraine and auditory symptoms have been relatively well studied and characterized, offering important insights for understanding this association. In 1996, it was hypothesized that idiopathic hearing loss in migraine might be related to migraine-induced cochlear vasospasm40. Although the exact mechanisms underlying migraine remain incompletely understood, cortical spreading depression triggering neuropeptide release and activating the trigeminovascular system is considered to play a key role41,42. Trigeminal nerve fibers innervate the spiral modiolar artery and the stria vascularis, both of which are critical components of the cochlear vascular network43,44. Animal studies have shown that stimulation of the ophthalmic branch of the trigeminal nerve induces fluid extravasation within the cochlea, suggesting that trigeminal nerve activation may affect the inner ear microenvironment45. Anatomically, the cochlea is particularly vulnerable to ischemic injury due to its dependence on terminal arteries and lack of effective collateral circulation46,47. Based on this, it is plausible that headache may indirectly contribute to cochlear hypoperfusion through its effects on vascular function. In addition, elevated intracranial pressure may represent an important cause of headache48. Patients with elevated intracranial pressure often present with audiovestibular symptoms characteristic of endolymphatic hydrops, including HI. Studies have found that radiological markers of idiopathic intracranial hypertension frequently coexist in patients with endolymphatic hydrops49. Elevated intracranial pressure is associated with increased dural sinus pressure and compression of the dural sinuses50. The dural sinuses are innervated by trigeminal nerve fibers that predominantly express calcitonin gene-related peptide (CGRP), and stimulation of the dural venous sinuses has been shown to trigger CGRP release51,52. Animal studies also suggest that migraine may contribute to hearing loss through a CGRP-driven, ZBP1-mediated cell death pathway53. Furthermore, reducing intracranial pressure has been shown to decrease migraine frequency54. Although the present results focus on general headache and hearing loss, we propose that these mechanisms may partially account for the observed association. These mechanistic hypotheses, however, remain speculative and should be interpreted with caution, as direct evidence linking general headache to hearing loss is currently limited. Given that both headache and HI significantly affect the quality of life of older adults, clinicians should adopt integrated management strategies to facilitate early identification and improve patient outcomes.

Although the sex-stratified model showed a stronger association in males than in females (OR 2.18 vs. 1.50), the subgroup analysis did not reveal a significant interaction between gender and the outcome (P for interaction > 0.05). Therefore, this finding should be interpreted with caution, and further studies are warranted to clarify whether gender modifies the association between headache and HI. Overall, the subgroup analyses indicated that the association between headache and hearing loss was generally robust across different population subgroups (all P for interaction > 0.05).

This study has several strengths. Firstly, this work leveraged data from nationally representative CHARLS database with a large sample size enhances the generalizability of the findings. Participants were selected through a rigorous multistage probability sampling procedure, ensuring a valid representation of middle-aged and older adults. Additionally, we employed multivariable logistic regression models. Subgroup analyses and interaction tests were also conducted to explore potential variations across different populations, thereby strengthening the robustness of our findings. To our knowledge, this is the first study to investigate the association between headache and HI specifically in middle-aged and older adults. However, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the reliance on self-reported measures for both headache and HI may have introduced recall bias and reduced diagnostic accuracy, potentially leading to misclassification bias. Second, due to database constraints, we were unable to obtain information on specific subtypes or severity levels of headache and HI, which limits the clinical specificity and generalizability of our findings. Additionally, we could not adjust for the potential impact of headache-related medications (e.g., acetaminophen, aspirin) on the observed association, which represents an important confounding factor. Third, selection bias may arise from the stepwise sample selection and complete-case analysis. We did not compare included and excluded participants, precluding quantitative assessment of attrition bias. Although multiple imputation is an alternative, we opted for complete-case analysis as imputation might itself introduce bias. The sample size reduction reflects both study design requirements and the need for complete covariate data. We also acknowledge that sensitivity analyses were not performed, which represents an additional limitation. Meanwhile, given that the exact timing of HI onset was unavailable due to discrete-wave data collection, our results represent the odds association with HI occurrence over the follow-up period rather than hazard estimates, thereby reducing the interpretability of the temporal relationship between exposure and outcome. Therefore, the generalizability of our findings warrants caution. Future studies with more comprehensive model validation are needed to confirm our results. Furthermore, as an observational study, our findings cannot establish causality. And the possibility of reverse causation cannot be entirely ruled out, such as the potential for undetected subclinical HI at baseline. Although we adjusted for relevant covariates based on existing literature, residual confounding from unmeasured factors (e.g., noise exposure, psychological stress, social factors) may still persist. Future studies incorporating objective clinical assessments, detailed phenotyping, and medication data are warranted to validate and extend our findings.

Conclusion

Our longitudinal analysis of middle-aged and older Chinese adults revealed a significant positive association between headache and HI, with this relationship being particularly pronounced in male participants. These results highlight the clinical importance of early headache intervention as a potential preventive strategy against HI. Further research is warranted to elucidate the underlying pathophysiological mechanisms and validate the generalizability of these findings across diverse populations.
