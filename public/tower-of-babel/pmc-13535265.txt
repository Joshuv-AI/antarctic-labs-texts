Innovative Xenotransplantation of Reconstructed Human Artificial Ovary Using 3D-Printed Scaffolds and Umbilical Cord Mesenchymal Stem Cells
Authors: Mohammad Ayoubipour, Hussein Eimani, Rouhollah Fathi, Abdolhossien Shahverdi, 2, 3, Morteza Zarrabi, Mohammad Kazemi Ashtiani, Somayeh Tavana, Seyed Akbar Moosavi, Leila Sadat Tahaei, Zeinab Ghezelayagh, Majid Halvaei, Masoumeh Nouri, Parviz Golmohammadi Gerdkohi, Ashraf Moini
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background: The primary objective of this study was to develop an artificial ovary made of polylactic acid (PLA) material, which could potentially be used for fertility preservation. This scaffold was used to carry umbilical cord mesenchymal stem cells (UCMSCs) for xenotransplantation to study follicular growth, viability, and angiogenesis.Materials and Methods: The experimental study involved transplanting human ovarian cortical pieces into 3D-printed baskets, with or without human UCMSCs. Four groups were considered as control: vitrified-warmed ovarian tissue (OT), OT transplantation without 3D-printed baskets without UCMSCs, OT+D; OT transplantation with 3D-printed baskets and without UCMSCs, and OT+D+C; OT transplantation with 3D-printed baskets and UCMSCs. Eighteen rats were divided into three transplantation groups, with grafts implanted into their back muscles. After one week, comparisons were made between OT, OT+D, and OT+D+C with control groups. Histological evaluations and gene expression analyses, including CX37, CX43, KL, GDF9, and VEGF, were conducted to evaluate factors such as angiogenesis, cell proliferation, and follicle development.Results:The results revealed that OT+D had the highest follicular growth (99.3%) and transition rate (74.08%). OT+D+C indicated increased follicular survival, reduced mortality (P<0.0001), and a higher percentage of primordial follicles (59.1%) compared to OT and OT+D groups. UCMSCs’ paracrine secretions likely preserved the follicular reserve. Additionally, OT+D+C exhibited significantly increased blood vessel number and diameter (P<0.0001) compared to other groups. Conclusion:This study underscores the significance of utilizing a three-dimensional basket scaffold to enhance the development of ovarian follicles and facilitate angiogenesis. The integration of stem cells with the scaffold demonstrated a noteworthy synergistic effect, resulting in improved preservation and viability of the follicles and Reduced fibrosis. These findings illuminate the promising potential of scaffold-based strategies in advancing reproductive therapies and tissue engineering, thereby offering prospects for innovative treatments in the future.

Introduction

Ovarian cancer is one of the more common types of cancer, making up about 3.7% of all cases and accounting for approximately 4.7% of cancer-related deaths in 2020. The disease is quite diverse, with epithelial ovarian cancer being the most frequently occurring form. This type can be further divided into five different histotypes, each with unique clinical features and genetic profiles (1).

It is vital to understand the impact of these treatments on reproductive health and take proactive steps to protect fertility for those facing cancer (2). To address this risk, researchers are exploring xenotransplantation-the transplantation of ovarian tissue (OT) from one species to another. Additionally, the development of artificial ovaries, which utilize scaffolds to support the maturation of antral follicles, offers a promising avenue for preserving fertility in primary ovarian insufficiency (POI), premature ovarian failure (POF), ovarian cancer and age-related infertility, cancer patients (3, 4). To enhance the efficiency of transplantation, researchers are investigating using 3D -printed tissue trapper baskets. These innovative baskets secure the transplanted OT, creating optimal conditions for graft survival and function. A crucial element of this approach is the incorporation of human umbilical cord mesenchymal stem cells (UCMSCs), which play a vital role in promoting angiogenesis and supporting the recovery of ovarian function (5). By creating a conducive environment for both the transplanted tissue and UCMSCs, the 3D-printed structures aim to significantly improve the success rates of xenotransplantation. Co-culturing stem cells with OT grafts has shown potential benefits, with various stem cell types being explored for their ability to treat POF. Mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs) from bone marrow, for example, have demonstrated promise in fostering angiogenesis and follicular survival in human OT (6). Furthermore, studies have examined the differentiation of stem cells into ovarian-like tissue cells and their role in ovarian regeneration (7). UCMSCs present unique advantages over other MSCs, such as their ability to activate the immune system mildly due to low expression of human white blood cell antigens, especially MHC I, and the absence of MHC II expression. They inhibit the proliferation of immune cells, promote the development of anti-inflammatory macrophages, and secrete interleukin-4 4 and 10, which help reduce inflammation while supporting tissue repair (8). Moreover, UCMSCs exhibit anti-inflammatory properties by inhibiting the secretion of interleukin β1, tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α), and gamma interleukin, thereby alleviating inflammation and reducing oxidative stress levels, which can lead to cellular apoptosis. Their role in repairing damaged ovarian function and tissue is further reinforced by their secretion of various paracrine molecules and growth factors such as vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF), transforming growth factor β1 (TGFβ), hepatocyte growth factor (HGF), and placental growth factor (PGF) (5). The field of artificial ovary development has evolved significantly over the past two decades, addressing concerns about cancer recurrence after treatment. While synthetic polymers can be tailored for specific mechanical properties, they often lack the inherent cellular adhesion molecules integral to successful tissue integration. This challenge can be addressed by incorporating bioactive agents (9). On the other hand, natural polymers, rich in bioactive components, support essential cellular processes like adhesion, migration, proliferation, and differentiation (10). Various materials and techniques have been utilized to facilitate oocyte maturation within artificial ovaries (4).

Polylactic acid (PLA) and its copolymers are noteworthy examples of synthetic polymers widely applied in medical applications. They are biocompatible and biodegradable and exhibit low toxicity with minimal stimulation of the immune system (11). The primary objective of this experimental study was to create an artificial ovary using a 3D-printed scaffold made from PLA to support fertility preservation. The design of the scaffold aimed to nurture human OT in conjunction with UCMSCs for potential xenotransplantation. The researchers concentrated on evaluating the OT's growth, the follicles’ survival, the formation of new blood vessels (angiogenesis), and the extent of tissue damage, including necrosis and fibrosis. Samples have been collected one week post-transplantation for histological, immunohistochemical, and polymerase chain reaction (PCR) analyses.

Materials and Methods

Ethical approval

In this experimental study, we obtained ethical approval from the Royan Research Institute’s Ethical Committee (IR.ACECR.ROYAN.REC.1400.038) to use human OT, human umbilical cord cells, and laboratory animals. We obtained written consent from the transsexual patients who provided the OT and from the women who donated the umbilical cords.

Production of 3D-printed baskets and porosity assessments

A basket-shaped structure was created using FDM-3D printing technology. The design was formulated in CATIA V5 r19 software and translated into a printable format using Slic3r software. The design allowed for containers with variable porosities. The basket was designed as a cylindrical shape with a 3 mm height and 8 mm diameter and incorporated with a 0.6 mm-thick cap. The wall of the basket was approximately 0.6 mm thick, with a 50-70% porosity on the bottom and cap surfaces. The primary material for the enclosure was PLA. Due to its temperature sensitivity, autoclaving was impractical, so the baskets were sterilized using hydrochlorothiazide (Microzide). The fabrication process included washing with deionized water, immersion in Microzide solution for 1 hour, three rounds of washing with sterile water, and a 30-minute water soak to eliminate residual Microzide. Finally, the baskets were stored under a laminar hood for safekeeping (Fig .1) (12). To evaluate the porosity of the device, we measured the diameters of several randomly selected pores in both the basket and its cap using ImageJ software. We calculated the average pore diameter along with the standard deviation (mean ± SD). Using these average diameters, we then determined the corresponding pore areas for further analysis.

Human ovarian tissue manipulation and cryopreservation

OT samples were retrieved from 20 transsexual patients, aged 30 to 35, who had not undergone hormone therapy or at least four months had passed since their last hormone therapy. These samples were obtained at Arash Gynecology Hospital and transported to the laboratory promptly within less than an hour in a DMEM-F12 culture medium (Gibco, UK), enriched with fetal bovine serum (FBS), penicillin G, and streptomycin on ice. Following the removal of the medulla, the cortex was sectioned into 3×3×3 mm fragments (13).

The OTs went through a carefully managed two-step freezing process. First, during the equilibration phase, the tissues were soaked in a solution containing human tissue culture medium (HTCM), 7.5% ethylene glycol (EG), and 7.5% dimethyl sulphoxide (DMSO, Sigma-Aldrich, Germany) along with 20% human serum albumin (HAS, Biotestpharma, Germany) for 15 minutes at 4°C, followed by washing. Then, during the vitrification step, the cortical tissue segments were exposed to a solution containing HTCM, 15% EG, 15% DMSO, 0.25 M sucrose (Sigma-Aldrich, USA), and 20% HSA for 10 minutes at 4°C before washing. Finally, all excess solution was removed before the tissues were directly transferred into liquid nitrogen for storage. The strips were warmed using a 4-stage process that involved lowering sucrose concentrations to 1, 0.5, 0.25, and 0.125 M. During this process, the basic medium used was HTCM and 20% HSA (13).

Surgical procedure and ovarian tissue transplantation

Adult female hooded rats (240-280 g) sourced from Helijard Karaj, Iran, were housed in temperature-controlled environments with a 12/12 hour light/dark cycle and 50-55% humidity, with enough access to food and water (14). Cyclosporine-A (210 mg/l) (Sandimmune, Novartis Pharmaceutical, East Hanover, NJ, USA) was administered in their drinking water two days before transplantation for immunosuppression (15). Xenograft of the ovarian cortex was performed 14 days after oophorectomy (16). Rats were randomly allocated into three groups for xenotransplantation and then anesthetized. The area was shaved and sterilized with iodine solution, and then a 1 cm long incision was made. OTs were warmed and transplanted into the back of the neck muscle. Four groups were considered: i. Control: vitrified-warmed OT, ii. OT transplantation without 3D-printed baskets and UCMSCs, iii. OT+D; OT transplantation in 3D-printed baskets without UCMSCs, and iv. OT+D+C; OT transplantation in 3D-printed baskets with UCMSCs. Groups ii, iii, and iv contained six rats each, who received cyclosporine in a water-soluble form daily and were treated with 5 IU of human menopausal gonadotropin (PDHOMOG 75 I.U, Iran) (17). Each vial was aliquoted into 1.5 cc of distilled water, followed by the injection of 100 microliters into each animal. Hormone injection was done one day after transplantation, once every 2 days for a week.

Histological studies

After one week post-transplantation, the rats were put under anesthesia to retrieve the OT samples. These samples were then fixed in formalin and embedded in paraffin. Sections of 5 micrometers were taken and stained with hematoxylin-eosin (H&E). To avoid counting the same follicle multiple times, only follicles containing oocytes with nuclei were counted in every 5th section. The evaluation involved assessing the density of follicles and quantifying follicles at different developmental stages (18, 19). Based on granulosa cell (GC) shape and the number of layers, follicles were classified as primordial, transitional, primary, and preantral. Degenerated follicles were characterized by pyknotic cells, eosinophilia of the ooplasm, and clumping of chromatin material. The percentage of non-growing (primordial) and growing follicles (transitional, primary, and preantral) was also determined (18).

After transplanting vitrified-warmed human ovarian tissue, we used the Image J software to count the number and measure the diameter of blood vessels in H&E sections from the control, OT, OT+D, and OT+D+C groups seven days later. The blood vessel count was conducted at five randomly selected fields in every 5th section of each tissue sample. Moreover, we used the Image J software to measure the diameter of the vessels (20).

Isolation and culture of human umbilical cord cells

UCMSCs were obtained from the Royan Cord Blood Bank and cultured at a density of 1×106 cells in a cell culture dish. In the bank, the umbilical cord tissue was washed with PBS, then cut into 2-3 mm pieces and enzymatically digested. The required culture medium consisted of 10% FBS (Sigma-Aldrich, USA), L-DMEM (Gibco, UK), and 1% v/v penicillin/streptomycin (Invitrogen Gibco, UK), and then the cells were incubated at 37°C with 5% CO2. After three days, non -adherent cells were removed, resulting in the formation of primary colonies of UCMSCs in the culture medium. Subsequently, single cells suitable for transplantation were generated by re-passaging using trypsin-EDTA (Gibco, UK). For transplantation, 25 μl of the obtained cells, containing 2×106 cells, were injected into each graft basket along with human ovarian tissue. The cells used in this study were at passage 4 (21). In the OT+D+C group, OT was combined with UCMSCs. First, a 1 cm incision was made in the neck muscle of the mouse, and the basket containing the tissue was carefully placed in the target area under sterile conditions. Subsequently, 2×106 cells were injected into the basket. Then, the incision was sutured.

Gene expression analysis

To explore Folliculogenesis, the expression of CX43, CX37, KL, and GDF9 genes was examined, along with the VEGF gene, to evaluate the rate of angiogenesis across different groups. Total RNA was extracted using the column method (Biotchrabbit/Germany). Reverse transcription was performed using the cDNA synthesis kit (Add Script, Korea) according to the manufacturer's instructions. The samples were amplified in Azure Cielo (USA) using SYBR Green real-time PCR master mix (amplification), related primers (Table S1, See Supplementary Online Information at www.ijfs.ir), and 50 ng CDNA. Samples were obtained from three to five independent biological replicates, undergoing two technical repeats. The Ct values were normalized to the Ct of GAPDH expression and analyzed via a standard curve, using 2-Ct calculation (22, 23).

Immunohistochemistry studies

The tissue samples underwent immunohistochemical (IHC) analysis using antibodies against CD31 (to assess angiogenesis) and PCNA (for cell proliferation) markers. PCNA is a nuclear protein, crucial in cell cycle regulation, and particularly highlights GC proliferation (24). The formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded tissue sections were placed on Poly-L-Lysine-coated slides, and endogenous peroxidase was inhibited by incubating with 10% hydrogen peroxide in PBS for 10 minutes after deparaffinization. Heat antigen retrieval was achieved by heating the tissue sections at 95°C with sodium citrate buffer (10 mM, pH=6) for 30 minutes. After washing with PBS, the sections were incubated with anti-CD31 (ab28364, Abcam, USA) and anti-PCNA (BD 610665, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) for 50 minutes at room temperature (RT). The slides were washed twice in PBS and immersed with secondary antibodies Anti-CD31 (ab97051, Abcam, USA) and anti-PCNA (ab97245, Abcam, USA) for 45 minutes at RT. After washing the slides twice in PBS, the sections were incubated with the chromogen 3,3′-diaminobenzidine (DAB) for 10 minutes at RT and then washed with tap water. Mayer's hematoxylin dye was employed to stain the nuclei. The slides were mounted and examined under a light microscope.

Analysis of fibrosis and necrosis

To investigate the presence of fibrotic and necrotic regions in various xenotransplanted tissues, we utilized Hematoxylin and Eosin (H&E) staining sections. Fibrosis was identified by several key features: a noticeable reduction in cell density, fewer visible cell nuclei, and an accumulation of collagen fibers. In contrast, necrotic areas displayed distinct characteristics, including condensed nuclei and stromal cells showing signs of degeneration, such as residual cell fragments and empty cytoplasmic spaces. To provide further clarity on the fibrotic regions, we employed Masson’s trichrome staining, which effectively highlights collagen fibers in blue. The tissue sections were examined under a magnification of 100x, and we quantified using Image J software to ensure accurate measurement of the fibrotic and necrotic areas and total section areas. Results were expressed as fibrotic or necrotic areas relative to total section areas (23).

Statistical analysis

The statistical analysis was conducted using SPSS version 16 (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA) for Windows software (version 16). The normality of the parameters was assessed using the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test. For follicular counts, the non-parametric Kruskal-Wallis test, followed by Dunn's multiple comparisons test, From the median and interquartile range (IQR) was applied. Vessel counts, diameters, gene expression, and fibrotic surface areas were analyzed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), followed by Tukey’s test, Data were presented as the mean ± standard deviation (SD). The significance level was set at P<0.05.

Results

Analysis of porosity in 3D printed baskets

An important factor influencing vascularization is the pore size of the structure, which was determined using Image J software. The results indicated an average pore diameter of 700 ± 46.57 micrometers (or 0.7 mm). Additionally, the area of each pore was measured to be 0.49 mm² (Fig .S1, See Supplementary Online Information at <uri>www.ijfs.ir</uri>).

Follicular count and histology of follicles

Follicular counts and classifications were assessed in H&E-stained tissue sections (Fig .2) seven days after human OT transplantation. The evaluation included healthy and degenerative primordial, transitional, primary, preantral, and antral follicles.

The results demonstrated a significant reduction (P<0.001) in the number of healthy primordial follicles in both the OT and OT+D groups compared to the control group. Conversely, the OT+D+C group showed a marked increase (P<0.001) in healthy primordial follicles relative to the OT and OT+D groups (Fig .3A). Degenerative primordial follicles decreased significantly (P<0.001) in the OT and OT+D groups compared to controls, but the OT+D+C group exhibited a higher rate of degenerative primordial follicles than the OT+D group (Fig .3B).

For transitional follicles, healthy counts were higher in the OT+D, OT, and OT+D+C groups than in controls, though this difference was not statistically significant (Fig .3C). Degenerative transitional follicles, however, increased significantly (P<0.001) in the OT+D group compared to controls and the OT group, with the OT+D+C group also showing higher counts than controls (Fig .3D).

Regarding primary follicles, the percentage of healthy primary follicles was significantly increased (P<0.001) in the OT group compared to controls. However, both the OT+D and OT+D+C groups showed a significant reduction in healthy primary follicles relative to the OT group (Fig .3E). Degenerative primary follicles were significantly elevated (P<0.001, 5 [1.25-7.75]), in the OT+D group compared to controls and the OT group, while the OT+D+C group had fewer degenerative primary follicles than the OT+D group (Fig .3F).

Revascularizations and angiogenesis of human ovarian grafts

Significant improvements (P<0.001, P<0.003) in the average number of blood vessels were observed in the OT+D+C and OT+D groups compared to the control group (14[7.5-55]). Additionally, the diameter of blood vessels significantly increased (P<0.001, P<0.008) in the OT+D+C and OT+D groups compared to the control group (7110 [2777-13830]). When assessing the level of angiogenesis in the transplanted tissues, the expression of the VEGF gene, which is a key factor in angiogenesis, was examined. The results revealed a higher expression level of this gene in the OT+D+C and OT+D groups compared to the control and OT groups (Fig .4A- C).

Quantitative gene expression

Compared to the control group, the expression of CX37 and CX43 genes increased significantly (P<0.001) in the OT, OT+D, and OT+D+C groups, with the highest levels observed in the OT and OT+D+C groups, respectively. Notably, CX43 expression in the OT+D+C group was significantly higher than in the OT+D group (P<0.001, 6.270 ± 0.035). The KL gene also showed a significant increase (P<0.001) across all treatment groups compared to the control, with the greatest increase seen in the OT+D and OT+D+C groups. GDF9 expression was markedly elevated (P<0.001, 4.158 ± 0.033) in the OT, OT+D, and OT+D+C groups, with the largest increase in the OT+D+C group, which also exceeded the OT+D group (P<0.001, 4.158 ± 0.033). Both KL and GDF9 expression levels in the OT+D+C group were significantly higher than those in the sham group. Furthermore, VEGF gene expression was significantly increased (P<0.001, OT+D+C: 27.13 ± 0.028), OT+D: 33.92 ± 0.028 in the OT+D+C and OT+D groups compared to the control and OT groups (Fig .4C-G).

Immunohistology staining for PCNA and CD31

The qualitative analysis of the CD31 marker was used to observe blood vessel formation and tissue blood supply after xenotransplantation. The results showed successful blood vessel formation in the OT, OT+D, and OT+D+C groups seven days after the transplantation (Fig .6A-D). We also detected the PCNA marker to track the proliferation and growth of granulosa cells during human OT transplantation. Our examination revealed the presence of stained granulosa cells and growing follicles in all the transplant groups, indicating active cell proliferation and follicular development (Fig .5E-H).

Stromal tissue necrosis and fibrosis

Quantitative analysis of the fibrotic and necrotic areas measured in H&E sections, which were also confirmed by Masson trichrome staining, showed that this condition existed in the OT and OT+D groups as well as in the OT+D+C group. Comparative analysis did show statistically significant differences between the groups (P<0.05, OT: 56.08 ± 0.243, OT+D: 56.08 ± 0.243), but the OT+D+C group (P<0.05, OT+D+C: 42.11 ± 0.243) showed a smaller fibrotic area compared to the OT and OT+D groups, and it seems that cell injection reduced fibrosis slightly compared to the other groups. Furthermore, these transplant groups exhibited more degenerate follicles than the control group, where healthy follicles were predominant (Fig .6, Fig .S2, See Supplementary Online Information at www.ijfs.ir).

Discussion

Women and girls diagnosed with cancer who undergo treatments such as radiation therapy and chemotherapy before the age of 45 may face long-term reproductive issues due to ovarian damage (25). To safeguard fertility following treatment and reduce the chances of cancer recurrence, specialists have developed various strategies, including xenotransplantation and artificial ovaries (5). In this study, for the first time, researchers fabricated a porous basket designed to replicate an artificial ovary and promote angiogenesis. The primary clinical application of this innovative research lies in its potential to enable the retrieval of mature oocytes, which could play a crucial role in preserving fertility and addressing infertility in a range of patients, including women with cancer, POI, and POF.

In this study, using a 3D basket scaffold improved ovarian follicle development and supported the formation of new blood vessels. These findings support the hypothesis that this construct is effective, especially in promoting angiogenesis, and confirm the results of previous studies that used this construct for the transplantation of pancreatic organoids (12), heart tissue (26), and bone transplantation (27), all of which achieved very positive outcomes. The increased expression of the VEGF gene in the OT+D group, likely due to the use of baskets, may explain the occurrence of new blood vessel formation (angiogenesis). Additionally, the higher percentage of developing follicles observed in this group could also be a result of the side effects associated with angiogenesis.

Evaluation of follicular count and histology provides insights into tissue health post-transplantation. In our study, we found that 7 days after xenotransplantation, both the OT and OT+D groups showed activation of primordial follicles, leading to a reduction in follicular density. The reduction was lower in the OT+D+C group compared to the other groups. Moreover, the percentages of growing follicles were 99.3% in the OT+D group, 94.22% in the OT group, and 40.88% in the OT+D+C group. The significant decline in primordial follicle count suggests potential impacts on fertility (28), while an increase in certain follicle types indicates variations in graft response, potentially influenced by transplantation techniques (29). Based on the findings of Cacciottola et al. (30), significant follicle loss occurs within the initial 10 days posttransplantation. This initial activation of primordial follicles in our research may be consistent with the study conducted by researchers show that after OT transplan tation, there is a significant reduction in primordial fol licles mainly due to a period of low oxygen (hypoxia) or reduced blood flow (ischemia). Studies in humans have found that, following transplantation, the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway-which is linked to responses to low oxygen levels-becomes active. This hypoxic phase causes the ovarian reserve to diminish more rapidly, especially in the cortex. The loss of primordial follicles happens not only because of their direct destruction but also because many dormant follicles become abnormally activated and start to grow prematurely. Interestingly, as the primordial follicle pool decreases, there is often a rise in the number of follicles that are actively growing (10).

Consistent with the above observations, there was a significant increase in follicular cell death across transitional, primary, and preantral follicles in the OT and OT+D groups. In contrast, a reduction in cell death was observed in the OT+D+C group, which will be discussed in more detail later. This variation may also be related to the hypoxia/ischemia phenomenon, a factor that has been closely associated with other studies on follicle survival in grafts and the rapid establishment of blood flow (21).

In this cell therapy study, the OT+D+C group demonstrated significant improvement by combining UCMSCs with ovarian transplantation. The benefits included the preservation of ovarian follicle reserve and viability, as well as a reduction in follicle mortality. Additionally, this group showed increased angiogenesis, characterized by a higher number and size of blood vessels, along with elevated expression of VEGF. The positive outcomes observed in this group are consistent with the findings of Cheng et al. (21), who highlighted the paracrine effects of human UCMSCs. They indicated the high expression of markers such as CD31, CD34, and VEGF, as well as other paracrine factors produced by the cells, including basic fibroblast growth factor (bFGF), TGFβ1, insulinlike growth factor 1 (IGF1), and HGF. Furthermore, the therapy may suppress inflammatory cytokines like BCL-2 modifier factor and tumor necrosis factor α (TNFα), potentially due to substances such as the secretome released by mesenchymal cells (21).

The early stage of ovarian follicle development relies on proliferation and differentiation of granulosa cells. In our prospective study, we observed proliferation using the marker PCNA by IHC. Positive expression of PCNA in all groups indicated active growth and proliferation. These findings were consistent with the study by Abir et al. (24) where they observed PCNA expression in most grafts after transplantation of human OT into the back muscle of nude mice. Another study showed brown granulosa cells in developing follicles that expressed PCNA (20).

Subsequent CD31 staining allows quantification and calculation of the number and average number of blood vessels (31). In our study, the OT+D+C graft group showed the highest number of blood vessels and blood vessel diameter, as confirmed by CD31 staining. These findings are consistent with our results.

Our observations and quantitative analyses of tissue fibrosis and necrosis demonstrated that these phenomena occurred across all transplant groups, primarily as a consequence of ischemia. The OT+D group exhibited challenges in follicle maturation and increased degeneration, which were accompanied by elevated fibrosis and necrosis; these effects were relatively less pronounced in the OT+D+C group. The introduction of UCMSCs in the OT+D+C group likely contributed to the reduction of fibrosis and necrosis, as well as improved follicle survival and decreased degeneration, effects that are probably mediated by the paracrine secretions of UCMSCs.

Key drivers of fibrosis include inflammation, signaling pathways, metabolic homeostasis, and the extracellular matrix, which collectively promote fibroblast activation and extracellular matrix (ECM) deposition (32). Human ovarian transplantation studies conducted at one and three weeks post-transplantation have indicated that the transplantation environment significantly affects stromal tissue maintenance (23). To improve the transplantation environment, external interventions such as N-acetylcysteine have been shown to protect ovarian follicles from ischemia-reperfusion injury and reduce collagen deposition in transplanted human ovarian tissue (33). Furthermore, cell therapy involving human umbilical cord cells combined with transplantation has demonstrated that these cells, through their secretions, can reduce necrosis and apoptosis in stromal cells (21). These findings are consistent with our results.

Furthermore, in line with other studies, in our research, the examination of CX37, CX43, and VEGF genes in all three graft groups indicated that their expression was significantly higher than in the control group, demonstrating the activation of primordial follicles in the early stages of grafting, healthy growth of both oocytes and granulosa cells, as well as proper angiogenesis. Yang et al. (34) observed an increased expression of connexin 37 and connexin 43, as well as VEGF, in frozen OT using FSH supplementation in vitro culture, while also observing in avascular grafts. Connexin 43 is located spatially in granulosa cells, whereas connexin 37 is situated between granulosa and oocyte. In 2008, Lee et al. (35) investigated Gap junction gene expression following the transplantation of cryopreserved OT into the subcutaneous area. They noted significantly lower expression levels of these genes compared to normal ovarian tissue. However, our study findings revealed elevated expression of these genes, contradicting their results.

In 2019, Shams Mofarahe et al. (36) found that KL expression remained stable in frozen human OT grafts in mice subjected to γ radiation. However, GDF-9 expression significantly increased in the grafted tissue.

Although our group’s findings have been consistent with the author study regarding the GDF-9 expression, unlike their work, the expression of the gene KL likely demonstrated a considerable increase due to the high percentage of transitional follicle growth. GDF-9 is expressed in growing ovarian follicles (36, 37), and its increased expression may be related to the increase in growing follicles after grafting. However, this activation may lead to a decline and loss of follicular reserve in the grafted ovarian tissue, reducing the lifespan of the grafted tissue (38). Furthermore, our findings align with prior research on the correlation between GDF-9 gene expression and enhanced follicular growth. However, the upregulation of the GDF9 gene in the OT+D+C group could be attributed to the potent paracrine influences of UCMSCs. In short, quantitative gene expression analysis elucidates molecular mechanisms underlying graft response. Upregulation of genes associated with follicular development and angiogenesis suggests tissue remodeling and functional recovery post-transplantation. Understanding gene expression profiles informs potential therapeutic targets for improving graft outcomes (38). On the other hand, addressing the regulatory challenges set by agencies like the Food and Drug Administration (FDA) and the European Medicines Agency (EMA) -such as meeting clinical trial requirements and adhering to Good Manufacturing Practices- is crucial for translating our findings into clinical practice. Future efforts should prioritize navigating these pathways to ensure compliance and facilitate the eventual approval of our therapeutic approach.

Conclusion

Based on our findings, the basket supported ovarian follicle development by promoting angiogenesis and facilitating maturation. However, when combined with stem cells, the paracrine secretions enhance follicular preservation, preventing apoptosis and maintaining follicle viability. Given the basket’s efficacy in angiogenesis and the protective role of stem cells against follicle death, further investigation into their long-term transplantation potential is recommended. Additionally, exploring additional factors for advancing developmental stages is warranted.
