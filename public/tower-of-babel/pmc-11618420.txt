Multistage Template-Oriented Design and Tailoring of the Hierarchical Porous Biocarbon Materials for High-Performance Supercapacitors
Authors: Guimei Jin, Qihang Zhou, Zhiyuan Duan, Kaiyuan Shen, Zhiwei Dong, Shukang Deng, Peizhi Yang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractInterfacial regulation is crucial for the enhancement of the specific surface area and supercapacitive performance in porous carbon materials. However, traditional porous carbon obtained through potassium hydroxide activation is confronted with a complicated preparation process. Herein, hierarchical porous biocarbons (HPBC) were facilely prepared by using the multiscale template method and wheat flour as a precursor without any activation. A spherical SiO2 template and wheat flour can be closely combined by gelatinization so as to effectively confine and spatially orient carbon materials. Benefiting from the hierarchical porous structure achieved through the use of templates at different sizes, HPBC-3 has the best electrochemical performance of 223.6 F·g–1 at 1 A·g–1. Moreover, the assembled HPBC-3//HPBC-3 symmetrical supercapacitor demonstrates a remarkable energy density of 15.6 W h·kg–1 and exhibits exceptional cycling performance with a capacitance retention rate of 106% after 5000 cycles. The results fully demonstrate the promising application of the obtained hierarchical porous carbon in supercapacitors.

Introduction

Currently, energy storage systems such as batteries, fuel cells, and supercapacitors play a crucial role in facilitating the integration of renewable energy sources by effectively storing surplus energy and enhancing overall energy efficiency.1−4 Notably, supercapacitors are particularly noteworthy among energy storage devices due to their superior advantages in terms of high-rate capability, exceptional cycle stability, minimal maintenance requirements, and safety features.5−8 According to the energy storage mechanism, supercapacitors can be categorized into electrical double layers (EDLCs) and pseudocapacitor mechanisms. The EDLC mechanism primarily relies on the electrostatic adsorption and occurs at the electrode–electrolyte interface.9 Compared to pseudocapacitors, EDLCs exhibit enhanced electrochemical stability and rate performance, positioning them as the preferred option for commercialization.10−12 Unfortunately, the energy density of commercial supercapacitors remains inadequate due to the constraints of the EDLC mechanism.13,14 Hence, in order to enhance the competitiveness of EDLC-based materials, a meticulous engineering of pore structure and precise distribution of pore structure are essential prerequisites for achieving outstanding performance in supercapacitors.15,16

Carbon materials, particularly porous carbon, have been extensively researched as EDLC-based materials for supercapacitors due to their cost-effectiveness and consistent electrochemical performance.17 Obviously, extensive research has been undertaken in the field of carbon materials engineering, specifically focusing on the manipulation of the pore structure. Typically, the preparation of hierarchically porous carbon materials involves methods such as activation,18,19 hydrothermal synthesis,20 and template fabrication.21−28 Among the aforementioned options, the activation method effectively increases the specific surface area of carbon materials, but the distribution of pore structure remains uneven, which hinders the achievement of a well-ordered pore structure in carbon materials. Besides, the elevated temperature experimental conditions and prolonged preparation process linked to the hydrothermal synthesis appear to reduce the effectiveness of the synthetic electrode, making it unsuitable for large-scale industrial production. In contrast to other methods, the template method allows for precise control over the pore structure and ensures a high level of structural integrity and uniformity in the resulting materials.

Common hard templates widely used in research include zeolite,29 mesoporous silica,30 ZnO,31 SiO2,32 and MgO.33 The hard templates demonstrate effective control over the hierarchical pore structure of carbon materials. In general, the pore sizes are commensurate with its functional significance. Macroporous structures (>50 nm) contribute to high specific surface areas for adsorption–desorption reactions and ion–electron transport, leading to increased specific capacitance. Mesoporous structures (2–50 nm) serve as ion-buffering reservoirs, effectively shortening the ion-transport pathways. Microporous structures (<2 nm) play a predominant role in the charge storage process, thereby significantly impacting the specific surface area of carbon materials.34 As a low-cost inorganic substance, spherical SiO2 is available in a variety of sizes, which provides convenience for the controllable structural design of graded porous carbon.

Focusing on the fabrication of eco-friendly and hierarchically structured carbon materials, the gelatinization of flour was employed to ensure the homogeneous dispersion of the template and precursor, and then hierarchical porous biocarbons (HPBC) were fabricated via one-step pyrolysis of the mixtures of SiO2 templates and wheat flour precursor. By utilization of multistage size spherical SiO2 templates to tailor the pore structure of carbon, the impact of the intentionally designed pore architecture on the electrochemical performance of carbon-based electrode materials was investigated. To be endowed with a deeply developed three-dimensional interconnected micro–meso–macroporous structure, the obtained carbon-based materials were employed as electrodes for supercapacitors, exhibiting high specific capacitance, good rate characteristics, stable electrochemical performance, and high powder density.

Experimental Section

Materials

The pure wheat flour used in the experiment was purchased from a local supermarket. The wheat flour is produced by grinding wheat based on the implementation standard of GB/T1355 (China), with all of the bran and germ completely removed. It is standard flour with a moisture content of less than 14.5% and an ash content of less than 1.1%, with no other additives. Spherical silica with different particle sizes was purchased from Shanghai MCC New Materials Co., Ltd. Polytetrafluoroethylene (PTFE) was purchased from the Cyber Electrochemical Materials Network. The carbon black is Dutch carbon black. Anhydrous sodium sulfate, potassium hydroxide KOH, and hydrofluoric acid (HF) (40%) were purchased from China National Pharmaceutical Group Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd. These chemicals were analytical-grade reagents without further purification. Deionized water was used throughout all of the experiments.

Synthesis of HPBC

The wheat flour was blended with SiO2 templates at a mass ratio of 1:4, and the proportion of different sizes of SiO2 was adjusted accordingly (Table 1). The wheat flour and SiO2 were evenly dispersed in deionized water and heated to 60 °C by magnetic stirring in a water bath for 30 min. The mixture is desiccated and pulverized into a fine powder. The powder was then carbonized in a tube furnace under a N2 atmosphere, heated to 800 °C with a ramping rate of 5 °C·min–1, and maintained at that temperature for 2 h. After cooling to room temperature, the obtained powders were soaked in a 40% HF solution over 24 h to remove SiO2 and then washed with deionized water to neutral. The resulting dark products were collected and denoted as HPBC-x.

Material Characterization

The morphologies were acquired through ultrahigh-resolution field-emission scanning electron microscopy (SEM, JEOL JSM-7610F Plus, 5 kV) and transmission electron microscopy (TEM, JEOL JEM-2100 Plus, 200 kV). The crystalline structures were investigated through X-ray diffraction (XRD, Rigaku MiniFlex II, Cu Kα radiation source). Raman spectra were determined by using a Jobin Yvon micro-Raman spectrometer (iHR-550) under illumination with a 488 nm laser. Nitrogen adsorption–desorption isotherms were examined through a Micromeritics ASAP 2060. The surface area S, pore size distribution, pore volume V, and micropore area were calculated through Brunauer–Emmett–Teller (BET) analysis. X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) was performed using a PHI 5000 VersaProbe and monochromatic Al Kα radiation.

Electrode Fabrication and Electrochemical Measurements

The electrodes were prepared by thoroughly mixing HPBCC) (or B, carbon black, and poly binder PTFE in a mass ratio of 75:20:5 with ethanol to form a uniform slurry. This slurry was then pressed onto a nickel foam current collector (1 × 1 cm2). Subsequently, the electrodes were dried at 110 °C overnight.

All electrochemical measurements were conducted using a CHI 660E electrochemical workstation. The three-electrode electrochemical measurements used 6 M KOH aqueous solutions as the electrolytes with Hg/HgO electrodes as the reference electrodes. Symmetric supercapacitors were assembled in a 1 M Na2SO4 aqueous solution by using a glassy fibrous separator.

Results and Discussion

The synthesis method for HPBC is outlined in Figure 1. By utilizing wheat flour as the carbon precursor, SiO2 spherical templates of varying size (depicted as differently colored dots in Figure 1—red corresponds to 20 nm, blue corresponds to 300 nm, and gray corresponds to 1000 nm) were homogeneously dispersed into the system through stirring and hydrothermal gelatinization. During this process, the flour can cross-link to the surface of colloidal silica nanoparticles, leading to the formation of the uniform paste. Subsequent processes of drying, mixing, carbonization of the spherical templates, and elution with 40% HF led to the formation of spongiform carbon materials. Through utilization of SiO2 spherical templates with diverse particle sizes for proportioning, it is possible to effectively confine and orient carbon materials spatially. This method of preparation establishes a foundation for providing active potentials for abundant ion channels.

Formation of HPBCs by altering the ratios of SiO2 spherical templates with different sizes in the precursors.

SEM was employed to investigate the morphology and microstructure of the samples. The SEM image of BC (Figure 2a) reveals that the carbon material obtained without the addition of template material exhibits a dense structure with only a limited number of pores related to the agglomeration of starch molecules during flour gelatinization and subsequent carbonization. Molecular chains of flour break during carbonization, resulting in structural collapse and agglomeration, ultimately forming a compact structure with minimal porosity.35 Conversely, the uppermost image in Figure 2b demonstrates that when SiO2 templates are added to the precursor in specific proportions, SiO2 can be embedded in the assembled lamellar structure of HPBC-3. Carbonization and elution processes yield a spongy morphology characterized by multiple layers, specific pore sizes, and a proportionate distribution.

Morphology of BC and HPBC. SEM images of (a) BC and (b,c) HPBC-3. (d,e) TEM image of HPBC-3. (f) HRTEM image of HPBC-3.

The HPBC-3 shown in Figure 2c reveals a vacant inner space with the white arrows highlighted, which can serve as a reservoir for electrolytes and provide a stable interface for ions. Thus, HPBC-3 exhibits a hollow spherical structure characterized by multistage channels, which is determined by SiO2 templates of varying sizes. The detailed morphology of HPBC-3 was studied by TEM. The transparent and curled nanosheets observed in Figure 2d provide additional evidence supporting the composition of HPBC-3 as ultrathin carbon sheets, which assemble into spherical pores.36 Additionally, Figure 2e demonstrates the presence of spherical mesopores with diameters of approximately 20 nm, which can be attributed to the effective control of the thermal cracking process through SiO2 templates of varying sizes. The high-resolution TEM (HRTEM) image in Figure 2f shows that the thickness of the carbon nanosheet is about 4 nm, which is crucial for improving ion diffusion kinetics.38

The porosity of the biocarbon derived from the flour was characterized by nitrogen adsorption–desorption measurements. As shown in Figure 3a, the isotherms of pristine BC demonstrate a typical IV-type-shaped isotherm, suggesting the existence of rich macro-/mesoporosities, which is in accordance with the TEM analysis result. The volume increases rapidly under low relative pressure, and there is an obvious hysteresis ring when P/P0 > 0.4, demonstrating the coexistence of micropores and mesopores.37,38 Moreover, the almost vertical tail at a relative pressure near 0.99 confirms the existence of macropores. Obviously, all of the HPBC samples have a much higher N2 adsorbed amount as compared to BC.39,40 The pore size distributions of BC and HPBCs were additionally assessed through application of the density functional theory approach (Figure 3b); the utilization of SiO2 (1000 nm) for HPBC-1 is sufficient, resulting in the highest pores (787 m2·g–1). The reduction in SiO2 content (1000 nm) to 10% resulted in a decrease in the specific surface area to 457 m2·g–1, suggesting that a higher percentage of SiO2 (1000 nm) may promote the formation of a thin-walled pore structure, thereby enhancing the specific surface area. However, this elevated specific surface area does not contribute to an increase in the specific capacity of the material. Previous studies have also demonstrated that the presence of an excessively thin pore wall results in mutual interference between the counterions adsorbed on both sides of the pore, consequently diminishing the number of counterions and leading to a reduction in capacitance.41 Further decreasing the percentage of SiO2 (1000 nm) to zero, the specific surface area of HPBC-3 and HPBC-4 increased to 686 and 655m2·g–1, respectively. The proportion of meso-/macropores increased, possibly due to the inhibitory effect of SiO2 (20 nm) on the thermal degradation of flour.

(a) N2 adsorption–desorption isotherms and (b) pore size distribution curves of the samples.

Figure 4a demonstrates the XRD spectrometer patterns of BC and the HPBC-x. The presence of a characteristic diffraction peak at 22° corresponds to the (002) peak of graphite nanocrystallites, while the broad peak width indicates that the carbonized material is amorphous carbon,42 consistent with HRTEM test results. Compared to BC, HPBCs exhibit a significant shift toward lower angles for its (002) diffraction peak, demonstrating reduced crystallinity in the stacking direction due to changes in the material structure caused by different scales of SiO2 template introduction. Spatial confinement from the template increases carbon layer spacing and reduces crystal size. Additionally, a significant reduction in the intensity of the (100) peak is observed, indicating a decrease of the HPBCs in in-plane crystallinity potentially attributed to carbon layer bending, point defects, and diminished crystal size.43 Furthermore, small-angle diffraction signals confirm abundant pore structures within HPBCs suitable for electric double-layer energy storage in carbon materials. Clearly, SiO2 templates play a decisive role in determining whether the resulting carbonized material becomes soft carbon while also enhancing energy storage characteristics of flour-based carbons through structural modifications. The relaxed structure of the carbon layers provides more sites for electrolyte ion adsorption and desorption without compromising conductivity, thereby improving electrochemical performance.

(a) XRD patterns, (b) Raman spectra and XPS spectra of HPBC-3, (c) full survey scan (inset: content of C 1s, O 1s, and N 1s), and (d) high-resolution C 1s spectra.

The variation of crystallinity and textural structure was further analyzed by Raman spectra (Figure 4b). The peaks positioned at around 1350 cm–1 represented the D band, which arises from sp3 carbon structures or structural defects in graphitic domains. Besides, the G band at around 1580 cm–1 corresponds to sp2-bonded carbon and is a characteristic feature of graphitic layers. Typically, the shift in the G and D peaks can be attributed to changes in the crystal structure,44−46 specifically due to doping with different levels of N elements, which alters the structural composition of carbon material. Furthermore, the relative intensity ratio of the D band to G band (ID/IG) is an index of the graphitic degree of carbon materials; higher ID/IG ratios of HPBC indicate that a large number defect sites can be generated by utilizing the SiO2 template. These defect sites have the propensity to enhance the ion diffusion kinetics.47

The surface elemental composition of carbon materials was examined by XPS. The XPS spectra exhibit two distinct characteristic peaks at 284.8 and 532 eV, corresponding to C 1s and O 1s,48 respectively (Figure 4c). Additionally, the samples also contained a minor amount of nitrogen resulting from flour pyrolysis. Obviously, HPBC-3 consists mainly of C and O, with the oxygen content reaching as high as 4.6 atom %. The high-resolution scan of the C 1s spectrum in Figure 4d includes four subpeaks at 284.8, 285.7, 288.9, and 291.0 eV, corresponding to sp2-hybridized graphitic carbon (C=C), C–O, C=O, and O–C=O, respectively.49 These oxygen-containing groups enable active participation in faradaic redox reactions and facilitate material permeability, thereby contributing supplementary pseudocapacitance to the electrode.

To evaluate the superiority of HPBC as a conductive base for supercapacitors, the electrochemical performances of BC and HPBC electrodes were investigated in a conventional three-electrode system. In comparison to the irregularly shaped BC samples (Figure S1), the cyclic voltammetry (CV) curves at a scan rate of 50 mV·s–1 for HPBC electrodes display relatively rectangular shapes without obvious distortion, indicating an ideal electrical double-layer behavior (Figure 5a). Besides, HPBC exhibits a pair of wide and well-symmetrical redox peaks at around −0.5 V, indicating that the template can effectively enhance the pseudocapacitance of the flour-based carbon material by regulating its morphology. Typically, the largest CV integrated area of HPBC-3 demonstrates its superior specific capacitance performance. Furthermore, Figure 5b illustrates the nearly rectangular shape of the CV curve for the HPBC-3 electrode, indicating typical electrical double-layer behavior and exceptional rate performance.50

Three-electrode electrochemical characteristics: (a) CV curves of BC and HPBC at 50 mV·s–1, (b) CV curves at different scan rates of HPBC-3, and (c) GCD curves at different current densities of HPBC-3. (d) Specific capacitance obtained from GCD curves at different current densities.

The galvanostatic charge/discharge curves of the HPBC-3 exhibit a highly symmetrical triangle without obvious IR drop (Figure 5c), demonstrating little overall resistance and ideal capacitance characteristics.51 Meanwhile, all GCD curves for the other HPBCs exhibit a quasi-triangular shape, indicating that the electrodes demonstrate typical electrical double-layer capacitive behavior. The GCD curves of BC exhibit pronounced asymmetry and substantial voltage drops, indicating that the electrochemical response is relatively sluggish (Figure S2). The specific capacitance of the HPBC-3 electrode calculated by the discharge curves is 223.6 F·g–1 at 1 A·g–1 (Figure 5d), which is higher than that of BC (13.4 F·g–1), HPBC-1 (99.0 F·g–1), HPBC-2 (117.8 F·g–1), and HPBC-4 (188.5 F·g–1). Furthermore, the HPBC-3 electrode continues to exhibit a high capacitance of 119 F·g–1 at 100 A·g–1, demonstrating an excellent rate performance.

Electrochemical impedance spectroscopy was performed to evaluate the resistance of the internal diffusion and ion transportation. The Nyquist plots of HPBC-3 in Figure 6a reveal a steep linear slope in the low-frequency region, indicating rapid transportation processes and ideal capacitive behavior.52 Furthermore, the diagram clearly illustrates that the semicircle of HPBC-3 is smaller than that of BC, indicating a lower charge transfer resistance. The real-axis intersection also indicates that the lower HPBC-3 has an equivalent series resistance (Rs) value, demonstrating high ionic conductivity at the electrode–electrolyte interface. In Figure S3, all samples expose almost similar impedance spectra, with a vertical line at the low-frequency range and an arc in the high-frequency region. The Rs of BC, HPBC-1, HPBC-2, HPBC-3, and HPBC-4 are 0.53, 0.62, 0.39, 0.48, and 0.47 Ω, respectively. Furthermore, Bode phase plots of HPBC-3 exhibit a low relaxation time constant (τ0) of 740 ms (Figure 6b), which is significantly lower than that of BC (973 ms) and activated carbon (1670 ms).53 This corresponds to relatively faster ion-transport dynamics. The electrochemical stability was demonstrated by repeated CV tests. As shown in Figure 6c, the HPBC-3 electrode still exhibits 103% capacitance retention after 5000 cycles, suggesting its outstanding structural stability.

(a) Nyquist plot of BC and HPBC-3. The inset of (a) displays an enlarged partial view. (b) Bode plot of BC and HPBC-3. (c) Cyclic stability of HPBC-3 at a scan rate of 50 mV·s–1. The inset of (c) shows CV curves of the first and 5,000th cycles.

In order to evaluate the energy density for practical potential of supercapacitors, HPBC-3//HPBC-3 symmetrical supercapacitors were fabricated in 1 M Na2SO4. The nearly rectangular CV curves at different scan rates demonstrate capacitive behavior and fast response properties (Figure 7a).54−56 The galvanostatic charge/discharge curves in Figure 7b exhibit good symmetry, indicating a high efficiency and rapid energy storage capabilities. Figure 7c illustrates the Ragone plot of the HPBC-3//HPBC-3 symmetrical supercapacitor, which achieves an energy density of 15.6 W h·kg–1 at a power density of 70 W·kg–1, which makes it superior to the commercial active carbon (5–8 W h kg–1). Even at a high power density of 5 kW·kg–1, the energy density remains at 4.5 W h·kg–1, which is higher than that of electrochemical capacitors.

Electrochemical measurement results of the HPBC-3//HPBC-3 symmetrical supercapacitor in a 1 M Na2SO4 electrolyte. (a) CV curves at various scan rates. (b) GCD curves at various current densities. (c) Ragone plots. (d) Electrochemical stability tested at 100 mV·s–1 for 5000 cycles.

The comparative electrochemical performances are presented in Table 2, demonstrating that the energy density of the device investigated in this study surpasses those of previously reported supercapacitors utilizing carbon-based electrode materials prepared via the template or activation method.57−64 It is worth mentioning that the long-cycle testing experiments were tested at 100 mV·s–1, resulting in an excellent lifetime performance of 106% after 5000 cycles (Figure 7d). The increasing performance can be attributed to the improved infiltration of the electrode material during the charge and discharge process. Additionally, the electrolyte used in assembling the device utilizes Na2SO4, with a smaller ionic radius for Na+ compared to that for K+. The multistage pore structure of the electrode material is efficiently utilized, resulting in an excellent lifetime performance. Besides, the structure of the electrode was characterized both before and after the cycling test, revealing its exceptional stability (Figure S4). Furthermore, the stable hollow spherical structure revealed that its morphology remained basically unchanged, indicating that HPBC-3 exhibits excellent electrochemical and chemical stability (Figure S5).

For comparison, a BC//BC symmetrical supercapacitor fabricated under identical conditions was evaluated. The shapes of CV curves bear resemblance to that of a rectangle when the potential window is extended up to 1.4 V. Thus, the electrochemical capability of the BC//BC symmetrical supercapacitor was tested in the voltage range from 0 to 1.4 V (Figure S6). The nearly rectangular CV curves and symmetry galvanostatic charge/discharge curves demonstrate capacitive behavior (Figure S7a, b). However, as illustrated in Figure S7c, the reduced potential window and decreased specific capacitance of the BC//BC symmetrical supercapacitor led to a lower energy density of 7.3 W h·kg–1 at a power density of 37.6 W·kg–1. Furthermore, the BC//BC symmetrical supercapacitor exhibits a specific capacitance retention of 101.2% after 5000 cycles measured at 100 mV·s–1 (Figure S7d). Ultimately, the stability of BC is slightly lower than that of HPBC-3.

Conclusions

In summary, a novel strategy for the fabrication of HPBC through the carbonization of wheat flour utilizing SiO2 templates of varying sizes was implemented. This approach results in a spherical structure with multistage channels that endows the carbon electrode with enhanced specific capacitance and excellent electrochemical stability. More significantly, a desirable energy density of about 15.6 W h·kg–1 at a power density of 70 W·kg–1 has been achieved for the symmetric supercapacitor devices. Additionally, the as-assembled supercapacitors exhibit high energy densities at high power densities and outstanding cycling stability. This innovative strategy will facilitate the design of carbon-based materials for ultrahigh energy density storage devices.
