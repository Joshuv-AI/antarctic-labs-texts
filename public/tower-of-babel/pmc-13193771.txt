Speciation of arsenic in chicken thigh and breast meat using solvent extraction and selective reduction HG-AAS
Authors: Jenna-Marie Alvarez, Terry I Mohammed
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe analysis of Arsenic species in poultry is commonly performed using high-performance liquid chromatography coupled with inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry (HPLC-ICP-MS). However, for laboratories without access to this instrumentation, solvent extraction/selective reduction (HG-AAS) provides a viable alternative. While this method does not separate individual organic Arsenic species, it enables effective isolation and quantification of extractable inorganic and organic Arsenic species specifically As(III) and As(V) from the chicken matrix, which can be utilized for risk based assessments. Non-extractable Arsenic containing organic Arsenic and protein bound inorganic Arsenic was also determined from the solid residue, whereas the soluble extract was analyzed for Inorganic Arsenic species.•Extraction: Solvent extraction was used to isolate Arsenic species from chicken meat samples using a 30–70 methanol–water mixture.•Optimization: Extraction time (2, 4, 6, 8, and 18 h) and solvent composition (type and methanol–water ratios) were optimized to maximize recovery. 6 hrs extraction was optimum.•Quantification: Arsenic species were quantified using hydride generation atomic absorption spectroscopy (HG-AAS) with selective chemical reduction to distinguish As(III) from As(V).Keywords: Arsenic speciation, Solvent extraction, Total arsenic, Inorganic arsenic, Arsenic (III), Arsenic(V), Organic arsenic, Chicken HG-AAS, Atomic absorption

Background

Arsenic levels in poultry are of particular concern to developing countries and other countries where Arsenic regulations in meats are lacking. Chronic Arsenic consumption is linked to a higher risk of cancer, diabetes and hypertension [1]. Arsenic is added to animal feed in the form of Roxarsone, which is an Organic form of Arsenic and not considered toxic, but when ingested by animals, they partly convert to the more toxic Inorganic form in the liver [1]. This combined Organic and Inorganic Arsenic are distributed to soil and crops through the use of poultry manure. There is evidence that Inorganic Arsenic can be methylated to the trivalent form in the human intestines, which is even more toxic than the Inorganic forms of Arsenic [1]. Speciation of Arsenic is therefore critical in assessing the human and environmental health risk these species pose.

Arsenic regulations for meat and poultry vary considerably among countries. The United States, China, and Canada have established a maximum limit of 0.5 mg kg⁻¹ for total Arsenic (tAs) in meat products [2]. In contrast, the U.S. Environmental Protection Agency (USEPA) has defined a reference dose (RfD) of 0.3 µg kg⁻¹ body weight per day for inorganic Arsenic (iAs) [2]. This EPA reference dose is not a legally enforceable food standard, but a scientific benchmark used to evaluate potential health risks from dietary exposure.

The most common technology used to monitor Arsenic species in poultry meat, wastes, and feed are HPLC-ICPMS [3], and IC-ICP-MS. However, for many developing countries, these technologies are not available. The use of alternative, less costly techniques for the speciation of Arsenic in poultry, meat, and feed can significantly improve public health monitoring and reduce the risks of chronic Arsenic exposure. This paper describes the optimization of a simple liquid extraction method followed by selective hydride generation atomic absorption spectrophotometry (Selective-HGAAS).

The EPA RfD considers total inorganic Arsenic, that is, the sum of As(III) and As(V) without distinguishing between the individual species. Most international health standards and health risk assessments focus primarily on the total Inorganic Arsenic content (iAs) [4]. Only a few studies differentiate the risks associated with As(III) and As(V) species [5].

However, differentiating these species is scientifically important because As(III) is significantly more toxic than As(V). Arsenite [As(III)] is more soluble, more readily absorbed in the gastrointestinal tract, binds strongly to sulfhydryl groups in proteins, and is excreted more slowly [5]. Arsenate [As(V)], while still toxic, is generally less reactive and is reduced to As(III) inside the body before exerting its toxicity. These characteristics make the selective determination of As(III) particularly important for accurately assessing potential human health risks.

Therefore, even though regulatory frameworks often treat iAs as a combined value (As(III) + As(V)), Arsenic speciation provides a more accurate assessment of potential health risks. Distinguishing between the two species can help refine exposure assessments, improve risk characterization, and support the development of future health standards that more accurately reflect the toxicological differences between Arsenic forms.

Method selection

The analysis of Inorganic Arsenic (iAs) involves the separation of Organic and Inorganic species from the sample matrix, followed by the analysis of each component. In several studies, the further separation of Organic species is achieved using HPLC-ICP-MS, where HPLC separates chemical species such As(III), As(V), Dimethylarsinic Acid, Monomethylarsinic Acid, p-Arsanilic Acid, and Roxarsone [6].

The extraction of Inorganic Arsenic from biological tissues can be accomplished using solvent extraction. Several techniques have been employed for this, including water, water–methanol, water-ethanol, and water-acetonitrile, with methods such as shaking, ultrasonic, solvent, and microwave-assisted extraction [6]. While there is limited research specifically focusing on solvent extraction for Arsenic analysis in chicken tissues, this method is less commonly used for this matrix. More advanced techniques, such as HPLC (High-Performance Liquid Chromatography), are often preferred due to their higher sensitivity and resolution. Most studies on Arsenic speciation in chicken tissues typically employ methods like HPLC-ICP-MS or other instrumental techniques, which are favored for their ability to efficiently separate and detect various Arsenic species.

In developing the extraction method for this research, elements from various studies on other matrices, such as fish and rice, were adapted and modified. These existing studies served as the foundation for optimizing the solvent extraction protocol, combining the strengths of different techniques to achieve efficient Arsenic extraction and speciation in chicken tissues. This approach resulted in a method that is both practical and effective, considering the limitations of available methodologies and the unique characteristics of the chicken matrix.

Effects of different solvents on recoveries

The choice of extraction solvent greatly influences the recovery of Arsenic species from meat matrices. Among various solvent systems, methanol-water mixtures, particularly in ratios like 30:70 or 50:50 ( % v/v), have been reported to provide superior recoveries for both Inorganic and Organic Arsenic species [9]. This is largely due to methanol's ability to disrupt lipid membranes and denature proteins, enhancing the solubilization of Arsenic species embedded in tissue matrices [6].

Zhang et al. (2015) optimized the extraction using 22 ( % v/v) methanol, 90 mmol/L ammonium phosphate, and 0.07( % v/v) trifluoroacetic acid (TFA), reporting recoveries of 91.6–104.0 % for various Arsenic species from chicken tissue [6]. Enzymatic methods, which utilize aqueous buffers and enzymes like Tripsin, also offer excellent recoveries, particularly for sensitive Arsenic species like As(III) [10]. However, these enzymatic systems often require careful optimization of pH and temperature conditions to match the performance of methanol–water mixtures.

In general, methanol–water mixtures appear to be the most used extractant for inorganic arsenic determination. It remains the preferred solvents for meat matrix extraction due to their polarity, which facilitates the dissolution of both Inorganic and Organic-bound Inorganic Arsenic compounds without significant degradation. The methanol-water mixtures therefore appear to be the most reliable and cost effective extractant.

Effects of different extraction techniques on recoveries

The method of extraction significantly affects the yield and accuracy of Arsenic species recovery. Among the methods studied, Solvent extraction has consistently demonstrated superior recoveries when compared to simpler techniques like shaking or static leaching. Solvent extraction offers continuous solvent percolation, which maintains high concentration gradients, enhancing mass transfer and promoting better recoveries over time.

In comparison, Microwave-Assisted Extraction (MAE) provides faster extraction times but occasionally at the cost of slightly lower recoveries for sensitive species due to potential decomposition under high temperatures [9]. Enzymatic extraction has proven particularly useful for the extraction of unstable species like Arsenite (As(III)), and when combined with ultra sonication, it rivals Solvent recoveries for some matrices [11].

Nevertheless, solvent extraction remains advantageous for full recovery of Total Arsenic and robust separation of bound species in complex matrices like chicken tissues and liver, it aids in liberating Arsenic species without degradation.

Effects of extraction time on recoveries

Extraction time is a critical parameter in the Arsenic extraction process, and contrary to common assumptions, longer extraction times do not always result in higher recoveries. In Solvent extraction trials, for example, extending the extraction time from 6 to 8 h or even 18 h only yielded marginal improvements in recovery rates. The recovery plateaued after about 6 h, suggesting that prolonged extraction results in diminishing returns. Excessive extraction times may even cause re-adsorption of Arsenic species onto the matrix or lead to minor degradation, ultimately offering no significant improvement in recovery [13].

Similarly, Microwave-Assisted Extraction (MAE) techniques achieved high recoveries within just 7 to 21 min. This demonstrates that solvent penetration and heating can efficiently release Arsenic species without the need for prolonged processing [6]. While longer extraction times in traditional Solvent extraction methods ensure a comprehensive complete extraction, shorter times in MAE and Ultra-Sound Assisted Extraction (UAE) can also yield comparable recoveries, provided there is sufficient energy input and optimized solvent selection [14].

However, extended heating, especially in the presence of strong acids, can result in the degradation of Arsenic species or their conversion to other forms, compromising the accuracy of speciation. For instance, prolonged heating at elevated temperatures can break down Arsenic species, converting As(III) to As(V) or vice versa, which might skew recovery results [14].

Therefore, the key to maximizing recovery and maintaining species integrity lies in finding a balance between extraction time, the specific extraction technique employed, and the solvent composition. Shorter extraction times using MAE or UAE can be just as effective as longer solvent extraction times, but only if the technique is appropriately optimized. This makes it essential to consider the specific characteristics of the sample matrix and the target Arsenic species when determining the ideal extraction time.

Glassware preparation

All glassware was washed with an anionic detergent, rinsed with tap water followed by deionized water, then soaked for 24 h in a 10 % v/v nitric acid (HNO₃) bath and rinsed again with deionized water. The glassware was air-dried at room temperature before use. The acid bath was prepared in a 32-quart (≈30 L) polypropylene container by adding 3.0 L of concentrated HNO₃ (15.7 mol L⁻¹) to ∼27 L of deionized water (acid into water) to obtain a 10 ( % v/v) solution.

Sample selection

Chicken thigh and breast samples were collected randomly from poultry depots and retail supermarkets in various parts of the country. These samples were selected as typical chicken consumed by the population of Trinidad and Tobago.

Sample preparation

Chicken samples were stored in new Ziploc bags at - 20 °C until analysis. They were then thawed to room temperature and homogenized using a food processor, which was previously washed and rinsed with nitric acid and deionized water. The samples were “coned and quartered” down to about 40–50 g subsample. These subsamples were further ground using a Dounce tissue grinder to rupture the cell walls.

Analysis of total arsenic (tAs)

Approximately 0.5 g of processed and homogenized sample was weighed in triplicate to four decimal places on an analytical balance and transferred directly into boiling tubes. The samples were pre-digested with 10 mL of ACS grade concentrated Nitric Acid at room temperature for 24 h to ensure complete digestion. Following the pre-digestion step, the samples were heated to 130 °C for 120 min on a temperature-controlled block (with covers/reflux to minimize evaporative loss), which aided in further breakdown of the sample matrix. After digestion, the samples were allowed to cool to room temperature. They were then filtered through Whatman No 541 filter paper into 50 mL volumetric flasks. The filtrates were diluted to a final volume of 50 mL with deionized water to ensure the appropriate concentration for further analysis [15].

For Arsenic (As) speciation, 5 mL aliquots of the digest were transferred into 25 mL volumetric flasks. To reduce arsenate (As(V)) to arsenite (As(III)), 5 mL of 5 mol L⁻¹ HCl and 5 mL of 1 ( % w/v) KI were added to each flask. The samples were diluted to 25 mL with deionized water and allowed to stand at room temperature for 50 min, facilitating the reduction of As(V) to the more stable As(III) species required for hydride generation [15].

The prepared samples were analyzed for total Arsenic by hydride-generation atomic absorption spectrometry (HG-AAS). Hydrides were formed by reacting the acidified sample stream (HCl, 10 % v/v) with a reductant stream of sodium borohydride (NaBH₄, 0.6 % w/v prepared in 0.5 % w/v NaOH). Under these conditions, Arsenic is converted to the volatile hydride species, enabling detection at 193.7 nm. As(V) was quantitatively reduced to As(III) in the KI/HCl pre-reduction step prior to hydride generation [15].

Analysis of total extractable arsenic (iAs)

Methanol–water (30:70 % v/v) were added to a 250 mL round-bottom flask along with 5–8 boiling chips. Approximately 10 g of the homogenized sample was placed in a cellulose thimble and extracted with the methanol–water (30:70) solvent using optimized conditions for solvent extraction. The solvent was then removed from the extract using a rotary evaporator, leaving behind the inorganic extract with minimal residual solvent. This inorganic extract was dried overnight in a desiccator and then solubilized with 10 mL of 1 mol L⁻¹ nitric acid at room temperature. The extract was quantitatively transferred to a 50 mL volumetric flask, and the final volume was made up to 50 mL with deionized water.

Samples were prepared in triplicate. To 5 mL of the diluted extract, 0.25 g KI, 0.05 g ascorbic acid, and 2.5 mL of 1.2 mol L⁻¹ HCl were added. The mixture was left to stand for 50 min to facilitate the complete reduction of As(V) to As(III). The volume was made up to 25 mL with deionized water, yielding final concentrations of 1 % w/v KI and 0.2 ( % w/v) Ascorbic Acid, Sodium borohydride (0.6 % w/v NaBH₄) and Hydrochloric acid (10 % v/v HCl) were used as the reductant and acid, respectively. The addition of KI ensures the reduction of As(V) to As(III), as only As(III) can form a stable hydride suitable for detection in HG-AAS [16].

Analysis of extractable As(III) and As(V)

The same procedure used for the analysis of Total Inorganic Arsenic was followed, with the exception that the reduction step using potassium iodide (KI) was omitted to allow selective detection of As(III). After extracting the sample with a 30:70 % v/v methanol–water solution, the solvent was removed using a rotary evaporator. The remaining residue in the round bottom flask was weighed and dissolved in 10 mL of 1 mol L⁻¹ nitric acid, then transferred to a 50 mL volumetric flask and diluted to volume with deionized water. Aliquots of 5 mL of the digest were transferred into 25 mL volumetric flasks then diluted to 25 mL with deionized water and analyzed for Arsenic without the KI reduction step. The Soxhlet extraction was performed using a methanol–water (30:70 v/v) solvent system under reflux conditions. Methanol has a boiling point of 64.7 °C and water 100 °C; therefore, the extraction temperature was governed by the boiling behavior of the solvent mixture and remained below 100 °C throughout the extraction. The estimated temperature of reflux is 78 °C based on the standard methanol/water boiling point curve at atmospheric pressure. Although elevated temperatures may promote arsenic species interconversion, the use of a mixed organic–aqueous solvent under reflux, combined with limited oxygen exposure, is expected to minimize extensive oxidation of As(III) to As(V). Literature reports that Arsenic species interconversion typically starts at 120 °C [17]. This approach enables only the As(III) species, which are already present in the solution, to form a stable hydride for detection, while As(V), which does not form a hydride under these conditions, remains undetected. Analysis of non-extractable arsenic oAs

During the extraction process with methanol and water, the residue retained in the cellulose thimble is free of extractable Arsenic (primarily inorganic Arsenic and loosely bound organic Arsenic species) and contains only non-extractable Arsenic (primarily organic Arsenic and tightly bound inorganic Arsenic species). This is because the extraction procedure effectively removes the mobile species As(III) and As(V). Analysis of this residue using the Total Arsenic analysis method, as described earlier, will yield only the concentration of non-extractable Arsenic.

Instrumentation

Analysis of Total Arsenic was performed using the Varian Atomic Absorption Spectrophotometer (SpectrAA880), equipped with the VGA77 hydride generation accessory and the quartz flow cell. Arsenic high-intensity UltraAA coded single element hollow cathode lamp with Agilent Ultra-AA boosted lamp supply were also used [16].

Quality assurance

To ensure that the method was free from contamination or interferences, triplicate extraction blanks and digestion blanks were included in the analysis for every batch of samples. These blanks underwent the entire extraction and digestion process, and their values were used to correct for any background contamination or procedural errors. The blank values were subtracted from the corresponding sample values to obtain accurate concentrations of Arsenic species.

Precision was assessed by calculating the Relative Standard Deviation (RSD) or Standard Deviation of the Mean (SDM) [18]. A low RSD indicates high precision and reproducibility of the method and is an important parameter for determining the reliability of the results.

In this study, an RSD value of ≤5 % was considered acceptable for the measurement of Arsenic species [18]. This threshold ensures that the method provides consistent and repeatable results, which is essential for accurate quantification.

To measure the quality of the correlation between absorbance and concentration values in the calibration curves used, the correlation coefficient (R2) was utilized. A R2 value ranging from 0.9950 to 0.9999 was deemed acceptable and a good fit for purpose [19] . A calibration curve was generated from serial dilutions of the 1000 µg kg⁻¹ stock Arsenic atomic absorption standard. Working standard concentrations were 1,5 10, 30, and 50 µg L⁻¹ and were matrix matched to ensure consistency. Calibration curves having R2 values external to this range were rejected. Preparation of arsenic-spiked samples

A homogenized sample of 500 g of organic chicken breast was prepared using a laboratory food processor, then analyzed for total Arsenic (tAs), to confirm it was Arsenic free. A 10 µg mL⁻¹ intermediate stock solution of inorganic Arsenic, prepared from a 1000 µg mL⁻¹ certified standard, was used as the spiking solution. To spike the sample, 2.5 mL of the 10 µg mL⁻¹ stock solution was added to the 500 g homogenized chicken and thoroughly mixed to ensure complete incorporation. This increased the inorganic Arsenic concentration by 30 µg kg⁻¹ (ppb), resulting in a total inorganic Arsenic concentration of 50 µg kg⁻¹.

Selection of extraction solution

The selection of an appropriate extraction solvent was critical to ensure maximum recovery of both Organic and Inorganic Arsenic species while maintaining their chemical stability during solvent extraction. A Methanol–water extraction solution of various ratios has been used successfully to extract Inorganic Arsenic from various sample matrices using microwave-assisted extraction, ultrasonic extraction or a combination of both. However, solvent selection using solvent extraction is scarcely covered in literature when it comes to Arsenic species. The Methanol–water extraction solvent appears to be the most popular solution used, and selecting the optimal methanol–water ratio is a crucial step.

Optimization involved testing various methanol–water ratios, specifically, 50:50 ( %v/v), 30:70 ( %v/v), 100 % methanol and 100( %v/v) water, on a homogenized spiked meat bulk sample for a period of 8 hrs. Although all mixtures containing methanol yielded high recoveries, the 30:70 methanol–water mixture provided slightly better efficiency and consistency. In contrast, 100 % water resulted in significantly lower recovery, indicating poor solubilization of Inorganic Arsenic species. Extraction rationale and matrix considerations

The choice of solvent was guided by the need to recover both Inorganic Arsenic which is hydride- active and residue- associated organic bound Arsenic species from protein and lipid-rich tissue. This study shows 100 % methanol yielded lower recoveries than hydro-organic mixtures, which is consistent with prior work showing that mixed MeOH–H₂O systems promote desorption/solubilization of zwitterionic organoArsenicals (e.g., AsB) while maintaining adequate recovery of inorganic species. Chicken meat contains a mix of Arsenic species, including Inorganic (As(III), As(V)) and Organic (e.g., Dimethylarsinic Acid [DMA], Arsenobetaine [AsB]) forms, especially if derived from feed additives like Roxarsone [21].

Pure Methanol is highly effective for extracting less polar organic bound species like DMA and AsB due to its organic solvent properties [7] . However, inorganic Arsenic species, which are highly polar, are more soluble in water than in methanol. The 30:70 blend likely provides an optimal polarity balance, enhancing matrix penetration and species solubility, resulting in the highest recovery. Chicken meat is a protein- and lipid-rich matrix, where Arsenic species can bind to proteins or be trapped in hydrophobic regions. Methanol-water mixtures (e.g., 30:70 or 50:50) balance the ability to disrupt protein interactions (via methanol) and solubilize polar species (via water), optimizing extraction [6]. Pure methanol is less effective for polar species and can destabilize some Arsenic forms, leading to lower recoveries in complex matrices [9].Pure methanol may denature proteins excessively, potentially trapping some Arsenic species in precipitated protein aggregates, reducing their release into the solvent [9]. This is supported by studies showing that high methanol concentrations can cause matrix interference in biological tissues.

By contrast, water-only sequential extractions can be effective for highly water-soluble species and in some fish matrices, but they may under-recover organo-Arsenicals or require multiple passes to reach comparable totals, and often times low water- only extraction efficiencies are related to high inorganic Arsenic content [8]. Recent reviews and comparative studies report highest or near-highest efficiencies with MeOH–H₂O (1:1 v/v) or with sequential extractions, whereas single-solvent protocols (water or MeOH alone) are often sub-optimal for multi-species recovery [22]. Our final selection, MeOH–H₂O (30:70 % v/v) follows this principle and aligns with established tissue methods that used MeOH–H₂O plus physical assistance to achieve ∼80–100 % recovery across species. In this study, the single-step 30:70 mixture was selected for its balance of efficiency and simplicity. It was found that repeated water extractions (three steps) achieved recoveries of 95–98 % for extractable Arsenic species including (As(III) and As(V)), as water effectively solubilizes polar species. However, organic bound inorganic species show lower recoveries with water alone compared to Methanol–water mixtures [14].

While the solvent system preferentially extracts hydride-active arsenic species, certain polar organic arsenic compounds may be partially solubilized under certain conditions. Hydride-active organic species such as monomethylarsonic acid (MMA) and dimethylarsinic acid (DMA) may contribute to the oAs fraction. However, many of these species, such as arsenobetaine, are hydride-inactive and therefore remain undetected by HG-AAS without prior digestion. Consequently, organic arsenic quantified from the solid residue represents a conservative estimate of residue-associated arsenic rather than an absolute measure of total organic arsenic present in the sample.

The comparison of solvent mixtures in supplementary Table 9 shows that all methanol-containing blends (30:70, 50:50, and 100:0) produced significantly higher recoveries than pure water (0:100), with large differences of 19–24 % (t >> t₍crit₎). Among the methanol mixtures, only the comparison between 100:0 and 30:70 was statistically significant, with 30:70 giving higher recovery. Differences between 100:0 vs 50:50 and 50:50 vs 30:70 were not significant. Overall, pure water performed poorly, while moderate methanol levels (30–50 %) provided the most consistent and effective extraction.

ANOVA

The one-way ANOVA in supplementary Table 8 revealed a highly significant effect of solvent composition on Arsenic recovery (F(3, 8) = 268.3, p < 0.0001). This confirms that varying the methanol-to-water ratio exerts a measurable and substantial influence on extraction efficiency.

Tukey’s HSD

Post-hoc analysis (Supplementary Table 10) showed that extraction with pure water (0:100) yielded significantly lower recoveries (47.0 ± 1.24 %) than all methanol-containing systems (p < 0.001). No significant differences were observed among the 100:0, 50:50, and 30:70 methanol–water blends, although the 30:70 v/v mixture produced the highest mean recovery.

These results indicate that the presence of methanol is critical for efficient extraction, whereas water alone is inadequate. The methanol–water (30:70 % v/v) system was therefore selected, as it provided high recovery while maintaining statistical equivalence with the 50:50 system and using less organic solvent, reducing cost and environmental impact.

Variance

Nearly all the observed variability was attributable to differences between solvent groups, with minimal error within replicates. This suggests that the method is highly reproducible, and that solvent composition is the dominant factor influencing recovery.

Discussion summary

Overall, the results confirm that solvent composition plays a crucial role in extraction efficiency. The inclusion of methanol significantly enhanced recovery, with pure water performing poorly. While the 30:70 methanol: water mixture produced the highest numerical recovery, its performance was not statistically superior to the other methanol-containing blends. This suggests that methanol is essential for disrupting the meat matrix and improving Arsenic extraction, and that 30:70 may represent an optimal balance of polarity, though any methanol content markedly outperforms water alone.

Independent sample t-tests confirmed that pure water (0:100) gave significantly lower recoveries than all methanol-containing blends (p < 0.05). However, there were no significant differences between 100:0, 50:50, and 30:70 mixtures. This finding complemented the ANOVA results, where the significant overall effect was attributable to the poor performance of water relative to methanol solvents, rather than differences among methanol ratios.

Optimization of extraction times

The extraction of inorganic Arsenic from the sample matrix was performed using the Soxhlet extraction method. Initially, 10 g of homogenized sample was placed into a cellulose thimble and extracted using a Methanol–water (30:70 % v/v) solvent blend for various time points: 2, 4, 6, 8, and 18 h. The extraction was conducted in triplicate to ensure reproducibility. After extraction, the Arsenic concentration was measured for each sample to determine the maximum concentration achieved, which allowed for identification of the optimal extraction time. Spiked recoveries were first conducted to optimize the extraction parameters, including the solvent blend and extraction time.

A known amount of inorganic Arsenic standard was added to the sample, aiming for a final concentration of (30 µg L⁻¹, by spiking the samples with 0.75 mL of a 1 µg mL⁻¹ inorganic Arsenic stock solution in a 25 mL final sample volume of the extract.

The samples were extracted at the same time points (2, 4, 6, 8, and 18 h) and analyzed to evaluate the efficiency and accuracy of the extraction process. The recoveries were calculated by comparing the Arsenic concentrations from the spiked samples with the measured concentrations of extractable Arsenic in the unspiked samples.

Method validation was then performed using the DORM-5 fish protein certified reference material (CRM), which contains a known Arsenic concentration of 0.0160 ± 0.0010 mg kg⁻¹. This allowed for the optimization of the extraction parameters and validated the method's ability to recover the added Arsenic efficiently and accurately.

The data in supplementary Table 11 shows that there was no significant difference (p < 0.05) in extraction efficiencies with the 30 %, 50 % and 100 % methanol blends. However, all were significantly different (p < 0.05) from the 100 % water extraction. Thus, 30:70 methanol–water was selected not only because it provided the highest mean recovery (96.0 %), but also because it maintained statistical equivalence to the 50:50 system while using less methanol, reducing both cost and environmental impact.

These results confirm that all methanol-containing systems perform well except for the 100 % water. The 30:70 methanol–water ratio is optimal for extracting a broad range of Arsenic species from complex meat matrices, providing high recovery while avoiding excessive use of organic solvents, making this a more cost-effective option. This solvent system also supports the preservation of Arsenic species during the extraction process, which is crucial for accurate speciation analysis using HGAAS.

In supplementary Table 13, independent t-tests comparing shorter extraction times with the 18-hour extraction confirmed that the 2-hour and 4-hour extractions were significantly less effective (p < 0.05). In contrast, no significant differences were observed among the 6-hour, 8-hour, and 18-hour extractions, indicating that maximum recovery is achieved within 6 h. These findings are consistent with the ANOVA and Tukey post-hoc results, both of which demonstrate that extending the extraction beyond 6 h provides no additional benefit. The 18-hour extraction yielded the highest recovery (97.0 ± 3.43 %), followed closely by the 8-hour (96.5 ± 2.34 %) and 6-hour (96.0 ± 2.45 %) extractions (Table 3). However, as shown in Table 9, there were no statistically significant differences between the 6-hour and 18-hour or the 8-hour and 18-hour recoveries. The 6-hour extraction was therefore deemed optimal, offering high efficiency while minimizing time and solvent consumption.

Analysis of arsenic (III) and Arsenic (V)

As(III) spikes were prepared from the certified As(III) stock and As(V) spikes were prepared by quantitative oxidation of the As(III) stock using Potassium permanganate. To evaluate recovery through spiking, both As(III) and As(V) standards were required. The As(III) standard was used directly from a 1000 µg mL⁻¹ atomic absorption spectroscopy (AAS) stock solution, which consists entirely of As(III). The As(V) standard was prepared by oxidizing the As(III) stock solution using an appropriate oxidizing agent to ensure complete conversion to As(V).

A 1000 µg mL⁻¹ Arsenic atomic absorption standard (Sigma-Aldrich), containing 100 % As(III), was used as the starting material. To prepare an As(V) standard, a portion of the As(III) stock solution was oxidized using potassium permanganate (KMnO₄). Both As(III) and As(V) stock solutions (1000 µg mL⁻¹) were sequentially diluted to 10 µg mL⁻¹ and then to 1 µg mL⁻¹ working standards. For spiking, 0.75 mL of the 1 µg mL⁻¹ As(III) solution and 0.75 mL of the 1 µg mL⁻¹ As(V) solution were each added to separate 25 mL volumetric flasks containing the extract from the Soxhlet extraction.

In the extraction, 10 g of crushed chicken samples were thoroughly homogenized and 0.3 mL of the 1 µg mL⁻¹ As(III) and 0.3 mL of the 1 µg mL⁻¹ As(V) solutions were added to the samples and thoroughly mixed to incorporate the standard. This produced a final spike concentration of 30 µg kg⁻¹ for each Arsenic species. The samples were placed in the Soxhlet Thimble, and extraction was carried out using a Methanol–water (30:70 % v/v) mixture for 6 h. Un-spiked chicken samples were identically extracted, and all extracts were analyses for the Arsenic species as previously described.

The results in Table 5 show that spike recoveries across all samples were within acceptable limits, indicating reliable recovery of added Arsenic species from the chicken-meat matrix. As(III) recoveries ranged from 93.3 to 105 %, and As(V) recoveries from 93.9 to 105 %. These values fall within commonly accepted ranges for trace-level food/environmental analyses (80–120 % [19]), suggesting minimal matrix interference and good method accuracy. The results also confirm the stability of As(III) throughout the process with no evidence of species interconversion observed. Notably, samples with higher native (unspiked) Arsenic concentrations, such as Fine Choice Breast and Fine Choice Thigh also showed consistent recoveries, supporting the robustness of the method across a range of concentrations. Overall, the spike recovery results confirm that the HG-AAS method, combined with solvent extraction, is suitable for the accurate quantification of As(III), As(V), and organic Arsenic in poultry meat samples.

The species comprising ΣAs include Arsenite (As(III)), Arsenate (As(V)), and organic Arsenic (oAs).

The 30 % methanol extract was analyzed for total Arsenic in four (4) of the extracts by digestion with HNO3. The Arsenic concentrations shown in Table 7, align closely to the sum of As (III) and As (V) from the extracts. This infers that the primary arsenic species in the extract are As (III) and As (V).The presence of other hydride active species in the 30 % methanol extract may also be possible, but can only be confirmed by HPLC-ICP-MS.

The consistent recoveries obtained for both As(III) and As(V) during spike recovery experiments indicate that no significant net interconversion occurred under the applied conditions. The stability of the Arsenic species was maintained during the extraction process by the low temperature of the reflux at 78 °C throughout the process (which is the boiling point of the 30 % methanol/water mixture), an absence of significant oxidizing agents and the neutral pH of the extraction solution. Previous studies have shown that even under low pH (4.5), species interconversion does not occur until temperatures exceed 120 °C [17], which supports the findings of this study. However, in consideration of the solvent extractability, and the possibility that inorganic protein bound species may not be completely extracted, the As(III) and As(V) species analyzed in the 30 % methanol solution may be more accurately described as “Extractable Arsenic”. Conversely, since the residue may contain inorganic protein bound polar species in addition to organic Arsenic species, the Arsenic contained in the residue may more accurately be described as “Non-Extractable Arsenic”. Although analysis of the DORM-5 CRM showed that the residue contained exclusively organic Arsenic, analysis of other sample types containing significant inorganic bound Arsenic may lead to an overestimate of the organic Arsenic fraction.

Overall, these results confirm that solvent extraction followed by selective reduction and HG-AAS is a reliable and robust technique for determining both extractable and total Arsenic in poultry meat. This method offers a practical and cost-effective alternative to more costly instrumentation such as HPLC-ICP-MS, particularly in resource-limited laboratories.

The method shows excellent robustness in Arsenic speciation using Solvent Extraction-HGAAS with selective reduction. The mean recoveries of Arsenic species were consistent across changes in extraction time, solvent ratios, and spiked Arsenic concentrations, all while maintaining low variability as indicated by the standard deviations.

This robustness means the method is well-suited for routine analyses in laboratories, even when faced with small fluctuations in experimental conditions. It also highlights the flexibility of the procedure, making it a reliable choice for determining both Inorganic and Organic Arsenic in complex matrices like chicken meat, without significant loss in accuracy or precision.

Given its stable performance, this method is well-suited for implementation in real-world applications, especially in resource-limited settings where more expensive instruments like HPLC-ICP-MS are not available.

Limitations

A key limitation of this research is the inability of the solvent extraction method to speciate organic Arsenic compounds. Accurate identification and quantification of individual Arsenic species in any matrix would require high-performance liquid chromatography coupled with inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry (HPLC-ICP-MS). In contrast, the current method relies on solvent extraction using a 30:70 methanol–water mixture and quantifies total Arsenic content via hydride generation atomic absorption spectroscopy (HGAAS). Due to the similar solubilities of As(III), As(V), and organic Arsenic species in the methanol–water solvent system, even with pH adjustments a minor degree of cross-species extraction can occur. This is reflected in some results exceeding 100 %, indicating limited carry-over of unintended species.
