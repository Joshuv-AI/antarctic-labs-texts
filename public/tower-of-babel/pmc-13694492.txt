HucMSC-EVs attenuate renal fibrosis via miR-874-3p-mediated inhibition of cGAS–STING signaling
Authors: Zhuocheng Shi, Chenxi Jia, Meiling Chen, Yihang Yu, Feng Liu, Deying Zhang, Guanghui Wei
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Chronic kidney disease (CKD) is characterized by progressive renal interstitial fibrosis, which is closely associated with tubular epithelial cell injury, mitochondrial dysfunction, and aberrant inflammatory signaling. Our previous studies demonstrated that extracellular vesicles derived from human umbilical cord mesenchymal stem cells (HucMSC-EVs) effectively alleviate renal fibrosis in a unilateral ureteral obstruction (UUO) model. Based on these findings, the present study further investigated the molecular mechanisms underlying the anti-fibrotic effects of HucMSC-EVs. Using a cisplatin-induced HK-2 cell injury model, we evaluated the effects of HucMSC-EVs on epithelial–mesenchymal transition (EMT) and fibrosis. The results showed that HucMSC-EVs attenuated EMT and fibrotic phenotypes in vitro. Consistently, in UUO-induced kidneys, HucMSC-EVs improved renal histopathological injury and alleviated fibrosis. Mechanistically, HucMSC-EVs reduced mitochondrial reactive oxygen species (mtROS) levels and inhibited abnormal opening of the mitochondrial permeability transition pore (mPTP), thereby preserving mitochondrial homeostasis and suppressing activation of the cGAS-STING signaling pathway. The involvement of this pathway was further confirmed using the STING inhibitor C-176. In addition, we validated that miR-874-3p contributes to these protective effects, as its overexpression partially recapitulated the function of HucMSC-EVs. Collectively, these findings suggest that HucMSC-EVs attenuate renal fibrosis by modulating mitochondrial function and inhibiting the cGAS–STING pathway, providing mechanistic insight into EV-mediated protection in kidney injury.Graphical Abstract1. Introduction

Chronic kidney disease (CKD) is a progressive disorder characterized by a gradual decline in renal function, with a global prevalence of approximately 10%, posing a serious threat to human healt (Romagnani et al. 2017). Regardless of the initiating factors, persistent renal injury triggers a cascade of pathological events, including inflammation, oxidative stress, and metabolic disturbances, ultimately leading to structural remodeling of the kidney (Zhang et al. 2024). Renal interstitial fibrosis, characterized by excessive deposition of extracellular matrix (ECM), represents the common final pathway of CKD progression toward end-stage renal disease (ESRD) (Li et al. 2022; Livingston et al. 2023). Injury to tubular epithelial cells (TECs) is recognized as a key initiating event in fibrosis, promoting disease progression through induction of inflammation, fibroblast activation, and epithelial–mesenchymal transition (EMT) (Li et al. 2025).

In recent years, mitochondrial dysfunction has emerged as a central hub linking metabolic stress, oxidative damage, and cell death, playing a critical role in the development of renal fibrosis (Doke and Susztak 2022; Hoogstraten et al. 2024). During mitochondrial injury, abnormal opening of the mitochondrial permeability transition pore (mPTP) leads to loss of mitochondrial membrane potential, excessive production of mitochondrial reactive oxygen species (mtROS), and release of mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) into the cytoplas (Zhao et al. 2021; Su et al. 2023). As an endogenous double-stranded DNA, mtDNA can be recognized by the cytosolic DNA sensor cyclic GMP–AMP synthase (cGAS), thereby activating the stimulator of interferon genes (STING) signaling pathway and inducing the expression of inflammation and fibrosis-related genes (Maekawa et al. 2019; Skopelja-Gardner et al. 2022). Increasing evidence indicates that sustained activation of the cGAS–STING pathway plays a pivotal role in renal injury and fibrosis (Chung et al. 2019; Decout et al. 2021; Huang et al. 2025).

Human umbilical cord–derived mesenchymal stem cells (HucMSCs) possess potent immunomodulatory and regenerative capacities. As key mediators of their paracrine effects, HucMSC-derived extracellular vesicles (EVs) carry a variety of functional molecules, including microRNAs (miRNAs) and proteins, and exert protective effects in multiple models of tissue injur (Liang et al. 2023; Huang et al. 2025). Our previous studies demonstrated that HucMSC-EVs alleviate cisplatin-induced programmed cell death in TECs by preserving mitochondrial function (Yu et al. 2023). However, whether HucMSC-EVs exert their protective effects through modulation of mitochondrial dysfunction-associated cGAS–STING signaling remains unclear.

MicroRNAs, as critical functional cargos of EVs, play essential roles in regulating cell fate and disease progression. Our previous work identified miR-874-3p as an abundant component of HucMSC-EVs, which targets receptor-interacting protein kinase 1 (RIPK1), thereby restoring mitochondrial homeostasis and attenuating cisplatin-induced TEC injury (Yu et al. 2023). Nevertheless, whether miR-874-3p regulates renal fibrosis by modulating mitochondrial function and subsequently influencing the cGAS–STING signaling pathway remains to be elucidated.

Based on these observations, the present study aimed to investigate the protective effects of HucMSC-EVs against renal fibrosis and to elucidate the underlying molecular mechanisms, with a particular focus on miR-874-3p–mediated regulation of mitochondrial function and the cGAS–STING signaling pathway. Using both an in vivo UUO mouse model and an in vitro cisplatin-induced HK-2 cell injury model, we systematically demonstrate that HucMSC-EVs suppress cGAS–STING pathway activation by maintaining mitochondrial homeostasis, thereby attenuating EMT and renal fibrosis.

Materials and methods

Animal ethics and experimental procedures

Male C57BL/6 J mice (8–10 weeks old, 20–25 g) were purchased from Chongqing Medical University and maintained under specific pathogen-free (SPF) conditions with a 12-h light/dark cycle, standard diet, and free access to water. All experimental procedures were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Chongqing Medical University (Approval No. CHCMU-IACUC20250429004). Mice were anesthetized with 2% isoflurane and subjected to unilateral ureteral obstruction (UUO) by ligating the left ureter at two points using 5–0 silk sutures. Sham-operated mice underwent the same procedure without ureteral ligation. Mice were randomly divided into three groups (n = 6 per group): Sham, UUO, and UUO + HucMSC-EVs (100 μg/mouse, diluted in PBS) were administered via tail vein injection on days 1 and 3 post-surgery. Mice were sacrificed on day 7 post-operation. Kidney tissues were collected for histological, molecular, and protein analyses. Histological evaluations were performed in a blinded manner.

Isolation and characterization of hucMSC-EVs

HucMSC-derived extracellular vesicles (HucMSC-EVs) were isolated from conditioned medium by differential ultracentrifugation. Briefly, HucMSCs (Chongqing Stem Cell Biotechnology R&D Base, Chongqing, China) were maintained in exosome-depleted culture medium, and the collected supernatant was sequentially centrifuged to eliminate cells, apoptotic bodies, and larger vesicles before ultracentrifugation at 100,000 × g. The resulting EV pellet was washed once with PBS and subjected to a second ultracentrifugation step to improve sample purity.

The isolated vesicles were characterized according to the MISEV guidelines using transmission electron microscopy (TEM), nanoparticle tracking analysis (NTA), and Western blotting. Typical EV markers, including CD63, Alix, and TSG101, were detected, whereas the endoplasmic reticulum protein Calnexin was absent, confirming the purity and identity of the isolated HucMSC-EVs.

Cell culture and treatments

Human renal proximal tubular epithelial cells (HK-2) were cultured in DMEM/F12 supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere with 5% CO₂. To induce fibrotic injury, cells were treated with cisplatin (5 μg/mL) for 24 h. The medium was then replaced with fresh complete medium, and cells were cultured for an additional 24, 48, or 72 h to mimic the repair-associated fibrotic process. For intervention experiments, HucMSC-EVs (100 μg/mL) were added to the culture medium and pre-incubated with HK-2 cells for 12 h prior to cisplatin exposure. For pharmacological inhibition, cells were pretreated with the STING inhibitor C-176 (2 μM, MedChemExpress, USA; dissolved in DMSO) for 2 h before cisplatin stimulation.

EV uptake assay

HucMSC-EVs were labeled with PKH26 fluorescent dye (Sigma-Aldrich) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Excess dye was removed by ultracentrifugation. Labeled EVs (100 μg/mL) were incubated with HK-2 cells for 4, 8, and 12 h. Cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde, counterstained with DAPI, and visualized under a confocal microscope. A dye-only control group was included to exclude nonspecific fluorescence.

Histological staining (H&E, PAS, and masson's trichrome)

Paraffin-embedded kidney tissues were cut into 4-μm sections, followed by routine deparaffinization and rehydration. Histological staining was performed using standard protocols. Briefly, H&E staining was used to evaluate renal morphology, PAS staining was applied to assess tubular injury and basement membrane alterations, and Masson's trichrome staining was performed to visualize collagen deposition and interstitial fibrosis. After staining, sections were dehydrated, cleared, and mounted with neutral resin. Representative images were captured using a light microscope under identical imaging parameters for all groups.

Immunohistochemistry (IHC)

Kidney sections were routinely deparaffinized, rehydrated, and subjected to heat-induced antigen retrieval using citrate buffer (pH 6.0). After blocking endogenous peroxidase activity and nonspecific binding, the sections were incubated overnight at 4 °C with the indicated primary antibodies (1:200), followed by HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature. Immunoreactive signals were developed using DAB substrate and counterstained with hematoxylin before microscopic examination.

Tissue immunofluorescence

Paraffin-embedded kidney sections were deparaffinized, rehydrated, and subjected to antigen retrieval as described above. After blocking nonspecific binding, the sections were incubated overnight at 4 °C with the indicated primary antibodies (1:200), followed by the appropriate fluorophore-conjugated secondary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature in the dark. Cell nuclei were counterstained with DAPI, and fluorescent images were obtained using a confocal laser scanning microscope under identical acquisition settings.

Cell Immunofluorescence

HK-2 cells cultured on coverslips were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min and permeabilized with 0.2% Triton X-100 for 10 min. After blocking with 5% BSA for 30 min, cells were incubated with primary antibodies (1:200) at 4 °C overnight. After washing, fluorophore-conjugated secondary antibodies were applied for 1 h at room temperature in the dark. Nuclei were stained with DAPI. Coverslips were mounted with anti-fade mounting medium, and images were acquired using a fluorescence or confocal microscope.

Transfection of miR-874-3p mimics and inhibitor

HK-2 cells were transfected with miR-874-3p mimics or the corresponding negative control (NC) (Tsingke Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Beijing, China) using Lipofectamine 3000 (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA) when cell confluence reached approximately 60%–70%. Transfection was performed in Opti-MEM following the manufacturer's recommendations. After 6 h, the transfection medium was replaced with complete medium, and cells were harvested 48 h later for downstream analyses.

For in vivo loss-of-function experiments, HucMSCs were transfected with a miR-874-3p inhibitor or inhibitor negative control (Tsingke Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Beijing, China) using the same transfection protocol. Forty-eight hours after transfection, the culture medium was replaced with exosome-depleted medium, and cells were cultured for an additional 48 h before extracellular vesicles were isolated by differential ultracentrifugation as described above. EVs derived from miR-874-3p inhibitor-transfected HucMSCs (EVs-inhibitor) were subsequently collected and administered intravenously to UUO mice for functional validation.

The sequences were as follows: miR-874-3p mimics (sense: 5′-CUGCCCUGGCCC GAGGGACCGA-3′, antisense: 5′-UCGGUCCCUCGGGCCAGGGCAG-3′); negative control (sense: 5′-UUUGUACUACACAAAAGUACUG-3′, antisense: 5′-CAGUACUU UUGUGUAGUACAAA-3′).

Quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR)

Total RNA was isolated from cells using TRIzol reagent according to the manufacturer's instructions. RNA quality and concentration were determined spectrophotometrically before reverse transcription using a miRNA-specific cDNA synthesis kit. Quantitative real-time PCR was subsequently performed using SYBR Green Master Mix on a real-time PCR detection system. Relative miRNA expression was calculated using the 2^ − ΔΔCt method with U6 serving as the endogenous reference. The primer sequences were as follows: miR-874-3p forward, 5′-TATAACTGCCCTGGCCCGAG-3′; U6 forward, 5′-CTCGCTTCGGCAGCACATA-3′. GAPDH was used as the internal control for mRNA expression, The primer sequences for Collagen I, α-SMA and GAPDH are listed in Supplementary Table S1.

Western blotting

Whole-cell or tissue lysates were prepared in RIPA buffer containing protease and phosphatase inhibitors. Following protein quantification by the BCA method, equivalent amounts of protein (20–40 μg) were electrophoretically separated by SDS–PAGE and electrotransferred to PVDF membranes. Membranes were subsequently incubated with the indicated primary antibodies (1:1000; Supplementary Table S2) and the HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies. Protein signals were visualized using ECL reagents and analyzed with Image Lab software, with GAPDH serving as the internal loading control.

Measurement of mtROS

Mitochondrial ROS levels were assessed using the MitoSOX™ Red probe. HK-2 cells were incubated with 5 μM MitoSOX reagent in HBSS at 37 °C for 10 min in the dark. After washing three times with HBSS, nuclei were stained with Hoechst for 10 min. Fluorescence images were captured using a fluorescence microscope.

Measurement of mPTP opening

mPTP opening was evaluated using the calcein-AM/CoCl₂ fluorescence quenching assay. HK-2 cells were incubated with staining solution containing calcein-AM and CoCl₂ at 37 °C for 45 min in the dark. After incubation, cells were washed and maintained in fresh medium for an additional 30 min. Nuclei were stained with Hoechst, and fluorescence images were acquired using a fluorescence microscope. The mitochondrial calcein fluorescence intensity was quantified to reflect mPTP opening.

Renal organoid culture

Fresh kidney tissues were washed with sterile PBS, minced into small fragments, and enzymatically digested with collagenase II (1 mg/mL, Sigma-Aldrich) at 37 °C for 30 min. The resulting cell suspension was filtered through a 70-μm cell strainer, centrifuged, resuspended in renal organoid culture medium, and embedded in growth factor-reduced Matrigel (Corning). Organoids were cultured in renal organoid medium supplemented with B27, N2, EGF, FGF10, Noggin, and R-spondin1 at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO₂. The culture medium was replaced every 2–3 days, and organoids were maintained for 7–14 days before subsequent experiments.

For in vivo biodistribution analysis, HucMSC-EVs were labeled with DiR (Beyotime Biotechnology, Shanghai, China) according to the manufacturer's instructions and intravenously injected into UUO mice. Fluorescence signals were detected using an in vivo imaging system (IVIS). Kidneys were subsequently harvested, enzymatically dissociated, and re-established as renal organoids. Uptake of DiR-labeled HucMSC-EVs was evaluated by immunofluorescence staining using Lotus tetragonolobus lectin (LTL) to identify renal tubular epithelial cells.

To evaluate mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) release, renal organoids were subjected to immunofluorescence staining using an anti-TOM20 antibody to label mitochondria together with the DNA fluorescent dye used in this study. Images were acquired using a confocal laser scanning microscope. Colocalization of DNA signals with TOM20 was considered indicative of mitochondrial localization, whereas reduced colocalization was interpreted as mtDNA release from mitochondria into the cytoplasm.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were carried out using GraphPad Prism 9.0 software (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA). Data are expressed as the mean ± SD from at least three independent experiments. Differences between two groups were analyzed using an unpaired Student's t-test, whereas comparisons among multiple groups were performed by one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey's multiple-comparison test. Statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05.

In contrast, the endoplasmic reticulum marker Calnexin was detected only in MSCs but was barely detectable in EVs, indicating the high purity of the isolated EVs (Fig. 1B). To investigate the biodistribution of HucMSC-EVs in vivo, EVs were labeled with the near-infrared fluorescent dye DiR and intravenously injected into UUO mice. In vivo imaging demonstrated that DiR-labeled HucMSC-EVs accumulated predominantly in the liver and kidneys, with significantly stronger fluorescence detected in the obstructed kidney than in the contralateral kidney (Fig. 1E), indicating preferential accumulation of HucMSC-EVs in the injured kidney.

To further determine the renal cell types that internalized HucMSC-EVs following systemic administration, kidneys were harvested after EV injection, enzymatically dissociated, and cultured to generate renal organoids. Fluorescence imaging demonstrated that DiR-labeled HucMSC-EVs were predominantly localized within the cytoplasm of Lotus tetragonolobus lectin (LTL)-positive renal tubular epithelial cells, indicating that HucMSC-EVs were preferentially taken up by tubular epithelial cells in the injured kidney (Fig. 1F).

In addition, to verify cellular uptake in vitro, HucMSC-EVs were labeled with PKH26 and co-cultured with HK-2 cells for 12 h. Fluorescence microscopy showed that Dir-labeled EVs were efficiently internalized and mainly localized in the cytoplasm of HK-2 cells (Fig. 1D).

Collectively, these results demonstrate that HucMSC-EVs possess the typical physicochemical characteristics of extracellular vesicles, preferentially accumulate in the injured kidney following intravenous administration, are predominantly internalized by renal tubular epithelial cells, providing a foundation for their subsequent renoprotective effects.

To further evaluate the regulatory effects of HucMSC-EVs on cisplatin-induced fibrosis and epithelial–mesenchymal transition (EMT), immunofluorescence staining was performed. Compared with the control group, cisplatin treatment significantly upregulated the mesenchymal markers Vimentin and N-cadherin, while downregulating the epithelial marker E-cadherin. HucMSC-EVs alleviate mitochondrial dysfunction and inhibit the cGAS-STING axis in cisplatin-induced tubular injury

Previous studies have demonstrated that cisplatin accumulates in mitochondria, leading to impairment of the electron transport chain and disruption of mitochondrial structure and functio(Holditch et al. 2019; Doke et al. 2023). To further elucidate the molecular mechanisms underlying cisplatin-induced epithelial–mesenchymal transition (EMT) and fibrosis in renal tubular epithelial cells, mitochondrial reactive oxygen species (mtROS) levels in HK-2 cells were assessed by measuring MitoSOX fluorescence intensity. The results showed that, compared with the control group, cisplatin treatment markedly increased punctate red fluorescence signals in the cytoplasm, indicating elevated mtROS levels. In contrast, HucMSC-EVs treatment significantly attenuated this increase, suggesting a partial alleviation of mtROS accumulation (Fig. 4A). Next, mitochondrial permeability transition pore (mPTP) opening was evaluated using the calcein-AM/CoCl₂ fluorescence quenching assay. Cisplatin treatment significantly reduced mitochondrial calcein fluorescence intensity in HK-2 cells, indicating increased mPTP opening. To further determine whether mitochondrial injury resulted in the release of mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA), renal organoids were first stained with Lotus tetragonolobus lectin (LTL) to identify renal tubular epithelial cells (Fig. 4C). Immunofluorescence co-staining of TOM20 and dsDNA demonstrated that, in the control group, DNA signals were predominantly colocalized with the mitochondrial marker TOM20. In contrast, cisplatin treatment markedly reduced TOM20–dsDNA colocalization, indicating the release of mtDNA from mitochondria into the cytoplasm. Notably, HucMSC-EVs treatment largely restored TOM20–dsDNA colocalization, suggesting that HucMSC-EVs effectively prevented mtDNA release by preserving mitochondrial integrity (Fig. 4D).

Previous studies have shown that mPTP opening can lead to the release of mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) into the cytoplasm, where it acts as an endogenous double-stranded DNA ligand recognized by the cytosolic DNA sensor cGAS, thereby activating the STING signaling pathway and inducing downstream inflammatory and fibrotic gene expression(Yu et al. 2020; Ouyang et al. 2023). Based on this mechanism, we hypothesized that cisplatin may promote renal tubular epithelial cell fibrosis by inducing mitochondrial damage and activating the cGAS–STING pathway. To verify this hypothesis, the expression levels of cGAS/STING pathway-related proteins were assessed by Western blot analysis. The results showed that cisplatin treatment significantly upregulated the expression of cGAS and STING, whereas HucMSC-EVs treatment markedly suppressed this upregulation (Fig. 4E-G). Immunofluorescence analysis revealed a consistent trend (Fig. 4H).

To further clarify the role of STING in cisplatin-induced EMT in HK-2 cells, cells were pretreated with the STING-specific small-molecule inhibitor C-176 (2 μM) for 2 h prior to cisplatin exposure, ensuring effective inhibition of STING signaling without affecting cell viability(Xiao et al. 2023). The results demonstrated that C-176 intervention significantly reduced the expression of EMT markers, including Vimentin and α-SMA, induced by cisplatin (Fig. 4I-J).

Collectively, these findings indicate that cisplatin induces mitochondrial dysfunction, characterized by increased mtROS production and mPTP opening, and mtDNA release, leading to activation of the cGAS–STING signaling pathway and promotion of EMT and fibrosis. In contrast, HucMSC-EVs exert anti-fibrotic effects by maintaining mitochondrial homeostasis and suppressing the activation of this pathway.

HucMSC-EVs suppress cGAS–STING pathway activation in UUO kidneys

Our previous study demonstrated that the UUO model induces significant mitochondrial structural and functional damage in renal tubular epithelial cells, which can be effectively ameliorated by HucMSC-EVs treatment(Yu et al. 2023). Based on this, we further investigated whether HucMSC-EVs regulate downstream signaling pathways associated with mitochondrial dysfunction, particularly the cytosolic DNA-sensing cGAS–STING pathway.

Discussion

Renal interstitial fibrosis represents the final common pathological outcome of chronic kidney disease (CKD), characterized by persistent tubular epithelial cell injury, aberrant fibroblast activation, and excessive extracellular matrix (ECM) deposition(Li et al. 2023; Peng et al. 2023). Increasing evidence has highlighted that mitochondrial dysfunction and aberrant activation of the innate immune DNA-sensing pathway, particularly the cGAS–STING axis, play critical roles in the progression of renal fibrosis(Bai and Liu 2021). In the present study, using both a unilateral ureteral obstruction (UUO) mouse model and a cisplatin-induced HK-2 cell injury model, we systematically demonstrated that HucMSC-derived extracellular vesicles (HucMSC-EVs) exert anti-fibrotic effects by preserving mitochondrial function and suppressing cGAS–STING signaling.

Consistent with our previous findings, HucMSC-EVs significantly improved renal histopathological damage in UUO mice, reduced collagen deposition, and attenuated epithelial–mesenchymal transition (EMT) and fibrotic phenotypes in vitro. Mechanistically, we found that cisplatin-induced mitochondrial dysfunction was characterized by increased mitochondrial reactive oxygen species (mtROS) production and abnormal opening of the mitochondrial permeability transition pore (mPTP). Notably, HucMSC-EVs effectively reversed these alterations, suggesting a crucial role in maintaining mitochondrial homeostasis.

Importantly, mPTP opening facilitates the release of mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) into the cytoplasm, where it acts as an endogenous ligand for cGAS, thereby activating the STING signaling pathway. This establishes a critical link between mitochondrial injury and innate immune activation. In this study, we demonstrated that HucMSC-EVs markedly suppressed the cisplatin-induced upregulation of cGAS and STING, indicating that preservation of mitochondrial integrity may prevent mtDNA-mediated activation of the cGAS-STING inflammatory signaling cascade.

Furthermore, the use of the STING-specific inhibitor C-176 provided additional evidence supporting the pivotal role of this pathway in renal fibrosis. Pharmacological inhibition of STING significantly reduced the expression of mesenchymal markers such as Vimentin and α-SMA, confirming that activation of the cGAS-STING pathway contributes to EMT and fibrotic progression. These findings support the existence of a “mitochondrial dysfunction-cGAS-STING-fibrosis” signaling axis in renal tubular epithelial cells.

At the mechanistic level, our study further identified miR-874-3p as a key mediator of the protective effects of HucMSC-EVs. Our previous work demonstrated that miR-874-3p targets RIPK1 to regulate mitochondrial homeostasis and alleviate cellular injury(Yu et al. 2023). Given that RIPK1-mediated mitochondrial dysfunction has been implicated in mtDNA release and subsequent activation of the cGAS–STING pathway, our findings further extend this mechanism by linking RIPK1-regulated mitochondrial homeostasis to innate immune signaling. In the present study, we found that overexpression of miR-874-3p effectively mimicked the protective effects of HucMSC-EVs. Specifically, miR-874-3p reduced mtROS levels, inhibited mPTP opening, and suppressed activation of the cGAS–STING pathway, thereby attenuating EMT and fibrotic changes. These findings suggest that miR-874-3p may function as a critical molecular link connecting mitochondrial dysfunction and innate immune activation.

In the present study, HucMSC-derived EVs were selected because they represent one of the most extensively investigated and clinically relevant sources of therapeutic extracellular vesicles. Previous studies have demonstrated that human MSC-derived EVs retain therapeutic efficacy in murine disease models owing to their low immunogenicity and conserved biological cargos (Elahi et al. 2020). Moreover, because the protective mechanism identified in this study is primarily mediated by miR-874-3p, we compared the mature miR-874-3p sequence using the miRBase database and found that it is completely conserved between humans and mice (5′-CUGCCCUGGCCCGAGGGAC CGA-3′) (Kozomara et al. 2019). This high degree of sequence conservation further supports the rationale for using HucMSC-derived EVs in the murine UUO model. Nevertheless, future studies directly comparing human- and mouse-derived EVs would be valuable to further evaluate potential species-specific differences in therapeutic efficacy.

Nevertheless, several limitations should be acknowledged. Although our data demonstrate that miR-874-3p regulates mitochondrial function and the cGAS–STING pathway, the precise molecular mechanisms linking its target gene RIPK1 to this signaling axis remain to be fully elucidated. For instance, whether miR-874-3p regulates mtDNA release through RIPK1-dependent pathways requires further investigation. In addition, although HucMSC-EVs exhibited promising therapeutic efficacy in the present preclinical study, further investigations are required to facilitate clinical translation, including standardized large-scale EV production, quality control, optimization of dosing strategies, and comprehensive long-term safety evaluation.

In conclusion, this study demonstrates that HucMSC-EVs attenuate renal fibrosis by regulating mitochondrial homeostasis and suppressing activation of the cGAS–STING signaling pathway, with miR-874-3p acting as a key mediator. These findings provide new insights into the interplay between mitochondrial dysfunction and innate immune activation in renal fibrosis and suggest potential therapeutic targets for CKD intervention.

Conclusion

Our study demonstrates that HucMSC-EVs attenuate renal fibrosis by targeting mitochondrial dysfunction and inhibiting cGAS–STING–mediated innate immune activation. Mechanistically, HucMSC-EVs reduce mtROS production and suppress mPTP opening, thereby alleviating mitochondrial stress and limiting activation of downstream inflammatory signaling pathways. Importantly, miR-874-3p was identified as a critical mediator that recapitulates the protective effects of EVs by modulating mitochondrial homeostasis and restraining cGAS–STING activation, ultimately mitigating EMT and fibrotic remodeling. These findings uncover a previously unrecognized mitochondria–innate immunity regulatory axis governed by HucMSC-EVs and highlight miR-874-3p as a potential therapeutic target for CKD-associated fibrosis.
