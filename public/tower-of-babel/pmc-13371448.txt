Effect of optimal operator positioning on transcranial Doppler acquisition: a randomized controlled trial
Authors: Théophile Vieux, Tatiana Randriamandimby, Roger Kadji Kalabang, Jérôme Bokobza, Rémi Girerd, Tomislav Petrovic, Frédéric Lapostolle
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundErgonomic conditions during ultrasound procedures may influence both acquisition time and image quality. In transcranial Doppler, positioning at the patient’s head could improve operator comfort and performance. This study aimed to evaluate whether a standardized seated position at the patient’s head enables faster acquisition of interpretable images and could improve image quality.MethodsWe conducted a multicenter randomized controlled trial between August and December 2024 including emergency medicine residents without prior training in transcranial Doppler. Participants were randomized to either a free positioning group or a standardized seated position at the patient’s head. Each participant performed bilateral acquisitions of the middle cerebral artery. Acquisition time was recorded, and image quality was assessed blindly by an expert panel using a semi-quantitative scale.ResultsForty-four residents were included, yielding 88 measurements (46 in the intervention group and 42 in the control group). Baseline characteristics were similar except for a higher number of validated semesters in the intervention group. Mean acquisition time was shorter in the intervention group (99.4 ± 47.1 s) than in the control group (121.7 ± 52.9 s), with a mean difference of 22.3 s (95% CI 1.2–43.3; p = 0.038). Image quality scores did not differ significantly between groups (16.6 ± 5.0 vs. 18.0 ± 5.0; mean difference − 1.4, 95% CI -3.5–0.7; p = 0.20).ConclusionsStandardized operator positioning at the patient’s head was associated with faster acquisition of transcranial Doppler images, without a significant difference in image quality.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13049-026-01619-z.

Background

Over the past few decades, ultrasound has become a key tool in patient management [1]. It allows both morphological assessment through B-mode imaging and functional evaluation using Doppler modalities (color, pulsed, tissue, and M-mode). Advances in portability, battery autonomy, and image quality have fostered the development of point-of-care ultrasound (POCUS) since the 1990s [2]. POCUS is particularly suited to emergency and critical care medicine, where it enables rapid, bedside decision-making while reducing delays associated with conventional diagnostic imaging or laboratory testing [3, 4]. Its clinical utility has been increasingly supported by a growing body of literature validating new indications and demonstrating its impact on patient outcomes [5–7].

Among ultrasound modalities, transcranial Doppler, first introduced in 1982 [8], is validated for several indications, including the assessment of traumatic brain injury and ischemic stroke, and is now considered part of the POCUS skill set [4, 9]. Morphological imaging can reveal intracranial hematomas and midline shift, while functional Doppler analysis provides information on cerebral perfusion and intracranial pressure. Insonation of the middle cerebral artery, typically through the temporal window, relies on parameters such as systolic and diastolic flow velocities and the pulsatility index, derived from their ratio [10]. Despite its diagnostic value, transcranial Doppler remains infrequently used in emergency and prehospital medicine, partly because of several technical limitations. Transcranial Doppler is more difficult to learn than other point-of-care ultrasound modalities, requires greater operator skill to obtain and maintain an adequate acoustic window, and is generally more time-consuming to perform [11–13]. These constraints make operator ergonomics and positioning particularly important, which motivated the present study.

In recent years, ergonomics in ultrasound practice has become an area of growing research interest, with several studies highlighting its impact on operator performance and musculoskeletal health [14]. Ultrasound practitioners are exposed to a high prevalence of work-related musculoskeletal disorders, largely related to repetitive movements, sustained postures, and suboptimal equipment positioning [15]. These constraints may affect not only operator comfort but also technical performance and overall examination efficiency [16]. Such considerations may be particularly relevant for novice operators, for whom optimizing posture and probe handling can facilitate both image acquisition and skill learning. Beyond transcranial Doppler, the principles of ergonomic positioning could likely apply to many ultrasound examinations performed in emergency and critical care settings [17].

However, to our knowledge, no study has specifically examined ergonomic factors in transcranial Doppler acquisition. In this context, a “head position” has been described as potentially advantageous, but it has never been formally validated [18, 19]. We therefore hypothesized that performing transcranial Doppler examinations from an optimized operator setup including a seated position, standardized placement of the ultrasound machine, and consistent alignment with the patient, as described below would allow operators to obtain interpretable images more rapidly and could improve image quality, compared with a freely chosen, unstandardized operator setup.

Methods

Study design and setting

This was a multicenter, parallel-group, superiority randomized controlled trial conducted between August and December 2024 across four French university hospitals. The study compared two operator setup strategies during transcranial Doppler acquisition: a control group allowed to use an unstandardized operator setup, and an intervention group required to adopt a standardized and optimized operator setup that included a seated position, predefined placement of the ultrasound machine, and a standardized orientation with the operator seated at the patient’s head and facing the model’s longitudinal axis.

The trial was implemented during emergency ultrasound training courses routinely delivered in each participating center. These courses included an introductory session on the principles of transcranial Doppler, followed by hands-on practice sessions under supervision. All acquisitions were performed under standardized conditions, following a unified acquisition protocol across centers to ensure procedural consistency.

No substantial methodological changes occurred after the start of the trial; eligibility criteria, randomization methods, and outcome definitions remained consistent throughout the study.

Participants

Participants were emergency medicine residents enrolled in postgraduate ultrasound training courses held at four French university hospitals: Avicenne Hospital (Bobigny), Cochin Hospital (Paris), Melun Hospital, and the University Hospital of La Réunion. These centers regularly host educational sessions in point-of-care ultrasound, including transcranial Doppler training. The study was conducted during scheduled training sessions dedicated to hands-on transcranial Doppler acquisition. During these sessions, residents usually practice on one another as volunteer models under instructor supervision. In the present study, this same framework was maintained: participants alternated between the role of operator and model, ensuring realistic training conditions consistent with routine educational practice. Eligible participants were residents with no prior formal training or experience in transcranial Doppler. Participation was voluntary, and all residents provided informed consent before inclusion. No exclusion criteria were applied other than previous experience with transcranial Doppler or refusal to participate. Baseline demographic and educational data, including age, sex, and the number of validated residency semesters, were collected before group assignment. The number of validated residency semesters was used as a proxy for overall clinical experience and may partially reflect differences in prior exposure to ultrasound practice.

Intervention

Before randomization, all participants received a standardized theoretical and practical training session on transcranial Doppler. The theoretical component lasted approximately 30 min and was based on standardized teaching materials developed by WINFOCUS France. It focused on the identification of the temporal acoustic window and insonation of the middle cerebral artery. Key elements of the technique were introduced, including anatomical landmarks in B-mode, probe positioning and orientation, and basic principles of Doppler imaging. Reference ultrasound images were presented to illustrate expected findings. This was followed by a supervised hands-on session during which participants practiced transcranial Doppler acquisition on themselves, with up to two attempts per participant before the timed acquisition phase. Instructors were experienced clinicians with regular practice in transcranial Doppler and teaching. A live demonstration of the technique was performed by the instructor on themselves before the hands-on session.

All acquisitions took place during the same hands-on training session, using standardized ultrasound device presets and acquisition protocols across centers.

In the control condition, residents were free to choose their position relative to the model during insonation, either standing or seated, as typically done during ultrasound workshops. In the intervention condition, residents were instructed to sit at the head of the model, in line with the longitudinal axis of the body. The model lay supine on a stretcher, and the ultrasound device was positioned directly in front of the operator. The respective operator positions for the control and intervention groups are shown in Fig. 1.

Each participant performed two acquisitions—one on each side (right and left middle cerebral artery). The acquisition consisted of locating the artery, visualizing the Doppler spectrum, and recording the final image including the pulsed Doppler trace. No external assistance was allowed during the acquisitions, either from other participants or from the evaluator.

Outcomes

The primary outcome was the acquisition time required to obtain a transcranial Doppler image including a pulsed Doppler trace of the middle cerebral artery. Acquisition time was defined as the interval between the first probe contact on the model’s temple and the moment the image was recorded. For both sides, acquisition times were capped at 180 s. When this limit was exceeded or the attempt was abandoned, a value of 180 s was assigned for analysis.

Secondary outcomes included the total acquisition time, defined as the sum of the right- and left-sided times (capped at 360 s), and image quality. Image quality was assessed in a second phase, after all acquisitions had been completed. The recorded images were anonymized and independently evaluated by a panel of five experienced physicians who were unaware of group allocation, operator identity, and the ratings assigned by the other experts. Images were rated on a five-point scale, from 1 (uninterpretable) to 5 (perfect image), based on the expert’s assessment of the interpretability of the pulsed Doppler recording. To our knowledge, no validated scale specifically exists for this purpose. The five ratings were summed to obtain a total score out of 25, to reduce the impact of inter-observer variability.

Randomization and blinding

Participants were randomly assigned in a 1:1 ratio. The randomization sequence was computer-generated, stratified by center and by the side of the first acquisition, and used permuted blocks of four to ensure balanced allocation within each site. Participants were unaware of the positioning conditions used in the other group, and no information about the study hypothesis was provided during the training sessions. All recorded images were assessed in a blinded manner by independent evaluators.

Sample size

No prior data were available on transcranial Doppler acquisition times among novice operators. Preliminary observations suggested that a reduction of approximately 10 s would be clinically relevant, given baseline acquisition times of around 120 s in this population. Assuming a standard deviation of 15 s, a two-sided α risk of 5% and a β risk of 20%, the required sample size was estimated at 50 acquisitions. Sample size was therefore calculated at the acquisition level. This corresponded to approximately 25 operators, each contributing two acquisitions. To ensure that at least 25 operators would complete both acquisitions, and to account for the possibility of unusable temporal windows as well as the need to include a minimum number of participants from each study site, the target enrollment was increased to 36 operators.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.3.3). Continuous variables were expressed as means ± standard deviations, and categorical variables as counts and percentages. Comparisons between groups for the primary and secondary outcomes were performed using Z tests. A two-sided p value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

The primary analysis was performed at the acquisition level, considering acquisitions as independent observations. This analysis was first conducted according to the intention-to-treat principle.

A per-protocol analysis was then performed, reassigning participants according to their actual positioning during the acquisitions. In addition, a sensitivity analysis was conducted using a linear mixed-effects model, including group allocation and number of validated residency semesters as fixed effects, and subject as a random effect, to account for potential intra-subject correlation. To explore a potential learning effect, acquisition times for the first and second measurements were compared as paired data, independently of randomization groups. Raw acquisition times were used for this analysis, without truncation or capping. The comparison was performed using the Wilcoxon signed-rank test.

The statistical analysis was also conducted by a blinded statistician who had no access to group identifiers.

Ethics

The study protocol was reviewed and approved by the Comité Local d’Éthique pour les Activités de Recherche (CLEA) under reference CLEA-2024-n°379 and was registered with the Commission Nationale de l’Informatique et des Libertés (CNIL) under declaration number 2,236,119. The protocol involved no intervention beyond standard teaching activities. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants, and no identifying data were collected.

Results

A total of 44 residents were included across the four participating centers, with 21 assigned to the control group and 23 to the intervention group. Each participant performed two acquisitions, resulting in 42 acquisitions in the control group and 46 in the intervention group. Primary outcome

The mean acquisition time was significantly shorter in the intervention group compared with the control group (99.4 ± 47.1 s vs. 121.7 ± 52.9 s; p = 0.038). This corresponds to a mean difference of 22.3 s (95% CI 1.2–43.3). This result is presented in Fig. 3.

Secondary outcomes

The total acquisition time was not significantly different between groups (198.8 ± 89.3 s in the intervention group vs. 243.4 ± 96.7 s in the control group; p = 0.07). Image quality scores did not differ significantly between groups (respectively 16.6 ± 5.0 vs. 18.0 ± 5.0; p = 0.20).

Learning effect

The median acquisition time was 94.1 [74.6–132.0] seconds for the first measurement and 94.7 [61.1–149.2] seconds for the second. No statistically significant difference was observed between the two measurements.

Discussion

Main findings

This randomized controlled trial demonstrated that adopting an ergonomically optimized seated position, combining a standardized posture, probe orientation, and ultrasound system placement, improved operator efficiency in transcranial Doppler acquisition compared with a free-positioning approach. Participants using this ergonomic setup obtained interpretable images in a shorter time, without a significant difference in image quality. Notably, the reduction in acquisition time exceeded the effect size initially anticipated during the sample-size calculation, underscoring the practical relevance of this ergonomic setup.

Operator experience, as reflected by the number of validated residency semesters, may influence acquisition performance, as this variable likely captures both overall clinical experience and prior exposure to ultrasound practice. In our study, a higher number of validated semesters was associated with shorter acquisition times. However, in a sensitivity analysis using a mixed-effects model, the effect of operator positioning remained statistically significant after adjustment for this variable. Although this analysis was secondary and should be interpreted with caution, it suggests that the observed effect of operator positioning may be independent of operator experience.

Although the reduction in acquisition time was statistically significant, its clinical relevance should be interpreted with caution. A mean difference of approximately 22 s may appear modest when considered in isolation. However, when extrapolated to more comprehensive examinations involving multiple measurements, such time savings may accumulate and contribute to improved workflow efficiency. Importantly, our study involved novice operators, for whom procedural optimization may have a greater impact on performance. In contrast, experienced operators may achieve rapid acquisition regardless of positioning, which could reduce the relative benefit of ergonomic optimization in routine clinical practice.

The middle cerebral artery has a mean diameter of approximately three millimeters [20], and successful insonation requires precise and stable probe handling. The validated seated position appears to facilitate such stability. In real-world emergency conditions, patients are often immobilized or difficult to reposition, particularly in prehospital settings, making an ergonomically optimized posture important to maintain efficiency and image quality. Beyond its educational relevance, this standardized positioning may also be of interest in certain clinical applications of transcranial Doppler, notably in the management of ischemic stroke [21], where maintaining a stable Doppler signal over a defined target is critical. Moreover, improving operator comfort and promoting ergonomic awareness contribute to clinician well-being and may help prevent long-term musculoskeletal disorders increasingly reported among ultrasound practitioners [14, 22, 23].

Context within existing literature

To our knowledge, this is the first study to evaluate the impact of operator positioning in transcranial Doppler acquisition. While ergonomics has been investigated in other procedural domains, such as orotracheal intubation [24], the influence of operator posture on technical performance or examination quality remains only marginally explored in the literature. Likewise, the effect of operator positioning on the operator’s own comfort and ergonomics has received limited attention.

Reported acquisition times in the literature vary widely depending on operator experience and the scope of the examination. In experienced operators, full transcranial Doppler assessments, including identification and measurement of multiple vessels, may be completed within approximately 10 min, although detailed procedural breakdowns are rarely provided [25]. Conversely, studies involving novice operators performing more comprehensive examinations have reported substantially longer durations, up to 30–40 min [26]. These differences in examination scope and operator experience limit direct comparison with our findings, which focused on a targeted acquisition of the middle cerebral artery following a brief training session.

Several studies have shown that structured ultrasound training programs can improve acquisition performance among novice operators, particularly when combining brief theoretical instruction with supervised or guided hands-on practice [26–28]. Similar approaches have been described across different ultrasound applications, including transcranial ultrasound, where simulator-based and structured training programs have been shown to improve acquisition skills among novice operators [29]. These findings support the importance of standardized teaching and controlled acquisition conditions in facilitating early skill development. Beyond technical performance, integrating ergonomic principles into ultrasound training may help reduce operator discomfort and contribute to improved operator well-being [30].

Study limitations

However, several limitations must be acknowledged. First, the study involved residents with no prior experience in transcranial Doppler who were evaluated immediately after a brief training session, performing examinations on fellow trainees acting as healthy volunteers. These experimental conditions differ from routine clinical practice, where patients may present with more challenging acoustic windows and where operators often have greater experience. As such, these factors may limit the generalizability of our findings to real-world settings.

Second, participants in the control group were free to choose their positioning, which led to variability in operator setup, including a small number adopting the position used in the intervention group. This may have reduced the contrast between groups and potentially attenuated the observed effect. The primary analysis of the main outcome was conducted according to the intention-to-treat principle to reflect this variability, while a per-protocol analysis based on actual positioning was performed as a secondary analysis.

Third, the study focused on acquisition performance and image quality without directly evaluating operator physical strain. Nonetheless, it is highly plausible that adopting a more ergonomic and stable posture would also improve operator comfort, an aspect that warrants dedicated investigation in future studies.

In addition, image quality was assessed using a non-validated expert-based scale, which may introduce some degree of subjectivity despite the use of multiple blinded raters.

Finally, the sample size calculation was based on empirical estimates derived from preliminary observations, as no prior data were available on transcranial Doppler acquisition times among novice operators. The variability observed in the present study was substantially higher than anticipated, likely reflecting the heterogeneity in performance within this population. This increased variability may have reduced the precision of the estimates and the ability to detect differences, particularly for secondary outcomes such as image quality. However, the primary outcome remained statistically significant despite this, supporting the robustness of the observed effect on acquisition time.

Conclusion

This multicenter randomized study suggests that a standardized operator setup at the head of the patient may facilitate faster acquisition of interpretable transcranial Doppler images without compromising quality. These findings support the potential value of ergonomic considerations in ultrasound training. Further studies are needed to confirm these results in real patient settings and to assess their relevance in experienced operators.
