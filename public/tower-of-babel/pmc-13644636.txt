Engineered nanovaccines for prophylactic and therapeutic immunotherapy against cancer and infectious disease
Authors: Muhammad Inam, Muhammad Jamshed, Abuzar Osman
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTNanotechnology has transformed vaccine development by engineering nanovaccines that offer precise control over antigen performance, immune modulation and targeted delivery. These platforms influence physicochemical properties size, charge, and ligand expression to direct antigen trafficking, co-deliver adjuvants and antigen-presenting cells, stimulating potent and robust adaptive immune responses. This review discusses the development of nanovaccines for prophylactic vaccines which induce antibody and memory B-cell responses and therapeutic cancer vaccines, which overcome tolerance and immunosuppression to activate CD8+ T cells with particular emphasis on lipid nanoparticles (LNPs), polymeric NPs, inorganic-core NPs, virus-like particles (VLP) and mechanisms by which each platform exploits physicochemical properties to enhance the antigen encapsulation, adjuvant incorporation, lymphatic transferring, quality and quantity of adaptive immune responses. We reviewed the clinical and preclinical developments of these nanovaccine platforms in cancer and infectious disease immunotherapy, including major successes and new opportunities that will shape the future of vaccines for prevention and treatment.

Introduction

Cancer and infectious diseases remain threat to the global public health system, and there is a pending need to develop more effective immunotherapeutic and prophylactic agents.1 While there have been great successes with vaccines against a wide spectrum of viral and bacterial disease, vaccines have not always been successful against complex intracellular pathogens, chronic infections or against malignant tumors.2,3 One of their main weaknesses is the lack of strong and lasting cell-mediated immune responses, notably cytotoxic T-lymphocytes, that is necessary for the clearance of infected and transformed cells.4 In addition, vaccines are often formulated in ways that fail to produce long-lasting immunological memory, thus exposing populations to the risk of therapeutic recurrence of disease.5 All of these fundamental limitations have encouraged a pattern change toward rational designing of vaccine platforms based on nanotechnology.6 Researchers are now able to exploit the unique physicochemical characteristics of nanocarriers to precisely target antigen presentation, co-deliver immunostimulatory adjuvants and target antigen presenting cells in vivo.7,8 Furthermore, nanoparticle systems have several additional benefits such as the ability to protect labile antigens from early degradation, sustained release properties and the ability to deliver multifunctional systems.9 Nanotechnology has great potential to address some of the limitations of current vaccinology and to change the definition of immunological outcome at the molecular and cellular level,10 and thus offer groundbreaking vaccines for infectious diseases and cancer.11 Engineering of nanovaccines is a combination of materials sciences, immunology and clinical medicine that is designed with diverse architectures, similar to that of a natural pathogen, and with tunable physicochemical properties for enhanced immunogenicity.12 The basic concept of the design of nanovaccines is based on the fact that the immune system has developed very refined mechanisms to recognize and react to particulate antigens, which would be in the size range of the pathogens which are 20–200 nanometers.13,14 Engineering synthetic or semi‑synthetic carriers within this dimension allows exploitation of innate immune recognition pathways and efficient uptake by antigen-presenting cells and optimization of antigen processing toward either major histocompatibility complex class I or class II presentation pathway.15 Lacking this degree of immunological fate control, nanovaccines are emerging as key tools for both oncology and infectious disease applications and are becoming more prominent.16 Prophylactic and therapeutic nanovaccines pursue fundamentally different immunological goals, and these differences directly shape engineering choices.17 Prophylactic vaccines are given to healthy individuals to establish durable protection before exposure, prioritizing high-magnitude neutralizing antibodies, long-lived memory B cells, and for mucosal pathogens local mucosal immunity, with minimal reactogenicity at population scale.18,19 Therapeutic cancer vaccines instead face a hostile setting characterized by tumor antigen heterogeneity, pre-existing tolerance, T-cell dysfunction, and an immunosuppressive microenvironment dominated by regulatory T cells, myeloid-derived suppressor cells, and checkpoint-mediated exhaustion.20 They are therefore engineered to drive cytotoxic CD8+ T-cell responses through efficient MHC-I cross-presentation, co-deliver innate agonists such as STING and TLR ligands, and synergize with immune checkpoint blockade.21

This review critically examines the molecular design, mechanistic function, and progress of major engineered nanovaccine platforms LNPs, polymeric carriers, inorganic core systems, and VLP in the context of cancer and infectious disease immunotherapy (Figure 1). By evaluating their successes, prevailing limitations, and future directions, we aim to establish a unified framework for the rational design of next-generation vaccines, explicitly contrasting how prophylactic and therapeutic objectives shape platform selection, antigen routing, adjuvant choice, and route of administration. Literature search and selection methodology

We performed a critical narrative synthesis of the literature on engineered nanovaccines for cancer and infectious disease immunotherapy. A systematic literature search of PubMed, Web of Science, and Scopus was conducted using combinations of terms such as “nanovaccine,” “lipid nanoparticle,” “polymeric nanoparticle,” “inorganic nanoparticle,” “virus-like particle,” “nanoparticle vaccine,” “cancer immunotherapy,” “infectious disease vaccine,” “antigen delivery,” “dendritic cell targeting,” “lymph node targeting,” “clinical trial,” and “mRNA vaccine.” Although priority was given to studies recently published to capture recent advances. Foundational works were retained where they remain the definitive citation for a given concept. Studies were included if they (i) reported defined immunological, physicochemical, or clinical endpoints as original research or authoritative reviews; (ii) described nanovaccine design, mechanism, or translational status. Studies lacking defined immunological or clinical endpoints, conference abstracts without full data, and non-peer-reviewed sources were excluded.

Design of nanovaccine

Nanovaccines design is challenging due to the requirement to optimize several physicochemical parameters, all of which have significant effects on biodistribution, cellular internalization and immunological outcome.22 Particle size is a principal yet context-dependent design parameter governing biodistribution, cellular internalization, and immunological outcome.23,24 Commonly cited thresholds including renal filtration below ~10 nm, lymphatic access below ~200 nm, and optimal dendritic cell uptake within 20–100 nm should not be treated as universal.25–27 Rather, their relevance is contingent on the administration route as well as on particle shape, deformability, surface chemistry, aggregation state, and protein corona formation.28,29 Another critical design parameter is the surface charge, particles with low or neutral charge have typically shown good circulation half-life and low opsonization, while particles with high surface charge have worse circulation half-life and higher opsonization.30 Surface charge, commonly quantified as zeta potential, governs colloidal stability, opsonization, cellular uptake, and endosomal escape; however, its influence is highly context-dependent and defies reduction to a simple rule in which low or neutral charge is universally beneficial and high charge detrimental.31 The electrostatic interaction with negatively charged cell membranes and the more efficient endosomal escape that can be achieved with cationic surface modifications have stimulated the design of strategies that modify the surface properties in response to the acidic microenvironment of endosomes or tumor tissues, such as charge reversal or pH responsive strategies.32,33 Lastly, the organization of the particle surface, such as the density and orientation of targeting ligands, polyethylene glycol chains, and adjuvant molecules, further regulates immune recognition and cellular interactions which are only starting to be fully appreciated.34,35

Enzymatic protection and co-delivery of the antigen and adjuvant molecules to the same antigen-presenting cell are other important benefits of the particle’s interior encapsulation that can greatly amplify responses of T cells.36 Surface conjugation displays antigens in a multivalent fashion, more closely resembling the repetitive epitope display of viral surfaces, which leads to efficient crosslinking of B‑cell receptors and enhanced antibody production.37 For nucleic acid antigens, electrostatic complexation would be very versatile, because mRNA or DNA molecules form stable complexes with the cationic carrier components, owing to their polyanionic backbone.38,39 The design of nanovaccines to control antigen distribution and adjuvant placement has a profound impact on the immunological processing.40 The simultaneous delivery of antigen and adjuvant to APCs, without the need to dilute and separate the ingredients, is achieved by co-encapsulating them in the same particle.41 There are also some more advanced designs that include stimuli-responsive components that can release antigen based on specific intracellular conditions, such as glutathione concentration, enzymatic activity, or pH gradients, allowing for spatiotemporal control of antigen presentation.42,43 The orientation and valence of surface displayed antigen can be carefully designed in order to promote optimal B‑cell activation, with the highly ordered repetitive array especially potent in triggering germinal center reactions and affinity maturation.44 Nanovaccines should successfully deliver to the secondary lymphoid organs after administration where adaptive immune responses are initiated. The lymphatic system is the main route for transport of particles from peripheral tissue sites to draining lymph nodes where resident dendritic cells, macrophages and B cells await antigenic stimulation.45 The efficiency of lymphatic uptake depends upon particle size, the surface hydrophobicity and deformability of the particles, with smaller, more deformable particles having better lymph node accumulation.46 After entering the lymph node, the nanoparticles come into contact with a specialized microenvironment where subcapsular sinus macrophages and dendritic cells migrate through the lymph node act as gatekeepers for antigen presentation.47 Dendritic cells are the most powerful antigen-presenting cell population for the initiation of naïve T‑cell responses, and design of nanovaccines is increasingly focused on dendritic cell-specific targeting and activation.48 This can be done by surface modification with dendritic cell receptor binding antibodies and/or ligands, such as the DEC-205, CD11c, and mannose receptors, or by the addition of danger signals and adjuvants that promote dendritic cell maturation and up-regulation of co-stimulatory molecules.49,50 Dendritic cells’ maturation at the moment of encountering the antigen has a major influence on T-cell activation or tolerance, highlighting the need to include suitable activation stimuli in nanovaccine formulations.51 Advanced designs make use of the natural tropism of certain particle types to specific parts of the DC subsets to selectively engage the relevant cell populations to elicit the desired immune response.

Rather than presenting each platform in isolation, this review compares them across key coupled design variables, composition and biodegradability; size and surface charge; antigen type; mode of adjuvant incorporation; lymph-node trafficking; APC targets; MHC-I vs. MHC-II routing; humoral vs. cellular bias; safety and clearance; and manufacturability. Lipid nanoparticles

One of the most clinically successful nanovaccine platforms that has reached outstanding significance is based on LNPs (Figure 2(A)), due to their presence in messenger RNA vaccines used in the world fight against the coronavirus epidemic.72,75 The flexibility of the LNP structure is demonstrated beyond the typical vesicular morphology with the realization of solid LNPs, nanostructured lipid carriers tailored for different applications.76 The enhanced physical stability and controlled release properties of solid LNPs in comparison to the conventional liposomal formulation are attributed to their solid lipid core.77,78 The exact lipid composition can be tailored for each administration route with intramuscular and intradermal delivery benefiting from compositions that facilitate efficient trafficking to the lymphatic organs and uptake by antigen-presenting cells.79 Ko et al formulated the TLR3 agonist Nexavant into SM-102- or DOTAP-based LNPs, achieving high encapsulation and cellular uptake. The LNP-mRNA vaccines have now been shown to be highly effective in achieving a strong immune response that is protective.81 Cellular uptake of the mRNA load into cells means that it needs to cross the endosomal membrane and get to the cytoplasmic translation apparatus to generate the antigen protein.82 San Hae ln et al introduced a versatile platform based on cholesterol-modified oligonucleotides that anchor to mRNA and facilitate its encapsulation within LNPs.73 This system enables efficient delivery of mRNA into target cells. The resulting formulation demonstrates markedly enhanced cellular uptake and transfection efficiency (Figure 2(B)), outperforming conventional LNPs formulations. Antigen availability kinetics differs fundamentally between mRNA-LNP vaccines and conventional protein vaccines. Because mRNA-LNP platforms require cellular uptake, endosomal escape and cytoplasmic translation, antigen expression is dictated by mRNA stability, translational efficiency and the persistence of transfected cells.83 In contrast, protein vaccines deliver preformed exogenous antigen whose persistence is determined by depot formation, lymphatic drainage, and antigen-presenting cell uptake and processing rather than by cellular transfection or de novo intracellular antigen synthesis.84 This long-lasting antigen expression can generate an even greater immune response and increase the magnitude of germinal center reactions that lead to high‑affinity antibodies and memory B cells.85 mRNA structural changes and the efficiency of cellular uptake, and the rate of mRNA degradation in the cytoplasm, affect the duration and level of antigen expression.86 Rodríguez et al developed hybrisomes LNP-functionalized with cell-derived membrane proteins isolated via mild detergent extraction. Cy5-labeled eGFP-mRNA was encapsulated in LNP and hybrisomes, with membrane protein extracts (MPEs) integrated during microfluidic assembly.74 Hybrisomes showed up to 15-fold higher cellular uptake and 8-fold greater mRNA delivery than unmodified LNPs (Figure 2(C)), in an MPE-content-dependent manner. All these parameters can be manipulated by mRNA sequence design and the formulation of the LNPs to optimize their immunogenicity and safety.

Lipid nanoparticles (LNP) stand out as the most clinically advanced nanovaccine platform to date, a status cemented by the COVID-19 mRNA-LNP vaccines. These vaccines have secured regulatory approval across the globe and have been delivered in billions of doses, a scale of deployment never before seen for any nanomedicine.87 This extraordinary reach has firmly positioned LNPs technology as the gold standard for translating nanovaccines from bench to bedside. Building on the success of prophylactic COVID-19 vaccines, the clinical landscape for LNP-based nanovaccines has broadened substantially into therapeutic cancer applications.88 Yet despite this remarkable progress, the formulation of LNPs remains a critical determinant of vaccine performance, requiring careful optimization to balance robust immunogenicity against potential toxicity.

Self-amplifying RNA and multivalent formulations

Engineering of LNPs has been very successful in delivering self-amplifying RNA constructs containing the target antigen and viral replicase genes that can amplify the RNA template in the cells being transfected.89 The self-amplifying property enables robust antigen expression from substantially lower starting doses than those required by nonreplicating mRNA (Figure 3), potentially improving the vaccine cost effectiveness.90 However, while lower doses may in principle reduce reactogenicity, this effect is not inherent to dose reduction and depends on the platform, formulation, and innate immune activation profile, necessitating empirical confirmation for each candidate.91,92 Multivalent LNP formulations co-delivering multiple mRNA species coding for distinct antigens/antigen variants are showing special promise for applications where an extensive immune coverage is desired.93 Felgner et al synthesized and screened ionizable cationic lipid libraries to optimize mRNA-LNP formulations, selecting lead candidates for a bivalent Fluvid vaccine.94 This vaccine comprised unmodified mRNA encoding transmembrane-deleted hemagglutinin and full-length spike protein, encapsulated in LNPs. Immunization induced robust T- and B-cell responses and conferred 100% protection against influenza and SARS-CoV-2 in mice, with no loss of efficacy against either virus. Zeng et al developed an optimized LNP platform via high-throughput ionizable lipid screening and co-encapsulated tumor antigen–encoding mRNA with STING agonists c-di-AMP and Mn2+. This co-delivery synergistically enhanced type I interferon signaling, dendritic cell maturation, antigen presentation, and cytotoxic T-cell responses, driving potent and durable antitumor immunity.95 This strategy has been used for the next-generation coronavirus vaccines that target multiple variants of the virus at once and for personalized cancer vaccines that include neoantigens unique to the cancer patient.96 In one study, Younis et al developed a bivalent neoantigen mRNA-LNP vaccine against hepatocellular carcinoma.97 The lead formulation, CL15H6 LNPs, outperformed clinically relevant benchmarks in splenic dendritic cell transfection, antigen presentation, and co-stimulatory molecule expression, while inducing higher frequencies of antigen-specific CTLs. Recent advances have established mRNA vaccines as safe and effective tools for human immunization. Singh et al compared self-amplifying and circular RNA vaccines encoding SARS-CoV-2 RBD. Both elicited comparable neutralizing antibody titers, but circRBD induced superior memory T-cell responses and remained stable for 4 weeks at 4°C. A bivalent Delta Omicron circRBD formulation neutralized both variants.98 These findings validate circRBD as a potent COVID-19 vaccine candidate and support circRNA platforms for multivalent design against emerging variants. Beyond conventional carrier taxonomy, an emerging class of cancer nanovaccines is defined by antigen provenance and the biomolecular interfaces they emulate. Neoantigen vaccines are personalized, immunogenic synthetic peptide constructs derived from tumor-specific mutational landscapes, thereby circumventing central tolerance and minimizing off-target autoimmunity.99 LNPs delivery of neoantigen-encoding mRNA represents the most clinically advanced platform, with candidates such as mRNA-4157 and BNT111 undergoing clinical evaluation; bivalent neoantigen constructs have demonstrated preclinical efficacy in hepatocellular carcinoma.100 This pipeline is increasingly coupled with computational neoantigen prioritization, exemplified by in silico-designed mRNA vaccines targeting CA-125 neoantigens in breast and ovarian cancer.101,102

Polymeric nanoparticles

The versatility of polymeric NPs in nanovaccine design is wide that can be used to control the degradation time, the surface function and release profile of a nanovaccine by a broad spectrum of synthetic and natural polymers.103 Poly (lactic-co-glycolic acid) (PLGA) is a widely studied synthetic polymer that is biodegradable, biocompatible, and has a long history of clinical use for drug delivery applications. The hydrolysis rates of PLGA nanoparticles can be controlled by varying the ratio of lactide to glycolide and the molecular weight of the polymer.104 This controlled degradation allows for days to weeks of antigen presentation, which is similar to the natural infection and leads to strong germinal center response.105 PLGA NPs have been applied in vaccinology as antigen delivery vehicles capable of protecting antigens from degradation and being efficiently captured by antigen presenting cells.106 Shigellosis remains a major global health concern, with a disproportionate burden of morbidity and mortality among children under 5 y of age in low- and middle-income countries.107 Fazeli et al developed a chimeric nanovaccine comprising a fusion antigen of Shigella flexneri IpaD and StxB encapsulated in PLGA NPs and evaluated its immunogenicity in a murine model. The PLGA-based NPs enabled sustained antigen release, thereby potentially enhancing antigen stability and prolonging its immunological exposure. Compared with free IdSb, Nano-IdSb elicited significantly higher IgG antibody responses in immunized mice, indicating that PLGA encapsulation enhanced the immunogenicity and stability of the chimeric antigen.108 In a study by Cui et al non-toxic mutants of alpha-hemolysin (rHlaH35L) and protein SpA were covalently conjugated to PLGA-PEG NPs as nanovaccine antigens.109 The results demonstrate that antigen type critically determines vaccine efficacy when paired with a given nanoparticle adjuvant. This highlights the antigen-dependent performance of 25% NP adjuvants and provides a pathway for designing safer, more potent vaccines.

Other synthetic polymers, such as polyanhydride and polyamino acids, have different degradation rates and chemistries and can be tailored to meet specific immunological goals.110,111 Polyanhydride NPs can undergo surface erosion but not bulk erosion, thus maintaining the integrity of the particle while releasing antigen from the surface over time. This mechanism of erosion can generate a persistent antigen presentation that is especially efficient in inducing T helper type 1 responses that are beneficial for the clearance and control of intracellular pathogens and tumors.112 Diaz et al formulated innate-stimulating nanovaccine using polyanhydride copolymers and PRR agonists to boost mucosal innate immunity113 which upregulated transcriptional responses for inflammatory, antiviral, and antimicrobial factors. Lopez et al used multiple IAV antigens to validate the plug-and-play polyanhydride nanovaccine platform, showing humoral and cellular immunity comparable to IAV-nanovax. H3N2 formulations induced systemic/mucosal antibody responses and enhanced lung-resident CD4+/CD8+ T cells, correlating with protection against homologous and heterosubtypic IAV.114 Additionally, polyamino acid nanovaccines are biodegradable systems that employ amino acid chains for safe antigen delivery and immune potentiation with no reported toxicity.115 Zhao et al constructed biodegradable nanovaccines (OMPP) from ovalbumin (OVA)-manganese oxide NPs, amphiphilic poly(γ-glutamic acid) (γ-PGA), and ε-polylysine (PL) for enhanced cancer immunotherapy. Notably, amphiphilic γ-PGA and PL functioned dually as carriers and immunoadjuvants, facilitating cytosolic antigen delivery and promoting dendritic cell maturation.116 Nevagi et al chemically conjugated GAS M-protein-derived B-cell epitope J8 and universal T-helper epitope P25 to various anionic amino acid-based polymers. The resulting poly(anionic amino acid)-peptide conjugates were formulated with trimethyl chitosan (TMC) from two sources to generate self-adjuvanting nanovaccines.117 Azuar et al evaluated polyleucine-conjugated cationic liposomes as vaccine candidates against GAS. Their pHAA-liposome delivery system induced high opsonic antibody titers against GAS clinical isolates. However, despite efficient liposomal anchoring by polyleucine, the cationic formulation did not enhance immunogenicity of the lead candidate.118

Therapeutic vaccines and immune checkpoint blockade represent recent immunotherapeutic advances offering targeted antitumor immunity with reduced toxicity.119 Vaccine efficacy relies on antigen choice.120 Wang et al engineered HM-NPs@LP, a PLGA nanovaccine co-encapsulating tumor membranes (TMs), bacterial membranes (EMs), and HPV16 long peptides (LP) for antitumor immunotherapy.121 HM-NPs@LP was readily taken up by BMDCs in vitro, driving DC maturation and tumor-specific T-cell activation. In vivo, the nanovaccine accumulated in lymph nodes and effected robust tumor suppression in murine models. Polymeric nanovaccines have yet to earn clinical validation for vaccine use. Most polymeric candidates including the PLGA-based Shigella, polyanhydride influenza, and polyamino acid cancer vaccines remain stuck at the preclinical proof-of-concept stage.122,123 This is a striking gap, particularly when one considers how favorable the biocompatibility and biodegradability profiles of polymers like PLGA have proven to be.124 The absence of clear regulatory precedents and the limited interest from major pharmaceutical companies have together created a translational bottleneck one that mRNA-LNPs technology has only deepened by drawing investment125 away from polymeric platforms, despite their theoretical strengths in controlled release and modularity.

Inorganic core nanoparticles

Inorganic NPs serve as structurally robust, physicochemically tunable platforms for nanovaccine design.126 Gold (Au), iron oxide (IO), silica, and related metal oxides (Figure 5(A)) can be engineered with defined size, morphology, and surface chemistry to optimize immune cell targeting and antigen presentation.130,131 Functionalization with antigens, adjuvants, or ligands enhances cellular uptake and delivery to dendritic cells and macrophages, while intrinsic photothermal, magnetic, or catalytic properties further potentiate immune activation.132 Overall, inorganic cores provide a versatile foundation for multifunctional nanovaccines with enhanced therapeutic efficacy. Inorganic core nanoparticles have gained clinical validation in diagnostic imaging, with IONPs approved as MRI contrast agents and AuNPs under clinical investigation for photothermal therapy (PTT).133 However, no inorganic nanovaccine has yet reached clinical approval, and the entire field remains preclinical.14 The candidates discussed in this section including Au-peptide conjugates, mesoporous silica-based vaccines, and IO-mediated DNA delivery system are proof-of-concept demonstrations but have not progressed in human testing. Gold nanoparticles

The nanovaccine delivery system of AuNPs has been garnered a lot of attention as they are easily synthesized, tunable optical properties, and have well-developed surface chemistry for versatile bioconjugation.134,135 AuNPs functionality is their ability to locally heat cells using near-infrared (NIR) irradiation, which increases the permeability of the cellular membranes and leads to the release of antigens.136 The antigen release ability can be externally induced, providing spatiotemporal control over antigen presentation not easily obtained using passive delivery systems.137 Castro et al constructed epitope-displaying nanovaccine prototypes through glutaraldehyde-mediated chemisorption of five SARS-CoV-2 spike (S) protein-derived linear peptides (P1–P5) onto heterobifunctional polyethylene glycol-prefunctionalized AuNPs. The immunization regimen induced rapid and significant IgM seroconversion against each peptide epitope, with titers plateauing after the second immunization; conversely, IgG responses exhibited delayed kinetics, requiring a third booster dose to achieve peak concentrations.138 Javier et al engineered bivalent and monovalent nanovaccine candidates by covalently tethering two EHEC-specific immunogens to AuNPs scaffolds: LomW, a phage BP-933W-encoded putative outer membrane protein, and EscC, a type III secretion system ring-forming structural protein localized to the outer membrane.127 The derived nanoconjugates, designated AuNP-LomW and AuNP-EscC, exhibited stable antigen loading and retention, and induced potent antigen-specific immune responses following subcutaneous administration in mice (Figure 5(B)). This application of AuNPs is a good scaffold for the display of multiple copies of antigens, mediated by close packing of antigens on the surface, which promotes efficient B cell receptor crosslinking and germinal center formation.139 In another study, Zdrehus et al prepared citrate-capped AuNPs by aqueous chemical reduction and subsequently decorated their surfaces with carcinoembryonic antigen via electrostatic adsorption. Confocal or flow cytometric analysis revealed robust uptake of this nanovaccine by RAW 264.7 murine macrophages, with extensive colocalization within LAMP-1–positive or LysoTracker-positive lysosomal compartments findings consistent with successful endolysosomal routing and antigen proteolytic processing.140 The stability and persistence of non-degradable AuNPs have sparked interest in the development of AuNPs and hybrid systems where the synthetic versatility of Au is coupled with the clearance benefits of biodegradable particles.141

Mesoporous silica nanoparticle

The mesoporous silica NPs have extremely high surface area and well-defined pore structure, which leads to a significant antigen and adjuvant loading capacity.142 The ordered pore networks typically have small diameter of which can be used to accommodate proteins, peptides and small‑molecule adjuvants while simultaneously protecting the cargo from degradation during transport.143 Silica surface can be easily modified with silane chemistry to provide targeting ligands, charge-modifying groups, or stimuli-responsive gatekeeping groups to regulate the release of the cargo based on specific environmental cues.144 Nanoengineered mesoporous silica NPs is a promising platform for augmenting cancer immunotherapy, leveraging their inherent high porosity, biocompatibility, facile surface functionalization, and intrinsic self-adjuvanticity.145 Huang et al developed a multifunctional nanoplatform comprising mesoporous silica NPs as a structural vector, co-loaded with the PTT agent polydopamine, model antigen ovalbumin, and the pH-responsive antigen release promoter ammonium bicarbonate. This integrated system was rationally designed for combined PTT and immunotherapy against melanoma.146 Yu et al developed a virus-inspired oral nanovaccine based on spiky mesoporous silica NPs surface-decorated with Rehmannia glutinosa polysaccharide (Figure 5(C)), a naturally derived polysaccharide exhibiting favorable intestinal biocompatibility.128 The resulting conjugates demonstrated enhanced intestinal retention and epithelial engagement, concomitant with increased antigen accessibility within the mucosal microenvironment, as evidenced by amplified antigen-associated signals at mucosal immune inductive sites. The biodegradability of mesoporous silica can be controlled by adding organic functionalities to the silica structure or by changing the degree of condensation, which overcomes the issue of accumulation of non-degradable materials.147 Ho et al demonstrated a composite microsphere formulation comprising mesoporous silica spheres and PLGA that enables controlled, compartmentalized delivery of a prime-boost vaccine through differential encapsulation of plasmid DNA and protein within distinct spatial domains. In vitro release studies revealed divergent kinetics for plasmid DNA and protein over a 40 d period, while the dual-concentric-feeding ultrasonic atomization process exhibited excellent batch-to-batch reproducibility.148 Hence, the distinctive combination of architectural tunability, established biocompatibility, and intrinsic immunomodulatory functionality establishes mesoporous silica nanoparticles as a premier nanoplatform for advancing cancer immunotherapy. Their capacity for rational engineering enabling precise control over antigen loading kinetics, surface functionalization, and stimuli-responsive release directly addresses critical bottlenecks in vaccine efficacy and combination therapy integration, underscoring substantial translational potential.

Magnetic nanoparticles

Iron Oxide nanoparticles (IONPs) have specific properties that enable them to be used in external targeting to deliver vaccines and in MRI to track vaccines.149 Because the crystal size of the IONPs is in the superparamagnetic range, these particles can be manipulated with external magnetic field, which could be useful for concentrating vaccine formulations at specific locations within the body, such as a draining lymph node or some tumor tissue.150 This magnetic targeting ability may prove useful in the uptake of antigen-presenting cells and decrease the overall dose of the vaccine needed to provide protective immunity.151 Another advantage of the MRI contrast properties of the NPs is that they can be tracked in the body without any invasive techniques,152 allowing for valuable pharmacokinetic data to be gained in the process of vaccine development, given that the accumulation of the particles in the lymph nodes is also known.153 Li et al investigated the formation kinetics and immune-regulatory dynamics of the protein corona in acute myeloid leukemia mouse models using clinically relevant IONPs with systematically varied surface chemistries, including an FDA-approved formulation. Their findings demonstrate that carboxyl-functionalized IONPs function as potent immune activators in the acute myeloid leukemia microenvironment, establishing a proof-of-concept for rationally engineered surface chemistry to enhance nanoparticle-mediated immunotherapy against hematological malignancies (Figure 5(D)).129 DNA vaccines generally exhibit lower transfection efficiency than protein-based counterparts.154 Superparamagnetic IONPs‑mediated gene delivery represents a promising strategy to enhance transfection efficacy and overcome this limitation.155,156 Al-Deen et al developed an IONPs-PEI-based magnetofection system to potentiate delivery of VR1020-PyMSP119, a DNA vaccine encoding the immunodominant 19-kDa carboxyl-terminal fragment of Plasmodium yoelii merozoite surface protein. This fragment elicits protective humoral immunity by blocking erythrocyte invasion.157 Collectively, superparamagnetic IONPs function as dual purpose platforms for precision immunotherapy, combining magnetic field-guided vaccine targeting to lymphoid or tumor tissues with real-time MRI-based biodistribution tracking. These convergent capabilities underscore substantial translational potential in next-generation vaccine and immune oncology.

Comparative degradation and safety

The inorganic platforms have different biological fate and hence have to be evaluated separately.158 Unlike most other NPs, AuNPs are not biodegradable.159 Mesoporous NPs are seen to degrade into orthosilicic acid and local inflammation upon intramuscular or subcutaneous injection.160 IONPs are metabolized through native iron pathways; however, they have shown oxidative stress, teratogenicity and epigenetic changes at high doses.161 Therefore, AuNPs are more appropriate for single-dose or biodegradable hybrid formulations, while silica needs to be optimized for intramuscular injection and iron oxide is the most compatible with repeated administration as long as doses are not in excess of the oxidative stress threshold. Such differences also necessitate the evaluation of dosage, persistence, organ accumulation, inflammatory and immune reactions, multiple administration, and long-term toxicity per material, as opposed to a platform-wide conclusion. Characterization of particle size, surface charge, endotoxin degradation products and chronic toxicology has been a requirement for regulatory approval on all three platforms.162,163

Virus-like nanoparticles

Virus-like particles (VLPs) are self-assembling nanostructures derived from viral structural proteins.164 These particles display antigens in highly ordered multivalent presentations that have been optimized by evolution for optimal immune recognition, and represent extremely powerful immunogens, even in the absence of any further adjuvants.165 The self-assembly process is based on the inherent attractiveness of viral capsid proteins to form higher order structures, which are determined by the sequence and folding of the capsid proteins. This biomimetic design exploits millions years of evolutionary optimization to produce particles that are recognized as pathogenic by the immune system without requiring to be engineered for this purpose.166 A number of VLPs, however, are large enough and geometrically regular enough to directly activate complement pathways and to stimulate innate immune responses to deliver danger signals that promote efficient priming of productive T cells and dendritic cell maturation.167 These particles are genetically non-contagious, non-replicative, non-pathogenic and have the immunological properties that make viruses so good immunogens.

Chimeric particle designs

Engineered strategies to present heterologous antigens of pathogens of other families or tumor-associated targets can be carried out due to the modularity of virus-like particle assembly.168 Chimeric VLPs are designed by attaching foreign antigen sequences to the capsid protein surface, which leads to the presentation of the target epitope on the surface of the self-assembling particle scaffold.169 Kou et al reported a modular strategy for constructing complex protein architectures integrating natural L‑ and mirror‑image D‑motifs. Key to this approach is D‑SpyStapler, a chemically synthesized ligase with inverted chiral specificity, enabling efficient conjugation of D‑SpyTag and D‑BDTag. Exploiting glycine’s achirality, L‑sortase A covalently links D‑peptides (SpyTag or BDTag with N‑terminal poly‑Gly) to L‑proteins bearing a C‑terminal LPXTG motif. Resulting D‑tagged conjugates are assembled via D‑SpyStapler. This method generates chirally chimeric VLP vaccines displaying malaria and coronavirus antigens as recombinant proteins or synthetic peptides, offering substantial modularity for vaccine design.170 Choo et al assessed the immunogenicity and protective efficacy of BinJ/DENV2-prME, a chimeric vaccine based on Binjari virus displaying DENV prM/E proteins. They immunized AG129 mice with this vaccine via a high-density microarray patch at a single 1 µg dose. HD-MAP delivery accelerated neutralizing antibody induction relative to needle administration and conferred complete protection against lethal DENV challenge.171 Next-generation chimeric VLPs vaccines require development to enhance immunogenicity.172 Singh et al designed a bivalent Chimeric Alphavirus-Coronavirus VLPs displaying Chikungunya virus fusion glycoproteins and the SARS-CoV-2 receptor-binding domain on its surface.173 Cell-based assays confirmed the display of immunogenic antigens. Bacteriophage virus scaffolds

Bacteriophage-based nanovaccines distinguished from true VLPs. The bacteriophages are intact, genome-containing vectors, whereas VLPs are empty protein shells.178 Bacteriophage particle ability to survive harsh processing and chemical modification allows for scaling up production and functionalization of its surface with antigens, adjuvants, and targeting ligands.179 Filamentous types of bacteriophage have a geometry that is long and may improve cell interactions and tissue penetrating properties compared to spherical particles.180 Bacteriophage nanovaccines synergistically combine three functions include multivalent antigen display, targeted delivery, and intrinsic adjuvanticity.181 Phage genomic CpG motifs activate TLR9-driven innate immunity, and their nanoscale architecture may stabilize dendritic cell–T cell synapses. Together, these properties induce robust, long lasting adaptive immunity without external adjuvants.182 Xia et al engineered bacteriophage nanoassemblies delivering influenza hemagglutinin stem genes into DC cytoplasm with a DC-targeting peptide, enhancing DC–T cell immunological synapse stability in vivo.174 This targeted approach improved vaccine protective potency, highlighting the feasibility of synapse-stabilization strategies (Figure 6(B)). Bacteriophage M13 offers structural versatility, genetic programmability, and diverse antigen display capabilities. M13-based vaccines induce robust humoral and cellular immunity against animal viral, bacterial, and parasitic pathogens. This review critically examines advances in M13 phage vaccine development, focusing on structure, display strategies, immune mechanisms, and veterinary applications.183 Dong et al showed that a hybrid M13 phage personalized tumor antigen platform delivers antigens to lymph nodes and activates antigen-presenting cells via Toll-like receptor 9 signaling, enhancing innate and adaptive immunity.184 In another study, AuYeung et al developed an M13 phage vector with SpyCatcher SpyTag antigen conjugation and DC-targeting peptides, which activates dendritic cells in vitro and enhances T cell cytotoxicity via co-stimulatory and inflammatory gene upregulation.185 Nazeri et al aimed to design high-affinity mimotopes to trastuzumab using bioinformatics tools and evaluate M13 bacteriophages as display platforms for these mimotopes.175 Using the Peptiderive server, peptide sequences involved in HER2–trastuzumab binding were identified and refined. The optimized mimotopes were displayed on the pVIII coat protein of M13 bacteriophages using the PAK8 phagemid vector. ELISA confirmed the binding of these recombinant phages to trastuzumab (Figure 6(C)). Hence, bacteriophages offer a tunable, immunogenic, adjuvant-free nanoplatform for next-generation vaccines, leveraging physical design and innate immune cues to achieve durable, targeted immunity.

Virus-like particles (VLPs) rank as the most clinically successful nanovaccine platform after LNps, with approved vaccines against HPV,186 hepatitis B187 and malaria.187 VLPs nanovaccines still face barriers to broader use, because production is complex, demanding cell culture expression, purification, and strict quality control regulatory, costs remain higher than other platforms and scaling up requires optimizing yields,188 ensuring consistent assembly, and preserving antigen structure.

Challenges and future perspectives

Clinical translation of nanovaccine platforms requires scalable, reproducible, and affordable manufacturing processes for worldwide use.189 The process of making, scaling up, and preparing LNPs has come a long way to meet the demand for coronavirus vaccines, but many new nanovaccine platforms have issues with consistency, purification, and shelf stability across batches. Multicomponent formulations, especially those composed of carefully specified ratios of lipids, polymers, antigens, and adjuvants, require a close controlling of the process as well as analytical characterization for product quality.190 To overcome these challenges and improve the production of a wide range of nanovaccine formulations, continuous manufacturing processes, microfluidic synthesis, and advanced process analytical technology are being pursued.191 Another practical limitation that affects the accessibility of nanovaccines, especially in resource poor environments, is the requirement for a cold chain. There are numerous lipid and polymeric NPs formulations that are only stable when stored frozen, considerable research and development strides have been made in the field of lyophilized formulations and thermostable compositions, which are suitable for relatively high storage temperatures.192,193 Stabilizing excipients like trehalose and sucrose and optimizing NPs composition for thermal stability are hot research topics that have significant implications for global vaccine equity.194 Ongoing nanovaccine development efforts must focus on making the vaccines stable and readily available for distribution without sacrificing immunogenicity to achieve the greatest public health benefit. Nanovaccines safety profile is affected by the safety characteristics of the carrier material, the biological activity of the adjuvants used, and their interaction with the host immune and physiological systems.195 The relationship between immunogenicity and reactogenicity is an important parameter to optimize and strategies to improve antigen presentation and immune activation may have the potential to trigger more local and systemic inflammatory reactions.196 Long‑term biodistribution and disposition of non-biodegradable components of the NPs are important concerns for safety and should be carefully evaluated. Inorganic components in NPs are generally not metabolized and can remain in tissues for longer time periods.197 Therefore, extensive toxicological studies will be required for poly type NPs. Standardization of characterization procedures for NPs is crucial in terms of NPs size distribution, surface charge, stability and endotoxin level to guarantee product quality and regulatory review.198 Developing nanovaccines is likely to involve a combination of the many new technologies that are coming together, such as synthetic biology, new designs enabled by artificial intelligence (AI), and new manufacturing capabilities. Comprehensively large numbers of NPs formulations and immunological results are used to train machine learning algorithms to predict best formulations, thereby decreasing the need for experimental testing of formulations.199 Combining nanovaccines and other therapeutic approaches, such as gene and cell therapy and small molecule drugs, can generate multifunctional therapeutic systems that can tackle several disease pathogenesis aspects simultaneously.200,201 Coordinated immune responses at the tumor site can be achieved with NPs containing tumor antigens and immune checkpoint inhibitors, which remain functional in the periphery.202 Together, these convergent approaches reflect the more and more interdisciplinary character of nanomedicine and underscore the pivotal role that engineered nanovaccines will play in the future of immunotherapy.

Conclusion

The development of engineered nanovaccines represents a paradigm shift in vaccinology as it now provides unprecedented capabilities to control antigen presentation, to modulate immune responses and to target specific cell populations with high specificity. Nanotechnology has opened up a whole new world of vaccine formulation possibilities. The selection of the platform is largely empirical, each platform having its own mechanistic niche and no predictive model for the mapping of a clinical objective into a rational platform choice. Perhaps the most important determinant of efficacy is the choice of antigen, and this is the least engineered of factors, with the prioritization of neoantigens still being based on computational prediction. Immune programming is not fully regulated. A more quantitative approach is needed to safety, especially for long-term biodistribution of non-biodegradable inorganic cores. However, mRNA-LNP is not the only obstacle to distribute vaccines equitably worldwide because of the need for scalable manufacturing and stability in cold chains. Most importantly, there is a clear clinical need for proof of clinical benefit the polymeric and inorganic platforms are almost entirely in the preclinical stages, and only mRNA-LNP and VLP vaccines have been approved clinically. Only if they are aimed at these unrequited, translation challenges will AI, synthetic biology, and advanced materials science make a real difference in the future of nanovaccines. The true potential of engineered nanovaccines in the battle against cancer, infectious diseases and global health challenges of the future will require ongoing investments in fundamental research, translational development and manufacturing infrastructure.
