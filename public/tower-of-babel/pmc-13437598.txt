EZH2/DUSP1/Akt signaling axis mediates the inhibitory effect of crebanine on hepatocellular carcinoma progression
Authors: Jinxia Qi, Zhikai Wen, Yanju Ren, Gang Huang, Yuan Wang, Qingqing Ruan, Chunmei Wen, Jiale Xu, Mengyao Wang, Xingyue Wang, Hanbin Chen, Yihu Zheng, Jie Deng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractHepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) is characterized by a subtle onset, rapid development, and high malignancy. Traditional treatments have struggled to cure patients with advanced HCC. Crebanine is an isoquinoline aporphine alkaloid extracted from Stephania venosa. Few studies have focused on the therapeutic potential and molecular mechanisms of crebanine in HCC. Herein, to comprehensively evaluate the anti-HCC effect of crebanine in HCC and to further investigate the underlying mechanisms, potential therapeutic role of crebanine against HCC malignancy was assessed through in vitro and in vivo experiments. We carried out a panel of experiments covering cell viability, colony formation, apoptosis detection, migration, invasion and western blot analysis. Results revealed that crebanine suppresses proliferation, migration and invasion, and triggers apoptosis in HepG2 and Huh7 cells. Mechanistically, crebanine treatment increased DUSP1 expression while decreasing EZH2 and p-Akt expressions. Crebanine promoted DUSP1 transcription through repressing EZH2 expression, thereby inhibiting Akt phosphorylation. Knockdown of DUSP1 diminished the anti-HCC effects of crebanine while activating Akt signaling. Overexpression of EZH2 abolished crebanine-induced DUSP1 upregulation and the cytostatic effects in HCC both in vitro and vivo. Overall, crebanine effectively suppresses HCC tumor growth and progression via modulation of the EZH2/DUSP1/Akt signaling pathway. Introduction

Accounting for 90% of liver cancer diagnoses, hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) remains one of the predominant malignant neoplasms around the world (Vogel et al. 2022). As a severe public health threat, HCC ranks among China’s most prevalent and lethal malignancies (Yi et al. 2016). Current therapeutic approaches for HCC include surgery, chemoradiotherapy, targeted drugs, and immunotherapy, all of which have limitations in terms of safety and efficacy (Kudo 2020). Poor survival outcomes persist among HCC patients (Liu et al. 2021), highlighting an imperative demand for innovative therapies and mechanistic exploration.

Over the past decade, numerous bioactive chemicals from natural sources have been investigated as possible agents for chemoprophylaxis and chemotherapy (Ma et al. 2019; Xiang et al. 2019). Crebanine, an isoquinoline aporphine alkaloid extracted from Stephania venosa, has shown pharmacological effects against arrhythmia, sedation, analgesia, and tumors (Wang et al. 2016). In terms of antitumor potency, crebanine exerts extensive antiproliferative effects on diverse human tumor cell lines including leukemia, fibrosarcoma and cervical carcinoma via regulating cell cycle and apoptosis (Wongsirisin et al. 2012). It also enhances TNF-α-stimulated apoptosis and inhibits invasion in lung A549 cells via diminishing NF-κB network perturbation and inducing downstream regulon plasticity (Yodkeeree et al. 2014), and it induces cell apoptosis via the PI3K-Akt pathway in glioblastoma multiforme (Yeh et al. 2024). Additionally, in HCC, crebanine has been shown to exert cytotoxic effects on hepatic HepG2 cells by modulating the ROS-AKT-FoxO3a molecular signaling network (Tan et al. 2023). However, the anti-HCC effect of crebanine requires thorough investigation using multiple cell lines and in vivo studies, and it is also crucial to clarify whether additional significant mechanisms are involved.

Herein, we comprehensively assessed therapeutic potential of crebanine against HCC by examining its: (1) anti-proliferative and pro-apoptotic effects, (2) inhibition of metastatic behaviors (migration/invasion), and (3) modulation of key oncogenic pathways, using complementary in vitro and in vivo approaches.

Materials and methods

Cells and instruments

Crebanine (HY-N2255; purity > 99%) was purchased from MCE (Beijing, China). HuH7, HepG2 and LO2 cells (Cell Bank of the Chinese Academy of Sciences, Shanghai, China) were cultivated in DMEM medium (Gibco, Carlsbad, CA, USA) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; Gibco) and 1% penicillin–streptomycin (Solarbio, Beijing, China) in a humidified environment at 37 °C with 5% CO2. Trypsin–EDTA was bought from Thermo Fisher (Waltham, MA, USA). Major instruments herein used were a plate reader (Varioskan Flash, Thermo Fisher), a flow cytometer (BD FACS VerseTM, USA), and a fluorescence microscope (Leica, Germany). Software tools included FlowJo (version 10.4, BD Biosciences), Image J (version 1.4.3, USA), and GraphPad Prism (version 8.0.1, USA).

CCK-8 assay

Cells (6 × 103/well) were plated in 96-well plates and incubated overnight for attachment. Cells were then exposed to crebanine treatment for 24 h. CCK-8 assay was applied to detect the cell viability of HCC cells, with cells incubated in 100 μL of 10% CCK-8 solution (Beyotime, Beijing, China). After 2 h of incubation under standard conditions, plates were read at 450 nm on a microplate reader.

Colony formation assay

Cells were seeded at 250 cells per well in 12-well plates and maintained them in complete growth medium for 4 days to assess colony-forming ability. Cells were treated with crebanine for 4 days, then fixed and stained with 0.1% crystal violet (Solarbio; methanol-dissolved) for 1 h. Colonies containing > 50 cells were counted using ImageJ software (NIH).

EdU and TUNEL assays

Cells (6 × 103/well) were cultured overnight on coverslip-containing 12-well plates. To assess its pharmacological activity, cells underwent a 24-h crebanine treatment. For the EdU assay, cells were sequentially subjected to a 2 h incubation with 10 μM EdU (Beyotime), a 15-min fixation with paraformaldehyde, and a 10 min permeabilization at ambient temperature. Staining was performed using DAPI (Solarbio) in conjunction with Click Reaction Mixture (Beyotime). For TUNEL staining, cells or tissue specimens were fixed (4% PFA, 30 min) and permeabilized with Triton X-100 (0.5%, 5 min) at ambient temperature. TUNEL-positive cells were identified after 1 h incubation with the reaction mixture (Beyotime), with DAPI used for nuclear visualization (5 min). Images were acquired via the fluorescence microscopy and analyzed using Image J.

Flow cytometry assay

Cells (4 × 105/well) were initially seeded in 6-well plates and allowed 24 h for attachment. For cell cycle profiling, cells were fixed overnight at −20 °C with 70% ethanol, resuspended in PBS, and incubated with PI (50 μg/mL, BD Biosciences) staining solution in the dark at 37 °C for 30 min. For apoptotic assessment, resuspended cells were co-stained with Annexin V-FITC (BD Biosciences) and PI (BD Biosciences), then incubated for 15 min at room temperature away from light. Flow cytometry (BD FACSCanto II) was applied to detect cell cycle distribution and apoptotic ratio, and data were further processed with FlowJo software.

Wound healing assay

Cells were grown in 6-well plates till they achieved 100% confluence. The center region was demarcated by scoring with a pipette tip (10 μL). Cells were then refreshed with medium containing 1% FBS and treated with crebanine for 24 h. Images were acquired under light microscopy at 0, 24 and 48 h post treatment. The distance of the open gap was quantified using Image J.

Transwell invasion assay

0.2% Matrigel (BD Biosciences) was applied to 24-well Transwell chambers for overnight coating. The top chamber was inoculated with 5 × 103 cells in serum-free media (200 μL). The bottom reservoir held 600 μL of medium supplemented with 15% FBS. Following treatment and incubation for the specified duration, cells in the top chamber were carefully eliminated with cotton swabs. Residual cells underwent 20-min staining with 0.1% crystal violet (in methanol). Images from 5 randomly selected fields were captured and analyzed using ImageJ.

Western blot

Cell lysis was performed in inhibitor ((PMSF from Beyotime; inhibitor cocktail from Biomake))-containing RIPA buffer (Solarbio). Protein concentration was determined by BCA assay. Samples were subjected to SDS-PAGE separation and membrane transfer, then blocked with 5% TBST-diluted skim milk at room temperature for 1 h. Subsequently, the membrances were subjected to overnight incubation at 4 °C with diluted primary antibodies (Table S1) in blocking buffer, followed by secondary antibody incubation for another 1 h at room temperature (Cell Signaling Technology, Danvers, MA, USA). Protein bands were visualized via ECL chemiluminescence system (Pierce, Rockford, IL, USA), and band intensities were quantified with ImageJ software.

Stable transfected cell lines

Recombinant lentivirus packages for knocking down DUSP1 and overexpressing EZH2 were acquired from Genepharma (Shanghai, China). Recombinant or control lentivirus was used to infect cells for 12 h. Following treatment, cells were cultured in fresh medium for 48 h and subjected to selection using 2 µg/mL puromycin.

Molecular docking

The structure of crebanine was retrieved from PubChem (CID:159999; https://pubchem.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/). The crystal structure of the EZH2 protein was prepared by removing water molecules and native ligands using PyMOL 2.1.0. Hydrogen atoms and Gasteiger charges were then added to the protein using AutoDock Tools 1.5.6. The docking simulation was carried out with Vina 2.0 integrated within the PyRx platform to calculate the binding affinity. The resulting docking poses were visualized and analyzed using PyMOL.

Animal experiments

Six-week-old male BALB/c nude mice (Wenzhou Medical University) were maintained in SPF conditions. Mice were subcutaneously inoculated in the right flank with 200 µL of HepG2 cell suspensions (1 × 10⁷ cells/mL) carrying either DUSP1 knockdown and EZH2 overexpression, and were subsequently randomized into four groups (n = 6 per group). Crebanine was prepared in DMSO and diluted with saline. Once the tumor size neared 100 mm3, the mice received an intraperitoneal administration of saline or crebanine (10 mg/kg) every two days. Tumor volume and body weight were evaluated bi-daily. After 18 days of crebanine treatment, the mice were anesthetized and photographed. Following euthanasia, tumors were resected, photographed alongside a scale ruler, and weighed.

Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) and Immunohistochemical (IHC) staining

Tissues were fixed with paraformaldehyde, dehydrated, embedded in paraffin, and sectioned as previously described (Zhang et al. 2021). Slides were subjected to H&E staining for histological evaluation. Tissue sections were processed via deparaffinization, rehydration and antigen retrieval for IHC analysis, and subsequently incubated with primary antibodies (Table S2) overnight at 4 °C. After rinsing, tissue sections were incubated with species-matched secondary antibodies at room temperature for 30 min. Chromogenic development was conducted using 3,3'-diaminobenzidine (DAB) substrate, followed by hematoxylin counterstaining. Specimens were dehydrated through graded ethanol and xylene, then mounted with neutral balsam.

Statistical analysis

We analyzed all data using GraphPad Prism software for comprehensive statistical evaluation. Statistical analyses were performed using Student's t-test (two groups) or one-way ANOVA (three or more groups) followed by Tukey's post-hoc test for multiple comparisons. Data were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD) and obtained it from a minimum of three distinct investigations. Results with p < 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Results

Crebanine suppresses HCC cell proliferation

Cell viability assays were first performed to evaluate the anti-proliferative potential of crebanine (Fig. 1a) against HCC cells. As shown in Fig. 1b, crebanine remarkedly reduced the cell viability of HCC cells in a dose-dependent manner, with IC₅₀ values of 46.52 μM for Huh7 and 76.62 μM for HepG2 at 24 h. Of note, normal hepatic LO2 cells displayed markedly higher tolerance to crebanine, with an IC₅₀ of 152.2 μM, indicating the favorable selective cytotoxicity of crebanine toward HCC cells. To further validate the inhibitory effect of crebanine on HCC proliferation, we conducted colony formation and EdU incorporation assays, which reflect long-term proliferative capacity and DNA synthesis activity. Consistent with the cell viability results, crebanine treatment significantly reduced colony formation numbers (Fig. 1c) and the proportion of EdU-positive HCC cells (Fig. 1d). To explore the intrinsic mechanism driving the proliferation suppression, we further detected alterations in cell cycle distribution. Flow cytometry analysis demonstrated that crebanine efficiently blocked G1-to-S phase transition, as evidenced by an obvious accumulation of cells in the G0/G1 phase and a concomitant reduction of cells in S and G2/M phases (Fig. 1e). Meanwhile, the key G1-phase regulatory proteins CyclinD1 and CDK4 were significantly downregulated following crebanine intervention (Fig. 1f). Collectively, these results confirm that crebanine exerts robust anti-proliferative effects on HCC cells by triggering G1-phase cell cycle arrest.

Crebanine promotes HCC cell apoptosis

To characterize the pro-apoptotic property of crebanine in HCC cells, we firstly observed cellular morphological changes and quantified apoptotic cell populations via flow cytometry. Compared with the control group, crebanine treatment distinctly altered cell morphology and obviously elevated the proportions of both early (Annexin V⁺/PI⁻) and late (Annexin V⁺/PI⁺) apoptotic cells (Fig. 2b). The enhanced number of TUNEL-positive cells further consolidated the pro-apoptotic capacity of crebanine (Fig. 2c). Mechanistically, mitochondrial apoptosis is primarily modulated by Bcl-2 (anti-apoptotic) and Bax (pro-apoptotic). Western blot analysis revealed that crebanine treatment remarkably elevated the Bax/Bcl-2 expression ratio (Fig. 2d). Taken together, these evidences fully illustrate that crebanine triggers mitochondrial-dependent apoptotic signaling cascades to facilitate HCC cell apoptosis.

Crebanine suppresses HCC cell migration and invasion

To evaluate crebanine’s effect on HCC cell metastasis, wound healing and Transwell invasion assays were conducted. Data showed that crebanine evidently restrained cell migration and invasion abilities, as reflected by impaired wound closure capacity (Fig. 3a) and reduced invasive cell number (Fig. 3b). Epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) is a core biological process driving tumor metastasis. To clarify the underlying molecular mechanism, we further detected the expression levels of typical EMT-related markers. Crebanine treatment markedly elevated epithelial biomarker E-cadherin, whereas mesenchymal markers vimentin, α-SMA and Snail were sharply decreased (Fig. 3c). Collectively, crebanine restrains HCC cell migration and invasion by reversing EMT progression.

Crebanine blunts HCC cell malignancy through DUSP1-Akt signaling

Accumulated evidences has demonstrated that DUSP1 is markedly downregulated in HCC tissues, and its high expression is closely correlated with better clinical prognosis of HCC patients (Calvisi et al. 2008; Tsujita et al. 2005), which prompted us to further explore the regulatory relationship between crebanine and DUSP1. Indeed, crebanine treatment markedly upregulated DUSP1 expression and the levels of its downstream mediators p-ERK1/2 and p-p38, whereas it significantly inhibited the phosphorylation of Akt and JNK1/2 (Fig. 4a, Figs. S1a–c). To confirm whether DUSP1 serves as the essential functional mediator of crebanine, we further conducted loss-of-function experiments via DUSP1 knockdown. As expected, genetic knockdown of DUSP1 successfully abolished the suppressive effects of crebanine on HCC cell proliferation, apoptosis induction, migration and invasion capacity (Figs. 4b–f, Figs. S1d–g). Consistent with these changes, DUSP1 deficiency also largely reversed the molecular alterations triggered by crebanine, including restored expression of p-Akt, Snail and α-SMA, as well as recovered levels of Bax/Bcl-2 ratio and E-cadherin (Fig. 4g, Figs. S1h–l). Collectively, these results solidly confirm that crebanine restrains malignant biological behaviors of HCC cells predominantly through modulating the DUSP1-Akt signaling axis.

DUSP1 mediates the HCC inhibitory potential of crebanine in vivo

Subcutaneous xenograft models were constructed to further validate the in vivo anti-HCC efficacy of crebanine and confirm its reliance on DUSP1 signaling. Consistent with our in vitro observations, crebanine treatment significantly suppressed the growth of HCC xenograft tumors, whereas DUSP1 knockdown markedly compromised the tumor-suppressive capacity of crebanine (Fig. 5a–d). Body weight monitoring demonstrated that the applied dosage of crebanine exhibited good in vivo tolerability, as mice in all experimental groups maintained steady body weight gain without obvious systemic toxicity (Fig. 5e). H&E staining further confirmed that crebanine ameliorated the malignant pathological features of tumor tissues, and this improvement was also substantially offset by DUSP1 depletion (Fig. 5f). Moreover, crebanine treatment reduced Ki67 expression and elevated the number of TUNEL-positive cells in tumor tissues, indicative of suppressed proliferation and enhanced apoptosis. However, DUSP1 knockdown effectively abolished these beneficial effects induced by crebanine (Fig. 5f, g). Taken together, these data strongly support that crebanine impedes HCC progression in vivo by upregulating DUSP1 expression.

Crebanine blunts HCC progression via the EZH2-DUSP1-AKT axis

To further elucidate the upstream molecular mechanism by which crebanine modulates DUSP1 signaling, we screened key reported upstream regulators of DUSP1, including EZH2, E2F6, and HDAC1 (Chen et al. 2020; Jing et al. 2021; Lin et al. 2015). We demonstrated that crebanine significantly downregulated EZH2 expression in HCC cells (Fig. 6a, Fig. S2a), while exerting no obvious regulatory effect on E2F6 and HDAC1 levels (Fig. S2c). These results suggest that EZH2 might serve as a specific upstream mediator responsible for crebanine-induced DUSP1 activation. Subsequent gain-of-function experiments were performed to verify the regulatory axis between EZH2, DUSP1 and AKT. As expected, forced overexpression of EZH2 (Fig. 6b, Fig. S2b) effectively reversed the molecular changes induced by crebanine, including the crebanine-mediated upregulation of DUSP1 and downregulation of p-Akt (Fig. 6c, Fig. S2d–f). In addition, EZH2 overexpression also markedly abolished the biological functions of crebanine. Specifically, the inhibitory effects of crebanine on HCC cell proliferation, migration and invasion, as well as its pro-apoptotic activity, were substantially impaired following EZH2 overexpression (Figs. 6d–g, Figs. S2g–i). Further detection of EMT and apoptosis-related markers confirmed these phenotypic alterations. EZH2 overexpression abrogated crebanine-induced upregulation of E-cadherin and Bax/Bcl-2 ratio, and restored the expression of vimentin, α-SMA and Snail (Fig. 6h, Fig. S2j–n). Molecular docking analysis was further conducted to verify the direct binding relationship between crebanine and EZH2. The results showed that crebanine could stably bind to EZH2 protein by forming hydrogen bonds with TYR574 and LYS602 residues, as well as Pi-Cation and Pi-Anion interactions with LYS612 and GLU636 residues, respectively. The favorable binding energy of −7.5 kcal/mol further confirmed the high binding affinity between crebanine and EZH2 (Fig. 6i). Collectively, these results demonstrate that crebanine suppresses the malignant progression of HCC via targeting the EZH2-DUSP1-Akt signaling cascade.

The EZH2-DUSP1 axis mediates the HCC inhibitory potential of crebanine in vivo

To consolidate the in vitro mechanistic findings, we further validated the EZH2-dependent anti-tumor mechanism of crebanine in xenograft tumor models. Notably, EZH2 intrinsically acted as a driver that accelerated HCC progression. Ectopic overexpression of EZH2 largely abolished the anti-tumor efficacy of crebanine against HCC tumor growth (Figs. 7a–e). IHC and TUNEL staining analyses further revealed the underlying molecular changes. The decreased Ki67 level, elevated TUNEL-positive cells, and upregulated DUSP1 expression triggered by crebanine treatment were significantly rescued upon EZH2 overexpression (Fig. 7f, g). In summary, these results strongly corroborate that crebanine exerts its inhibitory effects on HCC progression via modulating the EZH2-DUSP1 signaling axis.

Discussion

Our previous study had validated the aberrant expression and vital biological function of DUSP1 in the initiation and malignant progression of HCC (Wen et al. 2025). We further confirmed that upregulation of DUSP1 could remarkably restrain HCC progression (Wen et al. 2025), thereby indicating that DUSP1 represents a promising therapeutic target and confers great clinical translational potential for HCC intervention. Here we found that crebanine markedly elevates endogenous DUSP1 expression in HCC cells, and also exerts satisfactory anti-HCC efficacy. It has been noted earlier that crebanine modulates the ROS-AKT-FoxO3a signaling in hepatic HepG2 cells (Tan et al. 2023). However, this mechanism appears modest as it only participates in regulating the impact of crebanine on apoptosis, and the anti-HCC efficacy of crebanine was only validated in a single cell line. In this study, we demonstrate for the first time that crebanine represses HCC malignancies both in vitro and in vivo, and provide the fundamental processes underlying for these effects of crebanine, that is, via modulation of the EZH2-DUSP1-Akt signaling pathway (Fig. 8).

Schematic diagram illustrating the anti-tumor mechanisms of crebanine in HCC

DUSP1/MKP1, as a member of DUSPs family, negatively regulates the activity of MAPK pathway (Franklin et al. 2000). We show that crebanine treatment upregulates DUSP1 expression and accordingly inhibits JNK1/2 signaling, while enhancing ERK1/2 and p38 signaling in HCC. The differential outcome may result from a collective modulation by multiple DUSPs (Mills et al. 2017). Crebanine still exerts a portion of its effects against HCC malignancies despite the downregulation of DUSP1. However, the majority of its effects have been eliminated under this circumstance, suggesting that DUSP1 is a key, though not the only, downstream gene of crebanine. ROS-AKT-FoxO3a signal might also contribute to the therapeutic potential of crebanine against HCC (Tan et al. 2023).

Emerging evidence suggests crosstalk between Akt signaling and DUSP1 regulation. E2F6 promotes Akt activation by inhibiting DUSP1 expression (Jing et al. 2021). Moreover, enforced DUSP1 expression suppresses Akt phosphorylation and provokes p53/p16-mediated senescence (Cheng et al. 2018). Furthermore, the CD133 signal drives PI3K-AKT-DUSP1-p38 and PI3K-AKT-DUSP1-JNK pathways, contributing to melanoma resistance to fotemustine (Jamal et al. 2020). Tuning the DUSP1-AKT axis represents a promising and broadly applicable strategy for cancer therapy.

EZH2 exerts epigenetic suppression by catalyzing methylation of lysine 27 of histone H3 (H3K27), thereby promoting oncogene activation or suppressing tumor-suppressor gene function (Bai et al. 2022). Multiple EZH2 inhibitors have demonstrated promising anti-tumor efficacy across different cancer types (Italiano et al. 2018; Knutson et al. 2013). Targeting EZH2 has emerged as a promising therapeutic strategy for HCC. It has been noted that the loss of EZH2 results in significant upregulation of DUSP1 at both mRNA and protein levels in NSCLC (Chen et al. 2020). Mechanistically, EZH2 attaches to the DUSP1 promoter region and induces H3K27 trimethylation (Bai et al. 2022). E2F6 acts as a transcriptional repressor by interacting with the DUSP1 promoter region (Jing et al. 2021). DUSP1 upregulation via inhibiting HDAC1 has been reported to boost gefitinib responsiveness to overcome resistance in non-small cell lung cancers. The mechanism is connected with the HDAC1-mediated deacetylation of DUSP1 and the subsequent increase of MAPK signaling (Lin et al. 2015). However, both E2F6 and HDAC1 appear not to be involved in the anti-HCC effect of crebanine.

Numerous studies have proven that crebanine modulates oxidative stress response (Mohammed et al. 2026; Tan et al. 2023; Zhang et al. 2026; Zhao et al. 2025) and participates in AKT pathway regulation (Intayoung et al. 2016; Tan et al. 2023; Yeh et al. 2024). Consistent with these findings, our study also verified that crebanine could interfere with AKT signaling in HCC cells. It should be noticed that EZH2 is capable of eliminating intracellular ROS and further forming a feedback regulatory loop among EZH2, Akt and ROS (Yu et al. 2021). Within this work, we verified that ectopic overexpression of EZH2 could effectively abrogate crebanine-mediated downregulation of Akt phosphorylation, as well as its suppressive effects on the malignant biological behaviors of HCC cells. Nevertheless, it still remains to be further clarified whether EZH2 exerts such antagonistic effects mainly through scavenging intracellular ROS, or directly counteracts the pharmacological function of crebanine independent of ROS regulation. Therefore, more in-depth studies are warranted to decipher the interplay mechanism of EZH2-related axis and ROS-Akt pathway in HCC malignant progression.

Conclusion

In summary, our study reveals a novel therapeutic mechanism of crebanine in HCC, demonstrating its action in enhancing DUSP1 expression through EZH2 attenuation, thus leading to Akt inactivation and the inhibition of HCC tumor malignancy. Our findings indicate that crebanine is potentially a new medication option for treating HCC.
