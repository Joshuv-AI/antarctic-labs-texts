Physician- and Patient-Reported Outcomes Following Contemporary Image Guided Intensity Modulated Proton Therapy for Prostate Cancer: Results From the Prospective PRO-Cube Study
Authors: Sham Sundar, Srinivas Chilukuri, Manikandan Arjunan, Ramakrishna Kamath, Abhilasha Sinha, Mahalakshmi Palani, Pankaj Kumar Panda, Sujith K. Mullapally, Srivathsan Ramani, Narasimhan Ragavan, Prashant Jawahar, Jaykanth Amalachandran, Dayananda Shamurailatpam Sharma, Rakesh Jalali
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeMultiple prospective studies have established the safety of proton therapy for localized prostate cancer. However, prospective data defining physician-reported adverse events (AEs) and patient-reported outcomes (PROs) with contemporary image guided intensity modulated proton therapy (IG-IMPT) in high-risk and node-positive disease treated with hypofractionation, including extreme hypofractionation (stereotactic body proton therapy) and pelvic nodal irradiation (PNI), remain limited. We report prospectively collected clinical outcomes from the PRO-Cube registry.Methods and MaterialsPRO-Cube is a prospective, single-center registry enrolling men with prostate cancer treated with IG-IMPT across all risk groups using hypofractionation schedules with or without PNI. This analysis included 155 consecutive patients treated definitively using daily cone beam computed tomography–guided robustly optimized intensity modulated proton therapy. Physician-reported genitourinary (GU) and gastrointestinal (GI) AEs were graded using the National Cancer Institute Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events (CTCAE) version 5.0, and PROs were analyzed longitudinally usingEuropean Organisation for Research and Treatment of Cancer Quality of Life Questionnaire – Prostate Cancer Module - 25 items (EORTC QLQ-PR25) with mixed-effects linear models to characterize temporal symptom trajectories and minimal clinically important difference.ResultsMedian age was 70 years (IQR, 62-75); 73.6% had high or very high-risk disease, including 24.5% node-positive. Stereotactic body proton therapy was used in 46.5%, PNI in 60%, and androgen deprivation therapy in 87.1% of patients. With a median follow-up of 35.8 months (IQR, 24-52), acute grade ≥2 GU AEs occurred in 23.9%, driven primarily by transient irritative urinary symptoms, while no acute grade ≥2 GI AEs were observed. Cumulative late grade ≥2 GU and GI AEs were infrequent, occurring in 5% and 4.3%, respectively. Severe late AEs (grade ≥3) were rare (<1%). PRO trajectories demonstrated early posttreatment worsening in urinary and bowel symptoms, followed by gradual improvement over time, concordant with physician-reported AEs. No patient-, tumor-, or treatment-related factors were associated with higher AEs or adverse PROs.ConclusionsIG-IMPT demonstrated low rates of physician-reported AEs and favorable PRO trajectories, despite frequent use of hypofractionation and PNI in a predominantly high-risk and node-positive cohort. These findings establish a benchmark for modern intensity modulated proton therapy practice and provide a rationale for prospective comparison with contemporary intensity modulated radiation therapy.

Introduction

Radiation therapy (RT) represents a standard of care in the management of localized and locally advanced prostate cancer and is commonly employed in selected patients with oligometastatic disease.1 The therapeutic objective is to deliver tumoricidal doses to the prostate, with or without pelvic nodal irradiation (PNI), while minimizing exposure to surrounding organs at risk (OARs). Proton beam therapy (PBT), owing to its distinct physical dose distribution, has the potential to reduce radiation dose to most OARs compared with photon-based techniques.2 Despite these theoretical advantages, the role of PBT in prostate cancer remains debated for several reasons. First, the capital, operational, and maintenance costs of PBT facilities are substantially higher than those of linear accelerators delivering modern intensity modulated radiation therapy (IMRT).3,4 Second, physical and biological uncertainties inherent to PBT may partially offset its dosimetric benefits.5 Third, earlier PBT systems lacked volumetric image guidance, potentially resulting in treatment plans that were comparable to or inferior to current-day IMRT plans. Finally, some argue that adverse event (AE) rates achieved with the recent IMRT techniques are sufficiently low that they may not justify a more complex and resource-intensive modality such as PBT.

Although the PARTIQoL trial demonstrated the long-term safety and efficacy of PBT, it did not show superiority over IMRT.6,7 Importantly, nearly half of the patients in that trial were treated using passive scattering; all patients receiving PBT were treated without cone beam computed tomography (CT) guidance, conventional fractionation was predominantly used, and PNI was not delivered, as high-risk patients were excluded. However, contemporary PBT offers several advantages over both IMRT and earlier proton delivery techniques. Pencil-beam scanning PBT with integrated cone beam CT (CBCT) guidance enables the use of crisp treatment margins of approximately 3 to 5 mm, comparable to those used in current IMRT. Modern PBT plans have been shown to substantially reduce intermediate- to low-dose exposure to surrounding OARs while maintaining conformity comparable to or better than IMRT.8, 9, 10 These dose ranges are clinically relevant, as intermediate- and low-dose exposure of the rectum and bladder has been associated with gastrointestinal (GI) AEs such as bowel frequency, incontinence, and rectal pain, as well as genitourinary (GU) AEs including dysuria, urgency, frequency, and incontinence.11, 12, 13, 14 This dosimetric advantage becomes increasingly relevant as hypofractionated and ultrahypofractionated regimens are adopted, especially in the context of PNI, where modest increases in grade 2 toxicities have been reported.15,16 In this setting, the dosimetric advantages of PBT could potentially translate into clinically meaningful reductions in treatment-related AEs. Additionally, the lower integral dose associated with PBT, particularly in patients with high-risk disease, may mitigate radiation-related lymphopenia and its impact on circulating T-cell populations, with potential implications for clinical outcomes.17

Against this background, there remains limited prospective evidence which report physician-reported AE and patient-reported outcome (PRO) of contemporary image guided intensity modulated proton therapy (IG-IMPT), particularly in cohorts enriched for high-risk and node-positive disease, frequent PNI, and hypofractionated regimens, including extreme hypofractionation. To address this gap, the PRO-Cube prospective registry was initiated at our center in 2020 to systematically capture these outcomes in men undergoing PBT for prostate cancer. The present analysis reports physician AEs and PROs from the first 155 patients treated with definitive IMPT for intact prostate cancer within this registry, providing a benchmark for AEs with modern proton therapy in prostate cancer using hypofractionated regimens.

Methods and Materials

The PRO-Cube registry is a prospective, single-center observational study enrolling men with prostate cancer treated with IG-IMPT. The registry captures outcomes across definitive and postoperative settings using conventional, moderate, and extreme hypofractionation, with or without PNI. The study protocol was approved by the institutional ethics committee, and all participants provided written informed consent. This analysis includes the first 155 consecutively enrolled patients treated with definitive PBT. The primary endpoint was physician-reported AEs. Secondary endpoints included PROs and disease outcomes: biochemical progression-free survival (bPFS), metastasis-free survival, and overall survival.

Eligible patients were men with histologically confirmed prostate adenocarcinoma across all risk groups (low, intermediate, high, and very high) and selected oligometastatic disease cases planned for PBT. No restrictions were placed on prior transurethral resection, comorbidities, or systemic therapies, except prior pelvic RT, reflecting a real-world cohort. Androgen deprivation therapy, with or without androgen receptor pathway inhibitors, was administered as clinically indicated. During the initial phase of the registry, patients were predominantly treated with moderate hypofractionation, with or without PNI, as clinically indicated. Extreme hypofractionation (stereotactic body proton therapy [SBPT]) was introduced in a stepwise manner, initially for prostate-only treatment. With increasing institutional experience, SBPT was subsequently extended to selected patients receiving prostate and PNI. Eligibility for extreme hypofractionation required a baseline International Prostate Symptom Score of ≤14.

Simulation

Patients were simulated supine with indexed knee and ankle support for immobilization. Bowel preparation (laxatives) and a comfortably full bladder protocol were used for CT simulation and daily treatment. An endorectal balloon (ERB) filled with 90 to 120 mL of normal saline was inserted, with the tip positioned above the prostate base, as confirmed on a check CT scan. The ERB stopper level was documented to ensure reproducibility. CT simulation was acquired with a 2 mm slice thickness from L2 to midthigh. Volumetric pelvic magnetic resonance imaging (T2-weighted and diffusion-weighted sequences) was acquired using the same ERB protocol for coregistration with planning CT.

Target and OAR delineation

The planning CT and pelvic magnetic resonance imaging were registered prior to contouring. The clinical target volume (CTV) for prostate (CTV-P) included the entire prostate and proximal 1 cm of seminal vesicles, or the entire seminal vesicles in T3b disease. Grossly visible lymph nodes on planning CT were contoured as nodal gross tumor volume. Patients with high-risk disease per National Comprehensive Cancer Network criteria1 or >20% nodal involvement risk (Roach formula) received elective nodal irradiation. The nodal CTV included common iliac lymph nodes from the great vessel bifurcation, external iliac, internal iliac, and obturator nodes,18 plus a 5-mm isometric expansion around nodal gross tumor volume. Treatment plans used 5-mm robustness settings, ensuring CTV D95 ≥95% of the prescribed dose, except 3 mm posteriorly for CTV-P. Treatment planning

All treatment plans were created using RayStation (version 12A, RaySearch Laboratories AB). For CTV-P only cases, 2 opposing lateral fields (90° and 270°) with multifield optimization were used. For PNI, a 3-field multifield optimization strategy was employed. Spot assignment was configured such that ipsilateral lateral and posterior fields each treated their respective nodal regions without contralateral beam crossover, while all 3 fields contributed to central nodal coverage (Fig. E1). In obese patients or those with abdominal skin folds in the beam path, tilted postero-lateral beams were used. Dose calculation used the Monte Carlo algorithm (version 4.4) with a 3 × 3 × 3 mm grid size.18 Doses were reported as cobalt gray equivalent (CGE), assuming a relative biological effectiveness of 1.1. Spot spacing was 1.06 times the average projected sigma with institutional scaling applied. All targets were robustly optimized using minimax robust optimization, accounting for 5 mm translational uncertainties, except 3 mm posteriorly, and 3.5% range uncertainty.19,20

Image guidance and treatment delivery

Daily on-board CBCT was performed to confirm target alignment, pelvic setup, and bladder filling. Particular attention was paid to anatomy posterior to the prostate along the ERB and within proton beam paths (femoral head position and body contour changes), given proton range sensitivity. Treatments were corrected with couch shifts or paused for replanning if anatomy was unacceptable. Treatment was delivered using an IBA ProteusPLUS pencil-beam scanning system (IBA) with rotating gantry and robotic patient-positioning couch.

Physician- and patient-reported assessments

Physician-reported AEs were prospectively recorded by a radiation oncologist using National Cancer Institute Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events (CTCAE) version 5.0 21 at baseline, during treatment, end of treatment, and at 3, 6, 12, 24, 36, 48, and 60 months follow-up. Follow-up evaluations included symptom assessment, toxicity evaluation, and serum Prostate specfic antigen (PSA) monitoring via in-person or telehealth consultations. Patient-reported quality of life (QOL) was assessed using the European Organisation for Research and Treatment of Cancer Quality of Life Questionnaire – Prostate Cancer Module - 25 items (EORTC QLQ-PR25),22 focusing on urinary and bowel symptom scales, at the same scheduled follow-up windows. Expected PRO assessments were defined according to whether patients had reached the corresponding follow-up window within the available data set. Items were scored per the EORTC scoring manual, with raw scores linearly transformed to a 0 to 100 scale. Minimal clinically important difference (MCID) was defined as the absolute change from baseline of ≥10 or ≥20 points, evaluated at each timepoint and cumulatively across follow-up.23, 24, 25

Biochemical-clinical failure was defined as the first occurrence of clinical failure (local, regional, or distant recurrence) or biochemical failure per Phoenix criteria26 (PSA >2 ng/mL above nadir). Whole-body prostate-specific membrane antigen positron emission tomography–computed tomography (PSMA PET-CT) was typically performed at biochemical-clinical failure or when PSA exceeded 1 ng/mL to identify sites of active disease.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics summarized baseline patient, disease, and treatment characteristics. Physician-reported acute and late GU and GI AEs were evaluated separately. Median follow-up was estimated using the reverse Kaplan-Meier method. Analyses were performed in Python (Python Software Foundation) using pandas, NumPy, SciPy, and statsmodels, after importing the SPSS data set (IBM SPSS Statistics). Associations between predictors and AE outcomes were assessed using Fisher’s exact test (categorical variables) and logistic regression (continuous variables), reporting odds ratios with 95% CIs. Haldane-Anscombe correction was applied for sparse data. Multilevel categorical predictors were assessed using likelihood-ratio tests with estimates relative to reference categories. Mann-Whitney U and Spearman correlation tests were used for nonparametric comparisons.

Survival outcomes were estimated using the Kaplan-Meier method per Phoenix criteria.26 Longitudinal QOL scores were analyzed using mixed-effects linear models with time and clinical/treatment factors as fixed effects and patient random intercepts. All tests were 2-sided, with P <.05 considered statistically significant.

Results

In this prospective cohort of 155 patients, the median follow-up was 35.8 months (95% CI, 21.0-39.8). Patient, disease, and treatment characteristics are summarized in Table 1. High-risk or very high-risk disease predominated (73.6%), with nodal involvement in 24.5%. Androgen deprivation therapy was administered in 87.1%; 67.1% received long-term therapy, and 24.5% received an androgen receptor pathway inhibitor. Fractionation was balanced between moderate hypofractionation (53.5%) and extreme hypofractionation (46.5%), with median doses of 68 CGE and 36.25 CGE, respectively. PNI was delivered in 60% overall: 40.6% with moderate hypofractionation and 19.4% with extreme hypofractionation. Acute physician-reported AEs

Acute GU AEs at the end of treatment comprised grade 1 (39 of 155, 25.2%) and grade 2 (36 of 155, 23.2%) events, with no grade 3 AEs (Table 2). By 3 months, grade 1 GU AEs persisted in 17.9% (27 of 151), whereas grade 2 events had largely resolved (2 of 151, 1.3%). GU AEs were numerically higher with PNI versus prostate-only treatment at the end of treatment (any grade, 52.7% vs 41.9%) and 3 months (22.2% vs 14.8%), though not statistically significant.

GU AEs did not differ significantly between moderate and extreme hypofractionation at any time point. In paired within-patient analyses from the end of treatment to 3 months, moderate hypofractionation showed numerically higher improvement than extreme hypofractionation (48.2% vs 38.2%; P = .391) (Fig. 1a). Subdomain analyses indicated grade ≥2 acute GU AEs at the end of treatment were predominantly cystitis-like symptoms (23%), urinary frequency (20%), dysuria (18%), and urgency (10%), and were uncommon by 3 months (≤1%-2% overall) (Fig. 1b). Acute GI AEs were infrequent, limited to grade 1 proctitis at the end of treatment (11 of 155, 7.1%), with no events at 3 months and no grade >2 AEs (Fig. E2). Late physician-reported AEs

Physician-reported late AEs were infrequent and predominantly low grade. Late GU AEs showed modest early rise in grade 1 symptoms between 6 to 24 months, while grade ≥2 events remained uncommon (Fig. 2a), with 5.0% cumulative incidence for late grade >2 GU AEs (Fig. 2b). Late GI AEs were also rare, with grade >2 events occurring mainly between 12 to 18 months (Fig. 3a) and 4.3% cumulative incidence (Fig. 3b). Grade 3 events in either domain were <1%. Subdomain analyses indicated late grade ≥2 GU AEs were primarily cystitis-like symptoms, whereas late grade ≥2 GI AEs were largely rectal bleeding (Fig. 3a, b). PROs

Across scheduled EORTC QLQ-PR25 assessment windows, 535 of 750 expected assessments were completed (71.3% form-level compliance), and 137 patients contributed baseline plus ≥1 follow-up for change-from-baseline analyses. Compliance was highest early (baseline and 3 months) and declined with longer follow-up (Fig. E4). MCID analyses demonstrated modest urinary change rates (Δ>10: ∼14%-25%; Δ>20: ∼5%-18%) and infrequent bowel MCID events (Δ>10: ∼5.4%; Δ>20: ∼1.4%) (Fig. E5a, b). Using a Δ = 10 threshold, most patients were stable at each follow-up (urinary ∼75%-86%; bowel ∼95%-100%), with low worsened proportions. Cumulatively, urinary status was 70% stable, 13% improved, and 17% worsened, while bowel status was 96% stable and 4% worsened (Fig. E6a, b).

Exploratory subgroup analyses showed similar urinary trajectories by nodal irradiation and fractionation; urinary symptoms were numerically higher among patients with grade >2 GU AEs, though estimates at later timepoints were limited by small subgroup counts (Fig. E7a-c). In univariate testing, no clinical or treatment factor predicted urinary or bowel QOL change or MCID (all P > .05; Table E3 and E4).

Oncologic outcomes

The estimated 3-year bPFS was 98.6% (Fig. E8a). Two biochemical failures occurred at 24 and 48 months, both representing nodal recurrences outside the prior radiation field: one in a high-risk patient with nodal relapse above the aortic bifurcation, and another in a low-risk patient treated without elective nodal irradiation. No in-field local recurrences or metastatic events were observed. At 3 years, overall survival was 99.3%; 2 deaths occurred from nonprostate cancer causes (Fig. E8b).

Discussion

In this prospective registry, volumetric IG-IMPT was associated with low rates of acute and late GU and GI AEs, despite frequent use of hypofractionation, including extreme hypofractionation, and PNI in a cohort with predominantly high-risk and node-positive disease. Acute grade ≥2 GU AEs occurred in approximately 24% of patients, with no acute grade ≥2 GI AEs, while cumulative late grade ≥2 GU and GI AEs were observed in only 5% and 4.3%, respectively. Severe late AEs were rare (<1%). Longitudinal PRO trajectories were concordant with physician-reported AEs, characterized by transient early symptom worsening followed by recovery over time, without evidence of persistent urinary or bowel morbidity attributable to fractionation strategy or nodal irradiation.

To contextualize these findings, recent photon-based trials provide relevant reference standards. Both moderate hypofractionation and ultrahypofractionation have been established as viable strategies with acceptable AEs in predominantly prostate-only settings. When PNI is incorporated, randomized studies such as POP-RT and PIVOTAL have reported modestly higher late grade ≥2 GU and GI AE rates, reflecting larger treatment volumes.15,27, 28, 29, 30 For extreme hypofractionation, HYPO-RT-PC and PACE-B together demonstrate overall low rates of severe AEs, while highlighting the influence of treatment volume and follow-up duration on late AE burden.16,31,32 Our SBPT regimen used 36.25 CGE in 5 fractions without intraprostatic or whole-prostate dose escalation, in keeping with the PRIME trial33 regimen from India. Although the absence of PACE-B-style 40 Gy/5-fraction dose escalation may have contributed to lower GU toxicity, it is unlikely to be the major explanation, as NRG-GU005 34 also used 36.25 Gy in 5 fractions and still identified urinary QOL decline as a clinically relevant endpoint. The favorable GU profile observed in our cohort likely reflects the combined contribution of dose selection, daily CBCT guidance, ERB immobilization, robust optimization, and IMPT dosimetry.

Prior prospective PBT studies offer more direct comparisons, but with important methodological distinctions. Most have enrolled predominantly low- to intermediate-risk patients treated to the prostate-only using conventional or moderate hypofractionation. Within this context, the PARTIQoL trial provided important prospective evidence supporting the long-term safety of PBT relative to IMRT, with approximately half of patients treated using moderate hypofractionation.6,7 Similarly, the University of Florida’s image guided PBT series demonstrated favorable long-term control and acceptable toxicity profiles.35 However, both were limited to prostate-only treatment without PNI, and neither employed robust optimization or daily volumetric image guidance characteristic of contemporary IG-IMPT.36

The PCG GU002 trial comparing conventional fractionation versus SBPT reported 5-year cumulative late grade ≥2 GU AEs in 37% and 44% of patients, respectively, with late grade ≥2 GI AEs in 29% and 34%, respectively.37 Although late grade ≥3 events were infrequent, these rates highlight substantial persistent morbidity, likely reflecting multi-institutional variability in proton platforms, image guidance, and planning workflows.

Prospective data incorporating PNI were reported through the Proton Collaborative Group REG001-09 registry38, which included high-risk patients treated with PBT to prostate and pelvic lymph nodes using conventional fractionation and rectal spacer. Acute grade ≥2 GU and GI AEs occurred in 30% to 35% and <5%, respectively, while late grade ≥2 GI and GU AEs occurred in 5% and 7.5%, respectively.36,37 Another phase 2 prospective study from Mayo Clinic using moderately hypofractionated IMPT with elective PNI, similar to our approach, reported late grade ≥2 GI and GU toxicity of 7.4% and 20.5% at 2 years, and 29.2% at 3 years.39 With longer follow-up (median, 62 months), 5-year late grade ≥2 GI and GU AEs were 16% and 41%, respectively.40

Our cohort was heterogeneous and included prostate-only treatment, but a substantial subset received PNI, and we additionally report outcomes after extreme hypofractionation with PNI using proton therapy, for which prospective toxicity evidence remains limited. Also, the present PRO-Cube registry differs importantly in employing uniform pencil-beam scanning, daily CBCT, ERB, and robust optimization across all patients. This standardized approach achieved cumulative late grade ≥2 GU and GI AE rates of 5% and 4.3%, respectively. Although longer follow-up may reveal additional late events, current trends demonstrate excellent tolerance. Importantly, in univariate analysis, no patient-, tumor-, or treatment-related factors, including fractionation regimen, PNI, age, diabetes, or prostate volume, were associated with acute or late GU or GI AEs, suggesting that favorable outcomes with IG-IMPT are achievable across diverse clinical scenarios.

No patients underwent rectal spacer placement. The absence of acute grade ≥2 GI toxicity and low incidence of late grade ≥2 GI AEs in this cohort raises important questions regarding the incremental value of rectal spacers when IG-IMPT is delivered with ERB immobilization. The saline-filled ERB provides effective prostate immobilization while consistently displacing the posterior rectal wall, achieving substantial sparing of rectal and rectal wall volumes, particularly in the intermediate- and low-dose ranges.39, 40, 41 Additionally, ERB immobilization minimizes interfractional rectal shape and position variability, decreasing discordance between planned and delivered rectal doses. Continued evolution of PBT planning, including linear energy transfer–based optimization and variable relative biological effectiveness modeling, may further reduce physical-biological dose discordance and enhance normal tissue sparing.42,43

A key strength of the present study is the availability of longitudinal PROs collected alongside prospectively assessed physician-reported AEs. The completeness of longitudinal PRO capture (71.3% overall) is comparable to expected attrition in PR25 validation work, where completion decreased over time.22 In this prospective cohort, PR25 symptom trajectories showed early urinary symptom signal with subsequent stabilization, while bowel symptoms remained low throughout follow-up. This pattern—early, transient urinary worsening after prostate RT followed by recovery toward baseline—has been reported across prostate RT modalities, including hypofractionated approaches, where acute urinary symptoms typically peak soon after treatment and improve by 6 months in most patients.44, 45, 46, 47, 48, 49 In contrast, bowel symptoms remained near 0 with only a small, time-limited rise, supporting low late bowel symptom burden with contemporary IG-IMPT.

To interpret change magnitudes, we evaluated thresholds of 10 and 20 points, aligning with widely used EORTC-based interpretive anchors where 5 to 10 points represent a small change, 10 to 20 points represent a moderate change, and >20 points represent a large change.25 The absence of detectable clinical or treatment predictors for urinary/bowel change or MCID in univariate analyses should be interpreted in context of modest event rates and diminishing denominators at later timepoints—a common limitation in long-term PRO series.50, 51, 52 Even with PNI, our bowel findings are directionally consistent with comparative PRO literature, suggesting proton-based approaches may mitigate bowel QOL decrement relative to IMRT, while urinary domains are often similar between modalities.53

At a median follow-up of 36 months, 3-year bPFS was 98.6% in this predominant high-risk, node-positive cohort. No in-field failures occurred; both biochemical recurrences represented out-of-field nodal progression, supporting adequate target coverage and dose prescription. Although follow-up remains limited for definitive long-term control assessment, these early results align with outcomes reported in comparable risk population.54

Building on these findings, the planned PROTO-PRIME study will prospectively compare contemporary IG-IMPT with modern IMRT in an exclusive high-risk and node-positive cohort treated with SBRT. The IMRT comparator will be derived from the test arm of the PRIME trial,33 a randomized phase 3 study evaluating moderate versus extreme hypofractionation in this population, enabling a rigorously defined contemporary control group. As a preparatory step, the Pre PROTO-PRIME study was published recently, establishing standardized target definitions, dose prescriptions, and OAR constraints for PBT in the setting of extreme hypofractionation with PNI.55 This work provides a protocolized foundation for proton delivery and informs the design and feasibility assumptions of the planned comparative study.

Several limitations of this study need consideration. As a single-center, nonrandomized registry, generalizability may be limited, and direct photon comparisons are precluded.

Although follow-up is sufficient to assess early and intermediate toxicity and disease control, longer observation is required to fully characterize late treatment-related effects. The absence of a contemporaneous photon comparator further limits comparative assessment. These limitations are partly balanced by the prospective design, standardized treatment workflows, and comprehensive capture of both physician-reported outcomes and PROs. Ongoing registry follow-up will enable more mature toxicity, PRO, and disease control assessment. Importantly, these findings underscore the need for prospective head-to-head comparison of IG-IMPT and standard IMRT in patients with high-risk and node-positive prostate cancer, particularly in the setting of hypofractionation and PNI.

Conclusions

This prospective registry demonstrates that modern IG-IMPT maintains favorable toxicity and QOL outcomes in high-risk prostate cancer patients requiring treatment intensification. These findings establish an important benchmark for contemporary proton therapy and underscore the need for prospective comparison with photon-based approaches in this setting.
