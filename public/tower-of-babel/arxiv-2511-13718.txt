Bosonisation Cohomology: Spin Structure Summation in Every Dimension
Philip Boyle Smith, and Joe Davighi

Abstract
Gauging fermion parity and summing over spin structures are subtly distinct operations. We introduce ‘bosonisation cohomology’ groups H B d + 2 ​ ( X ) H_{B}^{d+2}(X) to capture this difference, for theories in spacetime dimension d d equipped with maps to some X X . Non-trivial classes in H B d + 2 ​ ( X ) H_{B}^{d+2}(X) contain theories for which ( − 1 ) F (-1)^{F} is anomaly-free, but spin structure summation is anomalous. We formulate a sequence of cobordism groups whose failure to be exact is measured by H B d + 2 ​ ( X ) H_{B}^{d+2}(X) , and from here we compute it for X = pt X=\mathrm{pt} . The result is non-trivial only in dimensions d ∈ 4 ​ ℤ + 2 d\in 4\mathbb{Z}+2 , being due to the presence of gravitational anomalies. The first few are H B 4 = ℤ 2 H_{B}^{4}=\mathbb{Z}_{2} , probed by a theory of 8 Majorana–Weyl fermions in d = 2 d=2 , then H B 8 = ℤ 8 H_{B}^{8}=\mathbb{Z}_{8} , H B 12 = ℤ 16 × ℤ 2 H_{B}^{12}=\mathbb{Z}_{16}\times\mathbb{Z}_{2} . We rigorously derive a general formula extending this to every spacetime dimension. Along the way, we compile many general facts about (fermionic and bosonic) anomaly polynomials, and about spin and pin - (co)bordism generators, that we hope might serve as a useful reference for physicists working with these objects. We briefly discuss some physics applications, including how the H B 12 H_{B}^{12} class is trivialised in supergravity. Despite the name, and notation, we make no claim that H B ∙ ​ ( X ) H_{B}^{\bullet}(X) actually defines a cohomology theory (in the Eilenberg–Steenrod sense).

Introduction

Bosonisation is a transformation from a fermionic theory to a bosonic theory that was originally defined in two dimensions
21
;
45
. Since then, it has been reformulated, refined and generalised in various ways,
to provide a web of dualities that help us understand the space of physically inequivalent quantum field theories – an important challenge in theoretical physics.

In the beginning, bosonisation referred to an exact equivalence between two particular quantum field theories: a
(-1)^{F}
-gauged Dirac fermion and a free compact boson. An equivalent viewpoint is that of a transformation: starting from a Dirac fermion, one gauges the fermion parity symmetry
(-1)^{F}
which converts it into a compact boson.

The above idea has since been extended to arbitrary spacetime dimension
d
, and to arbitrary input theories. The step to three dimensions was made with the beautiful story of
flux attachment
, where one instead gauges a
U(1)
fermion number symmetry, and a Chern–Simons term is responsible for transmuting the statistics of the particles from fermions to bosons
42
. An analogous mechanism using
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
gauge fields also exists
18
, which gives a slightly different version of 3d bosonisation that looks a lot more like its 2d counterpart. This latter mechanism admits a uniform generalisation to all dimensions
35
, both in the continuum
20
and on the lattice
52
;
19
, with the lattice version generalising the Jordan–Wigner transformation in two dimensions. See also
17
for a recent review.

These analyses typically assume that the theory to be bosonised is free of anomalies. In this paper, we are interested in the additional richness brought to the story when this is not the case. Even in the simplest case of two dimensions, it is known that something interesting and rather subtle can occur: a fermionic theory can carry a perturbative gravitational anomaly that still allows for gauging
(-1)^{F}
, but this anomaly cannot be carried by any bosonic theory. The
(-1)^{F}
-gauged theory therefore has no choice but to be fermionic, typically a new theory
13
. In
d=3
by contrast, things are simpler, for lack of a suitable gravitational anomaly
40
. But what happens in other dimensions? Perturbative gravitational anomalies exist in all dimensions
d\in 4\mathbb{Z}+2
, and one would expect them to have a similar effect. Global (discrete) gravitational anomalies also exist in various dimensions, but are more subtle to analyse. Can they also play a role? Our goal will be to answer such questions.

One additional motivation for our investigation is the connection between bosonisation and higher symmetry structures. For example, the Kramers–Wannier defect is the prototypical example of a non-invertible symmetry; under bosonisation it is dual to stacking an invertible fermionic phase, and under a lattice version of bosonisation, it is also understood as being emanant from a lattice translation
47
. This connection has been generalised to
d
dimensions to produce various Kramers–Wannier-like defects
50
. Such defects naturally form part of the symmetry structure of a
(d+1)
-dimensional TQFT known as the SymTFT, which for non-anomalous bosonisation is a twisted
\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{[d-2]}
gauge theory. Our interest is in how this symmetry structure shifts when we add gravitational anomalies. Although we defer a fuller investigation to further work, we will write down the corresponding SymTFT, and comment on some of its properties that follow from our results. Other applications we will consider include the cancellation of fermionic anomalies by bosonic fields, particularly in 10d supergravity, where it turns out these bosonic fields must include secret fermionic dependence, as has recently been explained from a different point of view in
39
;
38
.

1.1
A sequence of anomalies

In this paper the key questions we are interested in asking are: given a fermionic theory
\mathcal{T}_{F}
in
d
spacetime dimensions, when can we gauge fermion parity
(-1)^{F}
? When can we sum over spin structures to bosonise? And when do these conditions differ?

The answers to all of the above questions are governed by anomalies. In recent years it has come to be understood that all anomalies in quantum field theory, including those for
(-1)^{F}
and spin structure summation, are classified by a mathematical gadget called
cobordism
(at least for unitary theories, which we assume). Roughly speaking (see
section

2.1
for slightly more precision),
bordism
is an equivalence relation between manifolds equipped with certain structures – like a spin structure or a background gauge field. Two such manifolds are equivalent under bordism if they can be smoothly connected by an interpolating manifold in one dimension higher. Anomalies, both perturbative and non-perturbative ones, are detected by invariants of manifolds that are constant on such bordism classes, in dimension
d+2
(for perturbative anomalies) and
d+1
(for non-perturbative ones). For example, a 2d perturbative anomaly for a
U(1)
symmetry is captured by a 4d instanton number, which is quantised and does not change under a smooth interpolation of field configurations.
Mathematically, this means the anomaly is an element in an abelian group dual to bordism, namely a
co
bordism group that we denote by
\mho_{H}^{d+2}(X)
for theories with spacetime symmetry type
H
(typically
\mathrm{Spin}
or
\mathrm{SO}
) and equipped with maps to some
X
(typically the classifying space
\mathit{B}G
for some group
G
).
This has been shown to follow, rigorously, from quite general properties of the anomaly theory
41
;
57
;
30
;
37
.

To get started, we first need to answer: when is
(-1)^{F}
anomaly-free, and thus gaugable? Let us, for now, restrict ourselves to unitary theories
\mathcal{T}_{F}
in
d
dimensions in which fermions are defined using a spin structure, and suppose we do not couple to any background gauge fields. If we think of
(-1)^{F}
as an independent
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
symmetry, then its anomaly is an element in the cobordism group

\text{Anomaly}[(-1)^{F},\mathcal{T}_{F}]\;\in\;\widetilde{\mho}^{d+2}_{\mathrm{Spin}}(\mathit{B}\mathbb{Z}_{2})

(1)

The symmetry is then gaugable if the anomaly is zero. For instance, in
d=2
, we have
\widetilde{\mho}^{4}_{\mathrm{Spin}}(B\mathbb{Z}_{2})\cong\mathbb{Z}_{8}
; a valid choice of generator for this cobordism group is the anomaly for a single Majorana–Weyl fermion, and the mod-8 nature means that collections of such fermions can in fact be gapped (without breaking the
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
) in groups of 8, matching the well-known symmetric mass generation story uncovered by Fidkowski and Kitaev
28
;
46
.

Of course, fermion parity is not, in fact, an independent
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
symmetry. Because
(-1)^{F}\subseteq\mathrm{Spin}(d)
, its anomaly must be fully determined by the gravitational anomaly associated with
\mathrm{Spin}(d)
. Again assuming our fermions are defined with a spin structure (and turning off any other background gauge fields), the most general fermionic anomaly is captured by the spin cobordism group

\text{Gravitational-Anomaly}[\mathcal{T}_{F}]\;\in\;\mho_{\mathrm{Spin}}^{d+2}(\mathrm{pt})

(2)

For instance, for
d=2
we have
\mho_{\mathrm{Spin}}^{4}(\mathrm{pt})\cong\mathbb{Z}
, generated by
\sigma/16
where
\sigma
is the signature, which happens to equal the anomaly polynomial of a single Majorana–Weyl. We moreover expect there should be a map

q\;:\;\mho_{\mathrm{Spin}}^{d+2}(\mathrm{pt})\;\longrightarrow\;\widetilde{\mho}^{d+2}_{\mathrm{Spin}}(\mathit{B}\mathbb{Z}_{2})

(3)

that sends a gravitational anomaly for a particular fermionic system to the induced anomaly in
(-1)^{F}
. Explicitly, we can model
q
by a map of partition functions
\mathcal{Z}
for the associated
(d+1)
-dimensional anomaly theories, which are functions of the choice of spin structure
\rho
: we have
q:\mathcal{Z}[\rho]\mapsto\frac{\mathcal{Z}[\rho+A]}{\mathcal{Z}[\rho]}
where
A
is a background gauge field for the
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
. (We suppress an additional metric dependence of
\mathcal{Z}
.)

The fermion parity symmetry of
\mathcal{T}_{F}
can thus be gauged when the map (
3
) sends the gravitational anomaly (
2
) to the zero element in the cobordism group (
1
) that classifies
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
anomalies. Thus, these theories form the kernel of the map
q
.

On the other hand, we also wish to know whether
\mathcal{T}_{F}
can be bosonised. In general, bosonisation can be defined as gauging
(-1)^{F}
followed by stacking with a topological counterterm to remove the spin structure dependence.
1
1

1

Or, at the lattice level, applying a ‘disentangling unitary’
50
.

35
So the question is: when can this procedure produce a purely bosonic theory
\mathcal{T}_{B}
? The key point is that, if so, then this process can be reversed (by first stacking the inverse counterterm, then gauging the quantum symmetry) to recover
\mathcal{T}_{F}
from
\mathcal{T}_{B}
, a process known as
fermionisation
. Since neither of these steps modifies the gravitational anomaly, this means the gravitational anomaly of
\mathcal{T}_{F}
must be induced from that of
\mathcal{T}_{B}
.

A bosonic theory is defined not with a spin structure, but simply (we assume) with an orientation. Bosonic anomaly theories are therefore detected by the cobordism group

\text{Gravitational-Anomaly}[\mathcal{T}_{B}]\;\in\;\mho_{\mathrm{SO}}^{d+2}(\mathrm{pt})

(4)

In order to be consistent on all oriented manifolds, an element in this cobordism group must make sense on a much larger set of manifolds than the corresponding element in spin cobordism. Because oriented manifolds are defined with strictly less structure than spin manifolds, an element of (
4
) will also make sense on a spin manifold (even though it may, of course, evaluate to zero on all spin manifolds). This means there is always a map

p\;:\;\mho_{\mathrm{SO}}^{d+2}(\mathrm{pt})\;\longrightarrow\;\mho_{\mathrm{Spin}}^{d+2}(\mathrm{pt})

(5)

defined by evaluating each oriented cobordism element only on spin manifolds. It can be interpreted as the map of gravitational anomalies induced by fermionisation.

2

The bosonised theory
\mathcal{T}_{B}
also has a quantum
\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{[d-2]}
symmetry with a fixed anomaly
(-1)^{\int A_{d-1}w_{2}}

;
17
. Since it is fixed, and not part of the pure gravitational anomaly, for us it will play no role. It would be interesting to revisit this claim in view of symmetry fractionalisation
40
.

To get a feel for this, let us return to our illustrative example of
d=2
. The generator
\sigma/16
we described above for spin cobordism only made sense because Rokhlin’s theorem tells us that
\sigma(M_{\mathrm{Spin}})\in 16\mathbb{Z}
for all spin manifolds. However, a merely oriented 4-manifold can have
\sigma\in\mathbb{Z}
in general; for example,
\sigma(\mathbb{CP}^{2})=1
. This means the corresponding generator of oriented cobordism is 16 times the generator of spin cobordism,
i.e.
we deduce that
p:\mho_{\mathrm{SO}}^{4}(\mathrm{pt})\to\mho_{\mathrm{Spin}}^{4}(\mathrm{pt})
sends
1\mapsto 16
. So the map
p
is certainly not surjective. Nor, in general, is it injective: there can, in particular, be additional torsion elements in oriented cobordism, thanks to the non-vanishing of the Stiefel–Whitney number
w_{2}
which we can use to build bordism invariants: any such element would map to zero under
p
because
w_{2}(M_{\mathrm{Spin}})
is trivial.

We argued above that a fermionic theory
\mathcal{T}_{F}
can be bosonised if and only if its gravitational anomaly (
2
) is induced from a bosonic anomaly (
4
) under the map (
5
). Thus, bosonisable theories form the image of the map
p
.

The astute reader will notice that we can chain together the maps
p
and
q
to form the following sequence:

\mho^{d+2}_{\mathrm{SO}}(\mathrm{pt})\xrightarrow[\hskip 15.06943pt]{p}\mho^{d+2}_{\mathrm{Spin}}(\mathrm{pt})\xrightarrow[\hskip 15.06943pt]{q}\;\widetilde{\mho}^{d+2}_{\mathrm{Spin}}(\mathit{B}\mathbb{Z}_{2})

(6)

To reiterate, the first map
p
sends bosonic anomalies to their image in spin cobordism, while the second map
q
sends a fermionic gravitational anomaly to the induced anomaly in fermion parity
(-1)^{F}
. This sequence, which we shall mathematically justify in
section

2.2
, is our primary tool for investigating gravitationally-anomalous bosonisation in any dimension. It is clear that

q\circ p=0

(7)

because
p
hits only the bosonic theories, for which
(-1)^{F}
is trivial and so necessarily anomaly-free. Thus,
\im(p)\subseteq\ker(q)
. We also know that this sequence need not be exact, as we can already see from our example in
d=2
for which the sequence reads

\mathbb{Z}\xrightarrow[\hskip 15.06943pt]{\times 16}\mathbb{Z}\xrightarrow[\hskip 15.06943pt]{\text{mod~}8}\mathbb{Z}_{8}

(8)

so
\im(p)=16\mathbb{Z}\neq\ker(q)=8\mathbb{Z}
.
We can distil the failure of exactness into a set of abelian groups, defined by

H_{B}^{d+2}(\mathrm{pt})\coloneqq\ker(q)/\im(p)

(9)

that we shall refer to as ‘bosonisation cohomology’. Its non-trivial classes contain all theories for which
(-1)^{F}
can be gauged, but that are nonetheless intrinsically fermionic
i.e.
they are not the ‘fermionisation’ of any bosonic theory. For example,
H_{B}^{4}(\mathrm{pt})=8\mathbb{Z}/16\mathbb{Z}=\mathbb{Z}_{2}
. In
section

2
we also show how the construction generalises to the presence of a non-zero internal symmetry
G
, or indeed for maps to any
X
, which lets us define
H_{B}^{\bullet}(X)
for a topological space
X
.

Spin structure summation and gauging

2.1
Bordism, cobordism, and anomalies

In the introduction we sketched how anomalies are classified by so-called cobordism groups, without really explaining what cobordism groups are. In this section we give a definition of bordism and cobordism, and motivate in more detail why it is these objects that classify anomalies.

The key physics hypothesis that unlocked the algebraic classification of anomaly theories is that of
anomaly inflow

16
, which asserts that anomalies can themselves be identified with quantum field theories in one dimension higher (and satisfying certain special properties). To see how this works in its most pedestrian setting, let’s consider the case of a perturbative chiral fermion anomaly in
d=4
dimensions à la Adler, Bell, and Jackiw (ABJ)
1
;
12
. Under a chiral
U(1)_{A}
background gauge transformation
A\mapsto A+d\alpha
, the fermion path integral transforms by a phase
Z[A]\mapsto Z[A]\exp\left(in\int_{\Sigma}\alpha\frac{dA\wedge dA}{8\pi^{2}}\right)
, for some anomaly coefficient
n\in\mathbb{Z}
. Inflow says that this anomalous transformation can be captured by the variation of a classical Chern–Simons action defined on a bulk 5-manifold
X
whose boundary is the physical spacetime,
Z_{\mathrm{CS}}[A]=\exp\left(in\int_{X}\frac{1}{8\pi^{2}}A\wedge dA\wedge dA\right)
. In condensed matter systems this bulk may be part of the physical system; famously, the
d=2
version of this abelian perturbative anomaly inflow describes the integer quantum Hall effect.

For a perturbative anomaly such as this, the gauge-invariant object that determines the anomaly is called an
anomaly polynomial
, a creature that will play a central role in this paper. Here, it is
\Phi_{6}=\frac{n}{8\pi^{2}}dA\wedge dA\wedge dA
, the curvature of the Chern–Simons form. Being a closed differential
(d+2)
-form, its integral on any manifold that is a boundary, to which we assume all requisite structures (such as an orientation, the spin structure and the gauge field) can be extended, will vanish. This property makes the anomaly polynomial a simple example of a
bordism invariant
in degree
d+2
.

We pause to recall a few mathematical notions needed to understand this last statement. Firstly,
bordism
is an equivalence relation on
k
-dimensional manifolds equipped with given structures, whereby
M_{0}\sim M_{1}
if there exists some
(k+1)
-dimensional smooth manifold
X
such that
\partial X=M_{0}\sqcup(-M_{1})
and to which all the structures smoothly extend, where
-M_{1}
denotes the orientation reversal of
M_{1}
(assuming our manifolds come with an orientation). This equivalence relation partitions the set of manifolds with given structures into classes, which can moreover be given an abelian group structure under disjoint union of manifolds, thus defining
bordism groups
. For the case of manifolds with spin structure and
G
-gauge bundles, the bordism groups are denoted
\Omega_{k}^{\mathrm{Spin}}(\mathit{B}G)
, where
BG
is the classifying space of
G
.
3
3

3

Recall that
BG
is a topological space such that, given some manifold
M
and some group
G
, principal
G
-bundles over
M
(without connection) are classified by homotopy classes of maps from
M
into
BG
.

The zero class in
\Omega_{k}^{\mathrm{Spin}}(BG)
contains all manifolds that are boundaries. From this description, one can appreciate that bordism is a kind of homology theory that works directly with manifolds (rather than cycles), and which incorporates all the structures we need to define quantum field theories. Any quantity that vanishes on all boundaries is a
bordism invariant
, meaning it will moreover be constant on each non-zero bordism class: the anomaly polynomial therefore defines an element in the group

\Hom(\Omega_{d+2}^{\mathrm{Spin}}(\mathit{B}G),\mathbb{Z})\qquad\{\text{local anomalies}\}

(11)

via its integration, given also the quantisation condition on the anomaly coefficient.

Witten taught us that not all anomalies can, like the ABJ example, be detected by Feynman diagrams

While we consider the anomaly theories of higher-spin fermions throughout this paper, we do not expect this issue to pose a problem for our rigorous results, because the anomalies in question persist in the limit of a free theory and the anomaly theories themselves remain well-defined quantum field theories.

The anomaly theory in question is a generalisation of Chern–Simons called the

The APS index theorem tells us something more: that, when the perturbative anomaly vanishes, the exponentiated eta invariant is trivial on all boundaries. Thus, when

A rough guide to spectra

A spectrum is something like a topological space, but one that is decomposed into distinct components spread across infinitely many dimensions. Spectra provide an ideal setting for describing things like cohomology groups (which also are defined in every dimension
d\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
) in a unified way, and they enable mathematicians to discuss
stable
behaviour that emerges only in some limit where
d
becomes large.

More precisely, a spectrum is a set of topological spaces
\{E_{n}\}
, for each
n\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
, together with homeomorphisms relating them
\sigma_{n}:\Sigma E_{n}\to E_{n+1}
, where
\Sigma
denotes the suspension operation – illustrated in Fig.
2
– defined as
\Sigma X=(X\times I)/{\sim}
, where
(x_{1},0)\sim(x_{2},0)
,
(x_{1},1)\sim(x_{2},1)
,
\forall x_{1},x_{2}\in X
.
The simplest example is the sphere spectrum
S^{0}
, for which
E_{n}=S^{n}
and the
\sigma_{n}
are the canonical homeomorphisms. (It should be clear from Fig.
2
that the suspension of
S^{n}
is homeomorphic to
S^{n+1}
, with the endpoints of the interval
I
ending up as the North and South pole.) The sphere spectrum is not only the simplest example of a spectrum; for mathematicians, it is the monoidal unit in the category of spectra, so it is sometimes denoted
S^{0}=1
.
We can also generate examples of spectra
ad libitum
just by iterating the suspension operation. To wit, given some topological space
E
, define the
suspension spectrum
of
E
(often also denoted
E
, a notation that is clear enough once one has arrived in the category of spectra) whose component spaces are
\Sigma^{n}E
, with
\sigma_{n}
being identity maps.

X

Figure 2:
An illustration of the suspension
\Sigma X
for some space
X
indicated by the blue disc. One can think of ‘hanging’
X
between two endpoints. For
reduced

As we anticipated, spectra provide a mathematical tool for unifying the description of cohomology theories.
Key to this is a powerful theorem of Brown
14

can always be

by a target spectrum

, meaning that

E^{d}(X)\cong[X_{+},E_{d}]

(16)

where

is the space of homotopy classes of based maps from

B

denotes

Let us illustrate how this works with familiar examples of cohomology.
First, consider the

H\mathbb{Z}_{2}

E_{n}\cong K(\mathbb{Z}_{2},n)
the Eilenberg–MacLane spaces, that is

In a similar way, the Eilenberg–MacLane spectrum with spaces

Turning our attention to physics, spectra are a useful gadget when it comes to describing and/or classifying local quantum field theories, including those that capture anomalies via inflow.

2.3
Generalisation for internal symmetry

So far our discussion has been limited to purely gravitational theories. That means we have not demanded our theories have any internal symmetry, and if they had any, we ignored it.

In general we can ask exactly the questions we have asked but preserving some internal symmetry. Let us consider
d
-dimensional fermionic theories equipped with an internal, invertible symmetry classified by maps to some space
X
(for example
X=\mathit{B}G
for a 0-form symmetry group
G
). Then the sequence of spectra governing spin structure summation and gauging
(-1)^{F}
while preserving the symmetry is simply

\mathit{MT}\mathrm{Spin}\wedge\mathit{B}\mathbb{Z}_{2}\wedge X_{+}\xrightarrow[\hskip 15.06943pt]{q\wedge\text{id}}\mathit{MT}\mathrm{Spin}\wedge X_{+}\xrightarrow[\hskip 15.06943pt]{p\wedge\text{id}}\mathit{MT}\mathrm{SO}\wedge X_{+}

(27)

or (
23
) smashed with
X_{+}
. To justify this, let us examine the induced sequence of cobordism groups:

\mho_{\mathrm{SO}}^{d+2}(X)\xrightarrow[\hskip 15.06943pt]{p}\mho_{\mathrm{Spin}}^{d+2}(X)\xrightarrow[\hskip 15.06943pt]{q}\frac{\mho_{\mathrm{Spin}}^{d+2}(\mathit{B}\mathbb{Z}_{2}\times X)}{\mho_{\mathrm{Spin}}^{d+2}(X)}

(28)

It is clear that
\im(p)
consists of the theories that are bosonisable while preserving symmetry
X
. To interpret the last term, note that a theory with combined
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
and
X
symmetry can have (1) pure gravitational anomalies, (2) pure
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
anomalies, (3) pure
X
anomalies, and (4) mixed
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
-
X
anomalies. We require (2) to vanish for
(-1)^{F}
to be gaugable, and (4) to vanish for gauging
(-1)^{F}
to preserve symmetry
X
. Thus the last term of (
28

(-1)^{F}
to be gaugable while preserving
X
. This construction allows us to define

for any space
X
.

Perhaps ironically,

does
not
define a generalised cohomology theory, in the Eilenberg–Steenrod sense. Although it satisfies all of the Eilenberg–Steenrod axioms bar one, there is no reason to believe it obeys the exactness axiom. Thus the ‘bosonisation cohomology’ groups that we define are only a cohomology theory at the Kindergarten-level understanding of the word, that is, as a measure of inexactness associated to a complex.

From fermionic to bosonic anomaly polynomials

We have given an entirely explicit account of general fermionic anomaly polynomials, and how these can be taken to generate the spin cobordism group appearing in our all-important bosonisation sequence (
41

From Stong

(Bosonic Hattori–Stong) The set of all bosonic anomaly polynomials in dimension

is

\Hom(\Omega_{4k}^{\mathrm{SO}}(\mathrm{pt}),\mathbb{Z})=\{2^{d(\Phi)}\Phi:\Phi\in\Hom(\Omega_{4k}^{\mathrm{Spin}}(\mathrm{pt}),\mathbb{Z})\}

is the smallest power of 2 that clears all powers of 2 in the denominator of

Before we unpack this in general dimensions, let us illustrate how this recipe is implemented through an example.

Example:

3.3
The map
p
and

-localisation

Having described how the spaces of bosonic and fermionic anomaly polynomials are calculated, we now describe how to pick bases thereon such that the map
p
between them takes a particularly simple (diagonal) form. This motivates introducing an algebraic process known as

Consider the special subset of anomaly polynomials built from the Pontryagin classes with integer coefficients. Explicitly, we can write this as
\text{span}\{p^{J}:n(J)=k\}
, where we have taken the opportunity to shoehorn in some new notation:

has differential form degree
4k

is a fermionic anomaly polynomial, we have the inclusion of lattices

\text{span}\{p^{J}:n(J)=k\}\subseteq\Hom(\Omega_{4k}^{\mathrm{Spin}}(\mathrm{pt}),\mathbb{Z})

(54)

where both sides are isomorphic to
\mathbb{Z}^{p(k)}
.

An inclusion of lattices is ripe for applying the Smith normal form. This furnishes us with a basis
\{P_{1},\dots,P_{p(k)}\}
for the left hand side such that
\{\frac{1}{\lambda_{1}}P_{1},\dots,\frac{1}{\lambda_{p(k)}}P_{p(k)}\}
is a basis for the right hand side, with the
\lambda_{i}

\lambda_{i}=2^{d_{i}}r_{i}
where the

are odd numbers, we deduce that

\displaystyle\Hom(\Omega_{4k}^{\mathrm{Spin}}(\mathrm{pt}),\mathbb{Z})

\displaystyle=\text{span}\bigg\{

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2^{d_{i}}r_{i}}

\displaystyle P_{i}:i=1,\dots,p(k)\bigg\}

(55)

\displaystyle\Hom(\Omega_{4k}^{\mathrm{SO}}(\mathrm{pt}),\mathbb{Z})

\displaystyle=\text{span}\bigg\{

\displaystyle\frac{1}{r_{i}}

\displaystyle P_{i}:i=1,\dots,p(k)\bigg\}

where the bottom line follows from the oriented Hattori-Stong theorem (
53
). Thus, with respect to these bases, the inclusion map

takes the simple diagonal form

p=\begin{pmatrix}2^{d_{1}}&0&\cdots&0\\
0&2^{d_{2}}&\cdots&0\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\

(56)

Such a map is said to be

2

The above result implies the bosonisation cohomology groups

are always 2-torsion, meaning

is always isomorphic to a product of cyclic groups each of order a power of 2. To prove this, consider localising the sequence (

bordism groups are always 2-torsion, tensoring with

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathbb{Z}[\tfrac{1}{2}]^{p(k)}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathbb{Z}[\tfrac{1}{2}]^{p(k)}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces 0}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces 1}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces 0}

(57)

This is a very boring sequence. It has trivial homology. Since taking homology commutes with localisation, this proves the claim. All the information in the sequence (

\mathbb{Z}_{(2)}
, the ring of all rationals with odd denominator.

of order 8, and from the odd partition
J=\{3\}
we get a ? generator of order 2 (which is secretly
N_{\{3\}}
). So
\Omega^{\mathrm{Pin}^{-}}_{10}(\mathrm{pt})\cong\mathbb{Z}_{128}\times\mathbb{Z}_{8}\times\mathbb{Z}_{2}
.

3.5
The map
q
and eta invariants

We now turn to the map
q
, which recall sends a perturbative fermionic anomaly to the induced anomaly for gauging
(-1)^{F}\in\mathrm{Spin}(d)
. To compute how
q
acts, we work at the level of partition functions of anomaly theories, as discussed in
section

1
.

Recipe for
q
:

•

Start with a fermionic anomaly polynomial
\Phi_{4k}\in\Hom(\Omega_{4k}^{\mathrm{Spin}}(\mathrm{pt}),\mathbb{Z})
.

•

Pick any fermionic anomaly theory
\mathcal{Z}_{4k-1}[g,\rho]
with perturbative anomaly
\Phi_{4k}
. The choice is not unique, but does not matter by results of
section

3.1
.

•

Consider the metric-independent ratio

\frac{\mathcal{Z}_{4k-1}[g,\rho+A]}{\mathcal{Z}_{4k-1}[g,\rho]}\in\Hom(\widetilde{\Omega}_{4k-1}^{\mathrm{Spin}}(\mathit{B}\mathbb{Z}_{2}),U(1))

•

Apply the Smith isomorphism, landing in
\Hom(\Omega_{4k-2}^{\mathrm{Pin}^{-}}(\mathrm{pt}),U(1))
.

We will need to evaluate
\big(q(\Phi_{4k})\big)(M_{4k-2})\in U(1)
for various anomaly polynomials and pin manifolds. This can be done by expressing
q(\Phi_{4k})
as an eta invariant, and then using various tools for eta invariants to evaluate it.

For our purposes, the eta invariant of a Hermitian operator
\mathcal{D}
is defined using the convention (
12
), which ensures that it jumps by
\pm 1
as an eigenvalue of
\mathcal{D}
passes through zero. Thus, usually, only
e^{2\pi i\eta(\mathcal{D})}
is continuous as an eigenvalue goes through zero. But if it happens that
\mathcal{D}
has a doubly degenerate spectrum, then actually
e^{2\pi i\eta(\mathcal{D})/2}
is continuous.

Let’s start with the simplest case of
\theta=1
, and work up to the general case in steps. So consider the fermionic anomaly polynomial
\frac{\Phi_{4k}(i\not{D})}{\gcd(2,k+1)}
. We describe how to apply
q
to it. Running the recipe,

•

The corresponding fermionic anomaly theory is the exponentiated eta invariant of a massive fermion,

\mathcal{Z}_{4k-1}[g,\rho]=\exp\!\left(2\pi i\frac{\eta(i\not{D})}{\gcd(2,k+1)}\right)

(66)

•

We consider the difference

\exp\!\left(2\pi i\frac{\eta(i\not{D}_{\rho+A})-\eta(i\not{D}_{\rho})}{\gcd(2,k+1)}\right)\in\Hom(\widetilde{\Omega}_{4k-1}^{\text{Spin}}(\mathbb{Z}_{2}),U(1))

(67)

where the subscripts here indicate spin structures (not
\theta
).

•

Under the Smith isomorphism, this eta invariant becomes
(
11
, Lemma 3.3a)

\exp\!\left(2\pi i\frac{\eta(\bar{\gamma}\not{D})}{\gcd(2,k+1)}\right)\in\Hom(\Omega_{4k-2}^{\mathrm{Pin}^{-}}(\mathrm{pt}),U(1))

(68)

where
\bar{\gamma}
is the chirality matrix, needed to make the Dirac operator well-defined on an even-dimensional pin manifold.

Thus the final answer is

q\!\left(\frac{\Phi_{4k}(i\not{D})}{\gcd(2,k+1)}\right)=\exp\!\left(2\pi i\frac{\eta(\bar{\gamma}\not{D})}{\gcd(2,k+1)}\right)

(69)

The right-hand side is a quantity that can be evaluated on any
4k-2
dimensional
\mathrm{pin}^{-}
manifold, and is a bordism invariant.

Note that in all cases, the factors of
\gcd(2,k+1)
are either 1 or 2, and take the value 2 precisely when the Dirac operator in question has a doubly degenerate spectrum. So all exponentiated eta invariants are well-defined.

We must now confront the task of evaluating eta invariants on various
\mathrm{pin}^{-}
manifolds in the different classes set out above. To make our life easier, there are two tricks we employ, as follows.

Trick 1: orbifolding

Let us consider evaluating (
69
) on the simplest
4k-2
dimensional
\mathrm{pin}^{-}
manifold,
\mathbb{RP}^{4k-2}
. This can be done using the “orbifolding trick” (see
57
;
51
for exposition).
To wit, consider the orbifold
T^{4k-1}/\mathbb{Z}_{2}
where the torus
T^{4k-1}
has the periodic spin structure and the
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
action that we quotient by sends
x\mapsto-x
. This group action has
2^{4k-1}
fixed points, and cutting out balls around them gives
2^{4k-1}

\mathbb{RP}^{4k-2}
boundaries. The APS index theorem says

2^{4k-1}\eta(\bar{\gamma}\not{D},\mathbb{RP}^{4k-2})=\text{index}\left(i\not{D},\tfrac{T^{4k-1}\backslash\text{balls}}{\mathbb{Z}_{2}}\right)

(70)

and the latter is given by the number of
\mathbb{Z}_{2}=+1
zero modes of
\not{D}
on
T^{4k-1}
minus the number of
\mathbb{Z}_{2}=-1
zero modes. Since the zero modes are constant Dirac spinors, they all have
\mathbb{Z}_{2}=+1
and there are
2^{2k-1}
of them. So the eta invariant is

\eta(\bar{\gamma}\not{D},\mathbb{RP}^{4k-2})=\frac{2^{2k-1}}{2^{4k-1}}=\frac{1}{2^{2k}}

(71)

Combined with the previous result, this gives

q\!\left(\frac{\Phi_{4k}(i\not{D})}{\gcd(2,k+1)}\right)(\mathbb{RP}^{4k-2})=\exp\!\left(\frac{2\pi i}{2^{2k+(k\text{ mod }2)}}\right)

(72)

Example:
If
k=1
, this gives
e^{2\pi i/8}
.

Trick 2: factorisation

To generalise this calculation to the manifolds
M_{J}\times\mathbb{RP}^{4l-2}
we need another trick: namely, a factorisation formula for evaluating eta invariants on product spaces, due to Bahri and Gilkey
11
.
For the ordinary (untwisted) Dirac operator, this factorisation formula takes the guise

\eta({\bar{\gamma}\not{D}},M_{J}\times\mathbb{RP}^{4l-2})=\mathrm{index}(i\not{D},M_{J})\,\eta({\bar{\gamma}\not{D}},\mathbb{RP}^{4l-2})

(73)

Here we take a moment to point out the slightly subtle way the spectrum doubling works out in this formula. When the Dirac operator on the left has a doubled spectrum (
i.e.
when
n(J)+l
is odd), then exactly one of the Dirac operators on the right also has a doubled spectrum (when
n(J)
is odd
or

l
is odd). This ensures that the exponentiated eta invariant on the left can always be expressed in terms of that on the right, without a sign ambiguity.

Applied to our case, and using (
71
) to evaluate the eta invariant on the
\mathbb{RP}^{4l-2}
part,
we find the appropriate generalisation of the evaluation of (
72
) is

q\!\left(\frac{\Phi_{4k}(i\not{D})}{\gcd(2,k+1)}\right)(M_{J}\times\mathbb{RP}^{4l-2})=\exp\!\left(\frac{2\pi i}{2^{2l+(k\text{ mod }2)}}\int_{M_{J}}\Phi_{4(k-l)}(i\not{D})\right)

(74)

Generalisation for twisted Dirac operators

On the other hand, we need a further generalisation of (
69
) to the case with a twisting bundle,
i.e.

\theta=\theta(\pi^{1},\pi^{2},\dots)\neq 1
. Recall from §
3.2
that we need such twisted bundles to generate the oriented and spin cobordism groups, hence they are needed to compute the map
q
. In the following, let
\theta(V)
be shorthand for
\theta(\pi^{1}(V),\pi^{2}(V),\dots)
, so that
\theta
describes an operation on vector bundles.

The first main change is that when dimensionally reducing the anomaly polynomial to the anomaly theory, the tangent bundle splits up as
TM\rightarrow TM+1
, so we have

\frac{\Phi_{4k}(i\not{D}_{\theta(TM)})}{\gcd(2,k+1)}\quad\rightarrow\quad\mathcal{Z}[g,\rho]=\exp\!\left(2\pi i\frac{\eta(i\not{D}_{\theta(TM+1)})}{\gcd(2,k+1)}\right)

(75)

Next, upon reducing the anomaly theory further to a
\mathrm{pin}^{-}
bordism invariant, the tangent bundle further splits as
TM\rightarrow TM+\xi
where
\xi
is the orientation bundle
11
, as motivated in
fig.

3
, so

q\!\left(\frac{\Phi_{4k}(i\not{D}_{\theta(TM)})}{\gcd(2,k+1)}\right)=\exp\!\left(2\pi i\frac{\eta(\bar{\gamma}\not{D}_{\theta(TM+\xi+1)})}{\gcd(2,k+1)}\right)

(76)

Note that twisting by
\xi
negates all bordism invariants
(
29
, Proposition 3.30)
, but it is too early to cancel
\xi+1
to zero, because it is still inside a vector bundle operation.

Now we would like to evaluate this on various manifolds. Let us start with
\mathbb{RP}^{4k-2}
. The tangent bundle is
TM=(4k-1)\xi-1
, so the total bundle appearing above is

TM+\xi+1=4k\xi

(77)

We need to apply
\theta
to this. By definition
(
29
, Appendix B)
,
\pi_{t}(2\xi)=1+2t(\xi-1)
. Thus
\pi_{t}(4k\xi)=(1+2t(\xi-1))^{2k}
. As far as the eta invariant is concerned,
\xi=-1
, and we are free to make this substitution at this point because we are done with the
\mathit{KO}
-theory operations. So we can replace
\pi_{t}(4k\xi)\rightarrow(1-4t)^{2k}
. Thus
\pi^{r}(4k\xi)\rightarrow\binom{2k}{r}(-4)^{r}
, and

q\!\left(\frac{\Phi_{4k}(i\not{D}_{\theta})}{\gcd(2,k+1)}\right)(\mathbb{RP}^{4k-2})=\exp\!\left(\frac{2\pi i}{2^{2k+(k\text{ mod }2)}}\theta(\pi^{r}=\tbinom{2k}{r}(-4)^{r})\right)

(78)

As a consistency check, we should get
1
when we put
\theta=\pi^{r}
with
r>k
, because then
\Phi_{4k}(i\not{D}_{\theta})=0
for degree reasons. This is indeed the case, because the factor of
(-4)^{r}
swamps the denominator.

Finally, we assert that when one simultaneously turns on a twisting bundle
\theta
and evaluates on a product manifold (which we have only done separately so far), the appropriate generalisation is

q\!\left(\frac{\Phi_{4k}(i\not{D}_{\theta})}{\gcd(2,k+1)}\right)(M_{J}\times\mathbb{RP}^{4l-2})\\
=\exp\!\left[\frac{2\pi i}{2^{2l+(k\text{ mod }2)}}\int_{M_{J}}\Phi_{4(k-l)}\bigg(i\not{D}_{\theta\left(\pi^{r}=\sum_{a+b=r}\pi^{a}(TM_{J})\tbinom{2l}{b}(-4)^{b}\right)}\bigg)\right]

(79)

3.6
Putting things together

In this section we combine the previous results to give a computation of
H_{B}^{4k}(\mathrm{pt})
, in every dimension. The computation is essentially rigorous; in particular, as we shall see there is no issue from some of the pin bordism generators not having explicit representatives. We defer some of the more laborious proofs to
appendix

C
.

The key remaining insight that we need is that only anomaly polynomials with
even coefficients
end up mattering when we compute the bosonisation cohomology. This implies that we do not need to evaluate eta invariants on manifolds in
\mathrm{pin}^{-}
bordism that have order 2, at a stroke removing all ambiguities associated with mystery manifolds in the tables in
section

3.4
. This in turn means that the tricks we described in
section

3.5
, that let us evaluate eta invariants on product manifolds of the form
\mathbb{RP}^{4l-2}\times M_{\mathrm{Spin}}
, are all we need to compute the kernel of
q
and thence
H_{B}^{\bullet}(\mathrm{pt})
.

So, we now show why only the “even” anomaly polynomials appear in
\ker(q)/\im(p)
.
Fix a
k
, and let
A=\Hom(\Omega_{4k}^{\mathrm{Spin}}(\mathrm{pt}),\mathbb{Z})\cong\mathbb{Z}^{p(k)}
be the set of fermionic anomaly polynomials in
4k
dimensions. We will show that

\ker(q)\subseteq\begin{cases}2A&k\text{ odd}\\
2A+\im(p)&k\text{ even}\end{cases}

(80)

The argument for this is very different depending on whether
k
is odd or even, so we split our discussion into these two cases.

The case
\bm{k}
is odd

In this case, we note from the classification of spin bordism that there exist
p(k)
spin manifolds of dimension
4k-2
, denoted
\{N_{J}:n(J)\leq k,1\notin J\}
, such that

\text{mod-2-index}(i\not{D}_{\pi^{J}},N_{J^{\prime}})=\delta_{J,J^{\prime}}

(81)

These mod-2 indices generalise the familiar Arf invariant from 2 dimensions. One can straightforwardly prove that

q\Big(\tfrac{1}{2}\Phi_{4k}(i\not{D}_{\pi^{J}})\Big)\,(N_{J^{\prime}})=(-1)^{\delta_{J,J^{\prime}}}

(82)

since the
\mathrm{pin}^{-}
bordism invariant on the left hand side becomes the mod-2 index when restricted to a spin manifold. Thus if an integer-linear combination of the
\tfrac{1}{2}\Phi_{4k}(i\not{D}_{\pi^{J}})
is to have a chance at being in the kernel of
q
, it must be an
even
-integer-linear combination. Since the
\tfrac{1}{2}\Phi_{4k}(i\not{D}_{\pi^{J}})
form a 2-local basis for
A
, this establishes

\ker(q)\subseteq 2A

(83)

The case
\bm{k}
is even

Suppose
k
is even. Then the previous argument is no longer applicable, because a nontrivial mod-2-index no longer exists. Instead, we note that the
p(k)-p(k-1)
anomaly polynomials in (
58
)

\{\Phi_{4k}(\not{D}_{\pi^{J}}):n(J)=k\text{ and }1\notin J\}

(84)

are simply given by
p^{J}
(the Pontryagin classes), which are trivially in the image of
p
, and therefore guaranteed to map to zero under
q
. Therefore, we only need to consider the remaining
p(k-1)
anomaly polynomials of the form

\{\Phi_{4k}(\not{D}_{\pi^{J}}):n(J)\leq k-1\text{ and }1\notin J\}

(85)

We claim that these are perfectly paired with the
p(k-1)

\mathrm{pin}^{-}
manifolds

\{M_{J}\times 2^{2l-1-(l\text{ mod }2)}\,\mathbb{RP}^{4l-2}:1\leq l\leq k,\,n(J)=k-l,\,1\notin J\}

(86)

which all have order 2. This follows immediately from the Bahri–Gilkey formula (
79
), which in the case above reduces to

q\Big(\Phi_{4k}(\not{D}_{\pi^{J^{\prime}}})\Big)\,(M_{J}\times 2^{2l-1-(l\text{ mod }2)}\,\mathbb{RP}^{4l-2})=(-1)^{\int_{M_{J}}\frac{\scriptstyle\Phi_{4n(J)}(\not{D}_{\pi^{J^{\prime}}})}{\scriptstyle\gcd(2,n(J)+1)}}=(-1)^{\delta_{J,J^{\prime}}}

(87)

Since the
\Phi_{4k}(\not{D}_{\pi^{J}})
form a 2-local basis of
A
, this establishes that

\ker(q)\subseteq 2A+\im(p)

(88)

and concludes our proof of (
80
).

Evaluating the kernel of
\bm{q}

The simple result (
80
) is very powerful: it eliminates any ambiguity that might otherwise arise from the unknown or “halved” pin bordism generators in the calculation of
\ker(q)
. This is because our task is reduced to finding all
\Phi\in A
such that

\Big(q(2\Phi)\Big)(\Omega^{\mathrm{Pin}^{-}}_{4k-2}(\mathrm{pt}))=0

(89)

But this is equivalent to the condition

\Big(q(\Phi)\Big)(2\Omega^{\mathrm{Pin}^{-}}_{4k-2}(\mathrm{pt}))=0

(90)

by linearity.

Taking the set of
\mathrm{pin}^{-}
bordism generators as collated in Tables (
63
–
65
), and multiplying by two, we can infer that a set of generators for
2\Omega^{\mathrm{Pin}^{-}}_{4k-2}(\mathrm{pt})
is given by

\{M_{J}\times 2^{(k+l+1)\text{ mod }2}\,\mathbb{RP}^{4l-2}:1\leq l\leq k,\,n(J)=k-l,\,1\notin J\}

(91)

These are all product manifolds, so can be attacked with tools described in the previous subsection, in particular the Bahri–Gilkey factorisation formula. Doing so, from (
79
) we have

q\Big(\tfrac{1}{\gcd(2,k+1)}\Phi_{4k}(\not{D}_{\pi^{J}})\Big)(M_{4(k-l)}\times\mathbb{RP}^{4l-2})=\\
\exp\!\left(\frac{2\pi i}{2^{2l+(k\text{ mod }2)}}\int_{M_{4(k-l)}}\Phi_{4(k-l)}\big(i\not{D}_{\prod_{j\in J}\sum_{a+b=j}\pi^{a}\tbinom{2l}{b}(-4)^{b}}\big)\right)

(92)

Recall the pre-factor in the exponential comes from evaluating an eta invariant on the
\mathbb{RP}^{4l-2}
factor via orbifolding. Now, the subscript of the Dirac operator can be expanded as a polynomial in the
\pi^{r}
. To avoid getting too many
\pi^{a}
s, which would make the integral vanish by degree reasons, we have to take a certain number of
(-4)^{b}
s; specifically, the sum of all
b
must be
\geq n(J)-(k-l)
. This reduces the denominator outside the integral to

\frac{2^{2(n(J)-(k-l))}}{2^{2l+(k\text{ mod }2)}}=\frac{1}{2^{2(k-n(J))+(k\text{ mod }2)}}

(93)

Because the Dirac indices
\int_{M_{4(k-l)}}\Phi_{4(k-l)}(i\not{D}_{\theta})
appearing in (
92
) are even integers when
k+l
is odd, and otherwise integers, this implies

q\Big(\tfrac{1}{\gcd(2,k+1)}\Phi_{4k}(\not{D}_{\pi^{J}})\Big)(2\Omega^{\mathrm{Pin}^{-}}_{4k-2}(\mathrm{pt}))\subseteq\mathbb{Z}_{2^{2(k-n(J))-(k+1\text{ mod }2)}}\subseteq U(1)

(94)

In other words, the more complicated the anomaly polynomial (that is, the larger
n(J)
is), the less values its eta invariant can take.

Next, we claim that

q:A\rightarrow\Hom(2\Omega^{\mathrm{Pin}^{-}}_{4k-2}(\mathrm{pt}),U(1))

(95)

is surjective. This is straightforward, because we’ve already shown it! Or rather, we have in the
k
even case, when we showed in (
86
) that the anomaly polynomials with
n(J)\leq k-1
and
1\notin J
are perfectly paired with the order-2 subgroup of
2\Omega^{\mathrm{Pin}^{-}}_{4k-2}(\mathrm{pt})
, which implies the claim. The same result is also true in the
k
odd case. Now, as an abstract group, the right hand side of (
95
) is isomorphic to

\Hom(2\Omega^{\mathrm{Pin}^{-}}_{4k-2}(\mathrm{pt}),U(1))\cong\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}n(J)\leq k-1\\
1\notin J\end{subarray}}\mathbb{Z}_{2^{2(k-n(J))-(k+1\text{ mod }2)}}

(96)

The cyclic factors appearing here are the same as those in (
94
). Together with the surjectivity result (
95
), this means that the set of eta invariants, namely those for the
\pi^{J}
twisted bundles, effect the isomorphism in question. That is,

\displaystyle 2\Omega^{\mathrm{Pin}^{-}}_{4k-2}(\mathrm{pt})

\displaystyle\xrightarrow{\hskip 8.19447pt}\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}n(J)\leq k-1\\
1\notin J\end{subarray}}\mathbb{Z}_{2^{2(k-n(J))-(k+1\text{ mod }2)}}

(97)

\displaystyle M

\displaystyle\xmapsto{\hskip 8.19447pt}\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}n(J)\leq k-1\\
1\notin J\end{subarray}}q\Big(\tfrac{1}{\gcd(2,k+1)}\Phi_{4k}(i\not{D}_{\pi^{J}})\Big)(M)

is the desired isomorphism.

The above result allows us to read off
\ker(q)
. We find that a 2-local basis for
\ker(q)
is

\{2^{2(k-n(J))}\Phi_{4k}(i\not{D}_{\pi^{J}}):n(J)\leq k\text{ and }1\notin J\}

Meanwhile, recall from (
58
) that a 2-local basis for
\im(p)
is

\{2^{4(k-n(J))-\mathrm{bitcount}(k-n(J))}\Phi_{4k}(i\not{D}_{\pi^{J}}):n(J)\leq k\text{ and }1\notin J\}

Final result

It is then straightforward to compute
H_{B}^{4k}(\mathrm{pt})=\ker(q)/\im(p)
, where recall that the caveats “2-locally” above do not affect the answer.

Theorem:
The bosonisation cohomology groups in dimension
4k
are given by

H_{B}^{4k}(\mathrm{pt})=\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}n(J)\leq k-1\\
1\notin J\end{subarray}}\mathbb{Z}_{2^{2(k-n(J))-\mathrm{bitcount}(k-n(J))}}

(98)

where the
J^{\text{th}}
cyclic factor is generated by
2^{2(k-n(J))}\Phi_{4k}(i\not{D}_{\pi^{J}})
.

Examples

To illustrate the general results of

, here we show how to unpack them into a less abstract form in the physically interesting dimensions

. We will give explicit anomaly polynomials for the generators of

, and discuss applications.

4.1
d=2

In degree 4, as pertains to anomalies of QFTs in 2 dimensions, the standard 2-local bases for the space of fermionic anomaly polynomials and its subspaces
\ker(q)
and
\im(p)
are

{
2-locally
11
11

11

The prefix ‘2-locally’ reminds us that these statements are strictly only true after taking the 2-localisation, as in
section

3.3
. We remind the reader that the resulting bosonisation cohomology group is, however, always independent of this 2-local caveat.
}

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \Hom(\Omega_{4}^{\mathrm{Spin}}(\mathrm{pt}),\mathbb{Z})&=\text{span}\big\{&\tfrac{1}{2}\Phi_{4}(i\not{D})\big\}\\
\ker(q)&=\text{span}\big\{&2^{2}\Phi_{4}(i\not{D})\big\}\\
\im(p)&=\text{span}\big\{&2^{3}\Phi_{4}(i\not{D})\big\}\end{aligned}

(99)

where

is the anomaly polynomial of a single Majorana–Weyl fermion. We can therefore read off:

The bosonisation cohomology group

, generated by

, which is the anomaly polynomial of 8 Majorana–Weyl fermions.

The fact that bosonisation “backfires” for such a fermion chain was spelled out in Ref.
13
.

To upgrade the 2-local to an actual basis, we use the Hattori–Stong theorem to get an overcomplete set

\Hom(\Omega_{4}^{\mathrm{Spin}}(\mathrm{pt}),\mathbb{Z})=\text{span}\big\{\tfrac{1}{2}\Phi_{4}(i\not{D}),\tfrac{1}{2}\Phi_{4}(i\not{D}_{\pi^{1}})\big\}

We need to refine this to a basis. Since

, the second generator is redundant. Therefore our 2-local basis was already a basis, so we can actually just delete the caveat ‘2-locally’ from (

).

For illustrative purposes, we also rederive
\ker(q)
from scratch to demonstrate how various intermediate results from
section

3
are used in the general proof. The general fermionic anomaly polynomial is
n\cdot\frac{1}{2}\Phi_{4}(i\not{D})
. We wish to determine when

\mathrm{pin}^{-}

\mathbb{RP}^{2}

\exp(2\pi in/8)
. Thus
\ker(q)
corresponds to
n=0
mod 8, as claimed.

4.2
d=6

In degree 8, pertaining to anomalies in 6 dimensions, we know that there are
p(2)=2
independent gravitational anomaly polynomials, corresponding to different linear combinations of the Pontryagin numbers
p_{1}^{2}
and
p_{2}
.
Applying our general formalism, the standard 2-local bases are

{
2-locally
}

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \Hom(\Omega_{8}^{\mathrm{Spin}}(\mathrm{pt}),\mathbb{Z})&=\text{span}\big\{&\Phi_{8}(i\not{D}),\,&\Phi_{8}(i\not{D}_{\pi^{2}})&\big\}\\
\ker(q)&=\text{span}\big\{&2^{4}\Phi_{8}(i\not{D}),\,&\Phi_{8}(i\not{D}_{\pi^{2}})&\big\}\\

(101)

Note that this time, there are no

This is a new result. It says that, while
(-1)^{F}

Let us give a few more details relevant to this example. Firstly, to upgrade the 2-local basis to an actual basis, by Hattori–Stong we need to find a basis refinement of

\Hom(\Omega_{8}^{\mathrm{Spin}}(\mathrm{pt}),\mathbb{Z})=\text{span}\big\{\Phi_{8}(i\not{D}_{\theta})\,:\,\theta=1,\pi^{1},(\pi^{1})^{2},\pi^{2}\big\}

(102)

Explicit calculations give
\Phi_{8}(i\not{D})

Recall that the fields of Type IIB supergravity which carry perturbative gravitational anomalies are

5
Discussion and Outlook

To finish, we list some open questions from our work, including comments on the SymTFT interpretation.

First of all, we strongly suspect that it is possible to give a shorter, more abstract calculation of
H_{B}^{n}(\mathrm{pt})
by computing the action of
p
and
q
on the cohomology of the spectra and deducing the action on cobordism. However we lack the topological chops to do so. Although one can use the Adams spectral sequence to deduce the action on bordism, one cannot use the split exact sequence (
15
) to turn this into an action on cobordism, because the splitting is not natural. In any case, we would still prefer our eta invariant based calculation for its clear connection to the physics of gapped fermion SPT phases.

Symmetry TFT

Second, all the features of
H_{B}^{d+2}(\mathrm{pt})
we have calculated should be encoded in a
(d+1)
-dimensional TQFT called the
SymTFT
. Conversely,
H_{B}^{d+2}(\mathrm{pt})
can be used to glean a small amount of information about the SymTFT.

For
d
-dimensional fermionic theories without a gravitational anomaly, the SymTFT is known to take the form
35

17

\mathcal{Z}_{\text{SymTFT}}[M_{d+1}]=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}a_{1}\in C^{1}(M_{d+1};\mathbb{Z}_{2})\\

(115)

The most important feature for us is the shift of
\delta a_{1}
by (a cocycle representative of) the second Stiefel–Whitney class
w_{2}
. If not for this, (
115

\mathbb{Z}_{2}
gauge theory written in BF form.

Now let us ask: what boundary conditions does (
115
) have? The easiest way to answer this question is to integrate out fields. Integrating out
a_{1}
renders
\delta a_{d-1}=0
, leaving us with a twisted
\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{[d-2]}
gauge theory. Alternatively, integrating out
a_{d-1}
sets
\delta a_{1}=w_{2}
and so
a_{1}
is a spin structure, which we duly rename
\rho
, and we find an untwisted ‘spin structure gauge theory’. Thus we have the equivalences

\eqref{eq:tft}\qquad\leftrightarrow\qquad\sum_{a_{d-1}\in H^{d-1}(M_{d+1};\mathbb{Z}_{2})}(-1)^{\int_{M_{d+1}}w_{2}\smile a_{d-1}}\qquad\leftrightarrow\qquad\sum_{\rho}1

(116)

up to normalisation factors. It is then clear that we have a Dirichlet boundary condition

for

, and a Dirichlet boundary condition
\ket{\rho}
for
\rho
.

By colliding these states with a dynamical boundary condition
\ket{\mathcal{Z}}
, we obtain the two sets of partition functions

\mathcal{Z}_{F}[\rho]=\braket{\rho|\mathcal{Z}}\qquad\xrightleftharpoons[\;\text{fer}\;]{\text{bos}}\qquad\mathcal{Z}_{B}[A_{d-1}]=\braket{A_{d-1}|\mathcal{Z}}

which can be converted into each other via the change of basis matrix between the states

Adding anomalies

It is simple to generalise (
116
) to anomalous theories. Suppose we consider fermionic theories with an anomaly specified by
\mathcal{Z}_{\text{AnomSPT}}\in\mho_{\mathrm{Spin}}^{d+2}(\mathrm{pt})

Of primary interest to us is the fate of the boundary conditions
\ket{\rho}

\ket{A_{d-2}}

\ket{\rho}

At specific, other values of the anomaly, namely the nontrivial classes in

One can ask similar questions at the level of bulk topological operators. For every

However, just as for boundary conditions, the spectrum of operators is typically enriched at the various classes in

Other symmetry types

The curious reader might notice that, while we formulated bosonisation cohomology

for theories with maps to some internal space

We found that

As a purely mathematical question, one can ask if the same is true of

A well-motivated stack of examples to consider is those where

Finally, we comment that in addition to imposing internal symmetries as in

Appendix B
Stiefel–Whitney SPT Phases

For completeness, we list the technology we used to determine the SPT phases involving Stiefel–Whitney numbers stated in
section

1
. The
Sage
notebook
stiefel-whitneys.ipynb
was used to generate these results, and is shared as ancillary material with the
arXiv
submission of this paper.

Given a smooth unoriented
n
-manifold
M
, the Stiefel–Whitney numbers of
M
are subject to certain relations called the
Wu relations
. To derive them, start with the defining relation of the Wu classes

\nu_{i}\smile w^{J}=\Sq^{i}(w^{J})

(142)

where
w^{J}
represents a monomial in the Stiefel–Whitney classes, and
i+n(J)=n
so that both sides have degree
n
. The right hand side can be reduced to a polynomial in the Stiefel–Whitney classes by recursively applying the Cartan formula

\Sq^{i}(ab)=\sum_{j+k=i}\Sq^{j}(a)\Sq^{k}(b)

(143)

and the Wu formula

\Sq^{i}(w_{j})=\begin{cases}0&i>j\\
w_{j}^{2}&i=j\\
\sum_{t=0}^{i}\binom{j+t-i-1}{t}w_{i-t}w_{j+t}&i<j\end{cases}

(144)

The left hand side can be similarly reduced using additionally the recursive definition of the Wu classes

\nu_{i}=w_{i}+\sum_{j=1}^{i}\Sq^{j}(\nu_{i-j})

(145)

Thus, for each
J
with
n(J)\leq n
, we obtain a (generally nontrivial) relation among Stiefel–Whitney numbers. Dold
27
proved that these give
all
relations among Stiefel–Whitney numbers of an unoriented manifold.

For other tangential structures
H
, there are additional relations among Stiefel–Whitney numbers. They are

H

\mathrm{SO}

\mathrm{Spin}

\mathrm{Pin}^{+}

\mathrm{Pin}^{-}

additional relations

w_{1}=0

w_{1}=w_{2}=0

w_{2}=0

w_{2}+w_{1}^{2}=0

By supplementing these with the Wu relations, one can determine all Stiefel–Whitney numbers of oriented, spin, and
\mathrm{pin}^{\pm}
manifolds. It is then straightforward to prove:

•

The only nonzero Stiefel–Whitney number for
\Omega_{5}^{\mathrm{SO}}(\mathrm{pt})
is
w_{2}w_{3}
.

•

The only nonzero Stiefel–Whitney number for
\Omega_{10}^{\mathrm{SO}}(\mathrm{pt})
is
w_{4}w_{6}
.

•

The nonzero Stiefel–Whitney numbers for
\Omega_{11}^{\mathrm{Pin}^{-}}(\mathrm{pt})
are
w_{1}w_{4}w_{6}=w_{5}w_{6}
.

Under the Smith isomorphism to
\widetilde{\Omega}_{12}^{\mathrm{Spin}}(\mathit{B}\mathbb{Z}_{2})
, the first of these maps to
A^{2}w_{4}w_{6}
.

•

The only nonzero Stiefel–Whitney number for
\Omega_{20}^{\mathrm{Spin}}(\mathrm{pt})
is
w_{8}w_{12}
.

This is the first non-free-fermion SPT phase, namely the
t^{20}
in
E(t)
.

Appendix C
Clearing the powers of two in Anomaly Polynomials

In order to complete our computation of the map
p
, which goes from bosonic anomaly polynomials into fermionic ones, we need to prove some technical properties related to the normalisation of the fermionic anomaly polynomials; in particular, we need to know how to clear the 2-torsion in a general polynomial. We fill in these somewhat laborious details in this appendix.

To accompany and verify the proofs, we upload the
Sage
notebook
anom-polys.ipynb
with the
arXiv
version of this paper, which uses the formalism in this appendix to generate anomaly polynomials up to high degrees.

Recall that a fermionic polynomial for a general twisted Dirac operator, as discussed at length in the main text, takes the form

\Phi(i\not{D}_{\theta})=\hat{A}(TM)\,\theta(\mathrm{ph}(\pi^{r}(TM)))

with
\hat{A}(TM)
and
\mathrm{ph}(\pi^{r}(TM))
given by Eqs. (
49
) and (
51
) respectively. We need to analyse the powers of 2 appearing in the denominators of both parts of this general anomaly polynomial.

Claim 1:
The
\hat{A}
-genus has the following property:

\hat{A}_{n}(TM)=\frac{1}{24^{n}n!}\Big(p_{1}^{n}+2\text{(terms with odd denominator)}\Big)

(146)

This equation says that if we look at the
\hat{A}
-genus in dimension
4n
as a polynomial in the Pontryagin classes, then the term with the most powers of two in the denominator is
p_{1}^{n}
, and that power of two is read off from the equation.

Note that the factor of
n!
appearing in the denominator of this
p_{1}^{n}
term is the origin of the bitcount function appearing in many of our formulae, such as our theorem for the 2-local version of Hattori–Stong (
60
), since
\nu_{2}(n!)=n-\text{bitcount}(n)
. Here
\nu_{2}(n!)
denotes the 2-adic valuation, namely the exponent of the highest power of two that divides
n!
(for example,
\nu_{2}(12)=2
because
12
is divisible by 4 but not 8).

Proof:
This follows directly from the explicit formula
(
44
, (5.2))
for the coefficient of
p^{J}
in the
\hat{A}
-genus, which reads

\hat{A}(TM)\big|_{p^{J}}=\frac{(-1)^{|J|}}{\prod_{j}m_{j}(J)!}\sum_{S\in\Pi(|J|)}(-1)^{|S|}\prod_{s\in S}(|s|-1)!\,h_{n(J_{s})}

(147)

Here
|J|
is the number of elements of
J
,
m_{j\geq 1}(J)
is the multiplicity of
j
in
J
,
\Pi(n)
denotes all partitions of
\{1,\dots,n\}
into disjoint subsets, and
J_{s\subseteq\{1,\dots,|J|\}}=\{J_{i}:i\in s\}
. For example if
J=\{6,2,2,1\}
, then
|J|=4
,
m_{2}(J)=2
, a typical partition
S\in\Pi(4)
is
S=\{\{1,3\},\{2,4\}\}
, for which
J_{\{1,3\}}=\{6,2\}
and
J_{\{2,4\}}=\{2,1\}
. The coefficients
h_{n}
appearing above are defined by

h_{n\geq 1}=(-1)^{n-1}\left(\frac{d}{dx}\log\frac{\sqrt{x}/2}{\sinh(\sqrt{x}/2)}\right)_{x^{n-1}}=\frac{(-1)^{n}B_{2n}}{2(2n)!}=\{-\tfrac{1}{24},-\tfrac{1}{1440},\dots\}

(148)

where
B_{n}
are the Bernoulli numbers. Using the fact
B_{2n}=\frac{1}{2\cdot\text{odd}}
, we have
\nu_{2}(h_{n})=\text{bitcount}(n)-2(n+1)\geq-3n
with equality iff
n=1
. Therefore, when we compute the coefficient of
p_{1}^{n}
, the
S=\{\{1\},\dots,\{1\}\}
term is the unique term in the sum with the most powers of 2 in the denominator, and hence

\hat{A}(TM)\big|_{p_{1}^{n}}=\frac{1}{24^{n}n!}+\text{(terms with fewer powers of 2 in denominator)}

(149)

Using the exact same logic, the coefficient of any other Pontryagin monomial
p^{J}
has strictly fewer powers of two in the denominator than this.

Having dealt with the
\hat{A}(TM)
part, we now turn to the Pontryagin character part.

Claim 2:
The
\mathrm{ph}(\pi^{r})
have the following property:

\mathrm{ph}_{n}(\pi^{r})=\begin{cases}0&n<r\\
p_{r}&n=r\\
\frac{1}{2^{2(n-r)}}\text{(terms with odd denominator)}&n>r\end{cases}

(150)

Proof:

The first two of these properties were proven in the main text.
Recapping, we start
from the generating function for
\mathrm{ph}(\pi^{r})
given in Eq. (
50
).
The
t^{r}
coefficient is

\mathrm{ph}(\pi^{r}(TM))=2^{r}e_{r}(\{\cosh x_{i}-1\})
.
Taylor expanding gives

\mathrm{ph}(\pi^{r}(TM))=p_{r}+\mathcal{O}(x^{2r+2})

using
p_{r}=e_{r}(\{x_{i}^{2}\})
.

To prove the third property, start by considering the first subleading term
i.e.
the
n=r+1
term, which is

2^{r}\frac{1}{2^{r-1}}\frac{1}{4!}\sum_{j}x_{j}^{4}\cdot\left[\text{coeff.\ of $t^{r-1}$ in~}\prod_{i\neq j}(1+x_{i}^{2}t)\right]

(151)

The prefactor is

2^{r}\frac{1}{2^{r-1}}\frac{1}{4!}=\frac{1}{2^{2}\times\text{odd}}
.
We can write

\prod_{i\neq j}(1+x_{i}^{2}t)=\frac{1}{1+x_{j}^{2}t}\prod_{i}(1+x_{i}^{2}t)=\sum_{k\geq 0}(-1)^{k}x_{j}^{2k}t^{k}\times\sum_{m\geq 0}e_{m}(\{x_{i}^{2}\})t^{m}

(152)

Extracting the
t^{r-1}
term, the part in large square brackets above is the finite sum
\sum_{k=0}^{r-1}(-1)^{k}e_{r-1-k}(\{x_{i}^{2}\})x_{j}^{2k}
. Thus, the
\mathcal{O}(x^{2r+2})
term we want is

\mathrm{ph}(\pi^{r}(TM))\supset\frac{1}{2^{2}\times\text{odd}}\sum_{k=0}^{r-1}(-1)^{k}p_{r-1-k}\sum_{j}x_{j}^{2(k+2)}

(153)

Finally, one can relate the power sum
\sum_{j}x_{j}^{2k+4}
to elementary symmetric polynomials via the following partition formula, in which
J
runs over partitions of
k+2
:

\displaystyle\sum_{j}(x_{j}^{2})^{k+2}=\sum_{J\,\vdash\,k+2}(-1)^{k-|J|}(k+2)\frac{(|J|-1)!}{\prod_{j}m_{j}!}e_{\lambda}(\{x_{i}^{2}\})=p_{1}^{k+2}+\text{integral}

(154)

where
m_{j}
is, as above, the multiplicity of
j
occurring in the partition
J
.
The first term that we write explicitly on the RHS sets the normalisation of this power sum. The important thing is that it is integral, so

\mathrm{ph}(\pi^{r}(TM))\supset\frac{1}{2^{2}\times\text{odd}}\left(\text{integral stuff}\right)

(155)

This matches the property we sought to prove, for the case
n-r=1
.

This generalises going to higher order. Being a little schematic, we again pick out integral combinations of symmetric polynomials, with the same factor of
2^{r}
in the numerator but larger factorials in the denominator. In particular, the piece of order
\mathcal{O}(x^{2r+2l})
with the fewest number of powers of 2 in the denominator is that with
l
factors of
x_{i}^{4}
pulled out and
r-l
factors of
x_{i}^{2}
, which has coefficient

\frac{2^{r}}{2^{r-l}4!^{l}\times\text{odd}}\times\text{integral}\sim\frac{1}{4^{l}\times\text{odd}}\times\text{integral}

(156)

Noting
l=n-r
, this proves the claim.

Claim 3:

We now present the key claim that we needed in the main text, which is that

\hat{A}(TM)\,\mathrm{ph}(\pi^{J})|_{n}=\frac{1}{2^{4(n-n(J))-g(n-n(J))}}p_{1}^{n-n(J)}p^{J}+\text{(less powers of two in denominator)}

(157)

Proof:
Put together the previous two results. The basic point is that you can take higher-degree terms from the expansion of
\mathrm{ph}(\pi^{r})
, instead of
p_{r}
, and you get a factor of
\frac{1}{4}
for each degree you go up; but then you get a factor of
8
from going down one degree in
\hat{A}(TM)
, so the overall result is more powers of 2 in the numerator.

This result proves the structure of the map
p
that we state in the main text, which is a key ingredient in computing the bosonisation cohomology
H_{B}^{4k}=\ker(q_{4k})/\im(p_{4k})
.
