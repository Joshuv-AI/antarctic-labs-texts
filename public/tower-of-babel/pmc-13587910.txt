A national approach in a European context: Multidisciplinary and multiprofessional recommendations on the clinical use of psychedelic substances
Authors: Carolina Seybert, Ana Matos Pires, Luís Madeira, Maria João Heitor, João M. Bessa, Miguel Ricou, Helder Mota- Filipe, Albino J. Oliveira-Maia
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The therapeutic benefits of psychedelics for neuropsychiatric conditions have gathered significant attention in Europe and around the world, with treatments using psilocybin, MDMA, and ketamine as notable examples. However, the mind-altering effects of these substances, and the complexity of their regulatory frameworks, both within and outside of therapeutic use, imply careful clinical guidance. Across Europe, associations dedicated to therapeutic use of psychedelics are flourishing, and have produced clinical recommendations, while more generalist professional boards have contributed with various clinical reports, statements and guidelines. In Portugal this prompted a unique collaborative movement of professional associations of Pharmacists, Psychologists and Physicians to work with national ethics authorities, scientific societies and both research and clinical stakeholders, to produce a consensual recommendation addressing core aspects of psychedelic treatments. This statement reflects positions regarding informed consent, the determinants of medical and psychological interventions, ethical considerations and regulatory recommendations. The initial document was open for public consultation, inviting other stakeholders to contribute to the final version, published in Portuguese and translated here into English towards broader access. The statement underscores the importance of multidisciplinary and multiprofessional collaboration among psychiatrists, psychologists and pharmacists, towards establishing common guidelines and regulations to ensure safe and effective clinical applications. By addressing these factors, the recommendations aim to foster responsible therapeutic use, while safeguarding patient well-being.

Introduction

Paths for medical use of psychedelics that do not depend on the centralized European Medicines Agency pathways of approval are emerging across Europe. Switzerland has the longest-running and well-structured regulation model for psychedelic treatments (MDMA, LSD and psilocybin). It is independent of pharmaceutical approval procedures, and physicians with an additional licence are allowed to offer psychedelic treatments. These treatments are subject to a case-by-case authorisation for each patient. Processes are submitted by the physicians to the Swiss Federal Office of Public Health (FOPH), which monitors the treatment process for each of the approved patients (Liechti et al., 2025). More recently, several countries have established other Compassionate Use/Special Access Programmes, such as the recently approved psilocybin programme for treatment-resistant depression in Germany (Gründer et al., 2026), with similarly strict access, approval and/or oversight procedures. Early in 2026, the Czech Republic introduced limited access to medical psilocybin for cases of treatment-resistant depression, cancer-related depression, and severe clinical depression for whom standard treatments have failed. This decision was based on experience gathered from clinical trials testing psilocybin and ketamine, as well as qualitative findings from a ketamine-assisted therapy programme conducted as part of the ‘Czech Destigmatizing the Therapeutic Use of Psychedelics in Psychiatry Project’ (Dlestikova, 2025; Postránecká et al., 2026). In March 2026, France's approval of intravenous ketamine for severe suicidal crises marked another significant advance, offering a rapid-acting but time-limited intervention, while highlighting the need for more comprehensive, long-term suicide prevention strategies (Jollant et al., 2026). Outside of Europe, Health Canada's Special Access Programmes (SAPs) allow physicians to request case-by-case access to psilocybin or MDMA for individual patients with serious or life-threatening conditions when conventional therapies have failed, are unsuitable or are unavailable (Canada, H., 2026; Rosenblat et al., 2023). In Australia psilocybin for treatment-resistant depression and MDMA for post-traumatic stress disorder have been available on prescription under the Therapeutic Goods Administration's Authorised Prescriber Scheme since July 2023 (Australian Government - Therapeutic Goods Administration, 2023). This advancement in countries where psychedelic treatments are regulated emphasize the importance of adopting cautious measures in other countries where such regulatory frameworks do not yet exist.

Psychedelic drugs are thus addressing pressing clinical needs and gaining space within clinical care in regulated settings (Nutt et al., 2024; Page et al., 2021; PAREA, 2025), while pressing the more usual processes of approval, and raising concerns regarding the adequacy of existing processes for oversight and patient protection within special access programmes (Zeines and Evans, 2026), or the need to develop specific programmes, such as those existing in Switzerland (Liechti et al., 2025). These developments have prompted a series of guidelines and recommendations concerning the clinical use of psychedelic drugs for the treatment of neuropsychiatric disorders, published by European and national medical regulatory associations (Butlen-Ducuing et al., 2025; European Medicines Agency, 2023; Food and Drug Administration, 2023; Therapeutic Goods Administration, 2025) organized groups of clinicians (Rosenblat et al., 2023), psychedelic researchers (Pronovost-Morgan et al., 2025), and scientific associations (Destoop et al., 2025). To our knowledge, however, the definition of multidisciplinary guidelines and recommendations across the multiple professions working with these treatments is lacking.

In some European countries, professional associations hold sections dedicated to psychedelic research or clinical use, such as the Psychedelic Medicine Section in the Psychopharmacology Association in France (https://www.afpbn.org/sections/medecine-psychedelique/). On the other hand, several individual professional associations, across different countries, have issued recommendations regarding psychedelic clinical use or research. Unsurprisingly, the first was prepared and published in Switzerland by the Swiss Society for Psychiatry and Psychotherapy ‘Swiss treatment guidelines for psychedelic therapy’ prepared by (Krähenmann et al., 2023; Aicher and Gasser, 2024). This guideline was developed by psychiatrists and psychotherapists working jointly within this medical society. In the United Kingdom, the Royal College of Psychiatrists published in 2025 a position statement ‘Psychedelic and related substances for medical use (including pharmacologically assisted psychotherapy)’ and ‘Psychotherapy assisted by psychedelic and related substances: Guidelines for psychiatrists taking part in approved research trials’ (The Royal College of Psychiatrists, 2025b, 2025a). In Portugal, the Portuguese Psychologists Association published the document ‘Psychedelics and Mental Health: Definitions, Evidence and Recommendations for Psychologists’ (Ordem dos Psicólogos Portugueses, 2025). In the Czech Republic, the Czech Psychiatric Society and the Czech Neuropsychopharmacological Society, both within the Czech Medical Association of Jan Evangelista Purkyně, collaborated on the joint document ‘Recommended Guidelines for Assisted Psychotherapy with Psilocybin for Therapeutic Use’ published in a Bulletin of the Ministry of Health (Ministerstva Zdravotnictví České Republiky, 2025) with guidelines for medical use of psilocybin-assisted psychotherapy. While, to the best of our knowledge, no other country has had multiple non-psychedelic professional societies cooperating to produce guidelines, we note that the two Czech societies mentioned above are within a single medical association, limiting their interdisciplinary nature. Finally, in the Netherlands, The Dutch Psychiatric Association published the ‘Memorandum Psychedelics in Psychiatry - Guidelines for the therapeutic use of psychedelics’(Nederlandse Vereniging voor PsychedelicAlpha, 2024). We mention it last because it includes input from multiple professional groups, including hospital pharmacists, legal experts, and academic psychiatry departments, making it the closest example of a coordinated national effort. However, this was not a formal joint statement by the Dutch professional associations and thus falls short of a fully multiprofessional national consensus with representation of the associations that regulate the medical, psychological and pharmacist professions.

To address the lack of consensual recommendations across the relevant professional associations, in Portugal, a working group was established across the main associations regulating professional activity in the domain of psychopharmacological and psychological treatment of neuropsychiatric disorders, to develop recommendations on the therapeutic use of psychedelic substances. This initiative is likely representative of what is happening among most mental health practitioners in other countries who are facing these developments in psychedelic therapies and research. The development of consensual clinical recommendations about these treatments across the main professions that are or will be involved in psychedelic treatments is particularly timely and significant. Indeed, many countries are in the process of reevaluating drug policy in light of therapeutic use of psychedelic substances (Andrews et al., 2025; PsychedelicAlpha, 2026; Reddick, 2026; UC Berkeley Center for the Science of Psychedelics, 2025), and multidisciplinary and multiprofessional recommendations can assist in shaping evidence-based practice and regulation.

Material and methods

In Portugal the multidisciplinary and multiprofessional recommendations were a joint initiative by the Portuguese Society of Psychiatry and Mental Health (Sociedade Portuguesa de Psiquiatria e Saúde Mental - SPPSM), a scientific entity with evident interest and concerns with regards to therapeutic interventions in the fields of Psychiatry and Mental Health, and the Champalimaud Foundation (CF), as the entity that hosted the national coordination of clinical trials with psychedelic substances that had taken place in Portugal at that time. The regulatory structures of the main professions involved in the use of these substances, namely physicians (Portuguese Medical Association; Ordem dos Médicos - OM), pharmacists (Portuguese Pharmaceutical Society; Ordem dos Farmacêuticos - OF), and psychologists (Portuguese Psychologists Association; Ordem dos Psicólogos Portugueses - OPP) were invited to participate in this project. Since the specificities of these substances raise important ethical issues, the National Ethics Council for Life Sciences (Conselho Nacional de Ética para as Ciências da Vida - CNECV) was also invited to participate, as well as the institutions involved in the first projects for clinical or investigational use of psychedelic substances, namely the Department of Psychiatry and Mental Health of Hospital Beatriz Ângelo (DPSM-HBA; currently ULS Loures-Odivelas) and the Neuropsychiatry Unit from the Champalimaud Clinical Center (NPU-CCC).

All partners agreed to participate and appointed representatives with professional and/or academic expertise to form this multidisciplinary and multiprofessional working group to make recommendations on the clinical use of psychedelics, resulting in the creation of the expert panel of representatives that are the authors of this publication. SPPSM was represented by its president (JB), CF was represented by the national coordinator of previous clinical trials with psychedelic substances (AJOM), OM was represented by a member of the direction of the College of Psychiatry (AMP), OF was represented by its president (HMF), OPP was represented by the president of the College of Clinical and Health Psychology (MR), CNECV was represented by one its counselors (LM), DPSM-HBA was represented by its director (MJH) and NPU-CCC was represented by the lead psychologist from a clinical trial with a psychedelic substance (CS).

The procedures for development of the final recommendations followed a modified consensus methodology (Jackson et al., 2021), designed mainly to ensure continued support from the professional associations represented within the group. Following an in-person kick-off meeting in June, 2023, the representatives forming the working-group were divided into medical (AJOM, AMP, JB, MJH), psychological (CS, MR) and ethical (HMF, LM) sub-groups. Across three months, each sub-group met online once per month and collected their proposals and discussion in a shared document specific for each sub-group, edited by its members and available for viewing and commenting by members of other sub-groups. Starting in September 2023 and for an additional 3-month period, the full working-group met online once per month, for presentation and discussion of the work from each sub-group, allowing for integration of the feedback from the other experts and further refinement of the documents from each sub-group. Subsequently, AJOM and CS worked on merging all sub-group documents into a common document, that was also maintained available online for viewing and commenting by all working-group members.

The first draft of the common document was then reviewed by a legal expert external to the working-group, with all of the proposed changes validated by working-group members prior to inclusion in the final draft document. This final draft document was presented publicly in an event, occurring in May 2024 at the Champalimaud Foundation, in Lisbon, to initiate a period of public consultation of the draft recommendations. This event was publicized to the general public, with several national stakeholders invited and in attendance, including the Institute for Addictive Behaviors and Dependencies (ICAD), the National Authority of Medicines and Health Products (INFARMED), and the National Coordination of Mental Health Policy, as well as scientific societies, clinicians, advocacy groups and associations of persons with lived experience, involved or with interest in the field of clinical use of psychedelics. The national press was also present, which increased public dissemination of the public consultation period. Indeed, the draft document was distributed at the event, and made public on the website for the SPPSM following the public event, with comments collected over 2 months through a dedicated e-mail account that was setup by the CF. The comments collected using this method, while limited in number, were reviewed and discussed by all working group members in online meetings for integration into the document when consensus could be reached among the experts. The final version of the document was prepared in Portuguese and made public in an event on May 2025. A printed booklet was offered to the general public and to stakeholders who attended the event, or it was mailed to them, and was also made available online at the sites of the hosting institutions (CF, 2025; SPPSM, 2025).

Results

The original document for the Recommendations of the Multidisciplinary and Multiprofessional Working Group on the Clinical Use of Psychedelic Substances, written in Portuguese, is provided here as a supplementary document (Appendix A). Towards appropriate international dissemination, for the current manuscript, the text of the final document was translated from Portuguese to English by a professional translation service (without backtranslation) and revised by CS, with a final consensus translation approved by all authors. The English version is also available as a supplementary document (Appendix B).

Discussion

The result of the Portuguese Working Group on the Clinical Use of Psychedelic Substances was a consensual position statement discussing ethical and regulatory challenges, determinants of medical and psychological intervention, and specificities of informed consent (Seybert et al., 2023). Indeed, psychedelics are substances that have the potential to temporarily cause a subjective experience involving a qualitative change in the state of consciousness (Kelmendi et al., 2022). Administration of several such substances has been proposed as a therapeutic alternative for certain neuropsychiatric conditions, with several clinical trials reporting promising results (Goodwin et al., 2022; Mertens et al., 2026; Mitchell et al., 2023), although there is still great heterogeneity in this practice (Seybert et al., 2024), and very limited clinical use (Liechti et al., 2025). Given the clinical potential of psychedelic substances and anticipating approvals for clinical use, the multidisciplinary and multiprofessional working-group set out the emerging challenges in this area by presenting a set of recommendations in this consensus statement.

The working group focused on factors that make it difficult to protect the patient's safety and wishes, namely the change in the state of consciousness during the treatment process and the subjectivity of the psychedelic experience. Additionally, the still limited information and scientific evidence regarding the efficacy and safety of these substances (Hinkle et al., 2024), and the plurality of areas and professionals involved in clinical research and intervention, contribute to increase the complexity of the process of reflection and production of recommendations. When used for therapeutic purposes, psychedelic substances must be classified as a medicine, requiring a diagnosis, a prescription and medical intervention. On the other hand, administration of psychedelic substances is commonly accompanied by a psychological intervention, proposed as necessary to support the patient during treatment and potentially beneficial to prolong the therapeutic effects (Florineth et al., 2026). This scenario justifies that special attention be paid to specific ethical issues and to the coordination between professionals and settings, possibly with the need for special regulation. It is this special consideration for the clinical use of psychedelic substances that will, on the one hand, increase confidence in their use and, on the other, ensure that the approval of new substances will not result in disregard for the safety, well-being and autonomy of patients throughout the entire process.

It is important to note that, while this document reflects an unprecedented effort from multiple professional regulators and ethics stakeholders on the topic of psychedelic treatments for neuropsychiatric disorders, this nature of the work entails that potential limitations must also be considered. Specifically, the methodology used here was designed to ensure continued representation and support of the professional associations involved in the consensus across time, rather than following Delphi or Nominal Group Techniques, that are more typical in expert consensus (Pronovost-Morgan et al., 2025). Indeed, our methodology intentionally prioritized focused discussions among representatives of national professional regulators, that presented and negotiated the views of these regulators, rather than producing a broad academic and expert panel where participants represent individual views (Gründer et al., 2026). In a related point, the group composition was also intentionally reflecting the balance between theme-specific experts and profession-specific experts, providing societal and regulatory perspectives across medical, psychological and pharmaceutical fields, often missing in the literature. Professional regulators, as a group, have been underrepresented in consensus discussions on psychedelics, with existing consensus documents mostly involving psychedelic researchers, academics and therapists (McGuire et al., 2024; Pronovost-Morgan et al., 2025). This contribution is thus distinctive, rather than insufficiently representative, but may nevertheless lead to points where the construction of the consensus may have led to more generic, rather than specific, recommendations. This work was also not a systematic review, and reflects available evidence at the time of our consensus-building and public consultation phases, rather than a fully updated perspective of the available scientific and regulatory literature (Destoop et al., 2025). As any document of this nature, it must be considered jointly with other sources of evidence and consensus, namely those that may have been produced following its conclusion, and ideally be updated if and when necessary.

Conclusions

The recommendations prepared by this working-group recognize the problems generated by the specificities of these psychedelic substances and proposes solutions adapted to them, reflecting also a consensus between several professional associations with regulatory responsibilities. The aim was to contribute to the first steps in the development of ethical and trust-generating clinical use, towards safe use of these promising therapeutic alternatives by patients that may benefit from them (Oliveira-Maia and Seybert, 2025). The multidisciplinary nature of the group, involving different professions through their regulatory associations, aims to provide and encourage a diverse, but simultaneously global, integrated and coordinated vision, encompassing various disciplines and professions entrusted with clinical care beyond psychedelic use (Cohen and Marks, 2025). Only through cooperation and harmonizing of regulations and practices can the clinical use of psychedelics occur in a way that best protects the patient's safety and autonomy. In a spirit of cooperation and social responsibility, the Multidisciplinary and Multiprofessional Working Group on the Clinical Use of Psychedelic Substances proposed these recommendations and considerations to be submitted to public discussion, opening the space for new contributions to integrate this consensus statement and guarantee ethical, equitable, safe and beneficial clinical use of psychedelic substances, for regulated therapeutic purposes.
