Variations in post-operative cataract surgery instructions among eye health workers in Tanzania: a cross-sectional survey
Authors: Kazim Akber Dhalla, Saqalain S Kassamali, Thomas Mbezi, Kibrom Teferi, Eustochius G Rugabela
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeTo investigate variations in post-operative cataract surgery instructions among eye health workers in Tanzania and identify areas requiring standardization.MethodsA cross-sectional survey was conducted among 142 eye health workers across all health workers providing cataract surgery or post-operative care in Tanzania. The survey assessed counselling practices, timing recommendations for resuming 23 daily activities, medication prescription patterns, and follow-up protocols. Variation indices (VI) were calculated to quantify the degree of disagreement among providers using the formula: VI = 1 - (modal frequency/total responses). Variation was classified as low (VI < 0.40), moderate (0.40–0.60), high (0.60–0.70), or very high (> 0.70).ResultsSurvey respondents included ophthalmology residents (28%), consultant ophthalmologists (22%), ophthalmic nursing officers (17%), assistant medical officers (13%), and optometrists (12.0%). Only 30%of providers routinely provide written post-operative instructions to patients. Substantial variations existed across most activities examined: watching television (VI = 0.775), computer use (VI = 0.761), smartphone use (VI = 0.754), bending for prayers (VI = 0.725), and sexual activity (VI = 0.690). Recommendations for activity resumption ranged from Day 1 to 6 weeks post-operatively (a 41-day span) for most activities. Good consensus existed only for phone use (59.2% allow Day 1, VI = 0.323) and eating normal diet (48.6% allow Day 1, VI = 0.434). Provision of written instructions varied significantly by professional designation (χ²=28.85, p = 0.0013), but activity recommendations showed no significant correlation with designation, years of experience, or surgical volume. The modal number of follow-up visits was 4 (32.4%), with the last visit most commonly scheduled at 8 weeks (30.3%).ConclusionsSubstantial variations in post-operative cataract surgery instructions reflect the absence of evidence-based guidelines rather than individual provider factors. The critical deficit in written instruction provision (only 30%) and wide variation in activity restrictions have significant implications for patient compliance, quality of life, and economic productivity. Development of standardized, evidence-based, culturally appropriate guidelines is urgently needed for Tanzania and similar settings in sub-Saharan Africa.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12886-026-04976-6.Keywords: Cataract surgery, Post-operative care, Practice variation, Tanzania, Sub-saharan Africa, Patient instructions, Quality improvement, Evidence-based guidelines

Introduction

Cataract remains the leading cause of blindness worldwide and contributes substantially to the global burden of visual impairment, especially in sub-Saharan countries [1]. Despite recent improvements, Tanzania’s cataract surgical rate remains below the Vision2020 targets of 3,000 cataract surgeries per million population per year [2, 3]. As efforts intensify to increase cataract surgical coverage, ensuring high-quality post-operative care becomes increasingly important for achieving optimal visual outcomes and patient satisfaction.

Post-operative instructions following cataract surgery traditionally include comprehensive guidance on topical medications, activity restrictions, hygiene practices, and follow-up visit schedules [4, 5]. These instructions aim to minimize complications such as endophthalmitis, wound dehiscence, and increased intraocular pressure while promoting healing and visual rehabilitation. However, the evidence base supporting many commonly recommended activity restrictions remains surprisingly limited, particularly regarding the optimal duration and necessity of specific restrictions [6, 7].

A landmark study by Wu and Morrell in 2001 surveyed ophthalmologists across the United Kingdom and revealed considerable heterogeneity in post-operative cataract surgery instructions, particularly regarding activities such as heavy lifting, driving, and bending [8]. This variation suggested that many restrictions may be based on tradition, personal training experience, or theoretical concerns rather than robust scientific evidence. Since that time, advances in surgical technique—particularly the widespread adoption of small-incision phacoemulsification with suture less clear corneal incisions—have fundamentally changed the biomechanical considerations that might justify activity restrictions.

The context in Tanzania and East Africa presents unique challenges and considerations. Multiple cadres of eye health workers perform cataract surgery and provide post-operative care, including consultant ophthalmologists, ophthalmology residents, and assistant medical officers with specialty training in ophthalmology, ophthalmic nursing officers, and optometrists. These professionals have diverse training backgrounds, varying levels of access to current evidence-based resources, and work within institutions with different protocols and resource.

Availability. Additionally, cultural factors such as the importance of prayer postures, economic considerations regarding work resumption, and literacy levels that may affect comprehension of written versus verbal instructions all influence the appropriateness and implementation of post-operative care protocols.

To date, no studies have quantitatively assessed the extent of variations in post-operative cataract surgery instructions in sub-Saharan Africa. Understanding current practices and identifying areas of significant disagreement among providers is essential for developing appropriate, evidence-based guidelines that can improve the consistency and quality of post-operative care. Furthermore, identifying areas where providers already demonstrate consensus can help validate existing practices and focus guideline development efforts on areas of greatest need.

This study aimed to assess current postoperative instruction practices among eye health workers in Tanzania, evaluate the extent of variation across different domains of postoperative care, identify areas of consensus and those requiring urgent standardization, and provide an evidence base to support the development of national or regional postoperative cataract surgery guidelines appropriate for the East African context.

Methods

A cross-sectional survey study was conducted among eye health workers providing cataract surgery or post-operative care in Tanzania. The study was conducted at the Tanzania Ophthalmology Society (TOS) annual scientific congress held in Morogoro on 23rd and 24th June 2022. This was the 3rd “All Eye Health Workers Congress in Tanzania” whose theme was “2030 in Sight: Primary Eye Care in Tanzania, Why It Matters”. This is the annual scientific meeting where all the eye health professionals of different cadres and the stake holders meet. Permission to conduct the survey was sought and approval received from the TOS secretariat.

The study population comprised consultant ophthalmologists, ophthalmology residents, assistant medical officers (AMOs) with specialty training in ophthalmology, ophthalmic nursing officers, optometrists, and other eye care professionals involved in post-operative cataract care working in different parts of Tanzania. Conference participants were informed about the survey study and its importance to the post-operative eye care. Participants had the option of not to participate in the study. Pre tested data collection forms were distributed to all the participants and collected for data entry and analysis. There were no exclusion criteria other than non-involvement in cataract surgical care.

Statistical analysis

Data were entered and analyzed using Microsoft Excel (Microsoft Corporation, Redmond, WA, USA). Descriptive statistics including means, standard deviations, and frequencies were calculated. To quantify the degree of variation or disagreement among providers for each activity recommendation, we calculated a variation index (VI) using the formula: VI = 1 - (modal frequency / total valid responses).

This index ranges from 0 (complete consensus, all respondents chose the same option) to approaching 1(maximum disagreement, responses evenly distributed across all options). We classified variation as follows: low variation (VI < 0.40), moderate variation (VI 0.40–0.60), high variation (VI 0.60–0.70), and very high variation (VI > 0.70).

Chi-square tests were used to examine associations between categorical variables.

Kruskal-Wallis test were used for non-parametric comparisons of continuous variables across multiple groups. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05 (two-tailed). Stratified analyses were performed to assess whether activity recommendations varied by professional designation, years of experience, organization type, or surgical volume.

Results

Respondent characteristics

A total of 142 of the 180 eye health workers completed the survey giving a response rate of 79%. Professional backgrounds were diverse. Majority of the participants were Ophthalmic surgeons, 63% (n = 89), divided into consultant Ophthalmologist 22%(n = 31), Ophthalmology residents 28% (n = 39) and Assistant Medical Officers specialized in ophthalmology (AMO-O) also known as non-physician ophthalmologists or Cataract surgeons 13% (n = 19). Other cadres included ophthalmic nursing officers 17% (n = 24), optometrists 12.0% (n = 17), and other 9% (n = 12).

The majority of respondents worked in government facilities (63%), followed by private facilities (18%), mission hospitals (12.0%), and non-governmental organizations (7.0%). Regarding experience, 32.4% had less than 5 years of experience, 28.2% had 5–10 years, 23.9% had 11–20 years, and 15.5% had more than 20 years of experience in eye care.

Half of all respondents (50.0%, n = 71) reported currently performing cataract surgery. Among those performing surgery, the annual surgical volume varied widely: 29.6% performed fewer than 50 cases annually, 25.4%performed 50–100 cases, 23.9% performed 101–200 cases, 12.7% performed 201–500 cases, and 8.5%performed more than 500 cases annually. Counselling practices and written instructions

The majority of respondents (81.0%, n = 115) reported providing routine post-operative counselling to cataract surgery patients. However, only 30% (n = 43) of all respondents reported providing written instruction sheets to patients, while 61% (n = 87) provided no written instructions and 9% (n = 12) were uncertain or gave nonresponse.

The provision of written instructions varied significantly by professional designation (χ²=28.85, df = 10, p = 0.0013). Ophthalmic nursing officers had the highest rate of providing written instructions (50.0%), followed by consultant ophthalmologists (38%), residents (19%), and AMOs (5.3%). When examining combined patterns of instruction delivery: 26.1% provided both verbal and written instructions, 51.4% provided verbal instructions only, 14.8% provided no clear pattern of instruction, and 7.7% provided neither routine verbal nor written instructions.

Activity resumption recommendations and variations

Substantial variation was observed across nearly all 23 activities assessed. Three activities showed very high variation (VI > 0.70): watching television (VI = 0.775), computer use (VI = 0.761), and smartphone chatting (VI = 0.754), while five activities demonstrated high variation (VI = 0.60–0.70), including bending for prayers (VI = 0.725), walking (VI = 0.718), cooking (VI = 0.639), office work (VI = 0.619), and sexual activity (VI = 0.690). Moderate variation (VI = 0.40–0.60) was noted for washing the face (VI = 0.535), reading (VI = 0.549), manual labor (VI = 0.577), and driving (VI = 0.577). (Fig. 2) For most activities, recommendations ranged widely from postoperative Day 1 to 6 weeks, spanning up to 41 days and reflecting clinically meaningful differences in patient guidance; for example, face washing was permitted from Day 1 by 46.5% of respondents but delayed up to 6 weeks by 2.8% (VI = 0.535), sexual activity ranged from Day 1 (1.4%) to beyond 6 weeks with 1 month most common (31.0%, VI = 0.690), walking was allowed from Day 1 by 25.4% yet restricted until 6 weeks by 28.2% (VI = 0.718), bending for prayers varied from Day 1 (12.7%) to 6 weeks (19.7%) with 2 weeks most frequent (26.1%, VI = 0.725), computer use ranged from Day 1 (15.5%) to 6 weeks (22.5%, VI = 0.761), and watching television from Day 1 (14.1%) to 6 weeks (23.9%, VI = 0.775). In contrast, only two activities demonstrated relatively good consensus with low variation: talking on the phone (59.2% recommended Day 1, VI = 0.323) and eating a normal diet (48.6% recommended Day 1, VI = 0.434). Medication prescription patterns

Among respondents, the modal number of routine post-operative oral medications prescribed was 2 drugs (55.6%), followed by 5 drugs (26.1%), 1 drug (15.5%), 3 drugs (1.4%), and 4 drugs (1.4%). Among the 52respondents who specified generic drug names, paracetamol (acetaminophen) was by far the most commonly prescribed (51.9%), typically given 8-hourly (77.8%) for a duration of 3 days (63.0%). Acetazolamide, a carbonic anhydrase inhibitor used for intraocular pressure control, was prescribed routinely by 11.5% of respondents.

Topical medication regimens (antibiotic and anti-inflammatory eye drops) were not systematically assessed in this survey, as these are considered standard care with less variation.

Follow-up care protocols

The number of routine post-operative visits scheduled varied among providers. The modal number was 4 visits (32.4%), with a mean of 3.32 ± 1.47 visits (range 1–8). The distribution was: 1 visit (4.2%), 2 visits (14.1%), 3 visits (23.9%), 4 visits (32.4%), 5 visits (16.2%), and 6 or more visits (9.2%). Regarding timing of the last scheduled post-operative visit: 30.3% scheduled it at 8 weeks, 21.8% at 12 weeks, 14.1% at 6 weeks, 12.0% at 4 weeks, and smaller percentages at other time points ranging from 1 week to 6months.

The timing when final spectacle prescription is given also varied: 21.8% at 12 weeks, 21.1% at 8 weeks, 19.7%at 6 weeks, 18.3% at 4 weeks, and the remainder at other time points. No significant variation in follow-up patterns was found by professional designation (Kruskal-Wallis test, p > 0.05).

Discussion

This study provides the first quantitative assessment of variations in post-operative cataract surgery instructions in sub-Saharan Africa. Our findings reveal substantial and clinically significant heterogeneity across all domains examined, with particularly concerning deficits in the provision of written instructions and extreme variations in recommendations for activity resumption.

The variation indices exceeding 0.70 for computer use, television watching, and smartphone use indicate extreme disagreement among providers—responses were nearly evenly distributed across all time periods from Day 1 to 6 weeks. For most activities, the 41-day span of recommendations (from Day 1 to 6 weeks) represents profound clinical inconsistency that would be immediately apparent to patients who compare notes with others who had surgery with different providers.

Some specific findings merit particular attention. The 28% of providers who restrict walking until 6 weeks post-operatively lacks biological plausibility, as modern small-incision cataract surgery with self-sealing wounds does not justify such prolonged immobilization. Conversely, the 46.5% allowing face washing from Day 1 aligns more closely with current evidence, which suggests this is safe provided patients avoid directing forceful stream of water directly into the operated eye.(Fig. 3) [9].

Recent guidelines from the American Academy of Ophthalmology and the European Society of Cataract and Refractive Surgeons recommend considerably less restrictive approaches than many of the practices observed in this study [8, 10, 11]. current evidence-based recommendations supporting face and hair washing from Day 1 (while avoiding direct water streams into the eye), return to most normal activities such as reading, watching television, and computer use within days, return to work for most occupations within a few days, swimming after 2–4 weeks, no specific restrictions on bending, walking, or stair climbing with modern small-incision techniques, and individualized assessment only for activities involving heavy physical exertion or risk of eye trauma. Our finding that recommendations for computer use range from Day 1 to 6 weeks, with a variability index (VI) of 0.761, highlights the urgent need for clear, evidence-based guidance adapted to the Tanzanian context, as the biological rationale for restricting screen-based activities after uncomplicated small-incision cataract surgery remains unclear, yet such restrictions are commonly imposed and may unnecessarily limit patients’ ability to work and participate in contemporary daily activities.

Several findings have particular relevance in the Tanzanian cultural and economic context. Bending for prayers (VI = 0.725) is especially important for both Muslim patients and Christians who constitute a significant proportion of Tanzania’s population.

Perhaps most significantly, variations in recommendations for returning to work have direct economic consequences. Office work recommendations varied from Day 1 to 6 weeks (VI = 0.619), while manual labor recommendations showed VI = 0.577. In a low-income country where many patients work in the informal sector with no paid leave, unnecessarily prolonged work restrictions impose substantial economic hardship. Assuming approximately 15,000 cataract surgeries performed annually in Tanzania, with roughly 60% of patients being of working age, and using conservative estimates of daily wages (average 10,000–20,000 Tanzanian Shillings or$4–8 USD), unnecessary work restrictions lasting even 1–2 weeks could collectively cost patients and the national economy $2–3 million annually in lost productivity. This does not account for the multiplier effects on family members who may need to take time off to care for patients unnecessarily restricted from normal activities.

Perhaps the most actionable finding of this study is that only 30% of providers routinely give patients written post-operative instructions (Fig. 1c). This is concerning because, without written guidance, patients must rely entirely on imperfect recall of verbal information provided during a stressful peri-operative period. Extensive research in medical education and patient communication demonstrates that patients typically forget 40–80% of verbal medical information within 24 h [12, 14, 15]. Complex instructions involving multiple medications, activity restrictions, warning signs, and follow-up timing are particularly vulnerable to recall failures.

The wide variations in post-operative advice documented in this study have important implications for patient care and health system quality in Tanzania. Inconsistent recommendations across facilities indicate a lack of standardized care, which can confuse patients, increase anxiety, and undermine trust in providers [13]. Many restrictions appear to lack an evidence base and may unnecessarily limit patients’ quality of life, ability to work, and social or religious participation, while overly restrictive or unclear advice may also increase the risk of non-compliance with essential post-operative instructions [6, 7, 11]. Furthermore, substantial variability in post-operative care complicates the assessment of surgical outcomes and hinders quality improvement efforts, and unnecessary work restrictions impose avoidable economic costs on patients and the wider economy [16, 17].

Our findings align with the global shift in ophthalmology toward less restrictive postoperative guidance following advances in small-incision cataract surgery, which have reduced the biomechanical risks that previously justified prolonged activity limitations. While early studies, such as those from the UK in the early 2000s, reported similar variability, more recent evidence from developed countries shows increasing convergence toward less restrictive practices. The continued use of highly restrictive recommendations in Tanzania likely reflects delayed dissemination of updated evidence, persistence of training based on older surgical techniques [16], concerns about managing complications in resource-limited settings, and the absence of litigation pressures that have encouraged evidence-based practice elsewhere [18, 19].

Recommendation

Based on these findings, we recommend developing context-appropriate post-operative cataract surgery guidelines for Tanzania that focus on activities with the greatest variation, particularly screen use, bending for prayers, sexual activity, return to work, and physical activity. These guidelines should be grounded in the best available evidence, drawing on recent international guidance and expert consensus where data are limited, while remaining culturally relevant to local practices and occupations. Each recommendation should include a clear biological rationale, incorporate risk stratification for uncomplicated versus complicated cases, and be supported by standardized patient education materials in both Kiswahili and English tailored to different literacy levels.

Limitation

The survey relied on self-reported usual practices rather than actual patient-specific instructions or outcomes, which may differ in real clinical settings. Although participants represented diverse cadres and facilities, the sample may not fully reflect national practice. Fixed timing categories may have oversimplified nuanced advice, and patient perspectives, adherence, and clinical outcomes were not assessed. Despite these limitations, the study provides valuable baseline data on post-cataract surgery instruction practices in Tanzania and offers a foundation for future guideline development and quality improvement. We also acknowledge that the type of surgery was not explicitly mentioned in the questionnaire. However, during data collection, participants were verbally informed that the surgical technique was Manual Small Incision Cataract Surgery (MSICS), as the vast majority of cataract extractions in our region are performed using this method.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates substantial and clinically meaningful variation in post-operative cataract surgery instructions among eye health workers in Tanzania, alongside a major deficit in written patient guidance. The wide variability in activity recommendations and the absence of associations with provider characteristics indicate a systemic issue that requires standardized, system-level solutions rather than individual provider training. Key priorities include mandating written post-operative instructions, developing evidence-based and culturally appropriate regional guidelines, and implementing quality monitoring mechanisms to support adoption. Guideline development should focus on activities with the greatest variability, such as screen use, bending for prayers [20], sexual activity, return to work, and walking. Overall, these findings provide a strong evidence base for improving consistency, quality, and patient-centeredness of post-operative cataract care in Tanzania and similar sub-Saharan African settings.
