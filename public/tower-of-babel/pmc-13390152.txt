Bilayer nerve guidance conduits for continuous delivery of NGF@ZIF-8 nanoparticles for peripheral nerve injury repair
Authors: Xu Zhang, Qian Zhu, Hao Rong, Siyao Zhu, Pan Feng, Jing Li, Yusong Wang, Hong Cheng, Wenbo Liu, Jie Sun, Zhisen Shen, Jilai Li, Yangnan Hu, Renjie Chai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Peripheral nerve injury remains a major clinical challenge due to limited regenerative capacity and insufficient therapeutic strategies. In this study, we developed biomimetic nerve guidance conduits composed of high-concentration gelatin methacryloyl (GelMA) hydrogel (GMH), providing mechanical support and structural integrity. The conduits lumen was filled with a softer, low-concentration GelMA hydrogel (GML) incorporating zeolitic imidazolate framework-8 (ZIF-8) nanoparticles encapsulating nerve growth factor (NGF), enabling sustained neurotrophic delivery. This bilayer design combines mechanical reinforcement with localized biochemical stimulation. In vitro experiments demonstrate that the system can continuously release NGF, exhibits good cellular compatibility, promotes neurite outgrowth in PC-12 cells, and can facilitate the conversion of RAW264.7 cells to an anti-inflammatory phenotype. Rat sciatic nerve injury model experiments have demonstrated that these nerve guidance conduits have a beneficial effect on the regeneration and functional recovery of nerve axons. Together, these findings highlight a versatile platform for nerve guidance conduits with the potential to improve functional recovery in peripheral nerve regeneration.Graphical abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12951-026-04553-5.

Introduction

Peripheral nerve injury (PNI) remains a critical clinical challenge, often leading to chronic disability and poor functional recovery [1–4]. These injuries frequently result in the immediate loss of motor and sensory functions, and despite advancements in microsurgical techniques, recovery is often slow and incomplete. This leads to significant lifelong impairments for patients and constitutes a substantial socioeconomic burden due to healthcare costs and lost productivity [5–8]. Autologous nerve grafting is the current gold standard, yet its clinical application is severely limited by donor-site morbidity, limited available graft length, and structural mismatches [9–13]. Consequently, engineered nerve guidance conduits (NGCs) have emerged as promising alternatives, aiming to replicate the structural, mechanical, and biochemical features of native nerves. However, conventional NGCs often lack sufficient mechanical strength, bioactivity, and controlled delivery of neurotrophic factors, leading to inadequate axonal regeneration and incomplete functional restoration [14–17]. Therefore, the development of a versatile platform for nerve guidance conduits holds great significance for advancing clinical therapies of peripheral nerve injuries.

To address these shortcomings, advanced biomaterials with tunable physicochemical properties are required. Hydrogels, particularly those derived from the extracellular matrix, have shown great promise in neural tissue engineering due to their high water content, inherent biocompatibility, and similarity to soft tissues [18–22]. Among these, gelatin methacryloyl (GelMA) has emerged as a leading candidate, as its mechanical stiffness can be precisely controlled through photocrosslinking to match that of nerve tissue, and it provides a favorable environment for cell adhesion and proliferation [23–30]. An ideal NGC must not only offer a protective sheath for the regenerating nerve but also present a permissive and stimulating internal environment. This necessitates a sophisticated design that combines robust structural support with a bioactive inner matrix capable of guiding axonal growth and modulating the local cellular responses [31–35]. Notably, self-assembled nanostructured materials have attracted increasing attention for optimizing NGC design, as exemplified by virus-assembled biofunctional microarrays with hierarchical 3D nano-reticular networks and self-assembling nanofibrous bacteriophage microgels—these materials enable the construction of biofunctional platforms with tailored structural hierarchy and targeted functionalities, providing valuable insights for engineering high-performance NGCs [36, 37]. For effective regeneration, structural support must be complemented with localized and sustained biochemical cues. Nerve growth factor (NGF) is a key neurotrophin that promotes neuronal survival and axonal elongation, but its clinical use is hampered by instability and rapid degradation in vivo [38–41]. To overcome this issue, nanoparticle-based delivery systems have been developed. Zeolitic imidazolate framework-8 (ZIF-8), a zinc-based metal–organic framework, offers a high specific surface area, tunable pore size, and pH-responsive degradation, making it an efficient nanocarrier for biomolecules [10, 42–44]. Encapsulation of NGF within ZIF-8 nanoparticles enhances its stability and enables sustained release within hydrogel matrices, thus maintaining prolonged bioactivity at the site of nerve injury [45]. This approach addresses the key limitation of burst release observed in conventional hydrogel-based delivery systems.

Here, we propose biomimetic nerve conduits constructed from dual-concentration gelatin methacryloyl (GelMA) hydrogels, in which the outer layer of 15 wt% high-concentration GelMA hydrogel (GMH) provides robust mechanical integrity, while the inner lumen of 5 wt% low-concentration GelMA hydrogel (GML) encapsulates ZIF-8 nanoparticles loaded with NGF (Fig. 1). This bilayer design integrates structural reinforcement with controlled neurotrophic delivery. These composite conduits can promote the differentiation of neuron-like PC-12 cells and the M2-type conversion of RAW264.7 cells in vitro. In vivo, they can improve axon guidance and continuously promote neurite extension, ultimately enhancing the regeneration and functional recovery of peripheral nerves in rats. By combining hydrogels with growth factors mediated by nanocarriers, this study provides a new strategy for peripheral nerve regeneration, with translational clinical potential for peripheral nerve repair.

Results and discussion

To prepare bilayer GelMA hydrogel conduits with different concentrations, we first prepared GMH for the outer layer and GML for the inner filling. Both hydrogels were rapidly cured under ultraviolet light [46, 47]. Subsequently, GMH and GML were characterized using scanning electron microscopy, revealing uniform porous structures in both, with GML showing larger pore sizes than GMH (Fig. 2A, B). Then, we synthesized ZIF-8 nanoparticles and in situ synthesized NGF-loaded ZIF-8 nanoparticles (NGF@ZIF-8). Scanning electron microscopy showed that the particle sizes distributions of both ZIF-8 and NGF@ZIF-8 were evenly distributed (Fig. 2C, D). The Zeta potential of NGF@ZIF-8 was slightly higher than that of ZIF-8, and its particle size was also significantly larger (Fig. 2E, F). The DLS test results showed that the particle sizes of ZIF-8 and NGF@ZIF-8 groups were 177.5 nm and 226.9 nm, respectively, which were consistent with the statistical trends of the scanning electron microscopy results, indicating successful loading of NGF (Figure S1A). Furthermore, the experimental determination revealed that the encapsulation efficiency of NGF in NGF@ZIF-8 nanoparticles was 27.55 ± 1.665%, with a drug loading capacity of 547.8 ± 15.84 ng/mg (Figure S1B).

The FTIR spectra of GML, ZIF-8, and NGF@ZIF-8 are shown in Fig. 2G. For the GML group, characteristic amide absorption bands were observed at 1636.6 cm⁻¹ (amide I, C = O stretching), 1526.1 cm⁻¹ (amide II, N–H bending and C–N stretching), and 1232.1 cm⁻¹ (amide III, C–N stretching and N–H bending), which are typical for gelatin-based polymers. In the ZIF-8 spectrum, the characteristic peaks of the imidazole framework were detected at 1565.7 cm⁻¹ (C = N stretching), 1382.8 cm⁻¹ (C–N stretching), and 423.8 cm⁻¹ (Zn–N coordination bond), confirming the successful formation of the ZIF-8 structure. Compared with ZIF-8, the NGF@ZIF-8 group exhibited the retention of the Zn–N vibration at 423.8 cm⁻¹ and the imidazole-related peaks, while additional amide-related signals appeared at 1649.5 cm⁻¹ (amide I), indicating the successful encapsulation of NGF into the ZIF-8 framework. The slight shift of the amide I band from 1636.6 cm⁻¹ (GML) to 1649.5 cm⁻¹ (NGF@ZIF-8) suggests potential interactions between NGF and the ZIF-8 host matrix. These results collectively demonstrate that ZIF-8 was successfully synthesized and that NGF was effectively incorporated into the ZIF-8 framework while maintaining its protein structural features. The crystalline structures of GML, ZIF-8, and NGF@ZIF-8 were further characterized by XRD (Fig. 2H). The GML group displayed a broad and weak diffraction hump, suggesting its amorphous nature. In contrast, the ZIF-8 group exhibited sharp diffraction peaks at 7.4°, 10.5°, 12.8°, and 18.1°, which are consistent with the characteristic diffraction planes of the sodalite topology of ZIF-8, confirming its successful crystallization. For the NGF@ZIF-8 group, the diffraction pattern retained the characteristic peaks of ZIF-8 without significant peak shifts, indicating that the crystalline framework of ZIF-8 was well preserved after NGF loading. The comparable peak intensities further suggest that the incorporation of NGF did not destroy the ordered crystalline structure of ZIF-8. These results confirm the successful synthesis of crystalline ZIF-8 and demonstrate that NGF was effectively encapsulated while maintaining the structural integrity of the ZIF-8 framework.

To investigate the effect of pH value changes in the neural injury microenvironment on factor release, we examined the release kinetics of NGF and zinc ions (Zn²⁺) at physiological pH (7.4) and acidic pH (6.0). At pH 6.0, the release of NGF and Zn²⁺ increased significantly over time. In contrast, at pH 7.4, NGF release remained at a low level without a significant time-dependent increase. Furthermore, compared to directly exposing NGF@ZIF-8 particles to a pH 6.0 sustained-release solution, the NGF@ZIF-8/GML group, where NGF@ZIF-8 particles are encapsulated within a hydrogel, exhibited a more gradual release, providing a longer duration of NGF release (Fig. 2I, J). Given that neural injury induces local acidification (decrease in pH) at the injury site, this result indicates that an acidic microenvironment promotes the sustained release of NGF and Zn²⁺, which may provide continuous neurotrophic support locally for neural repair, thus modulating the injured microenvironment and contributing to neural repair.

Next, the swelling and degradation properties of four groups of hydrogels were tested. The results showed that the GMH group exhibited lower swelling rates and slower degradation compared to the other three groups, providing foundational support for its use as the outer layer of NGCs. The other three groups gradually degraded over time, providing ample space for regenerating nerves (Fig. 2K, S2). Subsequently, NGCs were fabricated using molds, with those produced from GMH named Hollow, the group internally filled with GML named GML, the group internally filled with ZIF-8/GML named ZIF-8/GML, and the group internally filled with NGF@ZIF-8/GML named NGF@ZIF-8/GML (Figure S3). Compression tests were conducted on the four groups of NGCs, revealing that the NGCs filled with hydrogels demonstrated enhanced compressive strength, capable of providing robust support for neural regeneration (Fig. 2L). To characterize the rheological properties of the hydrogels, we evaluated the modulus and viscosity of GMH, GML, ZIF-8/GML, and NGF@ZIF-8/GML. GMH exhibited significantly higher storage modulus compared to the other groups, demonstrating a solid-like elastic behavior for structural support. In contrast, the storage modulus of GML, ZIF-8/GML, and NGF@ZIF-8/GML was considerably lower, and the loading of ZIF-8 and NGF did not alter this soft profile (Fig. 2M). In terms of viscosity, all four groups exhibited shear-thinning behavior, with ZIF-8/GML and NGF@ZIF-8/GML retaining this fluidity to support NGF delivery (Fig. 2N). Overall, GMH provides robust support, while NGF@ZIF-8/GML enables efficient biologically active transport.

First, we detected the cytotoxic effects of NGF@ZIF-8 nanoparticles at different concentrations on RSC96 cells and found that 50 µg/mL exerted no cytotoxicity on the cells. Further increasing the concentration to 100 µg/mL revealed that the nanoparticles exhibited higher cytotoxicity. However, when we encapsulated NGF@ZIF-8 nanoparticles into GML hydrogel to form NGF@ZIF-8/GML, we found that it could encapsulate up to 1000 µg/mL of nanoparticles without causing cytotoxicity. (Figure S4). Subsequently, we cultured cells in a hydrogel containing 1000 µg/mL nanoparticle concentration and performed live-dead cell staining, which consistently demonstrated the excellent biocompatibility of the hydrogel. (Figure S5).

To elucidate the effects of various hydrogel groups on cellular behavior and immunomodulation during the nerve regeneration process, we conducted functional assays. The cell scratch migration assay demonstrated that after 24 h, NGF@ZIF-8/GML promoted a greater cell migration area than the GML and ZIF-8/GML groups. This indicates enhanced cellular recruitment potential, which is crucial for tissue regeneration (Fig. 3A, B). In promoting PC-12 cell differentiation, Tuj-1 staining showed that the NGF@ZIF-8/GML group significantly enhanced the extension of neurites, effectively inducing PC-12 cell differentiation (Fig. 3C, D). Under LPS-induced M1 polarization, NGF@ZIF-8/GML significantly reduced CD86 fluorescence (M1 marker), thereby inhibiting pro-inflammatory M1 activation (Fig. 3E, F). Under IL-4-induced M2 polarization, NGF@ZIF-8/GML increased CD206 fluorescence (M2 marker) compared with the GML and ZIF-8/GML groups, promoting reparative M2 polarization (Fig. 3E, G). This dual regulation of macrophage phenotypes creates an anti-inflammatory, repair-friendly immune microenvironment. Overall, NGF@ZIF-8/GML can promote cell migration, induce PC-12 cell differentiation, and polarization of RAW264.7 cells toward an anti-inflammatory M2 phenotype, all favoring nerve regeneration [48, 49].

To evaluate the efficacy of hydrogels in promoting neural regeneration in vivo, we established a 10 mm sciatic nerve transection model in SD rats. Subsequently, we assessed the structural and functional recovery of the bridged nerves in the Hollow, GML, NGF@ZIF-8/GML, and autograft (AUTO) groups. Histologically, Schwann cells were labeled with S100β and neurofilaments with NF200. Immunofluorescence staining revealed that after 3 weeks of repair, NGF@ZIF-8/GML significantly increased the length of regenerated nerve fibers (Fig. 4A, B). After 12 weeks of repair, NGF@ZIF-8/GML also demonstrated the optimal repair outcome. Compared to the other two groups, its percentage of S100β and NF200 positive areas was significantly higher and showed no statistically significant difference compared with the AUTO group, indicating that its regenerative efficacy was comparable to AUTO (Fig. 4C-E). Furthermore, immunofluorescence staining for MBP and Tuj-1 was performed to further evaluate axonal myelination and regeneration. The NGF@ZIF-8/GML group exhibited very abundant MBP-positive myelin structures and Tuj-1-positive regenerating axons. These results further validate that the NGF@ZIF-8/GML bilayer conduits can effectively promote myelin maturation and sustained axonal regeneration, achieving a regenerative effect close to that of autologous nerve transplantation (Figure S6). The sciatic functional index (SFI) is a well-recognized classic behavioral gold standard for evaluating peripheral nerve functional recovery in rodent models and is further applied to quantitatively assess the status of nerve function recovery [5, 50]. SFI can comprehensively reflect the recovery of motor nerve conduction, target muscle nerve innervation, and coordinated movement, and has been widely used in high-quality studies on peripheral nerve regeneration mediated by nerve guidance conduits [51, 52]. In this study, the SFI values of the NGF@ZIF-8/GML group were significantly higher than those of the Hollow group and showed no significant difference compared to the AUTO group, directly indicating that our bilayer conduits significantly promotes the recovery of motor nerve function (Fig. 4F, G).

To evaluate the efficacy of each hydrogel group in promoting target tissue recovery and modulating the immune microenvironment after nerve repair, we analyzed muscle recovery at 12 weeks postoperative repair and inflammatory levels at 3 weeks post-repair. The muscle wet weight ratio results indicated that NGF@ZIF-8/GML more effectively alleviated the atrophy of the gastrocnemius muscle, comparable to the AUTO group (Fig. 5A, B). Masson’s trichrome staining revealed that the NGF@ZIF-8/GML group exhibited a larger muscle cross-sectional area, and statistical analysis demonstrated that this group’s muscle cross-sectional area was more similar to that of the AUTO group compared to the other two groups (Fig. 5C, D). Macrophage polarization staining demonstrated that NGF@ZIF-8/GML had a significantly lower proportion of pro-inflammatory CD86⁺ M1 macrophages compared with the Hollow group, while showing an increase in anti-inflammatory CD206⁺ M2 macrophages, approaching levels comparable to the AUTO group (Fig. 5E-G). This suggests that NGF@ZIF-8/GML can remodel the immune microenvironment to a pro-reparative state. Therefore, NGF@ZIF-8/GML can promote axonal myelination recovery, alleviate muscle atrophy and fibrosis, and modulate macrophage polarization, thereby facilitating comprehensive recovery of target tissues after nerve injury [1, 53].

Given the observed promotion of neuronal differentiation by NGF@ZIF-8/GML hydrogel, and given that the 2 weeks following PNI represent a critical window for neural regeneration, this phase is characterized by the core processes of Schwann cell activation, immune cell polarization, and initial axonal extension [54, 55]. It enables the capture of key molecular changes in the regenerative microenvironment. Therefore, we will conduct RNA sequencing analysis on neural tissues 2 weeks post-injury to explore potential regulatory mechanisms. Following sciatic nerve transection, different scaffold strategies elicited distinct transcriptional responses, reflecting their variable capacity in shaping the regenerative microenvironment. Compared with the Hollow group, the GML group induced 530 upregulated and 285 downregulated genes, including CD300A, CCN1, and MMP8, which are associated with immune regulation, extracellular matrix remodeling, and angiogenesis (Fig. 6A). GO enrichment highlighted inflammatory response regulation, leukocyte-mediated immunity, and phagocytosis, while KEGG pathways were enriched for lysosome activity, phagosome formation, and B cell receptor signaling (Fig. 6B, C and S7). These findings are consistent with the biomaterial properties of GelMA, which provides a hydrated, extracellular matrix–like scaffold that facilitates immune cell infiltration and structural remodeling. Nevertheless, the transcriptional signatures remained largely confined to innate immune responses, with limited activation of adaptive immunity, suggesting that GelMA alone provides only partial support for effective nerve repair.

Building upon the baseline effects of GML, the incorporation of NGF@ZIF-8 markedly amplified and diversified the molecular responses. Compared with GML, NGF@ZIF-8/GML scaffolds induced 331 upregulated and 195 downregulated genes, including GATA3 (driving Th2 differentiation), HDC (inflammation mediator), and SLC4A11 (oxidative stress regulator), while suppressing stress-related genes such as NQO2 and HSPA8-PS23 (Fig. 6D). GO and KEGG analyses revealed strong enrichment for leukocyte activation, antigen receptor-mediated signaling, T cell receptor signaling, chemokine signaling, and Th1/Th2/Th17 differentiation, indicating robust engagement of adaptive immune pathways (Fig. 6E, F and S8). Venn analysis further showed that while Thbs1 and Ptgs2 were commonly upregulated in the GML group, NGF@ZIF-8/GML uniquely activated key cytokines and chemokines (IL-10, IL-6, Cxcl1, Cxcl9, Trem2, Ccl2, Ccl5, Cxcr4), IL-10 (suppresses excessive inflammation), IL-6 (promotes Schwann cell activation), Cxcl1/Cxcl9 (recruits neural precursor cells), Trem2 (regulates macrophage phagocytosis of debris), and Ccl2/Ccl5/Cxcr4 (facilitates lymphocyte homing)—all of which synergistically optimized the neuroregenerative microenvironment (Fig. 6G-I and S9). Consistently, GSEA confirmed significant enrichment of NFAT5-, IL10-, and TREM1-associated macrophage and monocyte activation signatures (Fig. 6J). Finally, we used GAPDH as an internal control to validate the expression of five key genes (IL-10, CD206, Cxcl1, Trem2, and SLC4A11) via qPCR. The results showed that the expression levels in the NGF@ZIF-8/GML group were significantly elevated. The expression trends of these genes were highly consistent with the RNA-seq results, confirming the reliability of the transcriptomic analysis (Fig. 6K).

The transcriptional regulatory patterns of the NGF@ZIF-8/GML scaffold used in this study are highly consistent with established models of peripheral nerve injury (PNI) and regeneration, while also revealing novel molecular regulatory nodes. In terms of immunoregulation, the significant upregulation of the anti-inflammatory cytokine IL-10 and the induction of RAW264.7 cells toward the M2 anti-inflammatory phenotype in our study are core elements in reshaping the pro-repair immunomicroenvironment after PNI, effectively promoting Schwann cell activation, myelin debris clearance, and axonal growth. This aligns closely with classical conclusions in the field of PNI, further validating the universality of immunomicroenvironment modulation in PNI regeneration [8, 56–58]. Additionally, the upregulation of chemokine genes (Cxcl1, Cxcl9) and the functional regulation gene of macrophages, Trem2, in this study is consistent with previous findings, confirming that chemokine-mediated recruitment of neural precursor cells and Trem2-regulated macrophage phagocytosis are critical steps in PNI regeneration [59–61], further supporting the molecular regulatory efficacy of the prepared scaffold. Notably, our study also identified novel molecular regulatory events in the field of PNI that remain largely unreported, with the most prominent being the significant upregulation of SLC4A11 (a key gene regulating cellular oxidative stress and ion homeostasis). Oxidative stress is a significant pathological factor exacerbating neural tissue damage and inhibiting axonal regeneration after PNI, yet the specific molecular mechanisms of oxidative stress regulation in PNI regeneration remain unclear [62–64]. SLC4A11 is a sodium borate cotransporter that can regulate intracellular reactive oxygen species clearance and maintain mitochondrial redox homeostasis by modulating intracellular pH and ion balance [65, 66]. Our findings demonstrate that the upregulation of SLC4A11 is closely associated with PNI regeneration mediated by the NGF@ZIF-8/GML scaffold, suggesting that the scaffold may mitigate oxidative stress damage in regenerating nerves through SLC4A11 regulation, thereby creating a more favorable microenvironment for axonal elongation and Schwann cell myelination. This discovery complements the potential molecular links between oxidative stress regulation and PNI regeneration, providing a novel candidate target for developing PNI repair strategies based on oxidative stress intervention. Furthermore, the downregulation of stress-related genes (NQO2, HSPA8-PS23) in this study also provides new experimental evidence for the negative regulation of cellular stress responses in PNI regeneration.

Taken together, these results indicate that NGF@ZIF-8/GML scaffolds not only augment the innate immune and remodeling responses of GelMA but also uniquely drive adaptive immunity and immunoregulatory signaling, establishing a dynamic microenvironment that integrates inflammation control, angiogenesis, and neurotrophic support, thereby outperforming GML in promoting nerve regeneration.

Compared with previously reported ZIF-8-loaded hydrogel nerve conduits, our bilayer construct exhibits distinct superiorities. The dual-concentration GelMA architecture harmonizes mechanical robustness and biological compatibility, where the outer GMH layer delivers reliable structural support while the inner GML lumen fosters axonal growth. The pH-responsive degradation of ZIF-8 enables targeted and sustained NGF release to potentiate neurotrophic bioactivity. This integrated system modulates both neurogenesis and immune microenvironment remodeling, promoting neuronal differentiation and M2 macrophage polarization. Notably, our conduit achieves nerve regeneration efficacy comparable to autologous nerve grafts, transcriptomic analysis further identifies the upregulation of SLC4A11 as a novel mechanism for mitigating oxidative stress, which effectively boosts nerve regeneration.

Conclusion

This study proposes a bilayer GelMA neural conduits that combine mechanical support with sustained nerve growth factor delivery for peripheral nerve repair. The GMH shell provides structural integrity, while the internal GML hydrogel encapsulates ZIF-8 nanoparticles loaded with NGF, thereby extending the release of bioactive factors. This layered design effectively overcomes the limitations of conventional conduits, including poor mechanical performance and rapid release. In vitro results confirmed sustained NGF release, cellular compatibility, and enhanced neurite growth, while in vivo experiments demonstrated improved axonal regeneration and functional recovery in a rat sciatic nerve injury model. Collectively, these findings indicate that the NGF@ZIF-8/GML bilayer nerve conduits are a promising preliminary candidate for peripheral nerve regeneration, and lay a preliminary experimental foundation for its subsequent preclinical exploration and potential translational research.

Methods

Materials

Gelatin, 2-Hydroxy-2-methylpropiophenone (HMPP, 97%) and methacrylic anhydride (MA) were obtained from Sigma-Aldrich. Zinc nitrate hexahydrate and 2-Methylimidazole (2-MI) were obtained from Aladdin. Nerve growth factor (NGF) was purchased from MedChemExpress. Phosphate Buffered Saline (PBS), 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA), CCK-8, H&E Staining Kit, Masson Staining Kit, purchased from Beyotime Biotechnology. Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM), Roswell Park Memorial Institute 1640 (RPMI-1640), Horse serum (HS), Fetal Bovine Serum (FBS) and Penicillin-Streptomycin (P/S) purchased from Sigma-Aldrich. The primary antibody and its corresponding secondary antibody were obtained from Abcam.

The synthesis of ZIF-8 and NGF loading

Initially, 0.4 mmol zinc nitrate hexahydrate was dissolved in 5 mL ultrapure water, while 12.8 mmol 2-methylimidazole was independently dissolved in 5 mL ultrapure water. After that, the 2-methylimidazole solution was added to the stirred zinc nitrate hexahydrate solution, and the resulting mixture was stirred at room temperature at a speed of 1000 rpm for 1 h. The pellet was then collected and precipitated at 10,000 rpm for 5 min, washed twice with ultrapure water, and ZIF-8 was obtained and stored at -20 °C sealed. Except for adding 100 µg of NGF to the 2-MI solution and stirring for 10 min, the other steps were the same as before to obtain NGF-loaded ZIF-8 (NGF@ZIF-8).

The preparation of GelMA hydrogel

Initially, 20 g of gelatin was dissolved in 200 mL of PBS at 50 °C. After that, 12 mL of MA was added gradually, and the mixture was stirred for a duration of 3 h. Later, the supernatant was centrifuged at a speed of 3500 ×g; the solution was then diluted using 400 mL of ultrapure water and placed into a 12–14 kDa dialysis bag, where it underwent dialyzed at 40 °C for one week. The solution pH was then adjusted to 7.2 and filtered through a 0.22 μm filter, followed by lyophilization for preservation. GelMA hydrogels were prepared at different concentrations by dissolving GelMA in PBS containing HMPP (0.5 vol%) and irradiating it with UV light for 30 s.

Characterization

The morphology of the hydrogels and ZIF-8 was observed using a scanning electron microscope (Hitachi SU8010, Japan), and the particle size and Zeta potential were tested using a nanoparticle size and Zeta potential analyzer (Malvern Zetasizer Nano ZS90, UK). Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spectra were recorded on a Thermo Fisher Scientific Nicolet iS5 spectrometer (USA), X-ray diffraction (XRD) patterns were collected using a Bruker D8 Advance diffractometer (DE). The compressive strength of the samples was tested using an electronic universal testing machine (CMT6103, CN), and the rheological properties of the samples were tested using a rheometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific Mars40, USA).

NGF and zinc ion release

5 mg of NGF@ZIF-8 nanoparticles were immersed in 1 mL of PBS buffer (pH 7.4 or 6.0) and incubated at 37 °C gently shaken. At predetermined time intervals, the suspension was centrifuged at 10,000 rpm for 5 min, the supernatant was collected for detection, and an equal volume of fresh PBS was added to maintain the sink condition. The concentration of released NGF was determined using a human β-NGF ELISA kit, and the concentration of released Zn²⁺ was measured using an inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometer (ICP-MS, Agilent 5110, USA).

Cell culture

The medium for RAW264.7 and RSC96 cells was DMEM + 10% FBS + 1% P/S, and the medium for PC-12 cells was RPMI-1640 + 5% FBS + 10% HS + 1% P/S. All cells were cultured in a cell incubator at 37 °C, 5% CO2.

Cytotoxicity testing

Cells were seeded into 96-well plates and treated with NGF@ZIF-8 particles at various concentrations in the culture medium for 1 and 3 days. Afterward, 10% CCK-8 reagent was added to each well, and following 1 h of incubation, the absorbance of each well at 450 nm was determined using a microplate reader.

Cell migration

Cells were seeded in 24-well plates and cultured using serum-free medium. Hydrogels were co-cultured at a concentration of 0.1 g/mL in each well. After the cells grew to confluence, scratches were created using a 200 µL pipette tip, and photographed immediately under a microscope. Then, after 24 h, photos were taken again to obtain images, and the migration rate was statistically analyzed.

PC-12 cell differentiation

Cells were seeded in 24-well plates, and the hydrogel was placed above the cells using a transwell chamber. The plate was incubated in a 37 °C, 5% CO2 incubator for 3 days, followed by fixation with 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) at room temperature for 15 min, then stained with TUJ-1 and phalloidin for immunofluorescence, and with DAPI for nuclear staining. Images were captured using a fluorescence microscope and analyzed statistically.

Differentiation of RAW264.7 cells

The cells were seeded in 24-well plates and treated with Lipopolysaccharide (LPS) at a concentration of 1 µg/mL. In the experimental groups, hydrogels were co-cultured in each well for 24 h. The cells were then fixed with 4% PFA and subjected to immunofluorescence staining with CD86, followed by DAPI staining for the nuclei. LPS was replaced with 20 ng/mL of IL-4, and the culture steps were identical, with fluorescence staining performed using CD206. Images were captured using a fluorescence microscope, and statistical analysis was conducted.

Fabrication and grouping of NGCs

A glass tube with an outer diameter of 1.5 mm and a glass tube with an inner diameter of 3 mm were nested in a concentric manner. A high-concentration 15 wt% GelMA (GMH) solution was added to the gap between them, followed by UV irradiation for 30 s to obtain hollow outer-layer NGCs, named the Hollow group. Subsequently, a low-concentration 5 wt% GelMA (GML) solution was injected as a filler into the hollow conduits, followed by UV irradiation for 30 s to obtain NGCs containing low concentrations of GelMA, named the GML group. In the 5 wt% GelMA solution, NGF@ZIF-8 was added, and the same UV irradiation was performed to obtain conduits named the NGF@ZIF-8/GML group.

Animal experimental design

The animal experiments in this study employed SD rats. During the second week, when tissue sequencing experiments were conducted, the study was divided into three groups: Hollow, GML, and NGF@ZIF-8/GML, with three rats in each group. In the third week and the twelfth week, when tissue staining and behavioral experiments were performed, the study was divided into four groups: Hollow, GML, NGF@ZIF-8/GML, and AUTO, with three rats in each group. Thirty-three rats were used in all animal experiments.

Surgery procedures

All animal experiments were approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of Southeast University (approval number: 20241225002) and conducted in accordance with the guidelines governing the care and use of experimental animals. Simultaneously, we strictly adhered to the ARRIVE guidelines and the 3R principles (Replacement, Reduction, Refinement). At each time point, only three male rats were used per experimental group. This study employed male Sprague-Dawley (SD) rats (body weight: ~200 g) to establish a 10 mm sciatic nerve defect model. Briefly, the male rats received an intraperitoneal injection of pentobarbital (0.05 g/kg) for anesthesia. The skin was incised with a surgical knife, and the leg muscles were bluntly separated to expose the sciatic nerve, which was then completely transected to create a 10 mm defect. In the experimental group, NGCs were inserted and sutured at both ends. In the AUTO group, the severed nerves were reversed and then sutured. Finally, the muscles and skin were closed, and the wound was disinfected with iodophor. Subsequently, after the rats recovered, they were returned to a ventilated and temperature-controlled room for housing. At weeks 3 and 12, after completing the necessary assessments, all rats were euthanized using carbon dioxide, and corresponding tissue samples were collected.

Immunohistochemical staining

The gastrocnemius muscle was excised from the rat leg and fixed in 4% PFA solution. The tissues were sectioned after complete fixation, followed by Masson staining of the sections, and bright-field photographs were taken for statistical analysis.

Immunofluorescence staining

After removing the nerve tissue, it was fixed with 4% PFA. Frozen sectioning was performed, the nerve tissue was stained with NF200, S100β, CD86 and CD206 antibodies, and photographed under a fluorescence microscope for statistical analysis.

Rat tissue RNA sequencing

Three rats were used per group in the experiment, and the rats were euthanized with carbon dioxide at 2 weeks postoperative. Sciatic nerve tissues were harvested from rats in each group, ground into a fine powder in liquid nitrogen, and total RNA was extracted using TRIzol reagent combined with the RNeasy Mini Kit. The extracted RNA was assessed for integrity and purity with an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer, and only RNA samples with an RNA Integrity Number (RIN) ≥ 8.0 were selected for subsequent library construction to ensure the reliability of sequencing data. cDNA libraries were constructed using the NEBNext® Ultra™ RNA Library Prep Kit for Illumina in strict accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions, with core procedures including enrichment of poly(A)-tailed mRNA from total RNA, reverse transcription to synthesize the first strand of cDNA, second-strand cDNA synthesis, adapter ligation and PCR amplification. Following library construction, the fragment size distribution of the libraries was detected via an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer, and the effective concentration of the libraries was quantified using a Qubit 4.0 Fluorometer to complete library quality validation. Qualified cDNA libraries were pooled at an equimolar ratio and loaded onto the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform for 150 bp paired-end (PE150) sequencing, with a target sequencing depth of 6 Gb of clean data per sample. Raw sequencing data were generated in the FASTQ format, and high-quality clean reads were obtained after filtering out low-quality reads, adapter sequences and contaminant reads, which were used for subsequent bioinformatics analysis.

Bioinformatics and statistical analyses

High-quality reads were aligned to the rat reference genome (GRCr8) with HISAT2 (v2.0.5) using a two-pass strategy, and gene annotation was based on ENSEMBL release 114. Gene counts were obtained with featureCounts (v1.5.0) and normalized as FPKM. Heatmaps were generated with ComplexHeatmap. Functional enrichment of DEGs, including GO and KEGG analyses, was performed using clusterProfiler, with significance set at p < 0.05. Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) was also conducted, ranking genes by log2 fold change. Results were visualized with enrichplot and ggplot2 in R.

Quantitative real-time PCR (q-PCR)

Total RNA was extracted from the sciatic nerve of SD rats using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, USA). RNA was then converted to cDNA via reverse transcription. Subsequently, q-PCR was performed using a 20 µL reaction system, including cDNA template, a pair of forward and reverse primers, 2 × SYBR qPCR Master Mix, and enzyme-free water (Vazyme, CN). Statistics

Quantitative data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Student’s t-test was used for comparison between two groups, and one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey’s post hoc test was used for multiple group comparisons. Statistical significance was defined as *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001; ns indicates no significant difference.
