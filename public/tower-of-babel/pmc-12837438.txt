Non-housing assets and all-cause mortality in middle-aged and older Chinese adults: a National cohort study
Authors: Yuanyuan Qin, Biheng Feng, Qingjiang Cai, Liuyun Huang, Mingjie Xie, Ling Li, Debin Huang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundFinancial resources beyond housing may influence survival in later life. Given China’s rapid population aging and high home ownership, focusing on non-housing assets can clarify wealth–health links. We therefore examined the association between total non-housing assets and all-cause mortality among Chinese middle-aged and older adults.MethodsA nationwide cohort of 12,670 adults (aged 45–85) was established using the harmonized CHARLS dataset (2011–2018). All-cause mortality was ascertained through 2020 by supplementing harmonized data with vital status information from the raw CHARLS 2020 wave. The main exposure was total non-housing assets. In addition, non-housing assets were combined with household consumption (median split) to create four joint groups: Group 1 (low assets/low consumption), Group 2 (low assets/high consumption), Group 3 (high assets/low consumption), and Group 4 (high assets/high consumption). All-cause mortality was tracked. Baseline characteristics and mortality were presented by asset quartile and asset consumption group. Survival curves, Cox models (adjusted for confounders), and restricted cubic splines assessed associations. Subgroup and interaction analyses, especially for marital status, were visualized using forest and stratified plots.ResultsDuring a 9-year follow-up, 2,418 deaths occurred. Higher total non-housing assets were associated with lower mortality: Q4 (highest) vs. Q1 (lowest), adjusted HR = 0.79 (95% CI 0.68–0.91). In fully adjusted models, we also observed a graded inverse association across asset–consumption groups (P for trend < 0.001); high-consumption categories remained protective (Group 2: HR = 0.85, 95% CI 0.74–0.97; Group 4: HR = 0.75, 95% CI 0.65–0.85). Marital status showed a significant interaction with asset level (P‑interaction < 0.001).ConclusionsGreater non-housing assets was associated with lower mortality. Marital status has a significant interacting effect on this association. Focus should be on vulnerable elderly groups with middle-low assets, low consumption, or those who are non-married.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12963-025-00452-3.Keywords: Total non-housing assets, All-cause mortality, Aging population, Wealth-health gradient

Background

China faces a dual challenge of rapid population aging and a persistently high chronic disease burden, amplifying the risk of all-cause mortality among older adults [1]. Population aging has contributed to a higher global burden of mortality [2]. Individuals aged 50 years and above account for the highest all-cause mortality among all age groups. Notably, this age group makes up approximately 70% of total deaths worldwide [3, 4]. National cohort studies in China have also confirmed that urban-rural distribution and regional socioeconomic status influence mortality risk, with higher mortality rates observed among populations with lower socioeconomic status [5].

Total non-housing assets refer to all tangible and financial assets owned by a household excluding housing, including liquid wealth such as savings, stocks, and pensions [6]. As a sensitive indicator of wealth stratification, non-housing assets reflect the household’s liquidity and economic strength, and also play a crucial role in responding to poverty shocks, securing retirement, and coping with illnesses. Studies have shown that a low level of total non-housing assets is closely associated with a higher incidence and adverse outcomes of chronic diseases such as cardiovascular disease [6]. The underlying mechanisms may involve reduced access to healthcare, limited capacity to engage in health-promoting behaviors, and insufficient social support [7, 8]. Wealth status is closely related to health conditions. Some researchers have used housing wealth as a measure and found that decreases in housing assets not only increase the risk of psychological distress and depression, but may also affect health outcomes by increasing financial stress [9]. Meanwhile, other studies using total non-housing assets as a wealth indicator have shown that higher levels of total non-housing assets are associated with lower all-cause mortality rates [10]. These findings further suggest that different dimensions of wealth are all closely related to major health outcomes.

Recent studies increasingly explore the link between wealth and all-cause mortality. However, long-term data on middle-aged and older adults in China are still limited. On one hand, some studies have focused on Western developed countries, but there is a lack of systematic examination of the health effects of total non-housing assets within the context of China’s unique socioeconomic structure and pension system [11]. On the other hand, previous studies have limitations in wealth measurement methods, consideration of regional differences, and control of confounding factors [12, 13], leaving the independent and long-term impact of total non-housing assets on mortality risk among older Chinese populations unclear. So we need more large-scale studies across China to find out how total non-housing assets influence people’s health. This evidence can help improve health-related policies.

Our study used data from the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS), we systematically evaluated the association between levels of non-housing assets and all-cause mortality risk. At the same time, we considered multiple economic indicators including assets and total household consumption to comprehensively explore the relationship between wealth and mortality risk. The aim is to provide empirical evidence for understanding the relationship between non-housing assets and mortality risk in China, and to support the optimization of health intervention policies and risk assessment.

Methods

Data sources

The primary data source for this study was the harmonized CHARLS dataset (Waves 1–4, 2011–2018), which provides standardized variables across waves, enhancing comparability. To extend the observation period for all-cause mortality to 2020, the mortality status and date of death between the 2018 and 2020 waves were obtained from the raw CHARLS Wave 5 dataset. This combined approach ensured both data consistency and a longer, more complete follow-up for the mortality endpoint [14]. This harmonization process enhances the usability and comparability of the data. We enrolled participants aged 45 to 85 years. Individuals lacking data on total non-housing assets were excluded, and the selection process is detailed in Fig. 1.

Flowchart of participant selection. The cohort was derived from the harmonised CHARLS dataset. Mortality follow-up utilized harmonised data through 2018 and was extended to 2020 using vital status records from the raw CHARLS Wave 5 dataset

Key variables

The primary exposure variable in our study was total non-housing assets, defined as the sum of non-housing financial wealth and non-housing tangible wealth, with the latter calculated by aggregating the value of vehicles (hh1atran: w1), consumer durables (hh1adurbl: w1), fixed capital assets (hh1afixc: w1), irrigable land (hh1aland: w1), and livestock and fisheries (hh1aagri: w1) in the CHARLS dataset.

Total household consumption was used as a joint exposure variable alongside non-housing assets. Total household consumption was obtained from the harmonized CHARLS dataset. These were constructed by aggregating consumption across different time frames and converting them to annual estimates: food consumption from the last week (multiplied by 52), non-food consumption from the past 30 days (multiplied by 12), and other non-food consumption from the past year (used directly). The sum of these components yielded the total annual household consumption. Furthermore, participants were categorized into four groups according to the median values of baseline total non-housing assets and total household consumption: Group 1 (low assets/low consumption), Group 2 (low assets/high consumption), Group 3 (high assets/low consumption), and Group 4 (high assets/high consumption).

To provide a clearer understanding of participants’ socioeconomic background, we also present the variable total wealth in the baseline characteristics table. Total wealth is defined as the household’s comprehensive net worth, including the estimated value of housing property. This variable differs from the primary exposure, Total non-housing assets, which, as described above, excludes housing wealth.

The outcome variable was all-cause mortality. Mortality status was tracked across all follow-up waves (Waves 2–5). The date of death was directly available for deaths recorded in Waves 2, 4, and 5. For Wave 3, only the mortality status was available without a precise date. Following previous studies utilizing CHARLS data, the time of death for these cases was defined as the midpoint between the latest wave when the participant was known to be alive and the wave when death was recorded [15]. Participants lost to follow-up were censored.

The distribution of total non-housing assets was heavy-tailed. We pre-specified the 1 st percentile (P1) and the 99th percentile (P99) as tail thresholds. Trimming was defined as excluding observations below P1 or above P99. The overall missing rate across all covariates was 3.19%, and multiple imputation was applied to handle missing values. Missing covariate data were handled using multiple imputation with the mice package in R. Ten imputed datasets were generated using default settings and combined using Rubin’s rules.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) or median (interquartile range), and categorical variables were expressed as frequencies or percentages. Baseline characteristics across quartiles of non-housing asset were compared using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) for continuous variables, and the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test for categorical variables.

Age-standardized mortality rates (ASMRs) were calculated to describe mortality patterns across subgroups independent of age structure. ASMRs (per 1,000 person-years) were computed using the direct method, with the age distribution of the entire study population at baseline serving as the standard population.

Kaplan-Meier survival curves were subsequently used to estimate survival across each non-housing asset quartile and the four asset-consumption combined groups, with comparisons performed using the log-rank test. The proportional hazards (PH) assumption was assessed for all variables using Schoenfeld residuals. No significant violations were observed for the main exposure variables (non-housing asset quartiles and asset-consumption groups). Two covariates, cancer and hospital admission, showed evidence of non-proportional hazards (see in Additional File 1: Table S1 and Figure S1). To assess the robustness of our findings, we performed sensitivity analyses by refitting the Cox proportional hazards models after excluding these two variables. Associations between total non-housing assets and all-cause mortality were evaluated using Cox proportional hazards regression models to estimate hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs). We employed a series of sequentially adjusted models to assess the potential confounding effect of different covariate sets and to examine the robustness of the observed association.

The models were specified as follows: Model 1 was unadjusted, providing the crude association. Model 2 was adjusted for basic demographic factors (age and sex). Model 3 was further adjusted for sociodemographic characteristics, including marital status, education, household registration (hukou), and region. Model 4 was fully adjusted for all covariates, including those in Model 3 plus lifestyle factors (smoking and alcohol use) and chronic diseases (hypertension, diabetes, cancer, pulmonary disease, heart disease, and stroke).

We further explored potential non-linear relationships between total non-housing assets and all-cause mortality using smoothed restricted cubic spline (RCS) with four knots placed at the 5th, 35th, 65th, and 95th percentiles of the asset distribution. The RCS analysis was incorporated into the fully adjusted Cox model (Model 4) to visualize the shape of the association after accounting for all potential confounders. To assess heterogeneity across subgroups, we performed subgroup analyses by fitting separate Cox regression models within each stratum. We evaluated subgroup interactions with total non-housing assets using likelihood ratio tests. Forest plots and stratified curve fitting visually illustrated these interaction effects.

We conducted a series of sensitivity analyses to assess the robustness of the results to alternative data specifications and model assumptions. First, we re-estimated all main models using the complete-case dataset (excluding imputed values) to evaluate the potential influence of multiple imputation. Second, we applied Winsorization to trim extreme values of continuous variables (top and bottom 1%) and re-analyzed the associations. Third, key analyses were repeated using the inverse hyperbolic sine (IHS) transformation of asset variables to address their heavy-tailed distributions. Finally, we checked model stability by excluding covariates violating the proportional hazards (PH) assumption and by estimating fully adjusted models including all covariates.

All statistical analyses were performed using R 4.4.1 software. A two-sided P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

The average age of participants was 59.51 ± 9.53 years, with Q2 having the highest mean age (63.0 years) and Q4 the lowest (58.0 years); the difference was statistically significant (p < 0.001). The sex distribution was relatively balanced across groups. Regarding marital status, the proportion of married participants was significantly higher in Q4 (91.7%) and Q3 (89%) (p < 0.001). For educational attainment, The proportion with lower secondary education was highest in Q2 (93.9%), where upper secondary/vocational and tertiary education were most common in Q4.In terms of health status (Additional File 2: Table S2), Additional File 2: Table S2 shows that hypertension (p = 0.013), lung disease (p < 0.001), heart disease (p < 0.001), and stroke (p = 0.003) differed significantly across asset quartiles, whereas diabetes, cancer, drinking, smoking, and hospitalization did not (all p ≥ 0.058). Notably, lung disease and heart disease were least prevalent in Q4, while stroke prevalence was highest in Q2 and lowest in Q4. With respect to economic characteristics, there were significant differences in the median values of total household wealth, household consumption, and non-housing financial wealth across the groups (all p < 0.001). Notably, Q4 exhibited the most favorable financial situation, with median wealth of CNY 152,250.0 and consumption of CNY 22,200.0, significantly exceeding the other groups.

Baseline characteristics by asset–consumption joint groups are summarized in Additional File 2, Table S3. Participants in the high-asset groups (G3 and G4) tended to be younger, better educated, more likely to be married, and urban residents (p < 0.001 for all). In contrast, G1 (low assets/low consumption) included older participants with a higher proportion of rural and agricultural hukou. All wealth components increased markedly across the groups from G1 to G4 (p < 0.001), confirming that the asset–consumption categorization effectively differentiates participants by socioeconomic status.

All-Cause mortality

During follow-up of 9 years (2011 to 2020), 2418 participants (19.1%) had died from all causes. A total of 3,276 participants were lost to follow-up, corresponding to a loss rate of 25.8%.

Table 2 summarizes mortality rates by non-housing asset quartiles and asset–consumption groups. Overall, mortality rates declined consistently with increasing asset levels, even after accounting for age differences. Across non-housing asset quartiles, the age-adjusted mortality rate decreased from 19.46 (0.92) per 1,000 person-years in Q1 to 13.27 (0.76) in Q4. Participants in Q1 accounted for 25.8% of all deaths, whereas those in Q4 contributed only 17.2%.

After adjustment for all potential confounders including demographics, lifestyle, and chronic diseases in Model 4, higher total non-housing assets remained significantly associated with lower all-cause mortality. Compared with the lowest asset quartile (Q1), participants in the highest quartile (Q4) had a 21% lower risk of death (HR = 0.79, 95% CI: 0.68–0.91). A significant inverse trend across asset quartiles was observed (P for trend < 0.0001). For reference, the unadjusted associations (Model 1) are presented in Table 3, where the effects were more pronounced, particularly in Q2 and Q4, likely reflecting the confounding influence of factors such as age and health status.

In the analysis combining assets and consumption, participants in Group 2 showed a 42% reduction in mortality risk (HR = 0.58, 95% CI: 0.51 to 0.66, P < 0.001). Group 3 yielded an HR of 0.64 (95% CI: 0.57 to 0.73, P < 0.001), corresponding to a 36% reduction in risk. Group 4 had the lowest HR at 0.45 (95% CI: 0.40 to 0.51, P < 0.001). After full adjustment, Groups 4 and 2 remained significantly lower than Group 1, with P for trend remaining significant.

In the age-stratified analysis, the inverse association between non-housing assets and all-cause mortality was mainly observed among participants aged ≤ 65 years (Additional File 2: Table S4), while it became attenuated and statistically non-significant among those aged > 65 years (Additional File 2 Table S5).

Sensitivity analyses using different datasets and model specifications produced results consistent with the main findings (all in Additional File 3). Specifically, similar associations were observed when using the complete-case (non-imputed) dataset (Table S6), the Winsorized dataset (Table S7), and the inverse hyperbolic sine (IHS) transformed dataset (Table S8). In addition, results remained stable in models excluding variables that violated the PH assumption or in fully adjusted models (Table S9). These findings confirm the robustness of the observed associations.

Restricted cubic spline (RCS) analyses were applied to assess potential non-linear associations between continuous exposure variables and mortality. RCS models were fitted for total non-housing assets and total consumption, while the joint asset–consumption groups were treated as categorical variables. The RCS results for non-housing assets are shown in Fig. 3. Specifically, the population with the lowest level of total non-housing assets did not exhibit the highest mortality rate. Instead, mortality initially increased with rising asset levels and subsequently decreased as assets continued to grow. This pattern is consistent with the results from survival analysis curves and multiple regression models, further confirming the complex impact of total non-housing assets on mortality risk. Additionally, the relationship between consumption level and mortality demonstrates that higher levels of consumption are significantly associated with lower mortality rates, as illustrated in Additional File 2: Figure S2.

Discussion

In this 9-year cohort study, After full adjustment for confounders (Model 4), we found that higher levels of total non-housing assets were significantly associated with a lower risk of all-cause mortality among middle-aged and older adults in China. This inverse association remained robust after adjusting for multiple confounders. Our findings are consistent with several international cohort studies, all of which have demonstrated the long-term protective effect of wealth accumulation on health. Notably, our unadjusted analyses revealed a potential U-shaped, non-linear relationship, a phenomenon rarely reported in previous literature. Furthermore, joint stratification by assets and consumption showed that individuals with high assets but low consumption had a higher mortality risk than those with low assets and high consumption. Subgroup analysis also revealed that marital status played an important interactive role. The possible mechanisms and implications of these findings will be discussed further below.

Our study found that people with more non-housing assets have a lower risk of death. This result is similar to findings from other major international studies. Finegood et al. [16], using the Midlife in the United States (MIDUS) study (N = 5,414), demonstrated that higher net assets in midlife are significantly associated with lower all-cause mortality risk. Machado et al. [10], based on a large cross-national sample from the U.S. and Europe (N = 73,838), found that individuals at the top of the wealth distribution have the lowest mortality risks, further highlighting the critical role of wealth in health outcomes. Glei et al. [17], using data from the U.S., confirmed that the association between wealth and mortality is even more pronounced in older populations. Cohort studies from Denmark reported that the risk of sudden death among the lowest income and education groups is more than twice that of the highest income group [18]. Collectively, these results provide foundational theoretical evidence for the relationship between wealth and health.

However, our study also differs from some previous literature, primarily in terms of sample sources, exposure measurement, and modeling strategies. Unlike Finegood et al. [16], who used “net assets” as the main indicator, we specifically evaluated the independent effect of total non-housing assets. Compared with the social security structures in the U.S. and Europe discussed by Machado et al. [10], the coexistence of China’s urban–rural dual structure and the household registration (hukou) system may create unique mechanisms linking wealth distribution and health outcomes. Some studies, such as Peng et al. [5], have analyzed only regional socioeconomic status (SES) without assessing household or individual assets, making it difficult to directly compare SES measures with specific wealth components. Together, these differences highlight the unique incremental value of our study design.

Our primary finding, based on fully adjusted models, confirms a graded inverse association between non-housing assets and all-cause mortality, consistent with prior literature. It is noteworthy, however, that this clear gradient was obscured in the unadjusted analysis, where the second asset quartile (Q2) exhibited the highest risk. This initial pattern aligns with the Q2 group’s distinct baseline profile—namely, older average age and a greater burden of chronic conditions (Table 1). Once these potent confounders were accounted for, the association uniformly reflected the protective effect of higher asset levels. Thus, our analysis underscores that the well-documented wealth-health gradient emerges clearly only after rigorous adjustment for key demographic and health disparities.

Moreover, in stratified analyses by baseline chronic disease status, the inverse association between non-housing assets and all-cause mortality remained broadly consistent across health strata. Although the gradient appeared somewhat stronger among participants with chronic diseases, the interaction between non-housing assets and disease status was not statistically significant (P for interaction = 0.265). This finding further supports the robustness of the observed association.

Given the strong age dependency of both asset accumulation and mortality, we further examined age-specific patterns in this association The attenuated association observed in participants aged > 65 years is consistent with the notion that the impact of financial resources on survival diminishes at older ages [19]. This may reflect the higher prevalence of chronic conditions, greater health homogeneity, and broader access to social security and family support among older adults [20]. These findings highlight that the protective role of non-housing assets is more pronounced before retirement, whereas in later life, health and social factors become more dominant determinants of mortality risk.

The analysis of asset-consumption cross-classification further validated and expanded on the mechanisms discussed above. According to the Kaplan-Meier survival curves, the high asset–low consumption group (Group 3) had a higher mortality risk than the low asset–high consumption group (Group 2), although this difference did not reach statistical significance after multivariable adjustment (P = 0.095). These results suggest that “asset–consumption mismatch stress” may represent an important but latent mechanism influencing health risks [21]. Particularly noteworthy is that individuals with high assets but insufficient consumption did not effectively convert accumulated wealth into health benefits. This phenomenon may be associated with “time scarcity,” barriers to health information access, or insufficient healthy behaviors, which consequently weaken the protective effect of wealth on health [22]. The health disadvantages faced by this group are not merely due to unequal resource allocation, but are also closely related to structural social factors such as time allocation, information access, and behavioral patterns, ultimately impeding the asset-to-health conversion pathway. Chronic stress may represent the primary biological mechanism underlying these effects [22, 23]. When interpreting the results related to household consumption, it should be noted that this measure included medical expenditures. While this introduces the possibility of reverse causality, it also represents the household’s total consumption burden and capacity. The association remained significant after adjusting for baseline health status, suggesting that consumption encapsulates a broader dimension of economic resilience.

In addition, we found a prominent moderating effect of marital status on the association between assets and mortality risk. Our results showed that the overall mortality rate among unmarried individuals was substantially higher than that of married individuals, highlighting the protective role of social support. At low or negative asset levels, mortality risk increases much more steeply for non-married individuals. However, as assets become higher, the decline in mortality risk is not obvious for this group. This suggests that, lacking support from a spouse, unmarried middle-aged and older adults are more reliant on their own economic resources, making them more sensitive to the benefits of increased assets in reducing mortality risk [24]. Our findings are consistent with the systematic review and meta-analysis by Wang et al., which showed that unmarried individuals have higher mortality risks, and that the lack of social and marital resources may aggravate health disadvantages [25]. Similarly, Liu et al.’s cohort study in a Chinese population found that marital status independently influenced mortality in stroke patients, with married individuals having better survival outcomes, indirectly supporting the notion that marriage offers multiple protections for vulnerable groups [26]. Wei et al. confirmed that unmarried patients with acute myocardial infarction faced higher long-term mortality risks, further underlining that the impact of economic vulnerability on mortality may be amplified in the absence of marital social capital [27]. Jang et al. also revealed that unmarried older adults are more likely to experience a lack of caregiving, reflecting that the health and economic security of single individuals depends more on their personal disposable resources [28]. This is further demonstrated in our results, where the protective effect of assets was particularly crucial among unmarried older adults. Mechanistically, marriage can offer both emotional and practical support, and may also help mitigate health risks through joint financial resources [25]. For unmarried individuals with higher assets, greater financial capacity may provide better protection against mortality risk, especially in the context of unexpected health expenditures or lack of family caregiving. These findings provide practical evidence for the optimization of income subsidies and chronic disease management interventions targeted at high-risk, single, middle-aged and older populations.

However, this protective effect of marriage is not uniform across all economic contexts. As shown in Fig. 4, the association between wealth and mortality is nonlinear. Among married adults with low or negative assets, financial strain and household responsibilities may attenuate the benefits of marital support, leading to a weaker or even reversed protective effect. In contrast, as assets increase, greater economic resources may amplify the advantages of social and emotional support, resulting in a stronger overall reduction in mortality risk.

Our study possesses multiple strengths in methodology and sample representativeness. Based on a nationwide, large-scale cohort followed for nine years, our research covers both urban and rural populations across various asset levels, and specifically focuses on total non-housing assets which are more liquid and better reflect individuals’ economic buffering capacity. This substantially enhances the external validity and policy relevance of our findings. Age standardization of mortality rates reduced confounding from age structure differences across socioeconomic groups and improved the reliability of the findings. By combining assets and consumption into joint groupings, the study deepens the understanding of wealth stratification, while minimizing the influence of housing asset fluctuations and structural biases, thus improving the robustness of the analytical results. The use of multivariable-adjusted Cox models, together with restricted cubic splines to reveal non-linear characteristics, and the inclusion of interaction analyses involving marital status and consumption, allow for a systematic exploration of the complex effects of socioeconomic pathways and health behaviors. These methodological features provide a richer foundation for both theoretical interpretation and policy application.

Our study has limitations. First, the sample is restricted to Chinese adults, and given cultural differences in asset management and social security systems, caution should be exercised when extrapolating these findings to other populations or social contexts [14], this limitation is consistent with previous research based on the CHARLS dataset. In addition, attrition during long-term follow-up may have led to a residual underestimation of all-cause mortality, resulting in somewhat conservative estimates. This is also similar to related studies [29, 30]. The observational design and single-time measurement of economic variables leave room for residual confounding and measurement error. Moreover, given the self-reported nature of many key variables in CHARLS, recall bias cannot be fully excluded. As the exact date of death was not available in Wave 3 of CHARLS, the precision of event-time estimation might be limited. Our analyses did not incorporate the complex survey design and sample weights of the CHARLS dataset. Future studies could consider applying sampling weights to verify the robustness of our findings. Future research should leverage repeated measures to model dynamic trajectories of assets and consumption, examine mediating pathways (e.g., healthcare access, catastrophic spending, health behaviors), and test generalizability across cohorts and settings.

Our study provides empirical evidence from China on the association between non-housing asset levels and mortality risk, contributing to the fields of epidemiology and health economics. Furthermore, the study offers precise data for risk identification and health disparities management in aging societies [31]. For public health policymakers, we recommend tiered health subsidies and early screening strategies based on the asset-to-consumption ratio. Enterprises and primary healthcare institutions could use these findings to innovate practical programs, such as health leave benefits and health-related consumption incentives, thereby enhancing the effectiveness of policy implementation and translation into healthy behaviors. For families, this research provides a scientific basis for rational allocation of liquid assets and health investment decisions [32]. Additionally, given changes in marriage, fertility perceptions, and demographic structure, policy should pay particular attention to the economic and social support of unmarried middle-aged and older adults. This includes strengthening their access to health, psychological, and social engagement services to prevent them from becoming a high-risk group for adverse health outcomes. Such measures can help to more precisely address health inequalities and the public health challenges posed by an aging population. For families and providers, aligning liquid asset al.location with preventive health investment could enhance the translation of economic capacity into healthier ageing.

Our study not only confirms the general pattern that increasing wealth is associated with decreasing health risks, but also, for the first time in a large Chinese sample, reveals the particular health vulnerability of the lower-middle asset group and identifies the interactive effect of marital structure on the asset–mortality risk association. These findings move beyond a purely economic stratification analysis and broaden the theoretical understanding of the mechanisms underlying health inequalities. For future research, the impact of specific consumption categories on health risks should be explored in depth. Embedded intervention studies are encouraged to evaluate the effectiveness of asset-based, precise, and multi-level health management measures, thus promoting full-chain optimization from theory to empirical evidence to policy advancement.

Conclusion

In conclusion, based on a nationwide Chinese cohort, our study systematically demonstrates that increment in total non-housing assets can sustainably and significantly reduce all-cause mortality risk among middle-aged and older adults. Age-stratified analyses further revealed that this inverse association was primarily evident among participants aged ≤ 65 years and became attenuated in those aged > 65 years. The descriptive vulnerability observed in the middle-low asset group was explained by confounding factors, directing policy attention instead to groups consistently at risk across models and emphasizes the critical moderating roles of marriage, consumption, and other social structural factors in the relationship between wealth and health risk. These findings provide a solid foundation for the innovation of healthy aging and health equity policies.
