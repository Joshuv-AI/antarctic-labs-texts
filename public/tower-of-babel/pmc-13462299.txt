Low Prevalence of Human T‐Cell Lymphotropic Virus Type 1 (HTLV‐1) in a Hospital Triage Population in Tehran During 2024–2025
Authors: Arash Letafati, Shima Sadeghipour Marvi, Negar Asghari Hosori, Ghazale Molaverdi, Alireza Esfandiari, Nima Afshar Moghadam, Shabnam Siavoshi, Mehdi Norouzi, Sayed‐Hamidreza Mozhgani
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground and AimsHuman T‐cell lymphotropic virus type 1 (HTLV‐1) is a retrovirus that is associated to adult T‐cell leukemia/lymphoma (ATLL) and HTLV‐1–associated myelopathy/tropical spastic paraparesis (HAM/TSP) as two major diseases of the virus. Due to limited data on its prevalence in general populations in Tehran and as this virus is endemic in Iran, this study aimed to assess HTLV‐1 prevalence among patients attending the triage unit of Imam Khomeini Hospital during 2024–2025.MethodsIn this cross‐sectional study conducted between 2024 and 2025, patients presenting to the triage unit were randomly selected. Exclusion criteria included active infection with HBV, HCV, or HIV, and receiving antiretroviral therapy (ART). After obtaining informed consent, 5 mL of whole blood was collected from each participant. The DNJIA kit (ROJE Technologies, Iran) was used to extract DNA. We used primers that targeted the HBZ and Tax regions of the viral genome to do PCR amplification to find HTLV‐1.ResultsOut of 197 individuals enrolled in this cross‐sectional study, 1 participant (0.51%) was positive for HTLV‐1. The HTLV‐1‐positive case was a 40‐year‐old single male with a history of both blood transfusion and prior surgery, but no history of organ transplantation, substance addiction, or travel. Most of the people who took part were men (54.3%) and married (58.9%), and their average age was 39.7 ± 12.1 years. Fisher's exact test showed a statistically significant association between HTLV‐1 positivity and a history of blood transfusion (p = 0.015) and a significant link with a history of surgery (p = 0.020). History of blood transfusion, organ transplantation, surgery, and addiction were reported by 3 (1.5%), 1 (0.5%), 4 (2.0%), and 1 (0.5%) participants, respectively. Foreign travel was reported by 77 (39.1%) participants, whereas 100 (50.8%) had a history of domestic travel. The most frequently visited foreign destinations were Turkey (n = 25) and the United Arab Emirates (n = 20). Among domestic destinations, the most commonly visited cities were Isfahan (n = 22) and Mazandaran (n = 19).ConclusionThe only positive case (0.51%) had known risk factors associated to HTLV‐1 infection including prior surgery and blood transfusion. Given the higher reported prevalence of HTLV‐1 in endemic regions including Iran, and the increased risk among certain populations—such as individuals with a history of intravenous drug use, multiple sexual partners, or co‐infection with other viruses—future studies should focus on these high‐risk groups and the virus prevalence over time. Targeted screening in such populations may provide a more accurate picture of the virus's distribution during different time frames for more effective control and prevention measures.

Introduction

Human T‐cell lymphotropic virus type 1 (HTLV‐1) is a retrovirus which belongs to Retroviridae virus family and is responsible for major clinical diseases. The virus is responsible for both malignant and inflammatory diseases. One of the major diseases is adult T‐cell leukemia/lymphoma (ATLL), an aggressive and often treatment‐resistant cancer of mature CD4+ T lymphocytes, and HTLV‐1‐associated myelopathy/tropical spastic paraparesis (HAM/TSP)—a chronic, progressive inflammatory disease of the central nervous system that is characterized by spastic paraparesis, urinary dysfunction, and sensory disturbances [1, 2, 3]. Estimates indicate that infected individuals possess a 2%–5% lifetime risk of developing ATLL and a 0.25%–3.8% lifetime risk of developing HAM/TSP [4].

HTLV‐1 infection has been associated with various systemic inflammatory disorders due to its capacity to dysregulate host immune responses. These include uveitis, polymyositis, bronchiectasis, infective dermatitis (particularly in children), and Sjögren syndrome [5, 6, 7]. The first human retrovirus to be identified was HTLV‐1, even before HIV. However, it is still a disease that is mostly ignored and not given enough attention in clinical and public health settings [8]. The main ways that HTLV‐1 is transmitted are from mother to child, especially through breastfeeding; sexual contact; and parenteral exposure, mainly through sharing injection equipment, organ transplantation, or transfusion of tainted blood products and being bitten by a nonhuman primate [9, 10, 11, 12].

Regional and demographic differences in seroprevalence are significant. Among the endemic regions are Southern Japan, the Caribbean, sub‐Saharan Africa, South America (especially Brazil), Indigenous Australians, and the Middle East [13, 14, 15]. The World Health Organization (WHO) has recently highlighted HTLV‐1 as a global health concern requiring coordinated surveillance and public health response [16, 17]. HTLV‐1 infection in Iran shows a geographic concentration, with the highest burden reported in the northeastern regions including Razavi Khorasan and North Khorasan provinces. Studies that are done in these regions, estimates ranging from approximately 2%–7% HTLV‐1 positive across various population groups [18, 19]. In contrast, the virus appears to be relatively uncommon throughout most other regions of Iran, where reported prevalence rates generally remain below 1%. Nevertheless, certain northeastern urban centers, including Mashhad and Neyshabur, continue to demonstrate comparatively elevated infection rates, with HTLV‐1 detected in as many as 3% of residents [18, 19].

For diagnostic confirmation, molecular testing or serological screening are required; polymerase chain reaction (PCR) assays that target conserved genetic viral regions, such as the long terminal repeat (LTR) and the tax, provide high sensitivity and specificity [2]. These assays are essential for both individual diagnosis and precise prevalence estimation in epidemiological studies.

However, in several regions, including parts of the Middle East due to low prevalence of the virus, the absence of updated population‐based data limits the ability to define transmission dynamics and direct policy development. In this study, we used data from the triage unit of the Imam Khomeini Hospital in 2024–2025 to investigate the virus's prevalence in the population.

Material and Methods

Preparing the Study Population and Sample

The study included 197 patients in total, 90 of whom were female and 107 of whom were male (IR.TUMS.SPH.REC.1403.320). From each participant, 5 mL of whole blood were drawn for analysis. Patient records were reviewed to ensure completeness of demographic data, including gender, age. Only persons aged 18 years and older were eligible for inclusion. Sample collection was conducted at Imam Khomeini Hospitals in Tehran, based on patient records acquired. Patients with incomplete data, specifically missing any of the required demographic information, individuals under 18 years of age, and patients with known medical conditions or treatments that could affect the prevalence of HTLV‐1 or complicate the interpretation of results (e.g., immunosuppressive disorders) were excluded.

Genome Extraction and Conventional PCR

We used the DNJia Plus Blood and Cell Kit (ROJE Technologies, Iran) to get genomic DNA from a whole blood sample according to manufacturer instruction. In total, 40 µL of RJ‐Protease was added to a clean 1.5 mL microtube to begin. Then, 200 µL of whole blood and 200 µL of GLB were added. After vortexing the mixture for 15 s, incubated at 70°C for 10 min. This caused all the cells to break down.

Next, 200 µL of ethanol (96%) was added to the sample, and it was put into a column on top of a collection tube. After the column was spun for 1 min at 13,000 rpm, the flow‐through was thrown away. The column was then placed into a new collection tube, and 600 µL of BWB1 was added for the first wash step.

The next two washes were performed using 500 µL of BWB2. After each step of centrifugation, the column was moved to a new tube, and the flow‐through and collection tubes were thrown away.

Finally, 100 µL of RRB that had been heated to 70°C was added directly to the column to get the DNA out. After being incubated at 56°C for 5 min, the column was spun for 1 min at 13,000 rpm. After that, the genomic DNA was put into a clean microtube.

Conventional PCR was used to amplify the pTax (forward: AACCCCCAACATTCCACCCT, reverse: CATGCAGACAACGGAGCCTC) and pHBZ (forward: ACGTCGCCCGGAGAAAACA, reverse: CTCCACCTCGCCTTCCAACT) genes. The reaction took place in a total of 25 µL, which included 12.5 µL of Super Master Mix (Yekta Tajhiz), 2 µL of a mixed primer solution (forward and reverse), 5 µL of template, and 5.5 µL of water. The cycling program began with a 4 min predenaturation step at 94°C, followed by 45 cycles consisting of 40 s denaturation step at 95°C, 40 s annealing step for the pTax gene at 59°C and for the pHBZ gene at 61.8°C, and 30 s extension step at 72°C. A final extension step was performed at 72°C for 5 min.

To make a 1.5% agarose gel, 1.5 g of agarose was mixed with 100 mL of 1X TBE buffer, and then 6 µL of Safe Stain was added. The electrophoresis was conducted for 45 min at 65 mA. A 100 bp DNA ladder (Sinaclon) was used to measure the size of the PCR products.

Statistical Analysis

Both descriptive and inferential statistics were employed to analyze the data by IBM SPSS Statistics, version 27.0.1. Frequencies and percentages were utilized to summarize categorical information, including gender, marital status, travel history, and medical history (including history of organ transplants, surgeries, blood transfusions, and addiction). Age and other continuous variables were displayed as means and standard deviations (mean ± SD). To evaluate associations between HTLV‐1 positivity and personal risk factors, Fisher's exact test was utilized. Statistical significance was considered as a p value of less than 0.05.

Results

A total of 197 subjects that were aged between 18 and 60 years (mean ± SD: 39.7 ± 12.1) were participated in this study. All participants were recruited from the hospital triage unit.

Demographic data showed that the majority of subjects were male (n = 107, 54.3%) and the remaining were female (n = 90, 45.7%), as explained in Table 1. Marital status analysis showed that 116 individuals (58.9%) were married, while 81 (41.1%) were single. The HTLV‐1‐positive case occurred among the single male group.

Regarding HTLV‐1–associated risk‐factors, three individuals had a history of blood transfusion, four reported previous surgery, one had undergone organ transplantation, and one person reported a history of substance use. Notably, the only HTLV‐1‐positive participant was the one who had both transfusion and surgical history. Table 1 provides a summary of these data and shows the gender‐specific HTLV‐1 status, with all female participants testing negative.

In total, 77 participants (39.1%) were traveled abroad (Table 1) and Turkey was the most of them (n = 25). Furthermore, 100 participants (50.8%) mentioned domestic travel in Iran, specifically to Shiraz, Mazandaran, and Isfahan (Table 1). No history of domestic or international travel was reported by the HTLV‐1‐positive participant.

Regarding our result, only one participant tested positive for HTLV‐1, resulting in an overall prevalence of 0.51%. The positive individual was a 40‐year‐old single male with a history of both blood transfusion and prior surgery. He did not report any history of organ transplantation, addiction, or any history of travel. All remaining 196 participants tested negative for HTLV‐1.

Regarding statistical analysis, it did not show a significant relationship between different parameters including HTLV‐1 status and gender (p = 0.364) or marital status (p = 0.291). However, a statistically significant association has been found between HTLV‐1 positivity and history of blood transfusion (p = 0.015) and history of surgery (p = 0.020). There was no statistical significance between travel history with HTLV‐1 status, as all individuals who had traveled tested negative.

Discussion

The findings of the present study indicate a probable low prevalence of HTLV‐1 infection in the screened general population in Iran, with a rate of 0.51%; one out of 197 participants tested positive.

In several studies conducted in low‐risk or nonendemic regions globally, HTLV‐1 prevalence is reported to be below 2%. A study conducted in Vitória, Brazil, reported a prevalence of 0.47% [20]. Also in another study, it was seen that HTLV‐1 seropositivity in Salvador, Brazil was 1.7% [21]. In the general population of East and West Africa (Senegal and Rwanda), the incidence ranges from 0.2% to 1.2%. Reports say that the seroprevalence among pregnant women were 0.02% in Italy, 0.1% in France, Slovenia and Spain, 0.03% in Unitedkingdom respectively [22, 23].

In one study, the average prevalence of HTLV‐1 infection among patients receiving MHD in several regions of Japan was 2.7%, reaching 21.2% only in Okinawa, a highly endemic area of HTLV‐1 among asymptomatic blood donors [24]. These results have contrast compared to ours which may contribute to the subjects of the study as patients receiving MHD may be at higher risk of the infection.

In Guinea‐Bissau, prevalence has been reported to range from 2.3% to 3.6% in the general population and up to 8.8% among people over the age of 55 [25]. In certain areas of the United States, prevalence rates have been 8% among intravenous drug users and 19% in sexually transmitted diseases (STD) clinics [26]. Perhaps the most striking figures have been reported in remote central Australian Aboriginal communities, where HTLV‐1 prevalence was 3.5% among children, 36.8% among adults, and as high as 49.3% among individuals over 45 years old [27].

Our reported prevalence is significantly lower than the rates observed in certain regions of Iran in previous studies, such as Mashhad, where a prevalence of 2.12% has been reported [28]. The sole positive case involved a 40‐year‐old male with no prior history of drug use or travel, who had undergone surgery and blood transfusions. This observation aligns with our current understanding of the primary modes of HTLV‐1 transmission, which encompass the transfusion of contaminated blood products, extended breastfeeding, and sexual contact [2, 29].

In Iran, the rate of HTLV‐1 among people who donate blood varies by location, from 0.11% to 0.21% in places like Karaj, Alborz Province, Hormozgan, and Ilam. South Khorasan (0.042%) and Bushehr (0.013%) have the lowest rates [30]. Also, a study conducted in Golestan Province, Iran, indicated that the actual prevalence of HTLV‐1 in that community was 0.3% [31]. In contrast, a study in Mazandaran Province, Iran, among hemodialysis patients showed a prevalence of HTLV‐1 of 0.6% which is align with our study in the context of our study [32, 33].

However, higher prevalence rates have been found in endemic areas and among high‐risk groups, such as in Neyshabur, Iran, where 14.49% of hemodialysis patients tested positive for HTLV‐1, with higher prevalence among older people [32, 33].

In a community‐based study in Torbat‐e Heydarieh of Iran, HTLV‐1 prevalence was assessed through serological testing followed by molecular confirmation. Serological evidence of infection was detected in 2% of participants, whereas confirmed proviral infection was observed in 1.25%. The prevalence observed in this study was lower than estimates previously reported from the neighboring endemic cities in Iran [34]. In addition, another study in Alborz mentioned a 0.13% HTLV‐1 positivity rate among blood donors, which is lower than but relatively closer to our findings [35]. Overall, all these studies including ours, suggest that the prevalence observed in our hospital‐based triage population falls within the lower range compared to different regions and population groups in Iran.

However, in certain populations it is expected that higher percentage of infected individuals are expected but since our study target were general population, this low prevalence shows we must target high‐risk groups to check the HTLV‐1 infection prevalence in these groups. Furthermore, the cross‐sectional design of the study makes it more difficult to evaluate changes in prevalence over time. To gain a more detailed grasp of HTLV‐1 epidemiology in Iran, subsequent studies should employ with larger and more diverse population samples. In addition, relatively small sample size, single‐center nature of the study, hospital‐based sampling strategy, and the limited generalizability of the findings beyond this specific triage population in Tehran are also other limitations of this study. Given that only one HTLV‐1‐positive case was identified, these statistical associations should be interpreted with caution.

Conclusion

This finding should not be construed as indicative of low risk, despite the study's observed prevalence of HTLV‐1 being minimal (0.51%). The identification of a single positive case within a relatively small sample underscores the importance of implementing targeted surveillance strategies, particularly in light of the potential for asymptomatic carriage and the severe long‐term consequences of HTLV‐1 infection, including ATLL and HAM/TSP. Individuals with medical histories involving blood exposure or surgical interventions should be accorded additional consideration. To gain a deeper understanding of the real impact of HTLV‐1 infection in Iran, future research should prioritize targeted epidemiological studies within these vulnerable populations. These initiatives are essential for guiding public health strategies, screening protocols, and the allocation of resources for HTLV‐1 prevention and management.
