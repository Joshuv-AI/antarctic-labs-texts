Abstract
. The classical modular polynomial for j j -invariants describes the relation between two elliptic curves connected by isogenies. This polynomial has been applied to various algorithms in computational number theory, such as point counting on elliptic curves. In addition, computing the modular polynomial itself is also an important problem, and various algorithms to compute it have been proposed. On the other hand, modular polynomials for other invariants of higher level structures have also been studied. For example, the modular polynomials for the Legendre λ \lambda -invariant and the Weber functions are well-known. In this paper, we give another approach to construct modular polynomials of higher level purely algebraically. In particular, we show the existence of modular polynomials for invariants directly related to models of elliptic curves, such as the coefficients of Montgomery and Hessian curves. We also show that these modular polynomials have integer coefficients and are symmetric and irreducible in certain cases, and give an algorithm to compute them, which is based on the deformation method by Kunzweiler and Robert.

1.
Introduction

For a positive integer
m
,
the
classical modular polynomial

\Phi_{m}(X,Y)\in\mathbb{Z}[X,Y]

is a polynomial that describes the relation between two
m
-isogenous
j
-invariants.
More precisely,
for
\tau
in the upper half plane
\mathbb{H}
of the complex number field,
we denote by
E_{\tau}
the elliptic curve over
\mathbb{C}
defined by

\mathbb{C}/(\mathbb{Z}+\mathbb{Z}\tau)
,
and by
j(\tau)
the
j
-invariant of
E_{\tau}
.
Then, we have

\Phi_{m}(j(\tau),j(\tau^{\prime}))=0

if and only if there exists a cyclic isogeny of degree
m

between the elliptic curves
E_{\tau}
and
E_{\tau^{\prime}}
.
The polynomial
\Phi_{m}(X,Y)
is symmetric,
i.e.,
\Phi_{m}(X,Y)=\Phi_{m}(Y,X)
,
and monic of degree
\psi(m)
in both
X
and
Y
,
where
\psi
is the Dedekind function, i.e.,

\psi(m)=m\prod_{p\mid m}\left(1+\frac{1}{p}\right)

with the product over all prime divisors
p
of
m
.

1.1.
Algorithms for computing modular polynomials

Computing the classical modular polynomials is a well-studied problem.
The algorithms to compute them include
a method using Fourier expansion
[
11
]

and a method using isogenies between supersingular elliptic curves
with the Chinese Remainder Theorem (CRT)
[
7
]
.
Enge
[
12
]
provides an algorithm in
O(\ell^{3}(\log\ell)^{4+\epsilon})
using interpolation and floating-point evaluation
under some heuristics.
However, this algorithm needs an excessive amount of memory for large
\ell
.
Then, Bröker, Lauter, and Sutherland
[
3
]
provided
an algorithm in
O(\ell^{3}\log^{3}\ell\log\log\ell)
using
isogeny volcanoes with the CRT method under Generalized Riemann Hypothesis (GRH),
which requires a practical amount of memory.
Recently, Kunzweiler and Robert
[
16
]
proposed
an algorithm in the same complexity without GRH.
Their algorithm also uses the CRT method,
and is based on the deformation theory and
a higher dimensional representation of isogenies between elliptic curves
based on Kani’s work
[
15
]
,
developed by
[
6
,
19
,
26
]
.

1.2.
Modular polynomials of higher level

Another characterization of the classical modular polynomial
is that
\Phi_{m}(X,j(\tau))
is the minimal polynomial of
j(m\tau)

over
\mathbb{Q}(j(\tau))
for
\tau\in\mathbb{H}
such that
j(\tau)
is transcendental over
\mathbb{Q}
.
Note that
E_{m\tau}
is the codomain of the isogeny defined by

z\mapsto mz
,
whose kernel is the cyclic subgroup of order
m
generated by

\frac{1}{m}
in
E_{\tau}
.

This characterization allows us to define
modular polynomials of higher level
as follows:
Let
f
be a modular function for a congruence subgroup
\Gamma
of level
N
in
\mathrm{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z})
,
and let
\tau
be an element in
\mathbb{H}
such that
f(\tau)
is a transcendental number.
Let
m
be a positive integer prime to
N
,
and
\Phi^{\prime}(X)
be the minimal polynomial of
f(m\tau)
over
\mathbb{Q}(f(\tau))

Then, we define the
modular polynomial of order
m
for
f

to be the polynomial
\Phi_{m}^{f}(X,Y)\in\mathbb{Q}[X,Y]
obtained by
replacing
f(\tau)
by
Y
in
\Phi^{\prime}(X)
,
and denote it by
\Phi_{m}^{f}(X,Y)
.
For example,
it is known that there exist the modular polynomials
for the Weber function and the Legendre
\lambda
-invariant
(see
[
5
,
3
]
and
[
2
, Chap. 4.4]
for example).
As mentioned in
[
3
]
,
if the Fourier expansion of
f
has integer coefficients
then
\Phi_{m}^{f}(X,Y)
has integer coefficients.
In addition,
the modular polynomial
\Phi_{\ell}^{f}(X,Y)
for a prime
\ell\nmid N

is symmetric if
f
is invariant under the action of
certain matrices
[
5
, Lemma 5.5]
.

1.3.
Geometric viewpoint of modular polynomials of higher level

From geometric viewpoints,
the modular polynomial
\Phi_{m}^{f}(X,Y)

describes the relation between isomorphism classes of enhanced elliptic curves
of level
\Gamma

m

By observing the images of
\tau/N
and
1/N

under the isogeny
E_{\tau}\to E_{m\tau}

we can define

•

An

\Gamma(N)

(E,(P,Q))
of an elliptic curve

\mathbb{C}

and a basis

An

between enhanced elliptic curves

(E,(P,Q))
and
(E^{\prime},(P^{\prime},Q^{\prime}))

\Gamma(N)

is an isogeny
\varphi:E\to E^{\prime}
of degree

such that
\varphi(P)=[m]P^{\prime}
and
\varphi(Q)=Q^{\prime}
.

•

An
enhanced elliptic curve of level
\Gamma_{1}(N)

is a pair
(E,P)
of an elliptic curve
E
over
\mathbb{C}

and a point

of order
N
.
An
isogeny of degree

(E,P)

(E^{\prime},P^{\prime})
of level
\Gamma_{1}(N)

is an isogeny
\varphi:E\to E^{\prime}
of degree

such that
\varphi(P)=P^{\prime}

•

An
enhanced elliptic curve of level
\Gamma_{0}(N)

is a pair
(E,C)
of an elliptic curve
E
over
\mathbb{C}

and a cyclic subgroup

of

.
An
isogeny of degree
m
between enhanced elliptic curves

(E,C)

(E^{\prime},C^{\prime})

\varphi:E\to E^{\prime}

To simplify the notation,
we denote by

\Gamma

where
\Gamma

,
\Gamma_{1}(N)
, and
\Gamma_{0}(N)

The main target of this paper is

which we define as follows:

Definition 1.1
.

Let
\Gamma

\Gamma(N)
,
\Gamma_{1}(N)
, or
\Gamma_{0}(N)

•

\Phi_{m}^{f}(f(E,\ast),f(E^{\prime},\ast))=0

(E^{\prime},\ast)

•

\Phi_{m}^{f}(X,Y)

\Phi_{m}^{f}(X,Y)

is symmetric
1

, and monic of degree

in both

]

We note that the proof in

.

1.4.
Contributions

The Legendre
\lambda
-invariant has the elliptic curve model

(1.1)

E_{\lambda}:Y^{2}Z=X(X-Z)(X-\lambda Z),

and its basis
((0:0:1),(1:0:1))
of the
2
-torsion subgroup.
Therefore, we can regard the modular polynomial for the Legendre
\lambda
-invariant
as the modular polynomial of higher level in Definition
1.1
.
On the other hand,
there are other forms of elliptic curves
which can be regarded as enhanced elliptic curves of higher level.
For example,
a Montgomery curve
is known to be an enhanced elliptic curve of level
\Gamma_{0}(4)

(we will explain this in Section
5.1
).
Therefore, it is natural to expect the existence of modular polynomials
for the coefficients of Montgomery curves.
However, to the best of our knowledge,
there is no literature proving the existence of modular polynomials
for such forms of elliptic curves.

One possible approach to prove the existence of modular polynomials
is to find an expression of the coefficients of Montgomery curves
as a modular function for
\Gamma_{0}(4)
,
and to show that its Fourier expansion has integer coefficients.

In this paper, we take another approach to construct modular polynomials of higher level.
In particular,
we show the existence of modular polynomials for a map
f
in Definition
1.1

when
f
has scalar multiplication formulas, division polynomials, and isogeny formulas
satisfying certain conditions.
These conditions are designed to make
f(E^{\prime},\ast)
integral
over
\mathbb{Z}[f(E,\ast)]
, where
(E,\ast)
and
(E^{\prime},\ast)
are enhanced elliptic curves
connected by a cyclic isogeny.
Under these conditions,
we prove that the modular polynomial
\Phi_{m}^{f}(X,Y)

exists and has integer coefficients for any positive integer
m
prime to
N
.
In addition,
we show that
\Phi_{m}^{f}(X,Y)
is symmetric
if
m>1
, and the level of
f
is
\Gamma_{0}(N)
or
m\equiv\pm 1\pmod{N}

when the level of
f
is
\Gamma_{1}(N)
or
\Gamma(N)
.
Examples of such maps
f
include
the coefficients of Montgomery and Hessian curves.
We also give an algorithm to compute such modular polynomials,
which is based on the algorithm in
[
16
]
.

We do not claim that our examples are not modular functions.
Rather, we believe that they are modular functions.
Our contribution is to show another approach to construct modular polynomials,
which is purely algebraic and without using the theory of modular forms.
We believe that our approach could be useful to construct modular polynomials
for other forms of elliptic curves.

2.
Invariants and models for enhanced elliptic curves

In this section, we introduce the notion of
an
invariant of level
\Gamma
and its
good model
for a congruence subgroup
\Gamma
,
which are necessary to prove the existence of modular polynomials of higher level.

Definition 2.1
.

Let
\Gamma
be
\Gamma(N)
,
\Gamma_{1}(N)
, or
\Gamma_{0}(N)
,
and
S
be the set of isomorphism classes of
enhanced elliptic curves of level
\Gamma
.
An
invariant of level
\Gamma
is an injection

\alpha:S\to\mathbb{C}

accompanied by a rational function
j_{\alpha}(X)\in\mathbb{Q}(X)

satisfying the following conditions:

•

For a representative
(E,*)
of an enhanced elliptic curve in
S
,
the
j
-invariant of
E
is given by
j(E)=j_{\alpha}(\alpha(E,*))
.

•

Let
J_{1}(X)/J_{0}(X)
be the reduced form of
j_{\alpha}(X)

with
J_{0}(X),J_{1}(X)\in\mathbb{Z}[X]
.
Then, the following conditions hold:

–

\deg J_{1}(X)>\deg J_{0}(X)
,

–

J_{0}(z)=0
for all
z\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\operatorname{Im}\alpha
,

–

J_{0}(X)
is primitive,

–

the leading coefficient of
J_{1}(X)
is not divisible by any prime not dividing
N
.

Remark 2.2
.

Although we do not restrict the degree of
J_{1}(X)
,
it should be equal to the index
[\mathrm{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z}):\Gamma]

since the number of isomorphism classes of enhanced elliptic curves of level
\Gamma

with a fixed
j
-invariant is equal to
[\mathrm{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z}):\Gamma]
.

Obviously, the
j
-invariant itself is an invariant of level
\mathrm{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z})
.
For another example,
it is known that the Legendre
\lambda
-invariant
is an invariant of level
\Gamma(2)
(see
[
18
, Chapter 18, §6]
).
Since the
j
-invariant of the Legendre form (
1.1
) is given by

j(\lambda)=\frac{2^{8}(\lambda^{2}-\lambda+1)^{3}}{\lambda^{2}(\lambda-1)^{2}},

the polynomials
J_{1}(X)=2^{8}(X^{2}-X+1)^{3}
and
J_{0}(X)=X^{2}(X-1)^{2}

satisfy the above conditions for
N=2
.
In addition,
as we will see in Section
5
,
the coefficients of Montgomery and Hessian curves are invariants of level
\Gamma_{0}(4)
and
\Gamma(3)
, respectively.

In addition to the above conditions,
we require a complete set of representatives of enhanced elliptic curves
with coordinates having formulas for scalar multiplications, division polynomials, and isogenies.
We call such a set a
good model
of the invariant.
Intuitively,
for a certain form of elliptic curves,
an invariant is the coefficient of an elliptic curve in that form,
and a model of the invariant is a pair of the form of elliptic curves itself
and the
x
-coordinate of the form.
More precisely, we define a
model of an invariant of level
\Gamma
as follows:

Definition 2.3
.

Let
\Gamma
be
\Gamma(N)
,
\Gamma_{1}(N)
, or
\Gamma_{0}(N)
,
and
\alpha
be an invariant of level
\Gamma
.
A
model
of
\alpha
is
a complete set of representatives

\{(E_{z}^{\alpha},\ast)\mid z\in\operatorname{Im}\alpha\}

of enhanced elliptic curves of level
\Gamma

accompanied by
a set of functions

\{\chi_{z}^{\alpha}:E_{z}^{\alpha}(\mathbb{C})\to\mathbb{C}\cup\{\infty\}\mid z\in\operatorname{Im}\alpha\}

satisfying the following conditions:

(1)

For any
z\in\operatorname{Im}\alpha
,
the function
\chi_{z}^{\alpha}
is surjective.

(2)

For any
z\in\operatorname{Im}\alpha
, we have

(\chi_{z}^{\alpha})^{-1}(\infty)\subset E_{z}^{\alpha}[N]
.

(3)

For any
R_{1},R_{2}\in E_{z}^{\alpha}(\mathbb{C})
,
we have
\chi_{z}^{\alpha}(R_{1})=\chi_{z}^{\alpha}(R_{2})

if and only if
R_{1}=R_{2}+R_{\infty}
or
R_{1}=-R_{2}+R_{\infty}

for some
R_{\infty}\in(\chi_{z}^{\alpha})^{-1}(\infty)
.

We say that a model
\{((E_{z}^{\alpha},\ast),\chi_{z}^{\alpha})\}

is a
good model
if
there exist sets of rational functions and polynomials,
which we call the
scalar multiplication formulas
,

division polynomials
, and
isogeny formulas
,
defined as follows:

•

(
Scalar multiplication formula
)
For an integer
m
,
a
scalar multiplication formula by
m

is a rational function
M_{m}(X;Y)\in\mathbb{Z}(X;Y)

satisfying

\chi_{z}^{\alpha}([m]R)=M_{m}(z;\chi_{z}^{\alpha}(R))

for any
z\in\operatorname{Im}\alpha
and
R\in E_{z}^{\alpha}(\mathbb{C})
.

•

(
Division polynomials
)
For a positive integer
m
,
a
division polynomial of order
m

is a polynomial

\Psi_{m}(X;Y)\in\mathbb{Z}[X;Y]
satisfying the following conditions:

–

The leading coefficient of
\Psi_{m}(X;Y)
as a polynomial in
Y
is an integer not divisible by any prime not dividing
m
.

–

For any
z\in\operatorname{Im}\alpha
and
R\in E_{z}^{\alpha}(\mathbb{C})\setminus(\chi_{z}^{\alpha})^{-1}(\infty)
,

\Psi_{m}(z;\chi_{z}^{\alpha}(R))=0

if and only if
\chi_{z}^{\alpha}([m]R)=\chi_{z}^{\alpha}(0_{E_{z}^{\alpha}})
,
where
0_{E_{z}^{\alpha}}
is the identity of
E_{z}^{\alpha}
.

•

(
Isogeny formula
)
For a positive integer
m
prime to
N
,
an
isogeny formula of degree
m

is a polynomial

V_{m}(X;Y_{1},\dots,Y_{m-1})\in\mathbb{Z}\left[\frac{1}{m}\right][X;Y_{1},\dots,Y_{m-1}]

satisfying the following condition:
For any
z,z^{\prime}\in\operatorname{Im}\alpha
such that
there exists
a cyclic isogeny
\varphi:E_{z}^{\alpha}\to E_{z^{\prime}}^{\alpha}
of degree
m
,
it holds that

z^{\prime}=V_{m}\left(z;\chi_{z}^{\alpha}(R),\chi_{z}^{\alpha}([2]R),\dots,\chi_{z}^{\alpha}([(m-1)]R)\right)

for any generator
R\in E_{z}^{\alpha}(\mathbb{C})
of
\ker\varphi
.

From the properties of a good model,
the function
\chi_{z}^{\alpha}
induces a surjection from
E_{z}^{\alpha}[m]\setminus\{0_{E_{z}^{\alpha}}\}
to
the set
\{\tau\in\mathbb{C}\mid\Psi_{m}(z;\tau)=0\text{ and }\tau\neq\chi_{z}^{\alpha}(0_{E_{z}^{\alpha}})\}

for any
z\in\operatorname{Im}\alpha
and positive integer

prime to
N
.
In addition, two points
R_{1},R_{2}\in E_{z}^{\alpha}[m]
have the same image under
\chi_{z}^{\alpha}

if and only if
R_{1}=\pm R_{2}
.

It is obvious that the
j
-invariant has a model; for example,
we can take

E_{j}^{\mathrm{jinv}}:Y^{2}Z=X^{3}-\frac{27j}{j-1728}XZ^{2}-\frac{54j}{j-1728}Z^{3}

with the
x
-coordinate as a model of the

-invariant
except for
j=0,1728
.
However, the division polynomials and isogeny formulas
for this model are not expressed in polynomials with coefficients in
\mathbb{Z}[j]
.
Therefore, the above model is not a good model.
On the other hand,
the Montgomery form and Hessian form
give good models of the invariants defined by their coefficients
(see Section
5
).

We also define a property of invariants
necessary to prove the irreducibility of modular polynomials
and to construct an algorithm to compute them.

Definition 2.4
.

Let
\Gamma
be
\Gamma(N)
,
\Gamma_{1}(N)
, or
\Gamma_{0}(N)
,
and
\alpha
be an invariant of level
\Gamma
.
We say that
\alpha
is
computable
if,
for any enhanced elliptic curve
(E^{\mathrm{jinv}}_{j},S)
of level
\Gamma

with
j
-invariant
j\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\{0,1728\}
,
the invariant
\alpha(E^{\mathrm{jinv}}_{j},S)
is represented by
a rational function in
j

and the coordinates of the points in
S
,
and the rational function does not depend on the choice of
j
and
S
.

Since our definition of invariants is motivated by examples such as
the coefficient of a Montgomery curve or a Hessian curve,

Definition

2.4
is a natural condition.
Indeed, these examples of invariants are computable
(see
Proposition

5.1
and
Lemma

5.5
).

3.
The existence and properties of modular polynomials

In this section, we state and prove the main theorems of this paper.
First, we show the existence of modular polynomials
for an invariant with a good model.
Next, we show that such modular polynomials have integer coefficients,
and are symmetric and irreducible in certain cases.
Finally, we give some conjectures for further properties of modular polynomials.

Throughout the rest of this paper,
we let
\Gamma
be
\Gamma(N)
,
\Gamma_{1}(N)
, or
\Gamma_{0}(N)

for a positive integer
N
,
and
\alpha
be an invariant of level
\Gamma
with a good model

\{((E_{z}^{\alpha},*),\chi_{z}^{\alpha})\}
.
In addition,
we let
J_{1}(X)/J_{0}(X)
be the reduced form of
j_{\alpha}(X)
,
and
M_{m}(X;Y)
,
\Psi_{m}(X;Y)
, and
V_{m}(X;Y_{1},\dots,Y_{m-1})

be the scalar multiplication formulas,
division polynomials, and isogeny formulas of the good model

\{((E_{z}^{\alpha},*),\chi_{z}^{\alpha})\}
.
We also let
\Phi_{m}
be the classical modular polynomial.

Main theorems

First, we show the existence of modular polynomials

.

For any positive integer

\mathbb{Q}

\mathbb{Q}(X)

\mathbb{Q}[X,\tau]

Let
\tilde{f}(Y)\in\mathbb{Q}(X)[Y]
be the minimal polynomial of
\tau
over
\mathbb{Q}(X)
,
and
f(Y)\in\mathbb{Q}[X][Y]
be a polynomial obtained by multiplying
a suitable polynomial in
\mathbb{Q}[X]
to
\tilde{f}(Y)

For

,
we denote by

the polynomial in
\mathbb{Q}[z][Y]

obtained by replacing

z

.
Let
\tau_{z}
be a root of

in

.
Then we have a ring homomorphism

Let

\mathbb{Q}
.
Then
\rho_{t}

\mathbb{Q}[t,\tau_{t}]

Let
\mathcal{R}
be
\rho_{t}^{-1}(\{\chi_{t}^{\alpha}(R)\mid R\in E_{t}^{\alpha}[m]\setminus\{0_{E_{t}^{\alpha}}\}\})

Then
\mathcal{R}
is contained in the set of roots of
\Psi_{m}(X;Y)
in
K

By the choice of

we have

.
As we mentioned in Section

,
we can identify the set
\mathcal{R}
with the quotient

(E_{t}^{\alpha}[m]\setminus\{0_{E_{t}^{\alpha}}\})/\{\pm 1\}

via the isomorphism
\rho_{t}
.
Therefore, we can take the subsets of
\mathcal{R}

corresponding to a cyclic subgroup of order
m
in
E_{t}^{\alpha}
.
Let
C_{1},\dots,C_{\psi(m)}
be all such subsets of
\mathcal{R}
.
Then we show below that

\rho_{z}(C_{1}),\dots,\rho_{z}(C_{\psi(m)})

are the sets corresponding to
the cyclic subgroups of order
m
in
E_{z}^{\alpha}

for any
z\in\operatorname{Im}\alpha
.

Since
\Psi_{m}(X;Y)
splits into linear factors over
\mathbb{Q}[X,\tau]
,
the image
\Psi_{m}(z;Y)
splits into linear factors over
\mathbb{Q}[z,\tau_{z}]
.
In addition,
the leading coefficient of
\Psi_{m}(z;Y)
in
Y
is in
\mathbb{Q}
.
This means that all roots of
\Psi_{m}(z;Y)
are contained in
\mathbb{Q}[z,\tau_{z}]
.
Therefore,
\rho_{z}
induces a surjection from
\mathcal{R}

to the set of
\chi_{z}^{\alpha}(R)
for
R\in E_{z}^{\alpha}[m]\setminus\{0_{E_{z}^{\alpha}}\}
.
Since the cardinalities of both sets are equal to each other,
the map
\rho_{z}
induces a bijection between them.
For any integer
i
and
\mu\in\mathcal{R}
,
it holds that
M_{i}(X;\mu)=\infty
implies
M_{i}(z;\rho_{z}(\mu))=\infty
.
Thus, the order of the point corresponding to
\rho_{t}(\mu)
in
E_{t}^{\alpha}

and that of
\rho_{z}(\mu)
are equal.
In addition,
it holds that
M_{i}(X;\mu)=\mu^{\prime}
implies

M_{i}(z;\rho_{z}(\mu))=\rho_{z}(\mu^{\prime})

for any integer
i
and
\mu,\mu^{\prime}\in\mathcal{R}
.
Therefore, the image
\rho_{z}(C_{i})

is the set corresponding to
a cyclic subgroup of order
m
in
E_{z}^{\alpha}
.
It follows that
\rho_{z}(C_{i})\neq\rho_{z}(C_{j})
for
i\neq j

since
\rho_{z}
induces a surjection from
\mathcal{R}
to
the set of
\chi_{z}^{\alpha}(R)
for
R\in E_{z}^{\alpha}[m]\setminus\{0_{E_{z}^{\alpha}}\}
.

Let
\mu_{i}
be an element of
C_{i}
corresponding to a generator of the cyclic subgroup.
We define a polynomial in
\mathbb{Q}[X,\tau][Y]
by

(3.1)

F(Y)=\prod_{i=1}^{\psi(m)}(Y-\sigma_{i}),

where

\sigma_{i}=V_{m}\left(X;\mu_{i},M_{2}(X;\mu_{i}),\dots,M_{m-1}(X;\mu_{i})\right)\in\mathbb{Q}[X,\tau]
.
Then, for any
z\in\operatorname{Im}\alpha
, the image
\rho_{z}(F(Y))\in\mathbb{Q}[z,\tau_{z}][Y]
satisfies,
for any
z^{\prime}\in\operatorname{Im}\alpha
,

\rho_{z}(F(z^{\prime}))=0

if and only if there exists a cyclic isogeny of degree
m

between the enhanced elliptic curves

(E_{z}^{\alpha},\ast)
and
(E_{z^{\prime}}^{\alpha},\ast)
.
Since
M_{2},\dots,M_{m-1}
and
V_{m}
are invariant under
the action of the Galois group of
K/\mathbb{Q}(X)
,
any element in the Galois group acts on the set
\{\sigma_{i}\mid 1\leq i\leq\psi(m)\}

as a permutation.
Therefore, the coefficients of
F(Y)
are contained in
\mathbb{Q}(X)
.
In addition,
since
\mu_{i},M_{2}(X;\mu_{i}),\dots,M_{m-1}(X;\mu_{i})

are roots of
\Psi_{m}(X;Y)
whose leading coefficient in
Y
is in
\mathbb{Z}
,
the coefficients of
F(Y)
are contained in
\mathbb{Q}[X]
.
We define
\Phi_{m}^{\alpha}(X,Y)\in\mathbb{Q}[X,Y]

as
F(Y)
viewed as a polynomial in
\mathbb{Q}[X,Y]
.
By the construction,

\Phi_{m}^{\alpha}(X,Y)
satisfies the conditions for the modular polynomial of order
m
for
\alpha
.
∎

Next, we show that the modular polynomial constructed in Theorem
3.1

has integer coefficients and is of degree
\psi(m)
in
X
.

Theorem 3.2
.

The modular polynomial
\Phi_{m}^{\alpha}(X,Y)
satisfies the following:

(1)

\Phi_{m}^{\alpha}(X,Y)\in\mathbb{Z}[X,Y]
.

(2)

\deg_{X}(\Phi_{m}^{\alpha})=\psi(m)
.

Proof.

(
1
):
Recall the definition of
\Phi_{m}^{\alpha}(X,Y)
in (
3.1
).
Since the leading coefficient of
\Psi_{m}(X;Y)
in
Y
is an integer not divisible by any prime not dividing
m
,
and
V_{m}
has coefficients in
\mathbb{Z}\left[\frac{1}{m}\right]
,
the roots
\sigma_{1},\dots,\sigma_{\psi(m)}
of
\Phi_{m}^{\alpha}(X,Y)

as a polynomial in
Y

are integral over
\mathbb{Z}\left[\frac{1}{m},X\right]
.

Let
j_{1},\dots,j_{\psi(m)}
be the roots of
the classical modular polynomial
\Phi_{m}\left(\frac{J_{1}(X)}{J_{0}(X)},Y\right)

in an algebraic closure of
\mathbb{Q}(X)
.
Then, we have
j_{i}=J_{1}(\sigma_{i})/J_{0}(\sigma_{i})
for all
1\leq i\leq\psi(m)

by reindexing if necessary.
Since
\Phi_{m}
has integer coefficients and is monic in
Y
,
the root
j_{i}
is integral over
\mathbb{Z}\left[\frac{J_{1}(X)}{J_{0}(X)}\right]
for all
1\leq i\leq\psi(m)
.
Let
c
be the leading coefficient of
J_{1}(X)
.
Then,
\sigma_{i}
is integral over
\mathbb{Z}\left[\frac{1}{c},j_{i}\right]

since we have
J_{1}(\sigma_{i})-J_{0}(\sigma_{i})j_{i}=0
.
Thus,

\sigma_{i}
is integral over
\mathbb{Z}\left[\frac{1}{c},\frac{J_{1}(X)}{J_{0}(X)}\right]
.

Combining the above results,
we see that
\sigma_{i}
is integral over
\mathbb{Z}\left[\frac{1}{m},X\right]\cap\mathbb{Z}\left[\frac{1}{c},\frac{J_{1}(X)}{J_{0}(X)}\right]
.
Since
m
is prime to
c
and
J_{0}(X)
is primitive,
we have
\mathbb{Z}\left[\frac{1}{m},X\right]\cap\mathbb{Z}\left[\frac{1}{c},\frac{J_{1}(X)}{J_{0}(X)}\right]=\mathbb{Z}[X]
.
This implies that the coefficients of
\Phi_{m}^{\alpha}(X,Y)
are in
\mathbb{Z}
.

(
2
):
Let
F(X,Y)=J_{1}(X)-J_{0}(X)Y
,
and
d=\deg J_{1}
.
Consider four variables
X,Y,Z,W
,
and define a polynomial
G(X,Y)\in\mathbb{Z}[X,Y]
by

G(X,Y)=\operatorname{Res}_{Z}(\operatorname{Res}_{W}(F(W,X),\Phi_{m}^{\alpha}(W,Z)),F(Z,Y))\in\mathbb{Z}[X,Y].

Here,
\operatorname{Res}_{Z}
and
\operatorname{Res}_{W}
denote the resultant with respect to
variables
Z
and
W
, respectively.
By the property of resultant,
for any
j,j^{\prime}\in\mathbb{C}
,
it holds that
G(j,j^{\prime})=0

if and only if there exist
z,z^{\prime}\in\mathbb{C}

such that
F(z,j)=0
,
F(z^{\prime},j^{\prime})=0
,
and
\Phi_{m}^{\alpha}(z,z^{\prime})=0
.
If
z\not\in\operatorname{Im}\alpha
then
J_{0}(z)=0
,
and thus
F(z,j)\neq 0
for any
j\in\mathbb{C}
.
This means that
G(j,j^{\prime})=0
if and only if
there exist
z,z^{\prime}\in\operatorname{Im}\alpha

such that
E_{z}^{\alpha}
and
E_{z^{\prime}}^{\alpha}
are connected by
a cyclic isogeny of degree
m
,
and
j=j(E_{z}^{\alpha})
,
j^{\prime}=j(E_{z^{\prime}}^{\alpha})
.
Therefore,
G(j,j^{\prime})=0
if and only if
\Phi_{m}(j,j^{\prime})=0
.
From the irreducibility of
\Phi_{m}(X,Y)
,
it follows that
G(X,Y)=\gamma\cdot\Phi_{m}(X,Y)^{n}
for some
\gamma\in\mathbb{C}^{\times}
and
n\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 1}
.

For
j\in\mathbb{C}
, we let
\omega_{1},\dots,\omega_{d}

be the roots of
F(W,j)
in
\mathbb{C}
.
For
i=1,\dots,d
,
let
z_{i,1},\dots,z_{i,\psi(m)}
be the roots of
\Phi_{m}^{\alpha}(\omega_{i},Z)
in
\mathbb{C}
.
Then, we have

\displaystyle G(j,Y)

\displaystyle=\operatorname{Res}_{Z}\left(c^{\deg_{X}(\Phi_{m}^{\alpha})}\prod_{i=1}^{d}\Phi_{m}^{\alpha}(\omega_{i},Z),F(Z,Y)\right)

\displaystyle=c^{d\cdot\deg_{X}(\Phi_{m}^{\alpha})}\prod_{i=1}^{d}\prod_{j=1}^{\psi(m)}F(z_{i,j},Y).

Therefore, we have
\deg_{Y}(G(j,Y))=d\cdot\psi(m)
.
Since this holds for any
j\in\mathbb{C}
,
we have
\deg_{Y}(G(X,Y))=d\cdot\psi(m)
.
Thus, we have
G(X,Y)=\gamma\cdot\Phi_{m}(X,Y)^{d}
.

Similar discussion considering
G(X,j)
for
j\in\mathbb{C}

shows that
\deg_{X}(G(X,Y))=d\cdot\deg_{X}(\Phi_{m}^{\alpha})
.
By comparing the degrees of
G(X,Y)
and
\Phi_{m}(X,Y)^{d}
in
X
,
we obtain
\deg_{X}(\Phi_{m}^{\alpha})=\psi(m)
.
∎

If
m>1
, then the classical modular polynomial
\Phi_{m}(X,Y)
is symmetric.
This property reflects the fact that the dual isogeny of a cyclic isogeny of degree
m
is also
a cyclic isogeny of degree
m
.
However, this property does not always hold
for modular polynomials of
\Gamma(N)
for
N>4
.
Let
\varphi:(E,(P,Q))\to(E^{\prime},(P^{\prime},Q^{\prime}))
be a cyclic isogeny of degree
m

between enhanced elliptic curves of level
\Gamma(N)
.
Then, we have
\varphi(P)=[m]P^{\prime}
and
\varphi(Q)=Q^{\prime}

by the definition of isogenies between
enhanced elliptic curves of level
\Gamma(N)
.
On the other hand, we have
\hat{\varphi}(P^{\prime})=P
and
\hat{\varphi}(Q^{\prime})=[m]Q

since
\hat{\varphi}\circ\varphi=[m]
.
Therefore, the dual isogeny
\hat{\varphi}

is an isogeny between enhanced elliptic curves of level
\Gamma(N)

if and only if
m\equiv 1\pmod{N}
.
If
m\equiv-1\pmod{N}
,
then
-\hat{\varphi}
is an isogeny between enhanced elliptic curves of level
\Gamma(N)
.
However, in the other cases, there is no guarantee that
there exists an isogeny from
(E^{\prime},(P^{\prime},Q^{\prime}))
to
(E,(P,Q))
of degree
m
.
Therefore, the modular polynomial for
\Gamma(N)

is not necessarily symmetric in general.
A similar argument holds for
\Gamma_{1}(N)
.
Conversely, if
m\equiv\pm 1\pmod{N}
or
\Gamma=\Gamma_{0}(N)
,
then we can show that the modular polynomial is symmetric.

Theorem 3.3
.

Let
m>1
be an integer prime to
N
.
Suppose that

m\equiv\pm 1\pmod{N}
or
\Gamma=\Gamma_{0}(N)
.
Then, the modular polynomial
\Phi_{m}^{\alpha}(X,Y)
is symmetric,
i.e.,
\Phi_{m}^{\alpha}(X,Y)=\Phi_{m}^{\alpha}(Y,X)
.

Proof.

Let
z,z^{\prime}\in\operatorname{Im}\alpha
such that
there exists a cyclic isogeny
\varphi
of degree
m

between the enhanced elliptic curves

(E_{z}^{\alpha},\ast)
and
(E_{z^{\prime}}^{\alpha},\ast)
.
Then, as discussed above,
the dual isogeny
\hat{\varphi}
or its negation
is a cyclic isogeny of degree
m

between
(E_{z^{\prime}}^{\alpha},\ast)
and
(E_{z}^{\alpha},\ast)
.
Therefore, we have
\Phi_{m}^{\alpha}(z,z^{\prime})=0

if and only if
\Phi_{m}^{\alpha}(z^{\prime},z)=0

for any
z,z^{\prime}\in\operatorname{Im}\alpha
.

Let
c(Y)\in\mathbb{Z}[Y]
be the leading coefficient of
\Phi_{m}^{\alpha}(X,Y)
in
X
.
We define a polynomial
\Upsilon(X,Y)
as
\Phi_{m}^{\alpha}(Y,X)-c(X)\Phi_{m}^{\alpha}(X,Y)
.
Then, for any
z,z^{\prime}\in\operatorname{Im}\alpha
such that
\Phi_{m}^{\alpha}(z,z^{\prime})=0
,
we have
\Upsilon(z,z^{\prime})=0
by the above argument.
In addition, we have
\deg_{Y}(\Upsilon)<\psi(m)
.
Therefore, we have
\Upsilon(X,Y)=0
,
thus
\Phi_{m}^{\alpha}(Y,X)=c(X)\Phi_{m}^{\alpha}(X,Y)
.
By exchanging the roles of
X
and
Y
,
we have
\Phi_{m}^{\alpha}(X,Y)=c(Y)\Phi_{m}^{\alpha}(Y,X)
.
This implies
\Phi_{m}^{\alpha}(X,Y)=c(X)c(Y)\Phi_{m}^{\alpha}(X,Y)
.
Therefore, we have
c(X)=c(Y)=\pm 1
.

Suppose that
c(Y)=-1
.
Then, we have
\Phi_{m}^{\alpha}(X,X)=-\Phi_{m}^{\alpha}(X,X)
,
thus
\Phi_{m}^{\alpha}(X,X)=0
.
This means that,
for any
z\in\operatorname{Im}\alpha
,
the elliptic curve
E_{z}^{\alpha}
is
m
-isogenous to itself.
This is a contradiction since
m>1
.
Therefore, we obtain
c(Y)=1
,
thus
\Phi_{m}^{\alpha}(X,Y)=\Phi_{m}^{\alpha}(Y,X)
.
∎

If
\alpha
is computable in the sense of Definition
2.4
,
then we can prove the irreducibility of the modular polynomial for
\alpha
.

Theorem 3.4
.

Assume that the invariant
\alpha
is computable.
Let
m
be a positive integer prime to
N
.
Then, the modular polynomial
\Phi_{m}^{\alpha}(X,Y)

is irreducible as a polynomial in
Y
over
\mathbb{C}(X)
.

Proof.

Consider the univariate rational function field
\mathbb{C}(j)
over
\mathbb{C}

and its algebraic closure
\overline{\mathbb{C}(j)}
.
Let
\tilde{\jmath}
be a root of
\Phi_{m}(j,X)
in
\overline{\mathbb{C}(j)}
,
and
K
be the minimal splitting field of
J_{1}(X)-J_{0}(X)j
in
\overline{\mathbb{C}(j)}
.

Let
E_{j}^{\mathrm{jinv}}
be the elliptic curve over
\mathbb{C}(j)

defined in Section
2
.
Then it is known that
the Galois group of
\mathbb{C}(j,E_{j}^{\mathrm{jinv}}[n])
over
\mathbb{C}(j)

is isomorphic to
\mathrm{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z})
for any positive integer
n

(see
[
10
, Section 7.5]
).
Since
m
is prime to
N
,
we have
\mathrm{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z}/mN\mathbb{Z})\cong\mathrm{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z}/m\mathbb{Z})\times\mathrm{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z}/N\mathbb{Z})
.
Under this isomorphism,
the subgroups
\mathrm{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z}/N\mathbb{Z})
and
\mathrm{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z}/m\mathbb{Z})
of
\mathrm{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z}/mN\mathbb{Z})

correspond to
the intermediate fields
\mathbb{C}(j,E_{j}^{\mathrm{jinv}}[m])
and
\mathbb{C}(j,E_{j}^{\mathrm{jinv}}[N])

of
\mathbb{C}(j,E_{j}^{\mathrm{jinv}}[mN])/\mathbb{C}(j)
, respectively.
Therefore, we have

\mathbb{C}(j,E_{j}^{\mathrm{jinv}}[m])\cap\mathbb{C}(j,E_{j}^{\mathrm{jinv}}[N])=\mathbb{C}(j)
.

Let
S
be a level structure of level
\Gamma
on
E_{j}^{\mathrm{jinv}}
.
Since the invariant
\alpha
is computable,
we can define the invariant
\tilde{\alpha}=\alpha(E_{j}^{\mathrm{jinv}},S)

in
\overline{\mathbb{C}(j)}
,
and
\tilde{\alpha}
is contained in
\mathbb{C}(j,E_{j}^{\mathrm{jinv}}[N])
.
This implies that
K
is contained in
\mathbb{C}(j,E_{j}^{\mathrm{jinv}}[N])
.
On the other hand,
we have
\mathbb{C}(j,\tilde{\jmath})\subset\mathbb{C}(j,E_{j}^{\mathrm{jinv}}[m])

since
\tilde{\jmath}
can be obtained by Vélu’s formula
[
29
]
.
Therefore, we have
K\cap\mathbb{C}(j,\tilde{\jmath})=\mathbb{C}(j)
.

Let
\varphi(X,Y)
be a monic factor of
\Phi_{m}^{\alpha}(X,Y)

in
\mathbb{C}(X)[Y]
.
Since
\Phi_{m}^{\alpha}(X,Y)
is monic in
Y
and has coefficients in
\mathbb{C}[X]
,
we have
\varphi(X,Y)\in\mathbb{C}[X][Y]
.
Let
d
be the degree of
\varphi(X,Y)
in
Y
.
As we showed in the proof of Theorem
3.2
,
we have

\operatorname{Res}_{Z}(\operatorname{Res}_{W}(F(W,X),\Phi_{m}^{\alpha}(W,Z)),F(Z,Y))=\gamma\cdot\Phi_{m}(X,Y)^{\deg J_{1}}

for some nonzero constant
\gamma\in\mathbb{C}
.
By the irreducibility of
\Phi_{m}(X,Y)
in
\mathbb{C}(X)[Y]

and the degree consideration,
we have

(3.2)

\operatorname{Res}_{Z}(\operatorname{Res}_{W}(F(W,X),\varphi(W,Z)),F(Z,Y))=\gamma^{\prime}\cdot\Phi_{m}(X,Y)^{D}

for some nonzero constant
\gamma^{\prime}\in\mathbb{C}
,
where
D=d\cdot\deg J_{1}/\psi(m)
.

Let
\tilde{\alpha}
be a root of
F(X,j)
in
\overline{\mathbb{C}(j)}
.
By the definition of modular polynomials,
we have
\operatorname{Res}_{Y}(\Phi_{m}^{\alpha}(\tilde{\alpha},Y),F(Y,\tilde{\jmath}))=0
.
Therefore, we can take the above
\varphi(X,Y)

such that
\operatorname{Res}_{Y}(\varphi(\tilde{\alpha},Y),F(Y,\tilde{\jmath}))=0
.
Since
K\cap\mathbb{C}(j,\tilde{\jmath})=\mathbb{C}(j)
,
for any two roots
\tilde{\alpha}_{1},\tilde{\alpha}_{2}
of
F(X,j)
,
there exists an automorphism
\sigma
of
\overline{\mathbb{C}(j)}
over
\mathbb{C}(j)

such that
\sigma(\tilde{\alpha}_{1})=\tilde{\alpha}_{2}
and
\sigma(\tilde{\jmath})=\tilde{\jmath}
.
This implies that

\operatorname{Res}_{Y}(\varphi(\tilde{\alpha},Y),F(Y,\tilde{\jmath}))=0

for any root
\tilde{\alpha}
of
F(X,j)
.

By substituting
X=j
in the left-hand side of (
3.2
),
we have

\operatorname{Res}_{Z}(\operatorname{Res}_{W}(F(W,j),\varphi(W,Z)),F(Z,Y))=\prod_{\tilde{\alpha}}\operatorname{Res}_{Z}(\varphi(\tilde{\alpha},Z),F(Z,Y)),

where
\tilde{\alpha}
runs through all roots of
F(X,j)
.
Since each factor
\operatorname{Res}_{Z}(\varphi(\tilde{\alpha},Z),F(Z,Y))
has a root
\tilde{\jmath}
,
the equation (
3.2
) implies that

(j-\tilde{\jmath})^{\deg J_{1}}
divides
\Phi_{m}(j,Y)^{D}
.
By the irreducibility of
\Phi_{m}(j,Y)
in
\mathbb{C}(j)[Y]
,
we have
D=\deg J_{1}
, thus
d=\psi(m)
.
Therefore,
\varphi(X,Y)=\Phi_{m}^{\alpha}(X,Y)
,
which shows the irreducibility of
\Phi_{m}^{\alpha}(X,Y)
in
\mathbb{C}(X)[Y]
.
∎

3.2.
Unproved conjectures

Here, we state two unproved conjectures
related to our modular polynomials.

The first conjecture is about bounds of the coefficients of
\Phi_{m}^{\alpha}
.
For this purpose,
we define the
logarithmic height
of a polynomial as follows:

Definition 3.5
.

For a polynomial
F(X,Y)=\sum_{i,j}c_{i,j}X^{i}Y^{j}\in\mathbb{Z}[X,Y]
,
we define the
logarithmic height
of
F(X,Y)
as

h(F)=\max_{i,j}\log|c_{i,j}|,

where
\log
denotes the natural logarithm,
and we set
\log 0=0
.

The bound of the logarithmic height of the classical modular polynomial
\Phi_{\ell}

for a prime number
\ell

is given by Theorem 1 and Corollary 9 in
[
4
]
:
For every prime number
\ell
, we have

(3.3)

h(\Phi_{\ell})\leq 6\ell\log\ell+16\ell+\min\{2\ell,14\sqrt{\ell}\log\ell\}.

We denote by
B_{\ell}
the right-hand side of (
3.3
).
Our conjecture is as follows:

Conjecture 3.6
.

There exists a bound
L
depending on
\alpha

such that,
for any prime number
\ell>L
not dividing
N
, we have

h(\Phi_{\ell}^{\alpha})\leq B_{\ell}/\deg(J_{1}).

This conjecture is a natural generalization of a heuristic
stated in the equation (23) in
[
4
]
.
Note that
\deg(J_{1})=[\mathrm{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z}):\Gamma]

as we mentioned in Remark
2.2
.
We can justify this conjecture by the following discussion.
As we showed in the proof of Theorem
3.2
,
we have

\operatorname{Res}_{Z}(\operatorname{Res}_{W}(F(W,X),\Phi_{\ell}^{\alpha}(W,Z)),F(Z,Y))=\gamma\cdot\Phi_{\ell}(X,Y)^{\deg(J_{1})},

where
F(X,Y)=J_{1}(X)-J_{0}(X)Y

and
\gamma
is some integer independent of
\ell
.
Roughly speaking,
the logarithmic height of the left-hand side
is about
\deg(J_{1})^{2}\cdot h(\Phi_{\ell}^{\alpha})
,
and that of the right-hand side is about
\deg(J_{1})\cdot h(\Phi_{\ell})
.
This gives a heuristic reason for the conjecture.

The second conjecture is about the evaluation of our modular polynomials
at a point not contained in the image of the invariant.

Conjecture 3.7
.

For any positive integer
m
prime to
N
,
there exists a permutation
\sigma
on
\mathbb{C}\setminus\operatorname{Im}\alpha

such that

\Phi_{m}^{\alpha}(z,Y)=(Y-\sigma(z))^{\psi(m)},

for any
z\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\operatorname{Im}\alpha
.

It is known that
the modular polynomial for the Legendre
\lambda
-invariant
satisfies this property with the identity permutation
(see exercise 6 in Chap. 4.4 in
[
2
]
).
The proof is based on the fact that the cusps
0
and
i_{\infty}
of
X(2)

are invariant under the action of certain elements of
\operatorname{GL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z})
.
Thus, this proof cannot be directly applied to our modular polynomials.
Our experimental results show that
the permutation
\sigma
in Conjecture
3.7

could be nontrivial in general.
See Section
6
for details.

4.
Algorithm

In this section, we present an algorithm to compute
the modular polynomial
\Phi_{m}^{\alpha}
of order
m
for the invariant
\alpha
.
To do so, we assume that the invariant
\alpha
is computable.
Then,
we can define the invariant
\alpha
over a finite field whose characteristic does not divide
N

by considering the reduction of the rational function in
Definition

2.4
.
Therefore, we denote by
\alpha(E,*)

the invariant
\alpha
of an enhanced elliptic curve
(E,*)
over a finite field.

4.2.
Kunzweiler–Robert algorithm

The Kunzweiler–Robert algorithm uses the CRT method,
i.e., computing
\Phi_{\ell}\bmod p
for many primes
p

and lifting them to
\mathbb{Z}[X,Y]
by the CRT.

A rough sketch of the Kunzweiler–Robert algorithm to compute
the classical modular polynomial
\Phi_{\ell}\bmod p

(1)

Let
E/\mathbb{F}_{p^{2}}
be an elliptic curve such that
E[\ell]\subset E(\mathbb{F}_{p^{2}})
.

(2)

Take a deformation
\mathcal{E}/R
of
E
such that
j(\mathcal{E})=j(E)+\epsilon
.

(3)

Compute all isogenies
f:E\to E^{\prime}
of degree
\ell
over
\mathbb{F}_{p^{2}}
.

(4)

For each isogeny

\mathcal{E}^{\prime}

(5)

Let

\mathcal{E}^{\prime}
runs through all deformations obtained in the previous step.

(6)

By substituting
\epsilon=X-j(E)

\Phi_{\ell}(X,Y)

\mathbb{F}_{p}[X,Y]

Suppose that

4.3.
Algorithms to compute modular polynomials for invariants

We present an algorithm to compute the modular polynomials
\Phi_{\ell}^{\alpha}(X,Y)\in\mathbb{Z}[X,Y]
.

First, we explain how to compute
\Phi_{\ell}^{\alpha}\bmod p
.
For simplicity,
we consider the case where
\Gamma=\Gamma_{1}(N)
.
The other cases are obtained similarly.
Our algorithm to compute
\Phi_{\ell}^{\alpha}\bmod p
is as follows:

(1)

Let
(E,P)
be an enhanced elliptic curve over
\mathbb{F}_{p^{2}}
.

(2)

Take a lift
\tilde{a}
such that
\tilde{a}=\alpha(E,P)+\epsilon
,
and let
\mathcal{E}
be a deformation of
E
such that
j(\mathcal{E})=j_{\alpha}(\tilde{a})
.

(3)

Compute all isogenies
f:E\to E^{\prime}
of degree
\ell
and the images
P^{\prime}=f(P)
.

(4)

For each isogeny
f:E\to E^{\prime}
of degree
\ell
,
compute the deformation
\mathcal{E}^{\prime}
of
E^{\prime}
such that
f
lifts to an isogeny
\tilde{f}:\mathcal{E}\to\mathcal{E}^{\prime}
.

(5)

Compute the lift
\tilde{a}^{\prime}
of
\alpha(E^{\prime},P^{\prime})
such that
j_{\alpha}(\tilde{a}^{\prime})=j(\mathcal{E}^{\prime})
.

(6)

Let
\varphi(Y):=\prod_{\tilde{a}^{\prime}}(Y-\tilde{a}^{\prime})\in R[Y]
, where
\tilde{a}^{\prime}

runs through all lifts obtained in the previous step.

(7)

By substituting
\epsilon=X-\alpha(E,P)
, obtain
\Phi_{\ell}^{\alpha}(X,Y)\pmod{p}
.

The main difference from the Kunzweiler–Robert algorithm
is step (
5
).
Thus, we explain how to compute the lift
\tilde{a}^{\prime}
in this step.

Let
F(X)=J_{1}(X)-j(\mathcal{E}^{\prime})J_{0}(X)\in R[X]
.
By the definition of
\alpha
,
the condition
j_{\alpha}(\tilde{a}^{\prime})=j(\mathcal{E}^{\prime})

is equivalent to
F(\tilde{a}^{\prime})=0
.
Therefore, if
\frac{dF}{dX}(\alpha(E^{\prime},P^{\prime}))\not\equiv 0\pmod{(\epsilon)}
,
we can compute
\tilde{a}^{\prime}
from
\alpha(E^{\prime},P^{\prime})
and
j(\mathcal{E}^{\prime})

by using
\mathtt{Newton\_lift}

[
16
, Algorithm 1]
.
The equation
\frac{dF}{dX}(\alpha(E^{\prime},P^{\prime}))\equiv 0\pmod{(\epsilon)}
holds
if and only if
J_{1}(X)-j(E^{\prime})J_{0}(X)
has a multiple root at
X=\alpha(E^{\prime},P^{\prime})
.
This happens only when
j(E^{\prime})
is
1728
or
0
.
If this case occurs, we can simply discard
p
and choose another prime.

Note that we have
\tilde{a}^{\prime}=\alpha(\mathcal{E}^{\prime},\tilde{P}^{\prime})
,
where
\tilde{P}^{\prime}
is the unique lift of
P^{\prime}
in
\mathcal{E}^{\prime}
by
Theorem

4.3

since
\alpha(\mathcal{E}^{\prime},\tilde{P}^{\prime})
is the unique lift of
\alpha(E^{\prime},P^{\prime})

satisfying
F(\alpha(\mathcal{E}^{\prime},\tilde{P}^{\prime}))=0
by Hensel’s lemma.
Therefore, at the final step,
we obtain
\Phi_{\ell}^{\alpha}(X,Y)\bmod{(X-\alpha(E,P))^{\ell+2}}

as in the Kunzweiler–Robert algorithm,
which is equal to
\Phi_{\ell}^{\alpha}(X,Y)\bmod p
.

Based on the above algorithm to compute
\Phi_{\ell}^{\alpha}\bmod p
,
we can compute
\Phi_{\ell}^{\alpha}(X,Y)\in\mathbb{Z}[X,Y]
.
The following theorem states the complexity of our algorithm.

Theorem 4.6
.

Let
\ell
be an odd prime number prime to
N
.
Assume that
N\in O(1)
.
Then,
under
3.6
,
we can compute
\Phi_{\ell}^{\alpha}
in

O(\ell^{3}\log^{3}\ell\log\log\ell)
bit operations.

Proof.

The proof is similar to that of
[
16
, Theorem 4.4]
.
Here, we only explain the differences.
Since we require the level structure to be defined over
\mathbb{F}_{p^{2}}
,
we use a set of primes
\mathcal{P}_{\ell,N}
defined as

\mathcal{P}_{\ell,N}:=\{p>11~\text{prime}\mid\exists n,a,b~\text{with}~2^{n}-c_{\ell}\ell=a^{2}+4b^{2}~\text{and}~2^{n}c_{\ell}\ell|(p+1)~\text{and}~N\mid(p+1)\}

for computing
\Phi_{\ell}^{\alpha}\bmod p

By a similar argument as in Lemma 4.3 in
[

we can show that

\mathcal{P}_{\ell,N}

p

By

E_{A^{\prime}}^{\mathsf{Mont}}\to E_{-A^{\prime}}^{\mathsf{Mont}}
.
Then, it is easy to see that
the composition
\iota_{A^{\prime}}\circ\varphi\circ\iota_{A}^{-1}

is a cyclic isogeny of degree
m

between
(E_{-A}^{\mathsf{Mont}},C_{-A}^{\mathsf{Mont}})
and
(E_{-A^{\prime}}^{\mathsf{Mont}},C_{-A^{\prime}}^{\mathsf{Mont}})
.
Therefore, we have
\Phi_{m}^{\mathsf{Mont}}(-A,-A^{\prime})=0
.
This means that the polynomial
\Phi_{m}^{\mathsf{Mont}}(-X,-Y)

satisfies the conditions for the modular polynomial of order
m
for
\mathsf{Mont}
.
By the uniqueness of the modular polynomial,
we have
\Phi_{m}^{\mathsf{Mont}}(-X,-Y)=\Phi_{m}^{\mathsf{Mont}}(X,Y)
.
This implies the desired result.
∎

5.2.
Hessian curve

A
Hessian curve
over
\mathbb{C}
is an elliptic curve defined by
the equation

E_{d}^{\mathsf{Hess}}:X^{3}+Y^{3}+Z^{3}=dXYZ\text{ for }d\in\mathbb{C},\ d^{3}\neq 27

and a point
(1:-1:0)
.
The arithmetic on Hessian curves
can be found in
[
13
, §2.1]
.
In particular,
a point
(x:y:z)\in E_{d}^{\mathsf{Hess}}(\mathbb{C})
is in the
3
-torsion subgroup
if and only if
xyz=0
.
Let
\omega=e^{2\pi i/3}
.
Then,
we define

P_{d}^{\mathsf{Hess}}=(-\omega:1:0)

and

Q_{d}^{\mathsf{Hess}}=(0:-1:1)
.
As we show in the following lemma,
the points
P_{d}^{\mathsf{Hess}}
and
Q_{d}^{\mathsf{Hess}}

form a basis of the
3
-torsion subgroup of
E_{d}^{\mathsf{Hess}}
,
and the Weil pairing satisfies
e_{3}(P_{d}^{\mathsf{Hess}},Q_{d}^{\mathsf{Hess}})=\omega
.

Let
S(3)
be the set of isomorphism classes of
enhanced elliptic curves of level
\Gamma(3)
.
We define a map

\mathsf{Hess}:S(3)\to\mathbb{C};\ (E_{d}^{\mathsf{Hess}},(P_{d}^{\mathsf{Hess}},Q_{d}^{\mathsf{Hess}}))\mapsto d.

In the following,
we show that this map is well-defined
and gives an invariant of level
\Gamma(3)
.

We first note that,
we can take a representative
(E,(P,Q))

of any element in
S(3)

such that

E
is given by

(5.1)

E:Y^{2}Z+a_{1}XYZ+a_{3}YZ^{2}=X^{3}

for some
a_{1},a_{3}\in\mathbb{C}
,
and that
P=(0:0:1)

(see
[
14
, Chap. 4 §2]
).
In addition,
for
j\in\mathbb{C}
and the level structure
(P_{j},Q_{j})
on
E_{j}^{\mathrm{jinv}}
,
the coefficients
a_{1}
and
a_{3}

satisfying
(E_{j}^{\mathrm{jinv}},(P_{j},Q_{j}))\cong(E,(P,Q))

can be represented as rational functions in
j
and the coordinates of
P_{j}
.

The following lemma gives an isomorphism
from such an elliptic curve
E
to a Hessian curve.

Lemma 5.5
.

Let
E
be the elliptic curve defined by (
5.1
),

P=(0:0:1)
,
and
Q=(\xi:\eta:1)
be an order-
3
point on
E

such that the Weil pairing
e_{3}(P,Q)=\omega
.
Then the map
\mathbb{P}^{2}_{\mathbb{C}}\to\mathbb{P}^{2}_{\mathbb{C}}
defined by

(X:Y:Z)\mapsto\begin{pmatrix}\omega a_{1}&(2\omega+1)&(\omega-1)a_{3}\\
-(\omega+1)a_{1}&-(2\omega+1)&(-\omega-2)a_{3}\\
a_{1}+3\frac{a_{3}}{\xi}&0&0\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}X\\
Y\\
Z\end{pmatrix}

induces an isomorphism from
E
to
E_{d}^{\mathrm{Hess}}
for

d=\frac{3a_{1}\xi}{a_{1}\xi+3a_{3}}
.
In addition, this isomorphism maps
P
to
(-\omega:1:0)

and
Q
to
(0:-1:1)
.

Proof.

We first note that
\xi\neq 0

since the only points with
X
-coordinate
0
on
E

are
P
,
(0:-a_{3}:1)=-P
, and the identity point
(0:1:0)
.

For
c^{\prime},d^{\prime}\in\mathbb{C}
with
c^{\prime}\neq 0,d^{\prime 3}\neq 27c^{\prime}
,
we define a
generalized Hessian curve

H_{c^{\prime},d^{\prime}}
by

H_{c^{\prime},d^{\prime}}:X^{3}+Y^{3}+c^{\prime}Z^{3}=d^{\prime}XYZ\text{ and }(1:-1:0).

Let
\gamma
be a cube root of
c^{\prime}
.
Then there exists an isomorphism from
H_{c^{\prime},d^{\prime}}
to
E_{d^{\prime}/\gamma}^{\mathrm{Hess}}

defined by
(X:Y:Z)\mapsto(X:Y:\gamma Z)
.

From the proof of Theorem 1 in
[
13
]
,
there exists an isomorphism from
E
to
H_{c^{\prime},d^{\prime}}

for
c^{\prime}=a_{1}^{3}-27a_{3}
and
d^{\prime}=3a_{1}

defined by

(5.2)

(X:Y:Z)\mapsto\begin{pmatrix}\omega a_{1}&(2\omega+1)&(\omega-1)a_{3}\\
-(\omega+1)a_{1}&-(2\omega+1)&(-\omega-2)a_{3}\\
1&0&0\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}X\\
Y\\
Z\end{pmatrix}.

Therefore, it suffices to show that
a_{1}+3a_{3}/\xi
is a cube root of
a_{1}^{3}-27a_{3}

for proving the first part of this lemma.

The
3
rd division polynomial of
E
is given by

3x^{4}+a_{1}^{2}x^{3}+3a_{1}a_{3}x^{2}+3a_{3}^{2}x.

Since
\xi\neq 0
,
we have
3\xi^{3}+a_{1}^{2}\xi^{2}+3a_{1}a_{3}\xi+3a_{3}^{2}=0
.
From this, it is easy to verify that

(a_{1}+3a_{3}/\xi)^{3}=a_{1}^{3}-27a_{3}
.

It is straightforward to check that
the above isomorphism maps
P
to
(-\omega:1:0)
.
To see that it maps
Q
to
(0:-1:1)
,
we calculate the Weil pairing
e_{3}(P,Q)
.
For a point
R
on
E
of order
3
,
the Miller function
f_{3,R}(x,y)

is the tangent line at
R
on the curve
E
whose coefficient of
y
is
1

(see
[
20
, §4.1]
for the definition of Miller function).
From Proposition 8 in
[
20
]
,
we have

e_{3}(P,Q)=-\frac{f_{3,P}(Q)}{f_{3,Q}(P)}
.
An easy calculation shows that
e_{3}(P,Q)=\omega
is equivalent to

\omega a_{1}\xi+(2\omega+1)\eta+(\omega-1)a_{3}=0.

This implies that
the image of
Q
under the above isomorphism is
(0:-1:1)
.
∎

Next, we show that the coefficient
d
of a Hessian curve
is unchanged under isomorphisms of enhanced elliptic curves of level
\Gamma(3)
.

Lemma 5.6
.

Let
d,d^{\prime}\in\mathbb{C}
with
d^{3}\neq 27
and
d^{\prime 3}\neq 27
,
and
\iota:E_{d}^{\mathrm{Hess}}\to E_{d^{\prime}}^{\mathrm{Hess}}
be an isomorphism
mapping
(-\omega:1:0)
to
(-\omega:1:0)
.
Then,

d^{\prime}=\omega^{i}d
and
\iota
is given by

(X:Y:Z)\mapsto(X:Y:\omega^{-i}Z)

for some
i\in\{0,1,2\}
.

Proof.

Let
a_{1}=d/3
,
a_{3}=(d^{3}-3^{3})/3^{6}
, and
E_{a_{1},a_{3}}

be the elliptic curve defined by

E_{a_{1},a_{3}}:Y^{2}Z+a_{1}XYZ+a_{3}YZ^{2}=X^{3}.

Then, the isomorphism defined in (
5.2
)
gives an isomorphism from
E_{a_{1},a_{3}}
to
E_{d}^{\mathrm{Hess}}

mapping
(0:0:1)
to
(-\omega:1:0)
.
We denote this isomorphism by
\iota_{d}
.
Similarly, we define
a_{1}^{\prime}=d^{\prime}/3
,
a_{3}^{\prime}=(d^{\prime 3}-3^{3})/3^{6}
,
and the isomorphism
\iota_{d^{\prime}}:E_{a_{1}^{\prime},a_{3}^{\prime}}\to E_{d^{\prime}}^{\mathrm{Hess}}
.

Then, the isomorphism

\iota_{d^{\prime}}^{-1}\circ\iota\circ\iota_{d}:E_{a_{1},a_{3}}\to E_{a_{1}^{\prime},a_{3}^{\prime}}

maps
(0:0:1)
to
(0:0:1)
.
From the well-known fact about isomorphisms of elliptic curves in Weierstrass form
(see Proposition III.3.1 in
[
28
]
),
there exists
u\in\mathbb{C}^{\times}
such that

a_{1}^{\prime}=ua_{1}
,
a_{3}^{\prime}=u^{3}a_{3}
,
and the isomorphism is given by
(X:Y:Z)\mapsto(u^{2}X:u^{3}Y:Z)
.
In particular, we have
a_{1}^{3}/a_{3}=a_{1}^{\prime 3}/a_{3}^{\prime}
,
thus
d^{\prime 3}=d^{3}
.
Therefore, we have
d^{\prime}=\omega^{i}d
for some
i\in\{0,1,2\}
.
From this, we obtain
u=\omega^{i}
.
This means that

\iota_{d^{\prime}}^{-1}\circ\iota\circ\iota_{d}
is given by

(X:Y:Z)\mapsto(\omega^{2i}X:Y:Z).

An easy calculation using the definitions of
\iota_{d}
and
\iota_{d^{\prime}}

shows that

\iota
is given by the desired form.
∎

Using these lemmas,
we obtain the following proposition.

Proposition 5.7
.

The map
\mathsf{Hess}
defined above
is well-defined
and gives a computable invariant of level
\Gamma(3)
.

Proof.

The well-definedness of
\mathsf{Hess}
follows from
Lemmas
5.5
and
5.6
.
In addition,
\mathsf{Hess}
is computable
from the expressions of
d
in Lemma
5.5
.

The
j
-invariant of
E_{d}^{\mathsf{Hess}}
is given by

j(E_{d}^{\mathsf{Hess}})=\frac{d^{3}(d^{3}+6^{3})^{3}}{(d^{3}-3^{3})^{3}}

(see
[
13
, §2]
).
It is easy to see that this satisfies the required conditions.
∎

To see the invariant
\mathsf{Hess}
has a good model,
we use the
t
-coordinate arithmetic on Hessian curves,
which is introduced in
[
25
]
.
In particular,
we define the
t
-coordinate as follows:

t_{d}:E_{d}^{\mathsf{Hess}}(\mathbb{C})\to\mathbb{C}\cup\{\infty\};\ (X:Y:Z)\mapsto\begin{cases}XY/Z^{2}&\text{if }Z\neq 0,\\
\infty&\text{if }Z=0.\end{cases}

In the following,
we show that
\{((E_{d}^{\mathsf{Hess}},(P_{d}^{\mathsf{Hess}},Q_{d}^{\mathsf{Hess}})),t_{d})\}

is a good model of
\mathsf{Hess}
.
To do this,
we give scalar multiplication formulas,
division polynomials, and isogeny formulas
for the
t
-coordinate on Hessian curves.

We use the following theorem on scalar multiplication formulas
for the
t
-coordinate on Hessian curves,
which is given in
[
25
]
.

Theorem 5.8
.

We define
h(t)=-4t^{3}+d^{2}t^{2}-2dt+1
,

P_{1}(t)=P_{2}(t)=1
,

V_{1}(t)=t
, and
V_{2}(t)=t^{4}-dt^{2}+2t
in
\mathbb{Z}[d][t]
.
For a positive integer
k
,
we define polynomials inductively by

\displaystyle P_{2k+1}(t)

\displaystyle=\frac{-th(t)P_{2k}(t)^{2}-V_{2k}(t)^{2}}{P_{2k-1}(t)},

\displaystyle V_{2k+1}(t)

\displaystyle=\frac{-th(t)^{2}P_{2k}(t)^{4}+t^{2}V_{2k}(t)^{2}+(-1+dt)h(t)P_{2k}(t)^{2}V_{2k}(t)}{V_{2k-1}(t)},

\displaystyle P_{2k+2}(t)

\displaystyle=\frac{tP_{2k+1}(t)^{2}-V_{2k+1}}{-h(t)P_{2k}(t)},

\displaystyle V_{2k+2}(t)

\displaystyle=\frac{tP_{2k+1}(t)^{4}+t^{2}V_{2k+1}(t)^{2}-(-1+dt)P_{2k+1}(t)^{2}V_{2k+1}(t)}{V_{2k}(t)}.

Then, the following holds:

(1)

P_{m}(t),V_{m}(t)\in\mathbb{Q}[d][t]

for any positive integer
m
,

(2)

for any
P\in E_{d}^{\mathsf{Hess}}(\mathbb{C})
such that
t_{d}(P)\neq\infty
,
we have

t_{d}([m]P)=\begin{cases}\frac{V_{m}(t_{d}(P))}{P_{m}(t_{d}(P))^{2}}&\text{if }m\text{ is odd},\\
-\frac{V_{m}(t_{d}(P))}{h(t_{d}(P))P_{m}(t_{d}(P))^{2}}&\text{if }m\text{ is even},\end{cases}

(3)

if
m
is odd then
V_{m}(t)
and
P_{m}(t)
do not have common roots,

(4)

if
m
is even then
V_{m}(t)
and
h(t)P_{m}(t)
do not have common roots,

(5)

V_{m}(t)
is monic for any positive integer
m
,

(6)

the leading coefficient of
P_{m}(t)
in
t
is
m
if
m
is odd,
and
m/2
if
m
is even.

Proof.

The properties (
1
)–(
4
)
follow from Theorem 1 in
[
25
]
.
The properties (
5
)–(
6
)
follow from Proposition 1 in
[
25
]
.
∎

This theorem gives us the scalar multiplication formulas
for the
t
-coordinate on Hessian curves.
For division polynomials,
we define the
m
-th division polynomial for the
t
-coordinate on Hessian curves
as
P_{m}(t)
if
m
is odd,
and
-h(t)P_{m}(t)
if
m
is even.
Theorem
5.8

proves the properties required for division polynomials
except for that they have coefficients in
\mathbb{Z}[d]
.
To show this,
we use the following lemma.

Lemma 5.9
.

For any positive integer
k
,
we have

\displaystyle tV_{2k+1}

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}V_{k}^{2}V_{k+1}^{2}-hP_{k}^{2}P_{k+1}^{2}(-hP_{k}^{2}V_{k+1}+P_{k+1}^{2}V_{k}-dV_{k}V_{k+1})\text{ if $k$ is even,}\\
V_{k}^{2}V_{k+1}^{2}-hP_{k}^{2}P_{k+1}^{2}(P_{k}^{2}V_{k+1}-hP_{k+1}^{2}V_{k}-dV_{k}V_{k+1})\text{ if $k$ is odd,}\end{cases}

\displaystyle P_{2k+1}^{2}

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}(hP_{k}^{2}V_{k+1}+P_{k+1}^{2}V_{k})^{2}\text{ if $k$ is even,}\\
(P_{k}^{2}V_{k+1}+hP_{k+1}^{2}V_{k})^{2}\text{ if $k$ is odd,}\end{cases}

\displaystyle V_{2k}

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}V_{k}(V_{k}^{3}-dh^{2}P_{k}^{4}V_{k}-2h^{3}P_{k}^{6})\text{ if $k$ is even,}\\
V_{k}(V_{k}^{3}-dP_{k}^{4}V_{k}+2P_{k}^{6})\text{ if $k$ is odd,}\end{cases}

\displaystyle-hP_{2k}^{2}

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}-4hV_{k}^{3}-d^{2}h^{2}P_{k}^{4}V_{k}-2dh^{3}V_{k}^{2}P_{k}^{6}-h^{4}P_{k}^{8}\text{ if $k$ is even,}\\
4V_{k}^{3}+d^{2}P_{k}^{4}V_{k}+2dV_{k}^{2}P_{k}^{6}-P_{k}^{8}\text{ if $k$ is odd.}\end{cases}

Here, we omit the variable
t
from the polynomials for simplicity.

Proof.

We can derive differential addition and doubling formulas
for the
t
-coordinate from the addition formulas in
[
13
, §2.1]
.
From the differential addition formula and
(
2
) in Theorem
5.8
,
if
k
is even, we have

\frac{tV_{2k+1}}{P_{2k+1}^{2}}=\frac{V_{k}^{2}V_{k+1}^{2}-hP_{k}^{2}P_{k+1}^{2}(-hP_{k}^{2}V_{k+1}+P_{k+1}^{2}V_{k}-dV_{k}V_{k+1})}{(hP_{k}^{2}V_{k+1}+hP_{k+1}^{2}V_{k})^{2}}.

By taking
P=(0:-1:1)
in (
2
) in Theorem
5.8
,
it follows that
V_{k}(0)=0
if and only if
k\not\equiv 0\pmod{3}
,
and
P_{k}(0)=0
if and only if
k\equiv 0\pmod{3}
.
Therefore, the numerator of the right-hand side is divisible by
t
.
Since
V_{2k+1}
and
P_{2k+1}
are coprime from
(
3
) and (
4
) in Theorem
5.8
,
we obtain the first two equalities for even
k

by comparing the degrees and the leading coefficients.

The other equalities can be shown similarly by using
the doubling formula for the
t
-coordinate.
∎

Using this lemma,
we can show that
P_{m}(t)\in\mathbb{Z}[d][t]
for any positive integer
m

by induction on
m
.

Next,
we give isogeny formulas for the
t
-coordinate on Hessian curves.
Theorem 4 in
[
24
]
gives a formula of isogenies of degree
prime to
3
on Hessian curves,
which uses
(X:Y:Z)
-coordinates.
The following lemma is a restatement of this theorem for
our
t
-coordinate.

Lemma 5.10
.

Let
d\in\operatorname{Im}\mathsf{Hess}
and
m
be a positive integer
prime to
3
.
Let
K
be a point on
E_{d}^{\mathrm{Hess}}
of order
m
,
and we define
d^{\prime}\in\mathbb{C}
by

d^{\prime}=c_{1}\left(\left(2\left\lceil\frac{m}{2}\right\rceil-3\right)d-6\sum_{i=1}^{\left\lceil\frac{m}{2}\right\rceil-1}\frac{1}{t_{d}([i]K)}+6c_{2}\right)\left(\prod_{i=1}^{\left\lceil\frac{m}{2}\right\rceil-1}t_{d}([i]K)\right),

where

\displaystyle c_{1}=1\text{ and }c_{2}=0\text{ if }m\text{ is odd},

\displaystyle c_{1}=c_{2}=-2t_{d}\left(\left[\frac{m}{2}\right]K\right)^{2}+\frac{d^{2}}{2}t_{d}\left(\left[\frac{m}{2}\right]K\right)-\frac{d}{2}\text{ if }m\text{ is even}.

Then, the map defined by

R\mapsto\left(\prod_{i=0}^{m-1}X(R+[i]K):\prod_{i=0}^{m-1}Y(R+[i]K):\prod_{i=0}^{m-1}Z(R+[i]K)\right)

is an isogeny from
E_{d}^{\mathrm{Hess}}
to
E_{d^{\prime}}^{\mathrm{Hess}}

with kernel generated by
K
.

Proof.

Note that the specified point on a Hessian curve in
[
24
]

is
(0:-1:1)
, different from our definition.
Therefore, we need to compose the isomorphism
defined by
(X:Y:Z)\mapsto(Z:Y:X)

to their isogeny formula.

Let
(X_{i},Y_{i},Z_{i})\in\mathbb{C}^{3}
be a representative of
[i]K
for
i=1,\dots,m-1
.
Then,
Theorem 4 in
[
24
]
shows that the lemma holds for

d^{\prime}=\left((3-2m)d+6\sum_{i=1}^{m-1}\frac{X_{i}^{2}}{Y_{i}Z_{i}}\right)\left(\prod_{i=1}^{m-1}\frac{X_{i}}{Z_{i}}\right).

Therefore, it suffices to show that
this is equal to the expression in the lemma.

Since
-(X:Y:Z)=(Y:X:Z)
on Hessian curves,
we have

\frac{X_{i}^{2}}{Y_{i}Z_{i}}+\frac{X_{m-i}^{2}}{Y_{m-i}Z_{m-i}}=\frac{X_{i}^{3}+Y_{i}^{3}}{X_{i}Y_{i}Z_{i}}=d-\frac{1}{t_{d}([i]K)}.

If
m
is even, we have

\frac{X_{m/2}^{2}}{Y_{m/2}Z_{m/2}}=\frac{X_{m/2}}{Z_{m/2}}=\frac{d}{2}-\frac{1}{2t_{d}\left(\left[\frac{m}{2}\right]K\right)}.

Since
h(t_{d}([\frac{m}{2}]K))=0
, we have
-1/(2t_{d}([\frac{m}{2}]K))
equals
to
c_{1},c_{2}
for even
m
.
By combining these equations,
we obtain the desired expression of
d^{\prime}
.
∎

An easy calculation using the following formulas
shows that this isogeny preserves the level structure:

\displaystyle(X:Y:Z)+P^{\mathsf{Hess}}_{d}

\displaystyle=(\omega^{2}X:\omega Y:Z),

\displaystyle(X:Y:Z)+Q^{\mathsf{Hess}}_{d}

\displaystyle=(Z:X:Y).

By the above results,
we obtain the following proposition.

Proposition 5.11
.

The set of pairs

\{((E_{d}^{\mathsf{Hess}},(P_{d}^{\mathsf{Hess}},Q_{d}^{\mathsf{Hess}})),t_{d})\}

is a good model of
\mathsf{Hess}
.

From the theorems in Section
3.1
,
we obtain the following corollary.

Corollary 5.12
.

For any positive integer
m
prime to
3
,
there exists the modular polynomial
\Phi_{m}^{\mathsf{Hess}}(X,Y)\in\mathbb{Z}[X,Y]

of order
m
for
\mathsf{Hess}
.
In addition,
if
m>1
, then
\Phi_{m}^{\mathsf{Hess}}(X,Y)
is symmetric.

As in the case of Montgomery curves,
we can show some congruence properties of the coefficients
of our modular polynomials for Hessian curves.

Proposition 5.13
.

Let
m>1
be an integer prime to
3
.
The coefficient of
X^{i}Y^{j}
of
\Phi_{m}^{\mathsf{Hess}}(X,Y)
is zero
unless
i+j\equiv\psi(m)\pmod{3}
if
m\equiv 1\pmod{3}
,
and unless
i-j\equiv 0\pmod{3}
if
m\equiv 2\pmod{3}
.

Proof.

Let
d\in\operatorname{Im}\mathsf{Hess}
.
Then, the isomorphism
\iota_{d}:E_{d}^{\mathsf{Hess}}\to E_{\omega d}^{\mathsf{Hess}}

defined by
(X:Y:Z)\mapsto(X:Y:\omega^{-1}Z)

sends
(P_{d}^{\mathsf{Hess}},Q_{d}^{\mathsf{Hess}})
to
(P_{\omega d}^{\mathsf{Hess}},P_{\omega d}^{\mathsf{Hess}}+Q_{\omega d}^{\mathsf{Hess}})
.

A similar argument to the proof for Proposition
5.4

using this isomorphism
shows that

\Phi_{m}^{\mathsf{Hess}}(X,Y)=\begin{cases}\omega^{-\psi(m)}\Phi_{m}^{\mathsf{Hess}}(\omega X,\omega Y)&\text{if }m\equiv 1\pmod{3},\\
\Phi_{m}^{\mathsf{Hess}}(\omega X,\omega^{-1}Y)&\text{if }m\equiv 2\pmod{3}.\end{cases}

This implies the desired result.
∎
