USP13-Mediated selective autophagy of eIF5B promotes pathological cardiac hypertrophy
Authors: Yutong Ye, Juan Xu, Chenyang Jin, XinTao Li, Xiaoyu Wu, Changzuan Zhou, Longzhe Gao, Zikan Zhong, Xudong Li, Songwen Chen, Yong Wei, Shi Peng, Shaowen Liu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPathological cardiac hypertrophy and heart failure pose significant global health challenges. Maintaining cardiomyocyte homeostasis is crucial for slowing disease progression. Our findings reveal that Ubiquitin-specific peptidase 13 (USP13), a key component of the Protein Quality Control (PQC) system and a deubiquitinase highly expressed in cardiac tissue, is significantly upregulated in both human and experimental models of pathological cardiac hypertrophy and heart failure. Comprehensive in vivo and in vitro studies demonstrate that USP13 overexpression accelerates the progression of pathological cardiac hypertrophy and heart failure, whereas genetic silencing of USP13 in vitro mitigates phenylephrine (PE)-induced cardiomyocyte hypertrophy. Mechanistically, as shown by immunoprecipitation-mass spectrometry (IP-MS), co-immunoprecipitation (co-IP), and immunofluorescence analysis, USP13 promotes the interaction between eukaryotic translation initiation factor 5B (eIF5B) and the autophagy receptor SQSTM1/P62, directing eIF5B to selective autophagic degradation. This process leads to eIF5B depletion, which exacerbates pathological cardiac hypertrophy and heart failure while counteracting the protective effects of USP13 silencing. This study is the first to elucidate the pivotal role of the USP13-eIF5B axis in pathological cardiac hypertrophy and heart failure, shedding light on the molecular mechanisms and identifying a promising therapeutic target in human end-stage heart failure.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00018-025-05980-7.Keywords: Deubiquitinating enzyme, Proteostasis, Transverse aortic constriction (TAC), Endoplasmic reticulum stress, JNK signaling

Introduction

In recent decades, the escalating morbidity and mortality rates of heart failure (HF), driven by population aging, have posed a formidable global public health challenge [1, 2]. Pathological cardiac hypertrophy, triggered by hypertension, ischemia, and other pathological stimuli, functions as both a compensatory response and a significant risk factor for heart failure. Initially, the heart responds to heightened hemodynamic stress through hypertrophic adaptation, a process known as adaptive remodeling. However, once the compensatory capacity is surpassed, the transition to decompensated heart failure significantly elevates the risk of sudden cardiac death and severely compromises patients’ quality of life [3, 4]. Currently, drug therapies for myocardial hypertrophy merely delay disease progression [5, 6], while the mechanisms underlying the transition to decompensated heart failure remain poorly understood. This highlights the urgent need for more in-depth and comprehensive research to develop novel treatment strategies.

Given the restricted regenerative capacity of cardiomyocytes, maintaining intracellular homeostasis is critical [7, 8]. The Protein Quality Control System (PQC) plays a pivotal role in preserving cardiomyocyte protein homeostasis. The PQC ensures intracellular protein homeostasis by facilitating proper protein folding, assembly, localization, and degradation [9]. Impaired PQC function can lead to protein misfolding and aggregation, endoplasmic reticulum stress, oxidative stress, and disruptions in cellular signaling and metabolism. This sustained stress may ultimately trigger apoptosis [10, 11]. Investigating PQC imbalances in pathological myocardial hypertrophy holds promise for uncovering novel insights into the mechanisms underlying the progression from myocardial hypertrophy to decompensated heart failure.

Within the intricate framework of the PQC system, deubiquitinating enzymes (DUBs) regulate protein stability and subcellular localization by removing ubiquitin chains from specific substrates, thereby modulating critical cellular processes. Growing evidence underscores the involvement of DUBs in the pathogenesis of pathological cardiac hypertrophy [12, 13]. Analysis of the GEO dataset (GSE133054) reveals that multiple DUB genes are dysregulated in human pathological cardiac hypertrophy. Among these, USP13 is distinguished by its marked cardiac enrichment, exhibiting expression levels at least fourfold higher than in other organs [14]. This tissue specificity corresponds to USP13’s established roles in autophagy [15], immune regulation [16], and DNA damage repair [17]—pathways critically involved in cardiac pathophysiology. Although USP13 has been strongly implicated in the progression of various cancers [18, 19], its role in cardiac disease remains largely unexplored.

We found that USP13 is upregulated in pathological cardiac hypertrophy and heart failure, and accelerates pressure-overload-induced myocardial hypertrophy and heart failure. Mechanistically, USP13 enhances the binding of eIF5B to the autophagy receptor SQSTM1/P62, thereby promoting eIF5B degradation via selective autophagy and impairing cardiomyocyte function. This study is the first to elucidate the roles of the cardiac-enriched deubiquitinase USP13 and eIF5B in pathological cardiac hypertrophy, providing a foundation for developing new therapeutic strategies.

Methods

Mouse transverse aortic constriction (TAC) model and overexpression of the USP13 gene in vivo

C57BL/6J mice were sourced from the Animal Experimental Center of Shanghai General Hospital, affiliated with Shanghai Jiaotong University. All in vivo experimental protocols were approved by the institutional Animal Care and Use Committee and complied with the European Parliament’s Directive 2010/63/EU for the protection of animals used in scientific research.

The recombinant adeno-associated virus (rAAV) serotype 9 overexpression and control vectors, in which USP13 expression is driven by the MYH6 promoter, were obtained from OBioO Technology (Shanghai). Expression efficiency was evaluated in mice two weeks after injection of the viral vector at a dose of 1 × 10^12 vg per mouse. Thereafter, 24 male C57BL/6J mice, aged 6–7 weeks and weighing 19–21 g, were randomly divided into four groups: sham + rAAV9-GFP (n = 5), TAC + rAAV9-GFP (n = 7), sham + rAAV9-USP13 (n = 5), and TAC + rAAV9-USP13 (n = 7). Viral injections were administered via the tail vein, followed by TAC or sham surgery two weeks later.

Two weeks post-injection, mice (now weighing 22–24 g) were anesthetized with 1% pentobarbital and underwent aortic arch constriction. The procedure included a midline incision of the neck and chest skin, blunt dissection of the fascia and muscle tissue, partial exposure of the thoracic cavity by cutting the proximal sternum, and isolation of the aortic arch. The aortic arch was ligated between the innominate and left carotid arteries using a 6−0 nylon suture. After triply ligating around a blunt needle, the needle was promptly removed, and successful constriction was confirmed by the filling of the innominate artery. The sham-operated group underwent the same thoracotomy without aortic arch ligation.

Cell culture

Neonatal rat ventricular myocytes (NRVMs) and neonatal rat cardiac fibroblasts (NRCFs) were isolated from 1−3-day-old Sprague-Dawley rats via enzyme digestion. Hearts were minced and incubated in PBS containing 0.25% trypsin-EDTA (Thermo Fisher, 25200056) at 4 °C overnight, followed by digestion in 1 mg/ml collagenase II (Worthington Biochemical, LS004174) and 5 mg/ml bovine serum albumin (BSA, Sigma-Aldrich, V900933) the next day. Cells were harvested by low-speed centrifugation, resuspended in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM, Gibco, 11995065) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, AusGeneX, FBS500-S) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin (Gibco), and plated in Petri dishes at 37 °C and 5% CO2. After 2 h, non-adherent myocardial cells were collected, supplemented with BrdU (0.1 mM, MCE, HY−15910) to inhibit fibroblast proliferation, and cultured for 24 h. The medium was then changed to DMEM containing 2% FBS and 0.1 mM BrdU, and NRVMs were treated with 10 µM phenylephrine (Sigma-Aldrich, P6126) to induce hypertrophy, with or without prior 24-hour transfection with adenovirus or siRNA.

NIH−3T3 and HEK−293T cells were obtained from HyCyte (China) and maintained in DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% penicillin/streptomycin, under standard culture conditions (37 °C, 5% CO2). The culture medium was refreshed every 2–3 days, and cells were passaged when confluence reached 80–90%. All experiments were conducted using cells within 20 passages to ensure cell line integrity and experimental reproducibility.

Adenovirus infection, siRNA, and plasmid transfection

Overexpression adenovirus vectors (ADV-USP13) and control vectors (ADV-GFP) were provided by OBioO Technology (Shanghai). SiRNA targeting siUSP13 and siEIF5B in rats was obtained from Ribobio Co.Ltd. (Shanghai, China). The target sequence for siUSP13 is TGACTACTCACCATTAGAT, and the target sequence for siEIF5B is CATTGTCACTCA GATTCGA. Overexpression plasmids HA-USP13, Flag-EIF5B, Flag-Sqstm1/p62, and His-Ub were purchased from QEgene (Shanghai, China).

NRVMs were infected with ADV-USP13 at a multiplicity of infection (MOI) of 20. For siRNA transfection, NRVMs were transfected using Lipofectamine RNAiMAX (Invitrogen, 13778100) according to the manufacturer’s instructions; the siRNA was used at a concentration of 7.5 nM. NIH−3T3 and HEK−293T cells were transfected with plasmids using Lipofectamine 3000 (Invitrogen, L3000015), following the provided protocol.

Echocardiography

Echocardiograms were conducted using a Vevo 3100 system (Visual Sonics, Toronto, Canada) equipped with the MS400 probe, 4 weeks after transverse aortic constriction (TAC) or sham surgery. Under isoflurane anesthesia, mice were maintained at a heart rate of 450–550 bpm during testing. In M-mode, the short-axis view of the left ventricle was acquired, and the average left ventricular shortening fraction (LVFS), ejection fraction (LVEF), end-systolic inner diameter (LVESD), and end-diastolic inner diameter (LVEDD) were measured over at least three cardiac cycles. All measurements and analyses were performed by investigators blinded to the experimental groups.

Histological analysis

Hearts were harvested, washed in normal saline, weighed, photographed, fixed in 10% formalin, embedded in paraffin, and sectioned into 5-µm-thick slices for histopathological evaluation. Sections from each group (5–6 per group) were stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E), Masson’s trichrome, Sirius Red, and wheat germ agglutinin (WGA) following standard protocols. Imaging of four to six random fields per slice was performed by investigators blinded to the experimental groups. ImageJ software was used for quantitative analysis of tissue fibrosis and cardiomyocyte cross-sectional area.

Immunofluorescence

For α-actin staining, cardiomyocytes cultured in confocal dishes were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min, permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X-100 in PBS for 10 min, and blocked with 5% bovine serum albumin (BSA) in PBS for 1 h. Cells were then incubated with anti-α-actin (1:300, Sigma-Aldrich, A7811) overnight at 4℃. On the following day, cells were incubated with a fluorescent secondary antibody at room temperature for 1.5 h and stained with DAPI for 15 min. Imaging was performed using a Leica TCS-SP8 SR confocal microscope, with random field selection conducted in a blinded manner. Obtained images were quantitatively analyzed using ImageJ software.

Multiplex immunohistochemistry (mIHC)

The mIHC kit (AiFang Biological, Hunan, China) was used according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The optimal antibody dilutions and staining sequence were determined through preliminary experiments. Primary antibodies used were USP13 (1:1500, Proteintech, 16840−1-AP), eIF5B (1:2000, Proteintech, 13527−1-AP), SQSTM1/P62 (1:1000, Abclonal, A19700). Fluorophore-conjugated tyramide was applied for signal amplification to enable multiplexing. A negative control was included to ensure the elution of antibodies from the previous round. Imaging was conducted using a Leica TCS-SP8 SR confocal microscope.

Co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP) and immunoprecipitation-mass spectrometry (IP-MS)

Cells were lysed in mild lysis buffer (Beyotime, P0013) containing protease and phosphatase inhibitors (NCM Biotechnology, P002) for 0.5–1 h on ice. The supernatant was collected by centrifugation at 12,000 rpm for 10 min at 4 °C. Lysates were incubated with anti-Flag (Sigma-Aldrich, A2220) or anti-HA (Thermo Scientific Pierce, 26181) agarose beads/magnetic beads (MCE, HY-K0202) at 4 °C for 12–16 h to precipitate target proteins. The beads were washed with pre-cooled buffer at least six times the next day and then denatured and eluted with 1× SDS loading buffer for subsequent mass spectrometry analysis or immunoblotting experiments.

For IP-MS, proteins were separated by SDS-PAGE and stained with Coomassie brilliant blue. Gel fragments were excised and prepared for mass spectrometry analysis. Liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS) was performed by Applied Protein Technology (Shanghai, China).

Real-time quantitative polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR)

Total RNA was extracted from samples using Trizol reagent (TAKARA, 9109) and quantified with a NanoDrop ND−1000 spectrophotometer. The RNA was then reverse transcribed into cDNA using the PrimeScript RT Reagent Kit (TAKARA, RR036A) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Real-time fluorescence quantitative PCR was performed using SYBR mix (TAKARA, RR420), with α-actin serving as the endogenous reference. The primer sequences are provided in Supplement Table S1.

Western blot

To extract total cell proteins, cells were lysed on ice for 30 min using RIPA lysis buffer (Beyotime, P0013B) supplemented with protease and phosphatase inhibitors. The lysates were then centrifuged at 12,000 g for 10 min, and the supernatant was collected. Protein concentrations were determined using the BCA assay kit (Beyotime, P0009) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. For immunoblotting, 20 µg of total protein was separated by SDS-PAGE and transferred onto a polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membrane. The membrane was blocked with 5% skim milk or 5% BSA prepared in TBST (Tris-buffered saline with Tween−20) at room temperature for 1 h or at 4 °C overnight. After incubation with primary antibodies (as specified in Supplement Table S2) at 4 °C overnight, the membrane was incubated with an HRP-conjugated secondary antibody at room temperature for 1 h. Following washes with TBST, enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) detection was performed using ECL chemiluminescent substrate (WB012, ShareBio, Shanghai, China) and a Tanon 4600 system. The band intensities were quantitatively analyzed using ImageJ software.

RNA sequencing and microarray data analysis

For RNA sequencing, total RNA was extracted from NRVMs transfected with siEIF5B or control siNC using Trizol reagent. Libraries were constructed and sequenced by Novogene (Shanghai) on the Illumina Hiseq platform. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified using DESeq2, with a significance threshold of P-adjust < 0.05 and | log2FC | >1.5. GO and KEGG enrichment analyses were conducted using the clusterProfiler R package. Results were visualized using https://www.bioinformatics.com.cn/static/others/daohang.html or GraphPad Prism 8.0.

Microarray data (GSE133054) were obtained from the GEO database. After principal component analysis to exclude samples with high intra-group variability, DEGs between groups (pathological cardiac hypertrophy vs. Normal, HF vs. Normal) were identified using DESeq2. Genes encoding ubiquitination enzymes were specifically screened, with those having a P-adjusted value < 0.05 and | log2FC | >1.5 highlighted. These results were also visualized using GraphPad Prism 8.0.

Materials

Cycloheximide (HY−12320), Rapamycin (HY−10219), Chloroquine (HY−17589 A), and MG−132 (HY−13259) were obtained from Med Chem Express (China).

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using GraphPad Prism 8.0 (San Diego, CA, USA) and presented as the mean ± standard deviation. All data collected from our experiments is consistent with the normal distribution. Unpaired two-tailed Student’s t-tests were used for two-group comparisons, and one-way ANOVA/two-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s post-hoc test/Bonferroni correction was employed for multiple-group comparisons. Significance was set at P < 0.05.

Result

USP13 is specifically highly expressed in cardiomyocytes and upregulated in human heart failure and cardiac hypertrophy mice models

Analysis of differential gene expression in human cardiac hypertrophy and heart failure samples from the GEO database (GSE133054) revealed several ubiquitination enzyme-encoding genes (P adjust < 0.05) with significant mRNA changes during compensatory hypertrophy and/or heart failure (EF% < 25%) (Fig. 1A). Notably, ubiquitin-specific peptidase 13 (USP13) was significantly upregulated during heart failure (Fig. 1A and Figure S1B). USP13 is a deubiquitinating enzyme-encoding gene highly enriched in cardiac tissue, with mRNA levels at least four times higher than in any other tissue [14]. Furthermore, single-cell sequencing data demonstrated that USP13 is predominantly expressed in cardiomyocytes in the human heart (Figure S1C). Immunoblot analysis of mouse tissues confirmed high USP13 expression in the heart (Fig. 1B), suggesting its potential role in regulating cardiovascular diseases.

We then conducted a series of experiments. First, tissue immunofluorescence revealed that USP13 fluorescence in the mouse left ventricle colocalized with α-actin-labeled cardiomyocytes but not with vimentin-labeled non-cardiomyocytes, and USP13 fluorescence intensity was significantly higher in the TAC group compared to the sham group (Fig. 1C).

Then, real-time qPCR showed significant increases in USP13 and hypertrophy marker genes Anp and Bnp after TAC (Fig. 1H-J). Protein samples from mouse ventricular tissue at various time points post-TAC revealed time-dependent upregulation of Myh7 and Anp, as well as USP13, particularly during heart failure (EF% < 30% at 8 weeks post-TAC) (Fig. 1D-G).

Finally, we isolated neonatal rat ventricular myocytes (NRVMs) and cardiac fibroblasts (NRCFs) and induced cardiomyocyte hypertrophy in vitro with phenylephrine (PE). In NRVMs treated with PE, mRNA levels of Anp, Bnp, and USP13 increased (Fig. 1K-M), and protein levels of Myh7 and USP13 gradually rose with prolonged PE treatment (Fig. 1N-O). Furthermore, USP13 expression was significantly higher in NRVMs than in NRCFs, as shown by immunoblot analysis (Fig. 1P).

Collectively, these findings suggest that USP13 plays a critical role in the regulation of pressure-overload-induced cardiac hypertrophy and heart failure.

USP13 overexpression in mice promotes transition from compensated cardiac hypertrophy to heart failure

To further elucidate the role of USP13 upregulation in the development of pathological cardiac hypertrophy, we utilized serotype 9 recombinant adeno-associated virus (rAAV9) containing the Myh6 promoter to specifically overexpress USP13 in murine cardiomyocytes by tail vein injection. As shown in Fig. 2B, USP13 protein levels in rAAV9-USP13-injected mice were twice as high as those in controls two weeks after injection. Both groups then underwent TAC and were sacrificed 4 weeks post-TAC.

Four weeks after TAC, rAAV9-USP13 mice showed more severe cardiac hypertrophy than the rAAV−9-GFP-injected control group, as witnessed by significantly increased heart weight-to-body weight (HW/BW) and heart weight-to-tibia length (HW/TL) ratios (Fig. 2C). Correspondingly, levels of myocardial hypertrophy marker proteins (Anp, Myh7, Rcan1.4) were elevated in rAAV9-USP13 mice post-TAC (Fig. 2F and H-K), and mRNA levels of hypertrophy markers (Anp, Bnp, Myh7) were upregulated compared to controls (Fig. 2D). Additionally, we assessed cardiac remodeling and fibrosis. Masson’s trichrome and Sirius red staining revealed greater collagen deposition in the myocardial interstitium and blood vessels of rAAV9-USP13 mice (Fig. 3C, D, F). mRNA levels of fibrosis-related genes (Col1a1, Col3a1, TGF-β) were upregulated compared to controls (Fig. 3E).

Overall, USP13 overexpression in mice exacerbated pressure-overload-induced myocardial hypertrophy and cardiac remodeling, promoting the transition from hypertrophy to decompensated heart failure.

USP13 regulates phenylephrine-induced in vitro myocardial hypertrophy in neonatal rat ventricular cardiomyocytes

We then tested the regulatory effect of USP13 on the hypertrophy model of neonatal rat ventricular cardiomyocytes (NRVMs) induced by phenylephrine (PE). Adenoviral-mediated USP13 overexpression exacerbated in vitro cardiomyocyte hypertrophy, characterized by increased cell surface area (Fig. 4A-B), elevated mRNA levels of hypertrophy marker genes (Anp, Bnp, Myh7) (Fig. 4C), and increased protein levels of Myh7 and Rcan1.4 (Fig. 4D-G).

Conversely, silencing USP13 expression with small interfering RNA (siRNA) mitigated cardiomyocyte hypertrophy. Compared to the control siRNA (siNC), siUSP13 transfection reduced the cell surface area of NRVMs following PE treatment (Fig. 4H-I) and reversed the PE-induced increases in mRNA levels of Anp, Bnp, and Myh7 (Fig. 4J), as well as protein levels of Myh7 and Rcan1.4 (Fig. 4K-N). Potential off-target effects were ruled out by verifying NRVMs purity and using second siRNA targeting a different USP13 sequence (Figure S4).

Through in vitro experiments, we further confirmed the promotive effect of USP13 on pathological cardiomyocyte hypertrophy and demonstrated that silencing USP13 in vitro can alleviate cardiomyocyte hypertrophy.

USP13 interacts with eIF5B and negatively regulates its post-transcriptional expression

To elucidate the mechanism, we performed immunoprecipitation coupled with mass spectrometry to identify proteins interacting with USP13. We immunoprecipitated USP13 from myocardial cell lysates overexpressing USP13 and analyzed the precipitated protein peptides by mass spectrometry, identifying 107 candidate USP13 substrates (Fig. 5A). Enrichment analysis in DisGeNET and Gene Ontology databases revealed significant associations of these proteins with pathological cardiac hypertrophy, dilated cardiomyopathy, heart failure, and sudden death, and their involvement in processes such as oxidative stress response, translation, proteolysis, and myocardial contraction, further confirming USP13’s key role in pathological cardiac hypertrophy (Figure S2A-B).

Among these candidates, eukaryotic translation initiation factor 5B (eIF5B) showed the highest specificity and abundance (Fig. 5B). Co-immunoprecipitation and immunoblot analysis confirmed the interaction between exogenous USP13 and endogenous eIF5B in NRVMs, with similar observations in the HEK−293T cell line (Fig. 5C, E). Immunofluorescence further demonstrated that endogenous USP13 and eIF5B co-localized in the cytoplasm of NRVMs (Pearson correlation coefficient = 0.9, indicating a high degree of spatial co-localization) (Fig. 5D-G).

To further investigate the regulatory relationship between USP13 and eIF5B, we conducted experiments at both mRNA and protein levels. RT-qPCR and immunoblotting showed that silencing USP13 in NRVMs did not affect eIF5B mRNA levels but increased its protein expression (Fig. 5H-I). Conversely, overexpression of USP13 in NRVMs and mice decreased eIF5B protein levels and did not affect its mRNA levels, indicating that USP13 negatively regulates eIF5B expression post-transcriptionally (Fig. 5J-M).

USP13 accelerates eIF5B degradation by promoting eIF5B-SQSTM1/P62 interaction

Based on USP13’s structural and functional features, we investigated its role in eIF5B protein stability. In NRVMs, elevated USP13 levels correlated with reduced eIF5B levels (Figure S3A). To assess eIF5B degradation, cycloheximide (CHX) was used to inhibit new protein synthesis in NIH−3T3 cells. We observed time-dependent degradation of endogenous eIF5B, with substantial depletion evident at 24 h (Figure S3B). Overexpressing USP13 accelerated eIF5B degradation compared to controls (Fig. 6A-B), suggesting USP13 promotes eIF5B proteolysis.

Considering the major intracellular protein degradation pathways—ubiquitin-proteasome system, autophagy-lysosome pathway, and ER-associated degradation—and prior evidence linking USP13 to autophagy regulation, we examined the relationship between eIF5B stability and autophagy. In NRVMs, rapamycin-induced autophagy (marked by increased LC3BII/I conversion and decreased SQSTM1/P62 levels) coincided with reduced eIF5B levels (Fig. 6E-H). While silencing USP13 protein expression inhibited the rapamycin-induced decrease in eIF5B levels (Fig. 6I-J), demonstrating USP13-dependent autophagic degradation of eIF5B. Correspondingly, in the NIH−3T3 cell line, autophagy activation was shown to increase the degradation rate of eIF5B, as confirmed by Western blot (Fig. 6L-N).

To clarify whether USP13’s regulation of eIF5B relies on global autophagy changes, we conducted gain- and loss-of-function studies in NRVMs. Silencing USP13 did not affect baseline autophagy markers (Figure S3C), its overexpression enhanced LC3BII/I conversion slightly (Fig. 6D). While in TAC model, USP13 upregulation was accompanied by suppressed global autophagy (Figure S3D), indicating that USP13 regulates eIF5B independently of bulk autophagy flux. Nevertheless, it has been reported that USP13 stabilizes the autophagy receptor protein SQSTM1/P62 via deubiquitination [20], aligning with our co-immunoprecipitation data showing USP13-SQSTM1/P62 interaction in NRVMs (Fig. 6C). Given SQSTM1/P62’s role in selective autophagy [21], we hypothesized USP13 enhances eIF5B degradation via SQSTM1/P62-mediated pathways.

We then tested this using immunoprecipitation in HEK−293T and NIH−3T3 cells. Overexpression of USP13 enhanced the interaction between eIF5B and SQSTM1/P62 (Fig. 6K and O). Ubiquitination pull-down assays further revealed that eIF5B exhibits basal ubiquitination, which remained unaltered upon USP13 overexpression (Fig. 6K). Pathway-specific inhibition experiments revealed that chloroquine (an autophagy-lysosome inhibitor) blocked eIF5B degradation, whereas MG132 (a proteasome inhibitor) was significantly weaker than chloroquine (Fig. 6P–Q), underscoring the primary role of the autophagy-lysosomal pathway in eIF5B turnover. Additionally, immunofluorescence analysis in NRVMs demonstrated spatial co-localization of USP13, eIF5B, and SQSTM1/P62 (Fig. 6R). Consistent with this, treatment with chloroquine led to eIF5B accumulation within autophagosomes (Fig. 6S), providing further evidence for USP13-driven, SQSTM1/P62-mediated selective autophagy in eIF5B degradation.

Insufficient eIF5B promotes the development of myocardial hypertrophy and counteracts the protective effect of silent USP13 on pathological cardiac hypertrophy

After TAC, immunoblotting analysis of murine ventricular tissues at 4 and 8 weeks revealed an inverse correlation between USP13 upregulation and eIF5B downregulation during cardiac hypertrophy and heart failure progression (Fig. 7A-B). To investigate the functional impact of eIF5B deficiency, we knocked down eIF5B in NRVMs using siRNA (Fig. 7C), reducing its protein level by 50% and mimicking the expression pattern observed in post-TAC heart tissue. RT-qPCR showed significant upregulation of hypertrophy markers: Anp (2.5 ± 0.24-fold, p < 0.001), Bnp (1.83 ± 0.22-fold, p < 0.05), and Myh7 (1.49 ± 0.05-fold, p < 0.05) (Fig. 7D). Whole-transcriptome sequencing identified 1,089 DEGs (Padj < 0.05) (Fig. 7F). Notably, genes associated with endoplasmic reticulum (ER) stress (e.g., Atf4, Hspa5, Xbp1) and inflammation (e.g., Tnfrsf1a, Cx3cl1, Ets1) were significantly upregulated, concurrent with dysregulated expression of hypertrophic markers (Nppa) and extracellular matrix remodeling factors (Smad4, Hdac2). Moreover, genes encoding key components of the cardiac contractile apparatus, such as those in the KCN and CACNA families, were substantially downregulated (Fig. 7G-H). GO and KEGG enrichment analyses revealed upregulation of the inflammatory response, MAPK signaling cascade, and Erk1/2 signaling cascade, as well as downregulation of membrane potential regulation and myocardial contraction (Fig. 7I-K). Subsequent rescue experiments validated these results. Immunofluorescence quantification showed that the cardioprotective effects of USP13 silencing were partially abolished by eIF5B knockdown, with an increase in cardiomyocyte surface area (Fig. 7L-M). Immunoblotting demonstrated that USP13 suppression attenuated PE-induced JNK and Erk1/2 pathway activation, an effect that was abrogated by eIF5B knockdown (Fig. 7N, O-Q). Consistent with this, the reductions in hypertrophy marker proteins induced by USP13 silencing were mitigated upon eIF5B knockdown (Fig. 7N, R-S).

Discussion

This study is the first to elucidate the crucial role of USP13 in pathological cardiac hypertrophy and its underlying mechanisms. We demonstrate that USP13 exhibits cardiac-specific expression, with myocardial levels at least 4-fold higher than in other tissues, and is significantly upregulated during hypertrophy and heart failure (HF) in both human and animal hearts. Through analyses of animal experiments, we confirmed that USP13 overexpression drives pathological myocardial remodeling, while its genetic silencing ameliorates PE-induced cardiomyocyte hypertrophy in vitro. Mechanistically, USP13 facilitates the interaction between eIF5B and SQSTM1/P62, promoting selective autophagic degradation of eIF5B via the autophagy-lysosomal pathway. This eIF5B depletion triggers ER-stress, pro-inflammatory signaling, and MAPK pathway activation, collectively exacerbating cardiac dysfunction and counteracting the protective effects of USP13 inhibition in animal cardiomyocytes (Fig. 8).

While deubiquitinating enzymes (DUBs) are increasingly recognized as molecular switches regulating cardiac remodeling, the cardiac-enriched DUB USP13 remains understudied. This study not only clarifies the expression characteristics of USP13 in pathological cardiac hypertrophy and its promoting effect but also identifies its unique expression pattern and significance in the heart. The higher expression of USP13 in heart tissue suggests a heart-specific interacting protein network, confirmed by enrichment analysis of the USP13 interacting protein network, which is significantly related to pathological cardiac hypertrophy, dilated cardiomyopathy, HF, and sudden death. Additionally, conserved USP13 upregulation across species during HF highlights its therapeutic relevance in late-stage disease transitions, making USP13 inhibitors promising candidates for arresting the progression from compensated hypertrophy to end-stage failure.

Another key finding is the downregulation of eIF5B in hypertrophic and failing hearts (Fig. 7A-B). Although eIF5B is known as a translation initiation factor that delivers initiator methionyl-tRNA to the ribosome and facilitates IRES-mediated translation under stress [22, 23], its role in cardiac pathology is unclear. Our data suggest that eIF5B deficiency may promotes pathological cardiac hypertrophy through the following mechanistic cascade: First, eIF5B loss attenuates the translational repression of ATF4 mRNA [24, 25] (Fig. 7G), elevating ATF4 protein—a master regulator of the integrated stress response (ISR). This likely induces ER stress, as supported by upregulation of HSPA5/GRP78 (Fig. 7G). Upregulated XBP1 mRNA (Fig. 7G) suggests that subsequent IRE1–XBP1 activation may amplify ER stress signals, though causality requires validation. Second, ER stress activates the pro-apoptotic JNK pathway, likely contributing to increased JNK phosphorylation in eIF5B-deficient hearts (Fig. 7N–Q). Third, both ATF4 and activated JNK serve as potent transcriptional inducers of proinflammatory mediators. This links eIF5B deficiency to inflammation, a well-established driver of cardiac fibrosis and dysfunction. Our RNA-seq results strongly support this, showing a significantly enhanced inflammatory response (Fig. 7G, I). Furthermore, transcriptomic analysis revealed the downregulation of key ion channel genes (e.g., KCN/CACNA families; Fig. 7H), implicating impaired excitation–contraction coupling. While this may stem from global cellular stress, a direct role of eIF5B in regulating ion channel transcription cannot be excluded. In summary, we propose that eIF5B deficiency initiates a pathogenic cascade involving ATF4-mediated ER stress and JNK-induced inflammation, ultimately leading to contractile dysfunction. However, future studies are essential to rigorously delineate the causality of these pathways and exclude potential compensatory mechanisms.

Our data identify eIF5B as the principal effector through which USP13 drives pathological cardiac hypertrophy and HF. eIF5B was identified as the central node in the USP13 interactome via immunoprecipitation-mass spectrometry, with colocalization analysis (Pearson’s R = 0.91) demonstrating a robust interaction. Functional coherence further strengthens this link: USP13’s interaction network governs oxidative stress, proteostasis, and contractility pathways, all of which eIF5B modulates through ER stress, inflammation, and autophagy-lysosomal crosstalk. Notably, eIF5B knockdown counteracts the protective effects of USP13 inhibition in vitro, underscoring its critical role in this regulatory pathway. We have considered whether USP13 affects the process of pathological cardiac hypertrophy and HF by regulating autophagy, as previous studies have shown that USP13 can promote the enhancement of autophagy through various mechanisms [26–28]. However, in this study, the protein level of USP13 was upregulated at 4 weeks and 8 weeks after TAC in mice, while the overall autophagy flux level decreased. The research of Zhang et al. [29], Sun et al. [30] and Li et al. [31] also demonstrated that autophagy flux was impaired in cardiac hypertrophy models induced by isoproterenol (ISO), angiotensin II and TAC in vivo and in vitro, suggesting that the promotion of USP13 on pathological cardiac hypertrophy and HF did not depend on bulk autophagic activity. While our focus was on eIF5B-mediated mechanisms, the substrate diversity of USP13 (e.g., Bcl-xL, ZHX2, TAK1 ubiquitination [32–34]) is acknowledged. The absence of these interactions in our study likely reflects tissue-dependent interactome variability or technical limitations, highlighting the need for further exploration of USP13’s multifaceted roles in cardiovascular pathophysiology.

We found that USP13 mediates eIF5B degradation primarily via selective autophagy. This conclusion is supported by multiple lines of evidence autophagy activator enhanced eIF5B degradation while inhibitors stabilized it (Fig. 6L-N, P-Q); eIF5B colocalized with autophagosomes (Fig. 6S); and USP13 enhanced the interaction between eIF5B and SQSTM1/P62 (Fig. 6K, O). The regulatory mechanism of USP13 on eIF5B is dual. First, USP13 binds to eIF5B and enhances its interaction with SQSTM1/P62, promoting autophagy-lysosomal degradation of eIF5B. Second, previous studies show the functions of USP13 in SQSTM1/P62–USP13 stabilizes SQSTM1/P62 through depubiquitination, thus promoting the oligomerization of SQSTM1/P62 [20], augmenting SQSTM1/P62’s substrate recruitment. As a selective autophagy receptor (SAR), SQSTM1/P62 targets ubiquitinated substrates for autophagy-mediated degradation [35], a process termed selective autophagy, while the ubiquitinated form of eIF5B (Fig. 6K) makes it possible to be recognized by SQSTM1/P62. Despite macro-autophagy inhibition post-TAC, the selective degradation of eIF5B persisted. This is not contradictory. On one hand, macro-autophagy is typically activated under conditions of nutrient deficiency and abnormal energy metabolism, non-selectively degrading intracellular components to provide amino acids and energy for cells to maintain energy balance and survival [36]. Selective autophagy is mediated by SARs, and the mobility of SARs is key to the degradation of selective autophagy cargo [37]. It recognizes cargo marked by ubiquitin and delivers them to autophagy for degradation [38]. Even if macro-autophagy is not activated or is inhibited, selective autophagy can still be effectively executed. On the other hand, the recognition mechanism of selective autophagy for specific cargo is accomplished by different SAR proteins [35], enabling flexible regulation of the degradation of various substrates. In this study, USP13, acting as a bridge between eIF5B and SQSTM1/P62, is upregulated in pathological cardiac hypertrophy and HF, and can promote the recognition and degradation of eIF5B by the selective autophagy system even when macro-autophagy is inhibited. Although selective autophagy is the dominant pathway, we cannot exclude contributions from other degradation systems under different conditions. For instance, the proteasome may participate in eIF5B turnover under basal conditions, and crosstalk among degradation pathways may occur under certain stress conditions.

We should note that that the deficiency of eIF5B may not solely result from USP13-mediated degradation. Importantly, eIF5B mRNA levels remained stable upon USP13 modulation (Fig. 5H, J, L), excluding transcriptional suppression or mRNA instability as major factors. Nevertheless, we cannot discount possible involvement of non-coding RNAs or epigenetic regulators—such as miR−5787, which represses eIF5 in fibroblasts to inhibit proliferation [39]. Moreover, while our data confirm autophagy as the dominant degradation route for eIF5B, other post-translational modifications (PTMs) could fine-tune eIF5B activity or stability. This notion is supported by evidence that homologous factors such as eIF2α undergo regulatory phosphorylation [40], suggesting that similar mechanisms may influence eIF5B. Although current evidence does not directly implicate such PTMs, they may serve as co-regulators of eIF5B function. In summary, selective autophagy represents the most straightforward mechanism consistent with our results. Future work should explore its interplay with transcriptional, translational, and post-translational regulatory networks in human heart failure.

Although USP13 silencing attenuates cardiomyocyte hypertrophy by inhibiting eIF5B degradation, compensatory DUB pathways may be involved. Experimental evidence from our work shows that USP13-silenced cardiomyocytes showed no baseline abnormalities (Fig. 4H-N), and eIF5B knockdown did not fully reverse protection (Fig. 7L-S), suggesting that compensatory mechanisms may help maintain cellular homeostasis. For instance, cardioprotective DUBs such as USP25 [12] and OTUD1 [13] can ameliorate cardiac hypertrophy by targeting distinct downstream factors. Additionally, other DUBs with overlapping substrate specificity, such as USP19—which also modulates autophagy degradation [41]—might potentially compensate for the loss of USP13. Future studies should systematically map the DUB regulatory network in stressed cardiomyocytes to identify such compensatory pathways.

Our study reveals a non-canonical mechanism for USP13. Unlike canonical DUBs that stabilize their substrates, USP13 exhibits a distinct mechanism: it facilitates the formation of a complex between eIF5B and SQSTM1/p62, thereby driving eIF5B degradation via selective autophagy (Fig. 6). This “substrate degradation-promoting” function is novel among DUBs in the cardiac context. Functionally, USP13-mediated eIF5B depletion initiates a pathological cascade that activates ATF4-mediated ER stress and JNK-induced inflammation (Fig. 7), ultimately accelerating the transition from compensatory hypertrophy to heart failure. This positions USP13 as a unique integrator of three fundamental processes: autophagic degradation, translational control, and stress signaling. This contrasts with other DUBs that typically modulate specific factors in pathways like calcium handling or fibrosis, thereby establishing USP13’s non-redundant role in cardiac pathophysiology. Notably, the cardio-enriched expression profile of USP13 offers a significant translational advantage, as targeted inhibitors could achieve myocardial-specific effects while minimizing off-tissue toxicity, creating a strategic therapeutic window absent in broad-spectrum DUB modulators.

Targeting the USP13-eIF5B axis represents a novel therapeutic strategy for pathological myocardial hypertrophy and decompensated heart failure. Targeted protein degradation is an emerging treatment approach, with DUBs inhibitors development showing promise [42]. Identified USP13 inhibitors exhibit therapeutic potential [43], although their clinical application remains pending. Completing the structural characterization of USP13 is crucial for inhibitor design. Given USP13’s diverse functions, intervention timing and dosing require careful consideration, necessitating further studies to clarify its dynamic roles. This study suggests that eIF5B supplementation may provide myocardial protection in pathological hypertrophy models, while eIF5B deficiency might reverse the effects of USP13 overactivation, warranting further experimental validation.

This study has some limitations. First, due to resource and time constraints, and the lack of a high-specificity USP13 inhibitor, it was not feasible to conduct USP13 silencing or inhibition experiments in animals, and the protective effect of forced eIF5B expression on pathological cardiac hypertrophy in animals was not verified. Second, although we demonstrated USP13’s promoting effect on pathological cardiac hypertrophy in rodents and discussed the mechanism in depth, whether these results can be extrapolated to human pathological cardiac hypertrophy requires additional human samples for verification. Finally, the discussion on the relationship between eIF5B and pathological cardiac hypertrophy remains at the level of phenotypic correlation, and further elucidation of eIF5B’s specific regulatory mechanisms in pathological cardiac hypertrophy may identify new therapeutic targets .

Mechanistic diagram of the key findings in this study: USP13 enhances the interaction between eIF5B and SQSTM1/P62, thereby promoting SQSTM1/P62-mediated selective autophagic degradation of eIF5B. The resulting eIF5B depletion initiates transcriptome reprogramming in cardiomyocytes, leading to heightened inflammatory responses, exacerbated endoplasmic reticulum (ER) stress, and reduced myocardial contractility, collectively driving cardiac hypertrophy and heart failure progression

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study demonstrates that USP13 exacerbates pathological cardiac hypertrophy and heart failure by promoting eIF5B degradation, providing novel insights and potential therapeutic targets. We anticipate that future research will comprehensively investigate these mechanisms and translate the findings into clinically applicable therapeutic strategies.
