Correlation between aging, periodontal diagnoses and systemic diseases: a retrospective analysis of 3,000 patients
Authors: Sukran Acipinar, Merve Yalcin
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveAging is a complex biological process driven by interconnected mechanisms such as inflammaging and immunosenescence. It serves as a significant risk factor for numerous chronic conditions. With increasing life expectancy, understanding the interplay between aging, systemic inflammation, and periodontitis has gained renewed research interest. This study aims to identify medical profiles that may pose a risk in periodontology and to explore their relationship with periodontal status, age and gender.Materials and methodsThe retrospective study analyzed medical and dental records of 3000 patients who presented to the Department of Periodontology at Sivas Cumhuriyet University. Standard anamnesis forms were examined to collect demographic data, systemic diseases, allergy and infection status, medication use, smoking habits, oral hygiene, periodontal complaints, and diagnoses. Associations among age, periodontal diagnosis, and medical status were statistically evaluated.ResultsThe analysis revealed a high prevalence of both systemic and periodontal diseases, with a notable increase in frequency with age. Systemic diseases were significantly associated with a progression from periodontal health to periodontitis. The mean patient age was 36.3 ± 14.9 years. 69.9% of participants had no systemic diseases. Additionally, 25.8% reported regular medication use. Periodontal complaints were present in 65.5% of the subjects.ConclusionTo enhance oral and systemic health outcomes in the aging population, it is crucial for dentists to conduct detailed anamnesis and tailor personalized treatment protocols based on individual risk factors. Integrated collaboration between medical doctors and dental professionals is essential. Patients should also be educated about the bidirectional link between systemic and periodontal inflammation, especially in older adults.Keywords: Dental records, Prevalence, Aging, Periodontal diseases

Introduction

With global life expectancy on the rise, the proportion of the older population is steadily increasing. Projections indicate that by 2050, approximately 16% of the world’s population will be over the age of 65 [1]. Aging is a well-established risk factor for the development of numerous chronic conditions [2]. Notably, over 70% of individuals aged 65 and older are affected by two or more chronic diseases [3]. Chronic diseases contribute to a significant proportion of global mortality and presents a growing public health challenge [4].

‘’Old age itself is a disease” and aging is governed by complex biological processes, including chronic systemic inflammation and immune system decline. Inflammation has been recognized as a critical mediator in the onset and progression of age-related diseases [5]. The term inflammaging describes the persistent low-grade inflammation that develops with advancing age and contributes to the pathogenesis of age-related diseases [6]. This pro-inflammatory state accelerates biological aging and, in turn, exacerbates inflammatory responses, forming a self-perpetuating cycle. Closely linked to this process is immunosenescence, the progressive deterioration of immune function with age, which compromises host defense and increases susceptibility to infections and chronic diseases [7].

Successful aging has been associated with reduced disease burden [8] and in the context of oral health, it is often defined as retaining more than 20 natural teeth by age 80 [9]. Recent data indicate that periodontitis affects approximately 46% of U.S. adults, with both prevalence and severity rising in older populations [10, 11].

As the number of older individuals seeking dental care continues to grow, the intersection of systemic diseases and oral health has garnered increased attention. Periodontal tissue health is intricately linked not only to local oral factors but also to systemic health. Bidirectional relationships have been documented between periodontal conditions and systemic diseases such as cardiovascular disease and diabetes, suggesting that periodontal inflammation may exacerbate systemic conditions and vice versa [12, 13]. In this context, studies report that 49.4% to 82.5% of patients with periodontal disease have concurrent systemic diseases that may influence dental treatment outcomes [12, 13].

Understanding common systemic conditions, medication use, and periodontal diagnoses in older adults can inform more effective and individualized treatment protocols. A comprehensive medical and dental history, along with a thorough risk analysis, is essential for identifying potential complications and guiding personalized periodontal therapy.

Despite growing awareness, the tripartite relationship between aging, systemic inflammation, and periodontal disease remains insufficiently explored. Investigating these connections may provide critical insights and guide multidisciplinary approaches to management. By advancing early diagnosis and tailored interventions, research in this area has the potential to mitigate the impact of aging on both systemic and periodontal health.

Given these considerations, the aim of the present study is to examine the medical profiles of patients presenting to the Department of Periodontology at Sivas Cumhuriyet University, and to assess the correlations among systemic health status, periodontal diagnosis, age, and gender. A secondary aim is to promote awareness among patients and healthcare professionals about the importance of integrated care and personalized treatment strategies in managing oral health among the aging population.

Materials and methods

This retrospective study was conducted in accordance with the principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki and received approval from the Ethics Committee of Sivas Cumhuriyet University (Approval No: 24.07.2024/24_07_01). Due to the retrospective nature of the study, the clinical trial number was not provided. A total of 3,000 patients (1701 females and 1299 males), aged 18 years and older and classified as ASA physical status Class I–III, were included. These patients applied to the Department of Periodontology at the Faculty of Dentistry, Sivas Cumhuriyet University, between September 1, 2023 and June 30, 2024 and had medical and periodontal records.

Medical histories were obtained from all participants through structured verbal interviews using standardized anamnesis forms. These forms included a wide range of systemic conditions, such as cardiovascular (CVS) diseases, diabetes mellitus (DM), thyroid dysfunction, malignancy (current or past), psychiatric disorders, gastrointestinal (GIS) conditions, pulmonary diseases, rheumatic disorders, hematological diseases, infectious diseases, neurological conditions, osteoporosis, drug allergies, and routine medication use. Conditions not explicitly categorized—such as urological, hepatic, ophthalmological, dermatological disorders, pregnancy—were documented under the category “other diseases.” Limitations of a physical or psychological nature, which may have an impact on oral hygiene practices (e.g. paralysis, severe Parkinson’s disease, severe psychiatric disorders, autism, Down syndrome, mental retardation) and records with missing information were excluded based on an evaluation of the data in the anamnesis forms (Fig. 1).

In addition to systemic health data, periodontal diagnoses were classified along a spectrum ranging from periodontal health to stage (S) 4, grade (G) C periodontitis (P) (S4GCP) in accordance with the 2017 World Workshop on the Classification of Periodontal and Peri-Implant Diseases and Conditions [14, 15]. Forms containing clinical periodontal measurements (plaque index (PI), gingival index (GI), pocket depth (PD), clinical attachment level (CAL), bleeding on probing (BOP)) performed with a standard periodontal probe and panoramic radiographic evaluation records taken with the same device (Instrumentarium, Orthopantomograph OP200 D, KaVo Dental, Germany) were included in the study.

Periodontal clinical measurements

The periodontal status of all participants was assessed using the following clinical parameters:

Patients included in this retrospective analysis were those who applied to the routine clinic, many of whom were initially evaluated by multiple examiners. However, only cases whose periodontal diagnoses were later reviewed and confirmed by two periodontologists (S.A. and M.Y.) based on the clinical measurements in the registration form and panoramic radiographs were included in the study. Calibration was deemed to have ensured consistency and was considered to be acceptable, with a kappa κ value greater than 0.80 for repeat periodontal diagnosis, both intra- and inter-examiner. The study also recorded demographic and behavioral variables, including age, gender, smoking status, oral hygiene habits (tooth brushing and interdental flossing frequency), and periodontal complaints (e.g., bad breath, gingival bleeding, tooth mobility, bruxism, temporomandibular joint disorders (TMJD), sensitivity, gingival recession or hyperplasia, and food impaction). The aim of the study was to investigate associations among demographic factors, systemic medical conditions, and periodontal diagnoses, thereby contributing to a deeper understanding of the systemic-periodontal interface.

Statistical analysis

All data were analyzed using SPSS software version 23. Patient ages were grouped into four categories: 18–25, 26–40, 41–60, and 61 years or older. Descriptive statistics, including means, standard deviations, frequencies, and percentages, were calculated for the variables related to systemic conditions and those under the “other diseases” category. Since the data were distributed non-normally according to the Kolmogorov Smirnov test, non-parametric tests were used in the evaluations according to gender, age and oral hygiene habits.The associations between systemic diseases and both gender and age groups were assessed using the chi-square test. A p-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant for all analyses.

Results

A total of 3,000 patients (1,701 females and 1,299 males), aged between 18 and 97 years, were included in the study. The mean age was 36.3 ± 14.9 years, with the age distribution as follows: 18–25 years (n = 930), 26–40 years (n = 921), 41–60 years (n = 840), and ≥ 61 years (n = 309). Information pertaining to the daily tooth brushing and interdental floss use of the participants is presented in Table 1. Among the participants, 69.9% did not present with any systemic disease, while 30.1% had at least one systemic condition that could potentially impact dental care. Moreover, 11.5% of individuals were found to have more than one systemic disease. The prevalence of systemic diseases increased significantly with age (p < 0.05), and the frequency was significantly higher among females than males (p = 0.00).

In terms of medication use, 25.8% of participants reported taking at least one regular medication, while the remaining 74.2% were not on any ongoing pharmacological treatment. Regarding periodontal diagnosis, dental plaque-induced gingivitis (DIG) was the most frequently observed condition, diagnosed in 51.7% of patients, followed by gingivitis on a reduced periodontium (GRP) in 30.7%. Other diagnoses included stage 3, grade C periodontitis (S3GCP, 3.1%), S3GBP (2.8%), S2GBP (2.6%), S2GAP (2.0%), S1GAP (1.3%), S2GCP (1.3%), S3GAP (1.1%), and S4GCP (1.0%). Periodontal health was observed in 0.8% of patients. Other less common diagnoses included S1GBP (0.8%), S4GBP (0.5%), S1GCP (0.2%), and S4GAP (0.1%).

In relation to smoking habits, 69.6% of participants were non-smokers, 13.7% smoked fewer than 10 cigarettes per day, and 16.7% smoked 10 or more cigarettes daily. When stratified by gender, 79.9% of women were non-smokers, compared to 56.1% of men. 43.9% of men and 20.1% of women are smokers. In contrast, heavy smoking (≥ 10 cigarettes/day) was reported by 25.8% of males, compared to 9.7% of females. No statistically significant association was observed between smoking and periodontal or systemic disease. However, there was a significant negative correlation between age and smoking frequency (p = 0.000, r = − 0.077) Additionally, it was noted that periodontal diagnosis exhibited a worsening trend with age (p = 0.00) (Table 6).

Complaint-specific findings by diagnosis revealed that bleeding was the most commonly reported symptom across nearly all categories, including periodontal health (H), DIG, GRG, and all stages of periodontitis. Sensitivity was frequently reported in S1GCP, while bad breath and food impaction were most prominent in S4GAP. In S4GBP, the most frequent complaints were gingival recession and bleeding. Sensitivity was also notably common among patients diagnosed with GRP.

Among patients with systemic diseases, GRP was the most frequently observed periodontal diagnosis, particularly among those with hypertension, diabetes, cardiovascular diseases, thyroid disorders, and other systemic conditions. GRP was also prevalent among patients with pulmonary, rheumatic, gastrointestinal, psychological, infectious, oncological, and osteoporotic conditions. DIG was more common among patients with hematological, neurological and allergic conditions, as well as those with GIS issues. Additionally, the use of rheumatologic agents and hematological medications was more frequently associated with DIG, whereas other drug classes were more commonly seen in patients diagnosed with GRP.

As the condition progresses from periodontal health to severe forms of periodontitis, periodontal diagnosis has been found to be correlated with all systemic diseases except GIS, infectious, neurological and blood diseases, osteoporosis and allergies (p < 0.05). Furthermore, it was observed that as age increased, there was a corresponding correlation with all systemic diseases except blood diseases and allergies (Tables 7 and 8).

Discussion

The etiology of periodontal diseases, which rank as the sixth most prevalent chronic condition globally is multifactorial, with host susceptibility, immunoinflammatory responses, and both environmental and hereditary risk factors playing critical roles in disease development [19, 20]. Although aging itself does not directly cause periodontitis, it significantly impacts the host immune system through processes such as inflammaging and immunosenescence. These age-related mechanisms delay tissue healing, reduce regenerative capacity, and disrupt several key processes related to tissue maturation, including cellular activity, apoptosis, and collagen turnover [21, 22]. Additionally, aging is associated with hormonal changes, increased levels of osteoclastogenesis-stimulating cytokines, diminished osteogenic differentiation, and heightened susceptibility to inflammation and alveolar bone loss [21, 23]. As a result, maintaining oral health in older adults has become a priority for international organizations such as the International Dental Federation and the World Health Organization (WHO).

Advancements in healthcare have contributed to an increased prevalence of non-communicable systemic diseases [24]. These systemic conditions have been recognized as risk factors for the development and progression of periodontal diseases [25]. The 2017 World Workshop reported that various systemic diseases and conditions can affect periodontal tissues and should therefore be included in the classification of periodontal disease. The workshop identified systemic diseases that can affect the course of periodontitis, such as rare diseases like Papillon le fèvre, common diseases/conditions such as diabetes that increase the prevalence and severity of periodontitis, and conditions that affect periodontal supporting tissues independently of inflammation caused by dental plaque biofilm, such as squamous cell carcinoma, which is mostly rare [26]. Systemic diseases and conditions that can affect periodontal supporting tissues have been listed in broad categories by Albandar et al. [27].

There is a strong association between advancing age and a higher prevalence of systemic disorders in individuals with periodontal disease [13]. Moreover, systemic diseases, along with their treatments and associated pharmacological interventions, can exacerbate the risk of periodontal disease and influence the efficacy of periodontal therapies [28]. Periodontal diseases have been shown to negatively impact overall health and quality of life [13]. High-sensitivity C-reactive protein, a marker used in the current periodontal disease classification, serves as a valuable biomarker in assessing the interaction between periodontal and systemic diseases. This bidirectional relationship is largely driven by shared lifestyle-related risk factors such as smoking, stress, aging, low socioeconomic status and chronic inflammation [25, 29]. The localized inflammatory response initiated by periodontal disease can activate systemic immune responses, promoting widespread inflammation and microbial dysbiosis, which in turn may contribute to systemic conditions [29, 30]. Additionally, the presence of a dysbiotic microbiome and elevated levels of proinflammatory cytokines within periodontal tissues suggests these tissues may serve as reservoirs that amplify the systemic inflammatory burden [31, 32].

It is well-established that the severity of periodontal disease increases with advancing age and is more commonly observed in males [11, 19]. Consistent with this, our study found that the severity of periodontitis significantly increased with age and was statistically more prevalent in men (p = 0.000). These findings align with previous research indicating that male sex and older age are strongly associated with the prevalence, severity, and progression of periodontal disease [4, 33]. The prevalence of systemic disease in our sample was determined to be 30.1%. Studies have shown a wide variation, with reported prevalence rates ranging from 11.7% [34] to 48.27% [35]. These discrepancies underscore the importance of considering several influencing factors, including sample size, patients’ self-awareness and reporting of medical conditions, and variations in demographic characteristics across populations.

In the present study, systemic diseases were found to be more prevalent among women—a finding that is consistent with numerous reports in the existing literature [24, 33]. This higher prevalence may be attributed to women’s heightened sensitivity to inflammation, increased participation in routine medical check-ups and oral health practices, and the maintenance of more comprehensive medical records. It is also important to note that the proportion of female participants in our study sample was relatively high.

Our findings also indicate that systemic diseases were more frequently observed among older age groups. This trend is consistent with previous studies reporting an age-related increase in the prevalence of systemic conditions [4, 33]. Furthermore, the current study identified a strong correlation between periodontal diagnosis and the presence of systemic diseases, particularly as periodontal conditions progress to more severe forms of periodontitis. These observations support earlier findings that the relationship between periodontal and systemic diseases becomes more pronounced with advancing age [13]. Additionally; CVS, endocrine, neurolocal, and psychological diseases were significantly more prevalent in the 41–60 age group in our cohort. However, it should be given the relatively small number of participants over the age of 61 in our sample.

Smoking is recognized as one of the most significant risk factors for both periodontal and systemic diseases. Although tobacco use was once classified as a habit, it is now recognized as nicotine addiction and a chronic, recurrent medical condition (International Classification of Diseases, Tenth Revision) [26]. The components of cigarette smoke have adverse effects on periodontal tissues and systemic health, such as triggering microvascular vasoconstriction and fibrosis. The study examining the relationship between clinically defined periodontitis indices and various systemic conditions found significant associations between systemic diseases, male gender, and smoking status [36]. It is well known that smoking has destructive effects on periodontal supporting tissues and increases the risk of periodontitis [26]. Smoking is associated with increased PI, GI, PD and CAL in periodontal status [36, 37]. Similarly, in a retrospective analysis of 2069 records in 2023, smoking was clearly associated with accelerated progression of periodontitis [33]. For all these reasons, in the current periodontal disease classification system, smoking status—particularly the daily number of cigarettes consumed—is one of the factors used to adjust disease grading. In the present study, the overall prevalence of smoking was 30.4%, however, our findings did not reveal a statistically significant association between smoking and either periodontal or systemic diseases. This discrepancy may be explained by the possibility that many participants who had smoked previously had ceased smoking due to health complications, thereby obscuring the long-term impact of smoking on disease prevalence in the current sample.

Studies investigating the distribution of systemic diseases consistently indicate that CVS diseases represent the most prevalent category, with hypertension being the most frequently observed condition within this group [24, 28, 34]. Our study also identified CVS diseases as the most common systemic condition, with a prevalence of 12.7%, and hypertension emerged as the leading subgroup at 8.6%. A comprehensive global analysis indicated that the number of adults aged 30–79 years with hypertension increased from 650 million to 1.28 billion over the past three decades [38]. Given that CVS diseases are the leading cause of mortality worldwide and exhibit a bidirectional relationship with periodontal diseases, it is imperative for periodontologists to develop and implement well-structured treatment plans tailored to this patient population [39]. Furthermore, the use of antihypertensive medications necessitates careful clinical consideration due to potential side effects such as gingival hyperplasia and lichenoid reactions. Medications that inhibit platelet aggregation or alter coagulation processes also require special attention, as they may influence bleeding parameters and necessitate adjustments in dental treatment protocols.

In the present study, DM emerged as the second most prevalent systemic condition, with a frequency of 7.1%. The bidirectional relationship between DM and periodontitis is well-established in the literature [40]. Periodontitis has been shown to contribute to the etiopathogenesis of diabetes by disrupting glucose metabolism and promoting insulin resistance through chronic inflammatory processes [41, 42]. The accumulation of advanced glycation end products (AGEs) in individuals with DM, along with their interaction with the receptor for AGEs, microvascular and macrovascular complications, altered immune responses, shifts in subgingival microbiota, oxidative stress, hyperlipidemia, and pro-inflammatory cytokine activity, all play a role in exacerbating periodontal conditions [41, 42]. Current periodontal disease classification systems incorporate HbA1c values as a modifying factor in grading, emphasizing the clinical importance of DM management. Periodontitis is known as the 6th complication of DM and periodontal therapy can lead to significant reductions in HbA1c levels and its effectiveness is comparable to some antidiabetic drugs [43].

GIS system pathologies were observed in 2.2% of participants in this study. Reported prevalence rates vary across the literature. These conditions are thought to be influenced by lifestyle factors such as dietary habits and stress. In general, GIS diseases do not necessitate major alterations in dental treatment plans; however, it remains essential to consider the pharmacological interactions of prescribed medications. Importantly, Helicobacter pylori has been identified in supragingival dental biofilms, even though it is not typically considered part of the oral microbiota. Dental plaque and calculus may serve as reservoirs for this microorganism [44]. Increasing awareness regarding the potential role of periodontal treatment in improving GIS health and minimizing disease recurrence is warranted.

Allergic conditions were reported by 3.5% of study participants. Consistent with existing research, penicillin allergy was the most commonly reported, accounting for 59.6% of cases. The prevalence of drug allergies varies among different populations. Previous a study has indicated that allergies are more frequently reported among women and individuals with mild periodontal bone loss compared to those with more severe forms of the disease [45]. In our study, in accordance with the results, 76% of allergy sufferers were female, and no significant correlation was found between allergy status and the severity of periodontitis. In clinical dental practice, it is crucial to verify patient-reported allergies and to assess the active ingredients in both prescribed and administered medications. Appropriate precautions should be taken to mitigate the risk of anaphylaxis, including having emergency interventions readily available when treating at-risk individuals.

Infectious diseases were detected in 1.0% of the patients. This underscores the importance of conducting thorough anamnesis to identify infectious diseases and to support the prevention of cross-infection. In a 6-year retrospective study conducted in the Turkish population, HBsAg seroprevalence was reported as 2.97% [46]. The underreporting observed in this study may be attributed to patients’ lack of awareness or concerns about stigmatization by healthcare professionals. Dentists, as essential public health practitioners, are well-positioned to raise awareness and ensure early detection of infectious conditions, thus playing a vital role in safeguarding community health.

Dental treatment protocols should be individualized, especially for patients undergoing cancer therapy, as chemotherapy and radiotherapy can necessitate prophylactic measures, promote bacterial colonization, and induce xerostomia, thereby worsening periodontal disease. In cases involving bone metastasis, bisphosphonates may be prescribed. In this study, individuals with a history of cancer represented 0.9% of the sample. Periodontal disease is considered a modifiable risk factor for certain malignancies. Bacteremia associated with periodontitis may contribute to neoplastic progression, and including periodontal care in cancer treatment regimens has been shown to lower inflammatory cytokine levels [47, 48]. A retrospective cohort study revealed that the cumulative incidence of cancer in the periodontitis group was 2.2 times higher than in those without periodontitis, after appropriate adjustments [49]. These findings suggest that maintaining periodontal health may serve as an effective component of cancer prevention strategies.

Periodontitis and osteoporosis represent major public health concerns. In this study, osteoporosis was observed in only 0.4% of the participants; however, its presence was not associated with increased severity of periodontal disease. Inflammation serves as the principal link between the two conditions. Common risk factors may mediate this association by promoting oxidative stress and impairing host immune responses [50]. Penoni et al. identified osteoporosis as a potential risk factor for periodontal disease, hypothesising that patients at risk of fractures are more likely to develop severe periodontitis and experience tooth loss [51]. However, the 2017 World Workshop reported that there is conflicting evidence regarding the relationship between osteoporosis and periodontitis [26]. Furthermore, bisphosphonate therapy for osteoporosis is associated with a significantly elevated risk of jaw osteonecrosis when compared to non-users [52]. Recognizing the interconnection between these two diseases may guide the development of integrated therapeutic strategies for systemic and localized bone loss. Routine dental examinations and radiographic screenings may offer a cost-effective approach for early detection of reduced bone mineral density and fracture risk. It is advisable to refer patients for dental evaluation before initiating bisphosphonate therapy, and to defer invasive dental procedures in such patients, following consultation with an endocrinologist [53].

The present study identified rheumatological diseases in 2.8% of participants. Although periodontal and rheumatic diseases have distinct etiologies, they share overlapping pathogenic mechanisms—particularly regarding alterations in immune response. Periodontal disease has been shown to influence the production of specific antibodies, which may contribute to the development of rheumatic conditions [54]. Moreover, existing literature suggests a bidirectional relationship between the two, with the risk of periodontitis being 4.7 times higher among individuals diagnosed with rheumatoid arthritis [55]. In our study, rheumatological diseases were found to be associated with the severity of periodontal disease. However, inconsistent findings exist in the literature, with some reports indicating that arthritis is more common among individuals with mild bone loss compared to those with more advanced loss [45]. Furthermore, it should be noted that immunosuppressive therapies may be utilized in the management of rheumatological diseases.

The bidirectional association between thyroid dysfunction and periodontal disease is similarly rooted in systemic inflammation [56]. Alterations in thyroid hormone levels may impact both periodontal tissue and bone metabolism through immune modulation and oxidative stress pathways [57, 58]. Conversely, oral dysbiosis and the chronic inflammation accompanying advanced periodontitis may adversely affect thyroid function [59, 60]. The current study has found that, as periodontal health deteriorates into severe forms of periodontitis, periodontal diagnosis is correlated with thyroid diseases. Nonetheless, findings in the literature remain inconclusive; some studies report a higher prevalence of thyroid disorders in patients with mild bone loss compared to those with more severe disease [45]. In our study, the prevalence of thyroid disease was 4.4%. Periodontal treatment has been shown to reduce systemic inflammation and favorably influence thyroid function parameters [61]. Therefore, periodontists must be proficient in recognizing symptoms of thyroid dysfunction, conducting regular periodontal assessments in affected patients, and timing interventions appropriately. Patient education regarding the connection between oral and thyroid health is also essential for holistic disease management.

In the present study, 1.8% of participants reported a history of psychological illness. Stress and mental health disorders are increasingly recognized as significant risk factors for the development and progression of periodontal disease. Conversely, systemic inflammation induced by periodontal disease has also been implicated as a potential contributor to mental health deterioration. The shared risk factors between these conditions—including advanced age, smoking, and genetic predispositions—highlight the complex, bidirectional relationship between psychological well-being and oral health [62]. The detrimental effects of psychological stress and psychiatric disorders on periodontal status are believed to occur through mechanisms such as dysregulation of the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal axis and sustained hyperinflammatory immune responses [63, 64]. These disorders are also associated with worsened clinical periodontal indicators, including increased PD, BOP, and attachment loss [65]. Psychiatric conditions can impair immune response via alterations in neurotransmitters and hormonal levels, such as serotonin and cortisol. Furthermore, mental health disorders with behavioral factors such as smoking and reduced motivation for self-care may increase susceptibility to periodontal disease and diminish treatment response [66, 67]. Beyond physiological mechanisms, periodontal disease may exert a psychosocial burden on patients, manifesting as halitosis, tooth migration, and gingival recession, all of which can impact self-esteem and social interaction [68]. Given this reciprocal influence, it is essential to adopt a multidisciplinary management approach. This includes increasing patient motivation, promoting more frequent periodontal evaluations, and implementing stress-coping strategies to mitigate disease progression and improve overall health outcomes.

In this study, a 1.4% prevalence of neurological disease was observed, and the association between neurological conditions and periodontal disease is hypothesized to be mediated through multiple biological mechanisms. These include microbial and metabolite translocation, neuroinflammatory processes, central nervous system signaling, and neurohormonal interactions [69]. Systemic inflammation resulting from periodontal disease, along with elevated levels of circulating inflammatory biomarkers, has been shown to impact the brain by altering neurovascular functions and increasing the production of neurotoxic compounds [70, 71]. It is well-documented that patients with Parkinson’s disease, particularly those with impaired motor function, exhibit poorer periodontal health compared to unaffected individuals [72]. Both the disease itself and the medications prescribed for its management can negatively influence periodontal status [72, 73]. Given the rising prevalence of both neurological and periodontal diseases, periodontists are increasingly likely to encounter patients within this demographic. Therefore, it is imperative to adopt a multidisciplinary approach that integrates dental, neurological, and general medical care. Such an approach should prioritize risk identification, early prevention, and personalized care strategies to ensure optimal oral hygiene and overall health outcomes for patients with neurological conditions.

Pulmonary diseases, identified in 2.6% of participants in the present study, are among the most prevalent causes of morbidity and mortality, especially in older and hospitalized populations [74]. Our findings suggest a possible association between pulmonary diseases and periodontal conditions. This connection is thought to be mediated by the anatomical continuity between the oral cavity and the respiratory tract, whereby the dental biofilm may act as a reservoir for respiratory pathogens. The aspiration of periodontopathogens, combined with compromised host defense mechanisms, may contribute to the initiation and progression of respiratory diseases [75]. Several studies have established associations between periodontal disease and diminished respiratory function, including increased airway obstruction [76, 77]. Maintaining optimal oral hygiene has been shown to reduce the pathogen load, thereby lowering the risk and severity of pulmonary diseases [78]. These findings highlight the importance of integrating oral health monitoring into the management of patients with respiratory illnesses, as an adjunctive strategy to prevent or mitigate disease progression.

A range of mechanisms also underlies the relationship between hematological diseases and periodontal pathology. Many hematological disorders increase in prevalence with age and are more commonly observed in men [79, 80]. These disorders can promote hyperinflammation through the activation of myeloid cells, which may contribute to both local and systemic inflammatory responses within the periodontium, thereby compromising immune function [81]. These conditions may heighten susceptibility to periodontal disease and worsen clinical outcomes. Periodontal disease has also been demonstrated to contribute to hematological disorders by affecting oxidative stress and inflammatory responses through systemic inflammation [81]. Given this reciprocal relationship, multidisciplinary management of such patients is crucial to ensure systemic stability and improve quality of life. Special attention should also be paid to hematologic conditions directly associated with periodontal diseases, including leukemia and neutropenia. Clinicians should maintain a high index of suspicion for underlying hematological malignancies. Periodontal disease may be a critical indicator of hematological conditions and the adverse effects of treatment. Periodontal interventions in these patients should be carefully tailored and frequent follow-up is essential to ensure treatment safety and effectiveness. Additionally, periodontists must be cautious with patients undergoing bisphosphonate therapy, immunosuppressive or chemotherapeutic regimens, or hematopoietic stem cell transplantation. Understanding and raising awareness of the complex interplay is vital for fostering a sustained, integrative, and multidisciplinary approach to diagnosis and treatment.

In addition to the rising prevalence of systemic diseases, the increased use of medications among the ageing population may also exert a detrimental impact on oral health [82]. In the present study, 25.9% of participants reported regular medication use. The use of pharmaceuticals poses specific challenges to dental treatment and may necessitate modifications in treatment planning to ensure patient safety and therapeutic efficacy [53, 72]. For example, drugs prescribed to prevent thrombosis and embolism, which may increase bleeding risk, are frequently used by older individuals. Nonetheless, periodontal procedures can typically be performed safely with appropriate dose adjustments, careful monitoring, and control of bleeding parameters.

The importance of periodontal care, maintaining oral hygiene, and routine dental visits is increasingly recognized, particularly within populations burdened by chronic conditions. It is well-established that oral hygiene behaviors are closely linked not only to periodontal health but also to systemic well-being. According to the findings of the current study, 37.7% of participants reported brushing their teeth at least twice a day, whereas only 2.8% reported flossing with the same frequency. The biological and psychological consequences of ageing, coupled with functional limitations, are known to impair the ability to sustain adequate oral hygiene, thereby contributing to the progression of periodontal disease [72]. Dentists play a critical role in mitigating these effects by recommending assistive oral hygiene tools, such as electric toothbrushes and wide-handled manual brushes, which may enhance cleaning efficacy and accommodate the reduced manual dexterity common among geriatric individuals [83]. Longitudinal evidence also supports the importance of routine supportive periodontal therapy. A study with a follow-up period exceeding 15 years demonstrated that individuals who underwent infrequent maintenance therapy experienced higher rates of progressive periodontitis compared to those who received regular professional care [84]. These findings underscore the necessity for individualized, continuous, and long-term periodontal maintenance programs, particularly for older patients. Increasing patient awareness about the importance of ongoing periodontal care is essential for promoting both oral and systemic health in this vulnerable population.

Despite the deleterious effects, the deterioration of systemic health in older individuals does not inevitably compromise the success of periodontal treatment. The implementation of a well-structured and evidence-based treatment plan is crucial for achieving favorable clinical outcomes. Regular periodontal check-ups are essential, and interdisciplinary collaboration among healthcare providers is strongly recommended to manage the interplay.

Medical practitioners should incorporate dental assessments into their routine clinical workflows, particularly in patients with systemic diseases or those undergoing systemic treatments. Similarly, periodontists must maintain a high index of suspicion for oral manifestations of systemic conditions. In accordance with the medical axiom “primum non nocere” (first, do no harm), a thorough and updated anamnesis should be undertaken to identify potential risk factors, ensure appropriate precautions are in place, and prevent complications before initiating any dental intervention. When indicated, premedication and medical consultations should be arranged proactively. Anamnesis records should be regularly revised during follow-up visits, and all treatment plans should be tailored to the individual’s specific risk profile. Furthermore, patients should be educated about their existing systemic conditions and informed of the relevance of these diseases in the context of dental procedures to promote awareness and shared decision-making.

The present study is subject to primary limitations. It employed a single center cross-sectional design, which relies on self-reported medical histories, potentially introducing recall or reporting bias. The fact that the study was conducted in a single center limits the diversity of the study population and the generalizability of the findings. This shortcoming hinders the establishment of causality. In addition, the inclusion of patients with medical records for a specific period, use of panoramic radiography, which has limited sensitivity, to assess alveolar bone loss, and the relatively small number of individuals aged 61 and older limit the generalisability of the results to the elderly population. Furthermore, although all final diagnoses were verified by two experienced periodontologists, the fact that initial clinical measurements were performed by multiple examiners may have introduced examiner variability, which represents a potential limitation of this study. Despite this limitation, the study possesses several notable strengths. These include a large sample size, detailed demographic profiling, analysis of patient health behaviors, and a focus on the association between systemic diseases and periodontal diagnoses. Furthermore, in line with WHO recommendations, which advocate for quinquennial epidemiological studies to track trends in oral diseases and contribute to the formation of a global oral health database, our study offers valuable data for both national and international monitoring. Finally, the study supports the implementation of preventive and integrated public health strategies grounded in the common risk factor approach, thereby contributing to a broader understanding of how periodontal care can mitigate disease burden at the population level.

Conclusion

The findings of the present study indicate that periodontal and systemic diseases are both highly prevalent within the examined population, and their incidence increases significantly with age. The results reveal a strong association between periodontal disease and various systemic conditions supporting the hypothesis of a bidirectional relationship mediated by common risk factors. Notably, periodontal diagnosis was correlated with the severity and frequency of systemic diseases, underscoring the systemic burden of periodontitis in aging individuals.

Our data suggest that oral health emerges as an integral component of overall health, particularly in geriatric and medically compromised populations. The relatively low prevalence of optimal oral hygiene behaviors observed in the study population calls for urgent awareness campaigns and preventive strategies. Moreover, the present study identified a substantial correlation between male gender and smoking, and the transition of periodontal diagnosis towards periodontitis. It is imperative that periodontal risk assessment is conducted in all patients. These risk factors must be given due consideration in the management of periodontal disease, and the significance of regular follow-up protocols and smoking prevention programmes, particularly among male individuals, must be underscored. Furthermore, the frequent use of systemic medications points to the necessity of multidisciplinary care models that integrate dental assessments into broader chronic disease management protocols. Longitudinal and interventional studies are needed to determine causal pathways and the effectiveness of periodontal treatment.

In conclusion, this study offers a compelling argument for the inclusion of periodontal health as a significant determinant in systemic disease prevention and management, particularly in aging populations. Strengthening interprofessional collaboration between dental and medical fields could play a pivotal role in reducing the burden of chronic diseases and improving overall quality of life.
