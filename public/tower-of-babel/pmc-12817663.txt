Effect of hand hygiene program among school children in Bangladesh
Authors: Ferdushi Akhter, Shanzida Khatun, Happy Bandana Biswas
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeSchool children are silent carrier and higher risk to acquire infectious diseases. Good hand hygiene can be an effective preventive measure to protect transmissions of infections. This study aimed to examine the effect of a hand hygiene program among school-aged children at City Corporation Adarsha High School in Dhaka, Bangladesh.Design and methodsA quasi experimental one group pre-test post-test study was conducted among 40 students conveniently. Hand hygiene education intervention was given for two weeks. This study was guided by the KAP survey model which is influence behavior change begins with knowledge, shapes attitude and results in improved practice. Data were collected by using structured self-administered questionnaires including (1) Demographic Questionnaire, (2) Hand Hygiene Knowledge Questionnaire, (3) Hand Hygiene Attitude Questionnaire, and (4) Hand Hygiene Practice Questionnaire. Data were analyzed using both descriptive statistics and inferential statistics.ResultsThe results of the present study showed that the average age of school students was 11.62 ± 1.44 years. There was a statistically significant mean difference between pre- and post-test scores of knowledges (t = -5.605, p < .001), attitude (t = -3.839, p = < .001) and practice (t = -8.273, p = < .001) of hand hygiene among school children’s. Knowledge was positively correlated with practice (r = 0.33, p = .040), and regression analysis showed knowledge was a significant predictor of practice (β = 0.31, p = .045).ConclusionThe findings of this study provide baseline information to the policymaker to implement hand hygiene program using KAP model effective for school students. Further intervention study is needed on diverse setting and population.Implications for practiceIn order to improve hand hygiene knowledge, attitude and practice among school children can be integrating in school curriculum play an effective role in preventing the spread of infectious disease. Nurse and Health educators can get the guideline for developing school-based hygiene promotion intervention particularly in Low middle income countries.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-025-25592-x.Keywords: Hand hygiene, School children, Knowledge, Attitude, Practice, Bangladesh

Introduction

Hand hygiene is recognized as one of the most effective measures for preventing the spread of infectious diseases [1]. It involves cleaning the hands with water, often with soap, to remove or kill microbes acquired from daily activities, thereby limiting the risk of transmission [2]. Despite its importance, access to adequate sanitation remains limited globally. Approximately 2.3 billion people lack basic sanitation, including 892 million who do not have any household toilet facilities [3]. The World Health Organization [40] estimates that one-third of the global population does not have access to basic sanitation, and poor hygiene contributes to around 7% of the global disease burden, particularly respiratory and diarrheal diseases [4, 5]. In Bangladesh, many children face barriers to regular handwashing due to limited access to soap, clean water, and poor school sanitation. Moreover, children’s knowledge and attitudes strongly influence their hygiene behavior [6]. Evidence shows that washing hands, particularly after toilet use, substantially decreases the spread of parasitic infections, which are common among school-aged children in many countries [7, 8].

School-age years are a critical period for developing lifelong hygiene habits [9]. Schools are strategic settings for health promotion because children can learn healthy practices and influence their peers and families [10]. However, in low- and middle-income countries, inadequate hygiene infrastructure contributes to preventable childhood illnesses such as intestinal worms, diarrhea, and dental cavities [11–13]. These conditions negatively affect school attendance, academic performance, and overall well-being, perpetuating a cycle of poor health and poverty [14].

Previous studies have demonstrated that implementing school-based hand hygiene interventions can increase compliance by up to 34% [15]. Interventions conducted in the Netherlands [16], Israel [17], Thailand [18], India [19], New Zealand [20], and Spain [21] have shown that promoting hand hygiene in schools reduces absenteeism and infection rates. However, these studies used varied methods, such as providing soap, hand sanitizer, or health education, making it difficult to determine a single best approach applicable in all contexts. Few studies have assessed interventions emphasizing education and behavioral change without ongoing external resources or costs [22]. These interventions made use of health education sessions and interactive learning regarding hand washing and increase hand hygiene knowledge, attitude and practice.

In Bangladesh, research on Hand hygiene among schoolchildren remains limited, particularly in the nursing field [23]. Budgetary constraints in schools often hinder the consistent availability of hygiene materials such as soap and water. Many schoolchildren remain unaware that simple practices like handwashing before eating and after toilet use can prevent infections and improve health outcomes. Traditional hand hygiene interventions have primarily relied on lectures, posters, and verbal demonstrations, which may increase awareness but often fail to produce lasting behavioral change because learners receive little direct feedback. To enhance engagement and promote experiential learning, the present study used the “Glow Gel” technique, which allows children to visualize residual fluorescent markers under ultraviolet light after handwashing. This method provides immediate visual feedback and reinforces correct handwashing behavior, thereby addressing a key limitation of prior school-based programs that lacked active, participatory components [15, 24]. To address these gaps, this study implemented a school-based hand hygiene education program using the “Glow Gel” technique, which allows children to visualize microorganisms on their hands under ultraviolet light. This interactive approach was expected to enhance students’ knowledge, attitude, and practice regarding hand hygiene. Therefore, this study aimed to evaluate the effectiveness of a structured hand hygiene education program in improving the knowledge, attitude, and practice of school-aged children in Dhaka, Bangladesh.

Method

This study adopted a quasi-experimental one-group pre-test–post-test design. This design was chosen because comparable control groups were not available within the same school system, and the purpose was to assess the preliminary effectiveness and feasibility of a school-based hand hygiene education program. Such exploratory designs are suitable for evaluating behavioral interventions in real-world educational settings with logistical and resource constraints. The study was conducted at City Corporation Adarsho High School, Dhaka, which was selected based on administrative cooperation, accessibility, and feasibility for implementing a pilot health education program. As an urban public school, it represents a typical government-run educational environment; however, the limitation regarding generalizability to other school types (e.g., rural or private) is acknowledged. This study period began from July 2022 to June 2023. This study was carried out on City Corporation Adorsho High School, Dholpur, Dhaka. There was total 1200 students in City Corporation Adorsho High School who were studying in different classes.

The participants of this study were 5th and 6th grade school children. A convenience sampling method was used to recruit participants. Forty students aged 10–12 years who were available and willing to participate were selected from City Corporation Adarsha High School in Dhaka, Bangladesh. Generally, most of the school children were busy with their schedule class. Therefore, the research works were done to intend to give hand hygiene intervention and collect data from students who were available and willing to participate.

The sample size of this study was estimated by using G* power analysis software. The sample size was calculated for an accepted minimum significance level (α) 0.05, an expected power of 0.80, and an estimated population effect size of 0.25 as the medium effect size used in the nursing studies. Sample size was (34). In considering 10% attrition the final sample size was (40). The inclusion criteria of the participants were: (1) children between 10 to 12 years old enrolled at city corporation adorsho high school, (2) school attendance for at least 25 h per week, (3) whose parents and/or guardians was signed an informed consent document. Exclusion criteria included students with chronic illness, irregular attendance, or absence during the intervention period, as these factors could influence participation or outcomes.

Data was collected by using Structured Self-administered questionnaire. The Instrument consists of five sections. Section I is related to participants Demographic Data Questionnaire (DDQ), Section II is related to Hand Hygiene Knowledge Questionnaire (HHKQ), Section III is related to Hand Hygiene Attitude Questionnaire (HHAQ), Section IV is related to Hand Hygiene Practice Questionnaire (HHPQ). Based on cultural context of our country the instruments were validated by three panel experts that is three PhD faculties of National Institute of Advanced Nursing Education and Research. It consisted of 12 items including age, religion, class, gender, family size, housing status, fathers’ education, source of water, type of toilet, nails status, nails length, illness diagnosed.

The original version of the instruments was developed in the English language. After that the English version instruments were translated into the Bengali language. The method of translation was the back translation technique [41]. It is a translation process which ensures accuracy and the culturally equivalence of the instruments when translated to another language (Brislin). Three bilingual translators who were fluent in both English and Bengali translated the instruments (e.g., they were Nursing faculty and one English editor). The process of back translation was conducted as follows.

After completing the back translation process, the researcher reviewed and compared both English versions. The researcher analyzed each item in details and revised based on the two translations. Then, the researcher modified the words of the instruments as needed in order to establish the same meaning within acceptable limits.

The Knowledge, Attitude, and Practice (KAP) questionnaires were developed after reviewing relevant literature and adapted to the school context. Content validity was evaluated by three experts in public health and nursing education. A pilot test was conducted among 10 students aged 10–12 years to assess clarity, wording, and age appropriateness; minor language modifications were made based on feedback. The pilot data were used only for refinement and not for analysis.Internal consistency was assessed using data from the main study sample (n = 40). Cronbach’s alpha coefficients demonstrated good reliability for all three domains: knowledge = 0.81, attitude = 0.87, and practice = 0.84. These values indicate that the instruments were consistent and suitable for measuring hand hygiene KAP among school-aged children. The English version of the questionnaires is provided as Supplementary File 1.

Data collection process consists of two phases including the preparation phase and implementation phase. In the preparation phase, researcher completed the following procedures: Approval of the research proposal was given by the Institutional Review Board at National Institute of Advanced Nursing Education and Research, Bangabandhu Sheikh Mujib Medical University. The researcher was taking permission from authority of City Corporation Adorsho High School in Dholpur, Dhaka, Bangladesh through written order issue by the Director of NIANER. During Data collection phase, the researcher explained the study objectives, benefits, confidentiality and methods or process of data collection to the authority and study participants. All participants are ensured that their participation would be voluntary.

In the implementation phase, data was collected by using Demographic questionnaire, Hand Hygiene Knowledge Questionnaire, Hand Hygiene Attitude Questionnaire, Hand Hygiene Practice Questionnaire from January 2023 to February 2023. Data collection and Hand hygiene program was conducted by the researcher with the help of the respective school teacher. Before collecting data, the researcher approached the children who was meet the inclusion criteria and introduced themselves.

The day before baseline data collection, the researcher was communicating with students over telephone to come to city corporation Adorsho high school. On the day of pre-test, the researcher was explained study purpose, benefit and ethical consideration of the study. The researcher was informed the participants regarding signing the consent form. The participants were informed by the researcher that they have full right to withdraw from this study at any time without any obligation. All participants’ confidentiality and anonymity were strictly maintained with code numbers. Participants were also informed that the findings of the study were submitted to a scientific journal for publication and presentation at conferences, code sheets were secured in a locked file up to five years and the data was accessible only to the researcher, all necessary information collected from the participants was kept confidential. After obtaining verbal permission from the participants, researcher was collecting written consent from the participating students. The researcher explained the questionnaire to the participants. Each student was be given a code number and were divided into four groups (A-D) based on the age of them. Each group was consisting of ten members. The participants were instructed to answer the questionnaires spontaneously within 20–30 min. The pretest was done through structured questionnaires by the researcher. After that, the researcher was checked that the questionnaires would be completed. After the researcher performed three days face –to-face group session of hand hygiene education intervention, Post-test data were collected two weeks after completion of the final session to evaluate short-term retention and behavioral change. This interval was selected based on previous school-based intervention studies that used a similar follow-up period [17, 19].

Data analysis

Data were analyzed using SPSS version 26. Descriptive statistics were computed for all variables. The normality assumption for continuous variables was evaluated using the Shapiro–Wilk test, visual inspection of Q–Q plots, and assessment of skewness and kurtosis values (acceptable range = –2 to + 2). Homogeneity of variance was checked with Levene’s test. Since the data met these assumptions, parametric tests were considered appropriate. A paired t-test was performed to compare pre- and post-intervention scores for knowledge, attitude, and practice. Linear regression analysis was conducted to explore predictors of post-intervention practice scores. A two-tailed p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. In addition to descriptive statistics, baseline comparisons, and paired t-tests, a multiple linear regression analysis was performed to identify predictors of post-test hand hygiene practice, with knowledge and attitude entered as independent variables. Model fit (F-test, R2), standardized beta coefficients (β), and significance levels were reported. Assumptions of regression, including multicollinearity, were checked and met.

Hand hygiene program

The hand hygiene education program was developed based on the World Health Organization (WHO) handwashing technique guidelines. The intervention module was designed for three consecutive days of sessions, each lasting 40 min, and implemented over two consecutive weeks for different student groups. The educational content was reviewed for content validity by expert faculty members from the Child Health Nursing Department at the National Institute of Advanced Nursing Education and Research. The module was originally prepared in English and translated into Bangla by three bilingual experts following a forward–backward translation process to ensure cultural and linguistic accuracy. The intervention began the day after baseline data collection and was conducted at City Corporation Adarsho High School, Dhaka. Participants were divided into four groups (A–D), each consisting of ten students, and sessions were scheduled at times convenient for the students. Baseline (pre-test) data were collected one day before the start of the intervention (Day 1). The educational sessions were then delivered from Day 2 to Day 16, depending on each group’s assigned dates. The post-test (endline) assessment was conducted two weeks after completion of the final session for each group to evaluate knowledge, attitude, and practice changes. Each group attended three sessions on three consecutive days. On the day before the intervention, the class teacher contacted students to confirm attendance at the school health center. During the sessions, the researcher and a trained school health nurse introduced themselves, explained the purpose and procedures, and obtained verbal consent from participants. The content of each session was structured as follows: Day 1: Introduction to the importance of hand hygiene and the routes of infection transmission, delivered through posters and a short video presentation. Day 2: Demonstration of proper handwashing techniques using soap and water, supplemented with a printed leaflet illustrating each step. Day 3: Practical reinforcement using the “Glow Gel” technique, which enabled students to visualize areas missed during handwashing under ultraviolet light. All sessions were delivered face-to-face in small groups by the principal investigator and the trained school health nurse, who received a one-hour preparatory orientation on educational materials and demonstration procedures to ensure consistency. Attendance for each session was recorded, and identical visual aids and demonstration methods were used for all groups to maintain intervention fidelity. No deviations from the planned content were reported. Educational materials included printed leaflets illustrating the WHO handwashing steps, posters displayed in classrooms, and a short educational video developed in Bangla. Sample copies of the materials are provided in the Supplementary File for replicability.

Discussion

This study demonstrated significant improvements in schoolchildren’s hand hygiene knowledge, attitudes, and self-reported practices following a short, structured education program. The mean knowledge score increased from 17.00 to 22.35, attitudes from 50.65 to 56.60, and correct hand-washing technique from 14.70 to 23.33 at two-week follow-up, indicating that brief, school-based education can positively influence hygiene outcomes in this setting. The average age of participants was approximately 11 years, which is consistent with prior school-based research in comparable age groups [25], though some studies from Bangladesh report different age distributions depending on school type and location [26]. Interpreted through the Knowledge–Attitude–Practice (KAP) lens, gains in knowledge likely contributed to more favorable attitudes and, in turn, better reported practice. For example, children’s understanding that unwashed hands transmit disease and that soap is necessary improved meaningfully like as soap use rising from 70 to 85%, aligning with studies that reported post-education gains in key hand hygiene concepts [27–29]. Nonetheless, our correlation coefficients were positive but weak, and a regression model showed that knowledge and attitude together explained only 17.2% of variance in practice (R2 = 0.172). This modest explanatory power suggests that cognitive gains alone do not fully translate into behavior, and that additional determinants—motivation, supervision, peer norms, and the physical environment—likely play important roles in shaping children’s handwashing. A teacher-led, low-cost school program also improved behavior but emphasized soap/water access and routine reinforcement for maintenance. Local KAP surveys similarly document gaps between knowledge and consistent soap use due to infrastructural and routine barriers [23, 30]. Broader Bangladesh evidence links handwashing to reduced diarrheal illness yet stresses that timing, materials, and setting determine real-world impact [31, 32]. Together, these findings—and our modest R2—argue for multifaceted approaches pairing education with enabling environments, social norms, and policy supports. This interpretation is consistent with intervention studies emphasizing experiential or participatory components. In Pakistan, Khan et al. [33] found that adding a Glow-Gel–based visual feedback to education improved children’s handwashing technique more than education alone. Similarly, Younie et al. [34] used regression models in a UK school program and showed that improvements in knowledge did not fully predict observed handwashing behavior, reinforcing the idea that context and feedback mechanisms mediate behavior change. Together with our findings, these studies support the need for multifaceted programs that pair education with practice opportunities and immediate feedback. Contextual factors in and around schools also matter. Even after our program, some students reported barriers at school and home, notably inconsistent soap availability and inadequate facilities—issues echoed in earlier school studies [35]. It was stated that people will not have change in behavior unless they see the benefits [36]. This shows that change in attitude among the children in the intervention group was based on the information receiving during the hand hygiene education. This finding was almost comparable with the studies conducted in other parts of Ethiopia: 59.4% in Hosanna [37] and 61.7% in northern Ethiopia [38]. Such constraints help explain why some contexts a South African study reporting 91.4% positive attitudes [39], achieve higher post-intervention levels than single-site programs in resource-constrained settings. These differences likely reflect infrastructure, policy, and routine reinforcement rather than education content alone. Overall, the pattern across our outcomes and the literature suggests that education is necessary but insufficient for durable hand hygiene improvement. Programs that integrate engaging pedagogy demonstrations, visual feedback, reliable access to soap and water, teacher/parent reinforcement, and supportive school routines are more likely to convert knowledge and attitude gains into sustained practice.

Limitations of the study

This study used a single-group pre–post design, which limits the ability to rule out alternative explanations such as maturation, seasonal influences, testing effects, Hawthorne effects, or regression to the mean. The convenience sampling of students from one school in Dhaka further restricts the external validity and generalizability of the findings. Practice was self-reported, and the short two-week follow-up period may not reflect long-term behavioral change. Future research should employ randomized controlled or multi-site designs with longer follow-up periods to strengthen causal inference and assess sustainability.

Implications for policy and practice

This study highlights the importance of integrating hand hygiene education into routine school health activities. The findings indicate that structured and interactive hygiene sessions—supported by teachers, school nurses, and parents—can help develop positive hygiene behaviors among children. Strengthening collaboration between education and health sectors may promote sustainable hygiene practices and reduce infection-related school absenteeism.

Conclusion

This quasi-experimental one-group pretest–posttest study, conducted from July 2022 to June 2023 at City Corporation Adarsha High School in Dhaka, Bangladesh, explored the potential effect of a two-week hand hygiene education program among 40 students. After the program, improvements were observed in students’ knowledge, attitude, and practice scores. While the findings suggest that school-based hand hygiene education may enhance children’s handwashing behavior, the study’s small sample size, single-site setting, short follow-up, and absence of a control group limit the strength and generalizability of the conclusions. Further large-scale, controlled studies are recommended to confirm these findings and assess long-term outcomes.

Recommendations

Based on the limitation of the study following recommendations are suggested- Curriculum should be revised taking the hand hygiene into consideration for good health of the school children. The training of teachers and parents, their motivation, both are needed for their role in improving hand washing practice. Further studies are needed to understand how hand hygiene is practiced at home and in school while relating this practice to infectious disease transmission and risk factors. Furthermore, extensive controlled studies are required to address other potential factors that contribute to knowledge, attitudes and practices of hand hygiene.
