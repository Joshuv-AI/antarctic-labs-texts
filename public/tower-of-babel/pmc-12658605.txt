Integrative Transcriptome and Machine Learning Analysis Uncovers Critical STAT3/GREM2 Signaling Mechanisms in Dexamethasone Treatment of Hashimoto’s Thyroiditis
Authors: Huarong Zeng, Yingjie Chen, Tianchi Hu, Yi Zhou, Meimei Su, Lin Lin, Zhifu Ke
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Hashimoto’s thyroiditis (HT) is a common autoimmune thyroid disorder influenced by genetic, environmental, and immunological factors. Current treatment primarily relies on thyroid hormone replacement therapy, yet the underlying mechanisms of the disease and its response to therapy remain poorly understood. Therefore, elucidating the molecular mechanisms of HT, especially in response to immunosuppressive agents, is essential for improving therapeutic strategies. In this study, we integrated transcriptomic profiling, machine learning, and experimental validation to elucidate the immunoregulatory effects of Dex in HT. Peripheral blood samples from healthy individuals, untreated HT patients, and Dex-treated HT patients were analyzed via RNA sequencing, with differentially expressed genes (DEGs) identified using the “limma” R package. Functional enrichment analyses (GO, KEGG, and GSEA) revealed that Dex treatment downregulated key immune and inflammatory pathways, particularly those involving cytokine signaling and the JAK-STAT axis. Integrative machine learning approaches (LASSO, SVM-RFE, and Random Forest) consistently identified GREM2 as a Dex-responsive gene, which was further validated in an independent cohort using correlation and ROC analyses, supporting its predictive value for treatment response. CIBERSORT analysis demonstrated substantial alterations in immune cell infiltration, including shifts in B-cell subtypes. Mechanistically, GREM2 was validated as a downstream target of STAT3 through dual-luciferase assays and siRNA interference. In vitro studies using LPS-induced Nthy-ori 3-1 thyroid cells confirmed that Dex exerts its anti-inflammatory effects by inhibiting STAT3 phosphorylation, thereby reducing GREM2 expression and downstream proinflammatory cytokines such as TNF-α, IL-1β, and CCL2. These findings highlight the STAT3/GREM2 signaling axis as a critical mediator of Dex’s therapeutic action in HT and provide a mechanistic framework for its potential clinical application in autoimmune thyroid diseases.

Introduction

Hashimoto’s thyroiditis (HT) is an organ-specific autoimmune condition commonly associated with the development of hypothyroidism, affecting approximately 20–30% of patients. In recent decades, the prevalence of HT has risen markedly, and individuals with HT exhibit an elevated risk of cardiovascular comorbidities, with some progressing toward thyroid malignancies. Standard management of HT primarily relies on lifelong thyroid hormone replacement therapy, often combined with micronutrient interventions such as selenium and vitamin D supplementation. Nevertheless, these therapies do not target the immune mechanisms underlying the disease. Consequently, there has been growing interest in exploring immunomodulatory strategies, including the use of glucocorticoids such as dexamethasone (Dex), as potential adjunct treatments for HT.

The pathogenesis of HT is complex and not yet fully understood, involving genetic predisposition, environmental factors, and immune dysfunction. Both cellular and humoral immunity play key roles in the pathological process of HT, particularly the autoreactive lymphocyte infiltration of the thyroid tissue, consisting of B cells and T cells. Additionally, T cell dysfunction is closely related to the disruption of immune homeostasis in the thyroid. Overall, immune dysfunction and inflammation are critical in the progression of HT. Dex is a potent synthetic glucocorticoid with strong anti-inflammatory and immunosuppressive properties, widely used in the treatment of autoimmune and inflammatory diseases. , Its mechanism of action mainly involves binding to the glucocorticoid receptor (GR), thereby regulating gene expression and inhibiting the production of pro-inflammatory cytokines. Although Dex exerts broad regulatory effects in various diseases, its specific molecular mechanisms in HT remain insufficiently characterized.

Importantly, recent studies have explored the feasibility of intrathyroidal glucocorticoid injection in thyroid-related autoimmune diseases. For example, in subacute thyroiditis (SAT), ultrasound-guided intrathyroidal glucocorticoid injection has been shown to provide faster pain relief, reduce cumulative steroid doses, and minimize adverse effects compared with systemic therapy. , In painful Hashimoto’s thyroiditis, case series and reviews have suggested that intrathyroidal corticosteroid injection (e.g., triamcinolone) can rapidly relieve symptoms and avoid surgery when oral treatment is ineffective. , Furthermore, in Graves’ disease and other thyroid autoimmune conditions, intrathyroidal Dex injection has been reported to reduce recurrence rates and rebalance Th1/Th2 immune responses. These findings highlight the clinical feasibility and potential advantages of local Dex administration in thyroid autoimmunity, providing methodological and theoretical support for its application in HT.

In recent years, the rapid development of bioinformatics has provided a valuable tool for studying the molecular mechanisms of complex diseases. Through these approaches, researchers can gain deeper insights into disease progression and treatment, and bioinformatics has become an important resource for identifying disease-specific biomarkers in HT. Indeed, numerous genes and pathways related to HT have been identified and validated, providing a solid theoretical foundation for understanding the disease mechanism and developing potential therapeutic targets. , For example, studies have shown that LTF is closely associated with the progression of HT and papillary thyroid carcinoma (PTC). Other studies have demonstrated significant upregulation of chemokines in HT tissues, including CCL2 and CCL3, which play crucial roles in HT pathogenesis. However, the key genes associated with Dex treatment in HT have not been systematically identified.

In summary, the primary aim of this study was to investigate the differentially expressed genes (DEGs), associated biological functions, and signaling pathways involved in intrathyroidal Dex treatment of HT and to elucidate their molecular mechanisms. Specifically, we treated HT patients with intrathyroidal Dex injection and collected peripheral blood samples for transcriptomic sequencing to identify DEGs among healthy individuals and HT patients before and after treatment. Multiple enrichment analyses, including GO, KEGG, and GSEA, were performed to characterize the biological functions and signaling pathways associated with HT and Dex treatment. Subsequently, three machine learning algorithmsShrinkage and Selection Operator (LASSO), Support Vector Machine-Recursive Feature Elimination (SVM-RFE), and Random Forest (RF)were applied to screen for key feature genes, which were further validated in an independent clinical cohort using correlation analyses and ROC curve evaluation. In parallel, immune cell infiltration induced by Dex treatment and its association with feature genes were assessed using the CIBERSORT algorithm. Finally, the mechanistic role of the identified genes in Dex treatment of HT was examined and verified by in vitro experiments. Collectively, these results provide a new theoretical basis for Dex treatment of HT and offer deeper insights into its therapeutic mechanisms.

Materials and Methods

Headings Study Design and Participant Recruitment

This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Xiamen Traditional Chinese Medicine Hospital (Approval No.: 2020-K003–01), and all participants provided written informed consent after being fully informed of the study details.

The inclusion criteria for HT patients were: newly diagnosed HT with elevated serum TgAb and/or TPOAb; meeting the diagnostic criteria for subclinical hypothyroidism, defined as elevated TSH with normal levels of FT3 and FT4, specifically TSH values between 4.9 mIU/L and 10 mIU/L; TgAb or TPOAb levels exceeding 50%; aged between 18 and 70 years, with normal cognitive and behavioral capabilities; and voluntarily participating in the study after being fully informed.

Exclusion criteria included: individuals with primary cardiovascular, cerebrovascular, liver, kidney, or hematopoietic diseases, as well as those with psychiatric disorders; patients with other autoimmune or inflammatory diseases; patients with other thyroid conditions such as polycystic ovary syndrome, thyroid nodules, or thyroid cancer; individuals with allergic conditions or a known allergy to dexamethasone; pregnant, planning to become pregnant, or breastfeeding women; individuals taking glucocorticoids or other medications for the treatment of Hashimoto’s thyroiditis; and participants currently enrolled in other clinical trials.

Intrathyroidal injection of Dex was employed to achieve high local drug concentrations while minimizing systemic exposure. A dose of 5 mg Dex (Dexamethasone Sodium Phosphate Injection, 5 mg/1 mL, Cisen Pharmaceutical, China) was injected into each thyroid lobe under real-time ultrasound guidance using a sterile disposable syringe, with multiple directional punctures per lobe (left followed by right). The treatment consisted of three courses: the first and second courses each included 6 weekly injections followed by a 4-week interval, and the third course included 6 injections given biweekly. Thus, the full regimen lasted approximately 32 weeks, corresponding to Week 10, Week 20, and Week 32 assessments. All procedures were performed by the same experienced physician to ensure consistency, and patients were instructed to apply firm local pressure for 15 min postinjection to minimize hematoma risk. After completion of the three courses, patients entered a structured follow-up phase for efficacy and safety monitoring.

In this study, we first established a discovery and preliminary validation cohort consisting of 5 healthy individuals and 15 patients with HT. For the healthy individuals, one fasting venous blood sample was collected in the morning and used for both transcriptomic sequencing and RT-qPCR analysis. For the HT patients, two fasting venous blood samples were collected: one on the day prior to treatment initiation and another on the day following completion of the first course of Dex therapy. Among these, 8 HT patients were initially intended for transcriptomic sequencing; however, 4 pretreatment samples were contaminated due to improper preservation. As a result, a total of 12 HT samples (4 pretreatment and 8 post-treatment) were ultimately included in the sequencing analysis. In addition, blood samples from 7 other HT patients were subjected to RT-qPCR for gene expression validation. Baseline characteristics of this cohort are provided in Table S1.

Building on this, we further recruited an extended validation cohort of 36 HT patients who completed three full courses of Dex therapy. Peripheral blood samples were collected at four time points: before treatment (Week 0), after the first course (Week 10), after the second course (Week 20), and after the third course (Week 32). These samples were analyzed for serum TSH, TPOAb, TgAb, and GREM2 levels to further assess the relationship between molecular markers and clinical outcomes. Baseline characteristics of this cohort are summarized in Table S2.

RNA Extraction, Library Construction, and RNA Sequencing

Total RNA was extracted using TRIzol Reagent (Invitrogen, Cat# 15596018, China). RNA purity was assessed with a NanoDrop Spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Cat# 912A1101, China), while concentration and integrity were measured using the Qubit 4 Fluorometer (Invitrogen, Cat# Q33238, China) and the 4150 Bioanalyzer system (Agilent Technologies, Cat# G2992AA, USA). RNA libraries were constructed using the NEBNext Ultra II RNA Library Prep Kit (New England Biolabs, Cat# E7775, USA). After validation of library quality, sequencing was performed on the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform (Illumina, Cat# 20012850, USA).

Differential Gene Expression Analysis

DEGs were identified by comparing healthy individuals with pretreatment HT patients, as well as pretreatment versus post-treatment HT groups. The analysis was conducted using the ‘limma’ package. Genes with an adjusted p-value <0.05 and |log2FC| > 1 were considered significantly differentially expressed. Visualization of results was performed using volcano plots and heatmaps generated with the ‘ggplot2’ and ‘pheatmap’ packages.

GO and KEGG Analysis

GO and KEGG analyses were performed to explore gene-associated biological functions and pathways. Enrichment analysis was conducted using the ‘clusterProfiler’ package, with a q-value threshold set at <0.05. The top 10 enriched terms were visualized using the ‘enrichplot’, ‘GOplot’, and ‘ggplot2’ packages, based on the filtering criteria of qvalueFilter <0.05.

Gene Set Enrichment Analysis

GSEA was conducted on DEGs derived from pre- and post-treatment RNA-seq data using the ‘clusterProfiler’ package in R. The hallmark gene set h.all.v2024.1.Hs.symbols.gmt was obtained from the Molecular Signatures Database (MSigDB) and applied for pathway enrichment analysis. Relevant signaling pathways associated with Dex treatment in HT were identified through this approach. Visualizations were generated using the OmicShare platform (http://www.omicshare.com/tools). Results with a q-value <0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Key Gene Screening for Dex Treatment of HT Using Three Machine Learning Algorithms

To identify genes most relevant to Dex treatment in HT, three established machine learning methods were employed: LASSO, SVM-RFE, and RF. A fixed random seed of 123 was set for both HT and treatment groups to ensure reproducibility. For LASSO analysis, we used the ‘glmnet’ package was used with 10-fold cross-validation. The “family” parameter was set to “binomial,” and the optimal regularization parameter λ was determined by minimizing the mean cross-validation error (lambda.min). The selection process was guided by evaluating the binomial deviance and the log­(λ) plot, thereby avoiding overfitting and ensuring robust feature selection. For Random Forest modeling, the ‘randomForest’ package was used. Model parameters, including the number of decision trees and the number of variables randomly sampled at each split, were optimized through grid search combined with 10-fold cross-validation. Genes were ranked by variable importance scores, and an accuracy threshold of 0.7 was applied to filter key variables. For SVM-RFE, recursive feature elimination was implemented using the ‘e1071’ and ‘SVM-RFE’ packages, with model accuracy as the primary evaluation metric. 10-fold cross-validation was applied during the feature elimination process to identify the optimal subset of features with maximal predictive performance. This integrated approach enabled the identification of central genes most associated with Dex-mediated regulation in HT. Finally, to further validate the robustness and clinical relevance of the identified feature gene, an extended validation cohort of 36 HT patients who completed the full Dex treatment regimen was analyzed, and both correlation analysis and receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve evaluation were performed to assess its predictive value for treatment response.

Estimation of Immune Cell Infiltration

The proportion and distribution of immune infiltrating cells were estimated using the CIBERSORT deconvolution algorithm, implemented via the CIBERSORT R package. The frequencies of 22 immune cell types were calculated using the LM22 signature matrix, which profiles immune cell subsets from human tissues. Correlation analysis between the signature genes and immune cell fractions was conducted using the ‘limma’ package, with visualizations generated using ‘ggpubr’ and ‘ggExtra’ R packages.

Cell Culture and Processing

The immortalized human thyroid follicular epithelial cell line Nthy-ori 3–1 (BTCC, Cat# BTCC-1139, UK) was maintained in RPMI-1640 medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS), 100 U/mL penicillin, and 100 U/mL streptomycin at 37 °C in a humidified incubator with 5% CO2. Inflammatory stimulation was induced by treating cells with 20 mM lipopolysaccharide (LPS, Sigma-Aldrich, Cat# L5293, China) for 12 h.

Cell Transfection Procedure

Nthy-ori 3–1 cells in the logarithmic growth phase were seeded into 14-well plates for transfection. The reagents included the pcDNA3.1 plasmid for GREM2 overexpression (oe-GREM2) and an empty vector control (oe-NC). STAT3-targeting siRNA (si-STAT3) was transfected, with sequences: sense, 5′-CGUCAUUUAGCAGAAUCUCAtt-3′; antisense, 5′-UGAGAUUCUGCUAAUGACGtt-3′. Negative control siRNA (si-NC) was also introduced: sense, 5′-UUCUCCGAACGUGUCACGUTT-3′; antisense, 5′-ACGUGACACGUUCGGAGAATT-3′. All oligonucleotides were purchased from Sangon Biotech (Shanghai, China). Transfections were carried out using Lipofectamine 3000 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Cat# L3000008, China) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. After 48 h, transfected cells were collected for qPCR-based gene expression analysis. A scrambled siRNA (NC-siRNA) was used as an additional control: sense, 5′-CGUACGCGGAAUACUUCGA dTdT-3′; antisense, 5′-UCGAA-GUAUUCCGCGUACG dTdT-3′.

Luciferase Reporter Assay

A putative STAT3 binding site within the GREM2 promoter was predicted using the JASPAR database. Based on this prediction, luciferase reporter constructs containing either wild-type or mutant GREM2 promoter sequences were synthesized by Sangon Biotech. Luciferase activity was evaluated 48 h post-transfection using the Dual-Luciferase Reporter Assay Kit (MCE, Cat# HY-K1013–100T, China).

Quantitative Real-Time PCR

Total RNA was isolated using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, Cat# 15596018, China), and reverse transcription was performed with the SuperScript RT kit (Invitrogen, Cat# 118091200, China). qRT-PCR amplification was carried out on a Roche LightCycler 480 II system using PowerTrack SYBR Green Master Mix (Invitrogen, Cat# A46110, China). Relative gene expression levels were calculated using the 2̂−ΔΔCt method, with GAPDH employed as the internal reference. Primer sequences are provided in Table .

Western Blot Analysis

Cells were lysed using RIPA buffer, and total protein concentration was determined using the BCA assay. Equal amounts of protein were separated by SDS-PAGE and transferred onto PVDF membranes. After blocking with 5% nonfat milk for 1 h at room temperature, membranes were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against GREM2 (Amylet Scientific, Cat# BGT-ANT-28637, China; 1:1000), STAT3 (Abcam, Cat# ab109085, China; 1:1000), p-STAT3 (Abcam, Cat# ab76315, China; 1:1000), and GAPDH (Abcam, Cat# ab8245, China; 1:5000, used as loading control). The following day, HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies (1:5000) were applied for 1 h at room temperature. Protein signals were visualized using enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) and captured with a gel documentation system.

Enzyme-Linked Immunosorbent Assay

ELISA kits specific for human TNF-α (Abcam, Cat# ab100654, China), IL-1β (Abcam, Cat# ab214025, China), and CCL2 (Abcam, Cat# ab100777, China) were used to quantify cytokine levels in the culture supernatant of Nthy-ori 3–1 cells. For each sample, 10 μL of supernatant was transferred to a 96-well microplate, and absorbance at 450 nm was recorded using an automated microplate reader. Data analysis was conducted according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Statistical Methods

Data were analyzed using R 4.3.2 and Prism 9.0. Results are presented as mean ± standard deviation. Statistical comparisons were made using the unpaired t test, while correlations were evaluated using the Pearson test. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05, and all experiments were conducted in triplicate.

Results

Analysis of DEGs in Healthy Subjects, Hashimoto’s Thyroiditis and Dex-Treated Hashimoto’s Thyroiditis Patients

We conducted differential analyses of peripheral blood transcriptome data using the ‘limma’ R package. This study included gene expression matrices from healthy subjects (n = 5), Hashimoto’s thyroiditis patients prior to treatment (n = 4), and Dex-treated patients (n = 8). Principal component analysis (PCA) clearly distinguished the three groups, as shown in Figure S1. Our goal was to identify genes differentially expressed in association with Hashimoto’s thyroiditis and to evaluate changes in gene expression following dexamethasone treatment. Our findings revealed a total of 1,837 DEGs linked to Hashimoto’s thyroiditis, comprising 1,279 upregulated and 558 downregulated genes (Figure A and B). Additionally, we identified 816 DEGs associated with the effects of dexamethasone treatment, including 332 upregulated and 484 downregulated genes (Figure C and D).

GO and KEGG analyses of DEGs Pre- and Post-Dex Treatment

To elucidate the potential molecular mechanisms underlying Dex intervention in HT, we first examined GO and KEGG enrichment of DEGs between HT patients and healthy individuals (Figure S2), and then performed enrichment analyses on DEGs identified before and after treatment. GO analysis for Biological Processes (BP) indicated that upregulated genes were significantly associated with biological functions such as the apical region of the cell. In the Cellular Component (CC) category, these genes were primarily enriched in structural domains including the Z-disc and I-band. Molecular Function (MF) analysis identified “monooxygenase activity” as a dominant feature among upregulated DEGs (Figure A). KEGG analysis further revealed that these genes were mainly enriched in the cytokine–cytokine receptor interaction and TGF-β signaling pathways (Figure B). For downregulated DEGs, BP terms were linked to immune-related processes such as the positive regulation of leukocyte activation and receptor signaling mediated by STAT. In the CC category, these genes were predominantly associated with the collagen-containing extracellular matrix and the MHC protein complex. MF analysis demonstrated enrichment in functions including “cytokine activity,” “integrin binding,” and “oxygen carrier activity” (Figure C). KEGG analysis of the downregulated genes revealed enrichment in pathways such as cytokine–cytokine receptor interaction, the JAK–STAT signaling pathway, and autoimmune thyroid disease (Figure D). In addition, GSEA identified significant enrichment of the IL6–JAK–STAT3 signaling axis following Dex treatment (Figure E). Collectively, these findings suggest that Dex modulates gene expression in HT through pathways related to cytokine signaling, immune regulation, and extracellular matrix remodeling. Comprehensive results of the GO and KEGG analyses are summarized in Tables S3–S6, while the GSEA results are shown in Table S7.

Identification of Key Genes in Dex Treatment of HT Using Machine Learning Algorithms

To further identify key genes involved in Dex treatment of HT, we first took the intersection of DEGs from HT vs healthy and HT pre- vs post-Dex comparisons. Since HT is an autoimmune disease, we hypothesized that immune-related genes play a crucial role. Therefore, we downloaded the complete list of immunologically relevant genes from the ImmPort database (https://www.immport.org) and intersected it with the previously identified DEGs. This process resulted in the identification of 27 key genes (Figure A).

In order to identify the key genes involved in Dex treatment of HT, we applied three machine learning algorithms: LASSO, SVM-RFE, and Random Forest to perform gene selection. Based on the LASSO coefficient profiles and the optimal tuning parameter selection map, λ was set to 0.066068705, and seven genes-ELN, NR5A2, CELA1, INHBB, IGF2, HJV, and GREM2-were identified (Figure B and C). For the random forest algorithm, using an accuracy threshold of 0.7, we identified the identification of 12 significant genes (Figure D and E). In addition, the SVM algorithm identified one gene (GREM) with the lowest cross-validation error (Figure F). Finally, we took the intersection of the genes selected by the three algorithms, and GREM2 was identified as the key gene involved in Dex treatment of HT (Figure G). Thus, it can be concluded that GREM2 plays an important role in the therapeutic mechanism of Dex for HT.

Clinical Relevance and Validation of GREM2 in Dex Treatment of HT

To further evaluate the clinical relevance of GREM2 in Dex treatment of HT, we analyzed longitudinal changes in thyroid function and antibody levels in HT patients across three treatment courses. Serum TSH (Figure A), TPOAb (Figure B), and TgAb (Figure C) levels showed significant reductions following Dex treatment, accompanied by a progressive decrease in peripheral blood GREM2 expression (Figure D). Correlation analyses revealed that reductions in GREM2 expression were positively associated with improvements in TSH, TPOAb, and TgAb across different treatment time points (Figure E–M). Furthermore, ROC curve analysis demonstrated that GREM2 exhibited robust predictive value for Dex treatment response, with AUC values exceeding 0.7 at multiple time points (Figure N). Collectively, these results reinforce the importance of GREM2 as a key biomarker in Dex treatment of HT, with potential clinical utility in evaluating therapeutic efficacy.

Immune Cell Infiltration and Correlation Analysis of Key Genes with Immune Cells

Given the close association between HT and immune responses, we utilized the CIBERSORT algorithm to predict the relationship between DEGs and various types of immune cells before and after Dex treatment in HT patients. Figure A displays the proportion of 22 immune cell types in 12 samples collected before and after Dex treatment. The correlation between different immune cell types is shown in Figure B, where Macrophages M1 and Monocytes exhibited the strongest positive correlation (r = 0.82), while resting NK cells and activated NK cells showed the strongest negative correlation (r = −0.78). Additionally, we performed a Pearson correlation analysis to investigate the association between GREM2 and 22 immune cell types (Figure C). As depicted in Figure D, GREM2 demonstrated a significant negative correlation with naive B cells (R = −0.65, p = 0.023), while showing a significant positive correlation with memory B cells (R = 0.58, p = 0.049). The results showed that GREM2 is an important factor in the immune response to HT treatment.

GREM2 Is a Downstream Target of STAT3

To investigate the potential mechanism of GREM2 during Dex treatment of HT, we used several databases to predict the upstream transcription factors of GREM2, including GTRD (https://gtrd.biouml.org/), KnockTF (https://bio.liclab.net/KnockTF/index.php), JASPAR (https://jaspar.elixir.no) and ChIP-Atlas (https://chip-atlas.org/). Through comprehensive analysis of these databases’ prediction results, we identified two possible upstream transcription factors: STAT3 and MYC (Myelocytomatosis viral oncogene homologue), as shown in Figure A. Transcriptomic data analysis revealed that the expression level of GREM2 was significantly downregulated after Dex treatment, suggesting that GREM2 may play an important role in the treatment process. Using the JASPAR database, we predicted the binding sites of STAT3 and MYC to GREM2 (Figure B). With a threshold relative profile score of 90%, only STAT3 was found to bind to the promoter region of GREM2 (Figure C), indicating that STAT3 might be a key factor in regulating GREM2 expression.

(B) GREM2 expression levels in HT patients before and after Dex treatment (**p < 0.01). (C) Schematic representation of the STAT3 binding site in the GREM2 promoter region. To verify STAT3′s regulatory role on GREM2 expression, we performed a dual luciferase reporter gene assay. The results showed that si-STAT3 significantly reduced the luciferase activity of cells carrying the GREM2-WT promoter reporter gene, while it had no significant effect on cells carrying the GREM2-MT promoter reporter gene (Figure D). This further supports the direct interaction between STAT3 and GREM2. Additionally, we examined the mRNA expression level of GREM2 using qRT-PCR, which showed that GREM2 expression was significantly downregulated in the si-STAT3-treated group compared to the control group (si-NC) (Figure E). These data collectively indicate that STAT3 regulates GREM2 expression at the transcriptional level, confirming that STAT3 is a key transcription factor upstream of GREM2. Our results provide preliminary evidence that STAT3 plays an important role in Dex treatment of HT by regulating GREM2 expression, laying a theoretical foundation for further elucidating the role of GREM2 and its regulatory mechanism in Dex treatment of HT.

Changes in GREM2 and STAT3 Expression and Inflammatory Cytokine Levels before and after Dex Treatment in HT Patients

To further validate the previous findings, we performed RT-qPCR and ELISA to assess GREM2 and STAT3 mRNA expression as well as inflammatory cytokine levels in healthy individuals and an additional cohort of 7 HT patients. Compared to the control group, the expression levels of GREM2 and STAT3 mRNA were significantly elevated in HT patients before Dex treatment. After Dex treatment, the expression of both GREM2 and STAT3 was significantly reduced (Figure A and B). Additionally, the serum levels of TNF-α, IL-1β, and CCL2 were markedly higher in HT patients before treatment, but were significantly decreased following Dex treatment (Figure C–D).

Dex Suppresses the Inflammatory Response in Nthy-ori 3-1 by Inhibiting the STAT3/GREM2 Signaling Axis

To further investigate the molecular mechanisms of STAT3 and GREM2 in Dex-treated HT, we performed the following studies using Nthy-ori 3–1 cells. Overexpression of GREM2 significantly increased its expression level in Nthy-ori 3–1 cells, as shown in Figure A. According to the Western blot results (Figure B), Dex significantly regulated the phosphorylation of STAT3 and the expression of GREM2. Before Dex treatment, the LPS group showed a significant increase in the phosphorylation level of STAT3 (p-STAT3), indicating that LPS induced the activation of STAT3. In the si-STAT3 group and the LPS + si-STAT3 + oe-GREM2 group, the levels of STAT3 and p-STAT3 were significantly decreased, confirming the successful silencing of STAT3. After Dex treatment, the levels of p-STAT3 were generally reduced in the treatment groups, especially in the LPS and si-STAT3 groups, indicating that Dex can inhibit the phosphorylation of STAT3. In addition, Dex treatment also inhibited the expression of GREM2, particularly under GREM2 overexpression conditions, indicating that Dex can downregulate GREM2 expression by inhibiting STAT3 activation.

Then, using ELISA and RT-qPCR assays, we analyzed the expression levels of inflammatory factors (TNF-α, IL-1β, and CCL2) in these cell lines before and after Dex treatment. ELISA results (Figure C-E) showed that LPS induction significantly increased the levels of TNF-α, IL-1β, and CCL2 compared to the control group. However, silencing STAT3 significantly reduced the expression levels of these inflammatory factors. Notably, GREM2 overexpression was able to reverse the inhibitory effect of STAT3 silencing on these inflammatory factors. Importantly, these patterns persisted following Dex treatment, suggesting that Dex treatment exerts a therapeutic effect by suppressing cellular inflammation through the STAT3/GREM2 signaling axis. RT-qPCR results (Figure F–H) confirmed similar trends at the nucleic acid level. These findings confirm that Dex treatment reduces LPS-induced inflammation in Nthy-ori 3–1 by inhibiting STAT3 phosphorylation, thereby downregulating GREM2 expression.

Discussion

In this study, we provide novel insights into the therapeutic mechanisms of Hashimoto’s thyroiditis. Through transcriptomic analysis combined with machine learning algorithms, we identified GREM2 as a key potential target for Dex treatment of HT. Transcription factor predictions and in vitro experiments confirmed that STAT3 is the upstream regulator of GREM2. Further in vitro experiments validated the critical role of the STAT3/GREM2 signaling pathway in alleviating inflammation in HT.

GREM2 is a secreted BMP antagonist that has been shown to play an important role in several diseases, including lung or kidney fibrosis, , diabetic nephropathy and cancer. More recently, GREM2 was identified as a circulating protein factor associated with visceral obesity in humans, inhibiting the browning capacity of visceral preadipocytes via the BMP4/7-BMPR2 signaling pathway. GREM2 has also been implicated in the age-related decline in the ability to maintain skin homeostasis by inhibiting the differentiation of epidermal and dermal stem/progenitor cells. In addition, the GREM2 rs4454537 polymorphism has been shown to be significantly associated with decreased bone mineral density and increased risk of osteoporosis in postmenopausal women. Studies have also shown that GREM2 regulates adipocyte differentiation through the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway, making it an important regulator of adipogenesis. However, research on the role of GREM2 in HT is limited.

Our study found that LPS-induced inflammation significantly upregulated the phosphorylation of STAT3, which further promoted the expression of GREM2. By silencing STAT3, we observed a reduction in both p-STAT3 levels and GREM2 expression. Dex exerts its regulatory effects in HT by inhibiting STAT3 phosphorylation, which is consistent with previous studies on the role of Grem2 in regulating the extent of inflammation after MI (myocardial infarction). These studies suggested that Grem2 limits the infiltration of inflammatory cells into damaged tissue, thereby improving cardiac function. Our research further demonstrated that Dex not only downregulates GREM2 expression by inhibiting STAT3 phosphorylation, but also significantly reduces the expression levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines such as TNF-α, IL-1β, and CCL2. These findings suggest that the role of GREM2 in inflammation is dependent on STAT3 activation and that GREM2 overexpression can reverse the anti-inflammatory effects of STAT3 knockdown.

In addition, in our immune infiltration analysis, we observed a correlation between GREM2 expression and specific B-cell subsets. As a secreted BMP antagonist, GREM2 can bind and inhibit BMP2/4/7 ligands, thereby regulating downstream SMAD1/5/8 signaling. , Review studies have reported that BMP signaling suppresses immunoglobulin (IgM, IgA, IgG) secretion from human naïve and memory B cells and limits their differentiation into plasma cells. , Based on this evidence, we speculate that reduced GREM2 expression may relieve inhibition of the BMP pathway, leading to relatively enhanced BMP activity, which in turn further restricts antibody production and plasma cell differentiation. This hypothesis is consistent with our clinical cohort findings, where both TPOAb and TgAb levels significantly decreased following Dex treatment. It is noteworthy that B cells themselves exhibit low levels of GREM2 expression according to public single-cell and transcriptomic data sets, suggesting that GREM2 may mainly originate from thyroid follicular epithelial or stromal cells and act in a paracrine manner to regulate B-cell function. Similar tissue-specific paracrine effects have been described in other disease models. For instance, in myocardial infarction, Grem2 was shown to suppress classical BMP signaling and limit inflammatory cell infiltration, highlighting the context-dependent and plastic nature of the GREM2–BMP axis in inflammatory homeostasis.

Through comprehensive enrichment analyses, we found that STAT3-related pathways play a critical role in the process of Dex treatment in HT. This finding was supported by subsequent transcription factor experiments. STAT3 is a key cytoplasmic transcription factor whose hyperactivation or loss of function is associated with various human diseases, including diabetes, lymphadenopathy, autoimmune anemia, primary hypothyroidism and cancer. In addition, STAT3 regulates cell differentiation, inflammation, and immune responses. Previous studies have shown that IFN-γ and IFN-α inflammatory pathways may contribute to the development of HT. In addition, studies have shown that STAT3 activation increases IL-17 levels and promotes Th17 cell differentiation, whereas Tubastatin A (TSA) or HDAC6i (ACY-1215) inhibits IL-17 levels, thereby affecting HT. Our research shows that STAT3 regulates GREM2 in HT and affects the inflammatory response. The interaction between STAT3 and GREM2 enhances inflammation in HT, thereby exacerbating disease progression. Previous studies have shown that STAT3 plays a key role in various diseases, including cancer, osteoporosis, nephropathy, and immune disorders. Our study further confirms the important role of STAT3 in regulating the inflammatory response in HT by modulating GREM2. However, research on the function of STAT3 in the treatment of HT remains limited. For the first time, our study demonstrates that Dex downregulates GREM2 expression and reduces inflammation in HT by inhibiting STAT3 phosphorylation, suggesting that STAT3 may be a critical target in the treatment of HT.

Importantly, our findings on the STAT3/GREM2 axis are not only of mechanistic significance but also carry potential implications for clinical translation. First, we observed that GREM2 expression in HT patients progressively decreased with prolonged Dex treatment and was correlated with improvements in classical clinical indicators (TSH, TPOAb, TgAb), suggesting that GREM2 may serve as a useful reference marker in evaluating the therapeutic response to Dex. Furthermore, STAT3 has been well established as a critical therapeutic target in various immune-related diseases, and multiple STAT3 inhibitors are currently under preclinical or clinical investigation. These agents act by directly or indirectly inhibiting STAT3 phosphorylation, thereby downregulating GREM2 expression, suppressing local thyroid inflammation, and improving disease progression. Collectively, these findings indicate that targeting the STAT3/GREM2 axis may provide a novel immunomodulatory strategy for HT. In parallel, our study, by exploring the use of local Dex injection in HT, has preliminarily revealed its potential molecular mechanisms and feasibility, laying the groundwork for future clinical validation and application.

This study has several limitations. First, the transcriptomic sample size was relatively small and reflected only the transcriptional effects after the first course of Dex treatment in HT, which may limit the robustness of the results. Although we validated our findings in an independent clinical cohort, larger sample sizes will be required to confirm these results. Second, we only validated the mechanisms at the cellular level and lacked in vivo studies, such as mouse models, to provide further mechanistic insight. Finally, although we focused on the STAT3/GREM2 signaling axis, the downstream pathways and their specific effects on immune cell function were not explored in detail. Future studies combining larger patient cohorts with in vivo experiments are warranted to address these limitations. Taken together, our study demonstrates that Dex suppresses inflammation in HT by regulating the STAT3/GREM2 signaling axis to achieve its therapeutic effects.

Conclusions

In this study, transcriptomic and machine learning analyses consistently identified GREM2 as a key gene associated with Dex treatment response in HT, with subsequent validation in an independent clinical cohort. Mechanistically, Dex reduced STAT3 phosphorylation, leading to downregulation of GREM2 and suppression of pro-inflammatory mediators, while immune infiltration analysis suggested a link to B cell–mediated immunity. Collectively, these findings demonstrate that the STAT3/GREM2 axis plays a central role in the therapeutic effects of Dex in HT, providing a mechanistic basis and theoretical support for its clinical application and laying the foundation for future translational and immunomodulatory strategies.
