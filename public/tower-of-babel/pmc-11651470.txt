Genetic prediction of the effect of gut microbiota on uveitis via blood metabolites: A mediated Mendelian randomization investigation
Authors: Xiaodong Xie, Weina Ren, Weiping Zhou, Xixi Zhang, Xiaoyu Deng, Xinyi Wang, Yufei Wu, Qinkang Lu
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
The gut microbiota (GM) may be associated with uveitis. However, the causal relationship between the GM and uveitis and whether blood metabolites act as mediators of the GM remain unclear. We extracted the GM, blood metabolites, and uveitis data from genome-wide association study (GWAS) summary data. We used Mendelian randomization (MR) to investigate the causal relationships among GM, blood metabolites, and uveitis. The primary statistical method used was the inverse variance weighted (IVW) method. In addition, we used 2-sample MR, bidirectional MR, 2-step method and multiple MR to explore whether blood metabolites were mediators of the association between the GM and uveitis. After removing confounding factors, the abundances of the order Bacillales and the genus Holdemanella are risk factors for uveitis, and the abundances of Peptococcus and Ruminococcaceae UCG010 are protective factors. The inverse analysis revealed that uveitis affected 6 GM taxa – 4 positively and 2 negatively. In addition, N-methyl proline and 2-hydroxy sebacate were identified as risk factors for uveitis, and N-formy1 phenylalanine, 1-ribosyl-imidazole acetate, 1-palmitoyl-2-arachidonoyl-GPE (16:0/20:4) and alpha-ketoglutarate/pyruvate were identified as protective factors for uveitis. Finally, there was a causal association between 3 GM taxa and 6 blood metabolites, with 6 positive and 2 negative effects. N-methylproline possessed the greatest mediated effect (9.41%) between Ruminococcaceae UCG010 and uveitis. These results provide new insights into the pathogenesis of uveitis and offer a new approach to uveitis prevention and treatment from GM and blood metabolites perspective.

1. Introduction

Uveitis is a common inflammatory disease of the eye that ultimately leads to severe visual impairment, and the incidence and severity of uveitis are increasing worldwide. The incidence of uveitis varies across regions and populations, with a global incidence of 50.45 cases per 100,000, according to relevant statistics.[1] Numerous variables, including genetic, infection, environmental, and immunological factors, are significant contributors to the pathophysiology of uveitis.[2] The current standard of care for uveitis involving the eyes consists of corticosteroids and immunosuppressive medications, such as cyclosporine and azathioprine.[3] However, these therapies are prone to relapse, have long treatment cycles and are expensive. Thus, it is critical to discover new biomarkers and targets that can aid in the diagnosis, prophylaxis, and investigation of the pathophysiological mechanisms of uveitis.

The most important immunological organ in the body is the gut, which is involved in complex immune responses. The human gut microbiota (GM) is a varied ecosystem mostly composed of bacteria, fungi, viruses, and archaea. It is linked to several intestinal and systemic disorders and is essential for human health. Many studies have shown that obesity[4] and inflammatory bowel disease[5] are associated with disruption of the GM ecosystem. In recent years, some scholars have found that some systemic diseases, such as liver disease,[6] cardiovascular disease,[7] and chronic kidney disease (CKD),[8] are also closely related to intestinal dysbiosis. Recently, with the proposed theory of the “gut-eye axis,” numerous scholars have proposed that the GM and its metabolites are closely related to the etiology of several eye diseases, including glaucoma,[9] central serous chorioretinopathy (CSC), age-related macular degeneration (AMD), retinal vascular occlusion (RVO), and uveitis.[10]

The etiology and predisposing factors of uveitis remain unclear, and its pathogenesis is highly heterogeneous. Uveitis may occur through 4 different mechanisms and is influenced by various factors in the GM, including molecular mimicry,[11,12] dysregulation of the GM,[13,14] elevated intestinal permeability, disturbance of the intestinal ocular axis barrier, and an imbalance between regulatory and effector T cells.[15,16] The metabolism of lipids, carbohydrates, and phosphatidylethanolamines is altered in the plasma of patients with Behcet disease (BD), with a notable increase in ether-linked lysophosphatidylethanolamines (EtherLPEs) and lysophosphatidylcholines (LPCs).[17] Another study revealed 15 metabolites whose expression differed between active Vogt-Koyanagi-Harada (VKH) patients and healthy individuals and 9 metabolites whose expression differed between inactive VKH patients and healthy individuals. Notably, 4 metabolites (sarcosine, D-mannose, stearic acid, and L-lysine) were identified as reliable biomarkers for differentiating patients with active VKH from healthy individuals.[18] These findings suggest that blood metabolites are not negligible in uveitis.

Recent studies have indicated that uveitis may be related to the GM and blood metabolites, but there are few investigations on the pathophysiology of this condition. However, the previous studies are not sufficiently convincing because of their small sample sizes and because they are mostly correlational studies. In addition, these investigations have not shown a causal link between the GM and blood metabolites and uveitis, and it is not known whether the GM participates in the pathogenesis of uveitis by influencing blood metabolites. Thus, we aimed to elucidate these correlations and pinpoint putative GM and blood metabolites that may function as preliminary diagnostic and therapeutic targets in clinical settings.

In epidemiological studies, Mendelian randomization (MR) is a statistical method used for inferring causality in research that evaluates causality between exposure factors and outcomes through the use of genetic variations as instrumental variables (IVs) that have strong correlations with exposure factors, which accounts for the influence of possible confounding variables and prevents reverse causality bias.[19] This study applied MR analysis to examine the causal relationship between the GM and uveitis, with the GM serving as the exposure and uveitis serving as the outcome. To provide a theoretical foundation for future studies on the pathogenesis of uveitis and identifying targets for uveitis prevention and treatment, we conducted bidirectional MR studies and mediation analyses using summary data derived from genome-wide association study (GWAS) of the GM, blood metabolites, and uveitis.

2. Materials and methods

2.1. Study design

A flowchart of our study and the 3 primary MR hypotheses[20] are displayed (Fig. 1): Assumption of association: Single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) are the genetic variations used for IVs, and there is a significant association between SNPs and exposure variables; Assumption of independence: Confounding has no effect on SNPs; and The exclusivity assumption: SNPs can influence results only by way of exposure variables. The data that we utilized for our research are freely downloadable, and the article and its supplements include all the derived results. In this investigation, 2-sample MR, bidirectional MR, 2-step method and multiple MR was utilized to examine the causal relationship between the GM and uveitis.

2.2. GWAS summary data sources

The human GM data for this study were obtained from the GWAS meta-analysis conducted by the MiBioGen collaboration (https://mibiogen.gcc.rug.nl/).[21] Characterization of 16S rRNA gene sequencing patterns and genome-wide genotyping information from participants were obtained from 18,340 samples from 24 cohorts, 78% of which were European, to explore the causal link between genetic diversity and GM using 211 known taxa, including 131 genera, 35 families, 20 orders, 16 classes and 9 phyla.[21]

The summary statistics for uveitis were acquired from the IEU Open GWAS database, whose detailed information is available on the website (https://gwas.mrcieu.ac.uk/). A total of 24,194,599 SNPs from 480,742 subjects (including 2616 cases and 478,126 controls), all from European populations,[22] were analyzed. This cohort study screened individuals by utilizing the International Classification of Diseases (ICD) diagnosis codes. All patients with uveitis in this study had iridocyclitis (anterior uveitis), and the original data consisted of the UK Biobank (mean age 56.8 years; 53.8% women and 46.2% men) and FinnGen (mean age 51.8 years; 56.3% women and 43.7% men) data sets. The GWAS catalogue database, established in 2008 by the National Human Genome Research Institute (NHGRI), is a widely used database designed to summarize and make key information available for GWAS.

To examine the intermediary role of blood metabolites in uveitis, we analyzed comprehensive data on 1400 blood metabolites from 8299 European participants.[23] Metabolic processes can impact the likelihood of developing diseases and offer potential targets for treatment. The GWAS included a total of 1091 blood metabolites and 309 metabolite ratios and revealed relationships between 248 genetic loci and 690 metabolites and between 69 loci and 143 metabolite ratios.[23] Of the 1091 blood metabolites analyzed, 850 were identified among 8 major pathways, including lipids, amino acids, nucleotides, cofactors, vitamins, carbs, peptides, energy, and exogenous chemicals.[23] The GWAS summary statistics are stored in the GWAS catalogue and can be found at the following link: https://www.ebi.ac.uk/gwas/. The accession numbers range from GCST90199621 to 90201020. The detailed characteristics are listed in Table S1, Supplemental Digital Content, http://links.lww.com/MD/O132.

2.3. Data extraction

Before performing MR, the genetic variations utilized needed to be verified as reflective of the GM, and qualified IV screening of SNPs was carried out to ensure the stability and accuracy of the results. Due to the rarity of typical IVs (P < 5 × 10-8), we selected SNPs linked to the GM with a significance level of P < 1 × 10-5, which is a common filtering condition for the GM that has been widely recognized in previous studies. Then, we used the “TwoSampleMR” software package to cluster independent SNPs with the linkage disequilibrium (LD) threshold established at r2 < 0.001 with a clumping range of 10,000 kb.[24,25] The resulting data included chromosomal (CHR), effect allele (EA), other allele (OA), effect size (β), standard error (SE), sample size (N), and P values. In addition, we calculated the F-statistic by using the formula F=R2(N−2)(1−R2) (1) and explained variance (R2) by using the formula R2=2β2(2β2+2N(SE)2) (2) to determine the validity of the relationships between the identified independent IVs and the GM. Finally, we selected SNPs with F-statistic values >10 to avoid any potential mild instrumental bias.[26] We used the same filtering conditions for uveitis and blood metabolites to select associated SNPs.

2.4. Primary MR analysis between GM and uveitis

We performed a 2-sample bidirectional MR study to evaluate the causal association between the GM and uveitis, known as the total effect. To determine how different IVs affected causal estimates, the Wald ratio (WR) method was used. When horizontal pleiotropy is not present, the primary approach for estimating the effect in MR studies is the inverse variance weighting (IVW) method.[27,28] The use of IVW assumes that all SNPs are qualified IVs, resulting in precise estimations. The MR findings are presented as odds ratio (OR) with associated 95% confidence interval (CI), and P < .05 was considered to indicate statistical significance.

Weighted median (WM), MR-Egger, simple mode, and weighted mode tests were also employed as supplementary techniques for our study. If the number of SNPs showing heterogeneity exceeded 50%, the WM method findings were considered significant causal effect values.[29] If more than 50% of the SNPs exhibited pleiotropy, the eventual findings of MR-Egger analysis were considered reliable.[30] Even in cases in which IVs are faulty, the weighted mode technique remains consistent when several comparable individual instrument causal effect estimations are derived from reliable instruments.[31] The empirical density function of causal estimation has an unweighted mode known as the simple mode.[27]

2.5. Mediation analysis

We performed a mediation analysis by applying 2-step MR[32] analysis to explore whether blood metabolites play a role in causing uveitis through the GM. We first identified the GM that had a significant causal effect on uveitis and determined the role of uveitis on the GM through bidirectional MR analysis. GM with positive or negative results could not be subjected to MR analysis. We then used the GM with positive results in the first step as the exposure and the 1400 blood metabolites as the outcome to explore the causal connection between the GM and blood metabolites. We further explored the causal connection between blood metabolites and uveitis by using blood metabolites causally related to the GM as an exposure and uveitis as an outcome. Finally, we performed multiple MR analysis to explore whether blood metabolites were the mediation factors in the pathway from GM to uveitis.

Overall effects included indirect effects (through mediators) and direct effects (without mediators).[33] The effect of the GM on uveitis is total, and the impact of the GM on uveitis via blood metabolites is indirect. The indirect effect is divided by the overall effect to determine the proportion of the mediating influence. If the indirect impact is opposite to the overall effect, the proportion of the mediating effect is not computed.

2.6. Sensitivity analyses

We assessed the heterogeneity of each SNP using the Cochran Q test at a significance level of P < .05 (higher heterogeneity and a greater possibility of directional pleiotropy are indicated by smaller P-values).[34,35] We conducted a detailed analysis of horizontal pleiotropy by using the MR-Egger intercept method[36] and the MR-PRESSO method[37] to verify the accuracy of the data on the causal association between the GM and uveitis. If any outliers were found, we eliminated them and reassessed the MR causal estimations. A P value of <.05 for the MR-Egger intercept indicated the presence of horizontal pleiotropy.

MR analyses were performed using R statistical software (version 4.3.3), and MR analysis was conducted with the “TwoSampleMR,” “ggplot2,” and “foreach” packages.[27] To identify appropriate IVs for this study, the GWAS Catalogue website (https://www.ebi.ac.uk/gwas/) was used. Potential confounding SNPs directly related to outcome were excluded prior to MR analysis. Fortunately, none of the SNPs showed any signs of removal after this assessment was performed.

2.7. Ethics statement

The study utilized data obtained from publicly accessible GWAS summary statistics. The relevant institutional review boards approved individual studies for each GWAS, and this study did not utilize data at the individual level. Therefore, the approval of a new ethics review board was not necessary.

3. Results

3.1. Selection of IVs associated with the GM and uveitis

First, we selected 2808 SNPs as IVs for 211 GM taxa, including 9 phyla (125 SNPs), 16 classes (223 SNPs), 20 orders (277 SNPs), 35 families (485 SNPs) and 131 genera (1698 SNPs). All SNPs showed sufficient validity (F-statistics between 14.58 and 88.42), and detailed information on 2808 SNPs for 211 GM taxa can be found in Table S2, Supplemental Digital Content, http://links.lww.com/MD/O132. We then detected 2679 SNPs in the reverse MR analysis (F-statistics between 19.54 and 869.21). The information for all SNPs is included in the Table S5, Supplemental Digital Content, http://links.lww.com/MD/O132. Again, we identified 2099 SNPs to analyze the causal association between 4 GM taxa and 1400 blood metabolites (F-statistics between 18.94 and 32.25). All SNP information can be found in the Table S8, Supplemental Digital Content, http://links.lww.com/MD/O132. Finally, we analyzed 6 metabolites causally associated with 4 GM taxa as exposures and uveitis as an outcome. We identified 149 SNPs (F-statistics between 19.51 and 659.98); detailed information is provided in the Table S11, Supplemental Digital Content, http://links.lww.com/MD/O132.

3.2. Causality and correlation between GM and uveitis

We used the IVW method to calculate the causal relationship between the GM and uveitis through a total of 4 GM taxa, consisting of 1 order and 3 genera, which were linked to uveitis (Fig. 2). The Bacillales order and Holdemanella genus were shown to be strongly associated with the risk of developing uveitis (OR = 1.202; 95% CI between 1.034 and 1.396; P = .017 and OR = 1.203; 95% CI between 1.008 and 1.437; P = .041, respectively), and Ruminococcaceae UCG010 and the Peptococcus genus were substantially inversely correlated with the likelihood of uveitis development (OR = 0.699; 95% CI between 0.511 and 0.958; P = .026 and OR = 0.791; 95% CI between 0.683 and 0.951; P = .002, respectively). When assessing the causal effect of uveitis on the GM (Fig. 2), the relative abundance of the most important taxa, namely, 1 order, 1 phylum, 1 family, and 1 genus increased following uveitis. The Coriobacteriia class, Coriobacteriales order, and Coriobacteriaceae family belong to the same phylum and showed comparable effect sizes. The genus Desulfovibrio was the most affected by uveitis (OR = 1.060; 95% CI between 1.009 and 1.113; P = .021). In addition, the abundance of 2 genera was reduced by uveitis, and the reduction of LachnospiraceaeUCG001 was the most significant (OR = 0.935; 95% CI between 0.893 and 0.980; P = .005). 3.3. Mediation analyses of 1400 blood metabolites

Our analysis revealed that only 6 blood metabolites were causally associated with uveitis, and these 6 metabolites were closely related to 3 genera, namely, Holdemanella, Peptococcus, and Ruminococcaceae UCG010 (Figs. 2 and 3). Holdemanella, which has deleterious effects on uveitis, decreases 1-ribosyl-imidazoleacetate (OR = 0.907; 95% CI between 0.823 and 0.999; P = .049) and 1-palmitoyl-2-arachidonoyl-GPE (16:0/20:4) (OR = 0.856; 95% CI between 0.758 and 0.967; P = .013) levels and increases alpha-ketoglutarate/pyruvate levels (OR = 1.151; 95% CI between 1.038 and 1.276; P = .008). Peptococcus affects uveitis by increasing the levels of 1-palmitoyl-2-arachidonoyl-GPE (16:0/20:4) (OR = 1.100; 95% CI between 1.011 and 1.196; P = .027), N-formylphenylalanine (OR = 1.097; 95% CI between 1.002 and 1.200; P = .0451) and alpha-ketoglutarate/pyruvate (OR = 1.096; 95% CI between 1.003 and 1.198; P = .044). In addition, we found that Ruminococcaceae UCG010 has a protective effect against uveitis by increasing the levels of N-methylproline (OR = 1.203; 95% CI between 1.006 and 1.438; P = .043) and 2-hydroxysebacate (OR = 1.197; 95% CI between 1.001 and 1.432; P = .049).

4. Discussion

It is challenging to adequately summarize the intestinal microbiota that influences uveitis in an observational study due to the variety of uveitis types and the complexity of the GM. Dysregulation of the gut-eye-microbiome axis may result in impairment of the intestinal epithelial barrier, leading to the invasion of dangerous bacteria and their byproducts. We assessed the possible causal relationship between GM taxa and uveitis from a host genetic standpoint using MR analysis, and we showed that GM taxa modulate uveitis susceptibility at the order and genus levels. This study assessed the impact of the GM on uveitis, categorizing it as either beneficial or harmful. However, the specific mechanism by which the GM influences uveitis remains unknown. We hypothesized that blood metabolites are mediators of the GM. Recent studies[38] have confirmed the correlation between the GM and uveitis. Microecological dysbiosis, an alteration from a normal core microbiome to an abnormal GM, has been associated with intestinal and extraintestinal autoimmune diseases, such as uveitis. Current data are restricted to observational research, making it challenging to confirm causality. The purpose of this study was to demonstrate the causal relationship between the GM and uveitis. We utilized the MR method to explore the connection between the GM and uveitis using available GWAS data to determine whether their causal link is influenced by blood metabolites.

Our findings suggest that intestinal dysbiosis has a causal relationship with uveitis (risk and protective factors), and this relationship is mediated through blood metabolites. According to our findings, the genera RuminococcaceaeUCG00 and Peptococcus are protective factors against uveitis, whereas the order Bacillales and the genus Holdemanella are risk factors. Bacillales[39] is a bacterium with pathogenic potential that can cause intraocular inflammation, which partly explains its increased risk for developing uveitis. The Holdemanella genus positively influenced uveitis and had the greatest impact in our study. Clinical studies have shown a strong positive association between the Holdemanella genus and cancer[40] and autoimmune diseases, such as rheumatoid arthritis,[41] and autoimmune diseases, can also lead to uveitis. The genus Ruminococcaceae is recognized for its advantageous health properties and is 1 of the main probiotics. According to our results, the abundance of the genus Ruminococcaceae was negatively correlated with uveitis, and the genus Ruminococcaceae was protective against diabetic retinopathy[42] and glaucoma.[43] In addition, correlation research indicated that the genus Ruminococcaceae was negatively associated with proinflammatory cytokines and other injury factors, ameliorating mucosal inflammation and thus protecting intestinal barrier function.[44] Finally, our findings revealed a protective effect of the genus Peptococcus against uveitis. The findings of relevant research revealed that the genus Peptococcus has a protective effect against endocarditis and attenuates the progression of inflammatory diseases.[45]

Our MR work offers genetic evidence that the cause-and-effect connection between the GM and uveitis is mediated by several blood metabolites. Previous biomarkers associated with uveitis have focused on protein,[46] RNA,[47] and cytokine levels,[48] and few have used blood metabolites as biomarkers. However, our findings emphasize the causal role of blood metabolites in uveitis. We identified 6 metabolites causally linked to uveitis. 1-Ribosyl-imidazoleacetate has been found to be protective against ischemic stroke,[49] but it has been less well studied. Thus, we hypothesize that it is equally protective against uveitis as a beneficial metabolite, but further studies on its mechanism of action are needed. N-methylproline, a metabolite of the intestinal flora, can be detected in blood and urine in response to the activation of peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor-alpha (PPAR-α) and is intimately linked to alterations in body function.[50] It has also been shown that N-methylproline is negatively correlated with the occurrence of CKD.[51] In the present study, it was identified as a risk factor for uveitis. However, the mechanism of action is unclear and requires further study. Intestinal bacteria synthesize proteogenic amino acids through protein fermentation, resulting in the production of a range of metabolites that are known to have beneficial or deleterious effects on the host. Our finding that N-formylphenylalanine has a protective effect against uveitis suggests a significant surge in glutamate metabolism. 1-Palmitoyl-2-arachidonoyl-GPE (16:0/20:4) is a lipid metabolite that was identified in a systematic MR study as a potentially helpful mediator in the development of atopic dermatitis. It is a promising target for atopic dermatitis treatment and prevention, with no anticipated negative side effects.[52] In another investigation, 1-palmitoyl-2-arachidonoyl-GPE (16:0/20:4) was found to consistently correlate negatively with body mass index, waist circumference, and percentage of body fat.[53] These findings partially explain the mechanism by which it is a protective factor against uveitis. We found that 2-hydroxysebacate, which is an oxidation product of sebacic acid, was the risk factor with the highest risk for uveitis. However, it has been poorly studied, and its mechanism of action has not been elucidated. Interestingly, the alpha-ketoglutarate (AKG)/pyruvate ratio was the strongest protective factor against uveitis. In several metabolic processes, including the tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle, amino acid catabolism and anabolism, and collagen production, AKG is an essential intermediary.[54] AKG is a significant metabolite in the mitochondrial TCA cycle that plays several roles in physiological processes, including anti-inflammatory and antioxidant activities, and the serum levels of AKG decrease with age. AKG promotes mitochondrial autophagy and inhibits ROS production.[55] Therefore, we hypothesized that supplementing the body with AKG to reduce pyruvate levels could prevent uveitis. Finally, we found that increasing Ruminococcaceae UCG010 increased N-methylproline levels, thus preventing uveitis, and it had the highest mediation ratio of 9.41%.

Furthermore, genetic evidence of a link between the GM and blood metabolites was provided by our mediator study. To our knowledge, no previous study has causally linked the GM to blood metabolites. These results elucidated the causal relationship between the GM and blood metabolites. We also highlight the possibility of using targeted regulation of the GM as a tactic to enhance the health of people with uveitis and indicate the need for more research in the future to fully understand the interactions among the GM, blood metabolites, and uveitis.

Nevertheless, our study has certain limitations. Microbiome profiles in the MiBioGen collaboration were defined using 16S rRNA gene sequencing, which only offers resolution from genus to phylum. Macrogenomic sequencing results offer enhanced information, particularly for individual species. Determining the relevance of our findings to different ethnic groups is necessary, considering that over 70% of participants in the study were of European ancestry. Moreover, variations in lifestyle, host metabolism, and indigenous microbiota exist throughout global populations. Disparity in genetic diversity distribution across various ethnic or racial groupings might result in population stratification, thereby skewing study outcomes.[20] The use of gut flora data from macrogenetic sequencing is critical, and more diverse populations should be included to enhance the generalizability of our study. Third, although we found that some intestinal flora had promoting and protective effects on uveitis through blood metabolites, the exact mechanism of action remains unclear and requires further study. In addition, we did not categorize uveitis into various subtypes, and further studies with larger sample sizes are needed to validate our findings.

5. Conclusions

Our study is the first to thoroughly evaluate the causal relationships among the GM, blood metabolites, and uveitis. These findings suggest the importance of a potential mechanism underlying the association between the GM and uveitis, whereby the GM acts on uveitis through blood metabolites. The results offer new perspectives for microbiome-based therapy for uveitis and therapies focused on metabolites.
