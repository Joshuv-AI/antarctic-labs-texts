Abstract
. Federated learning is a decentralized approach that enables collaborative model training across multiple devices while preserving data privacy. It has shown significant potential in various domains, including healthcare and personalized recommendation systems. However, most existing work on federated recommendation systems has focused primarily on improving accuracy, with limited attention to fairness and diversity. In this paper, we introduce FedFlex , a federated recommender system for Netflix-style TV series recommendations. FedFlex integrates two state-of-the-art matrix factorization algorithms for personalized fine-tuning. FedFlex also applies Maximal Marginal Relevance (MMR) to re-rank items and enhance diversity. We conduct extensive experiments comparing recommendations generated by SVD and BPR algorithms. In a live two-week user study, participants received two recommendation lists: List A, based on SVD or BPR, and List B, a re-ranked version emphasizing diversity. Participants were asked to click on the movies they were interested in watching. Our findings demonstrate that FedFlex effectively introduces diverse content, such as new genres, into recommendations without necessarily compromising user satisfaction.



1.
Introduction

Top-N recommender systems (RSs) influence much of how we experience the online world and aim to solve the information overload problem. By training machine learning models on large amounts of user data, it becomes possible to turn the database of all possible Netflix series into an ordered list of what a user is most likely to watch. However, highly personalized RSs (called over-personalization) can have negative side effects
(
Pariser 2012
)
. Firstly, processing a large amount of user data comes with privacy concerns
(
Slokom et al. 2019
;
Slokom et al. 2021
)
. In the case of Netflix, the user is not in control of what parts of their viewing history they share with the company. Secondly, by creating highly personalized recommendations a
filter bubble
can be created. This is an effect in which users only receive recommendations for what they already like, which can stifle exploration and reinforce existing preferences and biases
(
Pariser 2012
)
.

Federated learning is a machine learning paradigm that enables the preservation of privacy by training models on user data locally and only sharing the tuned parameters with a global model
(
Qiang 2019
;
Nguyen et al. 2024
)
. Based on this global model, recommendations can be generated without user data needing to be shared at any point
(
Lin et al. 2021
;
Anelli et al. 2021
)
. To further strengthen the privacy aspect of federated learning, techniques like
differential privacy
, where noise is added to model updates, can be applied to prevent inferring user data from the shared parameters
(
Dwork 2008
)
.

There are several studies exploring different methods for federated recommendation. One study focuses on reducing communication costs in federated learning through a novel framework that substantially lowers overhead when transmitting model updates
(
Nguyen et al. 2024
)
. Another study addresses privacy preservation, allowing users to explicitly control the amount of sensitive data they share
(
Anelli et al. 2021
)
.
However, when it comes to going beyond accuracy, particularly in terms of fairness and diversity, very few works exist. One fairness-oriented example is
FairFed
, which promotes group fairness through a fairness-aware aggregation method
(
Ezzeldin et al. 2022
)
. For diversity,
FDRS

(
Li et al. 2023
)
introduces a graph-based approach to generate diverse recommendations in a federated learning setting.
We observe that many existing works suffer from
reproducibility issues
: source code is often unavailable, or the published code is not aligned with the dataset or algorithm described in the paper. To the best of our knowledge, responsible federated recommendation remains in its infancy. We found no live user study that targets fairness or diversity in a federated recommendation context.

Our study aims to address this gap in the research by developing a user study with live participants on a federated learning-based recommender with a focus on diverse recommendations. The main contribution is our approach, implementation, and evaluation of a diverse federated recommender developed for a live user study. We do this according to the following research questions:

•

RQ1:

How to design a “reproducible” federated recommender?

•

RQ2:

How can we go beyond accuracy to promote diversity in a federated recommender system for a live user study?

•

RQ3:

How to evaluate a diverse federated recommender in a live user study?

2.
Background and Related Work

In this section, we briefly cover a select number of works related to recommendation based on federated learning.

2.1.
Federated Recommendations

Federated recommendation has emerged as a privacy-focused alternative to traditional centralized recommender systems. Early signs of the potential of federated learning can be traced back to a blog post from researchers at Google
(
Goo 2025
)
testing out federated learning technologies and discussing progress they have made in developing the underlying algorithms. In the literature, existing recommender technologies are commonly adapted for a federated environment. For example, Federated Collaborative Filtering (FCF)
(
ud din et al. 2019
)
proposes a framework with the goal of ranking items with implicit feedback. This is achieved by sharing gradient updates instead of raw data, obscuring the user data on which the model is trained. FedRec
(
Lin et al. 2021
)
follows up on FCF by proposing a generic federated learning framework for rating prediction with explicit feedback. Their framework is flexible and can be applied to a wide variety of factorization-based models.

2.2.
Fairness and Diversity in Federated Learning

Research on fairness and diversity within federated learning is sparse. Existing works often only address one aspect of fairness, and there is only one work focusing on diversity to the best of our knowledge. One such work is the aforementioned FairFed
(
Ezzeldin et al. 2022
)
. FairFed aims to tackle the problem of group fairness, a problem often solved by having access to sensitive information which is not the case in federated learning. In their approach, they train a model using fairness-aware aggregation with local debiasing and then evaluate the fairness of the global model on a local dataset. Their work raises the concern of a difference between local and global fairness due to the distributed nature of the system.

FDRS
(
Li et al. 2023
)
, on the other hand, focuses on introducing diversity into federated learning. Their proposed framework for a Federated Diversified Recommender System (FDRS) is based on heterogeneous graph convolutional networks (HGCN). They first construct heterogeneous user-item graphs, based on which the HGCN learns rich user and item representations. Clients can then generate diversity-aware recommendations locally.

3.
Methodology

This section details our methodology for building and evaluating our proposed application: FedFlex. We first describe its architecture based on SytfBox
1
1

1

SyftBox:
https://github.com/OpenMined/syftbox
. We then outline the core recommending algorithms considering also the diversity implementation, and finally, the design of the live user study to assess FedFlex performance.

3.1.
SyftBox

Our experimental methodology is built on SyftBox, an open-source protocol based on PySyft
(
Ziller et al. 2021
)
for deploying privacy-preserving computations across a network. Using a network-first architecture, it enables computations on distributed datasets without centralizing them. While the platform is modular and agnostic to the specific Privacy-Enhancing Technology (PET), our work employs its Federated Learning capabilities.

We selected SyftBox to operationalize our federated recommender system as it directly addresses the core privacy challenge of our research: training a model on sensitive user interaction data (e.g., viewing history, clicks) while ensuring the data remains on users’ device. To facilitate this, SyftBox orchestrates secure communication between a central aggregator server and the participants. Its permission and synchronization system is then used to exchange the results of local training – specifically, the model updates – without granting direct access to the raw private data.

For our live user study, this approach lowers the entry barrier for participants. SyftBox abstracts the complexities of the federated learning setup, allowing us to package our recommender system into a self-contained application. The participants are only required to install and run this application, as SyftBox automates the subsequent steps of the federated learning process.

3.2.
FedFlex

FedFlex, our application, is a federated recommendation system for Netflix series. Figure
1
shows the flow of data throughout the application, with private data (that is, not accessible by anyone but the user) marked with a lock icon. The system consists of two types of actors: live user participants and a virtual aggregator. The participants represent the users of the system, and the aggregator is the orchestrator of the global model, which is trained on results from the participants’ local training.

Initially, the participant loads their Netflix viewing history to be stored in the private local folder. Then, once the application is deployed and running, it performs a local computation to convert this raw viewing history into a vector of ratings for each show watched. This is done based on how much of a show is watched and in what time frame. For example, if a participant watched three episodes of a series in a week, the show will get a rating of five stars in the participant’s local vector.

It is noteworthy that due to this design decision, movies get a low rating by default, and the application focuses on recommending TV series only. Once a vector that represents the participant’s viewing history is made, it is processed through collaborative filtering based on matrix factorization. Differential privacy is applied over the local training results by adding a controlled amount of noise to the model updates shared with the Aggregator. This is to prevent the reverse engineering of user data. Optionally, differential privacy can also be applied at the aggregator’s end during the aggregation process as a second layer of privacy insurance. The aggregator then updates the global model with the gradients shared by the participants. Then, the participant can generate recommendations locally by combining the global model and their own ratings.

FedFlex uses static data from whats-on-netflix.com gathered in December 2024, which is when the project was initially created, to initialize the model and generate recommendations.

3.2.1.
Recommendation Algorithms

FedFlex was originally developed with collaborative filtering based on Singular Value Decomposition (SVD) as a fine-tuning method. This method is based on matrix factorization and uses explicit feedback to adjust the user and item vectors based on individual items. To obtain a more varied baseline to compare with, we also made the application work with a FedBPR
2
2

2

https://github.com/sisinflab/FedBPR
-inspired algorithm. Bayesian personalized Ranking (BPR)
(
Rendle et al. 2009
)
is also based on matrix factorization, but instead adjusts the vectors by using implicit feedback, comparing a pair of positive (clicked) and negative (non-clicked) items. We chose this method because it is also based on matrix factorization, making it easier to introduce. We leveraged the idea that matrix factorization algorithms can be easily swapped out as long as the dimensions of the input and output vectors are preserved, alongside the existing open-source implementation of a federated matrix factorization algorithm to use as reference.

3.2.2.
Diversity Implementation

To account for diversity, we opted to go with a re-ranking approach as they are the easiest to implement in an existing system. For this, we found Maximal Marginal Relevance (MMR)
(
Carbonell and Stewart 1999
)
to be a straightforward solution. To measure similarity, we opted to use the titles for the embedding using Python’s
sentence-transformers
module with the
all-MiniLM-L6-v2
model since it is made for small bits of text.
We chose title-based embeddings because recommending sequels or series with identical titles is the most straightforward and least diverse approach. While we considered using richer data, such as genre and cast, to enhance diversity, this was beyond the scope of our initial study.

Firstly, the re-ranker always maintains the item with the highest predicted rating. Then, all future items are compared with the list of final recommendations and generate a similarity score using cosine similarity. This similarity score is then used as a penalty, meaning that the higher the similarity to already recommended items, the less likely an item is to be recommended. We chose our parameter lambda to be 0.3, meaning that the predicted rating weighs for 30% towards an item being recommended, and the similarity penalty accounts for 70%. This decision was made due to this being a live study, and it being difficult to make accurate recommendations for a small user base because of the cold start problem. For that reason, we wanted to focus on generating highly diverse results and seeing how it would impact the users clicking behavior.

To execute the user study, we introduced a web application pictured in Figure
2
. This web application runs locally for each participant. In this web application, the participant is presented with two columns of five thumbnails of TV series. The column on the left represents the series with the highest predicted rating in order, and the column on the right represents the top-5 recommendations after re-ranking them using MMR. These are shown simultaneously in the web application so that after the experiment, we can gather data on perceived differences between the two lists.

The users are then instructed to click on the shows they would be likely to watch if Netflix were to recommend them. These clicks, the time they were performed, the participant who clicked, and the two recommended lists are then stored and shared with the aggregator for the purpose of this experiment. The viewing history of the participants remains private.

3.2.4.
User Study

For this experiment, we invited participants by asking family, friends, and other acquaintances through a variety of methods, such as social networks (e.g., Slack, others), and asking individually, and no incentive was offered. We acknowledge that this could cause biased results, due to having spoken about the topic beforehand or being familiar with the subject matter. However, this is a risk we accepted due to the main focus of our contribution being the approach, and a small sample size inherently leading to less conclusive results. As part of the invitation, textual instructions as well as video instructions were provided to support the participants on retrieving their viewing history data from the Netflix website, how to install the FedFlex application, and how to run the study.

In summary, over a period of two weeks, 13 participants ran the experiment at least once (i.e., sharing their data to train the global model), with 10 successfully completing the full experiment by sharing their preferred clicks with us. The main feedback we received from participants was that the system based on SyftBox requires users to have either Linux or macOS as their operating system, which limited the pool of potential participants. Relying on just 13 participants means the amount of data used to train the models was relatively small, likely resulting in less accurate recommendations, an expected limitation of a live user study, which cannot match the data volumes available to large-scale platforms like Netflix.

The user study consists of the participants running two versions of FedFlex, one with the SVD-based finetuning algorithm and the other with the BPR-based finetuning algorithm.
We split the apps into two separate versions to make it easier to train two separate global models, which are publicly available as part of our contribution

4.
Results

As part of our experiments, we first aimed to determine whether participants would find interesting TV series and click on them. Second, by applying post-processing with MMR, we introduced more diverse recommendations at the top of the list. The goal was to observe whether participants would also like and click on items from the post-processed list.
In Table
1
, we present the metrics used to evaluate our experiment’s performance. For accuracy metrics such as CTR, P@5, and nDCG@5, both SVD and BPR outperform their reranked counterparts, SVD+MMR and BPR+MMR. As for diversity, the results are mixed: while intra-list diversity (ILD) improves slightly with MMR for BPR and remains stable for SVD, the coverage ratio increases for SVD+MMR but decreases for BPR+MMR. We attribute these mixed results to the small sample size, which may limit the conclusiveness of our findings.
Looking at the raw data, however, we find that both reranked methods produced more varied recommendations. SVD+MMR recommended 43 unique items, compared to 35 unique items for SVD alone. Similarly, BPR+MMR recommended 58 unique items, compared to 46 for BPR. We also observed that more reranked items were clicked than non-reranked ones. However, in the case of BPR, this increase was smaller relative to the increase in uniquely recommended items, resulting in a lower coverage ratio despite a broader exposure to different items.

shows that we did succeed in introducing diversity into the recommendations. The reranked recommendations consistently introduce new genres to the users, whilst still adhering to strong preferences (i.e., if a user has many high-scoring items in the drama genre, the reranked recommendations will still mostly contain drama). Note that especially BPR with MMR introduces many new genres. This is mainly due to the difference between SVD and BPR. During experimentation, we noticed that due to the nature of BPR – being based on pairwise comparison and applying a sigmoid gradient – predicted ratings were much more bounded compared to SVD, which would often have very high individual ratings. This also means that SVD would occasionally rate shows for users very high and overwhelm the reranker to an extent, which explains why, in the case of SVD, the reranker sticks closer to the original recommended genres.

Finally, we asked our participants to fill out a questionnaire after the experiment ended. From this, we learned that users were not sure that the recommendations were personalized for them, which again can be attributed to the small sample size. The majority did not have a clear preference between the two recommendation lists, indicating that the introduced diversity did not substantially decrease user satisfaction.

5.
Conclusion and Future Work

In this work, we propose
FedFlex
, the first live federated recommender study for Netflix-style TV series, to the best of our knowledge. FedFlex uses BPR and or SVD for personalized fine-tuning and applies Maximal Marginal Relevance (MMR) to re-rank items and improve diversity. Our results demonstrate that we can deliver accurate recommendations while keeping user click data local. MMR also contributes to promoting more diverse recommendations.

We acknowledge the limitations of our study, primarily the small sample size. A larger-scale, longitudinal deployment could offer deeper insights. Additionally, we believe that click-based evaluation would benefit from pre-trained models with personalized parameters (e.g., user-specific MMR
\lambda
). Future work could also incorporate richer data, such as explicit ratings and enhanced similarity features using cast and genre metadata, to improve personalization and diversity further.
