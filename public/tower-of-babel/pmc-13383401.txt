Pituitary blastoma in a dog: comparative clinical, imaging, and pathologic features of a newly recognized and rare human entity
Authors: Giuseppe Giglia, Cristian Falzone, Mariagiovanna Lorena Graceffa, Silvia Uccella, Maria Teresa Mandara
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPituitary blastoma (PitB) is an exceedingly rare tumor originating from the anterior pituitary gland, first documented in 2008 in humans. It is considered pathognomonic for DICER1 syndrome, primarily affecting children, although cases in adults have been noted. Clinical manifestations vary widely, attributed to hormone hypersecretion (such as Cushing’s syndrome) and mass-related effects (like visual impairment and intracranial pressure changes). Histopathologically, PitB exhibits distinct features: proliferating Rathke’s epithelium with rosette formations, adenohypophyseal neuroendocrine cells expressing ACTH and occasionally GH, and primitive small blastemal cells. This report describes the first case of PitB in a dog, presenting with ACTH-dependent hypercortisolism. The canine patient exhibited clinical signs consistent with ACTH-hypersecretion related to a pituitary mass. Histologically, the pituitary mass revealed hallmark features resembling human PitB. Immunohistochemistry confirmed ACTH expression and nuclear expression of TBX19 (TPIT), supporting a corticotroph lineage. CAM 5.2 expression in tubule-forming cells suggested an immature epithelial origin, likely from Rathke’s pouch. Although DICER1 mutation analysis was not performed due to technical limitations with FFPE tissue, this case raises the possibility of a similar pathogenetic mechanism in dogs. This canine case expands the known species range of PitB and highlights important clinical, morphological and immunohistochemical parallels with the human counterpart, providing new insights into comparative pituitary tumor pathology.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40478-026-02281-3.

Introduction

Pituitary blastoma (PitB) is a rare tumor of the anterior pituitary gland, described for the first time in 2008 [21] and has been identified as a pathognomonic lesion of the human DICER1 syndrome [3, 4, 17]. PitB is an embryogenic tumor typically observed in children under 24 months of age with DICER1 gene mutation, but cases in adults have been described [2, 3]. The human clinical presentation encompasses a variety of symptoms and signs, due to hormone hypersecretion (Cushing’s syndrome) and mass-effect related disturbances (visual impairment and nervous signs related to increased intracranial pressure) [2, 5–7, 12]. PitB main histopathological features include the presence of three cellular populations: (1) proliferating Rathke’s epithelium with rosette and tubule formation, (2) adenohypophyseal neuroendocrine cells, which often express ACTH and, less frequently, GH, and (3) primitive blastemal cells [1, 2, 4, 20, 21, 23]. Until now, PitB has not been reported in dogs. However, proliferative lesions of the anterior pituitary gland are relatively common in dogs [15, 16]. Particularly, brachycephalic dogs seem to be predisposed in developing such lesions. These proliferations can often manifest as either hyperplastic or neoplastic lesions, and are often associated with hormone hypersecretion syndrome [8, 15, 16]. Proliferative pituitary lesions are more often reported in old dogs with an increase in the median age from hyperplastic to benign and malignant primary pituitary neoplasm [15]. Secondary (metastatic) pituitary tumors are exceedingly rare in dogs and their knowledge is still limited to few case reports [22, 24]. This manuscript describes for the first time the clinical, imaging and pathologic features of a PitB in a dog, suggesting possible similarities in all these aspects with the human counterpart.

Clinical history, neurological examination and diagnostic imaging

An 8-year-old crossbreed dog was presented with a history of polyuria and polydipsia (PU/PD) for about 8 months, at the veterinary clinic Diagnostica Piccoli Animali, Zugliano (VI), Italy. Referring veterinarian performed routine complete blood count (CBC), biochemistry profile and a low dose ATCH suppression test, that showed lack of suppression (cortisol at three different times was respectively 2.5 µg/dl, 2.5 µg/dl, and 1.9 µg/dl; IR 0.9–4.5 µg/dl). Results were consistent with a hyperadrenocorticism, and the dog was started on Trilostane therapy (Vetoryl, 1 mg/kg BID), with no improvement of PU/PD, despite an increase over months of the Trilostane (Vetoryl, 2 mg/kg BID). About 6 months lather the dog manifested a tonic-clonic generalized epileptic seizure, with a short post-ictal phase during which the dog was slightly disorientated and partly blind, according to the owners; three other consecutive seizures followed three weeks later and antiepileptic therapy was then started (phenobarbital, 3 mg/kg BID). Physical and neurological examinations performed about 5 days after the last seizuring event were within normal range, except for the bilaterally absent menace response; no pupillary light reflex (PLR) or visual deficits were identified. Based on the clinical and neurological signs, in association with the laboratory findings, an intracranial lesion and more specifically a pituitary tumour was suspected, and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI; Hitachi AIRIS light 0.25T) was performed with FSE T1, FSE T2, FLAIR, 3D sequences in the three spatial planes, with the T1 WI repeated after intravenous administration of paramagnetic contrast (gadolinium). The MRI revealed an extra-axial mass arising from the sella turcica, developing dorsally along the midline in the middle cranial fossa, with a well-defined margin, an overall rounded shape and maximum diameter of about 14 mm (Fig. 1). The mass was weakly hyperintense in T2-weighted and FLAIR sequences, isointense in T1 ones and, after gadolinium, showed a strong and diffuse contrast enhancement. The mass resulted in marked compression of the thalamus with partial stenosis of the third ventricle and mild compression of the rostral mesencephalon. The diagnosis was that of a middle cranial fossa neoplasm, most likely referable to a pituitary tumour with marked thalamic compression. Radiotherapy and surgery options were both discussed with the owners, who elected for surgical removal of the suspected neoplastic mass. Before the surgery, further hematological tests and urinalysis were performed; more specifically hematology showed mild erythrocytosis (HCT: 63.9%; RI: 37.3–61.7%) and lymphopenia (799 K/µl; reference interval - RI: 1050–5000 K/µl), suggestive of subtle stress leukogram. Biochemistry showed a moderate increase in alanine aminotransferase (ALT) activity (405 U/l; RI: 22–78 U/l) and aspartate aminotransferase (AST) (98 U/l; RI: 21–44 U/l), a marked increase in alkaline phosphatase (ALP) activity (9379 U/l; RI: 16–119 U/l) and gamma-glutamyl transferase (GGT) activity (64.9 U/l; RI: 1.2–10.9 U/l) due to hyperadrenocorticism, as well as a mild increase of phosphorus (7.52 mg/dl; RI: 2.6–6.2 U/l). Urinalysis showed urine specific gravity (USG) of 1014 and severe proteinuria 5.7 (RI < 0.5), suggestive of hyperadrenocorticism. Trilostane was suspended two days before the surgery and an endogenous ACTH test was performed (276 pg/ml; RI: 9.0–67.7 pg/ml).

The dog underwent a transsphenoidal hypophysectomy, as previously described [12–14]. After surgery ACTH test was repeated (33.2 pg/ml; RI 9.0-67.7 pg/ml) showing a relevant postoperative decrease due the hypophysectomy; oral hormone substitution therapy consisted of hydrocortisone (0.5 mg/kg QID for the first 24 h and TID from the second day), and L-Thyroxine (10 µg/kg BID). Desmopressin was administered in a dose of a single drop in the conjunctival sac, three times a day. Other postoperative medications included amoxicillin/clavulanic acid. Serum electrolyte concentrations, arterial blood pressure, fluid input, urine output, urine specific gravity, and bodyweight were measured every day, and these parameters guided the administration of IV fluids. Overall, straight after the surgery the dog neurologically deteriorated, becoming severely depressed and non-ambulatory tetraparetic. During the first week only a mild neurologic improvement was noticed, and a MRI was repeated 10 days after the surgery (1.5 T Philips Achieva) with T2WI, SWI, DWI and T1WI and identified a mild, intra-axial changes of the thalamus and also the rostral mesencephalon, that were hypointense and hyperintensity on T2 WI, mildly hyperintense on T1WI and more evidently hypointense on SWI, particularly distributed along the midline (Fig. 1S; Supplemental material); the same area was also mildly hyperintense on DWI and partly hypointense on ADC map. These changes were mostly compatible with a parenchymal lesion of vascular nature, haemorrhagic and partly ischemic. Despite a continuous but slow neurological improvement, the owners elected for euthanasia 25 days after surgery, because the dog still required support to walk and at times showed profound depression in his mental status. The surgically removed mass was subsequently submitted to the veterinary neuropathology laboratory at the Department of Veterinary Medicine of the University of Perugia, Italy.

Pathology

Histology

A tissue sample of irregular shape, 16 × 8 × 4 mm, white with black to brown marginal area was received at the neuropathology laboratory. Additionally, 2 fragments of about 3 mm in diameter were also retrieved in the submission and processed. Tissue samples were trimmed and after routine processing and paraffin embedding, 3–4 μm sections were routinely stained with Hematoxylin and Eosin for examination on light microscopy (Olympus BX53). The histologic examination of all samples revealed newly formed tissue consisting of various cell populations. A cell population (20% of the cells) consisted of cylindrical cells arranged to form rosettes or tubules. They showed poorly defined cytoplasmic margins, moderate amount of eosinophilic cytoplasm, basal oval nuclei with irregularly dispersed chromatin; these features were considered consistent with a morphology reminiscent of the Rathke’s pouch epithelium. Next to the previous, a second and main cell population was represented by large cells arranged to form sheets and small lobules supported by fine vascular stroma (70% of the cell population). The large cells showed generally defined margins, moderate to high amounts of light basophilic to eosinophilic cytoplasm to clear cytoplasm and central oval nucleus with irregularly dispersed chromatin (“salt and pepper” pattern); this morphology was considered to be compatible with adenohypophyseal neuroendocrine cells. Finally, small cells with a high nuclear to cytoplasmic ratio, scant eosinophilic cytoplasm, central, hyperchromatic oval nucleus with finely dispersed chromatin (10% of the cell population) were scattered and admixed with the two cell populations previously described; this morphology was consistent with undifferentiated blastema-like cells. Neuroendocrine cells showed marked anisocytosis and anisokaryosis. The mitotic count was 13 on 2.37 mm2 (10 HPF; FN 22/40x) and mitoses often limited to the neuroendocrine and blastema-like cells. Additionally, multifocal foci of necrosis were also present associated with mild infiltrates of neutrophils and lymphocytes and scattered plasma cells. The morphological features of the tumour are depicted in Fig. 2. Based on the presence of these three different cell populations, a presumptive diagnosis of a PitB was established.

Electron microscopy

To further investigate the morphological aspects of the canine PitB, 1 mm3 fragments were processed from the FFPE-samples, following standard protocols. Semithin (0.5 μm) sections were stained with Toluidine Blue stain and examined on light microscopy (Olympus BX53). Additionally, Twenty-nanometer-thick sections were mounted on copper grids and examined on a Transmission Electron Microscope (TEM) FEI Tecnai G2 equipped with a side-mounted camera Olympus Veleta at the Electron Microscopy Facility of the University of Padua (DeBioEMF), Italy.

On transmission electron microscopy, the neoplasm showed characteristic features for each of the three distinct cell populations: (1) cylindrical epithelioid cells with tubular and acinar-like architecture showed apical tight junctions and rare neurosecretory cytoplasmic granules, compatible with immature adenohypophyseal differentiation (Rathke pouch cells); these cells were associated with a high number of (2) large polygonal to round cells with numerous and prominent dense-core cytoplasmic neuroendocrine granules and (3) small cells, scattered or arranged in small groups, with a high nuclear to cytoplasmic ratio, compatible with blastema-like undifferentiated cells. Immunohistochemistry

To further confirm the diagnosis, additional 3–4 μm tissue slides were sent to the Pathology pituitary Service at the IRCCS Humanitas Research Hospital in Milan, Italy. The expression of pituitary transcription factors (Pit-1, SF-1, and TPIT) and hormones (ACTH, GH, PRL, FSH, LH, TSH), as well as low molecular weight cytokeratins (CAM 5.2) and Ki67 was tested in the neoplasm. Details regarding the antigen retrieval, dilution and clonality of the antibody and manufacturer are reported in Table 1. Immunohistochemical procedures were performed in accordance with the validated diagnostic protocols of the Department of Pathology, IRCCS Humanitas Research Hospital, using appropriate positive and negative control tissues. In-silico validation was carried out for each antibody not previously used on canine tissues. The amino acid sequence of the target antigen was retrieved from the UniProt database (https://www.uniprot.org), and sequence alignment between the canine and human proteins was performed to assess the degree of sequence homology (Table 1S; Supplemental material). Furthermore, the expected positive and negative expression pattern of each marker in the canine pituitary tissue, was used as an internal control.

Discussion

This study reports the first case of a PitB in a canine patient; the clinical presentation with ACTH-dependent hypercortisolism, and the histological, immunohistochemical and electron microscopic features overlap some of the human cases. Scheithauer and colleagues firstly described in 2008 a lesion with morphological characteristics suggestive of a combination of developmental arrest along with subsequent unregulated cellular proliferation, proposing the name “pituitary blastoma” (PitB) [21]. Since 2008 a few other cases have been described in humans in both, children and adults, with similar morphological features including primitive-appearing blastema cells, glandular epithelium and large adenohypophyseal-like cells [2, 4, 20, 21].

Clinically, human cases of PitB are often associated with Cushing’s syndrome early in infancy, with characteristic features such as truncal obesity, moon facies, hypertension, hypertrichosis, short stature, acne, and muscle weakness [10]. Additionally, several ophthalmologic abnormalities have been documented, including strabismus, proptosis, ophthalmoplegia, and ptosis [10]. These are likely the consequences of the mass effect or due to the involvement of surrounding intracranial structures. The canine case described here showed no evidence of visual deficits, possibly due to a more anterior location of the optic chiasm and to a minor involvement of surrounding intracranial structures related to the vision ability, but demonstrated an ACTH-dependent hypercortisolism, suggesting functional activity of the tumor as observed in some human cases [4, 20, 21]. The absence of menace responses in the presence of no other evidence of visual impairment suggests that this finding was more likely related to forebrain dysfunction or residual post-ictal effects rather than true visual pathway impairment.

Imaging features of the human and canine cases are somehow similar; in the current canine case, a solid and well-defined mass, isointense at T1-weighted sequence and weakly hyperintense at T2-weighted sequence, was reported. In humans, both solid and cystic appearance have been reported, although no detailed description are available for the MRI appearance of the human PitB at different weighted sequences [4, 6]. Strong and homogenous contrast enhancement was noted in the canine case; similarly human PitB have been described as enhancing mass lesions [20]. Compared with the MRI features of other canine pituitary proliferative lesions, the findings overlapped with those described for large pituitary lesions (so-called ‘macroadenomas’) and lacked specific distinguishing features [11].

As for the histomorphology and immunophenotyping of PitB, in the current case the diffuse expression of ACTH and TPIT suggests a corticotroph lineage of differentiation. The absence of PIT1 and hormone immunoreactivity related to the somatotroph, lactotroph, thyrotrope, and gonadotroph lineages supports the exclusive corticotroph phenotype of the neuroendocrine component. The presence of CAM 5.2 in tubule-forming cells is consistent with an immature epithelial origin, possibly derived from Rathke’s pouch, which aligns with human PitB morphology as described in the human cases. The observed triphasic immunophenotype including the corticotroph neuroendocrine cells, the Rathke’s pouch-like epithelium, and the undifferentiated proliferative elements, mirrors the hallmark cellular populations of the human pituitary blastoma [1, 21]. On the electron microscopy, cellular populations showed features overlapping to the human PitB [20, 21]. In our study, an aberrant cytoplasmic expression of the SF1 transcription factor was noted in the large adenohypophyseal-like cells. This feature is well-known in ACTH-secreting human pituitary tumors [18].

Regarding the biological behavior, according to the human reports, many PitBs exhibit aggressive behavior, with approximately 40% of cases resulting in death [4]. Fatal outcomes are often attributed to local tumor effects with increased intracranial pressure or to excessive cortisol secretion leading to severe and fatal Cushing’s disease [4]. In the dog presented herein, due to the neurological deterioration and non-ambulatory tetraparesis, euthanasia was opted by the owner. In humans’ PitB the cell proliferation has been assessed by the Ki-67 labeling index and has shown a considerable variability and uncertain predictive value [4]. Notably, some patients with high Ki-67 indices experienced tumor recurrence [4]. In our canine case, the Ki-67 index was measured at 7%, indicating a moderate level of proliferative activity. While this value does not directly align with any clear clinical outcome in the human literature, it provides a baseline for future comparisons and may contribute to a better understanding of the biological behavior of PitBs across species.

In a previous study on canine pituitary adenomas obtained by transsphenoidal hypophysectomy, a Ki-67 proliferation index greater than 3% in corticotroph adenomas was associated with shorter post-surgical survival [16]. In our case, the Ki-67 index (7%) of the PitB, could potentially indicates a less favorable prognosis, although further studies including follow-up data are needed to clarify its biological behavior compared to other canine pituitary proliferative lesions.

In the present case, a limitation on the assessment of the biological behavior of PitB is the lack of a longer follow-up period, as the postoperative clinical course following transsphenoidal hypophysectomy ultimately led the owners to elect euthanasia. The post-operative clinical course was markedly influenced by the surgical complications reported. Among prognostic factors in canine transsphenoidal surgery, pituitary size is recognized as one of the predictors of long-term remission, with enlarged pituitary glands, as reported in the current case, being associated with poorer outcomes and a higher risk of perioperative complications [7].The molecular basis of PitB development is well-known in humans. Specifically, PitB is considered a pathognomonic lesion of the human DICER1 syndrome with both, germline mutations, and somatic mutations reported as associated to this lesion [3, 4, 10, 17]. DICER1 encodes an endoribonuclease that plays an essential role in the biogenesis of microRNAs regulating the cell cycle progression and differentiation processes, particularly during the vertebrate development [5]. Within the context of the DICER1-tumor predisposition syndrome, DICER1 demonstrates a tumor suppressor gene function when affected by loss-of-function mutations [19]. In the context of the DICER1 tumor predisposition syndrome, DICER1 could be considered as both, a tumor suppressor gene in case of loss-of-function mutations or an oncogene, in case of gain-of-function mutations [5]. Although the mechanisms underlying tumorigenesis of PitB are still not fully understood and require further investigation, the involvement of the DICER1 activity on miRNAs related to differentiation pathways appears particularly significant, as features of this tumor suggest that these processes are often arrested or dysregulated in the presence of DICER1 mutations [4]. Consistent with observations in other DICER1-associated neoplasms, PitB exhibits embryonal or blastomatous features and, as proposed by previous authors, this phenotype supports the hypothesis that PitB may actually represent the consequence of aberrant tissue development [9]. Although the clear involvement of the DICER1 gene in the human cases warrants the investigation of this gene in the described canine case, limits applied in its investigation. Since the canine DICER1 gene is large, and its molecular characterization requires complex, technically demanding and expensive analysis, despite the interest in potential genetic similarities, the molecular analysis of this gene in the examined canine tumour sample was not performed. The lack of current knowledge on the canine PitB limits the understanding of the underlying pathogenesis in the canine case.

Conclusion

This is the first detailed description of pituitary blastoma in dogs. Neuroimaging, histopathology, electron microscopy and immunohistochemistry highlight strong similarities on this human and canine entity. Similarities are reported also on the signs of pituitary dysfunction. This case enriches the medical literature and aids in differential diagnosis of proliferative pituitary lesions in dogs. Unfortunately, the impossibility to perform genetic testing (e.g., DICER1 mutation) limits the understanding of the mechanisms behind the development of this rare neoplasm in humans and dogs.
