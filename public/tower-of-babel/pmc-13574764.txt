Interface-shrunk regulated synthesis of asymmetric ultrafine mono-mesopore Nepenthes with tailored window
Authors: Jie Wang, Xiankai Fan, Luxiao Zhang, Guangmei Jiang, Ziyan Han, Jin Xu, Kun Wang, Wenhui Li, Hao Li, Tao Wang, Yujuan Zhao, Ruicong Wang, Ruizheng Zhao, Ziyang Guo, Qingnuan Zhang, Jinyu Li, Dongliang Chao, Dongyuan Zhao, Zaiwang Zhao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Asymmetric ultrafine mono-mesopore architectures with ultrasmall size, an open window, and unique physicochemical properties offer great opportunities in energy storage, yet their synthesis remains challenging. Inspired by the fast, unidirectional capture ability of Nepenthes, we propose an interface-shrunk monomicelle assembly strategy to synthesize asymmetric ultrafine mono-mesopore nanoparticles (AUMNs) with an open window on hollow shells. These biomimetic nanoparticles (∼45 nm) possess a mono-mesopore cavity (∼40 nm) and an opening window (∼20 nm). Their structural parameters, including window size (15–28 nm), shell thickness (7–14 nm), and cavity size (38–59 nm), are precisely tunable. The resulting AUMNs exhibit a high surface area (1312 m² g⁻¹) and abundant pseudocapacitive sites. As cathodes in zinc-ion hybrid capacitors (ZIHCs), they show low charge-transfer impedance and deliver a maximum capacity of 210 mAh g⁻¹.

Introduction

Bionic functional hollow nanomaterials with large internal voids, diverse nanoscale configurations, porous cavities, and tunable chemical compositions aree able to bring some tailored synergies between the functional framework and the encapsulated guest substance, and thus have been widely used in catalysis1–4, energy5–7, biomedicine8–13, and other research fields14. In recent years, achieving precise morphological and meso-structural control over asymmetric biomimetic nanoparticles has become a major objective. Particular interest has shifted toward asymmetric hollow architectures with open windows. These open-window functional nanostructures are inspired by nature’s highly efficient mass-exchange systems. Unlike previous reports that only mimic the overall shape, our work further explores the functional commonality between the Nepenthes peristome and the asymmetric window: both break symmetry to create a localized driving force (capillary pressure or electric field gradient) that directionally captures target species (insects or Zn²⁺ ions). By breaking spherical symmetry, the innovative geometries trigger localized field enhancements and provide open pathways for the directional capture and enrichment of reactants or ions, fundamentally accelerating interfacial kinetics. This design uniquely reconciles stability with openness by facilitating stress release to prevent shell collapse while maximizing active-site exposure. Developing such architectures that harmonize spatial confinement with interfacial accessibility is of universal importance for advancing catalysis, biosensing, and high-performance energy storage. To date, conventional synthesis methods such as the emulsion method15, anisotropic growth16, extrusion17, swelling18, and hard template methods19–21 have been used to synthesize the desirable opened asymmetric mesoporous hollow nanoparticles. However, the asymmetric hollow mesoporous nanoparticles previously reported were typically closed-shell structures, with uneven configurations and uncontrollable synthesis processes.

Oriented soft micelle co-assembly methods of amphiphilic molecules, such as cetyltrimethylammonium bromide (CTAB) and Pluronic F127 triblock copolymers, are versatile and potential methods for the solution-based synthesis of asymmetric hollow mesoporous nanoparticles22–26. The rational introduction of appropriate amphiphilic soft molecular templates into the reaction system can create hollow nanostructures with asymmetric mesoporous channels27–29. The morphology of the assemblies (e.g., spherical micelles, rod-shaped micelles, or Janus micelles) can be controlled by adjusting the concentration of amphiphilic molecules, solvent polarity, or temperature. Over the past two decades, these soft micelles-directed assembly methods have been widely used to synthesize a variety of asymmetric functional mesoporous materials30,31. For example, badminton-like inorganic mesoporous hollow nanomaterials have been synthesized and used to explore the functions of the porous hollow structure in biofluid dynamics, organ clearance and blood circulation32. Bowl-shaped inorganic mesoporous hollow nanoparticles have also been prepared and applied to sodium and zinc-ion capacitors, demonstrating excellent electrochemical properties33. However, the size of the aforementioned asymmetric mesoporous hollow nanoparticles is large, generally in hundreds of nanometers or even above the micron level, which hampers the transfer dynamics, resulting in limited activity. This is because the micelles used in the conventional methods are unstable and prone to agglomeration or phase change to form large-sized mesostructures. In addition, these asymmetric nanoparticles are closed-shell structures, without a window on their shells, retarding the practical applications due to low permeability and limited mass/electron transport. So far, controlling the nucleation and growth of the inorganics on an organic monomicelle to construct an open window architecture, and simultaneously inhibiting the agglomeration to obtain an ultrasmall single-micelle-scale asymmetric architecture is indispensable. To date, the synthesis of asymmetric, ultrafine (<50 nm), mono-mesopore-level, hollow nanomaterials, especially with a bionic tunable opening on hollow shells, remains a major challenge.

Herein, we have developed an interface-shrunk monomicelle assembly method to synthesize asymmetric ultrafine mono-mesopore carbon nanoparticles with a unique opening on the shell. Just as Nepenthes utilizes its specialized rim and hollow cavity to directionally trap and digest nutrients, the designed nanoparticles leverage a window to facilitate ion accessibility. The key features of this approach are first controlling the assembly of the micelle on the interface of silica to form earth-satellite silica@micelles superparticles with a dense silica-monomicelle interface, and then selectively manipulating of the polymerization of dopamine precursors on the surface of the single micelle, except for the dense and shrunken micelle-silica interfaces. A unique asymmetric ultrafine mono-mesopore carbon nanoparticles with an opening window (due to the concave shape of the micelle at the micelle/silica interface) were successfully constructed after removal of silica and the monomicelle. This asymmetric ultrafine mesoporous nanoparticle possessed a small mono-mesopore-sized cavity (~38 nm) in the center, and an opened window (~20 nm) was created on the shell. Additionally, the cavity (38−59 nm), shell thickness (7.0−14 nm), and especially the opened window (15−28 nm) of mesopores can also be accurately manipulated. At the same time, this bionic asymmetric ultrafine mono-mesopore nanoparticle exhibits an ultrahigh specific surface area (1312 m2 g−1) and abundant accessible pseudocapacitive active sites. Lastly, we demonstrate the potential use of this asymmetric mono-mesopore carbon nanoparticle as an advanced cathode for aqueous ZIHCs with a maximum capacity of 210 mAh g−1 and good cycling stability.

Results

Material preparation and structural characterization

Asymmetric ultrafine mono-mesopore carbon nanoparticles with a controllable opening window on the shell could be fabricated via an interface-shrunk monomicelle assembly strategy (Fig. 1). Initially, in a typical synthesis, the SiO2 nanospheres (~300 nm) with a hydrophilic surface were prepared through the Stöber method (Fig. S1a) and the monodispersed and stable polystyrene-block-poly(4-vinylpyridine)-block-poly(ethylene oxide) (PS-b-PVP-b-PEO) monomicelles were prepared by a thermo-kinetics-mediated methodology (Fig. S2)34. Then, the surface modification of the SiO2 nanospheres was performed with the dimethyldimethoxysilane (DMDMS) coupling agent to form a hydrophobic surface (Fig. S1b). Next, the modified SiO2 nanospheres and the PS-PVP-PEO triblock monomicelles were well mixed in an ethanol environment. At this time, under electrostatic attraction, the soft monomicelle (positively charged due to protonation of PVP) acted as the assembled units to assemble on the surface of the spherical SiO2 substrate (negatively charged) to form the earth-satellite-type SiO2@micelles superparticles (Fig. S3). The dispersion of the soft monomicelle building units on the SiO2 substrate is discontinuous. Afterward, the precursor of dopamine hydrochloride was added to polymerize to form polydopamine, selectively grown on the surface of the monomicelle, not on the SiO2. This is because dopamine is only polymerized on the hydrophilic surfaces instead of the hydrophobic ones (Fig. S4a). After the subsequent annealing, a uniform N-doped carbon shell was generated on the surface of the monomicelle for the “earth-satellites” SiO2@micelles superparticles (Fig. S4b). The HRTEM images further show that the diameters of the opening in the shell could be precisely controlled from 27 ± 5 nm (Fig. 3a), 21 ± 5 (Fig. 3b), to 15 ± 5 (Fig. 3c). Modelling analysis of the morphology of the obtained AUMNs reveals that as the aperture angle (θ) increases, the electrostatic force strengthens, causing a gradual transformation in the morphology of the AUMNs (Fig. 3d–s). Initially, at θ = 20−30°, the AUMNs are spherical with a small notch. As the θ increases to the range of 40−60°, the notch becomes more prominent. Eventually, when θ reaches 70−90°, the AUMNs take on a jar-like shape. At even larger aperture angles (θ > 90°), the electrostatic forces continue to intensify, leading to further morphological evolution. When θ approaches 110−130°, the jar-like structure develops a distinct inward concavity. Finally, in the range of θ = 140−180°, the morphology transforms into a well-defined, open-ended bowl-shaped structure. This progression demonstrates a continuous and predictable structural modulation governed by the balance of electrostatic forces. The diagram shows the key process parameters influencing the morphology of the assembly (Fig. 3t). The linear dependence of the opening size on the aperture angles indicates that the hollow structures can be tailored with high fidelity, ranging from narrow-pored to wide-mouthed configurations (Fig. 3u). A moderate NH3·H2O concentration is necessary for achieving opening morphology. This morphological transition indicates that electrostatic forces play a crucial role in the interfacial assembly behavior of SiO2 with micelles, effectively acting as a tunable parameter to precisely control the final nanomaterial architecture.

The thickness of the shell layer of the nanostructures could be well controlled by adjusting the reaction time. The shell layer thickness of the asymmetric ultrafine mono-mesopore nanoparticles is decreased from ~14, 12, to 7 nm when the reaction time is shortened from 24, 15 to 5 h, respectively (Fig. S9g–l). In addition, the cavity size of these asymmetric ultrafine mono-mesopore nanoparticles can also be tuned by controlling the size of the soft monomicelle template on the silica interface. The diameter of the cavity for the asymmetric ultrafine mono-mesopore nanoparticles is decreased from ~58, 44 to 38 nm (Fig. S9a–f) when the size of the monomicelle is shortened from ~60, 45 to 40 nm (Fig. S10a–c), respectively.

Additionally, a schematic representation illustrates the modulation of the assembly process through methyl-group modification (via DMDMS), which influences the interaction between the PVP blocks of the monomicelles and the silica surface (Fig. S11a). By varying the amount of DMDMS modifier, the surface chemical groups and thus the interaction strength can be directly controlled, allowing further regulation of the number of monomicelles assembled on the central nanoparticle (Fig. S11b). Increasing the ammonia concentration from 5, 10, 15, to 20 wt% results in a precise tuning of the average number of assembled monomicelles to 5 ± 3 (Fig. S11c, g), 10 ± 3 (Fig. S11d, h), 35 ± 5 (Fig. S11g, i), and 70 ± 5 (Fig. S11h, j), respectively. Correspondingly, the number of resulting mesopores also systematically increases from 5 ± 3, 10 ± 3, 35 ± 5, to 70 ± 5. These findings demonstrate that both molecular-level chemical modification and reaction-condition regulation serve as effective and complementary strategies for fine-tuning the hierarchical architecture of the earth-satellite nanoparticles and opening of the carbon nanoparticles.

Electrochemical performance for ZIHCs

The electrochemical properties of the AUMNs featuring ultrasmall asymmetric open shells, along with the hollow spheres (HCS-50 and HCS-300), were studied as cathode materials for ZIHCs in a 2 M ZnSO4 electrolyte. Cyclic voltammetry (CV) curves in the voltage window of 0.4–1.6 V reveal the different electrochemical redox reactions between AUMNs, HCS-50, and HCS-300 products (Fig. 4a). The CV curves of all cathodes consist of a near-rectangle and a pair of redox peaks, which is consistent with a hybrid energy-storage mechanism (double-layer capacitance and redox pseudocapacitance due to N-doped carbon)44. Moreover, the larger area indicates a higher specific capacity, originating from the synergistic effect of the double-layer capacitance and redox pseudocapacitance of the N-doped carbon nanomaterial45,46. Besides, the position of the peaks and the integrated areas remain stable across consecutive CV cycles, indicating the robust reversibility of the AUMNs electrode (Fig. S12a). To further analyze the Zn2+ storage kinetics, CV curves were measured at various scan rates (Fig. 4b). AUMNs display a surface-capacitive-controlled contribution of ~89% and a diffusion-controlled contribution of ~11% at 1 mV s⁻¹ (Fig. 4c). Meanwhile, as the scanning rate rises, the capacitive contribution to the total capacity gradually increases, suggesting rapid and efficient surface electrochemical kinetics47. The galvanostatic charge-discharge test results illustrate that the discharge capacity of AUMNs reaches up to 210 mAh g⁻¹, significantly outperforming the discharge capacities of HCS-50 (123 mAh g⁻¹) and HCS-300 (104 mAh g⁻¹) at a current density of 0.1 A g⁻¹ (Fig. 4d). These results confirm that the unique open architecture is highly favorable for unlocking reversible capacity. During the rate performance test, the discharge capacities of AUMNs at specific current densities of 0.1, 0.2, 0.5, 1.0, 2.0, 5.0, and 10 A g⁻¹ are measured as 207, 185, 166, 143, 132, 110, and a 98 mAh g⁻¹, respectively (Fig. 4e). When the current density is decreased back to 0.1 A g⁻¹, the reversible discharge capacity of AUMNs could return to 197 mAh g⁻¹. Electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) measurements were performed to understand how the structural features influence the kinetics of charge transfer. A sloping line in the low-frequency area and a semicircle in the high-to-medium frequency zone universally make up the Nyquist plots48. Notably, the Nyquist plot (Fig. 4f) reveals a smaller semicircle diameter for the AUMNs cathode than for the HCS-50 and HCS-300 electrodes. Equivalent circuit fitting (inset in Fig. 4f; detailed parameters in Table S1) shows that the charge transfer resistance (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${R}_{{\mbox{ct}}}$$\end{document}Rct) of AUMNs is 66.79 Ω, which is significantly lower than those of HCS-50 (95.48 Ω) and HCS-300 (176.9 Ω). This reduced Rct indicates faster charge transfer kinetics at the electrode/electrolyte interface for AUMNs, which can be attributed to their higher volume packing density49 and the unique open-aperture structure33 that facilitates ion access to the internal cavity. Furthermore, in-situ EIS measurements were employed to track the dynamic storage mechanism. The contour plots reveal that during the initial charging stage of the first cycle, the radius of the high-to-medium frequency semicircle gradually decreases (Figs. 4g and S12b), reaching its minimum at the 1.6 V cut-off. Conversely, this trend is perfectly inverted during the discharge process (Figs. 4h and S12c). This dynamic evolution clearly demonstrates that the AUMNs possess superior capability in facilitating rapid interfacial ionic reactions, delivering ideal electrochemical reversibility. This kinetic superiority is fundamentally ascribed to the asymmetric open window, which guarantees the swift and unimpeded penetration of the electrolyte into the internal voids50.

The outstanding charge transfer resistance (Rct) and specific discharge capacity of the AUMNs surpass those of several previously reported typical carbon cathodes (Fig. 4i). Additionally, excellent long-term cycling stability is exhibited by the AUMNs, which maintain approximately 97% of their capacity after 5000 cycles at a low current density of 0.1 A g⁻¹ (Fig. 4j). In contrast, the capacity of the hollow particles (HCS-300) drops to around 82.6% of its starting value over the same 5000 cycles. At a current density of 5 A g⁻¹, the AUMNs electrode exhibits stable cycling performance over 5000 cycles (Fig. S18). Moreover, the Coulombic efficiency (CE) remains above 99.5% throughout the cycling process, with negligible fluctuations, demonstrating highly reversible Zn²⁺ storage behavior. Overall, these findings thoroughly demonstrate that the distinct open-aperture design of the AUMNs leads to highly reversible capacity, remarkably high-rate performance, and outstanding stability.

Density functional theory and finite element simulation

Relevant theoretical calculations were conducted to verify the excellent electrochemical performance of AUMNs as cathode materials for zinc-ion hybrid capacitors (ZIHCs), and the results were further corroborated by finite element simulations, collectively revealing the intrinsic mechanism of AUMNs in enhancing ZIHCs’ performance. The cross-sectional schematic diagrams of AUMNs and hollow carbon spheres (HCS) were first exhibited to clarify their structural differences (Fig. 5a, b). From the theoretical calculation perspective, the adsorption energy of zinc ions on the surface of traditional carbon spheres and AUMNs was −1.86 eV and −3.38 eV, respectively (Fig. S13), indicating that AUMNs exhibit a much stronger adsorption capacity for zinc ions, leading to a solid foundation for efficient zinc ion storage and transfer in ZIHCs. To gain deeper insight into the bonding nature of the asymmetric structure, electron localization function (ELF) maps were calculated (Fig. 5c, d). Compared to the pristine carbon sphere (Fig. 5d), the regions between Zn and carbon atoms in the AUMNs exhibit higher ELF values (Fig. 5c), signifying an enhanced electron localization triggered by the strong attractive interaction between the framework and zinc ions. Furthermore, the ELF distribution at the aperture visualizes the unique electronic environment of the asymmetric window (Fig. 5c). This stronger adsorption phenomenon was further confirmed by charge density difference analysis (Fig. 5e, f). A large number of yellow and cyan areas aggregated around AUMNs, which manifested that the charge transfer between zinc ions and surface atoms of AUMNs was significantly more abundant than that of carbon spheres. Notably, the density of states analysis (Fig. 5g) showed that the C-2p orbital of AUMNs was flatter and the peak shape of the Zn-3d orbital was sharper than those of the carbon sphere (Fig. 5h), accompanied by obvious interfacial charge transfer, further verifying the superior electrochemical reaction activity of AUMNs.Fig. 5Engineering nanomaterials for enhancedelectrochemical performance: asymmetric vs. symmetric.Density functional theory and finite element simulation of HCS and AUMNs electrodes. To further uncover the intrinsic reason for the enhanced electrochemical performance of AUMNs, a series of characterizations combined with finite element simulations were carried out to analyze the electric field distribution and ion diffusion behavior of AUMNs (asymmetric) and HCS (symmetric) electrodes. The simulation results (Fig. 5i) showed that the asymmetric structure of AUMNs led to a significant local electric field enhancement: a strong electric field (about 2.8×10⁸ V/m) was concentrated at the opening of AUMNs, which could effectively attract a large number of free electrons and further induce directional migration of zinc ions. Correspondingly, the opening region of AUMNs exhibited a significantly higher ion concentration, showing a distinct ion enrichment effect. Specifically, the electrolyte concentration on the surface of AUMNs opening was 1.8 × 10¹⁰ mol/m³, which was about twice that of HCS, and this significant difference was precisely caused by the locally enhanced electrostatic field of AUMNs (Fig. 5k, l, n). This local electric field enhancement effect not only effectively draws zinc ions to the opening of AUMNs, but also further reduces the charge transfer resistance and optimizes the reaction kinetics, which is consistent with the theoretical calculation result that AUMNs have stronger zinc ion adsorption capacity and more sufficient charge transfer. In sharp contrast, due to the structural symmetry of HCS, their electric field and ion migration showed a uniform distribution without local enhancement: the average electric field intensity at site 4 of HCS was only about 0.5 × 10⁸ V/m, which was only 0.5 times that at the opening site (site 1) of AUMNs (Fig. 5j, m). The uniform electric field and concentration field of HCS failed to generate a directional driving force for zinc ions, resulting in slow diffusion of zinc ions on the sphere surface and poor reaction kinetics. More importantly, HCS lacked the local electric field enhancement effect, making it difficult to achieve efficient enrichment of zinc ions, which was consistent with the theoretical calculation result that the adsorption capacity of HCS for zinc ions was much weaker than that of AUMNs.

To sum up, the foregoing DFT theoretical calculations and finite element simulations complement each other and mutually confirm: the asymmetric structure of AUMNs not only enhances the adsorption capacity and charge transfer efficiency of zinc ions (verified by theoretical calculations), but also forms a local enhanced electric field to promote directional ion migration and enrichment (proven by finite element simulations), thereby comprehensively accelerating the adsorption, storage and transfer of zinc ions in ZIHCs and significantly improving the electrochemical performance of ZIHCs cathodes. The functional analogy between the AUMNs and the Nepenthes pitcher goes beyond morphological resemblance. In the carnivorous plant, the peristome surface features micro-grooves with curved profiles and hydrophilic/hydrophobic patterning, which generate a capillary-driven directional liquid flow toward the interior. This mechanism, known as “prey trapping without active motion”, relies on the asymmetric geometry to break the isotropy of the surrounding environment, creating a gradient of surface energy and Laplace pressure that guides the movement of insects or water unidirectionally. Inspired by this, our AUMNs utilize an asymmetric open-window architecture to induce a localized electrostatic field enhancement at the window edge. Finite element simulations (Fig. 5i) reveal that the curvature discontinuity at the window periphery concentrates the electric field lines, producing a strong local field (≈2.8 × 10⁸ V/m) that is nearly six times higher than that on a conventional closed hollow sphere. This enhanced field acts as an electrostatic peristome, attracting Zn²⁺ ions preferentially toward the window and generating a concentration gradient (1.8 × 10¹⁰ mol/m³ at the window and 0.9 × 10¹⁰ mol/m³ on the opposite side). Consequently, the asymmetric structure not only facilitates ion ingress but also establishes a directional ion flux toward the interior cavity, analogous to the directional fluid transport on the Nepenthes peristome. This cross-scale mechanistic parallel between biological capillarity and nanoscale electrostatic trapping provides a new perspective for designing biomimetic energy storage materials.

Discussion

In this interface-shrunk monomicelle assembly approach, the control of asymmetric ultrafine mono-mesopore N-doped carbon nanoparticles was achieved through the precise manipulation of the synthesis process, involving the stabilization of the initial polymeric PS-PVP-PEO monomicelles, the assembly on the SiO2 interfaces, the contraction and deformation of the monomicelles, and selective polymerization of dopamine precursors on the interfaces (Fig. 1). For this synthesis, two key aspects jointly contribute to the formation of such asymmetric ultrafine mono-mesopore nanoparticles.

First, the surface of the silica spheres was modified by DMDMS, which caused the groups on the surface to be changed from -OH to -CH3 (Fig. S14). This procedure yielded the result: the reduction of -OH endowing the silica spheres to become hydrophobic. The positively charged monomicelles (whose PVP blocks can be protonated in acidic solution) can be tightly anchored onto the surface of negatively charged SiO2 via electrostatic adsorption, resulting in a silica@monomicelles composite structure with a relatively discontinuous distribution of monomicelles. Owing to the strong interfacial interaction between the two components, the monomicelles (as soft matter) undergo contraction at the silica interface. This interfacial contraction plays a critical role in the subsequent formation of a perforated shell structure. Second, the polymerization of dopamine could only occur on soft monomicelles and not on the surface of surface-modified SiO2 nanoparticles aforementioned. This is because the silica spheres behave hydrophobic when modified with -CH3 and thus the hydrophilic dopamine is only hydrolyzed, polymerized and coated on the hydrophilic monomicelles, not the hydrophobic SiO2 (Fig. 6a). So, after the coating of the polydopamine, the surface of the monomicelle shows obvious differences from before, while the surface of the SiO2 nanoparticles still retained its original smoothness (Fig. S4a). Asymmetric ultrafine mono-mesopore carbon nanoparticles were obtained after the removal of monomicelles by calcination and the removal of SiO2 by etching. As a validation, SiO2 spheres modified with dimethyldimethoxysilane could not achieve uniform dopamine coating (Figs. S5, S16 and 17). The ultrahigh stability and high dispersity of the PS-PVP-PEO monomicelles were obtained by the thermokinetics-mediated methodology51. Last but not least, the size of the window on the shell of the asymmetric ultrafine mono-mesopore carbon nanoparticle is controlled by adjusting the electrostatic force. Asymmetric ultrafine mono-mesopore carbon nanoparticles with diameters of opening windows ranging from 15 ± 5, 21 ± 5, to 27 ± 5 nm can be obtained. The intensity of the electrostatic attraction between the monomicelles and the silica can be varied by adjusting the concentration of ammonia52, thus precisely controlling the size of the contact interface area between the two with a fixed diameter. The zeta potential of the SiO2 (negatively charged) is −13.46 mV in an ethanol aqueous solution (pH 7.2). When ammonia (15 wt%) is added, the zeta potential is reversely decreased to −52.70 mV (pH 10.3). Furthermore, when the concentration of ammonia is increased from 10, 15 to 20 wt%, the zeta potential of the SiO2 gradually decreases from approximately −35.83, −52.70 to −64.15 mV (Fig. 6g), respectively. Such an obvious decrease in zeta potential can greatly boost the mutual electrostatic attractions between the monomicelle subunits and the SiO2 substrate, resulting in the increased size of the contact surface between the monomicelle and the silica. Additionally, due to the soft and flexible properties of the polymeric monomicelles, the deformation in morphology normally can occur53 (Figs. 6b and S15) when electrostatic forces are applied to the surface. The bigger the electrostatic attraction is, the greater the deformation of the monomicelles affords. And the greater interface deformation of monomicelles can lead to a larger contact surface between the monomicelles and the silica sphere substrate, and vice versa (Fig. 6c).

Hence, by adjusting the concentration of ammonia, not only can the size of the open windows of asymmetric ultrafine mono-mesopore nanoparticles be precisely controlled, but the number of monomicelles on the earth-satellites superparticles can be adjusted. Subsequently, after the subsequent selective coating of dopamine and the etching processes, the asymmetric mono-mesopore nanoparticles with a controlled opening can be obtained. Large deformation contributes to big windows on the shell, and vice versa.

The asymmetric ultrafine mono-mesopore nanoparticles exhibit remarkable electrochemical properties, including excellent specific capacity and long-term cycling stability (97% capacity retention over 5000 cycles). This high performance is attributed to the open window structure on the shell, abundant oxygen functional groups, and N doping of the opened asymmetric ultrafine mono-mesopore carbon nanoparticles. During the discharge/charge process, the electrolyte can rapidly diffuse through the opened window on the shell of the AUMNs, and the internal and external electrochemically active sites of the mono-mesopore cavity particles can be promptly activated (Fig. S20). In addition, the open structure alters the electric field distribution around the cathode, which in turn helps to homogenize the Zn²⁺ flux migrating toward the zinc anode. This more uniform ionic environment at the anode/electrolyte interface reduces local current crowding, thereby indirectly suppressing the formation of large zinc dendrites on the anode side. As a result, the cycling stability and safety of the full cell are significantly improved. The opening structure mitigates the concentration gradient by enhancing ion convection and diffusion. This powerful transport capability rapidly replenishes consumed reaction ions or removes generated ions, thereby maintaining a relatively gentle ion concentration distribution at the electrode/electrolyte interface and preventing the formation of a steep concentration gradient during charge-discharge cycles, which effectively reduces concentration polarization54. The presence of open windows also alleviates the confinement effect typically encountered in microporous structures for zinc ion storage and boosts the utilization of electrochemically active sites, thereby enhancing Zn²⁺ accumulation and delivering excellent Zn²⁺ storage capacity55. Asymmetric nanoparticles with open-window structures can also shorten ion transfer distances by increasing the volume packing density while maintaining a high specific surface area compared to conventional hollow nanoparticles56. In addition, the abundant oxygen functional groups and N doping can significantly improve the wettability of the electrolyte, which is conducive to lowering the interfacial impedance, achieving rapid electrolyte penetration and improving ion transport kinetics57.

In summary, an asymmetric ultrafine mono-mesopore bionic nanoparticle with an opened window was successfully prepared via an interface-shrunk monomicelle assembly. This asymmetric mono-mesopore nanoparticle exhibits ultrafine size (~45 nm) and a radial opening window on the shell. The asymmetric ultrafine mono-mesopore carbon nanoparticles can endow an ultrahigh surface area (1312 m2 g−1). In addition, the wall thickness (7–14 nm), cavity size (38–59 nm) of the asymmetric mono-mesopore nanoparticle, and the size of the window (15–28 nm) on the shell can also be precisely adjusted. As a result, these opened AUMNs can perform as an efficient cathode material for realizing high-performance (their maximum capacity is 210 mAh g−1) in ZIHCs. The capacity is 200 mAh g-1 after 5000 cycles at a current density of 0.1 A g−1. The reversible capacity of the AUMNs can retain about 97% when the number of cycles is increased to 5000. Ultimately, the primary objective of this work is to provide a specific cathode material to establish a precise methodology for constructing asymmetric windowed mesostructures. Beyond electrochemical energy storage, we believe this proof-of-concept structural paradigm lays a solid foundation for the future development of advanced functional materials in broad fields, including selective catalysis, targeted drug delivery, and confined nanoreactors.

Methods

Preparation of SiO2-CH3 nanoparticles (300 nm)

Typically, 16.25 mL of ethanol, 24.75 mL of deionized water and 9.0 mL of concentrated ammonia solution were mixed to form solution A. Then, 45.5 mL of ethanol and 4.50 mL TEOS were mixed to form solution B. Then, solution B was quickly poured into solution A and stirred vigorously. After reacting for 2 h at 25 °C, then 250 μL of DMDMS was added to react for 2 h. SiO2-CH3 spheres with uniform sizes of ~300 nm were obtained after purification with ethanol three times and drying in an oven at 60 °C.

Preparation of PS-PVP-PEO monomicelles

Typically, 0.023 g of the triblock copolymer PS-PVP-PEO was added to 20 mL of ethanol and kept at 80 °C for 3 h. After it was cooled back to room temperature, a homogeneous solution of stable and monodispersed PS-PVP-PEO monomicelles was obtained.

Preparation of the earth-satellite type silica@monomicelles templates

The SiO2 spheres were dispersed in ethanol (1.0 mg/mL). Then, under intense agitation, 2.0 mL ethanol solution containing the above PS-PVP-PEO monomicelle was added. After ultrasonic treatment for 5 min, 25 μL ammonia water was added to the solution and kept reacting for 12 h. Finally, the earth-satellite type SiO2@monomicelles templates were successfully obtained after purification with ethanol three times and drying in an oven at 60 °C.

Preparation of asymmetric ultrafine mono-mesopore carbon nanoparticles (AUMNs)

In a typical fabrication, 2.0 mL of ethanol solution of the earth-satellite SiO2@monomicelle multi-level templates obtained previously was added into 6.0 mL of deionized water under vigorous stirring. Then, 6.0 mg of Tris buffer was added to the solution with ultrasonic treatment for 10 min. Afterward, 1.0 mg of dopamine was added to the solution under vigorous stirring. After reacting for 6 h, the polydopamine-coated linked SiO2@monomicelles nanoparticles were successfully obtained. The final product was centrifuged, washed three times with deionized ethanol and dried for 12 h at 60 °C.

Afterwards, these dry products were put into a tube furnace, and the temperature was increased from 20 to 350 °C at a constant rate of 1 °C min−1 and kept at 350 °C for 2 h under a nitrogen atmosphere. Subsequently, the temperature was further raised to 800 °C at a constant rate of 5 °C min−1 and kept at this temperature for 3 h to obtain the asymmetric ultrafine mesoporous N-doped carbon-coated SiO2 nanoparticles. After a simple ultrasound treatment, these nanoparticles became monodisperse. Finally, the nanoparticles were further etched with 5 wt% NaOH aqueous solution for 10 h to remove the SiO2 core and acquire the asymmetric ultrafine mono-mesopore nanoparticles with an adjustable open window. According to the size of the opening structure, they are denoted as AUMNs-x (x = 16, 21, 27), respectively.

Characterization

TEM, HRTEM, and high-angle annular dark field images in the scanning TEM (HAADF-STEM) observations were taken on a JEM-2100 F microscope (JEOL, Japan) operated at 200 kV. Field-emission scanning electron microscopy (FESEM) images were conducted on a Regulus 8100 microscope. Samples for TEM and FESEM tests were prepared by drying the nanoparticles on amorphous carbon-coated copper grids. The Brunauer-Emmett-Teller (BET) method was utilized to calculate the specific surface areas (Quantachrom; Autosorb iQ). By using the Barrett-Joyner-Halenda (BJH) model, the pore volume and pore size distribution were derived from the adsorption branches of isotherms. The FT-IR spectra were analyzed by iraffinity-1s (Shimadzu; range of 400–4000 cm−1). Raman spectra were carried out by a confocal Raman microscope (Renishaw via Raman microscope; RM 1000-Invia; range of 100–2000 cm−1; λ = 514 nm). XPS was recorded on ESCALAB QXi (Select 900 μm perform routine testing on M-beam spot, and energy parameters including full spectrum energy of 160 eV and high-resolution narrow scan spectrum energy of 40 eV).
