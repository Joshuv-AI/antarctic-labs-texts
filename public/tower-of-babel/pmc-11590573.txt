Enhancing the timeliness of EMR documentation in resident doctors: the role of PDCA cycle management
Authors: Jiaoting Chen, Zihan Li, Wananqi Ma, Yu Tang, Can Liu, Shanshan Ma, Ming Xu, Qiongwen Zhang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe impact of the Plan-Do-Check-Act (PDCA) cycle in improving the timeliness of electronic medical record documentation (EMRd) remains uncertain. EMRd that is completed beyond the specified time is classified as late EMRd, while unqualified EMRd refers to electronic medical records with important sections that are not completed on time according to the Chinese Basic Norms of Medical Records Writing. This study aimed to evaluate the effectiveness of PDCA management in improving the timeliness of EMRd among resident doctors.MethodThis study utilized a before and after design. Resident doctors rotating in the Head and Neck Oncology Department of West China Hospital, Sichuan University, from November 2021 to August 2022 were classified as the control group and did not receive specific training on the timeliness of EMRd. Those rotating from September 2022 to June 2023 were assigned to the PDCA group, which was managed using the PDCA cycle. The effectiveness of PDCA cycle management was evaluated by comparing the incidence of late EMRd and unqualified EMRd between two groups. Univariate and multivariate binary logistic regression analyses were conducted tocontrol for confounding factors.ResultsA total of 245 resident doctors were included, with 162 doctors in the PDCA group and 152 doctors in the control group. The incidences of late EMRd (5.40% vs. 2.56%, P = 0.005) and unqualified EMRd (1.05% vs. 0.00%, P < 0.001) were significantly lower in the PDCA group than those in the control group. After adjusting confounding factors, PDCA management still significantly reduced the occurrence of unqualified EMRd (P < 0.001) with an adjusted odds ratio (OR) of 0.166 (95% CI 0.067–0.416) and late EMRd (P < 0.001) with an adjusted OR of 0.318 (95% CI 0.181–0.557).ConclusionThis study successfully developed PDCA management and demonstrated its effectiveness in improving the timeliness of EMRd while concurrently reducing the incidence of unqualified and late entries among resident doctors.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12909-024-06134-2.Keywords: Electronic medical records, Plan-do-check-act cycle, Timeliness, Resident doctors

Introduction

Electronic medical records have become an integral part of modern medical practice, serving as essential tools for doctors [1]. Electronic medical records form a rich repository of information, that supports the development of better clinical prognostic tools [2], surgical scheduling tools [3], decision support systems [4], public health surveillance [1, 5]. However, in the context of the Chinese clinical training system, limited working hours and a growing patient population heighten the importance of timely EMRd [6–8]. The timeliness of EMRd refers to the ability of doctors to complete medical records within the prescribed time. EMRd completed beyond the specified time is considered late EMRd. Several important parts of EMRd are more likely to cause medical risks, including the first disease course records, shift records, stage summaries, the first ward-round records, and surgical records. According to the Chinese Basic Norms of Medical Records Writing, the EMRd containing these parts that were not completed on time is classified as an unqualified EMRd. Few articles have been published on approaches to improving the timeliness of EMRd, including enhancing the application of electronic medical records [9], conducting assessments and public displays [10], and designing new structured data-entry forms [6]. Given the current unsatisfactory rate of timely EMRd, additional new improvement measures are necessary.

The Plan-Do-Check-Act (PDCA) cycle, also known as the “quality loop,” is a management model for continuous improvement [11] and has demonstrated its effectiveness in various medical scenarios, including hyperglycemia management [12], nutrition management [13], and infection rate control in operating rooms [14]. Furthermore, the PDCA cycle has been applied in modern hospital quality management and demonstrated its efficiency [15–17]. Considering the wide application of electronic medical records and the lack of effective management models to reduce late EMRd, this study aimed to explore whether the implementation of PDCA management among residents can improve the timeliness of EMRd compared to traditional management approaches.

Materials and methods

Study population

This study employed a before and after design. Resident doctors rotating in the Head and Neck Oncology Department of West China Hospital, Sichuan University were recruited for the study. Those who rotated between November 2021 and August 2022 were classified as the control group and did not receive training on the timeliness of EMRd. Resident doctors rotating from September 2022 to June 2023 were included in the PDCA group, which implemented the PDCA cycle for quality management.

In this study, resident doctors refer to those undergoing clinical training in our hospital. The duration of rotation in our department varied depending on the majors, ranging from 2 months to 12 months. Therefore, the impact of this rotation time was considered as part of the influence of majors on the timeliness of EMRd. Moreover, there were some overlapping resident doctors between the two groups, and the specific number of these overlapping doctors was recorded.

Study variables

(1) Basic information of resident doctors was recorded, including age, sex, postgraduate year (PGY), major, and weekly working hours. Doctors with missing information were not eligible for inclusion.

(2) Timeliness issues often occur in several parts of electronic medical records, including homepages, disease course records, shift records, stage summaries, ward-round records, and surgical records.

The homepage contained the basic patient information, diagnosis and treatment, hospitalization process, and expenses. Disease course records encompassed the first disease course records, daily disease course records, superior physician ward-round records, shift handover (succession) records, surgical records, and stage summaries. The first disease course records were the first documents written after admission, detailing the case characteristics, diagnostic basis, differential diagnoses, and treatment plan. Daily disease course records were continuous records during hospitalization, covering patient status, specialized examinations, diagnosis, treatments, and their efficacy, as well as the opinions from superior physicians when ward rounds. The superior physician ward-round records captured the superior physician’s analysis of the patient’s condition, diagnosis, differential diagnosis, current treatments and efficacy, and plans for further diagnosis and treatment. The first ward-round records were the first superior physician ward-round records written after admission. The shift handover (succession) records summarized the patient’s condition, diagnosis, and treatment, by the handover (succession) physician when changing the patient’s attending physician. The surgical record was a specialized document that reflected the overall surgical situation, surgical process, intraoperative findings and measures. For patients with prolonged hospital stays, stage summaries offered monthly overviews of patients’ conditions, diagnoses, and treatments.

(3) The late EMRd were defined as those that were not written promptly. The timeliness issues of EMRd were categorized as follows: (a) for patients with stable conditions, the completion time of daily disease course records exceeded 3 days; (b) the first disease course records were not completed within 8 h after admission; (c) shift records were not finished before the succession records were written; (d) the completion time of stage summaries exceeded 30 days during the hospitalization; (e) the first ward-round records by the superior physicians were not completed within 48 h of admission; (f) surgical records were not completed within 24 h after surgery; (g) important examination results were not documented in the disease course records; (h) EMR homepages were not completed within 24 h after discharge; EMRd with one or more of severe timeliness problems [(b) to (f)] were classified as unqualified EMRd according to the Chinese Basic Norms of Medical Records Writing. All these timeliness issues can be identified by the medical records system of our hospital.

Intervention

Based on previous research on the successful application of PDCA cycle management and the work experience of medical team leaders and EMRd quality controllers [13, 18], the following PDCA cycle management measures were developed to improve the timeliness of medical records. Evaluation indicators

The department’s timeliness of EMRd was evaluated by the proportion of electronic medical records with different timeliness issues each month. The effectiveness of PDCA management was examined by comparing these proportions between the two groups.

Statistical analysis

Normality tests were conducted for continuous variables. Normally distributed continuous variables were presented as mean ± standard deviation, and non-normally distributed continuous variables were reported as the median and range [25th-75th percentile]. Categorical variables were expressed as counts and percentages. Independent t-test and Mann-Whitney U test were used for continuous variables as appropriate. The chi-square test and Fisher’s exact test were applied for categorical variables. Univariate and multivariate binary logistic regression analyses were performed to identify independent factors affecting the timeliness of EMRd, with results presented as odds ratios (ORs). The results were considered statistically significant at P < 0.05 and all tests were 2-tailed. Data analysis was conducted using the Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS) software (version 26.0, SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA).

Results

Between November 2021 and June 2023, we included 245 resident doctors, and no one was excluded due to incomplete information. The control group included 152 doctors who underwent rotation from November 2021 to August 2022, while 162 doctors from September 2022 to June 2023 were assigned to the PDCA group. The PDCA group adopted the PDCA management for the timeliness of EMRd. Notably, there were 69 resident doctors who were present in both groups. The basic characteristics of all participating doctors were presented in Table 1. The majority of the participants were under 30 years old (n = 152, 62.0%) held a bachelor’s degree (n = 149, 60.8%), and worked 40 to 60 hours per week (n = 203, 82.9%). There were no significant differences in most of the basic information between the two groups, while the doctors in the PDCA group had a significantly shorter PGY compared to those in the control group (P < 0.001).

The effectiveness of PDCA management

There was no significant difference in the total number of electronic medical records between the two groups. As indicated in Table 2, the implementation of PDCA cycle management model significantly decreased the percentage of late EMRd (5.40% vs. 2.56%, P = 0.005) and unqualified EMRd (1.05% vs. 0.00%, P < 0.001). Moreover, the percentage of EMRd with first disease course records not completed within 8 h of patient admission in the PDCA group was significantly lower than that in the control group (0.24% vs. 0.00%, P = 0.023). In addition, the timeliness of the 3-day disease course records (2.93% vs. 0.61%, P = 0.001) and the first ward-round records (0.36% vs. 0.00%, P = 0.035) also improved significantly due to the implementation of the PDCA cycle. Overall, the numbers and percentages of EMRd with different timeliness issues were reduced after employing PDCA management.

Factors influencing unqualified medical records

To further investigate the effectiveness of the PDCA cycle management model, we identified factors influencing the occurrence of unqualified EMRd and controlled for potential confounders using univariate and multivariate binary logistic regression analyses. Six potential confounding factors were examined: age, sex, PGY, academic degree, working hours, and major, of which only age significantly affected the occurrence of unqualified EMRd (P = 0.010). Compared with doctors older than 30 years old, those younger than 30 years old have a higher occurrence of unqualified EMRd, with an OR of 3.622 (95% CI 1.363–9.621). Details of all ORs for the influencing factors were presented in Table 3. Incorporating age and PDCA management into a multivariate regression analysis, the results showed that PDCA cycle management can still significantly reduce the occurrence of unqualified EMRd (P < 0.001) with an adjusted OR of 0.166 (95% CI 0.067–0.416) (Table 4).

Factors influencing late medical records

Univariate regression analysis identified age (P = 0.028), sex (P = 0.044), PGY, and major as potential confounding factors (Table 3), which were subsequently included in the multivariate regression analysis. The results showed that the PDCA cycle management could still effectively reduced the occurrence of late EMRd (P < 0.001), with an adjusted OR of 0.318 (95% CI 0.181–0.557). Age may be a potential predictor for the occurrence of late EMRd (P = 0.055), while sex did not have a statistically significant effect on the occurrence of late EMRd (P = 0.155). Compared to doctors who had graduated for 4 years or more, those who graduated 2 years ago had a higher incidence of late EMRd (P = 0.008), with an OR of 1.027 (95% CI 1.301–5.994). Furthermore, the incidence of late EMRd was significantly lower among doctors in internal medicine and other majors compared to those in head and neck tumors (P = 0.021), with an OR of 0.414 (95% CI 0.196–0.873) (Table 4).

Discussion

The ability of resident doctors to efficiently share patient information through electronic medical records has become a critical focus for medical educators and policymakers. This focus is justified by the crucial role that timely EMRd plays in effective patient care and coordination among healthcare professionals [6]. Timely EMRd are essential not just for emergency scenarios, where they can be life-saving, but also for reducing the risk of medical errors across all areas of healthcare delivery [27–29]. However, the implementation of EMRd has introduced unintended challenges, including increased work hours, time constraints, and potential miscommunication between patients and healthcare providers [30]. Therefore, the application of scientific management tools and the provision of EMRd training are indispensable components in the training of resident doctors, ensuring they are equipped to navigate these complexities effectively and maintain the highest standards of patient care.

In response to these challenges, our study explored the Plan-Do-Check-Act (PDCA) cycle as a strategic intervention to enhance the timeliness of EMRd among resident doctors. The PDCA cycle, a structured quality management model, involved establishing a medical records review team and formulating a comprehensive training plan in the Plan phase [31]. During the Do phase, training was delivered according to predefined education standards. In the subsequent Check phase, we identified challenges, analyzed underlying timeliness issues, and incorporated improvement measures into subsequent PDCA cycles, fostering a continuous enhancement environment. Notably, practical measures such as network meetings and real-time communication were employed for immediate response to late EMRd. Our findings demonstrated a significant increase in the proportion of timely EMRd following the PDCA implementation, underscoring its effectiveness in improving EMRd management practices among residents, which has been proven to improve the clinical skills of resident doctors [32]. This suggests that structured, cyclic approaches to process management can profoundly impact the timeliness of EMRd.

Additionally, our analysis identified key factors contributing to late EMRd. We noted correlations between late EMRd and variables such as age, PGY, and majors. For instance, residents in their second PGY often handle higher patient volumes compared to their first year, which can lead to increased workload and time constraints, potentially impeding timely documentation. This finding is consistent with previous research indicating that after 12 months of ambulatory experience, resident doctors saw more patients per day and spent less time reviewing each patient’s electronic health record [33]. Despite the influence of these variables, the implementation of the PDCA cycle was the most significant factor in reducing late entries, highlighting its potential to transform EMRd practices.

This study has several limitations that warrant discussion. The use of a before-and-after design raised concerns about potential systematic bias between the two groups. However, given the prominent timeliness issues of EMRd within the same department, it was impractical not to implement measures for some doctors. Moreover, during the research period, the criteria for determining the timeliness issues of EMRd did not change. Timeliness issues in both groups were evaluated through a three-tier supervision process.

In the context of the Chinese clinical training system, a certain proportion of resident doctors were present in both groups. Despite this overlap, none of these doctors had previously received training on the timeliness of EMRd, and the impact of working hours and PGY on the timeliness of EMRd was analyzed. We also verified the effectiveness of PDCA cycle management in improving the timeliness of medical records in both repeated and non-repeated doctors (Supplementary Tables 1–5). Therefore, it was believed that the presence of duplicate populations between the two groups did not affect the assessment of PDCA effectiveness. Additionally, although the doctors in the PDCA group had a significantly shorter PGY compared to those in the control group, after incorporating PGY as a confounding factor, PDCA management still significantly reduced the occurrence of late EMRd and unqualified EMRd. A higher PGY may indicate an increase in workload as well as work efficiency [33, 34].

Furthermore, the application of the PDCA cycle in this study was restricted to resident doctors, and its effectiveness in enhancing EMRd timeliness among more experienced physicians warrants further exploration. Moreover, our study’s findings, derived from a single Chinese hospital, may not be universally applicable due to variations in medical records management practices across different healthcare systems. Additionally, this article discussed the impact of PDCA cycle management on the timeliness of EMRd, but it did not address whether such management affected the quality of EMRd, including accuracy and completeness [35]. To overcome these limitations, future research should extend to multi-center studies in varied healthcare settings, include larger sample sizes, and explore both the timeliness and quality of EMRd. Such studies could provide a more comprehensive evaluation of the PDCA cycle’s efficacy in improving EMRd practices across diverse medical environments.

Conclusions

In conclusion, this study successfully developed and implemented a PDCA cycle tailored for managing the timeliness of EMRd among resident doctors. Our findings demonstrated that the application of the PDCA management method significantly enhanced the timeliness of EMRd while concurrently reducing the incidence of unqualified or late entries. These encouraging results underscore the potential of PDCA cycle management as a robust framework not only for improving EMRd management but also as a versatile tool in the broader context of resident training and skill development.
