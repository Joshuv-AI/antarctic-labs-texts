Performance in reducing child stunting in Africa: a stochastic frontier analysis of 54 countries over the past two decades (2000–2022)
Authors: Brice Ulrich Foudjo Saha, Ismael Teta, Dia Sanou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionAfrica remains off-track to meet 2030 stunting targets despite relative economic growth. We aimed to quantify the ‘nutrition conversion gap’, the efficiency with which national resources are translated into stunting reduction, and examine factors associated with technical inefficiency across 54 African nations.MethodsThis longitudinal panel study (2000–2022; N=1134 country-years) used stochastic frontier analysis to estimate a health production frontier, defined as the maximum achievable health production score (100−stunting prevalence). Inputs (gross domestic product (GDP) per capita, dietary adequacy and maternal education) were modelled as 3-year weighted moving averages. Technical inefficiency was modelled as a function of lagged diarrhoea incidence, electrification, urbanisation and political stability.ResultsSignificant performance heterogeneity exists across the continent. High technical inefficiency (γ=0.976, λ=6.33) accounted for 97.6% of the observed nutritional disparities. Low GDP elasticity (β=0.042, p<0.001) suggests economic growth alone is an insufficient standalone strategy for stunting eradication. Senegal and Somalia achieved near-optimal efficiency despite low national wealth, whereas nations like Equatorial Guinea and Botswana exhibited high systemic inefficiency despite significant economic resources. Electricity access was significantly associated with a narrower conversion gap (p<0.001). Furthermore, political stability in the absence of institutional reform correlated with increased inefficiency (δ=0.39, p<0.001).ConclusionNutritional progress is profoundly decoupled from national wealth. Policy must pivot from aggregate resource accumulation towards mitigating systemic bottlenecks, specifically infection control and electrification. Strengthening health system efficiency through decentralised, community-led models represents a more potent roadmap for human capital development than simple macroeconomic expansion.Keywords: Africa, Child health, Health economics, Health policy

Introduction

Childhood stunting, defined as low height-for-age due to chronic malnutrition, remains a public health emergency in Africa.1 While global trends indicate a decline, the African continent remains the only region where the absolute number of affected children has risen since the turn of the century.2 According to the 2000–2024 Joint Child Malnutrition Estimates (JME), approximately 65 million children in Africa were stunted in 2024 compared with 56 million in 2000, representing a critical failure in human capital protection and a structural threat to long-term economic resilience.3 This downward stunting trajectory persists despite two decades of relative economic growth and large-scale nutritional interventions.4

The Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs) commit to eradicating stunting by 2030.5 However, recent evidence shows that most African nations are far off track to meet these targets.3 6 Traditional strategies have historically focused on increasing aggregate caloric availability and micronutrient scaling. Yet a growing nutritional paradox is emerging: several nations experiencing rapid gross domestic product (GDP) expansion continue to report stagnant or slowly declining stunting rates.3 6 This suggests a deep-seated failure in the mechanisms that convert national wealth into physiological development, a phenomenon we define as the ‘nutrition conversion gap’. While efficiency in clinical settings often denotes cost-effectiveness, it is used in this study from an econometric perspective and refers specifically to the systemic ability of a nation to transform its structural inputs into the highest possible level of nutritional health.

Most existing literature on national nutrition performance relies on traditional regression models to estimate average correlations between income (eg, GDP per capita) and health outcomes like stunting prevalence.6 7 While these studies establish that wealth is generally associated with lower malnutrition, they fail to account for the ‘frontier of potential’, the maximum health output a nation could theoretically achieve given its specific resource stock.8 By focusing only on average performance, the global health community may overlook the systemic inefficiencies where economic and nutritional resources leak out of the health production system before they manifest as linear growth.6 8

Central to this leakage is the ‘biological tax’ imposed by highly pathogenic environments. The nutrient theft hypothesis posits that in environments with poor water, sanitation and hygiene (WASH) infrastructure, frequent enteric infections negate the benefits of dietary intake.9 Data from the Global Burden of Disease (GBD) study confirm that while diarrhoea-related mortality has decreased, the morbidity burden remains a dominant factor associated with developmental deficits in sub-Saharan Africa.10 Enteric pathogens act as biological bottlenecks, causing malabsorption and chronic systemic inflammation that diverts metabolic energy away from bone growth towards immune activation.9 10

Furthermore, the conversion of economic resources into health is closely linked to critical infrastructure. Reliable electricity access enables cold-chain integrity, essential for the preservation of nutrient-dense proteins and the reduction of post-harvest losses.11 Longitudinal analyses suggest that household electrification is independently associated with significant improvements in child height, even after adjusting for income levels.11 Similarly, maternal education serves as a structural foundation for progress, empowering caregivers to optimise resource allocation and clinical service utilisation, yet the efficiency of this conversion remains underexplored.12

Health systems governance introduces another layer of complexity. While political stability is generally viewed as a prerequisite for development, some authors have identified a potential ‘stability paradox’.13 Predictability required for long-term planning can simultaneously eliminate the political competition and accountability necessary to promote public welfare, a dynamic often associated with bureaucratic inertia or the entrenchment of inefficient health delivery models. For instance, nations like Equatorial Guinea have maintained decades of regime stability without corresponding health gains.14 Conversely, identifying ‘positive deviants’, nations that achieve near-frontier nutritional outcomes despite significant resource constraints, offers a unique opportunity to identify scalable delivery models. Rwanda serves as a prime example; its political stability facilitated a centralised agenda that dramatically improved nutrition by optimising systems.15

In this study, we employ a 23-year longitudinal panel of 54 African nations (2000–2022) to estimate a stochastic health production frontier. By applying the Battese and Coelli16 framework, we aim to quantify the nutrition conversion gap across the continent. Our approach moves beyond descriptive epidemiology to evaluate national performance by assessing conversion efficiency rather than just resource levels. Against this background, this study seeks to quantify cross-country differences in the efficiency with which African nations convert economic, nutritional and social resources into reductions in child stunting.

Methods

Study design and spatiotemporal scope

We performed a longitudinal panel study to assess the systemic performance of nutritional outcomes across the African continent. The study population included all 54 sovereign member states of the African Union. The temporal scope spans 23 years, from 2000 to 2022, encompassing major developmental shifts from the inception of the Millennium Development Goals (MDGs) to the mid-term evaluation of the SDGs. This longitudinal design was chosen to account for time-varying efficiency and to observe the long-term relationship between structural investments and child health and nutrition. By employing a stochastic health frontier framework, the study design moves beyond descriptive epidemiology towards a performance-based evaluation of national health systems.

Analytical framework and variable selection

Data were synthesised from FAOSTAT, the World Development Indicators and the GBD to construct a multidimensional panel for the 54 African Union member states.17 Data were aligned using universal ISO-3 alpha-3 country codes. The selection of variables for the stochastic frontier model was guided by the UNICEF Conceptual Framework on Malnutrition, distinguishing between immediate biological factors and underlying structural determinants.

Independent structural and clinical measures

Within this framework, GDP per capita and maternal education were treated as structural inputs that define the production frontier, representing a nation’s capacity to achieve nutritional health. Conversely, environmental and institutional factors, such as disease incidence and political stability, were modelled as technical inefficiency covariates to account for the gap between a country’s potential and its observed nutritional outcomes. This distinction is central to the derivation of the health production score (HPS), which measures a country’s technical efficiency in transforming available resources into health gains (online supplemental table S1).

Unlike traditional stunting metrics that measure a deficit, the HPS serves as a positive indicator of a nation’s success in reducing stunting, that is, preserving linear growth in children. This transformation is mathematically necessary for a production frontier analysis, where the objective is to identify the maximum possible health output achievable for any given level of resource input. All monetary values, specifically GDP per capita, were log-transformed and scaled to ensure numerical stability and to interpret results as elasticities. By modelling stunting prevalence as a function of economic and social endowment, the HPS identifies positive deviant nations achieving near-frontier results despite significant constraints, thereby decoupling human development from pure economic expansion.

Addressing biological inertia: the 1000-day development window

Child stunting is a cumulative indicator of anthropometric stock rather than a current flow. Physiological growth deficits observed at age 2 reflect cumulative nutritional, health and economic exposure spanning from conception through the first 24 months of life, although known as the first 1000-day window. To align our econometric model with this biological reality, we integrated primary frontier inputs, GDP per capita, dietary energy supply adequacy and maternal education, using 3-year weighted moving averages (WMA).

By doing so, we minimise the risk of overestimating efficiency due to transient economic shocks and prioritise the health system’s long-term structural capacity.

Data quality and imputation strategy (Guidelines for Accurate and Transparent Health Estimates Reporting compliance)

To ensure geographical representativeness across the continent and minimise selection bias, we addressed non-random missingness, particularly in educational attainment and infrastructural variables, using multiple imputation by chained equations. The proportion of missing data ranged from 0.0% for urban population to 80.7% for survey-derived variables such as maternal education, reflecting the 3- to 5-year intervals between national Demographic and Health Surveys (DHS)/Multiple Indicator Cluster Surveys (MICS) (detailed missingness proportions are provided in online supplemental table S1). To ensure robust SE estimation in accordance with modern imputation guidelines, we performed 80 iterations (m=80) using a predictive mean matching algorithm, strictly matching the number of imputations to the maximum missingness percentage. This method was selected to preserve the non-linear relationships between socioeconomic covariates and to ensure that imputed values remained within the plausible observed range of the African developmental context.

This study adheres to the Guidelines for Accurate and Transparent Health Estimates Reporting (GATHER). To maintain the empirical integrity of the frontier, imputation was restricted to independent covariates; country-years lacking primary anthropometric output (stunting prevalence) were excluded from the main frontier estimation but retained for sensitivity analyses. All data cleaning protocols and R-based transformation scripts were documented to ensure full reproducibility of the analytical pipeline.

To minimise the risk of data circularity, where relationships might be pre-defined during the data-generation processes of external agencies, we used the JME. The JME harmonises primary anthropometric stock data from national DHS and MICS, ensuring the outcome reflects measured child growth rather than purely modelled projections. Furthermore, the use of temporal lags and WMA for structural inputs helps decouple the production frontier from contemporaneous modelling assumptions used in secondary databases.

The stochastic health frontier framework

To determine the maximum achievable nutritional outcome for each nation, we employed a stochastic frontier analysis (SFA) based on the Battese and Coelli specification with a heteroskedastic half-normal distribution. The heteroskedastic half-normal distribution was selected for its empirical stability and superior convergence in our large-scale longitudinal panel. Unlike traditional ordinary least squares (OLS) models, which estimate an average performance, the SFA identifies a theoretical best-practice frontier.16 This frontier represents the maximum biological growth potential (HPS) attainable given a specific stock of economic and educational resources.

The model decomposes the error term into two independent components: Vit, representing idiosyncratic random shocks (eg, climatic events or data noise) and Uit, representing systemic technical inefficiency. We specified Uit using a heteroskedastic half-normal distribution (uit∼N+(0|σuit2)). This choice is empirically superior to the traditional homoscedastic half-normal distribution as it acknowledges that the scale (variance) of systemic inefficiency is not constant, but rather depends on specific structural covariates. The robustness of this framework was validated by the lambda index, which confirmed that the overwhelming majority of the observed variance in child stunting results from modellable systemic gaps rather than random chance.

In this framework, efficiency is defined as technical efficiency, the ratio of observed health output to the maximum achievable output on the frontier. This differs from cost-effectiveness in that it evaluates the productive performance of the health and food system as a whole, rather than the marginal cost of a specific intervention.

Modelling technical inefficiency and biological load

The second stage of our model, estimated simultaneously with the frontier to avoid bias, identifies the environmental and clinical factors associated with the nutrition conversion gap (Uit). A primary focus was placed on the pathogenic load as a key factor associated with biological malabsorption. To establish temporal precedence and address potential bidirectional associations (where malnutrition increases susceptibility to infection), we included the incidence of diarrhoeal diseases with a 1-year lag (T-1). This variable serves as a proxy for biological nutrient theft, where enteric infections negate nutritional intake before physiological absorption can occur.

Beyond disease burden, we included access to electricity as a proxy for the robustness of the cold-chain infrastructure and food preservation capacity. Urban population density was also considered, taking into account proximity to primary healthcare services. Finally, political stability was included to assess the role of good governance and institutional continuity in the effective delivery of nutritional programmes. By modelling these factors as covariates of the variance in technical inefficiency, we isolate the specific bottlenecks associated with the failure to translate economic and structural endowments, represented here by aggregate wealth and education, into human capital, as measured by child stunting.

Statistical analysis and model validation

Data cleaning and harmonisation were conducted in R V.4.5.0. Databases were merged using universal ISO-3 alpha-3 codes to ensure spatial alignment across 54 jurisdictions. To satisfy the assumptions of the stochastic frontier framework, all continuous variables were log-transformed and standardised to stabilise variance and enable interpretation of coefficients as elasticities. To account for the panel structure while preserving slow-moving structural variables (which are often problematically absorbed by country-level fixed effects in macroeconomic panels), temporal dependence was explicitly modelled through 3-year WMA and lagged inefficiency covariates. This feature engineering aligns the statistical mitigation of serial correlation with the biological reality of the 1000-day developmental window.

To determine the maximum achievable nutritional outcome, we estimated a stochastic health production frontier using the Broyden-Fletcher-Goldfarb-Shanno maximum likelihood algorithm. Model selection was guided by the Akaike Information Criterion and the Bayesian Information Criterion, where lower values indicated superior parsimony and fit. The technical inefficiency term (uit) was specified using a heteroskedastic half-normal distribution, which provided superior empirical stability and parsimony for our longitudinal panel (N=1134).

The presence of technical inefficiency was rigorously tested to justify the application of SFA over traditional OLS models. We formally performed Coelli’s skewness test (M3T) on the OLS residuals, where a negative and significant skewness indicates the existence of technical inefficiency, necessitating a frontier approach.16 Furthermore, a one-sided likelihood ratio (LR) test was conducted to compare the log-likelihood of the full SFA model against a restricted OLS null hypothesis (H0:σu2=0).

To ensure the robustness of the frontier estimation and the consistency of national rankings, we performed a series of sensitivity analyses. We tested the stability of the model against: (1) alternative distributional assumptions for the inefficiency term (exponential distribution); (2) variations in the input weighting structure (unweighted 3-year moving averages); (3) alternative temporal lags for technical inefficiency covariates (2-year lag for diarrhoeal incidence); and (4) the influence of extreme economic outliers (leave-one-out analysis excluding Equatorial Guinea). The stability of the resulting efficiency hierarchy was assessed using Spearman’s rank correlation (ρ), with values near 1.0 indicating that the identification of performance outliers is not an artefact of specific modelling assumptions.

To quantify the magnitude of the nutrition conversion gap, we calculated the lambda (λ) and gamma (γ) parameters. Lambda represents the ratio of the SD of the technical inefficiency term to that of the idiosyncratic error (σu/σv), while gamma represents the proportion of total variance attributable to technical inefficiency (σu2/(σu2+σv2)).

To assess the robustness of our simulation findings, we conducted a multi-scenario sensitivity analysis testing alternative diarrhoeal reduction thresholds (10%, 30%, 50% and 75%) in addition to the primary 20% scenario. The rank order of priority nations was compared using Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient (ρ), with values approaching 1.0 indicating stability of the ranking. Results of these sensitivity analyses are presented in online supplemental table S6.

All visualisations were constructed using the ggplot2 and ggrepel packages. The forest plot was generated by mapping standardised coefficient estimates and their 95% CIs from the technical inefficiency sub-model (uit). The quadrant analysis was developed by plotting national mean technical efficiency scores against average GDP per capita, with bubble sizes scaled proportionally to the country-specific mean stunting prevalence. Horizontal and vertical reference lines were set at the continental median values for efficiency and wealth, respectively, to identify performance outliers. Statistical significance was set at a two-tailed p<0.05.

Crucially, we acknowledge that primary inputs (GDP, education) and covariates of technical inefficiency (diarrhoea, stability) are highly interrelated within complex developmental feedback loops. Therefore, the objective of this SFA application is not to establish direct cause-and-effect relationships, but rather to quantify comparative system performance under observed structural constraints. By identifying the frontier of potential, we aim to highlight where resources are most effectively leveraged, regardless of the underlying directional pathways.

Results

Baseline characteristics and systemic disparities

Longitudinal baseline characteristics (2000–2022) revealed profound developmental heterogeneity across 54 nations. Mean HPS was 81.0 (SD 7.0; range 56.0–95.0). Mean stunting prevalence was 19.0% (SD 7.0), with a maximum of 44.0% (online supplemental table S2).

Economic distribution was highly skewed; mean GDP per capita was Int$6134 (SD $6410), with a minimum of $703. Dietary energy supply adequacy remained stable with a continental mean of 111.0% (SD 14.0). Structural variables showed high variance, including maternal education (3.0%–98.0%) and electricity access (mean 44.0%, SD 29.0). The mean burden of diarrhoeal diseases was 12 556 per 100 000 children (SD 8137).

Stochastic frontier validation and technical inefficiency dominance

Diagnostic testing confirmed the validity of the stochastic frontier framework. The LR test was highly significant (LR=267.02, p<0.001), rejecting the null hypothesis of no technical inefficiency. Coelli’s skewness test (M3T=−11.69, p<0.001) confirmed that the residual distribution was characterised by systematic inefficiency.

Sensitivity analyses confirmed the stability of the health production frontier (online supplemental table S5). Crucially, the adoption of a highly rigorous imputation protocol (m=80) to address survey missingness yielded stable parameter estimates and national efficiency rankings, validating the integrity of the underlying data. In addition, a sensitivity model incorporating a linear time trend to control for unobserved continent-wide temporal shocks (eg, MDG/SDG policy shifts) yielded highly consistent parameter estimates (eg, gamma=0.974, GDP elasticity=0.053) and near-perfect rank stability (Spearman’s ρ=0.9959), justifying the parsimony of the primary model. Moreover, the Spearman rank correlation between the primary model and all sensitivity specifications remained remarkably high (ρ=1.000 for alternative distributions and lag structures, and ρ=0.995 for the outlier-adjusted model). Furthermore, the GDP elasticity coefficient remained consistently low across all tests (ranging from β=0.042 to β=0.051), reinforcing the finding that national wealth is only weakly associated with stunting reduction potential when systemic inefficiencies are present. These results indicate that the classification of ‘positive deviants’ and ‘wealth paradox’ nations is a robust structural feature of the data, providing a stable foundation for the subsequent discussion.

Model diagnostics indicated that technical inefficiency is the dominant factor explaining observed disparities. The calculated lambda (λ=6.33) and gamma (γ=0.976) indicate that 97.6% of the variance in child growth potential results from modelable systemic gaps rather than random shocks. Average technical efficiency across the continent was estimated at 0.906. The calculated gamma (γ=0.976) indicates that technical inefficiency accounts for nearly all the observed variance in child growth potential. While high, this figure reflects the structural nature of the African nutritional landscape; the disparities between nations like Senegal and Equatorial Guinea are reflected by deep-seated institutional and infrastructural differences that persist over decades, rather than by transient, random idiosyncratic shocks (vit). By using 3-year WMA, our methodology intentionally smooths year-on-year noise, thereby isolating these persistent systemic gaps as the primary source of variance.

Frontier inputs and inefficiency covariates

Within the production frontier, we identified a low GDP elasticity (β=0.042, p<0.001), where a 10% increase in national wealth correlated with a 0.42% improvement in child growth potential (table 1). Caloric adequacy was not significantly associated with the frontier (p=0.728), while maternal education showed a slight negative correlation with the theoretical maximum (β=−0.0338, p=0.011).

Geographical distribution and clustering

Spatial distribution (figure 3) showed regional clustering of conversion efficiency. An ‘efficiency corridor’ was observed in Northern Africa (Morocco, Algeria, Tunisia) and coastal West Africa (Senegal), with technical efficiency scores consistently exceeding 0.95. High performance was also noted in the Horn of Africa (Somalia). In contrast, an entrenched efficiency gap was visible across the Central African belt and parts of Southern Africa (Botswana, Namibia), characterised by lighter shading in the spatial model.

The Central African Republic (CAR) and DR Congo emerged as primary beneficiaries, with potential stunting reductions of 0.99% and 0.91%, respectively. Significant impacts were also observed in West African nations, including Liberia and Côte d’Ivoire, all achieving a 0.75% reduction.

Anatomy of inefficiency: national rankings

The comprehensive ranking (online supplemental table S3) confirmed that failure to translate resources into growth is nation-specific. Technical efficiency scores ranged from 0.986 in Somalia to 0.774 in Comoros. Middle-income nations often underperformed relative to their lower-income counterparts; Gabon (Int$19 631) achieved an efficiency score of 0.865, lower than that of South Sudan (0.946) and Liberia (0.951), despite significantly higher resource envelopes.

Discussion

This 23-year analysis of 54 African nations mandates an adaptive conceptual evolution of nutrition policy. Our primary finding, that 97.6% (γ=0.976) of the variance in child stunting across the continent is explained by technical inefficiency rather than random idiosyncratic shocks, shifts the nutritional discourse from aggregate resource availability to systemic conversion effectiveness. With a continental lambda (λ) of 6.33, we demonstrate that stunting in Africa is not exclusively tied to absolute poverty, but rather reflects a structural failure of food and health systems to translate resources into human capital. Supporting this, cross-country analyses by Headey18 reveal that while economic growth correlates with success, nutritional gains are closely linked to a nation’s structural capacity to convert wealth into specific social assets, such as healthcare access and female secondary education. Consequently, high stunting rates in resource-rich nations represent a systemic failure to leverage economic gains into these essential pillars of human development.

The strikingly low elasticity of child growth to GDP (0.042, p<0.001) is a sobering result for regional policymakers. It indicates that a 10% increase in national wealth is associated with less than a 0.5% improvement in child growth potential if systemic inefficiencies remain unaddressed. This aligns with the findings of Vollmer et al,7 who observed a null association between economic growth and undernutrition in many low-income contexts. The persistent nutrition conversion gap suggests that available economic assets are lost before they can manifest as linear growth, a decoupling supported by evidence that macroeconomic expansion often fails to reach the poorest quintiles.7 This systemic leakage reinforces the argument that growth alone is insufficient; instead, direct nutrition-sensitive investments are required to bypass the failures of trickle-down effects and ensure that economic gains are translated into human capital.19 20

The quadrant analysis identifies a profound nutrition-wealth paradox in nations such as Equatorial Guinea (rank 53) and Botswana (rank 50). This paradox also mathematically explains the counterintuitive negative coefficients observed in the model. For instance, high diarrhoeal incidence was associated with a smaller inefficiency gap (δ=−0.0167). This does not imply diarrhoea improves efficiency; rather, the poorest nations with the highest pathogenic burdens (eg, CAR, Somalia) have severely depressed theoretical frontiers and thus operate close to their limits (low inefficiency gap). Conversely, wealthier nations (eg, Equatorial Guinea, Botswana) have lower diarrhoeal rates but massive systemic failures, corresponding to a large inefficiency gap. Similarly, the negative coefficient for maternal education on the production frontier equation (β=−0.0338) reflects expected macroeconomic multicollinearity. Post-estimation diagnostics confirmed a strong, highly significant correlation between national wealth and maternal education (r=0.734, p<0.001; variance inflation factor=3.31), a dynamic which frequently induces partial-effect sign-flipping in non-linear maximum likelihood estimations. Setting aside these modelling artefacts, the overarching quadrant analysis reinforces that the highest systemic waste of potential is currently occurring in resource-rich nations. Despite significant resource stocks, these nations fail to reach the biological ‘last mile’ of development. This indicates that while these economies have transitioned, their health and infrastructural conversion systems have not kept pace, a decoupling associated with nutritional waste.21 22 As evidenced in Equatorial Guinea, the highest GDP in our sample has not translated into improved human capital, reinforcing that the quality of public spending is more critical than the volume of wealth.23

Senegal’s high efficiency (TE=0.986) reflects deliberate political design, not merely stable governance. Unlike the inertia identified above, ‘Senegal’s Cellule de Lutte contre la Malnutrition (CLM)’ was placed directly under the prime minister in 2001, with a dedicated national budget line and a formal mandate to coordinate health, agriculture and education ministries. This institutional architecture helped mitigate the collective action dilemma inherent in multisectoral nutrition. Critically, the CLM’s reputation for transparent management and results, reaching 70%–80% of children under five through the ‘Programme national de Renforcement de la Nutrition’, shielded it from political interference, surviving an attempted abolition in 2012. Thus, Senegal converted political stability into accountable, cross-sectoral action rather than bureaucratic drift. The lesson extends beyond institutionalisation: effective coordination requires political insulation and performance-based legitimacy, not just stable governance.24 25 Similarly, Rwanda achieved high efficiency (stunting declining from 33% in 2020 to 27% in 2025) by embedding nutrition within its flagship social protection programme, Vision Umurenge.26 A pilot project (Modelling Nutrition-Responsive Social Protection Interventions) used para-social workers for integrated case management, linking poor households to nutrition-specific services (community health worker-led growth monitoring) and nutrition-sensitive support (kitchen gardens, village savings and loan associations and ‘smart spending’ financial literacy training). This helped decouple child nutrition from household income through deliberate multi-sectoral coordination at the cell level.27 In contrast, Somalia’s near-frontier efficiency, particularly in specific indicators like exclusive breastfeeding, suggests a localised resilience within community networks. This supports the maintenance of essential nutritional services despite the significant infrastructure and data challenges inherent in fragile state structures.28

The exploratory finding regarding the positive association between political stability and technical inefficiency (δ=0.39, p<0.001) suggests a preliminary hypothesis of incumbent inertia. While political stability is a fundamental prerequisite for socioeconomic development, institutional continuity does not necessarily correlate with improved nutrition if it lacks concurrent sectoral reform.29 Botswana exemplifies this pattern: decades of political stability and upper-middle-income status (mean GDP Int$16 000), yet stunting persisted at ~24% with technical efficiency below 0.80 (online supplemental table S3). Here, stability coexisted with bureaucratic continuity without the cross-sectoral accountability mechanisms, such as Senegal’s CLM or Rwanda’s cell-level case management, that help translate wealth into child growth. We posit that political stability without policy translation is often associated with path dependency, potentially delaying the adoption of innovative, nutrition-sensitive policies.30 It is to be noted that political stability goes beyond institutional continuity and encompasses, among other rule of law, governance effectiveness, low corruption, accountability, strong economic performance, absence of violence, etc. Therefore, the relationship between political stability and health conversion gap in each country is mediated by the interplay between the different components mentioned above. Nevertheless, this finding highlights the urgent need for actionable governance, where institutional agility and accountability that enable strong economic performance matter more than the mere absence of conflict for positive health and nutrition outcomes.31 The technical inefficiency sub-model identifies access to electricity as a potent correlate of efficiency (δ=−0.0122, p<0.001). Reliable electrification facilitates cold-chain integrity, which is essential for preserving nutrient-dense animal-source proteins and reducing post-harvest losses within transforming agrifood systems.32 Furthermore, the association with lagged diarrhoea incidence yielded an unexpected negative coefficient (δ=−0.0167), mathematically correlating higher pathogenic loads with a narrower inefficiency gap. We explicitly acknowledge this as an unexpected statistical artefact of macro-level SFA modelling, rather than a biological finding. Because the poorest nations with the highest diarrhoeal burdens (eg, CAR, Somalia) have severely depressed economic inputs, their maximum theoretical frontier is exceptionally low. Consequently, they operate mathematically close to their limits (a small gap). We caution that this structural modelling artefact contradicts the established individual-level biological rationale of ‘nutrient theft’, where enteric infections negate dietary intake through malabsorption and systemic inflammation,33 highlighting a fundamental limitation of using aggregate macroeconomic frontiers to evaluate clinical epidemiological variables.

Policy implications and the roadmap to 2030

Closing the nutrition conversion gap is a systemic engineering challenge rather than a purely economic one. Because macroeconomic expansion alone is insufficient, policymakers must prioritise addressing structural bottlenecks. Evidence from high-efficiency ‘positive deviants’ suggests that strengthening ‘last-mile’ infrastructure, specifically primary healthcare and rural electrification, is vital for human capital. Additionally, scaling WASH systems remains the most cost-effective approach to mitigate biological nutrient theft. Ultimately, governance must shift from mere stability towards performance-based accountability, using frontier diagnostics to reallocate existing budgets towards high-impact delivery models demonstrated by leading nations (online supplemental table S4).

Strengths and limitations

This study has several strengths. First, to our knowledge, it is the first application of SFA to assess national efficiency in reducing child stunting across all African Union member states over more than two decades. The long panel (2000–2022) allows examination of persistent performance differences that are not observable in cross-sectional analyses.

Second, by using a frontier framework, the analysis moves beyond conventional association-based approaches and explicitly quantifies the contribution of technical inefficiency to cross-country variation in stunting outcomes. This perspective is particularly relevant given the finding that technical inefficiency accounts for a substantial share of outcome differences even among countries with similar income and food availability.

Third, while our longitudinal approach minimises idiosyncratic noise, we acknowledge that a γ of 0.976 may suggest that some systematic measurement errors inherent in national-level datasets could be absorbed into the technical inefficiency term. In this context, the conversion gap should be interpreted as a comparative measure of system performance under real-world structural and data constraints, rather than a purely biological measure of efficiency.

Fourth, the use of lagged and smoothed covariates for key inputs and technical inefficiency covariates reduces sensitivity to short-term fluctuations and better reflects the timeframes through which child growth responds to underlying conditions, without asserting causal effects.

The study also has limitations. The stochastic frontier approach relies on distributional assumptions about inefficiency; although diagnostics supported the selected specification, alternative assumptions could yield different estimates. Results should therefore be interpreted as comparative rather than absolute measures of performance.

In addition, the national-level analysis cannot capture substantial subnational heterogeneity in stunting, service delivery or governance. Countries estimated to operate close to the frontier may still exhibit within-country inequalities, while lower-efficiency countries may include high-performing regions not visible in national aggregates.

Moreover, data availability constrains the set of explanatory variables. Notably, survey-derived structural covariates, such as maternal education, exhibited up to 80% missingness across the annual panel due to the 3- to 5-year gaps between national DHS/MICS waves. While multiple imputation (m=80) yielded highly stable rank hierarchies, this heavy reliance on interpolated data underscores the need for caution; the resulting efficiency scores should be interpreted as comparative estimates rather than absolute, precise measures of performance.

Furthermore, our stochastic frontier model relies on the standard assumption of independently distributed idiosyncratic errors (vit). In a 23-year longitudinal panel, unobserved shocks and measurement errors inevitably exhibit within-country serial correlation. While we engineered covariates (eg, moving averages) to account for temporal biological dependence, imposing an autoregressive (AR(1)) structure on the residuals within a heteroskedastic SFA framework proved computationally intractable. Consequently, the assumption of independent residuals is likely violated. While this does not bias the point estimates of the frontier, it may lead to underestimated SEs, and the statistical significance of the covariates should be interpreted with this econometric limitation in mind.

We also acknowledge the limitations inherent in large-scale secondary data. While the JME and GBD use rigorous harmonisation, the potential for smoothed relationships in modelled data exists. Furthermore, because standard SFA relies on point estimates, the inherent estimation uncertainty (credible intervals) of the survey-based data could not be directly weighted into the model. However, the stochastic nature of the frontier specifically accounts for this: the idiosyncratic error term (vit) mathematically absorbs data noise and random measurement error, preventing it from biasing the estimation of systemic technical inefficiency. Indeed, the discovery of a profound and highly variable conversion gap (γ=0.976) across the 54-country panel suggests that the data contain significant systemic residuals that are not explained by aggregate economic or social inputs. This reinforces the conclusion that the identified inefficiencies reflect actual system performance rather than statistical artefacts of the data-generation process.

Finally, data availability constrains the set of explanatory variables. Several associated factors of child growth, such as dietary intake or quality, caregiving practices and health service quality, are imperfectly measured or omitted, and associations between infrastructure, governance and technical inefficiency should not be interpreted as causal.

Conclusion

Our 23-year analysis across 54 African nations suggests that childhood stunting is not an inevitable correlate of poverty, but rather a reflection of significant performance heterogeneity in national health production systems. With systematic conversion gap accounting for a substantial proportion of stunting disparities (γ=0.976), the African nutritional landscape is characterised by a profound conversion gap where economic and caloric assets fail to translate effectively into linear child growth.

Our findings indicate that the incremental association of simple GDP growth for stunting reduction is remarkably low (elasticity: 0.042), suggesting that macroeconomic expansion alone is an insufficient standalone strategy to reach the 2030 SDG targets. The roadmap towards stunting eradication lies in a multifaceted strategy to address systemic bottlenecks. First, targeted clinical interventions to mitigate ‘biological nutrient theft’ through diarrhoea control are essential to improve the biological efficiency of nutritional intake. Second, infrastructural scaling to build cold-chain resilience through electrification is required to preserve dietary diversity, minimise food loss and increase cross-seasonal availability and access to nutritious food.

The performance of Senegal and Somalia as efficiency leaders demonstrates that near-frontier nutritional outcomes are achievable even in resource-constrained or fragile environments. Conversely, the wealth-stunting paradox observed in nations such as Equatorial Guinea and Botswana serves as a cautionary tale: national wealth, in the absence of system optimisation, is associated with significant unrealised health potential.

We conclude that public health policy in Africa must pivot from aggregate resource accumulation towards an efficiency-centred governance model. Bridging the identified conversion gap is not only a statistical possibility but a public health imperative. Prioritising efficiency in governance and delivery systems, therefore, represents one of the most actionable pathways to accelerate progress in child nutrition and strengthen the foundations of the continent’s future human capital.
