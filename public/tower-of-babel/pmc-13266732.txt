How Ethical Principlism Informs Health Care Access Advocacy
Authors: G Richard Holt
Published: 2026

The United States health care system is one of the most technologically advanced in the world, with a notably high sophistication in the provision of advanced care and emergency care for most of the country’s population. Future and current practitioners are comprehensively educated and trained in the contemporary excellence of the science of medicine. Colleagues around the globe envy many American medical schools and hospitals for teaching and providing the state-of-the-art science of health care delivery, including through their research enterprises. However, what is not envied is the inequitable access to care for many patients who cannot access fundamental preventive and longitudinal care throughout their lives. Internationally, many other health care systems achieve a greater level of access to essential care for a wider range of their population.

The American Medical Association’s Declaration of Professional Responsibility guides us with its document on “Medicine’s Social Contract With Humanity” as an overarching reminder to physicians and other health care professionals to remember our mandate to maximize opportunity for all persons to achieve a basic level of positive health in their daily lives. This declaration identifies the commitment to “advocate for social, economic, educational, and political changes that ameliorate suffering and contribute to human well-being.” 1 To that end, it is essential to identify the guidance for moving forward with the access advocacy inherent in the 4 ethical principles of autonomy, beneficence, nonmaleficence, and social justice, the foundations of the moral and ethical responsibilities of physicians and health care systems to individual patients and to society at large. These pillars of medical ethics inform us of our duty to advocate for improving health care access in the United States, with autonomy in health care decision-making representing the important aspect of informed choice and individual rights. However, if access to preventive and basic health care is limited, owing to socioeconomic, geographical, language, and cultural barriers, true autonomy is limited due to a lack of options and freedom of choice. Toward the end of removing barriers to health care access for all persons, we must advocate for population wide access as well as for the smaller community of practice for each practitioner. We can be guided in these efforts by the fundamentals of ethical principlism and by understanding how to apply them to health care access advocacy.

For a country with the level of clinical and scientific resources inherent within the United States, access to health care has become a worrisome sign of the inadequate protection of the health needs of its greater population. In “The 1988 State of Human Rights Address,” the late President Jimmy Carter opined that “A civilized society is judged by how it treats its weakest members. It is therefore not inappropriate to measure countries and cultures by the adequacy of their response to illness, medical care, homelessness, and malnutrition, especially among the helpless, the very young, and the very old.” 2

Whether health care is a “right” or a “privilege” continues to be debated, even in the face of strong support for society’s right to health care by a wide range of medical associations. The American Medical Association’s Code of Ethics Opinion 11.2.7: Responsibilities to Promote Equitable Care notes that “Neither individual physicians nor health care institutions can entirely resolve the problems of discrimination and inequity that underlie health disparities, but they can and must accept responsibility to be agents for change.” 3 Furthermore, the document details potential activities to address barriers to equitable care by both the individual practitioner and their health care institution as strong ethical responsibilities owing to the positions, skills, and knowledge they collectively hold.

In further support of the concept that access to health care is a right, the American College of Physicians published “Health as a Human Right: A Position Paper From the American College of Physicians” in 2023. 4 This paper outlined 4 positions on the “intersection of human rights, ethical obligations, and health reform in recognizing health as a human right,” 2 of which have direct implications for a duty to advocate for equity in health care access:

“Understanding health as a human right can inform the ethical design, implementation, and evaluation of health care delivery.”

“Understanding health as a human right can inform the ethical design, implementation, and evaluation of health care delivery.”

“Health as a human right aligns with—but does not fully encompass—the ethical obligations of physicians, the medical profession, and a just society.”

“Health as a human right aligns with—but does not fully encompass—the ethical obligations of physicians, the medical profession, and a just society.”

Additionally, the position document concluded with the recognition that “a just society will recognize the special role of medical ethics and virtues like compassion, humility, and integrity, among others that are essential to the delivery of health care—traits that are independent of rights.” This emphasis on medical ethics and virtue-based duties well highlights the importance of the professional responsibility to advocate for advancing health policies and methodologies for increased access to health care for all citizens.

Part and parcel to utilizing ethics principlism requires identifying the main barriers to access in the United States. It is commonly held that barriers to access are additive and complex, requiring consideration of strategies for each barrier as part of a more comprehensive plan for health care reform. Financial barriers are myriad and represent a patchwork of “fixes” over decades, none of which has successfully alleviated the ongoing deficiencies in health care access. Although some progress has been made with each iteration of effort, political and socioeconomic challenges continue to frustrate health care practitioners with no clear best solutions in sight. Systemic and geographic barriers are well-known to the health care professions, with both urban and rural populations recognized as disadvantaged. Shortages in primary care and mental health practitioners are effectively blocking timely and comprehensive access care. All practitioners are aware of the cultural and language barriers that can discourage access, or at least effective access, to longitudinal health care. Poverty and food insecurity are nearing a critical level across the country, further contributing to inadequate nutrition and the consequential, downstream health effects for a substantial portion of the population. Institutional indifference to health care access for underserved populations is not new and represents a rich target for advocacy efforts.

Applying ethical principlism to access advocacy across the spectrum of barrier points requires altruism, service-learning, dedication, collaboration, and training. The 4 principles of ethics in health care—autonomy, beneficence, nonmaleficence, and social justice—are fundamentally inherent to our duties to patients and society. The dyadic principles of beneficence and nonmaleficence reflect the importance for practitioners to always be mindful of their duty to protect patients from harm and to provide care that has the best likelihood of a positive health outcome. Morally, these principles entreat us to not only identify the specific needs of a patient, but also to advance the overarching health needs of a community or population, by targeting barriers to access and opportunity for care. Advocation for improved access can be aligned with a professional duty to intervene for better health and reduce negative outcomes across the spectrum of barriers.

While beneficence and nonmaleficence are practitioner-centered requirements, the principle of autonomy has its justification in its patient-centered focus. Respect for autonomy, that is, a person’s right to make informed choices, is particularly important in the United States, which was founded on the principle of its citizens’ individual rights and responsibilities. Not all societies in the world emphasize this person-centered, codified, and expressed freedom of personal autonomy like our society. Balancing the freedom of personal choice is the responsibility for one’s own health care and health choices. Although health care professionals have a duty to encourage personal health choices in their patients, there is also a duty to advocate for the opportunities to make those lifestyle choices possible through access to health evaluations, counseling, and assistive programs. Where there is no effective access to health care, there will be diminished opportunities for health care professionals to impact healthy choices and provide assistance for positive lifestyle outcomes.

The ethical principle most commonly linked to advocacy for health care access is social justice. It reflects the goal of providing opportunities for populations to receive equitable health care, distributed across the plenum of resources available to a society. The barriers to health care and its resources are many, and, in the United States to date, no efforts have been universally effective; in fact, quite the opposite. The recent COVID-19 pandemic uncovered the weaknesses in our public health system, primarily in the provision of basic and longitudinal preventive health care for a major swath of the population. Perhaps one of most substantial efforts to improve access to care in this country would be to support the expansion of a dynamic, inclusive, and highly responsive public health system, particularly in preventive health care. Inequities in access to health care most certainly point to social injustice in all senses of the term.

The Council of Medical Specialty Societies developed an ethics statement in 2018 on the core values for physicians relative to the relationships and responsibilities across the spectrum of health care. 5 These statements directed to physicians include the following. “The physician’s primary, inviolate role is as an active advocate for each patient’s care and well-being. . . . The physician should not exclude or discriminate against any patient. . . . The physician’s commitment to patients includes health education and continuity of care. . . . Physicians have a responsibility to serve the health care needs of all members of society. . . . The physician should seek to reform any system of care in which there is exclusion or discrimination. . . . Physicians should provide knowledgeable input into organizational decisions on the allocation of medical resources and the process of health care delivery. . . . [And T]he physician’s duty of patient advocacy should not be altered by the system of health care delivery in which the physician practices.” Although the statements are directed toward physicians, they are certainly applicable to all health care practitioners and professionals.

Given that improving access to health care is a requirement for health care professionals, the issue becomes how to adequately prepare for acts of advocacy. Understanding ethical principlism is a fundamental requirement because it gives meaning and impetus to the effort. Not all health care professionals are intrinsically suited to be effective advocates or lobbyists for health care access. Yet, there are many roles practitioners can play in advocative efforts. Most require some level of self- or collective education and training to become effective advocates, and that preparation should at least begin in medical school, and continue through residency and into health care practice. At the medical school level, ethical principlism and humanism education furthers the sense of duty for advocacy and health care access. Ensuring that physician advocacy is well-embedded in the curriculum is an important advocacy itself. 6,7

The next level of education and training in health advocacy based on ethical principlism should take place during the graduate medical education period, when resident physicians begin to develop their physician personhood. Having been exposed to ethical principlism in medical school, the reality of barriers to health care access are more keenly appreciated, and the opportunities for advocacy become goals for their future practices. 8,9 Comprehensive ethics curricula focusing on duties and responsibilities to marginalized populations and barriers to health care access are woefully lacking in graduate medical education programs. Yet, in this time of the expansive inculcation of assistive intelligence and technology into the practice of medicine, there has yet to be seen a positive impact on improving health care access. Providing insights into the responsibilities of resident physicians and fellows to engage in advocacy for disenfranchised and marginalized populations, and the mechanisms for such advocacy, would better prepare them to support expanding access within their own communities and broader health policy venues. 10–12

Finally, continuing medical education and engagement through local, state, and national medical society advocacy programs can further strengthen a practitioner’s capabilities for advocacy and provide a solid platform for successful approaches to advocacy, especially among those professionals who feel a sense of duty but do not know quite how and where to start their efforts. 13–15 Throughout the process of preparing for, and undertaking access to, health care advocacy, ethical principlism will be a faithful guide.
