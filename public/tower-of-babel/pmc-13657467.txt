Cost-effectiveness analysis of FundoRing versus standard one-anastomosis gastric bypass: up to 3-year results of a randomized controlled trial
Authors: Oral Ospanov, Galymjan Duysenov, Bakhtiyar Yelembayev, Galymzhan Yeleuov, Zhanbolat Dildbekov
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:The FundoRing modification of one-anastomosis gastric bypass (OAGB) has demonstrated superior clinical outcomes compared with standard OAGB in patients with metabolic syndrome. However, the cost-effectiveness of this innovative technique remains unclear. This study aimed to perform a cost-effectiveness analysis of FundoRing versus standard OAGB using the incremental cost-effectiveness ratio (ICER) and the cost-effectiveness plane.Methods:A cost-effectiveness analysis was conducted alongside a randomized controlled trial including 1000 patients (n = 500 FundoRing, n = 500 standard OAGB) with metabolic syndrome and up to 3 years of follow-up (median: 28 months, IQR: 22–34 months). Effectiveness was measured as the proportion of patients who achieved a target BMI of <30 kg/m2 at their last follow-up visit. Direct medical costs, including surgical intervention, hospitalization, postoperative follow-up, and management of late complications, were calculated from the healthcare-system perspective. Costs were converted from the Kazakhstani tenge (KZT) to US dollars (USD) using the 2025 exchange rate (521.59 KZT/USD). The willingness-to-pay (WTP) threshold was used as a contextual benchmark, with the understanding that the primary conclusion on cost-effectiveness relies on the dominance finding. The incremental cost-effectiveness ratio (ICER) was calculated as the difference in costs divided by the difference in effectiveness. A probabilistic sensitivity analysis with 5000 bootstrap iterations was performed to construct the cost-effectiveness plane.Results:At the last follow-up (median 28 months), the FundoRing group demonstrated significantly higher effectiveness (94.2% vs. 86.7% achieving BMI <30 kg/m2; P = 0.002) and significantly lower total costs per patient (5928.10 USD vs. 6758.00 USD, difference −829.90 USD, 95% CI: −1184.2 to −475.6). The cost savings in the FundoRing group were primarily driven by a reduced incidence of late complications such as dumping syndrome (13.7% vs. 36.5%; P < 0.001), marginal ulcers (0.2% vs. 2.9%; P < 0.001), and bile reflux esophagitis (0.2% vs. 8.1%; P < 0.001). The ICER was −11 065 USD per patient achieving the target BMI. On the cost-effectiveness plane, 100% of the bootstrap iterations fell in the southeast quadrant, indicating that FundoRing is a dominant strategy (lower costs and higher effectiveness).Conclusion:FundoRing modification of OAGB is a more cost-effective strategy than standard OAGB within the studied population and single-center setting. The reduction in late postoperative complications generates substantial healthcare savings while achieving superior clinical outcomes. These findings provide economic evidence to support the use of the FundoRing technique in bariatric surgery. Multi-center studies are needed to confirm generalizability.

Background

Metabolic syndrome (MetS) is a cluster of interconnected risk factors, including central obesity, hypertension, insulin resistance, and atherogenic dyslipidemia, affecting 20–25% of the global adult population[1,2]. This condition substantially increases the risk of cardiovascular disease, type 2 diabetes mellitus, and premature mortality, imposing a significant economic burden on healthcare systems worldwide[3,4].

Among the various bariatric procedures, one-anastomosis gastric bypass (OAGB) has gained widespread acceptance owing to its technical simplicity, shorter operative time, and comparable efficacy to Roux-en-Y gastric bypass[5,6]. The International Federation for the Surgery of Obesity and Metabolic Disorders (IFSO) has recognized OAGB as an established mainstream bariatric procedure[7,8].

Despite its advantages, standard OAGB is associated with several late postoperative complications that affect the patient’s quality of life and increase healthcare utilization. Bile reflux esophagitis, affecting up to 30% of patients, represents the “Achilles heel” of OAGB and often necessitates conversion to Roux-en-Y gastric bypass[9,10]. Marginal ulcers occur in 2–3% of patients and require prolonged proton pump inhibitor therapy and endoscopic surveillance[11,12]. Dumping syndrome affects 35–40% of patients and substantially impairs dietary quality and daily functioning[13,14].

A FundoRing modification of the OAGB was introduced[15]. This technique creates a “living band” around the gastric pouch, providing several theoretical advantages: (1) prevention of bile reflux through mechanical obstruction, (2) an anti-dilatation effect on the gastric pouch, maintaining long-term restriction, (3) protection of the staple line, reducing marginal ulcer risk, and (4) enhancement of lower esophageal sphincter function[16]. Endoscopic ultrasound studies have revealed that FundoRing exerts a significant anti-dilatation effect on the gastric pouch, maintaining a volume of approximately 20 mL, compared with progressive dilatation in standard OAGB[17,18].

Economic rationale

Although the clinical benefits of FundoRing have been established[16,19,20], its economic impact relative to standard OAGB remains unknown. Healthcare decision-makers increasingly require economic evidence alongside clinical efficacy data to inform resource allocation and reimbursement decisions[21]. Cost-effectiveness analysis provides a formal framework for comparing the incremental costs and benefits of competing interventions, with the incremental cost-effectiveness ratio (ICER) serving as the primary summary measure[22].

The cost-effectiveness plane offers a visual representation of the joint distribution of incremental costs and effects, facilitating the interpretation of uncertainty in economic evaluations[23].

Study objectives

Given the absence of economic evaluations comparing FundoRing with standard OAGB, we conducted a cost-effectiveness analysis alongside our randomized controlled trial with the following objectives: To compare the total direct medical costs of FundoRing versus standard OAGB over the follow-up period (up to 3 years).To calculate the incremental cost-effectiveness ratio (ICER) for FundoRing versus standard OAGB.

Materials and methods

Study design and setting

This economic evaluation was conducted alongside a single-center, prospective, randomized controlled trial[24]. The Local Ethical Commission approved the study, which was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. All participants provided written informed consent. The economic analysis adhered to the Consolidated Health Economic Evaluation Reporting Standards (CHEERS) 2022 statement[25] and followed the recommendations of the Second Panel on Cost-Effectiveness in Health and Medicine[26].

Participants

The trial enrolled 1000 patients between January 2021 and December 2024, randomized 1:1 to either FundoRing OAGB (f-OAGB, n = 500) or standard OAGB (s-OAGB, n = 500)[16,19,24]. The inclusion criteria were: (1) metabolic syndrome as defined by the International Diabetes Federation, (2) class I–III obesity (BMI: 30.0–50.0 kg/m2), (3) 18–60 years of age, and (4) American Society of Anesthesiologists Physical Status class I–II. The exclusion criteria included previous bariatric surgery, giant hiatal hernia (>5 cm), reflux esophagitis grade C or D (Los Angeles classification), and previous hiatal herniorrhaphy or fundoplication.

Interventions

FundoRing OAGB (investigational intervention): The procedure included creation of a 10-cm gastric pouch followed by total fundoplication using the excluded (remnant) stomach, wrapping it around the gastric pouch to create a “living ring”[15]. Standard OAGB (comparator): The control procedure followed the Carbajo technique with a 15–18-cm gastric pouch without fundoplication[27].

Both groups received identical perioperative care, including postoperative proton pump inhibitor prophylaxis for 2–6 months and standardized vitamin supplementation protocols.

Perspective and time horizon

The analysis was conducted from a healthcare system perspective, including all direct medical costs related to surgical interventions and the subsequent follow-up period. Patients were enrolled between January 2021 and December 2024, with follow-up data available up to June 2026 (median follow-up of 28 months; IQR: 22–34 months). Indirect costs (productivity losses, patient time, and travel expenses) and non-medical costs were excluded, consistent with the study perspective[28].

Cost measurement and valuation

All costs were measured in Kazakhstani tenge (KZT) for the 2025 price year and converted to United States dollars (USD) using the official exchange rate provided by the Bureau of National Statistics of the Agency for Strategic Planning and Reforms of the Republic of Kazakhstan (521.59 KZT per USD)[29]. Costs incurred in earlier years (2021–2024) were inflated to 2025 values using the Consumer Price Index (CPI) for healthcare services in Kazakhstan, with an average annual inflation rate of 2.8% during the study period.

Willingness-to-pay threshold

The willingness-to-pay (WTP) threshold was referenced as a contextual benchmark, with the understanding that the primary conclusion of cost-effectiveness relies on the dominance finding (negative ICER). According to the Bureau of National Statistics, the GDP per capita for 2025 is 15 003 USD. Following World Health Organization recommendations, interventions with an ICER below the GDP per capita are considered very cost-effective, while those below three times the GDP per capita are considered cost-effective[30]. For this analysis, we used a conservative threshold of three times the GDP per capita (45 009 USD) as a reference value. We emphasize that formal WTP threshold comparison using GDP per capita is typically applied to QALY-based outcomes; therefore, our primary interpretation focuses on the dominance result. EQ-5D-5L data were collected within the trial and will be presented in a separate QALY-based analysis.

Incremental cost-effectiveness ratio

The incremental cost-effectiveness ratio (ICER) was calculated as follows:

ICER = (C_f − C_s)/(E_f − E_s)

where C_f and C_s represent the mean total costs per patient in the FundoRing and standard OAGB groups, respectively, and E_f and E_s represent the proportion of patients achieving the target BMI <30 kg/m2 in each group[31].

The ICER represents the additional cost required to achieve one additional successful outcome (patient reaching target BMI) with FundoRing compared with standard OAGB. A negative ICER indicates that FundoRing is both less costly and more effective (dominant strategy)[32].

The economic framework for this analysis follows standard principles of trial-based cost-effectiveness analysis. The ICER is calculated as the ratio of the difference in mean costs to the difference in effectiveness, where effectiveness is defined as the proportion of patients achieving a BMI <30 kg/m2. This approach is appropriate when the primary clinical outcome of the RCT serves as the effectiveness measure, allowing direct linkage between clinical and economic findings.

Cost-effectiveness plane

Probabilistic sensitivity analysis was performed using non-parametric bootstrap resampling with 5000 iterations[33,34]. For each bootstrap sample, we calculated the mean incremental costs and effectiveness between groups. These 5000 pairs of incremental cost-effectiveness estimates were plotted on the cost-effectiveness plane, with incremental effectiveness on the x-axis and incremental costs on the y-axis.

The distribution of bootstrap replicates across the four quadrants of the cost-effectiveness plane was interpreted as follows: Southeast quadrant: Lower costs, higher effectiveness (dominant strategy)Northwest quadrant: Higher costs, lower effectiveness (dominated strategy)Northeast quadrant: higher costs and higher effectiveness (a trade-off requiring ICER evaluation)Southwest quadrant: lower costs and lower effectiveness (a trade-off requiring ICER evaluation)

Statistical analysis

All analyses were performed on a modified intention-to-treat basis, including all randomized patients with available follow-up data (n = 956). Patients were excluded from the analysis only if they were lost to follow-up (n = 44 in total; 24 in the FundoRing group, 20 in the standard OAGB group). Denominators differ across outcomes because some patients had missing data for specific laboratory tests or clinical assessments at the time of analysis, and these missing values were not imputed, which is consistent with a complete-case analysis within the modified intention-to-treat framework. A worst-case-scenario sensitivity analysis was conducted to assess the impact of missing data on the results.

Baseline characteristics were compared using an independent samples t-test for continuous variables and the chi-square test for categorical variables. Between-group differences in cost and effectiveness were assessed using two-sample t-tests with 95% confidence intervals. For non-normally distributed cost data, non-parametric bootstrap confidence intervals (percentile method with 5000 replications) were calculated[35].

One-way deterministic sensitivity analyses were conducted to assess the robustness of the findings to variations in key parameters: (1) discount rate (0%, 3%, and 5%), (2) exchange rate (±10%), (3) complication costs (±20%), (4) procedure costs (±15%), and (5) effectiveness measure (alternative definition of success: BMI <25 kg/m2, ≥50% excess weight loss, or ≥5% TWL)[36]. A worst-case scenario analysis was also performed to assess the impact of missing data.

Statistical significance was set at P < 0.05 (two-tailed). All analyses were performed using Microsoft Excel for Mac, StatPlus:mac Pro (AnalystSoft Inc., Walnut, CA, USA), and R version 4.3.1 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria) with the “boot,” “BCEA,” and “dampack” packages for economic analysis.

Clinical outcomes at last follow-up (median: 28 months).

The proportion of patients achieving the primary effectiveness outcome (BMI <30 kg/m2) was significantly higher in the FundoRing group (94.2% vs. 86.7%; absolute difference, 7.5%; 95% CI: 3.7–11.3%; P = 0.002). Mean BMI at last follow-up was 25.92 ± 2.03 kg/m2 in the FundoRing group compared to 27.53 ± 3.25 kg/m2 in the standard group (difference: −1.61 kg/m2; 95% CI: − 2.00 to −1.22; P < 0.001)[16,19]. The change in BMI from baseline was also significantly greater in the FundoRing group (−14.12 vs. −12.96 kg/m2; P = 0.006).

Cardiovascular risk, assessed by the TC/HDL-C ratio, improved significantly more in the FundoRing group (2.74 ± 0.69 vs. 2.93 ± 0.67; P < 0.001), primarily driven by a reduction in total cholesterol and LDL-C rather than by an increase in HDL-C. Glycemic control was superior in the FundoRing group, as evidenced by lower HbA1c levels (5.25% vs. 5.5%; P = 0.004) and HOMA-IR (1.8 vs. 2.23; P < 0.001).

ICER = −11 065 USD per additional successfully treated patient

Using alternative definitions of success (BMI <25 kg/m2: 68.7% vs. 60.1%, difference 8.6%), the ICER was −9650 USD; using ≥50% excess weight loss (90.2% vs. 82.1%, difference 8.1%), the ICER was −10 246 USD; and using ≥5% total weight loss (96.8% vs. 89.2%, difference 7.6%), the ICER was −10 920 USD, confirming that the findings are robust to different effectiveness definitions.

A negative ICER indicates that FundoRing is both less costly and more effective than standard OAGB, meeting the formal definition of a dominant strategy in health economic evaluation.

Cost-effectiveness plane

The cost-effectiveness plane generated from 5000 bootstrap resamples of incremental cost and effectiveness pairs showed that all 5000 bootstrap iterations fell in the southeast quadrant, indicating that FundoRing is consistently less costly and more effective than standard OAGB across the entire range of sampling uncertainty. This provides strong evidence that this dominance is robust and not attributable to chance variation.

Discussion

Principal findings

This first cost-effectiveness analysis of the FundoRing modification versus standard OAGB, conducted alongside a large randomized controlled trial with follow-up of up to 3 years (median: 28 months)[16,19,24], demonstrates that FundoRing is a dominant strategy – simultaneously less costly and more effective than standard OAGB. The mean cost savings of USD 829.90 per patient (a 12.3% reduction in total health care expenditures), combined with a 7.5% absolute improvement in achieving a target BMI <30 kg/m2 yield a negative ICER of USD −11 065 per additional successfully treated patient. The cost-effectiveness plane confirms the robustness of these findings, with 100% of 5000 bootstrap iterations in the dominant south-east quadrant.

Interpretation of economic findings

The economic superiority of FundoRing derives primarily from substantial reductions in late postoperative complications, which generate significant downstream cost savings. Bile reflux esophagitis, the “Achilles heel” of OAGB, was reduced by 97.5% (from 8.1% to 0.2%)[16,19], eliminating the need for endoscopic surveillance, prolonged acid-suppressive therapy, and potential revision surgery. Marginal ulcers decreased by 93.1% (from 2.9% to 0.2%)[16], avoiding costs associated with extended proton pump inhibitor therapy, repeated endoscopies, and rare but expensive complications. Dumping syndrome was reduced by 62.5% (from 36.5% to 13.7%)[20], decreasing the requirement for dietary counseling, pharmacotherapy, and additional outpatient consultations.

These complication-related cost savings outweigh the modest incremental cost of the FundoRing procedure itself (+60.50 USD, 1.0% of total costs), resulting in net cost savings that emerge within the first postoperative year and accumulate over time. The extended follow-up (up to 3 years) captures most of these benefits.

Regarding the surgeon's learning curve, our institutional experience suggests that proficiency is achieved after 20–30 cases[15]. Analysis of early cases (the first 25 procedures in each group) versus late cases (the subsequent 475 procedures) showed no statistically significant differences in complication rates or costs (P = 0.34 for complications; P = 0.28 for costs), suggesting that the technique is reproducible with acceptable consistency.

While our analysis is limited to the 3-year follow-up period, the cost savings observed are likely to accumulate over time, as the FundoRing group demonstrated significantly lower rates of chronic complications that require ongoing management (dumping syndrome, bile reflux esophagitis, and marginal ulcers). Markov modeling for lifetime cost-effectiveness is planned as part of our ongoing research, using the observed complication rates and costs to project long-term outcomes.

Comparison with published literature

To the best of our knowledge, no previous economic evaluations have compared modified fundoplication techniques in OAGB. However, our findings are consistent with cost-effectiveness analyses of other bariatric procedures. Campbell et al reported that Roux-en-Y gastric bypass was more cost-effective than sleeve gastrectomy, with an ICER of 16 500 USD per QALY gained[38]. Klebanoff et al found that bariatric surgery was cost-saving within 2–4 years postoperatively, owing to reduced diabetes and cardiovascular medications[39]. Our finding of cost savings within the follow-up period aligns with these studies, while demonstrating even more favorable economics for the FundoRing modification.

Implications for clinical practice and health policy

These findings have several important implications:

For bariatric surgeons: The economic evidence supporting FundoRing adds to the established clinical benefits[16,19,20], providing a compelling rationale for adopting this technique. The modest learning curve (20–30 cases for proficiency, based on our institutional experience[15]) is justified by substantial long-term benefits.

For hospital administrators: Implementing FundoRing as the standard OAGB technique would reduce overall costs by approximately 0.83 million USD per 1000 procedures, freeing resources for other priorities.

For payers and health policymakers: The dominance of FundoRing suggests that reimbursement policies should favor this technique. For dominant strategies, the decision is unequivocal: adoption improves outcomes while reducing costs.

For patients: Beyond the clinical benefits of fewer complications and better weight loss, patients may experience reduced out-of-pocket expenses for medications, fewer work absences for medical appointments, and improved quality of life.

Comparison with willingness-to-pay threshold

While the GDP-based WTP threshold is commonly used in health economics, it is most appropriately applied to QALY-based outcomes. As our primary effectiveness measure was BMI achievement, we do not formally compare our ICER to this threshold. Instead, the economic superiority of FundoRing is established through its dominance – simultaneously lower costs and higher effectiveness – which requires no threshold for decision-making. This methodological approach is consistent with best practices for trial-based economic evaluations when the primary outcome is not a utility-based measure.

Conclusions

This cost-effectiveness analysis, conducted alongside a large randomized controlled trial, demonstrated that the FundoRing modification of one-anastomosis gastric bypass (OAGB) is a dominant strategy compared to standard OAGB for patients with metabolic syndrome[16,19,24]. The FundoRing technique achieves superior clinical outcomes, including higher rates of achieving target BMI, better weight loss maintenance, improved metabolic parameters, and dramatically reduced late complications[16,19,20,37], while simultaneously generating substantial cost savings of 829.90 USD per patient over the follow-up period (up to 3 years). The incremental cost-effectiveness ratio was negative (−11 065 USD per additional successfully treated patient), and the cost-effectiveness plane confirmed dominance with a 100% probability across all 5000 bootstrap iterations. Sensitivity analyses demonstrated robustness to variations in key parameters, exchange rates, discount rates, and effectiveness definitions.

These findings provide economic evidence that supports the adoption of FundoRing as the preferred technique for one-anastomosis gastric bypass within the framework of this analysis and in the studied population. For the healthcare system in Kazakhstan, the implementation of FundoRing as the standard of care would generate annual savings exceeding USD 0.83 million per 1000 procedures. For bariatric surgeons, the combination of superior clinical outcomes and economic benefits justifies the modest learning curve required to master this technique[15]. For patients, reduced complication rates translate into improved quality of life and lower out-of-pocket expenses. In an era of constrained healthcare resources, interventions that simultaneously improve outcomes and reduce costs – the dominant strategies – represent the highest value for money and should be prioritized for adoption and reimbursement. However, given the single-center nature of this study, multi-center validation studies are needed before definitive policy recommendations can be made.
