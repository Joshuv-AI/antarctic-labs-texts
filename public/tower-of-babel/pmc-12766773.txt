Culturally adapting and evaluating an evidence-based communication intervention with HPV self-sampling to improve cervical cancer screening among women living with HIV in Ghana: a mixed-methods study
Authors: Matthew Asare, Nancy Innocentia Ebu Enyan, Victoria Leighton Sencherey, Esther Lamptey-Mills, Sebastian Ken-Amoah, Patrick Kafui Akakpo, Rodney X Sturdivant, Dorcas Obiri-Yeboah
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesWe aimed to refine and culturally adapt an evidence-based communication intervention (EBCI), which consists of the 3R message framing model (Reframe, Reprioritise, Reform) and human papillomavirus self-sampling (HPVSS) for use in a teaching hospital in Ghana, and evaluated its acceptability, feasibility, appropriateness and adoption potential among stakeholders.DesignConvergent mixed-methods design.SettingThe study was conducted at a teaching hospital and its surrounding communities in the Central Region of Ghana.ParticipantsA 36-member stakeholder advisory board comprising women living with HIV (WLHIV) (n = 14), healthcare providers (HCPs) (n = 11) and community members (n = 11) participated in Nominal Group Technique sessions to adapt the intervention. The adapted EBCI was subsequently evaluated by 45 participants (WLHIV=30 and HCPs=15).Outcome measures included key characteristics of the EBCI, acceptability, feasibility, appropriateness and its potential for adoption, which were assessed using validated Likert-type scales and structured interview guides.ResultsCore components of the intervention (HPVSS+3R) were retained. Hospitals and community pharmacies were the preferred self-sampling venues (97%). WhatsApp audio in English and Fante/Akan was the most favoured delivery mode for 3R messages (81%). Evaluation results revealed high acceptability (mean=22.84), feasibility (mean=22.40) and adoption (mean=21.73) on a 5–25 point scale, as well as appropriateness (mean=13.3) on a 3–15 point scale. Qualitative findings highlighted convenience, privacy, empowerment and cultural relevance, which reduced fear and increased participant engagement.ConclusionThe adapted EBCI demonstrated high acceptability, feasibility, appropriateness and adoption potential among key stakeholders, supporting its integration into the Ghanaian health systems to advance cervical cancer elimination goals.Trial registration numberNCT06739772.

Introduction

Cervical cancer (CC) is a leading cause of cancer-related deaths among women in Ghana, with an annual incidence rate of 29.3 cases and a mortality of 23.8 per 100 000 women.1 2 Each year, nearly 2800 women in the country are diagnosed with the disease.3 For women living with HIV (WLHIV), the CC burden is compounded due to a combination of factors such as biological susceptibility,46 limited healthcare access,79 socioeconomic constraints10 and societal stigmas.9 11 Addressing these multifaceted challenges requires comprehensive strategies, including screening and vaccination against human papillomavirus (HPV), which are known to reduce the CC burden.12 Current screening methods in Ghana include Pap smear, visual inspection with acetic acid and HPV DNA testing.13 However, these screening methods remain underused in Ghana, and thus, screening rates are alarmingly low, with reports indicating coverage below 4%.14 Factors such as cultural beliefs, misinformation, inadequate access to healthcare services, high cost, infrastructure limitations, low public awareness and no organised national screening programme have contributed to the underutilisation of those screening methods, hence low screening rates in Ghana.15 16

HPV self-sampling (HPVSS)

The introduction of HPVSS has the potential to address challenges and improve CC screening uptake among WLHIV.1721 Research demonstrates that HPVSS is sensitive to HPV DNA testing2225 and serves as a viable alternative in low-resource settings.23 24 Our studies in Ghana found self-sampling to be culturally acceptable1721 and effective in detecting HPV infections and potential precancerous lesions among WLHIV.20 Other studies further indicate that self-sampling is acceptable, feasible, cost-effective and sustainable in LMICs.23 24 26 27 However, limited awareness, uncertainty about self-collection and fear of results continue to hinder scale-up.28 Effective communication strategies are therefore essential to counter misinformation and build public confidence in HPVSS. The 3R (Reframing, Reprioritising and Reforming) communication model2932 has the potential to increase HPVSS.

The 3R communication model

The 3R (Reframing, Reprioritising and Reforming) model2932 is a motivational communication framework designed to enhance awareness and confidence in self-sampling while reducing fear, misconceptions and stigma surrounding CC screening. Reframing shifts how screening is communicated by highlighting both potential gains and losses, for example, emphasising early detection benefits while noting the risks of delayed care. Reprioritising encourages individuals to recognise that screening now is easier and less costly than managing cancer later. Reforming promotes a positive view of self-sampling by correcting myths and misunderstandings. In our recent studies, integrating HPVSS with the 3R model, collectively referred to as the evidence-based communication intervention (EBCI), led to increased screening and treatment uptake and reduced stigma.29 30 Despite these promising outcomes, the practical adoption and integration of EBCI into hospital systems remain uncertain. Therefore, a systematic cultural adaptation process to refine the EBCI and ensure it aligns with the sociocultural and healthcare context of Ghana is needed. This study, which is part of an ongoing larger clinical trial with its protocol published elsewhere32, reports findings from the EBCI adaptation and evaluation processes. The primary purpose of the current study was to describe the development and cultural adaptation of the EBCI (HPVSS+3R model) in a teaching hospital in Ghana. The secondary purpose was to evaluate the characteristics (ie, feasibility, acceptability, appropriateness and adoptability) of the EBCI among WLHIV and healthcare providers (HCPs).

Materials and methods

Study design

This study used a mixed-methods design,33 in which qualitative data informed the adaptation of the intervention, and quantitative data were used to evaluate the intervention characteristics. The qualitative component used Nominal Group Techniques (NGTs),34 35 and the quantitative component used a survey.

Participant recruitment

We established a 35-Stakeholder Advisory Board (SAB) comprising WLHIV (n=13), community members (CMs) (n=11), and HCPs (n=11). The SAB members were recruited from the Cape Coast Teaching Hospital (CCTH) and the surrounding community, with eligibility criteria tailored to each group. WLHIV were eligible if they were HIV-positive and due for cervical screening; they were identified and recruited through the hospital’s e-tracker system. CMs were eligible if they were 18 years or older and held a leadership role in the Cape Coast area, such as a chief, political leader, teacher, pastor or clergy member. Recruitment was conducted through face-to-face invitations and snowball sampling. HCPs at CCTH were recommended by hospital management based on their roles and relevance to the study. The research team subsequently met with each recommended provider, described the study and invited those interested to participate.

NGT procedure

During the NGT sessions, the HPVSS and the 3R communication model were introduced to the SAB members. We explained the step-by-step guidance on specimen collection and also asked them to listen to the prerecorded audio of the 3R messages. The messages were in three modules, with one module for each of the 3R concepts. Module 1 focused on Reframing (changing perspectives on HPVSS). Module 2 focused on Reprioritising (highlighting the importance of screening) and Module 3 focused on Reforming (encouraging behaviour change towards screening uptake). Following these presentations, the board members engaged in structured discussions using the NGT framework. Each member was asked to generate ideas on how they want self-sampling to be distributed and how 3R communication content should be disseminated. SAB members discussed those generated ideas for clarification, finalised them by grouping the ideas and then ranked them by preference. The highest-ranked ideas were selected for further evaluation in the pilot-testing phase.

Evaluation of the EBCI

During the evaluation phase, we retained all the HCPs and WLHIV who were part of our SAB. To increase the sample size for the evaluation, we recruited an additional four HCPs and 17 WLHIV from the same hospital. In total, 45 participants, HCPs (n=15) and WLHIV (n=30), evaluated the EBCI. They individually reviewed the final version of the EBCI components and were offered the opportunity to ask questions for clarification. After that, they completed a hand-delivered survey and follow-up interviews.

Survey

We adapted Weiner et al’s validated Likert scale survey36 to assess the intervention characteristics informed by Damschroder’s Consolidated Framework for Implementation Research (CFIR).37 The survey included subscales for acceptability (five items), feasibility (five items) and appropriateness (three items). In addition to these validated subscales, we developed five items to assess adoptability, a subscale not included in the original survey. The adoption potential subscale assesses the respondents’ own potential willingness to adopt the intervention. All items were rated on a 5-point Likert scale, ranging from ‘strongly disagree’ (1) to ‘strongly agree’ (5). See online supplemental document for the survey items.

Interview

To gain a deeper understanding of the participants’ survey responses, we conducted follow-up face-to-face interviews with WLHIV and HCPs who had completed the postsurvey. Questions explored reasons behind their ratings, such as perceptions of self-sampling and audio messages and factors influencing their potential for adoption. A trained qualitative researcher fluent in English and local languages conducted the interviews, which lasted 25–30 min each. Data collection concluded after 15 interviews with WLHIV and 10 with providers, when thematic saturation was reached.38 See the online supplemental document for the interview guide.

Data analysis

We analysed the data sequentially, where the NGT data were analysed first, followed by the survey data, and then the interview data.

Survey

Quantitative data were analysed using descriptive statistics (ie, frequencies and means) to summarise participant ratings of the intervention characteristics. We assessed the differences in scores by HCPs and WLHIV. All quantitative data analysis was performed using R software.39

Qualitative

Data from the NGT discussions and follow-up interviews were transcribed verbatim and analysed. NGT rankings were analysed to guide intervention refinement and improve relevance for the target population. We used a hybrid deductive–inductive thematic approach in NVivo for the interview data. The initial codebook was deductively derived from the CFIR conceptual framework with predefined domains, including feasibility, acceptability, appropriateness and adoption potential. We then used open coding on a subset of transcripts to identify emergent codes, allowing for inductive refinement. This iterative process ensured that the codebook captured both predefined theoretical constructs and novel insights from the data. Two coders (EA and AE) independently read and coded all transcripts, identifying patterns, shared experiences and divergent perspectives.40 Coders met regularly to compare coding decisions and refine code definitions to maintain consistency. As new codes were identified, the coding team rereviewed previously coded transcripts to ensure consistency across all data. Where coding discrepancies arose, they were discussed and reconciled through consensus. If an agreement could not be reached, a third team member (MA) with qualitative expertise reviewed the transcripts and provided an independent judgement to resolve the issue, thereby ensuring analytical rigour and credibility. The analysis revealed subthemes reflecting participants’ perspectives on the EBCI.

Patient and public involvement

Stakeholders (ie, WLHIV, HCP and CM) were actively involved in developing and evaluating the toolkit. However, stakeholders did not participate in shaping the dissemination plans for this research.

Results

Demographics

A total of 36 SAB members: WLHIV (n=14), HCP (n=11) and CM (n=11) participated in the NGT. For the evaluation phase, 30 WLHIV and 15 HCPs completed the survey, and follow-up interviews were conducted with 15 WLHIV and 10 HCPs. Among WLHIV, over 85% had health insurance, 80% had lived with HIV for more than 5 years, yet 64.3% had never been screened for CC, and 71.4% had never heard of self-sampling. CM included political leaders, teachers, clergy and professionals, while HCP were primarily midwives, physicians and nurses (see table 1).

Nominal group results

The core components of the EBCI, the self-sampling kit and the 3R communication model were retained, but the peripheries of the intervention were modified. Participants showed clear preferences for how HPVSS kits and the 3R messages should be delivered. Hospitals and community pharmacies were the most preferred kit distribution points, and WhatsApp was the preferred mode of delivery for the messages. WLHIV strongly favoured these locations, while CMs and HCPs also supported community and home-based options. Final adapted intervention features were selected based on participant rankings. Evaluation results

Overall, participants rated the intervention on scales ranging from 5 to 25. The rates for acceptability (mean=22.84, SD=2.57), feasibility (mean=22.40, SD=1.99), and adoption potential (mean=21.73, SD=3.17) reflected strong positive perceptions. Appropriateness was also rated highly, with a mean of 13.33 (SD=1.86) on a 3–15 point scale, indicating strong perceived relevance of the intervention. Subgroup analyses showed statistically significant differences between HCPs and patient participants. However, the mean scores for both groups indicated that the intervention was rated favourably (see table 2).

Acceptability subscale analysis

Survey results showed high acceptability of the intervention, with 80–100% of participants agreeing or strongly agreeing that the EBCI was acceptable. Qualitative findings reinforced these results, revealing three key themes: convenience, privacy and reduced fear that explain participants’ positive perceptions.

Theme 1: convenience

Participants described self-sampling as convenient, highlighting the ability to collect samples privately, at a preferred time and without the constraints of clinic schedules.

Self-sampling…is more convenient because women can take it in the comfort of their homes (Participant 11, HCP)

Theme 2: privacy

Participants described privacy as central to the intervention’s acceptability, reporting that the ability to self-sample at home increased their sense of security and confidence in participating.

People who feel uncomfortable in a hospital setting can take the sample at home…they can feel more secure and maintain their privacy (Participant 6, WLHIV)

Initially, we were concerned about our privacy. We didn’t know who would be taking the sample…. But now that I know I can take the sample myself, it gives me hope, and I am willing to do it (Participant 10, WLHIV)

Theme 3: allay fear

The 3R messages effectively eased emotional barriers by addressing fears related to screening and potential diagnoses. Participants noted that the messages reassured them and increased their willingness to participate.

Initially, I was fearful, but the audio explained that even if you’re infected…it can be treated. Hearing this was very reassuring (Participant 7, WLHIV)

Appropriate subscale analysis

Survey findings indicate that 73–100% of participants agreed or strongly agreed that the intervention was appropriate. Qualitative results supported these perceptions and revealed four central themes that explain why participants viewed the EBCI components as suitable for their needs: minimal time commitment, literacy support, cultural sensitivity and autonomy.

Theme 1: minimal time commitment

Participants indicated that the 3R messages and the self-sampling process required minimal time to complete. The concise, focused audio messages and the quick procedure made the intervention manageable, even for those with busy schedules. While WLHIV viewed the time commitment as reasonable, a few providers expressed concern that some patients might find the amount of information and time overwhelming.

…yes, it was reasonable because time was not wasted (Participant 4, WLHIV)

The audio wasn’t that long… the messages were not overloaded and the contents were simple, with detailed information for you to know what you need to know (Participant 2, HCP)

For most patients, the time needed for the messages and self-sampling seems manageable, but I worry that some may feel overwhelmed by the amount of information provided (Participant 3, HCP)

Theme 2: literacy support

Participants noted that they could replay the messages, listen to them at their own pace or privately seek help for an explanation of the messages when needed.

Sometimes they play the audio twice because it will help them understand better… could ask for explanation from a trusted friend or family member if needed … thereby supporting patients with limited literacy. (Participant 6, HCP)

Theme 3: culturally sensitive and reduced stigma

Participants described the EBCI messages as culturally appropriate. Self-sampling also enhanced cultural sensitivity by allowing women who were uncomfortable with male providers, often for religious or cultural reasons, to test privately. Participants further noted that the discreet nature of the process reduced fears of stigma.

It was recorded in my native language… nothing in my beliefs or culture goes against it (Participant 8, WLHIV)

… the content and everything were culturally appropriate for me (Participant 9, WLHIV)

There is nothing like stigmatization because nobody will know you have the kit to test… Doing it by myself means no one will start rumors (Participant 8, HCP)

Themes 4: sense of autonomy

Participants emphasised that self-sampling provided a sense of control and dignity. The ability to collect their own samples reduced embarrassment, encouraged proactive health behaviour and reframed CC screening as an act of self-care.

Doing it myself makes me feel like I’m taking care of my health… I have freedom to do something for my own body (Participant 12, WLHIV)

Self-sampling is the way to go because I can take my own sample and feel comfortable… If someone else does it for me, I might feel shy (Participant 13, WLHIV)

Feasibility subscale analysis

Survey findings indicated that 87–100% of participants agreed or strongly agreed that the intervention was feasible. Qualitative data reinforced these findings, revealing three themes that explain why participants viewed the EBCI as practical and easy to implement: effective messaging and the practicality of self-sampling.

Theme 1: effectiveness of messaging

Participants noted that the prerecorded messages were clear, informative and motivating. They indicated that the messages emphasised early detection, the importance of knowing one’s health status and the availability of treatment.

The audio outlined three steps in our native language… taking your own sample gives you hope; screening is the only way to know your health status; and using the kits means taking a proactive step. Treatments are available if your results come back positive… (Participant 17, WLHIV)

Theme 2: self-sampling as a practical and alternative option

Participants indicated that it is practical to collect samples without requiring clinic visits, thereby reducing waiting times or other logistical challenges. Participants also noted self-sampling as an alternative to traditional clinic-based cervical screening.

It’s practical because waiting in line to be screened by a health professional would take too much time. Self-sampling would save time for everyone, and I believe it’s the best approach (Participant 17, WLHIV)

Taking the kits home and doing it myself would be easier than being screened by someone else. I think it is feasible, … solves transportation and other barriers (Participant 13, WLHIV)

When … people know they can take the samples themselves without coming to the facility where they may be stigmatized… this will bring in more people to get screened (Participant 4, HCP)

Adoption potential subscale analysis

Survey findings showed that 77–100% of participants agreed or strongly agreed that they would be willing to adopt the intervention. Qualitative data further supported strong potential for adoption, highlighting two key themes: preferences for kit delivery locations and the cost-effectiveness of the approach.

Theme 1: self-sampling location preference

Participants reported that hospitals and pharmacy stores are the preferred locations for self-sample kit delivery. However, HCPs viewed home-based collection as a practical way to reduce logistical barriers, but many WLHIV preferred clinic or pharmacy pick-up.

Home-based collection seems like an efficient … But we also understand that some patients may still prefer picking up the kit at a clinic or pharmacy where they feel more supported (Participant 7, WLHIV)

I wouldn’t feel comfortable doing the test at home. … I’d rather get it from the clinic or pharmacy where it feels more private (Participant 15, WLHIV)

Theme 2: affordability

Participants also recognised the cost-effectiveness of the intervention. Self-sampling reduces travel expenses, limits time away from work and can be implemented within health facilities with minimal operational costs.

…, everybody will buy it. Because it saves transportation cost. …and less costly. … it does not involve heavy overhead costs for implementing it in the hospital (Participant 7, HCP)

Discussion

The study aimed to refine and culturally adapt the EBCI to ensure its relevance and suitability for implementation within a teaching hospital in Ghana. We also evaluated key implementation outcomes: acceptability, feasibility, appropriateness and adoptability, among stakeholders.

The EBCI’s core components, self-sampling and the 3R communication model, were intentionally preserved during adaptation because of their strong empirical grounding, relevance to CC prevention and demonstrated effectiveness in prior research.29 30 Retaining these evidence-based components ensured fidelity while allowing for culturally meaningful refinements. This approach reflects best practices in implementation science, which emphasise balancing fidelity with contextual adaptation to enhance relevance and uptake.41 42 The cultural adaptation process, particularly through the NGT, was critical for refining language, delivery preferences and implementation strategies. These adjustments improved cultural fit and acceptability, aligning with literature demonstrating that successful implementation depends on both evidence strength and contextual alignment with stakeholder needs and local systems.43 44

3R communication model

Our findings show that the 3R communication messages were viewed as highly acceptable, appropriate, feasible and adoptable. The culturally tailored audio format improved understanding, comfort and motivation, with clarity and brevity, especially benefiting participants with lower literacy and supporting informed screening decisions.41 42 Emotional reassurance reduced fears and increased confidence in screening,43 while clear explanations and emphasis on treatability helped counter fatalistic beliefs that hindered participation.44 WhatsApp emerged as the preferred delivery method due to its accessibility, user-friendliness and privacy, echoing broader evidence supporting mHealth tools in resource-limited settings.45 46 However, participants also recommended complementary platforms like radio, television and community events, highlighting the importance of multimodal outreach. Bilingual messaging, especially combining English with Fante/Akan, was praised for improving both comprehension and emotional connection. Furthermore, by framing screening as a form of self-care and emphasising autonomy, the intervention promoted personal agency and self-efficacy, critical drivers of health behaviour change.47 A notable and contrasting finding from our study concerns perceptions of the time allocated for delivering the 3R model messages. While patients generally found the 5 min duration per module appropriate, some even wanted more time and information, providers viewed it as excessive, fearing it might overwhelm or disengage patients. This divergence suggests that providers may act protectively, assuming that brevity benefits patients when, in fact, patients are open to more comprehensive communication. Such paternalistic assumptions may unintentionally restrict meaningful engagement. Our findings echo previous research documenting misalignments between provider assumptions and patient preferences.45 46

HPV self-sampling

HPVSS was widely endorsed as acceptable, appropriate, feasible and adoptable across participant groups. Participants consistently described self-sampling as a convenient and discreet alternative to clinic-based screening. The opportunity to collect samples at a personally preferred time and location enhanced comfort and autonomy, aligning with prior evidence showing that privacy and control substantially improve screening uptake, particularly in contexts where embarrassment and modesty are significant barriers.48 The findings also highlighted self-sampling as a key strategy for reducing stigma-related obstacles, particularly among WLHIV, who frequently experience discrimination in clinical settings.4951 The ability to complete the test privately, whether at home, a community centre or a pharmacy, was viewed as restoring dignity and transforming screening from a source of fear or shame47 52 into an act of self-care. Self-sampling was further perceived as a practical response to structural barriers such as long waiting times, travel demands, financial burdens and overcrowded clinics, challenges that often deter participation in traditional screening programmes.53 54 Its simplicity, minimal infrastructure requirements and cost-effectiveness strengthened perceptions of its adoptability, reinforcing its suitability for integration into health systems in low-resource settings.55 56 Both patients and providers recognised its economic and operational benefits, supporting its potential for scale-up in low- and middle-income countries.57 58 Participants also reported feeling more in control of their health through the self-sampling process, which aligns with research showing that increasing women’s agency in screening enhances participation and self-efficacy.59 60 A notable finding was the strong preference regarding the delivery of self-sampling kits. Whereas some CMs and HCPs endorsed mailing kits to reduce logistical barriers, WLHIV expressed discomfort with home delivery due to privacy concerns and fear of stigma if family members encountered the kits. Instead, patients preferred to pick up the kits from pharmacies or hospitals. This contrasts with studies showing that home-based self-sampling is often preferred in underserved settings,61 62 but aligns with evidence from sub-Saharan Africa showing that stigma and fear of disclosure strongly shape preferences.63

Limitations

Conducting the study at a single site limits generalisability, as perspectives may differ in regions with varying healthcare systems and cultural contexts. Although acceptability and feasibility were assessed, long-term screening uptake and clinical outcomes were not measured. Future studies should evaluate sustained behaviour change and health impact. Broader dissemination strategies will be needed to ensure equitable access. Expanding the intervention to other women at risk could further increase its public health value. Finally, this study did not account for potential overlap between SAB members involved in intervention adaptation and those who participated only in the evaluation, which may have resulted in more favourable assessments of the intervention. Future research should distinguish between developers and independent evaluators to strengthen rigour and generalisability.

Strengths

The study highlights the synergistic effect of integrating self-sampling with the 3R communication model to improve CC screening among WLHIV in Ghana. This combined approach addressed structural and psychosocial barriers by pairing a practical, stigma-reducing method with culturally resonant messaging, resulting in a user-centred, scalable strategy aligned with implementation science principles. The rigorous participatory process, using the NGT, ensured culturally relevant message content and delivery.

Implications

This study underscores key considerations for strengthening and scaling EBCIs in low-resource settings. Targeted training and support for HCPs are essential for smooth integration. While English messaging enhances broader reach, incorporating local languages improves cultural relevance and comprehension. Participants preferred shorter, more engaging audio messages, and although WhatsApp was favoured, a multimodal approach, including radio, TV and community forums, can expand access. Strong patient support for self-sampling and alignment with existing health systems indicates strong potential for scale-up, though rural logistical issues must be addressed.

In conclusion, the adapted EBCI shows strong potential to improve CC screening uptake among WLHIV and other underserved groups in Ghana. Its high implementation ratings, cultural fit and low resource demands support broader adoption within the hospital system. Continued provider engagement, integration into existing health structures and assessment of long-term effectiveness will be essential for sustaining impact.
