A personal sensing technology enabled service versus a digital psychoeducation control for primary care patients with depression and anxiety: a pilot randomized controlled trial
Authors: Colleen Stiles-Shields, Karen M Reyes, Tanvi Lakhtakia, Shannon R Smith, Olga E Barnas, Elizabeth L Gray, Charles J Krause, Kaylee P Kruzan, Mary J Kwasny, Zara Mir, Sameer Panjwani, Steven K Rothschild, Lisa Sánchez-Johnsen, Nathan W Winquist, Emily G Lattie, Nicholas B Allen, Madhu Reddy, David C Mohr
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTechnology-enabled services (TES; clinical services that include both technology-driven [e.g., personal sensing technologies] and person-powered support elements) may address gaps in depression and anxiety treatments in healthcare settings. The current study: (1) developed a TES tailored for Primary Care patients with depression and/or anxiety, and (2) conducted a pilot randomized controlled trial to assess the efficacy of the TES compared to a digital psychoeducation control app.MethodsParticipants were randomized to either: (1) TES: the “Vira” smartphone app (Ksana Health Inc.), informed by behavioral activation and using passive sensing technology to provide behavioral “insights” and target behaviors associated with mental health symptoms, alongside lay-provider coaching, or (2) Control: the Mood Education mobile app (ME), containing static psychoeducational resources designed to target mental health symptoms. Both apps collected usage data. Participants completed assessments on depression (PHQ-9), anxiety (GAD-7), health-related quality of life (PedsQL), and engagement (TWEETs) at baseline, mid-treatment (week 4), end-of-treatment (week 8), and post-treatment (week 12).ResultsParticipants (N = 130) were randomized to receive either the TES (Vira; Mage= 30) or ME (Mage= 33). Linear mixed-effects models determined significant improvements in PHQ-9 and GAD-7 scores for participants across both conditions (ps < 0.001). There was no interactive effect of intervention and time for the PHQ-9 (p = .90) nor the GAD-7 (p = .49). Adjusting for baseline differences and randomization strata, TES participants reported a greater change in a quality of life rating of Physical Functioning (PedsQL) across time (p = .018). TES participants also reported higher levels of engagement and demonstrated higher app usage. However, adjusting for baseline symptom severity, neither app usage nor coach interaction frequency moderated outcomes (ps ≥ 0.2).ConclusionsWhile the TES demonstrated superior engagement, improvements in depressive and anxious symptoms for both conditions speak to the potential benefit of both TES and low-intensity psychoeducation treatments in care settings. Future research is needed to better understand which patients might differentially benefit from TESs and broader personal sensing technologies over low-intensity treatments.Trial registrationClinicalTrials.gov NCT05406791.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12888-024-06284-z.Keywords: Technology-enabled service, Passive sensing technology, Low-intensity treatments, Depression, Anxiety, Primary care

Introduction

Primary Care is often the de facto treatment site for depression and anxiety for youth and adults alike in the U.S [1]. These healthcare services can be particularly important for those living in communities that are excluded from accessible and timely specialist mental healthcare [2, 3]. Yet, multiple systemically-driven factors impact the ability of primary care to meet the growing mental healthcare needs of patients, including a lack of symptom assessment or identification, suboptimal treatment and limited referral options, and treatment access barriers [4–9]. Notwithstanding the success of collaborative and integrated care, and community health worker models [10–12], additional methods are necessary to meet the mental health needs of patients in these healthcare settings.

Digital approaches are increasingly considered to be viable options for improving capacity for depression and anxiety care [13]. Technology-Enabled Services (TESs) - clinical services that include both technological and person-powered support elements - have demonstrated efficacy and may be delivered more efficiently than traditional care [14, 15]. Further, TESs may also integrate personal sensing technologies, using data from sensors in consumer devices (e.g., smartphones) to estimate behaviors, psychological states, and environmental conditions [16]. The integration of personal sensing technology into TESs promotes the ability to passively track behaviors, which may provide a better assessment of behavioral patterns outside the consultation, and inform timely interventions to support behavior changes for patients with depression and anxiety.

Despite their success in research settings, challenges with engaging patients and clinicians have been encountered in the implementation of many TESs in real-world healthcare settings [17, 18]. There are multiple systemic factors that contribute to this failure, including practices whereby the design of digital interventions is primarily informed by input from more privileged groups who are not representative of real-world patients [19–21]. Additionally, the design of these digital interventions and the associated human support processes have often failed to take into account the implementation needs of healthcare organizations (e.g., time for training, equipment, competing priorities). This can result in a mismatch between the technological solution and needs of the healthcare system and their existing workflows [17].

To address these contributors to the research-practice gap, the Accelerated Creation-to-Sustainment (ACTS) Model [22], informed by human-centered design (HCD) and implementation science, specifies iterative TES development and implementation processes throughout the design, trial, and sustainment phases. The first phase, Create (or Adapt) employs HCD to design or adapt a digital intervention, the human support protocol, and the implementation plan concurrently, engaging with all key informants (patients, clinicians, staff, etc.). The second phase, Trial, optimizes the TES and implementation plan and evaluates efficacy and sustainability. The final phase is Sustainment, which permanently maintains the implemented TES in a real-world setting [22].

Purpose

Grounded in the ACTS Model [22], during the first phase we adapted a TES for primary care patients with depression and anxiety who are served by an Academic Medical Center situated on the West Side of Chicago, IL. The results of this phase are described in the Methods. The purpose of this pilot trial was to assess the TES on reductions in symptom severity and engagement relative to a control condition (i.e., a psychoeducation app). Primary outcomes were primary care patients’ depression and anxiety symptoms, and secondarily, their health-related quality of life (QOL). We hypothesized that: (1) participants in the TES condition would have greater reductions in depressive and anxious symptoms, compared to participants in the control condition; (2) those allocated to the TES condition would exhibit higher levels of engagement, as demonstrated by objective and subjective markers; and (3) higher engagement would have downstream effects on depression and anxiety symptoms. Exploratory analyses were also conducted to examine whether patient characteristics (e.g., experiences of perceived discrimination, behavioral health characteristics) moderated outcomes across treatments.

Methods

Ethical considerations

All procedures were approved by the Northwestern University Institutional Review Board (IRB; STU# 00211887) and via a relying protocol approved by the IRB at Rush University Medical Center (“RUSH”; ORA# 18050901). Participants completed electronic informed consent, and teen participants completed electronic assent and parent/guardian permission. The trial was preregistered on ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT05406791).

ACTS model, create: TES for primary care

To provide context for the trial described in this current study, here we provide background on the first phase of the ACTS model, Create, focusing on the development of the technology, the coaching protocol, and an implementation plan for the deployment of a TES [22]. Of note, the launch of the Create phase for the development of this TES coincided with the start of the COVID-19 pandemic. Further, the study setting was Rush University Medical Center (“RUSH”), which is situated on the West Side of Chicago, IL, and serves a diverse patient population, including its nearby community members who are systemically afflicted with health disparities [23, 24]. As such, increased agility in design and implementation planning was required to serve patients and a healthcare system responding to unprecedented crises and implementing novel safety protocols.

Responding to pandemic-related delays in technology development, the study team identified an existing app, Vira by Ksana Health (Ksana Health Inc.) [25], which met the study criteria for digital intervention tools supported by personal sensing data collection and the use of phone sensors (see “Interventions” below for more information about Vira). Taking a human-centered design approach, RUSH Family Medicine patients were recruited to complete semi-structured interviews to investigate their preferences and perceived utility of passive sensing and wearable tracking technologies to improve mental health targets (e.g., topics of symptom self-management, current and desired use of sensor data, and comfort sharing such data with providers) [26]. Specifically, patients were interested in using sensor data to help them anticipate and manage their mental health symptoms. Consistent with a behavioral activation approach, patients described difficulties initiating activities that they knew to be beneficial for themselves when they were experiencing symptoms. They felt that a TES incorporating sensing could help them to: (1) quantify the good things that they do for themselves daily; and (2) discern patterns that they could change to improve their mental health when they experienced many symptoms [26]. While self-tracking mood was perceived to be beneficial, patients expressed preferences for passive sensing, rather than more intensive active self-tracking, since it would add little burden to their day-to-day life and could uncover patterns in behavior that could be changed to better support their mental health. Because we were working with a commercial product, the study team worked with Ksana Health to prioritize modifications that fit with the company’s product development plan, which included adopting daily check-ins on patients’ sense of enjoyment and accomplishment. In addition to these daily questions, patient feedback on patterns that they would like to be more aware of (such as sleep time) and their desire to connect these patterns to meaningful change led to both changes in data visualizations within the app as well as the ways coaches supported patients through weekly texts. In accordance with the ACTS Model, the trial was launched once essential changes to the Vira app for this study had been completed [22]. However, additional improvements to the app continued to be made during the trial.

Concurrent with the technology development, RUSH Family Medicine physicians and staff participated in iterative HCD processes to develop an implementation plan and service protocol. The original implementation plan was developed with Family Medicine leadership and envisioned that the service clinician, or coach, would be either a Care Manager housed within Collaborative Care or Medical Assistants already engaged in enrolled patients’ care. However, discussions with key informants revealed that pandemic-related changes in workflows, including a shift in Care Manager roles to focus only on medication adherence, and increased duties for Medical Assistants, had made the initial plans no longer viable. As such, consensus among key Family Medicine collaborators drove the decision that in the context of intensified care and pandemic-related needs, service provision needed to be redesigned and first be evaluated through providers without clinic responsibilities. Therefore, coaching responsibilities were managed centrally by study staff (i.e., lay-provider coaches) and the service protocol was amended to fit the needs and talents of these types of coaches [27].

Rather than iterating to perfection [22], once major flaws in these TES elements were identified and addressed, the TES progressed to a pilot feasibility trial. Our description of the multi-level design and pre-implementation activities is meant to illustrate how the Create phase activities provided opportunities to engage key players (e.g., patients, clinicians) to form a pragmatic and feasible foundation for eventual implementation and sustainment of TESs [22].

ACTS model, trial: TES for primary care

Participants

Participants were eligible if they: (1) were a U.S. citizen/resident (for compensation purposes); (2) lived in the U.S. during the 12-week study period (due to passive sensing differences across international spaces); (3) were 13 years or older (if aged 13–17, a parent/caregiver provided permission); (4) were English speaking; (5) were eligible to receive care through RUSH University Medical Center or its clinical affiliates; (6) owned a smartphone running an up-to-date operating system on an Android (9 or greater) or iPhone (13 or greater); (7) had used a smartphone in the last 7 days; and (8) reported at least moderate symptoms of depression (Patient Health Questionnaire-9 [PHQ-9] [28] score ≥ 10) and/or symptoms of anxiety (Generalized Anxiety Disorder-7 [GAD-7] [29] score ≥ 8). Participants were ineligible if they reported: (1) severe suicidality at baseline, as defined by the presence of a plan and intent to act on that plan, or (2) having had a diagnosis of a severe mental illness such as schizophrenia, for which the current TES would be inappropriate.

Site

The trial took place at RUSH clinic-based sites situated on the West Side of Chicago, IL (i.e., family medicine, primary care, internal medicine, collaborative care, psychiatry, pediatrics, community health clinics, the Adolescent Family Center).

Procedures

Recruitment took place between June 2022 and June 2023. Patients were recruited in-person from RUSH clinic-based sites described above and through community events hosted by RUSH and its community partners (e.g., back-to-school fairs, wellness events); the RUSH electronic health record (EHR; MyChart/Epic); social media and other platforms; and through subject recruitment registries managed by RUSH.

Interested individuals completed an initial online screening survey to assess eligibility. Potential participants reported age and, for those between 13 and 19, school enrollment status; as well as depressive symptoms via the Patient Health Questionnaire-4 (PHQ-4; first two items assess anxiety, latter two items assess depression) [30], with a score of 3 or higher acting as a pre-qualifier (i.e., in clinical practice, indicates need for further assessment). A member of the study team contacted individuals as needed to clarify unclear eligibility (e.g., unknown phone software version, eligibility for receiving care at RUSH). Eligible individuals were routed to an IRB-approved online consent form. Those under 18 years of age were asked to provide contact information for a parent or legal guardian, who was contacted by a member of the study team for relationship verification and permission to participate. Of note, guardian permission was available in both English and Spanish and a RUSH qualified bilingual study staff member was available to complete verbal guardian permission in Spanish.

Consented individuals completed a baseline assessment containing the PHQ-9 and GAD-7 questionnaires, by which eligibility for randomization was determined. Participants were subsequently randomly assigned to either the TES [Vira app + Coaching] or the control condition (Mood Education mobile app [ME]). Randomization was stratified by age across three categories: (1) minors/adolescents (13–17 years; in the U.S., this age group typically requires guardian consent); (2) emerging adulthood or young adults (18–25 years; an underserved age range in which young adults are more likely to have behavioral health needs and engagement in risky behaviors while simultaneously less likely to access care [31]); and (3) later adults (26 + years). Participants in both groups received a phone call from either their coach (TES) or a research assistant (ME) informing them of their randomization assignment. Those in the TES condition were scheduled for a follow-up 45-minute onboarding phone call, which served to ensure that the Vira app was downloaded and functional, establish a relationship between the participant and coach, clarify expectations and roles of the participant and coach, and guide and support the participant in setting goals. Those in the control condition were scheduled for a 10-minute call to ensure the Mood Education app was downloaded and functioning properly.

Interventions

Technology enabled service: vira + coaching

Participants randomized to the TES condition used Ksana Health’s “Vira” Mental Health Platform with support from a study coach for eight weeks [25]. Vira’s passive sensing technology harnesses principles of behavioral activation (BA), an evidence-based treatment for depression and anxiety [32], to: passively collect data indicative of risk-relevant behavioral patterns and psychological states from phone sensors (i.e., measures of physical activity, sleep patterns, mobility, and language patterns reflecting mood states and cognitive styles). Upon installation, participants were asked to grant the app permission to collect various sensor based data streams, which were then processed on their device using validated algorithms and displayed to the user on their Today (home) screen. The Vira app also prompted users to reflect on their senses of enjoyment and accomplishment over the previous day through two daily questions. During an initial 10-day period, the app assessed the relationship between the user’s patterns of behavior and their day-to-day variations in mood and well-being using passive mobile sensing. After this period, the app continued to collect data and offered the user up to two personalized insights per week about behavioral factors (e.g., sleep, movement, location, or communication patterns) that may relate to their mood. For example, Vira could suggest that “you seem to enjoy the days when you drive more” or “you tend to enjoy the day more when you wake up earlier.” The Vira app guided users to reflect on these insights and create a behavior change plan by exploring more detailed information about the insight (i.e., insight details) and accessing in-app and external resources, such as links to articles about health behaviors and behavior change. A detailed description of the Vira Platform and its use in a coach supported mode can be found in a prior publication [25]. Vira also sent users mental health surveys (i.e., PHQ-8 and GAD-7) every two weeks to measure changes in depression and anxiety symptoms. In addition, Vira contains psychoeducational resources for managing symptoms of depression and anxiety. Participants were reminded that completion of the first ten check-ins was necessary to unlock insights. Beyond this, participants were reminded that daily check-ins help to improve the accuracy and utility of the insights.

Participants in the TES condition also received support from bachelor’s-level coaches who received training and supervision. The manualized coaching protocol [27] uses the Supportive Accountability and Efficiency models of coaching [33, 34], which focus on encouraging app use, ensuring that participants understand how to use the app, and supporting participants to use the psychological strategies represented in the Vira app in their daily lives. Coaches monitored participants’ activity and insights through the Vira provider dashboard to provide appropriate affirmations (e.g., “You’re on a roll using Vira! What’s been helping you to use it so regularly?”, “Even small changes are still changes.”, etc.) and help participants with weekly goal-setting (e.g., Walk around outside two times this week, Make plans to get dinner with a friend and catch up, etc.). Coaches sent participants text messages twice weekly, either positively reinforcing use or offering assistance if no app usage was observed (platform supported by Audacious Software). Coaches also sent pre-formatted nudges (notifications), recommended based on passive data by the Vira program, to participants through the Vira app to encourage behavior change. In rare cases, coaches also had phone calls with participants to re-engage participants and address persistent problems related to app usage. Coaches did not engage in clinical support that might be considered consistent with psychological treatment.

During the trial, there were several updates to the Vira app. These included conceptual changes, such as home screen and graphical visualization alterations, added and revised iconography, insight structure and location, and minor updates to the existing written content. These updates also included numerous performance-based changes, such as faster data display, improved global positioning system (GPS) uploads, and data accuracy indicators so that participants could weigh-in on whether the sensing data collected seemed accurate. Consistent with evaluating intervention principles rather than specific designs [35], none of these updates changed the basic methods of interaction with the app or the psychological strategies provided by the app.

Control: mood education mobile app (ME)

Participants randomized to the control condition used the Mood Education Mobile App (ME) for eight weeks. ME was designed by researchers at the University of Virginia to deliver psychoeducational content to help people understand and self-manage symptoms of stress, depression, and anxiety [36]. The content provided on ME was not updated at any time throughout the duration of the study and remained static across participant use. Participants in the control condition did not receive any human support (i.e., coaching), nor did they receive any nudges or messages across treatment.

Assessments

Participants completed a battery of questionnaires via REDCap [37] at baseline, mid-treatment (week four), end of treatment (week eight), and post-treatment follow-up (12 weeks after app installation). The assessments required 10–20 min. Participants received a $25 gift card (or check, if preferred) for completion of each survey.

Demographics

At baseline, participants self-reported age, gender, sex assigned at birth, ethnicity, race, insurance status, if they currently see a primary care physician, education (including current school enrollment status), employment, marital status, and income.

Mental and behavioral health

Depression and anxiety symptoms were assessed at all time points. Depression was measured via the Patient Health Questionnaire-9 (PHQ-9), a 9-item instrument for screening, diagnosing, monitoring, and measuring the severity of depression [28]. Anxiety was measured via the General Anxiety Disorder-7 (GAD-7), a 7-item questionnaire used as a screening tool and severity measure for generalized anxiety disorder (GAD) [29]. For both measures, higher rated items indicated greater symptom severity. Both measures also demonstrated acceptable reliability with the current sample (i.e., PHQ-9 α = 0.75, GAD-7 α = 0.83).

To characterize the broader mental health of participants, the DSM-5 Level 1 Cross-Cutting Symptom Measure was completed at baseline. The DSM-5 Level 1 Cross-Cutting Symptom Measure is a 25-item instrument that assesses mental health domains that are important across psychiatric diagnoses [38]. The adult version of this measure includes domains of: Depression, Anger, Mania, Anxiety, Somatic Symptoms, Suicidal Ideation, Psychosis, Sleep Problems, Memory, Repetitive Thoughts and Behaviors, Dissociation, Personality Functioning, and Substance Use. For adolescent participants, the “Teen” version of this measure was administered, which includes domains of: Somatic Symptoms, Sleep Problems, Inattention, Depression, Anger, Irritability, Mania, Anxiety, Psychosis, Repetitive Thoughts and Behaviors, Substance Use, and Suicidal Ideation. Items for the domain of Suicidal Ideation were not administered for adult or adolescent participants to alleviate assessment burden, as this construct was already assessed via the PHQ-9.

The Alcohol Use Disorders Identification Test-Concise (AUDIT-C), a 3-item instrument that assesses alcohol use, abuse, and/or dependence [39]. The measure is scored on a scale of 0–12, with a score of 4 and 3 considered positive in men and women, respectively. The higher the score, the more likely it is that a person’s drinking is affecting their safety.

Engagement

Objective engagement was measured via app launches. Subjective engagement was assessed at mid-treatment (week four) and end of treatment (week eight) via the Twente Engagement with Ehealth Technologies Scale (TWEETS), a 9-item self-report instrument that measures behavioral, cognitive, and affective engagement with e-health technologies [40].

Health-related quality of life

Health-related quality of life was assessed at baseline, mid-treatment (week four), at the end of treatment (week eight), and post-treatment (week twelve) via the Pediatric Quality of Life Inventory (PedsQL). The PedsQL is a 23-item, self-rated measure of health-related QOL in children, adolescents, and adults who are considered healthy and/or with acute and chronic health conditions [41]. The PedsQL yields a total score (0-100, with higher scores indicating better health-related QOL), as well as subscales measuring Psychosocial Health (emotional, social, and work/school functioning) and Physical Health.

Perceived discrimination

Perceived discrimination was assessed at baseline and end of treatment (week eight) via the Everyday Discrimination Scale (EDS). The EDS is a 9-item measure of subjective experiences of daily discrimination, followed by a follow-up question about what the respondent believes is the reason for that daily discrimination (e.g., race, age, weight) [42].

Self-stigma for help seeking

Stigma toward seeking care was assessed at baseline, mid-treatment (week four), and end of treatment (week eight) via the Self-Stigma of Seeking Psychology Help (SSOSH). The SSOSH is a 10-item scale measure of self-stigma associated with seeking psychological support services [43].

Data analysis

Categorical demographic and clinical variables were reported as frequency and percent, while continuous variables were reported as median and interquartile range (IQR). Linear mixed-effects models were used to evaluate continuous primary (PHQ-9, GAD-7) and secondary (PedsQL) scores over time on the full sample. The trial used Intention to Treat; for participants with interim missing data (e.g., missed a mid- or end-of-treatment assessment, but had a subsequent outcome recorded), values were imputed using Markov Chain Monte Carlo methods (1 imputation), and then five datasets were created for the missing data for the participants considered “drop outs” or “lost to follow-up” (i.e. monotone missingness) using a propensity score method of imputation (using SAS v9.4 PROC MI). Results from mixed models were then combined using PROC MIANALYZE to obtain appropriate inferences. Forty-eight tests of moderation were conducted for both the PHQ-9 and GAD-7 to evaluate changes across treatment and through follow-up (i.e., age, sex assigned at birth, race, marital status, household income, education, therapy status, medication status, SSOSH, AUDIT, DSM-5 Level 1 Cross-Cutting Symptom Measure Domains, EDS [total score and by reason for discrimination]). Given the pilot nature of the current study, the statistical approach relied upon the resulting estimates and confidence intervals. For this reason, we did not control for multiple comparisons. Analyses were performed using SAS, version 9.4 (Cary, NC, USA), and R (Vienna, Austria). A total sample of 130 provided 80% power to detect effect sizes of 0.5, using an independent t-test at a Type I error rate of 0.05, which equates to a difference in PHQ-9/GAD-7 scores of 2.5 (assuming a standard deviation of 5).

Results

Participants

Figure 1 displays the CONSORT diagram. Of the 398 people who completed initial screeners, 220 were originally reached out to via a MyChart invitation, 77 through the RUSH Clinical Trials website, 74 through a clinic flyer or clinician referral, 14 from communications deployed by the RUSH Education and Career Hub (REACH), ten from a RUSH-sponsored Community event, and three from unknown recruitment sources. Of the 130 participants enrolled and randomized, 11.5% (15/130) met criteria for depression only (10 TES, 5 Control), 22.31% (29/130) met criteria for anxiety only (13 TES, 16 Control), and 64.62% (84/130) had comorbid depression and anxiety (42 TES, 42 Control). Approximately half of the participants in the sample (54%, 70/130) were engaged in therapy or taking psychotropic medication (52%, 67/130) at enrollment, with 38% (49/130) engaged in both. No participants endorsed severe suicidality (i.e., presence of a plan and intent to act on that plan) at any assessment time point and there were no adverse events (e.g., suicide attempts, psychiatric hospitalization). Health-related quality of life

Adjusting for baseline differences and randomization strata, the secondary outcome, PedsQL, demonstrated significant changes across time. The PedsQL Physical Functioning subscale, adjusted for baseline differences and randomization strata, showed significantly greater improvements over time in the TES condition (e.g., week 8 [end of treatment] change of 3.0 [-1.7, 7.7]) relative to the Control condition (change of 0.8 [-3.7, 5.4], P = .018). However, the TES condition overall scored significantly lower QOL than the Control condition throughout the trial, including at baseline (i.e., the TES condition least square mean [95% confidence interval]: 58.6 [51.1, 66.0], and the Control condition: 70.5 [63.1, 78.0]).

While there was an effect for time for Emotional Functioning (change of 11.26 [7.82, 14.70]), Social Functioning (change of 5.08 [2.08, 8.08]), and School/Work Functioning (change of 4.19 [0.69, 7.69]), there was no evidence to suggest differences by treatment assignment.

Moderation

As exploratory analyses of moderation, we conducted 48 tests for both of the primary outcome measures using demographic characteristics (age strata, sex, race, marital status, household income, education level, current therapy, current medication) and the total and subscale scores of the SSOSH, AUDIT, DSM-5 Level 1 Cross-Cutting Symptom Measure, EDS (see Supplemental Table 1). For the PHQ-9 from baseline to week 8, there was some evidence that the TES may be more effective than ME for participants endorsing higher scores on the AUDIT ([estimated slopes] TES: − 0.074 [-1.12, -0.27]; Control: 0.28 [-0.18, 0.74]). Additionally, there was some evidence that the TES may be more effective than ME in sustained impact (from baseline to week 12) for those reporting more experiences of discrimination on the EDS (TES: -0.06 [-0.20, 0.09]; Control: 0.14 [0.01, 0.28]). For the GAD-7, there was some evidence that the TES may be more effective than ME from baseline to week 8 for participants who endorsed less sleep problems on the DSM-5 Level 1 Cross-Cutting Symptom Measure (TES: 6.9 [4.5, 9.2]; Control: 11.1 [9.1, 13.1]). For those who reported more experiences of discrimination on the EDS, there was some signal that ME may be more effective in sustained impact (from baseline to week 12) for those reporting perceived discrimination by ancestry (TES: 10.6 [8.3, 12.9]; Control: 7.7 [5.3, 10.1]), race (TES: 9.7 [7.6, 11.9]; Control: 8.1 [6.1, 10.1]), or physical appearance(TES: 8.8 [7.1, 10.5]; Control: 6.0 [3.7, 8.3]). No other potential moderation was observed for the PHQ-9 or GAD-7.

Discussion

Principal results

The current study conducted a preliminary examination of the effectiveness of a TES (Vira app + coaching) relative to a psychoeducation control app (ME) for treating symptoms of depression and anxiety. Both interventions demonstrated improvements in depression and anxiety symptoms, however, there was no evidence to suggest superiority in symptom reduction for a specific treatment arm. Participants allocated to the TES reported greater improvements in their physical functioning in terms of their health-related QOL. The Vira app used in the TES, relative to the ME app, showed higher levels of objective (i.e., number of launches) and subjective markers of engagement (i.e., self-report on the TWEETS). While used less than Vira, participants did engage with the static ME app over the course of treatment. However, there was no evidence of a dose relationship, as app use was not significantly associated with changes in depression or anxiety for either app.

The broadly equivalent outcomes between treatments in the current trial may be related to multiple factors. First, some or all of the benefits may have occurred through the research components present in all trials (i.e., Hawthorne effects associated with assessment reach outs, onboarding call, contact for compensation) [44]. Second, most of the sample was already receiving mental healthcare (i.e., 54% in therapy, 52% taking medication, 38% doing both). While engagement in therapy and/or medication did not demonstrate a moderation effect with the primary outcomes, mental healthcare use may indicate that this sample included an especially engaged or privileged group, particularly compared with other community samples [3, 45]. Moreover, given that it is known to be challenging to show treatment effects over and above the effects of established treatments (e.g [46]), the number of participants already engaged in treatment may have created a “ceiling effect” that made it more difficult to observe differential outcomes between the treatment arms. This may be especially relevant for the lack of findings driven by coaching support—as the coaches provided encouragement to support app use, as opposed to more robust clinical service coaching models. Further, the lack of severe symptomatology (e.g., no suicidal intent or plan at any assessment point) may indicate that this sample had relatively well-managed symptoms, and as such, any intervention would have provided some benefit. Indeed, placebo effects (which could have equally applied to both interventions) have been found to be stronger among those with milder levels of depressive symptoms [47, 48]. Finally, Vira and ME both contain psychoeducation, which is an effective intervention in its own right. The control condition, ME, was a passive psychoeducation app that was designed to control for placebo and Hawthorne effects, as well as the impact of providing mental health information. However, systematic reviews and meta-analyses have shown that they can significantly reduce symptoms [49, 50]. This overlapping content across treatments that demonstrated efficacy in the current study might demonstrate the value of low-intensity treatments as a first step intervention for patients in routine care settings [51]. Indeed, it is possible that minimal psychoeducation is sufficient for many people with milder presentations to obtain improvements in depression and anxiety symptoms [49, 50], and that many of the interactive features in digital mental health applications are unnecessary for some groups. Future dismantling studies that include a no treatment (or treatment as usual) control arm would help to clarify some of these issues.

Exploratory moderation analyses indicated that the TES and psychoeducation control may have differentially benefited patients based on their experiences, such as experiencing discrimination. Indeed, a strength of the current study was the measurement of perceived discrimination, including the option to specify what identity patients believed drove their experiences (e.g., gender, race, weight) [42]. This differs from the more typical practice using demographic factors (e.g., race) as a proxy for the effects of experiencing systemic racism or other forms of systemic oppression. However, individuals can experience multiple forms of discrimination (e.g., racism, sexism). Therefore, relying on demographic factors to characterize experiences of discrimination lacks nuance and may lead to incorrect attributions or assumptions. Experiences of discrimination are dynamic and associated with both higher risk for mental health symptoms and capacities for building and using greater resilience [52]. While we are hesitant to overinterpret the moderation findings due to the pilot nature of the current study and the number of exploratory moderation analyses conducted, the emergence of experiences of discrimination as a driver of benefit for depressive or anxious symptoms is a relationship worth exploring in future work.

Engagement with key players with diverse experiences and perspectives were an integral aspect of the HCD methodologies that drove the iterative design of the technology, service protocol, and implementation planning for the TES [22]. Subsequently, there were a higher number of app launches as well as patient-reported metrics of engagement during the trial compared to the control condition. Yet, the activities across the Create phase of the ACTS Model were inherently impacted by, and responsive to, the COVID-19 pandemic. For example, the role of coach was filled by lay-providers, rather than providers affiliated with the clinics, to account for the burden placed on healthcare providers in the clinics early in the pandemic. During the trial recruitment period, social distancing mandates were no longer enforced in Chicago. However, it is unclear how the pandemic may have potentially impacted symptoms that were assessed in this trial. While pandemic-related mandates, such as social distancing, have relaxed, fundamental changes and burdens resulting from the pandemic continue to impact healthcare settings. Indeed, substantial behavioral healthcare needs, particularly for youth and those with minoritized identities and experiences [53, 54], are growing within an overburdened healthcare system that often has few timely or accessible referral options [55]. As such, changes driven by pandemic-related needs, such as the level of training and direct clinic involvement of the coach, may still be necessary or beneficial as the TES iterates to larger trials and sustainment [22].

The current study had multiple strengths, including following the ACTS Model, an academic-research and industry collaboration, recruitment of a diverse sample, and a relatively high retention rate. Yet, results should be considered alongside specific limitations. First, the trial was not fully integrated into a healthcare site, as originally planned. This limits our ability to comment on the implementation outcomes for the TES. Second and related to the lack of direct integration into the care system, the primary recruitment source was through MyChart messaging (i.e., the patient-facing portal of Epic, an electronic medical record). This was in contrast to the originally intended recruitment method of direct invitations from care providers in real-time as they detected mental health concerns during clinic appointments. This may have over-represented participants who have established comfort or familiarity with digital tools. Further, given the low use of MyChart by teen patients (i.e., teens under the age of 18 are more likely to have a caregiver proxy manage their MyChart account, even if one is established) [55], the use of MyChart contact as a primary recruitment method may have contributed to the low number of teens who enrolled. Given that only six teens enrolled in the current study, our ability to speak to differential outcomes based on age were limited. Third, despite the West Side communities of Chicago having a high Spanish-speaking population [56], the study excluded participants who could not read or speak English. To increase the equitable reach of TESs and other digital tools harnessing personal sensing technologies, it is imperative that future iterations directly involve non-English speakers to address issues of acceptability, as well as increasing linguistic and cultural inclusivity [57]. Finally, nearly all participants had insurance. It is unclear how the current findings extend to community samples and healthcare environments that more frequently serve patients without insurance or who use medical assistance programs. As TESs and digital, low-intensity treatments are increasingly being considered as a potential first line of care in healthcare and mental healthcare environments [51], it is important to better understand the benefits and limitations of such tools for populations with diverse needs and experiences, particularly in community-based settings.

Conclusion

The current study supports the benefit of both TESs that use personal sensing technology and digital psychoeducation for Primary Care teen and adult patients with symptoms of anxiety and/or depression. While the use of HCD methodologies and human support may have contributed to higher engagement with the TES than with a static psychoeducation app, this use was not related to greater symptom improvement. As such, it is important to determine for which patients higher intensity digital tools, such as TESs, are beneficial above and beyond more scalable digital low-intensity treatments.
