Improved density estimation via pair production correction and experimental validation in neutron gamma logging
Authors: Abolfazl Rafizade, Seyed Abolfazl Hosseini
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study introduces an improved model for neutron-gamma density (NGD) measurements, specifically designed to reduce the impact of pair production on density estimations. Using the MCNPX simulation software, the model simulates the interaction of the NGD tool with geological formations, focusing on the energy distribution of neutrons and gamma rays, and the effect of mudcake thickness on gamma flux ratios. The study investigates how varying hydrogen weight fractions, particularly those below 10%, influence gamma and neutron flux. The new model effectively mitigates errors caused by pair production, offering more accurate density estimations, especially for formations with low hydrogen content. Experimental benchmarking with a Cf-252 source was conducted to validate the simulation results, providing a comparison with real-world data. Introduction

Density logging is a critical technique in petroleum and geological exploration, delivering essential insights into subsurface formations, including bulk density, lithology, and porosity1,2. Among the commercially available methods, gamma-gamma density (GGD) and neutron-gamma density (NGD) are the two primary approaches, each offering unique advantages and applications in the accurate assessment of reservoir properties3,4. GGD logging has long been a cornerstone of nuclear logging tools. By measuring gamma-ray attenuation, GGD effectively determines formation density and lithology, making it indispensable for evaluating hydrocarbon reservoirs5.

The principles of traditional GGD logging are based on using a cesium-137 radioisotope as a gamma-ray source6. This source emits monoenergetic gamma rays (662 keV), which interact with the formation material, and the resulting attenuation is measured by detectors placed at fixed distances. The response of the tool is then used to infer formation properties, often using empirical models such as the “spine and ribs” approach described by Ellis (1987)7. This model simplifies the complex interactions of gamma rays with matter, treating attenuation as an exponential function dependent solely on bulk density. These models often fail to account for critical interactions like photoelectric absorption and gamma-ray scattering, treating key parameters such as electron density and bulk density as uniform across lithologies. This oversimplification neglects the variations introduced by formation fluids like oil or water and frequently underrepresents the photoelectric effect, a crucial factor in lithology differentiation. These shortcomings reduce the accuracy and applicability of GGD tools in complex or heterogeneous formations. To address these challenges, semi-empirical models have been developed to provide a more comprehensive treatment of gamma-ray interactions. Gardner proposed an advanced semi-empirical approach that incorporates scattering and absorption effects using more appropriate parameters to describe gamma-ray behavior, significantly improving on traditional methods8.

growing concerns about health, safety, and environmental (HS&E) risks associated with radioisotope sources have spurred the search for alternative methods3,9. The use of radioactive materials poses potential hazards during transportation, handling, and disposal, prompting the industry to explore safer, radioisotope-free technologies10. These challenges have driven the development of innovative alternatives such as neutron-gamma density (NGD) logging, which employs a pulsed neutron generator instead of a radioisotope source, paving the way for safer and more environmentally sustainable density logging practices11,12.

The NGD method measures density based on inelastic gamma rays produced by fast neutron interactions with formation nuclei. These gamma rays are characterized by higher energy levels compared to the monoenergetic 662 keV gamma rays used in GGD. As a result, NGD requires consideration of additional factors, such as neutron field distribution, inelastic scattering cross sections, and gamma attenuation. Based on the correction method for the hydrogen index (HI), NGD techniques are categorized into three main approaches4:

Fast-neutron correction: This method utilizes a fast-neutron detector along with gamma detectors to account for neutron field effects13.

Recent studies in radiation measurement and shielding emphasize two critical challenges that are directly relevant to nuclear well-logging technologies: (i) accurate quantification of gamma-ray interactions in complex media, and (ii) management of radiation exposure to both environment and personnel. Extensive gamma-ray spectrometric work on naturally occurring radionuclides (e.g., ^226Ra, ^232Th, ^40K) in geological materials such as black-sand deposits and industrial byproducts has shown that local mineral composition can strongly influence both gamma emission intensity and dose rate, and therefore determines the radiological hazard index of the material under investigation16–18. These surveys—performed using high-efficiency HPGe systems on coastal black sands in North Sinai, Egypt16, and on phosphate fertilizer production lines17—demonstrate two things that matter for neutron–gamma density (NGD) logging: first, gamma fields in real geological or industrial environments are rarely clean, but instead are shaped by mineralogy, density, and elemental content; and second, reliable interpretation requires physically grounded calibration rather than purely empirical fitting. Similar conclusions appear in black-sand separation plants, where monazite-, zircon-, and rutile-rich fractions were identified as radiologically significant because of their high activity concentrations and their elevated hazard indices for workers18. In other words, modern gamma-based assessment is no longer just about obtaining a number—it is about correcting that number for composition, geometry, attenuation, and safety relevance.

At the same time, advances in engineered shielding materials show how sensitive high-energy photons and neutrons are to both density and microstructure, and how controllable their transport can become when material composition is known. High-strength concretes and composite matrices reinforced with high-Z or functional additives (e.g., PbO, Cd-MOFs, nano-CuO, granodiorite, ceramic powders, or industrial waste fillers) have been systematically tuned to enhance linear attenuation coefficients, reduce half-value layers, and improve fast-neutron removal, while still maintaining or even improving mechanical performance at ambient and elevated temperatures19–23. This philosophy—coupling transport physics with calibrated material response—is also central to our approach. In the present NGD tool model, we use MCNPX-based neutron–gamma transport to predict detector flux ratios under controlled source geometry, then invert those ratios to recover the bulk density of the surrounding formation.

The complexity of the NGD method arises from its reliance on solving coupled neutron and gamma transport equations, as both neutron interactions and gamma production influence the measurements. Furthermore, the higher energy gamma rays used in NGD introduce additional interactions, such as pair production, which must be accounted for in density calculations24–26. These factors necessitate the use of correction algorithms to address the effects of borehole conditions, neutron scattering, and lithological variations, complicating the analysis compared to GGD27.

Therefore, to address the critical challenge of pair production in high-energy neutron–gamma density (NGD) measurements and to advance the development of safer, radioisotope-free logging tools, this study introduces a novel semi-empirical model designed to mitigate density estimation errors. The model incorporates a directional neutron source and explicitly accounts for pair production and Compton scattering effects to improve accuracy in complex geological formations. Using the MCNPX simulation software, the interaction of the NGD tool with various formation conditions was modeled, including the influence of water-based mudcake thickness and hydrogen weight fraction on gamma and neutron flux distributions. The proposed approach was further validated through experimental benchmarking, ensuring consistency between simulated and measured data. The successful implementation of this model establishes a robust framework for obtaining high-precision density logs with direct applications in enhanced petroleum reservoir characterization and mineral exploration, providing a reliable and radiation-safe alternative to conventional gamma–gamma density tools.

The comparison of mean squared error (MSE) and R2 values across various NGD methods highlights the improvements achieved through the introduction of directional neutron emission and pair-production correction. MSE serves as a measure of the average squared differences between predicted and true densities, with lower values indicating better model performance. In our study, the MSE for the improved model was significantly reduced to 0.02013, compared to 0.030 for Wang et al.28 and 0.035 for Dong et al. 29, demonstrating a more accurate prediction of formation density. Similarly, R2, the coefficient of determination, quantifies the proportion of variance explained by the model, with values closer to 1 indicating better fit. The R2 for the proposed model was 0.92, showing that it explains 92% of the variance in the true densities, which is an improvement over other methods, such as Dong et al.29 (R2 = 0.88) and H. Yu et al.24 (R2 = 0.87). These results emphasize the enhanced predictive accuracy and reliability of the model proposed in this study, particularly for formations with varying hydrogen weight fractions and complex geological structures.

However, discrepancies between the model’s predictions and the actual densities become more pronounced when the hydrogen weight fraction exceeds approximately 10%. This is likely due to the increasing significance of the photoelectric effect, which is more prominent in materials with high hydrogen content. Furthermore, the complex interactions between neutrons and formation materials, especially in formations with higher hydrogen content, contribute to the inaccuracies. The absorption and scattering cross-sections of hydrogen, which influence neutron behavior in the formation, are not fully captured by the model, resulting in additional errors.

Despite these limitations, the model demonstrates strong performance overall, providing accurate density estimations for materials with lower hydrogen content. The model is well-suited for materials with densities within the specified range and lower hydrogen fractions. However, for materials with higher hydrogen concentrations, particularly those with a weight fraction exceeding 10%, further refinement may be necessary to account for the photoelectric effect and the detailed neutron interactions. Therefore, while the model is highly effective in most cases, there is potential for improvement in scenarios involving complex material behaviors at high hydrogen concentrations.

Methods

Exploring the forward model approach

In this study, a collimator is used to direct the neutron flux at an angle θ relative to the vertical axis. To accurately determine the response of each detector to the inelastic gamma flux, the coupled neutron and gamma transport equation must be solved. In NGD measurements, governed by coupled-field theory, the interplay of two primary transport phenomena—fast neutron transport and gamma-ray transport—determines the tool’s response. These processes are closely interconnected, with the fast neutrons generating gamma rays through inelastic scattering. The gamma rays, in turn, propagate through the medium and are attenuated before reaching the detector. MCNPX’s advanced multi-energy particle transport capabilities enable precise modeling of neutron and gamma interactions within heterogeneous formations.

Benchmarking

In this section of the paper, we aim to benchmark the simulation results from previous stages against experimental measurements. To achieve this, we have simulated the structure of the probe used in the tests, and then compared the simulation results with the experimental data. It is important to note that while a D-T neutron source was used in the theoretical part of the study, due to the unavailability of such a source in our laboratory, a Cf-252 source was employed for benchmarking the simulation results. Consequently, all experimental tests were re-simulated using the Cf-252 source. The gamma rays emitted from the Cf-252 source were also included in the simulation.

In the simulation, only the neutrons and photons resulting from radiation absorption are tracked. For the Cf-252 source, a Watt distribution energy spectrum is employed. The tallies used in this project are F4 and F8. The F4 tally measures the neutron flux at the detector location, while the F8 tally counts the pulses generated by the detector. A Boron Lined NaI scintillator is used as the detector. In the simulation, the F8 tally is configured to count the number of 0.48 MeV gamma rays, specifically photons with energies between 0.47 MeV and 0.49 MeV.

The experimental data were normalized to facilitate a direct comparison between the experimental measurements and the simulation results. The normalization procedure was necessary to account for differences in counting efficiencies and to ensure that both the experimental and simulated results could be compared on the same scale. This was achieved by dividing the detector counts by the activity of the Cf-252 source and the counting duration, ensuring that the comparison was based on identical conditions for both the experimental and simulated data. This normalization step helps mitigate any variations caused by different experimental setups or varying detector sensitivities.

Regarding the potential interference between the gamma-ray peaks at 478 keV and 511 keV, it is important to note that these peaks are likely to overlap due to the resolution of the NaI detector. The Full Width at Half Maximum (FWHM) resolution of a NaI scintillator at both 478 keV and 511 keV energies is broad enough that these two peaks, which are close in energy, will not be fully resolved. As a result, the 478 keV peak from the Cf-252 source and the 511 keV peak, which is a characteristic gamma from positron annihilation, may interfere with each other, leading to potential errors in energy measurement and spectrum interpretation.

The geometric specifications of the key components are as follows: the NaI detector has an active length of 5 cm and an active diameter of 6.5 cm. The polyethylene shield is 11 cm long, and the lead shield measures 15 cm in length. The neutron source features a length of 4 cm and a diameter of 5.0 cm. The probe casing has an internal diameter of 6.5 cm and a thickness of 5.0 cm, while the well has a diameter of 13 cm. The probe casing is made of stainless steel, the well medium is air, and the surrounding soil consists of sandstone.

The experimental tests were conducted for porosities of 30 p.u., 35 p.u., 40 p.u., 45 p.u., 50 p.u., and 100 p.u., with the pores filled with water. These configurations were used to evaluate the system’s performance under varying conditions. It is important to acknowledge that the experimental setup used in this study, while carefully designed, does not fully replicate the exact conditions of a real NGD logging environment, particularly in terms of the neutron source. Our experimental tests utilized a Cf-252 source as a surrogate for the D–T neutron source typically used in well-logging applications. This substitution was made due to the unavailability of a D–T source, which presents a significant challenge in experimental validation, as the neutron spectra and energy distributions between Cf-252 and D–T are different.

While both sources emit neutrons capable of interacting with geological formations, Cf-252 primarily emits neutrons with a broad energy spectrum, including thermal and fast neutrons, whereas the D–T source produces neutrons with a more distinct and higher-energy peak (14.1 MeV), which is often preferred in traditional NGD logging for its uniformity and specific interaction characteristics with formation materials. The absence of a D–T source in our tests introduces certain discrepancies, including differences in neutron flux distribution and interactions, particularly at higher energy ranges.

Despite these limitations, the Cf-252 source has been widely accepted in the literature for similar benchmarking exercises, and the methodology used in this study—coupling Cf-252 with advanced MCNPX simulations—helps mitigate these discrepancies by providing a theoretical framework that accurately simulates the interactions of the neutron flux with geological materials. We have validated our simulations against experimental data to ensure their reliability, and the results support the conclusion that our proposed model remains robust even with these substitutional differences.

While the ideal scenario would involve testing with a D–T source to more closely replicate the real logging environment, we believe that the Cf-252 source provides a reasonable approximation for the purposes of this study. Future work would benefit from direct comparisons using both sources to refine and further validate the model, particularly in terms of its sensitivity to neutron energy variations.

The challenge of implementing neutron collimation, particularly for high-energy (14 MeV) neutrons, is of paramount importance in the practical application of neutron-gamma density (NGD) logging tools. Due to the significant penetration ability of fast neutrons, effectively collimating them necessitates the use of thick shielding layers made from dense materials, which inherently reduces the neutron flux. This reduction in flux directly impacts the measurement efficiency of the NGD tool, a critical factor in the accuracy of density estimation. The trade-off between collimation effectiveness and reduced flux has been widely studied in other fields, such as neutron imaging and therapy. For instance, Chen et al. (2022) explored multilayer collimator structures incorporating both hydrogenous and heavy-metal materials, achieving a collimation ratio of 29 for compact systems, which could be adapted for well-logging applications22. In similar vein, Li et al. (2020) designed a moderator-collimator system enabling mobile neutron radiography using compact neutron sources23. Although these studies focus on different applications, they provide useful insights into the design considerations for neutron collimators and the challenges they may present in logging tools.

Neutron collimation, while improving angular resolution and vertical sensitivity, inevitably introduces a significant challenge: the reduction in neutron flux. This issue is particularly relevant in well-logging applications, where maintaining high measurement efficiency is critical for obtaining reliable data. In our previous study24, we acknowledged this trade-off, emphasizing that although collimation improves measurement precision, it simultaneously leads to a reduction in the neutron flux, which increases statistical uncertainty. This aspect was discussed in detail, as we explored how increasing the emission angle enhances sensitivity but results in lower neutron flux, which could compromise efficiency.

To address the impact of reduced flux, we proposed the use of high-flux neutron sources, such as accelerator-based D–T generators, which can provide sufficient intensity even at higher emission angles. This approach effectively mitigates the reduction in detection efficiency caused by collimation, ensuring that the signal strength remains adequate for reliable measurements. Our study showed that while collimation reduces flux, this decrease can be balanced by the use of high-flux sources, thereby maintaining an optimal trade-off between sensitivity and flux.

Furthermore, our research highlighted the benefits of higher emission angles, particularly in challenging formation environments, such as thinly bedded, tight, and gas-bearing formations, where conventional tools struggle to detect small variations in formation properties. The increased sensitivity provided by collimation allows for better vertical resolution and the ability to detect subtle density and porosity changes. Although this comes with a reduction in flux, the improved vertical resolution and overall measurement precision make it a promising solution for well-logging applications, especially in formations requiring high sensitivity and fine resolution.

In gamma-ray spectroscopy, practical challenges such as detector efficiency and dead-time effects significantly impact measurement accuracy. Studies like35 highlight the importance of efficiency correction methods, such as Monte Carlo simulations and calibration factors, to account for variations in detector response under different conditions. These corrections are crucial in ensuring accurate results, particularly in complex measurement setups. Additionally, dead-time effects—which occur at high count rates—can distort the accuracy of measurements36. Introduces a dead-time correction method based on the attenuation law, which helps address these errors by adjusting for the loss of events during data collection. Other approaches, such as those discussed by Bergeson37, involve the use of faster CMOS-coupled detectors to minimize dead-time, further improving measurement reliability. In this study, similar efficiency and dead-time corrections would be essential to enhance the accuracy of neutron-gamma density (NGD) measurements, ensuring the robustness of the proposed model in real-world applications.

Our present study incorporates idealized simulations to isolate the theoretical benefits of directional neutron emission without explicitly modeling the physical collimators. This approach allowed us to assess the upper bounds of performance in terms of sensitivity and statistical error. However, it is critical to acknowledge that these theoretical scenarios do not account for the real-world complexities of implementing neutron collimators in well-logging environments. Therefore, future research must delve into several key aspects. By considering these practical constraints alongside the theoretical framework, it will be possible to refine existing models and enhance the precision of NGD logging tools, paving the way for next-generation, high-performance systems.

Conclusion

This study addresses the significant challenges posed by pair production in high-energy gamma rays, particularly in neutron-gamma density (NGD) measurements. At higher energies, such as those encountered with 14 MeV neutrons, the pair production effect becomes pronounced, leading to erroneous measurements and a reduced accuracy in density estimation. To mitigate this issue, we have developed a semi-empirical model for directional neutron emission, which offers a more accurate representation of the interactions between neutrons and gamma rays in geological formations. This model helps to reduce the influence of pair production by directing the neutron flux at an optimal angle, thereby enhancing the precision of gamma-ray measurements and providing a more reliable tool for evaluating formation properties.

In this paper, we present a semi-empirical model that successfully simulates directional neutron behavior, overcoming some of the limitations associated with isotropic neutron sources. The model incorporates the dynamics of neutron and gamma-ray transport within a geological medium, offering an improved framework for understanding the attenuation and scattering processes. By using MCNPX simulation software, we have shown that the directional neutron approach leads to better sensitivity and vertical resolution, particularly in challenging formation environments. Our findings suggest that this model can significantly improve measurement accuracy in the presence of complex material properties and varying hydrogen weight fractions.

To validate the model’s performance, comprehensive simulation studies were carried out, followed by experimental benchmarking using a Cf-252 source. The simulation results were benchmarked against real-world data, demonstrating the model’s effectiveness in accurately predicting gamma flux and density estimates. The experimental tests involved variations in geological conditions, including different hydrogen content and mudcake thicknesses, providing critical insights into the model’s applicability and robustness. However, we acknowledge the trade-off between improving measurement precision and the potential reduction in measurement efficiency due to neutron collimation. This reduction in neutron flux is a key concern, as it can lead to diminished count rates and increased statistical uncertainty in well-logging applications.

The implementation of a neutron collimator remains a challenging aspect of NGD tool design. Previous studies have explored neutron collimation techniques in various applications, such as neutron imaging and medical therapy, providing valuable insights into the potential for adapting these methods to well-logging environments. Collimators, though effective in improving angular resolution and vertical sensitivity, lead to a reduction in neutron flux, which impacts the measurement efficiency of the NGD tool. Future work should focus on optimizing the collimator’s divergence and beamline design to balance flux attenuation and angular enhancement. In addition, further research into the use of high-flux neutron sources, as well as incorporating advanced simulation models, could pave the way for developing more efficient collimator systems that minimize measurement loss while maximizing tool performance.
