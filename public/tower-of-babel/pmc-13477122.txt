Drivers of PVS and the pathway to targeted management and clinical decision-making.

Central Message

An integrative framework for PVS is presented that explains disease progression beyond anatomy, enabling earlier risk stratification, targeted intervention, and precision-guided clinical management.

Perspective

PVS is a high-mortality condition for which management is largely reactive and anatomy-driven, with limited ability to predict disease progression or recurrence. This review presents an integrative framework linking genetic susceptibility, hemodynamic forces, and cellular remodeling to enable earlier risk stratification, targeted intervention, and the development of precision-guided therapies.

Pulmonary vein stenosis (PVS) is a rare but aggressive pediatric vascular disorder characterized by progressive intimal thickening, myofibroblast proliferation, and venous obstruction.1 Infants typically present within the first year of life, and despite advances in imaging and interventional techniques, PVS continues to be associated a poor prognosis. Mortality can approach 50% within 2 to 3 years of life, reflecting both the rapid disease progression and the high rate of restenosis after interventions.1 Reintervention is common, and many patients require multiple catheterizations or operations during early childhood, underscoring the chronic and recurrent nature of the disease.

Management remains challenging. Current treatment strategies—including balloon angioplasty, stent placement, and surgical repair—often provide only temporary relief, because fibroproliferative occurrence and recurrent narrowing are frequent occurences.1 Disease biology varies across phenotypes such as primary PVS and postrepair total anomalous pulmonary venous connection–associated PVS, adding further heterogeneity to clinical outcomes.1 Although recent reports have improved understanding of clinical course and treatment strategies,2 the field still lacks reliable predictors of recurrence or progression.

These challenges underscore the need to couple current clinical knowledge with emerging translational frameworks capable of defining mechanisms, predicting recurrence risk, and guiding more individualized management. The underlying molecular mechanism of PVS remains unknown. Emerging evidence suggests that endothelial injury, smooth muscle proliferation, and extracellular matrix remodeling are drivers of recurrent phenotype.3 Hemodynamic stressors potentially have a role in mediating phenotype,4 although the exact nature of this stimulus remains unknown. Identifying drivers of the PVS phenotype is necessary to understand disease pathophysiology and is equally important for the development of targeted therapies.

There are no reliable early prognostic tools to predict PVS progression and severity. As a result, current treatment is mainly reactive to the disease state, and clinical prognostication is often late in the disease process to change the clinical course of many patients. A prognostic model will allow tailored risk stratification and care of patients with PVS. Development of hemodynamic and biological metrics are a prerequisite for the development of prognostic models.

This review proposes a forward-looking translational framework, integrating hemodynamic metrics, biomarker discovery, patient-specific disease-in-a-dish modeling, and development of early prognostic tools for risk stratification. This framework lays the foundation for precision medicine, enabling novel therapies and risk-based management to improve clinical decision making.

Diagnostics Tools for PVS

Diagnosis and longitudinal assessment of PVS relies on a complementary multimodal imaging strategy (Figure 1). Echocardiography remains the first-line modality and provides rapid, bedside assessment of pulmonary venous flow patterns, Doppler acceleration, and interval changes—indispensable for initial screening and follow-up.1 Computed tomography (CT) angiography offers excellent spatial resolution and reliably defines stenosis location, length, distal extension, and involvement of multiple veins, enabling precise anatomical characterization essential for procedural planning.1 Magnetic resonance imaging, although less commonly used in infants, provides radiation-free flow quantification and can be used to assess pulmonary venous return and right-sided volume loading in cooperative older children.1 Cardiac catheterization with angiography remains the reference standard for physiological assessment, providing definitive transstenotic gradients, pulmonary venous and arterial pressures, detailed venous morphology, and catheter-based intervention during the same procedure.2 Quantitative lung perfusion scans provide a noninvasive, segmental assessment of pulmonary blood flow distribution, enabling the detection and longitudinal monitoring of perfusion asymmetry and functional impairment in patients with PVS.5 Together, these modalities provide a comprehensive overview of anatomy and hemodynamic significance, guide selection of catheter-based versus surgical intervention, and support close surveillance for restenosis.2 However, even with these options, current imaging primarily characterizes the anatomical and physiological effects of PVS, offering limited insight into early biological activity or predictors of recurrence. These constraints motivate the development of translational tools that complement imaging by capturing underlying mechanistic drivers of PVS. Catheter-Based Interventions

Catheter-based intervention is central to the management of PVS (Figure 1) and remains the most frequently used strategy for both initial relief of obstruction and treatment of recurrent lesions.1 Balloon angioplasty, often the first interventional approach, can provide immediate luminal expansion and symptomatic improvement.1 High-pressure and cutting balloons have improved the ability to treat discrete, focal lesions, especially in larger-caliber veins.1 Stent implantation offers a more long-term alternative in appropriately sized vessels, helping maintain patency and reduce early elastic recoil.6 Drug-eluting stents (DES) are associated with improved 2-year survival compared with bare metal stents, with reported rates of 74.7% versus 53.1%.6

Advances in stent design, including high-radial-force and low-profile platforms, have expanded their applicability in small children and challenging anatomies.6 DES technologies with antiproliferative compounds are commonly used and may further enhance vessel stability by attenuating fibroproliferative response.6 Stents implanted in children will require serial dilation over time to account for somatic growth, and the use of DES in smaller patients necessitate intentional stent fracture procedures in the future.7 Catheterization laboratories have refined procedural strategies for PVS, including staged interventions, treatment of multifocal or distal disease through selective venous angiography, and careful assessment of vein caliber, lesion geometry, and associated pulmonary arterial pressures. These approaches support individualized planning and facilitate repeated interventions during early childhood, reinforcing catheter-based therapy as a central component of longitudinal management. Despite these advances, restenosis remains common, particularly in small-caliber veins, long-segment disease, and lesions with aggressive fibroproliferative behavior.6 Current interventions primarily address the mechanical obstruction caused by intimal thickening but offer limited insight into the biological drivers of recurrence. Emerging opportunities to integrate hemodynamic metrics—derived from routine catheterization data such as detailed flow patterns or energy-based indices—may help identify lesions at greater risk for early restenosis and refine procedural timing and strategy. In parallel, catheter-based platforms provide an attractive vehicle for targeted therapies, where future iterations of stent or balloon technologies could deliver agents developed by mechanistic understanding rather than nonspecific antiproliferative coatings. These approaches, explored in detail in later sections, may ultimately pair anatomical relief with biologically directed modulation of disease activity.

Surgical Approaches to Managing PVS

Surgery remains a central component of multimodal management for PVS (Figure 1), particularly in infants and children with multivessel obstruction, long-segment obstruction, or lesions not amenable to catheter-based intervention. Surgical practice has evolved significantly, with sutureless repair and modified sutureless techniques now representing a significant percentage of operative cases.8 These approaches reduce suture-line tension and minimize the risk of restenosis and have become especially important in postrepair total anomalous pulmonary venous connection–associated PVS.8 Results for primary pulmonary vein stenosis have been less favorable, with 5- and 10-year survival rates of 47% and 31%, respectively.9 More recently, centers have adopted anatomically focused repair strategies that address pulmonary vein angulation and the fulcrum effect of adjacent mediastinal structures.10 These techniques aim to straighten the pulmonary venous course, eliminate geometric points of shear amplification, and create a more laminar flow pathway into the left atrium.10

Recent large single-center surgical series demonstrate improving midterm outcomes, reflecting refined operative techniques and coordinated multidisciplinary care.11 In a cohort of 174 patients, 1- and 5-year survival rates after surgical repair were approximately 71% and 61%, respectively, with the anatomically focused repair associated with a significant reduction in mortality.11 Earlier studies similarly reported that bilateral disease, multivessel involvement, and greater severity scores were strongly associated with the risk of mortality and reintervention.12 These findings underscore both the progress made and the ongoing difficulty in managing aggressive phenotypes of PVS.

Despite these significant advances, surgical repair primarily addresses anatomical obstruction, and restenosis remains common, often necessitating postoperative catheter-based reintervention in a large number of patients.11 Surgical techniques do not modify the underlying fibroproliferative and hemodynamic mechanisms that drive disease progression. Future refinements in operative strategy may benefit from integrating mechanistic insight into preoperative planning and postoperative surveillance. Hemodynamic modeling—whether derived from computational fluid dynamics (CFD), catheterization data, or advanced imaging—can identify veins exposed to elevated wall shear stress or geometric angulation, helping surgeons determine which segments require more extensive sutureless or anatomically focused repair.4,11 Such information may also guide technical decisions, including the extent of vein mobilization, lateralization, or trajectory straightening, and inform selection among sutureless, modified sutureless, or resection-based techniques.11 Beyond anatomy, emerging biological markers of endothelial injury or fibroproliferative activity may help determine which veins are predisposed to early recurrence, enabling surgeons to tailor follow-up strategy and integrate adjunct medical therapy within a multimodal care plan.8

Adjunct Medical Therapies

Adjunct medical therapy has become an important component of multimodal care for PVS (Figure 1), complementing surgical and catheter-based interventions. Several centers now use systemic antiproliferative therapies to suppress the aggressive myofibroblast-driven intimal proliferation characteristic of PVS. Early clinical experience, particularly with sirolimus-based regimens, suggests improvement in transplant-free survival when used in conjunction with anatomic interventions, especially in patients with multivessel or recurrent disease.13, 14, 15 In published single-center experiences, survival at the time of sirolimus initiation was 83% at 5 years, supporting its use as a therapeutic strategy in patients with PVS.16 These therapies aim to stabilize disease between procedures, slow the rate of restenosis, and potentially extend the longevity of both catheter-based and surgical repair. DES have also been used in pediatric PVS to reduce recurrent neointimal proliferation.6 A wide range of antiproliferative agents has been tested, including paclitaxel, everolimus, zotarolimus, vinblastine, imatinib mesylate, and sirolimus, many of which originate from oncology because of their ability to inhibit generalized cellular proliferation. These agents were selected on the basis of the hypothesis that suppressing nonspecific proliferative activity would mitigate restenosis. However, comparative studies now show no significant difference in restenosis rates between DES and bare-metal stents.6 Recurrent in-stent stenosis, adjacent-segment stenosis, and new-lesion formation remain common. This occurs because DES platforms currently deliver drugs that do not target the molecular pathways specific to PVS, which involve endothelial injury, aberrant mechanotransduction, myofibroblast activation, and extracellular matrix dysregulation17—processes distinct from cancer biology for which these drugs were originally designed. As a result, DES function primarily as mechanical scaffolds, providing temporary luminal stability but limited biological benefit. These limitations highlight the need for next-generation device-based and systemic therapies that leverage PVS-specific molecular insight, enabling delivery of agents targeted to the true drivers of recurrent stenosis. Once PVS-specific therapeutic targets are discovered, DES become an immediately deployable delivery platform, because the workflow, catheter techniques, and safety profile are already mature.

Current Biological Insights Into PVS

PVS is recognized as a primary proliferative vasculopathy driven by a distinctive population of myofibroblast-like intimal cells.18,19 Foundational human pathology demonstrated that stenotic veins are filled with spindle-shaped myofibroblasts within a loose mucopolysaccharide matrix, lacking inflammation, thrombosis, or fibrosis—establishing a noninflammatory, nonthrombotic proliferative process.18 Immunohistochemistry further confirmed strong smooth-muscle marker expression and absence of endothelial or hematopoietic markers, firmly defining the lesion as myofibroblast-rich intimal hyperplasia.19 Mechanistically, human specimens show diffuse expression of receptor tyrosine kinases, including platelet-derived growth factor-α/β, fibroblast growth factor receptor, and vascular endothelial growth factor receptor-2, with activated platelet-derived growth factor-β and focal vascular endothelial growth factor ligand expression, supporting an autocrine/paracrine growth-factor signaling loop that sustains myofibroblast activation and matrix production even during phases of low proliferation.19 These features parallel restenosis biology, positioning PVS within the broader family of growth-factor–driven, matrix-expanding vascular disorders.

Large-animal modeling has provided insight into how these lesions progress.20 A surgical cut-and-suture pig model reproduces human PVS with synthetic-phenotype smooth muscle cells, proteoglycan-rich matrix, and mammalian target of rapamycin (mTOR) pathway activation in both anastomotic and upstream pulmonary veins.20 Local rapamycin delivery delays but does not prevent stenosis, underscoring the role of mTOR-dependent proliferative signaling.20 Small-animal neonatal rat models reinforce these findings.21 Unilateral pulmonary vein banding produces upstream intimal hyperplasia, myofibroblast accumulation, smooth-muscle–layer thickening, elastic and collagen fiber deposition, and lumen loss, along with secondary pulmonary congestion, small-vessel muscularization, alveolar simplification, pulmonary hypertension, and right-ventricular hypertrophy—a close approximation of human disease biology.

The most mechanistically detailed model, bilateral vein banding, minimizes collateral formation and enables molecular profiling.22 RNA-seq reveals >1100 differentially expressed genes enriched for cell-cycle, extracellular-matrix, and myofibroblast-proliferation pathways, with signatures of EndMT-related endothelial populations and macrophage-associated inflammatory signaling.22 Sirolimus therapy in this model markedly reduces proliferating smooth muscle actin positive cells, intimal thickness, extracellular matrix deposition, lung congestion, and right ventricular hypertrophy, supporting mTOR-dependent myofibroblast proliferation as a major driver of disease in mechanically induced and postrepair forms of PVS.22 It is important to note that most animal models replicate mechanically induced injury and therefore most closely resemble postrepair PVS, whereas whether identical pathways dominate primary PVS remains an active area of investigation.

Together, these human and animal studies establish a coherent framework: PVS originates from a myofibroblast-rich, receptor tyrosine kinase- and mTOR-driven proliferative vasculopathy that propagates upstream, remodels the lung vasculature, and ultimately impairs right-heart structure and function.

Unmet Needs Motivating a New Framework

Despite advances in defining the cellular and molecular drivers of PVS, current approaches still lack a unifying model explaining why the disease initiates, why it propagates upstream, and why it recurs despite technically successful interventions. Existing therapies relieve focal obstruction but do not address the underlying proliferative biology or the growth factor– and mTOR-dependent signaling loops that sustain progression. Understanding PVS requires an integrated framework linking biology, hemodynamics, and cardiopulmonary remodeling. Such a framework is essential for developing targeted therapies, improving risk stratification, and predicting disease trajectory. Building this framework will require diverse, complementary tools—including hemodynamic metrics for surgical decision-making, circulating biomarkers for mechanistic insight, disease-in-a-dish models to dissect signaling pathways, and whole-genome sequencing to identify variants that alter endothelial susceptibility to wall shear stress—each contributing a piece of the larger conceptual model needed to change clinical practice.

Genetic priming may result in heightened sensitivity of endothelial cells to mechanical or molecular stimuli, creating a vascular environment that is more susceptible to maladaptive remodeling. Hemodynamic stress, including increased wall shear stress, disturbed flow patterns, and nonphysiological geometric angulation, places sustained mechanical load on the venous endothelium. These forces trigger endothelial activation, reflected broadly by altered flow sensing, disrupted barrier function, and initiation of pro-remodeling signaling pathways. Endothelial cells in this state influence the underlying mesenchyme through paracrine cues and early shifts toward mesenchymal-like phenotypes.

This endothelial response promotes smooth muscle remodeling, including proliferation, migration into the neointima, and transition toward a synthetic phenotype characterized by extracellular matrix production. As matrix accumulation increases, fibrotic remodeling ensues, with progressive stiffening, loss of compliance, and concentric luminal narrowing. These changes further distort flow, establishing a feed-forward cycle that accelerates restenosis.

Although each node in this cascade is supported to varying degrees across human, animal, and hypothesis-generating studies, this integrated model provides a coherent pathophysiological framework. Near-term translational opportunities include correlating hemodynamic metrics with endothelial activation markers and using patient-specific in vitro systems to validate key steps in the cascade.

Translational Platforms for PVS

Multiple translational platforms now enable complementary insights into the genetic, hemodynamic, and cellular drivers of PVS (Figure 2). Together, these tools provide a path toward early risk detection, mechanism-guided therapies, and individualized procedural planning.

Whole-genome sequencing (WGS) offers an unbiased approach for identifying rare variants and structural alterations associated with PVS susceptibility. Although the genetic architecture of PVS remains incompletely defined, early studies linking chromosomal loci and syndromic associations illustrate the feasibility of detecting germline contributors to disease risk. In current practice, WGS can be incorporated through noninvasive saliva-based testing, allowing identification of inherited or de novo variants that may influence recurrence risk or phenotype. Near-term applications include integrating WGS findings with clinical data to identify high-risk subgroups and refining inclusion criteria for biomarker and therapeutic studies.

CFD modeling enables patient-specific quantification of wall shear stress, oscillatory shear, pressure gradients, and energy loss—hemodynamic features that may correlate with disease severity and restenosis risk.4 CFD can be generated from standard catheterization and CT datasets, making it feasible across most clinical centers.4 CFD can support decision-making by identifying veins exposed to elevated wall shear stress, helping determine timing and modality of intervention, and highlighting segments at risk for progression. As datasets expand, CFD-derived thresholds may guide surveillance intervals and procedural strategy.

Induced pluripotent stem cell (iPSC)-based platforms, including 2-dimensional endothelial models and emerging 3-dimensional multicellular systems, provide mechanistic resolution at the cellular level.23 Patient-specific iPSC lines preserve the full genomic landscape,23 enabling controlled studies of endothelial dysfunction, smooth muscle activation, and fibrosis under PVS-relevant mechanical cues. These platforms are feasible for hypothesis testing, validation of candidate pathways, and preclinical screening of targeted agents.

Animal models, including neonatal rodent21 and porcine models,24 provide critical in vivo confirmation of mechanisms and therapeutic effects. These models replicate key features of human disease, allowing validation of molecular targets and evaluation of disease-modifying therapies before clinical translation. They also serve as essential bridges linking CFD-derived forces and in vitro findings with whole-organ physiology.

Together, these platforms form an integrated translational ecosystem that connects molecular mechanisms to actionable clinical decisions, supporting the development of risk-based, biologically informed management strategies for PVS.

Clinical Decision Integration and Use Cases

Integrating hemodynamic metrics with emerging circulating biomarkers offers a practical strategy for progressing PVS management beyond an anatomy-centered framework and toward mechanism-guided decision-making. CFD-derived parameters—including wall shear stress, oscillatory shear index, pressure gradients, and energy loss—can now be generated from standard CT and catheterization data, providing quantitative insight into the mechanical forces affecting each pulmonary vein. When these signals are combined with biomarkers reflecting endothelial injury, proliferative activity, or fibrosis, clinicians gain a more complete assessment of disease behavior that may not be apparent from anatomical imaging alone. This dual-signal approach may enable earlier identification of aggressive lesions, informs the choice between catheter-based and surgical intervention, and helps tailor surveillance strategies to each patient's biological and hemodynamic risk profile.

In practice, high hemodynamic burden paired with rising biomarkers may justify earlier surgical evaluation, particularly when recurrent stenosis is likely despite technically adequate catheterization. Conversely, moderate hemodynamic abnormalities accompanied by stable biomarker profiles may support a staged catheter-based approach, allowing clinicians to defer operative intervention when the biological activity appears controlled. These integrated signals can also refine follow-up intervals, prompting earlier re-evaluation in patients showing discordant or evolving features across flow metrics and biomarker trends.

Two brief examples illustrate how these data tracks can influence management. In one scenario, an infant with multivessel PVS demonstrates markedly elevated oscillatory shear and high upstream energy loss on CFD modeling despite only moderate anatomic narrowing, while endothelial-injury biomarkers rise over serial assessments. Together, these findings indicate aggressive disease biology and prompt earlier surgical referral, avoiding multiple catheter procedures with limited expected durability. In contrast, a toddler with recurrent unilateral stenosis shows moderately increased wall shear stress but stable biomarkers over several months, suggesting a relatively quiescent biological state. Here, a staged catheter-based strategy would be better, supported by interval CFD reassessment to confirm improvement in flow patterns and guide subsequent surveillance.

Mechanistically Informed Therapies

A mechanistic understanding of PVS creates new opportunities to design therapies that directly target the biological drivers of restenosis rather than relying on nonspecific antiproliferative agents. Because the disease appears to arise from a sequential cascade—mechanosensing abnormalities, endothelial activation, smooth muscle phenotypic switching, and ultimately fibrosis—each node offers a distinct therapeutic entry point with potential to improve procedural longevity and reduce recurrence after intervention. At the level of mechanosensing, strategies that modulate maladaptive responses to abnormal wall shear stress or disturbed flow are increasingly relevant. Agents that stabilize endothelial flow-sensing pathways or blunt the early mechanotransduction response could reduce downstream activation and limit the initiation of the proliferative cascade. By dampening these upstream triggers, such therapies may decrease the likelihood of restenosis following otherwise successful catheter-based or surgical repair. Targeting EndoMT and endothelial activation represents a second therapeutic opportunity. Inhibitors directed toward pathways governing endothelial plasticity and inflammation could slow the transition to mesenchymal-like states that promote fibroproliferation. Incorporating such modulators peri-interventionally—either systemically or through device-based delivery—may enhance long-term patency. At the level of smooth muscle cell remodeling, therapies that prevent phenotype switching from contractile to synthetic states have the potential to directly interrupt intimal expansion. Modulation of proliferative, migratory, or matrix-producing signaling programs could reduce neointimal thickening and improve the stability of both stents and surgical repair. In parallel, antifibrotic approaches aimed at limiting extracellular matrix accumulation may attenuate late-stage stiffening and prevent the recurrence of flow-limiting narrowing. Together, these mechanistically driven strategies could shift the therapeutic paradigm toward biology-driven intervention.

Biomarker-Driven Risk Stratification

Biomarkers reflecting endothelial injury, inflammation, and fibrosis offer a promising avenue for earlier and more precise risk assessment in PVS.25 Because current imaging and hemodynamic evaluation primarily capture anatomical obstruction rather than underlying biological activity, circulating markers have the potential to reveal disease acceleration before overt restenosis develops. Because circulating biomarkers reflect global biological activity rather than vein-specific injury, their clinical utility emerges when interpreted in conjunction with anatomical imaging and hemodynamic assessments, allowing focal lesions to be contextualized within systemic disease activity. Candidate endothelial-injury markers include molecules released during shear-mediated dysfunction or barrier disruption, which may correspond to the early endothelial activation described in PVS biology.25 Inflammatory markers, including cytokines or macrophage-associated signatures identified in animal and tissue studies, may reflect the paracrine signaling that drives smooth muscle remodeling.25 Fibrosis-related markers—such as those associated with collagen deposition or extracellular matrix turnover—parallel the late-stage stiffening and matrix accumulation seen in human and experimental models.25 A practical clinical framework would incorporate biomarker sampling at key intervals: at diagnosis, immediately postintervention, and then at regular surveillance points such as 4 to 6 weeks and 3 months, aligning with routine catheterization or imaging schedules. Trends, rather than absolute values, are most informative. Rising endothelial-injury or fibrosis markers after catheter-based repair may indicate early biological recurrence even when imaging shows only mild anatomical change, prompting clinicians to consider earlier re-intervention or escalate from catheter-based to surgical management. Conversely, stable biomarkers in the setting of moderate hemodynamic abnormalities may support continued catheter-based strategies and standard surveillance intervals. By integrating biomarker trajectories with anatomical and CFD-derived hemodynamic data, clinicians can transition toward a biologically anchored risk model, enabling more timely intervention and offering a path toward genuinely individualized PVS management.

Multicenter Research and Data Ecosystem

Advancing the clinical and biological understanding of PVS will require a coordinated multicenter research infrastructure capable of capturing the full spectrum of disease heterogeneity. Prospective, harmonized registries can aggregate clinical characteristics, imaging data, catheterization metrics, and outcomes across institutions, enabling adequately powered analyses that are not feasible within single-center cohorts. Tissue pipelines that support standardized collection of blood sample and resected venous tissue will facilitate molecular profiling and accelerate discovery of therapeutic targets. In parallel, harmonized analytics—integrating CFD-derived hemodynamic metrics, biomarker assays, and shared computational workflows—will ensure consistent data interpretation across sites. Together, these efforts form a scalable ecosystem that can drive longitudinal studies, validate mechanistic signals, and support the development of targeted therapies for PVS.

Conclusions

PVS remains a uniquely aggressive pediatric vasculopathy in which anatomical interventions alone cannot fully address the biological forces driving recurrence. By integrating genetic susceptibility, patient-specific hemodynamics, cellular remodeling, and emerging biomarkers, a more coherent and predictive framework for disease behavior will evolve. The translational platforms described—from WGS and CFD modeling to iPSC-based systems and animal models—provide the experimental foundation needed to validate mechanistic pathways and identify actionable therapeutic targets. When combined with decision-support tools that incorporate hemodynamic signals and biomarker trends, these approaches enable a shift from reactive, anatomy-driven management toward proactive, risk-stratified care. Ultimately, a coordinated ecosystem of multicenter registries, harmonized analytics, and shared tissue resources will be essential for scaling discoveries and accelerating clinical translation. Together, these efforts position the field to develop more targeted interventions, reduce restenosis, and deliver personalized therapy for patients affected by this challenging disease.
