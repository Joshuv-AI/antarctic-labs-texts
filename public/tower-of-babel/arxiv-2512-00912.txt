ForamDeepSlice: A High-Accuracy Deep Learning Framework for Foraminifera Species Classification from 2D Micro-CT Slices
Abdelghafour Halimi, Ali Alibrahim, Didier Barradas-Bautista, Ronell Sicat, Abdulkader M. Afifi

Abstract
This study presents a comprehensive deep learning pipeline for the automated classification of 12 foraminifera species using 2D micro-CT slices derived from 3D scans. We curated a scientifically rigorous dataset comprising 97 micro-CT scanned specimens across 27 species, selecting 12 species with sufficient representation for robust machine learning. To ensure methodological integrity and prevent data leakage, we employed specimen-level data splitting, resulting in 109,617 high-quality 2D slices (44,103 for training, 14,046 for validation, and 51,468 for testing). We evaluated seven state-of-the-art 2D convolutional neural network (CNN) architectures using transfer learning. Our final ensemble model, combining ConvNeXt-Large and EfficientNetV2-Small, achieved a test accuracy of 95.64% , with a top-3 accuracy of 99.6% and an area under the ROC curve (AUC) of 0.998 across all species. To facilitate practical deployment, we developed an interactive advanced dashboard that supports real-time slice classification and 3D slice matching using advanced similarity metrics, including SSIM, NCC, and the Dice coefficient. This work establishes new benchmarks for AI-assisted micropaleontological identification and provides a fully reproducible framework for foraminifera classification research, bridging the gap between deep learning and applied geosciences.

foraminifera classification, deep learning, transfer learning, 2D slices, 3D micro-CT data

Introduction

Paleontology is undergoing a transformative data revolution, catalyzed by the widespread adoption of high-resolution, non-destructive imaging technologies such as micro-Computed Tomography (micro-CT)
(
17

5

(

As imaging resolutions increase, the volumetric datasets grow exponentially, making segmentation—especially for low-contrast specimens like calcareous fossils in carbonate-rich matrices—the most labor-intensive and error-prone step in the workflow
(
5

To address this challenge, the field has increasingly turned to deep learning, a subset of machine learning that excels in automated image analysis. Early applications demonstrated that convolutional neural networks (CNNs), particularly U-Net architectures, could segment fossils with accuracies rivaling manual annotation, but in a fraction of the time
(

;

)
. These breakthroughs have laid the foundation for a broader integration of artificial intelligence (AI) into paleontological research, extending beyond segmentation to include fossil classification and reconstruction, as summarized in Table

One of the most promising directions in this domain is the classification of microfossils using transfer learning. Several studies have shown that pretrained CNN architectures—such as VGG16, ResNet50, MobileNetV2, InceptionV3, and Xception—can be fine-tuned to classify microfossils from low-resolution optical images with remarkable accuracy. For instance, classification of the genus
Globotruncanita
achieved up to
96.97%
accuracy and an AUC of
0.978

(
23
)
, while broader genus-level classification reached
99.78%
accuracy
(
24
)
. These approaches are particularly valuable for laboratories with limited computational resources, offering high performance with minimal infrastructure.

Beyond transfer learning, researchers have explored custom architectures built from scratch, combining CNNs with recurrent neural networks (LSTM, BiLSTM) to classify species within the genus
Globotruncana
, achieving up to
97.35%
accuracy and an AUC of
0.968

(
25
)
. Classical machine learning models such as SVM, LDA, and kNN have also demonstrated strong performance when paired with tailored CNNs, validating the utility of lightweight models in resource-constrained settings
(
8
)
.

In parallel, semantic segmentation and 3D reconstruction of microfossils have advanced through hybrid architectures combining U-Net and ResNet, enabling precise segmentation in CT and micro-CT images with IoU scores exceeding
94%
and segmentation accuracies up to
98%

(
12
;
1
)
. These methods preserve spatial fidelity and support detailed reconstructions essential for paleoenvironmental and geological analyses.

Finally, synthetic data generation has emerged as a powerful tool to overcome the scarcity of labeled paleontological datasets. The ForamViT-GAN framework, which integrates Vision Transformers (SwinIR) with GANs (StyleGAN2), produces high-fidelity synthetic images of foraminifera
(
7
)
. Few-shot segmentation using only five examples further demonstrates the model’s efficiency. Complementary work using fractal pretraining (FDSL) with Vision Transformers has enabled multiclass classification of
32 radiolarian species
with
86.3%

(

)
.

Together, these innovations mark a paradigm shift in paleontological research, where deep learning not only accelerates traditional workflows but also opens new avenues for fossil discovery, classification, and interpretation.

Foraminifera (forams) are unicellular aquatic organisms that produce calcareous tests (shells), which fossilize abundantly in carbonate rocks, making them essential for reconstructing Earth’s evolutionary history and past climate
(
6
)
. The introduction of 30 µm-thick thin sections by Henry Clifton Sorby in 1852 enabled the first systematic study of foraminifera from carbonate rocks—a technique still central to biostratigraphy today
(
2
;
29
)
. While this work focuses on foram micro-CT data, the proposed workflow is broadly applicable to other microfossils, providing a framework to evaluate and optimize deep learning pipelines for automated classification.

2.1
Dataset Creation and Curation

We constructed a scientifically rigorous dataset from 97 micro-CT scanned fossil specimens representing 27 foraminifera species. Twelve species with at least four 3D models each were selected to ensure robust training (we use the term 3D model to refer to individual 3D micro-CT image stacks). We provide a general overview of this AI-based workflow for the reader in Figure
1
.The selected species are summarized in Figure
2
and Table
2
. To prevent data leakage
(
30
)
, we implemented a strict specimen-level splitting protocol, assigning all slices from a single 3D model exclusively to one of the training, validation, or test sets. This approach avoids the train-test contamination common in slice-level splits, which can overestimate accuracy by up to 55%.

Slice extraction employed Otsu’s
(
22
)
thresholding to remove low-content slices, followed by advanced segmentation to isolate fossil silhouettes. The final dataset comprises 109,617 high-quality 224×224 RGB images: 44,103 for training, 14,046 for validation, and 51,468 for testing ( Table
2
). Dataset balance was optimized using brute-force coefficient of variation minimization (Table
3
).

2.2
Model Architecture and Training Strategy

We evaluated seven state-of-the-art 2D CNN architectures: ConvNeXt
(
19
)
(Base and Large), EfficientNetV2
(
28
)
(Small and Large), NASNet
(
35
)
, MobileNet
(
14
)
, and ResNet101V2
(
9
)
. Transfer learning was applied using ImageNet-pretrained weights. Training followed a two-phase strategy:

•

Phase 1: Backbone Freezing
— 10 epochs with frozen pretrained weights; only classifier head trained. Learning rate: 1e-4 with cosine scheduling.

•

Phase 2: Fine-Tuning
— 20 epochs with full network unfrozen; learning rate: 5e-5 with warm restarts. Mixed precision (fp16) training was used for efficiency. Early stopping was applied with patience=5 based on validation loss.

All models used standardized configurations: input resolution (224×224 or 384×384), batch size 32, AdamW optimizer
(
20
)
, and categorical crossentropy loss with label smoothing (0.1)
(
27
)
. Training was conducted on NVIDIA GPUs with fixed seeds and deterministic operations for reproducibility.

2.3
Data Augmentation and Preprocessing

To enhance generalization, we applied intelligent bounding-box cropping and a suite of augmentations:

•

Geometric
: rotations (±45°), scaling (0.8–1.2×), horizontal flips.

•

Color
: brightness/contrast adjustments, jittering.

•

Regularization
: CutMix
(
31
)
and Mixup
(
32
)
strategies.

2.4
Ensemble Modeling

Our final model is a weighted PatchEnsemble combining ConvNeXt-Large and EfficientNetV2-Small. PatchEnsemble refers to an ensemble strategy that combines predictions from multiple models using confidence-based switching logic
(
34
)
. This adaptive approach leverages complementary strengths across architectures and improves robustness. The ensemble achieves 95.64% test accuracy and 99.6% top-3 accuracy.

Results

3.1
State-of-the-art Models Performance Overview

We systematically evaluated seven state-of-the-art 2D convolutional neural network (CNN) architectures on a balanced dataset comprising 109,617 micro-CT slices. To benchmark model performance, we computed all standard classification metrics, including accuracy, F1-score, and area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) (Figure
3
).

As a baseline, we selected ResNet101V2, against which all other models were compared. Classification accuracy across models ranged from 86% to 95%, with ConvNeXt-Large achieving the highest accuracy of 95%. The F1-score, used as a proxy for classification quality, revealed that ConvNeXt-Large and NASNet exhibited the most favorable balance between precision and recall, indicating robust performance across species.

All models demonstrated excellent calibration, with AUC values consistently exceeding
0.98
, underscoring their reliability in distinguishing between classes.

3.2
PatchEnsemble Model Performance

To further enhance classification performance, particularly in challenging cases, we implemented a
PatchEnsemble
strategy. Usually, the PatchEnsemble uses different models for different patches of one image, and their outputs are aggregated to form a final prediction. Unlike traditional ensemble methods, which typically rely on majority voting or averaging across models trained on the full image. PatchEnsemble introduces diversity by leveraging specific context, thereby improving robustness and sensitivity for the classification.

In our study, we used the F1-score as a guiding metric to identify the most difficult cases and selectively apply the PatchEnsemble strategy to support the best-performing fine-tuned model. This targeted approach led to a measurable improvement in classification performance. In contrast, a conventional ensemble method based on majority voting did not yield any significant gains, highlighting the advantage of spatially-aware ensemble techniques in this domain.

We compare the performance metrics of the traditional ensemble combination of the Top-2( ConvNeXt-Large + NASNet), Top-3 (Top-2 + EfficientNetV2-Large), and Top-4 (Top-3 + EfficientNetV2-Small) models according to the results of our systematic analysis of the state-of-the-art models and the PatchEnsemble model of ConvNeXt-Large and EfficientNetV2-Small.

The final PatchEnsemble model, combining ConvNeXt-Large and EfficientNetV2-Small, was named
ForamDeepSlice
, achieved
95.64%
test accuracy and
99.6%
top-3 accuracy (Table
8
)

3.2.1
Species-specific classification challenges

Species-level analysis revealed notable disparities in classification performance, particularly for
Baculogypsina
and
Orbitoides
. While most species achieved F1-scores above 90%, these two taxa consistently underperformed across ensemble configurations.
Baculogypsina
exhibited exceptionally high precision (up to 0.999), yet suffered from markedly low recall (as low as 0.227), indicating that the model was highly conservative in predicting this class and frequently missed true instances. Conversely,
Orbitoides
maintained strong recall values (above 0.90), but its precision was comparatively low (e.g., 0.666 in Top-3), suggesting a tendency to misclassify other species as
Orbitoides
, leading to false positives.

These imbalances resulted in reduced F1-scores, highlighting the limitations of conventional ensemble methods in handling species with ambiguous or overlapping morphological features (Figure
5
; Figure
6
).
The PatchEnsemble strategy mitigated some of these issues by leveraging localized image regions, leading to improved recall for
Baculogypsina
and better precision for
Orbitoides
. This targeted enhancement underscores the importance of spatially-aware ensemble techniques in fine-grained biological classification tasks.

3.3
User Interface Development for Domain Scientists

To facilitate the practical deployment of fossil classification tools, we developed an interactive dashboard tailored to the needs of paleontologists and domain scientists. The interface does not require coding background and is designed to be intuitive and accessible, lowering the barrier to entry for non-technical users while providing advanced functionality for expert analysis. The supplementary video provides a complete view of the interactive dashboard as well as an example workflow for foraminifera species classification and 3D slice matching. The supplementary video can be found here:
https://youtu.be/betkJ3gsmNQ
.

The application is structured into three main layers (shown in Figure
7
):

1. Landing Page

Upon launching the tool, users are greeted with a landing page that introduces the system’s capabilities and a quick start guide. The page describes the datasets and the overall classification performance. It also provides the entry points for classification and slice-matching pages.

Figure 7:
Our interactive dashboard provides a user-friendly access to the classifier and 3D matching algorithm.
A complete view of the dashboard in action is provided in our supplementary video (
https://youtu.be/betkJ3gsmNQ
).

2. Classification Page

This page features a drag-and-drop interface for uploading micro-CT images and provides basic preprocessing options, including contrast enhancement, image cropping, and image sharpening, to enhance input quality before analysis. Once an image is uploaded, an automatic cropping, denoising, and segmentation is performed to isolate the foram. A preview of the result is shown on the page. The user can adjust this result by manually cropping and adjusting the segmentation sensitivity control. Once the user is satisfied with the input image, the classifier can be executed - this uses our ForamDeepSlice model to perform species-level classification. The result interface displays ranked predictions with associated confidence scores, probability distributions for each class, and additional metadata. The top-5 predictions are presented to support expert validation and further exploration, allowing users to interpret results in the context of their domain knowledge.

3. 3D Slice Matching Page

Once the correct species is identified, users may choose to perform 3D slice matching to find the approximate slice orientation of the input image slice within a 3D model. This module treats the input image as a slice from a volumetric micro-CT scan and compares it against a curated database of 3D fossil models. The matching process employs a two-stage pipeline:

•

Coarse Matching:
Based on Dice coefficient and Hu moments.

•

Fine Matching:
Incorporates rotation-invariant analysis using Structural Similarity Index (SSIM)
(
33
)
, Normalized Cross-Correlation (NCC)
(
18
)
, and Oriented FAST and Rotated BRIEF (ORB) feature matching
(
26
)
.

Users can optionally restrict the search to a subset of models based on prior knowledge or hypotheses, enabling targeted exploration. The best matching slice can also be visualized in the context of the 3D model using a simplified 3D visualization.

4
Discussion

The benefits of computed tomography (CT) scanning in paleontology are undeniable, offering unprecedented access to the internal and external morphology of fossil specimens. In particular, micro-CT imaging enables the detailed study of complex structures in foraminifera, allowing experts to classify specimens with greater accuracy and based on a richer set of morphological features than traditional 2D imaging techniques.

In this study, we addressed the challenge of classifying 12 unique species of foraminifera using micro-CT data. Given the similarity of this task to medical image classification, we adopted a transfer learning approach, leveraging pretrained convolutional neural networks (CNNs) to benchmark performance on this domain-specific dataset. All tested models outperformed the baseline (ResNet101V2), with ConvNeXt-Large emerging as the top-performing architecture in terms of overall accuracy and F1-score.

However, species-level analysis revealed that the models struggled with two particularly challenging taxa:
Orbitoides
and
Baculogypsina
. We attribute this diminished performance to class imbalance and limited representation of these species in the training data. To address this, we implemented a targeted strategy using a PatchEnsemble approach, which combines spatially localized predictions from multiple models to enhance classification robustness. Specifically, we patched the best-performing model (ConvNeXt-Large) with models that showed superior performance on the difficult species.

In contrast, a traditional ensemble approach—combining the Top-2 to Top-4 models via majority voting—did not yield improved results. We hypothesize that this is due to the propagation of false positives and false negatives across models, which diluted the ensemble’s ability to correct errors. The PatchEnsemble strategy, by contrast, enabled us to selectively integrate complementary strengths, resulting in improved overall performance and notably better classification of the most challenging species.

While this AI-based workflow represents an important step toward automated micropaleontological classification, it does not replace careful taxonomic analysis. Geological context remains indispensable, particularly in cases of homeomorphy—where unrelated foraminifera from different ages evolve similar morphologies. For example, confusion can arise between homeomorphic taxa such as
Lepidorbitoides
spp. from the Cretaceous and
Lepidocyclina
spp. from the Cenozoic. Although homeomorphic taxa were not included in this study, it is critical to acknowledge that the algorithm is trained only on the listed species and may misclassify specimens that resemble others outside the training set. Thus, the system should be viewed as a decision-support tool, guiding experts toward the most likely matches rather than providing definitive identifications.
