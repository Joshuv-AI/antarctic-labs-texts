Controllable Information Production
Tristan Shah, Stas Tiomkin

Abstract
Intrinsic Motivation (IM) is a paradigm for generating intelligent behavior without external utilities. The existing information-theoretic methods for IM are predominantly based on information transmission, which explicitly depends on the designer’s choice of which random variables engage in transmission. In this work, we introduce a novel IM principle, Controllable Information Production (CIP), that avoids both external utilities and designer-specified variables. We derive the CIP objective from Optimal Control, showing a connection between extrinsic and intrinsic behaviors. CIP appears as the gap between open-loop and closed-loop Kolmogorov–Sinai entropies, which simultaneously rewards the pursuit and regulation of chaos. We establish key theoretical properties of CIP and demonstrate its effectiveness on standard IM benchmarks.



1
Introduction

Intrinsic Motivation (IM) is a biologically inspired paradigm for generating intelligent behavior without externally specified goals. A range of plausible information-theoretic objectives such as Empowerment
(
Klyubin et al. 2005
)
, Curiosity
(
Schmidhuber 2010
)
, Information Gain
(
Bhavya et al. 2025
)
, Diversity (DIAYN)
(
Eysenbach et al. 2019
)
, and Predictive Information have been proposed for IM and successfully applied to control problems including stabilization
(
Tiomkin et al. 2024
)
, object manipulation, locomotion
(
Karl et al. 2022
)
, and transportation. Despite these successes, several fundamental gaps remain.

First, existing IM methods are predominantly based on mutual information, which implicitly assumes information transmission and requires designers to explicitly specify which random variables (RV) participate in information exchange
(
Shannon 1948
)
. This introduces external knowledge, in the form of design choices, into otherwise goal-free formulations. Second, although empirically effective for solving Optimal Control (OC) tasks, the connection between existing IM objectives and OC theory remains unclear, limiting principled interpretation and leading to ad hoc heuristics that produce non-robust solutions
(
Tiomkin et al. 2024
)
. Third, beyond plausibility arguments, rigorous derivations of IM objectives from intrinsic properties of the underlying dynamical system are lacking.

We address these gaps by formally deriving an IM objective from OC, grounded in the native information production properties of dynamical systems, which are independent of design choices. The resulting objective emerges as a problem of information production rather than transmission, and is characterized by the gap between open-loop and closed-loop Kolmogorov–Sinai entropies (KSE),
h_{\mbox{ks}}
,
(
Kolmogorov 1958
)
:

Controllable Information Production

\mathrm{CIP}=h_{\mbox{ks}}(f^{\mathbf{ol}})-h_{\mbox{ks}}(f^{\mathbf{cl}}),

(1)

where
f^{\mathbf{ol}}
and
f^{\mathbf{cl}}
are dynamics parametrized by open-loop and closed-loop actions, respectively.

Intuitively, Controllable Information Production (CIP) rewards an agent for seeking out and suppressing controllable sources of chaos. The objective compares the rate at which the environment produces entropy when actions are injected blindly (open-loop) versus when they are selected adaptively in response to state feedback (closed-loop). A large gap indicates regimes where the environment rapidly produces entropy yet remains sensitive to control: open-loop behavior generates high entropy, while closed-loop actions suppress it. Hence, the agent is motivated to position itself near the
edge of chaos
, where small interventions have maximal impact on system evolution
(
Tower 2024
;
Zhang et al. 2025
;
Bertschinger et al. 2004
)
. Crucially, purely chaotic or uncontrollable dynamics are not rewarded: when entropy cannot be influenced by the agent’s actions, open-loop and closed-loop entropy production coincide, yielding low CIP. As such, the objective values chaos that can be consumed through control.

To understand how CIP differs fundamentally from existing IM methods, consider two questions:

Q1.

How is information transmitted through dynamics?

Q2.

How is information produced by dynamics?

Existing IM methods based on Shannon mutual information respond to Q1. They assume information already exists somewhere (e.g., in actions, messages, or skills) and that dynamics propagate it to future observations or states. This requires choosing which variables are deemed important sources of information and which are relevant targets for transmission. As such, the resulting IM agent is endowed with a designer-specified information interface, in which the choice of source and target variables implicitly constrains how the agent perceives, acts, and exchanges information with the environment.

CIP responds to Q2, which involves no designer choice but rather captures an intrinsic property of dynamics with only two possible modes of interaction between agent and environment: open-loop or closed-loop. Q2 does not privilege any variables as sources or targets of information. Instead, it has a clear interpretation grounded solely in dynamical systems properties and, as we show, emerges naturally from OC theory.

Our main contributions are as follows:

1.

We formulate a new approach to IM as a problem of information production rather than transmission, which emerges from OC theory. This closes the knowledge gap in understanding the connection between IM and OC, and opens doors for novel optimization schemes grounded in OC.

2.

We establish key theoretical properties of CIP including its positiveness and its connection to the Riccati Equation: the fundamental equation in OC. The latter provides a new method for estimating KSE through Riccati Recursions.

In this paper we focus on a new conceptual paradigm in IM and its theoretical properties, deferring the development of efficient optimization methods for large scale experiment to future work. We demonstrate the effectiveness of CIP on important benchmark problems in IM, including chaotic dynamics, while experiments with arbitrary large scale environments are beyond scope of the current work, and as such deferred to follow up work. https://anonymous.4open.science/r/controllable-information-production-E0C8/readme.md
.

2
Existing Intrinsic Motivation Methods

A wide variety of IM objectives have been proposed to generate intelligent behavior without external goals. These methods can be categorized based on which random variables (RVs) participate in information exchange. For example, Empowerment
(
Klyubin et al. 2005
;
Salge et al. 2014
)
computes the channel capacity from an open-loop control sequence to the final state, effectively measuring an agent’s controllability. Curiosity-based IM methods such as Active Inference
(
Parr et al. 2022
;
Friston 2010
)
and Surprise Maximization
(
Schmidhuber 2010
;
Burda et al. 2019
)
measure the information gained by the agent’s model parameters upon observing states. Diversity-based methods such as DIAYN
(
Eysenbach et al. 2019
)
measure information between latent skill representations and states.

By selecting which variables transmit and receive information, an IM objective can be obtained that encodes an implicit bias about what the agent “should” consider to be important or privileged. In
(
Tiomkin et al. 2024
)
, it is shown that an entire family of IM objectives can be derived from a single
Proto Objective
through different designer choices regarding which input RVs are “important” (equivalently, the information source) and which relevance RVs allow reconstruction of the input RVs after transmission through dynamics. This variety of IM objectives emphasizes the designer choice and subsequent bias in the resulting methods.

IM methods have been shown to be effective in optimal control problems which otherwise require engineered reward in order to solve. For example, the challenging task of swinging up and balancing the chaotic double pendulum was shown to be solved with Empowerment
(
Tiomkin et al. 2024
)
. Other work shows that an Active Inference approach leads to unsupervised object manipulation
(
Ferraro et al. 2025
)
. Diversity-based methods such as DIAYN have been used to learn a repertoire of reusable skills that can be composed or fine-tuned to solve downstream control tasks, effectively replacing hand-designed reward shaping with unsupervised skill discovery
(
Eysenbach et al. 2019
)
. While such results demonstrate the practical use of IM for control, the objectives themselves are typically introduced independently of OC principles. As a result, the relationship between existing IM, OC, and dynamical systems theory remains unclear, which hinders the interpretation and limits applicability of IM methods in many domains.

3
Overview of Kolmogorov-Sinai Entropy

Entropy production plays a fundamental role in the analysis of dynamical systems and control. Kolmogorov-Sinai entropy (KSE) quantifies in
\mathrm{b}\mathrm{i}\mathrm{t}\mathrm{/}\mathrm{s}\mathrm{e}\mathrm{c}
how rapidly a dynamical system generates new, distinguishable trajectories over time
(
Kolmogorov 1958
;
Sinai 1959
;
Walters 1982
;
Petersen 1983
)
. Given a finite partition of the state space, observing the system over multiple steps refines this partition according to the dynamics; the average growth rate of the resulting uncertainty defines an entropy rate. KS entropy is the maximum such rate over all finite partitions, yielding a representation-independent measure of dynamical complexity. In this work, we compute KSE for both open-loop dynamics (fixed action sequences) and closed-loop dynamics (state-feedback control). The difference between these two quantities captures how much dynamical complexity can be actively suppressed through feedback, separating intrinsic environmental richness from controllable structure.

Other work has examined the gap between open-loop and closed-loop entropy from a Shannon-theoretic perspective
(
Touchette & Lloyd 2000
)
, demonstrating that this gap obeys certain physical constraints such as conservation of information. However, these studies do not connect KSE to IM, nor do they propose IM objectives grounded in OC.

In this work, we introduce the CIP principle, which to the best of our knowledge is the first IM derived directly from OC rather than postulated. We show that CIP emerges naturally from the structure of OC in nonlinear dynamical systems. Unlike prior IM objectives that impose mutual information criteria on the dynamics, CIP emerges in terms of KSE, a quantity native to dynamical systems. As a result, CIP does not require specifying sender or receiver variables for information transmission and instead captures the intrinsic gap between open-loop and closed-loop entropy production as a fundamental consequence of control.

5
Experiments

We conduct a series of experiments to evaluate the effectiveness of CIP-based control across dynamical systems of increasing complexity. Our primary goal is to understand how a CIP-based agent behaves in environments with varying degrees of chaos, and whether a controller that maximizes CIP can reliably steer the system toward states that are both highly informative (high open-loop entropy) and controllable (low closed-loop entropy). In particular, we investigate whether CIP naturally induces exploratory behavior in low-chaos regimes and stabilization in highly sensitive regions of the state space.

We evaluate our approach on three classical benchmark systems that span a range of dynamical complexity:

•

Single Pendulum:
A low-dimensional system with one degree of freedom, used to demonstrate basic energy accumulation, swing-up, and stabilization.

•

Cart Pole:
A system with indirect actuation, where control is applied through horizontal cart motion rather than direct torque on the pendulum, introducing intermediate complexity and delayed effects.

•

Double Pendulum:
A two-degree-of-freedom system with strongly chaotic dynamics, serving as a challenging testbed for evaluating whether CIP can guide both exploration and fine-grained stabilization in highly sensitive regimes.

To isolate the role of CIP as an IM, we deliberately adopt a simple controller architecture. Specifically, we use a random-sampling-based model predictive control (MPC) scheme optimized solely with respect to the CIP objective. We demonstrate the effectiveness of this approach on classical IM benchmark environments, including the single and double pendulum. All experiments are conducted using the MuJoCo-MJX physics simulator
(
Todorov et al. 2012
)
, which provides efficient and differentiable simulation for continuous control tasks.

6
Discussion

This work introduces Controllable Information Production (CIP), a principled IM for continuous control. Unlike most existing IM formulations, which are postulated from heuristics or biological inspiration, CIP emerges directly from OC theory. It quantifies the
controllable chaos
produced in an environment. We observe that CIP gives rise to behaviors that closely align with prior intuitions about how intrinsically motivated agents should act when deployed in a dynamical control system. Using a deliberately simple MPC-based controller, we show that maximizing CIP consistently drives agents toward states at the
edge of chaos
across dynamical systems of increasing complexity.

6.1
Interpretation of CIP

CIP naturally decomposes into two complementary incentives. The first component, open-loop entropy production, captures the tendency of the uncontrolled system to increase uncertainty. Maximizing this term drives the agent toward regions of the state space with chaotic dynamics, analogous to exploratory behavior. Importantly, this quantity is blind to feedback and thus reflects the environment’s intrinsic capacity for information generation.

The second component, closed-loop entropy production, measures the residual chaos that remains once feedback control is applied. Minimizing this term encourages the agent to actively suppress uncertainty through its actions, favoring regions of the dynamics where chaos is not merely present but
controllable
, analogous to exploitation. Defined as the difference between these two quantities, CIP therefore measures precisely how much of the system’s information production is amenable to control.

Taken together, CIP formalizes the notion of
controllable chaos
. It rewards agents for finding pockets of controllability within the chaos, where the dynamics are rich and highly highly sensitive to control, unifying exploration and exploitation within a single objective.

6.2
Emergence of CIP from Optimal Control

To the best of our knowledge, CIP is the first IM derived directly from the structure of optimal control. It arises from a decomposition of the Hessian of the optimal value function into open- and closed-loop entropy production rates, which we show are equivalent to the KSE of the corresponding dynamics. In light of this principled derivation, the resulting behavior closely aligns with long-standing intuitions in intrinsic motivation: an agent should seek states in which it has maximal influence over future outcomes.

Importantly, although CIP is derived using the machinery of optimal control, it remains entirely goal-agnostic. Within OC, the optimal feedback policy naturally decomposes into extrinsic and intrinsic components, where CIP emerges solely from the intrinsic part, specifically from the second-order structure of the value function. This reveals a direct and previously unexplored connection between value curvature and closed-loop information production. In particular, dynamics which are well-controlled correspond to low closed-loop KSE and therefore to a Hessian which converges, whereas uncontrolled dynamics lead to larger closed-loop KSE, indicating that DARE does not converge.

Our theoretical results further indicate that intrinsic motivation should be framed as a
rate
of information production (e.g., nats per second), rather than as an absolute information measure. This perspective aligns more naturally with dynamical systems theory and resolves ambiguities associated with variable-length trajectories.

6.3
Information Transmission and Production

A key conceptual distinction between CIP and most existing IM methods lies in the difference between information
transmission
and information
production
. Prior approaches typically ask questions such as, “How much do my actions tell me about my future states?” These formulations require the designer to specify specific random variables such as actions, states, or latent representations between which information is transmitted. Such choices inevitably introduce design bias.

CIP, in contrast, asks a more fundamental question: “How much chaos would the system generate in the absence of feedback, and how much of that chaos can my control suppress?” By focusing on information production rather than transmission, CIP avoids privileging any specific variables or representations. No random variables must be selected, encoded, or predicted; the IM signal arises solely from the act of controlling the system.

From the perspective of OC, this distinction is natural: the objective is not to transmit information between variables, but to regulate the growth of uncertainty along the system’s trajectories. CIP therefore provides a bias-free, representation-independent IM grounded directly in the physics of the dynamical system.

6.4
Behavior Generated By a CIP-Based Controller

CIP is particularly well suited to trajectory-based planning methods such as MPC, as it is defined along system trajectories and can be efficiently computed via Riccati-like recursions. Even when implemented with a simple random-shooting MPC controller, maximizing CIP produces deliberate, interpretable, and non-trivial behavior across all tested environments.

Empirically, the agent seeks out highly chaotic regions of the state space and then actively stabilizes itself within them. This behavior is particularly impressive in the chaotic double pendulum, where the agent consistently reaches and maintains configurations that are both highly sensitive and controllable. The resulting policy demonstrate an awareness of where chaos arises in the environment and how it can be regulated through feedback, despite the absence of any task-specific reward.

These observations suggest that CIP captures a fundamental aspect of intelligent behavior: the drive to place oneself in situations that are maximally informative yet amenable to control.

6.5
Limitations and Future Work

One limitation of the current implementation lies in the numerical calculation of open-loop entropy. Our approach involves backward recursions which can develop extremely large values in the absence of stabilizing feedback. This issue is most pronounced for open-loop entropy and can lead to numerical instability in systems with very high information production rates. Future work will focus on developing more stable computational schemes for estimating open-loop entropy, potentially through regularization or alternative formulations of the recursion.

A second limitation is the simplicity of the controller used to instantiate CIP. While random-shooting MPC with iCEM suffices to demonstrate the principle, we found that it does not scale well to high-dimensional or long-horizon tasks. An important direction for future work is to integrate CIP into learning-based controllers, such as policy gradient or actor–critic methods.

Finally, an exciting avenue for future research is the integration of CIP with extrinsic objectives. Since CIP emerges naturally as the intrinsic component of optimal control, it provides a principled foundation for studying how intrinsic and extrinsic motivations interact. We believe this combination has great potential to improve performance in a wide range of continuous control problems.

Impact Statement

This paper introduces a novel theoretical framework for IM in continuous environments. The development of theoretically grounded methods for IM has the potential to improve robot learning by reducing reliance on human-designed reward functions, which are often difficult to engineer.

Agents driven by IM may exhibit unpredictable behavior due to the lack of explicit human guidance. In real-world robotic systems, such behavior could pose risks to human safety if deployed without appropriate oversight or safeguards.

As a primarily theoretical contribution evaluated in simulated environments, the immediate societal impact of this work is limited. Any real-world effects would depend on downstream applications, integration with safety mechanisms, and deployment decisions made by practitioners.

Proof of the Value Hessian Decomposition Lemma

We prove the equivalence between the asymptotic growth of the auxiliary recursions in Equation (
6

Proof.

From Equation (
7

Y_{0}=\sum_{t=0}^{T}\left(\frac{\partial x_{t}}{\partial x_{0}}\right)^{\top}c_{xx_{t}}\frac{\partial x_{t}}{\partial x_{0}},\qquad\frac{\partial x_{t}}{\partial x_{0}}=\prod_{k=0}^{t-1}f_{x_{k}}.

As

, the state-dependent Hessians converge to their mean value,

, which can be factored out and vanishes in the limit for the logarithmic determinant:

\lim_{T\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{T}\ln\det Y_{0}=\underbrace{\lim_{T\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{T}\ln\det\bar{c}_{xx}}_{0}+\lim_{T\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{T}\ln\det\sum_{t=0}^{T}\bigg(\frac{\partial x_{t}}{\partial x_{0}}\bigg)^{\top}\frac{\partial x_{t}}{\partial x_{0}}

Using the spectral property of the determinant, we write

\ln\det Y_{0}=\sum_{i=1}^{d_{x}}\ln\mu_{i}(Y_{0}),

where
\mu_{i}(Y_{0})
are the eigenvalues. By the min-max theorem,

\mu_{i}(Y_{0})=\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}S\subset\mathbb{R}^{d_{x}}\\
\dim(S)=i\end{subarray}}\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}v\in S\\
\|v\|=1\end{subarray}}v^{\top}Y_{0}v.

By Oseledets’ multiplicative ergodic theorem
(
Oseledets 1968
)
, the state space decomposes into invariant subspaces

associated with Lyapunov exponents
\lambda_{i}^{\textbf{ol}}
:

\mathbb{R}^{d_{x}}=E_{1}^{\textbf{ol}}\oplus\cdots\oplus E_{d_{x}}^{\textbf{ol}},\quad\left\|\frac{\partial x_{t}}{\partial x_{0}}v_{i}\right\|\sim e^{t\lambda_{i}^{\textbf{ol}}},\quad v_{i}\in E_{i}^{\textbf{ol}}.

Due to the Multiplicative Ergodic Theorem
(
Oseledets 1968
)
the
i
th eigenvalue of
Y_{0}
can be written as a summation of exponentials of LE.

\lim_{T\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{T}\ln\mu_{i}(Y_{0})=\lim_{T\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{T}\ln\sum_{t=0}^{T}\bigg|\bigg|\frac{\partial x_{t}}{\partial x_{0}}v_{i}\bigg|\bigg|^{2}=\lim_{T\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{T}\ln\sum_{t=0}^{T}e^{2t\lambda_{i}^{\textbf{ol}}}

If
\lambda_{i}<0
then
e^{2\lambda_{i}^{\textbf{ol}}}<1
and the infinite summation converges because it is the geometric series with rate

\lambda_{i}^{\textbf{ol}}=0
then the summation vanishes due to the logarithm followed by asymptotic averaging.

\lim_{T\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{T}\ln\mu_{i}(Y_{0})=\begin{cases}0,\quad&\lambda_{i}^{\textbf{ol}}<0\\

2\lambda_{i}^{\textbf{ol}},\quad&\lambda_{i}^{\textbf{ol}}>0\end{cases}

Therefore, only the positive open-loop LE survive and the quantity is equal to twice the KSE by Pesin’s Theorem
