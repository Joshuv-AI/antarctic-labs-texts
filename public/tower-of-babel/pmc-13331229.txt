Unexpected intercostal fusion of total thoracic fracture dislocation with ankylosing spondylitis
Authors: Ryo Nogami, Satoru Tochigi, So Ohashi, Kostadin Karagiozov, Yuichi Murayama, Yuzuru Hasegawa, Hiroki Ohashi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background: Fractures associated with ankylosing spondylitis (AS) are often challenging due to long-segment rigidity and severe displacement. Here, a 50-year-old male presented with a total T10 fracture/dislocation. After posterior fusion instrumentation was removed due to wound dehiscence, spontaneous intercostal fusion between the T10 and T11 ribs provided sufficient stability.Case Description: A 50-year-old male with AS after sustaining a severe T10 fracture/dislocation underwent an emergency posterior fusion. At surgery, the dural sac was ruptured and the spinal cord was completely transected. The thecal sac was tied off, and the patient underwent an instrumented T10–T11 fusion despite the inability to attain a significant reduction and the lack of adequate bone mass for the arthrodesis. Within 6 months, wound dehiscence due to a pressure sore resulted in implant exposure necessitating its removal. During that second surgery, there was prominent osseous bridging between the adjacent 10th and 11th ribs which appeared to adequately stabilize the spine.Conclusion: Thoracic spine fractures in patients with AS are not always amenable to sufficient spinal correction/instrumented fusion. Here, a 50-year-old male with AS following a T10 total fracture/dislocation, whose fusion construct failed, was sufficiently stabilized secondary to spontaneous T10–T11 intercostal fusion.

INTRODUCTION

Patients with ankylosing spondylitis (AS) severe fracture/dislocations are often difficult to realign and treat/fuse.[5,6] Here, a 50-year-old male with AS who presented with a T10 complete fracture/dislocation initially underwent an instrumented fusion that failed (i.e., due to wound dehiscence/breakdown) but due to spontaneous fusion of the T10–T11 ribs achieved adequate stability.

CASE DESCRIPTION

A 50-year-old male sustained a fall. His neurological examination demonstrated paraplegia with a bilateral T10 sensory level (American Spinal Injury Association (ASIA) grade A). The computed tomography demonstrated ankylosis of the thoracolumbar spine with complete fracture–dislocation at the T10–T11 level [Figure 1a and b]. The sacroiliac joints were also fused, and the patient was diagnosed with AS. Surgical stabilization was indicated for this obese patient with respiratory compromise and a significant pleural effusion; due to ventilatory difficulty, surgery was performed in the lateral position. Postural reduction and/or open reduction was not feasible, but the patient still underwent a long-segment posterior pedicle screw T7 to L2 fusion. At surgery, the spinal cord and dura mater were completely transected. To prevent future cerebrospinal fluid leakage, the dural defect was sealed using autologous fat graft and fibrin glue (i.e., primary dural closure was not feasible). The fusion mass consisted of a limited volume of local autograft bone harvested during the laminectomy. Because of the patient’s obesity, extensive elevation of the periosteum from the adjacent ribs and rib heads was required. Postoperatively, the patient remained neurologically unchanged with complete paraplegia and was transferred to a rehabilitation hospital.

Postoperative course

Six months later, wound breakdown over a screw required removal of the instrumentation. At that time, although no solid bony fusion was observed at the fracture level, new bone formation bridged the 10th and 11th ribs that appeared to provide stability [Figure 2a and b]. Postoperatively, the patient remained mechanically stable and was able to mobilize in a wheelchair without difficulty.

(a) Thoracolumbar computed tomography obtained 6 months after surgery demonstrates absence of intervertebral bony fusion at T10–T11, suggesting persistent segmental instability. (b) Three-dimensional reconstructed imaging reveals intercostal fusion between the posterior aspects of the 10th and 11th ribs (white arrow).

DISCUSSION

AS can cause long-segment spinal ankylosis that reduces mobility and predisposes patients to highly unstable fractures, even three-column injuries with total fracture/dislocation, even after minor trauma.[6] In this case, total disruption of the spinal cord and dura mater required that the dural sac be repaired, followed by an instrumented fusion to address the total subluxation. Nevertheless, when wound dehiscence required that the instrumentation be removed, the patient, due to extensive operative dissection including the adjacent ribs, resulted in spontaneous T10– T11 rib fusion and subsequent stability. Heterotopic ossification, in the present case, resulted in spontaneous T10–T11 intercostal fusion resulting in a successful arthrodesis.

This outcome likely reflects the convergence of multiple osteogenic factors—periosteal stripping, spinal cord injury, AS-related chronic inflammation, and surgical fixation—each of which can independently trigger heterotopic ossification or bony fusion [Table 1].[1-4]

CONCLUSION

Here, in a 50-year-old male with AS, spontaneous T10/T11 intercostal fusion provided adequate stability when the instrumentation had to be removed due to wound breakdown.
