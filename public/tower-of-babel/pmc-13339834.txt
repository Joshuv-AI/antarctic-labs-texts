Automated Microvolume Secretome Proteomics Enables Sensitive and Deep Profiling and Noninvasive Single-Embryo Analysis
Authors: Jingsheng Xie, Jiwei Wang, Yuhe Ge, Zilu Ye, Hong Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Cells continuously deliver proteins into the extracellular space, forming the secretome, which provides a dynamic, biologically informative, and noninvasive readout of cellular functional states. However, conventional secretome proteomics typically requires large sample volumes greater than 2 mL, delivers limited proteome depth, and supports low processing throughput of less than 30 samples a day. Here, we developed an automated microvolume secretome profiling workflow that integrates optimized sample pretreatment and magnetic bead-based proteome sample preparation. This workflow enables efficient secretome profiling from less than 20 μL of conditioned medium, achieving deep proteome coverage of over 3000 proteins with a sample processing throughput exceeding 96 samples per day. Using this workflow, we achieved high-depth, time-resolved secretome profiling from microscale culture medium, capturing temporal changes of secretome. We further applied the method to single mouse embryo culture medium and consistently identified more than 200 secretome proteins per embryo. Notably, Sdc4 and Ooep were consistently observed across developmental stages, highlighting the potential for noninvasive secretome profiling at the single-embryo level. Together, this work establishes a robust and scalable framework for high-depth secretome profiling from ultralow-input samples, broadening the scope of LC–MS-based analysis to microscale and longitudinal biological applications.

Introduction

Cells continuously release a diverse array of proteins into the extracellular space; these released proteins occasionally have low abundance, play essential roles in cell–cell communication, extracellular homeostasis, and signaling regulation, reflect cellular transcriptional activity, stress responses, metabolic states, and differentiation trajectories. − Collectively, these proteins define the secretome, which encompasses proteins released via classical secretion pathways, extracellular vesicles, and membrane-associated shedding. − Therefore, temporal profiling of the secretome provides a dynamic, biologically informative, and noninvasive readout of cellular functional states and responses to experimental perturbations.

Liquid chromatography coupled with tandem mass spectrometry (LC–MS/MS)-based proteomics has become the method of choice for systematic secretome profiling. From an analytical workflow perspective, secretome analysis consists of two major stages: extraction and enrichment of extracellular proteins from conditioned media and downstream proteomic sample preparation, followed by mass spectrometric analysis. Efficient and reproducible sample preparation is therefore a critical determinant of overall analytical performance.

The first stage focuses on protein extraction and enrichment, which aims to recover low-abundance secreted proteins from large volumes of dilute culture medium while minimizing sample loss and interference from background components. Existing extraction strategies can be broadly classified into precipitation-based, , filter-based, − and direct in-solution ,, approaches. Precipitation-based methods typically employ organic solvents such as acetone, alone or supplemented with trichloroacetic acid, to concentrate proteins from conditioned media and may be combined with additional centrifugation or gel-based separation steps. , Filter-based methods use ultrafiltration devices to retain secreted proteins and in some cases incorporate depletion of high-abundance background proteins, such as albumin, prior to proteomic analysis. − In contrast, in-solution approaches rely on direct solubilization of conditioned media in denaturing buffers or concentration of the medium by evaporation before digestion. ,− In the second stage, regardless of the extraction strategy employed, recovered proteins are subjected to standard proteomic processing, including reduction and alkylation of disulfide bonds followed by enzymatic digestion, most commonly with trypsin. The resulting peptide mixtures are then analyzed by LC–MS/MS to enable protein identification and quantification.

For applications involving microscale culture systems or time-resolved secretome sampling, analytical performance is primarily determined by protein recovery efficiency under low-input conditions. Precipitation-based and filter-based extraction methods are well suited for processing large volumes of conditioned medium but inherently involve multiple separation and transfer steps that lead to substantial protein loss. − Consequently, these approaches typically require milliliter-scale starting volumes to achieve sufficient proteome coverage and are poorly suited for low-volume or sequential sampling designs. In-solution digestion strategies mitigate some of these losses by enabling direct processing of conditioned media without physical separation; however, most reported protocols still rely on milliliter-scale inputs and extensive manual operations. ,,, These requirements limit scalability, reproducibility, and practical implementation in applications where sample volume and perturbation must be minimized.

To develop a practical high-throughput secretome profiling method, building on in-solution digestion approaches, we present an automated microvolume secretome profiling workflow that integrates optimized sample pretreatment and automated strong anion-exchange (SAX) magnetic bead-based protein sample preparation. SAX beads have previously been shown to enrich exosome-associated proteins in blood plasma, supporting their suitability for capturing low-abundance extracellular proteins. Through systematic benchmarking of pretreatment strategies and bead chemistries across a range of starting volumes, we establish a robust analytical framework that enables deep and reproducible secretome coverage from less than 20 μL of conditioned medium. We further demonstrate the performance of this workflow by applying it to time-resolved secretome profiling in cultured cells and to noninvasive protein analysis of individual mouse embryos. Together, this work addresses key analytical bottlenecks in secretome proteomics and provides a generalizable strategy for ultralow-input, automated secretome analysis with broad applicability.

ICR mice used in this study were obtained from and housed at the Animal Center of the Suzhou Institute of Systems Medicine, Chinese Academy of Medical Sciences and Peking Union Medical College.

Materials

LC–MS grade water (W6-4), acetonitrile (34851), and ethanol (1117270500) were purchased from Thermo Fisher. Fetal bovine serum (10099141), DPBS (D8537), DMEM (D1152), and DMEM without phenol red (21063029) were purchased from Gibco Thermo Fisher. Penicillin–streptomycin solution (100×, 143195) was purchased from Biosharp. PMSG (M2630), hCG (M2530), TYH (M2036), and HTF (M1135) medium were purchased from AibeiBio. EmbryoMax KSOM (MR-121), mineral oil (M5310), SDS (15-21-3), TCEP (tris(2-carboxyethyl) phosphine, C4706), CAA (2-chloroacetamide, 79-07-2), ABC (1066-33-7), BTP (bis-tris propane, 64431-96-5), NaCl (C111541), FA (formic acid, 64-18-6), trypsin (V5117-1), Lys-C (20420ES60), Bicinchoninic Acid Kit (23225), and isopropanol (34863) were purchased from Millipore Sigma.

HEK293T Cell Lines

HEK293T cells were cultured in DMEM (Gibco, Invitrogen), supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum and penicillin–streptomycin solution (100×, biosharp), at 37 °C, in a humidified incubator with 5% CO2. At ∼70% confluence, cells were washed twice with Dulbecco’s phosphate-buffered saline (DPBS) and cultured in DMEM (Gibco, Invitrogen), which was only supplemented with penicillin–streptomycin solution (100×, Biosharp), at 37 °C, in a humidified incubator with 5% CO2 for 16 h before secretome acquisition.

In Vitro Fertilization and Mouse Embryo Culture

For IVF, sperm were collected from 10 week old ICR male mice. The cauda epididymis was excised and punctured with a needle to release sperm into TYH medium drops, followed by capacitation at 37 °C under 5% CO2 for 1 h. Oocytes were obtained from 6 week old ICR females stimulated with 10 IU PMSG and 10 IU hCG 48 h later. Twelve hours after hCG injection, mice were sacrificed, and COCs were collected by puncturing antral follicles and placed into HTF medium drops. Activated sperm (3 μL) were added to medium drops containing COCs and incubated at 37 °C under 5% CO2 for 4 h. After coincubation, oocytes were collected under a stereomicroscope and transferred into fresh KSOM medium. Fertilization was assessed 6 h after insemination, and zygotes with two pronuclei were selected and transferred into individual 35 μL KSOM micro drops for further culture at 37 °C under 5% CO2.

Secretome Preparation

After 16 h of cell culture, the supernatant was sequentially centrifuged at 300g and 1000g for 10 min each to remove cell debris. The centrifuged supernatant was collected and stored at −20 °C before processing. Acetone precipitation: acetone was added to centrifuged supernatant to reach a final concentration of 80%. The mixture was gently inverted and incubated at room temperature for 30–45 min, followed by centrifugation at 4700g for 10 min. The supernatant was carefully discarded, and the protein pellet was washed twice with 95% ethanol, each time followed by centrifugation at 4700g for 10 min. After removing the ethanol, the pellet was air-dried in a fume hood with the lid open, then stored at −20 °C until further processing. Concentration-reconstitution (CRC): centrifuged supernatant was concentrated by vacuum centrifugation until dry (45 °C, 1400 rpm, ∼1.5 h). The resulting pellet was stored at −20 °C until redissolved and further processed. Direct digestion: no additional sample treatment before proteome preparation.

Proteome Preparation

For manual protein aggregation capture, pretreated medium was adjusted to contain 1% SDS, 5 mM TCEP (tris(2-carboxyethyl) phosphine), 10 mM CAA (2-chloroacetamide), and heated at 95 °C for 10 min. Protein concentration was assessed using a NanoDrop. MagReSyn hydroxyl beads were added based on a protein:bead ratio of 1:2 and rotated at room temperature for 10 min. Aggregation was induced by the addition of acetonitrile (unless stated otherwise), and magnetic microparticles were added to solution, followed by mixing to uniformly mix the bead solution. The solution was allowed to settle for 10 min, and beads were separated using a magnet for 60 s. Beads were washed using acetonitrile once followed by one wash with 70% ethanol. After ethanol wash, beads are air-dried and 300 μL of digestion buffer (50 mM ABC + trypsin + Lys-C; protein: (trypsin + Lys-C) = 100:1) was added, followed by 37 °C incubation overnight. The digestion was terminated by adding 10 μL of 1% FA.

For automatic protein preparation based on hydroxyl-modified beads, after concentration, all samples were redissolved using 25 μL of lysis buffer (1% SDS, 5 mM TCEP, 10 mM CAA, 100 mM Tris, pH = 8.5) and incubated at 95 °C for 10 min. Then, 5 μL of MagReSyn hydroxyl-modified beads (20 mg/mL) was added and incubated at room temperature for 20 min, followed by protein aggregation capture. PAC-purified proteins were digested on the beads with 30 μL digestion buffer (final concentration: 50 mM ABC; 20 μg trypsin + 5 μg Lys-C per 6 mL buffer) at 37 °C while rotating at 800 rpm for 6 h. Digestion was terminated by adding 10 μL of 1% formic acid.

For automatic protein preparation based on quaternary ammonium-modified SAX beads (strong anion-exchange, SAX), a mixture of 5 μL DPBS redissolved medium, 5 μL binding buffer (100 mM bis-tris propane (BTP), 150 mM NaCl, pH 6.3–6.4), and 1 μL beads was incubated at room temperature for 20 min. The beads were then washed 4× with wash buffer (50 mM BTP, 150 mM NaCl, pH 6.3–6.5). After reduction and alkylation (50 mM TEAB, 1% SDS, 5 mM TCEP, 10 mM CAA, RT, 1 h), the beads were washed twice with 100% ACN and subsequently digested on the beads with 30 μL digestion buffer (50 mM TEAB, 10 ng/μL trypsin, 2.5 ng/μL Lys-C) at 37 °C for 6 h. Digestion was terminated by adding 10 μL of 1% FA. Fifty percent of the resulting tryptic peptides was loaded for proteome analysis. All automatic procedures were conducted using the Opentrons Flex liquid handling robot (Supporting Information).

LC–MS/MS

For beads comparation, the Evosep One-Orbitrap Exploris 480 system was operated in data-independent acquisition (DIA) mode using a 30 SPD (sample per day) gradient (44 min). MS1 scans were acquired at a resolution of 120,000 over an m/z range of 380–980, with a normalized AGC target of 1000%. The MS/MS scans were acquired at a resolution of 150,000, scan range was set to m/z 150–2000, with precursor isolation limited to m/z 380–980. All other parameters were kept at default. For other proteomics acquisitions in this project, the Evosep One-Orbitrap Astral system was operated in DIA mode using a 60 SPD gradient (21 min). MS1 scans were acquired at a resolution of 240,000 over an m/z range of 380–980. The MS/MS scan range was set to m/z 150–2000, with the precursor mass range limited to m/z 380–980, and a normalized AGC target of 500%. All other parameters were kept at default.

Proteomic Data Analysis

For further proteomic analysis, all raw files underwent a library-free directDIA + approach using Spectronaut v.19 (Biognosys), employing the human reference database from the UniProt 2025 release, which contains 20,586 sequences, and the mouse reference database from the UniProt 2025 release, which contains 21,837 sequences, alongside an additional 246 common contaminant sequences. Variable modifications were set for methionine oxidation and protein N-terminal acetylation. In this study, proteins were considered reliably identified only if detected in at least 2 of 3 replicates (or 3 of 5 replicates). To correct for differences in protein loading, protein intensities were normalized on a per-sample basis using the total summed protein intensity of each individual sample, and missing values will be imputed based on the mean intensities of replicates. R (ver. 4.5.1) was used for downstream bioinformatic analyses. Venn diagrams were generated by using the VennDiagram package. Functional enrichment analysis of identified proteins was performed with the clusterProfiler package, and relationships among enriched Gene Ontology (GO) terms were visualized using emapplot. The temporal expression patterns of proteins in time-series samples were clustered using the Mfuzz package.

Results and Discussion

Analytical Evaluation of Secretome Pretreatment Strategies under Micro-Volume Constraints

To establish an automated workflow for magnetic bead-based enrichment of secreted proteins, it is essential to use input samples with high uniformity. However, in practical applications, the volume and concentration of conditioned medium vary across culture systems, necessitating standardized pretreatment before automation. Such preprocessing, however, may alter protein recovery, detection depth, and reproducibility, potentially introducing bias into the results. To address this requirement, we evaluated three pretreatment options, including acetone precipitation, direct digestion, and a simple solvent-based volume normalization step involving centrifugal concentration followed by reconstitution (concentration- reconstitution; CRC) (Figure A). Conditioned medium collected from HEK293T cells was examined across decreasing starting volumes. Acetone precipitation consistently produced the lowest protein recovery, indicating poor suitability for low-volume inputs. In contrast, the CRC method generated higher protein concentration when the starting volume exceeded 15 μL, while direct digestion produced better recovery at volumes below 15 μL (Figure B).

To further evaluate how these treatments influence stability and proteome depth, all three methods were tested using a constant 2 mL starting volume. Proteomic preparation followed a PAC workflow, and data acquisition was performed on an Evosep One-Orbitrap Astral system. The CRC method achieved the highest identification depth, detecting an average of 4016 protein groups, whereas acetone precipitation (3285) and direct digestion (3595) yielded fewer identifications (Figure C and Table S1). All methods shared a large set of 3789 protein groups, indicating broad overlap in recovered secretome components (Figure D). Only proteins detected in all replicates were included in the downstream analysis. Principal component analysis revealed high reproducibility among replicates for most secretome preparation methods, while acetone precipitation showed noticeably weaker clustering (Figure S1A).

Comprehensive evaluation of interreplicate correlation, data completeness, and missed cleavage rates across the three pretreatment methods (Figure S1B–D) revealed that acetone precipitation exhibited the lowest within-group reproducibility and data completeness, together with the highest missed cleavage rate. In contrast, both direct digestion and CRC showed superior performance across all of the evaluated metrics, indicating improved reproducibility, proteome coverage, and digestion efficiency. In terms of pretreatment stability, CRC showed the lowest median CV % at 15.5%, outperforming acetone precipitation (29.9%) and slightly surpassing direct digestion (15.6%) (Figure E). Examination of abundance-dependent CV patterns further showed that CRC consistently produced fewer proteins with elevated variability than did direct digestion (Figure F). Together, these findings demonstrate that the CRC method accommodates a broad range of input volumes, offers superior quantitative stability, and achieves the deepest proteome coverage, supporting it as the most reliable pretreatment method before automated secretome workflows.

SAX Achieve Higher Secretome Coverage with Low Starting Volumes

For automated magnetic bead-based secretome proteomic sample preparation, an Opentrons Flex robot was used to compare MagReSyn hydrophilic and MagReSyn SAX (quaternary ammonium-modified) beads, which also evaluated how starting medium volume affects secretome identification depth. Conditioned medium ranging from 5 to 50 μL was processed using a concentrate–redissolve workflow, with 100 μL samples serving as bulk controls. Using hydrophilic beads, the number of identified protein groups remained largely unchanged across starting volumes. In contrast, SAX beads showed an input-dependent increase in proteome depth. At the bulk level, SAX beads identified over 3100 protein groups versus ∼2045 with hydrophilic beads (Figure A and Table S2). Despite using only 20% of the bead amount, SAX beads captured nearly all proteins detected by hydrophilic beads (1866) and yielded 1239 unique proteins (Figure B). Correlations across different starting volumes were high for each bead type (r > 0.9), whereas correlations between SAX and hydrophilic beads were lower (∼0.75, Figure S2A). Differential expression analysis of co-identified proteins revealed that 302 were significantly enriched by SAX beads (Figure C). Functional analysis indicated that proteins uniquely or significantly enriched by SAX beads are predominantly involved in RNA processing and splicing, organelle and RNA localization, and vesicle organization and transport, with a subset linked to mitotic chromatid segregation (Figure D). These results suggest that many secreted proteins possess RNA-related intracellular functions and may be exported through vesicle-mediated pathways, indicating preferential capture of exosome-derived components by SAX beads.

Across starting volumes, SAX beads primarily recovered high-abundance secreted proteins at low input and progressively detected lower-abundance components as starting volume increased. Meanwhile, hydrophilic beads showed minimal variation across volumes. SAX beads achieved ∼60% of the 100 μL bulk coverage at 15 μL input, consistently outperforming hydrophilic beads at volumes ≥10 μL, whereas hydrophilic beads slightly exceeded SAX at 5 μL (Figures E and S2B). Overall, MagReSyn SAX beads provide deeper secretome coverage, stronger enrichment of low-abundance and vesicle-derived proteins, and a broader range of uniquely detected components, highlighting their effectiveness for low-volume secretome analysis.

In conclusion, by comprehensively evaluating three pretreatment methods and two magnetic bead-based automated workflows, we identified an optimized workflow combining CRC for sample volume normalization with a SAX-modified magnetic bead-based automated proteome preparation. The entire process can be completed within a single 96-well plate in 24 h, minimizing sample loss from transfers and improving overall processing throughput.

Automated Micro-Volume Secretome Profiling Enables Time-Resolved Analysis with Minimal Perturbation

The secretome changes over time in response to cellular secretion activity and environmental influences and can therefore rapidly reflect the physiological state of cultured cells, underscoring the need for longitudinal monitoring. However, conventional methods require large volumes of conditioned medium, limiting frequent sampling and potentially perturbing cell physiology. Now, using our optimized secretome workflow, time-series monitoring became feasible at low input volumes, enabling sensitive detection of changes associated with compromised cell states.

HEK293T cells were cultured in five independent T25 flasks using complete DMEM and grown to ∼70% confluence. Cells were washed with DPBS 3× and switched to 3 mL FBS-free DMEM for subsequent culture. Conditioned medium (20 μL per flask) was collected at 3, 5, 7, 12, and 24 h, with the 24 h time point serving as a control representing compromised cell conditions. Samples were sequentially centrifuged (300g and 1000g for 10 min each) to remove cellular debris, followed by CRC-based pretreatment for secretome preparation. Subsequent proteome sample preparation was then performed using SAX modified magnetic bead-based automatic workflow (Figure A). Data were acquired on the Evosep One-Orbitrap Astral system using a 60 SPD gradient. The number of identified protein groups remained stable from 3 h (3358 on average) to 7 h (3329), then increased between 12 h (3939) and 24 h (4537), with a similar trend in summed protein intensities, indicating that secretory activity and the diversity of secreted proteins both increase over time, likely coinciding with rising cell density and enhanced cell–cell contact (Figure B,C and Table S3).

(B) Number of protein groups identified at each time point. Bars show the mean number of proteins identified; dots represent individual samples. (C) Summed protein intensity of every sample at each time point. Bars show the mean of summed proteins intensities; dots represent individual samples summed proteins intensities. The top five enriched GO biological processes associated with this cluster are displayed on the right.

To investigate temporal secretome changes, PCA and time-series analyses were performed on 2932 proteins detected in at least 3 of 5 replicates per time point. Samples clustered closely at 3, 5, and 7 h along PC1, whereas 12 and 24 h samples separated progressively, reflecting global secretome changes (Figure D). A time-series analysis using Mfuzz grouped proteins into four clusters. Clusters 1 and 3 contained proteins stable from 3 to 7 h, then markedly increased at 12 and 24 h; high-membership proteins were enriched in RNA splicing, organelle localization, and ribonucleoprotein complex biogenesis, with Cluster 1 also enriched in nonmembrane-bounded organelle assembly. Cluster 2 proteins decreased after initial high expression at 3 h and were enriched in epidermis development, alcohol metabolism, and oxidative metabolic processes, suggesting transient cellular stress and adaptation to serum-free conditions around 5 h. Cluster 4 exhibited a gradual increase, enriched in protein folding and negative regulation of neuron projection development, likely reflecting density-dependent changes and enhanced cell–cell interactions (Figure E).

Overall, the optimized workflow enables secretome profiling from low starting volumes and captures temporal changes in the secretome, allowing for high-resolution monitoring of secretome proteins under defined culture conditions. The workflow is also sufficiently sensitive to detect compromised cell states with potential for broader applications.

Non-Invasive Secretome Profiling from Single Embryos under Ultra-Low-Input Conditions

Assessing embryo quality during in vitro culture remains challenging, as current evaluations rely primarily on time-lapse morphology and lack clinically applicable molecular indicators. In vivo, embryos secrete numerous proteins within the oviduct and uterus that mediate embryo–endometrium communication and modulate the uterine environment to support implantation. Thus, monitoring the temporal changes of the embryo secretome represents an ideal, noninvasive strategy for evaluating embryo developmental competence. However, embryos are highly sensitive to culture conditions, and protein-free medium may adversely affect development. Existing secretome profiling strategies are generally not well-suited for culture systems containing background proteins. To address this limitation, we employed a coculture strategy using matched blank control media alongside four embryo groups throughout preimplantation development. Protein consistently identified in the blank controls will be excluded, thereby minimizing the influence of culture medium background on secretome profiling.

After IVF, single 2PN (Pronucleus) embryos without cumulus cells were independently cultured in KSOM (Sigma) to the 2 cells, 4 cells, morula, or blastocyst stage, and 20 μL of the total 35 μL conditioned medium was collected from each embryo for secretome analysis. The collected medium was then volume-normalized using CRC, followed by automated proteome sample preparation using the SAX-modified magnetic bead-based workflow. Proteomic analysis was performed on an Evosep One-Orbitrap Astral system using a 60 SPD gradient (Figure A). Although the number of identified proteins varied among individual embryos, stage-to-stage differences were minor, with each sample yielding ∼220 proteins on average and >510 proteins detected per stage when replicates were combined (Figure B and Table S4). Although no significant differences were observed in the number of proteins detected in the culture medium, the total protein abundance exhibited a slight decreasing trend from the two-cell stage to the morula stage (Figure S3A). After removing 53 proteins present in blank controls, 361 proteins overlapped across all stages, while each stage also contained uniquely detected proteins (2-cell: 28; 4-cell: 12; morula: 6; and blastocyst: 11), indicating stage-specific secretome features (Figure C). Proteome coverage per sample increased modestly with development and stabilized at ∼40%; each sample typically contained >25% of the proteins identified for its corresponding stage (Figure D). PCA of secretome profiles showed that 4 cells and morula samples clustered tightly along PC1, whereas 2 cells and blastocyst stages were more distinct. Along PC2, 4 cells and morula separated from each other, while 2 cells and blastocyst partially overlapped, reflecting stage-dependent secretome variation (Figure E).

Further GO analysis of the 361 shared proteins revealed enrichment in purine-containing compound metabolism, precursor metabolite pathways, establishment of organelle localization, and proteasome-mediated ubiquitin-dependent protein catabolism. These enrichments indicate that stage-invariant components of the embryo secretome primarily contribute to metabolic regulation and protein homeostasis (Figures F and S3B).

A total of 56 proteins exhibited specific developmental expression patterns, representing potential stage-specific secretome markers. Several proteins increased progressively and reached higher abundance at the morula and blastocyst stages, including Ooep, Tle6, and Nlrp5, core components of the subcortical maternal complex; Ddx3x, an essential regulator of embryogenesis and placentation; − Ywhag, a paracrine mediator associated with PRDM signaling that was markedly enriched at the blastocyst stage; Skp1, whose loss causes early embryonic arrest; and Ldhb, may reflect metabolic remodeling during early cleavage-stage development. Notably, Sdc4, a heparan-sulfate proteoglycan involved in extracellular-vesicle-mediated communication, and might function in placentation, was detected in nearly all blastocyst-conditioned media and absent in other stages, highlighting its potential as a candidate biomarker for blastocyst quality assessment. We also identified proteins that were highly expressed before the 4-cell stage but diminished thereafter. These include Afdn, a regulator of cell junction formation and signal transduction; Rab10 and Vcp, whose depletion results in early embryonic lethality; Pfn1, required for cell survival and division; and Uba1, essential for the DNA-damage response (Figure G). These early stage secreted proteins may contribute to the ZGA process and thus serve as potential markers of early developmental competence.

Together, these results demonstrate that our optimized method enables high-throughput, sensitive, and time-resolved secretome profiling from a single-embryo culture medium and highlights its compatibility with diverse culture medium compositions across different experimental requirements. This workflow, therefore, provides a potential method for noninvasive embryo quality assessment in clinical practice.

Conclusion

This study presents a novel automatic microvolume secretome preparation workflow that enables high-depth proteomic profiling from minimal culture medium volumes. By integrating concentration–reconstitution (CRC) for volume normalization with a quaternary ammonium-modified (SAX) magnetic bead-based automated proteome preparation workflow, we achieve highly sensitive, reproducible, and scalable high-throughput secretome analysis. Applying this workflow, we examined temporal changes in the secretome of HEK293T cells following the transition to serum-free culture and during in vitro embryo development. HEK293T cells displayed time-dependent secretion patterns, with a substantial portion of proteins linked to RNA splicing and RNA processing and oxidative metabolic processes, indicating adaptive cellular responses to environmental changes. These results demonstrate that the workflow is sufficiently sensitive to capture temporal secretome dynamics, reflecting adaptive cellular responses. We further turn to a more encouraging application of noninvasive embryo quality assessment, in which single-embryo secretomes are analyzed from minimally processed culture medium. Through time-resolved secretome profiling of such single embryos, we identified embryo stage-specific secreted proteins, including subsets whose abundance progressively increased across development. These findings highlight their potential as candidate noninvasive molecular biomarkers for assessing embryo quality.

Collectively, our optimized automatic microvolume secretome workflow provides a versatile and robust workflow for standardized, high-throughput secretome profiling with deep identification coverage. It is compatible with various culture media systems for both clinical and experimental needs and ultimately enables noninvasive analysis of single-embryo secretomes for investigating embryo developmental or environmental effects on cellular secretory behavior.
