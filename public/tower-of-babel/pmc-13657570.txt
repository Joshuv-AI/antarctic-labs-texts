Multi-model comparison and nomogram development for predicting in-hospital mortality in Trauma patients based on NLR and other indicators
Authors: Kang Chao, Fan Jiawei, Xu Tairui, Ma Youyun, Zhang Bo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Objective:This study aimed to systematically compare the predictive performance of logistic regression (LR), random forest (RF), and extreme gradient boosting (XGBoost) for predicting in-hospital mortality among trauma patients, evaluate the prognostic value of key indicators, including the neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR), and develop a visual clinical nomogram.Methods:A retrospective analysis was conducted on 318 severe trauma patients [Injury Severity Score (ISS) ≥ 16] admitted between January 2022 and October 2024 as the derivation cohort; an independent temporal validation cohort of 161 patients admitted between January 2025 and May 2026 from the same institution was also included. LR, RF, and XGBoost models were trained and evaluated for AUC, sensitivity, specificity, and calibration. LASSO regression was used for variable selection, and a multivariable LR model was constructed to develop a nomogram.Results:On the internal test set, RF achieved the highest AUC (0.919) with a sensitivity of 0.963. LR had the highest specificity (0.868), with a calibration plot indicating good agreement. In the temporal validation cohort, LR demonstrated robust performance with an AUC of 0.906. Multivariable LR identified age [odds ratios (OR) = 1.071], ISS (OR = 1.234), and shock index (OR = 3.963) as independent risk factors, while NLR was a paradoxical protective factor (OR = 0.872). Variable importance analysis ranked NLR as the top contributor. A nomogram incorporating these variables demonstrated favorable discrimination and clinical utility, supported by DCA.Conclusion:The developed nomogram integrates key predictors into a visual tool for rapid mortality risk assessment in trauma patients, thereby facilitating early risk stratification and optimized resource allocation. The RF model is superior for identifying high-risk patients (high sensitivity), whereas the LR model excels at ruling out low-risk patients (high specificity) and demonstrates better probability calibration. NLR is a significant predictor of early post-traumatic mortality, although its direction of effect warrants further investigation.

Introduction

Trauma remains a leading global cause of death and permanent disability among young adults. Studies report in-hospital mortality rates among severe trauma patients ranging from 20 to 30%[1]. The early clinical course in these patients is often insidious and rapidly progressive. Current clinical prognostic assessment systems in trauma are centered on the Injury Severity Score (ISS), which stratifies risk by quantifying anatomical injury severity. Its foundational role has been validated in numerous studies[3]. However, the ISS focuses exclusively on the anatomical dimension of trauma and fails to adequately integrate key pathophysiological indicators of the post-injury state, such as inflammatory stress and circulatory dysfunction. This results in notable limitations in the accuracy and comprehensiveness of early prognostic assessment[4].

In recent years, machine learning techniques have emerged as a focus in trauma prognosis prediction due to their capacity to capture complex nonlinear relationships among multiple variables. Established models such as logistic regression (LR), random forest (RF), and extreme gradient boosting (XGBoost) have been shown to enhance predictive performance[5,6]. Nevertheless, existing research commonly exhibits three main shortcomings: first, studies are often confined to validating a single model’s performance, lacking systematic comparisons between different algorithms, which obscures their respective clinical applicability boundaries[7]; second, the “black-box” nature and operational complexity of many machine learning models hinder their direct translation into practical tools for widespread use in clinical settings, particularly at the primary care level[8]; third, while inflammation-immune imbalance is a core pathological mechanism underlying secondary injury and poor prognosis following trauma[9], the integration and validation of easily accessible, low-cost inflammatory markers like the neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR) in current prediction models remain insufficient[10–12].

In this context, the present study aimed to systematically evaluate the mortality risk prediction performance of LR, RF, and XGBoost models in a clinical trauma cohort across four progressive dimensions: “overall quantitative metrics, discrimination ability trends, inter-model relative differences, and probability calibration.” Concurrently, the clinical value of core predictive indicators was elucidated, and a visual nomogram tool was constructed. To rigorously assess the model’s generalizability and robustness, we additionally performed an independent temporal validation using a subsequent cohort from the same institution, complemented by decision curve analysis (DCA) and a sensitivity analysis to evaluate the contribution of individual predictors. The goal was to provide a scientifically robust and practical solution for the early prognostic assessment of trauma patients[13,14].

Materials and methods

General data

Derivation cohort

Clinical data were retrospectively collected from 318 adult patients meeting the diagnostic criteria for severe trauma who were admitted between January 2022 and October 2024.

Independent temporal validation cohort

An independent cohort of 161 consecutive severe trauma patients admitted between January 2025 and May 2026 from the same institution was used for temporal validation.

Data included routine blood test results on admission (white blood cell count, neutrophil count, lymphocyte count, platelet count, and hemoglobin), D-dimer levels, as well as patient gender, age, pulse, blood pressure, injury sites, and severity. The NLR, shock index (SI), and ISS were calculated.

Inclusion criteria

(1) Age ≥18 years; (2) diagnosis of multiple injuries at admission with an ISS score ≥ 16; (3) Hospital admission within 6 hours post-injury.

Exclusion criteria

(1) Age <18 years; (2) arrival at the emergency department >6 hours post-injury; (3) incomplete medical records (laboratory or imaging data); (4) withdrawal from treatment due to patient preference, without definitive outcome documentation; and (5) history of hematological diseases or coagulation dysfunction.

This study protocol was reviewed and approved by the Ethics Committee of the participating institution (Approval No. [Anonymized for review]) and complied with ethical standards for clinical research.

Study variables and data preprocessing

Patients were dichotomized into death and survival groups based on their 28-day prognosis. Differences in sex, age, pulse, systolic and diastolic blood pressure; white blood cell, neutrophil, lymphocyte, and platelet counts; hemoglobin, NLR, D-dimer, and ISS score between the two groups were analyzed statistically. Regression and receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analyses were employed to evaluate the predictive value of each indicator for the prognosis of patients with severe trauma.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.4.1), with a two-sided significance level set at α = 0.05.

Data quality and baseline characteristics

Initial data quality checks assessed the outcome distribution, identified potential “perfect predictor” variables (with completely non-overlapping ranges between groups), and evaluated multicollinearity (an absolute value of Pearson’s correlation coefficient > 0.7 was considered indicative of strong correlation). Normality was tested for all continuous variables using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Normally distributed variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD), and non-normally distributed variables as median and interquartile range [M(Q1, Q3)]. Categorical variables (e.g., gender) are presented as frequency and percentage. Continuous variables with missing data were imputed using the MICE algorithm (five imputations).

Univariate analysis

Univariate analyses were conducted to screen for predictors associated with in-hospital mortality. The chi-squared test was used for categorical variables. For continuous variables, the independent-samples t-test (normal distribution) or Mann-Whitney U test (non-normal distribution) was applied as appropriate. Variables with a P-value < 0.05 were included in the subsequent multivariate analysis.

Variable selection and multivariate analysis

To avoid overfitting and identify core predictors, Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator (LASSO) regression was used for variable selection. All variables significant in the univariate analysis formed the initial feature matrix. The optimal penalty coefficient (λ) was determined via 10-fold cross-validation using the “one standard error” rule to favor a more parsimonious model. Variables with non-zero coefficients at the selected λ were retained. A multivariable binary LR model was then built using these variables, reporting odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs).

Prediction model development, validation, and comparison

The dataset was randomly partitioned into a training set (n = 223) and an independent test set (n = 95) in a 7:3 ratio. Three prediction models were developed on the training set: (1) an LR model using LASSO-selected variables; (2) a RF model with parameters set to control overfitting (number of trees: ntree = 300; minimum node size: nodesize = 10; maxnodes = 20); and (3) an XGBoost model with conservative hyperparameters (max_depth = 4, learning rate eta = 0.05, gamma = 1, subsample = 0.7, colsample_bytree = 0.7, min_child_weight = 5, lambda = 1, and alpha = 0.5) and L1/L2 regularization.

Model performance was evaluated on the independent test set. The primary evaluation metric was the area under the ROC curve (AUC). Sensitivity, specificity, and accuracy at the optimal cutoff (determined by Youden’s index) were also reported. Discriminatory abilities were compared based on test set AUC values.

To rigorously assess generalizability, all three models were subsequently validated on the independent temporal validation cohort (n = 161). The primary analysis focused on the LR model used for the nomogram, for which we also performed DCA to evaluate its clinical net benefit across a range of threshold probabilities, comparing it against a “treat-all” or “treat-none” strategy and against the ISS score alone. Furthermore, a sensitivity analysis was conducted by excluding D-dimer from the LR model to assess its incremental predictive value, with AUCs compared using DeLong’s test.

Clinical application translation

A simplified clinical risk scoring system was derived from the coefficients of the final multivariable LR model. To facilitate rapid bedside assessment, a nomogram was constructed based on the final model, allowing visual translation of individual predictor values into a total score and the corresponding probability of mortality.

Variable selection and multivariable LR

The six variables significant in univariate analysis (age, ISS, D-dimer, hemoglobin, NLR, and shock index) were entered into LASSO regression. The variance inflation factors were all less than 5, indicating no multicollinearity. LASSO regression (λ = 0.036) selected five variables for the final model, excluding hemoglobin.

Multivariable LR (Table 3, Fig. 1) identified age, ISS score, and shock index as independent risk factors for in-hospital mortality (OR > 1, P < 0.05), while NLR was a paradoxical protective factor (OR = 0.872, P < 0.001). Prediction model performance comparison

The performance of LR, RF, and XGBoost models was comprehensively assessed on the independent test set (n = 95) and further validated on the independent temporal validation cohort from the same institution (n = 161; mortality rate, 20.5%).

On the internal test set, RF achieved the highest AUC (0.919) and sensitivity (0.963); LR had the highest specificity (0.868); XGBoost showed intermediate values (AUC = 0.912, sensitivity = 0.852, specificity = 0.853). All performance metrics were calculated at the optimal threshold determined by the Youden index for each model individually, ensuring a fair comparison. ROC curves (Fig. 2A) illustrated that, while RF and XGBoost had higher AUCs, XGBoost achieved the most balanced sensitivity-specificity trade-off, whereas LR demonstrated a more balanced performance across most threshold ranges. RF’s curve rose rapidly in the high-sensitivity region, aligning with its high sensitivity. A decile calibration plot for the LR model (Fig. 4), which exhibited high specificity, showed good agreement between predicted probabilities and observed event rates in the medium-to-high probability range (0.25–0.75) and only slight underestimation in the low-probability range (≤0.25), indicating overall satisfactory calibration. The models were further validated in an independent temporal validation cohort of 161 patients (33 deaths, 20.5%). In the temporal validation cohort, LR demonstrated robust discriminative ability with an AUC of 0.906 (95% CI: 0.848–0.965), outperforming RF (AUC = 0.865) and XGBoost (AUC = 0.868). Notably, LR achieved the highest specificity (0.938) and accuracy (0.894), while RF showed the highest sensitivity (0.939) (Figs 2B and 3B). These findings are consistent with those from the internal test set, where LR excelled in specificity and calibration, whereas RF was superior in sensitivity.

In summary, RF demonstrated the highest sensitivity, making it suitable for rapid identification of high-risk patients, while LR excelled in specificity, calibration, and generalizability. XGBoost offered a balanced performance profile. The consistent performance of LR across both the internal and temporal validation cohorts supports its selection for the final clinical nomogram.

Clinical translation and application

Feature importance analysis from the RF model (assessed by the mean decrease in the Gini index; Fig. 5) revealed a clear hierarchy: NLR was the most important predictor, followed by ISS, shock index, age, and D-dimer. This ranking underscores the significant prognostic value of NLR as a composite marker of post-traumatic inflammation-immune imbalance, alongside established indicators of injury severity, circulatory status, physiological reserve, and coagulation function. The paradoxical direction of NLR’s effect (lower levels being associated with higher mortality) is a key finding that is addressed in the discussion. The mortality risk nomogram (Fig. 6) translates the final LR model into a practical clinical tool. By visually aligning patient values for age, ISS, D-dimer, NLR, and shock index on respective axes to obtain points, summing these points yields a total score corresponding to an individual’s mortality probability. This tool bypasses the need for complex software, enabling rapid risk assessment at the bedside in fast-paced environments like emergency departments. It can aid in prioritizing intensive care for high-risk patients and guiding appropriate management for low-risk individuals. DCA and sensitivity analysis

To evaluate clinical utility, DCA was performed on the temporal validation cohort. As depicted in Figure 7, the nomogram (LR model) demonstrated a higher net benefit across a wide range of threshold probabilities compared with the “treat-all” or “treat-none” strategies and the ISS score alone, supporting its clinical usefulness. To assess the robustness of the final LR model, we conducted a sensitivity analysis by excluding D-dimer – the only variable that did not reach statistical significance in the multivariable model (P = 0.083) – and re-evaluated its performance in the temporal validation cohort. As shown in Table 4, the AUC remained virtually unchanged (0.906 vs. 0.902, DeLong’s P = 0.546), indicating overall model stability. However, the full model demonstrated improved specificity (0.938 vs. 0.898) with a modest trade-off in sensitivity (0.727 vs. 0.758). Based on its LASSO-selected status and incremental clinical value, D-dimer was retained in the final nomogram. Discussion

This study performed a multi-dimensional evaluation of machine learning models for predicting in-hospital mortality in trauma patients and developed a directly applicable clinical nomogram. A major strength of this study is the robust, independent temporal validation, which significantly enhances the generalizability of our findings. The findings provide evidence-based insights for model selection in trauma prognosis and present a practical framework for translating machine learning into clinical practice.

Differentiated model performance and clinical applicability

The three models exhibited distinct performance profiles. The RF model demonstrated superior overall discriminatory ability (highest AUC) and exceptional sensitivity on the internal test set (AUC: 0.919; sensitivity: 0.963). This aligns with the strengths of ensemble learning algorithms in capturing complex, nonlinear interactions among diverse predictors such as injury severity and inflammatory status[6]. Prior systematic reviews also support the advantage of ensemble methods over traditional linear models for identifying high-risk trauma patients[5]. Consequently, RF’s high sensitivity makes it particularly suitable for the urgent need in emergency settings to “rapidly identify critical cases for prioritized intervention.”

In contrast, the LR model, while having a lower AUC on the internal test set (0.877 vs. 0.919 for RF), achieved the highest specificity (0.868) and demonstrated excellent probability calibration. Its inherent interpretability, through direct quantification of predictor contributions via regression coefficients, is a major advantage[7]. LR is, therefore, well-suited for clinical scenarios where accurately ruling out low-risk patients is paramount to avoid unnecessary interventions or resource allocation, such as in patient triage or determining appropriate ward monitoring levels.

The XGBoost model presented a balanced performance profile (AUC: 0.912; sensitivity: 0.852; specificity: 0.853), making it a versatile choice for general prognostic assessment where no single metric is preferentially emphasized[4]. This comparative analysis addresses a common limitation of studies evaluating only a single model[15] and helps define the clinical niches for different algorithmic approaches.

Importantly, the independent temporal validation confirmed the robustness of these findings, with the LR model demonstrating particularly stable and superior discriminative ability (AUC = 0.906), suggesting that it may be the most generalizable model across different temporal cohorts. RF maintained its high sensitivity (0.939) in the temporal validation cohort, consistent with its role as a sensitive screening tool.

Clinical mechanisms and value of predictive indicators

The finding that NLR was the most important predictor aligns with the established pathophysiology, in which inflammation-immune dysregulation is central to post-traumatic sequelae and outcomes[2]. NLR integrates information on both innate inflammatory activation (via neutrophils) and adaptive immune suppression (via lymphocytes)[16]. Its low cost and rapid availability (often within 1–2 hours of admission) grant it far greater clinical utility than more complex immunological assays, solidifying its role as an ideal early prognostic biomarker in trauma. Meta-analytic evidence further supports the robust and consistent predictive value of NLR for adverse outcomes in trauma populations[10].

However, a noteworthy and seemingly paradoxical finding in our study is that a lower NLR was associated with higher mortality (OR = 0.87), which contradicts the widely reported literature in which elevated NLR typically predicts worse outcomes. We propose two explanations for this observation. First, our cohort consists exclusively of severe trauma patients (ISS ≥ 16). In this specific population, an extremely low NLR may reflect a state of post-traumatic immune paralysis rather than a benign inflammatory profile. Following massive tissue injury and hemorrhagic shock, a profound immunosuppressive response can occur, characterized by reduced neutrophil mobilization and lymphocyte dysfunction, which has been linked to sepsis and multiple organ failure. Second, all blood samples were collected within 6 hours post-injury. The most severely injured patients who died early may not have had sufficient time to mount a detectable neutrophilic rise, and their admission NLR values likely represent a very early, pre-shock immune status rather than the peak inflammatory response. Therefore, while NLR is a powerful predictor, its interpretation in the specific context of early severe trauma must be nuanced. However, these proposed explanations should be regarded as hypotheses rather than established mechanisms. Future prospective studies should investigate the dynamic trajectory of NLR and its relationship to the timing of death.

The notable contribution of the ISS score reaffirms the foundational importance of anatomical injury assessment in trauma prognosis[3]. However, ISS primarily quantifies structural damage and does not capture dynamic physiological or inflammatory changes; moreover, accurate scoring early after injury can be challenging due to limitations of initial imaging. Our DCA (Fig. 7) further demonstrated that the nomogram, which integrates ISS with inflammatory (NLR) and circulatory (shock index) indicators, provided a higher net benefit than ISS alone across a range of threshold probabilities. These findings suggest that future prognostic tools should evolve from a singular focus on “injury severity” toward a more holistic model that “integrates injury severity with the host’s inflammatory/immune status”[12].

Although shock index, age, and D-dimer showed relatively lower importance scores, they represent critical dimensions of circulatory competence, physiological reserve, and coagulation status, respectively[17,18]. Their inclusion ensures the model captures key systemic pathophysiological derangements, enhancing the comprehensiveness of risk assessment. Our sensitivity analysis on the temporal validation cohort showed that while excluding D-dimer did not significantly change the AUC (0.906 vs. 0.902, DeLong’s P = 0.546), it reduced the specificity from 0.938 to 0.898, with a modest increase in sensitivity (0.727–0.758). This indicates that D-dimer contributes clinically meaningful incremental value by improving the correct identification of low-risk (surviving) patients, despite not reaching statistical significance as an independent predictor. Therefore, the variable was retained in the final nomogram based on its LASSO-selected status and incremental clinical value.

Practical value of the clinical translation tool

The developed nomogram represents a key step in bridging the gap between algorithmic performance and bedside utility. Visual nomograms translate complex model outputs into an accessible format. By providing intuitive visualizations of how individual predictor values contribute to the overall risk estimate, nomograms can help demystify “black-box” models and facilitate clinician adoption[19]. The present nomogram builds on this by incorporating trauma-specific, routinely available indicators. Unlike prediction models that require specialized software or computational expertise[8], this tool employs a simple “locate-sum-interpret” process accessible to any clinician. This addresses a critical need for highly accessible prognostic tools to improve trauma care efficiency, as highlighted in clinical guidelines[20]. By enabling rapid, accurate risk stratification, the nomogram can guide resource allocation – prioritizing intensive care for high-risk patients while supporting appropriate, less intensive monitoring for low-risk individuals – thus offering an integrated scientific and practical solution. The DCA further substantiates its clinical value by demonstrating a positive net benefit across a range of clinically relevant threshold probabilities.

Study limitations and future directions

This study has several limitations. First, the sample size of the independent test set (n = 95) is modest, which may affect the stability of the performance estimates. While we performed an independent temporal validation on a subsequent cohort (n = 161), these patients were from the same institution, which may still introduce selection bias. Nevertheless, the consistent performance across the derivation and validation cohorts, along with the use of regularization techniques (LASSO) and cross-validation in model training, supports the robustness of our findings despite the modest sample size. Second, although we included an independent temporal validation cohort, the study lacks multicenter external validation and remains a single-center retrospective study; the patient cohort may therefore have inherent homogeneity, potentially limiting the generalizability of the findings to other settings. External validation in multi-center cohorts is needed[21]. Third, the paradoxical finding regarding NLR, while discussed, requires further prospective validation and mechanistic studies. Specifically, future research should investigate the dynamic time-course of NLR and its interaction with other inflammatory markers. Fourth, the model did not incorporate dynamic factors such as specific comorbidities, neurological status indicators (e.g., Glasgow Coma Scale), detailed treatment interventions, or temporal changes in biomarkers such as NLR[22]. Future research should focus on validating the nomogram in larger, multi-center populations and exploring the integration of serial measurements and treatment variables to develop dynamic, more personalized prediction models[23].

Conclusion

This multi-dimensional evaluation clarified the distinct clinical applications of different machine learning models in trauma prognosis, confirmed the central prognostic value of the NLR, and, through rigorous temporal validation and DCA, successfully developed and validated a directly applicable clinical nomogram with demonstrated generalizability and clinical utility. This work provides a novel, evidence-based tool to aid in the early risk assessment of trauma patients.
