Translation and content validity of the Arabic Communication and Symbolic Behaviour Scales Developmental Profile (CSBS DP) Infant–Toddler Checklist
Authors: Ghada S M Al-Bluwi, Emad Masuadi, Alexandra Marquis, Rami H Al-Rifai, Iffat Elbarazi, Luai A Ahmed
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Abstract AbstractObjectiveThe Communication and Symbolic Behaviour Scales Developmental Profile Infant–Toddler Checklist (CSBS DP ITC) is a screening tool designed to identify early deficits in social communication, expressive speech/language and symbolic functioning in children aged 6–24 months. This study aimed to translate and content validate the CSBS DP ITC into Modern Standard Arabic.DesignMethodological study involving translation and content validation.SettingThe study was conducted in the United Arab Emirates.ParticipantsThe translation process involved five bilingual translators and one monolingual Arabic language expert. Ten experts participated in the content validation phase, and 10 parents of young children participated in the face validity assessment.Outcome measuresContent Validity Indices (CVIs), including the Item-level CVI (I-CVI), the Scale-level CVI by Average (S-CVI/Ave) and the S-CVI by Universal Agreement (S-CVI/UA), along with modified kappa statistics, were calculated to assess item-level and scale-level clarity, relevance and comprehensiveness.ResultsExpert panel ratings showed high clarity (I-CVI: 0.8–1, S-CVI/Ave: 0.98, S-CVI/UA: 0.88) and similar relevance scores. Face validity assessments yielded clarity I-CVI scores of 0.9–1, with S-CVI/Ave at 0.98 and S-CVI/UA at 0.8. The modified kappa statistic ranged from 0.89 to 1, indicating strong agreement among parents.ConclusionsThe CSBS DP ITC was effectively translated and content validated into Modern Standard Arabic. The calculated CVI values ranged from excellent to acceptable. This step establishes a foundation before proceeding to full psychometric testing of the instrument, paving the way for a reliable and culturally appropriate tool to identify early communication delays for use across the Arab-speaking population.Keywords: Child, Primary Prevention, Speech pathology, Community child health

Introduction

The progression and maturation of communication and language skills are essential elements of child development.1 Difficulties in communication and language during childhood can lead to social, behavioural, learning, psychosocial and adulthood-related problems.2 3 Furthermore, impairments in communication and language are often the first sign of developmental and intellectual disorders.4

Screening for communication and language difficulties at a very young age (ie, ≤24 months) is crucial to initiate early intervention. Mounting evidence suggests that initiating interventions at a very young age has a more significant effect on outcomes for children and families than offering services during school age.5,7 To accurately identify children at risk of communication and language delay at 24 months of age or younger, a screening tool needs to incorporate prelinguistic and linguistic domains.5 In infants, prelinguistic communication skills, including gestures, emotions, early vocal sounds that have shared meaning with the caregiver, symbolic and functional play, dominate initially.8,10 Around 19 months of age, linguistic communication begins to accelerate, reaching hundreds of words and multiple sentences by the second birthday.11 12 Although there is a wide variation in the age and rate of acquisition of linguistic communication, the variation is strongly associated with prelinguistic development.13,15 Therefore, instead of waiting for children to start using words, evaluating different prelinguistic language predictors is a preferable option to enhance early identification.

Currently, there exist few Arabic validated or culturally adapted screening tools that are used to assess the development of children’s communication and language. Most of those instruments focus on linguistic skills alone, are typically dialect specific, target children older than 2 years of age, are generally time-consuming, rely mostly on face-to-face interactions with a practitioner and are not freely available.16,20 In this context, where resources are frequently limited and time is of the essence, having a broadband (covering prelinguistic and linguistic domains), fast, freely available, Arabic validated and culturally adapted parental questionnaire focusing on children aged 24 months and below is needed.

The Communication and Symbolic Behaviour Scales Developmental Profile (CSBS DP) is a tool that identifies early deficiencies in social communication, expressive speech/language and symbolic functioning in children aged 6–24 months.21 It is distinguished by its comprehensive scope, as it not only assesses vocabulary and word comprehension but also evaluates broader prelinguistic and linguistic domains, making it particularly suitable for the early identification of difficulties in very young children.

The CSBS DP consists of three forms that can be used separately or in combination. The first form is the Infant–Toddler Checklist (ITC) which includes 24 multiple-choice questions (MCQs) and one open-ended question that asks parents whether they have any concerns about their child’s development and, if so, to describe them. These MCQs are grouped into seven language predictor clusters: Emotion and Eye Gaze, Communication, Gestures, Sounds, Words, Understanding and Object Use. The primary aim of this screening checklist is to determine whether a referral for a developmental evaluation is needed and to document changes over time. The checklist is copyrighted21 but can be downloaded freely from the internet.22 It only takes the parent or the caregiver of the child 5–10 min to complete the ITC. However, calculating the score and categorising the child as ‘concern’ or ‘no concern’ should be done by a healthcare provider. If the ITC score indicates the need for further evaluation, the follow-up step involves two companion forms: The Caregiver Questionnaire and the Behaviour Sample. The Caregiver Questionnaire, completed by parents, is a more detailed follow-up to the ITC and takes approximately 15–20 min, gathering in-depth information on the seven developmental clusters. The Behaviour Sample requires about 30 min and involves direct, face-to-face interaction between the child and a trained professional, with the caregiver present.

Growing evidence suggests that the ITC, which was originally established on American English-speaking children, can identify children who are at risk of language and communication difficulties, regardless of their language or cultural background.23,32 Although the ITC has been adapted and validated in multiple languages and cultures, including, but not limited to South African English,26 Chinese,29 Persian,23 Finnish,28 Swedish,25 Polish30 and European Portuguese,27 it has not been adapted and validated in the Arabic population yet.

This study builds on the findings of our recently published scoping review on the epidemiology of developmental language disorder (DLD) among children in the Arab region.33 That review revealed substantial heterogeneity in study methodologies, particularly in the screening tools employed to assess children at risk of DLD, and highlighted the urgent need for culturally adapted and validated instruments. Such instruments should be applicable across all Arab countries to ensure both cultural and linguistic appropriateness, enhance comparability of results and support the early identification of children under 2 years of age who may be at risk of DLD before a formal diagnosis is possible, thereby allowing for timely intervention and ongoing developmental monitoring.

The current study is the first step of the CSBS DP ITC adaptation and validation in the Arabic language. This study aimed to translate the CSBS DP ITC into Modern Standard Arabic (MSA) and to assess its content validity.

Methods

The COSMIN (COnsensus-based Standards for the selection of health Measurement INstruments) guidelines served as the primary framework in the translation and initial validation of the CSBS DP ITC, including the use of its agreed terminology and definitions.34 The process was further guided by the practical recommendations by Cruchinho et al35 and by Sousa and Rojjanasrirat.36 Sousa and Rojjanasrirat’s guidelines were applied mainly to the translation process, as they provide detailed stepwise descriptions. From Cruchinho et al’s guidance, we adopted the recommendation to document all procedures in detail and report the involvement of every contributor to enhance transparency and reproducibility.

Preparation

A comprehensive protocol for the translation and adaptation of the CSBS DP ITC was developed to guide the process. This protocol was designed as part of a larger project aiming to conduct three studies, with the current paper representing study 1 (online supplemental file 1). The protocol was carefully designed to preserve the integrity of the original instrument, maintaining its core constructs and purpose while ensuring cultural relevance for Arabic-speaking populations.

Permission to use and adapt the CSBS DP ITC was obtained from the copyright holders, Paul H. Brookes Publishing Co. The Final Arabic Language Version (F-AL) was subsequently submitted to the copyright holders for their review and formal approval.

Setting

The United Arab Emirates (UAE) was selected as the setting for this study due to its unique multicultural composition. Expatriates constitute approximately 88% of the total population,37 which includes a significant number of Arabic speakers from all Arab countries. This rich linguistic and cultural diversity allows for the inclusion of participants from different Arabic-speaking backgrounds, ensuring a comprehensive evaluation of the instrument across various Arabic cultural contexts.

Arabic language

MSA serves as the formal language universally understood and used across all Arabic-speaking countries.38 Therefore, it was chosen for the translation, adaptation and validation of the original American English version of the CSBS DP ITC. No specific Arabic dialect was incorporated, to ensure the instrument’s broad applicability across various Arabic-speaking countries.

Translation

The translation process involved five translators, one monolingual Arabic specialist, and the research team, and was divided into four steps (figure 1). The research team instructed translators 1, 2 and 3 to use MSA and avoid dialects, emphasising cultural adaptation over a word-for-word translation. Translators were also advised to note any doubts or comments for discussion in later steps.

Flow chart of the translation and content validation process. CSBS-DP, Communication and Symbolic Behaviour Scales Developmental Profile; ITC, Infant–Toddler Checklist; PI-FL, Preliminary Initial Arabic Language Version; P-FAL, Pre-Final Arabic Language Version.

In step 1 (forward translation): The CSBS DP ITC was independently translated by translator 1, a bilingual native Arabic speaker with expertise in CSBS DP ITC instrument, and translator 2, a bilingual parent with no prior knowledge of the tool. Translator 2 was unaware of the upcoming back-translation process to ensure culturally relevant language usage.

In step 2 (Synthesis I): A team-based approach consolidated the two forward translations into one Arabic version. Translator 3, an expert in instrument validation, compared both translations with the original American English version and resolved any discrepancies. The consolidated version was reviewed by translators 1 and 2 and discussed in online meetings to reach consensus. The final version was proofread by a monolingual Arabic specialist and approved as the ‘Preliminary Initial Arabic Language Version’ (PI-AL).

In step 3 (backward translation): The PI-AL was sent to two independent professional translators (translator 4 and translator 5) to translate it back into English. Both translators were unaware of the original version and had no contact with each other to ensure unbiased translations.

In step 4 (synthesis II): A team-based approach was used to compare all versions (original, forward translations, PI-AL and back-translations). Translators 1, 2 and 3 worked together to identify ambiguities and discrepancies, ultimately deciding on the most appropriate translation. The research team then met to reach consensus on issues such as cultural meanings, colloquialisms and idiomatic expressions. The final version of the translation process was called the ‘Pre-Final Version’ (P-FAL).

Content validity

Expert panel

Using convenience and snowball sampling approaches, an expert panel of ten native Arabic speakers was recruited. The panel consisted of two public health specialists, one academic paediatrician, one clinical paediatrician, one psychologist, two speech therapists, one general practitioner and two paediatric nurses. The sample size and characteristics of the expert panel were determined based on previously published recommendations.36 39 40 Each panel member evaluated the instructions, items and response format of the P-FAL for clarity, relevance and comprehensiveness. For clarity, each item was evaluated using a 4-point scale: 1=unclear, 2=partially clear, 3=mostly clear and 4=very clear. Panel members were asked to provide suggestions for rewriting items that could be improved. For relevance, each item was evaluated again using a 4-point scale: 1=not relevant, 2=unable to assess relevance, 3=relevant but needs minor alteration and 4=very relevant and succinct. For comprehensiveness, each cluster—Emotion and Eye Gaze, Communication, Gestures, Sounds, Words, Understanding and Object Use—was evaluated for its comprehensiveness. The scale as a whole was also assessed using a dichotomous scale of ‘comprehensive’ or ‘not comprehensive’. This version was referred to as the Expert Panel FAL Version (Expert-FAL).

Participants (face validity)

The Expert-FAL version was given to ten Arabic-speaking parents living in the UAE with children aged 6–24 months. These parents were recruited using convenience and snowball sampling techniques. The sample size was guided by published recommendations for translation and cross-cultural adaptation studies, which indicate that 10–40 participants are generally sufficient to evaluate face validity.36 In addition, the COSMIN manual notes that for qualitative evaluations of clarity and comprehensibility, 7–10 interviews may be sufficient, with saturation considered more important than absolute numbers.41 As our parent participants provided both quantitative ratings and qualitative verbal input on clarity, the inclusion of 10 parents was considered adequate. Efforts were made to select parents from diverse Arabic nationalities. Participants evaluated the instructions, items and response format for clarity using the same Likert scale used by the expert panel and provided suggestions for rewriting any unclear items.

Data collection and processing

The Research Electronic Data Capture (REDCap) tool, hosted at the United Arab Emirates University, was used to collect and manage the demographic data of the expert panel and participants, as well as the content validity assessments.42 REDCap is a secure, web-based application designed to support data capture for research studies. A smart device was provided to participants to obtain consent and complete the survey. The author (GSMA-B) was available for discussion to address any inquiries or provide further clarification. For some participants, the face-to-face method was not possible; therefore, they completed the survey while the author (GSMA-B) explained and clarified any questions over the phone. Data were collected from 9 November 2024 to 28 January 2025.

Data analysis

All data were exported from REDCap to Microsoft Excel for analysis. Scores were dichotomised for clarity and relevance: scores of 3 or 4 were categorised as ‘clear’ and ‘relevant’, while scores of 1 or 2 were categorised as ‘unclear’ and ‘not relevant’. Content Validity Indices (CVIs) were calculated, including the Item-level CVI (I-CVI), the Scale-level CVI using the average method (S-CVI by Average, S-CVI/Ave), and the universal agreement method (S-CVI by Universal Agreement, S-CVI/UA). The expected thresholds for content validity were an I-CVI of 0.78 or higher and S-CVI/Ave and S-CVI/UA of 0.90 and 0.80 or higher, respectively.39 40 43 Items in the instrument were revised and rewritten if more than 20% of participants rated them as unclear, irrelevant or not comprehensive, or if an expert suggested modifying an item and the research team found the recommendation reasonable, with new indices subsequently recalculated.44 Inter-rater agreement was evaluated using the modified kappa statistic (K) to increase the confidence in the content validity of the checklist, as it accounts for chance agreement.45 The K was computed using the following formula:

Patient and public involvement

This study involved both patient and public participation throughout the content validity process of the CSBS DP ITC into Arabic. Ten parents of children aged 6–24 months, from diverse Arabic-speaking backgrounds, helped refine the checklist’s language and content based on families’ real-world experiences. An expert panel of public health specialists and clinicians also reviewed the tool to ensure content validity and cultural relevance. Parents and caregivers were actively engaged and had opportunities to provide ongoing feedback.

Results

Translation

The translation process involved adapting the sounds and words from the original American English version into Arabic. As expected, the selection of sounds, consonants and words did not always correspond directly to those in the original version.

For items 15 (Does your child string sounds together, such as uh oh, mama, gaga, bye bye, bada?), 17 (About how many different words does your child use meaningfully that you recognise, such as baba for bottle; gaggie for doggie?) and 18 (Does your child put two words together, eg, more cookie, bye bye Daddy?), the goal was to ensure the inclusion of the highest-frequency sounds, sound combinations and words commonly used by Arabic-speaking children. These sounds and words were selected based on the MacArthur CDI, Saudi and various Arabic dialect versions.18 47 For example, item 15 in the American English version features five sound strings: ‘uh oh’, ‘mama’, ‘gaga’, ‘bye bye’ and ‘bada’. In the Arabic version, these were replaced with بابا (baba), دادا (dada), تاتا (tata), نانا (nana) and باي (bye). The example ماما (mama) was retained from the original version, and a sixth example was added to provide additional clarity.

In item 17, ‘gaggie’ for ‘doggie’ was replaced with ‘haw haw for dog’ (هو هو للكلب) to reflect the sound commonly used for dogs in Arabic. Additionally, the example ‘baba’ for ‘bottle’ was replaced with the sound associated with ‘car’ in Arabic. In item 18, the example ‘more cookies’ was replaced with ‘more food’, while ‘bye-bye daddy’ was retained, and an additional example, ‘I don’t want’, was included. For item 16 (About how many of the following consonant sounds does your child use: ma, na, ba, da, ga, wa, la, ya, sa, sha?), the selection of consonants was guided by known developmental stages of consonant production in the Arabic language, as described in prior research.48,50

The final translated version was reviewed and approved by translators 1, 2 and 3, along with the research team and was designated as the P-FAL.

Discussion

This paper reported the process of translating the original CSBS DP ITC into MSA and assessing its content validity. The findings resulted in an overall S-CVI/Ave of 0.98, indicating excellent content validity.39

In the literature, some adaptation studies rely on a single methodological framework,51,54 whereas others combine elements from multiple resources to fit their context.55,57 In our study, we integrated three complementary resources to ensure a rigorous translation and content validity process.34,36 However, previously published CSBS DP ITC adaptations did not explicitly report the methodological guidelines used, which limits direct comparison with our work.25 27 29

MSA was chosen for adaptation as it is the official language across Arab countries, used in education, law, religion and official media.58 As the standardised written variety, it is widely understood by literate parents, making it a common choice for self-administered questionnaires with broad applicability.59,62 To strengthen the reliability and generalisability of the adapted version, several measures were taken: (1) dialect-specific expressions were avoided; (2) examples of commonly recognised child sounds and words drawn from Arabic developmental inventories documenting terms recognised across dialects18 47 63 and (iii) expert panel members and parents representing ten nationalities were involved, in line with Khalaila’s recommendation to include individuals from diverse dialect backgrounds.64 Nevertheless, caution is advised when administering the instrument to parents with limited MSA proficiency.

Most methodological guidelines recommend using at least two translators in the translation and content validity processes.65 In our study, we involved five translators and a monolingual Arabic specialist, in addition to the research team. The monolingual specialist ensured that the translation aligned with the linguistic norms and grammatical structures of MSA. According to Erkut, the inclusion of a monolingual specialist is particularly valuable, as they are better able to identify unfamiliar constructions than bilingual translators, who may be influenced by their familiarity with the source language.66

In the translation process, MacArthur CDI Arabic versions were used as a guide to select the most common sounds and words used by very young Arabic-speaking children, such as تاتا (tata), نانا (nana), familiar sounds like a dog bark or car noise, as well as words used to request more food.18 47 63 It is noteworthy that the MacArthur CDI list, created by Fenson et al, had also been employed in the development of the American English version of the CSBS DP.67 In the F-AL, significant adaptations were made to item 15 under the Sounds cluster, as well as items 17 and 18 under the Words cluster. These items also underwent substantial revisions in other previously adapted versions.27 29 Furthermore, item 23, which relates to stacking blocks and rings, was modified to better align with the context of Arabic-speaking children. In many low-income Arabic countries, blocks and rings may not be commonly available, and children often use plastic cups or other household items as substitutes. A similar modification had been made to this item in the Chinese version of the CSBS DP ITC,29 and a concern regarding its cultural relevance was noted in the Polish version.30

In the current study, 10 experts participated, and the I-CVI score for all questions in terms of relevance and clarity ranged from 0.8 to 1. According to Polit and Beck, an I-CVI of 0.78 or higher is recommended for expert panels of 10 or more judges. Reporting these indices is essential in the translation and adaptation process to ensure transparency and the accuracy of content validity assessments.68 Table 2 shows that both the I-CVI and S-CVI met satisfactory levels, indicating that the questionnaire achieved a high level of content validity. In the other published adapted versions, the I-CVI indices were not reported, thus making a direct comparison with our results not possible.2325 27,29

In our study, we report both S-CVI average (S-CVI/Ave) and S-CVI universal (S-CVI/UA) for experts and participants. The S-CVI/Ave for clarity among experts was 0.98, while the S-CVI/UA was 0.88. For participants, the S-CVI/Ave was 0.98, and the S-CVI/UA was 0.8. The S-CVI/Ave is the average of the I-CVI scores for all items on the scale, providing an overall measure of the content validity of the scale, while the S-CVI/UA reflects the proportion of items on the scale that receive a relevance/clarity score of 3 or 4 from all experts.69 Differences between these two indices are common. The S-CVI/UA typically has lower values than the S-CVI/Ave because it is rare for all experts to reach full agreement on every item.68

In the original American English version of the CSBS DP ITC, caregivers complete demographic questions about themselves and their child before proceeding to the checklist. These questions were also translated and adapted in the current Arabic version. However, some experts recommended adding an additional demographic question about the child’s gender in the Arabic version. This suggestion arose because certain Arabic names are unisex and can be used for both males and females. Additionally, experts recommended providing a white paper for parents to list words, helping them answer the questions more accurately.

A notable strength of the current study lies in the thorough documentation and tracking of all changes made during the translation and content validity processes, with detailed records available in the supplementary materials, ensuring transparency and reproducibility. The use of five translators, including a monolingual Arabic language specialist, along with the involvement of the research team, added strength to the translation process, as reflected in the high CVIs obtained. Additionally, the discussions between the author (GSMA-B), the expert panels and the participants further enhanced the content validity process through qualitative input. As part of this process, we calculated both the CVI average (S-CVI/Ave) and the CVI universal (S-CVI/UA) to comprehensively assess the content validity from multiple perspectives.

Nevertheless, this study has certain limitations. One key constraint was the inability to modify the ITC by adding or removing items due to copyright restrictions, as such changes would affect the scoring system. However, we still sought expert input on the relevance of each item to the construct and the overall comprehensiveness of the clusters and the scale. All experts confirmed that the clusters were comprehensive, and the S-CVI/Ave for relevance was 0.98. However, the experts’ awareness of these limitations may have influenced their ratings, potentially leading to higher scores for comprehensiveness and relevance to avoid suggesting changes to the checklist.

Another limitation of this study was that the majority of parents participating in the face validity process were female (n=9 (90%)) and had a high level of education. Similar patterns were reported in the original development of the CSBS DP and in subsequent adaptations cross-cultural adaptations where mothers and highly educated parents predominated.21 26 29 30 Evidence consistently shows that research participation tends to be higher among individuals with greater educational attainment, while in child-focused studies, mothers are more likely to participate than fathers, reflecting their primary caregiving role.70 71 Nevertheless, caution should be exercised regarding the clarity of the Arabic version for parents with lower educational attainment. We recommend that, in the next step of validation—full psychometric testing—greater effort be made to recruit fathers and parents with lower education levels to improve representativeness. Despite these limitations, we successfully recruited parents from diverse Arab regions, including Gulf countries, African Arab countries and the Levant. It is important to note that parents from the Levant were over-represented, comprising 50% of the participants.

One additional limitation was the variation in the method of discussion with parents and participants; some were conducted face-to-face, while others were done over the phone. Despite this, author GSMA-B personally conducted phone interviews with the participants, recording their qualitative comments and feedback.

In conclusion, the CSBS DP ITC is a valuable parental screening tool for assessing communication development in young children. The checklist was effectively translated and content validated into MSA. This step establishes a foundation before proceeding to full psychometric testing of the instrument, paving the way for a reliable and culturally appropriate tool to identify early communication delays that can be used in clinical settings and research across all the Arab countries.
