Restless nights, demanding days: assessment of the prevalence and associated factors of poor sleep quality among medical students in Mogadishu, Somalia
Authors: Nur Adam Mohamed, Rahma Yusuf Haji Mohamud, Mohamed Sheikh Hassan, Hassan Ibrahim Nur, Abdishakur Mohamud Hassan Hidigow, Adan Ali Gabow, Mohamed Yaqub Hassan, Faiza Abdulkadir Farah, Nor Osman Sidow, Hamdi Mohamed Yusuf
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSleep is a fundamental biological process essential for cognitive performance, emotional regulation, and academic functioning. Medical students are particularly vulnerable to poor sleep quality due to demanding coursework, irregular schedules, and heightened psychological stress. Although global studies have documented a high prevalence of sleep disturbances in this group, evidence from low-resource educational settings such as Somalia remains limited. This study aimed to determine the prevalence of poor sleep quality, identify associated factors, and evaluate whether depressive symptoms mediate the relationship between social support and sleep quality among medical students in Mogadishu.MethodsA multicenter cross-sectional study was conducted between July and August 2025 among 272 medical students from four universities in Mogadishu. Data were collected electronically using a structured questionnaire on sociodemographic, academic, behavioral, and psychosocial characteristics, along with validated instruments: the Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI), the Patient Health Questionnaire-9 (PHQ-9), and the Oslo Social Support Scale-3 (OSSS-3). Descriptive statistics summarized participant characteristics, while logistic regression identified independent predictors of poor sleep quality. Spearman’s rank-order correlations and Hayes’ PROCESS macro (Model 4) were used to explore associations and mediation effects. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.ResultsThe prevalence of poor sleep quality among medical students was 41.5% (95% CI: 35.6–47.6). Poor sleep predictors included nighttime screen use (AOR = 2.71, 95% CI: 1.24–5.95), stressful life events (AOR = 2.05, 95% CI: 1.12–3.77), physical inactivity (AOR = 2.70, 95% CI: 1.20–6.10), depressive symptoms (AOR = 2.73, 95% CI: 1.51–4.91), and poor social support (AOR = 2.30, 95% CI: 1.04–5.10). Mediation analysis indicated that depressive symptoms fully mediated the association between social support and sleep quality, implying that lower social support may contribute to poorer sleep primarily through its influence on depressive symptomatology.ConclusionPoor sleep quality affects more than two-fifths of medical students in Mogadishu and is linked to key behavioral and psychosocial factors relevant to student well-being. Institutional strategies promoting healthy digital habits, regular physical activity, and psychosocial support are needed to enhance student well-being and educational success.

Background

Sleep is a fundamental biological process closely linked to cognitive performance, emotional regulation, and overall well-being [1]. Among university students—especially those in demanding fields such as medicine—sleep disturbances have become a significant public health and educational concern [2–5]. The demanding nature of medical training, characterized by sustained cognitive effort, irregular schedules, extended study hours, and academic stress, makes medical students particularly vulnerable to poor sleep quality [3, 4, 6–8]. Inadequate sleep has also been associated with reduced learning capacity, academic underperformance, and long-term mental and physical health problems [5, 9, 10].

Globally, poor sleep quality is highly prevalent among medical students, with a recent meta-analysis from 31 countries reporting a pooled prevalence of 55.6% [3]. Regional data show similar patterns—62.2% in Latin America [11] and 64% in Southeast Asia [12]—and country-level estimates remain high in Ethiopia (62%) [13], India (69.3%) [14], Egypt (71.2%) [15], and Nepal (38.2%) [16].

The determinants of poor sleep quality are multifactorial, including demographic, psychological, and behavioral factors [2, 13, 14, 16]. Common correlates include female sex, depressive and anxiety symptoms, stress, poor sleep hygiene, physical inactivity, low social support, substance use, and stressful life events [2, 13, 14, 16]. Among these, social support has emerged as a key protective factor; meta-analytic evidence shows that stronger social ties are associated with better sleep quality [17]. Recent studies also suggest that depressive symptoms may mediate this relationship, indicating that low social support may impair sleep partly through its influence on mood [18, 19].

Despite growing evidence, most studies on sleep quality among medical students come from high-income countries, and research from low- and middle-income or fragile settings remains scarce [3]. Somali medical students encounter unique academic and psychosocial challenges—including limited educational infrastructure, socio-political instability, and restricted mental health resources [20, 21]—which may heighten susceptibility to poor sleep and emotional distress.

To address these gaps, this study aims to: (1) estimate the prevalence of poor sleep quality among medical students in Mogadishu, (2) identify associated risk factors, and (3) examine whether depressive symptoms mediate the relationship between social support and sleep quality. The findings aim to support contextually appropriate interventions to enhance student well-being in resource-limited medical education settings.

Methods

Study design and setting

This multicenter cross-sectional study was conducted between 1 July and 31 August 2025 to determine the prevalence of poor sleep quality and its associated factors among undergraduate medical students in four universities in Mogadishu, Somalia. Historically, medical and health-professional training in Somalia has been affected by decades of conflict, political instability, and limited resources. Over the past two decades, however, the sector has gradually improved [20]. Mogadishu University, Al Hayat Medical University, Zamzam University of Science and Technology, and SIMAD University offers a structured medical training pathway consisting of a one-year foundation program followed by a six-year MBBS curriculum, culminating in a supervised internship. These programs integrate basic and clinical sciences and are delivered by locally trained specialists and international collaborators, reflecting ongoing efforts to strengthen medical education in the country.

Participating universities were selected through convenience sampling, using existing institutional collaborations to facilitate recruitment and data collection. This approach enhanced feasibility and participation while maintaining data quality. Including multiple institutions improved sample diversity and strengthened generalizability within Mogadishu’s academic context. A proportional allocation method was used to distribute the sample across the four universities: Mogadishu University (n = 99), Al-Hayat Medical University (n = 85), SIMAD University (n = 52), and Zamzam University (n = 43).

Sample size determination

The required sample size was calculated using the single-population proportion formula, n = Z2p(1 − p)/d2 [22], where n denotes the required sample, Z = 1.96 corresponds to a 95% confidence level, p = 0.62 represents the expected prevalence of poor sleep quality from a prior Ethiopian study [13], and 𝑑 = 0.05 is the margin of error. The computation yielded an initial sample of 362 participants. Applying the finite-population correction for a total eligible population of N = 982 produced an adjusted sample of 265. To account for a 10% anticipated non-response rate, the final target was increased to 294 students.

Because a centralized student registry was unavailable, recruitment followed convenience sampling. Class leaders at each university distributed a secure survey link via WhatsApp, enabling efficient participation. Although convenience sampling was dictated by logistical constraints, the carefully determined sample size preserved statistical precision and adequate power to estimate prevalence and identify associated factors.

Study population and recruitment

The study population comprised undergraduate students enrolled in the Bachelor of Medicine and Bachelor of Surgery (MBBS) programs at the participating universities. The MBBS curriculum spans six years and is organized into three preclinical years focused on basic biomedical sciences, followed by two clinical years of hospital rotations and a final one-year internship. This six-year curriculum is preceded by a mandatory one-year foundation course recommended by the Somali Federal Government.

Recruitment followed a structured communication cascade. A designated member of the research team contacted student class leaders through in-person meetings, phone calls, or brief in-class announcements. The study’s objectives, procedures, and ethical safeguards were clearly explained, and class leaders were invited to assist with participant mobilization. They were then provided with detailed instructions and a Google Forms survey link, which they shared through WhatsApp groups. To enhance participation and ensure data completeness, reminder messages were sent periodically throughout the data-collection period.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Eligible participants were all undergraduate MBBS students enrolled at the participating universities in Mogadishu. Inclusion criteria required students to be (i) aged 18 years or older, (ii) actively enrolled during the study period, and (iii) willing to provide informed electronic consent. Exclusion criteria included (i) inability to complete the questionnaire due to acute illness, (ii) invalid submissions such as duplicates or inconsistent responses, and (iii) self-reported psychiatric or medical conditions known to affect sleep and currently under treatment.

Dependent variable

The primary outcome variable was sleep quality, assessed using the Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI). Following standard scoring guidelines, PSQI global scores >5 were classified as poor sleep quality, while scores ≤ 5 indicated good sleep quality [23]. This binary categorization allowed for a clear estimate of prevalence and subsequent risk-factor analysis.

Independent variables

A wide range of covariates was assessed. Sociodemographic factors included age, sex, marital status, monthly household income, employment status, family and personal history of mental illness, and chronic medical conditions. Behavioral and psychosocial variables encompassed bedtime screen use, physical activity, substance use, stressful life events within the preceding three months (e.g., bereavement, accident, assault, breakup, hospitalization), depressive symptoms, and perceived social support. Academic factors included program stage, year of study, academic performance, and institutional affiliation. All variables were examined as potential determinants of poor sleep quality.

Data collection tools

Data were collected electronically via four self-administered questionnaires distributed through WhatsApp class groups using Google Forms. The survey package included: (i) a structured socio-demographic and behavioral questionnaire, (ii) the PSQI, (iii) the Patient Health Questionnaire-9 (PHQ-9), and (iv) the Oslo Social Support Scale-3 (OSSS-3).

Except for age and the initial PSQI items, all variables were recorded categorically with closed-ended response options to ensure consistency and facilitate analysis.

Sleep quality

The PSQI, a validated self-report tool by Buysse et al. [23], assesses seven components—subjective sleep quality, sleep latency, sleep duration, habitual sleep efficiency, sleep disturbances, use of sleep medication, and daytime dysfunction—over the preceding month. Each component is rated 0–3, yielding a global score (0–21), where higher scores denote poorer sleep. The PSQI is commonly applied in psychological and health sciences research and has demonstrated strong measurement performance across diverse populations [13, 24]. In the present sample, the PSQI demonstrated acceptable internal consistency (Cronbach’s α = 0.71).

Depressive symptoms

Depressive symptoms were measured using the PHQ-9, developed by Kroenke et al. [25]. The scale consists of nine DSM-IV–based items rated from 0 (“not at all”) to 3 (“nearly every day”), yielding a total score of 0–27. Standard severity thresholds were applied: minimal (0–4), mild (5–9), moderate (10–14), moderately severe (15–19), and severe (20–27). The PHQ-9 is extensively utilized in health research and exhibits robust psychometric performance across a range of populations [25, 26]. A Somali-language adaptation also demonstrated acceptable internal consistency [27]. In this study, the PHQ-9 demonstrated strong internal consistency (Cronbach’s α = 0.89).

Perceived social support

Perceived social support was assessed using the OSSS-3, developed by Dalgard [28]. This brief measure evaluates the availability and adequacy of social connections and generates a total score ranging from 3 to 14, categorized as low (3–8), moderate (9–11), or high (12–14). The OSSS-3 is frequently used in epidemiological research due to its simplicity and well-established psychometric properties [13, 29]. In the present sample, the OSSS-3 demonstrated acceptable internal consistency (Cronbach’s α = 0.69).

Prior to the main data collection, the electronic questionnaire was pilot tested with a small group of medical students who were not included in the final sample. The PSQI and OSSS-3 were administered in English, which is the primary language of instruction in the participating universities; therefore, translation or back-translation was not required. The pilot assessed clarity of wording, contextual relevance, and the functionality of the Google Form platform. Minor revisions were made based on participant feedback to enhance item comprehension and flow. This process confirmed that the standardized instruments (PSQI and OSSS-3) were appropriate and well understood within the local setting.

Data collection and management

Participants completed the online questionnaires through Google Forms. All fields were mandatory, eliminating partial submissions and ensuring data completeness. At the end of the study, responses were exported to Microsoft Excel, reviewed for accuracy, and cleaned. Invalid or duplicate entries were removed, and categorical variables were recoded numerically to facilitate analysis. These steps ensured a consistent, high-quality dataset.

Statistical analysis

The cleaned dataset was imported into IBM SPSS Statistics v27.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Descriptive statistics summarized socio-demographic, behavioral, academic, and psychosocial variables. Data normality was assessed using both the Shapiro–Wilk and Kolmogorov–Smirnov tests, emphasizing the former due to sample size considerations. The point prevalence of poor sleep quality and its 95% confidence interval were estimated using the Clopper–Pearson exact method [30]. Potential predictors were first examined using bivariate logistic regression. Variables with p ≤ 0.25 were entered into a multivariable logistic regression model to adjust for confounding [31]. Multicollinearity was assessed via variance inflation factors (VIF < 2.5) and correlation matrices, indicating no significant collinearity.

Model fit was evaluated using the Hosmer–Lemeshow test, while discrimination was quantified through receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves [32]. The final models demonstrated acceptable to excellent discrimination (AUC = 0.79). Adjusted odds ratios (AORs) with 95% CIs were reported. Given the non-normality of several psychosocial variables, Spearman’s rank-order correlations were computed to explore associations between sleep quality, depressive symptoms, and social support. Statistical significance was defined as two-tailed p < 0.05. Mediation analysis was performed using Hayes’ PROCESS macro for SPSS (Model 4) with 5,000 bootstrap resamples and bias-corrected 95% CIs [33]. Social support was treated as the independent variable, sleep quality as the dependent variable, and depressive symptoms as the mediator. Indirect effects were deemed significant if the 95% CI excluded zero [33].

Ethical considerations

This study was conducted in accordance with institutional and national ethical standards and the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. Ethical clearance was obtained from the Institutional Review Board of Mogadishu Somali-Turkiye Recep Tayyip Erdogan Training and Research Hospital (Ref. No. MSTH/21982). All participants were informed about the study’s aims, procedures, confidentiality safeguards, and their rights. Electronic informed consent was obtained via Google Forms before accessing the survey. Participation was voluntary, and withdrawal was permitted at any time without penalty. No personally identifiable information was collected. Responses were anonymized at the point of entry, and all data were securely stored with restricted access in accordance with data-protection protocols.

Results

Socio-demographic and behavioral characteristics

A total of 272 medical students participated in the survey, yielding a high response rate of 92.5%. Female students represented just over half of the sample (54.0%). Most respondents (62.1%) were 22 years of age or younger, with a mean age of 22.1 years (SD = 2.2). Approximately 13.2% reported a chronic medical condition, and 5.9% reported a history of mental illness. Regarding health-related behaviors, the majority (83.1%) engaged in regular physical activity, while substance use was uncommon (2.9%). Prevalence and components of poor sleep quality

Overall, 41.5% of students reported poor sleep quality (95% CI: 35.6–47.6), indicating that approximately two in five participants experienced meaningful sleep difficulties. Despite this, nearly half (47.1%) rated their subjective sleep quality as very good. More than two-fifths of respondents reported sleeping more than seven hours per night, suggesting adequate sleep duration for a substantial proportion of the sample. Correlations between psychosocial variables

Spearman’s correlation analyses showed significant associations among sleep quality, depressive symptoms, and social support. Sleep quality scores were positively correlated with depression scores (r = 0.520, p < 0.01), indicating that greater depressive symptom severity was linked to poorer sleep quality. In contrast, sleep quality was negatively correlated with perceived social support (r = − 0.252, p < 0.01), meaning that students with stronger social support tended to report better sleep. Depression scores were also inversely correlated with social support (r = − 0.282, p < 0.01), suggesting that higher levels of social support were associated with fewer depressive symptoms (Table 4).

Risk factors associated with poor sleep quality

In the bivariate logistic regression analyses, several factors—including living conditions, stressful life events, chronic medical conditions, history of mental illness, institutional affiliation, academic performance, physical activity, substance use, nighttime screen exposure, depressive symptoms, and social support—were significantly associated with poor sleep quality. After adjustment for potential confounders in the multivariable model, five variables remained independently associated with poor sleep: stressful life events, physical activity, nighttime screen use, depressive symptoms, and social support.

Students who engaged in nighttime screen use had higher odds of poor sleep quality compared with those who did not (AOR = 2.71, 95% CI: 1.24–5.95). Those who experienced stressful life events also showed increased odds of poor sleep (AOR = 2.05, 95% CI: 1.12–3.77). Students with depressive symptoms were nearly three times more likely to report poor sleep quality (AOR = 2.73, 95% CI: 1.51–4.91). Poor social support remained a significant predictor as well (AOR = 2.30, 95% CI: 1.04–5.10). These results are summarized in Table 5.

Mediating role of depressive symptoms

Mediation analysis was conducted using Hayes’ PROCESS macro (Model 4) to evaluate whether depressive symptoms mediated the association between social support and sleep quality (Fig. 1). The total effect of social support on sleep quality was significant and negative (B = − 0.284, SE = 0.076, p < 0.001, 95% CI [–0.433, − 0.135]), indicating that higher levels of social support were associated with better sleep quality. Discussion

This multicenter study investigated the prevalence and determinants of poor sleep quality among medical students across four universities in Mogadishu, Somalia. Of the 272 participants, 41.5% were classified as having poor sleep quality according to a validated instrument (PSQI). Multivariable analyses identified nighttime screen use, recent stressful life events, physical inactivity, depressive symptoms, and low perceived social support as independent predictors. Mediation analysis further revealed that depressive symptoms fully mediated the relationship between social support and sleep quality, highlighting a key psychosocial pathway. Collectively, these findings underscore the urgent need for medical education institutions to implement structured wellness programs that promote healthy sleep habits, enhance learning outcomes, and safeguard students’ academic and psychological functioning.

Sleep problems are increasingly recognized as a major concern among medical students due to their direct impact on learning efficiency, cognitive performance, and mental health [5, 10]. The demanding nature of medical training—characterized by irregular schedules, long study hours, and persistent academic pressure—creates an environment that predisposes students to sleep difficulties [2, 8]. In this study, over two-fifths of students reported poor sleep quality, a prevalence consistent with findings from Nepal [16], India [34], and Sri Lanka [35]. The observed rate was lower than those reported in Ethiopia [13, 36], Croatia [2], and the global pooled prevalence reported by Binjabr et al. [3], yet higher than estimates from India [37], Nepal [38], China [39], and Pakistan [40]. Such variations across studies employing similar tools (e.g., PSQI) likely reflect contextual differences rather than methodological inconsistencies. Medical education systems differ widely in curricular intensity, examination frequency, and clinical workload—all of which influence sleep behavior. Moreover, sociocultural factors such as family expectations, economic pressures, and attitudes toward rest further modulate sleep quality.

In Somalia, persistent socioeconomic instability, limited access to psychosocial support, and underdeveloped institutional welfare frameworks may amplify stress and sleep disturbances among medical students. Conversely, in high-resource settings where student wellness programs, counseling services, and mentorship systems are established, sleep quality tends to improve. These disparities illustrate that the burden of poor sleep among medical students is contextually determined, shaped not only by academic demands but also by the surrounding psychosocial and institutional environment. Addressing these structural determinants within Somali medical schools could therefore yield meaningful improvements in both mental health and academic performance.

Regular physical exercise is well-established as a protective factor for healthy sleep, benefiting both clinical and non-clinical populations [41, 42]. In this study, students who reported physical inactivity were significantly more likely to experience poor sleep quality than their active peers. This finding aligns with prior research showing that regular, moderate-intensity exercise reduces sleep latency, extends total sleep duration, and enhances overall sleep efficiency [42, 43] Physiologically, exercise alleviates stress through endorphin release, stabilizes circadian rhythms, lowers evening cortisol levels, promotes autonomic balance, enhances serotonin and norepinephrine availability, and facilitates thermoregulatory cooling—collectively supporting the initiation, maintenance, and overall quality of restorative sleep [42, 44]. Within the context of medical education, encouraging structured physical activity—such as scheduled recreation hours, sports programs, or fitness initiatives—offers a low-cost, non-pharmacological strategy to improve sleep health, strengthen psychological resilience, and ultimately support academic performance.

The relationship between depression and sleep disturbance is bidirectional, with each capable of exacerbating the other [45]. Although improved sleep typically enhances psychological well-being [46], paradoxical evidence suggests that short-term sleep deprivation may temporarily alleviate depressive symptoms [47], underscoring the complex neurobiological mechanisms linking mood regulation and sleep. In this study, medical students exhibiting depressive symptoms were nearly three times more likely to report poor sleep quality than their peers without such symptoms, consistent with findings from Nepal [16], India [34], and Croatia [2]. Depression disrupts sleep through serotonergic dysregulation, circadian misalignment, and increased psychophysiological arousal [48, 49]. It also alters sleep architecture by reducing slow-wave sleep and increasing rapid eye movement (REM) pressure, leading to fragmented and non-restorative rest [48]. These findings highlight the importance of integrating comprehensive mental health support within medical education—through early screening, counseling access, and stress-reduction initiatives—to safeguard students’ psychological well-being and academic performance.

Social support plays a critical role in maintaining psychological stability and healthy sleep, with strong evidence linking robust interpersonal relationships to better sleep outcomes across populations [17, 50]. In this study, medical students with low perceived social support were significantly more likely to experience poor sleep quality, consistent with findings from Ethiopia [13]. Supportive social networks buffer stress, enhance emotional regulation, and modulate the hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal (HPA) axis—dampening cortisol release and reducing physiological hyperarousal—to promote more stable and restorative sleep [51–53]. Within medical education, fostering a supportive academic environment—through peer mentorship, collaborative learning, and accessible counseling—can help mitigate stress-related sleep disruption. Embedding structured peer-support programs and faculty mentorship initiatives into medical curricula may therefore serve as effective, low-cost interventions to strengthen student well-being, resilience, and academic performance.

Stressful life events are well-established contributors to poor sleep quality, disrupting both sleep initiation and maintenance [54, 55]. In this study, medical students who reported recent stressful experiences were twice as likely to experience poor sleep compared to those without such events, consistent with findings from both student and occupational populations [54, 56].

Physiologically, stress activates the hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal (HPA) axis and sympathetic nervous system, leading to elevated cortisol levels, heightened arousal, and circadian disruption [54, 55]. Cognitively, it amplifies worry and rumination, perpetuating pre-sleep hyperarousal and delayed sleep onset [55]. Within the demanding environment of medical education, cumulative stress from academic workload, examinations, and personal challenges may further increase vulnerability to sleep disturbances. Integrating stress-management training, resilience workshops, and mindfulness-based interventions into medical curricula could therefore play a crucial role in promoting healthier sleep patterns, enhancing emotional regulation, and improving academic performance among medical students.

Excessive screen exposure has emerged as an important behavioral determinant of poor sleep, particularly among young adults and university students who heavily depend on digital devices for study and communication [57, 58]. In this study, nighttime screen users exhibited significantly higher odds of poor sleep, consistent with previous research among medical students [59]. Mechanistically, screen use delays sleep onset through time displacement, increases cognitive and physiological arousal, and suppresses melatonin secretion via blue-light exposure, thereby disrupting circadian rhythms [60]. Given that digital technology is integral to modern medical education, promoting digital hygiene is crucial. Integrating sleep-health education, screen-time awareness campaigns, and “tech-free” study intervals into medical curricula could meaningfully enhance sleep quality, attention, and learning outcomes among students.

In addition to behavioral influences, our findings reveal a meaningful psychosocial mechanism that helps explain sleep difficulties among medical students. The mediation analysis provides insight into the pathways linking social support and sleep quality, demonstrating that depressive symptoms fully mediated this association. Consistent with our results, Cai et al. [18] found that depressive symptoms mediated the relationship between social support and sleep quality among psychiatric outpatients, highlighting a similar emotional pathway. Likewise, Liu et al. [19] reported that both anxiety and depression mediated this association among individuals with schizophrenia, reinforcing the role of mood dysregulation as an intermediary mechanism. Compared with these clinical populations, our findings show that depressive symptoms alone mediated the social support–sleep link in a non-clinical academic setting. This suggests that even among relatively healthy young adults, low social support may impair sleep primarily through its effects on mood. These parallels strengthen the plausibility of the psychosocial pathway identified in our study, while the differences underscore population-specific expressions of these mechanisms. Understanding this pathway may inform targeted approaches—such as peer-support programs, mentorship models, and counseling services—to enhance emotional well-being and indirectly improve sleep quality within medical education environments.

Strengths and limitations

This study has several methodological strengths. The multicenter design spanning multiple universities increased representativeness and captured heterogeneity across academic and socioeconomic environments in Mogadishu. The use of validated instruments—the PSQI, PHQ-9, and OSSS-3—ensured robust measurement and comparability with international research. Additionally, the analytical strategy, incorporating both multivariable logistic regression and mediation analysis, allowed for a nuanced understanding of independent and indirect effects. Diagnostic checks, including Hosmer–Lemeshow and ROC analyses, confirmed model adequacy and reliability.

Nonetheless, some limitations should be acknowledged. The cross-sectional design precludes causal inference between sleep quality and its predictors. Self-reported data may introduce recall or social-desirability bias, though anonymity and standardized measures helped mitigate these effects. Convenience sampling may limit generalizability; however, inclusion of multiple institutions and a statistically adequate sample size strengthened external validity. Furthermore, online data collection could have excluded participants with limited internet access, though broad dissemination and reminder messaging were used to minimize this risk. Despite these limitations, this study contributes valuable preliminary evidence on sleep health among medical students in Somalia and highlights important behavioral and psychosocial factors that warrant further investigation. The findings may help inform the development of context-appropriate strategies to support student well-being within resource-limited educational settings.

Practical and policy implications

The findings of this study provide useful preliminary insights for medical education and student support systems within Somalia and similar low-resource settings. The high prevalence of poor sleep, together with its association with behavioral and psychosocial factors, suggests that student well-being warrants greater attention within medical training environments. Educational institutions may consider integrating basic sleep-health awareness, digital hygiene guidance, and stress-management resources into existing student support activities to help address modifiable contributors to poor sleep. Student affairs offices could further strengthen these efforts by facilitating peer-support opportunities and ensuring that counseling services are accessible to students who may be experiencing emotional distress.

At a broader level, collaboration between academic institutions and relevant health or education authorities could help inform the development of context-appropriate student wellness initiatives. These may include promoting help-seeking pathways, enhancing mentorship structures, and encouraging balanced academic demands. While this study cannot determine causality, its findings highlight several areas where targeted support may be beneficial. Strengthening such systems has the potential to contribute to a more supportive learning environment and, over time, may foster greater well-being among medical students in Somalia.

Conclusion and recommendations

This multicenter study found that poor sleep quality affects more than two-fifths of medical students in Mogadishu. Nighttime screen use, stressful life events, physical inactivity, depressive symptoms, and low social support emerged as significant predictors, indicating that sleep disturbances among medical students arise from a combination of behavioral and psychosocial factors. These findings highlight the need for increased attention to students’ well-being within medical training environments in resource-limited settings.

Educational institutions may consider introducing initiatives that address the modifiable factors identified in this study. Strategies such as promoting physical activity, encouraging healthier digital habits, and strengthening psychosocial support systems could contribute to improved student well-being. Integrating basic sleep-hygiene awareness into existing wellness activities may also be beneficial.

At the administrative level, universities may explore approaches to reduce stress-related triggers—such as providing access to counseling services or facilitating peer-support programs—while remaining aligned with institutional capacity and context.

Future research should incorporate qualitative methods to better understand students’ lived experiences and contextual factors surrounding sleep disturbances. Longitudinal and interventional studies are also warranted to clarify causal pathways and to evaluate targeted strategies aimed at improving sleep health among medical students in Somalia.
