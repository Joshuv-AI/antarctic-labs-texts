Characterization of posttranslational modification in metabolic dysfunction-associated steatohepatitis: acetylation, lactylation, and phosphorylation
Authors: Kuan Li, Yundong Li, Yuqing Zhang, Shuxian Song, Chengzhou Pa, Chen Du, Lianjun Yang, Guoyou Xue, Hongqiang Gao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIn metabolic dysfunction-associated steatohepatitis (MASH), abnormalities in posttranslational modification (PTM) are both a consequence of the pathological process and a driving force for disease progression. Therefore, this study intends to characterize the PTM features (acetylation, lactylation, and phosphorylation) of MASH, which will provide a crucial theoretical basis for the diagnosis and development of new targets for MASH. In this study, protein/modification profiles of clinical liver tissues from Normal and MASH were analyzed by LC-MS to identify differentially expressed proteins (DEPs) and differentially modified proteins with acetylation, lactylation, and phosphorylation. This study found that acetylation and lactylation occurred mainly in K, whose upstream and downstream amino acids consisted of A, G, K, R, and V. Phosphorylation occurred mainly in S and T, whose upstream and downstream ones consisted of D, E, P, R, and S. Proteomics with ordinary, acetylation, lactylation, and phosphorylation identified 468, 433, 434, and 1,471 DEPs/differentially modified proteins, respectively, which mainly regulate cytoskeleton, immunity, proliferation, oxidative stress, inflammatory cascades, and various metabolisms (sugars, amino acids, lipids, and carbon). CMKLR1, CYP2E1, GLRX, and XRCC1 were identified as key regulators in the shared DEPs of ordinary proteomics and modification proteomics. Notably, the differences in expression of the four proteins in clinical liver tissues from Normal and MASH were consistent with the proteomics results. In conclusion, this study systematically revealed the protein expression profiles associated with acetylation, lactylation, and phosphorylation in MASH, and highlighted that modifications of CMKLR1, CYP2E1, GLRX, and XRCC1 represent potential therapeutic targets for MASH.Supplementary key words: metabolic dysfunction-associated steatohepatitis, proteomics, acetylation, lactylation, phosphorylation

Metabolic dysfunction-associated steatohepatitis (MASH) is a progressive form of metabolic dysfunction-ssociated steatotic liver disease (MASLD) and one of the main causes of end-stage liver disease (liver fibrosis, cirrhosis, and even hepatocellular carcinoma) (1, 2). The pathogenesis of MASH involves a multifactorial interaction of insulin resistance, lipid metabolism disorders, oxidative stress, mitochondrial dysfunction, and chronic inflammation (1, 2, 3). Globally, MASH has seen explosive growth along with the prevalence of obesity and diabetes, with rates of 34% and 66.4% respectively (4). Despite its rising burden, there are currently no broadly effective pharmacotherapies that directly address the underlying molecular mechanisms of MASH (5, 6). Therefore, in-depth characterization of the molecular regulatory mechanisms of MASH is essential for the development of early diagnostic markers and precise therapeutic targets.

Posttranslational modifications (PTMs) dynamically regulate the activity, subcellular localization, conformational stability, and molecular interaction of proteins through reversible chemical modifications including, but not limited to, acetylation, phosphorylation, and lactylation (7, 8, 9). These modulations not only endow proteins with multilevel functional diversity, but also regulate a variety of physiological or pathological processes such as cellular metabolism, inflammatory signaling, and cellular stress responses. Recent studies have demonstrated extensive PTM dysregulation in the MASH process. For instance, acetylation is involved in the MASH process by regulating pyroptosis, mitochondrial function, ferroptosis, inflammatory response, and oxidative stress, implemented by the BRD4/VDAC/NLRP3/GSDMD (10), MRG15/TUFM (11), and tRF-31R9J/HDAC1 (12) pathways. As a novel metabolism modification, lactylation directly associates glycolytic fluxes with epigenetic regulation of inflammatory genes, involving the MPC1/FASN (13), tRF-31R9J/HDAC1 (12), and LDHA/METTL3/YTHDF1/SCD1 (14) pathways. Phosphorylation regulates insulin signaling, cell proliferation, and fibrosis in MASH, including the MAPK (15), PI3K/AKT/mTOR (16) and NF-κB (17) pathways. Nevertheless, current research mainly focuses on a single PTM type, lacking a systematic resolution of the global landscape of PTMs in MASH. From a biological perspective, acetylation and phosphorylation are the most extensively characterized PTMs in MASH. In contrast, lactylation is a recently identified modification that directly couples glycolytic flux to epigenetic regulation. Given that MASH is characterized by both hyperglycolysis and chronic inflammation, lactylation may represent an underexplored but potentially critical axis. Thus, profiling them together allows us to capture complementary layers of PTM dysregulation in MASH.

Based on this, this study intends to systematically characterize the PTM profiles of liver tissues from MASH patients by integrating ordinary proteomics and modification proteomics (acetylation, lactylation, and phosphorylation). The aim is to screen the key proteins and modification sites involved in the core pathological processes of MASH and to reveal the molecular mechanisms of PTM dysregulation in lipotoxicity, oxidative stress, inflammatory cascade, and fibrosis, which will provide a new perspective for elucidating the PTM-driven mechanisms of MASH.

Materials and methods

Clinical sample

Clinical samples of liver tissue were obtained from donor livers donated by brain-dead patients. Wedge-shaped liver tissue was obtained during back-table procedure and was divided into two groups based on pathological assessment: Normal and MASH (n = 5). According to the 2023 Chinese guideline for lipid management (18), patients with TC, TG, and LDL-C levels exceeding 5.2 mmol/L, 1.7 mmol/L, and 3.4 mmol/L, respectively, are diagnosed with hyperlipidemia. The patient information is shown in Table 1. The pathology of liver tissue in the MASH group was characterized by macrovesicular steatosis greater than 30%. The pathology of the liver tissue in the Normal group was characterized by steatosis of less than 5% and the absence of other pathological changes such as fibrosis, inflammation, and necrosis. Inclusion criteria: donor livers derived exclusively from brain-dead donors; samples were obtained solely during the back-table procedure. Exclusion criteria: lack of essential clinicopathological data; history of chronic alcoholism; long-term use of known hepatotoxic drugs; concomitant other liver diseases (including viral hepatitis, hepatic malignancy, drug-induced liver injury, autoimmune liver disease, liver cirrhosis, and active infectious lesions); severe metabolic disorders; or specimen quality issues. The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Affiliated Calmette Hospital of Kunming Medical University (YLS2024-81) and adhered to the Declaration of Helsinki.

Sample preparation

Protein extraction

Each sample was analyzed individually, rather than being mixed before digestion. Liquid nitrogen was added to the liver tissue samples (150 mg) from the Normal and MASH groups for grinding. Four times the volume of lysis buffer was added to 5 ml of tissue powder, containing 1% protease inhibitor cocktail (Millipore) and Triton X-100 (Sigma-Aldrich), followed by shaking in ultrasonic processor (Scientz, China) for 3 min. For samples from modification proteomics, lysis buffer was spiked with inhibitors including 3 μm trichostatin A (HY-15144; MCE) and 50 mM nicotinamide (383619; Sigma-Aldrich) for acetylation and lactylation and 1% of the phosphorylase inhibitor (P0044; Millipore) for phosphorylation. After centrifugation at 12,000g for 10 min, the supernatant was collected for protein concentration assay using BCA kit (Beyotime, China).

Trypsin digestion

Protein precipitates after trichloroacetic acid (TCA) (T6399; Sigma-Aldrich) washing were redissolved in 200 mm tetraethylammonium bromide (140023; Sigma-Aldrich) and subjected to first digestion with 1/50 (m/m) trypsin (V5117; Promega) overnight at 37°C. This initial digestion step was necessary because the precipitated proteins remained largely insoluble in the digestion buffer. A brief proteolysis helped solubilize the pellet and disperse the peptides into solution, thereby allowing efficient subsequent reduction and alkylation. After the first digestion, the peptide mixture was reduced with 5 mm DL-dithiothreitol (43819; Sigma-Aldrich) at 37°C for 60 min, followed by alkylation with 11 mm iodoacetamide (I6125; Sigma-Aldrich) in the dark at room temperature for 45 min. To ensure complete cleavage of sites that were originally shielded by disulfide bonds and thus missed during the first digestion, a second digestion with 1/100 (m/m) trypsin was performed overnight. The final peptide solution was desalted using a Strata X SPE column (KS0-9529; Phenomenex).

Affinity enrichment (only modification proteomics)

For samples of acetylation and lactylation proteomics, trypsin-digested peptide samples were mixed with antibody beads (PTM1204/PTM1404; PTB BIO, China), followed by the addition of a NETN buffer (pH 8.0) containing 50 mm Tris-HCl, 100 mm NaCl, 0.5% NP-40, and 1 mm EDTA. The following day, the enriched acetylation/lactylation peptides were eluted from the antibody beads using 1% (m/v) TCA. For samples of phosphorylation proteomics, trypsin-digested peptide samples were mixed with immobilized metal ion affinity chromatography (IMAC) microspheres (PTB BIO), followed by the addition of sample loading buffer containing 50% acetonitrile and 0.5% acetic acid. Peptides bound to IMAC microspheres were washed sequentially using 50% acetonitrile/0.5% acetic acid and 30% acetonitrile/0.1% TCA to remove nonspecifically adsorbed peptides. The enriched phosphorylation peptides were eluted from IMAC microspheres using elution buffer containing 10% NH4OH. After vacuum drying, the affinity-enriched peptides were desalted using C18 ZipTips (Z720038; Sigma-Aldrich).

LC-MS/MS analysis

Mass spectrometer

Tryptic or affinity-enriched peptides were initially dissolved in solvent A and subsequently introduced onto an in-house packed reversed-phase analytical column with an internal diameter of 100 μm and a length of 25 cm. The chromatographic separation was performed using a binary solvent system, comprising solvent A (2% acetonitrile with 0.1% formic acid in water) and solvent B (0.1% formic acid prepared in acetonitrile). Peptide elution was achieved under a gradient program as follows: from 9% to 24% B over 0–32 min, ramping to 35% B between 32–36 min, increasing further to 80% B during 36–38 min, and maintained at 80% B until 40 min, at a consistent flow rate of 600 nl/min using a NanoElute Ultra-High Performance Liquid Chromatography instrument (Bruker). The eluted peptides were ionized via a nano-electrospray interface operated at 1.6 kV and analyzed on a timsTOF HT mass spectrometer (Bruker). The timsTOF Pro (Bruker) was operated in the DIA-PASEF mode. The instrument parameters included a full MS scan range set between m/z 300 and 1500, and 21 dia-PASEF MS/MS events collected per analytical cycle. MS/MS spectra were recorded within the m/z window of 380–1170, employing an isolation width of 10 m/z.

Database search

Construction of the spectral library was performed by processing the data-dependent acquisition results using Spectronaut software (v.18; Biognosys), integrated with the Pulsar search engine. Tandem mass spectra were matched against the Homo_sapiens_9606_SP_20231220.fasta database, which comprises 20,429 protein entries and was concatenated with a reversed decoy database to facilitate false discovery rate (FDR) estimation. The enzymatic digestion parameters allowed up to two missed cleavages. Carbamidomethyl on Cys was defined as a static modification, while N-terminal acetylation and methionine oxidation were considered dynamic modifications. The FDR thresholds for protein, peptide, and peptide-spectrum matches were stringently controlled below 1%. Subsequently, the generated spectral library was imported into Spectronaut software, where retention times were predicted through nonlinear alignment algorithms, and the library was utilized for searching the data-independent acquisition datasets. All proteins, including those matched to only a single peptide, were retained in the final identification table, and the workflow did not exclude proteins with unique peptides = 0. However, for differential abundance analysis, only proteins quantified with one or more proteome-specific unique peptides were considered quantifiable; single-peptide matches were excluded from subsequent statistical comparisons to ensure quantitative robustness. For missing data handling, peptide-level intensities ≤1 were converted to NaN, and peptides with no detectable signal across all samples were removed. The same threshold was applied at the protein level, and proteins lacking valid quantitative values in all samples were excluded. After missing-value filtering, relative quantification was achieved by horizontal mean normalization, where each sample intensity was divided by the global mean intensity across all samples. Of note, “identified” and “quantifiable in all samples” are distinct concepts: an entry flagged as identified required the PG.Identified and EG.Identified flags to be true in at least one sample, but did not mandate detection across every sample. Quantifiable proteins required both ≥1 unique peptide and at least one sample with intensity>1.

Bioinformatics methods

Based on the EggNOG (19), Pfam (20), and KEGG Mapper (21) databases, the data were annotated for GO, Domain, KEGG, and subcellular localization using the EggNOG-mapper (22), PfamScan (23), DIAMOND (https://github.com/bbuchfink/diamond/releases), and WolF Psort (24) software. The formula: FCA/B,k = Mean(Rik, i ∈ A)/Mean(Rik, i ∈ B) was used to calculate the fold change (FC) of the MASH compared to the Normal group. The statistical significance of the difference between the MASH and Normal groups was calculated using the formula: Pk = T.test(Log2(Rik, i ∈ A), Log2(Rik, i ∈ B)). The Benjamini-Hochberg Method was used to correct the statistical results. Proteins meeting a FDR <0.05 and FC <1/1.5 or >1.5 were defined as DEPs/DMPs. The clusterProfiler package (v.4.0) (25) was used for enrichment analysis (KEGG and GO), followed by visualization using the ggplot2 package (v.3.3.5) (26). String database (27), Cytoscape software (28), and the MCODE algorithm (29) were used for the construction, analysis, and visualization of DEP/DMP-related protein-protein interaction (PPI) networks.

Immunohistochemical (IHC) staining

Liver tissue sections from the Normal and MASH groups were sequentially deparaffinized, hydrated, microwave thermal repair, endogenous peroxidase inactivated and sealed using xylene, anhydrous ethanol, 1×Tris-EDTA solution (pH 9.0), 3% hydrogen peroxide, and 5% BSA. Liver tissue sections were added with rabbit pAb anti-Glutaredoxin 1 (GLRX; 1:150; ab45953; Abcam), mouse mAb anti-Cytochrome P450 2E1 (CYP2E1; 1:100; sc-390193; Santa), rabbit pAb anti-Chemerin chemokine-like receptor 1 (CMKLR1; 1:200; PA5-50932; Invitrogen), rabbit pAb anti-X-Ray repair cross complementing 1 (XRCC1; 1:100; PA5-80237; Invitrogen), and Mouse/Rabbit Enhanced Polymer Detection System (PV-9000; ZSGB-BIO, China) for immunoreactivity. Liver tissue sections were color developed using a DAB substrate kit (ab64238; Abcam) for 5 min, followed by restaining with hematoxylin (E607317-0500; Sangon, China) for 5 min. Liver tissue sections were subjected to image collection and optical density analysis using Win Digital Section Scanning System (WINMEDIC, China) and Image-Pro Plus software.

Statistical analysis

Data analysis and visualization were performed using GraphPad Prism software. The normality and lognormality of IHC staining data were determined using the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test. Data conformed to a normal distribution, expressed as mean ± SD, and were analyzed for differences using student t test. P < 0.05 represents a statistical difference.

Results

Quality control and motif analysis of proteomics with ordinary, acetylation, lactylation, and phosphorylation

Clinical information comparison showed that BMI, waistline, TC, TG, LDL-C, HbA1c, and blood fat in the MASH cohort were significantly higher than those in the Normal cohort, and there were no differences in age, gender, complications (diabetes mellitus and hypertension), and time interval from brain death confirmation to tissue procurement (Table 2). A total of 70,920, 12,118, 8,181, and 27,852 peptides were identified in Proteomics with ordinary, acetylation, lactylation, and phosphorylation, respectively, and they were matched to 7,054, 2,892, 1,347, and 3,410 proteins (Table 3). Notably, a total of 8,903, 3,993, and 15,016 modified peptides and 8,870, 4,044, and 13,362 comparable sites were identified in proteomics with acetylation, lactylation, and phosphorylation, respectively. Quality control revealed that the peptide lengths in proteomics with ordinary, acetylation, and lactylation were mainly distributed in 7–20 amino acid residues, which was consistent with the peptide length distribution obtained by enzymolysis (Fig. 1A–C). Interestingly, peptides with a length greater than 26 amino acid residues predominate in phosphorylation proteomics (Fig. 1D). Distribution histograms and density distribution graphs revealed that the intensity of protein/modification sites in each proteomics was mainly distributed in the range of 0.8–3.2 (Fig. 1A–D). Motif analysis demonstrated that acetylation and lactylation mainly occurred in K and the upstream and downstream amino acids mainly consisted of A, G, K, R, and V. Phosphorylation mainly occurred in S and T and the upstream and downstream amino acids mainly consisted of D, E, P, R, and S (Fig. 2A–C).

Identification of DEPs and DMPs

Across proteomics, principal component analysis and Euclidean distance analyses revealed that the protein expression profiles of samples from the Normal and MASH groups exhibited a clear separation, especially for phosphorylation proteomics (Fig. 3A–D). Moreover, the samples within the group exhibited pronounced clustering, especially proteomics with ordinary and phosphorylation. In ordinary proteomics, there were a total of 330 DEPs in the MASH group compared to the Normal group, including 137 upregulated and 193 downregulated (Fig. 3A and supplemental File 1). TOP30 DEPs with log10FDR as shown in the radar diagram, mainly including PPOX, ACAD8, RAB3IP, XRCC1, and SLC27A3 (Fig. 3A). In acetylation proteomics, a total of 122 DMPs were identified in the MASH group compared to the Normal group, including 86 up-regulated and 36 down-regulated (Fig. 3B and supplemental File 2). Among them, DCPS_K142, ALDH6A1_K52, GPD1_K272, RALY_K165, and MTHFD1_K553 were TOP5 DMPs with log10FDR (Fig. 3B). In lactylation proteomics, 200 upregulated and 65 downregulated DMPs were identified in the MASH group compared to the Normal group, totaling 265 (Fig. 3C and supplemental File 3). Among them, FECH_K138, TALDO1_K321, ALDH1B1_K511, RDX_K344, and LDHA_K224 were the most significant DMPs (Fig. 3C). In phosphorylation proteomics, 1,155 DMPs were present in the MASH group, which had 624 upregulated and 531 downregulated (Fig. 3D and supplemental File 4). The most significant DMPs include YTHDC1_S308, ZKSCAN_S13, ATP2B1_S1137, NMD3_T470, and TMEM87B_Y460 (Fig. 3D). These results suggest that these DEPs and DMPs may be related to the MASH process.

Enrichment analysis of DEPs and DMPs

DEPs in ordinary proteomics were mainly localized extracellularly and regulated immune response, wound healing, cell recognition, inflammatory response, oxidative stress, and multiple metabolic pathways, including carbohydrate, lipid, and nucleotide (Fig. 4A and supplemental File 5). Moreover, they modulated metabolism including carbohydrate, lipid, nucleotide, glutathione, drugs, and amino acids. DMPs in acetylation proteomics were primarily localized to cytoplasm, regulating fatty acid beta-oxidation, liver development, lipid modification, urea cycle, protein folding in endoplasmic reticulum, and cellular respiration, and were associated with a variety of metabolisms (glycolysis, amino acids, fatty acids, lipids, and carbon) and PPAR pathway (Fig. 4B and supplemental File 6). DMPs in lactylation proteomics were mainly localized in the cytoplasm, regulating oxidative stress, urea cycle, oxidative phosphorylation, regulation of translation, fatty acid oxidation, and glycolytic process, and were implicated in a wide variety of metabolisms (carbohydrate, fatty acid, amino acids, energy, nucleotides, and alcohols) (Fig. 4C and supplemental File 7). DMPs in phosphorylation proteomics were mainly localized in the nucleus, regulating mRNA processing, protein import, nuclear export, response to insulin, and protein depolymerization, and were implicated in a variety of metabolisms (carbohydrate, nucleotides, lipids, energy, and amino acids) and pathways (AMPK, NOD-like receptor, ErbB, HIF-1, and B cell receptor) (Fig. 4D and supplemental File 8).

CMKLR1, CYP2E1, GLRX, and XRCC1 are differentially expressed in MASH tissues

Further, we selected four shared DEPs (CMKLR1, CYP2E1, GLRX, and XRCC1) and validated their expression in previous proteomics samples using IHC staining. In ordinary proteomics, the levels of CYP2E1 and CMKLR1 were markedly higher in the MASH samples than in the Normal samples, whereas GLRX and XRCC1 exhibited the opposite expression pattern (Fig. 8A). In acetylation proteomics, the acetylation levels of the K169 site of XRCC1 and the K14 site of GLRX were remarkably higher in the MASH samples than in the Normal samples (Fig. 8A). In lactylation proteomics, the acetylation levels of K140 and K187 sites of CYP2E1 in MASH samples were considerably lower than that of Normal samples, whereas the lactylation level of K169 site of XRCC1 was markedly higher than that of Normal samples (Fig. 8A). In phosphorylation proteomics, the phosphorylation level of the S447 site of XRCC1 was considerably higher in the MASH samples than in the Normal samples, whereas the phosphorylation of the S345 site of CMKLR1 showed the opposite pattern (Fig. 8A). IHC staining revealed a marked increase in the levels of CYP2E1 and CMKLR1 and a marked decrease in the levels of GLRX and XRCC1 in the MASH group compared to the Normal group (Fig. 8B). This result is consistent with ordinary proteomics.

Discussion

MASH is a key link in the progression of MASLD to end-stage liver disease, and its pathogenesis involves a complex interaction of lipotoxicity, oxidative stress, inflammatory cascade, and fibrosis (1, 2, 3). This study is the first to systematically map the global dysregulation of PTM in hepatic tissues of MASH patients by integrating ordinary proteomics and multimodification proteomics (acetylation, lactylation, and phosphorylation). We not only identified the core regulatory network of DEPs versus DMPs, but also emphasized CMKLR1, CYP2E1, GLRX and XRCC1 as hub proteins across modification types. However, it should be emphasized that the cross-sectional design and modest sample size preclude any definitive conclusion about whether these PTM changes drive MASH progression, are consequences of the disease, or reflect underlying environmental or behavioral exposures. The associations reported here should be interpreted as hypothesis-generating rather than causal.

In this study, the MASH cohort had significantly higher BMI, waistline, TC, TG, LDL-C, HbA1c, and hyperlipidemia prevalence than the Normal cohort. These metabolic parameters are well-established drivers of MASH pathogenesis, and their elevation in the MASH group aligns with the expected phenotype of steatohepatitis. Importantly, the absence of differences in age and gender between the two groups reduces potential confounding, supporting that the observed PTM alterations are attributable to disease status rather than demographic disparities. Furthermore, the comparable time interval from brain death confirmation to tissue acquisition ensures that postmortem degradation or agonal stress did not differentially affect protein or modification profiles. The slightly higher rates of hypertension and diabetes in the MASH group did not reach statistical significance, likely due to the modest sample size. Nonetheless, these clinical discrepancies mirror real-world MASH patients with comorbid metabolic conditions. Collectively, the clinical characteristics of our two cohorts are both distinct in metabolically relevant features and sufficiently matched in key confounders, affirming their suitability for comparative PTM profiling.

Acetylation and lactylation occur mainly in K, with upstream and downstream preferences for hydrophobic/basic amino acids (A, G, K, R, and V), suggesting that HDACs and LDHA may be involved in regulation. This echoes the reported mechanisms of histone lactylation mediated by the tRF-31R9J/HDAC1 pathway (12) and LDHA (14). Phosphorylation occurs predominantly at S and T, and upstream and downstream are enriched in acidic/polar residues (D, E, P, R, and S), consistent with the substrate profile of kinases (MAPK and PI3K) (15, 16). Notably, 468, 433, 434 and 1,471 DEPs/DMPs were identified in the ordinary, acetylated, lactated, and phosphorylated proteomes, respectively. Acetylation modifications mainly target mitochondrial proteins and regulate oxidative stress, fatty acid metabolism, and PPAR and mTOR pathways, suggesting that acetylation modifications in MASH are mainly directly related to mitochondrial dysfunction and lipid accumulation. Lactylation modifications are localized in the cytoplasm, linking glycolytic flux to inflammation through pathways such as NF-κB and RIG-I, suggesting that lactylation modifications in MASH focus on the metabolism-inflammation axis. Phosphorylation modifications are concentrated in the nucleus and regulate mRNA processing, the cell cycle, and the VEGF and cGMP-PKG pathways, suggesting that phosphorylation modifications in MASH may predominantly drive the fibrotic and proliferative phenotypes. These phenotypes driven by acetylation, lactylation and phosphorylation are all involved in MASH ontogeny, suggesting that PTM dysregulation is a common molecular mechanism of multicellular organelle dysfunction in MASH hepatocytes, and that the different PTM types collectively drive disease progression through subcellular localization specificity and their complementary regulatory functions.

Importantly, this study confirms that CMKLR1, CYP2E1, GLRX, and XRCC1 are key proteins in the global dysregulation map of PTM in MASH. As a functionally diverse G protein-coupled receptor, CMKLR1 regulates immune cell recruitment, polarization, maturation and migration, bone metabolism, adipocyte metabolism, and angiogenesis through the activation of its ligand chemerin (30, 31, 32, 33). Interestingly, CMKLR1 was demonstrated to be high-expressed in MASH mice and cellular models (34, 35), which is similar to the findings of the present study. Moreover, the chemerin/CMKLR1 pathway contributes to Treg/Th17 imbalance and Kupffer cell-mediated inflammatory response, thereby accelerating fat deposition and degeneration in MASH (34, 35). Notably, this study found that the level of phosphorylation at the S345 site of CMKLR1 was increased in MASH liver tissue samples. Currently, there is no literature report on the effect of this phosphorylation on CMKLR1 expression and function. Together with previous studies, we hypothesized that phosphorylation of the S345 site may lead to attenuation of CMKLR1 activity and impede the activating effect of chemerin, which is beneficial for alleviating the MASH process.

The core biological function of CYP2E1 is to act as a monooxygenase, catalyzing small-molecule lipophilic compounds, thereby regulating ethanol metabolism, drug metabolism, detoxification, and oxidative stress (36, 37, 38). Cumulative studies have amply demonstrated that CYP2E1 contributes to the inflammatory response and fibrosis induced by oxidative and endoplasmic reticulum stress in MASH by catalyzing the production of ROS and RNS from a wide range of small molecules (fatty acids, ketone bodies, acetone, ethanol, and drugs) (36, 37, 38). This study confirmed that CYP2E1 was hyper-expressed in MASH liver tissues and that lactylation levels were reduced at the K140 and K187 sites. Currently, no studies have reported lactylation modifications of CYP2E1. In the present study, we hypothesized that lactylation of the K140 and K187 sites may be detrimental to the expression and function of CYP2E1 in MASH.

As a key redox sensor and regulator, GLRX catalyzes reversible S-glutathionylation, maintains cellular redox homeostasis, and is involved in the biosynthesis and translocation of Fe-S cluster (39, 40, 41). Previous studies have revealed that GLRX is low-expressed in the liver tissue of MASH mice (42), which is analogous to the findings of the present study. Moreover, GLRX alleviated apoptosis, validation cascade and hepatic fibrosis in MASH by inhibiting Zbtb16 transcription and negatively regulating immune pathways (42). Unlike that study, the present study found, for the first time, the presence of acetylation modification of GLRX and an increased level of acetylation at the K14 site in MASH liver tissues. Therefore, K14 acetylation may be a negative regulatory mechanism that exacerbates the pathological process of MASH by inhibiting GLRX activity and leading to the loss of its protective function.

XRCC1 is an organizer of DNA single-strand break repair and maintains genomic stability by precisely recruiting, stabilizing and coordinating a series of key repair enzymes (LigIIIα, PNKP, APTX, and Polβ) at the damage site (43, 44, 45). Previous studies have demonstrated that XRCC1 is low-expressed in the antifibrotic process of MASLD (46). However, no studies have reported the function of XRCC1 in the pathologic process of MASH. Notably, cumulative studies have revealed that XRCC1 is associated with fibrosis progression in a variety of tissues/organs, including the aorta (47), nasopharyngeal carcinoma (48), lung (49), intestinal tract (50), and heart (51). In the present study, XRCC1 was low-expressed in MASH liver tissues and had increased levels of acetylation and lactylation at the K169 site and phosphorylation at the S447 site. It is suggested that PTMs may directly cause loss of XRCC1 function, leading to defective DNA repair and driving the progression of MASH to fibrosis.

Notably, the bottom-up LC-MS/MS approach used in this study, while effective for large-scale identification of individual PTM sites, cannot resolve the full spectrum of proteoforms arising from coexisting or mutually exclusive PTMs. For example, XRCC1 carries acetylation (K169), lactylation (K169), and phosphorylation (S447) in MASH samples. Whether these PTMs occur simultaneously on the same XRCC1 molecule or represent distinct subpopulations of the protein remains unknown. Given that K169 is a shared site for acetylation and lactylation, it is plausible that these two PTMs compete for the same residue, thereby fine-tuning XRCC1 function in a context-dependent manner. Similarly, CYP2E1 shows reduced lactylation at K140 and K187 alongside increased total protein expression, raising the possibility that the hypo-lactylation may relieve autoinhibition or affect protein turnover. Proteoform-level resolution, ideally achieved through integrative structural biology approaches, will be essential to decipher how combinatorial PTM codes orchestrate the pathogenic functions of these hub proteins in MASH.

Our analytical strategy, which prioritizes proteins showing both differential expression and concurrent modification by acetylation, lactylation or phosphorylation, represents a hypothesis driven approach to nominate hub regulators with multilayered dysregulation. However, this does not imply that posttranslational modifications occurring independently of total protein abundance are less biologically relevant. In many settings, PTMs alone can profoundly alter protein function, such as enzymatic activity, PPI, or subcellular localization, without any detectable change in overall protein level (8, 9). In fact, our datasets contain numerous DMPs that were not identified as DEPs in ordinary proteomics, and these may represent critical fine-tuning events that drive MASH pathogenesis. Furthermore, each individual PTM class in isolation exhibited distinct subcellular preferences and pathway enrichments. Acetylation targets mitochondria and oxidative stress, lactylation links glycolysis to inflammation, and phosphorylation regulates nuclear signaling and fibrosis. This suggests that even single modality dysregulation could meaningfully mediate specific phenotypic aspects of MASH. Therefore, while the overlapping analysis robustly identified CMKLR1, CYP2E1, GLRX, and XRCC1 as multimodified core proteins, we acknowledge that PTM changes independent of expression levels and individual PTM classes are equally valuable for understanding the full landscape of MASH pathophysiology. These should be explored in future hypothesis testing studies.

Several limitations warrant consideration before translating these findings into clinical or mechanistic conclusions. First, this is a cross-sectional study with only five cases per group. We therefore cannot distinguish whether the observed PTM changes are drivers of MASH, consequences of established disease, or bystander effects of chronic metabolic stress. Second, although we collected basic medication and comorbidity data, we did not control for dietary patterns, physical activity, alcohol intake, or other environmental factors that could independently shape PTM profiles. Third, the sample size is too small for subgroup analyses or robust adjustment for confounders, and the IHC validation was performed in a limited subset. Thus, our findings should be viewed as hypothesis-generating rather than confirmatory. Establishing directionality and mechanism will require longitudinal sampling, larger independent cohorts, and functional experiments that directly test whether altering these PTM sites modifies MASH-related phenotypes.

In conclusion, this study systematically resolved the global dysregulation of PTM in MASH through a multidimensional proteomics strategy, suggesting that acetylation, lactylation, and phosphorylation modifications may synergistically regulate metabolic, oxidative stress and fibrotic pathways. As core hub proteins, abnormal changes in the PTMs of CMKLR1, CYP2E1, GLRX, and XRCC1 offer novel directions for the development of early diagnostic markers and targeted therapies for MASH.
