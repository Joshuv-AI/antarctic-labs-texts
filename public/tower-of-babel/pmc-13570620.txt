Ethical Prioritization of Healthcare Access During Pandemics in Jordan: A Cross-Sectional Study of Physician Awareness, Confidence in Ethical Decision-Making and the Role of COVID-19–Specific Training
Authors: Faris M Tbayshat, Omar F Khabour, Karem H Alzoubi, Wael K Al-Delaimy
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe COVID-19 pandemic posed unprecedented ethical challenges to healthcare professionals, particularly in resource-limited settings. This study aimed to assess physicians’ awareness of ethical dilemmas, confidence in ethical decision-making, and the impact of training during the COVID-19 pandemic in Jordan.MethodsA cross-sectional survey was conducted among 300 physicians who worked in Jordanian hospitals during the COVID-19 pandemic. Data were collected on experience, perceived workload, COVID-19–specific training, awareness of ethical dilemmas, and confidence in decision-making. Chi-square tests, one-way ANOVA (Welch’s), and multivariable ordinal logistic regression analysis were used for analysis.ResultsWorkload was significantly associated with confidence in ethical decision-making in bivariate analysis (Welch’s F (4, 63.0) = 3.26, p = 0.017), with heavier workload groups reporting higher confidence; however, workload was not independently associated with confidence in the adjusted ordinal logistic regression model. General practitioners and specialists reported higher awareness than physicians who are sub-specialists or work in non-clinical or other healthcare-related roles (χ2(6) = 13.9, p = 0.031). In addition, physicians who received COVID-19–specific training were significantly more aware of ethical dilemmas than those who did not (χ2(2) = 8.58, p = 0.014). Finally, physicians with more than 10 years of experience reported significantly greater confidence in ethical decision-making than those with 5–10 years of experience (β = 1.71, p = 0.010).ConclusionCOVID-19–specific training was associated with higher ethical awareness, while clinical experience was associated with confidence in ethical decision-making. These findings underscore the need for structured COVID-19–specific training and support systems to prepare healthcare workers for future public health emergencies through targeted preparedness training and institutional support strategies in Jordan and similar low- and middle-income settings, including integration into crisis standards of care, aimed at promoting equitable, transparent, and ethically consistent patient prioritization during future public health emergencies.Keywords: COVID-19, ethics, patient prioritization, healthcare workers, pandemics, Jordan

Introduction

Pandemics represent one of the most demanding stress tests for health systems, placing simultaneous pressure on clinical capacity, operational resilience, and the ethical foundations of medical practice.1,2 During the coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) pandemic, many health systems faced scenarios in which demand for critical care, oxygen delivery systems, intensive care unit (ICU) beds, trained staff, and personal protective equipment outstripped supply.3,4 In addition, the COVID-19 pandemic created unprecedented ethical challenges for healthcare systems, forcing clinicians worldwide to make difficult decisions regarding the allocation of scarce life-saving resources.5–7 Ethical frameworks such as utilitarianism, egalitarianism, and prioritizing the worst-off have been proposed to guide these decisions, yet their real-world application remains complex and controversial.8–10

This investigation focuses on ethical dilemmas related to patient prioritization during pandemics, including the allocation of scarce resources, ventilator prioritization, ICU access, treatment escalation, and balancing individual patient needs with population-level fairness. These issues are particularly important in resource-constrained settings, where physicians may face difficult prioritization decisions without sufficient institutional guidance or structured preparedness training.

In resource-constrained settings, including many middle-income countries, these decisions were compounded by limited institutional guidance and insufficient COVID-19–specific training, exacerbating uncertainty and moral distress among healthcare providers (HCWs).11,12 Studies have shown that insufficient knowledge of ethical criteria in resource allocation was common among frontline health professionals during the pandemic, highlighting a need for structured ethical preparedness and education.13,14

Early in the COVID-19 pandemic, leading ethical analyses emphasized that scarce resource allocation should be guided by transparent, consistently applied principles rather than ad hoc bedside rationing. Emanuel et al proposed core principles such as maximizing benefits, treating people equally, promoting instrumental value, and prioritizing the worst off within appropriate constraints.15 Other scholars stressed the importance of procedural safeguards, public legitimacy, and careful communication around triage and reallocation decisions.16,17 Recent studies identified equity, transparency, stewardship, justice, and reciprocity among the most frequently cited values guiding ethical resource allocation across pandemics.18–20

Organizational readiness is also essential. Crisis Standards of Care (CSC) aim to structure prioritization decisions during emergencies, yet CSC policies vary in clarity and comprehensiveness, raising concerns about equity and consistency in application.21 For individual HCWs, ethically fraught decisions, especially those perceived as “impossible choices”, may trigger moral distress and injury. Strategies to mitigate distress include separating triage roles, providing ethics support, and clarifying escalation pathways.22–24

In low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), these challenges are magnified by workforce shortages, inadequate ICU capacity, and limited access to COVID-19–specific training. Jordan represents a particularly relevant case.25 During the COVID-19 pandemic, Jordan’s health system contended with high demand while maintaining services for a diverse population. Previous regional surveys noted significant ethical burden among physicians, including gaps in knowledge and ethical preparedness.26–28 However, empirical data specifically examining physicians’ ethical awareness and decision-making confidence in Jordan remain limited. Physician awareness and confidence are distinct but interrelated. Awareness refers to the ability to recognize ethically complex situations and understand applicable principles. Confidence, in contrast, may influence decisiveness and readiness under moral pressure. Both are shaped by role, training, experience, and institutional context.29,30 Ethical frameworks provide structured approaches to decision-making during resource scarcity, grounded in core ethical principles such as fairness, justice, transparency, equity, and maximizing benefit. Within this context, ethical awareness denotes a physician’s self-reported recognition of patient prioritization dilemmas, while confidence reflects their perceived ability to navigate these challenges. More broadly, ethical preparedness encompasses the collective awareness, training, institutional guidance, and decision-support structures required during public health emergencies. When institutional constraints disrupt these processes, clinicians suffer: moral distress arises from the immediate psychological tension of being unable to act on ethical judgment, whereas moral injury represents deeper, lasting psychological harm from witnessing or participating in actions that violate core moral beliefs.

Accordingly, this study aimed to assess Jordanian physicians’ self-reported ethical awareness and confidence in ethical decision-making during pandemics, and to examine associations between these outcomes and sociodemographic, professional, and contextual characteristics, including age, sex, years of experience, job title, workplace, perceived workload, and COVID-19–specific training.

Methods

Study Design and Setting

A cross-sectional, questionnaire-based study was conducted to evaluate physicians’ awareness of ethical dilemmas related to patient prioritization during pandemics and their confidence in ethical decision-making. The study focused on physicians actively involved in clinical care during the COVID-19 pandemic in Jordan, a low-middle-income country whose healthcare system experienced substantial strain across successive waves.

Study Population and Eligibility Criteria

Eligible participants were physicians practicing in Jordan during the COVID-19 pandemic, including general practitioners, specialists and sub-specialists, public hospitals, private hospitals, outpatient clinics, and other healthcare settings. Physicians involved in direct or indirect patient care during the pandemic were eligible to participate. The category of “other roles” included physicians working in administrative, academic, training, or non-core clinical positions. Physicians who were not practicing during the COVID-19 period or who submitted incomplete survey responses were excluded from the analysis.

Sample Size and Recruitment

Participants were recruited using convenience sampling between 2024 and 2025. The questionnaire was distributed electronically through professional networks and online communication platforms, such as email, WhatsApp groups, and social media groups. No formal a priori sample size calculation was performed. A minimal sample size of 260 was calculated using G*Power 3.1.9.4 (Universität Kiel, Düsseldorf, Germany) with an effect size of 0.2, α = 0.05, and power = 0.95. A total of 300 physicians were included in the final analysis. Eligible physicians were invited to complete the self-administered questionnaire voluntarily. The recruitment phase concluded once the predetermined sample size of 300 participants was achieved. The sample size was considered adequate for detecting moderate associations in bivariate analyses and for multivariable ordinal regression modeling, in line with commonly accepted recommendations for observational studies involving ordinal outcomes.

Data Collection Instrument

Data were collected using a structured, self-administered questionnaire developed for this study. The questionnaire was informed by existing ethical frameworks for pandemic response and by previously published surveys examining ethical decision-making among healthcare professionals during public health emergencies.

The questionnaire was developed specifically for this study and was reviewed for content relevance by the study team. However, no formal psychometric validation, construct validity testing, internal consistency assessment, or pilot testing was performed before data collection.

The questionnaire consisted of the following domains. The first domain covered sociodemographic characteristics (age group and sex) and professional characteristics (years of clinical experience, current job title, and primary workplace). The second domain included pandemic-related factors (perceived workload during the COVID-19 pandemic and receipt of COVID-19–specific. The third domain asked about ethical awareness (self-reported awareness of ethical dilemmas related to patient prioritization during pandemics), and confidence in ethical decision-making (self-reported confidence in navigating ethical dilemmas during pandemics). Higher scores on awareness and confidence indicated greater perceived awareness or confidence, respectively.

The full questionnaire, including the exact wording of the items, response options, and instructions provided to participants, is provided as Supplementary File 1.

Study Outcomes

The primary outcomes of interest were ethical awareness, defined as the level of self-reported awareness of ethical dilemmas involved in prioritizing patients during pandemics; and the confidence in ethical decision-making, defined as the level of self-reported confidence in navigating ethical dilemmas arising during pandemics. Both outcomes were treated as ordinal variables in the primary analyses.

Statistical Analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to summarize participant characteristics and study variables. Categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages, while continuous or ordinal variables were summarized using means and standard deviations. Bivariate associations between categorical variables were examined using chi-square tests. Fisher’s exact test with Monte Carlo simulation was applied when expected cell counts were less than or equal to 5. For chi-square analyses, the patient-prioritization awareness item was analyzed using its three original response categories: “No”, “Yes, to some extent”, and “Yes, to a good extent”. The broader awareness variable was analyzed as a five-point ordinal scale in ordinal regression analyses. Differences in confidence scores across groups were assessed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) with Welch’s correction to account for heterogeneity of variances. Because confidence was measured using a five-point ordinal scale, Kruskal–Wallis testing was also performed as a non-parametric sensitivity analysis. To identify independent factor associated with ethical awareness and confidence, multivariable ordinal logistic regression models were constructed, however the proportional odds assumption could not be directly assessed using the available Jamovi ordinal logistic regression module; therefore, ordinal regression findings were interpreted cautiously. Factor associated with variables included age group, sex, years of experience, job title, perceived workload, and receipt of COVID-19–specific training. Results were reported as odds ratios with corresponding 95% confidence intervals and p-values. Collinearity among independent variables was assessed using variance inflation factors. All statistical tests were two-sided, and a p-value <0.05 was considered statistically significant. Statistical analyses were performed using Jamovi statistical software.

Ethical Considerations

The study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. Participation was voluntary, and informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to survey completion. Responses were collected anonymously, and no identifiable personal information was recorded. Ethical approval was obtained from the Jordan University of Science and Technology Institutional Review Boward (ID: 2024/395) prior to study initiation.

Results

Participant Characteristics

A total of 300 physicians were included in the final analysis. The percentages of male and female participants were comparable (53.7% versus 46.3%, respectively). Participants represented a range of ages, years of clinical experience, and professional roles, including general practitioners (48.7%), specialists (23.0%), subspecialists (18.7%), and physicians in other clinical or healthcare-related positions (9.6%). Most respondents were employed in public hospitals, with others working in private hospitals, clinics, or mixed settings. Perceived workload during the COVID-19 pandemic ranged from very light to very heavy. Ethical Awareness of Patient Prioritization During Pandemics

Overall, the majority of physicians reported some awareness of ethical dilemmas related to patient prioritization during pandemics. Bivariate analysis showed no statistically significant association between years of experience and ethical awareness (χ2(4) = 2.34, p = 0.674). In contrast, ethical awareness differed significantly by professional role. General practitioners and specialists reported higher awareness than subspecialists, and physicians in other roles (χ2(6) = 13.9, p = 0.031). When ethical awareness was analyzed using a five-point ordinal scale, a strong association with job title was observed (χ2(12) = 34.0, p < 0.001).

Receipt of COVID-19–specific training was significantly associated with higher ethical awareness (χ2(2) = 8.58, p = 0.014), with trained physicians more frequently reporting higher levels of awareness.

Confidence in Ethical Decision-Making

Confidence in navigating ethical dilemmas varied significantly across workload categories. One-way ANOVA with Welch’s correction demonstrated a statistically significant difference in mean confidence scores by workload intensity (Welch’s F(4, 63.0) = 3.26, p = 0.017). Because confidence was measured using a five-point ordinal scale, Kruskal–Wallis testing was performed as a non-parametric sensitivity analysis. The Kruskal–Wallis test also showed a statistically significant difference in confidence scores across workload categories (χ2 = 12.3, df = 4, p = 0.015). Confidence scores differed significantly across workload categories; however, the relationship between workload intensity and confidence was not consistently linear across all groups. Additionally, in the adjusted ordinal logistic regression model, workload categories were not independently associated with confidence after accounting for age, sex, years of experience, job title, and COVID-19–specific training. In the ordinal logistic regression model examining factors associated with ethical awareness, COVID-19–specific training was associated with higher ethical awareness (OR = 1.87, 95% CI: 1.18–2.99, p = 0.008). Male sex was associated with lower ethical awareness compared with female sex (OR = 0.61, 95% CI: 0.38–0.97, p = 0.036). Physicians working in non-core clinical or other healthcare-related roles had lower ethical awareness compared with general practitioners (OR = 0.38, 95% CI: 0.17–0.83, p = 0.016). More than 10 years of clinical experience showed a borderline association with higher ethical awareness compared with 5–10 years of experience (OR = 3.58, 95% CI: 1.00–13.22, p = 0.051). Discussion

This study provides insight into physicians’ awareness of ethical dilemmas related to patient prioritization and their confidence in ethical decision-making during the COVID-19 pandemic in Jordan. The findings indicated a strong relationship between professional experience, training, and workload of physicians and ethical preparedness in a public health emergency. These findings build on prior ethical analyses of pandemic triage and crisis standards of care, which emphasize the importance of institutional preparedness and structured allocation frameworks during resource scarcity.5,15,31

One of the central findings of this study is that receiving COVID-19–specific training was an independent factor associated with higher ethical awareness. Physicians who reported receiving training were significantly more likely to recognize ethical dilemmas related to prioritization. This finding is consistent with evidence demonstrating that pandemic preparedness and COVID-19–specific training programs can enhance clinicians’ ability to recognize ethical conflicts and apply ethical principles during crisis situations.16,17 Institutional frameworks such as those proposed by Emanuel et al and the Hastings Center emphasize that structured guidance and training improve clarity and reduce reliance on ad hoc bedside decision-making.15,22,32

Professional role was another factor associated with ethical awareness. Physicians working in non-core clinical roles, particularly those with limited direct bedside involvement during the pandemic, reported lower awareness compared with general practitioners. This may reflect reduced exposure to triage-related discussions, real-time crisis decision-making, and institutional COVID-19 preparedness activities. In contrast, frontline physicians were more likely to encounter ethically challenging situations involving resource allocation, ventilator prioritization, and treatment escalation decisions during routine clinical practice. Similar disparities have been reported in studies examining clinician engagement in ethical decision-making during the COVID-19 pandemic, where physicians with greater frontline involvement demonstrated higher familiarity with ethical frameworks and crisis standards of care.29,33 These findings suggest that ethical preparedness may depend not only on formal training but also on the degree of clinical exposure during public health emergencies. Therefore, healthcare institutions should consider implementing broader interdisciplinary preparedness programs to ensure that all physicians, including those outside frontline clinical roles, receive adequate exposure to ethical decision-making frameworks during future healthcare crises.

The observed association between sex and ethical awareness, with female physicians demonstrating higher awareness, warrants cautious interpretation. Prior studies examining ethical sensitivity and moral reasoning in healthcare contexts have reported similar trends, suggesting that female healthcare professionals may exhibit greater attentiveness to the relational and ethical dimensions of care.34,35 Possible explanations include differences in communication styles, ethical socialization, or professional role expectations; however, these hypotheses require further investigation and should not be overgeneralized.

Unlike awareness about pandemics, the study showed that confidence in ethical decision-making was potentially linked to higher experiential factors. Physicians with more than 10 years of experience demonstrated significantly higher confidence. This finding aligns with literature suggesting that cumulative clinical exposure enhances perceived self-efficacy and decisiveness in complex care situations.29,36 Repeated exposure to high-stakes decisions during crises may reinforce professional confidence, even amid ethical uncertainty. In addition, experiential factors reflect overlap with seniority, leadership roles, and prior exposure to emergency contexts. However, clinical experience alone is not sufficient to ensure ethically consistent decision-making during public health emergencies. Translating experience into ethically sound practice requires evidence-informed organizational support, structured decision-making processes, institutional ethics guidance, and multidisciplinary accountability mechanisms that promote fairness, consistency, and transparency during resource allocation.37 Confidence does not necessarily equate to ethical correctness. Allocation frameworks require principled consistency and transparency beyond individual intuition.5,15 Crisis standards of care were developed precisely to avoid reliance solely on clinician confidence or discretion.38

In bivariate analysis, confidence differed significantly across perceived workload categories; however, workload was not independently associated with confidence in the adjusted ordinal logistic regression model. This suggests that the unadjusted relationship between workload and confidence may partly reflect differences in other professional factors, such as experience, seniority, or job role. Therefore, the workload findings should be interpreted cautiously and should not be considered an independent association. Nevertheless, perceived workload remains relevant in pandemic settings, as elevated workload during COVID-19 has been associated with moral distress, burnout, and psychological strain among healthcare workers.22,23,39 Therefore, higher confidence under heavy workload conditions may coexist with moral injury or emotional burden.36

Taken together, the findings reveal a potential gap between awareness and confidence. COVID-19–specific training was strongly associated with ethical awareness but not with confidence, whereas clinical experience was more closely linked to confidence than awareness. This divergence reinforces the conceptual distinction between ethical recognition and decision-making self-efficacy. Ethical frameworks emphasize that awareness of competing values and structured procedural safeguards are essential components of equitable allocation.8,15,18 Clinicians with high confidence but limited ethical awareness may be at risk of oversimplifying complex allocation decisions, while those with high awareness but lower confidence may experience greater moral distress.23,36 Balanced preparedness may therefore require structured pandemic preparedness programs, supervised clinical exposure, institutional ethical guidance, and multidisciplinary decision-support systems during public health emergencies.32

These findings support the integration of structured pandemic preparedness and ethics-related training into undergraduate medical curricula and continuing professional development. Crisis preparedness planning should ensure that training extends beyond ICU and emergency physicians to include all clinicians who may be involved in patient prioritization. Such training should include crisis standards of care, transparent triage policies, scarce-resource allocation, ventilator prioritization, communication of difficult decisions, and institutional ethics-support pathways. Institutional adoption of transparent triage policies aligned with internationally recognized ethical principles may reduce ambiguity and promote fairness during future public health emergencies.6,17 However, resource allocation decisions are also shaped by cultural and religious contexts. In Jordan, where the majority of the population is Muslim, preparedness programs should incorporate culturally relevant ethical guidance alongside international frameworks. Common secular allocation frameworks often emphasize maximizing benefit, saving the most lives or life-years, instrumental value, and procedural fairness.8,17 In contrast, Islamic ethical approaches place strong emphasis on justice, preservation of human dignity, protection of life, social responsibility, ihsan, and altruism.40 Al-Balas and Al-Delaimy argued that prioritizing limited medical resources in Muslim societies requires a just and ethically acceptable system informed by Islamic jurisprudential principles.40 Importantly, age, disability, social status, or likelihood of survival alone should not be considered sufficient criteria for prioritization; rather, decisions should be transparent, fair, and consistent with Islamic ethical values while maintaining the sustainability of healthcare services.40 Incorporating these culturally relevant principles into pandemic preparedness training may improve the acceptability, legitimacy, and public trustworthiness of triage decisions in predominantly Muslim societies.

Future research should use prospective or interventional designs to evaluate which educational approaches most effectively improve ethical awareness, decision-making confidence, and ethically consistent clinical practice during public health emergencies. Studies could assess simulation-based training, case-based ethics discussions, multidisciplinary triage exercises, and institutional ethics-support pathways. Future work should also examine whether structured organizational support systems help bridge the gap between ethical awareness and confidence in real-world prioritization decisions.

This study has several limitations. The cross-sectional design limits causal inference. Data were self-reported and may be subject to social desirability bias. The study focused on physicians in Jordan, which may limit generalizability. The use of convenience sampling and electronic questionnaire distribution may have introduced selection bias, as physicians who were more active in professional networks, email, WhatsApp groups, or social media platforms may have been more likely to participate. This may limit the representativeness of the sample and the generalizability of the findings to all physicians practicing in Jordan. In addition, the sample was not designed to match national physician workforce statistics; therefore, comparisons with the broader physician population should be interpreted cautiously. Furthermore, confidence was measured subjectively and does not necessarily reflect alignment with established ethical standards.5,6 The question about COVID training lacked details on the type and duration of training, which would have made the recommendations more specific. The questionnaire was developed for this study and was not formally psychometrically validated. Therefore, the ordinal measures of ethical awareness and confidence should be interpreted as self-reported indicators rather than validated measures of ethical competence. The lack of construct validity testing, internal consistency assessment, and pilot testing may limit the interpretability of the findings.

Conclusion

During the COVID-19 pandemic, physicians in Jordan demonstrated varying levels of ethical awareness and confidence in patient prioritization decisions. Training emerged as a key factor associated with ethical awareness, while confidence was primarily associated with experience. There are expectations of future pandemics similar to COVID-19 due to global connectivity and travel, and more contact with animals that transmit viruses to which humans do not have any immunity. It is therefore important to prepare the current and next generation of physicians to deal with complex clinical decision-making and prioritization during pandemics that undermine clinical resources, similar to what happened with the COVID pandemic. Strengthening ethics education and preparedness training is essential to support healthcare professionals facing future public health emergencies. These findings may inform evidence-based educational initiatives, institutional preparedness programs, and national pandemic planning aimed at promoting equitable, transparent, and ethically consistent patient prioritization during future public health emergencies.
