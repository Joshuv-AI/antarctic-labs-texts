Roles and responsibilities of government chief nursing and midwifery officers in the Americas
Authors: Bruna Moreno Dias, Jordana Salma, Susan R. Cooper, Natalia Andrea Henao Murillo, Patsy Edwards Henry, Sonia Abad Vásquez, Edwin Vicente C. Bolastig, Eduardo Benjamín Puertas Donoso, Wendy Denise Sealy
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectives.To analyze the roles, responsibilities and scope of current government chief nursing and midwifery officers or equivalent senior leaders in the Region of the Americas.MethodsThis was a descriptive observational study. All government chief nursing or midwifery officers or equivalent senior leaders from 35 countries in the Region of the Americas were considered eligible to participate. An online survey was conducted that included items on demographic variables, the officer’s roles and responsibilities, and a self-assessment of competencies. Data were analyzed using descriptive statistics, disaggregated by role, subregion and country income level. Content analysis was used for responses to open-ended questions.ResultsTwenty eight officers from 28 countries participated in this study, representing 80% of the 35 countries in the Americas. About 82.1% (23/28) of the officers or equivalent leaders were exclusively responsible for nursing issues. Most of the participants had the job title of chief nursing officer (53.6%, 15/28), were female (92.9%, 26/28) and were aged 55–64 years (46.4%, 13/28). Regarding leadership roles, 57.1% (16) of participants reported that their countries had an executive model, with the officer having line authority over nursing or midwifery, or both. The responsibilities of these officers were mainly related to leadership, influence and policy advice. In the self-assessment of competencies, participants rated themselves as less skilled in global health priorities and management.Conclusions.Although the role of a government chief nursing officer or midwifery officer exists in most countries in the Americas, there is a need to further strengthen it. It is important for governments and stakeholders to create a policy environment conducive to empowering these leaders, recognizing their strategic role in the human resources for health agenda, and in expanding the contributions of nursing and midwifery towards achieving more resilient and equitable health systems.

Government chief nursing and midwifery officers (GCNMOs) are professionals with policy and management roles responsible for supporting governments in strengthening the nursing and midwifery workforce and providing leadership and advice to support countries in achieving their health goals through this workforce (1).

In order to establish and strengthen senior leadership roles for nurses and midwives, World Health Assembly Resolution 74.15 urges Member States to consider appointing a GCNMO (2). The implementation of this role is important to ensure that health policies take into account the experience and expertise of nurses and midwives; to increase female representation in leadership positions; to strengthen governance in human resources for health by establishing coordination between levels and interprofessional and intersectoral actions; to implement actions and programs that strengthen the regulatory framework, education and working conditions for these professionals; and to implement programs that improve the health of the population (3).

GCNMOs bring essential insights from frontline health care, advocate for workforce needs and promote evidence-based policies that address pressing health care challenges. Their leadership can bridge the gap between government decision-making and community health priorities, fostering interdisciplinary collaboration. The involvement of nurses and midwives at all levels of health policy development and implementation ensures the acceptability, effectiveness and sustainability of such policies; therefore, the absence of GCNMO’s contributions to developing policy and the absence of women in policy-making processes undermine progress toward achieving the Sustainable Development Goals and universal health coverage (2).

Five models are recognized for classifying the roles of GCNMOs. The focal point model involves designating an officer to liaise among government, the World Health Organization (WHO) and professional forums. The dispersal model places nursing experts across different divisions in the ministry of health to contribute to various initiatives. The program model assigns an officer to be responsible for a specific initiative (e.g. patient safety), without giving them authority over workforce regulation. In the advisory model, an officer provides expert input to policy-makers but has no authority over the profession. The executive model, in contrast, grants line authority and direct influence over workforce policy and, in some cases, over education and the scope of practice (2). The executive model is generally considered the most desirable, as it enables strategic leadership, institutional authority and meaningful participation in high-level decision-making processes.

In the WHO’s Region of the Americas, the Pan American Health Organization (PAHO), through its technical cooperation actions (4–7), and the International Council of Nurses have consistently championed the establishment and implementation of the GCNMO role to embed nursing leadership in health policy development.

Despite the significance of the GCNMO role, some countries underutilize this position or lack a GCNMO altogether, undermining the potential for integrating nursing and midwifery perspectives into critical areas such as workforce planning, system development and health equity (8, 9).

According to a previous regional analysis, the role of the GCNMO has been reported by 29 of the 35 countries of the Americas, with a strong presence among Caribbean countries, although it is relatively weaker in South American countries. However, the existence of a formally designated position does not necessarily ensure effective integration into policy formulation and decision-making processes, nor does it guarantee the level of autonomy, authority, competencies and institutional resources required to fulfill the role strategically (7).

Despite being an essential component of health systems, the health workforce, mostly represented by nurses and midwives, faces a crisis due to chronic underinvestment in actions to improve governance, education and working conditions. To address these challenges, PAHO’s Policy on the health workforce 2030: strengthening human resources for health to achieve resilient health systems offers Member States strategic guidelines for strengthening governance, developing and consolidating regulatory mechanisms, implementing and consolidating interprofessional teams, enhancing capacity-building, and promoting decent working conditions, including protecting the physical and mental health of health workers (4).

There are about 7.4 million nursing and midwifery professionals in the Region of the Americas (10); however, this workforce faces challenges related to inequity in the distribution and availability of these professionals, heterogeneity in education, a lack of development opportunities and leadership positions, inadequate working conditions, low adoption of advanced practice roles and high migration rates. These factors limit the Region’s ability to meet the current and future challenges of health systems, in addition to contributing to the poor retention of nurses in the national labor market and the field’s lessened attractiveness to future professionals (6). By promoting the role of the GCNMO, it is possible to address these systemic issues, improve workforce development, and promote equitable and effective health policies throughout the Region of the Americas.

GCNMOs serve as pivotal agents for health system transformation, policy reform and international collaboration to achieve global health goals. Their roles are multifaceted, encompassing leadership, policy guidance, and the planning and delivery of health systems. This study aims to analyze the roles, responsibilities and scope of practice of current GCNMOs or equivalent senior leaders in the Region.

METHODS

Study design

This descriptive observational study used a cross-sectional approach to provide a comprehensive analysis of the current profile and institutional positioning of GCNMOs in the Region. The Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology guidelines (known as STROBE) were used to design and report the study, ensuring transparency, consistency and methodological rigor in the presentation of findings (11).

Participants

Participants were selected using a non-probabilistic sample. All GCNMOs or equivalent senior leaders from all 35 countries in the Region were considered eligible. Invitations were extended to all eligible officers, and participation was voluntary. The final sample consisted of 28 participants, representing 28 countries in the Region.

Data collection

Data were collected via an online form using the Qualtrics tool (Qualtrics, Provo, UT, USA), available in three languages: English, Portuguese and Spanish. The invitation to participate was sent by a PAHO representative via email, with up to four reminders also sent by email. In addition, representatives of PAHO’s subregional or country offices, the Grupo de Profesionales de Enfermería de Centroamérica y el Caribe (Group of Nursing Professionals of Central America and the Caribbean), the Regional Nursing Body and the International Council of Nurses supported the distribution of the survey to potential participants by sharing the survey link to help ensure the broadest possible participation. Data were collected from May 30 to July 15, 2025. Duplicate entries and inconsistent records identified within the survey data set were excluded.

The online questionnaire was adapted from the WHO technical document Roles and responsibilities of government chief nursing and midwifery officers: a capacity-building manual, and used the same items (1). Additional demographic variables were included to characterize participants, along with six supplementary items addressing competencies related to global priority areas. The questionnaire consisted of three sections and the variables described below.

Demographic variables and characterization data.

The variables collected for each respondent included their country, gender, age, employment status, type of primary training, level of education, and length of time in the position. In addition, respondents were asked to classify the GCNMO model applied in their country, selecting among focal point, dispersal, program, advisory or executive.

Responsibilities.

Using a scale with ratings from strongly disagree to strongly agree, participants were asked to indicate their degree of agreement with statements about their responsibilities as a GCNMO or equivalent senior leader. This section had 31 items, divided into 4 domains: leadership and influence (10 items), policy advice (9 items), planning and delivery of health systems and services (7 items), and programs for health status improvement (5 items). In addition, there were three open-ended questions about the challenges and opportunities of their role, and respondents were asked provide any additional information they considered relevant.

Self-assessment of competencies.

Participants were asked to assess their competencies on a 5-point visual scale, according to importance (from little to a lot) and performance (from poor to excellent). This section consisted of 33 items, divided into four domains: leadership (10 items), policy (8 items), management (9 items) and global health priorities (6 items). The global health priority items took into account those presented in WHO’s Fourteenth General Programme of Work 2025–2028 (12).

Data analysis

Descriptive statistics were analyzed using IBM’s Statistical Package for the Social Sciences, version 28 (IBM, Armonk, NY, USA). Data were analyzed according to GCNMO model (i.e. focal point, dispersal, program, advisory or executive), subregion (i.e. Central America, Latin Caribbean and Mexico; non-Latin Caribbean; North America; and South America) (13) and country income level (i.e. high-income, upper-middle-income, lower-middle-income, low-income) (14). Content analysis was applied to responses to the open-ended questions.

Ethical aspects

Informed consent to participate was sought from all potential respondents. Only responses from those who provided consent were considered. The research protocol was reviewed and approved by the PAHO Ethics Review Committee, under protocol 0840.01.

RESULTS

Respondents from a total of 28 countries participated in this study, representing 80% of the 35 countries in the Region of the Americas. Respondents represented 81.8% (9/11) of Central American and Latin Caribbean countries plus Mexico, 91.7% (11/12) of the non-Latin Caribbean, 100% (2/2) of North American countries and 60.0% (6/10) of South American countries.

Among the 28 participants, 15 held the position of chief or principal nursing officer (53.6%), 9 were directors (32.1%) and 4 held other positions (14.3%) (e.g. specialized professional, head of unit, coordinator or adviser). Respondents indicated that in 23 participating countries (82.1%), the chief or principal nursing officer or other equivalent leaders were responsible exclusively for nursing issues, while in the remaining five (17.9%), representatives of the health ministry are responsible not only for nursing issues but also other areas, such as human resources for health, education and research (data not shown).

The profile of the participants is shown in Table 1. The majority of participants were female (92.9%, 26/28) and aged 55–64 years (46.4%, 13/28). All participants had a degree in nursing or nursing and midwifery, and 57.1% (16/28) had a master’s degree as their highest level of training. Most participants worked full time (96.4%, 27/28), and half had been in their position for between 1 and 5 years (50.0%, 14/28), with 32.1% (9/28) having held their position for more than 6 years. Altogether, 100% (2/2) of respondents from North America, 77.8% (7/9) of those from Central America and 66.7% (4/6) of those from South America had been in their position for 5 years or fewer (data not shown). In contrast, respondents from non-Latin Caribbean countries had the highest proportion of people in post for more than 6 years (45.5%, 5/11).

The low scores for some responsibilities in the North American subregion are due to the fact that respondents indicated several items in the domains were not applicable to one or both countries. However, in all four domains, on average more respondents from high-income countries indicated that they had more responsibilities than those from low- and middle-income countries.

The highest percentages of respondents indicated that they were responsible for “establishing appropriate partnerships and facilitating collaboration with both the private and public sectors, and with academic institutions,” “representing and advocating for nursing and midwifery interests and their contributions nationally and internationally” and “providing advice on nursing and midwifery workforce capacity, capability and skill mix” (85.7%, 24/28, for all three items), while “contributing to the health status assessment in the country” was the responsibility of only 67.9% (19/28) of respondents.

In the self-assessment of the importance and performance of the selected competencies, importance was scored at 4.5/5 points or higher in all four domains analyzed. However, the average performance in each domain was rated as 4.5 for leadership, 4.3 for policy, 4.0 for management and 3.7 for global health priorities (Figure 2).

Items related to the self-assessment of competence in global health priorities had lower ratings, with the lowest being competence in digital health and artificial intelligence (3.3). Also noteworthy were the lower scores in the self-assessment of performance in financial management (3.5), the use of research and evidence (3.8), occupational health (3.9) and political participation (knowledge, astuteness and development, 4.1). When comparing the models for GCNMOs, respondents working under the focal point model rated themselves as having lower average performance than respondents working under other models.

The content analysis of the open-ended questions indicated that common challenges for the subregions included a lack of participation in decision-making processes and limited institutional support and resources for activities being carried out. In terms of the need for interventions to strengthen the roles of GCNMOs, there was consensus on the need to increase the availability of resources, and to guarantee the stability of the position and the sustainability of their actions, as well as to strengthen mechanisms for alliance-building, collaboration and cooperation with different actors, and to develop the capacities and competencies of these professionals. In addition, the need to establish or review normative and legal frameworks for nursing was highlighted.

Framework for strengthening the role of government chief nursing and midwifery officers, Region of the Americas, 2025

In the section of the survey that asked for additional comments, seven (25%) participants highlighted that their work includes actions such as coordinating with leaders of health and education institutions; workforce planning; strengthening regulatory frameworks and developing a strategic plan for nursing and midwifery, nursing policies and scope of practice; supporting the establishment of standards for nursing education and practice; professional development; conducting research; recruiting professionals from other countries; managing and controlling the operation of nursing services and ensuring the quality and safety of nursing care; coordinating with a nursing or midwifery council; and for chief nursing officers in the Caribbean, coordinating with the regional nursing body.

DISCUSSION

The extension of WHO’s global strategic directions for nursing and midwifery beyond 2025 and to 2030 presents a timely opportunity to reinforce the roles of GCNMOs and strengthen their capacity to have an impact across the Region of the Americas. GCNMOs are pivotal to advancing population health, strengthening health care systems, and improving the quality of life of their colleagues and communities (7).

Amid pressures on health systems from workforce shortages, climate threats and growing inequities, the complex role of GCNMOs demands broad and deep competencies, alongside authority, resources and regional platforms for effective advocacy and action (10, 15). Strengthening their position within ministries of health would align with the goals of establishing universal health coverage, advance the priorities of nursing and midwifery, and foster innovation and leadership, while addressing occupational risks, such as long shifts, irregular schedules, physical strain, stress and workplace violence (16–18). These risks are compounded by weak legislation, unsuitable working conditions and reliance on informal labor. Therefore, it is critical to integrate GCNMOs into policy- and decision-making processes to ensure that workforce planning and occupational health protections reflect the realities of nursing and midwifery practice.

Characteristics of officers and implications for the future

GCNMOs in the Region are predominantly senior professionals with advanced education and leadership experience, the majority of whom are women. This profile underscores the role’s importance as a pathway for advancing gender equity in health governance. While data on tenure in the position point to some stability, high turnover in certain subregions reflects both the relatively recent institutionalization of the role and political cycles tied to ministries of health. The age profile of respondents, most of whom were aged 55 to 64 years, suggests that many will retire within the next decade, raising concerns about who will take their place. Building leadership pipelines and mentorship programs is, therefore, essential to sustaining and expanding nursing leadership in the Region.

The responses underscored the structural challenges faced by GCNMOs, particularly their limited participation in policy and influence, while highlighting the need for greater visibility, resources and training to strengthen their roles. Self-assessments revealed gaps in their management, global health, digital health and artificial intelligence skills, despite respondents recognizing the importance of these areas. While GCNMOs acknowledged priorities such as building climate-resilient systems, ensuring emergency preparedness and preventing chronic diseases, they often lacked the resources, training or institutional support to effectively translate these priorities into concrete actions and strategies. Low self-assessment ratings for their use of research, knowledge of occupational health and political participation further highlight challenges in applying evidence, ensuring safe working conditions and influencing policy.

Systemic barriers, including entrenched power differentials among policy actors, continue to constrain the influence of nurse leaders (19). Limited recognition of GCNMOs, restricted involvement in policy processes and insufficient institutional resources undermine their effectiveness, with similar barriers reported for other nurse leaders, all of which diminish nursing’s political impact (20, 21). Although the number of GCNMOs is increasing, countries must ensure that these roles are integrated into governance structures and supported with professional development, policy engagement and broader representation of nursing in decision-making (10, 22). National governments should also incorporate GCNMOs into key decision-making forums so that their expertise can inform ministerial deliberations and enable these professionals to engage effectively in global policy arenas (23).

In this study, the executive model was dominant in non-Latin Caribbean and Central American states, but absent in North and South America. Among low- and middle-income countries, 55.6% of respondents reported that they worked under the executive model, while none reported working under the advisory model. It has been suggested that the executive model should be the standard for health care organizations, while the advisory model remains most relevant for national and international policy-making arenas (24). The absence of advisory models in parts of the Region may limit opportunities for international collaboration and global policy impact.

Comparative perspective

An analysis conducted by WHO’s European Region provides a valuable reference point for interpreting the survey results presented in this study. The analysis in the European Region was based on a similar survey instrument and applied comparable definitions and role classifications, which allows for cautious cross-regional comparison. Nevertheless, the contrasts presented here are exploratory and descriptive in nature and were not subjected to statistical testing. Compared with the Americas, appointees in the European Region had a more balanced gender distribution, were slightly younger and more likely to come from non-nursing backgrounds. Leadership models also differed: the executive model was more common in the Americas (57.1%, 16/28 countries) than in Europe (25.7%, 9/35 countries), and the focal point model was more prevalent in Europe than in the Americas (28.5% [10/35] versus 14.3% [4/28]) (2).

Additionally, more respondents in the European Region reported they were involved in activities focusing on health status improvement and offering policy advice, while those in the Americas reported they were involved in leadership and influence. In both Regions, political participation emerged as the weakest competency. In the Americas, this limitation reflects historical and structural barriers, including the relegation of nursing to caregiving functions, exclusion from policy spaces and limited training in political leadership (4, 6). By contrast, European chief nursing officers reported stronger policy influence that was supported by robust unions, advocacy training and longstanding traditions of nursing authority (2). Closing this gap in the Americas will require significant investment in political leadership training and mentorship, and the development of structured pathways for GCNMOs to engage in governance.

Implications for nursing and health policy

Findings from this study reinforce the notion that strengthening GCNMO roles is critical to advancing nursing and midwifery leadership and health system resilience across the Americas. The gaps identified point to clear priorities for action. Governments and regional stakeholders must create an enabling environment by institutionalizing GCNMO roles at senior levels within ministries of health, ensuring that GCNMOs have authority, resources and policy engagement opportunities to place nursing leadership at the center of building resilient and equitable health systems (7).

Targeted professional development is needed especially with regards to policy influence and engagement, while mentorship and succession planning are essential to prepare the next generation of leaders. Regional collaboration can facilitate knowledge exchange and solidarity, helping to harmonize approaches while respecting the need for tailored, localized models of leadership. At a broader level, advancing the executive and advisory models will ensure that nursing leadership has both the institutional authority to act locally and the influence to contribute internationally.

The professionalization of nursing through the implementation of competency frameworks and strategic mandates marks a reclaiming of authority and legitimacy, and challenges historical subordination while positioning nurses as central actors in health system transformation (6, 10). However, sustained progress requires Regional voices to actively shape reforms and dismantle established hierarchies of knowledge and power. GCNMOs must also take a more proactive role in national reporting processes and in strengthening information systems for human resources for health, thereby improving the generation and use of data to support evidence-informed policy- and decision-making.

Study limitations and contributions

GCNMO positions remain insufficiently documented in official reports and are rarely subject to systematic evaluation, limiting visibility of their actions and impact. This underscores the need to consistently track data over time. This study provides valuable insights into the profiles of GCNMOs in the Americas. Although the survey achieved a high response rate, its cross-sectional design does not allow for an analysis of how roles may evolve over time. In addition, the reliance on self-reported measures raises issues of potential bias in the competency and performance assessments. Furthermore, a fuller understanding of the barriers and enablers to policy engagement requires perspectives from diverse policy actors beyond nurses (19), which should be the focus of future research.

Participants’ self-assessments may be influenced by their specific perceptions and contexts, thus limiting the generalizability of these results to other settings. Furthermore, historical, social and cultural factors cannot always be fully captured through surveys, which may leave some root causes of low political participation unexplored.

Despite these challenges, the study concludes that the empowerment and political inclusion of leaders in nursing and midwifery are essential to strengthen health systems and expand the impact of the profession in the public sphere, which underscores the need for training and policy structures that favor greater nursing representation and leadership in decision-making processes

CONCLUSIONS

The role of GCNMOs is established in most countries of the Americas, but further action is needed to increase their leadership competencies, expand their political influence, and strengthen their strategic government and global health functions. Competencies related to political participation, evidence-informed policy-making, and global health leadership are critical areas to that must be addressed. Governments and other relevant stakeholders must create enabling environments for GCNMOs by ensuring they have access to adequate resources, establishing appropriate placement within government structures and granting appropriate authority in decision-making processes. Strengthening the role of GCNMOs is central to achieving resilient and equitable health systems.
