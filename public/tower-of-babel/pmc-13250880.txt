From research to routine care: implementation of guided digital therapy with Spring PTSD
Authors: Catrin Lewis, Neil J. Kitchiner, Neil P. Roberts, Jonathan I. Bisson
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground: Cognitive behavioural therapy with a trauma-focus (CBT-TF) is a recommended first-line treatment for post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD), but access is limited by high costs and logistical barriers. Guided digital CBT-TF offers a scalable and accessible alternative.Objective: To evaluate the real-world effectiveness of Spring PTSD, a guided digital CBT-TF, within routine National Health Service (NHS) practice.Method: Adults with a primary diagnosis of PTSD were offered Spring PTSD with brief therapist guidance. Outcomes were assessed via an online survey that measured PTSD symptoms (International Trauma Questionnaire; ITQ), psychological distress (Clinical Outcomes in Routine Evaluation – Outcome Measure; CORE-OM), functional impairment (Work and Social Adjustment Scale; WSAS), subjective distress (Subjective Units of Distress Scale; SUDS), and satisfaction (Client Satisfaction Questionnaire; CSQ-8). Pre-to-post-treatment changes were analysed using paired t-tests or Wilcoxon signed-rank tests, as appropriate. Analyses were conducted for the sub-sample who self-reported completing treatment and the full sample, with Multiple Imputation (MI) and Last Observation Carried Forward (LOCF).Results: Data from 460 participants were analysed. Significant reductions were observed from pre-to post-treatment in PTSD symptoms, psychological distress, functional impairment, and subjective distress in the full sample (using MI and LOCF) and the sub-sample who self-reported having finished treatment. Satisfaction was high among those who reported finishing treatment, with the majority rating the programme as excellent or good. However, substantial missing data were noted, primarily due to reliance on therapists for demographic data collection and governance restrictions limiting the collection of identifiable information during this service evaluation.Conclusions: Findings support the feasibility, scalability, and acceptability of guided digital CBT-TF. However, the substantial missing data and limited ability to assess engagement highlight the challenges of collecting real-world effectiveness data outside of research. Future service evaluations should prioritise more robust and integrated data collection processes to improve the quality and utility of real-world evidence.

Introduction

Post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD) is a debilitating mental health condition that can develop following exposure to traumatic events (APA, 2013). It is characterised by intrusive re-experiencing, avoidance, hyperarousal, and negative alterations in mood and cognition (APA, 2013). Cognitive behavioural therapy with a trauma focus (CBT-TF) is widely recognised as a first-line treatment for PTSD, and it is well supported by a robust evidence base (Lewis et al., 2020). However, access to face-to-face CBT-TF is often limited by systemic barriers such as shortages of trained therapists, high treatment costs, and limited service-capacity, as well as the logistical difficulties of attending in-person sessions (Lewis & Olff, 2020).

Guided digital CBT-TF has been developed to address many of these barriers while maintaining clinical effectiveness (Lewis et al., 2019). Delivered via digital platforms with structured therapist support, this approach offers a more flexible and scalable alternative to traditional face-to-face therapy. Spring PTSD is one such guided digital CBT-TF programme, designed to increase access to evidence-based PTSD treatment without compromising clinical outcomes (Bisson et al., 2022; Lewis et al., 2013; Lewis et al., 2017).

Early development of Spring PTSD included pilot studies that produced promising results (Lewis et al., 2013), followed by a feasibility randomised controlled trial (RCT) that demonstrated its superiority to a waitlist (Lewis et al., 2017). Building on this evidence, the RAPID pragmatic, multicentre, non-inferiority RCT showed that Spring PTSD was equivalent to face-to-face CBT-TF in reducing clinician-rated PTSD symptoms at 16 weeks, with improvements maintained at 52-week follow-up (Bisson et al., 2022), for individuals with mild to moderate severity PTSD to a single traumatic experience.

This evidence fits within a growing body of research supporting guided digital CBT-TF more broadly. A Cochrane review of digital cognitive and behavioural therapies for PTSD, including 13 RCTs with 808 participants, found that therapist-guided digital interventions were associated with clinically meaningful reductions in PTSD symptoms compared with waitlist controls (Simon et al., 2021). Following this, both the RAPID study of Spring PTSD and the STOP-PTSD trial of iCT-PTSD, another guided digital intervention, demonstrated that guided digital CBT-TF can produce substantial reductions in PTSD, anxiety, and depressive symptoms (Bisson et al., 2022; Ehlers et al., 2023). These two interventions have been provisionally recommended by the UK National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (NICE) (National Institute for Health and Care Excellence, 2024).

Although the efficacy of Spring PTSD has been demonstrated in RCTs, these results were obtained under controlled research conditions. Based on this evidence, Spring PTSD was recommended by the National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (NICE) for use within the NHS to further evaluate its effectiveness in routine clinical settings (NICE, 2023). This service evaluation therefore examines the real-world implementation and outcomes of Spring PTSD in NHS practice, providing insight into its effectiveness and feasibility outside of research.

Method

Design

Using a within-subjects design, this service evaluation was conducted within National Health Service (NHS) Wales. Ethical approval was not required; however, regulatory approval was obtained from all participating NHS Health Boards.

Eligibility

Participants were adults aged 18 years or older with a clinician-determined primary diagnosis of PTSD, who were assessed by their therapist as suitable for guided digital therapy with Spring PTSD. Suitability was based on PTSD symptoms primarily related to a single traumatic event, fluency in English, regular internet access, and no prior completion of trauma-focused therapy for the current PTSD. Individuals with current psychosis, substance dependence, or active suicidal ideation were not considered suitable. Completion of the pre-treatment International Trauma Questionnaire (ITQ) (Simon et al., 2021) was required for inclusion in the analysis, regardless of treatment completion status.

Intervention

Spring PTSD is an eight-step, eight-week guided digital CBT-TF (Bisson et al., 2022). The programme covers psychoeducation, grounding, anxiety management, behavioural activation, trauma-focused exposure, cognitive restructuring, and relapse prevention, delivered digitally with therapist guidance. After a one-hour introductory session, participants work through the programme independently with four planned 30-minuite guidance sessions and four planned brief check-ins with a therapist by telephone or email, with therapist monitoring via a clinician dashboard. Full details, including programme content and features, are provided in Supplement 1.

Therapists

Therapists were clinicians working within NHS Wales mental health services that provide psychological therapies. As Wales does not operate within the NHS Talking Therapies programme used in England, clinicians were recruited from a range of NHS mental health services delivering psychological therapy. Clinicians came from diverse professional backgrounds, including counselling, nursing, psychology, social work and midwifery. Most had no prior training in CBT-TF.

Clinicians received up to seven hours of specific training in the Spring PTSD intervention through two half day workshops prior to delivering the programme. Training introduced the programme rationale, theoretical basis, and structure, followed by therapist and patient manual walkthroughs and role-playing exercises led by the trainer. After the workshop, therapists practised delivering the intervention in pairs and subsequently participated in fortnightly clinical group supervision sessions to support delivery of the programme.

Data collection

Outcome data were collected via online questionnaires completed by participants as part of a service evaluation of Spring PTSD. Participants who were actively engaged in the programme were sent a link to complete these questionnaires by their therapist. Governance restrictions prevented linkage to wider NHS Wales clinical records. Self-report outcome measures were completed through the online survey platform Alchemer (Alchemer Survey Platform, 2025). Therapists documented demographic information (including age and gender), which were later integrated with the survey data.

Measures

The primary outcome was PTSD symptom severity, assessed with the ITQ (Cloitre et al., 2018), which was administered at baseline, prior to each guidance session, and at post-treatment. Current distress was assessed with a 0-10 Subjective Units of Distress Scale (SUDS), also administered at baseline, each guidance session, and post-treatment. Broader psychological distress was measured with the Clinical Outcomes in Routine Evaluation – Outcome Measure (CORE-OM) (Evans et al., 2002), and functional impairment with the Work and Social Adjustment Scale (WSAS) (Mundt et al., 2002); both were collected at baseline and post-treatment. Participant satisfaction was measured post-treatment using the Client Satisfaction Questionnaire (CSQ-8) (Attkisson & Zwick, 1982). In this evaluation, ‘post-treatment’ was operationalised as the timepoint at which participants self-reported that they had finished therapy, regardless of whether the programme was fully completed.

Statistical analysis

Analyses were conducted using Stata 18.0 (StataCorp., 2023). Descriptive statistics summarised sample characteristics and satisfaction ratings. Pre-post changes in continuous outcomes were examined using paired t-tests or Wilcoxon signed-rank tests, depending on data distribution. Missing post-treatment outcome data were addressed using multiple imputation by chained equations (MICE). Twenty imputed datasets were generated using Stata’s mi impute chained command. Only outcome variables were included in the imputation model, as potential auxiliary variables contained a high proportion of missing data. Parameter estimates were pooled across imputations using Rubin’s rules. We also conducted analyses using a Last Observation Carried Forward (LOCF) approach. LOCF was applied to participants with baseline data to create a complete dataset. For measures with multiple interim assessments (ITQ, SUDS), the most recent available score was carried forward when post-treatment data were missing. For measures collected only at baseline and post-treatment (CORE-OM, WSAS), the baseline score was substituted if no post-treatment value was available.

Results

Sample characteristics

A total of 460 participants were included in the analysis. A total of 460 participants were included in the analysis. These were individuals deemed suitable for Spring PTSD who agreed to take part in the service evaluation and completed the pre-treatment ITQ, regardless of whether they subsequently completed the programme. No information was available on the total number of patients initially offered the intervention.

Gender data were available for 244 participants, with most identifying as female (66%, n = 161). Age was reported by 236 participants (51.1%), with a mean of 39.5 years (SD = 12.7). Primary trauma exposure was reported by 455 participants (see Table 1), with the most common types being birth trauma (15.2%, n = 69), transportation accident (10.1%, n = 46), sudden unexpected death of someone close (10.1%, n = 46), and physical assault (5.7%, n = 26). Most participants (79.2%) reported trauma within the past five years, while smaller proportions reported trauma 5–10 years ago (7.0%), 10–20 years ago (8.5%), or more than 20 years ago (5.3%). Sensitivity analyses for missing data (LOCF and MI)

Analyses using both LOCF and MI produced consistent results across all outcome measures. All findings remained statistically significant, indicating that the improvements observed in the completer sample were robust to missing data.

Participant satisfaction

Participant satisfaction was assessed using the CSQ-8, completed only by those who reported finishing therapy with Spring PTSD (Table 3). Of the 460 participants who started treatment, 166 (36.0%) completed the CSQ-8; data for the remainder were missing. Overall satisfaction was high across all domains. Most participants rated the quality of treatment as Excellent (78.3%) or Good (19.3%). The majority felt they received the treatment they wanted (71.1% ‘Yes, definitely’; 24.7% ‘Yes, generally’), and that their needs were met (62.1% ‘Almost all’; 28.3% ‘Most’). Willingness to recommend was strong, with 83.1% responding ‘Yes, definitely’ and 14.5% ‘Yes, I think so.’ Similarly, 80.1% were very satisfied with the amount of support received, and 79.5% reported treatment helped ‘a great deal.’ Overall satisfaction was very high (84.3% very satisfied; 13.3% mostly satisfied), and 75.3% stated they would definitely return for treatment if needed. Discussion

Main findings

This service evaluation examined the real-world effectiveness and implementation of Spring PTSD, a guided digital CBT-TF programme, within routine NHS clinical practice. Findings demonstrated substantial reductions in PTSD and DSO symptoms, psychological distress, functional impairment, and SUDs across the full analytic sample (using both MI and LOCF) and among those who self-reported having finished treatment. These symptom improvements are consistent with prior research on Spring PTSD in RCTs Lewis et al., 2017; Bisson et al., 2022), providing evidence that its therapeutic benefits extend beyond research settings into real-world clinical environments.

High levels of treatment satisfaction among those who finished therapy support the acceptability of Spring PTSD. Most participants rated the programme as excellent or good, reported that their needs were met, and indicated they would recommend the treatment to others (Table 2).

Key limitations

Satisfaction data were collected only from participants who self-reported having finished therapy; those who discontinued or did not provide post-treatment data may have experienced lower satisfaction levels. Consequently, findings regarding acceptability should be interpreted with caution.

A further limitation was the substantial amount of missing data. Collection of demographic data relied on therapist capturing and entering it. High staff turnover, heavy caseloads, and low prioritisation of data collection contributed to incomplete records. Governance requirements also restricted clinical outcomes to a pseudo-anonymised survey, limiting the ability to link participant- and therapist-level data and preventing detailed reporting on engagement or therapist input. We were unable to track what happened to participants after completing or discontinuing the programme. This includes whether individuals were stepped up to alternative NHS interventions. As a result, our findings cannot speak to longer-term outcomes or subsequent care pathways beyond the immediate treatment period.

To address missing outcome data, we conducted analyses using both MI and LOCF. Neither approach is without limitations. LOCF is likely to be overly pessimistic, as it assumes no change after the last observed measurement. MI relies on the assumption that data are missing at random, but in this real-world NHS context, it was difficult to evaluate whether this assumption held. Furthermore, the MI model did not include auxiliary variables such as age, sex, or treatment engagement measures because these were often missing, which may have reduced the robustness of the imputed estimates. Despite these limitations, both MI and LOCF analyses produced consistent and statistically significant results across all outcomes, suggesting that observed improvements were robust.

Despite these challenges, the available data provide valuable insights into the use and outcomes of Spring PTSD in routine practice, demonstrating that the programme can deliver meaningful clinical improvements. Work is now underway to enable integrated collection of participant and therapist data directly via the Spring platform, allowing for more complete, linked information on engagement, therapist input, and outcomes.

Implications for practice and policy

The findings have important implications for NHS mental health service delivery. Guided digital CBT-TF offers a pragmatic and scalable solution to longstanding barriers to evidence-based PTSD treatment, including therapist shortages, and limited service-capacity, as part of a stepped or matched care or pathway. Spring PTSD has the potential to substantially reduce therapist time compared with traditional CBT-TF while maintaining clinical effectiveness. This model may therefore help services increase capacity without compromising treatment quality.

Integrating digital CBT-TF into stepped-care pathways could optimise resource allocation, preferencing intensive face-to-face therapy for individuals with more complex presentations or those who do not respond to guided digital treatment. However, successful implementation will require investment in digital infrastructure, clinician training, and integrated data systems to embed quality assurance and continuous evaluation.

Overall, this evaluation demonstrates that Spring PTSD can deliver clinically meaningful improvements when implemented in real-world NHS settings by training individuals with low levels of psychological therapy training, supporting its scalability and feasibility as an accessible, evidence-based intervention for adults with PTSD related to a single traumatic event.
