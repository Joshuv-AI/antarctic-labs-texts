jaxFMM: An Adaptive, GPU-Parallel Implementation of the Fast Multipole Method in JAX Preprint: APS/123-QED
Robert Kraft, Florian Bruckner, Dieter Suess, Claas Abert

Abstract
We introduce jaxFMM, an open-source, adaptive, highly parallel point-charge Fast Multipole Method implementation for the Laplace kernel written in JAX. It is based on a non-uniform refinement strategy, which results in extremely concise and simple code. Benchmarks show that the algorithm performs well even for highly non-uniform charge distributions. JaxFMM already massively speeds up stray-field computations in micromagnetics and with JAX features like autodiff, novel applications such as inverse-design problems and machine-learning tasks can be tackled with ease in the future.



I
Introduction

Since its inception in 1987
7
, the Fast Multipole Method (FMM) has been successfully used to massively speed up a variety of numerical tasks. This ranges from the particle simulations the algorithm was initially designed to solve, to convolutions with arbitrary kernels via kernel-independent volume FMM formulations
17
or even purely algebraic use cases such as fast matrix multiplication
18
. Implementations of the algorithm must maintain a fine balance between far- and near-field interactions to maximize performance for a given accuracy. Furthermore, while the FMM can be parallelized efficiently, adaptive variants in particular require careful consideration to remain efficient. As a result, performant yet flexible FMM codes are often rather involved and difficult to develop. Notable examples here include exafmm
16
, pvfmm
11
and scalfmm
3
.

With the rise of machine learning, Python libraries such as pytorch
13
or JAX
4
seek to offer efficient GPU-parallel execution with comparatively little coding effort. Increasingly, this is also exploited for non-machine-learning tasks (see e.g. the "Awesome JAX"
2
list of software using JAX). Another benefit of such frameworks is the availability of automatic differentiation, which can be used to compute gradients for inverse-design problems. In this work, we detail the implementation of jaxFMM, an adaptive point charge FMM library for the Laplace kernel written in JAX. Through JAX’s just-in-time compilation, only roughly 600 lines of code yield an adaptive and GPU-parallel implementation of the FMM, with additional features such as periodic boundary conditions or arbitrary evaluation positions.

We begin with a general description of the FMM, followed by the unique aspects of our implementation and finish with benchmarks showing the performance of jaxFMM.

II
Algorithm and Implementation

The Fast Multipole Method approximates a potential
u
arising from the convolutional integral over a domain
\Omega
,

\displaystyle u(\bm{r})=\int_{\Omega}K(\bm{r}-\bm{r}^{\prime})\rho(\bm{r}^{\prime})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\bm{r}^{\prime},

(1)

where
K
is referred to as the kernel and
\rho
is the source density. Here, the underlying idea of the algorithm is to hierarchically form and recombine expansions which approximate the potential caused by sources at long distances. Then, wherever the error bound permits, expansions are used to compute contributions to the potential, which drastically decreases the total amount of interactions that must be computed. JaxFMM currently only supports solving Poisson’s equation for point charges, i.e.

\displaystyle K(\bm{r},\bm{r}^{\prime})

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{4\pi}\frac{1}{\lVert\bm{r}-\bm{r}^{\prime}\rVert},

(2)

\displaystyle\rho(\bm{r}^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{N}q_{i}\cdot\delta(\bm{r}^{\prime}-\bm{r}_{i}),

(3)

where
N
charges
q_{i}
are placed at positions
\bm{r}_{i}
and
\delta
is the Dirac delta function. For the Laplacian kernel (
2
), expansions are commonly done in spherical coordinates
\bm{r}=(r,\theta,\varphi)
using spherical harmonic functions
Y_{n}^{m}
:

\displaystyle K(\bm{r},\bm{r}^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\sum_{m=-n}^{n}R^{-m}_{n}(\bm{r}^{\prime})S^{m}_{n}(\bm{r}),

(4)

\displaystyle\quad R^{m}_{n}(\bm{r})

\displaystyle=r^{n}Y_{n}^{m}(\theta,\varphi),

(5)

\displaystyle\quad S^{m}_{n}(\bm{r})

\displaystyle=\frac{Y_{n}^{m}(\theta,\varphi)}{r^{n+1}}.

(6)

Here, we refer to
R^{m}_{n}
as the regular basis and
S^{m}_{n}
as the singular basis. Inserting (
3
) and (
4
) into (
1
) and truncating the series at finite order
p
leads to
multipole expansions
of the form

\displaystyle u(\bm{r})

\displaystyle\approx-\frac{1}{4\pi}\sum_{n=0}^{p}\sum_{m=-n}^{n}C^{m}_{n}S^{m}_{n}(\bm{r}),

(7)

\displaystyle C_{n}^{m}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{N}q_{i}R_{n}^{-m}(\bm{r}_{i}),

(8)

with multipole coefficients
C_{n}^{m}
.
It can be shown that the error of expansion (
7
) scales as
(a/r)^{p+1}
for evaluation positions
\bm{r}
outside a ball with radius
a
around the origin, where
a
refers to the distance of the charge furthest from the origin
8
. On the other hand, one can also generate a
local expansion
,

\displaystyle u(\bm{r})

\displaystyle\approx-\frac{1}{4\pi}\sum_{n=0}^{p}\sum_{m=-n}^{n}D^{m}_{n}R^{m}_{n}(\bm{r}),

(9)

\displaystyle D_{n}^{m}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{N}q_{i}S_{n}^{-m}(\bm{r}_{i}),

(10)

with local coefficients
D_{n}^{m}
. The local expansion error scales like
(r/a)^{p+1}
inside the ball with radius
a
, where
a
now represents the distance of the charge closest to the origin.

The centers of multipole/local expansions can be shifted with so-called multipole-to-multipole (M2M) and local-to-local (L2L) transformation operators. Additionally, it is possible to transform multipole expansions into local expansions at another position with multipole-to-local (M2L) transformations. We refer interested readers to
8
for the definitions and error bounds of these operators in case of kernel (
2
). Armed with the above expansions and transformation operators, the FMM now approximates (
1
) as follows:

1.

Generate an octree hierarchy by enclosing the charges with a minimum bounding cube and recursively generating new levels by splitting each
parent
cube into 8 equally sized
children
. Repeat until the number of charges in each box is lower than a specified threshold.

2.

On the finest level, compute initial multipole expansion coefficients around the center of each box (
8
) with the charges contained therein.

3.

"Going down": Obtain parent multipole coefficients by M2M transforming multipole expansions of children to the center of their parent and adding their coefficients. Repeat until reaching the coarsest level.

4.

"Going up": Generate local expansions by M2L transforming and summing the multipole expansions of the children of nearest-neighbor parents, excluding nearest neighbors on the same level. Then, distribute parent local expansions to children via L2L transformations. Repeat until reaching the finest level.

5.

For every box on the finest level, use the obtained local expansion coefficients to compute the far field contributions and evaluate the remaining near field contributions from the nearest neighbors directly with (
1
).

Figure
1
shows a schematic FMM setup in two dimensions, illustrating the role of M2M/M2L/L2L transformations. The truncation error of the above FMM algorithm decreases exponentially with the expansion order
p

14
and the computational cost is linear in the number of charges
8
, a significant improvement over the direct computation with quadratic scaling. As initially mentioned, there are many variants of the FMM, extending its applicability and efficiency. We continue by discussing the unique aspects of our implementation.

Figure 1
:
Schematic 2D-FMM setup: Combining multipoles (blue) into parent via M2M, adding them to locals (orange) via M2L and distributing locals to children via L2L.

II.1
Hierarchy Generation

The original formulation of the FMM uses a uniform refinement strategy for its hierarchy, i.e. boxes in the hierarchy are always cubes and split into 8 equally sized children. As a result, the algorithm is not adaptive: For the same number of charges
N
, non-homogenous charge distributions can increase the computational effort significantly since the number of charges contained in each simulation box will vary considerably. In fact, any charge distribution that does not conform to the cubic shape of the initial simulation box quickly results in many empty boxes which do not contribute to the result at all and should therefore be ignored.

To counter this increase in cost for such geometries or non-uniform charge distributions, we follow a non-uniform splitting approach first published in
6
: Instead of requiring strictly cubic simulation boxes, we allow rectangles of arbitrary shape and size and split not in the geometric center, but at the median value of the coordinates. More specifically, boxes are subdivided as follows:

1.

Find the longest side of the box along the principal axes by examining maxima and minima of the particle positions in the box.

2.

Split the box perpendicular to the corresponding axis direction by finding the median value of the particle coordinates on this axis and partitioning the position array into a half lower than the median and a half greater than the median.

3.

Repeat the above procedure for each half. The depth of this recursion is limited to a user-defined number of splits
s
per level, such that each box has exactly
2^{s}
children.

As can be seen above, we permit an arbitrary number of children per box and split perpendicular to the longest side of the box. The parameter
s
can then be used to e.g. balance the cost of near- and far-field interactions. Note that currently,
s
is set globally, i.e. every box is split into the same number of children. Furthermore, the default value in jaxFMM is
s=3
, similar to the uniform splitting case. Figure
2
shows an example hierarchy generated by jaxFMM.

Figure 2
:
From left to right: Double helix point distribution, jaxFMM hierarchy levels
0-3
(
s=3
splits per box).

The non-uniform splitting algorithm in this form has the following advantages/disadvantages:

+

Straightforward implementation.

+

The resulting
2^{s}
-tree hierarchy is
balanced
, i.e. each simulation box contains almost exactly the same number of charges. In fact, boxes contain either
\lceil N/2^{l\cdot s}\rceil
(ceiling) or
\lfloor N/2^{l\cdot s}\rfloor
(floor) particles on level
l
.

+

As a byproduct of the hierarchy generation process, the data is intrinsically arranged like a Z-order curve, which improves data locality
12
.

+

\mathcal{O}(N\log N)
average complexity (
\mathcal{O}(\log N)
levels with
\mathcal{O}(N)
average complexity argpartitions each).

-

Boxes may have poor aspect ratio in some cases, increasing the number of interactions that must be computed.

-

Interaction lists must be generated and stored.

-

Efficiently precomputing transformation operators is no longer possible since box shapes vary.

Overall, compared to other adaptive FMM variants, the non-uniform splitting is very simple to implement whilst still yielding balanced trees. However, at the same time it loses access to precomputed transformation operators which are required for e.g. faster transformations or efficient kernel-independent formulations.

II.2
Interaction Lists

For the generation of local expansions on the finest level, the FMM uses the largest possible multipole expansions that still satisfy an error bound to save computational cost. In the case of a uniform refinement FMM, the interaction partners of each box are known ahead of time, since the shape of the boxes and their relative positions are always the same. Non-uniform splitting however requires the computation of interaction lists. To determine if two boxes are well-separated, we employ the criterion from
6
,

\displaystyle R+\theta r\leq\theta d,

(11)

where
\theta
is a parameter controlling the error set by the user,
R
refers to the radius of the larger box,
r
to the radius of the smaller box and
d
to the distance between their centers. We use this condition to generate interaction lists as follows:

1.

Starting on the coarsest level, compare every box with all other boxes using (
11
). Every box that fulfills the criterion gets added to the list of weakly coupled boxes on this level. The interactions of weakly coupled boxes will be computed via expansions later on.

2.

Proceed to the next level and repeat the above, but ignore the children of all well-separated parent boxes, as indicated by the previously generated interaction list. Do this until reaching the finest level.

3.

At the finest level, some boxes that are not weakly coupled will remain. They are added to a list of strongly coupled boxes, whose interactions will be computed directly later on.

In
6
, it is shown that the relative error
\varepsilon
of the
p
-th order FMM solution
u_{p}
compared to the analytic potential
u
then is bounded as follows:

\varepsilon=\left|\frac{u-u_{p}}{u}\right|\leq C\frac{\theta^{p+1}}{(1-\theta)^{2}}\,,

(12)

where
C
is a constant.
By examining the geometry of a nearest-neighbor uniform FMM splitting, we see that
\theta=\sqrt{3}/(4-\sqrt{3})\approx 0.7637
in this case, which is why we choose a similar value (
\theta=0.77
) as the default in jaxFMM.

The interaction list generation can be generalized further to allow separate sources and targets, i.e. evaluating the potential at positions different from the charge positions. In this case, two hierarchies must be generated, one for the sources and one for the targets. Then, source boxes are compared with target boxes using (
11
) to generate the interaction lists. Note however that the number of levels in the source and target hierarchy are not necessarily the same and hence it no longer makes sense to only compare boxes on the same level. In other words, we now allow cross-level interactions such as M2L-transforming multipole expansions at source level
l_{\text{src}}=0
into local expansions at target level
l_{\text{trg}}=1
. While comparing all source levels with all target levels would certainly be optimal, it would also be prohibitively expensive. Therefore, we start at
l_{\text{src}},l_{\text{trg}}=0
and then proceed as follows:

1.

Increase the level with larger average box radius.

2.

If the average radii are equal, increase the source level.

3.

If there are no more source levels, increase the target level and vice versa.

4.

Quit if all interactions have been accounted for or if there are no more levels available.

The above algorithm aims to decrease
R
in (
11
), ensuring that levels with similar box sizes are compared. We can also apply this level-marching scheme in the case where the source and target points are the same, which actually decreases the total number of interactions that must be computed. For example, in Figure
3
, we show all the source boxes considered for a given target box (red) in the double helix hierarchy of Figure
2
. The boxes are colored by the target level at which they are included in the local expansion via M2L transformation, from level 1 (dark blue) to level 3 (off-white). Orange boxes are not included in the local expansion, their contribution to the potential is evaluated directly instead. Including boxes at the earliest possible target level avoids handling their children individually and thus decreases the number of interactions. Cross-level interactions facilitate this, which is clearly visible by the increase in dark and light blue boxes in Figure

II.3
Basis Functions and Transformations

Laplace-kernel FMM is generally based on complex-valued spherical harmonic basis functions (
5
,
6
). However, it is straightforward to show that
C^{-m}_{n}=\bar{C}^{m}_{n}
and
D^{-m}_{n}=\bar{D}^{m}_{n}
, i.e. expansion coefficients of opposite sign in
m
are complex conjugates. Therefore, it is possible to either only save half of the coefficients or to use a real-valued basis instead. As shown in
9
, recursion relations are available for cheaply computing a real-valued regular basis (
A.1
). Similar recursion relations can not only be derived for the singular basis (
A.2
), but also for the gradient of the regular basis (
A.3
), which can be used to directly compute fields instead of potentials.

Due to the non-uniform shape and position of the boxes in the hierarchy, pre-computing fast transformation operators such as e.g.
\mathcal{O}(p^{3})
rotation based operators
8
is too expensive in terms of memory: Operators must be computed for every single interaction instead of one, relatively small reoccurring set. Therefore, we simply adapt the
\mathcal{O}(p^{4})
transformations from the original FMM publication
8
to our real basis (
B.1
-
B.3
). For completeness, we note that we generate the initial expansions using
R^{m}_{n}
instead of
R^{-m}_{n}
and then evaluate the local expansions with
(-1)^{n}\cdot R^{m}_{n}
(for
m=0
) or
2\cdot(-1)^{n}\cdot R^{m}_{n}
(for
m\neq 0
).

II.4
JAX Implementation

JAX’s just-in-time (jit) compilation enables simple and concise code but relies on static array sizes. Therefore, we must pad the particle coordinates and charges sorted by box, such that every box contains exactly the same number of particles. Because the tree is balanced, only a very small amount of padding is inserted here: at most one particle per box. Additionally, the interaction lists are padded such that every box has the same number of weakly/strongly connected interaction partners for a given source and target level pair. Depending on the source distribution, the amount of padding inserted here can become significantly large.

Every step of the algorithm is then compatible with jit-compilation, except for the interaction list generation: The maximum number of interaction partners varies and depends on the particle positions, neither of which is allowed in a jit-compiled function. However, if the particle positions remain constant, the hierarchy information can be cached and reused for different charges. For example, Listing

III
Benchmarks

In the following section, we evaluate the performance of jaxFMM for a variety of charge distributions, hardware configurations and parameter settings. Computations take place in single precision on the Vienna Scientific Cluster VSC5, where GPU nodes are equipped with Nvidia A100 (40GB) cards and CPU nodes use AMD Epyc 7713 processors
1
. The GPU benchmarks are limited to one GPU, since distribution of data across multiple GPUs is not yet supported. Also, the CPU benchmarks use only one CPU core to investigate sequential performance. Furthermore, the jaxFMM version used for the benchmarks can be found under the
"
v0.0.6_p
"
tag in the GitLab repository
10
.

Particle numbers are varied from
N=2^{11}
to
N=2^{25}
in 14 even logarithmic steps and a maximum of
N_{\text{max}}=128
particles are allowed per simulation box on the finest level. Additionally, we investigate four different parameter sets:

1) The jaxFMM default:

\displaystyle p=3,\,\theta=0.77,\,s=3

2) Higher expansion order:

\displaystyle p=6,\,\theta=0.77,\,s=3

3) Lower theta-parameter:

\displaystyle p=3,\,\theta=0.50,\,s=3

4) Lower splitting parameter:

\displaystyle p=3,\,\theta=0.77,\,s=2

Direct solutions are also computed for
N<10^{6}
to determine the relative L2-norm error of the FMM solutions. The reported runtimes are the minimum runtimes from 100 repeated potential evaluations. Finally, we note that some data points are missing due to running out of memory for temporary arrays. This issue is much more pronounced for CPU runs or nonuniform distributions but will be addressed in a future version of jaxFMM.

IV
Conclusions and Outlook

In summary, we achieved an extremely concise, adaptive and highly parallel FMM implementation by leveraging JAX’s just-in-time compilation. The code performs well, even for non-uniform charge distributions and performance can be fine-tuned through a variety of parameters. With JAX-intrinsic features like autodiff, exciting future applications such as coupling the FMM solver to inverse-design problems or machine-learning tasks can also be handled with ease.

One major drawback currently is long setup times due to jit-compilation. However, numerous improvements to the code are already in development: Fast Wigner-matrix assembly routines like those presented in
5
;
15
can be used to compute rotation matrices on-the-fly for efficient
\mathcal{O}(p^{3})
rotation-based transformations. This is expected to significantly decrease compilation- and runtimes, particularly for high expansion orders
p
. Furthermore, the splitting could be generalized even further, for example each level
l
could have its own number of splits
s_{l}
. As a result, complicated geometries would be handled much more efficiently. Additionally, multi-GPU parallelism could be implemented via the
jax.sharding
module. Finally, volume or kernel independent FMM formulations might be investigated in the future.

In closing, we remark that jaxFMM is primarily developed for fast stray-field evaluations in micromagnetic finite-element simulation codes. It enables dynamic simulations of very large systems with tens of millions of elements. A corresponding publication, which will also report on improvements to the underlying FMM algorithm is forthcoming. JaxFMM is open-source on GitLab
10
.
