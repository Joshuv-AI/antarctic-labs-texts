Effects of narrative nursing on delirium and outcomes in mechanically ventilated ICU patients: a randomized trial
Authors: Yan Lei, Yun Song, Yongxing Wang, Jing Liu, Dan Liu, Chenjing Shi, Hua He, Binghua Zhang, Yue Cai
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveTo evaluate whether a structured narrative nursing intervention reduces the incidence and duration of delirium, improves clinical outcomes, and increases patient satisfaction in mechanically ventilated intensive care unit (ICU) patients.MethodsIn this single-center, randomized controlled trial, 88 mechanically ventilated ICU patients were randomly assigned, by a computer-generated sequence, to an intervention group (n = 44) or a control group (n = 44). The control group received standard ICU care. In addition to standard care, the intervention group received a daily 30-min structured narrative nursing session throughout the period of mechanical ventilation. The intervention comprised four components: externalization of problems, deconstruction, re-authoring, and witnessing. The primary outcome was delirium incidence, assessed with the Confusion Assessment Method for the ICU (CAM-ICU). Secondary outcomes included ICU length of stay, duration of mechanical ventilation, ICU mortality, patient satisfaction, and Acute Physiology and Chronic Health Evaluation II (APACHE II) score. As this study evaluated standard nursing care interventions without the use of investigational drugs or devices, prospective clinical trial registration was not required.ResultsThe overall incidence of delirium was significantly lower in the intervention group compared with the control group (34.1% vs. 63.6%, P < 0.001), with the most pronounced reduction observed in hypoactive delirium (20.5% vs. 40.9%, P = 0.02). Both the onset and duration of delirium were markedly shortened in the intervention group (P < 0.05). Compared with controls, patients receiving narrative nursing exhibited shorter ICU stays and fewer days on mechanical ventilation (P < 0.05), a reduced ICU mortality rate (6.8% vs. 22.7%, P = 0.035), lower SOFA scores (P = 0.019), and significantly higher nursing satisfaction (P < 0.001). Multivariable logistic regression identified narrative nursing as an independent protective factor against delirium (OR = 0.27, 95% CI: 0.10–0.70, P = 0.008). Subgroup analysis by age demonstrated that the protective effect was particularly evident in older patients (≥65 years), where the intervention markedly reduced the risk of delirium (OR = 0.13, 95% CI: 0.04–0.48, P = 0.002). In contrast, among younger patients, the main benefit was reflected in improved satisfaction with nursing care.ConclusionA structured narrative nursing intervention reduced the incidence and duration of delirium among mechanically ventilated ICU patients, improved selected clinical outcomes, and increased patient satisfaction. Limitations include the single-center design, modest sample size, and limited blinding, which may introduce bias. Multicenter, adequately powered, prospectively registered randomized trials are warranted to confirm these findings.

Introduction

Mechanical ventilation is a cornerstone of life support for critically ill patients with acute respiratory failure, particularly those with severe pneumonia, acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS), or multi-organ failure [1]. Delirium is among the most frequent and serious neuropsychiatric complications in this setting, characterized by acute cognitive impairment, inattention, and fluctuating levels of consciousness [2]. Reported prevalence in the intensive care unit (ICU) varies widely, ranging from 20 to 80%, and is especially high among ventilated patients [3]. Beyond prolonging the duration of ventilation and length of stay, delirium substantially increases healthcare utilization, with median incremental costs per patient estimated at nearly USD 18,000 within 30 days [4]. Moreover, delirium has been linked to long-term cognitive decline, psychological trauma, and reduced quality of life, underscoring the urgent need for effective preventive strategies in critical care [5].

Conventional approaches to delirium prevention and management have focused on pharmacologic interventions, including antipsychotics and sedatives, as well as physiological measures such as sedation protocols and early mobilization. However, pharmacologic efficacy remains limited, and concerns regarding adverse drug effects restrict their use [6]. Consequently, attention has shifted toward non-pharmacological interventions. Strategies emphasizing psychosocial support and environmental modification have shown promise in reducing delirium incidence and mitigating its adverse consequences [7]. For mechanically ventilated patients, minimizing environmental disruption, reinforcing cognitive engagement, and enhancing communication are considered particularly effective [8]. Narrative nursing, an emerging non-pharmacological model, has recently gained attention. It involves nurses engaging with patients’ experiences through witnessing, understanding, and responding to their suffering [9]. By facilitating the reconstruction of positive and meaningful narratives, narrative nursing is thought to improve emotional well-being, strengthen treatment confidence, and promote recovery [10]. Existing studies indicate that narrative-based interventions can alleviate anxiety and depression, improve sleep quality and patient satisfaction, and even reduce post-traumatic stress symptoms among ICU survivors [11, 12].

Although narrative nursing has demonstrated benefits in fields such as oncology and chronic disease management [13, 14], its application in ICU settings—especially among mechanically ventilated patients—remains limited. To date, systematic evaluation of its impact on delirium incidence and clinical outcomes in this population is lacking. This study therefore aimed to conduct a prospective, randomized controlled trial to assess the effect of narrative nursing on delirium incidence, ICU length of stay, ventilation duration, mortality, and patient satisfaction. We hypothesized that narrative nursing would reduce delirium incidence, shorten ICU stay and ventilation days, enhance satisfaction, and improve overall clinical outcomes.

Materials and methods

Study subjects

A total of 88 ICU patients requiring mechanical ventilation were selected from our hospital between January 2024 and January 2025. The patients were randomly assigned to either the control group or the intervention group using a random number table, with 44 patients in each group (Fig. 1). This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of The Fourth Military Medical University Xijing Hospital (Approval No. KYLL2025-986-191) and conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. All patients or their legally authorized representatives provided written informed consent prior to enrollment. No identifiable patient information is included in the manuscript.Fig. 1CONSORT participant flow

CONSORT participant flow

Sample size estimation was based on previous studies [15, 16] using the two-sample mean comparison formula: n1 = n2 = 2[(Zα + Zβ)σ/δ]2, where α = 0.05 (two-sided), β = 0.20 (power = 80%), Zα = 1.96, and Zβ = 1.282. In this study, the permissible effect size was defined as δ/σ = 0.80. The calculation indicated a minimum of 33 patients per group. Allowing for an anticipated attrition rate of ~20%, the final target sample size was set at 88 patients, with 44 assigned to each group to ensure adequate power and robustness of the findings.

Randomization was performed using computer-generated block randomization (block size = 4). An independent research assistant, not involved in patient care or outcome assessment, generated the allocation sequence with a random number generator (SPSS Statistics 26.0, IBM Corp., USA). Allocation concealment was maintained using sequentially numbered, sealed, opaque envelopes to prevent foreknowledge of group assignment. Patient enrollment was conducted by ICU attending physicians, while randomization procedures and allocation sequence management were overseen by the research assistant.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

The inclusion criteria for this study were adult patients (≥18 years old) who required mechanical ventilation in the ICU for at least 24 h. Eligible patients must have no severe cognitive impairment, be able to communicate at a basic level with the nursing staff, and be able to understand and consent to participation in the study. The exclusion criteria included patients with severe cognitive impairment or a history of psychiatric disorders; pregnant or breastfeeding women; patients in deep coma or with severe brain organic damage; and patients with severe infectious diseases.

Intervention measures

Patients were randomized to receive either narrative nursing intervention (intervention group) or standard ICU care (control group). All outcome assessors and data analysts remained blinded to group allocation. To ensure blinding integrity: (i) narrative nursing was delivered at the bedside by a designated research nurse who did not participate in outcome assessments; (ii) outcome evaluation (e.g., anxiety scale scoring, vital sign collection) was conducted by another researcher uninvolved in the intervention; (iii) data entry and statistical analyses were performed by independent statisticians, with unblinding occurring only after the analyses were completed.

Control group

Patients received standard ICU care, including routine consciousness assessment, scheduled repositioning and chest physiotherapy, prevention of complications (e.g., pressure ulcers, deep vein thrombosis), standard ventilator management, continuous monitoring of vital signs (heart rate, blood pressure, oxygen saturation), airway care to maintain endotracheal tube and ventilator circuit patency, and respiratory support as required. No narrative nursing was provided.

Intervention group

In addition to standard ICU care, patients received structured narrative nursing. The intervention was delivered by a dedicated team consisting of one head nurse (team leader) and six staff nurses, all with ≥3 years of ICU experience. Prior to study initiation, team members underwent standardized training organized by the research group, covering narrative nursing theory, active listening and empathy techniques, structured interview protocols, documentation, and safety considerations. Only nurses who completed training and passed competency assessments participated in the intervention. Each patient received one daily session lasting approximately 30 min, continued throughout the mechanical ventilation period until successful extubation or ICU discharge.

Narrative nursing comprised four structured components [12, 17]:Externalization of problems: Patients were encouraged to freely share personal experiences and concerns. Nurses provided active listening and emotional support through verbal affirmation and nonverbal reassurance (e.g., hand-holding, gentle touch, physical presence).Deconstruction: Patients were guided to re-examine past experiences from new perspectives, to identify meaning in seemingly ordinary events, and to broaden their narrative space.Re-authoring: Nurses helped patients uncover overlooked strengths or positive experiences, transform negative emotions into constructive narratives, and promote self-reconstruction to strengthen coping capacity.Witnessing: When feasible, primary caregivers (family members) were invited to act as “witnesses,” observing or participating in selected sessions. The nursing team modeled supportive communication strategies for caregivers and encouraged them to reinforce positive feedback—an element recognized as critical in non-pharmacological interventions. All components were implemented in accordance with a standardized intervention manual, with key points and patient responses documented in structured intervention logs.

Outcome measures

The primary endpoint was delirium, assessed using the Richmond Agitation–Sedation Scale (RASS) and the Confusion Assessment Method for the ICU (CAM-ICU), both validated instruments for critically ill adults. RASS evaluates arousal and sedation levels on a scale from +4 (combative) to −5 (unarousable). Before each CAM-ICU assessment, patients were screened with RASS: those with RASS ≤ −4 were classified as deeply sedated or comatose and recorded as “unable to assess,” whereas those with RASS ≥ −3 proceeded to CAM-ICU testing [18].

The CAM-ICU evaluates four key features: (1) acute onset or fluctuating course, (2) inattention, (3) altered level of consciousness, and (4) disorganized thinking. Delirium was diagnosed when patients exhibited both features 1 and 2, plus either feature 3 or 4. For mechanically ventilated or nonverbal patients, task instructions were adapted to nonverbal responses (e.g., head nodding, hand squeezing) to ensure feasibility and accuracy. Assessments were conducted by trained nurses or physicians who had completed standardized certification prior to study initiation. Delirium evaluations were performed twice daily (morning and evening) until ICU discharge, discontinuation of mechanical ventilation, or day 28 post-enrollment.

Secondary endpoints included ICU length of stay, duration of mechanical ventilation, ICU mortality, Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (SOFA) score, and satisfaction with nursing care. Nursing satisfaction was measured using a 10-point Likert scale, with higher scores indicating greater satisfaction [19]. Baseline demographic and clinical variables (age, sex, BMI, comorbidities) were recorded at enrollment and incorporated into subsequent analyses. All secondary outcomes were summarized at ICU discharge, withdrawal of mechanical ventilation, or day 28, whichever occurred first.

Statistical analysis

All analyses were conducted using SPSS Statistics 26.0 (IBM Corp., USA). Continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation and compared between groups using independent-sample t-tests or Mann–Whitney U tests, as appropriate. Categorical variables were summarized as counts and percentages and compared using χ2 tests or Fisher’s exact tests.

Univariate logistic regression was first performed to examine associations between individual variables and delirium occurrence, with results presented as regression coefficient (β), standard error (S.E.), Z statistic, P value, and odds ratio (OR) with 95% confidence intervals. Variables with P < 0.05 were entered into a multivariable logistic regression model to adjust for potential confounders and to estimate the independent effect of narrative nursing on delirium risk.

Subgroup analyses by age (<65 vs. ≥65 years) were pre-specified in the study protocol to evaluate whether the effects of narrative nursing intervention on delirium incidence, duration, and patient satisfaction differed between younger and older patients. To formally test for effect modification, logistic regression models including an interaction term (intervention × age group) were constructed, with P values for interaction reported to assess heterogeneity of treatment effects.

Results

Baseline characteristics of patients

A total of 88 mechanically ventilated ICU patients were enrolled and randomized into the intervention group (n = 44) and control group (n = 44). Baseline characteristics were well balanced between groups. The mean age was 66.30 ± 9.79 years in the intervention group and 67.52 ± 10.24 years in the control group (P = 0.57). The sex distribution was comparable (P = 0.83). APACHE II scores were 18.35 ± 4.96 versus 18.85 ± 5.29 (P = 0.65), and SOFA scores were 8.19 ± 2.12 versus 8.76 ± 2.40 (P = 0.24) for the intervention and control groups, respectively. No significant differences were observed in the prevalence of hypertension, diabetes, or primary reasons for ICU admission (Table 1). Comparison of delirium incidence

The overall incidence of delirium was significantly lower in the intervention group compared with the control group (34.1% vs. 63.6%, P < 0.001). The reduction was particularly pronounced for hypoactive delirium (20.5% vs. 40.9%, P = 0.02). Although the incidence of hyperactive delirium also trended lower in the intervention group, the difference did not reach statistical significance (13.6% vs. 22.7%, P = 0.14). These findings suggest that narrative nursing substantially reduced the risk of delirium, with the strongest effect observed in hypoactive subtypes (Table 2). Patient-reported satisfaction with nursing care was markedly higher in the intervention group (8.60 ± 1.27 vs. 6.51 ± 1.54, P < 0.001), underscoring the positive impact on care experience. Moreover, SOFA scores at follow-up were significantly lower in the intervention group (5.68 ± 2.20 vs. 6.84 ± 2.32, P = 0.019). Collectively, these results indicate that narrative nursing improved not only delirium-related outcomes but also broader measures of clinical course, mortality, and perceived care quality.

Logistic regression analysis

Univariable logistic regression demonstrated that patients in the intervention group had a significantly lower risk of delirium compared with controls (OR = 0.30, 95% CI: 0.12–0.71, P = 0.006). Higher APACHE II scores (OR = 1.11, 95% CI: 1.01–1.22, P = 0.023) and SOFA scores at ICU admission (OR = 1.13, 95% CI: 1.01–1.27, P = 0.045) were independently associated with increased risk. By contrast, age, sex, BMI, hypertension, diabetes, and primary ICU admission diagnosis were not significantly correlated with delirium incidence (Table 5). Sensitivity analysis

Sensitivity analyses across prespecified subgroups suggested a potential modifying effect of age (P for interaction = 0.077) (Fig. 2). Among older patients (≥65 years), the intervention was associated with a marked reduction in delirium incidence (22.7% vs. 69.2%, P = 0.001), corresponding to an 87% lower risk (OR = 0.13, 95% CI: 0.04–0.48, P = 0.002). Narrative nursing also shortened delirium onset (3.31 ± 1.39 vs. 4.90 ± 1.43 days, P = 0.021) and showed a trend toward reduced duration (2.58 ± 1.31 vs. 3.41 ± 0.93 days), although the latter did not reach statistical significance (P = 0.119). In addition, patient satisfaction with nursing care was significantly higher in the intervention group (8.22 ± 1.23 vs. 6.38 ± 1.62, P < 0.001).Fig. 2Subgroup analysis forest plot. OR: odds ratio

In contrast, effects were attenuated in non-elderly patients (<65 years). No significant between-group differences were observed for delirium incidence (45.5% vs. 55.6%, P = 0.525), onset, or duration (all P > 0.05). Nevertheless, patient satisfaction remained consistently higher in the intervention group (8.98 ± 1.22 vs. 6.69 ± 1.43, P < 0.001) (Table 7). Taken together, these findings suggest that narrative nursing conferred greater protective benefit in elderly ICU patients, particularly in reducing delirium incidence and enhancing care experience, whereas in younger patients the primary benefit was improved satisfaction. Discussion

Our findings indicate that narrative nursing significantly reduces the incidence of delirium in mechanically ventilated ICU patients, particularly hypoactive delirium (intervention: 20.45% vs. control: 40.91%, P = 0.02). The intervention also shortened both the onset and duration of delirium. These results are consistent with previous studies demonstrating that non-pharmacological multi-component interventions—such as reality orientation, cognitive stimulation, and family engagement—effectively lower delirium incidence and duration [17, 20]. Narrative nursing, a patient-centered humanistic care strategy, may prevent delirium by alleviating psychological stress and enhancing communication and adherence, aligning with the principles of non-pharmacological interventions. Hypoactive delirium is particularly prevalent in the ICU, accounting for approximately 50.3% of all cases according to systematic reviews [21]. Its subtle presentation often leads to under-recognition. In this study, narrative nursing markedly reduced hypoactive delirium, suggesting its potential to identify and address otherwise hidden delirium. Notably, hypoactive delirium has been associated with poorer clinical outcomes, including increased mortality and long-term cognitive impairment [22]. However, current evidence remains limited, and causal relationships in mechanically ventilated patients have yet to be firmly established, necessitating confirmation in large-scale prospective trials. The observed intervention effect suggests that narrative nursing may exert protective benefits through deeper cognitive and emotional engagement, offering a novel avenue for ICU delirium prevention and management.

In this study, narrative nursing was associated with shorter ICU length of stay and reduced duration of mechanical ventilation, alongside a trend toward lower ICU mortality. This observation is logically linked to the reduction in delirium incidence, as fewer delirium episodes may decrease treatment-related complications and facilitate recovery [23]. Compared with conventional nursing interventions, narrative nursing fosters deeper communication and emotional engagement with patients, potentially enhancing treatment adherence and improving the efficiency of care team collaboration [24]. The marked increase in patient satisfaction (P < 0.001) further indicates that narrative nursing not only supports physiological recovery but also significantly elevates patients’ subjective evaluation of their care experience. This finding is particularly relevant given the established association between patient satisfaction, psychological well-being, clinical outcomes, and the quality of the patient–clinician relationship [25]. Improved satisfaction may therefore promote broader adoption and implementation of this intervention in clinical practice.

Multivariate logistic regression analysis demonstrated that narrative nursing remained an independent protective factor against delirium after adjustment for confounders (OR = 0.27, 95% CI: 0.10–0.70, P = 0.008), indicating that its effect persists even when accounting for disease severity. Consistent with prior studies, higher APACHE II and SOFA scores were positively associated with delirium risk, reflecting the greater vulnerability of more severely ill patients [26]. Subgroup analysis revealed an age-dependent modulation of the intervention effect: in elderly patients (≥65 years), narrative nursing significantly reduced delirium incidence, corresponding to an approximately 87% risk reduction, whereas no significant effect was observed in younger patients. This pattern likely reflects the higher baseline susceptibility of older adults to delirium [27, 28], particularly hypoactive subtypes, which are more prevalent in this population [21]. Emotional support inherent to narrative nursing may therefore exert a particularly pronounced benefit in this subgroup. Notably, sensitivity analyses suggest that in younger patients, the primary advantage of the intervention was an improved care experience rather than delirium prevention.

Elderly patients, due to lower cognitive reserve and poorer adaptability to environmental changes, are more susceptible to the high-pressure ICU environment [29]. Narrative nursing, through personalized communication and emotional support, may confer additional protection for this high-risk population. Active listening to patients’ concerns and fostering trust in their care are especially critical for elderly patients [30]. These findings suggest that future interventions should prioritize tailored strategies for older adults, potentially integrating narrative nursing with other non-pharmacological approaches such as music therapy or early mobilization to maximize efficacy. By establishing deep emotional connections, narrative nursing can substantially alleviate psychological stress and mitigate negative affective states, including anxiety and depression. This aligns with previous research on the mechanisms by which psychological interventions prevent delirium [31]. Evidence from oncology settings further indicates that narrative nursing reduces anxiety, improves patient satisfaction, and enhances sleep quality [32]. Additionally, the intervention may indirectly enhance sleep, a factor closely linked to delirium risk [32]. Collectively, these mechanisms provide a scientific rationale for the multifaceted benefits of narrative nursing and highlight avenues for future studies to elucidate its underlying biological and psychological pathways through preclinical and clinical investigations.

This randomized controlled trial systematically evaluated the impact of narrative nursing on mechanically ventilated ICU patients, offering a novel perspective on the application of non-pharmacological interventions in critical care. Nonetheless, several limitations should be noted. The single-center design and modest sample size restrict the generalizability of findings, particularly regarding mortality outcomes. Although outcome assessors and data analysts were blinded, patients and nurses delivering the intervention were not, which may have introduced behavioral bias. To mitigate this, standardized intervention protocols were followed, and outcome assessments were performed by blinded evaluators. Nonetheless, we recognize this as an inherent limitation. Although delirium assessment relied on CAM-ICU, measurement error cannot be excluded. Moreover, the study was conducted exclusively in a Chinese patient population, limiting cross-cultural applicability. Short-term follow-up precluded evaluation of long-term cognitive, psychological, and quality-of-life outcomes, and the absence of cost-effectiveness analysis prevents assessment of feasibility in resource-limited ICUs. Future research should employ multicenter, large-scale designs with extended follow-up, culturally adapted protocols, and economic evaluations to validate and optimize the clinical utility of narrative nursing.

Conclusion

This study indicates that narrative nursing, as a patient-centered non-pharmacological intervention, can substantially reduce delirium incidence in mechanically ventilated ICU patients while improving the overall care experience. However, given the small sample size, single-center design, and limited follow-up, the generalizability and long-term efficacy of these findings require further investigation. Future studies should focus on multicenter, large-scale trials with prolonged follow-up to verify the effectiveness and cost-effectiveness of narrative nursing, thereby facilitating its rational implementation in ICU clinical practice.
