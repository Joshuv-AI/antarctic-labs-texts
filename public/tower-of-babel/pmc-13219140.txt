Arginine-loaded silica nanoparticles inhibit E. faecalis in root canals
Authors: Meng-ya LI, Wen ZHANG, Yi-yuan XIA, Jian SUN, Qing-an XU
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBacterial infection of the root canal system, especially by Enterococcus faecalis (E. faecalis), is the primary cause of root canal treatment failure, and intracanal medication has been proven to be an effective method for controlling root canal infection. Mesoporous silica nanoparticles (MSNs) with their sponge-like structure, tunable particle size, surface functionalization, and favorable biocompatibility offer promise for intracanal drug delivery. This study evaluated the antibacterial efficacy of an arginine-loaded MSN system (MSN@Arg) against E. faecalis, its effects on osteogenic differentiation in MC3T3-E1 cells, and its ability to control infection within the root canal system. The physicochemical properties and drug release profile of MSN@Arg were assessed using electron microscopy and other analytical techniques. Antibacterial activity was evaluated through bacterial culture assays. Alkaline phosphatase and Alizarin Red staining were performed to evaluate osteogenic differentiation. An E. faecalis-infected root canal model was established in extracted teeth, and the antibacterial efficacy of MSN@Arg, chlorhexidine (CHX), and calcium hydroxide was compared at 3, 7, 14, and 21 days after drug sealing. The synthesized MSNs (diameter < 100 nm) exhibited extensive distribution within dentinal tubules. MSN@Arg enabled sustained and stable release of arginine, inhibited E. faecalis in both planktonic and biofilm states, and promoted ALP expression and mineralized nodule formation in MC3T3-E1 cells, with high biocompatibility. MSN@Arg demonstrated greater antibacterial efficacy than calcium hydroxide on days 3 and 7, was comparable to CHX on day 14, and slightly more effective than CHX on day 21. Given its deep penetration into dentinal tubules and sustained drug release capacity, MSN@Arg represents a promising agent for root canal disinfection.Graphical Abstract

Introduction

In modern dentistry, root canal therapy (RCT) is essential for preserving infected or damaged teeth. When dental pulp is compromised by bacterial infection or trauma, RCT prevents extraction [1]. It relieves pain, restores function, and supports oral health. Treatment success depends on multiple factors, including the choice of endodontic disinfectant, which eliminates residual microorganisms, reduces infection, and controls inflammation. Enterococcus faecalis, a Gram-positive facultative anaerobe [2], is often detected post-treatment. It penetrates dentinal tubules and forms single-species biofilm, increasing drug resistance [3–5]. Selecting an effective disinfectant is critical for improving outcomes, relieving symptoms, and preventing recurrence. Disinfectant efficacy is influenced by root canal morphology, infection type, and lesion depth [6]. Nanomaterials have opened new avenues for drug delivery. Mesoporous silica nanoparticles (MSN) are widely used due to their tunable properties and biocompatibility [7, 8]. Studies show D-amino acids inhibit and disrupt E. faecalis biofilms [9, 10], and MSN loaded with L-arginine (L-Arg) exhibits antibacterial effects in the oral environment [11–13]. Ho et al. [14] reported that L-Arg promotes osteoprotegerin secretion and nitric oxide synthase expression, enhancing osteoblast proliferation and function.

This study developed an Arg-loaded MSN system (MSN@Arg) to evaluate its antibacterial activity against E. faecalis, its osteogenic potential, and its ability to control infection in the root canal system.

Methods

All experiments adhered to ethical protocols for human specimen use, as approved by the University’s ethics committee.

MSN synthesis

Cetyltrimethylammonium bromide (2.000 g) was stirred in 20 mL ultrapure water for 10 min. Triethanolamine (80 mg) was added and stirred at 90 °C for 20 min. Sodium carbonate (1.272 g) was added and reacted at 50 °C for 30 min. After cooling to 25 °C, the mixture was centrifuged at 10,000 rpm for 15 min, and the supernatant was discarded. The precipitate was treated with 40 mL methanol and 2 mL concentrated hydrochloric acid at 40 °C for 12 h to remove the template, repeated thrice. The final product was washed with ultrapure water and freeze-dried to yield MSN.

For fluorescent MSN synthesis, 0.5 g MSN was dispersed in 100 mL ethanol, and 1.25 mL APTES was added for overnight reflux at 25 °C. After ethanol washing and drying, amine-modified MSN was obtained. Then, 0.1 g of this product was dispersed in 20 mL ethanol and reacted with 2.5 mg RITC in the dark for 4 h at room temperature, washed twice with ethanol, and vacuum-dried.

MSN penetration and distribution in the root canal

Single-rooted extracted teeth were collected and disinfected. After pulp chamber access, canals were prepared to F3 using a ProTaper nickel-titanium rotary file and irrigated with 5.25% NaOCl. Samples were randomly divided into three groups. In the ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA) group, canals were irrigated with 1 mL 17% EDTA, repeated three times. In the ultrasound group, sterile double-distilled water was injected, followed by ultrasonic agitation with an ISO 30 file tip (taper = 6, E mode: 25% power) for 20 s, repeated three times. The control group received no treatment.

A 0.5 mg/mL fluorescent MSN suspension was prepared in distilled water and introduced into the pulp chamber. The teeth were wrapped in a sealing membrane, and the procedure was repeated every 12 h for five cycles. Teeth were split longitudinally using a high-speed turbine. Dentin slices (0.5 mm thick) were collected from areas adjacent to the root canal wall and observed via confocal laser scanning microscopy (CLSM). The remaining halves were oven-dried at 60 °C and examined using field emission gun-scanning electron microscopy (FEG-SEM; SU8010; Hitachi, Tokyo, Japan). For CLSM and FEG-SEM observations, at least three independent biological replicates were prepared for each group. Multiple fields were randomly selected and imaged under consistent parameters, and representative images reflecting the general phenotype were presented.

Arg loading and release

MSN (20 mg) was dispersed in 5 mL ultrapure water, 10 mg Arg was added. The mixture was stirred in the dark for 24 h to allow Arg loading into the MSN pores. The product was centrifuged at 10,000 rpm for 5 min, and the supernatant was discarded. The precipitate was washed with ultrapure water and freeze-dried to obtain the MSN@Arg.

The supernatant was analyzed using an amino acid (AA) assay kit, and absorbance at 570 nm was measured. Arg concentration was calculated using a standard curve to determine encapsulation efficiency [10]. Thermogravimetric analysis (SDT Q600, Waters Corp., MS, USA) was performed to calculate drug-loading efficiency.

For in vitro release, 10 mg MSN@Arg was suspended in 10 mL PBS (pH 7.4) and shaken continuously in the dark. At 1, 2, 4, 8, 12, 24, 48, 72, and 96 h, 1 mL was withdrawn, centrifuged at 10,000 rpm for 5 min, and the supernatant was analyzed for Arg concentration using the AA assay kit. Equal volumes of PBS were replenished after each sampling.

MSN and MSN@Arg performance characterization

MSN morphology and porosity were assessed using FEG-SEM and transmission electron microscopy (TEM; HT7700 EXALENS; Hitachi). Pore size and surface area were determined via Brunauer–Emmett–Teller and Barrett–Joyner–Halenda methods based on nitrogen adsorption-desorption isotherms (ASAP 2020; Micromeritics, GA, USA). Particle size was analyzed using a zeta sizer (Nano ZS; Malvern Instruments, UK). Fourier Transform infrared (FTIR) spectra and thermogravimetric data (0 °C–800 °C) for MSN, Arg, and MSN@Arg were obtained using Nicolet iS10 spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, MA, USA) and an SDT Q600 analyzer.

MSN@Arg antimicrobial potency

MSN@Arg suspensions (0.5, 1, 2, 4, 8, 10, and 20 mg/mL in 100 µL) were prepared in brain heart infusion (BHI) medium and co-incubated with E. faecalis (ATCC29212; 107 CFU/mL in 100 µL) for 24 h under anaerobic conditions. Negative controls (no bacteria) included BHI medium (100 µL) and MSN@Arg (100 µL). Minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) was defined as the lowest concentration that cleared turbidity [15]. Minimum bactericidal concentration (MBC) was determined by subculturing 10 µL of bacteria with concentrations above the MIC onto solid BHI medium; the lowest concentration showing no colony growth was recorded as MBC [16].

MSN, Arg, and MSN@Arg (1, 2, 5, and 10 mg/mL) were incubated with E. faecalis for 1 and 3 days. After incubation, 10 µl was cultured on solid BHI medium for 24 h at 37 °C under anaerobic conditions. Colonies were counted and antimicrobial potency was calculated:

Effect of MSN@Arg on E. faecalis biofilms

Based on the results of preliminary antibacterial activity screening, L-Arg was excluded from subsequent experiments, and only D-arginine (D-Arg) was used for relevant assays. MSN, D-Arg, and MSN@D-Arg (1 and 10 mg/mL) were prepared in BHI medium. Glass slides or autoclaved dentin disks were placed in 24-well plates with 500 μL E. faecalis (107 CFU/mL) and 500 μL of each suspension. After 48 h anaerobic incubation at 37 °C, samples were fixed, dehydrated, dried, and examined using FEG-SEM. Dentin slices were stained with a live/dead kit and imaged by CLSM to assess biofilm inhibition and clearance. At least three independent replicates were performed, and representative images were shown.

Effect of MSN on osteoblast differentiation

MC3T3-E1 cells were seeded at 5000 cells/well in 96-well plates. After attachment, the medium was replaced with MSN, D-Arg, or MSN@D-Arg at various concentrations. After 24 h, CCK-8 reagent was added for 2 h, and absorbance was measured at 450 nm to calculate viability:

Cells (1 × 105/well) were also seeded in 12-well plates. After attachment, the osteogenic medium with MSN, MSN@D-Arg, or D-Arg (100, 50, 25, 12.5 µg/mL) was added. At days 7 and 28, ALP and Alizarin Red staining were performed and observed under an inverted phase-contrast microscope [17]. Calcium nodules (day 28) were dissolved using cetylpyridine chloride, and absorbance at 550 nm was recorded. At least three independent replicates were performed, and representative images were shown.

MSN@D-Arg for root canal disinfection

Synthesis of MSN@D-Arg hydrogel

MSN@D-Arg was dispersed in double-distilled water by ultrasound. Separately, 0.05 g sodium carboxymethyl cellulose (CMC-Na) was sterilized under ultraviolet irradiation and slowly added to 10 mL MSN@D-Arg suspension under continuous stirring to avoid clumping. The gel was stirred for 2 h, sealed overnight, and loaded into sterile syringes. Injectability was tested using a 26 G needle. D-Arg release was also assessed as previously described.

Disinfection effect of MSN@D-Arg in infected root canal model

Extracted single-rooted teeth with intact structure were obtained from Wuhan First Stomatological Hospital with informed patient consent. To establish an infected root canal model, the pulp chambers were opened and canals prepared to size F3 using ProTaper nickel-titanium rotary files. After alternate irrigation with 17% EDTA and saline, apical foramina were sealed using light-curing composite resin, and sterilized by autoclaving at 121 °C for 20 min.

An E. faecalis suspension (1 × 108 CFU/mL, 100 µL per canal) was injected, and inoculation was completed within 30 min. Teeth were placed into EP tubes containing 1 mL liquid BHI medium and cultured anaerobically at 37 °C, with medium replacement every other day. At 28 days, infection was confirmed by inoculating samples from the root canal orifice onto solid BHI medium, followed by anaerobic incubation (24 h at 37 °C) and bacterial identification via colony morphology and Gram staining.

Following infection confirmation, canals were rinsed with saline. A sterile #30 paper point was inserted to the working length for 1 min to absorb intracanal fluid, then to an Eppendorf tube containing 1 mL sterile water and ultrasonically agitated for 1 min. Teeth were randomly assigned to seven groups treated with: saline, MSN, D-Arg, MSN@D-Arg, MSN@D-Arg hydrogel, Ca(OH)2 (Guanya, Wuhan, China), and chlorhexidine digluconate (CHX; Longly, Wuhan, China). Root canals were sealed with light-curing composite resin and incubated at 37 °C.

Samples were collected on days 3, 7, 14, and 21 (n = 3 per group). After opening the access cavities, canals were flushed with 1 mL saline. Intracanal fluid was collected using paper points, and subjected to 10-fold serial dilutions. Aliquots (10 µL) were plated on solid BHI medium and incubated anaerobically at 37 °C for 24 h. Colony counts were performed to evaluate antibacterial efficacy. At least three independent replicates were performed, and representative images were shown.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using SPSS version 19.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) and reported as mean ± standard deviation. P < 0.05 was considered significant. Normality was tested using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test, homogeneity of variance by the f test. Group comparisons were performed using one-way analysis of variance with Tukey’s post hoc test.

Results

MSN and MSN@Arg performance characterization

FEG-SEM imaging (Fig. 1a) showed that MSN had a uniform spherical morphology with a diameter of 50–60 nm. TEM imaging (Fig. 1b) revealed a well-organized porous surface with relatively uniform pore sizes. Nitrogen adsorption-desorption analysis (Fig. 1c, d) demonstrated a type IV isotherm with an H1 hysteresis loop. The specific surface area was 841.9829 m2/g, and the average pore diameter was 5.0280 nm.

Performance characterization of MSN.

a FEG-SEM image of MSN, scale bar = 500 nm. b Transmission electron microscopy image of MSN, scale bar = 100 nm (scale of local magnification = 50 nm). c MSN nitrogen adsorption-desorption isotherms; d MSN pore size distribution. e MSN particle size distribution. f FTIR spectra of MSN, Arg, and MSN@Arg

Dynamic light scattering (Fig. e) showed that MSN had an average hydrated particle size of 196.6 nm±2.456 nm and a polydispersity index of 0.227 ± 0.012, indicating a relatively narrow size distribution.

FTIR spectra (Fig. f) revealed characteristic MSN peaks: 3700–2800 cm-1 (silyl group), 1100 cm-1 (asymmetric Si-O-Si), and 790 cm-1 (symmetric Si-O-Si). In MSN@Arg, additional peaks were observed at 1614 cm-1, corresponding to the guanidine group (-CHIN) of Arg, and at 1419 cm-1, representing symmetric COO- bending.

MSN infiltration in the root canal

CLSM imaging (Fig. 2a–c) showed the distribution of fluorescent MSN in the root canal. Red fluorescence was mainly concentrated in the canal wall of the control and ultrasound groups. The fluorescence area in the EDTA-treated group was significantly larger than that in the other groups, and fluorescent MSN penetrated the dentin tubules more effectively. The EDTA-treated group was observed with FEG-SEM (Fig. 2d, e). MSN accumulated in the root canal wall and dentinal tubule orifice, effectively entering the tubule through the open orifice.

Arg loading and release

During MSN@Arg loading, the encapsulation efficiency of Arg was 37.15%. The drug release profile (Fig. 3a) exhibited an initial burst release within 8 h, followed by a slower release phase, reaching peak concentration at 24 h. The cumulative release of Arg was 45.98%.

Arg loading and release trends.

a Cumulative percentage of Arg release from MSN@Arg. b Total amount of Arg loaded onto the MSN

Thermogravimetric analysis (Fig. 3b) showed water loss at 100 °C, and complete decomposition of Arg at 680 °C. At this point, the residual weight corresponded to MSN alone. The drug loading efficiency of Arg was calculated at 9.88%.

MSN@Arg antibacterial efficacy

MSN@L-Arg exhibited limited antibacterial activity against E. faecalis, with no detectable MIC or MBC across concentrations of 0.5–20 mg/mL. In contrast, MSN@D-Arg had a MIC of 1 mg/mL and an MBC of 10 mg/mL.

Antimicrobial potency at 24 and 48 h, as assessed by plate counts (Fig. 4), showed that MSN, Arg, and MSN@Arg at 1 mg/mL exhibited inhibitory effects against E. faecalis. At 24 h, MSN@D-Arg was more effective than MSN@L-Arg and D-Arg, but not significantly different from MSN. By 48 h, bacterial inhibition declined across all groups, though MSN@Arg showed a smaller reduction compared to MSN or Arg alone. MSN@D-Arg maintained significantly higher antibacterial activity than MSN and D-Arg, and MSN@L-Arg outperformed L-Arg.

Antibacterial efficacy of MSN, Arg, and MSN@Arg co-cultured with E. faecalis for 1 and 2 days, at.

a 1 mg/mL, b 2.5 mg/mL, c 5 mg/mL, d 10 mg/mL concentrations

At 2.5 mg/mL, MSN@D-Arg was more effective than MSN@L-Arg at both 24 and 48 h. At 5 mg/mL, MSN@D-Arg showed superior activity at 24 h, though differences were not statistically significant at 48 h. No significant difference was observed between MSN@D-Arg and MSN@L-Arg at 10 mg/mL.

Notably, MSN@L-Arg and L-Arg were excluded from subsequent experiments due to the absence of detectable MIC and MBC of MSN@L-Arg (0.5–20 mg/mL) against E. faecalis and the significantly lower antibacterial potency of MSN@L-Arg and L-Arg compared with MSN@D-Arg and D-Arg at multiple concentrations.

Effect of MSN@D-Arg on E. faecalis biofilm

Crystal violet staining was used to assess the effects of D-Arg at 0.01, 0.05, 0.1, 0.25, 0.5, 1, 2.5, 5, 10, and 20 mg/mL on E. faecalis biofilms. Inhibition and clearance rates were not strictly dose-dependent, with 1 and 10 mg/mL showing the highest efficacy (data not shown); these concentrations were selected for subsequent experiments.

Biofilm formation inhibition

FEG-SEM analysis (Fig. 5a) demonstrated that all treatment groups exhibited fewer bacteria than the control. No significant difference was observed between the 1 and 10 mg/mL D-Arg groups. Disruption of the E. faecalis cell envelope was noted. Bacterial counts in the MSN@D-Arg group were lower than in the MSN group.

Effect on E. faecalis biofilm.

a FEG-SEM images (scale bar = 5 µm, scale of local magnification = 2 µm) and b CLSM (scale bar = 100 µm) images of MSN, D-Arg, and MSN@D-Arg against E. faecalis biofilms. c FEG-SEM images (scale bar = 5 µm, scale of local magnification = 2 µm) and d CLSM (scale bar = 100 µm) images of MSN, D-Arg, and MSN@D-Arg disruption of E. faecalis biofilms

CLSM images of dentin slices (Fig. 5b) showed uniformly distributed green-fluorescence indicating live E. faecalis in the control. In the D-Arg group, fluorescence was mixed (red and green) and unevenly distributed. In the MSN group, biofilms appeared red and clustered within dentin tubules. In contrast, MSN@D-Arg displayed sparse, agglomerated structures, mainly red, with minimal green fluorescence and reduced overall biofilm area.

Mature biofilm disruption

Following 2 h of treatment, FEG-SEM (Fig. 5c) revealed that biofilm integrity was disrupted in all experimental groups. Envelope damage was evident in bacteria treated with D-Arg and MSN@D-Arg.

Live/dead staining results (Fig. 5d) showed predominantly green fluorescence in the control group. D-Arg treatment resulted in orange staining, while the MSN and MSN@D-Arg groups exhibited mostly red fluorescence, indicating extensive bacterial death. Both MSN and MSN@D-Arg groups showed pronounced aggregation of dead cells.

Biological safety and osteopromotive effect of MSN@Arg

Biological safety of MSN@D-Arg

As shown in Fig. 6a–c D-Arg had no significant cytotoxicity toward MC3T3-E1 cells at concentrations up to 1000 μg/mL. However, at 5000 and 10,000 μg/mL, cell viability decreased significantly. MSN concentrations up to 100 μg/mL showed no significant cytotoxicity, though viability declined with increasing concentration. At 200 μg/mL, viability dropped below 80%. In contrast, MSN@D-Arg did not significantly affect cell viability at concentrations up to 200 μg/mL.

Effect of MSN@D-Arg on osteoblast differentiation

After 7 days of osteogenic induction, ALP staining (Fig. 6d, f) revealed no dark brown precipitate in the control group. All experimental groups showed significantly larger ALP-positive areas. ALP expression in the MSN group decreased in a dose-dependent manner, whereas D-Arg slightly increased ALP levels without statistical significance. At 25 and 50 μg/mL, ALP activity in the MSN@D-Arg group was significantly higher than in the corresponding MSN and D-Arg groups.

Alizarin Red staining (Fig. 6e, g), showed minimal calcium deposition in controls, with increased mineralization across treatment groups. For MSN, mineralization declined with increasing concentration. In contrast, D-Arg promoted mineralization in a dose-dependent manner, with statistically significant increases between 12.5 and 100 μg/mL. MSN@D-Arg groups exhibited greater calcium nodule formation than either MSN or D-Arg alone. At 12.5 and 25 μg/mL, mineralization in MSN@D-Arg–treated cells was significantly greater than in the corresponding D-Arg groups.

Injectability and sustained release of MSN@Arg hydrogel

As shown in Fig. 7a, MSN@Arg hydrogel could be extruded as either a thin or thick filament, demonstrating favorable injectability. Drug release from the hydrogel (Fig. 7b) exhibited an initial burst phase within the first 8 h, followed by a slower, sustained release. Peak Arg concentration was reached at 48 h, with a cumulative release of 45.98%.

Disinfection effect of MSN@D-Arg

Fig. 7c–f presents the antibacterial efficacy at 3 and 7 days following drug sealing. No significant differences were observed between the CMC-Na and sterile water groups at either time point. At 3 days, reductions in colony counts were significantly greater in the MSN@D-Arg aqueous solution and hydrogel groups than in the D-Arg, MSN, and Ca(OH)2 groups, but remained lower than in the CHX group. The colony reduction in the MSN@D-Arg hydrogel group was slightly smaller than in the aqueous solution group, though the difference was not statistically significant. Similar trends were observed at 7 days; however, the magnitude of colony sizes reduction in the MSN@D-Arg groups declined slightly over time.

Extended incubation results at 14 and 21 days are shown in Fig. 7g–j. At day 14, no bacterial growth was detected in the MSN@D-Arg aqueous solution or hydrogel groups. Colony reduction was significantly greater than in the D-Arg and MSN groups, with no significant difference relative to the CHX group. By day 21, the antibacterial efficacy of CHX had diminished. At this time point, reduction in colony counts were significantly greater in the MSN@D-Arg aqueous solution and hydrogel groups compared with the D-Arg, MSN, and CHX groups. The MSN@D-Arg hydrogel group showed the largest reduction, which was statistically greater than that of the aqueous solution group.

Discussion

In this study, the synthesized MSN exhibited a uniform spherical morphology with small diameters. SEM and CLSM confirmed MSN accumulation along the inner root canal wall and penetration into dentinal tubules through open orifices, supporting their potential for deep drug delivery within complex root canal system, consistent with previous findings [18, 19]. Owing to their high specific surface area and well-defined porous architecture, MSN exhibited excellent drug-loading capacity[20, 21]. The hydrodynamic diameter of MSN measured by dynamic light scattering was 196.6 ± 2.456 nm, which was larger than the size observed via TEM. This discrepancy is likely attributable to the hydration effect of MSNs in aqueous suspension. FTIR analysis revealed characteristic functional groups of Arg in MSN@Arg, confirming successful Arg loading. Drug-loading and encapsulation efficiency were determined using TGA and AA quantification, which depended on MSN-specific surface area, pore size, surface chemistry, molecular size, polarity, and solubility [22–25].

In vitro release of Arg from MSN@Arg showed an initial burst phase followed by a slower release phase. After peaking at 24 h, the concentration remained stable, consistent with a prior report [26]. This release behavior likely results from Arg being adsorbed both on the MSN structure and within its channels [27]. The initial burst is attributed to surface-bound Arg, while the sustained phase reflects diffusion from internal pores. This release profile supports prolonged drug availability in vivo, potentially enhancing therapeutic efficacy.

The antibacterial activity of MSN@D-Arg was intermediate between MSN and D-Arg. We speculate that the D-Arg coating on MSN might reduce the direct contact between MSN and bacteria, thereby attenuating the intrinsic antibacterial properties of MSN[28]. This represents only one possible explanation for the observed antibacterial activity of MSN@D-Arg. Over time, antibacterial efficacy declined in all groups, though MSN@D-Arg showed greater activity than D-Arg alone. At 48 h, 1 mg/mL MSN@D-Arg exhibited more potent antibacterial effects than pure MSN, which suggests that the sustained release of D-Arg from MSN@D-Arg improves the long-term antibacterial efficacy of the nanosystem [29–32]. Crystal violet staining demonstrated that both forms of Arg (1 and 10 mg/mL) exerted notable antibacterial effects without apparent dose dependence, consistent with prior research [33] on AAs. Thus, optimizing MSN@D-Arg concentration is essential for maximizing efficacy. In biofilm experiments on glass and dentin slides, MSN@D-Arg inhibited E. faecalis biofilm formation and disrupted mature biofilms within 2 h, consistent with previous reports [9]. FEG-SEM imaging showed ruptured E. faecalis membranes in the D-Arg and MSN@D-Arg groups, but not in the MSN group, supporting the role of D-Arg in altering cell membrane permeability.

MC3T3-E1 cells were used to evaluate the cytocompatibility of D-Arg, MSN, and MSN@D-Arg. D-Arg concentrations up to 1000 μg/mL showed no significant cytotoxicity. In contrast, 200 μg/mL MSN reduced cell viability by 20%, whereas MSN@D-Arg did not. The D-Arg coating may reduce MSN–cell interactions, mitigating cytotoxicity. MC3T3-E1 cells were then induced to differentiate into osteoblasts to assess osteogenic effects. ALP staining and calcium nodule formation assays revealed that D-Arg, MSN, and MSN@D-Arg promoted osteogenic differentiation and calcium salt deposition. D-Arg and MSN promoted early osteogenic differentiation and mineralization. These findings align with previous studies showing that Arg enhances insulin-like growth factor I (IGF-I) production and collagen synthesis, while IGF-I in turn increases ALP activity [34]. Similarly, MSN may promote osteogenesis through synergistic actions of its components [35]. Among the tested concentrations (12.5–100 μg/mL), 25 μg/mL MSN@D-Arg yielded the most pronounced osteogenic effect. This may reflect opposing concentration–response trends: MSN-induced osteogenesis may plateau or decline at higher concentrations, while D-Arg exhibits dose-dependent enhancement. Further studies are needed to elucidate the mechanisms underlying this synergy.

To facilitate clinical translation, an injectable MSN@D-Arg hydrogel was formulated using CMC-Na. Its release profile resembled that of the aqueous formulation, with an initial burst followed by slower, sustained release. However, the hydrogel reached steady-state levels more gradually and provided enhanced sustained release. This may reflect the hydrogel’s three-dimensional network, which retains drug molecules and restricts diffusion, unlike in aqueous solutions [36].

MSN@D-Arg showed stronger antibacterial activity than Ca(OH)2 from days 3–21 post-sealing. Ca(OH)2 poorly inhibited E. faecalis, consistent with previous reports [37]. CHX had strong early activity but waned over time. By day 21, MSN@D-Arg outperformed CHX, supporting its prolonged antibacterial effect. Although the hydrogel’s initial activity was lower than that of the aqueous form on day 3, it surpassed the latter by day 21.

Conclusions

MSN exhibited uniform spherical morphology with well-defined channels, enabling deep penetration into dentinal tubules. MSN@D-Arg inhibited E. faecalis and promoted osteogenic differentiation in MC3T3-E1 cells with good biocompatibility. The hydrogel was injectable and provided sustained release, effectively controlling root canal infection. These findings support the potential of MSN@D-Arg as a platform for root canal disinfection and bone regeneration.
