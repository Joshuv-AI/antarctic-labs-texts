Fractional crystallization of colloids by sequential tuning of depletion forces
Authors: Sehee Yang, Sanghyuk Park, Yongseok Jung, Young Geon Kim, Jiwoo Lee, Shin-Hyun Kim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractFractional crystallization is a powerful and precise separation method capable of discriminating even between molecular isomers by exploiting subtle solubility differences. While colloidal particles are inherently insoluble, they undergo crystallization and melting transitions governed by interparticle interactions, akin to molecular systems. Inspired by this molecular approach, here we show a simple thermodynamic method to separate colloidal particles by size using depletion forces. By tuning interparticle potentials to emulate Mie-type interactions, we show that particles crystallize independently when the depth of the attractive well exceeds a species-specific threshold. Due to greater excluded volume overlap, larger particles experience stronger depletion attractions, allowing them to crystallize while smaller particles stay in the fluid. Building upon this principle, we establish a sequential fractional crystallization protocol—comprising crystallization, melting, and recrystallization—that achieves fractionation of colloids with <10% size differences. Notably, when all particle species marginally exceed the crystallization threshold, they preferentially crystallize into their respective lattices to maximize coordination numbers and enthalpic gains. Under stronger attractions, particles first form kinetically arrested glassy alloys that later relax into metastable crystalline alloys without macroscopic separation.Subject terms: Colloids, Nanoparticles, Self-assembly

Introduction

Mastery over particle attributes—size, shape, and composition—has transformed technologies across electronics, optics, materials science, and biomedicine. Uniform microparticles and nanoparticles, including dielectric colloids, metal nanoparticles, quantum dots, conductive microparticles, and drug carriers, are foundational to these advances1–9. Yet, variations arising from compositional heterogeneity, temperature gradients, and flow instabilities during synthesis continue to limit particle performance and functionality. Numerous separation strategies, such as membrane filtration, centrifugation, electrophoresis, dielectrophoresis, and microfluidic sorting, have been developed to address these challenges10–16. However, each method faces intrinsic trade-offs among precision, purity, scalability, and operational complexity, leaving a critical need for alternative approaches capable of high-fidelity particle separation.

In molecular systems, fractional crystallization17,18—exploiting slight differences in solubility—has long enabled the separation of chemically similar species, including rare earth elements and structural isomers. Translating this molecular-level resolution to particulate systems, however, remains a profound challenge, as colloidal particles do not differ in solubility but in phase behavior governed by interparticle interactions. Intriguingly, colloidal suspensions often exhibit phase transitions reminiscent of atomic and molecular systems, suggesting that a colloidal analog of fractional crystallization could be realized.

Here, we report fractional crystallization of colloids by engineering tunable depletion-mediated attractions to emulate molecular Mie potentials. By finely adjusting salt and depletant concentrations, we induce size-dependent attractive interactions: larger particles experience greater overlap of depletant-excluded volumes, resulting in a higher enthalpic driving force for crystallization that overcomes the entropic penalty. In contrast, smaller particles remain in a fluidic state, enabling selective phase separation by size. At moderate interaction strengths, binary mixtures fractionate into distinct single-component crystals of either large or small particles, maximizing lattice coordination and minimizing free energy. At stronger attractions, mixtures become kinetically trapped in glassy amorphous alloys, which, after about one week gradually anneal into crystalline alloys. By gradually increasing interparticle attraction, we achieve sequential crystallization: the largest particles crystallize first and sediment under gravity, followed by ‘sweating’—controlled melting and recrystallization—to enhance separation purity. Repeated cycling of crystallization and ‘sweating’ yields progressive fractionation, achieving purities exceeding 90% even for size differences as small as 10%. This approach establishes fractional crystallization as a versatile platform for colloidal separation, fundamentally overcoming the limitations of conventional techniques. Moreover, depletion-driven phase behavior offers an experimentally accessible model system for probing phase transitions and fractionation phenomena analogous to those in atomic and molecular materials.

Results

Phase behavior of binary colloidal mixtures

To realize a colloidal analogue of molecular fractional crystallization, we engineered interparticle interactions to approximate the Mie potential, characterized by strong short-range repulsion, intermediate-range attraction, and negligible long-range interaction. This interaction landscape is established by introducing a non-adsorbing long-chain polymer as a depletant and adding salt to aqueous particle suspensions19–22. Exclusion of depletants from the colloidal surface generates an osmotic pressure imbalance that drives an attractive force between neighboring particles, thereby driving their self-assembly (Fig. 1a)23–27. Simultaneously, salt screens electrostatic repulsions, reducing the Debye length. Consequently, the effective pair potential emerges from a balance of depletion attraction, electrostatic repulsion, and van der Waals forces, which can be finely tuned by controlling the depletant composition and molecular weight, as well as the depletant and salt concentrations28–35.

To systematically explore the crystallization behaviors of size-distinct colloids, we employed an aqueous binary suspension of polystyrene (PS) particles with diameters of 2Rp = 242 nm (ζ = –48.2 mV) and 2Rp = 199 nm (ζ = –45.7 mV) at a 1:1 volume ratio and 3% total volume fraction. Poly(acrylamide-co-acrylic acid) (Mw ≈ 5,000 kg mol-1; rh = 32.5 nm) served as the depletant, alongside sodium chloride as the charge screening agent. Given the hydrodynamic radius, depletion attractions operate for interparticle separations below ca. 65 nm. By varying the concentrations of depletant (Cdep) and salt (Csalt) while maintaining their ratio, we modulated the interparticle potentials and investigated phase behaviors (Fig. 1a). As Cdep and Csalt increased, crystalline grains exhibiting distinct diffraction colors emerged. Binary suspensions remained fluid at Cdep = 0.75 μM and Csalt = 7.5 mM, while red dendritic crystals emerged at Cdep = 0.80–0.85 μM (Fig. 1b, top row). At higher concentration of Cdep = 0.90–0.95 μM, green dendritic crystals appeared together with smaller red grains, some of which exhibited color variation or appeared colorless depending on their orientation. This dendritic growth arises from diffusion-limited aggregation at low supersaturation conditions31. Unary suspensions of each particle type independently mirrored these transitions: the larger particles (2Rp = 242 nm) formed red crystals, while the smaller particles (2Rp = 199 nm) produced green ones, occurring within the same Cdep and Csalt ranges as observed in the binary suspensions (Fig. 1b, middle and bottom rows). The binary suspension behavior reflects a simple superposition of the unary behaviors, indicating that crystallizations of larger and smaller particles occur independently. Furthermore, the identical diffraction colors between unary and binary systems suggest that the grains are overwhelmingly dominated by particles of a single size, with minimal cross-species incorporation.

The development of crystalline lattices leads to Bragg diffraction peaks in reflectance spectra. Although peaks emerged in an early stage, they slightly blue-shifted and stabilized over 1.5 months (Supplementary Fig. 1). Thus, all spectral measurements were performed after 1.5 months of incubation to ensure equilibrium. In binary suspensions, no peak was observed in the fluid phase; a single peak appeared for suspensions containing only red dendritic grains; and two distinct peaks were observed when both green and red grains formed (Fig. 1c). The peak positions matched those of the unary suspensions (Supplementary Fig. 2), reinforcing the conclusion that large and small particles predominantly crystallized into their own lattices. A slight blueshift in the red peak with increasing Cdep and Csalt suggests a minor decrease in interparticle separation.

The fluid-to-crystal transition reflects a competition between enthalpic gain from particle assembly and entropic penalties due to reduced translational freedom. Because the depth of the energy well in the pair potential contributes to the enthalpic stabilization of the crystalline phase through the coordination number—rather than serving as a single-bond trapping criterion—it provides a predictive indicator of the fluid–crystal phase boundary. The pair potential, calculated as the sum of depletion attraction, electrostatic repulsion, and van der Waals forces, was estimated by correlating interparticle separations with theoretical models28,30,36. By matching the interparticle separation—experimentally extracted from the reflectance peak position via Bragg’s law—to the separation corresponding to the potential well minimum, we estimated the concentrations of ionic impurities and the ratio of depletant osmolarity to molar concentration (Supplementary Notes 1, 2)30. The resulting potentials for both particle types exhibit strong short-range repulsion and mild intermediate-range attraction, closely resembling a Mie profile (Fig. 1d). With increasing Cdep and Csalt, the potential wells deepen and shift to shorter separations. The boundary between fluid and crystal phases corresponded to an energy well depth of |Wmin|≈ 3 kBT, with slightly higher values observed for larger particles than for smaller ones (Fig. 1e).

To test the generality of this phase boundary, we constructed experimental phase diagrams by systematically varying Cdep and Csalt at a total volume fraction of 3 vol.%, with a 1:1 volume ratio, and at room temperature (Fig. 1f, Supplementary Fig. 6). Fluid-to-crystal transitions occurred both by increasing Cdep at fixed Csalt and by increasing Csalt at fixed Cdep. Phase boundaries derived from |Wmin|≈ 3 kBT traced the observed transitions for both particle sizes (Fig. 1g), confirming the validity of the energy-well criterion at a fixed volume fraction. The boundaries were defined at |Wmin|≈ 3.2 kBT for 2Rp = 242 nm and |Wmin|≈ 3.1 kBT for 199 nm. Notably, several experimental and simulation studies of depletion-mediated or other short-ranged attractive colloidal systems have reported fluid–crystal coexistence at well depths on the order of a few kB T, particularly in three-dimensional systems at low volume fractions, supporting the plausibility of a |Wmin|≈ 3kB T boundary under such conditions37–42. At this boundary empirically identified by systematically mapping near-equilibrium phase behavior, crystalline domains coexist with a population of particles remaining in the fluid phase, where the free-energy difference between the fluid and crystalline phases is close to zero, indicating that the loss of translational entropy is comparable to the enthalpic gain zWmin associated with the coordination number z. Below this phase boundary (|Wmin|<≈ 3 kBT), suspensions remained fluid, dominated by thermal fluctuations. Above it (|Wmin|> ≈ 3 kBT), supersaturation drives spontaneous crystallization by overcoming the translational entropy penalty, with the fraction of free particles progressively decreasing as |Wmin| increases. Due to size-dependent attractions, there exists a regime where large particles selectively crystallize (|Wmin_L|> 3.2 kBT) while small particles remain fluidic (|Wmin_S|<3.1 kBT). At higher interaction strengths, both species crystallize into their own lattices. Notably, the larger particles crystallized within several hours, producing small red grains well before the smaller particles formed large green dendritic crystals over the course of a couple of weeks (Supplementary Fig. 7). This sequential nucleation reflects higher supersaturation for larger particles, indicated by greater deviations from the phase boundary in the phase diagram, resulting in faster nucleation kinetics with lower energy barriers. At excessive interaction strengths far exceeding the |Wmin|≈ 3 kBT threshold, the majority of particles kinetically co-assembled into disordered glassy alloys, where the grains predominantly composed of single particle sizes were co-produced. Over extended incubation, these glasses gradually annealed into crystalline alloys without phase separation (Supplementary Fig. 8). Collectively, these findings establish that fine-tuning depletion-mediated interactions enables control over crystallization pathways in binary colloidal mixtures, mirroring molecular fractional crystallization principles at the particle scale.

Selective crystallization of large particles in mixtures

A selective crystallization regime emerges when the interparticle attraction satisfies |Wmin_L|> 3.2 kBT for large particles while remaining below the crystallization threshold for small ones (|Wmin_S|<3.1 kBT). To examine the structure and compositional purity of the resulting crystalline grains, we prepared binary mixtures of PS particles with diameters of 2Rp = 248 nm (ζ = –43.3 mV) and 2Rp = 208 nm (ζ = –50.7 mV), fluorescently labeled in red and green, respectively. At Cdep = 0.75 μM and Csalt = 8 mM, only red, structurally colored grains were observed (Fig. 2a). Confocal laser scanning microscopy (CLSM) revealed that these grains were predominantly composed of large particles (Fig. 2b). Although some small particles were embedded within the grains, their volumetric fraction was low ≈ 4.3% indicating high compositional selectivity (Supplementary Fig. 9). The internal lattice exhibited an ABAB stacking sequence of hexagonal layers, consistent with a hexagonal close-packed (hcp) structure (Supplementary Fig. 10). This preference for hcp over face-centered cubic (fcc) arises from the linear polymer depletant-mediated interactions, which favor hcp lattices due to reduced entropy penalties associated with long, unbranched void channels, as reported in prior simulations43.

Most small particles remained dispersed in the fluid phase, although a small fraction was adsorbed onto the surfaces of the red crystallites (Fig. 2b). This interfacial localization is attributed to intermediate-strength attractive interactions between large and small particles—stronger than small–small but weaker than large–large interactions (Fig. 2c). The small particles appear to occupy interstitial sites on the crystallite surfaces, particularly triangular gaps bordered by three neighboring large particles. However, additional adsorption onto already-bound small particles was not favored. To support these observations, we performed coarse-grained molecular dynamics (MD) simulations using HOOMD-blue for a binary mixture of 2000 large and 3600 small particles, which corresponds to 1:1 volume ratio, at 3% total volume fraction. Interparticle interactions were modeled using Mie potentials calibrated for the pair potentials at the selective crystallization conditions of the larger particles (Fig. 2c). The simulations were designed to capture the generic form of interparticle interactions and to compare relative assembly pathways, rather than to explicitly model depletion interactions arising from macromolecular polymer depletants. Accordingly, the Mie potential—a generalized form of the Lennard-Jones potential—provides an effective approximation of the experimentally extracted pair potentials in this study. In the simulation, large particles reversibly assembled and disassembled into transient clusters; only those exceeding a critical size nucleated into stable crystals (Supplementary Movie 1). Small particles largely remained unclustered in the fluid phase, although some reversibly adsorbed to the surfaces of growing large-particle clusters. During crystallization, adsorbed small particles were displaced as large particles filled lattice positions. Upon energy convergence, most large particles had integrated into crystalline grains, while small particles remained fluidic or partially adsorbed at grain surfaces (Fig. 2d). The experimentally observed presence of small-particle impurities in red grains is thus attributed to occasional kinetic trapping during grain growth, where adsorbed small particles are not fully displaced by incoming large ones.

To visualize the enthalpic contributions to assembly, particles were color-coded according to their local potential energy. Large particles within crystals exhibited potential energies around –104 kJ mol−1 (–42 kBT) and coordination numbers of ca. 11–12, consistent with hcp lattices (Fig. 2e,f). While these coordination numbers are consistent with highly packed lattices, the experimentally observed preference for the hcp structure was not reproduced in the simulations. This discrepancy arises because configurational entropic contributions associated with linear polymer depletants are not captured in the pair-potential–based MD model. Small particles mostly remained unbound in the fluid, with negligible interaction energies and minimal clustering. A small fraction was observed on the surfaces of crystalline grains, with an average potential energy of –15.9 kJ mol−1 (–6.4 kBT) and coordination numbers of ca. 2–3 except free particles (Fig. 2g, h), confirming weak, surface-localized association. Consistent with the local potential energy and coordination analysis, MD simulations independently reproduce a distinct fluid–crystal transition, in which small particles with |Wmin|<3.1 kBT remain fluidic, whereas large particles with |Wmin|>3.2 kBT crystallize, in quantitative agreement with the experimental phase boundary.

Because the fluid–crystal phase boundary is governed by a balance between enthalpic gain and entropic penalty, the particle volume fraction is expected to influence the critical value of |Wmin| for crystallization. To examine this effect, we systematically varied the total particle volume fraction to 1.5, 3.0, and 5.0 vol.% while maintaining a 1:1 volume ratio. As the volume fraction increased from 1.5 to 5.0 vol.%, the threshold |Wmin| slightly decreased from ≈ 3.4 kBT to ≈ 3.0 kBT for larger particles and from ≈ 3.2 kBT to ≈ 2.9 kBT for smaller particles (Supplementary Fig. 11). Independent MD simulations of unary systems at volume fractions of 0.75, 1.5, and 2.5 vol.% exhibited the same trend for the larger particles (Supplementary Fig. 12). This behavior can be attributed to the reduction of the translational entropy penalty with increasing volume fraction, which lowers the enthalpic gain required to stabilize the crystalline phase. In contrast, particle size does not directly affect translational entropy. Nevertheless, a marginal difference in the crystallization threshold is observed for particles with similar sizes (Fig. 1e). To further examine the effect of particle size, we therefore investigated the phase behavior of much larger particles with 2Rp = 621 nm at a fixed volume fraction of 3 vol.%. The crystallization threshold was estimated as |Wmin | ≈ 4.2 kBT from experiments and MD simulations (Supplementary Fig. 13). This increase in the threshold with particle size arises from the narrower interaction range relative to particle diameter for larger particles, as the depletion interaction range is fixed by the depletant size. Although translational entropy dominates the free-energy balance at fluid–crystal coexistence under dilute conditions, a shorter relative interaction range reduces the configurational phase space available to bonded particle pairs in the crystalline phase, thereby increasing the entropic penalty. Consequently, a slightly deeper attractive well is required to compensate for this entropy loss and stabilize the crystal against the dilute fluid phase for larger particles. The crystallization threshold may further shift under different thermodynamic conditions, such as changes in temperature or pressure.

Exclusive and co-crystallization of binary particles

Upon increasing the depletant and salt concentrations to Cdep = 0.8 μM and Csalt = 8.5 mM, both large and small particles crystallized independently into their own lattices under the condition |Wmin_L | > 3.2 kBT and |Wmin_S | > 3.1 kBT, producing distinct red and green grains (Fig. 3a–c). Owing to higher supersaturation, large particles nucleated and crystallized faster, forming red grains first. Subsequently, green grains—composed of smaller particles—grew dendritically at low supersaturation. The green grains frequently grew from the periphery of the red grains and progressively filled the surrounding voids (Supplementary Fig. 14). At lower attraction strengths ( | Wmin_S | <3.1 kBT), small particles only adsorbed on red grain surfaces without initiating nucleation. However, at higher values (|Wmin_S | > 3.1 kBT), the red grain surfaces acted as heterogeneous nucleation sites for green grain formation.

The increased attraction strength led to impurity incorporation: the volume fraction of small-particle clusters embedded within red grains increased to ≈ 12%, compared to the lower impurity level observed under selective crystallization (Fig. 3b, Supplementary Fig. 15c). These embedded clusters are attributed to nucleation of small particles at the red grain surface during growth, followed by kinetic trapping due to continued accretion of large particles. In contrast, green grains exhibited higher compositional purity ≈ 3%, as their growth occurred after the red grains were mostly formed (Supplementary Fig. 15d).

In the MD simulations, while large and small particles each showed a tendency to cluster with their own species, distinct crystalline domains did not emerge (Fig. 3d, Supplementary Fig. 16, Supplementary Movie 2), likely due to insufficient thermal motion to facilitate reversible assembly. When the two particle types were separately initialized at 1.5% volume fraction each, the system yielded compositionally pure crystalline domains. These domains were subsequently overlaid to generate an idealized model of exclusive crystallization (Fig. 3g). This model exhibited higher crystallinity and coordination numbers, with an average potential energy of –227,300 kJ mol−1 —significantly lower than that of the partially co-assembled system (–175,000 kJ mol−1) (Supplementary Fig. 17). This energy difference indicates a thermodynamic preference for complete separation into pure lattices.

Depletion-mediated size-selective crystallization in binary colloidal mixtures has been reported previously and has been discussed in the context of eutectic crystallization44,45. However, eutectic crystallization is intrinsically defined for solvent-free or melt-like systems, in which two components solidify simultaneously from a common liquid at a specific composition. In the present system, crystallization occurs from a solvent-rich colloidal suspension and is driven by interaction strength rather than by melt solidification. By systematically examining near-equilibrium states over a wide range of particle mixing ratios, we find that the observed phase behavior is more consistently described by a species-wise crystallization threshold rather than by a eutectic composition (Supplementary Fig. 18). Even at high fractions of small particles, no equilibrium state is observed in which only the small particles selectively crystallize. Although small particles crystallize earlier than large particles in small-particle-rich mixtures, large particles also crystallize upon sufficiently long incubation under conditions where small particles crystallize. Because the depletion attraction increases monotonically with particle size, a stable equilibrium state in which only the small particles crystallize while larger particles remain fluid is thermodynamically forbidden, even though the threshold value of |Wmin| decreases at higher particle volume fractions. In highly asymmetric mixtures, it is in principle possible that only the small particles exceed their crystallization threshold while the large particles remain below theirs, which could allow small-only crystallization from a purely threshold-based argument. However, such conditions are far from eutectic-like compositions. Moreover, because the large-small interaction is stronger than small-small interaction, large particles are favored to participate in the growing crystal once crystallization of small particles begins, making a true equilibrium small-only crystalline state difficult to be realized under experimentally accessible conditions. Consequently, irrespective of the mixing ratio, the system exhibits only three regimes: a fluid state, selective crystallization of large particles, and exclusive crystallization of both species.

Importantly, exclusive crystallization does not originate from unfavorable heterotypic pair interactions, as the large–small interaction well depth is comparable to the average homotypic interaction (Fig. 3c), in contrast to prior discussions of eutectic colloidal crystallization46. Instead, exclusive crystallization minimizes the total free energy by enabling higher coordination numbers and improved packing efficiency in monodisperse lattices (Fig. 3g), whereas mixed assemblies suffer from geometric frustration arising from size disparity (Fig. 3d). Because the entropy loss due to translational confinement is comparable in both exclusive and mixed assemblies, the system favors compositionally pure crystallites to maximize coordination and enthalpic gain. This many-body packing effect, rather than eutectic energetics, underlies the observed fractional crystallization.

At higher concentrations (Cdep = 0.9 μM and Csalt = 9.5 mM), large and small particles predominantly co-assembled into randomly packed grains, although a minority of grains retained exclusively crystallized domains. These initially disordered aggregates gradually evolved into crystalline alloys, which were notably smaller in size and exhibited diminished structural coloration compared to exclusively crystallized grains (Fig. 3j). CLSM revealed a homogeneous co-distribution of both particle types throughout the entire three-dimensional grain volume (Fig. 3k; Supplementary Fig.19; Supplementary Movie 3). Within these alloys, small particles organized into alternately stacked triangular arrays—a structural motif corresponding to portions of a hcp lattice—while large particles occupied the interstitial sites adjacent to these triangular layers. This lattice configuration yields a coordination number of 12 for both particle types, akin to that of an ideal hcp lattice. However, whereas triangular arrays of small particles reside on one plane, large particles are positioned on adjacent planes, such that each small particle is coordinated by 8 small neighbors and 4 large neighbors, whereas each large particle is coordinated exclusively by 12 small neighbors. Because these grains formed via homogeneous nucleation, they adopted random lattice orientations throughout the sample volume (Fig. 3j,k).

At this regime, strong interparticle attractions across all particle types drive rapid formation of dense, disordered aggregates through random packing, leading to a relatively uniform co-distribution of large and small particles. This early-stage assembly is governed by kinetic trapping rather than thermodynamic equilibrium, as the attraction forces greatly exceed thermal fluctuations. Consequently, the particles cannot completely melt and reorganize into lower-energy, compositionally segregated crystalline phases. Over time, however, local rearrangements become possible, allowing for increases in coordination number and partial structural ordering. These slow, diffusive relaxations lower the free energy of the system and drive the transformation from amorphous glassy alloys into metastable crystalline alloys. Despite this structural evolution, the crystalline alloys remain compositionally mixed due to the lack of global demixing. This transition highlights a shift from a kinetically arrested unstable binary glass state to an enthalpy-driven, metastable co-crystal state—modulated by the interplay between attraction strength and particle diffusivity. There was no more transition to the energy minimum state of compositionally pure, exclusive crystal state in experimental timescale.

Fractional crystallization of colloidal mixtures

Solvent-driven fractional crystallization provides a more sustainable alternative to traditional fractional crystallization, offering enhanced energy efficiency and reduced reagent consumption17,47–49. Inspired by this principle at the molecular scale, we developed a sequential fractional crystallization strategy for colloidal mixtures with particle size differences below 10% by finely tuning interparticle attractions through controlled depletant and salt concentrations in aqueous solution (Fig. 4a). Under each set of conditions, the largest particles selectively crystallize and sediment, allowing removal of the supernatant. The crystalline grains are then melted and recrystallized under mild conditions to eliminate embedded impurities—a process known as ‘sweating’. After a second ‘sweating’ step under slightly milder conditions, the purified crystals are re-dissolved to yield a fluid enriched in the largest particles. The removed supernatant is then used as the starting solution for isolating the next-largest species, and the same cycle is repeated.

As a model system, we used an aqueous ternary suspension of PS colloids with diameters of 2Rp = 242 nm (ζ = –48.2 mV), 2Rp = 199 nm (ζ = –45.7 mV) and 2Rp = 177 nm (ζ = –46.1 mV), mixed at a 1:1:1 volume ratio with a total volume fraction of 3%. The suspension remained fluid at Cdep = 0.75 μM and Csalt = 7.5 mM. Red dendritic crystals began to form at Cdep = 0.80–0.85 μM, followed by the emergence of green grains and small red grains at Cdep = 0.90 μM, consistent with results from binary systems (Fig. 4b, top row; see also Fig. 1b). Notably, a difference in the volume fraction between 1.5 and 1.0 vol.% does not significantly alter the crystallization threshold. Further increasing Cdep to 0.95–1.00 μM led to the appearance of blue grains, which also formed in the unary suspension of the smallest particles. This consistency in crystallization thresholds across unary, binary, and ternary systems confirms that each particle species independently crystallizes into compositionally pure lattices. Reflectance spectra showed distinct Bragg diffraction peaks corresponding to each crystallite type: a single red peak at Cdep = 0.80 μM, red and green peaks at 0.90 μM, and three distinct peaks (red, green, blue) at 1.00 μM (Fig. 4c). Pair potential analysis confirmed that crystallization of each species occurred when the interaction well depth exceeded |Wmin |≈ 3 kBT, with a slight dependence on particle size (Fig. 4d; Supplementary Notes 1, 2).

Based on the identified crystallization thresholds, we carried out fractional crystallization and particle separation in a sequential manner. In the first stage, the largest particles selectively formed red crystalline grains at Cdep = 0.85 μM and Csalt = 8.5 mM, which sedimented to the bottom. The supernatant—containing uncrystallized particles—was carefully decanted. The collected crystals were then resuspended in an aqueous solution of the same composition to induce melting through convective flow, followed by recrystallization during incubation. Removal of the supernatant in this process lowered the overall particle volume fraction, increasing the free volume accessible to depletants. Consequently, the effective depletion attraction weakened, resulting in reduced incorporation of smaller and middle-sized particles during recrystallization. This ‘sweating’ process was repeated once more to further remove residual impurities. The purified crystals were subsequently redissolved by dilution with distilled water, yielding a fluid enriched in the largest particles. For the second stage, the supernatant collected from the first separation was used as the starting suspension. At Cdep = 0.90 μM and Csalt = 9.0 mM, green crystals composed of middle-sized particles were formed. After two additional ‘sweating’ steps under the same conditions, the purified green crystals were redissolved to obtain a suspension enriched in middle-sized particles. The remaining supernatant, now predominantly containing the smallest particles, was retained without further processing. The purity of each size-separated fluid was quantified via dynamic light scattering (DLS) and scanning electron microscopy (SEM). DLS revealed that two ‘sweating’ steps significantly reduced the dispersity in size (Đ), approaching values observed for unary systems (Supplementary Fig. 20). SEM images acquired from ten distinct regions per dried sample were analyzed to classify particle sizes. The resulting purities were 94.5% for the largest particles, 91.7% for the middle-sized particles, and 93.1% for the smallest particles (Fig. 4d, Supplementary Fig. 23b–d). The origin of residual impurities varied: smaller particles in a given fraction likely originated from embedded or surface-adsorbed species that were kinetically trapped during crystal growth, while larger particles originated from the uncrystallized in the previous stage. The yield of each size-separated particle fraction was determined from the masses of the initial and recovered particles, yielding 45% for the largest, 47% for the middle-sized, and 92% for the smallest fraction (Supplementary Table 1). Skipping the ‘sweating’ steps resulted in only a marginal increase in the yields of the largest and middle-sized particles, indicating that losses due to ‘sweating’ are negligible.

A trade-off exists between yield and purity. High yield and purity of the largest particles can be achieved by applying strong attraction and multiple ‘sweating’ steps at the first stage. This also improves the purity of middle-sized particles in the second stage by effectively excluding large-particle contamination. However, this comes at the cost of reduced recovery of middle-sized particles. The separated supernatant can be recycled in subsequent crystallization steps to improve the overall yield; however, dilution effects may slow crystallization kinetics and reduce throughput, requiring an enrichment step via solvent removal. Therefore, the depletant and salt concentrations should be carefully optimized depending on the relative importance of purity versus yield in the target application, and recycling of supernatant should be carefully decided by considering the material's value and process cost.

Discussion

In this study, we have established a scalable strategy for the fractional crystallization of colloidal particles by exploiting size-dependent depletion interactions. Through systematic modulation of interparticle attractions using polymer depletants and salt concentrations, we identified crystallization thresholds in terms of the pair-potential well depth for particles of various sizes and volume fractions. The threshold is approximately 3 kBT for particles around 200 nm at a volume fraction of 1.5 vol.%, which slightly decreases with increasing volume fraction and increases with particle size. The stronger depletion attraction experienced by larger particles enabled highly selective crystallization of larger particles within binary and ternary mixtures, as well as compositionally exclusive crystallization, effectively overcoming entropic mixing barriers without the need for chemical surface modification or external templates.

Leveraging these thermodynamic insights, we established a multistep fractional crystallization protocol that integrates repeated ‘sweating’ cycles—melting and recrystallization under modulated conditions—to progressively refine particle purity. Using this approach, we achieved the complete separation of ternary colloidal mixtures with sub-10% size differences into three monodisperse suspensions, each exceeding 90% purity and 45% yield. The practical resolution limit for particle separation can be reduced to approximately a 5% difference in particle size, corresponding to a size difference of ~ 10 nm for particles around 200 nm in diameter (Supplementary Fig. 24). Below this threshold, the geometric penalty associated with lattice mismatch diminishes, leading to co-assembly rather than size-selective crystallization. Although the separation throughput is approximately 0.25 mg h-1 based on the experimental scale used in this demonstration study, it can be readily increased by scaling up the batch volume, as both the crystallization and ‘sweating’ steps are equilibrium-driven processes whose durations are largely independent of batch size.

For disperse oil-in-water emulsion droplets stabilized by ionic surfactants, fractionation based on depletion attraction imposed by surfactant micelles has previously been demonstrated through multiple separation steps42. In contrast, the present study addresses equilibrium fractional crystallization of solid colloids using quantitatively controlled polymer depletion, enabling thermodynamic phase mapping and high-resolution separation of particles with small size differences. Nevertheless, the kinetically driven fractionation of fluid droplets offers a useful conceptual guide for separating the dispersed particles with a continuous and broad size distribution: an initial coarse fractionation into size groups can be achieved by inducing rapid particle assembly through strong attractive interactions, followed by more selective separation via targeted tuning of salt and depletant concentrations according to the crystallization thresholds identified in this work. Compared to centrifugation based on density mismatch, fractional crystallization requires a longer processing time and is therefore less suitable for the simple separation of particles with large size differences at the laboratory scale. Nevertheless, fractional crystallization offers a high-resolution limit (ca. 5% size difference) and strong potential for scale-up. Moreover, this approach can be extended to nanoparticle systems by appropriate selection and concentration optimization of depletants, where Brownian motion dominates particle dynamics and centrifugation becomes ineffective. Notably, the geometry dependence of depletion interactions further suggests the possibility of shape-selective separation alongside size-based fractionation50–53. By effectively overcoming the size-dispersity, this approach potentially addresses a key limitation that has limited material performance and reproducibility across optical, electronic, and functional applications.

In addition to practical applications, our findings provide a model system for investigating fundamental behaviors in soft matter. We observed crystallization pathway bifurcations, transitioning from enthalpy-driven selective and exclusive crystallization to kinetically trapped metastable crystalline alloys, as a function of attraction strength and particle mobility. These transitions illuminate the intricate balance between thermodynamic driving forces and kinetic constraints. Our results also show how particle packing and size differences influence the energy landscape of colloidal systems. Thus, this work provides a model system to investigate phase transitions, self-assembly, and metastability, which is beneficial for the rational design of colloidal building blocks and controlled assembly in optical and electronic materials.

Methods

Depletion-induced crystallization of colloidal mixtures

Monodisperse negatively charged PS nanoparticles were synthesized via emulsion polymerization. The average diameter of PS particles was adjusted to be 177, 199, and 242 nm by controlling the concentration of sodium dodecyl sulfate (SDS, 99%, Sigma-Aldrich). To synthesize PS particles with an average diameter of 621 nm, a second-stage seed polymerization was carried out using 242 nm PS seed particles. Red and green fluorescently labeled particles were synthesized by the same emulsion polymerization protocol, incorporating 0.1 wt.% of Nile red (98%, TCI) and BODIPY (99% HPLC, Sigma-Aldrich), respectively, relative to the monomer weight. The suspensions of PS particles were dialyzed for 1 month using a membrane tube (Spectra/Por 4, WMCO = 12 - 14 kg mol−1, Spectrum Laboratories) in distilled water. An ion-exchange resin (Sigma-Aldrich) was added to the water bath to remove residual ions.

Sodium chloride (NaCl, 99.5%, Junsei) and poly(acrylamide-co-acrylic acid) (p(Am-co-AAc), Mw ≈ 5,000 kg mol−1, Sigma–Aldrich) were dissolved in the aqueous unary, binary, and ternay suspensions of PS particles at various volume fractions and mixting ratios. The concentrations of salt and depletant were varied within the ranges of 5–10 mM and 0.5–1 μM, respectively. The suspensions were then incubated in a glass cell, created by sandwiching a double-sided adhesive spacer (Grace Bio-Labs SecureSeal imaging spacer, diameter × thickness of 13 mm × 0.12 mm, Sigma–Aldrich) between cover glass slips. The crystallization was observed over a period of 1.5 months at room temperature.

Separation of colloids by fractional crystallization

The fractional crystallization of colloidal mixtures was achieved by increasing the concentrations of salt and depletant stepwise. Initially, ternary colloidal mixtures consisting of 242, 199, and 177 nm particles were loaded with an aqueous solution of salt and depletant at concentrations of 8 mM and 0.8 μM, which selectively induces the crystallization of 242 nm particles predominantly. After 3 days, the colloidal crystallites were produced and sunken at the bottom, and the supernatant was carefully drained out by suction. To remove the smaller particles embedded within the crystallites, the particle-free aqueous solution of salt and depletant at the same concentrations was added and shaken to melt the crystallites, and incubated for 3 days to induce re-crystallization of 242 nm particles. This process, referred to as ‘sweating’, was repeated twice and the crystallites were molten by adding distilled water finally. The supernatant suspension was subjected to the same procedures, where an aqueous solution of salt and depletant at concentrations of 9 mM and 0.9 μM was used to selectively crystallize and separate 199 nm particles. The purity of the separated colloids was quantified using a field-emission SEM, with more than 10 images captured at randomly selected points for each sample. Image analysis was performed using Image Processing Toolbox in MATLAB R2022b. The yield was estimated by dividing the product of the dried mass of the separated colloidal fraction and its purity, determined by image analysis, by the initial colloid mass.

Computer simulation

All coarse-grained MD simulations were performed using the HOOMD-blue v 5.3.1. The pair potentials between large-to-large, large-to-small, and small-to-small particles were approximated using Mie potentials, which are built into the software. For the binary system, the simulation environment consisted of 5600 spherical particles, consisting of 2,000 large particles and 3,600 small particles, corresponding to a 1:1 volume ratio between the two species, initially arranged in random configurations within a cubic box. The simulation box was gradually compressed until the particle volume fraction reached the target volume fraction. For unary systems, simulations were performed independently for large and small particles using the same protocol. Finally, the particles were rearranged in the box under NVT (canonical) ensemble conditions using a Bussi thermostat at kBT = 2.5 kJ mol−1, with a dimensionless time step of 0.005, until the total system energy reached a stable value.

Statistics and Reproducibility

Experiments were independently repeated multiple times to confirm the reproducibility of the results. OM and CLSM measurements were repeated at least five times under identical conditions. Fractional crystallization experiments for SEM purity analysis were independently performed at least three times with consistent results. Representative data are shown in figures.

Characterization

The size and zeta potential of the synthesized PS particles were measured using SEM (SU-8230, Hitachi) and a dynamic light scattering (Zetasizer Nano ZS90, Malvern Panalytical). Zeta potentials were measured at 25 °C after diluting the particle dispersions to 0.01–0.05 wt.% in unbuffered deionized water (pH in the range 5.5–6.5). The crystallization process was monitored using optical microscopies (Eclipse L150, Nikon) in reflection mode and reflection spectra were acquired using a fiber-coupled spectrometer (USB 4000, Ocean Optics Inc.) equipped in the microscope. To directly visualize lattice structures, red and green fluorescently labeled particles were employed to form crystallites, which were observed using confocal laser scanning microscopes (LSM 980, Carl Zeiss) with 63× objectives.
