Duplex ddPCR detection of lytA and piaB in Streptococcus pneumoniae from nasopharyngeal swabs
Authors: Arnfinnur Kallsberg, Jarleivur H Magnussen, Anni Djurhuus, H-C Slotved, Marita Debess Magnussen, Shahin Gaini, Karen A Krogfelt
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractDetecting Streptococcus pneumoniae traditionally relies on complex and time-consuming culture methods, limiting sensitivity, especially in samples with low bacterial loads. Molecular diagnostics, particularly real-time PCR (qPCR), have significantly improved detection thresholds compared to culture. This pilot study aimed to assess the sensitivity and specificity of a duplex droplet digital PCR (ddPCR) assay targeting pneumococcal-specific genes lytA and piaB in nasopharyngeal swabs from asymptomatic children. A total of 165 nasopharyngeal swab samples were collected, of which ten samples were excluded during quality control, leaving 155 for analysis. These included 73 culture-positive (47%) and 82 culture-negative (53%) samples. The ddPCR analysis yielded 105 samples (68%) positive for both lytA and piaB, representing a statistically significant increase in detection compared to culture (McNemar’s test, p < 1 × 10⁻⁶). An additional 12 samples exhibited single-gene positivity (lytA only: 7, piaB only: 5) but were not classified as definitive positives due to specificity concerns. Duplex ddPCR demonstrated enhanced sensitivity, particularly in samples with low pneumococcal density, and the requirement of dual-target positivity ensured high specificity. These results underline duplex ddPCR as a promising and robust diagnostic tool for pneumococcal carriage detection, with potential implications for epidemiological surveillance and clinical diagnostics. Future research directions include assay optimization, quantitative bacterial load analysis, and evaluation of the method’s applicability for rapid pneumococcal detection in invasive disease contexts.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-25670-5.Keywords: Pneumococcal detection, Droplet digital PCR, Duplex PCR, Nasopharyngeal carriage, Bacterial diagnosticsSubject terms: Biological techniques, Diseases, Medical research, Microbiology

Introduction

Streptococcus pneumoniae is a Gram-positive bacterium responsible for diseases ranging from otitis media and pneumonia to invasive pneumococcal diseases (IPD) such as meningitis and sepsis1. Rapid and accurate identification of S. pneumoniae in clinical samples is crucial for prompt treatment and infection control. Traditional culture-based identification relies on phenotypic traits and biochemical tests, which can be time-consuming and occasionally yield atypical results or misidentify congeneric species. Furthermore, culture may fail to detect S. pneumoniae in carriers when bacterial load is low, or samples obtained after antibiotic use2.

In recent years molecular methods have significantly improved detection thresholds and sensitivity. PCR-based assays can detect far more carriers than culture, especially in post-vaccine era populations. In one study, 46–49% of paediatric swabs were positive by qPCR, compared to only ~ 9–10% by culture, illustrating the sensitivity gap between molecular and culture techniques3. Droplet digital PCR (ddPCR) is a newer technology offering high-precision, absolute quantification of DNA targets through partitioning of the PCR reaction into thousands of nanodroplets4. Each droplet acts as an individual PCR microreactor; after amplification, droplets are either classified as positive or negative for the target based on fluorescence. This enables direct counting of target DNA molecules via Poisson statistics, without the need for standard curves, and tends to be less susceptible to PCR inhibitors commonly present in clinical specimens​. These attributes suggest ddPCR could further improve detection of low-abundance bacterial DNA beyond what qPCR achieves. ddPCR has already shown higher sensitivity and accuracy than qPCR for other bacteria in samples with low template concentration, making it a compelling tool for pneumococcal carriage detection where target DNA may be scarce5.

It has previously been identified that specific pneumococcal genes include lytA and piaB. The gene lytA encodes the major autolysin of S. pneumoniae, an enzyme universally present in pneumococci, responsible for the bile solubility characteristic. These markers are considered the gold-standard marker for pneumococcal identification with high sensitivity and specificity6,7. However, instances of lytA sequences in congeneric species (e.g., Streptococcus mitis) have also been reported8. Another S. pneumoniae specific gene, piaB, encodes a permease component of an ABC iron transporter system essential for pneumococcal virulence. This gene complements lytA as a target; importantly, while piaB is present in most pneumococci, it is absent in a few serotypes (e.g., some 6B strains) and can be found in certain non-pneumococcal streptococci like Streptococcus pseudopneumoniae9. By adopting a dual-target approach, using both lytA and piaB simultaneously and requiring both targets to be positive, we greatly increase the accurate species detection. A dual-target strategy has been proposed to enhance the accuracy of qPCR-based pneumococcal detection, particularly due to the absence of a single universally specific marker gene7,10.

In this study, we deployed duplex ddPCR, to simultaneously detect lytA and piaB in nasopharyngeal swab specimens. We evaluate whether this method qualifies for pneumococcal carriage surveillance and if it demonstrates superior sensitivity over conventional culture. We also show how ddPCR results correlate with culture findings and what the implications are for clinical diagnostics. Our anticipation is that ddPCR, by virtue of its precision and sensitivity, will detect additional pneumococcal carriers missed by culture, thus providing a more accurate picture of carriage prevalence.

Materials and methods

Sample collection

A total of 165 nasopharyngeal swab samples were randomly selected from an existing anonymized biobank of specimens collected during surveillance of pneumococcal carriage in young children (aged 0–7 years). Samples were stored in serum broth (SSI Diagnostica, Denmark) with 20% glycerol at − 80 °C until processing. Each sample had previously undergone standard microbiological assessment, including culture on selective agar, optochin susceptibility, bile solubility testing, and the Immulex S. pneumoniae Omni latex test11. For culture, samples were inoculated using sterile 10 µL inoculation loops into serum broth (SSI Diagnostica, Denmark) and incubated at 36 °C under 5% CO₂ for 18–24 h prior to plating on 5% defibrinated horse blood agar supplemented with gentamicin (5 mg/L) and an optochin disk. A 10 µl inoculum from the enriched broth was used for culture, whereas ddPCR was performed directly on 200 µL of the original E-Swab transport medium. After quality control, 10 samples were excluded, leaving 155 for analysis (73 culture-positive, 82 culture-negative).

A panel of nine S.pneumoniae isolates from clinical specimens and two reference strains (ATCC 6305 and ATCC 49,619) were used as positive controls. All eleven isolates had previously been confirmed positive for the target genes by in-house PCR.

Nasopharyngeal swabs were transported at 4 °C. If cultured immediately, samples were processed upon arrival; otherwise, they were stored at − 80 °C within 8 h of collection. Aliquots were prepared in 20% glycerol to minimize freeze–thaw cycles, and all stored samples were analysed within ≤ 3 months.

Sample preparation

Before molecular analysis, samples were thawed, and genomic DNA was extracted using the KingFisher Flex automated purification system. Total DNA was isolated using the MagMAX Viral/Pathogen Nucleic Acid Isolation Kit (Applied Biosystems) from Copan E-Swab 482C transport medium swabs (Copan, Italy). Briefly, 200 µL of each thawed swab specimen was lysed with proteinase K at 56 °C for 30 min, followed by binding of nucleic acids to magnetic beads and successive wash steps and DNA was eluted in 100 µL of elution buffer. Each sample was extracted once using the MagMAX Viral/Pathogen kit on a KingFisher Flex instrument with 200 µL input and 100 µL elution. Extraction blanks (water in place of specimen) were included in each run and were carried through the ddPCR assay, all yielding negative results. The yield and concentration of purified DNA were measured using a Qubit dsDNA Quantification Assay (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Because DNA content varied greatly between samples, a 5 µL aliquot of each extract was used for Qubit measurement to improve accuracy on low concentration samples. Eluted DNA was stored at − 20 °C in 100 µL aliquots until ddPCR setup, avoiding repeated freeze–thaw cycles.

Prior to digital PCR, a conventional PCR with gel electrophoresis was performed to verify that the chosen primers for lytA and piaB produced correct amplicons. This step confirmed the primers amplified the expected target from positive control DNA without producing false bands. Gel analysis of the PCR products showed that both lytA and piaB primer sets were functional. Once both primer sets yielded clear single bands of the expected size on agarose gel, we proceeded to ddPCR. The primers/probes used are seen in Table 1. The amplicon lengths were 75 bp (lytA) and 104 bp (piaB). Both are single-copy loci in the pneumococcal genome.

Duplex ddPCR assay design

We designed a duplex ddPCR assay to detect lytA and piaB in the same reaction. Primer and probe sequences targeting conserved regions of lytA (autolysin gene) and piaB (pia operon permease gene) were used as originally described by Carvalho et al.7 and Trzciński et al.10, respectively, and have been widely applied in pneumococcal carriage surveillance. Each probe was labelled with a distinct fluorescent reporter dye: the lytA probe was 5′-labeled with HEX and the piaB probe with FAM, allowing simultaneous detection of the two targets in separate fluorescence channels​. Primers were synthesized by IDT and resuspended to 10 µM stocks. Optimal annealing temperature for the duplex was determined to be 55 °C based on preliminary gradient PCR and Bio-Rad manufacturer recommendations, ensuring both targets could be amplified efficiently in the same reaction. Assay optimization included gradient PCR to determine annealing temperature and probe concentration titration. Limits of blank and detection were not formally established in this pilot study. No dedicated inhibition testing was performed, which we acknowledge as a limitation.

Each 20 µL ddPCR reaction contained: 5 µL of template DNA, 900 nM of each primer (forward and reverse for lytA and piaB), 250 nM of each fluorescent hydrolysis probe, 1 × Bio-Rad ddPCR Supermix for Probes (No dUTP), and nuclease-free water to volume. We loaded each sample reaction into the sample well of a DG8 Droplet Generator Cartridge, along with 70 µL of Droplet Generation Oil in the oil well and created droplets using the Bio-Rad QX200 Droplet Generator according to the manufacturer’s instructions. This process partitioned each PCR mixture into ~ 15,000–20,000 nanodroplets. The droplet emulsions were then transferred to a 96-well PCR plate, sealed, and amplified in a conventional thermal cycler. The thermal cycling conditions for the duplex ddPCR assay were optimized to ensure efficient amplification while maintaining droplet stability. The cycling parameters are summarized in Table 2.

Post-PCR droplet reading and interpretation

After PCR, plates were loaded into the Bio-Rad QX200 Droplet Reader. Each droplet from every well was analysed individually for HEX and FAM fluorescence intensity. Bio-Rad QuantaSoft Software was used to threshold droplets and count the number of FAM-positive (green, piaB), HEX-positive (orange, lytA), double-positive (both genes), and double-negative (no target) droplets per sample. Thresholds for positivity in each channel were set manually using droplet amplitude profiles generated from control wells. Positive controls consisted of S. pneumoniae ATCC strains containing both lytA and piaB, and no-template controls consisted of nuclease-free water.

For each clinical sample, the presence of S. pneumoniae was determined by the pattern of droplet positivity: only samples with both lytA and piaB positive droplets were called pneumococcus positive. In practice, this means a sample showing a cluster of HEX + FAM + droplets (double-positive) or at least distinct clusters of single-positive droplets in both the HEX (lytA) and FAM (piaB) channels was interpreted as containing S. pneumoniae DNA. If droplets were positive for only one of the two targets (HEX-positive lytA without any FAM-positive piaB, or vice versa), the sample was not counted as definitively pneumococcal. This stringent interpretation follows from known specificity issues when using single markers: for example, piaB-only positives could arise from S. pseudopneumoniae (which lacks lytA), and lytA-only positives could be S. mitis or a pneumococcal strain missing the piaB gene. By requiring both genes, we aimed to minimize false positives. This approach is supported by genomic analyses from Tavares et al.9, who showed that while lytA and piaB can each occur individually in some non-pneumococcal Streptococcus species, there are no documented cases of both genes being present in the same non-pneumococcal strain. More recently, Ahlers et al.12 analysed over 9,300 pneumococcal and non-pneumococcal genomes and confirmed that combined detection of lytA and piaB yields 100% specificity for S. pneumoniae, further validating this dual-marker strategy. Although occasional runs exhibited less distinct separation in Channel 2 (piaB), the positive controls consistently demonstrated sufficient amplitude separation for reliable thresholding, ensuring robust classification of droplets.

To evaluate concordance between methods, the original culture results for each sample (recorded as positive or negative for S. pneumoniae by traditional methods) were compared against the ddPCR findings. Culture had been performed by plating the swabs on 5% defibrinated horse blood agar supplemented with gentamicin (5 mg/L) and an optochin disk after enrichment in serum broth, followed by identification of pneumococcal colonies via morphology, optochin sensitivity, and bile solubility in the routine diagnostic lab. For study purposes, we treated the culture result as the comparator “standard” method, while recognizing that culture has imperfect sensitivity. Any sample that was culture-positive but ddPCR-negative was flagged for further investigation, which involved reviewing the original culture records and, when residual material was available, repeating ddPCR to rule out technical or handling errors. In this study, only one such case occurred, where droplet generation failed due to a technical issue. Conversely, samples that were culture-negative but ddPCR-positive for both targets were considered probable additional pneumococcal carriers missed by culture. To ensure consistency, median accepted droplets per well were 16,985 (IQR 15,417–18,200; range 10,839–20,386). Wells with < 10,000 droplets were excluded. Droplet classification was performed in Bio-Rad QuantaSoft Software (v2.1), which used a partition volume of 0.85 nL for concentration calculations. Droplets in ‘rain’ regions were assigned based on proximity to cluster centroids and overall cluster density; thresholds were manually defined from control wells.

Statistical analysis

To evaluate differences in detection yield, a 2 × 2 contingency table was constructed (ddPCR dual-target rule vs. culture) using the 155 clinical samples (controls were excluded). Agreement between paired binary outcomes was tested using McNemar’s test. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

To further assess assay performance, ROC curve analysis was performed for lytA and piaB copy numbers (copies/µL) with culture as the reference standard. “No Call” results were treated as 0 copies/µL. The optimal cut-offs were determined using the Youden index (J = Sensitivity + Specificity − 1). Diagnostic accuracy (sensitivity, specificity, PPV, NPV, Cohen’s κ) was calculated at the Youden thresholds and compared with our predefined dual-target rule (positive if both lytA and piaB > 0 copies/µL).

Duplex ddPCR detection vs culture

Among the 155 samples analysed, ddPCR identified 105 dual-target positives (68%), compared to 73 positives (47%) detected by culture (Fig. 5), representing a ~ 40% increase in detection relative to culture. All dual-target negative samples were also culture-negative. Twelve samples showed single-gene positivity (7 lytA-only, 5 piaB-only) and, under our dual-gene criterion, were not classified as ddPCR positives. Of the 73 culture-positive samples, 70 were dual-target positive and 3 were single-gene positive (2 lytA-only, 1 piaB-only). Among the 82 culture-negative samples, ddPCR classified 35 as dual-target positive, 38 as dual-target negative, and 9 as single-gene positive (5 lytA-only, 4 piaB-only). For method comparison, single-gene results were grouped with ddPCR negatives, giving 70 true positives, 35 false positives, 3 false negatives, and 47 true negatives. This corresponded to a sensitivity of 95.9%, specificity of 57.3%, PPV of 66.7%, NPV of 94.0%, and McNemar’s χ2 ≈ 25.3 (p < 1 × 10⁻⁶).

Discussion

This study provides evidence that duplex ddPCR is a sensitive and specific approach for detecting S. pneumoniae in nasopharyngeal swabs, showing improved detection compared with culture. ddPCR identified 105 carriers (68%), compared to 73 (47%) detected by culture. All culture-positive samples were detected by ddPCR for at least one target gene; however, three culture-positive samples showed single-gene positivity (two lytA-only, one piaB-only) and were therefore classified as ddPCR-negative under our strict dual-target rule. This conservative approach ensures high specificity but may undercount rare pneumococcal variants lacking one target gene. In contrast, among the 82 culture-negative samples, ddPCR identified 35 dual-target positives and 9 single-gene positives (5 lytA-only, 4 piaB-only), highlighting ddPCR’s ability to detect carriers that culture missed, especially in low-density samples. These findings align with previous molecular studies13 and support ddPCR as a robust alternative for pneumococcal carriage detection.

In addition to detecting more carriers overall, an important observation was that all culture-positive samples were detected by ddPCR for at least one target gene, indicating that the assay did not yield any dual false negatives. This translates to an estimated sensitivity of 95.9% and a negative predictive value of 94.0%. As illustrated in Fig. 4, all dual-negative samples clustered separately, supporting the reliability of ddPCR in correctly identifying true negatives. These results highlight that ddPCR provides a highly sensitive diagnostic approach, particularly valuable in low-density carriage samples where culture frequently fails.

While this study did not include a direct qPCR comparison, prior work has consistently demonstrated a hierarchy in detection sensitivity: culture < qPCR < ddPCR. Multiple studies have shown that qPCR detects substantially more pneumococcal carriers than culture—for example, Ricketson et al. reported carriage prevalence by qPCR of ~ 46–49% compared to 9–10% by culture in children3 and Lemay et al. observed similar trends in post-vaccine populations13. In turn, ddPCR has been shown to outperform qPCR in other bacterial targets when template concentrations are low or PCR inhibitors are present, owing to its reaction partitioning and absolute molecule counting rather than reliance on Ct values4,5. For pathogens such as Legionella pneumophila and Mycobacterium tuberculosis, ddPCR has yielded improved sensitivity and reproducibility compared to qPCR, particularly near the limit of detection5. These features suggest that ddPCR may detect pneumococcal DNA in cases where qPCR yields equivocal or negative results, especially in low-density carriers or samples dominated by human DNA background. However, a direct head-to-head evaluation of ddPCR and qPCR for pneumococcal carriage detection remains an important goal for future research.

The dual-target design using lytA and piaB ensured high specificity, with all dual-positive cases considered true pneumococcal detections. While this approach sacrifices some sensitivity by excluding single-gene positives, it helps avoid false positives from related species such as S. pseudopneumoniae. As noted in the Results, we observed 12 single-gene positives (7 lytA-only, 5 piaB-only), including 2 lytA-only cases among culture-positive samples. These ambiguous results may reflect biological variability or stochastic effects in low-copy samples. Reflex testing or the addition of a third marker, such as Xisco12,14, could improve interpretability in future applications.

A theoretical limitation of dual-target ddPCR is the possibility that a droplet could contain DNA from two different bacterial species—one carrying lytA and another carrying piaB—producing an apparent double-positive signal. Given that ddPCR partitions reactions into ~ 20,000 droplets and typically operates under limiting dilution, Poisson statistics predict that the probability of such independent lytA- and piaB-containing genomes co-encapsulating is vanishingly small4. Nevertheless, in high-biomass or polymicrobial samples, this scenario cannot be entirely excluded. We therefore interpret double-positive signals as highly specific for S. pneumoniae but acknowledge that a small fraction could theoretically arise from mixed carriage. Future work could quantify this probability more precisely through droplet occupancy modelling or single-cell genomic approaches.

Of the 73 culture-positive samples, three showed positivity for only one target gene: two were lytA-only (no piaB detected) and one was piaB-only (no lytA detected). All three isolates were confirmed as S. pneumoniae by standard diagnostic methods (morphology, selective media, optochin susceptibility, bile solubility, and Immulex S. pneumoniae Omni latex agglutination11). Whole-genome sequencing was not pursued because species-level identification was already established. The absence of the second target in these cases may reflect biological variability or target dropout in low-copy samples8,12.

ddPCR’s capacity to detect low-copy targets stems from its partitioning principle, allowing amplification in isolated droplets with minimal interference from inhibitors4. Even in samples with high human DNA or potential inhibitors, ddPCR performed reliably. This characteristic makes it particularly suited for low-density carriage studies or variable-quality clinical samples. Moreover, this partitioning enables more accurate detection of rare targets than conventional PCR performed on non-partitioned reaction mixtures, making ddPCR an attractive option for studies requiring high analytical sensitivity.

The absolute quantification feature of ddPCR provides additional insight. Although this study focused on binary detection, the ability to measure gene copies per microliter opens the possibility of evaluating carriage density. Higher density has been linked to increased transmission risk and disease progression15. The quantitative output of ddPCR could also be used to establish bacterial load thresholds associated with heightened infectiousness, enabling identification of carriers most likely to contribute to onward transmission. Future studies could use ddPCR quantification to define thresholds distinguishing active colonization from transient or clinically insignificant presence. When correlated with epidemiological thresholds in larger, longitudinal datasets, such quantitative data could also inform targeted public health interventions. For instance, identifying and prioritizing individuals or communities at highest risk of transmitting pneumococcus during outbreak situations.

Despite its strengths, ddPCR has limitations. It requires specialized instruments, droplet generation oil, and longer processing times than qPCR. This can challenge high-throughput workflows and limit routine diagnostic use. However, the method is well suited to reference laboratories, epidemiological surveillance, and vaccine impact studies, where sensitivity is critical. Advances in microfluidics and multiplexing technologies may eventually reduce barriers to clinical implementation.

The assay could also be applied to normally sterile specimens (e.g., CSF or blood) to detect pneumococcus in cases of invasive disease where culture may fail due to prior antibiotic treatment. The high specificity from dual-target detection is particularly valuable in sterile-site diagnostics, where false positives carry clinical consequences. ddPCR may also aid in post-vaccine surveillance by identifying shifts in carriage prevalence or density and could be expanded to include serotype-specific targets in a multiplex format, like multiple pathogen detection16.

Although our sample size was limited and focused on a paediatric population, the findings support broader validation. Inclusion of adult cohorts, individuals with different vaccination statuses, and participants from varied geographic and epidemiological settings, particularly those with lower carriage density, could further evaluate assay performance and broaden its applicability for surveillance. In addition, integrating whole-genome sequencing (WGS), serotyping, and multilocus sequence typing (MLST) in future work would enable detailed strain-level characterization of pneumococci detected by ddPCR, as demonstrated in Danish serotype-focused investigations17. Such analyses could help distinguish vaccine and non-vaccine types, assess genetic diversity, and link carriage to transmission patterns or disease risk. These findings highlight that Qubit-derived DNA concentrations reflect host DNA rather than bacterial DNA. Including a human DNA marker in future ddPCR assays could therefore help verify sample integrity, assess extraction efficiency, and enable normalization of pneumococcal load to host cell content.

Conclusion

Duplex ddPCR targeting lytA and piaB shows promise as a sensitive and specific method for pneumococcal detection, demonstrating higher detection rates than culture in this pilot study. The method is robust, quantitative, and suitable for low-density or challenging samples. With further optimization, including multiplexing and automation, ddPCR could support clinical diagnostics, vaccine surveillance, and infectious disease monitoring in both research and clinical settings.
