Lattices from Pointed Building Sets: Generalized Ornamentation Lattices
Andrew Sack

Abstract
. We introduce a novel combinatorial structure called pointed building sets , which can be viewed as families of lattices equipped with compatibility relations. To each pointed building set 𝖡 \mathsf{B} , we associate a complete lattice 𝒪 ⁡ ( 𝖡 ) \mathcal{O}(\mathsf{B}) , called the ornamentation lattice of 𝖡 \mathsf{B} . Special cases of this construction have already proven useful in understanding the structure of three families of posets: operahedron lattices, the affine Tamari lattice, and hypergraphic posets of subhypergraphs of the path hypergraph of an increasing tree. The goal of this paper is to establish the theory of these generalized ornamentations. We examine several natural classes of pointed building sets which recover classical lattices such as the Tamari lattice, the lattice of topologies ordered by coarsening, and the lattice of naturally labeled partial orders. Furthermore, several theoretical directions are explored, including inverse limits and group actions. Notably, this leads to a straightforward construction of inverse limits of Tamari lattices, yielding infinite analogs of the Tamari lattice.

The main goal of this paper is to introduce and explore the theory of ornamentation lattices. The structure of this paper is as follows. In

Preliminaries

We assume familiarity with the theory of posets (partially ordered sets), a standard reference for which is
[
24
]
*Chapter 3. We further define
[n]:=\{1,\dots,n\}
.

Let
P=(X,\preceq)
be a poset and
D=(V,E)
be a directed graph.
The
dual
of
P
(denoted
P^{\mathrm{op}}=(X,\preceq_{\mathrm{op}})
) is a poset on
X
with
x\preceq_{\mathrm{op}}y
if and only if
y\preceq x
. The
dual
of
D
(denoted
D^{\mathrm{op}}
) is the directed graph on the same vertex set as
D
but with all edge orientations reversed. We say
D
is a
directed tree
if the underlying undirected graph of
D
is a tree. A
directed path
in

is a sequence of vertices (possibly containing only 1 element)
v_{1},\dots,v_{n}
such that there is an edge
(v_{i},v_{i+1})
for each
1\leq i\leq n-1
.
If
D
is acyclic, we define a poset
(V,\preceq_{D})
by
u\preceq_{D}v
if and only if there exists a directed path from
u
to
v
in
D
.

For
x,y\in P
with
x\preceq y
, the
(closed) interval
from
x
to
y
is the set

[x,y]:=\{z\in P:x\preceq z\preceq y\},

which we view as a subposet of
P
. Further define the
open interval

(x,y):=\{z\in P:x\prec z\prec y\}

and the
half-open
intervals

\displaystyle:=\{z\in P:x\prec z\preceq y\}.

For convenience, we define
[x,x):=\{x\}
in this paper.

If
x\prec y
and
[x,y]=\{x,y\}
, then we say
y

covers

x
and write
x\prec\mathrel{\mkern-5.0mu}\mathrel{\cdot}y
. For
x\in P
, let

\Delta_{P}(x)=\{z\in P:z\preceq x\}\quad\text{and}\quad\nabla_{P}(x)=\{z\in P:x\preceq z\}.

At times it will be convenient to use the notation
[x,\infty)
in place of
\nabla_{P}(x)
.

A poset
L
is called a lattice if any pair of elements
x,y\in L
has a least upper bound called the
join
and denoted
x\vee y
and a greatest lower bound called the
meet
and denoted
x\wedge y
. A lattice is called
complete
if any arbitrary subset
\Omega\subseteq L
has a least upper bound
\bigvee\Omega
and greatest lower bound
\bigwedge\Omega
. Note that all finite lattices are complete.

A lattice
L
is called
meet-semidistributive
if for all elements
x,y,z\in L
satisfying
x\wedge y=x\wedge z
, we have
x\wedge y=x\wedge(y\vee z)
. We say
L
is
join-semidistributive
if for all
x,y,z\in L
satisfying
x\vee y=x\vee z
, we have
x\vee y=x\vee(y\wedge z)
. We call
L

semidistributive
if
L
is both meet-semidistributive and join-semidistributive.
An element
x\in L
is called
join-irreducible
if for all finite non-empty subsets
\Omega\subseteq L
such that
\bigvee\Omega=x
we have
x\in\Omega
.

Let
P
be a poset with unique minimal element
\hat{0}
. An element
x\in P
is called an
atom
if it covers
\hat{0}
.
A complete lattice is called
atomic
(or sometimes
atomistic
) if every element is a join of a (possibly infinite) set of atoms.

Let
\mathcal{I}
be a finite set. A
building set

[
10
,
21
]

X
on
\mathcal{I}
is a collection of non-empty subsets of
\mathcal{I}
satisfying

(1)

for all
i\in\mathcal{I}
,
\{i\}\in X
;

(2)

for all
I,J\in X
, if
I\cap J\neq\emptyset
, then
I\cup J\in X
.

3.
Examples

Every finite lattice is an ornamentation lattice for some pointed building set as the following example shows.

Example 3.1
.

Let
X
be a set and let
Y\subseteq 2^{X}
be any collection of subsets of
X
that is closed under unions. We take
\mathcal{I}=X\sqcup\{\hat{0}\}
where
\sqcup
denotes disjoint union. Define

\mathsf{B}=\{(\{i\},i)\mid i\in\mathcal{I}\}\cup\{(Z\sqcup\{\hat{0}\},\hat{0})\mid Z\in Y\}.

It is known that every finite lattice
L=(X,\preceq)
is isomorphic (as a poset) to a union-closed family of sets ordered under inclusion. In particular, consider the union-closed family
Y=\{S_{x}\mid x\in X\}
where

S_{x}=\{y\in L\mid x\not\preceq y\}.

Then
\mathcal{O}(\mathsf{B})
is isomorphic to
L
.

As all lattices are ornamentation lattices, one should instead view this as a construction for combining the different lattices
\mathsf{B}|_{i}
to make a new lattice. However, in practice there are many natural classes of pointed building sets and therefore natural classes of ornamentation lattices.

Example 3.2
.

Let
G=(V,E)
be a graph. Let

\mathsf{B}(G)=\{(C,i)\subseteq V\mid C\text{ is connected in $G$ and }i\in C\}.

We call
\mathsf{B}(G)
the
graphical pointed building set
of

.

Example 3.3
.

Let

be an (unpointed) building set on

. Then take

Example 3.4

Let

Remark 4.1
.

For each
i\in\mathcal{I}
, we will identify
\mathsf{B}|_{i}
with an isomorphic copy of
\mathsf{B}|_{i}
in
\mathcal{O}(\mathsf{B})
as follows. For
(S,i)\in\mathsf{B}|_{i}

\rho_{(S,i)}(j):=\begin{cases}(S,i)&j=i\\
(\{j\},j)&j\neq i.\end{cases}

Furthermore, this copy is a principal order ideal in

with maximum
\rho_{\max(\mathsf{B}|_{i})}
.

Lemma 4.2
.

For all pointed building sets
\mathsf{B}
, the poset
(\mathcal{O}(\mathsf{B}),\preceq)
is bounded, that is, has a unique minimal and unique maximal element.

Proof.

It immediately follows that
\rho:\mathcal{I}\to\mathsf{B}
defined by
\rho(i)=(\{i\},i)
is an ornamentation and is minimal.
Define
\sigma:\mathcal{I}\to\mathsf{B}
by
\sigma(i)=\max(\mathsf{B}|_{i}).

We claim that
\sigma
is an ornamentation. Indeed, suppose that
j\in\sigma(i)
. Then
(\sigma(i)\cup\sigma(j),i)\in\mathsf{B}
which is contained in
\sigma(i)
because
\sigma(i)
is maximal in
\mathsf{B}|_{i}
. It is immediate that
\sigma
is maximal by construction.
∎

Proof of
Theorem

1.4
.

As
(\mathcal{O}(\mathsf{B}),\preceq)
is bounded, it suffices to show that it is a complete meet-semilattice. Let
\Omega\subseteq\mathcal{O}(\mathsf{B})
. We claim that
\bigwedge\Omega
is the pointwise meet of
\Omega
. More precisely, define
\lambda:\mathcal{I}\to\mathsf{B}
by
\lambda(i):=\bigwedge_{\rho\in\Omega}\rho(i)
where the meet of each
\rho(i)
is taken in
\mathsf{B}|_{i}
.

Alternatively, for
i\in\mathcal{I}
define
\Omega|_{i}:=\bigcap_{\rho\in\Omega}\rho(i)
. Then

\lambda(i)=\left(\bigcup\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}S\in\mathsf{B}|_{i}\\
S\subseteq\Omega|_{i}\end{subarray}}S,\ i\right).

That is,
\lambda(i)
is the largest pointed set in
\mathsf{B}
contained in
\Omega|_{i}
pointed at
i
.

We claim that
\lambda
is an ornamentation. Let
j\in\lambda(i)
. Then for all
\rho\in\Omega
we have
j\in\rho(i)
. We conclude that

\lambda(j)\subseteq\rho(j)\subseteq\rho(i),

and so
\lambda(j)\subseteq\Omega|_{i}
.

As
(\lambda(i),i),\;(\lambda(j),j)\in\mathsf{B}
we have that
\left(\lambda(i)\cup\lambda(j),\;i\right)\in\mathsf{B}
. Then by the maximality of
\lambda(i)
, we have that
\lambda(j)\subseteq\left(\lambda(i)\cup\lambda(j)\right)\subseteq\lambda(i)
. Hence
\lambda
is an ornamentation.

For all
\rho\in\Omega
and
i\in\mathcal{I}
,
\lambda(i)\subseteq\rho(i)
, and so
\lambda\preceq\rho
. Furthermore, if
\sigma\in\mathcal{O}(\mathsf{B})
such that for all
\rho\in\Omega
and
i\in\mathcal{I}
we have
\sigma(i)\subseteq\rho(i)
, then
\sigma(i)\subseteq\Omega|_{i}
and hence
\sigma(i)\subseteq\lambda(i)
.

Hence
\lambda=\bigwedge\Omega
.
∎

. Observe that

is not necessarily an ornamentation.

Definition 4.4
.

Define a directed graph
D(\sigma)
on
\mathcal{I}
with edge set

\{\left(i,\;j\right)\mid j\in\sigma(i)\}.

For
i\in\mathcal{I}
, let

\pi(i):=\{j\in\mathcal{I}\mid\text{there exists a directed path from $i$ to $j$ in }D(\sigma)\}.

Definition 4.5
.

Define
\nu:\mathcal{I}\to\mathsf{B}
by

\nu(i):=\left(\bigcup\limits_{j\in\pi(i)}\sigma(j),\,\,i\right).

We will show that
\nu=\bigvee\Omega
.

Lemma 4.6
.

If
i\in\mathcal{I}
, then for all
j\in\nu(i)
, there is a path from
i
to
j
in
D(\sigma)
.

Proof.

Observe that if
j\in\nu(i)
then there exists
k\in\mathcal{I}
such that
j\in\sigma(k)
and there is a path from
i
to
k
in
D(\sigma)
. But then there is an edge from
k
to
j
, so there is a path from
i
to
j
in
D(\sigma)
.
∎

Lemma 4.7
.

\nu
is an ornamentation.

Proof.

First we show that for each
i\in\mathcal{I}
, we have
\nu(i)\in\mathsf{B}
. Let

\mathcal{P}=\{P_{j}\mid j\in\mathcal{I}\text{ and $P_{j}$ is a path from $i$ to $j$ in $D(\sigma)$}\}.

As pointed building sets are transitively closed, for each
P\in\mathcal{P}
,
\left(\bigcup_{j\in P}\sigma(j),\,i\right)\in\mathsf{B}
.
We can write
\nu(i)
as

\nu(i)=\left(\bigcup\limits_{P\in\mathcal{P}}\bigcup_{j\in P}\sigma(j),\,\,i\right)

which is a union of sets pointed at
i
and is hence in
\mathsf{B}
.

Let
j\in\nu(i)
and let
k\in\nu(j)
. By
Lemma

4.6
, there exists a path from
i
to
j
and a path from
j
to
k
in
D(\sigma)
. Therefore, there is a path from
i
to
k
in
D(\sigma)
. We conclude that
k\in\sigma(k)\subseteq\nu(i)
and hence
\nu(j)\subseteq\nu(i)
.
∎

Theorem 4.8
.

For all
\Omega\subseteq\mathcal{O}(\mathsf{B})
, we have that
\nu=\bigvee\Omega
.

Proof.

First observe that by construction, for all
\rho\in\Omega
we have
\rho\preceq\nu
. Let
\nu^{\prime}\in\mathcal{O}(\mathsf{B})
be such that for all
\rho\in\Omega
we have
\rho\preceq\nu^{\prime}
. It is immediate that for all
i\in\mathcal{I}
,
\sigma(i)\subseteq\nu^{\prime}(i)
.

Define a directed graph
D(\nu^{\prime})
on
\mathcal{I}
with edge set
\{(i,j)\mid j\in\nu^{\prime}(i)\}.

For
i\in\mathcal{I}
let

\pi^{\prime}(i):=\{j\in\mathcal{I}\mid\text{there exists a path from $i$ to $j$ in }D(\nu^{\prime})\}.

Observe that if
(i,j)
is an edge in
D(\sigma)
then
(i,j)
is also an edge in
D(\nu^{\prime})
. Then it follows that
\pi(i)\subseteq\pi^{\prime}(i)
for all
i
. As
\nu^{\prime}
is an ornamentation,
\pi^{\prime}(i)=\nu^{\prime}(i)
for all
i
.

For
i\in\mathcal{I}
, we have

\displaystyle\nu(i)

\displaystyle=\bigcup\limits_{j\in\pi(i)}\sigma(j)

\displaystyle\subseteq\bigcup\limits_{j\in\pi^{\prime}(i)}\sigma(j)

\displaystyle\subseteq\bigcup\limits_{j\in\pi^{\prime}(i)}\nu^{\prime}(j)

\displaystyle=\nu^{\prime}(i).

Hence
\nu\preceq\nu^{\prime}
and
\nu=\bigvee\Omega
.
∎

4.2.
Lattice Properties

In this section, we discuss several properties of ornamentation lattices.

Proposition 4.9
.

\mathcal{O}(\mathsf{B})
is atomic if and only if
\mathsf{B}|_{i}
is atomic for each
i\in\mathcal{I}
.

Proof.

Suppose each
\mathsf{B}|_{i}
is atomic. Let
\rho\in\mathcal{O}(\mathsf{B})
and for
i\in\mathcal{I}
, let
\rho_{i}:\mathcal{I}\to\mathsf{B}
be defined by

\rho_{i}(j)=\begin{cases}\rho(j)&j=i\\
(\{j\},j)&j\neq i.\end{cases}

Clearly
\rho=\bigvee\limits_{i\in\mathcal{I}}\rho_{i}
, and as each
\mathsf{B}|_{i}
is atomic, each
\rho_{i}
is a join of atoms.

Conversely, suppose
\mathcal{O}(\mathsf{B})
is atomic. Then
\mathsf{B}|_{i}
is atomic as it is an order ideal in
\mathcal{O}(\mathsf{B})
.
∎

Lemma 4.10
.

\rho\in\mathcal{O}(\mathsf{B})
is join-irreducible if and only if

•

\rho=\rho_{(S,i)}
for some
(S,i)\in\mathsf{B}
and

•

(S,i)
is join-irreducible in
\mathsf{B}|_{i}
.

Proof.

It is automatic that if
(S,i)
is join-irreducible in
\mathsf{B}|_{i}
, then
\rho_{(S,i)}
is join-irreducible in
\mathcal{O}(\mathsf{B})
.

Now suppose that
\rho\in\mathcal{O}(\mathsf{B})
is join-irreducible. First we show that
\rho=\rho_{(S,i)}
for some
(S,i)\in\mathsf{B}
. Suppose not. Then there exist
i,j\in\mathcal{I}
such that
|\rho(i)|,|\rho(j)|\geq 2
. Then define
\rho^{i}\in\mathcal{O}(\mathsf{B})
by

\rho^{i}(k)=\begin{cases}(\{i\},i)&k=i\\
\rho(k)&k\neq i\end{cases}

and similarly for
\rho^{j}
. Then we have
\rho=\rho^{i}\vee\rho^{j}
, so
\rho
is not join-irreducible.

Then
\rho=\rho_{(S,i)}
for some
(S,i)\in\mathsf{B}
and it is automatic that
(S,i)
must be join-irreducible in
\mathsf{B}|_{i}
.

∎

Definition 4.11
.

A pointed building set
\mathsf{B}
is
acyclic
if whenever
(S,i),(T,j)\in\mathsf{B}
such that
i\in T\text{ and }j\in S
then
i=j
.

Remark 4.12
.

The digraphical pointed building set of an acyclic directed graph is acyclic.

Lemma 4.13
.

Suppose
\mathsf{B}
is finite and acyclic, and let
\rho,\sigma\in\mathcal{O}(\mathsf{B})
such that
\rho\prec\mathrel{\mkern-5.0mu}\mathrel{\cdot}\sigma
. Then there exists a unique
i\in\mathcal{I}
such that
\rho(i)\subsetneq\sigma(i)
and for all other
j\in\mathcal{I},\rho(j)=\sigma(j)
.
Furthermore, at least one of the following two holds:

•

there exists
(S,i)\in\mathsf{B}|_{i}
such that
\sigma(i)=\left(\bigcup\limits_{j\in S}\rho(j),\ i\right)

•

\rho(i)\prec\mathrel{\mkern-5.0mu}\mathrel{\cdot}\sigma(i)
in
\mathsf{B}|_{i}
.

Proof.

Let
\rho\prec\mathrel{\mkern-5.0mu}\mathrel{\cdot}\sigma
. By definition, there must exist
i\in\mathcal{I}
such that
\rho(i)\subsetneq\sigma(i)
. Now let

X:=\{i\in\mathcal{I}\mid\rho(i)\subsetneq\sigma(i)\}.

Suppose by way of contradiction that
|X|\geq 2
. Define a partial order on
X
where
i\preceq_{X}j
if
j\in\sigma(i)
, and pick
i\in X
minimal with respect to
\preceq_{X}
. We note that
\preceq_{X}
is a partial order as
\mathsf{B}
is acyclic and has a minimal element as
\mathcal{I}
is finite.
Then define
\pi\in\mathcal{O}(\mathsf{B})
by

\pi(k)=\begin{cases}\sigma(k)&k=i\\
\rho(k)&k\neq i.\end{cases}

We claim that
\pi
is an ornamentation. If this holds, we will have that
\rho\prec\pi\prec\sigma
, contradicting that
\rho\prec\mathrel{\mkern-5.0mu}\mathrel{\cdot}\sigma
. The only non-trivial condition to check is transitive closure when
k\neq i
and
i\in\pi(k)=\rho(k)
. We claim that
\rho(k)=\sigma(k)
. Otherwise,
k\in X
and

i\in\rho(k)\subseteq\sigma(k)
, so
k\preceq_{X}i
.
By minimality of
i\in X
, this forces
k\notin X
, a contradiction, so
\rho(k)=\sigma(k)
.
Since
\sigma
is an ornamentation and
i\in\sigma(k)
, we have

\sigma(i)\subseteq\sigma(k)=\rho(k)=\pi(k),

so the closure condition holds.

Now suppose that
\rho(i)\not\prec\mathrel{\mkern-5.0mu}\mathrel{\cdot}\sigma(i)
in
\mathsf{B}|_{i}
. Then there exists
(S,i)\in\mathsf{B}|_{i}
such that
\rho(i)\subsetneq(S,i)\subsetneq\sigma(i)
. Let

(T,i)=\left(\bigcup\limits_{j\in S}\rho(j),\ i\right).

Observe that for each
j\in S
, we have
\left(\rho(j)\cup S,i\right)\in\mathsf{B}|_{i}
, so

(T,i)=\bigcup\limits_{j\in S}(\rho(j)\cup(S,i))\in\mathsf{B}|_{i}.

Define
\tau:\mathcal{I}\to\mathsf{B}
by

\tau(j)=\begin{cases}(T,i)&j=i\\
\rho(j)&j\neq i.\end{cases}

As
(S,i)\subseteq\sigma(i)
and for each
j\in S,\rho(j)\subseteq\sigma(i)
we have
(T,i)\subseteq\sigma(i)
. We claim that
\tau\in\mathcal{O}(\mathsf{B})
. It suffices to check that

•

if
j\in\mathcal{I}-\{i\}
and
i\in\rho(j)
, then
(T,i)\subseteq\rho(j)
and

•

if
j\in\mathcal{I}
and
j\in(T,i)
, then
\rho(j)\subseteq(T,i)
.

In the first case, suppose that
j\in\mathcal{I}-\{i\}
and
i\in\rho(j)
. Then

(T,i)\subseteq\sigma(i)\subseteq\sigma(j)=\rho(j).

In the second case, suppose that
j\in(T,i)
. Then there exists
k\in S
such that
j\in\rho(k)
. Hence
\rho(j)\subseteq\rho(k)\subseteq(T,i)
.

Hence
\rho\prec\tau\preceq\sigma
and as
\rho\prec\mathrel{\mkern-5.0mu}\mathrel{\cdot}\sigma
we find that
\tau=\sigma
.

∎

Remark 4.14

If
\mathsf{B}

Lemma 4.15
.

Suppose
\mathsf{B}
is a finite, acyclic pointed building set over
\mathcal{I}
such that for all
i\in\mathcal{I}
, if
(S,i)\prec\mathrel{\mkern-5.0mu}\mathrel{\cdot}(T,i)
in
\mathsf{B}|_{i}
then
|T-S|=1
. Let
\rho\prec\mathrel{\mkern-5.0mu}\mathrel{\cdot}\sigma
in
\mathcal{O}(\mathsf{B})
and let
i\in\mathcal{I}
such that
\rho(i)\subsetneq\sigma(i)
. Then there exists
j\in\mathcal{I}
such that
\sigma(i)=\rho(i)\cup\rho(j)
.

Proof.

Let
\rho\prec\mathrel{\mkern-5.0mu}\mathrel{\cdot}\sigma
and let
i\in\mathcal{I}
such that
\rho(i)\subsetneq\sigma(i)
. By the assumptions, there exists
(S,i)\in\mathsf{B}|_{i}
such that
\rho(i)\subsetneq(S,i)\subseteq\sigma(i)
and
S=\rho(i)\cup\{j\}
for some
j\in\mathcal{I}
. Define
\tau:\mathcal{I}\to\mathsf{B}
by

\tau(k)=\begin{cases}\rho(k)&k\neq i\\
(\rho(i)\cup\rho(j),\,i)&k=i\end{cases}

We claim that
\tau\in\mathcal{O}(\mathsf{B})
. First observe that
(\rho(i)\cup\rho(j),i)=(S\cup\rho(j),i)\in\mathsf{B}|_{i}
by (2) of
Definition

1.1
. It suffices to show

•

if
k\in\mathcal{I}-\{i\}
and
i\in\tau(k)
, then
\tau(i)\subseteq\tau(k)
and

•

if
k\in\mathcal{I}-\{i\}
and
k\in\tau(i)
, then
\tau(k)\subseteq\tau(i)
.

Observe that
\tau(i)\subseteq\sigma(i)
as
j\in S\subseteq\sigma(i)
.

In the first case, if
i\in\tau(k)
then

\tau(i)\subseteq\sigma(i)\subseteq\sigma(k)=\rho(k)=\tau(k).

In the second case, if
k\in\tau(i)
, then
k\in\rho(i)
(and
\rho(k)\subseteq\rho(i)
) or
k\in\rho(j)
(and
\rho(k)\subseteq\rho(j)
). Therefore
\tau(k)=\rho(k)\subseteq\tau(i).

Hence
\rho\prec\tau\preceq\sigma
and as
\rho\prec\mathrel{\mkern-5.0mu}\mathrel{\cdot}\sigma
, we conclude that
\tau=\sigma
.

∎

We will make heavy use of the following classical result.

Proposition 4.16

(
[
17
]
*Theorem 2.56)
.

Let
L
be a finite lattice. Then:

•

L
is join-semidistributive if and only if for every cover relation
x\prec\mathrel{\mkern-5.0mu}\mathrel{\cdot}y
in
L
, the set
\Delta_{L}(y)-\Delta_{L}(x)
has a unique minimal element.

•

L
is meet-semidistributive if and only if for every cover relation
x\prec\mathrel{\mkern-5.0mu}\mathrel{\cdot}y
in
L
, the set
\nabla_{L}(x)-\nabla_{L}(y)
has a unique maximal element.

Theorem 4.17
.

Suppose that
\mathsf{B}
is a finite, acyclic pointed building set and that for all
i\in\mathcal{I}
,
\mathsf{B}|_{i}
is a chain. Then
\mathcal{O}(\mathsf{B})
is semidistributive.

Proof.

Let
\rho\prec\mathrel{\mkern-5.0mu}\mathrel{\cdot}\sigma
and let
i\in\mathcal{I}
such that
\rho(i)\subsetneq\sigma(i)
. We show that

(1)

\Delta_{\mathcal{O}(\mathsf{B})}(\sigma)-\Delta_{\mathcal{O}(\mathsf{B})}(\rho)
has a unique minimal element

(2)

\nabla_{\mathcal{O}(\mathsf{B})}(\rho)-\nabla_{\mathcal{O}(\mathsf{B})}(\sigma)
has a unique maximal element.

Define
\mu_{\downarrow}:\mathcal{I}\to\mathsf{B}\text{ and }\mu_{\uparrow}:\mathcal{I}\to\mathsf{B}
by

\displaystyle\mu_{\downarrow}(j)

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}(\{j\},j)&j\neq i\\
\min\{S\in\mathsf{B}|_{i}:\rho(i)\subsetneq S\}&j=i\end{cases}

\displaystyle\mu_{\uparrow}(j)

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\max\mathsf{B}|_{j}&j\neq i\\
\max\{S\in\mathsf{B}|_{i}:S\subsetneq\sigma(i)\}&j=i.\end{cases}

Both
\{S\in\mathsf{B}|_{i}:\rho(i)\subsetneq S\}
and
\{S\in\mathsf{B}|_{i}:S\subsetneq\sigma(i)\}
are non-empty as
\sigma(i)
is in the first set and
\rho(i)
is in the second respectively.
As each
\mathsf{B}|_{i}
is a chain, each of these sets has a unique minimum and unique maximum. Hence
\mu_{\downarrow}
and
\mu_{\uparrow}
are both well-defined. Furthermore, each is an ornamentation.

The following are straightforward to verify:

•

\mu_{\downarrow}\preceq\sigma
,

•

\mu_{\downarrow}\not\preceq\rho
,

•

if
\mu\preceq\sigma
and
\mu\not\preceq\rho
then
\mu_{\downarrow}\preceq\mu
,

•

\rho\preceq\mu_{\uparrow}
,

•

\sigma\not\preceq\mu_{\uparrow}
,

•

if
\rho\preceq\mu
and
\sigma\not\preceq\mu
then
\mu\preceq\mu_{\uparrow}
.

∎

Corollary 4.18
.

Let
\mathsf{T}
be a rooted tree with edges directed towards the root and let
\mathsf{B}(\mathsf{T})
be the digraphical pointed building set of
\mathsf{T}
. Then
\mathcal{O}(\mathsf{B}(\mathsf{T}))
is semidistributive.

Digraphical ornamentation lattices

In this section, we give special attention to digraphical ornamentation lattices with a special focus on directed trees.

Remark 5.1
.

In

]
, it is shown that if

Lemma 5.2
.

Let
D=(V,E)
be a directed acyclic graph and
\mathsf{B}(D)
the digraphical pointed building set of
D
. Then if
\rho\prec\mathrel{\mkern-5.0mu}\mathrel{\cdot}\sigma
in
\mathcal{O}(\mathsf{B}(D))
, there exist unique vertices

such that
\sigma(u)=\rho(u)\cup\rho(v)

By

4.15
, there exists a unique

and possibly not unique

such that
\sigma(u)=\rho(u)\cup\rho(v)
. We claim that

is the unique vertex with this property. Indeed, observe that for all

, there is a path from

to

in

. Furthermore as

,

is the unique minimum element of

to

in

•

for all vertices

in

Lemma 5.5
.

For all
\rho\in\mathcal{O}(\mathsf{B}(D))
,
\Psi_{D}(\rho)
is an ornamentation.

Proof.

First observe that for all
u\in V
,
\Psi_{D}(\rho)(u)\in\mathsf{B}(D^{\mathrm{op}})|_{u}
by construction.
Now let
u\in V
,
v\in\Psi_{D}(\rho)(u)
, and
w\in\Psi_{D}(\rho)(v)
.

There is a unique path from
w
to
v
and a unique path from
v
to
u
in
D
.
Let
x\in[w,u)
be a vertex on the unique path from
w
to
u
. We claim that
u\notin\rho(x)
.

One of the following holds:

•

x\in[v,u)
in
D
or

•

x\in[w,v)
in
D
.

In the first case,
u\notin\rho(x)
as
v\in\Psi_{D}(\rho)(u)
. In the second case,
v\notin\rho(x)
. As
v
is on the unique path from
x
to

in

,

as well. (As if

, the entire path from

.

Lemma 5.6
.

\Psi_{D}
is order-reversing.

Proof.

Let
\rho,\sigma\in\mathcal{O}(\mathsf{B}(D))
with
\rho\preceq\sigma
and
u,v\in V
with
v\in\omega_{D}(\sigma)(u)
. Then either
u=v
or
u\notin\sigma(v)
and hence
u\notin\rho(v)
. In either case,
v\in\omega_{D}(\rho)(u)
. Hence
\omega_{D}(\sigma)(u)\subseteq\omega_{D}(\rho)(u)
. Then by the construction of
\Psi_{D}
, we have that
\Psi_{D}(\sigma)(u)\subseteq\Psi_{D}(\rho)(u)
and so
\Psi_{D}
is order-reversing.

∎

Lemma 5.7
.

For any directed tree
D
, we have
\Psi_{D^{\mathrm{op}}}\circ\Psi_{D}=\id
.

Proof.

Let
\rho\in\mathcal{O}(\mathsf{B}(D))\text{ and }u\in V
. First we show that
\rho(u)\subseteq\Psi_{D^{\mathrm{op}}}(\Psi_{D}(\rho))(u)
.

Let
v\in\rho(u)
. If
v=u
then
u\in\Psi_{D^{\mathrm{op}}}(\Psi_{D}(\rho))(u)
as
\Psi_{D^{\mathrm{op}}}(\Psi_{D}(\rho))
is an ornamentation. Now we assume that
v\neq u
. Then for all vertices
w\in(u,v]
in
D
, we have
w\in\rho(u)
and so
u\notin\Psi_{D}(\rho)(w)
. Therefore
v\in\Psi_{D^{\mathrm{op}}}(\Psi_{D}(\rho))(u)
, and so
\rho(u)\subseteq\Psi_{D^{\mathrm{op}}}(\Psi_{D}(\rho))(u)
.

It remains to show that
\Psi_{D^{\mathrm{op}}}(\Psi_{D}(\rho))(u)\subseteq\rho(u)
. Let
v\in\Psi_{D^{\mathrm{op}}}(\Psi_{D}(\rho))(u)
and let
w\in[u,v]
be the unique minimal vertex (in
D
) such that
v\in\rho(w)
. One may observe that
w
exists as

Direct limits of pointed building sets induce inverse limits of ornamentation lattices

Remark 6.2
.

It would be interesting to make this notation rigorous by defining a category of pointed building sets.

Lemma 6.3

\varinjlim\mathsf{B}_{\alpha}

Proof.

Definition 6.4

Proof.

Theorem 6.7
.

In the category of posets,
\varprojlim\mathcal{O}(\mathsf{B}_{\alpha})\simeq\mathcal{O}\left(\varinjlim\mathsf{B}_{\alpha}\right)
.

Proof.

We show that
\mathcal{O}\left(\varinjlim\mathsf{B}_{\alpha}\right)
satisfies the universal property of an inverse limit. Let
P
be a poset and
(\phi_{\alpha}:P\to\mathcal{O}(\mathsf{B}_{\alpha}))_{\alpha\in\mathcal{A}}
be a family of order-preserving maps such that

\text{for all }\beta\geq\alpha\text{ in }\mathcal{A},\quad\Psi_{\beta\alpha}\circ\phi_{\beta}=\phi_{\alpha}.

We show there is a unique order-preserving map

\Phi:P\to\mathcal{O}\left(\varinjlim\mathsf{B}_{\alpha}\right)

such that for all
\alpha\in\mathcal{A}
,

\pi_{\alpha}\circ\Phi=\phi_{\alpha}.

Fix
x\in P
. For each
i\in\varinjlim\mathcal{I}_{\alpha}
and
\alpha\in\mathcal{A}
such that
i\in\mathcal{I}_{\alpha}
, define

\Phi(x)(i):=\left(\bigcup_{\alpha\text{ such that }i\in\mathcal{I}_{\alpha}}\phi_{\alpha}(x)(i),\ i\right).

We check that
\Phi
is well-defined. As
\varinjlim\mathsf{B}_{\alpha}
is closed under arbitrary pointwise unions,
\Phi(x)
is indeed an element of
\mathcal{O}(\varinjlim\mathsf{B}_{\alpha})
. Now suppose that
j\in\Phi(x)(i)
and
k\in\Phi(x)(j)
. Then there exists
\alpha,\beta,\gamma\in\mathcal{A}
such that

(1)

j\in\phi_{\alpha}(x)(i)
and

(2)

k\in\phi_{\beta}(x)(j)
and

(3)

\alpha,\beta\leq\gamma
.

As
k\in\Psi_{\gamma\beta}(\phi_{\gamma}(x))(j)
, we have

k\in\phi_{\gamma}(x)(j)\subseteq\phi_{\gamma}(x)(i)\subseteq\Phi(x)(i)

so
\Phi(x)\in\mathcal{O}\left(\varinjlim\mathsf{B}_{\alpha}\right)
.

Now we show that
\Phi
is order-preserving.
Suppose
x\leq y
in
P
. Then for each
\alpha
and each
i\in\mathcal{I}_{\alpha}
,

\phi_{\alpha}(x)(i)\subseteq\phi_{\alpha}(y)(i),

and taking unions gives
\Phi(x)(i)\subseteq\Phi(y)(i)
. Thus,
\Phi(x)\preceq\Phi(y)
.

Next we show that
\pi_{\alpha}\circ\Phi=\phi_{\alpha}
.
By construction, for
i\in\mathcal{I}_{\alpha},\phi_{\alpha}(x)(i)\subseteq\Phi(x)(i)
. Hence
\phi_{\alpha}(x)(i)\subseteq\pi_{\alpha}(\Phi(x))(i)
. Now suppose that
j\in\pi_{\alpha}(\Phi(x))(i)
. Then

•

j\in\Phi(x)(i)

•

there exists
(S,i)\in\mathsf{B}_{\alpha}
with
j\in S
and
S\subseteq\Phi(x)(i)

•

for all
k\in S
, there exists
\beta_{k}\in\mathcal{A}
with
k\in\phi_{\beta_{k}}(x)(i)
.

As
S
is finite, there exists
\gamma\in\mathcal{A}
such that for all
k\in S
,
\beta_{k}\leq\gamma
. As

k\in\Psi_{\gamma\beta_{k}}(\phi_{\gamma}(x))(i)=\phi_{\beta_{k}}(x)(i),

we have
k\in\phi_{\gamma}(x)(i)
as well. Hence
S\subseteq\phi_{\gamma}(x)(i)
. Hence

j\in(S,i)\subseteq\Psi_{\gamma\alpha}(\phi_{\gamma}(x))(i)=\phi_{\alpha}(x)(i).

Finally, we show that
\Phi
is unique.
Suppose there were another map
\Phi^{\prime}:P\to\mathcal{O}\left(\varinjlim\mathsf{B}_{\alpha}\right)
such that
\pi_{\alpha}\circ\Phi^{\prime}=\phi_{\alpha}
for all
\alpha
.
Then for each
x\in P,\alpha\in\mathcal{A}
, and
i\in\mathcal{I}_{\alpha}
we must have

\pi_{\alpha}(\Phi^{\prime}(x))(i)=\phi_{\alpha}(x)(i)=\pi_{\alpha}(\Phi(x))(i).

Then
\phi_{\alpha}(x)(i)\subseteq\Phi^{\prime}(x)(i)
and hence
\Phi(x)(i)\subseteq\Phi^{\prime}(x)(i)
.

Conversely, if
j\in\Phi^{\prime}(x)(i)
then there exists
\alpha\in\mathcal{A}
and
(S,i)\in\mathsf{B}_{\alpha}
such that
j\in S
and
S\subseteq\Phi^{\prime}(x)(i)
. Then

j\in S\subseteq\pi_{\alpha}(\Phi^{\prime}(x))=\phi_{\alpha}(x)(i)\subseteq\Phi(x)(i).

Hence for all
x\in P
, we have
\Phi(x)(i)=\Phi^{\prime}(x)(i)
so
\Phi=\Phi^{\prime}
.

Hence,
\mathcal{O}\left(\varinjlim\mathsf{B}_{\alpha}\right)
satisfies the universal property of the inverse limit.

7.
Infinite Tamari lattices and 312-avoiding total orders

Our goal in this section is to study the infinite and bi-infinite Tamari lattices, their generalizations, and connections to 312-avoiding total orders. We will also discuss the continuous Tamari lattice. First we review some properties of total orders. For this section, let
X
be a set that is totally ordered under a (fixed) relation
\leq
.

7.1.
The weak order on total orders

Definition 7.1
.

Let
\preceq
be a (possibly different) total order on
X
.

The set of
inversions
of
\preceq
relative to
\leq
is defined as

\mathrm{Inv}(\preceq):=\{(i,j)\in X\times X\mid i<j\text{ and }j\prec i\}.

We define
\mathrm{Weak}(X_{\leq})
to be the set of total orders on
X
ordered under inclusion of inversion sets relative to
\leq
.

Observe that
\preceq
is recoverable from
\mathrm{Inv}(\preceq)
. When
X_{\leq}=[n]
under their usual ordering,
\mathrm{Weak}(X_{\leq})\simeq\mathrm{Weak}(S_{n})
, the weak order on permutations.

Proposition 7.2
.

\mathrm{Weak}(X_{\leq})
is a complete lattice.

This proposition is well-known in the finite case. In the case that
X_{\leq}=\mathbb{Z}_{\leq}
, the integers under their usual ordering, it is proven in
[
7
]
, that
\mathrm{Weak}(\mathbb{Z}_{\leq})
is a lattice. We extend their proof to all total orders. We recall the following characterization of inversion sets of total orders.

Lemma 7.3
.

A subset
S\subseteq\mathrm{Inv}(\leq^{\mathrm{op}})
is the inversion set of a total order if and only if both
S
and
\mathrm{Inv}(\leq^{\mathrm{op}})-S
are transitively closed.

Proof.

Note that
\mathrm{Inv}(\leq^{\mathrm{op}})=\{(i,j)\in X\times X\mid i<j\}
.
Let
\preceq
be a total order on
X
. If
(a,b)\text{ and }(b,c)\in\mathrm{Inv}(\preceq)
then
a<b<c\text{ and }c\prec b\prec a
. Hence
(a,c)\in\mathrm{Inv}(\preceq)
. Similarly if
a<b<c
and
(a,b),(b,c)\notin S
then
a\prec b\prec c
so
(a,c)\notin S
. Hence both
S
and
\mathrm{Inv}(\leq^{\mathrm{op}})-S
are transitively closed.

Now suppose that both
S
and
\mathrm{Inv}(\leq^{\mathrm{op}})-S
are transitively closed and define
\prec
by

a\prec b\Leftrightarrow\begin{cases}(b,a)\in S\\
(a,b)\notin S\text{ and }a<b.\end{cases}\text{ or }

For
a<b
, exactly one of the following holds:

•

(a,b)\in S
so
b\prec a

•

(a,b)\notin S
and
a\prec b
.

Hence exactly one of
a\prec b
or
b\prec a
holds. Now we show that
\prec
is transitive. Suppose that
a\prec b
and
b\prec c
. The remainder of the proof reduces to checking the cases of the 6 possible orders of
a,b,\text{ and }c
under
<
.
∎

Note that
\mathrm{Inv}(\leq^{\mathrm{op}})-S
being transitively closed is equivalent to the property that if
(a,c)\in S
and
a<b<c
then at least one of
(a,b)
or
(b,c)
is in
S
. This property is also known as
S
being
transitively coclosed.

Proof of
Proposition

7.2
.

First observe that
\mathrm{Weak}(X_{\leq})
is bounded below by
\leq
and above by
\leq^{\mathrm{op}}
. Hence it suffices to show that
\mathrm{Weak}(X_{\leq})
is a complete join-semilattice. Now let
\Omega
be a collection of total orders. Define

\mathrm{Inv}(\Omega):=\bigcup\limits_{\preceq\in\Omega}\mathrm{Inv}(\preceq)

and
\overline{\mathrm{Inv}(\Omega)}
to be its transitive closure. We claim that
\overline{\mathrm{Inv}(\Omega)}=\mathrm{Inv}(\preceq_{\Omega})
for some total order
\preceq_{\Omega}
.

A well-known characterization of the transitive closure of a relation
R
is as the union

\overline{R}=\bigcup_{n=1}^{\infty}R^{n}.

In particular, this implies that
(a,b)\in\overline{\mathrm{Inv}(\Omega)}
if and only if there exists a sequence
a=x_{1},x_{2},\dots,x_{n}=b
such that for all
i
, we have
(x_{i},x_{i+1})\in\mathrm{Inv}(\preceq_{i})
for some
\preceq_{i}\in\Omega
.

In particular, if
(a,b)\in\overline{\mathrm{Inv}(\Omega)}
then
a<b
, and so
\overline{\mathrm{Inv}(\Omega)}\subseteq\mathrm{Inv}(\leq^{\mathrm{op}})
. By construction
\overline{\mathrm{Inv}(\Omega)}
is transitively closed. It remains to show that
\overline{\mathrm{Inv}(\Omega)}
is transitively coclosed.

Suppose that
a<b<c
and that
(a,c)\in\overline{\mathrm{Inv}(\Omega)}
. Then there exists a sequence

a=x_{1},x_{2},\dots,x_{n}=c

such that for all
i
, we have
(x_{i},x_{i+1})\in\mathrm{Inv}(\preceq_{i})
for some
\preceq_{i}\in\Omega
.

Because
\leq
is a total order and

a=x_{1}\leq\dots\leq x_{n}=c,

there exists an
i
such that
x_{i}\leq b<x_{i+1}
. If
x_{i}=b
then
(b,x_{i+1})\in\overline{\mathrm{Inv}(\Omega)}
and
(b,c)\in\overline{\mathrm{Inv}(\Omega)}
by transitivity. Otherwise,
x_{i}<b<x_{i+1}
and
(x_{i},x_{i+1})\in\mathrm{Inv}(\preceq_{i}).
As
\mathrm{Inv}(\preceq_{i})
is transitively coclosed, either
(x_{i},b)\in\mathrm{Inv}(\preceq_{i})
or
(b,x_{i+1})\in\mathrm{Inv}(\preceq_{i})
. Then
(a,b)\in\overline{\mathrm{Inv}(\Omega)}
or
(b,c)\in\overline{\mathrm{Inv}(\Omega)}
respectively. Therefore
\overline{\mathrm{Inv}(\Omega)}
is transitively coclosed.

Hence there exists a total order
\preceq_{\Omega}
such that
\overline{\mathrm{Inv}(\Omega)}=\mathrm{Inv}(\preceq_{\Omega})
. Now suppose that
\preceq_{\Omega}^{\prime}
is any other common upper bound to all
\preceq\in\Omega
. Then necessarily
\mathrm{Inv}(\Omega)\subseteq\mathrm{Inv}(\preceq_{\Omega}^{\prime})
, and as
\mathrm{Inv}(\preceq_{\Omega}^{\prime})
is transitively closed,
\mathrm{Inv}(\preceq_{\Omega})\subseteq\mathrm{Inv}(\preceq_{\Omega}^{\prime})
.

∎

7.2.
312-avoiding total orders and ornamentations

Definition 7.4
.

We say that a total order
\preceq
on
X
is
312-avoiding
(relative to
\leq
) if there does not exist
a,b,c\in X
such that
a<b<c
but
c\prec a\prec b
.

Equivalently,
\preceq
is 312-avoiding if whenever
(a,c)\in\mathrm{Inv}(\preceq)\text{ and }(b,c)\in\mathrm{Inv}(\preceq)
then
(a,b)\in\mathrm{Inv}(\preceq)
.

We define
\mathrm{Weak}^{312}(X_{\leq})
to be the subposet of
\mathrm{Weak}(X_{\leq})
of 312-avoiding total orders.

Definition 7.5
.

A subset
C\subseteq X
is called

if for all
a,b,c\in X
with
a\leq b\leq c
if
a,c\in C
then
b\in C
.

A
left segment

R\subseteq X
is a convex subset of
X
with a minimal element.

Example 7.6
.

We give some examples and non-examples of left segments.

•

Any closed interval
[a,b]
, half-open interval
[a,b)
, and ray
[a,\infty)
is a left segment.

•

The set
\{x\in\mathbb{Q}\mid 0\leq x<\sqrt{2}\}
is a left segment but not a half-open interval.

•

The open interval
(0,1)\subseteq\mathbb{R}
is convex but not a left segment.

Definition 7.7
.

The left segment pointed building set
\mathsf{B}(X_{\leq})
is the set of left segments of
X
, pointed at their left endpoint.

Proposition 7.8
.

For all totally ordered sets
(X,\leq)
we have
\mathrm{Weak}^{312}(X_{\leq})\simeq\mathcal{O}(\mathsf{B}(X_{\leq}))
.

Proof.

We define a map
\Psi:\mathrm{Weak}^{312}(X_{\leq})\to\mathcal{O}(\mathsf{B}(X_{\leq}))
by

\Psi(\preceq)(a):=\left(\{b\in X\mid(a,b)\in\mathrm{Inv}(\preceq)\}\cup\{a\},a\right).

We verify that
\Psi
is well-defined. First observe that if
a<b<c
and
c\in\Psi(\preceq)(a)
then either
(a,b)\in\mathrm{Inv}(\preceq)
or
(b,c)\in\mathrm{Inv}(\preceq)
. But if
(b,c)\in\mathrm{Inv}(\preceq)
then
(a,b)\in\mathrm{Inv}(\preceq)
as
\preceq
is 312-avoiding. Hence
(a,b)\in\mathrm{Inv}(\preceq)
so
b\in\Psi(\preceq)(a)
. Hence
\Psi(\preceq)(a)
is a left-segment.

Now suppose that
b\in\Psi(\preceq)(a)
. Then if
c\in\Psi(\preceq)(b)
we have
(a,b)\in\mathrm{Inv}(\preceq)
and
(b,c)\in\mathrm{Inv}(\preceq)
. Hence by transitivity,
(a,c)\in\mathrm{Inv}(\preceq)
so
c\in\Psi(\preceq)(a)
. Therefore
\Psi
is well-defined and is order-preserving by construction.

We can construct
\Psi^{-1}
as follows: let
\rho\in\mathcal{O}(\mathsf{B}(X_{\leq}))
. We define a total order
\preceq_{\rho}
whose inversion set is precisely

\{(a,b)\in X\times X\mid a<b\text{ and }b\in\rho(a)\}.

If
\Psi^{-1}
is well-defined it is immediate that it is actually the inverse of
\Psi
and is order-preserving. Observe that
\mathrm{Inv}(\preceq_{\rho})
is transitively closed as
\rho
is transitively closed and
\mathrm{Inv}(\preceq_{\rho})
is transitively coclosed and 312-avoiding as if
a<b<c
and
(a,c)\in\mathrm{Inv}(\preceq_{\rho})
then
(a,b)\in\mathrm{Inv}(\preceq_{\rho})
.
∎

7.4.
The continuous Tamari lattice

Recall from
Example

3.6
that the
continuous Tamari lattice
has
\mathcal{I}
equal to a closed interval in
\mathbb{R}
and has for its pointed building set the collection of half-open intervals of the form
[a,b)
where
a\leq b
pointed at their left endpoint. (And where
[a,a):=\{a\}
by convention.)

Example 7.12
.

Focusing on the example,
\mathcal{I}=[0,1]
, let
\rho\in\mathcal{O}(\mathsf{B})
and let
[0,1]^{[0,1]}
be the set of functions from
[0,1]\to[0,1]
. We can define an injective map
F:\mathcal{O}(\mathsf{B})\to[0,1]^{[0,1]}
by
F(\rho)(a)=b
where
\rho(a)=[a,b)
. The image of this map is precisely all functions
f:[0,1]\to[0,1]
such that

•

for all
x\in[0,1]

x\leq f(x)

•

for all
x,y\in[0,1]
if
x<y
and
y<f(x)
then
f(y)\leq f(x)
.

Examples of such functions include
f(x)=x,f(x)=1,f(x)=|x-\frac{1}{2}|+\frac{1}{2}
.

Furthermore, if we order
f\leq g
if and only if
f(x)\leq g(x)
for all
x\in[0,1]
, then this is a poset embedding. We note that these functions
f
are not necessarily continuous or even measurable as any function of the form

f(x)=\begin{cases}x&x\in A\\
1&x\notin A\end{cases}

is in the image of
F
for any
A\subseteq[0,1]
.

Proposition 7.13
.

The continuous Tamari lattice
\mathcal{O}(\mathsf{B})
is not isomorphic to
\mathcal{O}(\mathsf{B}([0,1]_{\leq}))
.

The intuition for why these two lattices are not isomorphic is that the pointed building set for the continuous Tamari lattice only includes half-open intervals whereas
\mathsf{B}([0,1]_{\leq})
also contains closed intervals. In applications, one may prefer the continuous Tamari lattice because it is more uniform and because of this identification with functions.

Proof of
Proposition

7.13
.

Let
\rho\in\mathcal{O}(\mathsf{B})
be defined by

\rho(i)=\begin{cases}([0,1),0)&i=0\\
(\{i\},i)&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

The principal order ideal generated by
\rho
is isomorphic to
[0,1]
which does not contain any cover relations. However, any non-trivial principal order ideal in
\mathcal{O}(\mathsf{B}([0,1]_{\leq}))
contains a cover relation, namely one of the form
\rho_{1}\prec\mathrel{\mkern-5.0mu}\mathrel{\cdot}\rho_{2}
where

\rho_{1}(i)=\begin{cases}([0,a),0)&i=0\\
(\{i\},i)&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}\qquad\qquad\rho_{2}(i)=\begin{cases}([0,a],0)&i=0\\
(\{i\},i)&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}

for some
a\in[0,1]
.

∎

In a poset, the
length
of a chain is the number of elements in the chain. (Some authors define it as 1 less than the number of elements in the chain.)

Theorem 8.12

(
[
5
]
*Theorem 8.4)
.

The maximal length of a chain in
\mathsf{CSymATam}_{n}
is
\binom{n+1}{2}+1
.

Proof.

For
\rho\in\mathsf{CSymATam}_{n}
, define
f(\rho)=\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}|\rho(i)|-\binom{|\ \{i\ :\ \rho(i)=\mathcal{I}\}\ |}{2}
.

Equivalently, let
X_{\rho}=\{i\in[n]\mid\rho(i)=\mathcal{I}\}
and
Y_{\rho}=[n]-X_{\rho}
and define
X_{\sigma}\text{ and }Y_{\sigma}
similarly.
Then

\displaystyle f(\rho)

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{i\in X_{\rho}}|\rho(i)|-\binom{|X_{\rho}|}{2}+\sum\limits_{i\in Y_{\rho}}|\rho(i)|

\displaystyle=|X_{\rho}|\left(n+1-\frac{|X_{\rho}|-1}{2}\right)+\sum\limits_{i\in Y_{\rho}}|\rho(i)|.

We claim that if
\rho\prec\sigma
then
f(\rho)<f(\sigma)
. Suppose that
\rho\prec\sigma
and define

Z:=\{i\in[n]\mid\rho(i)\subsetneq\sigma(i)\}.

Z
is non-empty as
\rho\prec\sigma
. If
i\in Y_{\sigma}
then the contribution of
|\sigma(i)|
to
f(\sigma)
is strictly greater than the contribution of
|\rho(i)|
to
f(\rho)
. Otherwise, suppose that
i\in Y_{\rho}
and
i\in X_{\sigma}
. Then the difference of contributions of
|\sigma(i)|
and
|\rho(i)|
to
f(\sigma)
and
f(\rho)
is exactly
n-|X_{\rho}|+1-|\rho(i)|
. Observe that
|\rho(i)|\leq n-|X_{\rho}|
as one of the first
n-|X_{\rho}|
vertices
j
along the path starting at
i
is in
X_{\rho}
, and therefore
j\notin\rho(i)
(as
\rho(i)\neq(\mathcal{I},i)
). Hence in either case, the contribution to
f(\sigma)
is strictly greater than the contribution to
f(\rho)
. As
Z
is non-empty,
f(\rho)<f(\sigma)
.

Let
\hat{0},\hat{1}\in\mathsf{CSymATam}_{n}
with
\hat{0}
minimal and
\hat{1}
maximal. To complete the proof, it suffices to find a chain of length
f(\hat{1})-f(\hat{0})+1=n(n+1)-\binom{n}{2}-n+1=\binom{n+1}{2}+1
. For
1\leq i\leq j\leq n
, we define
\rho_{i,j}\in\mathsf{CSymATam}_{n}
by

\rho_{i,j}(k):=\begin{cases}(\mathcal{I},k)&1\leq k<i\\
(\{i,\dots,j\},k)&k=i\\
(\{k\},k)&k>i\end{cases}

for
1\leq k\leq n
and for
-n\leq k\leq-1

\rho_{i,j}(k)=-\rho_{i,j}(-k)
.

Observe that if
i<i^{\prime}
or
i=i^{\prime}\text{ and }j<j^{\prime}
then
\rho_{i,j}\prec\rho_{i^{\prime},j^{\prime}}
. Finally,
\rho_{n,n}\prec\mathrel{\mkern-5.0mu}\mathrel{\cdot}\hat{1}
, completing the proof.
∎

9.
Geometric interpretations

In this section, we introduce a geometric interpretation of ornamentations.

Definition 9.1
.

Let
\Phi
be a set of vectors and let
\vec{u},\vec{v}\in\Phi
. A subset
X\subseteq\Phi
is called
closed in
\Phi
if whenever
\vec{u},\vec{v}\in X
and
\vec{w}:=\vec{u}+\vec{v}\in\Phi
then
\vec{w}\in X
. We call
X

coclosed in
\Phi
if whenever
\vec{w}\in X
and
\vec{w}=\vec{u}+\vec{v}
then
\vec{u}\in X
or
\vec{v}\in X
(or both).

Definition 9.2
.

Let
\mathsf{B}
be a pointed building set on
\mathcal{I}
. The set of vectors
\Phi_{\mathsf{B}}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{I}}
is defined by

\Phi_{\mathsf{B}}:=\{e_{j}-e_{i}\mid\text{there exists }(S,i)\in\mathsf{B}\text{ such that }j\in(S-\{i\})\}

where
e_{i}
is the standard basis vector corresponding to
i
.

For an ornamentation
\rho\in\mathcal{O}(\mathsf{B})
define

V(\rho):=\{e_{j}-e_{i}\mid i\in\mathcal{I}\text{ and }j\in\rho(i)\text{ such that }i\neq j\}.

Observe that if
\mathcal{I}=[n]
then transitive closure of pointed building sets (axiom (2) in
Definition

1.1
) implies that
\Phi_{\mathsf{B}}
is closed in the type-
A
root system
\Phi_{A_{n-1}}
. Furthermore, transitive closure of ornamentations (axiom (2) in
Definition

1.3
) is
equivalent
to the fact that
V(\rho)
is closed in
\Phi_{\mathsf{B}}
for all
\rho\in\mathcal{O}(\mathsf{B})
. With this in mind, one can define an ornamentation
\rho
to be biclosed if
V(\rho)
is biclosed in
\Phi_{\mathsf{B}}
.

Equivalently, an ornamentation
\rho\in\mathcal{O}(\mathsf{B})
is biclosed if for all
i\in\mathcal{I},k\in\rho(i)
, if there exist
(S,i),\,(T,j)\in\mathsf{B}
with
j\in S
and
k\in T
then either
j\in\rho(i)
or
k\in\rho(j)
.

Remark 9.3
.

It seems interesting to study the subposet of biclosed ornamentations in
\mathcal{O}(\mathsf{B})
. We will denote this subposet
\Bicl(\mathsf{B})
.

(1)

All digraphical ornamentations of a directed tree are biclosed.

(2)

Orient the edges of the complete graph
K_{n}
from
i\to j
with
i<j
and let
\mathsf{B}
be the digraphical pointed building set of
K_{n}
. Then
\Phi_{\mathsf{B}}
is the set of positive roots of
\Phi_{A_{n-1}}
and the poset of biclosed ornamentations is the weak order on
S_{n}

[
13
,
6
]
.

(3)

Let
\mathsf{B}
be the graphical pointed building set of
K_{n}
. For
\rho\in\mathcal{O}(\mathsf{B})
define a binary operation
*_{\rho}
by

i*_{\rho}j=\begin{cases}i&j\notin\rho(i)\\
j&j\in\rho(i)\end{cases}

The operation
*_{\rho}
is
quasitrivial
, i.e., for all
i,j\in[n]
we have
i*_{\rho}j\in\{i,j\}
. A straightforward calculation shows that
*_{\rho}
is associative if and only if
\rho
is biclosed. In fact, one can show that
\rho\to*_{\rho}
is a bijection from
\Bicl(\mathsf{B})
to associative, quasitrivial operations on
[n]
, also known as quasitrivial semigroups
[
9
]
. This construction recovers the bijection in
[
26
]
.

In each of the examples in

9.3
,
\Bicl(\mathsf{B})
is a lattice. The first was proven in this paper, the second is well-known, and the third was proven in
[
14
]
which is unpublished at the time of writing. It is not true in general that
\Bicl(\mathsf{B})
is a lattice, see
Figure

. However, experimentally it appears that
\Bicl(\mathsf{B})
is a lattice for many natural choices of
\mathsf{B}
.

Conjecture 9.4
.

For a finite (undirected) graph
G
, let
\mathsf{B}
be the graphical pointed building set of

\Bicl(\mathsf{B})
is a lattice.

Definition 10.1
.

A poset

The weak order on permutations, the Boolean lattice, and the Tamari lattice are all well known polytopal lattices.
In

]

is polytopal. In

Question 10.2
.

For graphical and digraphical
\mathsf{B}
, when are
\mathcal{O}(\mathsf{B}),\mathcal{O}_{G}(\mathsf{B}),\Bicl(\mathsf{B})
polytopal?

Question 10.3
.

In this paper, we focused on graphical and digraphical pointed building sets. Are there other natural classes of pointed building sets with interesting combinatorics?

Question 10.4
.

One can view a pointed building set as a collection of lattices with compatibility conditions induced by their embeddings into the Boolean lattice. Can one develop the theory of ornamentation lattices without relying on this embedding? Can lattices other than the Boolean lattice be used?

Remark 10.5
.

One can remove the axiom that pointed building sets contain all singletons (axiom (1) of
Definition

1.1
) and the axiom that ornamentations are pointed at their images (axiom (1) of
Definition

1.3
) and still obtain join-semilattices. It may be interesting to study the combinatorics under these relaxed axioms. It may also be interesting to study a version where sets can have multiple distinguished elements.

Remark 10.6
.

Building sets have been highly influential in matroid theory. It would be interesting to study if pointed building sets have any application to matroids and in particular oriented matroids.
