Stability conditions of chemical networks in a linear framework
Armand Despons, Jérémie Unterberger, David Lacoste

Abstract
Autocatalytic chemical reaction networks can collectively replicate or maintain their constituents despite degradation reactions only above a certain threshold, which we refer to as the decay threshold. When the chemical network has a Jacobian matrix with the Metzler property, we leverage analytical methods developed for Markov processes to show that the decay threshold can be calculated by solving a linear problem, instead of the standard eigenvalue problem. We explore how this decay threshold depends on the network parameters, such as its size, the directionality of the reactions (reversible or irreversible), and its connectivity, then we deduce design principles from this that might be relevant to research on the Origin of Life.



I
Introduction

Mechanisms that are able to explain how complexity can increase in chemistry are of great interest for research on the origins of life
Robinson et al. 2022
.
The appearance of large and functional molecules, which could serve as catalysts is likely to be an important step in this increase of chemical complexity. To compensate for the potential loss of catalysts, due to unwanted side reactions or unavoidable degradation mechanisms, one generally invokes autocatalytic reaction networks or cycles
Xavier et al. 2020
;
Lancet et al. 2018
;
Schuster 2019
;
Cornish-Bowden and Cárdenas 2020
;
Ameta et al. 2021
;
Orgel 2008
.
A relevant question is therefore to determine the threshold above which autocatalytic reaction networks typically overcome degradation and dilution effects, which we call the decay threshold.

Assuming autocatalytic species form a single cycle and participate in unimolecular irreversible reactions, King derived such a simple criterion for the onset of growth in such a network
King 1982
.
That criterion was recognized as key for understanding molecular replication in the origin of life, because it plays a similar role as the error threshold introduced by Eigen for the maintenance of information
Eigen and Schuster 2012
;
Szathmáry 2006
. While some generalizations of the King criterion to reversible reactions and to associations of coupled autocatalytic cycles have been considered before
Sarkar and England 2019
;
Blokhuis et al. 2020a
, there is no explicit criterion for an arbitrary complex chemical network.
On the numerical side, the situation is of course, much better: using simulations of autocatalytic sets (ACS), the threshold for the onset of growth in ACS, and interaction effects have also been studied in detail. For instance, it was observed that this threshold for onset of growth is typically lower in nested ACS due to reinforcement effects occurring between ACS
Giri and Jain 2012
. These reinforcement effects could be potentially important for research on the origin of life, since they could offer a route to produce large molecules in the absence of enzymes.

In a recent work
Blokhuis et al. 2020a
, a stoichiometric definition of autocatalysis was introduced (see also
Andersen et al. 2020
for a discussion of alternative approaches), which leads to a method of identification of autocatalysis within large and complex chemical networks that does not require any knowledge of kinetics
Gagrani et al. 2024
;
Peng et al. 2020
. In this work, autocatalytic networks were also shown to contain minimal subnetworks called autocatalytic cores, which can only exist in five types.
These cores have the remarkable property that their stoichiometric matrix is a Metzler matrix.
The relation between stoichiometric and dynamical definitions of autocatalysis is an active topic explored in several works
Lacoste and Ledoux 2025
. Let us mention some of them briefly here:
A first study relied on the dilute regime, in which the kinetics of all autocatalytic species is effectively linear; it becomes then possible to prove rigorously an equivalence between the stoichiometric definition of autocatalysis and dynamical autocatalysis in a fully connected network
Unterberger and Nghe 2022
.
Beyond the dilute regime, it was also shown that for a stoichiometrically autocatalytic network in the sense of
Blokhuis et al. 2020a
, there exists a choice of kinetic parameters for which the dynamics can be entirely dominated by the autocatalytic part, when the kinetics is parameter-rich
Vassena and Stadler 2024
.
Then, Kosc et al. showed that autocatalytic cores can be realized dynamically in a CRN, which means that a set of chemical species at positive concentrations and a set of thermodynamically consistent reactions satisfying mass-action law kinetics can be found for all of them
Kosc et al. 2025
. In addition, the fluxes within the cores share the same orientation.
Taken together, these results suggest that autocatalytic cores represent a part of the network that can be effectively described by irreversible reactions and linear kinetics. Because of this property, one should expect that typical non-linear features of chemical reaction networks (CRNs) such as multi-stability should be absent at the level of individual autocatalytic cores, a result that can indeed be proven
P. Nandan 2025
.

Inspired by these specific properties of autocatalytic cores, we consider in this paper a class of linear chemical reaction networks, for which the Jacobian matrix is Metzler
Smith 1986
. This is a central assumption for this work, but it does not need to concern a large network, it could hold at the level of a subnetwork within a larger network.
Metzler matrices have wide applications in economics, biology, and in control theory
Briat 2017
.
In the literature, a Metzler matrix is a square matrix with non-negative off-diagonal elements, with no constraint on the diagonal
Meyer 2023
.
We prove that the elements on the diagonal of the Jacobian are generally negative under mild conditions for CRNs, and detail conditions on the kinetics for the off-diagonal elements to be positive. CRNs with a Metzler Jacobian include, but are not limited to :
(i) catalytic
Ooka et al. 2024
and autocatalytic reaction networks with linear kinetics and degradation reactions
Unterberger and Nghe 2022
or (ii) autocatalytic cores with irreversible reactions.

Our paper is organized as follows.
In the next section, we show that this central Metzler property translates into a framework that we build on the theory of continuous-time Markov chains.
We illustrate the method on several networks of simple topology, and we use it to compare the decay thresholds for type I and type II autocatalytic cycles.
Then, we study the effect of mutual reinforcement of autocatalytic cycles through side branches or through coupling via shared chemical species and reactions.
This effect has similarities with the reinforcement coupling observed in
Giri and Jain 2012
, although there are also important differences since our approach is linear, while non-linear kinetics is an essential ingredient in that work. We conclude with a discussion of the possible relevance of these findings to the Origin of Life research.

II.2
Metzler property of the Jacobian matrix

Let us assume that the CRN admits at least one fixed point corresponding to the concentration vector
\boldsymbol{c}^{\star}
. Now, we introduce a perturbation around this fixed point
\boldsymbol{c}^{\star}
,
\boldsymbol{c}=\boldsymbol{c}^{\star}+\delta\boldsymbol{c}
, such that the kinetic equations become:

\mathrm{d}_{t}\delta\boldsymbol{c}=\mathbb{M}\cdot\delta\boldsymbol{c},

(4)

where
\mathbb{M}=\left\{M_{xx^{\prime}}\right\}
denotes the Jacobian matrix evaluated at
\boldsymbol{c}^{\star}
, which can be expressed as

\mathbb{M}=\mathbb{S}\cdot\left.\dfrac{\partial\boldsymbol{f}}{\partial\boldsymbol{c}}\right|_{\boldsymbol{c}^{\star}}.

(5)

This gradient, which contains the response coefficients of the fluxes, is called elasticities (or elastic coefficients) and is a central quantity in the literature on metabolic control analysis (MCA)
Liebermeister et al. 2010
;
Heuett et al. 2008
.

Let us first show that under mild assumptions about the CRN, the diagonal elements of this Jacobian matrix are negative (see details in appendix
V.1
).
We find that this property holds when the CRN satisfies the two following conditions: The first condition is that the chemical network is such that reactants (resp. products) of a given reaction are not simultaneously also products (resp. reactants) of the same reaction, which corresponds to the
non-ambiguity
condition introduced by Blokhuis
et al.

Blokhuis et al. 2020a
.
Mathematically, this is expressed as

\forall\rho,~S^{\pm}_{i\rho}>0~\Rightarrow~S^{\mp}_{i\rho}=0.

(6)

The second condition is that fluxes depend on concentrations in such a way that they are increasing functions of the reactant concentrations and, conversely, decreasing functions of the product concentrations, reflecting product inhibition.
In other words, this assumption reads:

\displaystyle S^{+}_{i\rho}>0\Rightarrow\partial_{c_{i}}f_{\rho}\geq 0

and

\displaystyle S^{-}_{i\rho}>0\Rightarrow\partial_{c_{i}}f_{\rho}\leq 0,

(7)

a condition that can be checked in particular for the rate law given by
3
.
Taken together, these two conditions guarantee the negativity of the diagonal elements of the Jacobian.

Let us now introduce the central assumption of this paper, namely that the Jacobian matrix evaluated at
\boldsymbol{c}^{\star}
should be a Metzler matrix.
In other words, all the off-diagonal elements in the Jacobian matrix are positive:

\forall x\neq x^{\prime},~~M_{xx^{\prime}}\geq 0.

(8)

This condition is
satisfied in particular in the special case when the fixed point is the zero concentration point, i.e. in the dilute regime.
In the absence of an external influx of species into the system, the point of zero concentration,
\boldsymbol{c}^{\star}=\boldsymbol{0}
, becomes a fixed point of the kinetic equations.
The stability of the zero fixed point is important on origin of life studies, as it determines whether the system can initiate growth or instead undergo decay.
In the dilute regime,
\boldsymbol{c}=\delta\boldsymbol{c}>0
element-wise, making the dynamics of the perturbation a positive linear system; hence, the Jacobian matrix must be Metzler in the dilute regime
Farina and Rinaldi 2011
.

Outside the dilute regime, the Jacobian matrix is generally non-Metzler, yet there may be certain regimes of parameters (such as the concentrations of species) under which the Jacobian matrix is still Metzler.
This is fully compatible with the proof mentioned before that there exists a choice of kinetic parameters for which the dynamics of a stoichiometric autocatalytic chemical network can be dominated by the autocatalytic part, when the kinetics is parameter-rich
Vassena and Stadler 2024
.
In the appendix
V.1
, we show that the Jacobian matrix is necessarily Metzler if (i) product inhibition is absent; and (ii) if all the reactions involve only one type of species as a reactant (see appendix
V.1
for a more precise definition).

II.3
A Markov chain approach

According to the Perron–Frobenius theorem for irreducible Metzler matrices
Meyer 2023
, there exists a unique eigenvalue
\lambda
, which is dominant and which is associated with an eigenvector
\boldsymbol{u}
whose entries are all strictly positive.
As a result, in the long run, the perturbation tends to align with this leading eigenvector:
\delta\boldsymbol{c}=e^{\lambda\,t}\ \boldsymbol{u}+\boldsymbol{o}\left(e^{\lambda\,t}\right)
.
In general, deriving an explicit analytic expression for
\lambda
as a function of the system’s parameters (e.g., stoichiometric coefficients, rate constants, chemostatted concentrations) is not feasible.
However, under the assumption that the Jacobian matrix is Metzler, the perturbation dynamics can be interpreted as analogous to those of a particle in a continuous-time Markov chain with birth–death transitions, whose non-conservative generator is the matrix
\mathbb{M}
.
Based on this analogy, we define the transition weights between species
x^{\prime}
and
x
in a manner similar to transition probabilities in continuous-time Markov chains
Norris 1998
:

w_{x^{\prime}\to x}=\dfrac{M_{xx^{\prime}}}{-M_{x^{\prime}x^{\prime}}}~~\text{for}~~x\neq x^{\prime}.

(9)

Crucially, due our assumptions, these transition weights are positive quantities.
Note that these weights do not represent transition probabilities except in the particular case where
\mathbb{M}
is a Markov matrix.
To an arbitrary path in the network of the form
\Gamma=\left\{x_{1}\to x_{2}\to\cdots\to x_{N}\right\}
, we associate a weight :

\mathbb{W}[\Gamma]=\prod_{k=1}^{N-1}w_{x_{k}\to x_{k+1}}.

(10)

II.4
Condition for stability

For any pair of species
a
,
b
in the network, we let

\mathcal{E}(a\to b)=\big\{a\hskip-1.42262pt\to x_{1}\hskip-1.42262pt\to\cdots\to\hskip-1.42262ptx_{\ell}\hskip-1.42262pt\to\hskip-1.42262ptb\big|\,\ell\geq 0\hskip 1.42262pt;\hskip 1.42262pt\forall n,\hskip 1.42262ptx_{n}\neq b\big\},

be the set containing all the paths of arbitrary length which start at
a
and terminate at
b
, without having visited
b
at any intermediate step.
Moreover, we introduce the total weight of all these paths:

\mathcal{W}(a\to b)=\sum_{\Gamma\in\mathcal{E}(a\to b)}\mathbb{W}[\Gamma].

(11)

Paths in the set
\mathcal{E}(a\to a)
are known as
a
-based excursions
in the Markov chain literature
Norris 1998
.
In this context,
\mathcal{W}(a\to a)
represents the
total excursion weight
.
Further, the sign of the leading eigenvalue
\lambda
is directly related to
\mathcal{W}(a\to a)
through the following criterion,

\lambda>0\hskip 10.00002pt\Leftrightarrow\hskip 10.00002pt\mathcal{W}(a\to a)>1,

(12)

a result originally derived in
Unterberger and Nghe 2022
. Due to the importance of this result for this paper, we recall in the appendix
V.2
the main steps of the proof. Note that in addition to the Metzler property, it is also required that the matrix
\mathbb{M}
corresponds to an irreductible network.

Note that the criterion is independent of the choice of the state
a
in the network.
Additionally, as shown in the appendix
V.3
and in the Example 1 below,
\mathcal{W}(a\to a)
is the solution of a linear problem, as opposed to
\lambda
which is the root of a polynomial of arbitrary order.

III
Onset of growth in autocatalytic cycles

In what follows, we apply our criterion to derive general conditions upon which autocatalytic networks initiate growth in the low concentration regime of autocatalytic species (i.e. the diluted regime).

III.1
Unicyclic autocatalytic cycles

Let us now focus on the simplest possible topology for an autocatalytic cycle, namely that of a single isolated cycle, composed of
N
unimolecular reaction steps, involving intermediate species
X_{i}
and ending with a single branching point as shown in Figure
2
a
. In the last reaction, species
X_{N}
splits, producing
\mathfrak{p}
of the first species
X_{1}
.
In Ref.
Blokhuis et al. 2020a
this topology was classified as the Type I core.
We first consider the case of irreversible reactions and then the case of reversible reactions.

Figure 2:

Uncoupled autocatalytic networks consists of an arbitrary number of intermediates species that reacts reversibly or irreversibly in a linear pathways.

(a)
In a unicyclic autocatalytic network, the last intermediate,
X_{N}
, gives back
\mathfrak{p}
of the first species ; while
(b)
in a Type II network, it gives back one unit of
X_{1}
and one other unit of an arbitrary upstream species,
X_{\mathfrak{u}}
.

III.1.1
Irreversible case

In the irreversible case, all the backward rates constants,
k^{-}_{i}
, are set to zero and only the forward weights remain, which can be expressed in terms of the specificity of the reaction of step
i
, namely
s_{i}=k_{i}^{+}/(k_{i}^{+}+\kappa_{i})

King 1982
. These specificities quantify the probability to move to the next step in the reaction path as opposed to finish there due to side-reaction or death.

With this simple topology, it is straightforward to obtain the total excursion weight:

\mathcal{W}\left(X_{1}\to X_{1}\right)=\mathfrak{p}\prod_{i=1}^{N}s_{i}.

(26)

Thus, the criterion Eq. (
12
) to achieve exponential growth reads :

\lambda>0~~\Leftrightarrow~~\prod_{i=1}^{N}s_{i}>\dfrac{1}{\mathfrak{p}}.

(27)

This condition was first derived by King
King 1982
for the particular case
\mathfrak{p}=2
, and
was then further discussed for arbitrary
\mathfrak{p}
by Szathmary
Szathmáry 2006
.

Note that the case
\mathfrak{p}=1
describes for instance a unicycle catalytic network, which is composed of a succession of unimolecular reactions and degradation steps.
In this case,
\lambda
is the largest eigenvalue of the Jacobian and it can be shown that the lifetime of the catalyst is then simply
-1/\lambda

Ooka et al. 2024
. The practical use of this method has been demonstrated in that work for the case of manganese oxide (
MnO_{2}
) in experimental reactions in which it is used as a catalyst.

Since
0<s_{i}<1
, the implication of King’s criterion is that smaller cycles are more likely
to grow than bigger ones. The corresponding critical size of these cycles reads,

N\leq-\dfrac{\log\mathfrak{p}}{\left\langle\log s_{i}\right\rangle},

(28)

where
\left\langle\log s_{i}\right\rangle=N^{-1}\sum_{i}\log s_{i}
represents the logarithm of the geometric mean of the weights
s_{i}

Szathmáry 2006
.

An equivalent statement to these formula is that the total excursion weight is upper-bounded by the connectivity of the single branching point measured by
\mathfrak{p}
,

\mathcal{W}\left(X_{1}\to X_{1}\right)\leq\mathfrak{p},

(29)

and the bound is saturated if, and only if, the degradation rates vanish.
This implies that the total excursion weight of unicyclic and irreversible cycles is intrinsically bounded and cannot reach arbitrary high values
while no such constraint exists for the growth rate.

Reversible case

have non-vanishing rates
k^{-}_{i}
.
Thus, the forward and backward propensities are respectively defined as:

\displaystyle s^{+}_{i}=\dfrac{k_{i}^{+}}{k_{i}^{+}+k_{i}^{-}+\kappa_{i}},

\displaystyle s^{-}_{i}=\dfrac{k_{i}^{-}}{k_{i}^{+}+k_{i}^{-}+\kappa_{i}}.

(30)

Note that, because there is no backward reaction having
X_{1}
as reactant,
k_{1}^{-}=0
.
As before, the elementary weights are related to the propensities :

\begin{gathered}\forall 1\leq i\leq N-1~,~w_{X_{i}\to X_{i\pm 1}}=s_{i}^{\pm}\ ;\\[5.0pt]
w_{X_{N}\to X_{1}}=\mathfrak{p}s_{N}^{+}.\end{gathered}

Performing a similar method to one presented in the Example 1 above (see the Section 3 of the supplemental materials for details), one can derive the analytic expression of the total excursion weights,
\mathcal{W}(X_{1}\to X_{1})
:

\mathcal{W}\left(X_{1}\to X_{1}\right)=\dfrac{s_{1}^{+}\,s_{2}^{-}\,\Delta_{3}+\mathfrak{p}\prod_{i=1}^{N}s_{i}^{+}}{\Delta_{2}},

(34)

where
\Delta_{2}
and
\Delta_{3}
are two determinants whose expressions are given in appendix
V.4
.
Our previous result Eq. (
29
) extends to the case where backwards reactions are included because we show in this appendix that :

\displaystyle\mathcal{W}\left(X_{1}\to X_{1}\right)\leq\mathfrak{p}.

(35)

As before, this means that, in the presence of backward reactions,
the total excursion weight of a single cycle is still limited by the connectivity of the branching point as in the case with no backward reactions.

Furthermore, assuming that the forward specificities take the same values in the reversible and irreversible version of the same network, the presence of backward reactions has a positive impact on the total excursion weight.
Indeed, the total excursion weight in the irreversible case given by Eq. (
26
) is always lower than its counterpart with reversible reactions given by Eq. (
34
), regardless of the values taken by backward rates as long as the
(s_{i}^{+})
take the same values in both networks.
The intuitive explanation is that the total weight contains more paths in the reversible case.
Note that fixing the values of the forward specificities to be the same in both networks imply different kinetic rates constants in both networks.

If we now fix instead the values of the forward rate constants, we recover the irreversible case where, for each reactions,
k_{i}^{+}\gg k_{i}^{-}
:

\mathcal{W}(X_{1}\to X_{1})\longrightarrow\mathfrak{p}\prod_{i=1}^{N}\dfrac{k_{i}^{+}}{k_{i}^{+}+\kappa_{i}}.

(36)

In the regime where the backward rates dominate,
{k^{+}_{i}\ll k^{-}_{i}}
, the total excursion weight is given by the elementary weight of the first reaction :

\mathcal{W}(X_{1}\to X_{1})\longrightarrow s_{1}^{+}=\dfrac{k_{1}^{+}}{k_{1}^{+}+\kappa_{1}}\leq 1.

(37)

Not surprisingly, in this regime the network goes extinct (i.e.
\lambda<0
) or, if there is no degradation on the first species, it neither grows nor decreases (i.e.
\lambda=0
and
\mathcal{W}=1
).

III.2
Unicyclic network with side-branches

When a side branch is introduced in an unicyclic network with
\mathfrak{p}=1
, one obtains a Type II network in the classification of Ref.
Blokhuis et al. 2020a
.
Such network is represented in Fig.
2
b
: one can see that there is a side-branch that connects species
X_{N}
back to species
X_{\mathfrak{u}}
.
In practice, this class of networks is large because there is a lot of freedom regarding the content (in terms of reactions) and length of this side branch.

Let us consider again first the case of irreversible reactions and then the case of reversible reactions in an example.

III.2.1
Irreversible case

By the same method used in previous sections, one can obtain an explicit form of the total excursion weight in the irreversible case:

\mathcal{W}(X_{1}\to X_{1})=\dfrac{\prod_{i=1}^{N}s_{i}^{+}}{1-\prod_{i=\mathfrak{u}}^{N}s_{i}^{+}}.

(38)

Some observations are, in order :
(i) the side branch plays an essential role in these Type II networks, without it, the total weight would be
\prod_{i=1}^{N}s_{i}^{+}
and thus would remain below threshold since
0<s_{i}^{+}<1
;
(ii) Type II networks are intrinsically more robust than Type I networks, because the total excursion weight of Type I networks is limited by the vertex connectivity
\mathfrak{p}
, which in practice can not be a very large number, while the total excursion weight of Type II networks is enhanced by the contribution of the side branch.
More precisely, if we assume that all
s_{i}^{+}
take the same values in both networks, the total excursion weight of Type II networks overcomes that of Type I when

\prod_{i=\mathfrak{u}}^{N}s_{i}^{+}>1-\frac{1}{\mathfrak{p}}.

(39)

Naturally, the benefice of side branches can only go so far, and the total weight of Type II networks still remains finite. Before, we present another strategy for further enhancing network connectivity and robustness thanks to coupling autocatalytic cycles to each other, let us study a particular example of a simple network with a side-branch.

III.3.1
Anti-parallel coupling

In the anti-parallel coupling, the two classes of excursion of
R_{i}
are identical to those of the uncoupled case,
R_{i}\to X\to Y\to 2\,R_{i}
or
R_{i}\to X\to R_{i}
, however, the coupling adds the possibility to loop an arbitrary number of time in cycle
(1-i)
before reaching
R_{i}
.
This is reflected in the expression of the total excursion weight in this case which reads:

\mathcal{W}(R_{i}\to R_{i})=\dfrac{W^{(i)}}{1-W^{(1-i)}}.

(51)

In Eq. (
51
), the numerator accounts for the fact no new class of excursion are present in the coupled network, compared to the uncoupled case; while the denominator describes the possibility of doing an arbitrary number of loops in cycle
(1-i)
.
Doing
n
such loops brings a weight
(W^{(1-i)})^{n}
, hence, the total contribution of all the back-and-forth in cycle
(1-i
) is the a geometric sum of
(W^{(1-i)})^{n}
.
From the expression of the total excursion weight, we can quantify the positive influence of coupling:

1.

if
W^{(1-i)}>1
or, equivalently, if the cycle of
R_{1-i}
already ensures exponential growth, the total excursion weight Eq. (
51
) becomes formally negative, meaning that, in truth, it diverges.
Indeed, in this case, the geometric summation of
W^{(1-i)}
diverges.
This occurs in the region before the black dotted curve in Fig.
3
e
.

2.

If
W^{(1-i)}<1
, the coupling lowers the threshold to achieve exponential growth in the coupled network for
W^{(i)}
:

W^{(i)}>1-W^{(1-i)},

(52)

which can be fulfilled for
W^{(i)}<1
.
In Fig.
3
e
, this case takes place between the black dotted curve and the solid white curve (which is the threshold
\mathcal{W}=1
).

In the first case, the coupled cycle achieves exponential growth through just one of the two cycles, which
sustains
the overall growth.
In the second case, both cycles, taken independently, are not able to trigger exponential growth but, once coupled, they can
cooperate
to grow as a whole.
Note that Eq. (
52
) implies that, as far as the threshold is concerned, the excursion weights
W^{(i)}
behaves in an additive way (cf. Fig.
3
d
).

III.3.2
Parallel coupling

Unlike the anti-parallel case, parallel coupling adds a new class excursion,
R_{0}\to X\to 2\,R_{1}\to Y\to 2\,R_{0}
(and similarly for
R_{1}
), that exists only in the coupled network.
The latter is represented as the blue path in Fig.
3
c
.
In addition, as before, if one looks for
{\mathcal{W}(R_{i}\to R_{i})}
,
parallel coupling also brings the possibility of doing an arbitrary number of loops in network
(1-i)
.
Consequently, in the parallel coupling the total excursion weight is:

\mathcal{W}(R_{i}\to R_{i})=\dfrac{W^{(i)}}{1-W^{(1-i)}}+\dfrac{\Delta}{1-W^{(1-i)}},

(53)

where

\Delta=\left.\left.\dfrac{w_{R_{0}\to X}w_{R_{1}\to Y}}{1-w_{X\to Y}w_{Y\to X}}\right[w_{Y\to R_{0}}w_{X\to R_{1}}-w_{X\to R_{0}}w_{Y\to R_{1}}\right].

(54)

In Eq. (
53
), the first term has the same interpretation as before and the additional term takes into account the new excursion class brought by the coupling.
Surprisingly, the influence of the new class (
R_{0}\to X\to 2\,R_{1}\to Y\to 2\,R_{0}
) on the total excursion weight has to be compared to the two classes associated with the backward reactions,
R_{0}\to X\to R_{0}
and
R_{1}\to Y\to R_{1}
, such that:

\Delta>0~~\Leftrightarrow~~w_{Y\to R_{0}}w_{X\to R_{1}}>w_{X\to R_{0}}w_{Y\to R_{1}}.

(55)

This implies that
\Delta>0
if the former dominates and, in the converse case,
\Delta<0
.
More precisely, writing explicitly the expression of the elementary weights, the condition Eq. (
55
) can be translated on a condition on the kinetic rate constants:
4\,k_{3}^{+}\nu_{3}^{+}>k_{1}^{-}\nu_{1}^{-}
.

Again, if one has
W^{(i)}<1
and
W^{(1-i)}>1
, cycle
(1-i)
support the growth of the coupled cycle, making
\mathcal{W}(R_{i}\to R_{i})>1
for any value of
W^{(i)}
.
However, in the parallel coupling, if
W^{(1-i)}<1
the effective threshold for
W^{(i)}
to reach exponential growth in the coupled network is altered compared to Eq. (
52
):

W^{(i)}>1-W^{(1-i)}-\Delta.

(56)

Hence, positive values of
\Delta
are even more favorable for the threshold.
This is not surprising, as
\Delta>0
favors the new excursion class compared to the two excursions associated with the backward reactions and the former is the only one that increases the number of species (while the two others are not creating additional species).

As for the example, the instability threshold of two coupled unicyclic cycles is straightforward using the approach based on the total excursion weight, even tough the underlying growth rate,
\lambda
, for the coupled network cannot be obtained analytically (in general, it will be the maximum root of an order 4 polynomial).
The present approach also makes clear that coupling two autocatalytic cycles to each other can lead to robust networks, potentially more robust than type II networks, because the total excursion weight can diverge when
W^{(1-i)}
approaches 1, while the total excursion weight of type II networks remains finite.

III.4
General case

Although Eqs. (
51
) and (
53
) do not hold for couplings involving more complex networks, they nevertheless capture the essential mechanisms by which coupling promotes the onset of growth.
First, coupling tends to reduce the threshold for growth associated with each of the autocatalytic cycles in the coupled network, enabling cycle cooperation.
Specifically, if
W^{(0)}<1
and
W^{(1)}<1
are the total excursion weights of two decaying autocatalytic cycles, the total excursion weight
\mathcal{W}
, obtained by coupling these two cycles, might still be able to overcome the threshold:
\mathcal{W}>1
.
Additionally, the growth of one individual cycle in a coupled network is able to sustain the growth of the whole network.
In that case, one of the individual weight already exceeds the threshold,
W^{(i)}>1
, such that
\mathcal{W}>1
for any value of
W^{(1-i)}
.
Finally, as illustrated in the anti-parallel case above, coupling also facilitates the onset of growth by enabling the emergence of new excursion paths that exist only in the coupled network.

IV
Summary and discussion

We have presented a framework to calculate the decay threshold of a CRN, above which replication can overcome degradation reactions.
The formalism is solely based on the assumption of a Metzler Jacobian and applies to various rate laws.
The Metzler property holds in particular for autocatalytic cores which represent a part of autocatalytic networks that can
be effectively described by linear kinetics.
This method circumvents the need to compute the eigenvalue spectrum of the Jacobian, which is generally intractable. Instead, it exploits the hypergraph description of a chemical network to extract the cumulative weight of all the excursion paths.
The latter is shown to be related to the sign of the leading eigenvalue and is computed as a solution of a linear system.
As a result, our criterion can be expressed in a simple way even when the parameters associated with the rates are not known.

Besides helping to determine whether a given network is growing (or is dynamically unstable), the total excursion weight
\mathcal{W}
can also inform on how far a given network is from the threshold of exponential growth (or instability): high values of
\mathcal{W}
imply that a perturbation in the network is less likely to disturb the regime of exponential growth, while a value of
\mathcal{W}
close to threshold may indicate a fragile or sensitive network.

Thanks to this insight, we can formulate a number of design principles for autocatalytic networks, which could have a potential relevance for Origin of Life studies. First, we find that unicyclic autocatalytic networks are rather fragile and their size is also limited: large cycles should grow more slowly and are thus more likely to be overcome by small cycles in a competitive setting
Könnyű et al. 2024
. For a fixed size, perturbations of kinetic parameters in type I networks are expected to easily produce extinction of the cycle as compared with other topologies. By introducing additional pathways in the unicyclic network, either in the form of side branches and/or thanks to coupling between cycles, the robustness can be improved
Blokhuis et al. 2020b
. Both effects indeed tend to lower the threshold for exponential growth and favor highly connected and intricate networks as observed in metabolic networks. These two options for improvement with respect to type I cycles are however, not equivalent.

Let us first consider the benefit of side branches. A peculiarity of type I cycles is the presence of a branching point in the network, where a dimer splits into exactly two identical molecules. Typical networks with organic molecules do not often contain such a reaction, which might be the reason why type I cycles are more commonly found in redox chemical networks based on non-organic molecules
Peng et al. 2023
. In this paper, we have shown that type II cycles are robust than type I cycles already at the level of a Markov chain description, which suggests the possibility of a transition from a type I cycle to a type II in the course of evolution. In this context, it is interesting to note that the reverse TCA cycle, is an autocatalytic cycle only because of its side branch
Orgel 2008
, which makes it a type II cycles. When exploring all possible cycles operating with the same chemical compounds as the r-TCA cycle, one finds that a significant fraction of them also possess at least one side branch
Zubarev et al. 2015
. Further, among known natural or synthetic carbon-fixing pathways, a majority of them possess at least one side branch
Bar-Even et al. 2010
. These observations tend to confirm the view that side-branches are a feature of CRN that is favored by evolution.

Another option to improve robustness is to couple two type I cycles together. Depending on how this coupling is done, various ecological interactions can arise between the two cycles, which can, for instance be of the competition, mutualism, predation or bistability types
Peng et al. 2023
;
Könnyű et al. 2024
. Our formalism shows that, irrespective of the details of the coupling, and again at a Markov chain level, coupling these two cycles together is an even more efficient strategy to improve robustness than the incorporation of side branches because this can lead to larger values (in fact, potentially infinite) of the total excursion weight.
Indeed, we found that a growing autocatalytic cycle can support the growth of the ensemble of the two coupled cycles even when the second cycle taken in isolation would not achieve exponential growth: in this situation, the coupled network achieves growth for arbitrary values of the kinetic rates in the second cycle. This leads to a divergence of the total excursion weight, which can not be achieved with type II topology.
Further, the possible synergy in the association of the two cycles is reminiscent of free energy transduction in biology and physics, where a thermodynamically unfavorable process can be realized in practice by coupling it with a favorable one.
This analogy suggests that important thermodynamic concepts such as that of free energy transduction could be useful to understand ecosystem dynamics whether at a molecular or at a larger scale
Goyal et al. 2023
with important consequences for research on the Origin of Life.

V.1
Structure of the Jacobian matrix

Let us first prove that the diagonal elements of the Jacobian are negative under conditions discussed in the main text. Since both stoichiometric coefficients

S^{\pm}_{i\rho}
are non-negative, one can separate the case where they are zero from the case where they are strictly positive in the following way:

\displaystyle M_{ii}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\rho}(S^{-}_{i\rho}-S^{+}_{i\rho})\ \partial_{c_{i}}f_{\rho}

(57)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\rho\ \text{s.t.}\\
S^{-}_{i\rho}>0\end{subarray}}S^{-}_{i\rho}\ \underbrace{\partial_{c_{i}}f_{\rho}}_{\leq 0}-\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\rho\ \text{s.t.}\\
S^{+}_{i\rho}>0\end{subarray}}S^{+}_{i\rho}\ \underbrace{\partial_{c_{i}}f_{\rho}}_{\geq 0}~~<0.

This splitting of the sum is only possible because the reactions are non-ambiguous, and the conclusion on the sign of the global expression uses the condition Eq. (
7
) on the rates.

The off-diagonal coefficients are more complicated to express in the general case:

\displaystyle M_{ij}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\rho}(S^{-}_{i\rho}-S^{+}_{i\rho})\ \partial_{c_{j}}f_{\rho}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\rho\ \text{s.t.}\\
S^{-}_{i\rho}>0\end{subarray}}S^{-}_{i\rho}\ \partial_{c_{j}}f_{\rho}-\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\rho\ \text{s.t.}\\
S^{+}_{i\rho}>0\end{subarray}}S^{+}_{i\rho}\ \partial_{c_{j}}f_{\rho}

(58)

\displaystyle=\Bigg(\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\rho\ \text{s.t.}\\
S^{-}_{i\rho}>0,\ S^{+}_{j\rho}>0\end{subarray}}\hskip-10.00002ptS^{-}_{i\rho}\ \partial_{c_{j}}f_{\rho}~-\hskip-10.00002pt\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\rho\ \text{s.t.}\\
S^{+}_{i\rho}>0,\ S^{-}_{j\rho}>0\end{subarray}}\hskip-10.00002ptS^{+}_{i\rho}\ \partial_{c_{j}}f_{\rho}\Bigg)+\Bigg(\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\rho\ \text{s.t.}\\
S^{-}_{i\rho}>0,\ S^{-}_{j\rho}>0\end{subarray}}\hskip-10.00002ptS^{-}_{i\rho}\ \partial_{c_{j}}f_{\rho}~-\hskip-10.00002pt\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\rho\ \text{s.t.}\\
S^{+}_{i\rho}>0,\ S^{+}_{j\rho}>0\end{subarray}}\hskip-10.00002ptS^{+}_{i\rho}\ \partial_{c_{j}}f_{\rho}\Bigg).

In the last equality, the first parenthesis contains positive terms only, while the second parenthesis contains negative terms.

Now, the off-diagonal elements of the Jacobian matrix take a simple form under the limiting case where the reaction rates are not influenced by the products concentrations (i.e. product inhibition is absent):

\forall\rho,~S^{-}_{j\rho}>0~\Rightarrow~\partial_{c_{j}}f_{\rho}=0.

In that case, the second and third sum in Eq. (
58

M_{ij}=\hskip-10.00002pt\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\rho\ \text{s.t.}\\
S^{-}_{i\rho}>0,\ S^{+}_{j\rho}>0\end{subarray}}\hskip-10.00002ptS^{-}_{i\rho}\ \underbrace{\partial_{c_{j}}f_{\rho}}_{\geq 0}\hskip 10.00002pt-\hskip-10.00002pt\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\rho\ \text{s.t.}\\
S^{+}_{i\rho}>0,\ S^{+}_{j\rho}>0\end{subarray}}\hskip-10.00002ptS^{+}_{i\rho}\ \underbrace{\partial_{c_{j}}f_{\rho}}_{\geq 0}.

(59)

M_{ij}

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\rho\ \text{s.t.}\\

V.2
Proof of the criterion

The formal solution of Eq. (3) in the main text is

\boldsymbol{c}(t)=e^{\mathbb{M}\,t}\cdot\boldsymbol{c}_{0}.

(63)

Taking the Laplace transform of the solution provides,

\hat{\boldsymbol{c}}(s)=\left(\int_{0}^{+\infty}\hskip-15.00002pt\mathrm{d}t~e^{t\left(\mathbb{M}-s\mathbbm{1}\right)}\right)\cdot\boldsymbol{c}_{0}.

(64)

When it converges, the integral in the RHS matches the resolvent of
\mathbb{M}
:

R(s)=\int_{0}^{+\infty}\hskip-15.00002pt\mathrm{d}t~e^{t\left(\mathbb{M}-s\mathbbm{1}\right)}=\left(s\mathbbm{1}-\mathbb{M}\right)^{-1}

(65)

The latter is tightly related to the spectrum of
\mathbb{M}
as, whenever
s\in\mathrm{Sp}\left[\mathbb{M}\right]
, the resolvent has a singularity.
Furthermore, the entries of the Laplace transform of
\exp(t\,\mathbb{M})
are decreasing with
s
and may converge only for
\Re(s)>\lambda
.
As a consequence, if for
s=0
the Laplace transform of
\exp(t\,\mathbb{M})
diverges then, necessarily,
\lambda\geq 0
.

Now, separating the diagonal part of
\mathbb{M}-s\mathbbm{1}
,
\mathbb{M}^{\text{diag}}-s\mathbbm{1}
, to its off-diagonal part,
\mathbb{M}^{\text{off}}
, and using the Trotter product formula,

e^{t\left(\mathbb{M}-s\mathbbm{1}\right)}=\lim_{n\to+\infty}\left(e^{\frac{t}{n}\left(\mathbb{M}^{\text{diag}}-s\mathbbm{1}\right)}\,e^{\frac{t}{n}\mathbb{M}^{\text{off}}}\right)^{n},

(66)

in the integral in Eq. (
65
) yields the path decomposition of the
(i,\,j)
-th entry of the Laplace transform:

R(s)_{i,\,j}=\sum_{\ell\geq 0}~\sum_{x_{1}\cdots x_{\ell}}\delta_{i,\,x_{1}}\,\delta_{j,\,x_{\ell}}\left(\prod_{k=1}^{\ell-1}w(s)_{x_{k+1}\to x_{k}}\right)\times\dfrac{1}{|M_{j,\,j}|+s}.

(67)

In Eq. (
67
), the summations run over all the paths
of arbitrary length
\ell\geq 0
connecting
i
and
j
,
i=x_{1}\to x_{2}\to\cdots\to x_{\ell-1}\to x_{\ell}=j
; and the elementary weights,
w(s)_{x\to y}
for
x\neq y
, are:

w(s)_{x\to y}=\dfrac{M_{y,\,x}}{-M_{x,\,x}+s}.

(68)

The latter converges to the elementary transition weights (Eq. (4) of the main text) as
s\to 0
:

w(s)_{x\to y}\longrightarrow w_{x\to y}
.
The diagonal elements of
R(s)
can be written as,

R(s)_{i,\,i}=\left(\sum_{\ell\geq 0}\mathcal{W}(i\to i)(s)^{\ell}\right)\times\dfrac{1}{|M_{i,i}|+s},

(69)

in which
\mathcal{W}(i\to i)(s)
corresponds to the total excursion weight associated to
i
computed with the elementary weights
w(s)
.
In the limit
s\to 0
, it converges to the total excursion weight
\mathcal{W}(i\to i)
.
Then, taking the limit
s\to 0
yields:

R(0)_{i,\,i}=\left(\sum_{\ell\geq 0}\mathcal{W}(i\to i)^{\ell}\right)\times\dfrac{1}{|M_{i,i}|},

(70)

which converges if, and only if,
\mathcal{W}(i\to i)<1
.
On the other hand, the Laplace transform converges for
s=0
then, necessarily,
\lambda<0
.
This finally proves the criterion for exponential growth given in Eq. (8) of the main text.

V.3
First-step method

For any Jacobian matrix
\mathbb{M}
, the expression of
\mathcal{W}(X_{1}\to X_{1})
can be obtained by solving a linear problem:

\begin{gathered}\mathcal{W}(X_{1}\to X_{1})=\sum_{i\neq 1}w_{j\to i}\,\mathcal{W}(X_{i}\to X_{1}),\\[5.0pt]
\mathcal{W}(X_{j}\to X_{1})=w_{j\to 1}+\sum_{i\neq 1,\,j}w_{j\to i}\,\mathcal{W}(X_{i}\to X_{1}),~~\text{for}~~j\neq 1,\end{gathered}

where
w_{j\to i}
stands for the elementary weight of the transition
j\to i
.
This method is similar to one used to compute mean first passage time in a Markov chain
Norris 1998
.
Using the matrix of the elementary weights,
\mathbb{W}
, whose
(i,\,j)
-th entry is
w_{j\to i}
, the system in Eq. (
V.3
) can be written as:

\mathbb{T}\cdot\boldsymbol{u}=\mathbf{w}_{\diamond\to 1},

(74)

in such a way that the
i
-th entry of
\boldsymbol{u}
is
\mathcal{W}(X_{i}\to X_{1})
and the matrix
\mathbb{T}=\left\{T_{i,\ j}\right\}
is obtained by setting all the off-diagonal entries in the first column of
-\mathbb{W}^{\top}
to zero:

\displaystyle T_{1,\,1}=1,

\displaystyle T_{i,\,1}=0~~\text{for}~~i>1,

\displaystyle T_{i,\,j}=-W_{j,\,i}=-w_{i\to j}~~\text{for}~~j>1.

(75)

Finally, the entries of
\mathbf{w}_{\diamond\to 1}
are

\displaystyle\left(\mathbf{w}_{\diamond\to 1}\right)_{1}=0,

\displaystyle\left(\mathbf{w}_{\diamond\to 1}\right)_{i}=W_{i,\,1}=w_{i\to 1},~~\text{for}~~i>1.

(76)

As a result, the total excursion weight,
i.e.
,
u_{1}=\mathcal{W}(X_{1}\to X_{1})
, is expressed as the ratio of two determinants using Cramer’s formula:

\mathcal{W}(X_{1}\to X_{1})=\dfrac{\mathrm{det}\left(\mathbf{w}_{\diamond\to 1};\,\mathbb{T}_{\setminus 1}\right)}{\mathrm{det}\left(\mathbb{T}\right)},

(77)

where
\left(\mathbf{w}_{\diamond\to 1};\,\mathbb{T}_{\setminus 1}\right)
is the matrix formed by replacing the first column of
\mathbb{T}
by
\mathbf{w}_{\diamond\to 1}
.
The sole knowledge of the jacobian matrix suffices to construct
\mathbb{W}
from which
\mathbb{T}
and
\mathbf{w}_{\diamond\to 1}
follow hence, this method provides a way to compute the total excursion weight for any given jacobian matrix.

Finally, as far as the total excursion weight is concerned, only the first solution of Eq. (
V.3
) is needed.

V.4
Details for the unicyclic autocatalytic cycle

Defining the following family of determinants :

\Delta_{i}=\begin{vNiceArray}.

(78)

for
2\leq i\leq N
.
This includes, as a particular case, the determinants
\Delta_{2}
and
\Delta_{3}
in the total excursion weight given in Eq. (27) of the main text.
All the determinants
\Delta_{i}
are related to each other by the recursive relation :

\displaystyle 2\leq i\leq N-2\,,~~\Delta_{i}=\Delta_{i+1}-s_{i}^{+}\,s_{i+1}^{-}\,\Delta_{i+2}.

(79)

Further,
\Delta_{N-1}=1-s_{N}^{-}s_{N-1}^{+}
and
\Delta_{N}=1
.
An important property which follows from Eq. (
30
)
is that for all
i
:

s_{i}^{-}+s_{i}^{+}\leq 1,

(80)

an inequality which is saturated when
\kappa_{i}=0
.
Since the matrix whose determinant is
\Delta_{i}
is diagonally dominated,
we can apply the Gershgorin circle theorem. It follows from this theorem that all eigenvalues of the matrix lie in at least one Gershgorin circle. These circles are centered at 1 and have a radius, which is either
|s_{i}^{+}|
,
|s_{N}^{-}|
or
|s_{i}^{+}+s_{i}^{-}|
.
Since in all these cases, the radius are less than one,
it follows that, for all
i
,
\Delta_{i}\geq 0
.
Now, using this property together with Eq. (
79
), we obtain :

\Delta_{i}-s_{i}^{-}\Delta_{i+1}\leq\Delta_{i-1}\leq\Delta_{i}\leq 1,

(81)

and, combining the recursion relation Eq. (
79
) and the inequality Eq. (
80
) yields :

\displaystyle\Delta_{i}-s_{i}^{-}\Delta_{i+1}

\displaystyle\geq s^{+}_{i}\left(\Delta_{i+1}-s_{i+1}^{-}\Delta_{i+2}\right)

(82)

\displaystyle\geq\prod_{k=i}^{N}s^{+}_{k}.

(83)

Note that in Eqs. (
82
)-(
83
), equality is also reached when degradation vanishes.
The last inequality implies that

\dfrac{s_{2}^{-}\Delta_{3}+\prod_{k=2}^{N}s_{k}^{+}}{\Delta_{2}}\leq 1,

(84)

and

\dfrac{\prod_{k=2}^{N}s_{k}^{+}}{\Delta_{2}}\leq 1.

(85)

Taken together, the above inequalities imply :

\displaystyle\mathcal{W}(X_{1}\to X_{1})

\displaystyle=s_{1}^{+}\left(\dfrac{s_{2}^{-}\Delta_{3}+\prod_{k=2}^{N}s_{k}^{+}}{\Delta_{2}}+(\mathfrak{p}-1)\,\dfrac{\prod_{k=2}^{N}s_{k}^{+}}{\Delta_{2}}\right)\leq\mathfrak{p}\,s_{1}^{+}\leq\mathfrak{p}.

(86)
