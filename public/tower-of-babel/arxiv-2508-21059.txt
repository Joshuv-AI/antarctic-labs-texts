Dynamics of the Fermion-Rotor System
Vazha Loladze, Takemichi Okui, and David Tong

Abstract
We explore the dynamics of the fermion-rotor system, a simple impurity model in d = 1 + 1 d=1+1 dimensions consisting of a collection of purely right-moving fermions interacting with a quantum mechanical rotor localised at the origin. This was first introduced by Polchinski as a toy model for monopole-fermion scattering and is surprisingly subtle, with ingoing and outgoing fermions carrying different quantum numbers. We show that the rotor acts as a twist operator in the low-energy theory, changing the quantum numbers of excitations that have previously passed through the origin to ensure scattering consistent with all symmetries. We further show how generalisations of this model with multiple rotors and unequal charges can be viewed as a UV-completion of boundary states for chiral theories, including the well-studied 3450 model. We compute correlation functions between ingoing and outgoing fermions, and show that fermions dressed with the rotor degree of freedom act as local operators and create single-particle states, generalizing an earlier result obtained in a theory with a single rotor and equal charges. Finally, we point out a mod 2 anomaly in these models that descends from the Witten anomaly in 4d.

Introduction

The fermion-rotor system is a deceptively simple model in
d=1+1
dimensions constructed long ago by Polchinski
joe
. The model consists of a collection of purely right-moving massless fermions, interacting with an impurity which takes the form of a quantum mechanical rotor localised at the origin of space. The subtlety in the model arises because of a quantum anomaly which ensures that ingoing fermions carry different quantum numbers from the outgoing fermions. This makes it challenging to see how consistent scattering can be achieved while preserving all symmetries.

This challenge was recently overcome in

lt

This is possible because the fermions are dressed with an operator associated to the rotor, evaluated at the time in the past when the fermion passed through the origin. At first glance, dressing a fermion far from the origin with the rotor would seem to give rise to something non-local. Yet this is not what happens. The net result is that, surprisingly, fermions propagate through the rotor as free single-particle states.

Polchinski’s motivation for designing the fermion-rotor system was to have a simple toy model that captures the physics of massless fermions scattering off magnetic monopoles in

Importantly, this paradox exists for monopoles in the Standard Model and there is no consensus in the community as to what happens when, say, a right-handed electron scatters off a magnetic monopole. In recent years, there has been renewed interest in this paradox, with a number of different resolutions proposed

2
The Fermion-Rotor System

The original Polchinski rotor model has
N
right-moving Weyl fermions,
\psi_{i}(t,x)
with
i=1,\ldots,N
, coupled to a rotor degree of freedom
\alpha(t)
, with action

\displaystyle S=\int d^{2}x\ i\sum_{i=1}^{N}\psi_{i}^{\dagger}\big(\partial_{+}+i\alpha(t)f^{\prime}(x)\big)\psi_{i}+\int dt\ \frac{I}{2}\dot{\alpha}^{2}\ .

(2.1)

Here the chiral derivative
\partial_{+}=\partial_{t}+\partial_{x}
reflects the fact that our fermions are purely right-moving,
I
is the moment of inertia for the rotor, while
f^{\prime}(x)
is a fixed background that should be viewed as a regularised delta-function sitting at the origin, as shown in Figure
1
. We take this to be an even function, normalised as

\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dx\ f^{\prime}(x)=1\ .

(2.2)

In Section
2.2
, we will generalise the rotor model to include different charges, and multiple rotors. But for now we summarise some of the key features of this simple theory.

The classical action has a global
SU(N)
symmetry, rotating the fermions
\psi_{i}
. This
SU(N)
is non-anomalous and survives in the quantum theory.
Classically, the action also enjoys a global
U(1)
symmetry, acting a

\displaystyle\psi_{i}(t,x)\rightarrow e^{i\beta}\psi_{i}(t,x)\,.

(2.3)

However, this is not a symmetry of the quantum theory as it suffers an anomaly. To see this, note that the rotor coupling could be viewed as a gauge field
A_{1}(t,x)=\alpha(t)f^{\prime}(x)
, albeit one that is slightly unusual because
\alpha(t)
is dynamical while
f^{\prime}(x)
is fixed. This corresponds to a field strength
F_{01}=\dot{\alpha}f^{\prime}(x)
. The would-be current associated to this symmetry is
J=\sum_{i}\psi^{\dagger}_{i}\psi_{i}
, but the anomaly means that this is not-conserved, instead obeying

\displaystyle\partial_{+}J(t,x)=\frac{N}{2\pi}\,\dot{\alpha}(t)f^{\prime}(x)\,.

(2.4)

The rotor model does, however, have a different global
U(1)
symmetry that is non-anomalous and survives in the quantum theory. This acts as

\displaystyle\psi_{i}(t,x)\rightarrow e^{i\beta f(x)}\psi_{i}(t,x)\ \ \ {\rm and}\ \ \ \alpha(t)\rightarrow\alpha(t)-\beta\,.

(2.5)

The presence of the function
f(x)
in the exponent is what makes things interesting. This is a non-anomalous symmetry providing that we take
f(x)
to be odd, with asymptotic behavior
f(x)\rightarrow\pm{\textstyle\frac{1}{2}}
as
x\rightarrow\pm\infty
. It is this requirement that fixes the integration constant in
f(x)
when we derive it from integrating
f^{\prime}(x)
. (The action is in terms of
f^{\prime}(x)
, not
f(x)
.) But the presence of
f(x)
in the
U(1)
transformation means that the ingoing fermions to the left of the rotor carry (up to a factor of
1/2
) charge
-1
under this
U(1)
, while the outgoing fermions to the right of the rotor carry charge
+1
. And therein lies the rub.

The upshot is that incoming (
x<0
) fermions
\psi_{i}(x)
sit in the representation
{\bf N}_{-1}
under the
SU(N)\times U(1)
symmetry, while outgoing (
x>0
) fermions
\psi_{i}(x)
sit in the representation
{\bf N}_{+1}
. How, then, can consistent scattering be achieved conserving both symmetries?

There is an obvious answer to this question that is wrong. This follows from the observation that the
U(1)
symmetry (
2.5
) shifts
\alpha
, and
so the corresponding Noether charge also depends on the rotor in addition to the fermions,
and it is given by

\displaystyle Q=I\dot{\alpha}(t)+\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dx\,f(x)\,J(t,x)\,.

(2.6)

This means that a spinning rotor carries charge
Q_{\rm dyon}=I\dot{\alpha}
which, following the connection to monopoles, we refer to as the dyon charge. This suggests that as a fermion passes through the origin, it deposits charge
-2
on the rotor. However, this cannot happen at low energies. The energy of the excited
rotor is
E_{\rm dyon}=I\dot{\alpha}^{2}/2=Q_{\rm dyon}^{2}/2I
. A charge
Q_{\rm dyon}\sim{\cal O}(1)
entails an energy
E_{\rm dyon}\sim 1/I
. If we send in the massless fermion with an energy
E\ll 1/I
then the resulting excited rotor
must be highly off-shell and cannot last longer than a time scale
\sim I\ll 1/E
. Indeed, as we will review below, any charge on the rotor quickly decays. So what does happen?

The correct answer is much less obvious and was given in
lt
: the fermion with quantum numbers
{\bf N}_{-1}
propagates freely through the rotor impurity, and continues as an outgoing state. This is surprising because, as we’ve seen, naively there is no propagating state with quantum numbers
{\bf N}_{-1}
to the right of the rotor. Instead, the operator
\psi_{i}^{\dagger}(t,x)
creates a state with quantum number
{\bf N}_{+1}
. The key result of
lt
is that the operator

\displaystyle{\cal O}_{i}(t,x)=e^{i\alpha(t-x)}\,\psi_{i}(t,x)

(2.7)

does the job, carrying the correct quantum numbers
{\bf N}_{-1}
. At first glance,
{\cal O}_{i}(t,x)
appears to be a non-local operator, with the fermion at the point
(t,x)
dressed with the rotor degree of freedom evaluated at the retarded time
t-x
when the fermion interacted with the impurity. Nonetheless, as shown in
lt
, there is no hint of this non-locality when the operator is evaluated in correlation functions. Indeed, the simplest such correlation function is

\displaystyle\bigl\langle e^{i\alpha(t-x)}\psi_{i}(t,x)\,\psi_{j}^{\dagger}(t^{\prime},x^{\prime})\bigr\rangle=\delta_{ij}G_{0}(t-t^{\prime},x-x^{\prime})

(2.8)

where
x^{\prime}<0
and
x>0
, so this corresponds to an
{\bf N}_{-1}
fermion sent in from the left, passing through the rotor. The right-hand side
G_{0}(t-t^{\prime},x-x^{\prime})
is simply the propagator for a free chiral fermion. We review the derivation of this result in Section
3
.

2.1
The Rotor and the Twist

We now look more closely at the rotor operator
e^{i\alpha(t)}
. We will show that this obeys the equal-time exchange relations appropriate for a twist operator.

To do this, we first solve the quantum equations of motion for the fermion-rotor system. The equation of motion for the rotor is

\displaystyle I\ddot{\alpha}(t)=-\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}dx\,f^{\prime}(x)\,J(t,x)\,.

(2.9)

The current
J
is, in turn, related to the rotor degree of freedom through the anomaly equation (
2.4
).
We can integrate (
2.4
) once to give

\displaystyle J(t,x)=J(t-x+x_{0},x_{0})+\frac{N}{2\pi}\int_{x_{0}}^{x}dx^{\prime}\,f^{\prime}(x^{\prime})\,\dot{\alpha}(t-x+x^{\prime})

(2.10)

where, at this stage,
x_{0}
is an arbitrary integration constant.

Setting the integration constant to the two values
x_{0}=\pm r_{0}
, with
r_{0}
the delta-function cut-off, provides different insights into the nature of the rotor dynamics. We start by setting
x_{0}=-r_{0}
, a point just to the left of the rotor. We then take the limit
r_{0}\rightarrow 0
, so that
f^{\prime}(x^{\prime})\rightarrow\delta(x^{\prime})
. This gives

\displaystyle J(t,x)=\tilde{J}_{\rm in}(t-x)+\frac{N}{2\pi}\theta(x)\dot{\alpha}(t-x)\ .

(2.11)

Here
\theta(x)
is the usual Heaviside step function, and we’ve introduced the incoming current immediately to the left of the rotor,

\displaystyle\tilde{J}_{\rm in}(t)=\displaystyle{\lim_{r_{0}\rightarrow 0}}\,J(t-r_{0},-r_{0})\ .

(2.12)

If we now substitute the expression (
2.11
) into the equation of motion (
2.9
), we get

\displaystyle I\ddot{\alpha}(t)=-\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dx\,f^{\prime}(x)\,\tilde{J}_{\rm in}(t-x)-\frac{N}{2\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{0}dx\,f^{\prime}(x)\,\dot{\alpha}(t-x)

(2.13)

Again, we take the
f^{\prime}(x)\rightarrow\delta(x)
limit in both terms here but, in the second term, we pick up a factor of
1/2
because the integral is only along the half-line. The end result is that the dynamics of the rotor is governed by

\displaystyle I\ddot{\alpha}(t)=-\tilde{J}_{\rm in}(t)-\frac{N}{4\pi}\dot{\alpha}(t)\,.

(2.14)

The result of the anomaly is manifest here: it appears in the second term above which acts as effective friction term for the rotor. Finding a friction term arising in a Hamiltonian system is rather unusual. But, as we’ve seen, the friction term doesn’t come from the classical equations of motion. Instead, it comes from the anomaly. Moreover, it’s clear what the resulting physics is: any excitation of the rotor caused by the incoming current
\tilde{J}_{\rm in}(t)
is quickly damped in a time scale
t\sim 4\pi I/N
. The energy, and any quantum charge, stored in the rotor must be emitted in outgoing fermions.

We can get a different perspective by returning to (
2.10
) and setting the integration constant to be
x_{0}=r_{0}
, the right-most cut-off of the delta-function. This will relate the rotor degree of freedom to the outgoing current, just to the right of the rotor,

\displaystyle\tilde{J}_{\rm out}(t)=\displaystyle{\lim_{r_{0}\rightarrow 0}}\,J(t+r_{0},r_{0})\ .

(2.15)

Repeating the steps above, including taking the limit
r_{0}\rightarrow 0
, means that the expression for the current (
2.11
) is replaced by

\displaystyle J(t,x)=\tilde{J}_{\rm out}(t-x)-\frac{N}{2\pi}\theta(-x)\dot{\alpha}(t-x)\ .

(2.16)

Substituting this into the equation of motion (
2.9
), and taking the
r_{0}\rightarrow 0
limit, now gives the result

\displaystyle I\ddot{\alpha}(t)=-\tilde{J}_{\rm out}(t)+\frac{N}{4\pi}\dot{\alpha}(t)\,.

(2.17)

Naively the
+
ve sign in the second term makes it look as if the rotor is speeding up! But that’s misleading because an excited rotor will induce an outgoing current
\tilde{J}_{\rm out}(t)
, so the first term above is non-vanishing in the situation where the rotor decays.

Our main interest lies in the quantum numbers of the outgoing fermions, and how they are affected by the rotor. To proceed, the expression (
2.17
) will prove most useful. In the low-energy limit
E\ll 1/I
, we may drop the inertia term in (
2.17
), leaving

\displaystyle\dot{\alpha}(t)=\frac{4\pi}{N}\tilde{J}_{\rm out}(t)\ .

(2.18)

Integrating this once gives

\displaystyle\alpha(t-x)=\alpha(t)-\frac{4\pi}{N}\int_{t-x}^{t}dt^{\prime}\,\tilde{J}_{\rm out}(t^{\prime})\,.

(2.19)

The current
\tilde{J}_{\rm out}(t^{\prime})
is defined in terms of
\psi_{i}(t,0^{+})
, the fermions just to the right of the rotor in the
r_{0}\rightarrow 0
limit.
Because the fermions are just free massless right-movers away from the rotor, we have
\psi_{i}(t,0^{+})=\psi_{i}(t+T,T)
for any
T>0
.
Similarly,
\psi_{i}(t,0^{-})=\psi_{i}(t-T,-T)
for any
T>0
.
Then, for any fermion
\psi(t,y)
and the rotor
\alpha(t-x)
with
x>0
,
we have from (
2.19
) that

\displaystyle[\alpha(t-x),\psi_{i}(t,y)]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{4\pi}{N}\int_{t-x}^{t}dt^{\prime}\,[\tilde{J}_{\rm out}(t^{\prime}),\psi_{i}(t,y)]

(2.20)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{4\pi}{N}\int_{t-x}^{t}dt^{\prime}\,\sum_{j}[\psi_{j}^{\dagger}\psi_{j}(t^{\prime},0^{+}),\psi_{i}(t,y)]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{4\pi}{N}\int_{t-x}^{t}dt^{\prime}\,\sum_{j}[\psi_{j}^{\dagger}\psi_{j}(T,-t^{\prime}+T),\psi_{i}(T,y-t+T)]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{4\pi}{N}\int_{t-x}^{t}dt^{\prime}\,\delta(t^{\prime}-t+y)\,\psi_{i}(t,y)\,.

Here, for
y<0
, the shifts are justified because there exists
T
such that
-t^{\prime}+T>0
and
y-t+T<0
over the entire integration range
t-x<t^{\prime}<t
.
However, the
\delta

For
y>0
(and still
x>0

T
such that
-t^{\prime}+T>0
and
y-t+T>0
,
and this time the
\delta
-function does get picked up as long as
-x+y<0
.
We thus have

\displaystyle[\alpha(t-x),\psi_{i}(t,y)]=-\frac{4\pi}{N}\,\theta(y)\,\theta(x-y)\,\psi_{i}(t,y)\,.

(2.21)

The product of step functions
\theta(y)\theta(x-y)
is telling us that the rotor and fermion fail to commute only if
0<y<x
, so that fermion insertion
\psi_{i}(t,y)
lies within the lightcone of the rotor interaction at time

, as shown in the figure to the right. For

or

We define the twist operator
{\cal T}(t,x)
, for

\displaystyle{\cal T}(t,x)=e^{i\alpha(t-x)}\ \ .

(2.22)

From (

\psi_{i}(t,y)
for

, but for outgoing fermions the operator enjoys the equal-time exchange relations

\displaystyle{\cal T}(t,x)\psi_{i}(t,y)=\psi_{i}(t,y){\cal T}(t,x)\ \ \

for

\displaystyle{\cal T}(t,x)\psi_{i}(t,y)=e^{-4\pi i/N}\psi_{i}(t,y){\cal T}(t,x)\ \ \

\displaystyle\mbox{for\ $y<x$}\ .

(2.23)

These are indeed the exchange relations of a twist operator (see, for example,

phase rotation on outgoing fermions. This agrees with the proposal of

2.2
More Rotors, More Twists

In this section, we look at a more general class of fermion-rotor models. We will again consider
N
fermions,
\psi_{i}(t,x)
, with
i=1,\ldots,N
, but now we take these to interact with
r
different rotor degrees of freedom,
\alpha_{a}(t)
, with
a=1,\ldots,r
. Each of these rotors is localised at some point, but they need not be at the same point. We introduce a collection of regularised delta-functions
f^{\prime}_{a}(x)
, each obeying

\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dx\ f^{\prime}_{a}(x)=1\ .

(2.27)

Moreover, we allow each fermion to interact with the rotors through different charges
q_{ai}\in{\mathds{Z}}
. The action of this model is

\displaystyle S=\int d^{2}x\ i\sum_{i=1}^{N}\psi_{i}^{\dagger}\big(\partial_{+}+i\sum_{a=1}^{r}q_{ai}\alpha_{a}(t)f^{\prime}_{a}(x)\big)\psi_{i}+\ \sum_{a=1}^{r}\int dt\ \frac{I_{a}}{2}\dot{\alpha_{a}}^{2}\ .

(2.28)

As in the previous section, we can think of this as
N
chiral fermions, coupled to
r
gauge fields. For a general choice of
f_{a}(x)
, with rotors localised at different points, the theory is consistent, with no mixed gauge anomalies, providing that the charges obey

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{N}q_{ai}q_{bi}=0\ \ {\rm for}\ a\neq b.

(2.29)

(We can relax this condition when some of the
f_{a}(x)
coincide, although we will choose not to here.) There is a global symmetry associated to each rotor, given by

\displaystyle\psi_{i}(t,x)\rightarrow e^{iq_{ai}\beta_{a}\,f_{a}(x)}\psi_{i}(t,x)\ \ \ {\rm and}\ \ \ \alpha_{a}(t)\rightarrow\alpha_{a}(t)-\beta_{a}\ \ \ {\rm with}\ a=1,\ldots,r\ .\ \ \ \

(2.30)

These are the extensions of the previous symmetry (
2.5
). In addition, there are further global symmetries that leave the rotors untouched. To describe these, we first introduce a collection of charges
p_{a^{\prime}i}\in{\mathds{Z}}
with
a^{\prime}=r+1,\cdots,N
, satisfying

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{N}q_{ai}\,p_{a^{\prime}i}=0\ .

(2.31)

This orthogonality condition ensures that the following
U(1)^{N-r}
symmetries labelled by
a^{\prime}
are non-anomalous

\displaystyle\psi_{i}(t,x)\rightarrow e^{ip_{a^{\prime}i}\beta_{a^{\prime}}}\psi_{i}(t,x)\ \ \ {\rm with}\ a^{\prime}=r+1,\ldots,N\ .

(2.32)

The upshot is that there are
U(1)^{N}
global symmetries, that naturally decompose as
U(1)^{r}\times U(1)^{N-r}
. The factors of
f_{a}(x)
in (
2.30
) mean that the
U(1)^{r}
symmetries act differently on the incoming and outgoing fermions (and, indeed, differently yet again on the fermions in regions between rotors). We denote the charges of fermions on the far left as
Q_{\alpha i}
and the charges of fermions on the far right as
\bar{Q}_{\alpha i}
, with
i=1,\ldots,N
labelling fermions and
\alpha=(a,a^{\prime})=1,\ldots,N
labelling symmetries. These are given by

\displaystyle Q_{\alpha i}=\left(\begin{array}[]{rr}-q_{ai}\\
p_{a^{\prime}i}\end{array}\right)\ \ \ {\rm and}\ \ \ \bar{Q}_{\alpha i}=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}q_{ai}\\
p_{a^{\prime}i}\end{array}\right)\ .

By construction, these obey
Q_{\alpha i}Q_{\beta i}=\bar{Q}_{\alpha i}\bar{Q}_{\beta i}
which is the condition for anomaly cancellation.

As an example, consider
N=2
fermions coupled to a single rotor with charges
q_{i}
. The charges for fermions to the left and right of the rotor are

\displaystyle Q=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}-q_{1}&-q_{2}\\
q_{2}&-q_{1}\end{array}\right)\ \ \ \ {\rm and}\ \ \ \bar{Q}=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}q_{1}&q_{2}\\
q_{2}&-q_{1}\end{array}\right)\ .

This simple set-up includes the well-studied 3450 model, in which the two incoming fermions carry charge 5 and 0 under a
U(1)
symmetry, while the two outgoing fermions carry charge 3 and 4. This arises, for example, if we take
q_{i}=(1,2)
. In this case, the 3450 symmetry arises from the linear combination
-Q_{1i}+2Q_{2i}=(5,0)
and
-\bar{Q}_{1i}+2\bar{Q}_{2i}=(3,-4)
,

In the context of BCFT, the boundary state arising from left and right-moving fermions carrying charges specified by charges
Q
and
\bar{Q}
respectively were discussed in detail in
philip1
;
philip2
. (Some results were found previously in
costas
.) There it was shown that the right-moving and left-moving currents are related by the rotation matrix

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}_{ij}=(\bar{Q}^{-1})_{i\alpha}Q_{\alpha j}=\delta_{ij}-2\sum_{a}\frac{q_{ai}q_{aj}}{\sum_{k}q_{ak}^{2}}\ .

(2.43)

Here we demonstrate that the same result arises in the low-energy limit of the rotors.

To connect with the BCFT result we determine the boundary condition implemented by rotors which, for simplicity, we assume are all located at the origin (although we still require (
2.31
)). Solving for the equation of motion near the core gives

\displaystyle\psi_{i}(t,-r_{0})=\psi_{i}(t,r_{0})e^{i\sum_{a}q_{ai}\alpha_{a}(t)}\ .

(2.44)

It is convenient to perform Abelian bosonization (
2.24
). Then, repeating the steps that lead to (
2.18
), we have, at low energies,

\displaystyle\dot{\alpha}_{a}(t)\approx\frac{4\pi}{\sum_{i}q_{ai}^{2}}\sum_{i}q_{ai}\tilde{J}_{{\rm out},i}(t)=-\frac{2}{\sum_{i}q_{ai}^{2}}\sum_{i}q_{ai}\partial_{t}\tilde{\chi}_{i}(t)

(2.45)

where we’ve introduced the outgoing current

\displaystyle\tilde{J}_{{\rm out},i}=\lim_{r_{0}\rightarrow 0}\psi^{\dagger}_{i}\psi_{i}(t+r_{0},r_{0})=-\frac{1}{2\pi}\,\partial_{t}\tilde{\chi}_{i}(t)\ .

(2.46)

Integrating the equation of motion (
2.45
) and substituting into the boundary condition (
2.44
) we get, up to an arbitrary phase, the matching condition across the rotor

\displaystyle e^{i\chi_{i}}=e^{i\sum_{j}{\cal R}_{ij}\tilde{\chi}_{j}}

(2.47)

with the rotation matrix
{\cal R}_{ij}
given by (
2.43
). This same rotation matrix can be used to match currents across the rotor, in agreement with the BCFT results.

We define a twist operator
{\cal T}_{a}(t,x)
, for
x>0
, for each rotor

\displaystyle{\cal T}_{a}(t,x)=e^{i\alpha_{a}(t-x)}\ \ .

(2.48)

Again, this commutes with ingoing fermions,
\psi_{i}(t,y)
for
y<0
. For outgoing fermions, with
y>0
, the generalisation of (
2.23
) is the equal-time exchange relations

\displaystyle{\cal T}_{a}(t,x)\psi_{i}(t,y)=\psi_{i}(t,y){\cal T}_{a}(t,x)\ \ \

for
y>x

\displaystyle{\cal T}_{a}(t,x)\psi_{i}(t,y)=\exp\left(-\frac{4\pi iq_{ai}}{\sum_{k}q_{ak}^{2}}\right)\psi_{i}(t,y){\cal T}_{a}(t,x)\ \ \

\displaystyle\mbox{for\ $y<x$}\ .

(2.49)

For example, in the 3450 model, which consists of a single rotor with charges
q_{i}=(1,2)
, the twist operator obeys, for
0<y<x
,

\displaystyle{\cal T}(t,x)\psi_{1}(t,y)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle e^{-4\pi i/5}\psi_{1}(t,y){\cal T}(t,x)

\displaystyle{\rm and}\ \ \ \ {\cal T}(t,x)\psi_{2}(t,y)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle e^{-8\pi i/5}\psi_{2}(t,y){\cal T}(t,x)\ .

(2.50)

These are the twists identified in
me
.

Again, there is free propagation across the rotor. This time, an incoming fermion
\psi_{i}
will evolve to an outgoing fermion
(\prod_{a}{\cal T}_{a}^{q_{ai}})\psi_{i}
.

3
Correlation Functions

In this section, we calculate correlation functions in the more general class of fermion-rotor models (
2.28
). To keep things simple, we restrict to just a single rotor
\alpha(t)
, localised at the origin, coupled to
N
fermions with charges
q_{i}
(which is a shorthand for saying they have charges
-q_{i}/2
and
q_{i}/2
at
x<-r_{0}
and
x>r_{0}
, respectively). The extension to multiple rotors is straightforward. We work with the action

\displaystyle S=\int d^{2}x\ i\sum_{i=1}^{N}\psi_{i}^{\dagger}\left(\partial_{+}+iq_{i}\alpha(t)f^{\prime}(x)\right)\psi_{i}+\int dt\ \frac{I}{2}\dot{\alpha}^{2}\ .

(3.51)

We evaluate the Lorentzian partition function of this theory in Appendix
A
, where we show that, at low energies
E\ll 1/I
, fermionic correlation functions take the form

\displaystyle\Big<\prod_{j=1}^{n}\psi_{i_{j}}(t_{j},x_{j})\prod_{k=1}^{n^{\prime}}\psi^{\dagger}_{i^{\prime}_{k}}(t^{\prime}_{k},x^{\prime}_{k})\Big>=(\mbox{free correlators})\times{\cal Z}

(3.52)

with

\displaystyle{\cal Z}=\exp\left(\frac{2}{\sum_{i}q_{i}^{2}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{d\omega}{\omega}AB\right)\ .

(3.53)

Here the two functions
A
and
B
are given by

\displaystyle A

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{n}q_{i_{j}}\theta(x_{j})e^{-i\omega(t_{j}-x_{j})}-\sum_{k=1}^{n^{\prime}}q_{i^{\prime}_{k}}\theta(x^{\prime}_{k})e^{-i\omega(t^{\prime}_{k}-x^{\prime}_{k})}

\displaystyle B

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{n}q_{i_{j}}\theta(-x_{j})e^{+i\omega(t_{j}-x_{j})}-\sum_{k=1}^{n^{\prime}}q_{i^{\prime}_{k}}\theta(-x^{\prime}_{k})e^{+i\omega(t^{\prime}_{k}-x^{\prime}_{k})}\ .

(3.54)

The function
A
gets contributions from insertions to the right of the rotor, and the function
B
from insertions to the left. We will also want to compute correlation functions with rotor insertions. Typically, we will want these insertions to take place at either
x>0
or
x<0
. In the former case, we write

\displaystyle e^{iq\alpha(\tau)\theta(x)}=\exp\left(iq\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{d\omega}{2\pi}\theta(x)\left[\alpha(\omega)e^{-i\omega\tau}+\alpha(-\omega)e^{i\omega\tau}\right]\right)\ .

(3.55)

Following the derivation in the appendix (see, in particular, the discussion around (
A.115
)), we can accommodate a rotor insertion
e^{iq\alpha(\tau)\theta(x)}
at
x>0
by shifting

\displaystyle A\rightarrow A-q\theta(x)e^{-i\omega\tau}\ \ \ {\rm and}\ \ \ B\rightarrow B+q\theta(x)e^{i\omega\tau}\ .

(3.56)

Similarly, an insertion of
e^{-iq\alpha(\tau)\theta(-x)}
at
x<0
is implemented by the shift

\displaystyle A\rightarrow A+q\theta(-x)e^{-i\omega\tau}\ \ \ {\rm and}\ \ \ B\rightarrow B-q\theta(-x)e^{i\omega\tau}\ .

(3.57)

Either with or without rotors, we’re left with the integral (
3.53
).
The integral is UV convergent owing to rapid oscillations of the form
e^{-i\omega s}
at large
\omega\gg 1/|s|
,
while it may be divergent in the IR.
We regularise with an IR cutoff
\mu>0
as

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{d\omega}{\omega}\,e^{-i\omega s}\longrightarrow\int_{\mu}^{\infty}\frac{d\omega}{\omega}\,e^{-i\omega s}=-\gamma-\log(i\mu s)+\ldots

(3.58)

where the
\ldots
represent terms that vanish as
\mu\rightarrow 0
, and
s
should be understood as
s-i0^{+}
to deal with the branch cut of the logarithm. This regularisation is not without issue; we will return to this in Section
3.1
.

We can now use this prescription to compute some simple correlation functions. We start with two-point functions. The result (
3.52
) is proportional to the free correlator, which means that
\langle\psi_{i}(t,x)\psi_{j}(t^{\prime},x^{\prime})\rangle=0
. (There is actually a subtlety here that we discuss further in Section
3.1
.) To get a non-vanishing answer, we should look at
\langle\psi_{i}(t,x)\psi^{\dagger}_{j}(t^{\prime},x^{\prime})\rangle
, which will be proportional to
\delta_{ij}
.

First, as a sanity check to make sure that the general formalism is sensible, if
xx^{\prime}>0
, so that fermions are created and annihilated without passing through the rotor, we get either
A=0
or
B=0
and so the integral (
3.53
) is just
{\cal Z}=1
and

\displaystyle\langle\psi_{i}(t,x)\psi^{\dagger}_{j}(t^{\prime},x^{\prime})\rangle=\delta_{ij}G_{0}(t-t^{\prime},x-x^{\prime})\ .

(3.59)

with

\displaystyle G_{0}(t,x)=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\frac{t+x}{t^{2}-x^{2}-i0^{+}}

(3.60)

the propagator for a free chiral fermion. This is the expected result.

Things are more interesting when fermions are created and annihilated on different sides of the rotor. If
x>0
and
x^{\prime}<0
and then

\displaystyle A=q_{i}e^{-i\omega(t-x)}\ \ \ {\rm and}\ \ \ B=-q_{j}e^{i\omega(t^{\prime}-x^{\prime})}\ .

(3.61)

From (
3.53
), the IR-regulated integral gives
{\cal Z}
a contribution that scales as
\mu^{q_{i}q_{j}}
which, for
i=j
where the free correlators are non-zero, vanishes as
\mu\rightarrow 0
. Thus,

\displaystyle\langle\psi_{i}(t,x)\psi^{\dagger}_{j}(t^{\prime},x^{\prime})\rangle=0\ .

(3.62)

The correlator vanishes also if
x<0
and
x^{\prime}>0
. This means that if a fermion is created one side of the rotor, and annihilated on the other side, then the correlation function vanishes
joe
. In some sense, this result is unsurprising because the vanishing is imposed by the
U(1)
global symmetry (
2.5
). Nonetheless, it does lay bare the general scattering paradox: if you create a fermion to the left of the rotor, what comes out the other side?

The key observation of
lt
is that a non-vanishing two-point function arises for
xx^{\prime}<0
if we insert the rotor to balance
U(1)
charge at exactly the right moment. For example, we can consider
x>0
,
x^{\prime}<0
and evaluate

\displaystyle\langle\psi_{i}(t,x)e^{iq_{i}\alpha(t-x)}\psi^{\dagger}_{j}(t^{\prime},x^{\prime})\rangle\ .

(3.63)

It is important that the rotor is evaluated at the time
t-x
at which the fermion passed through the origin.
As shown in (
3.56
), the rotor insertion shifts
A
and
B
compared to (
3.61
) as
A\to A-q_{i}e^{-i\omega(t-x)}=0
and
B\to B+q_{i}e^{i\omega(t-x)}
. This results in the free propagator

\displaystyle\langle\psi_{i}(t,x)e^{iq_{i}\alpha(t-x)}\psi^{\dagger}_{j}(t^{\prime},x^{\prime})\rangle=\delta_{ij}G_{0}(t-t^{\prime},x-x^{\prime}).

(3.64)

We see that dressing with the rotor, which, as shown in Section
2
, acts a twist operator, the fermion propagates freely through the impurity.

We could also ask what happens if we evaluate the rotor at a time different from
\tau=t-x
. In this case, the integral (
3.53
) has a UV-divergence. This can be traced to the fact that we’re working in the low-energy limit
E\ll 1/I
. If we identify the UV cut-off with
1/I
, then the integral scales as
I^{2/N}
, reflecting the fact that such a correlation function requires the on-shell excitation of the rotor.

In general, we can define the operator

\displaystyle\mathcal{O}_{i}(t,x)=\,{\rm e}^{iq_{i}\alpha(t-x)\theta(x)}\psi_{i}(t,x)\ .

(3.65)

This operator transforms as
\mathcal{O}_{i}(t,x)\to\mathcal{O}_{i}(t,x)e^{-iq_{i}\beta/2}
under the
U(1)
symmetry (
2.5
). The operator looks non-local: the fermion operator
\psi_{i}(t,x)
is dressed with the rotor evaluated at the time
t-x
in the past when the two interacted. And yet, as we’ve seen, it is the operator
{\cal O}_{i}(t,x)
that is free, with two-point function
\langle{\cal O}_{i}(t,x)\,{\cal O}^{\dagger}_{j}(t^{\prime},x^{\prime})\rangle=\delta_{ij}G_{0}(t-t^{\prime},x-x^{\prime})
for any
x
and
x^{\prime}
. Moreover, there is a straightforward way to create the seemingly “non-local” state
{\cal O}_{i}^{\dagger}(t,x)|0\rangle
with
x>0
: just create the state
{\cal O}_{i}^{\dagger}(t-x,x)|0\rangle=\psi^{\dagger}_{i}(t-x,x)|0\rangle
for
x<0
and wait. In the language of
fock
;
me
;
me2
, these are the states in the twisted Fock space.

We can also introduce the related operator

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathcal{O}}_{i}(t,x)=\,{\rm e}^{-iq_{i}\alpha(t-x)\theta(-x)}\psi_{i}(t,x)

(3.66)

which transforms as
\tilde{\mathcal{O}}_{i}(t,x)\to\tilde{\mathcal{O}}_{i}(t,x)e^{+iq_{i}\beta/2}
under the
U(1)
symmetry (
2.5
). This too obeys
\langle\tilde{\cal O}^{\dagger}_{i}(t,x)\,\tilde{\cal O}_{j}(t^{\prime},x^{\prime})\rangle=\delta_{ij}G_{0}(t-t^{\prime},x-x^{\prime})
. Now, note that the operator is inserted for fermions
\psi(t,x)
inserted to the left of the rotor, and is evaluated at the time
t-x
in the future (because
x<0
). While we can certainly write down this operator, in contrast to
{\cal O}(t,x)
, it is difficult to see how we can construct the state
\tilde{\cal O}(t,x)|0\rangle
, with
x<0
, by a local experiment.

3.1
Cluster Decomposition

Things are more subtle than we’ve so far made out. We can highlight this by considering the case of a single
N=1
fermion with charge
q=1
interacting with the rotor. Here there’s no ambiguity in identifying the final state: the symmetry (
2.5
) means that, up to an overall phase, an ingoing fermion must scatter to

\displaystyle\psi\rightarrow\psi^{\dagger}\ .

(3.67)

This equation only makes sense if
\psi
at
x<0
scatters to
\psi^{\dagger}
at
x>0
; only then is the
U(1)
symmetry (
2.5
) obeyed. Namely, this
\psi^{\dagger}
has the same
U(1)
charge as the
\psi
, and as such it does not refer to the antiparticle of the
\psi
. However, the
†
does indicate that the final state carries an opposite fermion number to the initial state, which is an anomalous symmetry (
2.3
) due to the rotor so the fermion number is not conserved as the fermion pass through the rotor.

If we were to fold the system, so the rotor acts as a boundary rather than an impurity, then this is Andreev reflection, in which an electron reflects off a superconductor and returns as a hole. In this context, the physical electric charge is identified with the symmetry (
2.3
), which is anomalous in our model, but is spontaneously broken in a superconductor.

This scattering process means that the two-point function
\langle\psi(t,x)\psi(t^{\prime},x^{\prime})\rangle
across the rotor should be non-zero. Yet the calculations described above suggests that it vanishes. Clearly something is afoot.

In fact, the calculation of the two-point correlator
\langle\psi(t,x)\psi(t^{\prime},x^{\prime})\rangle
has two factors, as shown in (
3.52
). The first is the free propagator which manifestly vanishes. The second is the integral and this has an IR divergence as we remove the regulator. So the answer is
0\times\infty
. What to do?

We can get some insight into this by noting that a non-vanishing correlator
\langle\psi\psi\rangle
violates the anomalous fermion number symmetry (
2.3
). The anomaly equation (
2.4
) means that this must come about from an instanton-like configuration in which the rotor winds, with the change in fermion number
\Delta Q_{\psi}
given by

\displaystyle\Delta Q_{\psi}=\frac{N}{2\pi}\big(\alpha(t\rightarrow+\infty)-\alpha(t\rightarrow-\infty)\big)\ .

(3.68)

Scattering processes of this form, that violate the anomalous symmetry but do not, otherwise, involve anything strange like twisted sectors, are analogous to the Callan-Rubakov effect for fermion-monopole scattering that results in proton decay
c
;
r
.
The method that we described above works for correlators with
\Delta Q_{\psi}=0
.
As noted in
joe
, the reason that it does not work for
\Delta Q_{\psi}\neq 0
is our IR cutoff. Since this removes low frequency modes with
\omega<\mu
in
\alpha(t)
, it is not possible for
\alpha(t)
to asymptote to different values as
t\rightarrow\pm\infty
. Then, the anomaly relation above restricts us to consider only
\Delta Q_{\psi}=0
.
Here we want to generalise to correlators with
\Delta Q_{\psi}\neq 0
.

A simple way to proceed was suggested by Polchinski in
joe
. Consider an arbitrary operator
\Theta(t_{1},\ldots,t_{n},x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})
depending on
n
spacetime positions. We want to calculate
\langle\Theta\rangle
. If this operator carries non-zero charge under the anomalous symmetry then the method used above is ambiguous:
0\times\infty
. Instead we compute the correlator

\displaystyle\left\langle\Theta(t_{1},...,t_{n},x_{1},...,x_{n})\,\Theta^{\dagger}(t^{\prime}_{1},...,t^{\prime}_{n},x^{\prime}_{1},...,x^{\prime}_{n})\right\rangle\,,

(3.69)

This has vanishing anomalous charge and can be calculated faithfully using the methods above. We then take the limit
t_{i}\gg t^{\prime}_{j}
. Cluster decomposition requires that

\displaystyle\left\langle\Theta(t_{1},...,t_{n},x_{1},...,x_{n})\,\Theta^{\dagger}(t^{\prime}_{1},...,t^{\prime}_{n},x^{\prime}_{1},...,x^{\prime}_{n})\right\rangle

\displaystyle\longrightarrow

\displaystyle\big\langle\Theta(t_{1},...,t_{n},x_{1},...,x_{n})\big\rangle

(3.70)

\displaystyle\ \ \ \ \ \ \times\ \big\langle\Theta^{\dagger}(t^{\prime}_{1},...,t^{\prime}_{n},x^{\prime}_{1},...,x^{\prime}_{n})\big\rangle\,.

We now apply this cluster decomposition method to the
N=1
case of Andreev scattering. We’ll then see what light it has to shed on more general examples, and the role of the twist operator.

For a single
N=1
fermion, we want to calculate
\langle\psi(t_{1},x_{1})\psi(t_{2},x_{2})\rangle
with
x_{1}<0
and
x_{2}>0
. This violates the anomalous fermion number symmetry with
\Delta Q_{\psi}=2
. It is, however, straightforward to compute the four-point function

\displaystyle\Gamma_{N=1}(t_{i},x_{i};t^{\prime}_{i},x^{\prime}_{i})=\big\langle\psi(t_{1},x_{1})\psi(t_{2},x_{2})\left(\psi(t^{\prime}_{1},x^{\prime}_{1})\psi(t^{\prime}_{2},x^{\prime}_{2})\right)^{\dagger}\big\rangle

(3.71)

using the methods of the previous section. For
x_{1},x^{\prime}_{1}<0
and
x_{2},x^{\prime}_{2}>0
, it is given by

\displaystyle\Gamma_{N=1}(t_{i},x_{i};t^{\prime}_{i},x^{\prime}_{i})=\big(G_{11^{\prime}}G_{22^{\prime}}-G_{12^{\prime}}G_{21^{\prime}}\big)\times\left(\frac{s_{12^{\prime}}s_{1^{\prime}2}}{s_{12}s_{1^{\prime}2^{\prime}}}\right)^{2}\ .\ \ \ \

(3.72)

Here we define the lightcone coordinate
s_{i}=t_{i}-x_{i}
and
s_{ij}=s_{i}-s_{j}
. The first factor in (
3.72
) contains the free propagators

\displaystyle G_{ij^{\prime}}=G_{0}(t_{i}-t^{\prime}_{j},x_{i}-x_{j}^{\prime})=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\frac{1}{s_{ij^{\prime}}}\ .

(3.73)

The second factor in (
3.72
) comes from the integration in (
3.52
). Now we seperate the clusters by a large time
T
, so that
t_{i}-t^{\prime}_{i}\sim T
. The contribution from the integration scales as
T^{4}
. Each of the propagators scales as
G_{ij^{\prime}}\sim 1/T
, but the fermionic Wick contractions, resulting in the minus sign in the first bracket in (
3.72
), ensures that the combination of propagators scales as
1/T^{4}
. The upshot is that, in the limit
T\rightarrow\infty
, we have

\displaystyle\Gamma_{N=1}(t_{i},x_{;}t^{\prime}_{i},x^{\prime}_{i})\longrightarrow-\left(\frac{1}{2\pi i}\right)^{2}\frac{1}{s_{12}}\frac{1}{s_{1^{\prime}2^{\prime}}}\ .

(3.74)

This exhibits the desired cluster decomposition. We learn that the two-point correlator is given by

\displaystyle\langle\psi(t_{1},x_{1})\psi(t_{2},x_{2})\rangle=\frac{e^{i\theta}}{2\pi i}\frac{1}{s_{12}}\ .

(3.75)

Here
e^{i\theta}
is some overall phase that is undetermined by the analysis above. (Indeed, the possible boundary conditions that implement Andreev reflection also allow for such a phase.)

There is a similar story when we look at more general models, but now with an additional ingredient. To see this, consider the case of
N=2
fermions interacting with a single rotor with charges
q_{i}=(1,1)
. In this case there is an
SU(2)\times U(1)
symmetry; incoming fermions carry charges
{\bf 2}_{-1}
and outgoing fermions
{\bf 2}_{+1}
. We start by studying the outgoing fermion attached to a rotor degree of freedom,

\displaystyle\psi_{i}\rightarrow e^{i\alpha}\psi_{i}\ .

(3.76)

Unlike for
N>2
, there is no fractional power in the exponent
e^{i\alpha}
, ensuring that, for
N=2
, this does not involve a twist. This scattering is confirmed by the two-point correlator (
3.64
).
Following our analyses of the twist operator in the theory with
N=2
(
2.26
), we have an operator relation

\displaystyle e^{i\alpha(t-x)}\psi_{i}(t,x)=\epsilon_{ij}\psi_{j}^{\dagger}(t,x)\ ,

(3.77)

hence, there is no twisted mystery for this particular scattering process, and scattering corresponds to the well-known Callan-Rubakov process

\displaystyle\psi_{i}\rightarrow\epsilon_{ij}\psi^{\dagger}_{j}\ .

(3.78)

This is possible only for
N=2
because the representation
{\bf 2}
is pseudoreal. In general, an operator containing
N/2
coinciding rotor insertions can be represented as an excitation in the naive Fock space. This can be easily seen by noting that the twist operator (
2.25
) is raised to the power of
N/2
, so
\mathcal{T}^{N/2}
, represents a well-defined vertex operator that can be decomposed into fermionic excitations in the naive Fock space.

It is straightforward to use cluster decomposition to confirm the operator relation (
3.78
) when inserted in the two-point function
\langle\psi_{i}(t_{i},x_{j})\psi_{j}(t_{j},x_{i})\rangle
, and show that it is proportional to
\epsilon_{ij}
multiplied by the free propagator. Indeed, these are calculations previously performed in
joe
. Specifically, we will calculate
\langle\psi_{2}(t_{2},x_{2})\psi_{1}(t_{1},x_{1})\rangle
. As in the previous example, we do this by instead looking at the four-point function

\displaystyle\Gamma_{N=2}(t_{i},x_{i};t^{\prime}_{i},x^{\prime}_{i})=\big\langle\psi_{2}(t_{2},x_{2})\psi_{1}(t_{1},x_{1})\left(\psi_{2}(t^{\prime}_{2},x^{\prime}_{2})\psi_{1}(t^{\prime}_{1},x^{\prime}_{1})\right)^{\dagger}\big\rangle

(3.79)

which is easily calculated using the methods using the general result (
3.52
). For
x_{1},x^{\prime}_{1}<0
and
x_{2},x^{\prime}_{2}>0
, it is given by

\displaystyle\Gamma_{N=2}(t_{i},x_{i};t^{\prime}_{i},x^{\prime}_{i})=G_{11^{\prime}}G_{22^{\prime}}\frac{s_{21^{\prime}}s_{2^{\prime}1}}{s_{21}s_{2^{\prime}1^{\prime}}}

(3.80)

In the limit
t_{i}\gg t^{\prime}_{j}
, the correlation function reduces to

\displaystyle\Gamma_{N=2}(t_{i},x_{i};t^{\prime}_{i},x^{\prime}_{i})

\displaystyle\longrightarrow

\displaystyle\big\langle\psi_{2}(t_{2},x_{2})\psi_{1}(t_{1},x_{1})\big\rangle\big\langle\left(\psi_{2}(t^{\prime}_{2},x^{\prime}_{2})\psi_{1}(t^{\prime}_{1},x^{\prime}_{1})\right)^{\dagger}\big\rangle

(3.81)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{(2\pi i)^{2}}\frac{1}{s_{21}s_{2^{\prime}1^{\prime}}}\,.

This allows us to read off two separate correlators, up to an overall phase

\displaystyle\big\langle\psi_{2}(t_{2},x_{2})\psi_{1}(t_{1},x_{1})\big\rangle=\frac{e^{i\theta}}{2\pi i}\frac{1}{s_{21}}

(3.82)

and

\displaystyle\big\langle\psi^{\dagger}_{1}(t^{\prime}_{1},x^{\prime}_{1})\psi^{\dagger}_{2}(t^{\prime}_{2},x^{\prime}_{2})\big\rangle=-\frac{e^{-i\theta}}{2\pi i}\frac{1}{s_{2^{\prime}1^{\prime}}}\,.

(3.83)

Again, we have the possibility of an overall phase
e^{i\theta}
. (For a discussion of such phases in the context of boundary conditions for chiral fermions, see
philip1
.) The correlation function (
3.82
) is, again, that of a free fermion.

It is also interesting to compute a similar four-point function

4
A Mod 2 Anomaly

The simplest version of the fermion-rotor system (
2.1
), consisiting of
N
fermions each with charge
q_{i}=1
, captures the s-wave scattering of
N
Weyl fermions in
d=3+1
dimensions, interacting with an
SU(2)
’t Hooft-Polyakov monopole. That begs an interesting question. The 4d theory is only consistent when
N
is even; otherwise it suffers a Witten anomaly
wsu2
. It’s natural to wonder if perhaps there is some remnant of the Witten anomaly in the lower dimensional theory.
In this section, we confirm that this is indeed the case.

We will see this in two different ways, the first by considering the theory on a spatial line (as we have so far), and the second by considering the theory on a spatial circle. In the first approach, we show that cluster decomposition arguments imply that fermionic (i.e. Grassmann-odd) operators have expectation values. This is reminiscent of the Witten anomaly, where instantons in theories that suffer a Witten anomaly have an odd number of fermionic zero modes.

In the second method, on a spatial circle, we will show that the fermion-rotor system has an odd number of Majorana zero modes when there is a Witten anomaly. We discuss this in Section
4.1
, where we also give the more general statement for the model with an arbitrary number of rotors and arbitrary charges.

To illustrate why the theory with
N
odd is worrisome, we consider the simple case of
N=1
. We compute the correlator
\langle\psi(t,x)e^{i\alpha(\tau)/2}\,\psi^{\dagger}(t^{\prime},x^{\prime})e^{i\alpha(\tau^{\prime})/2}\rangle
for
x>0
and
x^{\prime}<0
(Note that both rotor insertions have
e^{+i\alpha/2}
, rather than
e^{\pm i\alpha/2}
). It is straightforward to compute this correlator using the methods of Section
3
. It is independent of the IR cut-off
\mu
but, in contrast to the correlation functions that we computed previously, it is UV divergent. This reflects the fact that
e^{i\alpha(\tau)/2}
is not playing the role of a twist operator here: instead it excites the rotor.

The UV divergence is an artefact of working with the integral (
3.53
) that is derived in the low-energy limit
E\ll 1/I
, with
I
the moment of inertia of the rotor. If we include the original kinetic term for the rotor, as in (
A.119
), then the integral is fully convergent. If we just care about the scaling, then we can mimic this by imposing a UV cut-off on the integral (
3.53
) given by
\Lambda=1/I
.

The correlator factorises under cluster decomposition. We scale the insertion times as
|t-t^{\prime}|\sim T
and
|\tau-\tau^{\prime}|\sim T
and take the limit
T\rightarrow\infty
to find, up to numerical factors,

\displaystyle\langle\psi(t,x)e^{i\alpha(\tau)/2}\,\psi^{\dagger}(t^{\prime},x^{\prime})e^{i\alpha(\tau^{\prime})/2}\rangle\,\sim\,\frac{I}{(s-\tau)(s^{\prime}-\tau^{\prime})}\ .

(4.94)

Cluster decomposition then tells us that

\displaystyle\langle\psi(t,x)e^{i\alpha(\tau)/2}\rangle\,\sim\,\frac{\sqrt{I}}{s-\tau}\ .

(4.95)

This is the promised expectation value for a Grassmann-odd field. It is the first sign that something is fishy. Similar behaviour occurs for all theories with
N
odd, but not for those with
N
even.

4.1
The Fermion-Rotor System on a Circle

We can get a more precise handle on the anomaly of this theory if we compactify on a spatial circle, with periodic boundary conditions on the fermion. Now the global
U(1)
symmetry (
2.5
) associated to the rotor disappears, with the asymtotic form of
f(x)
incompatible with periodic boundary conditions.

It’s unusual to lose a global symmetry when compactifying, but it’s familiar for the global, or asymptotic, part of a gauge symmetry which also disappears when you put the theory on compact spatial manifold. Indeed, in the reduction from the monopole, the
U(1)
symmetry (
2.5
) does descend from the global part of the
U(1)
gauge symmetry in
d=3+1
.

Now we can ask: what is the effect of the rotor on the low-energy spectrum of the theory? The answer follows immediately from our analysis in Section
2
where we showed that the rotor acts as a twist operator. The low-energy theory on a circle is simply free fermions, but with appropriately twisted boundary conditions.
In terms of bosonised fields
\chi_{i}
, these twisted boundary conditions on a circle of radius
R
are

\displaystyle\chi_{i}(x+2\pi R)={\cal R}_{ij}\chi_{i}(x)\

(4.96)

where the rotation matrix
{\cal R}_{ij}
was defined in (
2.43
). For the theory with
N
fermions of charge
q_{i}=1
, the rotation matrix is

\displaystyle{\cal R}_{ij}=\delta_{ij}-\frac{2}{N}\ .

(4.97)

We can now see what is wrong with the theory when
N
is odd. This is simplest when we have just a single
N=1
fermion. In this case, the twisted boundary condition becomes

\displaystyle\psi(x+2\pi R)=\psi^{\dagger}(x)\ .

(4.98)

If we decompose the fermion
\psi
in terms of Majoranas, and write
\psi=\lambda_{1}+i\lambda_{2}
, then
\lambda_{1}
has periodic boundary conditions while
\lambda_{2}
has anti-periodic boundary conditions. That means that
\lambda_{1}
has a zero mode on the circle, while
\lambda_{2}
does not. But a theory with a single (or, indeed, odd) number of Majorana zero modes is a notoriously subtle object. A naive computation of the Euclidean partition function gives
\sqrt{2}
, which is clearly does not carry the usual interpretation of the dimension of the Hilbert space. See
dgg2
;
w
;
seiberg
;
freed
for recent discussions of this simple, but confusing, system.

The fermion-rotor system with any odd
N
is plagued with the same issue. This follows from the work of
philip1
;
philip2
, where it was shown that the boundary states specified by the rotation matrix
{\cal R}_{ij}
fall into one of two classes that were termed
vector
and
axial
. For a system on an interval, the boundary conditions on each end must lie in the same class to avoid an odd number of fermion zero modes. For the unfolded, chiral theory on a circle, only the vector-like class will avoid an odd number of zero modes. It was shown in
philip1
that the boundary condition (
4.97
) lies in the vector class when
N
is even and the axial class when
N
is odd.

The upshot is that the Witten anomaly in
d=3+1
dimensions descends to the mod 2 anomaly
d=1+1
dimensions which simply counts the number of Majorana zero modes.

This same connection between the Witten anomaly and the mod 2 anomaly of Majorana modes has been previously noted in a closely related context
john
;
yuji
. These papers considered fermion bound states, rather than the chiral scattering states under discussion here. There it was also noted that monopoles in theories that suffer a Witten anomaly have an odd number of Majorana zero modes.

The same story applies to the more general class of models described in Section
2.2
. The symmetries of these models are captured in the rotation matrix
{\cal R}_{ij}
defined in (
2.43
), and a recipe was provided in
philip1
to determine the class (vector or axial) of any such
{\cal R}_{ij}
. The theory on a spatial circle has an even number of Majorana zero modes only when the
{\cal R}_{ij}
lies in the vector class.

As an example, we can consider the 3450 model, which means that there is a
U(1)
global symmetry under which the incoming fermions have charges 5 and 0, and the outgoing fermions have charges 3 and 4. There are different ways to construct this. One way, already mentioned in Section
2.2
, is to consider two fermions coupled to a single rotor with charges
q_{i}=(1,2)
. In the notation of (
2.2
), the 3450 symmetry arises from the linear combination
-Q_{1i}+2Q_{2i}=(5,0)
and
-\bar{Q}_{1i}+2\bar{Q}_{2i}=(3,-4)
, with the corresponding rotation matrix

\displaystyle{\cal R}=\frac{1}{5}\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}3&-4\\
-4&-3\end{array}\right)\ .

From the diagnostic presented in
philip1
, this lies in the axial class: this model has an odd number of Majorana zero modes. One can show using cluster decomposition that there is a non-vanishing Grassmann-odd expectation value
\langle\psi_{1}\psi_{2}\psi_{2}e^{5i\alpha/2}\rangle\neq 0
.

There is a straightforward way to construct a 3450 model with an even number of Majorana modes. We again take two fermions coupled to a single rotor, this time with
q_{i}=(1,3)
. The 3450 symmetry, no longer faithfully acting, now arises from the linear combination
-Q_{1i}+3Q_{2i}=(10,9)
and
-\bar{Q}_{1i}+3\bar{Q}_{2i}=(8,-6)
. This time the rotation matrix is

\displaystyle{\cal R}=\frac{1}{5}\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}4&-3\\
-3&-4\end{array}\right)\ .

From
philip1
, this lies in the vector class, and the theory on the circle has an even number of Majorana zero modes. Correspondingly, the operator that gets an expectation is the Grassmann-even
\langle\psi_{1}\psi_{3}\psi_{3}\psi_{3}e^{5i\alpha}\rangle\neq 0
.

Appendix A
Appendix: The Rotor Partition Function

Our goal in this appendix is to compute the Lorentzian partition function for the action (
3.51
) and reproduce the correlation functions given in (
3.52
) and (
3.53
). We start by introducing fermionic sources
\xi
and
\xi^{\dagger}
, so the action reads

\displaystyle S=\int d^{2}x\ i\sum_{i=1}^{N}\Big(\psi_{i}^{\dagger}{\cal D}_{i}\psi_{i}+\xi^{\dagger}_{i}\psi_{i}+{\psi}^{\dagger}_{i}\xi_{i}\Big)+\int dt\ \frac{I}{2}\dot{\alpha}^{2}\ .

(A.105)

with

\displaystyle{\cal D}_{i}=\partial_{+}+iq_{i}\alpha(t)f^{\prime}(x)\ .

(A.106)

The partition function is then

\displaystyle Z[\xi,\xi^{\dagger}]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int{\cal D}\psi{\cal D}\psi^{\dagger}{\cal D}\alpha\ e^{iS}

(A.107)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int{\cal D}\alpha\ \left(\prod_{i=1}^{N}{\rm det}\,{\cal D}_{i}\right)\,\exp\left(i\int dt\ \frac{I}{2}\dot{\alpha}^{2}\right)\,\exp\left(\sum_{i=1}^{N}\int d^{2}x\ \xi_{i}^{\dagger}{\cal D}_{i}^{-1}\xi_{i}\right)\ .

We need to do three things: compute the propagator
{\cal D}_{i}^{-1}
; compute the determinant
{\rm det}\,{\cal D}_{i}
; and integrate out the rotor degree of freedom
\alpha(t)
. We now do each of these in turn.

First the propagator. In the absence of the rotor, we have a free chiral fermion with usual propagator

\displaystyle G_{0}(t-t^{\prime},x-x^{\prime})=\langle t,x|\partial_{+}^{-1}|t^{\prime},x^{\prime}\rangle=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\frac{t-t^{\prime}+x-x^{\prime}}{(t-t^{\prime})^{2}-(x-x^{\prime})^{2}-i\epsilon}\ .

(A.108)

But it’s a simple matter to absorb the effect of the rotor into a phase shift of the propagator. We have

\displaystyle{\cal D}_{i}^{-1}=e^{-iq_{i}\lambda}\partial_{+}^{-1}e^{iq_{i}\lambda}

(A.109)

where

\displaystyle\lambda(t,x)=\partial_{+}^{-1}\alpha(t)f^{\prime}(x)=\int dt^{\prime}dx^{\prime}\ G_{0}(t-t^{\prime},x-x^{\prime})\,\alpha(t^{\prime})f^{\prime}(x^{\prime})\ .

(A.110)

In Fourier space, we have

\displaystyle\lambda(t,x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int dx^{\prime}\frac{d\omega}{2\pi}\frac{dk}{2\pi}\ e^{-i\omega t}\alpha(\omega)f^{\prime}(x^{\prime})\,\frac{-i(k+\omega)}{k^{2}-\omega^{2}-i\epsilon}e^{ik(x-x^{\prime})}

(A.111)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int dx^{\prime}\frac{d\omega}{2\pi}\ e^{-i\omega t}\alpha(\omega)f^{\prime}(x^{\prime})\big[\theta(x-x^{\prime})\theta(\omega)-\theta(x^{\prime}-x)\theta(-\omega)\big]e^{i\omega(x-x^{\prime})}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int dx^{\prime}\frac{d\omega}{2\pi}\ \theta\big(\omega(x-x^{\prime})\big){\rm sign}(\omega)\,e^{-i\omega(t-(x-x^{\prime}))}\alpha(\omega)f^{\prime}(x^{\prime})\ .

At this point, we take the small core limit,
f^{\prime}(x)\rightarrow\delta(x)
. We have the phase shift

\displaystyle\lambda(t,x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int\frac{d\omega}{2\pi}\ \theta(\omega x){\rm sign}(\omega)\,e^{-i\omega(t-x)}\alpha(\omega)

(A.112)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{d\omega}{2\pi}\ \Big[\theta(x)\alpha(\omega)e^{-i\omega(t-x)}-\theta(-x)\alpha(-\omega)e^{i\omega(t-x)}\Big]\ .

This is the first of our three results. The partition function becomes

\displaystyle{\cal Z}[\xi,\xi^{\dagger}]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int{\cal D}\alpha\,\left(\prod_{i=1}^{N}{\rm det}\,{\cal D}_{i}\right)\,\exp\left(i\int dt\ \frac{I}{2}\dot{\alpha}^{2}\right)

(A.113)

\displaystyle\ \ \ \times\,\exp\left(\sum_{i=1}^{N}\int d^{2}x\,d^{2}x^{\prime}\ \xi_{i}^{\dagger}(t,x)e^{-iq_{i}\lambda(t,x)}G_{0}(t-t^{\prime},x-x^{\prime})e^{iq_{i}\lambda(t^{\prime},x^{\prime})}\xi_{i}(t^{\prime},x^{\prime})\right)\ .

Differentiating with respect to the sources
\xi
and
\xi^{\dagger}
then gives us the intermediate expression for fermion correlators

\displaystyle\Big<\prod_{j=1}^{n}\psi_{i_{j}}(t_{j},x_{j})\prod_{k=1}^{n^{\prime}}\psi^{\dagger}_{i^{\prime}_{k}}(t^{\prime}_{k},x^{\prime}_{k})\Big>=(\mbox{free correlators})\times{\cal Z}

(A.114)

with

\displaystyle{\cal Z}=\int{\cal D}\alpha\ \left(\prod_{i=1}^{N}{\rm det}\,{\cal D}_{i}\right)\exp\left(i\int dt\ \frac{I}{2}\dot{\alpha}^{2}\right)\exp\left(-i\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{d\omega}{2\pi}\ \big(A\,\alpha(\omega)-B\,\alpha(-\omega)\big)\right)\ .

Here the two functions
A
and
B
can be extracted from the expression (
A.112
) for
\lambda
and are given by

\displaystyle A

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{n}q_{i_{j}}\theta(x_{j})e^{-i\omega(t_{j}-x_{j})}-\sum_{k=1}^{n^{\prime}}q_{i^{\prime}_{k}}\theta(x^{\prime}_{k})e^{-i\omega(t^{\prime}_{k}-x^{\prime}_{k})}

\displaystyle B

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{n}q_{i_{j}}\theta(-x_{j})e^{+i\omega(t_{j}-x_{j})}-\sum_{k=1}^{n^{\prime}}q_{i^{\prime}_{k}}\theta(-x^{\prime}_{k})e^{+i\omega(t^{\prime}_{k}-x^{\prime}_{k})}\ .

(A.115)

This coincides with the expression (
3.54
) given in the main text.

Next, we turn to the determinant. For the operator
{\cal D}=\partial_{+}+iq\alpha(t)f^{\prime}(x)

\displaystyle{\rm det}\,{\cal D}=\exp\log{\rm det}\,{\cal D}=\exp{\rm Tr}\log\left[\partial_{+}(1+i\lambda(t,x))\right]

(A.116)

with
\lambda(t,x)
defined in (
A.110

\lambda

and, although it is not obvious from the expansion of the log, this is the exact result. This can be seen in a diagrammatic approach. (Alternatively, see the footnote below Eq. (41) of

\displaystyle{\rm det}\,{\cal D}=\exp\left(\frac{q^{2}}{2}\int d^{2}x\,d^{2}x^{\prime}\ \alpha(t)\alpha(t^{\prime})f^{\prime}(x)f^{\prime}(x^{\prime})\,G_{0}^{2}(t-t^{\prime},x-x^{\prime})\right)\ .

(A.117)

We evaluate this in Fourier space. After taking the small core limit,

\displaystyle{\rm det}\,{\cal D}=\exp\left(-\frac{q^{2}}{4\pi}\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{d\omega}{2\pi}\ \omega\alpha(\omega)\,\alpha(-\omega)\right)\ .

(A.118)

Crucially, this term is linear in

\displaystyle{\cal Z}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int{\cal D}\alpha\ \exp\left(-\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{d\omega}{2\pi}\left(\frac{\sum_{i}q_{i}^{2}}{4\pi}\omega-iI\omega^{2}\right)\alpha(\omega)\,\alpha(-\omega)\right)

\displaystyle\ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \times\,\exp\left(-i\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{d\omega}{2\pi}\ \big(A\,\alpha(\omega)-B\,\alpha(-\omega)\big)\right)

We’re left with a partition function that is quadratic in the rotor degree of freedom

\displaystyle{\cal Z}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\exp\left(2\int_{0}^{\infty}d\omega\ \frac{AB}{(\sum_{i}q_{i}^{2})\omega-4\pi iI\omega^{2}}\right).

\displaystyle{\cal Z}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\exp\left(\frac{2}{\sum_{i}q_{i}^{2}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{d\omega}{\omega}\ AB\right).

This is the result advertised in (
