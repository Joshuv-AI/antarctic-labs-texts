Prevalence of and factors associated with obesity during pregnancy in Ireland 2013–2022: a retrospective observational study from a large tertiary maternity hospital
Authors: Ellen Juliet Cosgrave, Mark Germaine, Peter Naughton, Margaret Mary Brennan, Patricia M Kearney, Graham Healy, Brendan Egan, Michael J Turner, Claire Buckley, Amy C O’Higgins
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionThis study describes the prevalence of and factors associated with obesity during pregnancy (body mass index (BMI) ≥30.0 kg/m²) among women attending a tertiary maternity hospital in Dublin, Ireland, where 1 in 8 deliveries occur nationally.MethodsThe study involved secondary analysis of electronic health records from The Coombe Hospital from 2013 to 2022. BMI was calculated using objective measurement of maternal height and weight in early pregnancy. Descriptive estimates of obesity prevalence during pregnancy were calculated by year. Linear mixed effects regression was used to examine factors associated with BMI during pregnancy.ResultsThere were 74 233 pregnancies. Mean BMI was 25.5 kg/m² in 2013 and 26.5 kg/m² in 2022. Obesity prevalence was 16.3% (95% CI 15.5% to 17.1%) in 2013 and 22.3% (95% CI 21.3% to 23.4%) in 2022. Prevalence of BMI ≥40.0 kg/m² was 1.6% (95% CI 1.3% to 1.9%) in 2013 and 2.4% (95% CI 2.0% to 2.8%) in 2022. Prevalence of normal-range BMI (18.5–24.9 kg/m2) was 52.9% (95% CI 51.8% to 54.0%) in 2013 and 45.3% (95% CI 44.1% to 46.5%) in 2022. Obesity prevalence was higher in 2022 than in 2013 across all age groups, with larger differences among those aged 20–24 years. Elevated BMI was associated with maternal age, parity, ethnicity, lower socioeconomic status, unplanned pregnancy, psychological problems and smoking.ConclusionThis study provides up-to-date descriptive estimates of obesity in early pregnancy in Ireland, allowing international comparisons. The higher obesity prevalence observed in 2022 than in 2013 across all age groups has important public health implications. Findings will support healthcare planning and inform universal and targeted public health strategies.

Introduction

Obesity, defined as body mass index (BMI) ≥30.0 kg/m², is an escalating public health concern, especially in high-income countries.1 In line with broader population trends, increases in prevalence of obesity during pregnancy have been reported globally. The global prevalence of obesity during pregnancy was estimated at 20.9% in 2024 and is projected to rise to 23.3% by 2030.2 The highest prevalence is observed in North America and the lowest prevalence in Asia, while recent studies indicate that approximately one in four pregnant women in Europe experience obesity.3 Obesity is also inversely associated with socioeconomic status, whereby individuals from lower socioeconomic groups experience disproportionately higher prevalence of obesity.4 In addition, BMI may increase during pregnancy due to a combination of physiological and behavioural factors, including growth of the fetus and placenta, expansion of maternal blood volume, hormonal and metabolic shifts and altered physical activity and dietary patterns.5

Obesity during pregnancy is associated with adverse maternal and neonatal outcomes, both short- and long-term, in addition to increased healthcare interventions during pregnancy, and resultant increases in healthcare costs.6–10 In particular, obesity is associated with a 3–6 fold increased risk of developing gestational diabetes mellitus, which in turn is associated with up to a 60% lifetime risk of developing type 2 diabetes mellitus.11–13 The intrauterine environment also influences longer-term child health, with metabolic, inflammatory and epigenetic changes induced by obesity during pregnancy associated with higher risk of later-life obesity, type 2 diabetes and cardiovascular disease for children.1415

Pregnancy represents a ‘teachable moment’ to influence health behaviours and improve family health.16 Given high healthcare engagement during this period, pregnancy represents an opportune data capture point to examine broader population health trends; large scale, objective BMI measurement can serve as a proxy indicator of BMI patterns in the wider population.

There is limited up-to-date, objectively measured data on prevalence of and factors associated with obesity during pregnancy in Ireland.17 Such evidence is essential to identify high-risk subpopulations, support effective population-level interventions and enable international comparisons. This study aims to address this gap by examining the following research questions: (1) What were the annual prevalence estimates of obesity during pregnancy in a large tertiary maternity hospital in Dublin, Ireland from 2013 to 2022 and (2) what sociodemographic and lifestyle factors are associated with obesity during pregnancy in this population?

Materials and methods

Study design and setting

This retrospective observational study involved secondary analysis of electronic health records of women attending The Coombe Hospital in Dublin, Ireland from 2013 to 2022.17 Descriptive analysis of prevalence of obesity during pregnancy was conducted. Factors associated with obesity during pregnancy were also assessed. Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines were followed.18

Study population

All those who attended for their first antenatal visit at The Coombe Hospital from 2013 to 2022 and subsequently delivered a baby weighing ≥500 g, with complete objectively measured height and weight data were included. ‘Late bookers’, defined as those who attended their first antenatal appointment at or after 16+0 weeks gestation, were excluded from the analysis. The threshold of ≥500 g was determined by hospital policy and reflected the standard in use at the time of data collection, consistent with definitions adopted by international bodies.19 Online supplemental table 3 provides a comparison of age, parity and ethnicity between women with missing BMI data and those included in the final analysis.

The Coombe Hospital is one of the largest maternity hospitals in Europe and cares for a broad and diverse population, including women from all socioeconomic backgrounds, urban and rural areas, and both public and private healthcare patients.2021 As a tertiary level maternity hospital, it manages both low and high-risk pregnancies. In Ireland, over 99% of births occur in hospitals.22 Although this is a single-centre study, approximately one in eight births in Ireland occur at The Coombe Hospital, and its patient demographics closely mirror national trends reported in the Irish National Perinatal Reports.23–25 For example, in 2022 the mean maternal age nationally was 32.7 years versus 32.8 years in this study; 8.8% of women in this study were aged ≥40 years compared with 9.0% nationally; parity distributions were similar (41.6% nulliparous in this study vs 39.9% nationally); the proportion of multiple pregnancies aligned closely (3.6% in this study vs 3.5% nationally) and although not directly comparable, the proportion of women of white European ethnicity in this study (85.5%) was similar to the proportion of births nationally to women of European nationality (86.8%). Demographic shifts over time were also consistent with national trends.23

Variables

Data were routinely computerised by trained midwives using a standardised data collection system as part of the medical records at the first antenatal visit. BMI was calculated based on objective measurement of weight and height, not based on self-reporting, and categorised into WHO classifications (see online supplemental table 1).26

Other variables based on self-report during the first antenatal visit included year, age, parity, pregnancy order (singleton/multiple), maternal occupation (a proxy for socioeconomic status categorised into five ordinal skill levels per the International Standard Classification of Occupations (ISCO) classification system27: (level 0=unemployed, level 1=elementary occupations, level 2=clerical support workers, service and sales workers, level 3=technicians and associate technicians, level 4=professional and managers)), ethnicity, pregnancy intention, presence of psychological problems (anxiety and/or depression) and smoking status (see online supplemental table 2). Gestational age at booking was determined using ultrasound dating and was converted to decimal weeks for analysis.

Data management

At the first antenatal visit, patients were routinely asked to consent to the use of their anonymised data for research and audit by responding to the standard statement indicating they understood their data may be used for these purposes. An anonymised data file was provided securely to the research team who analysed the data by the hospital information technology team.

Statistical analysis

Anonymised data were analysed using IBM SPSS Statistics V.29.0 (IBM Corp) and R statistical software (V.4.4.0, R Core Team, 2024).2829 Descriptive analyses summarised the study population by year. Prevalence of obesity during pregnancy was estimated for the total sample and stratified by key characteristics by year. Prevalence of obesity during pregnancy in 2013 and 2022 was compared descriptively. To account for repeat pregnancies within individuals over the 10-year period, a multivariable linear mixed effects model was used with a woman-level dummy variable as a random effect. This identified factors independently associated with BMI during pregnancy reported as β coefficients, 95% CIs and p values. Covariates were selected a priori based on clinical relevance and previous literature.

Patient and public involvement

Patients or the public were not involved in the design, conduct, reporting or dissemination plans of our research.

Results

Demographic information

BMI data were available for 74 233 pregnancies among 51 530 women. Those with no data on height or weight were excluded from the final analyses (n=991, 1.3%). Characteristics of missing observations are included in online supplemental table 3.

Obesity prevalence

Prevalence of obesity (BMI ≥30 kg/m2) during pregnancy was 16.3% (95% CI 15.5% to 17.1%) in 2013 and 22.3% (95% CI 21.3% to 23.4%) in 2022. Mean BMI was 25.5±5.1 kg/m2 in 2013 and 26.5±4.4 kg/m2 in 2022. Prevalence of Class III obesity (BMI ≥40.0 kg/m2) was 1.6% (95% CI 1.3% to 1.9%) in 2013 and 2.4% (95% CI 2.0% to 2.8%) in 2022, while normal BMI (18.5–24.9 kg/m2) was 52.9% (95% CI 51.8% to 54.0%) in 2013 and 45.3% (95% CI 44.1% to 46.5%) in 2022 (figure 1).

Distribution of body mass index (BMI) categories over time, 2013–2022 (n=74 233).

Obesity prevalence was higher in 2022 compared with 2013 across all age groups, with larger differences among those aged 20–24, 30–34 and 35–39 years (table 2). Differences were smaller among individuals aged ≥40 years. The difference in prevalence between 2013 and 2022 was greater among nulliparous than multiparous women and among singleton compared with multiple pregnancies. Higher prevalence in 2022 compared with 2013 was observed across all occupational skill levels, with larger differences in skill levels 1, 2 and 3. Higher prevalence in 2022 compared with 2013 was observed among those of White European, Asian and Other ethnicities. Higher prevalence in 2022 compared with 2013 was observed among both planned and unplanned pregnancies, and pregnancies following infertility treatment. Discussion

Key findings

This study assessed the prevalence of obesity during pregnancy (BMI ≥30.0 kg/m²) among women attending a large tertiary maternity hospital in Dublin between 2013 and 2022. It provides up-to-date, objectively measured data demonstrating higher obesity prevalence in 2022 compared with 2013 and a lower prevalence of normal BMI, with only 45.3% of pregnancies in the normal range by 2022. Mean BMI was also higher in 2022 compared with 2013. Higher obesity prevalence at this centre is consistent with broader national findings as nearly 60% of adults in Ireland now live with overweight or obesity.30 These patterns are driven by established factors such as dietary changes, reduced physical activity and socioeconomic inequalities, which are also recognised internationally.31 Shifts in maternal demographics, including in particular increasing maternal age are also likely to contribute to higher BMI among pregnant women.32 These findings represent descriptive comparisons between time points and should not be interpreted as evidence of formally modelled temporal trends. Nevertheless, they provide contemporary estimates of obesity prevalence during pregnancy and may inform future studies designed to formally assess temporal change using regression-based approaches.

Earlier studies from the same tertiary hospital provide useful context for the prevalence estimates reported in the present study. McKeating et al reported obesity prevalence of 16.8% between 2009 and 2013.33 Reynolds et al subsequently reported an 18.1% increase in obesity prevalence between 2010 and 2017, in addition to associations between obesity and maternal age, multiparity, depression, unemployment and unplanned pregnancy.17 Direct comparison should be interpreted with caution, as the present study reports descriptive prevalence estimates whereas previous studies differ in analytical approaches. International studies have also reported a substantial burden of obesity during pregnancy, with recent European evidence suggesting that approximately one in four pregnant women experience obesity.2334 Overall, these findings highlight the urgent need for strategies to address obesity during pregnancy.

Increasing obesity levels are associated with increased maternal and neonatal complications in addition to increased longer term maternal morbidity including earlier incidence and rising prevalence of metabolic and cardiovascular morbidity.9 In particular, higher obesity rates will likely result in an increase in the number of patients screened for and diagnosed with gestational diabetes mellitus,35 which will confer increased healthcare costs.3637 Meanwhile, intergenerational effects may lead to increased risk of obesity and cardiovascular disease in offspring,38 with wider implications for population health.

Class III obesity prevalence was higher in 2022 compared with 2013. Previous research from the same hospital also highlighted the burden of Class III obesity during pregnancy.33 However, direct comparisons should be interpreted with caution due to differences in study periods and analytical approaches. These findings are consistent with broader European findings where Class III obesity ranged from 0.4% to 2.9% based on 34 studies conducted between 2006 and 2021.2 In the present study higher Class III obesity prevalence in 2022 compared with 2013 was observed across nearly all subgroups, including all age groups except those aged ≥40 years, both nulliparous and multiparous women, all skill levels and across pregnancies that were planned, unplanned or occurred secondary to infertility treatment. This finding is particularly clinically and economically significant as Class III obesity is associated with the highest risk of pregnancy complications, interventional deliveries and other peri-partum interventions, contributing to increased maternal and neonatal morbidity and greater healthcare costs.739

This study also identifies factors associated with obesity during pregnancy. Observed obesity prevalence was higher in 2022 compared with 2013 across all age groups, and although older and multiparous women had higher absolute rates of obesity, larger differences were observed among younger women (<25 years) and nulliparous women. Although, compared with those aged 25–29 years women aged ≥30 years consistently had higher BMI across the study period, these differences in obesity prevalence were smaller in 2022; notably, younger age (<25 years). This is consistent with international findings demonstrating that higher prevalence of obesity during pregnancy has been observed across all reproductive ages,40 not only among older mothers and suggests that obesity during pregnancy now affects the full reproductive age population in Ireland. These findings have significant implications for health service demand, particularly as a high proportion of those who are pregnant were diagnosed early in their reproductive life.17

Congruent with findings by Reynolds et al in the same hospital,17 our study also identified that multiparity was associated with obesity, indicating a particular need for interventions to support maintaining a healthy weight in the postpartum period. Such findings should be used to inform health promotion initiatives in the postpartum period to combat obesity among women of reproductive age.

Consistent with international findings, obesity during pregnancy was also strongly patterned by socioeconomic status.4 Lower skill levels were linked to higher BMI and while higher obesity prevalence in 2022 compared with 2013 was observed across all skill levels, the largest differences in obesity prevalence were seen among middle skill levels, which may suggest widening health disparities for all but the most affluent. Similar patterns have been reported in other high-income countries,32 where maternal obesity disproportionately affects women from lower socioeconomic backgrounds, contributing to persistent inequalities in pregnancy outcomes and long-term health and reinforcing the need for equitable strategies and interventions.

Consistent with international evidence, ethnicity-related disparities in obesity prevalence were also evident.41 Women of Afro-Caribbean ethnicity had significantly higher BMI than those of White European ethnicity, while those of Asian ethnicity had significantly lower BMI. These findings may be influenced by complex interactions between underlying cultural, dietary, structural and healthcare access factors and highlight the need for culturally appropriate public health strategies. In addition, the study population is multi-ethnic and evidence shows that BMI, adiposity and related health risks differ across ethnicities, indicating how a single BMI threshold may not accurately identify risk,42 underscoring the importance of ethnicity-specific BMI cut-offs to support appropriate clinical surveillance and care.

In addition, although BMI is widely used in antenatal care and consistently associated with adverse maternal and neonatal outcomes and increased healthcare utilisation, BMI thresholds used in pregnancy are not adjusted for physiological gestational changes and existing guidelines rely on pre-pregnancy BMI cut-offs, highlighting the need for further research on pregnancy-specific adiposity measures to enable more precise identification of women at increased risk.43

Similar to previous findings, unplanned pregnancy was associated with higher BMI,17 suggesting fewer opportunities to optimise health pre-conception, underscoring the need for health promotion regardless of pregnancy intention. There was a higher prevalence of infertility treatment-related pregnancies in 2022 compared with 2013, suggesting evolving dynamics that warrant further investigation. The presence of psychological problems was associated with higher BMI aligning with previous research in the Irish context and internationally.4445 Furthermore, both past and current smoking were also associated with higher BMI, reinforcing the importance of integrated mental and behavioural health supports in reproductive care.

Findings highlight the urgent need for population-level interventions targeting women of reproductive age, particularly pre-conceptually, to support healthy weight and improve long-term maternal and child health. Long-term obesity reduction is difficult to achieve, with lifestyle interventions typically offering only short-term improvements and no country to date managing to reverse rising national obesity rates.46 However, existing evidence highlights the value of holistic, multi-component approaches, underscoring the need for more coordinated cross-disciplinary research to advance development and implementation of effective interventions to combat obesity during pregnancy and resulting consequences for both women and babies.4748 This is particularly relevant as obesity often begins before pregnancy and contributes to intergenerational and intrauterine effects.1415 Encouragingly, however, past initiatives have shown positive outcomes, reinforcing the effectiveness of proactive, preconception-focused strategies.4950 A combined approach involving universal and tailored interventions, including preconception and inter-pregnancy care, can leverage pregnancy as a critical window to promote lifelong healthy behaviours.

Strengths and limitations

Study strengths include the large sample size and use of routinely collected, objectively measured data. Standardised height and weight measurements by trained midwives minimised errors, enhancing reliability. Limitations include the use of hospital-based rather than population-based data, which may affect national representativeness, though previous analyses support the hospital’s representativeness and over 99% of births occur in hospitals in Ireland.22 Excluding participants with missing data, who likely presented for antenatal care after the first trimester or were in utero transfers to this tertiary referral hospital, may reduce internal validity although we are reassured that the proportion of missing data was low (1.3%). Small numbers in some ethnic minority groups may limit generalisability. Using ISCO skill level as the sole proxy for socioeconomic status, without markers such as maternal education or deprivation, may limit identification of high-risk groups. The cross-sectional nature of association limits the ability to determine causality. Finally, although prevalence estimates were reported annually between 2013 and 2022, the study was not designed to formally assess temporal trends. Consequently, differences observed between years should be interpreted as descriptive comparisons rather than statistically demonstrated changes over time.

Despite these limitations, this study provides robust, up-to-date estimates of the prevalence of and factors associated with obesity during pregnancy in Ireland and offers a valuable basis for international comparison and future policy development.

Conclusion

This study presents updated descriptive estimates of obesity during pregnancy in Ireland, using repeated cross-sectional data that enable comparison with patterns in other international pregnancy populations. Obesity prevalence was higher in 2022 compared with 2013, with differences observed across socioeconomic groups. As this study did not formally model temporal trends, these findings should be interpreted as descriptive prevalence estimates rather than evidence of a statistically demonstrated increase over time. Higher prevalence across all age groups in 2022 compared with 2013 is likely to increase maternal and infant morbidity, place additional strain on healthcare services and impact long-term population health. The socioeconomic burden underscores the need for equitable, needs-based service planning to address health inequities. In addition, the study identifies key demographic, socioeconomic and lifestyle factors associated with higher BMI during pregnancy, including maternal age, parity, ethnicity, socioeconomic status, pregnancy intention and smoking.

These findings call for urgent action from policymakers and healthcare providers. Innovative policies and both universal and targeted preventative and therapeutic interventions are needed to reduce obesity prevalence and guide resource allocation based on population needs. Future research could explore cross-European comparisons to deepen understanding of regional patterns and inform international best practices.
