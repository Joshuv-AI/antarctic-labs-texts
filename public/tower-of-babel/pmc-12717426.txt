Integrative analysis of pancreatic microbiota and metabolome in patients with pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma
Authors: Yiqing Zhu, Xiao Liang, Feng Li, Jianwei Xu, Changxu Chen, Haoyun Tang, Yifan Wang, Lixiang Li, Luping Xing, Yanyan Dong, Zhen Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe importance of microbiota-associated metabolites in pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC) tumorigenesis highlights the need to investigate the microbiota and metabolome along the normal-adjacent-carcinoma sequence to explore their mechanistic implications in PDAC tumorigenesis. We characterized the pancreatic microbiota and metabolome in four distinct tissue groups using 16S rRNA amplicon sequencing and untargeted metabolomics: tumor tissues from 23 PDAC patients (PDAC T), their matched para-carcinoma tissues (PCT), tumor tissues from 13 pancreatic benign tumor patients (Control T), and their matched non-tumor tissues (Control N). For the selected metabolites, we verified their functional activities using cell proliferation assay. From Control N, through PCT, to PDAC T, there were 90 significantly differential genera, of which seven genera increased and 65 genera decreased sequentially. Specifically, Peptostreptococcus, norank_f__Mitochondria, and Ileibacterium increased, while Faecalibacterium, Ruminococcus, Subdoligranulum, and Coprococcus decreased sequentially. Among altered metabolites, beta-guanidinopropionic acid (GPA), S-(2-Carboxyethyl)-L-cysteine, L-lactic acid, and 3-hydroxybutanoic acid increased, while (3Z)-2-Propylpent-3-enoic acid, linoleic acid, and monopalmitin decreased sequentially. PDAC-associated metabolites were enriched in the GABAergic synapse, butanoate metabolism, purine metabolism, and CCM in cancer pathways. GPA and S-(2-Carboxyethyl)-L-cysteine were also enriched in the chemotherapy-resistant patients compared with chemotherapy-sensitive patients, while Faecalibacterium and (3Z)-2-Propylpent-3-enoic acid were also reduced. In both PDAC tumorigenesis and chemotherapy-resistant patients, decreased Faecalibacterium was positively correlated with (3Z)-2-Propylpent-3-enoic acid, but negatively correlated with GPA and S-(2-carboxyethyl)-L-cysteine. CCK8 assays confirmed that GPA and S-(2-carboxyethyl)-L-cysteine promoted the proliferation of PDAC cell lines. GPA and S-(2-Carboxyethyl)-L-cysteine were enriched in both PDAC tumorigenesis and chemotherapy-resistant patients, while Faecalibacterium and (3Z)-2-Propylpent-3-enoic acid were reduced. Introduction

Pancreatic cancer, mainly pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC), is a leading cause of cancer death worldwide1. In recent years, the incidence of PDAC has been rapidly increasing2. Early diagnosis of PDAC remains challenging due to the lack of effective screening, and only 10–20% of PDAC patients are diagnosed with localized, surgically resectable disease3

The pancreatic microbiota has received increasing attention as a component of the tumor microenvironment (TME) that affects PDAC occurrence and development through multiple pathways4. The specific mechanisms involve metabolic influence, immune regulation via the system-microbiota-tumor axis, and inflammation promotion5. Meanwhile, metabolic remodeling is considered an important hallmark of cancer6, and the microbiome can disrupt metabolite balance, promoting tumorigenesis7. For example, the microbiota-derived tryptophan metabolite indole-3-acetic acid (3-IAA) can improve chemotherapy efficacy in PDAC8, and Bifidobacterium can ferment glucose to produce the short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs), including butyrate, propionate, and acetate, which support intestinal health and inhibit tumor cell growth9.

Para-carcinoma tissues (PCTs) are usually regarded as controls in tumor-related studies. Yet, PCT may be a transitional state between healthy and tumor tissue, potentially accumulating oncogenic events10. Previous studies have found that PCTs in many cancers have accumulated somatic mutations that drive clonal expansion and malignant transformation11. While some studies have compared the microbiome and metabolome between tumors and PCT in PDAC12, the sequential changes in the pancreatic microbiome and metabolome from genuine normal pancreas, through PCT, to tumor remain uncharacterized.

Given the potential roles of microbiota and metabolites in PDAC pathogenesis, in this exploratory study, we investigated the alterations in microbiome and metabolome from normal pancreatic tissues, to PCTs, and ultimately to PDAC by 16S ribosomal RNA (16S rRNA) amplicon sequencing and untargeted metabolomics. Our aim was to uncover the microbial and metabolic traits related to PDAC development, including the extremely early changes, providing insights for future therapeutic strategies.

Methods

Subjects and samples

We performed an observational study of 23 patients with PDAC and 13 patients with benign pancreatic tumors from January 2022 to December 2022 at the Qilu Hospital of Shandong University. Tumor and matched non-tumor tissue samples were collected from all patients during surgical resection. The PDAC tissues were classified as the PDAC T, their matched para-carcinoma tissues as the PCT, the pancreatic benign tumor tissues as Control T, and their matched non-tumor tissues as the normal pancreatic tissues (Control N). All PDAC patients were diagnosed by postoperative pathology. The PCTs were strictly defined as tissues obtained 1–3 cm from the tumor margin and confirmed to be free of carcinoma cells by pathological assessment. The exclusion criteria were as follows: (I) comorbidity with other cancers; (II) underwent preoperative chemotherapy, radiotherapy, or other biological treatment; and (III) use of routine antibiotics, probiotics, prebiotics, or synbiotics within the previous month. Patients who received postoperative adjuvant chemotherapy were categorized based on systematic radiographic follow-up. The chemotherapy-resistant group was defined as patients with radiographically confirmed disease progression (Response Evaluation Criteria in Solid Tumors [RECIST] version 1.113) during or within 6 months of completing adjuvant chemotherapy. Disease progression was characterized by the appearance of new lesions, indicative of local recurrence or distant metastasis. Conversely, the chemotherapy-sensitive group included patients with no evidence of disease recurrence on imaging for at least 6 months after completing chemotherapy. The research was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Qilu Hospital of Shandong University (KYLL-202107–097). Informed written consent was obtained from each patient before enrollment.

We implemented a stringent aseptic protocol during tissue acquisition: immediately after resection, the pancreatic surface was thoroughly cauterized with a sterile electrocautery blade to eliminate potential contaminants, followed by collection of core parenchymal tissue using fresh, sterile instruments for each patient. This approach, combined with immediate snap-freezing of samples, substantially reduces the risk of exogenous microbial introduction.

DNA extraction, 16S ribosomal RNA gene sequencing, and data analysis

According to the manufacturer’s instructions, total microbial genomic DNA was extracted from pancreatic samples using the E.Z.N.A.® soil DNA Kit (Omega Bio-tek, Norcross, GA, U.S.). The quality and concentration of DNA were determined by 1.0% agarose gel electrophoresis and a NanoDrop® ND-2000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Scientific Inc., USA). The hypervariable region V3-V4 of the microbial 16S rRNA gene was amplified with primer pairs 338F and 806R by an ABI GeneAmp® 9700 PCR thermocycler (ABI, CA, USA). Purified amplicons were pooled in equimolar amounts and paired-end sequenced on an Illumina MiSeq PE300 platform/NovaSeq PE250 platform (Illumina, San Diego, USA) following the standard protocols by Majorbio Bio-Pharm Technology Co. Ltd. (Shanghai, China). Raw FASTQ files were de-multiplexed using an in-house perl script and then quality-filtered by fastp version 0.19.6 and merged by FLASH version 1.2.714. Subsequently, the optimized sequences were clustered into operational taxonomic units (OTUs) using UPARSE 7.1 with a 97% sequence similarity level. The sequencing coverage and quality statistics for each sample were summarized in Table S1. Differential bacterial taxa were identified using the Kruskal–Wallis H test for the three-group comparison and the Wilcoxon signed-rank test for the two-group comparison (p < 0.05). Significant taxa were further validated and ranked using Linear Discriminant Analysis Effect Size (LEfSe) with a Linear Discriminant Analysis (LDA) score > 2.0.

Metabolome detection and data analysis

The metabolomic analysis was performed using an untargeted metabolomics approach, with data acquired in both positive and negative ionization modes. For metabolite extraction, 50 mg of tissue was homogenized and processed using a 400 μL methanol: water (4:1, v/v) solution containing L-2-chlorophenylalanin as an internal standard. Six pooled quality control (QC) samples were prepared by combining equal aliquots of all individual samples. The analysis was carried out on a UHPLC-Q Exactive system (Thermo Fisher Scientific) equipped with an HSS T3 column (100 mm × 2.1 mm, 1.8 μm). The mobile phases consisted of 0.1% formic acid in water: acetonitrile (95:5, v/v) (solvent A) and 0.1% formic acid in acetonitrile: isopropanol: water (47.5:47.5:5, v/v) (solvent B), with a gradient elution at a flow rate of 0.4 mL/min. Key mass spectrometry parameters included: heater temperature 400 °C, capillary temperature 320 °C, sheath gas flow 40 arb, auxiliary gas flow 10 arb, ion-spray voltage ± 3500 V (positive) and –2800 V (negative), and a full MS scanning range of m/z 70–1050. The raw data of LC/MS was preprocessed by Progenesis QI (Waters Corporation, Milford, USA) software. The metabolites were searched and identified, and the primary databases were the HMDB (http://www.hmdb.ca/), Metlin (https://metlin.scripps.edu/), and Majorbio Database. The confidence level of metabolite identification was assigned according to the Metabolomics Standards Initiative (MSI) guidelines15. After the database search, the data was uploaded to the Majorbio cloud platform (https://cloud.majorbio.com) for data analysis. The R package ropls (Version 1.6.2) performed partial least squares discriminant analysis (PLS-DA). The metabolites with variable importance in the projection (VIP) > 1 and p < 0.05 (from Kruskal–Wallis H test for three-group comparison or Wilcoxon signed-rank test for two-group comparison) were significantly differential. To control false discoveries, we further applied false discovery rate (FDR) correction, retaining metabolites with FDR-adjusted p < 0.05 as the final significantly differential metabolites. The functions of these metabolites and metabolic pathways were analyzed using the Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) database. The methodology applied follows the established protocols from Majorbio16.

Combined microbiome‑metabolome analysis

Correlations between differential microbiota and metabolites were calculated based on the one-to-one correspondence relationship between samples. The Spearman correlation method was utilized for the analysis.

Cell culture, treatment, and CCK-8 assay

Human PDAC cell lines PA-TU-8988t and Panc-1 were obtained from the Cell Bank of the Chinese Academy of Science (Shanghai, China). These two lines were selected as complementary models to explore metabolic impacts across PDAC stages: PA-TU-8988t is derived from a liver metastasis and represents advanced disease, whereas Panc-1 originates from a primary tumor17. All human cell lines had been authenticated using STR profiling within the past three years, and all experiments were performed with mycoplasma-free cells. PA-TU-8988t and Panc-1 cells were maintained in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM, Sigma-Aldrich, St. Gallen, Switzerland) with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, Sigma-Aldrich) and 1% antibiotics.18 All cells were cultured at 37 °C in 5% CO2 atmosphere. Cells were treated with different concentrations of metabolites, then cell viability was measured by Cell Counting Kit-8 kit (CCK-8, Dojindo, Japan). Briefly, cells were cultured in a 96-well plate at a density of 1 × 104 per well overnight, then treated with the specified agents for the indicated durations. At 24, 48, 72, and 96 h (Day 1, 2, 3, and 4) after medium change, 90 µl fresh medium and 10 µl CCK-8 solution were added to cells and incubated for 2 h at 37 °C. OD value was measured at 450 nm wavelengths by a microplate reader (Bio-tek, USA).

Results

Changes in the composition of pancreatic microbiota from control N, through PCT, to PDAC T

We enrolled 36 subjects, including 23 with PDAC and 13 with pancreatic benign tumors. Basic demographic and clinical data were collected during inclusion (Table S2). To investigate the alterations of microbiota and metabolome along the normal-adjacent-carcinoma sequence, we compared the profiles of microbiota and metabolome among Control N, PCT, and PDAC T. The Sobs index, which reflects species richness, was significantly lower in PDAC T than in PCT and Control N (p = 0.0017, Fig. 1A). There was no significant difference in Shannon index among the three groups, which reflects species richness and evenness (Fig. 1B). Principal co-ordinates analysis (PCoA) analysis based on unweighted UniFrac distance was performed to assess the overall diversity of the microbiota. Anosim demonstrated significant differences among Control N, PCT, and PDAC T (p = 0.017, Fig. 1C). These findings suggested pancreatic microbial dysbiosis in PDAC tumorigenesis. The top four phyla in the three groups were Proteobacteria, Firmicutes, Bacteroidota, and unclassified_k__norank_d__Bacteria, indicating a broad phyla-level similarity (Fig. 1D). However, differences emerged at the genus level. The top four genera were Achromobacter, Pedobacter, Streptococcus, and Pseudomonas in Control N, Achromobacter, Pedobacter, unclassified_k__norank_d__Bacteria, and Pseudomonas in PCT, while Achromobacter, Pedobacter, Acinetobacter, and Enterococcus in PDAC T (Fig. 1E). We provided stacked bar plots for all samples at the phylum and genus levels (Fig. S1 and Fig.S2).

Differential analysis of pancreatic microbiota from control N, through PCT, to PDAC T

There were 90 significantly differential genera among Control N, PCT, and PDAC T identified by the Kruskal–Wallis H test (p < 0.05, Fig. 1F and Table S3). From Control N, through PCT, to PDAC T, there were seven genera sequentially increasing but 65 genera sequentially decreasing. Box plots showed the relative abundance of these increasing or decreasing genera (Fig. S3 and Fig. S4). Specifically, the abundance of Lactobacillus, Peptostreptococcus, Actinomyces, norank_f__Mitochondria, Ileibacterium, Clostridium_sensu_stricto_7, and Faecalibaculum sequentially increased, while that of Clostridium_sensu_stricto_1, Prevotellaceae_NK3B31_group, unclassified_f__Lachnospiraceae, UCG-005, Megasphaera, Terrisporobacter, Ruminococcus, Faecalibacterium, Prevotellaceae_UCG-003, Subdoligranulum, norank_f__Prevotellaceae, Coprococcus, Eubacterium, etc. sequentially decreased. However, according to the heatmap, the increasing tendency of Lactobacillus may be attributed to the high abundance in very few PDAC T samples (Fig. 1G). The LEfSe (LDA > 2.5,) analysis also identified similar differential microbiota among the three groups (Fig. 1H and Fig. S5). The network analysis showed that most of the differential genera were positively correlated with each other (Fig. 1I). Through functional prediction analysis of the microbiota, PICRUSt2 identified the significantly differential KEGG categories among the three groups, including glyoxylate and dicarboxylate metabolism, taurine and hypotaurine metabolism, GABAergic synapse, glutamatergic synapse pathways, which were up-regulated from Control N, through PCT, to PDAC T (Fig. 1J and Fig. S6).

Alterations of pancreatic metabolome from control N, through PCT, to PDAC T

By LC/MS analysis, we further investigated the alterations in the metabolome among Control N, PCT, and PDAC T. PLS-DA showed that the metabolites of the PCT were partially coincident with the Control N, while the PDAC T was clearly distinguished (Fig. 2A). With decreasing permutation retention, R2 and Q2 decreased, and the regression line trended upward, indicating that there was no overfitting in the model (Fig. 2B). The Venn plot showed that PCT and Control N shared more identical metabolites, while fewer were shared between PDAC T and Control N (Fig. 2C). To explore continuous changes from Control N to PCT and then to PDAC T, we compared the distribution of metabolites across the three groups by Kruskal–Wallis H test. 470 metabolites were significantly differential among Control N, PCT and PDAC T. After FDR correction, 248 significantly different metabolites (FDR-corrected p < 0.05, VIP < 1, and FC > 1) were identified (Table S4). Among these, from Control N, through PCT, to PDAC T, 110 metabolites showed a sequentially increasing tendency, including L-lactic acid (MSI level 2), 3-hydroxybutanoic acid (MSI level 1), creatine (MSI level 1), glycocholic acid (GCA, MSI level 2), 2-hydroxybutyric acid (MSI level 1), L-glycine (MSI level 1), methylene bisacrylamide (MSI level 2), 2-hydroxy-3-methylbutyric acid (MSI level 1), zeranol (MSI level 2), etc. In contrast, 55 metabolites showed a sequentially decreasing tendency, including linoleic acid (MSI level 1), monopalmitin (MSI level 1), elaidic acid (MSI level 2), tyr-ile-gly-ser-arg (YIGSR, MSI level 2), etc. (Fig. 2D). KEGG enrichment analysis revealed that these differential metabolites were enriched in 32 metabolic pathways, including GABAergic synapse, purine metabolism, butanoate metabolism, protein digestion and absorption, synaptic vesicle cycle, glyoxylate and dicarboxylate metabolism, central carbon metabolism (CCM) in cancer pathways, etc. (Fig. 2E and Table S5) The GABAergic synapse pathway was the top enriched pathway. Box plots showed the relative abundance of differential metabolites in the top 10 enriched pathways (Fig. S7).

Alterations of pancreatic microbiota from control N to Control T

To investigate the alterations of microbiota and metabolome in the pancreatic benign tumors, we compared the profiles of microbiota and metabolome between Control N and Control T. We measured the pancreatic microbial alpha-diversity and beta-diversity and found no significant difference between Control N and Control T (Fig. 3A-C). Wilcoxon signed-rank test (p < 0.05, Fig. 3D and Table S3) and LEfSe (LDA > 2) were used to analyze the differential microbiota between the two groups (Fig. 3E and Fig. S8). At the genus level, the abundance of Ileibacterium and g__unclassified_p__Proteobacteria significantly increased in Control T, whereas that of Clostridium_sensu_stricto_1, Ruminococcus, Blautia, Coprococcus, Lachnospiraceae_AC2044_group, UCG-002, Stenotrophomonas, Lachnospiraceae_ND3007_group, Negativibacillus, Eubacterium_eligens_group, Fusicatenibacter, Campylobacter, and Bosea significantly decreased in Control T. A heatmap was used to describe the composition of these differential genera in each sample (Fig. 3F). Some of these differential genera were similar to those in PDAC tumorigenesis, for example, Ileibacterium also increased whereas Ruminococcus and Coprococcus also decreased significantly. Network analysis showed that most of the differential genera were positively correlated with each other (Fig. 3G). Through functional prediction analysis of the microbiota, PICRUSt2 identified no significantly differential KEGG categories between the two groups (Fig. 3H).

Alterations of pancreatic metabolome from control N to Control T

PLS-DA showed that Control N and Control T were separated into two distinct clusters (Fig. 4A). With the decrease of permutation retention, R2 and Q2 decreased, and the regression line showed an upward trend, indicating that there was no overfitting in the model (Fig. 4B). 401 metabolites (p < 0.05, VIP > 1, and FC > 1) were altered in Control T compared with Control N, including 75 up-regulated and 326 down-regulated (Fig. 4C). 195 differential metabolites were identified after FDR correction (Fig. 4D and Table S6). KEGG analysis showed that differential metabolites were distributed across multiple pathways (Fig. 4E and Table S7). Interestingly, some of these differential metabolites and pathways were similar to those in PDAC tumorigenesis. For example, L-lactic acid (MSI level 2), 3-hydroxybutanoic acid (MSI level 1), methylene bisacrylamide (MSI level 2), 2-hydroxy-3-methylbutyric acid (MSI level 1), and zeranol (MSI level 2) were also up-regulated in Control T, while linoleic acid (MSI level 1) and monopalmitin (MSI level 1) were also down-regulated; the arginine biosynthesis, cAMP signaling, alanine, aspartate and glutamate metabolism, CCM in cancer, butanoate metabolism pathways were also enriched. Additionally, Control T also exhibited some unique characteristics, such as the increase of glutaminylcysteine (MSI level 2), N-succinimidyl 6-maleimidohexanoate (MSI level 2), and citric acid (MSI level 1), the decrease of buthionine sulfoximine (MSI level 2), conophylline (MSI level 2), and meclizine (MSI level 2), and abnormalities in D-amino acid metabolism, glucagon signaling, and sphingolipid metabolism pathways, etc. Box plots showed the relative abundance of differential metabolites in these enrichment pathways (Fig. S9).

Microbial and metabolic differences in PDAC patients with different chemotherapy responses

Among the 23 PDAC patients, 15 received standard postoperative chemotherapy and were categorized by treatment response into a chemotherapy-resistant group (n = 6) and a chemotherapy-sensitive group (n = 9). No significant difference was observed in baseline characteristics between the two groups, including resectability status, preoperative CA19-9 values, the status of preoperative therapy, tumor size, the status of lymph node metastasis, TNM classification, the status of surgical margin, etc. (Table S8). LEfSe (LDA > 2) was used to analyze the differential microbiota in the PDAC tissue between the two groups (Fig. 5A, B and Table S3). At the genus level, the abundance of Rhodobacter, Faecalibacterium, and Brevundimonas significantly decreased in the PDAC tissue of chemotherapy-resistant group. 87 metabolites (p < 0.05, VIP > 1, and FC > 1) were altered in the chemotherapy-resistant group compared with the chemotherapy-sensitive group, including 14 up-regulated and 73 down-regulated (Fig. 5C and Table S9). Among these, beta-guanidinopropionic acid (GPA, MSI level 2) and S-(2-Carboxyethyl)-L-cysteine (MSI level 2) increased, while (3Z)-2-propylpent-3-enoic acid (MSI level 2) decreased in the chemotherapy-resistant group (Fig. 5D). Some similar differences were also observed across the three stages of PDAC tumorigenesis. GPA and S-(2-Carboxyethyl)-L-cysteine exhibited a continuous increase across PDAC tumorigenesis, and were significantly enriched in the chemotherapy-resistant group. In contrast, Faecalibacterium and (3Z)-2-Propylpent-3-enoic acid exhibited a continuous decrease across PDAC tumorigenesis, and were significantly reduced in the chemotherapy-resistant group. Spearman’s correlation analysis showed that Faecalibacterium was assessed to be positively correlated with (3Z)-2-Propylpent-3-enoic acid, but negatively correlated with GPA and S-(2-carboxyethyl)-L-cysteine (Fig. 5E).

GPA and S-(2-carboxyethyl)-L-cysteine promoted proliferation in PDAC cell lines

To verify the role of these metabolites in PDAC tumorigenesis, we performed cell line experiments. As shown in Fig. 6A-D, CCK8 assay showed that GPA and S-(2-carboxyethyl)-L-cysteine effectively promoted the proliferation of PA-TU-8988t and Panc-1 cells compared with the control group. The proliferative effect of GPA on PA-TU-8988t and Panc-1 cells was not significant on Days 1 and 2. However, from Day 3 onward, GPA at all concentrations significantly promoted the proliferation of both cell lines (Fig. 6A-B). In contrast, S-(2-carboxyethyl)-L-cysteine notably enhanced the proliferation of PA-TU-8988t and Panc-1 cells during the first two days, particularly on PA-TU-8988t cells. Furthermore, the proliferative effect of S-(2-carboxyethyl)-L-cysteine was well-maintained on Days 3 and 4 (Fig. 6C-D).

Discussion

To our knowledge, this is the first study to describe the alterations in microbiome and metabolome across multiple stages from normal pancreatic tissues to PDAC, while also describing the alterations from normal pancreatic tissues to pancreatic benign tumors, thereby providing an exploratory mapping of this dynamic progression. Most interestingly, our multi-omics data suggest that PCT may represent an intermediate state between normal pancreatic tissues and PDAC. In both PDAC tumorigenesis and chemotherapy-resistant patients, decreased Faecalibacterium was positively correlated with (3Z)-2-Propylpent-3-enoic acid, but negatively correlated with GPA and S-(2-carboxyethyl)-L-cysteine.

The alpha-diversity of pancreatic microbiota was significantly lower in PDAC T than in PCT and Control N. It has been reported that the microbial alpha-diversity in pancreatic tumors of long-term survival (LTS) PDAC patients was higher than short-term survival (STS) patients, which may contribute to the antitumor effect by promoting CD8 + T cells (CTLs) recruitment and shaping a differential enrichment of metabolic pathways 20. Whether higher microbial alpha-diversity can inhibit PDAC development warrants further investigation.

Our results support that PCT represents an intermediate state between normal pancreatic tissues and PDAC. From Control N, through PCT, to PDAC T, there were 90 differential genera among the three groups, with a one-third probability of sequentially increasing or decreasing from the probability calculation. However, 72 genera actually sequentially increased or decreased, accounting for up to four-fifths, and this phenomenon also occurred in metabolites. This further suggests that PCT may represent a unique intermediate state between normal pancreatic tissues and PDAC. While pathological evaluation confirmed that PCT exhibited characteristic mixed features including inflammation, fibrosis, and atrophy, the intricate intermingling of these pathological components presents challenges for establishing discrete quantitative scoring systems. This composite profile, however, effectively captures the essential characteristics of the reactive microenvironment adjacent to PDAC tumor tissues. The “field cancerization” theory implies the evolutionary process of tumorigenesis, that is, the gradual acquisition of microbial and metabolic alterations that place PCT in an intermediate preneoplastic state21. Another view suggests that tumors have an active role in shaping the microenvironment of adjacent tissues22.

Along the normal-adjacent-carcinoma sequence, microbial dysbiosis is associated with PDAC tumorigenesis. From Control N, through PCT, to PDAC T, Peptostreptococcus and Ileibacterium sequentially increased, while Faecalibacterium, Subdoligranulum, Coprococcus, and Eubacterium sequentially decreased significantly. A tryptophan metabolite derived from Peptostreptococcus anaerobius, trans-3-indoleacrylic acid, has been reported to facilitate colorectal carcinogenesis via ferroptosis inhibition23. Ileibacterium can promote colorectal cancer (CRC) progression by synthesizing bile acid-cholic acid (AA-CA)24. Regarding the decreasing microbiota, Faecalibacterium, Subdoligranulum, Coprococcus, and Eubacterium are butyrate producers, belonging to the Clostridium cluster of the phylum Firmicutes. Butyrate, a type of SCFA, has been reported to inhibit cancer growth and enhance gemcitabine effectiveness in PDAC25. Functional prediction analysis of microbiota identified the significantly up-regulated KEGG categories in PDAC tumorigenesis, including glutathione metabolism and GABAergic synapse pathways, which were consistent with our metabolome findings. Previous studies have also confirmed the abnormalities in these metabolic pathways in tumors26,27. This suggests that pancreatic microbiota is associated with a differential enrichment of the above-mentioned metabolic pathways during PDAC tumorigenesis; further studies are needed to confirm the mechanism.

Along the normal-adjacent-carcinoma sequence, metabolic alterations are associated with PDAC tumorigenesis. From Control N, through PCT, to PDAC T, L-lactic acid and 3-hydroxybutanoic acid sequentially increased, while linoleic acid and monopalmitin sequentially decreased. L-lactic acid is an essential contributor to the tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle in tumors and establishes a favorable acidic microenvironment for immunosuppression and therapy resistance for PDAC28. 3-hydroxybutanoic acid, also known as beta-hydroxybutyrate (betaOHB), serves as an alternative fuel to promote PDAC growth and progression29. Regarding the decreasing metabolites, linoleic acid, a main component of the linoleic acid pathway, can significantly inhibit the proliferation of PDAC and hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) cells30,31. Monopalmitin can induce death in lung cancer cells through PI3K/Akt pathway32. However, the specific roles of these metabolites in PDAC remain largely unclear and warrant further investigation. Pathway enrichment analysis revealed abnormalities in GABAergic synapse, butanoate metabolism, purine metabolism, CCM in cancer, etc., among Control N, PCT, and PDAC T. Recently, GABAergic signaling was shown to facilitate PDAC progression by shaping immune cell infiltration33,34. Interestingly, the less-studied butanoate metabolism pathway, which is strongly activated in CRC, was also identified in this study35. Purine metabolism and CCM in cancer are significantly altered in PDAC and linked to dysregulated energy metabolism36,37. The abnormalities in arginine and proline metabolism and arginine biosynthesis indicate active changes in amino acid metabolism. Enhanced biosynthetic activities are essential features of metabolic reprogramming in cancer, contributing to immune responses associated with tumorigenesis and metastasis38. These findings suggest that the above metabolic pathways may be associated with PDAC tumorigenesis and can serve as characteristic molecular events and therapeutic targets for PDAC.

Interestingly, microbial and metabolic alterations similar to those in PDAC tumorigenesis also occurred in pancreatic benign tumors. These microbes and metabolites were concurrently altered in both PDAC and pancreatic benign tumors, suggesting a possible association with pancreatic tumor development. However, distinct metabolic alterations also occurred. Citric acid, which increased in Control T, is a vital component of the CCM cycle and can enhance metastatic activity and therapeutic resistance of cancer cells39. Furthermore, the sphingolipid metabolism pathway was only enriched in pancreatic benign tumors. Sphingolipid metabolism is increasingly recognized as a key pathway in cancer biology, regulating tumor cell proliferation and drug resistance40. These results indicate that some precursor changes of PDAC may occur in pancreatic benign tumors, rather than being truly “benign”. However, the specific mechanisms underlying this phenomenon remain to be elucidated.

Some differences similar to PDAC tumorigenesis were also observed between chemotherapy-resistant and chemotherapy-sensitive patients. In both PDAC tumorigenesis and chemotherapy-resistant patients, GPA and S-(2-carboxyethyl)-L-cysteine increased, while Faecalibacterium and (3Z)-2-Propylpent-3-enoic acid decreased. Experimental evidence strongly support the use of Faecalibacterium as a next-generation probiotic (NGP)41. Several studies have shown that higher levels of Faecalibacterium positively correlate with better treatment responses in various cancers, such as melanoma and non-small cell lung cancer.42,43 GPA, a creatine analogue, has been rarely studied in cancer44. However, creatine can promote invasion and metastasis in PDAC and CRC 45. Correlation analysis showed that in PDAC tumorigenesis and chemotherapy-resistant group, decreased Faecalibacterium was positively correlated with (3Z)-2-Propylpent-3-enoic acid, but negatively correlated with GPA and S-(2-carboxyethyl)-L-cysteine. The results suggested a potential role of Faecalibacterium in inhibiting the occurrence and resistance to chemotherapy of PDAC by up-regulating (3Z)-2-Propylpent-3-enoic acid or down-regulating GPA and S-(2-carboxyethyl)-L-cysteine. But the relationship between microbiota and these metabolites in PDAC tissue was still unclear. Our experiments demonstrated that GPA and S-(2-carboxyethyl)-L-cysteine promoted PDAC cell proliferation. Notably, these pro-proliferative effects were observed in both PA-TU-8988t (a metastasis-derived model) and Panc-1 (a primary tumor-derived model), suggesting that the identified metabolites may play a fundamental role in PDAC growth, not limited to a specific disease stage. However, the underlying mechanisms remain to be clarified.

Our study had several limitations. First, the sample size of PDAC and pancreatic benign tumors was relatively small. Second, as an observational and exploratory study without experimental validation, the proposed causal relationships between the identified microbiota/metabolites and PDAC tumorigenesis need to be confirmed by further experiments. Additionally, the absence of negative controls, extraction blanks, and reagent sequencing in our study represents a limitation in ensuring contamination control. In the future, more research is required to validate our results with a larger sample size, with contamination control measures, corroborated by histopathological characterization of the intermediate pathological features of PCT and combined with in vivo and in vitro experiments.

To our knowledge, this is the first study to describe microbiome and metabolome alterations across the progression from normal pancreatic tissues, through PCT, and ultimately to PDAC. Further functional studies on the identified microbiota and metabolites are warranted, as they may reveal the mechanisms underlying the occurrence and development of PDAC and pancreatic benign tumors and provide a basis for future treatments.

Conclusions

As an exploratory analysis, our study demonstrates that PDAC tumorigenesis involves sequential restructuring of the pancreatic microbiome and metabolome. By mapping this progression from normal tissues, through PCTs, to malignant tumors, we identify a pattern of microbial dysbiosis and metabolic alteration, with PCTs representing a transitional state of cancer development. Furthermore, GPA and S-(2-Carboxyethyl)-L-cysteine are associated with tumor progression and chemotherapy resistance, offering new approaches for diagnostic and therapeutic strategies against this lethal disease.
