On Rees algebras and de Jonquières transformations
Matthew Weaver

Abstract
. We recall a higher dimension analog of the classic plane de Jonquières transformations, as given by Hassanzadeh and Simis in [ 9 ] . Such a parameterization defines a birational map from ℙ n − 1 \mathbb{P}^{n-1} to a hypersurface in ℙ n \mathbb{P}^{n} , and a natural question that arises is how to obtain its implicit equation. We pass from the image of this map to its graph, and implicitize the Rees algebra of the ideal of the de Jonquières map when its underlying Cremona support is tame. We then consider the Rees rings of ideals of generalized de Jonquières transformations, as introduced in [ 25 ] , and answer a conjecture of Ramos and Simis.



1.
Introduction

The study of birational transformations is classically rooted in algebraic geometry with a rich history. In the early 20th century, the generation of the
Cremona group
of
\mathbb{P}^{2}
, consisting of all such birational transformations of the projective plane, was a topic of great interest for both geometers and algebraists alike. In a seminal result, Noether showed that such a transformation could be expressed as a composition of quadratic transformations. Castelnuovo later showed that plane Cremona transformations could also be decomposed into
de Jonquières
transformations. Even in
\mathbb{P}^{3}
, the Cremona group and related constructions become significantly more complicated, however there are adaptations of the above notions to higher dimensions
[
9
,
23
,
24
,
25
]
.

The notion of a de Jonquières transformation in higher dimension typically relies on the existence of a supporting Cremona transformation in one dimension lower. In the case of plane de Jonquières transformations, this underlying Cremona map is the identity, but this fails to be the case beyond transformations of
\mathbb{P}^{2}
. As such, the nature of most higher dimensional analogs are elusive in general. In this article, we consider two such adaptions of this notion, namely the de Jonquières transformation
\mathbb{P}^{n-1}\dashrightarrow\mathbb{P}^{n}
as introduced by Hassanzadeh and Simis in
[
9
]
, and the later extension of a
generalized
de Jonquières transformation
\mathbb{P}^{n}\dashrightarrow\mathbb{P}^{n}
as introduced by Ramos and Simis in
[
25
]
.

Throughout we assume that
k
is an algebraically closed field, and consider a rational map
\Psi:\,\mathbb{P}^{n}\dashrightarrow\mathbb{P}^{m}
defined by homogeneous polynomials
f_{0},\ldots,f_{m}\in R=k[x_{0},\ldots,x_{n}]
of the same degree. The closed image of
\Psi
is a subvariety
X\subseteq\mathbb{P}^{m}
, with homogeneous coordinate ring
k[f_{0},\ldots,f_{m}]
as a
k
-subalgebra of
R
. However, as
X
is a subvariety of
\mathbb{P}^{m}
, there exists an ideal
\mathcal{I}(X)
of
S=k[y_{0},\ldots,y_{m}]
, the coordinate ring of
\mathbb{P}^{m}
, such that
k[f_{0},\ldots,f_{m}]\cong S/\mathcal{I}(X)
. In this sense, the polynomials
f_{0},\ldots,f_{m}
are commonly seen as a
parameterization
of
X
, and the ideal
\mathcal{I}(X)
in
k[y_{0},\ldots,y_{m}]
as the
implicitization
of
X
. Determining the generators of
\mathcal{I}(X)
is the so-called
implicitization problem
.

In the setting above, it is often more advantageous to implicitize the
graph
of
\Psi
, as this carries much more information, and then project onto the image. Recall that the coordinate ring of the graph is the
Rees algebra
of the ideal
I=(f_{0},\ldots,f_{m})
, which is the graded subring
\mathcal{R}(I)=R[f_{0}t,\ldots,f_{m}t]
of
R[t]
, for
t
an indeterminate. There is a natural epimorphism
\Psi:R[y_{0},\ldots,y_{m}]\rightarrow\mathcal{R}(I)
given by
y_{i}\mapsto f_{i}t
, which induces an isomorphism
\mathcal{R}(I)\cong R[y_{0},\ldots,y_{m}]/\mathcal{J}
, where
\mathcal{J}=\ker\Psi
is the
defining ideal
of
\mathcal{R}(I)
. With this, we note that
\mathcal{R}(I)/\mathfrak{m}\mathcal{R}(I)\cong S/\mathcal{I}(X)
, where
\mathfrak{m}=(x_{0},\ldots,x_{n})
, hence it is enough to determine the implicit equations of
\mathcal{R}(I)
.
The search for generators of
\mathcal{J}
has been well studied, yet a full set of
defining equations
of the Rees algebra is unknown in most settings. There has been success in particular settings when the ideal
I
has low codimension (see e.g.
[
1
,
3
,
19
,
20
,
21
,
22
,
30
,
31
]
), but the problem remains open in general.

In this paper, we consider the implicitization of de Jonquières transformations, taking all conventions from
[
9
]
, and study the Rees algebra of the ideals defining these rational maps. We proceed along a path similar to the one taken in
[
1
]
and employ the
method of divisors
, as introduced in
[
15
]
, to determine the defining ideal. We consider the Rees ring of the ideal of a de Jonquières transformation when its underlying Cremona support is as tame as possible, namely the identity. The main result, a formulation of
5.1
and
5.5
, is as follows.

Theorem A
.

Let
\mathfrak{J}:\,\mathbb{P}^{n-1}\dashrightarrow\mathbb{P}^{n}
be a de Jonquières transformation with Cremona support
\mathfrak{C}
the identity. Let
I=(fx_{1},\ldots,fx_{n},g)\subseteq R=k[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]
denote the ideal of this rational map for homogeneous polynomials
f
and
g
with
\deg g=\deg f+1=d\geq 2
. The Rees algebra of
I
is
\mathcal{R}(I)\cong R[y_{1},\ldots,y_{n+1}]/\mathcal{J}
where

\mathcal{J}=I_{2}(\psi)+(h_{1},\ldots,h_{d})

where
\psi
is a
2\times n
matrix of indeterminates, and
h_{1},\ldots,h_{d}
is a downgraded sequence of polynomials associated to
f
and
g
. Additionally,
\mathcal{R}(I)
is almost Cohen-Macaulay and is Cohen-Macaulay if and only if
d\leq n
.

The essential ingredient to the proof of
A
is the notion of a
downgraded sequence
of a particular syzygy associated to
f
and
g
. This recursive algorithm produces equations of the defining ideal at each iteration, and yields a full generating set in the setting above. Our description of this process is borrowed from similar processes used in
[
9
,
25
]
, but we also note that similar methods have been used to produce equations of the Rees ring in other instances
[
1
,
5
,
30
,
31
,
32
]
. Once the defining ideal
\mathcal{J}
has been determined, the Cohen-Macaulayness of
\mathcal{R}(I)
is studied by relating
\mathcal{J}
to an ideal whose powers have resolutions within the family of Eagon-Northcott complexes.

Once Rees rings of ideals of de Jonquières transformations have been studied, we show that the methods used in the proof of
A
can be adapted to the setting of
generalized
de Jonquières transformations
\mathbb{P}^{n}\dashrightarrow\mathbb{P}^{n}
, as introduced in
[
25
]
. In the case of identity support, the equations of the Rees algebra are known
[
25
, 2.6]
, and may be realized as a downgraded sequence. However, it was left as a question as to when the Rees ring is Cohen-Macaulay
[
25
, 2.10]
. Applying the methods of
A
to this setting, we show that the Cohen-Macaulayness of
\mathcal{R}(I)
may be determined. The main result for generalized de Jonquières transformations,
6.11
, is as follows.

Theorem B
.

Let
\mathfrak{J}:\,\mathbb{P}^{n}\dashrightarrow\mathbb{P}^{n}
be a generalized de Jonquières transformation with Cremona support
\mathfrak{C}
the identity. Let
I=(fx_{1},\ldots,fx_{n},g)\subseteq R=k[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n+1}]
denote the ideal of this transformation for homogeneous polynomials
f
and
g
with
\deg g=\deg f+1=d\geq 2
. The Rees algebra
\mathcal{R}(I)
is almost Cohen-Macaulay and is Cohen-Macaulay if and only if
d\leq n+1
.

In particular, this answers a conjecture of Ramos and Simis
[
25
, 2.10]
in the affirmative. As noted, the defining equations of the Rees ring are already known
[
25
, 2.6]
, hence we are only interested in the Cohen-Macaulay property of
\mathcal{R}(I)
in
B
. However, we note that the methods presented here can be used to provide an alternate proof for the generation of the defining ideal by a downgraded sequence.

We now briefly describe how this article is organized. In
Section

2
, we recall the notion of a de Jonquières transformation as in
[
9
]
, and begin our study of the Rees ring of the ideal of such a rational map in the case of identity support. In
Section

3
, we introduce a simpler algebra defined by a determinantal ideal which maps onto the Rees algebra, and we compare properties of these rings. In
Section

4
, we present the method of downgraded sequences, similar to the techniques used in
[
9
,
25
]
, and show that this process yields equations of the Rees algebra. In
Section

5
, we show that this method produces a complete generating set of the defining ideal, and use this description to describe the Cohen-Macaulayness of the Rees algebra. In
Section

6
, we recall the notion of a
generalized
de Jonquières transformation, as given in
[
25
]
. We show that many of the constructions developed in the previous sections are easily adapted to this setting. In particular, we determine the Cohen-Macaulayness of the Rees ring in a manner similar to the procedure of
Section

5
, and answer the aforementioned conjecture of Ramos and Simis.

2.
Rees algebras and de Jonquières maps

We begin by recalling the notion of a
de Jonquières transformation

\mathfrak{J}:\,\mathbb{P}^{n-1}\dashrightarrow\mathbb{P}^{n}
, as given by Hassanzadeh and Simis in
[
9
]
. We note that these maps and the Rees rings of their ideals have been studied in generality, hence we begin by recounting a few items at the start of this section. We then study the Rees ring of the ideal of forms defining this map, when its Cremona support is mild.

Recall that a rational map
\Psi:\,\mathbb{P}^{n}\dashrightarrow\mathbb{P}^{m}
is said to be
birational
onto its image
X
if there exists a reverse map
\Psi^{\prime}:\,\mathbb{P}^{m}\dashrightarrow\mathbb{P}^{n}
, which gives a proper inverse when restricted to
X\subseteq\mathbb{P}^{m}
. In particular, we have the special case of a
Cremona transformation
, namely a birational map
\mathfrak{C}:\,\mathbb{P}^{n}\dashrightarrow\mathbb{P}^{n}
of
\mathbb{P}^{n}
onto itself. With this, we may construct a de Jonquières transformation
\mathfrak{J}:\,\mathbb{P}^{n-1}\dashrightarrow\mathbb{P}^{n}
, as given in
[
9
]
. Writing
R=k[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]
to denote the coordinate ring of
\mathbb{P}^{n-1}
, let
\mathfrak{C}:\,\mathbb{P}^{n-1}\dashrightarrow\mathbb{P}^{n-1}
denote a Cremona transformation defined by homogeneous forms
g_{1},\ldots,g_{n}
of degree
\mathfrak{d}\geq 1
in
R
.

Definition 2.1

(
[
9
]
)
.

Let
f
and
g
be two additional homogeneous polynomials of
R=k[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]
with
\deg g=\deg f+\mathfrak{d}
and
\gcd(f,g)=1
. The rational map
\mathfrak{J}:\,\mathbb{P}^{n-1}\dashrightarrow\mathbb{P}^{n}
defined by
fg_{1},\ldots,fg_{n},g
will be called a
de Jonquières transformation
, with Cremona support
\mathfrak{C}
.

An important note is that the map
\mathfrak{J}
above is birational onto its image
[
6
, 1.11]
, which is a hypersurface
V(F)\subseteq\mathbb{P}^{n}
for some homogeneous polynomial
F
in
k[y_{1},\ldots,y_{n+1}]
, the coordinate ring of
\mathbb{P}^{n}
.

We now begin our study of the implicitization of a de Jonquières transformation when the underlying Cremona support is the identity. Our approach will be purely algebraic, but the geometric notions may be easily recovered, as noted above. Our primary setting is the following.

Setting 2.2
.

Let
R=k[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]
for
n\geq 2
, with homogeneous maximal ideal
\mathfrak{m}=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})
. Let
f
and
g
be homogeneous polynomials of
R
, with
\deg g=d
and
\deg f=d-1
, for some integer
d\geq 2
. Further assume that
\gcd(f,g)=1
and let
I=(f\mathfrak{m},g)=(fx_{1},\ldots,fx_{n},g)
.

From the previous definition,
I
is the ideal of the de Jonquières transformation
\mathfrak{J}
defined by
fx_{1},\ldots,fx_{n},g
, with supporting Cremona map the identity
\mathfrak{C}:\,\mathbb{P}^{n-1}\dashrightarrow\mathbb{P}^{n-1}
given by
x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}
. Notice that
I
is an ideal of codimension two as
V(I)=V(f,g)\cup\{\mathfrak{m}\}
, and
f,g
is a regular sequence since
\gcd(f,g)=1
.
Rees rings of ideals with codimension two have been studied extensively (see e.g.
[
1
,
20
,
21
,
22
,
30
]
), however most cases require the assumption of perfection, so as to use the Hilbert-Burch theorem
[
7
, 20.15]
. Whereas this will not be the case here (unless
n=2
), a presentation of this ideal is readily available from the mapping cone construction.

Proposition 2.3

(
[
9
, 2.2]
)
.

With respect to the generating set
\{fx_{1},\ldots,fx_{n},g\}
, the ideal
I
may be presented by

R^{\binom{n}{2}+1}\overset{\varphi}{\longrightarrow}R^{n+1}\rightarrow I\rightarrow 0

where

\varphi=\left(\begin{array}[]{ccc|c}&&&\\
&\delta_{1}(\underline{x})&&\partial g\\
&&&\\
\hline\cr 0&\cdots&0&-f\end{array}\right)

where
\delta_{1}(\underline{x})
is the first Koszul differential on
\underline{x}=x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}
, and
\partial g
is a column of homogeneous elements, of the same degree, with
[x_{1}\ldots x_{n}]\cdot\partial g=g
.

We note that
\partial g
is not unique in general, and there are often many choices. However, any such choice will do for our purposes and the presentation above. We will revisit this construction in
Section

4
.

Remark 2.4
.

Before we begin our study of the Rees ring of
I
, we introduce an algebra closely related to it, namely the
symmetric algebra

\mathcal{S}(I)
. With the presentation
\varphi
above, recall that
\mathcal{S}(I)\cong R[y_{1},\ldots,y_{n+1}]/\mathcal{L}
where
\mathcal{L}
is the ideal of entries
\mathcal{L}=([y_{1},\ldots,y_{n+1}]\cdot\varphi)
. Moreover, notice that the ideal of entries of the matrix product
[y_{1}\ldots y_{n}]\cdot\delta_{1}(\underline{x})
agrees with
I_{2}(\psi)

where

(2.1)

\psi=\begin{bmatrix}x_{1}&\ldots&x_{n}\\
y_{1}&\ldots&y_{n}\end{bmatrix}

and so
\mathcal{L}=I_{2}(\psi)+(h)
, where
h
is the product of
[y_{1}\ldots y_{n+1}]
and the last column of
\varphi
.

Writing
\mathcal{J}
to denote the defining ideal of the Rees ring
\mathcal{R}(I)
as in the introduction, recall that there is a natural epimorphism
\mathcal{S}(I)\rightarrow\mathcal{R}(I)
. As such,
\mathcal{L}\subseteq\mathcal{J}
and we will make use of this containment to compare these two ideals.

Proposition 2.5
.

With
\mathcal{J}
the defining ideal of
\mathcal{R}(I)
and
\mathcal{L}
as above, we have
\mathcal{J}=\mathcal{L}:\mathfrak{m}^{\infty}
.

Proof.

As
R
is a domain, we have that
\mathcal{R}(I)
is a domain and so
\mathcal{J}
is a prime ideal. With this and noting that
\mathfrak{m}\nsubseteq\mathcal{J}
and
\mathcal{L}\subseteq\mathcal{J}
, it is clear that
\mathcal{L}:\mathfrak{m}^{\infty}\subseteq\mathcal{J}
. For the reverse containment, notice that for any prime ideal
\mathfrak{p}\in\mathop{\rm Spec}(R)\setminus\{\mathfrak{m}\}
, we have
I_{\mathfrak{p}}=(f,g)_{\mathfrak{p}}
. As noted,
f,g
is a regular sequence, hence
(f,g)_{\mathfrak{p}}
is of linear type. Thus we see that
\mathcal{L}_{\mathfrak{p}}=\mathcal{J}_{\mathfrak{p}}
for any such prime
\mathfrak{p}
, and so the quotient
\mathcal{A}=\mathcal{J}/\mathcal{L}
is supported only at
\mathfrak{m}
. Thus
\mathcal{A}
is annihilated by some power of
\mathfrak{m}
, which shows that
\mathcal{J}\subseteq\mathcal{L}:\mathfrak{m}^{\infty}
.
∎

With the description of
\mathcal{J}
above, we note that it will be more beneficial to describe this saturation as colon ideal for a specific power of
\mathfrak{m}
. First however, we make a few observations on the symmetric algebra
\mathcal{S}(I)
.

Proposition 2.6
.

With the assumptions of
2.2
, we have
\mathop{\rm dim}\mathcal{S}(I)=n+1
. In particular,
\mathop{\rm ht}\mathcal{L}=n
.

Proof.

We make use of the well-known formula of Huneke and Rossi
[
14
, 2.6]
. Recall that

\mathop{\rm dim}\mathcal{S}(I)=\sup\big\{\mu(I_{\mathfrak{p}})+\mathop{\rm dim}R/\mathfrak{p}\hskip 2.84526pt\big|\hskip 2.84526pt\mathfrak{p}\in\mathop{\rm Spec}(R)\big\}

where
\mu(-)
denotes minimal number of generators. As such, we compute the value of
\mu(I_{\mathfrak{p}})+\mathop{\rm dim}R/\mathfrak{p}
in a few cases. If
\mathfrak{p}\nsupseteq I
, then
I_{\mathfrak{p}}
is the unit ideal and so
\mu(I_{\mathfrak{p}})+\mathop{\rm dim}R/\mathfrak{p}\leq n+1
. Now suppose that
\mathfrak{p}\supseteq I
, and note that then
\mathop{\rm ht}\mathfrak{p}\geq 2
. If
\mathfrak{p}\neq\mathfrak{m}
, then as noted in the proof of
2.5
, we have
I_{\mathfrak{p}}=(f,g)_{\mathfrak{p}}
, hence
\mu(I_{\mathfrak{p}})+\mathop{\rm dim}R/\mathfrak{p}\leq n
. Lastly, if
\mathfrak{p}=\mathfrak{m}
, then it is clear that
\mu(I_{\mathfrak{p}})+\mathop{\rm dim}R/\mathfrak{p}=n+1
. With all cases considered, it follows that
\mathop{\rm dim}\mathcal{S}(I)=n+1
as claimed. The second statement then follows immediately.
∎

As noted in
2.4
, we have that
\mathcal{S}(I)\cong R[y_{1},\ldots,y_{n+1}]/\mathcal{L}
where
\mathcal{L}=I_{2}(\psi)+(h)
. We adopt the bigrading on
R[y_{1},\ldots,y_{n+1}]
given by
\mathop{\rm bideg}x_{i}=(1,0)
and
\mathop{\rm bideg}y_{i}=(0,1)
throughout. With this, we now produce a minimal bigraded free resolution of
\mathcal{S}(I)
.

Proposition 2.7
.

Writing
B=R[y_{1},\ldots,y_{n+1}]
, the symmetric algebra
\mathcal{S}(I)
has a minimal bigraded free
B
-resolution

F_{\scalebox{0.5}{$\bullet$}}:\,0\rightarrow F_{n}\rightarrow\cdots\rightarrow F_{1}\rightarrow F_{0}

where

F_{n}=B^{n-1}(-d-n+2,-n),\quad F_{i}=\begin{array}[]{c}B^{\binom{n}{i}(i-1)}(-d-i+2,-i)\\
\oplus\\
B^{\binom{n}{i+1}i}(-i,-i)\end{array},\quad F_{1}=\begin{array}[]{c}B(-d+1,-1)\\
\oplus\\
B^{\binom{n}{2}}(-1,-1)\end{array},\quad F_{0}=B

for
2\leq i\leq n-1
. In particular,
\mathcal{S}(I)
is Cohen-Macaulay.

Proof.

First note that since
\psi
is a matrix of indeterminates, it is well known that
I_{2}(\psi)
is a prime ideal of height
n-1
. Since
\mathop{\rm ht}\mathcal{L}=n
by
2.6
, it follows that
h\notin I_{2}(\psi)
, and so
h
is regular modulo this ideal of minors. As such, there is a bigraded short exact sequence

(2.2)

0\rightarrow B/I_{2}(\psi)(-d+1,-1)\overset{\cdot h}{\longrightarrow}B/I_{2}(\psi)\rightarrow\mathcal{S}(I)\rightarrow 0.

As
I_{2}(\psi)
has generic height, we have that
B/I_{2}(\psi)
is resolved by the Eagon-Northcott complex
[
7
, A2.10]
. Moreover, multiplication by
h
lifts to a morphism of complexes from the Eagon-Northcott complex to itself. With this, applying the mapping cone construction within (
2.2
) gives the bigraded free resolution of
\mathcal{S}(I)
above. Moreover, from the bidegree shifts involved, it follows that this is a minimal free resolution. Lastly, since
\mathop{\rm ht}\mathcal{L}=n
by
2.6
, it follows that
\mathcal{S}(I)
is Cohen-Macaulay by the Auslander-Buchsbaum formula
[
7
, 19.9]
.
∎

We now describe the saturation
\mathcal{J}=\mathcal{L}:\mathfrak{m}^{\infty}
as a colon ideal for a particular power of
\mathfrak{m}
. We make use of the tools introduced in
[
17
]
to bound the generation degree of certain local cohomology modules. First however, we must briefly recall some notation and terminology of
[
17
]
. For
S
a nonnegatively graded ring with
S_{0}
local and
M
an
S
-module, write
b_{0}(M)=\inf\{\,j\,|\,S(\bigoplus_{i\leq j}M_{i})=M\}
to denote the
maximal generator degree
of
M
, and
a(M)=\sup\{\,i\,|\,[H_{\mathfrak{M}}^{\mathop{\rm dim}S}(M)]_{i}\neq 0\}
to denote the
a-invariant
of

, where
\mathfrak{M}
denotes the homogeneous maximal ideal of
S
.

Proposition 2.8
.

With the assumptions of
2.2
, we have
\mathcal{J}=\mathcal{L}:\mathfrak{m}^{d-1}
.

Proof.

Recall that there is an induced epimorphism
\mathcal{S}(I)\rightarrow\mathcal{R}(I)
and notice that, following
2.5
, the kernel of this map is precisely the local cohomology module
\mathcal{A}=H_{\mathfrak{m}}^{0}(\mathcal{S}(I))
. Moreover, notice that
\mathcal{A}
is naturally bigraded, where
\mathop{\rm bideg}x_{i}=(1,0)
and
\mathop{\rm bideg}y_{i}=(0,1)
as before. Hence for any fixed
q
,
we have

\mathcal{A}_{(*,q)}=\bigoplus_{p}\mathcal{A}_{(p,q)}\cong H_{\mathfrak{m}}^{0}(\mathcal{S}_{q}(I)).

As
\mathcal{A}
lives in finitely many degrees, we claim that
\mathcal{A}
vanishes past degree
d-2
in the first component of the bigrading, i.e. that
\mathcal{A}_{(p,q)}=0
for any
q
and
p>d-2
.

With the
B
-resolution
F_{\scalebox{0.5}{$\bullet$}}
of
\mathcal{S}(I)
given in
2.7
, taking the graded strand in degree
(*,q)
yields a graded
R
-resolution of
\mathcal{S}_{q}(I)
. With this and
[
17
, 3.8]
, it follows that
\mathcal{A}_{(p,q)}=0
for all
p>b_{0}([F_{n}]_{(*,q)})+a(R)
, where
[F_{n}]_{(*,q)}
is the free
R
-module
[F_{n}]_{(*,q)}=\bigoplus_{p}[F_{n}]_{(p,q)}
. From
2.7
it follows that
b_{0}([F_{n}]_{(*,q)})\leq d+n-2
. Additionally, since
R
is a standard graded polynomial ring in
n
variables, we have that
a(R)=-n

[
2
, 3.6.15]
. Hence
\mathcal{A}_{(p,q)}=0
for any
q
and
p>d-2
, as claimed. Thus it follows that
\mathfrak{m}^{d-1}\mathcal{A}=0
and so
\mathfrak{m}^{d-1}\mathcal{J}\subseteq\mathcal{L}
. Thus we have
\mathcal{J}\subseteq\mathcal{L}:\mathfrak{m}^{d-1}
, and the reverse containment follows from
2.5
.
∎

3.
Approximations of Rees algebras

In this section, we approximate the Rees ring
\mathcal{R}(I)
, in a sense, by employing the so-called
method of divisors
, as introduced in
[
15
]
. We map onto
\mathcal{R}(I)
with a closely related algebra, and study the kernel of this map. We then aim to lift a generating set of this kernel to generators of
\mathcal{J}
.

Consider the subideal
f\mathfrak{m}=(fx_{1},\ldots,fx_{n})
, and notice that we may factor the natural epimorphism
R[y_{1},\ldots,y_{n+1}]\rightarrow\mathcal{R}(I)=R[fx_{1}t,\ldots,fx_{n}t,gt]
, as in the introduction, through the Rees ring of this ideal. Indeed, we have the following commutative diagram

(3.1)

\textstyle{R[y_{1},\ldots,y_{n+1}]\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\textstyle{\mathcal{R}(I)}
\textstyle{\mathcal{R}(f\mathfrak{m})[y_{n+1}]\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}

where the horizontal map is the natural map above. Moreover, the leftmost map sends
y_{i}\mapsto fx_{i}t
for
1\leq i\leq n

4.
Downgraded sequences

In this section, we present an adaptation of the method of
downgraded sequences
given by Hassanzadeh and Simis in
[
9
]
. Whereas the relation between such a sequence and the defining ideal has been studied in greater generality
[
9
, §4]
, we present this algorithm from the specific viewpoint when the underlying Cremona support is the identity. With this, our approach throughout is remarkably similar to that of
[
32
, §4]
and this particular perspective will be of use in the following section, when it is shown that the defining ideal coincides with the ideal of a full downgraded sequence. Before we proceed with the construction of downgraded sequences, we recall some previous notation from
Section

2
.

Notation 4.1
.

For any bihomogeneous polynomial
p\in R[y_{1},\ldots,y_{n+1}]
, write
\partial p
to denote a column of bihomogeneous elements, of the same bidegree, such that

[x_{1}\ldots x_{n}]\cdot\partial p=p.

As a convention, we take
\partial p=0
if
p=0
.

As noted in the observation following
2.3
, there is generally not a unique choice for
\partial p
. Despite this non-uniqueness, any choice will suffice our purpose. Moreover, the ideals obtained from the following process will be shown to be independent of such a choice.

Definition 4.2
.

With the notation above and the assumptions of
2.2
, we recursively define a sequence of polynomials and ideals. For the initial pair, set
h_{1}=h
and
\mathcal{J}_{1}=\mathcal{L}
, as in
2.4
. For
1\leq i\leq d
, suppose that
(\mathcal{J}_{1},h_{1}),\ldots,(\mathcal{J}_{i-1},h_{i-1})
have been constructed. To construct the
i
th pair, set
h_{i}=[y_{1}\ldots y_{n}]\cdot\partial h_{i-1}
and let
\mathcal{J}_{i}=\mathcal{J}_{i-1}+(h_{i})
. The sequence
h_{1},\ldots,h_{i}
is called an
i
th
downgraded sequence
of
h
.

As noted, there are multiple choices for
\partial h_{i}
at each step, hence a downgraded sequence is not unique in general. However, we will show that each ideal
\mathcal{J}_{i}=I_{2}(\psi)+(h_{1},\ldots,h_{i})
is well defined. First however, we make a brief observation about the polynomials of a downgraded sequence.

Proposition 4.3
.

For
h_{1},\ldots,h_{d}
a downgraded sequence as above, we have
h_{i}\neq 0
for all
1\leq i\leq d
. Moreover, each
h_{i}
is bihomogeneous of bidegree
(d-i,i)
.

Proof.

As the process of
4.2
is recursive, we prove the first statement by induction. The second statement then follows immediately, by construction. Recall that
h=h_{1}
is a nonzero polynomial of bidegree
(d-1,1)
, as noted in
2.4
and from the presentation of
I
in
2.3
.

Now suppose, for a contradiction, that
h_{i}=0
for some
i\geq 2
. As such, we may take
i
to be minimal, in the sense that
h_{j}\neq 0
for
j<i
. Since
h_{i}=[y_{1}\ldots y_{n}]\cdot\partial h_{i-1}
and
h_{i}=0
, we see that
\partial h_{i-1}
is a syzygy on
y_{1},\ldots,y_{n}
. As this is a regular sequence,
\partial h_{i-1}
belongs to the span of its Koszul syzygies. With this, and since
h_{i-1}=[x_{1}\ldots x_{n}]\cdot\partial h_{i-1}
, it then follows that
h_{i-1}\in I_{2}(\psi)
.

If
i=2
, this is a contradiction as it was shown in the proof of
2.6
that
h_{1}=h\notin I_{2}(\psi)
, hence we must have
i\geq 3
here. Thus we have
h_{i-1}=[y_{1}\ldots y_{n}]\cdot\partial h_{i-2}
and, since
h_{i-1}\in I_{2}(\psi)
, it follows that
\partial h_{i-2}
is contained in the span of Koszul syzygies on
x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}
. However, then
h_{i-2}=[x_{1}\ldots x_{n}]\cdot\partial h_{i-2}=0
, which is a contradiction as
i
was taken to be smallest.
∎

Proposition 4.4
.

The ideals
\mathcal{J}_{1},\ldots,\mathcal{J}_{d}
of
4.2
are well defined.

Proof.

We proceed by induction, noting that the initial ideal
\mathcal{J}_{1}=\mathcal{L}=I_{2}(\psi)+(h_{1})
is well defined since this is the ideal defining
\mathcal{S}(I)
, as noted in
2.4
. Now suppose that
2\leq i\leq d
and the ideals
\mathcal{J}_{1},\ldots,\mathcal{J}_{i-1}
have been shown to be unique. Letting
\partial h_{i-1}
and
\partial h_{i-1}^{\prime}
denote two columns with

[x_{1}\ldots x_{n}]\cdot\partial h_{i-1}=h_{i-1}=[x_{1}\ldots x_{n}]\cdot\partial h_{i-1}^{\prime}

as in
4.2
, we must show that
\mathcal{J}_{i-1}+(h_{i})=\mathcal{J}_{i-1}+(h_{i}^{\prime})
, where
h_{i}=[y_{1}\ldots y_{n}]\cdot\partial h_{i-1}
and
h_{i}^{\prime}=[y_{1}\ldots y_{n}]\cdot\partial h_{i-1}^{\prime}
.

From the expression above, we see that
\partial h_{i-1}-\partial h_{i-1}^{\prime}
is a syzygy on
x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}
. Since this is a regular sequence,
\partial h_{i-1}-\partial h_{i-1}^{\prime}
belongs to the span of its Koszul syzygies. As such, it follows that
h_{i}=[y_{1}\ldots y_{n}]\cdot\partial h_{i-1}
and
h_{i}^{\prime}=[y_{1}\ldots y_{n}]\cdot\partial h_{i-1}^{\prime}
differ by an element of
I_{2}(\psi)
. Now since
I_{2}(\psi)\subseteq\mathcal{J}_{i-1}
, we have that
\mathcal{J}_{i-1}+(h_{i})=\mathcal{J}_{i-1}+(h_{i}^{\prime})
, and the claim follows.
∎

We note that the process of downgraded sequences is relatively simple in practice, and this algorithm may be easily implemented into a computer algebra system, such as
Macaulay2

[

]

Example 4.5
.

Let

\mathfrak{m}=(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3})

\begin{array}[]{ccccc}h_{1}=x_{2}^{2}y_{2}-x_{1}^{2}y_{4},&&h_{2}=x_{2}y_{2}^{2}-x_{1}y_{1}y_{4},&&h_{3}=y_{2}^{2}-y_{1}^{2}y_{4}.\end{array}

Proposition 4.6
.

For

We proceed by induction, noting that the assertion is clear when

the next downgraded polynomial. Indeed, modulo

(4.1)

\overline{x_{j}h_{i}}=[\overline{x_{j}y_{1}}\ldots\overline{x_{j}y_{n}}]\cdot\overline{\partial h_{i-1}}=[\overline{x_{1}y_{j}}\ldots\overline{x_{n}y_{j}}]\cdot\overline{\partial h_{i-1}}=\overline{y_{j}h_{i-1}},

Notice that

Theorem 4.7

With the assumptions of
2.2

\mathcal{J}_{d}
the ideal of the full downgraded sequence of

\overline{\mathcal{J}_{d}}=\frac{\overline{h}\overline{\mathcal{K}}^{d-1}}{\overline{x_{n}}^{d-1}}

We note first that

\overline{h_{1}}\overline{(x_{n},y_{n})}^{d-1}=\overline{x_{n}}^{d-1}\overline{(h_{1},\ldots,h_{d})}

\overline{h_{1}}\overline{x_{n}^{d-i}y_{n}^{i-1}}=\overline{x_{n}}^{d-1}\overline{h_{i}}

\overline{h_{1}}\overline{y_{n}}^{i-1}=\overline{x_{n}}^{i-1}\overline{h_{i}}

\overline{h_{1}y_{n}}=[\overline{y_{n}x_{1}}\ldots\overline{y_{n}x_{n}}]\cdot\overline{\partial h_{1}}=[\overline{y_{1}x_{n}}\ldots\overline{y_{n}x_{n}}]\cdot\overline{\partial h_{1}}=\overline{x_{n}h_{2}}

We are finished if

\overline{h_{1}}\overline{y_{n}}^{i-1}=\overline{x_{n}}^{i-1}\overline{h_{i}}
, we note that

\overline{h_{1}}\overline{y_{n}}^{i-1}=\overline{h_{1}}\overline{y_{n}}^{i-2}\cdot\overline{y_{n}}=\overline{x_{n}}^{i-2}\overline{h_{i-1}}\cdot\overline{y_{n}}=\overline{x_{n}}^{i-2}\cdot\overline{h_{i-1}}\overline{y_{n}},

by applying the induction hypothesis. Hence the proof will be complete once it has been shown that
\overline{h_{i-1}}\overline{y_{n}}=\overline{x_{n}}\overline{h_{i}}
. However, this follows exactly as above. Since

h_{i-1}=[x_{1}\ldots x_{n}]\cdot\partial h_{i-1}
and
h_{i}=[y_{1}\ldots y_{n}]\cdot\partial h_{i-1}
, modulo
I_{2}(\psi)
we have

\overline{h_{i-1}y_{n}}=[\overline{y_{n}x_{1}}\ldots\overline{y_{n}x_{n}}]\cdot\overline{\partial h_{i-1}}=[\overline{y_{1}x_{n}}\ldots\overline{y_{n}x_{n}}]\cdot\overline{\partial h_{i-1}}=\overline{x_{n}h_{i}}

and the claim follows.
∎

5.
Implicitization and Cohen-Macaulayness

We now show that the defining ideal
\mathcal{J}
of the Rees ring
\mathcal{R}(I)
coincides with the ideal
\mathcal{J}_{d}
of the fully downgraded sequence of
Section

4
. The remainder of the section is then devoted to the Cohen-Macaulay property of
\mathcal{R}(I)
.

Theorem 5.1
.

With the assumptions of
2.2
, the defining ideal of
\mathcal{R}(I)
is
\mathcal{J}=I_{2}(\psi)+(h_{1},\ldots,h_{d})
.

Proof.

From the containments
I_{2}(\psi)\subseteq\mathcal{J}_{d}\subseteq\mathcal{J}
, it suffices to show that
\overline{\mathcal{J}_{d}}=\overline{\mathcal{J}}
. With this, by
3.6
and
4.7
, it is then enough to show that
\overline{\mathcal{K}}^{d-1}=\overline{\mathcal{K}}^{(d-1)}
. Recall from
3.5
that
\overline{\mathcal{K}}
is generically a complete intersection and is a strongly Cohen-Macaulay ideal of height one. Thus by
[
27
, 3.4]
, it is enough to show that
\overline{\mathcal{K}}_{\mathfrak{p}}
is a principal
A_{\mathfrak{p}}
-ideal for any prime
A
-ideal
\mathfrak{p}
with
\mathop{\rm ht}\mathfrak{p}=2
, as then
\overline{\mathcal{K}}^{i}=\overline{\mathcal{K}}^{(i)}
for all
i\geq 1
.

First note that if
\mathfrak{p}\nsupseteq\overline{\mathfrak{m}}
, we have
\overline{\mathcal{K}}_{\mathfrak{p}}=(\overline{x_{n}})_{\mathfrak{p}}:A_{\mathfrak{p}}=(\overline{x_{n}})_{\mathfrak{p}}
by
3.4
. Hence we may assume that
\mathfrak{p}
is a prime
A
-ideal of height two with
\mathfrak{p}\supseteq\overline{\mathfrak{m}}=\overline{(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})}
. In this case, there must exist a
\overline{y_{i}}
for
1\leq i\leq n
such that
\overline{y_{i}}\notin\mathfrak{p}
. If not, then
\overline{(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n},y_{1},\ldots,y_{n})}\subseteq\mathfrak{p}
, which is impossible as
\mathop{\rm ht}\mathfrak{p}=2
and the former ideal has height
2n-(n-1)=n+1\geq 3
(as
n\geq\mathop{\rm ht}I=2
). Now since
\overline{y_{i}}\notin\mathfrak{p}
becomes a unit locally in
A_{\mathfrak{p}}
, the equation
\overline{x_{i}y_{n}}=\overline{x_{n}y_{i}}
shows that
\overline{\mathcal{K}}_{\mathfrak{p}}=\overline{(x_{n},y_{n})}_{\mathfrak{p}}=(\overline{y_{n}})_{\mathfrak{p}}
, which completes the proof.
∎

Corollary 5.2
.

With the assumptions of
2.2
, the fiber ring of
I
is
\mathcal{F}(I)\cong k[y_{1},\ldots,y_{n+1}]/(h_{d})
. In particular,
\mathcal{F}(I)
is Cohen-Macaulay.

Proof.

From
5.1
, the defining ideal of
\mathcal{R}(I)
is
\mathcal{J}=I_{2}(\psi)+(h_{1},\ldots,h_{d})
. We note that
I_{2}(\psi)
is generated in bidegree
(1,1)
and each
h_{i}
is nonzero of bidegree
(d-i,i)
by
4.3
. Hence from bidegree considerations, we have
\mathcal{J}+\mathfrak{m}=(h_{d})+\mathfrak{m}
, and so
\mathcal{F}(I)\cong R[y_{1},\ldots,y_{n+1}]/(\mathcal{J}+\mathfrak{m})\cong k[y_{1},\ldots,y_{n+1}]/(h_{d})
. The Cohen-Macaulayness is clear since
\mathcal{F}(I)
is a hypersurface ring.
∎

Remark 5.3
.

As noted, the de Jonquières transformation
\mathfrak{J}:\mathbb{P}^{n-1}\dashrightarrow\mathbb{P}^{n}
given by
fx_{1},\ldots,fx_{n},g
is birational onto its image, which is a hypersurface in
\mathbb{P}^{n}
. Following
5.2
, this hypersurface is
V(h_{d})
.

We end this section by describing the Cohen-Macaulayness of
\mathcal{R}(I)
. First however, we make a brief observation about the
A
-ideal
\overline{\mathcal{K}}
and its symmetric powers.

Lemma 5.4
.

The
A
-ideal
\overline{\mathcal{K}}
is of linear type. In particular,
\mathcal{S}_{i}(\overline{\mathcal{K}})\cong\overline{\mathcal{K}}^{i}
for all
i
.

Proof.

Recall that
\overline{\mathcal{K}}
is a Cohen-Macaulay ideal of height one and generically a complete intersection by
3.3
and
3.5
. With this, and noting that
\overline{\mathcal{K}}=\overline{(x_{n},y_{n})}
, it follows that
\mu(\overline{\mathcal{K}}_{\mathfrak{p}})\leq\mathop{\rm dim}A_{\mathfrak{p}}
for all
\mathfrak{p}\in\mathop{\rm Spec}(A)
, where
\mu(-)
denotes minimal number of generators. Furthermore, since
\overline{\mathcal{K}}
is strongly Cohen-Macaulay by
3.5
, it follows that
\overline{\mathcal{K}}
is of linear type by
[
10
, 2.6]
.
∎

With
Lemma

5.4
, we may now address the Cohen-Macaulayness of
\mathcal{R}(I)
. From
4.7
and the proof of
5.1
, we have an isomorphism
\overline{\mathcal{J}}\cong\overline{\mathcal{K}}^{d-1}
, hence the Cohen-Macaulayness of
\mathcal{R}(I)
is determined by when
\overline{\mathcal{K}}^{d-1}
is a Cohen-Macaulay ideal. Recall that a Noetherian local ring
S
is said to be
almost
Cohen-Macaulay if
\mathop{\rm dim}S-\mathop{\rm depth}S\leq 1
.

Theorem 5.5
.

With the assumptions of
2.2
,
\mathcal{R}(I)
is almost Cohen-Macaulay, and is Cohen-Macaulay if and only if
d\leq n
.

Proof.

Recall that
\mathcal{K}=I_{2}(\psi)+(x_{n},y_{n})
for
\psi
as in (
2.1
). Thus by
[
7
, A2.14]
, there is an isomorphism
\overline{\mathcal{K}}\cong M
, where
M=\mathop{\rm coker}\psi
. Since
\psi
consists of indeterminate entries, the symmetric powers
\mathcal{S}_{i}(M)
are resolved by the family of Eagon-Northcott complexes
[
7
, A2.10]
. Moreover, by
Lemma

5.4
we have that
\overline{\mathcal{K}}^{i}\cong\mathcal{S}_{i}(\overline{\mathcal{K}})\cong\mathcal{S}_{i}(M)
for all
i\geq 0
.

With this, if
i\leq n-1
, then
\overline{\mathcal{K}}^{i}\cong\mathcal{S}_{i}(M)
and has a minimal free resolution of length
n-1
, and is therefore a maximal Cohen-Macaulay
A
-module
[
7
, A2.13, A2.14a]
. Moreover, if
i\geq n
, then
\overline{\mathcal{K}}^{i}\cong\mathcal{S}_{i}(M)
has a minimal free resolution of length
n

[
7
, A2.10, A2.14b]
. Thus from the Auslander-Buchsbaum formula
[
7
, 19.9]
, the depth of each power, as a module, is

(5.1)

\mathop{\rm depth}\overline{\mathcal{K}}^{i}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}n+2&\text{if $0\leq i\leq n-1$,}\\[4.30554pt]
n+1&\text{if $i\geq n$.}\end{array}\right.

With (
5.1
) and the isomorphism
\overline{\mathcal{J}}\cong\overline{\mathcal{K}}^{d-1}
, comparing depths along the short exact sequence

0\rightarrow\overline{\mathcal{J}}\rightarrow A\rightarrow\mathcal{R}(I)\rightarrow 0

shows that

(5.2)

\mathop{\rm depth}\mathcal{R}(I)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}n+1&\text{if $0\leq d-1\leq n-1$,}\\[4.30554pt]
n&\text{if $d-1\geq n$.}\end{array}\right.

As
\mathop{\rm dim}\mathcal{R}(I)=n+1
, it follows that
\mathcal{R}(I)
is almost Cohen-Macaulay and is Cohen-Macaulay if and only if
d-1\leq n-1
, i.e.
d\leq n
.
∎

6.
Generalized de Jonquières parameterizations

With our treatment of the de Jonquières transformations of
[
9
]
concluded, we now consider the
generalized
de Jonquières transformations
\mathfrak{J}:\,\mathbb{P}^{n}\dashrightarrow\mathbb{P}^{n}
introduced and studied by Ramos and Simis in
[
25
]
. In the case of identity Cremona support, the defining ideal of the Rees ring is known and may be realized as the ideal of a downgraded sequence
[
25
, 2.6]
. However, methods different from those of the previous sections are used to show generation. Moreover, the Cohen-Macaulayness of the Rees ring was left as a question in this instance
[
25
, 2.10]
. The objective of this section is to show that the previous tools generalize to this new setting, and to determine the Cohen-Macaulayness of the Rees algebra of the ideal of a generalized de Jonquières transformation, and settle a conjecture of Ramos and Simis
[
25
, 2.10]
.

Similar to the notion of a de Jonquières transformation in
2.1
, a generalized de Jonquières transformation is constructed from a supporting Cremona map. Let
R^{\prime}=k[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]
denote the coordinate ring of
\mathbb{P}^{n-1}
, and let
\mathfrak{C}:\,\mathbb{P}^{n-1}\dashrightarrow\mathbb{P}^{n-1}
be a Cremona transformation defined by homogeneous forms
g_{1},\ldots,g_{n}
of degree
\mathfrak{d}\geq 1
in
R^{\prime}
.

Definition 6.1

(
[
25
]
)
.

With the Cremona map
\mathfrak{C}:\,\mathbb{P}^{n-1}\dashrightarrow\mathbb{P}^{n-1}
above, let
f,g\in R=k[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n+1}]
be two homogeneous polynomials with
\deg g=\deg f+\mathfrak{d}
. Moreover, assume that
f
and
g
are
x_{n+1}
-monoids with at least one involving
x_{n+1}
, and that
\gcd(f,g)=1
. The rational map
\mathfrak{J}:\,\mathbb{P}^{n}\dashrightarrow\mathbb{P}^{n}
defined by
fg_{1},\ldots,fg_{n},g
will be called a
generalized de Jonquières transformation
, with Cremona support
\mathfrak{C}
.

Here the terminology that
f
and
g
are
x_{n+1}
-
monoids
simply means that they are polynomials of degree one in
x_{n+1}
, i.e. as polynomials in
R[x_{n+1}]
regraded appropriately. As such, we may write

(6.1)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}f=f_{0}+f_{1}x_{n+1},\\[4.30554pt]
g=g_{0}+g_{1}x_{n+1},\end{array}\right.

for homogeneous polynomials
f_{0},f_{1},g_{0},g_{1}\in R^{\prime}
. We note that this condition guarantees that
\mathfrak{J}
is birational
[
25
, 1.6]
. In particular, a generalized de Jonquières transformation is itself a Cremona transformation, supported by
\mathfrak{C}
in one dimension lower.

With the conventions above, we now state the setting for the duration of this section, noting that it agrees with the situation of
[
25
, §3.2]
.

Setting 6.2

(
[
25
]
)
.

Let
R=k[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n+1}]
with subring
R^{\prime}=k[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]
and
\mathfrak{m}=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})
its homogeneous maximal ideal. Let
f
and
g
be
x_{n+1}
-monoids, at least one of which involves
x_{n+1}
, with
\gcd(f,g)=1
. Assume that
\deg g=d
and
\deg f=d-1
for some
d\geq 2
, and let
I=(f\mathfrak{m},g)=(fx_{1},\ldots,fx_{n},g)
.

With the assumption that
g
is
x_{n+1}
-monoidal of degree
d\geq 2
, from (
6.1
) it follows that
g\in\mathfrak{m}^{d-1}
. With this observation, one may produce a presentation of
I
from the mapping cone construction.

Proposition 6.3

(
[
25
, 2.1]
)
.

With respect to the generating set
\{fx_{1},\ldots,fx_{n},g\}
, the ideal
I
may be presented by

R^{\binom{n}{2}+1}\overset{\varphi}{\longrightarrow}R^{n+1}\rightarrow I\rightarrow 0

where

\varphi=\left(\begin{array}[]{ccc|c}&&&\\
&\delta_{1}(\underline{x}^{\prime})&&\partial g\\
&&&\\
\hline\cr 0&\cdots&0&-f\end{array}\right)

where
\delta_{1}(\underline{x}^{\prime})
is the first Koszul differential on
\underline{x}^{\prime}=x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}
, and
\partial g
is a column of homogeneous elements, of the same degree, with
[x_{1}\ldots x_{n}]\cdot\partial g=g
.

We note that the presentation of the ideal
I
is nearly identical to the presentation of the ideal of a de Jonquières transformation in
2.3
(
[
9
, 2.2]
). However, we note that the fact that
g\in\mathfrak{m}R
is crucial to this construction, so that
\partial g
exists.

Remark 6.4
.

As before, we may use the presentation matrix
\varphi
to describe the symmetric algebra of

. Indeed, the symmetric algebra is
\mathcal{S}(I)\cong R[y_{1},\ldots,y_{n+1}]/\mathcal{L}
, where
\mathcal{L}=([y_{1}\ldots y_{n+1}]\cdot\varphi)
. Noting that
\delta_{1}(\underline{x}^{\prime})
is the first Koszul differential on

, similar to the observation in
2.4
it follows that

where
\psi
is as before in (

) and

is the product of

and the last column of

.

Notice that

, yet the matrix
\psi
only involves the indeterminates

. As such, the constructions presented here will be analogous, and in most cases identical, to those of the previous sections. Moreover, as

, the ideal
\mathfrak{m}=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})
will play a similar role as before, but we must proceed with caution, as this is not the maximal
R
-ideal. Additionally, we must take care as the polynomial

does involve the last indeterminate

. In particular, from bidegree considerations and (
6.1
), we note that
h\in\mathfrak{m}^{d-2}
yet it has bidegree
(d-1,1)
.

Remark 6.5
.

We may employ the method of downgraded sequences as in
4.2
, where
\partial-
denotes a column as in
4.1
with respect to
x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}
, the generators of
\mathfrak{m}
. As noted,

and so this sequence has only

to denote the ideal

As we will see, the fact that the length of a full downgraded sequence is one less than the degree of

Example 6.6
.

Let

Remark 6.7

With the assumptions of

6.1.

Proposition 6.10

With the assumptions of

\overline{\mathcal{J}}=\frac{\overline{h}\overline{\mathcal{K}}^{d-2}}{\overline{x_{n}}^{d-2}}

, as noted in

\overline{\mathcal{J}}\nsubseteq\overline{\mathfrak{m}}
, as
\mathfrak{m}
is not the maximal
R
-ideal here. However, this is clear as both ideals are prime of height one and
\overline{\mathcal{J}}\neq\overline{\mathfrak{m}}
. Then one uses that the powers and symbolic powers of
\overline{\mathcal{K}}
agree, as noted in
6.9
and the proof of
5.1
.
∎

With the description of
\overline{\mathcal{J}}
in
6.10
, we may now complete the objective of this section and determine the Cohen-Macaulayness of
\mathcal{R}(I)
. From
[
25
, 2.9]
it is known that
\mathcal{R}(I)
is almost Cohen-Macaulay, but left as a conjecture as to when
\mathcal{R}(I)
is Cohen-Macaulay
[
25
, 2.10]
. As such, we conclude this section and the article by answering this question, as follows. Once again, as noted in
6.9
, all properties of
\overline{\mathcal{K}}
hold as in the previous sections, despite the slightly different assumptions of
6.2
.

Theorem 6.11
.

With the assumptions of
6.2
,
\mathcal{R}(I)
is almost Cohen-Macaulay, and is Cohen-Macaulay if and only if
d\leq n+1
.

Proof.

With the observations of
6.9
, by repeating the argument of the proof of
5.5
we see that

(6.2)

\mathop{\rm depth}\overline{\mathcal{K}}^{i}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}n+3&\text{if $0\leq i\leq n-1$,}\\[4.30554pt]
n+2&\text{if $i\geq n$.}\end{array}\right.

Here we note that
\mathop{\rm dim}R=n+1
in this setting, hence the difference from (
5.1
) after using the Auslander-Buchsbaum formula. From
6.10
, it follows that there is an isomorphism
\overline{\mathcal{J}}\cong\overline{\mathcal{K}}^{d-2}
. With this and (
6.2
), by comparing depths along the short exact sequence

0\rightarrow\overline{\mathcal{J}}\rightarrow A\rightarrow\mathcal{R}(I)\rightarrow 0,

we have

(6.3)

\mathop{\rm depth}\mathcal{R}(I)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}n+2&\text{if $0\leq d-2\leq n-1$,}\\[4.30554pt]
n+1&\text{if $d-2\geq n$.}\end{array}\right.

Again noting that
\mathop{\rm dim}R=n+1
, we have
\mathop{\rm dim}\mathcal{R}(I)=n+2
. Thus it follows that
\mathcal{R}(I)
is almost Cohen-Macaulay, and is Cohen-Macaulay if and only if
d-2\leq n-1
, i.e.
d\leq n+1
.
∎

As such,
6.11
answers Conjecture 2.10 of
[
25
]
in the affirmative.
