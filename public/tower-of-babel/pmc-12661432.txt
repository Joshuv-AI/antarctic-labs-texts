Polydopamine/chitosan layer-by-layer coating of Bacillus cereus enhances the robustness of live anti-oxidation materials for the treatment of radiation enteritis
Authors: Feng Zhang, Liuchan Zeng, Jinghua Zuo, Yao Tan, Ke Wang, Zhangyu Li, Yunxia Su, Yadan Hu, Ziyuan Chen, Jinyun Hong, Lina Du, Yiguang Jin, Bochuan Yuan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Radiation enteritis (RE) is a common disease following radiotherapy in patients with abdominal and pelvic malignant tumors, but no specific medications are available. Probiotics, as a potential biological antioxidant, have become an emerging live therapeutic for RE; however, their fragile nature in the gastrointestinal tract and colonization resistance hinder their clinical application. Here, Bacillus cereus (BC) was selected among several marketed probiotic strains due to its strong antioxidant ability. A polydopamine (PDA)/chitosan layer-by-layer coating highly enhanced the robustness of BC to obtain live anti-oxidation materials. The physically engineered BC, namely BC@PCS, showed high tolerance to the harsh environments, including ionizing radiation, freeze drying, long-term preservation at room temperature, and gastric acid. More importantly, the coating improved the in vitro/in vivo ROS-scavenging ability of live materials and prolonged their intestinal retention. After BC@PCS was orally administered to the 12-Gy γ-ray abdomen-irradiated mice, the RE symptoms were highly alleviated, rather than those of the BC, PDA/chitosan mixture, and BC/PDA/chitosan mixture groups, involving body weight, intestinal length, tissue histopathology, local ROS clearance, local inflammation, and mucosal permeability. Layer-by-layer coating is a promising approach to enhance the robustness of live materials for disease treatment.

Introduction

Radiation enteritis (RE) is a common disease following the radiotherapy of abdominal, retroperitoneal, or pelvic tumors [1]. Its primary symptoms include nausea, vomiting, abdominal pain, diarrhea, mucosal sloughing, and hematochezia, due to radiation damage to the small intestine, colon, and rectum [2]. Reactive oxygen species (ROS) play a crucial role in RE development, making antioxidation one of the primary strategies for treating RE [3]. Due to the lengthy treatment process for RE, the antioxidants that are effective, sustainable, and safe are essential in addressing this issue [1,3]. Amifostine (Ami), a free radical scavenger, is the only clinical drug used to prevent RE during radiotherapy; however, its administration has a narrow therapeutic window and causes notable hypotension, limiting its clinical use [4]. Given the short lifespan and potential safety concerns of current antioxidants, there is an urgent need to develop more effective, long-lasting, and safer alternatives.

Probiotics therapy is increasingly recognized for their potential in the prevention and treatment of major diseases, such as inflammatory bowel diseases (IBD), metabolic disease, and malignant tumors [5,6]. Specific gut bacteria have been shown to enhance radiation resistance in mice, including those from the Lachnospiraceae and Enterococcaceae families [7]. Previous reports have demonstrated that modified probiotics have the effect of alleviating RE, including Lactobacillus rhamnosus that was coated with chitosan/tannic acid and encapsulated within hydrogel microspheres, the multi-strain probiotics in inulin gels containing Clostridium butyricum, Bifidobacterium adolescentis, and Akkermansia muciniphila (AKK), and the engineered probiotic Escherichia coli overexpressing antioxidant enzymes and curli fibers [[8], [9], [10]]. Although some wild and engineered probiotics showed a certain effect against RE, the RE-specific strains based on ROS-scavenging have not been discovered [[11], [12], [13]]. Bacillus cereus (BC) is a Gram-positive, spore-forming, facultatively anaerobic bacterium [14]. Some BC strains can cause diseases in humans and animals, while others can serve as probiotics to improve enteritis [15,16]. Although it is generally believed that BC can eliminate oxygen in the intestine [17], its ability to protect against oxidative stress by scavenging ROS has never been explored. Given the pivotal role of ROS in RE, a probiotic with enhanced ROS-scavenging capability may demonstrate remarkable efficacy in treating RE, as it can continuously act as a live antioxidant in the gut.

Two additional key factors limiting the therapeutic efficacy of bacteria are the efficiency of oral delivery and the local retention time in the gut. The former affects the number of live bacteria reaching the disease site, while the latter determines the therapeutic time window of live bacteria [[18], [19], [20]]. Both factors significantly impact the efficacy of live bacteria. Although enteric capsules may address the impact of gastric acids on the delivery efficiency of live bacteria in clinical applications, they cannot mitigate the adverse effects of exposure to intestinal bile acids and digestive enzymes [21]. Moreover, enteric capsules may prolong the time for bacteria to transition from a freeze-dried state to an active state, thereby reducing efficacy [22,23]. In recent years, a novel solution strategy has been developed by coating bacteria with safe biomaterials, which can block the inhibitory effects of gastric acids, bile salts, and other factors without affecting bacterial viability [[24], [25], [26]]. Currently, a variety of materials suitable for bacterial surface modification have been discovered, such as polydopamine (PDA), hyaluronic acid, chitosan, and polyethylene glycol [[27], [28], [29], [30]]. Given that adult intestinal flora has been established, exogenous probiotics are often rapidly cleared from the intestine, leading to poor effects [31]. The primary methods for enhancing the retention time of probiotics in the gastrointestinal tract include genetic engineering and surface modification of biomaterials [32]. However, genetic engineering techniques carry a high risk of gene leakage, posing specific safety concerns [33]. Bacterial surface modification technology involves modifying the surface of bacteria using biocompatible polymer materials, enabling them to safely and effectively reach the lesion site [24,33]. In recent years, a series of materials, including inulin gels and PDA, have been developed that can enhance the intestinal retention time and gastrointestinal tolerance of probiotics without affecting their biological activity, thereby achieving the dual benefit of improving both aspects [28,34]. Among them, polydopamine and chitosan, as two common biocompatible materials, have demonstrated their oral safety in previous studies [[35], [36], [37], [38]]. Both chitosan and PDA have good bioadhesive capability. The bioadherence of chitosan results from its cationic nature [39]. PDA is a synthetic material inspired by the adhesive proteins of mussels based on its plentiful catechol groups [40]. PDA has widely been applied to the bioadhesive preparations [41,42]. However, the bacterial coating materials aimed at enhancing the antioxidant capacity of bacteria remain unclear; thus, it is necessary to develop a customized antioxidant coating for bacteria.

Here, five potentially active probiotics were screened for the treatment of RE, yielding a more efficacious strain, B. cereus. It can serve as live anti-oxidation materials, continuously scavenging ROS in the intestine, thereby exhibiting sound therapeutic effects on RE. To improve its oral delivery efficiency and effectiveness, PDA and chitosan were employed for bacterial coating to enhance the robustness of the live anti-oxidation materials. Their physicochemical properties and stability were examined. Subsequently, in vivo experiments were conducted to systematically assess the safety, preventive, and therapeutic effects of live materials on RE, as well as the underlying mechanisms. This study presents a novel therapeutic approach to enhance the robustness of live materials to exert high therapeutic efficiency.

Materials and methods

Materials

Fluorescein isothiocyanate (FITC)-dextran was purchased from Sigma (St. Louis, USA). Diamine oxidase (DAO) activity assay kits, mouse tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α) assay kits, and mouse interleukin-6 (IL-6) detection kits were purchased from Shanghai ELISA Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). Superoxide dismutase (SOD) activity assay kit (WST-8) was purchased from Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute (Nanjing, China). Nutrient broth media, sterile enzyme-free water, DCFH-DA, and FITC were obtained from Beijing Solarbio Technology Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China). FITC-D-Lys was purchased from Xiamen Shengguang Biological Technology Co., Ltd. (Xiamen, China). Applied Biosystems Platinum SYBR Green PremixTM was purchased from Thermo Fisher Technology Co., Ltd. (Waltham, USA). Low viscosity chitosan, dopamine hydrochloride, trihydroxymethylaminomethane hydrochloride, 3-hydroxymethylaminomethane, and rhodamine B were purchased from Beijing Innochem Technology Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China). DAPI staining reagent was purchased from Wuhan Servicebio Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Wuhan, China). SYTO9/PI bacterial fluorescent viability staining agents were obtained from Shanghai Ruiyu Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). Fecal genomic DNA extraction kits and bacterial genomic DNA extraction kits were purchased from Tiangen Biochemical Technology Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China). Ami was provided by Nanjing Luye Pharmaceutical Co., Ltd. (Nanjing, China). All water was deionized and prepared with a pure water instrument.

Preparation and characterization of BC@PDA and BC@PCS

BC was cultivated as above and centrifuged at 3500 rpm for 5 min to obtain the bacterial pellets, that was resuspended in 10 mM Tris-HCl buffer (pH 8.5) containing 0.5 mg/mL dopamine to a final bacterial density of 2 × 108 CFU/mL. A chitosan solution (5 mg/mL) was added to the mixture every 30 min (a total of three times) during the 2-h agitation reaction. After 30 min of the final addition, the reaction suspension was centrifuged, and PDA/chitosan-coated BC (BC@PCS) was obtained. The product was preserved at 4 °C until use. PDA-coated BC (BC@PDA) was also prepared, except that no chitosan was used. The coating materials (PCS) composed of polydopamine and chitosan were prepared in the same way, except that no bacteria were added. The diameters and zeta potentials of BC, BC@PDA, and BC@PCS were measured using a dynamic light scattering instrument (Zetasizer Nano ZS, Malvern Instruments Inc., Malvern City, UK). For zeta potential determination, BC, BC@PDA, and BC@PCS were centrifuged and resuspended with an equal amount of sterile enzyme-free water, and 700 μL was taken for measuring the zeta potential using above analyzer. Before the reaction, BC and PDA were labeled with FITC-D-Lys and rhodamine B, respectively, to obtain labeled BC@PDA and BC@PCS for fluorescence imaging (CLSM 880, Carl Zeiss AG, Oberkochen, Germany). Bacteria or bacteria coated with a 2 % sodium phosphotungstate solution were observed under a transmission electron microscope (TEM, H-7650, Hitachi, Tokyo, Japan).

Detection of bacterial growth

Lactobacillus reuteri (Lr, BNCC 192190) and L. rhamnosus GG (LGG, BNCC 134266) were purchased from Beijing Bena Culture Collection (Beijing, China). Probiotic B. cereus (BC, CMCC 63303) was purchased from Beijing Bio-bw Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China). ‌AKK (ATCC BAA-835) was generously donated by Prof. R Yang from the State Key Laboratory of Pathogen and Biosecurity (Beijing, China), and E. coli Nissle 1917 (EcN) was generously donated by Prof. G Liu from Beijing Institute of Biotechnology.

The Lr and LGG powders were separately dissolved in sterile enzyme-free water and added to de Man, Rogosa, and Sharpe liquid media (MRS, containing 4.83 g of MRS broth per 100 mL). The former was placed in an anaerobic bag for static culture at 37 °C, while the latter was placed in an oscillating incubator for culture at 37 °C and 220 rpm. Like LGG, BC powders were dissolved and cultured in the nutrient broth medium (NB, containing 3.0 g of beef extract, 0.5 g of NaCl, and 1.0 g of peptone per 100 mL) at 30 °C and 220 rpm. EcN suspensions were 5 % inoculated in the Luria-Bertani liquid medium (LB, containing 1.0 g of tryptone, 0.5 g of yeast extract, and 1.0 g of NaCl per 100 mL) and incubated at 37 °C and 220 rpm. AKK suspensions were 1 % inoculated in the modified brain-heart infusion medium (BHI, containing 0.44 g of N-acetylglucosamine, 3.7 g of brain-heart infusion medium, 0.6 g of L-threonine, and 0.5 mg of vancomycin per 100 mL) and incubated in an anaerobic bag at 37 °C. A microbial cell analyzer (Countstar Mira FL Pro, Shanghai Ruiyu Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China) was used for bacterial counting. After cultivation, the bacterial suspensions were centrifuged at 3500 rpm for 5 min. The bacterial precipitates were collected and resuspended in an equal amount of water, followed by bacterial counting.

BC, BC@PDA, and BC@PCS were separately inoculated in fresh media with the same density and cultured at 37 °C with gentle shaking, and their OD600 values were recorded using a microplate reader (Spark, TECAN Co., Ltd., Männedorf, Switzerland) to calculate growth curves.

Survival evaluation of BC after radiation, freeze drying, and long-term preservation

Three conditions (ionizing radiation, freeze drying, and long-term preservation) were investigated to assess their impact on the survival of BC in three formulations (BC, BC@PDA, and BC@PCS). The number of viable bacteria was counted using the microbial cell analyzer after the bacteria-containing formulations were stained with SYTO9/PI. The survival rates of BC were calculated before and after the conditions were conducted, including 12 Gy γ ray (60Co, Beijing Institute of Radiation Medicine), freeze drying (LGJ-30F, Beijing Songyuan Huaxing Technology Co., Ltd., Beijing, China), and preservation for 7 days at −20 °C, 4 °C, and 25 °C, respectively.

In vitro and in vivo ROS scavenging ability assays

LGG, Lr, AKK, EcN, and BC were cultured according to the aforementioned cultivation methods, and then the bacterial cultures or bacterial washing solutions were centrifuged at 3500 rpm for 5 min to collect the bacterial cells. Phosphate-buffered saline (PBS, pH 7.4) was used to resuspend the bacterial cells and dilute them to a concentration of 1 × 108 CFU/mL. The superoxide dismutase (SOD) activity assay kit (WST-8) was used to evaluate the •O2− scavenging rate of the bacteria above according to the manufacturer's instructions. The coated bacteria BC@PCS were prepared according to the method described above, and the bacterial densities of BC and BC@PCS were adjusted to 1 × 108 CFU/mL using PBS. The •O2− scavenging efficiency of BC and BC@PCS was also evaluated using the SOD activity assay kit, following the method above.

Fluorescence sections were utilized to observe the levels of ROS in the intestinal tissues of mice. Briefly, mice from each group were sacrificed, and their small intestine tissues were dissected. The small intestine tissues were washed with pre-cooled physiological saline and then snap-frozen to prepare frozen sections. The frozen sections were naturally dried at room temperature, followed by the addition of a background fluorescence quencher, and then rinsed with sterile water. DCFH-DA was applied to the sections and incubated in a dark incubator at 37 °C for 30 min. The sections were washed with sterile water and then stained with a DAPI solution for 10 min. After staining, the sections were placed in PBS for de-staining, followed by blocking for fluorescence microscopy observation. The obtained fluorescence images were analyzed using ImageJ to calculate the average fluorescence intensity.

Intestinal epithelial cell (IEC-6) was kindly donated by Prof. Y Cong, and the cells were cultured in the RPMI 1640 media supplemented with 10 % FBS at 37 °C in a 55 ± 5 % humidity and 5 % CO2 atmosphere. Intracellular ROS fluorescence imaging was conducted using the ROS fluorescent probe method. BC, PCS, and BC@PCS were prepared according to the process described above, and the bacterial densities of BC and BC@PCS were adjusted to 1 × 108 CFU/mL using PBS. IEC-6 cells were seeded at 20000 cells/well into 24-well plates and incubated for 12 h to adhere. The cells were divided into five groups based on different treatments: Control, Model, BC, PCS, and BC@PCS groups. One hour before irradiation, an aliquot (5 μL) of prepared BC, PCS, and BC@PCS were added to the BC, PCS, and BC@PCS groups, respectively. An aliquot (5 μL) of PBS was added to the Model and Control groups. Then, the plates were irradiated with 12 Gy γ-ray except the Control group, followed by incubation at 37 °C for 3 h. Next, a DCFH-DA (Ex/Em: 488/525 nm) diluent (10 μM, 250 μL) was added into every well, following incubation at 37 °C for 30 min. After washing with PBS, a DAPI staining solution (250 μL) was added to each well for staining for 10 min. After washing with PBS again, the plates were placed under a fluorescence microscope (Nikon, TiE-A1, Tokyo, Japan) for observation.

Tolerance test of BC to gastric acid

BC, BC@PDA, and BC@PCS (1 × 109 CFU/mL, 1 mL) were separately mixed with the simulated gastric fluid (SGF, pH 2.5, 1 mL, containing 5 g pepsin, 0.85 % NaCl), followed by incubation for 2 h at 37 °C. Two aliquots (50 μL) were pipetted at predetermined time points (every 30 min). One aliquot was centrifuged to obtain bacterial pellets, which were then washed with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS, pH 7.4). The pellets were serially diluted, spread on solid mannitol polymyxin agar plates, and incubated overnight at 37 °C. Another aliquot was used for calculating the survival rate.

Retention of BC in the intestine

Male C57BL/6J mice were purchased from Beijing Vital River Laboratory Animal Technology and used in all described experiments. The mice were raised under specific pathogen-free conditions with a 12-h light-dark cycle and free access to food and water. All experimental procedures complied with the institutional ethical guidelines and were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Beijing Institute of Radiation Medicine (approval number: IACUC-DWZX-2024-P510).

FITC-labeled BC and BC@PCS were centrifuged, collected, and washed three times with PBS. They (1 × 108 CFU/mL, 0.2 mL) were i.g. administered to mice. The retention of BC in the gut was observed by in vivo imaging (Tanon ABL X5, Shanghai Tianneng Technology Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China) at 4 h, 6 h, 8 h, and 12 h, respectively.

Establishment and treatment of radiation enteritis

Mice were randomly divided into 7 groups with 12 in each group, including the healthy, model, Ami (i.p. amifostine), PCS (intragastrical, i.g., mixing PDA and chitosan as Section 2.4 without bacteria), BC (i.g. BC), BC + PCS (i.g. the physical mixture of BC and PCS), and BC@PCS (i.g. BC@PCS) groups. Whole abdomen irradiation (WAI) was conducted with 12 Gy (71.39 cGy/min) γ ray to establish the RE mouse model. The treatment began 1 day pre-radiation except for Ami, which was continued until 3 days post-irradiation. Saline (0.2 mL) was i.g. administered to the mice in the healthy and model groups. Ami (150 mg/kg, 0.2 mL) was intraperitoneally (i.p.) injected into the mice in the Ami group 30 min pre-irradiation. Bacteria-contained formulations (1 × 108 CFU/mL, 0.2 mL) were i.g. administered to the mice in the BC, BC + PCS, and BC@PCS groups. The mixture (0.2 mL) of PDA and chitosan was i.g. administered to the mice in the PCS group.

In vivo biosafety assay of BC@PCS

Mice were randomly divided into three groups with 3 in each group, including the healthy, BC@PCS (1 × 108 CFU/mL, 0.2 mL), and 10 × BC@PCS (1 × 109 CFU/mL, 0.2 mL) groups. The formulations were administered once daily for 7 days. The mice were weighed daily and sacrificed after the final administration. Blood samples were collected for total bilirubin (TBIL), alkaline phosphatase (ALP), albumin (ALB), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), and urea (UREA) assays following the manufacturer's instructions. The hearts, livers, spleens, lungs, and kidneys of mice were removed and weighed to calculate the organ index, and were prepared into hematoxylin-eosin (H&E) sections. The small intestine and colon tissues were removed, and their lengths were measured. Meanwhile, the intestinal tissues were prepared into H&E sections.

Intestinal mucosal permeability test

The mice in the intestinal mucosal permeability test were fasted for 12 h followed by oral FITC-dextran (500 mg/kg, 0.2 mL). After 4 h, blood samples were collected and centrifuged at 3000 rpm, 4 °C for 20 min to obtain serum. The fluorescence intensity in the serum was measured using the TECAN microplate reader at Ex 490 nm and Em 520 nm.

Assay of DAO in serum and cytokines in tissues

A 2-cm segment of the small intestine tissue 5 cm below the stomach was excised. The tissues were added with saline (1:9, w/w) and homogenized with a tissue homogenizer (KI-II, Wuhan Servicebio Technology Co., Ltd., Wuhan, China) at 4 °C,70 Hz for 6 min. The homogenates were centrifuged at 5000×g for 15 min, and the supernatant was collected for ELISA detection. The levels of TNF-α, IL-6, and IL-1β were measured with ELISA kits following the manufacturer's instructions.

The eyeballs of mice were enucleated for blood collection. After being left to stand at 4 °C for 12 h, the blood was centrifuged at 1000×g for 15 min to prepare serum. The serum DAO content was measured using a DAO ELISA kit according to the manufacturer's instructions.

Pathology and immunofluorescent assay

The mice were sacrificed 3.5 and 7 days post-radiation. A 1-cm segment of the small intestine tissue 5 cm below the stomach was excised. The tissues were fixed in 4 % formaldehyde solutions and then embedded in paraffin according to the standard procedure. The sections were stained with H&E or Ki-67. ZO-1 and Occludin expressions in the small intestinal tissue were examined by the immunofluorescent assay of the tissue sections according to the instructions. The sections were stained with DAPI and visualized under a fluorescence microscope (CKX53, Olympus Corporation, Japan).

16S ribosomal RNA gene sequencing and microbiome analysis

The feces of the mice in Section 2.10 were collected in sterile tubes and preserved at −80 °C. 16S ribosomal (rRNA) gene sequencing analysis was conducted by Azenta Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Suzhou, China). The main steps included RNA extraction, RNA detection, polymerase chain reaction (PCR) amplification, product purification, sequencing library construction, quality testing, and sequencing.

Statistics

All experimental data were presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Statistical analyses and graphical output of the data were performed with GraphPad Prism (v 8.0.2). The statistical significance was determined by ordinary one-way ANOVA or two-way ANOVA with ns, no significant; *p < 0.05; **p < 0.01; ***p < 0.001.

Results and discussion

Probiotic BC exhibits strong in vitro and in vivo ROS-scavenging capability

Five strains of marketed probiotics were investigated to determine whether they possess sufficient ROS-scavenging capability, which would significantly impact their therapeutic efficacy in treating RE. The five strains can be divided into two types: anaerobic bacteria, including LGG, Lr, and AKK, and facultative anaerobic bacteria, including EcN and BC. In the in vitro •O2− scavenging test, the two types of bacteria showed great differences. The ROS-scavenging capability of the anaerobic bacteria was much less than that of the facultative anaerobic bacteria (Fig. 1A). The short-term WAI RE therapeutic effects of EcN and BC were compared (Fig. 1B). After radiation, the EcN and BC groups had significant weight loss, like the model group, although the BC group was a little better (Fig. 1C). RE made the length of the small intestine and colon short; however, EcN and BC alleviated the shortening effect (Fig. 1D and E). Furthermore, the tissue sections showed that the small intestinal villi and crypts in the BC group were protected compared to the EcN and model groups (Fig. 1F). The ROS immunofluorescence staining of the tissues revealed that the BC group had the lowest ROS levels with significant differences compared to the model and EcN groups (Fig. 1G and H). Therefore, BC is the best probiotic against RE among the five strains. The strong ROS-scavenging ability of BC could contribute to its effect on RE development. Moreover, BC can become a live antioxidant with the sustained anti-oxidation effect by continual ROS-scavenging locally. This great advantage makes BC very preferable in the therapy of RE, which continually and locally produces ROS.

Layer-by-layer coating enhances the robustness of BC

PDA was immobilized on the BC surface by covalent bonding due to the presence of the hydroxyl group. Chitosan was further coated on the PDA layer by ion cross-linking (Fig. 2A). The layer-by-layer coating would further protect BC. Due to the black nature of PDA, both BC@PDA and BC@PCS appeared black, whereas the uncoated bacteria were pale yellow (Fig. 2B). According to the TEM images (Fig. 2C), BC@PDA and BC@PCS had a thick outer layer without significant flagella, which could not affect the activity of BC. Based on the green FITC staining of BC and the red rhodamine B staining of PDA, we further demonstrated the completely tight coating of PDA on the surface of BC according to fluorescence microscopic imaging (Fig. 2D). PDA was first coated on the bacterial surface based on the chemical reaction that formed strong chemical bonds with the bacterial surface. The size of BC@PDA increased to 1.75 μm, compared to the smaller size (1.65 μm) of original BC (Fig. 2F). Moreover, the zeta potential (−21.7 ± 1.20 mV) of BC@PDA had a great increase compared to the small zeta potential (−5.45 ± 1.24 mV) of BC (Fig. 2G), resulting from PDA complete coating. We further demonstrated the completely tight coating of PDA on the surface of BC according to fluorescence microscopic imaging (Fig. 2D). When chitosan was coated on BC@PDA, these parameters showed significant changes. The size of BC@PCS increased to 1.85 μm that was much significant difference (P < 0.001) with that of BC@PDA (Fig. 2F). More importantly, the zeta potential (−18.2 ± 1.20 mV) of BC@PCS had significant changes (P < 0.01) with that of BC@PDA, resulting from the strong cationic nature of chitosan, which further demonstrated the second-layer coating of chitosan in BC@PCS (Fig. 2G). Moreover, the TEM images of BC@PDA and BC@PCS showed that the coating layer of BC@PCS was thicker than that of BC@PDA (Fig. 2C). Whether coating affects the growth of BC is a crucial issue. Within 48 h, the OD600 of all the formulations had no differences (Fig. 2H), indicating that the coating did not affect the growth of BC. Furthermore, we examined the fluorescence and TEM images of BC@PCS after proliferation (Fig. 2E and I), revealing that the coating on the proliferated BC was completely or partially lost. This indicated that the coating will gradually disappear as the bacteria proliferate. Chitosan is a cationic aminopolysaccharide capable of interacting with negatively charged bacterial membranes, disrupting their structure, and thus killing bacteria. However, the preparation process of this material involves only a small amount of chitosan incorporation (with a final concentration of 0.25 mg/mL added each time), which seems insufficient to produce an antibacterial effect. Additionally, chitosan was added only 30 min after the introduction of polydopamine, by which time a thin coating of polydopamine had already formed on the bacterial surface, separating chitosan from the bacterial surface and thus preventing its antibacterial effect. The contribution of chitosan in BC@PCS mainly lies in further enhancing mucosal adhesion and coating robustness, which helps to further improve the efficacy of BC@PCS.

More importantly, we surprisingly found that the coating protected BC from harsh conditions. Undergoing high-dose ionizing radiation, BC@PCS, BC@PDA, and BC maintained the survival rates of 85 %, 72 %, and 68 %, respectively (Fig. 2J); undergoing the freeze drying process, the survival rates were 90 %, 80 %, and 78 %, respectively (Fig. 2K); and after long-term preservation at different temperature, the survival rates were 97 %, 95 %, and 88 % at −20 °C, 98 %, 90 %, and 85 % at 4 °C, and 98 %, 95 %, and 75 % at 20 °C (Fig. 2L), respectively. All the information indicated that the coating, especially layer-by-layer coating, highly enhanced the robustness of BC. Therefore, the PCS coating would facilitate BC clinical translation due to its high environmental adaptation.

Layer-by-layer coating enhances BC's gastric acid tolerance, intestinal retention time, and ROS-scavenging capability

The clinical application of many probiotics often fails when administered orally because gastric acid severely destroys naked probiotics. Polymer coating protected BC from SGF (Fig. 3A). The acid tolerance of BC-containing formulations was determined by the layers of coating and time. After immersing in the SGF for 0.5 h, the BC viabilities of BC, BC@PDA, and BC@PCS were 70 %, 83 %, and 97 %, respectively; after 2 h, the viabilities of them decreased to 28 %, 35 %, and 44 %, respectively (Fig. 3A and B). Another reason for the failure of probiotic clinical application is the short intestinal retention time, which makes it hard for probiotics to perform their beneficial function. Layer-by-layer coating significantly prolonged the intestinal retention of BC compared to naked BC, according to the FITC-stained BC distribution in the whole intestinal route (Fig. 3C). After 12 h post-single oral administration, BC@PCS still kept a lot in the intestinal route compared to naked BC. The real-time quantitative PCR results of BC in the colon further demonstrated the high colon retention of BC@PCS. After 4 h post-BC@PCS administration, the BC in the colon content achieved 5.2 × 108 CFU/g, but naked BC only had 3.0 × 108 CFU/g (Fig. 3D). After 12 h, BC@PCS still had 0.3 × 108 CFU/g BC in the colon, while naked BC only had a little BC (0.07 × 107 CFU/g). Long intestinal retention benefits BC by allowing it to perform its function.

In the above section, we have found that ROS scavenging is a key factor of probiotics against RE. Does layer-by-layer coating affect the ROS-scavenging capability of BC? Surprisingly, BC@PCS had nearly two-fold •O2− scavenging efficiency of naked BC (Fig. 3E). Furthermore, the scavenging efficiency of BC, PCS, BC@PCS on DPPH [43], a chromogenic reactive oxygen species, was measured. As expected, BC@PCS exhibited the highest scavenging efficiency (Fig. 3F). The ROS scavenging effect of PDA is confirmed, indicating that BC@PCS shows much higher antioxidation effect than BC and PCS alone. More importantly, BC cannot resist gastric acid, so the PDA/CS coating protects BC from the harsh gastrointestinal environment and subsequently BC performs its ROS scavenging action. Besides the anti-oxidation effect of BC itself, a lot of phenolic groups in PDA should contribute to the clearance of ROS. This phenomenon is evidenced in the irradiated intestinal epithelial cell (IEC-6) model. High ROS levels appeared in the irradiated model. If incubation with BC, PCS, and BC@PCS, respectively, before radiation, the ROS levels in the cells were reduced (Fig. 3G). The ROS reduction extent of BC@PCS was nearly the sum of those of the other two (Fig. 3H). The ROS level of the BC@PCS group was about half of that of the model group. Therefore, the high anti-oxidation effect of BC@PCS is the result of the performance of BC and PDA together. Although the mechanisms related to ROS-mediated cellular inflammation have been reported [[44], [45], [46]], involving the NF-κB pathway, the MAPK signaling pathway, and the PI3K-Akt signaling pathway, etc., the changes in mRNA and protein levels in irradiated IEC-6 cells have not been explored in this study. Given the importance of this mechanism in elucidating the biological activity of BC@PCS, future research could focus on the transcriptomics and proteomics of cells after an increase in radiation-induced ROS. This will help us better understand the pathogenesis of radiation-induced enteritis and the pharmacological mechanism of BC@PCS.

Chitosan, a positively charged polysaccharide, has been utilized as a material for intestinal-specific drug delivery due to its accelerated degradation in the intestine and strong mucosal adhesion to inflammatory mucosal tissues [47,48]. For instance, the probiotics encapsulated in chitosan-based microspheres have been developed for the treatment of colitis [8,49]. Chitosan is introduced into the coating by the Michael addition reaction and the Schiff base reaction between PDA and the attached amino groups [50]. Existing literatures primarily report that PDA exerts antibacterial effects by increasing the adhesion of antibacterial substances to bacteria or utilizing the photothermal properties of PDA, while PDA molecules do not have direct bactericidal activity [[51], [52], [53]]. Therefore, PDA is biocompatible and has been used in previous studies for cell coating [54,55]. Consequently, the primary contributions of chitosan in BC@PCS include further enhancing mucosal adhesion and increasing coating robustness, which contribute to the improved efficacy of BC@PCS. Simultaneously, the abundant amino groups present on chitosan facilitate the crosslinking between PDA and chitosan, thereby increasing the coating thickness to enhance acid resistance. BC, as an antioxidant probiotic, can express a large number of antioxidant enzymes to continuously and efficiently eliminate ROS, while PDA, due to its polymeric indole structure, can absorb a large number of electrons to scavenge free radicals. PDA is used as a coating material, which can maintain bacterial activity and provide additional electron-withdrawing ability. Therefore, we propose that the strong ROS-scavenging ability of BC@PCS results from the combination of BC and PDA. However, chitosan does not have ROS-scavenging ability because it cannot absorb additional electrons. Its main function is to improve the robustness and adhesion of the coating.

BC@PCS shows good biosafety

As a long-term administration product, the safety of BC@PCS is important. After the i.g. administration of BC@PCS with the treatment dose and 10-fold dose to mice for 7 days, the systemic safety investigation was conducted. There were no differences between the healthy group and the BC@PCS treatment dose and 10-fold dose groups in many factors, including body weight, the length of small intestine and colon, organ indices, TBIL, ALP, ALB, AST, and UREA. Moreover, the H&E staining sections of the small intestine, heart, liver, spleen, lungs, and kidneys also had no differences (Fig. S1). Therefore, BC@PCS safety is ensured.

Ionizing radiation leads to a series of adverse events, including ROS burst and severe inflammation, finally resulting in heavy intestinal mucosal injury [57]. The integrity and permeability of intestinal mucosa are the key factors to evaluate damage extent. Occludin and ZO-1 are tight junction proteins to form intercellular adhesion complexes, regulating the permeability between cells [58]. DAO is an enzyme found in intestinal epithelial cells, which is released in the event of damage to the intestinal barrier. Therefore, high DAO levels in blood indicate damage to the intestinal mechanical barrier [59,60]. Ionizing radiation resulted in a decrease in the expression of Occludin and ZO-1 in the small intestine (Fig. 6A and B). After the treatment of BC-contained formulations, ZO-1 and Occludin were significantly improved (Fig. 6C and D), where BC@PCS was the best. Moreover, the BC@PCS group also showed the lowest DAO expression among them (Fig. 6E), also indicating the best intestinal mechanical barrier of the BC@PCS group. FITC-dextran is a macromolecular fluorescent agent. Oral administration of FITC-dextran cannot penetrate a healthy intestinal barrier, whereas in cases of intestinal damage, FITC-dextran can enter the bloodstream and be detected by fluorescent spectrophotometry [61]. Its appearance in the serum indicates that the intestinal mechanical barrier was damaged and the permeability increased [10]. Radiation results in a significant increase in intestinal mucosal permeability. After treating the BC-containing formulations, the integrity was significantly improved. Especially for BC@PCS, the permeability decreased to the lowest, close to the healthy group (Fig. 6F). In addition, Ami is the unique ROS and radical removal drug against ionizing radiation, which is often used as the control in the research of ionizing radiation injury therapy [4]. As expected, BC@PCS demonstrated superior efficacy compared to BC, PCS, and BC + PCS, and even outperformed the positive control drug, Ami. This suggests that the selected BC is suitable for treating RE, and the PCS layer-by-layer coating enhances its therapeutic effects. BC can alleviate radiation damage by scavenging ROS and exert a probiotic effect in the intestine. The enhancement effect of the PCS coating on BC is primarily attributed to its ability to improve the delivery efficiency of live bacteria and increase intestinal retention time. The superior efficacy of BC@PCS compared to BC + PCS is primarily attributed to the PCS coating, which enhances the oral delivery efficiency and intestinal adhesion of BC. Especially, PCS coating protects BC from gastric acid. BC + PCS is only the physical mixture and both of them keep independent so that gastric acid can still kill lots of probiotics after oral administration. Additionally, when some uncoated BC enters the intestine, they are easily cleared. Not only BC coated with PCS, i.e., BC@PCS, maintains the ROS-scavenging ability of BC and PDA, but also the coating increases the number and duration of live BC in the intestine, leading to superior therapeutic efficacy. Compared to other materials, such as alginate and PEG, the polyindole group structure of PDA can absorb electrons and provide additional antioxidant capacity. Furthermore, the catechol structure of PDA possesses adhesive properties, which can enhance the intestinal retention time of BC@PCS and thereby improve overall therapeutic efficacy. However, single-coated PDA exhibits insufficient acid resistance; therefore, chitosan is incorporated to further enhance mucosal adhesion and increase coating robustness. These factors collectively determine the therapeutic effect of BC@PCS on RE. Therefore, a strategy combining probiotics with biomaterials for the treatment of RE has been validated.

BC@PCS increases intestinal flora diversity and improves motor function

The relationship between the gut microbiota and radiation injury is gradually being unveiled [62,63]. For instance, Lachnospiraceae and Enterococcaceae can enhance radiation resistance in mice by producing short-chain fatty acids [7], while plant polysaccharides promote the repair of radiation injury by regulating the gut microbiota [64]. Consequently, we investigated the changes in gut microbiota in the colon of various groups. As the sample size increased, species accumulation exhibited a gradual stabilization trend, and the sequencing depth was sufficient for further analysis of species richness. Two parameters, Shannon and Chao1, reflecting microbiota diversity were evaluated [65,66]. The Healthy and BC@PCS groups exhibited equivalent diversity, suggesting that BC@PCS can protect the diversity of the gut microbiota (Fig. 7A and B). The Venn diagram (petal-like) indicated that BC@PCS group possessed the highest number of unique operational taxonomic units (OTUs), whereas the numbers of unique OTUs in the Model and PCS groups were significantly lower than that in BC@PCS, suggesting that BC@PCS induced greater gut microbiota diversity (Fig. 7C). We further analyzed the differences in the colonic microbiome across groups at the phylum and class levels. Sequencing results revealed that, at the phylum level, the abundance of Proteobacteria significantly increased in the Model group, but decreased after administration of BC@PCS (Fig. 7D). At the class level, BC@PCS increased the relative abundance of Bacteroidetes, which is the most abundant beneficial gut microbiota responsible for polysaccharide degradation, and reduced the abundance of Proteobacteria (Fig. 7E) [67,68]. These findings suggested that BC@PCS can modulate the gut microbiota of irradiated mice, which may constitute one of the potential mechanisms underlying the alleviation of RE. As the data demonstrated, the gut microbiota diversity of the BC group was essentially consistent with that of the BC@PCS group. We speculate that it may be the metabolites of BC, rather than the live bacteria, that play a crucial role in regulating gut microbiota [69]. However, current samples for gut microbiota analysis were relatively limited. Generally, the data dispersion of gut microbiota is relatively large, so a larger sample size is conducive to more accurate analysis. To more intuitively demonstrate the therapeutic efficacy of BC@PCS, the locomotor ability of mice was assessed. The Model group exhibited a significant reduction in motor ability, characterized by an increase in dead time, a decrease in average movement speed, and a reduction in total walking distance (Fig. 7F–H). However, the motor ability of mice administered different drugs improved, with BC@PCS displaying the best performance. These data indicated that BC@PCS had not only been proven effective at the molecular level but also promotes the overall recovery of locomotor ability, which can be attributed to the multifaceted capabilities of BC@PCS.

The optimal therapeutic effect of PCS-modified BC can be attributed to the following reasons. Actually, we screened a series of marketed probiotics and found BC owning the strongest antioxidant capacity. Moreover, PDA and chitosan layer-by-layer coating enhanced BC's acid resistance and adhesion. PDA also had strong ROS scavenging ability. Therefore, this modified BC showed the excellent anti-radiation effect. More comprehensive and systematic metagenomics and metabolomics can provide more details and evidence for elucidating the molecular mechanism of BC@PCS action, and we will explore the molecular mechanisms underlying its function in the next research.

Conclusion

An efficient, sustained, and safe antioxidant is the key to treat RE. Although probiotics have been demonstrated to own the capability against RE, the bad gastric tolerance and the short intestinal retention limit them application. We found a new strategy based on biomaterial layer-by-layer coating to address this key problem. BC, which was selected as the optimal live anti-oxidation material, was coated with PDA and chitosan in turn. The coating improves the oral delivery efficiency of live bacteria, prolongs their retention time in the intestine, and enhances the ROS scavenging ability. The obtained product, BC@PCS, can serve as an efficient, sustained, and safe live antioxidant for the treatment of RE. Compared to naked probiotics, the engineered probiotics showed many advantages, resulting from layer-by-layer coating. Biomaterial layer-by-layer coating is a promising way to modify live materials to exert their beneficial functions.
