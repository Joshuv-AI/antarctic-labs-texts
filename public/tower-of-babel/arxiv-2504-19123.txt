Fast and memory-efficient BWT construction of repetitive texts using Lyndon grammars
Jannik Olbrich

Abstract
The Burrows-Wheeler Transform ( 𝖡𝖶𝖳 \mathsf{BWT} ) serves as the basis for many important sequence indexes. On very large datasets (e.g. genomic databases), classical 𝖡𝖶𝖳 \mathsf{BWT} construction algorithms are often infeasible because they usually need to have the entire dataset in main memory. Fortunately, such large datasets are often highly repetitive. It can thus be beneficial to compute the 𝖡𝖶𝖳 \mathsf{BWT} from a compressed representation. We propose an algorithm for computing the 𝖡𝖶𝖳 \mathsf{BWT} via the Lyndon straight-line program, a grammar based on the standard factorization of Lyndon words. Our algorithm can also be used to compute the extended 𝖡𝖶𝖳 \mathsf{BWT} ( 𝖾𝖡𝖶𝖳 \mathsf{eBWT} ) of a multiset of sequences. We empirically evaluate our implementation and find that we can compute the 𝖡𝖶𝖳 \mathsf{BWT} and 𝖾𝖡𝖶𝖳 \mathsf{eBWT} of very large datasets faster and/or with less memory than competing methods.

1
Introduction

The
Burrows-Wheeler Transform
(
\mathsf{BWT}
)
[
13
]
is one of the most important data structures in sequence analysis and is applied in a wide range of fields from data compression to bioinformatics.
It is obtained by assigning the
i
th symbol of the
\mathsf{BWT}
to the last character of the
i
th lexicographically smallest conjugate of the input text.
The
\mathsf{BWT}
can be computed in linear time and space.
The run-length compressed
\mathsf{BWT}
(
\mathsf{RLBWT}
) uses the fact that the number of equal-character runs in the
\mathsf{BWT}
is small compared to the text length when the text is repetitive
[
13
]
.
Mantaci et al.
[
32
]
extended the notion of the
\mathsf{BWT}
to string collections: the
extended
\mathsf{BWT}
(
\mathsf{eBWT}
) of a string collection consists of the last characters of the strings in
\mathcal{M}
arranged in infinite periodic order (see Section
2
for definitions).

The
\mathsf{BWT}
serves as the basis of several
self-indexes
, i.e., data structures supporting access to the text as well as pattern matching queries.
One of the most successful such indexes is the FM-index
[
20
]
, which is used e.g. in the important bioinformatics programs BWA
[
30
]
and Bowtie
[
28
]
.
However, the FM-index uses space proportional to the size of the dataset and is thus not applicable for huge datasets that vastly exceed the size of main memory.
Such datasets often contain data from thousands or millions of individuals from the same species and are therefore extremely repetitive.
The
r-index

[
22
]
is based on the
\mathsf{RLBWT}
and is the first text index of size proportional to the number
r
of runs in the
\mathsf{BWT}
.
It can be constructed in space proportional to
r
from the
\mathsf{RLBWT}
, which raises the problem of constructing the
\mathsf{RLBWT}
of huge datasets with small working memory.
For this reason, various tools have emerged that exploit the repetitiveness of these datasets.

The Lyndon grammar of a text is a straight-line program (SLP), i.e., a context-free grammar in Chomsky normal form that generates a single text, where each symbol corresponds to a node in the Lyndon tree.
It was introduced in
[
25
]
as the basis for a self-index.
In this paper, we describe a method for efficiently computing several
\mathsf{BWT}
variants of a text or a text collection via the input’s Lyndon grammar and show that our method surpasses other tools with respect to time or memory used, especially when using multiple threads.

1.1
Related work

Boucher et al.
[
11
]
developed
prefix-free parsing (PFP)
,
a dictionary-compression technique that results in a dictionary and parse from which the (
\mathsf{RL}
)
\mathsf{BWT}
can be constructed in space proportional to their total size
and in time linear to the size of the dataset.
While the dictionary typically grows slowly as the size of the dataset increases, the parse grows linearly with the input size (with a small constant chosen at runtime).
Consequently, for extremely large and repetitive datasets, “the parse is more burdensome than the size of the dictionary”
[
40
]
.
In an effort to remedy this, Oliva et al.
[
40
]
developed
recursive prefix-free parsing
, which applies PFP to the parse.
PFP has also been used to construct the
\mathsf{eBWT}

[
10
]
.
The currently fastest implementation for computing the suffix array
\mathsf{SA}
(and
\mathsf{BWT}
) for general texts is
libsais
and is based on
the linear-time
suffix array induced sorting (SAIS)
algorithm
[
37
,
39
]
.

ropebwt3
uses
libsais
to compute the
\mathsf{BWT}
of chunks of the input and successively merges these
\mathsf{BWT}
s
[
29
]
.
Díaz-Domínguez and Navarro used grammar compression to maintain the intermediate data in the SAIS algorithm with low main memory usage, resulting in a linear-time semi-external algorithm that computes a variant of the
\mathsf{eBWT}

[
14
,
17
]
.
Specifically for a collection of sequences where each is similar to a given reference (e.g. human chromosomes), Masillo introduced
CMS-BWT
, which computes the
\mathsf{BWT}
via
matching statistics

[
35
]
.

Lyndon words have previously been used in the construction of
\mathsf{SA}
and play an important role in the original
\mathsf{eBWT}
.
In
[
33
,
34
]
, a strategy for computing
\mathsf{SA}
was presented where “local suffixes” (i.e., substrings ending at boundaries between Lyndon factors) can be processed separately in each Lyndon factor.
Later, Baier effectively generalized the underlying idea by showing that sorting the suffixes by their longest Lyndon prefixes can be used for linear time
\mathsf{SA}
construction
[
2
]
.
The
bijective
\mathsf{BWT}
(
\mathsf{BBWT}
) is the
\mathsf{eBWT}
of the text’s Lyndon factors
[
23
,
27
,
32
]
.
Bonomo et al. showed that it is possible to compute the
\mathsf{eBWT}
via the
\mathsf{BBWT}
and gave an
\mathcal{O}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(N\log N/\log\log N}}\right)
construction algorithm based on properties of Lyndon words
[
8
,
9
]
. Later, Bannai et al. modified the SAIS algorithm to compute the
\mathsf{BBWT}
in linear time
[
3
]
.
Since publication, Baier’s algorithm was improved in terms of time and memory usage
[
6
,
38
]
and further generalized such that it can be used to compute
\mathsf{eBWT}
and
\mathsf{BBWT}
in linear time
[
39
]
.
The principles used this generalized algorithm lie at the base of our algorithm for computing the
\mathsf{BWT}
,
\mathsf{BBWT}
and
\mathsf{eBWT}
variants from a Lyndon grammar.

1.2
Our contributions

Let
N
be the size of a text or text collection,
n_{\mathrm{max}}
the maximum size of a string in the text collection, and
g
the size of the Lyndon SLP of the text or text collection.
We give two online in-memory algorithms for constructing the Lyndon SLP with
\mathcal{O}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(Ng}}\right)
and
\mathcal{O}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(N\log g+g\log^{2}g}}\right)
worst-case time complexity, both using
\mathcal{O}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(g}}\right)
words of memory.
Notably, the text or text collection is streamed from right to left and does not have to reside in main memory.
Additionally, we give an expected linear-time algorithm that uses
\mathcal{O}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(g+n_{\mathrm{max}}}}\right)
words of memory.
Furthermore, we give an
\mathcal{O}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(g}}\right)
algorithm for sorting the symbols of a Lyndon SLP lexicographically by their generated strings,
and an
\mathcal{O}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(N}}\right)
algorithm for constructing the (run-length compressed)
\mathsf{BWT}
,
\mathsf{BBWT}
, or
\mathsf{eBWT}
from a Lyndon SLP.
We implemented our algorithms and demonstrate empirically that we can construct the
\mathsf{BWT}
,
\mathsf{BBWT}
or
\mathsf{eBWT}
of repetitive texts or text collections faster and/or with less space than competing methods.

We assume that distinct symbols generate distinct words.

A Lyndon SLP arises from renaming the nodes of a Lyndon Forest such that two nodes with isomorphic subtrees (i.e., representing the same string)
are assigned the same symbol.
The Lyndon Forest and Lyndon SLP of our running example can be seen in Figure

Clearly, the size of a Lyndon SLP

is bounded by
\mathcal{O}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\mathcal{G}}}\right]}}\right|}}\right)
.
This bound is tight and there is no non-trivial bound in terms of e.g. the number of runs in the
\mathsf{BWT}

of the smallest string attractor
[

]
or the size of the smallest SLP:
consider

for

The smallest SLP generating

has size
\mathcal{O}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\log n}}\right)

\mathsf{BWT}
has two runs, and the smallest string attractor has size

but every suffix of

In the following, we assume the set of symbols

3
Properties of sorted Lyndon Grammars

Consider a Lyndon grammar
\mathcal{G}
where the symbols are sorted lexicographically,
i.e.,
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[X_{i}}}\right]<_{\mathit{lex}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[X_{j}}}\right]\iff i<j
.
In this section we examine such Lyndon SLPs and find properties that enable sorting any Lyndon SLP lexicographically in linear time (Section
4
)
and give rise to an online algorithm for constructing Lyndon SLPs (Section
6.3
).
Note that the grammar shown in Figure
2
is lexicographically sorted.

Definition 10

(Prefix- and Suffix-symbols)
.

Define
\mathcal{P}_{L}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(X_{i}}}\right)
(
\mathcal{P}_{R}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(X_{i}}}\right)

X_{i}

More formally, if
X_{i}
is a terminal symbol define

\mathcal{P}_{R}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(X_{i}}}\right)=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{X_{i}}}\right\}\cup\mathcal{P}_{R}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(X_{b}}}\right)

We correspondingly define
\mathcal{P}_{L}^{-1}(X_{i})
(
\mathcal{P}_{R}^{-1}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(X_{i}}}\right)

\mathcal{P}_{L}^{-1}(X_{i})=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{X_{j}\mid X_{i}\in\mathcal{P}_{L}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(X_{j}}}\right)}}\right\}

For example, we have

Definition 11
.

Define
\mathcal{D}(X_{i})
as the set of symbols occurring in

’s derivation tree.
More formally, if
X_{i}
is a terminal symbol define

Lemma 13
.

Let
w=uv
be a Lyndon word with standard factorization
(u,v)
. Then
\operatorname{suf}_{k}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(u}}\right)v=\operatorname{suf}_{k}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(w}}\right)>_{\mathit{lex}}v
holds for all
k\in[1..\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|u}}\right|)
.

Proof.

Assume
\operatorname{suf}_{k}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(w}}\right)<_{\mathit{lex}}v=\operatorname{suf}_{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|u}}\right|}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(w}}\right)
with
k\in\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[1\,..\,\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|u}}\right|}}\right)
for a contradiction.
Consider the lexicographically smallest (not necessarily proper) suffix
\operatorname{suf}_{p}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(w}}\right)
of
\operatorname{suf}_{k}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(w}}\right)
(i.e.,
k\leq p
).
Since
\operatorname{suf}_{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|u}}\right|}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(w}}\right)=v
is Lyndon,
\operatorname{suf}_{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|u}}\right|}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(w}}\right)<_{\mathit{lex}}\operatorname{suf}_{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|u}}\right|+\ell}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(w}}\right)
holds for
1\leq\ell<\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|v}}\right|
and we thus have
k\leq p\leq\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|u}}\right|
.
Since
\operatorname{suf}_{k}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(w}}\right)<_{\mathit{lex}}v=\operatorname{suf}_{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|u}}\right|}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(w}}\right)
holds by assumption, we additionally have
p\neq\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|u}}\right|
and consequently
k\leq p<\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|u}}\right|
.
Because
\operatorname{suf}_{p}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(w}}\right)
is the lexicographically smallest suffix of
\operatorname{suf}_{k}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(w}}\right)

This is a contradiction because

is by definition the longest Lyndon suffix of

∎

Lemma 14
.

For all
X_{j}\in\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[l\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(X_{i}}}\right)\,..\,r\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(X_{i}}}\right)}}\right]
it holds

, i.e.,
X_{i}
occurs on the leftmost path of
X_{j}
’s derivation tree.
Equivalently,
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[l\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(X_{i}}}\right)\,..\,r\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(X_{i}}}\right)}}\right]=\mathcal{P}_{L}^{-1}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(X_{i}}}\right)
.

Proof.

Assume for a contradiction that there is some
X_{j}
with
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[X_{i}}}\right]<_{\mathit{lex}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[X_{j}}}\right]\leq_{\mathit{lex}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[r(X_{i})}}\right]
where

.
Because of simple properties of the lexicographical order and since
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[r(X_{i})}}\right]

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[X_{i}}}\right]
as proper prefix,
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[X_{j}}}\right]

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[X_{i}}}\right]

Therefore, there must be a symbol

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[X_{a}}}\right]}}\right|<\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[X_{i}}}\right]}}\right|<\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[X_{r}}}\right]}}\right|\leq\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[X_{j}}}\right]}}\right|

otherwise there would be a symbol generating
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[X_{i}}}\right]
in

Note that
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[X_{r}}}\right]\leq_{\mathit{lex}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[X_{j}}}\right]<_{\mathit{lex}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[r(X_{i})}}\right]
because
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[X_{r}}}\right]
is a prefix of
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[X_{j}}}\right]
,
and
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[X_{i}}}\right]<_{\mathit{lex}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[X_{r}}}\right]

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[X_{i}}}\right]
is a prefix of
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[X_{r}}}\right]

Also note that

We now show the claim by deducing

Because
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[X_{i}}}\right]
is a proper prefix of
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[X_{r}}}\right]
and
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[X_{a}}}\right]
is a proper prefix of
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[X_{i}}}\right]
, we can decompose
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[X_{r}}}\right]
into

with

Clearly,

.
Because of Lemma

From

4
Lexicographically sorting a Lyndon Grammar

Given a Lyndon SLP
\mathcal{G}
with roots
r_{1},\dots,r_{k}
, we want to
rename the symbols such that
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[X_{i}}}\right]<_{\mathit{lex}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[X_{j}}}\right]
holds if and only if
i<j
.
This is possible in
\mathcal{O}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|\mathcal{G}}}\right|}}\right)
using a similar principle to what
is used for computing the Lyndon grouping in
[
2
,
39
]
:
consider a symbol
X_{i}
. By Lemma
18
and simple properties of Lyndon words, the symbols in

\mathcal{D}(X_{i})\setminus\mathcal{P}_{L}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(X_{i}}}\right)
are lexicographically greater than
X_{i}
.
Therefore, when considering the symbols in lexicographically decreasing order
we can “induce” the position of a symbol
X_{i}\to X_{a}X_{b}
upon encountering
X_{b}
:
the symbols in
\mathcal{P}_{L}^{-1}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(X_{i}}}\right)
must be the lexicographically largest symbols with
X_{a}
as prefix symbol that have not yet been induced.

Algorithm
1
shows the procedure.
Throughout,
\mathsf{A}[i]
is either the
i
th symbol in the lexicographical order or
\bot
, and for each inserted
X_{i}
,
\mathsf{G}[i]
is the index in
\mathsf{A}
of the smallest child of
X_{i}
that has been inserted into
\mathsf{A}
(or
r(X_{i})+1
if no child has been inserted yet).
In the first for-loop, all terminal symbols are inserted into
\mathsf{A}
, and in the second for-loop, the remaining symbols are induced.
Consequently, after the second for-loop,
\mathsf{A}
contains the lexicographic order of the symbols, and
\mathsf{G}
is (almost) it’s inverse, i.e.
\mathsf{G}[\mathsf{A}[i]]=i+1
.

There are two operations in Algorithm
1
that are not immediately obvious, namely finding
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|\mathcal{P}_{L}^{-1}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(X_{i}}}\right)}}\right|
and iterating over all
X_{j}
with
X_{j}\to X_{a}X_{\mathsf{A}[i]}
for a given
\mathsf{A}[i]
.
For the first, note that the values
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|\mathcal{P}_{L}^{-1}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(X_{i}}}\right)}}\right|
can be trivially computed in linear-time using the recurrence

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|\mathcal{P}_{L}^{-1}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(X_{a}}}\right)}}\right|=1+\sum_{X_{i}\to X_{a}X_{b}}{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|\mathcal{P}_{L}^{-1}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(X_{i}}}\right)}}\right|}
.
Secondly, iterating efficiently over all
X_{j}
with
X_{j}\to X_{a}X_{\mathsf{A}[i]}

can be achieved using a linear-time preprocessing step where we collect for each symbol
X_{i}
the symbols which have
X_{i}
as second symbol on the right-hand side.

The following theorem follows.

Theorem 19
.

Algorithm
1
lexicographically sorts a Lyndon SLP in linear time.

Proof.

Define
\mathcal{PA}_{\ell}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(X_{i}}}\right)=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{j\mid X_{j}\to X_{i}X_{a}}}\right\}
.
We prove by induction that the following invariants hold before each iteration of the second for-loop in Algorithm
1
.
The claim then follows immediately.
Let
\pi
be the (unique) permutation of
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[0\,..\,n}}\right)
such that
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[X_{\pi[i]}}}\right]<_{\mathit{lex}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[X_{\pi[j]}}}\right]
if and only if

.
After the second for-loop finishes,
\mathsf{A}=\pi
.
Denote by
\mathcal{I}\subseteq\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[0\,..\,n}}\right)
the set of integers in
\mathsf{A}
, i.e. the symbols already inserted.

We claim that before the
k
-th iteration (i.e.

), the following holds:

[(1)]

1.

\mathcal{I}=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\pi[j]\mid j\in\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[i\,..\,n}}\right)}}\right\}\cup\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{j\mid X_{j}\to X_{a}X_{b}\text{ with }\pi^{-1}[b]>i}}\right\}\cup\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{j\mid X_{j}\to c\in\Sigma}}\right\}

\mathsf{A}[j]\neq\bot\iff\mathsf{A}[j]\in\mathcal{I}

3.

\mathsf{A}[j]\neq\bot\iff\mathsf{A}[j]=\pi[j]

4.

\mathsf{G}[k]=\min\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{l(X_{j})\mid j\in\mathcal{I},X_{j}\to X_{k}X_{b}}}\right\}\cup\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{r(X_{k})+1}}\right\}}}\right)
for each

Induction base (

)

After the first for-loop, we have
\mathsf{G}[i]=r(X_{i})
,
\mathsf{G}[i]=l(X_{i})-1
and
\mathsf{A}[\mathsf{G}[i]]=i
for each terminal symbol

.

to

5.1
Computing
\mathsf{BWT}
and
\mathsf{eBWT}

When the text

\mathsf{BBWT}
of

is equal to the original
\mathsf{BWT}
of
S

[
3
]

Since the
\mathsf{BWT}
is independent of the rotation of the input and

is a Lyndon word, we have
\mathsf{BBWT}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\$S}}\right)=\mathsf{BWT}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(S\$}}\right)

\mathsf{BWT}
commonly computed via the suffix array.
Therefore, we can simply compute the $-
\mathsf{BWT}
by prepending a sentinel character to the text. Note that this is trivial and can be done after building the Lyndon grammar of

The
\mathsf{eBWT}
of a set

of strings is the same as the
\mathsf{BBWT}
of the string
\mathcal{S}^{c}
that arises from arranging the canonical forms of the strings in

in lexicographically decreasing order
[
39
]

In particular, the Lyndon factors of
\mathcal{S}^{c}
are exactly the canonical forms of the input strings (assuming that the input strings are primitive).
Note that, in Algorithm
3
, sorting the canonical forms of the input strings is done implicitly in the first for-loop.

Note that the Lyndon grammars of the input strings are independent of each other (as long as equal Lyndon words are assigned equal grammar symbols).
Therefore, we can also construct their Lyndon grammars independently of each other while using the same dictionary.
As a consequence, it is easy to use our algorithms for parallel construction of the Lyndon grammar of a collection of sequences.
Although our algorithm for deriving the
\mathsf{BWT}
from the grammar is not parallelized, parallel construction of the Lyndon grammar leads to a substantial reduction in wall-clock-time because it is by far the most time-consuming part of the pipeline (see Section
7
).

Constant time suffix comparisons

Because the Lyndon forest (and thus the Lyndon SLP) is closely related to the Lyndon array, we can use the Lyndon array to decide whether

For a

Formally,
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[X_{t}}}\right]<_{\mathit{lex}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[X_{c}}}\right]\iff\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[X_{r}}}\right]<_{\mathit{lex}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[X_{r^{\prime}}}}\right]
.
If such a tuple
(\ell,t^{\prime},c^{\prime})
does not exist, either
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[X_{t}}}\right]
and
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[X_{c}}}\right]
do not share a non-empty prefix (i.e.,
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[X_{t}}}\right][0]\neq\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[X_{c}}}\right][0]
),
or one of
X_{t}
and
X_{c}
is a prefix of the other.

Note that the indices of the elements in
\mathcal{P}_{L}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(X_{i}}}\right)
have the same relative order as the lengths of the generated strings in the sense that
for all
a,b\in\mathcal{P}_{L}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(X_{i}}}\right)
we have
a<b
if and only if
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[X_{a}}}\right]}}\right|<\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[X_{b}}}\right]}}\right|
.
For this reason, we can proceed with a two-pointer search to find
\ell
(and
t^{\prime}
and
c^{\prime}
).
More specifically, assuming that the desired
\ell\in\mathcal{P}_{L}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(X_{t}}}\right)\cap\mathcal{P}_{L}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(X_{c}}}\right)
exists, we have
\ell\in\mathcal{P}_{L}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(X_{a}}}\right)
if
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[X_{t}}}\right]}}\right|<\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[X_{c}}}\right]}}\right|
for
X_{c}\to X_{a}X_{b}
, and vice versa.

shows the procedure.

6.3
Construction in
\mathcal{O}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(N\log g+g\log^{2}g}}\right)

In this Section, we describe an algorithm that is able to compute the Lyndon grammar online in
\mathcal{O}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(N\log g+g\log^{2}g}}\right)
deterministic time
from right to left in a streaming fashion using
\mathcal{O}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(g}}\right)
words of extra memory.

Basically, this is done by maintaining the grammar’s set of symbols
\mathcal{S}
in an ordered sequence, lexicographically sorted by their respective generated strings.
Using e.g. a B-Tree
[
5
]
, one can then find the rank of any symbol in
\mathcal{O}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\log\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|\mathcal{S}}}\right|}}\right)
time.
Thus, determining the lexicographical order of two symbols in
\mathcal{S}
can also be done in
\mathcal{O}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\log\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|\mathcal{S}}}\right|}}\right)
time.
What remains to be shown is how the symbols can be maintained in this sorted arrangement.

As shown in Section
3
, a lexicographically sorted Lyndon grammar has a forest-structure (cf. Figure
3
).
This first-symbol forest can be represented using a
balanced parenthesis sequence (BPS)
of length
2\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|\mathcal{S}}}\right|

[
24
]
, which can be obtained using a depth-first traversal of the first-symbol forest (starting at the roots) by writing an opening parenthesis
‘(’
when visiting a node for the first time and a closing parenthesis
‘)’
when all subtrees of a node have been visited
[
24
]
.
Each parenthesis pair corresponds to a symbol in the grammar, where the
i
th opening parenthesis corresponds to the
i
th smallest symbol (by lexicographical order).
For a grammar with symbol set
\mathcal{S}
, let this BPS be
\mathcal{B}_{\mathcal{S}}
.
We represent
\mathcal{B}_{\mathcal{S}}
as an ordered sequence
\mathcal{T}_{\mathcal{S}}
, which contains two markers
\texttt{(}_{i}
and
\texttt{)}_{i}
for each symbol
X_{i}
in
\mathcal{S}
, such that the ranks of
\texttt{(}_{i}
and
\texttt{)}_{i}
are the indices of
X_{i}
’s opening and closing parenthesis in
\mathcal{B}_{\mathcal{S}}
, respectively.
For instance, the sequence
\mathcal{T}_{\mathcal{S}}
for the lexicographically sorted Lyndon grammar of our running example (see Figure
3
) would be
\texttt{(}_{1}\texttt{(}_{2}\texttt{(}_{3}\texttt{(}_{4}\texttt{)}_{4}\texttt{)}_{3}\texttt{)}_{2}\texttt{(}_{5}\texttt{(}_{6}\texttt{)}_{6}\texttt{)}_{5}\texttt{)}_{1}\texttt{(}_{7}\texttt{(}_{8}\texttt{)}_{8}\texttt{)}_{7}\texttt{(}_{9}\texttt{)}_{9}
.

Now consider adding a new symbol
X_{i}\to X_{a}X_{b}
, where
X_{a}
and
X_{b}
are in
\mathcal{T}
(i.e.
X_{i}\notin\mathcal{S}
,
X_{a},X_{b}\in\mathcal{S}
).
By Lemma
16
, the parent of
X_{i}
’s parenthesis pair in
\mathcal{B}_{\mathcal{S}\cup\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{X_{i}}}\right\}}
must be
X_{a}
’s parenthesis pair.
Therefore, it suffices to determine
X_{i}
’s lexicographically smallest “sibling”
X_{j}\to X_{a}X_{c}
(
X_{j}\in\mathcal{S}
) with
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[X_{c}}}\right]>_{\mathit{lex}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[X_{b}}}\right]
;
X_{i}
’s parenthesis pair must appear immediately in front of
\texttt{(}_{j}
in
\mathcal{T}_{\mathcal{S}\cup\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{X_{i}}}\right\}}
.
If there is no such sibling,
X_{i}
is (currently) the largest child of
X_{a}
and thus
X_{i}
’s parenthesis pair must be immediately in front of
\texttt{)}_{a}
instead.
Note that comparing
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[X_{b}}}\right]
and
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[X_{c}}}\right]
is possible in
\mathcal{O}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\log\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|\mathcal{S}}}\right|}}\right)
because both
X_{b}
and
X_{c}
are in
\mathcal{T}_{\mathcal{S}}
.

In order to be able to find the correct sibling of
X_{i}
as described, we additionally maintain for each
X_{a}
an ordered sequence
\mathcal{T}_{a}
containing the symbols
\mathcal{S}_{a}=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{X_{k}\to X_{a}X_{b}\mid X_{k},X_{b}\in\mathcal{S}}}\right\}
in lexicographical order.
Inserting
X_{i}\to X_{a}X_{b}
into
\mathcal{T}_{a}
can then be accomplished with
\mathcal{O}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\log\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|\mathcal{S}_{a}}}\right|}}\right)\subseteq\mathcal{O}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\log\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|\mathcal{S}}}\right|}}\right)
comparisons, each of which is possible in
\mathcal{O}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\log{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|\mathcal{S}}}\right|}}}\right)
via
\mathcal{T}_{\mathcal{S}}
.

In total, we obtain a time complexity of
\mathcal{O}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|\mathcal{S}}}\right|\log^{2}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|\mathcal{S}}}\right|}}\right)
for maintaining the grammar’s symbols lexicographically sorted, and
\mathcal{O}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(N\log\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|\mathcal{S}}}\right|}}\right)
for constructing the Lyndon forest.

8
Conclusion and Further Work

We described an algorithm to compute the
\mathsf{BBWT}
—and by extension the common $-
\mathsf{BWT}
and various versions of the
\mathsf{eBWT}
—from the lexicographically sorted Lyndon grammar of a text or text collection.
Furthermore, we gave an algorithm that lexicographically sorts a Lyndon grammar and discussed approaches to efficiently compute the Lyndon grammar of a text or text collection.
We implemented the described algorithms and found that they outperform other current state-of-the art programs in terms of time or memory consumed (often both).

Currently, we use 32-bit integers for the grammar symbols.
For extremely large and diverse datasets, this may not suffice.
