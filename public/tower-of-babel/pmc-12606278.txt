Effect of maternal programming through polyherbal, multimineral, and multivitamin supplementation on behavioural and productive performance in transition murrah buffaloes
Authors: Sadhana Tiwari, Indu Devi, Divyanshu Singh Tomar, Rimee Dhakad
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The transition period around calving is a vulnerable phase for dairy animals, often marked by negative energy balance (NEB), metabolic disorders, and suppressed milk production. This study evaluated the impact of targeted supplementation with a polyherbal-multimineral-multivitamin blend on the behavioural patterns, metabolic status, and postpartum performance of Murrah buffaloes. In this study, advanced pregnant Murrah buffaloes were screened on the basis of their blood β-hydroxybutyrate (mmol/L), non-esterified fatty acids (µmol/L) and glucose (mg/dl) levels to identify animals at risk of metabolic stress in pre-partum period. A total of 21 advanced pregnant buffaloes were divided into three groups: a healthy control (T0), a NEB-affected unsupplemented group (T1 or positive control), and a NEB-affected supplemented group (T2 or treatment). Supplementation in T2 began 30 days before calving and continued until 15 days postpartum. Behavioural parameters such as feeding behaviour, drinking behaviour, rumination, and activity patterns were observed. The T1 group showed significant declines in feeding and drinking behaviour, increased lying time, and significantly lesser standing time, indicating metabolic stress. However, improved behavioural parameters and significantly higher milk yield were observed in the T2 group as compared to T1, and exhibited behaviour similar to that of healthy buffaloes (T0). Supplemented animals also exhibited faster metabolic recovery postpartum and produced significantly higher milk than the other two groups. The results suggest that early and strategic nutritional intervention with polyherbal, vitamin, and mineral support can mitigate the adverse effects of NEB.

Introduction

The transition period, which consists of the last 3 weeks of gestation and the first 3 weeks of lactation, is one of the most metabolically challenging phases in the life of a dairy animal. Dairy animals experience nutritional stress, metabolic stress, physiological stress, and behavioural stress during the pre- and post-partum period1 and are more susceptible to metabolic and infectious diseases2. These changes occur due to nutritional stress because of a reduction in dry matter intake (DMI) during the last three weeks of pregnancy, followed by a sudden increase in nutrient demands for the onset of lactation3,4. This negative energy balance (NEB) leads to increased levels of non-esterified fatty acids (NEFA) and ketone bodies, particularly β-hydroxybutyric acid (BHBA), which predispose animals to various metabolic disorders such as ketosis, fatty liver, and displaced abomasum5. Animals with ≥ 0.30 mEq/L serum concentration of NEFA during prepartum period were susceptible twice to develop one or more metabolic diseases (Displaced abomasum, ketosis, metritis, and RFM)6. Blood glucose, NEFA and BHBA are best indicators of energy balnce during transition period and dry matter intake is positively correlated with blood glucose level in dairy cattle7. The blood glucose level reflects the energy balnce and it is negatively correlated with milk yield, NEFA and BHBA levels during first two weeks of postpartum8.

Behavioural alterations occur during the transition period, for example, reduced feeding time, fewer feeding and drinking bouts, and changes in rumination and activity patterns (standing time, walking time, and resting time). These behavioural patterns serve as early indicators of metabolic imbalance9,10. Monitoring of these changes in behaviour can act as a tool for the early identification of health problems and to implement targeted nutritional interventions for well well-being of animals11,12.

Maternal programming in dairy animals refers to the influence of the dam’s nutritional and metabolic status during late pregnancy on her own health and productivity after calving, as well as on the development and future performance of the calf13.

Maternal nutritional programming is a promising strategy to counter the negative effects of NEB and improve both maternal and neonatal health. Micronutrients such as copper, zinc, and manganese, along with vitamins A and E, play a crucial role in maintaining immune function, antioxidant status, and metabolic homeostasis in periparturient animals14,15. Several traditional herbs are commonly used, either alone or in combination, to induce uterine contractions, stimulate the immune system, activate blood circulation, evacuate the placenta, and expel the lochia in postpartum animals16. Research studies have demonstrated that a combination of herbs can help minimise other post-partum metabolic problems such as ketosis, lameness, metritis, and foetal membrane retention etc.17,18,. Various herbs like Asparagus racemosus (shatavari), Trigonella foenum-graecum (fenugreek), Nigella sativa (black cumin), Tinospora cordifolia (giloy), Curcuma longa (turmeric), and Ocimum sanctum (tulsi) have been well-documented for benefits on health and productivity in ruminants19,20.

Although several studies have explored the effect of individual micronutrients and herbs feeding on animal health. However, limited studies have studied the effect of combined micronutrient and polyherbal supplementation, especially in the context of maternal programming during the prepartum phase in buffaloes. Furthermore, very little attention has been given to how such feeding interventions influence the feeding behaviour, activity patterns, and productive performance during the transition period. Therefore, the present study was designed to study the effect of supplementation of a combination of polyherbal mixture (shatavari, fenugreek, black cumin, giloy, turmeric, tulsi), multimineral (Cu, Mn, Zn) and multivitamin (Vitamin A, E) mixture during the pre and post-partum period on behavioural performance of dairy buffaloes. Special emphasis was given on animals identified to be in NEB through blood BHBA profiling, thereby allowing for targeted intervention to enhance health outcomes and milk productivity during early lactation.

Materials and methods

Ethics statement

The study protocol was reviewed and approved by the Institute Animal Ethics Committee (Ref: 47/IAEC/22/04). This study also strictly adhered to the ARRIVE (Animal Research: Reporting of In Vivo Experiments) guidelines. All methods were performed following the relevant guidelines and regulations.

Study location

This study was carried out at the Livestock Research Centre (LRC) of ICAR–National Dairy Research Institute, Karnal, Haryana, India (29.43°N, 77.20°E; elevation 250 m above sea level). The climate in this region is subtropical, with average annual rainfall between 700 and 960 mm. Relative humidity typically ranges from 41% to 85%, and vapour pressure varies between 7 and 25 mmHg. Temperatures range from as low as 1.7 °C in winter to highs of around 45 °C in summer.

Animals

The study involved 21 Murrah buffaloes (Bubalus bubalis) reared and living at the Livestock Research Centre (LRC) of ICAR–National Dairy Research Institute, Karnal, Haryana, India. The experimental buffaloes were housed in a loose housing system under group management practice. The total space of the paddock was 150 m2, with 10 m2 per animal. Three sides of the paddocks were fenced by metal pipe, and the remaining side was guarded by feeding mangers and water troughs with a concrete standing platform. Around 2–3 weeks before calving, buffaloes were moved into individual calving pens with brick-on-edge floors, rubber mats, and access to feed and clean water. Feeding of buffaloes was done as per ICAR standards based on nutrient requirements of cattle and buffalo, which is based on DM, DCP, TDN, Calcium and phosphorus intake. All the buffaloes were fed as per NDRI farm standard practices adopted during the prepartum period. Buffaloes were fed ad libitum available green fodder (Oct-Dec: maize (Zea mays) and jowar (Sorghum bicolor); Jan-Mar: berseem (Trifolium alexandrinum) and oat (Avena sativa); Apr-June: napier-bajra (hybrid grass between Napier grass (Pennisetum purpureum) and pearl millet (Pennisetum glaucum); July-Sept: berseem and mustard (Brassica juncea) and dry fodders and/or silage with limited concentrates based on the animal’s body weight. About 15–25 kg of green fodder was fed to each adult animal per day. Feeding schedule (particularly concentrate) of individual animals was adjusted fortnightly based on the stage of gestation of the animal so that the DMI of dry animal should be 3% of B.W. During the postpartum period, buffaloes were fed ad lib available fodders and concentrates based on their milk production level. The concentrate mixture (20–22% CP and 70–75% TDN) composition is given in Table 1. It was offered at 1.5 kg/day for maintenance, with an additional 1.5 kg in late gestation and 1 kg per 2.5 kg of milk in lactation. Ad lib water is provided in a water trough round the clock to all animals. In addition to routine feeding for all three groups, the animals in the treatment group (T2) were supplemented with a total of 225 g/day of a ground herbal mixture, 25 g of black salt, and 250 g of jaggery from 30 days prepartum to 15 days postpartum.

Experimental design

We began by screening a herd of advanced pregnant Murrah buffaloes for their blood β-hydroxybutyric acid (BHBA) levels using the eBketone Vet LK-01 meter and test strips. We did not find any published study for validation of the eBketone Vet LK-01 meter in buffalo species. However, considering the results of studies21,22 for use of handheld meters of other brands for measurement of BHBA in dairy cows and almost same levels of blood plasma BHBA in cows (1.0 −1.4 mmol/L) and buffaloes (1-1.4 mmol/L)23 for sub-clinical ketosis. We conducted an internal validation by comparing readings from the handheld device with BHBA concentrations obtained through manual laboratory analysis of blood plasma using a biochemistry analyser (Erba EM 200). The values were consistent across the clinical and subclinical range, providing confidence in the reliability of the handheld device for use in this study. Concentrations of BHBA ranging from 1.0 to 1.4 mmol/L of blood plasma are considered diagnostic of hyperketonemia in cattle24. Screenings were done every two weeks, starting 60 days before the expected calving date. A BHBA level of 0.6 mmol/L was used as the threshold for identifying animals at risk of metabolic stress in the transition period25. From this screening, 21 multiparous buffaloes were selected 30 days before calving. These animals were then divided into three groups of seven animals each:

The animals in the treatment group (T2) were supplemented with a total of 225 g/day of a ground herbal mixture, 25 g of black salt, and 250 g of jaggery from 30 days prepartum to 15 days postpartum. The polyherbal blend (detailed in Table 2) included six herbs selected based on previous studies and their traditional use in improving metabolic health in dairy animals26–28. Each individual herb was procured from the local market after assessing its quality in consultation with an Ayurveda practitioner. Each herb was ground thoroughly, and the required quantity was packed in individual packaging for further use. Vitamin A was provided at double the NRC29 (2001) recommended maintenance level to support immune function, while the vitamin E dosage was based on the equivalency of 1 IU = 1 mg α-tocopheryl acetate. Thus, to supplement 3000 IU of Vitamin E, 3000 mg (equivalent to 3 g) of alpha-tocopheryl acetate was fed. Mineral supplementation (Cu, Mn and Zn) followed Tiwari et al.30 recommendations for transition animals.

Sample collection, animal monitoring for production and metabolic diseases

Blood samples of animals were collected by an experienced veterinary doctor during morning hours (07:00–08:00 h) at −60, −45, −30, −15, −7, 0, 7, 15, 30 days of pre and postpartum from the jugular vein using heparinized vacutainers. The blood plasma was separated by centrifugation and stored at −20 °C for further analysis. Plasma glucose (mg/dl) and NEFA (µmol/L) were analysed using the fully automated chemistry analyser -EM 200 (Transasia Bio-medicals Ltd. Mumbai-2, India). Milking of postpartum buffaloes was done twice a day at 05:30 in the morning and 17:30 h in the evening in the milking barn and average milk yield of individual buffalo was calculated by taking average of morning and evening milk yield. Milk yield was recorded on a fixed (test) day at weekly interval to track the post-partum performance. Occurrence of metabolic diseases like ketosis, sub-clinical mastitis, milk fever, and lameness was observed at postpartum period and confirmation was done by the expert veterinary doctor at the farm. All the buffaloes were examined for ketosis by observing drop in milk yield, slight reduction in feed intake, depression, weight loss and acetone smell (fruity odour) in breath and confirmed by low blood glucose level and high BHBA and NEFA concentration in blood plasma25,33,34. All the buffaloes were examined for sub-clinical mastitis by screening of milk for somatic cell count (lactoscan milk analyser SCC kit, manufactured by Milkotronic Ltd.)) and observing swelling, inflammation of the teats at milking, physical aspects of the milk (colour and consistency) and the animals with symptoms of clinical mastitis were examined and confirmed by expert veterinarian. All the buffaloes were examined for milk fever by observing for low blood calcium level within first 24 h of post calving characterized by downer cow syndrome and confirmed by expert doctor. All the buffaloes were examined for lameness by observing leg pain, abnormal gait, unable to move in postpartum period35. Diagnosis and confirmation of lameness or lame walking36 was done by taking the help of expert veterinarian of institute farm.

Behavioural observations

Animal behaviour was recorded 24/7 from 60 days before to 10 days after calving using a 9-channel CP Plus DVR system (Model: CP-UVR-0401E1-CV2) with nine cameras positioned at a height of 243.83 cm to capture all areas including paddocks and calving pens. Animals were marked with temporary alphanumeric symbols using white colour paint for easy identification during video playback. Our goal was to get average weekly value (taking average of two fixed days per week) for individual behaviour recording for each animal. Video footage was reviewed at 2x to 16x speed, depending on activity, and start/end points of each behavioural event were identified by continuous observation method. Three expert observers were used to extract the behavioural activities information of buffaloes from the recorded videos and inter-observer reliability (percentage agreement) was > 95%. The duration of each behaviour (min/d) was measured as the difference in period between the start and end point of that particular activity37. We focused on the following daily behaviours: feeding time, number of feeding bouts, rumination time, drinking time and bouts, lying time, standing time, and walking time. Feeding time (min/d) was defined as active head movement in the manger; drinking as muzzle contact with water; a new bout was counted after at least 5 min of inactivity34. Rumination time (min/d) of each buffalo was considered when the buffalo was masticating regurgitated feed, swallowing of masticated feed/regurgitating feed with head in erect position in both phases standing and lying36. Lying time (min/d) was considered when the animal’s flank touched the ground, but the animal is not ruminating. When buffaloes were standing inactively without eating and swinging its head in any direction, it was measured as standing time38. Walking time (min/d) was characterized by at least 2 or 3 consecutive limb movements with even gait and animal was moving forward with medium to long strides36.

Statistical analysis

Effect of nutritional supplementation in three groups under study were studied using three-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) (factors: parity, pre-partum period, treatment) with SPSSv22.0 package (1996) (IBM SPSS Statistics)60. ‘Parity’ was used as a fixed effect or factor in statistical analysis to account for biological differences across lactations and to enable meaningful comparisons of traits such as milk yield, reproductive intervals, and disease Incidence. In this study, we used buffaloes of 2 to 8 parity (multiparous, av (mean ± SE) 4.90 ± 0.98). So, a large range of parity was there but buffaloes were classified in three groups by considering the point that it was not significantly different among the three groups at time of starting of experiment. ‘Period’ was second factor used for studying the interaction effect, in this study, factor effect was studied in relation to pre-and post partum period. Treatment (feeding supplementation) was third important factor to see the effect of supplementary feeding. Results of the study are presented as mean ± standard error (SE). Tukey’s post hoc test was used for pairwise comparisons. Differences were considered statistically significant at p ≤ 0.05 (5% level) and highly significant p < 0.01 (1% level).

Behavioural responses of buffaloes during pre- and post-partum periods

As shown in Table 4, feeding behaviour was significantly affected by elevated blood BHBA levels. Although the total feeding time did not differ significantly among the groups, the number of feeding bouts/day were significantly lower in the T1 group (10.54 ± 0.38) compared to the T0 (13.77 ± 0.39) and T2 (12.07 ± 0.35) groups (p ≤ 0.05). Drinking behaviour followed a similar trend. The positive control group spent significantly less time drinking (31.96 ± 0.81 min/day) compared to both control and treatment animals (p ≤ 0.05). Rumination time was significantly shorter in the T1 group (434.36 ± 9.57 min/day) as compared to T0 (502.30 ± 7.89) and T2 (500.41 ± 5.81). There was no any significant difference in standing and lying time of T0 and T2. However, we did not find any significant difference in the walking time of buffaloes across the groups.

‘Period’ effect (pre-partum) was found significant on feeding time, drinking time, lying time; period effect was found significant for all behaviour parameters except walking time (Table 7). ‘Parity’ significantly affects feeding time, drinking, lying time and ‘treatment’ affects feeding time, rumination time, standing time, and walking time. Interaction of ‘treatment’ and ‘period’ was significant for rumination only, and Interaction of ‘parity’ and ‘period’ was non-significant for all behavioural parameters (Table 7).

Post-calving behavioural data (Table 5) further reinforced the benefits of nutritional intervention. Buffaloes in the treatment group showed feeding time (213.02 ± 8.76 min/day) and feeding frequency (13.07 ± 0.41 bouts/day) that closely matched those of the healthy control group and were significantly better than the positive control group (p ≤ 0.05). Similarly, drinking behaviour was also improved in the treatment group, in terms of both drinking time and bout frequency. Both parameters were significantly higher than in the positive control group (p ≤ 0.05). Rumination time post-partum remained significantly lower in the positive control group (392.45 ± 10.54 min/day), while the treatment group not only recovered but exceeded the control group (511.25 ± 8.77 vs. 493.45 ± 10.14 min/day). Activity levels also varied, with the positive control group showing prolonged lying time and reduced standing time. The treatment group, however, maintained more balanced standing (657.98 ± 17.25 min/day) and lying durations.

Incidence of metabolic disorders during post-partum period

The incidence of common metabolic disorders in the post-partum period showed clear differences across the three groups of buffaloes (Table 6). No case of ketosis was reported from the control (T0) and the treatment group (T2), whereas three cases were recorded in the positive control group (T1). Similarly, milk fever and lameness were noted only in the T1 group (two cases each), whereas none were recorded for animals in the T0 and T2 groups. Cases of sub-clinical mastitis were diagnosed in T0 and T1 groups (one case each), though no incidence occurred in the treatment group.

Effect of nutritional interventions on milk yield during the post-partum period

Effect of nutritional interventions on the production performance of experimental Murrah buffaloes was studied from the 1 st to 8th week of lactation during the post-partum period (Fig. 1). The Murrah buffaloes that received nutritional supplementation (T2) consistently produced more milk compared to both the control (T0) and positive control (T1) groups. From 3rd week onwards, daily milk yield (kg/day) of Murrah buffaloes of the T2 group was found to be significantly (p < 0.05) higher compared to T0 and T1 groups. Overall (mean ± SE) milk yield (kg/day) of T0, T1 and T2 groups was 8.1 ± 0.14, 7.6 ± 0.2 and 10.53 ± 0.32, respectively. The overall milk yield of the T2 group was significantly (p < 0.05) higher than compared to T0 and T1, furthermore, the overall milk yield of the T0 group was found to be significantly (p < 0.05) higher than T1 group. For milk yield of buffaloes, each factor, i.e. parity, treatment and period, was affecting individually in a significant manner (Table 7), and the interaction of parity and period was also highly significant (p < 0.01).

Discussion

The most significant challenges during the transition period is the negative energy balance (NEB), due to decrease in dry matter intake in the peripartum period39. The early biomarkers of NEB and impending metabolic disorders, such as ketosis, is blood beta-hydroxybutyric acid (BHBA), non-esterified fatty acids (NEFA) and glucose, as noted in previous studies22,40,41. During the pre-partum period (3–9 days before), cows having BHBA concentration 0.6 mmol/L were 2.2 times more likely to develop ketosis within 1 week post calving, as were cows with a prepartum BHBA concentration < 0.6 mmol/L24. The cut-off value of BHBA for sub-clinical ketosis in buffaloes is between 1 and 1.4 mmol/L24. Before calving, cows with BHBA concentrations > 1.0-1.4 mmol/L had a higher risk of developing clinical ketosis, metritis, and displaced abomasum compared to similar cows with BHBA concentrations < 1.0 mmol/L42. Our study aligns with these findings, during the post-partum period, cases of ketosis, milk fever and lameness were reported only in T1 group, which indicates that the increased prepartum levels of BHBA and NEFA is a risk factor for ketosis. By day − 30, T1 and T2 animals had reached or exceeded the established threshold of 0.6 mmol/L25, indicative of increased risk for developing hyperketonemia post-calving. This confirmed their suitability for inclusion in the study as metabolically challenged groups. Behavioural observations during the transition period revealed distinct differences across the three experimental groups, with the positive control group consistently showing signs of stress and metabolic imbalance, while the treatment group showed improved behavioural parameters comparable to the healthy controls.

The lesser number of feeding bouts/day in the positive control group compared to the control and treatment groups indicates that buffaloes at metabolic risk (T1 group) were less motivated to consume feed frequently, potentially due to discomfort or altered energy levels. However, in the treatment group (T2), despite starting with significantly higher BHBA levels, animals showed improved feeding behaviour with more frequent feeding bouts, indicating a positive impact of the nutritional supplementation. These behavioural changes are critical early indicators of NEB and developing disease, and their predictive value is well supported in the literature. González et al.43 reported that DMI significantly decreases at least three days before the clinical signs of ketosis. Drackley et al.3 reported that reduced feed intake in late pregnancy predisposes cows to postpartum ketonemia. Further, Huzzey et al.44 found that cows with lower prepartum feeding activity had higher chances of developing postpartum diseases, indicating that behaviour can serve as a reliable indicator of metabolic health. In T1 group, drinking time and frequency were significantly reduced, indicating that reduced feed intake also affects water intake; a similar pattern was observed by Bareille et al.45 Reduced water intake affect feed intake, milk production and adding to NEB and overall metabolic stress. However, these adverse trends were alleviated in the T2 group of buffaloes, who received supplementation of polyherbal components (shatavari, fenugreek, black cumin, giloy, turmeric, tulsi), multiminerals (Cu, Mn, Zn), and vitamins (A and E) starting 30 days before calving. These nutraceuticals are well-known for increasing feed intake, improving liver function, also boosting immunity, and have anti-inflammatory and antioxidant effects46,47. For example, giloy and turmeric act as immunomodulators48,49fenugreek promotes appetite50while shatavari helps in hormonal balance and lactogenesis51. It was found that the milk yield of T2 buffaloes increased significantly during the postpartum period than the T0 and T1 groups. This suggests a strong association between improved prepartum energy status and lactation performance, which is in line with earlier findings that reported nutritional and antioxidant supplementation can enhance postpartum productivity in dairy animals31,52,53.

Rumination behaviour is an important behavioural indicator of well being of animal. Buffaloes in group T1 exhibited lower rumination time during the pre- and post-partum period, which is consistent with earlier studies that reported that sick or metabolically challenged animals ruminate less as compared to healthy animals54–56. Reduced rumination typically reflects both diminished DMI and systemic illness. Increased variability in rumination (higher standard error) among T1 animals further indicates individual inconsistency in metabolic adaptation as rumination time is considered a strong indicator of rumen health and functioning. On the other hand, T2 buffaloes showed more stable rumination, suggesting better adaptation to transition stress, which might be due to strategic supplementation of herbs, minerals and multivitamins aiding digestive efficiency during the transition period. The metabolic and physiological stress was also indicated by the animals’ standing and lying behaviour. The T1 group, along with higher BHBA and NEB, exhibited longer lying durations, which in turn gives an indication of fatigue or metabolic stress. Johnson57., Rodriguez et al.59 and Kaufman et al.60 also found that cows with a higher risk for postpartum disorders exhibited more lying and less standing. The animals in T2, who were offered supplementation, were following the same pattern of activity, which was more normal and highlighted the same necessity of nutritional supplementation during this critical period once again. Post-calving behavioural data further reinforced the benefits of nutritional intervention in the supplemented group. Buffaloes in the treatment group showed better feeding time, feeding frequency and drinking time and bout frequency and rumination time that closely matched those of the healthy control group and were significantly better than the positive control group, reflecting improved metabolic and rumen function.

The positive effect of supplementation was also seen on milk yield among the groups. Group T2 animals had the highest average daily milk yield during the early lactation period in comparison to T1, which indicates that the metabolic intervention not only positively affected the health and behaviour of the animal but also resulted in significant productivity increases during the early lactation period. This outcome is supported by the study of Bipate and Misra52who achieved a higher milk yield in buffalos through herbal and mineral supplementation. Sharma et al.53 also observed the increase in productivity of animals that were given antioxidant and vitamin preparations during the transition period. The improved milk yield in the T2 group might be due to improved DMI, nutrient absorption, anti-inflammatory and hepatoprotective effects of compounds such as turmeric and giloy and immune-boosting function of minerals such as Zn, Cu, and Mn, which in turn indirectly supported better lactation performance.

Conclusion - The present study found that pre-partum blood BHBA levels, accompanied by behavioural monitoring, may act as the first signals of incipient metabolic disease. Above all, the results show that the strategic supplementation of polyherbs, multiminerals, and vitamins was successful in changing the behavioural responses, metabolic status, and productivity of Murrah buffaloes during the periparturient period. The timely supplementation provided in the pre- and postpartum periods improves the metabolic health and milk production of buffaloes.
