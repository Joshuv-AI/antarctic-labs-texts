Predicting Major Amputation Rates in Diabetic Hindfoot Ulcers With Critical Infection and Ischemia: A Risk-Factor-Based Model
Authors: Kyu-Il Lee, Woo-Sung Kim, Seung-Kyu Han, Kyung-Chul Moon, Sik Namgoong, Seong-Ho Jeong, Eun-Sang Dhong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundDiabetic hindfoot ulcers pose a substantially greater risk of progressing to major amputation than forefoot or midfoot ulcers. Patients with these ulcers often experience severe psychological distress at the prospect of major limb loss. Consequently, patients, their families, and healthcare professionals prioritize understanding the actual risk of major amputation based on the patient’s current clinical status. However, practical tools addressing this critical and common concern remain limited. In a previous preliminary study, we proposed a model for predicting major amputation rates in diabetic hindfoot ulcers. Nonetheless, that model, which relied solely on ischemia and infection, demonstrated limited accuracy. The present study aimed to refine the prediction of major amputation in diabetic hindfoot ulcers by 1) identifying additional risk factors and 2) evaluating amputation rates according to these factors.MethodsA retrospective analysis was conducted involving 1,663 hospitalized patients diagnosed with diabetic foot ulcers. From this group, 50 patients with hindfoot ulcers presenting with critical ischemia and infection were included. A total of 88 baseline clinical and laboratory parameters were evaluated to determine key risk factors.ResultsChronic kidney disease (CKD) ≥ stage 3, hemoglobin < 10.35 g/dL, and albumin < 3.75 g/dL emerged as independent predictors of major amputation (P < 0.05). Univariable logistic regression demonstrated a strong positive correlation between the number of identified risk factors and the rate of major amputation. Patients without these three risk factors did not undergo major amputation, even in the presence of critical infection and ischemia. Conversely, amputation risk increased to 6% for individuals with one risk factor, 27% for those with two, and 67% for patients with all three (P < 0.001).ConclusionCKD, low serum hemoglobin, and low serum albumin were identified as significant risk factors for major amputation in patients with diabetic hindfoot ulcers complicated by critical infection and ischemia. The risk of major amputation increases substantially with the presence of multiple risk factors, from 6% amputation rate with one factor to 67% with three factors. Nevertheless, prospective studies with larger sample sizes are warranted to validate this risk-factor model.

INTRODUCTION

Although less frequent than forefoot and midfoot ulcers, diabetic hindfoot ulcers represent a substantially greater threat of progressing to major amputation, primarily due to their anatomically complex location. The probability of successful limb salvage in hindfoot ulcers is reported to be two to three times lower than that of midfoot ulcers.1 The increasing rate of major amputations among patients with diabetic foot ulcers (DFUs) is mainly attributed to hindfoot ulcer involvement.

Over the past two decades, the authors have managed numerous DFU cases and have often encountered patients experiencing profound distress at the prospect of major amputation. In East Asian societies shaped by Confucian principles, amputation is frequently viewed as a devastating outcome. It is at times regarded with even greater fear than death due to strong cultural convictions about preserving the body’s integrity.2 Multiple studies have reported risk factors associated with major amputation in individuals with DFUs.34567 Some investigations have specifically addressed the outcomes and clinical profiles of hindfoot ulcers.89 Nonetheless, according to our extensive clinical experience, the practical utility of simply cataloging risk factors is limited. Patients, their families, and healthcare professionals tend to prioritize understanding the actual risk of major amputation based on the patient’s current clinical status, rather than focusing on the mere presence or absence of specific risk factors.

In an earlier investigation intended to address this gap, we introduced a risk-scoring model to predict major amputation among individuals with diabetic hindfoot ulcers. The model utilized the severity of ischemia and infection—both recognized as central influences on wound healing and as principal drivers of lower limb amputation.10111213 Each patient’s composite risk score ranged from 0 to 4, calculated by summing the individual ischemia and infection severity ratings. Our findings demonstrated a strong positive association between advancing risk scores and the rate of major amputation (P for trend < 0.001). Importantly, a risk score of 3 was identified as a statistically robust threshold for forecasting major amputation. Patients with scores ≤ 2 had a major amputation rate of 4.8%. In contrast, those with scores ≥ 3, indicating concurrent critical infection and ischemia, showed a markedly higher rate of 20%.

Nonetheless, wound healing is affected by a variety of factors beyond infection and ischemia. To predict an individual patient’s risk of amputation more precisely, it is necessary to consider these additional variables. However, a comprehensive assessment involving this broad spectrum of complex, interrelated factors was not feasible in the initial study. As a result, the earlier model was limited to predictions based only on ischemia and infection. To address this limitation and improve the accuracy of predictions, the current follow-up study was designed to target high-risk patients who presented with both critical ischemia and infection.

The objectives of this study were 1) to determine major risk factors associated with major amputation among patients with infected, ischemic diabetic hindfoot ulcers, and 2) to provide quantified rates of major amputation in relation to identified risk factors.

METHODS

Summary of management protocol

All patients received care based on the International Consensus on the Diabetic Foot guidelines. Upon admission, clinicians obtained a detailed medical history. Baseline laboratory tests, including blood glucose and inflammatory markers, were also performed. Vascular status was assessed using measures such as transcutaneous oxygen pressure (TcPO2), toe pressures, and Doppler ultrasonography. For patients diagnosed with peripheral vascular disease (PVD), percutaneous transluminal angioplasty was arranged and performed by interventional cardiologists. Adjunctive treatment with hyperbaric or normobaric oxygen was provided when tissue hypoxia was identified.

Deep tissue cultures were taken when clinically warranted—for example, in wounds positive for a probe-to-bone test, ulcers persisting longer than 30 days, recurrent presentations, or cases with confirmed PVD, regardless of overt infection. Empiric intravenous antibiotic therapy was started and then specifically adjusted based on culture growth and antibiotic susceptibility profiles.

Surgical or sharp debridement was selected according to wound condition and performed either in the operating room or at bedside. In patients with osteomyelitis confirmed by magnetic resonance imaging and bone biopsy culture, systemic antibiotics were administered for at least 3 to 6 weeks. Neurological evaluation involved the Semmes-Weinstein monofilament test, nerve conduction studies, electromyography, pinprick testing, as well as thermal sensitivity assessment.14

Ulcer-specific off-loading techniques were used, and patients were discharged with individualized therapeutic footwear.

If wounds did not improve despite all interventions, major amputation was considered only as a final option after comprehensive limb-preservation procedures, including flap reconstruction. Amputation was also indicated if life-threatening systemic complications developed.

Risk-scoring system

In a previous study, we established a risk-scoring system incorporating the severity of ischemia and infection.13 Ischemia was stratified into three categories: iS0 (TcPO2 > 40 mmHg), iS1 (TcPO2 between 30–40 mmHg), and iS2 (TcPO2 < 30 mmHg). Infection severity was classified into three levels (iN0, iN1, and iN2) based on tissue biopsy culture results. iN0 represented patients with no clinical evidence of infection and a negative tissue culture. iN1 was assigned for cases showing typical signs or symptoms of infection or a positive soft tissue culture. iN2 indicated confirmed osteomyelitis, demonstrated by a positive bone culture.

The total risk score (ranging from 0 to 4) was determined by combining the assigned ischemia and infection grades as follows: 0 (iS0 iN0), 1 (iS0 iN1 or iS1 iN0), 2 (iS0 iN2, iS1 iN1, or iS2 iN0), 3 (iS1 iN2 or iS2 iN1), and 4 (iS2 iN2).

Data source and study patients

A retrospective analysis utilized medical records from 1,663 patients diagnosed with DFUs admitted to the diabetic wound center at the authors’ institution between January 2008 and December 2023. This center serves as a specialized, high-volume facility dedicated to diabetic wound management.1516 Patient data were extracted from the hospital’s electronic medical record system and linked to the institution’s Admitted Data Collection, a database regularly audited for data accuracy and completeness.

Upon admission, a comprehensive set of 88 clinical and demographic variables was systematically recorded, including demographic details, ulcer characteristics, wound bioburden, neurological evaluations, baseline vascular assessments, and pertinent serologic markers. Inclusion criteria for study participants were 1) confirmed diagnosis of diabetes for more than five years, 2) ulcer confined to the hindfoot area, 3) available TcPO2 measurements, 4) documented tissue culture results, 5) recorded final treatment outcomes, and 6) classification as risk score grade 3 or 4, reflecting the presence of critical infection and ischemia.

Clinical outcomes

Clinical outcomes were categorized as healed without major amputation or healed with major amputation. Assessment of these outcomes was based on the presence of risk factors associated with major amputation.

Statistical analysis

To identify potential risk factors for major amputation, all 88 collected variables were compared between patients who underwent major amputation and those who did not. Continuous variables were evaluated using the Student’s t-test or the Wilcoxon rank-sum test, depending on data distribution. Categorical variables were examined with the χ2 test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate.

Variables demonstrating significant differences between the two groups were subsequently dichotomized for further analysis. For continuous variables, optimal cut-off points were identified through receiver operating characteristic curve analysis. The threshold with the highest Youden index was chosen, provided the corresponding sensitivity was ≥ 0.70. Variables for which the cut-off points produced a Youden index below 0.50 were excluded from further consideration.

The finalized set of selected variables was regarded as significant risk factors for major amputation. Fisher’s exact test was applied to compare major amputation rates based on the number of risk factors present. Bar plots were used to visualize major amputation rates among groups stratified by the number of risk factors. Univariable logistic regression employing Firth’s penalized likelihood method was conducted to estimate odds ratios (ORs) across these groups.

Data collection was performed using Microsoft Excel (Microsoft Corp., Redmond, WA, USA). Statistical analyses were conducted with R software for Windows, version 4.4.1 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria; http://www.r-project.org). The ggplot2 package (version 3.5.1; Wickham, H., 2016, Houston, TX, USA; https://ggplot2.tidyverse.org) was utilized for visualizations, and the consort package (version 1.2.2; Dayim, A., 2024, Cambridge, UK; https://cran.r-project.org/package=consort) was used to generate the participant flow diagram. All P values were reported as two-tailed, with statistical significance defined as a P < 0.05.

Ethics statement

This retrospective study received approval from the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of Korea University Guro Hospital (IRB No. 2015GR0181), and was conducted in full accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Written informed consent was obtained from all included patients.

DISCUSSION

DFUs represent the predominant cause of lower extremity amputations, with approximately 65% of those undergoing these procedures having diabetes.17 Major amputations, including above-knee and below-knee procedures, often lead to severe functional impairment and are associated with increased mortality.18 The five-year mortality rate among DFU patients requiring amputation ranges between 45% and 55%.19

While forefoot and midfoot ulcers are often effectively managed through minor amputations if addressed promptly, hindfoot ulcers present a significantly greater risk for progression to major amputation. Within the European Study Group on Diabetes and the Lower Extremity (EURODIALE) study, Pickwell et al.20 demonstrated that ulcer location in diabetic patients had a notable influence on clinical outcomes, with a higher incidence of major amputations observed in those with hindfoot ulcers compared to midfoot or forefoot ulcers. Furthermore, individuals with hindfoot ulcers experienced the highest mortality rates. Chipchase et al.8 found that 7.1% of patients with diabetic hindfoot ulcers required major amputation, and in 19.5% of these patients, ulcers remained unhealed at the time of death. Similarly, Örneholm et al.9 documented an 8.6% rate of major amputations among patients managed at a multidisciplinary diabetic foot clinic in Sweden. The mean major amputation rate observed in our previous study (8.7%) aligned with these earlier findings. Nevertheless, the incidence of major amputation may fluctuate based on individual risk profiles. In line with this, our earlier analysis assessed major amputation rates stratified by the severity of ischemia and infection. Specifically, patients with risk scores below 3, which represented the statistically significant threshold for predicting major amputation, showed a relatively low amputation rate of 4.8%. By contrast, subjects with scores of 3 or above, reflecting the coexistence of severe infection and ischemia, demonstrated a substantially increased amputation rate of 20.0%.

The results of this study identified three significant risk factors associated with major amputation in patients presenting with critically infected and ischemic hindfoot ulcers: CKD stage ≥ 3, serum hemoglobin level < 10.35 g/dL, and serum albumin level < 3.75 g/dL. In addition to determining these predictors, the study provides an important clinical perspective. Among patients with comparably high-risk profiles (risk scores ≥ 3), the rate of major amputation varied significantly (0% to 67%) depending on the presence of these specific risk factors. Importantly, patients lacking all three risk factors had a 0% amputation rate, even in the context of critical ischemia and infection. By comparison, the amputation rate rose to 6% with one risk factor, 27% with two, and 67% with all three, indicating that two out of three patients in the last group required major amputation. Consequently, instead of offering a generalized estimate suggesting that the mean probability of major amputation is 20% among those with risk scores ≥ 3, it becomes both more accurate and clinically relevant to provide individualized amputation risk assessments based on each patient’s unique risk factor profile.

These results have several potential implications for the management of diabetic hindfoot ulcers. Firstly, the findings allow clinicians to tailor treatment strategies, ranging from early intensive intervention to more conservative management, according to each patient’s estimated prognosis. Secondly, the information supports clearer communication with patients and families regarding the likely disease trajectory, thereby aiding informed shared decision-making and future planning. Thirdly, stratifying amputation outcomes by risk category permits healthcare professionals to evaluate the relative effectiveness of different interventions and monitor response to new therapies. Finally, such data can assist healthcare systems in more effectively allocating resources by directing care and interventions toward those patients at greatest risk for major amputation.

In this study, CKD, low serum hemoglobin, and low serum albumin levels were identified as three primary risk factors for major amputation in patients with critically infected and ischemic hindfoot ulcers. CKD has previously been recognized as an independent risk factor for diabetic foot ulceration. CKD results in the accumulation of uremic toxins and metabolic acidosis, factors which can disrupt cellular regeneration and slow wound healing. Moreover, diminished renal function leads to volume overload, causing peripheral edema that further predisposes patients to ulceration and infection. Immune suppression induced by uremia in CKD impairs both the immune response and tissue repair processes, both of which are crucial for DFU healing. Advanced CKD stages (≥ stage 3) are associated not only with increased risks of mortality, cardiovascular complications, and hospitalization but also with a notably higher incidence of foot ulceration and risk of major amputation.21222324

Anemia was also identified as a strong, independent predictor of major amputation. Lowered hemoglobin levels compromise oxygen transport and tissue repair mechanisms, thereby impairing wound healing capacity. A systematic review and meta-analysis conducted by Yammine et al.25 established a significant association between low hemoglobin and an increased risk of both major amputation and mortality. In line with these findings, Costa et al.26 observed that 89.6% of patients who underwent major amputation presented with hemoglobin levels under 11 g/dL. Their multivariate analysis demonstrated anemia as the most powerful predictor, reflected by an OR of 5.57 (P < 0.001). Our study corroborates these results, despite employing a slightly lower hemoglobin cut-off (10.35 g/dL), which likely reflects the higher severity within our cohort of exclusively hospitalized, high-risk patients. While TIBC was not retained in the final risk score, its consideration highlights the prognostic importance of anemia.

Low serum albumin, recognized as a marker of poor nutritional status, further impedes wound healing by restricting the availability of protein needed for tissue regeneration. Hypoalbuminemia is linked to both ischemia and edema due to diminished oncotic pressure, resulting in the accumulation of interstitial fluid and compromised local perfusion. In addition, albumin supports immune responses and functions as an antioxidant. Therefore, decreased albumin concentrations heighten susceptibility to both soft tissue and bone infections. Albumin is not only a nutritional marker but also a broader indicator of frailty and negative clinical outcomes, such as an elevated risk of major amputation among patients with DFUs.27

Previous research on diabetic hindfoot ulcers has predominantly been conducted at multidisciplinary centers, where differences in management protocols are influenced by institutional policies and guidelines. By contrast, this study analyzed a carefully defined patient population, with thorough and standardized initial foot assessments carried out by experienced wound specialists, along with consistent follow-up adequate to assess true wound healing outcomes. Although the number of patients included may be considered relatively small, this stemmed from a selective process to include only individuals who fulfilled stringent high-risk criteria. The primary cohort originally consisted of 1,663 patients with diabetes.

A notable limitation of this study lies in its dependence on information obtained solely at the initial intake examination. This design was intended to generate early prognostic data at the commencement of treatment. Nevertheless, potential selection bias exists because only hospitalized patients were included, typically representing individuals with more advanced ulcers unsuitable for outpatient care. Univariable logistic regression analysis was performed in this study, as the number of major amputation events was limited. Fully adjusted multivariable models with numerous covariates would therefore be underpowered and prone to overfitting, potentially resulting in unstable estimates and limited clinical interpretability. Nevertheless, inter-variable confounding is possible. In the adjusted model with a multivariable Firth logistic regression including the three risk factors, low hemoglobin remained independently associated with major amputation (adjusted OR, 5.07; 95% CI, 1.02–32.4; P = 0.047), whereas CKD and low albumin were not statistically significant after adjustment. In addition, the sample size was limited and the number of major amputation events was small. This was particularly evident in subgroups with two or three risk factors and was associated with wide 95% CIs. Therefore, although the observed ORs appear strong, these findings should be interpreted as suggesting a risk gradient rather than a definitive conclusion regarding the magnitude of effect. Compared with prior studies on major amputation risk prediction, these statistical limitations should be taken into account.2829 Prospective, multicenter studies with larger sample sizes are needed to validate this risk-factor tool and to enhance its generalizability before clinical adoption. Moreover, since all enrolled patients received treatment at a tertiary care institution, the applicability of the results to primary care settings or to individuals presenting with less severe wounds may be restricted.

Despite these constraints, we established a simplified risk assessment model designed to estimate the likelihood of major amputation in cases of diabetic hindfoot ulcers complicated by critical infection and ischemia, a condition associated with a high incidence of limb loss. As far as we are aware, this represents the first study to generate predictive data regarding major amputation in patients with diabetic hindfoot ulcers based on individualized clinical characteristics. We anticipate that these results will provide meaningful support to patients, their families, and the healthcare professionals engaged in DFU management.
