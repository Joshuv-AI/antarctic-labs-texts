Structural Limitations on Constraining the Time Evolution of Dark Energy
Seokcheon Lee

Abstract
Cosmological constraints on a time-varying dark energy equation of state are often interpreted as evidence for a dynamical dark energy. In this Letter, we show that such interpretations are fundamentally limited by the integral structure through which the equation of state enters cosmological observables. The mapping from ω ⁡ ( z ) \omega(z) to distances involves successive integrations, which act as an intrinsic low-pass filter in redshift space. As a result, only a small number of slowly varying modes of ω ⁡ ( z ) \omega(z) are observationally accessible, while rapid or localized temporal variations are irretrievably suppressed. This limitation is independent of any specific parametrization and represents a structural property of Friedmann–Lemaître–Robertson–Walker cosmology with distance-based probes.



Introduction.

Whether the dark energy equation of state evolves with cosmic time remains one of the central questions in modern cosmology.
Distance-based observables—including Type Ia supernova luminosity distances, baryon acoustic oscillations, and compressed cosmic microwave background measurements—have been widely used to constrain phenomenological parametrizations of the dark energy equation of state,
\omega(z)
. Despite the steadily improving precision of these data sets, however, constraints on the detailed time dependence of
\omega(z)
remain weak and highly degenerate.

This difficulty is not merely a consequence of limited data quality or specific parametrization choices. Distance probes access the dark energy sector only indirectly through the cosmic expansion history, and therefore constrain
\omega(z)
only after successive integrations over redshift. Early work emphasized that reconstructing
\omega(z)
from distance measurements constitutes an ill-posed inverse problem, in which small observational uncertainties are strongly amplified when attempting to recover time-dependent features
Huterer:2000mj
. Related analyses demonstrated that luminosity-distance observables inherently average over the expansion history, making them insensitive to localized or rapidly varying behavior in
\omega(z)

Maor:2000jy
;
Bassett:2004wz
.

A complementary perspective was developed using principal component analysis (PCA), showing that distance data constrain only a small number of effective combinations of
\omega(z)
while higher-order modes remain essentially unconstrained
Huterer:2002hy
. Similar conclusions were reached using binned reconstructions and decorrelated bases
dePutter:2007kf
. These results are often interpreted as limitations of particular statistical techniques or as consequences of limited data volume.

While the ill-posedness of reconstructing
\omega(z)
from distance data has long been recognized
Maor:2000jy
;
Huterer:2000mj
;
Huterer:2002hy
, here we show that this limitation is enforced at a more fundamental level by the physical response kernel that maps
\omega(z)
onto cosmological distances, rather than by any particular parametrization or statistical method.

In this Letter, we demonstrate that these conclusions arise from a single underlying origin. The mapping from
\omega(z)
to distance observables involves a double integration over redshift, which induces an intrinsic low-pass filtering of the dark energy equation of state. This filtering is a structural property of Friedmann–Lemaître–Robertson–Walker (FLRW) cosmology with distance-based probes and is independent of any specific basis choice or analysis technique. We make this statement explicit by deriving the linear-response kernel that maps perturbations in
\omega(z)
onto observable distances. This kernel is broad and non-local in redshift, enforcing a compact, effectively low-rank response that suppresses rapidly varying or localized features in
\omega(z)
.

Unlike previous PCA-based demonstrations, our approach does not rely on PCA as a statistical tool. Instead, PCA is used only as a diagnostic language to visualize a limitation that is already enforced by the physical response kernel itself. This kernel-based perspective unifies earlier ill-posed reconstruction arguments
Huterer:2000mj
;
Maor:2000jy
and PCA results
Huterer:2002hy
, and clarifies why increasing the flexibility of
\omega(z)
—for example, through the CPL parametrization
Chevallier:2000qy
;
Linder:2002et
—cannot recover information about high-frequency or localized time evolution.

Linearized response of cosmological observables.

We linearize the cosmological response around a cosmological-constant background,

\omega(z)=-1+\delta\omega(z),

(1)

assuming
|\delta\omega(z)|\ll 1
.
The dark energy density perturbation satisfies

\frac{\delta\rho_{\rm DE}(z)}{\rho_{\rm DE}(z)}=3\int_{0}^{z}\frac{\delta\omega(z^{\prime})}{1+z^{\prime}}\,dz^{\prime}.

(2)

From the Friedmann equation, the corresponding perturbation of the expansion rate is

\frac{\delta H(z)}{H(z)}=\frac{3}{2}\,\Omega_{\rm DE}(z)\int_{0}^{z}\frac{\delta\omega(z^{\prime})}{1+z^{\prime}}\,dz^{\prime},

(3)

where
\Omega_{\rm DE}(z)
is the dark energy fraction at redshift
z
.
The perturbation of the comoving distance,

D(z)=\int_{0}^{z}\frac{cdz^{\prime}}{H(z^{\prime})},

(4)

is then

\delta D(z)=-\int_{0}^{z}\frac{cdz_{1}}{H(z_{1})}\,\frac{\delta H(z_{1})}{H(z_{1})}.

(5)

Exchanging the order of integration yields a linear convolution,

\delta D(z)=\int_{0}^{z}dz^{\prime}\,K_{D}(z;z^{\prime})\,\delta\omega(z^{\prime}),

(6)

with kernel

K_{D}(z;z^{\prime})=-\frac{3}{2}\,\frac{1}{1+z^{\prime}}\int_{z^{\prime}}^{z}\frac{cdz_{1}}{H(z_{1})}\,\Omega_{\rm DE}(z_{1}).

(7)

The negative sign reflects the fact that increasing
\omega(z)
(making it less negative) reduces the accelerated expansion relative to
\Lambda
CDM, thereby decreasing comoving distances. The kernel is broad in redshift and strongly weighted toward low redshift, owing to the prefactor
(1+z^{\prime})^{-1}
and the suppression of
\Omega_{\rm DE}
at high redshift. Consequently, localized perturbations in
\omega(z)
are mapped onto smooth, extended responses in
D(z)
, implying intrinsic smoothing that is independent of parametrization.

Low-pass filtering and effective modes.

Expanding
\delta\omega(z)
in a set of basis modes,

\delta\omega(z)=\sum_{k}c_{k}\phi_{k}(z),

(8)

modes with large
k
correspond to rapid variations over narrow redshift intervals. Because
D(z)
depends on
\delta\omega(z)
through two nested integrals, the response to such modes is strongly suppressed. Schematically, the distance response scales as

\delta D(k)\propto k^{-2}\,\delta w(k),

(9)

reflecting the double-integral structure rather than a true Fourier transform. Therefore, cosmological distances act as an intrinsic low-pass filter in redshift space.

For a mode
\delta\omega(z)\propto\sin(kz)
, the linear response implies
\delta H/H\propto k^{-1}
, while the distance response is further suppressed as
\delta D\propto k^{-2}
. This hierarchy directly reflects the single-versus double-integral structure of the observables and explains the strong attenuation of rapidly varying modes. This behavior is illustrated schematically in Fig.
1
.

To quantify this effect, we perform a toy Fisher analysis in which
\delta\omega(z)
is expanded in a finite set of smooth basis functions (Fourier or Chebyshev modes) and propagated to distance observables using the kernel
K_{D}(z;z^{\prime})
. Chebyshev polynomials provide a numerically stable and complete basis on a finite redshift interval and have been widely used for parametrization-independent reconstructions
dePutter:2007kf
;
Clarkson:2010bm
. The resulting Fisher matrix encodes only the physical response of distances to perturbations in
\omega(z)
.

Such an eigenvalue hierarchy is common in Fisher and Karhunen–Loève decompositions
Tegmark:1996bz
.
Here, however, it is not driven by noise or data volume but by the compact, low-rank nature of the distance response operator itself.

Role of direct expansion-rate probes.

Not all cosmological observables are affected equally. Direct measurements of the expansion rate, such as cosmic chronometers inferred from differential galaxy ages
Jimenez:2001gg
or radial BAO, depend on
\omega(z)
through a single integral,

\frac{\delta H(z)}{H(z)}\propto\int_{0}^{z}\frac{\delta\omega(z^{\prime})}{1+z^{\prime}}\,dz^{\prime},

(10)

and therefore experience a weaker suppression of high-
k
modes. Nevertheless, the response remains non-local in redshift, and even direct
H(z)
probes constrain only a limited number of smooth combinations of
\omega(z)
in practice.

In this experiment, the equation-of-state perturbation is expanded as Eq. (
8
), where
\{\phi_{k}(z)\}
denotes a set of smooth basis functions defined on a finite redshift interval
z\in[0,z_{\rm max}]
. We consider both Fourier modes and Chebyshev polynomials of the first kind, with
N\simeq 10
. Chebyshev modes are particularly convenient because they form a complete and
numerically stable basis on a bounded domain. And they have been widely used in parametrization-independent reconstructions of the expansion history.

For each basis function
\phi_{k}(z)
, its induced perturbation to the comoving distance is computed explicitly using the linear-response kernel,

\delta D_{k}(z)=\int_{0}^{z}dz^{\prime}\,K_{D}(z;z^{\prime})\,\phi_{k}(z^{\prime}).

(11)

This convolution is evaluated by numerical quadrature on a dense redshift grid, assuming a fiducial
\Lambda
CDM background for
H(z)
and
\Omega_{\rm DE}(z)
. No observational data, survey geometry, or noise model is introduced at this stage.

The Fisher matrix for the mode amplitudes
c_{k}
is then constructed as

F_{ij}=\sum_{z_{\alpha}}\frac{\partial D(z_{\alpha})}{\partial c_{i}}\frac{\partial D(z_{\alpha})}{\partial c_{j}},

(12)

where the sum runs over a set of redshift sampling points. For clarity, we adopt a unit covariance matrix, so that
F_{ij}
reduces to the
Gram matrix of the kernel-weighted response functions
\{\delta D_{k}(z)\}
. Therefore, the Fisher matrix probes only the intrinsic structure of the response operator encoded in
K_{D}(z;z^{\prime})
.

The eigenvalues
\lambda_{i}
of this Fisher matrix, shown in Fig.
2
, exhibit a steep hierarchy, decreasing rapidly with increasing mode index. In the Fisher eigenbasis, the variance of the
i
-th mode amplitude is

\sigma_{i}^{2}=\frac{1}{\lambda_{i}}.

(13)

As a result, the uncertainties
\sigma_{i}
grow rapidly with
i
, indicating that only the first few eigenmodes correspond to physically meaningful constraints. In the toy example shown, this transition occurs at
i\simeq 2
–
3
, beyond which the modes lie effectively in the null space of the distance response operator.

This behavior demonstrates that distance observables are sensitive to only a small number of smooth, kernel-weighted combinations of
\omega(z)
. Higher-order or more oscillatory modes produce distance responses that are either nearly linearly dependent or strongly suppressed by the double-integral structure of the kernel. Importantly, this rapid eigenvalue decay arises despite the absence of real data, instrumental noise, or survey-specific assumptions. It therefore reflects a physical limitation imposed by the integral mapping from
\omega(z)
to distance observables, rather than a statistical artifact of PCA or finite data quality.

Finally, we emphasize that the statement that only the first
i\simeq 1
–
2
Fisher eigenmodes are well constrained does not imply that the dark energy equation of state possesses only two physical degrees of freedom. Rather, it reflects the fact that distance observables project the infinite-dimensional functional space of
\omega(z)
onto a low-dimensional subspace determined by the
response kernel. This notion is fundamentally different from adopting a two-parameter ansatz such as the CPL parametrization, where the parameters
(w_{0},w_{a})
correspond to a specific
a priori
choice of basis. In contrast, the Fisher eigenmodes represent the directions that are genuinely accessible to observations.

Implications and conclusions.

The intrinsic filtering described above explains why distance-based analyses consistently constrain only one or two effective combinations of
\omega(z)
, irrespective of parametrization. Increasing the functional flexibility of
\omega(z)
cannot overcome this limitation.

The steep hierarchy of Fisher eigenvalues implies that the effective dimensionality of constraints on
\omega(z)
is intrinsically low.
This limitation is physical rather than methodological: it is fundamentally encoded in how cosmological observables respond to
\omega(z)
. Consequently, apparent signals of dynamical dark energy inferred from low-dimensional parametrizations must be interpreted with care, as they often reflect the freedom to absorb mild inconsistencies between probes rather than genuine time evolution.

Future progress will require observables that depend more directly on the expansion history or on the growth of cosmic structure, involving qualitatively different response kernels
Linder:2005in
. Joint analyses combining expansion and growth information may alleviate some of these limitations
Zhao:2011te
;
Lee:2025hjw
, although fundamental non-locality in redshift remains unavoidable.
