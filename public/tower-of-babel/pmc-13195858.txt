Dynamic changes in serum HER2-peptide-specific autoantibodies predict response to neoadjuvant therapy in HER2-positive breast cancer

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundNeoadjuvant chemotherapy (NAC) with anti-HER2 agents is standard for HER2-positive breast cancer, achieving pathological complete response (pCR) in 40–50% of patients; however, reliable predictors of response remain limited. HER2 is an immunogenic antigen capable of eliciting humoral responses, yet the predictive value of HER2-specific autoantibodies in the neoadjuvant setting remains unclear. We investigated whether HER2-peptide–specific antibody responses could serve as potential biomarkers of treatment response.MethodsPaired pre- and post-NAC sera from 112 patients enrolled in the JBCRG-16 (Neo-Lath) trial, which evaluated trastuzumab- and lapatinib-containing NAC, were analyzed. IgG titers against 63 non-overlapping 20-mer HER2-derived peptides were measured using a multiplex bead array, and serum HER2 protein levels were also quantified. Data were analyzed using Wilcoxon signed-rank and rank-sum tests, Spearman’s correlation analyses, and univariate logistic regression for pCR. Pretreatment IgG titers were positively correlated with the Boman index and negatively correlated with the aliphatic index. In univariate analyses, neither IgG titers nor serum HER2 levels at either time point, nor their treatment-induced changes, were significantly associated with pCR. In contrast, a predictive model based on treatment-induced changes in a subset of peptide-specific IgG titers demonstrated consistent predictive performance (AUC 0.78 in training and 0.79 in validation). Six peptides distributed across the HER2 sequence contributed to pCR prediction.ConclusionsDynamic changes in HER2-peptide-specific autoantibodies during NAC, rather than static titers alone, may provide informative biomarkers for predicting clinical response. Serum HER2 levels were not significantly associated with pCR, suggesting that autoantibody profiling may capture response-related biology not reflected by antigen levels alone. These findings provide proof-of-concept evidence that minimally invasive, longitudinal profiling of peptide-specific autoantibodies could complement established response-assessment modalities, such as imaging or molecular assays. Background

Anti-HER2 therapy is the standard care for patients with HER2-positive breast cancer. Originally developed for advanced or recurrent diseases, these targeted agents have been successfully incorporated into perioperative treatment strategies [1]. Neoadjuvant chemotherapy (NAC) administered before surgery aims to reduce tumor burden and increase the feasibility of breast-conserving surgery. In HER2-positive patients, NAC leads to substantial tumor shrinkage and results a pathologic complete response (pCR) in approximately 40–50% patients [2, 3]. Given that pCR is a well-established prognostic indicator [4, 5], and the absence of pCR informs the selection of subsequent adjuvant therapies [6], NAC has become a widely accepted standard approach for this patient population. Nevertheless, accurate identification of patients who achieve pCR after NAC remains a significant clinical challenge, despite several known potential predictive parameters [7–10]. Recently, the HER2DX genomic test, which integrates a 27-gene expression panel with a clinical feature-based classifier, has shown promising performance in predicting pCR in early stage HER2-positive breast cancer [9, 10]. In addition, serological approaches that assess autoantibodies against self-proteins may provide complementary information by capturing real-time, treatment-induced immune dynamics, thereby offering insights beyond static tumor genomic features [11]. Nevertheless, clinically reliable biomarkers for predicting a pCR remain unavailable.

In addition to its role as a therapeutic target for monoclonal antibodies and tyrosine kinase inhibitors, HER2 has garnered interest as a tumor-associated antigen capable of inducing cellular and humoral immune responses [12–15]. Azuma et al. identified HER2-derived peptides that elicit specific cellular and humoral immune responses, supporting the feasibility of peptide-based immunotherapeutic strategies [16]. Several studies have also highlighted the clinical relevance of HER2-specific autoantibodies in breast cancer [17–20]. Moreover, we previously demonstrated that plasma IgG titers against EGFR-derived peptides correlate with the clinical efficacy of gefitinib in patients with non-small cell lung cancer, highlighting the potential of peptide-specific antibody responses as predictive biomarkers [21]. Based on these findings, in this study, we aimed to conduct a comprehensive analysis of the HER2-specific humoral immune responses in serum samples from patients with HER2-positive breast cancer receiving NAC with anti-HER2 agents. Unlike previous studies that used the full-length HER2 protein or the extracellular domain (ECD) [17–20], our approach systematically evaluated specific humoral responses against a broad panel of HER2-derived linear peptides, allowing fine mapping of humoral immunogenicity. We identified a combination of immunogenic HER2-derived peptides that was significantly associated with pCR, which is a well-established surrogate endpoint for NAC efficacy [2–6]. These results suggest the potential utility of HER2-peptide-specific antibody responses as novel biomarkers for predicting treatment response in this patient population.

Methods

Patients, treatments, and sample collection

This study was approved by the Kanagawa Cancer Center Clinical Ethics Review Committee (28 Epidemic-56) and was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and relevant ethical guidelines for clinical and epidemiological studies. Among the 212 patients enrolled in the JBCRG-16 (Neo-Lath) trial (UMIN000007576), which evaluated the pCR rate following preoperative chemotherapy, including anti-HER2 therapy for HER2-positive breast cancer [22, 23], 112 (52.8%) provided written informed consent to participate in an ancillary study involving serum analysis. Institutional review board approval was obtained from each participating site for the original trial and follow-up study, including the National Hospital Organization Osaka National Hospital (approval no. 2011-0017 on January 17, 2012/approval no. 2014 − 1015 on June 17, 2014). Informed consent was obtained from all patients at the beginning of the JBCRG-16 (Neo-Lath) trial. Serum samples were collected before NAC (pre-NAC, n = 112) and after NAC but before surgery (post-NAC, n = 102) to measure serum IgG titers against HER2-derived peptides. Serum HER2 levels were measured using a chemiluminescent immunoassay (CLIA) method at specialized laboratories designated by each participating institution using a subset of samples collected before NAC (n = 66), after NAC (n = 65), and at both time points (n = 55).

The JBCRG-16 (Neo-Lath) trial evaluated five treatment arms defined by hormone receptor status and regimen, as shown in Supplementary Fig. S1. Patients with ER-negative disease received either (A) lapatinib plus trastuzumab for six weeks followed by the same doublet combined with weekly paclitaxel for 12 weeks, or (B) an 18-week course of lapatinib plus trastuzumab followed by the same doublet combined with weekly paclitaxel for 12 weeks. Patients with ER-positive disease received one of the following regimens: (C) lapatinib plus trastuzumab for six weeks followed by the same doublet combined with weekly paclitaxel for 12 weeks, without endocrine therapy; (D) the same regimen with concomitant endocrine therapy throughout the chemotherapy phase; or (E) an 18-week course of lapatinib plus trastuzumab followed by the same doublet combined with weekly paclitaxel for 12 weeks, with concomitant endocrine therapy throughout the chemotherapy phase. Thus, Groups A, C, and D received lapatinib and trastuzumab for six weeks, whereas Groups B and E received the doublet for 18 weeks. In all arms, weekly paclitaxel was administered for 12 weeks after the initial targeted-therapy phase, in combination with lapatinib plus trastuzumab, and endocrine therapy was maintained during chemotherapy in Groups D and E. Further details of the treatment protocol have been previously published [22, 23]. Supplementary Table S1 summarizes the distribution of patients who provided paired pre- and post-NAC serum samples. Notably, Group B contributed fewer paired samples than the overall study cohort, and pCR rates were higher in Groups B, C, and E (Supplementary Table S1).

Measurement of serum IgG titers to HER2-derived peptides

As shown in Fig. 1, 63 different non-overlapping 20-mer peptides (H1 – H63) were designed from the HER2 protein sequence and synthesized by Sigma-Aldrich (St. Louis, MO, USA). IgG titers specific to each peptide were measured using a multiplex bead suspension array on a Bio-Plex 200 system (Bio-Rad Laboratories, Hercules, CA, USA) as previously reported [21, 24]. Briefly, the serum was diluted with protein-free blocking buffer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA) containing 0.4% Block Ace (KAC, Kobe, Japan) at a ratio of 1:100. Subsequently, the diluted serum (100 µL) was incubated in duplicate with xMAP beads (Luminex Corp., Austin, TX, USA) coated with HER2-derived peptides in a 96-well filter plate (MultiScreen HTS BV Filter Plate, 1.2 μm, Merck-Millipore, Burlington, MA, USA) for 2 h at room temperature on a plate shaker. The beads were washed and incubated with 100 µL of x200 diluted biotin-conjugated goat anti-human IgG (BA-3080; Vector Laboratories, Burlingame, CA) for 1 h at room temperature on a plate shaker. After washing, 100 µL of 5 µg/ml streptavidin-PE (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA) was added to the wells, and the plate was incubated for 30 min at room temperature on a plate shaker. The beads were washed three times, and 100 µL of PBS containing 0.05% Tween-20 was added to each well. The fluorescence intensity was measured in 75 µL of each sample using a Bio-Plex 200 system (Bio-Rad Laboratories). Peptide-specific IgG titers were estimated from fluorescence intensity and expressed as fluorescence intensity units (FIUs), as previously reported [21, 24]. The xMAP beads without HER2-derived peptides were used as controls.

Measurement of serum cytokines and chemokines

A series of 27 cytokines and chemokines, including IL-1β, IL-1ra, IL-2, IL-4, IL-5, IL-6, IL-7, IL-8, IL-9, IL-10, IL-12, IL-13, IL-15, IL-17, eotaxin, FGF, G-CSF, GM-CSF, IFN-γ, IP-10, MCP-1, MIP-1α, MIP-1β, PDGF-BB, RANTES, TNF-α, and VEGF, were measured with a Bio-Plex multiplex assay system (Bio-Plex Pro Human Cytokine 27-Plex Panel; Bio-Rad Laboratories) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. This analysis was conducted on 12.5 µL serum sample collected before and after NAC from each patient (n = 102).

Evaluation of tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes (TILs)

TILs were evaluated on hematoxylin and eosin–stained sections of pretreatment core-needle biopsy specimens obtained before NAC (n = 62), according to the recommendations of the International TILs Working Group 2014 [25]. Briefly, stromal TILs were assessed as the percentage of stromal area occupied by mononuclear inflammatory cells within the borders of the invasive tumor, excluding areas of necrosis and artifacts. TIL scoring was performed independently by one breast oncologist and one pathologist trained in the Working Group methodology, both of whom were blinded to clinical outcomes and serological data. In cases of discrepant assessments, slides were jointly reviewed and a consensus score was determined.

Data preparation and statistical analysis

Raw fluorescence intensity data obtained from the multiplex bead suspension array were preprocessed by background subtraction using Z-score normalization [26]. A Z-score threshold of 1.96 (corresponding to a 95% confidence interval) was applied to distinguish true assay signals from background noise. Three datasets were generated from the normalized data to comprehensively investigate the associations between IgG titers specific to HER2-derived peptides and pCR: pre (baseline values before the initiation of NAC), post (values collected after NAC but prior to surgery), and fold (log₂-transformed fold change, calculated as log₂(post/pre)). All analyses were conducted using R statistical computing platform (version 4.4.1; https://www.r-project.org). A machine learning framework was employed to mitigate overfitting in multivariate predictive modeling [27]. The full dataset was stratified randomly into training (80%) and validation (20%) groups. Model development and hyperparameter optimization were performed exclusively on the training set via nested cross-validation (CV), ensuring strict separation between model development and evaluation to prevent data leakage. Subsequently, elastic net (EN) regression was performed, considering pCR (a binary outcome) as the target variable and the IgG titers or fold changes in 63 HER2-derived peptides as the input features. In accordance with the EN regression requirements, all training samples were standardized prior to model fitting, and the corresponding scaling parameters (center and standard deviation) were recorded. Hyperparameter tuning was performed via 10-fold CV with the cv.glmnet function (from the glmnet package), where α = 0.5 was fixed to balance L1 and L2 regularization, and λ was optimized to minimize classification error. The optimal λ value (associated with the minimum CV error) was then used to train the final model. The held-out validation set was scaled using the center and standard deviation derived from the training set for independent validation, ensuring that the test distribution was consistent with the training distribution and enabling a reliable generalization of the model. The predictions were generated using a prediction function. Bootstrap resampling (n = 1,000) was performed for each dataset and model discrimination was evaluated using the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) with 95% confidence intervals computed using the roc function.

The following theoretical physicochemical properties of the peptides were predicted based on their sequences using the Peptides R package: aliphatic index (aIndex function), Boman index (boman function), net charge (charge function), hydrophobic moment (hmoment function), hydrophobicity (hydrophobicity function), instability index (instaIndex function), molecular weight (mw function), and isoelectric point (pI function). Non-parametric group comparisons were conducted using either the Wilcoxon signed-rank test (for paired samples) or the Wilcoxon rank-sum test (for independent samples), as implemented in the wilcox.test function, with the paired parameter set to TRUE or FALSE, respectively. Spearman’s correlation coefficients were computed using the cor function and assessed using the cor.test function (all from base R). Univariate linear and logistic regression analyses were performed using the lm and glm functions, respectively, in the glmnet package.

Finally, the three-dimensional structure of HER2 (P04626, ERBB2_HUMAN) was obtained from UniProt (https://www.uniprot.org/) and visualized using PyMOL (https://www.pymol.org/) for structural characterization of the HER2-derived segments. Sequence similarity was then evaluated using 3-mer and 4-mer motif analyses in R, and secondary structure distributions were derived from the HER2 structure using PyMOL.

Humoral immune responses to HER2-derived peptides

IgG titers specific to the 63 HER2-derived peptides were analyzed using paired serum samples collected before and after NAC. As shown in Fig. 2A, the peptides elicited markedly variable levels of immune response, as reflected by the differences in peptide-specific IgG titers. Notably, IgG titers against 37 of the 63 peptides (58.7%) were significantly reduced after NAC, indicating a treatment-associated decrease in peptide-specific humoral immune responses. Simultaneously, serum HER2 levels significantly decreased after NAC treatment. To explore potential mechanisms underlying the reduction in HER2 peptide–specific IgG titers after NAC, we examined correlations between treatment-induced changes in HER2-peptide–specific IgG titers and changes in serum cytokines and chemokines. As shown in Supplementary Fig S2, changes in several chemokines, including MIP-1β, RANTES, MCP-1, eotaxin, and IL-8, showed trends toward inverse correlations with changes in HER2 peptide–specific IgG titers. In addition, stromal TILs were assessed using pretreatment biopsy specimens, and quantified as the percentage of stromal area occupied by mononuclear inflammatory cells within the borders of the invasive tumor. However, no significant correlations were observed between changes in HER2-peptide–specific antibody titers and pretreatment stromal TIL levels (Supplementary Fig S3).

The correlations between pretreatment IgG titers and various physicochemical properties of each peptide were then examined to investigate the characteristics of the immunogenic peptides. As shown in Fig. 2B, the Boman index, a metric that estimates the potential of a peptide to interact with membranes or other proteins based on residue solubility, was significantly positively correlated with IgG titers in pre-NAC sera (r = 0.41, p < 0.001). In contrast, the aliphatic index, which reflects the relative volume occupied by aliphatic side chains such as alanine, valine, isoleucine, and leucine, was significantly negatively correlated with pretreatment IgG titers (r = -0.29, p < 0.05). No significant correlations were observed for other physicochemical parameters.

We further mapped the top 10 immunogenic peptides with high pretreatment IgG titers onto the three-dimensional structure of the HER2 protein and compared their locations with known functional and exposed regions. As shown in Supplementary Fig. S4, most of these highly immunogenic peptides were located within the ECD of HER2 and were exposed on the protein surface. Notably, the three peptides with the highest immunogenicity (H10, H12, and H13) clustered on adjacent protruding surface regions of the ECD. These structural features are likely to facilitate antibody accessibility and may contribute to the observed differences in circulating antibody titers.

Relationships between serum HER2-peptide-specific IgG titers or HER2 levels and pCR

IgG titers against HER2-derived peptides were then compared between patients who achieved pCR and those who did not (non-pCR) using serum samples collected before and after NAC. As shown in Fig. 3A, only peptide H31 exhibited a significant difference in the IgG titer between the two groups prior to NAC, with significantly higher titers observed in the pCR group. Meanwhile, in the post-NAC serum samples, three peptides (H41, H47, and H49) showed significantly lower IgG titers in the pCR group than in the non-pCR group (Fig. 3B). Furthermore, peptides H11 and H27 exhibited a greater reduction in IgG titers from pre- to post-NAC in the pCR group than that in the non-pCR group (Fig. 3C).

However, none of the individual HER2-peptide–specific IgG titers (pretreatment, posttreatment, or fold changes) showed a statistically significant association with pCR (Supplementary Table S3).

Additionally, we further examined the relationships between serum HER2 levels and HER2-peptide-specific IgG titers. Serum HER2 protein levels tended to show positive associations with HER2-peptide-specific IgG titers before and after NAC, as well as with the magnitude of treatment-induced changes in both parameters (Supplementary Fig. S5).

Machine learning for the prediction of pCR using peptide-specific IgG titers

Next, the machine learning analyses were designed as exploratory, multivariate evaluations to assess whether combinatorial patterns across multiple peptides, rather than any single peptide, could improve discrimination of pCR. We applied EN regression analyses to quantitatively evaluate the potential of the clusters of peptide-specific IgG titers to predict pCR. IgG titers were analyzed from pre- and post-NAC serum samples as well as from the changes in titers following treatment. All patients were randomly assigned to either a training set or an independent validation test set at a 4:1 ratio, and the predictive performance was assessed using a machine learning pipeline (Fig. 5A). As shown in Fig. 5B, peptide-specific IgG titers in pre-NAC serum samples demonstrated moderate predictive performance, with an AUC of 0.74 in the training set and 0.64 in the validation set. This model incorporated IgG titers against eight peptides (H61, H9, H55, H19, H49, H58, H8, and H31), and IgG titers for all peptides except H31 were lower in the pCR group. Similarly, peptide-specific IgG titers in post-NAC serum samples showed comparable predictive performance, with AUCs of 0.76 and 0.69 in the training and validation sets, respectively (Fig. 5C). This model incorporated IgG titers against nine peptides (H54, H49, H39, H32, H3, H61, H59, H14, and H36), and IgG titers for all peptides except H36 were lower in the pCR group.

In contrast, the model based on fold changes in peptide-specific IgG titers from pre- to post-NAC exhibited a more consistent predictive performance, achieving an AUC of 0.78 in the training set and 0.79 in the validation set (Fig. 5D). This model incorporated changes in IgG titers for six peptides (H30, H27, H63, H11, H47, and H36). Although IgG titers against all six peptides decreased after NAC in both the pCR and non-pCR groups, the magnitude of the reduction was greater in the pCR group than in the non-pCR group for all peptides except H36. Notably, the changes in IgG titers against peptides H27 and H11 were significantly different between the two groups (Fig. 3C). These six peptides, selected based on model performance, were distributed across the entire HER2 protein sequence, including regions beyond the ECD, and were not restricted to known functional domains (Fig. 6). Thus, their diversity may reflect broad B cell epitope recognition and underscore the role of linear epitope-specific autoantibodies as indicators of systemic immune activation.

Characteristics of the selected peptides

We conducted univariate regression analyses to assess the relationships between IgG titers specific to the selected HER2-derived peptides and baseline clinical parameters. As shown in Supplementary Table S4, IgG titers specific to the peptides selected by EN regression analyses in pre- or post-NAC sera, as well as treatment-induced changes in these titers, showed no statistically significant associations with the baseline clinical factors, except that estrogen receptor status was significantly associated with the fold change in H63-specific IgG titers.

In addition, we performed structural and motif analyses of peptides selected by the EN regression models. First, we mapped these peptides onto the three-dimensional structure of HER2 and found that they were distributed across distinct regions of the molecule (Supplementary Fig. S6A). Motif similarity analysis indicated that the selected peptides shared only a limited number of 3-mer motifs and essentially no common 4-mer motifs (Supplementary Fig. S6B, C), suggesting the absence of a dominant sequence motif. We then compared predicted secondary structure compositions between the selected and non-selected peptides. The selected peptides showed a tendency to map to regions predicted to be structurally disordered relative to the non-selected peptides (Supplementary Fig. S6D, E).

Discussion

NAC is considered the standard of care for patients with advanced HER2-positive breast cancer [1]. However, accurately identifying patients who achieve pCR after NAC remains a significant clinical challenge. The present study investigated the clinical significance of HER2-peptide-specific autoantibodies in patients with HER2-positive breast cancer receiving NAC with anti-HER2 therapy. Notably, a predictive model incorporating coordinated changes in a cluster of IgG titers to multiple peptides appeared to show improved predictive performance compared with models based solely on pretreatment or posttreatment titers, highlighting the potential value of longitudinal immune profiling over static serological measurements for capturing biologically relevant shifts in humoral immunity during therapy. Our findings may contribute to the expanding literature on blood-based biomarkers for monitoring responses to NAC, including circulating tumor DNA (ctDNA) and other blood markers [7, 8]. While ctDNA reflects tumor-derived nucleic acids, peptide-specific autoantibodies may offer a complementary readout of host immune dynamics. Therefore, future studies evaluating integrated models that combine HER2-peptide-specific autoantibody signatures with other biomarkers (e.g., ctDNA kinetics) will be important to determine whether multimodal approaches can improve response assessment beyond any single modality.

Previous studies have reported clinical associations between HER2-specific autoantibodies and patient outcomes [17–20]. Tabuchi et al. [18] and Sato et al. [19] reported that elevated levels of HER2-specific autoantibodies were significantly associated with improved recurrence-free survival in patients with breast cancer. However, these studies quantified antibody titers against recombinant human HER2 ECD proteins. In contrast, we measured serum IgG titers against a panel of HER2-derived linear peptides in patients with HER2-positive breast cancer treated with NAC, enabling a broader assessment of humoral responses across the HER2 sequence. Notably, peptides spanning the full-length HER2 protein, including regions outside the ECD, showed substantial immunogenicity. These findings raise the possibility that the detected antibody responses may preferentially recognize processed linear epitopes generated during tumor cell death and antigen release and/or subsequent antigen presentation. At the same time, because the antibodies identified here were measured against linear peptides, they may not necessarily exert direct functional effects on tumor cells, which often depend on conformational epitopes present in the native full-length HER2 protein. In addition, immunogenicity in the present study was evaluated at the peptide level using IgG titers against a predefined panel of 20-mer HER2-derived linear peptides. But rigorous delineation of minimal immunogenic epitopes would require dedicated epitope-mapping strategies (e.g., overlapping shorter-peptide mapping and/or alanine-scanning mutagenesis). Although these experiments were beyond the scope of the current study, they represent important directions for future work.

In the present study, mapping highly immunogenic peptides onto the three-dimensional structure of the HER2 protein showed that most were located within the ECD of HER2 and exposed on the protein surface, consistent with prior reports [27]. Previous structural studies have demonstrated that B-cell epitope immunogenicity is influenced not only by surface accessibility/solvent exposure in the native folded antigen [28] but also by intrinsic physicochemical properties, including hydrophilicity, charge distribution, and peptide flexibility [29, 30]. Consistent with these principles, our correlation analyses suggested that peptides with a higher Boman index (reflecting protein-binding potential) and a lower aliphatic index (consistent with reduced hydrophobicity and relatively greater hydrophilicity) tended to elicit stronger IgG responses in pretreatment sera. These trends support the notion that such physicochemical features may contribute to heterogeneity in HER2-peptide-specific immunogenicity, potentially by favoring antibody recognition of solvent-accessible, hydrophilic linear segments. However, because these associations are correlative and our study did not directly quantify physicochemical properties, such as antibody–antigen binding kinetics, these findings should be regarded as hypothesis-generating and warrant further experimental validation, such as structure-based accessibility analyses and/or binding assays. Recent studies have also described extensive diversity of HER2 isoforms generated by alternative splicing [31, 32]. Such isoform-dependent structural differences may influence the immune system’s ability to recognize HER2-derived epitopes and could potentially affect the generation or maintenance of HER2-specific autoantibodies, although this possibility warrants further investigation.

Notably, the majority of immunogenic HER2-derived peptides in this study exhibited significant decreases in specific IgG titers following NAC. This observation is consistent with previous reports indicating that serum HER2 levels decreased after NAC in patients with HER2-positive breast cancer, particularly among those achieving favorable pathological responses [33–36]. Accordingly, one plausible explanation for the post-NAC decline in HER2-peptide-specific autoantibodies is that effective treatment could reduce HER2 protein abundance, thereby limiting antigen availability and potentially diminishing antigen-driven antibody production. In line with this interpretation, serum HER2 protein levels tended to be positively associated with HER2-peptide–specific antibody titers both before and after NAC, as well as with treatment-induced changes in both parameters. In addition, changes in several chemokines, including MIP-1β, RANTES, MCP-1, eotaxin, and IL-8, showed trends toward inverse correlations with changes in HER2-peptide–specific antibody titers. These mediators are primarily linked to leukocyte recruitment and activation (e.g., cytotoxic T cells and innate immune populations) [37], and might therefore reflect treatment-associated inflammatory remodeling rather than B-cell–dominant humoral immune responses. Thus, therapy-induced shifts toward inflammatory immune states that are immunologically distinct from humoral immunity could be associated with reductions in HER2-peptide–specific antibody titers, although these relationships remain correlative and would require further validation. Mechanistically, it is also conceivable that elevations in these chemokines could coincide with increased recruitment and activation of monocytes and macrophages, which might, in turn, influence the handling of antigen–antibody immune complexes [37, 38]. Under such conditions, a fraction of circulating antibodies could potentially be sequestered in immune complexes and cleared more efficiently via Fc receptor–dependent phagocytic pathways; however, this interpretation remains speculative in the absence of direct measurements of immune complexes or Fc receptor activity, Collectively, these findings suggest that the observed dynamics of HER2-peptide–specific antibody titers may reflect multiple, non-mutually exclusive mechanisms, including response-related reductions in HER2 antigen availability, broader shifts in the systemic immune milieu during therapy, and altered formation and/or clearance of immune complexes (e.g., sequestration by circulating HER2 and/or therapy-induced HER2 fragments). Nevertheless, given the exploratory nature of our analyses, further studies will be required to clarify the mechanistic basis of these antibody changes.

In the present study, none of the individual HER2-peptide–specific IgG titers (pretreatment, posttreatment, or fold changes) showed a statistically significant association with pCR in univariate regression analyses. Therefore, we conducted the machine learning analyses as exploratory, multivariate evaluations to assess whether combinatorial patterns across multiple peptides, rather than any single peptide, could improve discrimination of pCR. In this context, we observed that a cluster comprising multiple informative peptides achieved better predictive performance compared with any individual peptide alone. Importantly, these machine learning analyses do not replace the primary statistical analyses, but instead suggest that coordinated changes across multiple peptides may collectively capture response-associated information that is not detectable in univariate models. Accordingly, these machine learning results should be interpreted as hypothesis-generating rather than confirmatory evidence. Notably, secondary-structure predictions indicated that the peptides selected by the machine-learning–based models were preferentially located within regions predicted to be structurally disordered. While this observation is consistent with the notion that flexible, disordered regions may be more accessible to immune recognition and more likely to give rise to linear B-cell epitopes [39], further studies will be required to clarify the underlying mechanisms.

Nevertheless, this study has several limitations. First, the predictive models were exploratory and validated only using internal holdout sets; therefore, external validation in independent cohorts is required to confirm their generalizability and reproducibility. Second, the NAC regimens in this study included anti-HER2 agents and paclitaxel, with endocrine therapy administered in some cases. Thus, the observed changes in HER2-peptide-specific IgG titers may have been influenced by concomitant therapies and cannot be attributed solely to anti-HER2 therapy. Third, the neoadjuvant regimens in this study included lapatinib, which is not commonly used in contemporary standard NAC for HER2-positive breast cancer and is not approved for this indication. This limits the generalizability of our findings to current clinical practice, and it remains uncertain whether the observed associations would generalize to currently used anti-HER2 combinations. Therefore, larger, well-controlled studies incorporating contemporary regimens are warranted to validate and more definitively establish the clinical utility of HER2-peptide-specific autoantibodies.

Conclusions

This study shows that HER2-peptide-specific IgG responses are dynamic and heterogeneous and may relate to treatment response. Our results provide proof-of-concept evidence that minimally invasive, longitudinal peptide-specific autoantibody profiling could complement established modalities, such as imaging or molecular assays, for assessing clinical responses. Nevertheless, these findings are exploratory and require validation in larger independent cohorts before any clinical application can be considered.
