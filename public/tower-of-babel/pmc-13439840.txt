Barriers to healthcare and justice for sexual and gender-based violence survivors in Sierra Leone: A gender-integrated socio-ecological analysis
Authors: Rose McKeon Olson, Josephine Ndanemah, Daniel Nguyen, Ranu Dhillon, Daniel Kettor, Anthony Sam, Lydia E. Pace
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSexual and gender-based violence (SGBV) is a threat to women and girls' health and justice globally, and is widely prevalent in Sierra Leone. This study identifies key barriers to high-quality healthcare and justice for SGBV survivors in Sierra Leone using a gender-integrated socio-ecological model.MethodsIn this qualitative study, purposive sampling was performed with maximum variation by sector and region. From December 2023 to February 2024, Sierra Leonean and international researchers conducted semi-structured interviews and focus group discussions with health, legal, law enforcement, and government officials across Sierra Leone. Data analysis employed a hybrid thematic approach using a gender-integrated socio-ecological model (GI-SEM) framework while remaining open to emerging themes. Analysis was completed between August 2024-March 2025.ResultsAcross five sites in Sierra Leone, a total of 31 multidisciplinary staff participants were included through semi-structured individual (n=17) and focus group interviews (n=6), including healthcare providers (n=15; 48.4%), law enforcement officers (n=9; 29.0%), government officials (n=5; 16.1%), and legal professionals (n=2; 6.5%). At the individual level, perceived barriers included system fatigue, internalized stigma, and secondary trauma. Interpersonal barriers included power imbalances in provider interactions and coercion by survivor networks. Organizational barriers consisted of service accessibility gaps, operational inefficiencies, and resource constraints. Community-level barriers involved religious interference and insufficient prevention engagement. At the societal level, financial constraints, legal framework gaps, and stigmatizing attitudes further impeded access to care and justice.ConclusionDespite ongoing efforts, SGBV survivors in Sierra Leone face persistent challenges accessing care and justice. These findings highlight critical intervention points across multiple levels to strengthen response systems.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13690-026-01944-w.

Background

Sexual and gender-based violence (SGBV) is a major global public health issue that disproportionately affects women and girls [1]. It is estimated that one in three women experiences SGBV during their lifetime, and the situation in Sierra Leone is particularly severe [1]. A 2019 national survey revealed that approximately 62% of women aged 15–49 have endured physical or sexual violence [1, 2]. SGBV leads to significant and often enduring health consequences, including traumatic brain injuries, sexually transmitted infections, post-traumatic stress disorder, chronic pain conditions, and substance use disorders [3–5].

The high prevalence of SGBV in Sierra Leone reflects the compounding effects of persistent patriarchal gender norms, post-conflict reconstruction, and widespread economic vulnerability. SGBV stems from harmful patriarchal norms that reinforce gender inequality, limit women’s autonomy, and normalize mistreatment and abuse [6, 7]. Structural stressors such as poverty, political instability, and armed conflict can increase the risk of violence through several mechanisms, including increased economic dependence, weakened legal protections, and normalization of violence, which can drive perpetrators to assert control through traditional power hierarchies, ultimately worsening SGBV [8–11]. Sierra Leone’s historical context provides a particularly salient example of these dynamics. During the country’s 1991–2002 Civil War, an estimated 257,000 people suffered sexual violence, with 27% of cases involving multiple perpetrators [12]. In the aftermath of the war, poverty, weak institutional systems, and social destabilization further increased women’s vulnerability to violence, exacerbating barriers to protection, medical care, and legal recourse [8].

In response, Sierra Leone has implemented multisectoral initiatives to combat SGBV. The Sexual Offences Amendment Act (2019) increased penalties for sexual offenses, and the government has prioritized multisectoral coordination, establishing Family Support Units (FSUs) to facilitate legal and medical referrals [13]. The Rainbo Initiative, a national non-governmental organization, supplements these efforts by providing free medical, psychosocial, and legal services for survivors, recording 2,762 cases of SGBV in 2023 [14, 15]. Additionally, community-based initiatives such as the “Hands Off Our Girls” campaign and “husband schools” have aimed to transform cultural attitudes [16, 17].

Despite ongoing efforts, SGBV remains widespread in Sierra Leone [8, 15, 18]. Evidence has suggested that many survivors do not seek help due to stigma, fear of being shunned, or pressure to accept financial settlements from perpetrators’ families [16]. Those who try to access care often face long wait times, limited medical and forensic services, and dismissive treatment from healthcare workers or law enforcement. Survivors who pursue justice also encounter major barriers in the legal and law enforcement systems, including delayed investigations, lack of legal support, and limited coordination between police and courts. Professionals across sectors report frustration with under-resourced systems that make it difficult to provide timely, survivor-centered care and justice.

Multisectoral barriers to high-quality healthcare and medicolegal services for SGBV survivors in Sierra Leone have not previously been systematically examined in the literature. In this study, we use a hybrid gender-analysis and socio-ecological lens to identify factors impacting receipt of SGBV services [19, 20]. By identifying these factors, this research seeks to inform evidence-based interventions and policy recommendations that can strengthen Sierra Leone’s response to SGBV and improve outcomes for survivors.

Methods

Study design

This qualitative study examines how gender dynamics influence barriers to high-quality healthcare and medicolegal services in Sierra Leone across multiple levels of a survivor’s environment using community-based participatory research methods. Ethical approval was obtained from the Sierra Leone Ethics and Scientific Review Committee and the Mass General Brigham Institutional Review Board.

Overview of SGBV medicolegal process in Sierra Leone

In Sierra Leone, survivors of sexual violence navigate a complex medicolegal response pathway involving multiple sectors (Fig. 1). They may first present to dedicated SGBV centers for medical care and psychosocial services – either an NGO-run center known as “Rainbo Centers” or government-run “One Stop Centers.” Survivors may also present to a local hospital if they need emergency treatment or to a “Family Support Unit” (FSU), a specialized police division that investigates SGBV cases. From the FSU, cases may proceed through the legal system, first to magistrate courts and to the Directorate of Public Prosecutions (DPP), and may ultimately reach the Supreme Court, which handles sexual assault cases under the Sexual Offences Amendment Act (2019). Government agencies oversee policy implementation, resource allocation, and inter-agency coordination to support survivor-centered services.

Setting and participants

In partnership with local SGBV experts, we purposively sampled multisectoral professionals from Sierra Leone’s four major regions—Western Area, Northern Province, Southern Province, and Eastern Province. The final sites were chosen due to their reputation as SGBV service hubs, and to allow for urban–rural variation and feasibility. Eligible participants were ≥ 18 years old, fluent in English, able to provide informed consent, and professionally involved in SGBV service provision.

Sampling and data collection

We employed purposive expert sampling to recruit 31 multisectoral professionals with direct experience in SGBV service provision. We used maximum variation sampling, selecting participants across diverse institutional roles, geographic regions, and genders. Recruitment was facilitated by Rainbo Center site supervisors, who informed eligible staff about the study. Sampling and data collection continued until thematic saturation was reached, defined as the point at which three consecutive interviews yielded no new substantive themes or insights relevant to the research questions across the full dataset, rather than within individual regions or roles. This approach is consistent with the analytic goal to capture sufficient breadth of perspectives across a phenomenon rather than representativeness of each participant category. Findings from smaller subgroups should therefore be interpreted as illustrative rather than exhaustive of those perspectives.

Data collection occurred between December 2023 and February 2024, using semi-structured individual and focus group interviews conducted in-person or via private video or audio conference. The research team developed multiple interview guides tailored to participants’ professional roles, guided by a GI-SEM framework and focusing on gender-related experiences, challenges, and strategies within their respective domains of SGBV response (Appendix). Prior to full implementation, the interview guides were piloted with service professionals representing different sectors. Each interview or focus group was conducted in English by two trained interviewers (R.O, J.N.). Focus groups were sector-specific (e.g., police officers together), and participants were offered their preferred format. This flexible approach is consistent with established qualitative methodology, which recognizes that individual interviews are often more appropriate for eliciting personal experiences on sensitive topics, while focus groups can capture shared collective perspectives within a sector and shared professional norms. Interviews were audio-recorded using an encrypted digital recorder with participant consent. Professional human transcription services were used, and all transcripts were de-identified before analysis to protect participant confidentiality.

Research positionality

This study was grounded in community-based participatory principles. Our research positionality involved intentional partnership with the Rainbo Initiative, Sierra Leone’s leading SGBV service provider, whose experts collaborated throughout the study design, implementation, and analysis phases. Sierra Leonean team members contributed critical perspectives to interview questions to ensure cultural relevance, facilitated participant recruitment, and provided essential contextual and linguistic interpretation during data collection and analysis. The multidisciplinary team included U.S. researchers with expertise in SGBV and global health working alongside Rainbo Initiative specialists, creating a collaborative environment where local knowledge informed multiple aspects of the research.

Development and application of the gender-integrated socio-ecological model

Existing frameworks often fail to adequately capture how gender influences multiple levels of an individual’s environment, such as social norms, institutional constraints, and policy environments. For this investigation, we developed a Gender-Integrated Socio-Ecological Model (GI-SEM) by integrating aspects of the Socio-Ecological Model and Gender Analysis Framework. The integrated model is depicted in Fig. 2 [19, 20]. This GI-SEM facilitated the analysis of gendered power imbalances across multiple levels of a survivor’s environment.

Data analysis

Transcripts were uploaded into NVivo (Burlington, Massachusetts, USA), where two researchers (D.N., R.D.) independently conducted open coding alongside a GI-SEM framework to identify emergent themes. A third researcher provided consultation to resolve coding discrepancies and ensure rigor (R.O.). Data analysis employed a hybrid thematic approach using a GI-SEM framework while remaining open to emerging themes, ensuring a structured yet flexible exploration of gendered influences across multiple levels of the survivor’s environment [21]. Trustworthiness was enhanced through audit trails, independent coding, and team-based review processes. Preliminary findings were also presented to Sierra Leonean SGBV experts for review and feedback before finalization. To aid interpretive transparency, we use semi-quantitative language throughout the Results to indicate the prevalence of themes across participants, e.g. almost all” (the large majority of participants), “most” (more than half), “a few” (a small number of participants).This approach follows established practice in qualitative thematic analysis, where the goal is to convey the salience of themes without reducing them to frequency counts.

Results

We interviewed 31 professionals who provide SGBV medicolegal services across five sites in Sierra Leone through semi-structured individual (n = 17) and focus group interviews (n = 6; 2–4 participants per group). The sample included 16 women (51.6%) and 15 men (48.4%), with a mean age of 43.6 years (range: 27–59). Participants included healthcare providers (n = 15, 48.4%), law enforcement officers from the Family Support Unit (FSU) (n = 9, 29.0%), government officials (n = 5, 16.1%), and legal/judicial professionals (n = 2, 6.5%). Participants had served in their current roles for an average of 5.4 years. Regional representation was achieved with participants from five sites: Freetown (n = 9), Bo (n = 8), Kenema (n = 5), Makeni (n = 5), and Kailahun (n = 2). Theme 1: Perceived disruption of survivor agency

Respondents noted that survivors of SGBV face multiple, gendered barriers that undermine their personal agency, decision-making, and trust in both healthcare and legal systems, barriers shaped by patriarchal norms that stigmatize female survivors and reinforce deference to male authority. The following findings reflect the perceptions and observations of SGBV service providers and professionals rather than direct accounts from survivors, who were not participants in this study.

Subtheme 1A. System fatigue and disengagement

Case abandonment

Nearly half of respondents discussed how systemic delays may increase female survivors’ desire to drop cases early in the reporting process. One legal interviewee mentioned how “some of [the survivors] just lose interest” when they experience delays, especially with the courts in seeking legal justice. This pattern of disengagement is gendered: women and girls face additional coercive pressure from family members and perpetrators to abandon cases, compounding the systemic delays.

Missed legal appearances

Several informants reported that survivors struggle to keep up with the legal process, which can be overwhelming and opaque. As a result, respondents noted that many cases stall or are dismissed due to procedural lapses.

Medical nonadherence

Some survivors struggled to follow treatment plans due to limited health literacy and inadequate guidance, including critical medications including post-exposure prophylaxis (PEP), emergency contraception, and pain relief. A healthcare provider observed, “They return with the same discharge because they weren’t taking their drugs properly.” Without proper follow-up care and education, survivors risk worsening health outcomes.

Subtheme 1B. Internalized fear and stigma

Respondents reported that stigma and fear often prevented survivors from reporting their experiences and fully engaging with support services. Concerns about confidentiality, judgment, and pressure from external influences contributed to inconsistent testimonies and silence. This pattern of internalized stigma reflects patriarchal norms that locate shame in the survivor rather than the perpetrator, reinforcing silence as a mechanism of gendered social control.

Nondisclosure

Nearly half of all informants noted how stigma prevents survivors from reporting due to fear of being judged, blamed, or ostracized by their communities. One midwife noted, “The individuals, they are still scared to come out and speak out.” This fear may force survivors into isolation, preventing them from accessing the support they need.

Inconsistent testimonies

Some informants discussed that survivors may change or provide incomplete testimonies due to external pressures or fear of breached confidentiality, especially at the FSU. This inconsistency can weaken their cases, making it harder to achieve justice.

Subtheme 1C. Secondary psychological trauma

The process of seeking care and legal recourse may retraumatize survivors, reinforcing avoidance behaviors and psychological distress. Respondents noted that many survivors experience cognitive overload, emotional exhaustion, and a lack of trauma-informed support.

Retraumatization in care-seeking

Survivors often face repeated questioning and disclosure at multiple service points, which can compound psychological distress and delay healing. One SGBV center supervisor noted, “Survivors repeatedly telling her story means we are still recalling her memory to what has happened. She wants to forget, but we still ask her what happened…that alone prolongs the healing process.” This process forced survivors to relive their trauma, sometimes without adequate emotional support or safeguards in place.

Long-term psychological consequences

Respondents noted that many survivors experience long-term psychological distress following SGBV, manifesting as heightened anxiety, depression, post-traumatic distress symptoms, and, in some cases, suicidality. One SGBV center supervisor said, “The mind is very difficult to heal.”

Theme 2: interpersonal power dynamics and gendered interactions

Survivors of SGBV navigate complex interpersonal challenges that shape their access to care and justice. Perpetrator and family interference of this kind often reflected patriarchal structures in which male authority—whether exercised by husbands, fathers, male religious leaders, or other male relatives—overrides women’s and girls’ autonomous decision-making about their own healthcare and legal recourse. These power imbalances create significant barriers, often reinforcing gender inequities and limiting survivor autonomy.

Subtheme 2A. Power balance in survivor-provider interactions

Need for trust and transparency in care settings

Nearly half of the participants discussed that providers need to establish rapport to encourage survivors to speak about their stories. One midwife reported, “Everything we are doing is communication…if we don’t know how to communicate, then we won’t be able to get the full story.” Participants emphasized that healthcare providers should build trust by preparing survivors for difficult questions, explaining procedures beforehand, and using trauma-informed communication. Rainbo Centers were cited as a model for fostering survivor comfort, contrasting with more rigid or intimidating healthcare and legal settings.

Gatekeeping in post-assault healthcare

A few interviewees revealed that doctors often defer to midwives’ exams, potentially missing critical information or delaying care. Additionally, some informants mentioned how some medical providers reportedly request payment before signing medical certificates. When providers charge fees for medicolegal documentation, survivors face a financial barrier to accessing justice, which turns a critical service into a gatekeeping mechanism rather than a form of care.

Communication barriers for survivors with disabilities

Participants discussed how providers should have communication accommodations for certain groups, such as children, people who are illiterate, and people with special needs. A healthcare interviewee mentioned how “sometimes [they] have to call maybe a relative, to help [them]” because there are insufficient resources to support communication among these groups.

Subtheme 2B. Coercion by survivor network

Perpetrator fear tactics and retaliation

Participants discussed how external influential actors, such as perpetrators and politicians, often interfere with the reporting process, threatening and curtailing survivor safety and autonomy. One healthcare informant noted a particular instance that threatened survivor safety: “Sometimes they say, ‘You see this knife? I’m keeping it. You see? I’m sharpening it…’ So that girl will be in a big distress.”

Family pressure and undue influence

Nearly half of the informants discussed how family members can disrupt and interfere with the standard practices of the reporting process, either by manipulating the reporting process or discouraging engagement with formal services. While family involvement may sometimes reflect protective intent, respondents predominantly described it as interfering with survivor autonomy and self-determined care-seeking. One informant noted that survivors only “come to the center if…like…the parents drag them,” even if it’s “not [their] wish to come to the center.”

Theme 3: organizational challenges in gender-responsive service delivery

Interviews revealed how institutional structures and organizational practices systematically undermine gender-sensitive SGBV services in Sierra Leone. These organizational structures, shaped by male-dominated hierarchies and patriarchal professional norms, have historically deprioritized the specific needs of SGBV survivors, resulting in service delivery failures that disproportionately affect women and girls.

Subtheme 3A. Gaps in service accessibility and inclusivity

High cost of services

Institutional funding models create financial obstacles that disproportionately affect survivors, who often lack economic resources. While core SGBV services at Crisis Support Centers like Rainbo Centers are provided free of charge, survivors frequently incur costs for transportation, hospital fees for care received outside dedicated centers, and costs associated with legal proceedings. Informants noted that these costs prevent many survivors, particularly women with limited financial autonomy, from accessing critical care. One midwife noted, “If it was to be paid for, it would’ve been a huge stress on the people who cannot afford it and most of the cases wouldn’t be just swept under the carpet.”

Geographic inaccessibility

The concentration of services in urban centers systematically disadvantages rural survivors, especially women and girls who face greater mobility restrictions due to safety concerns, caregiving responsibilities, and limited access to transportation resources.

Limited inclusivity in service design

Informants revealed that organizational structures often failed to accommodate diverse survivor needs. This included a lack of interpreters and inadequate accommodations for survivors with disabilities, such as inaccessible buildings, as well as an absence of child-friendly spaces that address the specific vulnerabilities of young female survivors.

Subtheme 3B. Operational inefficiencies in SGBV service delivery

Fragmented multidisciplinary coordination

The lack of integration between health, legal, and social service sectors creates a disjointed system, which is difficult for survivors to navigate, particularly while also dealing with their recent trauma. This fragmentation particularly burdens female survivors who typically have fewer resources and greater safety concerns when traveling between service points. “The coordination is 60% for obvious reasons,” explained a government official.

Case processing delays

Half of the interviewees discussed the inefficient and bureaucratic nature of criminal procedures in SGBV cases, which ultimately harm the survivors’ ability to seek justice. “So at the end of the day, justice delayed, justice denied,” detailed a healthcare informant. Cases can remain with the state council for months and even years, causing delays that can make it unclear which actor in the medicolegal process actually has the files at a given time point.

Subtheme 3C: human resource limitations in service delivery

Critical staff shortages

Over half of the informants mentioned how chronic understaffing creates significant burdens that can impact quality of care. The limited availability of doctors, midwives, and trained counselors, particularly during weekends when many cases are reported, creates barriers to timely care for survivors. In particular, respondents noted that physician shortages led to delays in forensic examinations and completion of necessary medical certificates for medicolegal cases, often resulting in missed evidence and weakened prosecutions. To address this, some suggested training midwives to complete medical certificates, though concerns were raised about ensuring proper training and legal integrity. Additionally, respondents noted that lack of significant law enforcement providers for the investigation and state prosecutors can lead to delays in cases and evidence collection.

Role overload and staff burnout

Many staff reported needing to work multiple roles, which leads to staff burnout and increased turnover, which can exacerbate the existing staff shortages. One government ministry representative stated, “So you find out that one center manager is doing a lot of work, just one person carrying this huge burden…you find out it’s just one person managing dual roles.”

Lack of training in trauma-informed care

Findings revealed significant gaps in training in trauma-informed care across healthcare, law enforcement, and social services, contributing to gender-insensitive practices and barriers to survivor-centered care. Participants emphasized the need for training in confidentiality, privacy, and trauma-informed interviewing techniques. A SGBV response coordinator described negative attitudes among some healthcare staff, noting, “The way they talk to patients is not really encouraging…they used to shout at patients.”

Staff well-being deficiencies

Several informants also highlighted the absence of psychosocial support and resources for staff involved in SGBV care, noting how secondary trauma and burnout can diminish their ability to effectively support survivors. One midwife noted, “Even us, most times, we are traumatized. Based on the stories that we hear.”

Subtheme 3D: organizational resource constraints

Essential supply gaps

Many informants noted that basic supplies were often unavailable, such as critical medications and test supplies. A midwife stated, “We need a standard pregnancy test, not expired [ones]. We need also HIV tests…not the expired ones. And we also need the lights for me to see what I am even examining.” Additionally, survivor support items such as clean clothes, sanitary pads, and food and water were often in extreme shortages.

Space and infrastructure constraints

Facilities often lacked adequate space and infrastructure to ensure survivor confidentiality and comfort. Informants cited small waiting areas, poor evidence file storage, and limited private interview rooms as key barriers to providing safe, trauma-informed care.

Forensic capacity deficiencies

The absence of forensic testing capacity in Sierra Leone systematically weakens survivors’ access to justice. Without forensic capacity, cases often rely heavily on verbal testimony, which faces greater scrutiny in legal proceedings. One state prosecutor stated, “Are they applying forensic analysis to the evidence they’re gathering? No… we do not have everything factual because we don’t have forensic evidence.” Without accessible forensic services, critical evidence is not available, which can weaken legal cases and reduce the likelihood of convictions.

Theme 4: community responses to SGBV

Informants discussed community-level gendered barriers to post-SGBV care and justice, as community and religious structures in Sierra Leone are predominantly male-led and reflect patriarchal norms that prioritize family honor and community cohesion. Informants also discussed the important role of gender-sensitive community engagement and solidarity in increasing comfort and visibility for female survivors in Sierra Leone.

Subtheme 4A: community and religious interference

Informal justice mechanisms

Several participants discussed how communities often prefer resolving SGBV cases locally, which can undermine formal legal processes that comprise the medicolegal process in post-SGBV justice and care. “It’s not gonna come to court, it’s not going to the police station,” expressed one legal informant, expressing that communities would rather prefer to keep cases at the community level.

Intrusive accompaniment

Some participants mentioned that many survivors are accompanied by multiple members of their local communities, often intruding on survivors’ agency and confidentiality in buildings already limited for space. One healthcare worker recalled how survivors often arrive at the center “with a huge number of people from the community”, but they can only permit the “key person needed for signatories” to stay and provide support for the female survivor.

Religious interference

Some informants discussed how religious leaders sometimes play a role in pressuring survivors to drop cases. Religious leaders such as pastors and imams were described as influential figures in the community helping “not to take this thing forward”, indicating that organized religion can compromise post-SGBV justice and care for women and girls.

Compromised community environments

Participants discussed safety concerns for female survivors in the community, such as the chance of sharing transportation with perpetrators and the ineffectiveness of restraining orders, deterring survivors from feeling safe in their communities. Several informants also described that survivors often fled home or dropped out of school to avoid their perpetrator and/or the perpetrator’s family/allies. A government representative expressed that restraining orders are “not effective,” suggesting that survivor safety remains compromised in their living environments.

Subtheme 4B: need for positive community engagement for SGBV prevention

Public awareness initiatives

Several informants discussed the importance of community awareness campaigns, which can encourage survivors to disclose violence and increase transparency that fosters a safe space for disclosure. A law enforcement participant discussed how sectors engage in public awareness through campaigns such as “radio discussion panel[s]” and “going down to the grassroots of the community” to capture community perspectives and increase visibility for female survivors. Interviewees also discussed that by seeing legal action visibly taken against perpetrators at the community level, future cases should be minimized.

Collaborative advocacy

Half of all informants discussed the role of community partnerships and the importance of a steer-in or advisory committee to enhance awareness and advocacy for SGBV prevention. “To understand ourselves because we understand each other’s operatives,” stated one law enforcement interviewee, emphasizing the need to collaborate across sectors to streamline care for female survivors.

Theme 5: structural barriers to survivor healthcare and justice

Interviewees described how societal- and policy-level barriers limit care and justice for survivors. These structural gaps reflect a political environment in which gender-responsive services are chronically underfunded and undervalued because violence against women is not treated as a public health or governance priority.

Subtheme 5A: financial constraints

Poverty and financial insecurity

Almost all participants discussed how survivors often cannot afford medical care, legal fees, or transportation. “Their fare is an issue. Who is going to provide the fare?” asked a healthcare provider, questioning who should be held responsible to help cover the financial barriers to providing medical and legal services for survivors.

Unstable and donor-driven funding

Nearly half of all informants discussed the instability of funding sources and the reliance on donor-driven funding, leading to inconsistent service provision, ultimately subverting survivor care. Participants also expressed that providers need to lobby partners to meet funding needs. “But then it all boils down to who pays for these services,” mentioned a government interviewee, raising a question of who should take the responsibility of covering these compensations.

Subtheme 5B: gaps in legal and policy frameworks

Overemphasis on physical evidence

Half of all informants revealed how legal prosecution is heavily dependent on physical injuries, diminishing the role of survivor testimonies. One state prosecutor stated, “The main issue is that there is insufficient evidence…. For example, the slap, the scar on the faces of the victims.”

Medical certificate requirements

Only physicians, who are predominantly male in Sierra Leone, are authorized to issue medicolegal documentation required for legal proceedings. Despite this, midwives, who are predominantly female, typically perform the initial physical examinations. This gendered hierarchy limits female practitioners’ formal authority and creates delays that further marginalize survivors in low-resource settings.

Justice system design inefficiencies

Several respondents underscored how complexities in the judicial system created disproportionate barriers for survivors. The requirement for child penetration cases to be heard at the Supreme Court creates significant delays, often leading to case abandonment as survivors often cannot sustain extended engagement with legal institutions. A legal professional stated, “But when it leads to the legal system, it’s sometimes so cumbersome that that is where we lose most of our cases,” highlighting the consequences on survivors of inefficient justice systems.

Neglect of adult survivors

Some informants discussed that the legal focus is disproportionately on child sexual abuse cases, with less attention to adult survivors, impacting adult women who are survivors of SGBV. A law enforcement participant shared their perspective on how they handle cases: “You have to think about the age. That is the first element we look at. And the penetration itself. The age, because the child don’t have consent of sex”.

Subtheme 5C: stigma, prejudice, and discrimination

These judgments are rooted in patriarchal gender norms that hold women responsible for maintaining family honor, effectively penalizing survivors for the violence perpetrated against them. Such beliefs reflect patriarchal control over women’s sexuality, in which a woman’s credibility as a survivor is contingent on conforming to norms of sexual purity—norms that do not apply to perpetrators.

Social judgment

Most participants detailed that survivors face stigma and shame in their communities due to harmful gender norms and roles that undermine gender equity for survivors. A government participant expressed how survivors and their family members are “afraid of people pointing fingers at their family” and the stigma against SGBV “by an extended family member of their family, which they consider to be a taboo.”

Harmful cultural beliefs

Some informants discussed how certain misconceptions diminish survivor credibility in court, such as a common belief that if the survivor is not a virgin, then SGBV is not considered rape. “You should not tamper with a girl even if another man has virginated her,” discussed a healthcare participant, suggesting that there are misconceptions about SGBV among the population.

Discussion

This qualitative study identified key barriers to high-quality healthcare and justice for survivors of SGBV in Sierra Leone using a gender-informed and socio-ecologic model (GI-SEM). We found that: (1) survivors face significant individual and interpersonal barriers, including system fatigue, internalized stigma, and psychological trauma; (2) organizational challenges—such as staffing shortages, inadequate training, and poor multisectoral coordination—undermine trauma-informed, gender-sensitive care; and (3) structural and societal barriers, including harmful gender norms and legal gaps, perpetuate stigma and restrict access to justice. While Sierra Leone has taken important steps to strengthen its SGBV response—such as the establishment of Rainbo Centers, Family Support Units, and legal reforms like the Sexual Offences Amendment Act—our findings show persistent challenges across all levels of a survivor’s environment.

Across all GI-SEM levels, patriarchal norms emerge as an active structuring force that limit survivors’ access to healthcare and legal recourse. At the individual level, internalized stigma reflects patriarchal expectations that place shame on survivors rather than perpetrators [13, 17]. At the interpersonal level, family coercion and perpetrator retaliation reflect the normative dominance patriarchy affords men over women’s decisions and mobility [22, 23]. These findings align with research from other sub-Saharan African contexts, where stigma, avoidance, and institutional distrust commonly reduce survivors’ use of post-violence services [24].

At the organizational level, participants described chronic shortages of staff and medical supplies, and infrastructure, particularly in rural areas. The doctor-only medicolegal certificate policy inadvertently concentrates formal authority in predominantly male-dominated roles while midwives—more often women—conduct much of the relevant clinical work for survivors without equivalent legal recognition. These findings are consistent with broader literature on resource constraints in SGBV care across low-resource and post-conflict settings [22, 23, 25]. As in many contexts, access was particularly limited in rural areas, and survivors are particularly constrained by the high costs of some services [26].

At the community level, survivors experienced interference in seeking formal care and legal recourse by religious leaders, community members, and informal justice mechanisms, which may be due to the reinforcement of patriarchal norms by local religious and cultural traditions [27, 28]. This interference with formal reporting reinforces patriarchal hierarchies that prioritize community harmony over individual women’s rights. Finally, at the societal level, structural factors, such as poverty, insufficient funding, legal gaps, and problematic cultural attitudes, undermine the progress envisioned by the Sexual Offences Amendment Act and other important steps that Sierra Leone has to address this problem.This cross-level analysis highlights the importance of gender-transformative approaches that address patriarchal norms explicitly, rather than treating them as immutable background conditions.

While many themes were shared across professional roles, some differences emerged between healthcare providers and judicial actors. Healthcare providers emphasized trust-building, trauma-informed communication, and survivor-level system fatigue, whereas judicial professionals highlighted evidentiary challenges and procedural delays. These differing perspectives underscore the need for cross-sectoral training and collaboration to align priorities around survivor-centered care and justice.

In comparing our findings to existing research on SGBV care in sub-Saharan Africa, several common barriers emerge—most notably stigma, financial constraints, limited human resources, and poor multisectoral coordination [23–25, 29, 30]. However, this study also identified context-specific challenges in Sierra Leone that merit particular attention. One key barrier was Sierra Leone’s legal requirement that only doctors—rather than midwives—can complete medicolegal certificates after sexual assault. Given that midwives are often the first and only point of clinical contact, this regulation led to delays and evidence loss. Coupled with staff shortages across organizations and the emphasis of medical certification in SGBV care, this barrier spans across organizational and societal levels of survivors’ environments in Sierra Leone. Many participants strongly supported expanding midwives’ scope through training and formal recognition—an approach consistent with task-shifting models used in other low-resource settings to strengthen forensic capacity [31].

Another context-specific finding was the persistent overreliance on physical evidence in court and myths surrounding the “intact hymen” as proof of sexual assault. These medically inaccurate beliefs can reinforce harmful gender norms and contribute to case dismissals in court. Similar challenges related to “virginity testing” have been reported in other low- and middle-income countries, despite global consensus on its lack of scientific merit and ethical justification [32]. The widespread concept of “compromise” also stood out as a very common barrier to justice for survivors in Sierra Leone. Many participants described how perpetrators or their families pressure survivors to settle cases informally—often financially—outside formal legal channels. These arrangements, often driven by poverty and social pressure, bypass formal justice mechanisms and reflect patterns of economic coercion seen in other patriarchal or post-conflict settings [33, 34].

In addition, limited inclusivity in service design was a recurring issue. Participants reported inadequate accommodations for survivors with disabilities, including inaccessible buildings, lack of interpreters, and poor communication tools. These findings mirror global evidence that SGBV services often overlook the needs of disabled survivors, further compounding their vulnerability [35].

Limitations

To our knowledge, this is the first study to systematically examine multisectoral barriers to medicolegal services for SGBV survivors in Sierra Leone. Strengths of this work include our engagement of key community stakeholders throughout study design and implementation, our inclusion of participants representing multiple sectors and districts, and our use of a novel integrated thematic framework. Nonetheless, our study has limitations. First, while we purposively sampled professionals across healthcare, law enforcement, legal, and government sectors, the perspectives of survivors themselves are not represented. Survivors who never presented to formal services—often the most marginalized—remain an important but absent perspective. Second, the sample was disproportionately weighted toward healthcare providers, which may have emphasized more clinical barriers relative to legal or community-level concerns, and due to small numbers in some regions and among some professional categories (e.g., only two participants from Kailahun and two judicial officials), saturation may not have been reached within every subgroup. As such, findings should be interpreted with caution regarding their representativeness across all regions and sectors in Sierra Leone. Third, there is potential for interviewer bias and social desirability effects: participants may have felt pressure to represent their organizations positively when speaking with an external researcher, and might have downplayed challenges or presented overly optimistic perspectives. The presence of a foreign researcher may have influenced responses based on perceived expectations or power dynamics, potentially limiting candidness despite efforts to build rapport. Lastly, while this study aimed for national diversity, it may still underrepresent perspectives from more rural regions.

Conclusion

Survivors of SGBV in Sierra Leone continue to face major barriers to high-quality healthcare and justice, despite important efforts to improve services. This study highlights specific gaps across sectors and levels of the system. Addressing these barriers will require coordinated, trauma-informed, and community-driven solutions.
