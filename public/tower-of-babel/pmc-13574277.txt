Normal neuroimaging in an infant with SUCLA2-related mitochondrial DNA depletion syndrome type 5: a case report of likely pathogenic variant
Authors: Mohammad Obada Alsadi, Youssef Heikal, Farah Al-Hasani, Dana Al Khasawneh, Aia Nasab, Heba Hrizat, Fatema Almoustafa, Barah Hussain
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMitochondrial DNA depletion syndrome type 5 (MTDPS5), caused by SUCLA2 mutations, is a rare autosomal recessive disorder manifesting as early-onset encephalomyopathy. Clinical diagnosis is challenging due to phenotypic overlap and potentially unremarkable early investigations. We report a male infant of consanguineous parents who presented with progressive hypotonia, global developmental delay, and involuntary nocturnal movements at 3 months. Despite an unremarkable newborn metabolic screen and normal initial brain MRI, the patient demonstrated a significant failure to achieve age-appropriate gross motor milestones, including independent sitting and crawling, by 12 months. Whole-exome sequencing (WES) definitively identified a homozygous likely pathogenic SUCLA2 variant (p.Met329Val). Following the initiation of a mitochondrial cocktail, the patient showed substantial clinical improvement. This case emphasizes that MTDPS5 should be considered in infants with unexplained encephalomyopathy, even when initial findings are normal, underscoring the necessity of early WES to guide clinical management.

Introduction

Mitochondrial DNA depletion syndromes (MTDPS) are a spectrum of rare, autosomal recessive disorders characterized by a reduced mtDNA copy number in affected tissues, occurring independently of mitochondrial genome mutations [1]. As mtDNA encodes essential subunits of the mitochondrial respiratory chain, its depletion disrupts oxidative phosphorylation and cellular energy production, culminating in multisystem organ dysfunction [2]. These syndromes arise from pathogenic variants in several nuclear genes, including TK2, DGUOK, RRM2B, POLG, MPV17, MGME1, C10orf2, FBXL4, SUCLA2, and SUCLG1 [3].

Clinical manifestations are diverse, encompassing myopathic, cardiomyopathic, hepatocerebral, neurogastrointestinal, and encephalomyopathic forms [4].

Mitochondrial DNA depletion syndrome type 5 (MTDPS5), associated with SUCLA2 mutations, typically presents in early infancy with a progressive symptomatic stage characterized by hypotonia, psychomotor delay, and encephalomyopathy [3].

Specifically, SUCLA2 mutations—encoding the β-subunit of succinyl-CoA ligase—cause MTDPS5. This phenotype typically presents with early onset hypotonia, psychomotor delay, movement disorders (e.g. dystonia, chorea), skeletal deformities, sensorineural hearing loss, and characteristic laboratory findings, including lactic acidosis and elevated methylmalonic acid levels [2, 3]. Here, we report a male infant with SUCLA2-related mitochondrial DNA depletion syndrome type 5 (MTDPS5), who presented with early-onset generalized hypotonia, global developmental delay, and involuntary nocturnal movements, with the diagnosis definitively established via whole-exome sequencing.

Case presentation

A male infant was born to consanguineous parents following an uneventful gestation and cesarean section due to a previous cesarean delivery, with an uneventful neonatal period. A comprehensive newborn metabolic screening via Tandem Mass Spectrometry was performed at 12 days of age, during the pre-symptomatic stage. The screen, which included methylmalonic acidemia (MMA), was not remarkable.

The patient’s clinical course began at 3 months of age with the emergence of involuntary muscle contractions, which were initially misattributed to benign fasciculations. By 4 months of age, progressive hypotonia became evident. Due to the worsening hypotonia, neuroimaging via brain MRI was performed at 10 months of age, which remained unremarkable (Figs 1 and 2). Despite the lack of structural brain abnormalities, the patient demonstrated a significant failure to achieve age-appropriate gross motor milestones; most notably, he remained unable to attain independent sitting, perform purposeful crawling, or achieve the transition to a standing position through pulling-to-stand by the age of 12 months.

Initial axial T2-weighted brain MRI at 10 months of age. Three consecutive slices show unremarkable findings for infancy, with no pathological signal intensity within the basal ganglia or deep nuclei.

Initial axial T1-weighted brain MRI at 10 months of age. Three consecutive slices display normal gray-white matter differentiation and no evidence of cerebral atrophy or structural abnormalities.

Clinical examination confirmed severe generalized hypotonia and prominent muscle fasciculations. Laboratory findings—including lactic acidosis of 4.63 mmol/L (Reference range: 0.5–2.2 mmol/L), elevated transaminases (AST) of 69 U/L (Reference range: up to 40 U/L), and microcytic anemia with a hemoglobin (Hb) level of 9.7 g/dL (Reference range: 11.0–14.0 g/dL) and a mean corpuscular volume (MCV) of 66.4 fL (Reference range: 68.0–85.0 fL)—suggested a multisystemic metabolic disorder. Diagnosis was definitively confirmed via Whole Exome Sequencing (WES) at the 16 months of age, revealing a homozygous likely pathogenic variant in the SUCLA2 gene (p. Met329Val), consistent with mitochondrial DNA Depletion Syndrome type 5. The patient was subsequently initiated on a supportive mitochondrial cocktail, including Coenzyme Q10, L-Carnitine. Following the initiation of a mitochondrial cocktail, the patient showed significant clinical improvement. The nocturnal involuntary movements have completely resolved. Notably, the patient achieved independent ambulation, although fatigability becomes evident toward the end of the day.

Discussion

Mitochondrial DNA depletion syndrome type 5 (MTDPS5) is a rare autosomal recessive disorder arising from biallelic SUCLA2 mutations, which encode the beta-subunit of the ADP-forming succinyl-CoA ligase. This enzyme is essential for both the tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle and mtDNA integrity via interaction with mitochondrial nucleoside diphosphate kinase. Pathogenic SUCLA2 variants disrupt mitochondrial nucleotide synthesis, leading to mtDNA depletion, impaired oxidative phosphorylation, and a multisystemic phenotype including hypotonia, muscle atrophy, dystonia, sensorineural hearing loss, and postnatal growth retardation. Characteristic neuroimaging findings—basal ganglia involvement and cerebral atrophy—often overlap with Leigh syndrome criteria [2, 3, 5–7].

While globally rare, SUCLA2-related MTDPS5 is prevalent in the Faroe Islands due to a founder effect [5, 6]. Although our patient lacks any ancestral connection to this geographic region, the parental consanguinity serves as a critical genetic contributor in this case Prognosis is genotype-dependent; missense mutations, such as the patient’s p.Met329Val variant, are generally associated with better outcomes than loss-of-function mutations [6]. The expanding mutational landscape of SUCLA2 underscores the necessity of incorporating early whole-exome sequencing (WES) into the diagnostic workup for infants presenting with unexplained encephalomyopathy [3].

The mitochondrial enzyme succinyl-CoA ligase (SUCL) is encoded by the SUCLA2 and SUCLG1 genes and plays a dual role in the TCA cycle and mtDNA maintenance. Structurally, SUCL is a heterodimer featuring an α-subunit (from SUCLG1) and a β-subunit (from SUCLA2 or SUCLG2). Beyond its metabolic function in ATP/GTP production, SUCL stabilizes mitochondrial nucleoside diphosphate kinase. Consequently, any functional impairment in this enzyme disrupts nucleotide synthesis, leading to the clinical hallmark of mtDNA depletion [2]. Thus, the molecular findings in our patient support the previously described role of SUCLA2 dysfunction in mitochondrial DNA maintenance and energy metabolism.

As demonstrated by Reuter et al. [8] in a large cohort from the Middle East, consanguinity significantly increases the risk of autosomal recessive neurodevelopmental disorders [8]. This provides a robust genetic explanation for the manifestation of the rare SUCLA2 homozygous mutation in our patient, who was born to consanguineous parents.

The clinical spectrum of MTDPS5 typically involves severe encephalopathy, generalized dystonia, and metabolic derangements, such as elevated lactate and methylmalonic acid [3]. Our patient’s presentation aligns with these classic features, characterized by profound global developmental delay and a failure to achieve motor milestones, including head control, by 12 months. Notably, the persistent myoclonic movements observed in early infancy—initially misidentified as benign neonatal myoclonus—underscore the diagnostic challenges in differentiating early-onset mitochondrial encephalomyopathy from transient neonatal conditions.

Therapeutically, no curative options are available for SUCLA2-related mitochondrial DNA depletion syndrome. Current management is supportive, with the use of a mitochondrial cocktail—often composed of coenzyme Q10 and L-carnitine—which is widely accepted despite limited trial data [9]. In alignment with this standard, we initiated the cocktail for our patient, along with close follow-up and multidisciplinary support to optimize care.

The diagnosis of mitochondrial DNA depletion syndrome type 5 requires careful integration of biochemical and genetic findings. Lactic acidosis remains a hallmark indicator, reflecting impaired oxidative phosphorylation and mitochondrial dysfunction [7], in the context of our patient, the combination of marked lactic acidosis alongside elevated transaminases prompted a deeper investigation.

Notably, despite suggestive biochemical markers, our patient’s initial neuroimaging was unremarkable. This absence of characteristic findings poses a significant diagnostic challenge and may mislead clinical judgment. As documented in the multicenter study by Carrozzo et al. [6], a normal brain MRI—particularly during the early stages of the disease—does not preclude a diagnosis of MTDPS5, reinforcing the need for prompt genetic confirmation when clinical suspicion is high.

Ultimately, Whole Exome Sequencing (WES) provides a definitive diagnosis by identifying pathogenic SUCLA2 variants, thereby precluding the need for invasive muscle biopsies. Consistent with established diagnostic protocols [10], WES in our patient identified a homozygous likely pathogenic SUCLA2 variant according to the ACMG guidelines. This classification is supported by the study of Huang et al. [11], which was instrumental in establishing a definitive molecular diagnosis. A limitation of this report is the lack of repeated MMA measurement during the symptomatic phase. The diagnostic process relied on the initial normal newborn screen, which proved misleading as metabolic markers in MTDPS5 may remain dormant in the early neonatal period. This underscores that a normal early screen should not rule out the diagnosis.

Conclusion

In conclusion, SUCLA2-related MTDPS5 is a complex multisystemic disorder that necessitates a high index of clinical suspicion. Our findings confirm that a normal newborn metabolic screen and the absence of early neuroimaging abnormalities do not exclude the diagnosis of mitochondrial depletion syndromes. The reliance on WES as a primary diagnostic tool is essential for definitive confirmation and the timely implementation of supportive therapies. Although management remains symptomatic, our patient’s clinical improvement suggests that early intervention with a mitochondrial cocktail may influence the disease course. Further research is required to evaluate the long-term prognosis and therapeutic efficacy of such interventions in patients with SUCLA2 variants.
