Influencing factors analysis of asymmetry in knee adduction moment among patients with unilateral knee osteoarthritis
Authors: Yongjie Li, Runxin Luo, Shuwen Luo, Mengling Liu, Hongju Liu
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe knee adduction moment(KAM) of both lower limbs in patients with unilateral knee osteoarthritis(KOA) exhibits asymmetry during walking, but the factors influencing this asymmetry remain unclear. This study aimed to explore the influencing factors of KAM asymmetry in patients with unilateral KOA.MethodsA total of 148 patients with unilateral medial compartment KOA were selected for this retrospective study, and general data such as gender, age, and duration of disease were collected. The hip-knee-ankle (HKA) angle, degree of pain, and knee-extension muscle strength on the affected side were assessed through radiographic outcomes, the visual analog scale(VAS), and the Biodex isokinetic system. The peak KAM of both lower limbs was analyzed using a BTS motion-capture system and force platform. The asymmetry index(ASI) of KAM was calculated, and the patients were further categorized into the KAM symmetry group(ASI value ≤ 10%) and the KAM asymmetry group(ASI value>10%).Binary logistic regression analysis was employed to analyze the factors influencing the asymmetry of KAM.Results90 patients were categorized into the KAM asymmetry group, representing 60.8% of the cohort. A significant difference in the ASI value of KAM was observed between the two groups. Correlation analysis identified nine factors, including sex, age, and BMI, that were positively correlated with the ASI value of KAM. In contrast, knee-extension muscle strength and per-capita monthly household income were negatively correlated with the ASI value of KAM. Regression analysis revealed that being female(OR = 1.752), older age(OR = 2.472), increased BMI(OR = 1.535), larger varus angle(OR = 3.965), higher VAS score(OR = 2.617), Kellgren-Lawrence(K-L) grade IV(OR = 4.474), history of knee joint trauma(OR = 5.684), and living in a rural location(OR = 1.554) increased the risk of KAM asymmetry. Conversely, increased knee-extension muscle strength(OR = 0.758) and a per-capita monthly household income of 3000 ~ 6000 yuan(OR = 0.814) decreased the risk of KAM asymmetry.ConclusionFemale gender, older age, increased BMI, larger varus angle, higher VAS score, K-L grade IV, history of knee joint trauma, and living in a rural location are identified as risk factors for KAM asymmetry. Conversely, increased knee-extension muscle strength and a per-capita monthly household income of 3000 ~ 6000 yuan serve as protective factors against this asymmetry.

Introduction

The knee, as the largest weight-bearing joint in the human body, is particularly susceptible to degenerative pathological changes, leading to knee osteoarthritis (KOA) [1]. KOA is a significant cause of chronic pain and disability, imposing a substantial economic burden on families and society [2]. Typically, KOA begins in one lower limb, with approximately 80% of patients progressing from unilateral to bilateral KOA over the next 12 years [3]. Bilateral KOA patients tend to have a poorer prognosis, closely associated with more severe dysfunction and pain [4]. Thus, preventing the transition from unilateral to bilateral KOA is crucial.

The pathogenesis of KOA is multifaceted, with biomechanical factors currently being a major focus of research. It is widely accepted that KOA predominantly occurs in the medial compartment because the force line in patients with KOA tends to shift toward the medial side [5]. This shift increases the load on the medial compartment, thereby accelerating the development of the medial compartment KOA. Additionally, altered loads on the medial compartment may exacerbate clinical symptoms such as joint pain, stiffness, and unsteadiness, thus prompting KOA patients to adopt compensatory walking strategies [6, 7]. These strategies may further lead to the emergence of abnormal gait patterns [8]. The changes in medial compartment loading can be assessed through the knee adduction moment (KAM), which is considered a proxy measure rather than a direct measure [9]. Therefore, while there are some disadvantages to using the KAM, it provides a non-invasive, highly repeatable, and relatively accessible method for estimating medial compartment loads [10]. Previous studies [11–13]have indicated that the KAM in patients with KOA was significantly higher than that of the healthy subjects. Wang et al. [14] quantified the difference in KAM between both lower limbs in healthy controls and KOA patients using the Asymmetry Index (ASI) and found that the ASI value of KAM in KOA patients was significantly higher than that of healthy controls, suggesting that the ASI value of KAM is an effective indicator of asymmetry in medial compartment loading between both lower limbs. Despite these insights, no studies have yet explored the factors influencing the occurrence of KAM asymmetry in KOA patients. Thus, this study used KAM asymmetry as the dependent variable, categorizing patients into KAM symmetry and asymmetry groups based on ASI values, to investigate potential influencing factors.

Methods

Sample size

According to the sample size estimation method in logistic regression analysis as outlined by some previous studies [15–17], the total sample size should be at least 10 times the number of independent variables(10 events per variable). In this study, with 13 independent variables, the minimum required sample size was 130 cases. Taking into account a 10% sample dropout rate, the total sample size should be at least 143 cases.

Participants

Patients attending the outpatient clinic of the department of rehabilitation medicine from June 2022 to December 2023 were selected for this retrospective study. Inclusion criteria: (1)Diagnosis of unilateral KOA according to the American College of Rheumatology criteria [18]; (2) Kellgren-Lawrence (K-L) classification of ≥ 1; (3)Age between 45 and 75 years, with no restrictions based on gender; (4) Imaging examination confirming that only the medial compartment of the knee joint was affected and degenerated [14]; (5)No significant cognitive or consciousness disorders. Exclusion Criteria: (1) Unable to perform gait test due to severe knee pain; (2) History of knee surgery; (3) Recent or unhealed fracture of the lower limb; (4) Presence of severe cardiovascular, psychiatric, metabolic, or immune system diseases.

A total of 154 patients with KOA were collected according to the inclusion and exclusion criteria, while 6 patients were excluded due to incomplete data, so 148 patients were finally included. The study was approved by the Hospital Ethics Committee (approval number: LW20231016-2).In addition, informed consent was obtained from all the subjects.

Measures

General clinical data

General clinical data of KOA patients were collected, including sex (male/female), age, body mass index(BMI), duration of KOA, affected knee(left/right), K-L grade (mild: I-II grade; moderate: III grade; severe: IV grade) [14], history of knee joint trauma, education years, per capita monthly household income (< 3000 yuan, 3000 ~ 6000 yuan and > 6000 yuan) and residential location (urban/rural).

Pain intensity

The level of knee pain on the affected side in patients was assessed using the Visual Analogue Scale (VAS), where “0” indicated no pain and “10” represented severe pain [19]. Higher scores on this scale correspond to higher levels of pain.

Hip-knee-ankle (HKA) angle

The hip-knee-ankle (HKA) angle was measured from full-length weight-bearing X-ray films of both lower limbs in KOA patients, with malalignment defined as an HKA angle of 2° or more in the direction of varus (negative) or valgus (positive) [20, 21].

Knee-extension muscle strength measurement

Knee-extension muscle strength on the affected side was assessed using the Biodex isokinetic system. Patients were seated with their bodies naturally relaxed, and their torso, pelvis, and thigh were secured with straps. The isokinetic concentric contraction mode was used at an angular velocity of 60°/s, with the knee range of motion adjusted to each individual’s situation. Patients were instructed to perform five repetitive movements of knee extension at maximal force. The average peak torque from these five movements was recorded as the knee-extension muscle strength, measured in Nm.

Gait measurement

The gait measurement was conducted in the biomechanics laboratory, as depicted in Fig. 1A. The laboratory was equipped with a BTS motion-capture system, which includes 8 optical infrared cameras (BTS SMART DX4000, Milan, Italy; sampling rate: 250 Hz [22]), and 4 force platforms (BTS P-6000, Milan, Italy; sampling rate: 500 Hz [22]).These force platforms were positioned in the middle of a 6-meter walkway. Kinematic and dynamic data could be collected simultaneously.

Before the formal testing commenced, 22 spherical retroreflective markers were adhered to the skin surface of the patients following the “Helen Hayes with Medial Markers” protocol [23–25]. The specific locations and designations of these 22 markers are detailed in Fig. 1B and C. During the actual testing phase, kinematic and kinetic data were first collected from patients in a static standing position. Subsequently, patients were asked to walk along a walkway at their normal gait speed three times, ensuring that more than five gait cycles were recorded each time.

Data analysis was conducted using Opensim 4.1 software (Stanford University, USA). The Gait2392_Simbody model was loaded and scaled by importing the static standing kinematics data to tailor the model to the body dimensions of the KOA patients [26]. Walking kinematics and dynamics data were then imported to conduct inverse kinematics and dynamics calculations [27], respectively, allowing for the determination of the peak knee adduction moment (KAM) of both lower limbs.

In this study, the ASI of KAM in KOA patients was calculated using the ASI formula. Previous research [29–31] has suggested using a 10% ASI value as a threshold to identify asymmetry in both lower limbs during walking and exercise. Therefore, we categorized KOA patients into two groups based on the ASI of KAM: the KAM symmetry group (ASI value ≤ 10%) and the KAM asymmetry group (ASI value > 10%).

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS software (Version 23.0, IBM, USA). The study included 13 independent variables: sex, age, BMI, duration of KOA, affected knee, K-L grade, history of knee joint trauma, education years, per capita monthly household income, residential location, pain intensity, HKA angle, and knee-extension muscle strength. Univariate analysis [32, 33] was used to identify the variables differences between the KAM symmetry group (ASI value ≤ 10%) and the KAM asymmetry group (ASI value > 10%). Continuous variables adhering to a normal distribution were presented as mean ± standard deviation. Comparisons between and within groups were performed using the Independent-samples t-test and Paired-samples t-test, respectively. For continuous variables that did not follow a normal distribution, they were expressed as medians and interquartile ranges. Between-group and within-group comparisons for these variables were conducted using the Wilcoxon test or the Mann-Whitney test, as appropriate. Categorical variables were expressed as percentages, and comparisons between groups were made using the Chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test. The correlation between the ASI value of KAM and each independent variable was analyzed using either Pearson’s or Spearman’s correlation analysis [34]. Binary logistic regression analysis was then used to explore the influencing factors of KAM asymmetry. With the ASI of KAM as the dependent variable, the KAM symmetry group and the KAM asymmetry group were assigned values of 0 and 1, respectively. Statistically significant independent variables identified in univariate and correlation analyses were included together in the binary logistic regression model for analysis [35, 36].

Binary logistic regression analysis

Table 3 presents the results of a binary logistic regression analysis. The analysis showed that females had a higher risk of developing asymmetry of KAM compared to males [OR = 1.752, 95% CI: (1.245, 2.682)]. Additionally, increases in age, BMI, VAS score, and HKA (varus) were associated with elevated risks, with respective odds ratios (ORs) of 2.472, 1.535, 2.617, and 3.965 (P < 0.05). Patients with severe KOA (K-L grade IV) exhibited a higher risk compared to those with mild KOA (K-L grade I-II) [OR = 4.474, 95% CI: (1.577, 8.451)]. A history of knee joint trauma significantly increased the risk [OR = 5.684, 95% CI: (3.495, 9.874)], and residing in a rural location was associated with a higher risk than living in an urban location [OR = 1.554, 95% CI: (1.289, 2.263)]. Besides, increased muscle strength on the affected side and a per-capita monthly household income of 3000 ~ 6000 yuan acted as protective factors, with ORs of 0.758 and 0.814, respectively (P < 0.05).

Discussion

Under normal physiological conditions, gait is a coordinated and symmetrical activity. However, patients with KOA often adopt compensatory strategies during walking to minimize weight-bearing and pain on the affected side, which can lead to the development of gait asymmetry [37]. While gait asymmetry can be assessed through kinematics, kinetics, and surface electromyography, it is crucial to note that the primary factor in the development of KOA is a change in the mechanical environment of the medial compartment of the knee joint [38]. Therefore, calculating the asymmetry of medial compartment loading in both lower limbs may provide a more effective reflection of gait asymmetry in patients with KOA. KAM is widely regarded as an indicative measure of medial compartment loading due to its good repeatability, garnering considerable attention in both national and international studies [39]. In this study, we calculated the ASI value of KAM in KOA patients and found that 90 patients (60.8% of the cohort) exhibited an ASI value of KAM greater than 10%. Previous studies [29–31] have suggested using a 10% ASI value as a threshold to recognize limb asymmetry during walking and exercise. Our findings indicated a higher incidence of KAM asymmetry among KOA patients. However, as previous biomechanical studies have not documented the incidence of KAM asymmetry in KOA patients, the results of this study require further validation.

To further investigate the factors contributing to the occurrence of asymmetry in KAM, this study employed correlation analysis and binary logistic regression analysis. The correlation analysis revealed that nine factors, including sex, age, and BMI, were positively correlated with the ASI value of KAM. In contrast, knee-extension muscle strength and per-capita monthly household income were negatively correlated with the ASI value of KAM. The binary logistic regression analysis identified several significant predictors for the occurrence of KAM asymmetry in patients with unilateral KOA. These predictors included sex, age, BMI, VAS score, HKA angle (varus), K-L grade, history of knee joint trauma, knee extension muscle strength on the affected side, per capita monthly household income, and residential location. In terms of HKA angle, the HKA angle for the KAM symmetry and asymmetry groups were − 3.16 and − 6.97, respectively, indicating varus malalignment in both groups, with a more pronounced varus in the asymmetry group. The regression analysis suggested that an increasing varus angle significantly elevates the risk of developing KAM asymmetry, affirming that varus malalignment is a critical risk factor. Similar findings by Maneekittichot et al. [40] supported these results, highlighting that greater knee varus angles correlated with higher peak KAM during walking. Furthermore, the analysis revealed that females are at a higher risk of KAM asymmetry compared to males, potentially due to lower knee extension muscle strength and weaker ligaments around the knee joint in women. These biomechanical disadvantages may predispose the knee to varus alignment, thereby increasing the load on the medial compartment of the affected side [5].

Increases in age, BMI, K-L grade, and VAS score were associated with an elevated risk of developing asymmetry in the KAM. These factors likely contribute to knee varus malalignment in patients with KOA, which not only accelerates the degeneration of articular cartilage but also impairs knee function and range of motion [13, 41–45]. Such changes can affect the asymmetry of KAM and compromise gait stability. Moreover, a history of knee joint trauma and living in a rural location were identified as additional risk factors for asymmetry of KAM. Previous research [46–49] has highlighted knee joint trauma as a pivotal factor in the development of KOA and a common cause of knee varus malalignment. This trauma can lead to structural and functional changes in the knee joint, such as decreased joint mobility, and shift the lower limb force line toward the medial compartment. Consequently, this shift increases the varus angle during walking, resulting in significant discrepancies in the KAM between the lower limbs. Regarding residential location, KOA patients in rural areas often engage in more labor-intensive activities compared to those in urban settings. This results in prolonged exposure to excessive loads on the knee joints, leading to more severe articular cartilage wear and subsequent alterations in the knee varus angle [50, 51].

It is noteworthy that this study identified increased knee-extension muscle strength and a per-capita monthly household income of 3000 ~ 6000 yuan as protective factors against the asymmetry of the KAM. This may be attributed to the fact that strengthening the knee-extension muscles on the affected side in patients with KOA is beneficial for promoting knee joint stability and slowing the rate of degeneration [9, 52]. Furthermore, KOA patients with a per-capita monthly income within this range typically have sufficient financial resources, which are associated with better healthcare coverage and heightened health awareness. Consequently, these individuals are more likely to seek timely medical care, facilitating early treatment and reducing the risk of developing gait asymmetry [53].

Clinical implications and limitations of the study

KAM has significant predictive value for the progression and severity of KOA [54]. Studies [55, 56] have demonstrated that patients with unilateral KOA often exhibit KAM asymmetry in both lower limbs (as evidenced by our findings), leading to a shift of load from the affected to the contralateral side, thereby increasing the risk of osteoarthritis in the contralateral knee. Metcalfe et al. [3] noted that 80% of such patients progress to bilateral KOA within 12 years. According to the above, KAM asymmetry may play a crucial role in the progression from unilateral to bilateral knee osteoarthritis. Our study identified variables such as knee varus angle, pain, and BMI as influencing factors in KAM asymmetry, consistent with previous research [57–59] linking these factors to KAM changes during KOA progression. Notably, past studies [57–59] often focused solely on the affected lower limb, neglecting the potential impact of the contralateral limb, thus limiting their clinical utility to mitigating unilateral KOA progression. Addressing these gaps, our study analyzed both the affected and contralateral sides in patients with unilateral KOA, quantifying KAM asymmetry using the ASI formula. Our findings indicate an elevated risk of KAM asymmetry in patients with unilateral KOA when influenced by factors such as knee varus angle, pain, and BMI. Given the predictive significance of KAM asymmetry, the important clinical implication of our findings is that interventions targeting these factors could be crucial in preventing the progression of KOA from unilateral to bilateral. Furthermore, our study highlights a substantial prevalence of KAM asymmetry (60.8%) in the unilateral KOA population, underscoring the importance of addressing this issue in clinical settings. This prevalence serves as a reminder for healthcare professionals to consider the condition of the contralateral knee and to devise treatment plans that promote symmetry in medial compartment loading across both knees.

The study has several limitations that warrant consideration: (1) Although the sample size was initially estimated for this study, the final sample size remained relatively small; (2) The results presented are derived from data collected in this retrospective study, and as such, definitive judgments regarding causality cannot be made. It is suggested that the factors contributing to the asymmetry of KAM be further explored through longitudinal studies in the future; (3) This study did not include serum marker indicators, such as those related to inflammation and bone metabolism, which is a gap that needs to be addressed in future research.

Conclusion

In summary, this study revealed a higher incidence of asymmetry in the KAM among patients with KOA. Factors contributing to this asymmetry included being female, older age, increased BMI, larger varus angle, higher VAS score, K-L grade IV, a history of knee joint trauma, and residing in a rural location. Conversely, increased knee-extension muscle strength and a per-capita monthly household income of 3000 ~ 6000 yuan were identified as protective factors against the asymmetry of KAM.

These independent variables and KAM asymmetry perhaps can be used in an algorithm to better predict disease progression and those at greater risk of developing asymmetry in the future research.
