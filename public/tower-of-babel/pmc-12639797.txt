Gut microbiome restoring biogenic ferritin mineral as an effective oral iron supplement for iron deficiency anemia
Authors: Yingchun Yang, Luqiu Yang, Rong Yang, Fangqing Deng, Qing Tian, Zhaoxiang Yu, Lianbing Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractTraditional iron supplements for iron deficiency anemia (IDA) have several limitations, including suboptimal absorption, gastrointestinal discomfort, and concerns regarding long-term safety. In this study, we synthesized monodisperse 6-line ferrihydrite as an innovative iron supplement, featuring a structure analogous to the natural “iron core” present in ferritin. Notably, 6-line ferrihydrite (6-Fh) was internalized by Caco-2 cells via a process independent of divalent metal transporter 1 (DMT1). Additionally, its stability as nanoparticles within the gastrointestinal tract prevents the irritation typically associated with metal ions, thereby significantly enhancing iron utilization efficiency. Consequently, compared to FeSO₄ (a traditional oral iron supplement), 6-Fh effectively stabilized cellular free iron with low ROS toxicity and high biosafety, thereby effectively maintaining intestinal barrier integrity and avoiding liver injury. Furthermore, it restored intestinal dysbiosis caused by IDA and increased the proportion of beneficial bacterial strains, which was superior to FeSO₄. Introduction

The prevalence of iron deficiency and its bioavailability in numerous diets significantly contribute to the onset of iron deficiency anemia (IDA) [1, 2]. FeSO4 is the preferred oral iron supplement owing to its ease of administration and high patient compliance. Although these salts can rapidly replenish iron levels in deficient cells, their potential to induce considerable gastrointestinal side effects, including oxidative damage to the intestinal epithelium and discomfort from increased ferrous ions in the intestine, should not be underestimated [3–5]. Natural ferritins from both animal and plant sources have been investigated for iron supplementation [6]. However, animal ferritin is produced at low yields and is less frequently used. Legumes, such as peas, contain low concentrations of ferritin, and their protein shells must withstand degradation during dietary absorption [7–9], a process limited by the harsh gastric acid environment [8, 10].

In recent years, iron oxide nanoparticles (IONPs) have emerged as promising alternatives to conventional treatments for iron deficiency anemia (IDA) [11–14]. However, a significant challenge associated with IONPs is their hydrophobic nature and susceptibility to oxidation upon exposure to air. Furthermore, excessive release of bioactive iron poses a risk of oxidative damage to the intestinal tract. To mitigate these challenges, auxiliary coatings such as polyglucose-sorbitol-carboxymethyl ether (PSC) and polyethylene glycol (PEG) have been employed to enhance the stability, safety, and intestinal absorption of these nanoparticles [15–19]. Conversely, certain iron supplements require intravenous administration, which carries the risk of oxidative stress and infection [15, 16, 20]. At that time, a “nanoparticulate ferritin core mimetic,” specifically an unstable 2-line ferrihydrite, was used as an oral iron supplement [21]. However, this form of ferrihydrite can be digested in the small intestine, resulting in the release of free Fe3+. Although ferric iron is generally considered safer and better tolerated in the intestine, its absorption is limited [22]. It must be reduced to ferrous iron by cytochrome B (DCYTB) and requires divalent metal transporter 1 (DMT1) for absorption by intestinal cells [23, 24]. Consequently, individuals with inherited DMT1 mutations may experience diminished efficacy of iron supplementation [25].

When used as oral iron supplements, the digestive behavior of nanoparticles (NPs) is affected by their crystallinity [26]. Expanding on the “ferritin core mimetic” concept, we synthesized a 6-line ferrihydrite (6-Fh) with slightly enhanced crystallinity compared to the 2-line variant, employing a hydrothermal method. 6-Fh exhibits an excellent iron supplementation effect and maintains safety even at high doses, a characteristic not matched by conventional FeSO4 supplements [27]. The detailed mechanism is illustrated in Scheme 1. Upon oral administration, 6-Fh retains its nanoparticle form throughout intestinal absorption and transport and is internalized by intestinal epithelial cells via endocytosis, which operates independently of DMT1. Following internalization, lysosomal digestion of 6-Fh releases soluble iron in a bioavailable form. This controlled absorption pathway prevents oxidative damage caused by a transient increase in intracellular Fe²⁺ levels. Even when used in large amounts, excess iron is stored in ferritin following its controlled release through lysosomes, thereby maintaining the stability of the labile iron pool (LIP). This process avoided the overproduction of ROS in intestinal cells and prevented oxidative damage to intestinal tight junction proteins, thereby preserving both their abundance and structural integrity, which in turn protected the intestinal barrier. Concurrently, 6-Fh mitigated the intestinal microbiome imbalance caused by iron deficiency in IDA mice and increased the levels of intestinal probiotics. These findings demonstrate the significant potential of 6-Fh for iron supplementation.

Results and discussion

Synthesis and optimization of 6-Fh

To determine the most suitable iron source for the synthesis of 6-line ferrihydrite (6-Fh), we assessed several commonly used ferric iron salts (Fig. 1A). The reaction results showed that using water as the reaction solvent posed challenges in dissolving ferric citrate and ferric acetate, resulting in a suspension. Consequently, these iron salts are unsuitable as raw materials for the synthesis of biomimetic hydrous ferric oxides. In contrast, when ferric chloride and ferric nitrate nonahydrate were employed, the solution exhibited a pronounced Tyndall effect, indicating the successful formation of relatively uniform nanoparticles. In contrast, the solution derived from ferrous sulfate did not exhibit the Tyndall effect. Therefore, we initially selected ferric chloride and iron nitrate as the iron salts for further characterization of their products. Figure 1B(i) shows that the nanomaterials synthesized from ferric chloride do not correspond to the PDF standard card for ferrihydrite nanoparticles. Instead, they resembled hematite. Despite varying the temperature conditions that affected the hydrolysis and crystallinity of ferric ions, we were unable to produce nanoparticles that conformed to the required ferrihydrite [28]. Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) revealed a mixture of spherical particles and rods. When ferric nitrate nonahydrate was used as the iron source, the resulting product matched the ferrihydrite PDF specifications (Fig. 1B(ii)). To optimize the synthesis for a more effective iron supplement, we examined the influence of the synthesis temperature on product quality. An increase in the synthesis temperature resulted in a reduction in the sharpness of the X-ray diffraction (XRD) peaks. Nevertheless, six peaks were consistently observed and aligned with the standard card for 6-line ferrihydrite (6-Fh). Meanwhile, the TEM images demonstrate a highly dispersed state of particles with a uniform size of approximately 8 nm. These findings corroborate our previous research, which established that ferrihydrite nanoparticles with high dispersion and water solubility can be synthesized through the heating and hydrolysis of ferric nitrate nonahydrate [29]. In addition, we analyzed the XPS spectra of the nanoparticles to determine their Fe valence states (Fig. 1C). The binding energies at 724.8 and 711.2 eV were attributed to Fe 2p1/2 and Fe 2p3/2, respectively, indicating that the surface of 6-Fh is predominantly composed of ferric iron. Because the size and distribution of nanoparticles significantly affect their absorption and utilization within the intestinal tract [30, 31], the size and distribution of 6-Fh were evaluated at various synthesis temperatures to optimize its use as an effective iron source. Figure 1D shows that the average particle size of 6-Fh synthesized between 65 and 75 °C was centered at 8 nm. Upon increasing the temperature to 80–85 °C, the average particle size decreased to ~ 5 nm. The particle sizes synthesized under several temperature conditions were less than 10 nm, suggesting their promising applications in biological systems. Concurrently, the yield of 6-Fh steadily increased with increasing synthesis temperature. Thus, considering the size distribution, yield, and energy consumption, 75 °C was identified as the optimal synthesis temperature. The aqueous solution of 6-Fh without auxiliary coatings, which exhibited a positive charge, was highly homogeneous and stable, resembling natural horse spleen ferritin (hsFt) (Fig. 1E).

The excellent stability of 6-Fh

6-Fh demonstrated substantial stability at ambient temperature for at least six months (Fig. 2A), which is advantageous for pharmaceutical applications. The ability to resist gastric digestion and reach the small intestine is crucial during oral iron supplementation. To test this hypothesis, we developed in vitro models to simulate the digestive effects of 6-Fh. Utilizing the differential UV absorption of iron ions and nanoparticles at equimolar iron concentrations, we quantified the structural integrity of 6-Fh in simulated gastric fluid (SGF). The optical density at 420 nm (OD420) was monitored to observe changes in the UV-vis absorption spectrum of 6-Fh within the SGF over a two-hour period, corresponding to the gastric emptying time, revealing only a minor decrease (Fig. 2B). Furthermore, 6-Fh was introduced into a 2 M HCl solution, which is significantly more acidic than the SGF, to ensure complete cleavage of the nanoparticles into an ionic state. Compared with the SGF, the OD420 in the hydrochloric acid environment exhibited a significant decrease over time (Fig. 2C), reaching the lowest value after 40 min. These findings indicate that biomimetic 6-Fh experienced minimal degradation in the SGF, suggesting that most nanoparticles could transit intact to the intestinal tract. Figure 2D illustrates the superior dispersion of 6-Fh in simulated intestinal fluid (SIF), with no significant aggregation or release of labile free iron ions within 12 h, which is a key indicator of the safety and performance of oral iron supplementation. Enhanced dispersion is indicative of effective absorption and transport, suggesting that 6-Fh maintains its dispersibility and reaches its absorption site in the small intestine in vivo. In contrast, the 2-line ferrihydrite (2-Fh), synthesized in our laboratory, exhibited noticeable yellow insoluble matter within 0.5 h in SIF, consistent with previous findings (Fig. 2E) [21]. These findings indicate that 6-Fh maintains its stability and solubility throughout gastric emptying and intestinal absorption. Furthermore, we evaluated the stability of 6-Fh in the lysosomal environment to assess its capacity to release free Fe ions (Fig. 2F). UV-vis spectroscopy of the lysosomal simulation solution revealed that 6-Fh initially underwent degradation. However, the OD420 value gradually decreased with continued treatment, suggesting a controlled ion release. Simultaneously, the system retained a light-yellow color without any insoluble residues (inset of Fig. 2F). In this context, 6-Fh demonstrates enhanced biosafety compared with traditional iron salts, potentially mitigating the adverse reactions associated with a sudden increase in free iron levels.

DMT1-independent uptake and low cytotoxicity of 6-Fh

At the organismal level, the intestinal wall plays a crucial role in systemic iron absorption during oral administration, with DMT1 facilitating cellular Fe²⁺ absorption and FPN regulating cellular iron release [23]. To examine the uptake of 6-Fh by intestinal cells, we investigated DMT1-mediated cellular iron absorption using a cell model. As iron uptake by Caco-2 cells is a reliable indicator of relative iron availability, this cell line was used to evaluate the iron absorption efficacy of 6-Fh. FeSO4, a standard iron ion supplement, was selected for a comparative evaluation with 6-Fh.

To simulate the absorption pattern of 6-Fh more accurately, an iron-deficient Caco-2 model was developed using iron chelators. Initial observations focused on alterations in total iron content and cellular iron-related proteins before and after iron supplementation (Fig. 3A). The total iron level within cells was effectively restored by 6-Fh supplementation, surpassing the efficacy of FeSO4 (Fig. 3B). This finding suggests that 6-Fh is efficiently absorbed by cells. Ferritin, an iron storage protein within cells, is composed of two types of subunits (H and L-chain). All ferritins readily interact with Fe to induce its oxidation and deposition in the cavity in a mineral form, in a reaction catalyzed by a ferroxidase center. Thus, to avoid the generation of free radicals via the Fenton reaction [32, 33]. Concurrently, cellular iron-related proteins were more effectively restored by 6-Fh supplementation (Fig. 3C). Ferritin (H-chain) exhibited normalized levels (as indicated by changes in the H-chain) following 6-Fh supplementation. In contrast, FeSO4 supplementation resulted in ferritin levels exceeding normal levels, potentially due to an influx of free iron ions, prompting the cellular storage of ferritin for future use. Additionally, both 6-Fh and FeSO4 supplementation significantly reduced elevated DMT1 levels, which is a compensatory response to iron deficiency. Incorporating DMT1 blockers during iron supplementation diminished the effects of FeSO4, whereas 6-Fh preserved its efficacy. These results indicate that 6-Fh is internalized via a DMT1-independent pathway. This mechanism is distinct from that of FeSO₄ and unstable 2-line ferrihydrite, leading to a significantly enhanced iron-uptake efficiency. These findings suggest that 6-Fh, unlike ferrous salt iron supplements, does not induce a short-term increase in intracellular ferrous ion levels or disrupt homeostasis. While restoring cellular iron storage protein levels, 6-Fh prevented excessive increases in protein levels, thereby ensuring biological safety. Elevated FPN expression was observed exclusively in the FeSO₄ group, further demonstrating 6-Fh’s ability to maintain intracellular iron homeostasis and ensure cellular safety. Furthermore, TEM images demonstrated that 6-Fh was internalized by intestinal cells via endocytosis and subsequently concentrated in lysosomes (Fig. 3D). Subsequently, due to the acidic environment of the lysosome, 6-Fh gradually degraded and released free iron.

Iron, an essential trace element for cellular growth, can be harmful when present in excess, particularly as free iron ions. Such excess can lead to the generation of substantial quantities of reactive oxygen species (ROS), which can cause oxidative stress and cellular damage [34]. Therefore, a comprehensive assessment of the biotoxicity of 6-Fh is crucial prior to its application in biological contexts. Initially, we established a range of 6-Fh concentration gradients to treat the cells, with FeSO4 serving as a control to detect LIP and ROS. Upon treatment with 6-Fh, cellular proliferation notably increased at a concentration of 0.8 mM (Fig. 3E), whereas LIP remained comparable to that of the control group (Fig. 3F). Correspondingly, the ROS levels remained unchanged (Fig. 3G). Although FeSO4 did not significantly impede cell proliferation (Fig. 3E), LIP and ROS levels progressively increased (Fig. 3F-G). This suggests that 6-Fh stabilizes cellular free iron with high biosafety. As illustrated in Fig. 3E, cell viability increased markedly with increasing concentrations of 6-Fh. Given that fetal bovine serum (FBS) is the primary nutritional source in cell culture, we subsequently transitioned the cells to an FBS-free medium (with or without 0.8 mM 6-Fh) to simulate nutritional stress, thereby further elucidating the role of 6-Fh in sustaining cellular viability. Owing to the reliance on culture medium during in vitro cultivation, the cells demonstrated significant alterations in growth kinetics over short durations. In the absence of 6-Fh, cell viability decreased with prolonged incubation (Fig. 3H), whereas the groups supplemented with 6-Fh did not exhibit significant proliferation inhibition, indicating that 6-Fh is not only non-toxic but also effective in preserving cellular activity under conditions of nutritional stress. This observation is consistent with prior research indicating the essential role of iron in biological systems [30, 31, 35].

Outstanding iron supplementation efficacy and biosafety of 6-Fh

To evaluate the effectiveness and in vivo safety of 6-Fh, we conducted experiments on iron-deficient anemic (IDA) mice, which were categorized into high- (H), moderate- (M), and low-dose (L) groups (Fig. 4A) [36]. The selection of dosage is based on the previously published work and a clinical study [36, 37]. During the model development phase, a significant reduction in body weight was observed across different groups of mice (Fig. 4B). In addition, the mice exhibited markedly reduced locomotor activity. Iron is a necessary element for the generation of red blood cells (RBC) and hemoglobin (HGB). Deficient iron levels can result in impaired HGB synthesis and a decrease in RBCs counts, as documented in previous studies [38, 39]. By the 40th day, the weight of the mice was significantly decreased compared to that of the control group, and the RBC and HGB levels fell below the normal range (6.5 × 1012/L and 110 g/L, respectively) (Fig. 4B and C), confirming the successful induction of IDA in the mouse models. Following iron supplementation, both high and medium doses of 6-Fh and FeSO4 (denoted as -H and -M, respectively) effectively restored RBCs and HGB levels that were diminished by IDA. However, under the low-dose regimen, the 6-Fh-L group showed favorable outcomes, whereas the FeSO4-L group did not demonstrate significant improvement.

Serum iron levels are indicative of systemic iron status, whereas the total iron-binding capacity (TIBC) measures the maximum iron-binding potential of transferrin in circulation, which is notably elevated during iron deficiency [40, 41]. Simultaneous assessment of serum iron levels and TIBC enhances the diagnostic accuracy of the clinical evaluation. In IDA mice, serum iron levels were reduced and TIBC levels were increased (Fig. 4D). Administration of 6-Fh successfully normalized the serum iron and TIBC levels in IDA mice at high (6-Fh-H), moderate (6-Fh-M), and low (6-Fh-L) dosages. FeSO4 was effective at high (FeSO4-H) and medium (FeSO4-M) dosages, whereas the efficacy of FeSO4-L was considerably lower than that of the aforementioned groups. Similarly, the serum iron levels in all groups demonstrated comparable patterns of variation.

The spleen and liver are integral for iron metabolism and storage. In instances of iron deficiency, reduced serum iron levels can trigger the release of iron from the spleen or hepatocytes to meet physiological needs [42, 43]. In mice with IDA, the iron content in the spleen and liver was significantly reduced (Fig. 4E). Upon iron supplementation, both 6-Fh-H and 6-Fh-M effectively restored the iron levels in these organs. Although 6-Fh-L, FeSO4-H, and FeSO4-M also contributed to iron restoration, their efficacy was inferior to that of 6-Fh-H and 6-Fh-M. Notably, the FeSO4-H group exhibited iron levels that surpassed those of the control group, potentially leading to oxidative tissue damage. Among the treatment groups, 6-Fh-M demonstrated superior efficacy in iron recovery compared with FeSO4-M. At a lower dose, 6-Fh-L exhibited a comparable effect on spleen iron recovery to both FeSO4-H and FeSO4-M, whereas FeSO4-L did not significantly enhance iron levels in the spleen or liver. Similarly, in the small intestine of the mice (Fig. 4F), we observed that DMT1 levels were significantly increased in the intestines of IDA mice, whereas FPN levels showed a slight decrease. 6-Fh effectively restored the levels of these proteins at both high and low doses (6-Fh-H and -L). During iron supplementation with FeSO4, partial restoration of both proteins was observed only in the FeSO4-M group. FeSO4-H induced an excessive increase in FPN levels, consistent with the results from the cell model (Fig. 3C). However, in the FeSO4-L group, the restorative effect on the expression of both proteins was not significant. These findings suggest that 6-Fh is more effective than FeSO4 for iron supplementation, with enhanced iron utilization at equivalent doses.

To further assess the biosafety of 6-Fh as an iron supplement for in vivo applications, we examined the intestinal health and liver injury in mice administered a high dose and compared it with that of mice administered a moderate dose. As iron absorption predominantly occurs in the duodenum [44], we focused on evaluating changes in this intestinal segment. As previously noted, excess iron induces ROS production. ROS function as signaling molecules that regulate cellular damage or directly cause tissue damage by oxidizing biological macromolecules. We initially detected changes in the intestinal ROS levels in mice treated with 6-Fh and FeSO₄ (Fig. 5A). Compared to the control, high-dose FeSO₄ (FeSO₄-H) induced a strong ROS response, whereas the 6-Fh-H group exhibited only a slight increase. Furthermore, FeSO₄ promoted additional ROS generation even at a moderate dose (FeSO₄-M), whereas 6-Fh-M demonstrated excellent biocompatibility. Subsequently, we conducted hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining of paraffin sections of the duodenum from mice in each group and calculated the villus height-to-crypt depth (VH/CD) ratio to assess intestinal morphological characteristics and growth rate [45]. The results indicated that no significant morphological changes were observed in the intestinal tract of mice in any group during the 6-Fh treatment, and the villus and crypt architectures remained intact (Fig. 5B). Moreover, 6-Fh-H effectively restored the VH/CD ratio reduced by IDA (Fig. 5C), indicating that 6-Fh exhibited good biosafety and promoted the growth of mice. In contrast, the mucosal layer was damaged in the intestines of mice in the IDA and FeSO₄-H iron supplementation groups (Fig. 5B). Inflammatory cell infiltration was observed in the intestinal basal layer, and the muscularis mucosa and submucosa were thinner.

To further evaluate the integrity of the intestinal barrier [46, 47], we performed AB-PAS staining to quantify the goblet cell content, thereby assessing the secretion of gut-associated proteins and polysaccharides (Fig. 6A). After iron supplementation, the 6-Fh-H, 6-Fh-M, and FeSO4-M groups exhibited a slight increase in the number of goblet cells within intestinal villi and crypts. In contrast, the FeSO4-H group demonstrated a substantial increase, indicating potential intestinal inflammation in this cohort. Tight junctions are essential components of the intestinal epithelial barrier and primarily comprise protein complexes formed by transmembrane, peripheral membrane, and cytoskeletal proteins. Occludin is a pivotal transmembrane protein that regulates intestinal barrier permeability and maintains cell polarity. Zonula occludens-1 (ZO-1) proteins, part of the peripheral membrane protein family, primarily stabilize the intestinal tight junction complex by linking Occludin, Claudin, and the cytoskeleton [48, 49]. Consequently, we selected ZO-1 and Occludin to further assess the intestinal barrier integrity in each group (Fig. 6B). Compared to the control group, the intestinal barrier of IDA mice was compromised, with a significantly reduced expression of both ZO-1 and Occludin. Following iron supplementation, ZO-1 expression was effectively restored in 6-Fh-H, 6-Fh-M, and FeSO₄-M groups. Notably, in the 6-Fh-H group, Occludin expression in the long villi also showed significant recovery, indicating the effective restoration of the intestinal barrier in these treatment groups. Conversely, in the FeSO₄-H group, the expression of both proteins was further reduced compared with that in the IDA group, suggesting that toxicity induced by excessive iron may exacerbate intestinal barrier damage. Beyond the intestinal system, following substantial iron intake, the liver, which is the primary iron storage site, is susceptible to iron toxicity and oxidative damage [50, 51]. Therefore, we assessed the markers of hepatic oxidative damage in each group of mice. Consistent with the results shown in Fig. 4E, the level of malondialdehyde (MDA), a lipid peroxidation product, was significantly increased in the FeSO4-H group (Fig. 6C), whereas the activity of glutathione peroxidase (GPX) (Fig. 6D), an antioxidant enzyme, decreased significantly. This indicated a disruption in the oxidative-antioxidant balance in the liver. However, supplementation with an appropriate dose of iron (FeSO4-M, 6-Fh-H, and 6-Fh-M) effectively restored the MDA and GPX levels that had been reduced by IDA.

6-Fh is a pivotal modulator of the gut microbiome

Iron is a critical component of the gut microbiome, exerting influence on host physiological processes through the complex “iron-microbe-gut immunity” axis [52–54]. Oral iron is predominantly absorbed and utilized by gut microbes within the colon [53]. To evaluate the effect of 6-Fh on the amelioration of intestinal dysbiosis caused by iron deficiency, we performed a cluster analysis of the diversity and structural composition of the intestinal flora across various mouse groups. Based on the analysis of the effects of iron supplementation in each group, we selected representative moderate concentration (M) and high concentration (H) groups for further gut microbiota analysis.

Among the prokaryotic microorganisms, the 16 S rRNA gene sequence demonstrates considerable diversity across species, rendering it a “molecular clock” for bacterial taxonomic studies [55]. The Rank-abundance curve illustrates both species abundance and evenness. Species abundance is indicated by the extent of the curve along the horizontal axis; a broader range on this axis signifies a greater species abundance. Species evenness is represented by the smoothness of the curve; a flatter curve indicates higher species evenness [56]. As shown in Fig. 7A, the transverse wavy lines of each group of samples were extensive and eventually flattened, suggesting a uniform and rich composition. In Fig. 7B, the number of Operational Taxonomic Units (OTUs) increased progressively with sequencing depth and eventually plateaued, indicating that the data volume was adequate. To examine the species composition of each sample, all the samples were subjected to OTU clustering and species annotation, resulting in a Venn diagram. As illustrated in Fig. 7C, sequencing of 16 S rRNA gene amplicons revealed significant changes in gut microbiome composition during iron supplementation [57]. There were 61 shared OTUs among all groups and 158 unique OTUs in the IDA group, indicating that IDA induced changes in OTU numbers compared to normal mice. Furthermore, the Alpha Diversity method was used to assess the microbial community diversity (Fig.7D-F). In comparison with the control group, both the Chao1 and Simpson indices decreased in the IDA group (Fig. 7D and E), indicating a reduction in the number of gut microbiota species and a declining trend in the diversity and distribution uniformity. Compared to the IDA group, the Simpson and Shannon indices increased in the iron supplementation groups, particularly in the 6-Fh-H group (Fig. 7E and F). These findings suggest that, although species richness diminished, evenness improved following iron supplementation, indicating a trend toward microbial community restructuring. Beta diversity, an index that measures differences in species diversity between distinct microbial communities, reveals structural differences in microbial communities between samples by comparing their species composition. To visualize these differences, Principal Coordinate Analysis (PCoA) and non-metric multidimensional scaling (NMDS) were used to reflect the differences between samples (Fig. 7G). In the PCoA and NMDS plots, the distance between samples reflected their similarity in composition and abundance; the shorter the distance, the more similar was the microbial community composition and species abundance. The IDA group was distinctly separated from both the control and iron-supplemented groups, indicating that IDA can alter the structure of gut microbiota in mice. Iron supplementation through the aforementioned methods can regulate the composition and diversity of gut microbiota in IDA mice, thereby ameliorating gut microbiota disorders. Moreover, the effect of 6-Fh-H was more effective than that of the FeSO₄ groups.

Using the species annotation results, the top 20 species exhibiting the highest abundance at the phylum level were selected from each group to construct a bar chart illustrating the relative abundance of species (Fig. 8A) [36]. The predominant bacterial phyla in the mouse intestine were Firmicutes, Bacteroidetes, and Proteobacteria. Compared to the normal control group, the relative abundance of Firmicutes was elevated in the IDA group, whereas the relative abundance of Bacteroidetes and Proteobacteria was reduced. Following iron supplementation, the proportion of these populations exhibited partial recovery. This was consistent with existing research, which indicates that some members of the Bacteroidetes phylum are specialists in iron metabolism, highly efficient at acquiring and utilizing iron resources within the host. Meanwhile, many Proteobacteria also possess highly efficient and diverse iron acquisition systems and proliferate rapidly under iron-replete conditions [58, 59]. The top 20 species exhibiting the highest abundance at the family level were selected from each group to construct a bar chart illustrating the relative abundance of the species (Fig. 8B) [36]. Compared with the control group, the family Erysipelotrichaceae, which is associated with enteritis [35], exhibited an abnormal increase in the IDA group. This proportion was partially recovered following iron supplementation, with the 6-Fh-H group demonstrating the most pronounced effect. S24-7, which constitutes the highest proportion (up to 30%) in healthy mouse intestines, serves as a marker of stable gut microbiota and plays a key role in polysaccharide degradation and immune regulation [60]. The abundance of S24-7 decreased in response to IDA and subsequently recovered after iron supplementation, with the most significant recovery observed in the 6-Fh-H group. Similarly, compared with the control group, the abundance of Lachnospiraceae decreased because of IDA and recovered following iron supplementation, with the 6-Fh-H group exhibiting the greatest recovery. Most members of the Lachnospiraceae family are capable of producing butyrate, which possesses anti-inflammatory properties, enhances the intestinal barrier function, and maintains immune homeostasis [61]. Additionally, after iron supplementation, the proportion of Bifidobacteriaceae increased in all the groups. This probiotic produces acetate and lactate, inhibits pathogens, activates dendritic cells, and induces immune tolerance [62].

Subsequently, a differential abundance analysis of bacterial taxa was conducted across various groups, employing an LDA score threshold of 2 at the genus level (Fig. 8C). Compared with the control group, the IDA group exhibited an increased abundance of Erysipelotrichi-related taxa, which is consistent with the findings from the species-level composition analysis. Following iron supplementation, an increase in the abundance of probiotics, such as those from the family Rikenellaceae (phylum Bacteroidetes), were observed. These probiotics are known to degrade complex polysaccharides, produce propionate, participate in energy metabolism, maintain homeostasis in hypoxic colonic environments, and inhibit pathogen colonization [63]. Furthermore, an increase in the abundance of Clostridiales (phylum Firmicutes) was also observed. These bacteria produce butyrate, which is capable of enhancing intestinal barrier function and exhibits anti-inflammatory properties [64, 65]. This was consistent with the results in Fig. 7B, demonstrating that iron supplementation led to a partial recovery of intestinal barrier function in mice compared to IDA condition.

Conclusion

The 6-Fh synthesized in this study exhibited a significant iron supplementation effect independent of DMT1, and was free from discernible gastrointestinal side effects compared to FeSO4. 6-Fh maintained structural stability in both SGF and SIF, maintaining its nanoparticle morphology until reaching the small intestinal absorption site. This characteristic effectively mitigates the adverse reactions associated with free iron ions. Notably, even at elevated concentrations, 6-Fh induced minimal additional ROS production in the gut, surpassing the effects of conventional ferrous salt iron supplements. Furthermore, our findings suggest that 6-Fh ameliorates intestinal flora imbalances and restores microbial diversity, highlighting its potential therapeutic applications. Although ferrous sulfate is the conventional benchmark, it does not represent the full spectrum of clinically relevant iron sources, such as ferrous gluconate and polysaccharide-iron complex [66, 67]. Despite this limitation, our comparative framework effectively underscores the unique benefits of our compound by demonstrating its DMT1-independent uptake and minimal side effects. Additionally, 6-Fh is coadding-free, which reduces costs and favors future translation. Comprehensive immunogenicity studies (e.g., measuring anti-drug antibodies (ADAs) and cytokine levels in preclinical models upon repeat dosing) will be an essential component of subsequent clinical trials of novel pharmaceutical agents. In summary, 6-Fh is a safer and potentially more effective alternative to FeSO4 for treating IDA in future clinical applications and translational research. This is particularly true in scenarios involving individuals who cannot tolerate traditional iron supplements due to intestinal side effects, and individuals with impaired DMT1 function or other intestinal absorption disorders.

Materials and methods

Quantification of 6-Fh

The 6-Fh content was quantified as the total iron content. This assay is similar to that described in our previous study. First a standard curve was established with FeCl3 and xylenol orange (XO, 3,3′-bis[N, N-bis(carboxymethyl)aminomethyl]-o-cresolsulfone-phthalein, sodium salt) solution (1.25 µM, 25 mM H2SO4). The adduct of XO-Fe3+ complex has the absorption at 560 nm. To measure the concentration of 6-Fns, 10 µL 6-Fns was added to 200 µL XO-solution and incubated 10 min at 100 °C, followed with the absorption measurement. The pH value of the xylenol orange solution is 1, the 6-Fns can be completely dissolved after the incubation.

6-Fh stability

Prepared SGF, SIF and Lysosomal simulation solution: SGF and SIF were prepared according to the US Pharmacopeia (USP). For SGF (pH = 1.2): Add 2.0 g sodium chloride and 3.2 g pepsin to 7.0 mL hydrochloric acid. The volume was then set to 1000 mL with distilled water. For SIF (pH = 6.8): 6.8 g of potassium dihydrogen phosphate was dissolved in 250 mL distilled water. Then, 77 mL of sodium hydroxide solution (0.2 M) and 10 g of trypsin were added. The pH to 6.8 with a 0.2 M sodium hydroxide solution. The volume was then set to 1000 mL with distilled water. Lysosomal simulation solution (pH = 5): Equal volumes of 10 mM citric acid and 0.15 M NaCl solution, and the pH of the mixed solution was adjusted to 5 using 1 M NaOH and 1 M HCl.

Stability of 6-Fh in SGF: Add 1 mM 6-Fh to SGF and adjust the initial OD420 to 1. A 2 M hydrochloric acid solution was used as the control. The mixture was stirred slowly at 37 °C for 2 h. OD420 was measured every 20 min using a nanodrop. Finally, the relationship between each OD value and reaction time was determined based on the obtained results.

Stability of 6-Fh in SIF: 30 mL of SIF was added to a beaker and heated to 37 °C in a water bath. Equal amount of 1 mM 6-Fh. The mixture was stirred slowly at 37 °C for 12 h. Photographs were recorded every 2 h.

Stability of 6-Fh in lysosomal simulation solution: 30 mL lysosomal simulation solution was added to a wide-mouth glass bottle and heated to 37 °C in a water bath. Subsequently, 10 mL 6-Fh (1 mM) was added. The mixture was stirred slowly at 37 °C for 72 h. The UV-vis absorption spectrum of 6-Fh was recorded at different times.

Biosafety of 6-Fh

Caco-2 cells were purchased from Procell Life Science & Technology Co. Ltd. Cells were cultured and expanded according to the manufacturer’s instructions (DMEM + 20% FBS + 1% P/S, 37 °C, 5% CO2).

Cell uptake

The Aclar-embedded film was cut into squares (diameter, 0.5 cm) and placed in a 6-well plate. Caco-2 cell suspension (1.6 × 105 cells/mL) was added to the plate and the cells were cultured for 8 days (the medium was changed every 2 days). The complete medium was then replaced with 0.4 mM 6-Fh. After 24 h, the film was washed thrice with DPBS. The film was fixed with 2.5% glutaraldehyde and analyzed by TEM to detect the absorption of 6-Fh.

Cytotoxicity

For Cytotoxicity detection, the cells were seeded into 96-well plates at a density of 2 × 104 cells/well. When the cells reached 80% of the area at the bottom of the well, the complete medium was replaced with 0.2, 0.4, 0.8 mM 6-Fh or FeSO4 with/without FBS. CCK-8 assay was used to detect cell viability after incubation for 2 and 4 h.

LIP detection

Caco-2 cells were seeded into 96-well plates (5000 cells/well). When the cell grew to 80% of the area at the bottom of the hole, changed the complete medium to the news contained 0.2, 0.4, 0.8 mM 6-Fh or FeSO4 for 30 min. FerroOrange (1 µM, F374, Dojindo) and calcein-AM (1 µM, Y237214, Beyotime) were used to detect LIP in these cells.

Cell ROS detection

Caco-2 cells were seeded in 96-well plates (5000 cells/well). When the cell grew to 80% of the area at the bottom of the hole, changed the complete medium to the news contained 0.2, 0.4, 0.8 mM 6-Fh or FeSO4. A Cell ROS Detection Kit (S0033S, Beyotime) was used to detect ROS levels in each group after incubation for 12 h.

Iron-deficient Caco-2 model simulation and iron supplementation in vitro

The cells were seeded into 10 mm plates. When the cells reached 80% of the area at the bottom of the hole, the medium was replaced with DFO (200 µM) and HY-B1625 (MCE). Cells were incubated for 24 h to establish an iron-deficient Caco-2 model.

The effects of iron supplementation with 6-Fh and FeSO4 were assessed in the presence or absence of a DMT1 blocker (3 µM, HY-126302, MCE). The specific details are as follows: after incubation with the DMT1 blocker for 24 h, 6-Fh and FeSO4 (0.8 mM) were added, and the cells were further incubated for 24 h. Subsequently, the cells were collected and the M-PER ™ Mammalian Protein Extraction kit (78503, Thermo Fisher) was used. Reagent to extract total cell proteins and measured the concentration with Coomassie Plus (Bradford) Assay (23238, Thermo Fisher). The expression of ferritin heavy chain and DMT1 in each group of cells was detected using western blotting (H-chain: 1:2000, A10231, ABclonal; DMT1:1:1000, A23379, ABclonal). A cell iron detection kit (BC5315, Solarbio) was used to detect changes in intracellular iron content in the various groups.

Construction of an IDA mouse model and in vivo iron supplementation

All animal experiments involved in this study were approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of the First Affiliated Hospital of Xi’an Medical University (XYYFYLL-KTSB-2025-11).

Construction of the IDA mouse model

To establish the iron deficiency anemia model, mice were fed AIN-93G low-iron feed with weekly tail vein bleeding, which was recorded on the 0th day of the experiment. After 40 days, red blood cell (RBCs) and hemoglobin (HGB) concentrations of the mice were measured to determine whether the IDA model was successful. The blank groups were provided with AIN-93G standard feed and deionized water throughout the process.

IDA mice were randomly divided into seven groups and subjected to low-iron feeding. Through intragastric administration, these mice were given low, medium, and high dose of 6-Fh and FeSO4 (1.0, 2.0 and 3.0 mg Fe/kg, 200 µL) or an equal volume of distilled water, respectively. After 25 days of intragastric administration, significant differences in body weights were observed between the groups. The mice were fasted for 12 h and sacrificed by neck dislocation. Blood was collected for routine tests, and serum, liver, spleen, duodenum, colon, and cecum were collected.

Serum iron and TIBC

Serum iron content and TIBC in mice were detected using a serum iron detection kit (BC1735, Solarbio) and (BC2865, Solarbio).

Iron detection in liver and spleen

Fresh livers and spleens from each group were cut into pieces and homogenized in an ice bath using a tissue homogenizer. Iron content in the liver and spleen was detected using a tissue iron detection kit (BC4355, Solarbio).

H&E, AB-PAS, tight junction proteins (ZO-1 and Occludin), and ROS detection in intestinal tissue

Fresh duodenum and colon of each group were split into two parts, fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde, and frozen separately with liquid nitrogen. Intestinal tissues fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde were stained using hematoxylin-eosin (H&E) staining and periodic acid-Schiff and Alcian blue (AB-PAS) staining to examine morphological changes and alterations in mucin secretion, and immunofluorescence (IFC) staining was used to examine ZO-1 and Occludin. Intestinal tissues were snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen and stained with dihydroethidium (DHE) to detect ROS accumulation.

Liver oxidative damage detection

Fresh livers from each group were cut into pieces and homogenized in an ice bath using a tissue homogenizer. The glutathione peroxidase and Malondialdehyde (MDA) were detected using MDA Content Assay Kit (BC6415, Solarbio) and Glutathione peroxidase detection kit (BC1195, Solarbio), respectively.

Cecal microbial detection

Sterilized forceps were used to squeeze out the contents of the cecum and place them in a sterile EP tube. Then, 16 S rRNA high-throughput sequencing was used to perform PCR amplification on the V3-V4 region of the microbiota 16 rRNA gene.

Statistical analysis

Unless otherwise stated, all values were determined using a one-way ANOVA and Student’s t-test (t-test for direct comparison between two groups and one-way ANOVA for comparison among more than two groups). The level of significance was set at P < 0.05. Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism version 6.
