Implementation components and delivery structures of adolescent peer navigation as a workforce model: a scoping review
Authors: Ashley Chory, Ava Boal, Annabel Azziz, Carrie Levinson, Lonnie Embleton, Mary Ott, Rachel Vreeman, Edith Apondi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAdolescents face persistent barriers to accessing health services, particularly among vulnerable populations such as those living with HIV, adolescent mothers, and youth experiencing mental health or substance use challenges. Peer navigation has emerged as a promising strategy to address these gaps by leveraging shared lived experience to support engagement across health and social systems. However, existing literature has primarily focused on intervention effectiveness, with limited attention to how these programs are implemented. This scoping review aims to characterize the implementation architecture of adolescent peer navigation programs globally, with a focus on peer eligibility, training, supervision, and support systems.MethodsFollowing PRISMA-ScR guidelines, we systematically searched four databases for studies published between 2005 and 2025. Twenty-one programs met inclusion criteria. Data were extracted using a standardized, piloted template capturing study characteristics, peer navigator characteristics, training and supervision, navigator roles and responsibilities, health services and domains addressed, delivery modalities, and program focus. Given heterogeneity across studies, data were synthesized narratively using descriptive tables and iterative thematic coding. Two reviewers independently reviewed extracted data, developed and refined codes through discussion, and identified recurring implementation components and patterns to characterize program typologies and inform a conceptual model of adolescent peer navigation.ResultsAcross studies, peer navigation programs shared core design elements, including selection based on lived experience, structured training, supervision, and multidimensional roles spanning psychosocial support, health education, and service navigation. However, substantial variability was observed in how these components were operationalized. Training and supervision were widely implemented but inconsistently specified, and few studies examined peer navigator well-being or workforce sustainability. Programs were predominantly clinic-based and concentrated in countries in Africa, with emerging hybrid and community-based models.ConclusionsAdolescent peer navigation is best understood as a flexible workforce and service delivery model rather than a standardized intervention. Background

Adolescence is a critical period for establishing lifelong health behaviors, yet many young people face significant barriers to accessing health services, including stigma, limited autonomy, and structural constraints within health systems [1, 2]. These challenges are often amplified among vulnerable groups such as adolescents living with human immunodeficiency virus (HIV), adolescent mothers, street-connected youth, and those experiencing substance use or mental health challenges, who are at increased risk of disengagement from care [3–8]. As a result, there is a growing need for innovative strategies that support adolescents in navigating complex health and social systems and promote sustained engagement, retention, and well-being.

Peer-based approaches have become increasingly central to adolescent-responsive health strategies [4, 8–15]. The World Health Organization’s Global Standards for Quality Health Care Services for Adolescents emphasize participation, empowerment, non-discrimination, and supportive service environments, highlighting the importance of involving young people in the design and delivery of services [1]. Within this broader movement, peer-based approaches operate through a shared mechanism of leveraging individuals with similar age or lived experience to improve engagement, trust, and acceptability of services, while differing in how this mechanism is operationalized across intervention types. Peer education typically focuses on structured delivery of health information and health promotion activities, often in group-based or outreach formats [16–20]. Peer support emphasizes psychosocial support and shared lived experience, focusing on emotional, social, or coping-related needs, and may be delivered in both formal and informal settings [10, 21–23]. Peer navigation, by contrast, is a more structured implementation model in which trained peers with lived or contextual experience provide individualized, sustained support to facilitate engagement with health and social services, including system navigation, psychosocial support, and linkage to care across the continuum [12, 24–26]. This review focuses on peer navigation.

Peer navigators bridge gaps between adolescents and formal services, offering relational credibility, practical guidance, and psychosocial support; programs have been applied across HIV care, sexual and reproductive health (SRH), mental health, substance use, and pregnancy prevention [4, 8–11, 13–15, 20, 22, 27–29]. Despite growing interest, evidence remains fragmented, often limited to qualitative studies, pilots, or descriptive reports. Additionally, information around model implementation, like how to identify, train, supervise and provide adequate supports to peers, remains limited. While several recent systematic and scoping reviews have examined peer navigation and peer support interventions in adolescent and young adult populations (including in HIV care, sexual and reproductive health, and mental health contexts), these have primarily focused on intervention effectiveness or condition-specific outcomes, often in mixed-age populations, with limited attention to implementation processes [10, 22, 23, 30, 31].

Existing systematic reviews of adolescent peer navigator and peer support interventions have largely focused on evaluating effectiveness, with primary attention to clinical, behavioral, and psychosocial outcomes [10, 22, 29, 32, 33]. These reviews typically synthesize evidence on whether peer-based models improve measures such as mental health symptoms, treatment adherence, or engagement in care. In addition, many include broad descriptions of intervention characteristics—such as delivery format, setting, and general peer roles—but these elements are usually presented descriptively rather than analytically. Even when implementation is considered, it is often framed at a high level through summaries of general barriers and facilitators, such as organizational support or role clarity, rather than through detailed examination of how programs are operationalized in practice.

As a result, key components of implementation—particularly those related to the peer workforce itself—remain underexplored. Across studies, there is limited systematic attention to how adolescent peers are selected or deemed eligible, how they are trained and prepared for their roles, what forms of supervision they receive, and what ongoing supports are in place to sustain their engagement and well-being. While many reviews acknowledge the importance of these factors, they consistently note that reporting is inconsistent and insufficiently detailed to allow for meaningful comparison or replication. This lack of granularity constrains the field’s ability to understand not just whether peer navigator models are effective, but how they can be implemented successfully and sustainably across contexts.

This scoping review addresses a critical gap by shifting attention from intervention outcomes to the implementation of adolescent peer navigation programs. Specifically, this scoping review addressed the following questions: (1) What adolescent peer navigation models have been described in the literature (2)? What are the key implementation components of these models, including peer navigator selection, training, supervision, and service delivery structures (3)? How do these components vary across settings and populations?

Methods

Study design

We conducted a scoping review to identify and describe the characteristics, components, and implementation strategies of adolescent peer navigation programs globally. Scoping review methodology is particularly suited to mapping diverse models, summarizing their key features, and identifying gaps in the literature, without restricting to studies reporting intervention effectiveness. The review was guided by the framework proposed by Arksey and O’Malley which outlines a systematic process for identifying the research question, identifying and selecting relevant studies, charting the data, and collating, summarizing, and reporting the results [34, 35]. The review was further refined through the methodological recommendations of Levac and colleagues, which emphasize an iterative approach to study selection and data charting and enhanced methodological rigor [34, 35]. Reporting followed the PRISMA-ScR (Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses Extension for Scoping Reviews) guidelines (Supplementary Material 1: PRISMA-ScR Checklist) [34, 35].

In this scoping review, peer navigation is defined as adolescent or young adult peers with relevant lived experience who are trained to provide structured, individualized support to facilitate access to and sustained engagement with health and social services. Peer education and broader peer support interventions are included only when they explicitly incorporate a navigation function as part of their model. Rather than focusing on effectiveness, it examines how these programs are structured and operationalized, with particular emphasis on peer eligibility, training, supervision, and support systems. This reframes peer navigation as a workforce and service delivery model and aims to build a more detailed understanding of how these interventions are implemented in practice. Using a global, descriptive scoping review approach, we synthesize adolescent peer navigation models across diverse clinical and community settings and health conditions. By mapping key implementation components and their variation across contexts, this scoping review provides a foundation for informing program design, adaptation, and future research on the sustainability and optimization of peer navigation models.

Eligibility criteria

All study designs were eligible for inclusion. Studies were included if they reported on the use of adolescent peer navigators (aged 10–24) to support access to health or social services and provided sufficient detail on the design of the peer navigation model. Adolescents were defined as individuals aged 10–24 years, consistent with commonly used age ranges in adolescent and youth health research and implementation frameworks that extend beyond the WHO (World Health Organization) definition of adolescence (10–19 years) to capture the broader “young people” population relevant to health service delivery [1, 36]. Specifically, eligible studies were required to describe the following core components: peer navigator eligibility criteria, training, supervision and supports, as well as the populations served and services provided. To ensure meaningful synthesis of implementation features, only studies that included information across all of these domains were retained. No geographic restrictions were applied. Only studies published in English were included.

Studies were excluded if they described interventions delivered by adolescent peer navigators but did not report on the underlying model components outlined above, as insufficient methodological detail precluded extraction and comparison of implementation characteristics. We also excluded studies focused exclusively on adults (≥ 25 years), or those that did not disaggregate data for adolescent populations. In addition, systematic and scoping reviews, commentaries, editorials, conference abstracts, and study protocols (i.e., interventions not yet implemented) were excluded, as they did not provide primary, fully realized data on peer navigation model design.

Information sources, search strategy and study selection

A comprehensive search was conducted by a medical librarian on April 14, 2025 in Ovid Medline, Embase (Ovid), CINAHL (Cumulative Index to Nursing and Allied Health Literature), and Scopus databases. The search strategy included all appropriate controlled vocabulary and keywords terms related to: peer navigation, health care delivery, and adolescents. A date filter from 2005 to the date of the search was utilized. Before execution, the search was peer reviewed by another medical librarian utilizing the PRESS Checklist (Supplementary Material 2: PRESS Checklist) [37, 38]. Full search strategies are available in the Supplementary Material 3: Search Strategy. All records were imported into Covidence (Covidence systematic review software, Veritas Health Innovation, Melbourne, Australia. Available at www.covidence.org.) for duplicate removal and screening. Titles and abstracts were independently screened by three reviewers against eligibility criteria. Full-text articles were then assessed independently by two reviewers. Discrepancies were resolved through discussion and consensus, with input from an additional reviewer as needed.

Data extraction

A standardized data extraction template was developed a priori to systematically capture implementation-relevant features of adolescent peer navigation programs (Supplementary Material 4: Data Extraction Template). Extracted variables included study characteristics (first author, year of publication, country, and setting), peer navigator characteristics (age, gender, lived experience, and recruitment criteria), and key implementation components such as training and supervision (including duration, content, frequency, and supervisory structures). In addition, data were collected on peer navigator roles and responsibilities, including health domains addressed, services delivered, and modalities of engagement (e.g., in-person, telephone, digital, or social media-based delivery). Information on program type and focus population was also extracted to support categorization across implementation models. The extraction framework was piloted on a subset of studies and refined to ensure consistency and completeness across reviewers.

Data synthesis

Given the heterogeneity of study designs and reporting, a narrative synthesis was conducted [39–41]. Descriptive tables were used to summarize study characteristics, peer navigator profiles, training, supervision, roles, and delivery strategies. Recurring program components and patterns were identified through iterative thematic coding, whereby two reviewers independently reviewed extracted data and developed an initial set of codes based on the study objectives and key domains of peer navigation (e.g., roles, training, supervision, and delivery setting). These codes were then compared, refined, and consolidated through regular discussion, with additional codes added inductively as new concepts were identified during analysis. Coding was conducted in Microsoft Excel and was applied iteratively across included studies, allowing themes to be continuously refined as synthesis progressed. This process informed the development of a conceptual model of adolescent peer navigation.

Data charting was conducted independently by two reviewers using the standardized data extraction template developed for this scoping review. The form was iteratively refined throughout the review process to ensure consistent capture of study characteristics and peer navigation model components. Discrepancies between reviewers were resolved through discussion, with a third reviewer providing verification and adjudication when consensus could not be reached. Distinct program typologies were derived based on setting, population, and delivery approach. The synthesis focused on mapping models and structures, rather than evaluating effectiveness.

Results

Study selection and overview

Our search identified 21,016 records, with 142 full-text articles assessed for eligibility. From these, 32 articles were included, representing 21 unique adolescent peer navigation programs (Fig. 1). Multiple papers layered on the same peer navigator program were combined for the purpose of this analysis, resulting in 21 unique peer navigator programs. Table 1 summarizes study characteristics, including implementation country, setting, and peer worker titles. Programs were implemented across four World Health Organization (WHO) regions, with most in Africa (n = 15), followed by the Americas (n = 5), Southeast Asia (n = 1), and Europe (n = 1). Implementation was primarily urban (n = 12), with fewer in peri-urban (n = 3), rural (n = 2), or combined rural–urban contexts (n = 3). Most programs were clinic-based (n = 17), though several also incorporated community or school settings, and nine operated as hybrid clinic–community models. This distribution reflects a predominantly facility-anchored approach, with varying degrees of community integration, ranging from clinic-based services with community referrals to hybrid models in which peer navigators provided outreach and support in community and school settings.

Peer worker titles varied widely, reflecting local adaptation and program positioning. Common titles included peer mentor (n = 5), peer navigator (n = 3), and peer provider (n = 3), alongside context-specific designations such as Community Adolescent Treatment Supporter (CATS) and Young Mentor Mother (YMM). This heterogeneity underscores the absence of standardized terminology, which may contribute to challenges in comparing and replicating models across settings. Study designs were predominantly qualitative or descriptive (n = 15), with several pilot interventions (n = 6). Few studies incorporated rigorous outcome evaluation, and no fully powered randomized controlled trials were identified. Notably, very few studies assessed the experiences, skill development, or well-being of peer navigators themselves, highlighting a critical gap in understanding the sustainability and workforce implications of these models.

Characteristics of peer navigators

Across programs, eligibility criteria were consistently grounded in shared lived experience or social proximity to the focus population, positioning experiential credibility as a core design feature (Table 2). In most studies (n = 18), peer navigators were required to share key characteristics with participants, including health status, life experience, or community background. Matching criteria commonly included gender, sexual orientation, pregnancy or parenting status, HIV status, substance use recovery, disability, or shared community context.

The most frequently reported lived experience was living with HIV (n = 8), followed by adolescent pregnancy or young motherhood (n = 3), substance use recovery (n = 2), and experience of street-connectedness (n = 2). Fewer programs included peers with lived experience of mental health challenges (n = 2) or disability (n = 1). One program included both HIV-positive and HIV-negative peers with relevant prevention knowledge. These findings suggest that eligibility is typically narrowly defined around program-specific priorities, rather than conceptualized as a broader or standardized competency framework. Peer navigators were most commonly aged 18–24 years (n = 15), with some inclusion of younger adolescents or older adults. Gender representation was inclusive in most programs (n = 17), often supplemented by additional matching strategies to enhance relatability and trust. Overall, peer selection approaches emphasized relational alignment with participants, though criteria for recruitment and selection processes were rarely described in detail.

Training and supervision

Structured training was reported in 18 programs, indicating that formal preparation is a central component of adolescent peer navigation models. However, training approaches varied substantially in duration, content, and intensity. Trainings ranged from brief formats (≤ 5 days; n = 8) to 1–2 week programs (n = 5), with only one study describing an extended three-month certification. Four studies reported training content without specifying duration, limiting comparability.

Training curricula commonly included counseling and communication skills (n = 15), HIV-related education (n = 10), sexual and reproductive health (n = 8), and system navigation (n = 9). Fewer programs addressed confidentiality and ethics explicitly (n = 6), and specialized competencies—such as motivational interviewing, crisis response, or digital engagement (e.g., communication with clients via SMS, WhatsApp, phone calls, or other mobile-based follow-up platforms)—were inconsistently incorporated. Where these advanced competencies were included, they were typically delivered in more intensive training models (e.g., multi-week or certification-based programs) and often supplemented through ongoing mentorship or supervisor-led skills coaching rather than standalone formal instruction. Notably, only two programs reported formal competency assessment, suggesting that training is rarely linked to standardized evaluation of readiness or skill acquisition. Refresher or booster training was reported in nine programs, though the timing and structure of these sessions varied. Overall, training models appeared highly heterogeneous and were often insufficiently specified, limiting insight into what constitutes adequate preparation for the peer navigator role.

Supervision structures were described in 13 programs, with considerable variability in format and intensity. Models included one-on-one supervision (n = 6), group supervision (n = 5), and integration within multidisciplinary teams (n = 5). Supervisors ranged from social workers and nurses to program coordinators and technical advisors. Supervision commonly involved case discussion, problem-solving, and emotional support; however, only one program described structured supervision with systematic performance feedback. In programs where peer navigators were trained in more specialized competencies (e.g., motivational interviewing or crisis response), supervision structures more often included case-based coaching or skills reinforcement to support application of these techniques in practice. These findings point to supervision as a recognized but inconsistently operationalized component of implementation, with limited standardization or formalization across models.

Roles and responsibilities of peer navigators

Peer navigators fulfilled multidimensional roles spanning psychosocial support, service navigation, and community engagement (Table 3). Programs addressed a wide range of health domains, including HIV prevention and treatment, sexual and reproductive health, pregnancy prevention, mental health, and substance use. Responsibilities clustered into three core domains: (1) psychosocial support and behavior change counseling (2), navigation and care cascade support, and (3) outreach and community engagement. Individual counseling and mentorship were nearly universal (n = 19), while group-based peer support was reported in ten programs. Outreach and engagement activities were described in nine programs, often targeting marginalized populations through both in-person and digital modalities.

Tailoring vs. generalized programs

Peer navigation models were frequently tailored to specific populations or priority health issues, including adolescents living with HIV, adolescent mothers, and youth in substance use recovery. While core components—such as recruitment based on lived experience, structured training, and supervision—were broadly consistent, their operationalization varied substantially across contexts. This pattern suggests that adolescent peer navigation functions as a flexible implementation model rather than a standardized intervention. Programs appear to adapt core design elements to local needs, resulting in considerable heterogeneity in delivery modalities and role emphasis. While this flexibility may enhance contextual relevance, it also complicates efforts to define best practices or establish transferable implementation standards.

Discussion

This scoping review synthesizes the structural and operational features of 21 adolescent peer navigation programs implemented across diverse global contexts. Programs spanned multiple WHO regions, with a strong concentration in Africa and more limited representation from the Americas, Europe, and Southeast Asia. They served a range of adolescent populations, including adolescents living with HIV, adolescent mothers, street-connected youth, youth in substance use recovery, and adolescents with mental health or developmental conditions. Most programs were implemented in urban, clinic-based settings, with fewer embedded in rural, peri-urban, or fully community-based contexts. This geographic and setting distribution highlights both the concentration of evidence in specific regions and delivery platforms, and the importance of context in shaping how peer navigation models are operationalized.

A key contribution of this scoping review is its shift in analytic focus from intervention effectiveness to implementation architecture. Across studies, adolescent peer navigation was consistently defined by a small set of core design elements: selection based on shared lived experience or social proximity, structured but variably specified training, ongoing supervision, and multidimensional service delivery roles spanning psychosocial support, health education, and system navigation. While these components were consistently present, they were implemented with substantial variation in intensity, structure, and formalization. This pattern is consistent with broader evidence on peer-support models, which demonstrates considerable variation in role definition and delivery while identifying training, supervision, organizational support, and clearly defined responsibilities as important features of successful implementation [42]. Similarly, WHO guidance describes peer support as a model grounded in lived experience and relational support, with peers serving functions that may include education, advocacy, coaching, mentoring, and practical support [43]. This suggests that adolescent peer navigation is best understood not as a standardized intervention, but as a flexible workforce and service delivery model built around a shared set of foundational design principles.

Despite this shared core structure, considerable heterogeneity was observed in how programs were configured and adapted. Peer navigator eligibility criteria were primarily grounded in experiential matching, though the specificity and formalization of recruitment processes varied widely. This emphasis on shared experience is consistent with qualitative research among adolescents and young adults living with HIV in Kenya, who expressed preferences for navigators who were similar in age and HIV status and who could provide encouragement, education, and relationship-based support [43]. Training ranged from brief orientation-style models to more extended certification approaches, but was rarely linked to standardized competency assessment. Similarly, supervision was inconsistently described and often informal, with limited evidence of structured performance feedback mechanisms. These findings align with broader peer-support implementation evidence identifying adequate training and supervision as important facilitators, while role ambiguity, inadequate organizational support, and insufficient training may hinder effective integration of peer workers into health and social service systems [42]. These findings suggest that while training and supervision are widely recognized as essential components, they remain under-specified elements of implementation rather than clearly defined or standardized model features.

Several recurring implementation typologies emerged from the data. Clinic-anchored models positioned peer navigators primarily within health facilities, integrating them into existing service delivery teams. Community-embedded models extended peer navigation into schools, settlements, or community spaces, emphasizing outreach and engagement outside clinical environments. For example, peer navigators working with street-connected children and youth in Kenya engaged young people in the settings where they lived and congregated and supported linkage to HIV testing and care [4]. Hybrid models combined facility and community engagement, often incorporating digital tools such as phone calls, messaging platforms, or social media to support continuity of care. These configurations are also reflected in adolescent HIV peer-navigation research, in which both electronic and in-person navigation have been used to provide education, psychosocial support, relationship-building, and assistance with engagement in care [43, 44]. Similarly, the iCARE Nigeria intervention combined social media outreach with peer navigation and provided HIV testing and linkage to care through clinical, community, and home-based settings [45]. In addition, population-specific models were tailored to distinct adolescent groups, including adolescent mothers, youth in recovery, and street-connected youth. Together, these typologies illustrate that adolescent peer navigation is not a single intervention, but a family of adaptable delivery models that vary in setting, modality, and focus population while retaining a consistent underlying logic of peer-based relational support and system navigation.

Across these diverse configurations, several critical implementation gaps were identified. Reporting on peer navigator training, supervision, and skill development was often incomplete, limiting the ability to compare or replicate program components. This limitation mirrors broader concerns in the peer-support literature regarding inconsistent role definitions, variation in training and supervision, and insufficiently specified implementation guidance [42]. In addition, very few studies examined the experiences, professional development, or well-being of peer navigators themselves, despite their central role in service delivery. This is important because peer workers may experience both benefits and challenges associated with their roles, including the demands of supporting others while navigating their own lived experiences [42, 46]. Geographic representation was also uneven, with a strong concentration of evidence from countries in Africa and limited data from other regions. These gaps constrain the development of evidence-based guidance for workforce design and highlight the need for more standardized reporting frameworks that capture not only program outcomes, but also implementation processes and peer workforce characteristics.

This scoping review provides a descriptive foundation for understanding adolescent peer navigation as an implementation model composed of interrelated workforce and service delivery components. Future research should move beyond describing program presence toward evaluating which configurations of training, supervision, and peer selection are most effective in supporting both adolescent outcomes and peer navigator sustainability. Greater attention is also needed to hybrid and digital delivery models, which may expand reach but remain under-described in terms of implementation structure. This need is consistent with the broader digital-navigation literature, which has identified substantial heterogeneity in navigator training, educational background, communication frequency, and communication modalities and has called for clearer standards regarding navigator responsibilities and training [47].

To improve comparability across studies, a standardized reporting framework for peer navigation models should capture core implementation domains, including: peer navigator characteristics (e.g., age, lived experience, recruitment criteria), training content and duration (including core and advanced competencies), supervision structure and intensity, scope of peer navigator roles (including clinical, psychosocial, and digital engagement activities), delivery setting (facility-, community-, school-, or hybrid-based), and mechanisms of integration within health and social service systems. Consistent reporting of these domains is consistent with broader calls for clearly defined peer-worker roles and setting-specific implementation guidance, particularly where peer support is being integrated into formal health and social service systems [42, 46]. Consistent reporting of these domains would enable more robust cross-program comparison and synthesis of effective implementation strategies Importantly, establishing a common reporting framework would not require standardization of the peer navigation intervention itself; rather, it would provide a common structure for describing how different models are designed, adapted, implemented, and supported across contexts.

A major strength of this scoping review is its global scope and its explicit focus on implementation architecture rather than intervention outcomes. By systematically mapping peer selection, training, supervision, and role structures, it provides a detailed typology of adolescent peer navigation model components across diverse settings. Limitations include the predominance of qualitative and pilot studies, as well as heterogeneous and often incomplete reporting on key implementation features. As a result, the findings reflect variability in how programs are documented as well as how they are implemented. Nevertheless, the review offers a coherent synthesis of core design patterns and identifies key gaps in the operationalization of adolescent peer navigation models.

Conclusions

Adolescent peer navigation programs are widely implemented and share a common structural logic but remain highly variable in their operationalization and under-described in key implementation domains. This scoping review clarifies the core components and typologies of these models, reframing peer navigation as a flexible workforce and service delivery architecture rather than a uniform intervention. By mapping how programs are designed and adapted across contexts, this work provides a foundation for future refinement, evaluation, and scale-up of adolescent peer navigation models.
