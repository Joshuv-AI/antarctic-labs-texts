The Effects of High-Intensity Functional Training on the Perceptions of Exercise in Middle-Aged Females: A Pilot Study
Authors: HALLE N. BRIN, BAYLIE G. SIGMUND, NATHAN D. DICKS, KATHRYN J. DESHAW, TANIS J. WALCH, MICHAEL J. CARPER, ALLISON M. BARRY
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
The high prevalence of obesity and physical inactivity in the U.S. is a public health crisis. This study aims to examine the effects of high-intensity functional training (HIFT) on exercise perceptions and physiological changes in inactive females. A pre-test and post-test, quasi-experimental design was conducted with inactive, middle-aged females (n = 8) participating in an 8-week supervised and verified intervention (2-week High-Intensity Interval Training, 6-weeks HIFT). The Health Belief Model Scale for Exercise (HBMS-E) survey was completed pre-intervention, post-intervention, and 4-week follow-up to assess exercise perceptions. Pre- and post-intervention physiological assessments included: body composition, strength (1-repetition maximum (RM) bench press (BP), 5-RM deadlift (DL), and maximal oxygen consumption (V̇O2max). A within-subjects repeated-measures ANOVA assessed the HBM constructs between pre-intervention, post-intervention, and follow-up. Dependent t-tests compared physiological outcomes pre- to post-intervention. There was a significant decrease between pre-intervention, post-intervention, and follow-up for objective (F(2, 14)=22.24, p < .001) and subjective barriers (F(2, 14) = 11.20, p = .001), and a significant increase in self-efficacy (F(1.08, 7.53)= 8.96, p = .02). There was a significant increase in V̇O2max (p = .001, d = 1.81), 1-RM BP (p < .001, d = 2.51), and 5-RM DL (p = .001, d = 1.83) pre- to post-intervention. Findings suggest HIFT positively influenced middle-aged females’ perceptions of barriers and self-efficacy related to exercise and provides fitness benefits to improve health and wellness.

INTRODUCTION

Over the past few decades, the prevalence of overweight and obesity has increased within the United States (U.S.) (40). Coupled with high levels of inactivity, this has become a public health crisis. Obesity and low levels of physical activity (PA) are two well-established cardiovascular disease (CVD) risk factors, which is a leading cause of death for U.S. females (29). Current PA guidelines recommend 150-minutes of moderate-to-vigorous PA (MVPA) or 75-minutes of vigorous PA per week to prevent chronic disease risk and improve cardiorespiratory fitness (CRF) (23). PA engagement continues to be a challenge as approximately 52% of women are meeting PA guidelines (16). To potentially alleviate the obesity and physical inactivity epidemic, identifying determinants to PA and exercise engagement, along with maintenance, may be explained using behavior models.

The Health Belief Model (HBM) is a conceptual tool designed to identify underlying factors and explain what aspects influence certain health-related behaviors (4). More recently, the HBM has been used to explain and predict PA and exercise behaviors (20, 35, 36). The HBM includes six constructs: perceived susceptibility, perceived severity, perceived benefits, perceived objective and subjective barriers, self-efficacy, and cues to action (5). Previous studies have utilized this model to identify determinants to exercise adoption and adherence and increase PA levels among college students (36), pregnant (35) and middle-aged women (20). Perceived barriers, including lack of time and motivation, have been shown to be the most prevalent barriers to prevent initial and long-term exercise engagement (19), whereas aspects of self-efficacy, including social support, self-regulation, and mastery experiences are considered to be the most influential (2). Moreover, evidence suggests increasing self-efficacy is important early on in behavior change, as it makes it more likely for one to pursue and maintain a course of action (27), such as PA or exercise. Although the use of the HBM has been a strategy to understand perceptions towards participation in PA and exercise, it has yet to be used in specific exercise modalities, including high-intensity functional training.

High-Intensity Functional Training (HIFT) has gained popularity in recent years as it has similar components to high-intensity interval training (HIIT). HIIT incorporates aerobic exercises (e.g., running, rowing) with various bodyweight exercises (e.g., air squats, pushups), and is characterized by short bursts of vigorous activity in repeated bouts, with designated rest periods or low-intensity exercise (11). Conversely, HIFT incorporates a variety of multimodal, functional movements (e.g., pull, hinge, squat) combined with similar aerobic exercises, and performed at relatively high-intensity (i.e., self-selected) (11). HIFT typically excludes designated rest periods, allowing individuals to rest as needed (11). Moreover, utilizing self-selected intensity may be beneficial for inactive individuals to tailor workouts to suit their fitness level, as it has shown to develop a greater exercise tolerance, promote positive affective responses (e.g., enjoyment), and therefore, potentially increase adherence (32). Research has shown HIFT improves CRF, muscular strength and endurance, and body composition suggesting it may allow for more significant improvements than HIIT or more traditional exercise modalities alone (4). Furthermore, few studies have assessed the aspects of exercise behaviors and perceptions within HIFT specifically. Currently, HIFT-based programs have been shown to elicit high rates enjoyment (18), social support (38), motivation and mastery experience (33), and promote greater adherence to exercise (18). Despite evidence of HIFT’s benefits, it is unclear how to get inactive individuals to consider engaging in a HIFT regimen and engage in PA long-term.

Many studies have evaluated the effects of an intervention’s ability to improve PA levels or individually assess at least one aspect of behavior (18, 33, 38), although, to the author’s knowledge, no studies have yet investigated HBM within a HIFT exercise regimen. Moreover, it is unclear how parameters of the HBM are influenced by engaging in HIFT and subsequently, effect PA engagement and adherence. Therefore, the primary purpose of this study was to examine the effect of HIFT on exercise perceptions in middle-aged females using the HBM constructs. We hypothesized the intervention to improve participants’ exercise perceptions compared to baseline, specifically relating to their perceived barriers and self-efficacy. A secondary purpose was to examine the effect of an 8-week HIFT intervention on physiological changes in inactive females. Similarly, these parameters were hypothesized to improve relative to baseline.

METHODS

Participants

An a priori power analysis using statistical software (G*power VA 3.1.9.7) determined eight participants were needed as an adequate sample size and the following parameters were used: t-test matched pairs, effect size = 1.05, power = 0.80, and α = 0.05 (17). Nine inactive, middle-aged females were recruited to participate in the study. Inclusion criteria included: 1) participants between 30–49 years of age, 2) participants who had not engaged in more than 6 months of structured exercise in the previous 12 months and not currently meeting American College of Sports Medicine’s (ACSM) PA guidelines, and 3) participants who met the ACSM pre-participation health screening algorithm (1). Participants were excluded from the study if they had injuries that prevented them from completing a treadmill exercise test. The Institutional Review Board approved the study and all participants provided written informed consent prior to participation. This study adheres to the ethical standards of the International Journal of Exercise Science (31).

Protocol

Pre- and post-intervention measurements included anthropometric measures, a maximal oxygen consumption test, strength assessments, completion of the Health Belief Model Scale for Exercise (HBMS-E) (39) survey and the International Physical Activity Questionnaire – Long Form (IPAQ) (9). The HBMS-E and IPAQ surveys were distributed again 4-weeks post-intervention for a follow-up measurement.

Anthropometric measures consisted of height (cm), assessed using a portable stadiometer; (Seca Corp, Model 213, Hamburg, Germany); body mass (kg), percent body fat (%BF), fat mass (kg), fat-free mass (kg), and BMI (kg/m2) were assessed using a Tanita digital bioelectrical impedance scale (Denver Instruments, Model DA 150, Bohemia, New York).

Maximal oxygen consumption (V̇O2max) was measured via an individualized graded exercise test (GXT) using a walking protocol adapted from Dicks et al. (10) and conducted using a fitness treadmill (WOODWAY USA, Inc., Pro XL, Waukesha, Wisconsin, USA). During the GXT, participants were required to wear a face mask (Hans Rudolph, Inc. 7450 Series V2 Mask Oro-Nasal Custom Mask Adapters, Shawnee, Kansas, USA) and expire through a two-way nonrebreathing valve (Hans Rudolph, Inc., Two-way Non-Rebreathing T-Shape 2700 Series Valve, Shawnee, Kansas, USA) which was connected to a metabolic measurement analyzer (Parvo- Medics 2400, Logan, UT). Prior to the GXT, participants completed a self-reported Physical Activity Rating (PA-R) scale, ranging from 0–15, to estimate the participant’s relative V̇O2max using a non-exercise regression equation (21). Participants’ demographic and anthropometric measurements were utilized to individualize the GXT protocol, as previously described (10). Briefly the protocol consisted of a 3-min warm-up, GXT, 3-min active recovery stage, and square-wave verification bout.

Strength assessments included handgrip strength, 1-repetition maximum bench press (1-RM BP), and 5-repetition maximum deadlift (5-RM DL). Prior to all strength assessments, the participants completed a three-minute warmup of low-intensity aerobic exercise (e.g., rower, assault bike, or running) and a dynamic warm-up (e.g., floor sweeps, Samson lunge, inchworm, quad stretch). Handgrip strength was assessed using a handgrip dynamometer (Takei Scientific Instruments Co., T.K.K. 5001 Grip-A, Tokyo, Japan) in a bipedal position with arm fully extended. Participants were allowed two attempts in each hand and the scores for each hand were averaged to the nearest kg.

Upper and lower body strength were assessed using a 1-RM BP and 5-RM DL, respectively. Procedures for maximal strength assessments were adapted from the National Strength and Conditioning Association (NSCA) (30). The participants performed 1-RM BP using a training barbell (Rogue Fitness, Rogue T-15lb Technique Bar, Columbus, OH). Participants completed a warm-up protocol for the 1-RM BP according to standardized procedures (34). The 5-RM DL utilized a 27.3 kg trap bar (Rogue Fitness, Rogue TB-1 Trap Bar 2.0, Columbus, OH), although an exception was made for individuals who could not lift the trap bar by utilizing dual kettlebells. The participants were able to progress from dual 8.2 kg to 11.8 kg kettlebells (Rogue Fitness, Rogue Kettlebells, Columbus, OH) if needed until attempting the trap bar. The purpose of a submaximal leg strength test was to ensure the participant were performing the lift safely, while maintaining correct form (15). The criteria for completion of the maximal strength assessments included: 1) failure for a successful repetition; or 2) participant verbalized they achieved their maximal effort. The participants were limited to five total attempts at achieving their maximal effort for each strength assessment, either by increasing the load per attempt, reattempting a failure, or reducing the load after a failure.

The pre-intervention survey consisted of demographic questions including participants’ ethnicity, education level, perception of current health status, factors contributing to health status, motivations to exercise, previous engagement in PA or exercise, and current phase of the menstrual cycle. Following the demographic questions, participants completed the HBMS-E and IPAQ questionnaires, which were also completed at post- and 4-weeks post-intervention. The HBMS-E survey consisted of 18 total questions formatted into 5-point Likert scales and grouped into the five sub-categories: perceived objective barriers (4 questions on lack of time, facility or equipment, exercise modality, and workout partner), perceived subjective barriers (3 questions on lack of motivation and benefits, fear of pain), self-efficacy (3 questions on confidence to exercise with specific barriers), perceived severity (2 questions on increased risk of chronic disease and weight gain), and cues to action (3 questions regarding friends, family, and doctors’ influence on exercise) (39). The IPAQ consisted of 27 questions of self-reported PA in minutes per week across five activity domains (household activities, job-related activity, transportation, and leisure-time PA and sedentary activity). The HBMS-E and IPAQ have been previously validated and considered reliable instruments (9, 39). An additional question was asked during a 4-week follow-up to examine whether participants continued to exercise and identify barriers if they did not.

The 8-week intervention was led by a certified NSCA coach with First Aid and CPR certification. Each supervised, verified, and group-based exercise session was 35–45 minutes in length, three days per week. The first two weeks of the intervention consisted of an “on-ramp” exercise prescription incorporating HIIT techniques. The on-ramp focused primarily on body weight and aerobic exercises in a work-to-rest ratio of 1:1 (e.g., 30 seconds of work, 30 seconds of rest). The purpose of the on-ramp was to introduce participants to the various intensities and styles of training and allow them to work on exercise techniques. Following the on-ramp, participants completed a 6-week HIFT protocol consisting of functional movements (e.g., hinge, squat, lunge, push, pull, twist, and gait/carry movements) with the addition of resistance (e.g., dumbbells, bands, and kettlebells) and aerobic exercises (e.g., rower, assault bike), performed at a self-selected intensity. The main focus was to complete as many repetitions as possible in the fastest time possible or complete a series of exercises within a given time frame for as many rounds as possible (AMRAP). HIFT sessions increased in duration from 15 to 20 minutes over the course of 8-weeks. Additionally, progressive overload was achieved by increasing repetitions, ranging from 8 to 12, and encouraging participants to increase resistance using dumbbells, kettlebells, and resistance bands. Participants were provided heart rate monitors (Polar, Polar H10, Bethpage, NY) and smartwatches (Polar, Polar Vantage V, Bethpage, NY) during each session to monitor their heart rate via the Polar Teams app (Polar, Bethpage, NY). Before each exercise session, participants completed a warm-up of three minutes of low-intensity aerobic exercise followed by a dynamic warm-up session.

Statistical Analysis

Shapiro-Wilk’s test and Q-Q plots were used to assess the normalcy of the data. Descriptive statistics are reported using mean ± standard deviation (SD). Paired t-tests were used to assess PA levels reported from the IPAQ. Evaluation of body mass, BMI, BF%, FFM, FM, strength assessments, and V̇O2max between pre- and post-intervention were compared using dependent t-tests. A within-subjects repeated-measures ANOVA was used to assess the five HBM constructs between pre-intervention, post-intervention, and 4-week follow-up. Cohen’s d effect size was calculated with the following interpretations of < .25 = trivial, 0.25–0.5 = small, 0.5–1.0 = moderate, > 1.0 = large (6). Statistical significance was set at p-value ≤ 0.05. All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS (v.26) (IBM Corp., Armonk, New York).

RESULTS

A total of nine female participants started the study, with eight (age: 39.63 ± 8.14 years) completing the entire study protocol. One participant dropped out at week 5 due to health issues independent of the study, and therefore, was not included in statistical analysis. Table 1 reports means ± SD for body composition variables and resting heart rate pre- and post-intervention. During the 8-week intervention, participants on average completed 20 out of 24 workouts, therefore adherence was 84%. There was no significant difference in body composition and resting heart rate pre- and post-intervention (p > .05).

There was a significant decrease for objective barriers (F(2, 14) = 22.24, p < .001, η2 = .82) and subjective barriers (F(2, 14) = 11.20, p < .001, η2 = .62), and a significant increase for self-efficacy (F(1.08, 7.53) = 8.96, p = .02, η2 = .56) between pre-intervention, post-intervention, and followup. A Bonferroni post-hoc analysis reported a significant decrease in objective barriers pre- to post-intervention and pre-intervention to follow-up, respectively (p < .01 and p = .002). In contrast, there was no significant difference between post-intervention to follow-up (p > .05). Subjective barriers were trending towards significance pre- to post-intervention (p = .05). There was a significant decrease pre-intervention to follow-up (p = .01) for subjective barriers, although post-intervention to follow-up was not significant (p > .05). For self-efficacy, there was significant increase pre- to post-intervention (p < .05) and pre-intervention to follow-up was trending towards significance (p = .07), whereas post-intervention to follow-up was not significant (p > .05). There was no significant difference between pre-intervention, post-intervention, and follow-up for perceived benefits (F(2, 14) = 2.38, p = .13, η2 = .25), perceived severity (F(2, 14) = 1.54, p = .25, η2 = .18), and cues to action (F(2, 14) = .104, p = .90, η2 = .02) (Table 2).

There was a significant increase from pre- to post-intervention for daily MVPA (t(7) = −3.30, p = .01, d = 1.26 ; 7.77 ± 10.86 to 20.39 ± 9.07, respectively) (Figure 1(a)), and a significant decrease in weekday sedentary time (t(7) = 2.49, p = .04, d = .49; 442.50 ± 219.72 to 345.00 ± 171.96, respectively) and weekend day sedentary time (t(7) = 3.04, p = .02, d = 1.09; 330.00 ± 184.24 to 180.00 ± 64.14, respectively) (Figure 1(b)). Weekday sedentary time was trending towards significance post-intervention to follow-up (t(7) = −1.99, p = .09, d = .27; 345.00 ± 171.96 to 397.50 ± 212.59, respectively).

During each HIFT session, participants, on average, worked at a moderate to vigorous intensity of 71–77% of their HRmax. Pre- to post-intervention there was a significant increase in V̇O2max (p = .001, d = .73; 23.44 ± 3.95 to 26.39 ± 4.11 mL/kg−1/min−1), 1-RM BP (p < .001, d = .99; 25.23 ± 10.10 to 35.72 ± 11.09 kg), and 5-RM DL (p = .001, d = 1.15; 36.23 ± 12.35 to 49.05 ± 9.84 kg) (Table 3), whereas handgrip strength was not significant pre- to post-intervention.

DISCUSSION

This study examined the influence of an 8-week HIFT intervention on exercise perceptions and the effects on physiological changes in inactive middle-aged females. Results of the current study demonstrate significant improvements in perceived objective and subjective barriers, and self-efficacy. Additionally, the intervention led to an increase in participants’ daily MVPA and decreases in weekday and weekend day sedentary time. Improvements in HBM constructs suggest HIFT improved participant’s ability to overcome their barriers and improve their self-efficacy to engage in exercise. These changes can potentially be explained by aspects incorporated within the intervention including: 1) scheduled workouts, 2) having workouts led by an instructor/coach, 3) social support, and 4) mastery experience.

The results demonstrate a higher average score of objective barriers (i.e., lack of time, facility or equipment, exercise modality, and workout partner) to exercise at pre-intervention compared to subjective barriers (i.e., lack of motivation and benefits, fear of pain). These findings are similar to previous studies which note lack of time as the most common barrier to exercise (19). It may be likely that the intervention helped participants with time management skills such as scheduling, planning, and prioritizing exercise as it has been shown to significantly predict exercise participation among working mothers (25). Further, the HIFT intervention provided access to a facility, introduced a new modality and support system which potentially alleviated these perceived objective barriers within the 8-week intervention and follow-up. Thus, the continued decrease of objective barriers at follow-up may be explained by the increase in self-efficacy during the intervention, suggesting participants no longer recognize lack of time, facility, modality, or support as a barrier to exercise.

Although subjective barriers were not reported as constraining in this sample, the results show a continued decrease at follow-up. Previous literature demonstrates lack of motivation as a primary subjective barrier towards initial exercise and PA engagement and adherence (22), whereas enjoyment has been suggested to play a role in increasing exercise motivation, PA levels, and self-efficacy (14, 24). Heinrich et al. (18) demonstrated participants engaging in HIFT-based programs elicited higher levels of enjoyment compared to traditional aerobic and resistance training programs. Moreover, more participants engaging in HIFT planned to continue after completion of the intervention compared to an ACSM-consistent program (18). Although the present study did not assess enjoyment specifically, the significant decrease in subjective barriers from pre-intervention to follow-up may be explained by participants’ exercise motivation and enjoyment increasing due to engagement in the HIFT intervention. Therefore, the present study potentially allowed for the participants to become self-motivated and find a modality of exercise they enjoy, thus overcome their barriers to exercise and increase their self-efficacy to continually engage in exercise or PA.

Overcoming barriers to exercise requires a sense of self-efficacy, which has been identified as a fundamental aspect for predicting and improving exercise adherence (3). Simonavice & Wiggins (37) demonstrated participants perceive more barriers to exercise in the early stages of an exercise behavior change than those in later stages. This agrees with present data, as participants’ perceptions of objective and subjective barriers were high pre-intervention, and decreased throughout the 8-week HIFT intervention, while self-efficacy increased. Moreover, it suggests HIFT had an impact on participants to overcome barriers by improving their self-efficacy through social support and mastery experiences involving HIFT.

Supervised, verified, and structured exercise programs administered by professional facilitators (i.e., certified coach) have been shown to elicit greater success towards overall fitness adaptations (26) and in continuing exercise behavior (8). A study comparing supervised resistance training to unsupervised over 12-weeks showed the supervised group had a greater increase in 1-RM of squat and bench press, in addition to a greater rate of increasing training loads than the unsupervised group (26). In the present investigation, there was anecdotal evidence in which the coach would encourage the participants to increase weight or intensity of exercises, thus potentially developing mastery experience and contributing to significant improvements in cardiorespiratory and muscular fitness. Moreover, Cox and colleagues (8) compared the retention and adherence of home- vs. center-initiated exercise intervention in older women, which demonstrated the supervised program enhanced exercise retention and increased long-term PA. Therefore, supervised exercise programs may be an important factor for adherence and maintenance in exercise and PA long-term, in addition to improving fitness. To further improve self-efficacy to engage in exercise, many coach-led programs are typically in a group setting, which can add further support to overcome any barriers.

Social support has been shown to positively affect an individual’s confidence in exercise (28). Previous research has demonstrated that during structured exercise programs, social support from coparticipants and friends positively influences older adults’ adherence to those programs (7). Similarly, Whiteman-Sandland et al. (38) measured the sense of community in a HIFT-based gym compared to a traditional gym and found HIFT-based gyms have greater social support due to group training, thus creating a supportive, community environment. In the current investigation, the HIFT-intervention was a group-styled class where the participants could interact socially and develop a tight-knit community. Increasing self-efficacy through social support while utilizing HIFT can potentially increase exercise engagement and long-term adherence.

Similarly, mastery experience involved within an activity has been suggested to develop self-efficacy (2). For example, a previous study demonstrated new participants (< 6 months experience) engaging in HIFT-based program reported higher mastery-based goals (i.e., achieving task-based competence) (33). In the present study, participants were able to scale the exercises (e.g., exercises that require less skill and are effective given an individual’s capabilities) within the intervention, which allowed for exercises to suit a variety of skill levels. Anecdotally, improvements in functionality, form, and difficulty were observed by the NSCA certified coach throughout the 8-week HIFT intervention, which led to an increase in measures of strength, thus suggesting the participants increased their skill levels. The scalability of HIFT and increase in participant’s skill level allowed for the development of mastery experience, therefore potentially increasing their motivation and self-efficacy towards exercise.

Although there is evidence within the present study that HIFT improves perceived barriers and self-efficacy towards exercise, the results show self-efficacy slightly decreased from post-intervention follow-up. Moreover, MVPA and sedentary time were heading towards pre-intervention values. This may be explained by participants no longer having access to a facility, the HIFT-exercise program, or social support following the intervention, thus potentially establishing new barriers to exercise. Similarly, Fennel et al. (13) suggested previously sedentary individuals may not be equipped to exercise independently and need continued support from a supervised program. However, in the present investigation it is worth noting, although MVPA was not significant at 4-week follow-up, participants, on average, engaged in more MVPA than at pre-intervention. This is potentially a result of the continued decrease in perceived objective and subjective barriers. Regardless, maintaining a high self-efficacy is ideal for individuals to continue to overcome their barriers and adhere to exercise and PA long-term.

A secondary purpose of the study was to examine physiological adaptations due to HIFT. The results demonstrated HIFT improved cardiorespiratory and muscular fitness among inactive, middle-aged female participants, similar to previous research (4, 12). Therefore, HIFT may provide additional benefits in aerobic and anaerobic adaptations, and muscular fitness compared to more traditional exercise programs (4). Improving and maintaining CRF and muscular fitness in middle-aged females is essential to prevent many chronic diseases, including obesity and CVD, and improve overall health and well-being (23).

There were several strengths within the study. First, this is the first study to utilize the HBM (39) within HIFT, offering a novel approach to understand the influencers on exercise adoption and adherence in middle-aged females. Additionally, the study incorporates improvements in CRF and muscular strength. Second, the use of a 4-week follow-up examined adherence and exercise perceptions without ongoing accountability. Third, this study had a low attrition rate, with only one dropout due to non-study-related health issues and an 84% adherence rate. Fourth, the use of an individualized walking V̇O2max protocol utilizing the PA-R scale provided a valid and reliable measurement of CRF. Fifth, the HIFT intervention incorporated all six of the functional movements preventing repetitiveness and potentially fostering mastery experience. Lastly, each HIFT workout session was supervised and verified by an all-female research team.

A primary limitation of the study is the small sample size; however, as a pilot study it may provide a foundation for the development of larger scale studies. Additionally, pre- and post-testing for maximal oxygen consumption and strength assessments were not on the same days of the menstrual cycle which could have subsequent effects on exercise capacity and performance due to fluctuation in hormones (e.g., progesterone and estrogen).

The present study supports the idea that a guided HIFT intervention positively influences middle-aged female’s exercise perceptions by decreasing their perceived barriers and increasing their self-efficacy. Key elements such as scheduled, coach-led workouts, social support, and mastery experiences may have attributed to sufficient improvements towards these perceptions. Additionally, HIFT provided adequate physiological improvements in this sample similar to previous literature. As such, group-based HIFT-programs may be an effective exercise modality for middle-aged females, as fostering supportive relationships, enhancing self-efficacy, and developing mastery skills provide a foundation for optimal and sustained PA engagement. Further understanding of primary barriers to exercise for middle-aged females will help develop population-specific strategies, education, and training programs, thus improve their ability to adopt and adhere to exercise and increase long-term PA. Further understanding of primary barriers to exercise for middle-aged females will help develop population-specific strategies, education, and training programs to promote exercise adoption, sustainability, and long-term PA. Future research should implement an educational component focused on HBM constructs, especially for self-efficacy, within HIFT to explore if there is a greater impact on exercise beliefs and long-term PA. Moreover, additional follow-up periods should be implemented to assess when exercise perceptions return to baseline levels. While further research is needed, this study is the first to demonstrate that HIFT has a significant impact on exercise perceptions, specifically using the HBM, in inactive, middle-aged females. The inclusion of the HBM within HIFT may aid in creating educational and wellness programs to target population-specific barriers to exercise, emphasize long-term PA, and increase overall health and wellness in middle-aged females.
