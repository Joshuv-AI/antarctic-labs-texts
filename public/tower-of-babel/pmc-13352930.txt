Combining receptor engineering and intracellular gelation for cell-based antiviral therapy against coxsackievirus B3
Authors: Tonggong Liu, Wenjuan Liu, Ziyuan Peng, Jie Wen, Shengjie Wu, Xiaona Zhao, Jingzhe Wang, Dayong Gu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Viral myocarditis (VMC), caused by pathogens such as coxsackievirus B3 (CVB3), leads to severe cardiac injury and currently lacks specific therapeutic options. Here, we report a biomimetic antiviral strategy based on receptor engineering and intracellular gelation. By combining genetic and protein engineering, we generated a high-affinity Coxsackievirus and adenovirus receptor mutant (Mut-1_CAR) that markedly enhances the binding of host cardiomyocytes to CVB3. Using photochemical crosslinking, these engineered cells were converted into structurally stable, function-retaining gelated cells (PMs). PMs efficiently adsorb and neutralize virus particles, significantly reducing CVB3 plaque formation in vitro. In a murine model of viral myocarditis, PMs demonstrated excellent in vivo safety and biocompatibility while effectively lowering viral load and mitigating myocardial injury. This study establishes a “receptor enhancement + function fixation” approach for non-immune-dependent viral neutralization, providing a conceptual and technical foundation for the development of novel cell-based biomimetic antiviral therapies.Graphical Abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12951-026-04559-z.

Introduction

Viral myocarditis (VMC) is characterized by focal or diffuse inflammation of the myocardium, with lymphocytic infiltration and cardiomyocyte necrosis as its primary pathological features. The global incidence is estimated at approximately 10–22 cases per 100,000 individuals [1]. VMC can be triggered by a variety of pathogens, among which Coxsackievirus B3 (CVB3), parvovirus B19 (PVB19), SARS-CoV-2, and adenoviruses (AdV) are common etiological agents, with CVB3 infection representing the predominant cause across all age groups [2]. Current therapeutic strategies for VMC mainly focus on three core approaches: (1) inhibition of viral replication; (2) modulation of aberrant immune responses; and (3) reduction of myocardial workload through structured rest protocols. Representative antiviral interventions include small-molecule inhibitors, such as pleconaril, which block viral entry by binding to the viral capsid [3]; antiviral protein therapeutics, including ribavirin; and ubiquitination inhibitors targeting host proteins essential for viral replication [4, 5]. However, these agents frequently exhibit off-target effects and limited specificity, often resulting in significant toxicity [6]. Recently, strategies based on antibodies and soluble receptor analogs (SRAs) have emerged as promising approaches for CVB3 therapy. These agents neutralize the virus prior to its interaction with host cell receptors, thereby preventing attachment and internalization, and display high specificity [7, 8]. Nevertheless, the high mutation rate of viruses and the complexity of co-infections can diminish their antiviral efficacy. In addition, antibody-based therapeutics are costly and time-consuming to produce, limiting their adaptability to emerging viral variants. Interferons, another class of key therapeutic molecules, exhibit broad-spectrum antiviral and immunomodulatory activities but are often constrained by adverse effects in clinical use [9]. More recently, biological and cellular therapies, such as mesenchymal stem cell transplantation, have shown potential by inhibiting CVB3 infection through intrinsic antiviral mechanisms, though their long-term safety and efficacy remain to be fully established [10]. Collectively, despite the exploration of multiple treatment strategies, achieving high therapeutic efficacy while minimizing side effects remains a major challenge in the management of VMC.

CVB3 belongs to the genus Enterovirus within the family Picornaviridae. Its genome consists of a single-stranded positive-sense RNA (ssRNA) of approximately 7.4 kb, encoding four structural proteins (VP1-VP4) and seven nonstructural proteins (2 A-2 C, 3 A-3D) [11]. Nonstructural proteins 2 A-3D are generally recognized as the primary pathogenic factors driving tissue pathology during infection. Notably, proteases 2 A and 3 C mediate the self-cleavage of the viral polyprotein precursor and disrupt host cellular homeostasis, while protein 2B associates with cellular membranes and contributes to the formation of replication-related vesicles [12, 13]. The structural proteins VP1–VP4 assemble in a highly ordered manner to form the viral capsid, mediating recognition and binding to host cell membranes and facilitating the release of viral genetic material. VP1 has also been reported to modulate the host cell cycle, thereby promoting efficient viral protein expression [14]. CVB3 infection is initiated by specific interactions between the virus and host cell receptors, relying primarily on the Coxsackievirus and adenovirus receptor (CAR) and, secondarily, on decay-accelerating factor (CD55) [15]. Importantly, certain CVB3 strains can initiate infection solely through CAR [16]. Upon CAR engagement, the virus undergoes internalization and delivers its positive-sense RNA into the host cytoplasm, initiating the viral replication cycle. Based on this mechanism, CAR-targeted antiviral and gene therapy strategies—including soluble CAR proteins, CAR-targeting antibodies, and engineered approaches to modulate CAR activity have been extensively explored and shown potential therapeutic value [8, 16–18]. With the advent of biomimetic nanomedicine, cell membrane–derived formulations have emerged as promising platforms for viral neutralization [19, 20]. However, their neutralization efficiency is highly dependent on the intrinsic affinity between host cells and the virus, limiting precise control. Moreover, membrane isolation and reconstitution processes often result in the loss of critical membrane proteins or inversion of membrane architecture, significantly impairing viral adsorption [21]. Therefore, next-generation biomimetic cells that preserve intact membrane structures while maintaining high functionalization are highly desirable as efficient viral neutralization platforms.

This study aims to artificially modulate the affinity between CVB3 and host cells through genetic and protein engineering, and to transform the engineered host cells into “nanosponges” for viral neutralization (Fig. 1). Our previous work demonstrated that, compared with conventional biomimetic nanoparticles derived from isolated cell membranes, gelated cells (GCs) prepared via intracellular gelation exhibit superior in vivo biocompatibility and enhanced therapeutic efficacy [22]. It is well established that tissue susceptibility to CVB3 infection is largely determined by interactions between the virus and host receptors, primarily CAR and CD55 [23]. Accordingly, we employed lentiviral vectors to achieve high-level expression of CAR in the HL-1 cardiomyocyte cell line. The engineered cells exhibited markedly increased susceptibility to CVB3 infection. To further refine viral recognition, we used molecular dynamics simulations to analyze the interaction between CAR and the viral structural proteins, identifying key hotspot residues critical for binding. Based on these insights, we performed comprehensive amino acid saturation mutagenesis at these hotspots and subsequent dynamic simulations, leading to the design of a high-affinity CAR mutant. GCs incorporating this mutant (GCs-PM) efficiently adsorbed and neutralized CVB3, demonstrating substantially enhanced antiviral activity in vitro. In a murine model of viral myocarditis, GCs-PM also conferred superior protection against CVB3-induced cardiac injury. This strategy achieves antiviral protection via a non-direct inhibition mechanism, providing a novel approach for constructing controllable, rapid, and effective antiviral therapies. Importantly, the platform is highly versatile: by selecting appropriate host cells or optimizing receptor sequences, it can be rapidly adapted to target diverse or emerging pathogens, offering a framework for precise intervention against unknown viruses (e.g., “Pathogen X”).

Results and discussion

Molecular dynamics analysis of CVB3-CAR interactions

In the context of biomimetic cell nanosponges, it is widely recognized that abundant cell-surface receptors play a critical role in viral recognition and neutralization. CVB3 entry into host cells primarily depends on its specific interaction with the CAR. Therefore, enhancing CAR expression on the host cell surface and increasing its binding affinity for CVB3 constituted a central goal of this study. We used AlphaFold3 to predict the complex structure of CVB3 major structural proteins (VP1–VP3) bound to CAR (Fig. 2a) [24], and conducted 100-ns molecular dynamics (MD) simulations to evaluate complex stability via root-mean-square deviation (RMSD), radius of gyration (Rg), and root-mean-square fluctuation (RMSF) (Fig. 2b-d) [25]. The RMSD analysis revealed minor fluctuations during the initial 1–3 ns, followed by the formation of a first stable conformation between 5 and 25 ns. After further structural adjustment, the complex reached a second stable state that persisted for the remainder of the simulation. Free energy landscape analysis confirmed the presence of two relatively stable energy minima, indicating that the system can interconvert between two stable conformations at equilibrium. Rg analysis suggested that the complex maintained high compactness, with an average Rg of approximately 3.76 nm. RMSF results highlighted regions of increased flexibility within CAR at the binding interface, suggesting local conformational adjustments during complex formation. To quantify the binding strength and guide subsequent mutagenesis, we calculated the binding free energy of the CVB3-CAR complex using the molecular mechanics/poisson boltzmann surface area (MM/PBSA) approach (Fig. 2e) [26]. The Gibbs free energy at equilibrium (final 10 ns) was − 30.20 kcal/mol, indicating stable binding. Using a 6 Å cutoff to define the binding interface and performing alanine scanning (Fig. 2f), we identified key hotspot residues with ΔΔG > 0.5 kcal/mol. Nine residues (PRO25, PRO47, GLU48, VAL70, LEU73, TYR80, TYR83, LYS123, and PRO126) showed increased binding free energy upon alanine substitution, indicating their critical role in complex formation. Notably, the TYR83 mutation caused a ΔΔG of 2.6 kcal/mol, highlighting its significant contribution to stabilizing the interaction. In contrast, substitutions at SER21 and GLU27 enhanced binding, suggesting these residues may exert a repulsive or anti-binding effect during complex formation. Based on these insights, we performed saturation mutagenesis at the nine key residues (Fig. 2g) to identify optimal amino acids for enhanced binding, and verified predictions via complementary shielding mutations. Two CAR mutants with distinct binding characteristics were ultimately constructed (Figure S1A): a high-affinity mutant (Mut-1_CAR: PRO25> GLU, PRO47> PHE, GLU48> ARG, VAL70> GLU, LEU73> TRP, LYS123> ARG, PRO126> TRP) and an antagonistic mutant with reduced binding (Mut-2_CAR: PRO25> ARG, PRO47> LYS, GLU48> GLY, VAL70> PRO, LEU73> GLY, TYR80> GLU, TYR83> GLY, PRO126> ARG).

Structural optimization and molecular dynamics validation of CAR mutants

To further evaluate the impact of targeted mutations on CAR-CVB3 interactions, we generated three-dimensional structural models of two CAR mutants using AlphaFold3 (Figure S1B). Superimposition of the wild-type and mutant structures in PyMOL revealed minimal conformational deviations, with backbone RMSD values below 1 Å, indicating that the introduced mutations did not compromise overall protein structural stability. Subsequently, we constructed complex models of CVB3 major structural proteins (VP1-VP3) with each CAR mutant (Figure S2A-S2B) and performed 100-ns molecular dynamics (MD) simulations. All complexes exhibited similar RMSD trajectories: minor fluctuations were observed during the initial 1–5 ns, after which the systems stabilized with RMSD values consistently between 1.0 and 1.2 nm (Figure S2C–S2J), suggesting structural stability at equilibrium.To quantify the effect of the mutations on binding affinity, we computed binding free energies at the equilibrium phase using the MM/PBSA approach (Fig. 3b, d). The total binding free energy of the CVB3-Mut-1_CAR complex was − 53.24 kcal/mol, whereas CVB3-Mut-2_CAR showed − 34.35 kcal/mol. Compared to the wild-type CAR complex (Fig. 3e-f), both mutants exhibited a single, well-defined energy minimum, corresponding to a dominant binding conformation (Fig. 3a, c).Together, free energy calculations and MD analyses demonstrate that Mut-1_CAR exhibits enhanced binding affinity and higher conformational stability in complex with CVB3 (Fig. 3g), validating the rationality of the mutation design. These results further confirm that optimization of key CAR interface residues can substantially strengthen viral binding, providing a theoretical foundation for the subsequent engineering of host cells.

Construction of CAR-enhanced cells and assessment of CVB3 infection susceptibility

To achieve robust expression of wild-type and mutant CAR proteins, we generated three lentiviral vectors encoding wild-type CAR, Mut-1_CAR, and Mut-2_CAR. HL-1 cardiomyocytes were transduced with these viruses under identical conditions (MOI = 50) and selected with puromycin, yielding three stable cell lines: HL-1/CAR, HL-1/Mut-1, and HL-1/Mut-2. Real-time quantitative PCR analysis confirmed the presence of human CAR mRNA in all three engineered cell lines, whereas no signal was detected in untransduced HL-1 cells (Fig. 4a). Western blot analysis further verified that CAR protein was absent in wild-type HL-1 cells but markedly elevated in the transduced cell lines, demonstrating successful exogenous gene expression (Fig. 4b).Cell viability and apoptosis assessed by 7-AAD/PI dual staining indicated no significant differences between transduced and control cells (Fig. 4c-d), suggesting that lentiviral modification did not compromise cellular viability. Immunofluorescence analysis (Cy3-labeled CAR antibody) confirmed high CAR protein expression in all engineered cell lines, markedly exceeding that of untransduced HL-1 cells (Fig. 4e).To evaluate the effect of CAR and its mutants on CVB3 susceptibility, HL-1, the three engineered cell lines, and empty-vector controls were incubated with 100 TCID₅₀ of CVB3 for 2 h. Microscopic observation revealed pronounced cytopathic effects in HL-1/CAR and HL-1/Mut-1 cells at 24 h post-infection, with extensive cell death; by 48 h, HL-1/Mut-1 cells were almost completely lysed, leaving only cellular debris, while HL-1/CAR cells also exhibited severe damage. In contrast, wild-type HL-1 and HL-1/Mut-2 cells remained morphologically intact (Fig. 5a). Consistently, CVB3 RNA quantification at 12 h post-infection showed the highest viral load in HL-1/Mut-1 cells, indicating enhanced viral susceptibility (Fig. 5b). Dose-dependent infection experiments further demonstrated that HL-1/CAR and HL-1/Mut-1 cells exhibited a similar trend of increasing cytopathic effect with higher viral titers, whereas HL-1 and HL-1/Mut-2 cells displayed no significant growth changes (Fig. 5c). Plaque assays revealed significantly more viral plaques in the CAR and Mut-1 groups compared to controls, whereas the Mut-2 group showed no appreciable difference from wild-type HL-1 cells (Fig. 5d, S8A). Taken together, short-term CVB3 infection has minimal cytotoxicity in wild-type HL-1 cells, while overexpression of CAR or Mut-1_CAR substantially enhances viral binding and entry, with Mut-1_CAR exhibiting the strongest affinity. Conversely, the hotspot-masked Mut-2_CAR mutant diminishes viral binding. These results demonstrate that protein and gene engineering can generate CAR-mutant cells with enhanced viral attachment capacity, providing a foundation for the development of highly efficient virus-neutralizing cell-based nanosponges.

Cell gelation procedure

Cellular gelation was achieved using photochemical crosslinking of polyethylene glycol diacrylate (PEG-DA) with Irgacure 2959 (I2959) to form hydrogels [27]. In our previous studies, this approach was successfully applied intracellularly and thoroughly validated [22]. During freeze-thaw cycles, PEG-DA and I2959 penetrated the cells; subsequent placement on ice followed by ultraviolet (UV) irradiation enabled the formation of GCs. In the absence of UV exposure, HL-1 cells treated with PEG-DA and I2959 failed to form GCs (Figure S3A). Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) revealed that GCs prepared from HL-1 cells (Figure S3B) and from HL-1/Mut-1 cells (GC-PMs) retained normal cellular morphology, whereas cells in the PBS control or non-UV-treated groups appeared as fragmented debris (Fig. 5e). To examine the impact of intracellular gelation on protein composition, SDS-PAGE analysis was performed, showing that protein banding patterns in GCs were consistent with those of live cells (Fig. 5g), indicating negligible protein loss during gelation. Furthermore, co-culturing normal HL-1 cells with varying ratios of GCs demonstrated their excellent biocompatibility (Fig. 5f). The results indicate that the presence of gelated cells at different concentrations does not adversely affect the viability of normal cells. To evaluate viral neutralization, 1 × 10⁶ GCs were incubated with 100 TCID₅₀ of CVB3 for 2 h. Plaque assays showed that all GCs significantly reduced viral plaque formation compared with the positive control (Fig. 5h, S8B). Notably, GC-PMs exhibited superior neutralization efficiency relative to standard GCs and GCs derived from HL-1/CAR cells (GC-Pro). This enhanced effect is attributed to the stronger CVB3-binding capability of HL-1/Mut-1 cells, enabling GC-PM to more effectively capture and neutralize free viral particles, thereby inhibiting infection. To further verify the irreversible protective mechanism of viral adsorption by the gelated cells, 100 TCID₅₀ of CVB3 was pre-incubated with 1 × 10⁶ gelated cells at 37 °C for 4 h. Following low-speed centrifugation (1000 rpm for 5 min) to separate the supernatant from the gelated cell pellet, two parallel infection assays were conducted. First, 100 µL of the supernatant was applied to infect HeLa cells, and intracellular viral RNA was quantified after 24 h (Figure S8C). Second, the virus-bound gelated cell pellet was directly co-cultured with HeLa cells, followed by identical RNA quantification at 24 h (Figure S8D). The experimental results clearly demonstrated that pre-adsorption by the gelated cells significantly reduced the infectivity of the supernatant, as evidenced by a drastic decrease in the viral RNA load. Moreover, HeLa cells directly co-cultured with the virus-bound gelated cells showed no signs of CVB3 infection. Together, these mutually corroborating quantitative results provide direct evidence that the gelated cells effectively deplete infectious virions from the system and tightly sequester them, completely abrogating their capacity to cause secondary infections in target cells.

In vivo safety assessment of gelated cells

Given that cellular therapeutics may induce physiological toxicity and immunogenic responses, evaluating the systemic biocompatibility of GCs is critical. To assess acute toxicity, Balb/c mice were intravenously administered 1 × 10⁶ GCs every two days for a total of four doses. No significant changes in body weight were observed compared with the control group (Fig. 6a). On day 7, defined as the safety assessment endpoint, whole blood and serum samples were collected for hematological and biochemical analyses to evaluate potential systemic toxicity. Hematological analysis revealed no significant differences in red blood cells, lymphocytes, monocytes, neutrophils, hemoglobin, or platelet counts between GC-treated and control mice (Fig. 6b-c), indicating that GCs did not adversely affect hematopoiesis. Serum biochemical assays, including AST, ALT, ALB, UREA, BUN, CREA, and UA, showed no detectable hepatic or renal toxicity (Fig. 6e), suggesting low impact on liver and kidney function. Viral myocarditis typically presents with elevated cardiac enzyme levels, including CK, CK-MB, LDH, and LDH-1 (Fig. 6d). In GCs-treated mice, cardiac enzyme levels were comparable to controls, indicating no myocardial toxicity. Histopathological examination (H&E staining) of major organs—including heart, liver, spleen, lung, and kidney—revealed no evidence of immune cell infiltration, tissue lesions, or structural damage in GCs-treated mice (Fig. 6f). Collectively, these results demonstrate that GCs exhibit excellent short-term in vivo biocompatibility and safety. To further track the in vivo distribution and metabolism of GCs, HL-1 and HL-1/Mut-1 cells were pre-labeled with DiD fluorescence prior to gelation. In vivo fluorescence imaging showed that the control group (free dye) exhibited rapid signal decay within 12 h post-injection. In contrast, fluorescently labeled GCs and GC-PMs displayed slower signal decay and maintained high fluorescence intensity over 72 h (Fig. 6g). Quantitative analysis confirmed a significant difference in metabolic behavior between GCs and free dye in vivo (Fig. 6h). Analysis of biodistribution indicated that free dye was primarily cleared via the liver and partially via the spleen, whereas GCs and GC-PMs initially accumulated in the lungs before gradually being metabolized through hepatic and splenic pathways. This characteristic distribution enables GCs and GC-PMs to efficiently intercept and neutralize circulating viral particles via the highly perfused pulmonary vasculature. Moreover, as lung tissue is not a primary host for CVB3, this distribution pattern may further reduce potential off-target infection. During the acute phase of CVB3-induced viral myocarditis, this pilot safety study demonstrates the favorable short-term in vivo biocompatibility of GCs.

Long-term toxicity and immunogenicity analysis

To further evaluate the clinical translational potential and address concerns regarding long-term biosafety, we extended the observation period and conducted a comprehensive 28-day toxicity and immunogenicity assessment. Following repeated administration of PMs, histological analysis via H&E staining of major organs (heart, liver, spleen, lung, and kidney) revealed no significant pathological changes or chronic inflammatory infiltration compared to the control group (Figure S7J). To systematically profile the systemic immune response, we performed flow cytometry on splenic leukocytes using a panel of markers including CD4+/CD8 + T cells, CD19 + B cells, and F4/80+/CD11b+ macrophages (the gating strategy is detailed in Figure S6). The results demonstrated that the proportions of these immune cell subsets remained within normal physiological ranges, with no aberrant activation or depletion observed (Figure S7A-H). Furthermore, serum levels of immunoglobulins (IgG and IgE) showed no significant elevation, suggesting that the PMs did not trigger a delayed hypersensitivity or a robust anti-cell antibody response (Figure S7I). Collectively, these long-term data complement our acute safety profiles and indicate that the gelated cells possess excellent biocompatibility and low immunogenicity, supporting their safety for sustained therapeutic applications.

Neutralization efficacy of engineered gelated cells in CVB3-infected mice

To further evaluate the antiviral performance of engineered gelated cells (PMs) in vivo, Balb/c mice were assigned to six groups: normal control (NC), CVB3-infected, GC (gelated HL-1 cells), Pro (gelated HL-1/CAR cells), PM (gelated HL-1/Mut-1_CAR cells), and a positive control drug group (ribavirin). All groups received intravenous administration every two days for a total of seven doses, with day 14 set as the experimental endpoint (Fig. 7a). Body weight measurements indicated a gradual increase in the NC group, while CVB3-infected mice exhibited rapid weight loss beginning on day 3 post-infection, reflecting severe viral-induced damage. In contrast, GC, Pro, PM, and ribavirin treatments significantly alleviated weight loss, with the PM group showing the most pronounced recovery (Fig. 7b). To assess antiviral efficacy, CVB3 mRNA levels in cardiac tissues were quantified, and serum cardiac enzyme markers (CK, CK-MB, LDH, and LDH-1) were measured as indicators of myocardial injury. CVB3-infected mice displayed markedly elevated viral loads and cardiac enzyme levels. Treatment with GC, Pro, PM, or ribavirin substantially reduced both viral replication and myocardial injury (Fig. 7c-g). Notably, the viral mRNA expression in the PM treatment group was significantly lower than that in both the PRO group and the Ribavirin treatment group. Furthermore, blood biochemical indicators revealed that the PM treatment successfully restored the mouse myocardial enzyme profile (CK, CK-MB, LDH, and LDH-1) to normal levels. Among these, the serum levels of CK, LDH and LDH-1 in the PM group were significantly lower than those in the PRO and Ribavirin groups, tracking much closer to the physiological baseline of normal, healthy mice. Peripheral blood analyses showed no significant differences across groups, indicating the absence of systemic toxicity (Figure S4). Histopathological analysis further corroborated these findings. H&E staining of major organs (heart, liver, spleen, lung, and kidney) collected at multiple time points revealed that PM treatment effectively mitigated CVB3-induced myocarditis, preserving myocardial fiber architecture and significantly reducing inflammatory cell infiltration (Fig. 7h). Other organs showed no detectable histological abnormalities or tissue damage (Figure S5), confirming the in vivo biocompatibility of both GCs and PMs. Viral myocarditis is frequently accompanied by myocardial fibrosis. To evaluate the antifibrotic effects of PMs, cardiac tissues were subjected to Masson’s trichrome staining at the experimental endpoint. The PM group exhibited well-defined myocardial structure boundaries and substantially lower fibrosis compared with other groups (Fig. 7i), highlighting the superior virus-neutralizing and cardioprotective effects of engineered gelated cells.

Conclusion

In this study, we developed and validated a biomimetic antiviral strategy based on receptor engineering combined with intracellular cell gelation. By employing genetic and protein engineering, we enhanced host cell binding affinity toward CVB3, and through photochemical crosslinking, we achieved in situ intracellular gelation to construct structurally intact and functionally preserved engineered gelated cells (PMs) capable of physically adsorbing and neutralizing viruses. At the molecular level, molecular dynamics simulations and binding free energy analyses elucidated the recognition mechanism between CVB3 and CAR, and a high-affinity CAR mutant (Mut-1_CAR) was identified via saturated mutagenesis, exhibiting a markedly reduced binding free energy with CVB3, confirming the feasibility of structural optimization to improve viral recognition. At the cellular level, Mut-1_CAR-expressing cells showed significantly enhanced susceptibility to CVB3 infection, demonstrating that receptor interface modification can effectively regulate virus-host recognition. At the material level, intracellular gelation transformed living cells into structurally stable, functionally preserved gelated cells (GCs). Incorporating high-affinity CAR mutants, PMs efficiently reduced viral plaque formation in vitro, forming a robust and stable virus-adsorption platform. In vivo, PMs displayed excellent safety and biocompatibility, and effectively reduced viral load and myocardial damage in a CVB3-induced viral myocarditis mouse model, outperforming the conventional antiviral drug ribavirin. Overall, this study demonstrates that engineered gelated cells can neutralize viruses via a “receptor enhancement and functional stabilization” mechanism independent of immune activation, offering broad adaptability and scalability. This platform provides a feasible approach for developing novel cell-based biomimetic antiviral therapies and lays a theoretical foundation for rapid intervention against diverse viruses or emerging pathogens.

The biomimetic “receptor enhancement + function fixation” strategy presented here establishes a robust platform for neutralizing CVB3. To contextualize this work within clinical and nanobiotechnology frameworks, several key mechanistic and translational considerations merit further discussion. Unlike conventional membrane-coated nanoparticles (CNPs), our intracellularly gelated cells (PMs) provide intrinsic advantages in structural integrity and functional presentation. Structurally, PMs employ a 3D PEG-hydrogel network as an “artificial cytoskeleton,” reinforcing the entire cell and conferring superior mechanical stability against physiological shear stress compared with the potential delamination of lipid bilayers on synthetic cores. Importantly, in situ gelation preserves intact cells with 100% “right-side-out” receptor orientation, circumventing the stochastic membrane inversion frequently observed in CNPs generated via extrusion or sonication. This precise topological maintenance, coupled with full retention of the membrane proteome and auxiliary co-receptors, markedly enhances viral capture avidity. Moreover, the antiviral mechanism of PMs extends beyond simple passive adsorption. Traditional materials often rely on reversible electrostatic interactions, whereas PMs exploit the exceptionally strong binding of Mut-1_CAR. Although PMs are biologically inert and do not actively perform ATP-dependent endocytic trapping, the high-density receptor clusters act as a physical “sink,” ensuring effective neutralization until clearance. While our whole-cell-based functional assays provide robust validation of this enhanced viral affinity in a near-physiological context, a limitation of the current study is the absence of direct biophysical quantification (e.g., using Surface Plasmon Resonance) of the isolated purified CAR mutant proteins. Future investigations will incorporate these biophysical techniques to precisely dissect the binding kinetics at the molecular level.

A critical consideration in receptor engineering is the specificity of the enhanced Mut-1_CAR. Since CAR serves as a gateway for multiple pathogens, increased Mut-1_CAR affinity may extend to other CAR-tropic viruses, such as adenoviruses. We interpret this not as an off-target effect but as a potential advantage, allowing PMs to function as versatile interceptors against a spectrum of infections. Additionally, as biologically inert entities, PMs are unlikely to perturb endogenous CAR-mediated signaling, including cell adhesion processes. From a clinical perspective, the PM platform is mechanistically orthogonal to conventional antivirals like ribavirin. While ribavirin functions as an intracellular gatekeeper by inhibiting viral replication, PMs operate as an extracellular shield, sequestering viruses prior to host cell entry. This “dual-gate” strategy suggests combination therapy could confer synergistic benefits, potentially enabling dose reductions of ribavirin and mitigating systemic toxicity.

In our VMC model, PMs localized to the lungs without inducing respiratory distress or histopathological damage at therapeutic doses, likely reflecting the inherent deformability of gelated cells. For other viral contexts, such as respiratory infections, targeted accumulation could be achieved via intratracheal delivery or nebulized inhalation for site-specific neutralization. The versatility of this platform is further highlighted by its adaptability to various cell sources. Beyond HL-1 cells, this approach could be applied to human-derived lines (e.g., HeLa) or hiPSC-derived cardiomyocytes (hiPSC-CMs). The current validation in murine VMC models does not fully recapitulate the complexity of the human cardiac microenvironment and systemic immune responses. Human viral myocarditis often exhibits greater heterogeneity in inflammatory infiltration and a complex transition from acute injury to chronic dilated cardiomyopathy (DCM). Our study primarily focused on the acute neutralization phase, and the host’s systemic clearance of the “PM-virus complexes” via the mononuclear phagocyte system (MPS) might differ between mice and humans. Therefore, further evaluation using “humanized” mouse models or multi-cellular human induced pluripotent stem cell (hiPSC)-derived cardiac organoids is needed to bridge the gap between murine results and clinical efficacy.

Methods

Molecular dynamics simulations and binding energy calculations

To investigate the binding behavior of wild-type and mutant CAR proteins with the structural proteins of CVB3, molecular dynamics (MD) simulations were performed using GROMACS 2025. Three complexes: CVB3-CAR, CVB3-Mut-1_CAR, and CVB3-Mut-2_CAR were constructed based on structural predictions from AlphaFold 3 (https://alphafoldserver.com/). All systems were parameterized using the CHARMM36 all-atom force field and solvated in a periodic cubic box filled with TIP3P water molecules. To mimic physiological conditions, 0.15 M NaCl was added to neutralize the system. Prior to production runs, each complex underwent energy minimization using the steepest-descent algorithm, followed by a two-step equilibration procedure: 100 ps NVT equilibration at 300 K and 100 ps NPT equilibration at 1 bar, during which the protein backbone atoms were restrained. Subsequently, 100 ns production simulations were conducted under NPT ensemble conditions (300 K, 1 bar) without restraints. A 2 fs time step was applied, all covalent bonds were constrained using the LINCS algorithm, and long-range electrostatic interactions were treated with the Particle-Mesh Ewald (PME) method. Trajectories were saved every 10 ps for subsequent analysis. Structural stability and conformational dynamics were evaluated by calculating the RMSD, RMSF, Rg, and hydrogen bond number from the trajectory data. Representative conformations were visualized and analyzed in PyMOL to characterize key intermolecular interactions and conformational transitions within the complexes. To quantitatively assess binding affinities and identify critical interaction residues, the MM/PBSA approach was applied using the gmx_MMPBSA package. A total of 1,000 snapshots were extracted uniformly from the equilibrated phase of each trajectory for calculation. The resulting binding free energies and per-residue energy decompositions provided atomic-level insights into the energetic contributions driving complex formation between CVB3 and CAR variants.

Protein sequence design

Based on the MD trajectories of the CVB3–wild-type CAR complex, we first defined the viral–host binding interface. Specifically, a stable representative conformation was extracted from the equilibrated trajectory, and CAR residues located within 6 Å of the CVB3 structural protein were identified as interface residues. To evaluate the energetic contributions of these residues, per-residue free energy decomposition was performed using the gmx_MMPBSA module. The residues exhibiting the most favorable (i.e., most negative) ΔG values were selected as candidate key binding residues. To further identify binding “hot spots,” computational alanine scanning was conducted. Each candidate residue was individually mutated to alanine, and the corresponding change in binding free energy (ΔΔG) was recalculated. Residues whose alanine substitution led to a significant increase in binding free energy (ΔΔG > 0.5 kcal/mol) were defined as hot-spot residues for subsequent design. For affinity optimization, virtual saturation mutagenesis was performed on each identified hot spot. Each position was systematically substituted with all 19 other natural amino acids, and the resulting binding free energies were estimated using the MM/PBSA approach. For each site, the amino acid yielding the lowest (most negative) binding free energy was selected as the optimal substitution. The combination of these optimal residues formed a high-affinity CAR mutant designed to enhance viral binding, denoted as CVB3-Mut-1. Conversely, amino acid substitutions that resulted in the largest positive ΔΔG values indicative of weakened or disrupted interactions—were combined to construct an antagonistic CAR mutant, designated CVB3-Mut-2.To assess the performance of these designed mutants, complex structures of CVB3-Mut-1_CAR and CVB3-Mut-2_CAR were modeled and subjected to the same MD simulation and MM/PBSA energy decomposition protocols as the wild-type system. By comparing the binding free energies (ΔG) among the wild-type and mutant complexes, we quantitatively evaluated the enhancement or attenuation of binding affinity introduced by the mutations, thereby validating the computational design strategy.

Cell culture

Mouse cardiomyocyte cell line HL-1 and human cervical carcinoma cell line HeLa were purchased from Procell Life Science & Technology Co., Ltd. (Wuhan, China). HL-1 cells were cultured in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM, Gibco, Cat. No. 10567014), while HeLa cells were maintained in Minimum Essential Medium (MEM, Gibco, Cat. No. 11090-081). All culture media were supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, ExCell Bio, Cat. No. FSP500-GS) and 1% penicillin–streptomycin solution (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Cat. No. 15140122). Cells were incubated at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO₂, following the manufacturers’ instructions.

Plasmid construction and lentivirus production

Lentivirus production was carried out by Shanghai GeneChem Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). The human CXADR gene (NM_001338.5) was amplified from a cDNA library (GeneChem, Shanghai, China) using gene-specific primers (Table S1). The PCR-amplified fragment and the lentiviral expression vector GV492 (pGC-FU-3FLAG-CBh-gcGFP-IRES-puromycin; GeneChem) were digested with AgeI and NheI restriction enzymes, followed by assembly using the In-Fusion recombination method. The recombinant plasmid was verified by Sanger sequencing. For lentivirus production, the confirmed plasmid was co-transfected into HEK293T cells together with the packaging plasmids psPAX2 and pMD2.G using Lipofectamine 2000 (Invitrogen, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). After 72 h of transfection, the viral supernatant was harvested, centrifuged to remove cell debris, and filtered through a 0.45 μm cellulose acetate membrane. The viral titer, determined by fluorescence-activated cell sorting (FACS) analysis of GFP-positive 293T cells, reached approximately 1 × 10⁹ transducing units (TU)/mL. The viral stock was aliquoted and stored at -80 °C for subsequent experiments. The resulting lentivirus was used to transduce HL-1 cells, and stable transductants were selected using puromycin (Beyotime, Shanghai, China) according to the manufacturer’s protocol.

Real-time quantitative polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR)

Total RNA was extracted from HL-1 cells and cardiac tissue samples using the FastPure Cell/Tissue Total RNA Isolation Kit V2 (Vazyme, Cat. No. RC112-01) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. RNA concentration and purity were determined using a NanoDrop One spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). Subsequently, 1 µg of total RNA was reverse-transcribed into cDNA using the HiScript IV 1st Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit (Vazyme, Cat. No. R412-01).Real-time quantitative PCR (RT-qPCR) was performed on a QuantStudio 3 Real-Time PCR System (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) using SYBR Green qPCR Master Mix (Accurate Biology, China). All reactions were conducted following the manufacturer’s instructions. Human β-actin (h-β-actin) was used as an internal reference gene, and relative gene expression levels were calculated using the 2^–ΔΔCt method. The sequences of primers used in this study are listed in Table S1.

Western blot analysis

Total protein was extracted from HL-1 cells using a lysis buffer (Beyotime, Jiangsu, China) supplemented with a protease inhibitor cocktail (MCE, USA). Protein concentrations were determined using the Pierce BCA Protein Assay Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). Equal amounts of protein (30 µg) were mixed with loading buffer (Servicebio, China), boiled for 10 min, and separated by SDS–PAGE. The separated proteins were then transferred onto polyvinylidene difluoride (PVDF) membranes (Millipore, USA) using an electrotransfer system. Membranes were blocked with 5% nonfat milk in TBST buffer and incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies, including anti-human CAR antibody (Sino Biological, Cat. No. 10799-R271) and anti-GAPDH antibody (Beyotime, Cat. No. AG019) as a loading control. After three washes with TBST, the membranes were incubated for 80 min at room temperature with HRP-conjugated goat anti-rabbit IgG (H + L) secondary antibody (Beyotime, Cat. No. A0208). Protein bands were visualized using an enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) HRP substrate (Millipore, USA) and detected with a ChemiDoc MP Imaging System (Bio-Rad, USA). For visualization of total protein patterns, SDS–PAGE gels were stained with SolarFast Coomassie Brilliant Blue Staining Solution (ACMEC, Cat. No. AC11919) and destained with deionized water.

Immunofluorescence staining

HL-1 cells were seeded at a density of 1 × 10⁵ cells per well in 1 cm glass-bottom confocal dishes (NEST, Cat. No. 801001). When cells reached approximately 80% confluence, they were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 30 min at room temperature, followed by permeabilization with 0.1% Triton X-100 (APPLYGEN, Cat. No. A1009) for 10 min. After washing three times with PBS containing 0.1% Triton X-100, the cells were incubated overnight at 4 °C with appropriate primary antibodies. The samples were then counterstained with DAPI for nuclear visualization and examined under a laser scanning confocal microscope (Leica Microsystems, Germany).

Flow cytometry analysis

Cell apoptosis was assessed by flow cytometry using an Annexin V-PE/7-AAD Apoptosis Detection Kit. Cells were harvested, washed twice with PBS, and resuspended at a concentration of 1 × 10⁵ cells per 100 µL. Then, 5 µL of Annexin V-PE and 5 µL of 7-AAD were added to each sample, followed by incubation for 10 min at room temperature in the dark. Samples were analyzed using a flow cytometer (BD Biosciences, USA). Cells positive for both Annexin V and 7-AAD were considered late apoptotic or necrotic.

Peripheral blood was collected from mice via retro-orbital venous plexus puncture into EDTA-coated anticoagulant tubes. A total of 100 µL whole blood was transferred into a 15 mL centrifuge tube and incubated with 10 mL red blood cell (RBC) lysis buffer on ice for 10 min. Samples were centrifuged at 1500 rpm for 5 min, and the supernatant was discarded. The cell pellet was washed twice with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) (1500 rpm, 5 min each). The remaining mononuclear cells were collected for subsequent flow cytometric staining. Spleens were aseptically removed from mice and placed on a 70 μm cell strainer positioned in a 6 cm culture dish. Spleens were mechanically dissociated using the plunger of a 5 mL syringe. Cells were flushed through the strainer with 10 mL PBS II (PBS supplemented with 1% bovine serum albumin, BSA) and collected into a 15 mL centrifuge tube.An aliquot (10 µL) was used for cell counting. The remaining cell suspension was centrifuged at 1500 rpm for 5 min at 4 °C, and the supernatant was discarded. Cells were gently resuspended and adjusted to a concentration of 1 × 10⁸ cells/mL in PBS II. A total of 50 µL cell suspension was used for subsequent flow cytometric staining. Mice were euthanized by cervical dislocation. Five milliliters of sterile saline was injected intraperitoneally and allowed to dwell for 5 min. The abdomen was gently massaged, and the peritoneal lavage fluid was collected. Samples were centrifuged at 1500 rpm for 5 min, and the supernatant was discarded. The cell pellet was resuspended and counted. A total of 50 µL cell suspension was used for flow cytometric staining. Cells derived from peripheral blood, spleen, or peritoneal lavage were incubated with pre-mixed fluorochrome-conjugated antibodies at 4 °C for 30 min in the dark. Following incubation, cells were centrifuged at 1500 rpm for 5 min and washed twice with PBS. After the final wash, cells were resuspended in 500 µL staining buffer and filtered prior to acquisition.

Cell viability assay (CCK-8 and luminescence analysis)

HL-1 cells were seeded into 96-well plates at a density of 5 × 10³ cells per well, and co-cultured with GCs at varying ratios (1:10, 1:1, and 10:1). Cell viability was assessed at 0, 12, 24, and 48 h using a Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8; MCE, Shanghai, China). At each time point, 10 µL of CCK-8 reagent was added to each well and incubated for 2 h at 37 °C. The absorbance at 450 nm was measured using a BioTek Gen5 microplate reader (BioTek Instruments, Vermont, USA). The relative cell viability was normalized to the untreated control group. Because viral particles can interfere with CCK-8 colorimetric detection, cell viability following CVB3 infection at various multiplicities of infection (MOIs) was evaluated using a BacTiter-Lumi™ Plus II Luminescent Cell Viability Assay Kit (Beyotime, Cat. No. C0050S). Luminescence signals were recorded with a Spark 20 M multimode microplate reader (TECAN, Männedorf, Switzerland).

Cell gelation

HL-1 cells were seeded in 15 cm culture dishes and grown to approximately 80% confluence. The culture medium was removed, and the cells were washed twice with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS). Subsequently, 10 mL of a prepolymer solution containing 10% (w/v) poly(ethylene glycol) diacrylate (PEGDA; Aladdin, 26570-48-9) and 0.1% (w/v) photoinitiator I2959 (Aladdin, 106797-53-9) was added. The cells were frozen at − 80 °C for 10 min, followed by rapid thawing in a 37 °C water bath. After removing the supernatant, the samples were exposed to 6 W ultraviolet light for 15 min to induce photo-crosslinking and form gelled cell constructs. The resulting gelled cell aggregates were collected by gentle mechanical scraping, washed twice with PBS, and resuspended at a final concentration of 1 × 10⁶ cells/mL in PBS for storage at 4 °C until use. The morphology and microstructure of the gelled cells were characterized using scanning electron microscopy (SEM, SU8100, Hitachi, Japan). SEM imaging was performed by Servicebio Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Wuhan, China).

Virus plaque assay

The infectivity of the virus and the in vitro viral adsorption capacity of gelled cells were evaluated using a virus plaque assay. HeLa cells were seeded into 6-well plates at a density of 1 × 10⁶ cells per well and cultured until reaching approximately 90% confluence. The monolayers were then infected with 100 TCID₅₀ of CVB3 or with CVB3 viral suspensions that had been pre-incubated with gelled cells for 2 h. After infection, the cells were washed five times with PBS to remove unbound virus. To restrict viral spread, the cells were overlaid with solid medium containing 3% agarose and incubated for 60–84 h, until distinct cytopathic effects (CPE) were observed in the positive control group. Subsequently, each well was fixed with 1 mL of 4% paraformaldehyde at room temperature overnight. The solid overlay was gently removed under running tap water, and the monolayers were stained with 1 mL of 1% crystal violet solution for 20 min at room temperature. The plates were then washed 2–3 times with PBS, and images were captured for plaque counting and analysis.

Fluorescent labeling of gelled cells for in vivo biodistribution

To track the in vivo distribution of gelled cells (GCs) in mice, the cells were labeled using the Beyo3D™ Cell Membrane Far-Red Fluorescent Staining Kit (Beyotime, C1423S). The DiD fluorescent dye specifically integrates into the cell membrane, with an excitation wavelength of 644 nm and an emission wavelength of 665 nm. On day 0, 5 × 10⁵ DiD-labeled GCs suspended in 150 µL PBS were administered via intravenous injection. Subsequently, in vivo fluorescence imaging was performed at 0, 12, 24, 48, 72, and 120 h post-injection to monitor real-time biodistribution. At each time point, major organs including the heart, liver, spleen, lung, and kidney were collected and analyzed for fluorescence signals to assess the organ-specific accumulation of GCs.

Animal experiments

Animal care and ethics: Female Balb/c mice (5–6 weeks old) were purchased from Zhiyuan Biopharma Technology Co., Ltd. (Guangzhou, China). All animal procedures were approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of Shenzhen Second People’s Hospital and conducted in accordance with the regulations of the Ministry of Health of the People’s Republic of China on the management of laboratory animals. In vivo safety evaluation: To assess the biocompatibility of GCs, mice were intravenously injected with 1 × 10⁶ GCs in 100 µL PBS every other day for a total of four doses. Peripheral blood (150 µL) was collected via cardiac puncture into EDTA-coated tubes for complete blood count (CBC) analysis. An additional 500 µL of whole blood was collected in clot-promoting tubes, allowed to clot, and centrifuged at 3000 rpm to obtain serum for biochemical assays. BALB/c mice were randomly divided into three groups (n = 5 per group): normal control (NC), low-dose group (0.1 × 10⁶ GCs), and high-dose group (1 × 10⁶ GCs). Mice were intravenously injected via the tail vein once every 4 days. The experimental endpoint was set at Day 28. At the endpoint, whole blood, spleens, and peritoneal lavage fluid were collected for flow cytometric analysis to evaluate immune cell populations. Detailed information regarding the antibodies used for flow cytometry is provided in Table S2. Serum samples were isolated for the measurement of IgG and IgE levels to further assess systemic immunogenicity.In addition, major organs, including the heart, liver, spleen, lung, and kidney, were harvested for hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining and histopathological examination to evaluate potential long-term toxicity. In vivo antiviral efficacy against CVB3-induced viral myocarditis (VMC): Thirty mice were randomly divided into six groups: normal control (NC), CVB3 infection, GC (gelled HL-1 cells), Pro (gelled HL-1/CAR cells), PM (gelled Mut-1_CAR cells), and Ribavirin treatment. On day 0, except for the NC group, each mouse was intraperitoneally injected with 1 × 10⁵ TCID₅₀ CVB3 and body weight was recorded. Beginning on day 2, treatments were administered via tail vein injection every other day, following the same blood and tissue collection scheme as described for the safety evaluation. Histological analysis: Heart, liver, spleen, lung, and kidney tissues were collected, fixed, and subjected to Hematoxylin–Eosin (H&E) and Masson’s trichrome staining. All staining and slide preparation were performed by Saville Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Wuhan, China). Histological images were analyzed using SlideViewer software.

Statistical analysis

Comparisons between two groups were performed using Student’s t-test. For comparisons among three or more groups, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was applied, and two-way ANOVA was used when analyzing two independent variables simultaneously. Statistical significance was defined as P ≤ 0.05, P ≤ 0.01, and P ≤ 0.001. All experiments were independently repeated at least three times, and data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD).
