Data balancing improves mortality prediction for emergency department patients
Authors: Chinyang Henry Tseng, Yu-Sheng Lo, Yu-Juin Lin, Yi-Hsueh Liao, Chin-Hsin Tai, Ray-Jade Chen, Juinn-Yih Wu, Hsin-Che Chien, Kuan-Han Wu, Shy-Shin Chang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAccurate patient mortality prediction is crucial in the emergency department (ED) to improve emergency healthcare services. Current prediction models are limited in both accuracy and practicality, particularly in identifying high-risk patients early. Machine Learning is deeply affected by data quality because mortality samples are significantly fewer than survival samples. This study aimed to achieve balanced and better accuracy of patient mortality prediction and evaluate the effectiveness of data balancing methods.MethodsThis study analyzed 2,437,341 non-traumatic adult ED visit records collected between 2008 and 2016 from five medical centers in Taiwan, including four mortality timeframes: death within 24, 72, and 168 h, and final death, and evaluated three data balancing methods: Random Under Sampling (RUS), Synthesized Minority Oversampling Technique (SMOTE), and Random Over Sampling (ROS). We adopted Random Forest (RF), AdaBoost (ADA), XG Boost (XGB). Logistic Regression (LR) is the meta learner for these models. Besides, we performed feature importance analysis based on RF, ADA, AdaBoost with BootStrap (ADA-BS), and Information Gain (IG).ResultsOur model with XGB achieved the best AUROC, 91.41%, which is better than 90.2% in the previous study by Wu et al. using the same dataset in 168-hour mortality timeframe. Our True Positive Rate (TPR) and True Negative Rate (TNR) are 79.88% and 86.73%, which are more balanced than 25% and 100% in the previous study. ROS achieves the better results than RUS and SMOTE and becomes our primary data balancing method. While adopting XGB in 24-hour mortality timeframes, ROS achieved the best AUROC, 93.72%, RUS achieved 93.61% and SMOTE achieved 91.73%. Compared with the previous study by Lin et al., the feature importance analysis shows our balanced dataset has better feature importance impacts, especially for the “Age” and “Triage” features.ConclusionOur method achieves better AUROC than the previous study, especially in the long challenging death-hour mortality timeframe with XGB and ROS. Our method achieves balanced TPR and TNR, which are more practical than AUROC. Besides, feature importance analysis shows our balanced dataset has better feature importance impacts.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12911-026-03608-9.

Introduction

Mortality rates in emergency departments (EDs) have long been a significant challenge for healthcare systems worldwide. Studies indicate that mortality rates in EDs are closely linked to acuity, availability of medical resources, and level of overcrowding. A retrospective study conducted at Tikur Anbesa Specialized Hospital in Ethiopia [1] showed that adult ED mortality rates rose steadily over a two-year period, illustrating the gravity of the issue and its burden on healthcare systems [2]. In particular, a significant association is reported between the length of stay (LOS) in the emergency room (ER) and mortality rates for elderly patients. Studies have reported that mortality rates increase significantly after elderly patients spend the night in the ER, emphasizing the importance of prompt and effective patient management in emergency settings [3–6]. Additionally, ER overcrowding increases the 10-day mortality rate for non-critical patients, further emphasizing the importance of improving ED processes and resource allocation [4]. A Swiss study found that the mortality rates of patients who experienced out-of-hospital cardiac arrest were influenced by various factors after being admitted to the ED, highlighting the critical role of providing timely and effective care in reducing mortality [5]. Similarly, research conducted at Jimma University Medical Center, Ethiopia, revealed correlations between early mortality and various factors, supporting early identification of high-risk patients [6]. Results of these previous studies highlight the need to improve mortality prediction for ED patients, since early identification of high-risk patients significantly improves healthcare outcomes in fast-paced and crowded emergency environments. With the continuous development of machine learning (ML) technologies, these techniques are being widely applied to clinical prediction for ED patients. A systematic review by Naemi et al. [7] investigated the challenges and opportunities of various techniques in practical applications, providing a comprehensive background for future research. Zhai et al. [8] focused on using ML tools to predict outcomes for intensive care unit (ICU) patients, revealing the potential value of ML for identifying high-risk populations.

In the context of triage in EDs, study results demonstrate that ML models effectively predict clinical outcomes, providing more accurate decision support tools for emergency care [9]. Additionally, a systematic review and meta-analysis by Kareemi et al. [10] indicated that ML models generally outperform conventional clinical methods in diagnostic and prognostic prediction within EDs.

Additionally, Lin et al. [11] developed an ML monitoring system that performed exceptionally well in predicting mortality for patients with non-cancer terminal liver disease. That system combined several ML models, including Random Forest (RF) and AdaBoost (ADA), achieving high accuracy and stability in mortality prediction, and providing strong empirical support for the application of ML technologies in emergency departments.

Similarly, Wu et al. [12] compared the performance of a modified Early Warning Score (MEWS) and ML methods in predicting mortality for non-traumatic ED patients. Results of that study indicated that ML models significantly outperformed MEWS in predicting in-hospital mortality, particularly in delayed mortality prediction, further demonstrating the potential and value of ML technologies in EDs.

Sánchez-Salmerón et al. [13] reviewed the application of machine learning methods in emergency services, highlighting the potential and challenges of these techniques in triage systems The use of ML-based models to predict early mortality in ERs further confirmed the importance of ML technologies in the early identification of high-risk patients [14]. Artificial intelligence (AI) models have also shown promising results in predicting trauma-related mortality in ER patients, expanding the scope of ML applications in emergency mortality prediction [15]. Furthermore, ML technologies have been used to predict mortality and readmission rates for hospitalized patients with heart disease, providing valuable insight for emergency healthcare applications [16]. Artificial neural network models demonstrated excellent performance in predicting 90-day mortality of patients with acute liver failure, further emphasizing the broad potential of ML technologies in clinical applications [17].

Previous studies have also shown the versatility of ML technologies in EDs, and, in particular, Lin et al. [11] and Wu et al. [12] conducted in-depth comparisons and analyses of mortality prediction, laying a solid theoretical and empirical foundation for the present study.

In the present study, the application of data balancing techniques was necessary due to extreme imbalance in the experimental dataset. The issue of imbalanced data is a common and challenging problem in training ML models, particularly in mortality prediction models within healthcare settings [18]. Researchers have proposed various data balancing methods to address this issue, including Random Under Sampler (RUS), Random Over Sampler (ROS), and Synthetic Minority Over-sampling Technique (SMOTE), each of which has been used effectively to handle imbalanced data [19]. RUS achieves balance by reducing the number of majority class samples, but this may lead to information loss and model oversimplification [20]. ROS increases the number of minority class samples to avoid information loss; however, this may also introduce the risk of overfitting [21]. In contrast, SMOTE is a more advanced method that generates synthetic samples between existing samples to balance the dataset, aiming to enhance the model’s generalization ability. However, in some cases, it may introduce lower-quality synthetic samples, potentially reducing its effectiveness in mitigating overfitting [22].

Each of these methods has its own applicable range and limitations. For example, RUS may lose critical information, ROS may introduce redundancy in samples, and SMOTE may introduce lower-quality synthetic samples [18]. Therefore, the most suitable data balancing method must be chosen based on characteristics of the dataset and the specific application. The present study aimed to compare the effectiveness of these methods in predicting ED mortality, with the ultimate goal of improving model accuracy and stability.

Methods

Study participants and data collection

Data Source

This retrospective cohort study extracted patient data from the electronic medical records (EMRs) of five hospitals in Taiwan, using the same dataset as described previously by Wu et al. [12]. The dataset comprises patient data from Taipei Chang Gung Memorial Hospital, Keelung Chang Gung Memorial Hospital, Linkou Chang Gung Memorial Hospital, Chiayi Chang Gung Memorial Hospital, and Kaohsiung Chang Gung Memorial Hospital. As illustrated in Fig. 1, a total of 2,619,342 initial emergency department (ED) visit records were identified between 2008 and 2016. Figure 1 was reconstructed based on the dataset described in Wu et al. [12]. These records included data from two medical centers (Hospital A: 767,158 visits; Hospital B: 618,955 visits) and three regional hospitals (Hospital C: 298,960 visits; Hospital D: 366,563 visits; Hospital E: 385,705 visits). Validating the model across these diverse hospital environments ensured that the developed model remained effective and robust across different levels of medical institutions.

Inclusion and exclusion of the study population (based on the dataset described in Wu et al. [12])

Inclusion and Exclusion Criteria: The inclusion criteria were all non-traumatic adult ED patients aged 17 years or older who received treatment. As shown in the flowchart (Fig. 1), we excluded 182,001 records based on specific criteria: 5,263 patients who were dead on arrival (DOA), 97,692 who were discharged against medical advice (AAD), 32,764 who were transferred from the ED, and 46,282 who had incomplete data or were unable to undergo subsequent examinations. The final study cohort consisted of 2,437,341 eligible ED visits, including 60,445 in-hospital deaths (2.48%).

It should be noted that minor discrepancies exist between the dataset used in the present study and the dataset reported by Wu et al. [12]. Specifically, the total number of records and hospital-level distributions differ slightly due to updated data extraction procedures and refined data cleaning criteria applied in this study.

Data Integration and Generalizability: Integrating patient data from five distinct medical centers and regional hospitals was intentional to enhance the model’s generalizability and clinical applicability. We conducted a cross-institutional comparison of patient characteristics, and the detailed results are presented in Table 1. Demographic variables, including age distribution and sex proportion, as well as key clinical indicators such as MEWS, vital signs (HR, SBP, DBP, RR), were compared across hospital campuses. Although statistical tests revealed significant differences (p < 0.001), this is largely attributable to the extremely large sample size, which increases statistical power and renders even minimal differences statistically significant. Importantly, the median values and interquartile ranges of core clinical predictors were highly comparable across institutions. For example, the median MEWS was consistently 1.0 across all campuses, with overlapping interquartile ranges. Similarly, variations in age and vital signs were numerically small and clinically negligible. From a clinical perspective, these differences would not alter risk stratification or treatment decisions and are therefore considered practically insignificant. Furthermore, the Random Over Sampling (ROS) method was utilized to effectively stabilize model performance regardless of the hospital source, ensuring that potential institutional differences did not compromise predictive accuracy. All demographic and clinical data were recorded and analyzed within 24 h of the patient’s admission. The clinical variables used for model development are detailed in the Feature Definition.

Feature Definition: The variables used in this study were extracted from electronic medical records collected during emergency department visits. Based on data availability and clinical relevance, the following features were included as model inputs. Demographic variables included age and sex. Location-related variables included LOC (patient location within the hospital) and RGSDPT (registered department). Vital sign features included body temperature, heart rate (HR), systolic blood pressure (SBP), diastolic blood pressure (DBP), and respiratory rate (RR). Neurological and clinical assessment features included the Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS), AVPU scale, triage level, and the Modified Early Warning Score (MEWS).

Parameters for data balancing methods

RUS

In RUS, majority class samples (survival cases) were randomly removed until the number of majority samples matched that of the minority class (mortality cases), resulting in a 1:1 class ratio. Sample removal was performed without replacement, and a fixed random seed was applied to reduce sampling variability.

ROS

In ROS, minority class samples (mortality cases) were randomly duplicated with replacement until their number equaled that of the majority class, achieving a balanced 1:1 class distribution. The selection of minority samples for duplication was performed uniformly at random, ensuring that all original minority samples had equal probability of being replicated. This approach preserves all original samples and maintains the original data distribution.

SMOTE

For the SMOTE method, the number of nearest neighbors was set to k = 5, and the distance metric was defined as Euclidean distance. Synthetic minority samples were generated by linear interpolation between each minority sample and one of its k nearest minority neighbors in the feature space until class balance was achieved. This approach aims to improve model generalization by introducing synthetic diversity among minority class samples.

Feature importance analysis

Feature importance was computed using model-specific mechanisms corresponding to each learning algorithm. For tree-based ensemble models, feature importance was derived from the contribution of each feature to node splitting. In the Random Forest (RF) model, feature importance was calculated based on the mean decrease in impurity (MDI), which measures the total reduction in Gini impurity contributed by each feature across all trees. For AdaBoost (ADA) and AdaBoost with BootStrap (ADA-BS), feature importance was determined by aggregating the weighted contributions of weak learners, where features that appeared more frequently in high-weight weak classifiers were assigned higher importance. For the Information Gain (IG) model, feature importance was computed based on entropy reduction, measuring how much each feature decreased uncertainty in the class label. These model-specific importance measures were normalized for visualization and comparative analysis.

ML techniques

The study includes four primary ML prediction models: RF, ADA, and XGB. Each of these models has unique advantages and performs well when handling different types of data and prediction targets.

RF [23] is an ensemble method based on decision trees, capable of effectively handling high-dimensional data and preventing overfitting. RF enhances the robustness and accuracy of the model through random sampling and the voting mechanism of multiple decision trees. This stacked approach effectively reduces the bias of a single model and enhances overall predictive performance.

The RF model consisted of 150 decision trees, using Gini impurity as the splitting criterion. The maximum tree depth was set to 10, and class weights were automatically adjusted using the balanced mode (class_weight = “balanced”) to address class imbalance. A fixed random seed (random_state = 42) was applied to ensure reproducibility.

The ADA model employed decision stumps as weak learners with 150 boosting iterations. A fixed random seed (random_state = 42) was applied to ensure reproducibility.

XGB was implemented based on gradient boosting decision trees with 100 estimators, a maximum tree depth of 6, and a learning rate of 0.1. The evaluation metric was set to logarithmic loss. To emphasize minority class samples, XGB incorporated high class-weight adjustment (scale_pos_weight = 1.5), and this XGB version is called XGB-H. To maintain uniform treatment of classes, the other XGB version applied a low-class weight (scale_pos_weight = 0.85) and is called XGB-L.

LR was used as the final meta-learner to integrate predictions from base models, with L2 regularization applied to enhance stability.

Different data balancing methods were compared andtheir impact on prediction accuracy, True Positive Rate (TPR), True Negative Rate (TNR), AUROC, and AUPRC were evaluated. To assess statistical robustness, 95% confidence intervals (CI) for AUROC and AUPRC were estimated using bootstrap resampling with 1,000 iterations. Detailed results are provided in the subsequent results analysis section.

Model robustness and reproducibility

The dataset used in this study is extremely large, consisting of more than 2.4 million emergency department visits. Under such a scale, performance variability caused by random sampling or model initialization is relatively limited. In addition, statistical robustness was evaluated using 95% confidence intervals for AUROC and AUPRC in the tables of the following sections. Specifically, these confidence intervals were estimated using 1,000 bootstrap resampling iterations, providing a reliable assessment of model performance variability. Considering the large dataset size, bootstrap-based confidence interval estimation was adopted instead of repeated random experiments or extensive cross-validation, as it provides a computationally efficient and statistically sound approach for performance evaluation in large-scale clinical datasets. Furthermore, robustness was assessed through: (1) consistent performance patterns across multiple machine learning models; (2) stability of results across different mortality timeframes: 24 h, 72 h, 168 h, and final mortality, and (3) systematic comparison between balanced and unbalanced training settings. Therefore, bootstrap-based confidence intervals and multiple evaluation perspectives provides sufficient evidence of model robustness and reliability.

Interpretation of P-values in large-scale data analysis

In this study, non-parametric statistical tests were used to assess differences between groups. Given the extremely large sample size, it should be noted that very small differences may yield statistically significant P-values (e.g., P < 0.001). Therefore, statistical significance alone may not fully reflect clinical or practical importance. Accordingly, P-values were interpreted with caution, and clinical relevance was considered alongside statistical significance when analyzing feature differences and model performance.

For machine learning, TensorFlow, Pytorch, and Scikit-learn were used to build and train the models, providing stable and efficient support for the present study.

Data preprocessing and balancing

Data Balancing

We conducted four data balancing methods for the four mortality timeframes. After comparing different data balancing methods, Random Over Sampling (ROS) was selected based on experimental evidence rather than qualitative description alone.

In the experiment results of the mortality timeframes, ROS consistently improved minority class sensitivity across different mortality timeframes while maintaining stable AUROC performance. Unlike Random Under Sampling (RUS), which substantially reduces the number of survival samples and may lead to the loss of clinically informative data, ROS preserves the entire dataset and avoids performance degradation caused by data removal. In addition, compared with SMOTE, which introduces synthetic samples, ROS demonstrated more stable performance trends without introducing potential artificial patterns in clinical features. Therefore, considering its consistent performance improvements and stability observed in the experimental results, ROS was selected as the primary data balancing method in this study. When further analyzing the binary classification threshold of the model, the ROS threshold was closer to the standard 0.5. For example, in predicting 24-hour mortality, the optimal threshold for the RF model was 0.4, while in the unbalanced case, the optimal threshold was significantly lower (0.0032), indicating that balanced training data stabilizes and balances the prediction results.

Figure 4 illustrates the ROC curves of the XGB-H model under four data balancing conditions. Although the AUROC values of ROS, RUS, and the unbalanced setting are numerically similar, the selection of ROS was not based solely on marginal AUROC differences. From a data integrity perspective, RUS substantially reduces the number of survival samples, leading to a significant loss of clinically informative data, which may negatively affect model robustness in large-scale medical datasets. In contrast, ROS preserves all original samples while addressing class imbalance, thereby maintaining the original data distribution. Compared with SMOTE, which generates synthetic samples through interpolation, ROS avoids the potential risk of introducing artificial patterns that may not reflect true clinical characteristics. In addition, ROS consistently improved minority class sensitivity to balance TPR and TNR, and stabilized decision thresholds across different mortality timeframes and models.

Feature description

The features in this dataset include several patient-related medical variables covering basic information and clinical vital signs. First, LOC represents the hospital area where the patient is located, used to record the patient’s location during treatment, which is important for internal management and patient triage. RGSDPT refers to the department where the patient registered, reflecting the specialty or department where the patient was treated.

Additionally, basic demographic characteristics such as age and sex are included, helping medical personnel quickly understand the patient’s demography. Temperature and heart rate (HR) are important indicators of the patient’s body temperature and heart condition, and changes in these vital signs can provide early clues about the patient’s condition.

For blood pressure, the table includes SBP (Systolic Blood Pressure) and DBP (Diastolic Blood Pressure), which are key parameters for monitoring cardiovascular health. RR (Respiratory Rate) records the patients’ breathing frequency, with normal values ranging from 12 to 20 breaths per minute. Abnormal respiratory rates may signal a deterioration in the patient’s health. GCS (Glasgow Coma Scale) is used to assess the patient’s level of consciousness, providing a quick assessment of neurological impairment.

Triage and AVPU (Alert, Voice, Pain, Unresponsive) are essential for emergency triage, helping to prioritize patients based on acuity to ensure timely treatment. Finally, MEWS (Modified Early Warning Score) is a comprehensive scoring system that assesses the patient’s risk level based on multiple vital signs, helping medical staff intervene early if the patient is in a high-risk condition.

From the results of both studies, even though some features had similar rankings, the balanced data showed certain advantages. For example, age had high importance in our model, particularly in the RF and ADA-BS models, contrasting with the lower importance of age in the unbalanced data of a previous study [11]. This indicates that in our balanced data, age contributes significantly more to mortality prediction.

Regarding biological sex (Male, Female), the impact remains small in both our balanced data and the unbalanced data as shown previously [11]. This supports our recommendation to deprioritize or consider removing as a feature in the model.

Triage was highly important in the balanced data, especially in the AdaBoost and AdaBoost + Bootstrap models, whereas the importance of this feature was not as prominent in a previous study [11]. This suggests that Triage information in the balanced dataset contributes more to the prediction, as our model under balanced conditions can better capture the predictive value of this feature.

As for Blood Pressure features (SBP, DBP), both studies showed their medium contribution to mortality prediction, even in our balanced data. This further supports the recommendation to consider deprioritizing these features in future models to focus on features with more impact on predictive outcomes.

Discussion

The present study explored the advantages of different ML models in predicting ED mortality rates, comparing the performance of these models to that of conventional models. Results have shown that after using ROS for data balancing, the TPR and accuracy of the ML models improved significantly, effectively addressing data imbalance. These results demonstrate the crucial role of data balancing techniques in enhancing the performance of ED mortality prediction models.

Further, results of the present study have shown that data balancing techniques significantly improve the classification accuracy of ML models, addressing biases from imbalanced data. By segmenting data into four mortality timeframes (death within 24, 72, 168 h, and final mortality) and applying various balancing methods, these techniques enhanced predictive performance across all timeframes, highlighting their effectiveness in improving model accuracy, aiding early identification of high-risk patients and enhancing healthcare outcomes in emergency settings.

Strengths and limitations

The use of the ROS model resulted in marked increases in the True Positive Rate (TPR), contributing to a more balanced relationship between TPR and TNR in imbalanced datasets and enabling more accurate identification of the minority class (deceased cases). The ML models displayed stable performance across different time frames and data balancing methods, especially the XGB and RF models. This result highlights the models’ potential for practical application at various predictive intervals. Besides, although ROS shows better results than RUS and SMOTE in this study, the difference between ROS and RUS is not significant. Further comparisons between ROS and RUS will be needed for different datasets and scenarios.

Future research direction

Further Model Optimization: Research should delve deeper into various data balancing methods, exploring ways to prevent TNR decline while maintaining high TPR for an optimal balance.

Testing in Clinical Settings: Testing these ML models in real clinical environments is recommended to assess their practical effectiveness and validate their applicability in predicting mortality across different time intervals.

Expanding the use of ML models to other hospital departments: Applying the models to other hospital departments (e.g., internal medicine or palliative care) may help verify the broader applicability and observe predictive performance across various healthcare settings.

In unbalanced datasets, certain models (e.g., RF, ADA) showed low TPR despite high TNR, a typical sign of overfitting, particularly from excessive learning of the majority class patterns.

Although data balancing methods effectively improved TPR, TNR declined in certain instances, indicating an ongoing challenge in maintaining accuracy for the majority class while enhancing the minority class predictions.

Conclusions

Data balancing improves ML model accuracy in predicting mortality for ED patients. ROS provides the best results because mortality samples are significantly fewer than survival samples. Results of the present study confirm the effectiveness of data balancing techniques in improving ML model mortality rate prediction in EDs. The ROS method improves both TPR and accuracy, demonstrating stable performance across different mortality time frames.
