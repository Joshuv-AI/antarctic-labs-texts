Premature stopping of LEIA-HF: protecting trial participants while preserving clinical knowledge
Authors: Johannes Grand, Anne Sophie Overgaard Olesen, Janus C Jakobsen
Published: 2026

We read with great interest the LEIA-HF study by Kazimierczyk et al.,1 which evaluates the use of repetitive levosimendan infusions in ambulatory patients with advanced heart failure. The authors should be congratulated for conducting a clinically important, multicentre, randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled trial in a complex and vulnerable patient population. Such trials are difficult to conduct, yet they are essential because levosimendan remains widely used in European clinical practice despite persistent uncertainty about its effects on long-term outcomes.2

The trial was stopped prematurely after only 64 of 350 planned patients had been randomized, following a Data Safety Monitoring Board recommendation due to excess deaths in the active-treatment group.1 All observed deaths occurred among patients allocated to levosimendan, raising an important safety concern. These results should prompt careful reflection on the use of intermittent levosimendan in advanced heart failure.3

However, the premature termination also raises several broader methodological and ethical questions. Could the observed excess mortality represent a true safety signal? Yes. Could it represent a chance finding? Also yes. Could it be partly explained by baseline imbalances arising from early random variation in a small sample? The authors note that, despite randomization, patients allocated to levosimendan had higher baseline N-terminal prohormone of B-type natriuretic peptide and lactate concentrations, suggesting a potentially higher-risk phenotype.

This tension is familiar to trialists. Stopping a trial early due to potential harm may protect enrolled participants, particularly in frail populations with advanced disease. Yet early stopping may also leave clinicians with an unresolved estimate of benefit or harm. This is especially problematic when the intervention is already used in routine practice. In such circumstances, stopping the trial protects those enrolled in the study but may not protect the much larger number of patients treated outside the trial, where uncertainty persists, and monitoring may be less rigorous.4

It is questionable whether these findings, based on only 64 patients, will lead to a worldwide change in clinical practice resulting in the discontinuation of levosimendan infusions in ambulatory patients with advanced heart failure. The next intuitive step may be to call for a new, adequately powered randomized trial evaluating levosimendan. However, if the clinical equipoise remains and if the safety signal is uncertain, one might ask whether the more informative path would have been to continue the existing trial under modified oversight rather than terminate it. Options could include enhanced safety monitoring, protocol adaptation, or additional interim analyses.

This is not intended as criticism of the authors or the Data Safety Monitoring Board. On the contrary, LEIA-HF provides precisely the kind of evidence that should stimulate a wider discussion among clinicians, trialists, ethics committees, regulators, and monitoring boards. In high-risk populations, the ethical obligation is not only to protect patients enrolled in a trial but also to generate reliable evidence for patients receiving routine care.

The published trial report and the available design paper do not appear to describe a pre-specified statistical stopping rule, formal harm boundary, or mortality threshold for Data Safety Monitoring Board action.5 We would therefore be grateful if the authors could clarify the stopping process. Was there a pre-defined stopping rule for harm? Was the decision based on a formal interim analysis, an unplanned safety review, or clinical judgement by the monitoring board? What role, if any, did regulatory authorities or ethics committees have in the termination decision? Were the events assessed as being related to the intervention? Finally, did the authors consider continuing the trial with protocol modifications, and, if so, what factors led to its termination?

LEIA-HF is an important study. Its findings should not be dismissed because it stopped early, but neither should they be overinterpreted without understanding how and why the decision to stop was made.
