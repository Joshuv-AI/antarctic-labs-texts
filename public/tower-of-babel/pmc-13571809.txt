Pilot of a Micro‐Randomized Factorial Trial of Self‐Monitoring Feedback in a Behavioral Weight Loss Intervention
Authors: Kathryn M Ross, Jaime Ruiz, Meena N Shankar, Peihua Qiu, Zibo Tian, Lisa Anthony, Kelsey R Day, Rebecca A Krukowski
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundThe provision of self‐monitoring feedback has been demonstrated to improve weight loss program outcomes; however, there is little evidence to support how this feedback should be delivered. Micro‐randomized factorial trial designs can examine the proximal impact of intervention components, but their feasibility and acceptability in the context of behavioral weight management programs remain unknown.MethodsThe current pilot study evaluated the feasibility and acceptability of procedures for a micro‐randomized factorial trial designed to optimize the provision of self‐monitoring feedback messages in behavioral weight management programs. Adults with obesity were provided with an initial Weight Loss 101 education session and asked to self‐monitor dietary intake, physical activity, and weight daily (using an app, activity monitor, and e‐scale). They were then randomized each week for 16 weeks (using a 24 factorial design) to receive or not receive each of four feedback components (feedback on calorie goal attainment, diet quality, physical activity goal attainment, or to support goal setting) sent via text with a link to an individualized website.ResultsOf the 28 participants enrolled (86% women; mean ± SD BMI = 37.2 ± 6.6 kg/m2), most (93%) completed the study; participants self‐monitored dietary intake, physical activity, and weight on 65%, 87%, and 76% of possible days, respectively. Most weekly feedback messages were viewed (92%); 63% were rated and 90% of rated messages were deemed helpful.ConclusionsTrial procedures were both feasible and acceptable among adults with obesity. Findings can inform the design of future micro‐randomized factorial trials.

Introduction

Many behavioral interventions, including behavioral weight management interventions, were developed as “packages” with efficacy and effectiveness evaluated via traditional 2‐arm evaluation randomized control trials (RCTs) [1, 2]. Although RCTs are useful for determining whether a treatment package is effective or not, these designs do not allow researchers to examine which program components drive this effectiveness. Moreover, in the era of precision medicine, traditional RCTs preclude researchers' ability to investigate which components are effective for whom, when, and in what context (knowledge which is necessary to appropriately tailor precision health interventions).

Newer methods (e.g., factorial and micro‐randomized trial designs) have attempted to address this challenge. For example, in factorial designs, participants are randomized to receive (or not to receive) specific treatment components (i.e., factors) versus receiving or not receiving an entire treatment package. This allows for the estimation and comparison of effect sizes for each component individually and for evaluating interactions between components (i.e., determining whether the presence of one component enhances or weakens the effect of other components) [3]. In contrast, micro‐randomized trials randomize the same participant multiple times over the course of the study to facilitate the determination of when and for whom each component is most effective and how these effects may vary over time [4]. Micro‐randomized designs can also increase power for statistical comparisons, such that more randomization points result in greater power [4].

In the field of behavioral weight management, self‐monitoring (i.e., having participants track dietary intake, physical activity, and weight) is considered a core intervention component [5] and greater adherence to self‐monitoring has been closely linked with greater success at weight loss during these programs [6, 7]. Gold‐standard behavioral weight management programs typically provide feedback on self‐monitoring (as part of a broader treatment “package”), and a number of studies have shown that this feedback is important for promoting greater adherence to self‐monitoring, greater adherence to intervention goals [8] and, ultimately, greater weight loss [6, 7, 9, 10]. Less is known, however, regarding how this feedback should be delivered [11] as this feedback itself is also often delivered as a “package” (e.g., provided in an “all or nothing” approach, wherein interventionists provide full feedback on all areas of self‐monitoring or no feedback is provided at all). Critical questions remain, including: how much and what kind of self‐monitoring feedback should be provided to whom and in what context? These kinds of questions would be challenging to answer via traditional RCTs but are well suited for factorial and micro‐randomized trial designs.

When delivered as a typical package, written feedback messages are composed by trained interventionists, often on a weekly basis, and typically include comments on the frequency of self‐monitoring, goal attainment (in relation to caloric intake and physical activity goals), and dietary quality, with structured problem solving and goal setting techniques used to help individuals overcome barriers to changing dietary intake and physical activity behaviors [12]. Composing feedback in this manner is costly, however, as it takes trained interventionists around 25–30 mins per participant each week to compose these messages [13]. Thus, to optimize behavioral weight management intervention efficacy and efficiency (and to support dissemination of behavioral weight management interventions in community and clinical settings), it is critical to determine how much and what kind of self‐monitoring feedback should be provided, to whom and in what context.

These questions can be answered by combining the two trial designs mentioned previously; that is, using a micro‐randomized, factorial trial to optimize the provision of self‐monitoring feedback within behavioral weight management interventions. The current study was a pilot trial designed to evaluate the feasibility and acceptability of the proposed study and intervention methods prior to conducting a fully‐powered (and more costly) trial. In this pilot, adults with obesity were provided with an initial weight‐loss education session; after a week of self‐monitoring (serving as a run‐in period and data collection for the first feedback message), participants received interventionist‐composed feedback messages each week for 16 weeks, with message content based on randomization each week to one of 16 conditions (under a 24 factorial design, with each of four feedback components turned “on” or “off” for participants each week). The primary aim of the current study was to investigate the feasibility of trial implementation (i.e., implementation of the randomization scheme, participant recruitment and retention, view rates of feedback messages, ability to collect key outcome measures including data capture of dietary intake, physical activity, and weight data) and acceptability of intervention messages (i.e., weekly participant ratings of the “helpfulness” of each feedback message, post‐intervention ratings of message frequency, length, content, and tailoring). As a secondary aim, this study explored participants' views on the importance of different types of feedback messages and the balance of message valence.

Methods

Participants

Participants were adults with obesity (age ≥ 18 years, BMI ≥ 30 kg/m2, but weight < 175 kg due to the weight limit of study‐provided e‐scales) who owned a smartphone with a cellular data plan. Potential participants were excluded if they were currently participating in another behavioral weight loss program, had a history of metabolic and bariatric surgery, were currently using obesity management medications (or if they used obesity management medications in the 6 months prior to the study), if they had lost ≥ 10 lb in the 6 months prior to the study, if they were currently pregnant, planning to become pregnant, or less than 1‐year postpartum, or if they had any other conditions that would contraindicate participation in a behavioral weight loss program or prevent completion of the study (e.g., inability to walk ¼ mile without stopping, self‐report of an eating disorder within the past 5 years, use of a pacemaker, or life‐limiting diseases such as cancer). Finally, participants were also only eligible for randomization in the current study upon successful completion of at least 5 out of 7 days of self‐monitoring the first week after the initial weight loss education session (see details in the Weight Loss 101 and Behavioral Run‐In section).

Recruitment

Potential participants were recruited from throughout the United States via Research Match and a wait list of individuals who had been deemed ineligible for participation in another behavioral weight management program (primarily due to non‐rural residence, as this other program was recruiting individuals residing in rural areas [14]). Recruitment occurred during a 3‐week period in July 2024 and then was closed to avoid any intervention or data collection overlapping with the university closure in late December (in total, data collection occurred between July and December 2024). Individuals who met basic eligibility criteria on a REDCap pre‐screening questionnaire were invited to attend a videoconference‐based orientation session, during which interested individuals were provided with a detailed overview of the study and those who were interested in participating could provide informed consent via REDCap's e‐consent module. Participants were made aware during the orientation session (and via the informed consent form) that they would receive different amounts and types of feedback messages each week, but this was the extent of detail provided about the differences in feedback messages provided each week. After providing informed consent, individuals were emailed a hyperlink to complete baseline questionnaires via REDCap. Following completion of these questionnaires, participants who met initial eligibility criteria (i.e., all criteria except for completion of the behavioral run‐in described below) were mailed the Fitbit Inspire 3 activity tracker and Aria e‐scale, were asked to download and set up the Fitbit app, and were provided instructions for how to link the Fitbit account with the custom backend system.

Weight Loss 101 and Behavioral Run‐in

After completing baseline questionnaires and receiving the study self‐monitoring tools, all potential participants were asked to attend one 60‐min Weight Loss 101 videoconference session via a live, group‐based Zoom videoconference call. This session, led by a trained interventionist (either a registered dietician or graduate student in clinical psychology) with prior experience conducting behavioral weight loss programs, provided participants with core weight loss education behavioral skills training, with content and goals based on intervention protocols from gold‐standard behavioral weight management programs (e.g., Diabetes Prevention Program and Look AHEAD lifestyle interventions) [15, 16]. At the session, participants were taught how to self‐monitor caloric intake, physical activity, and weight using study‐provided tools and were asked to self‐monitor daily using these tools throughout the 17‐week study period. Specifically, participants were asked to self‐monitor dietary intake each day using the Fitbit app, to wear the Inspire 3 each day to self‐monitor physical activity, and to weigh themselves each day using the Aria scale (using the protocol described for weight assessments in the Measures section, with the exception that participants were only asked to weigh themselves once each day during the intervention versus the three weights captured for assessment timepoints). Consistent with previous weight loss studies [15, 17] and current clinical guidelines for the treatment of adult obesity [18] caloric intake goals were provided at this session based on initial body weight (i.e., 1200 calories per day if less than 200 pounds, 1500 calories per day if 200–275 pounds, and 1800 calories per day if more than 275 pounds). Participant calorie goals could be adjusted during the intervention as needed (e.g., if requested by the participant or for safety, e.g., in relation to rapid weight loss or high levels of physical activity). As a physical activity goal, participants were encouraged to gradually increase engagement in moderate‐intensity activity (e.g., brisk walking) to at least 150 Active Zone Minutes/week (consistent with existing physical activity guidelines [19] as 1 min of moderate‐intensity activity = 1 Active Zone Minute and 1 min of vigorous‐intensity activity = 2 Active Zone Minutes). Participants who began the study with baseline levels of physical activity above this goal were also encouraged to increase physical activity given that research has suggested higher levels of activity (e.g., 200–300 minutes/week) may be beneficial for supporting weight loss and weight loss maintenance [20]. The Weight Loss 101 session also included guidance about how to set helpful goals (e.g., setting goals that are positive, specific, focused on a behavior under the participant's control, time‐limited, and achievable) with examples of helpful goals related to weight loss.

Participants were asked to begin tracking dietary intake, physical activity, and weight each day beginning the day after the Weight Loss 101 session; only participants who completed this self‐monitoring on at least 5 of the next 7 days were eligible for final enrollment into the study. This served as a “behavioral run‐in” week to determine final eligibility for randomization and to provide data that could be used by the interventionist to write the first feedback message (which was delivered on the 8th day after the Weight Loss 101 session, or Day 1 of Week 1 of the 16‐week randomized intervention period). Participants who did not self‐monitor (or showed inconsistent self‐monitoring) the day or two after the Weight Loss 101 session were contacted to ensure there were no technical issues that needed to be resolved.

Randomization

Following successful completion of the behavioral run‐in, participants were randomized each week for 16 weeks to one of 16 conditions (under a 24 factorial model), which determined the type and amount of feedback provided. The study statistician (P.Q.) developed an allocation scheme that used a simple blocked randomization to assign each participant to one of the 16 conditions each week. This allocation scheme was uploaded to a custom‐built administrative dashboard connected to the data capture backend system. As participants were deemed eligible, the administrative dashboard automatically assigned participants to a randomization sequence; this sequence was hidden from the interventionist and all study team members other than the study co‐investigator who designed and maintained the backend system (J.R.)

Feedback Components

Participants were provided a text‐based feedback message on the morning of Day 1 of each study week, with all messages written by a registered dietitian experienced in delivering behavioral weight management programs; each participant was asked to select the earliest time to receive messages that day of the week, and each week were sent a text message at that time (automated via the administrative dashboard), which provided a custom hyperlink to an individualized website displaying the feedback message. Across all conditions, each text‐based feedback message contained a core message including the frequency of self‐monitoring of dietary intake, physical activity, and weight (e.g., number of days that the participant engaged in each self‐monitoring behavior, from 0 to 7 days for each) and weight trajectory. Along with this core feedback message, participants were randomly assigned each week to receive or not to receive each of the four additional feedback components (see Table 1). In total, each message consisted of 4–10 comments, depending on randomization condition. Participants received feedback messages regardless of their level of adherence to self‐monitoring; feedback messages for participants who did not have complete (or any) self‐monitoring data for a randomized component received a message that encouraged them to resume self‐monitoring and to problem solve strategies for overcoming barriers to self‐monitoring.

Each feedback message was composed by a trained interventionist using participant self‐monitoring data available on the custom‐built administrative dashboard; only information relevant to the randomized condition for that participant that week was accessible by the interventionist (e.g., interventionists could only view a participant's physical activity data only on weeks in which that participant was randomized to conditions in which this component was turned “on”). Component messages provided reinforcement to promote behavioral mastery and self‐efficacy [21], identified areas for change to promote problem identification (a key step in effective problem solving [22]), and suggested strategies to overcome barriers and promote goal attainment. The four components are described below (see Figure 1 for an example message displaying the standard message received every week, including a count of the number of days of self‐monitoring completed, feedback on weight change, and messages for all four components):

Example feedback message showing text from all four possible feedback components.

Component 1: Calorie Goal Attainment. Reductions in caloric intake play a key role in promoting weight loss [23]. On weeks that participants were randomized to receive this component, they were provided with feedback focused on the number of days that they met their caloric intake goal the prior week, with calorie goal attainment operationalized as the number of days that: (1) participants logged ≥ 800 kcal (to avoid reinforcing insufficient caloric intake or partial logging) and (2) the kcal total was less than 50 calories above the daily goal. Feedback focused on successes and/or challenges that the interventionist noted in meeting the participants' calorie goal in that week.

Component 2: Diet Quality. Theoretically, feedback about specific dietary choices should provide helpful scaffolding and shaping for participants attempting to make changes in dietary intake, ultimately supporting calorie goal attainment. On weeks that participants were randomized to receive this component, they received comments regarding specific dietary choices the prior week that might have impacted their weight loss success. Feedback on dietary choices was broadly based on established principles of healthy eating (e.g., consuming a diet rich in fruits and vegetables, whole grain products, and lean proteins while limiting consumption of foods with added sugars and sugar‐sweetened beverages), consistent with the 2020–2025 Dietary Guidelines for Americans [24]. Feedback messages focused on reinforcing choices consistent with these dietary guidelines and suggesting changes in eating patterns discordant with these guidelines.

Component 3: Physical Activity Goal Attainment. Physical activity has less of an impact on initial weight loss than on weight loss maintenance [23]; however, feedback on physical activity is a standard component of self‐monitoring feedback in both weight loss and weight maintenance phases of gold‐standard behavioral weight management programs. On weeks that participants were randomized to receive this component, they received feedback related to the attainment of the physical activity goal over the prior week (defined as Active Zone Minutes logged using the Fitbit tracker), including reinforcement for any/all activity that the participant engaged in over the course of the prior week and encouraging suggestions for increasing activity the following week.

Component 4: Goal Setting. An open‐text goal setting prompt was selected based on results from the Heart Steps micro‐randomized trial [25], which showed that a similar prompt was effective in promoting physical activity change. Further support for this component comes from other research demonstrating that action planning enhances the self‐monitoring of physical activity and sedentary behavior [26]. In the current study, goal setting was extended beyond physical activity and focused on all weight‐related behaviors. On weeks that participants were randomized to receive the goal setting component, they were asked to complete an open‐text prompt that encouraged them to set a specific weight‐related behavior change goal for that week. The study did not track whether participants chose to self‐monitor their self‐set goals, and no additional feedback on self‐set goals was provided.

Beyond the weekly feedback messages, participants did not receive any additional intervention contact from the study team (e.g., no reminders of the WL101 material were sent to participants). Moreover, since adherence to self‐monitoring was a study outcome, participants were not contacted if they displayed low adherence to self‐monitoring dietary intake, physical activity, or weight (although, when appropriate to the randomization condition for the week, adherence to self‐monitoring could be addressed within interventionist‐composed feedback messages, as discussed previously).

Measures

Participant demographic information was collected via a brief demographic questionnaire completed via REDCap as part of the baseline assessment. Weight was assessed at baseline (the morning of each participant's Weight Loss 101 visit) and Month 4 (after the end of the randomized study period) using the study‐provided Fitbit Aria e‐scales and a standardized e‐scale protocol [27]. Specifically, participants were asked to weigh themselves using the study‐provided e‐scale first thing in the morning, before eating or drinking but after voiding, in no more than light indoor clothing with shoes removed, and to step on and off the scale three times to avoid errors due to scale calibration. The two closest weights of these three were averaged to serve as the official assessment weight. Weight was also assessed daily via data collected from each participant's Aria e‐scale (participants were only asked to weigh once per day to reduce burden during the intervention; however, participants could choose to weigh multiple times in a row to resolve calibration issues if the scales were moved or stored on their side). In a future trial, these daily weights would be used to examine within‐week weight gain trajectories and investigate the impact of each feedback component on weight change over the week after it is received (Day 1–Day 7 of that week); thus, availability of self‐monitoring data for weight was considered a key measure of study feasibility in this pilot study. In the current study, frequency of weight self‐monitoring was defined as the number of days (0–7) each study week that a participant had a valid (e.g., non‐outlier) weight recorded via the study‐provided e‐scale.

Daily dietary intake and physical activity data were also collected from participants' linked Fitbit accounts; a future trial would quantify weekly patterns of adherence to self‐monitoring and goal attainment (i.e., number of days that participants met dietary intake and physical activity goals) and investigate the impact of each feedback component on self‐monitoring adherence and goal attainment the week after it is received; thus, availability of self‐monitoring data for dietary intake and physical activity were considered in the assessment of trial feasibility. In the current study, a valid “day” of self‐monitoring dietary intake within the Fitbit app was defined as ≥ 800 kcal, consistent with prior research [28]. For each study week, frequency of self‐monitoring dietary intake was defined as the number of valid days (0–7) recorded and frequency of self‐monitoring physical activity using the Fitbit Inspire 3 activity monitor was defined as the number of days (0–7) that the Fitbit device was worn (i.e., the number of days in a week when any activity data were synced from the Fitbit device and steps were > 0).

View rates of messages were assessed via page views collected from the backend system. Acceptability of the feedback messages was assessed via participant ratings of each feedback message each week (at the end of each message, participants were asked to rate the helpfulness of the message by clicking a “thumbs up” or “thumbs down” button). Moreover, at the end of the study (Month 4 assessment), participants were asked to complete self‐report questionnaires via REDCap, one of which asked participants about their satisfaction with the feedback messages received (frequency, length, and tailoring), to rate the perceived importance of eight different types of feedback messages, and to rate the optimal valence of messages (e.g., whether messages should include only positive comments, only strategies for change, or a mix of both). Participants received a $25 gift card for completing the Month 4 assessment.

Recruitment and Retention Targets

The current study initially aimed to recruit 32 participants, with this sample size pragmatically selected based on the funding available as part of the one‐year internal pilot award that supported the current study (including support for initial backend technology development and regulatory approvals). The target for retention was to retain at least 80% of the enrolled participants for the final Month 4 assessment.

Analyses

Analyses were conducted using SAS version 9.4. Descriptive statistics were used to characterize (1) participant demographics, (2) frequency of data capture of self‐monitoring dietary intake, physical activity, and weight across (and by week of) the 16‐week study period, (3) the proportion of messages viewed, proportion of viewed messages rated, and message ratings, (4) responses on the Month 4 program satisfaction questionnaire, and (5) change in weight from baseline to the Month 4 assessment (using a conservative assumption of no weight loss, i.e. baseline observation carried forward [BOCF], for any missing Month 4 weight data). Finally, paired sample t‐tests were used to compare the average number of days of data capture of self‐monitoring of dietary intake, physical activity, and weight (e.g., evaluating whether adherence to daily self‐monitoring differed by target), longitudinal mixed‐effects models were used to characterize changes in self‐monitoring over the 16‐week study period, with study week included as a fixed effect to model a linear time trend, and a generalized linear mixed‐effects model (with a logit link function) was used to examine whether the proportion of messages viewed changed over time, also including study week as a fixed effect.

Results

Feasibility of Trial Implementation

Figure 2 depicts participant flow through the study. Thirty participants attended a Weight Loss 101 session and began the study run‐in week; of these, 28 (93%) were ultimately enrolled in the study (two participants were excluded prior to final enrollment due to not completing the required self‐monitoring during the run‐in period). Participants were an average (mean ± SD) of 42.0 ± 12.1 years old, had baseline BMI of 37.2 ± 6.6 kg/m2, and predominately identified as women (n = 24, 86%; n = 3 identified as men and n = 1 as non‐binary). In terms of race/ethnicity, 57% (n = 16) of participants identified as White, 36% (n = 10) as African American or Black, 11% (n = 3) as Asian, and 4% (n = 1) as Middle Eastern or North African.

Participant flow through study recruitment and enrollment.

Of the 28 participants who were enrolled in the study, 2 were withdrawn; one per participant request (after week 3) and one for an unrelated medical reason (after week 4). Data from earlier weeks, prior to withdrawal, were retained; thus, data were collected across a total of 423 person‐weeks. At Month 4, all 26 participants who remained active (93% of those enrolled) completed the final weight assessment; n = 25 completed final questionnaires (89% of those enrolled).

Over the 16‐week study period, participants self‐monitored dietary intake on an average (mean ± SD) of 4.6 ± 2.8 days/week (65.2% ± 32.2% of possible days), physical activity on 6.1 ± 2.0 days/week (87.3% ± 18.7% of possible days), and weight on of 5.3 ± 2.5 days/week (75.7% ± 27.0%); participants self‐monitored dietary intake on significantly fewer days than they self‐monitored weight, t(27) = −3.82, p < 0.001, or physical activity, t(27) = −4.34, p < 0.001, and weight on significantly fewer days than physical activity, t(27) = −2.49, p = 0.019. There were significant decreases over time in adherence to self‐monitoring of dietary intake, B = −0.20, SE = 0.02, t(394) = −13.32, p < 0.0001, physical activity, B = −0.13, SE = 0.02, t(394) = −8.61, p < 0.0001, and weight, B = −0.16, SE = 0.01, t(394) = −10.96, p < 0.0001; see Figure 3.

Average (mean and standard error) number of days that participants self‐monitored their dietary intake, physical activity, and weight over the 16 study weeks.

Randomization occurred successfully for each participant each week, and per protocol each participant was sent feedback messages according to the randomization condition each week. Participants viewed 391 of 423 possible weekly feedback messages (92.4%). There was a decrease in the likelihood of a message being viewed over the course of the intervention period, B = −0.14, SE = 0.05, t(394) = −2.95, p = 0.003 (see Figure 4); 100% of participants viewed the weekly feedback message in Week 1, whereas 92.3% (24 of 26 remaining participants) viewed these messages in Week 16. Participants were randomized to the goal setting component on 211 of the 423 person‐weeks; of those, participants viewed their messages on 197 weeks (93.4% of possible weeks) and responded (via submitted text) to the goal‐setting prompt on 125 weeks (63.5% of weeks that the goal setting prompts were viewed).

Proportion of participants viewing feedback messages each week over the 16 study weeks.

Acceptability of Feedback Messages

Participants provided ratings for 245 of the 391 messages viewed (62.7%); 200 (89.8% of those rated) were rated as helpful and 25 (10.2% of those rated) were rated as not helpful. Participant ratings of the acceptability of feedback messages, as collected at the Month 4 assessment, are provided in Table 2. The majority of participants reported that the number of feedback messages was “just right” (n = 16, 57%) and that the length of messages was “just right” (n = 17; 61%). Half of the study participants (n = 14, 50%) rated a 4 or a 5 (where 5 = “Very satisfied”) on a 1–5 point Likert scale assessing satisfaction with the content of messages, and 46% of the participants (n = 13) rated a 4 or a 5 on a similar question assessing satisfaction with message tailoring to participants' individual situations. When asked to rate the importance of the different types of feedback messages (1 = most important, 8 = least important, using a forced‐choice matrix item; see Table 3), participants rated messages related to dietary quality as the most important and messages related to the frequency of self‐weighing and the frequency of monitoring physical activity as the least important. When asked about message valence (i.e., the ideal balance of positive comments versus suggestions of strategies for change; see Table 4), most (n = 22; 78.6%) indicated a preference for a mix of positive comments and strategies for change, with only 2 participants (7.1%) noting that the messages should include only positive comments and none indicating that messages should include only strategies for change. Similarly, most indicated that preference for a mix of positive comments and areas for change whether they were struggling with weight loss (n = 24, 85.7% of all participants, and 100% of those who completed the questionnaire) or succeeding with weight loss (n = 20, 71.4%).

Weight Loss Outcomes

Finally, assuming that the two participants who were withdrawn lost no weight during the program (BOCF), participants experienced weight changes, on average, of −3.0 ± 4.4 kg from baseline to Month 4 (representing average weight losses of 3.0% ± 4.0% from baseline weight). Using complete‐case analysis, the mean weight changes of the 26 participants who completed the study was −3.3 ± 4.5 kg, representing a change of −3.2% ± 4.1% from baseline.

Discussion

The current study aimed to assess the feasibility and acceptability of a micro‐randomized factorial trial examining the optimal delivery of tailored self‐monitoring feedback messages among adults with obesity attempting to make changes in dietary intake and physical activity to promote weight loss. Results support the feasibility of the trial design, demonstrating success in implementing the trial protocol and recruiting/retaining study participants. Although the study initially proposed recruiting 32 participants, recruitment was discontinued after 3 weeks (with only 28 participants enrolled, 87.5% of our goal) due to pragmatic considerations related to the one‐year pilot grant timeline (privacy office risk review of the technology developed to implement this study took longer than expected, allowing only 3 weeks in which to conduct recruitment in order to complete the project within the proposed timeline). This pace of recruitment was substantially faster than initially proposed, however, providing confidence for recruiting this population during a typical trial timeline. Overall, 93% of enrolled participants completed the full study (above the target of 80%), representing completion of 94% of possible person‐weeks; moreover, participants viewed feedback messages viewed on 92% of possible weeks.

Across the 16 randomized intervention weeks, participants self‐monitored dietary intake, physical activity, and weight on 65%, 87%, and 76% of days, respectively, rates that are consistent with previous research [29]. Adherence to self‐monitoring decreased over time, as has been documented previously in other weight‐loss interventions; [30, 31, 32] however, the overall patterns in self‐monitoring documented in this pilot support the ability of a future, fully‐powered trial to have the level of data capture and variability necessary to assess proposed trial outcomes (which include not only weekly weight change but also weekly self‐monitoring adherence).

Results also support the acceptability of intervention feedback messages. The majority of messages viewed were rated (63%), and most of those rated were rated as helpful (90% of those rated). Overall, ratings on a post‐intervention questionnaire indicate that participants reported being satisfied with the number of messages, the content, and the tailoring to individual situations. The variability in satisfaction ratings in Table 2, however, may suggest potential for improving message tailoring and content. Conversely, lower scores on satisfaction in relation to tailoring and content may also serve as an unfortunate artifact of the study design, reflecting dissatisfaction with the randomization assignments (e.g., if a participant was interested in receiving feedback related to dietary intake on a specific week but was randomized to a condition that did not include that component, they may have been dissatisfied with the message overall). Engagement with feedback messages (i.e., the proportion of participants viewing the weekly message each week) decreased over time, consistent with previous research; [33] however, there were no weeks with views < 80%, supporting retention of an adequate number of person‐weeks in final trial analyses. Overall, participants lost an average of 3% of baseline weight; this was lower than the 8%–10% losses typically observed in gold‐standard behavioral interventions [18] but in line with other low‐intensity and digital‐health weight‐loss programs [10, 34, 35]. Taken together, results suggest that a micro‐randomized factorial design to test tailored self‐monitoring feedback messages is both a feasible and acceptable approach among participants with obesity, laying the foundation for a future full‐scale trial.

Results from this pilot also offer novel contributions to the broader literature. The rates of self‐monitoring observed in the current study (∼5 days/week for weight and dietary intake, and ∼6 days/week for physical activity) were higher than those found in a recent review of studies that used digital self‐monitoring tools within weight loss interventions [7]. In this review, only half of the 20 studies that reported adherence to self‐monitoring of dietary intake, weight, or physical activity reported rates of ≥ 4 days/week and only 15% reported self‐monitoring engagement rates of ≥ 6 days/week.

Although the current pilot did not include a group of participants who did not receive feedback messages (precluding the ability to assess the impact of self‐monitoring feedback on self‐monitoring adherence), prior work by Tate and colleagues [36] demonstrated the ability of interventionist‐composed feedback messages to increase adherence to self‐monitoring (and, ultimately, to improve weight loss outcomes). Thus, it may be that the higher adherence rates observed in this study were supported by the provision of weekly personalized feedback messages, regardless of message content. A future trial, fully‐powered trial is needed to examine how the amount, behavioral target, and interactivity of feedback messages may impact adherence within a given study week (and how this may vary on an individual and contextual basis).

Whereas previous studies have examined the optimal length of feedback messages [11], this study was among the first to investigate participants' perceptions regarding the types of feedback that are important within the context of a weight loss intervention. This study also investigated the optimal balance of positive comments versus suggestions of strategies for change within interventionist‐composed feedback messages. Participants rated feedback on dietary quality and weight trajectory as the most important, and feedback on the frequency of self‐weighing and frequency of monitoring physical activity as the least important. In contrast, a recent qualitative study by Kariuki and colleagues [37] found that both detailed and actionable feedback on physical activity self‐monitoring was identified as important among weight loss intervention participants (although this study focused specifically on physical activity self‐monitoring feedback). Other recent studies have examined how different types of feedback messages (e.g., those referring to unmet goals [38]) relate to participant engagement in weight loss interventions; however, the current study also provides insight into participants' message preferences, which may also lead to enhanced engagement and weight loss.

In terms of positive versus negative comments, the results of this study indicated that participants preferred a balance of positive comments and strategies for change regardless of the level of success with weight loss. However, previous research indicates that interventionists should use caution in relation to message tone, as one recent study found that receiving goal‐discrepant feedback message content (compared to more neutral or positive messages) reduced the likelihood of participants viewing subsequent messages [38]. Using a Delphi study design, experts in behavioral weight management were asked to rate the importance of different types of feedback messages and whether feedback should be delivered with only positive comments or a mix of positive comments and strategies for change [39], providing an opportunity to contrast expert and participant views. Although both experts in this prior study and participants in the current pilot rated feedback on the frequency of self‐weighing and the frequency of monitoring physical activity as the least important, the feedback type rated as most important by experts (i.e., an opportunity to set goals) was ranked as third lowest in importance using average ratings by participants in the current study (see Table 3). Results on the optimal mix of positive comments versus strategies for change aligned with results from the prior study; [39] however, the balance of responses was somewhat divergent. In particular, the Delphi study found that 45% of experts believed that only positive comments would be best (with 55% supporting a balanced approach) when participants were experiencing weight loss success, whereas only 11% of participants in the current study supported this approach (and with 72% supporting a balanced approach). Previous research also found that a balance of 4 positive comments and 4 comments suggesting areas for change led to the highest intentions to self‐monitor in a single‐session, randomized factorial study examining the impact of different feedback messages on self‐reported intentions to self‐monitor [11]. Data regarding the optimal balance of positive comments versus suggested strategies for change in relation to actual behavior change or intervention outcomes (e.g., beyond self‐reported intentions to change) have not been collected; thus, this gap has informed the development of an additional study (ClinicalTrials.gov NCT07292389), which uses a similar, micro‐randomized design to investigate the impact of valence on self‐monitoring adherence, goal attainment, and weight change the week after each feedback message is received.

The current pilot trial had several strengths, including implementation of the full intervention protocol that was proposed for a fully‐powered micro‐randomized factorial trial (including the same backend administrative technology system, the same REDCap data collection methods, and the same recruitment and assessment protocols), supporting the likelihood that these methods can be successfully replicated in a future trial. The proposed micro‐randomized factorial design is novel and holds the potential to serve as a model for other research questions aimed at building an evidence base for developing individually‐ and contextually‐adaptable digital health interventions.

Limitations of this study include the lack of statistical power to examine the impact of feedback messages on participants' weight or weight‐related behaviors (self‐monitoring adherence, dietary intake, physical activity) and issues related to generalizability. Lack of statistical power was by design, as this study was a pilot study; this limitation will be addressed via a recently‐launched, fully‐powered randomized controlled trial (ClinicalTrials.gov # NCT07227051), which is manipulating the same four components over the same period of time. As a second limitation, results from the current pilot may not be generalizable to the broader population of adults with obesity, as the current study used convenience sampling conducted with no demographic targets. In particular, the current study included few men (11% of the sample) even though rates of obesity in the United States do not differ significantly by gender or sex, and did not include any adults who identified as Hispanic or Latino/a, although Hispanic adults in the United States have the second‐highest rate of obesity compared to other racial/ethnic groups [40, 41]. The recently‐launched fully‐powered trial described above aims to recruit a sample including at least 30% men and 30% individuals from racial/ethnic backgrounds historically under‐represented in research; this will support generalizability of trial results and facilitate replication of the results regarding participant perceptions of feedback (as participants of this trial will be asked to complete the same post‐intervention questionnaire as used in the current study).

As an additional limitation, the accuracy of assessing self‐monitoring of physical activity via the Inspire 3 may also be limited as participants may have worn the Inspire 3 but not opened the Fitbit app to allow it to sync; however, this is unlikely given that participants were also asked to use the app to self‐monitor dietary intake (moreover, adherence to physical activity self‐monitoring was high at 87.3% of possible days, suggesting minimal data loss). The app is also not required to be opened every day for physical activity to sync; the Inspire 3 preserves historic physical activity data and syncs this data when the app is opened again. Finally, it is possible that the multi‐step process to access each week's feedback message may have reduced viewing of the message; however, our rate of weekly messages viewed (92%) was higher than in previous research (42.1%–66.5% of messages delivered multiple times per day) [33, 42]. Furthermore, given that the vast majority of messages were viewed (100% of participants viewed their weekly feedback message in Week 1 whereas 92.3% of participants viewed the message in Week 16), it appears that the multi‐step process was not a key barrier.

Conclusions

Despite prior work establishing the importance of feedback messaging to self‐monitoring adherence [6, 7, 9, 10] little is known about which type(s) of feedback messages may be most effective, and when messages should be delivered [11]. The current study aimed to assess the feasibility and acceptability of a micro‐randomized factorial trial examining the optimal delivery of tailored self‐monitoring feedback messages for adults with obesity attempting to make changes in dietary intake and physical activity to promote weight loss, in support of a future, fully‐powered micro‐randomized factorial trial. Results provided evidence of feasibility and acceptability, and this team is currently conducting two parallel trials. The first will answer the questions of how much and what kind of self‐monitoring feedback should be provided, to whom and in what context? And the second which will assess the optimal mix of comment valence (e.g., use of positive reinforcement only versus a mix of positive reinforcement versus strategies for change), similarly evaluating this in the context of for whom and in what context? Taken together, the results from these trials will support the development of an optimized protocol for providing self‐monitoring feedback to adults with obesity engaged in lifestyle changes to support weight loss.
