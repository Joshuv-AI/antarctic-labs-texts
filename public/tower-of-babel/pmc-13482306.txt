Time–temperature superposition of silicone rubber embedded with irregular-shaped magnetic particles under different magnetic fields
Authors: Nupur Rathod, Ramesh V. Upadhyay, Kinnari Parekh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The present work reports an investigation of the thermomechanical dynamic viscoelastic properties of a magnetorheological (MR) elastomer embedded with electrolytic iron particles (EIP, 40% wt, irregular shapes) in a silicone matrix. Unlike conventional MREs produced from spherical carbonyl iron particles, irregular flake-shaped EIPs offer greater particle–particle interactions and enhanced field sensitivity. The temperature rise from 30 to 70 °C significantly influences the matrix properties, altering the magnetic field-induced changes in the modulus as particle orientation with the field improves and friction between the particles and the matrix decreases. The thermomechanical dynamic viscoelastic properties of the present MRE confirm these interactions, along with the effects of strain amplitude (0.01 to 10%), frequency rate (0.1–40 Hz), and applied magnetic field strength (0.016 to 640 mT). The increase in temperature from 30 to 70 °C shows an overall 12.5% decrease in G’ while there is a 67% increase in G” for silicone rubber. In MRE40, there is an 18.9% reduction in the storage modulus and a 4% increase in the loss modulus. This variation indicates that the high aspect ratio of the iron particles initially provides higher stiffness, but above a certain temperature, thermal energy overcomes it, leading to a higher rate of decrease of G’ with temperature. Additionally, the time–temperature superposition (TTS) master curve is established for the present MRE. The damping behavior also varies with both temperature and field intensity, demonstrating the material’s dual sensitivity. This work introduces a novel method for creating thermally adjustable MREs using flake-shaped magnetic fillers, which could enhance vibration control isolation pads used to minimize thrust force, operating under varying temperature conditions.

Introduction

Viscoelastic materials (which exhibit both elasticity and viscosity) are sensitive to their operating temperature, which may be influenced by environmental or operating conditions. Temperature changes influence their stiffness (storage modulus) and damping (loss modulus), thereby affecting their performance in various engineering devices. Magnetorheological elastomers (MREs) involve integrating micro-sized magnetic particles into a polymer matrix and then curing the mixture. The magneto-mechanical properties of MRE are primarily influenced by the matrix characteristics, the saturation magnetization of the magnetic particles (fillers), the volume fraction of the magnetic particles, and the strength of the external magnetic field. Silicone rubber is the preferred matrix material due to its non-toxic and odorless attributes and pronounced temperature resistance1–3. Larger magnetic particles foster stronger interactions and can enhance the magneto-mechanical properties of the MRE.

Variation in the viscoelastic properties of MREs with temperature degrades the performance of MRE-based devices4. Anisotropic MREs were studied over a temperature range of 25 to 70 °C5,6. The results show that the storage modulus decreases rapidly below the transition temperature and then increases slightly. Isotropic MREs were studied over a wide temperature range (from − 31 to 47 °C) in shear mode at high frequency, demonstrating that temperature has a greater impact than other parameters7. I. Agirre-Olabide et al.8 reported the influence of the matrix on the linear viscoelastic region (LVE) in both isotropic and anisotropic MREs. The LVE decreases as temperature increases from 25 to 60 °C. Q. Wen et al.9 studied the shear-mode viscoelastic properties of anisotropic MREs and demonstrated that the storage modulus and magnetic field-induced modulus decrease with increasing temperature from 20 to 50 °C. Strain relaxation was also found to be highly dependent on temperature. M. Hemmatian et al.10 reported the temperature effect (-10 to 50 °C) on the shear-mode properties of MRE at different magnetic fields. The results revealed a strong temperature dependence of the moduli and presented a model to explain it. W. Zhang et al.11 reported temperature-dependent mechanical properties over 20–90 °C and explained them using a model developed for anisotropic MRE.

The most interesting observation across the above studies is the influence of the matrix on the MRE’s viscoelastic properties. In the natural rubber matrix11, the moduli decreased at 50 °C and then increased, whereas in cis-polybutadiene rubber11, the moduli decreased linearly until 90 °C. Lejon and Kari7 also reported that temperature has a greater influence on MRE’s viscoelastic properties than other parameters; particularly as the temperature decreases, the material enters the transition phase.

The reported thermomechanical properties4–11 generally involve spherical carbonyl iron particles (CIP) in elastomers with various matrices. The friction between the matrix and spherical particles is notably lower due to point contact. However, it has been shown12 that when a soft matrix containing CIP particles is exposed to a magnetic field, particle rearrangement occurs. Similarly, the influence of the environment on the mechanical properties of MREs with CIP particles was recently studied13. The research revealed a distinctive degradation pattern of the MRE linked to natural weathering in tropical climates. The surface damage of the MRE samples became severe and uneven during environmental aging, and degradation initiated at the exposed surface, resulting in significant alterations to the MRE’s mechanical properties.

The primary contribution of this work is to investigate the thermomechanical dynamic viscoelastic properties of a magnetorheological (MR) elastomer embedded with irregularly shaped electrolytic iron particles (EIP) in a silicone matrix. The selection of EIP was based on earlier studies by the group14–18, which showed that modulus values were higher than those of MREs based on CIP particles. This increase in modulus is attributed to increased friction between particles and between particles and the matrix. Models were introduced to explain the observed changes in viscoelastic properties of MREs in shear mode at different frequencies and magnetic fields17,18. The temperature rise alters the matrix properties, thereby amplifying magnetic-field-induced changes in the modulus. This effect is caused by increased particle orientation within the field, as the friction between the particles and the matrix decreases. The novelty of the present work is to correlate the time–temperature superposition (TTS) master curve in the case of MRE and also to show how one can make a universal curve to superimpose the effective normalized modulus curve with the normalized magnetic field for different temperatures.

Experimental

Materials

The electrolytic iron particles (EIP) were procured from Industrial Metal Powder, Pune, India. The Microtrac particle analysis yields an average size (d50) of 7–8 μm, and the atomic mass absorption analysis indicates a 97% iron concentration. A liquid silicone rubber, "RTV-1010," and its curing agent were purchased from Aditya Silicone, New Delhi. All the materials were used without further purification.

MRE synthesis

The preparation process of the MRE includes material blending, casting, curing, and demoulding, as shown in Fig. 1. Two MRE samples were prepared: one without EIP (MRE0) and one with EIP (MRE40). The 40% weight fraction of EIP was used here based on our earlier study, which reported that 40% is the percolation threshold for flaky-shaped iron particles.Fig. 1The schematic representation of the synthesis process of magnetorheological elastomer. The samples were cut from the location shown in the last part of the figure.

The schematic representation of the synthesis process of magnetorheological elastomer. The samples were cut from the location shown in the last part of the figure.

To synthesise the MRE sample, EIP particles were initially added to the base material (silicone rubber) while continuously stirring at 250–300 rpm (revolutions per minute) for 45 min. This ensures uniform dispersion of the particles within the base material. The mixture was then degassed in a vacuum chamber at 28 inches of mercury (Hg) for 5 min. Next, the cross-linking reaction was initiated by adding the appropriate amount of the curing agent while stirring. After adding the curing agent, the mixture was returned to the vacuum chamber for 2 min. Upon removal, the mixture was poured into a steel mold (size 200 × 200 × 1.1 mm3) and cured at ambient temperature (30 °C) for 24 h under a compression force of 196 N. This process produced an isotropic MRE40 sample with a thickness of 1 ± 0.05 mm. A pure silicone rubber sample (MRE0) was also prepared using the same procedure but without EIP. For rheological tests, samples were cut to a radius of 10 mm from different parts of the elastomer (See Fig. 1) to assess the homogeneity of the EIP distribution.

MRE characterisations

Physical characterisation

The X-ray diffraction (XRD) pattern was recorded using a Bruker D2-Phaser powder diffractometer with Cu Kα radiation and a Lynx Eye detector. Data were captured in the angular range of 30° to 70°. The step size and scan speed were optimised to obtain high-quality data.

Thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) was conducted using a Mettler Toledo TGA/DSC Star system. The samples were cut into small pieces to ensure a suitable sample size of approximately 15–20 mg. The TGA curve was recorded from 25 to 800 °C at a heating rate of 10 °C/min under air.

The Thermo Scientific Phenom Pharos G2 model was used to characterize the morphology of (a) flake-shaped iron particles and (b) pure silicone rubber MRE0 and MRE40 using field-emission scanning electron microscopy (FE-SEM). The samples were coated with platinum to enhance surface conductivity and achieve sharp, high-resolution images. Electron-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDX) was performed on MRE40 to determine its elemental composition.

The crosslink density of the MREs was determined through swelling measurements using the process shown in Fig. 2. A pre-weighed dry MRE sample was immersed in 80 mL of toluene at 30 °C for 72 h in a vacuum chamber. The toluene was replaced every 36 h to ensure the samples reached swelling equilibrium. After 72 h, the sample was removed, and the excess toluene on its surface was wiped off with filter paper. The swollen sample was weighed (with an accuracy of ± 1 mg) to record its mass. Rheological characterization

To perform rheological characterizations of the elastomeric samples, a rotary rheometer (MCR 301, Anton-Paar) was used. The MCR 301 has a magneto-rheology accessory, MRD 1 T/170, that varies the magnetic field from 0.016 mT (a residual field of the magnet assembly) to 1 T. The temperature was controlled via a circulating oil bath. Figure 3 shows the schematic of the test setup and the magneto-rheology accessory. The magneto-rheology accessory, illustrated in Fig. 3, generates a homogeneous magnetic field normal to the sample’s surface. The sample is placed between two parallel plates with a 20 mm diameter. A Hall probe (model 1010) was also positioned beneath the MRE sample to measure the applied magnetic field.Fig. 3The rheometer test set-up and Schematic of the oscillatory setup for shear strain tests.

The rheometer test set-up and Schematic of the oscillatory setup for shear strain tests.

The 20 mm-diameter, 1 mm-thick MRE (rubber) sample was placed between the plates. The sample was subjected to a 5 N axial compressive load to ensure adhesion to the upper and lower plates without slippage between the sample and the plates during the rotational shear tests. The normal force of 5 N in the parallel-plate configuration induces significant pre-compression of the sample, leading to a multiaxial stress state. The imposed normal force can influence the internal microstructure of the magnetorheological elastomer (MRE), particularly by reducing inter-particle spacing and modifying particle–particle interactions. Under an applied magnetic field, such pre-compression may further affect the formation, alignment, and stability of field-induced particle chain structures, thereby contributing to the observed mechanical response. Therefore, the reported values of moduli are effective values, not absolute.

All tests were conducted in the controlled oscillatory shear strain mode. The motor torque (M) was initially controlled to apply the command shear strain, keeping the plate gap (h) fixed. The applied torque and angular displacement (θ) signals were recorded to evaluate the response stress and induced shear strain. The temperature was varied from 30 to 70 °C in 10 °C steps using a circulating oil bath.

The magneto-rheological properties of MRE depend on operating conditions, such as shear strain amplitude, frequency, and the applied magnetic field. They also depend on environmental factors, such as temperature. In this study, we first checked for sample homogeneity and data reproducibility using a controlled oscillatory test, sweeping the shear strain amplitude from 0.01 to 10% at 1 Hz. In the second set, we examined the effect of frequency on temperature dependence by varying frequencies from 0.1 to 40 Hz, while keeping the strain fixed at 0.1% (in the linear viscoelastic limit, LVE). All these experiments were conducted at various controlled temperatures (30, 40, 50, 60, and 70 °C) and at different magnetic fields (0.016, 160, 320, 480, and 640 mT). This experimental design was repeated for an unfilled silicone rubber (MRE0) sample.

The shear stress and shear strain data were analyzed to determine the viscoelastic properties of the MRE and unfilled silicone rubber samples, specifically the effective storage modulus (G') and loss modulus (G”). In parallel-plate geometry, the maximum strain and stress occur at the specimen’s edges; therefore, these values were used to determine the viscoelastic properties. The stress response exhibits oscillation to a given oscillatory strain, characterized by amplitude (γ) and frequency (f), leading to a strain input with a δ phase shift. Significantly, the stress response depends heavily on viscoelastic properties. A normal force (compression load) of 5 N was applied in this case. It is essential to note that this 5 N normal force can result in multiaxial loading on the sample. Consequently, the effective storage modulus (G’) and loss modulus (G”) are regarded as effective rather than absolute values. The study primarily aims to demonstrate qualitative performance differences among the materials under varying working conditions and temperatures, rather than to accurately measure their effective moduli.

Frequency sweep tests are essential for evaluating the dynamic and stiffness properties of MRE40 in vibration and acoustic devices. The frequency sweep experiment was conducted on MRE40, maintaining a strain of 0.1% (LVE regime) and varying the frequency from 0.01 to 40 Hz. These frequency sweeps were performed in off-state and on-state magnetic fields at different temperatures.

The storage modulus (G’) and loss modulus (G’’) were measured under a shear strain amplitude ranging from 0.01% to 10% at a constant frequency of 1.0 Hz. The low strain (10%) was selected to avoid slipping of sample during data acquisition. The oscillatory test was conducted on three samples. The influence of temperature on the G’ and G” of the (a) MRE0, (b) MRE40.

The increase in temperature provides the polymer chains in the silicone rubber with more thermal energy, allowing them to move more freely and increasing molecular mobility. At the same time, these chains can rearrange themselves and overcome intermolecular forces. The overall result is a decrease in the storage modulus of the pure elastomer. On the contrary, the increase in internal molecular friction and enhanced chain mobility leads to greater dissipation energy, resulting in a higher loss modulus with increasing temperature. It is apparent from Fig. 7a that the overall decrease in G’ is 12.5%, while there is a 67% increase in G” with an increase in temperature from 30 to 70 °C. However, the elastomer maintains its elasticity up to 1% strain and shows no significant degradation over the studied temperature range.

Figure 7b shows the influence of temperature on the MRE40 sample, measured over a shear strain range of 0.01–10% at 1 Hz. A similar trend of decreasing and increasing G’ and G” was observed. However, the storage modulus decreased from about 111 to 90 kPa as the temperature increased from 30 to 70 °C, while the loss modulus increased from 12.5 to 14.9 kPa. This represents an 18.9% reduction in the effective storage modulus and a 4% increase in the loss modulus, which is exactly the opposite of what was observed in the pure silicone elastomer (MRE0). This variation indicates that the high aspect ratio of the iron particles initially provides higher stiffness, but above a certain temperature, thermal energy overcomes it, leading to a higher rate of decrease of G’ with temperature. This conclusion supports the observed higher percentage decrease in effective G’ in MRE40 compared to MRE0.

At 30 °C, the filler effect of storage and loss modulus for MRE40 are 15.94% and 94.13%, respectively, in a zero magnetic field. Figure 8a shows the filler effect for G’ and G’’ as a function of temperature. When the temperature rises above 30 °C, the filler effect shows more enhancement in the loss modulus. The effective storage modulus of the silicone rubber is almost independent of temperature, whereas its loss modulus decreases with increasing temperature. As the temperature rises, the filling effect on the storage modulus of the MRE40 decreases more quickly than the temperature effect on the loss modulus.Fig. 8(a) The filler effect with temperature on the effective storage and loss modulus of MRE0 and MRE40, (b) At 30 °C, the effective storage and loss modulus of MRE40 for the shear strain amplitude at varying applied magnetic flux density levels.

(a) The filler effect with temperature on the effective storage and loss modulus of MRE0 and MRE40, (b) At 30 °C, the effective storage and loss modulus of MRE40 for the shear strain amplitude at varying applied magnetic flux density levels.

In Fig. 8b, both the storage and loss modulus increase when the temperature is constant at 30 °C, and the magnetic field varies from 0.016 to 640 mT. These results also show that the iron particles’ magnetic saturation point limits the modulus of the MRE40 as the magnetic flux density approaches 640 mT. Beyond this point, further increases in magnetic flux density do not substantially alter the interaction between the iron particles21. The same can be confirmed using the magnetic properties of EIP particles, as described by Laherisheth Z et al.21. The results indicate that the viscoelastic properties of MRE40 exhibit a highly nonlinear temperature dependence, which is influenced by changes in strain amplitude and magnetic field density.

Frequency sweep experiments at different temperatures and magnetic fields

The data acquired in the second series of experiments were analyzed to understand the influence of temperature on the viscoelastic properties of filled magneto-elastomer and unfilled silicone rubber. The amplitude sweep experiments were used to identify the linear viscoelastic region (LVE) (Fig. 7), where effective G′ remains nearly constant at low strain amplitudes and begins to decrease beyond a critical strain, indicating the onset of nonlinear behavior. The LVE region is defined here as the strain range over which the storage modulus (G′) deviates by less than 5% from its plateau value. Based on this criterion, a strain amplitude of 0.1% was selected for subsequent frequency sweep measurements, as it lies well within the LVE region for all samples and temperatures studied. As evident from Fig. 7, G′ exhibits a clear plateau at low strain and begins to decrease beyond approximately 0.3% strain at 30 °C, with an earlier and more pronounced deviation at elevated temperature (70 °C), supporting the conservative selection of 0.1%. It is important to note that the LVE limit can depend on frequency. Although strain amplitude sweeps were performed at a fixed frequency, the selected strain (0.1%) is sufficiently small and is therefore expected to remain within the LVE region across the entire frequency range investigated. This assumption is considered reasonable based on amplitude-sweep measurements at 1–10 Hz (data not shown), which showed a linear trend at 0.1%. So, the LVE region remains constant up to 10 Hz. Both elastomers were subjected to shear strain excitation within the frequency range of 0.1 to 40 Hz, with a fixed strain amplitude of 0.1%.

The reference field (Bref), the point from where a low-to-high magnetic field transformation occurs, decreases initially (till T = 40 °C) and thereafter increases. The critical transition temperature of the RTV rubber must be between 40 and 50 °C. Because RTV silicone cures at room temperature, accelerated curing can occur at up to 50 °C. However, this is not a structural “transition” from a solid to a brittle state. Thus, above 40 °C, the matrix becomes softer, and the particle’s orientation to the magnetic field increases because of the decreased friction between the particle and the matrix. Reducing friction enhances magnetic interaction, thereby increasing the reference magnetic field. The observed softening with increasing temperature is a characteristic of the matrix. Thus, it is also realized in the variation in the μ parameter. Here, it initially increases, then decreases, above 40 °C. The temperature between 40 and 50 °C is attributed to thermally activated changes in particle mobility and rearrangement within the elastomer matrix, which can influence particle–particle interactions and the formation of magnetic structures.

Time temperature superposition application

The time–temperature superposition principle (TTS) establishes the relationship between temperature and time for thermo-rheologically complex materials in the form of a master curve. Typically, the TTS model applies to simple materials, where the dependence of the elastic constant is independent of temperature, and the time constants exhibit identical temperature dependence23–25. To obtain the master curve for these materials, only a horizontal shift is necessary. This horizontal shift defines the temperature-dependence and aligns with various temperature dependence models26,27. However, few researchers have utilized the TTS approach to explain multi-phase composites that do not differ significantly from simple materials28–30; yet, this approach has not yielded successful results. In contrast, researchers have successfully demonstrated that, in a multicomponent system in which components may share the same temperature dependence or lack it29,30, the TTS model can be applied. A multi-component system may require both vertical and horizontal shifts to superpose curves, with the changes primarily affecting the vertical density shift of the polymer matrix or its components.

The MRE consists of iron particles and a rubber matrix, making it a multi-component system. Nevertheless, the iron particles possess temperature-independent properties (density and magnetic), so the TTS model can be used to create a master curve. As discussed by Takato Nakano (2013)30, the TTS model holds for a multi-phase system in which some components have no or nearly no temperature dependence, while others exhibit the same temperature dependence. The variation of horizontal shift with temperature is generally explained by considering the Arrhenius relation, Eq. (14), and/or the Williams–Landel–Ferry (WLF) relation30, Eq. To apply the TTS concept to thermo-rheological materials, it is crucial to determine whether the elastic constants are temperature-independent or exhibit similar behavior at each temperature by plotting a Cole–Cole plot.
