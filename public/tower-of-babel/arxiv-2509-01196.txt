Regularity and dynamics of weak solutions for one-dimensional compressible Navier-Stokes equations with vacuum Thanks: ∗ corresponding author
Jin Tan ∗ , Yan-Lin Wang and Lan Zhang

Abstract
. In the spirit of D. Hoff’s weak solution theory for the compressible Navier-Stokes equations (CNS) with bounded density, in this paper we establish the global existence and regularity properties of finite-energy weak solutions to an initial boundary value problem of one-dimensional CNS with general initial data and vacuum. The core of our proof is a global in time a priori estimate for one-dimensional CNS that holds for any H 1 H^{1} initial velocity and bounded initial density not necessarily strictly positive: it could be a density patch or a vacuum bubble. We also establish that the velocity and density decay exponentially to equilibrium. As a by-product, we obtain the quantitative dynamics of aforementioned two vacuum states.



1.
Introuduction

In this paper, we are concerned with the following initial boundary value problem (IBVP) of one-dimensional compressible Navier-Stokes (CNS) equations:

(1.1a)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\rho+\partial_{x}(\rho u)=0,

\displaystyle\ \ {\rm in}\ \ \Omega,

(1.1b)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}(\rho u)+\partial_{x}(\rho u^{2})+\partial_{x}p=\partial_{x}(\mu(\rho)\partial_{x}u),

\displaystyle\ \ {\rm in}\ \ \Omega,

(1.1c)

\displaystyle(\rho,u)(0,x)=(\rho_{0},u_{0})(x),

\displaystyle\ \ {\rm in}\ \ \Omega,

(1.1d)

\displaystyle u=0,\ \ \ p(\rho)-\mu(\rho)\partial_{x}u=0,

\displaystyle\ \ {\rm on}\ \ \partial\Omega,

where
\Omega
is taken as
\Omega:=(0,1)
and
(t,x)\in\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\Omega.
Here, the unknowns
\rho
and
u
denote fluid density and velocity, respectively. The pressure
p=p(\rho)
is a given function of density.
The viscosity coefficient
\mu(\rho)
is not necessarily a positive constant, as it is well known that the viscosity of a gas depends on the temperature and thus on the density in the current isentropic case. To cover a wide range of gas dynamics, in the sequel of the paper the pressure law and viscosity coefficient will be given in the general way that:

(1.2)

\displaystyle p(s)\in C^{1}(\mathbb{R}_{+}),~~p(s),~p^{\prime}(s)\geq 0\quad{\rm and}\quad p~~{\rm is~convex};

\displaystyle\mu(s)\in C^{1}(\mathbb{R}_{+}),~~\mu_{*}\leq\mu(s)\leq\mu^{*}~{\rm for~some~constants}~\mu_{*},~\mu^{*}>0,

(1.3)

\displaystyle\quad\qquad\quad\qquad\quad{\rm and}\quad s\,\int_{1}^{s}\frac{\mu(\bar{s})}{\bar{s}}\,d\bar{s}~\quad{\rm is~convex}.

Examples of admissible pressure and viscosity include

p(\rho)=C_{1}\rho^{\gamma}\quad{\rm and}\quad\mu(\rho)=\mu_{*}+C_{2}\rho^{\beta},\quad{\rm for~constants}~C_{1}>0,\,C_{2}\geq 0,~~\gamma\geq 1,\,\beta\geq 0,

which corresponds to polytropic perfect gases.

Thanks to the boundary condition (
1.1d
), it is clear that the total mass and momentum of sufficiently smooth solutions of IBVP (
1.1
) are conserved through evolution, namely, for any
t>0,

(1.4)

\int_{\Omega}\rho(t,x)\,dx=\int_{\Omega}\rho_{0}\,dx=M_{0}\quad{\rm and}\quad\int_{\Omega}(\rho u)(t,x)\,dx=\int_{\Omega}(\rho_{0}u_{0})(x)\,dx.

We shall always assume that

(1.5)

\int_{\Omega}\rho_{0}\,dx=1\quad{\rm and}\quad\int_{\Omega}(\rho_{0}u_{0})(x)\,dx=0.

Let us denote by
e
the potential energy of the fluid. By the relation
\rho e^{\prime\prime}(\rho)=p^{\prime}(\rho)
and the normalization
e(1)=e^{\prime}(1)=0,
then
e(\rho)
is given by

\displaystyle e(\rho)=\rho\int_{1}^{\rho}\frac{p(s)}{s^{2}}ds-p(1)(\rho-1).

Hence,
e
is a strictly convex function and
\|e\|_{L^{1}(\Omega)}
is essentially equivalent to
\|\rho-1\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}.
Next, introduce the total energy

E(t):=\int_{\Omega}[2e(\rho)+\rho u^{2}](t,x)\,dx,

one has the following energy balance: for any
t>0,

(1.6)

E(t)+2\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\Omega}(\mu(\rho)|\partial_{x}u|^{2})(\tau,x)\,dxd\tau=E_{0}:=E(0).

The compressible Navier-Stokes equations have been extensively investigated since the foundational work of Nash
[
37
]
, in which the local existence and uniqueness of classical solutions for isentropic flows in the absence of vacuum (where density is zero) is established. Building upon this fundamental local well-posedness result, substantial progress has been achieved in constructing
global-in-time solutions
, encompassing both vacuum and non-vacuum cases, through the development of three distinct frameworks: the strong solution theory, Lions’ weak solution theory, and Hoff’s weak solution theory with bounded density and small energy, as detailed in the following contributions.

Strong solution theory.
The global existence of classic or strong solutions with arbitrary large initial data is not known generally. Only the one-dimensional theory is quite satisfactory.
The foundational results on global well-posedness for one-dimensional flows with large initial data away from vacuum trace back to the seminal contributions of Kanel
′

[
27
]
and Kazhikhov-Shelukhin
[
28
]
. For higher dimensional case, initial data are usually required close to constant equilibrium and global small smooth solutions are constructed based on the corresponding linearized system, see for example Matsumura and Nishida
[
36
]
, and also Danchin
[
7
]
for a critical functional framework. Surprisingly, Vaĭgant and Kazhikhov
[
44
]
investigated a class of two-dimensional isentropic compressible Navier-Stokes equations with specified density-dependent viscosity coefficients and provided the existence and uniqueness of global strong solutions with general large initial data.
Regarding strong solutions with possible vacuum, the problem of local well-posedness for the isentropic case was resolved by Salvi and Stras̆kraba
[
42
]
, followed by contributions from Cho, Choe, and Kim
[
5
]
, and Cho and Kim
[
6
]
. The first proof of global strong solutions for the isentropic compressible Navier-Stokes equations allowing large initial oscillations and vacuum was given by Huang, Li, and Xin
[
23
]
. Further developments in this direction were contributed by Li and Xin
[
30
,
47
]
. Improvements in Vaĭgant and Kazhikhov type global large solutions to include vacuum were established in Jiu-Wang-Xin
[
26
]
. See also Huang and Li
[
22
]
for more general parameter case.

Lions’ weak solution theory.
Consider the isentropic compressible Navier-Stokes equations with general initial data, Lions
[
31
]
first proved the global existence of finite-energy weak solutions for polytropic perfect gases with
\gamma>\frac{9}{5}
. Subsequent work by Feireisl, Novotný, and Petzeltová
[
14
]
relaxed this requirement to
\gamma>\frac{3}{2}
, while Jiang and Zhang
[
25
]
further generalized it to
\gamma>1
in the axisymmetric case. Later, Feireisl and Novotný
[
13
]
generalized the analysis to the case of compressible viscous heat-conducting fluids. For more recent advances in the density-dependent viscosity case, see Bresch and Jabin
[
1
]
and references therein.
Solutions in this very general class possess rather little regularity so that analysis of their detailed qualitative properties seems difficult. In particular, the question of uniqueness is largely open.

Hoff’s weak solution theory.
It is started from the work
[
16
]
of Hoff, in which the global existence of solutions for one-dimensional isentropic flows with large initial velocity in
L^{2}
and initial density in
L^{2}\cap BV
is established. Chen, Hoff, and Trivisa
[
2
]
later generalized this result to the non-isentropic case in a bounded domain. See also recent developments in
[
33
,
3
,
45
,
46
,
4
]
.
For multi-dimensional case, this type of solutions is limited to have small initial energy and has been constructed by Hoff
[
17
,
18
]
. We also mention its extension to the compressible MHD system in
[
43
]
.
Solutions in this class usually have bounded density, which is physically relevant. Linearization method is not valid in this class, and solutions may exhibit truly nonlinear and physically interesting effects. For example, it allows piecewise smooth density profiles. Moreover, these solutions have more regularity properties than Lions’ type weak solutions such that a reasonable uniqueness theory is possible, at least for one-dimensional case, see Danchin, Fanelli, and Paicu
[
8
]
.
For higher dimensional case, the uniqueness issue of Hoff type weak solutions presents significant challenges. Recently, Danchin and Mucha
[
9
]
made important progress in this direction. More precisely, under the assumptions that the initial velocity lies in
H^{1}(\mathbb{T}^{3})
and initial density is merely bounded and nonnegative, and that bulk viscosity is sufficiently large, they established the global existence of solutions in the two-dimenisonal case. Moreover, in the case that the pressure law is linear, the solution is unique.
Similar result concerning existence and uniqueness were also obtained in
\mathbb{T}^{3}
, either locally in time for general large data or globally under an appropriate scaling-invariant smallness condition on the velocity field (with no smallness restriction on the density). Exponential decay of the corresponding solutions in the two-dimensional torus was proved later by Danchin and Wang
[
10
]
.

To the best of our knowledge, Hoff’s weak solution theory is not known for the one-dimensional isentropic flows with arbitrary large initial data and vacuum. This unsatisfactory gap in the theory motivates the present work.

1.1.
Main results

In this paper, we establish the global existence, regularity properties and decay estimates of finite-energy weak solutions
à la Hoff
for the IBVP (
1.1
) with large rough initial data in a vacuum. Throughout the paper, we use
\dot{v}
and sometimes
\frac{D}{Dt}v
to denote the material derivative of

i.e.
\dot{v}=\partial_{t}v+u\partial_{x}v.

To state our result, let us first give the definition of weak solutions.

Definition 1
.

A pair of function
(\rho,u)
is said to be a Hoff type global weak solution of system (
1.1
) provided that
\rho\geq 0
almost everywhere and

\displaystyle\rho\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\Omega)\cap\mathcal{C}(\mathbb{R}_{+};L^{r}(\Omega)),~\sqrt{\rho}u\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}_{+},L^{2}(\Omega)),

\displaystyle\partial_{x}u\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\Omega),

and that

\displaystyle\left.\int_{\Omega}\rho\phi\,dx\right|^{t_{2}}_{t_{1}}

\displaystyle=\int_{t_{1}}^{t_{2}}\int_{\Omega}(\rho\partial_{\tau}\phi+\rho u\partial_{x}\phi)\,dxdt,

\displaystyle\left.\int_{\Omega}\rho u\phi\,dx\right|^{t_{2}}_{t_{1}}

\displaystyle=\int_{t_{1}}^{t_{2}}\int_{\Omega}\{\rho u(\partial_{\tau}\phi+u\partial_{x}\phi)+(p(\rho)-\mu(\rho)\partial_{x}u)\partial_{x}\phi\}\,dxdt,

for any
t_{1}
and
t_{2}
satisfying
0<t_{1}<t_{2}<\infty
and test function
\phi\in\mathcal{C}_{0}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\Omega)
.

Theorem 1.1
.

Assume that the pressure
p(\rho)
and the viscosity
\mu(\rho)
satisfy conditions (
1.2

(\rho_{0},u_{0})
satisfy (

\displaystyle 0\leq\rho_{0}(x)\leq\rho^{*}~~{\rm~for~some~positive~constant}~\rho^{*},\quad{\rm and}\quad\sqrt{\rho_{0}}u_{0}\in L^{2}(\Omega).

Then,
IBVP

Let us make a few remarks on Theorem
1.1
.

Remark 1.1
.

1.

A major difficulty on the proof of Theorem
1.1
is the lack of regularity of the density, which also highlights our result on the other side.
Moreover, the case that the initial density contains a vacuum gives an extreme difficulty in obtaining robust compactness property for the density. Note that Hoff’s original Lagrangian type strategy
[
19
]
for proving strong convergence of density fails if there is a vacuum, because he use essentially the condition that density is far away from vacuum (it is satisfied initially) so that the Jacobian of particle trajectories is non-degenerate. Our strategy is quite different, it replies on a global-in-time a priori estimate (see Lemma
2.3
and also Lemma
2.4
) for (
1.1
) that valid for any
H^{1}
initial velocity and bounded initial density not necessarily strictly positive.
More precisely, we observe that Hoff type weak solutions are “strong solutions” instanously after time evolution, even in the presence of vacuum. With those additional regularity properties of the velocity, we finally obtain the global existence and (conditional) uniqueness of Hoff type weak solutions.

2.

The dependence of viscosity on density makes it hard to trace the trajectory of vacuum state. In fact, degenerate viscosity coefficient may even lead to singularity formation for (
1.1
). For example, in
[
29
]
it demonstrates that any possible vacuum state vanishes in finite time and the velocity blows up in finite time if a vacuum state appears initially. Our Theorem
1.1
excludes formation of singularity for general Hoff type weak solutions with vacuum whenever the density-dependent viscosity is not degenerate. Actually, our argument is still valid even if
the viscosity function
\mu(s)
vanishes as
s
tends to zero, in the case that initial densities are far away from vacuum.
Moreover, if
\mu\equiv{\rm Constant},
exponential time decay of
\partial_{x}u
is obtained in Proposition
5.2
.

3.

The regularity assumption
\partial_{x}u_{0}\in L^{2}
in the uniqueness statement is not essential, it should be possible to have less regular initial velocities, as in
[
4
]
. We do not pursue in this direction in the present paper.

4.

The boundary conditions in (
1.1d
) are used frequently in order to have a representation of the pressure, this plays an important role in our analysis. We leave the domain cases
\mathbb{T}
and
\mathbb{R}
for future work.

2.
A priori estimates

In this section, we will establish some necessary a priori bounds for smooth solutions away from vacuum for IBVP (
1.1

2.1.
Boundedness of density

At first, we provide a proof of the energy balance (
1.6
).

Lemma 2.1
.

Consider
(\rho,u)
with
\rho>0
a smooth solution of problem (
1.1
) defined on
[0,T]\times\Omega
for some
T>0.
Then for any
t\in[0,T],
we have

(2.1)

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}[e(\rho)+\rho u^{2}](t,x)dx+\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\Omega}|\sqrt{\mu(\rho)}\partial_{x}u|^{2}(s,x)dxds\leq C(\rho^{*},E_{0}),

where
C(\rho^{*},E_{0})
is a positive constant depending on
\rho^{*}
and
E_{0}.

Proof.
Multiply (
1.1a
) and (
1.1b
) by
e^{\prime}(\rho)
and
u
,respectively, and integrate with respect to space variable over
I
to obtain

(2.2)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\frac{d}{dt}\int_{\Omega}[2e(\rho)+\rho u^{2}](t,x)dx+\int_{\Omega}|\sqrt{\mu(\rho)}\partial_{x}u|^{2}(t,x)dx=0,

where we used integration by parts and boundary conditions in (
1.1d
). Then we integrate the above equality with respect to time variable to obtain (
1.6
).
Because
e(\cdot)
is continuous and
\rho_{0}
is bounded, we obtain (
2.1
) consequently.
\Box

Lemma 2.2
.

Assume that
(\rho,u)
with
\rho>0
is a smooth solution of problem (
1.1
) defined on
[0,T]\times\Omega
for some
T>0.
We have the following upper bound for density:

(2.3)

\displaystyle\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\|\rho(t,\cdot)\|_{L^{\infty}}\leq C(E_{0},\rho^{*},\mu_{*},\mu^{*}).

Proof.

Given that
\rho(t,x)
is smooth and positive, we can rewrite (
1.1a
) as

(2.4)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\ln\rho+u\partial_{x}\ln\rho+\partial_{x}u=0.

It follows from equation (
1.1b
) and boundary condition (
1.1d
) that

(2.5)

\displaystyle\mu(\rho)u_{x}-\rho u^{2}-p(\rho)=\partial_{t}(\partial_{x}^{-1}(\rho u)).

Recall that
\partial_{x}^{-1}(\rho u)
denotes the integral
\int_{0}^{x}(\rho u)(t,y)dy
for
x\in(0,1).

We then combine (
2.4
) and (
2.5
) to write

(2.6)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\left[\mathcal{U}(\rho)+\partial_{x}^{-1}(\rho u)\right]+u\partial_{x}\left[\mathcal{U}(\rho)+\partial_{x}^{-1}(\rho u)\right]=-p(\rho)

with

(2.7)

\mathcal{U}(\rho)=\int_{1}^{\rho}\frac{\mu(s)}{s}ds.

Next, we define a Lagrangian trajectory

associated with

\frac{d}{dt}X(t,y)=u(t,X(t,y)),\ \ X(t,y)|_{t=0}=y.

Then we obtain from equation (
2.5
) and the fact that the pressure is nonnegative that

\displaystyle\left[\mathcal{U}(\rho)+\partial_{x}^{-1}(\rho u)\right](t,X(t,y))=

\displaystyle\left[\mathcal{U}(\rho)+\partial_{x}^{-1}(\rho u)\right](0,x)-\int_{0}^{t}p(\rho)(s,X(s,y))ds

\displaystyle\leq\mathcal{U}(\rho_{0})+\partial_{x}^{-1}(\rho_{0}u_{0}).

Now, if
\rho\leq 1
for all
(t,x)\in[0,T]\times\Omega,
then we get (
2.3

1.3
) that

\displaystyle\mu_{*}\ln\rho(t,x)\leq\mathcal{U}(\rho)

\displaystyle\leq\mathcal{U}(\rho_{0})+\partial_{x}^{-1}(\rho_{0}u_{0})+\|\partial_{x}^{-1}(\rho u)\|_{L^{\infty}}

\displaystyle\leq\mu^{*}\ln{\rho^{*}}+\|\sqrt{\rho_{0}}u_{0}\|_{L^{2}}\|\sqrt{\rho_{0}}\|_{L^{2}}+\|\sqrt{\rho}u(t,\cdot)\|_{L^{2}}\|\sqrt{\rho(t,\cdot)}\|_{L^{2}}

(2.8)

\displaystyle\leq\mu^{*}\ln{\rho^{*}}+2E_{0},

where in the last inequality we used conservation of mass and energy balance (
1.6
).
Hence, we obtained (
2.3
). The proof of this lemma is completed.

2.2.
Lipschitz continuity of velocity for regular data

Here, we focus on the first-order spatial derivative estimate for the velocity that requires only a boundedness of density. To achieve it, we need to introduce the so-called effect viscous flux

G:=\mu(\rho)\partial_{x}u-p(\rho).

Lemma 2.3
.

Consider a smooth solution
(\rho,u)
with
\rho>0
defined on
[0,T]\times\Omega

1.1
).
Then we have

(2.9)

\sup_{[0,T]}\|\partial_{x}u(t,\cdot)\|^{2}_{L^{2}}+\int_{0}^{T}\|\partial_{x}u(t,\cdot)\|^{2}_{L^{\infty}}\,dt+\int_{0}^{T}\left(\|(\sqrt{\rho}\dot{u})(t,\cdot)\|^{2}_{L^{2}}+\|\partial_{x}G(t,\cdot)\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\right)\,dt\\
\leq C(\|\partial_{x}u_{0}\|_{L^{2}}^{2},E_{0},\rho^{*},\mu_{*}^{-1},\mu^{*},T).

Proof.
It is clear that with the aid of (
1.1a
), equation (
1.1b
) can be rewritten as

(2.10)

\displaystyle\rho\dot{u}+\partial_{x}p(\rho)=\partial_{x}(\mu(\rho)\partial_{x}u).

By taking

\dot{u},
we see that

\frac{1}{2}\frac{d}{dt}\|\sqrt{\mu(\rho)}\partial_{x}u\|^{2}_{L^{2}}+\|\sqrt{\rho}\dot{u}\|^{2}_{L^{2}}-\int_{I}p\partial_{x}\dot{u}dx\\
=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega}\partial_{t}(\mu(\rho))(\partial_{x}u)^{2}\,dx+\int_{\Omega}\partial_{x}(\mu(\rho)\partial_{x}u)(u\partial_{x}u)\,dx.

Notice that

(2.11)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}(\mu(\rho))+u\partial_{x}(\mu(\rho))+\mu^{\prime}(\rho)\rho\partial_{x}u=0

and

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\partial_{x}(\mu(\rho)\partial_{x}u)(u\partial_{x}u)\,dx=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega}\partial_{x}(\mu(\rho))(\partial_{x}u)^{2}u\,dx-\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega}\mu(\rho)(\partial_{x}u)^{3}\,dx,

we find from above that

(2.12)

\frac{1}{2}\frac{d}{dt}\|\sqrt{\mu(\rho)}\partial_{x}u\|^{2}_{L^{2}}+\|\sqrt{\rho}\dot{u}\|^{2}_{L^{2}}-\int_{\Omega}p\partial_{x}\dot{u}dx\\
=-\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega}\mu^{\prime}(\rho)\rho\partial_{x}u\,dx-\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega}\mu(\rho)(\partial_{x}u)^{3}\,dx.

Using that

\int_{\Omega}p\partial_{t}\partial_{x}udx=\frac{d}{dt}\int_{\Omega}p\partial_{x}udx-\int_{\Omega}\partial_{t}p\partial_{x}udx

and (
1.1a
), we rewrite (
2.12
) as

(2.13)

\frac{1}{2}\frac{d}{dt}\|\sqrt{\mu(\rho)}\partial_{x}u\|^{2}_{L^{2}}+\|\sqrt{\rho}\dot{u}\|^{2}_{L^{2}}-\frac{d}{dt}\int_{\Omega}p\partial_{x}udx\\
=-\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega}\left(\mu^{\prime}(\rho)\rho\partial_{x}u+\mu(\rho)(\partial_{x}u)^{3}\right)\,dx+\int_{\Omega}p^{\prime}(\rho)\rho(\partial_{x}u)^{2}\,dx.

Then, with the aid of
p,\mu\in C^{1}(\mathbb{R}_{+})
and the boundedness of
\rho
in (
2.3
), we get from (
2.12
) that

(2.14)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\frac{d}{dt}(\|\sqrt{\mu(\rho)}\partial_{x}u\|^{2}_{L^{2}}-2\int_{\Omega}p\partial_{x}udx)+\|\sqrt{\rho}\dot{u}\|^{2}_{L^{2}}\leq

\displaystyle C(\|\rho\|_{L^{\infty}})(\|\partial_{x}u\|_{L^{\infty}}+1)\|\partial_{x}u\|^{2}_{L^{2}}.

At this stage, it is necessary to estimate pressure. Thanks to (
1.1a
) and a basic energy estimate of
p(\rho),
one gets

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\|p(\rho)\|_{L^{2}}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\|\partial_{x}u\|_{L^{2}}\|p^{2}(\rho)\|_{L^{2}}+\|p^{\prime}(\rho)\rho p(\rho)\|_{L^{2}}\|\partial_{x}u\|_{L^{2}}

(2.15)

\displaystyle\leq C(\|\rho\|_{L^{\infty}})\|\partial_{x}u\|_{L^{2}},

where the boundedness of
\rho
is used again.

Now we combine (
2.14
) and (
2.15
) to write

\frac{d}{dt}\left(\|\sqrt{\mu(\rho)}\partial_{x}u\|^{2}_{L^{2}}+8{\mu_{*}}^{-1}\|p(\rho)\|^{2}_{L^{2}}-2\int_{\Omega}p\partial_{x}udx\right)+\|\sqrt{\rho}\dot{u}\|^{2}_{L^{2}}\\
\leq C(\|\rho\|_{L^{\infty}},\mu_{*}^{-1})\left((\|\partial_{x}u\|_{L^{\infty}}+1)\|\partial_{x}u\|^{2}_{L^{2}}+\|\partial_{x}u\|_{L^{2}}\right)

Due to the fact that

\displaystyle\|\sqrt{\mu(\rho)}\partial_{x}u\|^{2}_{L^{2}}+8\mu_{*}^{-1}\|p(\rho)\|^{2}_{L^{2}}-2\int_{\Omega}p\partial_{x}udx

\displaystyle\geq\mu_{*}\|\partial_{x}u\|^{2}_{L^{2}}+8\mu_{*}^{-1}\|p(\rho)\|^{2}_{L^{2}}-\frac{1}{2}(4\mu_{*}^{-1}\|p\|^{2}_{L^{2}}+\mu_{*}\|\partial_{x}u\|^{2}_{L^{2}})\geq\frac{1}{2}(\mu_{*}\|\partial_{x}u\|^{2}_{L^{2}}+\mu_{*}^{-1}\|p(\rho)\|_{L^{2}}^{2}),

it yields that

\displaystyle\mu_{*}\|\partial_{x}u\|^{2}_{L^{2}}+\int_{0}^{t}\|(\sqrt{\rho}\dot{u})(s,x)\|^{2}_{L^{2}}ds

(2.16)

\displaystyle\leq\mu^{*}\|\partial_{x}u_{0}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+C(\|\rho\|_{L^{\infty}},\mu_{*}^{-1})\int_{0}^{t}\left((\|\partial_{x}u\|_{L^{\infty}}+1)\|\partial_{x}u\|^{2}_{L^{2}}+\|\partial_{x}u\|_{L^{2}}\right)(s,x)ds.

Now, we read (
2.10
) as

\displaystyle\partial_{x}(\mu(\rho)\partial_{x}u-p(\rho))=\rho\dot{u}

and write that

\displaystyle\|\partial_{x}(\mu(\rho)\partial_{x}u-p)\|_{L^{1}}=\|\rho\dot{u}\|_{L^{1}}\leq

\displaystyle\|{\rho}\|^{\frac{1}{2}}_{L^{1}}\|{\sqrt{\rho}\dot{u}}\|_{L^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\|{\sqrt{\rho}\dot{u}}\|_{L^{2}}

This together with the fact that
\dot{W}^{1,1}(\Omega)\hookrightarrow L^{\infty}(\Omega)
is valid for
G
, the boundedness of
\rho
and condition (
1.3
) yield that

(2.17)

\displaystyle\mu_{*}\|\partial_{x}u\|_{L^{\infty}}\leq\|\mu(\rho)\partial_{x}u-p(\rho)\|_{L^{\infty}}+\|p(\rho)\|_{L^{\infty}}\leq\|\sqrt{\rho}\dot{u}\|_{L^{2}}+C(\|\rho\|_{L^{\infty}}).

Finally, in virtue of (
2.16
), (
2.17
) and (
2.1
) and (
2.3
), we conclude that

\sup_{[0,T]}\|\partial_{x}u(t,\cdot)\|^{2}_{L^{2}}+\int_{0}^{T}\|\partial_{x}u(t,\cdot)\|^{2}_{L^{\infty}}\,dt+\int_{0}^{T}\|(\sqrt{\rho}\dot{u})(t,\cdot)\|^{2}_{L^{2}}ds+\int_{0}^{T}\|\partial_{x}G(t,\cdot)\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\,dx\\
\leq C(\|\partial_{x}u_{0}\|_{L^{2}}^{2},E_{0},\rho^{*},\mu_{*}^{-1},\mu^{*},T).

This completes the proof of Lemma
2.3
.
\Box

2.3.
Time-weighted energy estimates

It is clear that Lemma
2.3
requires a control of derivative of initial velocity, and hence does not apply to merely finite-energy initial data. In what follows, we show that it is possible to capture higher-order regularity estimates that similar to Lemma
2.3
by attaching additional time weights. This is due to the smoothing effect of the momentum equation (
1.1b
) and is a key to the proof of Theorem
1.1
.

Lemma 2.4
.

Consider a smooth solution
(\rho,u)
with
\rho>0
defined on
[0,T]\times\Omega
of problem (
1.1
).
Then we have

(2.18)

\sup_{[0,T]}\|\sqrt{t}\partial_{x}u(t,\cdot)\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+\int_{0}^{T}\left(\|\sqrt{t}\partial_{x}u(t,\cdot)\|^{2}_{L^{\infty}}+\|(\sqrt{\rho\,t}\dot{u})(t,\cdot)\|^{2}_{L^{2}}+\|\sqrt{t}\partial_{x}G(t,\cdot)\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\right)\,dt\\
\leq C(E_{0},\rho^{*},\mu_{*},\mu^{*},T).

Proof.
The proof of Lemma
2.4
is basically the same as for the Lemma
2.3
, just in the current case one needs to start estimation from the following time-weighted momentum equation:

(2.19)

\displaystyle\sqrt{t}\rho\dot{u}+\partial_{x}(\sqrt{t}p(\rho))=\partial_{x}(\mu(\rho)\partial_{x}(\sqrt{t}u)).

For reader’s convenience, we provide full details at here. Precisely, take
L^{2}
-inner product of (
2.19
) with
\sqrt{t}\dot{u},
we obtain that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\frac{d}{dt}\int_{\Omega}\mu(\rho)|\partial_{x}(\sqrt{t}u)|^{2}dx+\int_{\Omega}(\sqrt{t\rho}\dot{u})^{2}dx-\int_{\Omega}tp\partial_{x}\dot{u}dx

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega}\partial_{t}\mu(\rho)|\partial_{x}(\sqrt{t}u)|^{2}dx+\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega}\mu(\rho)|\partial_{x}u|^{2}dx+\int_{\Omega}\partial_{x}\mu(\rho)|\partial_{x}(\sqrt{t}u)|^{2}dx

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega}\mu(\rho)|\partial_{x}(\sqrt{t}u)|^{2}\partial_{x}udx.

Using the fact (
2.11
), we rewrite the above equality as follows

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\frac{d}{dt}\int_{\Omega}\mu(\rho)|\partial_{x}(\sqrt{t}u)|^{2}dx+\int_{\Omega}(\sqrt{t\rho}\dot{u})^{2}dx-\int_{\Omega}tp\partial_{x}\dot{u}dx

(2.20)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega}\mu(\rho)|\partial_{x}u|^{2}dx-\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega}(\mu(\rho)+\mu^{\prime}(\rho)\rho)|\partial_{x}(\sqrt{t}u)|^{2}\partial_{x}udx.

Together with the fact that

\displaystyle-\int_{\Omega}tp\partial_{x}\dot{u}dx=-\frac{d}{dt}\int_{\Omega}tp\partial_{x}udx+\int_{\Omega}t\partial_{t}p\partial_{x}udx+\int_{\Omega}p\partial_{x}udx+\int_{\Omega}t\partial_{x}pu\partial_{x}udx

and

(2.21)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}p(\rho)+\partial_{x}(p(\rho))u+p^{\prime}(\rho)\rho\partial_{x}u=0,

we deduce from (
2.20
) that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\frac{d}{dt}\int_{\Omega}\left(\mu(\rho)|\partial_{x}(\sqrt{t}u)|^{2}-2tp\partial_{x}u\right)dx+\int_{\Omega}(\sqrt{t\rho}\dot{u})^{2}dx

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega}\mu(\rho)|\partial_{x}u|^{2}dx-\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega}(\mu(\rho)+\mu^{\prime}(\rho)\rho)|\partial_{x}(\sqrt{t}u)|^{2}\partial_{x}udx

(2.22)

\displaystyle-\int_{\Omega}p\partial_{x}udx+\int_{\Omega}p^{\prime}(\rho)\rho|\partial_{x}(\sqrt{t}u)|^{2}dx.

The basic energy estimates for the pressure equation
\sqrt{t}\times\eqref{preequ}
gives

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\|\sqrt{t}p(\rho)\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\leq

\displaystyle\|p(\rho)\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+\|p^{2}(\rho)\|_{L^{2}}\|\sqrt{t}\partial_{x}(\sqrt{t}u)\|_{L^{2}}

(2.23)

\displaystyle+\|p(\rho)p^{\prime}(\rho)\rho\|_{L^{2}}\|\sqrt{t}\partial_{x}(\sqrt{t}u)\|_{L^{2}}.

With the aid of (
2.1
) and (
2.3
), we combine (
2.22
) and (
2.23
) to get

\displaystyle\|\partial_{x}(\sqrt{t}u)\|^{2}_{L^{2}}+\int_{0}^{t}\|(\sqrt{s\rho}\dot{u})(s,x)\|^{2}_{L^{2}}ds

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle C(\mu_{*}^{-1})E(0)+C(\mu_{*}^{-1},\mu^{*})\int_{0}^{t}\left(\|p(\rho)\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+|\partial_{x}(\sqrt{s}u)|_{L^{\infty}}s^{-1/2}\|\partial_{x}(\sqrt{s}u)\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\right)ds

(2.24)

\displaystyle+C(\mu_{*}^{-1},\mu^{*})\int_{0}^{t}\left((1+s)\|\partial_{x}(\sqrt{s}u)\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+\|\partial_{x}(\sqrt{s}u)\|_{L^{2}}\right)ds.

Moreover, similar to derive (
2.17
), we have

\displaystyle\mu_{*}\|\partial_{x}(\sqrt{t}u)\|_{L^{\infty}}

\displaystyle\leq\|\mu(\rho)\partial_{x}(\sqrt{t}u)-\sqrt{t}p(\rho)\|_{L^{\infty}}+\|\sqrt{t}p(\rho)\|_{L^{\infty}}

(2.25)

\displaystyle\leq\|\sqrt{t\rho}\dot{u}\|_{L^{2}}+C(\mu^{*})\sqrt{t}.

Now, with the help of (
2.24
), (
2.25
) and Gronwall’s inequality, we prove the inequality (
2.18
).
The proof of Lemma
2.4
is completed.
\Box

Roughly speaking, Lemma
2.4
and Lemma
2.3
indicate that weak solutions will become regular instanously. As such, one can try to find higher-order estimates under a better control. The following lemma plays an important role on the compactness result of density.

Lemma 2.5
.

Consider a smooth solution
(\rho,u)
with
\rho>0
defined on
[0,T]\times\Omega
of problem (
1.1
). Then, for all
t_{0}\in[0,T]
we have

(2.26)

\displaystyle\sup_{[t_{0},T]}\|\sqrt{t\rho}\dot{u}(t,\cdot)\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+\int_{t_{0}}^{T}\|\sqrt{t}\partial_{x}\dot{u}(t,\cdot)\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\,dt\leq C(\|\partial_{x}u(t_{0})\|_{,}E_{0},\rho^{*},\mu_{*},\mu^{*},T).

Proof.
At first, multiplying the momentum equation (
1.1b
) by
\sqrt{t}
and taking material derivative on the resulting equation yield that

(2.27)

\rho\frac{D}{Dt}\left(\sqrt{t}\dot{u}\right)+\partial_{x}\left(\sqrt{t}\dot{p}\right)-\partial_{x}\left(\mu(\rho)\sqrt{t}\partial_{x}\dot{u}\right)-\frac{1}{2\sqrt{t}}\rho\dot{u}+\left(\sqrt{t}\partial_{x}u\right)\rho\dot{u}\\
=-\dot{\rho}\left(\sqrt{t}\dot{u}\right)+\partial_{x}\left(\left(\sqrt{t}\partial_{x}u\right)\frac{D}{Dt}\mu(\rho)-\mu(\rho)\sqrt{t}(\partial_{x}u)^{2}\right).

We integrate the product of (
2.27
) and
\sqrt{t}\dot{u}
over
\Omega
and use boundary condition (
1.1d
) to write

(2.28)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\frac{d}{dt}\|\sqrt{\rho\,t}\dot{u}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+\|\sqrt{t\,\mu(\rho)}\partial_{x}\dot{u}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}=R_{1}+\cdots R_{5},

where

\displaystyle R_{1}=\int_{\Omega}\partial_{x}\left(\sqrt{t}\dot{u}\right)\,\left(\sqrt{t}\dot{p}\right)\,dx,

\displaystyle R_{2}=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega}\rho(\dot{u})^{2}\,dx,

\displaystyle R_{3}=-\int_{\Omega}\left(t\partial_{x}u\right)\rho(\dot{u})^{2}\,dx,

\displaystyle R_{4}=-\int_{\Omega}\dot{\rho}\left(\sqrt{t}\dot{u}\right)^{2}\,dx,

\displaystyle R_{5}=-\int_{\Omega}\partial_{x}\left(\sqrt{t}\dot{u}\right)\left(\sqrt{t}\partial_{x}u\right)\frac{D}{Dt}\mu(\rho)\,dx,

\displaystyle R_{6}=\int_{\Omega}\partial_{x}\left(\sqrt{t}\dot{u}\right)\mu(\rho)\sqrt{t}(\partial_{x}u)^{2}\,dx.

Next, we will deal with the remainder terms. Firstly, notice that

\displaystyle R_{2}=\frac{1}{2}\|\sqrt{\rho}\dot{u}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}

and

\displaystyle|R_{3}|\leq\|\partial_{x}u\|_{L^{\infty}}\,\|\sqrt{t\rho}\dot{u}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}

and

\displaystyle|R_{6}|\leq\|\sqrt{\mu(\rho)}\|_{L^{\infty}}\,\|\partial_{x}u\|_{L^{\infty}}\,\|\sqrt{t}\partial_{x}u\|_{L^{2}}\|\sqrt{t\,\mu(\rho)}\,\partial_{x}\dot{u}\|_{L^{2}}.

To handle
R_{1},~R_{4},~R_{5}
, we take use of equation (
1.1a
) frequently and write that

\displaystyle|R_{1}|=

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\left|\partial_{x}\left(\sqrt{t}\dot{u}\right)\,\sqrt{t}\left(p^{\prime}(\rho)\,\rho\,\partial_{x}u\right)\right|\,dx

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\|p^{\prime}(\rho)\,\rho\|_{L^{\infty}}\,\|\sqrt{t}\,\partial_{x}\dot{u}\|_{L^{2}}\,\|\sqrt{t}\partial_{x}u\|_{L^{2}},

\displaystyle|R_{4}|=\int_{\Omega}\left|\rho\,\partial_{x}u\right|\left(\sqrt{t}\dot{u}\right)^{2}\,dx\leq\|\partial_{x}u\|_{L^{\infty}}\,\|\sqrt{t\,\rho}\dot{u}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}

and

\displaystyle|R_{5}|=

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\left|\partial_{x}\left(\sqrt{t}\dot{u}\right)\left(\sqrt{t}\partial_{x}u\right)(\mu^{\prime}(\rho)\,\rho\,\partial_{x}u)\right|\,dx

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\|\mu^{\prime}(\rho)\,\rho\|_{L^{\infty}}\,\|\partial_{x}u\|_{L^{\infty}}\,\|\sqrt{t}\partial_{x}\dot{u}\|_{L^{2}}\,\|\sqrt{t}\partial_{x}u\|_{L^{2}}.

Taking into account of (
2.28
) and above estimates of
R_{1},\cdots,R_{6},
it follows that

(2.29)

\frac{d}{dt}\|\sqrt{\rho\,t}\dot{u}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+\|\sqrt{t\,\mu(\rho)}\partial_{x}\dot{u}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\\
\leq\|\sqrt{\rho}\dot{u}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+\|\partial_{x}u\|_{L^{\infty}}\,\|\sqrt{t\rho}\dot{u}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+C(\|\rho\|_{L^{\infty}},\mu_{*})\left(\|\partial_{x}u\|_{L^{\infty}}^{2}+1\right)\|\sqrt{t}\partial_{x}u\|_{L^{2}}^{2},

where we used frequently the Young inequality and conditions (
1.2
), (
1.3
).

Finally, we apply Lemma
2.2
-
2.4
to (
2.29
) and use Grönwall’s inequality to conclude (
2.26
). The proof of this lemma is completed.
\Box

3.
Proof of existence part

This section is devoted to the proof of existence parts in Theorem
1.1
. In a first step, we need to construct a sequence of approximate solutions by using some ideas developed by Jiang-Xin-Zhang in
[
24
]

for one-dimensional compressible Navier-Stokes equations with free boundary. Note that the boundary condition in
[
24
]
is of the same type as (
1.1d
). Then, we will focus on establishing the compactness property for the global approximate solutions, in particular the one for the density that stated in Proposition
3.2
.
To begin with, we have to modify the initial data to fit the requirement on the regularity and make the density strictly positive.

Step 1. Modified initial data

We denote by
j_{\epsilon}(x)
the Friedrichs mollifier. Let
\psi(x)\in\mathcal{C}^{\infty}_{0}(\mathbb{R})
satisfy
\psi(x)\equiv 1
when
|x|\leq\frac{1}{2}
and
\psi(x)\equiv 0
when
|x|\geq 1,
and define
\psi_{\epsilon}(x):=\psi(\frac{x}{\epsilon}).
For simplicity we still denote by
(\rho_{0},u_{0})
the extension of
(\rho_{0},u_{0})
in
\mathbb{R}
, i.e.

(3.1)

\rho_{0}(x)=\left\{\begin{aligned} &\rho_{0}(1),\quad&&\ \ x\in(1,\infty),\\
&\rho_{0}(x),\quad&&\ \ x\in[0,1],\\
&\rho_{0}(0),\quad&&\ \ x\in(-\infty,0)\end{aligned}\right.\quad{\rm and}\quad u_{0}(x)=\left\{\begin{aligned} &u_{0}(x),\quad&&\ \ x\in[0,1],\\
&0,\quad&&\ \ {\rm otherwise}.\end{aligned}\right.

We define the approximate initial data to
(\rho_{0},u_{0}):

\displaystyle\rho_{0}^{\epsilon}(x):=

\displaystyle(\rho_{0}\star j_{\epsilon})(x)+\epsilon,

\displaystyle u_{0}^{\epsilon}(x):=

\displaystyle(u_{0}\star j_{\epsilon})(x)[1-\psi_{\epsilon}(x)-\psi_{\epsilon}(1-x)]+(u_{0}\star j_{\epsilon})(0)\,\psi_{\epsilon}(x)+(u_{0}\star j_{\epsilon})(1)\,\psi_{\epsilon}(1-x)

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{p(\rho_{0}^{\epsilon}(x))}{\mu(\rho_{0}^{\epsilon}(x))}\int_{0}^{x}\psi_{\epsilon}(y)\,dy+\frac{p(\rho_{0}^{\epsilon}(x))}{\mu(\rho_{0}^{\epsilon}(x))}\int_{x}^{1}\psi_{\epsilon}(1-y)\,dy.

Then
\rho_{0}^{\epsilon}\in\mathcal{C}^{1+s}([0,1]),

u_{0}^{\epsilon}\in\mathcal{C}^{2+s}([0,1])
for any
s\in(0,1),
and
\rho^{\epsilon}_{0}
and
u_{0}^{\epsilon}
are compatible with the boundary conditions (
1.1d
), such that as
\epsilon\to 0
we have

(3.2)

\rho_{0}^{\epsilon}\to\rho_{0}\quad{\rm in}~L^{2}(\Omega)\quad{\rm and}\quad u^{\epsilon}_{0}\to u_{0}\quad{\rm in}~L^{2}(\Omega).

Step 2. Global existence of approximate smooth solutions

We are going to solve (
1.1
) with data
(\rho_{0}^{\epsilon},u_{0}^{\epsilon}).
Since the viscosity
\mu(s)\geq\mu_{*}>0
for every
s\geq 0,
the following global in time existence result is in the same way as that of
[
24
, Page 246]
.

Proposition 3.1
.

Consider the
IBVP
(
1.1
) with initial data
(\rho_{0},u_{0})
replaced by
(\rho_{0}^{\epsilon},u_{0}^{\epsilon}).

There exists a unique global in time regular solution
(\rho^{\epsilon},u^{\epsilon})
on
[0,T]
for any
T>0,
such that
\rho^{\epsilon}(t,x)>0
and

(3.3)

\displaystyle u^{\epsilon}\in L^{\infty}(0,T;H^{1}(\Omega))\cap L^{2}(0,T;H^{2}(\Omega));\ \ \rho^{\epsilon}\in L^{\infty}(0,T;H^{1}(\Omega)).

Since
(\rho^{\epsilon},u^{\epsilon})
is smooth enough and away from vacuum, it satisfies all the estimates given in Lemmas
2.1
–
2.5
with all the same controls independent of
\epsilon.
In particular,

(3.4)

\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\|(\sqrt{\rho}u,\sqrt{t}\partial_{x}u^{\epsilon},)(t,\cdot)\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\|\rho(t,\cdot)\|_{L^{\infty}}+\int_{0}^{T}\|\partial_{x}u^{\epsilon}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\,dt\\
\leq C(E_{0},\rho^{*},\mu_{*},\mu^{*},T,)

and for any
t_{0}>0

(3.5)

\sup_{t\in[t_{0},T]}\|(\partial_{x}u^{\epsilon},\sqrt{\rho^{\epsilon}}\dot{u}^{\epsilon})(t,\cdot)\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+\int_{t_{0}}^{T}\left(\|\partial_{x}u^{\epsilon}\|_{L^{\infty}}^{2}+\|(\partial_{x}G^{\epsilon},\partial_{x}\dot{u}^{\epsilon})(t,\cdot)\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\right)\,dt\\
\leq C(E_{0},\rho^{*},\mu_{*},\mu^{*},T,t_{0}),

where
G^{\epsilon}=\mu(\rho^{\epsilon})\partial_{x}u^{\epsilon}-p(\rho^{\epsilon}).

Step 3. Strong convergence

At this stage, we are ready to establish robust compactness property for the sequence of approximate solutions. We first address on the velocity component.

Owing to the time-weighted estimates in(
3.5
) and
u|_{\partial\Omega}=\dot{u}^{\epsilon}|_{\partial\Omega}=0
, it follows that for any
t_{0},\,T>0,

\dot{u}^{\epsilon}\in L^{2}(t_{0},T;H^{1}(\Omega))\quad{\rm and}\quad u^{\epsilon}\in L^{\infty}(t_{0},T;H^{1}(\Omega)).

Then, with the aid of (
3.5
) again, we have

\partial_{t}u^{\epsilon}\in L^{2}(t_{0},T;L^{2}(\Omega)),

which together with the Aubin-Lions lemma gives that, up to a subsequence

(3.6)

\left\{\begin{aligned} &u^{\epsilon}\rightarrow u\ \ {\rm weakly~in}\ \ L^{2}(0,T;\Omega),\\
&u^{\epsilon}\rightarrow u\ \ {\rm strongly~in}\ \ L^{2}(t_{0},T;\Omega),\end{aligned}\right.

for some
u
satisfies (
3.4
) and (
3.5
).

We are now in a good position to prove the strong convergence of the density. We deduce from (
3.4
) that, up to a subsequence,

\rho^{\epsilon}\to\rho\quad{\rm weakly^{*}~in}\ \ L^{\infty}(0,T;\Omega).

But this is not enough to pass to the limit in the pressure term of the momentum equation. To achieve that purpose, one can prove some strong convergence property of the effective viscous flux
G^{\epsilon}:=\mu(\rho^{\epsilon})\partial_{x}u^{\epsilon}-p^{\epsilon}.
That is,

(3.7)

\displaystyle G^{\epsilon}\rightarrow G\ \ {\rm strongly~in}\ \ L^{2}(t_{0},T;\Omega).

Indeed, from (
3.4
) and (
3.5
), one has

(3.8)

\displaystyle G^{\epsilon}\in L^{\infty}(t_{0},T;L^{2}(\Omega))\cap L^{2}(t_{0},T;H^{1}(\Omega)).

Thanks to the indentity

\displaystyle G^{\epsilon}_{t}=

\displaystyle-\mu^{\prime}(\rho^{\epsilon})(\partial_{x}(\rho^{\epsilon}u^{\epsilon}))\partial_{x}u^{\epsilon}+\mu(\rho^{\epsilon})(\partial_{x}\dot{u}^{\epsilon}-\partial_{x}(u^{\epsilon}\partial_{x}u^{\epsilon}))-p^{\epsilon}_{t}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(-\partial_{x}(\mu(\rho^{\epsilon})u^{\epsilon})+(\mu(\rho^{\epsilon})-\mu^{\prime}(\rho^{\epsilon})\rho^{\epsilon}))\partial_{x}u^{\epsilon}

(3.9)

\displaystyle+\mu(\rho^{\epsilon})(\partial_{x}\dot{u}^{\epsilon}-\partial_{x}(u^{\epsilon}\partial_{x}u^{\epsilon}))+\partial_{x}(p(\rho^{\epsilon})u^{\epsilon})-(p(\rho^{\epsilon})-p^{\prime}(\rho^{\epsilon})\rho^{\epsilon})\partial_{x}u^{\epsilon},

one can further deduce that

(3.10)

\displaystyle G^{\epsilon}_{t}\in L^{2}(t_{0},T;H^{-1}(\Omega)),

which together with (
3.8
) and the Aubin-Lions lemma gives (
3.7
).
Now, we have

Proposition 3.2
.

Up to a subsequence,
\rho^{\epsilon}\to\rho
strongly in
L^{1}(t_{0},T;L^{1}(\Omega))
.

Proof.

The previous consideration of the approximate solution sequence ensures that there exists a subsequence
(\rho^{\epsilon_{n}},u^{\epsilon_{n}})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
of
(\rho^{\epsilon},u^{\epsilon})
such that

\displaystyle\rho^{\epsilon_{n}}\rightharpoonup^{*}\rho\ \ {\rm in}\ \ L^{\infty}(0,T;\Omega)\ \ {\rm and}\ \ u^{\epsilon_{n}}\rightarrow u\ \ {\rm in}\ \ L^{2}(t_{0},T;\Omega).

Of course, for all
n\in\mathbb{N},
it satisfies

(3.11)

\displaystyle\rho_{t}^{\epsilon_{n}}+\partial_{x}(\rho^{\epsilon_{n}}u^{\epsilon_{n}})=0,

and the limit
(\rho,u)

4.
Proof of uniqueness statement

In this section, we focus on the uniqueness of of solutions to problem (
1.1
) given the initial data

\partial_{x}u_{0}\in L^{2}(\Omega)

the stability analysis, it is convenient to perform a Lagrangian change of coordinates for the system (

). First of all, we introduce the Lagrangian flow map
\xi(y,t)
as follows:

\begin{cases}&\partial_{t}\xi(y,t)=u(\xi(y,t),t),\\
&\xi(y,0)=y.\end{cases}

Then we can define the following terms

\displaystyle\tilde{\rho}(y,t):=\rho(\xi(y,t),t),\quad\tilde{u}(y,t):=u(\xi(y,t),t),

\displaystyle\tilde{p}(y,t):=p(\xi(y,t),t),\quad\tilde{\mu}(y,t):=\mu(\xi(y,t),t).

Furthermore, we denote a function

as

\displaystyle\eta(y,t)=\xi_{y}(y,t)=1+\int_{0}^{t}\partial_{y}\tilde{u}(y,\tau)d\tau,

and rewrite the system (

) in the Lagrangian coordinates as follows

(4.1)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{rl}\partial_{t}\tilde{\rho}+\frac{\partial_{y}\tilde{u}}{\eta}\tilde{\rho}&=0,\\[1.42262pt]
\partial_{t}\tilde{\rho}\tilde{u}+\frac{\partial_{y}\tilde{p}}{\eta}&=\frac{1}{\eta}\partial_{y}\left(\frac{\tilde{\mu}\partial_{y}\tilde{u}}{\eta}\right).\end{array}\right.

Since
\partial_{t}\eta=\partial_{y}\tilde{u}
, it holds from
\eqref{refomulate-0}_{1}
that

\partial_{t}(\eta\tilde{\rho})=\partial_{y}\tilde{u}\tilde{\rho}-\eta\frac{\partial_{y}\tilde{u}}{\eta}\tilde{\rho}=0.

Setting

and combining with the fact that

, we obtain

\eta\tilde{\rho}=\rho_{0}.

Next, the equations (
4.1
) can be expressed in terms of
(\eta,\tilde{u})
(denote still by
(\eta,u)
without confusing) in variables
(y,t)
:

(4.2)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{rl}\partial_{t}\eta&=\partial_{y}u,\\[1.42262pt]
\rho_{0}\partial_{t}u+\partial_{y}p\left(\frac{\rho_{0}}{\eta}\right)&=\partial_{y}\left(\mu\left(\frac{\rho_{0}}{\eta}\right)\frac{\partial_{y}u}{\eta}\right).\end{array}\right.

with initial boundary condition

(4.3)

\displaystyle(\eta,u)(x,0)=(1,u_{0})(x),

\displaystyle\ \ {\rm in}\ \ \Omega,

(4.4)

\displaystyle u=0,\ \ \ p\left(\frac{\rho_{0}}{\eta}\right)-\mu\left(\frac{\rho_{0}}{\eta}\right)\frac{\partial_{y}u}{\eta}=0,

\displaystyle\ \ {\rm on}\ \ \partial\Omega.

We point out that the system (
4.2
) is equivalent to (
1.1
) whenever, say,

(4.5)

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}\|\partial_{y}\tilde{u}\|_{L^{\infty}}dt<k_{0}<1

for sufficiently small

. Actually, we have

which yields

(4.6)

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}\|\partial_{y}\tilde{u}\|_{L^{\infty}}dt\leq\frac{\int_{0}^{T}\|\partial_{x}u\|_{L^{\infty}}dt}{1-\int_{0}^{T}\|\partial_{x}u\|_{L^{\infty}}dt}<k_{0}<1

for sufficiently small

by using Lemma

.

With the above preparation, now we tackle the proof of uniqueness. Let
(\eta_{1},u_{1})
and
(\eta_{2},u_{2})
be two solutions of system (

), subject to (

)-(
4.4
), satisfying the regularities stated in

, on

, with the same initial data. Let

and
\delta\eta=\eta_{1}-\eta_{2}
. According to (

),
\delta u
satisfies the
following equation:

(4.7)

\displaystyle\rho_{0}\partial_{t}\delta u+\partial_{y}\left(p_{1}-p_{2}\right)=\partial_{y}\left(\mu_{1}\frac{\partial_{y}u_{1}}{\eta_{1}}-\mu_{2}\frac{\partial_{y}u_{2}}{\eta_{2}}\right),

where

and
\mu_{i}=\mu\big(\frac{\rho_{0}}{\eta_{i}}\big)
for

.
Multiplying (

\delta u

\Omega
, and integrating

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\frac{d}{dt}\int_{\Omega}\rho_{0}(\delta u)^{2}dx+\int_{\Omega}\mu_{2}(\partial_{y}\delta u)^{2}dx+\int_{\Omega}\mu_{2}\frac{(\partial_{y}\delta u)^{2}(1-\eta_{1})}{\eta_{1}}dx

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\int_{\Omega}(\mu_{1}-\mu_{2})\frac{\partial_{y}u_{1}\partial_{y}\delta u}{\eta_{1}}dx-\int_{\Omega}\mu_{2}\frac{\eta_{2}-\eta_{1}}{\eta_{1}\eta_{2}}\partial_{y}u_{2}\partial_{y}\delta udx+\int_{\Omega}(p_{1}-p_{2})\partial_{y}\delta udx.

Notice that

\displaystyle\|\eta_{2}-\eta_{1}\|_{L^{2}}\leq\int_{0}^{t}||\partial_{y}\delta u\|_{L^{2}}d\tau,\quad\forall t\in[0,T_{0}],

which yields

(4.8)

\displaystyle\|\mu_{1}-\mu_{2}\|_{L^{2}}\leq C\|\rho_{0}\|_{L^{\infty}}||\eta_{2}-\eta_{1}\|_{L^{2}}\leq C\int_{0}^{t}||\partial_{y}\delta u\|_{L^{2}}d\tau,\quad\forall t\in[0,T_{0}],

(4.9)

\displaystyle\|p_{1}-p_{2}\|_{L^{2}}\leq C\|\rho_{0}\|_{L^{\infty}}||\eta_{2}-\eta_{1}\|_{L^{2}}\leq C\int_{0}^{t}||\partial_{y}\delta u\|_{L^{2}}d\tau,\quad\forall t\in[0,T_{0}].

It follows from the Young and Sobolev embedding inequalities, (

)-(

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\int_{\Omega}\rho_{0}(\delta u)^{2}dx+\int_{\Omega}(\partial_{y}\delta u)^{2}dx

\displaystyle Ct\int_{0}^{t}\|\partial_{y}\delta u\|_{L^{2}}^{2}d\tau\left(1+\|\partial_{y}u_{1}\|_{L^{\infty}}^{2}+\|\partial_{y}u_{2}\|_{L^{\infty}}^{2}\right),

5

and Hölder’s inequality, we write that

\displaystyle\left|a(t)-M_{L}\right|

\displaystyle=\left|a(t)-\int_{0}^{a(t)}\rho(t,x)dx\right|

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\Omega}|1-\rho(t,x)|dx\leq\|\rho-1\|_{L^{2}}\leq Ce^{-\alpha t}.

As for
b(t)
, notice that
\|\rho(t,\cdot)\|_{L^{1}(\Omega)}=1
and thus

\left|M_{L}-b(t)\right|=\left|(1-M_{L})-(1-b(t))\right|=\left|\int_{b(t)}^{1(t)}(\rho(t,x)-1)dx\right|\leq Ce^{-\alpha t}.

Hence, we have

|a(t)-M_{L}|+|b(t)-M_{L}|\leq Ce^{-\alpha t}.

∎
