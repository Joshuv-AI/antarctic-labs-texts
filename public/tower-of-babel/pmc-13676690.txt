Timing of Insulin Glargine Administration During Intermittent Ramadan Fasting in patients With Type1 Diabetes: A Randomized Controlled Trial (GLARIF)
Authors: Reem Alamoudi, Ali Alqarni, Maram Alsubaiee, Ghofran Khogeer, Yousef Saleh, Abdulrhman Aljawharji, Hawazen Zarif, Salwa Alaidarous, Seham Khashwayn, Mohamed Hassanein
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjective:The effect of basal insulin timing on hypoglycemia during Ramadan fasting remains uncertain despite recommendations to administer basal insulin earlier in the evening. We evaluated whether early evening compared with bedtime administration of insulin glargine reduces hypoglycemia and improves glycemic outcomes in individuals with type1 diabetes (T1D) fasting during Ramadan.Methods:This multicenter open-label randomized controlled trial was conducted across 4 centers in Saudi Arabia. Participants with T1D intending to fast Ramadan were randomized to early evening or bedtime glargine administration. Glucose data were collected using self-monitoring of blood glucose (SMBG), intermittently scanned continuous glucose monitoring (FreeStyle Libre), and blinded continuous glucose monitoring (CGM; Medtronic iPro2).Results:A total of 185 participants were analyzed (91 early evening, 94 bedtime). Baseline characteristics were similar between groups. Fasting interruption occurred in 75.9% versus 76.3% of participants (P = .94), with hypoglycemia accounting for >90% of interruptions in both groups. Hypoglycemia occurred predominantly during the morning period regardless of basal insulin timing. Severe hypoglycemia was reported in 2 versus 5 participants, and 1 diabetic ketoacidosis admission occurred in each group. Continuous glucose monitoring–derived glycemic metrics were similar between groups. Time in range (70-180 mg/dL) was 44.6% versus 46.1% (P = .70), time below range (<70 mg/dL) was 7.9% versus 6.4% (P = .35), and time above range (>180 mg/dL) was 47.5% in both groups (P = .99). Self-monitoring of blood glucose-detected hypoglycemic episodes and CGM-detected low glucose excursions were also comparable. Area under the curve below range was higher in the early evening group (P = .04).Conclusion:Changing insulin glargine administration from bedtime to earlier evening hours during Ramadan fasting did not significantly reduce hypoglycemia or improve overall glucose profiles in individuals with T1D. These findings suggest that routine universal shifting of basal insulin timing during Ramadan may not be necessary and support an individualized approach based on patient preference and glucose patterns.ClinicalTrials.gov identifier: NCT04383990Keywords: type1 diabetes, insulin glargine, Ramadan fasting, basal insulin, hypoglycemia, CGM

Introduction

Managing glycemia during Ramadan intermittent fasting in individuals with type 1 diabetes mellitus (T1D) is challenging,1-6 with competing risks of hypoglycemia during fasting and hyperglycemia during the nocturnal period. Basal-bolus insulin regimens using long-acting insulin analogues are generally recommended and have been associated with improved outcomes compared with conventional regimens. 7 However, guidance on how best to adjust insulin during Ramadan intermittent fasting in people with T1D remains limited, and most available data are observational.

In clinical practice, basal insulin is often shifted to earlier in the evening, typically at Iftar, based on the assumption that this reduces insulin exposure during the late fasting hours and therefore lowers the risk of hypoglycemia. This approach is widely supported1-3 but is largely based on physiological reasoning rather than randomized evidence.8-14 Importantly, continuous glucose monitoring (CGM) studies suggest that the timing of hypoglycemia during Ramadan is not uniform and may not be confined to the late fasting period.15-18

Given this, it remains unclear whether changing the timing of basal insulin administration has a meaningful impact on glycemic outcomes during Ramadan intermittent fasting. In this multicenter randomized controlled trial, we compared early evening versus bedtime administration of insulin glargine in individuals with T1D who chose to fast during Ramadan, with a focus on hypoglycemia and CGM-derived glycemic metrics.

Methods

This was a multicenter, open-label, randomized controlled trial conducted in the diabetes and endocrine clinics of National Guard hospitals across 4 cities in Saudi Arabia (Jeddah, Riyadh, Al-Hasa, and Dammam). The study protocol was approved by the King Abdullah International Medical Research Center Research Office (IRBC/0302/20).

Individuals with T1D for more than 6 months who intended to fast during Ramadan were screened for eligibility. Inclusion criteria were age >14 years, treatment with a basal-bolus insulin regimen, and willingness to perform structured self-monitoring of blood glucose (SMBG). Exclusion criteria included renal or hepatic impairment, adrenal insufficiency, pregnancy, alcohol consumption, and diagnosed psychiatric illness.

Participants were randomized in a 1:1 ratio to either early evening glargine administration (18:00-19:00) or bedtime glargine administration (22:00-24:00). Randomization was computer-generated and stratified by study center. Due to the nature of the intervention, treatment allocation could not be blinded. However, outcome analysis was performed in a blinded manner. Non-identifiable study codes were used on all data collection forms, and the statistician performing the analysis remained unaware of treatment allocation throughout the study.

Study Procedures

Both study groups received standard diabetes care throughout the study period. Initial recruitment visits were conducted within 2 months before Ramadan. During this visit, eligibility was confirmed, written informed consent was obtained, and baseline demographic, clinical, and laboratory data were collected. Glycemic control was assessed as part of routine clinical care, and insulin adjustments were made when clinically indicated.

All participants received standardized pre-Ramadan education from a certified diabetes educator, including counseling regarding fasting safety, hypoglycemia recognition, indications for breaking the fast, and SMBG. Participants were instructed to perform SMBG 6 to 7 times daily during Ramadan, including before and 2 hours after Iftar (breakfast at sunset) and Suhoor (pre-dawn meal), midway between meals, and once or twice during fasting hours.

All participants were provided with an Accu-Chek Performa glucometer. Participants already using CGM (FreeStyle Libre) were allowed to continue its use during Ramadan. Patients who routinely adjusted insulin doses based on home glucose monitoring were permitted to continue self-adjustment according to their usual practice.

Outside of standard Ramadan education and study-related timing instructions, no additional protocol-driven changes to insulin regimen or dietary intake were implemented. Participants were instructed to break their fast if blood glucose reached ≤70 mg/dL (3.9 mmol/L) or if significant hyperglycemia ≥300 mg/dL (16.7 mmol/L).

Within 2 weeks before Ramadan, participants were informed of treatment allocation and received study-specific instructions accordingly. A pre-Ramadan questionnaire was completed, and serum fructosamine levels were obtained.

During Ramadan, participants using SMBG alone underwent blinded diagnostic CGM insertion using the Medtronic iPro2 system. Participants using FreeStyle Libre continued real-time glucose monitoring throughout Ramadan.

The final study visit occurred 2 to 4 weeks after Ramadan and included review of SMBG and CGM records, download of home glucose monitoring data, repeat glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c) and fructosamine measurements, and completion of a post-Ramadan questionnaire.

The study was funded through a grant from King Abdullah International Medical Research Center (KAIMRC). Medtronic provided the iPro2 CGM devices used in the study.

Outcomes

The primary objective of the study was to determine whether administration of basal insulin glargine at (18:00-19:00) was associated with lower rates of hypoglycemia during Ramadan fasting compared with bedtime administration (22:00-24:00) in individuals with T1D. Hypoglycemia was defined as a glucose level ≤70 mg/dL (3.9 mmol/L).

Secondary outcomes included number of days in which participants were needed to break their fast, glycemic control, daytime and nocturnal hyperglycemia, severe hyperglycemia and diabetes-related complications, CGM-derived ambulatory glucose profile (AGP) metrics, and patient treatment preference. Ambulatory glucose profile parameters included mean glucose, fasting glucose, 2-hour postprandial glucose, glucose management indicator (GMI), glucose variability, time in range (TIR; 70-180 mg/dL), time above range (TAR), and time below range (TBR).

Statistical Analysis

Sample size calculation estimated that 116 participants per arm would provide adequate precision to estimate the difference in hypoglycemia incidence between treatment groups, assuming an incidence of 10% in the early glargine group and 15% in the bedtime glargine group. This corresponded to a 2-sided 95% confidence interval width of 0.17. Statistical analyses were performed using SAS version 9.4. Continuous variables were summarized as mean ± standard deviation (SD) or median with interquartile range (IQR), as appropriate after assessment of normality assumptions. Categorical variables were expressed as frequency and percentage. Comparisons between treatment groups were performed using independent-samples t-test for normally distributed continuous variables, Mann-Whitney U test for non-normally distributed variables, and chi-square test for categorical variables. Within-group pre- and post-Ramadan comparisons were assessed using paired t tests for continuous variables and McNemar tests for categorical variables. A 2-sided P value <.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

A total of 206 participants were randomized: 105 to the early evening glargine group and 101 to the bedtime glargine group. Twenty-one participants subsequently withdrew consent, leaving 185 participants for the final analysis: 91 in the early glargine group (18:00-19:00) and 94 in the bedtime glargine group (22:00-24:00) (Figure 1).

During Ramadan, most participants in both groups reported sleeping predominantly during daytime hours (dawn to noon) for approximately 8 to 9 hours and being most active between 21:00 and dawn (04:00).

Fasting Outcomes and Acute Complications

Breaking the fast was common in both groups and did not differ significantly between treatment arms. Fasting was interrupted by 66/87 (75.9%) participants in the early glargine group and 71/93 (76.3%) in the bedtime glargine group (P = .94); responses were unavailable for five participants. The mean number of interrupted fasting days was similar between groups (4 ± 6 vs 4 ± 4 days, P = .50). Hypoglycemia was the most frequently reported reason for breaking the fast (90.6% vs 95.7%, P = .31), followed by hyperglycemia (29.3% vs 14.3%, P = .05).

Self-reported hypoglycemia requiring interruption of fasting occurred predominantly during the morning and early afternoon periods in both groups (Figure 2). Morning hypoglycemia was reported by 32 participants (59.3%) in the early glargine group, and 38 participants (60.3%) in the bedtime glargine group, P = .85. This was significantly more frequent than hypoglycemia occurring later in the afternoon or immediately before Iftar (P < .0001 for time-of-day distribution).

SMBG and CGM Outcomes

Self-monitoring of blood glucose data were extracted from Accu-Chek reports, while CGM data were obtained from blinded CGM (Medtronic iPro2) and intermittently scanned CGM (FreeStyle Libre) reports in the respective participant subsets. The SMBG analysis did not demonstrate significant differences between groups across the measured glycemic parameters. Mean glucose was 184 ± 49 versus 179 ± 49.5 mg/dL (P = .46), with no significant differences in glycemic variability (SD: 68.9 ± 21.6 vs 67.7 ± 18.0 mg/dL, P = .80; coefficient of variation [CV]: 37.4% vs 37.8%, P = .91) or the number of hypoglycemic events (4 ± 6 vs 3 ± 8, P = .83). Similarly, the lowest and highest recorded glucose values and the high and low blood glucose indices did not differ significantly between groups.

Blinded CGM Analysis

Blinded CGM data were available for 37 participants in the early group and 32 participants in the bedtime group. No significant differences were observed between groups in overall CGM-derived glycemic metrics (Figure 3). However, low glucose excursions detected by blinded CGM were numerically higher in the early glargine group (5 ± 6 vs 4 ± 5.5, P = .22). In addition, area under the curve below range was significantly higher in the early group (0.5 ± 1 vs 0.15 ± 0.85, P = .04) (Table 2).

Glycemic and Weight Changes After Ramadan

Glycemic parameters and weight were generally numerically lower after Ramadan; however, these differences were not statistically significant. In the early versus bedtime glargine groups, respectively: pre-Ramadan HbA1c: 9.0 ± 2.7% versus 8.75 ± 2.1%, P = .35. Post-Ramadan HbA1c:8.4 ± 2.9% versus 8.7 ± 2.7%, P = .50. Pre-Ramadan fructosamine levels were 407 ± 126.5 versus 409 ± 128 µmol/L, P = .79. Post-Ramadan fructosamine levels were: 383 ± 74 versus 411 ± 82 µmol/L, P = .10. Mean body weight before Ramadan was 63.15 ± 22.1 versus 62 ± 23 kg, P = .77. Post-Ramadan body weight was 61 ± 15.6 versus 58 ± 11 kg, P = .70.

Discussion

In this multicenter randomized controlled trial, we evaluated whether changing the timing of basal insulin glargine administration from bedtime to early evening as recommended by some guidelines1-3 reduces hypoglycemia during Ramadan fasting in individuals with T1D. To our knowledge, this is the first randomized controlled trial specifically designed to evaluate the effect of basal insulin timing on hypoglycemia risk during Ramadan intermittent fasting in T1D.

The available literature evaluating insulin strategies during Ramadan fasting in T1D remains limited and consists predominantly of small observational studies with heterogeneous designs and treatment approaches. Earlier studies7-14 mainly compared conventional insulin regimens with basal-bolus therapy or rapid-acting insulin analogues. Kadiri et al 9 demonstrated lower hypoglycemia rates with rapid-acting insulin analogues compared with regular insulin in a randomized crossover study, whereas Al-Khawari et al 7 and Kassem et al 10 suggested that basal-bolus regimens using long-acting insulin analogues could facilitate safer fasting when accompanied by insulin dose reduction and frequent glucose monitoring. The literature specifically evaluating basal insulin timing and dose adjustment during Ramadan fasting in T1D is even more limited. Earlier studies commonly recommended reducing basal insulin doses by approximately 10% to 30% during Ramadan and administering basal insulin in the evening to reduce fasting hypoglycemia risk.7,11,19 Subsequent CGM- and flash glucose monitoring-based studies continued to demonstrate persistent hypoglycemia despite these approaches. Afandi et al 20 reported that hypoglycemia remained common before Iftar despite reducing glargine doses by 25% to 30%, while Deeb et al 21 found no significant reduction in hypoglycemia risk with basal insulin dose reduction compared with no dose adjustment. Al-Ozairi et al 22 later demonstrated lower hypoglycemia rates during Ramadan with structured education and CGM use, although this was accompanied by higher TAR and persistent hyperglycemia. However, despite increasing emphasis in Ramadan guidelines1-3 on adjusting basal insulin dosing and timing, no previous study had directly evaluated in a randomized controlled fashion whether changing the timing of basal insulin administration itself reduces hypoglycemia during fasting.

The main finding of this study was that early evening administration of glargine did not significantly reduce hypoglycemia compared with bedtime administration. Rates of fasting interruption, severe hypoglycemia, and overall CGM-derived glycemic metrics were also comparable between groups. Although some CGM parameters numerically favored bedtime glargine, including lower TBR and lower hypoglycemia exposure during later fasting hours, the overall differences between groups were modest. Together, these findings suggest that altering basal insulin timing alone is unlikely to substantially change overall hypoglycemia risk during Ramadan fasting.

A key observation in this study was the timing of hypoglycemia. Self-reported hypoglycemia requiring interruption of fasting occurred predominantly during the morning and early afternoon periods rather than immediately before Iftar. Similarly, CGM profiles demonstrated that glucose levels were lowest around mid-day in both groups regardless of basal insulin timing. These findings suggest that hypoglycemia during Ramadan may be influenced more strongly by factors related to the Suhoor period, particularly prandial insulin dosing and meal composition, than by basal insulin timing itself. The carried over effect of rapid-acting insulin administered with Suhoor, combined with prolonged fasting and altered sleep–wake patterns, may therefore contribute more substantially to fasting-related hypoglycemia than the timing of basal glargine administration in our population. However, Suhoor timing, meal composition, and meal-specific prandial insulin adjustments were not standardized as part of the study protocol or evaluated as prespecified study variables; therefore, their individual contributions to the observed pattern of hypoglycemia cannot be determined from this study.

Importantly, this study was conducted in Saudi Arabia, where Ramadan is commonly associated with reduced daytime working hours, daytime sleeping, and increased nocturnal activity. Most participants in our cohort reported sleeping during daytime hours and being more active overnight. These behavioral changes likely influence insulin sensitivity, and glucose dynamics during fasting and may partly explain the predominance of early fasting hypoglycemia observed in this and other Saudi Ramadan studies.6,18,23

Another important finding was the persistent burden of hyperglycemia. Although hypoglycemia was the primary reason for breaking the fast, CGM data demonstrated that participants spent a substantial proportion of TAR, particularly during nocturnal hours. This likely reflects the difficulty of balancing hypoglycemia prevention with adequate insulin coverage during Ramadan intermittent fasting. Excessive insulin dose reduction out of concern for hypoglycemia, delayed nocturnal eating patterns, and large Iftar meals may all contribute to persistent overnight hyperglycemia.

Taken together, these findings suggest that focusing solely on insulin dosing or timing does not cover all related metabolic challenges associated with Ramadan intermittent fasting in T1D, and more emphasis and research is needed on other aspects of care related to meal and activity patterns.

While basal insulin dose adjustment remains important, our results do not support the routine universal recommendation1-3 to shift basal insulin glargine administration from bedtime to earlier evening hours solely to reduce hypoglycemia risk. Instead, basal insulin timing may be individualized according to patient preference, prior glucose patterns, and lifestyle, while greater emphasis should be directed toward optimization of Suhoor-related insulin dosing, meal composition, structured glucose monitoring, and patient education.

This study has several strengths, including its multicenter randomized design, relatively large sample size compared with prior Ramadan studies in T1D, and integration of SMBG with both blinded and real-time CGM data. However, several limitations should be acknowledged. The study did not achieve the planned sample size, which may have limited statistical power to detect modest differences between groups. The open-label design may also have influenced patient behavior and insulin adjustment practices. In addition, glucose-monitoring modalities differed among participants, with some using real-time FreeStyle Libre and others undergoing blinded iPro2 monitoring. Although CGM analyses were separated by modality where appropriate, access to real-time glucose information may have influenced participant behavior and insulin adjustments. Ramadan-related lifestyle patterns in Saudi Arabia may also differ from those in other populations, potentially limiting the generalizability of our findings.

In conclusion, changing basal insulin glargine administration from bedtime to early evening did not significantly reduce hypoglycemia during Ramadan fasting in individuals with T1D. The predominance of hypoglycemia during the early fasting period suggests that Suhoor-related prandial insulin exposure and behavioral factors may play a larger role in fasting-related hypoglycemia than basal insulin timing itself. These findings support a more flexible and individualized approach to basal insulin timing during Ramadan rather than routine universal timing modification for all patients.
