Metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease and colorectal neoplasms risk: a global propensity score-matched retrospective cohort study
Authors: Mohammad Aldiabat, Ali Osman, Malek Ayoub, Mahmoud Y Madi, Kamran Qureshi, Wing-Kin Syn
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesTo evaluate the association between metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD), metabolic-associated steatohepatitis (MASH), and the risk of colorectal cancer (CRC) and benign colorectal neoplasms (BCN), and to explore whether liver fibrosis/cirrhosis modifies these associations.DesignRetrospective cohort study with 1:1 propensity score matching.SettingGlobal, multicentre real-world analysis using deidentified electronic health records from over 130 healthcare organisations in the TriNetX Global Collaborative Network.ParticipantsHospitalised adults aged 45–75 years between October 2019 and October 2024. Patients with prior diagnoses of colorectal neoplasia or other chronic liver diseases were excluded. Final matched cohorts included 138 902 MASLD and non-MASLD patients, 3715 MASH and non-MASH patients, and 1312 MASH patients with and without fibrosis.Primary and secondary outcome measuresPrimary outcomes: Incidence of CRC and BCN. Secondary outcome: Combined incidence of CRC and BCN. Outcomes were assessed with and without controlling for metabolic risk factors using Cox proportional hazards models.ResultsMASLD was associated with increased risks of CRC (HR 2.71, 95% CI 2.29 to 3.20) and BCN (HR 2.50, 95% CI 2.38 to 2.63), both p<0.001. MASH patients had a 5-fold higher risk of CRC (HR 5.03, 95% CI 1.43 to 17.72, p=0.005) and nearly 2-fold risk of BCN (HR 1.91, 95% CI 1.38 to 2.67, p<0.001). No significant differences in CRC or BCN risk were observed between MASH patients with versus without fibrosis/cirrhosis.ConclusionsMASLD and MASH are independent risk factors for CRC and BCN, irrespective of metabolic comorbidities. Fibrosis/cirrhosis does not significantly influence CRC risk. These findings support the need to revisit CRC screening guidelines for patients with MASLD/MASH. Further prospective studies are warranted to explore underlying mechanisms and evaluate preventative interventions.

Introduction

Colorectal cancer (CRC) is the third most commonly diagnosed cancer and the second leading cause of cancer-related mortality globally, underscoring the substantial burden it imposes on healthcare systems worldwide.1 Recent epidemiological data suggest that the global incidence of CRC is increasing, driven in part by sedentary lifestyles, ageing populations and escalating rates of metabolic disorders. Among the metabolic conditions is metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD), formerly known as non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD).2 Increasingly, investigators have noted that MASLD extends beyond merely a hepatic condition, potentially contributing to an elevated risk of extrahepatic malignancies, such as CRC.3

Multiple cross-sectional and cohort studies have repeatedly demonstrated a higher prevalence of colorectal adenomas and carcinoma in patients with MASLD compared with individuals without underlying liver disease.46 A meta-analysis by Zeng et al7 synthesised 14 studies and reported that the risk of colorectal neoplasms increases with MASLD severity, particularly for left-sided lesions. Zhao et al8 conducted an updated cohort-only meta-analysis of nearly 10 million participants and found MASLD to be associated with higher risks of incident CRC and colorectal adenoma. Most recently, Celsa et al9 compared MASLD and Metabolic Dysfunction and Alcohol-Associated Steatotic Liver Disease across 24 cohort studies and demonstrated distinct risks for hepatic and extrahepatic cancers.

This association between MASLD and CRC stems from a shared pathophysiological mechanism involving insulin resistance, which promotes a pro-inflammatory state through adipocytokine dysregulation, hepatic lipid accumulation and oxidative stress.10 These processes can accelerate colorectal carcinogenesis by altering cell signalling pathways and fostering the development of adenomatous polyps.4 In addition, intestinal dysbiosis—a disturbance of gut microbiota that often accompanies obesity and metabolic syndrome—may further augment this risk by affecting epithelial integrity, inflammatory responses and local immune surveillance.10

Despite these insights, most available studies have been limited by region-specific populations, heterogeneous definitions of MASLD, MASH and fibrosis, and incomplete adjustment for metabolic comorbidities. Our global study addresses these gaps by (1) separately evaluating MASLD and MASH, (2) examining the influence of liver fibrosis/cirrhosis and (3) isolating the effect of these conditions on colorectal neoplasm risk after controlling for traditional metabolic risk factors using propensity score matching (PSM). This design allows for a more nuanced understanding of how metabolic liver disease contributes to CRC and benign colorectal neoplasm (BCN) risk, providing evidence to inform early detection and prevention strategies.

Methods

Study design and data source

This was a retrospective cohort study using the TriNetX Global Collaborative Network, a federated research platform designed for real-time analysis of deidentified patient data. The platform complies with HIPAA privacy standards and includes records from over 150 million individuals across 134 healthcare organisations (HCOs) worldwide. Participating HCOs span North America, Europe, Asia, the Middle East and other regions, providing a geographically diverse patient population. By integrating data from a diverse range of HCOs, TriNetX enables comprehensive real-world analyses that extend beyond the limitations of single-centre or region-specific studies. To ensure patient privacy, the platform obfuscates any patient counts ≤10, grouping them into a single category. As the database exclusively contains deidentified information, the study did not require informed consent. All clinical data were derived from electronic health records (EHRs) maintained by participating HCOs. This study adhered to the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology guidelines for observational research,11 following established standards for methodological rigour and transparent reporting of findings.

Participants, eligibility criteria and exposure

The study population was identified from the TriNetX Global Collaborative Network by screening hospitalised patients aged 45–75 years over a 5-year period (31 October 2019–31 October 2024). Patients were stratified based on the presence or absence of diagnoses for MASLD (International Classification of Diseases, Tenth Revision (ICD-10): K76.0), MASH (ICD-10: K75.81) and liver fibrosis/cirrhosis (ICD-10: K74) using standardised ICD-10 codes. ICD-10 codes for CRC have demonstrated high validity in administrative datasets; for example, Hirano et al reported a positive predictive value of 86.3% (rising to 94.0% when using site-specific codes) for identifying CRC from hospital claims data.12 The sample age range was chosen to align with the typical window for CRC screening, ensuring relevance to the study’s outcomes. Three primary comparisons were made: (1) MASLD versus non-MASLD controls, (2) MASH versus non-MASH controls and (3) MASH with liver fibrosis/cirrhosis vs MASH without liver fibrosis/cirrhosis. Liver fibrosis/cirrhosis were identified using ICD-10 codes recorded in the EHR; histological or non-invasive imaging confirmation was not uniformly available across TriNetX and, therefore, was not used as part of the case definition. Exclusion criteria were applied to minimise confounding factors. Patients younger than 18 years were excluded, as were those with chronic liver diseases other than MASLD, MASH or liver fibrosis/cirrhosis. Excluded liver conditions included alcoholic and toxic liver diseases, hepatic failure, chronic hepatitis, autoimmune hepatitis and viral hepatitis, as well as other specific liver diseases (ICD-10: K70, K71, K72, K73, K75.0-K75.9, K76.1-K76.9, K77, B15-B19). Furthermore, individuals with any prior diagnosis of BCN (ICD-10: D12.2–D12.8) or malignant colorectal neoplasms (ICD-10: C18–C20) before the diagnosis of MASLD, MASH or liver fibrosis/cirrhosis were excluded. This ensured that differences in outcomes were not influenced by pre-existing colorectal pathology. Variables

Baseline demographic data included age, gender, race (categorised as white, black or African American, Asian, Native Hawaiian or Other Pacific Islander) and ethnicity (Hispanic or Latino, Not Hispanic or Latino). Clinical comorbidities were captured to account for metabolic and cardiovascular risk factors. These included hypertension (ICD-10: I10), diabetes mellitus (ICD-10: E08–E13), dyslipidaemia (ICD-10: E78), tobacco use (ICD-10: Z72.0), chronic ischaemic heart disease (ICD-10: I25), atrial fibrillation and flutter (ICD-10: I48), chronic lower respiratory diseases (ICD-10: J40–J4A), and alcohol-related disorders (ICD-10: F10). Medication usage was assessed for Glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) analogues (ATC code: A10BJ) and aspirin (RxNorm: 1191). Additionally, laboratory measures were extracted to evaluate metabolic and hepatic function, including body mass index (BMI) (TNX Curated: 9083), aspartate aminotransferase (AST) (TNX Curated: 9047), alanine aminotransferase (ALT) (TNX Curated: 9044), low-density lipoprotein (LDL) cholesterol (TNX Curated: 9002), triglycerides (TNX Curated: 9004) and high-density lipoprotein (HDL) cholesterol (TNX Curated: 9001). Comorbid conditions and laboratory values were captured using the most recent codes and results documented at or before the index MASLD/MASH diagnosis date.

Outcomes

The primary outcomes were the risk of CRC and BCN, including adenomas and hyperplastic polyps, in the subgroups of patients with MASLD, MASH and MASH with liver fibrosis/cirrhosis compared with disease-free controls. Additionally, a composite outcome combining CRC and BCN was analysed to provide a broader assessment of colorectal pathology risk. Outcomes were identified using the standardised ICD-10 codes previously outlined. Patients were followed from the date of the index event, defined as the diagnosis of MASLD, MASH or MASH with liver fibrosis/cirrhosis, until the earliest of the following: the first recorded outcome (CRC or BCN), loss to follow-up or the conclusion of the study period on 31 October 2024.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted using the real-time analytics tool within the TriNetX Live platform. Baseline cohort characteristics were summarised as means and SD for continuous variables, while categorical variables were presented as counts and percentages. Continuous variables are presented as mean±SD as provided by the TriNetX analytic platform; because patient-level raw data are not accessible, formal testing for normality could not be performed. A one-to-one PSM was performed using a nearest-neighbour greedy matching algorithm with a calliper width of 0.25 times the SD to ensure close matching between groups. Variables used in the initial matching included age, sex, race and ethnicity. A second, separate PSM model was conducted for each subset of patients to additionally incorporate metabolic risk factors such as hypertension, diabetes mellitus, dyslipidaemia and BMI. This additional matching was designed to isolate the effect of MASLD, MASH and MASH with liver fibrosis/cirrhosis on the risks of CRC and BCN by accounting for the influence of these metabolic conditions. Following matching, Cox proportional hazards models were used to estimate HRs with 95% CIs for the risks of CRC, BCN and the composite outcome of colorectal neoplasms. A threshold of p<0.05 was used to determine statistical significance in all analyses.

The mean age at index was similar across cohorts, with MASLD and non-MASLD groups having a mean age of 58.0±8.6 years, MASH and non-MASH groups averaging 58.9±8.6 years and the MASH with and without fibrosis/cirrhosis subgroups showing slightly older means of 62.6±7.7 and 62.5±7.7 years, respectively. Male representation was consistent across comparisons, with proportions ranging from 40.5% to 46.5%. The majority of patients in all groups identified as white, with the highest percentage seen in the MASH with fibrosis/cirrhosis subgroup (79.0%).

Key metabolic risk factors were significantly more prevalent among MASLD and MASH patients compared with their respective controls. For instance, the prevalence of hypertension in MASLD was 55.4% vs 25.1% in non-MASLD patients, and in MASH, it was 56.6% compared with 26.2% in non-MASH. Similarly, diabetes mellitus was observed in 32.7% of MASLD patients and 40.2% of MASH patients compared with 12.2% and 12.1% in their non-MASLD and non-MASH counterparts, respectively. Patients with MASH with fibrosis/cirrhosis showed a slightly higher prevalence of hypertension (55.5% vs 59.8%) and diabetes mellitus (50.3% vs 42.4%) compared with those without fibrosis/cirrhosis. Dyslipidaemia was also more common in MASLD (45.2% vs 18.8%), MASH (48.5% vs 18.9%) and MASH with fibrosis/cirrhosis (44.6% vs 53.7%) groups than their respective controls.

Metabolic dysfunction was further reflected in laboratory profiles, with MASLD and MASH groups showing elevated BMI (33.8±8.4 kg/m² and 34.8±8.9 kg/m², respectively) compared with controls (30.4±7.8 kg/m² and 30.4±7.9 kg/m²). Liver enzymes, including AST and ALT, were higher in MASLD and MASH groups, with the highest values observed in MASH patients (AST: 38.1±48.2 U/L, ALT: 43.5±56.9 U/L). Lipid profiles showed higher triglycerides and lower HDL cholesterol in MASLD and MASH groups compared with controls, reflecting underlying metabolic disturbances. Interestingly, LDL cholesterol was lower in the MASH with fibrosis/cirrhosis subgroup (84.1±61.0 mg/dL) compared with the non-fibrosis/cirrhosis MASH group (94.3±40.3 mg/dL).

Outcomes

In the matched cohort of 138 902 MASLD and non-MASLD patients, without controlling for metabolic risk factors, MASLD was associated with significantly higher risks for CRC and BCN. The HR for CRC was 2.488 (95% CI 2.098 to 2.951, p<0.001), while for BCN, it was 2.954 (95% CI 2.790 to 3.128, p<0.001). For the composite outcome of CRC and BCN, the HR was 2.931 (95% CI 2.768 to 3.104, p<0.001). After controlling for metabolic risk factors, MASLD patients maintained elevated risks, with an HR of 2.708 (95% CI 2.291 to 3.201, p<0.001) for CRC, 2.501 (95% CI 2.378 to 2.631, p<0.001) for BCN and 2.505 (95% CI 2.380 to 2.636, p<0.001) for the composite outcome (table 2, figure 2).

Discussion

This study highlights critical findings regarding the association between metabolic-associated fatty liver disease (MASLD), metabolic-associated steatohepatitis (MASH) and CRC as well as benign neoplasms. Patients with MASLD demonstrated a 2.7-fold higher risk of CRC and a 2.5-fold higher risk of benign neoplasms even after adjusting for metabolic risk factors such as hypertension, diabetes and obesity, indicating that MASLD is an independent risk factor for these outcomes. For patients with MASH, a more severe form of fatty liver disease, the risk of CRC was even more pronounced, with a fivefold increase compared with non-MASH controls, and nearly a twofold increased risk of benign neoplasms. Importantly, these elevated risks persisted after adjustment for metabolic factors, underscoring the need to distinguish MASLD from MASH in clinical practice, as disease severity appears to modulate CRC risk. MASLD and MASH are closely linked to obesity, insulin resistance and other metabolic risk factors, which are themselves established contributors to CRC risk.13 In our propensity-score-matched analysis adjusting for these factors, MASLD and MASH remained significantly associated with CRC, supporting an independent effect. This finding aligns with proposed mechanisms, such as hepatic and systemic inflammation, metabolic dysregulation and alterations in gut microbiota, that may create a pro-tumourigenic environment in MASLD and MASH.1416 While these pathways are likely to influence both benign and malignant colorectal neoplasms, their intensity and persistence may play a greater role in driving progression from benign to malignant lesions, which could partly explain differences in observed risks.

Previous studies have suggested that liver fibrosis may be linked to increased risk of colorectal neoplasia. For example, Haimi et al17 reported that high FIB-4 scores were associated with both prevalent and incident malignancies, including colon cancer (OR 2.75 over 10 years), and Kim et al18 found that NAFLD patients with advanced fibrosis had significantly higher risks of colorectal adenoma compared with those without fibrosis. In contrast, in our large, globally representative cohort, we did not observe a significant difference in CRC or BCN risk between MASH patients with and without fibrosis/cirrhosis, even after controlling for metabolic risk factors. Several factors may explain this discrepancy: (1) our study included only hospitalised adults aged 45–75 years, aligning with CRC screening age and reducing detection bias; (2) we used 1:1 PSM to balance demographic and metabolic comorbidities, minimising residual confounding present in prior studies and (3) fibrosis/cirrhosis was defined by coded diagnoses rather than non-invasive scores such as FIB-4, which may capture a different patient subset. Taken together, these differences suggest that while fibrosis/cirrhosis is a key predictor of liver-related outcomes, its independent effect on colorectal neoplasia risk may be attenuated once metabolic risk factors are carefully balanced. Our findings support the view that the metabolic component of MASLD/MASH, rather than fibrosis itself, exerts a stronger influence on CRC risk.

Given that CRC is the second most common cancer worldwide and a leading cause of cancer-related mortality,19 these findings carry significant clinical implications. Our results suggest that patients with MASLD or MASH may benefit from enhanced CRC screening strategies. Revisiting current clinical guidelines to incorporate these populations into high-risk groups for CRC screening could potentially reduce CRC-related mortality. Furthermore, periodic liver imaging in patients with risk factors such as hypertension, diabetes, hyperlipidaemia and obesity may aid in the early detection of MASLD/MASH, enabling timely interventions to mitigate downstream risks, including CRC.

Our study has several notable strengths. The use of the TriNetX Global Collaborative Network, encompassing over 150 million patients from 134 HCOs worldwide, ensures a large and diverse sample size, enhancing the generalisability of the findings. The study’s focus on hospitalised patients aged 45–75 years aligns with CRC screening guidelines, ensuring clinical relevance. By leveraging real-world EHR data, the study provides insights applicable to routine clinical practice. However, several limitations must be acknowledged. First, the study does not account for the severity or duration of metabolic risk factors. For instance, uncontrolled diabetes, severe dyslipidaemia and morbid obesity may confer higher CRC risk than milder forms of these conditions. Second, as a retrospective cohort study, causal relationships cannot be definitively established. Despite the use of PSM, unmeasured confounders, such as dietary habits, specific alcohol intake, physical activity and family history of CRC may influence our results. The reliance on clinical records introduces potential biases such as selection bias and underreporting of conditions, potentially affecting the robustness of the findings. Additionally, reliance on ICD-10 codes introduces the potential for diagnostic misclassification. Because colonoscopy data were not available in TriNetX, we could not directly adjust for CRC screening practices. Variation in screening uptake, diagnostic intensity and local guidelines across participating HCOs could influence detection rates and potentially bias our observed associations, possibly leading to underestimation or overestimation of risk differences between groups. The cohort consisted primarily of hospitalised patients, which may not represent the general population, especially individuals with milder forms of MASLD or MASH. Inclusion of only hospitalised patients may reduce the applicability of findings to outpatient populations. This limits the generalisability of the results. Lastly, the 5-year follow-up period may be insufficient to capture the full spectrum of cancer progression or other long-term outcomes related to MASLD and MASH.

In conclusion, our findings emphasise the importance of MASLD and MASH as independent risk factors for CRC and benign neoplasms. This study highlights an opportunity to optimise CRC screening guidelines and preventive strategies in populations with metabolic liver diseases, ultimately improving gastrointestinal and overall health outcomes. Prospective, longitudinal studies are needed to confirm the causal relationship between MASLD/MASH and CRC or benign neoplasms. Such studies should account for the severity and duration of metabolic risk factors, as well as the degree of liver involvement, to provide a more nuanced understanding of these associations. Moreover, future research should explore how early interventions targeting MASLD/MASH might influence CRC risk and outcomes and incorporate information on potentially chemopreventive medications such as statins or metformin, which may influence cancer risk and further refine effect estimates.
