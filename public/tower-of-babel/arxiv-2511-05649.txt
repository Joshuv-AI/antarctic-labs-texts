Magnusian: Relating the Eikonal Phase, the On-Shell Action, and the Scattering Generator arXiv: 2511.05649
Jung-Wook Kim, Raj Patil, Trevor Scheopner, and Jan Steinhoff

Abstract
Two fundamentally distinct types of quantities are both called “eikonal” in present amplitudes literature. The unitarity of the S-matrix ensures it can be written as the exponential of a Hermitian operator. The eikonal generator or Magnusian , which is the classical limit of the expectation value of that operator, generates all scattering observables. The leading order classical behavior of the phase of an S-matrix element is called the classical eikonal phase, and it coincides with a classical on-shell action. We demonstrate that the eikonal generator (Magnusian) and the eikonal phase (classical on-shell action) are inequivalent and find the exact general relationship between them. That relationship explains the special case of integrable scattering in which the two do coincide up to a Legendre transformation and explains why such a correspondence fails in general when spin or radiation are included.

In this article we focus on one of the most fundamental concepts linking quantum and classical physics: the eikonal. The eikonal was originally motivated by geometric optics and later played a crucial role in the formulation of quantum mechanics. It has also been used recently to analyze the two-body problem. But, there are two competing and incompatible usages of the name “eikonal” in present amplitudes literature. The first is proportional to the expectation value of the operator logarithm of the S-matrix and in the classical limit becomes a function whose derivatives contain all classical scattering observables. The second is proportional to the logarithm of an S-matrix element and in the classical limit becomes the on-shell action of the corresponding classical theory. Even in the classical limit, the two do not agree. In the simple case of an integrable system, they coincide (up to a Legendre transformation). In this article we prove the non-trivial relationship between the two quantities in general. To understand that relationship, we need to put them in the context of four closely related but conceptually distinct quantities present in the literature which have the intent of capturing the physics of perturbative classical scattering.

(I) The On-Shell Action.
For a generic classical system, the action functional
\mathcal{S}
evaluated on the trajectory that solves the equation of motion (on-shell) is called the
on-shell action
, or Hamilton’s principal function. The on-shell action is a function of the boundary conditions (initial and final coordinates) of the trajectory, and through its derivatives via the Hamilton-Jacobi conditions, it determines the entire classical dynamics. Because it depends on boundary conditions, the classical on-shell action is naturally an “in-out” quantity.

2.1
The On-Shell Action

For a classical system of coordinates
Q(t)
, canonically conjugate momenta
P(t)
, and Hamiltonian
\mathcal{H}(t,Q,P)
, the action is fundamentally defined as the functional:

\mathcal{S}[Q,P](t_{f},Q_{f},t_{i},Q_{i})=\int_{t_{i}}^{t_{f}}(P\dot{Q}-\mathcal{H}(t,Q,P))dt

(10)

where the bracket versus parentheses notation
[],()
indicates that the action is a functional of the phase space path
(Q(t),P(t))
while it is an ordinary function of the boundary times
t_{i},t_{f}
and boundary coordinates
Q_{i}=Q(t_{i})
and
Q_{f}=Q(t_{f})
. When considering symmetries of the action, it will also be useful to consider it as a functional of the Hamiltonian. Under a variation of all its dependencies, the action satisfies:

\delta\mathcal{S}=P_{f}\delta Q_{f}-P_{i}\delta Q_{i}-\mathcal{H}_{f}\delta t_{f}+\mathcal{H}_{i}\delta t_{i}+\!\int_{t_{i}}^{t_{f}}\!\!\left(\delta P\left(\dot{Q}-\frac{\partial\mathcal{H}}{\partial P}\right)-\delta Q\left(\dot{P}+\frac{\partial\mathcal{H}}{\partial Q}\right)-\Delta\mathcal{H}\right)dt

(11)

where
\Delta\mathcal{H}
represents a possible variation in the
functional form of the Hamiltonian
, not simply a change in its
value
. When the path
(Q(t),P(t))
is placed on-shell, we have Hamilton’s equations:

\frac{dQ}{dt}=\{Q,\mathcal{H}\}=\frac{\partial\mathcal{H}}{\partial P},\hskip 21.52771pt\frac{dP}{dt}=\{P,\mathcal{H}\}=-\frac{\partial\mathcal{H}}{\partial Q},

(12)

and when the the action is evaluated on the on-shell path, the resulting function, known as the on-shell action (OSA) or Hamilton’s principal function, thus has the exact differential under changes in the boundary conditions:

d\mathcal{S}=P_{f}dQ_{f}-P_{i}dQ_{i}-\mathcal{H}_{f}dt_{f}+\mathcal{H}_{i}dt_{i}

(13)

where
\mathcal{H}_{f}=\mathcal{H}(t_{f},Q_{f},P_{f})
and
\mathcal{H}_{i}=\mathcal{H}(t_{i},Q_{i},P_{i})
. This exact differential is identical to the Hamilton-Jacobi conditions:

P_{f}=\frac{\partial\mathcal{S}}{\partial Q_{f}},\qquad P_{i}=-\frac{\partial\mathcal{S}}{\partial Q_{i}},\qquad\mathcal{H}_{f}=-\frac{\partial\mathcal{S}}{\partial t_{f}},\qquad\mathcal{H}_{i}=\frac{\partial\mathcal{S}}{\partial t_{i}}.

(14)

Formally, given solutions to the classical equations of motion, integrating these conditions determines the OSA and in converse, knowing the OSA determines the solutions to the classical equations of motion through these conditions. The Hamilton-Jacobi conditions are exactly equivalent to the statement that the classical OSA is the generating function of the canonical transformation corresponding to time evolution from
t_{i}
to
t_{f}
.

The OSA is properly a function of the
boundary conditions

(Q_{i},Q_{f})
as independent variables. However, for comparing with the Magnusian (in examples, to directly see their inequivalence), it will be convenient to use the Hamilton-Jacobi conditions to express it in terms of the
initial conditions

(Q_{i},P_{i})
. Importantly, the on-shell action expressed as a function of initial conditions
\mathcal{S}(t_{f},t_{i},Q_{i},P_{i})
does not act properly as a generating function in any sense.

2.2
The Eikonal Generator (Magnusian)

Whatever the actual Hamiltonian
\mathcal{H}(t,Q,P)
is, for fixed
t_{i}
and
t_{f}
, the relationship between
(Q_{i},P_{i})
and
(Q_{f},P_{f})
is a canonical transformation. In particular, it is a canonical transformation in the subgroup of canonical transformations which are continuously connected to the identity. The group of such canonical transformations is a connected Lie group and therefore there exists an element of the Lie algebra which performs that canonical transformation under exponentiation.
2
2

2

Technically, this requires the canonical transformation be within a neighborhood of the identity so that the logarithm in the Lie group is well-defined. This is precisely the same condition necessary for the on-shell action to determine the canonical transformation through the Hamilton-Jacobi conditions,
which is the context we are interested in.

The elements of the Lie algebra of canonical transformations are Hamiltonian vector fields, i.e. differential operators of the form
\{f,\,\text{\scriptsize\textbullet}\,\}
for a well-behaved function

on phase space. So, there must exist a function
\chi
so that the Lie algebra element
\{\chi,\,\text{\scriptsize\textbullet}\,\}
when exponentiated gives the canonical transformation of time evolution. This is the completely classical definition of the Magnusian:

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} A(Q_{f},P_{f})&=\left.e^{\{\chi(Q,P),\,\text{\scriptsize\textbullet}\,\}}A(Q,P)\right|_{(Q,P)=(Q_{i},P_{i})}\\
&=A(Q_{i},P_{i})+\{\chi,A\}+\frac{1}{2}\{\chi,\{\chi,A\}\}+\frac{1}{6}\{\chi,\{\chi,\{\chi,A\}\}\}+\cdots\end{aligned}

(15)

for

any analytic function on phase space. Because the canonical transformation between initial and final phase space coordinates depends on
t_{i},t_{f}
, the Magnusian is properly written as a function as
\chi(t_{f},t_{i},Q,P)
. The exponential representation in (

The original method for calculating the Magnusian, the Magnus formula

\Omega(t_{f},t_{i})=\{\chi(t_{f},t_{i},Q,P),\,\text{\scriptsize\textbullet}\,\}.

(16)

With

A(Q_{f},P_{f})=\left.e^{\Omega(t_{f},t_{i})}A\right|_{(Q_{i},P_{i})}.

(17)

The total

(Q_{f},P_{f})

\frac{dA_{f}}{dt_{f}}=\frac{d}{dt_{f}}\left(e^{\Omega}\right)e^{-\Omega}A_{f}=\{A_{f},\mathcal{H}_{f}\}

(18)

and so we can identify that as vector fields:

\frac{d}{dt_{f}}\left(e^{\Omega}\right)e^{-\Omega}=\{-\mathcal{H}_{f},\,\text{\scriptsize\textbullet}\,\}.

(19)

For

\frac{\partial}{\partial\varepsilon}\left(\frac{d}{dt_{f}}\left(e^{\varepsilon\Omega}\right)e^{-{\varepsilon\Omega}}\right)=e^{\varepsilon\Omega}\frac{d\Omega}{dt_{f}}e^{-\varepsilon\Omega}=e^{\varepsilon[\Omega,\,\text{\scriptsize\textbullet}\,]}\left(\frac{d\Omega}{dt_{f}}\right).

Integrating this from

\{-\mathcal{H}_{f},\,\text{\scriptsize\textbullet}\,\}=\frac{e^{[\Omega,\,\text{\scriptsize\textbullet}\,]}-1}{[\Omega,\,\text{\scriptsize\textbullet}\,]}\frac{d\Omega}{dt_{f}}

(21)

where the operator function expression is defined precisely through Taylor series. Formally inverting this operator, reexpressing
\Omega

[\{A,\,\text{\scriptsize\textbullet}\,\},\{B,\,\text{\scriptsize\textbullet}\,\}]=\{\{A,B\},\,\text{\scriptsize\textbullet}\,\}
gives:

\frac{d\chi(t_{f},Q(t_{f}),P(t_{f}))}{dt_{f}}=-\frac{\{\chi,\,\text{\scriptsize\textbullet}\,\}}{e^{\{\chi,\,\text{\scriptsize\textbullet}\,\}}-1}\mathcal{H}_{f}(t_{f},Q(t_{f}),P(t_{f})).

(22)

We are actually interested in the explicit
t_{f}
dependence of the Magnusian, and so we change to the partial
t_{f}
derivative by using the chain rule and Hamilton’s equations (meaning the point
(Q,P)
is no longer time evolved):

\frac{\partial\chi}{\partial t_{f}}=\frac{d\chi}{dt_{f}}-\{\chi,\mathcal{H}_{f}\}

(23)

which upon simplification gives:

\frac{\partial\chi(t_{f},t_{i},Q,P)}{\partial t_{f}}=-\frac{\{\,\text{\scriptsize\textbullet}\,,\chi\}}{e^{\{\,\text{\scriptsize\textbullet}\,,\chi\}}-1}\mathcal{H}(t_{f},Q,P).

(24)

Note the sign difference in the nested brackets between (
22
) and (
24
).
Repeating the same analysis for the initial time dependence gives:

\frac{\partial\chi(t_{f},t_{i},Q,P)}{\partial t_{i}}=\frac{\{\chi,\,\text{\scriptsize\textbullet}\,\}}{e^{\{\chi,\,\text{\scriptsize\textbullet}\,\}}-1}\mathcal{H}(t_{i},Q,P).

(25)

This formula can also be guessed by appreciating that interchanging the initial and final times then performs the inverse of time evolution as a canonical transformation, which by (
15
) must be generated by
-\chi
.

Equation (
24
) is the
Magnus formula
; it is formally exact, but is only useful for calculations when treated by series expansion. The corresponding series is the
Magnus series
. The relevant expansion is:

\frac{x}{e^{x}-1}=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{B_{k}x^{k}}{k!}

(26)

where
B_{k}
are the Bernoulli numbers (with
B_{1}=-\frac{1}{2}
). With this expansion, the Magnus formula (
24
) more properly becomes:

\frac{\partial\chi(t_{f},t_{i},Q,P))}{\partial t_{f}}=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{B_{k}}{k!}\{\,\text{\scriptsize\textbullet}\,,\chi\}^{k}(-\mathcal{H}(t_{f},Q,P))

(27)

to be solved with the initial condition:

\chi(t_{i},t_{i},Q,P)=0

(28)

so that
e^{\{\chi,\,\text{\scriptsize\textbullet}\,\}}
becomes the identity transformation when no time evolution is applied.

In practical implementations such as numerical integrators, the series (
27
) is truncated to some finite order
m
, and the Magnusian corresponding to the finite evolution time
\Delta t=t_{f}-t_{i}
computed from the truncated series to the
m
-th order has an error bound of
\mathcal{O}(\Delta t^{m+2})

Blanes:2008xlr
.

2.3
The Classical Interaction Picture

While we will prove the relationship between
\chi
and
\mathcal{S}
exactly, for most calculations of any use perturbation theory is necessary. Just as in quantum field theory, the interaction picture (Dirac picture) is the natural setting for perturbation theory, and in particular it is useful for calculating the Magnusian
Kim:2025olv
. The key idea is to separate the Hamiltonian
\mathcal{H}
into a (solvable) unperturbed Hamiltonian
\mathcal{H}_{0}
and an interaction term
\mathcal{H}_{1}
, and to describe the evolution under the full Hamiltonian
\mathcal{H}=\mathcal{H}_{0}+\mathcal{H}_{1}
by perturbative corrections to the (exact) solutions of the dynamics of the unperturbed Hamiltonian. To facilitate the perturbative expansion, it is convenient to introduce a formal expansion parameter
\lambda
and write the full Hamiltonian in terms of phase space variables
(q,p)
as

\mathcal{H}(t,q,p)=\mathcal{H}_{0}(t,q,p)+\lambda\mathcal{H}_{1}(t,q,p)\,.

(29)

Because we assume that the dynamics produced by
\mathcal{H}_{0}
alone are explicitly solvable, we are able to compute the on-shell action
\mathcal{S}_{0}(t_{f},q_{f},t_{i},q_{i})
corresponding to time evolution under
\mathcal{H}_{0}
alone. In the full system, we then use
-\mathcal{S}_{0}(t,q,t_{i},Q)
as a generating function for a canonical transformation to interaction picture variables
(Q,P)
:

P=-\frac{\partial\mathcal{S}_{0}(t,q,t_{i},Q)}{\partial Q},\hskip 21.52771ptp=\frac{\partial\mathcal{S}_{0}(t,q,t_{i},Q)}{\partial q}.

(30)

By the Hamilton-Jacobi conditions (
14
), if the free system was started with initial condition
Q
then to reach final condition
q
, it would need initial momentum
P
. The new variables
(Q,P)
time evolve using Hamilton’s equations with the new Hamiltonian:

\mathcal{H}^{\prime}(t,Q,P)=\mathcal{H}(t,q,p)+\frac{\partial\mathcal{S}_{0}(t,q,t_{i},Q)}{\partial t}=\lambda\mathcal{H}_{1}(t,q(t,Q,P),p(t,Q,P))=\lambda\mathcal{H}_{I}(t,Q,P)

(31)

due to the Hamilton-Jacobi conditions. In the above, the final step defines the interaction Hamiltonian
\mathcal{H}_{I}
. With this new Hamiltonian, Hamilton’s equations become:

\frac{dQ}{dt}=\{Q,\mathcal{H}^{\prime}\}=\lambda\frac{\partial\mathcal{H}_{I}}{\partial P},\hskip 21.52771pt\frac{dP}{dt}=\{P,\mathcal{H}^{\prime}\}=-\lambda\frac{\partial\mathcal{H}_{I}}{\partial Q}.

(32)

Time derivatives in the interaction picture are by construction of order
\lambda
and so give natural formal expansions in powers of
\lambda
, making the Magnus series (
27
) become perturbatively useful. Explicitly:

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\chi(t_{f},t_{i},Q,P))}{\partial t_{f}}=-\lambda\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{B_{k}}{k!}\{\,\text{\scriptsize\textbullet}\,,\chi\}^{k}(\mathcal{H}_{I}(t_{f},Q,P))\,.

(33)

\chi
is of
\mathcal{O}(\lambda)
and so each higher term in this series is suppressed by an additional order of
\lambda
. Due to this, equation (
33
) can be iteratively integrated to obtain the Magnusian
\chi
as a formal explicit series in
\lambda
.

If the interaction Hamiltonian is the interaction term of some scattering process, then because
\mathcal{H}_{I}
will go to 0 sufficiently quickly for early and late times, the
t_{f}\to\infty,t_{i}\to-\infty
limits of
\chi
exist. Taking such limits gives that in that case, by (
15
), incoming and outgoing observables exactly satisfy (
2
). It is (
2
) which defines precisely what we mean when we say that the Magnusian generates all classical scattering observables.

3
The Action from the Magnusian

3.1
Classically

We will now prove the right half of (
6
) which relates the on-shell action to the Magnusian completely classically. Momentarily consider a hypothetical system with time independent Hamiltonian
h(Q,P)
. Call the time parameter for this hypothetical system
\varepsilon
. For
A(Q,P)
any analytic function on phase space:

\displaystyle\frac{dA}{d\varepsilon}=\{A,h\}\implies\frac{d^{n}A}{d\varepsilon^{n}}=\{-h,\,\text{\scriptsize\textbullet}\,\}^{n}A

(34)

\displaystyle\implies A(Q_{f},P_{f})=\left.e^{\{-(\varepsilon_{f}-\varepsilon_{i})h,\,\text{\scriptsize\textbullet}\,\}}A\right|_{(Q,P)=(Q_{i},P_{i})}

(35)

by Taylor series. The first implication is only true because we assumed
h
is
\varepsilon
independent.

Now let us return to our real system with Hamiltonian
\mathcal{H}(t,Q,P)
giving true
t
time evolution. Notice that equation (
35
) is nearly identical to (
15
). In particular, if we consider the path
(q(\varepsilon),p(\varepsilon))
through phase space satisfying:

q(0)=Q_{i},\hskip 21.52771ptp(0)=P_{i},\hskip 21.52771pt\frac{dq}{d\varepsilon}=-\frac{\partial\chi(t_{f},t_{i},q,p)}{\partial p},\hskip 21.52771pt\frac{dp}{d\varepsilon}=\frac{\partial\chi(t_{f},t_{i},q,p)}{\partial q}

(36)

then (
35
) becomes (
15
) where we identify
-\chi
as the effective “
\varepsilon
-time”-independent Hamiltonian and the
\varepsilon
-time evolution interval as
[0,1]
. By comparing (
35
) and (
15
), it is automatic that:

q(1)=Q_{f},\hskip 21.52771ptp(1)=P_{f}.

(37)

Therefore, we can equivalently define the path
(q(\varepsilon),p(\varepsilon))
as satisfying the
\chi
equations of motion with
boundary conditions
:

q(0)=Q_{i},\hskip 21.52771ptq(1)=Q_{f},\hskip 21.52771pt\frac{dq}{d\varepsilon}=-\frac{\partial\chi}{\partial p},\hskip 21.52771pt\frac{dp}{d\varepsilon}=\frac{\partial\chi}{\partial q}.

(38)

The path
q(\varepsilon),p(\varepsilon)
is not the physical path
(Q(t),P(t))
produced by the Hamiltonian
\mathcal{H}
. The two only generically coincide at the end-points. By (
35
), the
(q(\varepsilon),p(\varepsilon))
path is formally given by (that for any analytic
A(Q,P)
:

A(q(\varepsilon),p(\varepsilon))=e^{\varepsilon\{\chi,\,\text{\scriptsize\textbullet}\,\}}A(Q_{i},P_{i}).

(39)

Continuing with the view that
-\chi
is like an effective Hamiltonian, the on-shell action corresponding to
\chi
is:

\mathcal{I}(t_{f},Q_{f},t_{i},Q_{i})=\int_{0}^{1}\left(p(\varepsilon)\frac{dq(\varepsilon)}{d\varepsilon}+\chi(t_{f},t_{i},q(\varepsilon),p(\varepsilon))\right)d\varepsilon.

(40)

We write that
\mathcal{I}
depends on
t_{f},t_{i}
because
\chi
does, but it is very important to understand that this is not transparently the same time dependence that an on-shell action normally has. The “time” corresponding to
\chi
is
\varepsilon
, and formally
\mathcal{I}
could be written as a function of the initial and final values of
\varepsilon
, but we always keep those equal to
0,1
, and so those hypothetical dependencies are not written. If we did carry them through, the initial and final
\varepsilon
derivatives of
\mathcal{I}
would give the initial and final
\chi
as usual by the Hamilton-Jacobi conditions.

Let us now understand how
\mathcal{I}
actually depends on its parameters. Under full variation of all of its arguments, we have:

\displaystyle\delta\mathcal{I}=

\displaystyle\ \left.p\delta q\right|^{1}_{0}+\delta t_{f}\int_{0}^{1}\frac{\partial\chi(t_{f},t_{i},q,p)}{\partial t_{f}}d\varepsilon+\delta t_{i}\int_{0}^{1}\frac{\partial\chi(t_{f},t_{i},q,p)}{\partial t_{i}}d\varepsilon

\displaystyle\ +\int_{0}^{1}\left(\delta p\left(\frac{dq}{d\varepsilon}+\frac{\partial\chi}{\partial p}\right)-\delta q\left(\frac{dp}{d\varepsilon}-\frac{\partial\chi}{\partial q}\right)\right)d\varepsilon.

(41)

Applying that we use the Magnusian path (
38
), this becomes:

\delta\mathcal{I}=P_{f}\delta Q_{f}-P_{i}\delta Q_{i}+\delta t_{f}\int_{0}^{1}\frac{\partial\chi}{\partial t_{f}}d\varepsilon+\delta t_{i}\int_{0}^{1}\frac{\partial\chi}{\partial t_{i}}d\varepsilon.

(42)

We can compute the remaining integrals by using the Magnus formulas (
24
) and (
25
). First, note that by (
39
):

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\chi(t_{f},t_{i},Q_{f},P_{f})}{\partial t_{f}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial\chi(t_{f},t_{i},q(1),p(1))}{\partial t_{f}}=e^{(1-\varepsilon)\{\chi,\,\text{\scriptsize\textbullet}\,\}}\frac{\partial\chi(t_{f},t_{i},q(\varepsilon),p(\varepsilon))}{\partial t_{f}}

(43)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\chi(t_{f},t_{i},Q_{i},P_{i})}{\partial t_{i}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial\chi(t_{f},t_{i},q(0),p(0))}{\partial t_{i}}=e^{-\varepsilon\{\chi,\,\text{\scriptsize\textbullet}\,\}}\frac{\partial\chi(t_{f},t_{i},q(\varepsilon),p(\varepsilon))}{\partial t_{i}}.

(44)

The first of these, fed into (
24
), results in:

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}\frac{\partial\chi(t_{f},t_{i},q(\varepsilon),p(\varepsilon))}{\partial t_{f}}d\varepsilon=

\displaystyle\ \int_{0}^{1}e^{(1-\varepsilon)\{\,\text{\scriptsize\textbullet}\,,\chi\}}d\varepsilon\frac{\partial\chi(t_{f},t_{i},Q_{f},P_{f})}{\partial t_{f}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\ \frac{e^{\{\,\text{\scriptsize\textbullet}\,,\chi\}}-1}{\{\,\text{\scriptsize\textbullet}\,,\chi\}}\frac{\{\,\text{\scriptsize\textbullet}\,,\chi\}}{e^{\{\,\text{\scriptsize\textbullet}\,,\chi\}}-1}(-\mathcal{H}(t_{f},Q_{f},P_{f}))=-\mathcal{H}_{f}

(45)

while the second, following the same steps but with (
25
), gives:

\int_{0}^{1}\frac{\partial\chi(t_{f},t_{i},q(\varepsilon),p(\varepsilon))}{\partial t_{i}}d\varepsilon=\mathcal{H}(t_{i},Q_{i},P_{i})=\mathcal{H}_{i}.

(46)

Therefore, the exact differential of
\mathcal{I}(t_{f},Q_{f},t_{i},Q_{i})
is:

d\mathcal{I}=P_{f}dQ_{f}-P_{i}dQ_{i}-\mathcal{H}_{f}dt_{f}+\mathcal{H}_{i}dt_{i}.

(47)

\mathcal{I}
precisely satisfies all of the Hamilton-Jacobi conditions (
14
) and owing to (
28
) vanishes when
t_{f}=t_{i}
. Thus, due to the uniqueness of solutions to Hamilton’s equations of motion,
\mathcal{I}
must
exactly
be the on-shell action corresponding to the true physical path
(Q(t),P(t))
evolved with the true Hamiltonian! That is:

\mathcal{S}(t_{f},Q_{f},t_{i},Q_{i})=\int_{t_{i}}^{t_{f}}\left(P\frac{dQ}{dt}-\mathcal{H}(t,Q,P)\right)dt=\int_{0}^{1}\left(p\frac{dq}{d\varepsilon}+\chi(t_{f},t_{i},q,p)\right)d\varepsilon

(48)

where
(Q(t),P(t))
is the physical path through phase space in time satisfying Hamilton’s equations (
12
) and
(q(\varepsilon),p(\varepsilon))
is the path satisfying (
38
) with the same boundary conditions as the physical path. We have therefore derived equation (
48
) as an exact, fully general relationship between the Magnusian and the on-shell action.
3
3

3

Shortly after the submission of this paper, Ref.
Kim:2025sey
appeared where (
48
) was stated with a description of an outline for its derivation.

We can complete the
\varepsilon
integration in (
48
) to arrive at a formal relation between the Magnusian and the OSA which explicitly only depends on the boundary conditions. By (
39
), the integrand of (
40
) can be written as:

p(\varepsilon)\frac{dq}{d\varepsilon}(\varepsilon)+\chi(t_{f},t_{i},q(\varepsilon),p(\varepsilon))=e^{\varepsilon\{\chi,\,\text{\scriptsize\textbullet}\,\}}(P_{i}\{\chi,Q_{i}\})+\chi(t_{f},t_{i},Q_{i},P_{i}).

(49)

The
\varepsilon
integral in (
48
) can then be computed to give the novel half of (
6
):

\mathcal{S}(t_{f},Q_{f},t_{i},Q_{i})=\frac{e^{\{\chi,\,\text{\scriptsize\textbullet}\,\}}-1}{\{\chi,\,\text{\scriptsize\textbullet}\,\}}(P_{i}\{\chi,Q_{i}\})+\chi(t_{f},t_{i},Q_{i},P_{i}).

(50)

Equation (
50
) is formally exact, but difficult to apply in a meaningful sense except perturbatively. If
\chi
is known to some order in perturbation theory, then everything on the right hand side can be computed to that same order, so that
\mathcal{S}
is then found to the appropriate order. To express
\mathcal{S}
properly, it must be written as a function of
Q_{f}
which can be found to the relevant order from
\chi
using (
15
) in terms of
(Q_{i},P_{i})
and then inverted to that order so that the
P_{i}
appearances in
\mathcal{S}
can be eliminated.

By applying the inverse operator to the differential operator acting on
P_{i}\{\chi,Q_{i}\}
in (
50
), we have:

\chi+P_{i}\{\chi,Q_{i}\}=\frac{\{\chi,\,\text{\scriptsize\textbullet}\,\}}{e^{\{\chi,\,\text{\scriptsize\textbullet}\,\}}-1}\mathcal{S}

(51)

which now is directly in the form appropriate for the Magnus series. This expression can be used to perturbatively compute
\chi
. To make sense of a Poisson bracket acting on the on-shell action, use that
Q_{f}
is an implicit function of
Q_{i}
and
P_{i}
which can be computed (perturbatively) using the Hamilton-Jacobi conditions.

In the case of an integrable system, it is possible to go to action-angle variables with angle coordinates
\theta
and adiabatically invariant momenta
J
. In such a case, the Hamiltonian is only a function of the
J
, and consequently the Magnusian is only a function of them as well. Immediately then (
15
) trivializes so that:

\theta_{f}=\theta_{i}-\frac{\partial\chi}{\partial J_{i}},\hskip 21.52771ptJ_{f}=J_{i}.

(52)

This further causes (
50
) to simplify significantly so that:

\mathcal{S}(t_{f},\theta_{f},t_{i},\theta_{i})=J_{i}(\theta_{f}-\theta_{i})+\chi(t_{f},t_{i},J_{i}).

(53)

So, in the case of an integrable system expressed in action-angle variables, the on-shell action and the Magnusian are related by a simple Legendre transformation exchanging the action variable
J
for the coordinate impulse
\theta_{f}-\theta_{i}
.

3.2
Quantumly

We will now prove (
48
) from quantum mechanics, which allows closer contact to traditional amplitudes techniques expressed in terms of the quantum S-matrix. In quantum mechanics, the Magnusian (for finite time evolution) is very simply defined in terms of the time evolution operator
\hat{U}
(which becomes the S-matrix in the interaction picture and in the long time evolution limit). As well, the matrix elements of the time evolution operator can be related, in the classical limit and by using the path integral, very directly to the classical on-shell action. So, it is the path integral which will provide us the direct link between the action and the Magnusian. To appreciate this, we first need to carefully review the interplay between the classical limit and the path integral.

Consider our Hilbert space
\mathbb{H}
to be the space of complex square-integrable functions of
d
real variables
(Q^{1},...,Q^{d})
. Let
\hat{Q}^{a}
be the usual coordinate operators on this space and
|Q\rangle
be the corresponding “coordinate-eigenstates”. Let
\hat{P}_{a}
be the usual momentum operators conjugate to
\hat{Q}^{a}
and let
|P\rangle
be the corresponding “momentum-eigenstates”. Assume we have chosen our coordinates so that
\langle\hat{Q}^{a}\rangle
and
\langle\hat{P}_{a}\rangle
have finite classical limits
Q^{a}_{\text{cl}}
and
P^{\text{cl}}_{a}
(in the sense of the limit defined by KMOC
Kosower:2018adc
). This classical-well-behaved-ness is automatic if our coordinates and momenta are the usual worldline coordinates and linear momenta of a finite collection of particles (massless or massive) or if they are the field operators of a massless field (which becomes a classical field in the classical limit of quantum field theory).
4
4

4

In the case of a massless quantum field theory, the coordinates can be the values of the fields at lattice points in a finite lattice with size regulated by
d
. The
d\to\infty
limit treated appropriately as usual will give the field theory limit.
As explained by KMOC, the classical limit (properly taken) of a massive quantum field is not a classical field theory but instead a collection of theories of different numbers of massive point-particles. Massive fields can be treated just fine by our description because within any given sector of particle numbers, the particles of that sector can be assigned classically finite worldline coordinates and linear momenta as usual, and in the classical limit the different sectors fully decouple.

Let
\hat{G}
be an operator on our Hilbert space. From that operator, define the function
G(Q,P)
, called the inverse Weyl transform
LEE1995147
;
Moyal_1949
of
\hat{G}
, by:

G(Q,P)=\mathcal{W}^{-1}(\hat{G})=\int e^{\frac{i}{\hbar}q^{a}P_{a}}\left\langle Q-\frac{q}{2}\right|\hat{G}\left|Q+\frac{q}{2}\right\rangle d^{d}q.

(54)

Taking the conjugate of both sides shows that
G
is a real-valued function if and only if
\hat{G}
is Hermitian. We call
G
the Wigner function associated to
\hat{G}
. Fourier transforming
G
with respect to
P
followed by a slight change of variables and use of the Baker-Campbell-Hausdorff formula allows one to invert the above and find that
\hat{G}
is determined by
G
as:

\hat{G}=\int e^{ik_{a}(\hat{Q}^{a}-Q^{a})+i\ell^{a}(\hat{P}_{a}-P_{a})}G(Q,P)\frac{d^{d}kd^{d}\ell d^{d}Qd^{d}P}{(2\pi)^{2d}}.

(55)

Very carefully note that this is not a quantization scheme. The definition of
G
and its relationship to
\hat{G}
make no use of or reference to any notion of classical limit and we have made no identification of
G
with being the classical function “corresponding” to
\hat{G}
. In general such an identification is incorrect.
5
5

5

Even if you make this identification in one set of coordinates, under a change of coordinates the correspondence will be broken.
If
\hat{G}
is such that
\langle\hat{G}\rangle
is a classically finite function
G_{\text{cl}}(Q_{\text{cl}},P_{\text{cl}})
, then the Wigner function
G(Q,P)
will have some
\hbar
expansion:

G(Q,P)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\hbar^{n}G_{n}(Q,P).

(56)

The classical limit will take
\hbar\to 0^{+}
and then squeeze the wavefunction to localize around
(Q_{\text{cl}},P_{\text{cl}})
. Consequently:

G_{0}(Q,P)=G_{\text{cl}}(Q,P).

(57)

The details deriving this are given in Appendix
A
. Only the
\hbar
-independent piece of
G
is fixed by the classical limit. Equation (
57
) is the proper statement of how classical functions on phase space are contained within quantum operators.

The Wigner transform of a product is not the product of Wigner transforms. Instead, the induced product on Wigner functions is the star product
\star
, which becomes the ordinary product at leading order in
\hbar
:

\mathcal{W}^{-1}(\hat{\mathcal{W}}(A)\hat{\mathcal{W}}(B))=A\star B=Ae^{\frac{i\hbar}{2}\{\leftarrow,\rightarrow\}}B=A_{0}B_{0}+\mathcal{O}(\hbar)

(58)

where
A_{0}
and
B_{0}
are the
\hbar\to 0^{+}
limits of the Wigner functions
A
and
B
. The leading order behavior ensures that in the classical limit the expectation of a product becomes the product of expectations. The notation
\{\leftarrow,\rightarrow\}
means the bi-differential operator that is the classical Poisson bracket with the left-arrow/right-arrow indicating differentiation in that position of only functions to its left/right. Using the Weyl transform, the induced bracket on Wigner functions is the Moyal bracket
\left\{\!\!\left\{\,\text{\scriptsize\textbullet}\,,\,\text{\scriptsize\textbullet}\,\right\}\!\!\right\}
, and it immediately follows that:

\mathcal{W}^{-1}\left(\frac{1}{i\hbar}[\hat{A},\hat{B}]\right)=\left\{\!\!\left\{A,B\right\}\!\!\right\}=A(Q,P)\frac{2}{\hbar}\sin\left(\frac{\hbar}{2}\{\leftarrow,\rightarrow\}\right)B(Q,P)=\{A_{0},B_{0}\}+\mathcal{O}(\hbar)

(59)

which is the proper statement relating commutators and classical Poisson brackets. For more thorough coverage of Wigner functions, the Weyl transform, and the Moyal bracket, see Refs.
LEE1995147
;
Moyal_1949
.

The path integral is most naturally expressed in terms of Wigner functions (and is likewise not a quantization scheme). Let
\hat{U}(t,t_{i})
be the usual time evolution operator and
U(t_{f},Q_{f},t_{i},Q_{i})
be the usual propagation amplitude satisfying:

\hat{U}(t,t)=\mathbbm{1},\hskip 21.52771pti\hbar\frac{\partial\hat{U}}{\partial t}(t,t_{i})=\hat{H}(t)\hat{U}(t,t_{i}),\hskip 21.52771ptU(t_{f},Q_{f},t_{i},Q_{i})=\langle Q_{f}|\hat{U}(t_{f},t_{i})|\hat{Q}_{i}\rangle

(60)

with
\hat{H}(t)
the Hamiltonian operator of the system. Define the functional
\mathcal{S}_{H}[Q,P]
of the path
(Q(t^{\prime}),P(t^{\prime}))
by:

\mathcal{S}_{H}[Q,P]=\int_{t_{i}}^{t}\Bigl(P_{a}(t^{\prime})dQ^{a}(t^{\prime})-H(t^{\prime},Q(t^{\prime}),P(t^{\prime}))dt^{\prime}\Bigr)

(61)

where
H
is the Wigner function associated to the Hamiltonian operator
\hat{H}
. The path integral measure
\mathcal{D}_{\hbar}Q\mathcal{D}_{\hbar}P
is formally defined by:

\mathcal{D}_{\hbar}Q\mathcal{D}_{\hbar}P=\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{d^{d}P_{0}}{(2\pi\hbar)^{d}}\frac{d^{d}Q_{1}d^{d}P_{1}}{(2\pi\hbar)^{d}}...\frac{d^{d}Q_{N-1}d^{d}P_{N-1}}{(2\pi\hbar)^{d}}.

(62)

The path integral is:

\displaystyle U(t_{f},Q_{f},t_{i},Q_{i})

\displaystyle=\int_{Q(t_{i})=Q_{i}}^{Q(t_{f})=Q_{f}}e^{\frac{i}{\hbar}\mathcal{S}_{H}[Q,P]}\mathcal{D}_{\hbar}Q\mathcal{D}_{\hbar}P

(63)

where the path
Q(t),P(t)
is broken up into pieces
Q(t_{n})=Q_{n}
,
P(t_{n})=P_{n}
with
Q_{0}=Q_{i}
,
Q_{N}=Q_{f}
,
t_{n}=t_{i}+\frac{n}{N}(t_{f}-t_{i})
and the Wigner action functional
\mathcal{S}_{H}
is understood through a Riemann sum. The path integral in this form is straightforwardly derived from the short-time evolution behavior of
\hat{U}
, combined with its composition rule
\hat{U}(t_{f},t_{i})=\hat{U}(t_{f},t^{\prime})\hat{U}(t^{\prime},t_{i})
, and further combined with the expression (
55
) for the function
H
.
H
cannot be generally replaced by
\mathcal{H}_{\text{cl}}
. Instead, by (
57
),
\mathcal{H}_{\text{cl}}=\lim_{\hbar\to 0^{+}}H
and a generic quantum theory will have
\mathcal{O}(\hbar)
corrections between the two. Such corrections may be strictly necessary in order to have the correct symmetries as a quantum theory.

The path integral provides a direct way to compute the leading classical behavior of the matrix elements of the time evolution operator. In the
\hbar\to 0^{+}
limit, it becomes a stationary phase integral with the relevant contribution coming precisely from the classical trajectory. Thus, we expect that
U(t_{f},Q_{f},t_{i},Q_{i})
should contain a prefactor
e^{\frac{i}{\hbar}\mathcal{S}(t_{f},Q_{f},t_{i},Q_{i})}
with
\mathcal{S}(t_{f},Q_{f},t_{i},Q_{i})
the classical on-shell action. As well, the integral measure of the path integral contains many powers of
\hbar
(formally infinite), and it is not clear a priori how many of these factors will survive in the classical limit. Direct calculation shows that almost all of those powers of
\hbar
are canceled in the stationary phase integration, so that the leading order classical behavior of the path integral is:

\lim_{\hbar\to 0^{+}}\hbar^{\frac{d}{2}}e^{-\frac{i}{\hbar}\mathcal{S}(t_{f},Q_{f},t_{i},Q_{i})}U(t_{f},Q_{f},t_{i},Q_{i})=\left(\frac{-1}{2\pi i}\right)^{\frac{d}{2}}\sqrt{\text{det}\frac{\partial^{2}\mathcal{S}}{\partial Q_{f}\partial Q_{i}}}e^{-i\int_{t_{i}}^{t_{f}}H_{1}dt}.

(64)

The details deriving this are given in Appendix
B
.

From (
64
), it immediately follows that the classical on-shell action can be extracted from the logarithm of the matrix elements of the time evolution oeprator as:

i\mathcal{S}(t_{f},Q_{f},t_{i},Q_{i})=\lim_{\hbar\to 0^{+}}\hbar\ln U(t_{f},Q_{f},t_{i},Q_{i}).

(65)

In the case of scattering, the interaction picture is used, the initial and final times are taken to
\pm\infty
, and the time evolution operator becomes the S-matrix. The resulting interaction picture on-shell action we write as
\mathcal{S}(Q_{f},Q_{i})
(in this case the coordinates must be selected to have well-defined values in the
t_{f/i}\to\pm\infty
limits). Comparing to (
1
), we find the left half of (
6
):

\delta=\mathcal{S}(Q_{f},Q_{i})

(66)

which confirms the well-known result that the classical behavior of the optical eikonal phase is just the on-shell action.

The time-evolution operator, by virtue of being unitary, can be written in the form:

\hat{U}(t_{f},t_{i})=e^{\frac{i}{\hbar}\hat{\chi}(t_{f},t_{i})}

(67)

where
\hat{\chi}
is the Hermitian eikonal operator. Let
\chi_{qm}
be its inverse Weyl transform and let
\chi
, the classical eikonal generator (Magnusian) be:

\chi(t_{f},t_{i},Q,P)=\lim_{\hbar\to 0^{+}}\chi_{\text{qm}}(t_{f},t_{i},Q,P).

(68)

Consider a quantum system with initial wavefunction
|\psi_{i}\rangle
to undergo time evolution to some final state
|\psi_{f}\rangle
. Let
\hat{A}
be a Hermitian operator so that
\langle\hat{A}\rangle
is classically finite and let
A(Q,P)
be the classical limit of its Wigner function. Then, the final value of
\hat{A}
is given by:

\langle\hat{A}\rangle_{f}=\langle\psi_{f}|\hat{A}|\psi_{f}\rangle=\langle\psi_{i}|\hat{U}^{\dagger}\hat{A}\hat{U}|\psi_{i}\rangle=\langle\psi_{i}|e^{-\frac{i}{\hbar}\hat{\chi}}\hat{A}e^{\frac{i}{\hbar}\hat{\chi}}|\psi_{i}\rangle=\langle\psi_{i}|\left(e^{\frac{1}{i\hbar}[\hat{\chi},\,\text{\scriptsize\textbullet}\,]}\hat{A}\right)|\psi_{i}\rangle.

(69)

Using (
58
), we recover (
15
) in the classical limit. There is no subtlety of
\hbar
over
\hbar
cancellations in connecting (
69
) and (
15
) because the commutator
[\,\text{\scriptsize\textbullet}\,,\,\text{\scriptsize\textbullet}\,]
of any operators with classically finite expectation values is of
\mathcal{O}(\hbar)
, so
\frac{1}{\hbar}[\,\text{\scriptsize\textbullet}\,,\,\text{\scriptsize\textbullet}\,]
contains no inverse powers of
\hbar
.

From (
67
) and (
64
) we can explicitly relate the Magnusian and the on-shell action. Let
\hat{V}(\varepsilon)
be the 1-parameter family of unitary operators defined by
\hat{V}(\varepsilon)=e^{\frac{i}{\hbar}\varepsilon\hat{\chi}}
. This
\hat{V}
immediately satisfies (

replaced by
\varepsilon

replaced by
0

replaced by
-\hat{\chi}
, and
\hat{U}
replaced by
\hat{V}
. As well,
\hat{V}(1)=\hat{U}(t_{f},t_{i})
. Thus, from the path integral it is immediately true that exactly:

\displaystyle U(t_{f},Q_{f},t_{i},Q_{i})

\displaystyle=\int_{Q(0)=Q_{i}}^{Q(1)=Q_{f}}e^{\frac{i}{\hbar}\int_{0}^{1}\left(P_{a}\frac{dQ^{a}}{d\varepsilon}+\chi_{\text{qm}}(t_{f},t_{i},Q,P)\right)d\varepsilon}\mathcal{D}_{\hbar}Q\mathcal{D}_{\hbar}P.

(70)

We can now study the leading classical behavior of the above expression using the same techniques as previously. In particular, let
(q(\varepsilon),p(\varepsilon))
again be the path satisfying (

\mathcal{I}
again be given by (

). Then, in the classical limit, the same calculation that led to (

\lim_{\hbar\to 0^{+}}\hbar^{\frac{d}{2}}e^{-\frac{i}{\hbar}\mathcal{I}}U(t_{f},Q_{f},t_{i},Q_{i})=\left(\frac{-1}{2\pi i}\right)^{\frac{d}{2}}\sqrt{\text{det}\frac{\partial^{2}\mathcal{I}}{\partial Q_{f}\partial Q_{i}}}e^{i\int_{0}^{1}\chi_{1}d\varepsilon}.

(71)

In order for this and (

\mathcal{S}
and

\mathcal{S}(t_{f},Q_{f},t_{i},Q_{i})=\int_{0}^{1}\left(p_{a}\frac{dq^{a}}{d\varepsilon}+\chi(t_{f},t_{i},q,p)\right)d\varepsilon

(72)

One perspective on the origin of the complicated relationship between

(73)

In particular, the operator exponential when projected into phase space is naturally expressed in terms of the star product:

\mathcal{W}^{-1}(e^{\frac{i}{\hbar}\hat{\mathcal{W}}(\chi_{\text{qm}})})=1+\frac{i}{\hbar}\chi_{\text{qm}}-\frac{1}{2\hbar^{2}}\chi_{\text{qm}}\star\chi_{\text{qm}}-\frac{i}{6\hbar^{3}}\chi_{\text{qm}}\star\chi_{\text{qm}}\star\chi_{\text{qm}}+\cdots

4
Properties of the Magnusian

4.1
Interpretation

Although the Magnusian has been of increasingly popular interest in amplitudes literature in recent years, we can see from the derivation of (
50
) that in fact its existence has nothing to do with the context of scattering or the consideration of any particular theory. The Magnusian is a generic quantity of interest in classical mechanics that captures, through (
15
), the entire time evolution of the system. Equation (
48
) gives us perspective on what the Magnusian is actually doing. Given a generically time dependent true Hamiltonian (as is the standard case in the interaction picture), the resultant physical path through phase space tends to be very complicated. The Magnusian acts as a sort of resummed/time-averaged Hamiltonian so that the path it gives through phase space (when treated as an effective Hamiltonian with the same boundary conditions as the true path) is a simple exponential (
39
). To make this precise, notice that by comparing (
15
) to (
35
), which is valid for a time independent Hamiltonian
\mathcal{H}
and interval of time evolution
[\varepsilon_{i},\varepsilon_{f}]
, we can define the time-averaged Hamiltonian:

\bar{\mathcal{H}}_{T}(t,Q,P)=-\frac{1}{T}\chi(t+T,t,Q,P)

(75)

and if the system is evolved from initial conditions
(Q_{i},P_{i})
using this Hamiltonian
\bar{\mathcal{H}_{T}}(t,Q,P)
(rather than the physical Hamiltonian
\mathcal{H}(t,Q,P)
) from an initial time
t
, then at final time
t+T
it will have the physically correct final conditions
(Q_{f},P_{f})
. By virtue of the time integrals over the
[t,t+T]
interval which must be completed to compute
\chi
from the physical Hamiltonian
\mathcal{H}
(by following the Magnus formula (
24
)), the high-frequency behavior (compared to
\frac{1}{T}
) of the Hamiltonian is effectively integrated out. Using the Magnus series to express
\bar{\mathcal{H}}_{T}
in terms of
\mathcal{H}
gives that the Magnusian path evolved using
\bar{\mathcal{H}}_{T}
is a “near-identity transformation” of the true physical path evolved using
\mathcal{H}

nit
.

The fact that the time-evolution generated by
\bar{\mathcal{H}}_{T}
is a simple exponential in the space of canonical transformations becomes geometrically meaningful when we think about that space as a Lie group. A Lie group, viewed as a manifold and given a connection made from only the structure constants of the algebra, has geodesics (parameterized by affine parameter
\varepsilon
) which are precisely exponentials
e^{\varepsilon X}
with
X
an element of the Lie algebra. Thus, the Magnusian essentially computes the geodesic through the group of canonical transformations which connects the identity to the canonical transformation provided by physical time evolution. That geodesic path as a group element is
e^{\varepsilon\{\chi,\,\text{\scriptsize\textbullet}\,\}}
.

We can make the sense in which that path is properly geodesic more concrete by looking at a low dimensional example. On a generic phase space, even of only one coordinate and one momentum, the group of canonical transformations is infinite dimensional. But, if we consider only transformations preserving some natural structure, we can produce simple finite dimensional cases. The smallest nontrivial possible example is the affine group over the real numbers. That is, the group of transformations of the form
x^{\prime}=e^{\beta}x+\alpha
with parameters
\alpha,\beta
giving the amounts of translation and dilation respectively. We write
G(\alpha,\beta)
for a general such element of the group. We can identify a matrix representation for the group by defining the generator matrices
\hat{\tau}_{1}
and
\hat{\tau}_{2}
:

\hat{\tau}_{1}=\begin{pmatrix}0&-1\\
0&0\end{pmatrix},\hskip 8.61108pt\hat{\tau}_{2}=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&0\end{pmatrix}\ \ \implies\ \ [\hat{\tau}_{1},\hat{\tau}_{2}]=-\hat{\tau}_{1},\hskip 8.61108pte^{\alpha\hat{\tau}_{1}}e^{\beta\hat{\tau}_{2}}\begin{pmatrix}x\\
-1\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}e^{\beta}x+\alpha\\
-1\end{pmatrix}.

(76)

From a matrix representation of a Lie group it is always possible to construct a classical mechanical system that represents that group through functions on phase space that are bilinear in
(Q,P)
. In particular, consider a 4 dimensional phase space
(Q_{1},Q_{2},P_{1},P_{2})
and define:

\tau_{1}=P_{a}(\hat{\tau}_{1})^{a}{}_{b}Q^{b}=-P_{1}Q_{2},\hskip 8.61108pt\tau_{2}=P_{a}(\hat{\tau}_{2})^{a}{}_{b}Q^{b}=P_{1}Q_{1}\ \ \implies\ \ \{\tau_{1},\tau_{2}\}=-\tau_{1}.

(77)

We can give arbitrary dynamics on the group by considering a Hamiltonian that is a generic element of the Lie algebra at each time:

\mathcal{H}(t,Q,P)=u(t)\tau_{1}+v(t)\tau_{2}

(78)

for some functions
u(t)
and
v(t)
. The equations of motion produced by
\mathcal{H}
are easily exactly solved, and give that
(Q(t),P(t))
is related to
(Q(0),P(0))
by the action of the group element
G(\alpha(t),\beta(t))
with the physical group trajectory given by:

\alpha(t)=\frac{\beta(t)}{1-e^{-\beta(t)}}\int_{0}^{t}u(t^{\prime})e^{-\beta(t^{\prime})}dt^{\prime},\hskip 21.52771pt\beta(t)=\int_{0}^{t}v(t^{\prime})dt^{\prime}.

(79)

Solving the
\varepsilon
-equations of motion produced by
\chi
and requiring a match at
\varepsilon=1
to
t=T
gives that the Magnusian trajectory is:

\alpha_{\chi}(\varepsilon)=\varepsilon\alpha(T),\hskip 21.52771pt\beta_{\chi}(\varepsilon)=\varepsilon\beta(T).

(80)

Suppose that we have a system for which
u
and
v
are naturally oscillatory, which could arise (in the interaction picture) by coupling to some external field of a given frequency
\omega
. A visualization of the evolution of parameters
(\alpha,\beta)
in such a case is shown in figure

.

The next simplest example after the affine group is motion on
SU(2)
, a similar analysis and visualization of which are given in appendix
C
.

4.2
Symmetries

Let us understand the symmetries of the Magnusian. Consider a generic continuous canonical transformation. That is, some map from old
t,Q,P,\mathcal{H}
into new
t^{\prime},Q^{\prime},P^{\prime},\mathcal{H}^{\prime}
so that Hamilton’s equations are satisfied in the primed quantities if and only if they are satisfied in the unprimed quantities, and so that this map is smooth with respect to a parameter
\alpha
with
\alpha=0
giving the identity transformation. Define the functions
\delta t(t,Q,P)
and
\delta Q(t,Q,P)
by:

\delta t(t,Q,P)=\left.\frac{\partial t^{\prime}(\alpha,t,Q,P)}{\partial\alpha}\right|_{\alpha=0},\hskip 21.52771pt\delta Q(t,Q,P)=\left.\frac{\partial Q^{\prime}(\alpha,t,Q,P)}{\partial\alpha}\right|_{\alpha=0}.

(81)

In order for such a continuous transformation to be canonical (and so preserve the Poisson bracket), there must exist a phase space function
G(t,Q,P)
called the generator of the transformation so that for any fixed function
A(Q,P)
on phase space:

\left.\frac{dA(Q^{\prime}(t),P^{\prime}(t))}{d\alpha}\right|_{\alpha=0}=\{A,G\}.

(82)

Under such a canonical transformation, the new Hamiltonian
\mathcal{H}^{\prime}(\alpha,t^{\prime},Q^{\prime},P^{\prime})
will generally have a different functional form than the untransformed Hamiltonian. We write
\Delta\mathcal{H}
to represent the variation in the functional form of the Hamiltonian at the identity. By the chain rule, this is related to the total
\alpha
derivative (in which its arguments
Q^{\prime},P^{\prime}
are also varied) by:

\left.\frac{d\mathcal{H}^{\prime}(\alpha,t^{\prime},Q^{\prime}(t^{\prime}),P^{\prime}(t^{\prime}))}{d\alpha}\right|_{\alpha=0}=\Delta\mathcal{H}+\frac{\partial\mathcal{H}}{\partial t}\delta t+\{\mathcal{H},G\}.

(83)

By Noether’s theorem, the change in functional form of the Hamiltonian exactly gives the rate of nonconservation of the generator. That is:

\frac{dG}{dt}=\Delta\mathcal{H}

(84)

so that the transformation is a symmetry if and only if its generator is conserved.

Similarly to the Hamiltonian, the transformed on-shell action
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}(\alpha,t_{f}^{\prime},Q^{\prime}_{f},t_{i}^{\prime},Q^{\prime}_{i})
will generally have a different functional form than its untransformed version. We again write
\Delta\mathcal{S}
to represent the variation in that functional form at the identity, and by the chain rule and Hamilton-Jacobi conditions (
14
):

\left.\frac{d\mathcal{S}^{\prime}}{d\alpha}\right|_{\alpha=0}=\Delta\mathcal{S}+P_{f}\delta Q_{f}-P_{i}\delta Q_{i}-\mathcal{H}_{f}\delta t_{f}+\mathcal{H}_{i}\delta t_{i}.

(85)

By comparing this to (
11
), we immediately have that the changes in functional form of the action and the Hamiltonian are related by:

\Delta\mathcal{S}=-\int_{t_{i}}^{t_{f}}\Delta\mathcal{H}dt.

(86)

Due to Noether’s theorem, this ultimately simplifies to:

\Delta\mathcal{S}=G(t_{i},Q_{i},P_{i})-G(t_{f},Q_{f},P_{f}).

(87)

The functional form of the action varies precisely by (negative of) the total nonconservation of the transformation generator.

Following identical steps but instead beginning from the right hand side of (
48
), we find that the change in functional form of the Magnusian is related to that of the OSA by:

\Delta\mathcal{S}=\int_{0}^{1}\Delta\chi(t_{f},t_{i},q(\varepsilon),p(\varepsilon))d\varepsilon.

(88)

Using (
39
), the integration can be completed to give:

\Delta\mathcal{S}=\frac{e^{\{\chi,\,\text{\scriptsize\textbullet}\,\}}-1}{\{\chi,\,\text{\scriptsize\textbullet}\,\}}\Delta\chi(t_{f},t_{i},Q_{i},P_{i}).

(89)

Manifestly, a symmetry of the action (a transformation for which the left side is 0) is automatically a symmetry of the Magnusian (for which the right is zero), and vice versa. That is, the Magnusian and the action have precisely the same symmetries.

By solving for the functional change of the Magnusian and using (
87
), we can rewrite (
89
) as:

\Delta\chi(t_{f},t_{i},Q,P)=\{G(t_{f},Q,P),\chi\}-\frac{\{\chi,\,\text{\scriptsize\textbullet}\,\}}{e^{\{\chi,\,\text{\scriptsize\textbullet}\,\}}-1}(G(t_{f},Q,P)-G(t_{i},Q,P)).

(90)

For most transformations of physical interest the generator has no explicit time dependence, in which case only the first term on the right hand side is nonvanishing and so the change in the Magnusian under such a transformation is given simply in terms of its Poisson bracket with the generator of that transformation.

For scattering applications, a particularly important class of transformations is the class of field redefinitions which become the identity at infinity. Suppose we perform such a field redefinition. Because it asymptotes to the identity transformation, the generator
G
of the transformation asymptotes to 0. Then, by (
87
), the on-shell action is unsurprisingly unchanged by the transformation. It immediately follows from (
89
) that the Magnusian is also unchanged by the transformation. Therefore, in the scattering case, the Magnusian is field redefinition invariant.

4.3
Perturbative Relations

Let us now see how (
48
) can be used to compute the Magnusian from the action or vice versa in perturbation theory. For the rest of this section, we assume that we have already gone to the interaction picture and we hold
t_{f},t_{i}
fixed (in the scattering context, they are taken to
\pm\infty
). Because
t_{f},t_{i}
are held fixed, we will not write them explicitly as arguments of functions which in principle depend on them. Because the Hamiltonian is
\mathcal{O}(\lambda)
, the velocity
\dot{Q}
and consequently the Lagrangian and then the action are also of
\mathcal{O}(\lambda)
. By the Hamilton-Jacobi conditions (
14
), the OSA is a natural function of the boundary conditions
Q_{f}
,
Q_{i}
. To see how this manifests in perturbation theory, we give
Q_{f}
and
P_{f}
perturbative expansions as:

Q_{f}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\lambda^{n}Q_{n},\hskip 21.52771ptP_{f}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\lambda^{n}P_{n}

(91)

where
Q_{0}=Q_{i}
and
P_{0}=P_{i}
. Expanding the Hamilton-Jacobi differential (
13
) (with
dt_{f}=dt_{i}=0
) in powers of
\lambda
gives:

d\mathcal{S}=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\lambda^{n}\sum_{k=0}^{n}P_{(n-k)}dQ_{k}.

(92)

Consequently,
\mathcal{S}
is naturally written as a series in the form:

\mathcal{S}(Q_{f},Q_{i})=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\lambda^{n}\mathcal{S}_{n}(Q_{n},Q_{n-1},...,Q_{1},Q_{0})

(93)

where the perturbation
\mathcal{S}_{n}
satisfy the perturbative Hamilton-Jacobi conditions:

P_{(n-k)}=\frac{\partial\mathcal{S}_{n}}{\partial Q_{k}}.

(94)

The
k=0
condition provides new information (the value of the perturbation
P_{n}
), while the
k>0
conditions determine derivatives of
\mathcal{S}_{n}
in terms of already known derivatives of
\mathcal{S}_{n-k}
.

The Magnusian also is of
\mathcal{O}(\lambda)
and so we give it a perturbative expansion in
\lambda
as:

\chi(Q,P)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\lambda^{n}\chi_{n}(Q,P).

(95)

We can now expand (
50
) directly in powers of
\lambda
. At
\mathcal{O}(\lambda)
, we find:

\mathcal{S}_{1}(Q_{1},Q_{i})=P_{i}Q_{1}+\chi_{1}(Q_{i},P_{i})

(96)

and so
\mathcal{S}_{1}
is indeed an ordinary Legendre transform of
\chi_{1}
, interchanging
P_{i}
and
Q_{1}
.

At
\mathcal{O}(\lambda^{2})
, we find:

\mathcal{S}_{2}(Q_{2},Q_{1},Q_{i})=\chi_{2}(Q_{i},P_{i})+P_{i}Q_{2}+\frac{1}{2}P_{1}Q_{1}.

(97)

This is no longer a simple Legendre transformation. However, given
\mathcal{S}_{1}
and
\mathcal{S}_{2}
, all of
Q_{1},P_{1},Q_{2}
can written in terms of
Q_{i},P_{i}
using the Hamilton-Jacobi conditions (
94
), allowing the determination of
\chi_{2}
. Expanding (
50
) to
\mathcal{O}(\lambda^{3})
gives:

\mathcal{S}_{3}=\chi_{3}+P_{i}Q_{3}+\frac{1}{2}P_{2}Q_{1}+\frac{1}{2}P_{1}Q_{2}+\frac{1}{12}P_{1}\{\chi_{1},Q_{1}\}-\frac{1}{12}Q_{1}\{\chi_{1},P_{1}\}.

(98)

Given
\mathcal{S}_{1},\mathcal{S}_{2},\mathcal{S}_{3}
, every piece in this equation aside from
\chi_{3}
can be computed in terms of
Q_{i},P_{i}
using (
94
), allowing the determination of
\chi_{3}
. Plainly based on what terms are equated at each order in
\lambda
in (
50
), this pattern continues at higher orders, so that the Magnusian at
\mathcal{O}(\lambda^{n})
can be determined from the
\mathcal{S}_{k}
for
k\leq n
. The converse is also true. Given the Magnusian up through order
\mathcal{O}(\lambda^{n})
, the on-shell action up to that same order can be computed by matching at each order in (
50
) and integrating the conditions (
94
) to the correct variable dependencies.

Integrable Scattering

In the case of integrable scattering, the Magnusian
\chi

This simple relationship is physically relevant, because the two-body problem in the conservative sector of scalar PM or PN scattering is integrable, as is scalar probe motion in a Kerr background. However, it breaks down in general for the two-body problem due to loss of integrability when spin (outside of the probe limit

Let us study how

arises for integrable scattering by looking at general motion under a spherical potential in detail. This example includes the case of conservative scattering in the center of momentum frame of two spinless bodies in the PM or PN expansions. The Hamiltonian

where

and

are always positive as evaluated here, we define them to be even functions when evaluated at general arguments. The dynamics are integrable and give the on-shell action:

\mathcal{S}(t_{f},r_{f},\varphi_{f},t_{i},r_{i},\varphi_{i})=R(E,L,r_{f},r_{i})+L(\varphi_{f}-\varphi_{i})-E(t_{f}-t_{i})

\displaystyle t_{f}-t_{i}

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial R}{\partial E},

\displaystyle p_{rf}

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial R}{\partial r_{f}},

\displaystyle p_{\varphi f}

\displaystyle=L,

\displaystyle E

\displaystyle=\mathcal{H}\left(r_{f},\sqrt{\left(\frac{\partial R}{\partial r_{f}}\right)^{2}+\frac{L^{2}}{r^{2}_{f}}}\right)

\displaystyle\varphi_{f}-\varphi_{i}

\displaystyle=-\frac{\partial R}{\partial L},

\displaystyle p_{ri}

\displaystyle=-\frac{\partial R}{\partial r_{i}},

\displaystyle p_{\varphi i}

\displaystyle=L,

\displaystyle=\mathcal{H}\left(r_{i},\sqrt{\left(\frac{\partial R}{\partial r_{i}}\right)^{2}+\frac{L^{2}}{r^{2}_{i}}}\right).

6
Conclusion

In this article we have shown that the “eikonal” refers to two fundamentally different quantities in present literature, one which is the logarithm of an S-matrix element and the other which is a matrix element of the logarithm of the S-matrix. In the classical limit, these become the on-shell action
\mathcal{S}
(eikonal phase) and the Magnusian
\chi
(eikonal generator), respectively. We showed that in the classical limit they are precisely related by:

\mathcal{S}(t_{f},Q_{f},t_{i},Q_{i})=\int_{0}^{1}\left(p\frac{dq}{d\varepsilon}+\chi(t_{f},t_{i},q,p)\right)d\varepsilon.

(134)

We hope to have shed light on the subtle difference between the two. It is specifically the Magnusian
\chi
which is of central usefulness in calculating observables efficiently, because it is a single scalar function which through (
15
) can be used to compute the impulse in any observable. While the matrix elements of the quantum S-matrix have a generally challenging classical limit, requiring care regarding
\hbar
-over-
\hbar
pieces to ensure cancellation of superclassical effects, the eikonal operator
\hat{\chi}
has a well-defined classical limit. This allows us to bring the powerful idea of the S-matrix from quantum mechanics into classical physics in the form of the classical S-matrix
e^{\{\chi,\,\text{\scriptsize\textbullet}\,\}}
.

In sec.
5.3
we showed that the radial action
I
and the Magnusian
\chi
coincide for planar scattering and argued that this equality holds in the generic integrable scattering case. This causes the on-shell action
\mathcal{S}
and Magnusian
\chi
to be related by a simple Legendre transform. Outside of the integrable case, such as when spin is included, that simple Legendre relationship breaks down. But, even for generic scattering, there remain relations between the two at the level of Feynman diagrams. The Magnusian can be computed using Feynman-like tree diagrams in a worldline description of massive particles using causal propagators
Kim:2024svw
, as opposed to the on-shell action which is naturally computed using Feynman diagrams through the Dyson series with Feynman propagators. The diagrams computing the two are related in that the full integrand only differs by different weightings of the propagator
i0^{+}
prescriptions, which can be stated as a sum rule for the graph function
\omega(\tau)
(which computes the weights associated to the Feynman-like graphs for the Magnusian) known as the Feynman reduction property; see eq. (4.20) of Ref.
Kim:2024svw
. This mechanism allows the use of unitarity based methods to construct integrands for the Magnusian, at least when a worldline description is adopted
Haddad:2025cmw
. Understanding the implications of this diagrammatic relationship between the on-shell action and the Magnusian we leave as a direction for future work.

While interest in the Magnusian has recently been motivated by scattering amplitudes applications, its existence is entirely generic to classical mechanics. In general, it acts as a resummed/time-averaged Hamiltonian over the interval of consideration, giving the correct final state in phase space derived from the true Hamiltonian, but through a time-independent flow that is a near-identity transformation of the physical trajectory. Because the Magnusian exists even outside of the context of scattering, it is a promising future direction to consider its application to bound orbits, where it would naturally become a function of orbital parameters and its derivatives would encode conjugate orbital impulses, relating the binding energy and orbital period, angular momentum and orbital precession, etc.

Classical Limit of Wigner Functions

The Weyl transform provides a clear prescription for how to write operators in a canonical ordering of
\hat{Q}
and
\hat{P}
. Define the symmetrized operator product
\text{Sym}(\hat{\mathcal{O}}_{1},...,\hat{\mathcal{O}}_{n})
as
\frac{1}{n!}
times the sum over all
n!
ways to order these operators in a product. If
G(Q,P)
is an analytic function, then series expanding it in
Q,P
about a point

allows the
Q,P

55
) to be performed. Afterward, the

integrals can be performed, and doing so gives:

\hat{G}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}m=0\\
n=0\end{subarray}}^{\infty,\infty}\frac{1}{m!n!}\frac{\partial^{m+n}G(q,p)}{\partial q^{m}\partial p^{n}}\text{Sym}(\hat{Q}-q,(m\text{ copies}),\hat{P}-p,(n\text{ copies})).

(135)

This is just the Taylor series for the function
G(q,p)
formally evaluated at
(\hat{Q},\hat{P})
where the prescription for how to resolve any ordering ambiguities is to fully symmetrize the operator products.

The classical limit of a quantum system is the limit in which the wavefunction for the system is sufficiently localized in both coordinate space and momentum space relative to the relevant experimental resolution to be treated as being described solely by its expected coordinates
Q_{\text{cl}}=\langle\hat{Q}\rangle
and expected momenta
P_{\text{cl}}=\langle\hat{P}\rangle
. This is under the assumption we have, as previously stated, chosen our coordinates and momenta such that their expectations are classically finite.

Let
M_{mn}
be the central moments of the wavefunction of our system defined as:

M_{mn}=\langle\psi|\text{Sym}(\hat{Q}-Q_{\text{cl}},(m\text{ copies}),\hat{P}-P_{\text{cl}},(n\text{ copies}))|\psi\rangle.

(136)

In the classical limit all of these moments effectively are squeezed to 0. The finite value of
\hbar
prevents the wavefunction from being simultaneously localized in position and momentum space, so
\hbar\to 0^{+}
is necessary but not sufficient for the classical limit. After taking
\hbar\to 0^{+}
, we must also consider a scaling of the wavefunction so that it properly localizes.

Define the dilation generator
\hat{D}
and the dilated wavefunction
|\psi_{s}\rangle
by:

\hat{D}=\frac{(\hat{Q}-Q_{\text{cl}})(\hat{P}-P_{\text{cl}})+(\hat{P}-P_{\text{cl}})(\hat{Q}-Q_{\text{cl}})}{2},\hskip 21.52771pt|\psi_{s}\rangle=e^{-\frac{i}{\hbar}s\hat{D}}|\psi\rangle.

(137)

Let
M_{mn}(s)
be the moments computed using
|\psi_{s}\rangle
as the wavefunction. Then:

M_{mn}(s)=e^{s(m-n)}M_{mn}(0).

(138)

The position space spread of the wavefunction is
M_{20}(s)
, which we want to send to 0 to localize the wavefunction in the classical limit. This requires we send
s\to-\infty
.

Localizing the wavefunction in position space will antilocalize it in momentum space unless we first take
\hbar\to 0^{+}
. To implement this correctly we need to keep the expected momentum
P_{\text{cl}}
of the wavefunction fixed. We can strip off the expected momentum and leave behind a wavefunction
|\phi\rangle
of expected momentum 0 by defining:

|\phi\rangle=e^{-\frac{i}{\hbar}P_{\text{cl}}\hat{Q}}|\psi\rangle\implies\langle\phi|\hat{P}|\phi\rangle=0.

(139)

The momentum operator acting on
|\phi\rangle
contains a factor of
\hbar
in its definition and so it is useful to define the
\hbar
-independent operator
\hat{K}=\frac{\hat{P}}{\hbar}
. Let
|\phi_{s}\rangle
be the dilated
|\phi\rangle
wavefunction and let
N_{mn}
be the central moments of
|\phi\rangle
using the now centered and
\hbar
-independent momentum operator
\hat{K}
:

N_{mn}=\langle\phi|\text{Sym}(\hat{Q}\!-\!Q_{\text{cl}},(m\text{ copies}),\hat{K},(n\text{ copies}))|\phi\rangle.

(140)

It is these
N_{mn}
moments that stay fixed in the classical limit. Then, the expectation of (
135
) becomes:

\langle\psi_{s}|\hat{G}|\psi_{s}\rangle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}m=0\\
n=0\end{subarray}}^{\infty,\infty}\frac{\hbar^{n}e^{s(m-n)}}{m!n!}\frac{\partial^{m+n}G(Q_{\text{cl}},P_{\text{cl}})}{\partial Q^{m}_{\text{cl}}\partial P^{n}_{\text{cl}}}N_{mn}

(141)

We are now in a position to take
\hbar\to 0^{+}
and then
s\to-\infty
. Doing so and recalling the
\hbar
expansion of the function
G
gives:

G_{\text{cl}}(Q_{\text{cl}},P_{\text{cl}})=\lim_{s\to-\infty}\lim_{\hbar\to 0^{+}}\langle\psi_{s}|\hat{G}|\psi_{s}\rangle=G_{0}(Q_{\text{cl}},P_{\text{cl}})

(142)

which is (
57
). The classical limit completely determines the
\hbar
independent piece of the Wigner function of each operator (with a classically finite expectation value) to be the corresponding classical observable. But, the classical limit determines nothing about the
\hbar
dependent pieces of an operator’s Wigner function. This is the essence of the statement that there is no unique canonical quantization scheme. Different choices of scheme amount to different selections of
\hbar
corrections. While no particular scheme is fixed by the classical limit alone, there is often more data about the quantum theory which is known than just the classical limit; symmetries of a quantum theory may constrain
\hbar
dependent terms of Wigner functions.

(144)

Let (
Q_{\text{cl}}(t),P_{\text{cl}}(t)
) be the classical trajectory which makes the classical action stationary. Define
\delta Q(t)
and
\delta P(t)
by:

Q(t)=Q_{\text{cl}}(t)+\sqrt{\hbar}\delta Q(t),\qquad P(t)=P_{\text{cl}}(t)+\sqrt{\hbar}\delta P(t).

(145)

Then, expanding
\mathcal{S}_{H}
in
\hbar
yields:

\mathcal{S}_{H}[Q,P]=\mathcal{S}(t_{f},Q_{f},t_{i},Q_{i})+\hbar\widetilde{\mathcal{S}}[\delta Q,\delta P]+\hbar\int_{t_{i}}^{t_{f}}H_{1}(t,Q_{\text{cl}}(t),P_{\text{cl}}(t))dt+\mathcal{O}(\hbar^{\frac{3}{2}})

(146)

where we have defined
\widetilde{\mathcal{S}}
and
\widetilde{H}
by:

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathcal{S}}=\int_{t_{0}}^{t_{f}}(\delta P_{a}\dot{\delta Q}^{a}-\widetilde{H}(t,\delta Q,\delta P))dt

(147)

\displaystyle\widetilde{H}=\frac{1}{2}\frac{\partial^{2}\mathcal{H}_{\text{cl}}(t,Q_{\text{cl}},P_{\text{cl}})}{\partial Q^{a}_{\text{cl}}\partial Q^{b}_{\text{cl}}}\delta Q^{a}\delta Q^{b}+\frac{\partial^{2}\mathcal{H}_{\text{cl}}(t,Q_{\text{cl}},P_{\text{cl}})}{\partial Q^{a}_{\text{cl}}\partial P_{b}^{\text{cl}}}\delta Q^{a}\delta P_{b}+\frac{1}{2}\frac{\partial^{2}\mathcal{H}_{\text{cl}}(t,Q_{\text{cl}},P_{\text{cl}})}{\partial P^{\text{cl}}_{a}\partial P^{\text{cl}}_{b}}\delta P_{a}\delta P_{b}.

(148)

Returning these leading expansions to (
63
) gives:

\displaystyle U=\frac{1}{\hbar^{\frac{d}{2}}}e^{\frac{i}{\hbar}\mathcal{S}-i\int_{t_{i}}^{t_{f}}H_{1}dt}\int_{\delta Q(t_{i})=0}^{\delta Q(t_{f})=0}e^{i\widetilde{\mathcal{S}}+\mathcal{O}(\sqrt{\hbar})}\mathcal{D}_{1}\delta Q\mathcal{D}_{1}\delta P

(149)

where
\mathcal{D}_{1}\delta Q\mathcal{D}_{1}\delta P
indicates we have taken the path integral measure of (
62
) but in terms of the
(\delta Q,\delta P)
variables and with
\hbar
replaced by 1.
Now that
\hbar
s are fully under control, we can identify
\alpha=\frac{d}{2}
, so that:

Re^{i\int_{t_{i}}^{t_{f}}H_{1}dt}=\int_{\delta Q(t_{i})=0}^{\delta Q(t_{f})=0}e^{i\widetilde{\mathcal{S}}}\mathcal{D}_{1}\delta Q\mathcal{D}_{1}\delta P

(150)

The result is now a path integral for an equivalent harmonic oscillator system. In particular, define the classical system with Hamiltonian
\widetilde{H}
and canonical coordinates
\delta Q
and
\delta P
. Then, define a quantum system with Hamiltonian operator so that its Wigner function is
\widetilde{H}
and in that quantization set
\hbar=1
. The right hand side of (
150
) is the path integral connecting
\delta Q(t_{i})=0
and
\delta Q(t_{f})=0
for that system. We can thus evaluate the right hand side here using standard results for the harmonic oscillator. Let
\widetilde{U}(t_{f},\delta Q_{f},t_{i},\delta Q_{i})
be the matrix elements of the time evolution operator of this system. For any Hamiltonian which is a quadratic polynomial of the coordinates and momenta, even with time dependent coefficients, the matrix elements of the time evolution operator can be explicitly computed using standard quantum mechanics to be exactly:

\widetilde{U}(t_{f},\delta Q_{f},t_{i},\delta Q_{i})=\left(\frac{-1}{2\pi i}\right)^{\frac{d}{2}}\sqrt{\text{det}\frac{\partial^{2}\widetilde{\mathcal{S}}}{\partial\delta Q_{f}\partial\delta Q_{i}}}e^{i\widetilde{\mathcal{S}}}

(151)

where
\widetilde{\mathcal{S}}
is the classical on-shell action of that system. We are interested in this at
\delta Q_{i}=\delta Q_{f}=0
. Under these boundary conditions,
\widetilde{\mathcal{S}}=0
and the matrix appearing in the determinant is:

\frac{\partial^{2}\widetilde{\mathcal{S}}}{\partial\delta Q_{f}^{a}\partial\delta Q_{i}^{a}}=\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{\partial\delta P_{fa}}{\partial\delta Q^{b}_{i}}-\frac{\partial\delta P_{ib}}{\partial\delta Q^{a}_{f}}\right).

(152)

By returning to (
145
) and considering varying the boundary conditions of the path, it immediately follows (using the Hamilton-Jacobi conditions) that:

\frac{\partial\delta P_{fa}}{\partial\delta Q^{b}_{i}}=\frac{\partial P_{fa}}{\partial Q_{i}^{b}}=\frac{\partial^{2}\mathcal{S}}{\partial Q_{f}^{a}Q_{i}^{b}},\qquad\frac{\partial\delta P_{ib}}{\partial\delta Q^{b}_{f}}=\frac{\partial P_{ib}}{\partial Q_{f}^{a}}=-\frac{\partial^{2}\mathcal{S}}{\partial Q_{f}^{a}Q_{i}^{b}}

(153)

and thus we ultimately have that the proper statement of the leading classical behavior of the matrix elements of the time evolution operator of a generic quantum system is (
64
).

Appendix C
Example: Rabi Oscillations

Figure 2:
Example of group trajectories for
SU(2)
transformations due to evolution using the physical Hamiltonian (shown in solid orange) and Magnusian (shown in dashed blue). This is computed for the case of
\Omega=\sqrt{\pi/2}
and
\omega=1
.

Another good example for visualizing the Magnusian path is given by motion on
SU(2)
. Consider a complex spinor
|\psi(t)\rangle
. It can be given arbitrary motion through the Bloch sphere via the Euler-Lagrange equations from a Lagrangian of the form:

\mathcal{L}=i\langle\psi|\dot{\psi}\rangle-\langle\psi|\hat{H}(t)|\psi\rangle

(154)

where the Hamiltonian operator
\hat{H}(t)
is an arbitrary element of the Lie Algebra
\mathfrak{su}(2)
:

\hat{H}(t)=H^{a}(t)\frac{\sigma_{a}}{2}.

(155)

A case of particular physical interest is resonant Rabi flopping, in which we take
H^{a}(t)
, for some constants
\omega,\Omega
, to be given by:

H^{a}(t)=(\Omega\cos\omega t,\Omega\sin\omega t,\omega)^{a}.

(156)

The resulting equations of motion are easily exactly solved, and at all times the state
|\psi(t)\rangle
is related to the initial conditions by an element
\hat{U}(t)
of
SU(2)
:

|\psi(t)\rangle=\hat{U}(t)|\psi(0)\rangle.

(157)

A generic
SU(2)
group element is parameterized by 3 coordinates
x^{a}=(x,y,z)^{a}
so that:

\hat{U}=e^{\frac{i}{2}x^{a}\sigma_{a}}.

(158)

The solution of the equations of motion thus defines a trajectory
x^{a}(t)
through the group. Let
\theta(t)
be given by:

\theta(t)=\sqrt{x^{2}(t)+y^{2}(t)+z^{2}(t)}

(159)

The exact group trajectory is:

x(t)=-\frac{\theta\sin\frac{\Omega t}{2}\cos\frac{\omega t}{2}}{\sin\frac{\theta}{2}},\hskip 21.52771pty(t)=-\frac{\theta\sin\frac{\Omega t}{2}\sin\frac{\omega t}{2}}{\sin\frac{\theta}{2}},\hskip 21.52771ptz(t)=-\frac{\theta\cos\frac{\Omega t}{2}\sin\frac{\omega t}{2}}{\sin\frac{\theta}{2}}.

(160)

SU(2)
is most properly visualized as the 3-sphere, but can be made easier to imagine using cylindrical-like-coordinates
(r(t),\varphi(t),z(t))
with
x=|r|\cos\varphi
and
y=|r|\sin\varphi
. In these coordinates,
\varphi=\frac{\omega t}{2}
and the sign of
r
is chosen so that
r(t)
remains continuous and differentiable. The trajectories of evolution are shown in figure
2
, where we can see the simple sense in which the Magnusian path is geodesic.
