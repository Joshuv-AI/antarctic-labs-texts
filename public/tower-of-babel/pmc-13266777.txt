Association between blood urea nitrogen-to-creatinine ratio trajectories and mortality in patients with severe pneumonia requiring invasive mechanical ventilation: a Medical Information Mart for Intensive Care IV (MIMIC-IV) database analysis
Authors: Shaopeng Zheng, Jiajia Chen, Zekai Huang, Zixun Huang, Qiangzhou Xu, Limin Ma
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundOnce pneumonia patients progress to requiring invasive mechanical ventilation (IMV), mortality in the intensive care unit (ICU) increases substantially. The blood urea nitrogen-to-creatinine ratio (BUNCR) is a potential biomarker of catabolic stress in critically ill patients, yet its dynamic prognostic value in ICU-admitted pneumonia patients remains unclear. This study employed group-based trajectory modeling (GBTM) to explore the association between early dynamic changes in BUNCR and mortality.MethodsThis retrospective cohort study utilized the Medical Information Mart for Intensive Care IV version 3.1 (MIMIC-IV v3.1). We included 3,609 adults (≥18 years) with pneumonia who initiated IMV within 24 hours of their first ICU admission and had ≥3 BUNCR measurements within 5 days. GBTM identified distinct BUNCR trajectories. Adjusted mortality hazard ratios (HRs) were calculated using Cox proportional hazards regression and multivariate logistic regression models. Finally, Kaplan-Meier survival analysis and subgroup analyses were performed.ResultsThe study identified three distinct BUNCR trajectories: Group 1 (medium, gradually increasing), Group 2 (lowest, slight initial decrease), and Group 3 (highest, rapid early increase). Overall, 14-day and 28-day mortality rates were 15.99% and 23.14%, respectively. Group 3 exhibited the highest mortality (25.95% at 14 days; 36.76% at 28 days). Compared to Group 2, Group 3 had a significantly increased mortality risk (14-day adjusted HR =1.93, 95% confidence interval: 1.40–2.65, P<0.001; 28-day adjusted HR 1.76, 95% confidence interval 1.35–2.31, P<0.001). Kaplan-Meier analysis confirmed that Group 3 had the lowest survival probability (P<0.001). The associations remained consistent across sex, age, and glycemic subgroups (P for interaction >0.05).ConclusionsDistinct BUNCR trajectories may serve as independent prognostic indicators for 14-day and 28-day all-cause mortality in critically ill pneumonia patients requiring IMV. These findings could help identify high-risk patients who require closer clinical monitoring, while the formal clinical utility of BUNCR trajectory assessment requires further validation in prospective studies.

Highlight box

Key findings

• Using group-based trajectory modeling (GBTM), we identified three distinct early blood urea nitrogen-to-creatinine ratio (BUNCR) trajectory patterns over intensive care unit (ICU) days 1–5 in pneumonia patients requiring invasive mechanical ventilation. Patients in the highest/rapidly increasing BUNCR trajectory had the greatest 14-day and 28-day in-hospital mortality, and this association remained significant after multivariable adjustment. The trajectory-mortality associations were consistent across sex, age, and glycemic subgroups (no significant interactions).

What is known and what is new?

• The BUNCR is associated with prognosis in critical illness, but single-time measurements may not capture early dynamic metabolic stress in ventilated pneumonia.

• This study characterizes early BUNCR trajectories in a large Medical Information Mart for Intensive Care IV cohort and demonstrates that trajectory-based phenotypes provide independent short-term mortality risk stratification beyond conventional severity scores and comorbidities.

What is the implication, and what should change now?

• Serial BUNCR monitoring during the first 5 ICU days, with attention to trajectory patterns, may help clinicians identify high-risk ventilated pneumonia patients earlier and guide intensified surveillance and timely optimization of supportive care. Future multicenter prospective studies should validate these trajectories and define actionable thresholds for clinical implementation.

Introduction

Pneumonia, as a common respiratory disease, has long posed a significant challenge to global public health (1). According to relevant statistics, hospitalization costs related to pneumonia amount to billions of dollars annually (2). In the intensive care unit (ICU), invasive mechanical ventilation (IMV) serves as a crucial cornerstone of life support for patients with severe pneumonia. These patients face an extremely high risk of short-term mortality, with the 14-day and 28-day time windows being critical for assessing the efficacy of early interventions and prognosis (3). The early and accurate identification of biomarkers and their dynamic patterns in patients with severe pneumonia is essential for evaluating disease severity, optimizing treatment strategies, and improving patient survival rates (4-7).

Blood urea nitrogen (BUN) and creatinine (Cre) are key indicators reflecting renal function and metabolic status (8). An elevated blood urea nitrogen-to-creatinine ratio (BUNCR) often suggests prerenal factors, such as effective circulating volume depletion, reduced cardiac output, or a hypercatabolic state (9). In critically ill patients, the urea-to-creatinine ratio (UCR) has emerged as a novel biomarker for predicting mortality risk, and its dynamic changes may reflect the patient’s metabolic stress and pathological progression (10,11). A retrospective analysis of critically ill patients with atrial fibrillation found that the BUNCR ratio at admission was an independent predictor of 28-day and 365-day mortality, exhibiting a U-shaped nonlinear relationship with mortality (12). In patients with non-traumatic intracranial hemorrhage and acute pancreatitis, BUNCR also demonstrated significant predictive value for 28-day mortality. In patients with severe pneumonia requiring IMV, systemic inflammatory response and inadequate organ perfusion are commonly observed (12).

Therefore, exploring the early trajectory of the BUNCR ratio in IMV pneumonia patients and evaluating its predictive value for short-term mortality hold profound clinical significance (13). Dynamic trajectory analysis can overcome the limitations of single-point measurements by untangling the temporal patterns of an indicator, thereby enabling more precise identification of high-risk patients (13,14). Consequently, this study conducted a retrospective cohort analysis based on the Medical Information Mart for Intensive Care IV (MIMIC-IV) database. Leveraging the database’s rich clinical information and serial laboratory data, we systematically investigated the characteristics of BUNCR trajectories in ICU pneumonia patients. The study aimed to explore the correlation between BUNCR trajectories and in-hospital mortality at 14 and 28 days. This approach can reveal potential patterns in the progression of IMV pneumonia, providing a more dynamic and precise indicator system for clinical prognosis assessment (15). We present this article in accordance with the STROBE reporting checklist (available at https://jtd.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/jtd-2026-1-0396/rc) (16).

Methods

Data source and study design

This retrospective cohort study utilized data from the MIMIC-IV v3.1 database, which contains anonymized clinical records for patients admitted to the ICU at Beth Israel Deaconess Medical Center in the United States between 2008 and 2019. The database provides multidimensional information, including demographics, vital signs, laboratory results, medication data, and comorbidities (17). Its use was approved by the Institutional Review Boards of the Massachusetts Institute of Technology and Beth Israel Deaconess Medical Center. The principal investigator, Zixun Huang, completed the National Institutes of Health-recognized data use training program (certification number 74230232) and obtained authorization for data access. As the study involved a secondary analysis of publicly available, anonymized data, it was exempt from further ethical review and informed consent. Data extraction was conducted using PostgreSQL. This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments.

The study cohort was derived from the MIMIC-IV v3.1 database and included patients aged ≥18 years with a diagnosis of pneumonia during their first ICU admission. Pneumonia were identified based on ICD-9/10 codes (code list: 0382, 0412, 4801, 481, 4820, 4821, 48241, 48242, 48283, 4829, 4846, 485, 486, 4870, 5070, 5168, 99732, B961, J1282, J1289, J13, J14, J150, J151, J15211, J15212, J155, J156, J158, J159, J181, J188, J189, J690, J851). A total of 94,458 patients admitted to the ICU were initially included in this study. The final analytical cohort was determined based on the following exclusion criteria: (I) age <18 years; (II) non-first ICU admission; (III) ICU length of stay <24 hours; (IV) not subjected to IMV within 24 hours of ICU admission; (V) not diagnosed with pneumonia; (VI) fewer than 3 recorded measurements of BUNCR within 5 days of ICU admission. Following this screening process, a total of 3,609 patients were ultimately included for analysis (Figure 1).

Exposure and outcome

The primary exposure in this study was the trajectory of the BUNCR. The primary clinical endpoint was in-hospital mortality at 14 and 28 days following ICU admission. In-hospital mortality was defined as death from any cause occurring within 14 or 28 days after admission to the ICU. Follow-up continued until either patient death or the end of the study period, whichever occurred first.

Construction of longitudinal trajectories for serum urea nitrogen and Cre

Serum urea nitrogen and Cre were recorded daily on ICU days 1–5. For each calendar day, all values obtained within that 24-hour window were averaged to yield a single daily mean for each biomarker.

Covariate collection

Demographics comprised age (years), sex (male/female) and weight (kg); disease-severity metrics comprised sequential organ failure assessment (SOFA) score, Oxford Acute Severity of Illness Score (OASIS) and Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) score; laboratory/physiologic variables comprised hemoglobin (g/dL), platelet count (×103/µL), red blood cell (RBC) count (×106/µL), white blood cell (WBC) count (×103/µL), anion gap (mmol/L), blood glucose (mg/dL), serum sodium (mmol/L), serum potassium (mmol/L), arterial pH, peripheral oxygen saturation (SpO2) (%), serum Cre (mg/dL), blood lactate (mmol/L), 24-hour fluid balance (mL) and ventilation hour (h); complications (ICD-9/ICD-10-identified) comprised hypertension, cirrhosis, chronic kidney disease (CKD), cancer, diabetes mellitus, heart failure, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), and sepsis (all coded as no/yes); treatments comprised sedatives/analgesics, glucocorticoids, antihypertensives, or vasopressors (all coded as no/yes).

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using R software (version 4.5.1). Statistical significance was defined as a two-tailed P value less than 0.05. Categorical variables are presented as frequencies and percentages, while continuous variables are summarized as mean ± standard deviation or median (interquartile range) depending on the data distribution. Group comparisons were performed using the Chi-squared test for categorical variables, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) for normally distributed continuous variables, and the Kruskal-Wallis H test for non-normally distributed continuous variables. For missing data, variables with a missing rate >20% were excluded. Missing values in the remaining variables were handled using multiple imputation via the mice package. Details of the missing data are provided in Figure S1.

Group-based trajectory modeling (GBTM) is a method used to analyze individual behavior changes during a specific period of time (18). To select the optimal number of groups, models containing 1 to 5 groups were built. According to the Bayesian information criterion (BIC) and Akaike information criterion (AIC), lower values indicate a better model fit. An average posterior probability (AvePP) >0.7 was considered acceptable for the model. Furthermore, model goodness-of-fit was confirmed by verifying that the AvePP for all classification members was at least 70% (19). Finally, the clinical interpretability of the model was taken into consideration. Details of the models are provided in Table S1 and Figure S2. For survival analysis, a Cox proportional hazards regression model was constructed to calculate hazard ratios (HRs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for different trajectory groups. The Kaplan-Meier method was used to plot 28‑day in‑hospital mortality curves.

Additionally, to further assess the consistency of the relationship between trajectories and in-hospital mortality across different populations, stratified analyses were conducted based on age, sex, and glycemic status.

Sensitivity analysis

To ensure the robustness of the study findings, sensitivity analyses were conducted. Multivariable logistic regression was applied to evaluate the association between different trajectory groups and in-hospital mortality, with adjusted odds ratios (ORs) and 95% CIs reported (Tables S2,S3). In addition, multivariable Cox regression analyses were performed as part of the sensitivity analyses, with the PaO2/FiO2 ratio, PEEP, pneumonia subtype (bacterial, viral, and aspiration pneumonia), and average fluid balance over the first 3 days of ICU admission incorporated into the models (Tables S4-S8).

Results

Research flow chart and participant characteristics

Figure 1 shows the flow chart of the study process. A total of 3,609 patients were enrolled in the study. Of these, 577 (15.99%) died within 14 days. 835 (23.14%) people died within 28 days.

Figure 2 presents the BUNCR trajectory identified by GBTM during the first 5 days of ICU admission. The characteristics of each trajectory group are described below. The estimated posterior probabilities and model parameters for each trajectory group are provided in Tables S1,S9. All patients were classified into three distinct trajectory groups: Group 1 exhibited a moderate BUNCR level, with levels increasing consistently from approximately 30 to 35. Group 2 demonstrated the lowest BUNCR level, with a decrease observed on the second day, followed by a gradual increase thereafter. Group 3 displayed the highest BUNCR level, characterized by the most rapid increase during the first 4 days, with a decreasing trend emerging only on the fourth day.

Logistic and Cox regression analyses

In Cox proportional hazards analysis, higher BUNCR levels were significantly associated with increased risk of both 14-day and 28-day in-hospital mortality (Table 2). Per standard deviation increase in BUNCR, the HR for 14-day mortality was 1.17 (95% CI: 1.08–1.26, P<0.001) in Model 3. For 28-day mortality, per SD increase showed consistent associations across all models: HR 1.16 (95% CI: 1.09–1.23). When BUNCR was analyzed in quartiles, patients in the highest quartile (Q4) had significantly elevated mortality risk compared to the lowest quartile (Q1). For 14-day mortality, the fully adjusted Model 3 showed an HR of 1.32 (95% CI: 1.03–1.71; P=0.03) for Q4 versus Q1. For 28-day mortality, the association was more pronounced: Q4 versus Q1 HRs were 1.42 (95% CI: 1.14–1.75; P=0.001) in Model 3.

Sensitivity analyses

To assess the robustness of our findings, logistic regression models examining the association between BUNCR levels and mortality outcomes (Table S2) and between BUNCR trajectory groups and mortality (Table S3) yielded results consistent with the primary Cox regression findings, supporting the stability of our conclusions. No statistically significant interactions were identified between Day 1 BUNCR quartiles and any stratification variable (all P for interaction >0.05), indicating that the association between trajectory patterns and mortality risk remained consistent across these subgroups (Table S4). Furthermore, these results remained robust after additional adjustment for positive end-expiratory pressure (PEEP), arterial partial pressure of oxygen to fraction of inspired oxygen (PaO2/FiO2) ratio, continuous renal replacement therapy (CRRT), pneumonia etiology, and average fluid balance over the first 3 days of ICU admission (Tables S5-S8).

Discussion

This study aimed to investigate the association between BUNCR trajectories and mortality in patients with pneumonia requiring IMV. Based on the modeling results, the BUNCR trajectories were categorized into three distinct groups. Group 3 exhibited the highest BUNCR levels with the most pronounced early upward trend and was associated with the highest 14-day and 28-day mortality. Furthermore, a significant dose-response relationship was observed between BUNCR and mortality in IMV pneumonia patients (P for trend <0.05), with each unit increase in BUNCR elevating the 14-day and 28-day in-hospital mortality by 17%. These findings remained robust even after adjusting for potential confounding variables, including duration of mechanical ventilation, PEEP, PaO2/FiO2 ratio, average fluid balance over the first 3 days of ICU admission, and pneumonia etiology.

Recent studies have similarly identified distinct BUNCR trajectories, linking the “persistently increasing” pattern to the poorest outcomes (8,13,20). This consistency across different disease states suggests that the early dynamic pattern of BUNCR serves as a sensitive indicator of intrinsic stress and metabolic status in critically ill patients, with its prognostic value demonstrating generalizability. However, it is noteworthy that, unlike the U-shaped relationship between BUNCR and mortality observed in hemorrhagic stroke (HS) studies (21), a more linear trend of increasing risk was observed in this cohort of IMV pneumonia patients. This discrepancy may stem from disease-specific pathophysiological mechanisms: extremely high BUNCR in the acute phase of HS may reflect extreme stress or neuroendocrine disturbances triggered by severe brain injury (22), whereas elevated BUNCR in pneumonia patients is more likely associated with persistent infection and inflammation (23).

Severe pulmonary infection and stress states drive the body into a hypercatabolic condition, characterized by significantly enhanced protein catabolism, which is a key mechanism underlying the rise in BUN levels (9,24). Severe pneumonia readily induces a robust systemic inflammatory response syndrome (SIRS), a core pathophysiological factor contributing to worsened prognosis (25). Studies indicate that in patients with severe pneumonia, elevated serum concentrations of inflammatory mediators such as interleukin-6 (IL-6) are significantly associated with higher rates of respiratory failure and mortality (26,27). Furthermore, inflammatory mediators can directly suppress myocardial function, leading to decreased cardiac output and exacerbating renal hypoperfusion. This inflammation-driven circulatory dysfunction establishes the pathophysiological basis for prerenal azotemia and an elevated BUNCR ratio (11,28). It is important to note that, compared to BUN or Cre alone, the BUNCR ratio is less susceptible to fluctuation and better reflects these complex pathophysiological interactions (29). However, its prognostic role in ventilated pneumonia remains unclear, and our trajectory-based approach has certain significance for identifying some high-risk groups in advance.

Pneumonia patients requiring IMV frequently develop hospital-acquired lower respiratory tract infections, which can further prolong mechanical ventilation duration, increase ICU length of stay, and elevate mortality (30). However, IMV itself, as a critical means of respiratory support for severe pneumonia, exerts significant additional effects on hemodynamics and renal function. In particular, the application of high PEEP reduces venous return, thereby decreasing cardiac output and aggravating any pre-existing prerenal state (31). This effect is especially pronounced in ARDS patients. Studies have demonstrated that for every 5cm H2O increase in PEEP, renal perfusion pressure can decrease by 8–12 mmHg, indirectly reflecting the sustained impact of ventilator settings on systemic circulation and organ perfusion (32,33).

An elevated BUNCR ratio can originate from prerenal factors (renal hypoperfusion) or reflect a hypercatabolic state (increased hepatic urea synthesis) (34). In our analysis, patients in the third group had significantly lower body weight compared to the other two groups. However, there was no statistically significant difference in the average fluid balance over the first 3 days between groups. When combined with the higher OASIS scores observed in the third group, these findings may suggest the presence of a cachectic state with substantial muscle wasting in this patient population—though we acknowledge that the MIMIC database does not provide standardized assessments for frailty or sarcopenia, and without direct confirmatory measurements, this interpretation remains a hypothesis-generating speculation based on our available clinical data, rather than a definitive result. According to previous studies, critically ill patients can lose up to 2% of muscle mass daily (35,36). Muscle breakdown reduces Cre generation, further elevating the BUNCR ratio and creating a “pseudo-elevation”. At the same time, Group 3 had a higher prevalence of COPD and sepsis but a lower proportion of CKD. This observed pattern may support a hypothesis that elevated BUNCR in this group is more likely associated with metabolic dysregulation triggered by severe infection, rather than intrinsic primary renal injury (37-40). While previous studies have found that hyperglycemia and high glycemic variability increase mortality risk (41,42), our subgroup analysis did not reveal significant modifying effects of age, sex, or glycemic status on the primary results.

Notably, patients in the third group exhibited lower anion gap and lactate levels but higher serum sodium compared to the other groups. This paradoxical pattern may reveal a unique pathophysiological mechanism in the high BUNCR group. A decreased anion gap may reflect severe hypoalbuminemia (43)—as the primary unmeasured anion, a decline in serum albumin can directly lower the anion gap, a condition occurring in 40–50% of ICU patients (44). This is consistent with this group having the lowest body weight and the highest proportions of COPD and sepsis. Elevated serum sodium may be indicative of a state of hemoconcentration, which aligns closely with the longer mechanical ventilation duration and lower cumulative fluid balance observed in this group (45). Prolonged mechanical ventilation may necessitate fluid restriction (46), which could potentially place patients at risk of prerenal volume depletion. This physiological state may increase renal BUN reabsorption while serum Cre levels remain relatively stable, which could represent a plausible pathway contributing to the elevated BUNCR ratio seen in this group. These preliminary findings raise the possibility that this “hypermetabolic-hemoconcentrated” pattern may warrant closer clinical monitoring and further dedicated research in patients with severe lung disease and concurrent hypermetabolic state. For patients presenting with a BUNCR trajectory similar to that of Group 3, our findings generate the hypothesis that targeted clinical monitoring and supportive interventions may improve patient outcomes, which requires formal validation in future prospective clinical studies. These hypotheses warrant prospective validation.

The primary strength of this study lies in being the first to dynamically assess the BUNCR trajectory and its prognostic value in patients with IMV pneumonia, offering a novel perspective for risk stratification within the ICU. However, this study also has several limitations. First, as this study is a retrospective analysis based on the single-center MIMIC-IV database, it is inevitably limited by selection bias and the potential impact of unmeasured confounding factors, including the absence of standardized, detailed assessments for frailty and skeletal muscle mass. Accordingly, our findings require further validation in multi-center external cohorts to confirm their robustness and generalizability especially in populations with different ventilatory management protocols. Second, to meet the requirements for trajectory modeling, we included only patients with at least three BUNCR measurements within the first 5 days of ICU admission. This criterion may have excluded cases of early mortality or rapid discharge, limiting the generalizability of the conclusions to patients in the very early stages (13). Specifically, our inclusion criterion of at least three BUNCR measurements within the first 5 days of ICU admission inevitably excluded patients with early ICU mortality. Supplementary analysis showed that only 1.37% of the total cohort (54 patients) were excluded due to a survival time of less than 2 days, accounting for 8.84% of all 14-day all-cause deaths and 6.07% of all 28-day all-cause deaths. Accordingly, the findings and conclusions of this study are more relevant and generalizable to patients with an ICU survival time longer than 2 days. Finally, the selection of the GBTM model involves a degree of subjectivity; although we chose the three-trajectory model based on clinical interpretability, different model specifications could lead to subtle differences in the results. Future multi-center, prospective studies are needed to validate these findings and explore the feasibility of integrating BUNCR trajectories into real-time clinical decision support systems.

Conclusions

In this retrospective observational study, we used the GBTM algorithm to identify three distinct BUNCR trajectories among critically ill patients with IMV pneumonia. Our findings demonstrate a significant association between BUNCR trajectories and 14-day and 28-day all-cause mortality in this cohort. These findings could help identify high-risk patients who require closer clinical monitoring, and further prospective clinical research is needed to confirm the applicability of BUNCR trajectory assessment in routine clinical practice.
