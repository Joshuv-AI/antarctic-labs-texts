Aeromedical retrieval for suspected preterm labour or rupture of membranes in the Northern Territory, Australia: may some cases be safely not retrieved?
Authors: Annie Langston-Cox, Emily Warton, Nadine Tipping, Harrison L Odgers, Adrian Nightingale, Sherihan Goni, Jane Thorn, Kiarna Brown, Holger W Unger
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSuspected preterm labour (PTL) and prelabour rupture of membranes (PPROM) are common indications for aeromedical retrieval in the Top End, Northern Territory, Australia, where many women reside remotely and preterm birth (< 37 completed weeks of gestation) is common. The primary objective of this study was to determine rate of delivery during the index admission following aeromedical transfers from remote clinics to Royal Darwin Hospital for suspected PTL/PPROM.MethodsA retrospective cohort study of aeromedical transfers for suspected PTL/PPROM from 1 January 2020 to 31 July 2022 was undertaken. Transfers were identified through CareFlight, the regional air ambulance service, and complemented with data from hospital records. Clinical and sociodemographic characteristics were compared by delivery status during the index (post-retrieval) admission using parametric and non-parametric tests and multivariable linear regression analysis.Results238 women with singleton pregnancies were retrieved for suspected PPROM (n = 77, 32.4%) or PTL (n = 161, 67.6%), together accounting for 49.2% of all obstetric transfers (n = 483). Of 77 patients transferred for suspected PPROM, 47 (61.0%) had ruptured membranes confirmed on arrival, and 45 (95.7%) of them delivered during the index admission. None of the 30 women transferred for suspected PPROM with intact membranes on arrival delivered during the index admission. Of 161 patients transferred for suspected PTL, 13 (8.1%) had ruptured membranes confirmed on arrival, and 12 (92.3%) of them delivered during the index admission. Amongst women transferred for suspected PTL with intact membranes confirmed on arrival, 14.9% (22/149) delivered during the index admission. Prior to arrival, 120 women (50.4%) had a documented speculum examination, and 15 (6.3%) and 9 (3.8%) had cervicovaginal swab tests to assess their risks of a PPROM and PTL, respectively. Half of women who did not deliver during the index admission had received antenatal corticosteroids (n = 76).ConclusionsMany aeromedical retrievals for suspected PTL/PPROM did not result in delivery during the index admission. Women retrieved for suspected PPROM with intact membranes on arrival were less likely to deliver. Upskilling remote clinic staff and better point-of-care testing may reduce retrievals and unnecessary interventions. Prospective cohort studies designed to enable accurate prediction of which cases can be safely not retrieved are required.Trial registrationNot applicable.Keywords: Pregnancy complications, Air ambulances, Diagnosis, Maternal health services, Preterm labour, Preterm premature rupture of membranes

Introduction

Preterm birth is the leading cause of death and disability in children under five years of age in the developed world [1–3]. Globally, approximately 10% of infants are born preterm and Australian data reports an 8% preterm birth rate [4]. First Nations and remote living women are overrepresented amongst women who birth preterm, with data from the Northern Territory (NT) and Western Australia indicating a two-fold increase in preterm birth rates amongst this population [5, 6]. Rates of preterm birth in the NT have remained relatively stagnant over the last decade, with improvements in perinatal outcomes largely driven by advances in neonatal care [4, 6, 7]. For this reason, it is imperative that women who deliver preterm do so in a tertiary centre, with available resources to support the neonate’s transition to extrauterine life.

Approximately 28% of Australia’s population live in rural or remote locations [8]. Aeromedical retrieval services are an essential component of Australia’s health care system architecture. Pregnancy-related retrievals make up 3–5% of all retrievals within Australia [9]. Of pregnancy-related aeromedical retrievals conducted by the Royal Flying Doctor Service from 2015 to 2019, 36% were for suspected preterm labour (PTL), and 15% for suspected preterm prelabour rupture of membranes (PPROM) [9]. Most of these retrievals were from rural and remote areas (93%), and most pregnant women retrieved were younger, with higher rates of obesity and smoking compared to the Australian average [10]. In the NT, aeromedical retrieval services are provided by CareFlight for the northernmost NT Health Service districts, the Top End, and the Royal Flying Doctor Service for Central Australia and Barkly regions. This service is a costly yet essential component of care provision, with an estimated average cost of AU$11,000 per retrieval [11].

In the context of the NT’s Top End, the threshold for transfer for suspected PTL/PPROM to a tertiary centre is low. Any suspicion of ruptured membranes or uterine contractions on assessment by remote area health practitioners necessarily triggers a request for retrieval and a cascade of interventions, including tocolysis, antenatal corticosteroids for fetal lung maturation, and antibiotics. These are frequently initiated prior to arrival at the tertiary hospital (Royal Darwin Hospital) and based on the clinical assessment of remote area nurses and retrieval crews in consultation with the hospital obstetrician. Identifying women at risk of preterm birth is challenging. However, speculum examination, together with validated tests such as fetal fibronectin (a test of vaginal secretions to predict PTB) to determine the probability of preterm labour, and rupture of membrane (ROM) tests (e.g., AmniSure), could aid as decision-making tools to identify women requiring urgent transfer, and women who could be safely not retrieved [12].

The objective of this study was to a determine the rate of aeromedical retrievals for suspected PTL/PPROM in a high-burden setting; to review the clinical management of women transferred for suspected PTL/PPROM; and to assess the rate of delivery during the hospital admission following retrieval.

Methods

Study design

We conducted a retrospective cohort study of women with singleton pregnancies between 22 and 36 completed gestational weeks who were retrieved by CareFlight for suspected PTL or PPROM from remote health centres between 1st January 2020 and 31st June 2022.

Study setting

The total population of the NT is approximately 230,000, spread across 1.3 million square kilometres. Of approximately 3,700 births annually, one-third are to women of Aboriginal and/or Torres Strait Islander ethnicity [13]. CareFlight conducts retrievals for patients residing at the Top End northern region of the NT, which for the purpose of this study encompasses the cities of Darwin and Palmerston City, and the rural/remote NT Health districts of the Top End, East Arnhem and Big Rivers. According to the 2022-23 NT Health report, 80% of the NT population resides in the Top End, the majority in Darwin [14]. Royal Darwin Hospital is the Top End’s only tertiary referral hospital and maintains an obstetric unit and a level six neonatal intensive care unit. A map of the NT and relevant Health districts is available on page 11 of the 2022-23 NT Health report:

https://health.nt.gov.au/__data/assets/pdf_file/0006/1302738/nt-health-annual-report-2022-23.pdf.

Data collection

Data were extracted from three sources: (a) CareFlight retrieval records, (b) Royal Darwin Hospital inpatient medical records, and (c) hospital and community electronic records. CareFlight data is entered by the receiver of the retrieval request call and clinical staff document information including patient history, reason for the transfer and any treatment undertaken at either the community health service or with the CareFlight team. Patient medical records at Royal Darwin Hospital were used to extract information pertaining to maternal demographics, treatment at the Royal Darwin Hospital upon arrival, and details of timing of birth and birth outcomes. Community-based medical records were used to ascertain and corroborate data on timing of the first ultrasound, maternal medical history, maternal body mass index and maternal smoking status. We extracted this data as measured and recorded by NT health staff in the aforementioned data sources.

Statistical analyses

All continuous data variables were assessed for normality. Maternal demographic and clinical characteristics were reported for women who did or did not deliver during the index admission, the primary outcome of this study. Index admission was defined as the hospital admission occurring directly following retrieval. Parametric continuous data was reported as mean [standard deviation] and compared using the Student’s t-test; continuous non-parametric data was reported as median {interquartile range} and compared using the Mann-Whitney U test; and categorical data was reported as proportions (number) and compared using the Chi-square or Fisher’s exact test.

Linear regression analysis adjusting for gestational age at birth was performed to assess the difference in mean birthweight in women who did not deliver during the index admission by exposure to antenatal corticosteroids compared to no corticosteroids.

In all analyses, a p-value (two-tailed) of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All data analysis was performed using SPSS (IBM version 20, IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA).

Results

During the study period there were a total of 483 retrievals for obstetric and gynaecological indications, of which 249 were transfers for suspected PTL or PPROM (Fig. 1). Among retrievals for suspected PTL/PPROM, nine women had twin pregnancies, and outcome data were missing for two women, leaving 238 transfers to be included in the analysis (49.2% of all obstetric transfers) (Fig. 2). Of included retrievals, 77 (32.4%) and 161 (67.6%) were undertaken for the indications of suspected PPROM and PTL, respectively. A total of 60 (25.2%) retrieved patients had ruptured membranes confirmed on arrival, 79 (33.2%) delivered during the index admission, and 89 (37.4%) ultimately birthed prior to 36 gestational weeks. None of the women retrieved for suspected PTL/PPROM delivered in flight.

Clinical and socio-demographic characteristics of transferred women, by delivery status on index admission

The characteristics of transferred women who delivered during the index admission (n = 79) compared to those who did not deliver (n = 159) are summarised in Table 1. Women who delivered during the index admission following retrieval for suspected PTL/PPROM were of lower parity, had a lower body mass index, and were less likely to have diabetes in pregnancy. Women who delivered during the index admission received their first (dating) ultrasound later in pregnancy. There were no statistically significant differences between groups in factors associated with preterm birth such as a known short cervix, progesterone use, cervical cerclage, and a history of previous preterm birth. Smoking status did not differ between groups; however, it was frequently not documented.

Indication of transfer, rupture of membrane status on arrival, and delivery status on index admission

Of 77 women transferred for suspected PPROM, 47 (61.0%) were confirmed PPROM on arrival: 45 (95.7%) of these patients delivered during the index admission, and 42 (89.4%) had a birth < 36 gestational weeks (Fig. 2). Conversely, of 30 (39.0%) of women transferred for suspected PPROM with intact membranes confirmed on arrival, none delivered during the index admission, and one (3.3%) had a birth < 36 gestational weeks.

Of 161 women transferred for suspected PTL, 13 (8.1%) were confirmed PPROM on arrival: 12 (92.3%) of these patients delivered during the index admission, and 10 (76.9%) delivered prior to 36 gestational weeks. Conversely, of 148 women (91.4%) transferred for suspected PTL with intact membranes on arrival, 22 (14.9%) delivered during the index admission, and 36 (24.3%) ultimately birthed before 36 gestational weeks.

Overall, women who went on to deliver during the index admission were more likely to have been transferred for the indication of suspected PPROM than for suspected PTL (Table 2). Similarly, women who delivered during the index admission were substantially more likely to have had ruptured membranes confirmed on arrival.

Peri-retrieval investigations and interventions, by delivery status on index admission

Overall, 138 (58.0%) of transferred women had a documented cervical assessment before or at retrieval, primarily by speculum examination (Table 2). A total of 86 women (32.7%) had a documented urine dipstick test. ROM and fetal fibronectin tests were documented for 15 (5.7%) and 9 (3.4%) women, respectively, and a negative test did not alter decision to transfer. 113 (43.0%) women had a documented first dose of antenatal corticosteroids before arrival at hospital, 8 (3.0%) received magnesium sulphate (MgSO4) for fetal neuroprotection, 147 (55.9%) received tocolysis, and 115 (43.7%) were commenced on PTL/PPROM antibiotics. Pre-transfer cervical assessment, antibiotics, and tocolysis were more common among women who delivered during the index admission compared to women who did not deliver after arrival. Antenatal corticosteroids were administered uniformly across groups.

Post-retrieval investigations and interventions, by delivery status on index admission

Upon arrival, 224 (94.1%) had a documented speculum examination, 211 (88.7%) had a urine dipstick, 153 (64.3%) had a ROM test, and 62 (26.0%) had a fetal fibronectin test (Table 2). ROM and fetal fibronectin tests were used more frequently at the hospital, particularly in women who did not deliver. There was more directed administration of antibiotics, tocolysis, steroids and magnesium sulphate following arrival and assessment at hospital. Overall, 81 (50.9%) of women who were retrieved but did not deliver during the index admission were diagnosed with and treated for a urinary tract infection.

Neonatal outcomes amongst women who did not deliver during index admission by receipt of antenatal corticosteroids

When assessing neonatal outcomes by in utero exposure to antenatal corticosteroids amongst women who did not deliver on index admission (n = 159), babies exposed to steroids tended to need more neonatal unit support and had a lower mean birthweight (2900 g vs. 3170 g; p = 0.005) (Table 3). Antenatal corticosteroids remained associated with lower mean birthweight in analyses adjusting for gestational age at birth (n = 146; mean difference − 178 g, 95% confidence interval − 349, -7; P = 0.042).

Discussion

As the consequences of giving birth to a very preterm infant in remote settings are highly significant, any suspicion of ruptured membranes or uterine activity necessarily triggers a cascade of interventions and prompt transfer to a major perinatal centre able to manage preterm births. Women with suspected PTL or suspected PPROM form a substantial proportion of obstetric and gynaecological transfers to Royal Darwin Hospital, confirming findings of a review of aeromedical retrieval data from three other Australian states [10]. None of the retrieved patients delivered during the flight, as reported previously [15]. However, a substantial proportion of retrieved women ultimately did not deliver during the admission following transfer.

A key finding of this study was the strong association between confirmed ruptured membranes and delivery on index admission, irrespective of whether the indication for transfer was for suspected PPROM or PTL; nearly all women who were confirmed to have ruptured membranes upon arrival at hospital delivered during the index admission (95.0%). The median interval between hospital diagnosis of ruptured membranes and delivery was 3 days for women with PPROM, and 37 days for women without PPROM. This suggests that extending the availability and use of ROM tests in remote clinics may aid in identifying women in need of rapid retrieval, whilst confirming the absence of ruptured membranes prior to transfer may assist with safely keeping some women in their communities. Overall, of women transferred for suspected PPROM, 39.0% had intact confirmed on arrival, and none of these patients delivered during index admission. Amongst women transferred for suspected PTL who had intact membranes confirmed on arrival, 14.9% delivered during the index admission. For the latter, point of care tests such as fetal fibronectin or Actim Partus could be used to better identify women at risk of preterm labour and allow for more targeted use of transfers and interventions.

The current approach to the management of suspected PPROM/PTL is designed to prevent very preterm births to occur in remote clinics, an event with potentially dire consequences, that drives a well-justified reluctance of clinical staff not to transfer cases. There is a paucity of research on the role of triaging for suspected PPROM/PTL in Australia, where a substantial part of the population resides in remote and rural locations. Our study indicates that under certain conditions not transferring some patients may be safe. However, this would need to be assessed in larger prospective cohort studies, designed to determine the conditions and parameters that are required to keep patients at home safely. This work would need to be complemented with, e.g., research to understand patient perspectives around the acceptability of clinician-collected vaginal tests [12]. Similarly, it would be important to review the possibility of self-collected vaginal tests [16], and validate their accuracy, remembering that some remote health centres may only have male staff available which may be culturally inappropriate for some women [17]. Extending use of these tests in remote clinics must be supported with comprehensive training and the upskilling and maintenance of a strong local workforce.

Improved identification of women with suspected PTL/PPROM who are unlikely to deliver preterm may not only reduce transfers but could also permit a more targeted administration of interventions, in particular the use of antenatal corticosteroids, antibiotics and MgSO4. This is particularly important when considering the high rates of steroids administration that, if ultimately unnecessary, may have negative implications for longer-term maternal and child health outcomes [18]. This potential adverse effect of antenatal corticosteroids is particularly relevant for remote living women of the NT. In a setting with high rates of diabetes in pregnancy [19], the impact of administration of antenatal corticosteroids on blood sugar level control may be substantial, as levels remain elevated for up to five days post administration of even one dose [20]. Minimising unnecessary administration of corticosteroids may be important in this population [21]. Similarly, there is a growing appreciation for the importance of the maternal microbiome for preterm birth prevention and unnecessary antibiotic administration may have unwanted impacts [22]. Certainly, the high rates of antimicrobial resistance in the NT would encourage judicious use of antibiotics [23]. Lastly, in our regression analysis we observed a lower mean birthweight in women who were exposed to steroids antenatally but did not go on to deliver until after 37 gestational weeks, reflecting findings from other settings [24].

Overall, women who went on to deliver their babies shortly after arrival tended to have lower BMIs and were of lower parity. Women with diabetes were more likely to be transferred, which may relate to greater linkage with the health care system and a lower threshold to transfer. Another finding of this study was the high rates of antibiotic prescriptions for urinary tract infection in women who were retrieved but did not go on to deliver during the index admission. Increased use of urine dipstick tests prior to retrieval to detect and treat urinary tract infections may play a role in the triaging process for retrieval. Women who went on to deliver during the index admission had higher rates of cervical assessment, tocolysis and antibiotic administration before and during retrieval than women who did not deliver, suggesting there is some recognition in the community of who appears clinically more likely to deliver. ROM and fetal fibronectin tests were more commonly used at Royal Darwin Hospital in women who did not deliver, suggesting that these tools are useful for ruling out PTL or PPROM.

Based on reported average cost of aeromedical retrievals (AU$11,000 per retrieval), retrievals alone for women who did not deliver during the index admission (n = 159) may amount to approximately AU$1,750,000 during the study period, with further uncharted costs associated with inpatient hospital stay and return transportation. While it is imperative that resources exist to transfer women from remote areas to a tertiary centre prior to delivery for improved maternal and neonatal outcomes, the current system is costly to the health service. These costs may be better utilised elsewhere, for example in upskilling local staff in cervical assessment or improving tools and resources available in remote health centres. Additional financial and psychosocial costs to a woman and to her family that resulted from the displacement associated with aeromedical retrieval, were not captured in this retrospective study. Health economic analyses are required to estimate the full cost of the current triaging system and any potential saving that could be made through investments in enhancing capacity to assess women with suspected PTL/PPROM in remote communities. These must be complemented with assessments of patient perspectives around retrievals to the tertiary centre.

A strength of the study is the linkage of retrieval, hospital and community health data, which enabled this first detailed characterisation of transfers for suspected PPROM/PTL at the Top End, an important contribution to the very limited amount of data on this aspect of remote and rural medicine in Australia. However, the study has several limitations. Firstly, data were extracted from several sources and some data were difficult to access, not recorded at all, or poorly recorded (e.g., maternal smoking status). It is possible that rates of ROM and fetal fibronectin tests undertaken at hospital were underreported in patient medical records, though speculum and vaginal examinations were well documented. Similarly, urine analysis may have been performed more commonly in remote clinics prior to transfer, but access to remote centre health records was not available to ascertain this further. Furthermore, we were unable to ascertain how many of the women who were discussed by remote health clinic staff with district medical officers regarding suspected PTL/PPROM were subsequently not referred on to CareFlight for consideration of retrieval, and what investigations were done remotely to aid that decision. Additionally, a small number of women may have been referred to CareFlight but were ultimately not transferred, either because remote clinical staff made the decision that they no longer require transfer or transfer was declined. Furthermore, the COVID-19 pandemic may have affected our findings, as the decision to retrieve is a function of capacity in remote clinics to assess patients (availability of test kits and staff) and a desire to keep patients away from areas where COVID-19 was transmitted if possible. Lastly, the risk of bias inherent to small sample cohorts and presence of unmeasured confounders must be taken into consideration when interpretating our findings. Larger prospective studies, starting at the point of remote clinic attendance for suspected PPROM/PTL may provide a more comprehensive assessment of the characteristics of clinical presentation and management, and should include long term neonatal outcome follow-up to better ascertain impacts of interventions for mothers and babies retrieved for suspected PPROM or PTL who ultimately do not deliver preterm.

Conclusions

Women transferred for suspected PTL or PPROM who were found to have intact membranes on arrival had low rates of delivery during the index admission and subsequent preterm birth overall. Strengthening capacity at remote health clinics to perform clinical assessments for suspected PTL/PPROM, including upscaled use of point-of-care test for ROM or PTL, could significantly reduce the socioeconomic burden of aeromedical retrieval and unnecessary intervention.
