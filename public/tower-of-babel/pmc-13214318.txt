The role of brain-derived neurotrophic factor in cardiometabolic disease and mental health within the spinal cord injury population: a systematic review
Authors: Thomas P Walden, Matthew Cleland, Philemon Tsang, Emilia Main, B Catharine Craven
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundBrain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF) is a protein crucial to the survival, growth, and differentiation of neurons in the brain and spinal cord. BDNF is monitored across many populations as an indicator of one’s cardiometabolic disease (CMD) and mental health (MH) risk. Adults living with a traumatic spinal cord injury (tSCI) are at a higher risk of developing CMD and MH issues, with symptoms often going unrecognized. Establishing serum BDNF as a screening tool within the tSCI population has the potential to improve CMD and MH symptom recognition.ObjectivesThis systematic review aims to: (1) explore the tSCI literature to determine whether an association exists between serum BDNF, MH, and CMD risk(s); and; (2) identify best-practice BDNF sampling techniques within the tSCI population.MethodsA comprehensive search strategy was developed in collaboration with a University Health Network Librarian. Six databases (MEDLINE, Embase, CENTRAL, APA PsycInfo, CINAHL Ultimate, and Web of Science Core Collection) were searched to identify English-language studies published from inception to July 2025. Studies which reported serum BDNF in the tSCI population in addition to either MH or CMD and have three or more human participants with acute or chronic tSCI were included. Duplicate abstracts were removed and the remaining titles and abstracts reviewed and selected for full-text screening. Study quality was assessed for potential risk of bias using Downs and Black Checklist (Clinical Trials), Newcastle–Ottawa Score (Case–Control Study), or Joanna Briggs Institute Checklist (Cross-sectional Study), prior to data extraction. The serum BDNF analytic methods were reviewed in detail.ResultsA total of 2,148 potential studies were identified via the searches, of which 631 duplicates were removed, 1,488 abstracts were excluded for inappropriate population, outcome measure, or study design, and 29 articles were selected for full-text screening, with four studies included in the final review. All studies sampled and analyzed serum BDNF. A total of 271 participants (AIS: A–D, NLI: C1–L5), predominantly male (n = 224), with acute (n = 165) and chronic (n = 51) injuries aged 14–75 as well as healthy controls (n = 55) were included. One study investigated the influence of an intervention and three studies were cross-sectional. No identified study included a description or indication of the prevalence for MH conditions or CMD risk factors.ConclusionsBased on the reviewed literature, links between serum BDNF and MH disorders or CMD risk have not yet been established for individuals with acute or chronic tSCI. The selected studies demonstrated no consistent sampling or analysis methods, with limited adherence to prior established standards in the general population, bringing into question the reliability, validity, and quality of the available outcome data.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12938-026-01566-x.Keywords: Brain-derived neurotrophic factor, Mental health disorders, Cardiometabolic disease, Spinal cord injury

Background

Spinal cord injury (SCI) refers to damage to the spinal cord, predominantly caused by severe trauma or disease impacting tissues in the region [1]. The neurological injury results in varying degrees of loss of motor, sensory, and autonomic functions [1]. The sudden loss of independence associated with SCI disrupts family relationships, social participation, community engagement, and professional endeavors, leading to a high prevalence of mental health (MH) disorders within the SCI community [2, 3]. Along with the MH implications, the significant increase in sedentary time predisposes individuals living with an SCI to cardiometabolic diseases (CMD), the leading cause of death within the population [4, 5]. A significant reduction in daily physical activity levels due to prolonged sitting [1], in addition to neurogenic[6] and inflammatory changes after injury [7], causes rapid declines in global muscle mass (50% decrease in muscle cross-sectional area in the first 6 months post-injury) and increases in visceral, and intra-muscular fat [8, 9]. These changes in body composition have a direct impact on an individual’s metabolic profile, escalating resting blood glucose levels, leading to health complications such as type Ⅱ diabetes mellitus, cardiovascular disease, and stroke [10]. As changes to mental and cardiometabolic health are rapid post-SCI, identifying reliable biomarkers that can quickly and accurately identify individuals at an increased risk of specific MH and CMD co-morbidities is of paramount importance and may provide valuable guidance regarding the efficacy of early intervention.

Brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF) is a signaling protein (neurotrophin) that upregulates physiological pathways, supporting the proliferation of satellite cells, neurogenesis, axon growth, synapse modulation, and neuron survival within the central nervous system [11]. Across the acute and subacute stages of SCI, the physiological pathways upregulated by this neurotrophin have been reported to promote functional and neurological recovery, and adaptive plasticity [12]. Chronically, less is understood about the utility of BDNF and its role in overall health. In the non-SCI population or among persons without a disability, reduced levels of BDNF are linked with several mood disorders, with important relationships observed between major depressive disorder and BDNF. [13] The systematic review and meta-analysis conducted by Tiwari et al. [13], which synthesized 24 studies within the non-SCI literature (1130 depressed vs. 1378 controls), reported a strong overall effect of peripheral BDNF which was significantly reduced in the depressed individuals.

Outside of the central nervous system, within the periphery, BDNF is hypothesized to be a contraction-induced myokine that plays an active role in the physiological processes within muscle cells [14]. First synthesized within the endoplasmic reticulum as a precursor protein (pro-BDNF), final maturation of myokines are achieved within the Golgi complex, where the terminal domain is cleaved, creating mature BDNF (mBDNF), which can exit the cell membrane into the circulatory system [15]. To begin its intracellular process, mBDNF binds with its membrane receptor (tropomyosin receptor kinase B [TrkB]) on the surface of a muscle cell, triggering three physiological pathways, reported to increase angiogenesis and the proliferation of vascular smooth muscle cells, cardiomyocytes, and endothelial cells, linking the neurotrophin to improved cardiovascular health in an non-SCI population [15]. Moreover, high concentration levels of mBDNF are reported to enhance protein synthesis, blood glucose regulation, enhanced oxidization of fatty acids (β-oxidation) within the mitochondria, reduce energy intake (reduced appetite), and protect pancreatic b-cells, potentially increasing their proliferation [11]. Combined, the physiological processes taking place within the central nervous system and periphery not only improve depressive symptoms and increase muscle density but also reduce excess adipose tissue and CMD risk [15], suggesting a tri-directional relationship between CMD, MH disorders, and serum mBDNF. In addition, CMD and MH disorders further suppress mBDNF, while physical activity, a mitigator of both conditions, stimulates its production, reinforcing the interconnected nature of this tri-directional relationship.

Over 90% of circulating mBDNF is stored within blood platelets, with a small amount in the plasma of whole blood [16]. Although mBDNF within the plasma is not as abundant, in this form it is hypothesized the neurotrophin can cross the blood–brain barrier [17], suggesting plasma mBDNF may be a potential proxy for central nervous system’s mBDNF levels [18]. In contrast, platelet mBDNF functions outside of the central nervous system. The most common method of testing platelet mBDNF is to measure the concentration level within blood serum [19]. Due to the different transport mechanisms, mBDNF levels measured within blood plasma or blood serum cannot be generalized to represent the level of one another [20]. In addition, current assay analysis methods do not allow the differentiation between muscle and brain-derived serum mBDNF, and due to the large amount of serum mBDNF source being through platelet aggregation, the variable is highly sensitive to pre-analytical handling [21].

Best practice methods for testing mBDNF concentration in blood serum have been established, with particular emphasis placed on the high sensitivity of mBDNF to individual (age, sex, fitness level, urbanicity, smoking status, and drinking [alcohol] behavior) and day-to-day factors (time of the blood draw, type of food ingested before the blood draw, and fasting state before the blood draw) [22, 23]. During analysis, commercially available enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) kits are commonly used to measure the concentration of mBDNF within serum samples. Not all ELISA kits are the same, with each having its own unique guidelines, detection ranges, and sensitivity levels [23]. These differences are critical, with Polacchini, Metelli et al. [23] suggesting that the selection of ELISA kit is the main source of inconsistency among recent studies into serum mBDNF concentration. Furthermore, comparisons of resting mBDNF between non-SCI and SCI populations have not been established. Therefore, it is unknown whether the suggested confounding variables that influence research in non-SCI individuals also apply within the SCI population, whether SCI will play a unique role in influencing concentration levels, and if or how experimental protocols will control for all the nuances of SCI.

Given MH disorders being substantially more prevalent in the SCI population compared to general public[24] and the impact CMD has on lifespan[25] within the SCI population, there is cause to investigate all avenues that may contribute to the identification and reduction of these life-threatening and life-altering health conditions. To date, we are not aware of other systematic reviews that have explored the relationship between MH disorders, CMD, and mBDNF among adults living with SCI. The primary aim of this systematic review was to investigate the SCI literature to determine whether there are established associations between mBDNF, MH, and CMD. A secondary aim of this review was to document the sampling techniques of the selected studies, highlighting consistencies in sampling methods and factors related to sampling quality.

Methods

Search strategy

The systematic review was registered on PROSPERO in 2023 (CRD42023482724). The search strategy was developed by select team members (MC, TW, and BCC) under the direction and guidance of a university health network librarian (EM). The search strategy was developed in the MEDLINE (Ovid) database as a multi-strand search, with SCI and BDNF as the primary search concepts, and cardiometabolic disease, MH disorders, (with a particular emphasis on depression and anxiety), and inflammatory markers as the three additional strands. To ensure that this strategy balanced sensitivity and specificity, a sample of 100 records unique to the two primary search concepts was reviewed by MC and EM. Once the MEDLINE search was finalized, it was translated to five additional databases: Embase (Ovid), Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials (Ovid), APA PsycInfo (Ovid), CINAHL Ultimate (EBSCOhost Research Databases), and Web of Science Core Collection (Clarivate). No limits to date, language, or study design were applied. The database searches were first executed on July 17, 2023. The search was updated on July 11, 2025, again with no date limits applied. Study screening process

The screening process for the study was conducted using Covidence systematic review software (Online – Veritas Health Innovation, Melbourne, AUS, 2023). Records were first deduplicated using a modified Bramer Method [26] in EndNote 20 (Desktop Application – Clarivate, Philadelphia, PA, 2013), which included an additional step to deduplicate records from 2025 against those from 2023 using database accession numbers. Remaining records were imported to Covidence. Titles and abstracts were screened to identify studies that met the inclusion criteria by two independent authors in both 2023 (MC and TW) and 2025 (TW and PT). Articles that passed the title and abstract screening underwent full-text review by two independent authors (MC and TW). Any discrepancies in study inclusion criteria were resolved by consensus. If authors could not come to a consensus, a third reviewer (BCC) settled inter-rater discrepancies. The reference list of included studies was screened for additional articles that met the inclusion criteria. Recent publications by authors of registered protocol documents were searched to identify studies derived from identified protocols. Secondary searches of citations and authors were conducted using Google Scholar.

Study selection

Articles evaluating serum mBDNF in the traumatic SCI (tSCI) population, in addition to either MH disorders (e.g., unipolar depression, adjustment disorder, anxiety, or post-traumatic stress disorder) [27] and/or cardiometabolic risk factors (e.g., sarcopenic obesity, arterial stiffness/carotid-femoral pulse wave velocity, dyslipidemia, high-density lipoprotein, triglycerides, heart disease, low-density lipoprotein, cholesterol, and Type-2 diabetes) [28, 29] and/or serum markers of inflammation (e.g., interleukin-6 [IL-6], interleukin-10 [IL-10], high-sensitivity C-reactive protein, C-reactive protein, and TNF-α) [30], as primary or secondary outcomes, were included. Studies involving neural recovery, rehabilitation, or plasticity were not the focus of this systematic review and, therefore, were excluded. Article types selected for review included randomized controlled trials, systematic reviews (with or without meta-analyses), controlled studies, cohort studies, case–control studies, research in progress, conference proceedings, and abstracts. All articles were required to be written in English and have three or more human participants with acute or chronic tSCI. Book chapters, animal models of SCI, theses, dissertations, gray literature, narrative reviews, case reports, and any articles not written in English were excluded.

Data extraction and quality assessment

Two reviewers (MC and TW) independently extracted data from the three journal articles identified through electronic database searches. Any discrepancies between the reviewers were resolved through in-person discussion after all documents were reviewed. In the event of a disagreement, a third reviewer (BCC) provided a resolution. The quality and potential risk of bias of each study were appraised separately using the most appropriate assessment tool. Briefly, the randomized controlled trial [31] was assessed via the Downs and Black Checklist [32], case–control study [33] via the Newcastle–Ottawa Score [34], and cross-sectional study [35] by the relevant Joanna Briggs Institute checklist [36], as appropriate for each study design.

Results

In July 2023, 1,858 records were retrieved by the database search strategy, 585 records were removed as duplicates, and 1,273 records were considered for inclusion. The search update in July 2025 retrieved 2,148 records, of which 1,904 were removed as duplicates, and an additional 244 records were reviewed for inclusion. In total, 1,517 abstracts were reviewed, and 1,488 were removed due to lack of relevance to the study objective. Twenty-nine articles were included in the full-text review (28 from the 2023 search and 1 from the 2025 search). We dismissed 4 articles due to the wrong population, 6 due to wrong outcomes, and 13 for incorrect study design (Fig. 1). Finally, four journal articles and two protocol documents were included in the review. Before data extraction, the citation lists of the three journal articles were screened for potential inclusion. Twenty-five articles were identified but were all excluded during full-text screening for not meeting the review requirements (Fig. 1). Five published manuscripts stemming from the protocol documents were also identified but were excluded as they did not meet the inclusion criterion. Based on these exclusions, the author group also removed the two protocol documents prior to the full-text review process (Fig. 1). Quality scores for the four reviewed articles are displayed in Tables 1, 2, 3.

Patient characteristics of the selected studies

Table 3 summarizes the study designs and participant characteristics of the studies selected for review. The reviewed studies included a total of 271 participants, predominantly male (n = 224), with acute (n = 165) and chronic (n = 51) injuries at all levels of the ASIA Impairment Scale (AIS) (A = 63, B = 28, C = 22, D = 23; no data available from Li, Cui et al. [31]), as well as non-SCI controls (n = 55). Table 2 details the measures collected, the number of sample time points, and BDNF-specific study outcomes. The reviewed studies have investigated movement and sensory function, infection rates, inflammation markers, lipid profiles, and antioxidant levels with samples collected either as a cross-sectional or longitudinal study design to highlight change instrumented by the implementation of an intervention or via time post-injury.

Risk of bias in included studies

The risk of bias and quality of methodological assessments categorized two studies (66.6%) either of “good quality” (Li, Cui et al. [31], Downs and Black Checklist) or “include” (Sadowska-Krepa, Zwierzchowska et al. [35], Joanna Briggs Institute Checklist) and two studies were evaluated as “poor quality” (Grassner, Klein et al. [33], Newcastle–Ottawa Score and Mayr et al. [37], Joanna Briggs Institute Checklist). Score breakdowns across the three scales are displayed in Tables 1, 2, 3. Although the reviewed studies all fell under separate study types, requiring the employment of different risk of bias tools, positive and negative commonalities were observed. The studies conducted by Li, Cui et al. [31] and Sadowska-Krepa, Zwierzchowska et al. [35] both scored highly in the areas of internal and external validity, with recruitment criteria clearly stated and sampling methods consistent across participants. However, all reviewed studies scored poorly in the identification and controlling of confounding variables (elements that may impact the daily variation of mBDNF serum levels) as well as adverse event reporting. In addition, Grassner, Klein et al. [33] scored poorly in the subsections of “selection of participants” and “exposure” of the Newcastle–Ottawa Quality Assessment Scale due to the limited justification for the healthy control group, the unclear documentation of previous or current infections at the time of sampling, and the inability to identify response rate as an expected outcome.

Overview of reviewed studies’ aims and BDNF outcomes

Li, Cui et al. [31] tested the clinical efficacy of two 4-week strategies to reduce inflammation in patients with sub-acute tSCI. All patients underwent posterior laminectomy and screw fixation and were then separated into control or experimental group. The control and experimental groups received conventional anti-inflammatory therapy and impact therapy with methylprednisolone. In addition, the experimental group received a combination of Riluzole with mannitol and hyperbaric oxygen. Both strategies significantly reduced levels of mBDNF from pre to post-intervention (p < 0.01). mBDNF was significantly lower within the experimental group at the post-intervention time point (p = 0.02). Grassner, Klein et al. [33] aimed to understand whether the immune profile of patients with sub-acute (4 weeks post-injury) tSCI could predict infection rate in the following 12 weeks. The authors reported a weak relationship between mBDNF, and infection rates, as mBDNF levels were low and not significantly different between individuals, who, did and did not, get an infection. Sadowska-Krepa, Zwierzchowska et al. [35] assessed the effects of long-term exercise adherence (wheelchair rugby) on lipid profiles, antioxidant status, and mBDNF. Participants were assigned to the sedentary or wheelchair rugby player group based on their level of involvement. The wheelchair rugby groups were further separated into positions: low-point (low-activity) and high-point (high-activity). The authors reported no difference in levels of mBDNF between the groups. Mayr et al. [37] also assessed the effects of exercise (wheelchair dancing, hand biking, basketball, and marathon) on exercise capacity, physical work capacity, blood lipids, pulse wave velocity, and pro-inflammatory endothelial blood markers. Participants were assigned to one of four groups (wheelchair dancing, other sports, non-exercise, and age-matched non-SCI controls). The authors reported no difference in levels of mBDNF between the groups. None of the reviewed studies directly analyzed relationships between mBDNF, CMD, and MH outcomes. Across all studies reviewed, CMD measures were secondary outcomes and measures of MH were not reported.

Overview of cardiometabolic disease and mental health outcomes

Table 2 outlines the outcomes related to CMD. Li, Cui et al. [31] sampled IL-6, a biomarker associated with CMD. The authors reported a significant reduction in the biomarker for both groups (p ≤ 0.03), with the experimental group significantly lower at the post-intervention time point (p < 0.01). Grassner, Klein et al. [33] sampled a broad spectrum of cytokines, including IL-6 and IL-10. However, due to absence or low proportion, the measures were deemed below the lower limit of quantification, and were excluded. In a study by Sadowska-Krepa, Zwierzchowska et al. [35], descriptive measures included body mass index and waist circumference. No analyses of the descriptive measures were conducted. A serum lipid profile was done including fasting glucose, total cholesterol, high-density lipoproteins, and low-density lipoproteins with no reported differences across the three groups (p ≥ 0.09). Mayr et al. [37] sampled IL-6, a biomarker associated with CMD. The authors reported a significant increase in the biomarker within the no-exercise SCI group (5.5 pg/ml−1) compared to the persons without a disability group (1.9 pg/ml−1). Pulse-wave velocity parameters reflected no between-group differences. Maximal volume of oxygen consumption (VO2MAX) was also collected. The authors reported significantly low relative scores within the wheelchair dancing (16.7 ml/kg−1/min−1) and non-exercise (12.6 ml/kg−1/min−1) SCI groups compared to the other sports SCI (23.7 ml/kg−1/min−1) and persons without a disability (23.6 ml/kg−1/min−1) groups. No outcome measures of MH were reported in the three reviewed studies. Although a broad spectrum of CMD measures were collected across the reviewed studies, no analyses of the relationship with mBDNF was conducted.

Overview of BDNF sampling methodology

Table 2 outlines the mBDNF sampling methods used by each reviewed study, demonstrating a high degree of variability in approaches. Grassner, Klein et al. [33] collected participant samples with non-heparinized tubes, leaving the samples to clot at room temperature before centrifuging at 1,500 g for 20 min. Whereas, Sadowska-Krepa, Zwierzchowska et al. [35] and Mayr et al. [37] collected participant samples using heparinized tubes. Sadowska-Krepa, Zwierzchowska et al. [35] centrifuged immediately at 1000 g-force for 10 min before washing samples three times with cold saline. Alternatively, Mayr et al. [37] left the samples to clot for 1–2 h before centrifuging at 2020 g-force for 10 min at 21° C. Li, Cui et al. [31] did not report their sample methods. Similar to sampling methods, there were no consistency across the reviewed studies for ELISA kit used for blood analysis. The ELISA kits used by Sadowska-Krepa, Zwierzchowska et al. [35], Mayr et al. [37], and Grassner, Klein et al. [33] reported mBDNF concentrations outside of the normative ranges described by the kit manufacturer. Normative ranges were not reported by Li, Cui et al. [31] or on the manufacturer’s website. None of the reviewed studies reported running their samples in duplicate. However, in contacting the research teams via email, Grassner, Klein et al. [33] and Mayr et al. [37] informed us that their samples were run in duplicate to establish within-session reliability of the ELISA kit. Methodological controls included diurnal variation (sampling at the same time of day) and fasting state, but there was no consistency between the reviewed studies.

Discussion

The purpose of this review was to explore the potential links between mBDNF, CMD, and MH disorder risks among individuals living with a tSCI. Based on the reviewed literature, no study has directly investigated relationships between serum concentration levels of BDNF and CMD or MH disorders, demonstrating a gap in the literature for individuals with acute and chronic tSCI. Our secondary aim was to document the serum preparation techniques used, highlighting consistencies in the quantification of mBDNF concentration methodology and related controlling factors. We identified no consistent methodology in collection, preparation, or mBDNF quantification across the reviewed studies. In addition, reported concentration levels of mBDNF were outside the limits of validity for the implemented ELISA kits and methodological controls were not implemented based on the non-SCI literature [38–41]. Based on the reviewed studies, limited information exists to inform the tSCI research, clinical, and patient community about the relationship between BDNF, CMD, and MH disorders. Further, it is difficult to infer potential relationships to the non-SCI literature as the serum preparation methods of the reviewed manuscripts do not mimic those commonly used for evaluating serum mBDNF [39], which severely limits the applicability and usability of the current data and literature across the broader tSCI population.

BDNF as a predictor of CMD and mental health disorders in the tSCI population

Li, Cui et al. [31] suggest that, during the acute and sub-acute phases, increased levels of mBDNF are an indication of increased inflammation caused by the injury, with protocols being investigated to reduce mBDNF levels during these phases. In contrast, Grassner, Klein et al. [33] suggest that low levels of mBDNF within the acute and subacute phases indicate an increased risk of a future infection during rehabilitation and recovery. Grassner, Klein et al. [33] also reported a significantly lower level of mBDNF within the tSCI groups compared to non-SCI controls [37]. No relationship was developed between mBDNF and markers of CMD or MH disorders. Overall, the reviewed studies do not allow a clear understanding of whether sustaining a tSCI compromises the utility of mBDNF as a marker of health or an identifier of disease. Sadowska-Krepa, Zwierzchowska et al. [35] and Mayr et al. [37] evaluated individuals with chronic tSCI. The cross-sectional studies reported no difference in mBDNF between tSCI individuals who were physically active and those who were not. Further, Mayr et al. [37] reported no difference between tSCI individuals and non-SCI controls although fitness levels (reported as VO2MAX) were significantly higher in the non-SCI control group. These results are in contrast to findings from younger[38] and older adults[42] within the non-SCI population. However, individuals living with tSCI can experience episodes of inflammation, briefly increasing the abundance of immune cells and cytokines [43], which could impede the chronic tracking of mBDNF. These episodes of inflammation are more common within tSCI individuals who present with a cascade of health complications, more often observed in patients with multimorbidities [44]. Although participants were grouped by physical activity level, other factors such as obesity, physical fitness levels, or baseline inflammation were not controlled for when comparing mBDNF across groups. Therefore, it is unclear whether potential confounding variables influenced the between-group comparison. Grassner, Klein et al. [33], Sadowska-Krepa, Zwierzchowska et al. [35], and Mayr et al. [37] all reported a broad spectrum of inflammatory blood biomarkers. However, the authors did not aim to establish relationships between the presence of specific inflammatory blood biomarkers and mBDNF. Furthermore, Grassner, Klein et al. [33] reported the abundance of blood markers within a single figure, restricting our ability to accurately interpret the study outcomes.

The reviewed studies highlight the potential of mBDNF as a novel indicator of cardiometabolic and MH health within the tSCI population. However, our review highlights key methodological limitations of the available literature. Before mBDNF can be used as a biomarker of health or an identifier of disease, specific tSCI nuances such as time since injury onset and level of injury, inflammatory markers, volume, intensity and type of physical activity, severity of obesity, and number of comorbidities need to be identified and accounted for during the analyses of this neurotrophin. In addition, the sampling of the tSCI population to establish normative ranges for mBDNF needs to be representative of the population in terms of age, sex, impairment, and activity levels. Currently, there is a heavy bias toward males and recently there have been increases in the average age at injury [45]. Both age and sex affect the concentration levels of mBDNF within the periphery [46]. In the non-SCI population, research has reported a gradual decline in resting mBDNF levels, attributed to vascular, metabolic, and hormonal changes associated with the aging process [47]. In addition, males generally have a lower baseline mBDNF concentration, as estrogen (more prominent in females) boosts the expression of mBDNF in the periphery [46]. How these confounding variables influence baseline mBDNF will need to be established during the development of normative ranges within the tSCI population, allowing for high levels of external validity. This would increase our understanding of health-related outcomes specifically linked to improved levels of mBDNF and whether the neurotrophin can be tracked chronically as an indicator of rehabilitation success. Until normative ranges are developed, the utility of mBDNF remains limited. The current lack of utility offers a large area of future research that requires attention.

Sampling standards and normative ranges for BDNF in the TSCI population

Across the reviewed studies, limited similarities were reported in the collection and preparation of blood samples or the quantification of mBDNF concentration methodology. Sadowska-Krepa, Zwierzchowska et al. [35] and Grassner, Klein et al. [33] both stored samples in a −80 °C freezer but reported no further similarities. Li, Cui et al. [31] did not disclose their methodology and limited methodological parameters where available from Mayr et al. [37] due to the style of publication (i.e., Rapid Report). Li, Cui et al. [31] and Mayr et al. [37] were both contacted for the opportunity to provide further information. Information provided by Mayr et al. [37] is presented (Tables 3 and 2). All reviewed studies used different ELISA kits for mBDNF detection. Based on the studies reviewed, no recommendations specific to the collection and preparation of blood samples or the quantification of mBDNF concentration for individuals living with a tSCI can be established. In addition, the sampling methods used by the reviewed manuscripts do not adhere to methods commonly used within the non-SCI population [38–41], diminishing the applicability and usability of the reported mBDNF data for adults with tSCI.

Within the non-SCI population, standard practices for collection, preparation, and quantification of mBDNF have begun to emerge [38–41]. These common methods indicate that when sampling serum mBDNF, participants should be in a fasting state, collection should occur in the morning (8 am to 12 pm), and samples should be collected in serum separator tubes, left to clot (room temperature or 4 °C [fridge]) for a short period (30 min to 1 h), centrifuged, and aliquoted into smaller tubes and stored (− 80 °C) until analyzed. These methods enforce standardization across participants, reducing the likelihood of confounding variables, while allowing for the maximum amount of mBDNF to be released from the platelets into the serum to be measured [48]. In addition to sampling methods, due to the high sensitivity of mBDNF in the periphery, variation in participant characteristics can influence concentration [40]. Ensuring a detailed assessment of each participant’s profile are collected, including demographics (e.g., gender, age, and body mass index) and lifestyle (e.g., diet, smoking status, alcohol consumption, urbanicity, and stress) will allow robust statistical models to be created, controlling for variance created by the individual [23]. The sensitivity of mBDNF highlights the importance of standardized sampling methods, and robust participant background assessments in the formulation of normative data ranges and the future use of the biomarker as an indicator of health within the tSCI population.

Establishing normative ranges for biomarkers is crucial as it allows clinicians and researchers to understand and compare biomarkers across varying and diverse populations. Due to the sampling limitations of the reviewed studies, we were unable to make comparisons between studies or the two populations (non-SCI and tSCI populations). Although Mayr et al. [37] included a non-exercise tSCI-specific control group, no across-tSCI group matching was implemented to account for any nuances related to each individual’s injury (e.g., level of injury and time since injury). Within non-SCI healthy Caucasian adults, a summation of the current literature estimates the normative value of mBDNF to be between 8 and 46 ng/ml, with the average between 18 and 26 ng/ml [23]. As discussed, an individual’s specific characteristics can positively or negatively influence mBDNF levels. Therefore, to use the reported normative values as a population benchmark, all testing variables would need to match between test groups except for the specific variable hypothesized to influence BDNF.

Clinical implications for BDNF within the TSCI population

The lack of methodological and reporting consistencies between the reviewed studies does not yet allow mBDNF to be established as a marker of CMD and MH risk within the tSCI population. However, based on the emerging body of evidence within the non-injured population, there are strong clinical implications for mBDNF’s potential to direct future tSCI research. Within non-SCI populations, prior reports have established a bi-directional relationship between CMD and MH disorders such as depression [49]. Epidemiological data on the co-occurrence of CMD and depression report a 1.5-fold higher risk of developing disease if one is already present [50]. Moreover, Bus, Molendijk et al. [22] expanded on disease development and discussed the expedition of disease progression when both CMD and depression are present, leading to increased mortality. More recently, researchers have reported a trend between low levels of mBDNF in individuals with either CMD[51] or depression [52], with significantly lower levels in individuals reporting both [53].

Effects of exercise on BDNF in tSCI

Although it was not the aim of the current review, it is important to acknowledge the relationship between mBDNF, exercise, and neurorecovery in SCI. A recent systematic review examined the impact of exercise on mBDNF in SCI [54], summarizing six studies that explored mBDNF changes in response to various forms of exercise, including a wheelchair half-marathon [55], activity-based training [56], body-weight-supported treadmill training [56–58], whole-body vibration [56], and hand biking [59]. The findings were heterogeneous, with some studies reporting no significant alterations in serum mBDNF concentration [56], while others documented increases in serum mBDNF concentration following exercise [58]. mBDNF response variability appears to be influenced by factors such as exercise intensity[58] and level of injury [55]. Despite these findings, several methodological challenges across studies limit their broader applicability. While cardiorespiratory and physiological measures such as oxygen consumption, blood lactate, cortisol, and IGF-1 were included [59], these were assessed over short pre- and post-exercise timeframes, failing to capture long-term cardiometabolic risk markers, which was the aim of our current review. In addition, these studies demonstrate similar inconsistencies and poor methodological controls in mBDNF sampling protocols such as variability in assay kits, results falling outside detection ranges, and differing or absent fasting protocols. These methodological limitations underscore the need for standardized approaches in future research to better understand the role of exercise in modulating mBDNF and the impact on MH and CMD risk for individuals with SCI.

Future direction for BDNF in tSCI

Moving forward, the established relationships and sampling techniques within the non-SCI population should frame future research development within the tSCI community. Establishing normative values and understanding how the nuances of tSCI influence the sensitivity and variability of mBDNF would have strong clinical implications for the detection of common comorbidities, which can adversely influence the quality and longevity of life within the tSCI community. The neurotrophin could also offer an indicator of improvement to determine whether a physical therapy (exercise) or pharmacotherapy is effective in reducing disease risk for individuals living with a tSCI. Establishing the clinical utility of mBDNF begins by identifying normative ranges within the diverse SCI population, which starts with the identification of factors leading to sample variation. The process will require researchers to adopt meticulous sampling methods mirroring the established non-SCI literature, while adhering to tight SCI controls, accounting for the specificity of each individual’s injury. By establishing normative ranges based upon data collected via SCI-specific sampling techniques, there is the possibility that mBDNF could be used as a marker of health and disease identification similar to non-injured populations.

Limitations of the reviewed studies

The significant heterogeneity in study design, small sample sizes, inconsistency or undisclosed serum preparation methodology does not allow for direct statistical comparisons of studies, nor does it allow us to infer possible relationships in comparison to the non-SCI literature. A major concern in the reviewed studies is the lack of methodological consistency and reporting for the collection and preparation of blood sample and quantification of mBDNF concentrations. With the emergence of measurement and evaluation standards for mBDNF in the non-injured population [23], investigators that examine mBDNF in SCI cohorts should adhere to these standards. Unfortunately, in the reviewed studies. Li, Cui et al. [31] did not report any of their sampling methods. Sadowska-Krepa, Zwierzchowska et al. [35] stated samples were left to clot and then centrifuged but did not provide any specific clotting time or centrifuge speed details, both of which can affect the reportable amount of mBDNF [48], and limited methodological details were available from Mayr et al. [37], even upon request, which brings into question the rigor of their methodological processes. Sadowska-Krepa, Zwierzchowska et al. [35] also suggest blood samples were collected in heparinized tubes, which, if correct, would severely influence the ability of the samples to clot, negatively influencing the ability to measure serum mBDNF. In addition, we can only assume Sadowska-Krepa, Zwierzchowska et al. [35] have reported their mBDNF findings with the incorrect unit of measurement, as their findings are three times higher than extreme outliers in the non-SCI population (80 ng/ml compared to 228.9 ng/ml).

In addition to the heterogeneity in sampling techniques, all reviewed studies used different ELISA kits. An ELISA kit is a common laboratory testing technique to detect the concentration of certain antibodies, antigens, proteins, and hormones in sampled body fluid. Each ELISA kit has specific protocols that must be followed to ensure the reliability and validity of the results it produces. The kits also have normative ranges, meaning results outside of these ranges may not be an accurate representation of the concentration of a certain product within the body fluid. Due to the lack of methodological clarity, it is unclear whether Li, Cui et al. [31] or Sadowska-Krepa, Zwierzchowska et al. [35] adhered to ELISA kit protocols. Further, the mBDNF values reported by Sadowska-Krepa, Zwierzchowska et al. [35], Grassner, Klein et al. [33], and Mayr et al. [37] are outside the normative ranges deemed valid and reliable by their selected kit. In addition to the relatively small combined sample size across the four studies, the wide array of age ranges provided limited scope to assess the impact of selection criteria on mBDNF variability. Further, the evidence is observational in nature and cannot be extrapolated to support conclusions of cause and effect. Due to methodological inconsistencies across sampling and testing procedures, and the small yet broad age range within the sampled population, comparisons within and between the reviewed studies and across the larger body of mBDNF literature in the non-SCI populations cannot be done at this time.

Limitation of the literature review

To our knowledge, this is the first systematic review that has investigated the links between CMD, MH disorders, and mBDNF within the tSCI population. Although the results from the reviewed literature are limited and unclear, we must also note the limitations of our review. Our research question was specific to mBDNF and its relationship to CMD and MH, which may have led to a limited number of research papers being available for review. We excluded studies that investigated neural recovery and neural plasticity. Although the focus of this review was the comorbidity and complications of CMD and MH disorders post tSCI, excluding neurorecovery and rehabilitation may have reduced our insight regarding the role of mBDNF in tSCI. The prevalence of CMD [60] and MH disorders [27] is extensively reported within the greater SCI literature but was not reported by the reviewed studies, and therefore the reported mBDNF levels should not be extrapolated to individuals living with an tSCI and a confirmed MH or CMD diagnosis. In addition, based on the findings of this review, we were unable to determine whether a tri-directional relationship exists between serum mBDNF, CMD, and MH as established in the non-SCI literature. We limited our primary outcome to serum mBDNF, which improves across-study comparability, as it is the most widely used form of BDNF within the tSCI literature, but does not adequately capture the full complexity of BDNF biology. The lack of clinical phenotype reporting (e.g., injury level, muscle characteristics, and muscle fiber compositions) limited our ability to explore potential myokine-related mechanisms. Lastly, we excluded all research that was not published in English. With tSCI being a global issue, it is possible that relevant information was excluded. However, dissemination of information in languages other than English would have been outside the available skill set of the reviewing authors.

Conclusion

The findings of this systematic review highlight the need for further investigations in SCI populations aimed at establishing relationships between CMD and MH disorders’ risk and mBDNF. Second, the review revealed great inconsistencies with the collection and preparation of blood samples and the quantification of mBDNF concentration, limiting the applicability and interpretability of the published literature. From the reviewed literature, there is no established standardized testing procedures, nor population-based reference ranges for mBDNF within individuals living with tSCI. By improving methodological consistency, identifying how tSCI specifically influence the concentration of mBDNF would pave the way for the development of normative ranges in the tSCI population, allowing researchers to later explore the relationships between mBDNF, CMD, and MH in tSCI.
