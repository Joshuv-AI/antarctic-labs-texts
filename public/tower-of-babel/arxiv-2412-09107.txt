Divisibility of the multiplicative order modulo monic irreducible polynomials over finite fields
Joaquim Cera Da Conceição

Abstract
. We consider the set of monic irreducible polynomials P \displaystyle P over a finite field 𝔽 q \displaystyle\mathbb{F}_{q} such that the multiplicative order modulo P \displaystyle P of some a \displaystyle a in 𝔽 q ​ ( T ) \displaystyle\mathbb{F}_{q}(T) is divisible by a fixed positive integer d \displaystyle d . Call R q ​ ( a , d ) \displaystyle R_{q}(a,d) this set. We show the existence or non-existence of the density of R q ​ ( a , d ) \displaystyle R_{q}(a,d) for three distinct notions of density. In particular, the sets R q ​ ( a , d ) \displaystyle R_{q}(a,d) have a Dirichlet density. Under some assumptions, we prove simple formulas for the density values.

1.
Introduction

Let
\displaystyle a\in\mathbb{Q}\setminus\{0,\pm 1\}
and
\displaystyle d
be a positive integer. The proportion of rational prime numbers
\displaystyle p
such that
\displaystyle d
divides the multiplicative order of
\displaystyle a
modulo
\displaystyle p
has been widely studied. It was originally considered by Hasse
[
7
,
8
]
in
\displaystyle 1965
and
\displaystyle 1966
, with
\displaystyle a
a square-free integer and
\displaystyle d
a prime number, in order to find the natural density of the set of primes dividing the sequence
\displaystyle(1+a^{n}+\cdots+a^{(d-1)n})_{n\geq 0}
at some
\displaystyle n\geq 0
. The problem was completely solved when Wiertelak
[
19
]
proved the following theorem:

Theorem 1
.

Let
\displaystyle N_{a}(d)
be the set of prime numbers
\displaystyle p
such that
\displaystyle d
divides the multiplicative order of
\displaystyle a
modulo
\displaystyle p
and
\displaystyle N_{a}(d;x)=\#N_{a}(d)\cap[1,x]
. Then

N_{a}(d;x)=\delta_{a}(d)\mathrm{Li}(x)+\mathcal{O}_{d,a}\bigg(\frac{x(\log{\log{x}})^{\omega(d)+1}}{(\log{x})^{3}}\bigg),

where the implied constant depends on
\displaystyle d
and
\displaystyle a
,
\displaystyle\mathrm{Li}
is the logarithmic integral function,
\displaystyle\omega
is the number of distinct-prime-divisor function, and
\displaystyle\delta_{a}(d)\in[0,1]
is the natural density of
\displaystyle N_{a}(d)
.

Moreover, Wiertelak gave a formula for
\displaystyle\delta_{a}(d)
that shows that
\displaystyle\delta_{a}(d)\in\mathbb{Q}_{>0}
. More recently, Pappalardi
[
13
]
took a different approach to this problem and obtained another equivalent formula for the density. It was given in a more compact form by Moree
[
12
]
, using a similar method. Note that in some cases, the set
\displaystyle N_{a}(d)
is also related to sets of prime divisors of some linear integral sequences. See
[
1
, Chapter 3]
for the definition of such sequences and the computation of some of the densities.

There is an analogy between
\displaystyle\mathbb{Z}
and the ring of polynomials
\displaystyle A=\mathbb{F}_{q}[T]
with positive characteristic
\displaystyle p
. Both are euclidean rings in which prime numbers and monic irreducible polynomials are prime elements. The analogue of
\displaystyle\mathbb{Q}
is the fraction field of
\displaystyle A
, that is,
\displaystyle K=\mathbb{F}_{q}(T)
. With the above problem in mind, it is natural to ask whether a similar investigation can be conducted for monic irreducible polynomials in
\displaystyle A
. This is the object of our paper. Let
\displaystyle a\in K^{\times}
and
\displaystyle d
be a positive integer. We define
\displaystyle R_{q}(a,d)
to be the set of primes
\displaystyle P\in A
such that the multiplicative order of
\displaystyle a
in the group
\displaystyle(A/P)^{\times}
is divisible by
\displaystyle d
. To our knowledge, the only instance of study of these sets are recent papers of Ballot
[
2
,
3
]
in which the case
\displaystyle a=T
and
\displaystyle d
a prime number is treated with elementary methods. To determine the proportion of such primes, we use prime densities on
\displaystyle A
. Let
\displaystyle S\subset A
be a set of monic irreducible polynomials and
\displaystyle S(N)
be the number of
\displaystyle P\in S
with polynomial degree
\displaystyle N
,
\displaystyle N\geq 1
. The most common densities are the
\displaystyle d_{1}
and
\displaystyle\delta
densities defined, when they exist, by the limits

d_{1}(S)=\lim_{N\to+\infty}\frac{S(N)}{\mathcal{P}_{+}(N)}\quad\text{and}\quad\delta(S)=\lim_{s\to 1^{+}}\frac{\sum_{P\in S}NP^{-s}}{\sum_{P\in\mathcal{P}_{+}}NP^{-s}}.

The letter
\displaystyle\mathcal{P}_{+}
denotes the set of monic irreducible polynomials in
\displaystyle A
and
\displaystyle NP=q^{\deg(P)}
is the norm of
\displaystyle P
. The quantity
\displaystyle\mathcal{P}_{+}(N)
is usually denoted
\displaystyle I_{N}
and is given by the sum

I_{N}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{d\mid N}\mu(d)q^{N/d}.

The number
\displaystyle\delta(S)
is called the
Dirichlet density
of
\displaystyle S
and is the analogue of the Dirichlet density used for rational prime numbers. However, in a discussion about prime densities on
\displaystyle A
, Ballot
[
4
]
defines five densities
\displaystyle d_{1},d_{2},d_{3},d_{4}
and
\displaystyle\delta
, and concludes two things. Denoting by
\displaystyle\delta_{1}\implies\delta_{2}
the fact that any set of primes in
\displaystyle A
having a
\displaystyle\delta_{1}
-density equal to
\displaystyle d
must have a
\displaystyle\delta_{2}
-density equal to
\displaystyle d
,
[
4
, Theorem A]
states the following:

d_{1}\iff d_{2}\implies d_{3}\implies d_{4}\iff\delta.

Moreover,
\displaystyle d_{3}
is not equivalent to
\displaystyle d_{2}
, nor
\displaystyle d_{4}
. In conclusion, there are three distinct densities to be considered. In this paper, we consider
\displaystyle d_{1}
,
\displaystyle\delta
and the
\displaystyle d_{3}
-density defined by the limit

d_{3}(S)=\lim_{N\to+\infty}\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\frac{S(n)}{I_{n}}=\lim_{N\to+\infty}\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\frac{S(n)}{q^{n}/n},

when it exists. Note that the second equality comes from the well-known equivalence
\displaystyle I_{n}\sim q^{n}/n
as
\displaystyle n
tends to infinity. Secondly, although there is some evidence of
\displaystyle d_{1}
being an analogue of the natural density commonly used on
\displaystyle\mathbb{N}
, Ballot concludes that
\displaystyle d_{3}
seems to be a better candidate. Indeed, various sets of rational prime numbers that are known to have natural density have analogues in
\displaystyle A
that do not have
\displaystyle d_{1}
-density but have
\displaystyle d_{3}
-density. In this work, we prove that the set
\displaystyle R_{q}(a,d)
does not usually have
\displaystyle d_{1}
-density, but always has
\displaystyle d_{3}
-density, thus confirming
\displaystyle d_{3}
as a strong analogue of the natural density. Our work is based on the method used by Pappalardi
[
13
]
and Moree
[
12
]
, and on the elementary approach taken by Ballot
[
2
,
3
]
.

Furthermore, this problem is closely related to Artin’s primitive root conjecture over function fields. Let
\displaystyle a\in K\setminus\mathbb{F}_{q}
not be an
\displaystyle l
-th power for all
\displaystyle l\mid q-1
. The conjecture states that there exist infinitely many
\displaystyle P\in\mathcal{P}_{+}
such that
\displaystyle a
is a primitive root modulo
\displaystyle P
, that is,
\displaystyle a
has order
\displaystyle NP-1
in
\displaystyle(A/P)^{\times}
. The conjecture has been widely studied in the function field setting. It was first proven by Bilharz
[
5
]
under the generalized Riemann hypothesis for function fields, which was later proved by Weil
[
18
]
. In his proof, Bilharz shows that the set
\displaystyle S(a)
of primes
\displaystyle P
that have
\displaystyle a
as a primitive root has positive Dirichlet density. Another proof was given in 1994 by Pappalardi and Shparlinski
[
14
]
by estimating the number of
\displaystyle P\in\mathcal{P}_{+}
of degree
\displaystyle n\geq 1
that satisfy the conjecture. More recently, Kim and Murty
[
10
]
managed to prove Artin’s conjecture without using the generalized Riemann hypothesis for function fields. Moreover, note that
\displaystyle S(T)
has positive
\displaystyle d_{3}
-density. It was shown by Ballot
[
4
]
through a theorem of Shparlinski
[
15
, Theorem 3]
. Using Theorem
7
with
\displaystyle d=q^{N}-1
for all
\displaystyle N\geq 1
, we see that
\displaystyle d_{3}(S(a))
can be estimated with
[
15
, Theorem 3]
, or some variation of it, in a similar way, thus showing that
\displaystyle S(a)
has positive
\displaystyle d_{3}
-density.

In Section
2
, we give definitions and results related to function fields that make up our main toolbox. An important tool is an analogue of the Chebotarev Density Theorem for global function fields. Another gives necessary and sufficient conditions for primes in a global function field
\displaystyle K
to completely split in a Galois extension
\displaystyle L/K
.

The idea is to describe
\displaystyle R_{q}(a,d)
as a union of sets of primes that completely split in Kummer extensions of
\displaystyle K=\mathbb{F}_{q}(T)
, i.e., fields of the form
\displaystyle K(\zeta_{n},a^{1/d})
, where
\displaystyle d,n
are positive integers such that
\displaystyle(n,p)=1
and
\displaystyle d\mid n
,
\displaystyle\zeta_{n}\in\bar{\mathbb{F}}_{q}
is a primitive
\displaystyle n
-th root of unity and
\displaystyle a\in K^{\times}
. From this and the above-mentioned analogue of Chebotarev’s Density Theorem, we obtain an asymptotic formula for
\displaystyle R_{q}(a,d,N):=R_{q}(a,d)(N)
that involves degrees of Kummer extensions. Therefore, to make our formula simpler and to later compute a closed-form formula for the density, we need to determine the degree of Kummer extensions of
\displaystyle K
. In Section
3
, we study the form an element
\displaystyle a\in K^{\times}
may take when
\displaystyle K(a^{1/n})/K
is a constant field extension, i.e., an extension of
\displaystyle\mathbb{F}_{q}
. These results are our primary tools for computing degrees of Kummer extensions in Section
4
.

In Section
5
, we show that
\displaystyle R_{q}(a,d,N)
may be expressed in terms of the cardinality of sets of primes that completely split in some Kummer extensions of
\displaystyle K
. Applying the analogue of the Chebotarev Density Theorem, we find an asymptotic formula for
\displaystyle R_{q}(a,d,N)
of the form

|R_{q}(a,d,N)-\delta_{q}(a,d,N)\cdot q^{N}/N|\ll f(N),

for some function
\displaystyle f
and where
\displaystyle\delta_{q}(a,d,N)
is the
proportion-density
of
\displaystyle R_{q}(a,d,N)
. (See Theorem
7
.) We obtain a formula for
\displaystyle\delta_{q}(a,d,N)
that involves degrees of Kummer extensions of
\displaystyle K
.

Section
6
is dedicated to preliminary results for the proofs of the main theorems. We prove a formula for the multiplicative order of an integer, and another for integers of the form
\displaystyle q^{n}-1
, where
\displaystyle n,q\geq 1
are integers. Moreover, we give a property of the degree of constant fields of some special Kummer extensions.

Our main results, Theorem
8
and Theorem
9
, on the existence or non-existence of the
\displaystyle d_{1}
and
\displaystyle d_{3}
-densities of
\displaystyle R_{q}(a,d)
are proved in Section
7
. The asymptotic formula given in Theorem
9
revolves around a technique used by Ballot that consists of partitioning
\displaystyle\mathbb{N}
into adequate disjoint arithmetic progressions. For all
\displaystyle n\geq 1
that belong in the same arithmetic progression, we find that
\displaystyle\delta_{q}(a,d,n)
is a constant independent of
\displaystyle n
, thus simplifying most calculations.

Under some assumption on the degree of constant fields of some Kummer extensions, we prove in Section
8
that
\displaystyle d_{3}(R_{q}(a,d))
can be written in a closed-form formula. (See Theorem
10
.)

2.
Known results

We use this section to state two important results for our work. The first theorem is a special case of a theorem that takes various forms in literature. It is usually referred to as the Chebotarev Density Theorem for global function fields. (See
[
6
, Proposition 6.4.8]
.) It gives a bound on the number of primes in
\displaystyle K
of a fixed degree satisfying a certain property. Since there are finitely many primes of degree
\displaystyle N
, the name “density” is not the most accurate. We use the term
proportion-density
instead to refer to the “density” number described in the theorem. We denote by
\displaystyle g_{L}
the genus of a field
\displaystyle L
and by
\displaystyle\mathcal{P}_{+}
the set of monic irreducible polynomials in
\displaystyle A
.

Theorem 2
.

Let
\displaystyle L/K
be a Galois extension of global function fields and
\displaystyle\mathbb{F}_{q^{n}}
be the constant field of
\displaystyle L
. Put
\displaystyle m:=[L:\mathbb{F}_{q^{n}}K]
and

\pi(N):=\#\{P\in\mathcal{P}_{+}:\deg(P)=N\text{ and }P\text{ splits completely in }L\}.

Then
\displaystyle\pi(N)=0
if
\displaystyle n\nmid N
, and otherwise, we have

\bigg|\pi(N)-\frac{q^{N}}{Nm}\bigg|\leq\frac{2}{Nm}\bigg((m+g_{L})q^{N/2}+mq^{N/4}+g_{L}+m\bigg).

The second result gives necessary and sufficient conditions for a prime to split completely in some Kummer extensions of
\displaystyle K
.

Theorem 3
.

Let
\displaystyle n\geq 1
be an integer with
\displaystyle p\nmid n
and
\displaystyle a\in K^{\times}
. A prime
\displaystyle P\in A
such that
\displaystyle v_{P}(a)=0
splits completely in
\displaystyle K(\zeta_{n},a^{1/d})
if and only if

NP\equiv 1\pmod{n}\quad\text{and}\quad a^{\frac{NP-1}{d}}\equiv 1\pmod{P}.

Proof.

It suffices to follow the proof of
[
16
, Proposition 10.6]
.
∎

3.
On constant field extensions

Let
\displaystyle M/L
be an algebraic extension of function fields. We say that
\displaystyle M
is a constant field extension of
\displaystyle L
if
\displaystyle M=(M\cap\bar{\mathbb{F}}_{q})L
. Similarly, we say that
\displaystyle M
is a geometric extension of
\displaystyle L
if
\displaystyle M\neq L
and
\displaystyle M\cap\bar{\mathbb{F}}_{q}=L\cap\bar{\mathbb{F}}_{q}
. Note that it is likely that
\displaystyle M/L
is neither a geometric nor a constant field extension, but we can always split it in two such extensions. In this section, we give necessary and sufficient conditions for an algebraic extension
\displaystyle K(a^{1/n})/K
,
\displaystyle a\in K^{\times}
, to be a constant field extension.

Theorem 4
.

Let
\displaystyle a\in K^{\times}
. Then,
\displaystyle K(a^{1/n})/K
is a constant field extension if and only if
\displaystyle a=\mu b^{n}
for some
\displaystyle b\in K^{\times}
and
\displaystyle\mu\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\times}
.

Proof.

The “if” part is trivial. For the converse, we start with the case
\displaystyle a\in A
. First assume that the result holds for prime powers and write
\displaystyle n=q_{1}\cdots q_{s}
, where the
\displaystyle q_{i}
’s are powers of distinct primes and
\displaystyle s\geq 2
. We have

a=\mu_{1}b_{1}^{q_{1}}=\cdots=\mu_{s}b_{s}^{q_{s}}\quad\text{and}\quad\tilde{a}=\tilde{b}_{1}^{q_{1}}=\cdots=\tilde{b}_{s}^{q_{s}},

for some non-zero
\displaystyle b_{i}\in A
and
\displaystyle\mu_{i}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\times}
. Since the
\displaystyle q_{i}
’s are powers of distinct primes, we see that
\displaystyle\tilde{b}_{1}\in(K^{\times})^{q_{i}}
for all
\displaystyle 1\leq i\leq s
. Thus,
\displaystyle\tilde{a}=b^{n}
for some non-zero
\displaystyle b\in A
, and
\displaystyle a=\mu\tilde{a}=\mu b^{n}
. Hence it suffices to prove the result for prime powers. Let
\displaystyle l
be a prime number and
\displaystyle k\geq 1
. We proceed by induction on
\displaystyle k\geq 1
to show the statement holds for all
\displaystyle n=l^{k}
. The base case follows from
[
9
, Lemma 3.3]
. Assume the statement holds for some
\displaystyle k\geq 1
and that
\displaystyle K(a^{1/l^{k+1}})/K
is a constant field extension. In particular,
\displaystyle K(a^{1/l})/K
a constant field extension and we may write
\displaystyle a=\mu b^{l}
by
[
9
, Lemma 3.3]
. Let
\displaystyle x
be an
\displaystyle l^{k+1}
-th root of
\displaystyle a
in
\displaystyle L:=K(a^{1/l^{k+1}})
. Then

\tilde{a}=\tilde{b}^{l}=\tilde{x}^{l^{k+1}},

and we obtain
\displaystyle\tilde{b}=\zeta_{l}\tilde{x}^{l^{k}}
for some
\displaystyle l
-th root of unity
\displaystyle\zeta_{l}
. But
\displaystyle\tilde{b}
and
\displaystyle\tilde{x}
are both monic polynomials in the ring of integers of
\displaystyle L
, so that
\displaystyle\zeta_{l}=1
. Because
\displaystyle\tilde{x}\in L
, we find that
\displaystyle K(\tilde{b}^{1/l^{k}})
is a subfield of
\displaystyle L
, thus
\displaystyle K(\tilde{b}^{1/l^{k}})/K
is a constant field extension. By the induction hypothesis, we have
\displaystyle\tilde{b}=\lambda c^{l^{k}}=\tilde{c}^{l^{k}}
for some
\displaystyle\lambda\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\times}
and
\displaystyle c\in K^{\times}
. Hence
\displaystyle\tilde{a}=\tilde{b}^{l}=\tilde{c}^{l^{k+1}}
and
\displaystyle a=\mu\tilde{c}^{l^{k+1}}
, where
\displaystyle\mu\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\times}
is the leading coefficient of
\displaystyle a
. We successfully proved the result for
\displaystyle a\in A
. If
\displaystyle a=f/g\in K^{\times}
, then
\displaystyle K(a^{1/n})=K((fg^{n-1})^{1/n})
and by the above,
\displaystyle fg^{n-1}
is of the form
\displaystyle\mu b^{n}
. Hence
\displaystyle a=\mu(b/g)^{n}
.
∎

4.
The degree of Kummer extensions of rational function fields

We extend the notation
\displaystyle\tilde{a}
to rational functions
\displaystyle a=f/g\in K^{\times}
by
\displaystyle\tilde{a}=\tilde{f}/\tilde{g}
. We denote by
\displaystyle\lambda\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\times}
the unique constant such that
\displaystyle a=\lambda\tilde{a}
. Let
\displaystyle h
denote the largest integer
\displaystyle t\geq 1
such that
\displaystyle\tilde{a}\in(K^{\times})^{t}
. In this section, we study Kummer extensions of
\displaystyle K=\mathbb{F}_{q}(T)
, i.e., fields of the form
\displaystyle K(\zeta_{n},a^{1/d})
, where
\displaystyle\zeta_{n}\in\bar{\mathbb{F}}_{q}
is a primitive
\displaystyle n
-th root of unity and
\displaystyle a\in K^{\times}
. We find formulas for the following field degrees:

[K(\zeta_{n},a^{1/d}):\mathbb{F}_{n,d}K]\quad\text{and}\quad[\mathbb{F}_{n,d}:\mathbb{F}_{q}],

where
\displaystyle\mathbb{F}_{n,d}
denotes the constant field of
\displaystyle K(\zeta_{n},a^{1/d})
. The key result we use is the following theorem on polynomials of the form
\displaystyle X^{n}-a
:

Theorem 5
.

Let
\displaystyle K
be a field and
\displaystyle a\in K^{\times}
. Then
\displaystyle X^{n}-a
is irreducible over
\displaystyle K
if and only if
\displaystyle a\not\in(K^{\times})^{l}
for all
\displaystyle l\mid n
and
\displaystyle a\not\in-4(K^{\times})^{4}
if
\displaystyle 4\mid n
.

Proof.

See
[
11
, Theorem 9.1]
.
∎

Lemma 1
.

Let
\displaystyle\mu\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\times}
. We have

[\mathbb{F}_{q}(\zeta_{n},\mu^{1/d}):\mathbb{F}_{q}]=\frac{\mathrm{ord}_{n}(q)d}{(\mathrm{ind}_{\mathbb{F}_{q}(\zeta_{n})^{\times}}(\mu),d)}.

Proof.

It is known that
\displaystyle[\mathbb{F}_{q}(\zeta_{n}):\mathbb{F}_{q}]=\mathrm{ord}_{n}(q)
. Let
\displaystyle u
be the index of
\displaystyle\mu
in
\displaystyle\mathbb{F}_{q}(\zeta_{n})^{\times}
, that is, the greatest positive divisor
\displaystyle t\mid q^{\mathrm{ord}_{n}(q)}-1
such that
\displaystyle\mu=x^{t}
for some
\displaystyle x\in\mathbb{F}_{q}(\zeta_{n})^{\times}
. We have
\displaystyle\mathbb{F}_{q}(\zeta_{n},\mu^{1/d})=\mathbb{F}_{q}(\zeta_{n},v^{1/d_{0}})
, where
\displaystyle d_{0}=d/(d,u)
and
\displaystyle v^{(u,d)}=\mu
. We claim that
\displaystyle d_{0}
is the degree of the extension
\displaystyle\mathbb{F}_{q}(\zeta_{n},\mu^{1/d})/\mathbb{F}_{q}(\zeta_{n})
. Indeed, let us show that
\displaystyle X^{d_{0}}-v
is irreducible over
\displaystyle\mathbb{F}_{q}(\zeta_{n})
using Theorem
5
. Let
\displaystyle l\mid d_{0}
be a prime. By contradiction, if we have
\displaystyle v=c^{l}
for some
\displaystyle c\in\mathbb{F}_{q}(\zeta_{n})
, then

x^{u}=\mu=v^{(u,d)}=c^{l(u,d)}.

Because
\displaystyle d_{0}\mid q^{\mathrm{ord}_{n}(q)}-1
and by the maximality of
\displaystyle u
, we have
\displaystyle l(u,d)\mid u
. This yields a contradiction since
\displaystyle l\mid d_{0}
. Now, if
\displaystyle 4\mid d_{0}
, assume by contradiction that
\displaystyle v=-4y^{4}
for some
\displaystyle y\in\mathbb{F}_{q}(\zeta_{n})
. Then, since
\displaystyle v
is not a square in
\displaystyle\mathbb{F}_{q}(\zeta_{n})
by the above, we find that
\displaystyle-1
is not a square in
\displaystyle\mathbb{F}_{q}(\zeta_{n})
. Hence
\displaystyle 4\nmid q^{\mathrm{ord}_{n}(q)}-1
, but
\displaystyle 4\mid d_{0}
and
\displaystyle d_{0}\mid n
imply that
\displaystyle 4\mid q^{\mathrm{ord}_{n}(q)}-1
. A contradiction.
∎

Lemma 2
.

Let
\displaystyle a\in K^{\times}
. Then the greatest divisor
\displaystyle v
of
\displaystyle d
such that the extension
\displaystyle K(\zeta_{n},a^{1/v})/K
is a constant field extension is equal to
\displaystyle(d,h)
. In particular, we have
\displaystyle\mathbb{F}_{n,d}=\mathbb{F}_{q}(\zeta_{n},\lambda^{1/(d,h)})
.

Proof.

We claim that
\displaystyle K(\zeta_{n},a^{1/D})
, with
\displaystyle D=(d,h)
, is the maximal subfield
\displaystyle M
of
\displaystyle K(\zeta_{n},a^{1/d})
such that
\displaystyle M/K
is a constant field extension. Write
\displaystyle h=Dk
and
\displaystyle\tilde{a}=\tilde{b}^{Dk}
for some
\displaystyle k\geq 1
and
\displaystyle\tilde{b}\in K^{\times}
. Then

K(\zeta_{n},a^{1/D})=K(\zeta_{n},\lambda^{1/D}\tilde{b}^{k})=K(\zeta_{n},\lambda^{1/D})=\mathbb{F}_{q}(\zeta_{n},\lambda^{1/D})K

is a constant field extension of
\displaystyle K
. Next, we prove that
\displaystyle D
is maximal. By contradiction, assume there exists a prime
\displaystyle l
such that
\displaystyle lD\mid d
and that
\displaystyle K(\zeta_{n},a^{1/lD})
is a constant field extension of
\displaystyle K
. Then,
\displaystyle K(a^{1/lD})/K
is a constant field extension and

a=\omega c^{lD}=\lambda\tilde{c}^{lD},

for some
\displaystyle\omega,\lambda\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\times}
and
\displaystyle c\in K^{\times}
, by Theorem
4
. Hence
\displaystyle\tilde{a}=\tilde{c}^{lD}
and, by maximality of
\displaystyle h
, we find that
\displaystyle lD\mid h
. A contradiction to
\displaystyle lD\mid d
.
∎

Theorem 6
.

Let
\displaystyle a\in K^{\times}
. We have
\displaystyle[K(\zeta_{n},a^{1/d}):\mathbb{F}_{n,d}K]=d/(d,h)
.

Proof.

Put
\displaystyle d_{0}=d/(d,h)
and write
\displaystyle a=b^{D}
, where
\displaystyle D=(d,h)
and
\displaystyle b\in\mathbb{F}_{n,d}K^{\times}
. The latter is possible since
\displaystyle a=\lambda\tilde{c}^{D}
for some
\displaystyle c\in K^{\times}
, by Lemma
2
and Theorem
4
. Thus
\displaystyle a=(\mu\tilde{c})^{D}
with
\displaystyle\mu\in\mathbb{F}_{n,d}
and
\displaystyle\mu^{D}=\lambda
. Using Theorem
5
, we show that the polynomial
\displaystyle X^{d_{0}}-b
is irreducible over
\displaystyle\mathbb{F}_{n,d}K^{\times}
. Let
\displaystyle l\mid d_{0}
be a prime and assume by contradiction that
\displaystyle b=x^{l}
for some
\displaystyle x\in\mathbb{F}_{n,d}K^{\times}
. Since
\displaystyle\mathbb{F}_{n,d}K
is a rational function field and because
\displaystyle a\in A
, we find that
\displaystyle x\in\mathbb{F}_{n,d}[T]
. Then
\displaystyle\lambda=x_{0}^{lD}
, where
\displaystyle x_{0}\in\mathbb{F}_{n,d}
is the leading coefficient of
\displaystyle x
. However, by Lemma
2
,
\displaystyle D
is the greatest positive integer
\displaystyle t\mid d
such that
\displaystyle\lambda
is a
\displaystyle t
-th power in
\displaystyle\mathbb{F}_{n,d}^{\times}
. Hence we have a contradiction and
\displaystyle b\not\in(\mathbb{F}_{n,d}K^{\times})^{l}
. If
\displaystyle 4\mid d_{0}
, then the rest of the proof follows in the same way as the proof of Lemma
1
.
∎

5.
The proportion-density

Throughout this paper, we denote by
\displaystyle\mathcal{P}_{+}
the set of monic irreducible polynomials in
\displaystyle A
, and by
\displaystyle R_{q}(a,d)
the set of
\displaystyle P\in\mathcal{P}_{+}
that satisfy
\displaystyle v_{P}(a)=0
and
\displaystyle d\mid\mathrm{ord}_{P}(a)
, where
\displaystyle a\in K^{\times}
and
\displaystyle d\geq 1
are fixed. For each
\displaystyle N\geq 1
, we consider the number of primes in
\displaystyle R_{q}(a,d)
with degree
\displaystyle N
, denoted
\displaystyle R_{q}(a,d,N)
. We write
\displaystyle I_{N}=\mathcal{P}_{+}(N)
.

For
\displaystyle d=1
, we trivially have
\displaystyle R_{q}(a,1,N)=I_{N}-a_{N}
, where
\displaystyle a_{N}
is the number of
\displaystyle P
of degree
\displaystyle N
such that
\displaystyle v_{P}(a)\neq 0
. Note that
\displaystyle a_{N}\neq 0
for only finitely many
\displaystyle N
. Moreover, if
\displaystyle a=\lambda\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\times}
, then

R_{q}(\lambda,d,N)=\begin{cases}I_{N},&\text{if }d\mid\mathrm{ord}_{\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\times}}(\lambda);\\
0,&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

Densities are easily computed here. We have
\displaystyle d_{1}(R_{q}(a,1))=1
and it follows that the
\displaystyle d_{i}
’s and the Dirichlet densities also exist and equal
\displaystyle 1
. The same goes for
\displaystyle R_{q}(a,\lambda)
for which the densities are
\displaystyle 1
or
\displaystyle 0
, whether
\displaystyle d\mid\mathrm{ord}_{\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\times}}(\lambda)
.

We assume that
\displaystyle d\geq 2
and
\displaystyle a
is not a constant for the rest of this paper. Since
\displaystyle f:=\mathrm{ord}_{d}(q)\nmid N
implies that
\displaystyle R_{q}(a,d,N)=0
, we only consider integers
\displaystyle N\equiv 0\pmod{f}
. We put

e_{N}(d)=e_{N}:=\bigg(\frac{q^{N}-1}{d},d^{\infty}\bigg).

Since
\displaystyle K
is fixed, we denote by
\displaystyle\{L\}
the set of primes in
\displaystyle K
that splits completely in
\displaystyle L
, where
\displaystyle L/K
is an algebraic extension. For each
\displaystyle N\geq 1
, we denote the number of primes in
\displaystyle\{L\}
with degree
\displaystyle N
by
\displaystyle\{L\}_{N}
. We assume throughout the paper that
\displaystyle a=\lambda\tilde{a}
and
\displaystyle\tilde{a}=b^{h}
, with the notation of the previous section.

Lemma 3
.

For each positive
\displaystyle N\equiv 0\pmod{f}
, we have

R_{q}(a,d,N)=\sum_{v\mid e_{N}}\sum_{u\mid d}\mu(u)\{K_{dv,uv}\}_{N},

Proof.

We follow the proof of
[
12
, Proposition 1]
. Note in particular that the condition
\displaystyle p\leq x
must be replaced by
\displaystyle\deg(P)=N
, and
\displaystyle p\equiv 1\pmod{dv}
by the condition
\displaystyle q^{N}\equiv 1\pmod{dv}
.
∎

Proposition 1
.

There exists
\displaystyle c_{0}>0
, that only depends on
\displaystyle a
, such that

g_{K_{n,d}}\leq c_{0}\cdot[K_{n,d}:\mathbb{F}_{n,d}K].

Proof.

It suffices to apply
[
17
, Proposition 3.7.3]
to
\displaystyle K_{n,d}
.
∎

Lemma 4
.

There exists an absolute constant
\displaystyle c_{1}>0
such that for each
\displaystyle N\geq 1
such that
\displaystyle[\mathbb{F}_{n,d}:\mathbb{F}_{q}]\mid N
, we have

\bigg|\{K_{n,d}\}_{N}-\frac{q^{N}}{N}\frac{1}{[K_{n,d}:\mathbb{F}_{n,d}K]}\bigg|\leq 2c_{1}\cdot\frac{q^{N/2}}{N}.

Proof.

We apply Theorem
2
to
\displaystyle\{K_{n,d}\}_{N}
. The error term is obtained using Proposition
1
and the bounds

\frac{1}{N}\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{q}}\frac{q^{N/2}}{N}\quad\text{and}\quad\frac{q^{N/4}}{N}\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt[4]{q}}\frac{q^{N/2}}{N},

valid for all
\displaystyle N\geq 1
.
∎

Let
\displaystyle\mathcal{P}
be a proposition. Throughout the rest of the paper, we use the Iverson symbol defined by
\displaystyle[\mathcal{P}]=1
if
\displaystyle\mathcal{P}
is true, and
\displaystyle[\mathcal{P}]=0
otherwise. For integers
\displaystyle v\mid d^{\infty}
and
\displaystyle u\mid d
, we let
\displaystyle f_{u,v}=[\mathbb{F}_{dv,uv}:\mathbb{F}_{q}]
, that is,

f_{u,v}=\frac{\mathrm{ord}_{dv}(q)(uv,h)}{(\mathrm{ind}_{\mathbb{F}_{q}(\zeta_{dv})^{\times}}(\lambda),uv,h)},

(1)

by Lemmas
1
and
2
.

Theorem 7
.

For each positive
\displaystyle N\equiv 0\pmod{f}
, we have

\bigg|R_{q}(a,d,N)-\frac{q^{N}}{N}\cdot\delta_{q}(a,d,N)\bigg|\leq 2^{\omega(d)+1}c_{1}\cdot\frac{\tau(e_{N})q^{N/2}}{N},

where
\displaystyle c_{1}
is the constant defined in Lemma
4
and
\displaystyle\delta_{q}(a,d,N)
is the proportion-density defined by

\delta_{q}(a,d,N)=\sum_{v\mid e_{N}}\sum_{u\mid d}\frac{\mu(u)[f_{u,v}\mid N]}{[K_{dv,uv}:\mathbb{F}_{dv,uv}K]}.

Proof.

We let
\displaystyle S_{q}(a,d,N):=R_{q}(a,d,N)-\delta_{q}(a,d,N)\cdot q^{N}/N
. By Lemmas
3
and
4
, we have

|S_{q}(a,d,N)|\leq 2c_{1}\cdot\frac{q^{N/2}}{N}\sum_{v\mid e_{N}}\sum_{u\mid d}|\mu(u)|=2^{\omega(d)+1}c_{1}\cdot\frac{\tau(e_{N})q^{N/2}}{N},

the sought result.
∎

6.
More preliminary results

We show in this section three preliminary results to the study of the
\displaystyle d_{1}
and
\displaystyle d_{3}
-densities of
\displaystyle R_{q}(a,d)
. Our first result gives basic arithmetic properties of certain numbers of the form
\displaystyle(q^{N}-1,d^{\infty})
. As a consequence, we prove a formula for
\displaystyle\mathrm{ord}_{dv}(q)
that generalizes a well-known formula for
\displaystyle\mathrm{ord}_{l^{k}}(q)
, with
\displaystyle k\geq 1
. Finally, we prove that, under the hypothesis
\displaystyle f_{1,v}\mid N
, the Iverson symbol
\displaystyle[f_{u,v}\mid N]
defines a multiplicative function in the variable
\displaystyle u
. We use the letter
\displaystyle\mathcal{P}
to denote the proposition

\mathcal{P}:\quad 2\|d,\quad q\equiv 3\pmod{4}\quad\text{and}\quad 2\nmid f.

The following lemma is enough to see that interesting things might happen when
\displaystyle\mathcal{P}
is true:

Lemma 5
.

Let
\displaystyle m,n,q\geq 1
be integers with
\displaystyle(d,q)=1
and
\displaystyle d\mid q^{m}-1
. Then

(q^{mn}-1,d^{\infty})=(q^{m}-1,d^{\infty})(n,d^{\infty})\cdot\begin{cases}2^{v_{2}(q^{m}+1)-1},&\text{if }[\mathcal{P}]=1\text{ and }2\mid n;\\
1,&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

Proof.

The map
\displaystyle d\mapsto(k,d^{\infty})
, where
\displaystyle k
is a fixed integer, defines a completely multiplicative function. Thus, it suffices to prove the result for
\displaystyle(q^{mn}-1,l^{\infty})
, where
\displaystyle l\mid d
. By
[
3
, Lemma 4]
and
[
2
, Proposition 2.4]
, and by replacing
\displaystyle\mathrm{ord}_{l}(q)
by
\displaystyle m
and
\displaystyle q
by
\displaystyle q^{m}
respectively in the proofs, which is allowed since it only uses that
\displaystyle l\mid q^{m}-1
, we obtain

v_{l}\left(\frac{q^{mn}-1}{q^{m}-1}\right)=v_{l}(n)+\begin{cases}2^{v_{2}(q^{m}+1)-1},&\text{if }l=2\text{ and }2\mid n;\\
1,&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

The result follows by noting that if
\displaystyle\mathcal{P}
is false, then
\displaystyle v_{2}(q^{m}+1)=1
.
∎

Lemma 6
.

Let
\displaystyle q\geq 1
be an integer prime to
\displaystyle d
and
\displaystyle f=\mathrm{ord}_{d}(q)
. Then, for all
\displaystyle v\mid d^{\infty}
, we have

\mathrm{ord}_{dv}(q)=fdv\cdot\begin{cases}\frac{2}{(q^{2f}-1,dv)},&\text{if $\displaystyle[\mathcal{P}]=1$ and }2\mid v;\\
\frac{1}{(q^{f}-1,dv)},&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

Proof.

Assume
\displaystyle v_{2}(d)\neq 1
and put
\displaystyle n=dv/(q^{f}-1,dv)
. By Lemma
5
, we see that
\displaystyle dv\mid(q^{fn}-1,d^{\infty})
. Hence
\displaystyle n=tm
, where
\displaystyle m\geq 1
and
\displaystyle t:=\mathrm{ord}_{dv}(q)/f
. By Lemma
5
, we have

dv\mid(q^{ft}-1,d^{\infty})=(q^{f}-1,d^{\infty})\cdot\frac{dv}{(q^{f}-1,dv)(m,d^{\infty})},

and we find that
\displaystyle m=(m,d^{\infty})
divides
\displaystyle(q^{f}-1,d^{\infty})/(q^{f}-1,dv)
. But the latter is coprime to
\displaystyle n
, which yields that
\displaystyle m=1
. Next, assume that
\displaystyle 2\,\|\,d
and note that for any odd integer
\displaystyle n\geq 1
, we have
\displaystyle\mathrm{ord}_{2n}(q)=\mathrm{ord}_{n}(q)
. Therefore, when
\displaystyle 2\nmid v
, we have
\displaystyle\mathrm{ord}_{dv}(q)=\mathrm{ord}_{dv/2}(q)
and we conclude using what we proved in the above. When
\displaystyle 2\mid v
, put
\displaystyle D=2d
and
\displaystyle u=v/2
so that

\mathrm{ord}_{dv}(q)=\mathrm{ord}_{Du}(q)=\frac{\mathrm{ord}_{D}(q)dv}{(q^{\mathrm{ord}_{D}(q)}-1,dv)},

by the above again. We have
\displaystyle\mathrm{ord}_{D}(q)=[\mathrm{ord}_{4}(q),\mathrm{ord}_{d/2}(q)]=[\mathrm{ord}_{4}(q),f]
because
\displaystyle 2^{2}\|D
, and we conclude using that
\displaystyle\mathrm{ord}_{4}(q)
equals
\displaystyle 1
or
\displaystyle 2
, whether
\displaystyle q\equiv 1\pmod{4}
or
\displaystyle q\equiv 3\pmod{4}
respectively.
∎

Remark 1
.

We know that
\displaystyle f_{u,v}=\mathrm{ord}_{dv}(q)k_{0}
for some
\displaystyle k_{0}\mid d^{\infty}
by (
1
). Hence Lemma
6
shows that
\displaystyle f_{u,v}=fk
for some
\displaystyle k\mid d^{\infty}
.

Lemma 7
.

Let
\displaystyle N\geq 1
be such that
\displaystyle f_{1,v}\mid N
. The function
\displaystyle u\mapsto[f_{u,v}\mid N]
is multiplicative for all
\displaystyle v\mid d^{\infty}
.

Proof.

We want to show that
\displaystyle[f_{u_{1}u_{2},v}\mid N]=[f_{u_{1},v}\mid N]\cdot[f_{u_{2},v}\mid N]
for coprime
\displaystyle u_{1},u_{2}\mid d
, or equivalently,
\displaystyle f_{u_{1}u_{2},v}=[f_{u_{1},v},f_{u_{2},v}]
. Let
\displaystyle r=\mathrm{ord}_{dv}(q)
and
\displaystyle m=\mathrm{ord}_{\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\times}}(\lambda)
. We see that
\displaystyle\mathrm{ind}_{\mathbb{F}_{q}(\zeta_{dv})^{\times}}(\lambda)=(q^{r}-1)/m
because
\displaystyle\lambda\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\times}
. Moreover, we note that

(uv,h)=(v,h)\left(\frac{uv}{(v,h)},\frac{h}{(v,h)}\right)=(v,h)\left(u,\frac{h}{(v,h)}\right),

for all
\displaystyle u\mid d
. From the above and Lemmas
1
and
2
, we obtain

f_{u,v}=\frac{r(uv,h)}{\left(\frac{q^{r}-1}{m},uv,h\right)}=\frac{r(v,h)}{(v,H_{r})}\cdot\frac{\left(u,\frac{h}{(v,h)}\right)}{\left(u,\frac{H_{r}}{(v,H_{r})}\right)},

(2)

for all
\displaystyle u\mid d
, where
\displaystyle H_{r}=(h,(q^{r}-1)/m)
. Now, we find

[f_{u_{1},v},f_{u_{2},v}]=\frac{r(v,h)}{(v,H_{r})}\cdot\left[\frac{\left(u_{1},\frac{h}{(v,h)}\right)}{\left(u_{1},\frac{H_{r}}{(v,H_{r})}\right)},\frac{\left(u_{2},\frac{h}{(v,h)}\right)}{\left(u_{2},\frac{H_{r}}{(v,H_{r})}\right)}\right].

Since
\displaystyle(u_{1},u_{2})=1
, the lcm is the product of the two numbers. Moreover, recall that
\displaystyle u\mapsto(u,k)
is a multiplicative function for all fixed integers
\displaystyle k
. Therefore, using (
2
), we find that
\displaystyle[f_{u_{1},v},f_{u_{2},v}]=f_{u_{1}u_{2},v}
.
∎

7.
The main theorems

In this section, we prove that the set
\displaystyle R_{q}(a,d)
does not have
\displaystyle d_{1}
-density, and thus no
\displaystyle d_{2}
-density by equivalence, if
\displaystyle f\geq 2
. However, we show that the
\displaystyle d_{3}
-density exists and we find a formula for it. From now on, we denote by
\displaystyle\bar{f}
the order of
\displaystyle q
modulo
\displaystyle d(h,d^{\infty})
.

Theorem 8
.

If
\displaystyle f\geq 2
, then the set
\displaystyle R_{q}(a,d)
has no
\displaystyle d_{1}
-density, nor
\displaystyle d_{2}
-density.

Proof.

It suffices to show that the limit, as
\displaystyle N
tends to infinity, of

\frac{R_{q}(a,d,N)N}{q^{N}}

(3)

does not exist. We proceed to show that (
3
) converges to different limits for distinct subsequences. Let
\displaystyle(x_{n})_{n\geq 1}
be an increasing sequence of integers
\displaystyle N
not divisible by
\displaystyle f
. By the discussion at the beginning of Section
5
, we have
\displaystyle R_{q}(a,d,x_{n})=0
for all
\displaystyle n\geq 0
. Let
\displaystyle(y_{n})_{n\geq 0}
be the sequence defined by
\displaystyle y_{n}=\bar{f}(h,d^{\infty})(dn+1)
for all
\displaystyle n\geq 0
. By Theorem
7
, we have

\bigg|\frac{R_{q}(a,d,y_{n})y_{n}}{q^{y_{n}}}-\delta_{q}(a,d,y_{n})\bigg|\leq 2^{\omega(d)+1}c_{1}\tau(e_{y_{n}})q^{-y_{n}/2},

(4)

and by Lemma
5
, we see that
\displaystyle e_{y_{n}}=e_{\bar{f}}(h,d^{\infty})\nu
, with
\displaystyle\nu\mid 2^{\infty}
, making
\displaystyle e_{y_{n}}
a constant, say
\displaystyle E
, that does not depend on
\displaystyle n
. In particular,
\displaystyle\tau(e_{y_{n}})
is also a constant and the right hand side of (
4
) converges to
\displaystyle 0
as
\displaystyle n\to+\infty
. Hence the limit of (
3
), where we substituted
\displaystyle N
for
\displaystyle y_{n}
, is the limit of
\displaystyle\delta_{q}(a,d,y_{n})
. By Theorems
6
and
7
,

\delta_{q}(a,d,y_{n})=\sum_{v\mid E}\sum_{u\mid d}\frac{\mu(u)(uv,h)}{uv}\cdot[f_{u,v}\mid\bar{f}(h,d^{\infty})],

where we used that
\displaystyle e_{y_{n}}=E
and that
\displaystyle f_{u,v}\mid y_{n}
if and only if
\displaystyle f_{u,v}\mid\bar{f}(h,d^{\infty})
. The latter follows from Remark
1
. It follows that
\displaystyle\delta_{q}(a,d,y_{n})
is a constant that does not depend on
\displaystyle n
, say
\displaystyle\delta
. Since
\displaystyle\mathbb{F}_{dv,v}
is a subfield of
\displaystyle\mathbb{F}_{dv,uv}
, we find that
\displaystyle f_{v}:=f_{1,v}
divides
\displaystyle f_{u,v}
for all
\displaystyle u\mid d
. We may write

\delta=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}v\mid E\\
f_{v}\mid\bar{f}(h,d^{\infty})\end{subarray}}\sum_{u\mid d}\frac{\mu(u)(uv,h)}{uv}\cdot[f_{u,v}\mid\bar{f}(h,d^{\infty})].

For all
\displaystyle v\mid E
such that
\displaystyle f_{v}\mid\bar{f}(h,d^{\infty})
, the function

u\mapsto\frac{\mu(u)(uv,h)}{u(v,h)}\cdot[f_{u,v}\mid\bar{f}(h,d^{\infty})]

is multiplicative by Lemma
7
. Therefore, we have

\delta=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}v\mid E\\
f_{v}\mid\bar{f}(h,d^{\infty})\end{subarray}}\frac{(v,h)}{v}\prod_{l\mid d}\left(1-\frac{(lv,h)}{l(v,h)}\cdot[f_{l,v}\mid\bar{f}(h,d^{\infty})]\right),

where we used the Euler product formula. We see that
\displaystyle\delta
is non-negative as each general term is. Hence it suffices to show that there is one non-zero term. We claim that the term in
\displaystyle v=(h,d^{\infty})
is positive. First, let us show that we have
\displaystyle f_{v}\mid\bar{f}(h,d^{\infty})
. Note that
\displaystyle(v,h)=v
, so that

f_{(h,d^{\infty})}=\frac{\mathrm{ord}_{dv}(q)(v,h)}{(\mathrm{ind}_{\mathbb{F}_{q}(\zeta_{dv})^{\times}}(\lambda),v,h)}=\frac{\bar{f}(h,d^{\infty})}{(\mathrm{ind}_{\mathbb{F}_{q}(\zeta_{dv})^{\times}}(\lambda),h,d^{\infty})}.

We find that
\displaystyle f_{(h,d^{\infty})}\mid\bar{f}(h,d^{\infty})
and the term in
\displaystyle v=(h,d^{\infty})
appears in the sum. Using again that
\displaystyle(v,h)=v
and that
\displaystyle(lv,h)=v
, we find the general term to be

\frac{(h,d^{\infty})}{v}\prod_{l\mid d}\left(1-\frac{[f_{l,v}\mid\bar{f}(h,d^{\infty})]}{l}\right),

which is non-zero, whether
\displaystyle f_{l,v}\mid\bar{f}(h,d^{\infty})
or not. We obtain that (
3
) converges to
\displaystyle 0
and
\displaystyle\delta>0
for distinct subsequences. Hence, the set
\displaystyle R_{q}(a,d)
has no
\displaystyle d_{1}
-density, and thus no
\displaystyle d_{2}
-density by
[
4
, Proposition 1.8]
.
∎

The proof of the existence and the computation of the
\displaystyle d_{3}
-density of
\displaystyle R_{q}(a,d)
requires to partition
\displaystyle\mathbb{N}
into a countable union of distinct arithmetic progressions, following a method of Ballot
[
3
]
. We have

\mathbb{N}=\bigsqcup_{j=1}^{f-1}S_{j}\sqcup\bigsqcup_{w\mid d^{\infty}}\bigsqcup_{\begin{subarray}{c}\alpha=1\\
(\alpha,d)=1\end{subarray}}^{d}A_{w,\alpha},

(5)

where
\displaystyle S_{j}=\{fn+j:n\geq 0\}
and
\displaystyle A_{w,\alpha}=\{fw(\alpha+dn):n\geq 0\}
. For
\displaystyle N\in S_{j}
, we have
\displaystyle\delta_{q}(a,d,N)=0
by the discussion at the beginning of Section
5
. For
\displaystyle N\in A_{w,\alpha}
, we have
\displaystyle e_{N}=e_{fw}
by Lemma
5
, which only depends on
\displaystyle w
. Moreover, we have
\displaystyle f_{u,v}\mid N
if and only if
\displaystyle f_{u,v}\mid fw
, by Remark
1
. We obtain

\delta_{q}(a,d,N)=\sum_{v\mid e_{fw}}\sum_{u\mid d}\frac{\mu(u)(uv,h)}{uv}\cdot[f_{u,v}\mid fw]

(6)

which is a constant that does not depend on
\displaystyle n
, nor
\displaystyle\alpha
. We denote this quantity by
\displaystyle\delta_{w}
. Moreover, we denote by
\displaystyle\delta_{q}(a,d)
the sum

\delta_{q}(a,d)=\frac{\varphi(d)}{df}\sum_{w\mid d^{\infty}}\frac{\delta_{w}}{w}.

(7)

We show that
\displaystyle\delta_{q}(a,d)
is the
\displaystyle d_{3}
-density of the set
\displaystyle R_{q}(a,d)
.

Lemma 8
.

There exists an absolute constant
\displaystyle c_{2}>0
such that for every
\displaystyle x\geq e^{2\omega(d)}
, we have

\sum_{\vbox{\Let@\restore@math@cr\default@tag\halign{\hfil$\m@th\scriptstyle#$&$\m@th\scriptstyle{}#$\hfil\cr\displaystyle\scriptstyle w&\displaystyle\scriptstyle{}\mid d^{\infty}\\
w&\leq x\crcr}}}1\leq c_{2}\log(x)^{\omega(d)}\quad\text{and}\quad\sum_{\vbox{\Let@\restore@math@cr\default@tag\halign{\hfil$\m@th\scriptstyle#$&$\m@th\scriptstyle{}#$\hfil\cr\displaystyle\scriptstyle w&\displaystyle\scriptstyle{}\mid d^{\infty}\\
w&>x\crcr}}}\frac{1}{w}\leq\frac{3c_{2}\log{(x)}^{\omega(d)}}{x}.

Proof.

Let
\displaystyle M_{d}(x)
denote the sum on the left. We see that
\displaystyle M_{d}(x)
is bounded above by the product of
\displaystyle\log_{l}(x)+1
for all
\displaystyle l\mid d
. We have
\displaystyle\log_{l}(x)+1\leq\log(x)
for all primes
\displaystyle e^{2}\leq l\leq x
. If
\displaystyle l\leq e^{2}
, then there exists a constant
\displaystyle C_{l}>0
such that
\displaystyle\log_{l}(x)+1\leq C_{l}\log(x)
. The result follows by choosing
\displaystyle c_{2}
as the product of the
\displaystyle C_{l}
’s for all primes
\displaystyle l\leq e^{2}
. Next, we apply Abel summation formula to the series on the right so that
\displaystyle M_{d}(x)
appears in the expression. We find

\displaystyle\displaystyle\sum_{\vbox{\Let@\restore@math@cr\default@tag\halign{\hfil$\m@th\scriptstyle#$&$\m@th\scriptstyle{}#$\hfil\cr\displaystyle\scriptstyle w&\displaystyle\scriptstyle{}\mid d^{\infty}\\
\displaystyle\scriptstyle w&\displaystyle\scriptstyle{}>x\crcr}}}\frac{1}{w}

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\frac{M_{d}(x)}{x}+\int_{x}^{+\infty}\frac{M_{d}(t)}{t^{2}}\mathrm{d}t\leq c_{2}\bigg(\frac{\log{(x)}^{\omega(d)}}{x}+\int_{x}^{+\infty}\frac{\log{(t)}^{\omega(d)}}{t^{2}}\mathrm{d}t\bigg).

Call
\displaystyle I(x)
the integral on the right-hand side of the inequality. For
\displaystyle x\geq e^{2\omega(d)}
, we see that
\displaystyle I(x)-2\log(x)^{\omega(d)}/x
is an increasing function that converges to
\displaystyle 0
as
\displaystyle x
tends to infinity. Hence
\displaystyle I(x)\leq 2\log(x)^{\omega(d)}/x
and the result follows.
∎

Lemma 9
.

For every
\displaystyle N\geq e^{2\omega(d)}
, we have

\bigg|\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\delta_{q}(a,d,n)-\delta_{q}(a,d)\bigg|\leq c_{2}\varphi(d)\bigg(1+\frac{3}{fd}\bigg)\frac{\log{(N)}^{\omega(d)}}{N},

where
\displaystyle c_{2}
is the absolute constant defined in Lemma
8
.

Proof.

From the partition of
\displaystyle\mathbb{N}
given in (
5
), we have

S_{N}:=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\delta_{q}(a,d,n)=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{w\mid d^{\infty}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\alpha=1\\
(\alpha,d)=1\end{subarray}}^{d}\sum_{n\in A_{w,\alpha}(N)}\delta_{w},

where
\displaystyle A_{w,\alpha}(N)=A_{w,\alpha}\cap\llbracket 1,N\rrbracket
, and using that
\displaystyle\delta_{q}(a,d,n)=0
if
\displaystyle n\in S_{j}
, and that
\displaystyle\delta_{q}(a,d,n)=\delta_{w}
if
\displaystyle n\in A_{w,\alpha}
. Note that
\displaystyle w\leq N
and

\#A_{w,\alpha}(N)=\bigg\lfloor\frac{N+fw(d-\alpha)}{fdw}\bigg\rfloor,

thus, we obtain

S_{N}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}w\mid d^{\infty}\\
w\leq N\end{subarray}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\alpha=1\\
(\alpha,d)=1\end{subarray}}^{d}\bigg\lfloor\frac{N+fw(d-\alpha)}{fdw}\bigg\rfloor\delta_{w}.

By definition of the floor function, on the one hand, we have

S_{N}\geq\frac{\varphi(d)}{N}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}w\mid d^{\infty}\\
w\leq N\end{subarray}}\delta_{w}\left(\frac{N}{fdw}-1\right)=\delta_{q}(a,d)-\frac{\varphi(d)}{N}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}w\mid d^{\infty}\\
w\leq N\end{subarray}}\delta_{w}-\frac{\varphi(d)}{fd}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}w\mid d^{\infty}\\
w>N\end{subarray}}\frac{\delta_{w}}{w}.

and on the other hand,

S_{N}\leq\delta_{q}(a,d)+\frac{\varphi(d)}{N}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}w\mid d^{\infty}\\
w\leq N\end{subarray}}\delta_{w}+\frac{\varphi(d)}{fd}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}w\mid d^{\infty}\\
w>N\end{subarray}}\frac{\delta_{w}}{w}.

We obtain

|S_{N}-\delta_{q}(a,d)|\leq\frac{\varphi(d)}{N}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}w\mid d^{\infty}\\
w\leq N\end{subarray}}\delta_{w}+\frac{\varphi(d)}{fd}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}w\mid d^{\infty}\\
w>N\end{subarray}}\frac{\delta_{w}}{w}\leq c_{2}\varphi(d)\left(1+\frac{3}{fd}\right)\frac{\log(N)^{\omega(d)}}{N},

where we used that
\displaystyle\delta_{w}\leq 1
, and Lemma
8
.
∎

Theorem 9
.

There exists an absolute constant
\displaystyle c_{3}>0
such that

\bigg|\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\frac{R_{q}(a,d,n)}{q^{n}/n}-\delta_{q}(a,d)\bigg|\leq c_{2}\varphi(d)\bigg(1+\frac{3}{fd}\bigg)\frac{\log{(N)}^{\omega(d)}}{N}+\frac{c_{3}}{N},

for all
\displaystyle N\geq e^{2\omega(d)}
, where
\displaystyle c_{2}
is the absolute constant defined in Lemma
8
. In particular,
\displaystyle R_{q}(a,d)
has
\displaystyle d_{3}
-density equal to
\displaystyle\delta_{q}(a,d)
.

Proof.

First, we put

R_{N}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\frac{R_{q}(a,d,n)}{q^{n}/n}\quad\text{and}\quad S_{N}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\delta_{q}(a,d,n).

We have

|R_{N}-\delta_{q}(a,d)|\leq|R_{N}-S_{N}|+c_{2}\varphi(d)\bigg(1+\frac{3}{fd}\bigg)\frac{\log{(N)}^{\omega(d)}}{N},

for all
\displaystyle N\geq e^{2\omega(d)}
, by Lemma
9
. Let us now bound the term
\displaystyle|R_{N}-S_{N}|
. Using Theorem
7
, and since
\displaystyle R_{q}(a,d,n)=\delta_{q}(a,d,n)=0
if
\displaystyle f\nmid n
, we have

|R_{N}-S_{N}|\leq\frac{1}{N}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n=1\\
f\mid n\end{subarray}}^{N}\bigg|\frac{R_{q}(a,d,n)}{q^{n}/n}-\delta_{q}(a,d,n)\bigg|\leq\frac{2^{\omega(d)+1}c_{1}}{N}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n=1\\
f\mid n\end{subarray}}^{N}\tau(e_{n})q^{-n/2}.

By Lemma
5
,
\displaystyle e_{n}\leq 2^{v_{2}(q^{f}+1)}e_{f}n/f
. Hence
\displaystyle\tau(e_{n})\leq v_{2}(q^{f}+1)\tau(e_{f})n/f
, and

|R_{N}-S_{N}|\leq\frac{2^{\omega(d)+1}v_{2}(q^{f}+1)c_{1}\tau(e_{f})}{Nf}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n=1\\
f\mid n\end{subarray}}^{N}nq^{-n/2}=:\frac{c}{Nf}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n=1\\
f\mid n\end{subarray}}^{N}nq^{-n/2}.

Since
\displaystyle q^{-1/2}<1
, we obtain

|R_{N}-S_{N}|\leq\frac{c}{Nf}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n=1\\
f\mid n\end{subarray}}^{+\infty}nq^{-n/2}=\frac{c}{Nf}\sum_{m=0}^{+\infty}fmq^{-fm/2}=\frac{c}{N}\frac{q^{-f/2}}{(q^{-f/2}-1)^{2}}=:\frac{c_{3}}{N}.

This completes the proof of the bound. Letting
\displaystyle N
tend to infinity shows that the set
\displaystyle R_{q}(a,d)
has
\displaystyle d_{3}
-density equal to
\displaystyle\delta_{q}(a,d)
.
∎

8.
Closed-form for the
\displaystyle d_{3}
-density

We proved in Section
7
that
\displaystyle\delta_{q}(a,d)
is the
\displaystyle d_{3}
-density of
\displaystyle R_{q}(a,d)
. However, this constant is defined via a series, meaning that there are infinitely many operations to carry out in order to compute it. We show in this section, under the assumption
\displaystyle f_{u,v}=\mathrm{ord}_{dv}(q)
for all
\displaystyle u\mid d
and
\displaystyle v\mid d^{\infty}
, that
\displaystyle\delta_{q}(a,d)
can be written in a closed-form formula, that is, a formula that requires only finitely many simple operations. We define a function
\displaystyle\eta:\mathbb{N}^{2}\longrightarrow\mathbb{N}
by
\displaystyle\eta(m,n)=1
if
\displaystyle 2\nmid(m,n)
and by
\displaystyle\eta(m,n)=2^{v_{2}(q^{\bar{f}}+1)-1}
otherwise. Note that
\displaystyle m\mapsto\eta(m,n)
is a multiplicative function for all
\displaystyle n\geq 1
.

Proposition 2
.

Assume that
\displaystyle f_{u,v}=\mathrm{ord}_{dv}(q)
for all
\displaystyle u\mid d
and
\displaystyle v\mid d^{\infty}
. For each
\displaystyle w\mid d^{\infty}
, we have

\delta_{w}=\begin{cases}\sum_{u\mid d}\frac{\mu(u)(dh,u^{\infty})}{u(q^{\bar{f}}-1,u^{\infty})(\nu,u^{\infty})\eta(\nu,u)},&\text{if }fw=\bar{f}\nu,\,\nu\mid d^{\infty};\\
0,&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

If
\displaystyle fw=\bar{f}\nu
, we may denote
\displaystyle\delta_{w}
by
\displaystyle\delta(\nu)
when it is written in the above form to make the dependence in
\displaystyle\nu
more obvious.

Proof.

The proof being quite similar to the proof of
[
12
, Lemma 4]
, we may skip a few details. Since
\displaystyle v\mid e_{fw}
if and only if
\displaystyle\mathrm{ord}_{dv}(q)\mid fw
, and because
\displaystyle f_{u,v}=\mathrm{ord}_{dv}(q)
, we find from (
6
) that

\delta_{w}=\sum_{v\mid e_{fw}}\sum_{u\mid d}\frac{\mu(u)(uv,h)}{uv}=\sum_{v\mid e_{fw}}\frac{(v,h)}{v}\prod_{l\mid d}\left(1-\frac{(lv,h)}{l(v,h)}\right),

where we used the Euler product formula on the inner sum. Note that the product is non-zero if and only if
\displaystyle(h,d^{\infty})\mid e_{fw}
, or equivalently,
\displaystyle\bar{f}\mid fw
. Assume
\displaystyle fw=\bar{f}\nu
for some
\displaystyle\nu\mid d^{\infty}
. Then, the product is equal to
\displaystyle\varphi(d)/d
. We use the Euler product formula twice on the remaining sum to obtain

\delta_{w}=\prod_{l\mid d}\bigg(1-\frac{l^{v_{l}(dh)}}{l^{v_{l}(q^{\bar{f}\nu}-1)+1}}\bigg)=\sum_{u\mid d}\frac{\mu(u)(dh,u^{\infty})}{u(q^{\bar{f}\nu}-1,u^{\infty})}.

Finally, we apply Lemma
5
to
\displaystyle(q^{\bar{f}\nu}-1,u^{\infty})
in the general term of the sum, which is allowed since
\displaystyle u\mid q^{\bar{f}}-1
, to obtain
\displaystyle\delta_{w}=\delta(\nu)
.
∎

Theorem 10
.

Put
\displaystyle C=3\cdot 4^{-1}+2^{-v_{2}(q^{\bar{f}}+1)-1}
. Assume that
\displaystyle f_{u,v}=\mathrm{ord}_{dv}(q)
for all
\displaystyle u\mid d
and
\displaystyle v\mid d^{\infty}
. Then, we have

\delta_{q}(a,d)=\frac{1}{\bar{f}}\prod_{l\mid d}\bigg(1-\frac{l^{v_{l}(dh)}C^{[l=2]}}{(l+1)l^{v_{l}(q^{\bar{f}}-1)}}\bigg).

Proof.

By Proposition
2
, we may only consider the indices
\displaystyle w\mid d^{\infty}
that satisfy
\displaystyle fw=\bar{f}\nu
for some
\displaystyle\nu\mid d^{\infty}
in the expression (
7
) of
\displaystyle\delta_{q}(a,d)
. We obtain

\delta_{q}(a,d)=\frac{\varphi(d)}{d}\sum_{w\mid d^{\infty}}\frac{\delta_{w}}{fw}=\frac{\varphi(d)}{d}\sum_{\nu\mid d^{\infty}}\frac{\delta(\nu)}{\bar{f}\nu}.

Since the series is absolutely convergent, we may interchange the sum in
\displaystyle\delta(\nu)
and the series. We find

\delta_{q}(a,d)=\frac{\varphi(d)}{d}\sum_{u\mid d}\frac{\mu(u)(dh,u^{\infty})}{u(q^{\bar{f}}-1,u^{\infty})}\sum_{\nu\mid d^{\infty}}\frac{1}{\nu(\nu,u^{\infty})\eta(\nu,u)}.

Let
\displaystyle S(u)
be the inner series. For all
\displaystyle u\in\mathbb{N}
, the function
\displaystyle\nu\mapsto\nu(\nu,u^{\infty})\eta(\nu,u)
is multiplicative. Moreover, we have
\displaystyle\eta(\nu,u)=\eta(\mathrm{rad}(\nu),u)
, where
\displaystyle\mathrm{rad}
is the radical of an integer function. By the Euler product formula, we have

\displaystyle\displaystyle S(u)=\prod_{l\mid d}\bigg(1+\sum_{r=1}^{+\infty}\frac{1}{l^{r(1+[l\mid u])}\eta(l,u)}\bigg)=\prod_{l\mid d}\bigg(1+\frac{1}{\eta(l,u)(l^{1+[l\mid u]}-1)}\bigg).

Since
\displaystyle\eta(l,u)=1
when
\displaystyle l\neq 2
or
\displaystyle l\nmid u
, we have

\displaystyle\displaystyle S(u)

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}l\mid d\\
l\nmid u\end{subarray}}\bigg(1+\frac{1}{l-1}\bigg)\prod_{l\mid u}\bigg(1+\frac{2^{-[l=2](v_{2}(q^{\bar{f}}-1)-1)}}{l^{2}-1}\bigg).

Writing
\displaystyle u^{\prime}=u/(u,2)
, we have

S(u)=\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}l\mid d\\
l\nmid u\end{subarray}}\bigg(\frac{l}{l-1}\bigg)\prod_{l\mid u^{\prime}}\bigg(\frac{l^{2}}{l^{2}-1}\bigg)\cdot\bigg(1+\frac{1}{2^{v_{2}(q^{\bar{f}}-1)-1}}\bigg)^{[2\mid u]}=\frac{duC^{[2\mid u]}}{\varphi(d)\psi(u)}.

Therefore, going back to
\displaystyle\delta_{q}(a,d)
, we obtain

\delta_{q}(a,d)=\frac{1}{\bar{f}}\sum_{u\mid d}\frac{\mu(u)(dh,u^{\infty})C^{[2\mid u]}}{(q^{\bar{f}}-1,u^{\infty})\psi(u)}.

The general term of the sum defines a multiplicative function in the variable
\displaystyle u
, thus the result follows from the Euler product formula.
∎

Note that Theorem
10
coincides with
[
2
, Theorem 3.3]
and
[
3
, Theorem 11]
in the case
\displaystyle a=T
and, respectively,
\displaystyle d=2
and
\displaystyle d
an odd prime.

In conclusion, we proved that the set
\displaystyle R_{q}(a,d)
has
\displaystyle d_{3}
-density
\displaystyle\delta_{q}(a,d)
in Section

\displaystyle\delta_{q}(a,d)

\displaystyle[\mathbb{F}_{dv,uv}:\mathbb{F}_{q}]

\displaystyle\mathrm{ord}_{dv}(q)
