Determinants of health-seeking behavior of people on institutional delivery: evidence from qualitative research
Authors: Sailaja Ghimire, Bishal Dahal Khatri, Jot Narayan Patel, Sadikshya Bhattarai, Leela Khanal, Swechhya Shrestha, Rabindra Bhandari, Namita Ghimire, Pradeep Gyanwali, Madhusudan Subedi, Meghnath Dhimal
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundInstitutional delivery is an evidence-based option that substantially reduces risks to mothers and newborns. However, the existing literature provides an incomplete account of the determinants that influence individuals’ choices about institutional delivery. This study explores the determinants (facilitators, barriers) of health-seeking behavior for institutional delivery in Nepal.MethodsThis study employed a focused ethnographic design, which allowed for an in-depth understanding of participants lived experiences in relation to health seeking behavior to institutional delivery. 60 in-depth interviews were conducted among mothers of children under two years, followed by transect walk, informal discussion with community people and field observations. Purposeful sampling included mothers who delivered in health facilities to explore facilitators of institutional delivery and mothers who delivered at home to identify barriers. Primary data were collected from both rural and urban sites across seven provinces of Nepal. All interviews were audio-recorded, transcribed in Nepali, and subsequently translated into English. Codes were generated from the translated transcripts, and those codes were then organized into overarching themes and sub-themes. An inductive approach was employed for data analysis, and R-based Qualitative Data Analysis (RQDA) software was used for data management.ResultsThe study revealed broad support for antenatal care and health facilities, emphasizing their safety and benefits. Information about institutional delivery was disseminated through multiple channels, although some participants lacked comprehensive knowledge, leading to decisions influenced by personal intuition and cultural beliefs. Household decision-making dynamics, geographic accessibility, and financial considerations were significant factors influencing the selection of delivery location. While some participants valued free services, others experienced financial hardships. Infrastructure limitations and behaviours of health workers also affected health-seeking behaviors.ConclusionAntenatal care and facility-based childbirth are widely accepted among women in Nepal; however, utilization of institutional delivery remains constrained by multiple barriers, including limited maternal knowledge, family decision-making dynamics, distance and transport challenges, hidden costs, shortages of facilities and personnel, disrespectful care, and COVID-19-related disruptions. Addressing these barriers is important to improve access to safe facility-based delivery.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12884-026-09321-9.

Background

Health-seeking behavior (HSB) broadly refers to the actions taken by individual in response to perceived health needs. Westgard et al. (2019) define it as “any action or inaction undertaken by individuals who perceive themselves to have a health problem for the purpose of finding an appropriate remedy” [1]. Similarly, Widayanti et al. (2020) describe HSB as “the steps taken by an individual who perceives a need for help as he or she attempts to solve a health problem” [2].

Institutional delivery (ID) is defined as childbirth occurring in a health facility under the care of skilled health personnel such as nurses or midwives and is widely recognized as essential for improving maternal and newborn health [3]. The utilization of ID services is a well-established and effective intervention for reducing maternal and neonatal morbidity and mortality, as it ensures access to skilled medical care and emergency obstetric services necessary for the timely management of labor and delivery complications. Evidence from Ethiopia suggests that increasing facility-based deliveries can prevent approximately 16–33% of maternal deaths [3]. Furthermore, institutional deliveries promote clean and safe birthing practices, enable timely newborn resuscitation, and ensure immediate postnatal care, thereby significantly enhancing the survival and overall well-being of both mothers and newborns [3, 4].

Health-seeking behavior related to childbirth refers to how women and their families perceive pregnancy-related health needs and the actions they take in response, such as attending antenatal care, seeking skilled birth attendance, and choosing a place of delivery. This behavior is influenced by awareness of maternal risks (e.g., danger signs during labor), trust in formal healthcare systems, and prevailing cultural expectations. Women with higher health literacy and education are more likely to recognize the benefits of facility-based care and, consequently, engage more actively with prenatal and delivery services [5]. In contrast, traditional beliefs and reliance on home remedies can delay or deter timely access to healthcare; for example, cultural norms in some communities favor home births over hospital deliveries [6].

Globally, ID rates have risen significantly since the early 2000s. By 2015, about three-fourths (76%) of deliveries worldwide occurred in health facilities, yet significant gaps persist as a result of persistent socio-economic, geographic barriers, educational, and urban-rural inequalities [7–9]. This increase is especially notable among wealthier and urban populations but has also extended to rural areas due to targeted interventions [8, 10]. High-income regions are now approaching near-universal facility births, whereas many low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) continue to lag [8]. For example, in sub-Saharan Africa, only about 18% of rural births occur in health facilities compared with 35% of urban births [5]. Wealth disparities are pronounced: analyses of data from 63 countries show that the poorest women have the lowest rates of facility-based delivery, and in some nations, income-related gaps have widened over time [8]. Similarly, south Asian countries has seen a steady rise in institutional deliveries, for instance: Pakistan ID increased from around 34% in mid 2000s to around 69% by late 2010s [11], Bangladesh also saw an increase in the ID from 10% to nearly 50% from 2000s to 2018 [11]. These care-seeking patterns have direct implications for maternal and neonatal mortality: 99% of the 2.6 million annual neonatal deaths occur in LMICs, and approximately 70% of maternal deaths in these countries are attributable to intrapartum complications [5]. Moreover, even as coverage improves, poor quality of care in facilities continues to contribute to preventable deaths [12]. Although global trends demonstrate progress in institutional deliveries, substantial disparities based on urban–rural location, region, and socioeconomic status remain. To promote facility-based deliveries and strengthen emergency obstetric care, several interventions have been implemented worldwide and nationally. These include the establishment of maternity waiting homes, birth centers, reduction of user fees, provision of financial incentives, and birth preparedness packages [13, 14].

Nepal have already joined the race to achieve sustainable development goals (SDG’s) and is committed to reducing maternal mortality rate to 70 per 100,000 [15]. To achieve this Nepal government have launched numerous public health programs resulting in increase in antenatal care visits to 81% from 47%from 2011 to 2022 [16]. Likewise, births in public health facilities increased from 36% to 63%, while in private health facilities it grew from 11% to 18%. However, Neonatal mortality remains a persistent challenge: the neonatal mortality rate is estimated at 21 deaths per 1,000 live births and the under-5 mortality rate at 33 per 1,000 live births [17]. Moreover, recent national maternal mortality estimates place the maternal mortality ratio at about 151 deaths per 100,000 live births [17].

Nepal’s health-care delivery is a decentralized, mixed public-private system led by the Ministry of Health and Population (MoHP) and implemented across three tiers of government (federal, provinces, and local levels), operating through a hierarchical network of basic health centers (for instance health post, urban health clinic, and primary health care center), primary hospitals, secondary hospitals, and tertiary hospitals [18]. Basic health center remains the system’s backbone, supported by an extensive community workforce such as Female Community Health Volunteers (FCHVs), auxiliary nurse midwife, health assistants, and nurses providing basic health services as reported in public health regulation, 2020 [18]. The Government has prioritized Universal Health Coverage through the National Health Insurance Program (NHIP), but implementation has faced persistent challenges: enrollment and retention are low and inequities in coverage remain a major concern [19].

In Nepal, the government has introduced multiple initiatives under the Safe Motherhood and Newborn Health Program, such as community-based maternal and newborn health interventions, rural ultrasound services, expansion and upgrading of delivery sites, emergency referral funds, the “Aama Surakshya” Program, and a free newborn care program. These combined efforts have contributed to a gradual increase in the utilization of ID services over time [20].

Despite these achievements, the existing literature still lacks a comprehensive understanding of the diverse factors influencing women’s decisions to seek ID services. This study aims to bridge that gap by exploring HSBs related to ID through a multidimensional approach that integrates field observations, transect walk and qualitative insights areas often overlooked in previous research. Understanding these underlying determinants is essential for identifying barriers and facilitators that shape women’s choices regarding ID, ultimately contributing to improved maternal health outcomes.

Methods

Study design

This study adopted a focused ethnographic approach to examine mothers’ lived experiences of health-seeking behaviour and pathways to ID. In-depth interviews with mothers were complemented by transect walks across study sites and sustained field observations that captured how sociocultural norms, personal beliefs, past experiences, and interactions with the health system shape care-seeking decisions. The design enabled rich exploration of the complexity and contextual nuances surrounding maternal health behaviours within a constrained timeframe, while retaining a sharp focus on the research objectives [21].To ensure standard reporting, the study adhered to the Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Studies (COREQ) guidelines (Details in Supplementary file 1) [22].

Study setting

This study was conducted across all seven provinces of Nepal, covering the country’s three ecological zones; mountain, hill, and terai, representing both urban and rural settings within each province (Fig. 1). The selection of study sites was guided by evidence from a comprehensive desk review and an analysis of ID data extracted from the integrated health management information system (IHIMS) on 9 June 2022. By incorporating local government units (Palikas) identified through this evidence-based approach, the study ensures that its findings more accurately reflect the diversity of Nepal’s rural and urban contexts.

Study participants

This study recruited mothers of children under two years of age for in-depth interviews to elicit their personal experiences, perceptions, and decision-making processes surrounding health seeking behaviour on childbirth. Eligible participants were women aged 18–45 years who had given birth at least once. The sample comprised multiparous women with diverse delivery histories, encompassing both home and facility-based births, thereby capturing a broad spectrum of maternal health experiences. All participants resided in the selected provinces and Palikas ensuring contextual relevance to local realities. Purposive sampling deliberately maximized variability across key dimensions to encompass a wide range of perspectives and experiences.

Study duration

Primary data collection was conducted over a period of two months, from August to September 2022.

Sample size and sampling

A total of 60 participants were included in the study from seven provinces, comprising 40 from rural areas and 20 from urban areas. The team reached thematic saturation at 60 participants, and hence the researchers stopped collecting more data at that point. Saturation indicators were the absence of new codes or themes in successive interviews, memos, and team consensus that further data would yield only redundant information [23]. This relatively large sample size was selected to compensate for the brief duration of field engagement, allowing the collection of rich, detailed qualitative data.

Secondary data from the national health information systems were first reviewed to understand the overall coverage of ID across different Palikas. Based on this analysis, the research team identified those Palikas where the rate of ID was lower than national average. This helped the team to select the study sites in a systematic and evidence-based manner.

After selecting the Palikas, the research team conducted a consultative meeting with local-level stakeholders, such as the health coordinator and ward chairpersons. During this meeting, the objectives of the study were clearly explained, and the findings from the secondary data analysis were shared and discussed. This discussion helped to validate the data and ensured that the selected Palikas truly had low ID coverage.

Following this, another consultative meeting was organized at the ward level. This meeting included ward chairpersons, ward members, and FCHVs who have close contact with the community. Their input was important in identifying specific areas or settlements within the Palika where ID rates were particularly low. Thus, the focus of the study became more specific and community-centered.

After identifying these low-coverage areas, the research team conducted transect walks and field observations. During these visits, the team carefully observed the community setting and identified households with recent births and identify the mothers and households. A purposive sampling technique [24] was employed to identify participants who met the predefined inclusion criteria that included mothers of children under two years of age who had either delivered in a health facility or at home and who had been residing in the respective province for at least six months. Participants were also required to be willing and able to provide informed consent to take part in the study. All individuals approached agreed to participate, and no refusals were recorded during the recruitment process.

Research team and reflexivity

This study was carried out by a multidisciplinary research team comprising professionals from diverse academic and professional backgrounds. The members involved in data collection and analysis included a professor and medical anthropologist (MS), and public health graduates (SG, SB, JNP, and RB). Investigators were the professor at Patan Academy of Health Sciences (MS), chief research officer (MD), the consultant (MS, SB), research officers (SG, JNP, RB) of Nepal Health Research Council (NHRC) and possessed substantial experience in the design and implementation of qualitative research. Efforts were made to minimize bias by using a semi-structured interview guide and maintaining neutrality during the discussions. The researchers maintained professional boundaries and had no prior relationships with participants and interviewers beyond their interactions during the study.

This institutional affiliation inevitably shaped aspects of the research process. As NHRC representatives, the research team gained enhanced access to key stakeholders, who were generally receptive to participation requests, and local leaders extended support for data collection upon learning the study was conducted under the auspices of a national body affiliated with the Ministry of Health and Population. The team reflected on this positionality throughout data collection and analysis, acknowledging that it may have influenced interviewers’ willingness to engage and the content of their responses.

Data collection

Data were collected through face-to-face in-depth interviews guided by a semi-structured interview guideline [24]. The guidelines were developed after a thorough review of pertinent literature and refined through expert consultation to ensure content validity and contextual appropriateness. Experts from academia, the ministry of health and population, non-governmental organizations, and qualitative research specialists reviewed the semi-structured guidelines. The guidelines underwent pilot testing in Nagarjun Municipality and an urban slum area of Kathmandu district. This phase allowed the research team to identify practical challenges, reflect on interview dynamics, and incorporate feedback during a dedicated tool-finalization meeting, with particular attention to enhancing linguistic clarity and question consistency.

Transect walks were conducted within selected communities and around health facilities to assess the physical environment, including accessibility, distance, transportation options, and surrounding social infrastructure relevant to institutional delivery. These walks revealed contextual influences on mothers’ access to maternity services and shaped the formulation of targeted interview probes.

Observations took place during visits to health facilities and community settings, focusing on the maternity care environment, provider–client interactions, privacy provisions, communication patterns, and routine childbirth-related processes. Detailed field notes captured observations and immediate reflections, later used to contextualize and triangulate interview findings.

Interview guidelines probed the determinants, facilitators, and barriers to ID while also eliciting participants’ recommendations for strengthening maternal health-seeking behaviours. Data collection involved a minimum of 20 days per study site. Participants reflected specifically on their most recent childbirth experience, which had occurred within the preceding 12 months.

Interviews were conducted in Nepali by the research team: MS, RB, and JNP (male) and SB and SG (female), all of whom were experienced qualitative researchers with prior publications and training in in-depth interviewing. No pre-existing relationships existed between interviewers and participants. At the outset of each interview, the study purpose, procedures, potential uses of findings, and confidentiality safeguards were explained, after which written informed consent was obtained. All invited women agreed to participate. Participants were informed that the researchers were affiliated with the Nepal Health Research Council and that the study aimed to understand community perspectives on determinants, barriers, and facilitators of health-seeking behavior for ID.

Most interviews occurred in participants’ homes, preferably in a separate or private room. Family members were courteously asked to step away to ensure privacy. In instances, where young children were reluctant to leave their mothers, the child remained present; every effort was made to minimize interference from other household members and preserve the participant’s comfort.

Interviews typically lasted 30–60 min. To deepen contextual understanding, the team employed transect walks and social-mapping exercises, guided field-note template. All data collectors received training and completed a pilot in two wards to align observation approaches. During field activities, researcher noted; location, time/season, visible infrastructure, transport options, health facility visibility, social and cultural cues, and verbatim remarks where feasible. Field notes were expanded into analytic memos at the end of each day, supported by daily team debriefings and peer review of documentation.

Credibility and confirmability were enhanced through real-time member checking: interviewers summarized key points during interviews and invited participants to confirm or clarify interpretations. Probing, rephrasing for clarity, and brief post-interview discussions further ensured alignment between participants’ intended meanings and researcher understanding. Each participant was interviewed only once and no repeat interview was carried out.

Data management and analysis

Audio recordings from all study sites were securely stored on a shared, password-protected drive accessible only to the core research team. Interviews conducted in Nepali were transcribed verbatim into Nepali and subsequently translated into English by members of the research team; an independent team member (SS and NG) then verified the translations to ensure accuracy and completeness. To preserve anonymity, each participant was assigned a unique code, and all files were kept in password-protected folders on a researcher’s laptop.

Verified English transcripts were imported into the R-based Qualitative Data Analysis (RQDA) package via Easy R (EZR) [25] for systematic coding. We used an inductive thematic approach [26], employing an iterative cycle of coding, interpretation, memo writing, diagramming, and revision. Inductive thematic approach was also used for the data analysis. Two independent researchers (SG and SB) conducted the initial coding of the translated transcripts. Inter-coder reliability was assessed using simple percent agreement. Two coders independently coded all transcripts, and the overall agreement across coding decisions was 78%. Coding discrepancies were subsequently reviewed through structured discussion, resolved by consensus, and used to iteratively refine the codebook, thereby enhancing consistency and analytic rigor.

Initial descriptive codes were generated from the data, grouped by similarity of meaning, and merged into subthemes; related subthemes were then consolidated into overarching themes to produce a coherent, understanding of the data. The findings and interpretations were shared with selected participants, who confirmed that the results accurately reflected their views, thereby supporting the validity of the findings.

Ethical considerations

Ethical standards were strictly maintained throughout the study. Ethical approval was obtained from the independent Ethical Review Board of the Nepal Health Research Council, Kathmandu, Nepal (Reg. No. 229/2022 P). Informed consent was secured from all participants after explaining the study’s purpose, procedures, potential risks, and voluntary nature of participation. To ensure confidentiality, data were anonymized using pseudonyms and unique identifiers. Audio recordings, transcripts, and field notes were stored in encrypted digital files accessible only to the research team, and physical documents were kept in locked cabinets within restricted-access areas. These measures ensured the privacy and data security of participants and maintained the ethical integrity of the research process.

Theme 1- Delays in recognizing problems and deciding to seek care

Maternal knowledge and health seeking decisions

Study participants generally demonstrated awareness of and support for ID and antenatal care (ANC), emphasizing the safety and benefits of healthcare facilities.“Institutional delivery means giving birth to your child in a well-equipped facility with minimum risk of delivery complications.” Koshi Province, Mangsebung, IDI 4.

“Institutional delivery means giving birth to your child in a well-equipped facility with minimum risk of delivery complications.” Koshi Province, Mangsebung, IDI 4.

Many participants were knowledgeable about when to seek care for complications; however, some lacked comprehensive information, particularly regarding free delivery services and the risks of home delivery. Participants often relied on FCHVs for guidance, but the sporadic nature of their visits sometimes led to outdated information.“I don’t know about the complications that can arise when delivering the child at home.” -Gandaki Province, Baudikali, IDI 2.

“I don’t know about the complications that can arise when delivering the child at home.” -Gandaki Province, Baudikali, IDI 2.

Sources of information on ID

Participants reported receiving information about ID through multiple channels, including school curricula, direct communication from healthcare workers during routine check-ups, and FCHVs who disseminated health messages within their communities.“We are informed by the health workers that, if there are any difficulties, like the flow of water or bleeding, we should go to the hospital.”- Bagmati Province, Dakshinkali, IDI 1.

“We are informed by the health workers that, if there are any difficulties, like the flow of water or bleeding, we should go to the hospital.”- Bagmati Province, Dakshinkali, IDI 1.

When study participant received treatment at the hospital, doctors and nurses said every time that they must deliver there. Health care providers and FCHVs significantly contributed to passing health messages about institutional delivery in the communities.“The sisters told me about the importance of institutional delivery.” Koshi Province, _Belbari_ IDI 1.

“The sisters told me about the importance of institutional delivery.” Koshi Province, _Belbari_ IDI 1.

Community-based forums, such as mother’s group meetings, were also important sources of information.“I got information about institutional delivery from a mother’s group meeting.” -Madhesh Province, Parsa, IDI 3.

“I got information about institutional delivery from a mother’s group meeting.” -Madhesh Province, Parsa, IDI 3.

Observations from field visits and transect walks indicated that exposure to maternal health information varied considerably across study sites. In more remote settlements, researchers observed infrequent outreach by FCHVs, irregular mother-group meetings, and limited visibility of health promotion activities. During transect walks, clusters of households were identified where recent information on ANC, ID, and free delivery services had not been disseminated, suggesting gaps in routine health communication.

Perceived importance of ID

Most participants recognized the benefits of ANC and facility-based delivery, highlighting improved access to health facilities and skilled care. Incentives such as skilled birth attendants and hygienic conditions motivated their preference for ID. Supportive care provided by healthcare staff further encouraged utilization.“At home, the umbilical cord is cut with the help of a sickle. Such a habit exists in the village, but now we do not have any idea how the umbilical cord is cut. The healthcare staffs have all the ideas about it.” -Sudurpaschim Province, Janaki, IDI 4.

“At home, the umbilical cord is cut with the help of a sickle. Such a habit exists in the village, but now we do not have any idea how the umbilical cord is cut. The healthcare staffs have all the ideas about it.” -Sudurpaschim Province, Janaki, IDI 4.

Some participants reported that choosing a health facility for childbirth was beneficial, as healthcare providers offer a range of services that help facilitate a safer and more manageable delivery process.“It is beneficial to deliver in a health care setting as hygiene is maintained, mother’s health can be good, and health workers have good skills to deliver a child, so it is really important to go to a health care institution to get delivery.” – Lumbini_Tilottama_IDI 3.

“It is beneficial to deliver in a health care setting as hygiene is maintained, mother’s health can be good, and health workers have good skills to deliver a child, so it is really important to go to a health care institution to get delivery.” – Lumbini_Tilottama_IDI 3.

However, some participants perceived ID as beneficial only for the newborn, expressed concerns about financial costs, and remained uncertain about the outcomes for both mother and child.“Normally, there is no benefit to delivering a baby at a health institution. Only, we need to spend more money at the hospital.” – Madhesh Province, Maulapur, IDI 1.

“Normally, there is no benefit to delivering a baby at a health institution. Only, we need to spend more money at the hospital.” – Madhesh Province, Maulapur, IDI 1.

Internal beliefs, personal motivation and shyness

Participants’ internal beliefs and personal motivations played a crucial role in shaping their decisions to opt for or against ID. For some, strong personal convictions, trust in healthcare providers, and confidence in modern medical care motivated them to choose hospital-based childbirth.“My personal instinct and belief compelled me to deliver at a health institution.” - Bagmati Province, Dakshinkali, IDI 4.

“My personal instinct and belief compelled me to deliver at a health institution.” - Bagmati Province, Dakshinkali, IDI 4.

Conversely, personal fears and discomforts discouraged others from seeking institutional care. Fear of injections, medical procedures, or unfamiliar hospital environments was a common deterrent.“I did not go for institutional delivery because I am scared of syringes. My husband told me several times to go for a check-up, but I did not care to go.”- Gandaki Province, Rishing, IDI 1.

“I did not go for institutional delivery because I am scared of syringes. My husband told me several times to go for a check-up, but I did not care to go.”- Gandaki Province, Rishing, IDI 1.

Feelings of shyness, embarrassment, and hesitation also influenced decisions, with some women avoiding health facilities to protect privacy or avoid disturbing family members.“I did not tell my husband about the labor pain I had at night because he and everyone else were in a deep sleep.”- Lumbini Province, Tilottama, IDI 2.

“I did not tell my husband about the labor pain I had at night because he and everyone else were in a deep sleep.”- Lumbini Province, Tilottama, IDI 2.

During field visits at the community level, researchers observed that expressions of endurance, self-reliance, and tolerance of pain were socially valued, particularly among women. Reluctance to seek care early or to openly communicate physical discomfort was commonly observed as a socially reinforced behavior, which may delay or prevent timely utilization of institutional delivery services.

Influence of past birth experiences

A prior uncomplicated home birth often reduced perceived need for facility delivery; rapid labor or failure to recognize labor also led to unintended home births.“I had my first baby at home and did not experience any complications. That’s why I decided to give birth at home again.”- Koshi Province, Belbari, IDI 2.“It took only half an hour for me to deliver the baby, and I did not face any difficulties. Even in my previous pregnancy, I delivered at home without any complications.”- Bagmati Province, Dakshinkali, IDI 1.

“I had my first baby at home and did not experience any complications. That’s why I decided to give birth at home again.”- Koshi Province, Belbari, IDI 2.

“It took only half an hour for me to deliver the baby, and I did not face any difficulties. Even in my previous pregnancy, I delivered at home without any complications.”- Bagmati Province, Dakshinkali, IDI 1.

Researchers noted that in several study communities, past successful home births were frequently cited during informal discussions as evidence of women’s ability to deliver safely without medical assistance. Such narratives were observed to reinforce confidence in home delivery and reduce perceived urgency for facility-based care. Field observations also suggested that rapid labor onset, particularly during night-time, limited opportunities for timely decision-making and contributed to unplanned home births, even among women who were otherwise aware of institutional delivery service.

Trust in specific health workers

Trust in specific health workers also influenced the decision to seek institutional care. Participants were more likely to visit a health facility if a trusted service provider was available. The absence of such a provider often discouraged facility visits.“Others do not conduct deliveries, only she (the skilled birth attendant) does. During my delivery, she had gone to Bhimad (a place where health institution is located), and I knew others could not attend. Since, she wasn’t there, I didn’t feel encouraged to go to the health facility, and the delivery happened quickly at home.” - Gandaki Province, Rishing, IDI 2.

“Others do not conduct deliveries, only she (the skilled birth attendant) does. During my delivery, she had gone to Bhimad (a place where health institution is located), and I knew others could not attend. Since, she wasn’t there, I didn’t feel encouraged to go to the health facility, and the delivery happened quickly at home.” - Gandaki Province, Rishing, IDI 2.

Family support and household responsibilities

Most participants reported receiving substantial family support and encouragement to opt for ID. Husbands and relatives often provided moral, financial, and emotional assistance, which played a vital role in motivating women to deliver at health facilities.“My husband had asked me to have the delivery at a health facility and had also sent money about 15–20 days earlier. He never let me face any financial shortages, even when I had to go for my X-rays.” -Lumbini Province, Rambha, IDI 1.

“My husband had asked me to have the delivery at a health facility and had also sent money about 15–20 days earlier. He never let me face any financial shortages, even when I had to go for my X-rays.” -Lumbini Province, Rambha, IDI 1.

However, some participants noted that their husbands did not accompany them to health facilities or lacked active involvement in the process. Others highlighted practical challenges, such as the absence of family members to care for household responsibilities and children during their hospital stay.“At that time, I was alone and my mother-in-law also had her work…We had to carry the baby clothes, there were no other accompanying people at home. There is the need to look at cows and goats, cut the grass for cows so I didn’t go to the health post.” – Gandaki_Rishing_IDI 3”.

“At that time, I was alone and my mother-in-law also had her work…We had to carry the baby clothes, there were no other accompanying people at home. There is the need to look at cows and goats, cut the grass for cows so I didn’t go to the health post.” – Gandaki_Rishing_IDI 3”.

A few participants also described how home deliveries were normalized within their communities, especially when no complications were anticipated, reducing the perceived need for institutional care.“I feel that if I do institutional delivery then they (family) may support us. However, due to problems, I did not go to health facilities. I feel like if I go to health centers, then who will handle my work, like in the village if something happens then they gossip about it.” -Gandaki Province, Rishing, IDI 3.

“I feel that if I do institutional delivery then they (family) may support us. However, due to problems, I did not go to health facilities. I feel like if I go to health centers, then who will handle my work, like in the village if something happens then they gossip about it.” -Gandaki Province, Rishing, IDI 3.

Social norms, reliance on traditional birth attendant

Some participants expressed fear and mistrust toward hospitals, particularly concerns about unnecessary medical interventions such as cesarean sections.“I did not go to the hospital because we were scared that doctors would needlessly do an operation in the hospital.”- Madhesh, Parsa, IDI 1.

“I did not go to the hospital because we were scared that doctors would needlessly do an operation in the hospital.”- Madhesh, Parsa, IDI 1.

Geographical barriers and cultural myths also contributed to the reluctance to seek institutional care. In some areas, women had to cross rivers to reach health posts, and this physical challenge was compounded by traditional beliefs about supernatural harm to pregnant women and unborn babies during such crossings.“The journey to the health post involves crossing a river, and local beliefs warn of ghosts and malevolent spirits that pose a threat to pregnant women and unborn babies during this crossing. There’s a story about a pregnant woman who fell seriously ill after crossing the river for delivery, and her newborn was weak afterward. They had to call a healer from another village to drive away the evil spirits.” – Karnali Province, Junechande, IDI 3.

The power dynamics within households particularly between husbands and wives had a significant influence on decisions regarding the place of delivery. Theme 2— Delays in transportation to reach appropriate care

Geographic accessibility

Geographical terrain, particularly in mountainous and hilly regions, posed a significant barrier to accessing ID services. The steep, uneven roads and long travel times made it difficult for pregnant women to reach health facilities.“I came here (to the health facility) once, but had to go to another center for the X-ray. It’s quite far, it takes a whole day to go there and return.”- Sudurpaschim Province, Durgathali, IDI 2.

“I came here (to the health facility) once, but had to go to another center for the X-ray. It’s quite far, it takes a whole day to go there and return.”- Sudurpaschim Province, Durgathali, IDI 2.

Field teams observed steep, slippery ‘goreto‘(narrow, often unpaved track) paths especially in hilly sites and reported ambulance access only during daytime or when roads were passable; a community respondent during the informal discussion said “the village road was rocky and only a four-wheeler jeep could reach it once a day,” underscoring how geography combines with transport schedules to create delay.

Distance and roadway conditions

The distance to health facilities and the condition of local roads were major determinants of institutional delivery utilization. Transect walk observations revealed regional differences, with the Terai region having relatively easier access compared to the hilly and mountainous areas. “We prefer delivering the baby at home since the health post is very far away from our house.” – Bagmati Province, Dakshinkali, IDI 2.

The condition of local roads further influenced HSB. During the monsoon season, landslides and flooding often blocked routes, making travel unsafe and time-consuming. FCHVs and health workers were also reluctant to visit remote areas under such conditions, limiting maternal healthcare outreach.“It takes about 10–15 minutes to reach the nearest birthing center by vehicle. In several upland settlements, households were located far from motorable roads, requiring prolonged walking to reach the nearest health post or birthing center.

Landslides, flooding, and muddy roads frequently obstructed access routes, making travel unsafe and time-consuming. Financial considerations played a decisive role in influencing participants’ choices regarding the place of delivery. While some women benefited from the free maternity services and government incentives available at health facilities, others experienced significant financial barriers that limited their access to institutional care.

Discussion

This study reveals a variety of characteristics that influence maternal healthcare-seeking behaviors. Participants usually favor ID and antenatal care, which are driven by a variety of sources such as hospital personnel and community health workers. Conversely, challenges such as misconception, financial concerns, and fears of medical procedures remains. Personal intuition, family support, and trust in certain health worker plays a huge role in healthcare delivery decisions. Although health care services are valued, infrastructure shortcomings and financial barriers limit accessibility. On the other hand, distance, road conditions, and financial factors all influence maternal healthcare decisions, underling the importance of focused interventions to address individual issues, build trust, and increase overall accessibility.

The findings of this study resonate with previous research in Nepal, emphasizing the critical role of awareness and knowledge in influencing antenatal and postnatal care utilization [27, 28]. Similar to the literature, our participants exhibited awareness and support for ID, underscoring the safety and benefits of healthcare facilities [29–37]. The reliance on FCHVs for information in our study echoes the role of community health workers highlighted in previous studies [27, 34]. Additionally, both studies underscore the positive association between antenatal care visits and ID [29–37]. However, our study delves deeper into diverse sources of information, uncovering gaps in knowledge, misconceptions, and the influence of community groups on understanding the importance of ID. This nuanced perspective contributes valuable insights for targeted interventions to enhance maternal healthcare-seeking behaviors.

The results of this study aligns with existing literature emphasizing the role of personal intuition, past experiences, and trust in health workers in maternal delivery decisions. Participants’ choices were motivated or dissuaded by individual beliefs and instincts, echoing the significance of a woman’s intention during pregnancy and perception of labor progress in previous studies [38–48]. Trust in specific health workers emerged as pivotal, resonating with findings indicating that consultation with healthcare providers increases ID rates [49]. Additionally, our study identifies unique barriers, such as a fear of injections and shyness. Nonetheless, our findings align with literature identifying negative health system factors as barriers, echoing concerns about infrastructure quality and challenges in accessing health facilities [50–54]. Overall, our study offers nuanced insights, supplementing existing literature on factors influencing ID choices.

The present findings align with existing literature underscoring the substantial impact of family members on maternal delivery decisions. Participants acknowledged significant family support for ID, consistent with findings from various studies emphasizing the role of family in the delivery process [51, 54–56]. However, our study sheds light on challenges such as husbands’ non-attendance at health centers and the absence of family members during delivery, providing additional insights beyond those addressed in prior research.

The results of this study echo existing literature, emphasizing the substantial impact of family dynamics on maternal delivery choices. The husband-wife relationship and the woman’s status within the family significantly influenced decisions, often leading to home births. Similar cultural influences were noted in marginalized communities, where cultural beliefs and resistance to modern delivery methods discouraged skilled ID [27, 50, 57, 58]. Disparities across castes were observed, with general castes demonstrating higher ID rates than scheduled castes and tribes [29].

Like previous research, this study found that adherence to local traditions, social norms, and meta norms influenced delivery choices. The reluctance to access healthcare facilities due to cultural beliefs, such as potential attacks from ghosts or evil spirits during river crossings, was identified in both studies. Additionally, the prevalent belief that health workers would opt for unnecessary Cesarean sections contributed to the reluctance to seek institutional care [59, 60].

Family decision-making processes played a pivotal role, aligning with literature indicating that decisions made by spouses and parents significantly decreased the likelihood of utilizing ID services [61–65]. Despite these influential roles, our study and previous research highlight that some mothers expressed personal choices regarding the location of childbirth, emphasizing their autonomy in decision-making [51, 66].

Both our study and prior research emphasize the pivotal role of healthcare facility conditions and service availability in maternal delivery decisions. Participants in both studies expressed satisfaction with healthcare workers and perceived safety in utilizing health facility services. However, challenges related to facility conditions were evident, including discomfort due to cold facilities, cleanliness issues, and a lack of proper waiting areas. Deficiencies in basic services, such as the absence of ultrasound services and additional charges, were identified, leading some participants to seek medications from private sources. Both studies underscore the crucial importance of adequate human resources within healthcare facilities, emphasizing the need for targeted interventions to address infrastructure and resource challenges in healthcare settings and enhance maternal healthcare utilization [57, 66, 67].

Both, this study and previous research emphasize the significant influence of geographical factors on ID decisions. Distance and road conditions emerged as crucial determinants in both studies, impacting HSB. Our findings align with existing literature, highlighting that reduced proximity to health facilities decreases the likelihood of choosing ID. Studies from Nepal consistently underscore regional disparities, particularly in mountainous regions, where challenges accessing quality healthcare services influence the choice of delivery location. The challenging terrain, coupled with inadequate facilities and medical supplies, acts as a barrier to ID. Recognizing the impact of geographical factors is essential for developing targeted interventions to improve accessibility and promote ID in regions with challenging terrain [31, 57, 60, 67–69].

The evidence of this study and previous research, both –underscore the substantial impact of financial considerations on the choice of delivery location. The findings align with existing literature that highlights economic disparities, with wealthier individuals more likely to opt for ID. Socioeconomic factors, such as hidden costs, emerge as significant barriers for poorer women, despite government subsidies. Studies from various regions, including Nepal, Africa, Pakistan, Bhutan, and Eritrea, consistently emphasize the influence of wealth status on childbirth decisions. The financial burden of accessing healthcare, including transportation costs, is a common thread across these studies. Understanding these economic challenges is vital for crafting targeted interventions to mitigate financial barriers and promote equitable access to ID services [29, 57, 61, 65, 70, 71].

The evidence from this study aligns with previous research, emphasizing the importance of supportive services in health facilities for promoting ID. Participants valued amenities but noted infrastructural shortcomings, echoing broader challenges highlighted in the literature. Past studies stress the role of supportive management in addressing barriers through community-focused awareness, ensuring skilled staff availability, and bridging informational gaps. Strategic management is crucial to overcoming infrastructure deficiencies, improving transportation, and aligning cultural practices with modern healthcare. This supports the idea that a comprehensive and supportive management approach is essential in enhancing the utilization of ID care services [27, 72, 73].

Our findings resonate with existing literature highlighting the significance of comprehensive counseling in influencing delivery choices. Participants in our study received counseling on institutional and home delivery risks, mirroring insights from previous research. However, discrepancies emerged, with instances of health workers downplaying the necessity of facility births in normal cases. Past studies emphasize the pivotal role of counseling during antenatal care (ANC), associating it with the decision to deliver in health facilities. Effective counseling, as indicated in the Nepal study, is linked to an increased likelihood of ID [46, 74, 75]. This underscores the crucial impact of counseling received during ANC on shaping delivery preferences.

The study results demonstrate specific policy needs together with particular operational requirements which policymakers must fulfil through an integrated system that combines community participation with financial protection measures and enhancements to medical facilities in order to increase institutional delivery rates and antenatal care access in low- and middle-income countries such as Nepal. The policy needs which require funding should provide targeted subsidies together with transport vouchers for the lowest income families while health worker roles at FCHV need formal recognition and regular training together with counseling incentives so that they will be more motivated. Moreover, investment should focus on basic facility improvements which include clean waiting areas and essential diagnostic equipment and staffing requirements in underserved and vulnerable regions (mountainous areas). The program design process requires local leaders and women’s groups to collaborate on co-designing interventions which will address their particular cultural barriers and misconceptions about the program while introducing male-involvement sessions that will change household decision dynamics and training FCHVs to conduct structured home visits and group antenatal education with birth-preparedness plans. The health systems and communities need to implement operationally viable solutions which will establish maternity waiting homes together with mobile outreach operations in remote areas and develop an uncomplicated transport network together with emergency phone services and community driver services and the complete removal of unofficial charges which prevents access to essential medicines and the establishment of regular training programs for staff which will teach them about respectful care and counseling techniques so that ANC visits will increase facility births. Moreover, the health system needs to establish a limited number of program indicators which will track ANC attendance and ID rates by caste and area and transport voucher usage and client satisfaction through monitoring systems which will use community feedback to continuously improve their interventions through demand-side support which includes culturally tailored communication and practical solutions.

Conclusion

In our study, we wanted to know what enables or hinders women in Nepal from accessing hospital birth services. We discovered that most women accept prenatal care and birth services in a health facility, though there are still some challenges such as limited knowledge, who makes decision in family, problems with traveling and distance to a health facility, hidden costs, not enough clinics and personnel, bad behavior from some health personnel, and COVID-19-related disruptions, which still discourage some women from accessing birth services in a health facility though prenatal care is accepted. The results imply that we need to take urgent action to reach more women with access to prenatal care and education for mothers, engage the family (especially the father) in the planning of the birth of the child, address the cost and transport issues of the mother-to-be, and make facility-based maternity care more accessible and of higher quality to save more lives of mothers and children in Nepal or similar settings.
