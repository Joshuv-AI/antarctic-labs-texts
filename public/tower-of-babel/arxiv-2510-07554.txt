Phase Diagram of Dropout for Two-Layer Neural Networks in the Mean-Field Regime
Lénaïc Chizat, Pierre Marion, Yerkin Yesbay

Abstract
Dropout is a standard training technique for neural networks that consists of randomly deactivating units at each step of their gradient-based training. It is known to improve performance in many settings, including in the large-scale training of language or vision models. As a first step towards understanding the role of dropout in large neural networks, we study the large-width asymptotics of gradient descent with dropout on two-layer neural networks with the mean-field initialization scale. We obtain a rich asymptotic phase diagram that exhibits five distinct nondegenerate phases depending on the relative magnitudes of the dropout rate, the learning rate, and the width. Notably, we find that the well-studied “penalty” effect of dropout only persists in the limit with impractically small learning rates of order O ⁡ ( 1 / width ) O(1/\text{width}) . For larger learning rates, this effect disappears and in the limit, dropout is equivalent to a “random geometry” technique, where the gradients are thinned randomly after the forward and backward pass have been computed. In this asymptotic regime, the limit is described by a mean-field jump process where the neurons’ update times follow independent Poisson or Bernoulli clocks (depending on whether the learning rate vanishes or not). For some of the phases, we obtain a description of the limit dynamics both in path-space and in distribution-space. The convergence proofs involve a mix of tools from mean-field particle systems and stochastic processes. Together, our results lay the groundwork for a renewed theoretical understanding of dropout in large-scale neural networks.



1
Introduction

Stochastic gradient descent (SGD) is the workhorse of deep learning optimization, but its raw form is rarely used in practice. Over the years, researchers have proposed many training techniques—adaptive learning rates, gradient clipping, weight decay, and more—on top of SGD to accelerate convergence or improve test performance. Among these heuristics,
dropout

[
Srivastava et al. 2014
]
has stood the test of time because it is easy to implement, architecture-agnostic, and the associated performance improvement is empirically robust across tasks. It consists in randomly deactivating a subset of units/neurons during each forward–backward pass. This random
thinning
of the network induces a structured, non-centered, perturbation of the gradients whose theoretical analysis remains challenging.

The original intuition for dropout’s performance is that “it prevents co-adaptation between units” and that it approximates the “combined opinions of exponentially many dropout nets”
[
Srivastava et al. 2014
]
. However, these intuitions remain hypothetical and the recent theoretical literature on dropout follows a quite different route, which we now outline.

Let
\theta
be the parameters of a neural network (NN),
\mathcal{L}(\theta)
the training loss and
\mathcal{L}^{\eta}(\theta)
the training loss perturbed with a dropout mask encoded by the random variable
\eta
.
Following e.g.
Wei et al. 2020
, one can decompose the noise induced by dropout on gradients as

\displaystyle\nabla\mathcal{L}^{\eta}(\theta)-\nabla\mathcal{L}(\theta)

\displaystyle=\underbrace{\mathbf{E}_{\eta}[\nabla\mathcal{L}^{\eta}(\theta)]-\nabla\mathcal{L}(\theta)}_{\text{bias}}+\underbrace{\nabla\mathcal{L}^{\eta}(\theta)-\mathbf{E}_{\eta}[\nabla\mathcal{L}^{\eta}(\theta)]}_{\text{centered noise}}.

(1)

Exchanging gradient and expectation, we notice that the bias is of the form
\nabla P(\theta)
with

\displaystyle P(\theta)=\mathbf{E}_{\eta}[\mathcal{L}^{\eta}(\theta)]-\mathcal{L}(\theta).

(2)

This function, known as
dropout penalty
, can be made explicit in simple settings, such as linearly-parameterized models or two-layer NNs with squared loss
[
Arora et al. 2021
, Proposition 4, and references therein]
. More generally, an informal but empirically accurate approximation of the penalty can be given in terms of the layer-to-layer Jacobians
[
Wei et al. 2020
]
. Much of the theoretical literature on dropout studies the optimization and generalization properties of this penalty, in particular by investigating its connection with more classical regularization terms such as the weight
\ell_{2}
norm or path norm
[
Wager et al. 2013
,
Helmbold and Long 2015
,
Mou et al. 2018
,
Arora et al. 2021
, notably,]
. As for the stochastic term in (
1
), its effect has been studied in
Mianjy and Arora 2020
,
Wei et al. 2020
,
Zhang and Xu 2024
,
Zhang et al. 2024
.

Despite this extensive literature, the question of the scaling of dropout to large-width networks has received little attention. This is a particularly important issue given that dropout is still used by practitioners for large-scale models
[
Vaswani et al. 2017
,
Radford et al. 2018
,
Devlin et al. 2019
,
Dosovitskiy et al. 2021
,
Hu et al. 2021
,
Ouyang et al. 2022
,
Ramesh et al. 2022
,
Taylor et al. 2022
]
. This begs the question of revisiting the theory of dropout in the context of large-scale networks, which is our focus in this paper. To the best of our knowledge, we are the first work to study the scaling of dropout for large NNs from a theoretical angle. A couple of experimental papers investigated specifically the question of the role of dropout in large NNs for language and vision tasks
[
Liu et al. 2023
,
Xue et al. 2023
]
. These studies confirm the effectiveness of dropout in these settings.

This question falls into the broader effort of understanding the scaling properties of large-scale NNs. In the absence of dropout, prior work has shown that the training dynamics of large NNs already exhibit a rich asymptotic phase diagram
[
Chizat et al. 2019
]
, with in particular the so-called neural tangent kernel (NTK) regime
[
Jacot et al. 2018
]
, where (overparameterized) models may reach zero training loss albeit without building data-dependent representations, and the richer feature learning
[
Chizat and Bach 2018
,
Mei et al. 2018
,
Sirignano and Spiliopoulos 2020
,
Rotskoff and Vanden-Eijnden 2022
]
associated to the so-called mean-field scaling or
\mu
-parameterization (
\mu
P)
[
Yang and Hu 2021
]
. These scalings were investigated in many works for various architectures
[
Geiger et al. 2020
,
Yang and Hu 2021
,
Vyas et al. 2023
, e.g.]
. A current active topic in this line of research concerns the large-depth scaling of ResNets
[see
Dey et al. 2025
,
Chizat 2025
, and references therein]
.

Contributions.

This paper is the first step in a broader effort to understand the theoretical properties of dropout in large-scale models. To establish a rigorous foundation, we adopt a mathematical and descriptive approach, focusing on a restricted yet illustrative setting: two-layer networks trained with gradient descent and dropout, using the squared loss and the mean-field scaling. Despite its simplicity, this setup already reveals surprising insights and challenges several widely-held beliefs about dropout.

Our main result (Theorem
1
in Section
3
) characterizes all possible scaling limits of the dynamics, depending on the relative scalings of the width, learning rate, and dropout rate. The proof, given in Section
5
, relies on different constructions depending on the scaling of parameters.

In Section
4
, we analyze the qualitative properties of these limiting dynamics. In particular:

•

All regimes involving the penalization term (
2
) require a learning rate of order
O(1/\text{width})
which is computationally impractical. A more efficient alternative that does not require small learning rates is to explicitly add a deterministic penalty
P(\theta)
to the risk. We show in Proposition
3
the equivalence of this procedure with dropout in the small learning rate regime.

•

For larger learning rates, we show that the asymptotic effect of dropout is equivalent to gradient descent in a random metric—specifically, a random block-coordinate descent scheme (Proposition
3
). We investigate the potential advantages of this regime and argue that it allows certain features to take larger steps than the local sharpness of the loss would otherwise permit (see Section
4.2.2
). We also show that a time-averaging of the NTK (Section
4.2.3
) occurs in this regime.

Let us now briefly comment on the mathematical techniques used in our proofs. The two main technical contributions correspond to cases
(II)
and
(III)
of Theorem
1
.

In case
(II)
, we establish convergence of the dynamics to a penalized Wasserstein gradient flow. The analysis relies on coupling techniques for mean-field particle systems
[
McKean 1967
,
Sznitman 1991
,
Lacker 2018
, see e.g.]
, as well as martingale methods to control the continuous-time limit of stochastic terms. While similar tools have been combined previously
[
Mei et al. 2019
, e.g.]
, our setting is different and requires careful error decomposition, as some of the relevant limits do not commute. We derive this result as a special case of a more general mean-field stochastic approximation theorem (Section
5.3.2
), which may be of independent interest. This approach allows to derive convergence both in path space and in distribution space.

In case
(III)
, we prove convergence to a mean-field jump process. This is handled by constructing an asynchronous coupling between the discrete dynamics—driven by binomial clocks—and the limiting process—driven by a Poisson clock. We also address the well-posedness of the limiting dynamics, both at the SDE and PDE levels. As we were not able to obtain a suitable uniform-in-time particle-level error control, our convergence result is formulated only in terms of time-marginal distributions, and we leave the question of pathwise convergence open for this case.

2
Setup

Two-layer neural networks.

Consider a two-layer neural network (2NN) of width
n\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
of the form
f(\theta,z)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\phi(x^{i},z)
where
\theta=(x^{1},\dots,x^{n})\in(\mathbb{R}^{p})^{n}
are the weights,
z\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
is the input and
\phi:\mathbb{R}^{p}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
. The typical structure of a 2NN is recovered when
{\phi}(x,z)=a\sigma(b^{\top}z+c)
where
\sigma:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
is the activation function and
x=(a,b,c)\in\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}
.

Given a training set
(z_{i},y_{i})_{i=1}^{m}\in(\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R})^{m}
, it will be convenient to introduce the feature map
\bm{\phi}:\mathbb{R}^{p}\to\mathbb{R}^{m}
defined by
\bm{\phi}(x)=(\phi(x,z_{1}),\dots,\phi(x,z_{m}))
. Analogously, we define
\bm{f}(\theta)=(f(\theta,z_{1}),\dots,f(\theta,z_{m}))
, which we call the
predictor
parameterized by
\theta
. By construction, it holds

\displaystyle\bm{f}(\theta)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\bm{\phi}(x^{i}),

\displaystyle\theta=(x^{1},\dots,x^{n}).

All our theoretical results are proved under the following assumption:

Assumption A

(Regularity of
\bm{\phi}
)
.

The function
\bm{\phi}:\mathbb{R}^{p}\to\mathbb{R}^{m}
is bounded and differentiable with a bounded and Lipschitz differential. We write
D\bm{\phi}(x)\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times p}
for the Jacobian matrix of
\bm{\phi}
at
x
.

Our boundedness and regularity assumptions on
\bm{\phi}
are quite strong (in particular they require bounded weights in the case of standard 2NN presented above) but they allow focusing on the core ideas in the proofs. We believe that it could be possible to relax these assumptions at the cost of an increased level of technicality (see e.g.
Wojtowytsch 2020
,
Chizat and Bach 2020
for the mean-field limit of ReLU 2NN, where
\bm{\phi}
satisfies none of these assumptions).

Dropout.

In our context, dropout consists in modifying the predictor at each step of the training process by applying a random mask to the sum defining
\bm{f}
. The mask is encoded by a vector
\eta=(\eta^{1},\dots,\eta^{n})\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
with i.i.d. entries and the
dropout predictor
is

\displaystyle\bm{f}^{\eta}(\theta)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(1+\eta^{i})\bm{\phi}(x^{i}),

where

\displaystyle\eta^{i}=\begin{cases}\frac{1-q}{q}&\text{with probability $q$}\\
-1&\text{with probability $1-q$}\end{cases}.

The
keep
rate
q\in[0,1]
is a hyperparameter that represents the probability of
not
masking a neuron. It is equal to one minus the dropout rate. Observe that
(1+\eta^{i})
equals
1/q
with probability
q
and equals
0
with probability
1-q
. We will repeatedly use the following identities:

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}[\eta^{i}]=0,\quad\mathbf{E}[(\eta^{i})^{2}]=\frac{1-q}{q},\quad\mathbf{E}[(1+\eta^{i})^{2}]=\frac{1}{q}.

GD-dropout.

We consider the squared loss on the training set
(z_{i},y_{i})_{i=1}^{m}
. Writing
\bm{y}=(y_{1},\dots,y_{m})\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
, this leads, in presence of dropout, to the following objective function (which inherits randomness from
\eta
):

\mathcal{L}^{\eta}(\theta)=\frac{1}{2}\|\bm{f}^{\eta}(\theta)-\bm{y}\|_{2}^{2}.

The dynamics under investigation, which we refer to as
GD-dropout
, is gradient descent (GD) on the dropout loss with
\eta
independently resampled at each iteration. Let
\mu_{0}\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{p})
be an initial probability distribution and
\xi^{1},\xi^{2},\dots
a sequence of independent samples from
\mu_{0}
. For a given width
n\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
, we define the (random) sequence
(\theta^{n}_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
recursively by
\theta^{n}_{0}=(\xi^{1},\dots,\xi^{n})
and

\displaystyle\theta^{n}_{k+1}=\theta^{n}_{k}-\tau\cdot n\cdot\nabla\mathcal{L}^{\eta_{k}}(\theta^{n}_{k})

where
\tau>0
is the master learning rate.
The factor
n
multiplying the gradient is introduced so that, in the absence of dropout, the limit is nondegenerate when
n\to\infty
(this is the mean-field/
\mu
P learning rate, see references in Section
1
). The dropout mask
\eta_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
is independently sampled at each iteration
k
. In this setting, the sources of randomness are thus (i) the random initialization and (ii) the random dropout masks.

By the chain rule, we obtain the following dynamics in terms of the weights
\theta^{n}_{k}=(X^{1,n}_{k},\dots,X^{n,n}_{k})
. From now on, we use capital letters for the parameters of the dynamics, to recall that we are dealing with random variables. For
i\in[1:n]
:

\left\{\begin{aligned} X^{i,n}_{0}&=\xi^{i},\\
X^{i,n}_{k}&=X^{i,n}_{k-1}-\tau\cdot(1+\eta^{i}_{k})D\bm{\phi}(X^{i,n}_{k-1})^{\top}\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(1+\eta^{i}_{k})\bm{\phi}(X^{i,n}_{k-1})-\bm{y}\right),\;k\in\mathbb{N}^{*}.\end{aligned}\right.

(3)

The rest of the paper is concerned with the analysis of this dynamical system.

3
Main result: characterization of the infinite-width limits

There are three hyperparameters (HP) involved in the definition the dynamics: width
n
, keep rate
q
, and learning rate
\tau
. Our goal is to describe how the dynamics behaves in the large-width limit
n\to\infty
and as a function of the scaling of other HP with
n
.

3.1
Notation for infinite-width 2NN

To formulate the limit dynamics, we allow
\bm{f}
to be parameterized by a probability measure (thereby overloading notations). For
\mu\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{p})
, let

\displaystyle\bm{f}(\mu)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{p}}\bm{\phi}(x)\mathrm{d}\mu(x).

A finite-width 2NN with weights
\theta=(x^{1},\dots,x^{n})
is obtained as the particular case
\bm{f}(\hat{\mu}^{n})
where
\hat{\mu}^{n}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\delta_{x^{i}}
is the empirical distribution of weights. This parameterization by a measure leads to an objective function
\mathcal{L}:\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{p})\to\mathbb{R}

\mathcal{L}(\mu)=\frac{1}{2}\|\bm{f}(\mu)-\bm{y}\|_{2}^{2}.

Before stating our main result, let us introduce some useful definitions and notations.

1.

The mean potential
V:\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{p})\times\mathbb{R}^{p}\to\mathbb{R}
is defined as
V[\mu](x)=\bm{\phi}(x)^{\top}(\bm{f}(\mu)-\bm{y})
. This function represents the Fréchet derivative of
\mathcal{L}
, seen as a functional over finite signed measures, in the sense that for any finite signed measure
\sigma
on
\mathbb{R}^{p}
, it holds as
\varepsilon\to 0

\mathcal{L}(\mu+\varepsilon\sigma)=\mathcal{L}(\mu)+\varepsilon\int V[\mu](x)\mathrm{d}\sigma(x)+o(\varepsilon).

In the following, we will frequently make use of the gradient of
V
with respect to its second argument, that is

\nabla V:\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{p})\times\mathbb{R}^{p}\to\mathbb{R}^{p}\,,\quad\nabla V[\mu](x)=D\bm{\phi}(x)^{\top}(\bm{f}(\mu)-\bm{y}).

2.

Given a probability measure
\mu\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{p})
and a measurable map
T:\mathbb{R}^{p}\to\mathbb{R}^{p}
, the pushforward measure
T_{\#}\mu
is defined as
T_{\#}\mu(B)=\mu(T^{-1}(B))
for all Borel sets
B\subset\mathbb{R}^{p}
. We also denote by
\nabla\cdot
the divergence operator, which is the opposite of the adjoint of the gradient operator.

3.

Given a Polish space
(\mathcal{X},\dist)
(such as
\mathbb{R}^{p}
, see more details in Sec.
5.1
), the
L^{1}
-Wasserstein distance on the space
\mathcal{P}_{1}(\mathcal{X})
of probability measures with finite first moment is defined as

W_{1}(\mu,\nu)=\sup_{\psi\in\text{1-}\mathrm{Lip}(\mathcal{X})}\int\psi\mathrm{d}(\mu-\nu)=\inf_{\pi\in\Pi(\mu,\nu)}\int\dist(x,y)\mathrm{d}\pi(x,y)

where the supremum runs over
\text{1-}\mathrm{Lip}(\mathcal{X})
the set of
1
-Lipschitz functions from
\mathcal{X}
to
\mathbb{R}
, and the infimum runs over the set of transport plans
\Pi(\mu,\nu)=\{\pi\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{X})\;;\;\int\pi(\cdot,\mathrm{d}y)=\mu\text{ and }\int\pi(\mathrm{d}x,\cdot)=\nu\}
.
The space
(\mathcal{P}_{1}(\mathcal{X}),W_{1})
is a Polish space
[
Villani 2008
, Chap. 6]
.

Main result

In this section, we describe the various limits for dropout-GD in the large-width limit

Assumption B

(Nondegenerate limits)

Assume that the sequences
\tau_{n}

•

The scalar
\alpha_{n}

\eta^{i}_{k}>0

\alpha_{n}

\alpha_{n}

\alpha_{n}\to+\infty

1
1

1

The overall effect on the predictor of these two opposite trends – fewer but larger updates – depends in general on the growth of

\bm{\phi}

. We thus assume that
\alpha=\lim_{n}\alpha_{n}<+\infty

•

The scalar
\beta_{n}

the stronger notion of convergence at the level of particle’s trajectories. In mathematical terms, for any
m\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
fixed, we obtain the convergence in distribution of the family of paths
(X^{1,n},\dots,X^{m,n})
towards
(Y^{1},\dots,Y^{m})
where
(Y^{i})
are independent realizations of a limit process given in Section
5
.

2.

Interestingly, in all nondegenerate limits,
\bm{f}(\rho_{t})
evolves deterministically.

3.

Our results are proved for the squared loss, but it would not be difficult to extend them to any smooth loss when
\beta=0

4
Qualitative properties of the various limits

In this section, we discuss some properties of the asymptotic processes presented previously.

4.1
The penalized Wasserstein gradient flow limit
(II)
: equivalence with explicit penalization

The only two limits that exhibit the dropout penalty, that is when dropout noise remains asymptotically biased (see (

(IV)
and
(II)
with
\beta>0
. These cases involve small learning rates
\tau_{n}=O(1/n)
and keep rates

If the penalty effect is indeed beneficial and desired, a more practical approach is to dispense with dropout altogether and instead add the deterministic penalty

directly to the objective. In the next proposition, we show that this procedure is asymptotically equivalent to dropout in regimes where the penalty emerges, focusing for simplicity on case

(Explicit-implicit penalty equivalence)

4.2
The jump process limits

In this section, we discuss properties of the jump process limits. Since the continuous-time jump process limit

4.2.1
Equivalence with the random metric (RaM) technique

First, let us show that in these asymptotics, dropout can be equivalently replaced by another, simpler training technique which consists in randomly removing units only
after
the gradient has been computed. In the two-layer case, this gives the following dynamics with
i\in[1:n]
(compare with (

\left\{\begin{aligned} \tilde{X}^{i,n}_{0}&=\xi^{i},\\
\tilde{X}^{i,n}_{k}&=\tilde{X}^{i,n}_{k-1}-\tau_{n}\cdot(1+\eta^{i}_{k})D\bm{\phi}(\tilde{X}^{i,n}_{k-1})^{\top}\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\bm{\phi}(\tilde{X}^{i,n}_{k-1})-\bm{y}\right),\;k\in\mathbb{N}^{*}\end{aligned}\right.

\alpha_{n}^{-1}
and

Since the sparsity is not the motivation here and since the idea can be generalized to metrics with more than two values (

Let us state the equivalence in the discrete-time case. This result is empirically illustrated on Figure

4.2.2
A benefit of RaM: large feature updates beyond the sharpness bound

RaM has an interesting consequence in the large learning rate regime: it makes it possible for certain neurons to take larger steps than what the sharpness of the loss would otherwise permit, as long as the average effective learning rate is small enough. To present this idea, let us study the evolution of the loss along one random-metric GD step in the infinite-width limit.

For the purpose of this discussion, instead of parameterizing the neural network by a measure
\mu\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{p})
, it is more convenient to parameterize it by a random variable
X\in L^{2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{p})
, in the spirit of Lions calculus
[
Cardaliaguet 2010
, Section 6.1]
(here
\Omega
is an abstract probability space). These two parameterizations lead to the same predictor if
\mathrm{Law}(X)=\mu
. In this parameterization, for
\mathbb{R}^{p}
-valued
L^{2}
random variables
X,Y_{1},Y_{2}
, we have the following loss and
L^{2}
-derivatives
[
Cardaliaguet 2010
, Section 6.1]

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}(X)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\big\|\mathbf{E}[\bm{\phi}(X)]-\bm{y}\big\|_{2}^{2},

\displaystyle D\mathcal{L}(X)(Y_{1})

\displaystyle=(\mathbf{E}[\bm{\phi}(X)]-\bm{y})^{\top}\mathbf{E}\big[D\bm{\phi}(X)[Y_{1}]\big]

\displaystyle D^{2}\mathcal{L}(X)(Y_{1},Y_{2})

\displaystyle=\mathbf{E}\big[D\bm{\phi}(X)[Y_{1}]\big]^{\top}\mathbf{E}\big[D\bm{\phi}(X)[Y_{2}]\big]+(\mathbf{E}[\bm{\phi}(X)]-\bm{y})^{\top}\mathbf{E}\big[D^{2}\bm{\phi}(X)[Y_{1},Y_{2}]\big]

From the expression of the first differential, we obtain that the gradient’s expression is
\nabla\mathcal{L}(X)=D\bm{\phi}(X)^{\top}(\mathbf{E}[\bm{\phi}(X)]-\bm{y})
(this is a bounded random variable under Assumption
A
). Now given
X
and an independent nonnegative
L^{2}
random variable
\eta
, the random geometry update is of the form
X_{+}=X-\eta\nabla\mathcal{L}(X)
.

In these notations, the one-step evolution of the loss is given, up to second-order terms, by

\mathcal{L}(X_{+})\approx\mathcal{L}(X)-D\mathcal{L}(X)(\eta\nabla\mathcal{L}(X))+\frac{1}{2}D^{2}\mathcal{L}(X)(\eta\nabla\mathcal{L}(X),\eta\nabla\mathcal{L}(X))

Ignoring higher order terms, the decrease of the objective is guaranteed provided
S(X)\leq 1
with

S(X)\coloneqq\frac{D^{2}\mathcal{L}(\eta\nabla\mathcal{L}(X),\eta\nabla\mathcal{L}(X))}{2D\mathcal{L}(X)(\eta\nabla\mathcal{L}(X))}.

Using the independence of
\eta
, the denominator is given by

2D\mathcal{L}(X)(\eta\nabla\mathcal{L}(X))=2\mathbf{E}[\eta](\mathbf{E}[\bm{\phi}(X)]-\bm{y})^{\top}\mathbf{E}[D\bm{\phi}(X)D\bm{\phi}(X)^{\top}](\mathbf{E}[\bm{\phi}(X)]-\bm{y})=A(X)\mathbf{E}[\eta]

for some
A(X)\geq 0
. The numerator is the sum of two terms
(I)+(II)
with

\displaystyle(I)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{E}\big[D\bm{\phi}(X)[\eta\nabla\mathcal{L}(X)]\big]^{\top}\mathbf{E}\big[D\bm{\phi}(X)[\eta\nabla\mathcal{L}(X)]\big]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{E}[\eta]^{2}\big\|\mathbf{E}\big[D\bm{\phi}(X)D\bm{\phi}(X)^{\top}(\mathbf{E}[\bm{\phi}(X)]-\bm{y})\big]\big\|^{2}

\displaystyle=B(X)\mathbf{E}[\eta]^{2}

for some
B(X)\geq 0
and

\displaystyle(II)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}(\mathbf{E}[\bm{\phi}(X)]-\bm{y})^{\top}\mathbf{E}\big[D^{2}\bm{\phi}(X)[\eta\nabla\mathcal{L}(X),\eta\nabla\mathcal{L}(X)]\big]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{E}[\eta^{2}](\mathbf{E}[\bm{\phi}(X)-\bm{y}])^{\top}\mathbf{E}\big[D^{2}\bm{\phi}(X)[D\bm{\phi}(X)^{\top}(\mathbf{E}[\bm{\phi}(X)]-\bm{y}),D\bm{\phi}(X)^{\top}(\mathbf{E}[\bm{\phi}(X)]-\bm{y})]\big]

\displaystyle=C(X)\mathbf{E}[\eta^{2}]

for some
C(X)\in\mathbb{R}
. It follows that

S(X)=\frac{B(X)\mathbf{E}[\eta]^{2}+C(X)\mathbf{E}[\eta^{2}]}{A(X)\mathbf{E}[\eta]}

If
\eta=\eta_{0}
is deterministic, then the descent condition
S(X)\leq 1
is
\eta_{0}\leq\eta_{\max}\coloneqq\frac{A(X)}{B(X)+C(X)}
which is a local version of the usual smoothness-based descent lemma in optimization.

But when
\eta
is random, the factor in front of the two terms of the Hessian is different. Moreover, it is a pervasive empirical observation in the context of neural networks
[
Wei et al. 2020
, e.g.]
that it typically holds
B(X)\gg|C(X)|
. This means that provided one chooses
\mathbf{E}[\eta]
a bit smaller than
\eta_{\max}
, there is ample room to increase the variance of
\eta
—and therefore allowing randomly chosen neurons to take large steps, beyond the sharpness threshold—while maintaining the descent property. We note that this discussion is valid as soon as
\mathbf{E}[\eta]>0
and
\mathbf{E}[\eta^{2}]<+\infty
, in particular the nonnegativity of
\eta
is not essential.

4.2.3
Another asymptotic property: dynamical NTK averaging

Let us mention another interesting property of dropout in the jump process limits: the associated Neural Tangent Kernel (NTK) is smoothed in time via an exponential moving average. Let us present this phenomenon, which is an intuitive consequence of the fact that only a subset of the neurons are updated at each step, for the continuous-time dynamics (case
(III)
) where its expression is the cleanest.

As shown in Proposition
13
, the limit dynamics
(\rho_{t})
in case
(III)
can be characterized as the unique solution to the following integral equation

\displaystyle\rho_{t}=\frac{1}{\alpha}\int_{0}^{t}e^{(s-t)/\alpha}T_{\alpha}(\rho_{s})\mathrm{d}s+e^{-t/\alpha}\mu_{0}

(7)

where
T_{\alpha}(\mu)=(\mathrm{Id}-\alpha\nabla V[\mu])_{\#}\mu
is the measure obtained from
\mu
by taking a single GD step with learning rate
\alpha
on the loss
\mathcal{L}
.

The expression has a counterpart in terms of the NTK. Indeed, the tangent kernel
3
3

3

With a slight abuse of notation, we identify the NTK with its Gram matrix on the training set.
of a 2NN is a linear function of the measure
\mu\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{p})
that parameterizes it:

\text{NTK}(\mu)=\int D\bm{\phi}(w)D\bm{\phi}(w)^{\top}\mathrm{d}\mu(w).

After one gradient step with learning rate
\alpha
, the updated tangent kernel is
\text{NTK}(T_{\alpha}(\mu))
. Hence, by (
7
), we see that the tangent kernel at time
t
writes as the exponential moving average

\text{NTK}(\rho_{t})=\frac{1}{\alpha}\int_{0}^{t}e^{(s-t)/\alpha}\text{NTK}(T_{\alpha}(\rho_{s}))\mathrm{d}s+e^{-t/\alpha}\text{NTK}(\rho_{0}).

4.3
Informal justification of the ansatz for the critical limit
(IV)

In this section, we justify
informally
the ansatz given in (
5
) for the limit in the critical case
\tau_{n}\to 0
,
\alpha,\beta>0
.

At each timestep, there is a probability
q_{n}
that a neuron moves and
1-q_{n}
that it stays still. When
n
is large, the distribution of neurons at iteration
k+1
is therefore of the form

\hat{\mu}^{n}_{k+1}=q_{n}\bar{\mu}^{n}_{k+1}+(1-q_{n})\hat{\mu}^{n}_{k}

where
\bar{\mu}^{n}_{k+1}
is the distribution of the new position of the active neurons. Reorganizing this update as
\frac{1}{\tau_{n}}(\hat{\mu}^{n}_{k+1}-\hat{\mu}^{n}_{k})=\frac{q_{n}}{\tau_{n}}(\bar{\mu}^{n}_{k+1}-\mu^{n}_{k})
, this suggests a limit equation of the form

\partial_{t}\rho_{t}=\frac{1}{\alpha}(\bar{\rho}_{t}-\rho_{t})

where
\bar{\rho}_{t}
is the distribution of the new positions of the neurons that have moved at time
t
(this is the same form as the jump process limit (
4
)).

To derive the expression for
\bar{\rho}_{t}
, let us take a closer look at the update equation (
3
) for a single neuron. Conditioning on the neuron
i
moving at iteration
k
(that is, conditioning on
(1+\eta^{i}_{k+1})=1/q_{n}
), its update can be decomposed as

\displaystyle X^{i,n}_{k+1}-X^{i,n}_{k}

\displaystyle=-\tau_{n}\cdot(1+\eta^{i}_{k+1})D\bm{\phi}(X^{i,n}_{k})^{\top}\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}(1+\eta^{j}_{k+1})\bm{\phi}(X^{j,n}_{k})-\bm{y}\right)

\displaystyle=\underbrace{-\frac{\tau_{n}}{q_{n}}D\bm{\phi}(X^{i,n}_{k})^{\top}\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j\neq i}(1+\eta^{j}_{k+1})\bm{\phi}(X^{j,n}_{k})-\bm{y}\right)}_{(I)}\underbrace{-\frac{\tau_{n}}{nq^{2}_{n}}D\bm{\phi}(X^{i,n}_{k})^{\top}\bm{\phi}(X^{i,n}_{k})}_{(II)}

The second term
(II)
is simple: as
n\to\infty
, it converges to
-\alpha\beta\nabla P(X^{i,n}_{k})
. The first term
(I)
is more subtle because the number of terms in the sum follows a
\text{Binom}(n-1,q_{n})
distribution, whose expectation
(n-1)q_{n}\to 1/\beta
does not diverge, so there is no limit theorem taking place to simplify this sum. Asymptotically, the sum behaves as drawing
N
according to a Poisson distribution with parameter
1/\beta
(this is the limit of
\text{Binom}(n-1,q_{n})
distributions) and drawing
N
independent samples
Z_{1},\dots,Z_{N}
according to
\rho_{t}
to form the sum
1/(nq_{n})\sum_{j=1}^{N}{\bm{\phi}}(Z_{j})
. Hence the first term as a whole
(I)
is, in the limit, sampled via the formula
-\alpha\nabla V[\widehat{M}(\rho_{t})](X_{k}^{i,n})
where
\widehat{M}
is the operator defined below (
5
).

Finally, the update of all the neurons that move at iteration
k
are certainly not independent; however, in the limit, since
\tau_{n}\to 0
there are infinitely many such moves over an infinitesimal time interval
[t,t+\delta t]
, and they are independent in time (considering that
\rho_{t}
changes negligibly over this time interval). This leads to an averaging-over-time effect: the neurons located near
x
and that move during this time interval are distributed, at time
t+\delta t
, according to

K[\rho_{t}](\cdot|x)=\text{Law}(x-\alpha\nabla V[\widehat{M}(\rho_{t})](x)-\alpha\beta\nabla P(x))

which is deterministic. This leads to the expression
\bar{\rho}_{t}=\int K[\rho_{t}](\cdot|x)\mathrm{d}\rho_{t}(x)
and concludes the justification of ansatz (
5
).

4.4
A synthesis: the three effects of dropout and experimental illustration

Our theory allows identifying three distinct effects of dropout, with each limit in the phase diagram characterized by the combination of one or several of these effects. This can be seen by suitably decomposing the update equation (
3
), which we recall is

X^{i,n}_{k+1}=X^{i,n}_{k}-\tau_{n}\cdot(1+\eta^{i}_{k+1})D\bm{\phi}(X^{i,n}_{k})^{\top}\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}(1+\eta^{j}_{k+1})\bm{\phi}(X^{j,n}_{k})-\bm{y}\right).

Introducing an independent copy
\tilde{\eta}^{j}_{k+1}
of
\eta^{j}_{k+1}
, we get

\displaystyle X^{i,n}_{k+1}

\displaystyle=\underbrace{X^{i,n}_{k}-\tau_{n}D\bm{\phi}(X^{i,n}_{k})^{\top}(\bm{f}(\hat{\mu}^{n}_{k})-\bm{y})}_{\text{update without dropout}}-\underbrace{\frac{\tau_{n}(1+\eta_{k+1}^{i})}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\tilde{\eta}^{j}_{k+1}D\bm{\phi}(X^{i,n}_{k})^{\top}\bm{\phi}(X^{j,n}_{k})}_{\text{PN}}

\displaystyle-\underbrace{\tau_{n}\eta^{i}_{k+1}D\bm{\phi}(X^{i,n}_{k})^{\top}(\bm{f}(\hat{\mu}^{n}_{k})-\bm{y})}_{\text{RaM}}+\underbrace{\tau_{n}\frac{\eta^{i}_{k+1}\tilde{\eta}^{i}_{k+1}-(\eta^{i}_{k+1})^{2}}{n}D\bm{\phi}(X^{i,n}_{k})^{\top}\bm{\phi}(X^{i,n}_{k})}_{\text{Penalty}}.

Let us now give an interpretation of each of the terms:

1.

Propagation noise (PN) - active in case
(IV)
.
This is the perturbation of the signal in the forward pass due to the random thinning and rescaling of the forward pass. It manifests itself in the randomness of the predictor. In our context, it is a centered perturbation of the update of units of variance
{\Theta(\frac{\tau^{2}}{n^{2}}\mathbf{E}[(1+\eta)^{2}]\mathbf{E}[(\sum_{i}\eta^{i})^{2}])}=\Theta(\tau\alpha\beta)
.

2.

Random metric (RaM) - active in cases
(I)
,
(III)
, and
(IV)
.
This is, given a full gradient, the effect of its random thinning and rescaling. It is due to the mask applied in the backward pass. It leads to a centered perturbation of the update of all units of variance
\Theta(\tau^{2}\mathbf{E}[\eta^{2}])=\Theta(\tau\alpha)
.

3.

Dropout penalty - active in cases
(II)
and
(IV)
.
This is the bias of the dropout gradient, which is precisely the effect of the non-independence between the masks used in the forward and the backward pass. In the limit, the randomness of this term vanishes, and it leads to a deterministic perturbation of the update of all units of average magnitude
\Theta(\tau\beta)
(see proof of Proposition
7
for details).

This decomposition can be used to intuit the phase diagram of Figure
1
, by separating the cases according to whether
\alpha
only is positive (case
(I)
with nonvanishing learning rate and case
(III)
with vanishing learning rate) or
\beta
only is positive (case
(II)
with
\beta>0
) or both (case
(IV)
) or neither (case
(II)
with
\beta=0
).

To give experimental insight on the effect of each of these terms, we plot in Figure
3
the training logs for a width 4000 two-layer ReLU NN trained on binary classification on two classes of MNIST with the logistic loss (see details in Appendix
A.2
). The training configurations illustrate various combinations of the three effects presented above:

•

“PN” refers to dropout applied in the forward pass only, and shows the isolated effect of propagation noise.

•

“RaM” refers to dropout applied in the backward pass only, and shows the isolated effect of RaM.

•

“PN + RaM” refers to dropout applied in both the forward and the backward passes, but with independent masks. This shows the combined effect of propagation noise and RaM. The difference with “dropout” is precisely the effect of the dropout penalty.

•

“dropout” corresponds to standard dropout, which combines the effect of PN, RaM, and dropout penalty, while “no dropout” refers to vanilla SGD.

We observe that RaM almost achieves the performance of dropout. Adding dropout in the forward pass does not improve, or even degrades, performance, unless one uses the same masks in the forward and backward passes—that is standard dropout. In the early phase of training, the dropout, RaM and PN+RaM curves match, which suggests that the speed-up observed for dropout in this early phase is entirely due to RaM.

5.1
Preliminaries

The following consequence of Assumption

will be used repeatedly in the proofs, often without explicit reference.

Lemma 4
.

Under Assumption
A
, there exists
C>0
such that, for any
\mu,\mu^{\prime}\in\mathcal{P}_{1}(\mathbb{R}^{p})
and

it holds

\displaystyle\|\nabla V[\mu](x)-\nabla V[\mu^{\prime}](x^{\prime})\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq C(\|x-x^{\prime}\|_{2}+W_{1}(\mu,\mu^{\prime})),

(8)

and
\|\nabla V[\mu](x)\|_{2}<C
,
\|\nabla P(x)\|_{2}\leq C
,
\mathcal{L}(\mu)<C
.

Proof.

We recall that Assumption
A
states that
\bm{\phi}
and
D\bm{\phi}
are uniformly bounded. The last three bounds directly follow from the expressions
\bm{f}(\mu)=\int\bm{\phi}\mathrm{d}\mu
,
\nabla V[\mu](x)=D\bm{\phi}(x)^{\top}(\bm{f}(\mu)-\bm{y})
,
\nabla P(x)=D\bm{\phi}(x)^{\top}\bm{\phi}(x)
and
\mathcal{L}(\mu)=\frac{1}{2}\|\bm{f}(\mu)-\bm{y}\|_{2}^{2}

8

\displaystyle\|\nabla V[\mu](x)-\nabla V[\mu^{\prime}](x^{\prime})\|_{2}

\displaystyle=\|D\bm{\phi}(x)^{\top}(\bm{f}(\mu)-\bm{y})-D\bm{\phi}(x^{\prime})^{\top}(\bm{f}(\mu^{\prime})-\bm{y})\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\|D\bm{\phi}(x)^{\top}(\bm{f}(\mu)-\bm{y})-D\bm{\phi}(x^{\prime})^{\top}(\bm{f}(\mu)-\bm{y})\|_{2}

\displaystyle\qquad+\|D\bm{\phi}(x^{\prime})^{\top}(\bm{f}(\mu)-\bm{y})-D\bm{\phi}(x^{\prime})^{\top}(\bm{f}(\mu^{\prime})-\bm{y})\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\|D\bm{\phi}(x)-D\bm{\phi}(x^{\prime})\|_{2\to 2}\cdot\|\bm{f}(\mu)-\bm{y}\|_{2}

\displaystyle\qquad+\|D\bm{\phi}(x^{\prime})\|_{2\to 2}\|\bm{f}(\mu)-\bm{f}(\mu^{\prime})\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\mathrm{Lip}(D\bm{\phi})\cdot\sqrt{2\mathcal{L}(\mu)}\cdot\|x-x^{\prime}\|_{2}+\mathrm{Lip}(\bm{\phi})\|\bm{f}(\mu)-\bm{f}(\mu^{\prime})\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\mathrm{Lip}(D\bm{\phi})\cdot(\|\bm{\phi}\|_{\infty}+\|\bm{y}\|_{2})\cdot\|x-x^{\prime}\|_{2}+\mathrm{Lip}(\bm{\phi})^{2}W_{1}(\mu,\mu^{\prime}).\quad\qed

We note that the term
W_{1}(\mu,\mu^{\prime})
gives in general pessimistic estimates when it comes to quantitative convergence estimates to the mean-field limit, but this is not our goal in this paper so we adopt this bound for convenience. See

We also recall a
W_{1}

Lemma 5
.

Let
(\mathcal{X},\dist)

\mu\in\mathcal{P}_{1}(\mathcal{X})
, let
X^{1},X^{2},\dots
be independent samples from
\mu
, and let
\hat{\mu}^{n}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\delta_{X^{i}}
be the corresponding sequence of empirical measures. Then it holds
W_{1}(\mu,\hat{\mu}^{n})\xrightarrow{a.s.}0
(hence, in particular
\mathbf{E}[W_{1}(\mu,\hat{\mu}^{n})]\to 0
).

Proof.

We provide a proof of this well-known fact for completeness. According to the law of large numbers for empirical measures
[
Varadarajan 1958
]

\hat{\mu}^{n}
converges weakly (in duality with continuous and bounded functions) to
\mu
, almost surely. Moreover, posing
x_{0}\in\mathcal{X}
and
\psi(x)={\dist}(x_{0},x)
, we have
\int\psi(x)\mathrm{d}\hat{\mu}^{n}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\psi(X^{i})
which is a sum of i.i.d. random variables with finite first moment because
\mu\in\mathcal{P}_{1}(\mathcal{X})
. So, by the strong law of large numbers,
\int{\dist}(x_{0},x)\mathrm{d}\hat{\mu}^{n}(x)
converges to
\int{\dist}(x_{0},x)\mathrm{d}\mu(x)
almost surely. It follows, by
Villani 2008
that
W_{1}(\hat{\mu}^{n},\mu)\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{}0
almost surely.
∎

We will consider the following Polish spaces:

•

The usual Euclidean space
((\mathbb{R}^{p})^{K},\|\cdot\|_{2})
;

•

The space of continuous paths
(\mathcal{C}([0,t];\mathbb{R}^{p}),d_{t})
where
d_{t}(x,x^{\prime})=\sup_{s\leq t}\|x(s)-x^{\prime}(s)\|_{2}
;

•

The Skorokhod space
(\mathcal{D}([0,t],\mathbb{R}^{p}),d^{\prime}_{t})
of càdlàg functions (right-continuous functions with left limits at all points) where
d^{\prime}_{t}
is the Skorokhod metric
[
Billingsley 2013
, Chap. 3]
:

d_{t}^{\prime}(x,x^{\prime})=\inf_{\lambda\in\Lambda}\sup_{s\in[0,t]}|\lambda(s)-s|+\sup_{s\in[0,t]}\|x(\lambda(s))-x^{\prime}(s)\|_{2}

where

is the set of strictly increasing continuous maps from
[0,t]
to
[0,t]
.

The topologies above are those that we associate to these spaces throughout.

5.2
Case
(I)
: the discrete-time jump process limit

In this section we assume that
n\to\infty
and that
q_{n}\to q\in]0,1]
and
\tau_{n}\to\tau>0
. This is the simplest limit to study, in particular because it remains discrete-in-time in the limit.

Limit dynamics.

Consider the (discrete-time) stochastic process
(Y_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
defined recursively by
Y_{0}\sim\mu_{0}
and

\displaystyle Y_{k+1}=\begin{cases}Y_{k}&\text{with probability $1-q$}\\
Y_{k}-\alpha\nabla V[\rho_{k}](Y_{k})&\text{with probability $q$}\\
\end{cases},

\displaystyle\rho_{k}=\mathrm{Law}(Y_{k}).

(9)

By construction, it holds

\displaystyle\rho_{0}=\mu_{0},

\displaystyle\rho_{k+1}=q(\mathrm{Id}-\alpha\nabla V[\rho_{k}])_{\#}\rho_{k}+(1-q)\rho_{k},\;\forall k\geq 1.

Then the discrete-time jump process limit in the main theorem (Theorem
1
(I)
) is a consequence of the following, more precise statement:

Proposition 6
.

Let Assumption
A
hold and assume that
q_{n}\to q\in]0,1]
, that
\tau_{n}\to\tau>0
. For any
k\in\mathbb{N}
, it holds
\mathbf{E}[W_{1}(\hat{\mu}^{n}_{k},\rho_{k})]\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{}0
. Moreover, for any fixed
m\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
and time horizon
K\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
, it holds

(X^{1,n},\dots,X^{m,n})\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{\text{distr.}}(Y^{1},\dots,Y^{m})

where
(Y^{1},\dots,Y^{m})
are independent copies of the
(\mathbb{R}^{p})^{K+1}
-valued random variable
Y
.

Proof.

Let us consider constant sequences
\tau_{n}=\tau
and
q_{n}=q
, as the general case can be proved along the same lines.

Preparation.

The update equation (
3
) can be decomposed, for
k\in\mathbb{N}
, as

\displaystyle X^{i,n}_{k+1}

\displaystyle=X^{i,n}_{k}-\tau\cdot(1+\eta^{i}_{k+1})D\bm{\phi}(X^{i,n}_{k})^{\top}\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}(1+\eta^{j}_{k+1})\bm{\phi}(X^{j,n}_{k})-\bm{y}\right)

\displaystyle=X^{i,n}_{k}-\tau\cdot(1+\eta^{i}_{k+1})\left(\nabla V[\hat{\mu}^{n}_{k}](X^{i,n}_{k})+\gamma^{i,n}_{k+1}\right)

where we have gathered errors terms in
\gamma^{i,n}_{k}
defined as

\displaystyle\gamma^{i,n}_{k}

\displaystyle={\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\eta^{j}_{k}D\bm{\phi}(X^{i,n}_{k-1})^{\top}\bm{\phi}(X^{j,n}_{k-1})}.

We have under Assumption
A

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}\|\gamma^{i,n}_{k}\|_{2}\leq\sqrt{\mathbf{E}\|\gamma^{i,n}_{k}\|_{2}^{2}}=\sqrt{O\Big({\frac{n\mathbf{E}[\eta^{2}]}{n^{2}}}\Big)}=O(\sqrt{\beta_{n}})\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{}0.

(10)

Coupling argument.

Consider
n
independent samples from
Y
, labelled
(Y^{1},\dots,Y^{n})
which we couple with
(X^{1,n},\dots,X^{n,n})
via the initialization and the random dropout masks as follows:
Y^{i}_{0}=X^{i,n}_{0}=\xi^{i}
and

Y^{i}_{k+1}=Y^{i}_{k}-\tau(1+\eta^{i}_{k+1})\nabla V[\rho_{k}](Y^{i}_{k}),

where we recall that
\rho_{k}
is defined in (
9
).
Observe that the definition of
Y^{i}_{k+1}
involves the same dropout masks as that of
X^{i,n}_{k+1}
. Let us also define
\hat{\rho}^{n}_{\ell}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\delta_{Y^{i}_{\ell}}
.

By construction, it holds
\|X^{i,n}_{0}-Y^{i}_{0}\|_{2}=0
and for
k\geq 1
, using Lemma
4
and the fact that
|1+\eta|\leq\frac{1}{q}
,

\displaystyle\|X^{i,n}_{k+1}-Y^{i}_{k+1}\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\|X^{i,n}_{k}-Y^{i}_{k}\|_{2}+\frac{\tau}{q}\|\nabla V[\hat{\mu}^{n}_{k}](X^{i,n}_{k})-\nabla V[\rho_{k}](Y^{i}_{k})\|_{2}+\frac{\tau}{q}\|\gamma_{k+1}^{i,n}\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq(1+C\alpha)\|X^{i,n}_{k}-Y^{i}_{k}\|_{2}+C\alpha W_{1}(\hat{\mu}^{n}_{k},\rho_{k})+\alpha\|\gamma_{k+1}^{i,n}\|_{2}

(11)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{\ell=0}^{k}(1+\alpha C)^{k-\ell}(\alpha CW_{1}(\hat{\mu}^{n}_{\ell},\rho_{\ell})+\alpha\|\gamma_{\ell+1}^{i,n}\|_{2}).

(12)

Denoting
h^{n}_{k}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\|X^{i,n}_{k}-Y^{i}_{k}\|_{2}
, it holds
h^{n}_{0}=0
, and we deduce from (

) and from the inequality
W_{1}(\hat{\mu}^{n}_{k},\hat{\rho}^{n}_{k})\leq h^{n}_{k}
that

\displaystyle h^{n}_{k+1}

\displaystyle\leq(1+C\alpha)h_{k}^{n}+C\alpha W_{1}(\hat{\mu}^{n}_{k},\hat{\rho}^{n}_{k})+C\alpha W_{1}(\hat{\rho}^{n}_{k},\rho_{k})+\frac{\alpha}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\|\gamma_{k+1}^{i,n}\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq(1+2C\alpha)h_{k}^{n}+E^{n}_{k}

where

Since
\mathbf{E}[W_{1}(\hat{\rho}_{\ell}^{n},\rho_{\ell})]\to 0,\forall\ell\in\mathbb{N}

), and
\mathbf{E}[\|\gamma_{k+1}^{i,n}\|_{2}]\to 0
(by (

. We deduce that

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}[h^{n}_{k}]\leq(1+2C\alpha)^{k}\sum_{\ell=0}^{k{-1}}\mathbf{E}[E_{\ell}^{n}]\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{}0,\;\forall k\in\mathbb{N}.

It follows

\mathbf{E}[W_{1}(\hat{\mu}^{n}_{k},\rho_{k})]\leq\mathbf{E}[W_{1}(\hat{\mu}^{n}_{k},\hat{\rho}_{k}^{n})]+\mathbf{E}[W_{1}(\hat{\rho}_{k}^{n},\rho_{k})]\leq\mathbf{E}[h^{n}_{k}]+\mathbf{E}[W_{1}(\hat{\rho}_{k}^{n},\rho_{k})]\xrightarrow{}0

which is the first claim. For the second claim, we have by (

) that for
m\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
,

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}\Big[\max_{k\in[1:K]}\max_{i\in[1:m]}\|X^{i,n}_{k}-Y^{i}_{k}\|_{2}\Big]\leq\sum_{\ell=0}^{{K-1}}(1+\alpha C)^{{K-1}-\ell}\mathbf{E}\Big[\alpha CW_{1}(\hat{\mu}^{n}_{\ell},\rho_{\ell})

\displaystyle+\alpha\max_{i\in[1:m]}\|\gamma_{\ell+1}^{i,n}\|_{2}\Big]

\displaystyle\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{}0.

It follows that
(X^{1,n},\dots,X^{m,n})
converges in
L^{1}
to
(Y^{1},\dots,Y^{m})
. This implies the second claim.
∎

5.3
Case
(II)
: the Wasserstein gradient flow limit

In this section we assume that
n\to\infty
, that
\tau_{n}\to 0
and that
\alpha_{n}=\frac{\tau_{n}}{q_{n}}\to 0
. Because
\alpha_{n}
, which represents the average time period between two updates of a neuron – vanishes in the limit, we get a flow-like dynamics in the limit, with a continuous trajectory for each neuron.

5.3.1
Precise statement and proof

Limit dynamics.

Consider the continuous-time stochastic process
Y
that satisfies
Y_{0}\sim\mu_{0}
and

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}Y_{t}=-\nabla V[\rho_{t}](Y_{t})\mathrm{d}t-\beta\nabla P(Y_{t})\mathrm{d}t,\qquad\rho_{t}=\mathrm{Law}(Y_{t})

where we recall that
P(x)=\frac{1}{2}\|\bm{\phi}(x)\|_{2}^{2}
(hence
\nabla P(x)=D\bm{\phi}(x)^{\top}\bm{\phi}(x)
).
This is a MacKean-Vlasov process of a simple type because it is noiseless and the only source of randomness is the initialization. Under Assumption
A
, the existence and uniqueness of strong solutions is guaranteed (see for instance
[
Bauer and Meyer-Brandis 2019
]
which deals with much weaker spatial regularity). Also we have for any time horizon
T
,
\mathrm{Law}(Y)\in\mathcal{P}_{1}(\mathcal{C}([0,T];\mathbb{R}^{p}))
. It is moreover not difficult to show that
\rho=(\rho_{t})_{t}
belongs to
\mathcal{C}(\mathbb{R}_{+};\mathcal{P}_{1}(\mathbb{R}^{p}))
and that it is the unique solution in the weak sense
4
4

4

By “weak solution”, we mean that
t\mapsto\rho_{t}
is absolutely continuous for
W_{1}
and that for any
\psi\in\mathcal{C}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{p})
and a.e.
t\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{*}
, it holds
\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\int\psi\mathrm{d}\rho_{t}=-\int\nabla\psi\cdot\nabla[V[\rho_{t}]+\beta P]\mathrm{d}\rho_{t}
, see e.g.
[
Santambrogio 2015
, Sec. 4.1.2]
.
of

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\rho_{t}=\nabla\cdot(\rho_{t}\nabla[V[\rho_{t}]+\beta P]),\qquad\rho_{0}=\mu_{0}.

If
\mu_{0}\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{p})
(that is, if
\mu_{0}
has finite second moments), then this dynamics can be interpreted as the
Wasserstein (
W_{2}
) gradient flow

[
Santambrogio 2015
, Chap. 8]
of the penalized risk

\mu\mapsto\frac{1}{2}\|\bm{f}(\mu)-\bm{y}\|_{2}^{2}+\beta\int P(x)\mathrm{d}\mu(x).

Interpolated dynamics.

Our statement will also involve the following continuous-time process. We denote by
(\bar{X}^{i,n})
the piecewise affine interpolation of the discrete-time dynamics, defined by

\bar{X}_{t}^{i,n}=X_{k}^{i,n}+\frac{t-k\tau_{n}}{\tau_{n}}(X_{k+1}^{i,n}-X_{k}^{i,n}),\quad\text{for $t\in[k\tau_{n},(k+1)\tau_{n}[$}.

The gradient flow limit of the main theorem (Theorem
1
(II)
) is then a consequence of the following, more precise statement:

Proposition 7
.

Assume that
\tau_{n}\to 0
, that
\alpha_{n}=\frac{\tau_{n}}{q_{n}}\to 0
, and Assumption
A
. Then for any
t\geq 0
, it holds
\mathbf{E}[W_{1}(\hat{\mu}^{n}_{\lfloor t/\tau{{}_{n}}\rfloor},\rho_{t})]\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{}0
. Moreover, for any fixed
m\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
and time horizon
T>0
, it holds (in
\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{C}([0,T];\mathbb{R}^{p})^{m})
)

(\bar{X}^{1,n},\dots,\bar{X}^{m,n})\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{\text{distr.}}(Y^{1},\dots,Y^{m})

where
(Y^{1},\dots,Y^{m})
are independent copies of
Y
.

Proof.

In order to better highlight the structure of the argument, we obtain this result as a corollary of a general and stronger
mean-field stochastic approximation
result, Proposition
8
, stated and proved the next subsection. Let us here show that the assumptions of that proposition are satisfied, that is, that we can indeed decompose the update as in (
13
), with
\gamma^{i,n}_{k}
a Martingale difference sequence (MDS) satisfying
\max_{k\leq T/\tau_{n},i\leq n}\mathbb{E}\|\gamma^{i,n}_{k}\|_{2}^{2}=o(\tau_{n})
.

By suitably decomposing the update of (
3
), which we recall is

X^{i,n}_{k+1}=X^{i,n}_{k}-\tau_{n}\cdot(1+\eta^{i}_{k+1})D\bm{\phi}(X^{i,n}_{k})^{\top}\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}(1+\eta^{j}_{k+1})\bm{\phi}(X^{j,n}_{k})-\bm{y}\right),

we get
X^{i,n}_{k+1}=X^{i,n}_{k}+\tau_{n}b_{n}(\hat{\mu}^{n}_{k},X^{i,n}_{k})+\gamma^{i,n}_{k+1}
with
b_{n}
defined by

\displaystyle b_{n}(\mu,x)

\displaystyle=-D\bm{\phi}(x)^{\top}(\bm{f}(\mu)-\bm{y})-\frac{\mathbf{E}[(\eta^{i}_{k+1})^{2}]}{n}D\bm{\phi}(x)^{\top}\bm{\phi}(x)

\displaystyle=-D\bm{\phi}(x)^{\top}(\bm{f}(\mu)-\bm{y})-\frac{(1-q_{n})}{nq_{n}}D\bm{\phi}(x)^{\top}\bm{\phi}(x)

and

\displaystyle\gamma^{i,n}_{k+1}

\displaystyle=-\underbrace{\tau_{n}\eta^{i}_{k+1}D\bm{\phi}(X^{i,n}_{k})^{\top}(\bm{f}(\hat{\mu}^{n}_{k})-\bm{y})}_{(I)}-\underbrace{\tau_{n}\frac{(\eta^{i}_{k+1})^{2}-\mathbf{E}(\eta^{i}_{k+1})^{2}}{n}D\bm{\phi}(X^{i,n}_{k})^{\top}\bm{\phi}(X^{i,n}_{k})}_{(II)}

\displaystyle\underbrace{-\frac{\tau_{n}}{n}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j=1\end{subarray}}^{n}\eta^{j}_{k+1}D\bm{\phi}(X^{i,n}_{k})^{\top}\bm{\phi}(X^{j,n}_{k})}_{(III)}\underbrace{-\frac{\tau_{n}(\eta_{k+1}^{i})}{n}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j=1\\
j\neq i\end{subarray}}^{n}\eta^{j}_{k+1}D\bm{\phi}(X^{i,n}_{k})^{\top}\bm{\phi}(X^{j,n}_{k})}_{(IV)}.

•

Under Assumption
A
, it is clear that
(b_{n})
converges uniformly to
b:(\mu,x)\mapsto-D\bm{\phi}(x)^{\top}(\bm{f}(\mu)-\bm{y})-\beta D\bm{\phi}(x)^{\top}\bm{\phi}(x)=-\nabla V[\mu](x)-\beta\nabla P(x)
and that the latter satisfies the required regularity assumptions by Lemma
4
.

•

By construction,
(\gamma^{\cdot,n}_{k})_{k}
is a
(\mathbb{R}^{p})^{n}
-valued MDS. It remains to check that it satisfies
\max_{k\leq T/\tau_{n},i\leq n}\mathbb{E}\|\gamma^{i,n}_{k}\|_{2}^{2}=o(\tau_{n})
. Since by Cauchy-Schwartz’s inequality, a family of centered random variables
(A_{i})_{i=1}^{k}
satisfies
\mathbf{E}[(\sum_{i=1}^{k}A_{i})^{2}]\leq\mathbf{E}[k\sum_{i=1}^{k}A_{i}^{2}]=k\sum_{i=1}^{k}\mathbf{E}[A_{i}^{2}]
, it is sufficient to check this property for the terms (I) to (IV) separately. Under Assumption
A
it holds

1.

\mathbf{E}\|(I)\|^{2}_{2}=O(\mathbf{E}[(\tau_{n}\eta)^{2}])=O(\tau_{n}^{2}\frac{1-q_{n}}{q_{n}})=O(\alpha_{n}\tau_{n})=o(\tau_{n})
since
\alpha_{n}\to 0
;

2.

\mathbf{E}\|(II)\|_{2}^{2}\leq O(\frac{\tau_{n}^{2}}{n^{2}}\mathbf{E}[(\eta^{2}-\mathbf{E}\eta^{2})^{2}])
. Note that
\mathbf{E}\eta^{4}=O(1/q_{n}^{3})
and
\mathbf{E}\eta^{2}=O(1/q_{n})
so
\mathbf{E}[(\eta^{2}-\mathbf{E}\eta^{2})^{2}]=\mathbf{E}[\eta^{4}]-(\mathbf{E}[\eta^{2}])^{2}=O(1/q_{n}^{3})
. Overall
\mathbf{E}\|(II)\|_{2}^{2}=O(\frac{\tau^{2}_{n}}{n^{2}q^{3}_{n}})=O(\tau_{n}\alpha_{n}\beta^{2}_{n})=o(\tau_{n})
, again because
\alpha_{n}\to 0
;

3.

\mathbf{E}\|(III)\|_{2}^{2}=O(\frac{\tau^{2}_{n}}{n^{2}}\mathbf{E}(\sum_{j}\eta^{j})^{2})=O(\frac{\tau^{2}_{n}}{n^{2}}\sum_{j}\mathbf{E}(\eta_{j})^{2})=O(\frac{\tau_{n}^{2}}{n^{2}}\frac{n}{q_{n}})=O(\frac{\tau_{n}\alpha_{n}}{n})=o(\tau_{n})
;

4.

\mathbf{E}\|(IV)\|_{2}^{2}=O(\frac{\tau_{n}^{2}}{n^{2}}\mathbf{E}[(\eta_{i}\sum_{j\neq i}\eta^{j})^{2}])=O(\frac{\tau_{n}^{2}}{n^{2}}\mathbf{E}[(\eta_{i})^{2}]\mathbf{E}[(\sum_{j\neq i}\eta^{j})^{2}])=O(\frac{\tau_{n}^{2}}{n^{2}}(n-1)(\mathbf{E}\eta^{2})^{2}=O(\frac{\tau_{n}^{2}}{nq_{n}^{2}})=O(\tau_{n}\alpha_{n}\beta_{n})=o(\tau_{n})
.

Since all these terms are
o(\tau_{n})
, this proves that Proposition
8
applies, and thus concludes the proof. ∎

5.3.2
A mean-field stochastic approximation result

In this section, we prove the general
mean-field stochastic approximation
result used in the previous section. Consider a sequence of step-sizes
(\tau_{n})
going to
0
and a measure-dependent drift vector field
b:\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{p})\times\mathbb{R}^{p}\to\mathbb{R}^{p}
that is uniformly bounded and such that there exists
C>0
such that

\displaystyle\|b(\mu,x)-b(\mu^{\prime},x^{\prime})\|_{2}\leq C(W_{1}(\mu,\mu^{\prime})+\|x-x^{\prime}\|_{2}),

\displaystyle\forall\mu,\mu\in\mathcal{P}_{1}(\mathbb{R}^{p}),\;x,x^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}.

Consider also a sequence of velocity fields
b_{n}
that converges uniformly on
\mathcal{P}_{1}(\mathbb{R}^{p})\times\mathbb{R}^{p}
to
b
.

Let
\mu_{0}\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{p})
and let
\xi^{1},\xi^{2},\dots
a sequence of i.i.d. samples from
\mu_{0}
. Consider
\mathbb{R}^{p}
-valued martingale difference sequences (MDS)
(\gamma^{i,n}_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}^{*}}
for
n\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
and
i\in[1:n]
. This means that we assume for all
n\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
,
i\in[1:n]
, and
k\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
:

1.

\mathbf{E}[\|\gamma^{i,n}_{k}\|_{2}]<+\infty
, and

2.

\mathbf{E}[\gamma^{i,n}_{k}|\mathcal{F}^{n}_{k-1}]=0

where the filtration is
\mathcal{F}^{n}_{k}=\sigma(\{\xi^{i}\}_{i=1}^{n},\{\gamma^{i,n}_{1}\}_{i=1}^{n},\dots,\{\gamma^{i,n}_{k}\}_{i=1}^{n})
and
\mathcal{F}^{n}_{0}=\sigma(\{\xi^{i}\}_{i=1}^{n})
.

Discrete and limit dynamics.

We consider the sequence of
\mathbb{R}^{p}
-valued stochastic processes
(X^{1,n},\dots,X^{n,n})
indexed by
n
and defined for all
n\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
,

and
k\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
by

\displaystyle X^{i,n}_{k+1}=X^{i,n}_{k}+\tau_{n}b_{n}(\hat{\mu}_{k}^{n},X^{i,n}_{k})+\gamma^{i,n}_{k+1},

\displaystyle X^{i,n}_{0}=\xi^{i},

\displaystyle\hat{\mu}_{k}^{n}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\delta_{X^{i,n}_{k}}.

(13)

Our goal is to prove, under suitable assumptions, that this process converges in some sense to the continuous-time
\mathbb{R}^{p}
-valued stochastic process solving

\displaystyle dY_{t}=b(\rho_{t},Y_{t})\mathrm{d}t,

\displaystyle Y_{0}\sim\mu_{0},

\displaystyle\rho_{t}=\mathrm{Law}(Y_{t}).

The classical theory of MacKean-Vlasov equations guarantees that there is a unique strong solution
Y
for this system with
\rho=(\rho_{t})_{t}\in\mathcal{C}(\mathbb{R}_{+};\mathcal{P}_{1}(\mathbb{R}^{p}))

[

Interpolated dynamics.

In order to formalize the sense in which
(X^{i,n})
converges to

into continuous-time ones via a piecewise affine interpolation of the trajectories. For
k\in\mathbb{N}

t_{k}=\tau_{n}k
, and for

let

\displaystyle\bar{X}^{i,n}_{t}

\displaystyle=X^{i,n}_{k}+\frac{t-t_{k}}{\tau_{n}}(X^{i,n}_{k+1}-X^{i,n}_{k})

\displaystyle=X^{i,n}_{k}+(t-t_{k})b_{n}(\hat{\mu}_{k},X^{i,n}_{k})+\frac{(t-t_{k})}{\tau_{n}}\gamma^{i,n}_{k+1}

\bar{\mu}^{n}_{t}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\delta_{\bar{X}^{i,n}_{t}}
which interpolates the sequence of discrete measures
(\hat{\mu}^{n}_{k})_{k}

Proposition 8
.

Fix a time horizon
T>0

n\to\infty

\max_{k\leq T/\tau_{n},i\leq n}\mathbb{E}\|\gamma^{i,n}_{k}\|_{2}^{2}=o(\tau_{n})

\mathbf{E}[\sup_{t\leq T}W_{1}(\bar{\mu}^{n}_{t},\mu_{t})]\xrightarrow{}{}0
. Moreover, for any fixed
m\in\mathbb{N}^{*}

(\bar{X}^{1,n},\dots,\bar{X}^{m,n})\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{\text{distr.}}(Y^{1},\dots,Y^{m}),

where
(Y^{1},\dots,Y^{m})
is a family of
m
independent copies of
Y
.

Proof.

The piecewise affine path
\bar{X}^{i,n}
is differentiable over each interval
]t_{k},t_{k+1}[
with

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\bar{X}^{i,n}_{t}

\displaystyle=b_{n}(\hat{\mu}^{n}_{k},X^{i,n}_{k})+\frac{1}{\tau_{n}}\gamma^{i,n}_{k+1}

\displaystyle=b(\bar{\mu}^{n}_{t},\bar{X}^{i,n}_{t})+(b(\hat{\mu}^{n}_{k},X^{i,n}_{k})-b(\bar{\mu}^{n}_{t},\bar{X}^{i,n}_{t}))+(b_{n}(\hat{\mu}^{n}_{k},X^{i,n}_{k})-b(\hat{\mu}^{n}_{k},X^{i,n}_{k}))+\frac{1}{\tau_{n}}\gamma^{i,n}_{k+1}.

Now consider
(Y^{1},Y^{2},\dots)
a sequence of independent copies of
Y
which is such that
Y^{i}_{0}=\xi^{i}
and let us define

\displaystyle e^{i,n}_{t}=\bar{X}^{i,n}_{t}-Y^{i}_{t},

\displaystyle h_{t}^{n}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\|e_{t}^{i,n}\|_{2}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\|\bar{X}^{i,n}_{t}-Y^{i}_{t}\|_{2}.

(14)

We recall that
\rho_{t}=\mathrm{Law}(Y_{t})
and
\hat{\rho}^{n}_{t}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\delta_{Y^{i}_{t}}
. Clearly,
t\mapsto e^{i,n}_{t}
is differentiable over each interval
]t_{k},t_{k+1}[
with

\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}e^{i,n}_{t}=\underbrace{(b_{n}(\hat{\mu}^{n}_{k},X^{i,n}_{k})-b(\hat{\mu}^{n}_{k},X^{i,n}_{k}))}_{o(1)}\\
+\underbrace{\big(b(\hat{\mu}_{k},X^{i,n}_{k})-b(\bar{\mu}^{n}_{t},\bar{X}^{i,n}_{t})\big)}_{I^{i,n}_{t}}+\underbrace{\big(b(\bar{\mu}_{t}^{n},\bar{X}^{i,n}_{t})-b(\rho_{t},Y^{i}_{t})\big)}_{J^{i,n}_{t}}+\frac{1}{\tau_{n}}\gamma^{i,n}_{k+1}.

Since
e^{i,n}_{t}
is absolutely continuous and
e^{i,n}_{0}=0
, it holds
e^{i,n}_{t}=\int_{0}^{t}\Big(\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}s}e^{i,n}_{s}\Big)\mathrm{d}s
and thus

\displaystyle\|e^{i,n}_{t}\|_{2}\leq\Big\|\int_{0}^{t}\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}s}e^{i,n}_{s}\mathrm{d}s\Big\|_{2}\leq o(1)+\int_{0}^{t}\left(\|I_{s}^{i,n}\|_{2}+\|J_{s}^{i,n}\|_{2}\right)\mathrm{d}s+\sup_{s\leq t}\|M_{s}^{i,n}\|_{2}

(15)

where we have defined
M_{t}^{i,n}=\sum_{k=1}^{\lfloor t/\tau_{n}\rfloor+1}\gamma^{i,n}_{k}
. Note that we make these computations over a bounded time domain
[0,T]
, hence we write
to(1)=o(1)
. The last term comes from bounding the integral of the martingale term which is

\sum_{k=0}^{\lfloor t/\tau_{n}\rfloor-1}\int_{t_{k}}^{t_{k+1}}\frac{1}{\tau_{n}}\gamma^{i,n}_{k+1}\mathrm{d}s+\int_{\lfloor t/\tau_{n}\rfloor\tau_{n}}^{t}\frac{1}{\tau_{n}}\gamma^{i,n}_{\lfloor t/\tau_{n}\rfloor+1}\mathrm{d}s\in[M_{t-{\tau_{n}}}^{i,n},M_{t}^{i,n}]

and the last inclusion is understood as inclusion in a line segment in
\mathbb{R}^{p}
. Let us now derive estimates for
I^{i,n}_{s}
and
J^{i,n}_{s}
. It holds for
t\in[t_{k},t_{k+1}[

\displaystyle\|I_{t}^{i,n}\|_{2}

\displaystyle=\|b(\hat{\mu}^{n}_{k},X^{i,n}_{k})-b(\bar{\mu}^{n}_{t},\bar{X}^{i,n}_{t})\|_{2}\leq C\Big(W_{1}(\hat{\mu}^{n}_{k},\bar{\mu}^{n}_{t})+\|X_{k}^{i,n}-\bar{X}_{t}^{i,n}\|_{2}\Big)

and using our assumption that
b
is uniformly bounded (and therefore
b_{n}
as well),

\displaystyle\|X^{i,n}_{k}-\bar{X}^{i,n}_{t}\|_{2}\leq\tau_{n}\|b_{n}(\hat{\mu}^{n}_{k},X^{i,n}_{k})\|_{2}+\|\gamma^{i,n}_{k+1}\|_{2}=O(\tau_{n})+\|\gamma^{i,n}_{k+1}\|_{2}

and

\displaystyle W_{1}(\hat{\mu}^{n}_{k},\bar{\mu}^{n}_{t})\leq\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\|X^{i,n}_{k}-\bar{X}^{i,n}_{t}\|_{2}\leq O(\tau_{n})+\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\|\gamma^{i,n}_{k+1}\|_{2}.

Plugging these in the previous estimate yields

\displaystyle\|I_{t}^{i,n}\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq C\Big(O(\tau_{n})+\|\gamma^{i,n}_{k+1}\|_{2}+\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\|\gamma^{j,n}_{k+1}\|_{2}\Big)

therefore

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\int_{0}^{t}\|I_{s}^{i,n}\|_{2}\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle=O(\tau_{n})+2C\sum_{k=1}^{\lfloor t/\tau_{n}\rfloor+1}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\tau_{n}\|\gamma_{k}^{i,n}\|_{2}.

Regarding the second error term
J^{i,n}_{t}
, we have

\displaystyle\|J_{t}^{i,n}\|_{2}

\displaystyle=\|b(\bar{\mu}^{n}_{t},\bar{X}^{i,n}_{t})-b(\rho_{t},Y^{i}_{t})\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq C\Big(W_{1}(\bar{\mu}^{n}_{t},\hat{\rho}^{n}_{t})+W_{1}(\hat{\rho}^{n}_{t},\rho_{t})+\|\bar{X}_{t}^{i,n}-Y_{t}^{i}\|_{2}\Big)

\displaystyle\leq C(h_{t}^{n}+W_{1}(\hat{\rho}^{n}_{t},\rho_{t})+\|\bar{X}_{t}^{i,n}-Y_{t}^{i}\|_{2})

where we have used the quantity
h^{n}_{t}
defined in (
14
). Returning to (
15
) with the estimates collected so far yields

\|e^{i,n}_{t}\|_{2}\leq o(1)+C\int_{0}^{t}\|e^{i,n}_{s}\|_{2}\mathrm{d}s+C\int_{0}^{t}h^{n}_{s}\mathrm{d}s+C\int_{0}^{t}W_{1}(\hat{\rho}^{n}_{s},\rho_{s})\mathrm{d}s\\
\qquad+O(\tau_{n})+C\sum_{k=1}^{\lfloor t/\tau_{n}\rfloor+1}\tau_{n}\left(\|\gamma_{k}^{i,n}\|_{2}+\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\|\gamma_{k}^{j,n}\|_{2}\right)+\sup_{s\leq t}\|M^{i,n}_{s}\|_{2}.

(16)

By averaging over all particles we get for any
t\in[0,T]

\displaystyle h_{t}^{n}\leq 2C\int_{0}^{t}h^{n}_{s}\mathrm{d}s+g_{t}^{n}

where

g_{t}^{n}=o(1)+O(\tau_{n})+C\int_{0}^{t}W_{1}(\hat{\rho}^{n}_{s},\rho_{s})\mathrm{d}s\\
\qquad+2C\sum_{k=1}^{\lfloor t/\tau_{n}\rfloor+1}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\tau_{n}\|\gamma_{k}^{i,n}\|_{2}+\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sup_{s\leq t}\|M^{i,n}_{s}\|_{2}.

Here we may choose
t\mapsto g_{t}^{n}
to be nonnegative and nondecreasing. Therefore by Grönwall’s lemma integral form (recalled in Lemma
10
) it holds

\displaystyle\sup_{t\leq T}h_{t}^{n}\leq e^{2CT}g_{T}^{n}

(17)

We now show that
\mathbf{E}[g_{T}^{n}]\to 0
. For this purpose, let investigate the terms involving
W_{1}(\hat{\rho}^{n}_{s},\rho_{s})
,
\gamma^{i,n}_{k}
and
M_{s}^{i,n}
in its definition. By Lemma
5
, it holds
\mathbf{E}[W_{1}(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\delta_{Y^{i}},\mathrm{Law}(Y))]\to 0
in
\mathcal{P}_{1}(\mathcal{C}([0,T];\mathbb{R}^{p}))
endowed with the distance
\|x-x^{\prime}\|_{T}=\sup_{s\leq T}\|x(s)-x^{\prime}(s)\|_{2}
. It follows (using
[
Lacker 2018
, Eq. (3.7)]
to upper-bound the supremum of marginal
W_{1}
distances by the
W_{1}
distance in path-space):

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}\Big[\int_{0}^{T}W_{1}(\hat{\rho}^{n}_{s},\rho_{s})\mathrm{d}s\Big]\leq T\mathbf{E}\Big[\sup_{s\leq T}W_{1}(\hat{\rho}_{s}^{n},\rho_{s})\Big]\leq T\mathbf{E}\Big[W_{1}\Big(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\delta_{Y^{i}},\mathrm{Law}(Y)\Big)\Big]\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{}0.

(18)

For the martingale term, we have by Doob’s inequality (recalled in Lemma
9
below)

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}\Big[\max_{s\leq T}\|M^{i,n}_{s}\|_{2}\Big]

\displaystyle\overset{\text{Jensen}}{\leq}\Big(\mathbf{E}\Big[\max_{s\leq T}\|M^{i,n}_{s}\|^{2}_{2}\Big]\Big)^{\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle\overset{\text{Lemma~\ref{lem:doob}}}{\leq}2\left(\mathbf{E}\Big[\sum_{1\leq k\leq\lfloor T/\tau_{n}\rfloor+1}\|\gamma^{i,n}_{k}\|_{2}^{2}\Big]\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle\overset{\text{assumpt.}}{=}2\left(\sum_{1\leq k\leq\lfloor T/\tau_{n}\rfloor+1}o(\tau_{n})\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}=o(1).

Finally, we have

\mathbf{E}\Big[\sum_{k=1}^{\lfloor T/\tau_{n}\rfloor+1}\tau_{n}\|\gamma_{k}^{i,n}\|_{2}\Big]\leq\sum_{k=1}^{\lfloor T/\tau_{n}\rfloor+1}\tau_{n}\Big(\mathbf{E}[\|\gamma_{k}^{i,n}\|_{2}^{2}]\Big)^{\frac{1}{2}}\leq\frac{T}{\tau_{n}}\tau_{n}\sqrt{o(\tau_{n})}=o(\tau_{n}^{1/2}).

All in all, and using our assumption that
\tau_{n}=o(1)
, we have shown that
\mathbf{E}[g^{n}_{T}]\to 0
. Plugging into (
17
), this gives

\mathbf{E}[\sup_{s\leq T}h^{n}_{s}]\leq e^{2C}\mathbf{E}[g^{n}_{T}]\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{}0

By the triangle inequality and (
18
), it follows

\mathbf{E}[\sup_{t\in[0,T]}W_{1}(\bar{\mu}^{n}_{t},\rho_{t})]\leq\mathbf{E}[\sup_{t\in[0,T]}W_{1}(\bar{\mu}^{n}_{t},\hat{\rho}^{n}_{t})+\sup_{t\in[0,T]}W_{1}(\hat{\rho}^{n}_{t},\rho_{t})]=\mathbf{E}[\sup_{t\in[0,T]}h^{n}_{t}]+o(1)\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{}0.

This proves the first claim.

As for the second claim of convergence in distribution in path space, we have, using (
16
) and the error estimates that follow:

\displaystyle\|e^{i,n}_{t}\|_{2}\leq C\int_{0}^{t}\|e^{i,n}_{s}\|_{2}\mathrm{d}s+\tilde{g}^{i,n}_{t}

for some nonnegative
\tilde{g}^{i,n}_{t}
that satisfies
\mathbf{E}[\tilde{g}^{i,n}_{T}]\to 0
. Therefore, by Grönwall’s lemma in integral form (Lemma
10
) it holds

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}[\sup_{t\leq T}\|e^{i,n}_{t}\|_{2}]\leq e^{Ct}\mathbf{E}[\tilde{g}^{i,n}_{T}]\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{}0.

For a fixed
m\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
, this implies

\mathbf{E}\big[\max_{i=[1:m]}\sup_{s\leq T}\|\bar{X}^{i,n}_{s}-Y^{i}_{s}\|_{2}\big]\leq\sum_{i=1}^{m}\mathbf{E}[\sup_{s\leq T}\|e_{s}^{i,n}\|_{2}]\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{}0.

This shows that
(\bar{X}^{i,n},\dots,\bar{X}^{m,n})
converges in
L^{1}
to
(Y^{1},\dots,Y^{m})
and the convergence in distribution follows.
∎

For convenience, we recall below two classical results which are used in the proof: Doob’s inequality (see, e.g.
[
Acciaio et al. 2013
, Theorem 1.1]
) and Grönwall’s lemma.

Lemma 9

(Doob’s inequality)
.

Let
(M_{k},\mathcal{F}_{k})_{k\geq 1}
be a real-valued martingale. Then for any
k\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
,

\mathbf{E}\Big[\max_{1\leq\ell\leq k}\|M_{\ell}\|_{2}^{2}\Big]\leq 4\mathbf{E}\|M_{k}\|_{2}^{2}=4\mathbf{E}\Big[\sum_{\ell=2}^{k}\|M_{\ell}-M_{\ell-1}\|_{2}^{2}\Big].

Lemma 10

(Grönwall lemma (integral form))
.

Suppose that
h
and
g
are nonnegative functions on
[0,T]
with
h
continuous and
g
nondecreasing, and
C>0
. If for all
t\in[0,T]
,
h(t)\leq g(t)+C\int_{0}^{t}h(s)\mathrm{d}s
then for all
t\in[0,T]
,
h(t)\leq e^{Ct}\alpha(t)
.

5.4
Case
(III)
: the continuous-time jump process limit

In this section we assume that
\tau_{n}\to 0
, that
\alpha_{n}\to\alpha>0
and that
\beta_{n}\to 0
. Because
\alpha_{n}
(representing the average elapsed time between two updates of a given neuron) does not converge to zero, we get a continuous-time jump process in the limit.

5.4.1
The limit process: definition and well-posedness

Let
\Delta T_{1},\Delta T_{2},\dots
be independent exponential random variables of expectation
\alpha
(usually denoted
\mathrm{Exp}(1/\alpha)
where
1/\alpha
is the
rate
), and let

T_{0}=0,\qquad T_{j}=\sum_{\ell=1}^{j}\Delta T_{\ell},\qquad N_{t}=\max\{j\;;\;T_{j}\leq t\}.

Remark that
T_{j}
follows a
\mathrm{Gamma}(j,1/\alpha)
distribution and
N_{t}
follows a
\mathrm{Poisson}(t/\alpha)
distribution (and more precisely,
t\mapsto N_{t}
is a Poisson process).
We consider
Y
the
\mathbb{R}^{p}
-valued continuous-time stochastic process that solves
Y_{0}\sim\mu_{0}
and

\displaystyle Y_{t}=Y_{0}-\alpha\sum_{\ell=1}^{N_{t}}\nabla V[\rho_{(T_{\ell})^{-}}](Y_{(T_{\ell})^{-}}),\qquad\rho_{t}=\text{Law}(Y_{t})

(19)

where
Y_{t^{-}}
denotes the left limit of
Y
at
t
and analogously for
\rho_{(T_{\ell})^{-}}
.
Here
T_{j}
represents the time at which the particle makes its
j
-th jump and
N_{t}
represents the total number of jumps before time
t
. It is not obvious at first that this self-referential equation has a solution and whether the solution is unique, so we first study its well-posedness. We recall the notation
\mathcal{D}(\mathbb{R}_{+};\mathbb{R}^{p})
for càdlag paths in
\mathbb{R}^{p}
.

Proposition 11
.

There exists a unique couple
(Y,\rho)
with
Y
a
\mathcal{D}(\mathbb{R}_{+};\mathbb{R}^{p})
-valued stochastic process and
\rho\in\mathcal{C}(\mathbb{R}_{+};\mathcal{P}_{1}(\mathbb{R}^{p}))
that solves (
19
). For any
T>0
, it holds
\mathrm{Law}(Y)\in\mathcal{P}_{1}(\mathcal{D}([0,T];\mathbb{R}^{p}))
. Moreover, there exists
L>0
such that for all
0\leq s,t\leq T
,
\mathbf{E}[\|Y_{t}-Y_{s}\|_{2}]\leq L\|t-s\|_{2}
. In particular,
t\mapsto\rho_{t}
is
L
-Lipschitz continuous for
W_{1}
.

Proof.

For
t\geq 0
, consider the space
\mathcal{S}_{t}=\mathcal{C}([0,t];\mathcal{P}_{1}(\mathbb{R}^{p}))
of continuous functions from
[0,t]
to the Wasserstein-
1
space
\mathcal{P}_{1}(\mathbb{R}^{p})
. Endowed with the distance
d_{t}(\mu,\mu^{\prime})=\sup_{s\in[0,t]}W_{1}(\mu_{s},\mu^{\prime}_{s})
the space
\mathcal{S}_{t}
is a complete metric space (because
(\mathcal{P}_{1}(\mathbb{R}^{p}),W_{1})
itself is complete).

Given
t>0
and the random variables
(\Delta T_{k})
and
Y_{0}
,
notice that for any fixed
\mu\in\mathcal{S}_{t}
, there is easily existence of
(Y^{\mu})
, the unique
\mathcal{D}([0,t];\mathbb{R}^{p})
-valued stochastic process that is a strong solution of

Y^{\mu}_{t}=Y_{0}-\alpha\sum_{\ell=1}^{N_{t}}\nabla V[\mu_{T_{\ell}^{-}}](Y^{\mu}_{T_{\ell}^{-}})=Y_{0}-\alpha\sum_{\ell=1}^{N_{t}}\nabla V[\mu_{T_{\ell}}](Y^{\mu}_{T_{\ell-1}})

where we have replaced
\mu_{T_{\ell}^{-}}
by
\mu_{T_{\ell}}
in the last equation using the continuity of
s\mapsto\nabla V[\mu_{s}]
for
\mu\in\mathcal{S}_{T}
, and we have replaced
Y^{\mu}_{T_{\ell}^{-}}
by
Y^{\mu}_{T_{\ell-1}}
which are equal, since
Y^{\mu}
is constant on the intervals
[T_{i},T_{i+1}[
.
The map
\Phi:\mathcal{S}_{t}\to\mathcal{S}_{t}
defined by
\Phi(\mu)=(\text{Law}(Y^{\mu}_{s}))_{s\in[0,t]}
is well-posed since, for
s\leq t
,

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}\|Y^{\mu}_{t}-Y^{\mu}_{s}\|_{2}=\alpha\mathbf{E}\Big\|\sum_{\ell=N_{s}+1}^{N_{t}}\nabla V[\mu_{T_{\ell}}](Y^{\mu}_{T_{\ell-1}})\Big\|_{2}=O(1)\mathbf{E}[|N_{s}-N_{t}|]=O(1)|t-s|.

(20)

We are looking to prove existence and uniqueness of a fixed point for
\Phi
, at least if
t
is small enough.

Let
\mu,\nu\in\mathcal{S}_{t}
. Our first step is to control the distance between the corresponding strong solutions
Y^{\mu}
and
Y^{\nu}
in terms of the distance between
\mu
and
\nu
. On the one hand, it holds

\displaystyle\|Y^{\mu}_{t}-Y^{\nu}_{t}\|_{2}

\displaystyle=\alpha\left\|\sum_{\ell=1}^{N_{t}}\nabla V[\mu_{T_{\ell}}](Y^{\mu}_{T_{\ell-1}})-\sum_{\ell=1}^{N_{t}}\nabla V[\nu_{T_{\ell}}](Y^{\nu}_{T_{\ell-1}})\right\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq C^{\prime}\sum_{\ell=1}^{N_{t}}\left(W_{1}(\mu_{T_{\ell}},\nu_{T_{\ell}})+\|Y^{\mu}_{T_{\ell-1}}-Y^{\nu}_{T_{\ell-1}}\|_{2}\right)

where
C^{\prime}=\alpha C
and
C
is the constant from (
8
).

On the other hand, we have by recursion that

\displaystyle\begin{split}\|Y_{T_{\ell}}^{\mu}-Y_{T_{\ell}}^{\nu}\|_{2}&\leq\|Y_{T_{\ell-1}}^{\mu}-Y_{T_{\ell-1}}^{\nu}\|_{2}+\alpha\|\nabla V[\mu_{T_{\ell}}](Y^{\mu}_{T_{\ell-1}})-\nabla V[\nu_{T_{\ell}}](Y^{\mu}_{T_{\ell-1}})\|_{2}\\
&\leq\|Y_{T_{\ell-1}}^{\mu}-Y_{T_{\ell-1}}^{\nu}\|_{2}+C^{\prime}\Big(W_{1}(\mu_{T_{\ell}},\nu_{T_{\ell}})+\|Y_{T_{\ell-1}}^{\mu}-Y_{T_{\ell-1}}^{\nu}\|_{2}\Big)\\
&\leq\sum_{k=1}^{\ell}C^{\prime}(1+C^{\prime})^{\ell-k}W_{1}(\mu_{T_{k}},\nu_{T_{k}}).\end{split}

Since
\|Y^{\mu}_{t}-Y^{\nu}_{t}\|_{2}=\|Y^{\mu}_{T_{N_{t}}}-Y^{\nu}_{T_{N_{t}}}\|_{2}
because
Y^{\mu}
and
Y^{\nu}
are constant on the intervals
[T_{i},T_{i+1}[
, this recursion gives

\displaystyle\sup_{s\in[0,t]}\|Y^{\mu}_{s}-Y^{\nu}_{s}\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq C^{\prime}(1+C^{\prime})^{N_{t}-1}\sum_{k=1}^{N_{t}}W_{1}(\mu_{T_{k}},\nu_{T_{k}}).

Now by the law of total expectation, it holds

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}[\sup_{s\in[0,t]}\|Y^{\mu}_{s}-Y^{\nu}_{s}\|_{2}]\leq C^{\prime}\sum_{N=0}^{+\infty}\mathbf{P}[N_{t}=N](1+C^{\prime})^{N-1}\mathbf{E}\Big[\sum_{k=1}^{N}W_{1}(\mu_{T_{k}},\nu_{T_{k}})\mid N_{t}=N\Big].

We recall that
N_{t}
follows a Poisson distribution with intensity
t/\alpha
. By the memoryless property of the Poisson distribution, conditioned on
N_{t}=N
, the random variables
(T_{1},\dots,T_{N})
are just a re-ordering (in increasing order) of
N
independent uniform samples on
[0,t]
. Hence

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}[\sup_{s\in[0,t]}\|Y^{\mu}_{s}-Y^{\nu}_{s}\|_{2}]

\displaystyle\leq C^{\prime}\sum_{N=0}^{+\infty}\mathbf{P}[N_{t}=N](1+C^{\prime})^{N-1}\frac{N}{t}\int_{0}^{t}W_{1}(\mu_{s},\nu_{s})\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle=\frac{C^{\prime}}{t}e^{-t/\alpha}\left(\sum_{N=0}^{+\infty}\frac{(t/\alpha)^{N}}{N!}(1+C^{\prime})^{N-1}N\right)\int_{0}^{t}W_{1}(\mu_{s},\nu_{s})\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle=\frac{C^{\prime}t}{\alpha t}e^{tC^{\prime}/\alpha}\int_{0}^{t}W_{1}(\mu_{s},\nu_{s})\mathrm{d}s

Since
(Y^{\mu}_{t},Y^{\nu}_{t})
is a coupling of
\Phi(\mu)_{t}
and
\Phi(\nu)_{t}
,
it follows

\displaystyle d_{t}(\Phi(\mu),\Phi(\nu))\leq\frac{C^{\prime}}{\alpha}e^{tC^{\prime}/\alpha}\int_{0}^{t}W_{1}(\mu_{s},\nu_{s})\mathrm{d}s\leq\frac{C^{\prime}t}{\alpha}e^{tC^{\prime}/\alpha}d_{t}(\mu,\nu)\,.

Hence by taking
t_{0}
small enough, the map
\Phi
is a contraction on
\mathcal{S}_{t_{0}}
. By Banach’s fixed point theorem,
\Phi
has a unique fixed-point, which proves existence and uniqueness of solutions to (
19
) on a time interval
[0,t_{0}]
.
Since the constant
C^{\prime}
above does not depend on the initial distribution
\mu_{0}
, we can repeat the same argument starting from
t_{0}
and recursively, we get existence and uniqueness over any time interval
[0,t]
for any
t>0
and hence, on
\mathbb{R}_{+}
.

Then, from (
20
) we deduce that there exists
L>0
such that for
0\leq s,t\leq T

\mathbf{E}[\|Y_{t}-Y_{s}\|_{2}]\leq L|t-s|
. Since
W_{1}(\mu_{s},\mu_{t})\leq\mathbf{E}\|Y_{s}-Y_{t}\|_{2}
, this implies that
t\mapsto\mu_{t}
is
L
-Lipschitz continuous for
W_{1}
.
∎

5.4.2
Evolution equation in the space of probability measures

In this subsection, we study the equation satisfied by
\rho_{t}=\mathrm{Law}(Y_{t})
which, as will be proven, is given by the following evolution equation

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\rho_{t}=\frac{1}{\alpha}((\mathrm{Id}-\alpha\nabla V[\rho_{t}])_{\#}\rho_{t}-\rho_{t}),

\displaystyle\rho_{0}=\mu_{0}.

(21)

We say that
\rho
solves this equation if
\rho\in\mathcal{C}(\mathbb{R}_{+};\mathcal{P}_{1}(\mathbb{R}^{p}))
and (
21
) is satisfied in the weak sense, that is

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\int\psi(x)\mathrm{d}\rho_{t}(x)=\frac{1}{\alpha}\int\Big(\psi(x-\alpha\nabla V[\rho_{t}](x))-\psi(x)\Big)\mathrm{d}\rho_{t}(x),

\displaystyle\forall\psi\in\mathcal{C}^{b}(\mathbb{R}^{p})

where
\mathcal{C}^{b}(\mathbb{R}^{p})
is the set of bounded and continuous functions on
\mathbb{R}^{p}
.
Here again, the fact that this equation is well-posed is not immediate. We first prove that the law of
(Y_{t})
where
Y
the solution to (
19
) is a solution – thereby proving existence of solutions – and separately, we will prove uniqueness of solutions below.

Proposition 12
.

If
(Y,\rho)
is the unique solution to (
19
), then
\rho
is a solution to (
21
).

Proof.

Let
\psi\in\mathcal{C}^{b}(\mathbb{R}^{p})
be a test function. For any
t,\Delta t>0
, by partitioning the expectation according to the number of jumps
\Delta N=N_{t+\Delta t}-N_{t}
between
t
and
t+\Delta t
(which is a Poisson random variable of intensity
\Delta t/\alpha
) we have, by continuity of
t\mapsto\nabla V[\rho_{t}]
,

\mathbf{E}[\psi(Y_{t+\Delta t})]=\sum_{N=0}^{\infty}\mathbf{P}(\Delta N=N)\mathbf{E}\Big[\psi\Big(Y_{t}-\alpha\sum_{\ell=1}^{N}\nabla V[\rho_{T_{N_{t}+\ell}}](Y_{T_{N_{t}+\ell}^{-}})\Big)\mid\Delta N=N\Big]\\
=e^{-\Delta t/\alpha}\mathbf{E}\Big[\psi(Y_{t})\Big]+\frac{\Delta t}{\alpha}e^{-\Delta t/\alpha}\mathbf{E}\Big[\psi(Y_{t}-\alpha\nabla V[\rho_{T_{N_{t}+1}}](Y_{T_{N_{t}+1}^{-}}))\mid\Delta N=1\Big]\\
+\sum_{N=2}^{\infty}\frac{(\Delta t/\alpha)^{N}}{N!}e^{-\Delta t/\alpha}\mathbf{E}\Big[\psi\Big(Y_{t}-\alpha\sum_{\ell=1}^{N}\nabla V[\rho_{T_{N_{t}+\ell}}](Y_{T_{N_{t}+\ell}^{-}})\Big)\mid\Delta N=N\Big].

Using that
Y_{T_{N_{t}+1}^{-}}=Y_{t}
, it follows

\frac{\mathbf{E}[\psi(Y_{t+\Delta t})]-\mathbf{E}[\psi(Y_{t})]}{\Delta t}\\
=\frac{e^{-\Delta t/\alpha}-1}{\Delta t}\mathbf{E}\Big[\psi(Y_{t})\Big]+\frac{1}{\alpha}e^{-\Delta t/\alpha}\mathbf{E}\Big[\psi(Y_{t}-\alpha\nabla V[\rho_{T_{N_{t}+1}}](Y_{t}))\mid\Delta N=1\Big]\\
\quad+\frac{1}{\Delta t}\sum_{N=2}^{\infty}\frac{(\Delta t/\alpha)^{N}}{N!}e^{-\Delta t/\alpha}\mathbf{E}\Big[\psi\Big(Y_{t}-\alpha\sum_{\ell=1}^{N}\nabla V[\rho_{T_{N_{t}+\ell}}]({Y_{T_{N_{t}+\ell}^{-}}})\Big)\mid\Delta N=N\Big].

We then get

\frac{\mathbf{E}[\psi(Y_{t+\Delta t})]-\mathbf{E}[\psi(Y_{t})]}{\Delta t}\xrightarrow[\Delta t\to 0]{}\frac{-1}{\alpha}\mathbf{E}[\psi(Y_{t})]+\frac{1}{\alpha}\mathbf{E}\Big[\psi(Y_{t}-\alpha\nabla V[\rho_{t}](Y_{t}))\Big]+0,

by the continuity of
t\mapsto\nabla V[\rho_{t}]
in the supremum norm for the second term (indeed, if
\Delta N=1
then
T_{N_{1}+1}\in[t,t+\Delta t]
), and the boundedness of
\psi
to obtain that the last expression converges to
0
.
This shows that
t\mapsto\mathbf{E}[\psi(Y_{t})]
is differentiable, with derivative

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\mathbf{E}[\psi(Y_{t})]=\frac{1}{\alpha}\mathbf{E}[\psi(Y_{t}-\alpha\nabla V[\rho_{t}](Y_{t}))-\psi(Y_{t})]

which, complemented with the initial condition
\mathbf{E}[\psi(Y_{0})]=\int\psi\mathrm{d}\mu_{0}
, shows that
\rho
is a weak solution to (
21
).
∎

Regarding uniqueness of solutions of the evolution equation (
21
), we will use a fixed-point argument that directly proves existence and uniqueness, independently of the stochastic process solving (
19
).

Proposition 13
.

Given
\mu_{0}\in\mathcal{P}_{1}(\mathbb{R}^{p})
, there exists a unique
\rho\in\mathcal{C}(\mathbb{R}_{+};\mathcal{P}_{1}(\mathbb{R}^{p}))
that solves (
21
). It is also the unique solution to the integral equation:

\displaystyle\rho_{t}=e^{-t/\alpha}\mu_{0}+\frac{1}{\alpha}\int_{0}^{t}e^{(s-t)/\alpha}(\mathrm{Id}-\alpha\nabla V[\rho_{s}])_{\#}\rho_{s}\mathrm{d}s.

Proof.

Consider the operator
\Phi:\mathcal{C}(\mathbb{R}_{+};\mathcal{P}_{1}(\mathbb{R}^{p}))\to\mathcal{C}(\mathbb{R}_{+};\mathcal{P}_{1}(\mathbb{R}^{p}))
defined as

\Phi(\rho)_{t}=e^{-t/\alpha}\mu_{0}+\frac{1}{\alpha}\int_{0}^{t}e^{(s-t)/\alpha}(\mathrm{Id}-\alpha\nabla V[\rho_{s}])_{\#}\rho_{s}\mathrm{d}s.

Let us check that this operator indeed maps into
\mathcal{C}(\mathbb{R}_{+};\mathcal{P}_{1}(\mathbb{R}^{p}))
. First, the right-hand side of this equation should be understood in duality with bounded continuous functions, that is
\Phi(\rho)_{t}
is characterized by

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{p}}\psi\mathrm{d}\Phi(\rho)_{t}=e^{-t/\alpha}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{p}}\psi\mathrm{d}\mu_{0}+\frac{1}{\alpha}\int_{0}^{t}e^{(s-t)/\alpha}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{p}}\psi(x-\alpha\nabla V[\rho_{s}](x))\mathrm{d}\rho_{s}(x)\mathrm{d}s.

Since the right-hand side of this equation defines a continuous linear map over
\mathcal{C}^{b}(\mathbb{R}^{p})
that is nonnegative and maps the constant function equal to
1
to
1
, this indeed defines a probability measure. Moreover, for any
\psi\in\mathcal{C}^{b}(\mathbb{R}^{p})
, the map
t\mapsto\int\psi\Phi(\rho)_{t}
is differentiable and

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\int\psi\mathrm{d}\Phi(\rho)_{t}

\displaystyle=-\frac{e^{-t/\alpha}}{\alpha}\int\psi\mathrm{d}\mu_{0}-\frac{1}{\alpha^{2}}\int_{0}^{t}e^{(s-t)/\alpha}\int\psi(x-\alpha\nabla V[\rho_{s}](x))\mathrm{d}\rho_{s}(x)\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle\qquad+\frac{1}{\alpha}\int\psi(x-\alpha\nabla V[\rho_{t}](x))\mathrm{d}\rho_{t}(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\alpha}\left[\int\psi(x-\alpha\nabla V[\rho_{t}](x))\mathrm{d}\rho_{t}(x)-\int\psi\mathrm{d}\Phi(\rho_{t})\right].

Clearly, if
\Phi(\rho)=\rho
then
\rho
solves (
21
). On the other hand assume that
\rho
solves (
21
); then by the previous computation, for
\psi\in\mathcal{C}^{b}(\mathbb{R}^{p})
it holds for almost every
t\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
,

\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\int\psi\mathrm{d}\Phi(\rho)_{t}=\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\int\psi\mathrm{d}\rho_{t}+\frac{1}{\alpha}\int\psi\mathrm{d}\rho_{t}-\frac{1}{\alpha}\int\psi\mathrm{d}\Phi(\rho)_{t}.

So if we let
h(t)=\int\psi\mathrm{d}(\Phi(\rho)_{t}-\rho_{t})
this function is absolutely continuous with
h(0)=0
and for almost every
t\in\mathbb{R}_{+}

h^{\prime}(t)=-\frac{1}{\alpha}h(t)

and so
h(t)=0
. This implies that
\rho
is a fixed point of
\Phi
. So
\rho
solves (
21
) iff
\Phi(\rho)=\rho
. We are thus looking for fixed points of
\Phi
.

Recall that
\mathcal{S}_{t}=\mathcal{C}([0,t];\mathcal{P}_{1}(\mathbb{R}^{p}))
is endowed with the metric
d_{t}(\rho,\rho^{\prime})=\sup_{s\in[0,t]}W_{1}(\rho_{s},\rho^{\prime}_{s})
. We will next show that
\Phi
is a contraction of the complete metric space
(\mathcal{S}_{t},d_{t})
for
t
small enough. By the subadditivity of
W_{1}
(
[
Villani 2008
, Theorem 4.8]
), we have

\displaystyle W_{1}(\Phi(\rho)_{t},\Phi(\rho^{\prime})_{t})

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{\alpha}\int_{0}^{t}e^{(s-t)/\alpha}W_{1}((I-\alpha\nabla V[\rho_{s}])_{\#}\rho_{s},(I-\alpha\nabla V[\rho^{\prime}_{s}])_{\#}\rho^{\prime}_{s})\mathrm{d}s.

Now for all Lipschitz maps
T,T^{\prime}:\mathbb{R}^{p}\to\mathbb{R}^{p}
it is not difficult to show that

\displaystyle W_{1}((\mathrm{Id}+T)_{\#}\rho,

\displaystyle(\mathrm{Id}+T^{\prime})_{\#}\rho^{\prime})

\displaystyle\leq W_{1}((\mathrm{Id}+T)_{\#}\rho,(\mathrm{Id}+T)_{\#}\rho^{\prime})+W_{1}((\mathrm{Id}+T)_{\#}\rho^{\prime},(\mathrm{Id}+T^{\prime})_{\#}\rho^{\prime})

\displaystyle\leq\text{Lip}(T)W_{1}(\rho,\rho^{\prime})+\|T-T^{\prime}\|_{L^{1}(\rho^{\prime})}.

Coming back to our previous formula, we deduce

\displaystyle W_{1}(\Phi(\rho)_{t},\Phi(\rho^{\prime})_{t})

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{\alpha}\int_{0}^{t}e^{(s-t)/\alpha}(LW_{1}(\rho_{s},\rho^{\prime}_{s})+\alpha\|\nabla V[\rho_{s}]-\nabla V[\rho^{\prime}_{s}]\|_{\infty})\mathrm{d}s

where
L
here refers to a bound on the Lipschitz constant of any map of the form
x\mapsto-\alpha\nabla V[\rho_{s}](x)
which exists under Assumption
A
. Since the last term is also bounded by
{C}W_{1}(\rho_{s},\rho^{\prime}_{s})
by Lemma
4
, one has that for some
{C^{\prime}}>0
,

W_{1}(\Phi(\rho)_{t},\Phi(\rho^{\prime})_{t})\leq{C^{\prime}}\int_{0}^{t}e^{(s-t)/\alpha}W_{1}(\rho_{s},\rho^{\prime}_{s})\mathrm{d}s.

This implies

d_{t}(\Phi(\rho),\Phi(\rho^{\prime}))\leq{C^{\prime}}\int_{0}^{t}e^{(s-t)/\alpha}W_{1}(\rho_{s},\rho^{\prime}_{s})\mathrm{d}s\leq{C^{\prime}}\alpha(1-e^{-t/\alpha})d_{t}(\rho,\rho^{\prime}).

Hence for
t
small enough,
\Phi
is a contraction and admits a unique fixed point. Since
{C^{\prime}}
does not depend on
\mu_{0}
, we can repeat the argument and get existence and uniqueness on
\mathbb{R}_{+}
.
∎

5.4.3
Proof of the mean-field limit

Piecewise constant interpolation.

In order to formalize the sense in which
(X^{i,n})
converges to
Y
, let us map the discrete-time processes
(X^{i,n})
into continuous-time ones via a piecewise constant interpolation of the trajectories. For
k\in\mathbb{N}
, let
t_{k}=\tau k
, and for
t\in[t_{k},t_{k+1}[
let

\displaystyle\bar{X}^{i,n}_{t}

\displaystyle=X^{i,n}_{k},

\displaystyle\bar{\mu}^{n}_{t}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\delta_{\bar{X}^{i,n}_{t}}.

Claim
(III)
of the main theorem (Theorem
1
) is then a consequence of the following statement:

Proposition 14
.

Assume that
\tau_{n}\to 0
, that
\alpha_{n}\to\alpha>0
and that
\beta_{n}\to 0
. For any
t\geq 0
, it holds
\mathbf{E}[W_{1}(\hat{\mu}^{n}_{\lfloor t/\tau{{}_{n}}\rfloor},\rho_{t})]\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{}0
where
\rho
is the unique solution to (
21
).

Proof.

For this proof, we will build a coupling between the discrete and the limit dynamics and estimate the errors in an asynchronous way. This requires first to reformulate the discrete dynamics in a form analogous to the limit one.

Step 1: Reformulation of the discrete dynamics.

The first step of this proof is identical to the beginning of the proof of Proposition
6
. The update equation (
3
) can be decomposed, for
k\in\mathbb{N}
, as

\displaystyle X^{i,n}_{k+1}

\displaystyle=X^{i,n}_{k}-\tau_{n}\cdot(1+\eta^{i}_{k+1})D\bm{\phi}(X^{i,n}_{k})^{\top}\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}(1+\eta^{j}_{k+1})\bm{\phi}(X^{j,n}_{k})-\bm{y}\right)

\displaystyle=X^{i,n}_{k}-\tau_{n}\cdot(1+\eta^{i}_{k+1})\left(\nabla V[\hat{\mu}^{n}_{k}](X^{i,n}_{k})+\gamma^{i,n}_{k+1}\right)

where we have gathered errors terms in
\gamma^{i,n}_{k}
defined as

\displaystyle\gamma^{i,n}_{k}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\eta^{j}_{k}D\bm{\phi}(X^{i,n}_{k-1})^{\top}\bm{\phi}(X^{j,n}_{k-1})

We have under Assumption
A

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}\|\gamma^{i,n}_{k}\|_{2}\leq\sqrt{\mathbf{E}\|\gamma^{i,n}_{k}\|_{2}^{2}}=\sqrt{{O\Big(\frac{n\mathbf{E}[\eta^{2}]}{n^{2}}}\Big)}=O(\sqrt{\beta_{n}})\to 0,

(22)

which suggests that these quantities indeed are error terms, as it will be confirmed in the rest of the proof.

Let
\Delta K^{i}_{\ell}
be the number of steps between jump number
\ell
and
\ell+1
of the
i
-th neuron, that is if
\eta^{i}_{k}>0
is the
\ell
-th nonnegative term in the sequence
(\eta^{i}_{k})_{k}
, we set
\Delta K^{i}_{\ell}=\min\{k^{\prime}\;;\;\eta^{i}_{k+k^{\prime}}>0\}
. By construction, the variables
\Delta K^{i}_{1},\Delta K^{i}_{2}\dots
are independent

\text{Geom}(q)
random variables, with support
\{1,2,\dots\}
. Also let

K^{i}_{0}=0,\qquad K^{i}_{j}=K_{0}^{i}+\sum_{\ell=1}^{j}\Delta K^{i}_{\ell},\qquad M^{i}_{k}=\max\{j\;;\;K^{i}_{j}\leq k\}

Here
(K^{i}_{j})
for
j\geq 1
is the index of the (landing of the)
j
-th jump and corresponds to the
j
-th time
(\eta^{i}_{k})_{k}
takes a positive value, while
M^{i}_{k}
is the number of jumps landing before or on step
k
.

Step 2: Coupling with the limit dynamics.

Now consider
(Y^{1},Y^{2},\dots)
independent copies of
Y
(the solution to (
19
)). Let us couple these copies with the discrete dynamics as follows: (i) we take
Y^{i}_{0}=\xi^{i}
and (ii) we take the coupling
(\Delta T^{i}_{j},\Delta K^{i}_{j})
given by Lemma
16
. For
k\in[K^{i}_{\ell}:K^{i}_{\ell+1}-1]
(hence it holds
\ell=M^{i}_{k}
), we define

e^{i,n}_{k}=X^{i,n}_{k}-Y^{i}_{T^{i}_{\ell}}.

Since
Y^{i}
is constant on the interval
[T^{i}_{\ell},T^{i}_{\ell+1}[
, we could have replaced in this definition
Y_{T^{i}_{\ell}}
by
Y_{t}
for any
t
in this interval without consequences. In words,
e^{i,n}_{k}
compares the coupled processes
X^{i,n}
and
Y^{i}
after the same number of jumps. Note that,
in expectation
, this quantity behaves similarly as the difference between these processes
at the same time
since

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}\|X^{i,n}_{k}-Y^{i}_{\tau_{n}k}\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\mathbf{E}\|X^{i,n}_{k}-Y^{i}_{T^{i}_{M^{i}_{k}}}\|_{2}+\mathbf{E}\|Y^{i}_{T^{i}_{M^{i}_{k}}}-Y^{i}_{\tau_{n}k}\|_{2}\leq\mathbf{E}\|e^{i,n}_{k}\|_{2}+{O(\tau_{n}+|\alpha_{n}-\alpha|)}

(23)

where the estimate
\mathbf{E}\|Y^{i}_{T^{i}_{M^{i}_{k}}}-Y^{i}_{\tau_{n}k}\|_{2}{=\mathbf{E}\|Y^{i}_{T^{i}_{M^{i}_{k}}}-Y^{i}_{T^{i}_{N^{i}_{\tau_{n}k}}}\|_{2}}=O(\tau_{n}+|\alpha_{n}-\alpha|)
is proved in Lemma
19
. However, the comparator
e^{i,n}_{k}
will prove simpler to work with in the following.

If
\eta^{i}_{k+1}=-1
, then it holds
e^{i,n}_{k+1}=e^{i,n}_{k}
(there is no jump), otherwise
\eta^{i}_{k+1}=(1-q_{n})/q_{n}
and
k+1=K^{i}_{\ell+1}
for
\ell=M^{i}_{k}
so it holds

\displaystyle e^{i,n}_{k+1}

\displaystyle=e^{i,n}_{k}-\frac{\tau_{n}}{q_{n}}\left(\nabla V[\hat{\mu}^{n}_{k}](X^{i,n}_{k})+\gamma^{i,n}_{k+1}\right)+\alpha\nabla V[\rho_{T^{i}_{\ell+1}}](Y^{i}_{T^{i}_{\ell}})

hence

\displaystyle\|e^{i,n}_{k+1}\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\|e^{i,n}_{k}\|_{2}+\alpha C\left(W_{1}(\hat{\mu}^{n}_{k},\rho_{T^{i}_{\ell+1}})+\|X^{i,n}_{k}-Y^{i}_{T^{i}_{\ell}}\|_{2}+\|\gamma^{i,n}_{k+1}\|_{2}\right)

\displaystyle\qquad+(C+\|\gamma^{i,n}_{k+1}\|_{2})|\alpha_{n}-\alpha|.

Moreover, the Wasserstein distance can be decomposed as

\displaystyle W_{1}(\hat{\mu}^{n}_{k},\rho_{T^{i}_{\ell+1}})

\displaystyle\leq W_{1}(\hat{\mu}^{n}_{k},\hat{\rho}^{n}_{k\tau_{n}})+W_{1}(\hat{\rho}^{n}_{k\tau_{n}},\rho_{k\tau_{n}})+W_{1}(\rho_{k\tau_{n}},\rho_{T^{i}_{\ell+1}}).

Also, by the Lipschitz property of Proposition
11
, it holds

W_{1}(\rho_{k\tau_{n}},\rho_{T^{i}_{\ell+1}})\leq L|k\tau_{n}-T^{i}_{\ell+1}|\leq L\cdot|\tau_{n}(K^{i}_{\ell+1}-1)-T^{i}_{\ell+1}|\leq L\cdot|\tau_{n}K^{i}_{\ell+1}-T^{i}_{\ell+1}|+o(1).

Hence, still when
k+1=K^{i}_{\ell+1}
, it holds (assuming
|\alpha_{n}-\alpha|\leq\alpha C
, which holds eventually):

\|e^{i,n}_{k+1}\|_{2}\leq\|e^{i,n}_{k}\|_{2}+\alpha C\Big(W_{1}(\hat{\mu}^{n}_{k},\hat{\rho}^{n}_{k\tau_{n}})+W_{1}(\hat{\rho}^{n}_{k\tau_{n}},\rho_{k\tau_{n}})\\
\qquad\qquad+L\cdot\sup_{\ell\leq M^{i}_{k}}|\tau_{n}K^{i}_{\ell+1}-T^{i}_{\ell+1}|+\|e^{i,n}_{k}\|_{2}+2\|\gamma^{i,n}_{k+1}\|_{2}\Big)+o(1)

Now, for a fixed (deterministic)
k
, accounting for the probability
q_{n}
of jumps, it follows taking expectation and using (
22
) that

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}\|e^{i,n}_{k+1}\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\mathbf{E}\|e^{i,n}_{k}\|_{2}

\displaystyle\,+\alpha q_{n}C\mathbf{E}\left(W_{1}(\hat{\mu}^{n}_{k},\hat{\rho}^{n}_{k\tau_{n}})+W_{1}(\hat{\rho}^{n}_{k\tau_{n}},\rho_{k\tau_{n}})+L\cdot|\tau_{n}K^{i}_{\ell+1}-T^{i}_{\ell+1}|+\|e^{i,n}_{k}\|_{2}\right)+o(q_{n})

\displaystyle\leq(1+\alpha Cq_{n})\mathbf{E}\|e^{i,n}_{k}\|_{2}+\alpha q_{n}C\mathbf{E}W_{1}(\hat{\mu}^{n}_{k},\hat{\rho}^{n}_{k\tau_{n}})+o(q_{n})

where the second inequality uses Corollary
18
(that shows
\mathbf{E}\sup_{\ell\leq M^{i}_{k}}|\tau_{n}K^{i}_{\ell+1}-T^{i}_{\ell+1}|=o(1)
) and Lemma
15
(that shows
\mathbf{E}[\sup_{s\leq t}W_{1}(\hat{\rho}^{n}_{s},\rho_{s})]\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{}0
). We note that the term
o(q_{n})
is uniform for
k\in[0,t/\tau_{n}]
.

Consider now the quantity
h^{n}_{k}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbf{E}\|e^{i,n}_{k}\|_{2}
which satisfies, by (
23
),

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}W_{1}(\hat{\mu}^{n}_{k},\hat{\rho}^{n}_{k\tau_{n}})\leq\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbf{E}\|X^{i,n}_{k}-Y^{i}_{k\tau_{n}}\|_{2}\leq h^{n}_{k}+o(1).

From the previous inequality, we deduce

\displaystyle h^{n}_{k+1}\leq(1+2\alpha Cq_{n})h^{n}_{k}+o(q_{n}).

Since
h^{n}_{0}=0
, it follows that

\sup_{k\leq t/\tau_{n}}h^{n}_{k}\leq o(q_{n})\sum_{i=0}^{k}(1+2\alpha Cq_{n})^{i}\leq\frac{o(q_{n})}{\tau_{n}}(1+2C\tau_{n})^{t/\tau_{n}}{=o\Big(\frac{1}{\alpha_{n}}\Big)}\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{}0.

This shows that for any
t\geq 0
,

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}W_{1}(\hat{\mu}^{n}_{\lfloor t/\tau_{n}\rfloor},\rho_{t})

\displaystyle\leq\mathbf{E}W_{1}(\hat{\mu}^{n}_{\lfloor t/\tau_{n}\rfloor},\hat{\rho}^{n}_{\tau_{n}\lfloor t/\tau_{n}\rfloor})+\mathbf{E}W_{1}(\hat{\rho}^{n}_{\tau_{n}\lfloor t/\tau_{n}\rfloor},\rho_{\tau_{n}\lfloor t/\tau_{n}\rfloor})+\mathbf{E}W_{1}(\rho_{\tau_{n}\lfloor t/\tau_{n}\rfloor},\rho_{t})

\displaystyle\leq h^{n}_{\lfloor t/\tau_{n}\rfloor}+o(1)+L|t-\tau_{n}\lfloor t/\tau_{n}\rfloor|\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{}0.\qed

Lemma 15
.

For any
t>0

\mathbf{E}[\sup_{s\leq t}W_{1}(\hat{\rho}^{n}_{s},\rho_{s})]\to 0

Proof.

We have seen in (

Lemma 16

(Jump times coupling)
.

Let
\tau_{n},q_{n}>0
be such that
\tau_{n}\to 0
and
\alpha_{n}=\frac{\tau_{n}}{q_{n}}\to\alpha>0
. If
X\sim\mathrm{Exp}(1/\alpha)
and
Y_{n}/\tau_{n}\sim\mathrm{Geom}(q_{n})
then there exists a sequence of couplings
(X,Y_{n})
such that
\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbf{E}|Y_{n}-X|=0
. Moreover, for any
x>0
, these couplings satisfy
\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathrm{ess\text{-}sup}\big[|Y_{n}-X|\big|X\leq x\big]=0
.

Proof.

For
\tau>0
, the random variable
Z=\lceil X/\tau\rceil
has distribution
\mathrm{Geom}\left(1-e^{-\tau/\alpha}\right)
. Indeed, for
k=1,2,\dots
we have

\displaystyle P(Z\geq k)=P(X>(k-1)\tau)=\exp(-(k-1)\tau/\alpha)=\left(e^{-\tau/\alpha}\right)^{k-1}

Recall that a random variable
\zeta
has distribution
\mathrm{Geom}(\pi)
iff for
k=1,2,\dots
it holds that

\displaystyle P(\zeta\geq k)=\sum_{j=k}^{\infty}(1-\pi)^{j-1}\pi=(1-\pi)^{k-1}\pi\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}(1-\pi)^{j}=(1-\pi)^{k-1}.

Hence,
Z\sim\mathrm{Geom}\left(1-e^{-\tau/\alpha}\right)
. Now consider
X^{\prime}_{n}=-X\cdot\tau_{n}/(\alpha\log(1-q_{n}))
and
Y_{n}=\tau_{n}\lceil X_{n}^{\prime}/\tau_{n}\rceil
. By construction,
X_{n}^{\prime}\sim\mathrm{Exp}\left(-\log(1-q_{n})/\tau_{n}\right)
and
Y_{n}/\tau_{n}\sim\mathrm{Geom}(q_{n})
. Moreover, we have

\displaystyle|X-Y_{n}|

\displaystyle\leq|X-X^{\prime}_{n}|+|X^{\prime}_{n}-\tau_{n}\lceil X^{\prime}_{n}/\tau_{n}\rceil|

\displaystyle\leq\left|1-\frac{\tau_{n}}{-\alpha\log(1-q_{n})}\right||X|+\tau_{n}

\displaystyle=o(1)|X|+\tau_{n}.

The first claim follows by taking the expectation, the second by taking the essential-supremum conditioned on
X\leq x
.
∎

Lemma 17
.

If
(\Delta T_{l},\Delta K_{l})
follows the coupling in Lemma
16
the following inequalities hold for large enough

\Delta T_{l}\frac{\alpha_{n}}{(1+q_{n})\alpha}\leq\tau_{n}\Delta K_{l}<\Delta T_{l}\frac{\alpha_{n}}{\alpha}+\tau_{n}.

Moreover, for any
j\leq N_{T}
and large enough

we have

T_{j}\frac{\alpha_{n}}{(1+q_{n})\alpha}\leq\tau_{n}K_{j}<T_{j}\frac{\alpha_{n}}{\alpha}+j\tau_{n}.

In particular, for
n
large enough, and any

,

M_{k}=\sup\{j\;;\;K_{j}\leq k\}\leq N_{2\tau_{n}k}=\sup\{j\;;\;T_{j}\leq 2\tau_{n}k\}.

From the Taylor expansion

it follows that for large enough

\frac{1}{\alpha(q_{n}+q_{n}^{2})}\leq\frac{1}{-\alpha\log(1-q_{n})}<\frac{1}{\alpha q_{n}}.

Now recalling that

\tau_{n}\Delta K_{l}\leq\tau_{n}\left\lceil\frac{\Delta T_{l}}{\alpha_{n}q_{n}}\cdot\frac{\alpha_{n}}{\alpha}\right\rceil\leq\tau_{n}\left(\frac{\Delta T_{l}}{\alpha_{n}q_{n}}\cdot\frac{\alpha_{n}}{\alpha}+1\right)=\tau_{n}+\Delta T_{l}\frac{\alpha_{n}}{\alpha}

and

\tau_{n}\Delta K_{l}\geq\tau_{n}\left\lceil\frac{\Delta T_{l}}{\alpha(q_{n}+q_{n}^{2})}\right\rceil\geq\Delta T_{l}\frac{\alpha_{n}}{(1+q_{n})\alpha}.

For the last expression, we use

M^{n}_{k}=\sup\{j\;;\;\tau_{n}K^{n}_{j}\leq\tau_{n}k\}\leq\sup\{j\;;\;T_{j}\frac{\alpha_{n}}{(1+q_{n})\alpha}\leq\tau_{n}k\}=N_{\tau_{n}k\frac{(1+q_{n})\alpha}{\alpha_{n}}}\leq N_{2\tau_{n}k}

for

∎

Corollary 18
.

It holds

\mathbf{E}\sup_{l\leq M_{T/\tau_{n}}}|\tau_{n}K_{l+1}-T_{l+1}|=o(1).

Proof.

By Lemma
17
we have

|\tau_{n}K_{l+1}-T_{l+1}|\leq\max\left\{T_{l+1}\Bigg|\frac{\alpha_{n}}{\alpha(1+q_{n})}-1\Bigg|,(l+1)\tau_{n}+T_{l+1}|\alpha_{n}/\alpha-1|\right\}.

Taking supremum and expectation we get

\mathbf{E}\sup_{l\leq M_{T/\tau_{n}}}|\tau_{n}K_{l+1}-T_{l+1}|\leq\\
\mathbf{E}\max\left\{o(1)T_{M_{\lfloor T/\tau_{n}\rfloor}+1},\tau_{n}(M_{\lfloor T/\tau_{n}\rfloor}+1)+o(1)T_{M_{\lfloor T/\tau_{n}\rfloor}+1}\right\}=o(1).

The last identity holds by observing that, by Lemma
17
, for
k:=\lfloor T/\tau_{n}\rfloor
,
M_{k}\leq N_{2\tau_{n}k}
, hence

\mathbf{E}(M_{k}+1)\leq\mathbf{E}(N_{2\tau_{n}k}+1)=\frac{2\tau_{n}k}{\alpha}+1=O(1),

and

\mathbf{E}(T_{M_{\lfloor T/\tau_{n}\rfloor}+1})\leq\mathbf{E}(T_{N_{2\tau_{n}k}+1})\leq\frac{2\tau_{n}k+1}{\alpha}=O(1).

∎

Lemma 19
.

We have

\sup_{k\leq T/\tau_{n}}\mathbf{E}\|Y_{T_{M_{k}}}-Y_{T_{N_{\tau k}}}\|\leq{C(\tau_{n}+|\alpha_{n}-\alpha|)}

for some constant
C>0
independent of
n
.

Proof.

Take some
k\leq T/\tau_{n}
. First we show that
|N_{\tau_{n}k}-M_{k}|
is dominated by the number of points
T_{j}
that land in a neighborhood of
\tau_{n}k
of size
\tau_{n}O(N_{T})
. Let
r
be a natural number,
\ell=N_{\tau_{n}k}
, and observe the following sequence of implications

\displaystyle N_{\tau_{n}k}-M_{k}\geq r\implies T_{l}\leq\tau_{n}k<T_{l+1}\text{ and }K_{l-r+1}>k

\displaystyle\implies T_{l-r+1}\leq\dots\leq T_{l}\leq\tau_{n}k\leq\tau_{n}K_{l-r+1}

\displaystyle\implies T_{l-r+1},\dots,T_{l}\in[\tau_{n}k-\varepsilon,\tau_{n}k+\varepsilon],

where
\varepsilon=\max\{T|\frac{\alpha_{n}}{\alpha(1+q_{n})}-1|,\tau_{n}N_{T}+T|\alpha_{n}/\alpha-1|\}
and the last implication uses the bound
|T_{l-r+1}-\tau_{n}K_{l-r+1}|\leq\varepsilon
from Lemma
17
.
Hence, if
N_{\tau_{n}k}-M_{k}\geq r
, then at least
r
points among
T_{1},\dots,T_{N_{T}}
fall into the interval
[\tau_{n}k-\varepsilon,\tau_{n}k+\varepsilon]
. The same way we can show that
M_{k}-N_{\tau_{n}k}\geq r
implies that at least
r
points fall into the interval
[\tau_{n}k-\varepsilon,\tau_{n}k+\varepsilon]
.
Thus we have

|N_{\tau_{n}k}-M_{k}|\leq\sum_{j=1}^{N_{T}}\mathbbm{1}\{T_{j}\in[\tau_{n}k-\varepsilon,\tau_{n}k+\varepsilon]\}

Recall that
T_{j}|N_{T}=m
has the same distribution as
j
-th order statistic
u_{(j)}
of i.i.d. uniform rvs
u_{1},\dots,u_{m}\sim U([0,T])
.
Before taking the conditional expectation, let us for convenience bound
\varepsilon
by
(N_{T}+1)\varepsilon^{\prime}
where

\varepsilon^{\prime}:=\max\Big\{T\Big|\frac{\alpha_{n}}{\alpha(1+q_{n})}-1\Big|,\tau_{n}+T\Big|\frac{\alpha_{n}}{\alpha}-1\Big|\Big\}.

(24)

Hence,

\mathbf{E}\big(|N_{\tau_{n}k}-M_{k}|\big|N_{T}=m\big)\leq\mathbf{E}\bigg(\sum_{j=1}^{m}\mathbbm{1}\{u_{j}\in[\tau_{n}k-(m+1)\varepsilon^{\prime},\tau_{n}k+(m+1)\varepsilon^{\prime}]\}\bigg)=\frac{2\varepsilon^{\prime}m(m+1)}{T}.

Now we can finally derive the upper bound for
\mathbf{E}\|Y_{T_{N_{\tau_{n}k}}}-Y_{T_{M_{k}}}\|
.
By construction we have for any
\ell,\ell^{\prime}>0
that
\|Y_{T_{\ell}}-Y_{T_{\ell^{\prime}}}\|\leq|\ell-\ell^{\prime}|\max\|\alpha\nabla V[\mu_{T_{j}}](T_{j})\|\leq\alpha C|\ell-\ell^{\prime}|
for some
C>0
. Finally, using the law of total expectation we get

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}\|Y_{T_{N_{\tau_{n}k}}}-Y_{T_{M_{k}}}\|

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}\alpha\max_{\ell\leq m}\|\nabla V[\mu_{T_{\ell}}](T_{\ell})\|\mathbf{E}\big(|N_{\tau_{n}k}-M_{k}|\big|N_{T}=m\big)\mathbf{P}(N_{T}=m)

\displaystyle\leq\alpha C\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}\frac{2\varepsilon^{\prime}m(m+1)}{T}\cdot e^{-T/\alpha}\frac{(T/\alpha)^{m}}{m!}

\displaystyle\leq C\varepsilon^{\prime},

where
C
is redefined on the last line. One can verify by inspection that
C
is independent of
k
and
n
. Furthermore, from its definition (
24
), we see that
\varepsilon^{\prime}
is bounded by a constant times
\tau_{n}+|\alpha_{n}-\alpha|
, which concludes the proof.
∎
