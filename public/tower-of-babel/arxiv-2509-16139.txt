Spatio-temporal, multi-field deep learning of shock propagation in meso-structured media Preprint: AIP/123-QED
M. Giselle Fernández-Godino ∗, Meir H. Shachar, Kevin Korner, Jonathan L. Belof, Mukul Kumar, Jonathan Lind, William J. Schill

Abstract
The ability to predict how shock waves traverse porous and architected materials is a key challenge in planetary defense and in the pursuit of inertial fusion energy. Yet capturing pore collapse, anomalous Hugoniot responses, and localized heating—phenomena that strongly influence asteroid deflection or fusion ignition—has remained a major challenge despite recent advances in single-field and reduced representations. We introduce a multi-field spatio-temporal deep learning model (MSTM) that unifies seven coupled fields—pressure, density, temperature, energy, material distribution, and two velocity components—into a single autoregressive surrogate. Trained on high-fidelity hydrocode data, MSTM captures nonlinear shock-driven dynamics across porous and architected configurations, achieving mean errors of 1.4% and 3.2% respectively, all while delivering over three orders of magnitude in speedup. This advance transforms problems once considered intractable into tractable design studies, establishing a practical framework for optimizing meso-structured materials in planetary impact mitigation and inertial fusion energy.



I
Introduction

Shock waves traveling through porous or architected materials drive phenomena across planetary defense and inertial confinement fusion, where controlling wave propagation is central to performance. When a shock traverses voided or lattice
-
structured media, pores collapse, localized heating develops and anomalous Hugoniot responses emerge—including counterintuitive regimes where higher shock pressures reduce post
-
shock density
Huy Pham et al. 2023
;
Kline et al. 2024a
.

In planetary-defense studies, simulations performed by LLNL for the Double Asteroid Redirection Test (DART) show that an asteroid’s porosity strongly influences the momentum transferred by a kinetic impact: porous targets absorb more energy and compact instead of ejecting material, which reduces crater ejecta and alters the post-impact velocity change
Housen, Sweet, and Holsapple 2018
;
Nichols-Fleming et al. 2022
.

For inertial-confinement fusion, LLNL’s polar-direct-drive wetted-foam targets use a thick liquid deuterium–tritium foam as both ablator and fuel; the foam lowers the in-flight aspect ratio and convergence ratio, suppresses Rayleigh–Taylor instabilities, and improves hydrodynamic stability compared with traditional ice layers
Dhakal, Haines, and Olson 2019
. Radiation-hydrodynamic simulations with HYDRA and xRAGE confirm that these thick wetted-foam shells couple laser energy efficiently and achieve high thermonuclear yield
Dhakal, Haines, and Olson 2019
. LLNL has fabricated such targets by chemically growing ultralow-density aerogel foams inside spherical mandrels, and reducing the foam mass through additive manufacturing further improves fusion performance
Zhao et al. 2020
.

Composite metal foams made of hollow metal spheres embedded in a steel or aluminium matrix can absorb large fractions of dynamic loading energy while maintaining a much lower weight than conventional solid metals. Studies of closed-cell aluminium foams show high energy absorption under dynamic loading, and graded-density designs further enhance performance
Zhao et al. 2020
;
Parveez et al. 2022
. Composite metal foam combined with ceramic systems has also demonstrated the ability to dissipate 70–80% of impact energy in laboratory tests
Garcia-Avila, Portanova, and Rabiei 2014
.

In porous and lattice materials, shock interaction with the densely distributed solid–void interfaces generates baroclinic vorticity in a manner analogous to the Richtmyer–Meshkov instability (RMI) at single interfaces. While the classical RMI describes jetting and mixing seeded by shock transit across a perturbed boundary, in meso-structured media the same mechanism is distributed across many interfaces, producing complex field evolution. Recent studies in high-energy-density physics have shown that RMI-driven jetting can be actively suppressed or enhanced through interface design and shock timing
Sterbentz et al. 2022a
;
Kline et al. 2024b
;
Sterbentz et al. 2024a
;
Schill et al. 2024a
, highlighting the broader opportunity to control shock-induced instabilities by tailoring material architecture. This perspective motivates our focus on porous and lattice configurations, where such interface-driven phenomena dominate the response.

Beyond these high
-
energy applications, shock
-
induced pore collapse also alters intrinsic material properties: it reduces ductility, strength, and toughness in metals and changes permeability and porosity in geological media, with implications for groundwater flow and resource recovery
Cheung et al. 2024
. Understanding these processes requires resolving sharp gradients and rapidly evolving multi
-
material fields across multiple scales.

High
-
fidelity hydrocodes—ranging from Eulerian and arbitrary Lagrangian–Eulerian schemes to material point and smoothed particle hydrodynamics methods are the current accuracy benchmark for shock simulations
Dobrev, Kolev, and Rieben 2012
;
Anderson et al. 2018
. However, they typically employ millions of cells and very small time steps, making each simulation prohibitively expensive and preventing brute
-
force design or uncertainty quantification. Researchers have therefore sought surrogates that maintain accuracy while accelerating inference by orders of magnitude.

Despite progress in surrogate modeling, most methods address single
-
field fluids or predict end states rather than full spatio
-
temporal evolution
Mufti, Perron, and Mavris 2024
;
Abbasi et al. 2025
. Operator
-
based surrogates oversmooth shocks
Li et al. 2020a
;
Rahman, Ross, and Azizzadenesheli 2023
, graph
-
based simulators focus on single
-
material systems
Pfaff et al. 2021
;
Covoni et al. 2024
, and scarce
-
data surrogates estimate Hugoniot curves or hotspot temperatures without capturing coupled dynamics
Li et al. 2023a
;
Bhattacharya, Seshadri, and Udaykumar 2023a
. Consequently, there remains a critical need for a surrogate capable of capturing the full, multi
-
field dynamics of shocks in meso
-
structured media.

We address this gap with a Multi
-
field Spatio
-
Temporal Model (MSTM) that autoregressively evolves the complete two
-
dimensional state of shocked porous and lattice materials. Our network simultaneously predicts density, pressure, temperature, energy, two velocity components and a material indicator using a hybrid convolutional–LSTM architecture trained on high
-
fidelity hydrocode data.

The main contributions of this work are:

1.

We develop a hybrid CNN–LSTM architecture that predicts seven coupled fields in time, representing, to our knowledge, the first multi
-
field, spatio
-
temporal surrogate for shock propagation in meso
-
structured materials.

2.

We demonstrate the model on both porous and lattice configurations, showing mean errors of 1.4% (porous) and 3.2% (lattice) and speedups of three orders of magnitude relative to direct simulation.

3.

We systematically assess performance and generalization, including tests on unseen porosities, lattice angles, and loading conditions, showing that in the most challenging lattice model, MSTM preserves integrated quantities (mass, pressure, temperature) with a mean error of 5% and supports design and uncertainty studies across a wide parameter space.

II
Related Work

A variety of surrogate families have been proposed to accelerate shock-driven simulations. Reduced-order models based on projection or manifold learning can speed up smooth flows, but linear bases smear moving discontinuities and nonlinear variants often require careful tuning and typically focus on a single physical field
Mufti, Perron, and Mavris 2024
. Physics-informed neural networks embed governing equations during training and solve parameterised PDEs, yet sharp shocks inhibit convergence and new scenarios usually require retraining
Abbasi et al. 2025
. Early convolutional accelerators replaced solver substeps, such as CNN surrogates for pressure projection, but these neglected compressibility and thermodynamics and therefore could not handle shocks
Tompson et al. 2017a
. Neural operators map functions to functions: the Fourier Neural Operator learns mesh-invariant solution maps
Li et al. 2020a
, DeepONet extends operator learning to broader nonlinear problems
Lu et al. 2021
and the U-shaped Neural Operator improves multi-scale accuracy by combining spectral and U-Net features
Rahman, Ross, and Azizzadenesheli 2023
. While fast, spectral representations tend to oversmooth localized shock fronts or introduce oscillations near discontinuities.

Graph-based simulators learn updates on meshes or particles; MeshGraphNets achieved resolution-robust rollouts for fluids and deformable solids
Pfaff et al. 2021
, and graph surrogates have reproduced explosion-driven transients in complex 3D domains with large geometric generalization and substantial speedups
Covoni et al. 2024
. These methods, however, largely address single-material fluids rather than porous or architected solids with multi-field coupling.

A complementary class of
outcome-focused
surrogates targets reduced descriptions of shock response rather than full spatio-temporal fields. Scarce-data models estimate Hugoniot curves across materials
Bhattacharya, Seshadri, and Udaykumar 2023a
; microstructure-to-hotspot mappings recover post-shock temperature fields
Li et al. 2023a
; physics-guided networks infer pore-collapse outcomes from limited simulations
Bhattacharya2023data
; and data-scarce surrogates reproduce pore-collapse dynamics in shocked porous media
Cheung et al. 2024
. More recently, latent-space dynamics frameworks have been introduced to track shock-induced pore-collapse interfaces, providing a compact representation of evolving morphology but without resolving the coupled multi-field state
Chung et al. 2025
. Bayesian neural fields supply spatio-temporal forecasts with uncertainty quantification, highlighting the importance of calibrated predictions for extreme-condition regimes
Saad et al. 2024
. Machine-learning-driven design has also been pursued, for instance to reduce Richtmyer–Meshkov jet velocity in shaped charges
Kline et al. 2024a
; such inverse design studies optimise a single metric, however, rather than the coupled, time-resolved state evolution required for porous and architected materials.

Collectively, these surrogates demonstrate the promise of data-driven modeling but are limited either to single fields, to fluid-only systems or to end-state predictions. Our MSTM framework addresses these limitations by delivering time-resolved, multi-field predictions that evolve density, pressure, temperature, velocity, energy and material distribution together, thereby enabling generalization across porosities, lattice types and loading conditions within the training distribution.

IV
Deep Learning Model

We employ a hybrid architecture combining convolutional neural networks (CNNs)
LeCun et al. 1998
and long short
-
term memory (LSTM) networks
Hochreiter and Schmidhuber 1997
to model the spatio
-
temporal evolution of seven fields—pressure, temperature, energy, density, material fraction, and two velocity components—in 2D shock problems. CNNs extract local spatial patterns such as wavefronts and pore collapses, whereas LSTMs capture temporal dependencies, enabling the model to integrate features over multiple time steps.

Architecture.
Figure
2

60\times 60
grid. The feature extractor contains two convolutional layers: a
3\times 3
Conv2D layer with 64 output channels (padding 1) followed by
2\times 2

3\times 3

2\times 2
max pool. These layers reduce the spatial resolution to
15\times 15
while preserving the receptive field, yielding spatial features of size
128\times 15\times 15
at each time step. The flattened features are processed sequentially by a four
-

(1,7,60,60)

Training procedure.
We train the model on data generated by the MARBL high
-
fidelity hydrocode. Each sample comprises five input frames and one target frame (the sixth). We normalize each field to the range
[0,1]
using min–max scaling based on training set extrema and apply the same parameters to validation and test data. The loss function is mean squared error (MSE), which penalizes large errors at shock fronts more strongly than mean absolute error (MAE); we found that MAE and cross
-
entropy losses converge more slowly and yield lower accuracy, consistent with deep
-
learning theory
Goodfellow et al. 2016
. Optimization employs the Adam algorithm with learning rate
5\times 10^{-4}
and batch size 256. We train for 1,000 epochs on LLNL’s Lassen supercomputer using PyTorch’s
DataParallel
on four NVIDIA Volta GPUs. Training takes roughly 7 hours for the porous dataset (679 training, 85 validation and 85 test sequences) and 24 hours for the lattice dataset (2,387 training, 298 validation and 325 test sequences). The train/val/test split is 80/10/10%. Because total training time scales with sequence count, the larger lattice dataset requires longer training.

Choice of a five
-
frame window.
The input window spans five time frames. This span (1.0
\mu
s for the porous simulations) was chosen to match the dominant shock transit time across mesoscale features. For the porous configuration, a shock traveling at 0.23 cm/
\mu
s crosses approximately 0.2–0.25 mm during this interval, comparable to ligament thickness in the porous aluminum. For lattice runs, the window spans 0.05–0.5
\mu
s (covering 50 frames) depending on shock speed; during this interval the shock front traverses 0.05–0.2mm, comparable to the 0.10 cm unit
-
cell pitch and typical strut thickness. During inference, this five-frame context is advanced autoregressively to generate the entire simulation sequence (60 frames for porous and 50 for lattice cases). Shorter windows would truncate the shock front before it interacts fully with microstructural features, while longer windows would require more memory and introduce vanishing gradients without significantly improving accuracy. This balanced choice thus captures the onset and progression of pore collapse, wave interactions and other nonlinearities while keeping the autoregressive rollout stable and computationally tractable.

Teacher forcing and autoregressive rollout.
During training, we employ teacher forcing: the model receives five consecutive ground
-
truth frames
\{z_{t_{0}},\dots,z_{t_{4}}\}
and predicts
\hat{z}_{t_{5}}
, without feeding its own outputs back. During inference, we seed the network with the first five true frames and then enter an autoregressive regime: each new prediction replaces the oldest frame in the input sequence, producing
\{\hat{z}_{t_{4}},\hat{z}_{t_{5}},\dots\}
until the end of the sequence (see Fig.
5
). This scheme enables the model to generate full spatio
-
temporal rollouts for up to 60 time steps in the porous case and 50 in the lattice case.

Implementation.
The MSTM model is implemented in PyTorch and parallelized across GPUs to accelerate training. Our code and trained models are available upon request for reproducibility. The layer
-
by
-
layer specification, hyperparameters and implementation details are summarized in Table
2
and Appendix
A
.

VI
Discussion

Our results demonstrate that data
-
driven surrogates can faithfully reproduce the full spatio
-
temporal evolution of shock waves in meso
-
structured materials, an achievement previously infeasible without extreme computational cost. MSTM achieves mean squared error (MSE) as low as
2\times 10^{-4}
on porous test cases and
1\times 10^{-3}
on lattice cases, with structural similarity index (SSIM) exceeding 0.98 and stable intersection
-
over
-
union (IoU) and mass conservation (CM) throughout autoregressive rollouts. These metrics indicate that the network captures both local and global features while preserving phase boundaries and total mass. Differences between porous and lattice performance are physically interpretable: smooth porous interfaces are easier to predict, whereas thin struts and sharp edges in the lattice amplify small phase shifts during rollout. Nevertheless, the model maintains robust accuracy across both configurations, achieving mean RMSE of 1.4% in porous simulations and 3.2% in lattice simulations. Given the greater difficulty of the lattice model, we further assessed integrated quantities (mass-averaged pressure, temperature, and density) showing a mean error of 5%.

By resolving multiple fields—density, pressure, temperature, energy, material fraction, and two velocity components—MSTM transcends single-field surrogates that evolve only one variable and operator-based approaches that approximate reduced mappings, providing full spatio-temporal field evolutions of the shocked state. Its approximately thousand
-
fold speedup relative to direct hydrocodes transforms once intractable shock problems into tractable design and uncertainty
-
quantification studies. Coupled with its demonstrated generalization across porosities, lattice angles and loading conditions, this makes MSTM a state
-
of
-
the
-
art tool for exploring meso-scale architectures in planetary defense and inertial fusion energy.

Looking ahead, several directions could further enhance MSTM’s impact. First, incorporating uncertainty quantification through Bayesian neural networks or ensemble methods would elevate MSTM from a fast emulator to a reliable decision
-
making core for scientific and engineering workflows. Second, coupling MSTM with differentiable physics or adjoint solvers could enable gradient
-
based optimization of porous and lattice architectures with respect to shock localization, dissipation or transmission targets. Third, the autoregressive CNN–LSTM framework is naturally extensible to higher
-
dimensional and multi
-
physics systems—such as reactive flows, phase transformations and multi
-
shock interactions, thereby broadening the design landscape for shock–material interactions. Finally, integrating MSTM with experimental data pipelines could pave the way for a paradigm of AI
-
augmented materials science, where simulations, surrogates and sensors form a closed learning loop, enabling adaptive sampling, accelerated validation and autonomous discovery of meso
-
architected materials optimized for extreme conditions.

Taken together, these advancements position MSTM not merely as a computational shortcut but as a scientific instrument—capable of supporting hypothesis generation, inverse design and interactive exploration of mesoscale structure–response relationships across diverse domains.
