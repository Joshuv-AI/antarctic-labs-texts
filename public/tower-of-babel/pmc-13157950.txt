Postoperative confusion, pneumocephalus, and seizure following uniportal endoscopic lumbar discectomy: A case report
Authors: Chris Huang, Ralph Jasper Mobbs, Alison Ma, Erin Cook
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background: Postoperative neurological disturbance after endoscopic lumbar discectomy is rare. Proposed mechanisms include dural breach, irrigation-pressure transmission into the cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) space, pneumocephalus, and chemical or inflammatory irritation. As utilization of uniportal systems with continuous irrigation increases, recognizing these interactions is increasingly important.Case Description: A 29-year-old man underwent uniportal endoscopic L5/S1 discectomy for left S1 radiculopathy. No durotomy was identified intraoperatively, and irrigation pressures were maintained between 25 and 40 mmHg. Four hours postoperatively, he developed acute confusion and a generalized tonic-clonic seizure. Computed tomography brain demonstrated intracranial pneumocephalus without hemorrhage, consistent with retrograde air entry through an unrecognized dural defect facilitated by pressure fluctuations and operative gas production. He was managed with supportive care, head elevation, supplemental oxygen, and a single loading dose of levetiracetam, with no further seizures. Cognition normalized within 24 h, and radicular symptoms resolved. Persistent postural headaches prompted re-intervention on day 7, where a small, contained durotomy adjacent to the traversing nerve root was identified and repaired endoscopically, resulting in complete symptom resolution.Conclusion: Although uncommon, acute confusion, seizure, and pneumocephalus may occur following endoscopic lumbar discectomy when dural injury allows pressure transmission or air ingress into the CSF compartment. Prompt neurological assessment, urgent imaging, and short-term monitored care support excellent outcomes. As endoscopic spine surgery continues to expand, understanding the interaction between irrigation dynamics, dural integrity, and intracranial physiology is essential for early recognition and prevention of this complication.

INTRODUCTION

Endoscopic lumbar discectomy is an established treatment for lumbar radiculopathy, offering reduced tissue disruption, shorter hospital stays, and faster functional recovery.[5,6] As utilization expands, rare but clinically significant procedure-specific complications are increasingly recognized. Postoperative confusion or seizure activity after lumbar decompression is uncommon but may arise from a combination of factors, including dural breach, irrigation-pressure transmission into the cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) compartment, pneumocephalus, intracranial hypotension or hypertension, and chemical irritation when blood products, pharmacological agents, or operative gases enter the subarachnoid space.[8,10]

Seizures following lumbar spine surgery, especially minimally invasive or endoscopic procedures, remain sparsely reported. Reported mechanisms include retrograde transmission of irrigation pressure into the cranial vault, rapid CSF pressure shifts through an occult or evident durotomy, migration of irrigation fluid or debris into the CSF, and pneumocephalus with resultant cortical irritation.[3,8,9]

With the rapid global uptake of endoscopic techniques, particularly uniportal systems reliant on continuous saline irrigation, understanding these physiological interactions has become increasingly relevant. We report a case of acute postoperative confusion and a generalized tonic-clonic seizure following uniportal endoscopic L5/S1 discectomy in a young adult.

CASE DESCRIPTION

Presentation

A 29-year-old carpenter presented with a 3-month history of progressive left S1 radicular pain. Magnetic resonance imaging demonstrated a left paracentral L5/S1 disc protrusion compressing the traversing S1 nerve root [Figure 1a and b]. Neurological examination was normal except for mild plantarflexion weakness (4+/5). After unsuccessful conservative management, he proceeded to uniportal endoscopic discectomy.

(a and b) Magnetic resonance imaging sagittal and axial with L5/S1 postero-lateral disc herniation with left S1 compression. (c) Intraoperative endoscopic discectomy with pituitary rongeur positioned on the disc fragment before removal of the fragment.

Surgical procedure

A standard transforaminal uniportal endoscopic discectomy was performed under general anesthesia with continuous saline irrigation (Elliquence, USA). Fluoroscopic localization is shown in Figure 1c. Intraoperative endoscopic views demonstrated disc material removal and neural decompression [Figure 2a and b]. A fragment retrieved is shown in Figure 2c. At the time of surgery, no durotomy was detected. No intraoperative durotomy was identified. Irrigation pressures were maintained between 25 and 40 mmHg, with brief elevation during hemostasis. The procedure was completed without hemodynamic instability, and the patient awoke neurologically intact.

(a) Retraction of S1 nerve to reveal the herniated disc fragment before removal. (b) S1 nerve decompressed following fragmentectomy. (c) Size of disc sequestration.

Postoperative course

Within 4 h of surgery, the patient developed acute confusion, agitation, and a witnessed generalized tonic-clonic seizure. Postictal neurological examination revealed no focal deficit. An urgent computed tomography (CT) brain demonstrated intracranial pneumocephalus without hemorrhage or infarction [Figure 3a-c], consistent with retrograde air entry through the spinal canal in the setting of a dural breach and irrigation-related pressure transmission. The air was suspected to have originated from diathermy gas generated during hemostasis. Management included (1) admission to a monitored high-dependency unit, (2) blood pressure optimization and supportive care, (3) a single loading dose of levetiracetam, and (4) supplemental oxygen and head elevation. No further seizures occurred. The patient’s confusion resolved within 24 h and he was discharged on postoperative day 3 neurologically normal, with complete resolution of radicular pain. Long-term antiepileptics were not required.

Computed tomography scan head in postoperative phase following seizure activity. (a-c) Diffuse subarachoid air pockets.

However, he re-presented with persistent postural headache and subsequently underwent re-exploration, which identified and allowed repair of a small contained durotomy adjacent to the traversing root, presumed to be the entry point for intracranial air noted on initial CT.

DISCUSSION

Neurological complications after endoscopic lumbar discectomy are uncommon, but seizures have been sporadically reported in association with pneumocephalus, CSF pressure dysregulation, and dural injury. Several contributing mechanisms were likely present in this case.

Dural breach enabling cranio-spinal pressure coupling

An endoscopic durotomy creates a conduit between the pressurized irrigation compartment and the CSF space. Even modest irrigation pressures may compress the thecal sac and transmit pressure cranially, raising intracranial pressure (ICP) and causing headache, confusion, or seizure.[4] Pressure fluctuations may also introduce air into the subarachnoid space, which aligns with the CT findings in this patient.

Retrograde pneumocephalus

Air may be aspirated into the dural sac during periods of negative pressure or instrument exchanges, especially if the working channel is not fully filled with fluid. Pneumocephalus has been documented to cause cortical irritation or mass effect, which can provoke seizures.[1,3]

Chemical or inflammatory irritation

Blood, disc debris, or irrigation by-products may enter the CSF through a dural defect, irritating the meninges or cortical surfaces. Experimental and clinical observations suggest that the presence of haem products or irritants within CSF can lower the seizure threshold.

CSF volume perturbation

Fluctuations in CSF volume or pressure, either intracranial hypotension from leakage or transient hypertension from irrigation, can produce diffuse cerebral dysfunction. With the increasing global adoption of endoscopic decompression, understanding these physiological dynamics is vital. Irrigation pressures should be maintained at the minimum required for visual clarity, durotomies recognized promptly, and instrument exchanges performed carefully to reduce air ingress.[4,10]

Management

A recommended management pathway based on this case and prior literature includes: Immediate neurological assessment and urgent CT imaging to exclude stroke, hemorrhage, tension pneumocephalus, or hydrocephalusAdmission to a monitored setting (high dependency unit/intensive care unit) for 24–72 h for blood pressure, agitation, and airway managementA single loading dose of an antiepileptic (e.g., levetiracetam) is reasonable. Long-term therapy is often unnecessary if imaging is benign and no further seizures occurObservation until cognition normalizes and imaging findings (e.g., pneumocephalus) resolve or stabilize.[3,7]

Prognosis

Most patients recover fully once pneumocephalus resorbs and ICP stabilizes. Long-term sequelae are rare when early intracranial pathology is excluded. However, persistent CSF leak or ongoing low-pressure headache warrants consideration of dural repair or further intervention.[2]

CONCLUSION

Postoperative confusion and seizures are rare but important complications of uniportal endoscopic lumbar discectomy, particularly in the context of dural breach and irrigation-mediated pressure changes. Prompt recognition of early warning signs, rapid imaging, and structured short-term neurological monitoring are critical to ensuring favorable outcomes. As endoscopic decompression continues to grow, understanding the interaction between irrigation dynamics, dural integrity, and intracranial physiology is essential for safe practice.
