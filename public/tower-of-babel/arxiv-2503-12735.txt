Sharp character bounds and cutoff for symmetric groups
Sam Olesker-Taylor, Lucas Teyssier, Paul Thévenin

Abstract
We develop a flexible technique to bound the characters of symmetric groups, via the Naruse hook length formula, the Larsen–Shalev character bounds, and appropriate diagram slicings. It allows us to prove a uniform exponential character bound with optimal constant 1 / 2 1/2 . We furthermore prove sharp character bounds for conjugacy classes having a macroscopic number of fixed points, and deduce that the random walks on the associated Cayley graphs exhibit a total variation and L 2 L^{2} cutoff.



1
Introduction

Fourier analysis on groups, also known as representation theory, is a rich topic connected to many areas of mathematics. Obtaining precise bounds on characters enables solving a variety of problems from mixing times, geometric group theory, word maps, covering numbers, and random maps. Strong character bounds were proved for finite groups of Lie type
[
Glu93
,
LST11
,
BLST18
,
LT24
]
, finite classical groups
[
GLT20
,
GLT24
]
, and symmetric groups
[
Roi96
,
MSP07
,
LS08
,
FŚ11
]
.
Of particular interest for applications are
exponential
character bounds, that is, bounds on characters written as powers of the dimensions of irreducible representations. In this paper we prove sharp exponential character bounds for symmetric groups that are optimal for applications to mixing times.

1.1
Main results

1.1.1
Bounds on characters

Let
{\mathfrak{S}}_{n}
be the symmetric group of index
n
, and denote by
\widehat{{\mathfrak{S}}_{n}}
its set of irreducible representations. If
\sigma\in{\mathfrak{S}}_{n}
and
\lambda\in\widehat{{\mathfrak{S}}_{n}}
, we denote the dimension of the irreducible representation
\lambda
by
d_{\lambda}
, the associated character by
\ch^{\lambda}(\sigma)
, and the associated reduced character by
\chi^{\lambda}(\sigma):=\ch^{\lambda}(\sigma)/d_{\lambda}
.
If
\sigma\in{\mathfrak{S}}_{n}
, we denote its conjugacy class by
{\mathcal{C}}(\sigma)
, its number of cycles of length
i
by
f_{i}(\sigma)
, its total number of cycles by
\cyc(\sigma)
, and we set
f(\sigma):=\max(f_{1}(\sigma),1)
.

The first exponential bound over a wide range of conjugacy classes was proved by Roichman
[
Roi96
]
. He showed that for every
\delta>0
, there exists a constant
q>0
such that uniformly over all irreducible representations
\lambda\in\widehat{{\mathfrak{S}}_{n}}
, and all
\sigma\in{\mathfrak{S}}_{n}
with at least
\delta n
fixed points,

\left|\chi^{\lambda}(\sigma)\right|\leq d_{\lambda}^{\left(-q+o(1)\right)\frac{\ln(n/f(\sigma))}{\ln n}}.

(1.1)

Later, Müller and Schlage-Puchta
[
MSP07
]
improved this to a uniform bound over all permutations, with an explicit constant
q=30
(and
q=12
if restricted to permutations with at least
n^{1-o(1)}
fixed points).
Then, alongside celebrated character bounds that depend on the whole cycle structure of permutations and are sharp for fixed-point-free permutations,
Larsen and Shalev
[
LS08
]
proved that (
1.1
) holds with constant
q=1/2
uniformly over permutations with at most
n^{1-o(1)}
fixed points, which is sharp for fixed-point-free involutions.
Recently, Lifschitz and Marmor
[
LM23
]
also proved a character bound similar to (
1.1
) for permutations with at most
n/(\ln n)^{O(1)}
cycles.
Finally, in
[
TT24
]
two of us extended the bounds of
[
LS08
]
, and proved in particular that (
1.1
) holds with constant
q=1/2
uniformly for permutations with
o(n)
fixed points. We complete this line of results, proving a uniform character bound for symmetric groups with optimal constant
1/2
.

Theorem 1.1
.

As
n\to\infty
, uniformly over all
\sigma\in{\mathfrak{S}}_{n}
and
\lambda\in\widehat{{\mathfrak{S}}_{n}}
, we have

\left|\chi^{\lambda}(\sigma)\right|\leq d_{\lambda}^{\left(-1/2+o(1)\right)\frac{\ln(n/f(\sigma))}{\ln n}}.

(1.2)

Probabilistic intuition from mixing times suggests that sharper bounds should hold given mild constraints on the cycle structure, and moreover that the number of fixed points is the most crucial parameter in this setup. We prove optimal character bounds for permutations with order
n
fixed points, which is the scale considered by Roichman
[
Roi96
]
.

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
\delta>0
. There exists
n_{0}=n_{0}(\delta)
such that for every
n\geq n_{0}
, uniformly over all
\lambda\in\widehat{{\mathfrak{S}}_{n}}
and
\sigma\in{\mathfrak{S}}_{n}
such that
f(\sigma)\geq\delta n
, we have

\left|\chi^{\lambda}(\sigma)\right|\leq d_{\lambda}^{-\frac{\ln(n/f(\sigma))}{\ln n}}.

(1.3)

Theorem
1.2
not only has the best possible constant
q=1
in (
1.1
), but also does not have any error term. Actually, allowing the bound to depend on the shape of the representation
\lambda
, we can obtain even stronger bounds, as we show in Theorem
5.8
.

1.1.2
Applications to mixing times

Let
{\mathcal{C}}
be a conjugacy class of
{\mathfrak{S}}_{n}
, let
\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2},\ldots
be i.i.d. random variables uniform on
{\mathcal{C}}
, and let
X_{t}=\sigma_{1}\cdots\sigma_{t}
for
t\geq 0
. The law of
X_{t}
is then given by
\Unif_{{\mathcal{C}}}^{*t}
, where
\Unif_{E}
denotes the uniform measure on a set
E
. Another point of view is that
X_{t}
is the position after
t
steps of the simple random walk started at the identity permutation
\Id
on the Cayley graph
\Cay({\mathfrak{S}}_{n},{\mathcal{C}})
, that is, on the graph with vertex set
{\mathfrak{S}}_{n}
and edge set
\left\{\left\{\sigma,\sigma\tau\right\}{\;:\;}(\sigma,\tau)\in{\mathfrak{S}}_{n}\times{\mathcal{C}}\right\}
.

Denote by
{\mathfrak{A}}_{n}
the alternating group of index
n
. Denote also by
\mathfrak{E}({\mathcal{C}},t)

the set
\mathfrak{S}_{n}\backslash\mathfrak{A}_{n}
if
{\mathcal{C}}\subset\mathfrak{S}_{n}\backslash\mathfrak{A}_{n}
and
t
is odd, and
\mathfrak{A}_{n}
otherwise; this is the coset of the alternating group
{\mathfrak{A}}_{n}
on which
X_{t}
lies.
Finally, we define the total variation and
L^{2}
(coset) distances to stationarity by

\dtv({\mathcal{C}},t)=\dtv\left(\Unif_{{\mathcal{C}}}^{*t},\Unif_{{\mathfrak{E}}_{n}({\mathcal{C}},t)}\right)=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\sigma\in{\mathfrak{E}}({\mathcal{C}},t)}\left|\Unif_{{\mathcal{C}}}^{*t}(\sigma)-\frac{2}{n!}\right|,

(1.4)

and

\textup{d}_{L^{2}}({\mathcal{C}},t)=\textup{d}_{L^{2}}\left(\Unif_{{\mathcal{C}}}^{*t},\Unif_{{\mathfrak{E}}_{n}({\mathcal{C}},t)}\right)=\sqrt{\frac{n!}{2}\sum_{\sigma\in{\mathfrak{E}}({\mathcal{C}},t)}\left|\Unif_{{\mathcal{C}}}^{*t}(\sigma)-\frac{2}{n!}\right|^{2}}.

(1.5)

In a landmark paper, Diaconis and Shahshahani
[
DS81
]
used representation theory to show a sharp phase transition, called
cutoff
, for the conjugacy class
{\mathcal{T}}^{(n)}
of transpositions on
{\mathfrak{S}}_{n}
. They showed that for any
\varepsilon>0
,

\dtv\left({\mathcal{T}}^{(n)},\left\lfloor(1-\varepsilon)\frac{1}{2}n\ln n\right\rfloor\right)\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{}1\quad\text{ and }\quad\dtv\left({\mathcal{T}}^{(n)},\left\lceil(1+\varepsilon)\frac{1}{2}n\ln n\right\rceil\right)\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{}0.

(1.6)

This not only initiated the theory of mixing times, together with Aldous
[
Ald83
,
AD86
]
, but also built a fruitful connection between probability and representation theory.

The phase transition result of Diaconis and Shahshashani
[
DS81
]
had several refinements and extensions to other conjugacy classes, that we discuss in Section
1.2
.
We show that cutoff holds in total variation and in
L^{2}
for all conjugacy classes with order
n
fixed points.

Denote the set of conjugacy classes of
{\mathfrak{S}}_{n}
by
\Conj({\mathfrak{S}}_{n})
, and set
\Conj^{*}({\mathfrak{S}}_{n})=\Conj({\mathfrak{S}}_{n})\backslash\left\{\left\{\Id\right\}\right\}
.
If
{\mathcal{C}}\in\Conj({\mathfrak{S}}_{n})
,
\sigma\in{\mathcal{C}}
, and
F
is a class function (that is, a function
F{\;:\;}{\mathfrak{S}}_{n}\to{\mathbb{C}}
such that
F(\sigma_{1})=F(\sigma_{2})
if
\sigma_{1}
and
\sigma_{2}
are in the same conjugacy class), we may write
F({\mathcal{C}})
for
F(\sigma)
.
For
{\mathcal{C}}\in\Conj^{*}({\mathfrak{S}}_{n})
, we set

t_{{\mathcal{C}}}=\frac{\ln n}{\ln(n/f({\mathcal{C}}))}.

(1.7)

Theorem 1.3
.

Let
\delta>0
and
\varepsilon>0
. As
n\to\infty
, uniformly over all conjugacy classes
{\mathcal{C}}\in\Conj^{*}({\mathfrak{S}}_{n})
such that
f({\mathcal{C}})\geq\delta n
, we have

\dtv({\mathcal{C}},\left\lfloor(1-\varepsilon)t_{{\mathcal{C}}}\right\rfloor)\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{}1\quad\text{ and }\quad\dtv({\mathcal{C}},\left\lceil(1+\varepsilon)t_{{\mathcal{C}}}\right\rceil)\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{}0,

(1.8)

and

\textup{d}_{L^{2}}({\mathcal{C}},\left\lfloor(1-\varepsilon)t_{{\mathcal{C}}}\right\rfloor)\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{}\infty\quad\text{ and }\quad\textup{d}_{L^{2}}({\mathcal{C}},\left\lceil(1+\varepsilon)t_{{\mathcal{C}}}\right\rceil)\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{}0.

(1.9)

This is the first
L^{2}
cutoff over a wide range of conjugacy classes, for any group. We also prove a refined
L^{2}
bound in Proposition
6.1
.

We conjecture that the results of Theorems
1.2
and
1.3
extend to all conjugacy classes with
\omega(\sqrt{n})
fixed points.
The results for conjugacy classes
{\mathcal{C}}
of
{\mathfrak{S}}_{n}
with
O(\sqrt{n})
fixed points must however be different, due to cancellations of transpositions that can prevent
\Unif_{{\mathcal{C}}}^{*2}
to be close to
\Unif_{{\mathfrak{A}}_{n}}
in total variation.

1.2
Additional background and related results

Since the foundational work of Diaconis and Shahshahani
[
DS81
]
, mixing times for random walk on groups have been widely studied, and the connections between mixing times and representation theory have kept growing stronger.
Diaconis
[
Dia88
,
Dia96
]
led further discussions about this topic, and wrote the following about character bounds leading to cutoff for conjugacy invariant walks on
{\mathfrak{S}}_{n}
in
[
Dia03
]
: “This is closely
related to earlier work of Roichman
[
Roi96
]
who derived useful bounds on character
ratios for large conjugacy classes in
{\mathfrak{S}}_{n}
. Alas his work has not been pushed through
to give sharp bounds in probability problems. This is a hard but potentially fruitful area of study.”

The phase transition result of Diaconis and Shahshahani
[
DS81
]
had refinements and alternative proofs
[
SCZn08
,
Whi19
]
, and several extensions to other conjugacy classes of
{\mathfrak{S}}_{n}
: conjugacy classes with support
\leq 6
and
7
-cycles in
[
Rou00
]
,
k
-cycles with
k=O(1)
in
[
BSZ11
]
,
k
-cycles with
k=o(n)
in
[
Hou16
]
, and conjugacy classes with support
o(n)
in
[
BŞ19
]
.
Also, soft techniques relying on entropy
[
Sal24
]
recently enabled recovering that conjugacy classes with support
n^{o(1)}
exhibit cutoff
[
PS25
]
. Theorem
1.3
proves that cutoff holds for all conjugacy classes with order
n
fixed points, both in total variation and in
L^{2}
.

For conjugacy classes with larger support, Lulov and Pak
[
LP02
]
studied long
k
-cycles, with
k>n/2
, and Lulov
[
Lul96
]
understood the mixing time of conjugacy classes with a unique cycle length (
n/m
cycles of length
m
), using the character bounds of Fomin and Lulov

[
FL95
]
. Then Larsen and Shalev
[
LS08
]
proved that fixed-point-free conjugacy classes mix in 2 or 3 steps, and building on the work of Larsen and Shalev, two of us characterized in
[
TT24
]
which fixed-point-free conjugacy classes mix in 2 steps.

What happens within the phase transition is also now better understood. In
[
Tey20
]
, the second named author found a character identity involving Poisson–Charlier polynomials, that enabled him to compute the
cutoff profile
. For fixed
a\in{\mathbb{R}}
,
the total variation distance to stationarity after
\frac{1}{2}(n\ln n+an)
steps converges to
\dtv\left(\Poiss(1),\Poiss\left(1+e^{-a}\right)\right)
, where
\Poiss(\alpha)
denotes the Poisson distribution with parameter
\alpha>0
.
This was extended to cycles of length
k=o(n)
in
[
NOT22
]
and another proof for transpositions was also recently found

[
JS24
]
. This will be further extended to conjugacy classes with order
n
fixed points in a follow up paper
[
Tey25b
]
, using the character bounds proved in the present paper.

Exponential character bounds as in (
1.1
) are equivalent to mixing time results in
L^{2}
(which imply upper bounds in total variation), and obtaining mixing time bounds was already one of the main aims for several of the aforementioned general representation theoretic results, including
[
Roi96
,
MSP07
,
LS08
]
. Roichman
[
Roi96
]
found the correct order of magnitude of the mixing time for conjugacy classes of
{\mathfrak{S}}_{n}
with order
n
fixed points. Müller and Schlage-Puchta’s uniform character bound
[
MSP07
]
showed that
\textup{d}_{L^{2}}({\mathcal{C}},31\frac{\ln n}{\ln(n/f({\mathcal{C}}))})\to 0
as
n\to\infty
, uniformly over all conjugacy classes
{\mathcal{C}}\neq\left\{\Id\right\}
. Finally, Larsen and Shalev
[
LS08
]
gave precise bounds for permutations with few fixed points, and in particular showed that
\textup{d}_{L^{2}}({\mathcal{C}},3)\to 0
as
n\to\infty
, uniformly all fixed-point-free conjugacy classes
{\mathcal{C}}
.

Several related conjectures were made. Roichman’s conjecture
[
Roi96
, Conjecture 6.7]
is strongly related to Theorem
1.2
. Character bounds are now usually written in exponential form, since this interacts well with bounds on the Witten zeta function, as explained in Section
6.1
, but the conjectured bound is similar for applications to mixing times. However the conjecture was too optimistically stated to hold uniformly over all conjugacy classes, and
[
MSP07
]
observed that it fails to hold for some fixed-point-free conjugacy classes.

Roussel
[
Rou00
, Conjecture 1.1]
conjectured that for any
\delta>0
, there exists a constant
C>0
such that for any
a\in{\mathbb{R}}
, as
n\to\infty
, uniformly over all conjugacy classes
{\mathcal{C}}
whose support
\supp({\mathcal{C}})
satisfies
2\leq\supp({\mathcal{C}})\leq(1-\delta)n
, we have
\dtv({\mathcal{C}},t_{{\mathcal{C}}}^{*}\left(1+a/\ln n\right))\leq Ce^{-a}
, where
t_{{\mathcal{C}}}^{*}:=\frac{n}{\supp({\mathcal{C}})}\ln n
. We always have
t_{{\mathcal{C}}}^{*}\geq t_{{\mathcal{C}}}
, therefore Proposition
6.1
proves a stronger version of Roussel’s conjecture.

Saloff-Coste conjectured, below Conjecture 9.3 in
[
SC04
]
, that the
L^{2}

cutoff time
for the non-lazy chain is
t^{*}_{{\mathcal{C}}}
, for conjugacy classes
{\mathcal{C}}
such that
\supp({\mathcal{C}})=o(n)
. The part about the lazy chain was proved to not hold in
[
SCZn08
]
, due to interactions between the
L^{2}
distance to stationarity and laziness. Proposition
6.1
proves a stronger version of Saloff-Coste’s conjectured upper bound for the non-lazy chain: this is proved replacing the time
t^{*}_{{\mathcal{C}}}=\frac{n}{\supp({\mathcal{C}})}\ln n
by the time
t_{\mathcal{C}}=\frac{\ln n}{\ln(n/f({\mathcal{C}}))}
and with the milder support size condition
n-\supp({\mathcal{C}})\gtrsim n
instead of
\supp({\mathcal{C}})=o(n)
.

Proofs via representation theory present some benefits. One is that bounds on characters enable solving other problems; see for instance the applications of
[
LS08
]
on covering numbers, normal subsets, and word maps, and the results of
[
Gam06
]
and
[
CP16
]
on random maps.
Another advantage is that character bounds give
L^{2}
bounds for mixing times, which can be used in comparison techniques
[
DSC93a
,
DSC93b
]
to bound the mixing times of other Markov chains.

The correct order of the mixing time is now understood in a wide framework. Larsen and Tiep
[
LT24
, Propositions 8.1 and 8.2]

recently showed that simple random walks on
\Cay(G,{\mathcal{C}})
are close to being uniformly distributed after order
\frac{\ln|G|}{\ln|{\mathcal{C}}|}
steps (and are not before), with implicit universal constants that apply to all (non-trivial) conjugacy classes
{\mathcal{C}}
of all quasi-simple groups
G
of Lie type.
It would be very interesting to obtain cutoff results also in this setting.

Finally, character bounds with a different flavour, not relying on the Murnaghan–Nakayama rule, were proved by Rattan and Śniady
[
RŚ08
]
using a character formula from Frobenius, and by Féray and Śniady
[
FŚ11
]
via Stanley’s character formula. These bounds are precise if representations have short rows and columns in their coding as Young diagrams, and permutations have small
O(\sqrt{n})
. This led to interesting applications to asymptotic combinatorics
[
DF19
]
.

1.3
General strategy

Our approach is mostly combinatorial, but strongly influenced by probabilistic intuition. For conjugacy invariant random walks on symmetric groups, the limiting observable is usually the number of fixed points, which should therefore also be the most important parameter for characters bounds. The rest of the cycle structure should play a minor role, as long as the associated random walk needs at least 3 steps to mix, which is always the case if the number of fixed points grows faster than
\sqrt{n}
.

With this in mind, we tried to split the contribution of the fixed points from the rest of the cycle structure in the Murnaghan–Nakayama rule. This led us to the iterated branching rule

\ch^{\lambda}(\sigma)=\sum_{\mu\subset_{\vdash k}\lambda}\ch^{\mu}(\sigma^{*})d_{\lambda\backslash\mu},

(1.10)

where
k
is the support size of
\sigma
, and
\sigma^{*}\in{\mathfrak{S}}_{k}
has the same cycle structure as
\sigma
, except that it has no fixed point. We give more details on this branching rule in Section
2.7
.
The fixed-point-free part (the term
\ch^{\mu}(\sigma^{*})
) is handled with the Larsen–Shalev character bounds
[
LS08
]
(and their sharpening from
[
TT24
]
for the limiting cases), while the fixed point part (the term
d_{\lambda\backslash\mu}
, which is the number of standard tableaux of the skew diagram
\lambda\backslash\mu
) is bounded using the Naruse hook length formula
[
Nar14
]
.
This converts the problem of bounding characters into bounding the number of standard tableaux of some shapes of diagrams.

Although initially designed with applications to mixing times in mind, our technique and some of our intermediate estimates are also applicable at other scales and for other problems. In a follow up paper
[
Tey25a
]
, the second named author improves the character bounds of
[
RŚ08
,
FŚ11
]
for permutations having a large support. Our technique also enables simple estimation of the characters of small conjugacy classes for balanced diagrams.

1.4
Notation

Recall that we denote the symmetric group of order
n
by
\mathfrak{S}_{n}
and the set of its irreducible representations by
\widehat{\mathfrak{S}_{n}}
. For convenience, the pieces of notation
\lambda\in\widehat{{\mathfrak{S}}_{n}}
and
\lambda\vdash n
will be used interchangeably to describe that
\lambda
is an irreducible representation of
{\mathfrak{S}}_{n}
, the associated integer partition of the integer
n
, or the associated Young diagram of size
n
.

Throughout the paper, we refer to a box, i.e. a unit square in the plane whose vertices have integer coordinates, by the horizontal and vertical coordinates of its top-right vertex. By extension, we see sets of boxes as subsets of
{\mathbb{Z}}^{2}
. In the case of a Young diagram
\lambda
, by default, we anchor its bottom-left corner at
(0,0)
; hence
(i,j)
denotes the
i
-th leftmost and
j
-th bottommost box in
\lambda
, and in particular
(1,1)
denotes the bottom-left box of
\lambda
.
We also write
\mu\subset_{\vdash k}\lambda
when
\mu
is a sub Young diagram of
\lambda
of size
k
, that is, a set of boxes in
\lambda
containing
(1,1)
and which is itself a Young diagram.

We make use of the following notation.
Let
E\subset{\mathbb{Z}}^{2}
, and
k\in{\mathbb{Z}}
.

•

E_{k}
is the
k
-th row of
E
, that is,
\{(i,j)\in E\mid i=k\}
,

•

E^{\prime}_{k}
is the
k
-th column of
E
, that is,
\{(i,j)\in E\mid j=k\}
,

•

E^{k}
is the
k
-th hook of
\lambda
, that is,
\{(i,j)\in\lambda\mid\min(i,j)=k\}
,

•

u_{k}(E)
is the
k
-th column truncated at 2, that is,
\{(i,j)\in E\mid i\geq 2,j=k\}
.

We also extend the notation for Frobenius coordinates to sets of boxes as follows.

•

a_{k}(E)
denotes the set
\{(i,j)\in E\mid i=k,j>k\}
,

•

b_{k}(E)
denotes the set
\{(i,j)\in E\mid i>k,j=k\}
,

•

\delta_{k}(E)=\left\{(k,k)\right\}\cap E
and

,

denotes

\Diamond\in\left\{\geq,\leq,>,<\right\}
, we denote

,

If

whose corner is

by
h_{u}
, that is,
\{(i,j)\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{2}\mid[i=i_{0}\text{ and }j\geq j_{0}]\text{ or }[i\geq i_{0}\text{ and }j=j_{0}]\}
,
and define the

of

in

by

H(E,u)=\left|E\cap h_{u}\right|.

(1.11)

If
E
and

are subsets of

, we define

(1.12)

If
i,j\in{\mathbb{Z}}
, we define

\Shift_{i,j}(E):=E+(i,j)=\left\{(x+i,y+j)\mid(x,y)\in E\right\}.

(1.13)

We call a set of boxes

a

(resp. a

) diagram of length

both for the set of boxes on the first row of a diagram

2.2.2
The Naruse hook length formula

Recall that if
\lambda
is an irreducible representation of
{\mathfrak{S}}_{n}
for some
n
, then
d_{\lambda}=|\ST(\lambda)|
, and that the hook length formula can be written as

|\ST(\lambda)|=\frac{|\lambda|!}{H(\lambda,\lambda)}.

(2.1)

If
\lambda
and
\mu
are partitions and
\mu\subset\lambda
we denote, by analogy with the hook length formula,

d_{\lambda\backslash\mu}:=|\ST(\lambda\backslash\mu)|,

(2.2)

and call it
dimension
of
\lambda\backslash\mu
by extension.
If
u
is a box in
\lambda
, we denote by
H(\lambda,u)
its hook length. If
E\subset\lambda
is a subset of the boxes of
\lambda
recall that we denote by
H(\lambda,E)=\prod_{u\in E}H(\lambda,u)
the product of the hook lengths (calculated in
\lambda
) of the boxes in
E
.

In
[
MPP18
, Theorem 1.2]
, the Naruse hook length formula is written as follows: for any partitions
\lambda
and
\mu\subset\lambda
, we have

d_{\lambda\backslash\mu}=|\lambda\backslash\mu|!\sum_{E\in{\mathcal{E}}(\lambda,\mu)}\frac{1}{H(\lambda,\lambda\backslash E)}.

(2.3)

This is a generalization of the hook length formula, since (
2.1
) is exactly (
2.3
) applied with
\mu=\emptyset
.

For our purposes, it is convenient to rewrite this formula differently.
For any partition
\lambda
and any subset
\mu\subset\lambda
, define

S(\lambda,\mu)=\sum_{E\in\dgex(\lambda,\mu)}H(\lambda,E).

(2.4)

We use the falling factorial notation: for any integers

,

m^{\downarrow p}:=\frac{m!}{p!}=m(m-1)\cdots(m-p+1).

(2.5)

Combining (
2.3
) and the hook length formula, we can rewrite, if

is a Young diagram,

\frac{d_{\lambda\backslash\mu}}{d_{\lambda}}=\frac{S(\lambda,\mu)}{|\lambda|^{\downarrow|\mu|}}.

(2.6)

We also refer to this identity as the Naruse hook length formula.

Larsen and Shalev
[

The quantity

Definition 2.12
.

Let

. Denote by

the number of cycles of length

of

n^{e_{1}+\ldots+e_{i}}=\max\left(\Sigma_{i},1\right)

(2.15)

In
[

(

Some classical bounds

We collect some elementary/classical lemmas that we will use throughout the article.

Lemma 2.14
.

Let
\lambda\vdash n
. Then,
d_{\lambda}\leq\sqrt{n!}

Proof.

We have the identity
\sum_{\nu\vdash n}d_{\nu}^{2}=n!
by the Fourier isomorphism (see
[
Mé17
, Section 1.3]
). Therefore
d_{\lambda}\leq\sqrt{\sum_{\nu\vdash n}d_{\nu}^{2}}=\sqrt{n!}
.
∎

The following lemma is a classical counting argument, see for instance
[
Dia88
, Fact 3.D.1 on Pages 39-40]
. We give a version with multiple diagrams and a computational proof.

Lemma 2.15
.

Let
\lambda
be a Young diagram and let
p\geq 2
be an integer. Write
\lambda
as a disjoint union
\lambda(1)\cup\cdots\cup\lambda(p)
, such that for each
1\leq j\leq p
,
\cup_{1\leq i\leq j}\lambda(i)
is a Young diagram. Then

\frac{d_{\lambda}}{|\lambda|!}\leq\prod_{i=1}^{p}\frac{d_{\lambda(i)}}{|\lambda(i)|!}.

(2.18)

Proof.

First assume that
p=2
. Then
\lambda\backslash\lambda(1)=\lambda(2)
. By the hook length formula, the Naruse hook length formula (
2.6
), and since
S(\lambda,\lambda(1))\geq H(\lambda,\lambda(1))\geq H({\lambda(1)},{\lambda(1)})
, we have

\frac{d_{\lambda(1)}d_{\lambda(2)}}{d_{\lambda}}\frac{|\lambda|!}{|\lambda(1)|!|\lambda(2)|!}=\frac{d_{\lambda(1)}}{|\lambda(1)|!}\left(\frac{d_{\lambda\backslash\lambda(1)}}{d_{\lambda}}|\lambda|^{\downarrow|\lambda(1)|}\right)=\frac{1}{H(\lambda(1),\lambda(1))}S(\lambda,\lambda(1))\geq 1,

(2.19)

which is the desired result for
p=2
. It follows by induction that the result holds for any
p\geq 2
.
∎

We also recall classical bounds on factorials and binomial coefficients.

Lemma 2.16
.

(a)

Let
k\geq 0
be an integer. Then
k!\geq(k/e)^{k}
.

(b)

Let
n
and
k
be integers with
0\leq k\leq n
.
Then,

\frac{n^{k}}{n^{\downarrow k}}\leq e^{k}\quad\text{and}\quad\binom{n}{k}\leq\biggl(\frac{en}{k}\biggr)^{k}.

(2.20)

Proof.

(a)

This holds for
k=0
, and for
k\geq 1
follows from rearranging the sum–integral inequality

\ln(k!)=\sum_{r=1}^{k}\ln r\geq\int_{1}^{k}\ln x\,dx=k\ln k-k+1\geq k\ln k-k.

(2.21)

(b)

By (a),

\binom{n}{k}=n^{\downarrow k}/k!\leq n^{k}/(k/e)^{k}=(en/k)^{k}.

Recall that

\binom{n}{k}=n^{\downarrow k}/k^{\downarrow k}\geq(n/k)^{k}.

So,

\frac{n^{k}}{n^{\downarrow k}}\bigg/\frac{k^{k}}{k^{\downarrow k}}=\biggl(\frac{n}{k}\biggr)^{k}\bigg/\binom{n}{k}\leq 1,\quad\text{and hence}\quad\frac{n^{k}}{n^{\downarrow k}}\leq\frac{k^{k}}{k!}\leq e^{k}.\qed

2.6
Bounds on dimensions of representations

The next two lemmas give useful bounds on
d_{\lambda}
.

Lemma 2.19
.

Let
\lambda
be a Young diagram. Denote by
\delta=\delta(\lambda)
the length of its diagonal. Then

d_{\lambda}\leq(2\delta)^{n}.

(2.24)

Proof.

The dimension
d_{\lambda}
is the number of standard tableaux of
\lambda
, that is, the number of ways we can remove corners one by one from
\lambda
until we get an empty diagram. At each step there are at most
2\delta
corners, so overall we have
d_{\lambda}\leq(2\delta)^{n}
.
∎

Lemma 2.20
.

Let
n,M\geq 1
be integers, and let
\lambda\vdash n
.
Denote
m=\left|\lambda^{>M}\right|
. Then

d_{\lambda}\leq(4M)^{n}m^{m/2}.

(2.25)

Proof.

We have
\binom{n}{m}\leq 2^{n}
,
d_{\lambda^{\leq M}}\leq(2M)^{n-m}
by Lemma
2.19
, and
d_{\lambda^{>M}}\leq\sqrt{m!}\leq{m}^{m/2}
, so

d_{\lambda}\leq\binom{n}{m}d_{\lambda^{\leq M}}d_{\lambda^{>M}}\leq 2^{n}(2M)^{n-m}{m}^{m/2}=(4M)^{n}\left(\frac{m}{4M^{2}}\right)^{m/2}\leq(4M)^{n}m^{m/2}.\qed

2.7
An iterated branching rule

There are many identities for characters of symmetric groups. In the language of the Murnaghan–Nakayama rule (see
[
Mé17
, Section 3.1]
), Larsen and Shalev
[
LS08
]
peeled the ribbons in decreasing order of length, which corresponds to writing the cycle structure in decreasing order of the lengths of cycles. What we do is that we peel first the ribbons of size 1 and then the other ribbons. This peeling order is equivalent to the iterated branching rule (
1.10
).

Let us define the terms in this branching rule in more details.
If
\sigma
is a permutation of
{\mathfrak{S}}_{n}
with support
k
(that is,
n-k
fixed points), we define the permutation
\sigma^{*}\in{\mathfrak{S}}_{k}
as follows: let
\ell_{1}\geq\ell_{2}\geq\ldots\geq\ell_{N}
be the ordered lengths of the cycles of length
\geq 2
of
\sigma
. Then we define

\sigma^{*}=(1\ldots\ell_{1})(\ell_{1}+1\ldots\ell_{1}+\ell_{2})\cdots(k-\ell_{N}+1\,\ldots\,k).

(2.27)

In particular,
\sigma^{*}
has the same number of
i
-cycles as
\sigma
for each
i\geq 2
, and
\sigma^{*}
has no fixed point. For example if
\sigma=(1\;3\;5\;8)(7\;2)\in{\mathfrak{S}}_{9}
, then
\sigma^{*}=(1\;2\;3\;4)(5\;6)\in{\mathfrak{S}}_{6}
.

If
\lambda
and
\mu
are two Young diagrams such that
\mu\subset\lambda
, we recall that we denote by
d_{\lambda\backslash\mu}
the number of standard tableaux of the skew diagram
\lambda\backslash\mu
. We finally recall that we write
\mu\subset_{\vdash k}\lambda
to emphasize that
\mu
is a sub Young diagram of
\lambda
with
k
boxes. We use the the following renormalized form of the iterated branching rule: for
\lambda\vdash n
and
\sigma\in{\mathfrak{S}}_{n}
, we have

\chi^{\lambda}(\sigma)=\sum_{\mu\subset_{\vdash k}\lambda}\ch^{\mu}(\sigma^{*})\frac{d_{\lambda\backslash\mu}}{d_{\lambda}}.

(2.28)

2.8
Combining branching rules with ideas of Larsen–Shalev and Naruse

We start with the following classical counting lemma.

Lemma 2.21
.

Let
\lambda,\mu
be Young diagrams and
n\geq 1
such that
\lambda\vdash n
and
\mu\subset\lambda
. Then

d_{\mu}d_{\lambda\backslash\mu}\leq d_{\lambda}.

(2.29)

In particular for any
\alpha\geq 0
we have

\left(d_{\mu}\right)^{\alpha}\frac{d_{\lambda\backslash\mu}}{d_{\lambda}}\leq\left(\frac{d_{\lambda\backslash\mu}}{d_{\lambda}}\right)^{1-\alpha}.

(2.30)

Proof.

By definition,
d_{\mu}d_{\lambda\backslash\mu}
counts the number of standard tableaux of
\lambda
with the numbers from 1 to
k
in
\mu
and the numbers from
k+1
to
n
in
\lambda\backslash\mu
, and is therefore at most the total number
d_{\lambda}
of standard tableaux of
\lambda
. Thus,
d_{\mu}d_{\lambda\backslash\mu}\leq d_{\lambda}
, or equivalently
\frac{d_{\mu}d_{\lambda\backslash\mu}}{d_{\lambda}}\leq 1
. For
\alpha\geq 0
, we therefore have

\left(d_{\mu}\right)^{\alpha}\frac{d_{\lambda\backslash\mu}}{d_{\lambda}}=\left(d_{\mu}\frac{d_{\lambda\backslash\mu}}{d_{\lambda}}\right)^{\alpha}\left(\frac{d_{\lambda\backslash\mu}}{d_{\lambda}}\right)^{1-\alpha}\leq\left(\frac{d_{\lambda\backslash\mu}}{d_{\lambda}}\right)^{1-\alpha}.\qed

(2.31)

Recall from Lemma
2.13
that
\beta(\sigma)=\left(1+\frac{C_{\textup{virt}}}{\ln n}\right)E(\sigma)
for
\sigma\in{\mathfrak{S}}_{n}
, and set
\theta_{k}=\frac{1}{2}\left(1+\frac{C_{\textup{virt}}}{\ln k}\right)
for
k\geq 2
.

Lemma 2.22
.

Let
n\geq 2
,
2\leq k\leq n
,
\sigma\in{\mathfrak{S}}_{n}
with support size
k
, and
\lambda\vdash n
. Then

\left|\chi^{\lambda}(\sigma)\right|\leq\sum_{\mu\subset_{\vdash k}\lambda}d_{\mu}^{\beta(\sigma^{*})}\frac{d_{\lambda\backslash\mu}}{d_{\lambda}}\leq\sum_{\mu\subset_{\vdash k}\lambda}d_{\mu}^{\theta_{k}}\frac{d_{\lambda\backslash\mu}}{d_{\lambda}}.

(2.32)

Proof.

Applying the triangle inequality in (
2.28
), we get

\left|\chi^{\lambda}(\sigma)\right|=\left|\sum_{\mu\subset_{\vdash k}\lambda}\ch^{\mu}(\sigma^{*})\frac{d_{\lambda\backslash\mu}}{d_{\lambda}}\right|\leq\sum_{\mu\subset_{\vdash k}\lambda}\left|\ch^{\mu}(\sigma^{*})\right|\frac{d_{\lambda\backslash\mu}}{d_{\lambda}}.

(2.33)

The first inequality follows from Lemma
2.13
, applied to each
\mu\subset_{\vdash k}\lambda
.
Note that
\sigma^{*}\in{\mathfrak{S}}_{k}
, so
\beta(\sigma^{*})=\left(1+\frac{C_{\textup{virt}}}{\ln k}\right)E(\sigma^{*})
. Moreover
\sigma^{*}
is a fixed-point-free permutation, so we have
E(\sigma^{*})\leq 1/2
, which proves the second inequality since
\theta_{k}=\frac{1}{2}\left(1+\frac{C_{\textup{virt}}}{\ln k}\right)
.
∎

Lemma 2.23
.

Let
1/2<\theta\leq 1
. Set
k_{0}(\theta)=\min\left\{k^{\prime}\geq 2{\;:\;}\frac{1}{2}\left(1+\frac{C_{\textup{virt}}}{\ln k^{\prime}}\right)\leq\theta\right\}

n
and

be integers such that
k_{0}(\theta)\leq k\leq n

\sigma\in{\mathfrak{S}}_{n}

(2.34)

3
Bounds for high-level representations

3.1
Bounds on the number of excited diagrams and excited sums

To bound characters, we need to understand better the ratios
\frac{d_{\lambda\backslash\mu}}{d_{\lambda}}=\frac{S(\lambda,\mu)}{|\lambda|^{\downarrow|\mu|}}
. Asymptotics for
d_{\lambda\backslash\mu}
were proved in different regimes for diagrams
\lambda
and
\mu
with short hook lengths; see
[
DF19
,
MPT22
]
.
Here we need bounds that combine well with deep triple slicings (see Figure
2
below). In order to do so we have to prove bounds on the excited sums

S(\lambda,\mu)=\sum_{E\in\dgex(\lambda,\mu)}H(\lambda,E).

(3.1)

We start with the following standard bound.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
\lambda,\mu
be Young diagrams such that
\mu\subset\lambda
. Denote
n=|\lambda|
. Then

S(\lambda,\mu)\leq 2^{n}H(\lambda,\mu).

(3.2)

Proof.

Since each excited diagram in
{\mathcal{E}}(\lambda,\mu)
is a subset of the
n
boxes of
\lambda
, we have
|{\mathcal{E}}(\lambda,\mu)|\leq 2^{n}
. Moreover,
\max_{E\in{\mathcal{E}}(\lambda,\mu)}H(\lambda,E)=H(\lambda,\mu)
. Therefore
S(\lambda,\mu)\leq|{\mathcal{E}}(\lambda,\mu)|\max_{E\in{\mathcal{E}}(\lambda,\mu)}H(\lambda,E)\leq 2^{n}H(\lambda,\mu)
.
∎

However this bound is too weak in general, and we need more precise bound on
|{\mathcal{E}}(\lambda,\mu)|
and
S(\lambda,\mu)
which depend on the shapes of
\lambda
and
\mu
. Let us first bound
|{\mathcal{E}}(\lambda,\mu)|
when
\mu
consists of a single row.

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
\lambda
be a Young diagram,
\delta
be the length of the diagonal of
\lambda
, and let
\ell\leq\lambda_{1}
. Then

|{\mathcal{E}}(\lambda,[\ell])|\leq\min\left(\binom{\ell+\left\lfloor n/\ell\right\rfloor}{\ell},\binom{\ell+\delta}{\ell}\right).

(3.3)

Proof.

Set
n=|\lambda|
. A diagram
E\in{\mathcal{E}}(\lambda,[\ell])
is totally determined by the horizontal coordinates of its boxes, which are distinct and are between
1
and
\ell+\left\lfloor n/\ell\right\rfloor
.
To see this, observe first that the maximum horizontal coordinate of a box in an excited diagram
E\in{\mathcal{E}}(\lambda,[\ell])
is reached by exciting the box
(1,\ell)
. Furthermore, if the box
(1,\ell)
can be excited
j
times, then necessarily the box
(1+j,\ell+j)
is in
\lambda
, and in particular
j\ell\leq n
. Hence, the maximum horizontal coordinate of a box in
E
is at most
\ell+\left\lfloor n/\ell\right\rfloor
.
Therefore, the
\ell
boxes must all have distinct horizontal coordinates, each in
\{1,\ldots,\left\lfloor n/\ell\right\rfloor\}
, and hence

|{\mathcal{E}}(\lambda,[\ell])|\leq\binom{\ell+\left\lfloor n/\ell\right\rfloor}{\ell}.

(3.4)

Similarly, each box cannot be moved more than
\delta-1\leq\delta
times.
So,
|{\mathcal{E}}(\lambda,[\ell])|
is at most the number of weakly increasing integer-valued sequences with values in
[0,\delta]
:

|{\mathcal{E}}(\lambda,[\ell])|\leq\binom{\ell+\delta}{\ell}

(3.5)

by the usual stars and bars argument, which concludes the proof.
∎

Lemma 3.3
.

Let
\lambda
be a Young diagram, and let
1\leq\ell\leq\lambda_{1}
. Then

|{\mathcal{E}}(\lambda,[\ell])|\leq e^{5\sqrt{|\lambda|}}.

(3.6)

Proof.

Denote
n=|\lambda|
, and by
\delta=\delta(\lambda)
the diagonal length of
\lambda
. Since the square
[\delta^{\delta}]
is a sub-diagram of
\lambda
, we have
\delta\leq\sqrt{n}
.
By Lemma
3.2
, if
\ell\leq\sqrt{n}
we have

|{\mathcal{E}}(\lambda,[\ell])|\leq\binom{\ell+\delta}{\ell}\leq 2^{\ell+\delta}\leq 2^{\sqrt{n}+\sqrt{n}}=2^{2\sqrt{n}}\leq e^{5\sqrt{|\lambda|}}.

(3.7)

On the other hand, again by Lemma
3.2
, if
\ell>\sqrt{n}
, using
\binom{b}{a}\leq(eb/a)^{a}
for all
a,b>0
, we get

|{\mathcal{E}}(\lambda,[\ell])|\leq\binom{\ell+\left\lfloor n/\ell\right\rfloor}{\ell}=\binom{\ell+\left\lfloor n/\ell\right\rfloor}{\left\lfloor n/\ell\right\rfloor}\leq\binom{2\ell}{\left\lfloor n/\ell\right\rfloor}\leq\binom{2\ell}{\left\lceil n/\ell\right\rceil}\leq\left(\frac{2e\ell}{\left\lceil n/\ell\right\rceil}\right)^{\left\lceil n/\ell\right\rceil},

(3.8)

so

|{\mathcal{E}}(\lambda,[\ell])|\leq\left(\frac{2e\ell}{n/\ell}\right)^{\left\lceil n/\ell\right\rceil}\leq\left(\frac{2e\ell^{2}}{n}\right)^{2n/\ell}.

(3.9)

Set
y=\sqrt{n}/\ell
. By assumption we have
0\leq y\leq 1
so
y^{y}\geq e^{-1/e}
. We conclude that

|{\mathcal{E}}(\lambda,[\ell])|^{1/\sqrt{n}}\leq\left(\frac{2e}{y^{2}}\right)^{2y}\leq(2e)^{2}y^{-4y}\leq(2e)^{2}e^{4/e}\leq e^{5}.\qed

(3.10)

Lemma 3.4
.

Let
\lambda
be a Young diagram. Let
\ell\geq 1
and
i,j\geq 0
be integers. Let
L
be either
[\ell]
or
[1^{\ell}]
and
\tilde{L}=\Shift_{i,j}(L)
. Assume that
\tilde{L}\subset\lambda
. Then

|{\mathcal{E}}(\lambda,\tilde{L})|\leq|{\mathcal{E}}(\lambda,L)|\quad\text{ and }\quad S(\lambda,\tilde{L})\leq S(\lambda,L).

(3.11)

Proof.

Since we can only excite boxes towards north east, the set
{\mathcal{E}}(\lambda,\tilde{L})
is in bijection with
{\mathcal{E}}(\nu,L)
, where
\nu=\Shift_{-i,-j}(\lambda\cap[i,\infty)\times[j,\infty))
, and the corresponding hook lengths are the same. The two inequalities then follow, since
\nu\subset\lambda
by definition.
∎

Lemma 3.5
.

Let
\lambda
be a Young diagram and
E_{0}
be a subset of the boxes of
\lambda
. Write
E_{0}
as a disjoint union of
p_{R}
row diagrams
R_{1},\ldots,R_{p_{R}}
and
p_{C}
column diagrams
C_{1},\ldots,C_{p_{C}}
, whose lengths we denote by
r_{1},\ldots,r_{p_{R}},c_{1},\ldots,c_{p_{C}}
. Then

|{\mathcal{E}}(\lambda,E_{0})|\leq\left(\prod_{i=1}^{p_{R}}\left|{\mathcal{E}}(\lambda,[r_{i}])\right|\right)\left(\prod_{i=1}^{p_{C}}\left|{\mathcal{E}}(\lambda,[1^{c_{i}}])\right|\right),

(3.12)

and

S(\lambda,E_{0})\leq\left(\prod_{i=1}^{p_{R}}S(\lambda,[r_{i}])\right)\left(\prod_{i=1}^{p_{C}}S(\lambda,[1^{c_{i}}])\right).

(3.13)

Proof.

This follows immediately from Lemma
2.9
and Lemma
3.4
.
∎

Lemma 3.6
.

Let
\lambda
be a Young diagram and
p\geq 1
be an integer. Let
E_{0}\subset\lambda
be a subset of the boxes of
\lambda
that can be written as a disjoint union of
p
row and column diagrams. Then

|{\mathcal{E}}(\lambda,E_{0})|\leq e^{5p\sqrt{|\lambda|}},

(3.14)

and in particular

S(\lambda,E_{0})\leq e^{5p\sqrt{|\lambda}|}H(\lambda,E_{0}).

(3.15)

Proof.

Write
E_{0}
as a disjoint union of row and column diagrams. For each of these row or column diagrams
L
, by Lemmas
3.3
and
3.4
we have
|{\mathcal{E}}(\lambda,L)|\leq e^{5\sqrt{|\lambda|}}
. We deduce from Lemma
3.5
that
|{\mathcal{E}}(\lambda,E_{0})|\leq e^{5p\sqrt{|\lambda|}}
.
The second point follows from bounding the sum
S(\lambda,E_{0})\penalty\ =\penalty\ \sum_{E\in{\mathcal{E}}(\lambda,E_{0})}H(\lambda,E)
by its maximal term
H(\lambda,E_{0})
, times its number of terms
|{\mathcal{E}}(\lambda,E_{0})|
.
∎

3.2
Deep triple slicings of diagrams

Diaconis and Shahshahani
[
DS81
]
decomposed diagrams according to how many boxes they have on the first row. While this works for transpositions, for other conjugacy classes it works in general only for low-level representations, that is, when the first row is very long. For exponential character bounds, or equivalently to bound mixing times in
L^{2}
distance for the simple random walk on
\Cay({\mathfrak{S}}_{n},{\mathcal{C}})
, the goal is to bound for each
\lambda
the quantity

d_{\lambda}\left|\chi^{\lambda}({\mathcal{C}})\right|^{t}

(3.16)

for
t
close to the mixing time. The key observation is that dimensions of diagrams depend almost only on the number of boxes deep inside the diagram, while characters depend almost only on the geometry of the first rows and columns. This motivates the following decomposition.

Definition 3.7
.

Let
\lambda
be a Young diagram, and let
M\geq 1
be an integer. The depth-
M
triple slicing (or decomposition) of
\lambda
is the triplet of diagrams
(\lambda(1),\lambda(2),\lambda(3))=(\lambda_{1},\lambda^{\leq M}\backslash\lambda_{1},\lambda^{>M})
. We illustrate this in Figure
2
.

{\color[rgb]{1,0.65,0.3}\lambda(3)}

{\color[rgb]{0.4,1,1}\lambda(2)}

{\color[rgb]{1,0.1,0.1}\lambda(1)}

Figure 2:
The depth-4 triple slicing for
\lambda=[25,18,15,14,11,10,9,8,,5,4,3,2,2,1]
.

In what follows, given an integer
M\geq 1
, two integers
0\leq k\leq n
, and two Young diagrams
\mu\subset\lambda
, we always write
n_{i}=|\lambda(i)|
and
k_{i}=|\mu(i)|
for
1\leq i\leq 3
, without specifying the dependence on
M
, and for compactness we may call
(\lambda(i),\mu(i),n_{i},k_{i})_{1\leq i\leq 3}
the parameters of the depth-
M
triple slicing.
Note that by definition we have
n_{1}+n_{2}+n_{3}=n
and
k_{1}+k_{2}+k_{3}=k
.

3.3
Bounds on biased skew dimensions via deep triple slicings

Let
\mu\subset\lambda
be two Young diagrams and let
\theta\in{\mathbb{R}}
. We define the
\theta
-biased skew dimension to be the quantity

B_{\theta}(\lambda,\mu):=d_{\mu}^{\theta}\frac{d_{\lambda\backslash\mu}}{d_{\lambda}}=d_{\mu}^{\theta}\frac{S(\lambda,\mu)}{n^{\downarrow k}},

(3.17)

where the last equality is due to the Naruse hook length formula (written as (
2.6
)).

In particular, if
0\leq\theta\leq 1
, by Lemma
2.21
we have

B_{\theta}(\lambda,\mu)\leq\left(\frac{d_{\lambda\backslash\mu}}{d_{\lambda}}\right)^{1-\theta}\leq 1.

(3.18)

Proposition 3.8
.

Let
\theta>0
. Let
n
and
k
be integers such that
0\leq k\leq n
. Let
\lambda\vdash n
and
\mu\vdash k
such that
\mu\subset\lambda
. Let
M\geq 1
be an integer, and denote the parameters of the depth-
M
triple slicings of
\lambda
and
\mu
by
(\lambda(i),\mu(i),n_{i},k_{i})_{1\leq i\leq 3}
. Then

B_{\theta}(\lambda,\mu)\leq e^{24M\sqrt{n}}\left(\frac{k!}{\prod_{i=1}^{3}k_{i}!}\right)^{\theta}\frac{\prod_{i=1}^{3}n_{i}^{\downarrow k_{i}}}{n^{\downarrow k}}\prod_{i=1}^{3}B_{\theta}(\lambda(i),\mu(i)).

(3.19)

Proof.

First, by Lemma
2.15
we have

d_{\mu}\leq\frac{k!}{k_{1}!k_{2}!k_{3}!}d_{\mu(1)}d_{\mu(2)}d_{\mu(3)}.

(3.20)

Also, by Lemma
2.9
we have

S(\lambda,\mu)\leq S(\lambda,\mu(1))S(\lambda,\mu(2))S(\lambda,\mu(3))=S(\lambda,\mu(1))S(\lambda,\mu(2))S(\lambda(3),\mu(3))

(3.21)

Let us bound the first two terms above separately. We have

S(\lambda,\mu(1))=\sum_{E\in{\mathcal{E}}(\lambda,\mu(1))}H(\lambda,E)\leq\sum_{E\in{\mathcal{E}}(\lambda,\mu(1))}H(\lambda,\mu(1))=|{\mathcal{E}}(\lambda,\mu(1))|H(\lambda,\mu(1)),

(3.22)

and therefore by Lemmas
3.3
and
2.11
(a) we have

S(\lambda,\mu(1))\leq e^{5\sqrt{n}}\left(e^{3\sqrt{n}}H(\lambda(1),\mu(1))\right)=e^{8\sqrt{n}}H(\lambda(1),\mu(1))\leq e^{8M\sqrt{n}}S(\lambda(1),\mu(1)).

(3.23)

By Lemma
3.6
, that is, applying the same argument to each (of the at most
2M
by definition) row and column diagram in the stairs decompositions (see Figure
1
) of
\lambda(2)
and
\mu(2)
, we obtain

\begin{split}S(\lambda,\mu(2))\leq|{\mathcal{E}}(\lambda,\mu(2))|H(\lambda,\mu(2))\leq&\left(e^{3\sqrt{n}}e^{5\sqrt{n}}\right)^{2M}H(\lambda(2),\mu(2))\\
&\leq e^{16M\sqrt{n}}S(\lambda(2),\mu(2)).\end{split}

(3.24)

Combining these bounds, we obtain

S(\lambda,\mu)\leq e^{24M\sqrt{n}}\prod_{i=1}^{3}S(\lambda(i),\mu(i)),

(3.25)

and deduce from the Naruse hook length formula that

\frac{d_{\lambda\backslash\mu}}{d_{\lambda}}=\frac{S(\lambda,\mu)}{n^{\downarrow k}}\leq e^{24M\sqrt{n}}\frac{\prod_{i=1}^{3}n_{i}^{\downarrow k_{i}}}{n^{\downarrow k}}\prod_{i=1}^{3}\frac{d_{\lambda(i)\backslash\mu(i)}}{d_{\lambda(i)}}.

(3.26)

Combining this with (
3.20
), we obtain the bound

B_{\theta}(\lambda,\mu)e^{-24M\sqrt{n}}\leq\left(\frac{k!}{\prod_{i=1}^{3}k_{i}!}\right)^{\theta}\frac{\prod_{i=1}^{3}n_{i}^{\downarrow k_{i}}}{n^{\downarrow k}}\prod_{i=1}^{3}d_{\mu(i)}^{\theta}\frac{d_{\lambda(i)\backslash\mu(i)}}{d_{\lambda(i)}},

(3.27)

which concludes the proof since
\prod_{i=1}^{3}d_{\mu(i)}^{\theta}\frac{d_{\lambda(i)\backslash\mu(i)}}{d_{\lambda(i)}}=\prod_{i=1}^{3}B_{\theta}(\lambda(i),\mu(i))
.
∎

Lemma 3.9
.

Let
n\geq 1
and
\lambda\vdash n
. Let
M\geq 1
be an integer. Let
u
be a box in
\lambda(3)=\lambda^{>M}
. Then
H(\lambda,u)\leq n/M
.

Proof.

Let us write
H(\lambda,u)=h(u)+v(u)-1
, where
h(u)
(resp.
v(u)
) is the number of boxes on the right of (resp. above)
u
in
\lambda
, including
u
. Then
\lambda
must contain an
h(u)\times M
(resp.
M\times v(u)
) rectangle below (resp. on the left of) the hook of
u
. Therefore
MH(\lambda,u)\leq M(h(u)+v(u))=h(u)M+Mv(u)\leq n
, which concludes the proof.
∎

3.4
Specific bounds on biased skew dimensions

Let us fix some notation for this section. Let
\delta>0
,
\eta>0
, and
\rho>0
. Let
\theta=2/3
. Let
(M_{n})
be a sequence of integers
\geq 1
such that
1\lll M_{n}\lll\ln\ln n
. Let
\lambda
be a Young diagram such that
\lambda_{1}^{\prime}\leq\lambda_{1}\leq(1-\rho)|\lambda|
. Let
\mu\subset\lambda
be a Young diagram such that
\eta|\lambda|\leq|\mu|\leq(1-\delta)|\lambda|
. We set
n=|\lambda|
,
k=|\mu|
and
f=n-k
.
Finally, denote the parameters of the depth-
M_{n}
triple decomposition of
\lambda
and
\mu
by
(\lambda(i),\mu(i),n_{i},k_{i})_{1\leq i\leq 3}
.

Lemma 3.10
.

There exist
\varepsilon_{1}\in(0,\rho)
and an integer
N_{0}
such that if
n\geq N_{0}
and
\lambda_{1}\leq\varepsilon_{1}n
,

B_{\theta}(\lambda,\mu)\leq e^{-5n\ln(1/\delta)}.

(3.28)

Proof.

By Lemmas
3.1
,
3.9
, and
2.16
(b), we have, using that the maximal hook length in
\lambda
is at most
2\lambda_{1}
,

\frac{S(\lambda,\mu)}{n^{\downarrow k}}\leq\frac{2^{n}H(\lambda,\mu)}{n^{\downarrow k}}\leq\frac{2^{n}(2\lambda_{1})^{k}}{(n/e)^{k}}\leq 12^{n}\left(\frac{\lambda_{1}}{n}\right)^{\eta n}.

(3.29)

and therefore, using that
k\gtrsim n
and recalling (
3.18
),

B_{\theta}(\lambda,\mu)\leq\left(\frac{S(\lambda,\mu)}{n^{\downarrow k}}\right)^{1/3}\leq\left(O(1)\frac{\lambda_{1}}{n}\right)^{\Omega_{+}(n)},

(3.30)

which concludes the proof.
∎

Let
\varepsilon_{1}
and
N_{0}
as in the previous lemma. The following lemma is intended to be applied to low dimensional diagrams
\lambda
, that is in this context, diagrams whose deep center
\lambda^{>M_{n}}
has size at most a small constant times
n
.

Lemma 3.11
.

There exist
\alpha>0
and
N_{1}\geq N_{0}
such that if
n\geq N_{1}
and
\varepsilon_{1}n\leq\lambda_{1}\leq(1-\varepsilon_{1})n
then we have

B_{\theta}(\lambda,\mu)\leq e^{-2\alpha n\ln(1/\delta)}.

(3.31)

Proof.

Bounding
B_{\theta}(\lambda(i),\mu(i))
by 1 for
i\in\left\{1,2,3\right\}
, we obtain by Proposition
3.8

\begin{split}B_{\theta}(\lambda,\mu)e^{-24M\sqrt{n}}&\leq\left(\frac{k!}{\prod_{i=1}^{3}k_{i}!}\right)^{\theta}\frac{\prod_{i=1}^{3}n_{i}^{\downarrow k_{i}}}{n^{\downarrow k}}\\
&=\left(\frac{\prod_{i=1}^{3}\binom{n_{i}}{k_{i}}}{\binom{n}{k}}\right)^{\theta}\left(\frac{\prod_{i=1}^{3}n_{i}^{\downarrow k_{i}}}{n^{\downarrow k}}\right)^{1-\theta}\leq\left(\frac{\prod_{i=1}^{3}n_{i}^{\downarrow k_{i}}}{n^{\downarrow k}}\right)^{1-\theta}.\end{split}

(3.32)

Moreover,
n_{2}^{\downarrow k_{2}}n_{3}^{\downarrow k_{3}}\leq(n_{2}+n_{3})^{\downarrow k_{2}+k_{3}}=(n-n_{1})^{\downarrow k-k_{1}}
, so

\frac{\prod_{i=1}^{3}n_{i}^{\downarrow k_{i}}}{n^{\downarrow k}}\leq\frac{n_{1}^{\downarrow k_{1}}(n-n_{1})^{\downarrow k-k_{1}}}{n^{\downarrow k}},

(3.33)

which is
e^{-\Omega_{+}(n)}
since
\varepsilon_{1}n\leq\lambda_{1}\leq(1-\varepsilon_{1})n
and
k=\Omega(n)
.
Finally, since
e^{-24M_{n}\sqrt{n}}=e^{o(n)}
and
\theta<1
, we deduce that
B_{\theta}(\lambda,\mu)=e^{-\Omega_{+}(n)}
, which concludes the proof.
∎

Let
N_{1}
as in the previous lemma.

Lemma 3.12
.

Let
\varepsilon^{\prime}>0
. There exists
N_{2}\geq N_{1}
such that if
n\geq N_{2}
,
\varepsilon_{1}n\leq\lambda_{1}\leq(1-\varepsilon_{1})n
, and
n_{3}\geq\varepsilon^{\prime}n
, then

B_{\theta}(\lambda,\mu)\leq\left(\frac{f}{n}\right)^{n_{3}}.

(3.34)

Proof.

First assume that
k_{3}\leq n/\sqrt{\ln M_{n}}
.
By Proposition
3.8
, upper bounding all three
B_{\theta}(\lambda(i),\mu(i))
by 1, and also using that
\theta\leq 1
, we get

B_{\theta}(\lambda,\mu)\leq\frac{k!}{k_{1}!k_{2}!k_{3}!}\frac{n_{1}^{\downarrow k_{1}}n_{2}^{\downarrow k_{2}}n_{3}^{\downarrow k_{3}}}{n^{\downarrow k}}=\frac{\binom{n_{1}}{k_{1}}\binom{n_{2}}{k_{2}}\binom{n_{3}}{k_{3}}}{\binom{n}{k}}.

(3.35)

Since
k_{3}=o(n)
and
n_{3}\gtrsim n
, we have
\binom{n_{3}}{k_{3}}\leq(en_{3}/k_{3})^{k_{3}}=e^{o(n)}
so

B_{\theta}(\lambda,\mu)\leq e^{o(n)}\frac{\binom{n_{1}}{k_{1}}\binom{n_{2}}{k_{2}}}{\binom{n}{k}}\leq e^{o(n)}\frac{\binom{n_{1}+n_{2}}{k_{1}+k_{2}}}{\binom{n}{k}}=e^{o(n)}\frac{\binom{n-n_{3}}{k_{1}+k_{2}}}{\binom{n}{k_{1}+k_{2}}}=e^{o(n)}\left(\frac{(n-n_{3})^{\downarrow k_{1}+k_{2}}}{n^{\downarrow k_{1}+k_{2}}}\right),

(3.36)

and

\begin{split}\left(\frac{(n-n_{3})^{\downarrow k_{1}+k_{2}}}{n^{\downarrow k_{1}+k_{2}}}\right)=\left(\frac{(n-(k_{1}+k_{2}))^{\downarrow n_{3}}}{n^{\downarrow n_{3}}}\right)=\left(\frac{(f+k_{3})^{\downarrow n_{3}}}{n^{\downarrow n_{3}}}\right)&\leq\left(\frac{f+k_{3}}{n}\right)^{n_{3}}e^{-\Omega_{+}(n)}\\
&=e^{o(n)}\left(\frac{f}{n}\right)^{n_{3}}e^{-\Omega_{+}(n)},\end{split}

(3.37)

so overall we have
B_{\theta}(\lambda,\mu)\leq e^{-\Omega_{+}(n)}\left(f/n\right)^{n_{3}}\leq\left(f/n\right)^{n_{3}}
, for
n
large enough.

Now assume that
k_{3}>n/\sqrt{\ln M_{n}}
. We proceed similarly as in the proof of Lemma
3.10
. By Lemmas
3.1
,
3.9
, and
2.16
(b), and since
M_{n}\to\infty
as

, we have

\begin{split}\frac{S(\lambda(3),\mu(3))}{n_{3}^{\downarrow k_{3}}}\leq\frac{2^{n_{3}}(n/M_{n})^{k_{3}}}{(n_{3}/e)^{k_{3}}}=e^{O(n)}\left(\frac{O(1)}{M_{n}}\right)^{k_{3}}&=e^{O(n)}\left(\frac{O(1)}{M_{n}}\right)^{\Omega_{+}(n/\sqrt{\ln M_{n}})}\\
&=e^{\Omega_{+}(n\sqrt{\ln M_{n}})}=e^{-\omega_{+}(n)}.\end{split}

(3.38)

Since
\theta<1
, we deduce that
B_{\theta}(\lambda(3),\mu(3))=e^{-\omega_{+}(n)}
, and it follows from Proposition
3.8
(and since
M_{n}=O(\sqrt{n})
) that

B_{\theta}(\lambda,\mu)\leq e^{24M_{n}\sqrt{n}}B_{\theta}(\lambda(3),\mu(3))=e^{-\omega_{+}(n)},

(3.39)

which, since
f\gtrsim n
and
n_{3}\gtrsim n
, is at most
\left(f/n\right)^{n_{3}}
for
n
large enough. This concludes the proof.
∎

3.5
Characters bounds

We now convert bounds on biased skew dimensions into character bounds.

Proposition 3.13
.

Let
\delta>0
,
\eta>0
,
\rho>0
, and set
\theta=2/3
. Let
\varepsilon_{0}>0
. There exists
N_{3}
such that the following holds. Let
n\geq N_{3}
,
\eta n\leq k\leq(1-\delta)n
, and set
f=n-k
. Let
\lambda\vdash n
such that
\lambda_{1}^{\prime}\leq\lambda_{1}\leq(1-\rho)n
. Finally, let
\sigma\in{\mathfrak{S}}_{n}
with
f
fixed points. Then

\left|\chi^{\lambda}(\sigma)\right|\leq d_{\lambda}^{-(2-\varepsilon_{0})\frac{\ln(n/f)}{\ln n}}.

(3.40)

Proof.

Set
t=\frac{1}{2-\varepsilon_{0}}\frac{\ln n}{\ln(n/f)}
. Since
f\geq\delta n
, we have
t\geq t_{0}:=\frac{1}{2}\frac{\ln n}{\ln(1/\delta)}
. We want to prove that
d_{\lambda}\left|\chi^{\lambda}(\sigma)\right|^{t}\leq 1
.
Here
k\geq\eta n
, so by Lemma
2.23
, for
n
large enough we have

\left|\chi^{\lambda}(\sigma)\right|\leq e^{C_{\textup{part}}\sqrt{n}}\max_{\mu\subset_{\vdash k}\lambda}B_{\theta}(\lambda,\mu).

(3.41)

As previously, let
(M_{n})
be a sequence of integers such that
1\lll M_{n}\lll\ln\ln n
, and denote the parameters of its depth-
M_{n}
triple slicing by
(\lambda(i),n_{i})_{1\leq i\leq 3}
.
Let
\varepsilon_{1}
as in Lemma
3.10
,
\alpha
as in Lemma
3.11
and set
\varepsilon^{\prime}=\alpha
. We split the proof into three cases.

\lambda_{1}\leq\varepsilon_{1}n

By Lemma
3.10
and since
d_{\lambda}\leq\sqrt{n!}\leq n^{n/2}
, for
n
large enough we have

\begin{split}d_{\lambda}\left|\chi^{\lambda}(\sigma)\right|^{t}\leq d_{\lambda}\left|\chi^{\lambda}(\sigma)\right|^{t_{0}}&\leq n^{n/2}\left(e^{C_{\textup{part}}\sqrt{n}}\max_{\mu\subset_{\vdash k}\lambda}B_{\theta}(\lambda,\mu)\right)^{t_{0}}\\
&\leq e^{O(\sqrt{n}\ln n)}n^{n/2}e^{-(5/2)n\ln n}=e^{(-2+o(1))n\ln n}\leq 1.\end{split}

(3.42)

\lambda_{1}>\varepsilon_{1}n
and
n_{3}\leq\varepsilon^{\prime}n

Here we use Lemma
2.20
and Lemma
3.11
. Since
M_{n}=n^{o(1)}
, this gives

d_{\lambda}\left|\chi^{\lambda}(\sigma)\right|^{t}\leq d_{\lambda}\left|\chi^{\lambda}(\sigma)\right|^{t_{0}}\leq(4M_{n})^{n}n^{n_{3}/2}e^{-\alpha n\ln n}\leq e^{(-\alpha/2+o(1))n\ln n}\leq 1.

(3.43)

\lambda_{1}>\varepsilon_{1}n
and
n_{3}>\varepsilon^{\prime}n

By Lemma
2.20
and Lemma
3.12
, and using again that
(4M_{n})^{n}=e^{o(n\ln n)}
and
e^{C_{\textup{part}}\sqrt{n}t}=e^{o(n\ln n)}
, we obtain

d_{\lambda}\left|\chi^{\lambda}(\sigma)\right|^{t}\leq e^{o(n\ln n)}n^{n_{3}/2}(f/n)^{n_{3}\frac{1}{2-\varepsilon_{0}}\frac{\ln n}{\ln(n/f)}}=n^{\left(\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{2-\varepsilon_{0}}+o(1)\right)\ln n}\leq 1.

(3.44)

Hence we have covered all cases, and the proof is complete.
∎

4
Bounds for low-level representations

4.1
The strategy of Diaconis and Shahshahani for transpositions

For transpositions we have the following elegant formula:

\chi^{\lambda}(\tau)=\frac{1}{\binom{n}{2}}\sum_{i}\left(\binom{\lambda_{i}}{2}-\binom{\lambda^{\prime}_{i}}{2}\right),

(4.1)

where
\lambda\vdash n
and
\tau\sim[2,1^{n-2}]
. One can also derive (
4.1
) from the Murnaghan–Nakayama rule and the Naruse hook length formula.
Finding asymptotic bounds for (
4.1
) for all diagrams is a key technical part of the proof of cutoff for random transpositions by Diaconis and Shahshahani
[
DS81
]
. To do so, they partitioned the set of diagrams according to how many boxes they have over the first row, in other words they did a first row decomposition as in Figure
3
, and then used the monotonicity of the formula (
4.1
) to bound the characters. This led to a character bound which was sufficient if
\lambda
has a long first row (that is, if
\lambda_{1}\geq n/2
).

{\color[rgb]{1,0.4,0.4}\lambda(4)=\lambda_{\geq 2}}

{\color[rgb]{0.4,0.4,1}\lambda(1)=\lambda_{1}}

Figure 3:
The first row decomposition for
\lambda=[14,4,3,2,2,1]
.

In this section we use hybrid notation. Let
\lambda
be a Young diagram. We denote again by
\lambda(1)
the first row of
\lambda
,
n_{1}=|\lambda(1)|
or
\lambda_{1}
its size. Now we set
\lambda(4)=\lambda\backslash\lambda(1)
, and denote by
n_{4}=|\lambda(4)|
or
r(\lambda)
its size.
Note that
\lambda(4)
corresponds to
\lambda(2)\cup\lambda(3)
from Section
3
.

For general conjugacy classes there is no usable formula such as (
4.1
), nor monotonicity, and first row decompositions do not lead to character bounds that are sufficient for
0\leq n_{4}\leq n/2
. We can however prove bounds for diagrams
\lambda
with a long enough first row. By symmetry of all our arguments (with respect to transposition of diagrams), the bounds extend to diagrams with a long first column.

4.2
Splitting the characters into two parts

Let
n
and
k
be integers such that
2\leq k\leq n-1
. Set
f=n-k
. Let
\lambda\vdash n
such that
\lambda_{1}\geq k
. Let
\sigma\in{\mathfrak{S}}_{n}
with support size
k
. Recall the branching rule (
2.28
):

\chi^{\lambda}(\sigma)=\sum_{\mu\subset_{\vdash k}\lambda}\ch^{\mu}(\sigma^{*})\frac{d_{\lambda\backslash\mu}}{d_{\lambda}}.

(4.2)

For low-level representations, applying directly the triangle inequality in the branching rule does not lead to precise bounds. One has to first extract the two diagrams
\mu
that matter (the most), namely
\mu=[k]
and
\mu=[k-1,1]
, and crucially also take into account the sign compensation coming from
\mu=[k-1,1]
. We therefore set

W(\lambda,\sigma)=\ch^{[k]}(\sigma^{*})\frac{d_{\lambda\backslash[k]}}{d_{\lambda}}+\ch^{[k-1,1]}(\sigma^{*})\frac{d_{\lambda\backslash[k-1,1]}}{d_{\lambda}}\quad\text{ and }\quad Z(\lambda,\sigma)=\chi^{\lambda}(\sigma)-W(\lambda,\sigma).

(4.3)

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
n
and
k
be integers such that
2\leq k\leq n-1
. Set
f=n-k
. Let
\lambda\vdash n
such that
\lambda_{1}\geq k
. Then

W(\lambda,\sigma)=\frac{1}{n^{\downarrow k}}(S(\lambda,[k])-S(\lambda,[k-1,1])).

(4.4)

Proof.

For any permutation
\tau\in{\mathfrak{S}}_{k}
, we have
\ch^{[k]}(\tau)=1
and
\ch^{[k-1,1]}(\tau)=f_{1}(\tau)-1
, where we recall that
f_{1}(\tau)
denotes the number of fixed points of
\tau
. Therefore
\ch^{[k]}(\sigma^{*})=1
and
\ch^{[k-1,1]}(\sigma^{*})=f_{1}(\sigma^{*})-1=-1
since
\sigma^{*}
is fixed-point-free by definition. It follows that
W(\lambda,\sigma)=\frac{d_{\lambda\backslash[k]}}{d_{\lambda}}-\frac{d_{\lambda\backslash[k-1,1]}}{d_{\lambda}}
, which rewrites as desired by the Naruse hook length formula (
2.6
).
∎

The previous lemma shows that
W(\lambda,\sigma)
depends only on the support size of
\sigma
. From now on we therefore write
W_{\lambda}(k)
for
W(\lambda,\sigma)
.

4.3
Bounding excited sums with geometric sums

We first bound excited sums
S(\lambda,\mu)
when
\mu
is a row diagram.

Lemma 4.2
.

Let
n
and
k
be integers such that
2\leq k\leq n-1
. Set
f=n-k
. Let
\lambda\vdash n
such that
\lambda_{1}\geq k
. Denote
r=n-\lambda_{1}
. Assume that
r\leq f/4
and let
0\leq\ell\leq k
. Then

S(\lambda,[\ell])\leq e^{6r/f}\lambda_{1}^{\downarrow\ell}.

(4.5)

Proof.

First, partitioning the set of excited diagrams depending on how many boxes they have over the first row, we have

S(\lambda,[\ell])=H(\lambda,[\ell])+\sum_{1\leq i\leq\ell}H(\lambda,[\ell-i])S(\lambda,\nu(\ell,i)),

(4.6)

where
\nu(\ell,i)=\Shift_{\ell-i+1,1}([i])
, as represented in Figure
4
.

Figure 4:
In orange: the diagram
[\ell-i]
(with
\ell=8
and
i=3
). In red: the diagram
\nu(\ell,i)=\Shift_{\ell-i+1,1}([i])
. Both diagrams are represented within the diagram
\lambda=[n-25,12,9,3,1]
.

Moreover, for each
0\leq i\leq\ell
,

S(\lambda,\nu(\ell,i))=\frac{d_{\lambda\backslash\nu(\ell,i)}}{d_{\lambda}}r^{\downarrow i}\leq r^{\downarrow i},

(4.7)

and by Lemma
2.11
(b), we have
H(\lambda,[\ell-i])\leq e^{2r/n}\lambda_{1}^{\downarrow\ell-i}\leq e^{2r/f}\lambda_{1}^{\downarrow\ell-i}
.
Therefore for each
0\leq i\leq\ell
we have, using that
\lambda_{1}-\ell=n-r-\ell\geq n-r-k=f-r\geq f/2
,

\frac{H(\lambda,[\ell-i])S(\lambda,\nu(\ell,i))}{\lambda_{1}^{\downarrow\ell}}e^{-2r/f}\leq\frac{\lambda_{1}^{\downarrow\ell-i}r^{\downarrow i}}{\lambda_{1}^{\downarrow\ell}}=\frac{r^{\downarrow i}}{(\lambda_{1}-\ell+i)^{\downarrow i}}\leq\frac{r^{i}}{(\lambda_{1}-\ell+i)^{i}}\leq\left(\frac{2r}{f}\right)^{i}.

(4.8)

Also, we have
\sum_{i=0}^{\ell}(2r/f)^{i}\leq 1+(2r/f)\sum_{i\geq 0}(1/2)^{i}=1+4r/f\leq e^{4r/f}
, since
2r/f\leq 1/2
. We conclude that

\frac{S(\lambda,[\ell])}{\lambda_{1}^{\downarrow\ell}}=\frac{H(\lambda,[\ell])}{\lambda_{1}^{\downarrow\ell}}+\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}\frac{H(\lambda,[\ell-i])S(\lambda,\nu(\ell,i))}{\lambda_{1}^{\downarrow\ell}}\leq e^{2r/f}\sum_{i=0}^{\ell}(2r/f)^{i}\leq e^{6r/f}.\qed

(4.9)

Let us generalize this to diagrams which may have boxes over the first row.

Lemma 4.3
.

Let
n
and
k
be integers such that
2\leq k\leq n-1
. Set
f=n-k
. Let
\lambda\vdash n
such that
\lambda_{1}\geq k
and
r\leq f/4
, where
r=n-\lambda_{1}
. Let
\mu\subset_{\vdash k}\lambda
, and denote
j=k-\mu_{1}
. Then

\frac{S(\lambda,\mu)}{\lambda_{1}^{\downarrow k}}\leq e^{6r/f}\left(\frac{2r}{f}\right)^{j}.

(4.10)

Proof.

Set
\ell:=\mu_{1}=k-j
. By Lemmas
2.9
and
4.2
, we have

S(\lambda,\mu)\leq S(\lambda,[\ell])S(\lambda,\mu_{\geq 2})\leq e^{6r/f}\lambda_{1}^{\downarrow\ell}r^{\downarrow j}.

(4.11)

Moreover, using again that
\lambda_{1}-\ell+j\geq f/2\geq r
, we get

\frac{\lambda_{1}^{\downarrow\ell}r^{\downarrow j}}{\lambda_{1}^{\downarrow k}}=\frac{r^{\downarrow j}}{(\lambda_{1}-\ell+j)^{\downarrow j}}\leq\left(\frac{2r}{f}\right)^{j}.

(4.12)

This concludes the proof.
∎

4.4
Bounds on the main term

Lemma 4.4
.

Let
n
and
k
be integers such that
2\leq k\leq n-1
. Set
f=n-k
. Let
\lambda\vdash n
such that
\lambda_{1}\geq k
and
r\leq f/6
, where
r=n-\lambda_{1}
. Then
W_{\lambda}(k)\geq 0
.

Proof.

On the one hand, we have
S(\lambda,[k])/\lambda_{1}^{\downarrow k}\geq H(\lambda,[k])/\lambda_{1}^{\downarrow k}\geq 1
. On the other hand, by Lemma
4.3
we have

\frac{S(\lambda,[k-1,1])}{\lambda_{1}^{\downarrow k}}\leq e^{6r/f}\frac{2r}{f}\leq e\frac{2}{6}\leq 1.

(4.13)

It follows that
S(\lambda,[k])-S(\lambda,[k-1,1])\geq 0
, and therefore that
W_{\lambda}(k)\geq 0
by Lemma
4.1
.
∎

For the next lemma we need additional notation on Young diagrams. Let
\lambda
be a Young diagram and
1\leq i\leq\lambda_{1}
. Then
u_{i}=u_{i}(\lambda):=H(\lambda,(i,1))-(\lambda_{1}-i+1)
is the number of boxes in the
i
-th column of
\lambda
that are strictly above the box with coordinates
(i,1)
, as represented in Figure
5
. For
1\leq j\leq\lambda_{1}
, we also denote
u_{\leq j}=\sum_{1\leq i\leq j}u_{i}
and
u_{\geq j}=\sum_{j\leq i\leq\lambda_{1}}u_{i}
.

{\color[rgb]{1,0.85,0.85}H(\lambda,b)}

{\color[rgb]{0.4,1,0.4}u_{i}}

{\color[rgb]{0.4,0.4,1}\lambda_{1}-(i-1)}

Lemma 4.5
.

Let
n
and
k
be integers such that
2\leq k\leq n-1
. Set
f=n-k
. Let
\lambda\vdash n
such that
\lambda_{1}\geq k
. Then

H(\lambda,[k-1])(\lambda_{1}-(k-1)-u_{\leq k-1})\leq\lambda_{1}^{\downarrow k}.

(4.14)

Proof.

By definition we have
H(\lambda,[k-1])=\prod_{1\leq i\leq k-1}(\lambda_{1}-(i-1)+u_{i})
. Therefore the quantity we want to bound can be rewritten as

\left(\prod_{1\leq i\leq k-1}\lambda_{1}-(i-1)+u_{i}\right)(\lambda_{1}-(k-1)-u_{\leq k-1}).

(4.15)

Moreover moving a box of
\lambda
from above the first row to the first row (weakly) increases all terms
\lambda_{1}+u_{i}-(i-1)
, while
\lambda_{1}-u_{\leq k-1}-(k-1)
does not change (since
\lambda_{1}
increases by 1, but so decreases
u_{\leq k-1}
). Therefore the diagram
\lambda=[n]
is maximal for the product above, and we conclude that

\left(\prod_{1\leq i\leq k-1}\lambda_{1}-(i-1)+u_{i}\right)(\lambda_{1}-(k-1)-u_{\leq k-1})\leq\lambda_{1}^{\downarrow k}.\qed

(4.16)

Lemma 4.6
.

Let
n
and
k
be integers such that
2\leq k\leq n-1
. Set
f=n-k
. Let
\lambda\vdash n
such that
\lambda_{1}\geq k
and
r\leq f/6
, where
r=n-\lambda_{1}
. Then

W_{\lambda}(k)\leq\left(\frac{f}{n}\right)^{r}e^{12r^{2}/f^{2}}.

(4.17)

Proof.

Recall that
\nu(k,i)=\Shift_{k-i+1,1}([i])
, as in Figure
4
. Let us first upper bound
S(\lambda,[k])
. Observe that

\begin{split}H(\lambda,[k])+H(\lambda,[k-1])S(\lambda,\nu(k,1))&=H(\lambda,[k-1])S(\lambda,(k,1))\\
&=H(\lambda,[k-1])(\lambda_{1}-(k-1)+u_{\geq k}).\end{split}

(4.18)

Then applying (
4.8
) with
\ell=k
for the terms
j\geq 2
we get

\begin{split}S(\lambda,[k])&\leq H(\lambda,[k])+H(\lambda,[k-1])S(\lambda,\nu(k,1))+\sum_{2\leq j\leq k}H(\lambda,[k-j])S(\lambda,\nu(k,j))\\
&=H(\lambda,[k-1])(\lambda_{1}-(k-1)+u_{\geq k})+\sum_{2\leq j\leq k}H(\lambda,[k-j])S(\lambda,\nu(k,j))\\
&\leq H(\lambda,[k-1])(\lambda_{1}-(k-1)+u_{\geq k})+e^{6r/f}\left(2r/f\right)^{2}\lambda_{1}^{\downarrow k}.\end{split}

(4.19)

On the other hand we have

S(\lambda,[k-1,1])\geq H(\lambda,[k-1])S(\lambda,(2,1))=H(\lambda,[k-1])r.

(4.20)

Since
r-u_{\geq k}=u_{\leq k-1}
, it follows from Lemma
4.5
that

S(\lambda,[k])-S(\lambda,[k-1,1])-e^{6r/f}\left(\frac{2r}{f}\right)^{2}\lambda_{1}^{\downarrow k}\leq H(\lambda,[k-1])(\lambda_{1}-(k-1)-u_{\leq k-1})\leq\lambda_{1}^{\downarrow k},

(4.21)

which can be rewritten as

W_{k}(\lambda)\leq\frac{\lambda_{1}^{\downarrow k}}{n^{\downarrow k}}\left(1+e^{6r/f}\left(\frac{2r}{f}\right)^{2}\right).

(4.22)

Finally, we have

\frac{\lambda_{1}^{\downarrow k}}{n^{\downarrow k}}=\frac{f^{\downarrow r}}{n^{\downarrow r}}\leq(f/n)^{r},

(4.23)

and since
r\leq f/6
, we also have

1+e^{6r/f}\left(2r/f\right)^{2}\leq 1+3\left(2r/f\right)^{2}=1+12\left(r/f\right)^{2}\leq e^{12r^{2}/f^{2}},

(4.24)

which concludes the proof.
∎

4.5
Bounds on the error term

Lemma 4.7
.

Let
0\leq\theta\leq 1
. Let
n
and
k
be integers such that
2\leq k\leq n-1
. Set
f=n-k
. Let
\lambda\vdash n
such that
\lambda_{1}\geq k
and
n-\lambda_{1}\leq f/6
. Let
(\lambda(i),\mu(i),n_{i},k_{i})_{i\in\left\{1,4\right\}}
be the parameters of the first row decompositions as in Figure
3
. Then

B_{\theta}(\lambda,\mu)=d_{\mu}^{\theta}\frac{S(\lambda,\mu)}{n^{\downarrow k}}\leq e^{6n_{4}/f}\binom{k}{k_{4}}^{\theta}\frac{n_{1}^{\downarrow k_{1}}n_{4}^{\downarrow k_{4}}}{n^{\downarrow k}}B_{\theta}(\lambda(1),\mu(1))B_{\theta}(\lambda(4),\mu(4)).

(4.25)

In particular, recalling that
\theta_{k}=\frac{1}{2}\left(1+\frac{C_{\textup{virt}}}{\ln k}\right)
and assuming also that
k_{4}\geq 2
, we have

p(j)B_{\theta_{k}}(\lambda,\mu)\leq\left(\frac{f}{n}\right)^{r}\left(C_{0}\frac{kr^{2}}{jf^{2}}\right)^{j/2}.

(4.26)

where
r=n-\lambda_{1}=n_{4}
,
j=|\mu_{\geq 2}|=k_{4}
,
C_{0}=16e^{C_{\textup{virt}}/2+2}
is a universal constant, and we recall that
p(j)
is the number of integer partitions of
j
.

Proof.

In this proof we use hybrid notation, with
n_{4}=r
and
k_{4}=j
. The arguments are similar to those of the proof of Proposition
3.8
.

First, we have
S(\lambda,\mu)\leq S(\lambda,\mu(1))S(\lambda,\mu(4))=S(\lambda,\mu(1))S(\lambda(4),\mu(4))
.
Moreover by Lemma
4.2
, we have
S(\lambda,\mu(1))\leq e^{6r/f}\lambda_{1}^{\downarrow\mu_{1}}\leq e^{6r/f}S(\lambda(1),\mu(1))
. Finally,
d_{\mu}\leq\binom{k}{j}d_{\mu(1)}d_{\mu(4)}
. The first point follows.
Now we have
\binom{k}{j}\leq k^{j}
and
\binom{k}{j}\leq(ek/j)^{j}
, so

\binom{k}{j}^{\theta_{k}}=\binom{k}{j}^{C_{\textup{virt}}/(2\ln k)}\binom{k}{j}^{1/2}\leq\left(k^{j}\right)^{C_{\textup{virt}}/(2\ln k)}\left((ek/j)^{j}\right)^{1/2}=\left(e^{C_{\textup{virt}}/2+1}k/j\right)^{j/2}.

(4.27)

Moreover, recalling (
4.12
) and (
4.23
), we have

\frac{n_{1}^{\downarrow k_{1}}n_{4}^{\downarrow k_{4}}}{n^{\downarrow k}}=\frac{n_{1}^{\downarrow k}}{n^{\downarrow k}}\frac{n_{1}^{\downarrow k_{1}}n_{4}^{\downarrow k_{4}}}{n_{1}^{\downarrow k}}=\frac{\lambda_{1}^{\downarrow k}}{n^{\downarrow k}}\frac{\lambda_{1}^{\downarrow k-j}r^{\downarrow j}}{\lambda_{1}^{\downarrow k}}\leq\left(\frac{f}{n}\right)^{r}\left(\frac{2r}{f}\right)^{j}

(4.28)

and we have
e^{6r/f}\leq e\leq e^{j/2}
by assumption. Finally, bounding the biased dimensions by 1 (recalling (
3.18
)), and since
p(j)\leq 2^{j}
, we conclude that

p(j)B_{\theta_{k}}(\lambda,\mu)\leq 2^{j}e^{j/2}\left(e^{C_{\textup{virt}}/2+1}\frac{k}{j}\right)^{j/2}\left(\frac{f}{n}\right)^{r}\left(\frac{2r}{f}\right)^{j}=\left(\frac{f}{n}\right)^{r}\left(C_{0}\frac{kr^{2}}{jf^{2}}\right)^{j/2}.\qed

(4.29)

Lemma 4.8
.

Let
\alpha>0
.
Then,

\sum_{j\geq 2}(\alpha/j)^{j/2}\leq e^{8\alpha}-1.

(4.30)

Proof.

Set for this proof

S(\alpha)=\sum_{j\geq 2}(\alpha/j)^{j/2},\quad\quad\text{and}\quad\quad u_{\alpha}(j)=(\alpha/j)^{j/2}\quad\text{ for any integer $j\geq 1$}.

(4.31)

Assume first that
\alpha\leq 1
.
Then
j\mapsto u_{\alpha}(j)
is decreasing, so

S(\alpha)=\sum_{j\geq 2}u_{\alpha}(j)=\sum_{m\geq 1}\left(u_{\alpha}(2m)+u_{\alpha}(2m+1)\right)\leq 2\sum_{m\geq 1}u_{\alpha}(2m).

(4.32)

Moreover, since
\alpha\leq 1
we have

\sum_{m\geq 1}u_{\alpha}(2m)=\sum_{m\geq 1}\left(\frac{\alpha/2}{m}\right)^{m}\leq\sum_{m\geq 1}\left(\alpha/2\right)^{m}\leq\alpha\sum_{m\geq 1}\left(1/2\right)^{m}=\alpha,

(4.33)

and we conclude that
S(\alpha)\leq 8\alpha\leq e^{8\alpha}-1
.

Assume now that
\alpha\geq 1
. Seen as a function over the positive real numbers, the function
j\in{\mathbb{R}}^{*}_{+}\mapsto(\alpha/j)^{j/2}
is maximized at
j=\alpha/e
, where it takes the value
e^{\alpha/(2e)}
. Therefore for any integer
j\geq 1
we have
(\alpha/j)^{j/2}\leq e^{\alpha/(2e)}
.
Set
m_{0}=3\left\lceil\alpha/e\right\rceil\in[\alpha,3\alpha]
.
Then,

\sum_{2\leq j\leq 2m_{0}}u_{\alpha}(j)\leq 2m_{0}e^{\alpha/(2e)}\leq 6\alpha e^{\alpha}\leq e^{7\alpha}.

(4.34)

Since
j\in[\alpha/e,\infty)\mapsto(\alpha/j)^{j/2}
is decreasing, starting as in (
4.32
) and using that
m^{m}\geq m!
we get the following bound for the tail of the sum:

\sum_{j>2m_{0}}u_{\alpha}(j)\leq 2\sum_{m\geq m_{0}}\left(\frac{\alpha/2}{m}\right)^{m}\leq\sum_{m\geq m_{0}}\left(\frac{\alpha}{m}\right)^{m}\leq\sum_{m\geq m_{0}}\frac{\alpha^{m}}{m!}\leq e^{\alpha}.

(4.35)

We conclude that

S(\alpha)=\sum_{2\leq j\leq 2m_{0}}u_{\alpha}(j)+\sum_{j>2m_{0}}u_{\alpha}(j)\leq e^{7\alpha}+e^{\alpha}\leq e^{8\alpha}-1.\qed

(4.36)

Proposition 4.9
.

There exists a universal constant
C>0
such that the following holds. Let
n
and
k
be integers such that
2\leq k\leq n-1
. Set
f=n-k
. Let
\lambda\vdash n
such that
\lambda_{1}\geq k
and
r\leq f/6
, where
r=n-\lambda_{1}
. Let
\sigma\in{\mathfrak{S}}_{n}
with support size
k
.
Then,

\left|Z(\lambda,\sigma)\right|\leq\left(\frac{f}{n}\right)^{r}\left(e^{Cr^{2}k/f^{2}}-1\right),

(4.37)

where
Z(\lambda,\sigma)
was defined in (
4.3
).

Proof.

First, for each
j\geq 2
there are at most
p(j)
Young diagrams
\mu\subset_{\vdash k}\lambda
with
j
boxes over the first row, and therefore by Lemma
4.7
we have

\sum_{\mu\subset_{\vdash k}\lambda{\;:\;}|\mu_{\geq 2}|=j}B_{\theta_{k}}(\lambda,\mu)\leq p(j)\max_{\mu\subset_{\vdash k}\lambda{\;:\;}|\mu_{\geq 2}|=j}B_{\theta_{k}}(\lambda,\mu)\leq\left(C_{0}\frac{kr^{2}}{jf^{2}}\right)^{j/2}\left(\frac{f}{n}\right)^{r},

(4.38)

where
C_{0}
is the universal constant from Lemma
4.7
.
We deduce from Lemma
4.8
, applied with
\alpha=Cr^{2}k/f^{2}
, and setting
C=8C_{0}
, that

\left|Z(\lambda,\sigma)\right|\leq\left(\frac{f}{n}\right)^{r}\left(e^{Cr^{2}k/f^{2}}-1\right),

(4.39)

which concludes the proof.
∎

4.6
Combined bound

Proposition 4.10
.

There exists a universal constant
C>0
such that the following holds. Let
n
and
k
be integers such that
2\leq k\leq n-1
. Set
f=n-k
. Let
\lambda\vdash n
such that
\lambda_{1}\geq k
and
r\leq f/6
, where
r=n-\lambda_{1}
. Let
\sigma\in{\mathfrak{S}}_{n}
with support size
k
.
Then,

\left|\chi^{\lambda}(\sigma)\right|\leq\left(\frac{f}{n}\right)^{r}e^{Cr^{2}k/f^{2}}.

(4.40)

Proof.

Recall that
\chi^{\lambda}(\sigma)=W(\lambda,\sigma)+Z(\lambda,\sigma)=W_{\lambda}(k)+Z(\lambda,\sigma)
by definition. By the triangle inequality, and since
W_{\lambda}(k)\geq 0
by Lemma
4.4
, we get

\left|\chi^{\lambda}(\sigma)\right|\leq|W_{\lambda}(k)|+|Z(\lambda,\sigma)|=W_{\lambda}(k)+|Z(\lambda,\sigma)|.

(4.41)

Applying Lemma
4.6
to
W_{\lambda}(k)
and Proposition
4.9
to
|Z(\lambda,\sigma)|
then concludes the proof.
∎

4.7
Character bound written in exponential form

We now combine the previous results to prove character bounds for low-level representations. We set
\zeta(r)=\ln\max(1,r)
for
r\geq 0
.

Proposition 4.11
.

There exist universal constants
n_{0}\geq 3
,
0<c_{0}<1
such that the following holds. Let
n
and
k
be integers such that
n\geq n_{0}
and
2\leq k\leq n-1
,
\sigma\in{\mathfrak{S}}_{n}
with support
k
, and
\lambda\vdash n
such that
r:=n-\lambda_{1}\leq c_{0}\frac{f^{2}}{n}
, where
f=n-k
. Then

d_{\lambda}\left|\chi^{\lambda}(\sigma)\right|^{\frac{\ln n}{\ln(n/f)}}\leq d_{\lambda}^{-\frac{\zeta(r)}{\ln n}}\quad\quad\text{ or equivalently }\quad\quad\left|\chi^{\lambda}(\sigma)\right|\leq d_{\lambda}^{-\frac{\ln(n/f)}{\ln n}\left(1+\frac{\zeta(r)}{\ln n}\right)}.

(4.42)

Proof.

If
r=0
then
d_{\lambda}=1=\chi^{\lambda}(\sigma)
. If
r=1
then
d_{\lambda}=n-1\leq n
and,
\ch^{\lambda}(\sigma)=f-1
so
\left|\chi^{\lambda}(\sigma)\right|=\frac{f-1}{n-1}\leq\frac{f}{n}
, and we have

d_{\lambda}\left|\chi^{\lambda}(\sigma)\right|^{\frac{\ln n}{\ln(n/f)}}\leq n(f/n)^{\frac{\ln n}{\ln(n/f)}}=1.

(4.43)

We may therefore assume that
r\geq 2
. Then we have
r!\geq r^{r/2}
. For the rest of the proof, we set

t=\frac{\ln n}{\ln(n/f)}.

(4.44)

By Proposition
4.10
, we have

\left|\chi^{\lambda}(\sigma)\right|\leq(f/n)^{r}e^{Cr^{2}k/f^{2}},

(4.45)

where
C
is a universal constant. Therefore, since
d_{\lambda}\leq n^{r}/\sqrt{r!}\leq n^{r}/r^{r/4}
, we get

d_{\lambda}\left|\chi^{\lambda}(\sigma)\right|^{t}\leq\frac{n^{r}}{r^{r/4}}(f/n)^{t}\exp\left(C\frac{r^{2}k}{f^{2}}t\right)=\exp\left(C\frac{r^{2}k}{f^{2}}t-\frac{1}{4}r\ln r\right).

(4.46)

Let
c_{0}=\frac{1}{96C}
. In particular
c_{0}\leq\frac{\ln 2}{32C}
.
We may assume that
f\geq\sqrt{n}
since for
f\leq\sqrt{n}
(and
c_{0}<1
) the condition
r\leq c_{0}f^{2}/n
is trivial. Let us bound
C\frac{r^{2}k}{f^{2}}t
for different values of
f
and
r
.

First case:
\sqrt{n}\leq f<n^{3/4}

Here we have
t\leq 4
, so

C\frac{r^{2}k}{f^{2}}t\leq 4C\frac{r^{2}n}{f^{2}}\leq 4Cc_{0}r\leq\frac{1}{8}r\ln 2\leq\frac{1}{8}r\ln r.

(4.47)

Second case:
f\geq n^{3/4}
and
r\geq n^{1/12}

We have
t=-(\ln n)/\ln(1-k/n)\leq\tfrac{k}{n}\ln n
. Therefore

C\frac{r^{2}k}{f^{2}}t\leq C\frac{r^{2}n}{f^{2}}\ln n\leq Cc_{0}r\ln n\leq 12Cc_{0}r\ln r\leq\frac{1}{8}r\ln r.

(4.48)

Third case:
f\geq n^{3/4}
and
r<n^{1/12}

For
n
large enough we have
C\ln n\leq n^{1/12}
and
e^{n^{-1/4}}\leq 2^{1/8}
, so by the same argument as before,

C\frac{r^{2}k}{f^{2}}t\leq C\frac{r^{2}n}{f^{2}}\ln n\leq n^{1/12}\frac{\left(n^{1/12}\right)^{2}n}{\left(n^{3/4}\right)^{2}}=n^{-1/4}\leq\frac{1}{8}2\ln 2\leq\frac{1}{8}r\ln r.

(4.49)

Conclusion

We have proved that in all cases we have
C\frac{r^{2}k}{f^{2}}t-\frac{1}{4}r\ln r\leq-\frac{1}{8}r\ln r
(for
n
large enough). We deduce that for
2\leq r\leq c_{0}f^{2}/n
, using that
d_{\lambda}\leq n^{r}=r^{r\frac{\ln n}{\ln r}}
, that is,
d_{\lambda}^{-\frac{\ln r}{\ln n}}\geq r^{-r}
, we have

d_{\lambda}\left|\chi^{\lambda}(\sigma)\right|^{t}\leq r^{-r/8}\leq d_{\lambda}^{-\frac{\ln r}{8\ln n}}.

(4.50)

Putting this together with the cases
r=0
and
r=1
we conclude that for any
0\leq r\leq c_{0}f^{2}/n
,

d_{\lambda}\left|\chi^{\lambda}(\sigma)\right|^{\frac{\ln n}{\ln(n/f)}}\leq d_{\lambda}^{-\frac{\ln\max(1,r)}{8\ln n}}=d_{\lambda}^{-\frac{\zeta(r)}{\ln n}}.\qed

(4.51)

Remark 4.1
.

The relevance of the previous proposition is to have some additional compensation, but the precise function (here we proved the statement with
r\mapsto\tfrac{1}{8}\ln\max(1,r)
) does not matter, as long as it tends to infinity. Adapting the arguments of the proof, one could show that the same statement holds when both
n
and
r
diverge, with
1/2-o(1)
in place of
1/8
. Informally, in terms of mixing times this means that the representations
\lambda
such that
n-\lambda_{1}
is close to
n^{\alpha}
stop contributing to the
L^{2}
distance to stationarity from time about
\left(1-\alpha/2\right)\frac{\ln n}{\ln(n/f)}
, which is long before the mixing time.

5
Bootstrapped and unified character bounds

Different techniques can be tailored to bound characters depending on the diagram shape we want to study. For example, Féray and Śniady
[
FŚ11
]
developed a technique that gives better bounds on characters
\ch^{\lambda}(\sigma)
if the outer hook
s(\lambda)
and the support of
\sigma\in{\mathfrak{S}}_{n}
are small. On the other hand, the bounds of Larsen and Shalev
[
LS08
]
are precise for permutations with few fixed points and all diagrams
\lambda
, and are especially good for diagrams with a long first row and cycle structures close to involving a single cycle length (i.e. a cycle type close to
[n/m]^{m}
for some fixed integer
m
).

So far, we proved bounds for permutations with
\omega(\sqrt{n})
fixed points and diagrams
\lambda
with a long first row, as well as permutations whose support and number of fixed points are of order
n
and all diagrams
\lambda
.
Our bounds for diagrams with a very long first row are already precise, but the arguments from the previous sections do not give uniform bounds for characters that have a small support. However, as we see in this section, probabilistic arguments allow the deduction of character bounds for permutations with a small support from those for larger support.

The idea is the following: the distribution of a Markov chain with transition matrix
P
at time
t
(which is a multiple of
m
) is the same as that of the Markov chain with transition matrix
P^{m}
at time
t/m
. For example if
\mu_{t}
denotes the distribution of the random transposition walk after
t
steps and
m=\left\lfloor\varepsilon n/2\right\rfloor
, then
\mu_{m}
is conjugacy invariant and concentrated on permutations whose support is close to
\varepsilon n
, so
\mu_{t}=\mu_{m}^{*t/m}
is the distribution of a product of
t/m
random permutations with support close to
\varepsilon n
. This simple yet powerful idea dates back at least to
[
KL85
]
and more complex versions were developed in comparison techniques for Markov chains
[
DSC93a
,
DSC93b
]
.

In the context of conjugacy invariant walks on groups, it was an important tool at least in
[
MSP07
]
, in
[
BŞ19
]
where it allowed studying the problem on a scale on which curvature behaves well, and in
[
LT24
]
where it recently allowed Larsen and Tiep to bootstrap character bounds for group elements with large
support
(for a suitable generalization of the notion of support to groups other than the symmetric group) into uniform character bounds. Our approach is therefore similar to that of
[
MSP07
]
and
[
LT24
]
. However, in order to prove character bounds with error terms precise enough for cutoff, we apply bootstrapping only to high enough frequencies.

5.1
Characters and powers of eigenvalues

Recall that if
{\mathcal{C}}\in\Conj^{*}({\mathfrak{S}}_{n})
, that is, if
{\mathcal{C}}
is a non-trivial conjugacy class of
{\mathfrak{S}}_{n}
, the distribution of the simple random walk on
\Cay({\mathfrak{S}}_{n},{\mathcal{C}})
started at
\Id
and after
t
steps is
\mu_{t}:=\Unif_{{\mathcal{C}}}^{*t}
, the
t
-fold convolution product of
\Unif_{{\mathcal{C}}}
.

More explicitly, let
t\geq 1
,
X_{t}\sim\mu_{t}
,
X_{1}\sim\mu_{1}=\Unif_{{\mathcal{C}}}
, and denote by
P
the transition matrix of the random walk on
{\mathfrak{S}}_{n}
driven by
\mu_{1}
. Then the transition matrix of the random walk on
{\mathfrak{S}}_{n}
driven by
\mu_{t}
is nothing else than
P^{t}
. In terms of eigenvalues, this means that
{\mathbb{E}}\left[\chi^{\lambda}(X_{1})\right]^{t}={\mathbb{E}}\left[\chi^{\lambda}(X_{t})\right]
. Therefore, we have

\chi^{\lambda}({\mathcal{C}})^{t}=\left(\sum_{\sigma\in{\mathfrak{S}}_{n}}\mu_{1}(\sigma)\right)={\mathbb{E}}\left[\chi^{\lambda}(X_{1})\right]^{t}={\mathbb{E}}\left[\chi^{\lambda}(X_{t})\right]=\sum_{{\mathcal{C}}^{\prime}\in\Conj(\mathfrak{S}_{n})}\mu_{t}({\mathcal{C}}^{\prime})\chi^{\lambda}({\mathcal{C}}^{\prime}).

(5.1)

A similar identity appeared for instance in the definition of
g_{1}

in
[
MSP07
]
, below Lemma 10 on Page 934.

Lemma 5.1
.

Let
n\geq 1
,
{\mathcal{C}}\in\Conj^{*}(\mathfrak{S}_{n})
,
\lambda\vdash n
, and
t\geq 0
.
Set
\mu_{t}=\Unif_{{\mathcal{C}}}^{*t}
. Let
B\subset\mathfrak{S}_{n}
, and denote its complement by
B^{c}
. Then we have

\left|\chi^{\lambda}({\mathcal{C}})^{t}\right|\leq\mu_{t}(B^{c})+\max_{\sigma\in B}\left|\chi^{\lambda}(\sigma)\right|.

(5.2)

Proof.

Applying the law of total probability and the triangle inequality in (
5.1
), we get

\begin{split}\left|\chi^{\lambda}({\mathcal{C}})^{t}\right|&=\left|\sum_{\sigma\in B^{c}}\mu_{t}(\sigma)\chi^{\lambda}(\sigma)+\sum_{\sigma\in B}\mu_{t}(\sigma)\chi^{\lambda}(\sigma)\right|\\
&\leq\mu_{t}(B^{c})\max_{\sigma\in B^{c}}\left|\chi^{\lambda}(\sigma)\right|+\mu_{t}(B)\max_{\sigma\in B}\left|\chi^{\lambda}(\sigma)\right|.\end{split}

(5.3)

Using that
\max_{\sigma\in B^{c}}\left|\chi^{\lambda}(\sigma)\right|\leq 1
and
\mu_{t}(B)\leq 1
gives the desired result.
∎

5.2
Multi-coupon collector estimates

The goal of this subsection is to control the size of the support of the product of i.i.d. random permutations uniform from a given conjugacy class.
To this end, we introduce the following notation. Let
n,p,q,t\geq 1
be integers. Let
S_{n,q}(1),S_{n,q}(2),\ldots
be a sequence of i.i.d. uniformly random subsets of
[n]=\left\{1,2,\ldots,n\right\}
of size
q
. Let

N_{n,p,q}(t)=[p]\cap\bigcup_{i=1}^{t}S_{n,q}(i)\,,

(5.4)

\begin{split}N^{(2)}_{n,p,q}(t)&=\left\{(i,j)\in[t]\times[p]{\;:\;}j\in S_{n,q}(i),\text{ and }j\in S_{n,q}(i^{\prime})\text{ for some }i^{\prime}<i\right\}\\
&=\left\{(i,j)\in[t]\times[p]{\;:\;}j\in(\cup_{i^{\prime}=1}^{i-1}S_{n,q}(i^{\prime}))\cap S_{n,q}(i)\right\},\end{split}

(5.5)

and

M^{(2)}_{n,p,q}(t)=\left|N^{(2)}_{n,p,q}(t)\right|=\sum_{i=1}^{t}\left|[p]\cap\left(\bigcup_{i^{\prime}=1}^{i-1}S_{n,q}(i^{\prime})\right)\cap S_{n,q}(i)\right|.

(5.6)

In words,
M^{(2)}_{n,p,q}(t)
is the number of times elements of
[p]
that were already picked previously in the process
S_{n,q}(1),S_{n,q}(2),\ldots,S_{n,q}(t)
are picked again afterwards, that is, the number of repetitions (possibly counting the same element of
[p]
multiple times). In particular, one has

M^{(2)}_{n,p,q}(t)=qt-\left|[p]\cap\bigcup_{i=1}^{t}S_{n,q}(i)\right|.

(5.7)

If
X
and
Y
are two
{\mathbb{R}}
-valued random variables, we say that
X
is (weakly)
stochastically dominated

by
Y
, and write
X\preceq Y
, if for every
x\in{\mathbb{R}}
,
{\mathbb{P}}(X\geq x)\leq{\mathbb{P}}(Y\geq x)
. We may also write stochastic domination with the laws of the random variables instead of the variables themselves.

Lemma 5.2
.

Let
n,p,q
be integers such that
1\leq p\leq n
and
1\leq q\leq n/2
. Then
[p]\cap S_{n,q}(1)\preceq\Bin(q,2p/n)
.

Proof.

By definition,
S_{n,q}(1)
is a random subset of
[n]
with
q
elements. This can be viewed as labelling
n
balls
1
through
n
, and picking
q
of them, one by one and without replacement. At the
i
-th draw (where
1\leq i\leq q
), the probability of picking a ball whose label is in
[p]
is at most
p/(n-(i-1))\leq 2p/n
, and this bound holds independently of whether the previously picked balls were in
[p]
.
Therefore,
[p]\cap S_{n,q}(1)
is stochastically dominated by a sum of
q
independent Bernoulli variables of parameter
2p/n
, that is, by a
\Bin(q,2p/n)
random variable.
∎

This allows us to stochastically dominate
M_{n,n,q}^{(2)}(t)
by a binomial law.

Lemma 5.3
.

Let
n,q,t\geq 1
be integers such that
qt\leq n/2
. Let
X\sim\Bin(qt,2qt/n)
. Then
M^{(2)}_{n,n,q}(t)\preceq X
.

Proof.

Consider again
n
balls, labelled
1
through
n
. Let
m\geq 1
be an integer. Set
X_{i}=N_{n,n,q}(i)\backslash N^{(2)}_{n,n,q}(i)
and
x_{i}=|X_{i}|
for
1\leq i\leq t
. By definition, recalling that we use the symbol
\sqcup
to emphasize that a union is disjoint, we have

M^{(2)}_{n,n,q}(t)=\left|N^{(2)}_{n,n,q}(t)\right|=\left|\bigsqcup_{i=2}^{t}N^{(2)}_{n,n,q}(i)\backslash N^{(2)}_{n,n,q}(i-1)\right|=\sum_{i=2}^{t}\left|X_{i-1}\cap S_{n,q}(i)\right|

(5.8)

Since for each
2\leq i\leq t
,
S_{n,q}(i)
is independent of
(S_{n,q}(j))_{1\leq j\leq i-1}
, and
|X_{i}|\leq\left|N_{n,n,q}(i)\right|\leq qt
, the variables
\left|\left[qt\right]\cap S_{n,q}(i)\right|
for
1\leq i\leq t
are independent, and we have

M^{(2)}_{n,n,q}(t)\preceq\sum_{i=1}^{t}\left|\left[qt\right]\cap S_{n,q}(i)\right|.

(5.9)

Moreover,
\left|\left[qt\right]\cap S_{n,q}(i)\right|\preceq\Bin(q,2qt/n)
for each
1\leq i\leq t
by Lemma
5.2
.
We conclude that
M^{(2)}_{n,n,q}(t)
is stochastically dominated by a sum of
t
independent
\Bin(q,2qt/n)
random variables, i.e. by a
\Bin(qt,2qt/n)
random variable.
∎

Lemma 5.4
.

Let
n,k,t
be integers such that
2\leq k\leq n
,
t\geq 2
, and
kt\leq n/2
. Let
{\mathcal{C}}
be a conjugacy class of
{\mathfrak{S}}_{n}
with support
k
. Let
Z\sim\Unif_{{\mathcal{C}}}^{*t}
and recall that
\supp(Z)
denotes the support size of
Z
. Then
kt-\supp(Z)\preceq\Bin(qt,2qt/n)
.

Proof.

First, by definition,
Z\sim\sigma_{1}\cdots\sigma_{t}
, where
\sigma_{1},\ldots,\sigma_{t}
are i.i.d. uniform permutations in
{\mathcal{C}}
.
Moreover, the elements in the support of the product permutation
Z
consists at least of the integers that are in the support of exactly one
\sigma_{i}
, whose distribution is that of
kt-M^{(2)}_{n,n,k}(t)
.
Hence
\supp(Z)\succeq kt-M^{(2)}_{n,n,k}(t)
, which is equivalent to
kt-\supp(Z)\preceq M^{(2)}_{n,n,k}(t)
. Moreover
M^{(2)}_{n,n,k}(t)\preceq\Bin(qt,2qt/n)
by Lemma
5.3
. We conclude that
kt-\supp(Z)\preceq\Bin(qt,2qt/n)
.
∎

5.3
Concentration of support

We start by establishing a bound on the tails of binomial random variables.

Lemma 5.5
.

Let
0<\varepsilon,\alpha<1/2
, and
A\geq 2
such that
\alpha\leq\frac{\varepsilon}{e}e^{-A}
. Let
n\geq 1/\varepsilon
be an integer and
X\sim\Bin(n,\alpha)
. Then

{\mathbb{P}}(X\geq\varepsilon n)\leq e^{-(\varepsilon A/2)n}.

(5.10)

Proof.

For each
j\geq\left\lceil\varepsilon n\right\rceil
we have
\binom{n}{j}\alpha^{j}\leq(en/j)^{j}\alpha^{j}\leq(e\alpha/\varepsilon)^{j}\leq e^{-jA}
.
It follows that

{\mathbb{P}}(X\geq\varepsilon n)=\sum_{j\geq\left\lceil\varepsilon n\right\rceil}\binom{n}{j}\alpha^{j}(1-\alpha)^{n-j}\leq\sum_{j\geq\left\lceil\varepsilon n\right\rceil}\binom{n}{j}\alpha^{j}\leq\sum_{j\geq\left\lceil\varepsilon n\right\rceil}e^{-jA}=\frac{e^{-\left\lceil\varepsilon n\right\rceil A}}{1-e^{-A}},

(5.11)

and we conclude, since
e^{-A}\leq 1/2
and
e^{-\varepsilon(A/2)n}\leq e^{-\varepsilon n}\leq e^{-1}\leq 1/2
, that

{\mathbb{P}}(X\geq\varepsilon n)\leq 2e^{-\left\lceil\varepsilon n\right\rceil A}\leq 2e^{-\varepsilon An}\leq e^{-(\varepsilon A/2)n}.\qed

(5.12)

We now combine this with the multi-coupon collector arguments to show that the support of a product of
t
invariant permutations in
{\mathfrak{S}}_{n}
with support
k
is close to
kt
, as long as
kt
is small. The scales we consider are the same as for the curvature argument of
[
BŞ19
]
. We emphasize that here
t
is of order order
n/k
, which is not the same as the mixing time, which is close to
\frac{n}{k}\ln n
.

Lemma 5.6
.

Let
0<\varepsilon,\alpha<1/2
, and
A\geq 2
such that
\alpha\leq\frac{\varepsilon}{e}e^{-A}
. Let
n,k,t
be integers such that
2\leq k\leq n
,
t\geq 2
, and
kt\leq\alpha n/2
. Let
{\mathcal{C}}
be a conjugacy class of
{\mathfrak{S}}_{n}
with support
k
. Let
Z\sim\Unif_{{\mathcal{C}}}^{*t}
. Set
y=\varepsilon kt
.
Then

{\mathbb{P}}(\supp(Z)\leq kt-y)=1-{\mathbb{P}}(kt-y<\supp(Z)\leq kt)\leq e^{-(\varepsilon A/2)kt}.

(5.13)

Proof.

We always have
\supp(Z)\leq kt
so the equality holds.
By Lemma
5.4
and since
2kt/n\leq\alpha
by assumption, we have

\supp(Z)\preceq\Bin(kt,2kt/n)\preceq\Bin(kt,\alpha).

(5.14)

Combining this with Lemma
5.5
, we finally get

{\mathbb{P}}(kt-\supp(Z)\geq y)\leq{\mathbb{P}}(X\geq y)\leq e^{-(\varepsilon A/2)kt}.\qed

(5.15)

5.4
Bootstrapped bounds for high-level representations

The following proposition extends the support condition
n\lesssim k\leq(1-\delta)n
from Proposition
3.13
, to all support sizes
2\leq k\leq(1-\delta)n
.

Proposition 5.7
.

Let
\delta>0
,
\rho>0
, and
\varepsilon_{0}>0
. There exists
n_{0}\geq 1
such that the following holds. Let
n\geq n_{0}
,
k
be an integer such that
2\leq k\leq(1-\delta)n
,
{\mathcal{C}}
be a conjugacy class of
{\mathfrak{S}}_{n}
with support
k
, and
\lambda\vdash n
satisfying
\lambda_{1}^{\prime}\leq\lambda_{1}\leq(1-\rho)n
.
Then, setting
f:=n-k
,

d_{\lambda}\left|\chi^{\lambda}({\mathcal{C}})\right|^{\left(\frac{1}{2-\varepsilon_{0}}\right)\frac{\ln n}{\ln(n/f)}}\leq 1,\quad\text{ or equivalently }\quad\left|\chi^{\lambda}({\mathcal{C}})\right|\leq d_{\lambda}^{-(2-\varepsilon_{0})\frac{\ln(n/f)}{\ln n}}.

(5.16)

Proof.

Without loss of generality we may assume that
\varepsilon_{0}\leq 1/1000
, and that
1/\varepsilon_{0}
is an integer. Set
\varepsilon=\varepsilon_{0}/12
, and let
q=1/\varepsilon
, which is an integer. If
n/q^{2}\leq k\leq(1-\delta)n
, then by Proposition
3.13
, for
n
large enough we have

d_{\lambda}\left|\chi^{\lambda}({\mathcal{C}})\right|^{\left(\frac{1}{2-\varepsilon}\right)\frac{\ln n}{\ln(n/f)}}\leq 1,

(5.17)

and in particular this holds for
n/q^{2}\leq k\leq(q+1)n/q^{2}
.

Assume from now on that
k\leq n/q^{2}
, and set
t=\left\lceil\frac{n}{kq}\right\rceil
. Since
\frac{n}{kq^{2}}\geq 1
, we have
\frac{1}{q}\frac{n}{k}\leq t\leq\frac{q+1}{q^{2}}\frac{n}{k}
. Consider the event
B=\left\{\sigma\in{\mathfrak{S}}_{n}{\;:\;}kt(1-1/q)\leq\supp(\sigma)\leq kt\right\}
, and let
\sigma\in B
. Then we have

\frac{f_{1}(\sigma)}{n}=1-\frac{\supp(\sigma)}{n}\leq 1-\left(1-\frac{1}{q}\right)\frac{kt}{n}\leq 1-\left(1-\frac{1}{q}\right)\frac{1}{q}\leq e^{-1/q+1/q^{2}}.

(5.18)

Moreover,

1-\frac{kt}{n}\geq 1-\frac{q+1}{q^{2}}=1-\frac{1}{q}-\frac{1}{q^{2}}\geq e^{-1/q-2/q^{2}},

(5.19)

where we used that
q
is large for the last inequality. It follows that

\frac{\ln(n/f_{1}(\sigma))}{\ln(n/(n-kt))}\geq\frac{\ln(e^{1/q-1/q^{2}})}{\ln(e^{1/q+2/q^{2}})}=\frac{1-1/q}{1+2/q}\geq 1-\frac{4}{q}=1-4\varepsilon.

(5.20)

Recall that
kt\in[n/q,(q+1)n/q^{2}]
. Then applying (
5.17
) we obtain that for any
\sigma\in B
we have, setting
z=1-kt/n
,

\left|\chi^{\lambda}(\sigma)\right|\leq d_{\lambda}^{-(2-\varepsilon)\frac{\ln(n/f_{1}(\sigma))}{\ln n}}\leq d_{\lambda}^{-\left(\frac{2-10\varepsilon}{1-4\varepsilon}\right)\frac{\ln(n/f_{1}(\sigma))}{\ln n}}\leq d_{\lambda}^{-\left(2-10\varepsilon\right)\frac{\ln(1/z)}{\ln n}}

(5.21)

Applying Lemma
5.6
with
A=20q
and
\alpha=\varepsilon e^{-1-A}
, and recalling that
kt/q=\varepsilon kt
, we then get

\Unif_{{\mathcal{C}}}^{*t}(B^{c})\leq e^{-(\varepsilon A/2)kt}=e^{-10kt}\leq e^{-5n\ln(1/z)}.

(5.22)

Rewriting
e^{5n}
as
(n^{n/2})^{10/\ln n}
, we obtain
\Unif_{{\mathcal{C}}}^{*t}(B^{c})\leq(n^{n/2})^{-10\frac{\ln(1/z)}{\ln n}}\leq d_{\lambda}^{-10\frac{\ln(1/z)}{\ln n}}
.
Moreover, we have
\tfrac{\ln(1/z)}{\ln n}\asymp\tfrac{1}{\ln n}
, and
d_{\lambda}=e^{\Omega_{+}(n)}=n^{\omega_{+}(1)}
by assumption, so
d_{\lambda}^{\frac{\ln(1/z)}{\ln n}}=o(1)
. It follows from Lemma
5.1
that for
n
large enough

|\chi^{\lambda}({\mathcal{C}})|^{t}\leq d_{\lambda}^{-10\frac{\ln(1/z)}{\ln n}}+d_{\lambda}^{-(2-10\varepsilon)\frac{\ln(1/z)}{\ln n}}\leq d_{\lambda}^{-(2-12\varepsilon)\frac{\ln(1/z)}{\ln n}}=d_{\lambda}^{-(2-\varepsilon_{0})\frac{\ln(1/z)}{\ln n}},

(5.23)

and therefore, since
-\ln(1/z)=\ln(1-kt/n)\leq t\ln(1-k/n)=t\ln(f/n)=-t\ln(n/f)
(using that
t\geq 1
), we conclude that

|\chi^{\lambda}({\mathcal{C}})|\leq d_{\lambda}^{-\frac{1}{t}(2-\varepsilon_{0})\frac{\ln(1/z)}{\ln n}}\leq d_{\lambda}^{-(2-\varepsilon_{0})\frac{\ln(n/f)}{\ln n}}.\qed

5.5
Unified character bounds

We now have all the tools to prove our main character bounds. In this section we prove a stronger version of Theorem
1.2
, and then deduce Theorems
1.1
and
1.2
from it.
Recall that the (symmetrized) level of a diagram
\lambda
is defined as
r_{\textup{sym}}(\lambda)=n-\max(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{1}^{\prime})
, and that if
\lambda_{1}\geq\lambda_{1}^{\prime}
, then
r_{\textup{sym}}(\lambda)=n-\lambda_{1}
.
We recall that
\zeta(r)=\ln\max(1,r^{1/8})
for
r\geq 0
.

Theorem 5.8
.

Let
\delta>0
. There exists
n_{0}\geq 2
such that for every
n\geq n_{0}
,
\lambda\in\widehat{{\mathfrak{S}}_{n}}
, and
\sigma\in{\mathfrak{S}}_{n}
such that
f(\sigma)\geq\delta n
, we have

\left|\chi^{\lambda}(\sigma)\right|\leq d_{\lambda}^{-\frac{\ln(n/f(\sigma))}{\ln n}\left(1+\frac{\zeta(r_{\textup{sym}}(\lambda))}{\ln n}\right)}.

(5.25)

Proof.

Let
n
be a large integer,
\lambda\vdash n
, and
\sigma\in{\mathfrak{S}}_{n}
. By symmetry (Lemma
2.18
) we may assume that
\lambda_{1}^{\prime}\leq\lambda_{1}
, and set
r:=r_{\textup{sym}}(\lambda)=n-\lambda_{1}
. Let
c_{0}
be the constant from Proposition
4.11
. If
r\leq c_{0}\delta^{2}n
, then the result holds by Proposition
4.11
.
Assume on the other hand that
r>c_{0}\delta^{2}n
. Set
\rho=c_{0}\delta^{2}
and
\varepsilon_{0}=7/8
. Then since
1+\frac{\zeta(r)}{\ln n}\leq 1+1/8=2-\varepsilon_{0}
, the result holds by Proposition
5.7
. This concludes the proof.
∎

Proof of Theorem
1.2
.

Theorem
1.2
follows from Theorem
5.8
, since
\zeta(r)\geq 0
for any
r\geq 0
.
∎

Proof of Theorem
1.1
.

Recall that if
\sigma
is a permutation
f_{1}(\sigma)
denotes its number of fixed points and
f(\sigma)=\max(f_{1}(\sigma),1)
.
By
[
TT24
, Example 1.4 (b) and (c)]
, for
\sigma\in{\mathfrak{S}}_{n}
we have

E(\sigma)-1\leq-\frac{1}{2}\frac{\ln(n/f(\sigma))}{\ln n}.

(5.26)

It follows from Lemma
2.13
that for all
n\geq 2
,
\sigma\in{\mathfrak{S}}_{n}
, and
\lambda\in\widehat{{\mathfrak{S}}_{n}}
, we have

\left|\chi^{\lambda}(\sigma)\right|\leq d_{\lambda}^{E(\sigma)-1+\frac{C_{\textup{virt}}}{\ln n}}\leq d_{\lambda}^{-\frac{1}{2}\frac{\ln(n/f(\sigma))}{\ln n}+\frac{C_{\textup{virt}}}{\ln n}}=d_{\lambda}^{-\frac{1}{2}\frac{\ln(n/f(\sigma))}{\ln n}\left(1-\frac{2C_{\textup{virt}}}{\ln(n/f(\sigma))}\right)}.

(5.27)

Let
\varepsilon>0
. Let
\delta>0
such that
2C_{\textup{virt}}/\ln(1/\delta)\leq\varepsilon
.
Assume that
n
is large, and let
\lambda\vdash n
.
Then applying (
5.27
) to permutations with at most

fixed points and Theorem
1.2
to permutations with at least

, we obtain that for any
\sigma\in{\mathfrak{S}}_{n}
we have

\left|\chi^{\lambda}(\sigma)\right|\leq d_{\lambda}^{-\frac{1-\varepsilon}{2}\frac{\ln(n/f(\sigma))}{\ln n}}.

(5.28)

Since this holds for any

and all

6
Cutoff for conjugacy invariant random walks

6.1
A classical technique

Converting character bounds into mixing time estimates dates back to Diaconis and Shahshahani
[
DS81
]
. The idea consists of first bounding the total variation distance, which we recall is half of the
L^{1}
distance, by the
L^{2}
distance, and then applying the Plancherel identity to rewrite the
L^{2}
distance in terms of characters and dimensions of irreducible representations. This gives a correspondence between eigenvalues and characters, and between multiplicities and squares of dimensions of irreducible representations.
This way of bounding the total variation distance is often referred to as the Diaconis–Shahshahani upper bound lemma, see also
[
Dia88
, Lemma 3.B.1 on page 24]
. Set
\widehat{\mathfrak{S}_{n}}^{**}=\widehat{\mathfrak{S}_{n}}\backslash\left\{[n],[1^{n}]\right\}
. For conjugacy classes
{\mathcal{C}}
of
{\mathfrak{S}}_{n}
, the Diaconis–Shahshahani upper bound lemma can be adapted to the coset distance to stationarity, see for instance
[
Hou16
, Section 2]
and Remark
6.1
regarding the additional constant
1/2
:

4\dtv({\mathcal{C}},t)^{2}\leq\textup{d}_{L^{2}}({\mathcal{C}},t)^{2}=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\lambda\in\widehat{\mathfrak{S}_{n}}^{**}}d_{\lambda}^{2}\left|\chi^{\lambda}({\mathcal{C}})\right|^{2t}.

(6.1)

Remark 6.1
.

In this paper we defined the coset distances in (
1.4
) and (
1.5
) with respect to the set
{\mathfrak{E}}_{n}({\mathcal{C}},t)
rather than
{\mathfrak{S}}_{n}
.
It enables not losing an extra factor
\sqrt{2}
when applying the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality to bound the
L^{1}
distance (which is twice the total variation distance) by the
L^{2}
distance, and having the same coset
L^{2}
profile as the lazy
L^{2}
profile, at least for conjugacy classes with small support.

A second important bound is the following. Liebeck and Shalev
[
LS04
]
showed that, for every
s>0
, as
n\to\infty
:

\sum_{\lambda\in\widehat{\mathfrak{S}_{n}}^{**}}d_{\lambda}^{-s}=O\left(n^{-s}\right).

(6.2)

Sums in the form of the left hand side of (
6.2
) are referred to as
Witten zeta functions
, in reference to the work of Witten
[
Wit91
]
. Liebeck and Shalev
[
LS05
]
also proved bounds on the Witten zeta functions of other groups.

Exponential character bounds are particularly convenient for applications. Informally, plugging a bound of the form
\left|\chi^{\lambda}({\mathcal{C}})\right|\leq d_{\lambda}^{1/t_{0}}
into (
6.1
) implies, using (
6.2
), that
\dtv({\mathcal{C}},t)=o(1)
for
t
a little larger than
t_{0}
. This is exactly how we will deduce Theorem
1.3
from Theorem
1.2
.

6.2
Cutoff for conjugacy classes

We now prove Theorem
1.3
.

Proof of Theorem
1.3
.

Let
0<\varepsilon<1
. Let
n
be a large integer and
{\mathcal{C}}\in\Conj^{*}({\mathfrak{S}}_{n})
such that
f({\mathcal{C}})\geq\delta n
.

Let us first prove the upper bounds. Set
t=\left\lceil(1+\varepsilon)t_{{\mathcal{C}}}\right\rceil
. Since the
L^{2}
distance is larger than the total variation distance, it is enough to upper bound the
L^{2}
distance, and hence the right hand side of (
6.1
).
By Theorem
1.2
and since
t\geq(1+\varepsilon)\frac{\ln n}{\ln(n/f({\mathcal{C}}))}
, we have

2\textup{d}_{L^{2}}({\mathcal{C}},t)=\sum_{\lambda\in\widehat{\mathfrak{S}_{n}}^{**}}d_{\lambda}^{2}\left|\chi^{\lambda}({\mathcal{C}})\right|^{2t}\leq\sum_{\lambda\in\widehat{\mathfrak{S}_{n}}^{**}}d_{\lambda}^{2}\left(d_{\lambda}^{\frac{\ln(n/f({\mathcal{C}}))}{\ln n}}\right)^{2(1+\varepsilon)\frac{\ln n}{\ln(n/f({\mathcal{C}}))}}=\sum_{\lambda\in\widehat{\mathfrak{S}_{n}}^{**}}d_{\lambda}^{-2\varepsilon}.

(6.3)

By the Liebeck–Shalev bounds on the Witten zeta function, recalled as (
6.2
), we conclude that
\textup{d}_{L^{2}}({\mathcal{C}},t)=O\left(n^{-2\varepsilon}\right)=o(1)
.

Let us now prove the lower bounds. Set
t=\left\lfloor(1-\varepsilon)t_{{\mathcal{C}}}\right\rfloor
and let
X_{t}\sim\Unif_{{\mathcal{C}}}^{*t}
. The number of fixed points of a uniform permutation in
{\mathfrak{E}}({\mathcal{C}},t)
is asymptotically
\Poiss(1)
, and is therefore
<n^{\varepsilon/3}
with probability
1-o(1)
. On the other hand, by classical coupon collector arguments,
X_{t}
has
\geq n^{\varepsilon/3}
fixed points with probability
1-o(1)
. It follows that
\dtv({\mathcal{C}},t)=1-o(1)
.
For the
L^{2}
lower bound, it suffices to consider the representation
\nu:=[n-1,1]
. We have
\left|\chi^{\nu}({\mathcal{C}})\right|=\frac{f({\mathcal{C}})-1}{n-1}\geq\frac{f({\mathcal{C}})-1}{n}
. Since
n
is large we have
\left|\chi^{\nu}({\mathcal{C}})\right|\geq(f({\mathcal{C}})/n)^{(1-\varepsilon/2)/(1-\varepsilon)}
since
f({\mathcal{C}})\to\infty
, and
d_{\nu}^{2}\geq n^{2}/2
since
d_{\nu}\sim n
, and therefore as
n\to\infty
,

2\textup{d}_{L^{2}}({\mathcal{C}},t)\geq d_{\nu}^{2}\left|\chi^{\nu}({\mathcal{C}})\right|^{2t}\geq d_{\nu}^{2}\left(\frac{f({\mathcal{C}})-1}{n}\right)^{2(1-\varepsilon)\frac{\ln n}{\ln(n/f({\mathcal{C}}))}}\geq\frac{n^{2}}{2}n^{-2+\varepsilon}=\frac{n^{\varepsilon}}{2}\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{}\infty.\qed

(6.4)

6.3
An improved upper bound

Since there is no error term in Theorem
1.2
, we can use the refined bounds on the Witten zeta function from
[
TT24
]
to obtain more precise mixing time bounds within the cutoff
window
. We write this in a similar form to
[
SC04
, Conjecture 9.3]
.

Proposition 6.1
.

Let
\delta>0
, and let
n_{0}=n_{0}(\delta)
as in Theorem
1.2
. There exists a universal constant
A
such that the following holds. Let
n\geq n_{0}
, let
{\mathcal{C}}\neq\left\{\Id\right\}
be a conjugacy class of
{\mathfrak{S}}_{n}
with at least
\delta n
fixed points, and let
a>0
such that
t:=\frac{\ln n}{\ln(n/f({\mathcal{C}}))}\left(1+\frac{a}{\ln(n-1)}\right)
is an integer. Then

2\dtv({\mathcal{C}},t)\leq\textup{d}_{L^{2}}({\mathcal{C}},t)\leq e^{-a}+Ae^{-3a/2}.

(6.5)

Proof.

The first inequality always holds by the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality. By the Diaconis–Shahshahani upper bound lemma (recalled as (
6.1
)) and Theorem
1.2
, we have

2\textup{d}_{L^{2}}({\mathcal{C}},t)^{2}=\sum_{\lambda\in\widehat{\mathfrak{S}_{n}}^{**}}d_{\lambda}^{2}\left(d_{\lambda}^{-\frac{\ln(n/f)}{\ln n}}\right)^{2t}=\sum_{\lambda\in\widehat{\mathfrak{S}_{n}}^{**}}d_{\lambda}^{-s_{n}},

(6.6)

where
s_{n}=2a/\ln(n-1)
. For level 1 representations we have
(d_{[n-1,1]})^{-s_{n}}=(d_{[2,1^{n-2}]})^{-s_{n}}=(n-1)^{-2a/\ln(n-1)}=e^{-2a}
.
Set
\Lambda=\left\{\lambda\in\widehat{\mathfrak{S}_{n}}^{**}{\;:\;}2\leq r_{\textup{sym}}(\lambda)\leq 18\right\}
. Then by symmetry
|\Lambda|/2\leq A_{0}:=\sum_{i=2}^{18}p(i)
. Also, for each
\lambda\in\Lambda
we have
d_{\lambda}\geq n(n-2)/2\geq(n-1)^{3/2}
(since
n
is large), so
d_{\lambda}^{-s_{n}}\leq e^{-3a}
. Therefore
\sum_{\lambda\in\Lambda}d_{\lambda}^{-s_{n}}\leq 2A_{0}e^{-3a}
.
On the other hand, without loss of generality (up to increasing the value of
A
) we may assume that
2a\geq 12\ln 2
, so by
[
TT24
, Proposition 6.1]
we have
\sum_{\lambda\in\widehat{\mathfrak{S}_{n}}^{**}{\;:\;}r_{\textup{sym}}(\lambda)>18}d_{\lambda}^{-s_{n}}\leq 2e^{-3a}
. Combining these two bounds and plugging them into (
6.6
), we obtain
2\textup{d}_{L^{2}}({\mathcal{C}},t)^{2}\leq 2e^{-2a}+2\left(A_{0}+1\right)e^{-3a}
. We conclude that

\textup{d}_{L^{2}}({\mathcal{C}},t)\leq\sqrt{e^{2a}+\left(A_{0}+1\right)e^{3a}}\leq e^{-a}+(A_{0}+1)^{1/2}e^{-3a/2}.\qed

(6.7)
