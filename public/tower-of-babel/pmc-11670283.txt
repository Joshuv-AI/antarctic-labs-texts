A cry for kelp: Evidence for polyphenolic inhibition of Oxford Nanopore sequencing of brown algae
Authors: William S Pearman, Vanessa Arranz, Jose I Carvajal, Annabel Whibley, Yusmiati Liau, Katherine Johnson, Rachel Gray, Jackson M Treece, Neil J Gemmell, Libby Liggins, Ceridwen I Fraser, Evelyn L Jensen, Nicholas J Green
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractGenomic resources have yielded unprecedented insights into ecological and evolutionary processes, not to mention their importance in economic and conservation management of specific organisms. However, the field of macroalgal genomics is hampered by difficulties in the isolation of suitable DNA. Even when DNA that appears high quality by standard metrics has been isolated, such samples may not perform well during the sequencing process. We here have compared Oxford Nanopore long‐read sequencing results for three species of macroalgae to those of nonmacroalgal species and determined that when using macroalgal samples, sequencing activity declined rapidly, resulting in reduced sequencing yield. Chemical analysis of macroalgal DNA that would be considered suitable for sequencing revealed that DNA derived from dried macroalgae was enriched for polyphenol–DNA adducts (DNA with large polyphenols chemically attached to it), which may have led to sequencing inhibition. Of note, we observed the strongest evidence of sequencing inhibition and reduced sequence output when using samples dried using silica gel—suggesting that such storage approaches may not be appropriate for samples destined for Oxford Nanopore sequencing. Our findings have wide‐ranging implications for the generation of genomic resources from macroalgae and suggest a need to develop new storage methods that are more amenable to Oxford Nanopore sequencing or to use fresh flash‐frozen tissue wherever possible for genome sequencing.Keywords: DNA extraction, genome, macroalgae, nanopore, polyphenols, sequencing

INTRODUCTION

Contaminants in algal and plant DNA extractions have long been a source of frustration for molecular biologists (Hoarau et al., 2007; Ramakrishnan et al., 2017; Wilson et al., 2016). As whole‐genome sequencing (WGS) has increasingly replaced more traditional genetic approaches, there has been a corresponding need for a shift from extraction methods that yield DNA suitable for polymerase chain reaction (PCR) to ones providing DNA suitable for WGS (De La Cerda et al., 2023; Jones et al., 2021). Long‐read sequencing approaches, which sequence larger pieces of DNA (rather than short‐read approaches, which typically focus on smaller fragments), are increasingly important for facilitating the creation of high‐quality genomic resources, as they provide a scaffold for shorter DNA sequences. However, one substantial drawback of long‐read sequencing is its sensitivity to DNA quality (Pinzauti et al., 2022).

The two dominant long‐read sequencing tools are Pacific Biosciences (hereafter PacBio) and Oxford Nanopore Technologies sequencing (hereafter Oxford Nanopore sequencing), the latter of which is often more accessible. Oxford Nanopore sequencing functions by passing a strand of DNA through a nanopore embedded in a membrane across which electrical current is measured. Changes in the electrical current as the DNA passes through the pore are measured and can then be converted into the associated DNA sequence. For an accessible overview of the dominant sequencing technologies and how they work (including PacBio and Oxford Nanopore sequencing), refer to Slatko et al. (2018).

The Oxford Nanopore sequencing approach means that the raw, or native, unamplified DNA is directly utilized, meaning chemicals that attach to the DNA (so‐called DNA adducts) may inhibit sequencing by blocking the nanopore. Because the nanopores for sequencing have multiple strands pass through them over time, a damaged pore can result in lower sequencing output. Thus, despite its popularity, Oxford Nanopore sequencing may require higher quality DNA than alternative sequencing approaches. DNA that is both high molecular weight (i.e., long strands of DNA) and of high quality (as judged by UV absorbance ratios, concentration, and electrophoresis) is therefore recommended for Oxford Nanopore sequencing. Ultraviolet (UV) absorbance ratios of 260/280 and 260/230 can be used to measure the purity of DNA. Electrophoresis is used to separate DNA by size across a physical gel, where shorter DNA can migrate a greater distance along a gel. Both UV absorbance and electrophoresis are routine laboratory procedures to verify DNA quality. For comprehensive explanations of DNA quality assessment both generally and within the context of Nanopore sequencing, refer to Quick and Loman (2018).

Many researchers globally, including the three separate laboratories involved in this research, have had consistent difficulties in generating Oxford Nanopore sequencing data from various species of brown algae (Ecklonia radiata, Durvillaea antarctica, Eisenia cockeri), despite high‐quality DNA input, as measured by UV absorbance ratios, electrophoresis (e.g., Agilent Tape Station or gel electrophoresis), and concentrations. Although much focus has been on removing polysaccharide contaminants such as alginates (e.g., Wilson et al., 2016), which often reduce DNA yields, another well‐known source of sequencing inhibition is the presence of polyphenols. These compounds can oxidize and lead to the formation of DNA–quinone adducts prior to and during DNA extraction (Heikrujam et al., 2020). These adducts may be at such low levels that they are not reliably detected using traditional quality metrics but may still cause problems in Oxford Nanopore WGS. Furthermore, polyphenols can share UV absorbance spectra with DNA, which can complicate efforts to measure DNA quality (Li et al., 2013).

We noted during numerous sequencing attempts undertaken over several years that our Oxford Nanopore libraries constructed using brown algal DNA consistently and quickly blocked pores on the Oxford Nanopore flowcells (the “single use” component that the sequencing is conducted on). Additionally, the average lengths of our sequenced reads were consistently much shorter than the average lengths recorded in quality assessments of our input libraries. We hypothesized that despite employing conventional techniques to suppress such adduct formation during DNA extraction, polyphenol–DNA adducts within our samples could explain the rapid pore blockage that we observed (De La Cerda et al., 2023; Mgwatyu et al., 2022).

Macroalgae are well known for having a high abundance of polyphenols (up to 20% of dry weight; Aminina et al., 2020); these can oxidize into ortho‐quinones (o‐quinones), many of which are capable of irreversibly covalently binding to DNA (Heikrujam et al., 2020). Our DNA extractions were conducted using methods optimized to minimize generation and interference by polyphenols, such as the inclusion of high levels of antioxidants (e.g., sodium metabisulphite or beta‐mercaptoethanol) and polyphenol sequestering compound polyvinyl‐pyrrolidone (PVP; Heikrujam et al., 2020). By inhibiting polyphenol oxidation and concurrently sequestering the polyphenols with PVP, the hope was that polyphenols could be removed from the samples during DNA extraction without binding to the DNA (Heikrujam et al., 2020; Nath et al., 2022). Nevertheless, if polyphenol–DNA adduct formation occurs prior to DNA purification (e.g., the DNA is natively modified within the cell or during storage of collected specimens), then such optimized protocols may be for nought.

In this study, we employed both bioinformatic and chemistry approaches to test the hypothesis that polyphenol‐derived DNA adducts may interfere with Oxford Nanopore DNA sequencing in brown algae, despite using DNA extraction methods aimed to prevent this. First, because of hypothesized polyphenol–DNA adduct formation, we expected that macroalgae DNA would tend to promote pore blockages and thus lead to greater pore inactivity than nonmacroalgal DNA samples. Second, we hypothesized that chemical characterization of the DNA extracts would reveal the presence of DNA–polyphenol adducts. Finally, given our theory that polyphenol–DNA adducts can form prior to extraction, we also expected that DNA derived from silica‐dried macroalgae would have higher abundances of these modified bases than fresh or flash‐frozen tissue due to cell rupture during storage and/or desiccation. During the drying process, cell rupture may lead to prolonged exposure of DNA to the extranuclear cell contents and air and, in turn, increase the likelihood of polyphenol oxidation and subsequent binding to DNA.

The storage and extraction approaches for the brown algal samples used for DNA sequencing were chosen based on the different approaches used by each contributing lab group rather than in any systematic manner, to explore the effects of storage. Among the outputs of Nanopore sequencing are specific details relating to each sequence that was generated, such as the time spent navigating the pore, the changes in the current during sequencing, and importantly, the reason each strand stopped sequencing. We compared the frequencies of terminal bases as A/G or T/C for each species for which we had sequence data and compared these to the reasons for sequence termination (whether the pore became blocked or the sequencing of a strand was completed). Sequencing data were available from three species of macroalgae and from the nonalgal taxa Vitis vinifera (grape), Drosophila melanogaster (fruit flies), and Homo sapiens (humans). Abbreviation: LC–MS, liquid chromatography–mass spectrometry.

DNA samples that were analyzed using liquid chromatography‐mass spectrometry (LC–MS) were extracted by a recently developed protocol optimized for macroalgal DNA extraction (Pearman et al., 2024) and then processed for LC–MS using a formic acid hydrolysis protocol detailed in Appendix S1. Liquid chromatography‐mass spectrometry separates and identifies the chemical composition of a sample and is a well‐established method in “adductomics” and the study of polyphenols (Balbo et al., 2014). DNA was extracted from single samples of ~200 mg of chicken breast muscle tissue or ~100 mg of silica‐dried Durvillaea antarctica tissue (stored for 1 month on silica at room temperature). Simultaneously, DNA was also extracted from ~200 mg of flash‐frozen fresh D. antarctica, frozen with liquid nitrogen, and ground to a fine powder. The LC–MS analysis also included a purchased salmon sperm DNA control (sonicated salmon sperm DNA—Agilent #201190).

To ascertain the presence of polyphenol adducts in our DNA samples, we calculated the predicted mass‐to‐charge (m/z) ratio distributions for polyphenol‐derived DNA adducts that would be expected after DNA hydrolysis (in essence, the predicted masses of each adduct—as mass spectrometry will measure the m/z for each ion in a sample). These were produced by summing the masses of each nucleobase and each positive polyphenolic ion previously observed in Durvillaea antarctica (Olate‐Gallegos et al., 2019) then deducting two to account for the oxidation. Although crude, this approach was intended to give a rough estimation of the distribution of adduct sizes.

First, we observed that macroalgal sequencing runs typically declined quickly in the number of pores available for sequencing, while nonmacroalgal sequencing had greater preservation of pores throughout the sequencing run (Figure 1a). One notable exception was sequencing of RNAlater‐stored Eisenia tissue, which had pore preservation closer in line with nonmacroalgal samples. Second, we observed reads that terminated sequencing due to blocked pores were more likely to terminate at a purine (an A or G; Figure 1b), while “complete” reads (reads that successfully navigated the pore in their entirety) were far more likely to terminate at pyrimidines (T or C). These purine/pyrimidine biases were typical across most sample types except for the human data set.

RESULTS

We observed the presence of four major peaks by UV at 280 nm, which aligned with the position of the extracted ion chromatograms of the DNA bases cytosine, adenine, guanine, and thymine (Figure 2a,b). Examination of the total ion chromatogram (TIC) of negatively ionized samples revealed no differences between any samples; however, the TIC of positively ionized samples revealed a singular major difference between the DNA from the silica‐dried algal sample and all others—an extremely large peak at 29.5 min (Figure 2c). Analysis of the ion composition of our data between 29 and 30 min revealed this peak was dominated by high molecular weight products aligning with the hypothesized distribution of polyphenol‐derived DNA base adducts (Figure 2d). A similar distribution was also observed in the flash‐frozen algal DNA over this retention time; however, that distribution was substantially smaller than in the silica‐dried algal DNA and was not apparent on the TIC (Figure 2e). Such distributions were not apparent for chicken DNA extracted in the same way nor for the salmon DNA control.

DISCUSSION

Despite increasing interest in the production of genomic resources for brown algae, there are many difficulties in producing high‐quality Oxford Nanopore sequencing data. Given the well‐established role of polyphenols in interfering with DNA extractions (Hoarau et al., 2007; Jones et al., 2021) and the known high abundance of polyphenolic compounds in macroalgae (Aminina et al., 2020), we suggest that polyphenol–DNA adduct formation, dependent on sample processing methods, may underlie problematic sequencing results. These polyphenol–DNA adducts form when polyphenols, which are abundant in algae, are oxidized and become reactive enough to spontaneously and chemically attach to bases of a DNA strand. First, we suggest that based on the available Oxford Nanopore data and additional LC–MS data generated, DNA–ortho‐quinone adducts can form during sample drying prior to DNA extraction. We have demonstrated here that DNA extracted from silica‐desiccated tissue of Durvillaea antarctica possesses a distribution of high molecular weight compounds aligning with hypothesized polyphenol‐derived DNA adducts. Conversely, DNA extracted in a similar fashion from either flash‐frozen tissue or from animal tissue possessed fewer (in the case of flash‐frozen tissue) or none of these compounds.

Notably, in all instances of both Oxford Nanopore sequencing and LC–MS, our extracted DNA would be considered high quality by traditional metrics of assessment (UV absorbance ratios, Qubit, gel electrophoresis). We suggest that this is likely because polyphenol‐modified bases make up a very small proportion of the overall base composition of our DNA extractions and because some polyphenols absorb at similar or nearby wavelengths as DNA, leading to interference with UV‐based quantification of DNA. For example, Durvillaea contains the polyphenols quercetin and luteorin, which have absorbance peaks near 260 nm, similar to DNA (Dobashi et al., 2008; Olate‐Gallegos et al., 2019). For many approaches, polyphenol‐modified bases may not represent a major source of inhibition, especially those that involve the use of PCR amplification, as sufficient levels of unmodified template DNA are likely present to minimize these issues. However, processes that directly utilize native DNA, such as Oxford Nanopore WGS, may be more susceptible to these issues. For example, if even 0.01% of bases are modified, then this would correspond to 10,000 bases per million (per megabase); given that sequencing yields are, ideally, in the gigabase (billions) range, the potential that the accumulation of modified bases blocking or damaging the pores could lead to reduced sequencing output is significant.

When analyzing formic acid‐hydrolyzed DNA by LC–MS, we did not observe intense peaks (compared to nucleobase signals) by UV detection or negative‐mode ionization for putative adducts, consistent with the idea that adduct formation is relatively minor. However, putative DNA–quinone adducts were identified under positive ionization mode, demonstrating the utility of LC–MS as a measure of DNA quality in this context. Previous research has used positive ionization to detect quinone and bisphenol adducts (Zhao et al., 2018), with some ortho‐quinones being better detected under positive ionization (Es‐Safi et al., 2007). Unoxidized polyphenols in Durvillaea have been well identified using both negative and positive ionization (Olate‐Gallegos et al., 2019). In addition, the formic‐acid treatment of DNA employed herein may promote the formylation of the hydroxyl groups of polyphenols, reducing their sensitivity to negative ionization while increasing their sensitivity to positive ionization.

Principally, we saw strong LC–MS‐based evidence for polyphenol‐derived DNA adducts within silica‐dried macroalgal tissue, with substantially less of these putative adducts in flash‐frozen macroalgal DNA. Indeed, given their lower abundance in flash‐frozen tissue, we suggest silica or dry storage of tissue may reduce the ability to sequence brown algae with Oxford Nanopore sequencing. In general, due to our observation of polyphenol–DNA adducts by LC–MS in silica‐dried tissue DNA extracts, we hypothesize that any methods of preserving tissue that minimizes cell rupture and exposure to air (both hallmarks of the silica‐drying process) are likely to increase the chances of successful sequencing by Oxford Nanopore Technology. The improved sequencing results of Eisenia tissue stored in RNAlater rather than on silica gel are encouraging, although it is a single example, and we observed both RNAlater‐ and ethanol‐stored samples were unsuitable for Ecklonia DNA isolation. We were limited by sample availability; however, future work extending our LC–MS‐based analysis to each of the sample preparation methods mentioned may determine more thoroughly whether RNAlater and ethanol, and the conditions in which they are used, are suitable tissue‐ storage media for limiting polyphenol‐based degradation of DNA. Although ethanol is an accessible fixative, it may also be problematic for tissue storage not only due to its ability to desiccate tissue but also, more consequentially, due to its ability to precipitate other DNA sequencing interfering compounds such as polysaccharides (Greco et al., 2014).

Alternative WGS approaches to Oxford Nanopore sequencing include those provided by PacBio and Illumina. PacBio uses a polymerase to traverse strands of DNA (these strands are anchored in a small well by themselves) and, like Illumina, incorporates fluorescent nucleotides into the DNA—these modified nucleotides emit light that can be associated with each specific DNA base. Although we may expect PacBio and Illumina sequencing technologies to be less affected by polyphenol adducts than Oxford Nanopore sequencing technologies because of this key difference in their methodologies, we cannot be sure. First, both Illumina and PacBio sequencing use polymerase enzymes that navigate the strands of DNA—polyphenols may physically obstruct these enzymes and reduce their effectiveness. This could lead to shorter sequences than expected or failure of a specific strand to sequence. Nevertheless, as we discussed earlier, because the sequencing “units” (i.e., the sequencing wells) are not reused as they are for Oxford Nanopore sequencing, these nonnanopore approaches may be more robust for the generation of macroalgal genomic data. Further investigation is warranted, as the effectiveness of different sequencing approaches will likely depend on the distribution of DNA adducts across molecules of DNA as well as on the specific elements of different sequencing technologies. Therefore, it remains to be seen whether the sample‐dependent issues we have highlighted cause problems across all three sequencing methodologies. Future work could compare sequencing outputs for standard samples across all three methods alongside our method or a similar method to determine the presence of polyphenol adducts in each sample.

Because our DNA extraction protocols were designed to reduce polyphenol effects on DNA (e.g., use of antioxidant and PVP), we suggest that the majority of DNA degradation arises prior to extraction, most likely during sample storage. Our results align with previous research that has suggested that silica desiccation may be problematic due to water stress and cell damage promoting DNA damage (Akinnagbe et al., 2011; Bainard et al., 2010). In essence, because the desiccation process ruptures cellular compartments, polyphenol oxidase, polyphenols, and nucleic acids are liberated into a common cellular medium—allowing for DNA damage to occur during the drying process (Savolainen et al., 1995). Therefore, based on our data and those of Savolainen et al. (1995), we hypothesize that such storage of samples leads to varying degrees of cell rupture, promoting the release of polyphenol oxidase from chloroplasts and vesicles (Jukanti, 2017), facilitating the oxidation of polyphenols to o‐quinones (Figure 3). Ortho‐Quinones are highly reactive species that undergo reaction with DNA, the products of which, we suggest, are the principal reason for poor Oxford Nanopore sequencing of brown algae (Figure 3). Therefore, sample storage approaches that preserve cellular integrity or maintain temperatures that prevent these reactions from occurring are likely the best steps forward in improving Oxford Nanopore sequencing yields.

Finally, the observation of a bias in kelp samples toward terminating on a purine (A or G) when the read terminated as a result of a blockage is notable. Although purines are known to be far more reactive than pyrimidines toward o‐quinones (Cavalieri et al., 2002; Wang et al., 2021; Xiong et al., 2022), the origins of this bias require further investigation, since the position of a blockage relative to a DNA–quinone adduct and the sequence‐ and structure‐dependent biases for o‐quinone‐adduct site formation remain unknown. One explanation consistent with our data would be the tendency of o‐quinone‐adducts to form in purine‐rich stretches of DNA, resulting in blockages during sequencing of that area. For example, G‐quadruplexes are guanine‐rich secondary DNA structures that are more readily cross‐linked by polyphenol derivatives (Yuan et al., 2013). Such polyphenol derivative compounds can also cross‐link double‐stranded DNA (Bai et al., 2010), which may also explain the rapid flow cell blockage observed with macroalgal samples.

Our inference that polyphenolic compounds in brown algae interfere with Oxford Nanopore sequencing implies that tissue storage conditions are important and should, in future, be considered in the context of WGS methods, especially for brown algal samples. Specifically, flash‐frozen tissue is best, storage over silica gel should be avoided, and alternative storage methods should be explored and developed.

Genomic resources are of importance not only for ecological and economic management of macroalgae but are also of key importance for understanding evolutionary processes, which has been hampered by the paucity of available genomic resources. As a result, there is a pressing need to resolve issues relating to DNA sequencing for macroalgae. Further work is required for quantitative comparisons between samples derived from different processing methods and sequencing methods, and our results provide a basis for which LC–MS analysis can be used in this application. Finally, although our work is related to Oxford Nanopore sequencing, further work could determine if other common sequencing methods provided by PacBio and Illumina are affected in a similar fashion by DNA–polyphenol adducts.
