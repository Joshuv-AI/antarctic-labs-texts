Dataset on experimental investigation of temperature mediated variations in the chemical composition of bio-oil from pyrolysis of wheat bran
Authors: Priya Kaushik, Ashwani Mathur
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe article reports data on the chemical profiling of Bio-Oil, produced as one of the byproducts from the pyrolysis of sieved wheat bran (particle size range (250–500 µm)), at two different temperatures, viz. 500 °C and 800 °C. The chemical composition of the oil fraction was determined using liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry (LC-MS) and gas chromatography-mass spectrometry (GC–MS). The catalogues of organic compounds observed in GC-MS analysis of the two Bio-Oil fractions revealed relatively higher numbers of compounds in the sample obtained on pyrolysis at 800 °C than in the oil obtained on pyrolysis at 500 °C The GC–MS analysis of wheat bran bio-oils produced at 500 and 800 °C revealed oxygenated compounds (3-furanmethanol and 1,4:3,6-dianhydro-α-D-glucopyranose), sulfur-containing compounds (trans-2,4-dimethylthiane S,S-dioxide), nitrogen-containing compounds (urea, imidazole, dimethylpyridine derivates and pyrozolinone derivatives) and minor hydrocarbons. These compounds originated primarily from the thermal degradation of cellulose, hemicellulose, proteins and amino acids while higher pyrolysis temperatures promoted secondary cracking and cyclization reactions. Several boron-containing compounds were also tentatively identified by NIST library matching and should be interpreted with caution. Alternatively, LC-MS analysis had shown similar trend with common organic compounds, including 2,4-Xylidine; Dimethylpyrazine; 2,4-Diaminotoluene; N-Methylcaprolactam; and Quinoline. The identified compounds demonstrate the conversion of wheat bran biomass into a range of value-added organic constituents through pyrolytic reactions. The results presented herein serve as a stepping stone to strengthen research on scalable process development for the valorisation of agro-waste, such as wheat bran, thereby minimizing the environmental burden.Keywords: Pyrolysis, Bio-oil, Wheat bran, LC-MS, GC-MS, Organic compounds

Background

The U.S. Department of Agriculture reported the average wheat production over the last decade (2016-2025), reaching 778.49 million tons. Wheat bran, a byproduct of wheat processing, grinding, and milling, has emerged as a pivotal source of polysaccharides, proteins, oils, minerals, and other nutritional components [[1], [2], [3]]. Wheat bran production exceeds its consumption, and its burning contributes to greenhouse gas emissions, especially in India [4], thereby increasing the carbon footprint of the emerging economy. This necessitates the development of valorisation techniques for producing value-added products [4,5]. Advancements in scientific interventions for the valorisation of agro-waste, particularly the use of pyrolysis, have emerged as a robust strategy for the anaerobic combustion of lignin-rich materials, primarily agricultural and forest biomass [6]. Limited studies on the pyrolysis of wheat bran have been conducted, and the products have been characterized [7], as well as their roles in environmental monitoring and agriculture [[7], [8], [9]]. The effects of particle size, temperature, and pyrolysis time on the pyrolysis products have not been explored in detail. The current dataset is significant for researchers and industry to develop an efficient, scalable process for producing chemicals as components of Bio-Oil (Figs. 2 and 3).

Data Description

The present dataset represents the temperature-mediated variation in the organic compound composition of Bio-Oil produced at 500 °C (henceforth BO500) and 800 °C (henceforth BO800) after 60 min of pyrolysis, using a controlled heating rate of 10 °C per minute during slow pyrolysis. The study focuses only on Bio-Oil byproducts, BO500 & BO800 (obtained along with biochar and other gases), to identify diverse organic compounds of industrial importance, as analysed using LC-MS and GC-MS (Fig. 1, Fig. 4 and 4). The GC-MS datasets for BO500 and BO800 are compared in Tables 1 & 2. The GC-MS analysis revealed a diverse range of tentatively identified volatile and semi-volatile organic compounds exhibiting varying relative abundances in the Bio-oil. Relatively higher numbers and diversity of organic compounds were observed in BO800 than in BO500, suggesting that operating parameters affect the composition of Bio-Oil produced during pyrolysis. Wheat bran bio-oil produced at 800 °C and 500 °C showed a complex mixture of oxygenated, nitrogen-containing, sulfur-containing and carbohydrate-derived compounds in the GC-MS analysis. A number of significant early-eluting peaks were tentatively identified as derivatives of borinic acid and related compounds containing boron. However, the moderate similarity scores (58.92-80.12%) and the absence of boron-containing precursors in wheat bran suggest that these assignments should be interpreted cautiously and may represent library-matching artifacts. Among the more plausible pyrolysis products of wheat bran bio-oil derived at 800 °C, propanoic acid (5.31%) was determined to be a low-molecular-weight organic acid produced by cellulose and hemicellulose fragmentation. Urea and methane isocyanate derivatives indicate the decomposition of proteinaceous components naturally present in wheat bran. Nitrogen-containing heterocyclic compounds, such as 2-amino-3-methylpyridine-N-oxide (8.98%) and triazole-related compounds, facilitate amino acid breakdown and secondary nitrogen transformation reaction during pyrolysis. The detection of 1,4:3,6-dianhydro-α-D-glucopyranose (6.22%) and xylopyranoside derivatives confirms the thermal degradation of cellulose and hemicellulose fractions, leading to the formation of anhydrosugars and oxygenated intermediates. Furthermore, trans-2,4-dimethylthiane S,S-dioxide (3.11%) indicates that sulfur-containing compounds are formed by secondary cyclization and oxidation reactions at elevated temperatures. Overall, the detected compounds show substantial cracking, decarboxylation depolymerization, dehydration and nitrogen transformation reactions, resulting in a bio-oil rich in oxygenated and nitrogen-containing molecules, typical of high-temperature lignocellulosic biomass pyrolysis. Among the identified compounds of wheat bran bio-oil produced at 500 °C, several nitrogen-containing species were considered more representative of biomass-derived pyrolysis products. These included urea, dimethylpyridine derivatives, 1H-imidazole-4-methanol and 4-methyl-2-pyrazolin-5-one, which are commonly associated with the thermal degradation of proteins and amino acids naturally present in wheat bran. Pyridine and pyridinium-related substances are frequently identified in biomass pyrolysis oils and are typically produced during nitrogen transformation reactions that occur during thermal conversion. The presence of 1H-imidazole-4-methanol further indicates the contribution of protein-derived intermediates to the overall bio-oil composition. The identification of 3-furanmethanol suggests the decomposition of cellulose and hemicellulose through dehydration and cyclization reactions, leading to the formation of furan-based intermediates that are widely reported in lignocellulosic pyrolysis oils. Furthermore, the identification of pyrazolinone compounds could be attributed to further secondary Maillard-type reactions involving carbohydrate and protein-derived intermediates produced during pyrolysis. Trace levels of long-chain hydrocarbons, such as hexacosane were also detected, indicating the presence of secondary cracking, recombination and condensation reactions at high temperatures. Overall, the bio-oil produced at both 800 °C and 500 °C was predominantly composed by nitrogen-containing heterocyclic compounds and oxygenated furan derivatives, emphasizing the importance of both the proteinaceous and lignocellulosic fractions of wheat bran. The approximate literature RI values from reported study using HP-5-type columns were included as supplementary information to support tentative compound assignments, mentioned in Tables 1 & 2 [10]. Therefore, the interpretation of the GC-MS results primarily emphasizes compounds with credible biomass-derived origins and those commonly reported in the literature on lignocellulosic biomass pyrolysis. Furthermore, the LC-MS datasets for BO500 and BO800 showed the presence of several common organic compounds, including 2,4-Xylidine, Dimethylpyrazine, 2,4-Diaminotoluene, N-Methylcaprolactam and Quinoline, as listed in Tables 3 & 4. Compound assignments were based on accurate mass measurements, isotopic distribution patterns, database matching and spectral annotation within the Compound Discoverer™ workflow. Therefore, the reported metabolites should be regarded as putative identifications unless confirmed using authentic reference standards. The raw data of the GC-MS and LC-MS analysis is available in the Mendeley data repository, which can be accessed through the following link; https://data.mendeley.com/datasets/xpgrt4bp5x/1.

Experimental Design, Materials, and Methods

Procurement of wheat bran and thermal activation for pyrolysis

The wheat bran used in the study was procured from a local wheat grinding and processing mill in Delhi, India. The collected biomass was naturally (sun) dried to reduce moisture content, to increase thermal efficiency, and prevent incomplete combustion during pyrolysis. The dried wheat bran was mechanically ground and sieved through a sieve assembly with different filters to separate particles in the range of 75-1000 µm. For the current study, the sieved fraction with a particle size range of 250-500 µm was the largest weight fraction and was therefore used. The rationale for selecting sieved wheat bran particles within a specific size range was to ensure uniform heat transfer, controlled volatilization and consistent bio-oil production. For each pyrolysis experiment, 50 g of dried and sieved wheat bran biomass was used as the feedstock. Pyrolysis was conducted independently at two different temperatures, viz., 500 °C and 800 °C into a closed, continuous nitrogen atmosphere to maintain oxygen-limited conditions in pyrolysis reactor (Ants Innovation Pvt. Ltd., India). The system was heated at a constant 10 °C / minute to avoid unexpected thermal shock and uncontrolled combustion. After completion of the heating cycle, the reactor was allowed to cool naturally to room temperature in oxygen-limited conditions. The pyrolysis process yielded approximately 30% and 20% biochar at 500 °C and 800 °C, respectively, while the corresponding bio-oil yields were approximately 15 mL and 7 mL. The collected bio-oil fractions were transferred to sealed containers and stored under appropriate laboratory conditions prior to analysis. GC-MS and LC-MS analyses were performed within one week of bio-oil production to minimize potential compositional changes during storage.

Comparative assessment of organic compounds compositions in aqueous fraction of Bio-oils using liquid chromatography mass spectroscopy (LC-MS)

The LC-MS analysis of BO500 and BO800 was performed in a single ionization mode for 8 min, as previously reported by Chandel and Mathur [11] with modifications. Chromatographic separation was carried out using a Dionex Ultimate 3000 LC-MS system coupled to a Q Exactive mass spectrometer (Thermo Scientific, Massachusetts, United States). Samples (15 µL injection volume) were analyzed using a Hypersil Gold C18 column (2.1 × 100 mm, 3.0 µm particle size) maintained at 25 °C. The flow rate was 0.350 mL min⁻¹, and the total run time was 8 min. The mobile phase consisted of solvent A (0.1% formic acid in water) and solvent B (0.1% formic acid in methanol). A gradient elution program was employed as follows: 5% B (0–1 min), increased linearly to 95% B (1–6 min), held at 95% B (6–7 min), and returned to the initial conditions for column re-equilibration (7–8 min). Mass spectrometric detection was performed using a Q Exactive Orbitrap mass spectrometer operated in positive electrospray ionization mode (ESI+). The capillary voltage was set at +3.5 kV, and full-scan mass spectra were acquired over an m/z range of 120–800. Additional source parameters included a probe heater temperature of 320 °C, capillary temperature of 270 °C, sheath gas flow rate of 30 arbitrary units, auxiliary gas flow rate of 5 arbitrary units, sweep gas flow rate of 0, and S-Lens RF level of 50. Data acquisition was performed at a mass resolution of 70,000 (FWHM). Raw LC-MS data were processed using Thermo Fisher Scientific Compound Discoverer™ version 3.3 [11]. Molecular features were detected based on accurate mass measurements and isotopic patterns. Common positive-ion adducts, including [M+H]⁺, [M+Na]⁺, and [M+K]⁺, were considered during feature annotation. The spectral libraries are pivotal in the identification of compounds based on the experimental fragmented patterns analysis and verified using mzCloud and mzConfidence. For the present data, identification of the constituents was done using integrated library, Chemspider. Furthermore, the detection of metabolites from the raw file of LC-MS analysis (available at https://data.mendeley.com/datasets/xpgrt4bp5x/1) were performed based on the ‘mzCloud best match’ and ‘mzCloud best confidence.

The two key parameters: ‘mzCloud best match’ and ‘mzCloud best confidence’, indicate compounds with higher similarity to the databases, as reflected in higher scores and are extensively reported for minimizing false positive results. In the current study, compounds with ‘mzCloud best match’ score > 80 or ‘mzCloud best confidence’ score > 60 were selected due to as the benchmark for identification of spectral data with minimum or no false positive interference, as reported previously [11,12]

Comparative assessment of organic compounds compositions in the organic hydrophobic fraction of Bio-oils using gas chromatography mass spectroscopy (GC-MS)

The Bio-Oil fractions were extracted using an equal volume of hexane to separate the organic, hydrophobic fractions of BO500 and BO800. The hydrophobic fractions were analysed using GC-MS analysis was performed using an (Agilent 5977B EI/CI Mass Selective Detector (California, United States)) coupled with a gas chromatographic system equipped with an HP-5MS capillary column (30 m × 0.25 mm i.d., 0.25 µm film thickness). Helium (99.999% purity) was used as the carrier gas at a flow rate of 1.21 mL min⁻¹. The injector temperature was maintained at 260 °C with a column pressure of 90.5 kPa. The instrument temperature was increased initially from 100 °C to 300 °C, as reported previously [13]. Mass spectra were acquired in electron impact (EI) mode at 70 eV. The ion source temperature was maintained at 230 °C. Samples were injected at a volume of 1 µL using a split ratio of 1:20. Compound annotation was performed through comparison of the acquired mass spectra with entries in the NIST Mass Spectral Library 2.0. Retention time (RT), peak area, relative peak area (%) and similarity score were recorded for all detected compounds. All compound assignments based solely on NIST library matching were considered tentative, particularly for compounds exhibiting similarity scores below 80% and should therefore be interpreted with caution presented in the Table 1, Table 2.

Limitations

The raw material used was a agricultural by-product, which are often cultivated at demographical diverse habitat, often affecting their composition. The variation in raw materials and their impact on spectral profile of by-products after pyrolysis cannot be ruled out.

Compound identifications were tentative and based on spectral library matching without validation using authentic standards or experimental retention indices. Furthermore, complete GC-MS acquisition parameters were unavailable from the external analytical laboratory. Therefore, compounds with moderate match scores should be interpreted cautiously and require further confirmation.
