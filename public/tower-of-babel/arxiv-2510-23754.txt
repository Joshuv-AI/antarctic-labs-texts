Some Plancherel identities for unbounded subsets of ℝ \mathbb{R} in duality
Piyali Chakraborty, Dorin Ervin Dutkay

Abstract
. In relation to Fuglede’s conjecture, we establish several Plancherel-type identities and demonstrate the surjectivity of the Fourier transform between certain unbounded tiling sets of ℝ \mathbb{R} that are in duality. In the terminology commonly used in the context of Fuglede’s conjecture, our result states that an open set tiles ℝ \mathbb{R} by the finite set { 0 , 1 , … , p − 1 } \{0,1,\dots,p-1\} if and only if it admits a spectrum (or, equivalently, a dual pair measure) given by the Lebesgue measure on [ − 1 2 ​ p , 1 2 ​ p ] + ℤ \left[-\tfrac{1}{2p},\tfrac{1}{2p}\right]+\mathbb{Z} .

47E05,42A16

1.
Introduction

In 1958, Irving Segal posed the following question to Bent Fuglede:
Let
\Omega
be an open subset of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Consider the partial differential operators

D_{1}=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{1}},\dots,D_{d}=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{d}},

defined on the space
C_{0}^{\infty}(\Omega)
of smooth, compactly supported functions on
\Omega
. Under what conditions do these differential operators admit commuting (unbounded) self-adjoint extensions
H_{1},\dots,H_{d}
on
L^{2}(\Omega)
, where commutation is understood in the sense of their spectral measures?

In his seminal 1974 paper
[
Fug74
]
, Fuglede provided an answer to Segal’s question in the case where
\Omega
is a
connected
,
finite-measure
,
Nikodym domain
—that is, when the Poincaré inequality holds:

Theorem 1.1
.

[
Fug74
, Theorem I]

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
be an open, connected Nikodym region of finite measure. Define

e_{\lambda}(x)=e^{2\pi i\lambda\cdot x},\quad(x\in\mathbb{R}^{d},\lambda\in\mathbb{R}^{d}).

There exist commuting self-adjoint extensions of the differential operators
\{D_{j}:j=1,\dots,d\}
if and only if there exists a subset
\Lambda
of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
such that the family of exponential functions

\{e_{\lambda}:\lambda\in\Lambda\}

forms an orthogonal basis for
L^{2}(\Omega)
.

Definition 1.2
.

A measurable set
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
of finite Lebesgue measure is called
spectral
if there exists a set
\Lambda\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
such that the family of exponential functions

\{e_{\lambda}:\lambda\in\Lambda\}

forms an orthogonal basis for
L^{2}(\Omega)
. In this case,
\Lambda
is called a
spectrum
for
\Omega
.

We say that
\Omega

tiles

\mathbb{R}^{d}
by translations if there exists a set
\mathcal{T}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
such that the translates
\{\Omega+t:t\in\mathcal{T}\}
form a partition of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
up to measure zero. The set
\mathcal{T}
is then called a
tiling set
for
\Omega
, and we say that
\Omega

tiles
\mathbb{R}^{d}
by

\mathcal{T}
.

Thus, in this framework, when
\Omega
is connected, has finite measure, and is a Nikodym domain, there exist commuting self-adjoint extensions of the differential operators
\{D_{j}\}
if and only if
\Omega
is spectral.
Because this characterization is somewhat abstract, Fuglede proposed his famous conjecture:

Conjecture 1.3
.

[Fuglede’s Conjecture]

A measurable subset
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
of finite Lebesgue measure is spectral if and only if it tiles
\mathbb{R}^{d}
by translations.

The conjecture was later shown to be false in dimensions
d\geq 3

[
Tao04
,
FMM06
]
, but it remains true for convex domains
[
LM22
]
.

In
[
Ped87
]
, Steen Pedersen extended Fuglede’s result by removing both the Nikodym condition and the finite-measure assumption. Naturally, the definition of a spectral set had to be adapted for infinite-measure domains (since
e_{\lambda}
is not square-integrable in that case).

Definition 1.4
.

For a function
f\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, define its classical Fourier transform as

\hat{f}(x)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}f(x)e^{-2\pi it\cdot x}\,dx,\quad(t\in\mathbb{R}^{d}).

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
be measurable and let
\mu
be a positive Radon measure on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. We say that
(\Omega,\mu)
is a
spectral pair
if:
(1) for each
f\in L^{1}(\Omega)\cap L^{2}(\Omega)
, the continuous function
t\mapsto\hat{f}(t)
satisfies
\int|\hat{f}|^{2}\,d\mu<\infty
; and
(2) the mapping
f\mapsto\hat{f}
from
L^{1}(\Omega)\cap L^{2}(\Omega)\subset L^{2}(\Omega)
into
L^{2}(\mu)
is isometric and has dense range.

This map then extends by continuity to an isometric isomorphism

\mathscr{F}:L^{2}(\Omega)\to L^{2}(\mu).

The isometry property means that we have a Plancherel identity implemented by taking the Fourier transform of functions in
L^{2}(\Omega)
and restricting it to the support of the measure
\mu
and integrating the absolute value squared of this restriction of the Fourier transform against the measure
\mu
.

The set
\Omega
is called
spectral
if there exists a measure
\mu
such that
(\Omega,\mu)
forms a spectral pair;
\mu
is then called a
pair measure
or a
dual measure
for
\Omega
. When
\Omega
has finite measure, this definition coincides with the earlier one
[
Ped87
, Corollary 1.11]
, and the dual measure
\mu
is the counting measure on the spectrum
\Lambda
.

With this broader definition, Pedersen generalized Fuglede’s theorem by eliminating both the Nikodym and finite-measure conditions, while maintaining the assumption that
\Omega
is connected.

Theorem 1.5
.

[
Ped87
, Theorem 2.2]

Let
\Omega
be an open, connected subset of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Then the partial differential operators
\{D_{j}\}
admit commuting self-adjoint extensions if and only if
\Omega
is a spectral set.

The results were then extended to disconnected domains in
[
CD25
]
. In this case, if
\Omega
is spectral, then there are commuting self-adjoint extensions of the differential operators
\{D_{j}\}
. However, the converse has to be adjusted a little: the Fourier transform in Definition
1.4
is allowed to have some weights which differ on the components of
\Omega
, and the resulting condition is necessary and sufficient.

In the 1974 paper Fuglede proved that his conjecture is true when the spectrum or the tiling set is a lattice in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

Theorem 1.6
.

Let
\Omega
be a measurable subset of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
of finite measure and let
A
be some invertible
d\times d
matrix. Then
\Omega
tiles
\mathbb{R}^{d}
by the lattice
A\mathbb{Z}^{d}
if and only if
\Omega
has spectrum the dual lattice
(A^{T})^{-1}\mathbb{Z}^{d}
.

Many examples of spectral sets are known in the case when
\Omega
has finite measure, and the spectrum can be a non-lattice, see e.g,
[
Kol24
]
. However, much less is known for sets of infinite measure. Of course, the simplest, and most important example of a spectral set of infinite measure is
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, with pair measure the Lebesgue measure on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. To obtain some other examples, a result similar to Theorem
1.6
was proved for sublattices of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
in
[
CD25
]
:

Theorem 1.7
.

Write
\mathbb{R}^{d}=\mathbb{R}^{d_{1}}\times\mathbb{R}^{d_{2}}
. Let
A
be an invertible real
d\times d
matrix. The set
\Omega
tiles with the discrete subgroup
\mathcal{T}=A(\mathbb{Z}^{d_{1}}\times\{0\})
if and only if
\Omega
has pair measure
A^{T}(\Sha_{\mathbb{Z}^{d_{1}}}\times\mathfrak{m}_{d_{2}})
which is supported on the dual set
\mathcal{T}^{*}
,

\mathcal{T}^{*}:=\{\gamma^{*}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:\gamma^{*}\cdot\gamma\in\mathbb{Z}\text{ for all }\gamma\in\mathcal{T}\}.

Here the Dirac comb
\Sha_{\mathbb{Z}^{d_{1}}}
is the counting measure on
\mathbb{Z}^{d_{1}}
, and
\mathfrak{m}_{d_{2}}
is the Lebesgue measure on
\mathbb{R}^{d_{2}}
.

For a Borel measure
\mu
on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, the measure
A^{T}\mu
is defined by
A^{T}\mu(E)=|\det(A^{T})^{-1}|\mu(A^{T}E)
for any Borel subset
E
in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Equivalently, for any compactly supported continuous function
f
on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
:

(1.1)

\int f\,dA^{T}\mu=|\det(A^{T})^{-1}|\int f((A^{T})^{-1}x)\,d\mu(x).

Note that if the measure
\mu
is supported on a set
\Lambda
, then
A^{T}\mu
is supported on
(A^{T})^{-1}\Lambda
.

In this paper we provide a new class of examples of unbounded spectral sets in
\mathbb{R}
and we prove the following theorem:

Theorem 1.8
.

Let
\Omega
be an open subset in
\mathbb{R}
and let
p\in\mathbb{N}
,
p\geq 2
. Then
\Omega
tiles
\mathbb{R}
by
\{0,1,\dots,p-1\}
if and only if
\Omega
is spectral set with pair measure
\mu
equal to a renormalized Lebesgue measure on
\left[-\frac{1}{2p},\frac{1}{2p}\right]+\mathbb{Z}
,
\mu=p\operatorname*{Leb}_{\left[-\frac{1}{2p},\frac{1}{2p}\right]+\mathbb{Z}}
.

2.
Proof of Theorem
1.8

We will need some definitions and notations.

Definition 2.1
.

Let
\Omega_{0}
and
\Omega
be two measurable subsets in
\mathbb{R}
, and let
\mathcal{T}
be a finite or countable subset of
\mathbb{R}
. We say that
\Omega_{0}

tiles

\Omega
by
\mathcal{T}
if
\{\Omega_{0}+t:t\in\mathcal{T}\}
is a partition of
\Omega
, up to measure zero.

We denote by
T_{a}
the translation operator on functions on
\mathbb{R}

(T_{a})f(x)=f(x-a),\quad(x\in\mathbb{R},a\in\mathbb{R}).

Definition 2.2
.

Let
E
and
F
be two measurable subsets of
\mathbb{R}
and let
p\in\mathbb{N}
. We say that
E
and
F
are
congruent

\operatorname{mod}p\mathbb{Z}
if there exists a partition (up to measure zero) of
E
into possibly empty sets
\{E_{k}:k\in\mathbb{Z}\}
such that
\{E_{k}+k:k\in\mathbb{Z}\}
is a partition of
F
(up to measure zero).

Definition 2.3
.

Let
\mu
be a finite Radon measure on
\mathbb{R}
and
\Lambda\subset\mathbb{R}
. We say that
\mu
is a
spectral measure
with
spectrum

\Lambda
if the family
\{e_{\lambda}:\lambda\in\Lambda\}
forms an orthogonal basis for
L^{2}(\mu)
.

Definition 2.4
.

For a measurable subset
E
of
\mathbb{R}
, we denote by
|E|
its Lebesgue measure and by
\chi_{E}
its characteristic function.
For a finite subset
F
, we denote by
|F|
its cardinality. We denote by
\delta_{\gamma}
the Dirac measure at the point
\gamma
in
\mathbb{R}
. For a discrete subset
\Gamma
of
\mathbb{R}
, we define the
Dirac comb
on
\Gamma
to be the counting measure on
\Gamma
,

\Sha_{\Gamma}=\sum_{\gamma\in\Gamma}\delta_{\gamma}.

For a measurable subset
E
of
\mathbb{R}
, we denote by
\mbox{Leb}_{E}
the Lebesgue measure restricted to
E
.

For a function
f
on
\mathbb{R}
we denote its periodization by

\operatorname*{Per}(f)(t)=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}f(t+k),\quad(t\in\mathbb{R}).

We begin now the proof of our main result.

Proof of Theorem
1.8
.

We assume that
\Omega
tiles
\mathbb{R}
by
\{0,1,\dots,p-1\}
.

Proposition 2.5
.

Suppose
\Omega
tiles
\mathbb{R}
by
\{0,1,\dots,p-1\}
.
Define
\Omega_{0}=\Omega\cap[0,p)
.

(i)

\Omega+kp\subset\Omega
for all
k\in\mathbb{Z}
.

(ii)

\Omega_{0}
tiles
\Omega
by
p\mathbb{Z}
; in particular

\Omega=\Omega_{0}+p\mathbb{Z}.

(iii)

\Omega_{0}
tiles
\mathbb{R}
by
\mathbb{Z}
. Therefore
\Omega_{0}
has measure 1, has spectrum
\mathbb{Z}
, and is congruent to
[0,1)
modulo
\mathbb{Z}
.

Proof.

Let
x\in\Omega
. Then
x+p\in\Omega
. Indeed, since
\Omega
tiles with
\{0,\dots,p-1\}
we can write
x+p=y+j
for
y\in\Omega
and
j\in\{0,\dots,p-1\}
. If
j\neq 0
, then
x+(p-j)=y
so
y\in\Omega\cap(\Omega+(p-j))
, which can happen only for
y
in a measure zero set.

If
x-p=y+j
with
x\in\Omega
and
j\in\{0,1,\dots,p-1\}
then
\Omega\ni x=y+p+j\in\Omega+j
and therefore
j
has to be
0
and
x-p\in\Omega
. By induction, we get
x+pk\in\Omega
, for
k\in\mathbb{Z}
. This proves (i).

For (ii), it is clear from (i) that
\Omega_{0}+p\mathbb{Z}\subseteq\Omega
. Now take
x\in\Omega
. Then we can write
x=y+pk
for some
y\in[0,p)
and
k\in\mathbb{Z}
. With (i),
y=x-kp\in\Omega
. Thus
y\in\Omega_{0}
and
x\in\Omega_{0}+p\mathbb{Z}
. This proves the reverse inclusion.

Since
\Omega_{0}
is contained in
[0,p)
, the sets
\Omega_{0}+pk
,
k\in\mathbb{Z}
, are mutually disjoint. This proves (ii).

For (iii), note that

\Omega_{0}+\mathbb{Z}=\Omega_{0}+p\mathbb{Z}+\{0,\dots,p-1\}=\Omega+\{0,\dots,p-1\}=\mathbb{R}.

The sets
\Omega_{0}+pk+j
, with
k\in\mathbb{Z}
and
j\in\{0,\dots,p-1\}
are disjoint by (ii) and the fact that
\Omega
tiles
\mathbb{R}
by
\{0,\dots,p-1\}
.
Thus
\Omega_{0}
tiles
\mathbb{R}
by
\mathbb{Z}
.

With Theorem
1.6
, we get that
\Omega
has spectrum
\mathbb{Z}
. Also, let
\Omega_{0,k}=\Omega_{0}\cap[k,k+1)
and let
I_{k}=\Omega_{0,k}-k\subset[0,1)
, for all
k\in\mathbb{Z}
. Then the sets
\{I_{k}:k\in\mathbb{Z}\}
are disjoint (otherwise the sets
\Omega_{0}+k
are not), and their union is
[0,1)
because, for
x\in[0,1)
there is a unique
k\in\mathbb{Z}
and
x_{0}\in\Omega_{0}
such that
x=x_{0}-k
. So
x_{0}\in\Omega_{0,k}
and
x\in I_{k}
. Thus
\Omega_{0}
is congruent to
[0,1)
modulo
\mathbb{Z}
and

|\Omega_{0}|=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}|\Omega_{0,k}|=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}|I_{k}|=1.

∎

Proposition 2.6
.

For
n\in\mathbb{N}
let

\Omega_{n}:=\Omega_{0}+p\{-n,-n+1,\dots,n-1,n\}.

(i)

The sets
\Omega_{n}
are increasing and cover
\Omega
:

\cup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\Omega_{n}=\Omega.

(ii)

The set
\Omega_{n}
tiles
\Omega
by
p(2n+1)\mathbb{Z}
.

(iii)

The set
\Omega_{n}
has spectrum

\Lambda_{n}:=\frac{1}{p(2n+1)}\left\{-n,-n+1,\dots,n\right\}+\mathbb{Z}.

Proof.

(i) is clear, from Proposition
2.5
(ii). For (ii), note that the set
L_{n}=\{-n,\dots,n\}
tiles
\mathbb{Z}
by
(2n+1)\mathbb{Z}
. Therefore

\Omega_{n}+p(2n+1)\mathbb{Z}=\Omega_{0}+pL_{n}+p(2n+1)\mathbb{Z}=\Omega_{0}+p\mathbb{Z},

and the sets
\Omega_{n}+p(2n+1)k=\Omega_{0}+pL_{n}+p(2n+1)k
,
k\in\mathbb{Z}
are disjoint.

For (iii), we will use the following well known lemma (see e.g.,
[
JP98
]
). We include the proof for convenience.

Lemma 2.7
.

Let
\mu
be finite Radon measure on
\mathbb{R}
with total measure
\mu(\mathbb{R})=m
, and let
\Lambda
be a finite or countable subset of
\mathbb{R}
. The measure
\mu
has spectrum
\Lambda
if and only if

(2.1)

\sum_{\lambda\in\Lambda}|\hat{\mu}(t+\lambda)|^{2}=m^{2}.

Proof.

Suppose
\Omega
has spectrum
\Lambda
. Then

\{\frac{1}{\sqrt{m}}e_{\lambda}:\lambda\in\Lambda\}

is an orthonormal basis for
L^{2}(\mu)
. Applying the Parseval identity to the function
e_{-t}
,
t\in\mathbb{R}
we obtain

m=\|e_{-t}\|^{2}=\sum_{\lambda\in\Lambda}\left|\left\langle e_{-t}\,,\,\frac{1}{\sqrt{m}}e_{\lambda}\right\rangle_{L^{2}(\mu)}\right|^{2}=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{\lambda\in\Lambda}\left|\hat{\mu}(t+\lambda)\right|^{2}

and this implies (
2.1
).

For the converse, if we plug
t=-\lambda_{0}
in (
2.1
), since
\hat{\mu}(0)=|\Omega|=m
, we get that
\hat{\mu}(-\lambda_{0}+\lambda)=0
for
\lambda\neq\lambda_{0}
, and this proves that the exponentials
e_{\lambda}
and
e_{\lambda_{0}}
are orthogonal. With the same computation as above, (
2.1
) then shows that

\|e_{-t}\|^{2}=\sum_{\lambda\in\Lambda}\left|\left\langle e_{-t}\,,\,\frac{1}{\sqrt{m}}e_{\lambda}\right\rangle_{L^{2}(\mu)}\right|^{2}.

On the right hand side we have the norm squared of the projection of
e_{-t}
onto the closed span of the functions
\{e_{\lambda}:\lambda\in\Lambda\}
. This shows that
e_{-t}
is in this closed span. By the Stone-Weierstrass theorem and by approximation by continuous compactly supported functions, the functions
e_{-t}
span the entire space
L^{2}(\mu)
. It follows that
\{e_{\lambda}:\lambda\in\Lambda\}
also span the entire space
L^{2}(\mu)
and therefore they form an orthogonal basis for it.
∎

Returning to the proof of Proposition
2.6
(iii), we apply Lemma
2.7
to the Lebesgue measure on
\Omega_{n}
. Note that this measure is the convolution
\mbox{Leb}_{\Omega_{n}}=\mbox{Leb}_{\Omega_{0}}\ast\Sha_{pL_{n}}
. Therefore its Fourier transform is

\hat{\chi}_{\Omega_{n}}(t)=\hat{\chi}_{\Omega_{0}}(t)\hat{\Sha}_{pL_{n}}(t).

We also have

\hat{\Sha}_{pL_{n}}(t)=\int e^{-2\pi itx}\,d\Sha_{pL_{n}}(x)=\sum_{l=-n}^{n}e^{-2\pi iplt},

which implies that
\hat{\Sha}_{pL_{n}}
has period
1/p
.

It is also easy to check that the measure
\Sha_{pL_{n}}
has spectrum
\frac{1}{p(2n+1)}L_{n}
(this is basically the discrete Fourier transform on
\mathbb{Z}_{2n+1}
, rescaled by
p
). Therefore, with Lemma
2.7
,

(2.2)

\sum_{j\in L_{n}}\left|\hat{\Sha}_{pL_{n}}\left(t+\frac{j}{p(2n+1)}\right)\right|^{2}=|L_{n}|^{2}=(2n+1)^{2},\quad(t\in\mathbb{R}).

Since
\Omega_{0}
has spectrum
\mathbb{Z}
, we have that

(2.3)

\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}\left|\hat{\chi}_{\Omega_{0}}(t+k)\right|^{2}=|\Omega_{0}|^{2}=1,\quad(t\in\mathbb{R}).

We compute

\sum_{j\in L_{n}}\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}\left|\hat{\chi}_{\Omega_{n}}\left(t+\frac{j}{p(2n+1)}+k\right)\right|^{2}

=\sum_{j\in L_{n}}\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}\left|\hat{\chi}_{\Omega_{0}}\left(t+\frac{j}{p(2n+1)}+k\right)\right|^{2}\left|\hat{\Sha}_{pL_{n}}\left(t+\frac{j}{p(2n+1)}+k\right)\right|^{2}

and using the periodicity of
\hat{\Sha}_{pL_{n}}
,

=\sum_{j\in L_{n}}\left|\hat{\Sha}_{pL_{n}}\left(t+\frac{j}{p(2n+1)}\right)\right|^{2}\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}\left|\hat{\chi}_{\Omega_{0}}\left(t+\frac{j}{p(2n+1)}+k\right)\right|^{2}

and with (
2.3
), and then (
2.2
),

=\sum_{j\in L_{n}}\left|\hat{\Sha}_{pL_{n}}\left(t+\frac{j}{p(2n+1)}\right)\right|^{2}\cdot 1=(2n+1)^{2}=|\Omega_{n}|^{2}.

Then, with Lemma
2.7
, we conclude that
\frac{1}{p(2n+1)}L_{n}+\mathbb{Z}
is a spectrum for
\Omega_{n}
.
∎

Next we prove the first condition for
\Omega
to be spectral, from Definition
1.4
, namely that the Fourier transform is isometric from
L^{2}(\Omega)
to the
L^{2}
-space of the pair measure, which is
p
times the Lebesgue measure on
\left[-\frac{1}{2p},\frac{1}{2p}\right]+\mathbb{Z}
. The notation for this
L^{2}
-space is
L^{2}\left(\left[-\frac{1}{2p},\frac{1}{2p}\right]+\mathbb{Z},pdx\right)
.

Proposition 2.8
.

The Fourier transform

L^{2}(\Omega)\ni f\to\hat{f}|_{\left[-\frac{1}{2p},\frac{1}{2p}\right]+\mathbb{Z}}\in L^{2}\left(\left[-\frac{1}{2p},\frac{1}{2p}\right]+\mathbb{Z},pdx\right)

is an isometry.

Proof.

Let
f
be a
C^{\infty}
-function compactly supported in
\Omega
. Then
\hat{f}
is a Schwartz function.

Let
n
be big enough so that the support of
f
is covered by
\Omega_{n}=\Omega_{0}+p\{-n,\dots,n\}
. Since
\Omega_{n}
has spectrum
\frac{1}{p(2n+1)}L_{n}+\mathbb{Z}
, we have, using the Parseval identity,

\int_{\Omega}|f(x)|^{2}\,dx=\int_{\Omega_{n}}|f(x)|^{2}\,dx=\sum_{j\in L_{n},k\in\mathbb{Z}}\left|\left\langle f\,,\,\frac{1}{\sqrt{2n+1}}e_{\frac{j}{p(2n+1)}+k}\right\rangle_{L^{2}(\Omega_{n})}\right|^{2}

=\frac{1}{2n+1}\sum_{j\in L_{n},k\in\mathbb{Z}}\left|\hat{f}\left(\frac{j}{p(2n+1)}+k\right)\right|^{2}.

This is a Riemann sum for the function
|\hat{f}|^{2}
with sampling points
\frac{j}{p(2n+1)}+k
,
j\in L_{n}
,
k\in\mathbb{Z}
, and thus the length of the intervals of the partition is
\frac{1}{p(2n+1)}
. Since the common factor is
\frac{1}{2n+1}
we have to adjust it by the constant
p
, and therefore the function that is integrated is actually
p|\hat{f}|^{2}
on the set
\left[-\frac{1}{2p},\frac{1}{2p}\right]+\mathbb{Z}
.

Since
\hat{f}
is a Schwartz function, these Riemann sums converge to the integral

\int_{\left[-\frac{1}{2p},\frac{1}{2p}\right]+\mathbb{Z}}|\hat{f}(t)|^{2}p\,dt.

This implies that

\int_{\Omega}|f(x)|^{2}\,dx=\int_{\left[-\frac{1}{2p},\frac{1}{2p}\right]+\mathbb{Z}}|\hat{f}(t)|^{2}p\,dt.

Since
C_{0}^{\infty}(\Omega)
is dense in
L^{2}(\Omega)
, we get that the Fourier transform establishes an isometry between the two
L^{2}
-spaces.

∎

Proposition 2.9
.

The Fourier transform from
L^{2}(\Omega)
to
L^{2}\left(\left[-\frac{1}{2p},\frac{1}{2p}\right]+\mathbb{Z},pdx\right)
is onto.

Proof.

First, note that since
\Omega_{0}
is congruent to
[0,1]

\operatorname{mod}\mathbb{Z}
, we have for
k\in\mathbb{Z}
,

(2.4)

\hat{\chi}_{\Omega_{0}}(k)=\int_{\Omega_{0}}e^{-2\pi ikx}\,dx=\int_{[0,1]}e^{-2\pi ikx}\,dx=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cc}1,&k=0,\\
0,&k\neq 0.\end{array}\right.

The function
\chi_{\Omega}=\chi_{\Omega_{0}+p\mathbb{Z}}
has period
p
. Therefore, its Fourier transform, as a tempered distribution, is

(2.5)

\hat{\chi}_{\Omega}=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}\hat{\chi}_{\Omega_{0}}(k/p)\delta_{k/p}.

This follows from the next lemma:

Lemma 2.10
.

Let
\tilde{h}
be a function of period
p
on
\mathbb{R}
and
h=\tilde{h}|_{[0,p]}\in L^{2}[0,p]
. Represent
\tilde{h}
in the Fourier basis
\frac{1}{\sqrt{p}}e_{k/p}
,
k\in\mathbb{Z}
:

\tilde{h}=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}\frac{1}{p}\left\langle h\,,\,e_{k/p}\right\rangle_{L^{2}[0,p]}e_{k/p}=\frac{1}{p}\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}\hat{h}(k/p)e_{k/p}.

Then the Fourier transform of
\tilde{h}
as a tempered distribution is

(2.6)

\hat{\tilde{h}}=\frac{1}{p}\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}\hat{h}(k/p)\delta_{k/p}.

Proof.

Let
\Lambda_{\tilde{h}}
be the tempered distribution associated to
\tilde{h}
,

\Lambda_{\tilde{h}}(\varphi)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\tilde{h}(x)\varphi(x)\,dx,\quad(\varphi\mbox{ Schwartz function}).

Then, for a Schwartz function
\varphi
,

\hat{\Lambda}_{\tilde{h}}(\varphi)=\Lambda_{\tilde{h}}(\hat{\varphi})=\frac{1}{p}\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}\hat{h}(k/p)\int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{2\pi itk/p}\hat{\varphi}(t)\,dt

with the Fourier Inversion Formula,

=\frac{1}{p}\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}\hat{h}(k/p)\varphi(k/p),

and (
2.6
) follows.
The sums are convergent because
\sum|\hat{h}(k/p)|^{2}<\infty
and
\varphi,\hat{\varphi}
are Schwartz functions, thus they have rapid decay.

∎

Take now some
C_{0}^{\infty}
-function
g
supported on one of the intervals
\left[-\frac{1}{2p},\frac{1}{2p}\right]+k_{0}
for some
k_{0}\in\mathbb{Z}
, and let
\varphi
be its inverse Fourier transform; it is a Schwartz function. Let

f=\chi_{\Omega}\cdot\varphi.

Then

\hat{f}(t)=\hat{\chi}_{\Omega}\ast\hat{\varphi}(t)=\left(\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}\hat{\chi}_{\Omega_{0}}(k/p)\delta_{k/p}\right)\ast g(t)=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}\hat{\chi}_{\Omega_{0}}(k/p)g(t-k/p).

We need to restrict
\hat{f}
to
\left[-\frac{1}{2p},\frac{1}{2p}\right]+\mathbb{Z}
. Note that
g(t-k/p)
is supported on
\left[-\frac{1}{2p},\frac{1}{2p}\right]+\frac{k}{p}+k_{0}
, and these sets are disjoint for different
k
’s. Thus, when we restrict
\hat{f}
to
\left[-\frac{1}{2p},\frac{1}{2p}\right]+\mathbb{Z}
, we will keep only the terms with
k=pl
for
l\in\mathbb{Z}
:

\hat{f}\cdot\chi_{\left[-\frac{1}{2p},\frac{1}{2p}\right]+\mathbb{Z}}(t)=\sum_{l\in\mathbb{Z}}\hat{\chi}_{\Omega_{0}}(pl/p)g(t-pl/p).

But, by (
2.4
),
\hat{\chi}_{\Omega_{0}}(l)=0
unless
l=0
, and therefore the only term left is
g(t)
. Thus

\hat{f}|_{\left[-\frac{1}{2p},\frac{1}{2p}\right]+\mathbb{Z}}=g.

Since the linear combinations of functions like
g
are dense in
L^{2}(\left[-\frac{1}{2p},\frac{1}{2p}\right]+\mathbb{Z},pdx)
and since we proved that the map is an isometry, it follows that the map is onto.
∎

Propositions
2.8
and
2.9
show the direct implication: if
\Omega
tiles with
\{0,1,\dots,p-1\}
then it is spectral with the desired pair measure.

We move on now to the converse and assume that
\Omega
is spectral with the given pair measure.

Proposition 2.11
.

Suppose
\Omega
is spectral with pair measure
p\operatorname*{Leb}_{\left[-\frac{1}{2p},\frac{1}{2p}\right]+\mathbb{Z}}
. Let
f,g
be in
L^{2}(\Omega)
. Then
\operatorname*{Per}(\hat{f}\overline{\hat{g}})
has period
1/p
.

Proof.

Let
a\in\mathbb{R}
. We have

\left\langle f\,,\,g\right\rangle_{L^{2}(\Omega)}=\left\langle e_{a}f\,,\,e_{a}g\right\rangle_{L^{2}(\Omega)}=\int_{\left[-\frac{1}{2p},\frac{1}{2p}\right]+\mathbb{Z}}\hat{f}(t-a)\overline{\hat{g}}(t-a)p\,dt

=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}\int_{\left[-\frac{1}{2p},\frac{1}{2p}\right]}\hat{f}(t-a+k)\overline{\hat{g}}(t-a+k)p\,dt

=\int_{\left[-\frac{1}{2p},\frac{1}{2p}\right]}\operatorname*{Per}(\hat{f}\overline{\hat{g}})(t-a)p\,dt=\int_{\left[-\frac{1}{2p},\frac{1}{2p}\right]-a}\operatorname*{Per}(\hat{f}\overline{\hat{g}})(t)p\,dt.

Let
h(t)=\operatorname*{Per}(\hat{f}\overline{\hat{g}})(t)
. Then the function

a\to\int_{\left[-\frac{1}{2p},\frac{1}{2p}\right]-a}h(t)\,dt

is constant.

Let
a_{1}=-\frac{1}{2p}-b-\epsilon
,
a_{2}=-\frac{1}{2p}-b
. We have

0=\int_{\left[-\frac{1}{2p},\frac{1}{2p}\right]-a_{1}}h(t)\,dt-\int_{\left[-\frac{1}{2p},\frac{1}{2p}\right]-a_{2}}h(t)\,dt=\int_{[b+\epsilon,b+\frac{1}{p}+\epsilon]}h(t)\,dt-\int_{[b,b+\frac{1}{p}]}h(t)\,dt

=\int_{[b+\frac{1}{p},b+\frac{1}{p}+\epsilon]}h(t)\,dt-\int_{[b,b+\epsilon]}h(t)\,dt.

Divide by
\epsilon
and, with the Lebesgue Differentiation Theorem, we get that for almost every
b\in\mathbb{R}
,

h(b+1/p)=h(b),

thus
\operatorname*{Per}(\hat{f}\overline{\hat{g}})
has period
\frac{1}{p}
.
∎

Proposition 2.12
.

Suppose
\Omega
is spectral with pair measure
p\operatorname*{Leb}_{\left[-\frac{1}{2p},\frac{1}{2p}\right]+\mathbb{Z}}
. Then
\Omega\cap(\Omega+j)=\emptyset
up to measure zero, for all
j\in\mathbb{Z}
,
j\not\equiv 0\operatorname{mod}p
.

Proof.

Let
f,g\in L^{2}(\Omega)
. We compute, with Plancherel’s identity,

\left\langle f\,,\,T_{j}g\right\rangle_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}=\int_{\mathbb{R}}{\hat{f}(t)}\overline{e^{-2\pi ijt}\hat{g}(t)}\,dt=\sum_{l\in\mathbb{Z}}\int_{\left[-\frac{1}{2p},\frac{1}{2p}\right]}\hat{f}(t+l/p)e^{2\pi ij(t+l/p)}\overline{\hat{g}}(t+l/p)\,dt

=\int_{\left[-\frac{1}{2p},\frac{1}{2p}\right]}\sum_{i=0}^{p-1}\sum_{m\in\mathbb{Z}}\hat{f}(t+i/p+m)\overline{\hat{g}}(t+i/p+m)e^{2\pi ij(t+i/p)}\,dt

=\int_{\left[-\frac{1}{2p},\frac{1}{2p}\right]}\sum_{i=0}^{p-1}e^{2\pi ij(t+i/p)}\operatorname*{Per}(\hat{f}\overline{\hat{g}})(t+i/p)\,dt

with Propositon
2.11
,

=\int_{\left[-\frac{1}{2p},\frac{1}{2p}\right]}\operatorname*{Per}(\hat{f}\overline{\hat{g}})(t)e^{2\pi ijt}\sum_{i=0}^{p-1}e^{2\pi iji/p}\,dt=0,

since
j\not\equiv 0(\operatorname{mod}p)
.

Suppose now that
\Omega\cap(\Omega+j)
has positive measure. Then take
E\subset\Omega\cap(\Omega+j)
of finite measure. Let
f=\chi_{E}
and
g=\chi_{E-j}
. Then
f,g\in L^{2}(\Omega)
and
T_{j}g=f
Thus
0=\left\langle f\,,\,T_{j}g\right\rangle_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}=\|f\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}^{2}>0
, a contradiction .
∎

We want to show that
\Omega
tiles
\mathbb{R}
by
\{0,\dots,p-1\}
. We will show next that
\Omega
is contained in a larger set
\tilde{\Omega}
which tiles
\mathbb{R}
by
\{0,\dots,p-1\}
.

The set
\Omega^{\prime}=\Omega+p\mathbb{Z}
is clearly invariant under translations by
p\mathbb{Z}
and we show that

(2.7)

\{\Omega^{\prime}+j:j\in\{0,1,\dots,p-1\}\}\mbox{ are disjoint sets}.

If not, there exist
k_{1},k_{2}\in\mathbb{Z}
,
j_{1},j_{2}\in\{0,\dots,p-1\}
such that

(\Omega+pk_{1}+j_{1})\cap(\Omega+pk_{2}+j_{2})\neq\emptyset
. We can assume
j_{1}>j_{2}
and then
j_{1}-j_{2}\in\{0,\dots,p-1\}
and

(\Omega+p(k_{1}-k_{2})+(j_{1}-j_{2}))\cap\Omega\neq\emptyset
, which contradicts Proposition
2.12
.

Lemma 2.13
.

Define

\Omega_{0}:=\Omega^{\prime}\cap[0,p).

(i)

The sets
\{\Omega_{0}+k:k\in\mathbb{Z}\}
are disjoint.

(ii)

\Omega^{\prime}=\Omega+p\mathbb{Z}=\Omega_{0}+p\mathbb{Z}
.

Proof.

Consider two sets
\Omega_{0}+pl+j
and
\Omega_{0}+pl^{\prime}+j^{\prime}
with
l,l^{\prime}\in\mathbb{Z}
and
j,j^{\prime}\in\{0,\dots,p-1\}
.
If
j\neq j^{\prime}
then the intersection of these two sets in contained in
(\Omega^{\prime}+j)\cap(\Omega^{\prime}+j^{\prime})=\emptyset
.
If
j=j^{\prime}
and the intersection of the two sets is nonempty, then
(\Omega_{0}+pl)\cap(\Omega_{0}+pl^{\prime})\neq\emptyset
and this is impossible because
\Omega_{0}
is contained in
[0,p)
. This proves (i).

For (ii), we clearly have
\Omega_{0}+p\mathbb{Z}\subset\Omega+p\mathbb{Z}
. For the reverse inclusion, let
x\in\Omega^{\prime}
. Then, there exists
k\in\mathbb{Z}
such that
x-pk\in[0,p)
. Since
\Omega^{\prime}
is invariant under translations by
p\mathbb{Z}
, it follows that
x-pk\in\Omega^{\prime}\cap[0,p)=\Omega_{0}
and thus
x\in\Omega_{0}+pk
.
∎

Lemma 2.14
.

Define
R:=[0,1)\setminus((\Omega_{0}+\mathbb{Z})\cap[0,1))
.
\Omega_{0}\cup R
tiles
\mathbb{R}
by
\mathbb{Z}
and
\tilde{\Omega}:=(\Omega_{0}\cup R)+p\mathbb{Z}
tiles
\mathbb{R}
by
\{0,\dots,p-1\}
and contains
\Omega
.

Proof.

Note that
\Omega_{0}+\mathbb{Z}
and
R+\mathbb{Z}
are disjoint. Otherwise, there exist
x_{0}\in\Omega_{0}
,
k\in\mathbb{Z}
,
r\in R
and
l\in\mathbb{Z}
such that
x_{0}+k=r+l
. So
r=x_{0}+(k-l)\in(\Omega_{0}+\mathbb{Z})\cap[0,1)
, which contradicts the definition of
R
. The sets
\{R+k:k\in\mathbb{Z}\}
are disjoint because
R
is contained in
[0,1)
. The sets
\{\Omega_{0}+k:k\in\mathbb{Z}\}
are disjoint, by Lemma
2.13
. It follows that the sets
\{(\Omega_{0}\cup R)+k:k\in\mathbb{Z}\}
are disjoint. Also,

(\Omega_{0}\cup R)+\mathbb{Z}=(\Omega_{0}+\mathbb{Z}+\mathbb{Z})\cup(R+\mathbb{Z})=((\Omega_{0}+\mathbb{Z})\cup R)+\mathbb{Z}\supseteq[0,1)+\mathbb{Z}=\mathbb{R}.

This shows that
\Omega_{0}\cup R
tiles
\mathbb{R}
by
[0,1)
.

For
\tilde{\Omega}=(\Omega_{0}\cup R)+p\mathbb{Z}
, since
\Omega_{0}
tiles
\mathbb{R}
with
\mathbb{Z}
, we get that the sets
\{\tilde{\Omega}+j=(\Omega_{0}\cup R)+j+p\mathbb{Z}:j\in\{0,\dots,p-1\}\}
are disjoint, and

\tilde{\Omega}+\{0,\dots,p-1\}=(\Omega_{0}\cup R)+p\mathbb{Z}+\{0,\dots,p-1\}=(\Omega_{0}\cup R)+\mathbb{Z}=\mathbb{R}.

Thus,
\tilde{\Omega}
tiles
\mathbb{R}
by
\{0,\dots,p-1\}
.

By Lemma
2.13
,
\Omega\subseteq\Omega_{0}+p\mathbb{Z}\subseteq\tilde{\Omega}
.

∎

Lemma 2.15
.

\Omega=\tilde{\Omega}
and therefore
\Omega
tiles
\mathbb{R}
by
\{0,\dots,p-1\}
.

Proof.

Suppose the inclusion
\Omega\subset\tilde{\Omega}
is proper, so the complement
\tilde{\Omega}\setminus\Omega
has positive Lebesgue measure.

Let
g\in L^{2}(\tilde{\Omega}\setminus\Omega)
,
g\neq 0
and let
f\in L^{2}(\Omega)
. Then
f\perp g
in
L^{2}(\tilde{\Omega})
. Since
\tilde{\Omega}
tiles
\mathbb{R}
by
\{0,\dots,p-1\}
, by the direct implication of Theorem
1.8
, we know that the Fourier transform

L^{2}(\tilde{\Omega})\ni f\to\hat{f}|_{\left[-\frac{1}{2p},\frac{1}{2p}\right]+\mathbb{Z}}=:\mathcal{F}_{r}f\in L^{2}\left(\left[-\frac{1}{2p},\frac{1}{2p}\right]+\mathbb{Z},p\,dx\right)

is a surjective isometry. Therefore
\mathcal{F}_{r}f\perp\mathcal{F}_{r}g
, and also
\mathcal{F}_{r}g\neq 0
.

On the other hand, by hypothesis,
\mathcal{F}_{r}
is a surjective isometry from
L^{2}(\Omega)
to the same range
L^{2}\left(\left[-\frac{1}{2p},\frac{1}{2p}\right]+\mathbb{Z},p\,dx\right)
. Thus
\{\mathcal{F}_{r}f:f\in L^{2}(\Omega)\}
is the entire range
L^{2}\left(\left[-\frac{1}{2p},\frac{1}{2p}\right]+\mathbb{Z},p\,dx\right)
; therefore,
\mathcal{F}_{r}g
is orthogonal to the entire range, and thus it is orthogonal to itself, so it has to be zero, a contradiction.

∎

In conclusion,
\Omega=\tilde{\Omega}
and thus
\Omega
tiles
\mathbb{R}
with
\{0,\dots,p-1\}
, and this shows the converse implication in our Theorem
1.8
.
∎
