Children with unilateral spastic cerebral palsy have smaller and weaker tibias in the spastic limb compared to the non-spastic limb
Authors: Jessica L. Stockhausen, Julio Carballido-Gamio, Dana Carpenter, Jason T. Rhodes, David R. Howell, Sayan De, Patrick M. Carry, Christine M. Swanson, Kellen T. Krajewski
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Cerebral palsy (CP) is associated with poor bone architecture. It is unclear if abnormal bone architecture is localized to the area affected by spasticity and whether these deficiencies are dependent on physical activity. This study aimed to examine differences in tibial bone architecture between the spastic and non-spastic limbs in children with unilateral spastic CP and the association between physical activity habits with spastic limb bone architecture. Twenty-four ambulatory children with CP (13 females, 11 males; 7.8 ± 1.7 years) completed bilateral high-resolution peripheral quantitative computed tomography (HR-pQCT) at the distal tibia metaphysis. The frequency of participation (FPS), bone specific physical activity questionnaire (BPAQ), and dynamic participation survey (DPS) were used to assess physical activity habits. Differences between limb architecture were assessed with paired t-tests. Linear regressions were performed on FPS/BPAQ/DPS scores with each HR-pQCT outcome separately. HR-pQCT data without significant motion artifacts were available for eighteen participants. The spastic limb had less total area (p = 0.013, 95%CI 4.87–35.13mm2), trabecular area (p = 0.035, 95%CI 1.22–29.96mm2), cortical area (p = 0.008, 95%CI 1.35–7.63mm2), cortical perimeter (p = 0.012, 95%CI 0.52–3.60 mm), cortical thickness (p = 0.046, 95%CI 0.001–0.112 mm), stiffness (p = 0.012, 95%CI 2065-13,429 N/mm), and failure load (p = 0.008, 95%CI 128-747 N) than the non-spastic limb. Only BPAQ was associated with limb length (p = 0.011, R = -0.584). The distal tibia metaphysis of the spastic limb had similar bone density but was smaller and weaker compared to the non-spastic limb. Differences in bone geometry likely accounted for between-limb differences in estimated bone strength.

Introduction

Cerebral palsy (CP) is a nonprogressive neurological condition associated with low bone mineral density (BMD) (Mus-Peters et al., 2019) and less bone mineral content (Henderson et al., 2002a; King et al., 2003) compared to typically developing peers. Consequently, individuals with CP have an increased risk of bone fractures throughout their lifespan (Presedo et al., 2007; Leet et al., 2006; Houlihan and Stevenson, 2009; Stevenson et al., 2006). Most fractures occur in the lower extremities (Presedo et al., 2007; McIvor and Samilson, 1966) with the tibia being one of the most frequently fractured long bones in youth with CP (Henderson et al., 2002a). Fractures in youth with CP reduce quality of life and increase the amount of healthcare needed (Henderson et al., 2002a; Henderson et al., 2002b). The high prevalence of fractures in CP populations has been primarily attributed to decreased weight-bearing related to non-ambulatory status (Henderson et al., 2002a; King et al., 2003). However, ∼50% of CP patients who experienced a fracture were ambulatory (Presedo et al., 2007; Kannikeswaran et al., 2022). Indeed, a couple population-based studies have indicated no difference in fracture risk between gross motor function classification system (GMFCS) levels (Granild-Jensen et al., 2022; Linton et al., 2022). While low BMD has been linked as a strong predictor of fracture (Bentley et al., 2026), clinically low BMD is mostly observed in moderate to severe CP (GMFCS III-V) as mild CP (GMFCS I-II) exhibits more variable and normal BMD levels (ljishi et al., 2025). Thus, risk of fracture in mild CP populations may be better explained by bone geometry, microstructure, and estimated strength (i.e., the architecture of the bone).

Preliminary research has observed that ambulatory children with mild CP exhibit less cortical bone volume and thickness, less trabecular cross-sectional area, greater bone marrow fat infiltration, and 30–40% less bone strength at the distal and mid-tibia (Zimmermann et al., 2025; Whitney et al., 2017). Ambulatory children with mild CP execute ∼150 min of physical activity and > 5000 steps per day (ljishi et al., 2025; Bjornson et al., 2020). Reduction of mechanical strain from spasticity interfering with a normal gait pattern might be the primary contributor to low BMD (Frost, 1987; Rubin and Lanyon, 1984; Slemenda et al., 1991; Schipilow et al., 2013; Macintosh et al., 2015) as greater deviation from normal gait has been associated with bone mass but not bone geometry or microstructure (Zimmermann et al., 2025). Alternatively, evidence from rodent models has demonstrated that abnormal neuromuscular activity negatively impacts bone formation independent of mechanical loading (Bain et al., 2019). Regardless of mechanism, spasticity appears to have a negative influence on bone density and architecture in this population.

Spasticity represents one of the most prevalent secondary sequelae of CP (Van Naarden Braun et al., 2016). It is a sensorimotor disturbance characterized as the hyperexcitability of reflex synapses that results in exaggerated stretch reflexes and involuntary muscle contractions (Lance, 1990; Pandyan et al., 2005). Muscles that cross the ankle joint account for ∼43% of all observed lower extremity spasticity (Nieuwenhuys et al., 2017) and ∼ 37% of children with spastic CP exhibit spasticity on one side of the body only, known as unilateral spasticity or hemiplegia (Himpens et al., 2008). Previous research on bone architecture among those with CP has primarily focused on the affected limb, often without clarifying whether the examined bone sites correlate with the spasticity affected muscles. Furthermore, few studies have directly compared affected and unaffected regions within the same individual to assess localized effects of spasticity on bone architecture. Unilateral spastic CP provides a unique opportunity to examine differences between the spastic and non-spastic limb as a necessary preliminary step to disentangle the roles of spasticity and physical activity in bone development. Therefore, the primary purpose of this investigation was to examine whether differences exist in distal tibial bone density, geometry, microstructure, and estimated strength obtained via high-resolution peripheral quantitative computed tomography (HR-pQCT) between the spastic and non-spastic limbs in ambulatory children with mild unilateral spastic CP. The secondary purpose was to perform an exploratory analysis to examine the association between self-reported physical activity habits with spastic limb bone geometry, microstructure, and estimated strength.

Methods

Participants

Children (5–10 years) diagnosed with CP with unilateral spasticity distribution were recruited from the Children's Hospital Colorado neuromuscular clinic. To be included, participants had to be independently ambulatory (i.e., GMFCS I or II), with spasticity in at least one muscle crossing the ankle (e.g., gastrocnemius, soleus, tibialis anterior, peroneal longus, etc.). Spasticity affecting the ankle was targeted due to the tibia being one of the most fractured long bones in children with CP (Henderson et al., 2002a) and to leverage the HR-pQCT's distal tibia imaging capabilities. Participants were excluded if they were non-ambulatory or required assistive devices such as walkers or canes (i.e., GMFCS III, IV, or V). Written informed consent was obtained from the parents, and assent was obtained from the child prior to participation in this investigation. The study was approved by the local ethical review committee.

Sample size calculation

An effect size of 2.60 has been observed in a study measuring the difference in distal tibia metaphysis total area via HR-pQCT between ambulatory children with CP (n = 5) and typically developing children (n = 7) (Zimmermann et al., 2025). Since the present study was a within subject design (spastic vs non-spastic limb), it was hypothesized that the differences would be less pronounced than they were between children with CP and typically developing children. Therefore, a priori power analysis was conducted with a conservative medium effect size of d = 0.75 and yielded a sample size of 16 participants to achieve 80% power, based on a paired t-test.

Procedures

Participants completed a single visit to obtain HR-pQCT images of the distal tibia and self-reported measures of physical activity habits. Bone density, geometry, microarchitecture, and estimated strength were assessed in both limbs at the tibial distal metaphysis (4% of the tibial length from distal physis) using the Stanford/UCSF relative-offset pediatric HR-pQCT (XtremeCT II, Scanco Medical AG, Brüttisellen, Switzerland) acquisition protocol (Gabel et al., 2023). Tibial length was measured from the medial aspect of the tibia plateau to the distal edge of the medial malleolus. The participant was instructed to remove their socks, shoes and any high attenuating objects such as metal jewelry. The lower extremity was immobilized in a carbon cast in conjunction with rolled up towels and koban tape, fixed within the scanning space. Participants were instructed to sit still and quietly during image acquisition. Scans were acquired with isotropic voxel size of 60.7 μm. Custom 4% relative-offset control files, representing the relative position of the center slice to the reference line – most proximal edge of the growth plate, growth plate remnant, or scar – were used for scan acquisition, generating 168 slices. Raw images were evaluated for motion artifacts immediately after acquisition. Each scan was graded using the current artifact grading scheme of the manufacturer (Pialat et al., 2012) consisting of a five-point movement artifact scale, with 1 representing no motion, and 5 representing extreme motion. Scans with movement artifact rated grades ≥3 were rescanned, up to a maximum of two scans per limb. A parent/guardian was always present in the exam room for the full HR-pQCT imaging session.

To quantify the child's physical activity habits, multiple questionnaires were filled out by the parents to ensure accuracy. The first questionnaire administered was the frequency of participation survey (FPS) (Michelsen et al., 2009) which determines the frequency of engaging in different ‘leisure participation’ based activities. The second questionnaire administered was the bone-specific physical activity questionnaire (BPAQ) which calculates an estimate of bone building potential based off of different sport participation each year of life (Weeks and Beck, 2008). The final questionnaire consisted of a series of specific questions about general dynamic participation that is not necessarily sport specific including 1) the number of different types of activities their child participates in (i.e., playing on the playground, an organized sport, hiking, etc.), 2) how many times per week their child engages in dynamic participation, 3) the hours per day their child engages in dynamic participation, and 4) whether their child executes these activities alone or with peers. These four questions together were collectively referred to as the dynamic participation survey (DPS). Clinical characteristics were obtained from the participants' medical charts which included GMFCS level, affected limb, affected compartment, and any spasticity treatments received in the past two years.

Data reduction

For HR-pQCT, after performing motion scoring based on all the 168 slices and discarding scans with a motion score > 3, parameters assessing cortical and trabecular bone density, microstructure, and geometry, as well as those estimating bone strength were generated using the automated standard in vivo analysis protocol provided by the manufacturer. The pipeline delineated the periosteal and endosteal bone contours and segmented the trabecular bone and cortical pores. Periosteal and endosteal bone contours were edited as needed by a single operator who was blinded to affected limb. The evaluation program generated the following bone outcomes of interest: total area (Tt.Ar, mm2), total volumetric bone mineral density (Tt.vBMD, mg HA/cm3), trabecular area (Tb.Ar, mm2), trabecular vBMD (Tb.vBMD, mg HA/cm3), trabecular number (Tb.N, 1/mm), trabecular thickness (Tb.Th, mm), trabecular separation (Tb.Sp, mm), trabecular bone volume fraction (Tb.BV/TV, unitless), cortical area (Ct.Ar, mm2), cortical vBMD (Ct.vBMD, mg HA/cm3), cortical bone thickness (Ct.Th, mm), cortical perimeter (Ct.Pm, mm), cortical porosity (Ct.Po, unitless), and cortical porosity diameter (Ct.Po.Dm, mm). In addition, micro finite element analysis (10 GPa, Poisson's ratio of 0.3, 2% of elements exceeding 0.7% strain) yielded estimates of failure load (N) and whole bone stiffness (N/mm). The second generation HR-pQCT used in this study has a root mean square coefficients of variation (RMS-CV) of <0.6% for bone density parameters (Tt.vBMD, Ct.vBMD, Tb.vBMD), <0.8% RMS-CV for bone geometry parameters (Tt.Ar, Tb.Ar, Ct.Ar), 0.7–3.2% RMS-CV for trabecular microstructure parameters (Tb.N, Tb.Th, Tb.Sp, Tb.BV/TV), 10.3–15.4% RMS-CV for Ct.Po, and 1.8–2.9% RMS-CV for estimated failure load (Warden et al., 2022).

For the self-reported measures of physical activity and participation, scores were determined based on instructions provided for the instrument. Specifically, answers to the FPS are a numerical Likert scale that is summed to provide a single conglomerate score of participation. The BPAQ calculates two separate scores, past activity (pBPAQ) and current activity (cBPAQ); specific details for the calculations refer to Weeks and Beck 2008 (Weeks and Beck, 2008). Typically, scores are combined to generate a single metric, however we analyzed them separately because observable changes in bone can occur quickly in youth populations (Eriksen, 2010). Although BPAQ has not been validated in CP populations, it was chosen due to the calculated score weighting sports based on estimated ground reaction forces thus influencing its osteogenic potential. For the DPS, answers were provided in the form of numeric values on a continuous scale. To generate a single DPS score, questions 1 through 3 were multiplied together (e.g., hours per day x days per week x number of different activities). If participants executed these activities alone (question 4) then the product of questions 1–3 was multiplied by 1. If the participant executed these activities with peers, then the product of questions 1–3 was multiplied by 2. Activity performed with peers was weighted greater due to peer interaction often leading to greater effort and intensity of task execution (Salvy et al., 2009).

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to describe the entire sample and stratified by sex. Independent t-tests or Chi-square tests were used to test for between sex differences of demographic characteristics. To determine the effect of spasticity on bone health, paired-t-tests were performed on each HR-pQCT-derived bone parameter separately comparing the spastic and non-spastic limbs. A strength of this within-subjects design is that it accounts for height, weight, and age which typically need to be adjusted when assessing differences between groups. Since sex can affect bone outcomes (Breehl and Caban, 2025), a linear regression was performed on each bone parameter difference separately with sex as a factor. Differences in bone length can further impact HR-pQCT outcomes so a sensitivity analysis was conducted normalizing all geometric, microstructural, and estimated strength outcomes by tibial length of the ipsilateral limb and then conducting the paired-t-tests. It has been suggested that botulinum toxin injection (BTN) has deleterious effects on bone (Moussa et al., 2024), which is a commonly used spasticity treatment for individuals with CP, it is a potentially confounding variable. Thus, a sensitivity analysis was conducted by performing paired t-tests excluding any participant that received BTN in the past two years. Cohen's D effect size (d) was calculated with d ≤ 0.2 being a small effect, d = 0.21–0.79 being a medium effect and d ≥ 0.8 being a large effect.

A secondary exploratory analysis was performed to determine the association between physical activity history and bone health of the spastic limb, a multi-step process was performed. Firstly, candidate bone parameters were identified as any outcome that was significantly (p ≤ 0.050) different between the spastic and non-spastic limbs via paired t-tests. Then linear regressions were performed on each candidate bone parameter and each physical activity survey score (FPS, cBPAQ, pBPAQ, and DPS) separately. Correlation coefficient (r) was reported with regression outcomes to indicate strength and direction of relationship; r < 0.3 is considered a weak association, r = 0.3–0.7 considered a moderate association, and r > 0.7 considered a strong association. Due to the exploratory nature of this study, no adjustments were made for multiple comparisons.

Results

General

Twenty-four children (13 female, 11 male) were recruited and participated in the present investigation. However, only 18 (12 female, 6 male) of the 24 enrolled participants had HR-pQCT scans in both limbs without major motion artifacts and were included in the final analysis. Therefore, 6 participants (5 male, 1 female, 3 GMFCS I, 3 GMFCS II, 8.4 ± 2.2 years; 1257.2 ± 142.3 mm; 27.3 ± 11.9 kg) were excluded from the analysis. For the 6 excluded participants, 2 had major motion artifacts in the non-spastic limb, 2 had major motion artifact in the spastic limb, 1 had major motion artifacts for both limbs, and the final participant upon seeing the HR-pQCT became inconsolable and no scans were attempted. Discussion

The present study compared tibial BMD, geometry, microstructure and estimated strength between the spastic and non-spastic limb in ambulatory children with mild unilateral spastic CP obtained via HR-pQCT finding that at the distal tibia metaphysis, the spastic limb had similar bone density but was smaller and weaker compared to the non-spastic limb. Specifically in terms of geometry and microstructural parameters, the spastic limb had less Tt.Ar, Tb.Ar, Ct.Ar, Ct.Pm, and Ct.Th. Regarding estimated mechanical properties, the spastic limb had less stiffness and a lower failure load threshold compared to the non-spastic limb. Even when normalized to limb length as the spastic tibia was significantly shorter than the non-spastic tibia, Ct.Ar, estimated stiffness and failure load threshold were significantly lower in the spastic limb compared to non-spastic limb. When a sensitivity analysis was performed removing participants who had BTN in the past 2 years, geometric parameters of cortical bone and estimated strength were no longer significantly different between limbs. Combined, these results indicate that there is no difference in bone density between limbs, but the limbs differ in terms of bone geometry and estimated strength at the site of spasticity.

Limited research has examined bone geometry, microstructure, and estimated strength directly in children with CP and there have been no direct comparisons between spastic and non-spastic limbs. Instead focus has been on comparisons between children with CP and typically developing children (Zimmermann et al., 2025; Whitney et al., 2017). Ambulatory children with mild CP had ∼30% less cortical bone volume, ∼24% less Ct.Th, 34% less section modulus in the medial-lateral direction, and 39% less torsional strength compared to typically developing children at the mid-tibia (diaphysis) as measured via magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) (Whitney et al., 2017). At the distal tibia metaphysis of the spastic or more severely spastic limb measured via HR-pQCT, ambulatory children with mild to moderate CP (GMFCS II – IV) had 35.8% less Tt.Ar, 22.5% less Ct.Ar, 42.1% less Tb.Ar, 39.8% less Tb.vBMD, 47.9% less Tb.N compared to typically developing children (Zimmermann et al., 2025). While the latter study examined a similar site via HR-pQCT, these children were younger, many were not independently ambulatory (e.g., required a walker), and a different acquisition protocol was used (Zimmermann et al., 2025) making direct comparison of absolute magnitudes inappropriate. However, our results are consistent with previous literature seeing similar deficits in cortical and trabecular bone geometry in children with mild to moderate CP and suggest a localized effect on bone geometry in the area affected by spasticity.

BTN is a common spasticity treatment with known negative effects on bone (Moussa et al., 2024; Warner et al., 2006). To account for this potential confounder, we performed a sensitivity analysis removing all participants who had BTN in an ankle-crossing muscle in the last two years and performed the between limb analysis to determine if the observed differences would be sustained. Intriguingly, Ct.Ar and Ct.Th were no longer significantly different between limbs for participants who had not had a BTN injection in the last two years. Moreover, estimated stiffness and failure load threshold were no longer significantly different between limbs. Similar trends regarding cortical bone have been observed in rodents when comparing BTN to tail suspension or peripheral nerve injury (PNI) where all conditions observed trabecular bone loss (decreased Tb.N and Tb.BV/TV) but only the BTN injected rodents exhibited significant loss of cortical bone (decreased Ct.Th) (Bain et al., 2019; Warden et al., 2013). Although the mechanism is still not fully understood, BTN's inhibition of muscle contraction potentially reduces the mechanical strain on the bone that results in changes to cortical bone (Niinimaki et al., 2013; Berman et al., 2020). The present investigation is a cross-sectional design and causal inference from this sensitivity analysis would be inappropriate, especially given the small number of participants who had BTN injection (n = 4). Likewise, it should be noted that all individuals who received BTN in our cohort were female. Therefore, sex as a potential confounder of cortical disruption cannot be excluded.

Sex has long been considered a mediator of bone metabolism due to dimorphic hormone profiles as typically developing pre-pubescent females typically less Tt.Ar and lower failure load threshold in the distal tibia compared to pre-pubescent males (as measured by HR-pQCT) (Gabel et al., 2017). The current investigation used a within subject design comparing the participant's spastic limb to non-spastic limb which controls for age, height, weight, and sex. However, to further elucidate the impact of sex on the magnitude of difference between limbs, we performed linear regressions on the bone parameter differences with sex as a modifier. Only Tb.Th limb difference exhibited a significant relationship with sex as spastic limb trabeculae thickness was greater in males whereas non-spastic limb trabeculae thickness was greater in females. It is unclear why Tb.Th difference between limbs was significantly affected by sex and this could represent a type I error due to no correction for multiple comparisons. Likewise, there were twice as many females as males in the present cohort and the minimal sample size for males may have contributed to a skewed representation of males in the CP population of children. A larger adequately powered sample is needed to evaluate the validity of this observation.

Motion artifacts are important when processing HR-pQCT images. In our cohort, the spastic limb had a mean motion grade of 2.28 while the non-spastic limb had a mean motion grade of 1.83 which was not significantly different. We did not find that spasticity was a hindrance for image acquisition in this group as all had mild spasticity. However, we did observe this age group had more difficulty sitting still in general and removing any potential distraction was paramount (including no tablet or phone). Additionally, we found it was beneficial to only allow one parent/guardian in the exam room with the participant. Regardless, severity of motion artifact increases the standard error of measurement. Tt.Ar, Tb.Ar, Ct.Ar, and Ct.Th are all derived from the periosteal and/or endosteal contours which are subjected to manual corrections if deemed necessary, yielding reliability to the observed differences between limbs. The secondary purpose of this investigation was an exploratory analysis to examine the relationship between physical activity habits and spastic limb bone geometry, microstructure and estimated strength, as lack of physical activity and abnormal loading patterns have been implicated in poor bone health in individuals with CP (Zimmermann et al., 2025; Whitney et al., 2017; Slemenda et al., 1991). The only significant association observed in our study was a negative association between limb length and cBPAQ, but it should be reiterated that the present study was not adequately powered for the secondary analysis and multiple comparisons were not corrected for. Our lack of significant relationships with FPS, BPAQ, and DPS are likely due to i) FPS is specific to ‘leisure’ participation which also includes participation of activities such as using a computer and not completely analogous to total mechanical loading; ii) BPAQ mostly includes sporting activities, thus there could be discordance between actual physical activity participation and sport participation; and iii) these questionnaires have not been validated in children with CP and scores are potentially inaccurate representations of physical activity habits.

Alternatively, mechanical loading profiles (i.e., limb specific mechanical strain during movements) and not cumulative mechanical loading may influence bone architecture more in the CP population as total activity fails to capture the reduction in site specific loading due to spasticity. Greater deviation from ‘normal’ gait has been correlated (r = 0.62, p = 0.04) with less BMD of the lateral distal femur in ambulatory children with mild to moderate CP (Zimmermann et al., 2025). Likewise, peak forces during take-off phase of a countermovement jump has a moderate correlation with aBMD Z-score in children with spastic CP (Jakiel et al., 2025) further suggesting the importance of the mechanical loading profile of the affected limb. Indeed, it has been observed that the strain rate and not necessarily the strain magnitude is the primary driver of mechanical sensing that leads to positive bone adaptation (Cowin, 2007; Han et al., 2004). Both of the aforementioned studies compared mechanical loading to measures of bone density derived from dual-energy x-ray absorptiometry (DXA) and not high-resolution modalities such as HR-pQCT. Future research should directly assess the relationship between mechanical loading profiles such as asymmetrical limb loading and bone architecture with high-resolution imaging.

There are additional limitations that must be acknowledged to inform interpretations of these results. No inclusion of typically developing peers as a comparator group makes it unclear if this difference between spastic and non-spastic limb bone geometry and estimated is similar to what would be observed between the dominant and non-dominant limb. However, it should be noted in a cohort of adolescent typically developing boys, those who suffered a fracture had 5.78% less estimated stiffness and 5.83% lower estimated failure load in the distal tibia compared to boys who did not suffer a fracture (Chevalley et al., 2011). By comparison, the cohort in the present study exhibited a 10.48% and 10.56% reduction of estimated stiffness and failure load respectively in the spastic limb compared to non-spastic limb. Due to the cross-sectional design of this study and the potentially inaccuracies with self-reported physical activity habits it is difficult to ascertain the role of physical activity in the interlimb differences in bone geometry and estimated strength. Furthermore, results can only be generalized to individuals with mild unilateral spastic CP and spasticity in the ankle crossing musculature. It is unknown if this trend will be exacerbated with more severe spasticity symptomology in CP. Lastly, we did not assess pubertal status (e.g., Tanner stage) which is known to influence bone metabolism. Even though our entire cohort was prepubescent, individuals mature at different rates and thus Tanner stage can correlate with different ages (Marceau et al., 2011). However, the strength of this within-subjects design is that it accounts for height, weight, and age which typically need to be adjusted when assessing differences between groups. Future research should use a prospective design that includes larger cohorts of both CP and typically developing individuals, track physical activity with more objective tools (e.g., inertial measurement units) and assess mechanical loading asymmetries during gait and other dynamic tasks.

Consistent with previous literature (Zimmermann et al., 2025; Whitney et al., 2017), our findings show that the tibia affected by spasticity have deficits in cortical and trabecular bone primarily regarding the geometry and estimated mechanical properties. The results of the present study further demonstrate that even in ambulatory children with mild unilateral spastic CP there is a significant difference in bone geometry and estimated strength but not bone density between the spastic and non-spastic limb. Ultimately, the distal tibia metaphysis of the spastic limb was smaller and weaker compared to the non-spastic limb indicating a potential localized effect driven by spasticity.
