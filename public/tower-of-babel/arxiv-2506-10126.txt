Classification of multiple segmented profiles, application to the study of the neuronal protein Tau Institute of Engineering Univ. Grenoble Alpes
Vincent Brault, Emilie Lebarbier, Amélie Rosier, Virginie Stoppin-Mellet

Abstract
This work is motivated by an application in neuroscience, in particular by the study of the (dys)functioning of a protein called Tau. The objective is to establish a classification of intensity profiles, according to the presence or absence of the protein and its monomer or dimer proportion. For this, we propose a Gaussian mixture model with a fixed number of clusters whose mean parameters are constrained and shared by the clusters. The inference of this model is done via the classical EM algorithm. The performance of the method is evaluated via simulation studies and an application on real data is done.



Keywords:

Functional data, Model-based clustering, Constrained parameters, ECM algorithm, Segmentation.

1
Introduction

Biological context.
The Tau protein, found in the brain, plays a crucial role in regulating the architecture and stability of microtubule networks, which are essential for the transmission of nerve signals. Dysfunctions in the Tau protein are linked to serious neurodegenerative disorders, such as Alzheimer’s disease. For a better understanding of how Tau interacts with microtubules and regulates their dynamics, Isabelle Arnal’s team at the Grenoble Institute of Neuroscience has developed an innovative method to reconstitute dynamic microtubule networks in vitro in the presence of Tau protein. This approach allows real-time observation at the single-molecule level using fluorescence microscopy
(
Elie et al. 2015
;
Stoppin-Mellet et al. 2020
)
.
In these experiments, the Tau protein is labeled with a fluorophore, enabling its detection and tracking as it interacts with microtubules. However, a comprehensive understanding of the reaction kinetics requires determining the stoichiometry of Tau proteins—i.e., the number of molecules involved in the process. This necessitates specific experiments to correlate the detected fluorescence intensity with the number of fluorophores responsible for the signal. Light intensity profiles are generated over time from these experiments. In theory, these profiles resemble stepwise decreasing functions, as shown in Figure
1
, where each step represents the extinction of a Tau protein. The number of steps, all of equal magnitude, indicates the number of proteins which are present.

Figure 1:
Example of two light intensity profiles in theory. In all cases, the jump density (green) is the same, but the initial intensity and background may differ from profile to profile.

Constrained segmentation.
A natural way to analyze such profile is to use segmentation methods. Indeed they aim at detecting abrupt changes, called change-points, in the distribution of a signal. Segmentation problems arise in many areas such as in biology for the detection of chromosomal aberrations for example
(
Picard et al. 2005
;
Lai et al. 2005
)
or in climatology for the detection of instrumental changes
(
Caussinus and Mestre 2004
;
Mestre et al. 2013
)
. For our biological problem, a simple segmentation model as a change-point detection in the mean model of a Gaussian process could be used (model that has been extensively studied in the literature, even recently
(
Harchaoui and Lévy-Leduc 2010
;
Killick et al. 2012
;
Frick et al. 2014
;
Dette and Wied 2016
;
Arlot and Celisse 2011
, see for example)
). However, in the biological experiments, proteins that have been switched off may be temporarily reignite before re-extinguishing, an event known as a “blink”. The consequence of such a phenomenon is the apparition of a positive crenel in the profile (with increasing then decreasing mean). The previous model will tend to detect this phenomenon, which is therefore undesirable. This situation is illustrated in Figure
2
on the left on a real profile. One solution is to impose a decay constraint on the mean in the previous model.

In an algorithmical point of view, it is now well known that segmentation methods have to deal with an inherent algorithmic complexity.
Indeed, the estimation of the change-point parameters requires to search over all the possible segmentations, which is prohibitive in terms of computational time when performed in a naive way. The Dynamic Programming (DP) algorithm
(
Bellman 1961
)
is the first algorithm that solved this problem exaclty in a fast way (quadratic with respect to the length of the profile). Unfortunately, DP only applies when the contrast to be
optimized is additive with respect to the segments
(
Caussinus and Mestre 2004
;
Picard et al. 2005
, see)
. The decay constraint imposes a link between segments and no longer allows the use of DP. To our knowledge, two papers deals with this problem and propose either a two-stage procedure
(
Gao et al. 2020
)
that consists in doing an isotonic regression then runing the DP on the resulting potential change-points or using a new exact algorithm allowing constraint between successive segments
(
Hocking et al. 2022
)
. Note that the two methods recover the exact solution. In Figure
2
on the right, the first method is applied. We can observe that the “blink” is not detected anymore as expected. Note that the decay size cannot be imposed in both strategies, it is estimated in the same time as the estimation of the change-points.

Figure 2:
Free segmentation (left) and constrained segmentation (right) of a given profile. The red line corresponds to the estimated mean and the vertical blue line(s) to the estimated change-point(s).

Constrained classification.
Our objective in this article is to classify the profiles according to biological constraints: (i) one (or two) decrease(s) in the mean but also (ii) the same decrease in height (same jump) for all profiles (see Figure
1
). A first and most simple approach to perform this classification could be to use a two-step strategy: segment each profile by considering the decay constraint and cluster the profiles based on the segmentation results (as, for example, the number of jumps and their sizes), as is done classically for times series clustering where the used basis can be Fourier or splines (see
Jacques and Preda 2014
for a review of these methods). However, constraint (ii) would not be taken into account, which argues for a unified clustering/segmentation model. In this paper, we propose a mixture model of Gaussian distributions whose mean is a decreasing piecewise constant function, differing according to four clusters in order to be in tune with the various biological phenomena: no jump for the cluster
1
, one jump of size
\delta
for cluster
2
, two jumps of size
\delta
each for cluster
3
and one jump of size
2\delta
for cluster
4
. We impose that the jump heights are shared by all profiles, while the jump times are profile-specific.

To infer the parameters, we propose to use the Expectation/Conditional Maximization algorithm
(
Meng and Rubin 1993
)
. This algorithm is an instance of the traditional EM algorithm
(
Dempster et al. 1977
)
, classically considered for the inference of mixture models, which replaces M-step of EM with several computationally CM-steps. Among the CM-steps, one is dedicated to the estimation of the change-point locations, thus once the size of the decay is given (previously estimated). DP-strategies can not still be applied because the segments remain linked through their means. However, here the profiles have a maximum of two change-points, thus a naive search is easily feasible, although a faster strategy would be preferable.

3
Inference

We propose to estimate the model parameters by using the classical maximum likelihood method. Our model being a mixture model, we consider the EM algorithm
(
Dempster et al. 1977
)
whose use is now well established in this framework. The use of EM lies in the complete-data
\log
-likelihood, that is the
\log
-likelihood of
(Y,Z)
where
Z=(Z^{s})_{s}
, and which is written

\displaystyle\log\mathbb{P}(Y,Z;{\bf{\theta}})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\log\left(\prod_{s=1}^{S}\prod_{k=1}^{4}\mathbb{(}\pi_{k}f(Y^{s};{\bf{\Phi}}_{k}^{s},\delta))^{Z_{k}^{s}}\right)=\sum_{s=1}^{S}\sum_{k=1}^{4}Z_{k}^{s}\log(\pi_{k}f(Y^{s};{\bf{\Phi}}_{k}^{s},\delta)),

where
f(Y^{s};{\bf{\Phi}}_{k}^{s},\delta)
is the conditional density of
Y^{s}
conditionally to cluster
k
, that is

f(Y_{s};{\bf{\Phi}}_{k}^{s},\delta)=\left(\frac{1}{\sigma_{k}^{s}\sqrt{2\pi}}\right)^{n^{s}}\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2\sigma_{k}^{2,s}}\|Y^{s}-m_{k}^{s}\|_{2}^{2}\right)=\left(\frac{1}{\sigma_{k}^{s}\sqrt{2\pi}}\right)^{n^{s}}\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2\sigma_{k}^{2,s}}\|Y^{s}-\mu_{k}^{s}-T^{s}_{k}\delta\|_{2}^{2}\right),

where
T^{s}_{k}
is given in (
1
).

The EM algorithm aims at maximizing the conditional expectation of the complete-data
\log
-likelihood, given the observed data
Y
, that is

Q({\bf{\theta}},{\bf{\theta}}^{\prime})=\mathbb{E}_{{\bf{\theta}}^{\prime}}[\log\mathbb{P}(Y,Z;{\bf{\theta}})\mid Y],

where
\mathbb{E}_{{\bf{\theta}}^{\prime}}[.]
denotes the expectation operator using
{\bf{\theta}}^{\prime}
as the parameter value. It is an iterative algorithm which combines two steps: the E-step and the M-step described in the two following subsections respectively.

3.1
E-step.

This step consists in computing the previous conditional expectation using the current fit
{\bf{\theta}}^{(h)}
for
{\bf{\theta}}
:

\displaystyle Q({\bf{\theta}},{\bf{\theta}}^{(h)})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{{\bf{\theta}}^{(h)}}\left[\sum_{s=1}^{S}\sum_{k=1}^{4}Z_{k}^{s}\log(\pi_{k}f(Y^{s};{\bf{\Phi}}_{k}^{s},\delta))\Bigg|Y\right]=\sum_{s=1}^{S}\mathbb{E}_{{\bf{\theta}}^{(h)}}\left[\sum_{k=1}^{4}Z_{k}^{s}\log(\pi_{k}f(Y^{s};{\bf{\Phi}}_{k}^{s},\delta))\Bigg|Y^{s}\right]

(2)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{s=1}^{S}\sum_{k=1}^{4}\mathbb{E}_{{\bf{\theta}}^{(h)}}\left[Z_{k}^{s}|Y^{s}\right]\log(\pi_{k}f(Y^{s};{\bf{\Phi}}_{k}^{s},\delta))=\sum_{s=1}^{S}\sum_{k=1}^{4}{\tau_{k}^{s}}^{(h+1)}\log(\pi_{k}f(Y^{s};{\bf{\Phi}}_{k}^{s},\delta)),

with

\displaystyle{\tau_{k}^{s}}^{(h+1)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{{\bf{\theta}}^{(h)}}(Z_{k}^{s}=1|Y^{s})=\frac{\pi_{k}^{(h)}f(Y^{s};{\bf{\Phi}}_{k}^{s,(h)},\delta^{(h)})}{\sum_{l=1}^{4}\pi_{l}^{(h)}f(Y^{s};{\bf{\Phi}}_{l}^{s,(h)},\delta^{(h)})},

which represents the posterior probability of the profile
Y^{s}
to belong to cluster
k
.

3.2
CM-steps.

This global M-step consists in updating the parameters by maximizing the conditional expectation resulting from the E-step:

{{\bf{\theta}}}^{(h+1)}=\argmax_{{\bf{\theta}}}\ Q({\bf{\theta}},{\bf{\theta}}^{(h)}).

We use here the ECM algorithm to break down the maximization of
Q({\bf{\theta}},{\bf{\theta}}^{(h)})
with respect to
{\bf{\theta}}
into simpler CM-steps which focus on one parameter, the others being fixed. The convergence properties of ECM are provided in
Meng and Rubin 1993
.

Estimation of
\pi
.
The proportions
\pi_{k}
are estimated under the constraint that
\sum_{k=1}^{4}\pi_{k}=1
. We get

\pi_{k}^{(h+1)}=\argmax_{{\bf{\pi}}}\ Q({\bf{\theta}},{\bf{\pi}},\delta^{(h)},{\bf{\Phi}}^{(h)})=\frac{\sum_{s=1}^{S}{\tau_{k}^{s}}^{(h+1)}}{S}\ \ \ \text{for $k=1,\ldots,4$.}

Estimation of
\delta
.
We get

\displaystyle\delta^{(h+1)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\argmax_{\delta}\ Q({\bf{\theta}},{\bf{\pi}}^{(h+1)},\delta,{\bf{\Phi}}^{(h)}),

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\argmin_{\delta}\sum_{s=1}^{S}\sum_{k=1}^{4}\frac{{\tau_{k}^{s}}^{(h+1)}}{\sigma_{k}^{2,(h)}}\|Y^{s}-\mu_{k}^{s(h)}-T_{k}^{s(h)}\delta\|_{2}^{2},

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\sum_{s=1}^{S}\sum_{k=2}^{4}\frac{{\tau_{k}^{s}}^{(h+1)}}{\sigma_{k}^{2,(h)}}\ {}^{t}T_{k}^{s(h)}(Y^{s}-\mu_{k}^{s(h)})}{\sum_{s=1}^{S}\sum_{k=2}^{4}\frac{{\tau_{k}^{s}}^{(h+1)}}{\sigma_{k}^{2,(h)}}\ {}^{t}T_{k}^{s(h)}T_{k}^{s(h)}},

where
{}^{t}H
denotes the transpose matrix of the matrix
H
. The detailed calculation gives
\delta^{(h+1)}=A/B
with

\displaystyle A

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{s=1}^{S}\left\{\sum_{k=2}^{3}\frac{{\tau_{k}^{s}}^{(h+1)}}{\sigma_{k}^{2,s(h)}}\sum_{i=2}^{K_{k}}(i-1)\sum_{t=t^{s(h)}_{k(i-1)}+1}^{t^{s(h)}_{ki}}(Y_{t}^{s}-\mu_{k}^{s(h)})+2\frac{{\tau_{4}^{s}}^{(h+1)}}{\sigma_{4}^{2,s(h)}}\sum_{t=t^{s(h)}_{41}+1}^{n^{s}}(Y_{t}^{s}-\mu_{4}^{s(h)})\right\},

and

B=\sum_{s=1}^{S}\left\{\frac{{\tau_{2}^{s}}^{(h+1)}}{\sigma_{2}^{2,s(h)}}{n_{22}^{s(h)}}+\frac{{\tau_{3}^{s}}^{(h+1)}}{\sigma_{3}^{2,s(h)}}({n_{32}^{s(h)}}+4{n_{33}^{s(h)}})+4\frac{{\tau_{4}^{s}}^{(h+1)}}{\sigma_{4}^{2,s(h)}}{n_{42}^{s(h)}}\right\}.

Estimation of
{\bf{\Phi}}
.
This step is reduced to a constraint segmentation framework in which each profile
Y^{s}
need to be segmented in each cluster with an fixed high jump
\delta^{(h+1)}
. For each
s=1,\ldots,S
and
k=1,\ldots,4
,

\displaystyle{\bf{\Phi}}_{k}^{s(h+1)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\argmax_{{\bf{\Phi}}_{k}^{s}}\ Q({\bf{\theta}},{\bf{\pi}}^{(h+1)},\delta^{(h+1)},{\bf{\Phi}}),

i.e.

\displaystyle(\mu_{k}^{s(h+1)},\sigma_{k}^{2,s(h+1)},T^{s(h+1)}_{k})

\displaystyle\in

\displaystyle\argmax_{\mu_{k}^{s},\sigma_{k}^{2,s},T^{s}_{k}}\ Q({\bf{\theta}},{\bf{\pi}}^{(h+1)},\delta^{(h+1)},\mu_{k}^{s},\sigma_{k}^{2,s},T^{s}_{k}),

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\argmax_{\mu_{k}^{s},\sigma_{k}^{2,s},T^{s}_{k}}\ \left[-\frac{n^{s}}{2}\log{(2\pi\sigma_{k}^{2,s})}-\frac{1}{2\sigma_{k}^{2,s}}\|Y^{s}-\mu_{k}^{s}-T^{s}_{k}\delta^{(h+1)}\|_{2}^{2}\right].

We differentiate the case of no change (cluster

) from the others:

•

for

\displaystyle(\mu_{1}^{s(h+1)},\sigma_{1}^{2,s(h+1)})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\argmax_{\mu_{1}^{s},\sigma_{1}^{2,s}}\ -\frac{n^{s}}{2}\log{(2\pi\sigma_{1}^{2,s})}-\frac{1}{2\sigma_{1}^{2,s}}\|Y^{s}-\mu_{1}^{s}\|_{2}^{2},

and we obtain

{\mu_{1}^{s}}^{(h+1)}=\overline{Y}^{s}=\frac{1}{n^{s}}\sum_{t=1}^{n^{s}}Y_{t}^{s}\ \ \ \ \text{and}\ \ \ \ \sigma_{1}^{2,s(h+1)}=\frac{1}{n^{s}}\sum_{t=1}^{n^{s}}\left(Y_{t}^{s}-\overline{Y}^{s}\right)^{2}.

•

The estimation of the change-points is then obtained as follows

\displaystyle T^{s(h+1)}_{k}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\argmax_{T^{s}_{k}}\max_{{\mu}_{k}^{s}}\max_{\sigma_{k}^{2,s}}\ Q({\bf{\theta}},{\bf{\pi}}^{(h+1)},\delta^{(h+1)},\mu_{k}^{s},\sigma_{k}^{2,s},T^{s}_{k})

\displaystyle\argmax_{T^{s}_{k}}\ Q({\bf{\theta}},{\bf{\pi}}^{(h+1)},\delta^{(h+1)},{\widehat{\mu}_{k}^{s}}(T^{s}_{k}),{\widehat{\sigma}_{k}^{2,s}}(T^{s}_{k}),T^{s}_{k}),

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\argmax_{T^{s}_{k}}\ \left[-\frac{n^{s}}{2}\log{(2\pi{\widehat{\sigma}_{k}^{2,s}}(T^{s}_{k}))}-\frac{1}{2{\widehat{\sigma}_{k}^{2,s}}(T^{s}_{k})}\|Y^{s}-{\widehat{\mu}_{k}^{s}}(T^{s}_{k})-T^{s}_{k}\delta^{(h+1)}\|_{2}^{2}\right],

\displaystyle\argmax_{T^{s}_{k}}\ -\frac{n^{s}}{2}\left[\log{(2\pi{\widehat{\sigma}_{k}^{2,s}}(T^{s}_{k}))}+1\right],

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\argmin_{T^{s}_{k}}\ \|Y^{s}-{\widehat{\mu}_{k}^{s}}(T^{s}_{k})-T^{s}_{k}\delta^{(h+1)}\|_{2}^{2},

and the resulting estimators of the mean baseline and the variance are

{\mu}_{k}^{s(h+1)}={\widehat{\mu}_{k}^{s}}(T^{s(h+1)}_{k})\ \ \ \ \text{and}\ \ \ \ {\sigma_{k}^{2,s(h+1)}}={\widehat{\sigma}_{k}^{2,s}}(T^{s(h+1)}_{k}).

4
Assessing the precision of estimates

In the missing-data framework where parameters are estimated using the EM algorithm, the observed Fisher information is often used to assess the precision of the estimators. However, because of the presence of latent variables, there is no simple form of it. Instead, the expected complete-data Fisher information matrix, taken with respect to the conditional distribution of the missing data given the observed data, provides a reasonable approximation of the uncertainty. Although it tends to overestimate precision, it remains useful for identifying the relative importance of various factors - such as model specification and sample size - that influence estimator precision
(
McLachlan and Krishnan 2008
;
Louis 1982
)
.

We aim at calculating

\mathcal{I}(\widehat{\theta})=\mathbb{E}_{X|Y}[I^{c}(\theta)]\big|_{\theta=\widehat{\theta}},

where
X=(Y,Z)
and
I^{c}(\theta)
is given by

I^{c}(\theta)=-\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial\theta\partial{}^{t}\theta}\log{\mathbb{P}(Y,Z;\theta)},

and so-called the complete-data information matrix. It is straightforward to see that this matrix is almost block diagonal

I^{c}(\theta)=\begin{bmatrix}I^{c}(\pi)&0&0&0\\
0&I^{c}(\delta)&I^{c}(\delta,\mu)&I^{c}(\delta,\sigma^{2})\\
0&I^{c}(\delta,\mu)&I^{c}(\mu)&0\\
0&I^{c}(\delta,\sigma^{2})&0&I^{c}(\sigma^{2})\\
\end{bmatrix}.

B
, we obtain that

•

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{X|Y}[I^{c}(\pi)]\big|_{\pi=\widehat{\pi}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle S\begin{bmatrix}\frac{1}{\widehat{\pi}_{1}}+\frac{1}{\widehat{\pi}_{4}}&\frac{1}{\widehat{\pi}_{4}}&\frac{1}{\widehat{\pi}_{4}}\\
\frac{1}{\widehat{\pi}_{4}}&\frac{1}{\widehat{\pi}_{2}}+\frac{1}{\widehat{\pi}_{4}}&\frac{1}{\widehat{\pi}_{4}}\\
\frac{1}{\widehat{\pi}_{4}}&\frac{1}{\widehat{\pi}_{4}}&\frac{1}{\widehat{\pi}_{3}}+\frac{1}{\widehat{\pi}_{4}}\end{bmatrix},

\displaystyle\sum_{s=1}^{S}\sum_{k=2}^{4}\frac{\tau^{s}_{k}}{\widehat{\sigma}^{2,s}_{k}}\ {}^{t}{T_{s}^{k}}T_{s}^{k}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{s=1}^{S}\left(\frac{n_{22}^{s}\tau^{s}_{2}}{\widehat{\sigma}^{2,s}_{2}}+\frac{\tau^{s}_{3}(n_{32}^{s}+4n_{33}^{s})}{\widehat{\sigma}^{2,s}_{3}}+\frac{4n_{42}^{s}\tau^{s}_{4}}{\widehat{\sigma}^{2,s}_{4}}\right),

•

for the mean parameter,
\mathbb{E}_{X|Y}[I^{c}(\mu)]\big|_{\theta=\widehat{\theta}}
is a

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{X|Y}[I^{c}(\mu)]\big|_{\theta=\widehat{\theta}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\text{diag}\left[\frac{n^{s}\tau^{s}_{k}}{\widehat{\sigma}^{2,s}_{k}}\right]_{ks},

•

and for the variance parameter,
\mathbb{E}_{X|Y}[I^{c}(\sigma^{2})]\big|_{\theta=\widehat{\theta}}

[4S\times 4S]

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{X|Y}[I^{c}(\sigma^{2})]\big|_{\theta=\widehat{\theta}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\text{diag}\left[\frac{n^{s}\tau^{s}_{k}}{\widehat{\sigma}^{4,s}_{k}}\right]_{ks}.

As expected, the precision of the different estimators is linked to the statistical unit used to estimate them: for the proportion parameter, it only depends on the number of profiles and not on the length of the profiles, contrary for the mean and variance parameters, whose precision depends on and increases with the length of the profiles. For the jump
\delta
, which is common to all clusters, the precision naturally depends on both the number of profiles and their lengths, more precisely on the length of the segments associated to means which include
\delta
. The two terms
I^{c}(\delta,\mu)
and
I^{c}(\delta,\sigma^{2})
are not nulls (not shown here, see Appendix
B
for their calculations), indicating that the estimator of the jump is correlated to those of the baseline mean (positively) and the variance, as logical.

Similar results have been found in
Picard 2005
for a different problem, but one that also involves classification and segmentation purposes. His problem is to classify the segments of a segmented profile inferred using a DP-EM algorithm. Similarity in the sense that the concatenation of the whole profile can be viewed as one profile with fixed boundaries, and so the number of profiles in our case plays the role of the number of segments in Picard’s model, but the difference is that the distribution parameters are here profile-specific.

7
Discussion

Classification procedure for multiple profiles.
We proposed a unified classification model for multiple profiles, based on their segmented profiles which are linked through the size of the jumps. This work was motivated by an application in neuroscience, in which the number of clusters and their profile structures were predefined: four scenarios were considered, corresponding to a profile with no change-point, a profile with a single change-point and a jump of size
\delta
, a profile with two change-points, each with a jump of size
\delta
, and a profile with one change-point and a jump of size
2\delta
. The number of clusters and the number of change-points were therefore not in this study treated as parameters to choose. The procedure involves a EM algorithm for parameter inference, during which combined with an exhaustive search to locate the change-points is performed. The classical dynamic programming (DP) algorithm could not be used in this context, as the segment-additivity condition required for its application is not satisfied here. Our results showed that cluster
3
, corresponding to profiles with two change-points, was the most attractive (i.e., most frequently selected by the algorithm).

Possible Improvements.
The procedure can be improved in two directions:

•

From an algorithmic perspective, computational efficiency could likely be improved by adopting more recent dynamic programming approaches, such as the gfpop algorithm proposed by
Runge et al. 2023
. This algorithm is not only faster but also allows for the integration of constraints between successive segments, which aligns well with the requirements of our setting.

•

From a statistical perspective, our model does not infer the number of change-points; instead, each profile is assumed to contain either
0
,
1
, or
2
change-points. Naturally, increasing the number of change-points improves the fit to the data, which may cause profiles with a single change-point to be misclassified into the cluster characterized by two closely spaced change-points (cluster
3
). To mitigate this issue, one could introduce a penalized criterion that accounts for both the number and the length of segments, such as the multiscale penalty proposed by
Verzelen et al. 2023
.

\displaystyle\sum_{t=1}^{n}(\widetilde{Y}^{\star,4}_{t}-\widehat{\mu}_{3})^{2}+\delta^{2}(n_{2}+n_{3})+2\delta\left(\sum_{t\in J_{3}}(\widetilde{Y}^{\star,4}_{t}-\widehat{\mu}_{3})-\sum_{t\in J_{2}}(\widetilde{Y}^{\star,4}_{t}-\widehat{\mu}_{3})\right).

Since
\widehat{\mu}_{3}=\widehat{\mu}_{4}-\frac{\delta}{n}(n_{2}-n_{3})
and
\widetilde{Y}^{\star,4}_{t}=\mu_{4}^{\star}+\epsilon^{\star}_{t}
, we get

\displaystyle Q_{n}(T_{3})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle Q_{n}(T_{4}^{\star})+2\frac{\delta}{n}(n_{2}-n_{3})\sum_{t=1}^{n}(\widetilde{Y}^{\star,4}_{t}-\widehat{\mu}_{4})+\frac{\delta^{2}}{n}(n_{2}-n_{3})^{2}+\delta^{2}(n_{2}+n_{3})

\displaystyle+2\delta\left(\sum_{t\in J_{3}}(\widetilde{Y}^{\star,4}_{t}-\widehat{\mu}_{4})-\sum_{t\in J_{2}}(\widetilde{Y}^{\star,4}_{t}-\widehat{\mu}_{4})\right)+2\frac{\delta^{2}}{n}(n_{2}-n_{3})(n_{3}-n_{2})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle Q_{n}(T_{4}^{\star})+2\delta\left(\frac{(n_{2}-n_{3})}{n}\sum_{t=1}^{n}(\widetilde{Y}^{\star,4}_{t}-\widehat{\mu}_{4})+\sum_{t\in J_{3}}(\widetilde{Y}^{\star,4}_{t}-\widehat{\mu}_{4})-\sum_{t\in J_{2}}(\widetilde{Y}^{\star,4}_{t}-\widehat{\mu}_{4})\right)

\displaystyle+\delta^{2}\left(n_{2}+n_{3}-\frac{(n_{2}-n_{3})^{2}}{n}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle Q_{n}(T_{4}^{\star})+\delta^{2}\left(n_{2}+n_{3}-\frac{(n_{2}-n_{3})^{2}}{n}\right)

\displaystyle+2\delta\left(\frac{(n_{2}-n_{3})}{n}\sum_{t\in J_{1}\cup J_{4}}\widetilde{Y}^{\star,4}_{t}+\left(\frac{(n_{2}-n_{3})}{n}+1\right)\sum_{t\in J_{3}}\widetilde{Y}^{\star,4}_{t}+\left(\frac{(n_{2}-n_{3})}{n}-1\right)\sum_{t\in J_{2}}\widetilde{Y}^{\star,4}_{t}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle Q_{n}(T_{4}^{\star})+\delta^{2}\left(n_{2}+n_{3}-\frac{(n_{2}-n_{3})^{2}}{n}\right)

\displaystyle+2\delta\left(\frac{(n_{2}-n_{3})}{n}\sum_{t\in J_{1}\cup J_{4}}\epsilon^{\star}+\left(\frac{(n_{2}-n_{3})}{n}+1\right)\sum_{t\in J_{3}}\epsilon^{\star}+\left(\frac{(n_{2}-n_{3})}{n}-1\right)\sum_{t\in J_{2}}\epsilon^{\star}\right).

Finally, we have that

\displaystyle Q_{n}(T_{3})-Q_{n}(T_{4}^{\star})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2\delta A+\delta^{2}V,

with

A=\frac{(n_{2}-n_{3})}{n}\sum_{J_{1}\cup J_{4}}\epsilon_{t}^{\star}+\left(\frac{(n_{2}-n_{3})}{n}+1\right)\sum_{t\in J_{3}}\epsilon_{t}^{\star}+\left(\frac{(n_{2}-n_{3})}{n}-1\right)\sum_{t\in J_{2}}\epsilon_{t}^{\star},

and

V=n_{2}+n_{3}-\frac{(n_{2}-n_{3})^{2}}{n}>0.

Since
\epsilon_{t}^{\star}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^{\star,2}_{4})
, it is straightforward to see that

A\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^{\star,2}_{4}V),

which concludes the proof.
∎

Appendix B
Calculation of the complete-data information matrix

In this section, we calculate
\mathcal{I}(\widehat{\theta})=\mathbb{E}_{X|Y}[I^{c}(\theta)]\big|_{\theta=\widehat{\theta}}
where

I^{c}(\theta)=-\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial\theta\partial{}^{t}\theta}\log{\mathbb{P}(Y,Z;\theta)},

and we recall that

\displaystyle\log{\mathbb{P}(Y,Z;\theta)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{s=1}^{S}\sum_{k=1}^{4}Z_{k}^{s}\left(\log(\pi_{k})-\frac{n^{s}}{2}\log{(2\pi\sigma_{k}^{2,s})}-\frac{1}{2\sigma_{k}^{2,s}}\|Y^{s}-\mu_{k}^{s}-T^{s}_{k}\delta\|_{2}^{2}\right).

We first focus on the ’diagonal’ term of the information matrix, thus for a second derivative of the complete-data information matrix w.r.t. each parameter:

•

proportion parameters
\pi_{k}
. Since
\sum_{k=1}^{4}\pi_{k}=1
, it is sufficient to differentiate with respect to the first three components. For
k=1,2,3
, we get

\displaystyle\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial\pi_{k}^{2}}\log{\mathbb{P}(Y,Z;\theta)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial\pi_{k}^{2}}\sum_{s=1}^{S}\left(\sum_{k=1}^{3}Z^{s}_{k}\log{\pi_{k}}+Z^{s}_{4}\log{(1-\sum_{k=1}^{3}\pi_{k})}\right)=-\sum_{s=1}^{S}\left(\frac{Z^{s}_{k}}{\pi_{k}^{2}}+\frac{Z^{s}_{4}}{\pi_{4}^{2}}\right),

thus it is straightforward to see that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{X|Y}[I^{c}(\pi)]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{s=1}^{S}\begin{bmatrix}\frac{\tau^{s}_{1}}{\pi_{1}^{2}}+\frac{\tau^{s}_{4}}{\pi_{4}^{2}}&\frac{\tau^{s}_{4}}{\pi_{4}^{2}}&\frac{\tau^{s}_{4}}{\pi_{4}^{2}}\\
\frac{\tau^{s}_{4}}{\pi_{4}^{2}}&\frac{\tau^{s}_{2}}{\pi_{2}^{2}}+\frac{\tau^{s}_{4}}{\pi_{4}^{2}}&\frac{\tau^{s}_{4}}{\pi_{4}^{2}}\\
\frac{\tau^{s}_{4}}{\pi_{4}^{2}}&\frac{\tau^{s}_{4}}{\pi_{4}^{2}}&\frac{\tau^{s}_{3}}{\pi_{3}^{2}}+\frac{\tau^{s}_{4}}{\pi_{4}^{2}},\end{bmatrix}

and since
\widehat{\pi}_{k}=\sum_{s}\tau^{s}_{k}/S
, we obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{X|Y}[I^{c}(\pi)]\big|_{\pi=\widehat{\pi}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle S\begin{bmatrix}\frac{1}{\widehat{\pi}_{1}}+\frac{1}{\widehat{\pi}_{4}}&\frac{1}{\widehat{\pi}_{4}}&\frac{1}{\widehat{\pi}_{4}}\\
\frac{1}{\widehat{\pi}_{4}}&\frac{1}{\widehat{\pi}_{2}}+\frac{1}{\widehat{\pi}_{4}}&\frac{1}{\widehat{\pi}_{4}}\\
\frac{1}{\widehat{\pi}_{4}}&\frac{1}{\widehat{\pi}_{4}}&\frac{1}{\widehat{\pi}_{3}}+\frac{1}{\widehat{\pi}_{4}},\end{bmatrix}

•

jump parameters
\delta
. To obtain the result, simply observe that

\displaystyle\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial\delta^{2}}\log{\mathbb{P}(Y,Z;\theta)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\sum_{s=1}^{S}\sum_{k=1}^{4}\frac{Z^{s}_{k}}{\widehat{\sigma}^{2,s}_{k}}\ {}^{t}{T_{s}^{k}}T_{s}^{k}.

•

mean parameters
\mu_{k}^{s}
. Similarly,

\displaystyle\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial(\mu_{k}^{s})^{2}}\log{\mathbb{P}(Y,Z;\theta)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{n_{s}Z^{s}_{k}}{{\sigma}^{2,s}_{k}}

•

variance parameters. We get

\displaystyle\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial(\sigma_{k}^{2,s})^{2}}\log{\mathbb{P}(Y,Z;\theta)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{Z^{s}_{k}}{2}\left(\frac{n_{s}}{{\sigma}^{4,s}_{k}}-\frac{2}{{\sigma}^{6,s}_{k}}\|Y^{s}-\mu_{k}^{s}-T^{s}_{k}\delta\|_{2}^{2}\right).

Taking the conditional expectation at
\widehat{\theta}
, we obtain

\displaystyle\left(\mathbb{E}_{X|Y}[I^{c}(\sigma^{2})]\big|_{\theta=\widehat{\theta}}\right)_{ks}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{n_{s}\tau^{s}_{k}}{\widehat{\sigma}^{4,s}_{k}}.

For the extra-diagonal of the information matrix, we have the following:

•

for the
(\mu,\sigma^{2})
block,

\displaystyle\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial\mu_{k}^{s}\partial{\sigma}^{2,s}_{k}}\log{\mathbb{P}(Y,Z;\theta)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial\mu_{k}^{s}}\left(\frac{Z^{s}_{k}}{2\sigma^{4,s}_{k}}\|Y^{s}-\mu_{k}^{s}-T^{s}_{k}\delta\|_{2}^{2}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{Z^{s}_{k}}{2\sigma^{4,s}_{k}}\left(-2\sum_{t=1}^{n_{s}}Y^{s}_{t}+2\mu_{k}^{s}n_{s}+2\ {}^{t}\mathbf{1}T_{s}^{k}\delta\right)

By definition of
\widehat{\mu}_{k}^{s}
, we easily obtain that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{X|Y}[I^{c}(\mu,\sigma^{2})]\big|_{\theta=\widehat{\theta}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 0

•

for the
(\delta,\mu)
block,

\displaystyle\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial\delta\partial\mu_{k}^{s}}\log{\mathbb{P}(Y,Z;\theta)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial\delta}\left(-\frac{Z^{s}_{k}}{2\sigma^{2,s}_{k}}\left(-2\ {}^{t}(Y^{s}_{t})T_{s}^{k}+2\mu_{k}^{s}n_{s}+2\ {}^{t}\mathbf{1}T_{s}^{k}\delta\right)\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{Z^{s}_{k}\ {}^{t}\mathbf{1}T_{s}^{k}}{\sigma^{2,s}_{k}},

thus

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{X|Y}[I^{c}(\delta,\mu)]\big|_{\theta=\widehat{\theta}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\tau^{s}_{k}\ {}^{t}\mathbf{1}T_{s}^{k}}{\widehat{\sigma}^{2,s}_{k}}

•

for the
(\delta,\sigma^{2})
block,

\displaystyle\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial\delta\partial\sigma_{k}^{2,s}}\log{\mathbb{P}(Y,Z;\theta)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial\delta}Z^{s}_{k}\left(-\frac{n_{s}}{2\sigma^{2,s}_{k}}+\frac{1}{2\sigma^{4,s}_{k}}\|Y^{s}-\mu_{k}^{s}-T^{s}_{k}\delta\|_{2}^{2}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{Z^{s}_{k}}{\sigma^{4,s}_{k}}\left(-\ {}^{t}(Y^{s}_{t})T_{s}^{k}+\mu_{k}^{s}\ {}^{t}\mathbf{1}T_{s}^{k}+\delta\ {}^{t}(T_{s}^{k})T_{s}^{k}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{Z^{s}_{k}}{\sigma^{4,s}_{k}}\left(\ {}^{t}(Y^{s}_{t})-\mu_{k}^{s}-\delta\ {}^{t}(T_{s}^{k})\right)\ T_{s}^{k}

thus

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{X|Y}[I^{c}(\delta,\sigma^{2})]\big|_{\theta=\widehat{\theta}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\tau^{s}_{k}}{\widehat{\sigma}^{4,s}_{k}}\left({}^{t}(Y^{s}_{t})-\widehat{\mu}_{k}^{s}-\widehat{\delta}\ {}^{t}(T_{s}^{k})\right)T_{s}^{k}
