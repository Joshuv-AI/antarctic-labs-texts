Uniformity of Strong Asymptotics in Angelesco Systems
Maxim L. YATTSELEV

Abstract
Let μ 1 \mu_{1} and μ 2 \mu_{2} be two complex-valued Borel measures on the real line such that supp ⁡ μ 1 = [ α 1 , β 1 ] < supp ⁡ μ 2 = [ α 2 , β 2 ] \operatorname{supp}\mu_{1}=[\alpha_{1},\beta_{1}]<\operatorname{supp}\mu_{2}=[\alpha_{2},\beta_{2}] and d μ i ( x ) = − ρ i ( x ) d x / 2 π i {\rm d}\mu_{i}(x)=-\rho_{i}(x){\rm d}x/2\pi\mathrm{i} , where ρ i ​ ( x ) \rho_{i}(x) is the restriction to [ α i , β i ] [\alpha_{i},\beta_{i}] of a function non-vanishing and holomorphic in some neighborhood of [ α i , β i ] [\alpha_{i},\beta_{i}] . Strong asymptotics of multiple orthogonal polynomials is considered as their multi-indices ( n 1 , n 2 ) (n_{1},n_{2}) tend to infinity in both coordinates. The main goal of this work is to show that the error terms in the asymptotic formulae are uniform with respect to min ⁡ { n 1 , n 2 } \min\{n_{1},n_{2}\} .

1.1
Multiple orthogonal polynomials

Let
\mu_{1}
and
\mu_{2}
be two complex-valued Borel measures on the real line and
{\vec{n}}=(n_{1},n_{2})
be a multi-index, where
n_{1}
,
n_{2}
are non-negative integers. A non-identically zero polynomial
P_{{\vec{n}}}(x)
of degree at most
|{\vec{n}}|:=n_{1}+n_{2}
is called a type II multiple orthogonal polynomial with respect to a system of measures
(\mu_{1},\mu_{2})
if it satisfies

\int P_{\vec{n}}(x)x^{l}{\rm d}\mu_{i}(x)=0,\qquad l\in\{0,\dots,n_{i}-1\},\quad i\in\{1,2\}.

(1.1)

In what follows, we take
P_{\vec{n}}(x)
to be the monic polynomial of minimal degree satisfying (
1.1
), which makes it unique. Type I multiple orthogonal polynomials are not identically zero polynomial coefficients of the linear form

\displaystyle Q_{\vec{n}}(x):=A_{\vec{n}}^{(1)}(x){\rm d}\mu_{1}(x)+A_{\vec{n}}^{(2)}(x){\rm d}\mu_{2}(x),\qquad\deg A_{\vec{n}}^{(i)}<n_{i},

\displaystyle\int x^{l}Q_{\vec{n}}(x)=0,\qquad l<|{\vec{n}}|-1,\qquad A_{(0,1)}^{(1)}=A_{(1,0)}^{(2)}\equiv 0.

(1.2)

It is known
[
17
, Section 23.1]
that when
\deg P_{\vec{n}}=|{\vec{n}}|
, in which case the multi-index
{\vec{n}}
is called
normal
, the linear form
Q_{\vec{n}}(x)
is defined uniquely up to multiplication by a constant. In this case, it is customary to normalize it by requiring

\int x^{|{\vec{n}}|-1}Q_{{\vec{n}}}(x)=1.

(1.3)

The polynomials
A_{\vec{n}}^{(i)}(x)
are no longer monic and their leading coefficients, which we denote by
1/h_{{\vec{n}}-\vec{e}_{i},i}
, are closely related to the type II polynomials
P_{\vec{n}}(x)
, where
\vec{e}_{1}=(1,0)
and
\vec{e}_{2}=(0,1)
. Indeed, it holds that

\displaystyle h_{{\vec{n}}-\vec{e}_{i},i}

\displaystyle=h_{{\vec{n}}-\vec{e}_{i},i}\int x^{|{\vec{n}}|-1}Q_{\vec{n}}(x)=h_{{\vec{n}}-\vec{e}_{i},i}\int P_{{\vec{n}}-\vec{e}_{i}}(x)Q_{\vec{n}}(x)

\displaystyle=h_{{\vec{n}}-\vec{e}_{i},i}\int P_{{\vec{n}}-\vec{e}_{i}}(x)A_{\vec{n}}^{(i)}(x){\rm d}\mu_{i}(x)=\int P_{{\vec{n}}-\vec{e}_{i}}(x)x^{n_{i}-1}{\rm d}\mu_{i}(x)

(1.4)

as follows from (
1.1
)–(
1.3
). It is known
[
17
, Theorem 23.1.11]
that if indices
{\vec{n}}
and
{\vec{n}}+\vec{e}_{i}
are normal, multiple orthogonal polynomials satisfy nearest-neighbor recurrence relations of the form

\displaystyle xP_{\vec{n}}(x)=P_{{\vec{n}}+\vec{e}_{i}}(x)+b_{{\vec{n}},i}P_{\vec{n}}(x)+a_{{\vec{n}},1}P_{{\vec{n}}-\vec{e}_{1}}(x)+a_{{\vec{n}},2}P_{{\vec{n}}-\vec{e}_{2}}(x),

\displaystyle xQ_{\vec{n}}(x)=Q_{{\vec{n}}-\vec{e}_{i}}(x)+b_{{\vec{n}}-\vec{e}_{i},i}Q_{\vec{n}}(x)+a_{{\vec{n}},1}Q_{{\vec{n}}+\vec{e}_{1}}(x)+a_{{\vec{n}},2}Q_{{\vec{n}}+\vec{e}_{2}}(x).

(1.5)

All these definitions can be formulated for a collection of more than two measures, however, we shall not pursue such an extension here.

1.2
Angelesco systems

Assume that each measure
\mu_{i}
is compactly supported and let
\Delta_{i}
be the smallest closed interval containing the support of
\mu_{i}
. If
\Delta_{1}\cap\Delta_{2}=\varnothing
, then the pair
(\mu_{1},\mu_{2})
is said to form an Angelesco system. Angelesco himself considered the case of non-negative measures and had shown that such systems are always
perfect
(all multi-indices are normal)
[
1
]
. In what follows, we are only interested in the case where

\operatorname{supp}\mu_{i}=\Delta_{i}=:[\alpha_{i},\beta_{i}]\qquad\text{and}\qquad{\rm d}\mu_{i}(x)=-\rho_{i}(x)\frac{{\rm d}x}{2\pi\mathrm{i}},

(1.6)

i.e., each
\mu_{i}
is supported by an interval and is absolutely continuous with respect to the Lebesgue measure. We allow densities
\rho_{i}(x)
to be complex-valued
1
1

1

The specific choice of the normalization in (
1.6
) is there for two reasons. First, under such a normalization the Markov function of
\mu_{i}
becomes the Cauchy transform of
\rho_{i}
, i.e.,
\int\frac{{\rm d}\mu_{i}(x)}{z-x}=\int\frac{\rho_{i}(x)}{x-z}\frac{{\rm d}x}{2\pi\mathrm{i}}
. Second, when
\mu_{i}
is a positive measure, the density
\log(\rho_{i}w_{c,i+})
, appearing in (
1.21
), is a real-valued function.
and assume for definiteness that
{\beta_{1}<\alpha_{2}}
.

When each
\mathrm{i}\rho_{i}(x)
is positive almost everywhere on the corresponding interval
\Delta_{i}
, the zero distribution of the polynomials
P_{\vec{n}}(x)
and their
|{\vec{n}}|
-th root (i.e., weak) asymptotic behavior were studied in
[
16
]
along subsequences of indices that satisfy

\lim_{|{\vec{n}}|\to\infty}n_{1}/|{\vec{n}}|\qquad\text{exists and belongs to}\ (0,1).

(1.7)

When the functions
\mathrm{i}\rho_{i}(x)
are non-negative with integrable logarithms (Szegő class), strong asymptotics of these polynomials along the diagonal sequence
n_{1}=n_{2}
was obtained in
[
2
]
, see also
[
19
,
23
]
, and more generally under assumption (
1.7
) in
[
5
]
. When each function
\rho_{i}(x)
is the product of a restriction to
\Delta_{i}
of a non-vanishing (complex-valued) holomorphic function and a Fisher–Hartwig weight, the strong asymptotics of type II polynomials along subsequences satisfying (
1.7
) was derived in
[
27
]
. Asymptotics of type I polynomials as well as of the recurrence coefficients was later deduced in
[
3
]
, but just for weights that are restrictions of holomorphic functions only. Moreover, assuming more stringently that each density
\rho_{i}(x)
is a restriction of a holomorphic function and is positive on
\Delta_{i}
while allowing the limit in (
1.7
) to be
0
or
1
under the additional assumption

\varepsilon_{\vec{n}}:=1/\min\{n_{1},n_{2}\}\to 0\qquad\text{as}\ |{\vec{n}}|\to\infty,

(1.8)

strong asymptotics of the polynomials of both types and the asymptotics of their recurrence coefficients were derived in
[
4
]
. The goal of this work is to show that the error rates obtained in
[
4
]
can be made uniform in
{\vec{n}}
; that is, the asymptotic formulae can be derived solely under condition (
1.8
), no assumption on the existence of the limit in (
1.7
) is needed. We shall assume that the densities
\rho_{i}(x)
are restrictions of analytic, non-vanishing, and in general complex-valued functions. Analyticity assumption can be relaxed, but this will be addressed in a subsequent publication.

1.3
Riemann surfaces

The functions describing strong asymptotics of multiple orthogonal polynomials naturally live on a sequences of Riemann surfaces. To describe these surfaces, we need to start with the already mentioned work by Gonchar and Rakhmanov
[
16
]
. There, assuming (
1.6
) and that each
\mathrm{i}\rho_{i}(x)
is almost everywhere positive function on
\Delta_{i}
, it was shown that if a subsequence of multi-indices satisfies (
1.7
) and
c
is the limit, then the normalized counting measures of the zeros of
P_{\vec{n}}(x)
converge weak
∗
to a certain measure
\omega_{c}
and

|{\vec{n}}|^{-1}\log|P_{\vec{n}}(z)|=-(1+o(1))V^{\omega_{c}}(z)

locally uniformly in
\overline{\mathbb{C}}\setminus(\Delta_{1}\cup\Delta_{2})
along this subsequence of multi-indices, where
V^{\omega}(z):=-\int\log|z-x|{\rm d}\omega(x)
is the logarithmic potential of a measure
\omega
and
\omega_{c}:=\omega_{c,1}+\omega_{c,2}
with
\omega_{c,1}
,
\omega_{c,2}
being the unique pair of measures such that
|\omega_{c,1}|=c
,
|\omega_{c,2}|=1-c
(here,
|\omega|
is the total mass
\omega
),
\operatorname{supp}\omega_{c,i}\subseteq\Delta_{i}
, and

\begin{cases}\ell_{c,i}-V^{\omega_{c}+\omega_{c,i}}(x)\equiv 0,&x\in\operatorname{supp}\omega_{c,i},\\
\ell_{c,i}-V^{\omega_{c}+\omega_{c,i}}(x)<0,&x\in\Delta_{i}\setminus\operatorname{supp}\omega_{c,i},\end{cases}

i\in\{1,2\}
, for some constants
\ell_{c,1}
,
\ell_{c,2}
(the measures
\omega_{c,1}
,
\omega_{c,2}
can also be defined through a certain vector energy minimization problem).

What is of main importance to us from the above results are the supports of the vector equilibrium measures
\omega_{c,1}
and
\omega_{c,2}
. It was explained in
[
16
]
that

\Delta_{c,i}:=\operatorname{supp}\omega_{c,i}=[\alpha_{c,i},\beta_{c,i}]\subseteq\Delta_{i}=[\alpha_{i},\beta_{i}]

i\in\{1,2\}
, where
\alpha_{c,1}:=\alpha_{1}
and
\beta_{c,2}:=\beta_{2}
for any
c\in(0,1)
. However, it is possible that
\beta_{c,1}<\beta_{1}
or
\alpha_{c,2}>\alpha_{2}
(this is so-called
pushing effect
). In fact, it is known
[
4
, Propositions 4.1 and 4.2]
that there exist
0<c^{*}<c^{**}<1
such that

\begin{cases}\beta_{c,1}<\beta_{1},&c<c^{*},\\
\beta_{c,1}=\beta_{1},&c\geq c^{*},\end{cases}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\begin{cases}\alpha_{c,2}=\alpha_{2},&c\leq c^{**},\\
\alpha_{c,2}>\alpha_{2},&c>c^{**}.\end{cases}

(1.9)

Moreover,
\beta_{c,1}
is a continuous strictly increasing function of
c
on
[0,c^{*}]
with
\beta_{0,1}:=\alpha_{1}
while
\alpha_{c,2}
is a continuous strictly increasing function of
c
on
[c^{**},1]
with
\alpha_{1,2}:=\beta_{2}
. It is also known that the constants
\ell_{c,1}
and
\ell_{c,2}
are continuous functions of
c
and so are the measures
\omega_{c,1}
and
\omega_{c,2}
in the sense of weak
∗
convergence of measures.

Realization of the surface
\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{S}}_{c}
(the notation is meant to emphasize that pushing effect happens on one interval only, i.e.,
\beta_{c,1}<\beta_{1}

\alpha_{c,2}=\alpha_{2}
for this value of
c

Given
c\in(0,1)
, let
\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{S}}_{c}
be a 3-sheeted Riemann surface realized as follows. Define

\Delta_{c}:=\Delta_{c,1}\cup\Delta_{c,2},\qquad E_{c}:=E_{c,1}\cup E_{c,2},\qquad\text{where}\quad E_{c,i}:=\{\alpha_{c,i},\beta_{c,i}\}.

Denote by
\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{S}}_{c}^{(0)}
,
\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{S}}_{c}^{(1)}
, and
\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{S}}_{c}^{(2)}
, three copies of
\overline{\mathbb{C}}
cut along
\Delta_{c}
,
\Delta_{c,1}
, and
\Delta_{c,2}
, respectively. These copies are then glued to each other crosswise along the corresponding cuts, see Figure
1
. It can be easily seen from the Riemann–-Hurwitz formula that
\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{S}}_{c}
has genus
0
. We denote by
\pi
the natural projection from
\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{S}}_{c}
to
\overline{\mathbb{C}}
and employ the notation
{\boldsymbol{z}}
for a generic point on
\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{S}}_{c}
with
{\pi({\boldsymbol{z}})=z}
. We let
\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{1}=\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{c,1},\boldsymbol{\beta}_{c,1},\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{c,2},\boldsymbol{\beta}_{2}=\boldsymbol{\beta}_{c,2}
to stand for the ramification points of
\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{S}}_{c}
with natural projections
\alpha_{1}
,
\beta_{c,1}
,
\alpha_{c,2}
,
\beta_{2}
, respectively. We set

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{E}_{c}:=\boldsymbol{E}_{c,1}\cup\boldsymbol{E}_{c,2},\qquad\boldsymbol{E}_{c,i}=\{\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{c,i},\boldsymbol{\beta}_{c,i}\},

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{c}:=\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{c,1}\cup\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{c,2},\qquad\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{c,i}:=\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{S}}_{c}^{(0)}\cap\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{S}}_{c}^{(i)}=\partial\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{S}}_{c}^{(i)}.

Notice that
\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{c,i}\setminus\boldsymbol{E}_{c,i}
is a two-to-one cover of
\Delta_{c,i}^{\circ}:=(\alpha_{c,i},\beta_{c,i})
.

We call a linear combination
\sum m_{i}{\boldsymbol{z}}_{i}
,
m_{i}\in\mathbb{Z}
, a divisor. Its degree is defined as
\sum m_{i}
. We say that
\sum m_{i}{\boldsymbol{z}}_{i}
is a zero/pole divisor of a meromorphic function if this function has a zero at
{\boldsymbol{z}}_{i}
of multiplicity
m_{i}
when
m_{i}>0
, a pole at
{\boldsymbol{z}}_{i}
of order
-m_{i}
when
m_{i}<0
, and has no other zeros or poles in the domain of its definition. Zero/pole divisors of rational functions on
\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{S}}_{c}
necessarily have degree zero. Conversely, since
\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{S}}_{c}
has genus
0
, any degree zero divisor is a zero/pole divisor of a rational function, which is unique up to multiplication by a constant. For any function
G({\boldsymbol{z}})
, defined on
\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{S}}_{c}\setminus\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{c}
, we denote by

G^{(k)}(z):=G({\boldsymbol{z}}),\qquad{\boldsymbol{z}}\in\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{S}}_{c}^{(k)}\setminus\partial\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{S}}_{c}^{(k)},\qquad k\in\{0,1,2\},

its pull-back under the natural projection from the
k
-th sheet to the cut complex plane.

1.4
Conformal maps

To proceed, we introduce a certain conformal map of
\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{S}}_{c}
onto
\overline{\mathbb{C}}
, say
\chi_{c}({\boldsymbol{z}})
, which is a rational function with one pole and one zero, defined by the relation

\chi_{c}^{(0)}(z)=z+\mathcal{O}\bigl(z^{-1}\bigr)\qquad\text{as}\ z\to\infty.

(1.10)

Since prescribing the absence of a constant term around
\infty^{(0)}
(
\infty^{(k)}
is a point on
\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{S}}_{c}^{(k)}
whose natural projection is the point at infinity) is equivalent to prescribing a zero, the function
\chi_{c}({\boldsymbol{z}})
is uniquely determined by (
1.10
). Further, let the numbers
A_{c,1}
,
A_{c,2}
,
B_{c,1}
,
B_{c,2}
be defined by

\chi_{c}^{(i)}(z)=:B_{c,i}+A_{c,i}z^{-1}+\mathcal{O}\bigl(z^{-2}\bigr)\qquad\text{as}\ z\to\infty,\ i\in\{1,2\}.

(1.11)

Since
\chi_{c}({\boldsymbol{z}})
is a conformal map, the numbers
A_{c,i}
are necessarily non-zero (otherwise
\chi_{c}({\boldsymbol{z}})-B_{c,i}
would have had a double zero). Moreover, by tracing the image of
\pi^{-1}(\mathbb{R})\setminus\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{c}
under
\chi_{c}({\boldsymbol{z}})
, which is necessarily equal to the real line, one gets that each
A_{c,i}>0
and
B_{c,2}>B_{c,1}
. Set

\displaystyle\varphi_{i}(z):=(z-(\beta_{i}+\alpha_{i})/2+w_{i}(z))/2,\qquad w_{i}(z):=\sqrt{(z-\alpha_{i})(z-\beta_{i})}=z+\mathcal{O}(1)

(1.12)

to be the branches holomorphic off
\Delta_{i}
,
i\in\{1,2\}
(
\varphi_{i}(z)
is the conformal map of the complement of
\Delta_{i}
onto the complement of
\{|z|>(\beta_{i}-\alpha_{i})/4\}
that behaves like
z
at infinity). It was also shown in
[
4
, Proposition 2.1]
that
A_{c,i}
and
B_{c,i}
are (real-valued) continuous functions of the parameter
c\in(0,1)
such that

\lim_{c\to 0}\begin{cases}A_{c,2}=\big[(\beta_{2}-\alpha_{2})/4\big]^{2}=:A_{0,2},\\
B_{c,2}=(\beta_{2}+\alpha_{2})/2=:B_{0,2},\\
A_{c,1}=0=:A_{0,1},\\
B_{c,1}=B_{0,2}+\varphi_{2}(\alpha_{1})=:B_{0,1},\end{cases}

(1.13)

and the analogous limits hold when
c\to 1
.

In the limit
c\to 0
(similar considerations apply to the case
c\to 1
), the Riemann surface
\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{S}}_{c}
becomes disconnected: one connected component is a copy of
\overline{\mathbb{C}}
and the other one is two copies of
\overline{\mathbb{C}}
glued together crosswise across
\Delta_{2}
. As expected, in this case the conformal maps
\chi_{c}({\boldsymbol{z}})
converge to a conformal map of the second connected component onto the Riemann sphere. More precisely, it was shown in
[
4
, equation (5.2)]
that

\chi_{c}({\boldsymbol{z}})-B_{c,2}=(1+o(1))\begin{cases}\varphi_{2}(z),&{\boldsymbol{z}}\in\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{S}}_{c}^{(0)},\\
\varphi_{2}(\alpha_{1}),&{\boldsymbol{z}}\in\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{S}}_{c}^{(1)},\\
\left(\dfrac{\beta_{2}-\alpha_{2}}{4}\right)^{2}/\varphi_{2}(z),&{\boldsymbol{z}}\in\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{S}}_{c}^{(2)},\end{cases}

uniformly on
\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{S}}_{c}
as
c\to 0
(the top two limits in (
1.13
) follow immediately from this formula).

The vector equilibrium measures discussed before admit the following explicit formulae. Let
\Pi_{c}({\boldsymbol{z}})
be the derivative of
\chi_{c}({\boldsymbol{z}})
, that is, the derivative of
\chi_{c}^{(k)}(z)
is equal to
\Pi_{c}^{(k)}(z)
, for each
{k\in\{0,1,2\}}
. Equivalently,
\Pi_{c}({\boldsymbol{z}})
is a rational function on
\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{S}}_{c}
with the zero/pole divisor and normalization given by

2\bigl(\infty^{(1)}+\infty^{(2)}\bigr)-\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{1}-\boldsymbol{\beta}_{c,1}-\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{c,2}-\boldsymbol{\beta}_{2}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\Pi_{c}^{(0)}(\infty)=1.

(1.14)

Observe that
\lim_{z\to\infty}z^{2}\Pi_{c}^{(i)}(z)=-A_{c,i}
,
i\in\{1,2\}
. Set

h_{c}({\boldsymbol{z}}):=\Pi_{c}({\boldsymbol{z}})\frac{\chi_{c}({\boldsymbol{z}})-(1-c)B_{c,1}-cB_{c,2}}{(\chi_{c}({\boldsymbol{z}})-B_{c,1})(\chi_{c}({\boldsymbol{z}})-B_{c,2})}.

(1.15)

Then
h_{c}({\boldsymbol{z}})
is a rational function on
\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{S}}_{c}
with the zero/pole divisor given by

\infty^{(0)}+\infty^{(1)}+\infty^{(2)}+{\boldsymbol{z}}_{c}-\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{1}-\boldsymbol{\beta}_{c,1}-\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{c,2}-\boldsymbol{\beta}_{2},

(1.16)

where
{\boldsymbol{z}}_{c}
is some point on
\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{S}}_{c}
whose existence is guaranteed by the fact that zero/pole divisors of rational functions on compact Riemann surfaces must have degree zero. Moreover, it holds that

\lim_{z\to\infty}zh_{c}^{(0)}(z)=1,\qquad\lim_{z\to\infty}zh_{c}^{(1)}(z)=-c,\qquad\text{and}\qquad\lim_{z\to\infty}zh_{c}^{(2)}(z)=c-1

(1.17)

(this, in particular, means that all three branches of
h_{c}({\boldsymbol{z}})
add up to the identically zero function because their sum must be an entire function that is equal to zero at infinity). The function
h_{c}({\boldsymbol{z}})
is uniquely determined by (
1.16
) and (
1.17
). Indeed, the ratio of any two such functions (that is, functions corresponding to possibly different points
{\boldsymbol{z}}_{c}
) minus
1
must have at most one necessarily simple pole by (
1.16
) and at least three zeros (at the points on top of infinity) by (
1.17
), which is only possible for the identically zero function. Now, it follows from
[
27
, Proposition 2.3]
, where the functions
h_{c}({\boldsymbol{z}})
were introduced differently, that

{\boldsymbol{z}}_{c}\in\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{S}}_{c}^{(0)}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\begin{cases}{\boldsymbol{z}}_{c}=\boldsymbol{\beta}_{c,1},&c\in(0,c^{*}],\\
\pi({\boldsymbol{z}}_{c})\in(\beta_{1},\alpha_{2}),&c\in(c^{*},c^{**}),\\
{\boldsymbol{z}}_{c}=\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{c,2},&c\in[c^{*},1)\end{cases}

(1.18)

(if either
{\boldsymbol{z}}_{c}=\boldsymbol{\beta}_{c,1}
or
{\boldsymbol{z}}_{c}=\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{c,2}
, then these points cancel each other out in (
1.16
) and
h_{c}({\boldsymbol{z}})
has only three poles and three zeros). Finally, the following claim can be found in
[
4
, Section 4]
. It holds that

{\rm d}\omega_{c,i}(x)=\bigl(h_{c+}^{(i)}(x)-h_{c-}^{(i)}(x)\bigr)\frac{{\rm d}x}{2\pi\mathrm{i}},\qquad x\in\Delta_{c,i},\ i\in\{1,2\}.

(1.19)

Notice that
h_{c\pm}^{(i)}(x)=h^{(0)}_{c\mp}(x)
for
x\in\Delta_{c,i}
. Using the limiting behavior of
\chi_{c}^{(0)}(z)
as
c\to 0
discussed above, one can show that
h_{c}({\boldsymbol{z}})
converges to
1/w_{2}(z)
on the zero-th sheet as
c\to 0
. This, in particular, implies that
\omega_{c,2}
converge to the arcsine distribution on
\Delta_{2}
as
c\to 0
(of course, similar limits take place as
c\to 1
).

1.5
Main asymptotic terms

The following construction was carried out in
[
27
, Section 6]
. Similarly to (
1.12
), for each
c\in(0,1)
, set

w_{c,i}(z):=\sqrt{(z-\alpha_{c,i})(z-\beta_{c,i})},\qquad z\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\Delta_{c,i},\ i\in\{1,2\},

(1.20)

to be the branch normalized so that
w_{c,i}(z)=z+\mathcal{O}(1)
as
z\to\infty
. Denote by
\mathcal{C}_{\boldsymbol{z}}
the discontinuous Cauchy kernel on
\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{S}}_{c}
, that is,
\mathcal{C}_{\boldsymbol{z}}
is the third kind differential with three simple poles, located at
{\boldsymbol{z}}
and the other two points with the same natural projection, and the residues
2
at
{\boldsymbol{z}}
and
-1
at the other points. Put

S_{c}({\boldsymbol{z}}):=\exp\left\{\frac{1}{6\pi\mathrm{i}}\sum_{i=1}^{2}\int_{\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{c,i}}\log(\rho_{i}w_{c,i+})\mathcal{C}_{\boldsymbol{z}}\right\},

(1.21)

where we choose a continuous determination of
\log\rho_{i}(x)
and set

\log w_{c,i+}(x):=\log|w_{c,i}(x)|+\pi\mathrm{i}/2

(when
\mu_{i}
is a positive measure, we can take
\log\rho_{i}(x)=\log|\rho_{i}(x)|-\pi\mathrm{i}/2
, see (
1.6
), so that

Let

3
Conformal maps

In what follows, it will be sometimes useful for us to recall that

\lim_{c\to 0}\frac{|\Delta_{c,1}|}{c}=4|w_{2}(\alpha_{1})|\qquad\text{and}\qquad\lim_{c\to 1}\frac{|\Delta_{c,2}|}{1-c}=4|w_{1}(\beta_{2})|,

\displaystyle\frac{z_{1}(z_{1}-\epsilon_{c})^{2}G_{c}(z_{1})-z_{2}(z_{2}-\epsilon_{c})^{2}G_{c}(z_{2})}{z_{1}-z_{2}}

\displaystyle={}

\displaystyle\epsilon_{c}^{2}G_{c}(0)+z_{1}\frac{(z_{1}-\epsilon_{c})^{2}G_{c}(z_{1})-\epsilon_{c}^{2}G_{c}(0)}{z_{1}}

\displaystyle+z_{2}\frac{(z_{1}-\epsilon_{c})^{2}G_{c}(z_{1})-(z_{2}-\epsilon_{c})^{2}G_{c}(z_{2})}{z_{1}-z_{2}}.

Let
z_{1}
,
z_{2}
be in
\{|z|\leq A\epsilon_{c}\}
. Since
\epsilon_{c}
,
A\leq 1
, we get that

\left|\frac{(z_{1}-\epsilon_{c})^{2}G_{c}(z_{1})-\epsilon_{c}^{2}G_{c}(0)}{z_{1}}\right|\leq|z_{1}G_{c}(z_{1})|+2\epsilon_{c}|G_{c}(z_{1})|+\epsilon_{c}^{2}\left|\frac{G_{c}(z_{1})-G_{c}(0)}{z_{1}}\right|\leq 4K\epsilon_{c}.

Similarly, we obtain that

\displaystyle\left|\frac{(z_{1}-\epsilon_{c})^{2}G_{c}(z_{1})-(z_{2}-\epsilon_{c})^{2}G_{c}(z_{2})}{z_{1}-z_{2}}\right|

\displaystyle\qquad\leq|z_{1}+z_{2}||G_{c}(z_{1})|+|z_{2}|^{2}\left|\frac{G_{c}(z_{1})-G_{c}(z_{2})}{z_{1}-z_{2}}\right|

\displaystyle\qquad\phantom{\leq}{}+2\epsilon_{c}\left|\frac{z_{1}G_{c}(z_{1})-z_{2}G_{c}(z_{2})}{z_{1}-z_{2}}\right|+\epsilon_{c}^{2}\left|\frac{G_{c}(z_{1})-G_{c}(z_{2})}{z_{1}-z_{2}}\right|\leq 6K\epsilon_{c}.

Hence, we can conclude that

|D(z_{1},z_{2})|\geq\epsilon_{c}^{2}G_{c}(0)-10AK\epsilon_{c}^{2}\geq\epsilon_{c}^{2}G_{c}(0)/2>0
.
That is, we have shown that
z(z-\epsilon_{c})^{2}G_{c}(z)
is conformal in
{\{|z|<A\epsilon_{c}\}}
. Thus,
\zeta_{c,\beta_{1}}(z)
is conformal in
{\{|z-\beta_{1}|<A\epsilon_{c}\}}
and therefore
\delta_{\beta_{1}}(c)\geq A\epsilon_{c}
. In view of the first claim of the lemma, the second one follows.
∎

Notice that we can assume that
\delta_{\beta_{1}}(c)
is an increasing function of
c\in(c^{*},1]
. Indeed, this always can be achieved by replacing
\delta_{\beta_{1}}(c)
with

will not change since

for some

, where the last inequality holds since

is an increasing function of

o(1)=(c({\vec{n}})-c({\vec{n}}-\vec{e}_{i}))\left((n_{i}-1)\frac{A_{\xi({\vec{n}}),i}^{\prime}}{A_{\xi({\vec{n}}),i}}+n_{3-i}\frac{B_{\eta({\vec{n}})}^{\prime}}{B_{\eta({\vec{n}})}}\right)

as
|{\vec{n}}|\to\infty
,
{\vec{n}}\in\mathcal{N}_{c}
, for some
\xi({\vec{n}})
,
\eta({\vec{n}})
that lie between
c({\vec{n}})
and
c({\vec{n}}-\vec{e}_{i})
. Notice that

c({\vec{n}})-c({\vec{n}}-\vec{e}_{i})=\frac{(-1)^{i-1}n_{3-i}}{|{\vec{n}}|(|{\vec{n}}|-1)}.

Since
n_{3-i}/|{\vec{n}}|
approaches either
c
or
1-c
along

, it therefore holds that

o(1)=\frac{n_{i}-1}{|{\vec{n}}|}\frac{A_{\xi({\vec{n}}),i}^{\prime}}{A_{\xi({\vec{n}}),i}}+\frac{n_{3-i}}{|{\vec{n}}|}\frac{B_{\eta({\vec{n}})}^{\prime}}{B_{\eta({\vec{n}})}}

as
|{\vec{n}}|\to\infty
,
{\vec{n}}\in\mathcal{N}_{c}
. Because
\xi({\vec{n}}),\eta({\vec{n}})\to c
as
|{\vec{n}}|\to\infty
,
{\vec{n}}\in\mathcal{N}_{c}
, the first two differential equation in the top line of (

) follow by taking the limit in the above equality.

9.2
Proof of (
1.35
)

In this subsection, we prove (
1.35
) and the last equality in the top line of (
1.36
). Let us set
{R_{1}(c):=c^{-2}A_{c,1}}
and
R_{2}(c):=(1-c)^{-2}A_{c,2}
, which are continuous non-vanishing functions of the parameter
c\in[0,1]
, see (
1.34
), that are continuously differentiable on
(0,1)\setminus\{c^{*},c^{**}\}
. It follows from (
1.33
) that

R_{1}^{\prime}(c)+R_{2}^{\prime}(c)=2B_{c}B_{c}^{\prime}.

(9.4)

On the other hand, expressing
A_{c,i}^{\prime}
through
A_{c,i}
,
B_{c}
, and
B_{c}^{\prime}
by using the first two equalities in the top line of (
1.36
) together with adding these expressions up gives

A_{c,1}^{\prime}+A_{c,2}^{\prime}=-\frac{B_{c}^{\prime}}{B_{c}}\left(\frac{1-c}{c}A_{c,1}+\frac{c}{1-c}A_{c,2}\right)=-c(1-c)B_{c}B_{c}^{\prime},

(9.5)

where we also used (
1.33
) for the last step. Combining equations (
9.4
) and (
9.5
) to eliminate
B_{c}B_{c}^{\prime}
as well as dividing by
R_{1}(c)
yields

(1-c)(2-c)R(c)\frac{R_{2}^{\prime}(c)}{R_{2}(c)}+c(1+c)\frac{R_{1}^{\prime}(c)}{R_{1}(c)}=-4c+4(1-c)R(c).

(9.6)

On the other hand, the first two relations in top line of (
1.36
) can be rewritten as

(1-c)^{2}\frac{R_{2}^{\prime}(c)}{R_{2}(c)}-c^{2}\frac{R_{1}^{\prime}(c)}{R_{1}(c)}=2.

(9.7)

Equations (
9.6
) and (
9.7
) form a two by two linear system whose solution is given by

\frac{R_{2}^{\prime}(c)}{R_{2}(c)}=2\frac{2c+1+2cR(c)}{1-c^{2}+c(2-c)R(c)}

(9.8)

and

\frac{R_{1}^{\prime}(c)}{R_{1}(c)}=-2\frac{2(1-c)+(3-2c)R(c)}{1-c^{2}+c(2-c)R(c)}.

(9.9)

Equation (
1.35
) now follows by taking the difference between (
9.8
) and (
9.9
). Finally, the last equality in the top line of (
1.36
) comes from (
9.9
) and the identity

\frac{B_{c}^{\prime}}{B_{c}}=-\frac{c}{1-c}\frac{A_{c,1}^{\prime}}{A_{c,1}}=-\frac{2}{1-c}-\frac{c}{1-c}\frac{R_{1}^{\prime}(c)}{R_{1}(c)}.

9.3
Differentiability of
\boldsymbol{\chi_{c}}
and
\boldsymbol{\beta_{c,1}}

In this subsection, we establish several facts that will be needed in the remainder of the proof of Theorems
1.10
and
1.11
. We investigate only the case
c\in(0,c^{*})
as the behavior for
c\in(c^{**},1)
can be deduced similarly.

Since the symmetric functions of the branches of
\chi_{c}({\boldsymbol{z}})
are necessarily polynomials, we can use (
1.10
) and (
1.11
) to derive the cubic equation satisfied by
\chi_{c}
. This equation, after some straightforward algebraic simplifications, can be written as

z=\chi_{c}({\boldsymbol{z}})+\frac{A_{c,1}}{\chi_{c}({\boldsymbol{z}})-B_{c,1}}+\frac{A_{c,2}}{\chi_{c}({\boldsymbol{z}})-B_{c,2}}.

(9.10)

Given
s\in\overline{\mathbb{C}}
, the above equation can be interpreted as

z(c,s):=\pi\bigl(\chi_{c}^{-1}(s)\bigr)=s+\frac{A_{c,1}}{s-B_{c,1}}+\frac{A_{c,2}}{s-B_{c,2}}.

(9.11)

In particular, we see from (
1.35
), the top line of (
1.36
), and the remark made after Theorem
1.11
that
\partial_{c}z(c,s)
exists and is locally uniformly bounded in
s\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\{B_{c,1},B_{c,2}\}
(this estimate is also uniform in
c\in(0,c^{*})
if
s
stays away from
\{B_{c,i}:c\in[0,c^{*}]\}
).

As pointed out right before (
1.33
), we have that
\chi_{c}(\beta_{c,1})=(1-c)B_{c,1}+cB_{c,2}
when
c\in(0,c^{*})
. Thus, we get from (
9.10
) that

\beta_{c,1}=(1-c)B_{c,1}+cB_{c,2}+\frac{1}{B_{c}}\left(\frac{A_{c,1}}{c}-\frac{A_{c,2}}{1-c}\right).

This, of course, immediately shows that
\beta_{c,1}
is a differentiable function of
c
on
(0,c^{*})
. The above two observations will be sufficient for us to finish the proof of Theorem
1.11
, that is, to prove the bottom line of (
1.36
).

Now we deduce from (
1.33
) and the bottom line of (
1.36
) (the use of the bottom line of (
1.36
) is not really necessary but allows us to get a more compact formula) that

\beta_{c,1}^{\prime}=(2c-1)B_{c}^{\prime}+\frac{1}{B_{c}}\left(\frac{A_{c,1}^{\prime}}{c}-\frac{A_{c,2}^{\prime}}{1-c}\right)-\frac{B_{c}^{\prime}}{B_{c}^{2}}(cR_{1}(c)-(1-c)R_{2}(c)).

Using the top line of (
1.36
), we obtain that

\frac{A_{c,1}^{\prime}}{c}-\frac{A_{c,2}^{\prime}}{1-c}=\frac{B_{c}^{\prime}}{B_{c}}(cR_{2}(c)-(1-c)R_{1}(c)).

Thus, using (
1.33
), (
1.35
), and (
1.36
) once more, we arrive at

\displaystyle\beta_{c,1}^{\prime}

\displaystyle=(2c-1)B_{c}^{\prime}+(R_{2}(c)-R_{1}(c))\frac{B_{c}^{\prime}}{B_{c}^{2}}

\displaystyle=2(cR_{2}(c)-(1-c)R_{1}(c))\frac{B_{c}^{\prime}}{B_{c}^{2}}=6\frac{R(c)}{R^{\prime}(c)}\left(\frac{B_{c}^{\prime}}{B_{c}}\right)^{2}.

(9.12)

Formula (
9.12
) clearly shows that
\beta_{c,1}^{\prime}
is a bounded continuous function of
c\in(0,c^{*})
(it also shows that
\beta_{c,1}
is an increasing function of
c
).

9.4
Differentiability of
\boldsymbol{\sigma(c)}

Before we go back to the proof of Theorems
1.10
and
1.11
, we need to analyze the behavior of one more auxiliary quantity, namely

, defined by

(9.13)

More precisely, we need to establish its differentiability with respect to

and the boundedness of
\sigma^{\prime}(c)

We shall utilize a representation of

different from (
1.21

be given by (
1.23

which is the standard Szegő function of
(\hat{\rho}_{1}w_{c,1+})(x)
considered as a weight on
\Delta_{c,1}
. That is, it is a non-vanishing holomorphic function off
\Delta_{c,1}
(including at infinity) with continuous traces on both sides of

that satisfy

and quarter-root singularities at
\alpha_{1}
,
\beta_{c,1}
. Next, recall that
\chi_{c}(\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{2})
is a Jordan curve that approaches the circle

as

1.13
) and (
1.32

clockwise. The conformal map
\chi_{c}({\boldsymbol{z}})

\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{S}}_{c}^{(2)}\setminus\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{2}
and
\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{S}}_{c}\setminus\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{S}}_{c}^{(2)}
onto the interior and exterior domains of
\chi_{c}(\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{2})
with
\infty^{(0)}
mapped into
\infty
, see (
1.10
). Recall (
9.11
). Set

which is a sectionally analytic and non-vanishing function in

\mathcal{S}_{c,2}\bigl(\infty^{(0)}\bigr)=1
, and
\mathcal{S}_{c,2-}({\boldsymbol{x}})=\mathcal{S}_{c,2+}({\boldsymbol{x}})S_{c,1}(x)

\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{2}
(according to our chosen orientation of

\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{S}}_{c}^{(0)}

readily yield that

S_{c}({\boldsymbol{z}})=k_{c}\begin{cases}S_{c,1}(z)\mathcal{S}_{c,2}({\boldsymbol{z}}),&{\boldsymbol{z}}\in\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{S}}_{c}^{(0)},\\
\mathcal{S}_{c,2}({\boldsymbol{z}})/S_{c,1}(z),&{\boldsymbol{z}}\in\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{S}}_{c}^{(1)},\\
\mathcal{S}_{c,2}({\boldsymbol{z}}),&{\boldsymbol{z}}\in\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{S}}_{c}^{(2)},\end{cases}

(

. Therefore,

\sigma(c)=F_{c,1}^{\prime}(\infty)-\frac{1}{2\pi\mathrm{i}}\oint_{\chi_{0}(\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{2})}F_{c,1}(z(c,s)){\rm d}s,

(9.14)

where we understand the derivative at infinity in local coordinates, that is,
f(z)=f(\infty)+f^{\prime}(\infty)/z+\cdots
, and we moved the curve of integration from
\chi_{c}(\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{2})
to the circle
\chi_{0}(\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{2})
by the analyticity of

(if
\chi_{0}(\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{2})

\Delta_{c,1}

Considerations virtually identical to the ones presented above show that
