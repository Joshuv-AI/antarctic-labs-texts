Stage IVa thymoma: optimizing surgical management according to anatomical boundaries of the pleural space
Authors: Laurens J. Ceulemans, Margherita Brivio, Lori M. Van Roozendaal, Paolo Mendogni, Paul Clement, Lorenzo Rosso, Filippo Lococo
Published: 2026

Introduction

Like stubborn weeds pushing through the cracks of a paved courtyard, pleural metastases in Stage IVa thymoma hide their true danger beneath a deceptively simple surface. When a weed takes root between concrete tiles without impacting the tiles themselves, it reveals only a fraction of its mass above ground; simply shearing the visible shoots offers nothing more than a temporary illusion of control, guaranteeing rapid regrowth. True eradication demands a comprehensive strategy: pulling the plant out from its deepest roots and treating the soil to eradicate the seeds.

A similar surgical philosophy applies for the management of advanced-stage thymoma with pleural dissemination (TPD) [Masaoka-Koga Stage IVa or TNM Stage IVA (9th edition)]. The tumor may spread over the pleura with tumor droplets (weeds) without invading the underlying tissues (concrete tiles), tempting the surgeon with the possibility of a localized resection. Yet, to achieve long-term disease-free survival, absolute radicality is demanded.

Although surgical resection remains the cornerstone of treatment for thymomas, the management of advanced-stage thymoma with TPD, persists as one of the most dynamic and contentious frontiers in mediastinal oncology (1). The rarity of this disease, combined with a heterogeneous body of literature and fragmented therapeutic strategies, has so far prevented the establishment of a definitive clinical consensus.

Within this complex disease, pleura may be involved in two different clinical scenarios:

At the moment of the initial diagnosis → Stage IVa with pleural “de novo” thymoma (DNT);

At the moment of the tumor relapse → thymoma pleural relapse (TPR).

The pleural space is anatomically a virtual space, due to the physiological negative intrapleural pressure, but it is also a well-defined single anatomical compartment, with distinct boundaries. For this reason, pleural involvement in DNT or TPD should be viewed not as systemic metastatic spread, but rather as compartmentalized local disease caused by tumor seeding and treated accordingly. This paradigm shift dictates that achieving maximal radicality (R0) through an ‘anatomical’ pleural resection is fundamental (Figure 1).

Diagnostic and therapeutic pathway proposal: the need for standardized definitions and consensus

To translate this pursuit into clinical reality, we should adopt a comprehensive and integrated framework. Patient’s fitness, the overall tumor burden and the number and distribution of pleural implants must be considered as the three cardinal elements dictating the therapeutic strategy. Based on these factors, limited local resection should be reserved for unfit patients, cases with bilateral pleural disease or in case of video-assisted thoracoscopic surgery (VATS)-proven low pleural burden (1–4 droplets). In contrast, radical resection combined with hyperthermic intrathoracic chemotherapy (HITHOC) should be reserved to fit patients with unilateral disease and in case of more than 4 droplets. Thymic carcinomas represent a distinct topic where a more personalized and less dogmatic approach should be applied, reserving surgery for highly selected, young, and fit patients with limited dissemination.

Within this last setting, three indispensable pillars should guide management: (I) a rigorous oncological staging protocol; (II) a surgical strategy aimed at complete macroscopic resection; (III) the use of innovative therapeutic procedures (i.e., HITHOC), designed to enhance local control and extend long-term survival (2).

First of all, a rigorous oncological staging protocol is mandatory to accurately select those disease stages where surgery can truly play a significant prognostic role (3). At our institution in Leuven, basing on previous clinical experience, this currently represents our standard diagnostic and staging workup. A multimodal imaging approach (Figure 2A) is recommended, incorporating whole-body positron emission tomography-computed tomography (PET-CT) and chest magnetic resonance imaging, to definitively exclude distant systemic spread and characterize the extent of local invasion. In case of uncertainty, uniportal VATS exploration (mid-axillary line, 5th or 6th intercostal space, in line with the planned thoracotomy) should be considered to assess the number of implants. Furthermore, a comprehensive functional workup, including cardiopulmonary exercise testing (CPET) and myocardial or ventilation/perfusion scintigraphy, is essential to stratify surgical risk and confirm the patient’s physiological reserves.

Surgical strategies and Leuven protocol

Extensive literature supports surgical intervention for advanced-stage thymoma with TPD, except in cases where a R0 resection is not achievable, or when lymph node involvement or thymic carcinoma histology is present (6).

Recent landmark studies by Ruffini et al. and Aprile et al. have further solidified the clinical consensus: R0 resection represent the true cornerstone of management, acting as the most fundamental prognostic factor for long-term survival in these patients (7,8).

Non-radical surgery, such as simple partial pleurectomy, should be reserved for specific clinical scenarios in which a rigorous preoperative workup identifies clear contraindications to radical surgery, such as poor functional reserve, limited pleural involvement (1 to 4 droplets), or overwhelming disease burden (i.e., bilateral involvement).

Pleurectomy and decortication, eventually extended to other surrounding organs [extended pleurectomy-decortication (ePD)], and EPP, offer comparable oncological outcomes and are considered the standard radical surgical approaches, supported by several pertinent reviews and meta-analysis supporting this radical therapeutic approach (9).

However, EPP is challenged by significant perioperative morbidity and mortality, as demonstrated in the treatment of malignant pleural diseases such as mesothelioma. Conversely, ePD is emerging as the superior strategy (Figure 2B), as it achieves equivalent radicality (R0) while offering improved survival outcomes (10,11). By preserving the lung, this approach better balances oncological efficacy with maintenance of the patient’s functional reserve.

In Leuven, based on the authors’ experience, ePD is the preferred surgical strategy for Stage IVa thymoma with unilateral pleural involvement presenting with more than four pleural droplets. This procedure follows a standardized technique including a complete parietal pleurectomy, visceral decortication, and intra- and extra-pulmonary lymph-node resection, followed by hemo-and aerostasis, and finally the construction of a neopleura (2). To achieve R0 radicality, the resection is extended to the diaphragm and when direct tumoral infiltration is present, to the pericardium (Figure 2C). Subsequently, following HITHOC procedure (Figure 2D), diaphragmatic and pericardial reconstruction is performed.

As a side note, for large defect reconstruction, the Leuven group pioneered in 2025 a specialized surgical strategy involving the use of non-vascularized rectus fascia (NVRF) allotransplantation, which currently represents our institutional standard of care for diaphragmatic reconstruction (12-14). It involves transplantation of the anterior and posterior rectus fascia sheaths and peritoneum from a deceased donor, without vascular anastomosis. This reconstructive strategy offers excellent biocompatibility with no risk of infection or functional rejection, ensuring long-term structural integrity and minimizing post-operative complications (Figure 2E). Notably, follow-up fluorodeoxyglucose (FDG)-PET scans show no abnormal tracer uptake, unlike other prosthetic materials, thereby ensuring an accurate oncological evaluation during subsequent follow-up.

HITHOC

Despite radical surgical cytoreduction, the persistence of microscopic residual disease within the chest cavity remains an inherent risk; in this context, HITHOC plays a crucial synergistic role. Originally developed for the treatment of peritoneal implants from several neoplasms (e.g., ovarian cancer, mesotheliomas and pseudomyxoma peritonei among others), this technique is based on the synergetic proven synergetic interaction between hyperthermia and locoregional chemotherapy. The therapeutic rationale relies on four mechanisms between:

Cytotoxic hyperthermia: mild hyperthermic (42–45 ℃) effect on malignant cells induces DNA damage, protein denaturation, cellular necrosis and enhanced lysosomal activity. Concurrently, it increases vascular permeability and impairs cellular DNA repair mechanisms against chemotherapy-induced damage.

Enhanced chemotherapeutic uptake of selected agents such as mitomycin, cisplatin, oxaliplatin and doxorubicin. A median cisplatin penetration depth of 3–4 mm appears promising, considering that thick portions of the tumor are surgically removed (macroscopic complete resection) before perfusion. Low tissue absorption is required after pleurectomy/decortication and this has been successfully demonstrated even with high intracavitary doses of cisplatin, limiting classic systemic toxicities such as renal failure (15).

HITHOC is generally considered a safe procedure with low morbidity and mortality. When comparing surgical procedures with and without HITHOC, no increased morbidity/mortality was reported in patients who received HITHOC (18). On the other hand, HITHOC has the potential to increase local disease control, prolong the disease-free interval, reduce local recurrence, and eventually improve survival in TPR and DNT patients (19).

Despite these premises based on logical assumptions and numerous promising clinical experiences, many critical issues remain in the large-scale use of HITHOC techniques (5). The main limit to the diffusion of HITHOC in research clinical trials first and in the clinical activities later is the lack of any standardization in the definition of the HITHOC protocol including the several variables to be defined, as summarized herein:

Chemotherapeutic agent and dosages

Platinum derivatives served as the mainstay agent in nearly all reported HITHOC protocols (18). In several studies, cisplatin monotherapy was used while other reported protocols describe a combination of cisplatin and doxorubicin/epirubicin, and other a combination of cisplatin and mitomycin C. As reported in several recent reviews, cisplatin dosage in the monotherapy setting ranged from 50 to 225 mg/m2 while cisplatin dosage in combination therapy ranged from 50 to 175 mg/m2, oxaliplatin was administered at 460 mg/m2, doxorubicin dose ranged from 15 to 100 mg/m2, and mitomycin C dose ranged from 15 to 25 mg/m2 (8,17,18).

Duration and temperature of the perfusion

Duration of perfusion ranges normally from 60 to 120 min, although most papers reported a perfusion time of a maximum of 90 min. The intrathoracic temperature during perfusion ranged from 41 to 45 ℃, but most papers reported a maximum temperature of 43 ℃. Oxaliplatin requires shorter exposure time to be effective (30–45 minutes) (2).

Perfusion technique

After temporary closure of the chest cavity, a perfusion dedicated system is connected to the chest through several in-and outflow pleural drains and, after starting circulating, perfusion saline isotonic solution is heated up to the target temperature. Chemotherapeutic agents are then instilled into the circuit through the inflow catheter, circulated continuously for the predetermined time frame, and subsequently washed out via the outflow catheter by reversing the direction of the flow.

Our institutional HITHOC protocol in Leuven provides that the patient typically receives 400 mg/m2 5-FU and 20 mg/m2 leucovorin intravenously one hour before HITHOC. During HITHOC, the thoracic cavity is rinsed with 460 mg/m2 of oxaliplatin at a temperature of 43 ℃ for 45 minutes (Figure 2D). Oxaliplatin monotherapy is emerging from the Leuven group as a promising alternative; due to its larger molecular weight, it seems to exhibit a highly favorable systemic safety profile with significantly reduced nephrotoxicity compared to cisplatin, while maintaining a robust synergistic effect with hyperthermia. However, robust evidence regarding the optimal chemotherapeutic agent remains limited, and clear consensus is lacking to date, making this choice primarily based on our institutional experience.

Future directions

The variability in surgical approach and HITHOC protocols, including differences in chemotherapy agents, dosages, and perfusion parameters, as well as variations in duration and temperature of the procedure, complicates the interpretation of outcomes across studies and centers. Current evidence supporting “aggressive” cytoreductive protocols is primarily derived from retrospective single-center series (20). In the absence of prospective randomized trials, selection and expert bias cannot be ruled out, and alternative surgical paradigms adopted by other expert centers remain fully valid. However, institutional protocols such as the one reported by Leuven, are thought provoking for building international guidelines and standardized pathways, which are essential to pave the way for prospective, reproducible multicenter studies.

It is therefore very important to pursue following initiatives among the scientific societies:

Reach a consensus on the definition of different surgical approaches and standardize them to maximize reproducibility and allow for the evaluation and comparison of results on a larger scale.

The results of these large-scale initiatives could lead to new guidelines that allow for the widespread use of the HITHOC technique in the treatment of pleural implants from thymoma (both DNT and PRT).

Conclusions

In conclusion, this editorial underscores that successfully managing stage IVa TPD requires the complete eradication of both the visible tumor and its underlying ‘roots’. Such absolute radicality is achievable through R0 surgery, such as ePD, involving diaphragmatic and pericardial resection as required, lymph node dissection, and advanced reconstruction. Within this therapeutic framework, HITHOC serves as an essential adjunctive ‘herbicide’, addressing the hidden risk of residual microscopic disease by targeting the least visible roots and ultimately optimizing local oncological control.
