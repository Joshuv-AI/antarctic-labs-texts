Information-Theoretic Proofs for Diffusion Sampling
Galen Reeves, Henry D. Pfister

Abstract
This paper provides an elementary, self-contained analysis of diffusion-based sampling methods for generative modeling. In contrast to existing approaches that rely on continuous-time processes and then discretize, our treatment works directly with discrete-time stochastic processes and yields precise non-asymptotic convergence guarantees under broad assumptions. The key insight is to couple the sampling process of interest with an idealized comparison process that has an explicit Gaussian–convolution structure. We then leverage simple identities from information theory, including the I-MMSE relationship, to bound the discrepancy (in terms of the Kullback–Leibler divergence) between these two discrete-time processes. In particular, we show that, if the diffusion step sizes are chosen sufficiently small and one can approximate certain conditional mean estimators well, then the sampling distribution is provably close to the target distribution. Our results also provide a transparent view on how to accelerate convergence by introducing additional randomness in each step to match higher order moments in the comparison process.

Introduction

Diffusion-based sampling methods have emerged as powerful tools for machine learning applications
[
1
,
2
,
3
,
4
,
5
]
. The high-level idea behind these methods is to define a stochastic process that transforms a sequence of samples from an easy-to-sample distribution (e.g., an isotropic Gaussian) into a sample from a target distribution on a high-dimensional space (e.g., a natural image)
[
1
,
2
]
.

The theoretical justification for these methods typically follows a two-stage argument
[
6
]
. First, one specifies a continuous-time process, called a diffusion, that models the underlying distribution of interest. Then, one argues that this process can be simulated accurately by a discrete-time process to generate approximate samples from the target distribution.

This paper presents a simple and intuitive
discrete-time
proof for why diffusion-based sampling methods work. The origin of this work lies in the authors’ desire to provide an elementary presentation of diffusion models suitable for first-year graduate students. Focusing directly on discrete-time stochastic processes, we derive precise non-asymptotic guarantees under very general assumptions.
Along the way, this approach unearths interesting connections between diffusion modeling and the celebrated I-MMSE relationship from information theory
[
7
]
, which provides a link between mutual information (the ’I’) and the minimum mean-squared error (MMSE) in additive Gaussian noise models.

We note that novelty in this work lies more in the path chosen for the presentation than in the mathematical details of the individual steps, which may have appeared in some form previously in the literature.

Diffusion sampling has become very popular for generative models due to its amazing performance on collections of digital images from the Web.
An important component of these models is that the generation is conditional (e.g., on a text prompt) and this corresponds to the functions

In 2022, the Imagen text-to-image model based on conditional diffusion sampling was released and widely celebrated

The results were clearly better than the groundbreaking DALLE-1 text-to-image model, released in 2021, which is based instead on transformers that generate visual tokens

Proof of Theorem
1

Lemma 1
.

The process
\{Y_{k}\}
defined in (
2
) is a Markov chain.

At its core, the Markov property is a consequence of the orthogonal invariance of the standard Gaussian distribution. We present two elementary proofs. The first proceeds by showing that
Y_{k}
is a sufficient statistic for estimating
X
from
(Y_{1},\dots,Y_{k})
. The second shows that the time-reversed process is a Markov chain with independent Gaussian increments.
We will denote the probability density function of
\mathsf{N}(0,\delta_{k}\mathrm{I})
by

\displaystyle\phi_{k}(z)\coloneqq(2\pi\delta_{k})^{-d/2}\exp\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{-\tfrac{1}{2\delta_{k}}\|z\|^{2}}}\right\}.

(9)

First Proof of Lemma
1
.

Consider the difference sequence

\displaystyle V_{k}\coloneqq Y_{k}-Y_{k-1}=\delta X+\sqrt{\delta_{k}}N_{k}.

(10)

Given
X=x
, the joint density of
V_{1},\dots,V_{k}
factors according to

\displaystyle\prod_{i=1}^{k}(2\pi\delta_{i})^{-d/2}\exp\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\tfrac{1}{2\delta_{i}}\|v_{i}-\delta_{i}x\|^{2}}}\right\}

\displaystyle=\phi_{1}(v_{1})\cdots\phi_{k}(v_{k})\exp\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\langle{\textstyle\sum_{i=1}^{k}}v_{i},x}}\right\rangle-\tfrac{t_{k}}{2}\|x\|^{2}}}\right\}.

(11)

By the Fisher-Neyman factorization theorem, it follows that
Y_{k}=\sum_{i=1}^{k}V_{i}
is a sufficient statistic for estimating
X
from the observations
(V_{1},\dots,V_{k})
. This sufficiency also holds with respect
(Y_{1},\dots,Y_{k})
which can be obtained from a one-to-one transformation of
(V_{1},\dots,V_{k})
.

This sufficiency implies that
X
and
(Y_{1},\dots,Y_{k-1})
are conditionally independent given
Y_{k}
.
The Markov property follows from combining this with the fact that
N_{k+1}
is independent of
(Y_{1},\dots,Y_{k})
and concluding that
Y_{k+1}
and
(Y_{1},\dots,Y_{k-1})
are conditionally independent given
Y_{k}
.
∎

Second Proof of Lemma
1
.

Consider the difference sequence

\displaystyle B_{k}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\sqrt{\frac{t_{k+1}}{t_{k}}}Y_{k}-\sqrt{\frac{t_{k}}{t_{k+1}}}Y_{k+1}=\sqrt{\frac{t_{k+1}}{t_{k}}}W_{k}-\sqrt{\frac{t_{k}}{t_{k+1}}}W_{k+1}.

(12)

The second step, which follows from the representation in (
3
), shows that the sequences
\{B_{k}\}
and
\{W_{k}\}
are jointly Gaussian and independent of
X
. Using the fact that
\cov(W_{k},W_{m})=\min\{t_{k},t_{m}\}\mathrm{I}
, a simple calculation reveals that

\displaystyle\cov(B_{k},W_{m})

\displaystyle=0\quad\text{for all $m>k$},

(13)

and thus
B_{k}
is independent of
(W_{k+1},W_{k+2},\dots)
. Putting everything together, we conclude that

\displaystyle Y_{k}

\displaystyle=\frac{t_{k}}{t_{k+1}}Y_{k+1}+\sqrt{\frac{t_{k}}{t_{k+1}}}B_{k},

(14)

where
B_{k}\sim\mathsf{N}(0,\delta_{k+1}\mathrm{I})
is independent of
(Y_{k+1},Y_{k+2},\dots)
. This shows that the time-reversed process is a Markov chain with independent Gaussian increments.
∎

2.1
Divergence Between Markov Chains

Having established the Markov property, we can now provide an exact expression for
\Delta_{n}
in terms of the mean-squared error
\mathbb{E}[\|X-f_{k}(Y_{k-1})\|^{2}]
and the mutual information

I(X;Y)=D(\mathsf{Law}(X,Y)\,\|\,\mathsf{Law}(X)\otimes\mathsf{Law}(Y))
.

Lemma 2
.

Under the assumptions of Theorem
1
,

\displaystyle\Delta_{n}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{n}\frac{\delta_{k}}{2}\mathbb{E}[\|X-\mathbb{E}[X\mid Y_{k-1}]\|^{2}]-I(X;Y_{n})+\sum_{k=1}^{n}\frac{\delta_{k}}{2}\mathbb{E}[\|f_{k}(Y_{k-1})-\mathbb{E}[X\mid Y_{k-1}]\|^{2}].

(15)

Proof.

Using the fact that both
\{Y_{k}\}
and
\{Z_{k}\}
are Markov chains, we can write

\displaystyle\Delta_{n}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\log\frac{p_{k}(Y_{k}\mid Y_{k-1})}{q_{k}(Y_{k}\mid Y_{k-1})}}}\right],

(16)

where
p_{k}
and
q_{k}
are the transition probability densities for
Y_{k}\mid Y_{k-1}
and
Z_{k}\mid Z_{k-1}
with respect to Lebesgue measure. Noting that
\phi_{k}
is the density of
\mathsf{N}(0,\delta_{k}\mathrm{I})
, the
k
-th summand can be expressed as the difference of two terms

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\log\frac{\phi_{k}(\sqrt{\delta_{k}}N_{k})}{q_{k}(Y_{k}\mid Y_{k-1})}}}\right]-\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\log\frac{\phi_{k}(\sqrt{\delta_{k}}N_{k})}{p_{k}(Y_{k}\mid Y_{k-1})}}}\right].

(17)

Observing that
q_{k}(Y_{k}\mid Y_{k-1})=\phi_{k}\big(\delta_{k}(X-f_{k}(Y_{k-1}))+\sqrt{\delta_{k}}N_{k}\big)
, we see that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\log\frac{\phi_{k}(\sqrt{\delta_{k}}N_{k})}{q_{k}(Y_{k}\mid Y_{k-1})}}}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[-\frac{1}{2}\|N_{k}\|^{2}+\frac{\delta_{k}}{2}\|X-f_{k}(Y_{k-1})\|^{2}-2\big(X-f_{k}(Y_{k-1})\big)\cdot N_{k}+\frac{1}{2}\|N_{k}\|^{2}}}\right]

(18)

\displaystyle=\frac{\delta_{k}}{2}\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\|X-f_{k}(Y_{k-1})\|^{2}}}\right]

(19)

\displaystyle=\frac{\delta_{k}}{2}\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\|X-\mathbb{E}[X\mid Y_{k-1}]\|^{2}}}\right]+\frac{\delta_{k}}{2}\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\|\mathbb{E}[X\mid Y_{k-1}]-f_{k}(Y_{k-1})\|^{2}}}\right],

(20)

where the second step follows from the orthogonality principle for conditional expectation.

Noting that
\phi_{k}(\delta_{k}N_{k})
is the density of
Y_{k}\mid(X,Y_{k-1})
, the second term equals the difference in mutual information

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\log\frac{\phi_{k}(\sqrt{\delta_{k}}N_{k})}{p_{k}(Y_{k}\mid Y_{k-1})}}}\right]

\displaystyle=I(X;Y_{k}\mid Y_{k-1})=I(X;Y_{k})-I(X;Y_{k-1}),

(21)

where the second step holds because
Y_{k-1}-Y_{k}-X
is a Markov chain. Plugging these expressions back into (
16
) and noting that
I(X;Y_{0})=0
gives the stated result.
∎

2.2
Proof of Theorem
1
via I-MMSE

For a distribution
\mu
on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
with finite second moments, we define the MMSE function
M\colon\mathbb{R}_{+}\to\mathbb{R}
by

\displaystyle M(s)

\displaystyle\coloneqq\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\|X-\mathbb{E}[X\mid\sqrt{s}X+N]\|^{2}}}\right],

(22)

where
X\sim\mu
and
N\sim\mathsf{N}(0,\mathrm{I})
are independent. It is well known that this function is non-increasing with
M(0)=\gtr(\cov(X))
. The I-MMSE relation
[
7
]
states that, for any
0\leq a<b
,

\displaystyle I(X;\sqrt{b}X+N)-I(X;\sqrt{a}X+N)=\frac{1}{2}\int_{a}^{b}M(s)\,ds.

(23)

In other words, one half the MMSE is equal to the derivative of the mutual information with respect to
s
.

Combining the I-MMSE relation with the monotonicity of the MMSE (see Figure
1
) gives the sandwich

\displaystyle\frac{\delta_{k}}{2}M(t_{k})\leq I(X;Y_{k})-I(X;Y_{k-1})\leq\frac{\delta_{k}}{2}M(t_{k-1}),

(24)

which follows from the invariance of mutual information under one-to-one transformations, and thus

I(X;Y_{k})=I(X;\delta_{k}X+W_{k})=I(X;\sqrt{\delta_{k}}X+N)
. Using (
24
), the first two terms in the expression of
\Delta_{n}
can be bounded from above as follows:

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{n}

\displaystyle\frac{\delta_{k}}{2}\mathbb{E}[\|X-\mathbb{E}[X\mid Y_{k-1}]\|^{2}]-I(X;Y_{n})

(25)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{n}\frac{\delta_{k}}{2}M(t_{k-1})-I(X;Y_{n})

(26)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{n}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\frac{\delta_{k}}{2}M(t_{k-1})+I(X;Y_{k-1})-I(X;Y_{k})}}\right]

(27)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{k=1}^{n}\frac{\delta_{k}}{2}(M(t_{k-1})-M(t_{k}))

(28)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\delta_{\mathrm{max}}}{2}\sum_{k=1}^{n}(M(t_{k-1})-M(t_{k}))

(29)

\displaystyle=\frac{\delta_{\mathrm{max}}}{2}(M(0)-M(t_{n}))\leq\frac{\delta_{\mathrm{max}}}{2}\gtr(\cov(X)).

(30)

Combining with Lemma
2
completes the proof of Theorem
1
.

3
Sampling Process: Intuition and Connections

The Markov property implies
that the sequence
\{Y_{k}\}
can be generated by sampling each
Y_{k}
conditionally given
Y_{k-1}
. This procedure can be implemented using the following steps:

1.

Draw
X_{k}
from
the conditional of
X
given
Y_{k-1}
;

2.

Draw
N^{\prime}_{k}\sim\mathsf{N}(0,\mathrm{I})
independently of
(X_{k},Y_{k-1})
;

3.

Set
Y_{k}=Y_{k-1}+\delta_{k}X_{k}+\sqrt{\delta_{k}}N^{\prime}_{k}
.

Viewed in terms of this sampling procedure, the process
\{Y_{t}\}
can be expressed as

\displaystyle Y_{k}=Y_{k-1}+\delta_{k}X_{k}+\sqrt{\delta_{k}}N^{\prime}_{k}

(31)

We emphasize that the above procedure defines a process with exactly the same distribution as the one defined by (
2
). In both cases, the sequences are driven by standard Gaussian processes
\{N_{k}\}
and
\{N_{k}^{\prime}\}
, but there are also some key differences:

•

In (
2
) the innovation term
X
is the same for every step. Conditional on
X
, the noise
N_{k}
is independent of increments
\delta_{m}X+\sqrt{\delta_{m}}N_{m}
for all
m\neq k
.

•

In (
31
) the innovation term
X_{n}
changes with each step. Conditional on
X_{k}
, the noise term
N^{\prime}_{k}
is independent of the increments
\delta_{m}X_{k}+\sqrt{\delta_{m}}N^{\prime}_{m}
for
m<k
, but not for
m>k
.

This dual representation of the same process provides the underlying intuition for the sampling procedure. On the one hand, the original representation in (
2
) verifies that
t_{k}^{-1}Y_{k}
is distributed according to
P\ast\mathsf{N}(0,t^{-1}_{k}\mathrm{I})
, and thus constitutes and approximate sample from
\mu
provided that
t_{k}
is large enough. But this representation does provide any guidance on how to produce the original sample
X
.

On the other hand, the sampling version of the process in (
31
) shows that the same process can be implemented by replacing the innovation
X
with a random term
X_{k}
that depends only on
Y_{k-1}
as well as some additional randomness that is independent of
(Y_{1},\dots,Y_{k-1})
.

From this point of view, the behavior of the sampling scheme in (
1
) is best understood by comparing with the sampling representation in (
31
). Specifically, one can view the function
f_{k}(Y_{k-1})
as providing a first-order approximation to
X_{n}
. After matching the noise term
N_{k}=N^{\prime}_{k}
, the only differences between these sequences are due to the fluctuations in
X_{k}-f_{k}(Y_{k-1})
. Theorem
1
shows that if each
f_{k}
provides a suitable approximation to the conditional mean estimator, then these fluctuations are negligible in the large-
n
limit.

3.1
Connection with Diffusion Models

Within the literature (e.g., see
[
1
,
2
,
6
]
), diffusion-based sampling is often described in terms of a “forward model” and a “backward model” for the underlying diffusion processes:

•

The
forward model
starts with a sample from the target distribution (often taken to be
\mu\ast\mathsf{N}(0,T^{-1}\mathrm{I})
) and then incrementally transforms it into a sample from a Gaussian distribution by adding noise and rescaling.

•

The
backward model
starts with a sample from Gaussian noise and then incrementally transforms it into a sample from the target distribution by via a process that combines additive noise with nonlinear transformations.

To connect these ideas with the results in this paper, we observe that the time-discretization of the forward model corresponds directly to
time-reversed
version of the “comparison process” given in (
2
), i.e., the process
Y_{n},Y_{n-1},\dots,Y_{1}
for some fixed
n\in\mathbb{N}
. This can can seen from (
14
), which shows that
Y_{k}
is obtained from
Y_{k+1}
by adding independent Gaussian noise and rescaling.

The connection with the backward model is more subtle. Formally, the transition probability for time-discretization of the backward model is given by (
31
), which requires sampling from the target measure at each time step. However, the functional form of the backwards model more closely resembles the original sampling process
\{Z_{k}\}
given in (
1
), which replaces the sampling step with deterministic function.

The justification for using (
1
) as a sampling scheme follows from the fact that its continuous time limit (as
n\to\infty
with
\delta_{k}=T/n
) corresponds exactly to the backwards diffusion model. This is precisely what is shown in Theorem
1
.

Connection with Stochastic Localization

Stochastic localization refers broadly to a framework for analyzing the mixing times of Markov chains
[
10
,
11
]

The process defined by (
2
) is an example of a stochastic localization scheme. To see this, consider the measure-valued random process
\mu_{1},\mu_{2},\dots,
where

\displaystyle\mu_{k}\coloneqq\mathbb{P}[X\in\cdot\mid Y_{k-1}]

(32)

is the conditional distribution of
X
given
Y_{k-1}
. As
t_{k}
increases, this sequence converges to a point-mass distribution centered at some point
X_{\infty}
. Since
\mathbb{E}[\mu_{k}]=\mu
for all
k
, the limit
X_{\infty}
is a sample from
\mu
.

Additional Results

4.1
Optimization of Step Sizes

In this section we assume that each
f_{k}
is the conditional mean estimator and study the dependence of
\delta_{n}
the on pair
(n,T)
for time steps given by

\displaystyle t_{k}=\frac{\alpha^{k}-1}{\alpha^{n}-1}T,\qquad k=1,\dots,n

(33)

where
\alpha>0
is rate parameter
2
2

2

By continuity, the case
\alpha=1
corresponds to uniform step sizes, i.e.,
t_{k}=(k/n)T
.
. Under this specification, the step sizes satisfy
\delta_{k+1}=\alpha\delta_{k}
for
k\geq 1
. The case
\alpha=1
corresponds to uniform increments, i.e.,
\delta_{k}=T/n
.

For this setting, the general bound in Theorem
1
depends on the maximum step size
\delta_{\mathrm{max}}\geq T/n
, and this results in a linear dependence on
T
. Using a refined analysis adapted to (
33
), we show that this can be improved to a poly-logarithmic dependence without any addition assumptions on
\mu
.

Let
I\colon\mathbb{R}_{+}\to\mathbb{R}
be defined according to

\displaystyle I(s)\coloneqq I(X;\sqrt{s}X+N)=\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{s}M(t)\,dt

(34)

where
X\sim\mu
and
N\sim\mathsf{N}(0,\mathrm{I})
are independent. By the I-MMSE relation,
I
is strictly increasing and concave. If
\mu
has finite entropy then
I(s)
is bounded. Otherwise,
I(s)
increases without bound. It is well known that
I(s)\leq\frac{1}{2}\log\det(\mathrm{I}+s\cov(X))
with equality if and only if
\mu
is Gaussian.

Theorem 2
.

Assume that, for a given time
T>0
and total number of steps
n\in\mathbb{N}
, the step sizes are given by (
33
) and each
f_{k}
is the conditional mean estimator of
X
given
Y_{k-1}
. Then,

\displaystyle\Delta_{n}

\displaystyle\leq(\alpha-1)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{T(M(0)-M(T))}{2(\alpha^{n}-1)}+I(T)-\frac{TM(T)}{2}}}\right)

(35)

Remark 4
.

The case
\alpha=1
recovers the bound for uniform step size. i.e.,
\Delta_{n}\leq\frac{1}{2n}T(M(0)-M(T))
. As
\alpha\to 0
the bound converges to
\Delta_{1}=\frac{1}{2}TM(0)-I(T)
. Optimizing over
\alpha
as a function of
(n,T)
improves on both of these cases.

Corollary 1
.

Let
T_{n}
be a sequence satisfying the growth condition

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\log\Big(1+\frac{T_{n}M(0)}{2I(T_{n})}\Big)=o_{n}(1)

(36)

Then, if
\alpha_{n}=(1+T_{n}M(0)/(2I(T_{n})))^{1/n}
, we have

\displaystyle\Delta_{n}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2I(T_{n})}{n}\log\Big(1+\frac{T_{n}M(0)}{2I(T_{n})}\Big)(1+o_{n}(1)).

(37)

Proof of Theorem
2
.

Starting with Lemma
2
, and using the fact that
\delta_{k}=\alpha\delta_{k-1}
for
k\geq 2
leads to

\displaystyle\Delta_{n}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{n}\frac{\delta_{k}}{2}M(t_{k-1})-I(t_{n})

(38)

\displaystyle=\frac{\delta_{1}}{2}M(t_{0})+\sum_{k=2}^{n}\frac{\delta_{k}}{2}M(t_{k-1})-I(t_{n})

(39)

\displaystyle=\frac{\delta_{1}}{2}M(t_{0})+\alpha\sum_{k=2}^{n}\frac{\delta_{k-1}}{2}M(t_{k-1})-I(t_{n})

(40)

\displaystyle=\frac{\delta_{1}}{2}M(t_{0})-\frac{\alpha\delta_{n}}{2}M(t_{n})+\alpha\sum_{k=1}^{n}\frac{\delta_{k}}{2}M(t_{k})-I(t_{n})

(41)

By the I-MMSE inequality in (
24
), we have

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{n}\frac{\delta_{k}}{2}M(t_{k})\leq\sum_{k=1}^{n}[I(t_{k})-I(t_{k-1})]=I(t_{n})

(42)

and this leads to

\displaystyle\Delta_{n}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\delta_{1}}{2}M(t_{0})-\frac{\alpha\delta_{n}}{2}M(t_{n})+(\alpha-1)I(t_{n}).

(43)

Noting that
\delta_{1}=\frac{\alpha-1}{\alpha^{n}-1}T
and
\delta_{n}=(\alpha-1)t_{n}+\frac{\alpha-1}{\alpha^{n}-1}T
completes the proof.
∎

4.2
Improved Rates via Moment Matching

The standard formulation of the diffusion-based sampler in (
1
) provides a deterministic approximation
f_{k}(Y_{k-1})
to the the conditional samples
X_{k}
appearing (
31
). With respect to the relative entropy, the optimal approximation is the one that matches the mean.

In this section, we show that our analysis extends naturally to sampling scheme that replaces the function evaluation
f_{k}(Y_{k-1})
with a stochastic approximation to
X_{n}
. The only requirement, is that the resulting process is Markov chain. Specifically, we consider the generalization of (
1
) given by

\displaystyle Z_{k}=Z_{k-1}+\delta_{k}\hat{X}_{k}+\sqrt{\delta_{k}}N_{k},

(44)

where
\hat{X}_{k}
is sampled conditionally on
Z_{k-1}
according to the Markov kernel
Q(\cdot\mid z,t)
evaluated at
(Z_{k-1},t_{k-1})
. Note that by (
31
), the process
\{Y_{k}\}
corresponds to the Markov kernel
P(\cdot\mid y,t)=\mathbb{P}[X\in\cdot\mid Y(t)=y]
where
Y(t)\coloneqq tX+\sqrt{t}N
with
X\sim\mu
and
N\sim\mathsf{N}(0,\mathrm{I})
are independent.

Condition 1
.

For
T>0
and
m\in\mathbb{N}
,

1.

Sub-Gaussian Tails:

There exists
L>0
such that

\displaystyle\int e^{\|x\|^{2}/L^{2}}\mu(dx)\leq 2,\quad\text{and}\quad\int e^{\|x\|^{2}/L^{2}}\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[Q(dx\mid Y(t),t)}}\right]\leq 2,\quad\forall t\in[0,T]

(45)

where
Y(t)=tX+\sqrt{t}N
with
X\sim\mu
and
N\sim\mathsf{N}(0,\mathrm{I})
independent.

2.

Matched Moments:

For all
\alpha\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{d}
with
|\alpha|\leq m
,

\displaystyle\int x^{\alpha}P(dx\mid y,t)=\int x^{\alpha}Q(dx\mid y,t)

(46)

for all
(y,t)\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\times[0,T]
where
x^{\alpha}\coloneqq\prod_{i=1}^{d}x_{i}^{\alpha_{i}}
.

The following theorem shows that matching higher order moments can increases the rate of convergence with respect to the number of time steps.

Theorem 3
.

Let
\{Z_{k}\}
be generated according to (
44
) and assume Condition
1
holds. Then, there exists a positive constant
c_{d,m}
depending only on
(d,m)
such that

\displaystyle\Delta_{n}\leq c_{d,m}L^{2(m+1)}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\delta_{k}^{m+1}.

(47)

In particular, if
\delta_{k}=T/n
, then

\displaystyle\Delta_{n}\leq c_{d,m,T,L}\,n^{-m}.

(48)

Remark 5
.

The case of matched second moments (
m=2
) can be realized as a modification of the original sampling process in (
1
) that also adapts the covariance of the Gaussian noise term, i.e.,

\displaystyle Z_{k}=Z_{k-1}+\delta_{k}f_{k}(Z_{k-1})+\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\delta_{k}^{2}\Sigma_{k}(Z_{k-1})+\delta_{k}\mathrm{I}}}\right)^{1/2}N_{k}

(49)

where
f_{k}(y)=\mathbb{E}[X\mid Y_{k-1}=y]
and
\Sigma_{k}(y)=\cov(X\mid Y_{k-1}=y)
are the conditional mean and covariance functions define with respect to (
2
).
By construction, the implied Markov kernel for this process satisfies the matched moments condition for
m=2
.
In the event that
\{f_{k}\}
and
\{\Sigma_{k}\}
can be approximated accurately, the divergence decreases at rate
1/n^{2}
. This analysis is closely related to acceleration schemes proposed in
[
20
,
21
]
.

Proof of Theorem
3
.

Define the conditional distributions:

\displaystyle\mu_{t}

\displaystyle=P(\cdot\mid Y(t),t)\quad\text{and}\quad\nu_{t}=Q(\cdot\mid Y(t),t)

(50)

where
Y(t)=tX+\sqrt{t}N
with
X\sim\mu
and
N\sim\mathsf{N}(0,\mathrm{I})
independent. Using the fact that both
\{Y_{k}\}
and
\{Z_{k}\}
are Markov chains along with the fact that relative entropy is invariant to one-to-one transformations, we can write

\displaystyle\Delta_{n}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}[D\big(\mu_{t_{k-1}}\ast\mathsf{N}(\delta_{k}^{-1})\,\|\,\nu_{t_{k-1}}\ast\mathsf{N}(\delta_{k}^{-1}\big)]

(51)

where we have introduced the notation
\mathsf{N}(u)=\mathsf{N}(0,u\mathrm{I})
.

By the moment matching assumption in Condition
1
and Lemma
3
, there is a constant
c_{d,m}
such that, for any
\beta>0
, the following holds almost surely:

\displaystyle D(\mu_{t_{k-1}}\ast\mathsf{N}(\delta_{k}^{-1})\,\|\,\nu_{t_{k-1}}\ast\mathsf{N}(\delta_{k}^{-1}))

(52)

\displaystyle\leq c_{d,m}\,(\delta_{k}/\beta)^{m+1}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\int e^{\beta\|x\|^{2}}P(dx\mid Y(t_{k-1}),t_{k-1})+\int e^{\beta\|x\|^{2}}Q(dx\mid Y(t_{k-1}),t_{k-1})}}\right).

(53)

Evaluating with
\beta=1/L^{2}
, taking the expectation of both sides and invoking the sub-Gaussian tail assumption in Condition
1
then yields

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[D(\mu_{t_{k-1}}\ast\mathsf{N}(\delta_{k}^{-1})\,\|\,\nu_{t_{k-1}}\ast\mathsf{N}(\delta_{k}^{-1}))]

\displaystyle\leq 4c_{d,m}L^{2(m+1)}\delta_{k}^{m+1}

(54)

Plugging this inequality back into (
51
) completes the proof.
∎

Appendix A
Moment Matching Divergence Bound

The following result provides a uniform upper bound on the divergence between distributions satisfying a moment matching condition. The proof is adapted from the proof of Theorem 2.5 in
[
22
]
, which provides the exact asymptotics in the
s\to 0
limit.

Lemma 3
.

Let
\mu
and
\nu
be distributions on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
such that, for every
\alpha\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{d}
with
|\alpha|\leq m
,

\displaystyle\int x^{\alpha}\mu(dx)=\int x^{\alpha}\nu(dx).

(55)

Then, for all
s,\beta>0
,

\displaystyle D(\mu\ast\mathsf{N}(s^{-1})\,\|\,\nu\ast\mathsf{N}(s^{-1}))\leq c_{d,m}\,(s/\beta)^{m+1}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\int e^{\beta\|x\|^{2}}\mu(dx)+\int e^{\beta\|x\|^{2}}\nu(dx)}}\right),

(56)

where
c_{d,m}
is a positive constant that depends only
(d,m)
.

Proof.

Throughout this proof, we use the notation
f\lesssim g
to indicate that the inequality
f\leq c_{d,n}g
holds for some positive constant
c_{d,n}
that may depend on
(d,n)
. Let
\gamma=\mathsf{N}(0,\mathrm{I})
be the standard Gaussian measure, and let
(A,B)
be independent of
N\sim\gamma
with marginals
A\sim\mu
and
B\sim\nu
. In the following, we will prove that

\displaystyle D(\mu\ast\mathsf{N}(s^{-1})\,\|\,\nu\ast\mathsf{N}(s^{-1}))

\displaystyle\lesssim s^{m+1}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathbb{E}\big[e^{6\|A\|^{2}}\big]+\mathbb{E}\big[e^{6\|B\|^{2}}\big]}}\right),\quad\text{for all $s>0$}.

(57)

For
\beta>0
, we then recover the stated inequality by observing that the relative entropy is invariant under the
simultaneous rescaling of
s
and
\mu,\nu
defined by
(s,A,B)\mapsto\big(\tfrac{6}{\beta}s,\sqrt{\tfrac{\beta}{6}}A,\sqrt{\tfrac{\beta}{6}}B\big)
. Making this change of variables and then absorbing the factor of
6^{m+1}
into the constant gives the desired result.

In order to prove (
57
), we first consider the case
s\geq 1
. By the convexity of relative entropy and Jensen’s inequality,

\displaystyle D(\mu\ast\mathsf{N}(s^{-1})\,\|\,\nu\ast\mathsf{N}(s^{-1}))

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[D(\mathsf{N}(A,s^{-1}\mathrm{I})\,\|\,\mathsf{N}(B,s^{-1}\mathrm{I}))}}\right]

(58)

\displaystyle=\frac{s}{2}\mathbb{E}[\|A-B\|^{2}]

\displaystyle\lesssim s^{m+1}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathbb{E}\big[e^{6\|A\|^{2}}\big]+\mathbb{E}\big[e^{6\|B\|^{2}}\big]}}\right),\quad\text{for $s\geq 1$}

(59)

where the last step holds because
\|A-B\|^{2}\leq 2(\|A\|^{2}+\|B\|^{2})\leq\frac{1}{3}(e^{6\|A\|^{2}}+e^{6\|B\|^{2}})
.

Next, we consider the case
s\in(0,1)
. Let the densities of
\sqrt{s}A+N
and
\sqrt{s}B+N
with respect to
\gamma
be denoted by

\displaystyle p_{s}(x)\coloneqq\mathbb{E}[e^{\sqrt{s}\langle x,A\rangle-\frac{s}{2}\|A\|^{2}}],\qquad q_{s}(x)\coloneqq\mathbb{E}[e^{\sqrt{s}\langle x,B\rangle-\frac{s}{2}\|B\|^{2}}].

(60)

Using the fact that relative entropy is bounded from above by the chi-square divergence, and then applying Hölder’s inequality with conjugate exponents
3
and
3/2
gives

\displaystyle D(\mu\ast\mathsf{N}(s^{-1})\,\|\,\nu\ast\mathsf{N}(s^{-1}))

\displaystyle\leq\int\frac{(p_{s}(x)-q_{s}(x)^{2}}{q_{s}(x)}\,\gamma(dx)

(61)

\displaystyle\leq\Big(\int\frac{\gamma(dx)}{q_{s}(x)^{3}}\Big)^{1/3}\Big(\int\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|p_{s}(x)-q_{s}(x)}}\right|^{3}\,\gamma(dx)\Big)^{2/3}.

(62)

For the first term, Jensen’s inequality gives the lower bound

q_{s}(x)\geq\exp\{\sqrt{s}\langle x,\mathbb{E}[B]\rangle-\frac{s}{2}\mathbb{E}[\|B\|^{2}]\}
,
which leads to

\displaystyle\int\frac{\gamma(dx)}{q_{s}(x)^{3}}

\displaystyle\leq\int e^{-3\sqrt{s}\langle x,\mathbb{E}[B]\rangle+\frac{3s}{2}\mathbb{E}[\|B\|^{2}]}\,\gamma(dx)=e^{\frac{9}{2}s\|\mathbb{E}[B]\|^{2}+\frac{3s}{2}\mathbb{E}[\|B\|^{2}]}\leq\mathbb{E}\big[e^{6s\|B\|^{2}}\big].

(63)

For the second term, we use the
m
-th order Taylor series expansion of
y\mapsto\exp\{\langle x,y\rangle-\frac{1}{2}\|y\|^{2}\}
about the point
y=0
to obtain

\displaystyle p_{s}(x)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\alpha\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{d}\,:\,|\alpha|\leq m}\frac{s^{|\alpha|/2}H_{\alpha}(x)}{\alpha!}\mathbb{E}[A^{\alpha}]+\mathbb{E}[r_{m+1}(x,\sqrt{s}A)]

(64)

\displaystyle q_{s}(x)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\alpha\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{d}\,:\,|\alpha|\leq m}\frac{s^{|\alpha|/2}H_{\alpha}(x)}{\alpha!}\mathbb{E}[B^{\alpha}]+\mathbb{E}[r_{m+1}(x,\sqrt{s}B)]

(65)

where
H_{\alpha}
are the Hermite polynomials and
r_{m+1}(x,y)
is the remainder term. The assumption that
A
and
B
have the same moments of up order to
m
along with the basic inequality
|u-v|^{3}\leq 4(|u|^{3}+|v|^{3})
then leads to

\displaystyle\int\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|p_{s}(x)-q_{s}(x)}}\right|^{3}\,\gamma(dx)

\displaystyle=\int\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|\mathbb{E}[r_{m+1}(x,\sqrt{s}A)]-\mathbb{E}[r_{m+1}(x,\sqrt{s}B)]}}\right|^{3}\,\gamma(dx)

(66)

\displaystyle\leq 4\int\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|\mathbb{E}[r_{m+1}(x,\sqrt{s}A)]}}\right|^{3}+\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|\mathbb{E}[r_{m+1}(x,\sqrt{s}B)]}}\right|^{3}}}\right)\,\gamma(dx)

(67)

To proceed, we use the integral form for the remainder given in Equation (4.3) of
[
22
]
,

\displaystyle r_{m+1}(x,y)

\displaystyle=(m+1)\int_{0}^{1}(1-u)^{m}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\alpha\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{d}\\
|\alpha|=n+1\end{subarray}}\frac{y^{\alpha}}{\alpha!}g_{\alpha}(x,uy)\,du

(68)

where
g_{\alpha}(x,y)\coloneqq H_{\alpha}(x-y)\exp\{\langle x,y\rangle-\frac{1}{2}\|y\|^{2}\}
. For
Y\in\{\sqrt{s}A,\sqrt{s}B\}
, we can now write

\displaystyle\int\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|\mathbb{E}[r_{m+1}(x,Y)]}}\right|^{3}\,\gamma(dx)

\displaystyle\lesssim\max_{0\leq u\leq 1}\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}\alpha\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{d}\\
|\alpha|=n+1\end{subarray}}\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\|Y\|^{3m+3}\int\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|g_{\alpha}(x,uY)}}\right|^{3}\,\gamma(dx)}}\right].

(69)

Since each
H_{\alpha}
is a polynomial of degree
m+1
,

\displaystyle\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|g_{\alpha}(x,y)}}\right|

\displaystyle\lesssim\,(1+\|x-y\|^{m+1})e^{\langle x,y\rangle-\frac{1}{2}\|y\|^{2}},

(70)

and this leads to

\displaystyle\int\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|g_{\alpha}(x,y)}}\right|^{3}\,\gamma(dx)

\displaystyle\lesssim\int(1+\|x-y\|^{3m+3})e^{3\langle x,y\rangle-\frac{3}{2}\|y\|^{2}}\,\gamma(dx)

(71)

\displaystyle=\int(2\pi)^{-d/2}(1+\|x-y\|^{3m+3})e^{-\frac{1}{2}\|x-3y\|^{2}+3\|y\|^{2}}\,dx

(72)

\displaystyle\lesssim(1+\|y\|^{3m+3})e^{3\|y\|^{2}}.

(73)

Combining with the display above, we see that

\displaystyle\int\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|\mathbb{E}[r_{m+1}(x,\sqrt{s}A)]}}\right|^{3}\,\gamma(dx)

\displaystyle\lesssim\,s^{\frac{3}{2}(m+1)}\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\|A\|^{3m+3}(1+\|\sqrt{s}A\|^{3m+3})e^{3s\|A\|^{2}}}}\right]

(74)

\displaystyle\lesssim\,s^{\frac{3}{2}(m+1)}\mathbb{E}\big[e^{6\|A\|^{2}}\big]\quad\text{ for all $0<s\leq 1$,}

(75)

with the same inequality holding for
B
.

Plugging (
63
) and (
75
) back into (
62
), we conclude that

\displaystyle D(\mu\ast\mathsf{N}(s^{-1})\,\|\,\nu\ast\mathsf{N}(s^{-1}))\lesssim s^{m+1}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathbb{E}\big[e^{6\|A\|^{2}}\big]+\mathbb{E}\big[e^{6\|B\|^{2}}\big]}}\right)\quad\text{for all $0\leq s\leq 1$}

(76)

Combining (
59
) and (
76
) completes the proof of (
57
).
∎
