Mesenchymal stem cells and their promise in reversing ovarian aging
Authors: Dongmei Yao, Hong Chen, Jianlong Yuan, Peixin Xu, Xin Guo, Jing Gao, Xin Li, Tong Wang, Yurong Wang, Bingchun Liu, Yang Liu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Ovarian aging is accompanied by a decline in the quantity and quality of follicles, leading to reduced fertility. Ovarian aging encompasses natural aging due to DNA damage, telomere attrition, and mitochondrial dysfunction, as well as a pathological functional failure caused by environmental toxins, known as a premature ovarian failure. Cell therapy is currently a focal point of research, with mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs) being particularly notable due to their wide availability, ease of expansion, strong self-renewal capabilities, multipotent differentiation, and paracrine functions. MSCs have shown great potential in the field of cell therapy, including delaying ovarian aging. MSCs can delay ovarian aging through various mechanisms: antioxidation, differentiation and regeneration, promotion of cell proliferation, inhibition of cell apoptosis, and anti-inflammatory responses. Currently, MSCs transplantation has achieved significant results in animal models, improving ovarian function and enhancing fertility. However, clinical applications still face numerous challenges, such as determining the optimal cell source, transplantation route, dosage, and long-term safety, which require further research. In this review, we will elaborate on the mechanisms of ovarian aging, the modes of action of MSCs, and the mechanisms by which MSCs delay ovarian aging, aiming to provide a theoretical basis for the clinical application of MSCs and to bring breakthroughs in the treatment of diseases such as premature ovarian failure.Graphical Abstract

Introduction

As societies become more prosperous, a new situation is gradually emerging in which the population growth rate began to decline in the 1960 s and is expected to become negative later this century. This change in population dynamics is caused by a sudden decline in fertility that has been going on for half a century [1]. There are many reasons for the decrease in fertility, including physical health problems caused by unhealthy lifestyles, late marriage, reproductive aging, etc. Among them female reproductive aging, which is gradually being emphasized, because the ovary is the source of maintaining female fertility and is a major supplier of endocrine homeostatic hormones [2]. It stores primordial follicles, which contain oocytes and their associated somatic cells. Primordial follicles are produced in the female fetus and do not appear to be replenished after birth [3]. Therefore, we focus on ovarian aging, including Premature Ovarian Failure (POF) and Natural Ovarian Aging (NOA). Conventional solutions such as hormone replacement therapy and antioxidants, while providing some relief, often have limited effects and may cause side effects [4, 5]. Therefore, it has become an important issue to find a safer and more effective method that can radically delay ovarian aging.

In recent years, cell therapy has gradually become the focus of the anti-aging field, among which Mesenchymal Stem Cells (MSCs), a kind of adult stem cells derived from mesoderm, have strong self-renewal ability and multidirectional differentiation potential and can be derived from various tissues, including umbilical cord, umbilical cord blood, placenta, periodontal ligaments, adipose tissue and bone marrow, which are relatively easy to obtain and expand. It is relatively easy to obtain and expand MSCs. More importantly, MSCs have immunomodulatory and tissue repair functions as well as paracrine signalling, which makes them an ideal choice for cell therapy because of their unique physiological characteristics [6–8].

MSCs have been shown to have significant therapeutic effects in the treatment of ovarian aging through their immunomodulatory, anti-inflammatory, and regenerative properties. MSCs promote tissue repair through the secretion of biologically active molecules, such as cytokines, growth factors, and exosomes [9]. These factors stimulate angiogenesis, which will increase blood flow to the ovarian tissue, thereby providing essential oxygen and nutrients. In addition, MSCs helps reduce apoptosis of ovarian cells, especially granulosa cells [10]. Their immunomodulatory effects are also significant and may lead to a decrease in inflammatory markers in the ovarian microenvironment [11]. Together, these effects create a supportive environment for ovarian tissue regeneration and improved ovarian function in patients with ovarian aging. This has led to MSCs-based cell therapy showing great potential for delaying ovarian aging and promoting tissue regeneration [12, 13]. In this paper, we will describe the aging mechanism of the ovary, the mechanism of action of cell therapy against aging, and the application and research progress of cell therapy in delaying ovarian aging.

Mechanisms of ovarian aging

The ovary is the most important regulator of female fertility [14]. The number of reserve follicles is about 400,000 at puberty. It decreases to 1,000 at menopause due to ovarian senescence, with only about 400 follicles capable of developing into dominant follicles and releasing oocytes. In contrast, the others degenerate to atresia at reproductive age [15, 16]. Because of age-related declines in oocyte quality and quantity, human female fertility declines dramatically in the fourth decade of life [17]. Ovarian granulosa cells surround the oocyte and play a key role in follicular development and fate determination. In addition to providing essential nutrients and growth factors for follicular development, they also secrete steroid hormones required for folliculogenesis [18]. Therefore, damage to ovarian granulosa cells may lead to follicular atresia, oocyte death, scanty menstruation, and even amenorrhea, which may negatively affect reproductive function [19].

DNA damage and ovarian senescence

Ovarian senescence is characterized by a gradual decline in the number and quality of oocytes, mainly due to the low number of primordial follicles at birth and high monthly consumption during the reproductive years [20]. The continued accumulation of DNA-damaged cells triggers cellular senescence and death, which ultimately leads to chronic inflammation, loss of function, and disease of cells and tissues [21]. In oocytes, this can lead to poor cell quality, apoptosis, and ultimately infertility and miscarriage [22].

Reactive Oxygen Species (ROS), chemotherapeutic drugs, environmental toxins and ionizing radiation therapy can attack DNA, resulting in Single-Strand Breaks (SSB) and Double-Strand Breaks (DSB), which are particularly severe and difficult to repair [23]. DNA repair occurs through two main pathways: Homologous Recombination (HR) and Non-Homologous End Joining (NHEJ) [24]. HR usually acts in the S to G2 phases, while NHEJ can act at any stage of the cell cycle but is error-prone. Both cooperate and compete with each other to maintain the effectiveness of the DNA repair system [25, 26]. Early primordial follicles are sensitive to DNA damage and may arrest or apoptosis if the damage cannot be repaired. Ataxia Telangiectasia Mutated (ATM) plays a central role in DNA damage monitoring, and HR is the main repair pathway [26]. With age, the DNA repair system is impaired, and DNA damage repair is dysfunctional, which plays an important role in the pathogenesis of ovarian senescence and can directly hinder oocyte meiosis, affect follicular development, accelerate follicular depletion, and ultimately lead to the onset of ovarian senescence [27] (Fig. 1). Genes such as Breast Cancer gene 1 (BRCA1) are closely related to ovarian function, and decreased expression or mutations may lead to reduced ovarian reserve [26, 28]. In addition to DSBs, other types of DNA damage have been associated with ovarian aging, including lesions appearing on a single DNA strand, such as depyrimidines, which the base excision repair process can repair, and in the mouse ovary, the deficiency of the key base excision repair enzyme, DNA polymerase β, which also contributes to ovarian aging [29].

Telomere depletion and ovarian senescence

Telomeres are found at the ends of chromosomes in eukaryotes and consist of tandem DNA repetitive sequences [30]. In most cell types in the human body, telomere shortening results in the loss of DNA during each DNA replication cycle [31, 32]. Some cells, including oocytes, have telomerase, an RNA-dependent DNA polymerase (also known as reverse transcriptase) that ensures proper DNA replication of highly repetitive sequences at telomeres [33]. The occurrence of phenotypic genetic shortening of telomeres can be manifested in reproductive aging in females (Fig. 1). A comparison of telomere length in female leukocytes showed that women with longer telomeres were found to enter menopause later than women with shorter telomeres [34]. Therefore, quantification of telomere length may be used as a biomarker of reproductive aging [35, 36]. Telomeres are susceptible to oxidative damage, and oxidative damage accelerates telomere shortening, leading to aging [37]. Important features of ovarian senescence, such as increased ROS accumulation and decreased ATP production, so Oxidative Stress (OS) induced telomere loss and telomere-driven replicative senescence may be important mechanisms of ovarian senescence [38].

Telomere length and telomerase activity have also been associated with specific age-related diseases. Studies on human granulosa cells have shown that downregulation of telomerase reverse transcriptase mRNA and telomerase activity in Granulosa Cells (GCs) surrounding oocytes is one of the main mechanisms of female gamete degeneration [39]. Specifically, the lack of telomerase activity in human granulosa cells is associated with ovarian insufficiency, and women with “biochemical” Primary Ovarian Insufficiency (POI), an intermediate stage of POI, have reduced telomerase activity and reduced telomerase activity in peripheral blood leukocytes and hair follicle granulosa cells. Telomerase activity is reduced, and telomere length is shorter. Women with biochemical POI have approximately 50% lower telomerase activity and telomere length in these cells compared to women of the same age without POI. Thus, telomere length and telomerase activity may have a significant effect on ovarian aging, and these parameters can be used to study changes and declines in ovarian function [40, 41].

Mitochondrial dysfunction and ovarian senescence

Mitochondria are the central organelles of the apoptotic pathway and have an irreplaceable role in apoptosis [42]. In mitochondria, Cytochrome C (Cytc) acts as an electron carrier in the electron transport chain [43]. B-cell lymphoma-2 (BCL2) and B-cell lymphoma-extra large (BCL-xL) are associated with the BCL2-associated X protein (BAX) and BCL2 Homologous Antagonist/Killer (BAK) to form a heterodimer that maintains the integrity of the mitochondrial outer membrane and prevents mitochondrial apoptosis. When stimulated by apoptosis, BAX/BAK then forms an oligomeric complex and inserts into the mitochondrial outer membrane pore [44], the Mitochondrial Membrane Potential (MMP) decreases, CytC accumulates in the cytoplasm, and the downstream caspase cascade reaction is initiated to induce apoptosis [45]. Consistent with this, many studies have shown that the expression of the apoptotic proteins BAX, Cytc, and Cysteine-aspartic-ase 9 (Caspase 9) is upregulated, while the expression of the anti-apoptotic protein BCL2 is down-regulated in senescent ovaries [46, 47]. In conclusion, mitochondria themselves can sense a decrease in membrane potential, sense apoptotic signals, and initiate the mitochondrial apoptotic pathway. In addition, disruption of oxidative and antioxidant homeostasis produces excessive OS, and ROS are by-products of ATP production in the mitochondrial electron transfer chain. With mitochondrial dysfunction, ROS production increases and can lead to oxidative stress, resulting in damage to cellular components and contributing to the aging process [48]. Elevated levels of ROS in the ovary can affect cellular components, leading to a decrease in ovarian reserve and compromising oocyte developmental capacity [49]. Most of the ROS produced internally are generated by dysfunctional mitochondrial Oxidative Phosphorylation (OXPHOS). This occurs when electrons leak from the respiratory chain pathway, leading to the accumulation of free radicals and disruption of mitochondrial function, and oocyte quality and ovarian function are affected by damaged mitochondria [50].

On the other hand, the ovarian follicle consists of the GCs, the oocyte and the follicular membrane cells, with the GCs providing a supportive environment for oocyte maturation, which is regulated by the surrounding somatic granulosa cells [51]. The GCs communicates directly with the oocyte through gap junctions, which are essential for oocyte competence, embryogenesis, and the transport of lipids, glucose, proteins, and metabolites through the gap junctions, and their interaction with the oocyte determines the quality of follicular development and thus female fertility [52, 53]. If mitochondrial dysfunction occurs, it may interfere with metabolite transport, leading to poor oocyte quality, ovarian senescence and infertility [53, 54].

Delivery route

The choice of route is one of the key factors affecting the distribution, retention, survival and efficacy of cell therapy. No standard has been established, and further research is needed. Mechanisms of action

The therapeutic potential of MSCs is mainly attributed to two aspects: firstly, the replacement of damaged tissues by differentiating into various cell lineages, and secondly, the paracrine function [56], which secretes a variety of soluble factors to exert immunomodulatory, anti-apoptotic, antioxidant and angiogenic effects. In addition, MSCs reduces inflammation and increases cell proliferation during tissue repair by releasing exosomes containing proteins, Messenger RNA (mRNA), Micro Ribonucleic Acid (miRNA) [57].

Differentiation

MSCs are capable of differentiating into various cell types and play a crucial role in the repair of aging tissues and diseased organs [58]. MSCs are isolated from a wide range of sources mainly from bone marrow, umbilical cord, adipose tissue, placenta, amniotic fluid, and dental pulp. Human umbilical cord MSCs, being the youngest primitive adult stem cells, are unaffected by any acquired disease and have the fastest population doubling time. Therefore, it has been identified as a promising source of inoculated cells and the best cell source for stem cell-based anti-aging therapies [59]. MSCs are pluripotent stem cells that have the ability to differentiate into other cells when exposed to an appropriate induction environment [60]. For example, MSCs play an important role in cardiac regenerative medicine because of their ability to differentiate into the major cell types in the vasculature, such as cardiomyocytes, vascular smooth muscle cells, and endothelial cells [61]. MSCs can also differentiate into hepatocyte-like cells, which perform basic hepatic functions such as albumin synthesis, detoxification, and glucose metabolism. These hepatocyte-like cells are essential for restoring liver function in damaged tissues, and thus, MSCs also offers multiple advantages in liver transplantation and regeneration [62].

Paracrine factors

MSCs release a large number of biologically active factors, and this large number of paracrine factors form part of a complex network that is used to protect injured tissues and to promote endogenous repair, regeneration, and immune-mediated effects, which may lead to long-term beneficial effects [63]. Low immunogenicity makes MSCs suitable for allogeneic transplantation [64]. MSCs secrete Monocyte Chemoattractant Protein-1 (MCP-1), Interleukin-2 (IL-2), Interleukin-8 (IL-8), Vascular Endothelial Growth Factor (VEGF), Stromal Cell-Derived Factor-1 (SDF-1), Transforming Growth Factor-beta (TGF-beta), and other immunomodulatory factors. They regulate the immune system [65]. In addition, the immunomodulatory effects of MSCs can be mediated by the interaction of secreted factors with immune cells [66]. Angiogenesis is a complex process that results in the formation of new blood networks and requires soluble growth factors related to endothelial cell growth and differentiation, such as VEGF and Fibroblast Growth Factor (FGF) [67]. VEGF and Angiopoietin-1 (Ang-1) secreted by MSCs can activate endothelial cells, improve metabolism, enhance blood supply and thus promote vascular repair and angiogenesis. Ang-1 can activate endothelial cells, improve metabolism, and enhance blood supply to promote vascular repair and angiogenesis, which helps to deliver oxygen and nutrients to damaged areas [68, 69]. IL-11 secreted by MSCs can exert cytoprotective functions and reduce apoptosis by upregulating the IL-11 receptor complex and heat shock protein 25. In addition, MSCs can paracrine Epidermal growth factor (EGF), Hepatocyte Growth Factor (HGF) and VEGF, leading to PI3K activation and phosphorylation of downstream targets AKT and MDM2, and inhibiting p53-mediated radiation-induced apoptosis [70]. Insulin-like Growth Factor-1 (IGF-1) and IL-6, which lead to elevated levels of secreted frizzled-related protein 2 (SFRP2), an important anti-apoptotic mediator in fibroblast-like cells [71]. In addition, MSCs also protect nerves by upregulating the expression of BCL-2, which exerts its anti-apoptotic effects, and by down-regulating the expression of BAX, BAD, Caspase3 and Caspase7 [72]. MSCs regulate the oxidative and reductive environment by secreting Stanniocalcin-1 (STC1), Heme Oxygenase-1 (HO-1) and Glial Cell Line-Derived Neurotrophic Factor (GDNF) [73]. STC1 secreted by MSCs reduces ROS-induced apoptosis. The release of Extracellular Vesicles (EVs) by MSCs increases cellular resistance to the hypoxic microenvironment, thereby decreasing apoptosis in the hypoxic environment [74]. In addition, MSCs can directly scavenge free radicals, immunomodulate through reactive oxygen species inhibition, promote endogenous antioxidant defences, and alter mitochondrial bioenergetics [75]. The regulation of the redox environment and oxidative stress by MSCs can also reduce oxidative damage by mediating its anti-inflammatory and cytoprotective properties [76]. The last, Exosomes (Exos) are extracellular vesicles secreted by stem cells that maintain cellular homeostasis, remove cellular debris, facilitate cell-organ communication, and play important roles in the regulation of cell death pathways, T-cell differentiation, stem cell regulation, and hematopoiesis [77, 78]. MSCs-Exos can improve the aging status of cells, tissues, and organs in vitro and in vivo. MSCs-Exos also play a role in the regulation of aging and aging-related diseases by modulating aging-related signalling pathways through miRNA regulation [79]. Exosomes have multiple roles and can also act on neurons by delivering miRNAs and growth factors such as FGF-1. They also play an immunomodulatory role by inducing IL10 and Transforming Growth Factor-beta (TGF-β) [80].

Mesenchymal stem cells delay ovarian aging

Antioxidant

Antioxidant is a way for MSCs to slow down ovarian aging, including their ability to scavenge intracellular free radicals and other oxidizing substances, which helps to alleviate oxidative stress in the ovary, protect the ovary to maintain its normal structure and function, and slow down the aging process and to regulate the redox environment through the secretion of STC1, HO-1, and GDNF [57, 76]. In vivo experiments support this mechanism: it has been shown that a cell suspension containing MSC was injected intravenously into the tail vein of 40-week-old mice. Increased levels of ROS, Malondialdehyde (MDA) and Interleukin-6 (IL-6), and decreased levels of Superoxide Dismutase (SOD), Glutathione (GSH) and Interleukin-10 (IL-10) were found in ovarian homogenates of aged mice as compared to controls. In the MSCs group, the levels of ROS, MDA and IL-6 were decreased, and SOD, GSH and IL-10 were increased. The demonstration that stem cells significantly restored the levels of inflammation and oxidative stress indicators in the ovaries of senescent mice provided prerequisites and clues for further studies [81]. In addition, MSCs from different sources have shown similar functions, such as Human Placenta-Derived Mesenchymal Stem Cells (HPD-MSC) inhibited oxidative stress-induced changes (e.g., centrosome expansion, hyperproliferation, and accumulation of DNA damage) to retard ovarian aging [82]. In situ transplantation of human umbilical cord-derived mesenchymal stem cells (hUC-MSCs) improved the level of oxidative stress and inhibited autophagy and apoptosis in the ovary [83] (Table 1).

Promote proliferation and inhibit apoptosis

MSCs are capable of secreting a variety of growth factors and cytokines (e.g., VEGF, HGF, IGF-I, STC1, TGF-β, FGF, and GM-CSF, etc.), which can directly promote the proliferation of ovarian cells, increase the number of healthy cells, and compensate for the reduction of cells due to senescence or injury, thus maintaining the homeostasis of ovarian tissues [71, 84]. In terms of anti-apoptosis, MSCs significantly increase the ratio of BCL-2/BAX by secreting anti-apoptotic factors such as BCL-2 and Survivin, which reduces the sensitivity of the cells to pathological stimuli and effectively blocks apoptotic signaling [57]. In addition, miR-644-5p carried by Bone Marrow-derived MSCs (BMSCs) and their derived Exosomes can significantly reduce apoptosis and follicular atresia in ovarian granulosa cells and promote follicular development and localized vascularization by targeting and inhibiting p53 protein expression [85] (Table 1).

Umbilical cord-derived MSCs (UC-MSCs) and their derived EVs exert therapeutic effects through multiple pathways. Studies have shown that Clusterin (CLU) in UC-MSCs-EVs can activate the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway, inhibit granulosa cell apoptosis and promote their proliferation, thus effectively restoring ovarian function in mice with premature ovarian failure [86]. Meanwhile, UC-MSCs also promoted the proliferation of human endometrial stromal cells through paracrine effects, inhibited apoptosis, and increased the expression levels of Estrogen Receptor α (ERα) and Progesterone Receptor (PR), which in turn improved fertility [85] (Table 1).

Recent studies have revealed that MSCs-derived exosomes (MSC-Exos) play a key role in the recovery of ovarian function. In a Natural Aging (NOA) mouse model, human Umbilical Cord MSCs-derived exosomes (hUCMSC-Exos) can inhibit PTEN expression by delivering miR-21-5p, significantly reduce the level of oxidative stress and the rate of apoptosis, while promoting ovarian cell proliferation and effectively improving ovarian function [87] (Table 1). In addition to paracrine exosomes, MSCs also secrete HGF, which improves the structure and function of ovarian tissues and delays the process of ovarian aging by activating the PI3K/AKT pathway [88] (Table 1). In addition, adipose tissue-derived MSCs can reduce apoptosis of GCs by secreting specific cytokines, whereas human menstrual blood-derived stem cells restore ovarian function by reducing ovarian tissue fibrosis and inhibiting apoptosis [16]. These findings suggest that different sources of MSCs and their secreted factors (including exosomes and various cytokines) act synergistically through multi-targets and multi-pathways to provide new strategies and ideas for the treatment of ovarian aging [89, 90]. MSCs and their derivatives can comprehensively improve the ovarian microenvironment through the modulation of multiple biological processes such as proliferation, apoptosis, angiogenesis, and fibrosis, and ultimately achieve the goal of delaying ovarian aging and restoring ovarian function.

Differentiation potential and regenerative capacity

MSCs have multidirectional differentiation potential and can differentiate to form a variety of cell types [60, 91, 92], a property that allows them to play an important role in ovarian tissue repair. Studies have shown that hUC-MSCs are capable of differentiating into oocyte-like cells under specific conditions, and these cells express a variety of germ cell-specific markers (Table 1), including: transcription factors: Octamer-Binding Transcription Factor 4 (Oct4) and Stimulated by Retinoic Acid 8 (Stra8); germ cell markers: Vasa Homolog (Vasa) and Azoospermia Deleted in Azoospermia-Like (DAZL); zona pellucida proteins: zona pellucida Glycoprotein 2 (ZP2), zona pellucida Glycoprotein 3 (ZP3) [93]. Remarkably, these oocyte-like cells obtained by differentiation not only express specific markers, but also secrete estradiol, which is essential for the maintenance of follicular pool reserve and ovarian endocrine function [93]. Further studies at the molecular level confirmed that hUC-MSCs were able to express germ cell-specific mRNA and protein markers during differentiation [92], suggesting their potential to differentiate into functional germ cells.

This differentiation ability of MSCs provides a new therapeutic strategy for the reconstruction of ovarian function, and by supplementing oocyte-like cells, it may be possible to reverse the decrease in the number of follicles due to aging or injury, thus restoring the normal physiological function of the ovary [91–93]. However, it should be noted that these studies are mainly in the experimental stage, and whether the oocyte-like cells obtained by differentiation possess complete meiotic ability and fertilization potential still need to be further verified.

Anti-inflammatory response

During ovarian aging, there is often an inflammatory response that is activated thus leading to intracellular oxidative stress in the ovary [94], and MSCs can slow down ovarian aging by producing anti-inflammatory factors to reduce the intracellular inflammatory response [95]. Several animal experiments confirmed the therapeutic efficacy of MSCs in a mouse model of cyclophosphamide-induced Premature Ovarian Failure (POF): a POF model was induced by intraperitoneal injection of cyclophosphamide, treated with UC-MSCs, and then examined for ovarian functional parameters, GC apoptosis, and inflammation, and morphological staining showed that UC-MSCs treatment increased the ovarian size and the number of primary and secondary follicles, but decreased the number of atretic follicles. Estradiol levels were increased, and follicle-stimulating hormone levels were decreased in the UC-MSCs-treated group compared to the POF group. UC-MSCs inhibited cyclophosphamide-induced GC apoptosis and inflammation, which suggests that UC-MSCs transplantation can attenuate chemotherapy-induced ovarian damage and improve ovarian function in POF mice through the anti-apoptotic and anti-inflammatory effects of the paracrine mechanism [96]. In the macaque model: some experiments were performed to sequence the single nucleus transcriptome of macaque ovaries treated with Highly Active Mesenchymal Stem Cells (HA-MSCs). A model of senescence of Human ovarian Granulosa Cells (hGCs) was established, and the migration capacity of cells after co-culture of HA-MSCs and senescent hGCs in culture was determined. It was found that the therapeutic effect of HA-MSCs on the senescent ovary was mainly to positively regulate the functions of endothelial cells, granulosa cells, and monocytes, including participation in the construction and remodelling of the vascular system. This promotes oocyte nutrition and maturation and inhibits the onset and development of inflammation [97]. In addition, it has been demonstrated that multiple introductions of human embryonic stem cell-derived mesenchymal progenitor cells may reduce inflammation and fibrosis by down-regulating the production of myeloid-derived suppressor cells in the bone marrow, which improves the ovarian environment and host, and prolongs fertility and fecundity in perimenopausal female mice [98], which, in turn, delays ovarian aging (Table 1).

Mechanisms of delayed ovarian aging by MSCs

Conclusion

In this review, the mechanisms of ovarian aging, the mode of action of MSCs, and how MSCs can slow down ovarian aging are presented. DNA damage, telomere depletion, and mitochondrial dysfunction all contribute to the aging of the ovary. MSCs have the potential to differentiate and can secrete immune regulatory factors, antioxidant factors, anti-apoptotic factors, proangiogenic factors, and exosomes, which make them ideal for the treatment of premature ovarian aging and the slowing down of ovarian aging. These properties make it an ideal choice for the treatment of premature ovarian failure and for delaying ovarian aging. It has also been demonstrated that MSCs can significantly improve ovarian function through animal experiments [99]. MSCs can reduce the apoptosis of ovarian cells and promote the generation of new cells through the secretion of various growth factors. Meanwhile, MSCs can also protect the ovaries from external factors by reducing inflammatory responses and oxidative damage through anti-inflammatory and anti-oxidative stress effects. In addition, MSCs can promote ovarian angiogenesis, improve ovarian blood supply, and provide a better microenvironment for follicular development [81]. The research on MSCs in the field of delaying ovarian aging has made remarkable progress and brings new hope for women’s health.

Looking ahead, the application of MSCs in delaying ovarian aging is promising. Although MSCs therapeutic technology has a research record in animals, its clinical implementation is still in its infancy. The long-term safety and potential risks remain unclear, with the survival, differentiation, and long-term effects of MSCs in vivo still uncharted territory lacking safety assurance. Critical technical bottlenecks persist in clinical application, including optimizing MSCs transplantation routes, determining optimal cell doses and treatment timing, and establishing monitoring technologies capable of accurately tracking cells in vivo and quantifying their regenerative effects, all representing unresolved technical barriers. Therefore, in the future, more in-depth clinical research should be conducted. Efforts should be made to develop personalized treatment plans for women with ovarian hypoplasia in order to address the challenges of facing fertility, protect women, and increase the fertility rate, as well as to further optimize the isolation, culture, and transplantation techniques of MSCs to improve the efficacy and safety of the treatment.

In conclusion, MSCs has great potential to delay ovarian aging. With continuous research and technological advancement, MSCs is expected to become an important means of treating premature ovarian failure and delaying ovarian aging for women’s health. We look forward to more breakthrough research results in the future that will benefit women.
