Surgical method selection for cataracts after pars plana vitrectomy in high myopia: comparison between femtosecond laser-assisted and conventional phacoemulsification
Authors: Lin Wen, Yukun Yang, Hao Lian
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe surgical safety of cataract patients with high myopia after pars plana vitrectomy (PPV) surgery deserves more attention. Currently, there are limited reported studies on the use of femtosecond laser-assisted cataract surgery (FLACS) to treat cataracts following PPV surgery in patients with high myopia. This study was designed to compare the clinical effects of FLACS and conventional phacoemulsification surgery (CPS) in these patients.MethodsA retrospective comparative cohort was conducted. The primary endpoint was best-corrected visual acuity (BCVA, logMAR) at 1 month after surgery. Confounding by indication was addressed using stabilized inverse probability of treatment weighting (IPTW) based on prespecified baseline covariates, with a doubly robust ANCOVA adjusting for baseline BCVA and clinical covariates. Longitudinal BCVA and intraocular pressure (IOP) were analyzed using linear mixed-effects models. Secondary endpoints were prespecified into families (Vision, IOP, Complication and adverse events). Per-time-point contrasts were descriptive and not multiplicity-adjusted; primary inference relied on Type III omnibus tests.ResultsSeventy-seven eyes were included (FLACS, n = 33; CPS, n = 44). Post-weighting covariate balance was excellent (all standardized mean differences ≤ 0.07). The adjusted mean difference in Month-1 BCVA (FLACS − CPS) was − 0.17 logMAR (95% CI − 0.36 to 0.03), suggesting a possible benefit with uncertainty spanning negligible to moderate improvement. Early vision favored FLACS at Day-1 (− 0.29 logMAR; 95% CI − 0.53 to − 0.06) and the difference at Week-1 was small and imprecise (− 0.22 logMAR; 95% CI − 0.45 to 0.02); estimates converged by Month-1 to Month-3. IOP changed over time without an overall group difference; a small and imprecise higher IOP at Week-1, with estimates crossing zero. Nd: YAG capsulotomy rates were low and similar between groups.ConclusionsIn highly myopic post-PPV eyes, FLACS was associated with faster early visual recovery versus CPS, whereas 1–3 month outcomes were broadly similar. Findings are associative and may be influenced by residual confounding.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12886-025-04472-3.Keywords: Pars plana vitrectomy, Femtosecond laser-assisted cataract surgery, High myopia, Best-corrected visual acuity, Intraocular pressure

Introduction

The incidence of retinal detachment is relatively high among the myopic population, and the risk of rhegmatogenous retinal detachment (RRD) increases with the progression of myopia [1]. Pars plana vitrectomy (PPV) is a common surgical procedure for treating retinal detachment [2]. However, with the widespread use of PPV and its combination with silicone oil or gas tamponade, cataracts have become the most common complication after PPV [3]. The incidence of cataracts can reach 100% within 2 years after PPV [4]. Currently, conventional phacoemulsification surgery (CPS) combined with intraocular lens (IOL) implantation represents the standard surgical approach for cataracts following PPV.

In cataract surgery for highly myopic eyes, factors such as long axial length (AL), a deep anterior chamber, a hard nucleus, and fragile zonules pose additional challenges. Femtosecond laser-assisted cataract surgery (FLACS) has demonstrated promising efficacy and safety in treating cataracts in patients with high myopia [5, 6].

Cataract surgery after PPV in high myopia, which combines the characteristics of high myopia and post-PPV conditions, presents greater surgical difficulties and a greater risk of complications. Therefore, the surgical safety of such patients deserves closer attention. In the context of cataracts following PPV, FLACS has demonstrated safe and effective results [7–9]. However, until now, there have been no reported studies on the use of FLACS to treat cataracts after PPV in high myopia patients. This retrospective study analyses the outcomes of both FLACS and CPS in the treatment of cataracts after PPV in high myopia, aiming to evaluate the effectiveness of FLACS in this specific patient population.

Materials and methods

Study design

The medical records of cataract patients who underwent PPV surgery and subsequently received cataract surgery at Chengdu Aidi Eye Hospital from January 2023 to August 2024 were reviewed. The subjects meeting the inclusion criteria for this study were divided into two groups on the basis of the surgical method. All patients were informed about both the FLACS and CPS procedures. Following clinical and socioeconomic considerations, patients eligible for laser treatment and willing to bear the associated costs were subjected to FLACS; otherwise, CPS was performed. Surgical informed consent was obtained from all patients.

The inclusion criteria were as follows

age ≥ 18 years; axial length (AL) ≥ 26 mm; spherical equivalent refractive error ≤ − 6.00 D prior to retinal detachment [10]; PPV surgery performed for RRD with a postoperative period of more than 6 months; presence of definite lens opacity; and log of minimum angle of resolution (logMAR) best corrected visual acuity (BCVA) less than 0.4.

The exclusion criteria included

a history of previous corneal surgery; a silicone oil-filled eye after PPV; significant lens dislocation; intraocular pressure (IOP) > 21 mmHg without treatment; preoperative detection of cystoid macular edema (CME) or retinal redetachment; and inadequate follow-up of less than three months after cataract surgery.

Ethical approval

This retrospective study adhered to the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Ethics Committee of Chengdu Aidi Eye Hospital.

Preoperative examination and preparation

All patients underwent comprehensive preoperative routine examinations, including uncorrected distance visual acuity (UCVA) and BCVA, IOP, slit-lamp and fundus examination, corneal topography, corneal endothelial cell count, A/B ultrasound, IOLMaster 700 (Carl Zeiss, Germany), macular OCT (Spectralis HRA + OCT, Germany), and wide-angle fundus photography. Initially, the measurement results from the IOLMaster 700 were used to calculate the IOL diopter. If the measurement could not be obtained, the results from A/B ultrasound were adopted. A single-focus foldable IOL was selected, with a target refractive power set in the range of − 2.00 D to − 3.00 D.

Surgical methods and perioperative medication

All surgeries were performed via the same phacoemulsification machine (Bausch and Lomb BL11110, USA) by the same experienced cataract surgeon.

The FLACS group underwent femtosecond laser surgery via the Catalys Precision Laser System (Johnson & Johnson Vision, USA). Before surgery, the routine parameter settings included a capsulotomy diameter of 5.2 to 5.5 mm, a sideport incision of 1.2 mm, and a main incision of 2.2 mm in the clear cornea. The prechopping mode adopted a radial combined with a grid pattern, and the capsulotomy was centered relative to the capsular bag. After the pupils were dilated, 0.5% proparacaine hydrochloride ophthalmic solution (S.A. ALCON-COUVREUR N.V., Belgium) was used for topical anaesthesia, followed by sterilization with 0.5% povidone-iodine. A femtosecond laser docking suction connection was subsequently performed, and cataract surgery was completed under a surgical microscope after the laser procedure. The parameters of the phacoemulsification machine were personalized on the basis of the axial length and anterior chamber stability. The sideport and main incisions were separated, and medical sodium hyaluronate gel (Bloomage Biotech, China) was injected into the anterior chamber. The incised anterior capsule was removed with manual capsulotomy. In cases of incomplete capsulotomy, careful manual capsulotomy was performed along the laser capsulotomy to ensure continuity of the capsulotomy. After sufficient hydrodissection and hydrodelineation, the lens nucleus was removed via phacoemulsification. For patients with a continuous anterior capsulotomy and an intact capsular bag, a capsular tension ring (CTR) was routinely implanted, followed by the implantation of a foldable IOL into the capsular bag. Tobramycin and dexamethasone ophthalmic ointment (ALCON CUSI s.a., Spain) was applied to the conjunctival sac, and the operated eye was bandaged.

In the CPS group, patients were placed in a supine position after dilation. Topical anaesthesia was achieved via the use of 0.5% proparacaine hydrochloride ophthalmic solution. A 1 mm side incision and a 2.2 mm main incision were made via a disposable surgical blade. Medical sodium hyaluronate gel was injected into the anterior chamber. If necessary, indocyanine green (Dandong Yichuang, China) was used for anterior capsule staining. Manual continuous circular capsulotomy was performed via capsulotomy forceps, with a diameter of approximately 5–5.5 mm. The subsequent steps were the same as those in the FLACS group.

The perioperative medications used included levofloxacin eye drops (produced by Santen Pharmaceutical Co., Ltd., China) and pranoprofen eye drops (produced by Senju Pharmaceutical Co., Ltd., Japan) before surgery. After surgery, patients were treated with tobramycin and dexamethasone eyedrops combined with pranoprofen eyedrops for one month. The tobramycin and dexamethasone eyedrops were gradually reduced weekly. For eyes with a postoperative IOP > 21 mmHg, antiglaucoma drugs were added to control the IOP. For eyes with an IOP ≥ 25 mmHg, antiglaucoma drugs were used in combination with fluid drainage through a secondary incision.

Observation indicators

Preoperative data, including age, sex, lens nucleus hardness (graded according to the Emery nuclear hardness classification system), BCVA, corneal endothelial cell density (ECD), AL, and IOP, were collected for all patients. Surgical records and postoperative follow-up notes were reviewed to gather information on intraoperative and postoperative complications, as well as BCVA and IOP at 1 day, 1 week, 1 month, and 3 months postoperatively.

Statistical analysis

Data management and descriptive summaries were performed in SPSS 27.0 (IBM Corp.), and all modeling and inference were conducted in R 4.4.1. Continuous variables are reported as mean (SD) or median [IQR] as appropriate. Baseline characteristics were summarized with standardized mean differences (SMDs) and, for exposure-covariate screening only, hypothesis tests (see below). All BCVA values were converted to logMAR. Model diagnostics included residual Q-Q plots and Shapiro-Wilk tests where appropriate.

To inform propensity-score specification, we compared pre-exposure covariates between FLACS and CPS using χ²/Fisher’s exact tests (categorical) and t/Wilcoxon tests (continuous, as appropriate). These P values are solely for screening exposure-covariate associations; they are not intended to establish baseline equivalence or to support causal claims. Variables measured after treatment assignment (e.g., post-op BCVA/IOP, Nd: YAG) were not included in the screening and were not entered into the propensity model.

The prespecified primary endpoint was Month-1 BCVA (logMAR). To address confounding by indication, we estimated stabilized inverse probability of treatment weights (IPTW) from a propensity model including age, sex, axial length, lens nucleus hardness, baseline BCVA, baseline IOP, ECD, planned CTR, and calendar time. Time since PPV and silicone oil history were not included in the main propensity model, but were considered in sensitivity analyses to test model robustness. Weighting quality was assessed by SMDs and effective sample size (ESS). The primary contrast (FLACS vs. CPS) was estimated using IPTW-weighted ANCOVA with group and baseline BCVA; a doubly robust specification additionally adjusted for age, sex, axial length, lens grade, ECD, baseline IOP, CTR planning, and calendar time. We report adjusted mean differences with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) (two-sided α = 0.05 for the primary endpoint).

Longitudinal endpoints. Repeated BCVA and IOP were analyzed using linear mixed-effects models with fixed effects for group, time, and group×time, a subject-level random intercept, and adjustment for the corresponding baseline value and prespecified covariates (age, sex, axial length, lens grade, ECD; plus baseline IOP for IOP models). Primary inference relied on Type III omnibus tests (time, group, group×time).Per-time-point EMM contrasts. We additionally report per-time-point estimated marginal mean (EMM) contrasts (FLACS–CPS) with β, SE, 95% CIs, t, and unadjusted P values for clinical interpretation. These exploratory tests were not used for formal hypothesis testing and were not multiplicity-adjusted.

Multiplicity plan for secondary endpoints. Secondary endpoints were prespecified into three families (Vision: BCVA at Day-1/Week-1/Month-1/Month-3; IOP: same four time points; adverse events). Because per-time-point contrasts for BCVA/IOP are exploratory, no multiplicity control was applied to families (i) and (ii). Adverse events were exploratory and reported descriptively.

In this cohort, some Nd: YAG procedures occurred at scheduled follow-up visits (Month-1 and Month-3). Therefore, we verified that the BCVA used for the primary analysis was recorded before any Nd: YAG capsulotomy in the study eye; the primary endpoint is not affected by Nd: YAG. Mixed-effects models for repeated BCVA included routine follow-up timepoints (Day-1, Week-1, Month-1, Month-3). Because Nd: YAG was performed at the Month-1 visit in 5 eyes and at the Month-3 visit in 3 eyes, we conducted prespecified sensitivity analyses: excluding post-Nd: YAG BCVA recorded.These sensitivity analyses were designed to confirm robustness of the longitudinal inferences to potential post-procedure improvements. Nd: YAG capsulotomy was summarized using cumulative incidences at scheduled follow-up windows (Month-1 and Month-3) with Wilson 95% CIs. Exact per-eye dates were not captured; events were recorded at the visit when performed.

Intraoperative and postoperative adverse events were summarized descriptively (counts and percentages). we reported risk difference (RD) in percentage points and risk ratio (RR) with two-sided 95% CIs. CIs for RD used the Newcombe–Wilson method; CIs for RR used Katz log method. P values for between-group comparisons were obtained from Fisher’s exact test. No multiplicity adjustment was applied to these exploratory safety endpoints; results should be interpreted descriptively.

Results

Baseline characteristics and confounding screening

A total of 77 cases (77 eyes) were included in the analysis (FLACS, n = 33; CPS, n = 44). Table 1 summarizes preoperative baseline characteristics only, with balance described by SMDs. The two groups were broadly similar in age and sex distribution (SMDs near zero). Small directional imbalances were observed for selected ophthalmic parameters: axial length tended to be shorter in FLACS (mean 28.4 mm vs. 29.3 mm; SMD − 0.43), and ECD was slightly lower (2675 vs. 2764 cells/mm²; SMD − 0.24). Baseline BCVA was comparable (1.63 vs. 1.70 logMAR; SMD − 0.14). Silicone-oil history and planned CTR were similarly distributed. (Table 2)

Postoperative variables (post-op IOP/BCVA) and Nd: YAG occurred after treatment assignment and were therefore not considered as confounders; they are summarized elsewhere for context.

To identify prognostic covariates for the primary endpoint (Month-1 BCVA), we fitted a univariable linear regression model using only pre-exposure variables (Table 3). Baseline BCVA was strongly associated with Month-1 BCVA (β = 0.441, 95% CI 0.200-0.683). Age showed a borderline association (β = −0.009 per year, 95% CI − 0.020 to 0.001), and ECD displayed a weak relationship (β ≈ 0.000, 95% CI 0.000 to 0.001). Axial length, pre-op IOP, and lens nucleus hardness grades were not materially associated with the outcome in this sample. And consistent with clinical judgment, we pre-specified the PS model to include age, sex, axial length, ECD, baseline BCVA, baseline IOP (plus surgical calendar time, silicone oil history, and planned CTR), while excluding any post-treatment measurements (post-op BCVA/IOP, Nd: YAG). Lens nucleus hardness was retained in primary adjustment for clinical relevance and examined in sensitivity analyses. This dual criterion—association with exposure and/or outcome and clinical plausibility—guided the subsequent IPTW and outcome modeling. (Table 3)

Weight estimation

Balance diagnostics

Covariate balance after weighting was excellent, with all SMDs ≤ 0.07, well below the conventional 0.10 threshold. Representative SMDs were: Age − 0.007; Sex 0.007; Axial length − 0.062; Lens nucleus hardness (Grade II − 0.025; Grade III − 0.005; Grade IV 0.008; Grade V 0.022); ECD − 0.017; BCVA_pre − 0.013; IOP_pre 0.014. These results indicate that IPTW achieved good balance across all prespecified baseline covariates. (Table S1, Figure S1)

Effective sample size (ESS)

The ESS decreased modestly from 77 unweighted (FLACS = 33; CPS = 44) to ≈ 71.8 after weighting (FLACS ≈ 41.1; CPS ≈ 30.7), suggesting stable weights without undue influence from a small number of observations. Such attenuation is expected and supports the adequacy of the weighting model.

Weighted outcome models

IPTW-only (crude weighted) model

In the IPTW-only analysis comparing Month-1 BCVA (logMAR) between groups, the weighted mean for the CPS reference was 0.758 (95% CI 0.598–0.918). The FLACS − CPS contrast was − 0.170 logMAR (95% CI − 0.384 to 0.045). The negative sign indicates better vision (lower logMAR) in FLACS on average; however, the compatibility interval includes zero, so estimates range from negligible to moderate benefit. (Table 3)

Doubly robust model

In a doubly robust specification that combined stabilized IPTW with an ANCOVA adjustment for baseline covariates, the between-group contrast in Month-1 BCVA (logMAR) favored FLACS: FLACS − CPS = − 0.17 (95% CI − 0.36 to 0.03; P = 0.09). This estimate is compatible with a possible benefit of FLACS, although the confidence interval includes negligible effects and statistical significance was not reached. Among baseline predictors, baseline BCVA was strongly associated with the outcome (β = 0.32, 95% CI 0.13–0.52), indicating that worse preoperative acuity predicted worse Month-1 acuity. ECD also showed an independent association with better visual outcome (β = 0.00035, 95% CI 0.00008-0.00063). Age exhibited a borderline association (β = −0.012 per year, 95% CI − 0.025 to 0.000). Sex, axial length, preoperative IOP, and lens nucleus hardness were not materially associated with Month-1 BCVA after adjustment. (Table 5, Table S2)

Longitudinal analysis

We modeled repeated measures using linear mixed-effects models with fixed effects for group (FLACS vs. CPS), time (pre-op, Day 1, Week 1, Month 1, Month 3), and their interaction (group×time), and a random intercept per eye to account for within-eye correlation. Models adjusted for prespecified baseline covariates (age, sex [Male/Female], axial length, lens grade, ECD, pre-op IOP), with IPTW applied to address baseline confounding. BCVA outcomes were defined as measurements recorded before any Nd: YAG in that eye; post-Nd: YAG values were handled in sensitivity analyses. Model-based EMMs and 95% CIs are presented at each timepoint; per-timepoint contrasts are descriptive and intended for clinical interpretation rather than formal hypothesis testing.

BCVA mixed-effects model

BCVA improved over time in both groups (Fig. 1), but the group main effect and group×time interaction was not significant. Descriptive EMM contrasts (FLACS − CPS, logMAR) indicated an early advantage for FLACS that attenuated over follow-up: Day 1: −0.29 (95% CI − 0.53 to − 0.06), Week 1: −0.22 (95% CI − 0.45 to 0.02), Month 1: −0.18 (95% CI − 0.42 to 0.05), Month 3: −0.16 (95% CI − 0.39 to 0.08).

The pattern was consistent with faster early visual recovery in FLACS, with between-group differences narrowing by Month 1–3. In covariate effects, worse baseline BCVA predicted worse postoperative acuity, and higher ECD was associated with slightly better vision; other adjusters contributed minimally. Random-intercept variability indicated meaningful between-eye heterogeneity, well accommodated by the model. Sensitivity analyses

To address potential influence of Nd: YAG, we repeated longitudinal models by excluding post-Nd: YAG BCVA. Estimates were materially unchanged compared with the main analysis (Table S5, Table S6).

Discussion

Nuclear cataracts are highly prevalent in eyes with high myopia [11], particularly in those undergoing PPV. In this study, all eyes exhibited varying degrees of lens nuclear opacity, and over 79% were graded as Emery III or above, highlighting the typical severity in this patient population. Our findings contribute to a growing body of literature exploring surgical strategies for this challenging group, especially regarding the relative merits of FLACS versus CPS.

Early advantage of FLACS and overall equivalence

In this cohort of eyes after prior PPV, FLACS was associated with faster early visual recovery compared with CPS, with Day-1 to Week-1 between-group differences on the order of ~ 0.2–0.3 logMAR. These findings align with the hypothesis that FLACS may contribute to faster visual rehabilitation, likely due to lower ultrasound energy use and reduced collateral tissue trauma [7–9]. By Month-1 to Month-3, point estimates favored FLACS but the 95% CIs were compatible with both small benefit and near-null effects, consistent with prior reports that final visual acuity outcomes are broadly comparable between FLACS and CPS [7–9, 12].

The doubly robust IPTW-adjusted model at Month 1 also showed a favorable trend for FLACS (mean difference − 0.17 logMAR), although the 95% CI crossed zero. Importantly, this analysis adjusted for potential confounding variables including baseline vision, age, axial length, corneal ECD, and other covariates, increasing the reliability of the observed trend. Notably, baseline BCVA and ECD were significant predictors of postoperative visual outcome, consistent with their known roles in determining surgical prognosis. These findings suggest that while FLACS may not offer superior final visual acuity, it facilitates faster early recovery, which may be clinically relevant for patient satisfaction and functional recovery.

The pattern of an early BCVA advantage with convergence by 1–3 months suggests FLACS may accelerate visual rehabilitation without a clear longer-term separation in average acuity. A difference near 0.2–0.3 logMAR in the first postoperative days can be noticeable to patients, particularly when early return to function is a priority (e.g., monocular patients, work demands). By contrast, month-scale averages indicate limited sustained difference at the population level.

Corneal edema and energy considerations

Although no statistically significant difference in corneal edema incidence was observed between groups, the earlier visual recovery in the FLACS group may reflect less early corneal compromise. Prior studies have shown that FLACS reduces phacoemulsification energy and effective phaco time, mitigating endothelial damage and stromal hydration [7, 8]. However, the absence of intraoperative ultrasound parameter records in this retrospective study limited direct comparisons. Nonetheless, the improved early BCVA offers indirect support for reduced tissue trauma in FLACS.

Intraocular pressure (IOP): transient elevation in FLACS group

In this study, both groups demonstrated a typical postoperative IOP course: elevation at Day 1 and Week 1, followed by gradual reduction toward or below baseline levels at Month 1 and Month 3. However, detailed longitudinal modeling revealed a transient IOP elevation in the FLACS group at Week 1 (estimated difference: +1.73 mmHg, 95% CI − 0.11 to 3.57), whereas other timepoints showed minimal between-group differences. This finding suggests that FLACS may be associated with a short-term rise in IOP, which deserves further attention, especially in eyes with glaucoma risk.

Notably, within-group comparisons revealed that Week-1 IOP in the FLACS group was significantly elevated compared to its own baseline, even though this difference was not statistically significant when compared to the CPS group. These observations differ from findings reported by Zhao L et al. [5], and warrant pathophysiological interpretation.

The transient IOP elevation observed after FLACS may be attributed to several factors. One possible explanation is the mechanical effect of suction and docking during femtosecond laser application, which can cause an acute rise in IOP during the procedure [13]. Previous studies have documented IOP surges immediately (within 1 h) after FLACS procedures due to mechanical compression and aqueous outflow disruption [14]. Second, FLACS triggers higher levels of prostaglandin and inflammatory cytokines in the anterior chamber compared to CPS, potentially due to laser-induced disruption of ocular tissues and capsulotomy-related cytokine release [15].

Patients with high myopia may exhibit altered biomechanical and anatomical responses to cataract surgery. High myopia has been suggested to attenuate the typical IOP-lowering effect of phacoemulsification, possibly due to increased scleral rigidity or deeper anterior chambers [16]. Furthermore, in post-PPV eyes, the trabecular meshwork may exhibit impaired inflammatory recovery, and the concurrent use of corticosteroid eye drops further predisposes these eyes to transient steroid-induced ocular hypertension [17, 18].

Although this IOP elevation was transient and manageable in all cases—with no sustained elevation or glaucoma-related complications—it highlights the need for close postoperative monitoring, particularly in high-risk patients such as those with preexisting optic nerve damage or glaucoma susceptibility. Prophylactic or early intervention with IOP-lowering medications may be considered in select cases undergoing FLACS after PPV.

These findings suggest that our findings provide mechanistic plausibility and quantitative support for a short-lived but notable IOP response after FLACS in vitrectomized highly myopic eyes. The transient IOP elevation may be attributed to multiple factors, including laser-induced physiological responses, changes in aqueous humor dynamics, structural vulnerability in highly myopic eyes, and early postoperative inflammation.

Intraoperative complications and postoperative safety outcomes

PPV has the potential to compromise the integrity of the zonular fibers, rendering the lens apparatus more vulnerable to intraoperative complications such as lens subluxation or capsular instability during subsequent cataract surgery [19, 20]. In our cohort of post-PPV high myopia patients, this risk was a key concern. Previous literature presents mixed findings: Asif MI et al. [7] and Cai L et al. [8] reported no intraoperative complications with either FLACS or CPS in similar populations, while Wang EF et al. [21] suggested a potential advantage of FLACS in reducing complications (0% vs. 12%).

In our study, however, intraoperative complication rates were comparable between FLACS and CPS. Specifically, two eyes (6.06%) in the FLACS group experienced incomplete capsulotomy and nuclear prechopping, both of which were attributed to silicone oil remnants interfering with laser penetration [22]. Nevertheless, manual rescue techniques—such as employing continuous curvilinear capsulorhexis to complete anterior capsule removal [23]—were effective, and no case of anterior capsular rupture or dropped nucleus occurred. In the CPS group, similar rates of lens dislocation or capsule instability were observed. These findings suggest that when intraoperative strategies are optimized—including the use of viscoelastic agents to stabilize the anterior chamber and pressure-balancing maneuvers as described by Li X et al. [24]—the overall complication rates can be minimized irrespective of the surgical modality.

Notably, our adjusted and weighted analyses confirmed no statistically significant differences in intraoperative complication rates between the groups, reinforcing the interpretation that FLACS does not offer a clear intraoperative safety advantage in this context.

Cystoid macular edema (CME) and posterior segment events

CME is one of the most common sight-threatening complications following cataract surgery. Its incidence is known to be influenced by inflammatory burden and vitreous integrity [25, 26]. In our study, no cases of CME were detected in either group during the follow-up period. One plausible explanation is that prior vitrectomy may exert a protective effect, potentially by removing inflammatory mediators from the vitreous reservoir. This is consistent with previous reports that the absence of vitreous humor may reduce the risk of CME, although this hypothesis warrants further mechanistic exploration in future imaging or biomarker-based studies.

Posterior capsule opacification (PCO) and Nd: YAG laser capsulotomy

During follow-up, posterior capsular plaques or PCO developed in 22 eyes (28.6%). The incidence was similar between FLACS and CPS groups (P >0.05), consistent with previous studies suggesting that FLACS does not reduce the long-term risk of PCO in post-PPV high myopia patients. While Nd: YAG treatment is typically effective, its use carries potential risks, particularly retinal tears or detachment in eyes with prior posterior segment pathology [27].

However, in our cohort, only two eyes (one per group) developed peripheral retinal breaks, and neither had undergone Nd: YAG prior to this event. Both cases were managed successfully with laser photocoagulation. Importantly, no retinal redetachment or cystoid changes were observed during follow-up, indicating that the risk of serious retinal sequelae remained low across both surgical strategies. We postulate that prior PPV may confer some degree of prophylaxis against post-capsulotomy retinal complications by stabilizing the peripheral vitreoretinal interface. Nonetheless, vigilant fundus surveillance remains essential, especially in highly myopic eyes with a history of complex vitreoretinal disease.

Clinical and economic implications

While FLACS conferred earlier visual recovery, our data do not directly address cost-effectiveness. Several studies have demonstrated that FLACS is not cost-effective compared with CPS [12, 28]. Cost considerations may influence technique selection and should be interpreted with external economic evidence, not from these clinical outcomes alone. Given the anatomical alterations in this population—such as long axial length, posterior staphyloma, and possible foveoschisis—visual gains following cataract removal are often limited by retinal comorbidities, irrespective of the anterior segment technique employed.

Therefore, the decision to employ FLACS should be individualized, factoring in patient preferences, socioeconomic context, and surgeon experience. For many patients, CPS remains a viable and resource-efficient alternative.

Study limitations

This study has several limitations. First, the retrospective design precluded randomization and may have introduced selection bias—patients undergoing FLACS may differ in socioeconomic background or access to care. Second, preoperative corneal parameters, including central corneal thickness (CCT), were not available, limiting the accuracy of IOP interpretation, especially when comparing across time points. Third, detailed intraoperative ultrasound energy data were not recorded, preventing direct analysis of phacoemulsification burden, which may underlie some corneal outcomes. Fourth, zonular status was not objectively imaged (e.g., by UBM), and thus any subtle preexisting instability could not be fully assessed. Lastly, follow-up duration varied, and for some patients, may have been insufficient to capture late-onset complications such as retinal detachment, PCO recurrence, or secondary glaucoma.

Future prospective studies with larger cohorts, longer follow-up, and standardized imaging and biomarker assessments are warranted to validate and extend our findings.

Conclusions

In highly myopic eyes undergoing cataract surgery after PPV, FLACS provided faster early visual recovery compared with CPS but was associated with a transient IOP elevation. However, both techniques demonstrated comparable safety and long-term efficacy, with no significant differences in visual acuity or complication rates at 3 months. Clinical decision-making should weigh early benefits against cost and patient-specific risk factors.
