A method of solution for the inverse problem for h h -functions of planar Brownian motion
Greg Markowsky, Clayton McDonald

Abstract
. Let D D be a planar domain and z z be a point in D D . The harmonic measure distribution function h D z ​ ( r ) h^{z}_{D}(r) , with base point z z , is the harmonic measure with pole at z z of the parts of the boundary which are within a distance r r of z z . Equivalently, it is the probability Brownian motion started from z z first strikes the boundary within a distance r r from z z . This paper is concerned with the following inverse problem: given a suitable function h h , does there exist a domain D D such that h = h D h=h_{D} ? To answer this, we first extend the concept of the h h -function of a domain to one of a stopping time τ \tau . Then, using the conformal invariance of Brownian motion, we solve the inverse problem for the h h -functions of stopping times. In many cases, the stopping time is the exit time of a domain D D , our technique therefore solves the original inverse problem. We will illustrate our methods with a large family of examples.



1.
Introduction

The harmonic measure distribution function, or “
h
-function”, of a domain
D\subset\mathbb{C}

with base-point
z\in D
is the probability that planar Brownian motion
Z_{t}
started at
z

first exits
D
within a distance
r
from
z
. That is, if we let
\tau_{D}
denote the first exit time of Brownian motion, then

h_{D}^{z}(r):=P_{z}(|Z_{\tau_{D}}-z|\leq r)=\omega_{z}(\overline{B_{r}(z)}\cap\partial D).

As far as we are aware, this function was first systematically studied by
Walden and Ward
[
38
]
, who credited Ken Stephensen

[
10
]
for the original idea; we note that in those and most subsequent papers on the topic, the
h
-function was defined analytically rather than probabilistically, with an equivalent definition in terms of harmonic measure. Since that time, a large
number of papers have appeared on the topic. Brownian motion is conformally
invariant (equivalently, harmonic measure is as well), so it is evident that a
major tool for attacking this problem is conformal mappings. Many papers have
utilized this approach, such as

[
39
,
34
,
21
,
3
,
2
,
10
,
23
,
24
,
35
,
22
,
16
,
5
,
6
]
.

To give a concrete example, in the upper-half plane
\mathbb{H}:=\{z\in\mathbb{C}:\text{Im}{z}>0\}
, the probability density

P_{i}(Z_{\tau_{D}}\in ds)
is well known to be the Poisson kernel on
\mathbb{H}
(See
[
28
,
12
]
for
derivations of this formula). Then for
r\geq 1
we have

h_{\mathbb{H}}^{i}(r)=\omega_{i}(B_{r}(i)\cap\mathbb{R})=\frac{2}{\pi}\int_{0}^{\sqrt{r^{2}-1}}\frac{1}{t^{2}+1}dt=\frac{2}{\pi}\arctan\sqrt{r^{2}-1},

and
h_{\mathbb{H}}^{i}(r)=0
for
r<1
. A diagram of this
h
-function and
its derivation is given below.

Figure 1.

-function of
\mathbb{H}
with base point
i

It is natural to ask which properties of a domain
D
are reflected in properties of
h_{D}^{z}
, and several things are known. For example, it is shown in
[
36
]
by Buerling’s projection theorem that
the
h
-function of a simply-connected domain
D
must satisfy

h_{D}^{z}(r)\leq 1-\frac{4}{\pi}\arctan\sqrt{\frac{d}{r}},

where
d
is the minimum distance to the boundary from
z
. Other geometric
aspects of the
h
-function can also be found in
[
36
]
. Another natural question is uniqueness: it is clear that the domain associated to a given
h
-function is, in general, not unique, because Brownian motion is rotationally
invariant, which means that domains which are rotations of each other around the basepoint will have the same
h
-functions. There are other examples as well which are not rotationally equivalent, and we will return to this point later in the paper.

This paper focuses on the inverse problem. That is, given a suitable function
h
, does
there exist a domain
D
such that
h=h_{D}^{z}
for some base point
z
? This problem has been studied in papers such as
[
34
,
3
]
. There are some trivial
necessary conditions that
h
must satisfy. To begin with,
h
must be right continuous
and non-decreasing, with
h(r)\to 1
as
r\to\infty
. Furthermore, for some
d>0
it must hold that
h\equiv 0
on
(0,d)
,
where
d
is the minimum distance from
z
to the boundary. A proposed technique to tackle
this inverse problem, found in
[
36
]
, is to approximate the
h
-function by step
functions, and consider domains of concentric circular arcs.

The method is to use these multiply-connected domains to approximate well-behaved
simply-connected domains. In that case, under certain conditions, the limiting domain will have the
prescribed
h
-function.

Our proposed solution is substantially different to the step-function program.
The methods are primarily complex-analytic and are inspired by a technique
developed by Gross to address the conformal Skorokhod embedding problem

[
17
]
. This problem asks for a domain
D
such that
Re(B_{\tau_{D}})
has a prescribed distribution. Gross’ method was further explored in subsequent works by other authors

[

,

,

; most notably, the authors of

[

]
suggested that Gross’ technique could be modified

-function inverse problem, which is essentially
the program we have followed in this paper. To see why this is a natural idea, note that
|e^{z}|=e^{Re(z)}
; therefore, as a rough idea, one may view the inverse problem for
h
-functions as the conformal Skorokhod embedding problem, exponentiated.

To make good on this idea, we first extend the notion of the
h
-function of a domain, to an
h
-function of a stopping time. This continues a general theme running through the papers
[
27
,
28
,
29
,
30

, with the idea being to take a classical quantity normally associated with a domain (e.g. Green’s function, harmonic measure, Hardy number) and associate it instead to a stopping time of Brownian motion. This approach is discussed in detail in
[
30
]
, where the class
\mathcal{H}
of stopping times was defined. We now recall this definition.

Given a nonconstant analytic function
f
on a domain
D
, let

(1)

\displaystyle\sigma_{t}:=\int_{0}^{\tau_{D}\wedge t}|f^{\prime}(Z_{s})|^{2}ds.

It is not hard to see that
\sigma_{t}
is strictly increasing and continuous a.s., and the same is therefore true of
C_{t}:=\sigma_{t}^{-1}
. Levy’s Theorem (see
[
4

13
,
14
,
32

) now states that the process
\hat{Z}_{t}=f(Z_{C_{t}})
is a planar Brownian motion on the random interval
[0,\sigma_{\tau_{D}}]
, and
\sigma_{\tau_{D}}
is a new stopping time, although it is not necessarily the exit time of a domain.

This is precisely the type of stopping time we are interested in. We let

\mathcal{H}=\{\tau:\tau=\sigma_{\tau_{\mathbb{H}}}\text{, with $\sigma$ defined by (\ref{skyfall}) for some $f$ analytic on $\mathbb{H}$}\}.

Note that here
\mathbb{H}
denotes the upper half-plane
\{Im(z)>0\}
; the definition given in
[
30
]
made use of the unit disk
\mathbb{D}
rather than
\mathbb{H}
, but since
\mathbb{D}
and
\mathbb{H}
are conformally equivalent it is clear that the two definitions are equivalent. A good example of a stopping time in
\mathcal{H}
which is not the exit time of a domain is
\inf\{t\geq 0:|arg(Z_{t})|=r\}
for
r>\pi
; this is the projection of
\tau_{\mathbb{H}}
under the analytic function
z\to(-iz)^{r/\pi}
.

In many cases, for
\tau\in\mathcal{H}
the quantity

P_{z}(|Z_{\tau}-z|\leq r)
makes sense even when
\tau
isn’t the first exit time of a domain. We
will define this quantity as the
h
-function of
\tau
with base-point
z
. When
\tau
is
the first exit time from a domain
D
, then clearly
h_{D}^{z}=h_{\tau}^{z}
. Our solution to the
inverse problem for the
h
-function will produce a stopping time in
\mathcal{H}
rather than a domain. To be precise, our main result is the following.

Theorem 1
.

Let
h
be right continuous, non-negative and increasing on
[0,\infty)
with
h(r)\to 1

as
r\to\infty
. Denote the generalised inverse of
h
by
g
. If
\ln(g)\in L^{p}([0,1])

for some
p>1
, then there exists a stopping time
\tau\in\mathcal{H}
such that,

h(r)=h_{\tau}^{0}(r)=P_{0}(|Z_{\tau}|\leq r).

The paper is organised as follows: In Section
2
we will define the relevant objects and
their relations. We will state and prove three lemmas required for the proof of Theorem

1
. We will also review the subtle aspects of conformal invariance of Brownian motion.
In Section
3
we will motivate and prove Theorem
1
. In Section

4
we will
present many examples where the solution to the inverse stopping time problem also solves the
original inverse problem. We will also cover the numerical approximation of the
function constructed in the proof of Theorem
1
.

2.
Definitions and Preliminaries

In this section we collect some basic definitions and results we will need.

2.1.
Harmonic Measure

The harmonic measure of a plane domain
D
is a family of probability measures

\{\omega_{a}\}_{a\in\overline{D}}
defined on the Borel sets of
\partial D
. It is given its name because for any
fixed boundary set
E
the map
a\mapsto\omega_{a}(E)
is harmonic. There are three standard
definitions of harmonic measure, and we will pass between these definitions freely and without comment,
so for clarity we will recall them here. Harmonicity and conformal invariance can be considered either
a trivial consequence of the definition, a simple corollary to a computation, or a theorem, depending on the chosen
definition. What follows is essentially an extension of the introductory paragraph in
[
25
]
.

Definition 1

(Complex Analytic Definition)
.

Let
D\subset\mathbb{C}
be simply connected, distinguish a point
a\in D
, and let
\varphi_{a}:\mathbb{D}\to D
be a
conformal (holomorphic and bijective) map sending
0
to
a
. Then harmonic measure
“with pole at a” is defined as the push-forward of normalised arc-length measure on
\mathbb{D}
by

\varphi_{a}
,

\omega_{a}(E):=\frac{1}{2\pi}(\varphi_{a})_{*}m(E)=\frac{1}{2\pi}m(\varphi_{a}^{-1}(E)),

for
E\in\mathcal{B}(\partial D)
, the Borel sets of the boundary.

The harmonic
measure of the unit disk can be found using the conformal map
z\mapsto(z-a)/(1-\overline{a}z)
, and in fact the density of
\omega_{a}
is exactly the Poisson kernel on the disk. Consequently, for all
simply connected domains
D
and fixed
E\in\mathcal{B}(\partial D)
the map
a\mapsto\omega_{a}(E)

is harmonic with boundary value
1_{E}
.

This property follows trivially from the potential theory
definition of Harmonic measure, as well, which is simply the Riesz-Markov measure associated to the Poisson integral.

Definition 2

(Potential Theoretic Definition)
.

Let
D
be a subset of
\mathbb{C}
which has an non-empty, non-polar boundary (see
[
33
]
). For a continuous and bounded
f:\partial D\to\mathbb{R}
, the
solution to the Dirichlet problem on
D
with boundary data
f
exists and is denoted
P_{D}f
. For each
z\in D
the Riesz Markov measure associated to the operator

f\mapsto P_{D}f(z)
is the harmonic measure with pole at
z
, that is,

P_{D}f(z)=\int_{\partial D}f(w)d\omega_{z}(w).

The measure exists as the operator
f\mapsto P_{D}f(z)
is positive in the sense that
f\geq 0
, implies
P_{D}f\geq 0
. Furthermore,
\omega_{z}
is a probability measure as
f\equiv 1
implies
P_{D}f\equiv 1
.
This definition places no requirements on the
connectivity of a domain, and agrees with the previous as
composition with a holomorphic function preserves harmonicity. Because the boundary of
every simply-connected strict subset of
\mathbb{C}
is non-polar, this definition is an extension of the
previous.
This naturally leads to the probabilistic
definition of harmonic measure. As shown initially by Kakutani
[
19
]
, the unique bounded solution to the
Dirichlet
problem can be expressed as
E_{z}f(Z_{\tau_{D}})
, where as usual
\tau_{D}
represents the first
exit from
D
, and
Z
is Brownian motion started at
z\in D
. For the choice of
f=1_{E}
, combining
Kakutani’s result with the potential theory definition of harmonic measure we find the following.

Definition 3

(Probabilistic Definition)
.

Let
D
be a subset of
\mathbb{C}
with
P^{a}(\tau_{D}<\infty)=1
for all
a\in D
where
\tau_{D}
is the first exit from
D
from Brownian motion
Z
started at
a\in D
.
Then harmonic measure with pole at
z
is given by

\omega_{z}(E)=P_{z}(Z_{\tau_{D}}\in E),

for
E\in\mathcal{B}(\partial D)
.

This definition is equivalent to the previous two by Levy’s conformal invariance of Brownian motion,
and the strong Markov property. The complex analytic definition of harmonic measure extends to the
cases above if we instead push-forward by the universal covering map.

2.2.
Periodic Integral Transforms

The holomorphic function mentioned in the introduction will be constructed by an integral transform
on
\mathbb{R}
. For reasons that will be apparent in the next section, we must consider
transforms applied to periodic functions. Consequently, we will spend this section studying
the form of these operators on
\mathbb{R}/\Gamma
, where
\Gamma=2\mathbb{Z}+1=\{2n+1:n\in\mathbb{Z}\}
. This will be vital when numerically
approximating the function, while also making some arguments simpler.

Define
T=\mathbb{R}/\Gamma
and equip it with the quotient topology. Let
L^{p}(T)
denote the measurable functions on
\mathbb{R}
which are periodic
with respect to
\Gamma
and also satisfy
\int_{-1}^{1}|f|^{p}<\infty
. These functions are in one-to-one correspondence with
L^{p}
integrable
functions on
T
. If
\mathcal{T}
is an integral operator defined on
L^{p}(\mathbb{R})
which preserves the periodicity with respect to
\Gamma
, then
the domain of definition can be extended to
L^{p}(T)
in the following manner: Let
K
be the associated kernel, and let
f\in L^{P}(T)
,

\mathcal{T}f=\int_{\mathbb{R}}K(t,x)f(t)dt=\sum_{k=-\infty}^{\infty}\int_{-1}^{1}K(t+2k,x)f(t)dt=\int_{-1}^{1}\tilde{K}(t,x)f(t)dt.

Here
\tilde{K}(x,t)=\sum_{k=-\infty}^{\infty}K(x+2k,t)
is the periodic form of
K
, assuming this exists, and the final expression on the right is taken to be the definition of
\mathcal{T}
on
L^{p}(T)
.

For example, the form obtained for the Poisson kernel (which we will require later) is contained in the following lemma.

Lemma 1
.

The periodic Poisson integral takes the form

\displaystyle(P_{y}\ast f)(x)=\frac{1}{2}\int_{T}\frac{\sinh\pi y}{\cosh\pi y-\cos\pi(x-t)}f(t)dt.

for
(x,y)\in T\times\mathbb{R}^{+}
. In the limit we have,

\displaystyle\lim_{y\to\infty}(P_{y}\ast f)=\frac{1}{2}\int_{T}f(t)dt.

This exists for
f\in L^{p}(T)
, and furthermore the Poisson integral is bounded on
L^{p}(T)
and
P_{y}\ast f
is harmonic on

T\times\mathbb{R}^{+}
with
\lim_{y\to 0}(P_{y}\ast f)=f
almost everywhere.

Proof.

Let
f\in L^{p}(T)
.

\displaystyle(P_{y}\ast f)(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{y}{(x-t)^{2}+y^{2}}f(t)dt

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{-1}^{1}\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}\frac{y}{(x-t-2k)^{2}+y^{2}}f(t)dt.

A well-known application of the residue theorem yields the following formula for evaluating sums of this type,

\sum_{k=-\infty}^{\infty}f(k)=\sum_{z_{j}}\mathrm{Res}(\pi\cot(\pi z)f(z);z_{j}),

where the summation on the right ranges over the poles of
g(z)=\pi\cot(\pi z)f(z)
(see
[
18
]
).
In the case of the Poisson kernel, there are two simple poles of
g
,
namely
z_{\pm}=\frac{1}{2}(\pm yi+t-x)
, extracting the residue at each, we find,

\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}\frac{y}{(x-t-2k)^{2}+y^{2}}=i\pi\left(\cot\frac{\pi}{2}(x-t+iy)-\cot\frac{\pi}{2}(x-t-iy)\right).

The above simplifies to the desired form using the complex definition of
\sin

and
\cos
, and this shows that
P_{y}\ast f
exists.
The limit as
y\to\infty
follows from the dominated convergence theorem,
as is boundedness through Hölder’s inequality with the periodic kernel.
Harmonicity and the limit as
y\to 0
is carried over by the standard Poisson integral.
∎

The principle motivation for this lemma is that the Poisson integral
in this form is much easier to compute numerically than the standard form
on
L_{\Gamma}^{p}(\mathbb{R})
.
An obvious difference in the Poisson integral on
L^{p}(\mathbb{R})
and
L^{p}(T)
is the behaviour at
\infty
,
and becomes more clear when the transform is put into the form given in the lemma.

We also require the periodic Hilbert transform.

Lemma 2
.

The periodic Hilbert transform takes the form

\displaystyle(Hf)(x)=\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{p.v.}\int_{T}f(t)\cot\frac{\pi}{2}(x-t)dt.

This exists for
f\in L^{p}(T)
, and furthermore
H
is bounded on
L^{p}(T)
, for
1<p<\infty
, and commutes with the Poisson integral.

Proof.

First we prove existence. Let
f\in L^{p}(T)
. Then

\displaystyle(Hf)(x)=\frac{1}{\pi}\mathrm{p.v.}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{f(t)}{x-t}dt=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\pi}\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\int_{|t|>\varepsilon}\frac{f(x-t)}{t}dt

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\pi}\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\int_{\varepsilon\leq|t|\leq 1}\frac{f(x-t)}{t}dt+\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{-1}^{1}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k\in\mathbb{Z}\\
k\neq 0\end{subarray}}\frac{f(x-t)}{t-2k}dt

This follows by the periodicity of
f
. The Hilbert transform of an

L^{p}(\mathbb{R})
function exists almost everywhere. Let
f_{1}:=\mathbf{1}_{[-2,2]}f
, so
f_{1}\in L^{p}(\mathbb{R})
and on
(-2,2)
we have

f_{1}=f
. That is, the first integral in the last equation is
nothing but the Hilbert transform of an
L^{p}(\mathbb{R})
function, hence it exists
for almost every
x
.
Again a routine application of the residue theorem yields the following equality.

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k\in\mathbb{Z}\\
k\neq 0\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{t-2k}=\frac{\pi}{2}\cot\frac{\pi}{2}t-\frac{1}{t}

This is bounded on
(-1,1)
, and so the second integral term is finite for
almost all
x
. This proves existence, the form trivially follows by
replacing
\int_{-1}^{1}f(x-t)\left(\frac{\pi}{2}\cot\frac{\pi}{2}t-\frac{1}{t}\right)dt
with

\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\int_{\varepsilon\leq|t|\leq 1}f(x-t)\left(\frac{\pi}{2}\cot\frac{\pi}{2}t-\frac{1}{t}\right)dt
and simplifying.

Now, to prove boundedness on
L^{p}(T)
for
1<p<\infty
. Let
H_{\varepsilon}f:=\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{|t|>\varepsilon}\frac{f(x-t)}{t}dt
, then

\displaystyle\|H_{\varepsilon}f\|_{p}

\displaystyle\leq\bigg\|\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{\varepsilon\leq|t|\leq 1}\frac{f(x-t)}{t}dt\bigg\|_{p}+\bigg\|\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{-1}^{1}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k\in\mathbb{Z}\\
k\neq 0\end{subarray}}\frac{f(x-t)}{t-2k}dt\bigg\|_{p}

\displaystyle\leq A\|f_{1}\|_{p}+B\|f\|_{p}\leq C\|f\|_{p}

The first bound follows as the Hilbert transform is bounded on
\mathbb{R}
and
f_{1}\in L^{p}(\mathbb{R})
agrees
with
f
on
(-1,1)
. The second term follows by repeated application of the triangle
inequality. Fatou’s lemma with
\varepsilon\to 0
gives boundedness on
L^{p}

for
1<p<\infty
.

Finally, as the Hilbert transform is translationally invariant it must commute with
convolution operators, so for
p\in(1,\infty)

P_{y}\ast Hf=H(P_{y}\ast f)
.
∎

In the case where
p=\infty
, since
T
is compact it still makes sense to
apply the Hilbert transform to the Poisson integral. In the
\mathbb{R}
case the
Hilbert transform is bounded between
L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})
and
\mathrm{BMO}
, and
the Poisson integral of a
\mathrm{BMO}
function remains
\mathrm{BMO}
,
so here the iteration also is well-defined
[
37
]
. For proof of the boundedness of the Hilbert transform on
\mathbb{R}
see

[
20
]
.

2.3.
Conformal Invariance of Brownian Motion

If
f
is a holomorphic function on
U\subset\mathbb{C}
, then an application of Itô’s lemma
and Levy’s characterisation of Brownian motion shows that
f(Z_{t})
is a time-changed planar Brownian defined on the
random interval
[0,\tau_{U})
, where the time change given by equation (
1
).
This result is typically referred to as “Levy’s Conformal Invariance of Brownian motion”.
We stress, however, that injectivity is not required for this result, nor do we require the derivative of
f
to never vanish,
meaning Brownian motion is invariant under functions which are not conformal in the two most common definitions of the word.
We will simply call this
“holomorphic invariance of Brownian motion”. The associated stopping time
\mathcal{H}
we will denote

f(\tau_{U})
, and will refer to
f(\tau_{U})
as the
projection of
\tau_{U}
under
f
.

For an example, take
D:=\{z:0<\text{Re}z<\ln r\}
and
\tilde{D}:=\{z:1<|z|<r\}
with

f(z)=e^{z}
, which takes
D
onto
\tilde{D}
. Here,
f
is a (holomorphic) covering map, and it is not hard to see that

f(\tau_{D})=\tau_{\tilde{D}}
(this also follows from Proposition
1
below).
For a more delicate example, let
S:=\mathbb{C}\setminus(-\infty,-1]
and
f(z)=z^{2}
. If we start Brownian motion
on
[1,\infty)
, then
\tau_{S}
projects under
f
to the first time the Brownian motion hits the positive real line after it winds
about
0
once . Therefore, in this example
f(\tau_{S})\neq\tau_{f(S)}
.

We do not want to restrict to the maps which continuously extend to the boundary, as this is far too restrictive.
Therefore, by
f(\partial U)
we mean the set
\{w=\lim_{z_{n}\to z}f(z_{n}):\{z_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\subset U\text{ with }z_{n}\to z\in\partial U\}
.
For it to hold that
f(\tau_{U})=\tau_{f(U)}
we require
f(U)\cap f(\partial U)=\emptyset
; this holds for instance if
f
is a covering
map.

Proposition 1
.

If
f:U\to f(U)
is a covering map, the projection
f(\tau_{U})
is the first exit time of Brownian motion in

f(U)
.

Proof.

Suppose
w\in f(U)\cap f(\partial U)
where
f
is a covering map.
Since
w\in f(U)
we can choose compact subset
K
with

w\in K\subset f(U)
. As
w\in f(\partial U)
, there exists some
z\in\partial U
and a sequence

\{z_{n}\}\subset U
with
z_{n}\to z
and
f(z_{n})\to f(z)
. By taking sub-sequences if necessary, we assume
for some large
n_{0}
,
we have
f(z_{n})\in K
for all
n\geq n_{0}
. As
f
is a covering map, it holds that

f^{-1}(K)=\bigcup_{j\in\mathbb{N}}K_{j}
, where each
K_{j}

is compact. It follows from the definition of a covering map that
z_{n}
is
contained in some
K_{j}
for
n\geq n_{0}
, which contradicts
z_{n}\to z
.
Thus
f(U)\cap f(\partial U)=\emptyset
, and the statement follows.
∎

The example above with
f(z)=z^{2}
on
S
is not a covering map, and
moreover
f((-\infty,1])=f([1,\infty))=[1,\infty)
. This explains the winding behaviour of the projection.

Define the cylinder
\mathcal{C}:=\mathbb{H}/(2\mathbb{Z}+1)
. There exists a natural projection
\pi:\mathbb{H}\to\mathcal{C}
and an obvious
complex structure, constructed identically to that of the torus
[
15
]
. Functions which are periodic with respect to
(2\mathbb{Z}+1)

are in one to one correspondence with functions on
\mathcal{C}
. The Poisson integral of a function of
f\in L^{p}(T)
can therefore be
considered as a harmonic function on
\mathcal{C}
. Define Brownian motion
on
\mathcal{C}

Proposition 2
.

Let
k
be a holomorphic function on
\{z:\text{Re}(z)<0\}
, such that
|k(iy)|=1
,

and
\lim_{x\to-\infty}k(x+iy)=0
. Then
k(z)=c\exp(\alpha z)
for some integer
\alpha
and constant

|c|=1
.

Proof.

Since
k
maps the imaginary axis to
the circle, by Schwarz reflection
k
extends to an entire function. Define

f(z)=k(\log(z))
, where we initially choose the branch of
\log
which is holomorphic on

\mathbb{C}\setminus[0,\infty)
, with
Im(\log(z))\in(0,2\pi)
. By the periodicity of
k
, the function
f
extends to be
continuous on
(0,\infty)
. A standard exercise in complex analysis shows
that the path integral of
f
must be zero over any triangle in the
punctured plane
\mathbb{C}\setminus\{0\}
, and therefore by Morera’s theorem

is holomorphic on
\mathbb{C}\setminus\{0\}
. At the origin
\log(z)
tends to

-\infty
, and since
k(x+yi)
is bounded as

,

is
bounded about the origin, and hence extends to an entire function by the Casorati–Weierstrass Theorem.

\log(z)
maps the circle to the imaginary axis, and since
k
maps
the imaginary axis to the circle,

maps the unit circle to itself. In
other words,
|f(z)|=1
whenever
|z|=1
. The function

fixes points on the unit circle, and thus the function

1/\overline{f(1/\overline{z})}
is a meromorphic function which agrees
with
f
on the unit circle. By the identity principle, we have
f(z)=1/\overline{f(1/\overline{z})}
, and this implies that

must be non-zero
at all points except possibly the origin. Suppose

has a zero of order

\alpha
at

is non-zero in
\mathbb{D}
. By the maximum
and minimum
modulus principle, the maximum and minimum must occur on the boundary. But

is constant here, so they must agree and be equal to one, and thus

is a constant

.
∎

4.2.
Numerical Construction of the Map

Numerically our only challenges are the accurate computation of Poisson integral and the Hilbert transform.
We employed the “tanh-sinh” quadrature method, discovered by H. Takahasi and M. Mori in 1974. The error can be expressed as
O(\exp(-CN/\ln N))
, where
N
is the number of
function evaluations
[
31
]
. First define
\varphi(t)=\tanh(\pi/2\sinh(t))
. Then by substitution and the trapezoidal rule with spacing
h
we obtain,

\displaystyle\int_{-1}^{1}f(t)dt=

\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}f(\varphi(t))\varphi^{\prime}(t)dt

\displaystyle\approx\frac{h\pi}{2}\sum_{k=-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{\cosh(kh)}{\cosh^{2}(\frac{\pi}{2}\sinh kh)}f\left(\tanh(\frac{1}{2}\pi\sinh kh)\right).

As a choice, if we calculate this summation to
M
terms, we choose
h=2/M
.
The integrand decays at a double exponential rate, so this method is suited for integrating functions that have singularities at the end points. We often are in this case as if a domain is unbounded,
then the inverse of it’s
h
-function has a singularity at
1
.
With this in mind, it was crucial that we found periodic expressions for the Poisson integral and Hilbert transform. We will first explain optimisations we used for the Poisson integral.

When
y\to 0

the Poisson kernel tends to a delta spike at
x
. We are then forced to make
h
very small. However most of this computation is wasted, as the mass of the integral
is at
x
. We instead compute

(P_{y}\ast f)(x)=\int_{-1}^{x}\frac{\sinh\pi y}{\cosh\pi y-\cos\pi(x-t)}f(t)dt+\int_{x}^{1}\frac{\sinh\pi y}{\cosh\pi y-\cos\pi(x-t)}f(t)dt,

and then make a linear substitution to each integral so the bounds are
-1\leq t\leq 1
. The chosen quadrature method sends the end points to
\infty
.
Given the rapid decay of the integrand decays, the delta spike behaviour can then
be accurately computed. If we restrict the sets in
\mathbb{H}
we wish to
compute to
\{x+it:t\in\mathbb{R}^{+}\}
, we pre-compute every term in the
quadrature formula which only involve
x
. This is necessary to numerically study the interior behaviour of the constructed holomorphic function.

For the Hilbert transform, one could use the fast Fourier transform algorithm,
as
\mathcal{F}(H(f))(\zeta)=-i\text{sgn}(\zeta)\mathcal{F}f(\zeta)
. However, given the bounded domain we opted for the quadrature method instead. In computing the integral

(Hf)(x)=\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\int_{\varepsilon}^{1}(f(x-t)-f(x+t))\cot\frac{\pi}{2}tdt,

again we make a linear substitution so the bounds are
-1\leq t\leq 1
, and
we choose some sufficiently small
\varepsilon
. We pre-compute any terms which do not
involve
t
, and then reuse these computations
wherever necessary.

When the Hilbert transform of a function is not known, the largest obstacle in
computing the interior behaviour of the holomorphic function of Theorem

1
is the
P_{y}\ast(H\ln g)
term. If we even want to compute one
value of
P_{y}\ast Hf
, we will need to compute the Hilbert transform for
every-point required in the quadrature expression for the Poisson integral. To
cut down on computation, we pre-compute the Hilbert transform on each of the points
which appear in the computation for the Poisson integral.
