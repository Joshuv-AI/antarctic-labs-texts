Application of an improved analytical protocol for the evaluation of biogenic amines in wines produced in the Canary Islands☆
Authors: Carlos Fernández-Casanova, Jesús Heras-Roger, Carlos Díaz-Romero, Antonio V Herrera-Herrera, Bárbara Socas-Rodríguez
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIn this work, an improved method for the determination of biogenic amines in wine was developed and validated. The method combines an optimized extraction with high performance liquid chromatography fluorescence detection, providing enhanced sensitivity, selectivity and robustness. The proposed method was applied to the analysis of 56 wines produced in the Canary Islands. Ethanolamine and ethylamine were the most frequently detected amines, whereas histamine was not detected in any of the samples. The profile was dominated by fermentative amines associated with yeast metabolism, while bacterial amines linked to amino acid decarboxylation were present at low levels. Significant differences in concentrations (p < 0.05) were observed among wine-producing areas, whereas wine type and residual sugar content showed no significant influence. The proposed method represents a reliable tool for monitoring biogenic amines and contributes to the wine quality and safety assessment.Keywords: Food quality, Safety assessment, Liquid chromatography, Florescence detector, Human health

Introduction

The economy of the Canary Islands, until the expansion of tourism, depended largely on the primary sector (Macías-Hernández, 2011). This was due to both geographical factors, as it is the only region of Spain with a subtropical climate, and historical factors, given their strategic position as a stopover point on voyages to the Americas. In this context, wine production and export started during the 15th century (Organización Interprofesional del Vino de España (OIVE), n.d.), with Canarian wines being highly demanded in European countries such as England and the Netherlands. Among the eleven Protected Designation of Origin (DOPs) of Canarian wines, Tenerife has six of these designations. In 2024, the area dedicated to vineyard cultivation in the Canary Islands was 8158.2 ha, representing 20.6% of the total agricultural surface area according to the Annual Statistics on Crop Areas and Production jointly published by the Canary Islands Institute of Statistics (ISTAC) and the Regional Ministry of Agriculture, Fisheries, Food, and Food Sovereignty (Instituto Canario de Estadística (ISTAC), n.d.). The most recent sectoral data indicate that the harvest collected for wineries under DOP in 2025 was approximately 4.84 million kilograms, representing a 27% decrease compared to the previous harvest. Despite this decline in yield, the wine sector maintained a significant position within food crop production, with a cultivated area surpassed only by herbaceous crops, fruits and berries, and bananas. Therefore, it is easy to deduce the importance of the wine industry in the Canary Islands.

Different agri-food products (including wine) have high added value due to their benefits for human health. These benefits are sometimes attributable to the presence of bioactive substances, which are compounds that, despite not having a nutritional function or not being essential for human health, can significantly contribute to disease prevention (Rodríguez et al., 2024). These substances are metabolites of diverse origin including flavonoids, carotenoids, and biogenic amines. Biogenic amines are generally formed through the degradation of amino acids by microorganisms. The high availability of amino acids promotes the formation of biogenic amines in a wide variety of protein-rich foods or with considerable amounts of free amino acids, such as fish, meat, vegetables, fruit, and foods that have been underwent to fermentation processes. Expired products or those manufactured under poor hygienic conditions may contain high concentrations of certain biogenic amines (Proestos et al., 2008). The formation of these amines occurs during protein catabolism in the processes of alcoholic fermentation and malolactic fermentation, involving enzymatic decarboxylation. However, they can also be formed from aldehydes and ketones through amination or transamination during wine fermentation or aging of wines (Liu et al., 2020). These compounds are involved in various biochemical processes, and their consumption in controlled amounts may promote better nutrient absorption, enhance brain activity, regulate gastric pH, or help maintain body temperature (Liu et al., 2020). Nevertheless, excessive intake may lead to acute intoxication, with symptoms such as headaches, nausea, flushing, hypo- or hypertension, sweating, skin irritation, vomiting, respiratory problems, or digestive disorders (Mir-Cerdà et al., 2021). It is important to note that, unlike other products such as fish (where histamine is strictly regulated under Commission Regulation (EC) No 2073/2005), there are currently no specific harmonised legal limits for biogenic amines in the European Union. In contrast, some countries (e.g., Germany, France, Belgium, the Netherlands, Finland, and Switzerland) have established maximum histamine levels for grapes and wine, generally ranging between 2 and 10 mg/L (Costantini et al., 2019). Thus, monitoring their presence in this kind of beverages is essential.

The determination of biogenic amines in wine is particularly difficult due to their low concentration levels and the complexity of the matrix. For this reason, the most common method for their determination in wine and other alcoholic beverages is high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) with ultraviolet (UV) or fluorescence (FD) detection (Loukou & Zotou, 2003). Since only aromatic amines produce a UV or fluorescence signal, pre- or post-column derivatization is used for their detection. Among the most effective and commonly used derivatizing agents are o-phthalaldehyde (OPA), diethyl 2-ethoxymethylenemalonate (DEEMM), and dansyl chloride (Dns-Cl), although other reagents have also been employed (Hernández-Cassou & Saurina, 2011; Tsiasioti et al., 2023). Following derivatization, it is common to perform liquid–liquid microextraction (LLME) or solid-phase extraction (SPE) using octadecylsilane (C18) cartridges to decrease interferences and matrix effects. Several examples describing extraction and determination methodologies can be found in literature. Regarding recent high-throughput literature, it is worth noting that methods utilizing dispersive solid-phase extraction (d-SPE) have been reported. Those alternative approaches frequently rely on highly toxic solvents (e.g., chloroform or dichloromethane) (Salimova et al., 2025) or require energy- and time-consuming steps, such as high-temperature reactions, drying, cooling, or long-term stirring for sorbent preparation and analyte modification (do Nascimento Barbosa et al., 2026; Kong et al., 2025; Raičević et al., 2025; Salimova et al., 2025; Shi et al., 2024). However, in member countries of the International Organisation of Vine and Wine (OIV), including Spain, the official method OIV-OENO 457-2014 (OIV, 2014) is commonly used. This method uses a photodiode array detector and DEEMM as the derivatizing agent (which causes alkylation of the amino group), and 2,4,6-trimethylphenethylamine hydrochloride as the internal standard. The method allows for the determination of eight amines (histamine, His; methylamine, MetA; ethylamine, EtA; tyramine, TyA; putrescine, Put; cadaverine, Cdv; 2-phenylethylamine, PEA; and isoamylamine; IaA) within a concentration range of 0.020 to 20 mg/L. However, studies reported in the literature have obtained better results using Dns-Cl due to its ability to react both with primary and secondary amines (Hernández-Cassou & Saurina, 2011; Mir-Cerdà et al., 2021; Proestos et al., 2008; Tsiasioti et al., 2023).

The analysis of biogenic amines has been widely reported in the literature for wines from different regions worldwide, particularly in European and Spanish wines finding concentrations of the studied amines (His, Put, TyA, Cdv, EtA, TyA) in the range 0.51–28 mg/L. However, to the best of our knowledge the determination of biogenic amines has not yet been addressed in wines from the Canary Islands. However, numerous factors during vineyard cultivation may influence the biogenic amine content of wine, including grape variety, local climate conditions, resource management (irrigation and fertilization), winemaking practices, and the maturation process (Liu et al., 2020). Therefore, a study focused specifically on the Canary archipelago is essential.

In light of the above, in this work the occurrence of biogenic amines was investigated for the first time in wines produced in the Canary Islands, a unique viticultural region characterized by autochthonous grape varieties and distinctive edaphoclimatic conditions. An analytical methodology based on HPLC-FD using columns packed with superficially porous particles (or core-shell) was implemented to achieve improved efficiency and resolution in the analysis of 16 biogenic amines. In addition, the derivatization and extraction step was modified in order to reduce the use of toxic organic solvents. This approach addresses a significant geographical and analytical gap in the literature, providing the first comprehensive data on biogenic amines in Canarian wines while advancing towards a greener, more sustainable sample preparation strategy without compromising analytical performance.

Materials and methods

Chemicals and materials

All chemicals were used without further purification. Analytical standards of 7 hydrochloride biogenic amines (i.e. Cdv, ethanolamine (EtOHA), EtA, MetA, 2-phenylethylamine (PEA), TyA, spermidine (Spd)), 9 biogenic amines (i.e. dopamine (Dop), 3,4-Dihidroxy-L-phenylalanine (L-DOPA), epinephrine (EpnA), His, IaA, isopropylamine (IpA), Put, spermine (Spm), serotonine (Ser)) and heptylamine (HpA) as the surrogate standard with purities ≥97% (w/w) were obtained from Sigma Aldrich Chemie (Steimhem, Germany). Stock solutions of each target analyte and HpA were prepared at 1000 mg/L in 0.1 M HCl and stored at −18 °C in the darkness. Standard solutions were prepared daily at the desired concentrations by diluting working solutions with appropriate volumes of 0.1 M HCl.

Acetone and n-heptane of HPLC grade, hydrochloric acid (HCl, 25%, w/w) and boric acid (H3BO4, 99.8%, w/w) were from Merck (Darmstadt, Germany). Dns-Cl for HPLC, calcium carbonate (CaCO3, 99%, w/w) and sodium hydroxide (NaOH, ≥98%, w/w) were from Sigma-Aldrich (Steinheim, Germany). Dichloromethane stabilized with 20 ppm of amylene and acetonitrile (MeCN) of HPLC grade were from Panreac (Darmstadt, Germany). Ethyl acetate for HPLC was from Honeywell (Seelze, Germany). Milli-Q water with resistivity ≤18.2 MΩ·cm was taken from Milli-Q gradient system A10 from Millipore (Massachusetts, United States).

Apparatus and software

Instruments and apparatus used during extraction procedure included an AW-224 analytical balance from Sartorius (Niedersachsen, Germany), a RCT Basic magnetic stirrer with hot plate from IKA® (Staufen, Germany), a vortex-Genie 2 agitator from Scientific Industrie Inc. (Bohemian, USA), a Branson 3510 Ultrasonic Cleaner from Branson Ultrasonics Corporation (Connecticut, United States) and a N-EVAP® 111 nitrogen evaporator from Organomation Associates, Inc. (Berlin, USA).

The analysis of biogenic amines was performed by HPLC using a Waters HPLC system (Milford, MA, USA) equipped with a binary pump (model 1525), an autosampler (model 717 plus), a fluorescence detector (model 2475 Multi λ), working with the Empower 2 software from Waters. An XBridge® C18 column (100 mm × 4.6 mm, 3.5 μm) and a Cortecs® C18+ (100 mm × 4.6 mm, 2.7 μm) solid-core (core-shell) column, both from Waters, were tested. Separations were finally carried out at 37 °C in the Cortecs® C18+ column using a Guard-Pak C18 pre-column (3.9 mm × 5 mm; 2,7 μm).

Chromatographic separation was performed using MeCN (A) and Milli-Q water (B). Different gradients modifying the initial composition of the mobile phase were tested from 35 to 50% of MeCN. The final gradient started at 40/60 (v/v) A/B, increasing to 65/35 in 10 min, and then to 80/20 over the next 10 min. The organic phase was further increased to 100% A in 10 min. Finally, initial conditions were restored in 3 min and held for 2 min to re-equilibrate the system. The flow rate and the injection volume were 1 mL/min and 10 μL, respectively. Comparative analysis of derivatized and non-derivatized samples via fluorescence detection (λexc/em = 320/532 nm) and diode array detection (DAD) (λ = 220, 250, 280, 300 and 335 nm) were carried out. The FD was used at λexc = 320 nm and λem = 532 nm.

Wine samples

Fifty six wine bottled samples were collected for analysis from various DOPs in the Canary Islands, Spain. The set included samples from the six Designations of Origin present in Tenerife Island: Abona (n = 6), Tacoronte Acentejo (n = 6), Valle de Güímar (n = 15), Valle de la Orotava (n = 6), Ycoden Daute Isora (n = 6), and from the global Designation of Origin of the whole island, Tenerife (n = 17). Sampling was determined according to availability and trying to enhance diversity in terms of sugar content (dry, off-dry, sweet) and type of wine (red, white and rosé) as detailed in Table S1. Samples were stored in 50 mL conical-bottom polypropylene screw-cap centrifuge tubes and maintained at −18 °C in the dark until analysis. All wine samples were characterized following the International Methods for Wine Analysis (International Organisation of Vine and Wine (OIV), 2025) in pH (OIV-MA-AS313-15), total acidity (OIV-MA-AS313-01), fixed acidity (OIV-MA-AS313-03), acetic acid (OIV-MA-AS313-05), free sulfur dioxide (OIV-MA-AS313-03), total sulfur dioxide (OIV-MA-AS323-04 A), glucose+fructose (OIV-MA-AS311-02), and alcoholic strength (OIV-MA-AS312-01).

Sample preparation and derivatization

The analytical procedure was adapted from the method described by (Mir-Cerdà et al., 2021). Optimization was carried out in order to improve extraction efficiency and accomplish green chemistry principles. With this aim, dichloromethane, ethyl acetate and n-heptane were tested. Solvent selection was performed by conducting extractions (in duplicate) using 750 μL of each candidate solvent. All other derivatization and extraction parameters remained constant: 250 μL of buffer, 250 μL of Dns-Cl, 250 μL of a biogenic amines' standard mixture at 1 mg/L. Briefly, 250 μL of sample, standard, or simulated wine matrix (comprising 13% ethanol, 5 g/L tartaric acid, 0.5 g/L acetic acid, and 30 mg/L sodium thiosulfate) were mixed with 250 μL of borate buffer and 250 μL of dansyl chloride in 2 mL centrifuge microtubes. The mixture was vigorously agitated and incubated at 40 °C for 60 min. Subsequently, a LLME was performed by addition of 750 μL of ethyl acetate, followed by vortexing for 10 min. The organic phase was collected in 15 mL evaporation tubes, and the extraction process was repeated twice. The combined organic fractions were evaporated to dryness under a nitrogen stream and reconstituted in 1 mL of the initial mobile phase. Finally, 10 μL of the solution were injected into the HPLC system. Each sample was injected in duplicate. Procedural blanks were processed in parallel with each batch.

Method validation

Once the analytical conditions were optimized, the methodology was validated in terms of matrix effect, calibration, sensitivity, precision, and accuracy.

For matrix effect study, the simulated wine matrix was prepared in the laboratory. The analysis was carried out by derivatizing and analyzing two mixtures of amine standards at 1 mg/L in parallel and in triplicate by HPLC-FD. In the first mixture, 0.1 M HCl was used as solvent, while in the second, the simulated wine matrix was used; all other conditions were kept identical for both (250 μL Dns-Cl, 250 μL buffer, 40 °C, 60 min). Once the chromatograms were generated, the peak areas obtained for each analyte in the HCl solution were compared with those obtained for the simulated matrix. The method of Matuszewski et al. (2003) (Eq. (1)) was followed for the evaluation of the matrix effect, whereby values between 80% and 120% indicate the absence of a significant matrix effect.

For calibration, seven solutions (n = 7) with increasing concentrations of the amines (0.15–10 mg/L) were prepared, subjected to the complete procedure, and injected in duplicate. Calibration curves were obtained by plotting the ratios of the peak areas of the amines to that of the surrogate standard (A/ASS) versus the concentration. HpA was spiked at 5.0 mg/L in all calibration points. For those amines for which no linear trend was observed the Mandel test (Andrade & Gómez-Carracedo, 2013) was applied in order to determine whether the calibration for these amines better fitted by non-linear equations. Sensitivity was evaluated through the determination of the limits of detection (LODs) and quantification (LOQs), calculated as the concentrations providing signal-to-noise ratios (S/N) of 3 and 10, respectively. This data was checked by injected spiked samples at the calculated concentrations and calculating the S/N.

Next, precision and accuracy were evaluated at two different concentration levels within the selected calibration range (level 1: 3.13–4.67 mg/L; level 2: 6.07–7.33 mg/L). For the determination of precision, one solution at each selected level was derivatized, extracted and injected five consecutive times (n = 5) each day for three consecutive days (n = 15). The relative standard deviations (RSDs) of peak areas and retention times were compared. For the assessment of accuracy, four replicates of each level were extracted and injected in duplicate. Once the results were obtained, the calibration curves were used to calculate the concentrations, and recovery values were determined relative to the expected concentrations.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed to evaluate relationships between physicochemical parameters, biogenic amine concentrations, and wine classification variables One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was applied to evaluate differences in individual amine concentrations and in total biogenic amine content among groups of wines classified according to geographical origin (DOP), wine type (red, white and rosé), and residual sugar content. When significant differences were detected (p < 0.05), Tukey's test was used as a post-hoc procedure to identify statistically homogeneous groups.

The total concentration of biogenic amines (ΣBA) was calculated as the sum of all quantified amines in each sample. To facilitate interpretation of the metabolic origin of the compounds, two additional grouped variables were calculated: fermentative amines, estimated as the sum of MetA, EtA and EtOHA, and bacterial amines, calculated as the sum of Put, TyA, IaA, and Cdv.

Results and discussion

Optimization of derivatization and chromatographic separation

To establish an efficient derivatization protocol for biogenic amines, procedures previously described by (Loukou & Zotou, 2003; Proestos et al., 2008) were initially evaluated using ultrapure water to eliminate matrix interferences. However, this protocol yielded no detectable chromatographic signals, leading to its rejection. Consequently, an alternative method based on Mir-Cerdà et al. (2021) was implemented with strategic modifications: dichloromethane replaced chloroform as the LLME solvent, and water volume was set at 1 mL. Additionally, the reconstitution solvent was MeCN/H2O (60/40, v/v) to ensure compatibility with initial chromatographic conditions. This approach provided robust signals for all analytes. Comparative analysis of derivatized and non-derivatized samples confirmed that biogenic amines lack sufficient intrinsic chromophores or fluorophores for direct detection (Mo Dugo et al., 2006; Soufleros et al., 2007). Fluorescence significantly outperformed DAD, as the latter failed to produce resolvable peaks at any tested wavelength (data not shown).

Chromatographic separation optimization prioritized simplicity to enhance routine laboratory throughput. A binary gradient of MeCN (A) and water (B) was selected within the previous literature, as the absence of additives did not compromise resolution or peak shape (Liu et al., 2020; Tuberoso et al., 2015). Comparison between an XBridge® C18 column and a Cortecs® C18+ core-shell column was carried out. Core-shell technology, featuring a solid nucleus with a porous outer layer, provides higher efficiency and resolution with significantly lower backpressure compared to fully porous sub-2 μm particles. In this study, although the XBridge® C18 column, yielded acceptable separation, 2.7 μm core-shell particles achieved better results with lower analytical time and improved efficiency. Thus, core-shell was selected as the most suitable column for the selected group of compounds.

The final optimized gradient (detailed in Section 2.2) successfully resolved the first eluting analyte from the Dns-Cl reagent peak, a critical improvement over literature-based gradients where co-elution was observed (Mir-Cerdà et al., 2021; Tuberoso et al., 2015). Finally, column temperature was set at 37 °C to ensure instrumental repeatability and bypass ambient fluctuations. Individual injections confirmed retention times, and a 1 mg/L mixed standard yielded a fully resolved chromatogram (Fig. 1).

Optimization of extraction method

Once the separation and detection conditions were optimized, improvements to the LLME procedure were evaluated. The primary modification involved the selection of the extraction solvent. The reference protocol described by Mir-Cerdà et al. (2021) utilized 750 μL of chloroform. However, due to the environmental impact of chloroform (specifically its low biodegradability and high environmental mobility (McCulloch, 2003)), dichloromethane was initially considered as a substitute. However, in alignment with current trends in Green Analytical Chemistry (GAC), which prioritize the reduction or elimination of hazardous substances to protect human health and the environment (Yin et al., 2024), more sustainable alternatives were explored, considering that the solvent must be immiscible with the aqueous phase to ensure efficient phase separation. Therefore, n-heptane and ethyl acetate were evaluated alongside dichloromethane. Ethyl acetate is classified as generally recognized as safe (GRAS) solvent (Endres & Guarino, 2025) and is a bio-based solvent compatible with GAC frameworks. Conversely, while n-heptane is not strictly a low-impact solvent, it exhibits lower toxicity and fewer chronic risks compared to the carcinogenic potential and systemic effects of dichloromethane (National Institute for Occupational Safety and Health, 2014; Phillips, 2018). Additionally, the use of n-heptane or ethyl acetate offers distinct functional advantages. Since both solvents have a lower density than the aqueous phase, they form the upper layer; this consequently simplifies the collection of the organic phase containing the target analytes.

As shown in Fig. 2, ethyl acetate yielded higher chromatographic areas for most analytes compared to dichloromethane and outperformed or matched n-heptane. Based on these analytical results and its superior ecological profile, ethyl acetate was selected for the final method.

Method validation

In the case of wines, the matrix effects could be common due to their high complexity, which may directly influence the detector signal, producing signal suppression or enhancement. Since all real wines may contain biogenic amines in their composition, which makes the study more complex, a simulated matrix was used. The use of this type of matrix has not only been previously documented in other protocols for the analysis of different wine parameters (Fernández-Vázquez et al., 2021) but is also included in the Compendium of International Methods of Analysis of Wines and Musts of OIV (International Organisation of Vine and Wine (OIV), 2025) as part of the validation strategies for different methodologies. Furthermore, this type of strategy is also adopted at the legislative level for complex samples such as food matrices (e.g., Commission Regulation (EU) No 10/2011 of 14 January 2011 is used to test plastic materials and articles intended to come into contact with food (European Union, 2011)). The results are included in Table S2. As can be observed, all analytes showed matrix effect values between 84% and 119%. Therefore, the matrix effect was considered negligible and, consequently, an instrumental calibration study was performed. This approach is also adopted in the official OIV method OIV-OENO 457-2014 (OIV, 2014).

The results obtained from calibration study are presented in Table 1, including the individual calibration range, the regression equations, the standard error of estimate (Sy/x), the coefficient of determination (R2), and the LODs and LOQs. It is important to note that for three of the biogenic amines (His, Dop, and Spd) no linear trend was observed. Therefore, in accordance with the recommendations of the IUPAC, the Mandel test (Andrade & Gómez-Carracedo, 2013) was applied. The calculated F values (27.79–738.25) for these biogenic amines were higher than the critical one (7.71). Thus, the null hypothesis was rejected, indicating that the quadratic model explained the variance better than the linear model. Consequently, the calibration curves for these three amines were fitted to quadratic equations. It should also be noted that for one of these amines (L-DOPA), no regression was observed between concentration and area ratios within the concentration range studied.

Analysis of wine samples. General considerations

The suitability of the developed method was tested across a diverse set of 56 wine samples from six different DOPs in Tenerife (Abona, Tacoronte-Acentejo, Valle de Güímar, Valle de la Orotava, Ycoden-Daute-Isora, and Island Blends). As shown in Table S1, the wine samples analyzed represent a wide oenological spectrum, ensuring the robustness of the method against different wine matrices. The ethanol content ranged from 10.6% vol to a maximum of 16.0% vol in a sweet wine. Regarding sugar content, the selection included extremely dry wines (glucose+fructose <0.30 g/L) and sweet wines reaching up to 117.5 g/L, with intermediate concentrations such as 67.0 g/L and 34.0 g/L. This diversity is essential, as high sugar concentrations are known to interfere with the derivatization or extraction steps in amine analysis due to the formation of sugar-derivative by-products (Costantini et al., 2019).

The acidity profile also showed significant heterogeneity. pH values fluctuated between 3.01 and 4.01, covering the typical range of Canary Islands wines, which are often characterized by relatively high total acidities despite their high pH, a phenomenon linked to the high potassium content in volcanic soils (Heras-Roger et al., 2016). Volatile acidity, expressed as acetic acid, remained generally within legal limits, although some samples showed relatively high values. Finally, the sulfur dioxide (SO₂) levels varied significantly, with total SO₂ reaching 183 mg/L but also being extremely low in others (15 mg/L), providing a comprehensive test for the method's selectivity in the presence of common wine additives.

The concentrations of biogenic amines for all samples were determined using the newly developed method (Table S3). Interestingly, two amines (His and Spd) were not detected in any of the samples, remaining below their respective limits of quantification (LOQ). For the detected biogenic amines, the results can be discussed based on their primary metabolic origin.

First, regarding the group of amines related to alcoholic fermentation and the raw material (grape), these are typically present in wines as a result of the yeast's normal metabolism or are already present in the initial grape must. The most ubiquitous amine of this group was EtOHA, detected in 55 out of 56 samples. Its concentration ranged from 10.3 mg/L to 73.8 mg/L. The prevalence of EtOHA is expected, as it is a natural component of the grape and is released during the breakdown of phosphatidylethanolamine in yeast cell membranes (Smit et al., 2008). Another amine frequently detected was EtA, showing a maximum concentration of 25.9 mg/L. Like EtOHA, its presence is generally associated with the initial fermentation stages and yeast amino acid metabolism (European Food Safety Authority (EFSA), 2011). Other aliphatic amines identified sporadically included MeA and IpA. A notable exception was sample TE-14, which presented a high concentration of MeA, presumably tied to specific amino acid degradation pathways during its particular vinification process (Macoviciuc et al., 2024) (see Fig. 3).

HPLC–FD chromatogram of the sample TE-14, showing the detection of EtOHA, MetA, EtA, PEA and Put using the developed methodology.

Secondly, the group of amines related to microbial alterations and spoilage is typically of greater concern for winemakers, as high concentrations often correlate with uncontrolled malolactic fermentation (MLF) in high-pH conditions or the presence of spoilage bacteria, such as Lactobacillus or Pediococcus genera (Barbieri et al., 2019). In this group, Put is a common product of the decarboxylation of ornithine and arginine by lactic acid bacteria (LAB), and it was quantifiable in several samples. The highest levels were found in red wines (24.9 mg/L and 21.0 mg/L). These samples also exhibited a relatively high pH (3.75), a condition that significantly favors the decarboxylase activity of bacteria during MLF (Lonvaud-Funel, 2006). Cdv and TyA are classic indicators of poor grape sanitary conditions or inadequate cellar hygiene, permitting the decarboxylating activity of Enterobacteriaceae or certain LAB in the tanks. In this study, they were detected at very low frequencies and concentrations (e.g., TyA maximum of 2.45 mg/L). The low incidence of these amines suggests that, despite the wide variety of matrices analyzed, winemakers maintained good sanitary standards. Similarly, Spm was only detected at a low level in a single sample. Its absence in the rest of the wine samples further confirms that the polyamine profile in these subtropical wines is dominated by simpler, and non-toxic structures.

In this sense, the fact that His, the most toxicologically relevant amine, was not detected in any sample is a significant finding. This suggests that the indigenous microflora of the Tenerife DOPs, or the winemaking practices employed (such as effective SO₂ management and temperature control), do not favor the accumulation of this compound, even in wines with high pH or low SO₂ levels (European Food Safety Authority (EFSA), 2011; Smit et al., 2008). The non-detection of His in any sample might be of importance for international trade. Although the legal limit of 10 mg/L in Switzerland was repealed in recent years, this threshold remains a gold standard for international importers and health-conscious markets, emphasizing that in none of our samples His was detected above the LOD of the method at 0.58 mg/L, as indicated in Table 1.

The concentrations of biogenic amines determined in the analyzed Tenerife wines were generally within or below the ranges reported for wines from other viticultural regions worldwide. Although quantitative data on EtOHA in wines are less frequently reported than for other amines, previous studies (Mir-Cerdà et al., 2022) have identified it as a recurrent component of wine biogenic amine profiles and as one of the compounds formed during fermentation processes.

For the biogenic amines typically associated with microbial activity during vinification, particularly Put, TyA, and Cdv, the concentrations observed were relatively low compared to those reported in other wine regions. While Put reached a maximum concentration of 24.9 mg/L in the present dataset, studies conducted on European red wines have reported median concentrations of approximately 19.4 mg/L and maximum values exceeding 100 mg/L (Konakovsky et al., 2011). Similarly, surveys of wines from the Rioja Alavesa region have reported average Put concentrations around 15.1 ± 8.3 mg/L (Diez-Ozaeta et al., 2022), while investigations of Italian commercial wines have found typical levels ranging between 5.4 and 9.5 mg/L (Patrignani et al., 2012). Therefore, although some wines analyzed in this study exhibited moderate concentrations of Put, most samples presented levels within the lower portion of the ranges described in the literature. TyA and Cdv were detected only sporadically and at low concentrations in the analyzed samples. The maximum TyA concentration observed in this study was 2.45 mg/L, which is lower than values commonly reported for red wines from other regions, where TyA concentrations were between 3 and 10 mg/L (Konakovsky et al., 2011). Likewise, Cdv was rarely detected in the wines of Tenerife analyzed here, whereas studies of commercial wines have reported typical concentrations around 0.5–2 mg/L or higher depending on vinification conditions (Konakovsky et al., 2011; Vinci et al., 2021).

His has not been detected (concentrations < LOD = 0.58 mg/L), in this study but concentrations ranging from approximately 0.5 to 26.9 mg/L have been measured in high-quality European red wines (Konakovsky et al., 2011). The absence of histamine in the Tenerife wines therefore suggests that the vinification conditions and microbial ecology involved in their production do not favor the accumulation of this compound, despite the relatively high pH values observed in some samples.

Overall, the results indicate that the biogenic amine profile of the analyzed Tenerife wines is characterized by relatively low concentrations of the most toxicologically relevant amines and by the predominance of fermentation-related amines. These findings are consistent with previous studies indicating that biogenic amine levels in wine strongly depend on grape sanitary quality, microbial populations present during fermentation, and the control of MLF processes (Mir-Cerdà et al., 2021; Smit et al., 2012).

Analysis of variance according to several factors

One-way ANOVA analyses were performed to further explore the factors influencing the occurrence of biogenic amines in the analyzed wines. Three grouping variables were considered: wine-producing area (Denomination of Origin) wine type (red, rosé and white), and residual sugar content. These statistical analyses allowed the evaluation of potential differences in both individual amines and total biogenic amine content (ΣBA).

Significant differences among wine-producing areas were observed for the individual biogenic amines, EtHOA, EtA, IaA, Put, and TyA, and consequently for total biogenic amine content (Table 3) Wines from the Abona DOP consistently exhibit the highest concentrations of several amines, with significant differences in relation to most of the remaining DOPs. Interestingly, TyA concentrations in wines from the Ycoden-Daute-Isora DOP were the highest compared with the rest of the considered regions; with significant differences, except Abona DOP. TyA formation has been associated with specific lactic acid bacteria strains possessing tyrosine decarboxylase activity (Marcobal et al., 2006). Consequently, the presence or absence of these microbial strains may significantly influence the amine profile of wines produced in different areas. Overall, the geographical variability observed in the present study confirms previous reports indicating that microbial ecology during fermentation is one of the main factors controlling the biogenic amine profile of wines (Costantini et al., 2019).

Conclusions

The analytical procedure developed in this work proved to be suitable for the determination of a wide range of biogenic amines in wine matrices. The method showed adequate sensitivity, selectivity and robustness, allowing its successful application to different types of wines.

The analysis of Tenerife wine samples revealed that the overall biogenic amine profile was dominated by compounds associated with fermentative metabolism, particularly EtOHA and EtA, whereas amines typically related to bacterial decarboxylation processes were detected at comparatively lower levels. In particular, His, one of the most toxicologically relevant amines in wine, was not detected in any of the analyzed samples. The total concentration of biogenic amines varied among wines but generally remained within moderate ranges commonly reported in the literature. Geographical origin exerts a stronger influence on biogenic amine composition than wine type or residual sugar content. Significant differences were observed among wine-producing areas, with wines from the Abona DOP presenting the highest values of total biogenic amines. These differences are likely related to variations in fermentation microbiota and winemaking practices rather than to intrinsic compositional factors. In contrast, wine type and residual sugar content did not show statistically significant effects on the overall amine composition, although red wines tended to present slightly higher levels of bacterial amines and biogenic amine index values.

The approach combining a new methodology for targeted determination of individual amines with derived indices and statistical analyses provides a useful tool for evaluating the safety and quality of wines and could be applied to broader surveys of biogenic amines in wines from other wine-producing regions.
