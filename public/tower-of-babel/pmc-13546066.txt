Scoping Review of Communication Assessment Tools for Family Caregivers: Current Status, Clinical Translation Gaps, and Future Directions
Authors: Jiechenming Xiao, Jiajing Tang, Weihong Zhang, Heng Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeFamily caregivers (FCs) serve as essential intermediaries in illness-related communication; however, the instruments used to assess FC communication vary widely in terms of conceptual scope and methodological quality. This scoping review aimed to systematically identify the available instruments for assessing FC communication, map their conceptual domains and key psychometric characteristics, and delineate research gaps and future directions.MethodsThis review was guided by the Arksey and O’Malley methodological framework and is reported in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses extension for Scoping Reviews. Six Chinese and English databases (China National Knowledge Infrastructure, Wanfang, PubMed, Embase, Web of Science Core Collection, and ProQuest) were searched from their inception to May 5, 2025, supplemented by citation tracking. Studies addressing the development, validation, cross-cultural adaptation, or application of FC communication instruments were eligible. Two reviewers independently screened the records and extracted the data using a structured form and synthesized the findings narratively using descriptive statistics.ResultsIn total, 21 studies published between 1997 and 2024 were analyzed, yielding nine instruments, most of which were developed after 2000 and were concentrated in the United States among patients with cancer. Conceptually, the instruments were mapped onto four domains: communication processes, attitudes, communication with healthcare professionals, and illness- and death-related communication. Most scales demonstrated satisfactory internal consistency (Cronbach’s α ≥ 0.8), with the notable exception of the Cancer Communication Assessment Tool for Patients and Families and its non-Chinese adaptations (α = 0.43–0.68), and reported acceptable structural validity. However, content and criterion validity have rarely been examined. External validation beyond the original development teams is scarce, and cross-cultural adaptations frequently require substantial restructuring.ConclusionEvidence on FC communication assessment is expanding but remains conceptually fragmented and methodologically uneven. Advancing the field requires a staged agenda: first, establishing a consensus-based conceptual taxonomy to clarify what each instrument measures; second, prioritizing rigorous validation and cross-cultural refinement of promising instruments over isolated new developments; and third, situating assessment within the organizational context of care, thereby strengthening the foundation for integrating communication assessment into clinical practice and intervention development.Keywords: family caregivers, scoping review, communication, psychometric properties, chronic diseases, aging

Introduction

Advances in medical science and public health have significantly improved the average global life expectancy.1 However, as mortality rates decline, the pace and extent of population aging have become more pronounced worldwide, and the proportion of the global population aged 60 years and older is expected to reach 13.9% in 2022.2 Population aging, rapid urbanization, and shifts in lifestyle patterns have collectively increased human health vulnerabilities, and the incidence of health conditions linked to these factors, such as cancer and chronic diseases.3–5 Consequently, these illnesses have become significant global public health concerns, contributing to increasing disease burden and imposing substantial economic pressure on affected families.6 As these illnesses progress, patients often require intensive care over extended periods. Within this context, the family serves as an essential source of multidimensional support, playing a crucial role in the patient care process and broader healthcare system. Family caregivers (FCs) are individuals without professional training who offer unpaid assistance to patients with chronic diseases, disabilities, or long-term health conditions.7 They typically include spouses, children, or other relatives who provide comprehensive support that addresses the physical, emotional, and social needs of the patients.8 Thus, these caregivers form an indispensable, yet often unrecognized, part of healthcare and social support networks.

In this context, FC communication requires careful conceptual unpacking. Drawing on established health communication frameworks and preliminary evidence from this field, FC communication encompasses at least four interrelated but conceptually distinct domains that comprise: (a) communication processes (the content, frequency, and quality of information exchange between caregivers, patients, and healthcare professionals, including perceived clarity, responsiveness, mutual understanding, and concordance between caregivers’ and patients’ perceptions); (b) communication attitudes (caregivers’ openness, avoidance, or willingness to engage in difficult conversations); (c) communication with healthcare professionals (caregivers’ perceptions of the quality of clinician interactions, including listening, respect, and shared decision-making); and (d) illness- and death-related communication (discussions about prognosis, end-of-life preferences, and death—content areas that are particularly emotionally charged and often avoided). Instruments developed to assess these domains target different communication partners, such as patients and professionals; different illness contexts, such as cancer, chronic illness, or dementia; and different evaluation perspectives, such as self-reporting by caregivers, patients, or dyadic assessments. Consequently, this conceptual heterogeneity presents a synthesis challenge and an opportunity to map the current landscape of available tools.

The number of patients newly diagnosed with cancer continues to increase annually. By 2022, approximately 19.98 million new cancer cases were reported worldwide. Demographic-based projections indicate that this burden will continue to rise, reaching over 35 million new cases by 2050, a 77% increase from that in 2022.3,9 This increasing prevalence of cancer places a substantial strain on healthcare systems and families. As the disease progresses, patients often lose the ability to care for themselves,10 and the associated psychological distress can lead to emotional difficulties. Although treatment options have become more diverse, they are often expensive, complex, and carry significant risks with varying outcomes among individuals. These challenges complicate the treatment decisions for many patients.11

Due to their close emotional connections and long-term trust with patients, FCs differ significantly from healthcare providers in their communication roles as they can navigate between objective clinical advice and personal patient preferences. In this capacity, FCs serve as authoritative interpreters of patient values, facilitators of family consensus, and continuous providers of emotional support. Therefore, patients increasingly rely on FCs as crucial intermediaries to facilitate communication among patients, relatives, and healthcare professionals.12 FCs bear a substantial proportion of the psychological burden associated with cancer diagnosis and frequently experience elevated levels of anxiety, depression, and sleep disturbances that parallel or exceed those of the patients themselves.13,14 A significant portion of this stress arises from communication challenges between patients and caregivers, often influenced by the high mortality risk of cancer and societal taboos surrounding discussions of death.15,16 Additionally, inadequate medical knowledge among families can hinder effective communication with healthcare professionals, intensifying the emotional and practical pressures faced by caregivers.

In addition to cancer, the prevalence of chronic illnesses has shown a consistent upward trend. In the United States, three out of four American adults have at least one chronic condition and over half of them have two or more chronic conditions.17 Similarly, a national survey showed that 81.1% of Chinese adults aged 60 years and older had at least one chronic disease and projected that the associated disease burden would rise substantially with population aging.18 Chronic illnesses generally progress slowly, persist over long periods, and result from multiple interacting causes, with few patients having the possibility of complete recovery.19 These patients often have a reduced ability to work and live independently, accompanied by pain, limited mobility, and greater reliance on others for daily support. As symptoms tend to be mild or absent during stable phases, most disease management occurs at home, where the critical role of FCs is frequently overlooked in routine medical evaluations.20 Given its prolonged and dynamic nature across both stable and acute stages, this care rests primarily on FCs.21

The demand for long-term care often leaves FCs fatigued and emotionally exhausted, resulting in substantial physical and psychological strain.22 Particularly, communication difficulties are among the most significant sources of this stress, involving emotional distress during patient interactions, anxiety-driven avoidance behaviors, and the complex responsibility of mediating between patients and healthcare professionals during treatment decisions. Notably, effective communication involving FCs promotes clearer information exchange with healthcare professionals, leading to medical decisions and care plans that better reflect patients’ preferences and needs, supporting stronger treatment adherence, and positively influencing health outcomes.23,24 Conversely, ineffective communication can intensify emotional distress; disrupt decision-making and treatment processes; increase safety concerns; and impose a greater financial burden on families, healthcare systems, and society.25 Although various instruments have been designed to assess and improve FC communication, differences in cultural settings, populations, and development approaches, and critically, the conceptual domains they purport to measure, have limited the development of a standardized framework.

Consequently, systematically examining the scope, diversity, and key features of the existing evidence on FC communication assessment tools, identifying gaps in the current research, and accounting for variations across studies is essential. Munn et al proposed six indications for conducting a scoping review, three of which the objectives of this study align with: examining how research is conducted in a specific field, identifying key characteristics or factors related to a concept, and mapping knowledge gaps.26 The fragmented and conceptually heterogeneous nature of the current research on FC communication assessment underscores the rationale for employing a scoping review methodology. Therefore, this scoping review aimed to systematically map, organize, and synthesize the current literature on instruments used to assess communication among FCs during illness, thereby equipping researchers and healthcare professionals with reliable and practical instruments to evaluate caregiver communication, while clarifying the conceptual boundaries and overlaps among existing tools. These findings are expected to enhance our understanding of family communication dynamics, support informed decision-making in patient care, and provide a conceptual basis for the development and application of communication assessment tools for FCs.

Methods

Study Design

This scoping review was guided by the methodological framework of Arksey and O’Malley and adhered to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses extension for Scoping Reviews (PRISMA-ScR) (See Table S1) guidelines to ensure methodological rigor.27,28 The review protocol was registered in the Open Science Framework (http://osf.io/yqgna) to promote transparency and prevent duplication.

Defining Research Questions

Based on a preliminary literature review and the conceptual framework outlined above, this study aimed to examine the features of the scales used to assess FC communication throughout a patient’s illness. Accordingly, this study addressed the following research questions: (1) Which tools are currently employed nationally and internationally to evaluate FC communication and what conceptual domains do they purport to measure? (2) What are the key psychometric properties and characteristics of the existing instruments in terms of cultural adaptation? (3) How widely and effectively are these tools implemented in different populations and clinical settings? and (4) What are the current research gaps in the development, validation, and application of these instruments and what directions should future research prioritize in advancing the field?

Identification of Relevant Studies

We comprehensively searched six Chinese and English databases, namely the China National Knowledge Infrastructure (CNKI), Wanfang, PubMed, Embase, Web of Science Core Collection, and ProQuest, covering records from the inception of each database until May 5, 2025. The search strategy integrated subject headings and free-text keywords, combined with Boolean operators, to ensure comprehensive coverage of the relevant literature. The search strategy was initially developed by a nursing graduate student skilled in literature retrieval, and subsequently validated by a specialized medical librarian to ensure adequate database coverage, which also helped optimize the combination of subject headings and Boolean operators. The research team reviewed the final strategy to confirm its alignment with the research question. We employed snowballing techniques, including screening reference lists of included studies, but excluded gray literature, such as conference abstracts and technical reports, to supplement the database search. The search strategy for CNKI represented the approach used for Chinese databases (see Table 1) and was managed using NoteExpress (version 3.7), whereas the PubMed search strategy illustrated the English database approach (see Table 1) and was managed using EndNote X9.

Study Selection

The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) studies that involved FCs as participants, including cases where patients completed the questionnaire, but the tool was designed for FC assessment; (2) original research focused on the development, validation, revision, adaptation, or application of communication assessment instruments for FCs; (3) studies employing quantitative, qualitative, or mixed-method designs; and (4) journal articles and academic theses. The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) studies involving professional or paid caregivers, (2) duplicate publications, (3) studies for which full texts were unavailable, and (4) studies published in languages other than Chinese or English.

All retrieved studies were imported into a reference management system to identify and remove duplicates. Two members of the research team independently screened the literature and extracted relevant data by evaluating titles, abstracts, and full texts. Any discrepancies between reviewers were resolved through discussion and consensus within the team.

Data Tables

A structured extraction table was developed to capture the essential information required to address the research questions and ensure thorough and accurate data collection. The extracted data included the title, author(s), year of publication, country or region, name of the assessment tool, type of tool (original, adapted, or culturally modified), theoretical framework, patient disease type, intended communication recipients, and psychometric properties. Two researchers (J.X. and H.Y). independently extracted data using a structured table Each researcher cross-checked the extracted data against the original articles to verify the accuracy and completeness. Any discrepancies between the two researchers were resolved through discussion; if consensus was not reached, a third independent reviewer (J.T.) was consulted for adjudication, and all decisions documented.

Organizing, Summarizing, and Reporting Results

This study used quantitative descriptive methods to compile and summarize the fundamental information from the included studies. This study provides a narrative overview of the features, usage patterns, and variations in research on communication assessment tools for FCs over the course of a patient’s illness.

Core Characteristics of the Scales

This review identified nine newly developed scales: the Cancer Communication Assessment Tool for Patients and Families (CCAT-PF), the Caregiver Centered Communication Questionnaire (CCCQ), the Difficulties in End-of-Life Discussions–Family Inventory (DEOLD-FI), the Caregiver Communication with Patients about Illness and Death Scale (CCID), the Openness to Discuss Cancer in the Family Scale (ODCF), the Family Caregiver Communication Tool (FCCT), the Family Caregiver Communication Tool for Chronic Illness (FCCT-CI), the Family Avoidance of Communication about Cancer (FACC), and the Experienced Communication in Dementia, which includes patient (ECD-P) and caregiver (ECD-C) versions. Conceptual domains were assessed using these instruments. While all nine instruments are positioned as measures of caregiver communication, they operationalize this construct in substantially different ways. Thus, based on our conceptual framework, the instruments identified can be grouped into four broad domains: 1) Communication process, 2) Communication attitudes, 3) Communication with healthcare professionals, and 4) Illness and death related communication (Figure 3).

Communication Process: Information Exchange and Interaction Quality

The CCAT-PF,31,32,34,35,43,47 FCCT,37 FCCT-CI,44,49 ECD-P, and ECD-C41,46 assess the content, quality, and perceived effectiveness of communication exchanges. These tools focus on the information that is communicated, such as treatment preferences, emotional concerns, and daily care needs, and how well it is communicated in terms of clarity, responsiveness, and mutual understanding. The CCAT-PF and its adaptations also capture concordance, which is the degree to which patients and caregivers agree on their communication experiences.

Communication Attitudes: Openness, Avoidance, and Willingness

The ODCF29 and FACC30,36,42 assess the dispositions of the caregivers and patients toward cancer-related discussions by measuring tendencies toward openness or avoidance rather than the content or quality of specific communication episodes.

Communication with Healthcare Professionals

The CCCQ38,45 is the only instrument specifically designed to assess perceptions of caregivers regarding communication quality with clinicians, focusing on whether providers listen, respect caregivers’ input, and facilitate shared decision-making.

Illness- and Death-Related Communication

The CCID33,39 and DEOLD-FI40,48 address communication regarding prognosis, death, and end-of-life preferences. This subset of communication content is emotionally charged and is often avoided. Our conceptual mapping revealed that family caregiver communication is not a monolithic construct, but rather a family of related yet distinct phenomena. The majority of the instruments (six of nine) focused on cancer populations; the remaining three addressed chronic illness as measured by the FCCT-CI, dementia as measured by the ECD-P and ECD-C, and home hospice as measured by the CCCQ. Among them, four were caregiver-reported, including the CCID, FCCT, FCCT-CI, and CCCQ; three were patient-reported, including the ODCF, FACC, and DEOLD-FI; and two included dyadic versions completed by patients and caregivers, including the CCAT-PF and the ECD-P and ECD-C.

Most of the identified scales demonstrated internal consistency. The ODCF (α = 0.86), FACC (α = 0.84–0.92 across versions), DEOLD-FI (α = 0.89–0.93 across versions), CCCQ (α = 0.97–0.98) and the Danish version of the CCID (α = 0.88) demonstrated strong reliability, with Cronbach’s α coefficients > 0.8, meanwhile the FCCT reached the 0.80 threshold. The clinimetric evaluation of the FCCT-CI (α = 0.74–0.86) and the ECD-P and ECD-C (α = 0.75–0.82) showed acceptable reliability across dimensions. In contrast, the CCAT-PF and its German, Korean, and Australian adaptations consistently yielded coefficients < 0.7 (α = 0.43–0.68), particularly for the concordance dimension, indicating that further evaluation is required; only the Chinese version achieved satisfactory reliability (α = 0.90). In contrast, reliability coefficients were not reported for the original CCID, the 30-item CCAT-PF, or the initial ECD development study. Discussion

This scoping review identified nine instruments designed to assess FC communication across diverse illness contexts. We synthesized the conceptual coverage, psychometric properties, and patterns of clinical application of these instruments. A key finding was the substantial conceptual heterogeneity among the identified tools. Rather than measuring a single, unified construct, these instruments operationalize caregiver communication as distinct facets: communication processes, communication attitudes, communication with professionals, and illness- and death-related discussions. Although this conceptual diversity reflects the multidimensional nature of caregiver communication in real-world clinical settings, it also complicates direct comparisons across studies and limits the development of a unified evidence base.

Conceptual Heterogeneity and Its Implications for Instrument Selection

Caregivers engage in multiple forms of communication that warrant distinct assessment approaches; however, this creates significant challenges for synthesis and translation. Without a shared conceptual vocabulary, researchers may inadvertently compare instruments that measure fundamentally different constructs, and clinicians may struggle to select the most appropriate tool for a given assessment goal.50

Consequently, the implications of this heterogeneity are two-fold. First, instrument selection must be guided by a clear specification of the facets of caregiver communication that are relevant to the clinical or research question. For example, using a tool designed to assess communication attitudes to evaluate communication processes can produce misleading findings. Second, the field would benefit substantially by developing a consensus-based conceptual taxonomy of FC communication, distinguishing among the following four domains defined in the Introduction: communication processes, communication attitudes, communication with healthcare professionals, and illness- and death-related communication. This taxonomy would enhance the precision of future reviews, facilitate the development of targeted instruments, and provide clinicians with practical guidance for tool selection.

Psychometric Quality and Methodological Gaps

The psychometric evidence for the identified instruments revealed notable strengths and persistent limitations. Eight of the nine scales reported internal consistency, which was acceptable for most instruments and dimensions. The ODCF, FACC, DEOLD-FI, and CCCQ showed particularly strong reliability, along with the Danish adaptation of the CCID, although the original CCID study did not report a reliability coefficient. The FCCT, FCCT-CI, and ECD demonstrated acceptable values, whereas the values for CCAT-PF and its non-Chinese adaptations consistently fell below 0.7, a pattern largely attributable to its concordance dimension. These findings indicate steady methodological progress in the operationalization of caregiver communication constructs, while identifying the cross-cultural reliability of the CCAT-PF as a specific target for refinement.

However, several psychometric deficiencies warrant critical attention. First, evaluations of content validity were relatively limited and only approximately half of the included studies examined criterion validity against relevant external benchmarks. Although several studies attributed this limitation to the absence of appropriate criterion-referenced questionnaires, the reliance on simple correlation analyses with psychological measures (eg, anxiety, depression, and quality of life) provides only indirect and insufficient evidence for robust validity.51 Consequently, future research should proactively identify and incorporate appropriate criteria and consider cross-validation methods to strengthen the validity of the evidence.

Second, most scales demonstrated adequate structural validity with acceptable model fit indices; however, the substantial modifications observed across cross-cultural adaptations, such as the condensation of the CCAT-PF from nine to four dimensions in Germany or the restructuring of the DEOLD-FI from two to five dimensions in China, highlight the contextual sensitivity of caregiver communication constructs. These adaptations are methodologically justified when guided by exploratory factor analysis and expert feedback, but raise questions regarding the cross-cultural generalizability of the original factor structures and underscore the need for rigorous theory-driven adaptation processes.

Third, the limited external validation of most of the instruments represents a critical gap. With the notable exception of the CCAT-PF, which has undergone successful independent validation across multiple countries, most tools, including the CCID, CCCQ, and ECD scales, remain confined to their original development teams. The scarcity of their external replications reflects several converging factors. Moreover, cross-cultural validation demands methodological rigor that extends beyond translation and back-translation to comprehensive psychometric re-evaluation and recruiting large heterogeneous caregiver samples during periods of high emotional burden is logistically challenging.52,53

Implementation Barriers—Cultural, Organizational, and Systemic Contexts

Beyond psychometric considerations, the successful implementation of caregiver communication assessment tools depends on a range of cultural, organizational, and systemic factors that are often overlooked in instrument development and validation studies.

Cultural Context

Cross-cultural adaptation studies have consistently demonstrated that dimensional structures require substantial modification when instruments are translated and validated in new settings. As the dimensional restructuring described in the previous section illustrates, such modifications reflect genuine cultural differences in communication norms, family dynamics, and illness perceptions rather than methodological artifacts.54 However, they also complicate cross-study comparisons and limit the development of universally applicable instruments. This suggests that, rather than pursuing a single “gold standard” instrument, the field may benefit from developing culturally adaptable core modules supplemented by context-specific items, a strategy that preserves conceptual consistency while accommodating cultural variation.

Disease-Specific Concentration

The predominance of cancer-focused instruments reflects the epidemiological urgency and communicative intensity of oncological care, where frequent high-stakes conversations about prognosis and end-of-life preferences are routine.55,56 In contrast, chronic conditions such as heart failure, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, and diabetes, while equally burdensome, involve less concentrated periods of crisis and more gradual functional decline, making caregiver communication less conspicuous and therefore, less likely to attract dedicated instrument development.57 Although the FCCT-CI represents a notable effort to address this gap, it remains one of the few such instruments. This disease-specific concentration is not a reflection of relative clinical importance but rather of historical research priorities and funding allocations. Consequently, addressing this imbalance requires intentional efforts to develop and validate instruments for underrepresented chronic disease populations, where the communication needs of caregivers may differ in content, timing, and intensity.57,58

Organizational Context

Notably, implementation barriers raise concerns regarding the organizational environment in which caregiver communication occurs. All nine instruments identified in this review conceptualize communication primarily as an individual- or dyadic-level construct, focusing on the attitudes, processes, or competencies of the caregivers. However, recent evidence indicates that the quality of these interactions is strongly influenced by organizational conditions. In a multicenter cross-sectional study of 3,949 nurses working in Italian home care settings, Cesare et al59 demonstrated that a higher workload, perceived inadequacy of managerial leadership and support, and higher levels of professional burnout were significantly associated with an increased risk of workplace violence, an extreme manifestation of the breakdown of care relationships and communication processes. These findings indicate that communication between FCs and healthcare professionals is embedded in and constrained by the organizational environment in which it takes place.60

This organizational perspective has three implications for caregiver communication assessment. First, at the interpretive level, scores on caregiver communication scales should not be regarded as purely individual attributes. For instance, low scores may partly reflect systemic constraints such as time pressure, inadequate staffing, or insufficient leadership support, rather than deficits in caregivers’ communication competence. Second, at the implementation level, integrating communication assessment into routine care requires concurrent attention to organizational conditions that enable or impede high-quality communication. Third, at the instrument development level, future research may benefit from complementing individual-level scales with organizational-level measures of the care environment or from validating existing tools across diverse organizational settings to more fully capture the determinants of caregiver communication. In summary, communication assessment tools should ultimately be interpreted and implemented within the organizational environments in which family caregivers and healthcare professionals interact.

From Psychometric Tools to Clinical Information Assets: The Imperative of Clinical Relevance

The value of caregiver communication instruments should be evaluated by their psychometric performance and their clinical relevance. Healthcare systems have traditionally prioritized measuring what is administratively convenient, rather than what truly reflects the complexity of care.61 FC communication is a clinically meaningful yet frequently overlooked aspect of care; it shapes treatment decisions, patient well-being, and caregiver burden, but is rarely captured in routine assessment.61,62 Therefore, the instruments identified in this review should be considered as psychometric tools and as potential enablers of more comprehensive care assessment, transforming caregiver communication into standardized, actionable information that can identify families in need of support, inform clinical decision-making, and monitor the effects of communication-focused interventions. However, realizing this potential requires explicit attention to clinical utility, including feasibility within routine workflows, interpretability of scores for clinicians, and demonstrated impact on care decisions and outcomes, along with traditional psychometric evaluations.

This perspective reframes caregiver communication assessment tools as clinical information assets rather than merely psychometric instruments, which has multiple implications for the development and implementation of the instruments. For instance, developers should prioritize psychometric performance and the usability and interpretability of tool outputs in clinical workflows. Additionally, integration into routine care requires compatibility with existing clinical documentation systems, such as mapping items to standardized terminologies.61 Consequently, future research should explicitly examine predictive validity against clinically relevant outcomes, including caregiver burnout, hospital readmission, and treatment adherence, to demonstrate that the measurement translates into improved care.55 More broadly, research priorities should shift from asking whether a tool measures what it intends to measure to asking whether measuring a given construct and acting on the information it provides ultimately improves patient and caregiver outcomes. Ultimately, the value of these tools lies not in their psychometric properties per se but in their capacity to make a clinically meaningful dimension of care visible. Therefore, translating visibility into actionable information can improve care delivery, support caregivers, and enhance patient outcomes.

Future Research Priorities—From Fragmentation to Integration

Based on the findings and limitations of this review, we propose a strategic agenda for advancing research on FC communication assessments. The agenda was organized into four interconnected priorities.

First, the field must invest in developing a consensus-based conceptual taxonomy for FC communication. As previously documented, measuring different facets of communication under a single label undermines comparability and synthesis. Conversely, a consensus taxonomy that operationalizes the four domains mapped in this review provides a common language for instrument selection, development, and evaluation. This effort should involve interdisciplinary collaboration among nursing, psychology, social work, palliative medicine, and communication studies and should incorporate patient and caregiver perspectives to ensure conceptual relevance.

Second, validation should take precedence over the development of novel instruments. Although this recommendation requires further validation, this heterogeneity, compounded by the disease-specific focus of the current instruments, suggests that straightforward validation alone, without a shared conceptual vocabulary, may perpetuate fragmentation. Therefore, we advocate for a two-stage approach. In the immediate term, researchers should prioritize rigorous cross-cultural validation and psychometric refinement of the most promising and widely used instruments, such as the CCAT-PF, while specifying which facet of caregiver communication each tool measures. Simultaneously, researchers should invest in developing the conceptual taxonomy described above, which will provide a common framework for future instrument selection and adaptation. Ultimately, validation efforts will be most productive when guided by such a taxonomy, enabling meaningful comparisons across studies and populations, and ensuring that validated instruments translate into effective clinical interventions.

Third, implementation research should expand beyond psychometric evaluations to address the cultural, organizational, and systemic contexts that shape caregiver communication. Accordingly, future studies should examine the associations between organizational factors, such as staffing adequacy, leadership quality, staff burnout, and FC-reported communication outcomes using longitudinal and multilevel designs to establish causal pathways. Similarly, efforts to develop and validate instruments for underrepresented chronic disease populations and adapt existing tools across diverse cultural settings are essential to ensure equitable applicability. Such evidence would not only enrich the validity framework of existing instruments but also position communication assessment as a tool for systemic improvement at the individual level and at the level of care delivery organizations, rather than merely individual evaluations.

Fourth, future studies should explicitly evaluate the clinical utility of the caregiver communication instruments. For instance, beyond psychometric validation, they should examine whether assessment results are interpretable and actionable for clinicians, whether they can be integrated into routine care workflows, and whether their use improves the identification of families in need of support, and ultimately, patient and caregiver outcomes. Embedding communication measurements within broader, standardized assessments of care complexity would help ensure that these tools function as instruments for clinical decision-making rather than solely as research measures.

Strengths and Limitations

This study has several notable strengths, including rigorous adherence to the PRISMA-ScR reporting standards and a well-defined methodological framework. The study also provides researchers with a structured overview of, and directions for quantitative research on caregiver communication. However, this study also has some limitations. First, although this review focused on a comprehensive synthesis, it did not employ standardized quality appraisal tools, such as COSMIN, to evaluate individual study quality. Second, the inclusion criteria were restricted to Chinese and English publications, which may have excluded relevant scales developed in other linguistic or cultural contexts, thus narrowing the scope of the findings. Third, the multidimensional and context-dependent nature of “family caregiving” and “communication” introduces conceptual ambiguity; many studies lack clearly articulated theoretical frameworks, resulting in a degree of theoretical inconsistency across the reviewed literature. Fourth, the limited evidence supporting criterion validity and the absence of externally replicated validation studies weaken the evidence base, preventing strong evidence-based recommendations at this stage. Fifth, the conceptual heterogeneity identified in this review, although a notable finding, indicates that the synthesized evidence does not represent a single coherent literature, but rather multiple intersecting literature. Therefore, future syntheses should consider narrower and more homogeneously defined constructs to enable more meaningful comparisons. Future reviews can achieve breakthroughs in two key directions: standardization and collaboration. Rigorous methodological evaluations should be considered to identify high-quality evidence through interdisciplinary cooperation and multicenter studies, validating promising tools in different patient populations, transforming isolated findings into beneficial evidence chains, and providing more precise recommendations for communication measures for healthcare professionals in clinical and research settings.

Conclusion

With the intensification in population aging and evolving disease patterns worldwide, FCs have become an increasingly visible component of healthcare systems, and communication is central to the care they provide. This scoping review systematically mapped existing instruments for assessing FC communication and identified nine tools with conceptual coverage spanning four distinct domains: communication processes, communication attitudes, communication with healthcare professionals, and illness- and death-related communication. Several instruments demonstrated acceptable psychometric properties, most notably robust internal consistency and reasonable structural validity, providing a quantitative basis for evaluating caregiver communication. Nevertheless, the current evidence base is constrained by limited content and criterion validity testing, scarcity of independent external validation, and substantial dimensional restructuring during cross-cultural adaptation, all of which temper the strength and generalizability of the available evidence.

Consequently, future research should pursue a dual-stage agenda to shift the field from fragmentation to integration. First, a shared consensus-based conceptual taxonomy of FC communication that clarifies the constructs being measured and enables meaningful comparisons across studies and populations should be urgently established. Second, guided by such a framework, researchers should place greater emphasis on the systematic validation, psychometric refinement, and cross-cultural adaptation of existing high-potential instruments, such as the CCAT-PF, rather than continuing to develop new tools in isolation. Moreover, because existing instruments conceptualize communication primarily at the individual or dyadic level, future studies should situate caregiver communication assessments within organizational environments, including workload, leadership support, and staff well-being, in which caregivers and healthcare professionals interact. Ultimately, the value of these instruments lies not in their psychometric properties alone but in their capacity to render a clinically meaningful dimension of care visible and actionable. Translating this measurement capability into routine assessments can support caregivers, inform them about tailored interventions, and improve patient and family outcomes.
