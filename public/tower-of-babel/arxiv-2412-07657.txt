Probabilistic Modelling of Multiple Long-Term Condition Onset Times
Kieran Richards, Kelly Fleetwood, Regina Prigge, Paolo Missier, Michael Barnes, Nick J. Reynolds, Bruce Guthrie, Sohan Seth

Abstract
The co-occurrence of multiple long-term conditions (MLTC), or multimorbidity, in an individual can reduce their lifespan and severely impact their quality of life. Exploring the longitudinal patterns, e.g. clusters, of disease accrual can help better understand the genetic and environmental drivers of multimorbidity, and potentially identify individuals who may benefit from early targeted intervention. We introduce probabilistic modelling of onset times , or ProMOTe , for clustering and forecasting MLTC trajectories. ProMOTe seamlessly learns from incomplete and unreliable disease trajectories that is commonplace in Electronic Health Records but often ignored in existing longitudinal clustering methods. We analyse data from 150,000 individuals in the UK Biobank and identify 50 clusters showing patterns of disease accrual that have also been reported by some recent studies. We further discuss the forecasting capabilities of the model given the history of disease accrual.



1
Introduction

Multimorbidity, or the presence of Multiple Long-Term Conditions (MLTC), is a widespread public health concern with around one third of adults globally estimated to have multimorbidity
[
13
]
. Multimorbidity is associated with poorer quality of life
[
20
]
and higher mortality
[
21
]
. Early prediction of multimorbidity can, therefore, facilitate intervention and improve quality of life in vulnerable individuals, while grouping together similar trajectories can facilitate exploring the underlying genetic and sociodemographic drivers of these trajectories
[
27
]
. Electronic Health Records (EHR) provide unprecedented information on the presence or absence of long-term conditions (LTCs) in individuals, which together with their respective date of diagnosis can be used to identify groups of individuals with similar accrual characteristics
[
4
]
, and predict their future disease accrual.

Most attempts at modelling MLTC groups have used a cross-sectional approach without temporal information
[
3
,
5
,
8
]
. Alternative longitudinal approaches are relatively recent, and offer insight into the
onset
of MLTC over life course
[
4
,
16
]
. Several longitudinal approaches model only the count of MLTC affecting an individual over time, ignoring the specific conditions themselves
[
6
,
24
]
. Few studies have considered specific conditions and their diagnosis dates in clustering
[
14
,
10
]
.
A more recent study introduced a topic modelling approach, i.e. Age-dependent Topic Modelling (ATM), that allows disease topic loadings to vary with age
[
16
]
.

None of the studies, to the best of our knowledge, address the unreliability and incompleteness of the longitudinal EHR data in the respective analysis while these features are ubiquitous. For example, chronic conditions which developed prior to registration often do not have their proper diagnosis date reported, i.e. they are
unreliable
, while any information of disease accrual is censored at the current age, i.e. one does not have knowledge of the conditions an individual will develop in the future or the respective trajectories are
incomplete
.
The existing approaches are also not fully generative, i.e. they do not allow, given history, forecasting future disease accrual alongside their respective onset times and they do not capture the associated uncertainty.

We propose a probabilistic framework, referred to as
Probabilistic Modelling of Onset-Time
or
ProMOTe
, for longitudinal clustering of multimorbidity accrual, that considers both the LTCs present in an individual and their respective time of onset (using respective age at diagnosis as proxy). Our approach, inspired by Latent Class Analysis (LCA)
[
12
]
, models the age at diagnosis of each LTC alongside their presence or absence in an individual. Unlike existing methods for clustering multimorbidity trajectories,
ProMOTe
(1) learns from incomplete and unreliable disease trajectories seamlessly, (2) allows predicting future LTC accrual based on the current,
possibly unreliable or partially observed
, disease trajectory, and (3) estimates uncertainty at a population (i.e. cluster) and at an individual level (i.e. personalized disease trajectory), both of which are desirable for clinical AI applications
[
1
]
. We assess
ProMOTe
’s clustering and predictive capabilities experimentally, using both synthetic and UK Biobank data.

\boldsymbol{\Phi}=\{\Phi^{(n)}\}_{n=1}^{N}
. A table of all the notation used here and throughout the rest of the paper is available in Appendix
A
.

Under this construction we can write the full likelihood as:

\displaystyle p\left(\boldsymbol{\Phi},\{\mathbf{z}^{(n)}\}\,|\,\{\pi_{m,k}\},\{{\boldsymbol{\eta}}_{m,k}\},{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\right)\propto

\displaystyle\prod_{n=1}^{N}\prod_{k=1}^{K}\left[\gamma_{k}\prod_{m=1}^{M}\left(1-\pi_{m,k}\right)^{\left(1-d_{m}^{\left(n\right)}\right)}\left(\pi_{m,k}f_{\mathrm{ExpFam}}\left(t^{(n)}_{m}\,|\,{\boldsymbol{\eta}}_{m,k}\right)\right)^{d_{m}^{\left(n\right)}}\right]^{z_{k}^{\left(n\right)}}

(1)

We complete the Bayesian framework by assigning independent priors to the model parameters as
{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\sim\mathrm{Dir}\left({\boldsymbol{\theta}}\right)
;
\pi_{m,k}\sim\mathrm{Beta}\left(a_{m,k},b_{m,k}\right)
; and
{\boldsymbol{\eta}}_{m,k}\sim\mathrm{ExpFamConj}\left(\nu_{m,k},{\boldsymbol{\chi}}_{m,k}\right)

where
{\boldsymbol{\theta}},a_{m,k},b_{m,k},\nu_{m,k},\text{ and }{\boldsymbol{\chi}}_{m,k}
are hyperparameters and
f_{\mathrm{ExpFamConj}}\left({\boldsymbol{\eta}}\,|\,\nu,{\boldsymbol{\chi}}\right)=g\left({\boldsymbol{\eta}}\right)^{\nu}f\left(\nu,{\boldsymbol{\chi}}\right)\exp\left({\boldsymbol{\eta}}^{\top}{\boldsymbol{\chi}}\right)

2.2
Variational Bayes

We propose a mean-field Variational Bayes (VB) approach to approximate the posterior distribution. VB scales much more efficiently to large EHR data than other methods
[
2
]
. We first describe the approach in the straightforward case of complete data i.e. assuming
\kappa^{(n)}_{m}={\color[rgb]{1,0.5,0}\textsc{o}}\;\forall n,m
. The full derivation is available in Appendix
C
. Later we will describe how the method can be extended to handle both unreliable and incomplete data.
We factorize the variational approximation as

q\left({\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\right)\prod_{m=1}^{M}\prod_{k=1}^{K}q\left(\pi_{m,k}\right)\prod_{m=1}^{M}\prod_{k=1}^{K}q\left({\boldsymbol{\eta}}_{m,k}\right)\prod_{n=1}^{N}q\left(\mathbf{z}^{(n)}\right)

and assume the following variational distributions:
{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\sim\mathrm{Dir}\left({\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{*}\right)
,
\pi_{m,k}\sim\mathrm{Beta}\left(a_{m,k}^{*},b_{m,k}^{*}\right)
,
{\boldsymbol{\eta}}_{m,k}\sim\mathrm{ExpFamConj}\left(\nu_{m,k}^{*},{\boldsymbol{\chi}}_{m,k}^{*}\right)
, and
\mathbf{z}^{\left(n\right)}\sim\mathrm{Mult}\left(1,\mathbf{r}^{(n)\ast}\right)
.

Due to conjugacy, the respective parameters have closed form update equations as follows (see Appendix
C
):

\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{\ast}

\displaystyle={\boldsymbol{\theta}}+\bar{\mathbf{z}}

(2)

\displaystyle a_{m,k}^{\ast}

\displaystyle=a_{m,k}+\bar{n}_{m,k};\quad b_{m,k}^{\ast}=b_{m,k}+\bar{z}_{k}-\bar{n}_{m,k}

(3)

\displaystyle\nu^{\ast}_{m,k}

\displaystyle=\nu_{m,k}+\bar{n}_{m,k};\quad{\boldsymbol{\chi}}^{\ast}_{m,k}={\boldsymbol{\chi}}_{m,k}+\bar{\mathbf{T}}_{m,k}

(4)

\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}_{k}^{(n)\ast}

\displaystyle\propto\exp\left(\zeta_{k}+\sum_{m=1}^{M}d_{m}^{(n)}\left(\lambda_{m,k}+u_{{\color[rgb]{1,0.5,0}\textsc{o}},m,k}^{(n)}\right)\right)

(5)

where expressions for
\bar{\mathbf{z}}
,
\bar{n}_{m,k}
,
\bar{\mathbf{T}}_{m,k}
,
\zeta_{k}
,
\lambda_{m,k}
, and
u_{{\color[rgb]{1,0.5,0}\textsc{o}},m,k}^{(n)}
can all be found in Appendix
C
.
The quantities
\bar{z}_{k},\bar{n}_{m,k}
and
\bar{\mathbf{T}}_{m,k}/\bar{n}_{m,k}
can be intuitively understood as the expected number of individuals in each cluster, the expected number of individuals with the
m
-th disease in cluster
k
and the expected (sufficient statistics of) diagnosis age of the
m
-th disease in cluster
k
respectively. The quantities
\zeta_{k}
and
\lambda_{m,k}
depend on the variational parameters
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{\ast}
,
a_{m,k}^{\ast}
, and
b_{m,k}^{\ast}
while
u_{{\color[rgb]{1,0.5,0}\textsc{o}},m,k}^{(n)}
depends on
\nu_{m,k}^{\ast}
and
{\boldsymbol{\chi}}_{m,k}^{\ast}
.

2.3
Incomplete Data, i.e.
\kappa^{(n)}_{m}={\color[rgb]{1,0.5,0}\textsc{i}}

To adapt the VB approach to handle right-censoring, or incomplete trajectories, we consider the respective
d_{m}^{(n)}
and

to be latent variables with

to be partially known, i.e.
t_{m}^{(n)}>\tau^{(n)}

\displaystyle d_{m}^{(n)}

\displaystyle\quad\sim\quad\textrm{Bern}\left(\pi^{(n)\ast}_{m}\right)

\displaystyle t_{m}^{(n)}

\displaystyle\quad\sim\quad\mathrm{ExpFamTrunc}_{l}({\boldsymbol{\eta}}_{m}^{(n)*})

(7)

where
\mathrm{ExpFamTrunc}_{l}
denotes a left truncated exponential family distribution, and unless otherwise stated left truncation is always done at
\tau^{(n)}
. The respective update equations are given as

\displaystyle\pi_{m}^{(n)\ast}

\displaystyle=\frac{\exp\left(\sum_{k=1}^{K}{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}_{k}^{(n)\ast}\left(\lambda_{m,k}+u_{{\color[rgb]{1,0.5,0}\textsc{i}},m,k}^{(n)}\right)\right)}{1+\exp\left(\sum_{k=1}^{K}{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}_{k}^{(n)\ast}\left(\lambda_{m,k}+u_{{\color[rgb]{1,0.5,0}\textsc{i}},m,k}^{(n)}\right)\right)}

(8)

\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{\eta}}_{m}^{(n)\ast}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{K}{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}_{k}^{(n)\ast}\mathbb{E}\left({\boldsymbol{\eta}}_{m,k}\right).

(9)

and an expression for
u_{{\color[rgb]{1,0.5,0}\textsc{i}},m,k}^{(n)}
can be found in Appendix
C
.

2.4
Unreliable Data, i.e.
\kappa^{(n)}_{m}={\color[rgb]{1,0.5,0}\textsc{u}}

To adapt the VB approach to handle left-censoring, or unreliable information prior to the beginning of the study,
we consider
d_{m}^{(n)}=1
to be observed (i.e. the respective condition to be present) but
t_{m}^{(n)}
to be a latent variable that is partially known, i.e.
t_{m}^{(n)}<\rho^{(n)}
, and

\displaystyle t_{m}^{(n)}

\displaystyle\quad\sim\quad\mathrm{ExpFamTrunc}_{r}({\boldsymbol{\eta}}_{m}^{(n)*})

(10)

where
\mathrm{ExpFamTrunc}_{r}
denotes a right truncated exponential family distribution, and unless otherwise stated right truncation is always done at
\rho^{(n)}
. The respective update equation is given as in (
9
). An expression for
u_{{\color[rgb]{1,0.5,0}\textsc{u}},m,k}^{(n)}
can be found in Appendix
C
.

2.5
Variational Bayes for Full Model

For the full model with observed, unreliable and incomplete data,
the update rules in (

), (

), (

), and (

{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}_{k}^{(n)\ast}\propto\exp\left(\zeta_{k}+\sum_{m:\kappa={{\color[rgb]{1,0.5,0}\textsc{o}}}}d_{m}^{(n)}\left(\lambda_{m,k}^{(n)}+u_{{\color[rgb]{1,0.5,0}\textsc{o}},m,k}^{(n)}\right)\right.+\left.\sum_{m:\kappa={{\color[rgb]{1,0.5,0}\textsc{u}}}}\left(\lambda_{m,k}^{(n)}+u_{{\color[rgb]{1,0.5,0}\textsc{u}},m,k}^{(n)}\right)+\sum_{m:\kappa={{\color[rgb]{1,0.5,0}\textsc{i}}}}\pi_{m}^{(n)\ast}\left(\lambda_{m,k}^{(n)}+u_{{\color[rgb]{1,0.5,0}\textsc{i}},m,k}^{(n)}\right)\right)

\displaystyle\bar{n}_{m,k}

\displaystyle=\sum_{n:\kappa_{m}^{(n)}={{\color[rgb]{1,0.5,0}\textsc{o}}},{{\color[rgb]{1,0.5,0}\textsc{u}}}}d_{m}^{\left(n\right)}{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}_{k}^{(n)\ast}+\sum_{n:\kappa_{m}^{(n)}={{\color[rgb]{1,0.5,0}\textsc{i}}}}\pi_{m}^{(n)\ast}{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}_{k}^{(n)\ast}

(12)

\displaystyle\bar{\mathbf{T}}_{m,k}

\displaystyle=\sum_{n:\kappa_{m}^{(n)}={{\color[rgb]{1,0.5,0}\textsc{o}}}}d_{m}^{\left(n\right)}{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}_{k}^{(n)\ast}\mathbf{T}_{m}^{(n)}+\sum_{n:\kappa_{m}^{(n)}={{\color[rgb]{1,0.5,0}\textsc{u}}}}{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}_{k}^{(n)\ast}\mathbb{E}_{r}\left(\mathbf{T}_{m}^{(n)}\right)+\sum_{n:\kappa_{m}^{(n)}={{\color[rgb]{1,0.5,0}\textsc{i}}}}\pi_{m}^{(n)\ast}{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}_{k}^{(n)\ast}\mathbb{E}_{l}\left(\mathbf{T}_{m}^{(n)}\right)

2.6
Predictive Posterior

We now consider a new individual where the trajectory can be partitioned into observed, unreliable and incomplete sets, i.e.
\Phi_{{\color[rgb]{1,0.5,0}\ast}}^{\prime}=\left(\mathbf{d}_{{\color[rgb]{1,0.5,0}\ast}}^{\prime},\mathbf{t}_{{\color[rgb]{1,0.5,0}\ast}}^{\prime},{\boldsymbol{\kappa}}_{{\color[rgb]{1,0.5,0}\ast}}^{\prime}\right)=\left(d^{\prime}_{m},t^{\prime}_{m},\kappa^{\prime}_{m}\right)_{m:\kappa_{m}^{\prime}={{\color[rgb]{1,0.5,0}\ast}}}
where
{\color[rgb]{1,0.5,0}\ast}={\color[rgb]{1,0.5,0}\textsc{o}},{\color[rgb]{1,0.5,0}\textsc{u}}
or
i
. We are interested in finding the missing values
\mathbf{d}_{{\color[rgb]{1,0.5,0}\textsc{i}}}^{\prime}
and
\mathbf{t}_{{\color[rgb]{1,0.5,0}\textsc{i}}}^{\prime}
or the probability of observing the presence and absence of LTCs that have not been observed by time
\tau^{\prime}
and their respective age at diagnosis after
\tau^{\prime}
, i.e. (with a slight abuse of notation)

p\left(\mathbf{d}_{{\color[rgb]{1,0.5,0}\textsc{i}}}^{\prime},\mathbf{t}_{{\color[rgb]{1,0.5,0}\textsc{i}}}^{\prime}\right.\,|\,\left.\Phi_{{\color[rgb]{1,0.5,0}\textsc{o}}}^{\prime},\Phi_{{\color[rgb]{1,0.5,0}\textsc{u}}}^{\prime},{\boldsymbol{\kappa}}_{{\color[rgb]{1,0.5,0}\textsc{i}}}^{\prime},\boldsymbol{\Phi},\rho^{\prime},\tau^{\prime}\right)=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\left[p(z_{k}\,|\,\Phi^{\prime}_{{\color[rgb]{1,0.5,0}\textsc{o}}},\Phi_{{\color[rgb]{1,0.5,0}\textsc{u}}}^{\prime},{\boldsymbol{\kappa}}_{{\color[rgb]{1,0.5,0}\textsc{i}}}^{\prime},\boldsymbol{\Phi},\rho^{\prime},\tau^{\prime})p(\mathbf{d}_{{\color[rgb]{1,0.5,0}\textsc{i}}}^{\prime},\mathbf{t}_{{\color[rgb]{1,0.5,0}\textsc{i}}}^{\prime}\,|\,z_{k},{\boldsymbol{\kappa}}_{{\color[rgb]{1,0.5,0}\textsc{i}}}^{\prime},\boldsymbol{\Phi},\tau^{\prime})\right]

(14)

Here the first term is the posterior probability of assigning the observed trajectory to a cluster, i.e.

\displaystyle p(z_{k}\,|\,\Phi^{\prime}_{{\color[rgb]{1,0.5,0}\textsc{o}}},\mathbf{d}_{{\color[rgb]{1,0.5,0}\textsc{u}}}^{\prime},{\boldsymbol{\kappa}}_{{\color[rgb]{1,0.5,0}\textsc{u}}}^{\prime},{\boldsymbol{\kappa}}_{{\color[rgb]{1,0.5,0}\textsc{i}}}^{\prime},\boldsymbol{\Phi})

\displaystyle\propto p(z_{k}\,|\,\boldsymbol{\Phi})p(\Phi^{\prime}_{{\color[rgb]{1,0.5,0}\textsc{o}}}\,|\,z_{k},\boldsymbol{\Phi})p(\mathbf{d}_{{\color[rgb]{1,0.5,0}\textsc{u}}}^{\prime},{\boldsymbol{\kappa}}_{{\color[rgb]{1,0.5,0}\textsc{u}}}^{\prime}\,|\,z_{k},\boldsymbol{\Phi},\rho^{\prime})p({\boldsymbol{\kappa}}_{{\color[rgb]{1,0.5,0}\textsc{i}}}^{\prime}\,|\,z_{k},\boldsymbol{\Phi},\tau^{\prime})

\displaystyle\propto\bar{\theta}_{k}\prod_{m:\kappa^{\prime}={\color[rgb]{1,0.5,0}\textsc{o}}}\bar{\pi}_{m,k}f_{\mathcal{P}}(t^{\prime}_{m}\,|\,z_{k})\prod_{m:\kappa^{\prime}={\color[rgb]{1,0.5,0}\textsc{u}}}\bar{\pi}_{m,k}F_{\mathcal{P}}\left(\rho^{\prime}\,|\,z_{k}\right)\prod_{m:\kappa^{\prime}={\color[rgb]{1,0.5,0}\textsc{i}}}\left(1-\bar{\pi}_{m,k}F_{\mathcal{P}}\left(\tau^{\prime}\,|\,z_{k}\right)\right),

(15)

where
\bar{\theta}
,
\bar{\pi}_{m,k}
and
\bar{{\boldsymbol{\eta}}}
are posterior estimates of the model parameters. The posterior predictive distribution for
t^{\prime}
for an individual who is zero years old and has no conditions:

t^{\prime}_{m}\,|\,z_{k},\boldsymbol{\Phi}\quad\sim\quad\mathcal{P}\left(\nu^{\ast}_{m,k},{\boldsymbol{\chi}}^{\ast}_{m,k}\right)

(16)

with probability density function,
f_{\mathcal{P}}(t^{\prime}\,|\,\nu,{\boldsymbol{\chi}})
, and respective cumulative density and survival functions,
F_{\mathcal{P}}(\rho^{\prime})
and
S_{\mathcal{P}}(\tau^{\prime})
.

The second term in (
14
) is the probability density over the trajectory beyond
\tau^{\prime}
given the cluster, i.e.

p(\mathbf{d}_{{\color[rgb]{1,0.5,0}\textsc{i}}}^{\prime},\mathbf{t}_{{\color[rgb]{1,0.5,0}\textsc{i}}}^{\prime}\,|\,z_{k},{\boldsymbol{\kappa}}_{{\color[rgb]{1,0.5,0}\textsc{i}}}^{\prime},\boldsymbol{\Phi},\tau^{\prime})=\prod_{m:\kappa^{\prime}_{m}={\color[rgb]{1,0.5,0}\textsc{i}}}(1-\tilde{\pi}_{m,k})^{(1-d^{\prime}_{m})}(\tilde{\pi}_{m,k}f_{\mathcal{P}}(t_{m}^{\prime}\,|\,t_{m}^{\prime}>\tau^{\prime}))^{d^{\prime}_{m}}

(17)

where
f_{\mathcal{P}}(\cdot\,|\,\cdot>\tau)
denotes truncated distribution and

\displaystyle\tilde{\pi}_{m,k}=

\displaystyle p(d_{m}\,|\,z_{k},\kappa_{m}^{\prime}={{\color[rgb]{1,0.5,0}\textsc{i}}},\boldsymbol{\Phi},\tau^{\prime})\propto\bar{\pi}_{m,k}S_{\mathcal{P}}(\tau^{\prime}\,|\,\nu_{m,k}^{*},{\boldsymbol{\chi}}_{m,k}^{*})

is the posterior probability of the
m
-th LTC being active in cluster
k
conditional on the event that it has not happened before
\tau^{\prime}
.

3
A Simulated Example

To evaluate
ProMOTe
empirically, we apply it to simulated data with
200,000
individuals,
M=80
conditions, and
K=10
known clusters with each cluster containing between
3\%
and
15\%
of the trajectories. Within each cluster the presence of each of the
80
conditions was generated independently with probabilities
\pi_{m,k}
generated from a
\mathrm{Beta}\left(0.07,0.49\right)
distribution, resulting in an average of
10
conditions per trajectory. For each condition that was present in the cluster, we simulated the respective diagnosis ages using Gaussian random variables whose means and variances were generated from a
\mathcal{NIG}\left(50,0.3,5,300\right)
distribution, resulting in diagnosis ages with an average mean of
50
and an average standard deviation of
8.66
. For each individual, we simulated baseline age as
\rho^{(n)}\sim\mathcal{U}\left(20,60\right)
and data before these ages was left-censored. The current ages were set to be thirty years later, i.e.
\tau^{(n)}=\rho^{(n)}+30
. Finally we simulated i.i.d. Bernoulli random variables where
p\left(\iota^{(n)}=\textsc{{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}d}}\right)=0.8
to determine whether each trajectory ended at
\tau^{(n)}
or was right-censored. We split the simulated data such that
160,000
trajectories formed a training set used to fit the model and the remaining
40,000
trajectories were used as a test set to evaluate the model.

Given the simulated data we fit
ProMOTe
assuming a Gaussian distribution for diagnosis ages
t^{(n)}_{m}\,|\,d^{(n)}_{m}=1\sim\mathcal{N}(\mu_{m,k},\sigma_{m,k})

with the following prior distributions,
{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\sim\mathrm{Dir}\left(\mathbf{1}\right)
,
\pi_{m,k}\sim\mathrm{Beta}\left(1,1\right)
, and
\mu_{m,k},\sigma_{m,k}^{2}\sim\mathcal{NIG}\left(50,0.3,5,750\right)
.
Derivations of the VB updating equations for the Gaussian case are available in Appendix
E
. Here we choose hyperparameters that construct weakly informative priors. The hyperparameters can be adjusted to incorporate stronger prior information.

To assess the predictive performance we simulate current ages uniformly between
50
and
90
. We then predict forward from the test data using data from before these ages to predict the data afterwards. We compare these predictions to the test data using both accuracy and area under receiver operator curve (AUROC)
[
11
]
as metrics. We also consider the mean absolute error (MAE) between the predicted diagnosis ages when the condition was present in the test data. We stress that the following results present a best case scenario for
ProMOTe
, since the data generating process for the synthetic data is same as the model.

We first assess the model’s ability to recover the true clusters from the synthetic data. We observe that
ProMOTe
performs well, recovering the true clusters in most cases from the test data with the most probable cluster matching the true cluster in
92\%
of trajectories. The inaccuracies largely belonged to cluster 8 which was conflated with cluster 1 and cluster 5 which contain similar trajectories but are much larger. The model still assigned high probability to that cluster for those individuals and we observe an AUROC of
0.75
for that cluster, compared to AUROC close to
1
for the other clusters. Plots comparing the estimated risk profiles of all clusters to the true risk profiles are available in Appendix
G
.

Next we assess the model’s ability to predict which diseases will occur in the test trajectories.
ProMOTe
achieves very high accuracy when predicting disease presence with an overall accuracy of
89\%
and AUROC of
0.99
. This reaffirms the ability of
ProMOTe
to cluster probabilistically, since even in test cases where the true cluster is not recovered with certainty, it still assigns high probability to the true cluster to predict disease outcomes.
We further assess the diagnosis ages predicted by
ProMOTe
for diseases which occur in the test data.
Over the test data the MAE of the observed and predicted age at diagnosis is
8.2
years.

The model was applied to data from the UK Biobank. The UK Biobank is a large prospective population-based cohort study of more than

Participants consented to linkage to EHRs, including GP, hospital, cancer registry and death records. Evaluated on the withheld UK Biobank test data,

5
Discussion

We present a novel method,
ProMOTe
, for longitudinal clustering of LTCs based upon both their presence and absence and their respective age at diagnosis.
ProMOTe
can make both population and individual level inference, by finding clusters of trajectories and forecasting future individual trajectories as a predictive mixture of the underlying clusters. At the population level, the clusters can be used to inform genetic and socio-economic drivers while at the individual level, the expected future LTC trajectories can be used for clinical decision support (CDS).

A direct comparison of
ProMOTe
and contemporary methods is difficult since they are applied on different datasets. However,
in comparison with the ATM method
[
16
]
that also used UK Biobank data, we describe some differences. The ATM model was fitted on the trajectories of
282,957
individuals compared to
159,145
individuals used for
ProMOTe
, and with different disease encoding of the conditions.
ATM focuses on the low-rank representation of EHRs through clustering. This contrasts with the approach of
ProMOTe
which focuses on the integration of unreliable and incomplete EHR data for the clustering of individuals and the forecasting of future trajectories. Thus, we emphasize that the clusters are not directly comparable.

While the model introduces meaningful and useful inference we note that there are several limitations. The model makes several parametric choices when modelling the data. In particular, the parametric choice for the distribution of diagnosis ages can impact the model’s ability to make useful forecasts at the individual level. However, our results allow any parametric choice within the exponential family, which affords the model considerable flexibility where appropriate care is taken. Moreover, our model attempts to mitigate the unreliability and incompleteness of the data by introducing left- and right-censoring but these are subject to parametric choices and may not fully remedy the problem. Within a cluster our model assumes conditional independence of LTC onset times, and while this greatly improves over a fully independent structure, our model does not capture the full dependence within the data. We also emphasize that the model does not attempt to infer causality.

Future work will attempt to address the model limitations and further improve inference. For example, generalisation of the Dirichlet prior to a Dirichlet process prior will enable the model to automatically determine an appropriate number of clusters from the data
[
7
]
. Furthermore, allowing uncertainty arising from the age at diagnosis being different from actual disease onset that is not observed, will introduce considerable flexibility. Moreover, incorporating uncertainty about the time of death by potentially treating death as a condition where no other conditions can occur after may improve predictive performance. Other improvements to the method could include modelling the dependency between different LTC onset times within a cluster. Finally replicating
ProMOTe
on additional data will add more value.

Appendix D
Predictive distribution

We can write the predictive posterior for a new individual
\Delta^{\prime}=\{\textsc{{\color[rgb]{0,1,1}a}},0,0,\varnothing\}
who is age
0
and has no observations as:

\displaystyle p\left(z^{\prime},\mathbf{d}^{\prime},\mathbf{t}^{\prime}\,|\,\boldsymbol{\Phi}\right)

\displaystyle=\int p\left(z^{\prime},\mathbf{d}^{\prime},\mathbf{t}^{\prime}\,|\,{\boldsymbol{\gamma}},{\boldsymbol{\pi}},{\boldsymbol{\eta}}\right)p\left({\boldsymbol{\gamma}},{\boldsymbol{\pi}},{\boldsymbol{\eta}}\,|\,\boldsymbol{\Phi}\right)\textrm{d}{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\textrm{d}{\boldsymbol{\pi}}\textrm{d}{\boldsymbol{\eta}}

\displaystyle=\int p\left(z^{\prime}\,|\,{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\right)p\left({\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\,|\,\boldsymbol{\Phi}\right)\textrm{d}{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\int p\left(\mathbf{d}^{\prime}\,|\,{\boldsymbol{\pi}},z^{\prime}\right)p\left({\boldsymbol{\pi}}\,|\,\boldsymbol{\Phi}\right)\textrm{d}{\boldsymbol{\pi}}\int p\left(\mathbf{t}^{\prime}\,|\,{\boldsymbol{\eta}},z^{\prime},\mathbf{d}^{\prime}\right)p\left({\boldsymbol{\eta}}\,|\,\boldsymbol{\Phi}\right)\textrm{d}{\boldsymbol{\eta}}

\displaystyle=p\left(z^{\prime}\,|\,\boldsymbol{\Phi}\right)p\left(\mathbf{d}^{\prime}\,|\,\boldsymbol{\Phi},z^{\prime}\right)p\left(\mathbf{t}^{\prime}\,|\,\boldsymbol{\Phi},z^{\prime},\mathbf{d}^{\prime}\right)

(57)

Now, we can derive the probability that the individual belongs to each cluster:

\displaystyle p\left(z^{\prime}_{i}=1\,|\,\boldsymbol{\Phi}\right)

\displaystyle=\int p\left(z^{\prime}_{i}=1\,|\,{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\right)p\left({\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\,|\,\boldsymbol{\Phi}\right)\textrm{d}{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}

\displaystyle\propto\int\prod_{k=1}^{K}\left(\gamma_{k}^{z^{\prime}_{k}}\right)\frac{\Gamma\left(\sum_{k=1}^{K}\theta_{k}^{\ast}\right)}{\prod_{k=1}^{K}\Gamma\left(\theta_{k}^{\ast}\right)}\prod_{k=1}^{K}\left(\gamma_{k}^{\theta_{k}-1}\right)\textrm{d}{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}

\displaystyle\propto\frac{\Gamma\left(\sum_{k=1}^{K}\theta_{k}^{\ast}\right)}{\prod_{k=1}^{K}\Gamma\left(\theta_{k}^{\ast}\right)}\int\prod_{k=1}^{K}\left(\gamma_{k}^{\mathbf{1}\left(k=i\right)+\theta_{k}-1}\right)\textrm{d}{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}

\displaystyle\propto\frac{\Gamma\left(\sum_{k=1}^{K}\theta_{k}^{\ast}\right)}{\prod_{k=1}^{K}\Gamma\left(\theta_{k}^{\ast}\right)}\frac{\prod_{k=1}^{K}\Gamma\left(\mathbf{1}\left(k=i\right)+\theta_{k}^{\ast}\right)}{\Gamma\left(1+\sum_{k=1}^{K}\theta_{k}^{\ast}\right)}

\displaystyle\propto\frac{\theta_{i}^{\ast}}{\sum_{k=1}^{K}\theta_{k}^{\ast}}

(58)

where we use
\Gamma\left(n+1\right)=n\Gamma\left(n\right)
to simplify the second last line and we can identify the distribution as

z^{\prime}\quad\sim\quad\mathrm{Mult}\left(1,\bar{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}\right)

(59)

where

\bar{\theta}_{i}=\frac{\theta_{i}^{\ast}}{\sum_{k=1}^{K}\theta_{k}^{\ast}}

(60)

Next we have that the probability that a condition is present in the individual’s trajectory given that they belong to a particular cluster is:

\displaystyle p\left(d^{\prime}_{m}=1\,|\,\boldsymbol{\Phi},z^{\prime}_{k}=1\right)

\displaystyle=\int p\left(d^{\prime}_{m}\,|\,\pi_{m,k},z^{\prime}_{k}=1\right)p\left(\pi_{m,k}\,|\,\boldsymbol{\Phi}\right)\textrm{d}\pi_{m,k}

\displaystyle=\int\pi_{m,k}\pi_{m,k}^{a_{m,k}^{\ast}-1}\left(1-\pi_{m,k}\right)^{b_{m,k}^{\ast}-1}\textrm{d}\pi_{m,k}

\displaystyle=\frac{a_{m,k}^{\ast}}{a_{m,k}^{\ast}+b_{m,k}^{\ast}}

(61)

from which we identify

d^{\prime}_{m}\,|\,z^{\prime}_{k}=1\quad\sim\quad\textrm{Bern}\left(\bar{\pi}_{m,k}\right)

(62)

where

\bar{\pi}_{m,k}=\frac{a_{m,k}^{\ast}}{a_{m,k}^{\ast}+b_{m,k}^{\ast}}

(63)

Finally we have the predictive posterior density function for the age at diagnosis of a condition given that the condition is present and the individual belongs to a particular cluster:

\displaystyle f_{\mathcal{P}}(t_{m}^{\prime}\,|\,\nu_{m,k}^{*},{\boldsymbol{\chi}}_{m,k}^{*})

\displaystyle=p\left(t^{\prime}_{m}\,|\,\boldsymbol{\Phi},z^{\prime}_{k}=1,d^{\prime}_{m}=1\right)

\displaystyle=\int p\left(t^{\prime}_{m}\,|\,{\boldsymbol{\eta}}_{m,k},z^{\prime}_{k}=1,d^{\prime}_{m}=1\right)p\left({\boldsymbol{\eta}}_{m,k}\,|\,\boldsymbol{\Phi}\right)\textrm{d}{\boldsymbol{\eta}}_{m,k}

\displaystyle=\int g\left({\boldsymbol{\eta}}_{m,k}\right)h\left(t^{\prime}_{m}\right)\exp\left({\boldsymbol{\eta}}_{m,k}^{\top}\mathbf{T}\left(t_{m}^{\prime}\right)\right)g\left({\boldsymbol{\eta}}_{m,k}\right)^{\nu_{m,k}^{\ast}}f\left(\nu_{m,k}^{\ast},{\boldsymbol{\chi}}_{m,k}^{\ast}\right)\exp\left({\boldsymbol{\eta}}_{m,k}^{\top}{\boldsymbol{\chi}}_{m,k}^{\ast}\right)\textrm{d}{\boldsymbol{\eta}}_{m,k}

\displaystyle=h\left(t^{\prime}_{m}\right)f\left(\nu_{m,k}^{\ast},{\boldsymbol{\chi}}_{m,k}^{\ast}\right)\int g\left({\boldsymbol{\eta}}_{m,k}\right)^{\nu_{m,k}^{\ast}+1}\exp\left({\boldsymbol{\eta}}_{m,k}^{\top}\left(\mathbf{T}\left(t_{m}^{\prime}\right)+{\boldsymbol{\chi}}_{m,k}^{\ast}\right)\right)\textrm{d}{\boldsymbol{\eta}}_{m,k}

\displaystyle=\frac{h\left(t^{\prime}_{m}\right)f\left(\nu_{m,k}^{\ast},{\boldsymbol{\chi}}_{m,k}^{\ast}\right)}{f\left(\nu_{m,k}^{\ast}+1,{\boldsymbol{\chi}}_{m,k}^{\ast}+\mathbf{T}\left(t_{m}^{\prime}\right)\right)}

(64)

From this probability density function we also find the cumulative and complimentary cumulative predictive density functions for
t_{m}^{\prime}
given disease presence
d_{m}^{\prime}=1
and a cluster
z_{k}^{\prime}=1.

\displaystyle F_{\mathcal{P}}(\rho^{\prime}\,|\,\nu_{m,k}^{*},{\boldsymbol{\chi}}_{m,k}^{*})

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\rho^{\prime}}f_{\mathcal{P}}(t_{m}^{\prime}\,|\,\nu_{m,k}^{*},{\boldsymbol{\chi}}_{m,k}^{*})\textrm{d}t_{m}^{\prime}

(65)

\displaystyle S_{\mathcal{P}}(\tau^{\prime}\,|\,\nu_{m,k}^{*},{\boldsymbol{\chi}}_{m,k}^{*})

\displaystyle=1-F_{\mathcal{P}}(\tau^{\prime}\,|\,\nu_{m,k}^{*},{\boldsymbol{\chi}}_{m,k}^{*})

(66)

Now we consider the more typical case of a new individual

\Delta^{\prime}=\{\textsc{{\color[rgb]{0,1,1}a}},\rho^{\prime},\tau^{\prime},\Phi^{\prime}\}
who is age
\tau^{\prime}
and who can also be represented as

\Phi_{{\color[rgb]{1,0.5,0}\ast}}^{\prime}=\left(\mathbf{d}_{{\color[rgb]{1,0.5,0}\ast}}^{\prime},\mathbf{t}_{{\color[rgb]{1,0.5,0}\ast}}^{\prime},{\boldsymbol{\kappa}}_{{\color[rgb]{1,0.5,0}\ast}}^{\prime}\right)=\left(d^{\prime}_{m},t^{\prime}_{m},\kappa^{\prime}_{m}\right)_{m:\kappa_{m}^{\prime}={{\color[rgb]{1,0.5,0}\ast}}}

(67)

where
{\color[rgb]{1,0.5,0}\ast}={\color[rgb]{1,0.5,0}\textsc{o}},{\color[rgb]{1,0.5,0}\textsc{u}}
or
i
.
For this new individual we want to derive the predictive posterior over the missing quantities
\mathbf{d}_{{\color[rgb]{1,0.5,0}\textsc{i}}}^{\prime}
and
\mathbf{t}_{{\color[rgb]{1,0.5,0}\textsc{i}}}^{\prime}
given the available data:

p\left(\mathbf{d}_{{\color[rgb]{1,0.5,0}\textsc{i}}}^{\prime},\mathbf{t}_{{\color[rgb]{1,0.5,0}\textsc{i}}}^{\prime}\,|\,\Phi_{{\color[rgb]{1,0.5,0}\textsc{o}}}^{\prime},\mathbf{d}_{{\color[rgb]{1,0.5,0}\textsc{u}}}^{\prime},{\boldsymbol{\kappa}}_{{\color[rgb]{1,0.5,0}\textsc{u}}}^{\prime},{\boldsymbol{\kappa}}_{{\color[rgb]{1,0.5,0}\textsc{i}}}^{\prime},\boldsymbol{\Phi},\rho^{\prime},\tau^{\prime}\right)=\sum_{k=1}^{K}p(\mathbf{d}_{{\color[rgb]{1,0.5,0}\textsc{i}}}^{\prime},\mathbf{t}_{{\color[rgb]{1,0.5,0}\textsc{i}}}^{\prime}\,|\,z_{k}^{\prime},{\boldsymbol{\kappa}}_{{\color[rgb]{1,0.5,0}\textsc{i}}}^{\prime},\boldsymbol{\Phi},\tau^{\prime})p(z_{k}^{\prime}\,|\,\Phi^{\prime}_{{\color[rgb]{1,0.5,0}\textsc{o}}},\mathbf{d}_{{\color[rgb]{1,0.5,0}\textsc{u}}}^{\prime},{\boldsymbol{\kappa}}_{{\color[rgb]{1,0.5,0}\textsc{u}}}^{\prime},{\boldsymbol{\kappa}}_{{\color[rgb]{1,0.5,0}\textsc{i}}}^{\prime},\boldsymbol{\Phi},\rho^{\prime},\tau^{\prime})

(68)

First we have the probability that the individual belongs to a particular cluster:

\displaystyle\tilde{\phi}_{k}

\displaystyle=p(z_{k}\,|\,\Phi^{\prime}_{{\color[rgb]{1,0.5,0}\textsc{o}}},\mathbf{d}_{{\color[rgb]{1,0.5,0}\textsc{u}}}^{\prime},{\boldsymbol{\kappa}}_{{\color[rgb]{1,0.5,0}\textsc{u}}}^{\prime},{\boldsymbol{\kappa}}_{{\color[rgb]{1,0.5,0}\textsc{i}}}^{\prime},\boldsymbol{\Phi},\rho^{\prime},\tau^{\prime})

\displaystyle\propto p\left(z_{k}^{\prime}\,|\,\boldsymbol{\Phi}\right)p\left(\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{{\color[rgb]{1,0.5,0}\textsc{o}}}^{\prime}\,|\,z_{k}^{\prime},\boldsymbol{\Phi}\right)p\left(\mathbf{d}_{{\color[rgb]{1,0.5,0}\textsc{u}}}^{\prime},{\boldsymbol{\kappa}}_{{\color[rgb]{1,0.5,0}\textsc{u}}}^{\prime}\,|\,z_{k}^{\prime},\boldsymbol{\Phi},\rho^{\prime}\right)p\left({\boldsymbol{\kappa}}_{{\color[rgb]{1,0.5,0}\textsc{i}}}^{\prime}\,|\,z_{k}^{\prime},\boldsymbol{\Phi},\tau^{\prime}\right)

\displaystyle\propto\bar{\theta}_{k}\prod_{m:\kappa^{\prime}={\color[rgb]{1,0.5,0}\textsc{o}}}\bar{\pi}_{m,k}f_{\mathcal{P}}(t^{\prime}_{m})\prod_{m:\kappa^{\prime}={\color[rgb]{1,0.5,0}\textsc{u}}}\bar{\pi}_{m,k}F_{\mathcal{P}}\left(\rho^{\prime}\right)\prod_{m:\kappa^{\prime}={\color[rgb]{1,0.5,0}\textsc{i}}}\left(1-\bar{\pi}_{m,k}F_{\mathcal{P}}\left(\tau^{\prime}\right)\right)

(69)

which is the second term in the desired predictive posterior. Next we have the probability that a disease is present in the trajectory given that it has not been observed thus far:

\displaystyle\tilde{\pi}_{m,k}

\displaystyle=p\left(d^{\prime}_{m}=1\,|\,z^{\prime}_{k}=1,\kappa_{m}^{\prime}={{\color[rgb]{1,0.5,0}\textsc{i}}},\boldsymbol{\Phi},\tau^{\prime}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{p\left(\kappa_{m}^{\prime}={{\color[rgb]{1,0.5,0}\textsc{i}}},d^{\prime}_{m}=1\,|\,\boldsymbol{\Phi},z^{\prime}_{k}=1,\tau^{\prime}\right)}{p\left(\kappa_{m}^{\prime}={{\color[rgb]{1,0.5,0}\textsc{i}}}\,|\,\boldsymbol{\Phi},z^{\prime}_{k}=1,\tau^{\prime}\right)}

\displaystyle\propto p\left(d^{\prime}_{m}=1\,|\,\boldsymbol{\Phi},z^{\prime}_{k}=1\right)p\left(\kappa_{m}^{\prime}={{\color[rgb]{1,0.5,0}\textsc{i}}}\,|\,\boldsymbol{\Phi},z^{\prime}_{k}=1,d^{\prime}_{m}=1,\tau^{\prime}\right)

\displaystyle\propto\bar{\pi}_{m,k}S_{\mathcal{P}}(\tau^{\prime}\,|\,\nu_{m,k}^{*},{\boldsymbol{\chi}}_{m,k}^{*})

(70)

Now we can derive the first term in the predictive posterior:

\displaystyle p\left(\mathbf{d}_{{\color[rgb]{1,0.5,0}\textsc{i}}}^{\prime},\mathbf{t}_{{\color[rgb]{1,0.5,0}\textsc{i}}}^{\prime}\,|\,z_{k}^{\prime},{\boldsymbol{\kappa}}_{{\color[rgb]{1,0.5,0}\textsc{i}}}^{\prime},\boldsymbol{\Phi},\tau^{\prime}\right)

\displaystyle=\prod_{m:\kappa_{m}^{\prime}={\color[rgb]{1,0.5,0}\textsc{i}}}\left(p\left(t_{m}^{\prime}\,|\,z_{k}^{\prime},\kappa_{m}^{\prime},\boldsymbol{\Phi},\tau^{\prime},d_{m}^{\prime}=1\right)p\left(d_{m}^{\prime}=1\,|\,z_{k}^{\prime},\kappa_{m}^{\prime},\boldsymbol{\Phi},\tau^{\prime}\right)\right)^{d_{m}^{\prime}}\left(p\left(d_{m}^{\prime}=0\,|\,z_{k}^{\prime},\kappa_{m}^{\prime},\boldsymbol{\Phi},\tau^{\prime}\right)\right)^{1-d_{m}^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=\prod_{m:\kappa_{m}^{\prime}={\color[rgb]{1,0.5,0}\textsc{i}}}\left(f_{\mathcal{P}}(t_{m}^{\prime}\,|\,t_{m}^{\prime}>\tau^{\prime})\tilde{\pi}_{m,k}\right)^{d_{m}^{\prime}}\left(1-\tilde{\pi}_{m,k}\right)^{1-d_{m}^{\prime}}

(71)

Finally we can construct the predictive posterior density function:

\displaystyle p\left(\mathbf{d}_{{\color[rgb]{1,0.5,0}\textsc{i}}}^{\prime},\mathbf{t}_{{\color[rgb]{1,0.5,0}\textsc{i}}}^{\prime}\,|\,\Phi_{{\color[rgb]{1,0.5,0}\textsc{o}}}^{\prime},\mathbf{d}_{{\color[rgb]{1,0.5,0}\textsc{u}}}^{\prime},{\boldsymbol{\kappa}}_{{\color[rgb]{1,0.5,0}\textsc{u}}}^{\prime},{\boldsymbol{\kappa}}_{{\color[rgb]{1,0.5,0}\textsc{i}}}^{\prime},\boldsymbol{\Phi},\rho^{\prime},\tau^{\prime}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{K}p(\mathbf{d}_{{\color[rgb]{1,0.5,0}\textsc{i}}}^{\prime},\mathbf{t}_{{\color[rgb]{1,0.5,0}\textsc{i}}}^{\prime}\,|\,z_{k}^{\prime},{\boldsymbol{\kappa}}_{{\color[rgb]{1,0.5,0}\textsc{i}}}^{\prime},\boldsymbol{\Phi},\tau^{\prime})p(z_{k}^{\prime}\,|\,\Phi^{\prime}_{{\color[rgb]{1,0.5,0}\textsc{o}}},\mathbf{d}_{{\color[rgb]{1,0.5,0}\textsc{u}}}^{\prime},{\boldsymbol{\kappa}}_{{\color[rgb]{1,0.5,0}\textsc{u}}}^{\prime},{\boldsymbol{\kappa}}_{{\color[rgb]{1,0.5,0}\textsc{i}}}^{\prime},\boldsymbol{\Phi},\rho^{\prime},\tau^{\prime})

\displaystyle\propto\sum_{k=1}^{K}\phi_{k}\prod_{m:\kappa_{m}^{\prime}={\color[rgb]{1,0.5,0}\textsc{i}}}\left(f_{\mathcal{P}}(t_{m}^{\prime}\,|\,t_{m}^{\prime}>\tau^{\prime})\tilde{\pi}_{m,k}\right)^{d_{m}^{\prime}}\left(1-\tilde{\pi}_{m,k}\right)^{1-d_{m}^{\prime}}

(72)

Appendix E
Gaussian Variational Bayes Updating Equations

For the Gaussian case with mean
\mu_{m,k}
and variance
\sigma_{m,k}^{2}
we have natural parameters
\eta_{m,k,1}=\frac{\mu_{m,k}}{\sigma_{m,k}^{2}}
and
\eta_{m,k,2}=-\frac{1}{2\sigma_{m,k}^{2}}
and sufficient statistics
T_{m,1}=t_{m}
and
T_{m,2}=t_{m}^{2}.
We also have that the exponential form of the Gaussian probability density function can be written as:

\displaystyle f_{\mathrm{ExpFam}}(t_{m}\,|\,{\boldsymbol{\eta}}_{m,k})=g\left({\boldsymbol{\eta}}_{m,k}\right)h\left(t_{m}\right)\exp\left({\boldsymbol{\eta}}_{m,k}^{\top}\mathbf{T}_{m}\right)

(73)

where

\displaystyle g\left({\boldsymbol{\eta}}_{m,k}\right)

\displaystyle=\exp\left(-\frac{\eta_{m,k,1}^{2}}{4\eta_{m,k,2}}-\frac{1}{2}\log\left(-2\eta_{m,k,2}\right)\right)

\displaystyle=\exp\left(\frac{\mu_{m,k}^{2}}{2\sigma_{m,k}^{2}}-\frac{1}{2}\log\left(\frac{1}{\sigma_{m,k}^{2}}\right)\right)

(74)

\displaystyle h\left(t_{m}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}

(75)

\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{\eta}}_{m,k}^{\top}\mathbf{T}_{m}

\displaystyle=\eta_{m,k,1}T_{m,1}^{(n)}+\eta_{m,k,2}T_{m,2}^{(n)}

\displaystyle=\frac{\mu_{m,k}^{2}t_{m}^{(n)}}{\sigma_{m,k}^{2}}-\frac{\left(t_{m}^{(n)}\right)^{2}}{2\sigma_{m,k}^{2}}

(76)

We further have that the Normal-Inverse gamma conjugate posterior on
\mu_{m,k}
and
\sigma_{m,k}^{2}
has hyperparameters
u_{m,k}^{\ast},v_{m,k}^{\ast},\alpha_{m,k}^{\ast},
and
\beta_{m,k}^{\ast}.
The normal-inverse gamma distribution also belongs to the exponential family with of natural statistics
\log\left(\frac{1}{\sigma_{m,k}^{2}}\right),\frac{1}{\sigma_{m,k}^{2}},\frac{\mu_{m,k}}{\sigma_{m,k}^{2}},\frac{\mu_{m,k}^{2}}{\sigma_{m,k}^{2}}.
The expectations of these natural statistics can be found using the moment generating function
[
26
]
and are as follows:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left(\log\left(\frac{1}{\sigma_{m,k}^{2}}\right)\right)

\displaystyle=\psi\left(\alpha_{m,k}^{\ast}\right)-\log\left(\beta_{m,k}^{\ast}\right)

(77)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left(\frac{1}{\sigma_{m,k}^{2}}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{\alpha_{m,k}^{\ast}}{\beta_{m,k}^{\ast}}

(78)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left(\frac{\mu_{m,k}}{\sigma_{m,k}^{2}}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{u_{m,k}^{\ast}\alpha_{m,k}^{\ast}}{\beta_{m,k}^{\ast}}

(79)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left(\frac{\mu_{m,k}^{2}}{\sigma_{m,k}^{2}}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{\left(u_{m,k}^{\ast}\right)^{2}\alpha_{m,k}^{\ast}}{v_{m,k}^{\ast}\beta_{m,k}^{\ast}}

(80)

We have the following expectations:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left(\log\left(g\left({\boldsymbol{\eta}}_{m,k}\right)\right)\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left(\frac{\mu_{m,k}^{2}}{2\sigma_{m,k}^{2}}+\frac{1}{2}\log\left(\sigma_{m,k}^{2}\right)\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}\left(\frac{\mu_{m,k}^{2}}{\sigma_{m,k}^{2}}\right)-\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}\left(\log\left(\frac{1}{\sigma_{m,k}^{2}}\right)\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{u_{m,k}^{2}\alpha_{m,k}}{2v_{m,k}\beta_{m,k}}-\frac{1}{2}\left(\psi\left(\alpha_{m,k}\right)-\log\left(\beta_{m,k}\right)\right)

(82)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left(\eta_{m,k,1}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{u_{m,k}^{\ast}\alpha_{m,k}^{\ast}}{\beta_{m,k}^{\ast}}

(83)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left(\eta_{m,k,2}\right)

\displaystyle=-\frac{\alpha_{m,k}^{\ast}}{2\beta_{m,k}^{\ast}}

(84)

We write
f_{Z},F_{Z}
and
S_{Z}
for the probability density, cumulative density and survival functions of the standard normal distribution. We transform the Gaussian parameters to the mean
\mu_{m}^{\ast}=\frac{\eta_{m,1}^{\ast}}{2\eta_{m,2}^{\ast}}
and standard deviation
\sigma_{m}^{\ast}=\sqrt{-\frac{1}{2\eta_{m,2}^{\ast}}}
. We transform the truncation thresholds such that
\dot{\tau}_{m}=\frac{\tau-\mu_{m}^{\ast}}{\sigma_{m}^{\ast}}
and
\dot{\rho}_{m}=\frac{\rho-\mu_{m}^{\ast}}{\sigma_{m}^{\ast}}
. Then we can write the truncated expectations as perturbations of the untruncated moments
[
17
]
:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{l}\left(T_{m,1}\right)=\mathbb{E}_{l}\left(t_{m}\right)

\displaystyle=\mu_{m}^{\ast}+\sigma_{m}^{\ast}\frac{f_{Z}\left(\dot{\tau}_{m}\right)}{S_{Z}\left(\dot{\tau}_{m}\right)}

(86)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{r}\left(T_{m,1}\right)=\mathbb{E}_{l}\left(t_{m}\right)

\displaystyle=\mu_{m}^{\ast}-\sigma_{m}^{\ast}\frac{f_{Z}\left(\dot{\rho}_{m}\right)}{F_{Z}\left(\dot{\rho}_{m}\right)}

(87)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{l}\left(T_{m,2}\right)=\mathbb{E}_{l}\left(t_{m}^{2}\right)

\displaystyle=\left(\sigma_{m}^{\ast}\right)^{2}\left(1+\frac{\dot{\tau}_{m}f_{Z}\left(\dot{\tau}_{m}\right)}{S_{Z}\left(\dot{\tau}_{m}\right)}-\left(\frac{f_{Z}\left(\dot{\tau}_{m}\right)}{S_{Z}\left(\dot{\tau}_{m}\right)}\right)^{2}\right)+\left(\mu_{m}^{\ast}+\sigma_{m}^{\ast}\frac{f_{Z}\left(\dot{\tau}_{m}\right)}{S_{Z}\left(\dot{\tau}_{m}\right)}\right)^{2}

(88)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{r}\left(T_{m,2}\right)=\mathbb{E}_{r}\left(t_{m}^{2}\right)

\displaystyle=\left(\sigma_{m}^{\ast}\right)^{2}\left(1-\frac{\dot{\rho}_{m}f_{Z}\left(\dot{\rho}_{m}\right)}{F_{Z}\left(\dot{\rho}_{m}\right)}-\left(\frac{f_{Z}\left(\dot{\rho}_{m}\right)}{F_{Z}\left(\dot{\rho}_{m}\right)}\right)^{2}\right)+\left(\mu_{m}^{\ast}-\sigma_{m}^{\ast}\frac{f_{Z}\left(\dot{\rho}_{m}\right)}{F_{Z}\left(\dot{\rho}_{m}\right)}\right)^{2}

(89)

Finally, since
h\left(t\right)
is a constant, we simply have:

\mathbb{E}_{l}\left(\log\left(h\left(t_{m}\right)\right)\right)=\mathbb{E}_{r}\left(\log\left(h\left(t_{m}\right)\right)\right)=\log\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\right)

(90)
