New insights into the mechanism of microvascular rarefaction via endothelial-mesenchymal transition in steroid-induced osteonecrosis of femoral head
Authors: Xiaoqiang Yang, Peng Yang, Jiaru Liao, Fan Yang, Mincong He, Ziqi Li, Qiushi Wei, Wei He
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study aimed to investigate the potential role of endothelial-to-mesenchymal transition (EndMT) in steroid-induced osteonecrosis of the femoral head (SONFH), evaluate its association with microvascular rarefaction and tissue damage, and preliminarily explore whether glucocorticoid-induced oxidative stress may be involved in this process. A combination of single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq), in vitro experiments, and animal models were employed. scRNA-seq was performed on SONFH cystic lesions to identify endothelial subpopulations and trace developmental trajectories. In vitro, human umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVECs) were treated with methylprednisolone and antioxidant (N-acetylcysteine) intervention to assess EndMT features and endothelial function. In vivo, a glucocorticoid-induced SONFH mouse model was established with or without antioxidant intervention to evaluate changes in microvascular density, EndMT occurrence, and oxidative stress levels. scRNA-seq analysis revealed endothelial subpopulations with EndMT-like features in the cystic areas of SONFH. In vitro experiments demonstrated that glucocorticoid treatment downregulated endothelial markers and upregulated mesenchymal markers in HUVECs, accompanied by impaired angiogenic function, and antioxidant therapy improves these conditions. In vivo, glucocorticoid administration led to significant microvascular rarefaction and increased EndMT marker expression in the femoral head, along with elevated oxidative stress; these changes were partially alleviated by antioxidant treatment. This study is the first to identify a potential involvement of EndMT in the pathogenesis of SONFH, highlighting its close association with microvascular rarefaction. Targeting EndMT and its upstream regulators, such as oxidative stress, may offer novel strategies for the early intervention and treatment of SONFH.Keywords: EndMT, Microvascular rarefaction, SONFHSubject terms: Cell biology, Diseases, Pathogenesis

Introduction

Steroid-induced osteonecrosis of femoral head (SONFH) is a bone damaging disease caused by prolonged use of glucocorticoids, primarily characterized by necrosis of the femoral head, bone collapse, and hip joint dysfunction1,2. The femoral head is a crucial load-bearing structure in the human body, with a unique anatomical structure that provides it with distinctive blood supply characteristics3. Due to the limited collateral circulation in the femoral head, any disruption to blood flow can easily result in local ischemia, necrosis, and bone collapse. Therefore, vascular damage, particularly the destruction of the microvascular network, is a significant pathological feature of femoral head necrosis4. Although various mechanisms of SONFH have been revealed in prior research, the precise pathophysiological mechanisms remain incompletely understood, especially with regard to the mechanisms of vascular injury.

In recent years, increasing attention has been focused on the role of microvascular damage in SONFH5–7. Microvascular rarefaction refers to the reduction in the density of the microvascular network, a decrease in the number of blood vessels, and restricted blood flow8,9. This phenomenon is commonly observed in various pathological conditions, such as diabetes10,11, hypertension12, atherosclerosis13, and aging. Microvascular rarefaction not only exacerbates tissue hypoxia and metabolic disturbances but may also promote bone necrosis by impairing bone metabolism and repair functions14. A substantial body of research8,15,16 suggests that microvascular rarefaction is closely linked to endothelial-mesenchymal transition (EndMT), a process that plays an important role in the development of numerous diseases. In addition, in the study of SONFH, we used single-cell RNA sequencing(scRNA-seq) to explore the cell types in cystic in SONFH since the cystic have an unusual repair response as we observed, and also found some unusual endothelial cell phenotypes, which have a significant impact on the prognosis of femoral head necrosis.

EndMT is a cellular trans-differentiation process in which vascular endothelial cells lose their endothelial characteristics and acquire features of mesenchymal cells, such as fibroblasts or smooth muscle cells17,18. EndMT has been reported in various pathological conditions, including cardiovascular diseases19, cancer metastasis20, and fibrosis21. Multiple signaling cascades, including TGF-β/Smad, Notch, Wnt/β-catenin and other protein molecule like CD4522, have been implicated in regulating EndMT under pathological stress. These pathways are often activated by oxidative stimuli, linking redox imbalance to vascular remodeling and fibrosis. However, its involvement in the reconstruction of the femoral head vascular network and in diseases related to bone metabolism remains underexplored. The occurrence of EndMT may exacerbate ischemic bone damage and marrow adiposity. Nonetheless, whether glucocorticoids induce microvascular rarefaction in the femoral head via EndMT remains inadequately verified.

Oxidative stress is considered one of the key factors in glucocorticoid-induced bone damage and antioxidant therapy can relieve the damage caused by glucocorticoids23,24. Prolonged use of glucocorticoids can lead to the accumulation of reactive oxygen species, thereby inducing damage to endothelial and other cells25. Studies26–28 have shown that oxidative stress is closely associated with the occurrence of EndMT.

While several studies have investigated the mechanisms underlying SONFH, the specific mechanisms by which glucocorticoids induce microvascular rarefaction through oxidative stress and EndMT remain unclear. Therefore, the aim of this study is to explore whether oxidative stress induced by glucocorticoids triggers EndMT, contributing to microvascular rarefaction and promoting femoral head necrosis.

Through this study, we aim to elucidate the pathological mechanisms of microvascular rarefaction induced by glucocorticoids, providing new theoretical foundations and experimental data to support early diagnosis and therapeutic strategies for SONFH.

Materials and methods

Single-cell RNA sequencing

Process of tissue samples

In this study, we collected a total of four clinical samples from female patients aged 39, 43, 45, and 49 years. All patients had a documented history of long-term glucocorticoid therapy for systemic lupus erythematosus and were clinically diagnosed with SONFH with cystic at ARCO stage III. All patients subsequently underwent total hip arthroplasty, from which the femoral head specimens were obtained for scRNA-seq. All procedures were performed in compliance with relevant laws and institutional guidelines and have been approved by the institutional ethics review board at the third Affiliated Hospital of Guangzhou University of Chinese Medicine (PJ-KY-20220420-013, 2022-04-20). The privacy rights of human subjects have been observed and that informed consent was obtained for experimentation with human subject.

The tissue was dissociated into a single-cell suspension using mechanical and enzymatic dissociation methods. We prepared four independent single-cell suspensions from four SONFH patients and one suspension per patient (libraries prepared separately and integrated with batch correction during analysis). Surgically resected cystic tissues were trimmed in ice-cold PBS to remove clots and fat and minced to ~ 1 mm3 fragments. If trabecular bone fragments were present, an optional mild pre-treatment with 0.5 mM EDTA for 10 min (RT, gentle inversion) was used to facilitate dissociation. Tissues were digested in HBSS + 0.04% BSA containing Collagenase II 2.0 mg/mL, Dispase II 1.0 mg/mL, and DNase I 100 U/mL at 37 °C for 30–45 min with gentle agitation (~ 120 rpm), with brief trituration every 10 min. Digestion was quenched with DMEM + 10% FBS, followed by sequential filtration through 70 µm and 40 µm strainers. Red blood cells were lysed with ACK buffer for 1–2 min, and cells were washed 2–3 times with PBS + 0.04% BSA. When viability was suboptimal or debris was excessive, a commercial dead-cell removal kit was used as needed.

Cell counts were obtained using Trypan Blue or AO/PI. Post-dissociation viability typically ranged from 85 to 92% (median ~ 88%); samples with viability < 80% were not taken forward for library preparation. Cells were resuspended to ~ 1,000 cells/µL in 0.04% BSA/PBS and loaded following the 10 × Genomics 3’ v3.1 workflow, targeting 8000–10,000 cells per sample.

During the single cell capture and library construction process, cells were distributed into individual microreactor chambers via microfluidic chips, followed by cell lysis, mRNA capture, reverse transcription, and amplification, culminating in the generation of sequencing libraries. The library quality was assessed using Qubit and Agilent chips to ensure that the library concentration and fragment length met the required standards. Sequencing was conducted on the Illumina NovaSeq platform. All raw data have been transferred to the GEO (accession numbers: GSE290411).

Cell annotation

Data normalization and batch effect removal were performed using seurat software. Principal component analysis (PCA) was applied to reduce the dimensionality of the data. The first 20 principal components were selected based on the PCA results, followed by further visualization of the cell population distribution using t-SNE and UMAP. Annotation of each cell population was carried out based on known marker genes, in combination with the cellmarker database and literature, to identify the types of each cell cluster.

Pseudotime analysis

To explore the developmental processes and state transitions of different cell populations in the sample, pseudotime analysis was performed. Monocle 3 was used to perform pseudotime analysis on endothelial cells. By selecting variant genes and constructing developmental trajectories, cells were ordered along the developmental timeline. Dynamic changes in each cell type during development were analyzed to uncover key genes and their roles in the developmental process.

Immunofluorescence

Samples of the femoral head of patients who required total hip arthroplasty due to SONFH were collected, and the area that had not yet been completely necrotic was selected for tissue cutting, and the bone tissue in this area was made into paraffin sections for immunofluorescence staining.

Paraffin-embedded femoral head tissue Sects. (5 μm thick) were deparaffinized in xylene and rehydrated through a graded ethanol series. Antigen retrieval was performed in citrate buffer (pH 6.0) using a microwave for 15 min, followed by cooling to room temperature. Sections were then washed with PBS and incubated with 0.3% Triton X-100 for 15 min to increase permeability. After blocking with 5% bovine serum albumin (BSA) for 1 h at room temperature to reduce nonspecific binding, the sections were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against CD31(Affinity, #BF0611), S100A4(Affinity, #DF6516), and COL1A1(Affinity, #AF7001). The following day, sections were washed three times with PBS and incubated with corresponding fluorescent secondary antibodies (FITC and CY3) for 1 h in the dark. Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI for 10 min, and the slides were mounted with an anti-fade mounting medium. Images were captured using a confocal laser scanning microscope.

EndMT in Vitro

Steroid-Induced EndMT in human umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVECs)

To more comprehensively verify whether glucocorticoids induce EndMT, HUVECs were treated with methylprednisolone. HUVECs was purchased from Shanghai Zhong Qiao Xin Zhou Biotechnology (DFSC-EC-01) .

HUVECs were cultured in an incubator at 37 °C with 5% CO2, and the methylprednisolone (0.1 mM) group was treated for 7 days (GCs group). The control group received no treatment. Western blot and PCR were used to detect endothelial cell markers and EndMT markers in HUVECs from both the control and methylprednisolone groups.

To investigate the temporal dynamics of EndMT, HUVECs were divided into three groups: control group (Ctrl), GCs-1 group (intervention for 3 days), GCs-2 group (intervention for 7 days). Following the interventions, cells were harvested using 0.25% trypsin–EDTA, washed with PBS, and resuspended in staining buffer. Cells were incubated with the following primary antibodies for 30 min at 4 °C in the dark: Anti-CD31-APC (endothelial marker, [BD Pharmingen, 564089]), Anti-S100A4-PE (mesenchymal marker, [Biolegend, 370010]). After staining, cells were washed, resuspended in PBS, and analyzed using a BD FACSCanto II flow cytometer (BD Biosciences). Data were processed and analyzed with FlowJo software (Version X.0.7, Tree Star Inc.).

To further evaluate whether glucocorticoid treatment leads to functional alterations of endothelial cells, we performed migration assay and matrigel tube formation assays to assess the migratory and angiogenic capacity of HUVECs.

Migration assay

HUVECs were seeded into 6-well plates and cultured until reaching over 90% confluence. A uniform scratch was made in the cell monolayer using a sterile 200μL pipette tip. Detached cells were gently washed away with PBS, and serum-free medium containing 0.1 mM methylprednisolone (GCs group) was added. Images of the scratched area were taken at 0 h and 24 h using a phase-contrast microscope. The migration distance was measured using ImageJ software to evaluate the migration rate.

Tube formation assay

Matrigel (Corning, 356234) was thawed on ice and coated (50μL/well) onto pre-chilled 96-well plates, then polymerized at 37 °C for 30 min. Treated HUVECs from the Ctrl and GCs groups (0.1 mM methylprednisolone, 5 days) were resuspended and seeded at a density of 2 × 104 cells/well onto the matrigel surface. After 6 h of incubation, tubular structures were observed under an inverted microscope, then images of the 12th and 24th hours were recorded. Tube formation was quantified by ImageJ (Angiogenesis Analyzer plugin), including the number of segments and nodes.

Increase of EndMT in Vitro

In vitro PCR experiments demonstrated that after methylprednisolone treatment, HUVECs exhibited a decreased expression of CD31, while CDH5 expression remained largely unchanged. Meanwhile, an increase in ACTA2 and S100A4 expression was observed, with S100A4 showing a particularly significant upregulation. Western blot analysis further confirmed these findings, showing that compared to the Ctrl group, the GCs group exhibited a significant reduction in CD31 protein expression and an increase in S100A4 protein expression as shown in Fig. 5b, consistent with the PCR results as shown in Fig. 5a. Flow cytometry results demonstrated that with the prolongation of glucocorticoid intervention, CD31 expression exhibited a slight decrease, whereas S100A4 expression gradually increased, particularly after 7 days of treatment (Fig. 5c). Specifically, compared to the control group, HUVECs exposed to glucocorticoids for 3 days showed a mild reduction in CD31-positive cells and a corresponding slight increase in S100A4-positive cells. Notably, after 7 days of treatment, a significant elevation in S100A4 expression was observed, accompanied by a more pronounced decline in CD31 expression.

To evaluate the functional changes of endothelial cells upon glucocorticoid exposure, we performed migration assay and matrigel tube formation assays. As shown in Fig. 5d, control group HUVECs effectively migrated to close the scratch area within 24 h, whereas the GCs-treated cells displayed increase migratory ability. Discussion

This study used single-cell sequencing, femoral head specimens, cell cultures, and animal experiments to analyze the potential role of EndMT in the pathogenesis of SONFH. Our data suggest that EndMT may play a critical role in the progression of SONFH, particularly in the development of microvascular rarefaction and bone necrosis. However, despite providing valuable insights, our results still have certain limitations that require further validation and supplementation.

ScRNA-seq

seRNA-seq analysis revealed gene expression changes associated with EndMT, particularly the upregulation of mesenchymal markers such as COL1A1 and S100A4. Further pseudotime analysis demonstrated a gradual loss of endothelial characteristics and acquisition of mesenchymal features in endothelial cells, suggesting that EndMT is a dynamic and progressive process in the pathogenesis of SONFH rather than merely a late-stage consequence. Our data support the potential role of EndMT in microvascular dysfunction; however, whether EndMT directly contributes to microvascular rarefaction requires further verification.

The role of oxidative stress in SONFH

Oxidative stress induced by glucocorticoids appears to be a mediator of vascular and bone injury25,31,32. and this study demonstrated that oxidative stress levels were elevated in the SONFH model. Immunohistochemical analysis revealed extensive positive expression of Nrf2 and HO-1 in the femoral heads of the GCs group, suggesting that glucocorticoid administration enhanced local oxidative stress responses. This finding is consistent with previous studies reporting that glucocorticoids induce ROS accumulation and oxidative tissue injury, indicating that oxidative stress may play an important role in the development of SONFH33–35. Besides, we initiated NAC treatment at week 4 based on our observations in the animal model. H&E staining at week 4 revealed partial bone lacunae and bone marrow fat vacuoles, indicating that the damage was still in its early stages and could benefit from conservative intervention. In contrast, by week 6, large-scale necrosis had occurred, making conservative treatments ineffective. Clinically, most patients present with hip pain after partial femoral head necrosis, and if the necrotic area is still small and has not collapsed, hip-preserving treatments can be effective. Thus, initiating NAC at week 4 aligns with both the experimental evidence and clinical practice, offering potential therapeutic benefits before extensive necrosis develops.

Morphologically, the trabecular bone in the GCs group appeared markedly sparser, and most of the bone marrow cavity was replaced by adipocytes, further supporting the notion that glucocorticoid exposure leads to structural deterioration and metabolic imbalance in bone tissue. Notably, antioxidant treatment with NAC partially alleviated these changes. Compared with the GCs group, the GCs + NAC group showed reduced Nrf2 and HO-1 (markers commonly associated with oxidative damage) expression, indicating that antioxidant therapy could decrease local oxidative stress levels. In addition, EndMT levels were alleviated in the GCs + NAC group, suggesting a potential association between oxidative stress and EndMT. This study provides experimental evidence supporting the role of oxidative stress in the pathogenesis of SONFH. Antioxidant intervention may partially protect against bone and vascular injury; however, the precise mechanisms require further investigation.

The impact of EndMT on microvascular rarefaction

Recent studies have shown that the EndMT process has shown potential in tissue engineering and regenerative medicine, particularly in the field of vascular regeneration and repair.36,37 Studies have shown that EndMT plays a key role in diseases such as atherosclerosis38, pulmonary fibrosis39and kidney damage21. ScRNA-seq provided high-resolution insights into microvascular injury and EndMT process in SONFH.

Microvascular rarefaction is one of the key pathological features of SONFH, characterized by a reduction in the density of the microvascular network, a decrease in the number of blood vessels, and restricted blood flow. Studies have shown that microvascular rarefaction not only exacerbates local hypoxia and metabolic disturbances but may also promote further bone necrosis by inhibiting bone metabolism and interfering with bone repair processes14. EndMT is a form of endothelial cell reprogramming, where vascular endothelial cells lose their typical phenotype and transform into cells with fibroblast or smooth muscle cell characteristics8,40. This process is often accompanied by changes in extracellular matrix components such as collagen and fibronectin41–43. Especially under the influence of glucocorticoids, these transformed cells may deposit excessive collagen and other matrix components, causing thickening of the microvascular walls, narrowing of the vascular lumen, increased fibrosis, and loss of elasticity and permeability, which further restricts blood flow and exacerbates the degree of microvascular rarefaction21,44.

Endothelial reprogramming can also inhibit angiogenesis. O'Riordan and colleagues45 found that in cells treated with NOS inhibitors, the synthesis of collagen XVIII increased, along with the overproduction of its anti-angiogenic fragments, leading to capillary rarefaction. This process was associated with an increase in the expression of transforming growth factor-β and connective tissue growth factor in the kidneys. Furthermore, inhibition of NOS triggers downstream signaling factors from the endostatin and TGF-beta pathways, reprogramming endothelial cells to adopt a myofibroblast phenotype and initiating the fibrotic process46. We demonstrated through single-cell sequencing, human specimen validation, cell experiments, and animal studies that glucocorticoids can promote the upregulation of mesenchymal-related markers in endothelial cells. These findings confirm the presence of EndMT in SONFH, as demonstrated by EndMT detection in regions of the femoral head samples from SONFH patients that had not yet undergone complete necrosis.

Clinical implications and future directions

This study enhances our understanding of the pathophysiological mechanisms behind SONFH. The findings suggest that managing oxidative stress may play a role in preventing or mitigating the vascular injury that drives the disease. Clinically, antioxidant therapies could potentially be employed to reduce the severity of SONFH, especially in patients who require long-term glucocorticoid therapy. This study also underscores the need for early diagnosis and intervention in patients at risk of SONFH, particularly those on chronic steroid treatments.

Limitations

However, there are limitations that should be addressed in future research. First, scRNA-seq was not performed directly on femoral head bone tissue. This decision was based on the technical challenges of isolating sufficient numbers of viable vascular endothelial cells from osseous tissue during single-cell dissociation. Moreover, most femoral head specimens available for research are from advanced-stage SONFH, which often fail to meet the stringent quality control requirements of single-cell transcriptomic analysis. Therefore, we instead analyzed cystic regions within SONFH lesions, which are known to exhibit atypical reparative responses. Although indirect, this approach provided valuable insights into the potential role of EndMT in SONFH pathogenesis. Besides, HUVECs were used for the in vitro investigation of EndMT in SONFH. Due to the inherent heterogeneity between HUVECs and bone microvascular endothelial cells, the results may not fully reflect the pathological changes occurring in the bone microenvironment. Furthermore, while we observed the activation of EndMT and oxidative stress in animal models, the precise molecular pathways, particularly involving the signaling pathway, were not fully explored. Further studies focusing on the detailed signaling mechanisms of EndMT could offer deeper insights into potential drug targets for preventing vascular injury in SONFH. Additionally, while NAC demonstrated some promise in reducing oxidative stress and EndMT, its long-term efficacy and safety in human clinical settings need further investigation. Future research should also consider exploring other antioxidant strategies or direct inhibitors of EndMT as potential therapeutic options. Moreover, clinical trials involving patients with SONFH are necessary to validate these preclinical findings and assess the clinical relevance of targeting oxidative stress and EndMT in preventing or reversing the disease.

Conclusion

This study integrated single-cell sequencing, human femoral head specimens, cell culture, and animal experiments to explore the role of EndMT in SONFH. The findings suggest that EndMT is involved in the pathological process of SONFH and may contribute to microvascular rarefaction and subsequent bone necrosis. In addition, oxidative stress appears to be a mediator linking glucocorticoid exposure to vascular and bone injury, and antioxidant intervention with NAC partially mitigated these effects in the animal model.

Our results provide experimental evidence supporting the interaction between oxidative stress and EndMT in the progression of SONFH. However, further mechanistic studies are needed to confirm these findings and clarify the causal relationships involved. Overall, this work offers new insights into the vascular pathology underlying SONFH and suggests that targeting oxidative stress and endothelial dysfunction may hold potential for future therapeutic strategies.
