Explicit and Non-asymptotic Query Complexities of Rank-Based Zeroth-order Algorithms on Smooth Functions
Haishan Ye

Abstract
Rank-based zeroth-order (ZO) optimization—which relies only on the ordering of function evaluations—offers strong robustness to noise and monotone transformations, and underlies many successful algorithms such as CMA-ES, natural evolution strategies, and rank-based genetic algorithms. Despite its widespread use, the theoretical understanding of rank-based ZO methods remains limited: existing analyses provide only asymptotic insights and do not yield explicit convergence rates for algorithms selecting the top- k k directions. This work closes this gap by analyzing a simple rank-based ZO algorithm and establishing the first explicit , and non-asymptotic query complexities. For a d d -dimension problem, if the function is L L -smooth and μ \mu -strongly convex, the algorithm achieves 𝒪 ~ ​ ( d ​ L μ ​ log ⁡ d ​ L μ ​ δ ​ log ​ 1 ε ) \widetilde{\mathcal{O}}\!\left(\frac{dL}{\mu}\log\!\frac{dL}{\mu\delta}\log\!\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\right) to find an ε \varepsilon -suboptimal solution, and for smooth nonconvex objectives it reaches 𝒪 ⁡ ( d ​ L ε ​ log ⁡ 1 ε ) \mathcal{O}\!\left(\frac{dL}{\varepsilon}\log\!\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\right) . Notation 𝒪 ⁡ ( ⋅ ) \mathcal{O}(\cdot) hides constant terms and 𝒪 ~ ​ ( ⋅ ) \widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(\cdot) hides extra log ⁡ log ⁡ 1 ε \log\log\frac{1}{\varepsilon} term. These query complexities hold with a probability at least 1 − δ 1-\delta with 0 < δ < 1 0<\delta<1 . The analysis in this paper is novel and avoids classical drift and information-geometric techniques. Our analysis offers new insight into why rank-based heuristics lead to efficient ZO optimization.



1
Introduction

Zeroth-order optimization—optimization using only function evaluations—has become indispensable in a broad range of modern applications. Among these methods,
rank-based
ZO algorithms hold a central and historically significant position. Unlike value-based or gradient-estimation-based methods, rank-based algorithms rely solely on the relative ordering of sampled candidate points. This makes them robust to noise, monotone transformations of the objective, and scaling issues, which in turn has led to widespread empirical success.
Accordingly, a lots of rank-based zeroth-order algorithms have been proposed such as CMA-ES
(
Hansen & Ostermeier, 2001
)
, natural evolution strategies
(
Wierstra et al., 2014
)
, rank-based genetic algorithm
(
Whitley, 1989
)
.

Rank-based ZO algorithms are now extensively used in fields such as evolutionary computation
(
Rechenberg, 1978
;
Beyer & Arnold, 2001
)
, machine learning and reinforcement learning via CMA-ES
(
Loshchilov & Hutter, 2016
;
Igel, 2003
;
Chrabaszcz et al., 2018
)
, natural evolution strategies
(
Wierstra et al., 2014
;
Such et al., 2017
)
, and engineering and scientific design optimization
(
Hasenjäger et al., 2005
;
Bäck et al., 2023
)
. Their success across such diverse domains underscores the practical power of rank-based mechanisms.

Despite their popularity, the theoretical foundations for rank-based ZO algorithms remain incomplete.
The rank-based selection which is the corner stone of rank-based ZO algorithms brings the nonlinear and discontinuous dependence on order statistics, which complicates standard analytical tools.
To conquer the difficulties of rank-based selection, drift analysis
(
He & Yao, 2001
)
and information-geometric optimization
(
Ollivier et al., 2017
;
Akimoto et al., 2012
)
have become the two main tools in convergence analysis of rank-based ZO algorithms.
However, these studies on evolution strategies provide asymptotic insights into stability and adaptive behavior
(
Beyer & Arnold, 2001
;
Ollivier et al., 2017
;
Wierstra et al., 2014
)
, but they do
not
yield explicit non-asymptotic convergence guarantees.

In contrast, comparison-based and value-based ZO methods now enjoy explicit complexity bounds under different assumptions
(
Morinaga et al., 2021
;
Nesterov & Spokoiny, 2017
;
Ghadimi & Lan, 2013
;
Ye et al., 2025
)
.
For example,
Nesterov & Spokoiny, 2017
show that a value-based ZO algorithm can achieve a
\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{dL}{\mu}\log\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\right)
query complexity if the function
L
-smooth and
\mu
-strongly convex.
Recently, several theoretical works on evolution strategies show that evolution strategies algorithms can achieve a linear convergence under proper assumptions
(
Morinaga et al., 2021
;
Akimoto et al., 2022
)
.
However, these works does
not
provided the convergence rate of rank-based zeroth-order algorithms which common select top-
k
directions to update the next point.
Instead, these works only provide the convergence rates of comparison-based algorithms which compare two candidate points.
Thus, despite decades of empirical success and extensive studies of rank-based ZO algorithms, a precise convergence and query analysis still remain an
open
challenge.

The present work aims to contribute toward bridging this theoretical gap by analyzing the structural obstacles in rank-based ZO optimization and by developing tools to characterize their convergence behavior.
We summary the main contribution as follows:

•

This paper proposes a simple rank-based zeroth-order algorithm and provides the queries complexities
\tilde{\mathcal{O}}\left(\frac{dL}{\mu}\log\frac{dL}{\mu\delta}\log\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\right)
for
L
-smooth and
\mu
-strongly convex function, and
\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{dL}{\varepsilon}\log\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\right)
to find an
\varepsilon
-suboptimal solution which hold with a probability at least
1-\delta
.
To my best knowledge, this paper provides the
first
,
explicit
, and
non-asymptotic
query complexities of a rank-based zeroth-order algorithm.

•

The analysis techniques in this paper is novel and does
not
lie on the drift analysis
(
He & Yao, 2001
)
and information-geometric optimization
(
Ollivier et al., 2017
)
which are popular in the convergence analysis of evolution strategies algorithms.
Thus, we believe the analysis technique has potential in the analysis of other rank-based algorithms and will promote the theoretical studies of evolution algorithms.

•

Our analysis also provide novel insights over the rank-based ZO algorithms. Our convergence analysis theoretically shows that rank-based ZO algorithm can almost achieve the same query complexity to the one of value-based ZO algorithms. Our convergence analysis also provides new perspective on “log weights” which is popular in the famous CMA-ES.

Notation and Preliminaries

First, we will introduce the notation of
\left\|\cdot\right\|
which is defined as
\left\|\bm{x}\right\|\triangleq\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{d}x_{i}^{2}}
for a
d
-dimension vector
\bm{x}
with

being
i
-th entry of
\bm{x}
.
Letting
\bm{U}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
be a matrix, the notation
\left\|\bm{U}\right\|
is the spectral norm of
\bm{U}
.
Given two vectors
\bm{x},\bm{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, we use
\left\langle\bm{x},\bm{y}\right\rangle=\sum_{i=1}^{d}x_{i}y_{i}
to denote the inner product of
\bm{x}
and
\bm{y}
.

Next, we will introduce the objective function which we aim to solve in this paper

\min_{\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}f(\bm{x})

(2.1)

where
f(\bm{x})
is a
L
-smooth function.
We will also introduce two widely used assumptions about the objective function.

Assumption 1
.

A function
f(\bm{x})
is called
L
-smooth with
L>0
if for any
\bm{x},\bm{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, it holds that

|d(\bm{y},\bm{x})|\leq\frac{L}{2}\left\|\bm{y}-\bm{x}\right\|^{2},\mbox{ with }d(\bm{y},\bm{x})\triangleq f(\bm{y})-f(\bm{x})-\left\langle\nabla f(\bm{x}),\bm{y}-\bm{x}\right\rangle.

(2.2)

Assumption 2
.

A function
f(\bm{x})
is called
\mu
-strongly convex with
\mu>0
if for any
\bm{x},\bm{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, it holds that

d(\bm{y},\bm{x})\geq\frac{\mu}{2}\left\|\bm{y}-\bm{x}\right\|^{2}.

(2.3)

The
L
-smooth and
\mu
-strongly convex assumptions are standard in the convergence analysis of optimization
(
Nesterov, 2013
)
.

4

This section will give the detailed convergence analysis and obtain the queries complexities of Algorithm
1
.
First, we will give a list of events that hold with high probabilities.
The high probabilities are determined by the randomness of the algorithm, concentration inequality of the Gaussian distribution, order-statistics of the Gaussian distribution.
Condition on the events, we provide how the function value will decay when an update is conducted in Algorithm
1
.
Then, we will provide the detailed convergence properties of Algorithm
1
and explicit query queries.
Finally, based on the convergence and query results, we discuss the relationship between rank-based and valued-based ZO algorithm, how to choose weights properly, and how the “negative” samples help to achieve faster convergence rate.

\displaystyle\left\langle\nabla f(\bm{x}_{t}),\bm{u}_{j_{3N/4+1}}\right\rangle\geq\frac{3}{2}\left\|\nabla f(\bm{x}_{t})\right\|.

Now, we have proved that with a probability at least
1-\delta
it holds that

\left\langle\nabla f(\bm{x}_{t}),\bm{u}_{j_{3N/4+1}}\right\rangle\geq\frac{3}{2}\left\|\nabla f(\bm{x}_{t})\right\|.

(4.14)

By Eq. (
4.10
), we have

\displaystyle f(\bm{x}_{t}+\alpha\bm{u}_{j_{3N/4+k}})\geq f(\bm{x}_{t}+\alpha\bm{u}_{j_{3N/4+1}}),\mbox{ for }k>1.

Equivalently,

\displaystyle\left\langle\nabla f(\bm{x}_{t}),\bm{u}_{j_{3N/4+k}}\right\rangle+\alpha^{-1}d(\bm{x}_{t}+\alpha\bm{u}_{j_{3N/4+k}},\bm{x}_{t})

(4.15)

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\left\langle\nabla f(\bm{x}_{t}),\bm{u}_{j_{3N/4+1}}\right\rangle+\alpha^{-1}d(\bm{x}_{t}+\alpha\bm{u}_{j_{3N/4+1}},\bm{x}_{t})

(4.16)

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{eq:j_low}}}{{\geq}}

\displaystyle\frac{3}{2}\left\|\nabla f(\bm{x}_{t})\right\|+\alpha^{-1}d(\bm{x}_{t}+\alpha\bm{u}_{j_{3N/4+1}},\bm{x}_{t}).

(4.17)

Combining with
\alpha\leq\frac{\left\|\nabla f(\bm{x}_{t})\right\|}{4LC_{d,\delta}}
, then we can obtain that for all
k>1
, it holds

\displaystyle\left\langle\nabla f(\bm{x}_{t}),\bm{u}_{j_{3N/4+k}}\right\rangle\geq

\displaystyle\frac{3}{2}\left\|\nabla f(\bm{x}_{t})\right\|+\alpha^{-1}d(\bm{x}_{t}+\alpha\bm{u}_{j_{3N/4+1}},\bm{x}_{t})-\alpha^{-1}d(\bm{x}_{t}+\alpha\bm{u}_{j_{3N/4+k}},\bm{x}_{t})

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{eq:d_up}}}{{\geq}}

\displaystyle\frac{3}{2}\left\|\nabla f(\bm{x}_{t})\right\|-2C_{d,\delta}L\alpha\geq\left\|\nabla f(\bm{x}_{t})\right\|.

Combining with Eq. (
4.14
), we have proved that

\left\langle\nabla f(\bm{x}_{t}),\bm{u}_{j_{3N/4+k}}\right\rangle\geq\left\|\nabla f(\bm{x}_{t})\right\|,\mbox{ for }k\geq 1.

Equivalently, we have proved that

\displaystyle\left\langle\nabla f(\bm{x}_{t}),\bm{u}_{(k)}\right\rangle\geq

\displaystyle\left\|\nabla f(\bm{x}_{t})\right\|,\mbox{ for }k=\frac{3N}{4}+1,\dots,N,

which concludes the proof.
∎

Lemma 6
.

Assume that function
f(\bm{x})
is
L
-smooth.
Given
0<\delta<\frac{2}{N}
, and assuming event
\mathcal{E}_{t,1}
defined in Eq. (
4.1
) holds.
Then we have

\displaystyle\left\langle\nabla f(\bm{x}_{t}),\bm{d}_{t}\right\rangle\leq

\displaystyle-\frac{\alpha}{2}\sum_{k\in\mathcal{K}}\frac{w_{(k)}}{f(\bm{x}_{t})-f(\bm{x}_{t}+\alpha\bm{u}_{(k)})}\cdot\left\langle\nabla f(\bm{x}_{t}),\bm{u}_{(k)}\right\rangle^{2}

(4.19)

\displaystyle+\frac{L^{2}C_{d,\delta}^{2}\alpha^{3}}{2}\sum_{k\in\mathcal{K}}\left|\frac{w_{(k)}}{f(\bm{x}_{t})-f(\bm{x}_{t}+\alpha\bm{u}_{(k)})}\right|.

where
\mathcal{K}=\{1,\dots,N/4,\;3N/4,\dots,N\}
,

Proof.

By the definition of descent direction
\bm{d}_{t}
, we have

\displaystyle\left\langle\nabla f(\bm{x}_{t}),\bm{d}_{t}\right\rangle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{eq:dt}}}{{=}}

\displaystyle\sum w_{(k)}\left\langle\nabla f(\bm{x}_{t}),\bm{u}_{(k)}\right\rangle=\sum_{k=1}^{N/4}w_{(k)}^{+}\left\langle\nabla f(\bm{x}_{t}),\bm{u}_{(k)}\right\rangle+\sum_{k=3N/4+1}^{N}w_{(k)}^{-}\left\langle\nabla f(\bm{x}_{t}),\bm{u}_{(k)}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{N/4}\frac{w_{(k)}^{+}}{f(\bm{x}_{t}+\alpha\bm{u}_{(k)})-f(\bm{x}_{t})}\cdot\Big(f(\bm{x}_{t}+\alpha\bm{u}_{(k)})-f(\bm{x}_{t})\Big)\left\langle\nabla f(\bm{x}_{t}),\bm{u}_{(k)}\right\rangle

\displaystyle+\sum_{k=3N/4+1}^{N}\frac{w_{(k)}^{-}}{f(\bm{x}_{t}+\alpha\bm{u}_{(k)})-f(\bm{x}_{t})}\cdot\Big(f(\bm{x}_{t}+\alpha\bm{u}_{(k)})-f(\bm{x}_{t})\Big)\left\langle\nabla f(\bm{x}_{t}),\bm{u}_{(k)}\right\rangle.

For
k=1,\dots,N/4
, we have

\displaystyle\frac{w_{(k)}^{+}}{f(\bm{x}_{t}+\alpha\bm{u}_{(k)})-f(\bm{x}_{t})}\cdot\Big(f(\bm{x}_{t}+\alpha\bm{u}_{(k)})-f(\bm{x}_{t})\Big)\left\langle\nabla f(\bm{x}_{t}),\bm{u}_{(k)}\right\rangle

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{eq:taylor}}}{{=}}

\displaystyle\frac{w_{(k)}^{+}}{f(\bm{x}_{t}+\alpha\bm{u}_{(k)})-f(\bm{x}_{t})}\cdot\Big(\alpha\left\langle\nabla f(\bm{x}_{t}),\bm{u}_{(k)}\right\rangle+d(\bm{x}_{t}+\alpha\bm{u}_{(k)},\bm{x}_{t})\Big)\left\langle\nabla f(\bm{x}_{t}),\bm{u}_{(k)}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\alpha w_{(k)}^{+}}{f(\bm{x}_{t}+\alpha\bm{u}_{(k)})-f(\bm{x}_{t})}\left\langle\nabla f(\bm{x}_{t}),\bm{u}_{(k)}\right\rangle^{2}+\frac{w_{(k)}^{+}\cdot d(\bm{x}_{t}+\alpha\bm{u}_{(k)},\bm{x}_{t})}{f(\bm{x}_{t}+\alpha\bm{u}_{(k)})-f(\bm{x}_{t})}\left\langle\nabla f(\bm{x}_{t}),\bm{u}_{(k)}\right\rangle

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{\alpha w_{(k)}^{+}}{f(\bm{x}_{t}+\alpha\bm{u}_{(k)})-f(\bm{x}_{t})}\left\langle\nabla f(\bm{x}_{t}),\bm{u}_{(k)}\right\rangle^{2}+\frac{\alpha w_{(k)}^{+}}{2(f(\bm{x}_{t})-f(\bm{x}_{t}+\alpha\bm{u}_{(k)}))}\left\langle\nabla f(\bm{x}_{t}),\bm{u}_{(k)}\right\rangle^{2}

\displaystyle+\frac{w_{(k)}^{+}}{2\alpha(f(\bm{x}_{t})-f(\bm{x}_{t}+\alpha\bm{u}_{(k)}))}\cdot d^{2}(\bm{x}_{t}+\alpha\bm{u}_{(k)},\bm{x}_{t})

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{\alpha w_{(k)}^{+}}{2(f(\bm{x}_{t}+\alpha\bm{u}_{(k)})-f(\bm{x}_{t}))}\left\langle\nabla f(\bm{x}_{t}),\bm{u}_{(k)}\right\rangle^{2}+\frac{L^{2}C_{d,\delta}^{2}\alpha^{3}w_{(k)}^{+}}{2(f(\bm{x}_{t})-f(\bm{x}_{t}+\alpha\bm{u}_{(k)}))}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{\alpha w_{(k)}^{+}}{2(f(\bm{x}_{t})-f(\bm{x}_{t}+\alpha\bm{u}_{(k)}))}\left\langle\nabla f(\bm{x}_{t}),\bm{u}_{(k)}\right\rangle^{2}+\frac{L^{2}C_{d,\delta}^{2}\alpha^{3}w_{(k)}^{+}}{2(f(\bm{x}_{t})-f(\bm{x}_{t}+\alpha\bm{u}_{(k)}))},

where the first inequality is because of fact that
ab\leq\frac{a^{2}+b^{2}}{2}
for all real numbers
a,b
and the last inequality is because event
\mathcal{E}_{t,1}
holds.

Similarly,

\displaystyle\frac{w_{(k)}^{-}}{f(\bm{x}_{t}+\alpha\bm{u}_{(k)})-f(\bm{x}_{t})}\cdot\Big(f(\bm{x}_{t}+\alpha\bm{u}_{(k)})-f(\bm{x}_{t})\Big)\left\langle\nabla f(\bm{x}_{t}),\bm{u}_{(k)}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\alpha w_{(k)}^{-}}{f(\bm{x}_{t}+\alpha\bm{u}_{(k)})-f(\bm{x}_{t})}\cdot\left\langle\nabla f(\bm{x}_{t}),\bm{u}_{(k)}\right\rangle^{2}+d(\bm{x}_{t}+\alpha\bm{u}_{(k)},\bm{x}_{t})\cdot\left\langle\nabla f(\bm{x}_{t}),\bm{u}_{(k)}\right\rangle

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle-\frac{\alpha w_{(k)}^{-}}{2(f(\bm{x}_{t})-f(\bm{x}_{t}+\alpha\bm{u}_{(k)}))}\cdot\left\langle\nabla f(\bm{x}_{t}),\bm{u}_{(k)}\right\rangle^{2}+\frac{L^{2}C_{d,\delta}^{2}\alpha^{3}w_{(k)}^{-}}{2(f(\bm{x}_{t})-f(\bm{x}_{t}+\alpha\bm{u}_{(k)}))}

Combining above results, we can obtain that

\displaystyle\left\langle\nabla f(\bm{x}_{t}),\bm{d}_{t}\right\rangle\leq-\frac{\alpha}{2}\sum_{k\in\mathcal{K}}\frac{w_{(k)}}{f(\bm{x}_{t})-f(\bm{x}_{t}+\alpha\bm{u}_{(k)})}\cdot\left\langle\nabla f(\bm{x}_{t}),\bm{u}_{(k)}\right\rangle^{2}+\frac{L^{2}C_{d,\delta}^{2}\alpha^{3}}{2}\sum_{k\in\mathcal{K}}\left|\frac{w_{(k)}}{f(\bm{x}_{t})-f(\bm{x}_{t}+\alpha\bm{u}_{(k)})}\right|.

∎

4.4
Discussion

The convergence analysis in previous subsections provide some interesting and novel insights on the rank-based ZO algorithms.
In this section, we will give an detailed discussion.

Rank-based versus Value-based.

When
f(\bm{x})
is
L
-smooth and
\mu
-strongly convex, Corollary
3
shows that Algorithm
1
achieve a query complexity

\displaystyle Q=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{dL}{\mu}\cdot\log\frac{dL}{\mu\delta^{\prime}}\cdot\log\frac{1}{\varepsilon}+\frac{dL}{\mu}\cdot\log\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\cdot\log\log\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\right),

which is almost the same to the query complexity

\displaystyle\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{dL}{\mu}\log\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\right),

achieved by the value based zeroth-order algorithm
(
Nesterov & Spokoiny, 2017
)
.
However, our complexity holds with a high probability at least
1-\delta^{\prime}
.
In contrast, the complexity in
Nesterov & Spokoiny, 2017
holds in expectation.
Except the difference in
\log\frac{1}{\delta^{\prime}}
term, we can observe that our rank-based algorithm requires at most
\log\log\frac{1}{\varepsilon}
more queries than valued based zeroth-order algorithm.

Similarly, when
f(\bm{x})
is
L
-smooth and may be nonconvex, Corollary
4
shows that our rank-based zeroth-order algorithm achieves a query complexity

Q=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{dL}{\varepsilon}\log\frac{dL}{\delta^{\prime}\varepsilon}\right).

This complexity is at most
\mathcal{O}(\log\frac{1}{\varepsilon})
larger than the value based zeroth-order algorithm
(
Ghadimi & Lan, 2013
)
.

Compared the query complexities of our rank-based zeroth-order algorithm with value-based ones, we can conclude that the rank-based zeroth-order algorithm can achieve almost the same query complexities to the valued-based counterparts.
This is an interesting theory result since only the relative ranking is used in a rank-based zeroth-order algorithm which valued-based algorithms can access the function value.
That is, valued-based algorithms use more information than rank-based zeroth-order algorithms but achieve almost the same query complexities.
It is natural to ask that whether the relative ranking is sufficient for the zeroth-order algorithm or the valued-based zeroth-order algorithms can achieve a lower query complexity.

How to choose weights and why log weights?

Convergence theories in Eq. (
4.30
) and Eq. (
4.33
) show that setting all weights
w_{(k)}
equally is the best strategy.
However, in practice, it is commonly to set a weight of
\bm{u}_{i}
according to its rank
f(\bm{x}+\alpha\bm{u}_{i})
.
Intuitively, one should set a larger weight on
\bm{u}_{k_{1}}
over
\bm{u}_{k_{2}}
in Algorithm
1
. That is, set
w_{k_{1}}^{+}>w_{k_{2}}^{+}>0
, if
(k_{1})<(k_{2})\leq\frac{N}{4}
.
For example, the famous CMA-ES set weights
w_{(k)}\propto\log(N+1)-\log(k)

(
Hansen & Ostermeier, 2001
)
.
Our theory in Theorem
1
and Theorem
2
can not explain the success of weight strategy of CMA-ES.

In fact, Eq. (
4.30
) and Eq. (
4.33
) over-relax the result in Eq. (
4.27
).
With a proper weight strategy, one may obtain

\displaystyle\min_{k\in\mathcal{K}}\left|\frac{\left\langle\nabla f(\bm{x}_{t}),\bm{u}_{(k)}\right\rangle}{w_{(k)}}\right|\cdot\min_{k\in\mathcal{K}}\left|\frac{w_{(k)}}{\left\langle\nabla f(\bm{x}_{t}),\bm{u}_{(k)}\right\rangle}\right|>\frac{\min_{k\in\mathcal{K}}\;w_{(k)}}{\max_{k\in\mathcal{K}}\;w_{(k)}}\cdot\frac{\min_{k\in\mathcal{K}}\;|\left\langle\nabla f(\bm{x}_{t}),\bm{u}_{(k)}\right\rangle|}{\max_{k\in\mathcal{K}}\;|\left\langle\nabla f(\bm{x}_{t}),\bm{u}_{(k)}\right\rangle|},

which implies a faster convergence.

However, the gain from a proper weight strategy has its upper bound over setting all weights
w_{(k)}
equally at most
\sqrt{2\log\frac{2N}{\delta}}
.
First, notice that the fact that it holds that

\displaystyle\min_{k\in\mathcal{K}}\left|\frac{\left\langle\nabla f(\bm{x}_{t}),\bm{u}_{(k)}\right\rangle}{w_{(k)}}\right|\cdot\min_{k\in\mathcal{K}}\left|\frac{w_{(k)}}{\left\langle\nabla f(\bm{x}_{t}),\bm{u}_{(k)}\right\rangle}\right|\leq 1.

Second, Eq. (
4.28
) gives a lower bound if
w_{(k)}
’s are set equally

\displaystyle\min_{k\in\mathcal{K}}\left|\frac{\left\langle\nabla f(\bm{x}_{t}),\bm{u}_{(k)}\right\rangle}{w_{(k)}}\right|\cdot\min_{k\in\mathcal{K}}\left|\frac{w_{(k)}}{\left\langle\nabla f(\bm{x}_{t}),\bm{u}_{(k)}\right\rangle}\right|\geq\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\log\frac{2N}{\delta}}}.

Next, we will try to set proper weights
w_{(k)}
to achieve a large descent value:

\max_{w_{(k)}}\left(\min_{k\in\mathcal{K}}\left|\frac{\left\langle\nabla f(\bm{x}_{t}),\bm{u}_{(k)}\right\rangle}{w_{(k)}}\right|\cdot\min_{k\in\mathcal{K}}\left|\frac{w_{(k)}}{\left\langle\nabla f(\bm{x}_{t}),\bm{u}_{(k)}\right\rangle}\right|\right)

(4.44)

In fact,
\left\langle\nabla f(\bm{x}_{t}),\bm{u}_{(k)}\right\rangle
shares the same distribution
\left\|\nabla f(\bm{x}_{t})\right\|\cdot\{X_{(1)},\dots,X_{(N)}\}
where
X_{(i)}
is the
i
-th order statistics of
N
i.i.d. Gaussian random variable, that is,
X_{i}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)
.
Accordingly, we have
\left\langle\nabla f(\bm{x}_{t}),\bm{u}_{(k)}\right\rangle=\left\|\nabla f(\bm{x}_{t})\right\||X_{(k)}|
.
To minimize Eq. (
4.44
) by setting proper
w_{(k)}
, a good option is to set
w_{(k)}\propto\mathbb{E}[X_{i}]
.
A widely used approximation for the expected value of the
k
-th order statistic is Blom’s formula
(
Blom, 1958
)
:

\mathbb{E}[X_{(k)}]\approx\Phi^{-1}\!\left(\frac{k-0.375}{\,N+0.25\,}\right),

(4.45)

where
\Phi^{-1}(\cdot)
is the inverse cumulative distribution function of the Gaussian distribution.

It is interesting that the weight
w_{(k)}^{+}\propto\Phi^{-1}\!\left(\frac{k-0.375}{\,N+0.25\,}\right)

is very close to the one
w_{(k)}^{+}\propto\log(N+1)-\log(k)
widely used in CMA-ES
(
Hansen & Ostermeier, 2001
)
and natural evolution strategies
(
Wierstra et al., 2014
)
.
Thus, we conjecture that the success of “log weights” strategy lies in the approximation of the weights in proportion to Eq. (
4.45
).

Can “negative” sample help to achieve faster convergence?

CMA-ES commonly give weights over directions achieve on the
\lambda
smallest values where
\lambda=\frac{N}{4}
in this paper.
Instead, natural evolution strategies commonly exploit “negative” directions and set “negative” weight accordingly
(
Glasmachers et al., 2010
;
Wierstra et al., 2014
)
.
However, the CMA-ES abandons the information in “negative” directions to update
\bm{x}_{t}

(
Hansen & Ostermeier, 2001
)
.
Our Algorithm
1
also set “negative” weight over those “negative” directions which achieve the
\lambda
largest values.
Next, we will discuss how these “negative” directions help to achieve faster convergence.

Assume that we only use
w_{(k)}^{+}
, Lemma
10
will become

\displaystyle f(\bm{x}_{t+1})\leq

\displaystyle f(\bm{x}_{t})-\frac{1}{16LC_{N,d,\delta}^{\prime}}\cdot\min_{k\in\mathcal{K^{+}}}\left|\frac{\left\langle\nabla f(\bm{x}_{t}),\bm{u}_{(k)}\right\rangle}{w_{(k)}}\right|\cdot\min_{k\in\mathcal{K^{+}}}\left|\frac{w_{(k)}}{\left\langle\nabla f(\bm{x}_{t}),\bm{u}_{(k)}\right\rangle}\right|

\displaystyle\cdot\sum_{k\in\mathcal{K^{+}}}\left\langle\nabla f(\bm{x}_{t}),\bm{u}_{(k)}\right\rangle^{2}+\Delta_{\alpha,1},

where we
\mathcal{K^{+}}=\{1,\dots,N/4\}
and
C_{N,d,\delta}^{\prime}=\left(\sqrt{N/4}+\sqrt{d}+\sqrt{2\log\frac{2}{\delta}}\right)^{2}
.
Since
\mathcal{K^{+}}
only has half samples of
\mathcal{K}
and
C_{N,d,\delta}^{\prime}\approx C_{N,d,\delta}=\left(\sqrt{N/2}+\sqrt{d}+\sqrt{2\log\frac{2}{\delta}}\right)^{2}
since it holds
N\ll d
,
Comparing above equation to Eq. (
4.27
), we can concludes that Algorithm
1
can be almost twice fast as the algorithm without “negative” directions.
Just as above equations shown, the “negative” directions should be fully exploited and they will help to achieve a twice faster convergence rate.

5
Conclusion

This paper establishes the first explicit convergence rates for rank-based zeroth-order optimization, addressing a long-standing theoretical open problem.
By analyzing a simple rank-based zeroth-order method, we obtain the non-asymptotic query complexities under standard smoothness assumptions. Our proof framework departs from traditional drift and information-geometric analyses, revealing structural principles behind ranking-based updates. These findings not only clarify the behavior of rank-based ZO methods but also provide tools that may facilitate the analysis of more advanced evolution strategies in future work.

Lemma 13

(equivalence to a binomial event)
.

Let
X_{1},\dots,X_{N}
be i.i.d. continuous random variables with cumulative distribution function
\Phi(\cdot)
.
Denote their order statistics by
X_{(1)}\leq X_{(2)}\leq\dots\leq X_{(N)}
.
Fix
\tau\in\mathbb{R}
and put
m\in\{1,\dots,N\}
.
Define

M:=X_{(N-m+1)}.

Then

\Pr(M>\tau)=\Pr\Big(\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathbf{1}_{\{X_{i}>\tau\}}\geq m\Big)=\Pr\big(\mathrm{Bin}(N,p)\geq m\big),

where
\mathrm{Bin}(N,p)
is the Binomial distribution with parameters
N
and
p:=\Pr(X_{i}>\tau)=1-\Phi(\tau)
.

Proof.

The event
\{M>\tau\}
means that the
(N-m+1)
-th smallest observation exceeds
\tau
.
Equivalently, at least
m
of the
N
observations exceed
\tau
. This is exactly the event

\{\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathbf{1}_{\{X_{i}>\tau\}}\geq m\}
. Since the indicators
\mathbf{1}_{\{X_{i}>\tau\}}
are i.i.d. Bernoulli
(p)

with
p=1-F(\tau)
, the sum has distribution
\mathrm{Bin}(N,p)
, which proves the lemma.
∎
