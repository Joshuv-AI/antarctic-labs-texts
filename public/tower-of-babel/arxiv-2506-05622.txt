Deformations of OP ensembles in a bulk critical scaling
Caio E. Candido, Victor Alves, Thomas Chouteau, Charles F. Santos, Guilherme L. F. Silva

Abstract
. We study orthogonal polynomial ensembles whose weights are deformations of exponential weights, in the limit of a large number of particles. The deformation symbols we consider affect local fluctuations of the ensemble around a bulk point of the limiting spectrum. We identify the limiting kernel in terms of a solution to an integrable non-local differential equation. This novel kernel is the correlation kernel of a conditional thinned process starting from the Sine point process, and it is also related to a finite temperature deformation of the Sine kernel as recently studied by Claeys and Tarricone. We also unravel the effect of the deformation on the recurrence coefficients of the associated orthogonal polynomials, which display oscillatory behavior even in a one-cut regular situation for the limiting spectrum.



1.
Introduction

There are many different reasons for studying orthogonal polynomials. The present work lies on two modest ones: the role they play in the calculation of statistics of random particle systems, and in the unraveling of novel solutions to integrable systems.

From a probabilistic perspective, orthogonal polynomial (shortly OP) ensembles consist of random points
x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}\in\mathbb{R}
with joint distribution of the form

(1.1)

\mathcal{P}_{n}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})\mathrm{d}x_{1}\cdots\mathrm{d}x_{n}\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=\frac{1}{\mathsf{Z}_{n}}\prod_{1\leq i<j\leq n}|x_{i}-x_{j}|^{2}\prod_{j=1}^{n}\omega(x_{j})\,\mathrm{d}x_{1}\cdots\mathrm{d}x_{n},

where
\omega
is the associated weight, and
\mathsf{Z}_{n}
is the normalization constant – also known as partition function – that turns (
1.1
) into a probability distribution in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
,

\mathsf{Z}_{n}\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\prod_{1\leq i<j\leq n}|x_{i}-x_{j}|^{2}\prod_{j=1}^{n}\omega(x_{j})\,\mathrm{d}x_{1}\cdots\mathrm{d}x_{n}.

The name
orthogonal polynomial ensemble
(shortly OPE) stems from the fact that statistics for (
1.1
) may be computed through orthogonal polynomials. With
P_{j}(x)=x^{j}+\text{(lower degree terms)}
being the
j
-th orthogonal polynomial with respect to the measure
\omega(x)\mathrm{d}x
, uniquely determined by

\int_{\mathbb{R}}P_{j}(x)x^{k}\omega(x)\,\mathrm{d}x=0,\quad k=0,\cdots,j-1,

and
h_{j}>0
the associated norming constant, determined from

\frac{1}{h_{j}^{2}}=\int_{\mathbb{R}}P_{j}(x)^{2}\omega(x)\mathrm{d}x,

we construct the kernel

(1.2)

\mathsf{K}_{n}(x,y)\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=\sqrt{\omega(x)}\sqrt{\omega(y)}\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}h_{j}^{2}P_{j}(x)P_{j}(y),

known as the correlation kernel. The kernel
\mathsf{K}_{n}
is of prominent relevance to the particle system (
1.1
): it is known that (
1.1
) may be represented in
determinantal form
, namely

(1.3)

\mathcal{P}_{n}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})=\det\left(\mathsf{K}_{n}(x_{i},x_{j})\right)_{i,j=1}^{n}.

In short, (
1.3
) means that all info on the particle system (
1.1
) is encoded in the correlation kernel
\mathsf{K}_{n}
and, in turn, on the orthogonal polynomial themselves.

As mentioned, our second motivation comes from integrability structures that emerge from OPs in various ways. The integrability we want to explore concerns the unraveling of connections with integrable equations that emerge from the asymptotic analysis of the recurrence coefficients
(\gamma_{n}),(\beta_{n})
in the three-term recurrence relation for the OPs,

(1.4)

xP_{n}(x)=P_{n+1}(x)+\beta_{n}P_{n}(x)+\gamma_{n}^{2}P_{n-1}(x),\quad\gamma_{n}>0,\;\beta_{n}\in\mathbb{R},

and, as it will turn out, on the kernel
\mathsf{K}_{n}
as well.

The class of weights we consider in this manuscript are of the form

(1.5)

\omega(x)=\omega_{n}(x)\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=\sigma_{n}(x)\ee^{-nV(x)},\quad x\in\mathbb{R},

where
V:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
is called the potential of the model, and

(1.6)

\sigma_{n}(x)\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=\frac{1}{1+\ee^{-\mathsf{s}-n^{2}Q(x)}},\quad x\in\mathbb{R},

for a fixed function
Q:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
. We consider
\mathsf{s}\in\mathbb{R}
as a deformation parameter, and when needed we write
\sigma_{n}(x)=\sigma_{n}(x\mid\mathsf{s})
,
\omega_{n}(x)=\omega_{n}(x\mid\mathsf{s})
,
\gamma_{n}=\gamma_{n}(\mathsf{s})
,
\beta_{n}=\beta_{n}(\mathsf{s})
,
\mathsf{K}_{n}=\mathsf{K}_{n}(\cdot,\cdot\mid\mathsf{s})
etcetera. Conditions on
Q
will be placed in a moment, but we anticipate that we will impose that

(1.7)

Q(p)=Q^{\prime}(p)=0\text{ and }Q^{\prime\prime}(p)>0,\text{ for a fixed }p\in\mathbb{R}\text{ for which }\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\mathsf{K}_{n}(p,p\mid\mathsf{s})>0.

In the language of random matrix theory, this last condition is simply saying that
p
is a regular bulk point of the limiting spectrum. In the language of OPs and potential theory, it means that the density of the underlying equilibrium measure is strictly positive at
p
. We will elaborate more on these aspects later on.

In the limit
\mathsf{s}\to+\infty
, we have
\sigma_{n}\to 1
, the weight
\omega_{n}(\cdot\mid\mathsf{s})
turns into the exponential weight
\omega_{n}(x\mid\infty)=\ee^{-nV(x)}
, and we refer to the corresponding point process (
1.1
) as the
ground point process
. Our main goal is to understand how the introduction of the term
\sigma_{n}
affects asymptotic properties of the correlation kernel and recurrence coefficients when compared to the ones

\mathsf{K}_{n}(x,y\mid\infty)\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=\lim_{\mathsf{s}\to+\infty}\mathsf{K}_{n}(x,y\mid\mathsf{s}),\quad\gamma_{n}(\infty)\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=\lim_{\mathsf{s}\to+\infty}\gamma_{n}(\mathsf{s}),\quad\beta_{n}(\infty)\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=\lim_{\mathsf{s}\to+\infty}\beta_{n}(\mathsf{s}),

corresponding to the ground process.

There are various reasons why we choose the factor
\sigma_{n}
as in (
1.6
).
The particular form of
\sigma_{n}
is inspired by the finite temperature deformation factors
(1+\ee^{-x})^{-1}
that appear in free-fermionic models in finite temperature. In fact, recently it was realized that such type of deformations of determinantal point processes lead to new interesting features, including connections with free-fermionic models
[
16
]
, the Kardar-Parisi-Zhang equation
[
27
,
1
]
, nonlocal integrable equations
[
6
,
9
]
, among others
[
29
,
33
]
. At the level of the OP ensemble (
1.1
) itself, the deformed kernel
\mathsf{K}_{n}(\cdot\mid\mathsf{s})
is the correlation kernel for a conditional thinning process from the ground process
[
12
]
, and
\mathsf{s}
may be viewed as a strength parameter for this thinning.

The choice of scaling factor
n^{2}
is explained by the order of fluctuations of the ground process. Near a point
p
satisfying (
1.7
), fluctuations of the ground point process happen at a scale of order
n^{-1}
, meaning that the process induced by a local variable
\zeta\approx n(x-p)
has fluctuations of order
1
. Under the conditions in (
1.7
),
n^{2}Q(x)\approx Q^{\prime\prime}(p)\zeta^{2}
, and we expect that local fluctuations are affected in a nontrivial manner. Our results are essentially showing that such heuristics are true, and quantitatively computing the effect of such perturbations.

More precisely, we probe the effect of the introduction of
\sigma_{n}
into the ground process through the asymptotic analysis of the correlation kernel
\mathsf{K}_{n}(\cdot\mid\mathsf{s})
and the recurrence coefficients
(\gamma_{n}(\mathsf{s}))
,
(\beta_{n}(\mathsf{s}))
. In short, our results say that in the large
n
limit, the correlation kernel
\mathsf{K}_{n}(\cdot\mid\mathsf{s})
converges to a novel kernel, which is constructed out of a special solution
\mathsf{\Phi}
to a nonlocal nonlinear integrable differential equation. When
\mathsf{s}\to+\infty
, this kernel converges to the celebrated Sine Kernel, and at the level of point processes our calculations imply that this novel kernel is precisely the correlation kernel of a conditional thinned version of the Sine point process. The function
\mathsf{\Phi}
is oscillatory, and thus may be viewed as a nonlinear deformation of the sine oscillations described by the Sine Kernel.

At the level of the recurrence coefficients, we show that as
n\to\infty
,
\beta_{n}(\mathsf{s})
and
\gamma_{n}(\mathsf{s})
have the same limits as their undeformed counterparts
\beta_{n}(\infty)
and
\gamma_{n}(\infty)
, but differ from the latter in the subleading order
\mathcal{O}(n^{-1})
. We compute the leading order correction to the differences
n(\beta_{n}(\mathsf{s})-\beta_{n}(\infty))
and
n(\gamma_{n}(\mathsf{s})^{2}-\gamma_{n}(\infty)^{2})
, and it turns out that they display two nontrivial features. The first feature is the appearance of explicit oscillatory terms, and the second feature is the appearance of a nonlinear term, which satisfies a nonlinear integrable PDE itself, and it may be alternatively characterized through a total integral of the function used to construct the limiting correlation kernel. The integrable equations underneath both the limiting kernel and the recurrence coefficients have recently arisen in the context of finite temperature deformations of the Sine kernel, as obtained by Claeys and Tarricone
[
14
]
.

The appearance of the nonlinear PDE in the subleading asymptotics of the recurrence coefficients could be anticipated, and it is the exact form of such term that may be viewed as one of our nontrivial contributions. The explicit oscillatory (in
n
) term that appears therein, though, came to us with some surprise. In previous works, oscillations were coming either because the support of the underlying equilibrium measure was disconnected
[
5
,
18
]
, or thanks to discontinuities in the weight itself
[
26
]
. However, in our work here the underlying measure always has a connected support, and the corresponding potential is analytic: the perturbation
\sigma_{n}
lives on a local scale and does not change the equilibrium measure of the system.

We now move on to describing our results in detail.

1.1.
Statement of results

To state our main results, let us introduce the basic conditions on
V
and
Q
under which we will work on.

Assumption 1.1

(Assumptions on the potential)
.

We assume that
V
is a polynomial of even degree and positive leading coefficient. Furthermore, we assume that its associated equilibrium measure
\mathrm{d}\mu_{V}(x)=\phi_{V}(x)\mathrm{d}x
is
one-cut regular
, with a regular bulk point at the origin. This means that
\supp\phi_{V}=[a,b]
, for some
a,b\in\mathbb{R}
,
a<0<b
, and that its density
\phi_{V}
takes the form

\phi_{V}(x)=\frac{1}{\pi}\sqrt{(b-x)(x-a)}q(x),\quad\text{with }\quad q(x)>0\text{ for every }x\in[a,b].

Furthermore, we also assume that the variational inequality in the Euler-Lagrange equations associated to the equilibrium problem for
V
are strict, we refer the reader to Section
3.1
for a detailed account of these assumptions on
V
.

Assumption 1.2

(Assumptions on the deformation)
.

We assume that the function
Q:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
extends to an analytic function in a complex neighborhood of
\mathbb{R}
,

(1.8)

Q(x)>0,\qquad x\in\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\},

and

(1.9)

Q(0)=Q^{\prime}(0)=0,\quad\frac{Q^{\prime\prime}(0)}{2}=\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}\mathsf{t}>0.

Placing conditions on
V
(or rather on its equilibrium measure
\phi_{V}(x)\mathrm{d}x
) while studying (critical) scaling limits in random matrix theory is rather standard. In our present situation, we are interested in a local scaling limit near a regular bulk point
p
in the limiting spectrum of particles
\supp\phi_{V}
, and the assumptions that
\supp\phi_{V}
is connected and
p=0\in\supp\phi_{V}
are placed only for concreteness and simplicity of presentation.

Conditions on
Q
are also based on the fact that
p=0
is a regular bulk point, as mentioned in the introduction. We did not have to restrict to
p=0
and could instead have considered any other regular bulk point in the limiting spectrum, but for simplicity of presentation we will from now on assume
p=0
.

Under the conditions we just placed, we have that
\sigma_{n}(x)\to 1
except for
x=0
, and
\sigma_{n}(0)=(1+\ee^{-\mathsf{s}})^{-1}
for every
n
. One then naturally expects that

\frac{1}{n}\mathsf{K}_{n}(x,x\mid\mathsf{s})\to\phi_{V}(x),\quad n\to\infty,

for
x\in\mathbb{R}
pointwise. When
\sigma_{n}\equiv 1
this result is standard in OPs theory and random matrix theory
[
28
,
36
,
24
]
, and in the presence of
\sigma_{n}
it follows from our methods in a standard way. In particular, such convergence explains that the factor
\sigma_{n}
does not change the large
n
global behavior of zeros of
P_{n}
or, equivalently, it does not change the large
n
global behavior of particles of the system (
1.1
).

In contrast, our first result concerns asymptotics of the correlation kernel (
1.2
) and shows that its local behavior at the origin is drastically changed by the presence of the deformation
\sigma_{n}
. Such asymptotics will be given in terms of

(1.10)

\mathsf{T}\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=\frac{\phi_{V}(0)\pi}{\sqrt{\mathsf{t}}}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\lambda_{\infty}(\zeta\mid\mathsf{s})\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=\frac{1}{1+\ee^{-\mathsf{s}-\mathsf{T}^{-2}\zeta^{2}}}.

Theorem 1.3

(The limiting correlation kernel)
.

For every
\mathsf{s}\in\mathbb{R}
and every
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
, the convergence

(1.11)

\frac{1}{n\phi_{V}(0)\pi}\mathsf{K}_{n}\left(\frac{\zeta}{n\phi_{V}(0)\pi},\frac{\xi}{n\phi_{V}(0)\pi}\mid\mathsf{s}\right)=\mathsf{K}_{\infty}(\zeta,\xi)+\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{n^{1-\varepsilon}}\right),\quad n\to\infty,

towards the limiting kernel

\mathsf{K}_{\infty}(\zeta,\xi)=\mathsf{K}_{\infty}(\zeta,\xi\mid\mathsf{s})\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=\frac{\sqrt{\lambda_{\infty}(\zeta\mid\mathsf{s})}\sqrt{\lambda_{\infty}(\xi\mid\mathsf{s})}}{2\pi\mathrm{i}(\zeta-\xi)}\left[\mathsf{\Phi}\left(\frac{\zeta}{\mathsf{T}}\right)\mathsf{\Phi}\left(-\frac{\xi}{\mathsf{T}}\right)-\mathsf{\Phi}\left(-\frac{\zeta}{\mathsf{T}}\right)\mathsf{\Phi}\left(\frac{\xi}{\mathsf{T}}\right)\right]

holds true uniformly for
\zeta,\xi
in compacts of
\mathbb{R}
, where the function
\mathsf{\Phi}(\zeta)=\mathsf{\Phi}(\zeta\mid\mathsf{s},\mathsf{T})
satisfies the non-local nonlinear equation

(1.12)

\partial_{\mathsf{T}}\mathsf{\Phi}(\zeta\mid\mathsf{s},\mathsf{T})=\mathrm{i}\zeta\mathsf{\Phi}(\zeta\mid\mathsf{s},\mathsf{T})+\frac{1}{2\pi\mathrm{i}}\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}}\mathsf{\Phi}(\xi\mid\mathsf{s},\mathsf{T})^{2}\lambda_{\infty}^{\prime}(\mathsf{T}\xi\mid\mathsf{s})\,\mathrm{d}\xi\right)\mathsf{\Phi}(-\zeta\mid\mathsf{s},\mathsf{T}),

with asymptotic behavior

(1.13)

\mathsf{\Phi}(\zeta)\sim\ee^{\mathrm{i}\mathsf{T}\zeta},\quad\zeta\to\pm\infty,

valid for any
\mathsf{s}\in\mathbb{R},\mathsf{T}>0
fixed.

Furthermore, as
\mathsf{s}\to+\infty
the convergence

(1.14)

\mathsf{K}_{\infty}(\zeta,\xi\mid\mathsf{s})=\frac{1}{\pi}\mathsf{S}(\zeta,\xi)+\mathcal{O}(\ee^{-\mathsf{s}}),\quad\mathsf{s}\to+\infty,\qquad\mathsf{S}(\zeta,\xi)\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=\frac{\sin(\zeta-\xi)}{\zeta-\xi},

holds true uniformly for
\zeta,\xi
in compacts of
\mathbb{R}
.

Theorem
1.3
is the bulk analogue of the soft edge convergence result in
[
27
]
, where Ghosal and the last author show that a finite-temperature type deformation of an OP ensemble, when critically tuned at a regular soft edge, leads to a kernel described in terms of an integro-differential generalization of the Painlevé II equation. Similar appearances of integro-differential integrable equations in random matrix theory have also been recently observed in non-hermitian random matrix models by Bothner and Little
[
8
,
7
]
.

Under the same scaling as (
1.11
), the convergence of the ground process kernel
\mathsf{K}_{n}(\cdot\mid\infty)
towards the Sine kernel
\mathsf{S}
is an instance of the celebrated Sine kernel universality
[
17
,
34
,
23
]
. In the language of point processes, it means that the random particle system determined by the distribution (
1.1
) for
\omega=\omega(\cdot\mid\infty)
converges to the random particle system determined by
\mathsf{S}
, which is known as the Sine point process. The convergence (
1.14
) is essentially saying that the limits
\mathsf{s}\to+\infty
and
n\to+\infty
commute.

The deformed kernel
\mathsf{K}_{n}(\cdot\mid\mathsf{s})
may be viewed as the correlation kernel of a conditional thinned particle system constructed from the ground OP ensemble determined by
\mathsf{K}_{n}(\cdot\mid\infty)
as follows. We start with the ground process (
1.1
) for
\omega=\omega(\cdot\mid\infty)
, and color each random particle
x_{j}
with probability
\sigma_{n}(x_{j}\mid\mathsf{s})
, leaving the particle uncolored with complementary probability
1-\sigma_{n}(x_{j}\mid\mathsf{s})
. Now, we create a new conditional process, which is the process of colored particles conditioned that no particle has been left uncolored.

As shown by Claeys and Glesner, the correlation kernel for this conditional process is precisely
\mathsf{K}_{n}(\cdot\mid\mathsf{s})
. As a consequence of recent results by Claeys and the last author of the present paper in
[
13
, Section 3.1]
, the conditional thinned ensemble determined from
\mathsf{K}_{n}(\cdot\mid\mathsf{s})
converges
weakly as a point process
towards a conditional thinned process constructed from the Sine process. The methods developed in
[
13
]
, however, do not give access to computing the correlation kernel of this limiting process. Theorem
1.3
is thus strengthening this weak convergence to a convergence of the correlation kernels, and furthermore it yields that the correlation kernel of the conditioned thinned Sine process is precisely
\mathsf{K}_{\infty}
as constructed here.

To our knowledge, the kernel
\mathsf{K}_{\infty}
is novel, but as mentioned before the function
\mathsf{\Phi}
itself and its characterization (
1.12
)–(
1.13
) has appeared recently in a work by Claeys and Tarricone
[
14
]
. A RHP studied in the latter work is also at the core of our results, and we will elaborate more on this connection in a moment.

Our second main result concerns asymptotics for the recurrence coefficients
\gamma_{n}^{2}=\gamma_{n}(\mathsf{s})^{2},\beta_{n}=\beta_{n}(\mathsf{s})
of the deformed orthogonal polynomials (recall (
1.4
) and the discussion thereafter).

Asymptotics of recurrence coefficients have a long and relevant historical importance, an interest which remains to our days. The excellent monograph
[
37
]
by Van Assche reviews many of such developments and history (see also the recent survey
[
38
]
by the same author), and
[
21
,
15
,
2
,
4
,
3
]
is a very limited list of references that encompasses various aspects of asymptotics of recurrence coefficients for orthogonal polynomials that have been considered recently in the literature.

In the case of classical orthogonal polynomials, such as Hermite and Laguerre, recurrence coefficients are explicit and their asymptotics have been known for more than a century. In the context of exponential weights, one of the seminal outputs of the introduction of the Riemann-Hilbert machinery to OP theory in the late 1990s is precisely towards the asymptotic analysis of recurrence coefficients. Already in the early RHP-OPs works, Deift et al. proved that for exponential weights with one-cut regular equilibrium measure, recurrence coefficients admit a full asymptotic expansion in inverse powers of
n

[
18
]
. More explicit expressions for the first few terms in this expansion were calculated by Kuijlaars and Tibboel
[
32
, Theorem 1.1]
, which in the case of the ground process
\mathsf{s}=+\infty
reads

(1.15)

\begin{split}\gamma_{n}(\infty)^{2}&=\frac{(b-a)^{2}}{16}+\mathcal{O}(n^{-2}),\quad n\to\infty;\\
\beta_{n}(\infty)&=\frac{b+a}{2}+\frac{1}{2n(b-a)}\left(\frac{1}{q(b)}-\frac{1}{q(a)}\right)+\mathcal{O}(n^{-2}),\quad n\to\infty.\end{split}

We emphasize that the results in
[
32
,
18
]
assume that the weight is exponential and the equilibrium measure is one-cut. Observe that in such a case the subleading term of
\gamma_{n}^{2}
is of order
n^{-2}
, and that the subleading term in
\beta_{n}
is a rather explicit function on the equilibrium density.

For our result on recurrence coefficients, we introduce the quantities

\kappa\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=\pi\mu_{V}([0,b])\qquad\text{and}\qquad G_{0}(\mathsf{s})\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\log\left(1+\ee^{-\mathsf{s}-x^{2}}\right)\,\mathrm{d}x=-\sqrt{\pi}\Li_{3/2}(-\ee^{-\mathsf{s}}),

and still use
\mathsf{T}
and
\mathsf{\lambda}_{\infty}
as defined earlier in (
1.10
).

Theorem 1.4

(Asymptotics for the recurrence coefficients)
.

For every
0<\varepsilon<1
, the expansions

(1.16)

\begin{split}\gamma_{n}(\mathsf{s})^{2}&=\gamma_{n}(\infty)^{2}+\frac{1}{n}\frac{\mathsf{T}}{\pi\phi_{V}(0)}\frac{b-a}{2}\mathsf{Q}(\mathsf{s})\cos\left(2n\kappa\right)+\mathcal{O}(n^{-2+\varepsilon}),\\
\beta_{n}(\mathsf{s})&=\beta_{n}(\infty)+\frac{1}{n}\frac{\mathsf{T}}{\pi\phi_{V}(0)}\left[\frac{a+b}{\sqrt{-ab}}\frac{G_{0}(\mathsf{s})}{2\pi}-\frac{2\mathsf{Q}(\mathsf{s})}{b-a}\left[(a+b)\cos(2n\kappa)+2\sqrt{-ab}\sin(2n\kappa)\right]\right]+\mathcal{O}(n^{-2+\varepsilon}),\end{split}

are valid as
n\to\infty
, where
\mathsf{Q}(\mathsf{s})=\mathsf{Q}(\mathsf{s},\mathsf{T})
is given by

(1.17)

\mathsf{Q}(\mathsf{s},\mathsf{T})\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=-\frac{1}{4\pi\mathrm{i}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\mathsf{\Phi}(\xi\mid\mathsf{s},\mathsf{T})^{2}\lambda_{\infty}^{\prime}(\mathsf{T}\xi)\mathrm{d}\xi.

Furthermore, the asymptotic decay

(1.18)

\mathsf{Q}(\mathsf{s})=\mathcal{O}(\ee^{-\mathsf{s}}),\quad\mathsf{s}\to+\infty,

holds true for any
\mathsf{T}>0
fixed.

By the very definition of the Polylog as a power series, it is immediate that
\mathsf{G}_{0}(\mathsf{s})=\mathcal{O}(\ee^{-\mathsf{s}})
as
\mathsf{s}\to+\infty
. Thus, from (
1.18
) we obtain that

\beta_{n}(\mathsf{s})=\beta_{n}(\infty)+\mathcal{O}(\ee^{-\mathsf{s}}),\quad\gamma_{n}^{2}(\mathsf{s})=\gamma_{n}^{2}(\infty)+\mathcal{O}(\ee^{-\mathsf{s}}),\quad\mathsf{s}\to+\infty.

In other words, each sequence of deformed recurrence coefficients converges to its correspondent in the ground process as
\mathsf{s}\to+\infty
.

The subleading terms in (
1.16
) involve several noteworthy terms. First of all, the parameters
\mathsf{s}
and
\mathsf{T}
are the sole terms that depend on the function
Q
used in the deformation (
1.6
) (recall also (
1.9
)).

Second, the term
G_{0}
albeit at first mysterious, is actually natural. The deformation
\sigma_{n}
does not change the equilibrium measure of the system, and as a consequence we have to account to it in subleading order terms throughout the analysis. As common, such subleading terms involve the construction of a so-called Szegő function, and
G_{0}
appears precisely due to this function.

Third, the subleading terms in (
1.16
) contain purely oscillatory factors
\cos(2n\kappa)
and
\sin(2n\kappa)
. The appearance of (quasi)-periodic terms in this type of asymptotic expansion is not uncommon. However, and as mentioned earlier, it is usually associated to either weights with discontinuities
[
26
]
or weights with multi-cut equilibrium measures
[
18
,
5
]
. Over here, however, we emphasize that our weight is analytic on a neighborhood of the real axis for any
n
fixed, and the underlying equilibrium measure is always one-cut and independent of the deformation
\sigma_{n}
. For each
x\in\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}
fixed, we indeed have that
\sigma_{n}(x)\to 1
pointwise; however
\sigma_{n}(0)=1/(1+\ee^{-\mathsf{s}})
. In other words, we may interpret that
\sigma_{n}
introduces a delta modification of the weight in the limit, and Theorem
1.4
is saying that this modification is already strong enough to generate oscillatory terms in the asymptotic expansion.

Last but not least, (
1.16
) involves the function
\mathsf{Q}
, which is given as a total weighted integral of the solution
\mathsf{\Phi}
to (
1.12
). Alternatively, the function
\mathsf{Q}
satisfies the PDE

(1.19)

\partial_{\mathsf{T}}\left(\frac{\partial_{\mathsf{T}}\partial_{\mathsf{s}}\mathsf{Q}}{2\mathsf{Q}}\right)=\partial_{\mathsf{s}}(\mathsf{Q}^{2})+1,

which provides a self-standing definition of
\mathsf{Q}
as the solution to this nonlinear equation, which is integrable. This function
\mathsf{Q}
also appeared in
[
14
]
. In this direction, the main contribution of Theorem
1.4
is in unraveling how precisely this function contributes to the asymptotics of recurrence coefficients, and in showing that nonlinear integrable systems may appear in this context even when the underlying equilibrium measure is one-cut regular or without the need of double-scaling limits.

From a broader perspective, one may view Theorems
1.3
and
1.4
as part of a broader program of describing critical scaling limits of finite-temperature like deformations of orthogonal polynomials, as initiated with critical (regular) edge scalings in
[
27
]
. At the level of finite temperature deformations of the universal limiting point processes of random matrix theory, such program has seen major developments in recent years

At the technical level, our approach relies on the characterization of OPs through a Riemann-Hilbert Problem

The remaining sections will be dedicated to obtain the proofs of Theorems

In Section

2.14
) to

(2.16)

\left[\left(\mathbf{I}-\frac{\mathbf{E}_{21}}{\lambda_{\infty}(\xi)}\right)\mathbf{\Phi}_{\infty}(\xi)^{-1}\mathbf{\Phi}_{\infty}(\zeta)\left(\mathbf{I}+\frac{\mathbf{E}_{21}}{\lambda_{\infty}(\zeta)}\right)\right]_{21,+}=\mathsf{\Phi}\left(\frac{\zeta}{\mathsf{T}}\right)\mathsf{\Phi}\left(-\frac{\xi}{\mathsf{T}}\right)-\mathsf{\Phi}\left(-\frac{\zeta}{\mathsf{T}}\right)\mathsf{\Phi}\left(\frac{\xi}{\mathsf{T}}\right).

By
[
14
, Theorem 2.1 and Corollary 5.2]
, the function
\mathsf{\Phi}=\mathsf{\Phi}(\zeta\mid\mathsf{T})
solves the nonlocal nonlinear equation

(2.17)

\displaystyle\partial_{\mathsf{T}}\mathsf{\Phi}(\zeta\mid\mathsf{T})=\mathrm{i}\zeta\mathsf{\Phi}(\zeta\mid\mathsf{T})+\left(\frac{1}{2\pi\mathrm{i}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\mathsf{\Phi}(\xi\mid\mathsf{T})^{2}\lambda_{\infty}^{\prime}\left(\mathsf{T}\xi\mid\mathsf{s}\right)\mathrm{d}\xi\right)\mathsf{\Phi}(-\zeta\mid\mathsf{T}),

\displaystyle\text{with}\quad\mathsf{\Phi}(\zeta\mid\mathsf{T})\sim\ee^{\mathrm{i}\mathsf{T}\zeta},\quad\zeta\to\pm\infty.

We note that we translated the equation from
[
14
]
to this equation using the relation (
2.11
). We stress that
{}^{\prime}=\partial_{\xi}
is the derivative with respect to the (spectral) variable of the RHP.

2.3.
The model problem: asymptotics I

We now analyze
\mathbf{\Phi}_{\infty}(\cdot\mid\mathsf{s})
as
\mathsf{s}\to+\infty
. As we will see, this RHP converges to the RHP
{\mathbf{\Phi}}_{\sin}
corresponding to the formal choice
\mathsf{s}=+\infty
, and which gives rise to the celebrated sine kernel.

We point out that
\mathbf{\Phi}_{\sin}
is explicitly constructed by

\mathbf{\Phi}_{\sin}(\zeta)\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=\begin{cases}\ee^{-\mathrm{i}\zeta\bm{\sigma}_{3}}\mathbf{U}^{\pm}(\mathbf{I}\pm\mathbf{E}_{21}),&\zeta\in\mathcal{S}^{\pm}_{1},\\
\ee^{-\mathrm{i}\zeta\bm{\sigma}_{3}}\mathbf{U}^{\pm},&\zeta\in\mathcal{S}^{\pm}_{0}\cup\mathcal{S}^{\pm}_{2}.\end{cases}

A direct calculation shows that

(2.18)

\frac{1}{2\mathrm{i}(u-v)}\left[\left(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{E}_{21}\right)\mathbf{\Phi}_{\sin}(v)^{-1}{\mathbf{\Phi}}_{\sin}(u)\left(\mathbf{I}+\mathbf{E}_{21}\right)\right]_{21}=\frac{\sin(u-v)}{u-v},

which justifies the use of the index
\sin
in
{\mathbf{\Phi}}_{\sin}
. With
\mathsf{\Phi}_{\sin}
being defined by (
2.15
) with
\mathbf{\Phi}_{\infty}=\mathbf{\Phi}_{\sin}
, the identities

\mathsf{\Phi}_{\sin}(\zeta)=\ee^{\mathrm{i}\mathsf{T}\zeta}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\partial_{\mathsf{T}}\mathsf{\Phi}(\zeta\mid\mathsf{T})=\mathrm{i}\zeta\mathsf{\Phi}(\zeta\mid\mathsf{T})

are trivial, so (
2.16
) and (
2.17
) hold with the choice
\lambda_{\infty}\equiv 1
.

To verify the claimed convergence, define

(2.19)

\mathbf{L}_{\mathsf{s}}(\zeta)\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=\mathbf{\Phi}_{\infty}(\zeta\mid\mathsf{s})\mathbf{\Phi}_{\sin}(\zeta)^{-1},\quad\zeta\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\Sigma_{\mathbf{\Phi}},\quad\lambda_{\mathsf{s}}(\zeta)\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=\frac{1}{1+\ee^{-\mathsf{s}-\mathsf{u}\zeta^{2}}}.

Proposition 2.8
.

The estimates

\|\mathbf{L}_{\mathsf{s},\pm}-\mathbf{I}\|_{L^{2}(\Sigma_{\mathbf{\Phi}})}=\mathcal{O}(\ee^{-\mathsf{s}}),\quad\|\mathbf{L}_{\mathsf{s}}-\mathbf{I}\|_{L^{\infty}\left(\mathbb{C}\setminus\Sigma_{\mathbf{\Phi}}\right)}=\mathcal{O}(\ee^{-\mathsf{s}}),

are valid as
\mathsf{s}\to+\infty
.

Proof.

Consider the identity

\mathbf{J}_{\mathbf{L}_{\mathsf{s}}}(\zeta)=\mathbf{\Phi}_{\sin,-}(\zeta)\mathbf{J}_{\mathbf{\Phi}_{\infty}}(\zeta)\mathbf{J}_{\mathbf{\Phi}_{\sin}}(\zeta)^{-1}\mathbf{\Phi}_{\sin,-}(\zeta)^{-1}.

For
\zeta\in\Sigma_{\mathbf{\Phi}}\setminus\mathbb{R}
, above takes the form

\begin{split}\mathbf{J}_{\mathbf{L}_{\mathsf{s}}}(\zeta)&=\mathbf{\Phi}_{\sin,-}(\zeta)\left(\mathbf{I}+\lambda_{\mathsf{s}}(\zeta)^{-1}\mathbf{E}_{21}\right)\left(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{E}_{21}\right)\mathbf{\Phi}_{\sin,-}(\zeta)^{-1}\\
&=\mathbf{I}+\left(\frac{1}{\lambda_{\mathsf{s}}(\zeta)}-1\right)\mathbf{\Phi}_{\sin,-}(\zeta)\mathbf{E}_{21}\mathbf{\Phi}_{\sin,-}(\zeta)^{-1}.\end{split}

For
\zeta\in\mathbb{R}
, it becomes

\begin{split}\mathbf{J}_{\mathbf{L}_{\mathsf{s}}}(\zeta)&=\mathbf{\Phi}_{\sin,-}(\zeta)\left(\lambda_{\mathsf{s}}(\zeta)\mathbf{E}_{12}-\frac{1}{\lambda_{\mathsf{s}}(\zeta)}\mathbf{E}_{21}\right)\left(-\mathbf{E}_{12}+\mathbf{E}_{21}\right)\mathbf{\Phi}_{\sin,-}(\zeta)^{-1}\\
&=\mathbf{I}+\left(\lambda_{\mathsf{s}}(\zeta)-1\right)\mathbf{\Phi}_{\sin,-}(\zeta)\mathbf{E}_{11}\mathbf{\Phi}_{\sin,-}(\zeta)^{-1}+\left(\frac{1}{\lambda_{\mathsf{s}}(\zeta)}-1\right)\mathbf{\Phi}_{\sin,-}(\zeta)\mathbf{E}_{22}\mathbf{\Phi}_{\sin,-}(\zeta)^{-1}\\
&=\mathbf{I}+\left(\frac{1}{\lambda_{\mathsf{s}}(\zeta)}-1\right)\left[\mathbf{\Phi}_{\sin,-}(\zeta)\mathbf{E}_{22}\mathbf{\Phi}_{\sin,-}(\zeta)^{-1}-\lambda_{\mathsf{s}}(\zeta)\mathbf{\Phi}_{\sin,-}(\zeta)\mathbf{E}_{11}\mathbf{\Phi}_{\sin,-}(\zeta)^{-1}\right].\end{split}

Now, from the definition of
\mathbf{\Phi}_{\sin}
we have, for
\pm\mathop{\rm Im}\zeta>0
,

\begin{split}\mathbf{\Phi}_{\sin,-}(\zeta)\mathbf{E}_{21}\mathbf{\Phi}_{\sin,-}(\zeta)^{-1}=\mathbf{U}^{\pm}\ee^{\mp\mathrm{i}\zeta\sigma_{3}}\mathbf{E}_{21}\ee^{\pm\mathrm{i}\zeta\sigma_{3}}(\mathbf{U}^{\pm})^{-1}=\ee^{\pm 2\mathrm{i}\zeta}\mathbf{U}^{\pm}\mathbf{E}_{21}(\mathbf{U}^{\pm})^{-1}.\end{split}

In particular,
\mathbf{\Phi}_{\sin,-}(\zeta)\mathbf{E}_{21}\mathbf{\Phi}_{\sin,-}(\zeta)^{-1}
is bounded for
\zeta\in\Sigma_{\mathbf{\Phi}}\setminus\mathbb{R}
. For
\zeta\in\mathbb{R}
we have

\mathbf{\Phi}_{\sin,-}(\zeta)\mathbf{E}_{jj}\mathbf{\Phi}_{\sin,-}(\zeta)^{-1}=\mathbf{U}^{-}\mathbf{E}_{jj}(\mathbf{U}^{-})^{-1}=\mathbf{E}_{3-j,3-j},\quad j=1,2.

In particular, the previous identities show that
\mathbf{\Phi}_{\sin,-}(\zeta)\mathbf{E}_{jj}\mathbf{\Phi}_{\sin,-}(\zeta)^{-1}
is bounded for
\zeta\in\mathbb{R}
. Thus, since
|\lambda_{\mathsf{s}}(\zeta)|\leq 2
for all
\mathsf{s}\in\mathbb{R}
and
\zeta\in\Sigma_{\mathbf{\Phi}}
, there exists
M>0
, independent of
\mathsf{s}
, such that

(2.20)

\|\mathbf{J}_{\mathbf{L}_{s}}(\zeta)-\mathbf{I}\|\leq M\ee^{-\mathsf{s}}|\ee^{-\mathsf{u}\zeta^{2}}|,\quad\zeta\in\Sigma_{\mathbf{\Phi}}.

Thus,

\|\mathbf{J}_{\mathbf{L}_{\mathsf{s}}}(\zeta)-\mathbf{I}\|_{L^{1}\cap L^{\infty}(\Sigma_{\mathbf{\Phi}})}=\mathcal{O}(\ee^{-\mathsf{s}}),\quad\mathsf{s}\to+\infty.

In other words, the jump of
\mathbf{L}_{\mathsf{s}}
converges in
L^{\infty}
and
L^{1}
(hence in
L^{2}
) to the identity matrix, and by the well-established small norm theory of RHPs (see for instance
[
17
, Section 7.5]
the result follows.
∎

As usual, from the proper decay of the jump matrix to the identity matrix, we now draw consequences for the solution to the RHP itself by a standard application of the small norm theory.

Theorem 2.9
.

The convergence

\mathbf{\Phi}_{\infty}(\zeta\mid\mathsf{s})=\mathbf{\Phi}_{\sin}(\zeta)\left(\mathbf{I}+\mathcal{O}(\ee^{-\mathsf{s}})\right),\quad\mathsf{s}\to+\infty,

holds uniformly
\zeta\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\Sigma_{\mathbf{\Phi}}
, including for boundary values along
\Sigma_{\mathbf{\Phi}}
.

Furthermore, the estimates

(2.21)

\mathsf{p}(\mathsf{s})=\mathcal{O}(\ee^{-\mathsf{s}}),\quad\mathsf{q}(\mathsf{s})=\mathcal{O}(\ee^{-\mathsf{s}}),\quad\mathsf{s}\to+\infty,

hold true, where
\mathsf{p}
and
\mathsf{q}
are as in (
2.12
).

Proof.

The convergence of
\mathbf{\Phi}_{\infty}
is just a rewriting of Proposition
2.8
and usual arguments. For the convergence of
\mathsf{p},\mathsf{q}
, we start with the perturbative expression

\displaystyle\mathbf{\Phi}_{\infty,1}=-\frac{1}{2\pi\mathrm{i}}\int_{\Sigma_{\mathbf{\Phi}}}(\mathbf{J}_{\mathbf{L}_{\mathsf{s}}}(\zeta)-\mathbf{I})\mathbf{L}_{\mathsf{s},-}(\zeta)\mathrm{d}\zeta=-\frac{1}{2\pi\mathrm{i}}\int_{\Sigma_{\mathbf{\Phi}}}(\mathbf{J}_{\mathbf{L}_{\mathsf{s}}}(\zeta)-\mathbf{I})\mathrm{d}\zeta+\mathcal{O}(\ee^{-\mathsf{s}}),

which follows again from the small norm theory of RHPs and (
2.19
). Now, inequality (
2.20
) gives

|\mathbf{\Phi}_{\infty,1}|\leq\frac{1}{2\pi}M\ee^{-\mathsf{s}}\int_{\Sigma_{\mathbf{\Phi}}}|\ee^{-\mathsf{u}\zeta^{2}}|\,\mathrm{d}\zeta+\mathcal{O}(\ee^{-\mathsf{s}}).

The result now follows from (
2.12
).
∎

2.4.
The model problem: asymptotics II

The main goal of this section is to prove Equation (
2.6
), that is

\mathbf{\Phi}_{n}(\zeta)\to\mathbf{\Phi}_{\infty}(\zeta),\quad n\to\infty.

For this purpose we define

\mathbf{L}_{n}(\zeta)\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=\mathbf{\Phi}_{n}(\zeta)\mathbf{\Phi}_{\infty}(\zeta)^{-1},

and, following the small norm theory, we next prove that
\mathbf{L}_{n}\to\mathbf{I}
in the appropriate sense.

Analogously to the approach used in Section
2.3
, we write

(2.22)

\mathbf{J}_{\mathbf{L}_{n}}(\zeta)=\mathbf{\Phi}_{\infty,-}(\zeta)\mathbf{J}_{\mathbf{\Phi}_{n}}(\zeta)\mathbf{J}_{\mathbf{\Phi}_{\infty}}(\zeta)^{-1}\mathbf{\Phi}_{\infty,-}(\zeta)^{-1}.

Expanding the right-hand side we find the identities

(2.23)

\mathbf{J}_{\mathbf{L}_{n}}(\zeta)-\mathbf{I}=\\
\begin{cases}\left(\frac{1}{\lambda_{n}(\zeta)}-\frac{1}{\lambda_{\infty}(\zeta)}\right)\mathbf{\Phi}_{\infty,-}(\zeta)\mathbf{E}_{21}\mathbf{\Phi}_{\infty,-}(\zeta)^{-1},\quad&\zeta\in\Sigma_{\mathbf{\Phi}}\setminus\mathbb{R},\\
\left(\frac{\lambda_{n}(\zeta)}{\lambda_{\infty}(\zeta)}-1\right)\mathbf{\Phi}_{\infty,-}(\zeta)\mathbf{E}_{11}\mathbf{\Phi}_{\infty,-}(\zeta)^{-1}+\left(\frac{\lambda_{\infty}(\zeta)}{\lambda_{n}(\zeta)}-1\right)\mathbf{\Phi}_{\infty,-}(\zeta)\mathbf{E}_{22}\mathbf{\Phi}_{\infty,-}(\zeta)^{-1},\quad&\zeta\in\mathbb{R}.\end{cases}

Next we establish the appropriate bounds that will allow us to prove that these jumps converge to the identity matrix in the appropriate sense.

Proposition 2.10
.

For every
0<\varepsilon<1
there exists
n_{0}\geq 0
and
M>0
such that the inequality

\left|\frac{1}{\lambda_{n}(\zeta)}-\frac{1}{\lambda_{\infty}(\zeta)}\right|\leq M\ee^{-\mathsf{s}}\frac{\ee^{-\mathsf{u}|\zeta|^{2}}}{n^{\varepsilon}}

holds for every
\zeta\in\Sigma_{\mathbf{\Phi}}
with
|\zeta|\leq n^{\frac{1-\varepsilon}{3}}
, every
\mathsf{s}\in\mathbb{R}
, and every
n>n_{0}
.

Proof.

Since
\mathsf{H}_{0}
is analytic on
D_{\delta}
,

\mathsf{H}_{0}\left(\frac{\zeta}{n}\right)=\mathsf{u}\zeta^{2}+\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{\zeta^{3}}{n}\right)

where the
\mathcal{O}
term is uniform for
|\zeta|\leq\frac{n\delta}{2}
. In particular, for
|\zeta|<n^{\frac{1-\varepsilon}{3}}<\frac{n\delta}{2}
,

\left|\frac{1}{\lambda_{n}(\zeta)}-\frac{1}{\lambda_{\infty}(\zeta)}\right|=\ee^{-\mathsf{s}}\left|\ee^{-n^{2}\mathsf{H}_{0}\left(\frac{\zeta}{n}\right)}-\ee^{-\mathsf{u}\zeta^{2}}\right|=\ee^{-\mathsf{s}}\left|\ee^{-\mathsf{u}\zeta^{2}}\right|\left|\ee^{\mathcal{O}\left(n^{-\varepsilon}\right)}-1\right|\leq\ee^{-\mathsf{s}}\ee^{-\mathsf{u}|\zeta|^{2}}\frac{M}{n^{\varepsilon}}

for some
M>0
, where we used that
0\leq\re(\zeta^{2})\leq|\zeta|^{2}
for
\zeta\in\Sigma_{\mathbf{\Phi}}
.
∎

Proposition 2.11
.

For some
\eta>0
, the following estimates holds for
\zeta\in\Sigma_{\mathbf{\Phi}}
with
|\zeta|\geq n^{\frac{1-\varepsilon}{3}}
:

\left|\frac{1}{\lambda_{n}(\zeta)}-\frac{1}{\lambda_{\infty}(\zeta)}\right|\leq 2\ee^{-\mathsf{s}}\ee^{-\eta\left(n^{\frac{2(1-\varepsilon)}{3}}+|\zeta^{2}|\right)}

Proof.

The triangle inequality gives the identity

\left|\frac{1}{\lambda_{n}(\zeta)}-\frac{1}{\lambda_{\infty}(\zeta)}\right|\leq\ee^{-\mathsf{s}}\left(\left|\ee^{-\mathsf{u}\zeta^{2}}\right|+\left|\ee^{-n^{2}\mathsf{H}_{0}\left(\frac{\zeta}{n}\right)}\right|\right).

Now, according to Lemma
2.3
,

\left|\ee^{-n^{2}\mathsf{H}_{0}(\zeta/n)}\right|=\ee^{-n^{2}\mathop{\rm Re}(\mathsf{H}_{0}(\zeta/n))}\leq\ee^{-\eta|\zeta|^{2}}.

Then one gets for
\zeta\in\Sigma_{\mathbf{\Phi}}
satisfying
|\zeta|\geq n^{\frac{1-\varepsilon}{3}}
,

\begin{split}\left|\frac{1}{\lambda_{n}(\zeta)}-\frac{1}{\lambda_{\infty}(\zeta)}\right|&\leq\ee^{-\mathsf{s}}\left(\ee^{-\mathsf{u}\mathop{\rm Re}(\zeta^{2})}+\ee^{-\eta|\zeta|^{2}}\right),\\
&\leq\ee^{-\mathsf{s}}\left(\ee^{-\mathsf{u}\alpha|\zeta|^{2}}\ee^{-\mathsf{u}\alpha n^{\frac{2(1-\varepsilon)}{3}}}+\ee^{-\frac{\eta}{2}|\zeta|^{2}}\ee^{-\frac{\eta}{2}n^{\frac{2(1-\varepsilon)}{3}}}\right),\end{split}

where
\alpha=\frac{1}{2}\cos(\frac{\pi}{4})
.
The result follows taking
\eta=\min(\mathsf{u}\alpha,\eta/2)
.
∎

Proposition 2.12
.

For
\zeta\in\mathbb{R}
, the inequalities

\left|\frac{\lambda_{n}(\zeta)}{\lambda_{\infty}(\zeta)}-1\right|\leq\left|\frac{1}{\lambda_{n}(\zeta)}-\frac{1}{\lambda_{\infty}(\zeta)}\right|\qquad\text{and}\qquad\left|\frac{\lambda_{\infty}(\zeta)}{\lambda_{n}(\zeta)}-1\right|\leq\left|\frac{1}{\lambda_{n}(\zeta)}-\frac{1}{\lambda_{\infty}(\zeta)}\right|

are valid.

Proof.

For
\zeta\in\mathbb{R}
, one has

\left|\frac{\lambda_{n}(\zeta)}{\lambda_{\infty}(\zeta)}-1\right|=\left|\lambda_{\infty}(\zeta)\right|\left|\frac{1}{\lambda_{n}(\zeta)}-\frac{1}{\lambda_{\infty}(\zeta)}\right|\leq\left|\frac{1}{\lambda_{n}(\zeta)}-\frac{1}{\lambda_{\infty}(\zeta)}\right|.

The inequality holds since
\lambda_{\infty}
is bounded by
1
on real line. The proof of the second estimate is analogous.

∎

Using the previous propositions, we are now able to apply the small norm theory of RHPs again.

Theorem 2.13
.

For every
0<\varepsilon<1
, the estimates

\|\mathbf{L}_{n,\pm}-\mathbf{I}\|_{L^{2}(\Sigma_{\mathbf{\Phi}})}=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{\ee^{-\mathsf{s}}}{n^{\varepsilon}}\right)\quad\text{and}\quad\|\mathbf{L}_{n}-\mathbf{I}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{C}\setminus\Sigma_{\mathbf{\Phi}})}=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{\ee^{-\mathsf{s}}}{n^{\varepsilon}}\right)

are valid as
n\to\infty
, for every
\mathsf{s}\in\mathbb{R}
.

Proof.

The proof consists in using the previous lemmas to study the expression for
\mathbf{L}_{n}-\mathbf{I}
given by Equation (
2.23
). The asymptotics of
\mathbf{\Phi}_{\infty}
shows that, for
|\zeta|
sufficiently large,
\zeta\in\Sigma_{\mathbf{\Phi}}
,
\pm\mathop{\rm Im}\zeta>0
,

\begin{split}\mathbf{\Phi}_{\infty,-}(\zeta)\mathbf{E}_{21}\mathbf{\Phi}_{\infty,-}(\zeta)^{-1}&=\left(\mathbf{I}+\mathcal{O}(\zeta^{-1})\right)\mathbf{U}^{\pm}\ee^{\mp\mathrm{i}\zeta\sigma_{3}}\mathbf{E}_{21}\ee^{\pm\mathrm{i}\zeta\sigma_{3}}(\mathbf{U}^{\pm})^{-1}\left(\mathbf{I}+\mathcal{O}(\zeta^{-1})\right)\\
&=\ee^{\pm\mathrm{i}\zeta}\left(\mathbf{I}+\mathcal{O}(\zeta^{-1})\right)\mathbf{U}^{\pm}\mathbf{E}_{21}(\mathbf{U}^{\pm})^{-1}\left(\mathbf{I}+\mathcal{O}(\zeta^{-1})\right)\end{split}

On the other hand, for
\zeta\in\mathbb{R}
,
|\zeta|
sufficiently large,

\begin{split}\mathbf{\Phi}_{\infty,-}(\zeta)\mathbf{E}_{jj}\mathbf{\Phi}_{\infty,-}(\zeta)^{-1}&=\left(\mathbf{I}+\mathcal{O}(\zeta^{-1})\right)\mathbf{U}^{\pm}\mathbf{E}_{jj}(\mathbf{U}^{\pm})^{-1}\left(\mathbf{I}+\mathcal{O}(\zeta^{-1})\right)\end{split}

Identities above and Propositions
2.10
,
2.11
and
2.12
imply that there exists
M>0
for which

\left|\mathbf{J}_{\mathbf{L}_{n}}(\zeta)-\mathbf{I}\right|\leq M\ee^{-\mathsf{s}}\times\begin{cases}\frac{\ee^{-\mathsf{u}|\zeta|^{2}}}{n^{\varepsilon}},\quad&\zeta\in\Sigma_{\mathbf{\Phi}},|\zeta|\leq n^{\frac{1-\varepsilon}{3}},\\
2\ee^{-\eta\left(n^{\frac{2(1-\varepsilon)}{3}}+|\zeta|^{2}\right)},\quad&\zeta\in\Sigma_{\mathbf{\Phi}},|\zeta|\geq n^{\frac{1-\varepsilon}{3}}.\\
\end{cases}

Thus,

(2.24)

\|\mathbf{J}_{\mathbf{L}_{n}}-\mathbf{I}\|_{L^{1}\cap L^{\infty}(\Sigma_{\mathbf{\Phi}})}\leq\widehat{M}\ee^{-\mathsf{s}}\left[\frac{1}{n^{\varepsilon}}\left\|\ee^{-\mathsf{u}|\zeta|^{2}}\right\|_{L^{1}\cap L^{\infty}(\Sigma_{\mathbf{\Phi}})}+2\ee^{-\eta n^{\frac{2(1-\varepsilon)}{3}}}\left\|\ee^{-\eta|\zeta|^{2}}\right\|_{L^{1}\cap L^{\infty}(\Sigma_{\mathbf{\Phi}})}\right].

The result now follows from the standard small norm theory for RHPs.
∎

We are finally able to conclude that the model problem RHP
2.1
with admissible data has a solution, and that in fact it is comparable to the one obtained from
\mathsf{H}_{\infty}
as previously claimed.

Theorem 2.14
.

Fix
\mathsf{s}_{0}\in\mathbb{R}
. For every
0<\varepsilon<1
, there exists
n_{0}>0
such that the solution
\mathbf{\Phi}_{n}
uniquely exists for
n>n_{0}
and
\mathsf{s}\geq\mathsf{s}_{0}
. As
n\to\infty
,

(2.25)

\mathbf{\Phi}_{n}(\zeta\mid\mathsf{s})=\mathbf{\Phi}_{\infty}(\zeta\mid\mathsf{s})\left(\mathbf{I}+\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{\ee^{-\mathsf{s}}}{n^{\varepsilon}}\right)\right)\quad\text{in}\quad L^{\infty}(\mathbb{C}\setminus\Sigma_{\mathbf{\Phi}}),

and the identity extends to boundary values
\mathbf{\Phi}_{n,\pm}
and
\mathbf{\Phi}_{\infty,\pm}
on
\Sigma_{\mathbf{\Phi}}
. Moreover,

(2.26)

\mathbf{\Phi}_{n,1}=\mathbf{\Phi}_{\infty,1}+\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{\ee^{-\mathsf{s}}}{n^{\varepsilon}}\right),\quad n\to+\infty.

Proof.

This is proven by arguments similar to the ones made for Theorem
2.9
, this time using Theorem
2.13
and the inequality (
2.24
). We omit the details for brevity.
∎

Remark 2.15
.

For each
n
fixed, the solution
\mathbf{\Phi}_{n}
is bounded for
\zeta
in compacts, and there exists a sufficiently large
R=R_{n}>0
such that the asymptotics given in (
2.3
) holds true for
|\zeta|\geq R_{n}
. Now, thanks to the convergence given by Theorem
2.14
, such
R
may in fact be chosen to be independent of
n
: simply choose an
R=R_{\infty}>0
for which the expansion (
2.3
) is valid for
|\zeta|\geq R_{\infty}
for the choice
\mathbf{\Phi}=\mathbf{\Phi}_{\infty}
.

As a consequence, we in fact obtain the mild bound

\mathbf{\Phi}_{n}(\zeta)=\mathcal{O}(1)\ee^{\mp\mathrm{i}\zeta\bm{\sigma}_{3}},

which is uniform in
n
,
\mathsf{s}\geq\mathsf{s}_{0}
and also uniform in
\zeta\in\mathbb{C}
, also holding for the boundary values at
\Sigma_{\mathbf{\Phi}}
. In particular we see that both
\mathbf{\Phi}_{n}
and
\mathbf{\Phi}_{\infty}
are bounded in horizontal lines.

3.
Asymptotic Analysis of the RHP for OPs

In this section, we move on to the asymptotic analysis of the OPs for the weight (
1.5
). As usual, this analysis is done using the Deift-Zhou’s nonlinear steepest descent method applied to the Fokas-Its-Kitaev RHP characterization of OPs. The main difference compared to the classical undeformed case is the necessity of a local analysis around the origin, due to the accumulation of poles of
\sigma_{n}
when
n
grows large. The construction of this local parametrix will make use of the model problem from Section
2
, which will be a key element in the conclusion of the proofs of our main results in Section
4
.

3.1.
Equilibrium measures and related quantities

The first ingredient we will need in the coming analysis is the equilibrium measure for the weight
V
, and we now collect known results about it in the form that will be needed later. Such results are standard
[
36
,
19
]
, and here we follow closely the notation and language of
[
31
]
.

The equilibrium measure
\mathrm{d}\mu_{V}(x)=\phi_{V}(x)\,\mathrm{d}x
is the unique minimizer to the energy functional

\iint\log\frac{1}{|x-y|}\,\mathrm{d}\mu(x)\,\mathrm{d}\mu(y)+\int V(x)\,\mathrm{d}\mu(x)

over all probability measures
\mu
supported on
\mathbb{R}
for which
V
is integrable. For
V
a polynomial as in Assumption
1.1
, this measure uniquely exists, it is absolutely continuous with respect to the Lebesgue measure with a continuous density
\phi_{V}
, and its support consists of finitely many compact intervals
[
19
]
. The one-cut assumption in Assumption
1.1
means that we assume
\mu_{V}
to have connected support, say
\supp\mu_{V}=[a,b]
, and Assumption
1.1
also says that
a<0<b
with
\phi_{V}(0)>0
.

With

U^{\mu_{V}}(z)\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=\int\log\frac{1}{|z-y|}\mathrm{d}\mu(y),\quad z\in\mathbb{C},

being the logarithmic potential of
\mu_{V}
, the measure
\mu_{V}
is uniquely characterized by the
Euler-Lagrange identities

(3.1)

\begin{split}2U^{\mu_{V}}(x)+V(x)&=\ell,\quad x\in[a,b],\\
2U^{\mu_{V}}(x)+V(x)&>\ell,\quad x\in\mathbb{R}\setminus[a,b].\end{split}

In general, the properties above are understood in the quasi-everywhere sense and with weak inequalities
[
36
]
, but the regularity condition in Assumption
1.1
ensures that these properties are true as stated.

The complexified logarithmic potential (also known as
\mathsf{g}
-function
)

(3.2)

\mathsf{g}(z)\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=\int\log(z-w)\,\mathrm{d}\mu_{V}(w)

is an analytic function on
\mathbb{C}\setminus(-\infty,b]
that admits continuous jumps on
(-\infty,b)
related by the identities

\begin{split}\mathsf{g}_{+}(x)-\mathsf{g}_{-}(x)&=2\pi\mathrm{i}\mu_{V}([x,b]),\\
\mathsf{g}_{+}(x)+\mathsf{g}_{-}(x)&=-2U^{\mu_{V}}(x).\end{split}

In particular, the Euler-Lagrange identities
imply that

\begin{split}\mathsf{g}_{+}(x)+\mathsf{g}_{-}(x)-V(x)+\ell&=0,\quad x\in[a,b],\\
\mathsf{g}_{+}(x)+\mathsf{g}_{-}(x)-V(x)+\ell&<0,\quad x\in\mathbb{R}\setminus[a,b].\end{split}

Let

C^{\mu}(z)\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=\int\frac{\mathrm{d}\mu_{V}(w)}{w-z},\quad z\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\supp\mu_{V},

be the Cauchy transform of the measure
\mu_{V}
. It satisfies the identity

(3.3)

\left(C^{\mu_{V}}(z)+\frac{V^{\prime}(z)}{2}\right)^{2}=(z-a)(z-b)q(z)^{2},\quad z\in\mathbb{C},

where
q(z)
is a polynomial of degree
\deg V-2
with
q(a),q(b)\neq 0
and
q(x)>0
for
x\in(a,b)
.
Moreover, Stieltjes Inversion Theorem allows the recovery of the density of
\mu_{V}
from the identity above, namely as

\phi_{V}(x)=\frac{1}{\pi}\sqrt{(x-a)(b-x)}q(x),\quad x\in(a,b).

We will also need certain functions constructed locally from
C^{\mu_{V}}
, the so-called
\phi
-functions. First of all, the
\phi_{b}
is defined by

(3.4)

\phi_{b}(z)\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=\int_{b}^{z}\left(C^{\mu_{V}}(x)+\frac{V^{\prime}(x)}{2}\right)\,\mathrm{d}x,\quad z\in\mathbb{C}\setminus(-\infty,b].

For
x\in(-\infty,b)
,
\phi_{b}
admits continuous boundary values

(3.5)

\phi_{b,\pm}(x)=\mp\int_{x}^{b}((s-a)(s-b))^{\frac{1}{2}}_{+}q(s)\,\mathrm{d}s=\mp\pi\mathrm{i}\mu_{V}([x,b]),

which are related by the following identities

(3.6)

\mathsf{a}_{k}\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=\frac{(-1)^{k+1}\Gamma\left(3k+\frac{1}{2}\right)}{36^{k}k!\Gamma\left(k+\frac{1}{2}\right)(6k-1)},\quad\mathbf{A}_{k}\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=\mathsf{a}_{k}\times\begin{cases}\left(\mathbf{I}+6k\bm{\sigma}_{2}\right),\quad&k\text{ even},\\
\left(\bm{\sigma}_{3}+6\mathrm{i}\bm{\sigma}_{1}\right),\quad&k\text{ odd}.\end{cases}

(iv)

\mathbf{A}
remains bounded as
\zeta\to 0
along
\zeta\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\Sigma_{\mathbf{A}}
.

The solution
\mathbf{A}
itself is given in terms of Airy functions in a canonical construction, namely

\mathbf{A}(\zeta)\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=\begin{pmatrix}\ai(\zeta)&-\ee^{4\pi\mathrm{i}/4}\ai(\ee^{4\pi\mathrm{i}/3}\zeta)\\
-\mathrm{i}\ai^{\prime}(\zeta)&\mathrm{i}\ee^{2\pi\mathrm{i}/4}\ai^{\prime}(\ee^{4\pi\mathrm{i}/3}\zeta)\end{pmatrix}\times\begin{cases}\mathbf{I},&0<\arg\zeta<\frac{2\pi}{3},\\
(\mathbf{I}+\mathbf{E}_{21}),&\frac{2\pi}{3}<\arg\zeta<\pi,\\
(\mathbf{I}+\mathbf{E}_{12}),&-\frac{2\pi}{3}<\arg\zeta<0,\\
(\mathbf{I}+\mathbf{E}_{12})(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{E}_{21}),&-\pi<\arg\zeta<-\frac{2\pi}{3}.\\
\end{cases}

For some
\delta>0
, which will be made sufficiently small as needed, let us set

U_{b}=U_{b,\delta}\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=\{z\in\mathbb{C}\mid|z-b|<\delta\}.

The local parametrix at
b
is the solution of the following RHP.

RHP 3.10
.

Find a matrix-valued function
\mathbf{P}_{b}:U_{b,\delta}\setminus\Gamma_{\mathbf{S}}\to\mathbb{C}^{2\times 2}
that satisfies the following:

(i)

\mathbf{P}_{b}
is analytic and admits a continuous extension to
\overline{U_{b,\delta}}\setminus\Gamma_{\mathbf{S}}
;

(ii)

The matrix
\mathbf{P}_{b}
has continuous boundary values
\mathbf{P}_{b,\pm}
along
U_{b,\delta}\cap\Gamma_{\mathbf{S}}
, related by the identity
\mathbf{P}_{b,+}(x)=\mathbf{P}_{b,-}(x)\mathbf{J}_{\mathbf{P}_{b}}(x)
, where

\mathbf{J}_{\mathbf{P}_{b}}(x)=\mathbf{J}_{\mathbf{S}}(x)=\begin{cases}\sigma_{n}(x)\mathbf{E}_{12}-\frac{1}{\sigma_{n}(x)}\mathbf{E}_{21},\quad&x\in(b-\delta,b),\\
\mathbf{I}+\sigma_{n}(x)\ee^{-2n\phi_{b}(x)}\mathbf{E}_{12},\quad&x\in(b,b+\delta),\\
\mathbf{I}+\frac{\ee^{2n\phi_{b}(x)}}{\sigma_{n}(x)}\mathbf{E}_{21},\quad x\in U_{b,\delta}\cap\Gamma_{\mathbf{S}}^{\pm}.\end{cases}

(iii)

As
n\to\infty
,

(3.24)

\mathbf{P}_{b}(z)=\left(\mathbf{I}+\mathit{o}(1)\right)\mathbf{G}(z)

uniformly on
\partial U_{b,\delta}
.

(iv)

\mathbf{P}_{b}(z)
remains bounded as
z\to b
.

The solution of this RHP is given in terms of the map

\varphi_{b}(z)\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=\left(\frac{3}{2}\phi_{b}(z)\right)^{\frac{2}{3}}.

The construction of
\phi_{b}
in terms of the equilibrium measure - which is regular - shows that
\varphi_{b}
is conformal and maps
(b-\delta,b)
to an interval contained in

. We assume that the lens were chosen in such a way that

maps

As usual, one maps RHP
3.10

\mathbf{P}_{b}(z)=\mathbf{E}_{b}(z)\mathbf{A}\left(n^{\frac{2}{3}}\varphi_{b}(z)\right)\ee^{(n\phi_{b}(z)-\frac{1}{2}\log\sigma_{n}(z))\bm{\sigma}_{3}},\quad\mathbf{E}_{b}(z)\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=\mathbf{G}(z)\ee^{\frac{1}{2}\log\sigma_{n}(z)\bm{\sigma}_{3}}\mathbf{U}_{0}^{-1}\left(n^{\frac{2}{3}}\varphi_{b}(z)\right)^{\frac{\bm{\sigma}_{3}}{4}}.

One can plug the asymptotic of
\mathbf{A}
from Equation (
3.23
) in above identity to obtain more precise information than (
3.24
). As
\sigma_{n}(z)=\mathbf{I}+\mathcal{O}\left(\ee^{-n^{2}\eta}\right)
, for
z
near
b
and some
\eta>0
, and
\ee^{\mathsf{h}\bm{\sigma}_{3}}=\mathbf{I}+\mathcal{O}(n^{-1})

(3.25)

\mathbf{P}_{b}(z)=\mathbf{G}(z)\sigma_{n}(z)^{\frac{\bm{\sigma}_{3}}{2}}\left(\mathbf{I}+\mathcal{O}(n^{-1})\right)\sigma_{n}(z)^{-\frac{\bm{\sigma}_{3}}{2}}=\left(\mathbf{I}+\mathcal{O}(n^{-1})\right)\mathbf{G}(z),

where the
\mathcal{O}(n)
term is uniform for
z\in\partial U_{b}
, for
\mathsf{s}\geq\mathsf{s}_{0}
for any
\mathsf{s}_{0}\in\mathbb{R}
fixed, and also uniform for
\mathsf{t}
in compacts of
(0,+\infty)
.

The analysis for the local parametrix at
a
follows analogously. The local parametrix itself is the solution to the following RHP.

RHP 3.11
.

Find a matrix-valued function
\mathbf{P}_{a}:U_{a,\delta}\setminus\Gamma_{\mathbf{S}}\to\mathbb{C}^{2\times 2}
satisfying:

(i)

\mathbf{P}_{a}
is analytic and admits a continuous extension to
\overline{U_{a,\delta}}\setminus\Gamma_{\mathbf{S}}
.

(ii)

The matrix
\mathbf{P}_{a}
admits continuous boundary values
\mathbf{P}_{\pm}
along

, related by
\mathbf{P}_{a,+}(x)=\mathbf{P}_{a,-}(x)\mathbf{J}_{\mathbf{P}_{a}}(x)
, where

\mathbf{J}_{\mathbf{P}_{a}}(x)\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=\mathbf{J}_{\mathbf{S}}(x),\quad x\in U_{a,\delta}\cap\Gamma_{\mathbf{S}}\setminus\{a\}.

(iii)

As
n\to\infty,

\mathbf{P}_{a}(z)=\left(\mathbf{I}+\mathit{o}(1)\right)\mathbf{G}(z)

uniformly for
z\in\partial U_{a,\delta}
;

(iv)

\mathbf{P}_{a}(z)

For

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}_{a}(z)=\mathbf{E}_{a}(z)\mathbf{A}\left(n^{\frac{2}{3}}\varphi_{a}(z)\right)\ee^{(n\phi_{a}(z)-\frac{1}{2}\log\sigma_{n}(z))\bm{\sigma}_{3}}\bm{\sigma}_{3},\qquad\text{with}

\displaystyle{\mathbf{E}}_{a}(z)\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=\mathbf{G}(z)\ee^{\frac{1}{2}\log\sigma_{n}(x)\bm{\sigma}_{3}}\bm{\sigma}_{3}\mathbf{U}_{0}^{-1}\left(n^{\frac{2}{3}}\varphi_{a}(z)\right)^{\frac{\bm{\sigma}_{3}}{4}}.

The precise asymptotics on

is

(3.26)

\mathbf{P}_{a}(z)=\left(\mathbf{I}+\mathcal{O}(n^{-1})\right)\mathbf{G}(z),\quad n\to\infty,

where the
\mathcal{O}(n^{-1})
term is uniform for

on

\mathsf{s}\geq\mathsf{s}_{0}
for any

\mathsf{t}

.

3.7.
The local parametrix near the origin through the model problem

Fix a neighborhood
U_{0}
of the origin, which will be taken appropriately small as needed along the way. Recall that we have not opened lenses around
x=0
, and instead have kept it as a fixed point in the opening of lenses process, see for instance Figure
2
. In order to cope with the remaining jumps of
\mathbf{S}
near
x=0
which are not uniformly decaying to the identity, we also need to construct a local parametrix at
x=0
. Concretely, this local parametrix is the solution to the following RHP.

RHP 3.12
.

Find a matrix-valued function
\mathbf{P}_{0}:U_{0}\setminus\Gamma_{\mathbf{S}}\to\mathbb{C}^{2\times 2}
that satisfies the following conditions:

(i)

\mathbf{P}_{0}
is analytic on
U_{0}\setminus\Gamma_{\mathbf{S}}
and admits a continuous extension to
\overline{U_{0}}\setminus\Gamma_{\mathbf{S}}
.

(ii)

The matrix
\mathbf{P}_{0}
has continuous boundary values
\mathbf{P}_{0,\pm}
along
U_{0}\cap\Gamma_{\mathbf{S}}
, and they are related by the identity
\mathbf{P}_{0,+}(x)=\mathbf{P}_{0,-}(x)\mathbf{J}_{\mathbf{S}}(x)
, where
\mathbf{J}_{\mathbf{S}}
is as in (
3.13
).

(iii)

As
n\to\infty
,

\mathbf{P}_{0}(z)=\left(\mathbf{I}+\mathcal{O}(n^{-1})\right)\mathbf{G}(z),

uniformly for
z\in\partial U_{0}
, and uniformly for
\mathsf{s}\geq\mathsf{s}_{0}
, for any
\mathsf{s}_{0}\in\mathbb{R}
fixed.

(iv)

\mathbf{P}_{0}(z)
remains bounded as
z\to 0
.

As usual in RHP literature, we construct the solution to this problem mapping it to a model RHP, in this case the model problem for
\mathbf{\Phi}
from Section
2
. This shall be done with the help of the conformal map
\varphi_{0}
defined in (
3.10
): we introduce,

(3.27)

\mathsf{H}_{0}(\zeta)\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=Q\left(\varphi_{0}^{-1}\left(\frac{\zeta}{n}\right)\right),\quad\mathsf{H}(\zeta)=\mathsf{H}_{n}(\zeta)\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=\mathsf{s}+n^{2}\mathsf{H}_{0}\left(\frac{\zeta}{n}\right),\quad\mathsf{u}\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=\frac{\mathsf{t}}{\varphi_{0}^{\prime}(0)^{2}},\quad\mathsf{H}_{\infty}(\zeta)\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=\mathsf{s}+\mathsf{u}\zeta^{2}.

Observe that
\mathsf{H}
is an admissible function in the sense of Definition
2.2
. Thus, we may consider the corresponding solution to the model problem

(3.28)

\mathbf{\Phi}_{n}(\zeta)\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=\mathbf{\Phi}(\zeta\mid\mathsf{H}=\mathsf{H}_{n}),\quad\text{with the correspondence}\quad\sigma_{n}(z)=\lambda_{n}(\zeta),\;\zeta=n\varphi_{0}(z),

and we use it to construct
\mathbf{P}_{0}
as

(3.29)

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}_{0}(z)\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=\mathbf{E}(z)\mathbf{\Phi}_{n}(n\varphi_{0}(z))\ee^{n\phi_{b}(z)\bm{\sigma}_{3}},\quad z\in U_{0}\setminus\Gamma,\quad\text{with}

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}(z)\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=\ee^{-\mathsf{h}_{0}\bm{\sigma}_{3}}\mathbf{M}(z)\ee^{\pm\mathrm{i}n\kappa\bm{\sigma}_{3}}(\mathbf{U}^{\pm})^{-1},\;\pm\im z>0.

At this point, we assume that the lenses were opened in such a way that
\varphi_{0}(z)
maps the lens segments to segments of
\Sigma_{\mathbf{\Phi}}\setminus\mathbb{R}
defined in Section
2
. Of course, as
n\to\infty
, the image of the lenses by
n\phi_{0}(z)
will be “filling out”
\Sigma_{\mathbf{\Phi}}\setminus\mathbb{R}
.

Theorem 3.13
.

The matrix-valued function
\mathbf{P}_{0}(z)
defined in (
3.29
) is a solution to the RHP
3.12
.

Proof.

First of all, notice that
\mathbf{E}
is analytic near the origin. Indeed, a direct calculation shows that the jump of
\mathbf{E}
across any interval of the form
(-\delta,\delta)\setminus\{0\}
with
\delta>0
sufficiently small is

\mathbf{E}_{-}(z)^{-1}\mathbf{E}_{+}(z)=\mathbf{U}^{-}\ee^{\mathrm{i}n\kappa\bm{\sigma}_{3}}\mathbf{J}_{\mathbf{M}}(z)\ee^{\mathrm{i}n\kappa\bm{\sigma}_{3}}\mathbf{U}^{+}=\mathbf{U}^{-}(\mathbf{E}_{12}-\mathbf{E}_{21})=\mathbf{I}.

This shows that
\mathbf{E}
has an isolated singularity at
z=0
. From the very definition of
\mathbf{E}
we also know that it remains bounded as
z\to 0
. Thus,
\mathbf{E}
is indeed analytic as claimed.

From this, one concludes that the jump of
\mathbf{P}_{0}
is given by

\mathbf{J}_{\mathbf{P}_{0}}(z)=\ee^{-n\phi_{b,-}(z)\bm{\sigma}_{3}}\mathbf{J}_{\mathbf{\Phi}_{n}}(n\varphi_{0}(z))\ee^{n\phi_{b,+}(z)\bm{\sigma}_{3}}=\mathbf{J}_{\mathbf{S}}(z),

where we use that
\phi_{b,-}(z)+\phi_{b,+}(z)=0,\ z\in(a,b)
.
Moreover, for
z\in\partial U_{0}
and as
n\to\infty
, we see that
\zeta=n\varphi_{0}(z)\to\infty
, and from the asymptotics of
\mathbf{\Phi}_{n}
given in RHP
2.1
–(iii) we obtain

\mathbf{P}_{0}(z)=\ee^{-\mathsf{h}_{0}\bm{\sigma}_{3}}\mathbf{M}(z)\ee^{\pm\mathrm{i}n\kappa\bm{\sigma}_{3}}(\mathbf{U}^{\pm})^{-1}(\mathbf{I}+\mathcal{O}(n^{-1}))\mathbf{U}^{\pm}\ee^{\mp\mathrm{i}\zeta\bm{\sigma}_{3}}\ee^{n\phi_{b}(z)\bm{\sigma}_{3}}.

Now, from the definition of
\varphi_{0}
in (
3.10
) we obtain that
\mp\mathrm{i}\zeta+n\phi_{b}(z)=\mp\mathrm{i}n\kappa
, and the identity above updates to

\begin{split}\mathbf{P}_{0}(z)&=\ee^{-\mathsf{h}_{0}\bm{\sigma}_{3}}\mathbf{M}(z)\ee^{\pm\mathrm{i}n\kappa\bm{\sigma}_{3}}(\mathbf{U}^{\pm})^{-1}(\mathbf{I}+\mathcal{O}(n^{-1}))\mathbf{U}^{\pm}\ee^{\mp\mathrm{i}n\kappa\bm{\sigma}_{3}}\\
&=\ee^{-\mathsf{h}_{0}\bm{\sigma}_{3}}\mathbf{M}(z)\left(\mathbf{I}+\mathcal{O}(n^{-1})\right)=\mathbf{G}(z)\ee^{-\mathsf{h}(z)\bm{\sigma}_{3}}\left(\mathbf{I}+\mathcal{O}(n^{-1})\right),\end{split}

where in the second step we used that
\mathbf{U}^{\pm}
and
\ee^{\mp\mathrm{i}n\kappa\bm{\sigma}_{3}}
are bounded. RHP
3.12
–(iii) now follows observing that
\mathbf{M}
is bounded too for
z
near the origin, and also using (
3.21
).
∎

For later convenience, we state a mild bound of
\mathbf{P}_{0}
on a full real neighborhood of the origin.

Lemma 3.14
.

Both
\mathbf{P}_{0,+}(x)
and
\mathbf{P}_{0,+}(x)^{-1}
remain bounded as
n\to\infty
, uniformly for
x\in U_{0}\cap\mathbb{R}
and uniformly for
\mathsf{s}\geq s_{0}
, for any
\mathsf{s}_{0}\in\mathbb{R}
.

Proof.

We start by using the uniform convergence given by Theorem
2.14
for
\zeta=n\varphi_{0}(x)
to write

\mathbf{P}_{0,+}(x)=\mathbf{E}_{n}(x)\mathbf{\Phi}_{\infty,+}(n\varphi_{0}(x))\ee^{n\phi_{b,+}(z)\bm{\sigma}_{3}}+\mathcal{O}(\ee^{-\mathsf{s}}n^{-\varepsilon}),

where we used that
\mathbf{E}_{n}(z)
is bounded around
z=0
and that
\phi_{b,+}(x)
is purely imaginary for
x\in(-\infty,b)
.

By Remark
2.15
,
\mathbf{\Phi}_{\infty,+}(\zeta)
is bounded for
\zeta\in\mathbb{R}
. Since
n\varphi_{0}(x)\in\mathbb{R}
for every
x\in U_{0}\cap\mathbb{R}
, the result follows.
∎

3.8.
Final transformation and small norm theory

For the final transformation, let us set

U\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=U_{a}\cup U_{b}\cup U_{0},\quad\Gamma_{\mathbf{R}}\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=(\Gamma_{\mathbf{S}}\cup\partial U)\setminus(U\cup[a,b]),

where we recall that
\Gamma_{\mathbf{S}}
is the jump contour for
\mathbf{S}
(see Figure
2
),
and introduce

(3.30)

\mathbf{P}(z)\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=\begin{cases}\mathbf{P}_{0}(z),&z\in U_{0},\\
\mathbf{P}_{a}(z),&z\in U_{a},\\
\mathbf{P}_{b}(z),&z\in U_{b}.\end{cases}

The final transformation then takes the form

\mathbf{R}(z)\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=\begin{cases}\mathbf{S}(z)\mathbf{P}(z)^{-1},\quad&z\in U\setminus\Gamma,\\
\mathbf{S}(z)\mathbf{G}(z)^{-1},&\text{elsewhere on }\mathbb{C}\setminus\Gamma_{\mathbf{R}}.\end{cases}

Both
\mathbf{S}
and
\mathbf{G}
have the same jumps on
(a,b)
. Likewise, both
\mathbf{S}
and
\mathbf{P}_{j}
,
j=0,a,b
, have the same jumps inside
U_{j}
. These jumps cancel one another in the construction of
\mathbf{R}
, so that
\mathbf{R}
is analytic across
[a,b]\cup(\Gamma_{\mathbf{S}}\cap U)
, and therefore it has jumps precisely across
\Gamma_{\mathbf{R}}
.

As a consequence, we obtain that
\mathbf{R}
satisfies the following RHP.

RHP 3.15
.

Seek for a matrix-valued function
\mathbf{R}:\mathbb{C}\setminus\Gamma_{\mathbf{R}}\to\mathbb{C}^{2\times 2}
satisfying the following.

(i)

\mathbf{R}
is analytic.

(ii)

\mathbf{R}
has continuous boundary values
\mathbf{R}_{\pm}
along
\Gamma_{\mathbf{R}}
, that are related by
\mathbf{R}_{+}(z)=\mathbf{R}_{-}(z)\mathbf{J}_{\mathbf{R}}(z)
, where

(3.31)

\mathbf{J}_{\mathbf{R}}(z)\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=\begin{cases}\mathbf{G}(z)\mathbf{J}_{\mathbf{S}}(z)\mathbf{G}(z)^{-1},&z\in\Gamma_{\mathbf{R}}\setminus\partial U,\\
\mathbf{P}(z)\mathbf{G}(z)^{-1},&z\in\partial U.\end{cases}

(iii)

As
z\to\infty
,
\mathbf{R}
behaves as

\mathbf{R}(z)=\mathbf{I}+\mathcal{O}\left(z^{-1}\right)

To conclude the asymptotic analysis, we follow the usual small-norm theory path, and now prove that the jump matrix for
\mathbf{R}
is asymptotically close to the identity matrix. We do it in the two separate lemmas that follow.

Lemma 3.16
.

The estimate

\|\mathbf{J}_{\mathbf{R}}-\mathbf{I}\|_{L^{1}\cap L^{\infty}(\partial U)}=\mathcal{O}(n^{-1}),\quad n\to\infty,

is valid uniformly for
\mathsf{s}\geq\mathsf{s}_{0}
, for any
\mathsf{s}_{0}\in\mathbb{R}
fixed, and uniformly for
\mathsf{t}
in compacts of
(0,+\infty)
.

Proof.

The claim on the
L^{\infty}
norm follows from the definition of
\mathbf{P}
in (
3.30
) and the asymptotics in (
3.25
), (
3.26
) and RHP
3.12
. The claim on the
L^{1}
norm then follows from the
L^{\infty}
norm simply because
\partial U
is a bounded set.
∎

Lemma 3.17
.

Given
\mathsf{s}_{0}\in\mathbb{R}
, there exists
\eta>0
such that the estimate

\|\mathbf{J}_{\mathbf{R}}-\mathbf{I}\|_{L^{1}\cap L^{\infty}(\Gamma_{\mathbf{R}}\setminus\partial U)}=\mathcal{O}(\ee^{-\eta n}),\quad n\to\infty,

is valid uniformly for
\mathsf{s}\geq\mathsf{s}_{0}
, and also uniformly for
\mathsf{t}
in compacts of
(0,+\infty)
.

Proof.

The claim follows from Proposition
3.6
and the fact that
\mathbf{G}
is bounded on compact subsets of
\mathbb{C}\setminus[a,b]
, we skip details.
∎

As a consequence, we finally conclude the small norm theory for
\mathbf{R}
.

Theorem 3.18
.

The estimate

\|\mathbf{R}-\mathbf{I}\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{C}\setminus\Gamma_{\mathbf{R}})}=\mathcal{O}(n^{-1})\qquad\text{and}\qquad\|\mathbf{R}_{\pm}-\mathbf{I}\|_{L^{2}(\Gamma_{\mathbf{R}})}=\mathcal{O}(n^{-1}),\quad n\to\infty,

are valid uniformly for
\mathsf{s}\geq\mathsf{s}_{0}
, for any
\mathsf{s}_{0}\in\mathbb{R}
fixed.

We now move to drawing the main conclusions of the asymptotic analysis.

4.
Consequences of the asymptotic analysis

Having completed the asymptotic analysis of the RHP for OPs we prove our main results in this section.

4.1.
Asymptotics for the kernel: proof of Theorem
1.3

We unwrap here all the transformations
\mathbf{Y}\mapsto\mathbf{T}\mapsto\mathbf{S}\mapsto\mathbf{R}
to express
\mathbf{Y}_{+}
in terms of the solution
\mathbf{\Phi}
to the model problem.

For
z\in\mathbb{R}
in a neighborhood of the origin, this unwrapping unravels the identity

(4.1)

\mathbf{Y}_{+}(z)=\ee^{-n\ell/2\bm{\sigma}_{3}}\mathbf{R}_{+}(z)\mathbf{P}_{0,+}(z)\left(\mathbf{I}+\dfrac{\ee^{2n\phi_{b,+}(z)}}{\sigma_{n}(z)}\mathbf{E}_{21}\right)\ee^{n(\mathsf{g}_{+}(z)+\ell/2)\bm{\sigma}_{3}}.

To lighten notation, let us introduce for a moment the unweighted version of the kernel, namely

(4.2)

\widehat{\mathsf{K}}_{n}(x,y)\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=\frac{2\pi\mathrm{i}(x-y)}{\sqrt{\omega_{n}(x)}\sqrt{\omega_{n}(y)}}\mathsf{K}_{n}(x,y).

For
x,y\in\mathbb{R}
and near
z=0
, (
3.11
) and (
4.1
) combined show that

(4.3)

\widehat{\mathsf{K}}_{n}(x,y)=\ee^{n(\mathsf{g}_{+}(x)+\mathsf{g}_{+}(y)+\ell)}\left[\left(\mathbf{I}-\frac{\ee^{2n\phi_{b}(y)}}{\sigma_{n}(y)}\mathbf{E}_{21}\right)\mathbf{P}_{0}(y)^{-1}\mathbf{R}(y)^{-1}\mathbf{R}(x)\mathbf{P}_{0}(x)\left(\mathbf{I}+\frac{\ee^{2n\phi_{b}(x)}}{\sigma_{n}(x)}\mathbf{E}_{21}\right)\right]_{21,+}.

Write

(4.4)

\mathbf{R}(y)^{-1}\mathbf{R}(x)=\mathbf{I}+\mathbf{R}(y)^{-1}\left(\mathbf{R}(x)-\mathbf{R}(y)\right),

Cauchy’s integral formula and Theorem
3.18
gives the estimate,

\mathbf{R}(x)-\mathbf{R}(y)=\frac{1}{2\pi\mathrm{i}}(x-y)\oint_{\gamma}\frac{\mathbf{R}(w)}{(w-x)(w-y)}\mathrm{d}w=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{x-y}{n}\right).

where
\gamma
is any positively oriented closed contour surrounding
x
and
y
.
This way, Equation (
4.3
) becomes

\ee^{-n(\mathsf{g}_{+}(x)+\mathsf{g}_{+}(y)+\ell)}\widehat{\mathsf{K}}_{n}\left(x,y\right)=\left[\left(\mathbf{I}-\frac{\ee^{2n\phi_{b}(y)}}{\sigma_{n}(y)}\mathbf{E}_{21}\right)\mathbf{P}_{0}(y)^{-1}\mathbf{P}_{0}(x)\left(\mathbf{I}+\frac{\ee^{2n\phi_{b}(x)}}{\sigma_{n}(x)}\mathbf{E}_{21}\right)\right]_{21,+}\\
+\left[\left(\mathbf{I}-\frac{\ee^{2n\phi_{b}(y)}}{\sigma_{n}(y)}\mathbf{E}_{21}\right)\mathbf{P}_{0}(y)^{-1}\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{x-y}{n}\right)\mathbf{P}_{0}(x)\left(\mathbf{I}+\frac{\ee^{2n\phi_{b}(x)}}{\sigma_{n}(x)}\mathbf{E}_{21}\right)\right]_{21,+}.

By Lemma
3.14
, both
\mathbf{P}_{0,+}(x)
and
\mathbf{P}_{0,+}(y)^{-1}
remain bounded for
x,y
real near the origin.
Moreover,
\phi_{b,+}
is purely imaginary on
(-\infty,b)
, implying that
\ee^{2n\phi_{b}(x)}/\sigma_{n}(x)
and
\ee^{2n\phi_{b}(x)}/\sigma_{n}(x)
are bounded for
x
and
y
real close to
0
. Hence,

\left[\left(\mathbf{I}-\frac{\ee^{2n\phi_{b}(y)}}{\sigma_{n}(y)}\mathbf{E}_{21}\right)\mathbf{P}_{0}(y)^{-1}\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{x-y}{n}\right)\mathbf{P}_{0}(x)\left(\mathbf{I}+\frac{\ee^{2n\phi_{b}(x)}}{\sigma_{n}(x)}\mathbf{E}_{21}\right)\right]_{21,+}=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{x-y}{n}\right)

where the
\mathcal{O}
term is uniform for
x,y
small.

\begin{split}\beta_{n}(\mathsf{s})&=\frac{\left(\mathbf{M}_{2}\right)_{12}}{\left(\mathbf{M}_{1}\right)_{12}}-\left(\mathbf{M}_{1}\right)_{22}\\
&\phantom{=}+\frac{1}{n}\left[-\frac{\left(\mathbf{M}_{2}\right)_{12}}{\left(\mathbf{M}_{1}\right)^{2}_{12}}\left(\widehat{\mathbf{G}}_{1}+\widehat{\mathbf{R}}_{1}\right)_{12}+\frac{1}{\left(\mathbf{M}_{1}\right)_{12}}\left(\widehat{\mathbf{G}}_{2}+\widehat{\mathbf{R}}_{2}+\widehat{\mathbf{R}}_{1}\mathbf{M}_{1}\right)_{12}-\left(\widehat{\mathbf{G}}_{1}+\widehat{\mathbf{R}}_{1}\right)_{22}\right]+\mathcal{O}(n^{-2}).\\
&=\frac{b+a}{2}+\frac{1}{2n(b-a)}\left(\frac{1}{q(b)}-\frac{1}{q(a)}\right)\\
&\phantom{=}+\frac{1}{n}\left[\frac{(a+b)}{\sqrt{-ab}}\frac{G_{0}(\mathsf{s})}{2\pi\sqrt{t}}-\frac{2\mathsf{q}(\mathsf{s})}{\pi\phi_{V}(0)}\frac{1}{b-a}\left[(a+b)\cos(2n\kappa)+2\sqrt{-ab}\sin(2n\kappa)\right]\right]+\mathcal{O}(n^{-2+\varepsilon}).\end{split}

where we remind that
\kappa\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=\pi\mu_{V}(0,b)
. Having in mind the transformation between
\mathsf{q}
and
\mathsf{Q}
from (

) and the explicit expressions for

from (

), the asymptotic expansions for

\mathsf{T}
.

The characterization of

The decay (

Appendix A
Laplace-Type Integrals

Motivated by the understanding of the asymptotics of
\mathsf{h}
as
z,n\to\infty
in Section
3
, we consider integrals of the type

F(t)\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=\int_{a}^{b}g(x)\log(1+\ee^{-y-tf(x)})\,\mathrm{d}x,\quad a<0<b.

More precisely, we are interested in the asymptotics of
F(t)
as
t\to+\infty
.

We start by considering the case
f(x)=x^{2}
.

Lemma A.1
.

Let
g
be a
L^{1}([a,b])
function that is
C^{\infty}
in a neighborhood of
0
and
f(x)=x^{2}
. Assume that

(A.1)

y>-M,

for some
M>0
fixed. For any
N>0
, the function
F(t)
admits an expansion of the form

(A.2)

F(t)=\frac{1}{t^{\frac{1}{2}}}\left[\sum_{k=0}^{2N}\frac{g^{(2k)}(0)}{(2k)!}\frac{G_{2k}(y)}{t^{k}}+\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{t^{2N+1}}\right)\right],\quad t\to+\infty,

where the coefficients
G_{\beta}(y)
are given by

G_{\beta}(y)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}u^{\beta}\log\left(1+\ee^{-y-u^{2}}\right)\,\mathrm{d}u,\quad\beta\geq 0,

and the
\mathcal{O}
term is valid uniformly for
y\geq-M
.

Remark A.2
.

•

Observe that
G_{\beta}(2k+1)=0
because the integrand in the definition of
G_{\beta}
is an odd function, explaining why only even indices
G_{2k}
appear in the expansion (
A.2
).

•

Assuming that
\beta
is even, we can write
G_{\beta}(y)
in terms of the
polylog function

\text{Li}_{\gamma}
:

G_{\beta}(y)=-\frac{\beta-1}{2}\Gamma\left(\frac{\beta-1}{2}\right)\text{Li}_{2+\frac{\beta-1}{2}}\left(-\ee^{-y}\right).

Proof.

For every
\delta>0
, write

\int_{a}^{b}g(x)\log\left(1+\ee^{-y-tx^{2}}\right)\,\mathrm{d}x=\left(\int_{a}^{-\delta}+\int_{-\delta}^{\delta}+\int_{\delta}^{b}\right)g(x)\log\left(1+\ee^{-y-tx^{2}}\right)\,\mathrm{d}x.

Note that

\int_{[a,-\delta]\cup[\delta,b]}g(x)\log\left(1+\ee^{-y-tx^{2}}\right)\,\mathrm{d}x\leq 2\|g\|_{1}\ee^{M}\ee^{-t\frac{\delta^{2}}{4}}.

Thus, for some
c=c(\delta)>0
,

(A.3)

\int_{a}^{b}g(x)\log\left(1+\ee^{-y-tx^{2}}\right)\,\mathrm{d}x=\int_{-\delta}^{\delta}g(x)\log\left(1+\ee^{-y-tx^{2}}\right)\,\mathrm{d}x+\mathcal{O}\left(\ee^{-ct}\right),

where the
\mathcal{O}
term is uniform for
y>-M
.
Now fix
\delta>0
for which
g
admits a Taylor expansion of order
N
at the interval
(-\delta,\delta)
. Then

\int_{-\delta}^{\delta}g(x)\log\left(1+\ee^{-y-tx^{2}}\right)\,\mathrm{d}x=\sum_{j=0}^{N}\frac{g^{(j)}(0)}{j!}\int_{-\delta}^{\delta}x^{j}\log\left(1+\ee^{-y-tx^{2}}\right)\,\mathrm{d}x+R_{N+1}(t)

where the remainder
R_{n+1}
satisfies

\left|R_{N+1}(t)\right|\leq\frac{1}{(N+1)!}\sup_{-\delta\leq\xi\leq\delta}\left|g^{(N+1)}(\xi)\right|\left|\int_{-\delta}^{\delta}x^{N+1}\log\left(1+\ee^{-y-tx^{2}}\right)\,\mathrm{d}x\right|.

For
\beta\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
, exchange variables
u=\sqrt{t}\cdot x
to obtain

\int_{-\delta}^{\delta}x^{\beta}\log(1+\ee^{-y-tx^{2}})\,\mathrm{d}x=\frac{1}{t^{\frac{\beta+1}{2}}}\left[G_{\beta}(y)-\int_{[-\infty,-\delta\sqrt{t}]\cup[\delta\sqrt{t},+\infty]}u^{\beta}\log(1+\ee^{-y-u^{2}})\,\mathrm{d}u\right].

Since

\int_{[-\infty,-\delta\sqrt{t}]\cup[\delta\sqrt{t},+\infty]}u^{\beta}\log(1+\ee^{-y-u^{2}})\,\mathrm{d}u\leq 2\left\|u^{\beta}\ee^{-\frac{u^{2}}{2}}\right\|_{1}\ee^{M}\ee^{-\frac{\delta^{2}t}{2}}

we get, for some
\widetilde{c}=\widetilde{c}(\delta)>0
,

\int_{-\delta}^{\delta}x^{\beta}\log(1+\ee^{-y-tx^{2}})\,\mathrm{d}x=\frac{1}{t^{\frac{\beta+1}{2}}}F_{\beta}(y)+\mathcal{O}(\ee^{-t\widetilde{c}}).

where, once again, the
\mathcal{O}
term is uniform for
y>-M
. Finally, plugging above identities on (
A.3
) we obtain

\begin{split}F(t)&=\int_{-\delta}^{\delta}g(x)\log\left(1+\ee^{-y-tx^{2}}\right)\,\mathrm{d}x+\mathcal{O}\left(\ee^{-ct}\right)\\
&=\sum_{j=0}^{N}\frac{g^{(j)}(0)}{j!}\frac{G_{j}(y)}{t^{\frac{j+1}{2}}}+\frac{1}{t^{\frac{N+2}{2}}}\frac{\sup_{-\delta<\xi<\delta}|g^{(N+1)}(\xi)|}{(N+1)!}G_{N+1}(y)+\mathcal{O}(\ee^{-t\widetilde{c}})+\mathcal{O}\left(\ee^{-ct}\right)\\
&=\sum_{j=0}^{N}\frac{g^{(j)}(0)}{j!}\frac{G_{j}(y)}{t^{\frac{j+1}{2}}}+\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{t^{\frac{N+2}{2}}}\right).\end{split}

Replacing
N\mapsto 2N
, the proof is complete.
∎

Proposition A.3
.

Assume that
g
is as in Lemma
A.1
and that
f
is
C^{\infty}
in a neighborhood of the origin with a unique global minimum on
[a,b]
at
x=0
, with
f(0)=f^{\prime}(0)=0
and
f^{\prime\prime}(0)\neq 0
. Assume also that
y>-M
for some
M>0
fixed. Then
F(t)
admits an expansion of the form

F(t)=\frac{1}{t^{1/2}}\left(\sum_{k=0}^{2N}\frac{\widehat{g}^{(2k)}(0)}{(2k)!}\frac{G_{2k}(y)}{t^{k}}+\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{t^{2N+1}}\right)\right),\quad t\to+\infty,

where
\widehat{g}
is a function that is
C^{\infty}
in a neighborhood of
0
whose derivatives at
0
are given in terms of the derivatives of
f
and
g
at
0
. Moreover the
\mathcal{O}
term is uniform for
y\geq-M
.

Proof.

Consider the two-variable function

H(u,v)=\frac{f(uv)}{u^{2}}-1

Then, for
v_{0}=\sqrt{\frac{2}{f^{\prime\prime}(0)}}
,

\frac{\partial H}{\partial v}(0,v_{0})=f^{\prime\prime}(0)\cdot v_{0}\neq 0.

Since
H(0,v_{0})=0
, the Implicit Function Theorem implies that there exists a function
v:[-\delta,\delta]\to\mathbb{R}
such that

H(u,v(u))=0,\quad u\in[-\delta,\delta].

The identity
f(u\cdot v(u))=u^{2}
implies that
v^{(k)}(0)
is given in terms of
f^{(j)}(0)
,
0\leq j\leq k+2
.

Consider

F(t)=\int_{-\delta}^{\delta}g(x)\log\left(1+\ee^{-y-tf(x)}\right)\,\mathrm{d}x+\int_{[a,-\delta]\cup[\delta,b]}g(x)\log\left(1+\ee^{-y-tf(x)}\right)\,\mathrm{d}x.

For the first term in the r.h.s. the change of variables
x=u\cdot v(u)
gives

\int_{a_{\delta}}^{b_{\delta}}g(u\cdot v(u))[v(u)+u\cdot v^{\prime}(u)]\log\left(1+\ee^{-y-tu^{2}}\right)\,\mathrm{d}u,\quad a_{\delta}\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=-\sqrt{f(-\delta}),\quad b_{\delta}\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=\sqrt{f(\delta)}.

The result follows by applying Lemma
A.1
to above integral, where
\widehat{g}(u)=g(u\cdot v(u))[v(u)+u\cdot v^{\prime}(u)]
. Differentiating above identity at
u=0
one verifies that
\widehat{g}^{(j)}(0)
is given in terms of
g^{(k)}(0)
and
f^{(l)}(0)
,
0\leq k\leq j
,
0\leq l\leq j+2
.
∎
