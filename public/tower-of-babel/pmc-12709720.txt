Doxycycline vs levofloxacin for macrolide-unresponsive Mycoplasma pneumoniae pneumonia in children: a real-world study from China
Authors: Ruling Yang, Hongmei Xu, Zhenzhen Zhang, Ruiqiu Zhao, Quanbo Liu, Mengyang Liu, Gaihuan Zheng, Xiaoying Wu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMacrolide-unresponsive Mycoplasma pneumoniae pneumonia (MUMPP) is a growing concern in paediatrics, yet comparative data on second-line antibiotics remain limited. This study aimed to evaluate the efficacy and safety of doxycycline and levofloxacin in treating MUMPP in children.MethodsThis single-centre, real-world observational cohort study was conducted in a tertiary paediatric hospital in China. We included children hospitalised with M. pneumoniae pneumonia between July and December 2023. Patients were classified as having macrolide-sensitive M. pneumoniae pneumonia (MSMPP) or MUMPP according to their clinical response to initial azithromycin therapy. MUMPP patients were further allocated to the doxycycline, levofloxacin, or azithromycin group, based on the second-line antibiotic prescribed. Clinical characteristics, treatment outcomes, and adverse events were compared across groups. The primary outcome measures were the duration of pulmonary signs and the duration of fever after the change of antibiotic. The secondary outcome measure was the incidence of adverse events.ResultsMUMPP accounted for 66.6% (365/548) of cases. Compared with MSMPP, children with MUMPP had higher rates of tachypnoea (17.5% vs 1.6%, p < 0.001) and high-grade fever (82.2% vs 60.7%, p < 0.001).The median fever duration after second-line antibiotic treatment was 0 days (IQR 0–1) in both the doxycycline (n = 190) and levofloxacin (n = 130) groups, with no significant difference between them (p = 0.088), but both were significantly shorter than in the azithromycin group (n = 45; median 3 days, IQR 2–3; both p < 0.001). IPTW-adjusted and age-stratified (3–6 years) analyses, in which baseline characteristics were fully balanced, yielded consistent results, and showed no significant difference between the doxycycline and levofloxacin groups in defervescence rates across the four time intervals (within 24 h, 24–48 h, 48–72 h, and > 72 h), and no differences in pulmonary sign resolution among any of the three groups. No cases of tooth discolouration or musculoskeletal complications were observed, and no gastrointestinal events of GSRS grade ≥5 or other CTCAE grade ≥3 adverse events occurred in any treatment group during the treatment period.ConclusionsThis study demonstrated that both doxycycline and levofloxacin are effective and safe treatments for paediatric MUMPP. Introduction

Mycoplasma pneumoniae (M. pneumoniae) is a leading pathogen of respiratory tract infections in children, accounting for approximately 20–40% of community-acquired pneumonia cases in this population [1]. Although some cases of M. pneumoniae infection are self-limiting, a subset may progress to severe illness, often leading to significant pulmonary damage and, in some cases, extrapulmonary involvement [2]. M. pneumoniae epidemics occur cyclically every few years, with a marked global resurgence reported in 2023 [3]. Surveillance data from Beijing’s respiratory pathogen network revealed a sustained increase in M. pneumoniae detection rates since May 2023, with PCR positivity rates reaching 25.4% among outpatient children and as high as 48.4% in hospitalised cases during October and November [4]. The significant increase in M. pneumoniae positivity rates among children has placed substantial strain on healthcare systems.

In recent years, macrolide-resistant M. pneumoniae (MRMP) infections have emerged as a significant therapeutic challenge, raising increasing concern with the international medical community. In several Asian countries, MRMP prevalence exceeds 60% and has remained persistently high [5–7]. Resistance is primarily linked to point mutations in domain V of the 23S rRNA gene, most notably the A2063G and A2064G substitutions [8]. Confirming MRMP requires either molecular detection of resistance-associated mutations or in vitro antimicrobial susceptibility testing, procedures that necessitate advanced laboratory capacity and are rarely feasible in routine clinical practice. Moreover, accumulating clinical evidence suggests that laboratory-detected resistance does not consistently correlate with disease severity or therapeutic response to macrolides [9]. Consequently, increasing clinical attention is being directed towards the early identification of macrolide-unresponsive M. pneumoniae pneumonia (MUMPP), defined by persistent fever ≥38.0 °C for ≥72 hours during macrolide therapy, alongside unimproved or worsening clinical or radiographic features [10]. Compared with macrolide-sensitive M. pneumoniae pneumonia (MSMPP), MUMPP is associated with an elevated risk of progression to refractory M. pneumoniae pneumonia and poorer clinical outcomes [10, 11]. Unlike MRMP infections, which necessitates laboratory confirmation, MUMPP is clinically diagnosed, facilitating earlier recognition and timely intervention. Timely initiation of effective second-line therapy in MUMPP may help prevent or attenuate disease progression and reduce long-term sequelae [12]. However, studies specifically focused on MUMPP remain limited to date.

Clinical guidelines in China and Japan currently recommend tetracyclines and fluoroquinolones as second-line antibiotic options for children with MUMPP [10, 13]. Nevertheless, tetracyclines have long been contraindicated in children under 8 years due to possible associations with permanent tooth discolouration and enamel hypoplasia [14, 15]. The term “tetracycline teeth” remains widely recognised. Similarly, early animal studies linked fluoroquinolones use to cartilage and joint toxicity in juvenile models, leading to recommendations against their use in individuals under 18 years [16]. These safety concerns contribute to therapeutic hesitancy, potentially delaying effective intervention. Currently, no consensus exists regarding the first-line alternative agent for MUMPP, and comparative paediatric data remain sparse. Given this context, the present study aimed to assess, in a real-world clinical setting, the comparative efficacy and safety of doxycycline (a representative tetracycline) and levofloxacin (a representative fluoroquinolone) in treating MUMPP in children.

Methods

Study population

Children hospitalised with M. pneumoniae pneumonia (MPP) at the Department of Infectious Diseases, Children’s Hospital of Chongqing Medical University between 1 July and 31 December 2023 were screened for eligibility. Eligible patients were required to meet all of the following inclusion criteria: (1) diagnosis of MPP in accordance with the 2023 Clinical Practice Guidelines for the Diagnosis and Treatment of MPP in Children [10]; (2) initial treatment with azithromycin (10 mg/kg once daily) for three consecutive days; (3) availability of complete clinical data required for analysis.

If a patient met the diagnostic criteria for MUMPP [10], antimicrobial therapy was subsequently adjusted following a fully informed discussion with caregivers, based on their preference for one of the following second-line antibiotics: doxycycline (2 mg/kg orally twice daily for 14 days), levofloxacin (10 mg/kg every 12 hours for children < 5 years and 10 mg/kg once daily for those ≥5 years, for 14 days, administered intravenously during hospitalisation and orally after discharge), or continued azithromycin (10 mg/kg once daily for 4 additional days after the diagnosis of MUMPP, followed by a 4-day drug-free interval, and then a second 3-day course, administered intravenously during hospitalisation and orally after discharge).

Exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) afebrile MPP cases; (2) defervescence prior to azithromycin initiation; (3) concomitant use of other antimicrobial agents or systemic corticosteroids following MUMPP diagnosis; (4) co-infection with other respiratory or non-respiratory pathogens; (5) underlying conditions potentially affecting treatment outcomes, including congenital or acquired immunodeficiency and airway malformations; (6) incomplete clinical records essential for study analysis.

Patients were stratified into two groups based on their clinical response during the initial three-day course of macrolide therapy: the MSMPP group and the MUMPP group. Patients in the MUMPP group were further categorised into three subgroups according to the second-line antibiotics prescribed: the doxycycline group, the levofloxacin group and the azithromycin group. Data collection and outcomes

Clinical data were retrieved from the hospital’s electronic medical record system. Information collected included: patient ID, age at onset, sex, clinical manifestations (such as fever, chest pain, tachypnoea, and pulmonary signs), ancillary test results (including peripheral blood counts, C-reactive protein [CRP], lactate dehydrogenase [LDH], D-dimer levels and chest imaging findings), genotypic test results identifying macrolide resistance-associated mutations, types and anatomical sites of co-infecting pathogens, clinical diagnosis, treatment regimens, and temperature trends throughout hospitalisation. Adverse events observed during treatment were also documented. Fever was defined as an axillary temperature exceeding 37.3 °C at least once during a 24-hour period, and high-grade fever was defined as a body temperature of ≥39 °C. Based on WHO criteria, tachypnoea was defined as : respiratory rate > 60 breaths/min for infants < 2 months; 50 breaths/min for infants aged 2–12 months; 40 breaths/min for children aged 1–5 years;30 breaths/min for children aged ≥6 years.

Efficacy was assessed using two primary indicators: duration of fever and duration of pulmonary signs following initiation of doxycycline, levofloxacin, or continued azithromycin. Pulmonary signs were defined as the presence of crackles (rales) and/or wheezes on auscultation. Safety was assessed based on the incidence and severity of adverse events. To ensure objective and standardised evaluation, the severity of adverse drug reactions was assessed using the Gastrointestinal Symptom Rating Scale (GSRS, seven-grade scale) for gastrointestinal events and graded according to the Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events (CTCAE, version 5.0; five-grade scale) for all other reactions.

Ethical considerations

The study protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Children’s Hospital of Chongqing Medical University (Approval No. 2024-Research-247) and was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. As the data used in this study were derived from routine clinical care and did not include any personally identifiable information, the requirement for written informed consent was waived. Off-label use of doxycycline and levofloxacin, as involved in this study, was fully disclosed to participants’ caregivers. Off-label azithromycin use in infants under 6 months for MPP, pertussis, pneumonia and other infections is supported by existing clinical guidelines and studies [10, 17], and was also fully disclosed to the caregivers of children aged under 6 months in this study. Informed consent was obtained from caregivers prior to treatment, and the off-label use was formally registered with the hospital.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics version 25.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). For normally distributed continuous variables, data were presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) and compared using Student’s t test or one-way ANOVA. Non-normally distributed variables were expressed as median and interquartile range (IQR) and analysed using the Mann–Whitney U test or Kruskal–Wallis test. Categorical data were reported as counts and percentages, with between-group comparisons conducted using Pearson’s χ2 test, continuity-corrected χ2 test, or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate. If baseline characteristics were imbalanced among groups, inverse probability of treatment weighting (IPTW) was performed using the ipw package in R (version 4.4.1; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria) to minimise potential confounding. Covariate balance after weighting was assessed using the standardised mean difference (SMD), with values > 0.2 indicating residual imbalance. Variables that remained imbalanced were further adjusted through weighted regression based on the IPTW sample, achieving doubly robust estimation. All subsequent between-group comparisons were conducted on the weighted data. A two-tailed p value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

A total of 548 paediatric patients were enrolled in the study, of whom 183 (33.4%) were diagnosed with MSMPP and 365 (66.6%) with MUMPP. The overall median age was 6 years (IQR 4–7), and patients in the MUMPP group were significantly older than those in the MSMPP group (6 years [IQR 4–8] vs 5 years [IQR 4–7], p = 0.002). Tachypnoea was observed in 17.5% (64/365) of MUMPP patients, compared with 1.6% (3/183) in the MSMPP group (p < 0.001). High-grade fever was also more frequently observed in the MUMPP group than in the MSMPP group (82.2% vs 60.7%, p < 0.001).

Laboratory results revealed significantly higher neutrophil percentages and levels of CRP, LDH, and D-dimer in the MUMPP group (all p < 0.05). The overall detection rate of macrolide resistance-associated mutations was 70.5%, with no significant difference between the two groups (p = 0.482). Among the 365 children with MUMPP, 190 were administered doxycycline, 130 were treated with levofloxacin, and 45 continued azithromycin therapy. The median age was 7 years (IQR 6–9) in the doxycycline group, 4 years (IQR 3–6) in the levofloxacin group, and 6 years (IQR 4.5–7) in the azithromycin group, with a statistically significant difference in age distribution (p < 0.001).

Median fever duration was 0 days (IQR 0–1) in both the doxycycline and levofloxacin groups(p = 0.088), markedly shorter than in the azithromycin group (median 3 days, IQR 2–3; both p < 0.001). Early defervescence (within 24 hours) occurred in 74.7% (142/190) of the doxycycline group and 67.7% (88/130) of the levofloxacin group, with no statistically significant difference between the two groups. In contrast, no patients in the azithromycin group achieved defervescence within 24 hours, and 64.4% (29/45) of patients receiving continued azithromycin therapy defervesced only after 72 hours (Fig. 2). The duration of pulmonary signs after second-line therapy was yet significantly shorter in the doxycycline(median 3 days, IQR 2–3) and levofloxacin(median 3 days, IQR 2–4) groups than in the azithromycin group (median 4 days, IQR 3–5; p < 0.001 and p = 0.003, respectively), with no significant difference between doxycycline and levofloxacin (p = 0.051).

Among the 365 children with MUMPP, the overall incidence of adverse events was 11.5% (42/365). The most common adverse events were gastrointestinal symptoms in the azithromycin (6.7%) and doxycycline (7.4%) groups, and skin rash/photosensitivity in the levofloxacin group (8.5%). Notably, no statistically significant differences were observed among the three groups regarding the incidence and the severity of the adverse events. Throughout the treatment period, no tooth-related adverse effects were noted in the doxycycline group, and no musculoskeletal or soft tissue complications were reported in the levofloxacin group. There were no gastrointestinal reactions of GSRS grade ≥5 and no CTCAE grade ≥3 adverse events in any treatment group. One patient in doxycycline group developed headache suggestive of increased intracranial pressure on day 12 of treatment, which resolved promptly after a 14-day discontinuation of doxycycline. To address the imbalances in several baseline variables among the three groups, age-stratified analyses and IPTW were performed. The subgroup of children aged < 3 years was excluded from the stratified analysis due to the small number of cases in each treatment group. After IPTW, all SMD values were below the 0.2 threshold, suggesting balanced covariates across the groups (Supplementary Figure). Both the 3–6-year and ≥6-year subgroups, as well as the IPTW-adjusted analysis, showed no significant difference in fever duration after second-line antibiotic treatment between the doxycycline and levofloxacin groups, while the two groups exhibited significantly shorter fever duration compared with the azithromycin group. However, in the age-stratified analyses and after IPTW adjustment, the differences in the duration of pulmonary sign resolution after second-line antibiotic treatment among the three groups were no longer statistically significant. In both the 3–6-year subgroup analysis and the IPTW-adjusted analysis, baseline characteristics were well balanced across treatment groups, and there were no significant differences in the proportions of patients achieving defervescence within 24 h, 24–48 h, 48–72 h, or after 72 h between the doxycycline and levofloxacin groups. IPTW analysis revealed that the incidence of skin rash or photosensitivity was significantly higher in the levofloxacin group than in the doxycycline group,but the severity of rash was comparable between the two groups (Table 3, Supplementary Table 1 and Supplementary Table 2).

Discussion

Early identification and appropriate management of MUMPP are vital for preventing clinical deterioration. Notably, the 2023 national guidelines issued by China’s National Health Commission marked the first formal definition of MUMPP within the Chinese clinical framework. The guidelines also recommended tetracyclines and fluoroquinolones as alternative antibiotics for second-line treatment options. However,direct comparative data on the efficacy and safety of these two antibiotic classes in Chinese paediatric populations are limited. In this context, the present study aimed to evaluate the comparative clinical efficacy and safety of doxycycline and levofloxacin in the treatment of MUMPP.

Although current national guidelines define MUMPP, they primarily focus on the timing of diagnosis and provide limited information on its distinguishing clinical features. Consequently, early identification of MUMPP remains challenging for both clinicians and caregivers. In this study, MUMPP accounted for approximately two-thirds of paediatric MPP cases, indicating its relatively high prevalence among Chinese children, which is consistent with previous reports [9, 18]. As in previous studies [9], children with MUMPP were generally presented with more severe symptoms than those with MSMPP, including higher rates of dyspnoea and high-grade fever. Laboratory results further supported this, with significantly elevated neutrophil percentages, CRP, LDH, and D-dimer levels in the MUMPP group. These results suggest that MUMPP may be associated with a more intense systemic inflammatory response, which may contribute to greater pulmonary tissue injury, capillary endothelial damage, and microthrombus formation [19]. Notably, LDH > 399 U/L has been proposed as a potential predictor of refractory MPP [20]. Further studies with larger cohorts are warranted to identify laboratory biomarkers that enable earlier recognition of MUMPP.

Previous studies have demonstrated a strong concordance between genotypic resistance mutations and the in vitro antimicrobial susceptibility of M.pneumoniae strains [21]. In the present study, macrolide resistance was determined by detecting resistance-associated mutations in domain V of the 23S rRNA gene, with a detection rate of 70.5% among all MPP patients. In line with the findings of Li et al. [9], our study found no significant difference in the detection rates of macrolide resistance-associated mutations between the MSMPP and MUMPP groups. Similarly, previous studies also reported no significant differences between refractory MPP and non-refractory MPP cases [22, 23]. Moreover, clinical severity was not significantly associated with the presence or absence of resistance mutations [23]. Based on current evidence, macrolide resistance-associated mutations do not necessarily reduce the therapeutic efficacy of macrolides in MPP [20, 24–27]. This may be attributed to several factors:first, the structural 23S rRNA mutations underlying macrolide resistance show variable phenotypic expression depending on mutation type, bacterial load, and host environment [28]; second, azithromycin attains high concentrations in respiratory tissues, partially overcoming moderate increases in MIC [29]; third, macrolides have immunomodulatory and anti-inflammatory effects that help alleviate symptoms;and finally, M. pneumoniae pneumonia is usually self-limiting, with recovery partly driven by host immune responses.

This suggest that the detection of resistance-associated mutations may not reliably predict disease severity or therapeutic response, highlighting the importance of clinical judgement for the diagnosis and management of MPP. Nevertheless, in clinical practice, some paediatricians may opt to discontinue azithromycin and initiate alternative antibiotics based solely on the detection of resistance mutations. In some instances, families travel from rural regions to tertiary care centres in major cities specifically to request macrolide resistance testing, an approach that may be of limited clinical justification. Despite the high prevalence of macrolide resistance in China, an initial 3-day course of azithromycin remains recommended as first-line therapy. A proportion of children with MRMP pneumonia can still exhibit clinical improvement with macrolide monotherapy [10].

In this study,across all analyses,before adjustment,after IPTW adjustment, and in age-stratified analyses, doxycycline and levofloxacin exhibited comparable efficacy in resolving fever in children with MUMPP, both significantly outperforming azithromycin.Both doxycycline and levofloxacin have oral bioavailability exceeding 90%, with oral and intravenous formulations achieving equivalent systemic exposure and comparable clinical outcomes. Therefore, although the routes of administration differed during hospitalisation due to the availability of drugs in our inpatient pharmacy at the time, this variation is unlikely to have affected the comparative assessment of efficacy or adverse events between the two agents.

After switching to doxycycline or levofloxacin, more than two-thirds of patients defervesced within 24 hours, with a median time to pulmonary sign resolution of 3 days. In contrast, 64.4% of patients who continued azithromycin remained febrile beyond 3 days, and their median pulmonary sign resolution time was 4 days. These results are consistent with earlier studies supporting the superior efficacy of tetracyclines and fluoroquinolones in managing MUMPP [30, 31]. Despite global surveillance indicating high and geographically variable rates of macrolide resistance among M. pneumoniae isolates, clinical MRMP strains remain highly susceptible to doxycycline (MIC ≤2 mg/L) and levofloxacin (MIC ≤1 mg/L) [6, 21, 32, 33]. This preserved susceptibility supports their therapeutic role in MUMPP and may be due, in part, to their historically limited use in paediatric populations, helping to retain their antimicrobial effectiveness.

Of note, a recent Japanese study reported that although all M. pneumoniae strains isolated between 2017 and 2020 remained within the susceptibility range for tosufloxacin, their MIC values were higher than those of strains isolated in 2011–2012 and 2015–2016, possibly reflecting the increased clinical use of the drug in recent years [33]. Given the significant burden of MUMPP in China and the anticipated rise in doxycycline and levofloxacin prescriptions following the 2023 national guidelines, proactive antimicrobial stewardship is crucial. Based on current evidence, doxycycline and levofloxacin may be equally effective therapeutic options for treating MUMPP in children. However, their use must strictly adhere to age-appropriate dosing guidelines, with treatment duration not exceeding the recommended course. We also recommend establishing regional antimicrobial susceptibility surveillance systems, with particular emphasis on monitoring longitudinal MIC trends. Where appropriate, antibiotic rotation strategies should be considered to help mitigate the development of further resistance.

In our study, doxycycline was more frequently prescribed for older children, while levofloxacin was preferred for younger ones. This prescribing pattern may reflect a common mindset among caregivers and clinicians: when both antibiotics are considered off-label, doxycycline may be preferred for children nearing the approved age threshold of 8 years, whereas younger children may be assigned either agent more arbitrarily. This pattern highlights the nuanced clinical decision-making that occurs in the absence of definitive paediatric guidelines for second-line therapy in MUMPP.

Tetracyclines can chelate calcium during tooth mineralisation (from the late foetal stage to around 8 years of age), leading to deposition in developing teeth and intrinsic discolouration after eruption [34]. There is growing evidence that doxycycline, a second-generation tetracycline, poses minimal risk of tooth discolouration when used for short durations [35]. It is currently recommended by both national and international guidelines for the short-term treatment(≤21 days) of selected paediatric infections [5, 36]. Similarly, previous studies have shown that musculoskeletal side effects of levofloxacin are rare and generally reversible [37–39]. In the present study, no cases of tooth discolouration were observed in children treated with doxycycline for up to 14 days, and no musculoskeletal complications, such as tendinitis or arthritis, were reported in the levofloxacin group. The most frequently reported adverse effects were gastrointestinal symptoms and mild skin rash or photosensitivity, which were self-limiting or easily managed in most cases. No serious adverse events (gastrointestinal reactions of GSRS grade ≥5 and CTCAE grade ≥3) were observed in any treatment group.These findings indicate that both doxycycline and levofloxacin were generally well tolerated when used for brief treatment durations.

Collectively, our findings support the use of a short-course (≤14 days) regimen of doxycycline or levofloxacin as a safe and effective treatment option for children with MUMPP.The mean age of children in the doxycycline group was 7 years, when enamel calcification is largely complete and the risk of tetracycline-related tooth discolouration is minimal. Thus, our findings support the short-term safety of doxycycline only in school-aged children and should not be generalised to younger age groups.In this study, one child developed symptoms suggestive of raised intracranial pressure during doxycycline therapy. Although intracranial hypertension has been occasionally reported with prolonged tetracycline use (≥1 month) and is rare with doxycycline [40], this case underscores the need for vigilance even during short-term treatment in children.

This study has several limitations. First, this study lacks dynamic radiological assessments that could have provided a more comprehensive evaluation of antibacterial efficacy. This limitation reflects the constraints of the real-world study design and practical factors–clinical improvement, concerns about radiation exposure, travel distance, and financial considerations–which led many parents to decline post-treatment imaging. Future prospective studies should incorporate radiological scoring systems to better validate the therapeutic efficacy of different antimicrobial regimens. Second, owing to the absence of long-term follow-up, delayed adverse events such as doxycycline-related tooth discolouration or levofloxacin-associated musculoskeletal toxicity could not be assessed [34, 41]. Future prospective studies with structured follow-up are warranted to evaluate long-term safety in paediatric patients.

Conclusions

Compared to MRMPP, the concept of MUMPP is based on early clinical response assessment, enabling earlier intervention and more timely therapeutic adjustments. This approach may accelerate symptom resolution, minimise pulmonary damage, and reduce the risk of long-term complications. In this study, MUMPP was found to be common among paediatric MPP cases, and both doxycycline and levofloxacin demonstrated comparable clinical efficacy and favourable safety profiles for MUMPP treatment. This suggests that clinicians and caregivers need not be overly constrained when choosing between these second-line agents. Treatment decisions should instead be tailored to the individual, considering factors such as patient age, drug availability, ease of administration, and caregiver preferences. To ensure the safe and rational use of doxycycline and levofloxacin in MUMPP, prescribing should be guided by clearly defined clinical indications, and treatment duration should be limited. Additionally, strengthening regional surveillance of M. pneumoniae resistance is crucial for supporting long-term antimicrobial stewardship in paediatric populations.
