Scalable lens-enhanced broadbeam mmWave harvester delivering tens of milliwatts for wireless power transfer in next-generation smart city environments
Authors: Marvin Joshi, Kexin Hu, Charles A. Lynch III, Manos M. Tentzeris
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
As smart cities and next-generation connected environments continue to grow in scale and complexity, the need for sustainable, maintenance-free energy solutions becomes increasingly urgent. The widespread reliance on batteries to power billions of IoT devices poses significant challenges, including frequent maintenance requirements and substantial environmental impact. A compelling alternative involves harnessing 5G networks, which not only enhance communication capabilities but also enable efficient wireless energy harvesting. In this paper, the authors propose a scalable, broadband, dielectric lens-based mmWave energy harvester with wide total solid angular coverage and mW-level harvesting capabilities. The proposed system features a ’pixel’ array of rectennas, each incorporating a circularly polarized aperture-coupled stacked patch antenna and a broadband, high-sensitivity rectifier, enhanced by a biconvex dielectric lens to achieve a wide solid angle coverage of 2.68 sr. The single unit cell, using a single transmitter setup, demonstrated a peak captured power of up to 20 mW. With its broadbeam coverage and high efficiency, this architecture presents a compelling pathway to support next-generation smart city applications while minimizing dependence on conventional power infrastructure.

Introduction

With rapid advances in smart city technologies and widespread deployment of 5G wireless networks, a total of more than 80 billion low-power IoT devices are expected to interact with urban infrastructure by the end of 20251. These devices enable next-generation urban sensing networks, which operate by dispersing sensor nodes throughout the urban environment to monitor and optimize key systems in real time. These sensor networks support a wide range of applications, including traffic flow regulation, environmental monitoring, energy management, and public safety2–4. These systems provide continuous, data-driven insights for improved efficiency, reduced operational costs, and increased sustainability. However, a proportional number of batteries would be required to power these distributed devices, thereby dramatically increasing the manufacturing, environmental, and maintenance costs of smart cities, impeding the widespread adoption of these technologies.

RF energy harvesting offers a scalable alternative by eliminating the reliance on batteries and reducing maintenance overhead. These harvesting systems convert incident radio frequency (RF) energy from the surrounding wireless environment into usable electrical power to drive low-power circuitry5–11. A vast amount of research has focused on RF harvesting at low frequencies; however, these systems suffer from low equivalent isotropic radiated power (EIRP), limiting their performance in dense deployments12–16. Additionally, these low incident power densities reduce the available energy, and the large size of conventional harvesters limits their practicality, especially in complex urban settings.

By shifting to millimeter-wave (mmWave) energy harvesting, one benefits from the wide bandwidth and increased EIRP available in 5G/mmWave bands, allowing dense spatial multiplexing of IoT devices throughout a city. Licensed 5G base stations operating in the 28 39 GHz range can emit highly directional beams with equivalent isotropically radiated power (EIRP) up to 75 dBm, as permitted by the Federal Communications Commission17,18. While some compact harvesters at lower frequencies achieve small form factors, broad coverage, and orientation-agnostic performance, their gain is inherently limited by the longer wavelength, restricting both the amount of harvested power and the effective operating range19–22. In contrast, the shorter wavelength at mmWave frequencies enables much higher gain in a compact design, which, together with the high-EIRP mmWave infrastructure, supports long-range wireless power transfer and dense deployment of IoT nodes in smart city environments. This focused delivery enhances the efficiency of wireless power transfer and supports longer range operation18,23–30. The short wavelength of mmWave signals also allows for the use of compact, high-gain antennas suitable for small-form-factor sensor nodes. Furthermore, beamforming and directional steering features of mmWave transmission enable spatial targeting of power toward specific sensors, improving performance even under challenging deployment scenarios. As the 5G infrastructure continues to densify, the increasing availability of high-power mmWave sources enables a ubiquitous power delivery platform for future battery-free systems in smart cities. Recent developments in lens-enabled antennas provide enhanced angular coverage and gain18,24,31–34. However, these devices exhibited poor efficiency when powered by a single transmitting source, preventing them from harvesting significant power across its full solid angle. Thus, there is a growing need for orientation-tolerant energy harvesters that combine high gain and wide total angular coverage with highly efficient rectifiers for greater amounts of harvested power. Achieving consistent power capture under movement or misalignment remains critical for practical deployment. Addressing this challenge requires orientation-tolerant designs that combine high gain, wide angular coverage, and efficient rectification.

To address these needs, this work introduces a compact mmWave energy harvester, as shown in Fig. 1(a), comprised of a 3D biconvex dielectric lens and a circularly polarized patch rectenna array for next-generation smart city environments, as demonstrated in Fig. 1(b). Building on our prior work in35, which presented a narrowband lens-assisted harvester, this design advances the concept through a new circularly polarized aperture-coupled stacked patch antenna with a smaller footprint and a higher realized gain of up to 22.3 dBi, while maintaining broadband circular polarization from 25.5 GHz–30.5 GHz for more stable and angle-resilient operation. With this updated design, the single-unit cell harvester achieved nearly double the captured power under identical excitation conditions, highlighting the benefit of the new antenna–rectifier configuration. A passive summation network with bypass diodes ensures that only the most strongly illuminated rectennas contribute, while full-band (26 GHz–30 GHz) characterization of gain, axial ratio, and rectifier efficiency confirms robust performance across the 5G/mmWave band. Dielectric lens-enabled broadband mmWave energy harvester

Broadband aperture coupled stacked patch based rectenna

A rectenna enables efficient RF-to-DC conversion by combining an antenna that captures incident signals with a rectifier that produces usable output power. To operate effectively in complex and dynamic environments, the antenna must exhibit high gain over a wide field of view, broadband characteristics, and a straightforward connection to the rectifier. To address polarization mismatch and orientation sensitivity, circular polarization was implemented, enabling robust power capture across arbitrary tag orientations and in multi-path environments36. This capability is particularly beneficial in scenarios where the transmitter orientation cannot be controlled, and it further allows seamless integration with existing 5G/mmWave infrastructure by being more orientation-tolerant. This capability is particularly beneficial in multipath environments or in cases where the orientation of the transmitter cannot be controlled, allowing easy integration with current 5G/mmWave infrastructure by being more orientation-tolerant.

Thus, to achieve compactness while maintaining broadband performance, a circularly polarized aperture-coupled stacked patch antenna, with operational bandwidth from 24 - 32 GHz, is employed. This design consists of four layers: the parasitic layer, the radiating layer, the ground slot, and the feed line37–39. The stacked parasitic and radiating patches are carefully designed to support multiple resonant modes, enhancing the overall bandwidth of the multi-layer antenna. The top parasitic patch is not directly fed but enhances overall antenna performance by increasing gain and widening the bandwidth. Beneath it, the radiating patch captures incoming electromagnetic waves and directs them through the ground slot. The ground plane includes a slot that couples energy to the microstrip feed line. Circular polarization was achieved by truncating the corners of the patch radiators in the parasitic and radiating layers. In addition to meeting the minimal requirements for the receiving antenna, the chosen configuration enables the antenna and DC signal to be separated on different layers, guaranteeing minimal coupling between the antenna and the rectifier. Biconvex lens-enabled mmwave focalization for broadbeam energy harvesting

To enable both high-gain performance and wide total solid angular coverage, a 3D biconvex lens was developed to focus incoming mmWave signals onto the antenna array. By directing and concentrating the electromagnetic signal, a lens helps increase the directivity and overall efficiency of the antenna, allowing for better signal capture across a large angular span40–46. This makes dielectric lenses ideal for energy harvesting systems, as they can focus incoming RF signals onto the harvesting antenna, boosting the signal strength and improving energy capture efficiency24,35. For the bi-convex lens, three main parameters shaped the design process: focal length, surface curvature, and diameter. The focal length, in particular, determines how wide of an angular space the lens can cover. This is expressed as the angular field of view (AOF), which can be calculated using1\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \textrm{AOF} = 2\arctan \left( \frac{h}{F} \right) , \end{aligned}$$\end{document}Here, h is the height of the harvester board in (mm) and F is the focal length of the lens in (mm), respectively. Utilizing an antenna topology similar to35,44, which is discussed in more detail in the following section, and to achieve an overall compact system size, h was set to 27 mm. By setting a minimum angular field of view of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${110}^\circ$$\end{document}, the focal length of the system was calculated to be 17 mm. With this estimated focal length, the front and back curvatures of the lens were optimized using ray-tracing simulations in Optometrika. These simulations, carried out at 27 GHz under normal incidence, helped identify the curvature values that would produce the desired focusing behavior of the bi-convex lens. The final design used symmetric curvatures of 55 mm and −55 mm for the front and rear surfaces, respectively, offering efficient energy focusing across the lens aperture. Utilizing these optimized curvatures, the standard deviation of this ray bundle that intersected the antenna board was recorded at varying focal length and is displayed in Fig. 4(a), confirming that the dielectric lens provides the desired focalization at the previously calculated focal length of 17 mm.

To evaluate its RF capabilities, the dielectric lens was then modeled in CST Microwave Studio and placed in front of the aperture-coupled stacked patch antenna at the previously calculated focal length. To further optimize the lens for both high gain and wide angular coverage performance, a parametric sweep was performed on the lens diameter, ranging from 40 mm to 74 mm in 2 mm steps. As shown in Fig. 4 (b), the 54 mm version achieved the best compromise between gain and angular coverage, delivering up to 22.7 dBi of realized gain while covering \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${114}^\circ$$\end{document}. These broadband capabilities, combined with the high gain and wide angular coverage, are particularly advantageous for energy harvesting, as they enable consistent energy capture from a variety of RF sources across different environments.

Design of the lens-based rectenna ‘Pixel’ array and power summation network

Combining the optimized bi-convex dielectric lens and the broadband circularly polarized mmWave rectennas, a proof-of-concept energy harvester prototype was constructed, visualized in Fig. 4 (d). The harvester prototype comprises 25 rectenna elements arranged in three concentric circles with diameters of 18 mm, 36 mm, and 54 mm. Each circle consists eight evenly spaced rectennas, with a single additional element positioned at the center of the harvester, as illustrated in Fig. 4(e). An inter-element spacing of 9 mm was selected as it represents the minimum distance required to minimize coupling between adjacent rectennas, while also providing sufficient space for routing the DC lines for each rectifier on the bottom layer of the board. Additionally, this configuration allows for the proposed system to achieve a spherical 3 dB angular coverage of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\pm {55}^\circ$$\end{document} around boresight, as well as effective operation irrespective of the harvesters orientation, ensuring consistent performance and coverage across all positions.

To efficiently manage the spatially varying power captured across the rectenna array, a passive power summation network was developed to consolidate the harvested energy while minimizing losses from under-illuminated elements. As visualized in Fig. 4 (f), the network incorporates a diode-based bypass structure, enabling the exclusion of rectennas that receive minimal RF power due to the transmitting signals angle of incidence. Specifically, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$(N - 1)$$\end{document} low-threshold Schottky diodes (Toshiba 1SS384TE85LF, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$V_{\text {on}} \approx$$\end{document} 0.15 V) were deployed to allow current to circumvent inactive rectifiers, preventing them from impeding the overall output. This arrangement ensures that only the two most excited rectenna elements contribute to the final output, thereby preserving high conversion efficiency across varying incident directions18,23. This summation network topology works well with the lens-based design, where the incoming signal naturally shifts to different rectennas depending on its incidence angle, enabling orientation-tolerant harvesting.

The results from this analysis, shown in Fig. 6(b), revealed a significant enhancement in harvested power due to the lens, with variations in power corresponding to the beamwidth of each illuminated rectenna. These variations arise from the lens tightly focalizing energy onto individual rectennas in sequence, producing distinct peaks as the angle of incidence shifts across the row. While a flatter response could be achieved by designing for weaker focalization, this would reduce realized gain and ultimately lower the peak harvested power. The chosen design therefore prioritizes maximizing gain and total captured power, while still maintaining robust harvesting performance across wide angular ranges. The harvester achieved a peak harvested power up to 11 mW at boresight, with seven distinct peaks observed, each corresponding to one of the rectennas along the array, resulting in angular coverage of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\pm {55}^\circ$$\end{document}. To further assess the system’s wide angular coverage and the effectiveness of the power summation network, a multi-transmitter experiment was performed. As illustrated in Fig. 6(d), two transmitting antennas were positioned 1.5 m from the harvester, each delivering an identical power density of 0.25 \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\text {mWcm}^{-2}$$\end{document}. In this setup, both horns were supplied by a single RF generator through a power splitter. The harvester does not depend on the relative phase of the signals, and the integrated DC summation network simply adds the contributions from the illuminated rectennas. This makes the configuration equivalent to the case of multiple independent transmitters, where the combiner ensures that all available incident power is delivered to the output. Unlike communication receivers that rely on coherent detection, the rectifier operates based on total received power. Any phase difference between transmitters only affects the instantaneous RF field and not the average incident power at the aperture. As a result, the harvested output remains determined by the combined power density rather than the relative phase between sources. Conclusion

In this work, the authors present a scalable, broadband, dielectric lens-based mmWave energy harvester with wide total solid angular coverage and mW harvesting capabilities. The proposed system consists of a ’pixel’ array of rectennas, each made up of a circularly polarized aperture coupled stacked patch antenna and a broadband high sensitivity rectifier, with a bi-convex dielectric lens to achieve a wide solid angle coverage of 2.68 sr, allowing for effective energy capture across a wide range of angles. The single unit cell harvester achieved a peak captured power up to 11 mW in a proof-of-concept demonstration utilizing a single transmitting source. When scaled to a 2\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\times$$\end{document}2 structure, the lens-based rectenna was able to harvest up to 82 mW when utilizing two transmitters, each with incident power density of 0.25 \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\text {mW/cm}^{2}$$\end{document}. By achieving both high harvested power and total solid angle coverage, the proposed system surpasses the performance of any existing state-of-the-art broadbeam mmWave energy harvester. When utilizing the full 75 dBm EIRP available at 5G/mmWave, the proposed scaled harvester can theoretically capture a peak power up to 105 mW in addition to harvesting mW levels of power at ranges extending up to 120 m. While the design employs several rectenna elements and diodes, the use of standard multilayer PCB processes and inexpensive dielectric machining ensures the system remains cost-effective. This balance of efficiency, scalability, and orientation-tolerant performance more than offsets the added complexity, yielding a favorable cost benefit tradeoff. Owing to its capability to harvest power over a wide solid-angle coverage, the proposed system offers a robust solution for enabling continuous and efficient energy supply in diverse orientations. This characteristic is particularly advantageous for supporting the deployment of next-generation smart city applications, such as distributed sensor networks, autonomous infrastructure monitoring, and communication gateways mounted on buildings, poles, or vehicles, as well as low-power IoT devices operating in dynamic urban environments. To the best the authors knowledge, this is the first mmWave ambient energy harvester approaching 100 mW, enabling for the first time the powering of practical devices such as distributed sensing nodes, low-power wireless transceivers, and localization or identification tags directly from harvested energy.

mmWave energy harvesting analysis

Millimeter-wave energy harvesting relies on converting incident RF power into usable DC through a high-frequency rectifier connected to a compact, high-gain antenna. Due to the short wavelength at mmWave, even small aperture antennas can provide high gain. However, the available power at the receiver drops sharply with distance, making both gain and beam alignment critical.

The received power in dBm is given by:5\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} P_{\textrm{Rx}} = P_{\textrm{Tx}} + G_{\textrm{Tx}} + G_{\textrm{Rx}} - L_{\textrm{path}}(R) \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$P_{\textrm{Tx}}$$\end{document} is the transmit power, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$G_{\textrm{Tx}}$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$G_{\textrm{Rx}}$$\end{document} are the transmit and receive antenna gains, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$L_{\textrm{path}}$$\end{document} is the total path loss at range \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$R$$\end{document}. In realistic environments, the path loss is modeled using a propagation exponent \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$n$$\end{document}:6\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} L_{\textrm{path}} = 20\log _{10}\left( \frac{4\pi }{\lambda }\right) + 10n\log _{10}(R) \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\lambda$$\end{document} is the free-space wavelength in meters. For mmWave links in open environments, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$n \approx 2$$\end{document}, while higher values account for blockage or clutter.

The captured RF signal is delivered to a rectifier consisting of a broadband matching network and a nonlinear diode. The resulting DC output power is:7\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} P_{\textrm{DC}} = \eta (P_{\textrm{Rx}}, f) \cdot P_{\textrm{Rx}} \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\eta$$\end{document} is the power conversion efficiency, which depends on both frequency and input power level. The matching network is designed to optimize power transfer at the expected operating point. Short-circuited or radial stubs are used to present a virtual RF ground while preserving the DC path.

In lens-based systems, the incoming beam is focused onto different areas of the rectenna array depending on the angle of arrival. As the angle shifts, the power is concentrated on a different subset of rectifiers. A passive bypass network routes current only from the illuminated elements, ensuring that inactive branches do not affect overall efficiency. This spatial redistribution allows the system to maintain output across a wide angular range without requiring mechanical alignment. The lens handles beam steering passively, and the summation network adaptively collects power from whichever rectennas are most strongly excited.
