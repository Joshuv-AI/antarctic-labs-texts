Study on the mechanical behavior of ballastless track continuous welded rail on multi-span simply supported steel truss bridge for mixed passenger and freight railway
Authors: Hao Xu, Kaize Xie, Jiayin Chen, Weixing Liu, Hongsong Lin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Ballastless track continuous welded rails (CWR) are used on a multi-span 100 m simply supported steel truss bridge of a mixed passenger and freight railway, to study the mechanical characteristics and influence factors of CWR on the 100 m simply supported steel truss bridge, a track-bridge-piers spatial finite element model was established based on the track-bridge interaction (TBI) principle. The influence of design parameters such as the number of bridge spans, the longitudinal stiffness of piers of the simply supported steel truss bridge, the arrangement of bridge bearings, the type of rail, and the track longitudinal resistance type on the mechanical characteristics of CWR on the simply supported steel truss bridge were systematically examined. The research results indicate that for multi-span simply supported steel truss bridges, the model can be simplified by considering 8 spans when the total number of spans exceeds 8. Likewise, when the number of adjacent concrete box girder spans exceeds 5, 5 spans can be adopted. The longitudinal stiffness of piers of the simply supported steel truss bridge has a significant influence on the rail braking force and the rail broken gap. Increasing the longitudinal stiffness of the piers from 399kN/cm to 5000kN/cm has a much greater effect on the forces and deformations of CWR on the bridge than increasing it further from 5000kN/cm to 10000kN/cm. The bridge expansion length is primarily governed by the arrangement of fixed bearings on the adjacent concrete box girder. Therefore, to reduce the forces in the CWR on the bridge, the simply supported steel truss bridges and the adjacent concrete box girders should adopt a consistent bearing arrangement. In addition, rails with a larger cross-sectional area can reduce the additional rail stress. The used of small resistance fastener systems for ballastless track decreases the rail expansion force and rail braking force by 56.72% and 18.05%, respectively, but it increases the rail broken gap by 37.49%. The research results can provide references for the design of CWR on the simply supported steel truss bridge.

Introduction

Due to its advantages in enhancing ride comfort and reducing track maintenance workload1–4, continuous welded rail (CWR) on bridges has been widely adopted in railway networks worldwide. Consequently, extensive research has been conducted on the track-bridge interaction (TBI) mechanisms for CWR on conventional bridge types, such as simply supported and continuous concrete bridges5–15. Song5 and Ruge6 proposed models and calculation methods for CWR on bridges. Wang et al.7 provided a detailed analysis of the TBI mechanisms for various track types on high-speed railway bridges, along with CWR condition detection and monitoring techniques. Gao8 investigated the forces and deformations in ballastless track CWR on long-span continuous girder bridges. Lou et al.9 established a coupled CWR-bridge model incorporating longitudinal nonlinear stiffness and examined the effects of nonlinear stiffness, number of spans, constant longitudinal restoring force, bearing arrangement, and bridge span length on the additional longitudinal rail stress and longitudinal rail displacement. Xu et al.10 investigated the effects of frictional resistance of movable bearings and longitudinal stiffness of bridge piers on the longitudinal forces and deformations of CWR on continuous girder bridge. Neridu et al.11 studied the influence of rail cross-sectional area, track longitudinal resistance, and bridge length on the distribution of longitudinal forces in CWR on a simply supported bridge. Papp et al.12 examined the effect of bridge expansion length on the forces of CWR on simply supported bridges. Diachenko et al.13 introduced a calculation method for CWR forces on bridges and investigated the impact of bridge span and pier longitudinal horizontal stiffness on the longitudinal forces. Xie et al.14 developed an integrated rail-guardrail-sleeper-bridge-pier simulation model, and investigated the influence of guardrail on the CWR in continuous girder bridges. Luo et al.15 proposed a new variation pattern of the longitudinal resistance of the fasteners considering loading history, and an algorithm for track-bridge interaction analysis considering loading history was derived based on the Ritz method and the principle of minimum potential energy, which was applied in a case study of an N-span, simply-supported girder bridge in a high-speed railway with a typical loading sequence. Through extensive research and engineering practice, corresponding design codes for CWR on bridges have been established16,17.

With ongoing advances in railway engineering, CWR has been increasingly adopted for special bridge types, including continuous rigid frame bridges, arch bridges, suspension bridges, and cable-stayed bridges. Regarding CWR on special bridges, Wang et al.18 proposed a calculation model and a simplified method for CWR on continuous rigid frame bridges, and validated the simplified model using an actual bridge calculation model. Yan et al.19 investigated the distribution patterns of longitudinal rail forces on arch bridges with different spans, exploring the influence of CWR layout on the mechanical behavior and deformation of CWR on arch bridges. Cai et al.20 and Yu et al.21 studied the deformation and mechanical characteristics of CWR on suspension bridges. Cai et al.22 examined the distribution patterns of longitudinal rail forces on cable-stayed bridges under various conditions and proposed an optimized arrangement method for rail expansion joints. Xie et al.23 introduced a calculation method for CWR on cable-stayed bridges that considers geometric nonlinearity and initial internal forces, and further investigated the mechanical behavior of CWR in such bridges.

The simply supported steel truss bridges have been increasingly adopted in railway engineering in recent years, owing to their advantages such as large span capacity, light self-weight, well-defined load paths, and rapid construction. Representative examples include the Yellow River Bridge on the Baotou-Shenmu Railway, the Yongning Yellow River Bridge on the Taiyuan-Zhongwei-Yinchuan Railway, and the New Yellow River Bridge on the Huang-Han-Hou railway24–26. Consequently, it is necessary to investigate the mechanical behavior of CWR on such bridges. Taking a single-span simply supported steel truss bridge as a case study, Chen et al.27 established a coupled track–bridge interaction model for CWR on spatial steel truss bridges and analyzed the longitudinal additional forces in CWR with and without stringer disconnection. Qu et al.28 developed a model of an 8 × 33.7 m simply supported steel truss bridge to examine the influence of different arrangement patterns of the K-type fastener system on the forces and deformations of CWR. Jiang et al.29 focused on a single span 80 m open-deck steel truss bridge, and evaluated the effects of braking bracket settings and longitudinal beam disconnection on the longitudinal additional forces of CWR. Yan et al.24 described the distribution pattern of longitudinal forces of CWR on a single span 156 m simply supported steel truss bridge. Lin25, Sha26, and Yu et al.30–32, focusing on a specific single-span 156 m simply supported steel truss bridge on the Huang-Han-Hou railway, investigated the impacts of steel truss configuration, track longitudinal resistance type, bearing arrangement of adjacent simply supported girders, and the longitudinal stiffness of piers on the forces and deformations of CWR. Miri et al.33 addressed the issue of fatigue cracks induced by wheel impact loads on jointed tracks on steel truss bridges and proposed replacing jointed tracks with CWR as a mitigation measure. Mirković et al.34 studied a single-span 60 m simply supported and a 2 × 60 m continuous steel truss bridge, and examined the effects of rail cross-section, track longitudinal resistance, bridge expansion length, and pier longitudinal horizontal stiffness on the additional temperature stresses in the CWR. Overall, existing studies on CWR on steel truss bridges have primarily focused on ballasted tracks and single-span simply supported steel truss bridges. There is a notable lack of studies addressing CWR on multi-span simply supported steel truss bridges, especially using ballastless track, and its influencing factors. Since ballastless tracks generally exhibit higher longitudinal resistance and more direct force transmission, it is necessary to conduct a systematic investigation into the mechanical behavior of CWR with ballastless track on multi-span simply supported steel truss bridges, considering the effects of various bridge and track parameters.

This paper investigates ballastless track CWR on a 5 × 100 m simply supported steel truss bridge of the Padma Bridge Rail Link Project in Bangladesh. Based on the TBI principle, a computational model for the CWR on the bridge is established, considering the track system and adjacent simply supported concrete box girders. The model is used to investigate the influence of key design parameters including the number of bridge spans, the longitudinal stiffness of piers of the simply supported steel truss bridge, the arrangement of bridge bearings, the rail type and the track longitudinal resistance type on the longitudinal forces of ballastless track CWR on the multi-span steel truss bridge for mixed passenger and freight railway, which could provide a reference for the design of CWR on the multi-span steel truss bridge for a mixed passenger and freight railway.

Numerical model and parameters

Numerical model

The Padma Bridge Rail Link Project, designed by China, is a single-track railway for mixed passenger and freight. A 100 m simply supported steel truss bridge is adopted to cross the Dhaleshwari River. The bridge span arrangement comprises 65 × 38 m concrete box girders + 5 × 100 m simply supported steel truss bridges + 21 × 38 m concrete box girders, as shown in Fig. 1. In Fig. 1, △ represents fixed bearings, while ○ denotes movable bearings.

Fig. 1The bridge span arrangement of the simply supported steel truss bridge and the adjacent concrete box girder.

The bridge span arrangement of the simply supported steel truss bridge and the adjacent concrete box girder.

To simplify the computation, the adjacent 38 m concrete box girders were approximated as a 5-span arrangement in the simulation. An integrated spatial finite element model of the track-bridge-pier system was established using the general-purpose finite element software ANSYS 12.0. In the model, BEAM188 elements were used to simulate structural members of the simply-supported steel truss girder, including the top chord, bottom chord, vertical members, diagonal members, top end cross beam, top intermediate cross beam, bottom end cross beam, bottom intermediate cross beam, bottom longitudinal beam, and web members. SHELL63 elements were employed to simulate the concrete bridge deck of the simply-supported steel truss girder. As the deck is rigidly connected to the bottom cross beam via shear studs, rigid arm elements were used to couple the deck and the bottom cross beam. BEAM54 elements were adopted to model the 38 m simply-supported girder. COMBIN14 linear elements were used to represent the longitudinal stiffness of the bridge piers. BEAM3 elements were utilized to simulate the rails. The interaction between the track and the bridge was modeled using COMBIN39 nonlinear spring elements connecting the rails to the bridge. Model parameters were assigned based on design documentation and the design code16,17. To eliminate the influence of boundary effects on the forces within the CWR on the bridge, the length of the subgrade sections at both ends of the bridge was set to 200m35. Based on the TBI principle, the computational model is shown in Fig. 2.

Fig. 2The numerical model of CWR on the simply supported steel truss bridge.

The numerical model of CWR on the simply supported steel truss bridge.

In the finite element model, the bottom of the subgrade section was fully constrained, the bridge fixed bearings were fully constrained, while the movable bearings were constrained in the vertical and transverse directions but remained free in the longitudinal direction to allow expansion and contraction. The ends of the rails were left unconstrained.

Calculation parameters of the track

The track on the bridge adopts a single-layer long-sleeper embedded ballastless track, and ballastless track slab is connected to the concrete deck slab throuth four rows of “door-shaped” reinforcing bars36,37. Due to the effective connection between the ballastless track slab and the concrete deck slab, the longitudinal resistance of the track is taken as equivalent to the longitudinal resistance provided by the fastener system in the model.

A UIC60 rail is adopted, with a cross-sectional area of 76.70cm2 and a moment of inertia of 3038.30cm4. The longitudinal resistance of the fastener system is described using a bilinear model. According to Chinese code16, the longitudinal resistance of the constant resistance fastener system for ballastless track is shown in Fig. 3.

Load parameters and loading scheme

In the absence of specific domestic provisions for temperature variations of ballastless tracks on steel bridges in China, the temperature change of the steel truss girder was determined with reference to UIC code 774 − 317, adopting a temperature drop of 35℃ for the steel structure. For the concrete deck slab and the adjacent concrete box girders, a temperature drop of 30 °C was adopted according to TB 10,015–201216. Results and discussion

Under the computational parameters and loading conditions described above, the mechanical behavior of the CWR on the multi-span 100 m simply supported steel truss bridge is illustrated in Fig. 6.

Fig. 6The mechanical behavior of the CWR on the multi-span simply supported steel truss bridge: (a) Rail expansion force; (b) Rail bending force; (c) Rail braking force; (d) Rail broken gap.

The mechanical behavior of the CWR on the multi-span simply supported steel truss bridge: (a) Rail expansion force; (b) Rail bending force; (c) Rail braking force; (d) Rail broken gap.

As shown in Fig. 6, under a temperature drop of 35℃ for the steel truss bridge and 30℃ for the concrete deck slab and the adjacent concrete box girders, the maximum tensile rail expansion force occurs near the movable bearing of the right abutment, with a value of 586.65kN. This is because the fixed bearing is located on the left side of the bridge; during cooling, the bridge contracts towards to the fixed bearing side, inducing tension in the rail at the right end. Under train loading, the maximum rail compressive force due to bending occurs in bending condition 4, with a value of 105.19kN. The maximum rail tensile force due to bending occurs in bending condition 1, with a value of 61.23kN. The maximum rail braking force occurs in braking condition 4, with a value of 611.57kN. When the rail experiences a temperature drop of 50 °C, the maximum rail broken gap is 67.94 mm, which satisfies the requirement that the gap should not exceed 70mm16.

Since the track on the bridge adopts a ballastless track, track stability is not considered to be governing in this study. Therefore, when analyzing the influence of different factors, only their effects on rail tensile forces are investigated. Given that the maximum tensile bending force occurs in bending condition 1 and the maximum braking force occurs in braking condition 4, the subsequent analyses of bending forces and braking forces consider the load arrangements for bending condition 1 and braking condition 4, respectively.

To investigate the influence of bridge and track parameters on the CWR on multi-span simply supported steel truss bridges, the bridge parameters considered include the number of bridge spans, the longitudinal stiffness of the piers of the simply supported steel truss bridge, and the bearing arrangements of adjacent simply supported concrete box girder. The track parameters considered include the rail type and the track longitudinal resistance type.

Number of bridge spans

By varying the number of spans of simply supported steel truss bridges and the adjacent simply supported concrete box girders, the influence of the number of bridge spans on the mechanical behavior of CWR on the bridge was investigated. The number of simply supported steel truss bridge spans was set to 1, 3, 5, 8, 10, and 15 spans, while the number of adjacent simply supported concrete box girder spans was set to 0, 1, 3, 5, 8, 10, and 15 spans. The influence of different bridge spans on the longitudinal force and deformation of CWR on the bridge is shown in Fig. 7.

Fig. 7The effect of different bridge span numbers on the mechanical characteristics of CWR on the bridge: (a) simply supported steel truss bridge; (b) concrete box girders.

The effect of different bridge span numbers on the mechanical characteristics of CWR on the bridge: (a) simply supported steel truss bridge; (b) concrete box girders.

As shown in Fig. 7a, with the increase in the number of simply supported steel truss bridge spans, both the rail expansion force and the rail broken gap increase, while the rail bending force and rail braking force decrease.

With the number of adjacent simply supported concrete girder spans set at 5, when the number of simply supported steel truss bridge spans is 1, the rail expansion force, rail bending force, rail braking force, and rail broken gap are 457.94kN, 126.19kN, 1017.34kN, and 56.30 mm. When the number of simply supported steel truss bridge spans increases to 5, these values become 586.65kN, 61.23kN, 611.57kN, and 67.94 mm. When the number of spans is 8, the corresponding values are 588.97kN, 61.25kN, 613.10kN, and 67.87 mm. It can be observed that when the number of simply supported steel truss bridge spans increases from 1 to 5, the rail expansion force and the rail broken gap increase by 28.11% and 20.67%, respectively, while the rail bending force and rail braking force decrease by 51.48% and 39.89%, respectively. When the number of spans further increases from 5 to 8, the rail expansion force, rail bending force, and rail braking force increase by 0.39%, 0.03%, and 0.25%, respectively, and the rail broken gap decreases by 0.10%. When the number of spans exceeds 8, the rail expansion force, rail bending force, rail braking force, and rail broken gap remain essentially constant.

As shown in Fig. 7b, with the increase in the number of adjacent simply supported concrete box girder spans, the rail expansion force, rail braking force, and rail broken gap decrease, while the rail bending force increases, and then remains essentially constant. When the number of adjacent simply supported concrete box girder spans is 0, the rail expansion force, rail bending force, rail braking force and rail broken gap are 773.97kN, 59.59kN, 785.45kN and 69.09 mm. When the number of adjacent simply supported concrete box girder spans is 5, the rail expansion force, rail bending force, rail braking force and rail broken gap are 586.65kN, 61.23kN, 611.57kN and 67.94 mm. When the number of adjacent simply supported concrete box girder spans is 8, the rail expansion force, rail bending force, rail braking force and rail broken gap are 584.41kN, 61.23kN, 610.48kN and 67.62 mm. When the number of adjacent simply supported concrete box girder spans increases from 0 to 5, the rail expansion force, rail braking force, and rail broken gap decrease by 24.20%, 22.13%, and 1.66% respectively, while the bending force increases by 2.75%. This demonstrates that arranging simply supported concrete box girders on both sides of the steel truss bridge can effectively reduce the forces in the CWR on the bridge. When the number of adjacent simply supported concrete box girder spans increases from 5 to 8, the rail expansion force, rail braking force, and rail broken gap further decrease by 0.38%, 0.18%, and 0.47% respectively, while the rail bending force remains unchanged.

Based on the influence of the number of simply supported steel truss bridge spans and adjacent simply supported concrete box girder spans on the forces and deformations of the CWR on the bridge, it is observed that when the number of steel truss bridge spans exceeds 8 and the number of adjacent simply supported concrete box girder spans exceeds 5, further increase in span numbers has negligible effects on the rail forces and deformations. Therefore, to simplify the calculation, when the number of simply supported steel truss bridge spans exceeds 8 spans, only 8 spans need to be considered in the analysis. If the actual number is less than 8 spans, all spans should be considered. Similarly, when the number of adjacent simply supported concrete box girder spans exceeds 5 spans, only 5 spans need to be considered, while all spans should be included if the number is less than 5. Therefore, in subsequent analyses in this paper, both the simply supported steel truss bridges and adjacent simply supported concrete box girders are modeled with 5 spans.

As shown in Table 5, with the increase in the longitudinal stiffness of piers of the simply supported steel truss bridge, the rail expansion force increases, while the rail bending force, rail braking force and rail broken gap decrease. Regarding the rail expansion force and the rail broken gap, higher longitudinal stiffness of piers imposes stronger constraints on the rail and the bridge displacement. This results in greater rail expansion force near the movable bearings and reduced rail broken gap. For rail braking force, as the longitudinal stiffness of piers of the simply supported steel truss bridge increases, the intermediate piers will bear most of the forces, resulting in a decrease in rail braking force.

For the rail expansion force on a simply supported steel truss bridge, when the longitudinal stiffness of the pier increases from 399kN/cm to 5000kN/cm, the rail expansion force rises from 586.65kN to 590.74kN, representing an increase of 0.69%. When the longitudinal stiffness of the piers further increases, the rail expansion force remains constant.

As the longitudinal stiffness of the pier increases from 399kN/cm to 5000kN/cm, the rail bending force, rail braking force, and rail broken gap decrease from 61.23kN, 611.57kN, and 67.94 mm to 59.33kN, 123.49kN, and 55.18 mm, representing reductions of 3.10%, 79.81%, and 18.78%, respectively. When the pier stiffness further increases from 5000kN/cm to 10000kN/cm, the rail bending force, rail braking force, and rail broken gap decrease from 59.33kN, 123.49kN, and 55.18 mm to 58.96kN, 85.23kN, and 54.17 mm, representing reductions of 0.62%, 30.98%, and 1.83%, respectively.

When the longitudinal horizontal stiffness of the piers increases from 399kN/cm to 10000kN/cm, the rail expansion force increases by 0.69%, while the rail bending force, rail braking force, and rail broken gap decrease by 3.71%, 86.06%, and 20.27% respectively. Increasing the longitudinal stiffness of the piers from 399kN/cm to 5000kN/cm has a much greater effect on the forces and deformations of CWR on the bridge than increasing it further from 5000kN/cm to 10000kN/cm.

It is evident that the longitudinal stiffness of the piers of the simply supported steel truss bridges has a significantly greater influence on the rail braking force compared to the rail expansion force, rail bending force, and rail broken gap. Moreover, as the pier stiffness increases, the rate of change in the rail expansion force, rail bending force, rail braking force, and rail broken gap gradually diminishes. To reduce the mechanical loads on the CWR on the bridge, appropriately increasing the longitudinal stiffness of the piers can be considered. However, enhancing pier stiffness typically leads to higher bridge construction costs. Therefore, the optimal longitudinal stiffness of the piers should be determined by comprehensively balancing the mechanical behavior of the rail on the bridge and overall project economy.

As shown in Fig. 8, the bearing arrangements of adjacent simply supported concrete box girders primarily influence the rail expansion force and the rail broken gap. The force and deformation of CWR on the bridge are nearly identical between scheme 1 and scheme 3, as well as between scheme 2 and scheme 4. Notably, the rail expansion forces and rail broken gaps in schemes 2 and 4 are significantly greater than those in schemes 1 and 3.

This is because, in the scheme 2 and scheme 4, the fixed bearing direction of the right side simply supported concrete box girder opposite to that of the simply supported steel truss bridge, which increases the bridge expansion length and consequently leads to higher rail expansion forces and larger rail broken gaps. The maximum bridge expansion length is 138 m in the schemes 2 and 4, while the bridge expansion length is 100 m in schemes 1 and 3.

Taking scheme 1 and scheme 2 as examples, the rail expansion force and rail broken gap are 586.65kN and 67.94 mm in scheme 1, the rail expansion force and rail broken gap are 713.67kN and 77.18 mm in scheme 2, which increase by 21.65% and 13.60%, respectively. Therefore, to reduce the force of CWR on multi-span simply supported steel truss bridges, it is recommended to adopt a consistent fixed bearing arrangement between the steel truss bridge and adjacent simply supported concrete box girders.

Conclusions

For ballastless track CWR on 100 m simply supported steel truss bridges with adjacent 38 m concrete box girder, a computational model for CWR on multi-span 100 m simply supported steel truss bridges was developed based on TBI principle. The influence of bridge parameters and track parameters such as the number of bridge spans, the longitudinal stiffness of piers of the simply supported steel truss bridge, the arrangement of bridge bearings, rail type and the track longitudinal resistance type on the mechanical behavior of CWR on the simply supported steel truss bridge for mixed passenger and freight railway were investigated. The main conclusions are summarized as follows:

(1) When the number of simply supported steel truss bridge spans exceeds 8 and the number of adjacent simply supported concrete box girder spans exceeds 5, the influence on the force of CWR on the bridge becomes negligible. To simplify calculations, the “8-span & 5-span” principle may be adopted in modeling, where simply supported steel truss bridge spans exceeding 8 are taken as 8 spans, and adjacent simply supported concrete box girder spans exceeding 5 are taken as 5 spans.

(2) Increasing the longitudinal stiffness of piers of the simply supported steel truss bridge significantly reduces the rail braking force and rail broken gap. When the longitudinal stiffness of piers of the simply supported steel truss bridge increases from 399kN/cm to 5000kN/cm, the rail braking force and rail broken gap decrease by 79.81% and 18.78%, respectively. When the pier stiffness further increases from 5000kN/cm to 10000kN/cm, rail braking force and rail broken gap decrease by 30.98% and 1.83%, respectively.

(3) The bridge expansion length is directly determined by the arrangement of fixed bearings on the adjacent concrete box girder. Therefore, to reduce the forces in the CWR on the bridge, the simply supported steel truss bridges and the adjacent concrete box girders should adopt a consistent bearing arrangement.

(4) Increasing the rail cross-sectional area reduces additional rail stresses in CWR on the bridges. From the perspective of controlling rail stresses, larger cross-sectional rail types should be prioritized.

(5) The longitudinal resistance of the track has a significant influence on the forces and deformations of the CWR on the bridge. Using the ballastless track small resistance fastener system of China can reduce the rail expansion force and rail braking force by 56.72% and 18.05%, respectively, but increases the rail broken gap by 37.49%. Therefore, the selection of the track longitudinal resistance type should be comprehensively considered in the design.
