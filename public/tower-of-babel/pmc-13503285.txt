The reverberations of famine: understanding the aftermath of the Holodomor, 1932–1933
Authors: Johnathon Vsetecka
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This article uses the 1932–1933 famine in Ukraine, now commonly known as the Holodomor, as a case study for understanding the short-term and long-term implications of famine on communities and the world. Much of the scholarship focused on famine continues to focus on the origins of famines and preventative famine modeling, but far fewer works have fully engaged with the repercussions of famine that follow mass starvation. This article argues that the reverberating effects of famines must be understood as part of the total famine experience, and it demonstrates how the Holodomor provides a useful multi-pronged model for understanding the aftermath of famine through social/cultural, political, global, and medical/health contexts. As such, the example of the Holodomor offers us new perspectives that can be used to model the aftershocks of famine that continue to resonate in both local and larger global contexts.

Introduction

Interest in the 1932–33 famine in Ukraine, now commonly known as the Holodomor, has come a long way. While the causes and consequences of the famine were studied acutely, the question of whether the Holodomor met the qualifications of genocide dominated much of the discourse and writing on the subject. Beyond having important implications for understanding the famine as one purposefully designed to starve Ukrainians—which current research supports—the focus on genocide, along with demography and death statistics, shaped and transformed research foci for years to come [1]. However, this concentrated focus on one aspect of the famine left wide gaps in research agendas, especially regarding additional effects present in the aftermath of the famine, that are just beginning to be filled.

The emergence of the famine as a serious topic of study during the Cold War period positioned it to become one of the most debated famines in history, especially regarding the question of intent, to enter academic discourse. Although interest in the famine was still quite small and considered a niche topic during that time, the Holodomor nonetheless became a focus for many scholars in the 1980s due to a number of converging factors. In the twilight years of the Soviet Union’s existence, scholars first began to study the Holodomor with academic rigor, which led both Ukrainian and non-Ukrainian scholars to produce an impressive amount of literature on the topic [2]. Despite the amount of scholarship that has been dedicated to the famine, what we know about the aftermath of the Holodomor remains understudied. The term ‘aftermath’ can be interpreted in many forms, but it is used here to designate more generally how the implications of famine during the years of hunger continued to reverberate, in varied ways, beyond the formal end of the famine in 1933. To engage in such a study is to move beyond the traditional temporal orders that have been assigned to the Holodomor. As Alon Confino states, “Linking the events to what came before and after is crucial to the interpretation of what actually happened” ([3] p.67). So, while a great deal of scholarship continues to understand the events leading up to the famine and the peak months of mass starvation, far fewer works have concerned themselves with detailed studies of the period after the famine [4]. This article provides an example of how we can model the repercussions that inevitably follow the occurrence of mass starvation through time from its formal end to the present.

This article contends that in order to better understand the full scope and impact of the 1932–1933 Holodomor, the effects and consequences that continued to reverberate in its aftermath must be considered and understood as part of the total famine experience. In other words, famines do not simply occur and then go away. Long after starvation passes, effects of famine continue to manifest in varied ways that continue to preoccupy those who lived through them. The Holodomor is a particularly useful example for helping us understand the multitude of ramifications that famines leave in their wake, giving us examples from past and present that inform our understanding of human-made catastrophes and their afterlives. Specifically, this article offers a three-pronged model for tracing the reverberations of the Holodomor in social, cultural, global, and medical contexts that articulate how profound the aftershocks of famines are on people and societies in both the short and long-term.

The aftermath in cultural and social contexts

To model the aftermath of the Holodomor means to trace its wake. Conceptually, it is useful to think about a ship passing through water and leaving tracks on the water’s surface. In this scenario, the ship is a stand-in for the famine. To study the ship is to understand how it got there, its purpose upon arrival, and its date of departure. But after the ship appears out of view, the ripple effects pushed outward by it are still present on the water, moving in different directions and shaping currents and flows. While the ripples in the water eventually dissipate, they do not immediately go away. The metaphor of the ship passing through water is useful for thinking about the Holodomor. Just like the tracks in the water, the effects of the 1932–33 famine continued to ebb and flow across Ukraine and beyond. In describing the end of the famine as a process, Anne Applebaum notes, “But instead of ending abruptly that summer, the tragedy slowly dwindled” ([5], p.283].

The famine’s slow conclusion left feelings of vulnerability in its wake. Those who succumbed to hunger and disease were, of course, the most vulnerable, but survivors felt vulnerability in different ways and carried it with them for much of their lives. Christina Sharpe posits that, “To be in the wake is also to recognize the ways that we are constituted through and by continued vulnerability to overwhelming force” ([6], p.16). The overwhelming force that was the famine did not cease to beset once mass starvation ended. The famine ended as a result of reduced grain requisitions, the availability of new crops, and very limited food aid that was targeted at specific agricultural and factory workers. For example, records from the Dnipropetrovsk Regional Committee of the CP(b)U show that machine tractor station (MTS) workers and collective and state farm workers who had worked enough days were the first ones to receive food aid [7]. Soviet leadership distributed aid with self-interest as a way to make sure that agricultural production continued. However, those who did not receive food aid were in desperate need, and many died as a result of not getting enough to eat [8].

What this meant was that issues of hunger continued to plague many in Ukraine after the summer of 1933 and into 1934. Demographic work by multinational teams used statistics to show that Holodomor-related deaths continued into 1934 [9]. Demographic work further develops this first prong of the famine aftermath model focused on social/cultural contexts. While a good harvest helped to wind down the famine, it also renewed expectations for quotas. Many collective farms were able to fulfill their annual quotas for the year already in August 1933, which led Soviet leadership to increase the amount of grain that was taken from certain regions of Ukraine. In Kharkiv, Chernihiv, Kyiv, Donetsk, Vinnytsia, Odesa, and Dnipropetrovsk oblasts, each faced an increase in the amount of grain they were to turn over to the state from the previous period. Together, the increased amount that they were forced to give up equated to 15,410 (1 pood = 36.11 lbs.) whereas areas outside of Ukraine were subject to less than half of that [10].

The continued requisition of grain from a population who just lost millions in the months prior contributed further to feelings of vulnerability in the wake of the Holodomor. Although they survived the worst of the famine, many continued to labor away on collective farms as they attempted to stave off hunger. Beyond hunger, these workers also processed the horrors they witnessed and grappled with issues of grief and trauma that remained. Within this vulnerability came mood shifts, outbursts, and feelings of what Sergei Maksudov calls “dehumanization” [11]. Maksudov describes this type of person as one who was “passive; submissively carried out the most absurd instructions issued by the authorities; was willing to work for the lowest wages or none at all; neither loved nor valued his labor; was uncertain of himself” [11, p.123]. In other words, Maksudov highlights how the reverberating effects of the famine contributed directly to the shaping of new types of workers that were molded by vulnerabilities of going hungry and apathy toward their work that was a direct result of living through 1932–1933. This insight implores us to think about the mental shift that famine survivors experience in the post-hunger period.

While many no doubt suffered from the consequences of dehumanization after the famine, others attempted to break through the Soviet’s silencing of the famine and address the realities of what transpired. Within the reverberations of famine come responses, and part of the famine experience are the moments when survivors work to address what they lived through and process their experiences. I argued, for example, in a previous article that poetry written between 1933 and1937 became a crucial way for survivors to challenge state narratives and speak about the real horrors through which they lived [12]. These critically written poems, I stated, helped to “constitute a literary response to the realities of the famine that official narratives purposefully suppressed” [12, p.17]. In essence, the Holodomor gives us examples of famine-afflicted populations actively responding to, and challenging, the silencing and power of the state. Such narratives remind us not to underprivilege views from below.

Further confrontation with narratives and memories surrounding the Holodomor continues even today, which gives insight into the ways that famines can affect the political structures of society. Tetiana Boriak, for example, details how the collective memory of the Holodomor shaped everything from presidential platforms to academic debates to museum narratives [13]. Now, in the midst of Russia’s ongoing war against Ukraine, Holodomor monuments are being destroyed by Russian occupiers in places like Mariupol [14]. The fact that Russia continues to attempt to alter and shape narratives about a famine that it perpetrated during the 1930s is indicative of the resonating effects of the Holodomor. We know from prominent famine scholars like Amartya Sen and Alex de Waal that all modern famines are human-made and specifically result from the withholding of food from certain populations for political means, but the Holodomor builds on these foundational theories and employs us with remembering that those who orchestrate famines can often continue to deny and attack the memory of it in the local cultures that endured it long after the famine is over [15, 16]. In the case of the Holodomor, this continues on today. One can look to the Irish famine as well to understand this phenomenon [17, 18]. This, too, is a fundamental part of understanding the social and cultural contexts of famines in their aftermath.

Global reverberations

In addition to the social and cultural reverberations of the Holodomor, the famine also played a significant role in global affairs. The full effects of famines hardly stay within the borders of the areas in which they take place, so the second prong of the famine aftermath model must account for a wider understanding of these effects. James Tyner, who writes about this within the context of Cambodia, suggests that “it is a mistake to limit our understanding of state-induced famines and famine crimes to the vantage point of the states in which famine occurs”([19], p.128). Tyner’s argument is based on the interpretation that famines cannot be easily separated from the larger mechanisms of geopolitics. Although Tyner’s arguments are concerned with Cambodia, something similar can be said about the Holodomor in Ukraine. Relevant new work investigates the full effect of the Holodomor’s impact on global affairs in the 1930s and beyond to show just how significant the famine was in shaping the world [20].

Understanding the aftermath of the Holodomor in global perspective is useful for understanding the politics of silence, fear, humanitarian aid, and justice that often take shape in the post-famine years. A unique aspect of the Holodomor is that sustained efforts to provide international aid and relief did not begin with much earnest until July 1933, when the worst of the famine came to an end. Overt Soviet silencing denied the famine and thus obstructed humanitarian bodies, such as the Red Cross, from providing aid. It was the reporting of Gareth Jones in spring 1933 that really awakened the world to the realities of famine in Ukraine, which mobilized Ukrainian émigré groups across Europe and other parts of the world to begin limited relief work. Some scholars, like Karolina Koziura, articulate the help of Ukrainian émigrés to their starved compatriots as acts of “national compassion,” demonstrating how Ukrainians abroad facilitated both national and transnational interest in the famine [21]. While compassion certainly served as a motivation, there were larger global concerns at play, one of them being the fear of future famine. An examination of varied aid and relief efforts in 1934, 1935, and even 1936 reveals that many international actors thought that the famine of 1933 in Ukraine was actually the beginning of a period of starvation that could get much worse. Letters to the Archbishop of Canterbury, Cosmo Gordon Lang, in 1934 highlight these fears directly [22]. As such, the Holodomor demonstrates how intangible fears of future famine—the haunting of hunger—become part of the global reverberations.

Another unique aspect of the Holodomor is the pursuit of famine justice. The lack of Soviet accountability only further repressed opportunities for justice in Ukraine during the Soviet period, which meant that famine justice for the Holodomor could not take place in the country where it happened and thus had to take place in Canada, Europe, and the United States. Important new work on the concept of famine justice shows how countries in the Global South have pursued various forms of justice ‘from below’ [23]. The case of Ukraine, too, adds much to this literature and expands its conceptual boundaries. During the 1980s—amidst the turn toward transitional justice—global actors that included Ukrainian famine survivors, academics, judges, lawyers, and politicians were hard at work investigating the Holodomor through formal investigative commissions. These commissions challenged official Soviet silencing and denial, offered Holodomor survivors the chance to share their experiences through official oral histories, and developed school curricula and other resources to make the famine better known and understood [24]. The case of Ukrainian famine justice further articulates the global reverberations of the Holodomor and can serve as an example for other famine models.

Reverberations of famine in medical and public health perspectives

The final part of this article considers the aftermath of the Holodomor through prisms of medicine and public health. As is now well known, the diseases that result from and accompany famines are themselves often more deadly than actual starvation [25]. The outbreak of disease followed the onset of famine in Ukraine, and controlling the spread of communicable diseases became a key concern for city sanitary commissions who wished to prevent the spread of disease from the countryside to the cities. Highlighting the foresight that medical workers had about the spread of famine diseases in 1932–33, Oksana Vynnyk contends that “doctors were well aware of the mass starvation in the countryside and urban areas, and they informed authorities long before the famine entered its most deadly phase”([26], p.273). Documents from sanitary commissions in Kyiv support these claims, and they reveal that medical workers went to great lengths to combat famine-related diseases across the city. For example, officials tracked cases of typhus and dysentery in order to understand how quickly the effects of famine were entering Kyiv. Their calculations show a dramatic increase in cases of dysentery between 1931 and 1933, increasing from 172 cases in 1931 to 1057 in 1932 to 2231 cases by September 1933 [27].

The increase in dysentery represented the manifestation of famine diseases that continued to develop throughout the fall and winter of 1933. Although records from this period are incomplete, documents from sanitary commissions in Kyiv show that the same concerns about the spread of disease continued through at least early 1934. In January 1934, for example, a note from a supervisor of a sanitary commission wrote that local Komsomol members were ordered to combat disease and uncleanliness around Kyiv by carrying out “raids” on streets and in apartments, prisons, and transportation stations [28]. In terms of famine modeling, data from organizations like the Soviet sanitary commissions indicate to us that while great effort went into combatting the spread of disease that went hand-in-hand with starvation, eliminating disease in full was much more difficult in practice. The records from Kyiv provide one example of how the problems that emanated from the Holodomor continued to be of the utmost concern in the famine’s early aftermath.

Not limited to the period immediately after the Holodomor, famine-related diseases and medical complications held, and continue to have, longer-term consequences as well, and these must be understood within the larger aftermath framework model. Work on the Dutch Hunger Winter by Ingrid de Zwarte provides one example that shows the effect of hunger on fertility, birth rates, and epigenetics [29]. Scholarship on the Holodomor is now trending in a similar direction. A team of researchers recently discovered that health effects related to the Holodomor led to an increased potential for developing type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) [30]. Such studies illuminate how physical bodies absorb the effects of famine and carry them forward in time, bridging the gap between past and present. Put another way, “It underscores the necessity for a comprehensive health care and policy framework that takes into account the lasting effects of early-life adversities on population health” [30, p.670].

In addition to the above, the long-term impact of the Holodomor also has important implications on mental health and emotional coping strategies that are passed down through intergenerational transmissions of trauma. Studies by Brent Bezo and Stefania Maggi, as well as Vitalii Klymchuk, found that Ukrainians who lived through and survived the famine transferred their emotional, inner-state, and trauma-coping strategies to the second and third generations of their families, which had direct impacts on individual, community, and public health [31, 32]. Studies such as these provide scientific studies that bring hidden famine impacts into focus and help us understand how the effects of starvation manifest in ways that directly affect public health, health policy, and the study of disease.

Conclusion

This article emphasized the importance of paying attention to the effects of famine, specifically the 1932–33 Holodomor, that continue to reverberate across time after periods of mass starvation. No single study can address all of the different elements that make up a famine’s aftermath, but the three-pronged model described here gives us one tool for tracking some of the broader effects of famine across social, cultural, global, and medical settings. The three-pronged model is advantageous because it can be built on using subsets of data and information. Each prong can incorporate other topics, such as environmentalism, gender, geography, justice, demography, memory, and politics, to name a few. Understanding the societal impacts both within Ukraine and beyond is useful for knowing how wide and far-ranging the impacts of starvation are on people and their communities. This alone should serve as motivation for academics, practitioners, and leaders to do more to fully grasp what is at stake when we let people starve. Despite our best efforts to combat famines, however, they still occur. When this happens, understanding the implications that arise from the legacies of hunger will prepare us more fully and adequately to respond to famine-stricken communities.
