Hydrolysis kinetics amino acid profiling and antioxidant properties of enzymatic hydrolysates from desalted egg white
Authors: Saowaluk Rungchang, Chayanid Sringarm, Sonthaya Numthuam, Naruemon Tosuk, Tipawan Thongsuk, Mahattanee Phinyo, Thunnalin Winuprasith, Sudarat Jiamyangyuen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractSalted egg white is a nutrient-rich by-product that is usually discarded due to its high salt content. It has strong potential for value-added transformation through enzymatic hydrolysis. This study aimed to generate peptide-rich hydrolysates with antioxidant potential from desalted salted egg white using single and combined protease systems. Hydrolysis was carried out using three enzymatic systems: MSD (a dual endo- and exo-protease), Thermoase GL (a thermostable endo-protease), and a combination of both. The hydrolysis process was monitored at 0, 1, 3, and 6 h. The hydrolysates were characterized in terms of degree of hydrolysis (DH), amino acid release, kinetic parameters, synergy analysis and antioxidant activity. MSD exhibited the highest hydrolytic efficiency, releasing up to 338.46 ± 0.05 mg/g d.b. of free amino acids, while GL showed limited activity under the test conditions. The combined enzymes system enhanced peptide release, showing a synergy factor of 1.87 at 3 h. Kinetic modeling (zero-, first-and second-order) suggested that GL enhanced MSD-mediated hydrolysis by increasing substrate accessibility. Antioxidant assays revealed that MSD alone had the highest activity, with 84.14% 2,2-diphenyl-1-picrylhydrazyl (DPPH) radical scavenging at 3 h and 31.20 nmol Trolox equivalents (TE)/mL ferric reducing antioxidant power (FRAP) at 1 h. These values were higher than those from GL and mixed treatments. Principal component analysis (PCA) revealed that amino acids such as phenylalanine, leucine, methionine, and tyrosine were key markers for distinguishing MSD and mixed enzyme hydrolysates from GL samples based on enzyme type and hydrolysis time. Overall, the results highlight the effectiveness of MSD in producing antioxidant-rich peptides and demonstrate that appropriate enzyme selection and combinations can yield functional hydrolysates from underutilized food by-products.Keywords: Desalted egg white, Enzymatic hydrolysis, Amino acid profiling, Antioxidant activity, Enzyme synergySubject terms: Biochemistry, Chemical biology

Introduction

Egg white is a rich source of protein with a balanced amino acid profile and good digestibility1. It is widely used in the food industry for its functional properties, including foaming, gelling, and emulsification. In salted egg production, the yolk is the main product, while the white becomes a by-product. While the yolks are often separated and used in high-value food products such as mooncakes and savory dishes, the remaining egg whites are frequently discarded due to their high salt content (approximately 7–12%) and unpleasant odor. During salting, egg white absorbs excess salt, making it unpalatable and unsuitable for direct use. Consequently, large quantities are discarded, contributing to food waste and environmental issues2. Finding ways to use this by-product can help improve sustainability in the egg industry as improper disposal of salted egg white contributes to food waste and represents a lost opportunity for protein recovery and value-added utilization. To overcome this challenge, researchers have investigated desalination approaches such as ultrafiltration, electrodialysis, ion exchange, and membrane separation, which not only reduce sodium content and improve the organoleptic properties of salted egg white but also support the Bio-Circular-Green (BCG) economic model by promoting the valorization of food industry by-products3–5. These advances make desalted egg white a promising raw material for further processing, particularly in the development of functional protein ingredients.

In recent years, enzymatically hydrolyzed egg white proteins have been reported to exhibit antioxidant, antihypertensive, antimicrobial, and anti-inflammatory activities. These bioactivities, along with their nutritional value, have led to the classification of such hydrolysates as functional hydrolysates, which are gaining increasing interest as ingredients in health-oriented food and nutraceutical applications6.

Enzymatic hydrolysis is widely regarded as an effective strategy for converting underutilized protein sources into functional and bioactive hydrolysates. This process breaks down native proteins into short peptides and free amino acids, which often exhibit antioxidant, antihypertensive and immunomodulatory activities7,8. Compared with chemical or thermal methods, enzymatic treatment allows a more controlled cleavage of protein chains, therefore helps preserve the structural integrity of bioactive peptide sequences.

Different proteases exhibit distinct cleavage specificities. Endo-proteases cleave internal peptide bonds, whereas exo-proteases release terminal amino acids. Using two proteases with complementary cleavage patterns may increase hydrolysis efficiency by exposing additional cleavage sites5. In this context, a dual-activity enzyme such as MSD (endo- and exo-mode) in combination with Thermoase GL30 (endo-protease) could enhance peptide release from salted egg white, but the potential synergistic effects between these two enzymes under moderate processing conditions have not yet been fully investigated.

The DH is important because it affects peptide size and composition. A higher DH generally yields smaller peptides and more free amino acids, enhancing bioactivity4,5. However, excessive hydrolysis can lead to the formation of bitter-tasting peptides, which may reduce the sensory acceptability of the product5. Therefore, monitoring and controlling the DH is essential to balance functionality and sensory quality. The amino acid profile of the hydrolysates is of particular interest because egg white contains many essential amino acids, and hydrolysis can increase their availability, offering insights into both nutritional and functional properties. Changes in the amounts of these amino acids can be measured by analyzing the amino acid profile of the hydrolysates.

This study investigated the enzymatic hydrolysis of desalted salted egg white using MSD, Thermoase GL30, and their combination under common processing conditions. The aim was to evaluate the synergistic hydrolytic performance of the two proteases and to assess the resulting hydrolysates in terms of peptide release, amino acid composition, and antioxidant properties.

Materials and methods

Salted egg white was desalted according to the optimal condition determined in a preliminary study, by mixing salted egg white with water (3:1 ratio) and heating the mixture at 100 °C for 10 min. The water was then replaced, and the process was repeated to further reduce the salt content. The desalted egg white was then separated from the water for subsequent use. Residual salt content was monitored after each washing step by Mohr titration (AgNO3/K2CrO4 method), and desalting was considered complete when the salt level was below 0.2% (w/w), which has been reported as acceptable for enzymatic hydrolysis without adverse effects on protein structure or protease activity10,11.

Two proteases were used: MSD protease and Thermoase GL30, both kindly provided by Amano Enzyme Asia Pacific Co., Ltd. (Pathum Thani, Thailand). MSD (CAS no. 9025-49-4), with an activity of 40,000 U/g protein, is a dual-activity protease (endopeptidase and exopeptidase) produced via fermentation of Aspergillus oryzae. Thermoase GL30 is a bacterial neutral metalloprotease (EC 3.4.24.27) derived from Geobacillus stearothermophilus, with an activity of 300,000 U/mL. MSD exhibits optimal activity at approximately 50 °C and pH 7.5, while Thermoase GL30 functions best at around 60 °C and pH 8.5. Hydrolysis in this study was conducted at 50 °C and pH 7.0 to maintain adequate functionality for both enzymes in single and combined treatments. These conditions (pH 7.0, 50 °C) were selected as a common practical compromise to enable direct comparison and potential synergistic interaction between the two enzymes under realistic food-processing conditions, even though they do not represent the optimal conditions for GL. Such sub-optimal but moderate conditions are commonly chosen in food-related enzyme processes, allowing direct comparison across different proteases without excessive denaturation of the substrate12,13.

Degree of hydrolysis (DH)

For enzymatic hydrolysis, 150 g of desalted egg white was mixed with 75 mL of deionized (DI) water and homogenized thoroughly. The pH was adjusted to 7 using 5% (v/v) acetic acid. Three treatments were performed: (1) 1% w/w MSD protease, (2) 1% w/w Thermoase GL30, and (3) Mixed enzymes 0.5% w/w each of MSD protease and Thermoase GL30. Hydrolysis was conducted in a water bath at 50 °C. Samples were collected at 0, 1, 3, and 6 h for analysis. Hydrolysis times of 1, 2, 3, and 6 h were selected to represent both the rapid and plateau stages of peptide release, based on preliminary experiments showing minimal change between 3 and 6 h and negligible differences at 4–5 h. Enzymes were applied on an equal weight (w/w) basis without normalization to specific activity units, in order to simulate practical processing conditions. However, this approach may introduce variability in catalytic efficiency between enzyme systems.

The DH was determined using the O-phthaldialdehyde (OPA) method14. The OPA reagent consisted of 50 mL of 100 mM sodium tetraborate, 5 mL of 20% (w/w) sodium dodecyl sulfate, and 2 mL of absolute ethanol containing 80 mg OPA, made up to 100 mL with DI water. In brief, 500 µL of hydrolysate was mixed with 1,000 µL of OPA reagent and incubated in the dark for 5 min. Absorbance was read at 340 nm using a UV-visible spectrophotometer. A standard curve was prepared with L-leucine.

Following the OPA assay, the enzymatic reaction was terminated by heating samples at 100 °C for 10 min. The mixtures were centrifuged at 10,000 rpm at 4 °C for 10 min, and the supernatant stored at −20 °C for further analysis.

Determination of free amino acids released by high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC)

The free amino acid (FAA) profile of the hydrolysates was analyzed using high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) with pre-column derivatization, following the method described by the Waters AccQ-Tag Manual15. Hydrolysate samples were centrifuged at 10,000 rpm for 10 min at 4 °C. The resulting supernatant was collected and filtered through a 0.22 μm membrane filter. Derivatization of free amino acids was performed using OPA reagent, which enhances detection sensitivity by forming fluorescent derivatives with amino groups. The derivatized samples were injected into an HPLC system equipped with a reversed-phase C18 column. The mobile phase consisted of sodium acetate buffer and methanol, delivered in a gradient elution mode to achieve optimal separation of amino acids. Detection of amino acid derivatives was carried out with a fluorescence detector set at an excitation wavelength of 340 nm and an emission wavelength of 450 nm. Quantification was performed by comparing the peak areas of the sample with those from a standard calibration curve prepared using known amino acid standards.

Principal component analysis (PCA)

PCA was conducted to investigate the variation in free amino acid profiles among samples subjected to different enzymatic hydrolysis treatments and incubation times. The amino acid concentration data were first normalized using min-max scaling to a range between 0 and 1 to ensure comparability across variables. PCA was performed using Python (version 3.11.7) and the scikit-learn library (version 1.2.2). The analysis focused on the first two principal components (PC1 and PC2), which explained the majority of the total variance. PCA score plots were generated using matplotlib to visualize the clustering of samples based on their amino acid profiles. The corresponding loading vectors of amino acids were overlaid on the same plots to illustrate the direction and magnitude of each variable’s contribution to the components. This approach enabled the interpretation of key amino acids responsible for the observed sample differentiation.

Antioxidant activity analysis

DPPH radical scavenging activity

The scavenging activity of desalted duck egg white hydrolysates (DSEWH) against the 2,2-diphenyl-1-picrylhydrazyl (DPPH) radical was determined following the method of Cui et al.16 with minor modifications for a 96-well microplate reader. Samples were dissolved in 95% ethanol in a 2:1 (v/v) ratio. DPPH was prepared at a concentration of 0.1 mM in 70% ethanol under dark conditions at an ambient temperature. Each sample (100 µL) was mixed with 100 µL DPPH solution in a 96–well microplate and incubated for 30 min at 25 ˚C using a microplate reader (Metertech M965, Thailand). Absorbance was measured at 517 nm for both samples and blanks, the latter prepared with distilled water and DPPH. Ferric reducing antioxidant power (FRAP)

The FRAP assay was conducted according to Benzie and Strain17 with slight modifications. Fresh FRAP reagent was prepared by mixing 300 mM acetate buffer (pH 3.6), 10 mM TPTZ (2,4,6-tripyridyl-s-triazine) in 40 mM HCl, and 20 mM ferric chloride (FeCl3·6H2O) in a 10:1:1 (v/v/v) ratio, and incubating at 37 °C prior to use. Trolox solutions (0–400 µM) were used to prepare a standard curve. For the assay, 20 µL of sample or standard was added to 150 µL of FRAP reagent in a 96-well microplate and incubated at 37 °C for 8 min. Absorbance was measured at 595 nm, and results were expressed as nmol Trolox equivalent (TE)/g. All sample analyses were performed in triplicate.

Statistical analysis

All experiments were performed in triplicate, and the results are expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Data were analyzed using ANOVA followed by Duncan’s multiple range test (DMRT) at a significance level of p < 0.05.

Results and discussion

Degree of hydrolysis (DH)

The DH is a key parameter reflecting the extent of protein breakdown and the release of peptides and free amino acids. In this study, DH progressively increased over time, with significant differences among enzyme treatments (Fig. 1). At 0 h, minimal hydrolysis was observed in all groups, confirming the absence of enzymatic activity before incubation.

Degree of hydrolysis (DH) of desalted egg white hydrolysate obtained using MSD, GL, and mixed enzyme as a function of time. a, b, c indicate significant differences of DH for the same enzyme at different hydrolysis times. A, B, C indicate significant differences of DH for the hydrolysis time of different enzymes

During hydrolysis, the mixed enzymes-treated samples exhibited a higher DH compared to single-enzyme treatments, with all samples reaching the highest DH at 6 h. In the GL treatment, the smaller increment in DH at 3 h is within the replicate variability and likely reflects a transient limitation in enzyme accessibility to peptide-bond cleavage sites. This may result from short-lived peptide aggregation and/or conformational changes in substrate proteins that reduce enzyme–substrate interactions under the sub-optimal conditions used for GL (50 °C, pH 7.0). These effects were temporary, and the DH continued to increase at 6 h. It should be emphasized that the hydrolysis conditions (pH 7.0 and 50 °C) were deliberately selected as moderate settings to allow both MSD and GL to act concurrently without causing enzyme deactivation. Although these parameters are slightly sub-optimal for GL, they represent realistic conditions typically used in mild food-processing applications. This practical design simulates co-hydrolysis under industrially relevant conditions rather than maximal catalytic activity, but it also imposes a limitation that must be considered when interpreting the observed synergy. Afterward, in mixed-enzymes system, the DH ranged from 9.53 to 16.32 suggesting that combining MSD and GL enhances hydrolysis efficiency due to their complementary cleavage mechanisms facilitating more efficient peptide bond cleavage. The synergistic effects on DH can be explained as follows: endo-proteases first cleave internal peptide bonds of large protein molecules, producing smaller peptide fragments. These fragments are then further degraded by exo-proteases, which sequentially remove amino acids or dipeptides from the terminal ends. This aligns with Pang et al.18, who reported that combining proteases with different specificities enhances hydrolysis efficiency. These findings support previous research19 indicating that combining endopeptidases and exopeptidases can accelerate hydrolysis beyond the efficiency of individual enzymes. Pokora et al.20reported that the DH of by-products obtained during the isolation of lecithin, lysozyme, and cystatin from yolk and white protein reached 27.6% and 20.9%, respectively, indicating significant protein breakdown.​ However, the progressive increase in DH highlights that prolonged hydrolysis enhances protein degradation and bioactive peptide release. For GL, the DH value at 3 h still increased compared to the previous time point but showed a smaller increment than expected. However, excessive hydrolysis may lead to over-degradation, potentially reducing bioactivity and affecting bitter sensory properties due to the release of hydrophobic amino acids21. Hence, optimizing hydrolysis time is crucial to balancing protein breakdown, bioactivity, and sensory characteristics.

Amino acid profiles

The enzymatic hydrolysis of desalted salted egg white was evaluated by analyzing free amino acid profiles at different time intervals. The obtained data were further examined using PCA to assess variations in hydrolysis efficiency and free amino acid composition.

The heatmap (Fig. 2) provides a comprehensive view of free amino acid concentrations over different time points for 3 enzymatic conditions. The results indicate distinct amino acid release patterns, with MSD exhibiting a broad-spectrum hydrolysis capability, GL displaying a preference for cystine, lysine, and tyrosine, and the mixed enzymes yielding the highest total amino acid release. The heatmap further illustrates the time-dependent nature of hydrolysis, where prolonged incubation leads to increased amino acid liberation, particularly in the mixed enzymes treatment. This suggests that the sequential and complementary action of both enzymes play a crucial role in maximizing hydrolysis efficiency.

Visualization of free amino acid concentrations over time for different enzymatic treatments.

The observed patterns indicate that MSD efficiently breaks down proteins into a wide range of free amino acids, likely due to its dual endopeptidase and exopeptidase activities. In contrast, GL demonstrates selectivity towards cystine-rich and aromatic residues, contributing to a more specialized hydrolysis process. The slightly higher total amino acid released was found in mixed enzymes compared to using MSD alone, suggesting a cooperative mechanism in which GL initiates partial hydrolysis by cleaving specific peptide bonds, particularly at accessible hydrophilic regions, and induces conformational loosening of the protein structure. These structural modifications expose additional hydrophobic and buried cleavage sites that MSD can subsequently target, enabling it to further degrade the proteins into a broader range of free amino acids. However, it should be noted that MSD and mixed enzyme hydrolysates showed > 300 mg/g d.b. of FAAs even at relatively low DH values (7–16%). This phenomenon can be attributed to the broad catalytic specificity of MSD derived from Aspergillus oryzae, which includes both endo- and exopeptidase activities capable of sequentially cleaving internal and terminal peptide bonds22. Such dual action facilitates rapid terminal amino acid release without proportionally increasing DH, consistent with reports that mixed endo-exo enzyme systems generate higher FAA levels than single enzymes23,24. The subsequent decrease in FAA at 6 h in the mixed system may result from secondary degradation or aggregation of hydrophobic peptides, leading to precipitation and reduced detectability21,25. Nonetheless, further confirmation of this dual catalytic behavior at the molecular level remains necessary. Pokora et al.20 reported that hydrolysates of egg white and egg yolk proteins obtained using Neutrase (Bacillus amyloliquefaciens) contained 2.04–12.51% hydrophobic and hydrophilic amino acids, which is notably higher than the 0.30–4.5% observed in our study. This difference is likely due to variations in enzyme specificity and the nature of the raw materials employed.

Enzyme kinetic analysis and modeling

Kinetic modeling results (Table 1) showed clear differences in hydrolysis patterns among the single and mixed enzyme treatments. MSD exhibited the highest rate constant (k₀) in the zero-order model and the highest k₁ and P∞ in the first-order model, indicating both rapid catalytic activity and a high final yield of FAA. This trend aligns with MSD’s dual endopeptidase-exopeptidase action, enabling both extensive peptide bond cleavage and terminal amino acid release. This is consistent with the known broad substrate specificity and strong proteolytic potential of alkaline proteases26,27. In contrast, GL showed the lowest constants, consistent with previous reports that its optimal catalytic efficiency occurs only at higher temperatures and alkaline pH29. Under the milder conditions of this study (50 °C, pH 7.0), GL’s structural constraints and active-site specificity likely limited its substrate accessibility, resulting in reduced hydrolysis. The mixed enzyme system displayed kinetic constants between those of the individual enzymes, suggesting partial enhancement of activity. This likely occurs because GL initiates partial hydrolysis by cleaving specific accessible bonds and reducing structural rigidity, thereby increasing the accessibility of additional cleavage sites for MSD. Across all treatments, the first-order model provided a better fit (higher R² values) than the zero-order model, indicating that FAA release followed a saturating exponential trend. This pattern reflects substrate depletion and possible product inhibition over time, which are typical in proteolysis, particularly when hydrolysis products interfere with enzyme-substrate interactions.

To further evaluate the interaction between the two enzymes, a synergy factor analysis was performed (Table 2). All synergy factor values exceeded 1.0, indicating positive interaction. The peak synergy factor of 1.87 at 3 h indicates that the combined system produced almost twice the FAA yield predicted from the sum of individual enzyme activities. This result suggests that GL may have enhanced MSD activity by partially modifying the substrate structure in the early stage of hydrolysis. By reducing steric hindrance and increasing access to cleavage sites, GL likely facilitated more efficient peptide bond cleavage by MSD29,30. Similar synergistic effects have been reported for mixed protease systems where one enzyme disrupts structural barriers, enabling the other to hydrolyze more effectively31,32.

The amino acid release profile at 6 h (Fig. 4) further supports the presence of complementary activity. MSD hydrolysates contained high levels of hydrophobic and branched-chain amino acids such as phenylalanine, leucine, and isoleucine, which are often linked to bioactive peptide formation with antioxidant or antihypertensive properties31. GL hydrolysates, in contrast, were richer in cystine and lysine, possibly reflecting its preference for cleaving peptide bonds near sulfur-containing or basic residues32. The mixed enzyme hydrolysates showed a more balanced amino acid spectrum, combining the strengths of both enzymes. This broader amino acid coverage may enhance functional properties and nutritional value, as diversity in amino acid composition is crucial for targeted peptide production in functional foods33.

It should be noted, however, that enzyme dosages were applied on an equal-weight basis without normalization to specific activity units. This practical approach may introduce differences in catalytic efficiency between MSD and GL, and should be taken into account when interpreting the observed synergy and functional outcomes. Although the limitation of using equal enzyme mass rather than equivalent activity was initially mentioned in the Methods section, it is further clarified here that this approach was intentionally applied to simulate realistic food-processing practices, where enzyme dosage is typically determined by weight for operational feasibility. This practical design, while suitable for process simulation, may introduce bias when comparing catalytic efficiencies between enzymes with different specific activities. Therefore, the observed synergistic effects and kinetic parameters should be interpreted within the context of industrial relevance rather than strict enzymatic equivalence.

PCA of FAA profiles (Fig. 5) captured 96.24% of the total variance with the first two principal components. PC1 (90.39%) separated samples mainly according to the extent of hydrolysis. Although the first two principal components explained a high proportion of total variance, such a value may indicate limited sample diversity. Nevertheless, the close clustering among replicates and the consistent separation among treatments suggest that the PCA model remained stable and not overfitted within the studied dataset. MSD showed the greatest temporal shift, reflecting extensive proteolysis, followed by the mixed enzyme system. GL samples remained closely clustered across time points, confirming its limited hydrolytic action under the experimental conditions. The loading plot revealed that amino acids such as phenylalanine, leucine, tyrosine, methionine, and proline were strongly associated with positive PC1 values, and thus with more extensive hydrolysis (MSD and mixed enzymes). In contrast, cystine and lysine were linked to negative PC1 values, characterizing the GL group. PC2 (5.85%) captured variance related to aspartic acid and glutamic acid, suggesting some treatment-specific differences in early hydrolysis stages. These PCA findings confirm that MSD drives the most extensive proteolysis, GL contributes minimally alone, but in combination, GL can enhance MSD’s access to certain peptide bonds, resulting in a more complete and functionally diverse hydrolysate33,34.

Antioxidant activity

The DPPH radical scavenging activity of hydrolysates obtained from enzymatic hydrolysis with MSD, GL, and their combination is presented in Fig. 6a. Significant variations were observed among hydrolysates and hydrolysis durations. At 0 h, although all treatments used the same desalted egg white substrate, the initial antioxidant activities differed among enzyme groups, likely reflecting the intrinsic antioxidant potential of the commercial proteases. Because these enzymes are produced from different microbial sources and fermentation processes, they may contain residual low-molecular-weight peptides or fermentation-derived metabolites with antioxidant activity, which contribute to the baseline values prior to hydrolysis21,25. MSD-treated hydrolysates exhibited the highest scavenging activity, reaching 84.14% at 3 h, before slightly declining to 79.15% at 6 h. This suggests that MSD generates peptides with strong electron-donating capacity, likely because it produces low-molecular-weight hydrophobic peptides enriched in aromatic amino acids (e.g., tryptophan and tyrosine), which are important contributors to radical scavenging activity21,25,35. These amino acids are also reported to play key roles in electron donation during radical scavenging processes25. The increased DPPH scavenging activity of desalted egg white hydrolysates is mainly attributed to the release of short peptides capable of acting as electron donors, thereby stabilizing free radicals and terminating radical chain reactions35,36. In contrast, GL hydrolysates showed a substantial decrease, from 45.07% (0 h) to 13.16% (6 h), suggesting that peptides generated by GL may lack key antioxidant residues or be more susceptible to degradation. Neutral proteases such as GL often generate larger peptides with lower radical scavenging potential25,37. The mixed enzymes hydrolysates displayed moderate, relatively stable activity (42.39–48.56%) implying that the co-treatment did not enhance radical scavenging capacity beyond MSD alone. This may reflect the generation of less potent peptide profiles and/or degradation of active peptides due to excessive hydrolysis. Although the DPPH assay involves the use of ethanol, which can potentially induce peptide denaturation, it remains widely employed for evaluating antioxidant activity in protein hydrolysates. In this study, the DPPH assay was chosen due to its simplicity and prevalence in literature for peptide-based systems. The ABTS assay, though more compatible with aqueous environments, was not included in the experimental design to maintain methodological consistency. It is acknowledged that the ethanolic nature of the DPPH assay may cause partial denaturation of peptides, which could underestimate their antioxidant potential25. Nevertheless, this method was selected to allow comparison with previous studies on egg white hydrolysates. The inclusion of complementary aqueous-based assays (ABTS or ORAC) will be considered in future investigations to provide a more comprehensive evaluation.

FRAP is often used to evaluate the ability of an antioxidant to donate an electron or hydrogen, and some research has indicated that there is a direct correlation between antioxidant activities and reducing power of peptide38,39. Enzymatic hydrolysis can enhance the iron-chelating ability of peptides, which contributes to higher FRAP values by binding Fe³⁺ ions and inhibiting their participation in redox cycling that generate reactive oxygen species21,25.

The FRAP values of desalted egg white are shown in Fig. 6b. Results from the FRAP assay further supported the antioxidant trends observed in DPPH. MSD hydrolysates showed the highest reducing power, reaching 31.20 nmol TE/mL at 1 h, with a gradual decline thereafter. This early peak suggests that the antioxidant peptides and free amino acids generated initially by MSD were highly effective in electron donation for iron reduction. The subsequent decline may be due to peptide degradation or conformational changes reducing their redox activity. In contrast, GL-treated samples maintained consistently low FRAP values across all time points, confirming the limited ability of GL to produce reducing peptides. Alkaline proteases, such as MSD, generate small, reactive peptides with strong redox potential34, whereas neutral proteases (GL) produce larger, less reactive peptides with lower FRAP activity. These findings align with previous studies showing that alkaline proteases produce hydrolysates with superior reducing power compared to neutral proteases17. Additionally, peptides rich in hydrophobic (Leu and Val) and sulfur-containing amino acids (Cys and Met) exhibit stronger reducing capacity25,33,35. The mixed enzyme system yielded intermediate FRAP values, resembling the MSD pattern but at lower magnitude, indicating that the presence of GL may partially attenuate MSD’s effect or lead to over-hydrolysis of functional peptides.

Although the mixed-enzyme treatment showed a higher degree of hydrolysis and a synergy factor above 1.0, this did not translate into the highest antioxidant activity. This apparent discrepancy suggests that synergistic hydrolysis mainly increases the quantity of released peptides rather than their functional activity. Antioxidant capacity largely depends on the specific sequence and structure of peptides, particularly the presence of hydrophobic, aromatic or sulfur-containing amino acid residues. It is possible that the combination of MSD and GL partially over-hydrolysed bioactive peptides, generating smaller fragments that lack the structural features required for effective radical scavenging or electron donation. Similar findings have been reported in previous studies, indicating that excessive hydrolysis may reduce antioxidant potency despite high DH values21,25,40. Therefore, in this case, synergistic enzyme interaction improved peptide yield but did not necessarily enhance antioxidant functionality.

Conclusion

In summary, enzymatic hydrolysis of desalted salted egg white using MSD, Thermoase GL, and their combination showed clear differences in hydrolytic efficiency, amino acid release and antioxidant activity. MSD was the most effective enzyme, producing the highest degree of hydrolysis and releasing the largest amount of free amino acids. The combination of MSD and GL resulted in higher peptide release and showed evidence of synergistic activity, with a maximum synergy factor of 1.87 at 3 h.

Based on kinetic fitting (first- and second‐ order models), GL appears to enhance MSD activity by partially loosening the protein structure, which improves substrate accessibility for MSD. However, MSD hydrolysates displayed the strongest antioxidant activity (DPPH and FRAP assays), while the mixed-enzyme treatment showed moderate activity and GL alone showed little effect.

These findings indicate that although enzyme combinations can increase peptide yield and diversity, the antioxidant activity mainly depends on the type and sequence of peptides produced. In this study, MSD was the most effective enzyme in terms of antioxidant potential. Further studies on other biological activities are required to comprehensively evaluate the functional potential of the resulting hydrolysates. Therefore, careful selection of proteases is essential when designing enzymatic processes for functional food or nutraceutical applications.
