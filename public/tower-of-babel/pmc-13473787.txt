Quantifying Risk Factors for Osteochondritis Dissecans in Children: A Propensity-Matched Retrospective Cohort Study
Authors: Joseph A. Magoline, Daniel F. Wagner, David C. Kaelber, R. Justin Mistovich
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:Osteochondritis dissecans (OCD) is a developmental orthopaedic condition in pediatric patients characterized by disruption of epiphyseal cartilage vascular supply leading to secondary subchondral bone changes and potential joint degeneration. While repetitive trauma is implicated, emerging evidence suggests a contributing role of biological and hereditary risk factors.Purpose:To quantify the relative risk (RR) of OCD with specific biological comorbidities and athletic participation in a large representative pediatric and adolescent population.Study Design:Cross-sectional study; Level of evidence, 3.Methods:We queried the TriNetX US Collaborative Network for patients aged ≤25 years between 2017 and 2024. Using International Classification of Diseases (ICD-10) diagnosis codes, we identified patients with potentially associated medical conditions (eg, hypopituitarism, vitamin D deficiency, coagulopathies, obesity, and nicotine dependence) and participation in common youth sports. The RR of OCD development for each condition and sport was calculated after propensity matching on age and sex. Annual incidence and prevalence were also assessed.Results:We identified 18,493 patients with OCD (mean age at first ICD diagnosis: 13.4 ± 3.45 years; 59% male). The most common OCD location was the knee (49%). From 2017 to 2024, the annual incidence rose from 15 to 23 per 100,000. Gymnastics (RR, 4.96; [95% CI, 3.14-7.83]), volleyball (RR, 2.96 [95% CI, 1.84-4.74]), and cheerleading (RR, 2.83 [95% CI, 1.47-5.47]) were associated with the highest OCD risk among included sports. Among medical comorbidities, primary thrombophilia (RR, 3.20 [95% CI, 1.57-6.51]), von Willebrand Factor deficiency (RR, 2.90 [95% CI, 1.41-5.95]), and Factor V Leiden (RR, 2.70 [95% CI, 1.31-5.58]) carried the highest risk. Hypopituitarism (RR, 1.71 [95% CI, 1.21-2.41]) and body mass index ≥35kg/m2 (RR, 1.14 [95% CI, 1.01-1.29]) were also significantly associated. Nicotine dependence was associated with decreased risk (RR, 0.72 [95% CI, 0.57-0.91]).Conclusion:This large-scale analysis provides updated epidemiologic trends and quantifies risk factors associated with pediatric OCD. Age, sex, specific medical conditions, and athletic participation were linked to increased OCD risk, and novel associations between OCD and coagulopathies were identified.

Osteochondritis dissecans (OCD) is a developmental disorder of the articular-epiphyseal physeal cartilage complex, characterized by vascular disruption and subsequent impairment of endochondral ossification, progressing to chondronecrosis and secondary subchondral bony defects.6,27,34 Untreated, OCD lesions may progress to early-onset osteoarthritis. 25 The condition can occur in any synovial joint in the human body, although the lateral aspect of the medial femoral condyle of the knee is the most common location. 23 Repetitive mechanical trauma during bone and joint development is hypothesized to be largely responsible for the occurrence of many OCD lesions in the knee. 35 Nevertheless, emerging literature suggests biological and hereditary risk factors may also contribute to disease development.4,8,26,35 Specifically, investigators have suggested vitamin D deficiency, human growth hormone (hGH) deficiency, hereditary coagulopathic conditions, and obesity as risk factors for OCD pathogenesis.4,11,15,22,23,35 Multiple reported cases of identical joint findings in monozygotic twins, as well as an increased incidence among family members, also suggest a genetic component of OCD may be critical in disease progression.7,8

Recent literature has proposed that juvenile OCD should be viewed within the broader context of developmental osteochondrosis, in which disrupted subchondral blood supply to growth cartilage plays a central role.27,34 This vascular insufficiency may act independently or synergistically with biomechanical stress to initiate lesion development. These findings are supported by both histologic observations and animal models of epiphyseal ischemia, suggesting that systemic factors, such as coagulopathies and endocrinopathies, may be relevant contributors to disease onset. 27

However, the increased likelihood of developing OCD associated with these risk factors has yet to be quantified. Therefore, we sought to quantify the relative risk (RR) associated with these proposed biological and sports-related risk factors utilizing a large, population-based dataset.

Furthermore, data are limited on the overall population-based incidence of OCD. Many authors have estimated the incidence of OCD of the knee,9,20,28,32 but Kessler et al 16 in 2014 performed the only large-scale population analysis and reported the incidence of OCD of the knee for patients aged 6 to 19 years was 9.5 per 100,000. Their study reported the single-year incidence of OCD of the knee based on a population of >3 million patients in Southern California. It was also the first to report on the association between race/ethnicity and OCD, finding that non-Hispanic African Americans have a markedly increased risk of disease compared with all other races/ethnicities. We therefore also sought to better quantify the incidence of OCD across the United States population and to identify changes in incidence over time.

Methods

Data Source

The design and results of this study were reported in accordance with the STROBE (Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology) guidelines. 36 The data used in this study were collected on April 14, 2025, and July 7, 2025, from the TriNetX US Collaborative Network platform, which provided access to aggregated, deidentified electronic health records (EHR) (encounter diagnoses, procedures, medication prescriptions, laboratory values, and genomic information) from approximately >100 million patients from 68 health care organizations. These contributed EHR data encompass all types of clinical encounters, including inpatient, outpatient, and emergency visits, within each participating health care organization. Patient information is extracted using standardized coding systems (International Classification of Diseases, 9th and 10th editions [ICD-9/ICD-10] encounter diagnoses and Current Procedural Terminology). This retrospective study is exempt from informed consent. The data reviewed are a secondary analysis of existing data, do not involve intervention or interaction with human subjects, and are deidentified per the deidentification standard defined in Section §164.514(a) of the Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act (HIPAA) Privacy Rule. The process by which the data is deidentified is attested to through a formal determination by a qualified expert as defined in Section §164.514(b)(1) of the HIPAA Privacy Rule. This formal determination by a qualified expert was refreshed in December 2020.

Cohort Selection

Patient cohorts and outcome measures were defined using ICD-10 encounter diagnosis codes. All patients in the United States Collaborative Network aged ≤25 years were included in the cohort creation. Eight medical condition exposure cohorts were created based on patients having at least 1 ICD-10 encounter diagnosis code for each condition in their available EHR history. These exposure cohorts were defined before assessment of OCD as the outcome and included hGH deficiency, coded under hypopituitarism (E23), vitamin D deficiency (E55), vWF deficiency (D68), primary thrombophilia (D68.5), hemophilia A (D66), immune thrombocytopenic purpura (D69.3), Factor V Leiden (D68.51 or D68.52), and nicotine dependence (F17). Additionally, 2 cohorts were created to assess obesity using a TriNetX-derived body mass index (BMI) variable. Cohorts for the most recent BMI ≥ 30kg/m2 and for the most recent BMI ≥35 kg/m2 were added for analysis. A comparison cohort was created for each risk factor by including patients with at least 1 ambulatory visit and no history of that risk factor. In a separate analysis, we examined the RR associated with participation in 10 common youth sports by comparing patients with ICD-10 activity code encounter diagnoses to those without the same activity code. To reduce confounding, 1-to-1 propensity-score matching was performed based on age at diagnosis and sex for each medical and activity risk factor cohort (Figure 1).

Outcome Measures

Cohorts were created, and calculations were performed within the TriNetX platform. The primary outcome was the RR of developing OCD in patients compared with a population without each risk factor. TriNetX calculates RR, 95% CIs, and 2-tailed P values using built-in analytics based on propensity-matched cohorts. For each risk factor, cohorts were balanced using propensity score matching for age at index diagnosis and sex, 2 variables that could confound the relationship between risk factor exposure and OCD development (Figure 1). For statistical deidentification purposes, any population query that yields a value between 1 and 10 is reported as 10 within the TriNetX platform.

In addition to risk factor analysis, we used the incidence, prevalence, and outcomes analysis tools within the TriNetX Analytics platform to update the epidemiology of OCD in the pediatric population. These tools enabled calculation of annual incidence proportions and prevalence from 2017 to 2024, stratified by age, sex, and race. Demographic characteristics of affected individuals, including age at index ICD encounter diagnosis, sex distribution, and racial composition, were also summarized to provide an updated descriptive profile of OCD. Patients in the cohort may have received >1 ICD-10 OCD-related encounter diagnosis code in their clinical history. Differences in total OCD patient counts across demographic analyses are attributable to health care organizations entering or leaving the TriNetX platform during the study period, which may lead to slight fluctuations in cohort sizes over time.

Results

Patient Characteristics

A total of 18,493 patients aged ≤25 years, with at least 1 ICD-10 encounter diagnosis of OCD, were identified. The mean current age of the cohort was 18.9 ± 4.1 years, while the mean age at the first OCD ICD-10 encounter diagnosis was 13.4 ± 3.45 years.

The cohort was predominantly male (59.43%, n = 10,990). Female patients represented 38.45% (n = 7110), and 2.12% (n = 393) had unknown sex. Most patients were White (66.76%, n = 12,347), followed by Black or African American (12.85%, n = 2376) and patients with unknown race (11.78%; n = 2178). The remaining racial groups, including Other (5.05%), Asian (2.6%), Native Hawaiian or Other Pacific Islander (0.6%), and American Indian or Alaska Native (0.36%), each constituted smaller portions of the cohort.

The most common OCD anatomical location was the knee (ICD-10-CM M93.26), accounting for 49% (n = 9134) of all patients. An additional 35% (n = 6633) had an “unspecified site” ICD-10 encounter diagnosis code related to their OCD (M93.20). ICD-10 encounter diagnosis codes related to the ankle and joints of the foot (M93.27) were the second most frequently specified location, representing 20% (n = 3810) of patients, followed by the elbow (M93.22) at 12% (n = 2293). Less commonly affected joints included the hip (2%; n = 315), shoulder (n = 89), wrist (n = 55), and hand (n = 15) (Table 1).

Incidence and Prevalence

The annual incidence proportion of ICD-10-related OCD encounter diagnoses increased from 15 cases per 100,000 patients in 2017 to 23 cases per 100,000 patients in 2024. A notable decline occurred in 2020, when the incidence proportion dropped to 12 cases per 100,000 patients. The annual point prevalence of OCD steadily increased over the study period from 51 patients per 100,000 individuals in 2017 to 98 patients per 100,000 individuals in 2024. From 2019 to 2021, annual prevalence plateaued, coinciding with a decrease in the incidence proportion over that period (Supplemental Figure 1).

Stratified analysis revealed that incidence proportions were consistently higher among male patients than among female patients throughout the study period. In 2024, the incidence proportion in males reached 28.4 cases per 100,000, compared with 18.1 cases per 100,000 in females (Figure 2A). Additional subgroup analyses examining sex-specific trends in knee and ankle OCD are included in the supplementary material.

Among racial groups, American Indian or Alaska Native patients exhibited the highest incidence proportions from 2017 through 2022, before being surpassed by White patients in 2023 and 2024. In 2024, White patients had the highest incidence proportion at 26.3 cases per 100,000, followed closely by Native Hawaiian or Other Pacific Islander patients at 25.4 per 100,000 and American Indian or Alaska Native patients at 23 per 100,000. White patients consistently demonstrated the second highest incidence across most of the study period, with Black or African American, Asian, and Other racial groups showing comparatively lower rates. All racial groups followed a similar temporal trend: a transient decline in 2020 likely attributable to pandemic-related disruptions in care, followed by rising incidence proportions through 2024 (Figure 2B).

In terms of age, the incidence of ICD-10-related OCD encounter diagnoses was highest in the 10 to 14 years age group, peaking at 53.2 cases per 100,000 patients in 2024, followed by the 15 to 19 years age group at 32.4 cases per 100,000. Incidence in the 10-14 and 15-19 years age groups showed a consistent upward trend over the study period, while rates in the 5 to 9 years age group remained comparatively lower but increased steadily (Figure 2C).

Quantifying Risk Factors

Of the medical condition-related ICD-10 encounter diagnosis codes analyzed, primary thrombophilia demonstrated the highest RR for an ICD-10-related OCD encounter diagnosis (RR, 3.20; 95% CI, 1.57-6.51), followed by von Willebrand Factor (vWF) deficiency (RR, 2.90; 95% CI, 1.41-5.95) and Factor V Leiden (RR, 2.70; 95% CI, 1.31-5.58). These findings represent novel associations, as coagulopathy-related risk factors have not been previously linked to OCD in large-scale studies. Additionally, growth hormone deficiency was associated with a significantly increased risk (RR, 1.71 [95% CI, 1.21-2.41]). Notably, several of the control groups for rare comorbidities had <10 patients (Figure 3A), a threshold under which TriNetX suppresses the true count and rounds values up to 10 for reporting. This practice likely results in an underestimation of the true effect size, particularly for conditions such as vWF deficiency, thrombophilia, and Factor V Leiden, all of which showed significant risk ratios despite this conservative rounding. For conditions that failed to reach significance (eg, hemophilia A [95% CI, 0.57-2.96]), we cannot confidently determine whether a true difference exists and interpret these results as “unable to determine significance.”

Discussion

To our knowledge, this is the first large-scale analysis to quantify population-based trends in incidence of OCD related ICD-10 encounter diagnoses and the RR of having OCD related ICD-10 encounter diagnoses associated with participation in common youth sports and medical comorbidities, based on ICD-10 encounter diagnoses.

Population-Based OCD Incidence

We found an increasing incidence of OCD ICD-10 encounter diagnoses, with 2037 (0.023%) new patients diagnosed in 2024 compared with 1326 new patients (0.015%) in 2017, the earliest year of included data. This may reflect increasing sport participation, sport specialization, and a lack of seasons outside organized sports.12,18 Further investigation is needed. We caution that other variables may impact this hypothesis. Increased clinical awareness, broadened diagnostic thresholds, greater use of imaging for knee pain in primary care, and higher referral rates for musculoskeletal complaints all may contribute to the observed rising incidence of OCD. 10 It is important to note that the TriNetX network used for this analysis employs date shifting of up to 365 days to preserve patient privacy. As a result, temporal trends such as annual incidence and prevalence should be interpreted with caution, particularly when comparing year-to-year changes.

Interestingly, we found that in 2020, the incidence of new OCD ICD-10 encounter diagnoses decreased. We hypothesized that this transient decline in OCD incidence would likely reflect coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) pandemic-related disruptions in clinical access, combined with a real reduction in exposure to organized athletics. Multiple studies documented steep drops in youth sports participation and sports-related injuries during lockdowns, paralleling broader decreases in pediatric orthopaedic presentations that year.19,37 For comparison, scoliosis programs reported fewer referrals and delayed care in 2020, followed by higher curve magnitudes and increased surgical indications as services resumed, supporting the idea that care pathway disruptions can meaningfully alter observed disease trends.5,30 Alternatively, for stable, skeletally immature OCD lesions, conservative management with activity restriction is effective in roughly half of cases, suggesting that the mandated “rest” of 2020 could plausibly have reduced progression to clinically apparent disease in some athletes. 33

The mean age at initial OCD ICD-10 encounter diagnosis code in this cohort was 13.4 ± 3.43 years, which is consistent with the findings of Kessler et al, 16 who reported the highest incidence of OCD of the knee at age 14 in their population-based study. Notably, our study's inclusion criteria were not limited to diagnoses of OCD of the knee, but this similar result suggests age as a critical component in OCD pathogenesis.

In our cohort, the male-to-female incidence ratio of OCD is approximately 1.6 to 1 (28.4 vs 18.1 cases per 100,000 in 2024), reflecting a narrower sex gap than previously reported. Previous research focusing specifically on knee OCD identified a 3.8-fold increased risk in males compared with females. The relatively smaller sex disparity in our findings may reflect our broader inclusion criteria encompassing OCD lesions at multiple joints, including the ankle and elbow. Notably, ankle OCD has been reported to occur more frequently in females, with an incidence of 6 per 100,000 in females versus 3.2 per 100,000 in males aged 6 to 19 years. 17 This expanded anatomic scope may partially explain the closer incidence rates between sexes in our study, suggesting that sex-based differences in OCD incidence may vary by lesion location. Subgroup analyses in the supplementary material confirm a higher incidence of OCD of the knee in males and a higher incidence of OCD of the ankle in females in this study's cohort (Supplemental Figure 2).

Together, these findings suggest that while OCD remains more common in males, the sex disparity may be decreasing over time, possibly due to increased sports participation among girls or improvements in diagnostic practices. 16 This evolving trend highlights the importance of maintaining vigilance for OCD across both sexes in contemporary clinical settings.

Sports and OCD Risk

All evaluated sports showed statistically significant associations, with gymnastics demonstrating the highest RR, followed by dancing, volleyball, and cheerleading. While OCD of the knee and its relationship to athletics has been extensively studied, recent literature has identified gymnastics as a risk factor for capitellar OCD lesions. 38 Kajiyama et al demonstrated distinct differences in lesion location between baseball players and gymnasts, suggesting sport-specific loading patterns contribute to OCD development at the humeral capitellum, with valgus overload in flexion predominant in baseball and weightbearing in extension more relevant in gymnastics. 13 These findings support the role of repetitive microtrauma in OCD pathogenesis and emphasize the need for targeted prevention strategies, particularly in high-risk individuals. Further research is warranted to quantify the risk of particular sports participation on the development of joint-specific OCD lesions. Despite elevated RRs in every sport analyzed, the absolute risk within each sport remained low (≤0.5%), emphasizing that OCD remains an uncommon condition even in higher-risk athletic populations.

Our findings regarding the elevated risk of OCD across multiple youth sports are supported by a recent narrative review by Poboży et al, 31 which synthesized joint-specific mechanisms of OCD in athletic populations. Their review highlights how different sports impose unique mechanical loads on developing joints, such as valgus stress at the elbow in baseball, axial compression in gymnastics, or high-impact pivoting in soccer and basketball, each contributing to localized subchondral stress and risk of lesion formation. This sport-specific biomechanical framework aligns with our quantitative results, which identified gymnastics, dancing, volleyball, and cheerleading as activities associated with the highest RR. The convergence of their joint-level insights with our population-based risk estimates emphasizes the need for early recognition and prevention strategies tailored to the sport-specific demands placed on young athletes.

Medical Comorbidities and OCD Risk

We demonstrate the increased RR of OCD related ICD-10 encounter diagnoses with several medical comorbidities. Primary thrombophilia, vWF deficiency, and Factor V Leiden represent 3 coagulopathic conditions that were associated with increased risk for OCD development. These findings represent a novel contribution to the literature, as specific coagulopathic conditions have not been previously identified as risk factors for OCD lesions. From a basic science perspective, the consistent elevation in risk across multiple distinct coagulopathic disorders aligns with the Tóth et al 34 imaging-based evidence of vascular insufficiency contributing to OCD pathogenesis.

Our findings also demonstrate a 71% higher risk of OCD related ICD-10 encounter diagnoses in children with growth hormone deficiency, providing the first quantitative signal that complements an earlier single-patient report of multifocal lesions in a patient with growth hormone deficiency. 11 Experimental work on growth-plate biology shows that hGH drives chondrocyte proliferation via receptors on physeal cartilage. 21 Disturbances in circulating levels, either from endogenous deficiency or exogenous replacement, can disrupt orderly ossification and lead to OCD development. However, it remains uncertain whether the heightened vulnerability arises from the deficiency itself or from the pharmacologic doses used to treat it. A similar ambiguity surrounds other hGH-linked orthopaedic disorders, as both slipped capital femoral epiphysis and idiopathic scoliosis are overrepresented in this cohort, underscoring the need for vigilant musculoskeletal surveillance in these patients.24,29

In line with previous research, this study demonstrates an increased risk of OCD related ICD-10 encounter diagnoses in patients with BMI >35 kg/m.2,15 Kessler et al 15 identified a strong association between obesity and OCD, reporting an 86% increased risk in extremely obese (BMI-for-age ≥ 1.2×95th percentile or a BMI ≥35 kg/m2) patients and a 60% increased risk in moderately obese (BMI-for-age ≥ 95th percentile or a BMI ≥30 kg/m2) patients. Their joint-specific analysis revealed that extreme obesity significantly elevated the risk for elbow and ankle OCD. In contrast, moderate obesity was more closely associated with knee OCD. 15 Notably, overweight status alone was not linked to increased risk at any site in their study, underscoring a threshold effect of BMI on disease development. 15 Similarly, the present study reports a significant increase in risk (14%) among patients with BMI >35 kg/m2, while patients with BMI >30 kg/m2 showed no difference in risk at the 95% CI. The discrepancy between the present study's findings and Kessler's previous work may be attributable to this study's matching design and large sample size. Together, these findings reinforce the importance of obesity as a modifiable risk factor in the pathogenesis of OCD.

Nicotine dependence, based on ICD-10 encounter diagnoses, was initially included in this analysis because of its known physiological effects on peripheral circulation. 14 Specifically, the transient vasoconstriction induced by nicotine was hypothesized to impair subchondral bone perfusion and thus contribute to the ischemic component of OCD pathogenesis. Interestingly, nicotine dependence was linked to a reduced risk of developing OCD lesions in this study, although this finding may reflect athletes having a lower likelihood of nicotine use.

Paralleling the associations observed in assessing athletic participation, several medical conditions were significantly associated with increased RR of OCD; however, the absolute risk of developing OCD in these populations remains very low. Across all evaluated medical conditions, the absolute risk of OCD ranged from 0.04% to 0.22%. Clinically, this distinction is critical for patient counseling, as individuals with these associated conditions or participating in higher-risk sports should not be led to believe that their overall risk of OCD is substantial.

Limitations

This study has several important limitations inherent to its retrospective design. As with any database analysis, there is a risk of misclassification or incomplete capture of relevant clinical information in the underlying EHR data contributing to TriNetX. In this analysis, ICD-10 encounter diagnosis codes used to identify comorbidities, such as growth hormone deficiency or nicotine dependence, may have been recorded at any point in a patient's clinical history before their first ICD-10 OCD-related encounter diagnosis code. As a result, the temporal relationship between these risk factors and OCD onset cannot be fully confirmed, and some associations may reflect resolved conditions that did not contribute directly to disease development. This limitation is particularly relevant for time-sensitive exposures like nicotine use, where physiologic status at the time of lesion formation may differ from that at the time of diagnosis or coding. Furthermore, undiagnosed or asymptomatic OCD cases may have been missed entirely, especially if no imaging was performed. Nonetheless, the use of a large, multicenter EHR platform and propensity-matched cohort design enhances the generalizability of our findings and provides a robust foundation for future prospective research.

As noted above, TriNetX suppresses exact patient counts <10 and rounds them up to 10 to protect patient privacy. This rounding may underestimate the effect size when rare events are concentrated in control groups, biasing RR estimates toward the null. This limitation is especially relevant to the interpretation of the analysis of vWF deficiency, thrombophilia, Factor V Leiden, hemophilia A, and dancing risk ratios, given their small control cohorts.

A specific, important limitation of our study is the reliance on proper coding for OCD diagnoses within the electronic health record system. The literature has demonstrated that miscoding is a well-documented issue in large databases, with substantial discrepancies between coded diagnoses and clinical or radiographic confirmation of musculoskeletal conditions.1,2 This limitation raises the possibility that some patients in our cohort may have been incorrectly labeled with OCD, while true OCD cases may also have been missed because of inaccurate coding. Future research incorporating radiographic data or chart review would strengthen diagnostic certainty and improve epidemiologic accuracy.

Similarly, another limitation of this study is its reliance on ICD-10 activity codes, which are not routinely or consistently used in clinical documentation. However, their use in clinical practice has increased in recent years. 3 As a result, the true frequency of sports participation among patients may be underrepresented, which introduces potential selection bias and limits the generalizability of our activity-related risk estimates. However, incomplete use of ICD-10 activity codes would most likely misclassify some athletes as nonathletes, thereby biasing estimates toward the null hypothesis. Therefore, the observed associations between athletic activity codes and OCD-related encounter diagnoses may underestimate the true association between sports participation and OCD.

A major limitation of our epidemiologic analysis stems from the use of a date-shifted database. While this approach safeguards patient privacy, it may affect the precision of year-specific incidence and prevalence estimates. Although we believe the overall trends observed are likely robust, shifting event dates by up to 1 year introduces uncertainty, particularly in identifying the exact timing of fluctuations such as the observed dip in 2020. Future work utilizing a non- or limited-date-shifted database would provide a more accurate trend of disease burden across the pediatric population.

Conclusion

This large-scale analysis provides updated epidemiologic trends and quantifies risk factors associated with OCD in the pediatric population. These findings highlight age, sex, specific medical conditions, and athletic participation as significant contributors to OCD risk, while also identifying novel associations with coagulopathic disorders. These insights support improved clinical vigilance, targeted prevention, and future research efforts aimed at early detection and joint-specific management strategies.
