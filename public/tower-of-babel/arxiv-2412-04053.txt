Demonstration of Enhanced Qubit Readout via Reinforcement Learning
Aniket Chatterjee, Jonathan Schwinger, Yvonne Y. Gao

Abstract
Measurement is an essential component for robust and practical quantum computation. For superconducting qubits, the measurement process involves the effective manipulation of the joint qubit-resonator dynamics, and should ideally provide the highest quality for qubit state discrimination with the shortest readout pulse and resonator reset time. Here, we harness model-free reinforcement learning (RL) together with a tailored training environment to achieve this multi-pronged optimization task. We demonstrate on the IBM quantum device that the measurement pulse obtained by the RL agent not only successfully achieves state-of-the-art performance, with an assignment error of ( 4.6 ± 0.4 ) × 10 − 3 (4.6\pm 0.4)\times 10^{-3} , but also executes the readout and the subsequent resonator reset almost 3x faster than the system’s default process. Furthermore, the learned waveforms are robust against realistic parameter drifts and follow a generalized analytical form, making them readily implementable in practice with no significant computation overhead. Our results provide an effective readout strategy to boost the performance of superconducting quantum processors and demonstrate the prowess of RL in providing optimal and experimentally informed solutions for complex quantum information processing tasks.



Efficient and accurate measurements are critical building blocks for quantum information processing. In circuit quantum electrodynamics (cQED) devices, information from qubits is typically extracted by mapping their states to a resonator, which is strongly coupled to a transmission line. This simple readout mechanism is capable of achieving high-fidelity single-shot state discrimination in typical cQED systems. However, with the continual enhancement in both the scale and robustness of current superconducting system, with gate fidelities exceeding 99.5%

or dynamic quantum circuits that demand many mid-circuit measurements

.

Thus, optimization of the readout process to maximize its overall efficacy consistently galvanizes active and multifarious efforts from the cQED community. Remarkable progress has been made in terms of hardware improvements, such as enhanced coherence properties of qubits

The process to read out the state of a superconducting qubit via a resonator can be described by the Coherent Quantum Langevin Equation
Blais et al. 2021
, with

\dot{\alpha}_{g/e}(t)=-\left(\frac{\kappa}{2}\mp i\chi\right)\alpha_{g/e}(t)-iA(t),

(1)

where
\alpha_{g/e}
is the coherent complex-valued resonator state corresponding to the qubit in
|g\rangle
and
|e\rangle
respectively,
\dot{\alpha}_{g/e}(t)
the time-derivative of the resonator state,
\kappa
the energy decay rate,
\chi
the qubit-resonator dispersive shift, and
A(t)
the coherent input field to the resonator mode, corresponding to the waveform used in the readout process. The simplest measurement in typical cQED devices uses a constant-amplitude square pulse to drive the resonator into a certain steady state that offers a large phase-space separation to enable state discrimination. Once the pulse is over, the resonator is left to passively decay to vacuum over a duration multiple times longer than its energy decay timescale
T_{1}
.

Naively, a longer and more energetic pulse should lead to the largest phase-space separation between the trajectories corresponding to
|g\rangle
and
|e\rangle
respectively, allowing the most effective state discrimination. However, the dynamics on real hardware is more subtle. Increasing the duration of the pulse also increases the likelihood of qubit decay due to its finite energy relaxation time. In addition, increments in the pulse amplitude and correspondingly, the photon population in the resonator, can also aggravate qubit coherence due to ionization
Dumas et al. 2024
. Thus, we must recognize that improving the overall readout performance is a complex, multi-faceted optimization process.

In this study, our goal is to develop a generalized qubit readout process for standard cQED devices that can achieve the best state discrimination rapidly without perturbing the qubit state. This translates into an optimization of the microwave waveform that affords the highest assignment fidelity in the shortest time and the lowest possible photon number in the resonator. To account for the competing dynamics during the readout process appropriately, qubit state assignment fidelity is defined as

\mathcal{F}=1-\frac{P(g|e)+P(e|g)}{2},

(2)

where
P(g|e)
and
P(e|g)
represent the probabilities of measuring
|g\rangle
(
|e\rangle
) state when an
|e\rangle
(
|g\rangle
) state was prepared. Apart from maximizing
\mathcal{F}
, we also aim to accelerate the resonator reset, which is another highly desirable feature as it affords more rapid repetition of measurements with minimal downtime in between.

To obtain optimal readout pulses, some past works have aimed at making suitable ansatzes about the structure of the readout waveform based on educated guesses. One of the most notable techniques under this framework is CLEAR
McClure et al. 2016
. It introduces two fixed-duration rectangular segments to the standard square readout pulse to accelerate the resonator ring-up and reset process for devices in the regimes of
\kappa\approx\chi
. In addition, other techniques, such as those investigated in Ref.
Bultink et al. 2016
;
Hoffer 2021
;
Lienhard 2021
, have shown effective reduction in ring-up and resonator reset time. However, these strategies are designed to single out a specific aspect of the readout process or device parameter regime and optimize it in isolation.

In contrast to these existing schemes, we employ a model-free deep RL
Sutton and Barto 2018
;
François-Lavet et al. 2018
framework which seeks out the best waveform that optimizes all relevant figures of merit associated with the readout concurrently and effectively accounts for known experimental constraints. A conceptual illustration of our implementation is shown in Fig.
1
. Compared to other numerical algorithms such as Nelder-Mead
Nelder and Mead 1965
and Evolutionary Strategies
Kennedy and Eberhart 1995
, which have been used towards classical-quantum optimization problems
Cheng et al. 2023
, deep RL leverages neural networks to achieve more efficient optimization for complex and multi-parameter quantum processes. By employing an effective decision-making agent for a reward-oriented problem, RL has been successfully applied to implement real-time QEC
Sivak et al. 2023b
, gate calibration
Baum et al. 2021
, and to discover novel decoders
Olle et al. 2023
. Given the strength of neural networks in learning arbitrary optimal function mappings with little model-based input, we identify that a model-free RL framework is particularly well-suited to optimize the multi-pronged readout processes.

We choose the Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO)
Schulman et al. 2017
algorithm in our deep RL strategy due to its strong convergence properties for computationally inexpensive environments. PPO is an Actor-Critic method
Konda and Tsitsiklis 2000
, where a policy neural network (Actor) learns to output optimal actions for a given reward function, and a value network (Critic) predicts the value, an estimate of the reward, for the policy. While an Actor-only
Williams 1992
or Critic-only
Mnih et al. 2013
method can be used, a combined Actor-Critic approach is more appealing for our task as it simultaneously ensures good exploration of the action space through the Actor and stable learning through the Critic.

Concretely, for each update of the learning process, the Actor outputs the means and standard deviations of a multivariate normal distribution from which an action is sampled. The Critic estimates the value of the actions sampled from this action distribution
Sutton and Barto 2018
(Fig.
1
). During training, the Critic network aims to minimise the value error, the difference between the value and reward, while the Actor aims to produce actions of high advantage, that produce higher rewards than the value estimates of the Critic. The Actor and Critic neural networks update their parameters during each update with losses calculated from the advantage and value error respectively. Training is performed until the Critic network accurately estimates the value of the actions, and the Actor cannot get higher reward than the value estimates, corresponding to finding optimal actions. For the readout problem, this corresponds to estimating the discretized waveform that most effectively maximizes the reward. One notable and advantageous feature of PPO is the clipped updates, which corresponds to updating the actor and critic networks with clipped losses, ie clipping the loss attributed to the value error and advantage to be between
-\epsilon
and
+\epsilon
where
\epsilon
is a small hyper-parameter. This prevents over-shooting, where the networks would fall into some local minima of reward with sub-optimal actions, and allows for better convergence to reduce the computational overhead incurred by the learning process. Further details about the algorithm implementation and hyper-parameters used are presented in Appendix
.1
.

We ensure the practical feasibility of RL-generated waveform by constructing a meaningful environment (Appendix
.2
) for an agent to test actions and receive observations, along with an experimentally motivated reward function from which they can update their action probability distributions to move closer to the optimum solution. The neural networks can be trained using either direct experimental data or realistic simulations of the readout dynamics. Here, due to constraints of the cloud access, we opted to perform the training using simulations that efficiently describe the dynamics of the system through simple differential equations (Eq.
1
). We use a Hamiltonian-free quasi-classical Langevin Equation to simulate the full dynamics of a resonator coupled to a transmon qubit
Koch et al. 2007
throughout a dispersive measurement. This method is computationally inexpensive as it allows us to accurately obtain all key metrics associated with the readout process without the need to model and simulate high-dimensional Hilbert space. In particular, we extract the phase-space separation of the resonator state
|\alpha_{+}-\alpha_{-}|
, where
\alpha_{\pm}
corresponds to the qubit in its ground and excited state, and
|\alpha|^{2}
the total photon population in the resonator. Using these two quantities, we compute the fidelity, the maximum number of photons used in the readout, and the residual photon population in the resonator after a reset.

With direct experimental implementation in mind, we also apply realistic hardware constraints to the waveforms before the simulation. We start by discretizing the pulse into real-valued sequences with time resolutions of 4-6 ns that conservatively match the specifications of standard control electronics in cQED. We then clip and scale the raw amplitudes to match the maximum amplitude used in the default readout configuration. Finally, we apply Gaussian convolution-based smoothing to these discrete waveforms to represent realistic electronics bandwidth in the range of 10s-100s MHz
Raftery et al. 2017
.

The crux of our optimization process involves implementing the appropriate reward function, which is crucial in improving learning and optimization efficiency
Sowerby et al. 2022
, as well as experimental viability. Along with maximizing fidelity and minimizing measurement time, we additionally want to produce smooth readout pulses to compatibility with realistic bandwidths of current control electronics. These objectives, along with several experimentally motivated penalties are integrated into our reward function

\displaystyle R=

\displaystyle-k_{1}\log_{10}(1-\max(\mathcal{F}(t)))-k_{2}\kappa\tau_{R}

\displaystyle-k_{3}\int(A^{\prime\prime}(t))^{2}dt-k_{4}(A(0)+A(t_{min,N}))

(3)

\displaystyle-k_{5}\mathrm{ReLU}(\max(N(t))-N_{0})

\displaystyle-k_{6}\heaviside(t_{max,F}-t_{min,N}),

where
k_{i}
are the individual coefficients associated with each reward term. Their specific values are detailed in Appendix
.2
. First, we maximize fidelity
\mathcal{F}
estimated from the phase-space separation
Blais et al. 2021
. We use a term proportional to the logarithm assignment error to a steady increase in the reward over a wide range in assignment error. This motivates the agent to focus on obtaining a maxima in assignment fidelity first before optimizing other auxiliary objectives. Next, we minimize the reset time, given by
\kappa\tau_{R}
where
\tau_{R}
is the resonator reset time. For ease of experimental implementation, we also encourage smooth pulses by minimizing
\int(A^{\prime\prime}(t))^{2}dt
, where
A^{\prime\prime}(t)
is the second derivative of the waveform, and penalize the terminal amplitudes
A(0)
and
A(t_{min,N})
at the start and reset point of the readout. Finally, the photon population
N
is constrained to be below the photon population of the default square readout (
N_{0}
) on the test device. A final order penalty term is used to ensure that the RL agent always achieves high fidelity and resets the resonator in the correct order.

To train and subsequently test the efficacy of our strategy on realistic quantum hardware, we use the IBMQ devices via cloud access. We obtain relevant system parameters to construct an appropriate learning environment through a series of standard calibration experiments. Specifically, we characterize the dispersive coupling strength
\chi
between the qubit and the readout resonator as well as the decay rate of the resonator
\kappa
. For the results reported in this work, we choose two devices: ibm_brisbane Qubit 2 (Brisbane) with
\kappa\approx 21.4
MHz,
{2\chi}/{2\pi}\approx 0.31
MHz operating in the regime
\kappa/\chi\approx 22
and ibm_kyoto Qubit 2 (Kyoto) with
\kappa\approx 10.4
MHz,
{2\chi}/{2\pi}\approx 0.85
MHz and
\kappa/\chi\approx 4
. Both devices have a default measurement time of 1400 ns, consisting of a 720 ns square readout pulse and a 680 ns delay for passive resonator reset. The resulting assignment errors using these default square pulses are
(5.8\pm 0.9)\times 10^{-3}
and
(7.0\pm 1.0)\times 10^{-3}
respectively. Testing our RL-enabled readout strategy on these two devices covers the range of typical system parameters used in superconducting qubit readout, with Brisbane representing the fast readout configuration
\kappa\gg\chi
made possible through the inclusion of Purcell filters
Jeffrey et al. 2014
while Kyoto operates at the more familiar
\kappa\sim\chi
point.

Using the parameters for Brisbane and Kyoto, we train the PPO agent with batch sizes of 128 steps for 5000 updates. This procedure is implemented on a Nvidia Tesla P100 GPU corresponding to a total wall-clock training time of approximately 3 minutes, which indicates a very efficient learning process and makes it a practical yet powerful strategy for readout optimization. The learning curve for the Brisbane environment is shown in Fig.
2
(a), where the normalized sum reward and its dominant contributions due to assignment fidelity, reset amplitude, pulse smoothness, and total reset time are shown as a function of the learning updates. We visualize the intermediate learned policies by plotting the mean action across the batch at the 80th, 300th, and 5000th updates, respectively in Fig.
2
(b) to gain insights on the agent’s progression during this process.

Initially, the agent is completely stochastic and produces random waveforms. However, within the first 80 updates, the agent starts to develop features corresponding to the ring-up, readout, and reset processes. At first, the agent prioritizes maximizing fidelity, leading to a slightly higher amplitude ring-up in the 80th update waveform, along with a flat readout segment to reach the appropriate fidelity. From the 80th to 300th update the agent iteratively improves the reset time, shown through the higher amplitude ring-up tone as well as the notable formation of two reset tones for high-quality reset. From the 300th update onward till the 5000th update the agent improves the reset amplitude deviation as well as smooths the waveform till we get a final converged waveform at the 5000th update. This is a clear demonstration that the agent approaches the task in a holistic manner, balancing the different figures of merit as well as practical constraints of an effective readout throughout the learning process to achieve the optimal solution.

We observe that the agent consistently obtains waveforms that completes the readout and reset process significantly faster comparable to the default readout while maintaining a comparable overall fidelity. For instance, on Kyoto, the learned pulse performs the readout (
\mathcal{F}\approx 99.5\%
) and reset in 470 ns, offering a three-fold reduction compared to the default 1400 ns. Remarkably, the sequences learned by our RL agent for both Brisbane and Kyoto consistently follow a well-defined structure consisting of three distinct segments. We dub this the Active Three Tone Readout (A3R). It follows a simple analytical form, with a single high-amplitude ring-up tone, a constant calibrated segment, and a two-tone reset. The detailed description of the parameters governing A3R is presented in Appendix
.4
. As an illustration, we show the the default square pulse, the learned pulse obtained by the RL agent, and the corresponding A3R waveform in fig
3
(a). In addition, we show that the action of the optimized pulses (RL and A3R) follow the intuitive trajectories in phase space (Fig.
3
(b)), starting with a sharp increase in the I-component, followed by a constant amplitude drive which leads to maximum separation in Q-component, without necessarily driving the resonator to its steady state. Finally, the active reset is performed to effectively reduce the final photon population to below
0.05
, which is similar to the residual population associated with the default readout on the test device. We note that while the first reset tone can clear the resonator state from
>50
to
<1
photon, a short second kickback tone is required to ensure the small fraction of remaining excitations are evacuated rapidly. This two-tone reset with segments of variable durations and amplitudes ensures a faster, more flexible, and more complete reset of the resonator as compared to the CLEAR sequence which imposes the two reset tones to be of equal duration
McClure et al. 2016
. A more detailed comparison with CLEAR is presented in Appendix
.6
. The key practical advantage of the A3R protocol is the generality of its dynamics. By modeling it as a photon transfer process, we can construct a set of simple calibration experiments detailed in Appendix
.4
to obtain the optimal A3R parameters without further numerical optimization.

|g\rangle
and

each and then play the appropriate measurement pulse. The fidelities are then calculated from the overlap of the resulting Gaussian distributions. A sample histogram obtained using the learned readout pulse is shown in Fig.

This stability test is reflective of the realistic readout performance under system parameter drifts

Furthermore, the reset component of the A3R readout can be fine-tuned using a Stark shift-based calibration. We note that all tuning can also be done by e.g. a Nelder-Mead optimization in less than 20 iterations, mitigating the effects of
\chi
and
\kappa

In summary, we constructed an experimentally informed RL agent to effectively optimize the readout process of a superconducting qubit with minimal computational overhead. We implemented the learned protocol obtained by the agent on IBMQ devices and showed that they successfully achieve high-fidelity readout with accelerated resonator reset, offering a significant reduction in the total measurement duration. Furthermore, the optimized sequences exhibit well-defined intuitive structures and are robust against hardware parameter drifts, highlighting their practical applicability to real quantum processors. The optimization pipeline is general and simple to use. It can be readily combined with other hardware-based readout enhancement strategies to further improve their overall performance. Overall, our work harnesses the intersectional knowledge between model-free reinforcement learning and quantum information science to achieve tangible enhancement in the real and concrete task of superconducting readout, which is a crucial element in enabling robust quantum computation using superconducting devices.

Reinforcement Learning Implementation

For the task of readout optimization, we utilize Policy-based Reinforcement Learning. The algorithm tries to find the optimal policy function
\pi_{\theta}(s|a)
which chooses the best action
a
to take at a given state
s
, parametrized by a neural network with weights
\theta
. Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) is a widely used policy-based algorithm known for its robust learning and convergence capabilities without expansive memory requirements. PPO is an Actor-Critic method where the Actor Network (Policy) outputs the actions to take at a given state, and the Critic Network tries to predict the value of the actions taken. The Actor tries to maximize its advantage
A=R-V
, where
R
is the reward for a given action and
V
is the value predicted by the Critic. The Critic focuses on improving its target estimates
(V-R)^{2}
. As the actor, through the learning process, increases its likelihood of producing high-advantage actions, the critic improves its estimates of the actor’s reward. Thus, the PPO algorithm can learn optimal policies.

.2
Readout Environment

The readout environment takes a 1D real vector of pulse amplitudes as input, which corresponds to the readout waveform sent to the resonator. Real-valued pulses are sufficient as we focus on systems with low self-Kerr, and operate within
n<<n_{c}
where
n_{c}
is the critical photon population
Curtis et al. 2021
. Before the simulation, we clip the amplitudes to
\textpm\mu
, where
\mu
is the maximum amplitude available on the IBMQ system relative to the default amplitude used for measurement. We also smooth the waveform using a Gaussian Convolution to mimic a low pass filter response by a typical Arbitrary Waveform Generator. Finally, we scale up the waveform by the steady-state amplitude
A_{0}=0.5N_{0}\sqrt{\kappa^{2}+4\chi^{2}}
where
N_{0}
is the default measurement photon population.

The processed waveform is passed to the Langevin Simulation, described by Eq.
1
. From the simulated coherent state amplitude we can obtain the photon population
N(t)=|\alpha(t)|^{2}
and the state separation
S(t)=|\alpha_{+}(t)-\alpha_{-}(t)|
. To calculate the resultant time-dependent fidelity, we model the resonator as a Gaussian state and assume we have perfect boxcar integration weights that can acquire the instantaneous signal. Hence, the extracted fidelity from the state separation in phase space is given by

F_{\mathrm{SNR}}(t)=0.5\left(1+\text{erf}\left(\lambda S(t)\right)\right),

(4)

where
\lambda
is a scaling constant that captures any amplification from a quantum-limited amplifier and assumes approximately constant noise for the distribution. The state separation fidelity
F_{\mathrm{SNR}}
represents the fidelity of the measurement if we assume perfect state preparation and no qubit evolution during the readout. We also account for the fidelity associated with qubit decay and measurement-induced state transitions by adding a qubit decay rate linear in the resonator photon population
Gambetta et al. 2006
. This qubit fidelity term,
F_{q}
, is given by

F_{q}(t)=0.5\left(1+\exp\left(-\gamma_{0}t+\gamma_{P}\int_{0}^{t}N(\tau)d\tau\right)\right).

(5)

Finally, including a state preparation fidelity of
F_{0}
, the overall fidelity is given by

\mathcal{F}(t)=F_{0}\times F_{q}(t)\times F_{\mathrm{SNR}}(t).

(6)

Notably, the qubit fidelity
F_{q}
decays with time while the state separation fidelity
F_{\mathrm{SNR}}
increases with time. Thus, it is clear that there is an optimal measurement duration that is not necessarily that of steady-state due to the realistic qubit decay. Finally, we evaluate the resulting assignment fidelity as
\max{\mathcal{F}(t)}
, as we are only interested in the maximum fidelity available in the readout, which is then used in the reward function.

To calculate the readout duration, we assume that the measurement ends when the photon population hits a minimum after ring-up. This corresponds to verifying the time of the first photon minima after the time of max fidelity, enforced by adding an order penalty in the reward function,
R_{order}=-100\times\heaviside{t_{max,F}-t_{min,N}}
. Hence, the reset time of the readout corresponds to
t_{min,N}
along with the additional time required to reach the target photon population
N_{I}
. We calculate this by assuming standard exponential decay of the photon population. Thus, the total reset time
t_{R}
is given by

t_{R}=t_{min,N}+\frac{m}{\kappa}*\log\left(\frac{N(t_{min,N})}{N_{I}}\right),

(7)

where
m
is a
T_{1}
scaling factor added to discourage the agent from doing partial reset and then waiting. We find a factor of
m=8
is sufficient for forcing the agent to actively reset all systems regardless of the system
\kappa
.

The reward function for the environment is given by Eq.

. We use the negative log error instead of fidelity to provide consistent reward across fidelity scales, while for the reset time, we use the duration of the readout in terms of the number of resonator lifetimes,
\kappa\tau_{r}
. This keeps it as a more relevant metric that does not drastically change when optimizing with different system
\kappa
hence requiring less reward tuning. To encode smoothness, we define the noisiness of a pulse as
\int(A^{\prime\prime}(t))^{2}dt
and positively reward the agent for lower noisiness, leading to smoother pulses. We additionally add a soft constraint term negative and proportional to the initial amplitude
A_{0}=A(t=0)
and final amplitude
A_{F}=A(t_{minima})
of the pulse such that the learned pulse satisfies zero amplitude boundary conditions. Rather than a hard constraint for the photon population we opt for a soft constraint
ReLU
term penalty, such that the agent does not get stuck producing waveforms achieving overly high photon populations in the case of poor network initialization. The order penalty is introduced to prevent the agent from taking the shortcut of first driving weakly and then resetting to a low minimum quickly. We choose reward coefficients
k_{i}
to prioritize fidelity, after which auxiliary objectives of faster readout and smoothing factor in. Hence, we set the fidelity coefficient
k_{1}=10
, the time coefficient
k_{2}=2
, and the smoothness coefficient
k_{3}=1
. Note that only the relative magnitude between coefficients is significant for training dynamics. We determine the time and smoothness coefficients through grid sweeps of training runs, and we find as long as the two coefficients are significantly less than that of the fidelity, we recover the waveforms found in the main text. We set penalty term coefficients such as the amplitude coefficient
k_{4}
and the photon coefficient
k_{5}
to be large values of 100 such that they are enforced throughout the training run.

We use Diffrax
Kidger 2021
and Jax for the ODE Simulation and environment implementation, which in conjunction with the Jax implementation of PPO allows us to leverage jit-compilation and automatic vectorization to run fast parallel training runs. This makes it possible to run fast hyperparameter optimization and optimization stability tests with nominal additional training time required.

.5
Active 3-Tone (A3R) Pulse Readout

The Active 3-Tone (A3R) Pulse Readout is a fast high-fidelity protocol that performs active ring-up, readout and reset in the high
\frac{\kappa}{\chi}
regime. It consists of a square readout augmented with a single calibrated high amplitude ring-up tone and two variable amplitude and duration reset tones. Compared to a standard measurement, where a readout resonator is driven to a steady-state photon population of
N_{0}
and then left to passively decay, the A3R readout aims to ring-up, readout, and reset as fast as possible in the high
\frac{\kappa}{\chi}
regime. Overall there are 7 variables defining the readout, which can be calibrated using a simple 3-step experiment routine.

For devices where the maximum amplitude,
\mu A_{0}
, is not too large compared to the default measurement amplitude,
A_{0}
, as in the case of Kyoto, all segments are driven at their maximum amplitude and only the durations need to be optimized. For a ring-up tone at amplitude
\mu A_{0}
, we can solve the Coherent Langevin Equation exactly for constant amplitude tones and neglecting self-Kerr, leading to an analytical duration for
\tau_{1}
given by

\tau_{1}=\frac{2}{\kappa}\log\left(\frac{\mu}{\mu-1}\right),

(8)

where first-order approximations were taken in
\chi t
to drop oscillating terms. Accounting for bandwidth constraints in real experiments,
\tau_{1}
is determined through a photon measurement sweep using the analytical value as an estimate. To determine the optimal flat readout duration,
\tau_{r}
, we play an acquisition scan with the calibrated ring-up tone and extract the duration that corresponds to the maximum fidelity.

For optimal resonator reset, two tones are required to account for the significant impact of
\chi
on the trajectory. A single reset tone only accounting for the
\kappa
contribution would be inaccurate and miss the origin in phase space, leading to an erroneous contribution to the photon population. Nevertheless, an estimate of the first reset tone duration
\tau_{2}
can be calculated with,

\tau_{2}=\frac{2}{\kappa}\ln\left(\frac{\mu+1}{\mu}\right),

(9)

which is independent of
\chi
. For very large
\frac{\kappa}{\chi}
this expression is accurate and a perfect reset can be achieved. Accounting for
\chi
contributions, the reset photon error
N_{e}
is

N_{e}=2N_{0}\left(\mu\ln\left(\frac{\mu+1}{\mu}\right)\frac{2\chi}{\kappa}\right)^{2}.

(10)

One significant point to note is that single-tone reset is effective in removing the bulk of the energy. For the case of Brisbane, it resets from 58.8 to 0.6 photons, a 98x reduction in population and equivalent to
4.6\times
resonator lifetimes in passive decay. Finally, the second tone evacuates the remaining photons to
<0.05
. As the two-tone reset contains a large change in amplitude during the pulse, the second reset tone is best calibrated through experiment. A grid sweep across the amplitude and duration using reset photon measurements is sufficient to find the parameters for optimal reset. This experimental protocol of calibrating the ring-up duration, readout duration, and second reset tone parameters is easily implementable in labs. Alternatively, a sample-efficient optimizer such as Nelder-Mead
Nelder and Mead 1965
or LBGFS
Nocedal and Wright 2006
, can also be used to identify optimal A3R parameters from a simulation model in less than 20 iterations further reducing experimental calibration time.

.6
Comparison with CLEAR

In this study, we have demonstrated that A3R provides a significant improvement in the readout efficacy for the test devices in the
\kappa\gg\chi
regime. Here, we provide an analysis of its performance against CLEAR (Ref.
McClure et al. 2016
), which enacts an active ring-up and reset alongside a standard steady-state square pulse. Focusing on the
\kappa\approx\chi
regime, CLEAR applies the ansatz of playing two segments of equal durations with variable amplitudes before and after a square pulse. The four optimal amplitudes associated with the ring-up and reset are analytically determined in the linear regime, and more generally, could be obtained numerically with the appropriate target photon populations in mind. For instance, in Ref.
McClure et al. 2016
, the authors used a target of
N_{0}
after ring-up and
\approx 0.1
photons after the reset. Overall, they find a speed-up of 50% in resonator reset compared to using a passive process following a square readout on the specific test device.

To compare the A3R readout with CLEAR, we reproduce the optimization protocol outlined in Ref.
McClure et al. 2016
to determine CLEAR pulse parameters for the Kyoto device using a Nelder-Mead
Nelder and Mead 1965
optimizer implemented from Scipy
Virtanen et al. 2020
. The resulting optimized parameters for CLEAR show a noticeably faster reset (
\approx 460\,
ns) compared to the default (
\approx 680\,
ns). However, no discernible speed-up is found for the ring-up stage in this
\kappa\gg\chi
regime when a maximum photon number constraint is imposed in the optimization. Overall, for Kyoto, CLEAR requires
\approx 1180
ns for a full readout cycle. While it offers an improvement over the default (1400 ns), it significantly under-performs compared to A3R, which completes the full readout process in 470 ns under the same optimization constraints.

Overall, alongside a 3x speed up over the simple square readout with passive decay, A3R demonstrates a 2.5x speed up over the CLEAR waveform. While A3R and CLEAR are quite reminiscent of each other structurally, several improvements in A3R such as a single segment ring-up, optimized readout durations, and flexible reset segment durations for the high
\frac{\kappa}{\chi}
regime, allow for significantly improved readout speeds while preserving a robust assignment fidelity.
