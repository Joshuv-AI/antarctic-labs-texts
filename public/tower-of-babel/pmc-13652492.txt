Isolated left-lobe metastatic hepatic abscess due to portal pyemia from microperforated ascending colon carcinoma: a rare case report
Authors: Suman Supratik, Manaswi Acharya, Rajani Neupane, Abhijeet Kumar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Introduction and importance:Liver abscess–like lesions are commonly managed as primary hepatobiliary infections; however, colorectal cancer can be an occult source, particularly when portal pyemia follows a microperforated tumor. Introduction and importance

Pyogenic liver abscess (PLA) is most often attributed to biliary infection, yet portal venous seeding from colorectal pathology is a well-recognized alternative. The association is clinically important because a liver abscess can precede or obscure the diagnosis of an underlying right-sided colon cancer. Diagnostic uncertainty is amplified when an abscess localizes to the left hepatic lobe, where mass effect can mimic a primary gastric process. In low-resource environments, lack of anaerobic culture capability and limited access to immunohistochemistry (IHC) make it even harder to disentangle infection from necrotic or cavitating metastasis. This is a SCARE-compliant case report using contemporaneous chart/imaging/operative/pathology records at B. P. Koirala Institute of Health Sciences, with joint computed tomography (CT) re-review and aerobic culture only (anaerobic unavailable)[1]. “Metastatic hepatic abscess” was defined histologically (gland-forming adenocarcinoma within an abscessed necroinflammatory background), and a targeted PubMed/Google Scholar search was performed to contextualize the case. The operating surgeon was a senior consultant colorectal and hepatobiliary surgeon with more than 15 years of experience in oncologic resections, operating at a tertiary academic referral center in Nepal with full general surgical, intensive care, and pathology support.

Case presentation

A middle-aged woman in her fifth decade presented with an 8-month history of dull, intermittent right iliac fossa pain and increasingly frequent watery, foul-smelling, mucoid, non-bloody stools, accompanied by approximately 6 kg of unintentional weight loss. She had one episode of non-bilious, non-bloody vomiting and denied jaundice, chest pain, dyspnea, urinary symptoms, constipation, or overt gastrointestinal bleeding. Eight months earlier, colonoscopy at a tertiary center had demonstrated a circumferential ulceroproliferative lesion in the ascending colon causing luminal narrowing that was highly suspicious for adenocarcinoma. Endoscopic images from the referring center were not retrievable at the time of this report because the patient had been seen at an outside institution and had not brought archival records. Surgery was advised, but she left against medical advice and pursued Ayurvedic treatments. Worsening symptoms culminated in fever and sepsis, prompting admission.

On examination, she was pale and febrile but hemodynamically stable. The abdomen revealed a firm, irregular, tender, mobile right iliac fossa mass measuring approximately 6 × 4 cm. There was no hepatosplenomegaly, and bowel sounds were present. Other systems were unremarkable. Labs showed severe anemia (hemoglobin 7.2 g/dL; packed cell volume 23%) and leukocytosis (total leukocyte count 21 040/mm3). Tumor markers were notable for CEA 13.81 ng/mL, CA-125 164.52 U/mL, and CA 19-9 2.06 U/mL. She received three units of packed red blood cells and was optimized preoperatively.

Ultrasonography suggested a large heterogeneous ascending-colon mass. Contrast-enhanced CT revealed a heterogeneously enhancing mass involving the cecum, ascending colon, and ileocecal valve (Fig. 1) with areas of necrosis and suspected microperforation, as well as right pericolic lymphadenopathy. There was evidence of ileocolic vein thrombosis and an appendiceal mucocele. Importantly, a left-lobe subcapsular hypodense hepatic collection demonstrated peripheral enhancement and internal gas, with features consistent with an abscess and suspected rupture into the peritoneal space (Fig. 2). A separate impression of a “gastric mass” along the greater curvature was subsequently clarified intraoperatively to represent mass effect from the adjacent left-lobe collection rather than intrinsic gastric pathology (Fig. 3). Surgical management

Using aseptic precautions, a midline laparotomy was performed. Systematic exploration identified a right-sided ascending colon mass involving the cecum and extending to the hepatic flexure, with an associated appendiceal mucocele. In the left hepatic lobe, there was a ruptured segment II lesion containing necrotic material, with abscess formation that tracked toward the greater curvature of the stomach and involved the left hemidiaphragm, explaining the apparent gastric abnormality on imaging. Mild ascites was present, and no additional intra-abdominal metastases were detected.

The ruptured hepatic abscess-like cavity was drained. A right hemicolectomy was then performed using standard oncologic technique: the right colon was mobilized from the cecum cranially along the white line of Toldt with division of lateral peritoneal attachments; retroperitoneal structures including the ureter and gonadal vessels were identified and preserved; the gastrocolic and hepatocolic ligaments were divided to mobilize the hepatic flexure; and central vascular ligation of the ileocolic artery, right colic vessels, and the right branch of the middle colic vessels was undertaken. The terminal ileum and transverse colon were adequately mobilized, the bowel was divided, and the specimen was removed. An end-to-end ileocolic anastomosis was fashioned, hemostasis was secured, and a right-sided pelvic drain was placed. The abdominal wall was closed in layers. The patient was extubated and transferred to the intensive care unit (ICU) for monitoring. Management for ileocolic vein thrombosis was instituted per institutional protocol.

Postoperative course and pathology

The postoperative course was overall favorable. The timeline of postoperative events is summarized in Table 1. On day 2, she passed flatus and oral intake was initiated; the nasogastric tube was removed on day 4. She was transferred from the ICU to the ward on day 2 and was managed with intravenous antibiotics, analgesia, intravenous fluids, continuous monitoring, daily dressing changes, dietary charting, chest physiotherapy, and incentive spirometry. A pulmonology consultation was obtained for a reactive pleural effusion, which was managed conservatively without drainage and resolved clinically prior to discharge.

On postoperative day 4, a superficial surgical site infection developed, classified as Clavien–Dindo Grade I. Staples were removed, and twice-daily wound dressing with topical gentamicin was initiated. A wound swab grew methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA) (linezolid-sensitive), and she improved with targeted therapy and wound care. Key postoperative antimicrobial therapy included piperacillin–tazobactam 4.5 g intravenously every 6 hours empirically and was subsequently transitioned to oral linezolid 600 mg twice daily following MRSA identification. She was discharged afebrile, tolerating oral feeds, passing stool and flatus, with wound healing and the drain in situ. She was instructed to chart drain output and to remove it when the output was less than 15 mL/day for 3 consecutive days.

An aerobic culture from the hepatic collection showed no growth after 48 hours; an anaerobic culture was not available.

Histopathology of the colon resection specimen confirmed moderately differentiated adenocarcinoma (pT3N1a) with lymphovascular invasion. All resection margins: proximal, distal, and mesenteric/radial, were negative (R0 resection), and 1 of 18 regional lymph nodes was positive. The appendix showed an LAMN. The hepatic lesion was sampled by core biopsies (four linear gray-brown soft tissue cores measuring 0.2–1.5 cm × 0.1 cm). Microscopy showed a background of acute hepatitis with necrosis and a marked mixed inflammatory infiltrate, cholangitis with ductular proliferation, and perisinusoidal fibrosis. Within this milieu, atypical gland-forming epithelium consistent with metastatic well-differentiated adenocarcinoma from the known colonic primary was identified, thereby confirming hepatic metastasis with superimposed abscess: a metastatic hepatic abscess. Iron and copper stains were negative. While limited IHC was recommended to further characterize the metastatic focus, the clinico-pathologic correlation was sufficient for diagnostic certainty.

Follow-up and adjuvant therapy

At discharge, the patient was scheduled for wound review and repeat imaging (CT) to document resolution of the hepatic collection, along with referral to medical oncology for restaging and systemic therapy planning appropriate for pT3N1a colon cancer with hepatic metastasis. FOLFOX-based adjuvant chemotherapy was considered the preferred systemic treatment, pending molecular profiling (RAS/RAF mutation status and mismatch repair/microsatellite instability testing) and multidisciplinary team review. Hepatic-directed therapy was to be re-evaluated after infectious source resolution and performance status recovery. Limited IHC on the hepatic cores may be pursued, if available, to further characterize the metastatic focus, though clinico-pathologic concordance already supports the diagnosis. No structured follow-up data beyond discharge were available at the time of manuscript submission.

Clinical discussion

PLA is a relatively rare but important complication of colorectal cancer, especially when it is the initial sign of an undiagnosed right-sided colonic tumor. Several studies have shown a clear link between PLA and hidden colorectal malignancy, which may contribute to delayed diagnosis when symptoms are vague or access to imaging is limited[2,3]. Colorectal carcinoma presenting with a sealed perforation and a perihepatic (subcapsular) abscess, particularly in the left lobe of the liver and accompanied by gas within the perihepatic collection, is uncommon and creates significant diagnostic difficulties for clinicians, radiologists, and surgeons[4].

It is important to clarify the sequence of clinical events in this case. The patient’s initial and dominant symptom was right iliac fossa pain attributable to the underlying ascending colon carcinoma, which had been colonoscopically diagnosed 8 months earlier. The left-lobe metastatic hepatic abscess was not the first symptom but rather the complicating event that precipitated sepsis and led to the final emergency presentation. This distinction is clinically relevant: the case does not represent a scenario where a liver abscess was the sole presenting feature of occult colorectal cancer, but rather one where a colorectal cancer known to the patient, yet untreated due to patient preference, progressed to microperforation, portal pyemia, and hepatic metastasis with superimposed abscess formation.

In the present case, an ascending colon adenocarcinoma complicated by suspected localized microperforation was associated with a left-lobe subcapsular abscess-like collection that ultimately ruptured and tracked superiorly, creating diagnostic noise by mimicking a gastric mass on imaging due to extrinsic compression. This pattern is distinctly uncommon, as most colorectal-source hepatic abscesses involve the right hepatic lobe due to vascular drainage patterns[5]. Damage to the intestinal mucosal barrier, such as that caused by a micro-perforating carcinoma, can enable bacteria from the colon to enter the portal circulation. These bacteria may then travel through the bloodstream to the liver, creating a pathophysiological connection between colonic disease and PLAs [3]. Hematogenous dissemination typically leads to round abscesses that do not contain gas, whereas direct spread or localized contamination often results in more complex, multiloculated, or gas-forming abscesses because of the presence of mixed intestinal bacteria[6,7].

The CT finding of an apparent mass along the greater curvature of the stomach warrants specific discussion, as it represents one of the most diagnostically challenging and educationally instructive aspects of this case. The differential diagnosis at the time of imaging interpretation included a primary gastric neoplasm such as a gastrointestinal stromal tumor (GIST), a gastric adenocarcinoma, or an extraluminal extrinsic process. Features that favor GIST include well-defined exophytic submucosal masses with heterogeneous enhancement and central necrosis, whereas extrinsic compression typically produces displacement without intrinsic wall thickening or mucosal irregularity. In this case, the absence of intrinsic gastric wall thickening on careful CT review, the proximity of the collection to the left hepatic lobe, and intraoperative confirmation that there was no primary gastric pathology, with the apparent mass representing displacement by the adjacent ruptured hepatic collection, allowed definitive exclusion of gastric pathology. This illustrates the importance of integrating operative findings with imaging interpretation, particularly in complex multi-compartment abdominal disease.

CT imaging in our case demonstrated a hypodense perihepatic collection in the left-lobe subcapsular region, with peripheral enhancement, internal gas, and apparent continuity with the peritoneal space, supporting an abscess-like process. Notably, a hepatic core biopsy also demonstrated a very scant atypical glandular focus suggestive of metastatic well-differentiated adenocarcinoma within an inflammatory, necrotic background, and limited IHC was recommended for definitive categorization. This overlap highlights that abscess-like liver lesions in colorectal cancer may reflect infection alone, superinfection, or cavitation of metastatic deposits, and that limited sampling can constrain certainty when only small tissue cores are available[8].

Comparison with previously reported cases further contextualizes this presentation. Qu et al described a right-sided colon cancer directly invading the liver, gallbladder, and duodenum, which initially presented as a PLA, highlighting direct extension as a distinct mechanism from the portal pyemic route seen in our case[4]. Jeong et al demonstrated that cryptogenic PLA was significantly associated with subsequent colorectal cancer diagnosis, supporting the concept that an undetected or untreated colonic tumor is the biological driver of portal bacteremia in many cryptogenic cases[3]. Rawla et al reported a case of colon carcinoma presenting with Streptococcus anginosus bacteremia and a liver abscess, emphasizing the microbiological signatures that should trigger colorectal investigation[8]. Tsai et al described perforated colonic cancers presenting as intra-abdominal abscesses with pericolic collections rather than frank free perforation, conceptually analogous to the microperforation mechanism in our case[6]. What distinguishes our case from these reports is the combination of: left-lobe rather than right-lobe localization of the hepatic collection; frank rupture of the collection with peritoneal communication and extrinsic gastric compression mimicking a primary gastric mass; the coexistence of ileocolic vein thrombosis (septic pylephlebitis) and a LAMN; and histological confirmation of metastatic adenocarcinoma within the abscessed hepatic tissue, satisfying the definition of a true metastatic hepatic abscess rather than a simple infectious complication.

A large cohort study found that the link between PLA and a later diagnosis of colorectal carcinoma occurred mainly in patients with cryptogenic PLA, indicating that an undetected cancer was probably the underlying source of the infection[2]. A major diagnostic challenge, particularly in low- and middle-income countries, is the absence of facilities for anaerobic cultures. This limitation can produce negative culture results even when an infection exists, potentially leading to an underestimation of the true causative pathogens[9]. Anaerobes and microaerophilic organisms, such as Streptococcus anginosus and Bacteroides species, may escape detection under standard laboratory conditions, aligning with sterile aerobic cultures despite imaging and operative evidence of infection[10].

This case also highlights additional coexisting findings, including ileocolic vein thrombosis and incidental LAMN. Venous thrombosis is increasingly recognized in intra-abdominal sepsis and malignancy, and PLA-related septic pylephlebitis has been described in case series, with significant morbidity if untreated[11,12].

Management requires early antibiotics and prompt source control, as a substantial proportion of PLAs are culture-negative, especially where anaerobic culture facilities are limited[13]. In this patient, rupture of the left-lobe collection and the need for oncologic source control warranted operative drainage with a right hemicolectomy. The incidental LAMN was definitively treated with hemicolectomy in line with current recommendations for localized disease[14]. After histologic confirmation of hepatic metastasis, restaging, surveillance imaging to document abscess resolution, and coordinated oncology planning become essential.

With a confirmed diagnosis of pT3N1a colon adenocarcinoma with hepatic metastasis (stage IV disease), adjuvant systemic therapy is an important consideration. FOLFOX-based chemotherapy (oxaliplatin, leucovorin, and fluorouracil) represents the current standard backbone for adjuvant and palliative treatment in this setting, with the addition of targeted agents such as bevacizumab depending on molecular profiling, including RAS/RAF and mismatch repair status. Hepatic-directed therapies, including repeat surgical resection or ablation of the metastatic deposit, may be considered after adequate source control and infectious resolution, contingent upon multidisciplinary team evaluation. In this case, the patient was referred to medical oncology at discharge for restaging imaging and therapy planning; however, no further follow-up data were available at the time of manuscript submission because the patient was lost to structured follow-up. This represents a recognized limitation of case reporting in resource-limited settings.

Patient perspective

“I had stomach pain and loose stools on and off for months. I was afraid of surgery and tried traditional medicine, but my symptoms got worse and I became very unwell with a fever. The doctors explained that the liver abscess was related to my colon cancer and required urgent surgery. After the operation and antibiotics, my pain settled and I felt relief. I wish I had agreed to surgery earlier, and I would tell others not to delay when cancer is suspected.”

Conclusion

This case illustrates a clinically instructive and diagnostically complex presentation of ascending colon adenocarcinoma, in which delayed surgical intervention, driven by the patient's preference for traditional medicine, allowed disease progression to microperforation, portal pyemia, ileocolic vein thrombosis, and the development of a ruptured left-lobe metastatic hepatic abscess. The rupture of this collection caused extrinsic gastric compression that initially mimicked a primary gastric neoplasm, adding a further layer of diagnostic complexity. Right hemicolectomy achieved an R0 resection (pT3N1a), and hepatic histology confirmed the rare entity of a metastatic hepatic abscess, defined by gland-forming adenocarcinoma within an abscessed, necrotic, inflammatory background.

The principal clinical lessons from this case are threefold. First, cryptogenic or atypically located PLAs, particularly those involving the left lobe with internal gas, should prompt systematic evaluation for an underlying colorectal malignancy. Second, in resource-constrained settings where anaerobic culture and IHC are limited, the integration of imaging, intraoperative findings, and core biopsy histology remains sufficient for clinico-pathologic diagnosis when concordance is strong. Third, the eight-month delay between colonoscopic diagnosis and definitive surgical treatment in this patient underscores the critical importance of patient education, early surgical intervention, and multidisciplinary follow-up when colorectal malignancy is identified. Early imaging, timely oncologic source control, and close pathology–oncology collaboration are pivotal to optimizing both infectious and oncologic outcomes in such complex presentations.
