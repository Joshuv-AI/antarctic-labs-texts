Convective rainfall drives behavioral shifts across multiple foraging scales in breeding seabirds
Authors: Wataru Takeda, Shiho Koyama, Yusuke Goto, Ken Yoda
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundRainfall can influence seabird foraging behavior through interactions with other climatic variables, such as wind speed and cloud cover, causing both immediate constraints on flight and foraging activity and longer-term changes via shifts in prey distribution. To understand the ecological significance of rainfall, it is therefore necessary to examine foraging behavior across multiple spatiotemporal scales. Rainfall can be broadly classified into convective and large-scale types based on spatial extent and persistence, yet their distinct effects on seabird foraging behavior have rarely been explicitly tested.MethodsWe examined how two types of rainfall (convective and large-scale rainfall), cloud cover, and wind speed relate to foraging behavior and chick growth in streaked shearwaters (Calonectris leucomelas) rearing chicks on Awashima Island, Japan, from 2011 to 2024. Foraging behavior was quantified at three spatiotemporal scales: (1) fine-scale behavioral states (traveling, foraging, and resting), estimated using a hidden Markov model; (2) meso-scale trip parameters, including maximum range and trip duration; and (3) broad-scale behavioral specialization, quantified as Individual Foraging Site Fidelity (IFSF). Chick growth rate was used as a proxy for the cumulative outcomes of foraging behavior.ResultsIncreased convective rainfall was associated with higher transition probabilities from other states to the foraging state and with higher IFSF. In contrast, increased large-scale rainfall was associated with lower transition probabilities from the foraging state to other states, longer maximum range, and lower IFSF. In addition, wind speed was associated with changes in fine-scale behavioral states and meso-scale trip parameters, whereas cloud cover showed no ecologically meaningful effects. None of the climatic factors examined were associated with chick growth.ConclusionsShort-term, localized convective rainfall is likely to alter foraging conditions in ways consistent with its defining characteristics, leading to rapid switches to foraging behavior and higher IFSF through increased patchiness in prey distribution. In contrast, because large-scale rainfall is persistent and spatially extensive, it likely promoted sustained foraging behavior and more homogeneous prey distributions, resulting in lower IFSF. By examining climatic effects across multiple spatiotemporal scales, this study contributes to a more comprehensive understanding of how climate change influences seabird foraging behavior.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40462-026-00648-8.

Background

In recent years, rapid temperature increases driven by human activities have been observed globally [1], significantly altering marine climatic conditions, particularly patterns of cloud cover, rainfall, and wind [2–4]. As top predators in marine ecosystems, seabirds are especially sensitive to climatic variability [5–7]. Climatic factors such as cloud cover [8], rainfall [9], and wind speed [9–11] can directly constrain seabird foraging behavior. In addition to these direct effects, rainfall [10, 12] and wind speed [10–12] may indirectly influence seabird foraging by altering the distribution and availability of prey resources. Understanding these complex interactions between climate and seabird foraging behavior is important for future conservation and population management strategies.

Rain may immediately constrain seabird flight and foraging activity by increasing flight costs [12] and reducing prey visibility [13]. For example, in magnificent frigatebirds (Fregata magnificens), foraging time decreased, whereas time spent at roosts increased during rain [9]. Similarly, in Cape gannets (Morus capensis), moderate rainfall increased the time spent foraging regardless of breeding period, likely reflecting compensation for impaired foraging conditions caused by rain-induced water turbidity [14]. In contrast, rainfall may exert longer-term effects on seabird foraging efficiency by modifying the distribution and abundance of fish, the primary prey of seabirds. For example, mulloway (Argyrosomus japonicus), which inhabit estuarine environments, tend to move into shallower waters following rain events [15]. Moreover, annual catches of mullet (Mugil cephalus) and barramundi (Lates calcarifer) have been reported to increase with greater rainfall [16]. Thus, these findings indicate that rainfall influences seabird foraging behavior through multiple pathways operating across distinct temporal scales.

While various studies have explored the relationship between rainfall and animal behavior, less attention has been paid to how rainfall influences spatial consistency in foraging. Individual Foraging Site Fidelity (IFSF) refers to a form of behavioral specialization in which animals repeatedly utilize the same foraging sites. In breeding seabirds, the degree of IFSF can vary depending on environmental conditions [17, 18]. This pattern can be explained by spatial heterogeneity in prey distribution and by the predictability of prey resources [19–21]. Specifically, in environments where prey is patchily distributed and highly predictable, IFSF tends to be high, whereas in environments with spatially uniform prey availability, repeatedly visiting specific locations provides little foraging advantage, resulting in lower IFSF. Consequently, rain-induced changes in prey distribution and abundance may affect IFSF, which reflects foraging patterns integrated over an entire breeding season.

Furthermore, rainfall at breeding colonies has been reported to negatively affect chick condition, resulting in reduced chick growth rates [22, 23]. By contrast, because foraging time and IFSF are associated with breeding performance [24–27], rainfall at sea may indirectly affect chick growth through changes in parental foraging behavior. Indeed, in blue-footed boobies (Sula nebouxii), years with heavier rainfall during the egg-laying period are associated with higher chick fledging rates, likely due to improved foraging conditions induced by rainfall [28]. Therefore, to comprehensively understand the impacts of rainfall on seabirds, it is necessary to examine its effects on foraging behavior across multiple spatiotemporal scales, including fine-scale behavioral states, meso-scale foraging time, and broad-scale behavioral specialization such as IFSF, as well as fitness-related outcomes, such as chick growth.

In addition to spatial and temporal scales, an often-overlooked aspect of studies on rainfall and animal behavior is that rainfall can be categorized into two distinct types: convective and large-scale rainfall (Supplementary Fig. 1). Large-scale rain typically originates from nimbostratus clouds, whereas convective rain is generated by cumulonimbus clouds [29]. Convective rain is generally more localized and shorter-lived than large-scale rain [30, 31], suggesting that these two rainfall types may exert distinct influences on foraging behavior across different spatiotemporal scales. Furthermore, temperature increases driven by global warming are expected to lead to increases in the frequency and intensity of convective rainfall in the future [32–34]. Therefore, to anticipate the potential impacts of future shifts in rainfall patterns on seabird behavior, it is necessary to distinguish between these two rain types and compare their respective effects on foraging behavior.

Breeding streaked shearwaters (Calonectris leucomelas) commute between their breeding colonies and offshore foraging grounds, returning to the colony for incubation and chick provisioning. These round-trip movements are referred to as foraging trips. During foraging trips, seabirds may be immediately constrained by rainfall through its effects on flight and foraging efficiency and/or influenced over longer timescales by rainfall-driven changes in prey distribution. In addition, because streaked shearwaters are burrow-nesting seabirds, the direct effects of rain on chicks are likely to be rare, whereas indirect effects mediated through parental foraging behavior are likely to be more substantial. In this study, we investigated how three aspects of foraging behavior at different spatial and temporal scales, as well as resulting chick growth rates, were related to rainfall. Our analysis focused on 311 individuals during the chick-rearing period over a 14-year span. Because the foraging behavior of streaked shearwaters can also be shaped by other climatic variables, we incorporated cloud cover and wind speed into the models to avoid biased interpretations of rainfall effects. These climatic factors have previously been shown to influence foraging behavior in Procellariiformes [8, 10, 35, 36]. Specifically, we examined the relationships between climatic factors and: (1) fine-scale behavioral states at 5-minute intervals during foraging trips, (2) meso-scale trip parameters such as maximum range and trip duration, (3) broad-scale behavioral specialization quantified as IFSF across the breeding period, and (4) chick growth rate, reflecting the cumulative outcomes of foraging behavior. Analyses proceeded from the effects of climate on fine-scale behavioral states to effects on meso-scale trip parameters, broad-scale behavioral specialization, and finally chick growth.

Methods

GPS tracking

During the chick-rearing period from 2011 to 2024, we attached GPS loggers to streaked shearwaters breeding on Awashima Island (38°27′N, 139°14′E), Niigata, Japan (Supplementary Table 1; Supplementary Fig. 2). The loggers used were GiPSy-2 (37 × 16 × 4 mm, 25 g), GiPSy-4 (37 × 19 × 6 mm, 25 g), and Axy-Trek (55 × 25 × 11 mm, 25 g), all manufactured by TechnoSmart (Rome, Italy). These loggers were attached to the birds’ backs using waterproof tape (Tesa tape; Beiersdorf AG, Hamburg, Germany) and adhesive (Henkel Loctite Adhesives Ltd., Hatfield, UK). GiPSy-2, GiPSy-4, and a subset of Axy-Trek loggers were waterproofed with heat-shrink tubing. The sampling interval varied among years and logger types, with data recorded at either 1- or 5-minute intervals. The weight of each attached logger was maintained below 5% of the mean body mass of the study individuals (536.2 ± 90.3 g), a threshold commonly applied in previous studies of streaked shearwaters [37–39] and generally considered to have negligible effects on breeding performance in this species [40, 41]. Nevertheless, because potential behavioral effects of logger attachment cannot be entirely ruled out, our findings should be interpreted with appropriate caution. GPS recording periods varied by year and logger type but generally spanned mid-August to early November.

Climate data

In this study, we focused on cloud cover, rainfall, and wind speed, which have been identified as key factors influencing seabird foraging behavior. Climate data were obtained from the ECMWF ERA5 dataset [42]. Four types of climatic variables were used in the analysis. Cloud cover was defined as the fraction of total cloud cover within a single grid cell. Rainfall was represented by two components: convective precipitation, the accumulated precipitation generated by the convective scheme, and large-scale precipitation, the accumulation generated by the cloud scheme. Wind speed was represented by the magnitude of the vector sum of the eastward and northward wind components (m/s), measured at 10 m above sea level. ERA5 climate data are provided as hourly values on a 0.25° × 0.25° spatial grid.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted using R version 4.2.0 [43]. All Bayesian models were fitted using either the brms package [44] or the rstan package [45]. We used uninformative priors and ran four independent chains. Each chain consisted of 4,000 iterations, with 2,000 warm-up samples. Model convergence was confirmed, as R-hat was less than 1.1 for all models. To assess multicollinearity among the explanatory variables, we computed a correlation matrix for all models (see Supplementary Table 2 for the complete set of values). All pairs of variables showed Pearson correlation coefficients less than 0.7; therefore, multicollinearity was considered negligible [46].

Relationship between climate and fine-scale behavioral states

GPS locations with flight speeds exceeding 90 km/h during foraging trips were excluded from the dataset. Missing locations were interpolated using the crawlWrap function in the momentuHMM package [47]. Incomplete data (e.g., due to battery depletion) were defined as trips for which no return was recorded within 3 km of the colony after departure. Such trips were excluded from subsequent analyses. Finally, GPS data were resampled at 5-minute intervals using the ddfilter function in the SDLfilter package [48] to ensure regular time steps for hidden Markov models (HMMs). The resampled data were divided into foraging trips based on the following criteria: a foraging trip was defined as a continuous movement in which an individual traveled more than 3 km away from the colony for a duration of at least 6 h before returning to the breeding site [40]. Trips in which recording was interrupted due to battery depletion or other issues after the individual had moved more than 3 km from the colony were excluded from the analysis. In addition, because chick-feeding frequency declines after early October (Supplementary Fig. 3), foraging trips occurring in October or later were also excluded. Behavioral states and transition probabilities during foraging trips were estimated using HMMs (Supplementary Fig. 4), implemented with the fitHMM function from the momentuHMM package [47]. The behavioral states of streaked shearwaters were classified into three categories based on step length and turning angle: points with large step lengths and small turning angles were classified as traveling; those with short step lengths and large turning angles as foraging, and those with short step lengths and small turning angles as resting (Supplementary Fig. 5).

To examine the effects of climate on fine-scale behavioral states during foraging trips, we fitted HMMs that included climatic variables as covariates. Climatic variables were extracted for each GPS location from the nearest raster cell in space and the closest available timestamp in time. Additionally, all climatic variables were standardized using variable-specific scaling procedures. Cloud cover and wind speed were standardized using z-score normalization. Meanwhile, convective and large-scale rainfall were log(x + 1)-transformed and subsequently standardized using z-score normalization, because rainfall values were non-negative, contained many zeros, and exhibited a right-skewed distribution. Because analyzing the full dataset across all years would impose a substantial computational burden, we randomly selected a subset comprising 50% of foraging trips from each year for analysis (N = 1116 trips). A covariate was considered to have a statistically significant effect on transition probabilities when the 95% confidence intervals (CI) of its beta-scale parameters did not include 0 [36]. We visualized the covariate effect on the relative use of behavioral states by plotting stationary state probability distributions (i.e., the equilibrium probability of occupying each state) as a function of each covariate [36]. Finally, for each time step, we calculated the posterior probabilities of the behavioral states and assessed the certainty of state estimation using two metrics: the mean posterior probability for each state and the proportion of time steps for which the posterior probability exceeded 95% (Supplementary Table 3) [49, 50].

Relationship between climate and meso-scale trip parameters

We first extracted climate data within the 50% kernel utilization distribution (UD) derived from all oceanic foraging trips combined. The UDs were estimated using kernel density estimation implemented with the kernelUD function in the adehabitatHR package, with the reference bandwidth (href) as the smoothing parameter. We then quantified representative climatic conditions for this area by averaging cloud cover, both rainfall indices, and wind speed across all data points within the area, with wind speed defined as the magnitude of the resultant vector of the eastward and northward wind components (Supplementary Fig. 6). Because this study focuses on overall climatic conditions during foraging trips, we used the mean value of each variable as an integrated summary metric. For temporal scaling, we extracted climate data from the time of departure for each foraging trip to 24 h thereafter (24 hourly values) and averaged them to derive trip-specific climatic metrics. These trip-level metrics were then used as climatic covariates and candidate explanatory variables in subsequent analysis.

For all foraging trips, we calculated maximum range and trip duration as indicators of foraging trip characteristics (N = 2,238 trips; Supplementary Fig. 7). Note that maximum range refers to the straight-line distance between the breeding site and the farthest point reached during each foraging trip. The geosphere package [51] was used to calculate distances between GPS points. When examining the relationships between climatic variables and trip parameters, it is important to note that a positive correlation is expected between maximum range and trip duration. This is because trip duration includes, at a minimum, the time required for a round-trip between the farthest point reached and the breeding site (direct round-trip travel time), which is proportional to the maximum range, assuming a constant flight speed. The actual trip duration also includes additional time components, including deviations from the direct path and time allocated to foraging activities and landing on water. Therefore, trip duration can be regarded as the sum of a distance-dependent component and other time expenditures (Supplementary Fig. 8). Consequently, the influence of climatic factors on trip duration can be conceptualized as operating through two distinct pathways: (1) changes associated with increases in maximum range and (2) changes arising from behaviors independent of maximum range, such as tortuous movement patterns and frequent landings on the sea surface. To account for this structure, we constructed Bayesian models with maximum range and trip duration as response variables, assuming that climatic parameters influence both variables and that maximum range also affects trip duration. Sex was included as a fixed effect, and individual- and year-specific random intercepts were included to account for variation among individuals and across years. For detailed specifications of the models, see Supplementary Method 1. All climatic variables were standardized using the same method described above. We then constructed models for all combinations of explanatory variables and evaluated model fit using the leave-one-out information criterion (LOOIC), computed with the loo package. Relationship between climate and IFSF

In this study, IFSF was calculated as the degree of spatial overlap in foraging sites between foraging trips. First, to identify foraging sites, we estimated behavioral states using an HMM that incorporated climatic variables as covariates and was fitted to all foraging trips (Supplementary Fig. 9). This model was selected because it provided higher average certainty in behavioral state estimation than an HMM without covariates (Supplementary Table 3). Foraging sites were defined as the centroid coordinates of consecutive GPS points, beginning with the transition from the traveling to the foraging state, continuing through successive foraging or resting states, and ending with the transition back to the traveling state. We then calculated the 50% kernel UDs of foraging sites for each foraging trip using fixed kernel density estimation implemented via the adehabitatHR package, with the reference bandwidth (href) used as the smoothing parameter. We used Bhattacharyya’s Affinity (BA), which represents the similarity of UDs, as an indicator of IFSF [54]. BA ranges from 0 to 1, where values closer to 0 indicate lower similarity while values closer to 1 indicate higher similarity. For each individual, we calculated BA values for all pairs of foraging trips (e.g., an individual with four trips yielded six BA values) and IFSF was defined as the mean of these values. To ensure reliability, individuals with fewer than three recorded foraging trips per year were excluded from the analysis.

Finally, to examine the effects of climate on IFSF, we constructed a Bayesian model with IFSF as the response variable, climatic parameters as explanatory variables, and individual ID and year (corresponding to when IFSF was calculated) included as random intercepts (N = 220 individuals). Sex was included as a fixed effect. Climatic variables were calculated following the procedure described here. First, for each climatic variable, we estimated representative area-based values at each time step by averaging climate data within the 50% kernel UD derived from all foraging trips combined. Next, we calculated the mean value of each climatic variable over the period from the start of the first foraging trip to the end of the last trip within an individual’s movement record. Because IFSF is a continuous variable ranging from 0 to 1, we assumed a beta distribution and applied a logit link function in the model. All climatic variables were standardized using the same method as described above. Following the same model-averaging procedure used for trip parameters, we obtained posterior distributions for each coefficient and their PIPs. For this model, the same transformation from the model scale to the response scale was applied to predictors identified as having important effects (see Supplementary Method 3).

Relationship between climate and chick growth rate

During the chick-rearing period from 2011 to 2024, chick body mass was measured at five-day intervals using a spring scale (accuracy: 5 g, Pesola AG, Baar, Switzerland) (Supplementary Fig. 3). Measurements began in mid-to-late August each year (between August 19 and 26), whereas the timing of the final measurement varied among years depending on the end of fieldwork (ranging from September 29 to November 16). Because this analysis focused on chick growth in relation to parental provisioning and the foraging dataset extends only through September, chick body mass data were likewise restricted to measurements obtained through September 30. Chick growth rate was calculated as the slope of a linear regression of body mass over time for each chick [55]. This approach was appropriate because the peak body mass of streaked shearwater chicks occurs in October (Supplementary Fig. 3), allowing fluctuations in mass during the study period to be approximated linearly. To examine the relationship between climatic factors and chick growth rate, we constructed a Bayesian model assuming a normal distribution. In this model, the growth rate of each chick during August and September served as the response variable. The mean values of cloud cover, convective rainfall, large-scale rainfall, and wind speed within the 50% kernel UDs of foraging sites (Supplementary Fig. 6) during the same period were included as explanatory variables. Year was included as a random intercept.

Results

Effects of climate on fine-scale behavioral states

Based on the beta-scale parameter estimates and their 95% CIs (Table 1), cloud cover was associated with changes in several transition probabilities between behavioral states. Specifically, increased cloud cover was associated with a higher transition probability from foraging to traveling (estimate = 0.029, 95% CI = [0.011, 0.046]), whereas the transition probabilities from traveling to foraging (estimate = -0.028, 95% CI = [-0.045, -0.010]) and from resting to foraging were lower (estimate = -0.042, 95% CI = [-0.058, -0.025]) (Fig. 1a, b,d). Increased convective rainfall was associated with higher transition probabilities from traveling to foraging (estimate = 0.040, 95% CI = [0.021, 0.059]), and from resting to foraging (estimate = 0.031, 95% CI = [0.012, 0.049]) (Fig. 1e, h). In contrast, increased large-scale rainfall was associated with lower transition probabilities from foraging to traveling (estimate = -0.032, 95% CI = [-0.052, -0.012]) and from foraging to resting (estimate = -0.085, 95% CI = [-0.108, -0.063]) (Fig. 1j, k). With increasing wind speed, the transition probability from foraging to traveling was higher (estimate = 0.076, 95% CI = [0.059, 0.093]), whereas the transition probabilities from traveling to foraging (estimate = -0.054, 95% CI = [-0.071, -0.037]), from foraging to resting (estimate = -0.129, 95% CI = [-0.146, -0.112]), and from resting to foraging (estimate = -0.183, 95% CI = [-0.200, -0.166]) were lower (Fig. 1m–p). Transition probabilities from traveling to resting and from resting to traveling were extremely low (estimate < 0.001) and were therefore not considered further (Supplementary Table 4). Stationary probability distributions showed little or no clear change in state proportions in response to cloud cover or convective rainfall (Fig. 2a, b). In contrast, increased large-scale rainfall was associated with a slightly higher proportion of time spent in the foraging state (Fig. 2c). Increased wind speed was associated with substantially higher proportions of the traveling state and a lower proportion of the foraging state (Fig. 2d). Finally, the proportion of time steps with posterior state probabilities exceeding 95% in the HMM incorporating climatic covariates was 79.84% (Supplementary Table 3).

Effects of climate on meso-scale trip parameters

According to LOOIC-based model comparisons, the best-fitting model for maximum range included cloud cover and large-scale rainfall as explanatory variables. The second-best model included cloud cover, convective rainfall, and large-scale rainfall, whereas the third-best model included convective rainfall and large-scale rainfall (Supplementary Table 5). For trip duration, the best-fitting model included convective rainfall and wind speed; the second-best model included large-scale rainfall and wind speed, and the third-best model included wind speed (Supplementary Table 5). PIPs derived from LOOIC-based model averaging indicated that large-scale rainfall was consistently supported as an important predictor in the maximum range model, while wind speed was consistently supported as an important predictor in the trip duration model (maximum range: PIP = 0.895; trip duration: PIP = 0.999; Table 2). Examination of the posterior distributions showed that maximum range tended to increase with greater large-scale rainfall. Specifically, when evaluated at the observed median maximum range, a 0.1 mm increase in large-scale rainfall was associated with an estimated increase of 4.53 km. In contrast, when evaluated at the observed median trip duration, trip duration tended to decrease with higher wind speed, with a 1 m/s increase associated with an estimated decrease of 0.37 h (Table 2; Figs. 3 and 4). Cloud cover showed a modest association with maximum range (PIP = 0.600), and convective rainfall showed a modest association with trip duration (PIP = 0.543). However, the 95% CrI of their posterior distributions included zero, indicating substantial uncertainty in both the direction and magnitude of these effects (cloud cover: mean = 0.046, CrI = [-0.002, 0.094]; convective rainfall: mean = -0.013, CrI = [-0.034, 0.010]).

Effects of climate on broad-scale behavioral specialization

According to LOOIC-based model evaluation, the best-fitting model for IFSF included convective rainfall and large-scale rainfall as explanatory variables. The second-best model included cloud cover, convective rainfall, and large-scale rainfall, whereas the third-best model included convective rainfall, large-scale rainfall, and wind speed (Supplementary Table 5). PIPs indicated strong support for convective rainfall and large-scale rainfall as important predictors of IFSF (convective rainfall: PIP = 0.896; large-scale rainfall: PIP = 0.938; Table 2). Examination of the posterior distributions showed that convective rainfall was associated with higher IFSF (mean = 0.105, CrI = [0.023, 0.185]), whereas large-scale rainfall was associated with lower IFSF (mean = -0.129, CrI = [-0.216, -0.031]) (Figs. 3 and 5). Specifically, when evaluated at the observed median IFSF, a 0.1-mm increase in convective rainfall was estimated to increase IFSF by 5.56%, whereas a 0.1-mm increase in large-scale rainfall was estimated to decrease IFSF by 3.81%. Cloud cover and wind speed showed no clear association with IFSF (cloud cover: mean = -0.037, 95% CrI = [-0.100, 0.026]; wind speed: mean = -0.027, 95% CrI = [-0.098, 0.042]).

Effects of climate on chick growth

Results from the model including all candidate climatic parameters as explanatory variables showed that climatic conditions within the 50% kernel UDs of foraging sites were not associated with chick growth rate (Supplementary Table 6).

Discussion

Because convective rainfall occurs over short timescales and in localized areas [29, 31, 56], it is expected to induce immediate and localized responses in the foraging behavior of streaked shearwaters. Increased convective rainfall was associated with higher transition probabilities from traveling to foraging and from resting to foraging states. This pattern may arise because convective rainfall alters the physical properties of the upper ocean, thereby modifying near-surface foraging conditions. During rainfall events, sea surface temperature and salinity can decrease, often over sub-hourly to hourly timescales [57–59], changes that may influence prey distribution and availability. Sardines, a primary prey resource of streaked shearwaters [60], tend to prefer low-salinity and low-temperature waters [61, 62]. Consequently, rainfall-associated changes in surface ocean conditions may alter the vertical distribution of small pelagic fish, including sardines. Although the precise timescale of fish responses remains unclear, any such shifts in prey distribution are likely to occur within the temporal window of rainfall-driven physical changes. These processes could enhance prey availability for streaked shearwaters in areas experiencing convective rainfall, potentially increasing transitions to the foraging state. However, the foraging states estimated by the HMMs are inferred from GPS-derived movement patterns, specifically slow and highly sinuous flight, and do not necessarily correspond to actual foraging behavior such as prey ingestion. Because we were unable to directly quantify foraging success in this study, rainfall-induced changes in these inferred foraging states may instead reflect immediate disruptions of straight-line flight caused by intense rainfall. Indeed, rainfall has been shown to increase the flight costs of birds [12]. A previous study has also reported that the proportion of slow flight increases during rain in magnificent frigatebirds [9]. Together, these findings suggest that seabirds may avoid direct flight during rain.

In contrast, because large-scale rainfall is persistent and spatially extensive [29, 30, 56], it is expected to exert cumulative effects on the foraging behavior of streaked shearwaters. In this study, increases in large-scale rainfall were associated with lower transition probabilities from the foraging state to other states. This suggests that once birds enter the foraging state, they tend to remain there longer under large-scale rainfall conditions. In contrast to convective rainfall, under which the transition probability from other states to the foraging state increased, large-scale rainfall did not promote such active transitions into the foraging state. These results suggest that the effect of large-scale rainfall on fine-scale foraging behavior differs from that of convective rainfall. Rather than facilitating active shifts into foraging, large-scale rainfall may prolong time spent in the foraging state, potentially because birds are less able to forage efficiently under such conditions. Relatedly, rainfall has been reported to reduce foraging efficiency, possibly by decreasing prey detectability and increasing flight costs [9, 10, 14]. In addition, increases in large-scale rainfall were associated with greater maximum range. This pattern suggests that reduced foraging efficiency under large-scale rain may force streaked shearwaters to travel farther in search of prey. Taken together, these patterns suggest that large-scale rainfall may influence foraging behavior through reduced foraging efficiency, leading to prolonged occupancy of the foraging state at fine scales and an expansion of foraging-trip range at meso-scales.

When we examined the effects of rainfall on seabird behavior across broader spatiotemporal scales, seabirds exhibited high fidelity to foraging areas under heavy convective rainfall, whereas foraging site fidelity was lower under heavy large-scale rainfall. These contrasting responses are likely mediated by rainfall-induced changes in prey distribution. Rainfall affects not only the ocean surface but also environments near river mouths via river discharge, which can enhance marine productivity by supplying nutrients to coastal waters [16, 63, 64]. Indeed, streaked shearwaters have been observed foraging in areas influenced by river outflows at sea [65]. IFSF is expected to increase when prey resources are patchily distributed and highly predictable, and to decrease when prey patches are diffuse and prey availability is more spatially uniform [19–21]. Accordingly, differences in the spatial extent over which distinct types of rainfall influence prey distribution may underlie the contrasting effects of rainfall on IFSF. Environmental changes induced by localized convective rainfall are confined to relatively limited areas (e.g., restricted marine regions or specific estuaries), thereby enhancing prey patchiness and predictability and ultimately increasing IFSF. In contrast, large-scale rainfall induces widespread environmental changes that may homogenize prey distributions, reducing reliance on specific foraging sites and resulting in lower IFSF.

Our analyses showed that increases in wind speed were associated with a higher proportion of time spent in the traveling state, a lower transition probability from traveling to foraging, and a higher transition probability from foraging to traveling. These results suggest that strong winds may promote both transitions to and the maintenance of traveling behavior in streaked shearwaters. Indeed, in both Manx shearwaters (Puffinus puffinus) and wandering albatrosses (Diomedea exulans), individuals have been reported to shift from flapping to soaring flight under strong wind conditions [36, 66–68]. Moreover, among foraging trips with similar maximum ranges, an increase in mean wind speed was associated with shorter trip durations. This pattern suggests that stronger winds may enhance flight efficiency, allowing long-distance movements to be completed in shorter periods. Consistent with this interpretation, increases in flight speed with higher wind speeds have also been reported in albatrosses [69, 70]. Taken together, these results suggest that strong winds may increase flight efficiency in streaked shearwaters and thereby promote active traveling behavior. Our findings indicate that wind speed can influence foraging behavior across multiple scales, from fine-scale behavioral states to meso-scale trip parameters.

On the other hand, increased wind speed was associated with a lower transition probability from resting to the foraging state and with a lower stationary probability of the foraging state. In the foraging state, individuals rely on low-speed flight involving flapping rather than soaring; thus, this pattern may indicate that strong winds increase the energetic costs of low-speed flight [71]. Consequently, individuals in the resting state may be less likely to transition to the foraging state until wind conditions weaken. Alternatively, this pattern may reflect reduced foraging efficiency under strong wind conditions. For example, in wandering albatrosses, strong winds reduced feeding rates, likely due to decreased prey visibility caused by sea surface disturbance [10]. Similarly, in little penguins (Eudyptula minor), strong winds deepen the mixed layer, thereby reducing prey encounter rates. Additionally, reduced water column stratification caused by strong winds lowers fish productivity, potentially reducing breeding success rates in kittiwakes [72]. Because streaked shearwaters forage at relatively shallow depths (approximately < 6 m) [73, 74], sea surface disturbance and deepened mixed layers caused by strong winds may reduce prey visibility and availability, resulting in decreased foraging efficiency. In particular, extremely strong winds, such as those associated with typhoons, may have negative effects on seabirds. Indeed [10], showed in albatrosses that landing frequency increases sharply at wind speeds exceeding 18 m/s, and that foraging probability decreases when wind speeds exceed 10 m/s. Therefore, although increasing wind speed may enhance flight efficiency, it may simultaneously increase the energetic costs of low-speed flight and reduce foraging efficiency, particularly under extreme wind conditions. These findings highlight that the effects of climate on seabird behavior are not unidirectional and underscore the need for a multifaceted perspective to fully understand their ecological implications.

Increased cloud cover was associated with higher transition probabilities from foraging to traveling states, as well as with reduced transition probabilities from traveling to foraging and from resting to foraging states. However, because these effects were extremely small, they are likely to be of limited ecological significance. By contrast, in Manx shearwaters, dive depth was greatest during clear weather and shallower under dense cloud cover [8]. Similarly, in Eastern kingbirds (Tyrannus tyranus), a terrestrial insectivorous species, predation rates declined under overcast conditions [75]. These findings suggest that reduced prey visibility under cloud cover may impair prey detection and capture efficiency. Therefore, increases in cloud cover may influence other aspects of foraging behavior that were not examined in this study.

Although climate had various effects on foraging behavior across different spatiotemporal scales, no significant association was observed between climatic conditions and chick growth rate, a proxy for overall foraging success. This may be because climate-induced changes in parental behavior were too subtle to propagate to chick growth, or because parental behavioral adjustments in response to climatic variation enabled stable provisioning to chicks [76]. In addition, the chick growth rate used in this study was derived from only a portion of the breeding period and therefore may not capture effects operating across the entire breeding season. To more accurately evaluate the effects of climate on reproduction, future studies should consider additional metrics such as laying, hatching, and fledging success. Finally, although our analyses focused on climatic conditions at foraging sites, the close spatial proximity between foraging areas and breeding sites means that direct effects of climate on chicks cannot be fully ruled out and should be considered when interpreting our results.

Conclusion

In this study, we examined the effects of climate on the foraging behavior of streaked shearwaters across multiple spatiotemporal scales, including fine-scale behavioral states, meso-scale trip parameters, and broad-scale behavioral specialization. The results revealed that fine-scale behavioral states during foraging trips were significantly associated with cloud cover, convective rainfall, large-scale rainfall, and wind speed. Additionally, meso-scale trip parameters were associated with large-scale rainfall and wind speed, while broad-scale behavioral specialization was associated with convective and large-scale rainfall. These findings demonstrate that climatic factors can influence the foraging behavior of streaked shearwaters differently across spatial and temporal scales. Regarding rainfall in particular, we found that convective rainfall may influence both fine-scale behavioral states and broad-scale IFSF, whereas large-scale rainfall may affect foraging behavior across all three scales. Under ongoing climate warming, convective rainfall is expected to increase in both frequency and intensity [32–34]. By explicitly distinguishing between these two types of rainfall, our analysis provides a more refined understanding of how seabird behavior may respond to future climate change. Specifically, rainfall and wind speed exerted distinct, and in some cases opposing, effects on foraging behavior, depending on their intensity and characteristics. Climate warming has been projected to further increase the frequency and intensity of extreme weather events such as heavy rainfall and storms [77, 78]. By examining climatic effects across multiple spatiotemporal scales, our findings provide a foundation for understanding how seabirds may respond behaviorally to future climate change and offer a framework for evaluating its multifaceted impacts on seabird foraging behavior.
