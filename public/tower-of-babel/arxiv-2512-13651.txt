Schrödinger Symmetry in Spherically-symmetric Static Mini-superspaces with Matter Fields
Taishi Sano, Yuki Yokokura

Abstract
Schrödinger symmetry emerged in a “fluid limit” from a full superspace to several mini-superspace models. We consider two spherically-symmetric static mini-superspace models with matter fields and verify the robustness of this emergent symmetry at the classical level: (i) Maxwell field with cosmological constant and (ii) n n massless scalar fields. We develop a method based on canonical transformations and show that: for model (i), 3D Schrödinger symmetry emerges, and the solution is the (anti-)de Sitter Reissner-Nordström spacetime, and for model (ii), ( 2 + n ) (2+n) D Schrödinger symmetry appears, and the solution is a generalized Janis–Newman–Winicour spacetime and its “interior”, a Kantowski-Sachs type closed universe. In the matter decoupling limit, both cases lead to 2D Schrödinger symmetry in different lapse functions and mini-superspace coordinates, which implies the covariance of Schrödinger symmetry. Finally, we propose a physical interpretation of the symmetry under Hamiltonian constraints H H and explain it with examples: Symmetry generators commuting with H H map a solution to another one, while those non-commuting with H H generate a new theory with the Schrödinger symmetry and the transformed configuration is a solution to the new theory. These support the robustness of the emergence of Schrödinger symmetry and open new possibilities for exploring quantum dynamics of matter and gravity based on the symmetry.

Another aspect is that the existence of such a canonical transformation is merely one sufficient condition for the existence of Schrödinger symmetries. How much broader is its applicability compared to the ED-lift method? What is the relationship between these two? We leave these as future challenges.

. We then integrate this action over time and 2-sphere, perform integration by parts, and drop boundary terms to obtain the reduced Lagrangian
L_{g}
via
S_{g}=\int d\tau L_{g}
:

\displaystyle L_{g}

\displaystyle=N\sqrt{\frac{X^{+}}{X^{-}}}\left(-\epsilon cl_{p}\frac{X^{-}}{4X^{+}}\frac{{\dot{X}^{+}}{\dot{X}^{-}}}{N^{2}}+\frac{cl_{p}}{l_{s}^{2}}-\frac{cl_{p}}{l_{\Lambda}^{2}}(X^{-})^{2}\right).

(28)

Here,
\dot{X}^{+}\equiv\frac{dX^{+}}{d\tau}
,
l_{\Lambda}\equiv\frac{2}{\sqrt{\Lambda}}
, and

c:=\frac{l_{0}l_{s}^{2}}{l_{p}^{3}}

(29)

is the dimensionless constant, the ratio between the UV cutoff scale
l_{p}\equiv\sqrt{\hbar G}
and the IR cutoff scale
l_{0}
.

We introduce the canonical momenta as

\displaystyle P_{\pm}:=\frac{\partial L_{g}}{\partial{\dot{X}^{\pm}}}=-\frac{\epsilon cl_{p}}{4}\sqrt{\frac{X^{-}}{X^{+}}}\frac{{\dot{X}^{\mp}}}{N},

(30)

so that the canonical relation holds:

\displaystyle\{X^{a},P_{b}\}=\delta^{a}_{b},~~~\{X^{a},X^{b}\}=0=\{P_{a},P_{b}\},

(31)

where
a,b=+,-
, and the Poisson bracket is defined as

\{f_{1},f_{2}\}\equiv\frac{\partial f_{1}}{\partial X^{a}}\frac{\partial f_{2}}{\partial P_{a}}-\frac{\partial f_{2}}{\partial X^{a}}\frac{\partial f_{1}}{\partial P_{a}},

(32)

for functions on the phase space:
f_{1}(X,P),~f_{2}(X,P)
.
We then obtain the Hamiltonian:

\displaystyle H_{g}:=

\displaystyle P_{+}\dot{X}^{+}+P_{-}\dot{X}^{-}-L_{g}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle N\sqrt{\frac{X^{+}}{X^{-}}}\left(-\frac{4}{\epsilon cl_{p}}P_{+}P_{-}-\frac{cl_{p}}{l_{s}^{2}}+\frac{cl_{p}}{l_{\Lambda}^{2}}(X^{-})^{2}\right)

\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle N\mathcal{H}_{g}.

(33)

\mathcal{H}_{g}
is the Hamiltonian constraint, which must be set to zero in order to gain the physical solution:
{\cal H}_{g}\approx 0
. Here,
\approx
is the weak equality that holds after calculating Poisson brackets, as usual.

We now fix the gauge as

N(\tau)=\sqrt{\frac{X^{-}}{4X^{+}}}.

(34)

Then, the Hamiltonian (
33
) becomes

\displaystyle H_{g}=

\displaystyle-\frac{2}{\epsilon cl_{p}}P_{+}P_{-}-\frac{cl_{p}}{2l_{s}^{2}}+\frac{cl_{p}}{2l_{\Lambda}^{2}}(X^{-})^{2}.

(35)

This can be expressed as the form of (
21
):

\displaystyle H_{g}=\frac{1}{2}\mathcal{G}^{ab}P_{a}P_{b}+\mathcal{V}_{\Lambda}(X)

(36)

with the quadratic potential

\mathcal{V}_{\Lambda}(X)=-\frac{cl_{p}}{2l_{s}^{2}}+\frac{cl_{p}}{2l_{\Lambda}^{2}}(X^{-})^{2}
and the metric of the 2D mini-superspace (
supermetric
)

\mathcal{G}_{ab}=\begin{pmatrix}0&-\frac{\epsilon cl_{p}}{2}\\
-\frac{\epsilon cl_{p}}{2}&0\end{pmatrix}\quad\text{and}\quad\mathcal{G}^{ab}=\begin{pmatrix}0&-\frac{2}{\epsilon cl_{p}}\\
-\frac{2}{\epsilon cl_{p}}&0\end{pmatrix},

(37)

where
a,b=+,-
, and the mini-superspace is “Lorentzian”,
\rm{det}\mathcal{G}_{ab}<0
, as consistent with the general result
DeWitt:1967yk
. Note here that the lifted space is not conformally flat due to
\Lambda\neq 0
, which does not allow one to apply the ED method and directly find Schrodinger symmetries
universe9120503
.

III.2
2D Schrödinger Symmetry

To find the symmetry of the Hamiltonian (

that rewrites the original Hamiltonian (

According to the explanation in the previous subsection, our first step is to find conserved quantities

\displaystyle=-\frac{2}{\epsilon cl_{p}}P_{+}\left[P_{-}-\frac{\epsilon c^{2}l_{p}^{2}}{4l_{\Lambda}^{2}}\frac{(X^{-})^{2}}{P_{+}}\right]-\frac{cl_{p}}{2l_{s}^{2}}

and notice that the bracket part
[\cdots]

(

Then, the new position coordinates are given through

III.3
(Anti)de-Sitter Schwarzschild metric

Before closing this section,
we here show that the solution of the Hamiltonian (
35
) is uniquely given by the (anti)de Sitter Schwarzschild metric, which provides a physical aspect of this system. In the derivation, we impose the Hamiltonian constraint
H_{g}\approx 0
as late as possible to make its role clear.

First, the canonical equations provide a uniform linear motion in the configuration space
\{\widetilde{X}^{a}\}
:

\displaystyle\dot{\widetilde{P}_{\pm}}

\displaystyle=\{\widetilde{P}_{\pm},H_{g}\}=0\Rightarrow\widetilde{P}_{\pm}(\tau)=\rm{const.}

\displaystyle\dot{\widetilde{X}^{\pm}}

\displaystyle=\{\widetilde{X}^{\pm},H_{g}\}=-\frac{2}{\epsilon cl_{p}}\widetilde{P}_{\mp}\Rightarrow\widetilde{X}^{\pm}(\tau)=-\frac{2}{\epsilon cl_{p}}\widetilde{P}_{\mp}\tau+\widetilde{B}^{\pm}.

(47)

Here and in the remainder of this section, we regard
\widetilde{B}^{\pm}
and
\widetilde{P}_{\pm}
as constants
\beta^{\pm}
and
\alpha_{\pm}
, respectively.

We note from the metric (
26
) that the areal radius of the 2-sphere,
r
, should be identified as

r=\frac{l_{s}}{2}X^{-}.

(48)

Therefore, from the canonical transformation (
41
) and the trajectory (
III.3
), we can have
\frac{2}{l_{s}}r=X^{-}(\tau)=\widetilde{X}^{-}(\tau)=-\frac{2}{\epsilon cl_{p}}\widetilde{P}_{+}\tau+\widetilde{B}^{-}
, leading to

\displaystyle\tau=-\frac{\epsilon\bar{c}}{\widetilde{P}_{+}}\left(r-\frac{l_{s}}{2}\widetilde{B}^{-}\right)\Rightarrow d\tau=-\frac{\epsilon\bar{c}}{\widetilde{P}_{+}}dr,

(49)

where we define

\bar{c}\equiv\frac{cl_{p}}{l_{s}}=\frac{l_{0}l_{s}}{l_{p}^{2}}.

(50)

Similarly, we have for
X^{+}

\displaystyle X^{+}=\frac{\widetilde{P}_{-}}{\widetilde{P}_{+}}X^{-}+\frac{1}{\widetilde{P}_{+}}(\widetilde{B}^{+}\widetilde{P}_{+}-\widetilde{B}^{-}\widetilde{P}_{-})+\frac{\epsilon c^{2}l_{p}^{2}}{12l_{\Lambda}^{2}}\frac{(X^{-})^{3}}{\widetilde{P}_{+}^{2}}

(51)

Combining these and the gauge condition (
34
), we can calculate

\displaystyle-\epsilon N^{2}d\tau^{2}

\displaystyle=-\epsilon\frac{X^{-}}{4X^{+}}\frac{\bar{c}^{2}}{\widetilde{P}_{+}^{2}}dr^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\frac{4\widetilde{P}_{+}\widetilde{P}_{-}}{-\epsilon\bar{c}^{2}}-\frac{2GM}{r}-\frac{\Lambda}{3}r^{2}}dr^{2}

\displaystyle\approx\frac{1}{1-\frac{2GM}{r}-\frac{\Lambda}{3}r^{2}}dr^{2},

(52)

where we have defined

\displaystyle M\equiv\frac{\epsilon l_{s}}{G\bar{c}^{2}}\widetilde{P}_{+}(\widetilde{P}_{+}\widetilde{B}^{+}-\widetilde{P}_{-}\widetilde{B}^{-})=\frac{2}{G\bar{c}^{3}}\widetilde{P}_{+}\widetilde{J}_{+-},

(53)

and applied the Hamiltonian constraint condition in the last step: (from (
43
))

H_{g}\approx 0\Rightarrow\widetilde{P}_{+}\widetilde{P}_{-}\approx-\frac{\epsilon\bar{c}^{2}}{4}.

(54)

Similarly, we can have

\displaystyle\epsilon\frac{4X^{+}}{X^{-}}dt^{2}

\displaystyle=\epsilon\frac{4X^{+}}{X^{-}}\frac{\widetilde{P}_{+}^{2}}{\bar{c}^{2}}\frac{\bar{c}^{2}}{\widetilde{P}_{+}^{2}}dt^{2}

\displaystyle=-\left(\frac{4\widetilde{P}_{+}\widetilde{P}_{-}}{-\epsilon\bar{c}^{2}}-\frac{2GM}{r}-\frac{\Lambda}{3}r^{2}\right)dt_{\infty}^{2}

\displaystyle\approx-\left(1-\frac{2GM}{r}-\frac{\Lambda}{3}r^{2}\right)dt_{\infty}^{2},

(55)

where we have defined the time at
r=\infty
as
dt_{\infty}\equiv\frac{\bar{c}}{\widetilde{P}_{+}}dt
.
Thus, after solving the canonical equations and imposing
H_{g}\approx 0
, the metric (
26
) becomes the (anti)de-Sitter Schwarzschild metric:

\displaystyle ds^{2}\approx

\displaystyle-\left(1-\frac{2GM}{r}-\frac{\Lambda}{3}r^{2}\right)dt_{\infty}^{2}

\displaystyle+\left(1-\frac{2GM}{r}-\frac{\Lambda}{3}r^{2}\right)^{-1}dr^{2}+r^{2}d\Omega^{2}.

(56)

Here, the formula (
53
) plays the role of the energy of the system. Indeed, in the case of
\Lambda=0
and
\epsilon=-1
, it agrees with the Komar mass
Wald:1984rg
(see Appendix
A
). Note that
M
commutes with
H_{g}
(
43
) and thus is gauge invariant.

IV
Maxwell Field

In the previous section, we have succeeded in applying the canonical-transformation method to the vacuum mini-superspace with cosmological constant
\Lambda
and reproducing the known result, the 2D Schrödinger symmetry. In the following sections, we will apply the method to new cases where matter fields exist, increasing the dimension of the mini-superspace. In this section, we will consider Maxwell field
A_{\mu}
with
\Lambda
and show that the 3D Schrödinger symmetry appears.

IV.1
3D Schrödinger symmetry

We follow the same steps as in the previous section to derive the symmetry. We first evaluate the Hamiltonian for the reduced Lagrangian of the action

\displaystyle S_{gE}[g_{\mu\nu},A_{\mu}]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{16\pi G\hbar}\int_{\mathcal{M}}d^{4}x\sqrt{-g}(R-2\Lambda)

\displaystyle~~~~~~~~~~-\frac{1}{4\hbar}\int_{\mathcal{M}}d^{4}x\sqrt{-g}F_{\mu\nu}F^{\mu\nu}

(57)

under the assumption
A_{\mu}(x)dx^{\mu}=A_{0}(\tau)dt
in the spherically-symmetric metric (
26
), where
F_{\mu\nu}\equiv\partial_{\mu}A_{\nu}-\partial_{\nu}A_{\mu}
.
Noting
F_{\mu\nu}F^{\mu\nu}=2g^{tt}g^{\tau\tau}\dot{A}_{0}^{2}
and integrating time and 2-sphere, the Maxwell part
L_{E}
is given through
S_{E}=\int d\tau L_{E}
by

\displaystyle L_{E}=\frac{cl_{p}}{4N}\sqrt{\frac{X^{-}}{X^{+}}}(X^{-})^{2}(\dot{X}^{A})^{2}

(58)

where we define
X^{A}\equiv\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{\hbar}}l_{p}A_{0}=\sqrt{\pi G}A_{0}
.
Introducing the canonical momentum for
X^{A}
as

P_{A}:=\frac{\partial L_{E}}{\partial\dot{X}_{A}}=\frac{cl_{p}}{2N}\sqrt{\frac{X^{-}}{X^{+}}}(X^{-})^{2}\dot{X}^{A},

(59)

we have the Hamiltonian for the Maxwell part:

\displaystyle H_{E}:=\dot{X}^{A}P_{A}-L_{E}=N\sqrt{\frac{X^{+}}{X^{-}}}\frac{P_{A}^{2}}{cl_{p}(X^{-})^{2}}.

(60)

Combing this and the gravity one (
33
), we gain the total Hamiltonian:

\displaystyle H_{gE}

\displaystyle=H_{g}+H_{E}

\displaystyle=N\sqrt{\frac{X^{+}}{X^{-}}}\left[-\frac{4}{\epsilon cl_{p}}P_{+}P_{-}+\frac{P_{A}^{2}}{cl_{p}(X^{-})^{2}}\right.

\displaystyle~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~\left.+\frac{cl_{p}}{l_{\Lambda}^{2}}(X^{-})^{2}-\frac{cl_{p}}{l_{s}^{2}}\right]

\displaystyle\equiv N\mathcal{H}_{gE}.

(61)

Here,
\mathcal{H}_{gE}
is the Hamiltonian constraint, which becomes
\mathcal{H}_{gE}\approx 0
on shell, and the canonical variables
(X^{+},X^{-},X^{A};P_{+},P_{-},P_{A})
satisfy the relation (
31
).

In the chosen
N
(
34
), the Hamiltonian (
IV.1
) becomes

\displaystyle H_{gE}

\displaystyle=-\frac{2}{\epsilon cl_{p}}P_{+}P_{-}+\frac{P_{A}^{2}}{2cl_{p}(X^{-})^{2}}+\frac{cl_{p}}{2l_{\Lambda}^{2}}(X^{-})^{2}-\frac{cl_{p}}{2l_{s}^{2}}

(62)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\mathcal{G}^{ab}_{E}P_{a}P_{b}+\mathcal{V}_{\Lambda}(X),

(63)

where
a,b=+,-,A
, and the supermetric is 3-dimensional:

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}_{Eab}

\displaystyle=cl_{p}\begin{pmatrix}0&-\frac{\epsilon}{2}&0\\
-\frac{\epsilon}{2}&0&0\\
0&0&(X^{-})^{2}\end{pmatrix},

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}_{E}^{ab}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{cl_{p}}\begin{pmatrix}0&-\frac{2}{\epsilon}&0\\
-\frac{2}{\epsilon}&0&0\\
0&0&\frac{1}{(X^{-})^{2}}\end{pmatrix},

(64)

This is again “Lorentzian”,
\det\mathcal{G}_{Eab}<0
, and the lifted space is not conformally flat.

We next perform a canonical transformation to express the Hamiltonian
H_{gE}
(
62
) as the free-particle form. We insert
\frac{1}{2cl_{p}}(-P_{A}^{2}+P_{A}^{2})=0
into
H_{gE}
and write it as

\displaystyle H_{gE}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{2}{\epsilon cl_{p}}P_{+}\left[P_{-}+\frac{\epsilon P_{A}^{2}}{4P_{+}}\left(1-\frac{1}{(X^{-})^{2}}\right)-\frac{\epsilon c^{2}l_{p}^{2}}{4l_{\Lambda}^{2}}\frac{(X^{-})^{2}}{P_{+}}\right]

\displaystyle~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~+\frac{1}{2cl_{p}}P_{A}^{2}-\frac{cl_{p}}{2l_{s}^{2}}.

(65)

Noting
\{H_{gE},H_{gE}\}=\{P_{+},H_{gE}\}=\{P_{A},H_{gE}\}=0
, the bracket
[\cdots]
also commutes with
H_{gE}
. Therefore, we set the new momenta as

\displaystyle\widetilde{P}_{+}

\displaystyle\equiv P_{+},

\displaystyle\widetilde{P}_{-}

\displaystyle\equiv P_{-}+\frac{\epsilon P_{A}^{2}}{4P_{+}}\left(1-\frac{1}{(X^{-})^{2}}\right)-\frac{\epsilon c^{2}l_{p}^{2}}{4l_{\Lambda}^{2}}\frac{(X^{-})^{2}}{P_{+}},

\displaystyle\widetilde{P}_{A}

\displaystyle\equiv P_{A}.

(66)

This can be reproduced by a generating function
F_{gE}(X,\widetilde{P})
, say,

\displaystyle F_{gE}(X,\widetilde{P})

\displaystyle=\int dX^{+}P_{+}(X,\widetilde{P})+\int dX^{-}P_{-}(X,\widetilde{P}),

\displaystyle~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~+\int dX^{A}P_{A}(X,\widetilde{P}),

\displaystyle=X^{+}\widetilde{P}^{+}+X_{-}\widetilde{P}^{-}+X^{A}\widetilde{P}_{A}

\displaystyle~~-\frac{\epsilon\widetilde{P}_{A}^{2}}{4\widetilde{P}_{+}}\left(X^{-}+\frac{1}{X^{-}}\right)+\frac{\epsilon c^{2}l_{p}^{2}}{12l_{\Lambda}^{2}}\frac{(X^{-})^{3}}{\widetilde{P}_{+}}.

(67)

In turn, the new coordinates
\widetilde{X}^{a}
are obtained through
\widetilde{X}^{a}=\frac{\partial F_{gE}}{\partial\widetilde{P}_{a}}
as

\displaystyle\widetilde{X}^{+}

\displaystyle=X^{+}+\frac{\epsilon P_{A}^{2}}{4P_{+}^{2}}\left(X^{-}+\frac{1}{X^{-}}\right)-\frac{\epsilon c^{2}l_{p}^{2}}{12l_{\Lambda}^{2}}\frac{(X^{-})^{3}}{P_{+}^{2}}

\displaystyle\widetilde{X}^{-}

\displaystyle=X^{-}

\displaystyle\widetilde{X}^{A}

\displaystyle=X^{A}-\frac{\epsilon P_{A}}{2P_{+}}\left(X^{-}+\frac{1}{X^{-}}\right).

(68)

Indeed, these new variables
(\widetilde{X}^{a},\widetilde{P}_{a})
satisfy the canonical relations (
42
). We thus reach the desired form:

\displaystyle H_{gE}

\displaystyle=-\frac{2}{\epsilon cl_{p}}\widetilde{P}_{+}\widetilde{P}_{-}+\frac{1}{2cl_{p}}\widetilde{P}_{A}^{2}-\frac{cl_{p}}{2l_{s}^{2}}

(69)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}^{ab}_{E}\widetilde{P}_{a}\widetilde{P}_{b}-\frac{cl_{p}}{2l_{s}^{2}}

with the constant supermetric

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}_{Eab}=cl_{p}\begin{pmatrix}0&-\frac{\epsilon}{2}&0\\
-\frac{\epsilon}{2}&0&0\\
0&0&1\end{pmatrix},~\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}_{E}^{ab}=\frac{1}{cl_{p}}\begin{pmatrix}0&-\frac{2}{\epsilon}&0\\
-\frac{2}{\epsilon}&0&0\\

(70)

Finally, we find the symmetry. According to the strategy of Sec.
II.2
, we set the generators as

\displaystyle\widetilde{P}_{a},~~\widetilde{B}^{a}:=\widetilde{X}^{a}-\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}^{ab}_{E}\widetilde{P}_{b}\tau,

(71a)

\displaystyle\widetilde{J}_{ab}:=\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}_{Eac}\widetilde{X}^{c}\widetilde{P}_{b}-\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}_{Ebc}\widetilde{X}^{c}\widetilde{P}_{a}

\displaystyle~~~~~~=\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}_{Eac}\widetilde{B}^{c}\widetilde{P}_{b}-\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}_{Ebc}\widetilde{B}^{c}\widetilde{P}_{a},

(71b)

\displaystyle\widetilde{Q}_{+}:=\frac{1}{2}\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}^{ab}_{E}\widetilde{P}_{a}\widetilde{P}_{b}=H_{gE}+\frac{cl_{p}}{2l_{s}^{2}}

(71c)

\displaystyle\widetilde{Q}_{0}:=\frac{1}{2}\widetilde{B}^{a}\widetilde{P}_{a},

(71d)

\displaystyle\widetilde{Q}_{-}:=\frac{1}{2}\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}_{Eab}\widetilde{B}^{a}\widetilde{B}^{b},

(71e)

where
(a,b,c)=(+,-,A)
. These constitute the 3D-Schrödinger algebra

\mathfrak{sh}(3)=\left(\mathfrak{sl}(2,\mathbb{R})\oplus\mathfrak{so}(2,1)\right)\oplus_{s}\left(\mathbb{R}^{3}\oplus\mathbb{R}^{3}\right),

(72)

meaning that the system possesses the 3D-Schrödinger symmetry.

This symmetry is a consequence of containing a mixture of electric and gravity components, say,
\widetilde{J}_{A\pm}=\frac{1}{2}cl_{p}(\epsilon\widetilde{B}^{\mp}\widetilde{P}_{A}+\widetilde{B}^{A}\widetilde{P}_{\pm})
. On the other hand, only the gravity sector
(\widetilde{B}^{\pm},\widetilde{P}_{\pm},\widetilde{J}_{+-})
together with

form the 2D-Schrödinger algebra as a sub-algebra of the 3D one, where
\widetilde{B}^{A}
and
\widetilde{P}_{A}
do not contribute. Therefore, taking the matter decoupling limit where
\widetilde{P}_{A}\to 0
, the 3D-Schrödinger algebra reduces to the 2D one.

IV.2
(Anti) de-Sitter Reissner-Nordström metric

As another case with matter fields, we discuss the mini-superspace with

V.1
Y-coordinate in the mini-superspace

We consider a coupled system of gravity and
n
free massless scalar fields
\phi_{I}=\phi_{I}(\tau)
and construct a Hamiltonian suitable for the canonical-transformation method. Before going to the details, it is interesting to see what would happen if we included mass terms and cosmological constant. So, let us start with a more general action:

\displaystyle S_{g\phi}[g_{\mu\nu},\phi_{I}]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{16\pi G\hbar}\int d^{4}x\sqrt{-g}(R-2\Lambda)

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2\hbar}\sum_{I=1}^{n}\int d^{4}x\sqrt{-g}\left(g^{\mu\nu}\partial_{\mu}\phi_{I}\partial_{\nu}\phi_{I}+m_{I}^{2}\phi_{I}^{2}\right).

(79)

First, if we use the ansatz metric (
26
), which we have considered so far, and evaluate the reduced Hamiltonian
H_{g\phi}
, we can see that it is difficult to apply the canonical-transformation method as in the previous cases.
4
4

4

For the metric (
26
) and
\phi_{I}=\phi_{I}(\tau)
, the action of scalar fields reduces to
S_{\phi}=\int d\tau L_{\phi}
with

L_{\phi}=cl_{p}N\sqrt{\frac{X^{+}}{X^{-}}}(X^{-})^{2}\sum_{I}\left(\frac{1}{\epsilon N^{2}}(\dot{X}^{I})^{2}-m_{I}^{2}(X^{I})^{2}\right),

where
X^{I}\equiv\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{\hbar}}l_{p}\phi_{I}
.
Defining the momenta
P_{I}\equiv\frac{\partial L_{\phi}}{\partial\dot{X}^{I}}
, we can calculate the Hamiltonian
H_{\phi}\equiv\sum_{I}P_{I}\dot{X}^{I}-L_{\phi}
and add the gravity part
H_{g}
(
33

H_{g\phi}=H_{g}+H_{\phi}

\displaystyle H_{g\phi}

\displaystyle=N\sqrt{\frac{X^{+}}{X^{-}}}\left[-\frac{4}{\epsilon cl_{p}}P_{+}P_{-}-\frac{cl_{p}}{l_{s}^{2}}+\frac{cl_{p}}{l_{\Lambda}^{2}}(X^{-})^{2}\right.

\displaystyle+\left.\sum_{I}\left(\frac{\epsilon}{4cl_{p}X^{+}X^{-}}P_{I}^{2}+cl_{p}m_{I}^{2}(X^{-})^{2}(X^{I})^{2}\right)\right].

Here, the mass terms lead to a non-trivial interaction between
X^{-}
and
X^{I}

34
),
N=\sqrt{\frac{X^{-}}{4X^{+}}}
, and set

would remain mixing
X^{+}
and
X^{-}

One idea for overcoming this situation is to employ another coordinate system in the mini-superspace. We consider the

-coordinate system related to the

-coordinate system as

\displaystyle Y^{0}:=\frac{1}{4}\log X^{+}X^{-},~Y^{1}:=\frac{1}{4}\log\frac{X^{+}}{X^{-}},~Y^{I}:=\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{\hbar}}l_{p}\phi_{I},

\displaystyle L_{g\phi}

\displaystyle=Ne^{4Y^{0}-2Y^{1}}cl_{p}\left[\frac{1}{\epsilon N^{2}}\left(-(\dot{Y}^{0})^{2}+(\dot{Y}^{1})^{2}+\sum_{I}(\dot{Y}^{I})^{2}\right)\right.

\displaystyle~~~~~~~~~~~~~\left.-\sum_{I}m_{I}^{2}(Y^{I})^{2}-\frac{1}{l_{\Lambda}^{2}}+\frac{1}{l_{s}^{2}}e^{-4Y^{0}+4Y^{1}}\right].

(82)

Defining the momenta
\Pi_{a}\equiv\frac{\partial L_{g\phi}}{\partial\dot{Y}^{a}}
(for

\displaystyle H_{g\phi}

\displaystyle=Ne^{-4Y^{0}+2Y^{1}}\left[\frac{1}{4\epsilon cl_{p}}\left(-\Pi_{0}^{2}+\Pi_{1}^{2}+\sum_{I}\Pi_{I}^{2}\right)\right.

\displaystyle\left.-\frac{cl_{p}}{l_{s}^{2}}e^{4Y^{0}}+cl_{p}e^{8Y^{0}-4Y^{1}}\left(\sum_{I}m_{I}^{2}(Y^{I})^{2}+\frac{1}{l_{\Lambda}^{2}}\right)\right].

The mass terms connect all the coordinates

and

In the following, we assume

(84)

(85)

Then, we reach a simple form of the Hamiltonian:

\displaystyle H_{g\phi}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\epsilon cl_{p}}\left(-\Pi_{0}^{2}+\Pi_{1}^{2}+\sum_{I}\Pi_{I}^{2}\right)-\frac{2cl_{p}}{l_{s}^{2}}e^{4Y^{0}}

V.2
(2+n)
D-Schrödinger symmetry

We now follow our strategy and show the appearance of the
(2+n)
D-Schrödinger symmetry.

We first change the canonical variables.
In the Hamiltonian
H_{g\phi}
(

\Pi_{1}
and
\Pi_{I}
commute with

, and therefore,
the sum of the remaining two terms must commute with

\displaystyle\widetilde{\Pi}_{0}\equiv\sqrt{\Pi_{0}^{2}+4\epsilon\bar{c}^{2}e^{4Y^{0}}},~\widetilde{\Pi}_{1}\equiv\Pi_{1},~\widetilde{\Pi}_{I}\equiv\Pi_{I}.

(87)

A generating function corresponding to these can be identified with

\displaystyle F_{g\phi}(Y,\widetilde{\Pi})

\displaystyle=\int dY^{0}\sqrt{\widetilde{\Pi}_{0}^{2}-4\epsilon\bar{c}^{2}e^{4Y^{0}}}+\int dY^{1}\widetilde{\Pi}_{1}+\sum_{I}\int dY^{I}\widetilde{\Pi}_{I}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{\widetilde{\Pi}_{0}^{2}-4\epsilon\bar{c}^{2}e^{4Y^{0}}}+\frac{\widetilde{\Pi}_{0}}{4}\log\left(\frac{\epsilon\widetilde{\Pi}_{0}-\epsilon\sqrt{\widetilde{\Pi}_{0}^{2}-4\epsilon\bar{c}^{2}e^{4Y^{0}}}}{\widetilde{\Pi}_{0}+\sqrt{\widetilde{\Pi}_{0}^{2}-4\epsilon\bar{c}^{2}e^{4Y^{0}}}}\right)

\displaystyle~~~~~~~~+Y^{1}\widetilde{\Pi}^{1}+\sum_{I}Y_{I}\widetilde{\Pi}^{I}.

(88)

In turn, this generates the new coordinates,

\displaystyle\widetilde{Y}^{0}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4}\log\left(\frac{\epsilon\Pi_{0}-\epsilon\sqrt{\Pi_{0}^{2}-4\epsilon\bar{c}^{2}e^{4Y^{0}}}}{\Pi_{0}+\sqrt{\Pi_{0}^{2}-4\epsilon\bar{c}^{2}e^{4Y^{0}}}}\right),

\displaystyle~~~~~~~~~\widetilde{Y}^{1}=Y^{1},~~\widetilde{Y}^{I}=Y^{I},

(89)

and the canonical relation holds indeed:

\{\widetilde{Y}^{a},\widetilde{\Pi}_{b}\}=\delta^{a}_{b},~\{\widetilde{Y}^{a},\widetilde{Y}^{b}\}=0=\{\widetilde{\Pi}_{a},\widetilde{\Pi}_{b}\}.

(90)

Thus, the Hamiltonian (
86

(\widetilde{Y}^{a},\widetilde{\Pi}_{a})
as

\displaystyle H_{g\phi}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2(-\epsilon)cl_{p}}\left(-\widetilde{\Pi}_{0}^{2}+\widetilde{\Pi}_{1}^{2}+\sum_{I}\widetilde{\Pi}_{I}^{2}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\mathcal{G}^{ab}_{\phi}\widetilde{\Pi}_{a}\widetilde{\Pi}_{b},

(91)

with the (2+n)-dimensional constant supermetric:

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}_{\phi ab}=(-\epsilon)cl_{p}\eta_{ab},~~\mathcal{G}_{\phi}^{ab}=\frac{1}{(-\epsilon)cl_{p}}\eta^{ab},

(92)

where
\eta_{ab}=\mathrm{diag}(-1,1,\cdots.,1)
.
Here, for convenience, we multiplied
-1
in the right hand side so that the Hamiltonian (
V.2
) for
\epsilon=-1
corresponds to the Hamiltonian constraint of a relativistic massless particle,
\mathcal{H}_{\rm{rel}}=-E^{2}+P_{i}^{2}
, where
\widetilde{\Pi}_{0}
plays a role of “energy
E

as discussed in Sec.
I

Now that the Hamiltonian (
V.2
) has exactly same form as the free-particle Hamiltonian (
2
), we can replace
(x^{i},p_{i},g^{ij})
with
(\widetilde{Y}^{a},\widetilde{\Pi}_{a},\mathcal{G}^{ab}_{\phi})
and apply the argument in Sec.

n

(2+n)

\mathrm{Sh}(2+n)=\left(\mathrm{SL}(2,\mathbb{R})\times\mathrm{SO}(1,1+n)\right)\ltimes\left(\mathbb{R}^{2+n}\times\mathbb{R}^{2+n}\right).

(93)

The
(2+n)
D-Schrödinger algebra
\mathfrak{sh}(2+n)
consists of three sectors. The gravity sector has only gravity components

Therefore, in the matter decoupling limit where

The remarkable point here is that this algebra

is expressed in terms of

-coordinates with the lapse choice (

III.2
is written in terms of
X
-coordinates under a different gauge (

VI
Interpretation of the Symmetries under Hamiltonian Constraints

In the previous sections, we have identified Schrödinger symmetry in the mini-superspace models. However, these systems differ from non-relativistic free particle systems due to the presence of Hamiltonian constraints. Indeed, they significantly complicate the interpretation of the symmetry.

We first summarize what we have done so far. For the mini-superspace models with matter fields, we have employed the canonical-transformation method and re-expressed the Hamiltonian constraints
H\approx 0
(for certain choices of
N
) as

\displaystyle H=\frac{1}{2}\mathcal{G}^{ab}P_{a}P_{b}+\lambda\equiv H_{0}+\lambda\approx 0,

(117)

to reach Schrödinger symmetries. Here,
\lambda
is a constant characterizing the dynamics, and
P_{a}
is used to denote
\widetilde{P}_{a}
or
\widetilde{\Pi}_{a}
for simplicity.

Now, before studying the physical meaning of this symmetry, we here list the points we should keep in mind when considering systems with gauge constraints in general. Consider a symmetry generator
G
satisfying the condition (
6
). Then, we have

(I)

When a state satisfying the constraints is transformed by
G
, does the transformed state satisfy the constraints again?

(II)

Does applying a transformation
G
to a quantity
X
after using the constraints yield the same result as using the constraints after transforming
X
by
G
?

(III)

States connected by gauge transformations are physically equivalent. When transformed by
G
, are they still physically equivalent?

Let us add a little more. For (I), the physical states are restricted on a hypersurface in the phase space determined by the constraints, and some transformation
G

may move such states outside the surface, rendering them no longer physical ones. For (II), consider, for example, transforming the ADM mass
M
by
G=\widetilde{B}^{-}
. The definition (
53
) contains
\widetilde{P}_{-}
, and the mass changes by the transformation. However, if we use the constraint (
43
) and express
\widetilde{P}_{-}
in
\widetilde{P}_{+}
first, then the mass does not. For (III), physically equivalent states are represented by a single gauge orbit. If a transformation
G
moves a gauge orbit to another one, that transformation is gauge-independent. In other words, the transformation of a gauge-invariant observable
\mathcal{O}
by such a
G
is gauge-invariant, where
\mathcal{O}
commutes with the gauge constraints.

The points (I)–(III) are satisfied if a symmetry generator
G
commutes with the gauge constraints, as shown below in an example. Actually, the transformation induced by such a generator does not alter the theory; that is, it acts as a symmetry transformation that leaves the Hamiltonian invariant.

On the other hand, for symmetry generators
G
that do not commute with the gauge constraints, the three points require careful attention. In our system (
117
), there exist such symmetry generators:
B^{a}
,
Q_{0}
, and
Q_{-}
. One possible interpretation of transformations by
Q_{0}
and
Q_{-}
involves incorporating the transformation of the lapse function
N

Achour2019
. In contrast, for
B^{a}
, to the best of our knowledge, no such interpretation exists.

We will propose another possible interpretation:
such non-commuting symmetries transform the theory itself, and the transformed configurations are physical solutions of this new theory
. First, we will develop a general argument for this view (Sec.
VI.1
), and then explicitly demonstrate it with examples (Sec.
VI.2
and Sec.
VI.3
).

VI.1
Symmetry generating a new theory

We argue that the symmetries by non-commuting generators
G

H
transform the theory.

We start with reviewing Noether’s theorem
without

H\approx 0

We first check the role of the symmetry condition (

\displaystyle x^{\prime i}=x^{i}+\epsilon\{x^{i},G\},

(118a)

\displaystyle p^{\prime}_{i}=p_{i}+\epsilon\{p_{i},G\},

\displaystyle H^{\prime}(x^{\prime},p^{\prime},t)=H(x,p)+\epsilon\frac{\partial G}{\partial t},

\displaystyle H^{\prime}(x,p)=H(x,p).

(119)

VII
Conclusions and Prospects

Symmetry should be a useful concept in exploring the principles of quantum gravity. The gauge symmetry of spacetime is general covariance, and Hamiltonian constraints play a central role in the dynamics of gravity. Then, what is the physical symmetry in the configuration space of gravity?
Hamiltonian constraints define an infinite-dimensional configuration space, “superspace”. Imposing geometric symmetries (such as homogeneity) on spacetime yields a finite-dimensional configuration space, “mini-superspace”. This reduction procedure can be viewed as a kind of fluid limit for (quantum) gravitational configurations. Generically, when many microscopic degrees of freedom are coarse-grained into a fluid description, symmetries like volume-preserving diffeomorphism invariance often emerge, independent of the details of the constituents. Therefore, it is expected that some common symmetry may emerge in various mini-superspaces, potentially carrying the universal symmetry of quantum gravity. Indeed, 2D Schrödinger symmetry was obtained in the homogeneous isotropic universe model and the spherically symmetric static vacuum model. In this paper, to investigate its robustness, we analyzed spherically symmetric static mini-superspace models coupled with matter fields at a classical level. Due to spherical symmetry and staticity, these become one-dimensional dynamical systems with Hamiltonian constraint
H=N\mathcal{H}\approx 0
, where the radial coordinate is the evolution parameter.

To find the symmetries of such systems, we developed the method utilizing canonical transformations. This method not only applies to a broader class of models than conventional approaches but also possesses the advantage of being systematically treated within the standard framework of canonical theory. First, applying this method to the spherically symmetric static mini-superspace model with Maxwell field and cosmological constant, we demonstrated that 3D Schrödinger symmetry emerges for a certain choice of
N
and the
X
coordinate system in mini-superspace. Here, the dimension of the mini-superspace increases by one for Maxwell field
A_{0}
, and the solution is given by the AdS-RN solution. Next, we analyzed the spherically symmetric static mini-superspace model coupled to
n
massless scalar fields. Using a different
N
and coordinate system
Y
than before, we showed that
(n+2)
D Schrödinger symmetry arises. Solving this model yielded the JNW solution and its “interior” solution in a unified manner. Taking the matter decoupling limit, both become vacuum mini-superspace models, yielding the 2D Schrödinger symmetry for different lapses and mini-superspace coordinate systems. This suggests the covariance of Schrödinger symmetry in the mini-superspace. Thus, the result that Schrödinger symmetry is obtained for mini-superspaces with different dimensions and different types of matter fields, along with its covariance, supports the robustness of the emergence of Schrödinger symmetry in the fluid limit.

We finally explored the physical meaning of Schrödinger symmetry under the Hamiltonian constraint
H\approx 0
. Generators
G
commuting with
H
transform a solution satisfying
H\approx 0
to another one (dynamical symmetry). On the other hand, this interpretation does not hold for generators
G
that do not commute with
H
. Then, we proposed one possible interpretation: such a symmetry transforms the theory itself, and the configuration obtained by the transformation is a solution of the new theory. We explained this with examples. While this interpretation remains open to debate, it provides a first step toward understanding the meaning of symmetry under Hamiltonian constraints.

Let us now consider several future prospects.

First, it is crucial to investigate the robustness of Schrödinger symmetry in a broader context. Our current discussion is limited to cases reducible to the free-particle Hamiltonian without potential via canonical transformations. However, potentials of special forms, such as
V(x)=\frac{k}{x^{2}}
, also preserve Schrödinger symmetry
Boyer:1974qt
;
Nikitin:2017gtu
;
Duval:2024eod
. Therefore, it is important to consider such more general cases and investigate whether other matter fields, such as massive scalar field or Dirac field, possess Schrödinger symmetry, or reconsider whether the scalar-field model in terms of the
X
coordinate in footnote
4
leads to the symmetry. Conversely, it would be interesting to assume such special potentials and examine the spacetime structure derived from them or consider the corresponding modified gravity theories.

A related problem is the exploration for dynamics that eliminates singularities. One approach is to construct an effective theory inspired by DeWitt’s idea
DeWitt:1967yk
. One would examine what behavior trajectories on the mini-superspace should exhibit to avoid singularities, and search for potentials that can realize it while preserving Schrödinger symmetry. The corresponding theory should contain effective dynamics for resolving singularities.

Also, a deeper understanding of the interpretation of symmetries that generate theories is needed. For this, it should be useful to consider an extended phase space formulation where time (and its conjugate, energy) is incorporated as canonical variables
Fahn:2018ahm
;
struckmeier2005hamiltonian
. This could allow the symmetry condition (
6
) to be written in the form of an extended Poisson bracket,
\{G,H\}_{*}=0
, enabling a more systematic investigation of the symmetries in systems with
H=0
, including generators that do not commute with
H
.

Furthermore, there are various possibilities for quantizing the mini-superspace models using Schrödinger symmetry. One approach is to study quantization techniques specific to particular potentials
Essin:2006sic
and to consider how they apply to the models. Another method adopts the idea of group quantization
Isham:1988bx
to quantize the Schrödinger group. Here, the dimension of our mini-superspace models is larger than 2, and it would be more non-trivial to construct the Casimirs and representations than the previous study
Sartini:2021ktb
.

In particular, quantization of the scalar-field mini-superspace model is interesting. The interior region of the horizon in the Schwarzschild metric corresponds—via swapping two coordinates
t
and
r
— to a Kantowski-Sachs-type cosmological spacetime, where no matter field exists, of course. From this corresponding, quantization models of black holes are expected to exhibit a strong link with quantum cosmology models
Modesto:2005zm
. On the other hand, a scalar field can be used as a “clock” in relational formalism to quantize models
Domagala:2010bm
;
Ashtekar:2011ni
. One of our interesting results in the scalar-field mini-superspace model is that, the JNW solution and the Kantowski-Sachs-type spacetime (with the causal-limit fluid) are unified into the single metric (
V.3
), meaning that the latter corresponds to the “interior” of the former. In particular, both possess the Schrödinger symmetry. Therefore, the quantization of this model based on the symmetry and this interior-exterior relationship should provide a promising avenue for connecting quantum black hole models and quantum universe models.

In any quantization method, the non-linearity inherent to gravity dynamics must be carefully considered. One feature of our canonical transformation method is that it reduces the system’s motion to a uniform linear motion on the mini-superspace, where the Hamiltonian constraint
H=0
governs the dispersion relation. Here, the linearity of the solution—and at the quantum level, the principle of superposition—holds true, making it possible to superpose states with different values of charges. However, translating it into geometric language requires us to use the relationship
\widetilde{X}(X,P),\widetilde{P}(X,P)
between the original canonical variables
(X,P)
and the new ones
(\widetilde{X},\widetilde{P})
, from which the non-linearity appears. This point will likely require careful attention during quantization.

Finally, it is crucial to examine the Schrödinger symmetry found in our mini-superspace models from the perspective of a hydrodynamic approximation derived from a full theory of quantum gravity. Ref.
Oriti:2024elx
focuses on the Schrödinger symmetry in the FLRW mini-superspace model and discusses the potential for removing cosmological singularities and realizing accelerated expansion through corrections to the quantum fluid equations that preserve the symmetry. It would be interesting to check whether a similar mechanism works in the spherically-symmetric static mini-superspace models. This test could serve as a crucial benchmark for assessing the universality of the hydrodynamic picture in quantum gravity. On the other hand, Ref.
BenAchour:2023dgj
pointed out the existence of Schrödinger symmetry in the spherically-symmetric static vacuum mini-superspace and suggested that nonlinear terms preserving the symmetry could serve as quantum corrections. However, the form of such corrections may depend on the choice of the lapse function. Indeed, the symmetry they identified corresponds to only one of the two possible choices of the lapse function and mini-superspace coordinate we found. It would be intriguing to consider different lapses and coordinates and investigate whether there are differences in the physical effects arising from the correction terms. Furthermore, we showed that Schrödinger symmetry persists in spherically-symmetric static spacetimes coupled with matter fields. If the emergence of Schrödinger symmetry in such a mini-superspace is rigorously linked to the validity of the hydrodynamic approximation in a full quantum gravity theory incorporating matter fields, the findings of this study would provide significant implications.
