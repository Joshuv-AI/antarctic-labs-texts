Differential Equations for Wilson Loops
in ABJM Theory

Abstract




We derive a system of differential equations which are satisfied by the vevs of BPS Wilson loops and ’t Hooft coupling of ABJM theory.
They are Picard-Fuchs equations of an algebraic curve defined by the derivative of the planar resolvent of the corresponding matrix model.
The weak and strong coupling behaviors can be reproduced by their local solutions around regular singularities.
We also obtain a recursion relation which can be used to determine the planar vevs of BPS Wilson loops in arbitrary representations.

2
Pure Chern-Simons theory

The quantum field theory for the Chern-Simons action with a non-Abelian gauge group was defined in
[
30
]
.
We call this pure Chern-Simons theory.
This is a three-dimensional gauge theory defined on a manifold
M
.
This theory is independent of the metric on
M
, and therefore it is a topological field theory.
Indeed, the Chern-Simons action is written without using the metric.
A metric dependence may arise from the gauge fixing, but it was shown in
[
30
]
that it can be canceled by a counterterm.

The partition function of pure Chern-Simons theory can be calculated exactly by using the topological properties of the theory.
The important observables of this theory is the Wilson loops.
The correlation functions of the Wilson loops can also be calculated, and they give topological invariants of knots and links in
M
.
For example, when the gauge group is
{\rm SU}(N)
and the level is
k
,
the vev of the Wilson loop in the fundamental representation for an unknotted circle in
S^{3}
is given as

\langle W\rangle\ =\ \frac{q^{N/2}-q^{-N/2}}{q^{1/2}-q^{-1/2}},\hskip 28.45274ptq\ :=\ e^{2\pi i/(k+N)}.

(2.1)

It was found in
[
31
]
that the exact expression for the partition function can be rewritten as a finite-dimensional integral.
For the case when the gauge group is
{\rm U}(N)
and the manifold
M
is
S^{3}
, the resulting integral expression is proportional to (
2.2
) below, up to a shift of the level
k
due to quantum corrections.
The integral resembles the partition function of a Hermitian matrix model, but the interaction between the eigenvalues is more complicated.

The matrix model of
[
31
]
was also derived in the context of string theory in
[
32
]
.
In this derivation, pure Chern-Simons theory is realized on D-branes in the topological A-model which wraps
S^{3}
in the deformed conifold
[
33
]
.
By mirror symmetry, this corresponds to D-branes in the topological B-model which wraps
\mathbb{P}^{1}
in the mirror geometry.
According to
[
34
]
, the world-volume theory on the D-branes is a matrix model which turns out to coincide with the one of
[
31
]
.
The planar resolvent of the matrix model was obtained in
[
32
]
which reproduces a geometry related the mirror geometry by the large
N
duality
[
35
]
.
The free energy of the matrix model is determined explicitly by solving the Picard-Fuchs equations for the geometry obtained from the planar resolvent.

It is now known that the matrix model of
[
31
]
can be obtained within the context of quantum field theory by using the supersymmetric localization
[
15
]
.
By this method, the vevs of BPS Wilson loops are also given as finite-dimensional integrals, in addition to the partition function.
Note that there is no shift of the level

in the formulas in
[
15

It is because the theory considered in
[

is actually
{\cal N}=2
version of pure Chern-Simons theory in which quantum corrections to the level cancel among them
[
36
]

2.1
Planar limit

Consider
{\cal N}=2
pure Chern-Simons theory on
S^{3}
with the gauge group
{\rm U}(N)
and the level
k
.
As reviewed above, the partition function is reduced to

Z\ =\ \int du\,\exp\left(\sum_{i=1}^{N}\frac{ik}{4\pi}u_{i}^{2}\right)\prod_{i<j}\sinh^{2}\frac{u_{i}-u_{j}}{2}.

(2.2)

The integral in the right-hand side is a function of
k
defined on the upper-half plane.
The partition function is then defined by its boundary value.

We focus our attention on the planar limit of the theory defined as

N,k\ \to\ \infty,\hskip 28.45274ptt\ :=\ \frac{2\pi iN}{k}\hskip 14.22636pt\mbox{fixed.}

(2.3)

In the following, we call
t
the ’t Hooft coupling, instead of
\lambda
defined above.
In this limit, the saddle point approximation becomes exact.
Then, various observables of the theory is determined by an appropriate solution of the saddle point equations

\frac{k}{2\pi i}u_{i}\ =\ \sum_{j\neq i}\coth\frac{u_{i}-u_{j}}{2}.

(2.4)

Let
\{\bar{u}_{i}\}
be a solution.
The definition (
2.2
) of the integral shows that
\bar{u}_{i}
will naturally distribute around
u_{i}=0
when
k
is large.
This is indeed the relevant saddle point which correctly reproduces known results of pure Chern-Simons theory.
Other solutions of the saddle point equations are discussed in
[
37
]
[
38
]
.

We are interested in the vev of the BPS Wilson loop in the fundamental representation.
In the planar limit, it is given in terms of
\bar{u}_{i}
as

W\ =\ \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}e^{\bar{u}_{i}}.

(2.5)

We always assume that the planar limit is implicitly taken for expressions like this one.

The information of the theory in the planar limit is encoded into the resolvent

v(z)\ :=\ \frac{t}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\frac{z+z_{i}}{z-z_{i}},\hskip 28.45274ptz_{i}\ :=\ e^{\bar{u}_{i}}.

(2.6)

This has a branch cut on a line segment
I
on which
z_{i}
condense.
By the symmetry of the saddle point equations (
2.4
), this satisfies
v(z^{-1})=-v(z)
.
The endpoints of
I
are therefore at
z=a,a^{-1}
for some
a\in\mathbb{C}
.
We assume
|a|\leq 1
.
See Figure
1
.

Figure 1:

The resolvent
v(z)
has a branch cut on the line segment
I
whose endpoints are at
z=a
and
z=a^{-1}
.
The integration contour
C
encircles
I
counter-clockwise.
The value
a
can be complex, so that
I
does not necessarily lie on the real axis in general.

The resolvent is determined explicitly in
[
32
]
.
In our notation, it is given as

v(z)\ =\ 2\log\frac{z+1-\sqrt{(z-a)(z-a^{-1})}}{\sqrt{a}+\sqrt{a^{-1}}}.

(2.7)

Physical observables can be written in terms of
v(z)
.
The Wilson loop (
2.5
) is given as

W\ =\ \frac{1}{2t}\int_{C}\frac{dz}{2\pi i}v(z),

(2.8)

where
C
is a closed contour which encircles the branch cut
I
counter-clockwise, as depicted in Figure
1
.
The ’t Hooft coupling
t
is also given as

t\ =\ \frac{1}{2}\int_{C}\frac{dz}{2\pi i}\frac{v(z)}{z}.

(2.9)

It turns out that the derivative of
v(z)
has a simpler form
[
28
]

zv^{\prime}(z)-1\ =\ -\frac{z-1}{\sqrt{(z-a)(z-a^{-1})}}.

(2.10)

This is a solution of an algebraic equation

\omega^{2}\ =\ \frac{(z-1)^{2}}{(z-a)(z-a^{-1})}.

(2.11)

Note that this equation can be derived directly from the saddle point equations (
2.4
).
See appendix
A
.
The solution
\omega(z)
plays the same role as the resolvent
v(z)
.
For example, the integration by parts in (
2.8
) gives

W\ =\ -\frac{1}{2t}\int_{C}\frac{dz}{2\pi i}\omega(z).

(2.12)

In the following, we also consider generalized quantities defined as

W_{n}\ :=\ \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}e^{n\bar{u}_{i}}.

(2.13)

These can be used to obtain the vev of a BPS Wilson loop in a general representation in the planar limit
[
39
]
.
These also have integral expressions as

W_{n}\ =\ -\frac{1}{2nt}\int_{C}\frac{dz}{2\pi i}z^{n-1}\omega(z).

(2.14)

The integration by parts in (
2.9
) gives

t\ =\ -\frac{1}{4\pi i}\int_{C}\frac{dz}{z}\log z\,\omega(z).

(2.15)

It turns out that the introduction of a variable

x\ :=\ z+z^{-1}

(2.16)

is convenient for the following analysis.
In terms of
x
, the equation (
2.11
) becomes

y^{2}\ =\ \frac{x-2}{x-\alpha},\hskip 28.45274pt\alpha\ :=\ a+a^{-1}.

(2.17)

The algebraic curve defined by this equation will play an important role in the following.
The Wilson loop can be written as

W\ =\ -\frac{1}{4t}\int_{x(C)}\frac{dx}{2\pi i}y(x),

(2.18)

where
x(C)
is the image on the curve of the contour
C
in the
z
-plane.
Rewrting
W_{n}
is not easy.
Instead, we consider

\pi_{n}\ :=\ \int_{x(C)}\frac{dx}{2\pi i}x^{n}y(x).

(2.19)

These are linear combinations of
W_{n}
.
The expression of
W_{n}
in terms of
\pi_{n}
will be given later.

2.2
Picard-Fuchs equations

We observed that the Wilson loop
W
and the ’t Hooft coupling
t
are given as integrals along a closed contour on an algebraic curve.
This suggests that they may satisfy some differential equations, which we call Picard-Fuchs equations.
We will derive these equations for
W
and
t
from the curve (
2.17
), and analyze their solutions.
A different equation for
t
obtained in a string theory context is discussed in
[
32
]
.
In the next subsection, we also determine
W_{n}
for general
n
using the properties of the curve (
2.17
).

2.2.1
Wilson loop

First, we derive a differential equation satisfied by

\pi_{0}\ =\ \int_{x(C)}\frac{dx}{2\pi i}y(x).

(2.20)

This is a function of the parameter
\alpha
defined in (
2.17
).
This is related to the Wilson loop
W
as

\pi_{0}\ =\ -4tW.

(2.21)

Note that
t
is also a function of
\alpha
.

The derivative of
\pi_{0}
with respect to
\alpha
is given by
\partial_{\alpha}y
.
The defining equation (
2.17
)
of the curve gives a relation

2\partial_{\alpha}y\ =\ \frac{y}{x-\alpha}.

(2.22)

The integration of both sides gives

2\partial_{\alpha}\pi_{0}\ =\ \int_{x(C)}\frac{dx}{2\pi i}\frac{y}{x-\alpha}.

(2.23)

In order to obtain a closed equation for
\pi_{0}
, we need to rewrite the right-hand side in term of
\pi_{0}
.

For this purpose, note that the equation (
2.17
) also gives

\displaystyle 2\partial_{x}y

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{y}{x-2}-\frac{y}{x-\alpha},

(2.24)

\displaystyle 2\partial_{x}(xy)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2y+2\frac{y}{x-2}-\alpha\frac{y}{x-\alpha}.

(2.25)

These relations then give

(\alpha-2)\frac{y}{x-\alpha}\ =\ 4\partial_{x}y-2\partial_{x}(xy)+2y.

(2.26)

Since the integration contour
x(C)
is closed, the terms
\partial_{x}y
and
\partial_{x}(xy)
are irrelevant after integration.
Finally, we obtain

(\alpha-2)\partial_{\alpha}\pi_{0}-\pi_{0}\ =\ 0.

(2.27)

2.2.2
’t Hooft coupling

Next, we derive another differential equation satisfied by the ’t Hooft coupling
t
.
Recall the integral expression

t\ =\ -\frac{1}{4\pi i}\int_{C}\frac{dz}{z}\log z\,y(z+z^{-1}).

(2.28)

From the relation (
2.22
), we obtain

2\partial_{\alpha}t\ =\ -\frac{1}{4\pi i}\int_{C}\frac{dz}{z}\log z\,\frac{y}{x-\alpha}.

(2.29)

We will rewrite the integrand
\log z\,y/(x-\alpha)
up to terms of the form
z\partial_{z}(\cdots)
which do not contribute to
\partial_{\alpha}t
.

For this purpose, we start with

2\partial_{x}y\ =\ \frac{y}{x-2}-\frac{y}{x-\alpha}

(2.30)

which we already used above.
Recalling
x=z+z^{-1}
, we notice that

2(z-z^{-1})z\partial_{z}y\ =\ -(\alpha-2)y-(\alpha^{2}-4)\frac{y}{x-\alpha}

(2.31)

holds.
We use this to obtain the desired relation

\displaystyle 2z\partial_{z}\Bigl[(z-z^{-1})\log z\,y\Bigr]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2\log z\,xy-(\alpha-2)\log z\,y+2(z-z^{-1})y

(2.32)

\displaystyle-(\alpha^{2}-4)\log z\,\frac{y}{x-\alpha}.

The integration of both sides gives

-\frac{\alpha^{2}-4}{4\pi i}\int_{C}\frac{dz}{z}\log z\frac{y}{x-\alpha}\ =\ 2\tau_{1}-(\alpha-2)t-\pi_{0},

(2.33)

where we have defined

\tau_{1}\ :=\ -\frac{1}{4\pi i}\int_{C}\frac{dz}{z}\log z\,xy(z+z^{-1}).

(2.34)

Then, we obtain a differential equation

2(\alpha^{2}-4)\partial_{\alpha}t\ =\ 2\tau_{1}-(\alpha-2)t-\pi_{0},

(2.35)

which is, however, not closed for
t
, even when
\pi_{0}
is known.

Interestingly, the unknown function
\tau_{1}
can be obtained from
t
as follows.
The relation (
2.22
) also gives

2\partial_{\alpha}(xy)\ =\ y+\alpha\frac{y}{x-\alpha}.

(2.36)

The integration then gives

2\partial_{\alpha}\tau_{1}\ =\ t+2\alpha\partial_{\alpha}t.

(2.37)

This can be used to eliminate
\tau_{1}
from (
2.35
).
As a result, we obtain

2(\alpha^{2}-4)\partial_{\alpha}^{2}t+(3\alpha-2)\partial_{\alpha}t\ =\ -\partial_{\alpha}\pi_{0}.

(2.38)

This is an inhomogeneous equation with the source term given by
\pi_{0}
.

2.2.3
Solution of the equations

Recall that
\pi_{0}
satisfies

(\alpha-2)\partial_{\alpha}\pi_{0}-\pi_{0}\ =\ 0.

(2.39)

The solution is simply

\pi_{0}\ =\ A(\alpha-2).

(2.40)

The constant
A
will be determined shortly.
Then
t
satisfies

2(\alpha^{2}-4)\partial_{\alpha}^{2}t+(3\alpha-2)\partial_{\alpha}t\ =\ -\partial_{\alpha}\pi_{0}\ =\ -A.

(2.41)

This is first-order in
\partial_{\alpha}t
, so this can be solved easily.
We obtain

t\ =\ -A\log(\alpha+2)+B\arctan\frac{\sqrt{\alpha-2}}{2}+\log C.

(2.42)

We determine these constants as follows.
Suppose that
k
is large, corresponding to small
t
.
The definition (
2.2
) of the partition function implies that the saddle point solution
\bar{u}_{i}
become close to zero.
Then, the branch cut of the resolvent
v(z)
becomes small, which is realized by the limit
a\to 1
or
\alpha\to 2
.
The relation (
2.21
) implies that
\pi_{0}
should behave as

\pi_{0}\ \sim\ -4t

(2.43)

for small
t
.
Therefore, we should have
t\sim-A(\alpha-2)/4
as the leading term.
This information tells us that
B
must vanish and
C
is determined such that
t
vanishies at
\alpha=2
.
As a result, we obtain

t\ =\ -A\log\frac{\alpha+2}{4}.

(2.44)

In order to fix the remaining constant
A
, we need an input from the curve (
2.17
).
Since the branch cut of
y(z+z^{-1})
shrinks to a point in the limit
a\to 1
, the contour integral (
2.28
) for
t
only depends on the local behavior of the curve around
z=1
.
This is given as

y(z)\ \sim\ -1-\frac{1}{2(z-1)^{2}}(\alpha-2)

(2.45)

for
\alpha\sim 2
.
This implies that the leading term of
t
is

t\ \sim\ \frac{1}{4}(\alpha-2),

(2.46)

which then implies
A=-1
.
In summary, we have found

\pi_{0}\ =\ 2-\alpha,\hskip 28.45274ptt\ =\ \log\frac{\alpha+2}{4}.

(2.47)

As a check, let us compare them with the exact result
[
15
]

W\ =\ \frac{1}{N}e^{\pi iN/k}\frac{\sin\pi N/k}{\sin\pi/k}.

(2.48)

Note that this is slightly different from (
2.1
) due to the absent of the shift of the level
k
, the inclusion of the framing factor, and the different normalization of the Wilson loop in
[
15
]
and
[
30
]
.
By eliminating
\alpha
, we obtain

W\ =\ \frac{e^{t}-1}{t}

(2.49)

for the Wilson loop .
This correctly reproduces the planar limit of (
2.48
).

2.3
Recursion relation

Let us reconsider the derivation of the Picard-Fuchs equation for
\pi_{0}
.
We started with

2\partial_{\alpha}y\ =\ \frac{y}{x-\alpha}.

(2.50)

We can parametrize
x
and
y
near
x=\alpha
on the curve (
2.17
) as

x\ \sim\ \alpha+\xi^{2},\hskip 28.45274pty\ \sim\ \frac{\sqrt{\alpha-2}}{\xi}.

(2.51)

In terms of the local coordinate
\xi
, we find that
\partial_{\alpha}y
has a triple pole at
\xi=0
.
It is holomorphic elsewhere.
Similarly,

2\partial_{x}y\ =\ \frac{y}{x-2}-\frac{y}{x-\alpha}

(2.52)

shows that
\partial_{x}y
also has a triple pole at
x=\alpha
.
The parametrization around
x=2
,

x\ \sim\ 2+\eta^{2},\hskip 28.45274pty\ \sim\ \frac{\eta}{\sqrt{2-\alpha}},

(2.53)

implies that
\partial_{x}y
has a simple pole at
\eta=0
.
Otherwise it is holomorphic.

Let
V
be a vector space of meromorphic functions on the curve (
2.17
), each member of which has at most a triple pole at
x=\alpha
and at most a simple pole at
x=2
, and is holomorphic elsewhere.
It is known that the dimension of
V
is finite.
The multiplication by
x
does not increase the orders of poles at
x=2,\alpha
.
This operation may produce poles at
x=\infty
.
After subtracting the poles at infinity, the resulting function is again a member of
V
.
For example,

2x\partial_{x}y\ =\ 2\frac{y}{x-2}-\alpha\frac{y}{x-\alpha}\ \in\ V.

(2.54)

If there are more than
\dim V
functions in
V
, then they are linearly dependent.

Our situation is much simpler.
We find that three functions
\partial_{\alpha}y
,
\partial_{x}y
,
\partial_{x}(xy)-y
form a two-dimensional subspace
V_{2}
of
V
for which

\frac{y}{x-2},\hskip 28.45274pt\frac{y}{x-\alpha}

(2.55)

is a basis.
Therefore, there must exist a non-trivial relation among them, namely

(\alpha-2)\partial_{\alpha}y\ =\ 2\partial_{x}y-\Bigl(\partial_{x}(xy)-y\Bigr),

(2.56)

from which we can derive the Picard-Fuchs equation (
2.27
).

In a similar manner, we can obtain an infinite number of non-trivial relations.
For example, we find that

2\partial_{x}(x^{n}y)\ =\ S_{n}+2^{n}\frac{y}{x-2}-\alpha^{n}\frac{y}{x-\alpha}

(2.57)

holds, where

\displaystyle S_{n}

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle 2nx^{n-1}y+\frac{x^{n}-2^{n}}{x-2}y-\frac{x^{n}-\alpha^{n}}{x-\alpha}y

(2.58)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2nx^{n-1}y+\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}(2^{n-1-k}-\alpha^{n-1-k})x^{k}y.

All of the functions
\partial_{x}(x^{n}y)-S_{n}
therefore belong to
V_{2}
, so any three of them are linearly dependent.
We can use these relations to obtain a recursion relation which determines
\pi_{n}
for general
n
as follows.

The relation (
2.57
) can be organized in the form

\left[\begin{array}[]{c}\partial_{x}(x^{n}y)-S_{n}\\[5.69054pt]
\partial_{x}(x^{n+1}y)-S_{n+1}\end{array}\right]\ =\ \left[\begin{array}[]{cc}2^{n}&\alpha^{n}\\[5.69054pt]
2^{n+1}&\alpha^{n+1}\end{array}\right]\left[\begin{array}[]{c}\frac{y}{x-2}\\[5.69054pt]
\frac{-y}{x-\alpha}\end{array}\right].

(2.59)

Using an identity

(-2\alpha,\alpha+2)\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}2^{n}&\alpha^{n}\\
2^{n+1}&\alpha^{n+1}\end{array}\right]\ =\ (2^{n+2},\alpha^{n+2}),

(2.60)

we find

2\alpha S_{n}-(\alpha+2)S_{n+1}+S_{n+2}\ =\ \partial_{x}(\cdots).

(2.61)

The integration of both sides gives a three-term recursion relation

4\alpha n\,\pi_{n-1}-\Bigl(2(\alpha+2)(n+1)+\alpha-2\Bigr)\pi_{n}+2(n+2)\pi_{n+1}\ =\ 0.

(2.62)

We need to determine
\pi_{1}
for this recursion relation to work.
Note that the above calculation with
n=0
gives

-(\alpha+2)S_{1}+S_{2}\ =\ -2(\alpha+2)y+4xy+(2-\alpha)y\ =\ \partial_{x}(\cdots).

(2.63)

This implies

\pi_{1}\ =\ \frac{1}{4}(3\alpha+2)\pi_{0}\ =\ \frac{1}{4}(3\alpha+2)(2-\alpha).

(2.64)

Then, the recursion relation gives

\pi_{2}\ =\ -\frac{1}{6}\left(4\alpha\pi_{0}-(5\alpha+6)\pi_{1}\right)\ =\ -\frac{1}{8}(\alpha-2)(5\alpha^{2}+4\alpha+4)

(2.65)

and so on.

Remarkably, the generating function of
\pi_{n}
defined as

g(t)\ :=\ \sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\pi_{n}t^{n+1}

(2.66)

can be determined explicitly.
The recursion relation (
2.62
) implies that
g(t)
satisfies

2(2t-1)(\alpha t-1)\partial_{t}g-(\alpha-2)g\ =\ \Bigl(-(3\alpha+2)t+2\Bigr)\pi_{0}+4t\pi_{1}.

(2.67)

The solution satisfying
g(0)=0
is simply

g(t)\ =\ 2-2\sqrt{\frac{1-2t}{1-\alpha t}}.

(2.68)

This function is actually related to
y(x)
.
By the definition of
g(t)
, we have

g(t)\ =\ \int_{x(C)}\frac{dx}{2\pi i}\frac{t}{1-tx}y(x)\ =\ 2+2y(t^{-1}).

(2.69)

This gives the expressions of
W_{n}
in terms of
\pi_{n}
.
Recall that
y(x)
is obtained from the resolvent
v(z)
defined as in (
2.6
) which has the expansion

v(z)\ =\ t+2\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}tW_{n}z^{-n}

(2.70)

given in terms of
W_{n}
.
Then,
y(x)
is given as

2+2y(x)\ =\ -4\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}ntW_{n}z^{-n}.

(2.71)

By comparing this expansion with

g(x^{-1})\ =\ \sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\pi_{n}\left(z+z^{-1}\right)^{n+1},

(2.72)

we obtain the desired relations.
For example, we obtain

W_{1}\ =\ -\frac{1}{4t}\pi_{0},\hskip 28.45274ptW_{2}\ =\ -\frac{1}{8t}\pi_{1},\hskip 28.45274ptW_{3}\ =\ -\frac{1}{12t}(\pi_{2}-\pi_{0}),

(2.73)

and so on.
Note that the integral expressions (
2.14
) and (
2.19
) for
W_{n}
and
\pi_{n}
, respectively, are formally independent of the theory under consideration.
Therefore, the same relation between
W_{n}
and
\pi_{n}
can be also used for ABJM theory.

In fact, the information on
W_{n}
can be obtained more easily and more explicitly for pure Chern-Simons theory.
Recall

zv^{\prime}(z)-1\ =\ -\frac{z-1}{\sqrt{(z-a)(z-a^{-1})}}.

(2.74)

Using the expansion (
2.70
), we find

tW_{n}\ =\ \frac{1}{2n}\left[P_{n}\left(\frac{\alpha}{2}\right)-P_{n-1}\left(\frac{\alpha}{2}\right)\right],

(2.75)

where
P_{n}(x)
are Legendre polynomials.

In the next section, we investigate ABJM theory for which the Picard-Fuchs equations may give us results which is not easily deduced from the resolvent.

3.1
Picard-Fuchs equations

We derive the Picard-Fuchs equations for the Wilson loops and the ’t Hooft coupling.
As for pure Chern-Simons theory, we consider instead

\pi_{n}\ =\int_{x(C)}\frac{dx}{2\pi i}x^{n}y(x).

(3.16)

It turns out that
\pi_{n}
satisfy a system of differential equations in which
\pi_{n}
couple among them.
The equations are independent of the choice of the integration contour
C
.
As a result, the vevs of the bosonic and fermionic Wilson loops are solutions of the same equation.
A geometric description of
W_{n}
which are linear combinations of
\pi_{n}
is given in
[
24
]
.

3.1.1
A basis of functions

From (
3.9
), we obtain

2\partial_{x}y\ =\ \frac{y}{x-2}+\frac{y}{x+2}-\frac{y}{x-\alpha}-\frac{y}{x-\beta}.

(3.17)

Based on the experience in pure Chern-Simons theory, we should consider a four-dimensional subspace
V_{4}
, spanned by

\frac{y}{x-2},\hskip 14.22636pt\frac{y}{x+2},\hskip 14.22636pt\frac{-y}{x-\alpha},\hskip 14.22636pt\frac{-y}{x-\beta},

(3.18)

of the vector space of meromorphic functions on the curve (
3.9
).
We would like to express these basis functions as linear combinations of terms of the form
x^{n}y
, up to total derivatives, like the expression (
2.26
) for pure Chern-Simons theory.

For this purpose, we use

\displaystyle 2\partial_{x}(x^{n}y)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2nx^{n-1}y+\frac{x^{n}-2^{n}}{x-2}y+\frac{x^{n}-(-2)^{n}}{x+2}y-\frac{x^{n}-\alpha^{n}}{x-\alpha}y-\frac{x^{n}-\beta^{n}}{x-\beta}y

(3.19)

\displaystyle+2^{n}\frac{y}{x-2}+(-2)^{n}\frac{y}{x+2}-\alpha^{n}\frac{y}{x-\alpha}-\beta^{n}\frac{y}{x-\beta},

which is derived from (
3.17
).
Note that the first line in the right-hand side is

\displaystyle\frac{x^{n}-2^{n}}{x-2}y+\frac{x^{n}-(-2)^{n}}{x+2}y-\frac{x^{n}-\alpha^{n}}{x-\alpha}y-\frac{x^{n}-\beta^{n}}{x-\beta}y

(3.20)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\left(2^{n-1-k}+(-2)^{n-1-k}-\alpha^{n-1-k}-\beta^{n-1-k}\right)x^{k}y.

We use four of these relations corresponding to
n=0,1,2,3
to rewrite the basis functions (
3.18
).
In order to organize these equations, we introduce vectors

Y\ :=\ \left[\begin{array}[]{c}y\\
xy\\
x^{2}y\\
x^{3}y\end{array}\right],\hskip 28.45274ptB\ :=\ \left[\begin{array}[]{c}\frac{y}{x-2}\\
\frac{y}{x+2}\\
\frac{-y}{x-\alpha}\\
\frac{-y}{x-\beta}\end{array}\right].

(3.21)

We find that they satisfy

2\partial_{x}Y\ =\ TY+UB,

(3.22)

where

T\ :=\ \left[\begin{array}[]{cccc}0&0&0&0\\
2&0&0&0\\
-\alpha-\beta&4&0&0\\
8-\alpha^{2}-\beta^{2}&-\alpha-\beta&6&0\end{array}\right],\hskip 28.45274ptU\ :=\ \left[\begin{array}[]{cccc}1&1&1&1\\
2&-2&\alpha&\beta\\
2^{2}&(-2)^{2}&\alpha^{2}&\beta^{2}\\
2^{3}&(-2)^{3}&\alpha^{3}&\beta^{3}\end{array}\right].

(3.23)

Then, the basis functions are given as

B\ =\ -U^{-1}TY+2U^{-1}\partial_{x}Y.

(3.24)

Note that
U
is invertible if and only if the Vandermonde determinant
\det U
is non-zero.
When
\det U=0
, the dimension of the vector space
V_{4}
decreases, and the curve (
3.9
) degenerates.
We will see that this degeneration corresponds to singularities of the resulting Picard-Fuchs equations.

3.1.2
Wilson loops

We restrict our analysis on the ABJM slice

\alpha\ =\ 2+\gamma,\hskip 28.45274pt\beta\ =\ -2+\gamma.

(3.25)

The derivative of the Wilson loop
W
, or
\pi_{0}
, with respect to
\gamma
is given by

2\partial_{\gamma}y\ =\ \frac{y}{x-\alpha}+\frac{y}{x-\beta}.

(3.26)

The relation (
3.24
) implies that
\pi_{0}
couples to other
\pi_{n}
.
Therefore, we also need to consider
\partial_{\gamma}\pi_{n}
, or
\partial_{\gamma}(x^{n}y)
which are given as

\displaystyle 2\partial_{\gamma}(x^{n}y)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{x^{n}-\alpha^{n}}{x-\alpha}y+\frac{x^{n}-\beta^{n}}{x-\beta}y+\alpha^{n}\frac{y}{x-\alpha}y+\beta^{n}\frac{y}{x-\beta}y.

(3.27)

Four of them corresponding to
n=0,1,2,3
can be organized as

2\partial_{\gamma}Y\ =\ \widetilde{T}Y+\widetilde{U}B,

(3.28)

where

\widetilde{T}\ :=\ \left[\begin{array}[]{cccc}0&0&0&0\\
2&0&0&0\\
\alpha+\beta&2&0&0\\
\alpha^{2}+\beta^{2}&\alpha+\beta&2&0\end{array}\right],\hskip 28.45274pt\widetilde{U}\ :=\ \left[\begin{array}[]{cccc}0&0&-1&-1\\
0&0&-\alpha&-\beta\\
0&0&-\alpha^{2}&-\beta^{2}\\
0&0&-\alpha^{3}&-\beta^{3}\end{array}\right].

(3.29)

Combining this equation with (
3.24
), we obtain

\displaystyle 2\partial_{\gamma}Y

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\widetilde{T}Y-\widetilde{U}U^{-1}TY+2\widetilde{U}U^{-1}\partial_{x}Y.

(3.30)

The integration of both sides gives a system of differential equations for
\pi_{0},\cdots,\pi_{3}
.
The last term in the right-hand side is irrelevant.
We find

\widetilde{T}-\widetilde{U}U^{-1}T\ =\ \frac{2}{\gamma(\gamma-4)(\gamma+4)}\left[\begin{array}[]{cccc}8-\gamma^{2}&8\gamma&-6&0\\
-4\gamma&-16+5\gamma^{2}&-3\gamma&0\\
32-4\gamma^{2}&-16\gamma+3\gamma^{3}&-24&0\\
48\gamma-4\gamma^{3}&-64+20\gamma^{2}&-76\gamma+4\gamma^{3}&0\end{array}\right].

(3.31)

This shows that
\pi_{0},\pi_{1},\pi_{2}
decouple from
\pi_{3}
.

After a slight modification, we obtain

\partial_{\gamma}\Pi\ =\ \frac{1}{\gamma(\gamma-4)(\gamma+4)}{\cal A}\,\Pi,

(3.32)

where

\Pi\ :=\ \left[\begin{array}[]{c}\pi_{0}\\
\pi_{1}\\
\pi_{2}-\gamma\pi_{1}\end{array}\right],\hskip 28.45274pt{\cal A}\ :=\ \left[\begin{array}[]{cccc}8-\gamma^{2}&2\gamma&-6\\
-4\gamma&-16+2\gamma^{2}&-3\gamma\\
32&-8\gamma&-24+3\gamma^{2}\end{array}\right]

(3.33)

This is a Fuchsian system of differential equations with the regular singularities at
\gamma=0,\pm 4,\infty
.
The remaining
\pi_{3}
is then given in terms of
\pi_{0},\pi_{1},\pi_{2}
as

\partial_{\gamma}\pi_{3}\ =\ \frac{1}{\gamma(\gamma-4)(\gamma+4)}\Bigl[(48\gamma-4\gamma^{3})\pi_{0}+(-64+20\gamma^{2})\pi_{1}+(-76\gamma+4\gamma^{3})\pi_{2}\Bigr].

(3.34)

Note that the regular singularities correspond to

\begin{array}[]{lcll}\gamma\ =\ 0&\hskip 14.22636pt\Leftrightarrow&\alpha\ =\ 2,&\beta\ =\ -2,\\[2.84526pt]
\gamma\ =\ 4&\Leftrightarrow&\alpha\ =\ 6,&\beta\ =\ 2,\\[2.84526pt]
\gamma\ =\ -4&\Leftrightarrow&\alpha\ =\ -2,&\beta\ =\ -6,\\[2.84526pt]
\gamma\ =\ \infty&\Leftrightarrow&\alpha\ =\ \infty,&\beta\ =\ \infty.\end{array}

(3.35)

They correspond to various degenerations of the curve (
3.9
).
These points on the ABJM slice are discussed in
[
17
]
[
18
]
.
For example,
\gamma=0
corresponds to the weak coupling limit, while
\gamma=\infty
describes the strong coupling limit or the gravity dual.

The Fuchsian system (
3.32
) can be reduced to a third-order differential equation for
\pi_{0}
only, which is easier to deal with.
As a result, we obtain

\gamma(\gamma-4)(\gamma+4)\partial_{\gamma}^{3}\pi_{0}+2\gamma^{2}\partial_{\gamma}^{2}\pi_{0}-\gamma\partial_{\gamma}\pi_{0}+\pi_{0}\ =\ 0.

(3.36)

The solution of this equation then gives
\pi_{1}
and
\pi_{2}
as

\displaystyle\pi_{1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2}\left[\gamma(\gamma-4)(\gamma+4)\partial_{\gamma}^{2}\pi_{0}-2\gamma\pi_{0}\right],

(3.37)

\displaystyle\pi_{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\gamma\pi_{1}-\frac{1}{6}\left[\gamma^{2}(\gamma-4)(\gamma+4)\partial_{\gamma}^{2}\pi_{0}+\gamma(\gamma-4)(\gamma+4)\partial_{\gamma}\pi_{0}-(\gamma^{2}+8)\pi_{0}\right].

(3.38)

We notice that
\pi_{0}=\gamma
is a solution of (
3.36
).
This solution corresponds to the vev
\cal W
of the fermionic BPS Wilson loop which is proportional to
\alpha+\beta

[
17
]
.
This is actually expected since, as was mentioned in the beginning of this subsection, the vevs of both the bosonic and the fermionic Wilson loops are solutions of the same equation (
3.36
).

Using this simple solution, we can further reduce the equation (
3.36
).
Let
\pi_{0}=\gamma\tilde{\pi}_{0}
.
Then, we find that
\pi:=\partial_{\gamma}\tilde{\pi}_{0}
satisfies

\gamma(\gamma-4)(\gamma+4)\partial_{\gamma}^{2}\pi+(5\gamma^{2}-48)\partial_{\gamma}\pi+3\gamma\pi\ =\ 0.

(3.39)

This is a Heun equation.
For a review, see
[
41
]
for example.
Indeed, by introducing new variables
\gamma=4z
and
\pi=16y
, we obtain the standard form of the Heun equation

\partial_{z}^{2}y+\left[\frac{3}{z}+\frac{1}{z-1}+\frac{1}{z+1}\right]\partial_{z}y+\frac{3z}{z(z-1)(z+1)}y\ =\ 0.

(3.40)

Let
f
and
g
be a basis of the space of solutions of (
3.39
).
Then,
\pi_{0}
is given by their integrals as

\pi_{0}\ =\ A\gamma+B\gamma\int d\gamma\,f(\gamma)+C\gamma\int d\gamma\,g(\gamma)

(3.41)

with appropriate constants
A,B,C
.

3.1.3
’t Hooft coupling

Next, we derive the Picard-Fuchs equation for the ’t Hooft coupling
t
.
As for
\pi_{n}
, we need to introduce auxiliary quantities

\tau_{n}\ :=\ -\frac{1}{4\pi i}\int_{C}\frac{dz}{z}\log z\,x^{n}y(x),\hskip 28.45274pt\tau_{0}\ =\ t,

(3.42)

in order to obtain a closed system of equations.

The derivation is similar to the one for pure Chern-Simons theory.
The counterpart of (
2.32
) for ABJM theory turns out to be

\displaystyle 2z\partial_{z}\left[(z-z^{-1})\log z\,y\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2\log z\,xy-(\alpha+\beta)\log z\,y+2(z-z^{-1})y

(3.43)

\displaystyle-(\alpha^{2}-4)\frac{\log z}{x-\alpha}y-(\beta^{2}-4)\frac{\log z}{x-\beta}y,

4
Discussion

We have derived the Picard-Fuchs equations from the curves (
2.17
) and (
3.9
) for pure Chern-Simons theory and ABJM theory, respectively, which are satisfied by the BPS Wilson loops and the ’t Hooft coupling.
The curves are obtained from the derivative of the resolvents of the theories which have simpler forms compared with the resolvents themselves, and therefore, the analysis becomes simpler.
We have checked that the Picard-Fuchs equations give the known results correctly.
We have also obtained recursion relations for the quantities
\pi_{n}
from which we can obtain the planar vevs of Wilson loops in an arbitrary representation.

Since our analysis does not rely on any relation to (topological) string theory, we expect that it can be extended to more general Chern-Simons-matter theories, irrespective of whether there exist gravity duals for them.
The exploration of various theories may help us to characterize the family of Chern-Simons-matter theories with gravity duals.

An immediate extension of our analysis will be to consider ABJ theory.
This requires us to vary two parameters
\alpha
and
\beta
independently.
This results in Picard-Fuchs equations which are a system of partial differential equations.
In this context, we are interested in the following two issues.
The one is the conjecture in
[
3
]
which claims that the theory does not exist as a unitary theory when the ’t Hooft couplings does not satisfy a certain condition.
It is interesting to see whether and how it appears in the Picard-Fuchs equations.
The other one is the strong coupling limit.
In
[
17
]
, it is observed that the strong coupling behavior of the Wilson loops may depend of how we take the strong coupling limit.
This should be reflected in the local behavior of the Picard-Fuchs equations.

It is known
[
43
]
that a similar phenomenon occurs in Chern-Simons theory with flavors in which the supersymmetry is broken in some parameter region.
This case is much simpler than ABJ theory, and the analysis of this theory will be helpful to understand ABJ theory.
It will be also interesting to reconsider ABJM theory with flavors, which is discussed in
[
44
]
[
45
]
, in light of our analysis.

Another interesting extension will be to consider GT theory.
In this theory, it is known
[
46
]
that the strong coupling behavior of the Wilson loop is qualitatively different from the one in ABJM theory.
It is interesting to clarify how the difference arises.
One more direction of the extension is to change the number of bi-fundamental matters.
Such theories are discussed in
[
28
]
.
Comparing these extensions, we hope to understand how information on various physically interesting limits is
encoded in the analytic properties of the Picard-Fuchs equations.

First, we consider the matrix model for pure Chern-Simons theory to illustrate the derivation.
The saddle point equations (

(A.1)

This equation can be simplified by differentiating both sides by

As a result, we obtain

\omega(x_{+})+\omega(x_{-})\ =\ 0,\hskip 28.45274pt\omega(z)\ :=\ zv^{\prime}(z)-1.

(A.2)

This implies that
\omega(z)
changes its sign when it passes the branch cut

Due to the Wigner law for

\omega(z)\ \sim\ \frac{c}{\sqrt{z-a}}

The asymptotic value of

\omega(z)^{2}\ =\ \frac{z^{2}+Az+B}{(z-a)(z-a^{-1})}.

(A.4)

We have the inversion symmetry
\omega(z^{-1})=\omega(z)

2.4
).
This implies

.
If the numerator of the right-hand side is not a complete square, then
\omega(z)
would have extra branch points

We are interested in the single-cut solution, so we should choose

To fix the sign of

In the limit

Now we conclude that

(A.5)

\displaystyle-2\log(-\tilde{x})\ =\ v_{2}(\tilde{x}_{+})+v_{2}(\tilde{x}_{-})-2v_{1}(\tilde{x}).\hskip 28.45274pt\tilde{x}\in I_{2}

(A.9)

These equations, the Wigner law, the asymptotic value, and the absence of extra branch points imply

\omega(z)^{2}\ =\ \frac{(z^{2}+Az+B)^{2}}{(z-a)(z-a^{-1})(z-b)(z-b^{-1})}.

(A.10)

The inversion symmetry of
\omega(z)
requires

B\ =\ \pm 1,\hskip 28.45274ptA\ =\ AB.

(A.11)

By the same argument as for pure Chern-Simons theory given above,
\omega(z)^{2}
must be holomorphic in the limit
a\to 1
and
b\to-1
simultaneously.
This can be realized only when we choose
A=0
and
B=-1
.
We conclude that
\omega(z)
satisfies

\omega(z)^{2}\ =\ \frac{(z^{2}-1)^{2}}{(z-a)(z-a^{-1})(z-b)(z-b^{-1})}.

(A.12)

This is the curve (
3.9
) for ABJM theory.
