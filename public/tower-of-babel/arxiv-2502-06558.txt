On the FirstFit Algorithm for Online Unit-Interval Coloring
Bob Krekelberg, Alison Hsiang-Hsuan Liu

Abstract
Online interval coloring is a fundamental problem in graph algorithms and scheduling. Although the optimal online algorithm for coloring arbitrary-length intervals is known as 3 3 -competitive (Kierstead and Trotter, 1981), the interest in coloring bounded-length intervals arose recently (Chybowska-Sokół et al., 2024). On the other hand, people are also interested in the FirstFit algorithm’s performance because of its elegance. The competitive ratio of FirstFit on unit-length intervals is exact 2 − 1 ω 2-\frac{1}{\omega} , where ω \omega is the optimal number of colors needed (Epstein and Levy, 2005). However, for arbitrary-length intervals, the competitive ratio is only known to be between 5 5 (Kierstead et al., 2005) and 8 8 (Narayanaswamy and Subhash Babu, 2008). It has been open for a long time what the actual competitive ratio of FirstFit is for arbitrary-length intervals. In this paper, we study the performance of the FirstFit algorithm for the online unit-length intervals coloring problem where the intervals can be either open or closed, which serves a further investigation towards the actual performance of FirstFit. We develop a sophisticated counting method by generalizing the classic neighborhood bound, which limits the color FirstFit can assign an interval by counting the potential intersections. In the generalization, we show that for any interval, there is a critical interval intersecting it that can help reduce the overestimation of the number of intersections, and it further helps bound the color an interval can be assigned. The technical challenge then falls on identifying these critical intervals that guarantee the effectiveness of counting. Using this new mechanism for bounding the color that FirstFit can assign an interval, we provide a tight analysis of 2 ​ ω 2\omega colors when all intervals have integral endpoints and an upper bound of ⌈ 7 3 ​ ω ⌉ − 2 \lceil\frac{7}{3}\omega\rceil-2 colors for the general case, where ω \omega is the optimal number of colors needed for the input set of intervals.

Introduction

This work studies the
online open/closed unit-length interval coloring
problem, which is a variant of
online interval coloring
problem.
The input consists of a sequence of unit-length intervals, where each interval is either an
open
interval
(r,r+1)=\{i\mid r<i<r+1\}
or a
closed
interval
[r,r+1]=\{i\mid r\leq i\leq r+1\}
.
The intervals are released to the algorithm one by one.
Once an interval is released, the online algorithm has to irrevocably assign the interval a color that is not assigned to any previously released interval that overlaps with the released interval.
The aim is to minimize the colors used.
In this work, we study the performance of the
FirstFit
algorithm for coloring the open/closed unit-length intervals.

As a fundamental problem in graph theory and scheduling areas, intensive research is being conducted on the online interval coloring problem.
The optimal online algorithm for the general case where intervals have arbitrary lengths uses at most
3\omega-2
colors where
\omega
is the optimal number of colors
[
1
,
10
,
13
]
.
Recently, interest in coloring bounded-length intervals arose, and better algorithms were found for special families of instances.
For intervals with the length within some fixed range between
1
and
\sigma\geq 1
, a
(\sigma+1)
-competitive algorithm was proposed
[
2
]
, which improved the performance of online algorithms for the cases where
\sigma<2
.

On the other hand, the
FirstFit
algorithm, a classical greedy algorithm, is interesting for its elegance.
The
FirstFit
algorithm is currently the best algorithm for (closed) unit-length intervals, which uses
2\omega-1
colors for any
\omega
-colorable set of unit-length intervals
[
1
,
5
]
.
For arbitrary-length intervals, the competitive ratio is between
5
and
8

[
9
,
11
]
, and it is open for a long time what the actual competitive ratio is.

The motivation to look into the performance of the
FirstFit
algorithm on open/closed unit-length intervals coloring is two-fold.
First, the performance of the
FirstFit
algorithm is not fully understood.
It is known that for unit-length closed intervals, the
FirstFit
algorithm is exactly
(2-\frac{1}{\omega})
-competitive, where
\omega
is the optimal number of colors needed. However, for general instances, the
FirstFit
algorithm is at least
5
-competitive.
The performance of the
FirstFit
algorithm is unclear for the instances between these cases.
As the open/closed unit-length intervals case is the smallest possible instance of the coloring problem on bounded length intervals, this case is key to knowing the actual competitive ratio of the
FirstFit
algorithm.

Moreover, this research helps us understand the impact of “
\varepsilon
-uncertainty”.
It can be considered that closed unit-length intervals are
\varepsilon\approx 0
longer than the open unit-length intervals.
This difference of
\varepsilon
increases the complexity of the problem as an open interval can be a proper subset of a closed interval, which was previously not the case when only closed unit-length intervals were considered.
It was shown that the
FirstFit
algorithm is at least
2
-competitive
[
3
]
, meaning that the open/closed unit-length intervals case is strictly “harder” for the
FirstFit
algorithm than the closed unit-length intervals case.
Finding the competitive ratio of the
FirstFit
algorithm on open/closed unit-length intervals coloring quantifies the power of the uncertainty of
\varepsilon
-difference by understanding how much the adversary can use this uncertainty to trap the
FirstFit
algorithm.

Related work

For the online interval coloring problem, Kierstead and Trotter designed an optimal online algorithm that uses at most
3\omega-2
colors on an
\omega
-colorable interval graph
[

]
.
Independently, Chrobak and Ślusarek found the same upper and lower bounds

Recently, interest in coloring bounded-length intervals arose.
Chybowska-Sokół et al.
[

When
\sigma=\infty

They proposed a

Curbelo

-competitive. Matching the upper bound from Kierstead and Trotter for coloring intervals of general length.

On the other hand, despite the aforementioned results that included sophisticated algorithms, people are also interested in the performance of the naive but easy-to-implement
FirstFit
algorithm.
The exact competitive ratio of the
FirstFit

As early as 1976, Witsenhausen
[
14

[

Later, Ślusarek

The best lower bound known to date is from Kierstead et al.

They prove that for every

For the upper bounds of the competitive ratio of

Later, this result was improved to

The next breakthrough was in 2003, when Pemmaraju et al.

The current best analysis of

The performance of the
FirstFit
algorithm has also been of interest in the special cases.
Chybowska-Sokół et al.
[
2

studied the

The authors first show a trivial bound of

In the case where only unit-length (closed) intervals are considered, Chrobak and Ślusarek

In 2022, instead of only closed intervals as is typically considered, Biró and Curbelo proved that
FirstFit

2\omega

\omega

Furthermore, Curbelo claimed that

The analysis of the
FirstFit
algorithm is generally done via careful counting intersections a critical interval can have.
The elegance of the analysis lies in determining from which interval to count its number of intersecting intervals.
The first analysis by Kierstead and Qin
[
7
,
8
]
identify a large set of mutual-intersecting intervals and then carefully count structural properties of the graph, such as the number of overlapping intervals.
The later results by Pemmaraju et al.
[
12

[
11

first represent the overlapping structure of intervals using “columns”.
The columns are assigned different labels that encrypt the information of the intervals structure and colors assigned.
Then, the analysis carefully counts the occurrences of each label to bound the total number of colors used by the
FirstFit

Also, for unit-length (closed) intervals, the analysis carefully counts for each interval at what positions an interval could intersect it and how many of such intervals there could be
[
1
,
5
]
.

Our contribution

Following the traditional counting approach, we analyze the
FirstFit
algorithm on open/closed unit-length intervals by counting how many intersections an interval can have.

Our main technical contribution is a generalization of the traditional neighborhood bound of the
FirstFit
algorithm (Lemma
4
).
Informally, this lemma shows that the color assigned to interval
I
by
FirstFit
can be bounded via another interval intersecting
I
that is critical in the sense that it is a relatively high-colored interval but with relatively few intersections.
Our results are built on this newly-observed property of
FirstFit
.
In a nutshell, for any interval
I
, by the fact that the input instance consists only of unit-length open or closed intervals, our analysis identifies the critical interval intersecting
I
. It shows that the number of intersections is bounded by sophisticated counting.

For a special case where all intervals have integral endpoints, we prove that
FirstFit
is
2
-competitive, which is tight as it matches the
FirstFit
algorithm lower bound by Biró and Curbelo
[
3
]
.
Formally, for any instance that can be colored by
\omega
colors, we show that

Theorem 1
.

For any unit interval graph with open and closed intervals with integral endpoints, the
FirstFit
algorithm for online coloring uses at most
2\omega
colors.

Then, for unit-length intervals with arbitrary endpoints, we prove the following.

Theorem 2
.

For any unit interval graph with open and closed intervals with arbitrary endpoints, the
FirstFit
algorithm for online coloring uses at most
\lceil\frac{7}{3}\omega\rceil-2
colors.

Preliminaries, key lemmas, and the framework

The input to our online coloring problem is an ordered set of open and closed unit-length intervals
\mathcal{I}=\{I_{1},I_{2},\cdots\}
, where the ordering of the set of intervals is the order in which they are revealed to the online algorithm.
That is,
I_{j}
is revealed earlier than
I_{k}
if
j<k
.
Each interval
I_{j}\in\mathcal{I}
is either an
open
unit-length interval
(r_{j},r_{j}+1)
or a
closed
unit-length interval
[r_{j},r_{j}+1]
for some real number
r
.
In either case, we say
r_{j}
and
r_{j}+1
are the
endpoints
of the interval.
Note that the value of
r_{j}
is unrelated to the order of
I_{j}
in
\mathcal{I}
.
Namely,
r_{j}
can be smaller than, equal to, or larger than
r_{k}
when
j<k
.
We ignore the suffix of intervals when the reveal ordering is not necessarily to be emphasized.

Two intervals are referred to as
twins
when they are identical in terms of the exact location.
That is, for interval
I=[r,r+1]
, all intervals
I^{\prime}=[r,r+1]
are its twins.
Similarly, for interval
I=(r,r+1)
, all intervals
I^{\prime}=(r,r+1)
are its twins.
However, an interval
(r,r+1)
is not a twin of
[r,r+1]
, and vice versa.
When intervals
I
and
I^{\prime}
are twins, we write
I\equiv I^{\prime}
.
We further denote the set of twins of
I
by
\mathcal{T}(I)
.
Note that we define
I\not\in\mathcal{T}(I)
for later usage.

A
proper coloring
of a set of intervals is a function
c:\mathcal{I}\rightarrow\mathbb{N}
, such that
c(I)\neq c(I^{\prime})
if
I\cap I^{\prime}\neq\emptyset
.
The algorithm aims to find a proper coloring of the intervals using the least number of colors.

We use terms from graph algorithms.
The intervals in
\mathcal{I}
form an
interval graph
, where each
I\in\mathcal{I}
is a vertex in the interval graph, and there is an edge between the vertices corresponding to
I
and
I^{\prime}
if and only if
I\cap I^{\prime}\neq\emptyset
.
We denote the
neighborhood
of
I
by
\mathcal{N}(I)
, which is the set of all the intervals interesting with
I
.

As the coloring depends on intersecting intervals as well as the ordering of the input, we define the
\ell
-
neighborhood
of an interval as its neighborhood at the moment when
I_{\ell}
is revealed to the algorithm.
Formally,
\mathcal{N}_{\ell}(I_{j})=\{I_{k}\mid I_{j}\cap I_{k}\neq\emptyset\text{ and }k<\ell\}
.

1

We only use
\mathcal{N}_{\ell}(I_{j})
for
\ell\geq j
.

Naturally,
\mathcal{N}(I_{j})=\mathcal{N}_{|\mathcal{I}|}(I_{j})\geq\mathcal{N}_{\ell}(I_{j})
for any
\ell\in[j,|\mathcal{I}|]
.
Note that
\mathcal{N}_{j}(I_{j})
is the set of all intervals that intersect with
I_{j}
when
I_{j}
is revealed.

Interval graphs are
perfect graphs

[
6
]
, and the chromatic number of the graph is equal to the maximum clique size
\omega
.
This clique corresponds to the maximum set of mutually intersecting intervals for the corresponding intervals.
We denote
\omega
as the number of colors an optimal solution uses for the given input
\mathcal{I}
.

The
FirstFit
algorithm assigns each interval the least available color, that is, using
\text{{FF} }(I_{j})
to denote the color assigned to
I_{j}
by
FirstFit
,

\text{{FF} }(I_{j})\leftarrow\min\{\mathbb{N}\setminus\{\text{{FF} }(I)\mid I\in N_{j}(I_{j})\}\}
.
This greedy strategy provides an upper bound for the color of an interval.
That is, the color of an interval can never be larger than
1
color above the number of intervals it intersects.
More specifically, the color of the interval will be at most
1
greater than the size of the neighborhood when it is revealed.
Formally,

Lemma 3
.

(Neighborhood bound)

For any interval
I_{j}\in\mathcal{I}
, its color assigned by
FirstFit
is at most
1+|N_{j}(I_{j})|
.

Proof.

Assume aiming towards contradiction that
\text{{FF} }(I_{j})\geq 2+|N_{j}(I_{j})|
.
Then, according to the pigeonhole principle, there must exist at least one color
1\leq c<\text{{FF} }(I_{j})
that is not assigned to any of the intervals in
N_{j}(I_{j})
.
Then
\text{{FF} }(I_{j})=\min\{\mathbb{N}\setminus\{\text{{FF} }(I)\mid I\in N_{j}(I_{j})\}\}\leq c
, and it leads to a contradiction.
∎

In this work, we further generalize Lemma
3
by considering a
pivot
interval in the neighborhood of the interval
I
and use the pivot interval to bound the color
\text{{FF} }(I)
more carefully.
Informally, given any interval
I
and a pivot interval
I^{*}
that can be any interval intersecting with
I
,
\text{{FF} }(I)
is no more than
1
plus the number of intervals in
I
’s neighborhood with color greater than the color of
I^{*}
. More formally,

Lemma 4
.

(Pivot bound)

For any interval
I_{j}\in\mathcal{I}
, let
I^{*}\in N(I_{j})
be any interval in
\mathcal{N}(I_{j})
and let
\mathcal{S}^{*}\subset\mathcal{N}(I_{j})
be the set of intervals in the neighborhood of
I_{j}
such that for all
I^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}^{*}
,
\texttt{FF}(I^{\prime})>\texttt{FF}(I^{*})
.
Then,
\texttt{FF}(I_{j})\leq\texttt{FF}(I^{*})+|\mathcal{S}^{*}|+1
.

Proof.

Assume aiming toward contradiction that
\text{{FF} }(I_{j})\geq 2+\text{{FF} }(I^{*})+|\mathcal{S}^{*}|
.
Then, according to the pigeonhole principle, there must exist at least one color
\text{{FF} }(I^{*})<c<\text{{FF} }(I_{j})
that is not assigned to any of the intervals in
\mathcal{S}^{*}
.
Then
\text{{FF} }(I_{j})=\min\{\mathbb{N}\setminus\{\text{{FF} }(I)\mid I\in N_{j}(I_{j})\}\}\leq c
, and it leads to a contradiction.
∎

Note that the Neighborhood bound is a special case of the Pivot bound by selecting an empty set as its pivot. In this extreme case, the pivot is colored by
0
by
FirstFit
, and
\mathcal{S}^{*}\subseteq\mathcal{N}(I)
.

Intuitively, for a smaller upper bound of
\text{{FF} }(I_{j})
, by the Pivot bound, we can choose a pivot
I^{*}
with a small color
\text{{FF} }(I^{*})
or choose a
I^{*}
with a small
|\mathcal{S}^{*}|
.
However, we have a two-fold challenge.
First, it is not trivial to find the best choice between minimizing
\text{{FF} }(I^{*})
or minimizing
|\mathcal{S}^{*}|
.
Second, even when a good pivot
I^{*}
is given, it does not necessarily have non-trivial upper bounds of
\text{{FF} }(I^{*})
and
|\mathcal{S}^{*}|
.
To cope with the difficulties, we construct a mechanism to balance the two choices of minimizing
\text{{FF} }(I^{*})
or
|\mathcal{S}^{*}|
and different manners to bound
\text{{FF} }(I^{*})
and
|\mathcal{S}^{*}|
.

In the analysis of
FirstFit

We first imagine the intervals in

That is, the rocks and bricks can be shifted vertically but not horizontally.
We imagine that the optimal solution orients these rocks and bricks into a wall row by row using the fewest rows (without violating the fixed horizontal position constraints).
More specifically, the intervals on the
i

i

OPT

Since

uses precisely
\omega

.
Furthermore, it naturally follows that no two intervals drawn in the same row will intersect with each other, as otherwise
OPT
admits no proper coloring.

or by

by its

or its

.
More specifically, the color of an interval is the color assigned by
FirstFit

OPT
.

(
b
) and (
c
) that only closed intervals
I
can have non-empty
\mathcal{R}_{\texttt{3}}(I)
.
Further, there is only a single way for a closed interval to intersect three intervals in the same row.
Therefore, all rows in
\mathcal{R}_{\texttt{3}}(I)
contain three sets of intervals, where the intervals in each set are twins.
Formally, given any interval
I=[r,r+1]
where
r
is some real number, we partition the intervals in
\mathcal{N}(I)
into three sets:

•

\mathcal{T}(I)
: Intervals
I^{\prime}\equiv[r,r+1]
.

•

\mathcal{A}(I)
: Intervals that are twins of intervals in
\mathcal{R}_{\texttt{3}}(I)
. More specifically,
\mathcal{A}(I)
consists of the intervals
I^{\prime}\equiv[r-1,r]
,
I^{\prime}\equiv(r,r+1)
, and
I^{\prime}\equiv[r+1,r+2]
.

•

\mathcal{M}(I)
: All other intervals in
\mathcal{N}(I)
.

Framework

Given that the optimal coloring uses
\omega
colors on
\mathcal{I}
.
For any interval
I\in\mathcal{I}
, we bound its color from
FirstFit
by using the Neighborhood bound (Lemma
3
) and the Pivot bound (Lemma
4
).
More specifically, we pick a pivot
I^{*}
with special structural property.
Using the property, we bound the size of the corresponding
\mathcal{S}^{*}
.
On the other hand, we bound the color
\text{{FF} }(I^{*})
using the Neighborhood bound.
Finally, we use the Pivot bound to bound the color of
I
using the pivot
I^{*}
.

Practically, we first identify the “easy cases” where the
\text{{FF} }(I)
is at most
2\omega
as desired.
Then, after peeling off these easy cases, we focus on the tough kernel of the analysis where there is no trivial choice for
I^{*}
such that the sum of
\text{{FF} }(I^{*})
and
|\mathcal{S}^{*}|
is small.
For this tough kernel of analysis, we identify two mediocre choices for
I^{*}
for which the sum of
\text{{FF} }(I^{*})
and
|\mathcal{S}^{*}|
cannot both be large at the same time.

These intervals have relatively small neighborhoods. Formally, by the Neighborhood bound (Lemma
3
) and Lemma
6
(
d
), we can show the following lemma.

Lemma 8
.

For each interval
I\in\mathcal{I}
,
if
r_{\texttt{3}}(I)<r_{\texttt{1}}(I)+2
, then
\text{{FF} }(I)\leq 2\omega
.

Proof.

By the Neighborhood bound (Lemma
3
),
\text{{FF} }(I)\leq 1+|\mathcal{N}_{I}(I)|\leq 1+|\mathcal{N}(I)|
.
By Lemma
6
(
d
),
\text{{FF} }(I)\leq 1+r_{\texttt{1}}(I)+2r_{\texttt{2}}(I)+3r_{\texttt{3}}(I)
.
The given condition
r_{\texttt{3}}(I)<r_{\texttt{1}}(I)+2
implies that
\text{{FF} }(I)\leq 2+2r_{\texttt{1}}(I)+2r_{\texttt{2}}(I)+2r_{\texttt{3}}(I)
.
Since
r_{\texttt{0}}(I)\geq 1
, it follows that
r_{\texttt{1}}(I)+r_{\texttt{2}}(I)+r_{\texttt{3}}(I)\leq\omega-1
.
Thus,
\text{{FF} }(I)\leq 2\omega
.
∎

Since any interval in
\mathcal{R}_{\texttt{3}}(I)
must be in
\mathcal{A}(I)
, an empty
\mathcal{A}(I)
implies
r_{\texttt{3}}(I)=0
.
Hence, we have a useful corollary:

Corollary 9
.

If
\mathcal{A}(I)=\emptyset
, then
\text{{FF} }(I)\leq 2\omega
.

By Lemma
6
(
c
), open intervals cannot have too many intersections per row. More specifically,

Theorem 10
.

For each open interval
I\in\mathcal{I}
,
\text{{FF} }(I)\leq 2\omega-1
.

Proof.

By Lemma
6
(
c
), an open interval
I
intersects at most
2
intervals per row.
Furthermore, since there are
\omega
rows and
I\in\mathcal{R}_{\texttt{0}}(I)
, at most
\omega-1
rows remain to have any intervals intersecting
I
.
It follows that
|\mathcal{N}_{I}(I)|\leq|\mathcal{N}(I)|\leq 2\omega-2
.
By the Neighborhood bound,
\text{{FF} }(I)\leq 2\omega-1
.
∎

Intuitively, because the intervals in
\mathcal{T}(I)
have an identical neighborhood to interval
I
, these intervals are tricky to deal with.
This leaves us to consider the intervals in the sets
\mathcal{A}(I)
and
\mathcal{M}(I)
as potential pivot intervals.
In the ideal scenario, by picking as a pivot the interval with the largest color in the sets
\mathcal{A}(I)
and
\mathcal{M}(I)
,
\mathcal{S}^{*}\subseteq\mathcal{T}(I)
.
Using the Neighborhood bound, we bound the color of this largest interval.
However, it is not always possible to pick such a pivot such that
\mathcal{S}^{*}\subseteq\mathcal{T}(I)
.
In the case that
\mathcal{S}^{*}\not\subseteq\mathcal{T}(I)
, we look more closely at what additional intervals are part of this set.
Finally, we apply the Pivot bound in order to prove an upper bound on the color of interval
I
.

In Sections
3
and
4
, we will focus on the last case where
I
is a closed interval with large
r_{\texttt{3}}(I)
.

3
Warm-up: Regular case where endpoints are integral

As a starter, we consider a special case where all intervals have integral endpoints.
More formally, for any interval
I\in\mathcal{I}
,
I=(i,i+1)
for some integer
i
if
I
is open, and
I=[i,i+1]
for some integer
i
if
I
is closed.
In this section, we show that any
I\in\mathcal{I}
has color
\text{{FF} }(I)\leq 2\omega
, where
\omega
is the number of colors used by the optimal solution.
Note that in the following, we focus on the closed intervals
I\in\mathcal{I}
with
r_{\texttt{3}}(I)\geq r_{\texttt{1}}(I)+2
(by Lemma
10
and Lemma
8
).

For such a set of integral-endpoints intervals, it is an important property that
\mathcal{M}(I)=\emptyset
for any
I\in\mathcal{I}
.
That is, the intervals in
\mathcal{N}(I)
are either in
\mathcal{T}(I)
(twins of
I
) or in
\mathcal{A}(I)
(twins of the intervals in
\mathcal{R}_{\texttt{3}}(I)
).
An example is shown in Figure

.

Pick a pivot
I^{*}

For any closed intervals
I\in\mathcal{I}
with
r_{\texttt{3}}(I)\geq r_{\texttt{1}}(I)+2
, we first identify the interval in
\mathcal{A}(I)
with the highest color by
FirstFit
.

Definition 11
.

(Dominating interval
D^{\texttt{A}}(I)
in
\mathcal{A}(I)
)

Let interval
D^{\texttt{A}}(I)\in\mathcal{A}(I)
be the highest colored interval. That is, there is no
I^{\prime}\in\mathcal{A}(I)
such that
\text{{FF} }(I^{\prime})>\text{{FF} }(D^{\texttt{A}}(I))
.

Note that
D^{\texttt{A}}(I)
must exist as
|\mathcal{A}(I)|\geq r_{\texttt{3}}(I)\geq r_{\texttt{1}}(I)+2\geq 2
.

In this analysis, we want to bound
\text{{FF} }(I)
by using
D^{\texttt{A}}(I)
as a pivot
I^{*}
and applying the Pivot bound (Lemma
4
).

Bound the size of
\mathcal{S}^{*}

Given that we use
D^{\texttt{A}}(I)
as the pivot
I^{*}
, we bound the size of
\mathcal{S}^{*}
.
Recall that
\mathcal{S}^{*}
is the set of intervals in
\mathcal{N}(I)
which are assigned higher color than
\text{{FF} }(I^{*})
.
Since all intervals in
\mathcal{I}
have integral endpoints, any interval in
\mathcal{N}(I)
is either in
\mathcal{A}(I)
or in
\mathcal{T}(I)
.
Therefore, by the definition of
D^{\texttt{A}}(I)
, only intervals in
\mathcal{T}(I)
are possible to contribute to
\mathcal{S}^{*}
.
Moreover, since we focus on
I
that is closed, all intervals in
\mathcal{T}(I)
must be in the rows in
\mathcal{R}_{\texttt{1}}(I)
.
We use the following definition to represent the fraction of rows in
\mathcal{R}_{\texttt{1}}(I)
that cannot contribute to
\mathcal{S}^{*}
.

Definition 12
.

Given any
\hat{I}\in\mathcal{R}_{\texttt{1}}(I)
,
\alpha(\hat{I})\in[0,1]
denotes the fraction of rows
\mathcal{R}\in\mathcal{R}_{\texttt{1}}(I)
where the interval
I^{\prime}
in
\mathcal{R}\cap\mathcal{N}(I)
satisfies
1)

I^{\prime}\notin\mathcal{A}(I)
and
2)

\text{{FF} }(I^{\prime})\leq\text{{FF} }(\hat{I})
.

Note that we make Definition
12
more general for later usage.
For the integral-endpoints case, it is sufficient to set
\hat{I}
as
D^{\texttt{A}}(I)
, and
\alpha(D^{\texttt{A}}(I))\cdot r_{\texttt{1}}(I)
is the number of intervals in
\mathcal{T}(I)
that are not in
\mathcal{S}^{*}
.

Lemma 13
.

If all intervals in
\mathcal{I}
have integral endpoints, by selecting
D^{\texttt{A}}(I)
as a pivot
I^{*}
,
|\mathcal{S}^{*}|=(1-\alpha(D^{\texttt{A}}(I)))\cdot r_{\texttt{1}}(I)
.

Proof.

First, by the definition of
\mathcal{R}_{\texttt{0}}(I)
,
I
does not intersect any interval in
\mathcal{R}_{\texttt{0}}(I)
. Thus, no interval in
\mathcal{R}_{\texttt{0}}(I)
can be in
\mathcal{S}^{*}
.
Next, as all intervals have integral endpoints, all intervals in
\mathcal{R}_{\texttt{2}}(I)
,
\mathcal{R}_{\texttt{3}}(I)
, or
\mathcal{R}_{\texttt{1}}(I)\setminus\mathcal{T}(I)
are in the sets
\mathcal{A}(I)
.
Thus, all these intervals are assigned a color below
\text{{FF} }(D^{\texttt{A}}(I))
and cannot contribute to
\mathcal{S}^{*}
.
The remaining intervals are those in
\mathcal{T}(I)
.
By definition,
\alpha(D^{\texttt{A}}(I))\cdot r_{\texttt{1}}(I)
of these intervals have a color at most
\text{{FF} }(D^{\texttt{A}}(I))
.
Hence, the
(1-\alpha(D^{\texttt{A}}(I)))\cdot r_{\texttt{1}}(I)
intervals are the only intervals intersecting
I
that have a color greater than
\text{{FF} }(D^{\texttt{A}}(I))
.
That is,
|\mathcal{S}^{*}|=(1-\alpha(D^{\texttt{A}}(I)))\cdot r_{\texttt{1}}(I)
.
∎

Bound the color
\text{{FF} }(I^{*})

We bound the color of

using the Neighborhood bound (Lemma
3
).
In general, apart from intervals in
\mathcal{T}(I)
, the number of intervals any interval in

can intersect is shown in the following observation (also see Figure

):

Observation 14
.

Any interval in the set
\mathcal{A}(I)
intersects

(a)

,

(b)

at most
2
intervals which are not twins with

per row in
\mathcal{R}_{\texttt{1}}(I)
,

(c)

at most
2
intervals per row in
\mathcal{R}_{\texttt{2}}(I)
, and

(d)

exactly
1
interval per row in
\mathcal{R}_{\texttt{3}}(I)
.

According to the definition of

, Observation

.

Lemma 15
.

The color of interval
D^{\texttt{A}}(I)
,
\text{{FF} }(D^{\texttt{A}}(I))\leq 2\omega+\alpha(D^{\texttt{A}}(I))\cdot r_{\texttt{1}}(I)-r_{\texttt{3}}(I)
.

Proof.

By the Neighborhood bound,
\text{{FF} }(D^{\texttt{A}}(I))\leq 1+|N(D^{\texttt{A}}(I))|
.
By Observation
14
, there are at most
2r_{\texttt{0}}(I)+2r_{\texttt{1}}(I)+2r_{\texttt{2}}(I)+r_{\texttt{3}}(I)
intervals in
\mathcal{A}(I)
that are in
\mathcal{N}(D^{\texttt{A}}(I))
.
Together with the
\alpha(D^{\texttt{A}}(I))\cdot r_{\texttt{1}}(I)
intervals that “occupied” the smaller colors from the choice of
D^{\texttt{A}}(I)
at the moment when
D^{\texttt{A}}(I)
arrives,
|\mathcal{N}_{D^{\texttt{A}}(I)}(D^{\texttt{A}}(I))|\leq 2r_{\texttt{0}}(I)+2r_{\texttt{1}}(I)+\alpha(D^{\texttt{A}}(I))\cdot r_{\texttt{1}}(I)+2r_{\texttt{2}}(I)+r_{\texttt{3}}(I)
.
Observe that by this bound, we count the row that contains interval
D^{\texttt{A}}(I)
itself.
Since
D^{\texttt{A}}(I)
cannot intersect any interval on this row, we should subtract at least
1
from this bound.
Thus,

\displaystyle\text{{FF} }(D^{\texttt{A}}(I))

\displaystyle\leq 1+2r_{\texttt{0}}(I)+2r_{\texttt{1}}(I)+\alpha(D^{\texttt{A}}(I))\cdot r_{\texttt{1}}(I)+2r_{\texttt{2}}(I)+r_{\texttt{3}}(I)-1

\displaystyle=2\omega+\alpha(D^{\texttt{A}}(I))\cdot r_{\texttt{1}}(I)-r_{\texttt{3}}(I)

∎

Note that due to the abstraction of

Corollary 16
.

Given instance
\mathcal{I}
with arbitrary endpoints, the color of interval

is at most
2\omega+\alpha(D^{\texttt{A}}(I))\cdot r_{\texttt{1}}(I)-r_{\texttt{3}}(I)
.

Proof of Theorem
1

Now, we are ready to prove Theorem

that for any

with open or closed unit-length intervals that have integral endpoints,
\text{{FF} }(I)\leq 2\omega
for all
I\in\mathcal{I}
, where
\omega
is the optimal color needed for properly coloring
\mathcal{I}
.

Proof.

By Lemma
8
, we assume that
r_{\texttt{1}}(I)\leq r_{\texttt{3}}(I)-2
.
Moreover, by Lemma
10
, we focus on closed
I
.
According to Corollary
9
, we assume that there exists a dominating interval
D^{\texttt{A}}(I)
in
\mathcal{A}(I)
that has the highest color by

.
By Lemma

,
\text{{FF} }(D^{\texttt{A}}(I))\leq 2\omega+\alpha(D^{\texttt{A}}(I))\cdot r_{\texttt{1}}(I)-r_{\texttt{3}}(I)
.

We now use the Pivot bound to bound the color of interval

from above, using

as the role of

.
By Lemma

,
|\mathcal{S}^{*}|=(1-\alpha(D^{\texttt{A}}(I)))\cdot r_{\texttt{1}}(I)
.

It follows that

\displaystyle\text{{FF} }(I)

\displaystyle\leq\text{{FF} }(I^{*})+|\mathcal{S}^{*}|+1

\displaystyle=\text{{FF} }(D^{\texttt{A}}(I))+(1-\alpha(D^{\texttt{A}}(I)))\cdot r_{\texttt{1}}(I)+1

\displaystyle\leq 2\omega+\alpha(D^{\texttt{A}}(I))\cdot r_{\texttt{1}}(I)-r_{\texttt{3}}(I)+(1-\alpha(D^{\texttt{A}}(I)))\cdot r_{\texttt{1}}(I)+1

\displaystyle\leq 2\omega+r_{\texttt{1}}(I)-r_{\texttt{3}}(I)+1

∎

4
General case: Intervals with arbitrary endpoints

In this section, we consider the instance
\mathcal{I}
, where intervals have arbitrary endpoints.
More specifically, by Lemma
8
and Lemma
10
, we focus on
I\in\mathcal{I}
that are closed and
r_{\texttt{3}}(I)\geq r_{\texttt{1}}(I)+2
.
Recall from Section
3
that we partition
\mathcal{N}(I)
into
\mathcal{T}(I)
,
\mathcal{A}(I)
, and
\mathcal{M}(I)
, that is, the set of twins, the set of intervals that are twins of intervals in
\mathcal{R}_{\texttt{3}}(I)
, and the rest of intervals with endpoints “misaligned” with the endpoints of
I
.

Applying the Pivot bound to bound the color
\text{{FF} }(I)
in this general case is more challenging than the integral-endpoints case.
First, unlike in the integral-endpoints case where all intervals that intersect
I
must be either in
\mathcal{T}(I)
or in
\mathcal{A}(I)
, the candidates in
\mathcal{S}^{*}
can also be in
\mathcal{M}(I)
.
These intervals in
\mathcal{M}(I)
can cross-interact with other intervals, making it difficult to bound the size of
\mathcal{S}^{*}
.
Second, selecting a good pivot interval
I^{*}
in the general case is more challenging.
We first show that using the same strategy as we used for the integral-endpoints case only guarantees a bound of
\text{{FF} }(I)\leq 3\omega
.

A naive attempt

As in the integral-endpoints case, let
I^{*}
be
D^{\texttt{A}}(I)
, the interval with the highest color in
\mathcal{A}(I)
.
In the general case, any interval in the sets
\mathcal{R}_{\texttt{1}}(I)
and
\mathcal{R}_{\texttt{2}}(I)
can be in
\mathcal{M}(I)
and can therefore be a candidate of
\mathcal{S}^{*}
.
If we now apply the same strategy as in the integral-endpoints setting, it is no longer the case that the set
\mathcal{S}^{*}
is a subset of
\mathcal{R}_{\texttt{1}}(I)
.
Then, in each row
\mathcal{R}\in\mathcal{R}_{\texttt{2}}(I)
, it can be the case that two intervals (of which one intersects
I
and the other does not) are in the neighborhood of the pivot
I^{*}
.
Furthermore, the remaining interval on row
\mathcal{R}
that also intersects with
I
can be considered for the set
\mathcal{S}^{*}
, resulting in us counting
3
intervals per row in
\mathcal{R}_{\texttt{2}}(I)
.
Thus, the bound tends to
3\omega
when
r_{\texttt{1}}(I)
and
r_{\texttt{3}}(I)
are both small.
To work around this, we need to closely look at the color of the intervals in
\mathcal{R}_{\texttt{2}}(I)
.

4.1
Roadmap

Primarily, we pick a pivot interval
I^{*}
and bound its color using the Neighborhood bound.
The choice of pivot should provide a plausible way to bound the size of the corresponding
\mathcal{S}^{*}
.

In the general case, we have another possible candidate of
I^{*}
.
Symmetric to the dominating aligned interval
D^{\texttt{A}}(I)
, we define interval
D^{\texttt{M}}(I)
as the dominating interval in rows in
\mathcal{R}_{\texttt{2}}(I)
that is misaligned with
I
and assigned the highest color by
FirstFit
.
Formally,

Definition 17
.

(Dominating interval
D^{\texttt{M}}(I)
in
\mathcal{R}_{\texttt{2}}(I)\cap\mathcal{M}(I)
)

Let interval
D^{\texttt{M}}(I)
be the interval in
\mathcal{R}_{\texttt{2}}(I)\cap\mathcal{M}(I)
with the highest color by
FirstFit
. That is, for all
I^{\prime}\in\mathcal{R}_{\texttt{2}}(I)
,
\text{{FF} }(I^{\prime})\leq\text{{FF} }(D^{\texttt{M}}(I))
.

A special property of the selection of
D^{\texttt{M}}(I)
is that since
D^{\texttt{M}}(I)\in\mathcal{M}(I)
, any row in
\mathcal{R}_{\texttt{0}}(I),\mathcal{R}_{\texttt{1}}(I)
or
\mathcal{R}_{\texttt{3}}(I)
, cannot be in
\mathcal{R}_{\texttt{3}}(D^{\texttt{M}}(I))
(Lemma
).
Therefore, all rows in
\mathcal{R}_{\texttt{3}}(D^{\texttt{M}}(I))
must also be in
\mathcal{R}_{\texttt{2}}(I)
.

In the general case, we pick one of
D^{\texttt{A}}(I)
and
D^{\texttt{M}}(I)
as the pivot and use the Pivot bound to bound the color
\text{{FF} }(I)
.
More specifically, we consider the following two cases.

(1)

\text{{FF} }(D^{\texttt{M}}(I))\leq\text{{FF} }(D^{\texttt{A}}(I))
.

In this case, the misaligned intervals in
\mathcal{R}_{\texttt{2}}(I)
are not in
\mathcal{S}^{*}
since they all have colors of at most
\text{{FF} }(D^{\texttt{M}}(I))\leq\text{{FF} }(D^{\texttt{A}}(I))
.
Using
D^{\texttt{A}}(I)
as the pivot and following a strategy
similar to the case of integral-endpoints, we can show that
\text{{FF} }(I)\leq 2\omega
(Lemma
21
).

(2)

\text{{FF} }(D^{\texttt{M}}(I))>\text{{FF} }(D^{\texttt{A}}(I))
.

In this case, applying the Pivot bound with
I^{*}=D^{\texttt{A}}(I)
leads to a bound of
\text{{FF} }(I)\leq 3\omega
as shown in the naive attempt.
To be precise, it is no longer true that
\mathcal{S}^{*}\subseteq\mathcal{R}_{\texttt{1}}(I)
, and any interval in
\mathcal{R}_{\texttt{2}}(I)
with a color greater than
\text{{FF} }(D^{\texttt{A}}(I))
is also a candidate of
\mathcal{S}^{*}
.

An alternative strategy is to pick the interval
D^{\texttt{M}}(I)
as the pivot in this case.
However, in the general case, the intervals in
\mathcal{R}_{\texttt{1}}(D^{\texttt{M}}(I))
are not particularly structured,
More precisely, we do not know which intervals also intersect with
I
, and the neighborhood of
D^{\texttt{M}}(I)
can be huge.
When
D^{\texttt{M}}(I)
has a large number of intersections, using the Neighborhood bound to bound the color
\text{{FF} }(D^{\texttt{M}}(I))
can lead to a high bound and further lead to a high bound of
\text{{FF} }(I)
by the Pivot bound.
To deal with this situation, we first identify the rows containing intervals in
\mathcal{R}_{\texttt{1}}(D^{\texttt{M}}(I))
that we know their locations, namely, the intervals that are twins with
D^{\texttt{M}}(I)
.

Definition 18
.

Let the set of rows in
\mathcal{R}_{\texttt{2}}(I)
with intervals identical to interval
D^{\texttt{M}}(I)
be

\mathcal{R}^{\texttt{TDM}}(I)=\{\text{row }\mathcal{R}\in\mathcal{R}_{\texttt{2}}(I)\mid\mathcal{R}\text{ contains at least one interval that is in }\mathcal{T}(D^{\texttt{M}}(I))\}.

Note that
\mathcal{R}^{\texttt{TDM}}(I)\subseteq\mathcal{R}_{\texttt{1}}(D^{\texttt{M}}(I))
, and thus
r_{\texttt{3}}(D^{\texttt{M}}(I))\leq|\mathcal{R}^{\texttt{TDM}}(I)|
implies that
r_{\texttt{3}}(D^{\texttt{M}}(I))\leq r_{\texttt{1}}(D^{\texttt{M}}(I))
. On the contrary,
D^{\texttt{M}}(I)
potentially has a large neighborhood if
r_{\texttt{3}}(D^{\texttt{M}}(I))>|\mathcal{R}^{\texttt{TDM}}(I)|
.
According to the size of
|\mathcal{R}^{\texttt{TDM}}(I)|
, we consider the following cases
2.a
and
2.b
.

(2.a)

r_{\texttt{3}}(D^{\texttt{M}}(I))\leq|\mathcal{R}^{\texttt{TDM}}(I)|
.

In this case, we pick
D^{\texttt{M}}(I)
as the pivot
I^{*}
.
By the selection of
D^{\texttt{M}}(I)
and
\text{{FF} }(D^{\texttt{M}}(I))>\text{{FF} }(D^{\texttt{A}}(I))
, the only intervals that can contribute to
\mathcal{S}^{*}
are those in the set
\mathcal{R}_{\texttt{1}}(I)
.
Furthermore, by
|\mathcal{R}^{\texttt{TDM}}(I)|\geq r_{\texttt{3}}(D^{\texttt{M}}(I))
, the neighborhood of
D^{\texttt{M}}(I)
is small, which enables us to use the Neighborhood bound to prove an upper bound the color of interval
D^{\texttt{M}}(I)
(Lemma
26
).

Then, by the Pivot bound (
\text{{FF} }(I)
is bounded by
2\omega
Lemma
28
).

(2.b)

r_{\texttt{3}}(D^{\texttt{M}}(I))>|\mathcal{R}^{\texttt{TDM}}(I)|
.

This is the most technical part of our analysis.
In this case,
D^{\texttt{M}}(I)
may intersect more than
2
intervals per row on average and have a large neighborhood, and using the Neighborhood bound on
\text{{FF} }(D^{\texttt{M}}(I))
can lead to a large bound.
We apply our framework recursively on
D^{\texttt{M}}(I)
by first showing that the intervals in
\mathcal{R}_{\texttt{3}}(D^{\texttt{M}}(I))
rows do not intersect many intervals when
r_{\texttt{3}}(D^{\texttt{M}}(I))
is large (Lemma

).
Specifically, we let

5
Conclusion

In this work, we develop a sophisticated counting method based on the Pivot bound and show that
FirstFit
uses at most
2\omega
colors in the case that all open and closed unit-length intervals have integral endpoints, which matches the lower bound.
We also show that
FirstFit
uses at most
\lceil\frac{7}{3}\omega\rceil-2
colors when the input open or closed unit-length intervals have arbitrary endpoints.
It remains open to find a tight bound for this problem.

In an attempt to improve the current upper bound for the number of colors used by the
FirstFit
algorithm for the general case, it may be of interest to investigate the number of colors used by the
FirstFit
algorithm for the so-called bounded length intervals as introduced by Chybowska-Sokól et al.
[
2
]
.
The technique and the results on open/closed unit-length intervals developed in this work serve as a first step towards investigating
FirstFit
’s performance via gradually relaxing the lengths bound.
