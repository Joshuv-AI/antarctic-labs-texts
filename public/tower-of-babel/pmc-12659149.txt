Decellularized bone extracellular matrix embedded with PLGA-linezolid microparticles for infected bone regeneration
Authors: Farnoosh Ebrahimi, Hanieh Jalali, Niloofar Kalantari, Mohammad Hossein Ghanian, Samaneh Hosseini
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundDual-functional antibacterial and osteogenic substitutes are advanced tools for treating bone infections while facilitating bone regeneration. These systems incorporate drug delivery mechanisms that allow for the local release of antibiotics and increased treatment efficacy. Decellularized bone extracellular matrix (dECM) offers key advantages for bone xenografts and local drug delivery due to its biocompatibility and low immunogenicity. This study developed a novel scaffold by loading linezolid-poly (lactic-co-glycolic acid) (PLGA-Lin) microparticles onto dECM derived from bovine tibia. The antibacterial activity, cytocompatibility, and osteoconductive properties of the resulting Lin-PLGA/dECM scaffold were assessed in vitro.ResultsThe results revealed that the dECM had a porous structure and the Lin-PLGA microparticles infiltrated the scaffold. None of the dECM, PLGA/dECM, or Lin-PLGA/dECM scaffolds had toxic effects on adipose tissue-derived mesenchymal stem cells (ADSCs), while the inhibitory effect of Lin-PLGA/dECM on the Staphylococcus aureus strain was significant. All scaffolds—dECM, PLGA/dECM, and Lin-PLGA/dECM—promoted the bone differentiation of ADSCs, as confirmed by alkaline phosphatase (ALP) and calcium content assays, as well as by the expression of the Runt-related transcription factor 2 (Runx2), osteopontin, osteocalcin, and collagen Iα genes.ConclusionsThe Lin-PLGA/dECM scaffold developed in this study can be considered a biocompatible bone xenograft substitute, as it possesses excellent antibacterial properties and substantial osteogenic potential.Graphical Abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12938-025-01478-2.

Background

Osteomyelitis is a serious condition that leads to various complications, including osteonecrosis, septic arthritis, impaired growth in children, and even life-threatening conditions such as septicemia [1]. Chronic osteomyelitis is a persistent infection that is usually treated with a combination of surgical debridement and long-term antibiotic therapy [2]. Owing to the spatial heterogeneity of bacterial colonization in bone, complete eradication of infection is difficult, making antibiotic therapy a common part of the treatment approach [3]. Even after surgical debridement, the concentration of antibiotics at the site of chronic infection is often insufficient due to vascular disruption. Furthermore, residual bacteria in the area—particularly those in biofilms or ‘small colony types’—typically require very high concentrations of topical antibiotics [4]. Bacterial osteomyelitis also causes bone loss both by impairing bone production and increasing bone erosion; the interaction of the invasive pathogen with bone cells and the host immune response appears to be the cause of bone loss during osteomyelitis. This allows bacteria to multiply and persist in an avascular environment, which is inaccessible to antibiotics [5].

The implant-associated infection or biofilm formation contributes to the chronicity of osteomyelitis; therefore, there is a need for techniques to save the bone graft in infectious conditions and provide the possibility of bone healing [6]. Antibacterial implants that also promote bone growth represent optimal strategies for addressing infectious bone defects. [7]. Decellularized bone is a natural scaffold that provides microarchitecture and mechanical support for the production of new bone at the site of bone defects [8]. Decellularized bone has high compatibility with the target tissue and does not cause immune stimulation in the recipient due to the removal of cellular components [9]. It is composed of fundamental elements of the bone extracellular matrix (ECM), such as hydroxyapatite (HA), collagen, and heparin sulfate, and promotes the production of mineralized ECM by osteoblasts [10]. In addition, scaffolds derived from decellularized xenografts have been exclusively tested as bone graft substitutes in various preclinical and clinical phases, resulting in new bone formation and improved bone damage in those models [11]. Bone substitutes derived from decellularized bone have also been used for the transfer of bone-inducing agents such as bone morphogenetic peptide-2, making them an ideal option for local drug delivery [12]. The biocompatibility of the decellularized bone matrix has made it a suitable option for producing implants with dual osteoinductive and anti-infection functions. For example, the use of a decellularized cancellous bone matrix containing the antibiotic vancomycin not only inhibited the growth of bacteria in the infected site of the rat bone but also led to successful bone repair and inhibition of osteoclast function [13].

Linezolid (Lin) is an methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA) reagent that is used orally for the treatment of a broad spectrum of infections, including osteomyelitis [14]. The Lin presents a strong alternative to commonly used antibiotics like vancomycin in the treatment of bone infections, especially for resistant pathogens, in patients with vancomycin allergy or intolerance [15]. While it has been used for longer durations in some cases, there are concerns about the potential for adverse effects with prolonged use, such as myelosuppression, neuropathy, and optic neuropathy [16]. Various studies have shown that the systemic side effects of antibiotics can be prevented by local administration [17]. In the present study, we aimed to develop a bifunctional bone substitute based on decellularized bone ECM (dECM) and polylactic-co-glycolic acid (PLGA)-Lin microparticles. For this purpose, Lin was loaded into the PLGA polymer, and then the complex was loaded on the surface of the dECM. Both the antibacterial potential and the osteoinductive potential of the produced construct were evaluated under in vitro conditions.

Bioactivity of the dECM scaffolds

The antibacterial activities of the dECM, PLGA/dECM, and Lin-PLGA/dECM scaffolds against MRSA were determined over 72 h. The data indicated that in the first 48 h, PLGA/dECM and Lin-PLGA/dECM both had inhibitory effects on MRSA, whereas dECM lacked any anti-bactericidal effects. After 48 h, the inhibition zone of PLGA/dECM was lost, and bacterial colonies started to grow within the inhibition zone (indicated by a black star in Fig. 3A‑f). However, the Lin-PLGA/dECM zone remained intact (Fig. 3A (a-c)). Discussion

The development of dual antibacterial and osteoprotective implants represents a significant advancement in the field of orthopedic biomaterials. These innovations not only aim to mitigate the risk of infections associated with implants, but also promote effective bone healing through enhanced osteogenic activity. By incorporating these functionalities directly into the implant design, the healing process can be accelerated, potentially reducing side effects associated with prolonged treatments [18]. A variety of materials have been explored for creating dual-functional implants. For example, calcium silicate–gelatin composites have been coated with chitosan polysaccharide and chitosan oligosaccharide to enhance their antibacterial properties. Studies have shown that CS coatings exhibit superior antibacterial and osteogenic activity under in vitro conditions [19]. The dECM has also been incorporated with various biomaterials and bioproducts for bone repair applications. In a study, an osteogenic bone filler composed of decellularized bovine bone particles, polyurethane, and HA was applied for local antibiotic delivery to prevent infection in bone defects contaminated with Staphylococcus aureus. The results showed that vancomycin passively loaded onto the scaffold effectively prevented infection in a rabbit model, significantly reducing bacterial load and preventing osteomyelitis signs [20]. Furthermore, in a recent study, ECM derived from porcine bone and cartilage was used in combination with mesenchymal stem cell-derived exosomes to create a hydrogel for bone defect repair. This approach leverages the natural composition and bioactivity of the decellularized ECM to provide a supportive environment for tissue regeneration while enhancing healing through the addition of exosomes [21].

Bone decellularized xenografts—derived from animal sources like bovine, equine, camel, or ostrich—serve as critical alternatives to human-derived grafts in reconstructive orthopedics because they eliminate the need for secondary surgical sites, reducing patient morbidity and surgical complexity [22]. Furthermore, the decellularization process helps minimize the immune response in the recipient, thereby reducing the risk of rejection compared to non-decellularized xenografts [23]. Although naturally dECMs have advantages in the field of tissue engineering because of their ability to mimic the native bone microenvironment, they also have certain disadvantages; especially if not properly sterilized, these scaffolds can serve as substrates for bacterial colonization, leading to potential infections post-implantation [24]. Antibiotic loading on dECMs could reduce the risk of bacterial transmission. Antibacterial coating is a promising method for developing such bifunctional materials and preventing bacterial adhesion and biofilm development [25]. Coating must facilitate host cell adhesion while simultaneously preventing bacterial adhesion, a challenge owing to the overlapping mechanisms of adhesion shared by bacteria and host cells [26]. S. aureus is the most common cause of bone infection and can lead to extensive bone loss and destruction. These properties are closely associated with the regulation of the balance between osteoblasts and osteoclasts activity. Upon infection with S. aureus, osteoblasts’ function is suppressed and the cells undergo necrosis and apoptosis. Concurrently, osteoclast proliferation and activation are enhanced, leading to disruption of bone homeostasis and subsequent inflammatory bone loss [27].

In the present study, we examined the impact of Lin-PLGA/dECM on the growth of S. aureus as well as ADSCs. Our findings revealed that while Lin-PLGA/dECM significantly inhibited S. aureus growth, it had no toxic effects on ADSCs. This outcome highlights the dual functionality of the scaffold; specifically, it suggests that, despite its antibacterial properties, the scaffold does not impede the growth of mesenchymal cells or osteoblast precursors. Instead, it might create a conducive environment for the growth of bone precursors. Furthermore, regarding the osteogenic potential of Lin-PLGA/dECM, our findings demonstrated that it did not inhibit the osteogenic differentiation of ADSCs and enhanced the expression of Runx2, osteocalcin, osteopontin, collagen Iα, and ALP—key factors essential for osteoblast differentiation and the regulation of multiple osteoblast-specific genes. [28]. Although no significant differences were observed in the expression of osteogenic genes among the dECM, PLGA-dECM, and Lin-PLGA-dECM groups, this outcome may be explained by the inherent properties and microenvironment of the bone-derived dECM, which inherently provides a favorable matrix for osteoblast differentiation. Moreover, as previous studies have not reported any osteogenic potential for PLGA or linezolid particles, their presence within the dECM implants is unlikely to exert stimulatory or inhibitory effects on osteogenic differentiation.

PLGA is a polymer approved by the American Food and Drug Administration (FDA) and widely used in drug delivery applications because of its unique properties, including biocompatibility, tunability and high versatility [29]. Research has shown that PLGA-loaded scaffolds can effectively support new bone formation in both in vitro and in vivo models; for example, PLGA has been utilized for the sustained release of simvastatin from 3D-printed bioactive glass scaffolds, increasing its osteoinductive properties [30]. In addition, PLGA-hyaluronic acid composite scaffolds have been tested in rabbit models for tibial defect repair and demonstrated good biocompatibility and promoted bone healing [31]. In our study, PLGA also functioned effectively as a biocompatible drug carrier, and scaffolds loaded with this polymer did not inhibit cell growth or osteogenic differentiation.

In recent years, numerous efforts have focused on developing antibacterial bone substitutes, primarily utilizing biological or chemical polymer materials alone or in combination and techniques like 3D-printing have been employed to create three-dimensional structures with suitable bone strength. For example, a 3D-printed scaffold combining polylactic acid (PLA) with HA has been developed to deliver vancomycin, providing both antibacterial action and mechanical strength suitable for bone repair [32]. Furthermore, the β-tricalcium phosphate/gelatin composite scaffolds incorporating gentamycin-loaded chitosan microspheres demonstrated sustained gentamycin release, suitable mechanical strength, and successful fusion with bone tissue [33]. However, in comparison to previous studies, a key approach in our research was the utilization of an accessible and cost-effective source to produce the bone xenograft, making it an environmentally friendly option. Unlike implants fabricated from synthetic or natural polymers, this type of implant also exhibited structural and physical properties closely matching those of natural bone, thereby significantly enhancing its bioactivity. Furthermore, we employed controlled drug loading into PLGA microparticles instead of using doping methods to combine the drug with the scaffold. This approach is particularly important for implants derived from decellularized bone, which inherently possess lower mechanical strength than synthetic polymers, as it reduces the risk of excessive drug release even if the scaffold collapses or undergoes rapid degradation due to biological factors.

While our study on developing an implant for the treatment or control of bone infections has yielded important and valuable results, further research and validation—particularly in vivo—are necessary to better understand its clinical potential. One of the challenges in producing this type of bone substitute is the risk of transmitting infections, including viral or bacterial agents, which complicates large-scale production. Additionally, controlling the degradation rate of PLGA, especially in acidic environments like those present during infection, remains a challenge. This issue may be addressed by employing a multilayered synthesis method to prevent an initial burst release. Moreover, protein-level validation as well as BMP-2 and TGF-β quantification was not performed in this study and will be addressed in future studies.

Conclusion

The findings of this study demonstrate that dECM derived from bovine tibia can function as an effective scaffold and bone xenograft, resulting in excellent cytocompatibility and substantial osteogenic potential. By loading the sscaffold with microparticles containing the Lin, we developed a dual-functional substitute with inherent antibacterial properties. This method also allows for the gradual release of the drug at the grafting site, positioning it as a promising solution for treating local bone infections.

Materials and methods

Preparation and characterization of the decellularized bone scaffold

Decellularized bone scaffolds were prepared according to a previously described protocol [34]. Briefly, bone samples obtained from fresh bovine tibia were cut into 4 × 4 × 4 mm pieces and decellularized by applying four decellularization solutions that contained ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA) (0.1% W/V), Tris (10 mM), sodium dodecyl sulfate (SDS) (0.5% W/V) and Triton (0.1% V/V). Decellularized bone was characterized in terms of morphology and mechanical strength by SEM (Crossbeam®, 1540XB by Zeiss), and Santam STM-20 was used for pressure tests at a speed of 1 mm/min at room temperature. DNA quantification (34.66 ± 2.9 ng/mg dry weight) and histological staining had already been performed to validate decellularization efficiency [34].

Loading the Lin antibiotic into PLGA microparticles

A single emulsion (O/W) solvent evaporation technique was employed to produce PLGA microparticles incorporating the antibiotic Lin (Virdev Intermediates Pvt. Ltd., India). Briefly, we dissolved 30 mg of PLGA polymer (Sigma) and 10 mg of Lin in 2 and 0.5 ml of dichloromethane (DCM), respectively, to create the oil phase. The oil phase was subsequently added to 5 ml of poly(vinyl alcohol) (1% w/v) as an aqueous solution that contained Tween 20. The mixture was homogenized for 3 min at 7000 rpm (Heidolph Silent Crusher M, Germany) while placed in an ice bath. Subsequently, the emulsion was promptly added to a PVA aqueous solution (0.1% w/v) and stirred for 3 h at 300 rpm at room temperature to facilitate the evaporation of the organic solvent and solidification of the microparticles. After centrifuging at 10,000 rpm for 20 min, the microparticles were harvested and rinsed three times with distilled water to eliminate any remaining surfactants. They were then frozen at -20 °C, freeze-dried and kept at -20 °C until needed. The morphology and size of the synthesized microparticles were determined via SEM.

Preparation of the Lin-PLGA microparticle-loaded scaffolds

The Lin-PLGA microparticle-loaded scaffold (Lin-PLGA/dECM) was fabricated using a post-seeding method. In summary, 1 mg of Lin-PLGA microparticles was dispersed in 1 ml of distilled water. A 500-µl aliquot of this suspension was applied to one side of the dECM scaffold, followed by another 500 µl applied to the opposite side of the scaffold loaded with microparticles; then, the microparticle-loaded scaffold was freeze-dried and stored at -20 °C [35].

Loading efficiency of the Lin

The total drug content in the microparticles was determined by dissolving a measured quantity of microparticles in DCM, followed by extracting the Lin using an equal volume of water. The drug loading efficiency was then calculated using the following equation:

Drug loading efficiency (%) = (amount of drug loaded/total amount of drug used) × 100.

Antibacterial disk diffusion assay

We assessed the antibacterial activity of dECM, PLGA/dECM, and Lin-PLGA/dECM by performing a disk diffusion susceptibility test. S. aureus (ATCC code 25,923) was purchased from the Institute Pasteur, Tehran, Iran, and cultivated based on the Kirby–Bauer standard protocol. Briefly, 52 g of brain heart infusion (BHI; DNAbioTech, Iran) agar was dissolved in one liter of distilled water and boiled for 1 min. Then, the medium was autoclaved, and after cooling, it was poured into sterile plates. The plates were kept at 4 °C. S. aureus bacteria were cultured; in this way, a small amount of the bacterial stock was removed under the microbial hood with a sterile swab and smeared on a BHI agar plate to grow single colonies of bacteria. A bacterial colony was removed and dissolved in sterile physiological serum to reach the appropriate turbidity equivalent to half McFarland. After the solution was prepared, some of the suspension was cultured on Muller–Hinton agar (Merck, Germany) medium in all directions in the form of grass until it was completely inoculated on the surface of the plate. The plates were left at room temperature for 5–10 min to absorb moisture. Then, the scaffolds of the dECM, PLGA/dECM, and Lin-PLGA/dECM groups, which were previously sterilized by 70% ethanol and UV radiation, were placed on the surface of the plate. The plates were incubated at 35 °C, and the diameters of the growth inhibition zones were measured at 24, 48, and 72 h, after which the data were statistically analyzed. Every measurement was repeated three times to ensure accuracy and reproducibility.

Mesenchymal stem cell culture and characterization

ADSCs were purchased from the cell banking of the National Center for Genetic and Biological Resources of Iran and were cultured in DMEM-F12 culture medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; Gibco, UK) in an incubator at 37 °C with 5% CO2 and 98% humidity. To confirm the mesenchymal phenotype of the isolated cells, third-passage cells were harvested, rinsed with PBS, and incubated with monoclonal antibodies against mesenchymal surface markers (CD44 and CD90) as well as hematopoietic markers (CD34) (Biosciences, San Francisco, CA, USA). Marker expression was subsequently assessed by flow cytometry using a BD FACS Canto II system (BD Biosciences, USA).

Cell seeding onto the scaffolds

Initially, the scaffolds were sterilized using a small volume of 70% ethanol and UV irradiation for 20 min, and then, 350,000 cells were transferred to the scaffold via the gradual transfer method. After the cells on the scaffold were transferred, a 1% antifungal solution was added to the culture medium to avoid fungal contamination.

Assessment of dECM structure and cell attachment via SEM imaging

To assess the ultrastructure of the dECMs and recellularized ECMs, the samples were fixed in 2.5% glutaraldehyde solution and then washed with PBS for 30 min. To fix the fats, the samples were placed in 1% osmium tetroxide solution for 30 min and then placed in ethanol solutions with increasing concentrations to dehydrate the samples. Finally, the samples were dried under a sterile hood, and after being coated with gold, photographs were taken with an SEM (VEGA\TESCAN-LMU, Czech Republic).

Cytocompatibility of the scaffolds

To evaluate the cytocompatibility of dECMs, PLGA-dECM, and Lin-PLGA-dECM, ADSCs were cultured on the scaffolds, and cell viability was evaluated using the 3-(4,5-dimethylthiazol-2-yl)-2,5-diphenyltetrazolium bromide (MTT) assay on days 1, 3 and 7. For this purpose, at the desired time point, the culture medium was removed, and 100 µl of the MTT solution (mg/ml; Sigma, UK) was added to each well, followed by incubation in the dark for 4 h. Then, the media were discarded, and 1000 µl of dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) was added to solubilize the formazan crystals by gentle pipetting. The optical density (OD) of each well was then measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader. Osteogenic potential of the prepared scaffolds

To determine the effects of the dECM, PLGA-dECM, and Lin-PLGA-dECM on the osteogenic differentiation of ADSCs, the cells were seeded on the scaffolds and exposed to osteogenic induction medium composed of low-glucose DMEM, 50 mg/ml ascorbic acid 2-phosphate, 10 nM/ml dexamethasone and 10 mM beta glycerol phosphate (all from Sigma, USA) for 14 and 21 days. To confirm osteogenic differentiation, an ALP activity assay, calcium content measurement and gene expression analysis were performed.

ALP activity

After 14 days of ADSC osteo-induction on the defined ECMs, total ALP activity was determined via an ALP assay kit (Abcam, USA). Briefly, the cells were homogenized via lysis followed by centrifugation at 10,000 × g. The supernatants were collected for the assessment of ALP activity and total protein concentration. ALP activity was determined according to the manufacturer’s instructions, by quantifying the release of p-nitrophenol from the p-nitrophenylphosphate substrate. The optical density of p-nitrophenol produced was measured at 405 nm, and the related ALP activity was then normalized to the total protein content obtained from the corresponding cell lysate. The total protein concentration was quantified with a BCA Assay Kit (Millipore, USA), and the absorbance was measured at 562 nm via a microplate spectrophotometer.

Calcium content

To assay the calcium content, a calcium assay kit (BioVision, USA) was used. First, the standard curve was drawn by the standard slope of the concentrations of 0, 0.4, 0.8, 1.2, 1.6 and 2 µg of calcium. Then, 10 μl of the original sample was added to each well, and the final volume reached 50 μl with deionized water. A total of 90 μl of chromogenic reagent and 60 μl of calcium assay buffer were added to each well, followed by a 10-min incubation at room temperature in the dark. The OD was then measured at 575 nm. The calcium concentration is obtained from the following formula = sample calcium concentration (mg)/sample volume in well (ml).

Real-time PCR analysis

Real-time PCR was performed to analyze the expression of the osteocalcin, osteopontin, Runx 2, Col-1, and ALP genes after 21 days of osteogenesis in ADSCs cultured on dECM, PLGA-dECM, and Lin-PLGA/dECM. Total RNA was isolated according to the company’s datasheet (Dena Zist, Iran) and then converted to cDNA via a cDNA synthesis kit (Dena Zist, Iran). Quantitative PCR (qPCR) was performed via a SYBR Green PCR kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Germany) with a LightCycler® 96 Instrument (Roche, Germany). The relative expression of the genes of interest was calculated via the ΔΔCt method, with β-actin used as a reference gene. Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses of three or more experimental groups were performed using GraphPad Prism 10.4.1. The normality of the data was checked using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Significant differences between groups were determined via one-way ANOVA with post hoc Tukey HSD assay. Differences were considered significant at p < 0.05. To analyze real-time PCR results REST 2009 Software was used.
