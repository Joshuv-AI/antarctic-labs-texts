A case of multiple evanescent white dot syndrome as epiphenomenon of cytomegalovirus infection
Authors: Jo-Hsuan Wu, Enrico Meduri, Royce WS Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeTo report a case of possible epiphenomenon of multiple evanescent white dot syndrome (epi-MEWDS) in setting of cytomegalovirus (CMV) infection.ObservationsA healthy 36-year-old male presented with bilateral painless floaters for 3 days and concurrent symptoms of fatigue, myalgia, and diarrhea. Clinical examination revealed bilateral multifocal retinal white dots and panuveitis that were more prominent in the right eye, with retinal imaging findings consistent with MEWDS. Panuveitis due to ocular or systemic infection was initially suspected. Anterior chamber paracentesis for viral polymerase chain reaction analysis was negative. However, systemic lab test revealed positive CMV IgM and IgG antibodies. Treatment was deferred given mild symptoms and immunocompetent status of the patient, and the retinal pathologies and panuveitis self-resolved in 2 months.Conclusions and importanceDiagnosis of epi-MEWDS requires careful clinical examination and supportive imaging findings. In our case, the combination of systemic symptoms, CMV serology, compatible multimodal imaging, and timeline of spontaneous resolution supports the CMV as the triggering event for a para-infectious MEWDS-like immune response, consistent with epi-MEWDS. This report adds to the currently limited literature on epi-MEWDS as a possible self-limiting immunologic response to an underlying infectious process.Keywords: CMV, Cytomegalovirus, Epi-MEWDS, MEWDS, Panuveitis, Secondary MEWDS, White dot syndrome

Introduction

Multiple evanescent white dot syndrome (MEWDS) is a self-resolving condition featuring inflammatory choriocapillaropathies or photoreceptoritis.1 Patients are typically young, healthy individuals, with about 30% of cases presenting with viral prodrome. On the other hand, “MEWDS as an epiphenomenon of other etiology” or “epi-MEWDS” is a relatively new term describing MEWDS-like clinical features that may be attributed to an immunological response to another underlying or predisposing condition.2 This entity has been increasingly recognized in association with other inflammatory or infectious chorioretinal diseases; however, its clinical spectrum, pathophysiology, and triggers remain poorly characterized. Here, we report a case of possible epi-MEWDS occurring in the setting of systemic cytomegalovirus (CMV) infection, highlighting a potential para-infectious mechanism and expanding the spectrum of conditions associated with this under-recognized entity.

Case report

A 36-year-old otherwise healthy male with no significant past ocular history presented with 2-3 days of bilateral painless flashes and floaters with mild blurry vision, more pronounced in the right eye (OD). Review of systems was notable for a 2-week history of fever, fatigue, myalgia, and diarrhea. There was no recent trauma, injury, travel, or exposure to infectious agents. The patient was on human immunodeficiency virus pre-exposure prophylaxis (PrEP).

At presentation, Snellen best corrected visual acuity (BCVA) was 20/40 in OD and 20/20 in the left eye (OS). The intraocular pressure was normal in both eyes (OU). Anterior segment exam revealed fine keratic precipitates (KPs) in OD and trace white cells in the anterior chamber OU, more prominent in OD. Dilated fundus exam showed bilateral mild vitreous haze and diffuse multifocal retinal white dots, more pronounced in OD (Fig. 1A–B).

A diagnosis of bilateral panuveitis with MEWDS-like outer retinal involvement was made. Given the bilateral presentation with clear signs of anterior inflammation, primary MEWDS was considered less likely, and visual field test was not performed. Systemic workup was pursued, as well as anterior chamber paracentesis for viral polymerase chain reaction (PCR) analysis. Prophylactic treatment of oral Valacyclovir 3 g per day was started and subsequently discontinued following a negative aqueous viral PCR result. Laboratory testing revealed slight leukocytosis, an elevated erythrocyte sedimentation rate, and elevated C-reactive protein. All infectious investigations, including syphilis, were negative, except for positive serologic cytomegalovirus (CMV) IgG and IgM.

Over a 2-month follow-up period, there was progressive spontaneous resolution of visual symptoms, intraocular inflammation, and imaging findings without additional treatment. BCVA improved to 20/20 OU, and posterior vitreous detachment developed in OD. Repeat imaging showed complete resolution of hyperautofluorescent lesions on FAF (Fig. 4), and restoration of EZ integrity on SD-OCT (Fig. 3B).

Discussion

This report describes a young, healthy male presenting with bilateral mild panuveitis and MEWDS-like retinal features that resolved spontaneously with conservative management. While a broad differential diagnosis, particularly infectious etiologies, was initially considered, laboratory testing was notable only for serologic CMV IgM and IgG. The final impression was a possible epi-MEWDS associated with recent CMV infection.

Epi-MEWDS are considered an immunological response triggered by other diseases. While the exact pathophysiology remains unknown, it is hypothesized that MEWDS features result from exposure of choroidal antigens secondary to disruption of the retinal pigment epithelium-Bruch membrane-choriocapillaris complex.2,3 More recent clinicopathologic and multimodal imaging-based interpretations suggest that primary MEWDS predominantly affects the outer retina and photoreceptor complex, with ongoing debate regarding the relative contribution of the choriocapillaris.4 In contrast, epi-MEWDS may be better understood as a secondary inflammatory response to an underlying insult involving the RPE–Bruch membrane–choriocapillaris complex, rather than as a primary isolated outer retinopathy.3

The diagnosis of epi-MEWDS is clinical and relies on SD-OCT and FA evidence of outer retinal disruption consistent with MEWDS, although the disease course and outcome may vary depending on the underlying disease. Despite similar imaging features, the underlying pathophysiology may differ, with epi-MEWDS representing a final common inflammatory phenotype triggered by diverse conditions.

Prior literature has described an association of epi-MEWDS with other white dot syndromes (WDS), including punctate inner choroidopathy, multifocal choroiditis, and acute zonal occult outer retinopathy. The temporal relationship between these entities can be variable, with reports of MEWDS-like findings preceding, coexisting with, or evolving into other WDS. In some cases, worse visual outcomes have been observed when MEWDS features precede the development of other inflammatory chorioretinopathies.5 Notably, when choroidal neovascularization develops in association with PIC/MFC, additional treatment might be required to control the underlying inflammatory process.6

Fewer reports describe epi-MEWDS associated with infectious chorioretinitis. However, a similar mechanism of immunologic response-triggered outer retinal layer disruption has been hypothesized.2,3 Infectious epi-MEWDS has most frequently been reported with toxoplasmosis, including in forms of acute ocular toxoplasmosis, congenital toxoplasmosis, or recurrent toxoplasmosis.3,7 Few reports of candida endophthalmitis, tubercular multifocal serpiginous-like choroiditis, and ocular syphilis have also been described.3 In most of the above cases, the imaging abnormalities resolved in a few weeks with conservative management. This concept has been further supported by a dedicated case series of infectious chorioretinopathies complicated by epi-MEWDS, reinforcing the view that MEWDS-like lesions can occur as an epiphenomenon across different infectious substrates rather than representing a single disease-specific entity.3

A notable challenge when diagnosing epi-MEWDS is its reliance on clinical presentation, concurrent lab test, and ocular imaging findings. In our case, the FAF, OCT, and FA findings are consistent with MEWDS. However, the bilateral presentation and anterior chamber inflammation is not typical of primary MEWDS and would instead support an inflammatory response to a systemic trigger. Meanwhile, the positive CMV IgM suggested a recent infection and would explain the systemic gastroenteritis symptoms in concurrence with the panuveitis.

The clinical picture was also not consistent with classic CMV retinitis, which would be unexpected in an immunocompetent patient and would more typically present with necrotizing retinitis and a more destructive retinal course. Given the patient's immunocompetent status and mild symptoms, after excluding the more vision-threatening conditions requiring immediate treatment, we elected to proceed with conservative management only. Still, a unique point to consider in the current case is the use of PrEP. Although the patient reports no past medical history and CMV is more frequently transmitted through non-sexual contact, clinicians should remain vigilant about the possibility of an increased risk of CMV exposure and infection given the specific epidemiological risk profile associated with PrEP use.

It is interesting that the aqueous PCR was negative. Given this finding, we hypothesize that the combination of systemic symptoms, CMV serology, compatible multimodal imaging, and spontaneous resolution supports the possibility that CMV acted as the triggering event for a para-infectious MEWDS-like immune response rather than cause a direct ocular infection. Serologic testing of CMV IgM can show false positive result due to cross-reactivity with antibodies to non-CMV entities, especially Epstein Barr Virus (EBV).8 Given the lack of aqueous PCR positivity and testing for EBV, a false positive result cannot be completely ruled out. Nonetheless, in addition to the aforementioned supportive findings from physical exam, eye exam, and retinal imaging, the timeline of symptomatic and pathologic evolution also supports a potential relationship between the systemic CMV infection and the ocular manifestations.

Conclusion

Epi-MEWDS is a presumed immunological response triggered by other entities causing disruption to the outer retinal layers. Diagnosis remains primarily clinical, requiring careful clinical examination and supportive imaging findings. Our case supports the concept that MEWDS-like features may represent a final common inflammatory phenotype rather than a distinct primary disease, particularly in the presence of a systemic trigger. This report suggests that CMV infection may act as a para-infectious trigger rather than a direct ocular pathogen in immunocompetent patients. In such cases, after exclusion of vision-threatening conditions, conservative management may be appropriate, as spontaneous resolution can occur. This case contributes to the expanding spectrum of triggers underlying this under-recognized clinical entity.
