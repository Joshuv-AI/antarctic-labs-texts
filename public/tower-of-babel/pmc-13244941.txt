Determinants of preterm neonatal mortality in two rural district hospitals of Gatsibo, Rwanda
Authors: Emmanuel Uwambajimana, Albert Ndagijimana, Richard Kalisa
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPreterm birth remains a critical global health issue, contributing to approximately one-third of neonatal deaths worldwide. In Rwanda, complications of preterm birth contributed to the second cause on neonatal mortality with specifically two district hospitals (i.e. Kiziguro and Ngarama) located in Gatsibo district reported threefold higher preterm neonatal mortality as compared to national average. We aimed to determine the proportion and determinants of preterm neonatal mortality in two rural hospitals in Gatsibo district, Rwanda.MethodsWe conducted a retrospective cross-sectional study using records of all preterm neonates admitted to Kiziguro and Ngarama hospital neonatal units, between January 2020 and December 2024. Data were extracted from neonatal files and maternity registers. Descriptive statistics were used to describe the study variables. Bivariate and multivariable logistic regression analyses were conducted to identify the determinants of preterm neonatal mortality. We reported odds ratios, 95% CIs, and p values, and we set the significance at the 95% confidence level.ResultsThe majority of preterm deaths occurred at Kiziguro district hospital (21.0%, n = 1217). The determinants of preterm neonatal mortality were early gestational age (aOR = 2.47, 95% CI: 1.30–4.65), neonatal sepsis (aOR = 3.35, 95% CI: 1.98–5.69), hypothermia (aOR = 1.72, 95% CI: 1.12–2.66), need for special care unit services (aOR = 5.32, 95% CI: 2.61–11.96), poor respiratory support (aOR = 7.63; 95% CI: 3.69–15.80), poor bilirubin management (aOR = 2.82, 95% CI: 1.50–5.27), and inadequate kangaroo mother care practices (aOR = 51.82, 95% CI: 31.77–84.51).ConclusionThe proportion of preterm mortality was approximately similar to the national figures. Key determinants associated with preterm neonatal mortality included: early gestational age, maternal infections, inadequate kangaroo mother care (KMC), neonatal sepsis and inadequate respiratory support. Thus, continuous neonatal capacity building, improved KMC practices, appropriate use of neonatal equipment and regular cleanliness are recommended.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40748-026-00272-9.Keywords: Kangaroo mother care, Mortality, Neonatal care, Preterm birth

Introduction

Background

Preterm death is still a global challenge, accounting for approximately one-third of total neonatal deaths [1]. Unfortunately, the highest number of those preterm deaths are from preventable causes and can be averted with improved quality of neonatal care [2]. Globally, preterm births contributed to approximately one million deaths as part of underfive-year deaths in 2022 [3]. In low-resource settings, poor quality of care and limited data use were the main barriers leading to neonatal mortality [4]. Yet, there are known high impact interventions such as immediate Kangaroo mother care (KMC), early initiation of breastfeeding, postnatal care 2 and 3 done at home, mothers/caregiver’s knowledge on importance of neonatal danger signs as recommended by the World Health Organization (WHO) to improve the outcomes of preterm neonates with limited implementation [5].

In sub-Saharan African countries, where recording systems are weak, identifying determinants of preterm neonatal mortality is rare [6]. Several studies reported determinants to preterm neonatal death such as inadequate attendance of antenatal care consultation (ANC), multiple pregnancies, preexisting maternal conditions such as diabetes, hypothermia, low APGAR score < 3, early gestational age, low birth weight, perinatal asphyxia, and poor resuscitation in Gabon [7]. While, in Ethiopia which is ranked as first sub-Saharan African country with highest preterm neonatal mortality reported determinants including low antenatal care (ANC) attendance, neonatal sepsis, low birth weight less and birth asphyxia [8].

Each year, Rwanda experiences approximately thirty-five thousand preterm deliveries, of which 7.4% die due to complication related with preterm birth [9]. Rwanda made progress in reducing neonatal mortality up to 17 per 1000 live births by 2025 [10]. Courtesy of Government of Rwanda to scale up several key initiatives such as community-based health insurance, performance-based financing for healthcare providers, nationwide scale-up of neonatal special care unit at every district hospital, increased sensitization campaigns by CHWs on neonatal danger signs at community and home visits as well as health workforce development with trained neonatal staff essential newborn care, management of small vulnerable newborns, emergency obstetric and newborn care (EMoNC) training etc [11, 12]. Despite later efforts to reduce preterm newborn mortality, Gatsibo district still reported a twofold increment as compared to average national neonatal mortality rate [13]. The Gatsibo district has experienced increased preterm neonatal mortality, as per hospital monthly reports from the health management information system (HMIS), with 1930 preterm births, including 382 (19.79%) who died from 2020 to 2024 [14]. There is limited information about the determinants of preterm neonatal mortality in the Gatsibo district. Thus, this study aimed to assess the determinants of preterm neonatal mortality in Kiziguro and Ngarama district hospitals (DHs) in the Gatsibo district, Rwanda.

Methods

Study design and population

A retrospective cross-sectional study was conducted through an obstetric and neonatal chart review. All neonates born preterm at Kiziguro and Ngarama DHs in Gatsibo district between January 2020 and December 2024 were included.

Study setting

Two hospitals, namely, Kiziguro DHs and Ngarama DHs, located within Gatsibo district in the Eastern Province of Rwanda, were visited during the study. The catchment area of these two hospitals is predominantly rural and comprises 14 sectors, with 19 health centers providing maternal, newborn, and child health services, as well as 29 health posts. According to 2022 census estimates, Gatsibo district has a population of approximately 551,164 [15].

Sample size and sampling strategy

Exhaustive sampling was applied for preterm newborns admitted to the neonatal care unit during the study period. A preterm neonate was a newborn not more than 36 weeks of gestation.

Data collection methods

The study used an electronic checklist, which was developed based on medical files/registers. The checklist included predefined fields based on the study variables and was designed to collect data systematically during chart reviews of preterm neonates admitted to neonatology units during the study period (January 2020-December 2024). At each hospital, one healthcare provider was trained on the use of the data collection tool. Data collection was performed with the help of one trained neonatal service representative at each hospital. The checklist was programmed in the Kobo toolbox and Android devices.

Study variables

The dependent variable was preterm neonatal status at discharge. This variable was binary, with two outcomes coded as 0 “Alive” and 1 “Dead”.

Independent variable data were collected on maternal sociodemographic information, antenatal care, obstetric factors, and neonatal and health system characteristics, as detailed in Supplementary file 1.

Missing data

There was no missing data for these data, as secondary data were collected from files and registers, and all patients were included in the study.

Data analysis

The data collected via the Kobo toolbox were exported to Excel so that they could be uploaded into StataCorp. Stata Statistical Software: Release 15. College Station, TX: StataCorp LLC; 2017 for analysis. Preterm neonatal mortality was calculated as the proportion of preterm neonatal deaths among preterm neonatal livebirths. Descriptive statistics were used to describe study variables, with frequencies and proportions for categorical variables and medians and interquartile ranges (IQRs) for continuous variables. In the bivariate analysis, binary logistic regression was used to determine the relationship between each independent variable and the outcome variable. All significant variables were included in the multivariable analysis to identify determinants of preterm neonatal mortality. We obtained the final model of the study through a stepwise approach to adjust for confounders. We reported odds ratios, 95% confidence intervals (CIs), and p values, and the findings were significant at the 95% confidence level.

Patient and public involvement

This study did not involve patients or any other member of the public in the design, conduct, reporting or dissemination plan of the research.

Ethical considerations

This study was carried out in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki, and following the guidelines of the Rwanda National Ethics Committee. The study protocol was approved by the College of Medicine and Health Sciences Institutional Review Board (CMHS/IRB) with approval No. CMHS/IRB/188/2025. Before data collection, a confidentiality agreement was signed between the main author and the hospitals where the data were collected. The information collected is secured and not shared with anyone who is not related to this study. The information was password protected.

Results

Maternal sociodemographic characteristics

The analysis was performed on a total of 1930 neonates from both Kiziguro and Ngarama district hospitals. The average age of the mothers was 27 years, ranging from 22 to 33 years, and the majority were between 20 and 34 years (71.20%). More than 70% of the mothers had received primary education, and the majority were married (62.30%) (Table 1).

Proportion of preterm neonatal mortality

Among the 1,930 preterm neonates admitted across the two hospitals, 382 died (mortality proportion of 19.8%). Preterm neonatal mortality in Kiziguro and Ngarama hospital was 21.0% and 17.7%, respectively (Fig. 1).

Proportion of preterm neonatal mortality by hospital in Gatsibo district, 2020–2024

Antenatal and obstetric characteristics

More than 60% of mothers delivered at the hospital, and more of the women delivered above 27 weeks of gestation. The majority of mothers (95.93%) had fewer than 4 ANC visits and delivered after a spontaneous vaginal delivery (81.01%) (see Supplementary file 2).

Newborn characteristics

Among the total of 1930 newborns, the majority were female, being born at gestational age above 27 weeks and born with low birth weight. In addition, more than one-fourth of the babies developed hypothermia, and sepsis developed in approximately 23% of the babies (see Supplementary file 3).

Health system characteristics

More than half of the preterm newborns were provided specialized care, and approximately 95% were cared for in incubators. Preterm newborns who were admitted to KMC accounted for approximately 95%, and more than half of them received respiratory support (Supplementary file 4).

Bivariate analysis of maternal sociodemographic variables

According to the bivariate logistic regression analysis, newborns who were born to mothers < 20 years old were 1.25 times more likely to die (crude odds ratio [cOR] = 1.25, 95% CI 0.88–1.81) than those born to mothers above 20 years old were. The odds of death were 2.9 times greater among newborns of mothers who were illiterate (cOR = 2.90, 95% CI 1.74–4.84) and 2.16 times greater among newborns of mothers who only attended primary school (cOR = 2.16, 95% CI 1.37–3.43) than among those who attended higher education (Table 2).

Antenatal and obstetric variables

Newborns who were delivered at home had 54% greater odds of dying than those who delivered at health centers or hospitals did (cOR = 1.54, 95% CI 1.04–2.27). The odds of death were 10.62 times greater among newborns born before 28 weeks of gestation than among those born after 28 weeks of gestation (cOR = 10.62, 95% CI 7.39–15.26). Newborns of mothers who attended fewer than four ANCs had 8.06 times greater odds of dying than did those of mothers who attended four or more ANCs (cOR = 8.06, 95% CI 1.97–33.08). For those mothers who attended their first ANC visit more than 12 weeks of gestation, the odds of dying for their babies were 74% higher than those whose mothers attended their first ANC visit on time (cOR = 1.74, 95% CI 1.34–2.27). Newborns from HIV-positive mothers were 4.92 times more likely to die than newborns from HIV-negative mothers were (cOR 4.92, 95% CI 1.49–16.22). In addition, preterm newborns from mothers who suffered from infection were 2.76 times more likely to die (cOR = 2.76, 95% CI 2.01–3.79) than those from mothers without infection. Newborns from mothers infected with malaria had 2.03 times greater odds of dying (cOR = 2.03; 95% CI 0.50–8.17) than those newborns from mothers who did not suffer malaria (Table 3).

Newborn characteristics

Those newborns delivered with a body temperature less than 36.5 °C had 3.11 times greater odds of death than those newborns with a body temperature of 36.5 °C or greater (cOR = 3.11, 95% CI 2.46–3.95). Newborns with hypothermia had 3.45 times greater odds of dying than did those without hypothermia (cOR = 3.45, 95% CI 2.72–4.37). Multivariate analysis of the factors associated with preterm neonatal mortality

Newborns with sepsis had 3.35 times greater odds of dying than those without sepsis (aOR = 3.35, 95% CI: 1.98–5.69), and those with hypothermia had 72% greater odds of dying than those without hypothermia (aOR = 1.72, 95% CI: 1.12–2.66). Babies admitted to the specialized care had 5.59 times greater odds of dying than those not admitted to the specialized care (aOR = 5.32, 95% CI: 2.61–11.96). The odds of dying among newborns who received respiratory support were 7.94 times greater than those among those who did not receive respiratory support (aOR = 7.63, 95% CI: 3.91–16.14). A significant association was also identified for newborns who were not cared for in an incubator, with 2.06 times greater odds of dying than those who were cared for in an incubator (aOR = 2.06, 95% CI: 1.06–4.01), and those who were not cared for by bilirubin light had 2.82 times greater odds of dying than newborns who were cared for by bilirubin light (aOR = 2.82, 95% CI: 1.50–5.27). For those not admitted to KMC, the odds of dying were 51.82 times greater than those for newborns admitted to KMC (aOR = 51.82, 95% CI: 31.77–84.51) (Table 6).

Discussion

Our findings demonstrated some of the determinants of preterm neonatal mortality among neonates born preterm from Kiziguro and Ngarama district hospitals located in the Gatsibo district, Rwanda. The proportion of mortality among preterm newborns in the two hospitals was approximately to the national figures of 20.8% [16] and two times compared to the global figures [17]. Similar findings were reported in Kenya [18] and in Gabon [7], but in contrast to the mortality levels documented in Ethiopia [19, 20]. These disparities from other countries may be due to differences in the quality of neonatal care provided and inadequate neonatal equipment and supplies in most remote hospitals in Rwanda.

Being at an early gestational age (22–27 weeks) was significantly associated with preterm neonatal mortality. This finding is consistent with studies in Ethiopia [21], Uganda [22], and Egypt [23]. This may be because newborns at an early age are prone to lower immunity, and their body is not yet built to face external environmental conditions [24]. In contrast, this finding was lower than that reported at Mbarara Hospital in Uganda and exceeded the levels of preterm neonatal mortality reported in Ethiopia [25, 26]. The difference may be due to the study settings and time. Another determinant was maternal history of STIs during pregnancy. This finding aligns with the results of a study conducted in Kenya [18]. This may be related to the fact that newborns may acquire infections from their mothers while they are still fetuses or during delivery. A different finding was reported in a study conducted in 43 low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) from WHO regions [27]. Emphasis should be placed on ANC and mandatory screening for the identification of obstetric complications.

The odds of death were greater among preterm newborns in their first seven hospitalization days. Similar associations were reported in studies from Kenya [22], Egypt [22] and Ethiopia [28]. This may be related to the fact that the first week of newborn development is a critical adaptation period. Special care services should be emphasized to improve the survival of preterm newborns in their first days after birth. In addition, hypothermia was associated with preterm neonatal mortality. Similar findings have been documented in Ethiopia [1] and Sierra Leone [29]. Hypothermia may lead to hypoglycemia, which results in an increased risk of sepsis and ultimately causes death among newborns [30]. The monitoring of temperature and the maintenance of external heat by incubators or radiant heaters are encouraged to reduce the likelihood of hypothermia. Moreover, stable newborns should be admitted to KMC, which also shows that it helps reduce the risk of hypothermia. Sepsis is another determinant of preterm neonatal mortality. This result was higher than that from studies conducted in Ethiopia [1, 26, 28]. Premature newborns have a fragile immune system, which is highly prone to sepsis and at high risk of death [31]. The high odds of mortality may be linked to the different capacities of health facilities in terms of equipment and the level of staff knowledge in managing sepsis among preterm neonates.

This study’s results revealed high odds of mortality for preterm babies admitted to specialized care. This finding is supported by the results of studies conducted in India and Pakistan [32]. This may be because newborns who need specialized care may be admitted to standardized NICUs, which are not available in rural hospitals in Rwanda, and some interventions may be missed. Furthermore, preterm babies who were not cared for in an incubator or with bilirubin light had increased odds of dying. This may be linked to the late decision to care for them in incubators and bilirubin lights, which are vital for consistent temperature, vital sign monitoring, and adequate moisture, increasing the risk of the baby being exposed to pathogens [33]. The screening and monitoring of preterm babies in their first hours after birth is critical and may be recommended. Failure to be admitted to KMC was another significant determinant of mortality in this study. Similar findings were reported in studies conducted at CHUB in Rwanda [34], Ethiopia [35, 36] and Sierra Leone [29]. The increased odds of preterm neonatal mortality may be due to inadequate control of temperature for preterm infants [1]. Respiratory support was also another determinant that was significantly associated with mortality among preterm babies. Increased mortality may be linked to the severity of the condition rather than to respiratory support itself. Depending on the severity of the condition, oxygen and aminophylline, which are used in rural district hospitals, may not be sufficient. In contrast, a study conducted in Uganda and southern Ethiopia reported that respiratory support was a protective factor [22, 26]. Thus, emphasis should be placed on the availability of caffeine and surfactants to improve respiratory outcomes.

Strengths

This study had a sufficient sample size because all the subjects in the study period were considered. This method initially contributed to reducing selection bias and improving the extent to which the study sample represented the study population. This allows for generalizability to similar healthcare settings.

Limitations

This study relied on retrospective data extracted from hospital medical records. For this purpose, variables that were not in the patient files may have been excluded, such as those related to maternal behaviors. By excluding these determinants means that their effect on preterm neonatal mortality was not assessed, and we may recommend further studies on this. It is possible that some preterm newborns died after being discharged from the hospital and these were not included in the study due to the study design.

Conclusion

The proportion of preterm neonatal mortality in the two hospitals was approximately similar to the national figures. The majority of deaths occurred within the first seven days of life. Key determinants were associated with preterm neonatal mortality, including maternal infection, early gestation age at delivery, neonatal sepsis, hypothermia, receiving special care services, inadequate KMC practices, not being cared for in an incubator, and bilirubin light. The findings clearly show that most determinants of preterm neonatal mortality are preventable if improvements are geared toward health system challenges; neonatal/preterm care management, which prioritizes infection prevention and control; medicines such as cafeine and/or equipment availability; and the routine implementation of KMC and thermal regulation strategies.
