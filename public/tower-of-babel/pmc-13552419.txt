DFT and molecular docking insights into barium- and polyacrylic acid-integrated nickel selenide for efficient dye reduction and biological applications
Authors: Anum Shahzadi, Ali Haider, Nelofer Aslam, Hafsa Saeed, Anwar Ul-Hamid, Muhammad Ali Khan, Samira Elaissi, Muhammad Ikram
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The escalating discharge of industrial waste has hampered access to clean and potable water. This study aims to explore the potential use of nanomaterials for eliminating organic pollutants and harmful microbes such as Escherichia coli (E. coli) from wastewater. Nickel selenide (NiSe) doped with varying concentrations (2% and 4%) of barium (Ba2+) and a fixed concentration (3%) of polyacrylic acid (PAA) was synthesized via a coprecipitation route. An array of cutting-edge characterization techniques were employed to analyze the crystallographic structure, morphological features, elemental composition, molecular vibrations and optical properties of the synthesized NSs. 4% Ba/PAA-doped NiSe displayed significant catalytic activity (CA) against rhodamine B (RhB) dye, exhibiting a removal efficiency of 85.37% under neutral conditions, attributed to energy level adjustments and morphological transformation caused by dopants (PAA and Ba2+). Furthermore, a potent bactericidal effect was shown against E. coli, with an inhibition zone of 6.90 mm. The inhibitory effect of Ba/PAA-doped NiSe NSs on DNA gyrase in E. coli was elucidated by molecular docking investigations, which validated their bactericidal activity. The DFT investigation, including MESP, HOMO, and LUMO analyses, provided significant insights into the electronic structure and active compound reactivities.

Introduction

The rapid increase in the global population and industrialization has reduced access to clean and potable water resources. Water pollution has emerged as a demanding global challenge, posing a serious threat to aquatic life and human beings, and has remained a focal point of research, aiming to develop solutions that can mitigate its ecological impacts.1,2 Dye discharge from manufacturing industries has become a point source of water pollution and has given rise to significant health risks affecting various life forms. Organic contaminants like rhodamine B, methylene blue and methyl orange are highly toxic and non-biodegradable, posing a significant danger to aquatic ecosystems and other forms of life.3 Numerous pathogenic bacteria, including multi-drug resistant (MDR) Escherichia coli, are associated with mastitis, the most challenging and costly infection in the global dairy industry.4 The primary pathogen considered to be involved in the disease's clinical form is E. coli. Due to this disease, people may be exposed to zoonotic conditions such as brucellosis, leptospirosis, streptococcal sore throat, and tuberculosis.5 Thus, creating novel anti-microbial agents through experimental or machine learning techniques is imperative due to the rise of MDR bacteria.6 Until now, numerous methods for dye removal have been proposed, including adsorption, photocatalysis and micro-electrolysis. Among these methods, catalysis plays a crucial role in water purification. Its significance has captured the scientific community's interest due to its ability to transform organic substances into inorganic compounds and its practical applicability.7

Metal chalcogenides have recently attracted significant interest for tackling environmental pollution challenges. Representative metal selenides, ZnSe, CdSe, NiSe, and CuSe, are frequently employed as catalysts.8–10 Due to their electronic and structural properties, including band gap, crystallinity, and surface area, as well as their chemical stability, earth abundance, sustainability and cost-effective nature, metal chalcogenides have emerged as promising materials for efficient catalysis. Among them, NiSe, an n-type material, has garnered significant interest as an efficient catalyst to eradicate pollutants from wastewater due to its active chalcogenide atoms, suitable band gap energy, cost-effectiveness, and excellent catalytic activity.11 Nickel selenide exhibits exceptional characteristics that have gained significant interest worldwide. Its appealing magnetic and electronic properties make nickel selenide highly suitable for various applications, including solar cells, conductivity, and sensing.12,13 However, NiSe has limitations such as poor conductivity, and its catalytic efficiency is hindered by fast exciton recombination.11,14 Doping a material with metal ions and carbon-based materials improves its overall catalytic efficiency.15,16 Various polymers, such as polyacrylic acid (PAA), polyvinylpyrrolidone (PVP), and polyvinyl alcohol (PVA), have been utilized as doping materials to address this issue. These polymers are particularly advantageous owing to their affordability and straightforward processing methods. Among the mentioned polymers, polyacrylic acid (PAA) was chosen because of its capability to extract metal ions and eliminate dyes from contaminated water effectively. Additionally, PAA possesses inherent antibacterial properties, attributed to the presence of carboxylic groups (COOH).17 The quantity of active sites available on the surface of nanocrystalline materials can be maximized by strategically engineering the surface structure, increasing the reactivity of a nanocatalyst.18 Hence, more active sites enable the nanocatalyst to degrade dye effectively. Metal ion doping is an efficient strategy to enhance the overall catalytic efficacy of a material. This enhancement in the material activity is attributed to the dopant-induced electronic modification.19 Barium (Ba) is an alkaline earth metal and has often been employed as a catalyst, as it enhances active sites to improve the degradation rate.20

Lakshmi reported 92% degradation of methylene blue (MB) after 120 min using NiSe NPs and a 55.5% antibacterial efficacy against E. coli with an inhibition zone of 10 mm.21 Sibghat Ullah Khan, in his study, reported that the NiSe2–ZnO composite degrades 97% of MB within 300 min.22 Alam S. M. Nur reported that the ZnO-char-PAA nanocomposite shows RhB degradation efficiencies of 78.33% and 92.54% under sunlight and UV irradiation, correspondingly.23 Nguyen Thi Quynh Anh reported that TiO2-PAA-GO can remove 98.16% of the DB-71 dye in 90 min 24. Behnaz Shirdel reported that Ba-doped ZnO achieves a RhB removal efficiency of 98.40% in 68 min 25. Maryam Al-Ejji revealed that Ba increases the degradation efficiency of TiO2 from 20% to 81% for azocarmine G and from 43% to 97% for benzopurpurin dye.26 M. H. Ghozza reported that Bi0.9Ba0.1FeO3 NPs achieve 96.5% degradation of CR dye.27 Based on the above-mentioned observations, Ba/PAA-NiSe was prepared, and its potential for degrading RhB and inhibiting bacterial growth was assessed. Ba and PAA were incorporated into NiSe to obtain more active sites and enhanced material performance. After a comprehensive assessment involving structural characteristics, morphological attributes, optical properties, antibacterial effectiveness, and catalytic activity, the underlying factors contributing to the enhanced catalytic and bactericidal efficacy of Ba/PAA-NiSe were thoroughly addressed. 4% Ba/PAA-NiSe achieved a RhB removal efficiency of 85.37% in 6 min and an inhibition zone of 6.90 mm against E. coli with a bactericidal efficacy of 84.6%.

Experimental details

Chemicals

Ni(NO3)2·6H2O, Se powder, polyacrylic acid, BaCl2·2H2O and N2H4·H2O were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (Germany). Each chemical reagent had analytical-grade purity and was utilized without further purification.

Ba/PAA-doped NiSe synthesis

NiSe was synthesized via a co-precipitation route using Ni(NO3)2·6H2O and the Se powder as Ni and Se sources, respectively. 0.6 M Ni(NO3)2 and the Se powder were mixed under constant stirring and heating at 120 °C for 10 min. To attain precipitates, N2H4·H2O (1 M) was incorporated dropwise, and pH 10 was maintained. Afterwards, the colloidal solution was centrifuged at 7000 rpm, heated overnight at 150 °C and ground to a fine powder. A fixed concentration of PAA (3%) was added to the previously mentioned solution of Ni and Se precursors, stirred and heated at 120 °C to obtain a PAA, Ni(NO3)2, and Se solution. Correspondingly, varying concentrations of Ba (2 and 4 wt%) were added to the solution of PAA, Ni(NO3)2, and Se to synthesize (2% and 4%) Ba/PAA-NiSe, respectively. The same methodology used for NiSe synthesis was repeated to obtain fine powders of PAA-NiSe and Ba/PAA/NiSe (Fig. 1). Sample 1 was reused in this study, and few characterization data were taken from our previously published work (reproduced with permission,28 copyright RSC 2024).

Schematic of the Ba/PAA-NiSe synthesis.

Catalysis

The catalytic performance of NiSe, PAA/NiSe, and (2% and 4%) Ba/PAA-NiSe was evaluated by analyzing their efficacy in degrading RhB into colorless leuco-RhB (LRhB), facilitated by a reducing agent, sodium borohydride (NaBH4). Specifically, 0.1 M NaBH4 (400 µL) and 3 mL of RhB were mixed in a cuvette, and the synthesized nanocatalyst (400 µL) was added to the solution of NaBH4 and RhB. The RhB reduction over a specific duration was assessed by continuously monitoring the absorbance spectra of the solution using a UV-Vis spectrophotometer. Introducing NaBH4 and the nanocatalyst (NCs) synergistically facilitated the successful conversion of RhB to LRhB.

Separation of MDR E. coli

Sample collection

Cows designated for nursing purposes, available across farms and veterinary clinics in various regions of Pakistan, underwent a direct milking process, with milk samples collected in aseptic glass containers. Immediately upon collection, the milk samples were stored at 4 °C in the laboratory. By employing MacConkey agar (MA), enumeration of coliform bacteria in unpasteurized milk was accomplished. At 37 °C, the culture plates underwent incubation for 48 hours.

Characterization and identification of bacterial isolates

Preliminary E. coli identification was in compliance with Bergey's Manual of Determinative Bacteriology and relied on assessing the colony morphology through Gram staining, accompanied by various biochemical assays.29

Antibiotic susceptibility

Mueller–Hinton agar (MHA) served as the medium to assess antibiotic susceptibility via the diffusion technique outlined by Bauer et al.30 The potential resistance of E. coli was ascertained against antibiotics (categories); 1 (Gm) 10 µg (aminoglycosides), 2 (Te) 30 µg (tetracyclines), 3 (A) 30 µg (penicillins), 4 (Cro) 30 µg (cephalosporins), 5 (Cip) 5 µg (quinolones), 6 (Imi) 10 µg (carbapenem) and 7 (Azm) 15 µg (macrolides).31 Purified cultures of E. coli were cultivated, and turbidity was tuned to 0.5 McFarland standards. Subsequently, the cultures were evenly spread onto MHA (Oxoid Limited, Basingstoke, UK). The potential overlap of inhibitory zones was avoided by careful positioning of antibiotic discs at a distance from the inoculated plate. Following a 24 hour incubation period (at 37 °C), the results were interpreted in accordance with the Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute.32 A bacterium that exhibited resistance to a minimum of three drugs was classified as multi-drug resistant (MDR).33 Antibiotics such as gentamicin, tetracycline, amoxicillin, ceftriaxone, ciprofloxacin, imipenem and azithromycin were denoted with numerals 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6 and 7, respectively.

Bactericidal activity

The in vitro antimicrobial effectiveness of Ba/PAA-NiSe was assessed via the agar well diffusion method on 10 selected MDR E. coli isolates originating from infected milk. MA plates were inoculated with 1.5 × 108 CFU mL−1 (0.5 McFarland standard) of multi-drug resistant (MDR) E. coli using sterile swabs. Depressions having 6 mm diameters were created by employing a sterilized cork borer. There were two different Ba/PAA-NiSe concentrations used: minimal (0.5 mg/50 µL) and maximal (1.0 mg/50 µL). The positive control was ciprofloxacin (0.005 mg/50 µL), while deionized water (50 µL) functioned as the negative control.34

Statistical analysis

By measuring the inhibitory zone sizes (mm), a statistical study of the bactericidal action was performed. The diameter of inhibition zones was measured through SPSS 20 software using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA).35

Molecular docking analysis

Numerous nanocomposite technologies have shown effectiveness, and their therapeutic potential necessitates further inquiry to determine the precise mechanism by which they induce their beneficial effects. Computational approaches have enabled researchers to conduct thorough analysis of numerous interactions. Our research aimed to perform molecular docking investigations in order to evaluate their potential as inhibitors targeting a particular enzyme. The three-dimensional conformations of DNA gyraseE. coli were acquired from the Protein Data Bank using accession number 6L01.36 The three-dimensional structures of compounds under inquiry were analyzed using the Sybyl-X2.0/SKETCH module.37,38 Following that, energy minimization was conducted with the Tripos force field and the Gasteiger–Hückel atomic charge. The purpose of these simulations was to investigate the binding interactions that take place between NSs and the active site residues of certain proteins, as also studied previously for other NSs.39

Results and discussion

The crystal structure, plane orientation, and phase purity of the as-synthesized NiSe and Ba/PAA-doped NiSe NSs were inspected by analyzing the diffraction patterns acquired from XRD analysis in the 2θ range of 20–60° (Fig. 2a). The orthorhombic structure of NiSe was identified by the presence of distinctive diffraction peaks at 2θ = 31.9° (042), 34.9° (131), 37.9° (115), 43.8° (135), 45.1° (028), 46.7° (151), 50.9° (118) and 55.8° (119), consistent with JCPDS no. 00-029-0935. The Ni6Se5 orthorhombic phase of NiSe was detected by peaks positioned at 2θ = 22.3° (122), 25.2° (132), 27.0° (201), 33.4° (114), 41.2° (163) and 51.9° (264), confirmed by JCPDS no. 00-029-0934. The diffraction peaks at 23.4° (110) and 29.9° (020) corresponded to the orthorhombic structure of NiSe2, consistent with JCPDS no. 00-018-0886. Peak broadening, along with a reduction in peak intensity, was observed after the introduction of PAA, attributed to its amorphous characteristics.37,40 The mismatched ionic radii compelled Ba2+ to occupy interstitial sites and enabled the translocation of the diffraction pattern towards lower angles. The intensity of diffraction peaks further decreased after the integration of barium ions.41 However, the intensity of the (201) plane increased with increasing dopants. Selected area electron diffraction (SAED) analysis of both the pristine and Ba/PAA-NiSe samples displayed circular bright rings corresponding to the lattice planes identified by XRD earlier (Fig. 2b and c). The luminous points making up the bright rings indicated the polycrystalline behavior of Ba/PAA-NiSe.

(a) X-ray crystallographic patterns of doped and undoped NiSe. (b and c) SAED patterns of NiSe (reproduced with permission,28 copyright RSC 2024) and 4% Ba/PAA-doped NiSe, respectively.

Molecular vibrations and functional groups associated with dopant-free (NiSe) and doped NiSe samples were corroborated from FTIR spectra (Fig. 3a). The stretching vibration related to NiSe appeared at 1465 cm−1.42 The molecular vibrations corresponding to NiSe were observed within 500–850 cm−1. The bands at ∼500 and 600 cm−1 were assigned to the asymmetrical stretching vibrational frequency of metal-selenium bonds. The subtle band at 3558 cm−1 was attributed to the resonance frequency of the –OH groups linked with NiSe structural moieties and peripheral hydrogen (–H) atoms.21 The transmittance bands at 816, 980, 1432, 1632, 1741.17 and 2950 cm−1 were attributed to CH bending, CH2 twisting, CH2 scissoring, carboxylic acid and methylene group vibrational modes of PAA, respectively, providing evidence of the surface functionalization of NiSe NSs.43–45 The bands observed between 2200 and 2400 cm−1 could be associated with CO2 vibrations in the prepared samples.46 Raman spectroscopy stands out as an exceptionally versatile method for examining the introduction of dopants, the crystallographic arrangement, and lattice imperfections within the original lattice structure. The Raman spectra of the prepared NSs are illustrated in Fig. 3b. The identified peaks were located at approximately 237 and 257 cm−1. The peak around 237 cm−1 corresponded to the Tg mode of NiSe, while the peak centered at 257 cm−1 was associated with the Eg mode.47,48 PAA addition clearly resulted in a discernible intensification of distinctive peaks and a positional variation in Raman modes, implying metal ion disordering and the presence of vacancies and lattice defects.49 After the incorporation of an alkaline metal, the Raman peak intensity decreased gradually and slightly shifted toward higher wavenumbers. Ba integration altered the defects and disorder, including vacant lattice sites, leading to lattice distortion and a reduction in lattice space symmetry.50 The optical properties and band gap energy (Eg) of NiSe and doped NiSe were monitored using a UV-Vis spectrophotometer (Fig. 3c). The translocation of electrons between the valence band (either 5s2 of Ni or 4p6 of Se) and unoccupied conduction band imparted semiconductor characteristics to the nanomaterials during the conformation of ionic solid NiSe. The UV-Vis spectrum of NiSe exhibited noticeable absorption peaks at 306 nm and 385 nm owing to quantum confinement from the bulk counterpart (620 nm).13,51 Following the introduction of dopants (PAA and Ba), a perceptible shift of the absorption spectra towards lower wavelengths (a blue shift) was noted, along with a rise in the Eg values.52,53 Using the Tauc plot and by virtue of the straight line equation, the Eg values of the prepared samples were determined to be 4.03–4.34 eV and 3.3–3.58 eV (Fig. S1a–d).51,54

EDS analysis was used to scrutinize the elemental composition of Ba/PAA-NiSe (Fig. S2a). The presence of Ni, Se, C, O and Ba peaks elucidated the successful formation of Ba/PAA-NiSe. Additionally, nitrogen (N) and Cl peaks were identified because of the use of Ni(NO)3 and Ba(Cl)2 as Ni and Ba precursors during synthesis, respectively. Fig. S2b–i depicts the elemental mapping of Ba/PAA-NiSe. Seven distinct elements (Ni, Se, C, O, Ba, N and Cl) were detected; each represented by a unique color, displaying an even distribution of elements in the sample.

The morphology of the synthesized materials at the nanoscale was investigated through TEM analysis. The pure sample (NiSe) exhibited agglomerated staggered vesicular-like nanostructures, as depicted in Fig. 4a. After introducing PAA (capping agent) into NiSe, a significant level of capping was observed (Fig. 4b). The incorporation of barium into the binary system of PAA/NiSe resulted in the creation of nanorods surrounded by the binary system, and small bead-like nanostructures appeared (Fig. 4c). The extent of agglomeration decreased as the quantity of barium (Ba) increased (Fig. 4d). Interestingly, the dimensions of the nanorods exhibited an increase upon doping with barium.

Lattice fringes were clearly observed in the HR-TEM micrographs of pure and doped NiSe with d-spacings of 0.356, 0.357, 0.358 and 0.358 nm, respectively (Fig. 5a–d).

For the catalysis study, a UV-visible spectrophotometer was employed to analyze the impact of pure and Ba/PAA-NiSe samples on the reduction of rhodamine B (RhB). Catalytically, a redox reaction is the underlying mechanism in the reduction of RhB dye, where RhB is an oxidizing species, and NaBH4 is an electron-donating species. However, when the reduction of the dye was attempted solely with NaBH4, undesirable reaction kinetics was observed. To rectify this problem, pure and doped NiSe were introduced into the system, which effectively lowered the energy barrier and boosted the charge transfer rate. The inclusion of NSs functioned as NCs, aiding the transfer of electrons from the source (NaBH4) to the sink (RhB) (Fig. 6). On the surface of NCs, the transfer of electrons and H-atoms turned RhB dye into a reduced colorless form, leuco-rhodamine B (LRhB).55,56

Mechanisms of RhB reduction using 4% Ba/PAA-doped NiSe NSs.

To assess the catalytic efficiency of (2% and 4%) Ba/PAA-NiSe, a range of RhB solutions, such as neutral, acidic and basic solutions, was prepared. In the absence of the catalyst, the RhB removal efficacy was 15.56%, 17.72%, and 20.80% in acidic, basic, and neutral media, respectively (Fig. S3). Pristine and doped NiSe demonstrated reduction rates of 58.33%, 57.85%, 67.28%, and 70.28% in an acidic medium, 60.20%, 67.85%, 71.42% and 76.42% in a basic medium and 59.71%, 69.28%, 81.28% and 85.37% in a neutral medium for a period of 6 min, respectively (Fig. 7a–c). The catalytic efficacy is predominantly controlled by factors like the crystallite size and surface area of the synthesized NSs, the pH of the medium and the nature of dopants (PAA and Ba). Under neutral conditions, the 4% Ba/PAA-NiSe sample showed an enhancement in rhodamine B (RhB) reduction, which was attributed to the strong electrostatic interaction between the positively charged dye and the oppositely charged NCs. The reduction efficacy was notably lower in acidic media than in basic and neutral media. A protonated environment triggered the repulsion of dye molecules and decreased their adsorption on the surface of the prepared nanostructures.57,58 The addition of dopants (PAA and Ba) inhibited the dimensional growth of NiSe NSs; consequently, this alteration increased the number of active adsorption sites for dye molecules. The inclusion of PAA introduced carboxylic groups, and a higher concentration of carboxylic groups resulted in an increased number of active adsorption sites, facilitating the removal of dyes.59 The RhB removal kinetics was examined using first- and second-order reaction kinetics, as shown in Fig. S4. The acquired results indicated that the correlation coefficients (R2 = 0.972 and 0.989) fitted well with the first-order reaction kinetics.

The antibacterial potency of pure and doped NiSe against the bacterial strain E. coli was probed by the agar well diffusion method. Antibacterial activity was assessed using growth inhibition zones, measured in millimeters (mm), as presented in Table 1. Notably, the maximal dosage (1.0 mg/50 µL) of pure and doped NiSe demonstrated inhibition zones of 3.35, 3.95, 5.65 and 6.90 mm, while at the minimal dosage (0.5 mg/50 µL), inhibition zones of 1.75, 2.95, 3.75 and 5.15 mm, respectively, were observed. The credibility of bactericidal activity was checked by comparing the results with ciprofloxacin and DIW, exhibiting inhibition zones of 8.15 and 0 mm, correspondingly.

Antibacterial behavior of Ba/PAA-NiSe against E. coli

Multifarious mechanisms have been proposed to elucidate the bacterial inhibition effect of nickel selenide NSs (Fig. S3). Possible mechanisms are the formation of ROS, release of metal ions, piercing of the plasma membrane with NiSe NSs and electrostatic interaction of NSs with DNA and proteins. ROS destroy cell membranes and interfere with intracellular metabolism, resulting in the suppression of bacterial growth and cell death. ROS include singlet oxygen, hydroxyl radicals and superoxide anion radicals. Malfunctioning DNA and proteins prohibit gene expression, which meritoriously circumvents the resistance mechanism of drug-resistant bacteria. The literature suggests the influence of certain factors, including the surface charge, shape and size of NiSe NSs, on the bacterial inhibition effectiveness.21,60 In comparison, the doped samples displayed improved antibacterial efficacy owing to their smaller crystallite size. The synergistic effect of dopants (carboxylic acid groups and barium ions) prompted the generation of ROS and release of metallic Ni2+ ions, which can effectively kill bacterial strains.61–63

The current study used molecular docking as a preliminary computational analysis to investigate the potential interactions of the synthesized NSs with the active site residues of DNA gyrase enzymeE. coli (Fig. 8a). Consequently, the docking results provide qualitative corroborative evidence of possible binding interactions instead of conclusive confirmation of enzyme inhibition. PAA-doped NiSe NSs created the best-docked complex with a binding score of 5.02, demonstrating significant binding interactions with the Thr165 residues of the active site of DNA gyraseE. coli (Fig. 8b). Ba/PAA-doped NiSe NSs had H-bond interactions with Val43 and Thr165, having the highest docking score of 6.12 (Fig. 8c). Ciprofloxacin exhibited H-bonds with Val43, as depicted in Fig. 8d.

DFT studies and MESP and HOMO–LUMO analyses

The electronic aspects of the Ba/PAA–NiSe complex were examined through density functional theory (DFT) in both gaseous and aqueous phases to evaluate its potential reactivity and stability across various environments (Fig. 9). This was achieved through the B3LYP functional in conjunction with the LanL2DZ basis set, as implemented in Gaussian 09 software (Revision E.01), adhering to recognized approaches.64,65 The HOMO–LUMO energy gap revealed a notable rise from 1.499 eV (gas) to 2.903 eV in an aqueous solution, indicating a reduction in chemical reactivity and an enhancement in kinetic stability in polar media. An evident rise in the dipole moment was noted during solvation, suggesting higher polarity and interaction, potentially improving the compound's solubility and biological compatibility with physiological surroundings, further corroborated by estimated global reactivity descriptors. The spots depicted in deep red on MESP maps signify areas with electronegative potentials, underscoring zones that are susceptible to electrophilic and nucleophilic interactions with molecules, which are essential for optimal binding. The selenium atoms within the Ba/PAA-NiSe ring displayed average Mulliken charges of −0.830359 and −1.120866, implying substantial involvement of negatively charged regions in both gas and aqueous phases. The docking results suggested that the presence of oxygen atoms markedly enhanced the potential for hydrogen bonding within both hinge and solvent-exposed areas of g-Ba/PAA-NiSe. The surface's verdant coloration indicated neutral regions, which could potentially influence hydrophobic or van der Waals interactions. Overall, the HOMO–LUMO and MEP distributions of g-Ba/PAA-NiSe indicated electrically active areas that might promote interactions with bacterial proteins, aligning with the robust binding affinity in molecular docking and antibacterial efficacy observed experimentally. The lowered ionization potential (IP) and electron affinity (EA) values in the aqueous phase indicated impaired charge transfer. While softness (S) reduced, hardness (η) increased in the water phase (1.452 eV compared to 0.749 eV), suggesting a less reactive and more rigid system (Table 2). The electrochemical potential (µ) shifted to less negative values in the aqueous medium, reflecting changes in chemical potential due to solvation effects. The electrophilicity index, reflecting the ligand's ability to accept electrons, was notably reduced under aqueous conditions, suggesting a diminished propensity to accept electrons under polar conditions.

The observed behavior holds importance in forecasting molecular interactions with biological or polar surroundings, as it enhances stability in the aqueous phase and diminishes reactivity. This screening explores intricate structural and functional activities, thereby promoting the progression of nanostructures designed to improve therapeutic efficacy. The results indicate that Ba/PAA-NiSe exhibits enhanced stability and diminished reactivity under aqueous conditions, which may improve its applicability in biomedical or catalytic systems where solvent interactions are essential for optimal functionality. Consequently, DFT computations provide electronic-level validation for the enhanced efficacy of Ba/PAA-doped NiSe.

Conclusion

In this research investigation, the coprecipitation route was adopted to synthesize (2% and 4%) Ba/PAA-doped NiSe NSs, and their potential as a dye degrader and an antibacterial agent was evaluated. XRD analysis verified the orthorhombic phase of NiSe, and the polycrystalline nature of the prepared samples was confirmed by SAED analysis. Upon the introduction of PAA, a reduction in crystallinity was observed owing to the polymer's amorphous nature. UV-Vis absorption spectra unveiled a blue shift, attributed to an increment in the band gap energy (Eg) from 4 to 4.34 eV. FTIR analysis confirmed the functional group moieties in the prepared NSs. An efficient reduction performance was observed against RhB, achieving 85.37%, 70.28%, and 76.42% reduction in neutral, acidic, and basic media, respectively. Ba/PAA-NiSe showed an inhibition zone of 6.90 mm against E. coli. The DFT studies, in conjunction with MESP and HOMO–LUMO analyses, yielded profound insights into electronic properties and active compound reactivity. This study concludes that the Ba/PAA-NiSe NSs demonstrate significant potential in combating pathogens and facilitating the catalytic reduction of organic contaminants.
