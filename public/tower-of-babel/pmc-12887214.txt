Differential Performance of Polygenic Score for Coronary Artery Disease Based on Coronary Artery Calcium Between Men Living With and Without HIV
Authors: Genevieve Wojcik, Marios Arvanitis, Sabina Haberlen, Eun‐Young Kim, Heejin Kim, Kireem Nam, Matthew J. Feinstein, Steven Wolinsky, Joseph B. Margolick, Matthew Budoff, Jared W. Magnani, Priya Duggal, Wendy S. Post
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundIndividuals with HIV have increased risk for coronary artery disease (CAD). However, the utility of the polygenic risk score (PRS) for estimating CAD risk (PRSCAD) is unknown in people living with HIV (PLWH). We evaluated the performance of PRSCAD on coronary artery calcium (CAC), a measure of subclinical atherosclerosis, by HIV serostatus.MethodsA PRSCAD was applied to 1071 men (PLWH, n=659; people living without HIV [PWOH], n=412) within MACS (Multicenter AIDS Cohort Study), then stratified by non‐Hispanic White or Black race and genetic similarity to references (non‐Hispanic White/genetically similar to European reference group, n=731; non‐Hispanic Black/genetically similar to African reference group, n=340). PRSCAD performance for CAC, estimated by Agatston score (AS) from cardiac computed tomography scans, was evaluated independently and jointly with the American College of Cardiology/American Heart Association Pooled Cohort Equations by HIV serostatus using adjusted R 2 for log‐transformed CAC and area under the curve (AUC) for binary outcomes (absence/presence of CAC, absence of CAC [AS=0] versus low CAC [AS=1–100] or high CAC [AS >100]).ResultsPredicting the presence/absence of CAC in non‐Hispanic White/genetically similar to European reference group adding PRSCAD significantly improved the performance compared with the base model (AUCPLWH =0.738, AUCPWOH =0.759; P=0.015) but not the Pooled Cohort Equations (AUCPLWH =0.722, AUCPWOH =0.736; P=0.26). Performance was lower in PLWH than PWOH among outcomes, especially comparing high with absent AS (AUCPLWH =0.785, AUCPWOH =0.865; P=0.025). Joint modeling of PRSCAD and Pooled Cohort Equations decreased these gaps by HIV serostatus. PRSCAD performed worse in non‐Hispanic Black/genetically similar to African reference groups compared with non‐Hispanic White/genetically similar to European reference groups, with lower performance in PLWH (AUCPLWH =0.671, AUCPWOH =0.769; P=0.22).ConclusionsThe application of a PRSCAD to CAC showed lower performance for PLWH than PWOH men. Further research is needed to develop accurate risk prediction models with clinical utility in all patients.

HIV infection, when treated, is a chronic inflammatory disease that affects more than a million individuals in the United States and many more worldwide. Although treatments have been developed that have transformed HIV disease from a rapidly fatal illness to a chronic condition, people living with HIV (PLWH) have a higher lifetime risk of coronary artery disease (CAD) than people living without HIV (PWOH), which is not eliminated by current therapies. 1 Therefore, developing effective tools to predict CAD risk in individuals with HIV is particularly important.

The polygenic risk score (PRS) has emerged as a valuable tool for identifying individuals at risk for various chronic diseases early in life, particularly diseases with high prevalence, significant societal impact, and available preventive measures. 2 Indeed, multiple research groups have developed a PRS for CAD (PRSCAD), with varying success. 3 , 4 Recent efforts to expand PRSCAD to include millions of variants genome‐wide with appropriate adjustment for linkage disequilibrium have shown promise but remain limited in their validation among diverse populations with respect to genetic ancestry and clinical contexts. 5

Despite its potential, the role of PRSCAD in clinical practice is still uncertain, and many inherent limitations of PRS hinder its widespread implementation. One of the most important limitations is the relatively poor performance of PRS in different populations. This is particularly relevant for individuals of different ancestries. 6 The PRSCAD is known to underperform in individuals of African and East Asian ancestry, 7 reflecting the overall limited representation of these populations in genome‐wide association studies used to generate these scores. 8 Recent research suggests that this variability in the predictive power of PRS extends beyond ancestry to other potential interactors such as age, sex, and comorbid conditions. 9

The aim of this study was to evaluate the performance of PRSCAD in predicting atherosclerotic risk for PLWH. To address this question, we evaluated the relative performance of PRSCAD in predicting coronary artery calcium (CAC), a subclinical marker of coronary atherosclerosis strongly associated with risk for future CAD events, in individuals with and without HIV participating in MACS (Multicenter AIDS Cohort Study). 10 We compared its performance with that of a nongenetic clinical risk calculator (American College of Cardiology/American Heart Association [ACC/AHA] Pooled Cohort Equations [PCE] 11 ).

METHODS

Data Availability

Because of the sensitive nature of the data collected for this study, requests to access the data that support the findings of this study from qualified researchers trained in human subject confidentiality protocols may be sent to the corresponding author. Requests will be evaluated on reasonable request. The code used to conduct this study is available on request.

Study Population

Participants for this analysis were included in a cardiac computed tomography (CT) substudy of MACS. MACS is a well‐characterized longitudinal, prospective observational cohort study of men who have sex with men which includes both PLWH and PWOH in Baltimore, MD/Washington, DC; Chicago, IL; Los Angeles, CA; Columbus, OH; and Pittsburgh, PA. 12 The MACS is now part of the Multicenter AIDS Cohort Study / Women’s Interagency HIV Study Combined Cohort Study (MWCCS). Data from men who participated in any of 3 substudies were included in this analysis: CVD1 (2004–2006), 13 , 14 CVD2 (2010–2013 15 ), or CVD3 (2015–2017 16 ). The most recent visit data were used for men who participated in multiple CT examinations. Enrollment criteria for the CT substudy included age between 40 and 70 years, weight below 300 pounds, and no history of cardiac surgery, coronary artery angioplasty, or stent placement. MACS and the MACS CVD ancillary studies were approved by the institutional review boards of all participating sites. Participants were recruited from active participants at the 4 MACS centers and all participants signed informed consent for the MACS ancillary study.

Phenotype Data

As part of the CT substudy, CAC was measured from noncontrast cardiac CT scans and quantified using the Agatston score (AS) at a centralized research CT reading center (Harbor, UCLA) with readers blinded to participant characteristics. The AS was then transformed into 3 different variables for this analysis: continuous measure [log (CAC+1)], a binary measure of presence (AS >0) or absence (AS=0) of CAC, and a categorical variable of absent CAC (AS=0), low CAC (AS >0 to AS=100), and moderate‐high CAC (AS >100). Atherosclerotic CVD risk was calculated using the ACC/AHA PCE from risk factors measured during the MACS clinical research examination among participants without missing data (n=685, non‐Hispanic White/genetically similar to European reference group [EUR]; n=325, non‐Hispanic Black/genetically similar to African reference group [AFR]) (Table S1).

Genetic Data

Participants were genotyped on Illumina’s Infinium Multi‐Ethnic Genotyping Array (MEGA) using Genome Reference Consortium Human Build 37 (GRCh37/hg19) with a total of 1,703,624 markers in the full MACS sample of 6169 male individuals. The overall genotyping rate was 99% and no samples had missing rates >3%. Chromosome X inbreeding coefficients were examined to estimate the sex of the samples; all individuals were classified as male as expected, with no outliers. Relatedness was estimated in Plink with no pairwise relationships of a PI_HAT>0.4; therefore, no individuals were removed for being first‐degree relatives or duplicates. Genetic similarity was inferred using principal components (PCs) using a subset of nonlinked markers within all MACS samples pooled, with the 1000 Genomes Project Phase 3 Data (1000Genomes) as the reference data set. Clusters were determined using 1000Genomes designations and were utilized in further checks for Hardy–Weinberg equilibrium (P<1×10−5; M=71) and heterozygosity outliers (>3 SD from the mean; n=37), as well as allele frequency inconsistencies (minor allele frequency >1%; M=783,363). After quality control steps, a total of 914,038 markers had a genotyping rate >98% and minor allele frequency >1%. Using this scaffold, individual genotypes were then imputed to the TOPMed reference panel using the TOPMed Imputation Server. 17 , 18 , 19 Participants for the analyses reported in this article were subset at this point in the quality control process during the merge with phenotype information as detailed below.

Race, Ethnicity, and Genetic Similarity Subsamples

Participants were stratified by both their self‐identified race and ethnicity and genetic similarity clusters as determined through the cohort‐wide PC analysis. Two groups were defined in this study. The first included individuals who identified as non‐Hispanic White (n=739) and clustered among European ancestry individuals in the PC analysis (EUR; n=741); these were denoted non‐Hispanic White/EUR (n=731). The second represented individuals who identified as non‐Hispanic Black (n=347) and clustered among African ancestry individuals (AFR; n=348); these were denoted as non‐Hispanic Black/AFR (n=340) (Table S2, Figures S1 and S2). Individuals who did not fit into these groups were excluded from further analyses (n=109) to provide a level of homogeneity both in terms of genetic architecture with respect to allele frequencies and linkage disequilibrium patterns, as well as potential environmental sources of heterogeneity. To ensure clarity as to how these groups were defined, these acronyms including both their racial and ethnic identities and genetic ancestry clusters are used throughout the article in accordance with a recent report on population descriptors in genetic studies. 20

Polygenic Risk Score

The polygenic score for CAD from Khera et al (2018) 5 was downloaded from the PGS Catalog on May 4, 2021, under ID PGS000013. Coordinates for PRSCAD were converted from GRCh37/hg19 to GRCh38 using liftover, and individual scores were computed in Plink. 21 A total of 6,483,297 variants in PRSCAD were included. Individual scores were adjusted for the first 10 PCs to account for population substructure biasing the PRS distribution, and the residuals were normalized to a mean of 0 and SD of 1. The distributions of both adjusted and unadjusted risk scores were evaluated both in the pooled sample and stratified by HIV status (Figures S3–S10). A polygenic score for coronary artery calcification 22 was downloaded from the PGS Catalog on May 28, 2025, under ID PGS005096 and applied to the data. This PRSCAC contained 11 variants previously selected from a genome‐wide association study of coronary artery calcification. 23 One variant, rs11063120 (chr12:4377452; β=0.1331, P=2.78×10−9), was not present in our data, so the SNP with the second smallest P value under the peak, rs11063116 (chr12:4372205; β=0.1355, P=1.68×10−6), was substituted for this variant. As with PRSCAD, individual scores were adjusted for the first 10 PCs and the residuals were normalized to a mean of 0 and an SD of 1. All results are presented in Table S3.

Statistical Analysis

All statistical analyses were conducted within R version 4.2.2 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing). Adjusted and partial R 2 values were estimated using the “rsq” package (https://cran.r‐project.org/web/packages/rsq/index.html) and area under the [receiver operating characteristic] curve (AUC) in the package pROC (https://cran.r‐project.org/web/packages/pROC/). The distributions of adjusted PRS and log‐transformed CAC (AS) were evaluated and assessed for correlation (Figures S11 and S12). For all models, the base model included age, sex, CT scanning site, and substudy (CVD1–3). Binary outcomes (absence or presence of CAC; absent versus low or high levels of CAC) were modeled with logistic regression. The continuous measure of CAC [log (CAC+1)] was modeled with linear regression. The statistical tests for evaluating differences between models and the estimation of AUC CIs were estimated using DeLong test. ANOVA F test was used to test differences between adjusted R 2 values of models. A P value of 0.05 was used to determine statistical significance. Integrated discrimination index (IDI) was utilized because of the lack of a clear cutoff for the use of this PRS with CAC and was estimated using the “PredictABEL” package (https://cran.r‐project.org/web/packages/PredictABEL/index.html).

Reporting Guidelines

The STROBE (Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology) cohort reporting guidelines were used throughout this article to ensure compliance in reporting for observational studies. 24

RESULTS

Performance of PRS for CAD Differences by HIV Status When Predicting CAC

A PRS previously developed to predict CAD in European ancestry participants 5 was applied to a total of 731 MACS participants who had undergone noncontrast cardiac CT scans for measurement of CAC and who both self‐identified as being of non‐Hispanic White race and were within the European genetic ancestry cluster. Among these participants, 424 were PLWH and 307 were PWOH (Table 1, Table S1). The standardized PRSCAD was higher on average among PWOH than PLWH (9.96×10−2 and −7.21×10−2, respectively; P=0.02). After adjustment for age, CT scanning center, and substudy (CVD1–3), the difference remained statistically significant (P=0.02). Three outcome definitions were assessed for the performance of PRSCAD based on the CAC AS: presence or absence of CAC (AS >0 versus AS=0), log‐transformed AS, and categorical bins of absent (AS=0), mild (AS >0–100), and moderate to severe (AS >100) CAC. While a higher mean AS was observed in PWOH (24.9; interquartile range [IQR], 0–205.5) than in PLWH (22.9; IQR, 0–163.9), this was largely due to older age distribution. After adjustment for age (as well as substudy and CT scanning center) we observed higher mean log‐transformed AS in PLWH than PWOH (β=0.187 [95% CI, 0.036–0.337]; P=0.015).

For presence and absence of CAC, a base model including age, CT scanning center, and substudy had an AUC of 0.738 (Figure 1; Table 2). With the addition of PRSCAD, the AUC increased to 0.761 (P=0.007). However, stratified analyses revealed that PLWH had a lower AUC of 0.750 compared with PWOH (AUC=0.810), although this difference was not statistically significant (P=0.088). The same analysis using the PRS for CAC (PRSCAC) yielded a similar trend with weaker results, with an AUC increase to 0.747 (P=0.12) in the pooled analysis and higher performance in PWOH (AUC=0.802) versus PLWH (AUC=0.734) (Table S3). On a continuous scale, the addition of PRSCAD to the base model predicting log‐transformed CAC increased the adjusted R 2 value from 0.180 to 0.213 (partial R 2=0.040, P ANOVA=3.11×10−8). As with presence/absence, this increase was mostly driven by better performance in PWOH, with PLWH having lower performance (adjusted R 2=0.185 compared with 0.281 in PWOH). In addition, an interaction term between PLWH versus PWOH and the PRSCAD was not statistically significant (P=0.70) but was consistent with a lower effect size among PLWH (Table S4). The same analyses with PRSCAC found substantially poorer performance, with the addition of PRSCAC to the base model leading to an increased adjusted R 2 to 0.189 (partial R 2=0.011, P ANOVA=0.002) and a null interaction term between PLWH versus PWOH and the PRSCAC (P=0.24).

These differences became even more apparent considering the extremes of CAC. Using categorical definitions, the difference in model fit between PLWH and PWOH was minimal when comparing those with low AS with those with an absence of CAC (AUCs of 0.714 versus 0.747, respectively; P=0.49). However, when comparing those with high CAC (AS >100) with those with absent CAC, the difference by HIV status was much greater (AUCs of 0.785 versus 0.865, respectively; P=0.03). These trends were also recapitulated in the effect sizes comparing the top decile of the PRSCAD distribution with the remainder of the individuals, where the strongest effect sizes were found in PWOH (log‐transformed CAC: β=0.488 [95% CI, 0.145–0.831]; P=0.006) and comparing high CAC (AS >100) with absence (AS=0) in both the pooled sample (odds ratio [OR], 1.94 [95% CI, 1.15–3.26]; P=0.013) and PWOH (OR, 2.45 [95% CI; 1.12–5.42], P=0.025) (Table S5). Again, much weaker effect sizes were observed using the PRS for CAC (Table S6). Because of this weaker performance among all outcomes for PRSCAC, all subsequent analyses were conducted using only PRSCAD.

Addition of PRS to PCE Narrows the Gap by HIV Status

We then compared the performance of PRSCAD with that of known traditional clinical risk factors as measured by the 2013 ACC/AHA ASCVD Risk Calculator, known as the PCE, for the presence or absence of CAC. In the pooled sample of both PLWH and PWOH, the model performance for age alone (AUC=0.738 [95% CI, 0.700–0.777) and PCE alone (AUC=0.722 [95% CI, 0.682–0.762]) were significantly higher than the PRSCAD alone, which had an AUC of 0.632 (95% CI, 0.589–0.676). The addition of PRSCAD to age increased the performance compared with age alone (AUC=0.759 [95% CI, 0.722–0.763]; P=0.015) (Table 3). The addition of PRSCAD to PCE did not significantly increase the performance of PCE alone (IDI=−0.024 [95% CI, −0.087 to 0.039]). When analyses were limited to individuals younger than the median age of 57 years, this trend persisted, with the addition of PRSCAD not significantly improving performance from PCE alone (AUC increase from 0.631 to 0.656, P=0.29; IDI=0.025 [95% CI, −0.067 to 0.117]).

When stratified by HIV infection status, PCE had lower performance in PLWH compared with PWOH (Table 3), with the AUC of PLWH (0.724 [95% CI, 0.671–0.776]) being 96.1% of the AUC of PWOH (0.753 [95% CI, 0.694–0.812]; P=0.34). This was comparable to the proportional difference in AUC between PLWH and PWOH for PRSCAD with age (PLWH 95.2% of PWOH model fit, P=0.29). However, when both PRSCAD and PCE were combined into a single risk model, the gap in performance between the two groups decreased to PLWH having 98.7% the AUC of PWOH (P=0.81). The narrowing of this disparity in performance came primarily from an increase in performance among PLWH. While these trends may indicate the added value of PRSCAD to PCE, none of these differences were statistically significant (IDIPLWH=0.068 [95% CI, −0.019 to 0.156]; IDIPWOH=0.006 [95% CI, −0.091 to 0.104]).

Further Decreased Performance Among Non‐Hispanic Black/AFR Men

Although the sample size was substantially smaller for men who identified as non‐Hispanic Black and fell within the African genetic ancestry cluster, the performance of the PRSCAD was evaluated using the same models as described above. Performance was lower among these participants compared with non‐Hispanic White/EUR participants. Overall performance for the presence or absence of CAC had an AUC of 0.681 among non‐Hispanic Black/AFR, compared with 0.761 in non‐Hispanic White/EUR participants. However, the majority of this performance was due to the base model (AUC=0.670) of age, CVD substudy (CVD1–3), and CT scanning center, with no notable addition of information from the PRSCAD (P=0.24). There was little difference in performance when stratified, with the full model of PRSCAD on presence/absence of CAC having an AUC of 0.689 among PWOH and 0.692 among PLWH (P=0.96). Comparing the highest and lowest categories of CAC showed diminished but reverse trends to non‐Hispanic White/EUR participants, with an AUC of 0.671 among PWOH and 0.769 among PLWH (P=0.22). Higher performance was also found among PLWH when examining CAC on a continuous scale, albeit much lower than among non‐Hispanic White/EUR participants. Overall, the adjusted R 2 among non‐Hispanic Black/AFR participants was 0.071. When stratified, the adjusted R 2 among PWOH was 0.044 compared with 0.073 among PLWH. However, these results should be considered cautiously given the smaller number of participants in these groups.

DISCUSSION

In the current study, we assessed the performance of a previously validated PRS for CAD in predicting CAC in PWOH and PLWH from the MACS cohort. In agreement with prior reports, we found that PRSCAD was effective at predicting CAC levels among the participants of our cohort but that its predictive capacity was modest and lower compared with traditional atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease risk factors. Importantly, when PRSCAD was added to the PCE, the predictive performance showed minimal and nonsignificant improvement compared with PCE alone. These findings are consistent with prior studies evaluating the additive effect of PRS estimation compared with existing clinical tools in atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease risk prediction. Indeed, most reports agree that PRS is able to predict risk of clinical and subclinical atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease among different subgroups but that adding the PRS to traditional risk prediction tools results in a small increase in performance, of uncertain clinical significance. 4 , 25 Similarly, albeit limited by the smaller sample size, our findings from the analysis of the African ancestry subpopulation were consistent with prior reports demonstrating poor transferability of PRS in populations of African ancestry. 26

Although prior reports have shown that PRSCAD effectively predicts CAC levels in PLWH, 27 this is, to our knowledge, the first study that directly compared PRSCAD performance between PLWH and PWOH. Interestingly, we found evidence for a slightly higher performance of the PRS in PWOH compared with PLWH in predicting subclinical coronary atherosclerosis as assessed by CAC levels. Although this trend was consistent across comparisons, the greater performance in PWOH was particularly evident when comparing extremes of CAC (CAC=0 compared with CAC >100). The observed lower performance of PRSCAD in PLWH possibly reflects biological differences in atherosclerosis biology between PLWH and PWOH. Indeed, PLWH are known to be at higher risk of CAD even when HIV is well‐controlled (ie, virally suppressed). 28 This risk is thought to be mediated primarily by inflammatory mechanisms. 29 , 30 Consequently, the higher relative importance of inflammatory pathways in the biology of plaque in PLWH could plausibly translate into differences in effect size of PRSCAD variants depending on whether they capture inflammation or other heritable pathways that contribute to CAD risk.

Our study has some important limitations. First, this study utilized a polygenic score for CAD to predict CAC, which was not the intended outcome in the development of the PRS. A previous study in MESA (Multi‐Ethnic Study of Atherosclerosis) that a CAC score had a C statistic of 0.76 for incident coronary heart disease events and the polygenic score for CAD had similar performance for incident coronary heart disease in their sample (C statistic of 0.69 [95% CI, 0.63–0.71]) to our findings predicting CAC. 31 This indicates that while the outcomes may differ, they are closely related and therefore the use of PRSCAD is valid for these comparisons. That said, the previously demonstrated higher prevalence of noncalcified plaque in PLWH compared with PWOH15 could be an alternative explanation of the lower performance of PRSCAD in that group. In addition, the PRSCAC tested in our study was found to have lower predictive performance than one for CAD for both continuous and categorical outcomes. This is likely due to the small number of variants in the published PRS, 22 suggesting the need for improved PRS development, which was not possible in our cohort due to the small sample sizes.

Second, the sample size limited our power to detect small differences in performance between different predictive tools both within and between PLWH and PWOH participants. Third, the small observed difference in baseline standardized PRSCAD levels between PWOH and PLWH may suggest differences in baseline recruitment characteristics and possibly a survival bias (PLWH who survived to be in the study may have had lower PRSCAD) which could limit our interpretation of PRS performance in PLWH. Fourth, our cohort consists entirely of men, which limits generalizability of our findings to women. Last, this study reflects current biases of MACS in representation by race and ethnicity as well as genetic ancestry, with the non‐Hispanic Black/AFR sample being substantially smaller than the non‐Hispanic White/EUR sample, limiting statistical power and therefore interpretations. However, this work represents the first step in better understanding how PRSCAD may perform in external groups from the training sets, most notably in this case by HIV infection compounded by differences in population genetics substructure due to differing allele frequencies and linkage disequilibrium patterns between groups.

In summary, we found that a previously validated PRSCAD results in minimal improvement in predictive performance for CAC when added to traditional risk prediction tools. The observed lower performance of PRSCAD in PLWH compared with PWOH, suggests that further research is needed to develop more accurate risk prediction models that have potential clinical utility in all patient populations.
