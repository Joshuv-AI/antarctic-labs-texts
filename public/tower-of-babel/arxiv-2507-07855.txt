Principled Foundations for Preference Optimization * {}^{\mbox{\small*}}
Wenxuan Zhou, Shujian Zhang, Brice Magdalou, John Lambert, Ehsan Amid, Richard Nock, Andrew Hard

Abstract
In this paper, we show that direct preference optimization (DPO) is a very specific form of a connection between two major theories in the ML context of learning from preferences: loss functions (Savage) and stochastic choice (Doignon-Falmagne and Machina). The connection is established for all of Savage’s losses and at this level of generality, (i) it includes support for abstention on the choice theory side, (ii) it includes support for non-convex objectives on the ML side, and (iii) it allows to frame for free some notable extensions of the DPO setting, including margins and corrections for length. Getting to understand how DPO operates from a general principled perspective is crucial because of the huge and diverse application landscape of models, because of the current momentum around DPO, but also – and importantly – because many state of the art variations on DPO definitely occupy a small region of the map that we cover. It also helps to understand the pitfalls of departing from this map, and figure out workarounds.

Introduction

Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) introduced a trick to bypass reward modelling in RLHF, which trains reward function
r_{\bm{\omega}}
then policy
\pi_{\bm{\theta}}
with parameters
\bm{\theta}
, by introducing an explicit direct link
r_{\bm{\theta}}=f(\pi_{\bm{\theta}})
that allows to skip learning the reward function parameters (
\bm{\omega}
)
(
Rafailov et al. 2023
)
. While the technique has met with experimental success, a trove of papers have elaborated on the DPO framework to generalize its functional parts
(
Alfano et al. 2025
;
Azar et al. 2024
;
Chen et al. 2025
;
Cui et al. 2024
;
Gu et al. 2024
;
Gupta et al. 2025
;
Hong et al. 2024
;
Lee et al. 2025
;
Li et al. 2025
;
Lu et al. 2024
;
Meng et al. 2024
;
Park et al. 2024
;
Ramesh et al. 2024
;
Yuan et al. 2023
;
Zhao et al. 2025
)
(and many others). This is understandable: there is lots of
apparent
freedom from this standpoint, with two directions to explore, loss functions — two in the DPO pipeline — and choice theory. It is an understatement to say that there is a push to explore those dimensions
(
Zhang et al. 2025
)
, but the risks faced without a principled understanding of how the connection works can be consequential: the core element of any learning algorithm, the objective to be optimized, involves design elements from both choice theory and loss functions. No work has carried out this principled grounding of the connection. This is our focus.

We show that DPO is a very particular case of intertwining two foundational theories for loss functions and stochastic choice in the ML context of learning from preferences. The former is Savage’s properness
(
Savage 1971
)
. The latter involves elements from Doignon-Falmagne’s stochastic choice theory
(
Doignon and Falmagne 1974
)
as simplified by Krantz, Luce, Suppes and Tversky in their book
(
Krantz et al. 1989
)
, and from Machina on lotteries
(
Machina 1985
;
Machina and Siniscalchi 2014
)
.

These many design elements lead to a generalization making the most of the connection since we encompass all of properness on Savage’s side (regardless of optional properties like symmetry, differentiability, etc). We also encompass all of the modelling’s power on Krantz, Luce, Suppes and Tversky’s side. At this level of generality and through the connection, we are able to cover on the choice theory side the case where users can abstain, provided abstention has a particular structure among alternatives; and we are able to cover non-convex losses in the final ML-optimized objective, provided they are strictly monotonic. If abstaining is forbidden, which is the setting of DPO and most of its current ML generalizations, we can reduce loss choices to symmetric losses on Savage’s side (an example of which is the log-loss, strictly proper, and its dual convex conjugate, the logistic loss, strictly convex) and Debreu models for the choice theory side
(
Debreu 1958
)
(an example of which is Bradley-Terry-Luce’s). Notably, our level of generalization is able to support "for free” important extensions of DPO that have been so far essentially ad-hoc: margins / home advantages
(
Meng et al. 2024
)
and corrections for length
(
Meng et al. 2024
;
Gu et al. 2024
;
Gupta et al. 2025
;
Park et al. 2024
;
Chen et al. 2025
)
.

This is an important task because DPO was designed with the objective to simplify RLHF and getting "above" DPO is mandatory to improve results by getting more freedom on reward shapes, trajectories and preference behaviours
(
Gupta et al. 2025
)
, all of which needs to be done while keeping DPO’s design tricks supporting simplification. What is striking, at the level of the panorama that we reach, is that it groups many DPO variants in a pretty small part, while the rest of it remains largely
terra incognita
. We provide several ways to navigate broad parts of this panorama: the most general which involves a loss eventually non-convex, and a particular one, convenient when all or part of the functional framework needs to be learned
(
Alfano et al. 2025
)
, which trades expensive properness checks for a cheap convexity knob.

Our work is also interesting for what may
depart
from this map, to understand the pitfalls and figure out workarounds. One perhaps unexpected pitfall comes from the RLHF/DPO inherited "gold standard" for the relationship
r(x,y)=f(\pi({\color[rgb]{1,0,0}y}|x))
, whereby
f
depends
only
on the action / prompt
y
involved in the reward. We show that there is only
one
proper loss for which such a representation is possible, DPO’s KL divergence. Our proof eliminates assumptions sprinkled on seven decades of related results
(
McCarthy 1956
;
Savage 1971
;
Bernardo 1979
;
Dawid 2007
)
: differentiability, the identity of pointwise losses, restrictions on the target, etc. . Without care, changing the KL divergence while keeping properness can bring
r(x,y)=f(\mbox{{many} }\pi({\color[rgb]{1,0,0}y^{\prime}}|x))
, potentially ladening the objective with a computational burden contrasting with DPO’s simplicity. Fortunately, we show that there exists a very simple workaround in the "gold standard", keeping properness with much freedom in loss design.

To preserve readability, all proofs are given in an appendix.

2.1
Properness

A loss function is a function

Definition 1
.

Fix

Strict
properness happens if the inequality is an equality only for

. Properness encourages guessing the ground truth. Savage proposed to use an appealing form for

\displaystyle L(\bm{p},\bm{q})

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{.}}}{{=}}

\displaystyle\bm{p}^{\top}\bm{\ell}(\bm{q})=\mathbb{E}_{i\sim\textsc{cat}(\bm{p})}[\ell_{i}(\bm{q})],

(1)

where "

" denotes a categorical distribution with parameter

. Properness is intensional

Walder and Nock 2020
)
and can be tricky to operate if (parts of) the loss itself need to be learned
(
Alfano et al. 2025
;
Gupta et al. 2025
)
. Fortunately, there is a characterization of proper losses that makes things a lot easier. A Bregman divergence
D_{\phi,\bm{H}}(\bm{u}\|\bm{v})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{.}}}{{=}}\phi(\bm{u})-\phi(\bm{u})-(\bm{u}-\bm{v})^{\top}\bm{H}(\bm{v})
is defined for convex
\phi
and a selection of its subdifferential,
\bm{H}\in\partial\phi
. Bregman divergences have a second form, introduced in information geometry by
Amari and Nagaoka 2000
and later introduced in ML under the name Fenchel-Young losses
(
Blondel et al. 2020
)
, whereby
D_{\phi}(\bm{u}\|\bm{v}^{*})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{.}}}{{=}}\phi(\bm{u})+\phi^{\star}(\bm{v}^{*})-\bm{u}^{\top}\bm{v}^{*}
, where
\varphi^{\star}(\bm{v})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{.}}}{{=}}\sup_{\bm{t}}\bm{t}^{\top}\bm{v}-\varphi(\bm{t})
is the convex conjugate. This Amari-Bregman-Fenchel (ABF) representation keeps implicit the dependence on the subgradient, but it is right there since
\bm{v}^{*}=\bm{H}(\bm{v})
for some
\bm{H}\in\partial\phi
. All proper losses can be represented as Bregman divergences, and reciprocally every Bregman divergence "encodes" a proper loss
(
Williamson et al. 2016
)
.

5
Limitations, conclusion and open problems

DPO has has a substantial impact on training LLMs
(
Tunstall et al. 2024
;
Ivison et al. 2023
;
Grattafiori et al. 2024
;
Nvidia et al. 2024
;
Xu et al. 2025
)
, and an even broader impact on research, with countless variants. It is thus of primary importance to define a wide "operating" normative framework in which any such variant may be framed and understood.

We show that DPO is a particular point in a huge panorama of losses and preference models, that everywhere keeps all of DPO’s neat design tricks and properties. Why does this matter ? Zooming out from DPO to the level of generality of this panorama, it is striking that most techniques that have built over DPO look
in fine
extremely close to DPO (for example, they all rely on convex objectives while our framework accomodates for non-convexity). There is thus a large unexplored map to explore for substantial variations to the specific DPO choices, and this map can accommodate a number of additional requirements that have been formulated in the realm of LLMs (it naturally embeds home advantages and length correction; it is not affected by "orthogonal" extensions to DPO like marginal/posterior optimization, noise handling, time-varying preferences, etc., that can be used without change). Given the variety of environments, requirements, data specificities of LLM training, development, and deployment, it is important to frame the full freedom offered, to then be able to make choices without losing any of its advantages. Such freedom is mandatory to be able escape eventual pitfalls of specific choices
(
Huang et al. 2025
;
Alfano et al. 2025
;
Gupta et al. 2025
)
, and it does indeed offer concrete leverage
(
Mitchell 2023
)
.

In terms of social impact, it would arguably be "distant" to the fundamentals we develop, even when one could expect
in fine
positive impacts to better understanding when preference optimization techniques can work (or not — and why), and how to design new techniques that fit in a principled framework with formal guarantees. This being said, our approach is kept it in the "straight jacket" imposed by the normative theories we use
(
Savage 1971
;
Doignon and Falmagne 1974
;
Krantz et al. 1989
;
Machina 1985
)
– however general they arguably may be. Further, it would be quite impossible to extend our results using Savage’s theory. What this means is that some DPO variants definitely escape our framework
(
Huang et al. 2025
;
Wang et al. 2024
)
. It does not mean that the quality of their experimental results cannot be obtained from some technique that would belong to the panorama we cover, it also does not mean that those techniques are without any connection to our framework
4
4

4

For example, since
\log(z)=\log(z-1+1)\sim_{1}(z-1)-(z-1)^{2}/2+o((z-1)^{2})
, we easily derive that the
\chi^{2}
divergence is a second-order approximation of a KL divergence, especially good near its optimum
Huang et al. 2025
.
. It just means that changes must be done to our setting to accommodate those in a normative framework, which is definitely an interesting open problem.

Remark X
.

The crux of our proof consists in bypassing the use of the solvability axiom of the original proof
(

, Chapter 17, Theorem 2)
– which is hard to motivate in the context of preference optimization – by creating a structure on top of alternatives / actions which can be (i) motivated in the context of preference optimization and (ii) compatible with the sampling process of preferences.
