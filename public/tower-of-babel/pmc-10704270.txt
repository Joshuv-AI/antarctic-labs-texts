Effects of advanced nanomaterials on the respiratory system of a murine COPD model
Authors: Katsuhide Fujita, Sawae Obara, Junko Maru, Shigehisa Endoh
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study assessed the effects of cellulose nanofibrils (CNFs) and multi-walled carbon nanotubes (MWCNTs) on lung inflammation in a cigarette smoke-induced chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) mouse model. Prior to instillation, COPD model mice displayed distinctive cellular compositions and elevated cytokine levels in bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BALF). After intratracheal instillation of 80 μg CNFs, no significant histopathological changes, BALF composition alterations, or cytokine level shifts were observed on day 28. This suggests minimal lung impact and no interference with reducing smoke-induced inflammation. In contrast, the instillation of 80 μg MWCNTs resulted in significant histopathological changes, increased cellular composition, and elevated cytokine levels in BALF on day 28. These findings indicate that CNF exposure had little effect on the lungs and did not impede the reduction of smoke-induced inflammation, while MWCNT exposure hindered the attenuation of pulmonary inflammatory response. The study emphasizes the importance of considering diverse cases, including individuals with pre-existing respiratory conditions, when assessing occupational safety and health risks associated with advanced nanomaterial exposure.Keywords: Cellulose nanofibrils, Multi-walled carbon nanotubes, Chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, Bronchoalveolar lavage fluid, Advanced nanomaterials

Introduction

Nanotechnology is poised to revolutionize a wide range of industries, including electronic, mechatronic, chemical, energy, biotechnological, and medical industries, as well as structural and functional materials and environmental applications. The potential for its application is vast, with major advancements anticipated in these fields. One notable example is the utilization of cellulose nanofibrils (CNFs), also known as cellulose nanofibers or nanofibrillated cellulose, as plant-derived nanomaterials. CNFs possess exceptional characteristics, such as light weight, high strength, low thermal expansion coefficients, gas barrier properties, transparency, thickening properties, and thixotropic properties [23]. These attributes make them attractive alternatives to petroleum-derived materials and facilitate diverse applications across various industries [17], [35]. Similarly, carbon nanotubes (CNTs) have demonstrated rapid industrial applications within the nanotechnology field [7]. Their incorporation in structural and functional materials expands the possibilities for developing lightweight, durable, and high-performance materials. However, it is critical to be aware of concerns regarding the potential human health and environmental impacts arising from the use of fibrous nanomaterials, such as CNFs and CNTs, has been reported to pose potential risks [5], [6], [12], [13], [25], [32]. These fibrous and ultrafine particles can be inhaled and may deeply permeate the respiratory system, potentially causing harmful effects to the lungs and other organs. Therefore, it is crucial to address these uncertainties, especially in industrial settings, to ensure the safe and responsible utilization of advanced nanomaterials. Furthermore, it is essential to highlight that these nanomaterials may exist as airborne particles at varying concentrations in real-life scenarios. Potential sources of airborne particles encompass industrial processes involving the production or utilization of nanomaterials. Additionally, incidental releases may occur during the disposal of products containing these materials. A comprehensive understanding of the concentrations and sources of airborne nanomaterials is crucial for accurately assessing potential exposure risks and implementing appropriate safety measures in occupational and environmental settings.

Airway remodeling, characterized by structural changes in the airways, is known to play a major role in the development of symptoms associated with lung dysfunction in asthma and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD). It has been suggested that multi-walled carbon nanotubes (MWCNTs) induce airway remodeling and fibrosis in rodent models following respiratory exposure [26], [31]. However, the exact mechanisms underlying this process are still not fully understood and require further investigation. Recent studies have reported the increased accumulation of alveolar macrophages in bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BALF) and the elevated expression of muc5AC transcripts in COPD mice after MWCNT treatment [3]. These findings suggest that an immune response induced by MWCNT exposure and mucus production in the airways may occur in COPD mice. Thus far, limited attention has been paid to the risks associated with occupational exposure to fibrous nanomaterials, particularly for individuals who may be more susceptible due to the high prevalence of COPD. It is essential to address these concerns, especially in industrial settings, to ensure the safe and responsible utilization of advanced nanomaterials in various applications. The present study aimed to characterize the effects of CNFs and MWCNTs on pulmonary inflammation in COPD model mice 28 days after intratracheal instillation. We induced a mouse model of COPD by exposing mice to cigarette smoke (CS). CS plays a major role in the development of COPD and various other diseases, serving as a major causative factor in their pathogenesis [18]. CS-induced rodent models are widely used to study inflammatory responses in COPD, and mice are the most commonly used species for establishing animal models of COPD [16].

CNFs and MWCNTs were selected due to concerns associated with these advanced nanomaterials, primarily stemming from their fibrous and robust structures, which could potentially lead to inhalation and deep penetration into the respiratory system. This could have detrimental effects on the lungs and other organs due to their biopersistence [4], [15]. The physicochemical characteristics of CNFs, including fiber diameter, fiber length, morphology, functional groups, and impurities, can vary depending on the raw materials and the chemical and mechanical treatments employed [21], [22], [29]. Thus, the safety of different grades of fibrillated celluloses should be assessed on a case-by-case basis [30]. In this study, we used 2,2,6,6-tetramethylpiperidine-1-oxyl radical (TEMPO)-oxidized CNFs, which is a type of CNF produced via chemical modification. Furthermore, as CNFs are plant-derived nanomaterials, it is important to confirm the presence of endotoxins and microbial contamination in the CNFs used for testing [20], [28]. We also selected commercially available MWCNTs, which have been used in many toxicity studies [24], [36]. Our previous in vitro cell-based assay indicated a significant contribution of the diameter of these MWCNTs to their cytotoxicity [11]. To address this, suspensions of CNFs or MWCNTs were prepared and their physicochemical and biological properties were characterized. Subsequently, the suspensions were introduced into mice through intratracheal instillation, and each mouse received an equivalent dose of 80 µg.

Many studies have been conducted to evaluate the pulmonary toxicity of CNTs in rodents using inhalation exposure and intratracheal instillation methods [27], [34]. Intratracheal instillation involves directly administering single or multiple doses of the substance into the trachea of rodents, followed by observation. This method offers advantages, such as simplicity, requiring smaller amounts of test material compared to inhalation exposure tests, and providing an accurate determination of the instilled dose to the lungs. Furthermore, we believe that analyzing the intensity of the effects exhibited by the test material immediately after its administration, as well as observing the subsequent increase or decrease of these effects during the rearing period, can offer valuable information about the toxicity profile of the individual test material. In our research, we have also employed intratracheal instillation to assess the pulmonary toxicity of CNFs, CNTs, or carbon fibers [8], [9], [10], [13], [14], [19], [27]. Based on these findings, we hypothesized that by evaluating the extent to which pulmonary inflammation induced by CNFs or MWCNTs subsides or persists, we could discern the differing adverse effects of each material. This study employed morphological observations, BALF analysis, and histopathological examination on day 28 following instillation in order to assess pulmonary inflammation in both the no-smoke control and COPD model mice.

Materials and methods

Test materials and their preparation

Aqueous slurries containing approximately 10 mg/mL TEMPO-oxidized CNFs, which were obtained from Nippon Paper Industries Co. Ltd. (Tokyo, Japan), were exposed to gamma rays at absorption doses of 1.0 kGy (Koga Isotope, Ltd., Shiga, Japan). The concentration of CNFs was determined using the dry weight method and adjusted to 2.0 mg/mL using UltraPure™ DNase/RNase-Free distilled water (Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc, Massachusetts, United States). Dispersion was performed for 30 min using a planetary centrifugal non-foaming mixer (ARE-310, THINKY CORPORATION, Tokyo, Japan). Henceforth, this suspension will be referred to as CNF. Bulk NC7000® Nanocyl-MWCNTs were purchased from Nanocyl SA (Nanocyl SA, Sambreville, Belgium). According to the manufacturer's specifications, the mean diameter of the MWCNTs was 9.5 nm, and the mean length of the MWCNTs was 1.5 µm. Bulk MWCNTs were dispersed in a 10 mg/mL bovine serum albumin (Nacalai Tesque Kyoto, Japan) solution dissolved in UltraPure™ DNase/RNase-Free distilled water (Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc., Waltham, MA) using a Branson 1510 J-DTH ultrasonic bath (Branson Ultrasonics Corp., Danbury, CT). The MWCNT dispersion was filtered through a cell strainer with a 40-μm nylon mesh (Becton, Dickinson and Company, Franklin Lakes, NJ) to remove carbon nanotube agglomerates. The filtrates were used as the MWCNT stock suspensions. The concentrations of MWCNTs in the stock suspensions were determined by measuring the ultraviolet-visible (UV–vis) absorption spectra using a UV-2550 spectrophotometer (Shimadzu Corporation, Kyoto, Japan) at wavelengths ranging from 600 nm to 800 nm. Based on the results, the MWCNT suspensions were adjusted to 2.0 mg/mL (hereinafter MWCNT).

Physicochemical and biological properties of CNF and MWCNT

A transmission electron microscope (TEM) system at 75 kV (H-7100; Hitachi, Tokyo, Japan) was used to observe CNFs and MWCNTs in each suspension. The diameters and lengths of CNFs and MWCNTs were measured from approximately 100 images of CNFs and MWCNTs, respectively using a JEM-1010 TEM (JEOL Ltd., Tokyo, Japan) at 100 kV. The pH, and zeta potential of the CNFs and MWCNTs were measured using a F-72 M pH meter (HORIBA, Ltd., Kyoto, Japan), and Zetasizer Pro (Malvern Panalytical, Worcestershire, United Kingdom) according to the manufacturer’s procedure. The number of bacterial or fungal colonies were detected in the CNF and MWCNT suspensions using 3 M™ Petrifilm™ (3 M Japan Limited, Tokyo, Japan). Mycoplasma contaminations were detected in the CNF suspensions using the MycoAlert™ Mycoplasma Detection Kit (Lonza, Basel, Switzerland). Endotoxins and (1, 3)-β-D-glucans in the CNF suspension were detected using a Pyrochrome® with Glucashield® Buffer or Pyrotell® (Associates of Cape Cod, Inc., East Falmouth, MA), and Endosafe® Portable Test System (PTSTM) Glucan Assay (Charles River Laboratories International, Inc., Wilmington, MA).

Animals

All animal tests were conducted at Hokudo Co., Ltd, Hokkaido, Japan. The mice were housed in polycarbonate cages in a room with a controlled temperature of 20–26 °C and humidity of 30–70% and were fed ad libitum with a chow diet (FEED ONE CO., LTD. Kanagawa, Japan). After a one-week acclimatization period, 90 nine-week-old female C57BL/6JJcl mice (CLEA Japan, Inc., Tokyo, Japan) were divided into two groups; a mainstream CS exposure group for COPD modeling and a no-smoke control (NSC) group. The mice had body weights ranging from 17.65 g to 21.16 g prior to exposure.

Experimental design

The experimental design is shown in Fig. 1. Mice were exposed to mainstream smoke obtained from commercial cigarettes (Marlboro Box, 12 mg tar, 0.9 mg nicotine, Philippe Morris Japan K.K.) using the Tobacco Smoke Inhalation Experimental Apparatus Model SG-300 (Shibata Kagaku K.K.) while kept in inhalation chambers. The position of the exposure chambers was rotated daily to equalize the effects of exposure. The concentration of tobacco smoke was set at 5%, and the frequency of exposure was 60 min/day, 5 days/week for a duration of 13 weeks. In the NSC group, the animals were housed in polycarbonate cages in a room with a controlled temperature of 20–26 °C and humidity of 30–70% and were fed ad libitum with a chow diet (Oriental Yeast Co., Ltd.) for a duration of 13 weeks.

Overview of the experimental design used in the animal study.

After 13 weeks, nine mice from each group (NSC and COPD model mice) were subjected to necropsy, while the remaining 36 animals received intratracheal instillation and were necropsied after 4 weeks of housing. The mice were anesthetized with isoflurane (Viatris Inc., Tokyo, Japan) and were given a single intratracheal instillation of 40 µL test solution per mouse using a feeding 20-gauge needle (Natsume Seisakusho Co., Ltd., Tokyo, Japan). The tip of the feeding needle was positioned carefully in the trachea, near but not touching the carina, to ensure appropriate intratracheal instillation. The dose of CNFs and MWCNTs instilled was 80 µg/mouse. Given that the average mouse body weight before instillation treatment ranged from approximately 20.61 g to 23.20 g, as shown in Table 3, the administered dose per unit body was calculated to be 3.7 mg/kg mouse. The decision to set a maximum dose of 80 µg/mouse was driven by safety concerns, supported by the lack of abnormal respiration and mortality observed in the preliminary test, even at a dose of 100 µg for both CNFs and MWCNTs per mouse seven days after intratracheal instillation (data not shown). The mice were monitored daily for viability and general condition until they were sacrificed, and their body weights were recorded before instillation and once daily thereafter. Euthanasia of the mice was performed by bleeding from the abdominal aorta under isoflurane (Viatris Inc.) anesthesia, and necropsy was performed. At 28 days post-instillation, the lung and liver weights of the mice were measured. Morphological observations, BALF analysis (five out of nine), and histopathological examination (four out of nine) were conducted on dissected lungs at 28 days post-instillation.

Blood biochemistry test

Hemoglobin A1c (HbA1c) was measured from the collected mouse blood using an automatic biochemical analyzer JCA-BM6050 (JEOL Ltd.). Serum was prepared from the collected blood, and total cholesterol (TC), triglycerides (TG), low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C), and high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C) were measured using JCA-BM6050 (JEOL Ltd.).

Cell counts and pro-inflammatory cytokine assays with BALFs

BALF was collected by instilling 1 mL of warm (37 °C) phosphate-buffered saline (PBS; Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc.) into the lungs and repeating the aspiration process four times. The BALF was then centrifuged to obtain the supernatant, which was used for measuring pro-inflammatory cytokines. Centrifuged pellets were suspended in PBS and used for cell counting. The total number of leukocytes (macrophages, neutrophils, lymphocytes, eosinophils, and basophils) was counted after Diff-Quik staining (Sysmex Corporation, Hyogo, Japan). The levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines, including interleukin-1 alpha (IL-1α), interleukin-1 beta (IL-1β), interleukin-6 (IL-6), interleukin-33/NF-HEV (IL-33), macrophage inflammatory protein-1 alpha (MIP-1α), macrophage chemoattractant protein-1 (MCP-1), tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNFα), matrix metalloproteinase-2 (MMP-2), promatrix metalloproteinase-9 (proMMP-9), and matrix metalloproteinase-12 (MMP-12), were measured using a MILLIPLEX® Mouse Cytokine/Chemokine Panel, MILLIPLEX® Mouse TH17 Panel, MILLIPLEX® Mouse MMP Panel3 (Merck KGaA, Darmstadt, Germany), and a Luminex 200 System (Luminex Corporation, Austin, TX, USA). IL-1α, IL-1β, IL-6, IL-33, MIP-1α, MCP-1, and TNFα were selected because they are key mediators of lung inflammation. MMP-2, proMMP-9, and MMP-12 were selected because they are involved in lung inflammation through their action on the extracellular matrix and remodeling of lung tissue.

Histopathological examinations

After the mice were sacrificed, their left lungs were filled with 10% buffered paraformaldehyde before processing for histopathological examination. Fixed lung tissues were embedded in paraffin and stained with hematoxylin and eosin. Digital images of each lung section were obtained for the alveoli, alveolar walls, bronchioles, and vessels for histopathological evaluation. Severity scores were given to individual animals after a complete histopathological examination using the following criteria: Negative: no significant inflammation; Slight: localized inflammation in a few areas/sparse inflammatory cellular infiltrations of alveolar space and interstitium; Mild: localized inflammation in four to nine areas/mild diffuse inflammatory cellular infiltrate with visible alveolar space; Moderate: more than 10 localized inflammation/diffuse inflammatory cellular infiltrate with a density so dense that the alveolar space is difficult to identify (up to 1/2 of the lung); Severe: widespread diffuse inflammatory cell infiltration with a density that makes it difficult to identify the alveolar space (1/2 to all lobes of the lung).

Statistical analysis

Numerical values are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). The Student's t-test was performed for equal variance and Welch's t-test was performed for unequal variance. Statistical significance was set at *P < 0.05 or **P < 0.01, and †P < 0.05 or ††P < 0.01. The quantitative values obtained from the test values in BALF, body weight gain, and organ weights were analyzed using the F-test for equality of variances (P < 0.05).

Results

CNF characterization

TEM analysis of CNF suspensions revealed a network structure composed of individually dispersed CNFs (Fig. 2A). The average diameter and length of the CNFs were approximately 12.3 nm and 1.7 µm, respectively (Fig. 2C). In contrast, the MWCNT suspension exhibited individually dispersed fibers with a bent morphology, differing from the rigid needle-shaped or elongated thin structures (Fig. 2B). The average diameter and length of the MWCNTs were 8.2 nm and 1.3 µm, respectively (Fig. 2C). No significant aggregation of CNFs or MWCNTs was observed during the preparation (data not shown). No bacteria, fungi, or mycoplasma were identified in the CNF suspensions, and neither bacteria nor fungi were detected in the MWCNT suspensions. Furthermore, the CNF suspension exhibited detectable levels of endotoxin and (1, 3)-β-D-glucan, whereas no detectable endotoxin was observed in the MWCNT suspension (Fig. 2C).

Characteristics of cellulose nanofibrils (CNFs) and multi-walled carbon nanotubes (MWCNTs) in each suspension. Transmission electron microscope (TEM) images of CNFs (A) and MWCNTs (B); physicochemical and biological properties of CNFs and MWCNTs (C).

Conditions of mice after CS exposure

After the 91-day exposure period, COPD model mice had significantly lower body weight values than those in the NSC group. The liver weights in COPD model mice were significantly lower than those in NSC mice (Table 1). There were no significant differences in the HbA1c, TC, TG, LDL-C, and HDL-C levels between the NSC group and COPD model mice (data not shown). In the NSC group, which included five animals, the mean total leukocyte count was 0.288 × 105 cells/mL, was primarily composed of macrophages, with minimal neutrophils, and lymphocytes (Table 2). However, in the CS exposure group, also consisting of five animals, there was a significant increase in total leukocytes (1.869 ×105 cells/mL) compared to that in the NSC group. This increase was primarily attributed to a higher macrophage count and a significant presence of neutrophils and lymphocytes. There were no eosinophils or basophils detected in both groups. Histopathological examination of the lungs of NSC mice (n = 4) indicated no abnormal findings. In contrast, the lungs of COPD model mice (n = 4) exhibited macrophage infiltration (two "slight", one "mild", one "moderate"), lymphocyte infiltration (three "slight", one "mild"), and neutrophil infiltration (one "mild").

General condition, body weight gain, and organ weights of mice after intratracheal instillation

Weight loss in mice in different groups was observed 1-day post-instillation (Table 3). However, both NSC and COPD model mice in the V1 and CNF groups exhibited weight gain that stabilized during days 1–28 post-instillation, which is similar to the mice in the V2 and MWCNT groups. No significant differences in weight were observed between the two groups. There were no significant differences in the HbA1c, TC, TG, LDL-C, and HDL-C levels between V1 and CNF groups, and V2 and MWCNT groups (data not shown). No statistically significant differences were observed in the right and left lung and liver weights in the V1 and CNF groups or the V2 and MWCNT groups 28 days post-instillation (Table 4).

Macroscopic findings and histopathological examination of lung tissues of mice after intratracheal instillation

The lung tissues of NSC and COPD model mice in the V1 and CNF groups 28 days post-instillation indicated no signs of macroscopic changes (Fig. 3, A1, A2, B1 and B2). Dark reddening of the lungs was observed in three of the nine NSC mice in the V2 group, whereas in the MWCNT group, five of the nine NSC mice exhibited dark reddening of the lungs and one of the nine mice exhibited blackening of the lungs (Fig. 3, C1 and D1). Similarly, one of the nine COPD model mice in the V2 group exhibited dark reddening of the lungs, whereas in the MWCNT group, blackening of the lungs was observed in five of the nine COPD model mice, and blackening of the trachea and esophagus was observed in one of the nine mice (Fig. 3, C2 and D2). The dark reddening observed in the lungs was presumably attributed to the residual MWCNTs.

BALF analysis after intratracheal instillation

No significant differences in the HbA1c, TC, TG, LDL-C, and HDL-C levels were observed between the blood samples collected from the NSC and COPD model mice 28 days post-instillation (Table 5). In the comparison between the NSC group mice (V1 vs CNF and V2 vs MWCNT) and the COPD model mice (V1 and CNF) 28 days post-instillation, there were no significant increases observed in the total number of leukocytes, macrophages, neutrophils, and lymphocytes in the BALF (Fig. 5). However, a significant increase in the total number of leukocytes and macrophages was observed in the BALF when comparing the V2 COPD model mice and the MWCNT mice 28 days post-instillation (Fig. 5, A and B). Furthermore, the MWCNT group in the COPD model mice group exhibited elevated counts of neutrophils and lymphocytes in the BALF (Fig. 5, C and D). Notably, there were no eosinophils or basophils detected in the BALF of either the NSC or COPD model mice in any of the groups (data not shown).

Discussion

With the aim of understanding the interaction between the physicochemical and biological properties of advanced nanomaterials and potential toxicity, important findings for the intratracheal instillation test were obtained regarding the morphology and dispersibility of CNFs and MWCNTs in each suspension, as well as the bacterial, fungal, mycoplasma, endotoxin and (1, 3)-β-D-glucan levels of the suspensions used in this study. TEM analysis revealed that the CNFs in the suspension exhibited a network structure composed of individually dispersed CNFs, with an average diameter of approximately 12.3 nm and an average length of 1.7 µm. However, the MWCNTs exhibited individually dispersed fibers with a bent morphology, in contrast to the rigid needle-shaped or elongated thin structures previously observed. The average diameter of the MWCNTs was 8.2 nm, and the average length was 1.3 µm, which is in line with previous findings [14]. No significant aggregation of CNFs and MWCNTs was observed during the preparation process. These results indicate that both CNFs and MWCNTs were well dispersed in the suspensions, suggesting that the preparation method effectively maintained the individual dispersion of the nanomaterials.

The origin of CNFs from plant or biological sources, along with the non-sterile manufacturing process, render CNF suspensions susceptible to contamination, potentially creating favorable conditions for microbial growth and increasing the risk of contamination. Sterilization is crucial when conducting toxicity studies on CNFs to assess their toxicity and minimize contamination risks [33]. By eliminating potential contaminants, sterilization helps to maintain controlled experimental conditions, enabling researchers to evaluate the toxicity of CNFs without interference from external factors. The absence of bacteria or fungi was observed in both suspensions, indicating that gamma irradiation potentially aided in sterilizing the CNF suspension, emphasizing the importance of proper sterilization protocols in CNF studies. Furthermore, our results indicated that the MWCNT suspension was also free from bacterial or fungal contamination. The CNF suspension contained detectable levels of both endotoxin and (1, 3)-β-D-glucans. Endotoxin is a component of bacterial cell walls, while (1, 3)-β-D-glucans are a type of fungal cell wall component [1], [2]. In contrast, no endotoxin was detected in the MWCNT suspension. These results suggest that the CNFs may have been contaminated with bacterial or fungal components during their production or handling. However, the effects of the detected levels of endotoxin and (1, 3)-β-D-glucan in the CNF suspension on the respiratory system of mice after intratracheal instillation were not directly examined in this study and will be the focus of future investigations.

In the present study, several notable changes were observed in the mice after 91 days of exposure to CS. The body weights of the COPD model mice were significantly lower than those of mice in the NSC group, indicating a potential impact on their overall health and growth. Additionally, the liver weights of COPD model mice were significantly lower than those of NSC mice, suggesting possible alterations in liver function. Regarding blood parameters, no significant differences were observed in the HbA1c, TC, TG, LDL-C, and HDL-C levels between NSC and COPD model mice. This suggests that CS exposure did not have a significant effect on these particular blood markers. An analysis of the BALF revealed significant increases in the counts of total leukocytes, macrophages, lymphocytes, and neutrophils in COPD model mice compared to NSC mice. This indicates an inflammatory response in the lungs of COPD model mice, potentially indicating lung injury or inflammation caused by CS exposure. Histopathological examination of the lungs of NSC mice did not indicate any abnormal findings. In contrast, the lungs of COPD model mice exhibited various degrees of infiltration, including macrophage infiltration, lymphocyte infiltration, and mild neutrophil infiltration. These findings suggest that CS exposure led to pulmonary inflammation and immune cell infiltration, highlighting the detrimental effects of CS exposure on multiple physiological aspects. However, it is important to note that the future reliability of the COPD murine model, as defined in this study, remains a subject for further investigation. This is because it is imperative to assess the methods used to induce COPD, the parameters used for evaluation, and how closely the model mirrors the characteristics of human COPD. Developing an animal model that accurately represents COPD necessitates a comprehensive understanding of these factors [16].

Although minor histopathological changes were noted in NSC mice treated with CNFs 28 days post-instillation, the cellular composition and levels of cytokines in the BALF, with the exception of IL-1a, were not significantly different when compared to NSC mice in the V1 group. Likewise, COPD model mice of the V1 and CNF groups exhibited similar patterns. These results suggest that in both NSC and COPD model mice, CNF induces minor inflammation immediately after administration; however, this inflammation is attenuated 28 days post-instillation. In our previous study on rats, we demonstrated that the CNFs used in this study induced mild inflammation after dosing (2.0 mg/kg body weight); however, this inflammation was attenuated 90 days post-instillation [13]. These findings suggest that CNFs have minimal long-term effects on the respiratory system in rodents. Interestingly, the presence of residual clear material, likely CNFs phagocytosed by alveolar macrophages, did not result in significant inflammation. This suggests that the phagocytosis of CNFs by alveolar macrophages does not lead to pronounced inflammatory responses.

Upon comparing NSC mice in the MWCNT groups with MWCNT COPD model mice 28 days post-instillation, we found that both groups exhibited inflammatory changes in the lung tissue. In NSC mice treated with MWCNTs, macrophage infiltration, phagocytosis of MWCNTs, and slight lymphocytic infiltration were observed. In contrast, COPD model mice in the MWCNT groups exhibited more significant inflammatory changes, including macrophage infiltration, phagocytosis of black substances, lymphocyte infiltration, and neutrophil infiltration. In terms of cellular composition, there was no significant increase in the total number of leukocytes, macrophages, neutrophils, and lymphocytes in the BALF of NSC mice treated with MWCNTs on day 28 post-instillation. However, COPD model mice exhibited a significant increase in the total number of leukocytes and macrophages in the BALF, along with high counts of neutrophils and lymphocytes in the MWCNT group. The analysis of cytokine levels in the BALF demonstrated a significant decrease in IL-1α in NSC and COPD model mice treated with MWCNTs 28 days post-instillation; however, the exact reason for this decrease remains unclear. COPD model mice displayed higher levels of MIP-1α, MCP-1, MMP-2, proMMP-9, and MMP-12 in the BALF on day 0 post-instillation. On day 28 post-instillation, the levels of these cytokines decreased in COPD model mice treated with V2, whereas they remained elevated in COPD model mice treated with MWCNTs. These study findings reveal that both NSC and COPD model mice exhibited inflammatory changes in lung tissue after the administration of MWCNTs. However, based on the observed increased cellular infiltration and elevated cytokine levels in the BALF, it is suggested that the lung inflammation induced by MWCNTs in the COPD model mice was not effectively attenuated at 28 days post-instillation. We concluded that the presence of pre-existing pulmonary disease in mice may hinder the recovery process after exposure to MWCNTs.

Beyeler et al. employed a mouse model of COPD to investigate the effects of MWCNTs on the pulmonary mucosal immune system [3]. They suggested that inhaled MWCNTs affect the pulmonary mucosal immune system by altering the numbers, phenotype, and activation status of antigen-presenting cell populations, and individuals with chronic respiratory disorders, such as COPD, may potentially be more susceptible to adverse health effects related to inhaled MWCNTs. Similarly, our findings suggested that individuals with chronic respiratory diseases, such as COPD, may be more susceptible to adverse health effects associated with inhaled MWCNTs. However, this study has several limitations that should be acknowledged. Firstly, it primarily focused on evaluating the inflammatory response 28 days after the intratracheal instillation of CNFs and MWCNTs, which served as an endpoint for assessing pulmonary toxicity. Future studies should aim to conduct more comprehensive evaluations to compare the toxicity profiles of CNFs and MWCNTs, including long-term assessments of the effects on animal models. Moreover, it is crucial to consider the diverse physicochemical properties of advanced nanomaterials. Therefore, it is important to further investigate and clarify the specific physicochemical properties of the materials used in animal studies and their potential toxicity. This information would enhance our understanding of the mechanisms underlying nanomaterial-induced effects and facilitate the development of safer nanomaterials in the future.

Conclusion

Our findings contribute to the current understanding of the potential health risks associated with fibrous nanomaterials, such as CNFs and MWCNTs, both in healthy individuals and those with pre-existing conditions such as COPD. Occupational exposure to fibrous nanomaterials poses a significant concern, particularly for individuals who may be more vulnerable due to conditions such as COPD. This study emphasizes the importance of implementing precautions to minimize potential health hazards when working with these materials. However, further research is necessary to comprehensively understand the development of clinical symptoms resulting from the exposure to advanced nanomaterials. These additional studies will help elucidate the precise mechanisms through which these materials impact human health and provide a more accurate risk assessment. Adopting such an approach is not only a matter of scientific inquiry but also has practical implications for creating a safer work environment, especially for employees with underlying respiratory conditions. This, in turn, promotes the responsible utilization of advanced nanomaterials across various industries.
