Abstract
. Railroad diagrams (also called “syntax diagrams”) are a common, intuitive visualization of grammars, but limited tooling and a lack of formal attention to their layout mostly confines them to hand-drawn documentation. We present the first formal treatment of railroad diagram layout along with a principled, practical implementation. We characterize the problem as compiling a diagram language (specifying conceptual components and how they connect and compose) to a layout language (specifying basic graphical shapes and their sizes and positions). We then implement a compiler that performs line wrapping to meet a target width, as well as vertical alignment and horizontal justification per user-specified policies. We frame line wrapping as an optimization problem, where we describe principled dimensions of optimality and implement corresponding heuristics. For front-end evaluation, we show that our diagram language is well-suited for common applications by describing how regular expressions and Backus-Naur form can be compiled to it. For back-end evaluation, we argue that our compiler is practical by comparing its output to diagrams laid out by hand and by other tools.

1.2.
Background

Railroad layout is a
1.5-dimensional
layout problem: intermediate between 1- and 2-dimensional ones.
Yet, compared to the extensive prior work on those problems, railroad layout is underexplored.

On one hand,
railroad layout is similar to 1-dimensional text layout in that components generally follow the reading order of text (for English, first left-to-right, then top-to-bottom), and that their linear flow must be wrapped across rows to fit a target width
(
KnuthPlassBreakingParagraphsLines1981
;
KenningaOptimalLineBreak2003
;
PeelsEtAlDocumentArchitectureText1985
;
BirdTransformationalProgrammingParagraph1986
;
deMoorGibbonsBridgingAlgorithmGap1999
)
.
Another related 1-dimensional problem is code pretty-printing, where in addition to obeying reading order and width constraints, the layout must systematically reflect the structure of the source term
(
PorncharoenwaseEtAlPrettyExpressivePrinter2023
;
BernardyPrettyNotGreedy2017
;
HughesDesignPrettyprintingLibrary1995
;
OppenPrettyprinting1980
;
PughSinofskyNewLanguageIndependentPrettyprinting1987
;
SwierstraChitilLinearBoundedFunctional2009
;
WadlerPrettierPrinter2003

Railroad layout also resembles box layout for the wrapped 1-dimensional layout of flexible 2-dimensional boxes (possibly box layouts themselves)
(
W3CCSSFlexibleBox2018
;
CiancariniEtAlHighqualityPaginationPublishing2011
;
HurstMarriottSatisficingScrollsShortcut2008
;
JacobsEtAlAdaptiveGridbasedDocument2003
;
JohariEtAlAutomaticYellowPagesPagination1997
)
, discussed in more detail in

All these problems have secondary objectives besides width, like minimizing wasted space or visual imbalance.
However, railroad layout goes beyond what they can achieve, because a row can split into two rows that independently follow the reading order until they merge, or turn backwards.

On the other hand,
railroad diagrams can be seen as labeled directed graphs or finite-state automata with stylized 2-dimensional layouts, but with many restrictions:
for instance, all published railroad layouts we found have exactly one start and end point and no crossing edges.
Conventional presentations of graphs and automata are much less rigid than railroad layouts, whether done by hand or by standard graph layout algorithms
(
TamassiaHandbookGraphDrawing2013
;

;
GraphvizcontributorsLayoutEngines2022

Series-parallel graphs and layouts come closest;
we discuss them in

2.
A formal account of railroad diagrams and their layout

There is a lot of variation in what are called “railroad diagrams” by their creators, because the term has never been formally defined before, and because they are often hand-drawn.
Much of it can be ascribed to stylistic choices that will naturally vary from author to author, like whether line wrapping is continuous (
5(b)
vs.
1(d)
) or whether boxes are centered; yet, we can still abstract a unifying schema or visual grammar.
However, we choose to exclude some of it from our scope, for three principled reasons explained below and illustrated in
Fig. 5
.
Our definitions nonetheless capture a large portion of the variation seen in the wild, which we quantify in
§4.2
and
.

First, we only consider
well-nested
diagrams:
diagrams composed of
n
-ary sequences of subdiagrams in the same direction, binary stacks of subdiagrams (potentially in opposite directions), and atomic tokens.
This almost describes
two-terminal series-parallel graphs
, except that they are usually defined to be either undirected or directed but acyclic
(
BrandstadtEtAlAlgebraicCompositionsRecursive1999
;
AlurEtAlRobustTheorySeries2023
)
, whereas we explicitly allow cycles.
(We further discuss series-parallel graphs in
and justify why stacks are binary in
§2.1
.)
This criterion naturally extends to layouts: the well-nesting of a diagram must be reflected in the visual nesting of its layout.
Specifically, we only consider layouts where the
bounding box
of each sublayout (corresponding to the decomposition described above) does not overlap with those of other, unrelated sublayouts.
The bounding boxes then have the same nesting structure as the diagram.
Figs.
5(a)
and
5(b)
are examples of ill-nested diagrams and layouts.

Next, we require layouts to have a
single semantic axis
:
all sublayouts (corresponding to the diagram decomposition) must be laid out along the same axis
(without loss of generality, the horizontal axis; vertical layouts are only a rotation away).

5(c)
is an example violation.

Lastly, we exclude constructs never seen in actual use (to our knowledge), even if some railroad diagram generators can produce them.

5(d)
is an example from a popular library.

(One additional condition is not essential but simplifies our presentation:
the backward component of a loop must not be above the forward component, as in
1(a)
.
Including such layouts would be technically straightforward but would make our formalism longer and more confusing.)

2.3.
Layout well-formedness

Unlike diagrams, not all layouts in the language above are
well-formed
.
For instance, a right-to-left horizontal concatenation does not draw its sublayouts in reverse order and direction, but must be constructed with right-to-left sublayouts in visual order (e.g. bottom left of
Fig. 9
).
In informal terms, our definition of well-formedness (to follow) aims to avoid “nonsense” layouts, where, say, a collapsed edge has no consistent direction, or sublayouts are visually disconnected.
Moreover, a well-formed layout leaves no ambiguity or context-dependence in its rendering.

To state the well-formedness rules, we must first define three other layout properties: width, number of logical and connectable rows on either side, and up- and down-connectability on either side.
The definition of
width

•

The width of a rail is its

•

•

•

•

3.

In the previous section, we defined the problem of railroad layout.

The space of possible layouts for a diagram is large, with several degrees of freedom at each level of nesting;

and the height of a horizontal concatenation can be computed by vertically aligning the internal tips of its subdiagrams to form a “baseline”, and taking the difference between the largest extent above and below that baseline of any subdiagram.
