Application of SHAP visualization and nomogram based on PET/CT radiomics combined models for risk stratification in high-grade invasive lung adenocarcinoma
Authors: Ning Ma, Hongyan Du, Yu Wu, Zhan Li, Jun Li, Xiaohua Lin, Duxia Yu, Yizhe Zhang, Mengjie Dong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThis study aimed to develop and validate a PET/CT radiomics combined model integrated with visualization tools for preoperative risk stratification of high-grade invasive lung adenocarcinoma (ILA).MethodsA total of 202 ILA patients with 207 pathologically confirmed lesions were retrospectively enrolled and randomly divided into training (70%) and validation (30%) sets. Patients with ≥ 20% micropapillary or solid subtypes were classified as the high-risk group. Multivariate logistic regression was used to develop combined clinical and radiomics models. Model performance was evaluated using ROC, calibration, and decision curve analyses, and visualized with Shapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) and a nomogram.ResultsThe combined model demonstrated superior diagnostic performance, with higher AUCs (Delong test, all P < 0.05) and calibration curves (Hosmer-Lemeshow test, all P > 0.05), outperforming the clinical model in both the training and validation datasets. It also provided greater clinical net benefit across the central decision threshold range of 0.15 to 0.85. SHAP analysis identified Radscore and SUVmax (mean SHAP values = 0.35 and 0.2, respectively) as the primary risk factors for high-grade ILA.ConclusionWe validated a PET/CT-based combined model utilizing SHAP analysis and a nomogram for preoperative risk stratification in high-grade ILA. The model exhibits high accuracy, commendable calibration, and clinical benefit. This noninvasive, interpretable instrument facilitates objective risk assessment, personalized decision-making, and precise management for ILA.

Introduction

Globally, lung cancer remains the leading cause of both cancer incidence and mortality. Invasive lung adenocarcinoma (ILA), the most common histological subtype, accounts for approximately 40% to 50% of all lung cancer cases and constitutes 70% to 90% of resected tumors, with its incidence showing a global upward trend [1–3]. Most patients are diagnosed at an advanced stage, resulting in a five-year survival rate below 20%. This adverse prognosis is partially attributable to tumor heterogeneity, especially high-grade variants like micropapillary and solid types, which are associated with worse outcomes, increased recurrence rates, lymph node metastasis, and decreased survival [4–6].

The 2021 WHO Guidelines for Lung Tumors, in conjunction with the 2020 International Association for the Study of Lung Cancer (IASLC) grading system for ILA, specify that a high-grade histological subtype comprising 20% or more of the tumor is associated with a less favorable prognosis [7, 8]. These patients experience a 30% to 40% reduction in the five-year disease-free survival rate and face a two- to threefold increase in the risk of recurrence compared to patients with less than 20% 2,3,7. Accurately estimating the proportion of high-grade tumors is essential for informing treatment decisions, especially for high-risk patients who necessitate intensive follow-up or early intervention. However, while such estimations are beneficial for assessing microscopic invasion, small tissue samples acquired through bronchoscopy or biopsy are insufficient to reliably determine the overall proportion of high-grade tumors within the entire tumor mass. Research indicates that changes in molecular stress regulation in the tumor microenvironment can influence tumor progression and subtype differentiation. Relying solely on tissue biopsy may not fully reveal the tumors’ complex biology [9, 10]. Therefore, the development of a non-invasive and accurate preoperative technique is essential for personalized treatment.

Currently, preoperative assessment methods in clinical practice primarily rely on imaging examinations; however, conventional techniques have inherent limitations. In CT morphological evaluation, clinicians assess tumor malignancy by visualizing features such as lobulation, spiculation, and pleural retraction. Nonetheless, these evaluations remain subjective and may vary among practitioners. Furthermore, they lack the capacity to identify high-grade histological subtypes. In PET assessment, parameters such as the standardized uptake value maximum (SUVmax), metabolic tumor volume (MTV), and total lesion glycolysis (TLG) reflect overall tumor activity. However, they do not capture metabolic heterogeneity, which is crucial for distinguishing tumor grades [11–13].

Radiomics technology converts tumor shape, density patterns, and metabolism into measurable digital markers, allowing non-invasive evaluation of tumor biological characteristics [14–16]. Research shows that CT radiomics features alone can predict high-grade histological subtypes, including micropapillary and solid variants [17–19]. PET/CT dual-modality data provide more comprehensive insights into tumor biology and more accurately characterize tumor features. However, the “interpretability” and “practicality” of radiomics-based clinical prediction models present significant challenges to their broader use and are often viewed as “black boxes.” This complicates clinicians’ ability to understand the contribution of each feature to the prediction process, thereby diminishing trust. Visualization tools, such as nomograms, translate complex multi-factor models into straightforward scoring systems, enabling clinicians to estimate patient risk and improve clinical application [20–22]. Shapley Additive Explanations (SHAP) is an interpretability technique rooted in game theory that facilitates the visualization of each variable’s importance within machine learning models and quantifies the predictive contribution of features. Through the utilization of SHAP mean plots and summary plots, the influence of individual features on model prediction outcomes can be clearly illustrated. When integrated with nomograms, SHAP has the potential to enhance the clinical interpretability of radiomics models and support clinicians in gaining a more comprehensive understanding of the model’s prediction process [23].

This study aims to integrate PET/CT dual-modal radiomics with clinical data to develop a predictive model, utilizing SHAP and a nomogram for visualization. It intends to validate the model’s effectiveness for risk stratification in high-grade ILA, offering a useful tool for preoperative clinical assessment and personalized treatment.

Materials and methods

Patient information

We collected data from patients with pulmonary nodules who underwent 18F-FDG PET/CT scans at Peking University Shenzhen Hospital between May 2022 and July 2025. The overall research process is summarized in Fig. 1. Participants were enrolled if their pathological diagnosis was not confirmed by other imaging methods. Clinical data, including gender, age, smoking history, and tumor markers (CEA, CA125, CYFRA21-1), were obtained from hospital records. All cases met the following inclusion criteria: (1) Lesions with visible edges on CT lung window images (width 1300, level − 700), measuring 0.5 to 5 cm; (2) No other chest diseases or lymphadenopathy; (3) High-quality images with accurate CT-PET registration; (4) Lesions not adjacent to the liver or mediastinal blood pool; (5) Pathological confirmation of lung adenocarcinoma within one month; (6) No prior tumors. The cases were randomly divided into a training set (70%) for model development and a validation set (30%) for performance testing (Fig. 2).

PET/CT imaging method

The scan was performed on a GE Discovery MI 128-slice digital PET/CT. Patients fasted for at least 6 h, with blood glucose kept below 10 mmol/L. The administered dose was 5.55 MBq/kg. After a 50-minute rest period, PET/CT images were taken from the top of the skull to the proximal femur. The CT scan used spiral acquisition at 120 kV, 80–200 mA, with a 0.5-second rotation time and a pitch of 0.984:1. Data were reconstructed with an iterative algorithm at a 3.75 mm slice thickness. PET imaging was acquired in 3D mode over 6–8 bed positions (2 min per position) and reconstructed with the Q-Clear algorithm using 25 super-iterations at a slice thickness of 3.75 mm.

Clinical image analysis

PET/CT images were evaluated using the Advantage Workstation (version 4.7). Two experienced nuclear medicine physicians with more than 10 years of experience performed an anonymous clinical review. They measured the maximum tumor diameter on CT scans and identified morphological features, including lobulation, spiculation, pleural retraction, and invasion of the tracheovascular bundle. SUVmax, MTV, and TLG were calculated from PET images by manually delineating the region of interest. Two nuclear medicine attending physicians performed an inter-observer consistency assessment of CT morphological features—including lobulation, spiculation, pleural retraction, and tracheovascular bundle invasion—and PET metabolic features such as SUVmax, MTV, and TLG. Quantitative evaluation was performed using the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC), with an ICC score above 0.75 indicating excellent inter-observer reliability.

Image preprocessing and segmentation

PET/CT images were imported into 3D Slicer (version 5.9.0, https://www.slicer.org) for preprocessing and segmentation. CT images were resampled to 1-mm isotropic voxels using linear interpolation, and their intensities were discretized with a bin width of 20. PET images were discretized with a bin width of 0.1 and then rigidly registered to the corresponding resampled CT images to eliminate spatial misalignment. Two nuclear medicine physicians independently delineated the region of interest for the primary lesion on PET images in a blinded manner, strictly adhering to clinical guidelines, defining the tumor volume exceeding 41% of the tumor SUVmax as the volume of interest (VOI) [24, 25]. The final VOI was determined by the intersection of the two delineations. The inter-observer consistency of VOI delineation was evaluated using the Dice similarity coefficient (DSC), with a threshold of ≥ 0.9 indicating excellent agreement. For lesions with a DSC below 0.9, a senior physician with over twenty years of clinical experience re-delineated the VOI to ensure accurate segmentation [26].

Calculation of radscore

The open-source PyRadiomics package (version 3.0.1, https://pyradiomics.readthedocs.io/) extracted radiomic features from CT and PET images within the VOI. These features included shape, first-order statistics, and texture features derived from the gray-level co-occurrence matrix (GLCM)…. All feature extraction procedures strictly adhered to the guidelines of the Image Biomarker Standardization Initiative (IBSI) [15, 26]. Additionally, wavelet transforms were applied to the original images to generate higher-order radiomic features. Previous studies have shown that this approach can increase the AUC by 0.08 with a statistically significant difference (p = 0.024) [27]. Before feature selection, the median imputation method was used to handle outliers in the extracted radiomic features. Then, Z-score normalization was applied to standardize all features, mitigating the impact of dimensional disparities. All the subsequent steps involving feature selection and model development were carried out solely in the training set. During feature selection, pairs of features with Pearson correlation coefficients of 0.7 or higher are considered redundant. A greedy iterative algorithm systematically removes features exhibiting higher correlations while retaining the counterpart in each pair. This process persists until all remaining features demonstrate correlation coefficients below the predetermined threshold. Next, the least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) with 5-fold cross-validation was used to optimize hyperparameters and select the optimal penalty coefficient λ. This optimal λ was then applied to eliminate high-dimensional redundant features and simplify the model structure. Finally, core features were identified using logistic regression. These features and their coefficients were combined linearly to calculate each patient’s Radscore, which served as the basis for developing a radiomics model.

Model evaluation

Multivariate logistic regression was utilized to develop a combined model integrating clinical and Radiomics data. The DeLong test was applied to compare the areas under the ROC curves for the training and validation datasets. To mitigate potential overfitting, 1000 bootstrap resamples were conducted on the training dataset to compute optimism-corrected AUC. Calibration performance was assessed through calibration curves, the Hosmer-Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test, as well as calibration intercept and slope for both training and validation datasets. Standard decision curve analysis (DCA) was performed, complemented by key clinical metrics at the threshold of maximum Youden index and clinical impact curve analysis, in order to thoroughly evaluate net clinical benefit and the influence on real-world decision-making. Furthermore, SHAP visualization plots were produced to quantify each predictive feature’s contribution to individual predictions. Additionally, a nomogram was developed to facilitate intuitive clinical use and improve the model’s practicality.

Pathological diagnosis

Two attending pathologists performed an anonymous, independent review and cross-verification of the surgically resected specimens. Consistent with the 2021 WHO Classification of Lung Tumors and the 2020 IASLC Grading System for Invasive Lung Adenocarcinoma, histological subtypes (lepidic, acinar, papillary, micropapillary, and solid) were classified, and all subtypes comprising ≥ 5% of the tumor volume were recorded. Cases with ≥ 20% high-grade subtypes (micropapillary and solid) were classified as high-risk, while remaining cases were classified as low-risk.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were conducted in IBM SPSS Statistics (version 26.0) and R (version 4.4.3). The unit of analysis was each individual lesion. To account for potential within-patient correlation among multiple lesions, robust standard errors clustered at the patient level were used in all regression analyses. The nomogram was developed using the rms package (version 6.7-0). The SHAP analysis was conducted employing shapviz (version 0.9.0) and xgboost (version 1.7.5). All graphical figures were made with ggplot2 (version 3.4.4). Quantitative data were reported as mean ± SD or median (interquartile range, IQR). Intergroup comparisons of quantitative data were performed using the independent samples t-test or Mann-Whitney U test, and categorical data were reported as counts (percentages) and compared using the Pearson chi-square test. The clinical performance of the models was evaluated using sensitivity, specificity, accuracy, positive predictive value, and negative predictive value.

Results

Clinical characteristics and consistency assessment

The study involved 202 patients with 207 lesions who underwent thoracoscopic surgical interventions. Pathological confirmation revealed a total of 107 low-risk cases and 100 high-risk cases. All lesions were divided in a 7:3 ratio, resulting in a training set of 144 cases (74 low-risk and 70 high-risk) and a validation set of 63 cases (33 low-risk and 30 high-risk). The clinical and imaging characteristics of both sets are summarized in Table 1. The consistency assessment among physicians revealed that all features had an ICC above 0.75. All Dice coefficients for the lesions were at least 0.91, confirming the high reliability of the radiomics feature extraction results in this study (Fig. 3).

Radiomics model development

PyRadiomics extracted 851 features from CT and PET images, comprising 14 morphological, 18 first-order, 75 texture, and 744 wavelet-based features. Correlation analysis identified 161 features with coefficients below 0.7. LASSO regression combined with five-fold cross-validation was used to determine the optimal regularization parameter by minimizing the mean bias. The results show that when the mean bias reaches its minimum (≈ 1.0), log(λ) ≈ − 3.4, corresponding to λ_(min) ≈ 0.03, and 25 features were selected (Fig. 4). Logistic regression further narrowed this down to 6 key features. The Radscore formula is as follows: Radscore = -1.01 + 2.59 × CT_original_firstorder_90Percentile − 0.67 × CT_wavelet-LHH_glszm_SizeZoneNonUniformityNormalized + 1.05 × CT_wavelet-HHL_firstorder_Mean − 0.57 × CT_wavelet-HLH_glszm_SmallAreaLowGrayLevelEmphasis + 1.97 × PET_wavelet-LLH_glcm_InverseVariance − 0.8 × PET_wavelet-HLH_glszm_GrayLevelNonUniformityNormalized. Figure 5 shows the importance of these features alongside the Radscore waterfall chart for both the training and testing sets.

Model integration

Univariate logistic regression analysis revealed that most variables were risk factors for the high-risk group, with odds ratios ranging from 1.029 to 225.0. The multivariate analysis incorporated SUVmax, TLG, and Radscore within the combined model (Table 2). The model estimates the probability for the high-risk group as P = 1 / (1 + e^(-x)), where X = -3.758 + 0.192 × SUVmax − 0.011 × TLG + 4.046 × Radscore. Logistic regression modeling results (Table 3) showed that the combined model had good calibration (Hosmer-Lemeshow p = 0.256) and no significant multicollinearity (all tolerances > 0.2 and all Variance Inflation Factor < 5).

Model comparison and visualization

Table 4 illustrates the clinical usefulness of each model. The combined model developed in this study showed significantly superior diagnostic performance than both the clinical model and radiomics model in the training set (apparent AUC = 0.917) and validation set (AUC = 0.884). After 1000 bootstrap optimism correction in the training set, the optimism was 0.009, and the corrected AUC was 0.908, indicating minimal overfitting and robust model stability. The DeLong test revealed statistically significant differences between the combined model and the clinical model (P = 0.0043, 0.0084; Fig. 6). Calibration curves indicated excellent fit for the combined model (Hosmer-Lemeshow tests: training set P = 0.621, validation set P = 0.447). Quantitative calibration metrics showed the training set had an intercept of 9.76 × 10⁻⁶ and a slope of 1.000, while the validation set had an intercept of -0.094 and a slope of 0.895, confirming favorable calibration consistency. The clinical model showed poor calibration (P = 0.032) with noticeable bias in the validation set (Fig. 7). DCA showed that the combined model offered a higher net benefit than other models within the threshold probability range of 0.15–0.85. This advantage was especially evident within the key decision interval of 0.2–0.7, highlighting its greater clinical value (Fig. 8). At the threshold corresponding to the maximum Youden index of 0.48, the model classified 52.78% and 53.97% of patients as high-risk in the training and validation sets, respectively, with true positive rates of 44.44% and 41.27%. The net benefits were 0.368 and 0.295, and unnecessary interventions were avoided in 38.54% and 32.80% of patients, respectively. Furthermore, a clinical impact curve was incorporated to illustrate the number of high-risk patients and true positive cases across various risk thresholds, thereby directly reflecting the model’s practical implications in clinical decision-making (Fig. 9). The SHAP mean and summary plots showed that Radscore (mean SHAP = 0.35) and SUVmax (mean SHAP = 0.2) were the main predictors in the combined model and were identified as risk factors for the high-risk group (Fig. 10). The nomogram allowed estimation of high-risk probabilities from 10% to 70%, significantly improving the model’s clinical utility (Fig. 11).

Discussion

Traditionally, limitations in pathological diagnostic methods resulted in a basic, imperfect classification system for lung adenocarcinoma. For instance, bronchioloalveolar carcinoma once included various adenocarcinoma subtypes with different prognoses; some had nearly 100% five-year survival rates, while others, representing advanced adenocarcinomas, had less favorable outcomes [28–30]. In 2011, the IASLC introduced a new classification for ILA, recommending that pathological examinations specify histological subtypes exceeding 5%. By 2021, they advised that ILAs containing 20% or more high-grade subtypes, classified as grade 3 (poorly differentiated), should be considered a significant prognostic factor. The latest WHO classifications and academic studies confirm the superiority of this revised system [31–33]. The new classification necessitates a more precise preoperative tumor risk assessment, thereby engendering an urgent requirement for a straightforward, accurate, and user-friendly method in clinical practice.

This study evaluated the risk of ILA employing a bimodal PET/CT model combined with radiomics data, achieving AUCs of 0.917 in the training set and 0.884 in the validation set. These results significantly outperform traditional diagnostic models (P < 0.05), and the AUC of 0.917 surpasses the range of 0.852–0.902 reported in several previous studies using enhanced CT for the differentiation of high-grade subtypes. This difference may be attributed to the use of bimodal imaging features and advanced high-order transformations in this study [34–36]. Previous studies have confirmed that the predictive performance following high-order transformation surpasses that of conventional unimodal and clinical models [37].

Both the odds ratio and the average SHAP value of Radscore indicate that it is the most influential variable in the combined model. CT_original_firstorder_90Percentile reflects the lesion’s CT value skewness, with a positive coefficient (2.59), suggesting that higher-density regions are associated with an increased risk of high-grade components. This aligns with features of high-grade lung adenocarcinoma, which involve dense tumor proliferation and increased density. CT wavelet features (e.g., CT_wavelet-HHL_firstorder_Mean) capture structural heterogeneity, with high-grade tumors showing abnormal wavelet patterns due to poor differentiation [38, 39]. PET features, such as PET_wavelet-LLH_glcm_InverseVariance, with a positive coefficient (1.97), indicate greater variability in metabolic signals, which is associated with a higher risk grade and corresponds to increased metabolic activity in high-grade tumors. Conversely, PET_wavelet-HLH_glszm_GrayLevelNonUniformityNormalized has a negative coefficient (-0.8), possibly due to the concentration of gray levels in metabolic hotspots, which requires further investigation [40]. These radiomics features more effectively capture microscopic lesion details than traditional PET/CT evaluations.

In multivariate analysis, TLG showed a modest negative correlation with the high-risk group (OR = 0.979, P = 0.044), unlike the positive correlation in univariate analysis (OR = 1.029, P < 0.001). This likely results from the univariate model’s inability to account for confounders. Since TLG is derived from MTV and SUVmean, its predictive power diminishes when the multivariate model includes Radscore and SUVmax, which better capture tumor biology and reduce SUVmean’s influence. MTV lacked significance in risk stratification in the combined model. Therefore, TLG should not be used alone for high-grade ILA risk assessment. Its weak negative correlation isn’t protective but reflects the interaction of tumor volume and metabolic activity. TLG should be interpreted with Radscore and SUVmax. The combined model’s DCA curve showed a higher net benefit within the 0.20–0.70 threshold, important for clinical decisions like biopsy or thoracoscopy.

A limitation of this study is its single-center, retrospective design and relatively modest sample size, coupled with the absence of an independent external validation cohort. These factors may limit the generalizability, robustness, and real-world applicability of the proposed model across diverse clinical settings. Furthermore, although this study visualized and attempted to interpret the model utilizing SHAP and a nomogram, the biological implications of the radiomic features necessitate additional validation through fundamental medical research to ensure the scientific rigor of the study.

In summary, this study has developed a PET/CT combined model integrating radiomics, which facilitates accurate risk stratification of high-grade ILA and provides a practical and interpretable tool for personalized treatment planning, thereby enhancing its clinical translational value.
