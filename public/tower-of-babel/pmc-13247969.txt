Optimization of X-ray acquisition parameters for reduced radiation exposure while maintaining 2D-DSA image quality during lower limb angioplasty
Authors: Charyl Jia Qi Yap, Byung Jun Kim, Muhammad Syadad Bin Sulaiman, Tze Tec Chong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAchieving optimal image quality with minimal radiation exposure allows operators to accurately assess and treat target lesions during percutaneous transluminal angioplasty (PTA) without being subjected to increased radiation risk. Depending on clinical needs, imaging protocols are optimized and evaluated. This study aims to compare two imaging protocols—conventional dose protocol (CDP) and low dose protocol (LDP)—to evaluate the impact of fine-tuned X-ray acquisition parameters (detector entrance dose, copper filtration, tube voltage) and post-processing parameters (edge enhancement, window contrast).MethodsThirty patients who underwent PTA between February to April 2021 were retrospectively reviewed. A representative two-dimensional digital subtraction angiography (2D-DSA) image was selected for both image quality and radiation dose comparison. Radiation dose was assessed using dose area product per frame (DAP/frame) and air kerma per frame (AK/frame) as normalized metrics to exclude effect of procedural duration. Each image was assessed quantitatively by measuring contrast-to-noise ratio (CNR) and qualitatively by two experienced vascular surgeons with a 5-point Likert scale.ResultsRadiation dose with LDP showed a significant reduction in DAP/frame (2.49±1.65 µGy·m2 in CDP vs. 0.48±0.42 µGy·m2 in LDP) and AK/frame (0.10±0.07 µGy in CDP vs. 0.02±0.02 µGy in LDP). CNR was higher with LDP than with CDP (0.83±0.81 in CDP vs. 2.99±2.44 in LDP). Qualitative image quality assessment demonstrated no significant differences between protocols across all criteria (P>0.05).ConclusionsParametric optimization of X-ray acquisition protocols in angiography system can potentially reduce radiation exposure without compromising image quality.

Introduction

Excessive ionizing radiation exposure to the patient increases the risk for skin cancer, tissue damage, eye cataracts, etc. (1). Statistically, medical procedures account for over 96% of human exposure to artificial radiation sources (2). According to the National Council of Radiation Protection and Measurements (NCRP) report, the risk from ionizing medical radiation exposures in the United States is estimated to have doubled over the past two decades. Additionally, an average US citizen receives approximately 3.6mSv of background radiation exposure annually (3). The most commonly used metrics for ionizing radiation in modern medical procedures include dose area product (DAP) and air kerma (AK) (4). DAP measures the total radiation exposure during medical radiation procedure in units of Gy.cm2, while AK measures radiation incident at a specific location in units of mGy. Although DAP provides a reference to the overall general dose administered as well as the potential risk, it does not indicate radiation dose at a specific anatomical location, as AK does. In radiography, the results of both DAP and AK are used complimentarily to establish the patient radiation dose (5).

Common strategies to reduce radiation exposure during ionizing radiation imaging include collimation, limiting exposure and fluoro time, reducing source imaging distance (SID) and keeping the table above the isocentre and the detector close to the patient (6). According to Sujar et al. [2022], these approaches are beneficial as they reduce patient radiation dose while simultaneously increasing clarity through enhanced contrast-to-noise ratio (CNR) (7). Inappropriate selection of patient exposure factors could result in either overexposure or underexposure to radiation (8). Underexposure leads to low CNR values that can result in unsatisfactory image quality for expert review, while overexposure could produce high CNR but unnecessarily increases radiation risk. The right amount of acceptable exposure values may vary based on the clinical setting. However, most hospitals typically employ established default settings that provide acceptable image quality while maintaining radiation exposure risk at minimal and clinically acceptable levels.

In our local cohort, most patients treated for chronic limb threatening ischemia (CLTI) present with significant comorbidities—over 80% are diabetic, 40% have end stage renal failure and 90% have hypertension (9). In addition, 70% of lower limb angioplasties performed were for multi-level disease and the arteries treated tend to be moderately to severely calcified. Achieving optimal image quality with the use of minimal radiation is of utmost importance for these patients to ensure that operators can accurately assess and adequately treat the disease.

In this study, a comparison of radiation exposure and image quality of the representative two-dimensional digital subtraction angiography (2D-DSA) obtained from conventional dose protocol (CDP) and low dose protocol (LDP) during lower limb angioplasty was conducted retrospectively to evaluate the impact of X-ray acquisition parameter adjustments. We present this article in accordance with the STARD reporting checklist (available at https://qims.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/qims-2026-1-0302/rc).

Methods

Study participants

In this retrospective study, 30 consecutive patients who underwent lower limb angioplasty at Singapore General Hospital from February to April 2021 were included. Patients whose lower limb angioplasty was performed using imaging protocols other than CDP or LDP were excluded. CDP was the default protocol used at our institution and LDP was later introduced in February 2021. Hence, the last 15 patients who underwent lower limb angioplasty from February 2021 were assigned to CDP cohort, and first 15 cases performed after February 2021 with the new protocol were assigned to LDP cohort. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments. This study was approved by SingHealth Centralised Institutional Review Board (CIRB Ref: 2020/3802). Informed consent was waived in this retrospective study.

To illustrate differences in imaging protocol, images from a routine angioplasty procedure were collected. This case study is separate from the 30 patients analyzed, and written consent for additional imaging and the use of angiographic images in publication was obtained from the patient.

X-ray acquisition protocols

All imaging was acquired with ARTIS Pheno (Siemens Healthineers, Forchheim, Germany) in the hybrid operating theatre of a tertiary hospital. During procedures, 2D-DSA scenes were acquired using either CDP or LDP. Representative parameters of both protocols are summarized in Table 1. As a key parameter, detector dose was reduced from 2.4 µGy/frame in CDP to 0.36 µGy/frame in LDP to achieve radiation dose saving. Consequently, other parameters were adjusted accordingly to compensate for this change without compromising image quality. For the comparison of radiation dose and image quality, a representative 2D-DSA image of a contrast-filled vessel was selected from each case.

Assessment of radiation dose and image quality

Due to the heterogenous characteristics in terms of acquisitions, one representative image that shows fully-filled contrast medium in infrainguinal vessel was chosen by the radiographer for quantitative analysis. CNR of this representative image was measured via imageJ software (10). CNR is defined by a function of gray-scale intensity and noise at both the signal (contrast enhanced area) as well as background (subtracted area without contrast).

Radiation dose of the representative 2D-DSA image was assessed using DAP and AK, which are provided from the angiography system. In this study, as an indicator of radiation dose, DAP and AK are divided by number of frames (DAP/frame, AK/frame) to avoid unwanted impact from various total frames for each case.

For qualitative analysis, de-identified 2D-DSA images were assessed individually by two high-volume senior vascular surgeons, both certified and are highly experienced in the subject matter. Assessment was based on a 5-point Likert scale ranging from 1 as “non-diagnostic or highly unsatisfactory” to 5 as “excellent visualization or highly satisfactory”. Six criteria were evaluated for image quality: overall image quality, image contrast, image brightness, motion artefact, edge enhancement and image sharpness.

Statistical analysis

As a small pilot study, a sample size of convenience (n=30) was selected. For statistical comparison, scores for image quality assessment and metrics for radiation dose were compared using non-parametric tests, specifically Mann-Whitney U test for continuous and ordinal variables, and chi-square test for categorical variables. Statistical significance was defined as P<0.05. To assess inter-rater reliability (IRR) between both surgeons, Cohen’s Kappa was used. Kappa values ≤0 indicate no agreement, 0.01–0.20 none to slight, 0.21–0.40 fair, 0.41–0.60 moderate, 0.61–0.80 substantial and 0.81–1.00 almost perfect agreement (11).

Results

Demographics

Thirty cases, 15 in each arm respectively, were included in analysis. Demographics of these patients are as summarized in Table 2. The majority of patients were male (80% in CDP vs. 53.3% in LDP, P=0.245) with similar mean ages (66.5±9.2 and 67.5±10.9 years for CDP and LDP respectively, P=0.520) and mean body mass indices (23.4±3.8 and 25.0±3.8 kg/m2 for CDP and LDP respectively, P=0.191). No significant differences were detected between the two cohorts.

Quantitative image quality assessment demonstrated that LDP had a significantly higher CNR than CDP (1.44±0.59 in CDP vs. 4.51±3.52 in LDP, P<0.001, Figure 1C). In the qualitative assessment across six assessment criteria, there were no significant differences observed between CDP and LDP (overall image quality: 4.17±0.53 in CDP vs. 3.98±0.77 in LDP, P=0.309; image contrast: 4.10±0.61 in CDP vs. 3.80±0.78 in LDP, P=0.084; image brightness: 3.97±0.61 in CDP vs. 3.83±0.64 in LDP, P=0.447; motion artefact: 3.93±0.94 in CDP vs. 3.80±0.72 in LDP, P=0.333; edge enhancement: 3.93±0.58 in CDP vs. 3.83±0.74 in LDP, P=0.509; image sharpness: 3.97±0.61 in CDP vs. 3.76±0.74 in LDP, P=0.241; Figure 1D).

However, inter-rater reliability showed none to slight agreement for most parameters, with kappa values ranging from 0.01 to 0.229 for each assessment criteria (Table 3).

Case study

We present a case study of a patient with complete occlusion of the anterior tibial artery (ATA). Pre-treatment angiograms acquired with both CDP and LDP at the same angle below-the-knee (Figure 2) and below-the-ankle (Figure 3) showed good contrast enhancement. Although the background noise was more prominent when imaged with LDP than with CDP in Figure 2, the clinician’s overall assessment was not affected. In terms of radiation dose, CDP and LDP have 59.27 µGy·m2 (2.28 µGy·m2/frame) and 8.74 µGy·m2 (0.40 µGy·m2/frame), respectively.

In Figure 3, pre-operative angiogram from both CDP and LDP showed flow to foot via dorsalis pedis artery (DPA) and plantar artery, with visualization of a complete pedal plantar loop. Similarly, background noise was more apparent when imaged with LDP. The smaller vessels such as the medial and lateral plantar artery, as well as its collaterals, were harder to visualized with LDP compared to the CDP. In this comparison, radiation dose was 56.45 µGy·m2 (1.71 µGy·m2/frame) in CDP and 8.87 µGy·m2 (0.33 µGy·m2/frame) in LDP, respectively.

Discussion

During image acquisition, any adjustment made to the image parameters such as pulse width, focus spot, processing mode, edge enhancement, noise reduction, and frame control rate results in changes in the radiation dose on the patient’s skin or internal organs (12). The most desirable parameter adjustments are those that achieve low DAP and AK while simultaneously maintaining high-quality image. From the results above, parameter adjustment from the default values to the new values led to an overall reduction in radiation as evidenced by reduced DAP and AK values while increasing CNR, which gives rise to improved image clarity. This is in line with the desired behaviour outlined by Sujar et al. [2022] (7).

There are possible explanations on a reduction of radiation dose. First of all, a reduced detector dose from 2.4 to 0.36 µGy/frame is the key parametric change to achieve a significant reduction of DAP and AK. The decrease in detector dose reduces amount of X-ray radiation per frame. Consequently, tube current (mA), another key parameter in radiation exposure, has also decreased from 415.8 to 216.7 mA. Secondly, an averaged thickness of copper filtration is increased from 0.2 to 0.3 mm. Although preset value in a range of copper filtration thickness is the same in both protocols as 0.1–0.3 mm, it is assumed that LDP provides a thicker copper filtration based on the automatic exposure control (AEC) in the angiography system as an autonomous adjustment of representative X-ray acquisition parameters such as tube voltage (kV), tube current (mA), pulse width (ms), focal spot size, copper filtration thickness to achieve the preset detector dose. As AEC could have more options for the radiation dose saving with the lower detector dose setting, naturally more thicker copper filtration could be applied which could contribute an additional radiation dose saving (13,14).

Despite reduced radiation exposure, a higher CNR was acquired. This is possible due to the decreased averaged tube voltage (kV) from 76.5 to 69.0 kV. Low tube voltage imaging could enhance the image contrast by exaggerating difference in gray scale intensity at the contrast-enhanced and background. In addition to that, background noise could be supressed by decreased edge enhancement (EE) setting for both native (from 15% to 10%) and subtracted (from 30% to 15%) images. Noise level could be increased as a trade-off for enhanced edge definition under higher EE. As a result of reduced EE setting, it could affect CNR which is a function the noise level (14,15).

Although there was higher CNR with LDP than with CDP, qualitative image quality revealed no significant difference between both protocols for all imaging parameters. However, there was poor inter-rater reliability. Subjective analysis by physicians could be affected by human factors such as preferrable image impressions or limited distinguishable resolution to be defined compared to the image processing (14). Future studies can consider inclusion of more raters and structured training for raters. As represented by the case study shared, despite background noises and challenges with visualizing collaterals, overall assessment was acceptable and may be worth the trade-off of a reduced radiation dose in LDP.

This study has several limitations. First of all, results in this single-centre study are obtained from the designated angiography system with a small number of samples (n=15 for each group). As such, these results might not be generalized to another institutions or systems. Secondly, due to the retrospective nature, there were several uncontrolled procedure-related variables (e.g., patient’s demographics, location of the catheter tip for injecting contrast, varying degrees of contrast filling) which might influence the results including procedure time, number of 2D-DSA acquisition scenes or CNR. The radiographer involved in selection of representative image is not blinded to the imaging protocol used, which may result in potential selection bias. Finally, this study compared image quality and radiation dose for the representative 2D-DSA image. Comprehensive analysis throughout interventional procedures is required to evaluate its clinical usefulness.

To further investigate the impact of parametric changes in the angiography system, a prospective study with controlled environment is necessary. A larger cohort is required to ensure generalization of results.

Conclusions

This study demonstrated radiation dose saving without compromising image quality by adjustment of angiography parameters for lower limb angioplasty. This strategy could inform machine level presets to reduce radiation exposure for both patients and medical staff working in angiography suite.
