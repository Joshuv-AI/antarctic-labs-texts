Cervical esophagogastric anastomosis using a retrosternal gastric conduit in an adult patient with congenital type A long-gap esophageal atresia after 18 years of gastrostomy: a case report

Background

Esophageal atresia (EA) is a congenital interruption of esophageal continuity, occurring in approximately 1 in 3,000 live births [1, 2]. Primary neonatal repair is the standard of care and generally yields favorable outcomes [3, 4]. In contrast, long-gap EA (LGEA)—wherein the proximal and distal segments are separated to a degree that precludes tension-free primary anastomosis—often necessitates staged strategies and, in selected cases, esophageal replacement [5–7]. Most reconstructions are completed in infancy or early childhood, and definitive reconstruction performed in adulthood, after prolonged enteral feeding, has rarely been reported [5, 7–9].

Three practical considerations support contemporary LGEA management: (i) confirming the anatomy and excluding the possibility of residual fistulae based on airway evaluation [10]; (ii) selecting the conduit transfer route-posterior mediastinal or retrosternal-according to prior thoracic surgery, adhesions, and anticipated conduit reach [8, 11–13]; and (iii) optimizing anastomotic perfusion and minimizing tension, using strategies such as traction-based lengthening (Foker), circular myotomy (Livaditis), and spiral myotomy (Kimura), described in pertinent studies on EA [6–9, 11, 14]. Delayed reconstruction of long-gap esophageal atresia in adulthood presents unique anatomical and technical challenges compared with infant repair. Prolonged gastrostomy dependence may result in nutritional adaptation, altered esophageal anatomy, and uncertainty regarding mediastinal conditions, which complicate conduit selection and route planning. Consequently, adult reconstruction after long-term enteral dependence remains extremely rare, and the optimal surgical strategy has not been well established [5, 7, 10]. We herein report durable restoration of oral feeding in an adult with type A LGEA after 18 years of gastrostomy dependence and describe the key surgical decision-making factors.

Case presentation

An 18-year-old woman (height: 131.4 cm; weight: 30.4 kg; body mass index: 17.6 kg/m2) presented for definitive reconstruction of type A LGEA. In the neonatal period, thoracotomy was performed in her home country with the intent to repair the atresia; definitive reconstruction was not completed, and a gastrostomy was created. At 2 years of age, a left cervical esophagostomy was performed for recurrent aspiration. No referral letters or operative notes were available; the medical history was reliant on family report and current imaging. The patient had congenital external ear anomalies, including left external auditory canal stenosis and right bony external auditory canal atresia, with no other associated major anomalies identified. The patient’s menstrual cycles were regular, with no clinical features of endocrine dysfunction. The serum albumin level was 4.5 g/dL preoperatively. No relevant family history or social risk factors were identified. The patient was not on any regular medications. Because of limitations within the healthcare system in her home country, definitive surgical treatment was not available. Moreover, socioeconomic circumstances hindered overseas referral; ultimately, she traveled to Japan and underwent reconstruction. Contrast-enhanced computed tomography (CT) demonstrated a dilated esophagus, left cervical esophagostomy, and absence of a visible mediastinal esophagus(Fig. 1a–c). Water-soluble contrast esophagography showed a J-shaped proximal pouch with egress via the cervical esophagostomy (Fig. 2a). Retrograde endoscopy via the gastrostomy identified a distal esophageal blind stump approximately 3 cm above the cardia (Fig. 2b–d).

Preoperative magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) did not identify a tracheoesophageal fistula (TEF). The internal diameter of the proximal esophagus was 70 mm on CT and MRI. The esophagostomy orifice was located at the apex of the proximal pouch (end-on) rather than the sidewall. Given the high likelihood of posterior mediastinal adhesions after prior thoracotomy and the anticipated difficulty in conduit transfer through the posterior mediastinum, a retrosternal route to the cervical anastomosis was planned.

Surgical technique

Under general anesthesia with endotracheal intubation, the patient was placed in the supine position with slight neck extension. Based on the findings of MRI and the clinical course, the likelihood of a residual TEF was considered extremely low; bronchoscopy after anesthesia induction was performed as a confirmatory safeguard, which did not find evidence of TEF.

The operation was commenced laparoscopically with a 5-cm mini-laparotomy. Dense adhesions between the prior gastrostomy site and the left lateral hepatic segment were released (Fig. 3a). Along the greater curvature, the omentum was divided approximately 3 cm from the right gastroepiploic arcade, and the short gastric vessels were divided sequentially to mobilize the fundus. The left gastric artery and vein were divided. A remnant distal blind esophageal stump approximately 3 cm proximal to the cardia was identified at the hiatus (Fig. 3b, c). The distal esophageal blind stump was circumferentially dissected and mobilized into the abdominal cavity without incision of the diaphragmatic crura. The esophageal hiatus was closed by approximating the right and left crura using 2 − 0 non-absorbable sutures. A subtotal gastric conduit was constructed extracorporeally using a single firing of a 55-mm linear stapler (blue cartridge) along the lesser curvature, while preserving the right gastric and right gastroepiploic arteries as the vascular inflow (Fig. 3d).

The conduit was elevated in an isoperistaltic fashion, and no additional antireflux procedure was performed. Adequate conduit reach was achieved without the need for a Kocher maneuver. No pyloric drainage procedure (e.g., pyloroplasty or pyloromyotomy) was performed. The intraoperative photograph and schematic illustration of gastric conduit reconstruction are shown in Fig. 4a and b.

The cervical esophagostomy was excised through a left oblique cervical incision. The orifice was located lateral to the sternocleidomastoid muscle. Dissection proceeded along the esophageal wall, mobilizing the proximal esophagus off the trachea. Severe adhesions involving the external and internal jugular veins and the common carotid artery were evident. A retrosternal tunnel was created laparoscopically, and the gastric conduit was delivered to the neck. No posterior mediastinal dissection was performed because reconstruction was planned via the retrosternal route. The cervical esophagogastric anastomosis was performed as a hand-sewn end-to-end anastomosis using interrupted 3 − 0 absorbable sutures (Gambee technique). Finally, a feeding jejunostomy (8-Fr tube) was created. The pleura was preserved during retrosternal tunneling, and no obvious pleural injury was recognized intraoperatively. Prophylactic chest drainage was not performed.

Postoperative course

A left pneumothorax was detected just after surgery, and a chest tube was inserted, which was removed on postoperative day (POD) 2 because of rapid improvement. On POD 1, bronchoscopic suction was required to assist sputum clearance due to left vocal cord paralysis, and no aspiration occurred despite left recurrent laryngeal nerve (RLN) palsy.

On POD 7, a cervical anastomotic leak developed, presenting as a neck abscess, and was managed conservatively with opening and local drainage of the cervical wound. Enteral nutrition was maintained via the feeding jejunostomy until oral intake was established and stable.

The local infection gradually improved, and the patient started oral intake on POD 12 and was discharged on POD 21. Dietary advancement was followed with good tolerability.

After 3 months, oral feeding remained stable, and her body weight had increased by 3kg; the jejunostomy tube was subsequently removed. At the latest follow-up, 10 months after surgery, the patient maintained adequate oral intake with good tolerance, and her body weight had increased by 10 kg compared with the preoperative value. Left recurrent laryngeal nerve palsy persisted but did not cause aspiration or swallowing difficulty, and no anastomotic stricture requiring dilation or clinically significant reflux symptoms were observed.

Discussion and conclusion

Adult reconstruction after prolonged enteral feeding in type A LGEA is exceptional [5–7]. Definitive reconstruction of long-gap esophageal atresia in adulthood is extremely rare. However, rare cases of prolonged survival without definitive repair have been reported, illustrating the unusual natural history of untreated esophageal atresia and the possibility of delayed presentation in adulthood [15]. Only a limited number of reports have described delayed reconstruction after prolonged enteral dependence, highlighting the technical challenges associated with altered mediastinal anatomy and long-standing enteral dependence [5, 10]. Compared with these reports, the present case is notable for successful reconstruction after 18 years of gastrostomy dependence with durable restoration of oral intake. In our case, three interlinked decisions facilitated success. First, airway confirmation excluded a residual TEF [10]. Preoperative imaging strongly suggested type A LGEA, and bronchoscopy at induction confirmed the absence of TEF without procedural burden. Second, route selection was individualized. Cervical anastomosis may be performed using either the posterior mediastinal or retrosternal route [8, 11–13]. Although the posterior mediastinal route is generally considered the most physiological pathway for esophageal reconstruction, the retrosternal route is a widely accepted alternative when mediastinal adhesions are anticipated or when a safer conduit pathway is preferred in complex cases [16]. In the present case, prior thoracotomy had been attempted during childhood, and the extent of posterior mediastinal manipulation was uncertain; therefore, the retrosternal route was selected to avoid additional thoracic procedures and potential increases in surgical invasiveness [16]. In addition, because the proximal esophagus had already been externalized as a cervical esophagostomy, reconstruction via the posterior mediastinal route would have required additional mediastinal dissection, which was considered undesirable in this case. Thus, the retrosternal route was considered a safer and less invasive option in this specific clinical setting. In adult patients, gastric size and capacity may be concerns when considering gastric conduit reconstruction. In the present patient, preoperative fluoroscopy-guided endoscopic assessment with sufficient gastric insufflation demonstrated adequate gastric morphology and distensibility, supporting the feasibility of gastric conduit reconstruction. In the present case, gastric conduit reconstruction was preferred over colon or jejunal interposition because it provides a reliable vascular supply, requires fewer anastomoses, and is technically less complex than other replacement options. Gastric reconstruction has been reported as a reliable option for esophageal replacement in patients with long-gap esophageal atresia and other complex esophageal conditions [8, 9].

Third, the cervical anastomosis strategy was tailored. Although some meta-analyses suggest that stapled anastomoses may reduce anastomotic leak rates relative to hand-sewn techniques [16], the EA-specific geometry in this case—a dilated cervical esophagus following adhesiolysis with attention to cervical reach and conduit perfusion along the retrosternal route—favored a hand-sewn end-to-end approach to allow precise caliber matching and tension distribution. A cervical anastomotic leak occurred but was successfully managed with local drainage, and oral intake was ultimately maintained. Cervical anastomotic leakage and recurrent laryngeal nerve palsy are recognized complications after cervical esophagogastric reconstruction, with reported incidences of approximately 5–20% and 10–30%, respectively [17, 18], and may influence long-term swallowing function or the risk of anastomotic stricture; however, no clinically significant swallowing dysfunction or stricture has been observed in the present case during follow-up.

Long-standing cervical esophagostomy produces significant neck scarring, where difficult adhesiolysis around major cervical vessels and the esophagus is expected [17]. Intraoperative nerve monitoring (NIM) has been shown to reduce RLN palsy or facilitate earlier recognition in thyroid surgery and esophagectomy [18, 19]. Although NIM was available but not used in this case, selective use may be beneficial when severe scarring is anticipated. This case highlights that individualized surgical planning based on prior operative history, mediastinal conditions, and conduit reach is essential when considering delayed reconstruction in adults with long-gap esophageal atresia.

Limitations

This report has several limitations. First, it describes a single case and therefore the findings cannot be generalized to all patients with long-gap esophageal atresia. Second, the duration of follow-up remains limited. Further studies are required to clarify the long-term functional outcomes after delayed reconstruction in adult patients. In addition, objective functional assessments such as swallowing studies or reflux evaluation were not systematically performed.

Clinical implications

Even after prolonged gastrostomy dependence, swallowing can be preserved in adults with confirmed type A anatomy; moreover, a feasible transfer route can be achieved through reconstruction within standard oncologic principles, provided that the technique is individualized to pouch geometry and conduit reach.

Patient perspective

From the patient’s perspective, regaining oral feeding after several years of gastrostomy improved daily comfort and enabled broader social participation.

In conclusion, restoration of durable oral feeding is feasible after long-term gastrostomy in adult patients with congenital type A long-gap esophageal atresia when the possibility of tracheoesophageal fistula is excluded and the reconstruction route and cervical anastomotic strategy are individualized to ensure adequate reach and perfusion. The retrosternal route represents a viable option when posterior mediastinal adhesions or conduit reach are concerns; however, its suitability should be evaluated on an individual patient basis. Postoperative complications such as cervical anastomotic leakage and recurrent laryngeal nerve palsy should be carefully considered, as they may influence functional recovery. Furthermore, this report describes a single case with limited follow-up, and therefore the findings should be interpreted with caution. Further studies are required to clarify the long-term outcomes of delayed reconstruction in adult patients with long-gap esophageal atresia.
