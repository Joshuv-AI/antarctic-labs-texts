Ruthenium-Catalyzed Carbocycle-Selective Hydrogenation of Fused Heteroarenes
Authors: Chenguang Luo, Chaozheng Wu, Xiaoming Wang, Zhaobin Han, Zheng Wang, Kuiling Ding
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe homogeneous catalytic hydrogenation of benzo-fused heteroarenes generally provides partially hydrogenated products wherein the heteroaryl ring is preferentially reduced, such as quinoline hydrogenation, leading to 1,2,3,4-tetrahydroquinoline. Herein, we report a carbocycle-selective hydrogenation of fused N-heteroarenes (quinoline, isoquinoline, quinoxaline, etc.) using the Ru complex of a chiral spiroketal-based diphosphine (SKP) as the catalyst, affording the corresponding 5,6,7,8-tetrahydro products in high chemoselectivities. This catalytic system is also effective for the asymmetric carbocycle hydrogenation of fused heteroarenes bearing a boryl or amino group. Experimental studies provided a strong support for the homogeneous nature of the catalysis, and an inner-sphere mechanism was proposed for the hydrogenation. DFT calculations indicated that the hydrogenation is initiated by η4-coordinative activation of quinoline carbocycle to Ru dihydride complex of SKP, followed by metal-to-ligand hydride transfer. Subsequent carbocycle reduction proceeds via consecutive steps of the H2 oxidative addition and C–H reductive elimination.

Introduction

Catalytic hydrogenation of arenes can be used to synthesize saturated or partially reduced cyclic compounds in a straightforward, facile, and atom-economic way.1 So far for the hydrogenation of monocyclic arenes in general and benzene derivatives in particular, heterogeneous catalysts were used in the vast majority of cases under relatively high temperatures and pressures.2 On the other hand, bicyclic and polycyclic aromatic rings have extended π-conjugated systems, and by virtue of the attenuated aromaticity, these arenes can be partially reduced in a homogeneous catalytic hydrogenation process under mild conditions.3 In this context, the control of carbocycle versus heterocycle chemoselectivity in partial hydrogenation of a fused heteroarene can be challenging as both rings are aromatic and often bear similar steric features. Under these circumstances, the hydrogenation of benzo-azaarenes such as quinoline, isoquinoline, and quinoxaline, may occur on both azacycle and carbocycle to yield a mixture of the corresponding 1,2,3,4- and 5,6,7,8-tetrahydro products.4 In general, the benzene ring in a benzo-fused heteroarene is believed to have stronger aromaticity by comparing its nucleus independent chemical shift (NICS)5 value with that of the heteroaromatic ring.1b Furthermore, the heteroatom such as nitrogen can provide a coordination site for substrate activation that is not present in the carbon ring.6 These factors can often contribute to the preferential reduction of the heterocyclic fragment to the corresponding 1,2,3,4-tetrahydro products,7 which have been well documented in the literature.1b,8 In contrast, catalytic systems that are effective for the carbocycle-selective partial hydrogenation of fused heteroarenes are rare so far. Apart from the difficulties mentioned above, stereoselectivity control also constitutes a nontrivial challenge for asymmetric arene hydrogenation (Figure 1a).

Notably, heteroaromtics containing a fused saturated carbocycle moiety, such as 5,6,7,8-tetrahydroquinolines and 5,6,7,8-tetrahydroisoquinolines, are structural motifs occurring in many bioactive natural products, therapeutic agents, and drug candidates (Figure 1b).9 Conventional synthetic methodologies developed for the construction of such architectures are often based on the ring-closure reactions of properly functionalized pyridines or cyclohexenes.10 However, these approaches can be plagued by lengthy and tedious synthetic routes. There is no doubt that carbocycle-selective hydrogenation of benzo-fused heteroarenes can constitute one of the most straightforward and environmentally friendly ways to achieve this type of compounds, yet only a handful of catalytic systems have been known to be effective for such a process so far. It has been reported in early studies that some quinolines and isoquinolines were reduced to their 5,6,7,8-tetrahydro products via PtO2-catalyzed hydrogenation under acidic conditions (Figure 1c(i)).4b,4c,11 Borowski et al. reported in 2003 the hydrogenation of some polynuclear N-heteroaromatic compounds with a bis(dihydrogen) ruthenium complex [RuH2(PCy3)2(H2)2] as the catalyst, and quinoline and isoquinoline were converted to their carbocycle-reduced 5,6,7,8-tetrahydro derivatives under mild conditions (Figure 1c(ii)).12 Recently, Glorius and co-workers disclosed the use of a chiral Ru complex of N-heterocyclic carbene as the catalyst for asymmetric hydrogenation of quinoxalines to afford the corresponding carbocycle-reduced products with good enantioselectivity (Figure 1c(iii)).13 Kuwano’s group also reported elegant studies on chemoselective asymmetric hydrogenation of quinolines and isoquinolines by using the Ru/PhTRAP catalyst system (Figure 1c(iv)).14 Despite these impressive progresses, there is still much room with respect to the substrate scope and catalyst types that need to be explored, and a mechanistic understanding of the molecular events occurring in the carbocycle hydrogenation remains largely elusive so far. Kuwano and co-workers have proposed that the extremely large bite angle of the diphosphine ligand (PhTRAP) might be crucial for the unusual carbocycle chemoselectivity,14b inspiring us to explore the reaction performance of our SKP ligands,15 featured by a large P···P distance (6.294 Å) and a flexible backbone in its free ligand state, in Ru-catalyzed carbocycle-selective partial hydrogenation of various fused heteroarenes. Herein, we report a comprehensive survey of the carbocycle-selective hydrogenation of a broad variety of polycyclic (hetero)arenes using the Ru/SKP catalyst system. The Ru/SKP catalyst was also efficient for asymmetric chemoselective hydrogenation of boryl- and amino-functionalized quinolines and isoquinolines, affording the corresponding carbocycle-hydrogenated products in high yields with good enantioselectivities (Figure 1d). Last but not least, the homogeneous nature and detailed reaction pathways for carbocycle-selective hydrogenation were elucidated by a combined array of experimental and theoretical studies.

Results and Discussion

Ru-Catalyzed Chemoselective Hydrogenation of Unsubstituted Fused Heteroarenes

We started the investigation with a survey of some representative ligands in the Ru-catalyzed hydrogenation of quinoline. The reactions were typically run under 50 bar of H2 in iPrOH at 80 °C for 24 h, in the presence of Ru(methallyl)2(cod) (2.5 mol %) with a catalytic amount of ligand as the catalyst and K2CO3 (20 mol %) as the additive, and the results are summarized in Table 1. Under these conditions, the reactions using Ph-SKP or Xyl-SKP ligand showed excellent reactivity and chemoselectivity, leading to almost exclusive formation of the carbocycle-hydrogenated 5,6,7,8-tetrahydroquinoline 1A (>99% GC yield, 1A/1B > 99:1, entries 1 and 2), respectively. In contrast, the reactions using several other mono- or diphosphine ligands with diversified steric features, e.g., PPh3, PCy3, dppp, dppbz, Xantphos, and BINAP, only gave inferior results in terms of both the reactivity and chemoselectivities (1A/1B = 1:3.5 to 11.4:1, entries 3–7, 9), and poor results were obtained in the reactions using dppf or MONOphos (5% conversion, entries 8 and 10). Though N-heterocyclic carbene ligand ICy was found highly reactive for the hydrogenation, the chemoselectivity was unsatisfactory (1A/1B ca. 1:1, entry 11). Intriguingly, the N,N′-chelating ligand 2,2′-bipyridine delivered azacycle-reduced 1B as the major product (entry 12). The subtle variation of the chemoselectivity with the ligand structure suggested that competitive carbo/heterocycle hydrogenation might be coexisting in these systems, and the prevailing reaction pathway is dependent on both electronic and steric properties of the ligand as defined by the donor groups and ligand skeletons. With Ph-SKP as the optimal ligand, the reaction using [Ru(p-cymene)Cl2]2 as an alternative Ru precursor gave results essentially identical to those using Ru(methallyl)2(cod) (entry 13 vs 1), suggesting that some common Ru species might predominate in the catalysis in both reactions. Using Cs2CO3 instead of K2CO3 as the base additive in the reaction led to almost identical results (entry 14), whereas the reaction in the absence of a base additive resulted in only partial conversion (45%) of quinoline with reversed chemoselectivity of the products (1A/1B = 1:4, entry 15). These results indicated that the use of a catalytic amount of external base is indispensable for the reaction, as otherwise the azacycle may undergo heterocycle reduction via N-protonation by metal-ligated dihydrogen species.16 The reaction still proceeded smoothly at 60 °C without loss of chemoselectivity, whereas further lowering the reaction temperature to 40 °C or room temperature led to incomplete conversions of 1 (entries 16–18). It is worth noting that none of the fully hydrogenated product 1C was detected in all of these reactions using a ligand. In sharp contrast, under otherwise identical conditions, the reaction in the absence of a ligand delivered only product 1C in a quantitative yield (entry 19). This result suggested that without stabilization by an external ligand, Ru(methallyl)2(cod) might undergo decomposition to some types of Ru particles or heterogeneous species, which can be responsible for the full hydrogenation of quinoline in this system.17

Subsequently, we proceeded to explore the Ru/Ph-SKP-catalyzed chemoselective hydrogenation of a range of unsubstituted fused (hetero)arenes, for which no comprehensive survey using a single catalyst has been reported so far to our knowledge. A variety of fused (hetero)arenes, including 6,6- (1–6, 14, and 15) and 6,5-bicyclic (7, 8, and 16), as well as tricyclic arenes (9–13 and 17–19) were tested under the optimized reaction conditions (Table 2). Only partially hydrogenated products were obtained in each case with at least one aryl ring remaining intact over the hydrogenation. For the reactions of bicyclic arenes 1–8, the corresponding tetrahydro products 1A–8A were obtained in nearly quantitative yields (>99%, entries 1–8). In particular, hydrogenation of benzo-azaarenes like quinoline (1), isoquinoline (2), and quinoxaline (3) yielded their corresponding 5,6,7,8-tetrahydro derivatives 1A-3A with almost exclusive carbocycle selectivity (A/B > 99:1, entries 1–3). Symmetric 6,6-bicyclic (hetero)arenes such as naphthalene (4), 1,8-naphthyridine (5), and 1,5-naphthyridine (6) were also hydrogenated efficiently to give their corresponding tetrahydro derivatives in nearly quantitative yields. For the hydrogenation of N-bridged 6,5-bicyclic azaarenes imidazo[1,2-a]pyridine (7) and indolizine (8), only the dienyl motifs in the six-membered rings of both substrates were selectively hydrogenated, to give the corresponding tetrahydro products 7A and 8A in quantitative yields, respectively. The reactions of tricyclic (hetero)arenes (e.g., anthracene 9, acridine 10, phenazine 11, phenanthrene 12, and phenanthridine 13) also proceeded smoothly to give good-to-excellent yields of the corresponding octahydrogenated products wherein both peripheral carbocycles were selectively reduced, while the central aromatic rings remained intact. This reaction pattern is intriguing, which seems to be contrary to the common belief that 9,10-sites of these tricyclic (hetero)arenes are often more reactive toward dearomatization reactions.18 We believed that this can be taken as an indication of the inner-sphere mechanism of the hydrogenation, wherein a proper π-facial coordination (and orientation) of an aryl ring to the catalytic Ru site is a prerequisite for its activation and reduction, and the peripheral carbocycles are more accessible to the Ru center than the sterically hindered central rings.19 It is notable that this catalytic system exhibited good reactivity in most cases, albeit led to the formation of mixtures in the hydrogenation of some substrates. Only intractable mixtures were obtained in the reactions of 6,6-bicyclic azaarenes 14 and 15, probably as a result of partial decomposition of the substrates as evidenced by GC-MS analysis. No conversions were observed in the reactions of heteroarenes 16–18, presumably owing to catalyst inhibition by N,N-chelation of substrate 18 with the Ru center, or C,N-chelation of Ru by 16 and 17 via neighboring group-assisted C–H bond cleavage. No hydrogenation product was detected in the reaction of tricyclic heteroarene 19, indicating that the Ru/SKP system is ineffective for reducing unfused benzene rings.

Encouraged by the excellent carbocycle selectivity in SKP/Ru-catalyzed hydrogenation of quinoline, isoquinoline, and quinoxaline, we proceeded to extend the protocol to related benzo-azaarenes bearing various substituents (Table 3). For heterocycle-substituted benzo-azaarenes 20a–x, the reactions proceeded smoothly under a low catalyst loading of 0.1 mol %, resulting in full substrate conversions and 88–99% isolated yields of the corresponding 5,6,7,8-tetrahydro derivatives (21a–x), and none of the heterocycle-reduced 1,2,3,4-tetrahydro counterparts were detected by 1H NMR in all cases. For the reactions of quinoline substrates, the substituents at different positions of the N-ring are well compatible with the protocol, and the products with an alkyl, phenyl, benzyl, or trifluoromethyl group at 2-position (21a–g) were obtained in >90% yields. Hydrogenation of 3-methoxy, boryl, or amino-substituted quinolines occurred smoothly to give 21i–k in high yields. Isoquinolines and quinoxalines with an alkyl or a phenyl substituent on the N-rings were also hydrogenated to the corresponding carbocycle-reduced products 21p–x in excellent yields. The hydrogenation of carbocycle-substituted benzo-azaarenes 20aa–ag has also been investigated using the (S,S,S)-Ph-SKP/Ru catalyst. The reactions of 5- or 8-Ph substituted quinoline 20aa and 20ab gave 5,6,7,8-tetrahydroquinolines 21aa and 21ab, respectively, in excellent yield with >20:1 chemoselectivity and moderate enantioselectivity. On the other hand, the hydrogenation of 20ac–af, bearing a substituent on 6 or 7-position, was much less satisfactory in terms of the chemoselectivities. In the case of 6-tert-butyl substituted quinoline 20ag, only the N-cycle-hydrogenated product was generated in 56% yield. These results indicated that the chemoselectivity of the hydrogenation is somehow more sensitive to the steric hindrance of the 6- and 7-substituents.

Chemoselective Asymmetric Hydrogenation of Carbocycle-Borylated Benzo-Azaarenes

Considering the synthetic versatility of organoboron compounds, we sought to develop a Ru/SKP-catalyzed asymmetric hydrogenation for the synthesis of chiral boryl-functionalized 5,6,7,8-tetrahydroquinolines that are valuable synthons yet are otherwise difficult to prepare. First, the reaction conditions were reoptimized with 6-quinolineboronic acid pinacol ester (22a) as the model substrate (for details, see Tables S4–S12), to give the carbocycle-hydrogenated product 23a in 62% yield (determined by 1H NMR) and 85:15 er using (S,S,S)-Xyl-SKP as the ligand. Meanwhile, the pyridyl-reduced 24a was also detected in 34% 1H NMR yield, obviously as a result of the more favorable steric hindrance of the pyridyl ring in this case. Subsequently, the asymmetric hydrogenation of carbocycle-borylated quinolines that bear a substituent on their pyridyl rings was examined using the (S,S,S)-Xyl-SKP/Ru catalyst, and the results are summarized in Table 4. In most cases, the corresponding carbocycle-hydrogenated products 23b–r bearing various substituents (e.g., alkyl, phenyl, CF3, OMe, etc.) on their pyridyl rings were obtained in moderate-to-high yields (55–95%) with moderate-to-good chemo- and enantioselectivities (C/N > 9:1, up to 91:9 er). These results are in line with our expectations, as the sterically more hindered pyridyl ring would be unfavorable for the hydrogenation, thereby enhancing the chemoselectivity toward carbocycle hydrogenation. The absolute configurations of 23e and 23o were established both as (S) by X-ray crystallographic analysis. However, this catalyst system was found to be ineffective for the hydrogenation of quinoline-6-boronic acid pinacol esters bearing an ethoxycarbonyl (22s) or methoxy (22t) group at the 2-position, resulting in a messy mixture or low conversion, respectively, probably owing to the catalyst poison by neighboring group-assisted substrate chelation. Furthermore, quinoline derivatives with a boronate moiety at 7-position (22u and 22v) were also found compatible with the protocol, and the reactions afforded the 5,6,7,8-tetrahydrogenated product 23u and 23v in good yields, respectively, albeit both are nearly racemic. Under otherwise identical conditions, the reactions of 5- or 8-borylated quinolines (22w and 22x) afforded the corresponding hydrodeborylated 5,6,7,8-tetrahydroquinoline as the major products, respectively, presumably as a result of the relatively labile nature of the otherwise produced benzylic boronate.20 To our delight, the catalyst system was also found to be effective for chemoselective hydrogenation of several carbocycle-borylated diazabicyclic arenes (22y, 22z, and 22za), furnishing the corresponding diene-hydrogenated products (23y, 23z, and 23za) exclusively in high yields, albeit with low enantioselectivities.

Chemoselective Asymmetric Hydrogenation of Carbocycle-Amino-Functionalized Benzo-Azaarenes

Chiral heterocycle-based exocyclic amines widely occur as structural motifs in numerous biologically active molecules, and hydrogenation of the aromatic framework can be considered as one of the most straightforward and atom-economic routes for construction of these valuable architectures. Despite the remarkable synthetic potential, however, the hydrogenation of aromatic rings in an amino-substituted heteroarene has been rarely explored so far to our knowledge, and only the heteroaromatic moieties were selectively reduced in most cases.18a,21 Encouraged by the above results, we extended the Ru/SKP catalytic system to the asymmetric hydrogenation of carbocycle-amino-functionalized benzo-azaarenes. Preliminary reaction condition optimization using N-benzyl-N-methylisoquinoline-5-amine (25a) as the model substrate (for details, see Tables S16–S21) indicated (S,S,S)-Cy-SKP as the optimal ligand for the Ru catalyst, affording the corresponding 5-amino-5,6,7,8-tetrahydroisoquinoline 26a in good yield (87%) with high levels of chemo- and enantioselectivities (26a/27a > 15:1, 94:6 er). Next, a variety of carbocycle-amino-functionalized benzo-azaarenes were evaluated using the procedure (Table 5). For 5-amino-substituted isoquinoline substrates 25a–r, the reactions proceeded smoothly to give the corresponding 5-amino-5,6,7,8-tetrahydroisoquinolines 26a–r in moderate-to-high yields (up to 93%) with good enantioselectivities (>90:10 er). Various substituents (OMe, F, CF3, etc.) on the phenyl ring of the N-benzyl group were well tolerated (26b–i). The absolute configuration of 26f was established as (R) by X-ray crystallographic analysis. Replacement of the N-methyl in 25a to N-ethyl, N-n-butyl, or N-benzyl in 25k–m, respectively, leading to the formation of the corresponding carbocycle-reduced products 26k–m in declined yields (50–60%), probably due to the increased steric hindrance of the amine group. On the other hand, the replacement of the N-Benzyl in 25a by some other alkyl groups in isoquinolines 25n–r resulted in the formation of the corresponding carbocycle-hydrogenated products 26n–r with largely the same level of enantioselectivities, indicating that the enantiocontrol of the reaction was less sensitive to the steric hindrance of dialkylamino groups. High levels of carbocycle selectivity (>10:1) were also observed in the hydrogenation of BocNH- and BzNH-substituted isoquinolines 25s and 25t, respectively, albeit the er values of the corresponding products 26s and 26t were unsatisfactory. No reaction was observed for hydrogenation of BnNH-substituted 25u, perhaps due to the coordination and inhibitory effect of the secondary amine. In addition, for the hydrogenation of isoquinoline derivatives 25v–x, which can be viewed as the heterocycle-alkylated analogs of 25a, the carbocycle-hydrogenated products 26v–x were obtained in excellent yields (>95%) and high enantioselectivities. Unfortunately, isoquinolines 25y and 25z bearing an amino group at 6- or 7-position were found incompatible with the reaction, affording only trace amounts of corresponding products under standard conditions. As a contrast, the reaction of 8-aminoisoquinoline 25za proceeded smoothly to afford the 5,6,7,8-tetrahydrogenated product 26za in 94% yield and 90:10 er. Furthermore, the reaction of 5-aminoquinoline 25zb gave its 5,6,7,8-tetrahydrogenated derivative 26zb in 90% yield with good enantioselectivity (85:15 er). The hydrogenation of 5-PhNH-substituted quinoline 25zc proceeded with only partial conversion of the substrate (60%), giving 5,6,7,8-tetrahydrogenated product 26zc in 48% yield with 82:18 er. However, 8-PhNH-substituted quinoline 26zd failed to be hydrogenated, probably owing to the catalyst poisoning by substrate chelation. Finally, control experiments on the hydrogenation of enamines such as 28 (for details, see the Supporting Information) gave no conversion under otherwise identical conditions, indicating that the enamine intermediate is unlikely to be involved in the reaction. A detailed DFT analysis suggested that the most favorable hydrogenation pathway for 25p should start with sequential transfer of two hydrides to C5 and C6 followed by olefin hydrogenation of the resulting Ru complex of 5-amino-5,6-dihydroisoquinoline intermediate (for details, see the Supporting Information).

Synthetic Applications

To showcase the synthetic potential of this method, scale-up reactions were conducted on the hydrogenation of several substrates under lowered catalyst loadings. First, an excellent reactivity of the catalyst was observed for the hydrogenation of quinoline, which was reduced smoothly to 1A in 98.4% GC yield within 48 h under a catalyst loading as low as 0.01 mol % (TON = 9840). Further reducing the catalyst loading to 0.005 mol % led to a 67.4% yield of 1A in 48 h, amounting to a TON value of 13,480 (Figure 2a). Gram-scale reactions on the asymmetric hydrogenation of borylated quinolines 22a and 22e or 5-aminoisoquinoline 25a also proceeded smoothly under a low catalyst loading of 1.0 mol %, to give the corresponding carbocycle-reduced products 23a, 23e, and 26a in 65, 91, and 88% yield with moderate-to-good er values, respectively (Figure 2b). Furthermore, the partially reduced borylated products proved to be valuable building blocks for rapid access to novel 5,6,7,8-tetrahydroquinoline structures. Structurally diverse 5,6,7,8-tetrahydroquinoline derivatives 29–34 were readily accessed from 23e (Figure 2c). In this context, oxidation of 23e using NaBO3·4H2O led to alcohol 29 in high yield (93%), while deprotection of the boronate in 23e with NaIO4 delivered boronic acid 30. Potassium trifluoroborate salt 31 was obtained in good yield via the transformation of 23e in the presence of KHF2. Treatment of 23e with the amination reagent H2N-DABCO22 followed by acetylation allowed rapid access to 6-acetamido-5,6,7,8-tetrahydroquinoline 32. Apart from the functional group interconversions involving the Bpin functionality, 23e was also competent as a quinoline building block in the transition metal free C–C bond cross-coupling reactions with (vinyl)MgBr and furan, to furnish the 6-vinyl or furyl substituted 5,6,7,8-tetrahydroquinolines 33 and 34, respectively, in good yields.

Mechanistic Studies

To probe the nature of the current catalysis, mechanistic studies were conducted on the SKP/Ru catalyst system using an array of experimental techniques (Figure 3). While the reaction of Ru(methallyl)2(cod) with an equimolar amount of SKP only afforded an intractable mixture, reaction of [Ru(p-cymene)Cl2]2 with two equivalents of (S,S,S)-Ph-SKP in THF led to isolation of crystalline (κ1-SKP)(η6-p-cymene)RuCl2 (Ru1) as determined by X-ray crystallographic analysis (Figure 3a). Further treatment of Ru1 with NaBArF (2 equiv) led to the formation of a SKP-bidentate coordinated Ru complex, [(κ2-SKP)(p-cymene)RuCl]BArF (Ru2), as identified by the 31P NMR analysis of the mixture (see Figure S8). Hydrogenation of quinoline using Ru1 or Ru2 delivered catalytic results comparable to those using the catalyst prepared in situ from Ru(methallyl)2(cod) and Ph-SKP (>99% GC yield of 1A), suggesting that common active intermediates are likely to be involved in these processes. The nonlinear effect (NLE)23 studies have often been used as a powerful diagnostic tool to probe the aggregation behavior of the catalyst in an asymmetric catalytic reaction, and the absence of NLE in the Ru(methallyl)2(cod)/(S,S,S)-Xyl-SKP-catalyzed asymmetric hydrogenation of 22e (Figure 3b) indicated that there is a single SKP ligand involved in the enantio-determining transition state of the reaction.

(b) Nonlinear effect (NLE) study with 22e as a substrate. (c) Deuterium-labeling experiment. (d) Control experiments on the homo/heterogeneous nature of Ru/SKP-catalyzed hydrogenation of quinoline. Notably, asymmetric hydrogenation of 22a and 25a using SKP/Ru complexes generated in situ from several different ruthenium precursors, including Ru(cod)Cl2, [(p-cymeme)RuCl2]2, and [(benzene)RuCl2]2, gave 23a and 26a with the same enantioselectivity, respectively (see Tables S4 and S20), suggesting that these catalytic reactions are most likely to proceed via identical active species. To gain insight into the hydrogen addition pattern of the reaction, deuterium labeling studies were conducted on the reaction of 8-phenylquinoline 20ab. Under 30 bar of D2, the reaction in d8-toluene at 80 °C delivered 21ab-D in 94% yield with all cis stereochemistry of the four incorporated deuterium atoms (>95% D, Figure 3c). This result is consistent with a process of the continuous coplanar addition of Ru hydride to the carbocycle of the substrate. In addition, a small amount of deuterium incorporation was detected on 2-, 3-, and 4-carbons of the pyridine ring, presumably as a result of H/D exchange induced by oxidative addition of the pyridyl C–H bond on the Ru center. Further deuterium labeling reactions in iPrOH and 2-propanol-d8 indicated that the reaction is dominated by hydrogenation with dihydrogen as the primary hydrogen source, albeit the iPrOH solvent may also contribute, to a much lesser degree, as a hydrogen-transfer agent (for details, see Scheme S1).

To further identify the nature of the catalysis, several control experiments were performed on the Ph-SKP/Ru(methallyl)2(cod)-catalyzed hydrogenation of quinoline 1 (Figure 3d). While the reaction under standard conditions provided almost full substrate conversion with the exclusive formation of carbocycle-reduced product 1A (entry 1), essentially, the same results were achieved in the reaction with the addition of elemental mercury to the system at 0 h (entry 2). This irrelevance of the catalytic activity by the Hg(0) poisoning test strongly supports the homogeneous nature of the current catalysis. Under otherwise identical conditions, the reaction without SKP also afforded complete substrate conversion but delivered exclusively the fully hydrogenated decahydroquinoline 1C (>99% yield, entry 3). Presumably, without the stabilization of a phosphine ligand such as SKP, Ru atoms may aggregate into heterogeneous ruthenium clusters or particles that are more likely to be responsible for the exhaustive hydrogenation.24 The reaction run in the absence of SKP but using mercury as the additive proceeded in low conversion, with only a trace amount of heterocycle-hydrogenated 1B being detected (entry 4). This observation is consistent with the results shown in entry 3, indicating that the otherwise generated heterogeneous Ru species in the absence of SKP was largely poisoned by Hg(0). Finally, the molecular nature of the catalytic species was further attested by several related experiments, including the mercury test on the continued reactivity study for hydrogenation of quinoline at 2 h, filtration test, as well as poly(4-vinylpyridine) poisoning test25 performed on the Ph-SKP/Ru(methallyl)2(cod)-catalyzed hydrogenation of quinoline (see the Supporting Information for details), all of which provided strong supports for the homogeneous nature of the catalysis.

Further efforts were devoted to the identification of SKP-ligated Ru species that are likely to be involved in the catalysis. To this end, 1H NMR analyses were performed on the hydrogenated mixture of stoichiometric catalysts generated from Ph-SKP and Ru(methallyl)2(cod) or [(p-cymene)RuCl2]2 in the absence of a heteroaromatic substrate under otherwise standard conditions (Figure 3e). A broad singlet (δ −8.18 ppm) was observed in the 1H NMR spectrum of the hydrogenation mixture of Ph-SKP and Ru(methallyl)2(cod) (molar ratio 1:1), which can be assigned as the signal of the Ru–H species generated under hydrogenation conditions (Figure 3e(i)). Similarly, hydrogenation of the Ph-SKP/[(p-cymene)RuCl2]2 mixture in the presence of K2CO3 gave rise to a 1H NMR spectrum, wherein a broad singlet appeared in the same location (δ −8.18 ppm) among a complex array of negative hydrogen signals (Figure 3e(ii)). This singlet disappeared after adding 2-trifluoromethylquinoline (1.0 equiv) into this mixture, while the other signals remained largely unchanged in the negative hydrogen region, and 19F NMR indicated the formation of some amount of 2-(trifluoromethyl)-5,6,7,8-tetrahydroquinoline (Figure 3e(iii)). These results strongly indicated that the broad singlet at −8.18 ppm is likely to correspond to the hydride of an active catalytic species that is responsible for carbocycle-selective hydrogenation. By analogy to the structure of (PCy3)2RuH2(H2)2 proposed previously by Chaudret and Poilblanc,26 which was believed to be an active catalytic species for hydrogenation of carbocycle in quinoline that is featured by a broad single peak at −7.84 ppm in 1H NMR, we tentatively proposed that the active Ru/SKP hydride species may possess a similar composition, i.e., (κ2-SKP)RuH2(H2)2, albeit no structural details can be identified experimentally at the present stage, as many attempts to isolate this species were unsuccessful so far (Figure 3f).

On the basis of these results and a catalytic cycle for the hydrogenation of acridine proposed previously by Borowski et al.,12 we proposed a simplified mechanism for SKP/Ru-catalyzed carbocycle-selective hydrogenation of quinoline (Figure 3g). First, treatment of SKP with either one of the two Ru precursors under hydrogenation conditions leads to the formation of SKP-bound ruthenium dihydride species I, which enters into the catalytic cycle. Ligand exchange of the two dihydrogen molecules with quinoline generates carbocycle-η4-bound27,13e intermediate II, which can be in equilibration with the heterocycle-η4-bound intermediate II′. Subsequent hydrogenation of the activated diene moiety of quinoline in II requires two dihydrogens, which can be activated and added to the carbocycle in a stepwise fashion. The addition of the first H2 can proceed through three possible pathways, i.e., 1,2-addition, 1,4-addition, and/or 3,4-addition, resulting in the possible formation of dihydroquinoline complexes IIIa–c as intermediates. This is followed by the addition of the second dihydrogen molecule, thus finally giving product 5,6,7,8-tetrahydroquinoline 1A that is released on coordination of two dihydrogen molecules, with regeneration of I to close the cycle. On the other hand, the heterocycle-η4-bound intermediate II′ may undergo a similar hydrogenation process, ultimately affording 1,2,3,4-tetrahydroquinoline 1B as the final product.

DFT Calculations

While quinoline hydrogenation using transition metal catalysts based on Ru,7b Pd,28 Mn,8f etc. has been extensively studied, in most cases, only 1,2,3,4-tetrahydrogenated products were obtained, and a large majority of catalysts were believed to operate on an outer-sphere and ionic hydrogenation mechanism. Previous quantum-chemical investigations on ruthenium hydride complexes have studied the heteroarene hydrogenation,13f,29 but no computational study on the full catalytic cycle for carbocycle hydrogenation of benzo-fused heteroarene has been reported and the precise details remain largely elusive thus far. In this study, we were intrigued by the activity and carbocycle selectivity in the Ru/SKP-catalyzed hydrogenation of benzannulated azaarenes. To probe further into the mechanistic details of the title reaction, we identified the key species and energetic profiles for hydrogenation of the model substrate quinoline. The simplified calculated reaction free energy profiles of quinoline are shown in Figure 4a (for a comparative DFT study on the key transition states for the plausible reaction pathways and detailed discussions of the full catalytic cycle for C5- and C2-pathways, see the Supporting Information), wherein the most favorable energy path is highlighted in red, and optimized geometries of selected key intermediates and transition states are shown in Figure 4b.

Density functional theory (DFT) calculation studies. (a) Free energy profile for Ru/SKP-catalyzed carbocycle hydrogenation of quinoline and a comparison of the key transition states for carbocycle and heterocycle hydrogenation. Free energies are with respect to separated quinoline, Int0, and 2H2. (b) Optimized geometries of the key intermediates and transition states. Key bond distances are given in Å. All energies are in kcal/mol and are calculated at the M06-2x/def2TZVP/SMD(iPrOH)//m06l/6-311+G(d,p)-LANL2DZ level of theory.

The catalysis is initiated by H2 dissociation from the (SKP)RuH2(H2)2 catalyst (Int0′, see the Supporting Information) to generate a highly unsaturated 14-electron species (SKP)RuH2 (Int0, 0.0 kcal/mol) with two vicinal vacant sites. η4-Coordination of carbocycle in quinoline takes up the vacant sites trans to the P donor atoms of SKP ligand (C5-Int1a), resulting in a nonplanar quinoline and thus partial destruction of aromaticity. This is an endothermic process (11.3 kcal/mol) for substrate activation. Subsequent insertion of the double bond at C5 into the Ru–H bond proceeds via C5-TS1a (12.8 kcal/mol) to give η3-allyl-bound Ru species (C5-Int2). The transition state structure of C5-TS1a is similar to that found in C5-Int1, and the hydride insertion occurs easily with a small energy barrier (ΔΔG = 1.5 kcal/mol). Following insertion, the coordination of H2 to the liberated site of C5-Int2 leads to hexa-coordinated distorted-octahedral intermediate C5-Int3 (8.2 kcal/mol), wherein H2 bounds trans to the hydride. The coordinated H2 cleaves to form the Ru(IV) trihydride intermediate C5-Int4 (20.7 kcal/mol) in a step occurring with a moderately high energy barrier (ΔΔG = 15.8 kcal/mol). The H–H distance (1.94 Å) can be unequivocally designated as a dihydride30 in C5-TS2a, which evolves further to Ru(IV) trihydride species C5-Int4. In this case, the formation of the Ru(IV) trihydride intermediate by oxidative addition of H2 to a Ru(II) precursor presumably reflects the stabilization of the Ru(IV) species by the strong π-donating allyl motif, which is conjugated with pyridine of the 5H-monohydroquinoline ligand (H1Q). In the following step of the catalytic cycle, a hydride ligand is transferred to a terminal carbon of the π-allyl group in H1Q, and subsequent reductive elimination generates alkene-bound C5-Int5 (4.4 kcal/mol). As the activation barrier for reductive elimination is very small (ΔG‡ = 1.5 kcal/mol), the formation of H2Q is facile and likely occurs immediately after H2 cleavage, with only the transient presence of the intervening Ru(IV) intermediate C5-Int4. Subsequent steps of consecutive H2 oxidative addition and C–H reductive elimination proceed similarly, finally leading to the production of 5,6,7,8-tetrahydroquinoline (see Figure S34 for details). Overall, the highest barrier of C5-TS2a (ΔG‡ = 25.0 kcal/mol) is consistent with a low experimental temperature (as low as 40 °C).

As shown in the right half of Figure 4a, compared to the C58 pathway of 1,4-addition, the C56 pathway of 1,2-addition is more advantageous. The energy difference (C5-TS3a vs C5-TS3b, 5.6 kcal/mol) may reflect the structural characteristics of these transition states, wherein a more delocalized π-conjugated system is preserved in C5-TS3a and disrupted in C5-TS3b. Significantly, the free energies for several key species along the C8 pathway are very close to those of their corresponding counterparts in the C5 pathway (e.g., C8-TS2a vs C5-TS2a), suggesting that the C5 and C8 hydrogenation pathways are both feasible and may be parallel in the carbocycle reduction of quinoline.

A comparative calculation of the key species involved in the azacycle hydrogenation was also performed, by assuming the reaction follows pathways similar to the above-discussed C5 pathway but starting with the hydride migration to N1 or C4 (left part of Figure 4a). The energy difference between C4 and C5 pathways (C4-TS3a vs C5-TS2a) is 3.7 kcal/mol, indicating that heterocycle hydrogenation is 194 times slower than that of the carbocycle. This is basically consistent with >99:1 chemoselectivity. The energetic difference is even more pronounced for the N1 pathway, since in this case, the energy barrier for the first hydride transfer to N1 is prohibitively high (N1-TS1a, 30.2 kcal/mol). In the turnover-limiting steps of the C5- and C4-pathways, the structures for key transition states are featured by a Ru(IV) mer-trihydride species ligated by an allyl or aza-allyl moiety of the monohydrogenated quinoline ligand, respectively. Herein, the electron-deficient aza-allyl from C4-pathway constitutes a weaker π-donor31 than the electron-rich allyl from the C5 pathway, and such an electronic bias would discriminate the C5- and C4-pathways to favor the carbocycle-selective C5-hydrogenation. The internal reaction coordinate (IRC) analyses of the key second H transfer transition states clearly showed that these steps are always accompanied by the rotation (and slippage) of the ligated quinoline ring around the Ru-substrate bond, which can thus lead to a change in the substrate ligating hapticity and may cause steric repulsion with the SKP backbone and substituents in the process. We proposed that with an extraordinarily large P···P distance (6.294 Å) in its free ligand state, the structurally adaptable feature of the SKP ligand can accommodate well the structural changes of the relevant intermediates and transition states without causing too much strain or the need for large energy compensation and hence render it viable for favoring the η4-coordination in the Ru catalytic pocket and carbocycle-selective hydrogenation of the heteroarenes.

Conclusions

In conclusion, we have successfully applied the aromatic spiroketal-based diphosphine ligand SKP to the Ru-catalyzed chemoselective hydrogenation of fused heteroarenes. This catalytic system demonstrated a high carbocycle selectivity in the hydrogenation of benzannulated azaarenes such as quinolines and isoquinolines, resulting in the formation of the corresponding 5,6,7,8-tetrahydrogenated products with good catalytic activity (TON up to 13480) and high chemoselectivity (mostly >99:1). Good enantioselectivities (up to 94:6 er) have also been achieved in the asymmetric hydrogenation of carbocycle-borylated quinolines and aminoisoquinolines. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first asymmetric hydrogenation of borylated arenes to access enantioenriched cyclic alkylboron compounds. The resulting Bpin-functionalized heterocyclic products were demonstrated to be valuable synthons for various 5,6,7,8-tetrahydroquinoline derivatives that are otherwise difficult to prepare. Mechanistic results from an array of experimental studies have provided strong support for the molecular nature of the catalysis. DFT calculations have provided a detailed survey on the competing pathways for the carbocycle hydrogenation of quinoline and 5-aminoisoquinoline, suggesting that changes in Ru coordination geometry and hapticity of the partially reduced heteroarene ligand should have occurred in the reaction course and that the high adaptability of the SKP ligand to these changes can be highly favorable for the catalysis. DFT analysis further showed that quinoline hydrogenation proceeds by stepwise transfer of the hydrides from Ru to the coordinated benzo-fused heteroarene substrate, and the observed carbocycle selectivity is primarily determined in the step of the second H transfer, wherein the key Ru(IV) trihydride species are better stabilized by coordination with the electron-rich allyl group rather than the electron-deficient aza-allyl moiety of the monohydrogenated quinoline ligand. Further efforts to enhance the enantioselectivity of the catalysis are underway in our laboratories. We anticipate that this work will stimulate further research on the chiral ligands and exploration of alternative metal catalysts for carbocycle-chemoselective asymmetric hydrogenation of fused heterocycles that ultimately can render this transformation a highly useful synthetic tool for the construction of partially reduced heterocycles.
