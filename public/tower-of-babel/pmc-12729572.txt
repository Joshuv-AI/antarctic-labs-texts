Non-pharmacological interventions for managing acute pain and delirium in hospitalized older patients: a systematic review
Authors: Peiman Prandeh Afshar, Eied Saber Alamain Ali, Fatemeh Dehghan, Mahlagha Dehghan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundDelirium and acute pain are common and serious problems in hospitalized older patients, often leading to adverse outcomes such as prolonged hospital stays, cognitive decline, and higher mortality rates. While pharmacological treatments can have significant side effects in older adults, non-pharmacological interventions offer safer alternatives.ObjectiveThis systematic review aimed to evaluate the effectiveness of non-pharmacological interventions in managing acute pain and delirium in hospitalized older patients.MethodsA comprehensive search was conducted across PubMed, Scopus, Web of Science, Embase, Cochrane Library, and Google Scholar databases up to July 2025. The review included randomized controlled trials and quasi-experimental studies involving patients aged ≥ 65 years that used non-pharmacological interventions.ResultsThe review included eight studies. Music therapy and hand massage consistently reduced acute pain and anxiety among older patients, while the results for delirium prevention were mixed. some interventions, like geriatric care bundles, significantly reduced delirium incidence, while others showed no effect. These findings highlight the clinical relevance of non-pharmacological interventions as safer alternatives to pharmacological management in geriatric care.ConclusionNon-pharmacological interventions are effective in reducing acute pain and anxiety among older inpatients, with some showing promise in delirium prevention. However, the variability in intervention types and study quality highlights the need for standardized protocols and further research.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12877-025-06764-1.

Introduction

Acute pain and delirium are two of the most frequent and difficult conditions for hospitalized older adults. Delirium, characterized by a sudden and fluctuating disruption in attention, awareness, perception, and motor activity, affects up to 20% of older inpatients and often remains undetected [1, 2].

Patients experiencing delirium face serious adverse outcomes, including readmission, prolonged hospital stay (LOS), increased mortality, and long-term cognitive decline [1–6]. Despite its high prevalence and consequences, there is no definitive and consistently effective treatment for delirium [1, 2, 7].Delirium is an acute neurocognitive disorder caused by systemic disturbances that temporarily disrupt brain function [8–10]. It typically presents as sudden changes in mental status with confusion, restlessness, and fluctuating attention levels [11]. Acute pain is another important factor contributing to delirium in hospitalized older patients. Studies have shown a strong correlation between pain intensity after acute illness or surgery and the likelihood of developing delirium. Moreover, patients with delirium often receive higher doses of analgesics, especially opioids [7].

Patients experiencing delirium face a higher risk of adverse outcomes, such as readmission, prolonged length of stay (LOS), increased hospital mortality, and long-term cognitive decline. Despite its high prevalence and serious implications, there is currently no definitive and consistently effective treatment for delirium [1, 2, 7]. Delirium is an acute neurocognitive disorder caused by temporary disruptions in brain function due to underlying systemic disturbances [8–10]. It presents as an abrupt change in mental status, with daily episodes of confusion, restlessness, and fluctuating attention levels [11]. Its occurrence is also linked to decreased function, lower treatment adherence, prolonged recovery times, higher healthcare costs, and increased mortality.

Acute pain is another major factor contributing to delirium among hospitalized older patients. Studies show a strong correlation between pain intensity after acute illness or surgery and the likelihood of developing delirium [12, 13]. Moreover, older patients experiencing delirium often receive higher doses of analgesics, especially opioids [7]. Inadequately managed pain impedes rehabilitation, disrupts sleep, increases anxiety, and may worsen cognitive function. Consequently, this leads to poorer recovery outcomes and increased discharge rates to long-term care facilities [3, 14]. While pain is generally seen as an uncomfortable and undesirable condition [15], healthcare professionals may under-recognize pain in older patients due to concerns about medication side effects or dependency [3, 14]. Older adults are also more susceptible to complications because of existing comorbidities like dementia, metabolic imbalances, or cerebrovascular conditions [16]. Therefore, optimizing pain management is critical, not only for comfort but also to prevent further cognitive decline [7, 14].

Uncontrolled pain severely affects the function of older adults, resulting in limited mobility, sleep disturbances, social withdrawal, depression, and difficulties with daily tasks. Research indicates that older adults in pain are significantly more likely to experience functional impairments compared to those without pain. In frail individuals, such impairments further compromise independence and cognitive functioning [17].

Managing acute pain in older adults presents distinct challenges. Although opioids are standard for pain relief, they can cause over-sedation, confusion, gastrointestinal issues, and even cardiopulmonary risks [7, 16]. Age-related physiological changes also influence how older adults perceive and report pain, often leading to underdiagnoses or inadequate treatments [17]. Additionally, chronic comorbidities like arthritis, Parkinson’s disease, and diabetic neuropathy contribute to complex pain presentations in older adults [17].

Although NSAIDs have been used as opioid alternatives, their application in older patients is limited due to side effects such as gastrointestinal bleeding, renal damage, and hypertension [16]. Many experts recommend prioritizing non-pharmacological interventions to decrease agitation in patients [18]. Recently, alternative treatment techniques have gain attraction due to the side effects associated with pharmacological treatments [15]. For instance, massage in nursing care combines comfort with emotional touch. While tactile sensitivity may decrease with age, sensitivity to emotional touch increases and helps reduce delusions and delirium [18]. In a recent study, Magi et al. (2024) highlighted the importance of non-pharmacological interventions in maintaining the comfort of hospitalized older adults, particularly during painful nursing procedures. They reported that interventions such as dressing changes, injections, catheterization, and patient mobilization can cause significant pain and anxiety, which, if not managed, may exacerbate anticipatory responses to pain in subsequent procedures. The authors emphasized that approaches including local anesthesia, patient education, behavioral and focusing techniques can effectively reduce pain and improve the patient experience, especially when pharmacological treatments are limited due to age, comorbidities, or adverse effects [19]. For decades, various interventions, both pharmacological and non-pharmacological measures, have been widely investigated to prevent or decrease the risk of delirium. The outcomes of implementing non-pharmacological interventions have been promising [4]. This systematic review aimed to evaluate the effectiveness of non-pharmacological interventions in managing acute pain and delirium in older patients. By highlighting safe and practical strategies, this study sought to inform clinical decision-making and improve care outcomes for this vulnerable population, addressing an urgent and growing need in aging healthcare systems.

Materials and methods

The PRISMA 2020 approach for Systematic Review (PRISMA-SR) was used to conduct this study [20].

Eligibility criteria

This systematic review used specific inclusion and exclusion criteria to identify relevant studies and analyze their results. Non-pharmacological interventions that have shown promise in reducing delirium and pain in older patients, include hand massage, music therapy, and educational packages [4, 15, 18, 21, 22].

Information sources

We extensively searched across electronic databases, including Embase, PubMed, Google Scholar, Scopus, Cochrane Library, and Web of Science [22] until 3 July 2025. Furthermore, we expanded our search by reviewing the reference lists of the studies included in our analysis to ensure a comprehensive exploration of pertinent literature.

Selection process

Initially, one researcher imported all search results into EndNote Desktop software and removed any duplicates. Subsequently, two researchers independently screened the titles and abstracts of the articles according to established eligibility criteria. Any discrepancies in study selection were resolved through a full-text review, with a third researcher involved if needed. Efforts were made to obtain inaccessible articles and unpublished data by contacting the corresponding authors of the eligible studies. The screening process was represented using a PRISMA 2020 flow diagram [20] (Fig. 1).

Data collection process

We developed a data extraction form to ensure consistency and performed a calibration exercise using three primary articles. Afterward, two authors independently extracted data from all articles. Any differences were addressed through discussions that included a third author. The form included key information such as author(s), year, country, objective, hospital inpatient ward, type of study, inclusion/exclusion criteria, sample size, delirium scale, pain scale, intervention group, control group, outcome, and side effects.

Study risk of bias assessment

To evaluate the quality of the studies, we employed the CASP Randomized Controlled Trial Standard Checklist. This checklist consists of 11 questions divided into four sections: (A) evaluating the validity of the core study design, (B) examining the strength of the study methodology, (C) analyzing the reporting of results, and (D) considering the local implications of the findings [23]. Studies that failed to meet more than half of the checklist criteria or were ambiguous were excluded. Additionally, we assessed the risk of bias for each study using the Revised Cochrane risk-of-bias tool for randomized trials (RoB 2). This tool identifies potential sources of bias across five areas: (1) bias from the randomization process (2), bias from deviations from intended interventions (3), bias from missing outcome data (4), bias in outcome measurement, and (5) bias in the selection of reported results [24]. Based on these quality assessments, studies were graded as follows: articles that fully met the quality criteria were defined as grade A, indicating a low possibility of various biases. Articles satisfying the quality standards were defined as grade B, indicating a moderate possibility of bias. Original articles that did not meet the quality standards were defined as grade C, indicating that a high possibility of bias [21] (Table 2).

Characteristics of included studies

Data synthesis

We performed a descriptive and qualitative synthesis of the study results for all included studies, which are presented both narratively and in tabular forms.

Results

Literature search

A total of 2283 articles were initially identified through database searches. After removing duplicates, 1605 studies underwent title and abstract screening. This process resulted in 85 full-text articles selected for eligibility. Finally, eight studies met the inclusion criteria and were included in this systematic review (Fig. 1).

Study characteristics

The included studies were conducted in various countries, including Switzerland, Germany, Turkey, The USA, Thailand, and China, between 2006 and 2025. All studies involved participants aged 65 years and older who were hospitalized for conditions such as hip fracture repair, cardiac surgery, or general postoperative recovery. Sample sizes of the included studies ranged from 6 to 300 participants. Most of the studies were randomized controlled trials; however, one applied a quasi-experimental design, and another used a mixed-methods approach. Delirium assessment tools employed were CAM-ICU, DSM-IV, Nu-DESC, OBS, and MMSE. Pain was assessed using scales such as VAS, NPRS, and NRS (Table 2). Due to substantial heterogeneity across interventions, outcome measures, and study designs, no quantitative meta-analysis was conducted. A narrative synthesis approach was therefore adopted.

Summary of intervention effects

Music therapy and nurse-delivered hand massage consistently reduced pain scores and/or anxiety, with six of eight included studies reporting significant improvements in pain or decreased analgesic use. In contrast, the effects of these interventions on delirium were mixed. Multicomponent geriatric care bundles and comprehensive perioperative programs provided the most robust evidence for reducing delirium incidence or duration, while regional nerve blocks reliably reduced pain but did not consistently impact delirium outcomes. Overall, four of nine studies demonstrated significant reductions in delirium incidence or duration. Across interventions, safety was favorable, with no major adverse events reported.

Non-pharmacological interventions for delirium

Lundström et al. (2007) [25] conducted a randomized controlled trial in Sweden on 199 patients aged ≥ 70 with femoral neck fractures. The intervention group received comprehensive geriatric care, including early mobilization, staff education, and individualized planning, while the control group received usual orthopedic care. The intervention group showed significantly lower delirium incidence and duration, fewer complications (e.g., UTIs, falls), shorter hospital stays, and higher rates of return to independent living. No serious side effects were reported.

Kaufmann et al. (2023) [26] in Germany explored the effects of intraoperative music and positive suggestions via MP3 audio in 118 TAVR patients. Delirium incidence was not significantly different between the intervention and placebo groups. POD was more common in males and those with lower MMSE scores. No adverse events or conscious recall were reported.

Unneby et al. (2020) [27] evaluated preoperative femoral nerve block (FNB) in 236 older patients with hip fractures in Sweden. They reported no significant difference in overall postoperative delirium rates. However, FNB might reduce preoperative delirium and opioid use, especially in patients with dementia. Eight postoperative adverse drug effects were reported in the FNB group, while none occurred in the control group.

Yuyen et al. (2025) [4] in Thailand used a multicomponent non-pharmacological protocol in the Surgical Intensive Care Unit (SICU) setting with 300 older patients. Although the intervention did not significantly reduce postoperative delirium incidence, it led to lower rates of endotracheal tube self-removal and nosocomial infections. No adverse events were reported; although, adherence to the protocol was 74.6%.

Schaub et al. (2018) [18] investigated seven nurse-delivered hand massages in 40 acutely hospitalized older adults with dementia in Switzerland. Although overall Cohen-Mansfield Agitation Inventory (CMAI) agitation scores did not differ significantly from routine care, the massage group showed a greater downward trend in agitation. Importantly, a significant week-2 reduction in stress biomarkers was observed: salivary α-amylase dropped sharply immediately after the fourth massage, and salivary cortisol fell 20 min later; both markers showed further reduction by the end of week 3 sessions. No adverse events were observed, suggesting hand massage is a low-risk, potentially calming sensory approach in acute geriatric psychiatry.

Lin et al. (2024) [28] assessed a comprehensive intervention program for 159 older patients undergoing cardiac surgery in China. The program reduced delirium incidence (24.68% vs. 47.56%), improved sleep, reduced pain scores, and shortened hospital stays. No adverse effects were reported.

Non-pharmacological interventions for pain

McCaffrey & Locsin (2006) [14] studied music therapy in 124 older patients undergoing hip or knee surgery in the USA. Patients who received music interventions experienced lower pain scores, reduced use of pain medications, fewer episodes of acute confusion, and higher satisfaction with care. No adverse effects were reported. Büdüş & Gökalp (2025) [15] in Turkey investigated the effect of Hüseyni maqam music therapy in 60 patients admitted to a CICU after angiography. They found significant reductions in pain, anxiety, agitation, and sedation levels, with effects persisting for at least 30 min post-intervention. No adverse effects were reported.

Unneby et al. (2020) [27] used the VAS and found that femoral nerve block might help reduce pain and opioid use in dementia patients, though overall delirium rates were unaffected. However, opioid-related side effects were observed in the intervention group. Lin et al. (2024) [28] noted that their comprehensive protocol also contributed to pain reduction alongside reduced delirium.

Discussion

This systematic review evaluated the effectiveness and safety of non-pharmacological interventions in pain management and delirium prevention in hospitalized older adults. Findings from eight selected studies showed that various interventions such as music therapy, hand massage, and multicomponent non-pharmacological protocols had positive effects on reducing pain and anxiety; however, results regarding the reduction of delirium were inconsistent. While some of these interventions were successful in improving pain and reducing anxiety, their impact on the incidence of delirium in patients undergoing major surgeries was limited.

Delirium is a common and often underdiagnosed complication in hospitalized older patients, characterized by sudden onset, fluctuating course, and disturbances in attention and cognition. Although typically temporary, delirium is significantly associated with adverse outcomes such as increased mortality, prolonged hospitalization, and reduced physical and cognitive functioning in older adults. Our review of several non-pharmacological approaches showed diverse results, with interventions targeting inflammatory pathways generally yielding more promising outcomes.

Nurses are pivotal in translating non-pharmacological interventions into daily clinical practice. Their constant presence at the bedside uniquely positions them to assess patients’ needs, initiate and deliver interventions such as massage or music therapy, and coordinate multicomponent care bundles. Beyond direct delivery, nurses play a crucial role in monitoring delirium risk factors, documenting patient responses, and ensuring continuity of care across shifts. Embedding these practices into routine nursing workflows enhances feasibility, cost-effectiveness, and patient-centered outcomes, underscoring nursing’s leadership in non-pharmacological delirium and pain management [29].

For instance, the study by Schaub et al. (2018) emphasized that hand massage significantly reduced biological stress markers (e.g., alpha-amylase and cortisol) and agitation in older patients with dementia [18]. These results indicate the positive effects of massage in lowering stress and anxiety, which could indirectly help prevent delirium. From a physiological perspective, moderate-pressure hand massage stimulates mechanoreceptors, which, according the gate control theory can inhibit pain signals. It also activates the parasympathetic nervous system, evidenced by increased vagal tone and decreased cortisol levels, ultimately reducing pain perception and stress [30–32]. McCaffrey and Locsin (2006) also demonstrated that music therapy in older adults undergoing hip and knee surgeries resulted in decreased pain, reduced use of analgesics, and fewer episodes of acute confusion. Music may help lower anxiety and pain by influencing the parasympathetic nervous system and reducing stress hormones such as cortisol. These effects can indirectly contribute to delirium reduction, as anxiety and stress are major contributors to its onset [32, 33]. Beyond the underlying physiological mechanisms, the practical implementation of these non-pharmacological interventions in clinical settings is primarily led by nurses. Nurses are central in the delivery of non-pharmacological interventions for delirium prevention and management. In several randomized trials, nursing-led music interventions in ICUs have demonstrated reductions in delirium severity, pain, sedation, and anxiety in mechanically ventilated patients [34].

Kaufmann et al. (2023) found that while intraoperative music and positive suggestions did not significantly reduce delirium incidence, they alleviated postoperative pain and anxiety. This study suggests that music therapy plays an important role in anxiety reduction and can be considered a complementary intervention in pain and delirium management [26]. Music is also believed to affect human biological rhythms by increasing the release of endorphins—natural painkillers—thus lowering the perception of pain [35].

Lundström et al. (2007) in Sweden showed that a geriatric multimodal intervention reduced delirium incidence, duration, and complications (e.g., urinary tract infections, pressure ulcers, and falls) and improved return to independent living in hospitalized older adults. This study particularly emphasized the importance of early mobilization and healthcare staff training in preventing delirium and reducing the need for analgesics and sedatives. It appears that non-pharmacological interventions directly help reduce stress and anxiety—key risk factors for delirium [25].

Unneby et al. (2020) examined the effect of preoperative femoral nerve block (FNB) in older adults with hip fracture. Although this method did not affect delirium incidence, it significantly reduced preoperative pain and analgesic use. The study further highlighted the importance of effective pain management and avoiding excessive opioid use, which may reduce delirium by decreasing stress and anxiety [27].

Yuyen et al. (2025) in Thailand showed the effectiveness of multimodal educational packages in reducing delirium incidence in older patients admitted to intensive care units. These packages, which included education for patients and healthcare staff provided information on stress management, mobility planning, and sleep improvement, all contributing to positive effects on delirium reduction [4]. The care bundle specifically emphasized prevention, early diagnosis, and minimizing the long-term consequences of delirium for both patients and families. Nevertheless, the critical care community continues to emphasize the need for stronger evidence in areas such as pain, agitation, and sleep quality in relation to delirium [36].

Stress, anxiety, and sleep disturbances are well-established contributors to delirium vulnerability. Recent evidence by Baldassini Rodriguez et al. (2021) demonstrated strong associations between poor sleep quality, anxiety, and reduced resilience, supporting the biopsychosocial framework relevant to older hospitalized adults [37].

This systematic review evaluated non-pharmacological interventions for pain reduction and delirium prevention in hospitalized older patients. A key strength is the use of meta-analysis to synthesize evidence from various studies, enhancing the robustness and reliability of our findings. Additionally, the review explores a diverse range of interventions, including music therapy, hand massage, and multi-component protocols, providing valuable insights into their effectiveness in reducing pain and delirium in older patients. The review also emphasized the importance of combining non-pharmacological interventions with personalized approaches to significantly improving patients’ quality of life.

Limitations

A primary limitation of this review lies in the variability of the intervention protocols used across the studies. Interventions such as music therapy and hand massage were implemented differently across studies, making direct comparisons challenging. Additionally, the use of various assessment tools for pain and delirium across studies further complicated data analysis and interpretation. Furthermore, only a few studies included appropriate control groups, and some studies had sampling limitations, which may have reduced the strength of their results. Exclusion of qualitative studies limited our ability to capture patient perspectives on the acceptability and subjective experience of non-pharmacological interventions.

Conclusion

This systematic review concluded that non-pharmacological interventions such as music therapy and hand massage can effectively reduce pain and prevent delirium in older patients. These results highlight the significant potential of non-pharmacological approaches to improve the quality of life for hospitalized older patients. However, there is a need for further studies with more rigorous designs and standardized protocols to assess various interventions. Ultimately, this study suggests that combining non-pharmacological interventions with personalized approaches can contribute to more effective pain and delirium management.
