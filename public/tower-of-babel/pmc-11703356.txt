A platform immunoassay for the quantification of biotherapeutics with glycine-serine linkers
Authors: Alexander Pöhler, Michael Antony, Janine Faigle, Gregor Jordan, Roland F Staack, Gregor P Lotz
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAssessing the pharmacokinetics of biotherapeutics is essential across all phases of drug development to understand drug exposure. At early stages, platform drug quantification immunoassays offer a versatile method for evaluating exposure for diverse biotherapeutics when specific reagents are not yet available, providing fast data for molecules with common structural features. To ensure clearly defined bioanalytical data, it is essential to conduct interference testing for anti-drug antibodies (ADA) or soluble target (starget), although these assays measure total exposure. In this study, a novel platform immunoassay has been developed that detects a variety of multi-domain drugs with the common structural feature of glycine-serine (G/S) linkers. The assay was successfully qualified and is suitable for early total drug exposure analysis of compounds with G/S linkers. Qualification parameters, including accuracy and precision, were successfully determined, and interference from ADAs and starget was assessed. ADA and starget interference tests showed no impact for one compound, but may affect total drug detection for another. Therefore, it remains recommended to assess the impact of ADAs and starget on assay performance. Overall, the platform G/S linker assay provides a rapid alternative for total exposure analysis in early development when specific assays are unavailable.

Introduction

The precise quantification of biotherapeutic concentration is crucial for the generation of pharmacokinetic (PK) data to understand the correlation between exposure and pharmacodynamic (PD) response, safety, and efficacy. Precise PK data, based on well-defined bioanalytical measurements and standardized nomenclature [1], is critical for dose and schedule selection in early drug development. Reliable PK data supports clinical decision-making and facilitates the development of safe and effective pharmaceuticals [2,3].

The development of a drug quantification immunoassay (PK assay) measuring free drug exposure is valuable because it is sensitive to ADA and soluble drug target (starget) interference, thereby improving PK/PD correlations and enhancing the understanding of pharmacological effects. However, developing a specific PK assay for free drug exposure requires significant investment in reagent generation, assay development, and validation [4–6]. In the early phases of drug development, specific reagent tools are often not yet available, and generating these reagents for specific PK assay development is time-consuming and costly. A valuable alternative at these early stages is the implementation of platform PK assays [7–9]. Although these PK assays do not cover functional drug-binding sites and measure total drug exposure rather than free drug exposure, they offer the advantage of being applicable to a wide range of molecules within the company’s biotherapeutic portfolio. All platform PK assays share the characteristic that their reagents bind to homologous epitopes beyond the specific complementarity-determining regions (functional-binding sites), but not to endogenous human immunoglobulins, enabling the detection of common structural elements (“features”) across different molecules. A common structural feature in various protein-engineered biotherapeutics is the use of flexible linkers composed of repeating units of glycine and serine (G/S linkers). These linkers are commonly used to connect protein domains, thereby enhancing the flexibility and functionality of engineered proteins [10–13]. These linkers are particularly relevant for the generation of multifunctional biotherapeutics [14–18].

In this study, a novel platform PK assay has been developed to detect multifunctional biotherapeutic drugs with G/S linkers. The platform G/S linker assay detects a variety of drugs with different designs and formats that incorporate one or more G/S linkers, enabling rapid implementation of total exposure analysis.

Materials and methods

Description of human matrices

Human pooled serum (Neo Biotech, Nanterre, France) and human individual sera from healthy donors (in.vent Diagnostica GmbH, Hennigsdorf, Germany) were used in these studies.

Description of biotherapeutic drugs

The eight biotherapeutic drugs (mAb 1 to mAb 8) are humanized monoclonal antibodies, each possessing distinct functional specificities. All biotherapeutic drugs were produced by Roche Diagnostics GmbH (Mannheim, Germany) and are not available commercially. These antibodies are based on the IgG1 framework but include additional binding domains or ligands connected by G/S linkers that vary in number, length, and amino acid composition. mAb 8 contains no G/S linkers and serves as a negative control antibody. Additionally, all antibodies contain the P329G mutation [14,19]. Description of interference substances

To evaluate potential biotin interference, D(+)-Biotin, ≥99% was procured from VWR International BVBA (Leuven, Belgium). To assess potential drug-specific interferences, four interfering substances were utilized in the analysis of mAb 1. These included two targets, each binding to different epitopes on mAb 1, and two monoclonal mouse-derived surrogate anti-drug antibodies (ADAs), which bind to the complementarity-determining regions (CDRs) on mAb 1 involved in target binding. The first starget (designated as starget 1 of mAb 1) was produced by Roche Diagnostics GmbH (Mannheim, Germany), while the second starget (starget 2 of mAb 1) was sourced from Proteros Biostructures GmbH (Planegg, Germany). The first anti-mAb 1 antibody (ADA 1 of mAb 1) was acquired from Sigma-Aldrich Chemie GmbH (Taufkirchen, Germany) and the second antibody (ADA 2 of mAb 1) was produced by Roche Diagnostics GmbH (Mannheim, Germany). For the assessment of drug-specific interference toward mAb 2, one starget was available (starget 3), which was produced by Roche Diagnostics GmbH (Mannheim, Germany).

Description of the platform assay for the quantification of biotherapeutic compounds with G/S linkers

The following enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) method (Figure 2) is based on the High Ionic Strength Dissociation Assay (HISDA) that was previously published in the field of immunogenicity testing [20,21]. The assay signal is generated by the addition of Anti-Dig Fab fragments conjugated to horseradish peroxidase. Schematic overview of the platform G/S linker assay.

All incubation steps of the method were performed for 1 hour at room temperature whilst shaking at 450 rpm except indicated otherwise. Various biotherapeutic drugs were utilized to generate titration curves by performing serial dilutions in pooled human serum. All samples, including negative controls, were diluted at a ratio of 1:10 using a 4 M MgCl2 *6 H2O solution (VWR International BVBA, Leuven, Belgium) and incubated for 30 minutes. Subsequently, all samples were diluted at a 1:10 ratio using Universal ELISA buffer (Roche Diagnostics GmbH, Mannheim, Germany), supplemented with 2000 ng/ml of a biotin-labeled antibody targeting G/S linkers (anti-G/S linker-Bi; Cell Signaling Technology Europe, Leiden, Netherlands) and 2000 ng/ml of a digoxigenin-labeled antibody targeting the P329G mutation (anti-PG-Dig; Roche Diagnostics GmbH, Mannheim, Germany) and incubated. The formed immune complexes were transferred to a streptavidin (SA)-coated microtiter plate (MTP; Microcoat Biotechnologie GmbH, Bernried, Germany) and incubated to immobilize the complexes via the biotin-labeled capture antibody. Following a single washing step with 300 μl of 1× phosphate-buffered saline (PBS; Roche Diagnostics GmbH, Mannheim, Germany) containing 0.05% (v/v) Tween20 (Roche Diagnostics GmbH, Mannheim, Germany), 50 mU/ml horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-labeled anti-digoxigenin Fab fragments (Roche Diagnostics GmbH, Mannheim, Germany), diluted in Universal ELISA buffer, were added to the MTP and incubated. Following three washing steps, the substrate reaction was initiated by adding 100 μl per well of 2,2′-azino-bis(3-ethylbenzothiazoline-6-sulfonic acid) (ABTS) solution. The optical density was measured at a wavelength of 405 nm, with a reference wavelength of 490 nm, using a microplate reader (Model: Sunrise; Tecan, Männedorf, Switzerland). Measurements were taken until the sample with the highest drug concentration reached an absorbance of 2.0 ± 0.2 units. The final absorbance was determined by subtracting the absorbance at 490 nm from the absorbance at 405 nm. Signal to blank (SB) values were calculated by dividing the signal obtained from each drug-spiked sample by the signal from a blank sample containing only human pooled serum. These SB values were subsequently used to generate the standard calibration curve by a non-linear four-parameter fit using a Wiemer-Rodbard function.

Description of the specific-target binding competent assay for mAb 1

The same procedure was followed as for the platform G/S linker assay, with the following modifications: A biotin-labeled antibody against the binding site of starget 2 of mAb 1 was used as the capture reagent, and a digoxigenin-labeled antibody against the binding site of starget 1 of mAb 1 was used as the detection reagent. Both antibodies, sourced from Roche Diagnostics GmbH (Mannheim, Germany), were used at a concentration of 500 ng/ml, diluted in 1× PBS containing 0.5% (w/v) bovine serum albumin (assay buffer). The ELISA was performed on half-well SA-coated MTPs (Microcoat Biotechnologie GmbH, Bernried, Germany), which were initially coated with the capture reagent, incubated, and washed three times. All samples were diluted 100-fold using the assay buffer and incubated in designated wells of the MTP. Following three washes, the detection reagent was added, incubated, and the MTP was washed again. Subsequently, all wells were incubated with 20 mU/ml HRP-labeled anti-digoxigenin Fab fragments, diluted in an assay buffer, and processed for enzymatic reaction using parameters similar to those in the platform G/S linker assay.

The assay qualifies as “target binding competent” because the anti-idiotypic antibodies used as capture and detection have been verified to mimic the target (data not shown) [1].

Preparation of titration curves for the initial feasibility testing using eight biotherapeutic drugs

For an initial pre-screening, eight biotherapeutic drugs (mAb 1 to mAb 8) were analyzed to test the feasibility and flexibility of the novel platform method. Each drug was spiked into a human pooled serum at an initial concentration of 14,580 ng/ml and subsequently subjected to six serial 3-fold dilution steps using human pooled serum.

Procedure for determining assay sensitivity

To evaluate the assay sensitivity, mAb 1 and mAb 2 were spiked into human pooled serum at an initial concentration of 1280 ng/ml. These samples were then subjected to a series of 10 two-fold serial dilutions using human pooled serum. Each dilution curve was independently prepared in triplicate and the mean values from these analyses were calculated. Based on the results of this analysis, the quantitative range for both mAb 1 and mAb 2 was determined, including the upper limit of quantification (ULOQ) and lower limit of quantification (LLOQ). An arbitrary SB value of 2 was employed to approximate the assay sensitivity through interpolation using a non-linear four-parameter Wiemer-Rodbard function.

Preparation of quality control (QC) samples

QC samples were prepared using mAb 1 and mAb 2, which were spiked into human pooled serum at various concentrations across the quantitative range. The set of controls included the ULOQ-QC at the upper limit of the quantification range, a high QC (HQC) at 75% of the ULOQ, a medium QC (MQC) at the median of the quantitative range, a low QC (LQC) at four times the LLOQ and the LLOQ-QC at the lower limit of the quantification range. Prepared QC samples were analyzed in triplicate and their concentrations were back-calculated using calibration curves that were analyzed within the same run. To confirm the accuracy of the assay, back-calculated concentrations of at least 4 out of 5 QC samples needed to be within ± 20% of the nominal (theoretical) concentrations for the HQC, MQC, and LQC and within ± 25% for the ULOQ-QC and LLOQ-QC. The precision of mean concentrations determined at each concentration level should not exceed 20%.

Procedure for evaluating assay selectivity

To evaluate assay selectivity, mAb 1 and mAb 2 were spiked into 10 individual human sera from healthy donors at both HQC and LLOQ levels. All concentrations were back-calculated using calibration curves. For the assay to be considered selective, at least 80% of the back-calculated concentrations are needed to agree with the nominal concentrations within ± 20% for the HQC and within ± 25% for the LLOQ.

Procedure for evaluating dilution linearity

To evaluate the dilution linearity of the assay, mAb 1 and mAb 2 were spiked into human pooled serum at an initial concentration of 50 µg/ml. These samples were then serially diluted using human pooled serum, ensuring that three dilutions fell within the quantitative calibration range. All concentrations of the dilutions within the calibration range were back-calculated using calibration curves. For the assay to demonstrate acceptable dilution linearity, the back-calculated concentrations needed to agree with the nominal concentrations within ± 20%.

Procedure for evaluating biotin interference

To evaluate potential biotin interference [22,23], mAb 1 and mAb 2 were spiked into human pooled serum at both the ULOQ and LQC levels. Biotin was concurrently spiked into these samples at final concentrations of 60 ng/ml and 200 ng/ml. For the assay to be considered free from relevant biotin interference, the back-calculated concentrations needed to agree with the nominal concentrations within ± 25% for the ULOQ and within ± 20% for the LQC in the presence of 200 ng/ml biotin.

Procedure for evaluating interference by stargets and surrogate ADAs

For the evaluation of starget interference, mAb 1 was spiked into human-pooled serum at the LQC level. Concurrently, two starget molecules, starget 1 and starget 2, were spiked into different aliquots of the drug-spiked sample at varying final concentrations of 0, 10, 100, and 1000 ng/ml. For the evaluation of interference by two surrogate ADAs, ADA 1 and ADA 2, a similar procedure was applied, with ADAs being spiked into different aliquots of the drug-spiked sample at varying concentrations of 0, 100, 1000, 10000, and 100,000 ng/ml. The mixed samples were incubated overnight at room temperature to allow for complex formation and analyzed on the next day. For the assay to be considered free from interference by stargets and surrogate ADAs, the back-calculated LQC concentrations needed to agree with the spiked concentration within ± 20%.

For mAb 2, the same procedure was used as for mAb 1, but only one starget was available (starget 3), and no surrogate ADA was included due to lack of availability.

Analysis of serum samples from a single-dose mouse PK study

Samples were obtained from three severe combined immunodeficient/beige (SCID/beige) mice following a single-dose intravenous administration of 0.5 mg/kg mAb 1. Blood samples were collected at pre-dose (0 h) and at subsequent time points: 0.083 h, 7 h, 1d, 2d, 3d, 7d, and 10d. The blood samples were collected into serum separator tubes and centrifuged. The obtained serum was immediately frozen and stored at −80°C until analysis.

All samples were analyzed using both the platform G/S linker assay and the specific target-binding competent assay for mAb 1 according to their respective protocols. Samples were diluted with an assay buffer until the signals fell within the respective calibration ranges.

Results and discussion

For this initial primary screen to demonstrate overall feasibility, the assay was configured using a concentration of 200 ng/ml for both the capture and detection reagents across all biotherapeutic analytes. mAb 8, which does not contain a G/S linker, was included as a negative control.

All biotherapeutics containing G/S linkers (mAb 1–7) were detected showing concentration-dependent signal-to-blank (SB) ratios, whereas the negative control, mAb 8, which lacks a G/S linker, generated a SB lower than 6 even at the highest concentration of 14,580 ng/ml (Figure 3). A comparison with the SB value of the least sensitive drug, mAb 6, which is 66.7 at the same drug concentration, underscores the minimal response observed for the negative control (Supplementary Table S1). This result suggests that the primary ELISA specifically detects biotherapeutic drugs with G/S linkers and PG mutation in a concentration-dependent manner and can be used to develop and qualify a platform PK assay. Based on the primary screening results, two of the seven mAbs, mAb 1 and mAb 2, were selected for the development and qualification of a platform PK assay, following criteria as described in regulatory guidelines [2,3].

Interestingly, the two selected mAbs have markedly different G/S linker positions (Figure 1).

The analytical sensitivity of mAb 1 and mAb 2 was determined to be as low as 5 ng/ml in serum, using an arbitrary SB value of 2 (Figure 4). Due to the comparable analytical curves of mAb 1 and mAb 2, the dynamic range for both drugs was established between 320 and 5 ng/ml. Five quality controls were prepared in a human pooled serum to determine the accuracy (recovery %) and precision (CV %) of the assay. The concentrations of the quality controls were as follows: ULOQ-QC at 320 ng/ml, HQC at 240 ng/ml, MQC at 40 ng/ml, LQC at 20 ng/ml and LLOQ-QC at 5 ng/ml. As shown in Supplementary Table S2, all QCs met the acceptance criteria, confirming the accuracy and precision of the assay.

To further support the accuracy and precision of the assay for the two mAbs, a selectivity experiment was conducted. Two concentrations, one at HQC level and one at LLOQ-QC level, were spiked into ten individual serum samples for both drugs (Supplementary Table S3). Both spiked concentrations of each mAb were recovered within ± 20% for HQC (240 ng/ml) and ± 25% for LLOQ-QC (5 ng/ml) in each individual serum sample. These data confirm that the assay accurately measures mAbs with G/S linkers within the serum matrix, regardless of their format or design.

Another important assessment to qualify the assay for drugs with G/S linkers is the dilution of high-concentration samples into the dynamic range of the assay and the evaluation of their linearity relative to the calibration curve (dilution linearity). This is particularly important because the platform PK assay should be applicable for analysis independent of the initial sample concentration, thereby supporting studies with different doses (e.g., dose-range finding studies). In Supplementary Table S4, samples of mAb 1 and mAb 2 were serially diluted by a factor of 4.

The results show that for both drugs, the concentrations of all three dilutions (195, 48.8 and 12.2 ng/ml) within the calibration range were recovered within the acceptance criteria of ± 20% (Supplementary Table S4). This confirms that the assay can accurately measure drug concentration and be broadly applicable for biotherapeutics with G/S linkers, provided the dilutions fall within the dynamic calibration range of the assay.

The design of the assay format and the incorporation of a dissociation step are important aspects for total exposure analysis. However, it is not yet known whether the assay is impacted by potential starget or ADA binding, despite the assay design and inclusion of a dissociation step.

To evaluate the potential interference caused by starget binding on drug detection, mAb 1 and mAb 2 were analyzed with and without different concentrations of spiked starget (Table 1). Two different stargets, starget 1 and starget 2, were tested for the bispecific mAb 1 (Figure 1). The data demonstrate that all drug recoveries, even in the presence of 1000 ng/ml of stargets, met the acceptance criteria within ± 20%, indicating no significant interference from these two targets in mAb 1 quantification (Table 1). Next, one starget, starget 3, was used for mAb 2 (Figure 1). Therefore, the interference of starget 3 on mAb 2 detection was tested under the same experimental conditions as those used in the mAb 1 assessment, with starget concentrations up to 1000 ng/ml. Interestingly, the recovery of mAb 2 detection was impacted by >60% when 1000 ng/ml starget 3 was present (Table 1). These results indicate that the starget may not interfere in one case, as observed for mAb 1, but might interfere in another case as seen with mAb 2. While the assay design, including the dissociation step, is optimal for total exposure analysis of mAb 1 in the presence of starget, it has limitations for detecting mAb 2 when starget 3 is binding. Consequently, the platform PK assay can be used as the default assay for drugs with G/S linkers when it is known that there is no starget present or no cross-reactivity with the starget in the animal species being studied. However, when a starget is present, an additional interference assessment is necessary before the assay can be used for total exposure analysis, especially if the G/S linker is in close proximity to the target-binding site of the drug.

Another important consideration for potential interference in the detection of drugs with G/S linkers using the platform PK assay is the ADA response and ADA binding during treatment. This may not be an issue for a single-dose PK study due to the delayed onset of an ADA response, but it could be critical for supporting multiple-dose studies.

Often, ADAs are anti-idiotypic and target the functional-binding site of the drug. Therefore, two anti-idiotypic monoclonal antibodies, ADA 1 and ADA 2, which serve as positive control ADA surrogates and are directed against the two target binding sites of mAb 1 were tested for their potential to interfere with drug detection (Figure 1).

ADA 1 significantly impacted the detection of mAb 1, reducing it by more than 70% at a concentration of just 100 ng/ml (Table 1). At a concentration of 100,000 ng/ml ADA 1, the mAb 1 recovery was at 13%. However, ADA 2 did not interfere with the detection of mAb 1, even at the highest concentration of 100,000 ng/ml, showing 100% mAb 1 recovery. While ADA 2 binding, similar to starget 2, has no effect on mAb 1 recovery, ADA 1 binding instead shows a strong impact on mAb 1. This contrasts with the observation of starget 1, which showed no impact on mAb 1 recovery. One possible explanation for this observed discrepancy could be the difference in valency between the ADA (bivalent) and the starget (monovalent) binding. This difference in valency may influence the binding dynamics, the formation of immune complexes and the conformation of mAb 1, thereby affecting the binding of the anti-G/S linker antibody to mAb 1. Affinities and molecular weights of the binding partners may also be influential.

The final set of interference tests to complete the qualification of the platform PK assay involves assessing the potential impact of biotin on assay performance. Nutritional supplements can result in high peaks of biotin concentration in the analytical samples. Given that the assay design is potentially susceptible to biotin interference [22,23], the interference was evaluated up to a concentration of 200 ng/ml biotin, which equals approximately to the median peak biotin concentration following the intake of 20 mg biotin [24]. As a result, all spiked samples were recovered within ± 20% in the presence of 200 ng/ml biotin, thereby confirming that the assay is not impacted by relevant biotin levels (Supplementary Table S5).

Taken together, these data indicate that the platform PK assay for biotherapeutics with a G/S linker is a valid methodology for measuring total exposure. However, in the presence of starget or ADAs, additional evaluation of their impact is recommended. The described recommendations for implementing dissociation steps were considered [25], but it is important to acknowledge that ADAs or starget may still impact the performance of the total exposure analysis.

The extent of the interference might depend on several factors, such as the structure of the drug, location of the binding sites relative to G/S linkers and the binding dynamics and features (e.g., valency and affinity) of the binding partners. These variables can significantly influence whether or not interference occurs despite the best efforts in assay development to minimize such effects. In some cases, the interference may not play a role, such as in pre-clinical analyses where there is no cross-reactivity of the starget toward the drug. In cases where interference may be relevant, it is recommended to perform an interference assessment to understand the assay’s potential limitations.

Alternatively, potential mitigation strategies to overcome this challenge can be considered. If available, incorporating blocking agents that specifically mask the binding of starget and ADAs can mitigate interference. Also, the use of high-affinity capture and detection reagents with rapid association rate constants to effectively outcompete the interfering binders can be a mitigation option. A final mitigation strategy involves using assay reagents that do not compete with target binding, thereby reducing the influence of starget and ADAs. All these mitigation strategies require additional specific reagents, which are typically not available during the early stages of development. Therefore, identifying reagents with platform characteristics that detect common structural features of biotherapeutics, in conjunction with a dissociation step, is the recommended approach for developing platform PK assays at early stages.

After the successful qualification of the PK assay for two mAbs with G/S linkers, the suitability of the assay for PK analysis without relying on drug-specific reagents was explored by supporting an in-vivo single-dose mouse study of mAb 1. mAb 1 has no cross-reactivity to stargets in the mice and no ADAs were detected during the study. For reference, the same PK samples were also analyzed using a specific target-binding competent assay. The results from both assays were nearly congruent, with the concentration–time curves closely aligning (Figure 5). The relative mean drug concentrations from the two assays were within a ± 20% variability range at all time points (Supplementary Table S6), indicating equivalent drug quantification. Therefore, in the absence of starget and ADA, the expected comparability between this platform total assay and a specific-target binding competent assay was demonstrated. In summary, a platform PK assay was developed that is capable of quantifying biotherapeutic compounds containing G/S linkers in biological matrices without the need for analyte-specific reagent generation. The assay qualification results demonstrate robustness in line with the acceptance criteria recommended by regulatory authorities [2,3]. The assay measures total drug exposure, providing valuable quantitative assessments for efficacy and/or safety during early pre-clinical development. Potential limitations due to starget or ADA impact need to be evaluated on a case-by-case basis, considering the intended use of the assay.

Conclusion

The novel platform G/S linker assay described herein has demonstrated effective detection of various multi-domain compounds bearing G/S linkers, establishing it as a versatile tool for the analysis of total exposure. This capability has significant implications for early drug development across all pre-clinical phases, from target identification and lead optimization to lead selection and support of Good Laboratory Practice (GLP) toxicology studies. The assay can serve as a PK screening tool for multi-domain drugs during candidate selection or for exposure control in early efficacy studies. Additionally, it is valuable for PK assessments in GLP toxicology studies lacking specific assays or involving certain drug combinations, as it could distinguish drugs containing G/S linkers from co-administered therapeutics lacking G/S linkers. Furthermore, the assay has the potential to detect drugs containing G/S linkers in human transgene animals that produce human IgGs. Although this platform G/S linker assay can be used for various applications in measuring total drug exposure, the assessment of starget and ADA interference should be considered.

In the future, the landscape of multi-domain therapeutic protein drug candidates will continue to evolve. The methods used for providing clearly defined bioanalytical data are of particular importance, as they generate critical information essential for the development of safe and effective drugs. The use of the platform PK assay will offer a fast approach for providing total drug data, which is particularly valuable during the early stages of drug development. The assay may exhibit flexibility by combining the anti-G/S linker antibody with target-specific reagents to develop alternative PK assays, either using immunoassay platforms or as an enrichment tool for LC-MS technology.
