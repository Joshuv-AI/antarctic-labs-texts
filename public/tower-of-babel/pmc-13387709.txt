Family typology among Mexican families after the COVID-19 pandemic
Authors: Brian González-Pérez, Kenia L. Villegas-Saldívar, Edgar E. Torres García, Félix I. Islas-Ruz, Dulce A. Rivera-Ávila, María V. Jiménez-Báez, Sujey A. Ruíz-Magallon
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTContext:The COVID-19 pandemic had a significant impact, profoundly altering household composition, income levels, and economic support structures.Aims:Explore the changes in family typology after the COVID-19 pandemic among Mexican families affiliated with a Family Medicine Unit (FMU) in Tampico, Tamaulipas.Settings and Design:An analytical, comparative, and exploratory study. A total of 73 families attended FMU No. 38 were included. Family typology was determined before and 4 years after the COVID-19 pandemic.Methods and Material:Data collected included identification, and sociodemographic variables. Family typology was determined based on the Mexican Consensus of Family Medicine and Life Cycle stage was classified according to Huerta. Statistical analysis was performed using IBM SPSS Statistics. Descriptive analysis of quantitative variables was conducted using measures of central tendency and standard deviation (SD), qualitative variables were described using frequencies and percentages. Comparisons between variables were performed using the Chi-square (χ2) test.Results:The mean age was 38.52 ± 12.77 years in the pre-pandemic period. Pre-pandemic marital status was predominantly married (57.5%), followed by single (32.9). An increase in the proportion of divorced and widowed individuals was observed in the post-pandemic period (6.8% and 2.7%, respectively). Statistically significant differences were identified in the post-pandemic period, including a decrease in the proportion of employees (from 78.1% to 65.8%). The proportion of nuclear families decreased (from 39.7% to 11.9%), while non-integrated nucleus increased (8.2% to 19.7% P = 0.029).Conclusions:The findings of this study confirm the profound impact of the COVID-19 pandemic on the structure and dynamics of Mexican families, in labor, economic and family organization aspects.

Introduction

In recent decades, family structures and typologies have undergone substantial transformations derived from socioeconomic, cultural, and technological factors; however, the COVID-19 pandemic accelerated and intensified these changes worldwide.[12] In the Latin American context, the family has historically been regarded as the primary unit of socialization and emotional support; however, lockdowns, the health crisis, and job losses led to significant changes in daily dynamics and household composition.[34] These transformations not only modified parental roles and the distribution of domestic work, but also the psychological well-being of intra-family communication and extended support networks.[56]

Family typology, defined as the classification of families according to their structure (nuclear, single-parent, extended, reconstituted or single-person households), has diversified during and after the pandemic.[7] Before the lockdown, nuclear and extended households predominated in Mexico and Latin America; however, public health measures, reverse migration and unemployment led to an increase in multigenerational and single-person households.[1] Similar changes were also documented in European and Asian countries, associated with the search for intergenerational support and emotional stability in the face of uncertainty.[89] These variations reflect the adaptive capacity of the family system in response to external crises, as supported by the literature on resilience and family cohesion.[1011]

The COVID-19 pandemic represented one of the greatest social, health, and economic challenges of the twenty-first century; it significantly altered household structure, organization, and emotional bonds, while evidencing the vulnerability of support systems and pre-existing social inequalities. In Mexico, lockdowns, job losses, and changes in domestic roles transformed the ways families interact, allocate resources, and cope with uncertainty, shaping a new family typology.

Several studies suggest that lockdowns reinforced the central role of the family as a space for emotional support, but also made latent conflicts and gender inequalities visible.[1213] Mothers assumed a disproportionate burden of domestic and educational responsibilities, increasing the risk of stress, anxiety, and emotional exhaustion.[141516] In contrast, other studies report that some households experienced a strengthening of affective ties and a positive reorganization of family routines.[1718] The coexistence of both phenomena evidences the heterogeneity of family experiences during the pandemic, depending on factors such as the type of family, socioeconomic level, and the availability of digital, and emotional resources.[192021]

In Latin America, the pandemic underscored the importance of informal and community-based support networks, particularly in contexts of vulnerability.[2223] The pandemic revealed structural gaps that already existed, linked to unequal access to housing, employment, and mental health.[24]

From a psychological and social perspective, the pandemic caused a reconfiguration of patterns of communication, family interaction, and intra-family hierarchy.[25] Recent studies have documented how the quality of family relationships and the level of family functioning predicted the psychological well-being of children and adolescents during lockdowns.[26] Likewise, differences were identified according to the type of family: nuclear households with high emotional cohesion showed greater adaptative capacity, whereas reconstituted homes or homes with previous tensions experienced more severe dysfunctions.[2728] These observations are consistent with family systems theory, which posits that external crises act as catalysts for structural and emotional change within the family system.

International comparative studies show that changes in family structure are also reflected in demographic indicators. For example, multigenerational households increased in Europe and Asia, while in North America there was a growing trend toward living alone.[27] The OECD[29] and the World Happiness Report[30] point out that subjective well-being during the pandemic was strongly mediated by cohabitation and household size. This suggests that family type not only influences internal dynamics, but also shapes perceptions of well-being and resilience in the context of adversity.

Post-pandemic analysis has revealed a gradual reconfiguration of families toward hybrid cohabitation patterns including partially in-person households, virtual relationships, and intergenerational care at a distance.[31] In this context, the family emerges as a dynamic and flexible institution, capable of integrating structural and symbolic changes in response to global challenges. The current family typology, therefore, must be understood not only in terms of composition, but also of functionality, resilience, and adaptability. Understanding these transformations is essential to guide public policies in health, education, and social development, as well as to design prevention and psychological support strategies that consider the diversity of new family configurations.

In summary, the COVID-19 pandemic represented a turning point in the contemporary history of the family, leading to multiple reconfigurations in its structure, roles, and meanings. Although these modifications are global in scope, they manifest differently depending on sociocultural context, social class, and gender.

Analyzing family typology in the post-pandemic context allows for an understanding not only of structural changes in households, but also of the factors that determine their resilience and functionality in the face of external shocks. In this context, the study of Mexican families offers an opportunity to explore how the pandemic modified family composition, roles, and cohesion in different socioeconomic contexts. Evidence indicates that families not only faced an immediate impact on their economic conditions and mental health but also experienced a transformation in how they organize themselves and are perceived as a social unit.

This study is based on the premise that Mexican families experienced a profound reconfiguration in their structure and dynamics because of the COVID-19 pandemic, the effects of which are still reflected in indicators of employment, income, and social cohesion. This study provides empirical evidence on the evolution of Mexican families in the post-pandemic context, contributing to a better understanding of their emerging challenges and strengths. Understanding these changes is essential for informing public policies and health strategies that acknowledge the diversity and complexity of contemporary family structures. This approach allows for the examination, from a local perspective, of the impact of a global phenomenon on the family as the core unit of society. To determine family typology before and after the COVID-19 pandemic in Mexican families affiliated with a Family Medicine Unit in Tampico, Tamaulipas.[32] To compare the primary economic provider, house conditions (owned, rented or family-owned housing), evaluate variations in the family life cycle according to Huerta’s classification.[33] within the family before and after the COVID-19 pandemic. Describe and compare the distribution of family poverty levels before and after the COVID-19 pandemic.[34]

Subjects and Methods

An analytical, comparative, and exploratory study was conducted and approved by the Local Health Research Committee No. 2802 of the Mexican Institute of Social Security (IMSS), under Institutional Registration Number F-2024-2802-014. A non-probability convenience sampling method was used. Families affiliated with the IMSS at Family Medicine Unit (FMU) No. 38, residing in Tampico, Tamaulipas, who agreed to participate in the study and provided written informed consent, were included. Families were excluded if they did not provide informed consent, were not affiliated with Family Medicine Unit No. 38, were not residents of Tampico or had incomplete information. The study protocol received approval from the Local Health Research Committee on June 4, 2024.

Family typology was assessed at two time points: prior to the COVID-19 pandemic (2020) and four years after it (2025). Identification data and sociodemographic variables were collected, including age, marital status, education level, occupation, place of residence, family typology, and role within the family unit. Data were obtained through direct interviews with participants. Family typology was classified according to the criteria established by the 2005 Mexican Consensus of Family Medicine for the study of Family Health,[32] and the family life cycle classification proposed by Huerta.[33]

After data collection, the information was entered into an electronic database using Microsoft Excel for Windows to ensure proper organization and cleaning. Statistical analysis was performed using the IBM SPSS Statistics version 27. A 95% confidence level was established, and statistical significance was set at P < 0.05.

Descriptive analysis included a univariate analysis of the study variables. Quantitative variables were described using measures of central tendency. Normality was assessed using the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test. Variables with normal distribution were summarized using mean and standard deviation (SD). Qualitative variables were described using absolute frequencies and relative percentages and were presented in tables and graphs.

Subsequently, a bivariate analysis was performed to explore associations between family typology and sociodemographic and contextual variables in the post-pandemic period. For qualitative variables, the Chi-square test (χ2) was used; when expected frequencies were low, Fisher’s exact test was applied.

Results

The study included a total of 73 families affiliated with the IMSS, and registered at FMU No. 38 in Tampico. Data were collected over a 4-month period in 2025, to compare sociodemographic characteristics and family typology before and after the COVID-19 pandemic. The sample consisted of 53.4% women and 46.6% men, with this proportion remaining consistent across both study periods.

With respect to age, the study population showed an average increase of 4 years, increasing from 38.52 ± 12.77 years in the pre-pandemic period to 42.49 ± 12.78 years in the post-pandemic period. This change reflects the passage of time between the two measurement moments, with no statistically significant differences in age distribution [Table 1].

Regarding marital status, a decrease was observed in the proportion of single individuals (from 32.9% to 20.5%), as well as a slight decline among married individuals (from 57.5% to 54.8%). In contrast, an increase was observed in cohabiting unions (from 6.8% to 15.1%), among divorced individuals (from 2.7% to 6.8%), and among widowed individuals (from 0% to 2.7%). Although these variations did not reach statistical significance (P > 0.05), they suggest a trend toward diversification of marital structures and dissolution of formal unions [Table 1].

With respect to education attainment, the distribution of educational levels remained stable across both periods, with no statistically significant differences [Table 1].

Employment status was one of the most relevant findings concerns, with a significant decrease in formal employment (from 78.1% to 65.8%) accompanied by an increase in unemployment (from 1.4% to 13.7%).

This change was statistically significant (χ2 = 16.172; P = 0.003), highlighting the impact of the pandemic on family employment stability. Additionally, a slight increase was observed in the proportion of pensioners or retirees (from 2.7% to 4.1%), while household work remained relatively stable (from 17.8% to 16.5%) [Table 1].

In the pre-pandemic period, economic income was reported as self-generated in 32.9% of participants, pension-based in 1.4%, and family support in 19.2%. In post-pandemic period, more than half of the population reported their self-generated income (68.5%), while 2.7% reported pension income and 28.8% received family support. This change was statistically significant (χ2 = 10.851; P = 0.004) [Table 1]

The analysis of the family structure revealed notable changes. The proportion of nuclear families decreased substantially in the post-pandemic period. In contrast, single-parent families have increased. Although not all these variations were statistically significant, it reflects a trend toward the fragmentation of traditional family units [Table 2].

Regarding the degree of family integration, a significant decrease was observed in integrated nucleus (from 86.3% to 63.6%) and an increase in not-integrated nucleus (from 8.2% to 19.7% P = 0.029). This finding indicates deterioration in family cohesion, possibly associated with economic pressures, employment changes, and the reconfiguration of family roles following the pandemic. Similarly, an increase in extended families was observed, suggesting the integration of older family members into the household as a response to economic and caregiver challenges [Table 2].

The analysis of the primary economic providers revealed a substantial modification in household breadwinner roles following the COVID-19 pandemic. Where in the pre-pandemic period the economic contribution predominantly relied on traditional male family members, in the post-pandemic scenario a redistribution of the provider role toward other household members was observed, particularly reflecting increased participation of the spouse and, other family members. Although the overall change did not reach statistical significance, this variation suggests an internal reorganization of household income support, possibly driven by employment disruptions, and the need to diversify income sources [Table 3].

Discussion

The findings of this study demonstrate that the COVID-19 pandemic represented a turning point in the structure and dynamics of Mexican families, consistent with evidence reported by previous authors regarding the reconfiguration of the households in response to the health, economic, and social crisis. The decrease in nuclear and integrated families, together with the increase in non-integrated and single-parent, aligns with trends observed in national and international studies documenting the diversification of family typologies following pandemic-related confinement measures. This transformation suggests the adaptive capacity of households in the face of uncertainty, employment loss, and the modification of traditional family roles.[147]

One of the most relevant findings concerns the association between the loss of job stability and the deterioration of family cohesion. The decrease in formal employment and the increase in unemployment observed in this study are consistent with reports that identify the economic crisis as a key factor in household fragmentation and reorganization, affecting family functioning and integration.[1421] The literature has consistently shown that sustained financial stress increases the risk of dysfunction, intra-family conflict, and role redefinition, particularly in contexts of social vulnerability.[622]

In contrast, the increase in extended families identified in the post-pandemic period suggests the use of adaptive strategies aimed at economic survival and intergenerational care. This phenomenon has been widely described in Latin America as a response to the crisis situations, in which households integrate older adults or other family members to share resources and mitigate the impact of unemployment and caregiving burden.[2423] These findings reinforce the notion of the family as a dynamic system, capable of reorganizing in response to adverse events, as proposed by family resilience models.[1011]

From a functional perspective, the increase in non-integrated nucleus observed in this study is consistent with research indicating that, although some families strengthened their ties during pandemic-related confinement, others experienced a deterioration in communication, cohesion, and emotional support, particularly in the presence of pre-existing tensions or limited resources.[161718] This underscores that post-pandemic family typology should not be analyzed solely in terms of structural composition, but also in relation to families capacity for functioning and adaptation.

The COVID-19 pandemic prompted a reorganization of economic roles within Mexican families. The shift from the traditional provider role toward greater participation by the spouse or other household members is consistent with studies describing a forced redistribution of economic responsibilities resulting from job loss, labor informality, and prolonged financial instability.[1421] This phenomenon has been described as an adaptive family strategy in response to crisis contexts, rather than as a definitive structural change. Similarly, the relative stability of housing conditions aligns with findings from national literature, in which the household serves as a source of resilience in the face of the economic crisis.[14]

The modifications observed in the family life cycle, characterized by a shift toward more advanced stages, are consistent with the evidence suggesting that the pandemic accelerated family transitions associated with aging, the dispersion of the nucleus, and processes of loss or household reconfiguration.[327] Similarly, the homogenization of the economic impact across sexes and the increase in post-pandemic family poverty align with reports describing a deepening of economic vulnerability in a proportion of households that were unable to recover pre-crisis levels.[629]

This study provides local evidence consistent with national and international trends regarding the transformation of post-pandemic family typology, highlighting the value of family medicine as a key setting for identifying these changes and design preventive interventions.[272930]

Conclusion

The COVID-19 pandemic resulted in significant changes in the typology and dynamics of Mexican families, highlighting a transition from traditional models toward more diverse and less integrated structures. The decline of nuclear family and the integrated nucleus, together with the increase in single parent, extended and non-integrated households, reflects the impact of the economic, labor, and social transformations derived from the health crisis. These findings confirm the adaptive capacity of families in context of vulnerability, while also revealing the fragility of economic support and family cohesion in the face of prolonged crises.

The identification of new family dynamics underscores the need to strengthen economic, employment and caregiving support policies, as well as to implement family medicine-based intervention programs that promote resilience and sustained support across different life stages. These findings reinforce the notion of the family as a dynamic and adaptable system, yet one that is vulnerable to deterioration in cohesion and well-being during prolonged crises, highlighting the importance of family medicine-based interventions and public policies responsive to the post-pandemic context.

This study has several limitations to be considered; the sample focuses exclusively on Mexican families. Additionally, the research is based on data collected during a specific phase of the pandemic, and it does not address long-term effects that may emerge in the coming years.

Future research should explore the evolution of family dynamics in the post-pandemic period using a longitudinal approach to capture deeper and sustained changes over time. The continuity of this line of research is both warranted and necessary to better understand the evolution of Mexican families in the medium and long term, and to generate evidence that informs the design of public policies responsive to post-pandemic family realities.
