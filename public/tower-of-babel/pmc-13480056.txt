Piceatannol attenuates thoracic aortic aneurysm progression by targeting STK33
Authors: Yubo Liu, Jie Liu, Ruizheng Shi, Liao Tan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThoracic aortic aneurysm (TAA) is a life-threatening vascular condition with limitedpharmacological interventions. This study aimed to identify novel causaldruggable genes and therapeutic candidates for TAA.MethodsWe performed Mendelian randomization (MR) by integrating cis-expression quantitativetrait loci (eQTL) and genome-wide association studies (GWAS) data to identifycausal genes for TAA. Therapeutic candidates were predicted via the DrugSignatures Database (DSigDB) and prioritized using molecular docking. Theclinical relevance of the lead target, STK33, was assessed in human TAA tissuesusing immunohistochemistry (IHC). The therapeutic efficacy of piceatannol wasrigorously validated across three distinct platforms: Angiotensin II(AngII)-stimulated vascular smooth muscle cells (VSMCs), and two complementaryanimal models (BAPN-induced and AngII-infused TAA mice).ResultsMR analysis identified 31 druggable genes significantly associated with TAA risk.Integration with DSigDB and molecular docking pinpointed piceatannol as a potentcandidate targeting STK33. IHC staining revealed that STK33 expression wasmarkedly downregulated in human TAA tissues, a trend consistently observed inAngII-stimulated VSMCs and mouse TAA models. In vivo, piceatannol treatmentsignificantly reduced TAA incidence, mortality, and aortic dilation in bothBAPN-induced and AngII-induced mouse models. Mechanistically, piceatannoleffectively restored STK33 protein levels and attenuated the loss of VSMCcontractile markers (MYH11, α-SMA, and CNN1) both in vivo and invitro. These protective effects were largely abolished by STK33 knockdown,confirming a target-specific mechanism.ConclusionsPiceatannol attenuates TAA progression, potentially through targeting and stabilizing STK33 expression. By utilizing a multi-model validation strategy (two in vivo modelsand an in vitro cell model), our findings highlight the piceatannol-STK33 axisas a promising therapeutic candidate for TAA.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13062-026-00858-5.

Introduction

Thoracic aortic aneurysm (TAA) is a life-threatening vascular disorder characterized by the progressive pathological dilation of the thoracic aorta [1]. This condition primarily results from a progressive degeneration of the aortic wall structure. The prevalence of TAA is lower than that of abdominal aortic aneurysm. Nevertheless, TAA is associated with more complications, including more likely to result in aortic rupture, which are life-threatening. Consequently, TAA represents a critical focus in clinical practice [2–4]. Established risk factors for TAA encompass several heritable disorders. Notable examples include Marfan syndrome, Ehlers-Danlos syndrome, and bicuspid aortic valve. Significant acquired risk factors consist of systemic hypertension, tobacco use, and advanced age [4]. Current pharmacological management of thoracic aortic aneurysm (TAA) primarily relies on hemodynamic interventions, including beta-blockers and angiotensin receptor blockers such as losartan. While these agents effectively reduce blood pressure and wall stress, large-scale clinical trials have shown that they have limited ability to halt progressive aortic dilation or prevent acute dissection. These limitations highlight a critical unmet need for disease-modifying therapies that target the underlying cellular mechanisms, such as vascular smooth muscle cell (VSMC) phenotypic switching [5]. Despite current therapeutic capabilities, arresting or reversing the progression of TAA remains a substantial clinical challenge. Thus, the investigation of novel pharmacological agents and therapeutic targets for preventing or delaying TAA progression is of paramount importance.

Human genome-wide association studies (GWAS) provide numerous potential therapeutic targets. These targets include genes that may act as disease mediators or drug targets [6, 7]. However, observational studies often face challenges in distinguishing between genes that actually cause disease and genes that are merely associated with TAA. Mendelian randomization (MR) offers an effective analytical framework to address this issue. MR is an approach used to assess the causality between a modifiable exposure or risk factor and a clinically relevant outcome [8]. MR analysis has been widely used to repurpose licensed drugs and discover novel therapeutic targets by integrating summary data from disease GWASs and expression quantitative trait locus (eQTL) studies [9–11]. The expression levels of a gene may be considered as a kind of lifelong exposure, and eQTL located in the genomic region of the druggable genes are always considered as proxies [12, 13]. In this study, we utilized cis-eQTL data from the eQTLgen Consortium and applied two-sample MR analysis to investigate causal relationships between druggable targets and TAA.

Although MR studies can create a strong basis for precision medicine in TAA, a major challenge remains. Researchers must still confirm that these targets are valid and that treatments targeting them will work. To address this, we used molecular docking simulations to evaluate how well proteins can be targeted by drugs and screen for potential drug-like molecules for further lab validation. We used transcriptomic data from a mouse model of TAA and from human aortic tissue. Additionally, clinical samples, vitro and in vivo TAA models were used to validate the candidate drug and its potential targets. In this study, we combine genetic epidemiology, cellular transcriptomics, and functional validation, to identify and prioritize promising drugs and targets of TAA.

Methods

Study design

Figure 1 outlines the study workflow designed to identify key genes associated with TAA risk, along with potential therapeutic targets and drugs. The process began with the collection of 4,302 druggable gene targets from existing literature [14]. We then obtained cis-eQTL data for these genes from the eQTLgen Consortium and performed a two-sample Mendelian randomization (MR) analysis to evaluate the relationship between drug target expression and TAA risk. Enrichment analysis was conducted to interpret the biological functions and pathways involved. Following the MR analysis, we carried out protein-protein interaction analysis on the identified targets and used the DSigDB database to predict potential drugs, with molecular docking simulations applied to confirm drug-target interactions. The most promising drugs were subsequently validated in a BAPN-induced TAA mouse model, while the expression levels of candidate targets were examined across multiple datasets, including sequencing data from an AngII-induced TAA mouse model (GSE290902), human TAA aortic tissue (GSE235161), AngII-stimulated vascular smooth muscle cells (VSMCs) in vitro, the BAPN-induced and AngII-induced mouse TAA model. Mendelian randomization analysis

Cis-eQtls data pertaining to druggable genes

The drug targets analyzed in this study were obtained from a prior investigation that identified 4,463 genes encoding druggable targets. From this set, we focused on 4,302 genes located on autosomes. These genes included 2,281 belonging to established drug-target families, 646 associated with proteins targeted by approved drugs or compounds, and 1,375 encoding proteins currently under clinical development [14]. Since cis-eQTLs located near their target genes are particularly valuable for drug development research, we extracted statistically significant cis-eQTLs (false discovery rate < 0.05) from the eQTLGen Consortium. This dataset represented a meta-analysis of 19,942 transcripts from 31,684 individuals. We then applied linkage disequilibrium clumping using the 1000 Genomes European reference panel, setting an LD r2 threshold of <0.1 and a clumping distance of 100 kb. A significance threshold of p < 5 × 10− 8 was used for selecting genetic instruments. Following this quality control process, 2,521 druggable genes with available cis-eQTLs were retained for subsequent analysis.

Data source of thoracic aortic aneurysm

FinnGen is a large-scale research project in genomics and personalized medicine. It collects and analyzes genomic and health data from 500,000 Finnish biobank participants. The project aims to improve our understanding of the genetic basis of diseases [15]. For this study, we used GWAS summary statistics from the FinnGen R12 release. This dataset includes 5,108 TAA cases and 463,106 population controls. The data are publicly available at: https://r12.finngen.fi/pheno/I9_THAORTANEUR. Because this GWAS data is publicly available and was already approved by the appropriate ethics committee, no additional ethical approval was required for our study.

Mendelian randomization

First, we assessed the statistical power of each eQTL by calculating F-statistics. We selected instruments with F-statistics greater than 10, which are considered strong instruments [16]. Next, we applied Steiger filtering to remove genetic variants that showed a stronger association with the outcome than with the exposure. This step helped reduce potential reverse causality. All statistical analyses were performed using R software and the TwoSampleMR package. For exposures that contained only one single nucleotide polymorphism (SNP), we used the Wald ratio method to estimate causal effects [17]. For exposures with multiple SNPs, we applied five different methods: inverse-variance weighted, MR-Egger, simple mode, weighted median, and weighted mode. To evaluate heterogeneity across genetic variants, we conducted the Cochran Q test [18]. We used both MR-PRESSO and MR-Egger regression tests to examine potential horizontal pleiotropy [19, 20]. All results are presented as odds ratios (ORs) with corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs). We applied the Bonferroni correction to account for multiple testing, setting the significance threshold at p < 1.98 × 10− 5.

Bayesian colocalization analysis

We conducted Bayesian colocalization to assess whether gene effects on TAA were driven by a shared causal variant or by nearby correlated SNPs. Prior probabilities were set as: 1 × 10− 4 for a SNP affecting only the protein, 1 × 10− 4 for affecting only TAA, and 1 × 10− 5 for affecting both. The analysis evaluates five scenarios: H0, no association; H1, association with protein only; H2, association with TAA only; H3, association with both but distinct causal variants; H4, association with both via a shared causal variant. Strong colocalization was defined as a posterior probability for H4 (PPH4) ≥0.8.

Enrichment analysis

Functional gene annotation was performed through GO (Gene Ontology) and KEGG (Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes) enrichment analyses. These analyses were conducted using the ClusterProfiler package in R. The GO analysis was organized into three categories: molecular function (MF), cellular component (CC), and biological process (BP). The enrichment results from both GO and KEGG were visualized using the ggplot2 package.

Protein-protein interaction analysis

In this study, the protein-protein interaction (PPI) network was constructed using STRING 12.0. The minimum required interaction score was set at 0.15, while all other parameters maintained their default configurations.

Potential therapeutic drug prediction

Potential drugs were identified using the Drug Signatures Database (DSigDB), accessible at http://tanlab.ucdenver.edu/DSigDB. The screening was performed through the Enrichr platform (http://maayanlab.cloud/Enrichr) to predict therapeutic options for TAA [21].

Molecular docking

The two-dimensional (2D) structures of small molecule ligands were retrieved from the PubChem database (http://pubchem.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/). These 2D structures were then imported into ChemOffice 20.0 to generate their three-dimensional (3D) configurations, which were saved in mol2 format. Subsequently, crystal structures of protein targets with high resolution were selected from the RCSB PDB database (http://www.rcsb.org/) to serve as molecular receptors. Using PyMOL 2.6.0, water molecules and phosphate groups were removed from the protein structures, and the resulting models were saved as PDB files. Energy minimization of the compounds and preprocessing of the target proteins, including identification of active pockets, were performed using Molecular Operating Environment (MOE) 2019. Finally, molecular docking was carried out with MOE 2019, with the number of runs set to 50. The binding affinity between the ligands and receptors was evaluated based on binding energy, and the results were visualized using PyMOL 2.6.0 and Discovery Studio 2019.

Molecular dynamics (MD) simulations

Molecular dynamics simulations were performed using GROMACS 2020.3. The simulated system was the piceatannol-STK33 complex. The simulation box size was optimized to ensure a distance greater than 1.0 nm between the protein and the box boundary. Energy minimization was then carried out to reduce atomic contacts or overlaps. The system was equilibrated using both NVT and NPT ensembles. The total simulation time was 100 nanoseconds. Temperature was controlled using the V-rescale method. Pressure was controlled using the Parrinello-Rahman method. Van der Waals interactions were calculated with the Lennard-Jones potential. A cutoff distance of 1.4 nm was used for non-bonded interactions. The LINCS algorithm was applied to constrain all bond lengths. Electrostatic interactions were computed using the Particle Mesh Ewald method. The Fourier spacing was set to 0.16 nm.

Analysis of sequencing data from GEO datasets

Two gene expression microarray datasets were analyzed in this study: GSE290902 (AngII-induced TAA mouse vs control mouse) and GSE235161 (human TAA vs healthy control). Both datasets underwent standardized preprocessing and normalization procedures using R software, with appropriate corrections applied to each dataset. Differential expression analysis was subsequently performed on three potential target genes: STK33, GLS, and ARSA.

Experimental design of β-aminopropionitrile-induced and AngII-induced mouse TAA

In this study, distinct pharmacological models were selected to maximize translational relevance based on their respective pathological mechanisms. For in vitro experiments, AngII was utilized as the primary stimulus because it robustly induces VSMC dysfunction, including phenotypic switching and oxidative stress, effectively replicating the cellular microenvironment of aneurysms in standard 2D cultures [22]. Conversely, β-Aminopropionitrile (BAPN), which promotes aneurysm formation primarily by disrupting extracellular matrix (ECM) cross-linking, is less applicable to isolated cell cultures [23, 24]. For in vivo modeling, the BAPN-induced mouse model was prioritized as the principal platform due to its high specificity and consistency in reproducing classical TAA histopathology, characterized by severe medial degeneration and elastin fragmentation. To ensure a direct translational link to our in vitro findings and comprehensively validate the therapeutic efficacy, an AngII-infused ApoE-/- mouse model was additionally employed as a complementary in vivo platform, despite its known propensity to concurrently induce abdominal aortic aneurysms.

The animal study protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee. This work has been reported in line with the ARRIVE criteria [25]. C57BL/6J mice were purchased from Hunan SJA Laboratory Animal Co.，Ltd. The BAPN (Sigma; A3134) -induced TAA model was established according to previous reports [26]. Briefly, 3-week-old male mice were fed 0.5% BAPN in drinking water. The drinking water was refreshed every 2 days. A 28-day BAPN treatment model was used to compare the incidence rate of TAA and related mortality. TAA was defined as thoracic aortic dissection (aortic false lumen formation) and/or thoracic aortic aneurysm (at least 1.5 times extension of aortic diameter). A 21-day BAPN treatment model was used for further pathological analyses. In our study, mice received piceatannol at 20 mg/kg/day, a dose validated in previous preclinical models of cardiovascular and fibrotic diseases for efficacy and safety. All mice were split into 4 groups [1]: Control groups: fed normal drinking water and received physiological saline intraperitoneally (i.p.) on a daily basis in an equivalent volume relative to the body weight [2]. Piceatannol groups: fed normal drinking water and received piceatannol (20 mg/kg; MCE; HY-13518) intraperitoneally (i.p.) on a daily [3]. BAPN groups: fed 0.5% BAPN in drinking water and received physiological saline intraperitoneally (i.p.) on a daily basis in an equivalent volume relative to the body weight [4]. BAPN+Piceatannol groups: fed 0.5% BAPN in drinking water and received piceatannol (20 mg/kg) intraperitoneally (i.p.) on a daily. The dosage of piceatannol followed previous studies [27, 28].

An AngII-induced TAA model was established using male apolipoprotein E deficient (ApoE-/-) mice implanted with osmotic minipumps (Alzet, Model 2004, California, USA). This system allowed continuous infusion of recombinant human angiotensin II (AngII; Sigma-Aldrich, Missouri, USA) at a rate of 1,000 ng/min/kg for 4 weeks, effectively inducing aneurysm formation. The mice were randomly assigned to two groups: the phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) control group and the AngII treatment group. Animals in the PBS group received equivalent volumes of normal saline, whereas those in the AngII group were administered AngII via the subcutaneous minipump. All male ApoE-/- mice were randomly assigned to four groups [1]: Control group: mice were implanted with subcutaneous osmotic minipumps delivering physiological saline at an equivalent volume relative to body weight [2]. AngII group: mice were infused with AngII (1,000 ng/min/kg) via subcutaneous osmotic minipumps for 4 weeks [3]. Piceatannol group: mice were infused with AngII as above, and simultaneously treated with piceatannol (20 mg/kg, intraperitoneally) on a daily basis [4]. AngII + Piceatannol group: mice were infused with AngII as above, and treated with piceatannol (20 mg/kg, intraperitoneally) daily.

All animals were anesthetized with 1.25% avertin before being sacrificed. For arterial morphology studies, the mice were successively perfused with 10% KCl, 10 × PBS, and 4% PFA. Then, the whole aorta was isolated, and ascending aorta (or vascular segment with the most obvious lesion) was used to prepare cryosections.

Immunohistochemical staining

Immunohistochemical staining for STK33 was performed using a standard protocol. The staining was carried out using a commercial kit (ZSGB-Bio, PV-9000) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Tissue sections were first treated with 3% hydrogen peroxide to block endogenous peroxidase activity. Subsequently, the sections were blocked with 5% normal serum to prevent non-specific binding. The sections were then incubated with a primary antibody against STK33 (Proteintech; 12,857–1-AP and abcam: ab206296) at a dilution of 1:200. This incubation was conducted at 37 °C for 1.5 hours. Following primary antibody incubation, the sections were treated with a secondary antibody for 1 hour. Detection was achieved using a DAB substrate kit (ZSGB-Bio, ZLI-9018). Finally, the sections were counterstained with hematoxylin (ZSGB-Bio, ZLI-9610) to visualize cell nuclei.

Elastic Van Gieson staining and degradation score evaluation

Paraffin sections of the aorta were analyzed by elastic Van Gieson staining (Pinuofei, Hubei, China) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Elastin degradation was graded as described in previous studies [29]. Specifically, level 1 means < 25% degradation; level 2 means 25% to 50% degradation; level 3 means 50% to 75% degradation; and level 4 means > 75% degradation.

Western blotting

Aortic tissues were lysed in RIPA buffer containing PMSF. Protein concentrations were determined using a bicinchoninic acid (BCA) assay. Equal amounts of protein were separated by SDS-PAGE and transferred onto PVDF membranes. Membranes were blocked with 5% non-fat milk for 1 h and incubated with primary antibodies (STK33: ab206296, abcam) overnight at 4 °C following the manufacturers’ instructions. After washing with TBST, membranes were incubated with horseradish peroxidase-conjugated secondary antibodies [1:5000) for 1 h at room temperature. Protein bands were detected and quantified using Image Lab software (Bio-Rad).

Real-time quantitative polymerase chain reaction (RT‒qPCR)

Total RNA was isolated using TRIzol reagent (Vazyme Biotech Co.). Subsequently, 1 μg of total RNA from each sample was reverse-transcribed into complementary DNA (cDNA) with a commercial reverse transcription kit (Vazyme), following the manufacturer’s protocol. Quantitative real-time PCR (qPCR) was performed using the 2× SYBR Green PCR master mix (Vazyme). The amplification procedure consisted of 40 cycles, and each sample was analyzed in three technical replicates. Gene expression levels were calculated by the 2(-ΔΔCT) method, with β-actin serving as the endogenous reference gene for normalization. All primer sequences used in this study are provided in Supplementary Table 1.

Rat primary aortic smooth muscle cell isolation

VSMCs were isolated from thoracic aortas of Sprague-Dawley rats (160–180 g) using an explant culture method. Briefly, freshly harvested aortas were washed twice with ice-cold phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) and digested in 1 mL of 0.2% collagenase I solution prepared in Ham’s F-12 medium at 37 °C for 30 minutes. Following enzymatic treatment, the adventitial layer was carefully removed using fine forceps. The aortas were then opened longitudinally, and endothelial cells were mechanically removed by gentle scraping. The prepared aortic tissues were sectioned into small fragments and placed in culture dishes. These explants were maintained in DMEM/F-12 medium supplemented with 20% fetal bovine serum (FBS) at 37 °C in a humidified incubator with 5% CO₂. VSMCs that migrated from the tissue explants were collected and expanded in culture. Cells at passages 3–6 were utilized for subsequent experiments. VSMC purity was verified through positive immunofluorescence staining for alpha-smooth muscle actin (α-SMA). siRNA sequences of STK33 used in this study are provided in Supplementary Table 2.

Statistical analysis

All data are presented as mean ± SEM. Statistical analyses were conducted using GraphPad Prism 9.0 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA) and SPSS 27.0 (IBM, Armonk, NY, USA). The normality of data distribution was assessed prior to statistical comparisons. For comparisons between two groups, Student’s t-test was applied to normally distributed data, while the Mann-Whitney test was used for non-normally distributed data. Correlation analyses were performed using Pearson’s test for quantitative variables and Spearman’s test for categorical variables. Fisher’s exact test was utilized for contingency data analysis. For multiple group comparisons, the Kruskal-Wallis test or two-way ANOVA with appropriate post-hoc tests were employed as required. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant for all analyses. Detailed descriptions of the specific statistical methods used for each experiment are provided in the corresponding figure legends.

3.Results

MR analysis identified 31 druggable genes significantly associated with the risk of thoracic aortic aneurysm

Based on the cis-eQTL data from the eQTLGen Consortium, we identified 2,521 druggable genes after clumping. This study utilized GWAS data from the FinnGen database, including 5,108 cases and 463,106 controls of European ancestry. We performed a two-sample MR analysis to evaluate the causal relationships. After applying Bonferroni correction (p < 1.98 × 10− 5), horizontal pleiotropy testing, and heterogeneity testing, we identified 31 druggable genes that showed significant associations with TAA risk. Piceatannol attenuates TAA development and preserves VSMC contractile phenotype in vivo

To prioritize candidates for in vivo validation, we evaluated their pharmacological safety and translational potential. Although beta-Solamarine exhibits strong binding affinity with the target druggable gene, its significant cytotoxicity prompted its exclusion [28, 29]. Similarly, 3,5-dinitrocatechol and periodate-oxidized adenosine (AdOx) were excluded due to high risks of hepatotoxicity and non-specific systemic toxicity, respectively [31–33]. Consequently, this study focused on validating the therapeutic effect of the most significant candidate drug, piceatannol, against TAA. As a natural polyphenol with established biosafety and cardioprotective properties, it emerged as the most promising translatable candidate.

We first established a 28-day BAPN-induded TAA model to evaluate whether piceatannol could attenuate BAPN-induced TAA formation and TAA rupture-related mortality. Comparing the control and BAPN-induced groups during the 28 days of follow-up, occurrence and mortality was significantly higher in the BAPN-treated groups. In BAPN group, deaths occurred after 2 weeks of BAPN intake. 18 of 21 mice finally developed TAA and 14 of 21 mice finally dead. Obviously, piceatannol treated mice exhibited markedly reduced TAA incidence (from 85.71% to 47.83%, p < 0.05) and mortality (from 66.67% to 20.43%, p < 0.05) (Fig. 5A–D). In addition, the maximum diameters of different aortic segments, including the ascending aorta (Asc) (BAPN vs. BAPN+Piceatannol = 1.661 ± 0.355 mm vs. 1.252 ± 0.287 mm, p < 0.05), and aortic arch (Arch) (BAPN vs. BAPN+Piceatannol = 1.579 ± 0.443 mm vs.1.019 ± 0.223 mm, p < 0.05)were significantly reduced in piceatannol treated mice compared with BAPN mice. Descending aorta (Des) (BAPN vs. BAPN+Piceatannol = 1.350± 0.170 mm vs. 1.122 ± 0.254 mm, p > 0.05) were also reduced in piceatannol treated mice but without significance (Fig. 5E). To explore the underlying molecular mechanism, we examined the expression of contractile phenotypic markers in aortic tissues via Western blot. BAPN induction led to a dramatic loss of key contractile proteins, including MYH11, α-SMA, and CNN1, indicating a pathological phenotypic switch of VSMCs. Piceatannol treatment effectively attenuated this process, significantly restoring the protein levels of MYH11, α-SMA, and CNN1 (Fig. 5F–I). Next, elastic layer staining and evaluations were performed. As showed in Fig. 6A, a total four groups of mice were included. EVG staining of the ascending aorta showed that BAPN-injured arteries exhibited serious elastin degradation compared with those in the control group of WT mice, as expected (Control vs. BAPN = 1.00 ± 0 vs. 2.500 ± 1.069, p < 0.05). Accordingly, piceatannol significantly reduced elastin degradation compared with that in the BAPN group in response to BAPN stimulation (BAPN vs. BAPN+Piceatannol = 2.500 ± 1.069 vs. 1.375 ± 0.518, p < 0.05) (Fig. 6A–B). Overall, piceatannol therapy reduced BAPN-induced TAA formation and related rupture in mice. These therapeutic outcomes were further replicated in the AngII-induced TAA model in ApoE-/- mice (Supplemental Figs. 1–3). MD simulation results

Based on the favorable docking of the compound piceatannol with the protein target STK33, and the significant downregulation of STK33 in TAA tissues and AngII-stimulated VSMCs, we hypothesized that STK33 might be a key target through which piceatannol inhibits the development of TAA. Therefore, we conducted a 100 ns molecular dynamics simulation of the piceatannol-STK33 complex to further evaluate the compound-target interactions.

The root mean square deviation (RMSD) was used to assess the stability of the ligand-receptor binding system. As shown in Fig. 9A, the piceatannol-STK33 system reached equilibrium after approximately 20 ns. The average RMSD of the complex was 10.60 Å, suggesting a relatively stable system throughout the simulation. The root mean square fluctuation (RMSF) reflects the flexibility of each protein region by calculating the fluctuation of each atom relative to its average position. The RMSF values for the entire protein over 100 ns are presented in Fig. 9B. The residue-specific fluctuations in the piceatannol-STK33 complex ranged from 1.17 Å to 38.36 Å, with an average of 15.52 Å. Binding of the compound induced slight changes in the RMSF profile, indicating that the presence of piceatannol may have caused subtle conformational adjustments in the STK33 protein structure. The radius of gyration (Rg) was used to evaluate the compactness of the overall structure, representing the distance between the mass center of all atoms and their terminals over a specific time interval. As shown in Fig. 9C, the Rg values of STK33 during the simulation fluctuated between 20.65 Å and 28.11 Å, with an average of 22.67 Å. Overall, the complex formed by piceatannol and STK33 remained relatively stable throughout the MD simulation. Figure 9D shows the total number of hydrogen bonds present in the complex during the 100 ns simulation. The number of hydrogen bonds between piceatannol and STK33 fluctuated roughly between 0 and 5, with an average of 2.07, which is consistent with the molecular docking results. Discussion

This study established a comprehensive research framework integrating multi-omics analysis and experimental validation to explore therapeutic strategies for TAA. Through Mendelian randomization analysis, we identified 31 druggable genes associated with TAA risk. Molecular docking and dynamics simulations further confirmed the stable binding between piceatannol and STK33, providing a structural basis for their interaction. In vivo experiments demonstrated that piceatannol markedly reduced the incidence and severity of TAA in a BAPN-induced mouse model, accompanied by improved vascular remodeling and inhibited elastin degradation. Mechanistically, piceatannol restored STK33 protein expression and counteracted the phenotypic transition of VSMCs, whereas STK33 knockdown abolished these protective effects. Importantly, the efficacy of piceatannol was achieved without affecting STK33 transcription, suggesting a unique post-translational regulatory mechanism. A notable strength of the current study is the integration of multiple, complementary experimental platforms to rigorously validate the therapeutic potential of STK33 inhibition. The BAPN-induced model was strategically chosen as the primary in vivo model because it provides a highly specific and clinically relevant representation of TAA, mirroring the profound structural weakening and ECM disruption seen in human patients. However, to bridge the mechanistic findings from our AngII-stimulated in vitro VSMC assays with complex in vivo pathophysiology, we also evaluated piceatannol in AngII-infused ApoE-/- mice. Importantly, piceatannol exhibited consistent and significant protective effects against TAA progression across all three distinct models: AngII-stimulated VSMCs in vitro, BAPN-induced TAA in vivo, and AngII-infused TAA in vivo. This consistent efficacy, observed across models with distinct initial pathological triggers (ECM disruption versus neurohumoral stress), strongly reinforces the robustness of STK33 as a reliable therapeutic target and highlights the broad translational value of piceatannol in treating TAA.

Piceatannol, a natural hydroxylated analog of resveratrol, has emerged as a promising therapeutic candidate for cardiovascular diseases due to its multi-target protective effects [34, 35]. Moreover, piceatannol exhibits favorable metabolic stability compared with resveratrol due to its catechol structure. Importantly, piceatannol demonstrates low toxicity and good tolerability in human cells [36]. Piceatannol exerts its antioxidant effects by upregulating endogenous antioxidant defenses, including superoxide dismutase and glutathione, while simultaneously reducing oxidative stress markers such as malondialdehyde and peroxynitrite [37]. In cardiomyocytes, piceatannol effectively inhibits hypoxia-induced oxidative stress at nano- or submicromolar concentrations, demonstrating higher bioactivity compared to resveratrol in this regard [38]. These antioxidant properties are particularly relevant for preventing cardiovascular complications associated with oxidative damage, including atherosclerosis and myocardial infarction. Regarding endothelial protection, piceatannol significantly enhances endothelial function through multiple pathways [39]. It stimulates dose- and time-dependent nitric oxide production via endothelial nitric oxide synthase phosphorylation, primarily through activation of the PI3K/Akt signaling pathway [40]. The compound also demonstrates superior efficacy in restoring dimethylarginine dimethylaminohydrolase expression and activity under high-glucose oxidative stress conditions, functioning at 10-fold lower concentrations than resveratrol [41]. Regarding VSMCs protection, piceatannol inhibits TNF-α-stimulated VSMC proliferation, which is associated with inhibition of MAP kinase activity, arrest of the G1-phase cell cycle and reduction of cyclin D1/CDK4 and cyclin E/CDK2 activities through p21WAF1 expression [42]. This study provides the first evidence that piceatannol attenuates the progression of TAA and inhibits AngII-induced phenotypic transformation of VSMCs.

STK33 is a serine/threonine kinase regulating vital cellular processes, but its cardiovascular role was previously unreported. This study first identifies STK33 as a protective target in TAA, though specific mechanisms require further validation. Since piceatannol restores STK33 without altering its transcription, it likely enhances STK33 stability post-translationally. This may involve direct thermodynamic binding to mask ubiquitination sites or indirect stabilization via SIRT1 activation [43]. Downstream, STK33 may preserve the VSMC contractile phenotype and elastin integrity through three potential pathways: (i) negatively regulating c-Myc, a core driver of phenotypic switching [44, 45]; (ii) inhibiting the hyperactive ERK-MMP axis to prevent elastin degradation [46]; or (iii) directly phosphorylating vimentin to stabilize the cellular cytoskeleton [47]. Future studies utilizing cycloheximide chase assays and comprehensive phosphoproteomics are required to fully elucidate this STK33-mediated network in TAA. Although our study demonstrates that piceatannol effectively restores STK33 protein levels and prevents vascular smooth muscle cell (VSMC) phenotypic switching, the precise molecular mechanisms governing this axis warrant further investigation. Given that piceatannol likely impacts STK33 post-translationally rather than transcriptionally, we hypothesize that the drug enhances STK33 protein stability. This stabilization may occur directly through thermodynamic binding to the STK33 kinase domain, potentially masking ubiquitination sites to prevent proteasomal degradation, or indirectly via the activation of SIRT1, a known target of piceatannol that mediates protein stabilization through deacetylation [43]. Downstream, while specific STK33 substrates in VSMCs remain to be mapped, existing evidence suggests STK33 may maintain the VSMC contractile phenotype by regulating key transcription factors and survival networks, such as c-Myc or the MAPK/ERK pathway [43, 46], both of which are critical drivers in AngII-induced pathogenic phenotypic transformation [45].

While this study provides evidence for the therapeutic potential of piceatannol in TAA, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, due to the inherent limitations of the MR approach, potential residual confounding cannot be completely ruled out, meaning that a definitive causal relationship between STK33 and TAA risk cannot be established. Second, while restricting our Mendelian randomization analysis to cis-eQTLs minimized horizontal pleiotropy, it inherently excluded potential trans-eQTL or splice-QTL signals, highlighting the need for future studies to incorporate these variants to fully elucidate STK33 regulation in TAA. Third, a notable limitation of this study involves the precise mechanistic dependence of piceatannol on STK33. As a polyphenolic compound, piceatannol may exhibit multi-target effects. Based on the current evidence, we cannot definitively conclude that STK33 is the primary or exclusive target mediating its protection against TAA, and STK33-independent mechanisms cannot be ruled out. Finally, the present study initiated piceatannol administration concurrently with TAA induction, which primarily demonstrates its preventive efficacy against elastic lamina degradation and early remodeling. Due to the rapid and lethal progression of the 0.5% BAPN model and the relatively low baseline rupture rate of the Ang II-induced ApoE-/- model, evaluating the therapeutic effects of piceatannol on already established aneurysms remains technically challenging. Future studies utilizing stable progressive models, such as the elastase-induction or Marfan syndrome models, are warranted to investigate whether piceatannol can arrest progression and prevent rupture post-TAA formation.

Conclusion

By integrating MR analysis, molecular docking, and multi-model validation (human tissues, VSMCs, and two TAA mouse models), we demonstrate that piceatannol effectively attenuates TAA progression, potentially by interacting with STK33 to preserve the VSMC contractile phenotype. These findings propose the Piceatannol-STK33 axis as a promising therapeutic target and provide a compelling rationale for its future clinical translation in TAA treatment.
