Hydroxychloroquine-induced renal phospholipidosis manifesting as proximal tubulopathy in systemic lupus erythematosus
Authors: Shun Manabe, Momoko Seki, Yusuke Ushio, Moeko Ochiai, Rina Takahashi, Shizuka Kobayashi, Shiho Makabe, Naoko Ito, Hiroshi Kataoka, Sekiko Taneda, Kazuho Honda, Kosaku Nitta, Junichi Hoshino
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundHydroxychloroquine (HCQ)-induced renal phospholipidosis typically manifests as glomerular “zebra bodies”, “myeloid bodies”, and “curvilinear bodies” and is generally considered a benign histological mimic of Fabry disease. We report a case of HCQ-induced renal phospholipidosis with proximal tubulopathy presenting as slowly progressive kidney dysfunction and Fanconi syndrome, challenging the notion that renal phospholipidosis is clinically silent.Case presentationA 36-year-old woman with systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) treated with HCQ for 18 months presented with slowly progressive kidney dysfunction. Urinalysis showed minimal proteinuria and no active sediment suggestive of a lupus nephritis flare; however, urinary markers of tubular injury were markedly elevated. She exhibited normoglycemic glycosuria, pan-aminoaciduria, hypophosphatemia, hypouricemia, and metabolic acidosis, consistent with mild but distinct Fanconi syndrome. Her estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) slope rapidly declined at − 11.2 mL/min/1.73 m²/year during HCQ treatment. Kidney biopsy revealed glomerular "zebra bodies”, “myeloid bodies”, and “curvilinear bodies” characteristic of HCQ-induced renal phospholipidosis, as well as lysosomes filled with electron-dense granules within glomeruli. Notably, lysosomes filled with electron-dense granules were also abundant in proximal tubular epithelial cells, resembling the “lysosomal accumulation of light chains” seen in light chain proximal tubulopathy (LCPT) without crystal formation and “lysosomes containing dark electron-dense aggregates” of chronic interstitial nephritis in agricultural communities (CINAC). Extensive clinical, biochemical, genetic, and histological evaluations excluded Fabry disease. Immunofluorescence demonstrated globotriaosylceramide (Gb3) minor and patchy positivity accumulation in both glomeruli and proximal tubules, suggesting that lysosomal metabolic dysfunction occurred similarly in glomerular cells and tubular epithelial cells. Based on these findings, a diagnosis of proximal tubulopathy secondary to HCQ-induced renal phospholipidosis was made. HCQ discontinuation resulted in the resolution of Fanconi syndrome and improvement of the eGFR slope to + 0.9 mL/min/1.73 m²/year.ConclusionsThis case indicates that HCQ-induced renal phospholipidosis is not merely a silent histological finding but can manifest as clinically significant proximal tubulopathy. The pathophysiology likely involves active tubular secretion of HCQ causing rapid lysosomal and transporter dysfunction analogous to LCPT without crystal formation and CINAC. While standard monitoring for lupus nephritis focuses on glomerular markers, monitoring tubular function markers in HCQ-treated patients may enable early detection of this potentially underdiagnosed complication.

Background

Hydroxychloroquine (HCQ) is a cornerstone therapy for systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) and is strongly recommended to improve long-term prognosis, including kidney outcomes [1–3]. However, recent guidelines have highlighted the risks of drug accumulation in tissues such as the retina, myocardium, and skin [1, 2]. Consequently, to mitigate the risk of retinal toxicity, the recommended maximum daily dose has been reduced to ≤ 5.0 mg/kg of actual body weight [1, 2]. HCQ is classified as a cationic amphiphilic drug (CAD) that permeates lipid bilayers and selectively accumulates within the acidic environment of lysosomes. This accumulation raises intralysosomal pH, leading to the generalized dysfunction of lysosomal enzymes [4] and the resultant intracellular buildup of undigested substrates, which is thought to provoke organ damage [5].

Regarding kidney involvement, Müller-Höcker et al. reported a seminal case in 2003 of chloroquine (CQ)-induced phospholipidosis associated with kidney dysfunction, characterized by “zebra bodies,” “myeloid bodies” and “curvilinear bodies” mimicking Fabry disease and neuronal ceroid lipofuscinosis (NCL), respectively [6, 7]. These findings suggested that generalized lysosomal enzyme failure could result in kidney impairment. Conversely, with increasing reports of “zebra bodies” and “myeloid bodies” in patients treated with HCQ and other CADs, renal phospholipidosis has largely been interpreted as a “silent” histological mimic without clinical consequences [8, 9]. Indeed, in our previous study of five consecutive patients with SLE on HCQ, we identified “zebra bodies” in all cases, yet their kidney function improved with standard therapy against lupus nephritis, leading us to conclude that these ultrastructural changes represented a clinically silent phenomenon [8].

Despite this prevailing view, we herein report a patient who developed clinically significant kidney dysfunction and proximal tubulopathy (Fanconi syndrome) following HCQ therapy. Notably, the patient exhibited a rapid decline in estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) during treatment, which ceased and gradually recovered upon discontinuation of the drug. This case challenges the current notion that renal phospholipidosis is a benign entity, highlighting a distinct nephrotoxic spectrum where HCQ can induce proximal tubulopathy.

Case report

A 36-year-old woman with a 19-year history of SLE was referred for progressive kidney dysfunction. She had a history of aseptic meningitis, but her SLE disease activity had been well-controlled on low-dose prednisolone (< 10 mg/day). The patients prescribed maintenance medications includes prednisolone (9 mg/day), loxoprofen (60 mg/day), esomeprazole (20 mg/day), beraprost (120 µg/day), alendronate (35 mg/week), sodium ferrous citrate (50–100 mg/day), and rebamipide (100–300 mg/day). Eighteen months prior to presentation, HCQ was initiated at 300 mg/day (4.8 mg/kg actual body weight) following its approval in Japan.

On presentation, she was stable with no subjective symptoms, physical findings, or serological evidence suggestive of active SLE flare. The patient was positive for ANA (1:640; homogenous and speckled patterns), anti-double-stranded DNA antibody (12.5 IU/mL; normal range < 12 IU/mL), anti-SS-A antibody (23.0 U/mL; normal range < 10 U/mL), and anti-U1-RNP antibody (> 550 U/mL; normal range < 10 U/mL). She was negative for anti-SS-B antibody (< 1.0 U/mL; normal range < 10 U/mL) and anti-Sm antibody (3.8 U/mL; normal range < 10 U/mL). Urinalysis revealed no hematuria or active sediment, with only trace proteinuria (0.20 g/gCr) and no albuminuria (0.03 g/gCr). Mild kidney impairment (creatinine 0.89 mg/dL) was observed. Serum potassium levels fluctuated near the lower limit of normal between 3.4 and 3.7 mEq/L. However, serum laboratory findings showed hypophosphatemia (2.3 mg/dL), hypouricemia (1.9 mg/dL), and metabolic acidosis (bicarbonate 17.4 mmol/L). Furthermore, urinalysis revealed markedly elevated urinary biomarkers of tubular injury: β2-microglobulin (β2MG), 19,868 µg/L; and α1-microglobulin, 28,200 µg/L. Evaluation for proximal tubular dysfunction revealed normoglycemic glycosuria, pan-aminoaciduria, an abnormally low tubular maximum reabsorption of phosphate per glomerular filtration rate (TmP/GFR) of 1.9 mg/dL, and abnormally high fractional excretion of uric acid of 21.4%. Based on these findings, a diagnosis of Fanconi syndrome was made. Retrospective analysis of her clinical records revealed that the eGFR slope had rapidly declined at a rate of − 11.2 mL/min/1.73 m²/year following the initiation of HCQ (Fig. 1).

To rigorously rule out other causes of tubulointerstitial damage, a detailed clinical and pharmacological assessment was performed. Serum and urine immunofixation revealed no monoclonal protein. Concomitant Sjögren’s syndrome was deemed unlikely as she had no subjective sicca symptoms, normal tear production on the Schirmer I test (27 mm right, 20 mm left), no corneal damage, and negative anti-SS-B (< 1.0 U/mL), despite positive anti-SS-A (23.0 U/mL). She did not reside in an agricultural community and denied the use of over-the-counter medications, dietary supplements, illegal drugs, or herbal remedies. A kidney biopsy was performed to investigate the cause of this unexplained decline in kidney function and proximal tubulopathy.

Kidney biopsy findings

Light microscopy and immunofluorescence (IF) staining for immunoglobulins and complement revealed ISN/RPS class I lupus nephritis, characterized by minor glomerular abnormalities and scattered immune deposits. Foamy podocytes were entirely absent (Fig. 2A). Periodic acid-methenamine silver (PAM) staining showed prominent black granules in glomerular epithelial cells (Fig. 2B). Notably, while inflammatory cell infiltration suggestive of tubulointerstitial nephritis was absent, proximal tubular epithelial cells were swollen and contained variably sized Periodic Acid-Schiff (PAS)-positive granules (Fig. 2C).

The epon-embedded block for electron microscopy (EM) contained two glomeruli. EM demonstrated “zebra bodies”, “myeloid bodies”, and “curvilinear bodies” in podocytes (Fig. 2D-G). Furthermore, small and faint “lamellar bodies” partially occupying lysosomes were observed (Fig. 2G). However, although approximately 120 podocytes were examined, only two podocytes demonstrated typical “zebra bodies” and “curvilinear bodies” (Fig. 2E) or “zebra bodies” and “myeloid bodies” (Fig. 2F) resembling those seen in Fabry disease, with a mere four and two bodies per cell, respectively. Small and faint “lamellar bodies” were observed within a few additional podocytes, as well as in one endothelial cell and one mesangial cell (Fig. 2G), with only one to five bodies per cell. Despite the absence of active glomerular inflammation, both glomeruli consistently showed fragmented cellular debris within Bowman’s space (Fig. 2D, H). These debris contained lysosomes filled with “proteinaceous material”, as previously reported (Fig. 2D, H) [6]. These vesicles closely resembled the “lysosomal accumulation of light chains” observed in light chain proximal tubulopathy (LCPT) without crystal formation [10, 11] and “lysosomes containing dark electron-dense aggregates” described in chronic interstitial nephritis in agricultural communities (CINAC) [12, 13]. Ultrastructurally, similar lysosomes filled with electron-dense granules were abundant in proximal tubular epithelial cells, some of which were enlarged and dysmorphic (Fig. 2I), corresponding to the intracellular granules observed on PAS staining (Fig. 2C). IF staining for globotriaosylceramide (Gb3), the major accumulation substrate in Fabry disease, revealed minor and patchy granular positivity in glomeruli (Fig. 2J) likely reflecting the sparse presence of “lamellar bodies”. Minor Gb3 positivity was also observed in proximal tubular epithelial cells (Fig. 2K, L).

Clinical course

Although the number of “zebra bodies” and “myeloid bodies,” as well as the proportion of Gb3-positive cells and granules, were distinctly lower than what is typically observed in female Fabry disease with late-onset GLA mutation (Fig. 2M), we comprehensively evaluated the possibility of Fabry disease. There were no known cases of Fabry disease among her relatives, and there was no family history of chronic kidney disease, heart failure, or cardiovascular death involving her parents or two siblings. Cardiac evaluation was normal (BNP 13.3 pg/mL; LVEF 58%, LVMI 65 g/m²). Physical and ophthalmological examinations confirmed the absence of angiokeratomas and cornea verticillata. A “targeted” urine sediment analysis for “mulberry cells” or “mulberry bodies” yielded negative results [14]. Leukocyte α-galactosidase A (α-Gal A) activity was normal (85.8 nmol/mg P/hr; reference 49.8–116.4). Furthermore, targeted next-generation sequencing (NGS) covering the exons and 10-bp flanking intronic splice-site regions of the GLA gene revealed no pathogenic variants. While a diagnosis of Fabry disease could not be completely excluded, aside from the minor and patchy “zebra bodies”, “myeloid bodies”, and Gb3 positivity, clinical and histological findings actively suggesting this condition were remarkably scarce.

Based on the temporal association between HCQ initiation and kidney function decline, combined with histological evidence consistent with HCQ-induced renal phospholipidosis, HCQ was discontinued. Although the normalization of urinary tubular markers was gradual, renal glycosuria and electrolyte abnormalities resolved within 4 months: potassium, 3.8 mEq/L (subsequently remaining ≥ 4.0 mEq/L); phosphate, 3.0 mg/dL; uric acid, 3.3 mg/dL; and bicarbonate, 21.3 mmol/L. Most notably, the progressive decline in eGFR was arrested. The eGFR slope improved to + 0.9 mL/min/1.73 m²/year, and tubular markers decreased and stabilized over time (Fig. 1). HCQ was the only medication permanently stopped during this time period. This clinical trajectory confirmed the diagnosis of reversible proximal tubulopathy secondary to HCQ-induced renal phospholipidosis.

Discussion

We report a case of HCQ-induced renal phospholipidosis manifesting as progressive kidney dysfunction and clinically significant proximal tubulopathy. Historically, early reports of CQ/HCQ-induced phospholipidosis were characterized by kidney insufficiency accompanied by PAS-positive granular material in the cytoplasm [6], as well as vacuolization and blebbing of the apical cytoplasm [15] of proximal tubular epithelial cells. Ultrastructurally, these cases exhibited massive lysosomal inclusions [6] and intracytoplasmic granular debris [15] within proximal tubular epithelial cells. The findings in our case are entirely consistent with these “toxic” phenotypes described in the early literature. In contrast, recent reports have increasingly identified “zebra bodies” and “myeloid bodies” as incidental findings in glomeruli, serving primarily as histological mimics of Fabry disease without clinical sequelae [8, 9]. However, as demonstrated in our case, it is crucial to recognize that drug-induced renal phospholipidosis is not invariably clinically silent; rather, the abundant accumulation of lysosomes filled with electron-dense granules within proximal tubular epithelial cells—unlike the glomerular-dominant “silent” form—can lead to severe functional impairment.

A distinct feature of this case was the minor unequivocal accumulation of Gb3 in both glomeruli and proximal tubules, despite the scarcity of findings actively suggesting Fabry disease. Unlike Fabry disease, which arises from a specific deficiency of α-galactosidase A, the Gb3 accumulation in this case is considered a bystander effect of generalized dysfunction of lysosomal enzyme induced by HCQ. While the appearance of “zebra bodies”, “myeloid bodies”, and “curvilinear bodies” in the glomeruli remains the standard diagnostic hallmark of HCQ-induced renal phospholipidosis (reflecting glomerular lysosomal enzyme failure), the tubular Gb3 positivity suggests that HCQ-induced lysosomal enzyme dysfunction is simultaneously occurring in the tubules, even though typical “lamellar bodies” could not be confirmed there ultrastructurally. Furthermore, the extent of Gb3 positivity was distinctly less than that typically observed in age-matched female patients carrying a late-onset GLA mutation—the mildest phenotype of Fabry disease. This finding aligns with recent experimental studies demonstrating that HCQ primarily induces the accumulation of phosphatidylcholine and phosphatidylethanolamine, rather than Gb3 [16]. In our case, the material predominantly accumulating within the lysosomes—specifically, the electron-dense granules engorging the tubular lysosomes—was likely composed of substrates other than Gb3.

The presence of numerous lysosomes filled with electron-dense granules in proximal tubular epithelial cells and cellular debris within Bowman’s space resembles the “lysosomal accumulation of light chains” seen in LCPT without crystal formation [10, 11] and “lysosomes containing dark electron-dense aggregates” described in chronic interstitial nephritis in agricultural communities (CINAC) [12, 13]. The link between lysosomal dysfunction and Fanconi syndrome has been elucidated in LCPT models. Luciani et al. demonstrated that the accumulation of light chains impairs lysosomal acidification and proteolytic function, triggering a stress response that disrupts the apical trafficking of specific transporters, notably the sodium-phosphate cotransporter (NaPi-IIa), thereby causing phosphaturia and generalized tubular dysfunction [17]. Meanwhile, in CINAC and other drug-induced nephropathies featuring “lysosomes containing dark electron-dense aggregates,” a potential association with the dysfunction of transporters such as Na+/K+-ATPase has been suggested. Furthermore, although based on a single case report, functional kidney potassium wasting has been reported to occur with therapeutic doses of HCQ [18]. Although kidney histology was unfortunately not evaluated in that report, it suggests a clinical similarity to our case regarding tubular dysfunction. In our case, the observed lysosomal electron-dense aggregates—confirmed by electron microscopy—presumably provoke a similar pathophysiological cascade, impairing the recycling and expression of apical transporters like NaPi-IIa, resulting in Fanconi syndrome.

Although the exact localization of drug-induced renal phospholipidosis remains variable, the proximal tubule is a known target for drug accumulation. For instance, gentamicin, an aminoglycoside antibiotic known to cause Fanconi syndrome, distributes at its highest concentration in the proximal tubule [19]. Similarly, kidney excretion accounts for approximately 40–70% of HCQ elimination [20], making the proximal tubule, the most active site of drug secretion, susceptible to high exposure. HCQ exists as a cationic drug and is thought to interact with organic cation transporters (OCTs) and multidrug and toxin extrusion proteins (MATEs). While the precise transport mechanisms are complex, reports suggest that CQ enters proximal tubular cells and is actively secreted into the urine via MATE1 [20]. Importantly, CQ is also a known inhibitor of MATE1 [20]. This dual interaction suggests that HCQ may inhibit its own efflux route, leading to high intracellular concentrations and subsequent sequestration within lysosomes due to ion trapping, thereby inducing localized phospholipidosis and tubular injury.

Following the discontinuation of HCQ, the patient’s tubular function gradually improved. Lysosomal electron-dense aggregates, which are implicated in the pathogenesis of our case, are known to appear not only with monoclonal proteins or agrochemicals but also with various medications such as cyclosporine and tacrolimus [12], and may also represent non-specific findings [13]. However, our patient had no history of using any of the other drugs reported to cause lysosomal aggregates [8, 12]. Furthermore, HCQ was the sole medication discontinued between the time of the kidney biopsy and the patient’s subsequent clinical improvement. Therefore, both the morphological alterations and the clinical manifestations of proximal tubulopathy strongly indicate HCQ-induced toxicity. This slow resolution is likely attributable to the extremely long terminal half-life of HCQ (approximately 40–50 days) [21], necessitating an extended period for the clearance of massive intracellular lysosomal accumulations. Crucially, however, the rapid decline in kidney function observed during treatment (eGFR slope − 11.2 mL/min/1.73 m²/year) was successfully arrested, stabilizing at + 0.9 mL/min/1.73 m²/year after drug withdrawal. This outcome underscores that early recognition and cessation of the drug can prevent irreversible kidney failure.

As a limitation of this case, although there was no family history, normal cardiac function, no dermatological or ophthalmological findings, no “mulberry cells” or “mulberry bodies”, normal leukocyte α-Gal A activity, and substantially less Gb3 accumulation in the tissues compared to female Fabry disease, the possibility of Fabry disease cannot be completely excluded. We performed targeted next-generation sequencing (NGS) covering the exons and 10-bp flanking intronic splice-site regions of the GLA gene, which revealed no sequence variants compared to the reference. However, routine genetic testing can occasionally miss pathogenic mutations [22]. To definitively exclude Fabry disease, it is necessary to sequence the entire GLA gene and flanking regions, and if negative, to perform microarray-based duplication/deletion testing [22]. Additionally, plasma lyso-Gb3 could not be evaluated in this case. Therefore, we cannot completely exclude the fascinating hypothesis that the patient might be an asymptomatic female heterozygote for Fabry disease whose lysosomal function was further compromised by HCQ. In such a ‘double-hit’ scenario, HCQ-induced lysosomal dysfunction would exacerbate the underlying heterozygous state, and drug withdrawal would ameliorate the phenotype by decreasing the acquired lysosomal injury. Nevertheless, as compelling evidence heavily weighing against a concomitant Fabry disease diagnosis, the patient developed lupus myocarditis and acute myocarditis leading to cardiogenic shock approximately three years after this episode. She achieved recovery of cardiac function following high-dose steroid therapy (including methylprednisolone pulses) and intravenous cyclophosphamide pulse therapy. Endomyocardial biopsies were performed at that time, which revealed severe diffuse lymphocytic infiltration but importantly showed no vacuolar degeneration of cardiomyocytes suggestive of Fabry disease. Given that the heart is a primary target in late-onset/female Fabry variants, this histological absence strongly supports a predominantly drug-induced etiology.

Finally, we postulate that the perceived rarity of HCQ-induced proximal tubulopathy may be partly attributable to detection bias in clinical practice. Standard monitoring for SLE predominantly focuses on glomerular markers (e.g., hematuria and proteinuria) to detect lupus nephritis, whereas specific markers of proximal tubular function are not routinely assessed. Analogous to HCQ-induced cardiac toxicity, which is considered “underdiagnosed” due to the lack of specific early symptoms [5], kidney tubular dysfunction may frequently be overlooked. Our findings suggest that incorporating tubular function markers into routine monitoring for HCQ-treated patients could enable the early detection of this potentially underrecognized complication.
