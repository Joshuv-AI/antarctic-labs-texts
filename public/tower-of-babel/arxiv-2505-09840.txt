Dynamical Zeta Functions and Resonance Chains for Infinite-Area Hyperbolic Surfaces with Large Funnel Widths
Henry Talbott

Abstract
We quantitatively relate the resonance sets of topologically finite infinite-area hyperbolic surfaces with no cusps to the resonance sets of certain metric graphs via the spine graph construction. In particular, we prove the existence of approximate resonance chains in resonance sets of these surfaces in the long-boundary-length regime. Our results are similar in spirit to those obtained in recent independent work by Li-Matheus-Pan-Tao, although our perspective and hypotheses are somewhat different. Our results also generalize older results obtained for three-funneled spheres by Weich. We primarily make use of transfer operators for holomorphic iterated function schemes, along with certain geometric bounds.



1
Introduction

Recall that
\mathcal{M}_{g,n}(L_{1},\dots,L_{n})
is the moduli space of hyperbolic surfaces with genus
g
,
n
boundary components, and prescribed boundary lengths
L_{1},\dots,L_{n}
. It is naturally also the moduli space of hyperbolic surfaces with genus
g
,
n
infinite-area funnels, and prescribed funnel widths
L_{1},\dots,L_{n}
. Here, the ‘width’ of a funnel is the length of the unique geodesic separating that funnel from the rest of the surface. To realize this equivalence, take each boundary geodesic of a surface with boundary, and glue on the unique funnel with the correct width. We will think of
\mathcal{M}_{g,n}(L_{1},\dots,L_{n})
using this second identification.

For any such
X\in\mathcal{M}_{g,n}(L_{1},\dots,L_{n})
, we may define its Laplacian operator
\Delta_{X}
. The
resolvent
of
X
is the following family of operators, varying according to a parameter
s\in\mathbb{C}
:

R_{X}(s)=(\Delta_{X}-s(s-1))^{-1}.

(1)

Formally, the above definition of
R_{X}(s)
is only well-defined for
\mathrm{Re}(s)\geq 1/2
. However, we have:

Theorem 1.1

(Mazzeo-Melrose
[
16
]
, Guillopé-Zworski, Theorem 1
[
13
]
)
.

The resolvent
R_{X}(s)
defined above extends to a meromorphic family of operators

R_{X}(s):L^{2}_{\textrm{comp}}(X)\to H^{2}_{\textrm{loc}}(X)

with poles of finite rank.

We may therefore define the
resonance set
of
X
:

\mathrm{Res}(X)=\{s\in\mathbb{C}:s\textrm{ is a pole of }R_{X}(s)\}

where poles are counted with multiplicity equal to their rank.

These resonances can be thought of as generalized Laplacian eigenvalues, and play an important role in the study of infinite-area hyperbolic surfaces. For a reference work on this theory, see
[
6
]
.

Many significant results have been proven about the general distributional properties of these resonances; a survey is provided in
[
19
]
. Furthermore, questions about resonances link hyperbolic geometry to several other fields, including number theory
[
7
]
and the study of chaotic quantum systems in theoretical physics
[
3
,
4
]
.

In this paper, we will show that the resonances of an infinite-area surface
X
can be related to the zeroes of a certain dynamical zeta function derived from a different geometric object, the
spine graph
of
X
. While this relation is only approximate, for ‘typical’ surfaces, the error terms decay exponentially in the minimum length of the funnel widths of
X
.

Specifically, for any
\mathcal{M}_{g,n}(L_{1},\dots,L_{n})
, there exists a ‘surface-to-spine’ homeomorphism

\Phi:\mathcal{M}_{g,n}(L_{1},\dots,L_{n})\to MRG_{g,n}(L_{1},\dots,L_{n})

(2)

where
MRG_{g,n}(L_{1},\dots,L_{n})
is the corresponding moduli space of
metric ribbon graphs
or
fatgraphs
. Metric ribbon graphs are metric graphs with extra vertex orientation information (see
[
1
]
). Note that here both spaces carry the standard topology; the topology of
\mathcal{M}_{g,n}(L_{1},...,L_{n})
may be defined through Fenchel-Nielsen coordinate charts, whereas the topology on
MRG_{g,n}(L_{1},...,L_{n})
may be defined via a natural cellularization, as in
[
1
]
. This homeomorphism was first defined by Bowditch-Epstein
[
8
]
, and has been used in the study of hyperbolic surfaces by Do, Mondello, and others
[
10
,
18
,
23
]
.

To set up the resonance correspondence, we first observe that associated to any
X\in\mathcal{M}_{g,n}(L_{1},\dots,L_{n})
is a
dynamical zeta function

d_{X}(s,z)
, an entire function in both complex variables satisfying

\mathrm{Res}(X)=\{s\in\mathbb{C}:d_{X}(s,1)=0\}\setminus(-\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 1}).

For any
\Gamma\in MRG_{g,n}(L_{1},\dots,L_{n})
, there likewise exists a metric graph dynamical zeta function
\widetilde{d}_{\Gamma}(s,z)
, and we may define

\widetilde{\mathrm{Res}}(\Gamma)=\{s\in\mathbb{C}:\widetilde{d}_{\Gamma}(s,1)=0\}.

The definition of
\widetilde{d}_{\Gamma}(s,z)
can be found in Subsection
2.1
, while the definition of
d_{X}(s,z)
is delayed until Lemma
3.12
. We will argue that
\widetilde{\mathrm{Res}}(\Phi(X))
converges to
\mathrm{Res}(X)
under the correct limit and the correct notion of convergence.

1.1
Main Results

Graph resonances are scaling-invariant, in the sense that for
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}
,
\widetilde{\mathrm{Res}}(\alpha\Gamma)=\frac{1}{\alpha}\widetilde{\mathrm{Res}}(\Gamma)
. Here,
\alpha\Gamma
is the metric ribbon graph obtained by scaling each edge length of
\Gamma
by
\alpha
. It therefore makes sense to define scaled resonance sets

\mathrm{Res}(X,L)=\{s\in\mathbb{C}:\frac{s}{L}\in\mathrm{Res}(X)\},

\widetilde{\mathrm{Res}}(\Gamma,L)=\{s\in\mathbb{C}:\frac{s}{L}{\in}\widetilde{\mathrm{Res}}(\Gamma)\}=\widetilde{\mathrm{Res}}(\frac{1}{L}\Gamma).

We also need a notion of an ‘
\eta
-great surface’, where
\eta
is some positive real number. This condition is almost that
\Phi(X)
is trivalent and all edges in
\Phi(X)
have length at least
2\eta
; see Definition
4.1
for a precise statement.

Lastly, we need the following definition:

Definition 1.2
.

For a moduli space
\mathcal{M}_{g,n}(L_{1},\dots,L_{n})
and given parameters
L
and
C\geq 1
, we say that the
L_{h}
(with
1\leq h\leq n
) are
C
-bounded by
L
if they satisfy
L_{h}\leq CL
for all
L_{h}
, and that the
L_{h}
are
C
-controlled by
L
if they satisfy

\frac{1}{C}L\leq L_{h}\leq CL

for all
L_{h}
.

Note that the
\eta
-greatness condition is not so strict. Take
\mathcal{M}_{g,n}(L_{1},\dots,L_{n})
and assume the
L_{h}
are
C
-controlled by
L
. Also set
\eta=L^{\varepsilon}
for some
0<\varepsilon<1
. Then by Corollary 4.13 in
[
23
]
, the Weil-Petersson probability that
X\in\mathcal{M}_{g,n}(L_{1},\dots,L_{n})
is
not

\eta
-great is
O_{g,n,C}(1/L^{2-2\varepsilon})
.

Our main results are as follows. We take a surface
X\in\mathcal{M}_{g,n}(L_{1},...,L_{n})
and the corresponding metric ribbon graph
\Gamma=\Phi(X)\in MRG_{g,n}(L_{1},...L_{n})
, and associate respective dynamical zeta functions
d_{X}(s,z)
and
\widetilde{d}_{\Gamma}(s,z)
to both objects. We then show that if all
L_{h}
are large, these two zeta functions approximately coincide on compact sets. Specifically:

Theorem 1.3
.

Fix
X\in\mathcal{M}_{g,n}(L_{1},\dots,L_{n})
, and assume that
X
is
\eta
-great and that the
L_{h}
are
C
-bounded by
L
. Set
\Gamma=\Phi(X)
, and fix
A\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}
. Also let
\varepsilon>0
. Assume that
\eta
is sufficiently large in terms of
g
,
n
,
C
,
A
, and
\varepsilon
. Then there exist dynamical zeta functions
d_{X}(s,z)
and
\widetilde{d}_{\Gamma}(s,z)
, entire in both variables, such that for all
s,z\in\mathbb{C}
satisfying
|z|\leq A
and
|s|\leq\frac{A}{L}
, we have

|d_{X}(s,z)-\widetilde{d}_{\Gamma}(s,z)|=O_{g,n,C,A,{\varepsilon}}(e^{-{(1-\varepsilon)\eta}})

(3)

where the implicit constant depends only on
g
,
n
,
C
, and
A
. Furthermore,
\widetilde{d}_{\Gamma}(s,z)
is a finite sum of terms of the form

az^{b}(e^{-cs})

for
a,b\in\mathbb{Z}
and
c\in\mathbb{R}
, and
a,b=O_{g,n}(1)
. If all edge lengths of
\Gamma
lie in
\mathbb{Z}
or
\mathbb{Q}
, then all
c
will respectively lie in
\mathbb{Z}
or
\mathbb{Q}
as well.

Remark 1.4
.

This theorem and the corollary below are analogous to results obtained in a more general context by Li-Matheus-Pan-Tao in their recent independent work
[
15
]
, although the hypotheses and framework used in our work are somewhat different from theirs. While it is possible the two setups can be reconciled, the author does not know of a way to do so. The author learned about this other work while this paper was in preparation. See Subsection
1.4
for more details.

1.2
Resonance Convergence and Resonance Chains

Any approximation result on
d_{X}(s,z)
and
\widetilde{d}_{\Gamma}(s,z)
as above can be used to obtain resonance-counting results via the argument principle. To set up this corollary, note that each
MRG_{g,n}(L_{1},\dots,L_{n})
lies inside a larger moduli space
MRG_{g,n}
where boundaries of any lengths are allowed, and in fact the individual
MRG_{g,n}(L_{1},\dots,L_{n})
stratify this space.

Corollary 1.5
.

Fix
\Gamma\in MRG_{g,n}(L_{1},\dots,L_{n})
, and assume
\Gamma
is trivalent. Let
(X_{{t}})
be a sequence of surfaces with topological type
(g,n)
(for
t\in\mathbb{N}
), and let
(\alpha_{{t}})
be a sequence of positive real numbers, such that the following properties hold:

1.

\lim_{{t}\to\infty}\alpha_{{t}}=\infty
.

2.

If the sequence
(\Gamma_{{t}})
in
MRG_{g,n}
is defined by
\Gamma_{{t}}=\frac{1}{\alpha_{{t}}}\Phi(X_{{t}})
, then

\lim_{{t}\to\infty}\frac{1}{\alpha_{{t}}}\Gamma_{{t}}=\Gamma.

Also choose some connected bounded open domain
U\subseteq\mathbb{C}
such that
\partial U\cap\widetilde{\mathrm{Res}}(\Gamma)=\emptyset
. Then

\lim_{{t}\to\infty}|U\cap\mathrm{Res}(X_{{t}},\alpha_{{t}})|=|U\cap\widetilde{\mathrm{Res}}(\Gamma)|.

(4)

Since
U
can be arbitrarily small, this result provides a sense in which the normalized resonances of the surfaces
X_{{t}}
converge to the resonances of
\Gamma
. One important consequence of such a result is that it shows that many hyperbolic surfaces with large funnel widths possess approximate
resonance chains
, or sets of resonances arranged with equal spacing along a vertical line in
\mathbb{C}
. Resonance chains have been extensively studied in connection to surface Laplacians and other dynamical objects; see Subsection
1.4
for a very brief background.

To explain the appearance of approximate resonance chains, consider
X\in\mathcal{M}_{g,n}(L_{1},\dots,L_{n})
and
\Gamma=\Phi(X)
. Recall the structure of
\widetilde{d}_{\Gamma}(s,z)
presented in Theorem
1.3
. If
\Gamma
has all integer edge lengths, then
\widetilde{d}_{\Gamma}(s,1)
is a polynomial in
e^{-s}
, and in particular its root set is the union of finitely many (exact) resonance chains of the form

\{s_{0}+2\pi ik:k\in\mathbb{Z}\}.

If instead all edges of
\Gamma
have rational lengths, we may obtain an analogous statement by finding a common denominator and replacing
k\in\mathbb{Z}
above with
k\in\alpha\mathbb{Z}
for some well-chosen
\alpha
. By the resonance scaling property, resonance chains also exist for
\widetilde{d}_{\Gamma}(s,1)
whenever
\Gamma
can be rescaled so that all edge lengths lie in
\mathbb{Z}
or
\mathbb{Q}
.

1.3
Symmetric Three-Funneled Spheres

As an example, consider the moduli space
\mathcal{M}_{0,3}(L,L,L)
; this space consists of a single three-funneled sphere
X_{L}
. Define
\Gamma_{L}=\Phi(X_{L})
. One may calculate that
\Gamma_{L}
is a theta graph with three edges of length
\frac{L}{2}
. In particular,

\Gamma_{L}=L\Gamma_{1}
. Furthermore, we have (see Example
2.1
for a similar calculation) that

\widetilde{d}_{\Gamma_{1}}(s,z)=-4z^{6}e^{-3s}+9z^{4}e^{-2s}-6z^{2}e^{-s}+1

(5)

Setting
z=1
, this expression factors to

-(e^{-s}-1)^{2}(4e^{-s}-1)

and so

\widetilde{\mathrm{Res}}(\Gamma_{1})=\{\ln(4)+2\pi ik,2\pi ik:k\in\mathbb{Z}\}.

In particular, the critical exponent of
\Gamma_{1}
(defined here as the real part of the rightmost resonance(s)) is
\ln(4)\approx 1.386
.

The surface
X_{L}
is
\frac{L}{4}
-great by symmetry, so for any fixed
A
and any
s,z\in\mathbb{C}
satisfying, say,
|z|\leq 2
and
|s|\leq\frac{2}{L}
, we have by Theorem
1.3
that

|d_{X_{L}}(s,z)-\widetilde{d}_{\Gamma_{L}}(s,z)|=O_{\varepsilon}(e^{-{(1-\varepsilon)L/4}}).

Recall that the
critical exponent

\delta_{X_{L}}
of
X_{L}
is likewise equal to the real part of the rightmost zero(s) of
d_{X_{L}}(s,z)
. By a straightforward application of Rouché’s theorem, we obtain the following result:

Corollary 1.6
.

Define
X_{L}
to be the sole surface in the moduli space
\mathcal{M}_{0,3}(L,L,L)
, and let
\delta_{X_{L}}
be its critical exponent. Then

\delta_{X_{L}}=\frac{\ln(4)}{L}+O_{\varepsilon}(e^{-{(1-\varepsilon)L/4}}).

(6)

Versions of this result have been known for some time. Specifically, convergence was proven without speed by McMullen
[
17
]
, was proven with error
O(L^{-3/2})
by Pollicott-Vytnova
[
22
]
. Recently, a matching error of
O_{\varepsilon}(e^{-(1-\varepsilon)L/4})
was proven by Li-Matheus-Pan-Tao
[
15
]
.

1.4
Background

This work is largely based on methods developed by Weich to study resonances for three-funneled spheres
[
24
]
. Specifically, Weich proved versions of Theorem
1.3
and Corollary
1.5
for these surfaces, albeit without the spine graph terminology. It is primarily the relation to spine graphs that allows his methods to be extended here. While the ideas of dynamical zeta functions for holomorphic iterated function schemes were first developed by Bowen-Series and Ruelle, their application in this context is due to Jenkinson-Pollicott
[
14
]
.

It should be noted that one of the primary motivations of analyzing surface resonances is to obtain precise estimates on the
critical exponents
of these surfaces; the critical exponent is a numerical invariant controlling both the growth rate of the geodesic counting function and the Hausdorff dimension of the boundary of the universal cover. The critical exponent of a surface is equal to the value of the rightmost resonance in
\mathrm{Res}(X)
; which is always real
[
20
,
21
]
. This line of inquiry was carried out by McMullen
[
17
]
, Weich
[
24
]
, Pollicott-Vytnova
[
22
]
, Dang-Mehmeti
[
9
]
, and Li-Matheus-Pan-Tao
[
15
]
.

The other motivation is to explain the appearance of approximate resonance chains in surface resonance sets. Resonance chains were first observed by Borthwick
[
5
]
, and have been studied by Weich
[
24
]
, Li-Matheus-Pan-Tao
[
15
]
, and others, as well as by Barkhofen-Kuhl-Poli-Schomerus-Weich in the context of theoretical physics
[
2
]
.

As advertised above, this work overlaps conceptually with recent independent work of Li-Matheus-Pan-Tao, trading a more limited scope for an approach some readers may find more geometrically concrete. The primary difference is in the geometric perspective taken and the model used. Specifically, Li-Matheus-Pan-Tao consider families of Schottky groups over
\mathbb{C}
degenerating to a non-Archimedean Schottky group, which respectively play the role of surfaces and spine graphs in our setup. This family is captured via a meromorphic function
z\to\Gamma_{z}
into Schottky space satisfying certain specific conditions, and the limit is taken as
z\to 0
. The implicit constants in their results depend on the family
\Gamma_{z}
chosen.

While it would be very interesting to compare their bounds to ours in cases more general than that given in Subsection
1.3
, doing so seems difficult. Specifically, for a degenerating family
\Gamma_{z}
consisting entirely of Schottky groups over
SL_{2}(\mathbb{R})
(and thus corresponding to surfaces), one would need a way to calculate our limit parameters
L
and
\eta
from their limit parameter
z
. At present, we do not know of a way to do so.

1.5
Organization

Section two contains a more thorough overview on resonances, resolvents, the Bowditch-Epstein spine construction, and zeta functions. In section three, we define dynamical zeta functions for surfaces in terms of holomorphic iterated function schemes (IFSes). In section four, we study how the geometry of
\eta
-great surfaces gives some control over the data defining these IFSes. In sections five and six, we use this control along with a cycle expansion of dynamical zeta functions to establish important estimates on these functions. Lastly, in section seven we briefly assemble these estimates to prove our main results.

1.6
Asymptotic Notation and Implicit Constants

Throughout this paper, we will generally consider a surface
X
lying in a moduli space
\mathcal{M}_{g,n}(L_{1},\dots,L_{n})
, where the
L_{h}
are
C
-bounded by a parameter
L
. The variable
\Gamma
will almost always represent the corresponding metric ribbon graph

\Gamma=\Phi(X)\in MRG_{g,n}(L_{1},...,L_{n})

We will also consider dynamical zeta functions
d_{X}(s,z)
and
\widetilde{d}_{\Gamma}(s,z)
, and will frequently assume
|z|\leq A
and
|s|\leq\frac{A}{L}
. There will also be a parameter
\varepsilon>0
introduced in Lemma
4.5
.

Furthermore, we will fix parameters
d
and
v
to respectively represent the number of edges and vertices of
\Gamma
. When
\Gamma
is trivalent, which will generally be assumed to be the case, a straightforward Euler characteristic computation shows that

d=6g-6+3n,\ v=4g-4+2n

In particular, both
d
and
v
are
O_{g,n}(1)
.

Due to the number of constants in simultaneous use, we will often also use the Landau notations
O(\cdot)
and
\Omega(\cdot)
. Recall that for real-valued functions
f
and
g
with the same domain,
f=O(g)
is shorthand for the statement that there exists some constant
c\geq 0
such that
f(x)\leq cg(x)
for all
x
in the common domain of
f
and
g
. The notation
\Omega(\cdot)
is shorthand for the statement that there exists some constant
c^{\prime}\geq 0
such that
f(x)\geq cg(x)
for all
x
. When variables are subscripted to
O
or
\Omega
, the implicit constants are allowed to depend on those variables. For example,
f=O_{g,n,C,A}(1)
means that
f
is uniformly bounded by some constant depending only on
g
,
n
,
C
, and
A
. Note that while big-
O
notation is fairly standard, the definition of big-
\Omega
notation used here is specifically the definition due to Knuth, as opposed to the alternate definition due to Hardy-Littlewood and Landau.

Since almost all implicit constants in this paper will depend on
g
,
n
,
C
,
A
, and/or
\varepsilon
, whenever big-
O
and big-
\Omega
notation is used, all such implicit constants will be allowed to depend on
g
,
n
,
C
,
A
, and
\varepsilon
unless otherwise stated.
Even with this standing assumption, these subscripts will sometimes be included anyways for clarity.

Furthermore, whenever we work with a moduli space
\mathcal{M}_{g,n}(L_{1},\dots,L_{n})
with the
L_{h}

C
-bounded by
L
and an
\eta
-great
X\in\mathcal{M}_{g,n}(L_{1},\dots,L_{n})
, we will assume
\eta
is positive and bounded above by
L^{{\upsilon}}
for some
0{<\upsilon}<1
.
We will also assume that
\eta
is sufficiently large in terms of
g
,
n
,
C
,
A
, and
\varepsilon
.

Preliminaries

2.1
Zeta Functions

The spectrum of a surface
X
is closely connected to its
Selberg zeta function
.

Definition 2.1
.

Let
X
be an infinite-area hyperbolic surface, and let
P_{X}
be the set of all oriented closed primitive geodesics on
X
. Then the Selberg zeta function of
X
is defined as

Z_{X}(s)=\prod_{\gamma\in P_{X}}\prod_{k\geq 0}\left(1-e^{-(s+k)l(\gamma)}\right).

(7)

Theorem 2.2

(Epstein-Patterson-Perry, Theorem 1.1
[
11
]
)
.

For an infinite-area hyperbolic surface
X
, the zero set of the zeta function
Z_{X}(s)
is the union of
\mathrm{Res}(X)
(with matching multiplicity) and the negative integers.

Defining the dynamical zeta function
d_{X}(s,z)
used in Theorem
1.3
requires significant additional setup, and is delayed until Lemma
3.12
.

We define dynamical zeta functions for metric graphs as follows. Let
\Gamma
be a finite (undirected) metric graph with
d
edges. Take two copies of each edge with opposite orientations and enumerate them as
\varepsilon_{1},\dots,\varepsilon_{2{d}}
. Say
\varepsilon_{{j}}
‘feeds into’
\varepsilon_{{k}}
if the terminal vertex of
\varepsilon_{{j}}
is also the initial vertex of
\varepsilon_{{k}}
, and
\varepsilon_{{j}}
and
\varepsilon_{{k}}
are not derived from the same undirected edge.

Next, define a family of
2{d}\times 2{d}
matrices
J_{s}
parameterized by
s\in\mathbb{C}
. We think of these matrices as operators on the space of functions
h:\{\varepsilon_{1},\dots,\varepsilon_{2{d}}\}\to\mathbb{C}
, defined by

(J_{s}h)(\varepsilon_{{j}})=\sum_{{k}:{j}\to{k}}e^{-s(l(\varepsilon_{{j}})+l(\varepsilon_{{k}}))/2}h(\varepsilon_{{k}}).

(8)

In matrix notation, this definition becomes

(J_{s})_{jk}=\begin{cases}e^{-s(l(\varepsilon_{j})+l(\varepsilon_{k}))}&j\to k\\
0&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}

(9)

The dynamical zeta function of
\Gamma
is then defined as

\widetilde{d}_{\Gamma}(s,z)=\det(I-zJ_{s}).

(10)

One can check that if all edge lengths of
\Gamma
lie in
\mathbb{Z}
, then in fact all exponents in
\widetilde{d}_{\Gamma}(s,z)
are of the form
e^{-cs}
for
c\in\mathbb{Z}
, rather than
c\in\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{Z}
as one might expect.

Example 2.1
.

Consider the metric ribbon graph
\Gamma
depicted in Figure
1
, with edge lengths
1
,
3
, and
1
. Label the directed edges as
a
,
b
,
c
,
d
,
e
, and
f
as in Figure
1
. Then the matrix family
J_{s}
can be written as

J_{s}=\begin{bmatrix}0&0&0&0&e^{-2s}&e^{-s}\\
0&0&0&e^{-2s}&0&e^{-2s}\\
0&0&0&e^{-s}&e^{-2s}&0\\
0&e^{-2s}&e^{-s}&0&0&0\\
e^{-2s}&0&e^{-2s}&0&0&0\\
e^{-s}&e^{-2s}&0&0&0&0\end{bmatrix}

so that

\widetilde{d}_{\Gamma}(s,z)=-4z^{6}e^{-10s}+z^{4}e^{-4s}+4z^{4}e^{-6s}+4z^{4}e^{-8s}-2z^{2}e^{-2s}-4z^{2}e^{-4s}+1.

(11)

Setting
z=1
, we obtain the following polynomial:

-4(e^{-s})^{10}+4(e^{-s})^{8}+4(e^{-s})^{6}-3(e^{-s})^{4}-2(e^{-s})^{2}+1

=-(e^{-s}-1)^{2}(e^{-s}+1)^{2}(4(e^{-s})^{6}+4(e^{-s})^{4}-1)

We then obtain the following resonance chains for
k\in\mathbb{Z}
:

s=\pi ik,\ s\approx 0.434+\pi ik,\ s\approx 0.129\pm 1.369i+\pi ik.

In particular, the critical exponent of
\Gamma
is approximately
0.434
.

2.2
Spines

For any
X\in\mathcal{M}_{g,n}(L_{1},\dots,L_{n})
, let
X_{C}
be its compact core; i.e. the region lying between all funnel geodesics. Note that any closed geodesic on
X
must lie entirely in
X_{C}
. Define the spine of
X
to be the set of points
x\in X_{C}
such that
d(x,\partial X_{C})
is realized by multiple points on
\partial X_{C}
. The spine has a nice geometric structure.

Theorem 2.3

(Bowditch-Epstein
[
8
]
)
.

Fix
X\in\mathcal{M}_{g,n}(L_{1},\dots,L_{n})
, let
E
be the set of
x\in X_{C}
such that
d(x,\partial X_{C})
is realized by exactly two boundary points, and let
V
be the set of
x\in X_{C}
such that
d(x,\partial X_{C})
is realized by at least three boundary points. Then
E\cup V
is an embedded metric ribbon graph, the spine graph
S_{X}
of
X
, with
V
as its vertex set. Furthermore,
X_{C}
deformation retracts onto
S_{X}
.

Here, a
metric ribbon graph
is a finite metric graph with an orientation around each vertex; one thinks of inflating each edge into a thin ribbon and using the vertex orientations to glue the ribbons together into a thin (topological) surface. For a metric graph embedded in an (oriented) surface, the orientation naturally induces a ribbon structure on the graph.

Definition 2.4
.

The space
MRG_{g,n}(L_{1},\dots,L_{n})
is the moduli space of metric ribbon graphs with genus
g
,
n
boundary components, and prescribed boundary lengths
L_{1},\dots,L_{n}
. Here, metric ribbon graphs are identified up to metric ribbon graph isometry.

Remark 2.5
.

Like
\mathcal{M}_{g,n}(L_{1},\dots,L_{n})
, the space
MRG_{g,n}(L_{1},\dots,L_{n})
possesses a rich geometry. For more information, see
[
1
]
.

We can now construct a surface-to-spine map

\Phi:\mathcal{M}_{g,n}(L_{1},\dots,L_{n})\to MRG_{g,n}(L_{1},\dots,L_{n}).

(12)

Topologically,
\Phi(X)
will be the same as the spine graph
S_{X}
defined above. However, defining the lengths of its edges is more subtle. First draw for each
x\in V
and
y\in\partial X_{C}
realizing
d(x,\partial X_{C})
the geodesic arc from
x
to
y
. These arcs are the
ribs
of the spine, and divide
X_{C}
into a union of hexagons we will call
corridors
. Each corridor contains one edge of
S_{X}
and two boundary arcs of
\partial X
with equal length; we define the length of the corresponding edge in
\Phi(X)
to be the length of either boundary arc. The key property of this construction is that the lengths of the boundary faces of
\Phi(X)
are identical to the funnel widths of
X
, so
\Phi
has the correct codomain.

Theorem 2.6

(Bowditch-Epstein, Theorem 9.5
[
8
]
)
.

The function
\Phi:\mathcal{M}_{g,n}(L_{1},\dots,L_{n})\to MRG_{g,n}(L_{1},\dots,L_{n})
defined above is a homeomorphism.

It will be handy to decompose a surface core
X_{C}
into polygons in a slightly different way: for each corridor, one may draw an arc contained in the corridor between the two boundary components of that corridor, and then find the minimal-length representative of the homotopy class of that arc relative to the boundary. These representatives are called
intercostals
, and cut a given surface core into polygons we will call
sectors
. If the intercostals are extended into bi-infinite rays on
X
, they instead divide
X
into
\emph{longsectors}
. Each long sector is isometric to a region in
\mathbb{H}
bounded between some number non-intersecting geodesics. When the spine graph
S_{X}
of
X
is trivalent, which will almost always be the case in our analysis, these sectors will be hexagons, and long sectors will be bounded by three geodesics.

For an illustration of the constructions made in this subsection, see Figure

.

3
Iterated Function Schemes and Dynamical Zeta Functions

3.1
Holomorphic Iterated Function Schemes

We would like to reformulate resonances in terms of a dynamical system. Our primary tools for doing so will be holomorphic iterated function schemes, transfer operators, and dynamical zeta functions. While we will use a slightly modified definition of holomorphic iterated function schemes, we first present the standard definition:

Definition 3.1
.

A
holomorphic iterated function scheme
(holomorphic IFS) consists of the following data:

1.

A collection of open disks
D_{1},\dots,D_{N}\subseteq\mathbb{C}
with pairwise disjoint closures.

2.

A
N\times N
matrix
A
with all entries equal to
0
or
1
. We denote
A_{{jk}}=1
by
{j}\to{k}
.

3.

For every
({j},{k})
pair with
{j}\to{k}
, a biholomorphism
f_{{jk}}:D_{{j}}\to D_{{k}}
, such that

f_{{jk}}(D_{{j}})\cap f_{{j^{\prime}k^{\prime}}}(D_{{j^{\prime}}})=\emptyset

unless
({j},{k})=({j^{\prime}},{k^{\prime}})
. We also define

D_{{jk}}=f_{{jk}}(D_{{j}}).

We will modify this definition in two ways. First, we will introduce spaces
C_{1},\dots,C_{N}
, each a copy of
\mathbb{C}
, and think of
D_{{j}}
as lying in
C_{{j}}
. The first condition above no longer makes sense, so may be discarded. Second, we will replace the third condition with the condition that for all
{j},{j^{\prime}}
and all
{k}
with
{j}\to{k}
,
{j^{\prime}}\to{k}
,

f_{{jk}}(D_{{j}})\cap f_{{j^{\prime}k}}(D_{{j^{\prime}}})=\emptyset

unless
{j}={j^{\prime}}
.

For us, holomorphic IFSes will be the only type of IFSes under consideration, so we will sometimes drop the ‘holomorphic’ qualifier.

Remark 3.2
.

Note that any such IFS can be modified into one that matches the original definition by translating each
D_{{j}}
so that they have disjoint closures when projected to a common copy of
\mathbb{C}
, performing the projection, and then replacing the
f_{{jk}}
with
\widetilde{f}_{{jk}}=f_{{jk}}+z_{{jk}}
for some carefully chosen translation terms
z_{{jk}}
. Modifying the
f_{{jk}}
in this way has no effect on the IFS properties we care about (in particular, the derivatives of the
f_{{jk}}
are unchanged).

Remark 3.3
.

While it is not strictly part of the definition, all IFSes we will consider will be such that the disks have centers in
\mathbb{R}
, and all
f_{{ij}}
will be linear fractional transformations with real coefficients.

An IFS generates a natural symbolic coding. The symbols of this coding are integers
1,\dots,N
, and a word of length
{m}
in this coding is a sequence
w_{0}\cdots w_{{m}}
such that
w_{{m^{\prime}}}\to w_{{m^{\prime}}+1}
for all
0\leq{m^{\prime}}\leq{m}-1
. Each word corresponds to a map

f_{w}=f_{w_{{m}-1}w_{{m}}}\circ\dots\circ f_{w_{0}w_{1}}:D_{w_{0}}\to D_{w_{{m}}}.

We define
W_{{m}}
to be the set of all words of length
{m}
, and
W_{{m}}^{C}
to be the set of words of length
{m}
satisfying
w_{0}=w_{{m}}
. Words satisfying this second condition are referred to as
closed words
.

For a given IFS with disks
D_{1},...,D_{N}
, we will also define

D=\bigcup_{{j}=1}^{{N}}D_{{j}}

and

f(D)=\bigcup_{{j}\to{k}}D_{{jk}}.

Notice that
f(D)
is a disjoint union under our definition.

Also, by the disjoint images condition and biholomorphicity, we may define a partial inverse map

f^{-1}:f(D)\to D,\ f^{-1}|_{D_{{jk}}}=f_{{jk}}^{-1}.

(13)

3.2
Transfer Operators

Given an IFS with disks
D_{1},\dots,D_{N}
, let
A_{\infty}(D)
be the Banach space of holomorphic functions on
D
that extend to continuous functions on the closure
\overline{D}
and are bounded on
\overline{D}
with the supremum norm.

Definition 3.4
.

Let
V\in A_{\infty}(f(D))
be a chosen
potential function
. Then the
transfer operator

{\mathcal{L}}_{V}:A_{\infty}(D)\to A_{\infty}(D)
associated to the given IFS is defined by

{\mathcal{L}}_{V}h|_{D_{{j}}}={\mathcal{L}}_{V}^{{j}}h,

{\mathcal{L}}^{{j}}_{V}:A_{\infty}(D)\to A_{\infty}(D_{{j}}),

({\mathcal{L}}^{{j}}_{V}h)(u)=\sum_{{k}:{j}\to{k}}V(f_{{jk}}(u))h(f_{{jk}}(u)).

(14)

We also define iterated potentials corresponding to words. For
w\in W_{{m}}
,

V_{w}(u)=\prod_{{m^{\prime}}=1}^{{m}}V(f_{w_{0,{m^{\prime}}}}(u))

(15)

where
w_{0,{m^{\prime}}}
is the truncated word obtained from the first
{m^{\prime}}+1
letters of
w
.

With this definition, a straightforward calculation shows that

({\mathcal{L}}_{V}^{{m}}h)(u)=\sum_{w\in W_{{m}},\ u\in D_{w_{0}}}V_{w}(u)h(f_{w}(u)).

(16)

Definition 3.5
.

An operator
{\mathcal{L}}:B\to B
on a Banach space
B
is a
nuclear operator
if there exist sequences
v_{{m}}\in B
,
\alpha_{{m}}\in B^{*}
, and
\lambda_{{m}}\in\mathbb{C}
such that
\|v_{{m}}\|=\|\alpha_{{m}}\|=1
for all
{m}
,
\sum_{{m}=0}^{\infty}|\lambda_{{m}}|<\infty
, and
{\mathcal{L}}
has the series representation

{\mathcal{L}}h=\sum_{{m}=0}^{\infty}\lambda_{{m}}\alpha_{{m}}(h)v_{{m}}.

(17)

Definition 3.6
.

A holomorphic IFS is
eventually contracting
if there exists some
\theta<1
such that for all sufficiently long words
w
,

|f_{w}^{\prime}(u)|\leq\theta.

Note that if a holomoprhic IFS is eventually contracting, then by taking powers, any closed word
w
is such that the function
f_{w}:D_{w_{0}}\to D_{w_{0}}
must have a unique fixed point in its domain. We denote this fixed point as
u_{w}
.

Lemma 3.7

(Jenkinson-Pollicott, Proposition 2
[
14
]
)
.

Consider an eventually contracting holomorphic IFS. The corresponding transfer operator (for any potential function
V\in A_{\infty}(D)
as defined above) is nuclear.

Definition 3.8
.

We define the
dynamical zeta function
associated to a nuclear transfer operator
\mathcal{L}_{V}
via the Fredholm determinant:

d_{V}(z)=\det(1-z{\mathcal{L}}_{V}).

(18)

These dynamical zeta functions satisfy a trace formula:

Lemma 3.9

(Jenkinson-Pollicott, Equation 3.26
[
14
]
)
.

Let
d_{V}(z)
be the dynamical zeta function associated to an eventually contracting holomorphic IFS. Then
d_{V}(z)
is an entire function, and the following formula holds for
|z|
sufficiently small:

d_{V}(z)=\mathrm{exp}\left(-\sum_{{m}>0}\frac{z^{{m}}}{{m}}\sum_{w\in W^{C}_{{m}}}V_{w}(u_{w})\frac{1}{1-f^{\prime}_{w}(u_{w})}\right)

(19)

where
W^{C}_{{m}}
is the set of closed words of length
{m}
and
u_{w}
is the unique fixed point of a given
f_{w}
.

3.3
Geodesic Coding

Fix
X\in\mathcal{M}_{g,n}(L_{1},\dots,L_{n})
. We want to set up a holomorphic IFS corresponding to
X
in a particular way, so that it aligns with the spine graph
\Gamma=\Phi(X)
. Assume that
\Gamma
is trivalent. Since
X
deformation retracts onto its spine
S_{X}
and
S_{X}
is naturally homeomorphic to
\Gamma=\Phi(X)
, there is a bijection between (directed) geodesics on
X
and (directed) geodesics on
\Gamma
. Now, take the edges
e_{1},\dots,e_{{d}}
of
\Gamma
. We will arbitrarily orient each edge, and use
e_{{j}}
for
1\leq{j}\leq{d}
to refer to the oriented edge and
e_{{j}+{d}}
to refer to that edge with the opposite orientation. This gives a collection
e_{1},\dots,e_{2{d}}
of all oriented edges on
X
. In general, for an edge
e_{{j}}
with
1\leq{j}\leq 2{d}
, we will use the notation
e_{{j}}^{-1}
for that edge with the opposite orientation.

Define a
2{d}\times 2{d}
matrix
A
by setting
A_{{jk}}=1
if
e_{{j}}
feeds into
e_{{k}}
in the sense of Subsection
2.1
, and
A_{{jk}}=0
otherwise. A length-
{m}
word
w=w_{0}\cdots w_{{m}}
in the alphabet
1,\dots,2{d}
is
permissible
if for every consecutive
{jk}
pair in
w
,
A_{{jk}}=1
. Recall that a word
w=w_{0}\cdots w_{{m}}
is
closed
if
w_{0}=w_{{m}}
.

Define the combinatorial length of a closed geodesic on
\Gamma
as the number of edges it traverses, counting with multiplicity. Then there is a clear map from closed permissible words of length
{m}
to closed geodesics on
\Gamma
of combinatorial length
{m}
by following directed edges, and this map is a bijection if one replaces closed permissible words with cyclic permutation classes of closed permissible words. If
w=w_{0}\cdots w_{{m}}
, then we use
\widetilde{\gamma}_{w}
to denote the corresponding geodesic:

[w_{0}\cdots w_{{m}}]\leftrightarrow\widetilde{\gamma}_{w}.

Of course, since there is a bijection
\gamma\leftrightarrow\widetilde{\gamma}
between geodesics on
X
and geodesics on
\Gamma
, we also get a bijection between cyclic permutation classes of closed permissible words and closed geodesics on
X
:

[w_{0}\cdots w_{{m}}]\leftrightarrow\gamma_{w}.

We may interpret this relation as follows: each directed edge on
\Gamma
corresponds to a directed intercostal on
X
, i.e. an intercostal together with a perpendicular vector field allowing us to distinguish one side of the intercostal. Then any oriented geodesic
\gamma
on
X
induces a word by tracking which intercostals it passes through and recording its direction at each such intercostal.

Powers
of closed words are obtained by concatenation, and a primitive word is one that is not a power of a shorter word.

3.4
The Flow-Adapted Iterated Function Scheme

Our goal is to build a holomorphic iterated function scheme derived from a surface
X\in\mathcal{M}_{g,n}(L_{1},\dots,L_{n})
. Since this iterated function scheme will follow a specific construction (and other ways of associating a surface to an IFS have been defined in the literature), we will refer to it as a
flow-adapted
iterated function scheme, following terminology used by Weich in
[
24
]
.

Fix
X\in\mathcal{M}_{g,n}(L_{1},\dots,L_{n})
, let
S_{X}
be its spine, and set
\Gamma=\Phi(X)
. Assume
\Gamma
is trivalent, and that all corridors of
S_{X}
contain their intercostals - this is true for
\eta
-great surfaces by definition.
X_{C}
admits a decomposition into hexagonal sectors, which extend to ‘long sectors’ on
X
by extending intercostals. Each sector is bounded by three boundary arcs and three intercostal arcs, and the intercostal arcs on
X_{C}
extend to bi-infinite geodesics we term
long intercostals
on
X
.

Let
e_{1},\dots,e_{{d}}
be the edges of
\Gamma
; it will be convenient for the entirety of this section to think of
\Gamma
as topologically embedded in
X
via identification with
S_{X}
. We arbitrarily orient each edge, and define
e_{{d}+j}
to be
e_{j}
with reversed orientation for
1\leq j\leq{d}
. This gives an enumeration
e_{1},\dots,e_{2{d}}
of all possible directed edges of
\Gamma
. The disks
D_{1},\dots,D_{2{d}}
of our IFS will be in bijection with these directed edges.

Fix some
e_{j}
, and define
H_{j}\subseteq X
to be the long sector in which
e_{j}
originates. The edge
e_{j}
intersects exactly one intercostal, which we will denote as
\iota_{j}
. This intercostal intersects
\partial X_{C}
in two points, which we will denote as
l_{j}
and
r_{j}
. The labels are chosen so that if the sector
H_{j}\cap X_{C}
is oriented clockwise,
l_{j}
lies to the left of
r_{j}
along
\iota_{j}
.

Consider a copy
\mathbb{H}_{j}
of
\mathbb{H}
defined using the upper-half-plane model. Let

\sigma_{j}:H_{j}\hookrightarrow\mathbb{H}_{j}

be an orientation-preserving, holomorphic, isometric embedding with the following properties:

1.

The intercostal
\iota_{j}
is mapped onto a subarc of the geodesic between
-1
and
1
on
\mathbb{H}_{j}
(i.e., the Euclidean half-circle with center
0
and radius
1
).

2.

The long sector
H_{j}
is mapped into the interior of the half-disk bounded by this geodesic.

3.

The points
\widetilde{l}_{j}=\sigma(l_{j})
and
\widetilde{r}_{j}=\sigma(r_{j})
are equidistant from the point
i
along
\sigma(\iota_{j})
.

These conditions can always be satisfied, and in fact define
\sigma_{j}
uniquely. The construction of the map
\sigma_{j}
is demonstrated in Figure
3
.

\sigma_{j}

We may now embed
\mathbb{H}_{j}
in
\mathbb{C}
, and define
D_{j}
to be the disk centered at
0
of radius
1
in this copy of
\mathbb{C}
. We also define
\widetilde{H}_{j}=\sigma_{j}(H_{j})
,
\widetilde{\iota}_{j}=\sigma_{j}(\iota_{j})
,
\widetilde{l}_{j}=\sigma_{j}(l_{j})
, and
\widetilde{r}_{j}=\sigma_{j}(r_{j})
.

The transition matrix
A
is defined as in the previous subsection, where
A_{{jk}}=1
if
e_{{j}}
feeds into
e_{{k}}
and
A_{{jk}}=0
otherwise.

Next, we need to define the transition maps
f_{{jk}}
. Letting
\widetilde{X}
be the universal cover of
X
, the first step is to note that
\sigma_{{k}}:H_{{k}}\hookrightarrow\mathbb{H}_{{k}}
extends uniquely to an isometry
\sigma_{{k}}:\widetilde{X}\to\mathbb{H}_{{k}}
. We think of these isometries as identifications and use them to define covering maps
\pi_{{k}}:\mathbb{H}_{{k}}\to X
.

For any
{j}\to{k}
, we observe that
e_{{j}}
originates in
H_{{j}}
and terminates in
H_{{k}}
, and in particular
H_{{j}}
and
H_{{k}}
are adjacent and separated by
\iota_{{j}}
. As a consequence,
\widetilde{H}_{{j}}
is adjacent to some lift
\widetilde{H}_{{jk}}
of
H_{{j}}
(lifting with respect to
\pi_{{k}}
), and separated from this lift by a lift
\widetilde{\iota}_{{jk}}
of
\iota_{{j}}
. Since
\iota_{{j}}\neq\iota_{{k}}
, this
\widetilde{\iota}_{{jk}}
must be one of the two bounding geodesics of
\widetilde{H}_{{k}}
contained strictly within
D_{{k}}
. Since the long sectors
\widetilde{H}_{{j}}
and
\widetilde{H}_{{jk}}
are isometric, there is a unique hyperbolic isometry
F_{{jk}}:\widetilde{H}_{{j}}\to\widetilde{H}_{{jk}}
, and this isometry extends uniquely to an isometry

F_{{jk}}:\mathbb{H}_{{j}}\to\mathbb{H}_{{k}}

making the following diagram commute:

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathbb{H}_{{j}}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathbb{H}_{{k}}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces X}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces F_{{jk}}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\pi_{{j}}}

As a complex map,
F_{{jk}}
is a linear fractional transformation, and so extends to a meromorphic map from
\widehat{\mathbb{C}}
to itself. Furthermore, its unique pole lies outside of
D_{{jk}}
, and if
D_{{jk}}
is the disk in
\mathbb{C}
half-bounded by the geodesic extension of
\iota_{{jk}}

F_{{jk}}(D_{{j}})=D_{{jk}}
. The second fact is clear by construction, while the first straightforwardly follows from the explicit formula proven in Lemma

. So we may define

f_{{jk}}:D_{{j}}\to D_{{k}},\ f_{{jk}}=F_{{jk}}|_{D_{{j}}}.

We associate two quantities to this map. The first is
\delta_{{j}}=d_{\mathbb{H}}(\widetilde{l}_{{j}},\widetilde{r}_{{j}})

\kappa_{{jk}}=d_{\mathbb{H}}({\partial}D_{{k}},{\partial}D_{{jk}})

H_{{k}}
. Specifically, the distance
\delta_{{j}}
is realized by the intercostal arc crossed by
e_{{j}}
, while the distance
\kappa_{{jk}}
is realized by the boundary arc incident to the intercostal arc crossed by
e_{{j}}

e_{{k}}
.

.

Note that the illustration provided in Figure

For any fixed
{k}
, trivalency implies there are exactly two indices
{j}_{1}
and
{j}_{2}
such that
{j}_{1}\to{k}
and
{j}_{2}\to{k}
. Furthermore, the disks
D_{{j}_{1}{k}}
and

are (half-)bounded by distinct intercostals of
\widetilde{H}_{{k}}\subseteq\mathbb{H}_{{k}}

f_{{jk}}(D_{{j}})=f_{{j^{\prime}}{k^{\prime}}}(D_{{j^{\prime}}})\Rightarrow({j},{k})=({j^{\prime}},{k^{\prime}}).

Each function
f_{w}
is a linear fractional transformation with real coefficients, and is a biholomorphism from
D_{w_{0}}
to
D_{w}
. While the eventually contracting property holds, its proof will be postponed until certain calculations are made in Section

How do these maps relate to geodesics? Say we have a permissible closed word
w=w_{0}\cdots w_{{m}}
identified with a closed geodesic
\widetilde{\gamma}_{w}
on
\Gamma
; we think of
\widetilde{\gamma}_{w}
as lying in
S_{X}
. Then it is straightforward to check by stitching together commuting diagrams of the form presented above that
f_{w}
maps
D_{w_{0}}
to itself, and extends to a
\pi_{w_{0}}
-invariant isometry on
\mathbb{H}_{w_{0}}
. As a consequence, this extension of
f_{w}
is a deck transformation, and corresponds up to conjugacy to a unique geodesic on
X
.

Lemma 3.11
.

With the setup given in the previous paragraph, the geodesic corresponding to
f_{w}
is
\gamma_{w}
, where
\gamma_{w}
is also the geodesic corresponding to
\widetilde{\gamma}_{w}
under the previously established correspondence between geodesics on
X
and geodesics on
\Gamma
.

Proof.
Choose
j_{0},\dots,j_{m},j_{m+1}
with
j_{m+1}=j_{0}
so that
w_{{q}}
corresponds to the edge pointing from the sector
H_{{j_{q}}}
to the sector
H_{{j_{q+1}}}
. Start with the unique lift to
D_{j_{0}}
of the spine vertex contained in
H_{{j}_{0}}
. After applying
f_{w_{0}w_{1}}
, we can follow a lift of
e_{w_{0}}
from the image of the lift of this vertex to the lift of the unique spine vertex
v_{w_{{1}}}
contained in
H_{{j}_{1}}
; this forms a path
\widetilde{\gamma}_{{1}}
. Now, given
\widetilde{\gamma}_{{q}}
ending at a lift of the unique spine vertex contained in
H_{{j_{q}}}
, we can take its image under
f_{w_{{q}}w_{{q}+1}}
and add a lift of
e_{w_{{q}+1}}
to a lift of the unique spine vertex in
H_{w_{{q}+1}}
to obtain
\widetilde{\gamma}_{{q}+1}
. Iterating this procedure, we obtain
\widetilde{\gamma}_{{m}+1}
, a partial lift of the closed path
\widetilde{\gamma}
on
X
, such that
\widetilde{\gamma}_{{m}+1}
originates at
f_{w}(v_{w_{0}})
and terminates at
v_{w_{0}}
. (Recall that we have embedded
\Gamma
in
X
, so that
\widetilde{\gamma}
can be thought of as both a geodesic on
\Gamma
and a path on
X
.) It is now straightforward to verify that that the
f_{w}
-orbit of
v_{w_{0}}
is of bounded distance from the lift of
\gamma
passing through
\widetilde{H}_{w_{0}}
, and so
f_{w}
corresponds to
\gamma
.
\square

3.5
Dynamical Zeta Functions for IFSes

Our reason for setting up this IFS is as follows. The function
d_{X}(s,z)
defined in Lemma
3.12
for a surface
X\in\mathcal{M}_{g,n}(L_{1},...,L_{n})
is the aforementioned
dynamical zeta function
for
X
.

Lemma 3.12
.

Fix
X\in\mathcal{M}_{g,n}(L_{1},\dots,L_{n})
, construct the flow-adapted IFS corresponding to
X
, and define the following family of potential functions:

V_{s}(u)=((f^{-1})^{\prime}(u))^{-s}.

(20)

Here,
f^{-1}
is defined as in Equation
13
. Let
\mathcal{L}_{V_{s}}
be the transfer operator associated to this IFS and to the potential function
V_{s}(u)
. Setting
d_{X}(s,z)=d_{V_{s}}(z)
, and letting
Z_{X}(s)
be the Selberg zeta function for
X
, we have that
d_{X}(s,z)
is an entire function in both variables and

d(s,1)=Z_{X}(s).

Proof.
This is a standard argument; see Theorem 3.5 in
[
24
]
and Theorem 15.8 in
[
6
]
. As such, our proof will gloss over the technical details.

The first step is the following formula (see Proposition 15.5 in
[
6
]
): for any
w\in W^{C}_{{m}}
, if
u_{w}
is the unique fixed point of
f_{w}
, then

f^{\prime}(u_{w})=e^{-l(\gamma_{w})}.

Next, calculate that
V_{w,s}(u_{w})
and
f^{\prime}_{w}(u_{w})
are independent of a choice of representative in
[w]
: this follows from the geometric interpretation in terms of translation distance given above. Furthermore, it is straightforward to calculate that

V_{w^{j},s}(u_{w^{j}})=(V_{w,s}(u_{w}))^{j},\ f^{\prime}_{w^{j}}(u_{w^{j}})=(f^{\prime}_{w}(u_{w}))^{j}

where
w^{j}
is the
j
-fold concatenation of
w
.
We apply a geometric series expansion to the formula for
d(s,z)
obtained in Lemma
3.9
, which holds for
|z|
sufficiently small:

d(s,z)=\textrm{exp}\left(-\sum_{j\geq 0}\sum_{{m}>0}\sum_{w\in W^{C}_{{m}}}\frac{z^{{m}}}{{m}}V_{w,s}(u_{w})(f^{\prime}_{w}(u_{w}))^{j}\right).

(21)

We now wish to replace the sum
\sum_{{m}>0}\sum_{w\in W^{C}_{{m}}}
over all closed words with the sum
\sum_{r>0}\sum_{[w]\in[W^{P}]}
over all powers of equivalence classes of prime words. If a given equivalence class
[w]
of length
{m}
has prime representation
[w]=[w_{p}^{r}]
where
w_{p}
is prime and has length
{m^{\prime}}
, then this equivalence class will be counted
r
times under the first sum. Under the second sum, it will be counted once. Since necessarily
{m}=r{m^{\prime}}
, we obtain

d(s,z)=\textrm{exp}\left(-\sum_{{j}\geq 0}\sum_{r>0}\sum_{[w]\in[W^{P}]}\frac{\left(z^{c(w)}V_{w,s}(u_{w})(f^{\prime}_{w}(u_{w}))^{{j}}\right)^{r}}{r}\right)

(22)

where
c(w)
is the word length of
w
. We now exchange the order of summation and apply the Taylor expansion for
\log(1-x)
to obtain

d(s,z)=\prod_{[w]\in[W^{P}]}\prod_{j\geq 0}\left(1-z^{c(w)}V_{w,s}(u_{w})(f^{\prime}_{w}(u_{w}))^{j}\right).

(23)

We now apply the chain rule and the inverse derivative formula. For a word
w
with length
{m}
,

V_{w,s}(u_{w})=\prod_{{q}=1}^{{m}}V_{s}(f_{w_{0,{q}}}(u_{w}))

=\prod_{{q}=1}^{{m}}((f^{-1})^{\prime}(f_{w_{0,{q}}}(u_{w})))^{-s}=f^{\prime}_{w}(u_{w})^{s}.

So

d(s,z)=\prod_{[w]\in[W^{P}]}\prod_{j\geq 0}\left(1-z^{c(w)}(f^{\prime}_{w}(u_{w}))^{j+s}\right).

(24)

Applying the translation length formula gives

d(s,z)=\prod_{\gamma}\prod_{j\geq 0}\left(1-z^{c(w)}e^{-l(\gamma)(j+s)}\right).

(25)

Setting
z=1
recovers Selberg’s zeta function.
\square

Whenever we have a surface
X
and refer to the flow-adapted IFS for
X
, we will assume the choice of potential function
V_{s}(u)=((f^{-1})^{\prime}(u))^{-s}
as above. Furthermore, we will use
{\mathcal{L}}^{i}_{s}
as shorthand for
{\mathcal{L}}^{i}_{V_{s}}
, and
{\mathcal{L}}_{s}
as shorthand for
{\mathcal{L}}_{V_{s}}
.

5
The Nuclear Representation and Cycle Expansion

5.1
Controlling the Nuclear Representation

Recall that transfer operators of eventually contracting holomorphic IFSes are known to be nuclear operators. In a sense, this property is the entire motivation for rewriting the Selberg zeta function as a dynamical zeta function, as it provides access to the following powerful result:

Lemma 5.1

(Grothendieck
[
12
]
)
.

If
B
is a Banach space and
L:B\to B
is a nuclear operator with the nuclear representation

{\mathcal{L}}h=\sum_{{m}=0}^{\infty}\lambda_{{m}}a_{{m}}(h)v_{{m}}.

(33)

Then the Fredholm determinant
\det(1-z{\mathcal{L}})
has a power series expansion

1+\sum_{{m}=0}^{\infty}z^{{m}}{c}_{{m}}

defined for all
z\in\mathbb{C}
and satisfying

{c}_{{m}}=(-1)^{{m}}\sum_{{q}_{1}<\cdots<{q}_{k}}\lambda_{{q}_{1}}\cdots\lambda_{{q}_{k}}\det\left((a_{{q}_{{m_{1}}}}(v_{{q}_{{m_{2}}}}))_{{m_{1}},{m_{2}}=1}^{{m}}\right)

(34)

where
(a_{{q}_{{m_{1}}}}(v_{{q}_{{m_{2}}}}))_{{m_{1}},{m_{2}}=1}^{{m}}
is the
{m}\times{m}
matrix with entries
\alpha_{{q}_{{m_{1}}}}(v_{{q}_{{m_{2}}}})
.

The power series expansion
\det(1-z{\mathcal{L}})=1+\sum_{{m}=0}^{\infty}z^{{m}}{c}_{{m}}
given above is known as the
cycle expansion
of the zeta function.

We will now discuss how a nuclear decomposition may be found for our setup. Note that since our operator is technically a family of operators parameterized by
s
, the terms in the nuclear decomposition will likewise be parameterized by
s
.

The starting point is the operators
{\mathcal{L}}_{s}^{{j}}:A_{\infty}(D)\to A_{\infty}(D_{{j}})
; recall that
{\mathcal{L}}_{s}^{{j}}
is shorthand for
{\mathcal{L}}^{{j}}_{V_{s}}
with
V_{s}
defined as in Lemma
3.12
. We may extend the range of each
{\mathcal{L}}_{s}^{{j}}
to
A_{\infty}(D)
by setting it equal to
0
on all other
A_{\infty}(D_{{k}})
, and then define
{\mathcal{L}}_{s}
as the sum of these extended operators. In particular, we can calculate the nuclear representations for each
{\mathcal{L}}^{{j}}_{s}
separately, and add them to obtain the nuclear representation for
{\mathcal{L}}_{s}
.

Lemma 5.2
.

Let
X\in\mathcal{M}_{g,n}(L_{1},\dots,L_{n})
be
\eta
-great with the
L_{{h}}

C
-bounded by
L
, and let the
{\mathcal{L}}_{s}^{{j}}
be the components of the transfer operator for the flow-adapted IFS corresponding to
X
. Also let
\varepsilon>0
. Assume that
\eta
is sufficiently large in terms of
g
,
n
,
C
, and
\varepsilon
. Then each
{\mathcal{L}}^{{j}}_{s}
has the nuclear representation

{\mathcal{L}}^{{j}}_{s}=\sum_{{m}=0}^{\infty}\lambda^{{j}}_{{m},s}a_{{m},s}^{{j}}v^{{j}}_{{m},s}

(35)

with terms satisfying the formulas

v^{{j}}_{{m},s}(u)=u^{{m}},

\widetilde{a}^{{j}}_{{m},s}(h)=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{C_{\rho}}\frac{{\mathcal{L}}_{s}^{{j}}h(w)}{w^{{m}+1}}dw,

a_{{m},s}^{{j}}(h)=\widetilde{a}^{{j}}_{{m},s}(h)/\|\widetilde{a}^{{j}}_{{m},s}\|,

|\lambda_{{m},s}^{{j}}|={O(e^{-{m}{(1-\varepsilon)}\eta}e^{L|\mathrm{Re}(s)|})}.

(36)

Here,
C_{\rho}
is the circle around the origin with radius
\rho={e^{(1-\varepsilon)\eta}}
.

Proof.
Fix
{\mathcal{L}}^{{j}}_{s}
, and recall the definition

{\mathcal{L}}^{{j}}_{s}h(u)=\sum_{{k}:{j}\to{k}}V_{s}(f_{{jk}}(u))h(f_{{jk}}(u)).

(37)

As was argued earlier,
f_{{jk}}
maps a disk centered at
0
with radius
\rho={e^{(1-\varepsilon)\eta}}
into
D_{{k}}
, and is holomorphic on a slightly larger disk (in fact, a much larger disk, with radius
\Omega(e^{\eta})
). Let
C_{\rho}
be the circle around
0
with radius
\rho
. By the Cauchy integral formula, for any
u\in D_{{j}}
,

{\mathcal{L}}^{{j}}_{s}h(u)=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{C_{\rho}}\frac{{\mathcal{L}}^{{j}}_{s}h(w)}{w-u}dw.

(38)

We rewrite this expression as

\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{C_{\rho}}\frac{{\mathcal{L}}_{s}^{{j}}h(w)}{w}\frac{1}{1-\frac{u}{w}}=\sum_{{m}=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{C_{\rho}}\frac{{\mathcal{L}}^{{j}}_{s}h(w)}{w}\left(\frac{u}{w}\right)^{n}dw.

(39)

We may therefore define

\widetilde{a}_{{m},s}^{{j}}(h)=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{C_{\rho}}\frac{{\mathcal{L}}^{{j}}_{s}h(w)}{w^{{m}+1}}dw,

\widetilde{v}_{{m},s}^{{j}}(u)=u^{{m}},

(40)

leading to

a_{{m},s}^{{j}}=\frac{\widetilde{a}_{{m},s}^{{j}}}{\|\widetilde{a}^{{j}}_{{m},s}\|},\ v^{{j}}_{{m},s}(u)=\frac{\widetilde{v}_{{m},s}^{{j}}}{\|\widetilde{v}_{{m},s}^{{j}}\|},

\lambda_{{m},s}^{{j}}=\|\widetilde{a}^{{j}}_{{m},s}\|\|\widetilde{v}^{{j}}_{{m},s}\|.

(41)

These terms give a valid nuclear decomposition for
{\mathcal{L}}^{{j}}_{s}
(we will check in a moment that the
\lambda^{{j}}_{{m},s}
satisfy the bounded series condition).

We would like to obtain estimates on the
\lambda^{{j}}_{{m},s}
. Since the norm on
\widetilde{v}^{{j}}_{{m},s}
is the supremum norm on
D_{{j}}
,
\|\widetilde{v}^{{j}}_{{m},s}\|=\|u^{{m}}\|
is clearly
1
. For
\|\widetilde{a}_{{m},s}^{{j}}\|
, we may take arbitrary
h\in A_{\infty}(D)
satisfying
\|h\|\leq 1
and apply a supremum bound:

|\widetilde{a}_{{m},s}^{{j}}(h)|=\left|\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{C_{\rho}}\frac{{\mathcal{L}}_{s}^{{j}}h(w)}{w^{{m}+1}}dw\right|\leq\rho^{-{m}}\|{\mathcal{L}}^{{j}}_{s}h\|_{L^{\infty}(C_{\rho})}.

(42)

Recall that

{\mathcal{L}}^{{j}}_{s}h(u)=\sum_{{k}:{j}\to{k}}V_{s}(f_{{jk}}(u))h(f_{{jk}}(u)).

(43)

By trivalency, the sum consists of two terms, so it suffices to bound each term individually. Furthermore,
|h(f_{{jk}}(u))|\leq 1
by assumption. On the other hand,

V_{s}(f_{{jk}}(u))=(f^{-1}(f_{{jk}}(u))^{-s}=f_{{jk}}^{\prime}(u)^{s}.

For
u\in C_{\rho}
, we have by Lemma
4.6
that
|f^{\prime}_{{jk}}(u)|{{}^{s}}=O(e^{L|\mathrm{Re}(s)|})
. Therefore

\|{\mathcal{L}}^{{j}}_{s}h\|_{L^{\infty}(C_{\rho})}=O(e^{L{|\mathrm{Re}(s)|}}).

As a result, we may plug in
\rho={e^{(1-\varepsilon)\eta}}
and obtain

|\lambda^{{j}}_{{m},s}|={O(e^{-{m}{(1-\varepsilon)\eta}}e^{L{|\mathrm{Re}(s)|}})}.\text{ $\square$}

We therefore have the following nuclear decomposition of
{\mathcal{L}}_{s}
:

{\mathcal{L}}_{s}h=\sum_{{j}=1}^{2{d}}\sum_{{m}=0}^{\infty}\lambda^{{j}}_{{m},s}a^{{j}}_{{m},s}(h)v^{{j}}_{{m},s}

(44)

and the exponential decay of the
\lambda^{{j}}_{{m},s}
in
{m}
obtained as part of Lemma
5.2
guarantees that
\sum_{{j}=1}^{2{d}}\sum_{{m}=0}^{\infty}|\lambda^{{j}}_{{m},s}|<\infty
for any fixed
s
.

5.2
Bounding the Tail

Lemma 5.3
.

Let
X\in\mathcal{M}_{g,n}(L_{1},\dots,L_{n})
be
\eta
-great, assume the
L_{{h}}
are
C
-bounded by
L
, and let
{\mathcal{L}}_{s}
be the transfer operator for the flow-adapted IFS corresponding to
X
. Fix some constant
A
, and complex numbers
s,z
with
|z|\leq A
,
|s|\leq\frac{A}{L}
. Let
\varepsilon>0
, and assume that
\eta
is sufficiently large in terms of
g
,
n
,
C
,
A
, and
\varepsilon
. Then the cycle expansion

\det(1-z{\mathcal{L}}_{s})=1+\sum_{{m}=1}^{\infty}z^{{m}}{c}_{{m}}(s)

(45)

satisfies the truncation bound

\left|\sum_{{m}=2{d}+1}^{\infty}z^{{m}}{c}_{{m}}(s)\right|{=O_{g,n,C,A,{\varepsilon}}(e^{-{(1-\varepsilon)\eta}})}.

(46)

Proof.
Note that our eventual goal is to show we obtain a convergent geometric series, so we need to be somewhat careful with implicit constants.

We begin from Formula
34
, and wish to use Lemma
5.2
to understand the cycle expansion coefficients. To start, fix
{m}
and consider
{c}_{{m}}(s)
. This term is defined via a sum indexed by
2{d}
-tuples of increasing sequences of nonnegative integers

({p}_{1,1}<{p}_{2,1}<\cdots<{p}_{r_{1},1},{p}_{1,2}<{p}_{2,2}<\cdots<{p}_{r_{2},2},\dots,{p}_{1,2k}<\cdots<{p}_{r_{2{d}},2{d}})

satisfying
r_{1}+\cdots+r_{2{d}}={m}
. Take such a tuple. Its contribution consists of a product of the determinant of a
{m}\times{m}
matrix (with all entries bounded by
1
) and the expression

\prod_{{j}=1}^{2{d}}\prod_{{q}=0}^{r_{{j}}}\lambda^{{j}}_{{p}_{{q},{j}},{s}}.

Notice that we allow some
r_{{j}}
to be
0
in this notation.

By the Hadamard bound, the determinant component is bounded in absolute value by
{m}^{{m}/2}
. Furthermore, by Lemma
5.2
, there is some constant
C_{1}
dependent only on
g
,
n
,
C
,
A
, and
\varepsilon
so that

\prod_{{j}=1}^{2{d}}\prod_{{q}=0}^{r_{{j}}}|\lambda^{{j}}_{{p}_{{q},{j}},s}|\leq\prod_{{j}=1}^{2{d}}\prod_{{q}=0}^{r_{{j}}}{C_{1}}e^{-{p}_{{q},{j}}{(1-\varepsilon)\eta}+L|\mathrm{Re}(s)|}

=C_{1}^{m}e^{{m}L|\mathrm{Re}(s)|}\prod_{{j}=1}^{2{d}}\prod_{{q}=0}^{r_{{j}}}e^{-{p}_{{q},{j}}{(1-\varepsilon)\eta}}.

(47)

Therefore, we have the following bound for
{m}\geq 1
:

|{c}_{{m}}(s)|{\leq}{C_{1}^{m}}{m}^{{m}/2}e^{{m}L|\mathrm{Re}(s)|}\sum_{*}\prod_{{j}=1}^{2{d}}\prod_{{q}=0}^{r_{{j}}}e^{-{p}_{{q},{j}}{(1-\varepsilon)\eta}}

(48)

where the sum is over all tuples

({p}_{1,1}<\cdots<{p}_{r_{1},1},\dots,{p}_{1,2k}<\cdots<{p}_{r_{2{d}},2{d}})

such that
r_{1}+\cdots+r_{2{d}}={m}
. To handle the sum, we first split it into two, fixing
r_{1},\dots,r_{2{d}}
in the first sum:

{\sum_{*}\prod_{{j}=1}^{2{d}}\prod_{{q}=0}^{r_{{j}}}e^{-{p}_{{q},{j}}{(1-\varepsilon)\eta}}=}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}r_{1},\dots,r_{2{d}}:\\
r_{1}+\cdots+r_{2{d}}={m}\end{subarray}}\sum_{*^{\prime}}\prod_{{j}=1}^{2{d}}\prod_{{q}=0}^{r_{{j}}}e^{-{p}_{{q},{j}}{(1-\varepsilon)\eta}}.

(49)

This expression becomes

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}r_{1},\dots,r_{2{d}}:\\
r_{1}+\cdots+r_{2{d}}={m}\end{subarray}}\prod_{{j}=1}^{2{d}}\sum_{{p}_{1,{j}}<\cdots<{p}_{r_{{j}},{j}}}e^{-({p}_{1,{j}}+\cdots+{p}_{r_{{j}},{j}}){(1-\varepsilon)\eta}}.

(50)

Let
\theta_{r}({t})
count the number of strictly increasing sequences
{p}_{1}<\cdots<{p}_{r}
satisfying
{p}_{1}+\cdots+{p}_{r}={t}
, so that

\sum_{{p}_{1,{j}}<\cdots<{p}_{r_{{j}},{j}}}e^{-({p}_{1,{j}}+\cdots+{p}_{r_{{j}},{j}})\eta/2}=\sum_{{t}=0}^{\infty}\theta_{r_{{j}}}({t})e^{-{t}{(1-\varepsilon)\eta}}.

(51)

The quantity
\theta_{r}({t})
is bounded by the number of
k
-partite partitions
{p}_{1}+\cdots+{p}_{r}={t}
(without the strictly increasing condition), which is in turn bounded above by
{t}^{r}
. Furthermore, the strictly increasing condition implies that
\theta_{{r}}({t})=0
for
{t}<{\frac{r^{2}+r}{2}:=T(r)}
. So

|{c}_{{m}}(s)|\leq{C_{1}^{m}}{m}^{{m}/2}e^{{m}L|\mathrm{Re}(s)|}\sum_{{r}_{1}+\cdots+{r}_{2{d}}={m}}\prod_{{j}=1}^{2{d}}\sum_{{t}={T(r_{j})}}^{\infty}{t}^{r_{{j}}}e^{-{t}{(1-\varepsilon)\eta}}.

(52)

To handle the general expression

\sum_{t=T(r)}^{\infty}t^{r}e^{-t{(1-\varepsilon)\eta}}

we first rewrite it as

\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}(t+T(r))^{r}e^{-(t+T(r)){(1-\varepsilon)\eta}}

=e^{-T(r)\eta/2}\sum_{r^{\prime}=0}^{r}\binom{r}{r^{\prime}}T(r)^{r-r^{\prime}}\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}t^{r^{\prime}}e^{-t{(1-\varepsilon)\eta}}

(53)

We can uniformly bound
\binom{r}{r^{\prime}}T(r)^{r-r^{\prime}}
by
r^{4r}
, while
\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}t^{r^{\prime}}e^{-t{(1-\varepsilon)\eta}}
is by standard Gamma function estimates at most
2\frac{r!}{({(1-\varepsilon)\eta})^{r+1}}\leq 2\frac{r^{r}}{({(1-\varepsilon)\eta})^{r+1}}
. For our purposes, the bound
\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}t^{r^{\prime}}e^{-t{(1-\varepsilon)\eta}}\leq 2r^{r}
is sufficient.

Returning to our previous bound on
|c_{m}(s)|
, we obtain

|{c}_{{m}}(s)|\leq{C_{2}^{m}}{m}^{{m}/2}e^{{m}L|\mathrm{Re}(s)|}\sum_{{r}_{1}+\cdots+{r}_{2{d}}={m}}\prod_{{j}=1}^{2{d}}r_{j}^{4r_{j}}e^{-T(r_{j}){(1-\varepsilon)\eta}}

(54)

where
C_{2}
is a different constant depending only on
g
,
n
,
C
,
A
, and
\varepsilon
.

Applying the bound
r_{j}\leq m
leads to a bound of

C_{2}^{m}m^{m/2}e^{mL|\mathrm{Re}(s)|}\sum_{r_{1}+...+r_{2d}=m}\prod_{j=1}^{2d}m^{4r_{j}}e^{-T(r_{j}){(1-\varepsilon)\eta}}

\leq C_{2}^{m}m^{5m}e^{mL|\mathrm{Re}(s)|}\sum_{r_{1}+...+r_{2d}=m}\prod_{j=1}^{2d}e^{-T(r_{j}){(1-\varepsilon)\eta}}

(55)

We now note that for any nonnegative integers
r_{1}+...+r_{2d}=m
we have that some
r_{j}\geq\frac{m}{2d}
, and so

T(r_{1})+...+T(r_{2d})\geq T\left(\frac{m}{2d}\right)\geq\frac{m^{2}}{8d^{2}}.

On the other hand,
T(r)\geq r
for all nonnegative
r
, so

T(r_{1})+...+T(r_{2d})\geq m

We will merge these inequalities by defining a piecewise function

\mathcal{T}(m)=\begin{cases}m&m\leq M\\
\frac{m^{2}}{8d^{2}}&m>M\end{cases}

dependent on some quantity
M
that will be chosen later in the proof. For all
r_{1}+...+r_{2d}=m
, we have
T(r_{1})+...+T(r_{2d})\geq\mathcal{T}(m)
by construction, leading to an upper bound for Equation
55
of

\leq C_{2}^{m}m^{5m}e^{mL|\mathrm{Re}(s)|}e^{-\mathcal{T}(m){(1-\varepsilon)\eta}}\sum_{r_{1}+...+r_{2d}=m}1.

(56)

A standard combinatorial fact states that the number of ordered nonnegative tuples
(r_{1},...,r_{2d})
such that
r_{1}+...+r_{2d}=m
is
\binom{m+2d-1}{2d-1}
, and by standard bounds

\binom{m+2d-1}{2d-1}\leq C_{3}m^{2d}

where
C_{3}:=C_{3}(d)
is a constant depending only on
d
. We thus obtain that

|c_{m}(s)|\leq C_{4}^{m}m^{5m+2d}e^{mL|\mathrm{Re}(s)|}e^{-\mathcal{T}(m){(1-\varepsilon)\eta}}

(57)

for some constant
C_{4}
depending only on
g
,
n
,
C
,
A
, and
\varepsilon
.
Recall that our goal is to bound

\left|\sum_{m=2d+1}^{\infty}z^{m}c_{m}(s)\right|.

Applying
|z|\leq A
and
L|\mathrm{Re}(s)|\leq A
by the assumptions on
z
and
s
, we have

\left|\sum_{m=2d+1}^{\infty}z^{m}c_{m}(s)\right|\leq\sum_{m=2d+1}^{\infty}|z|^{m}|c_{m}(s)|

\leq\sum_{m=2d+1}^{\infty}A^{m}C_{4}^{m}m^{5m+2d}e^{Am}e^{-\mathcal{T}(m){(1-\varepsilon)\eta}}

\leq\sum_{m=2d+1}^{\infty}\left(\frac{(C_{4}Ae^{A})m^{6}}{e^{\mathcal{T}(m){(1-\varepsilon)\eta}m}}\right)^{m}.

(58)

Recall the
M
used to define
\mathcal{T}(m)
, and consider

\sum_{m=M}^{\infty}\left(\frac{(C_{4}Ae^{A})m^{6}}{e^{\mathcal{T}(m){(1-\varepsilon)\eta}m}}\right)^{m}\leq\sum_{m=M}^{\infty}\left(\frac{(C_{4}Ae^{A})m^{6}}{e^{m{(1-\varepsilon)\eta}/4d^{2}}}\right)^{m}.

(59)

Standard asymptotics show that for
\eta
, say, at least
10
, the expression

\frac{(C_{4}Ae^{A})m^{6}}{e^{m{(1-\varepsilon)\eta}/4d^{2}}}

is strictly decreasing for all
m
sufficiently large in terms of
g
,
n
,
C
,
A
, and
\varepsilon
(and hence sufficiently large in terms of
d
). As a result, there is some
M_{0}
dependent only on
g
,
n
,
C
,
A
, and
\varepsilon
so that for
M\geq M_{0}
, we have by a standard geometric series bound that

\sum_{m=M}^{\infty}\left(\frac{(C_{4}Ae^{A})m^{6}}{e^{m{(1-\varepsilon)\eta}/4d^{2}}}\right)^{m}\leq\sum_{m=M}^{\infty}\left(\frac{(C_{4}Ae^{A})M^{6}}{e^{M{(1-\varepsilon)\eta}/4d^{2}}}\right)^{m}

\leq\frac{(C_{4}Ae^{A})M^{6M}}{e^{M^{2}{(1-\varepsilon)\eta}/4d^{2}}}*\frac{\frac{(C_{4}Ae^{A})M^{6}}{e^{M{(1-\varepsilon)\eta}/4d^{2}}}}{1-\frac{(C_{4}Ae^{A})M^{6}}{e^{M{(1-\varepsilon)\eta}/4d^{2}}}}

(60)

We now can find
M\geq M_{0}
(this will be our chosen value of
M
for the rest of the proof) dependent only on
g
,
n
,
C
,
A
, and
\varepsilon
, and
\eta_{0}
dependent only on
g
,
n
,
C
,
A
, and
\varepsilon
, so that for all
\eta\geq\eta_{0}
,

1.

\frac{(C_{4}Ae^{A})M^{6}}{e^{M{(1-\varepsilon)\eta}/4d^{2}}}\leq\frac{1}{2}
.

2.

\frac{M^{2}}{{4d^{2}}}\geq{\frac{1}{1-\varepsilon}}
.

With these inequalities, for all
\eta\geq\eta_{0}
, Equation
60
is
O_{g,n,C,A,{\varepsilon}}(e^{-\eta})

We now turn to

\sum_{m=2d+1}^{M}\left(\frac{(C_{4}Ae^{A})m^{6}}{e^{\mathcal{T}(m){(1-\varepsilon)\eta}m}}\right)^{m}.

(61)

By definition of
\mathcal{T}(m)
and
M
, this expression is bounded above by

\sum_{m=2d+1}^{M}\left(\frac{(C_{4}Ae^{A})m^{6}}{e^{{(1-\varepsilon)\eta}}}\right)^{m}.

(62)

In turn, we can use
1\leq m\leq M
to apply a simple bound of

\leq M\frac{(C_{r}Ae^{A})^{M}M^{6M}}{e^{{(1-\varepsilon)\eta}}}=O_{g,n,C,A,{\varepsilon}}(e^{-{(1-\varepsilon)\eta}})

(63)

Combining our bounds, we conclude that

\left|\sum_{m=2d+1}^{\infty}z^{m}c_{m}(s)\right|=O_{g,n,C,A,{\varepsilon}}(e^{-{(1-\varepsilon)\eta}}).\ \square

(64)

6
Convergence of Non-Decaying Terms

Having controlled
\sum_{{m}=2{d}+1}^{\infty}z^{{m}}{c}_{{m}}(s)
, we must now handle the rest of the expression. We start with an alternate formula for the cycle expansion terms.

Lemma 6.1

(Jenkinson-Pollicott, Proposition 8
[
14
]
)
.

Fix
X\in\mathcal{M}_{g,n}(L_{1},\dots,L_{n})
, and let
{\mathcal{L}}_{s}
be the transfer operator for the flow-adapted IFS corresponding to
X
. Then the terms of the cycle expansion

\det(1-z{\mathcal{L}}_{s})=1+\sum_{{m}=1}^{\infty}z^{{m}}{c}_{{m}}(s)

(65)

satisfy the formula

{c}_{{m}}(s)=\sum_{{r}=1}^{{m}}\sum_{({p}_{1},\dots,{p}_{{r}})\in P({m},{r})}\frac{(-1)^{{r}}}{{r}!}\prod_{{q}=1}^{{r}}\frac{1}{{p}_{{q}}}\sum_{w\in W^{C}_{{p}_{{q}}}}\frac{e^{-sl(\gamma_{w})}}{1-e^{-l(\gamma_{w})}}

(66)

where
P({m},{t})
is the set of all
{t}
-fold partitions of
{m}
.

An analogous formula holds for graphs:

Lemma 6.2
.

Let
\Gamma
be a finite (undirected) metric graph with
{d}
edges, and construct
J_{s}
as in Subsection
2.1
. For
{p}\geq 1
, let
W^{C}_{{p}}
be the set of all closed words with length
p
in the directed-edge symbolic coding. Then we have the formula

\det(1-zJ_{s})=1+\sum_{{m}=1}^{2{d}}z^{{m}}\widetilde{{c}}_{{m}}(s)

(67)

with

\widetilde{{c}}_{{m}}(s)=\sum_{{r}=1}^{{m}}\sum_{({p}_{1},\dots,{p}_{{r}})\in P({m},{r})}\frac{(-1)^{{r}}}{{r}!}\prod_{{q}=1}^{{r}}\frac{1}{{p}_{{q}}}\sum_{w\in W^{C}_{{p}_{{q}}}}e^{-sl(\widetilde{\gamma}_{w})}.

(68)

Proof.
We will only give a sketch, as the proof is very similar to that of Lemma
6.1
. As a matrix,
J_{s}
has dimension
2{d}\times 2{d}
. So the cycle expansion is a polynomial in
z
with degree at most
2{d}
. To obtain the exact formula, start from the identity

\det(1-zJ_{s})=\textrm{exp}\left(-\sum_{{m}=1}^{\infty}\frac{z^{{m}}}{{m}}\text{tr}(J_{s}^{{m}})\right)

(69)

and observe that
\text{tr}(J_{s}^{{m}})=\sum_{w\in W^{C}_{{m}}}e^{-sl(\widetilde{\gamma}_{w})}
. Then take a power series expansion and group terms.
\square

We aim to compare these two types of terms.

Lemma 6.3
.

Let
X\in\mathcal{M}_{g,n}(L_{1},\dots,L_{n})
be
\eta
-great with the
L_{h}

C
-bounded by
L
, and set
\Gamma=\Phi(X)
. Let
{\mathcal{L}}_{s}
be the transfer operator derived from the flow-adapted IFS corresponding to
X
, and let
J_{s}
be the transfer operator corresponding to
\Gamma
. Define the cycle expansion terms
{c}_{{m}}(s)
and
\widetilde{{c}}_{{m}}(s)
as in Lemmas
6.1
and
6.2
. Fix a particular
{m}\leq 2{d}
, and
z,s\in\mathbb{C}
satisfying
|z|\leq A
,
|s|\leq\frac{A}{L}
for some chosen constant
A
. Then

|{c}_{{m}}(s)-\widetilde{{c}}_{{m}}(s)|={O_{g,n,C,A}(e^{-\eta})}.

(70)

Proof.
From Lemma 4.20 in
[
23
]
(and after accounting for our slightly different definition of
\eta
-greatness), we know that for all
\gamma_{w}
on
X
with word length
{m}
,

l(\widetilde{\gamma}_{w})\leq l(\gamma_{w})\leq l(\widetilde{\gamma}_{w})+O({m}e^{-\eta}).

Now consider

\frac{e^{-sl(\gamma_{w})}}{1-e^{-l(\gamma_{w})}}-e^{-sl(\widetilde{\gamma}_{w})}

=\frac{e^{-sl(\gamma_{w})}-(1-e^{-l(\gamma_{w})})e^{-sl(\widetilde{\gamma}_{w})}}{1-e^{-l(\gamma_{w})}}

(71)

for some word
w
of length
{m}
. Since both
l(\gamma_{w})
and
l(\widetilde{\gamma}_{w})
are bounded below by
\eta
, for
\eta
absolutely large, the denominator is between, say,
1/2
and
2
. Meanwhile, the numerator expands to

e^{-sl(\gamma_{w})}-e^{-sl(\widetilde{\gamma}_{w})}+e^{-sl(\widetilde{\gamma}_{w})-l(\gamma_{w})}.

(72)

This expression factors to

e^{-sl(\gamma_{w})}(1-e^{-s(l(\widetilde{\gamma}_{w})-l(\gamma_{w}))}(1+e^{-l(\gamma_{w})}))

(73)

=e^{-sl(\gamma_{w})}(1-e^{-sO({m}e^{-\eta})}(1+O(e^{-\eta}))).

(74)

Since
l(\gamma_{w})\leq O(L{m})
and
|s|\leq\frac{A}{L}
, we may apply a standard Taylor estimate and obtain a bound on the preceding expression of

O(e^{A{m}})(1-(1+O(A{m}e^{-\eta}/L))(1+O(e^{-\eta})))=O(A{m}e^{A{m}}e^{-\eta}).

As a consequence, we may make the substitution

{c}_{{m}}(s)=\sum_{{r}=1}^{{m}}\sum_{({p}_{1},\dots,{p}_{{r}})\in P({m},{r})}\frac{(-1)^{{r}}}{{r}!}\prod_{{q}=1}^{{r}}\frac{1}{{p}_{{q}}}\sum_{w\in W^{C}_{{p}_{{q}}}}\left(e^{-sl(\widetilde{\gamma}_{w})}+O(A{p}_{{q}}e^{A{p}_{{q}}}e^{-\eta})\right)

=\sum_{{r}=1}^{{m}}\sum_{({p}_{1},\dots,{p}_{{r}})\in P({m},{r})}\frac{(-1)^{{r}}}{{r}!}\prod_{{q}=1}^{{r}}\frac{1}{{p}_{{q}}}\left(\left(\sum_{w\in W^{C}_{{p}_{{q}}}}e^{-sl(\widetilde{\gamma}_{w})}\right)+\left(\sum_{w\in W^{C}_{{p}_{{q}}}}O(A{p}_{{q}}e^{A{p}_{{q}}}e^{-\eta})\right)\right).

(75)

To handle this expression, first note that for any
\widetilde{\gamma}_{w}
of length
{m}
,
l(\widetilde{\gamma}_{w})\leq CLm
, so by
|s|\leq\frac{A}{L}
,

|e^{-sl(\widetilde{\gamma}_{w})}|\leq e^{{C}A{{m}}}.

Furthermore,
|W^{C}_{{p}_{{q}}}|\leq(2{d})^{{p}_{{q}}}\leq(2{d})^{2{d}}
, so

\sum_{w\in W^{C}_{p_{q}}}e^{-sl(\widetilde{\gamma}_{w})}\leq(2d)^{2d}e^{CAm}\leq(2d)^{2d}e^{2CAd}=O_{g,n,C,A}(1).

(76)

By the same logic,

\sum_{w\in W^{C}_{p_{q}}}O(Ap_{q}e^{Ap_{q}}e^{-\eta})\leq O(A(2d)^{2d+1}e^{2Ad}e^{-\eta})=O(e^{-\eta})

(77)

Applying Equation
77
,

c_{m}(s)=\sum_{r=1}^{m}\sum_{(p_{1},...,p_{r})\in P(m,r)}\frac{(-1)^{r}}{r!}\prod_{q=1}^{r}\frac{1}{p_{q}}\left(\left(\sum_{w\in W^{C}_{p_{q}}}e^{-sl(\widetilde{\gamma}_{w})}\right)+O(e^{-\eta})\right)

(78)

From here, expanding and applying Equation
76
,

\prod_{q=1}^{r}\frac{1}{p_{q}}\left(\left(\sum_{w\in W^{C}_{p_{q}}}e^{-sl(\widetilde{\gamma}_{w})}\right)+O(e^{-\eta})\right)

\leq\left(\prod_{q=1}^{r}\sum_{w\in W^{C}_{p_{q}}}e^{-sl(\widetilde{\gamma}_{w})}\right)+\sum_{q^{\prime}=1}^{r}\binom{r}{q^{\prime}}O(1)^{r-q^{\prime}}O(e^{-\eta})^{q^{\prime}}

(79)

where in the second term the
\frac{1}{p_{q}}
terms have been dropped, as this term will be an error term and all
\frac{1}{p_{q}}
are at most
1
.
As
q^{\prime}\geq 1
in the latter sum and
r
and
q^{\prime}
are bounded in terms of
g
and
n
, this expression becomes

\left(\prod_{q=1}^{r}\sum_{w\in W^{C}_{p_{q}}}e^{-sl(\widetilde{\gamma}_{w})}\right)+O(e^{-\eta}).

(80)

Therefore

c_{m}(s)=\sum_{r=1}^{m}\sum_{(p_{1},...,p_{r})\in P(m,r)}\frac{(-1)^{r}}{r!}\left(\left(\prod_{q=1}^{r}\sum_{w\in W^{C}_{p_{q}}}e^{-sl(\widetilde{\gamma}_{w})}\right)+O(e^{-\eta})\right).

(81)

Recalling the definition of
\widetilde{c}_{m}(s)
, this expression simplifies to

c_{m}(s)=\widetilde{c}_{m}(s)+\sum_{r=1}^{m}\sum_{(p_{1},...,p_{r})\in P(m,r)}\frac{(-1)^{r}}{r!}O(e^{-\eta}).

(82)

As
P(m,r)\leq(2d)^{2d}
, it is now straightforward to verify that

\sum_{r=1}^{m}\sum_{(p_{1},...,p_{r})\in P(m,r)}\frac{(-1)^{r}}{r!}O_{g,n,C,A}(e^{-\eta})=O_{g,n,C,A}(e^{-\eta}).

(83)

We conclude that

c_{m}(s)-\widetilde{c}_{m}(s)|=O_{g,n,C,A}(e^{-\eta}).\ \square

(84)

Proof of Theorem
1.3
Assume
\Gamma
has
{d}
edges, and recall that
{d}=O_{g,n}(1)
. Take cycle expansions and apply Lemmas
5.3
and
6.3
. Then:

|d(s,z)-\widetilde{d}(s,z)|

=\left|\sum_{{m}=1}^{\infty}z^{{m}}{c}_{{m}}(s)-\sum_{{m}=1}^{2{m}}z^{{m}}\widetilde{{c}}_{{m}}(s)\right|

Proof of Corollary
1.5
.
Recall that
MRG_{g,n}
has a natural cell complex structure, where cells correspond to topological ribbon graph isomorphism classes of ribbon graphs with genus
g
and
n
boundaries. The assumption that
\Gamma
is trivalent further implies that
\Gamma
lies in the interior of a top-dimensional cell, so for sufficiently large
{t}
, all
\frac{1}{\alpha_{{t}}}\Gamma_{{t}}
have the same topological type as
\Gamma
. Furthermore, the topology on
MRG_{g,n}
implies that the edge lengths of these
\frac{1}{\alpha_{{t}}}\Gamma_{{t}}
converge to the lengths of the corresponding edges of
\Gamma
. Formally, let
\varepsilon_{1},...,\varepsilon_{2d}
be the directed edges of
\Gamma
(as in Subsection
2.1
), and for sufficiently large
t
, let
\varepsilon_{1,t},...,\varepsilon_{2d,t}
be the corresponding edges of
\Gamma_{t}
. Then for each
\varepsilon_{j}
,
\lim_{t\to\infty}\frac{l(\varepsilon_{j,t})}{\alpha_{t}}\to l(\varepsilon_{j})
. As a consequence, if
\Gamma_{t}\in MRG_{g,n}(L_{1,t},...,L_{n,t})
, then each sequence
(L_{h,t})
satisfies
\lim_{t\to\infty}\frac{L_{h,t}}{\alpha_{t}}\to L_{h}
.

Therefore, there is some
T_{0}
so that for any fixed
X_{t}
with
t\geq T_{0}
, the
L_{h,t}
are, say, bounded above by
2\max(L_{1},...,L_{n})\alpha_{t}
. Furthermore, if
E
is defined as the minimum edge length of
\Gamma
, then there exists a
T_{1}
so that for any fixed
X_{t}
with
t\geq T_{1}
, every edge of
\Gamma_{t}
has length at least
\frac{E}{2}\alpha_{t}
, and it follows that
X_{t}
is
\frac{E}{2}\alpha_{t}
-great.

Recall that
U
is a bounded domain by assumption; we will restrict to points
(s,z)\in\mathbb{C}^{2}
with
s\in U
and
|z|\leq 2
. Let
J_{s}
be the matrix derived from
\Gamma
as in Subsection
2.1
, and let
J_{s,t}
be the corresponding matrix for
\frac{1}{\alpha_{t}}\Gamma_{t}
. Then by the edge length limits given above and by Equation
9
, we have that
\lim_{t\to\infty}J_{s,t}\to J_{s}
uniformly for
s\in U
, where the matrix convergence is entrywise. From Equation
10
, this limit is sufficient to conclude that the dynamical zeta functions
\widetilde{d}_{\frac{1}{\alpha_{{t}}}\Gamma_{{t}}}(s,z)
converge uniformly in
s
and
z
to
\widetilde{d}_{\Gamma}(s,z)
.

As
\alpha_{{t}}\to\infty
, Theorem
1.3
and the fact that
X_{t}
is
\frac{E}{2}\alpha_{t}
-great implies that
\sup_{|z|\leq 2,s\in\frac{1}{\alpha_{{t}}}U}|d_{X_{{t}}}(z,s)-\widetilde{d}_{\Gamma_{{t}}}(z,s)|
converges to
0
as
{t}\to\infty
. Since
\widetilde{d}_{\Gamma_{{t}}}(z,s)
and
\widetilde{d}_{\frac{1}{\alpha_{{t}}}\Gamma_{{t}}}(z,s)
are related by the easily verified formula

\widetilde{d}_{\Gamma_{{t}}}(z,\alpha_{{t}}s)=\widetilde{d}_{\frac{1}{\alpha_{{t}}}\Gamma_{{t}}}(z,s)

(86)

we may conclude that

\lim_{{t}\to\infty}\sup_{|z|\leq 2,s\in U}|d_{X_{{t}}}(z,\alpha_{{t}}s)-\widetilde{d}_{\Gamma_{{t}}}(z,s)|=0

(87)

Recalling the definition of
\mathrm{Res}(X_{{t}},\alpha_{{t}})
, the desired result now follows immediately from an application of Rouché’s Theorem.
\square
