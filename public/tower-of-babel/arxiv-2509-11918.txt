Extraction of Electron and Hole Drift Velocities in thin 4H-SiC PIN Detectors using High-Frequency Readout Electronics
Andreas Gsponer*, Sebastian Onder, Stefan Gundacker, Jürgen Burin, Matthias Knopf, Daniel Radmanovac, Simon Waid \orcidG, Thomas Bergauer \orcidH

Abstract
Silicon carbide (SiC) has been widely adopted in the semiconductor industry, particularly in power electronics, because of its high temperature stability, high breakdown field, and fast switching speeds. Its wide bandgap makes it an interesting candidate for radiation-hard particle detectors in high-energy physics and medical applications. Furthermore, the high electron and hole drift velocities in 4H-SiC enable devices suitable for ultra-fast particle detection and timing applications. However, currently, the front-end readout electronics used for 4H-SiC detectors constitute a bottleneck in investigations of the charge carrier drift. To address these limitations, a high-frequency readout board with an intrinsic bandwidth of 10 GHz 10\text{\,}\mathrm{GHz} was developed. With this readout, the transient current signals of a 4H-SiC diode with a diameter of 141 µ ​ m 141\text{\,}\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolMicro m} and a thickness of 50 µ ​ m 50\text{\,}\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolMicro m} upon UV-laser, alpha particle, and high-energy proton beam excitation were recorded. In all three cases, the electron and hole drift can clearly be separated, which enables the extraction of the charge carrier drift velocities as a function of the electric field. These velocities, for the first time directly measured, provide a valuable comparison to Monte-Carlo simulated literature values and constitute an essential input for TCAD simulations. Finally, a complete simulation environment combining TCAD, the Allpix 2 framework, and SPICE simulations is presented, in good agreement with the measured data.

1
Introduction

Silicon carbide (SiC) is an attractive candidate for future detectors in particle physics experiments due to its wide bandgap and, therefore, extremely low dark currents, even after irradiation and at room temperature
Gaggl et al. 2022
;
Gsponer et al. 2023
.
The 4H-SiC polytype (with a bandgap of
3.26\text{\,}\mathrm{eV}
) has been investigated as a radiation detector material for over 60 years
Babcock 1965
;
Nava et al. 2008
;
De Napoli 2022
and has recently received renewed interest in the community due to manufacturing advances in the power electronics industry.
Since the 1970s, the charge carrier drift properties inside semiconductors have been studied by injecting charge carriers and evaluating the transient currents
Jungclaussen
;
Zulliger et al.
, for example, to extract drift velocities as a function of the electric field and temperature
Canali et al.
.
In the last 30 years, this technique has also been applied very successfully to investigate solid-state detectors in high-energy physics, characterizing radiation damage-induced changes of the electric field in detectors
Eremin et al.
;
Li et al.
;
Kramberger et al. 2010
, and the term transient-current-technique (TCT) has been coined.
The material parameters of 4H-SiC are not as well known as for silicon, and significant uncertainties still exist, for example, in the high-field mobility models (especially for holes)
Burin et al. 2025
.
One shortcoming of 4H-SiC is currently the limited epitaxial thicknesses that can be grown and depleted, typically
100\text{\,}\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolMicro m}
or less
De Napoli 2022
.
Combined with an electron saturation drift velocity that is approximately twice as high as for silicon, this results in extremely fast signals.
Therefore, in order to characterize the transient current in 4H-SiC, much faster readout electronics than currently used to characterize silicon detectors are required.

This paper presents a novel readout scheme using a monolithic microwave integrated circuit (MMIC) based low-noise amplifier adapted to read out
50\text{\,}\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolMicro m}
thick 4H-SiC samples with low capacitance.
The maximum readout bandwidth of
10\text{\,}\mathrm{GHz}
makes it possible to resolve the transient current induced by charge carrier drift inside the detector for the first time experimentally.
Charge carriers were injected with different ionization profiles, using a
370\text{\,}\mathrm{nm}
pulsed UV-laser, alpha particles, and proton beams at a medical synchrotron (MedAustron), and the transient currents are compared.

2
Materials and Methods

In solid-state detector applications, the quest for higher readout bandwidths has been driven mainly by timing applications, such as silicon low-gain avalanche diodes (LGADs)
Sadrozinski et al. 2014
;
Allaire et al. 2018
or silicon photomultipliers (SiPMs)
Gundacker et al. 2019
;
Krake et al. 2022
.
However, timing applications do not necessarily call for the highest possible bandwidth, but instead limit the bandwidth to the frequency content of the detector signal in order to achieve the best slope-to-noise ratio and time resolution.
If, however, the signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) is sufficiently high, for example, when a large amount of charge is deposited (greater than that of a minimum ionizing particle) or when waveform averaging is used, the readout bandwidth can be further increased and the transient charge carrier drift can be resolved in detail.

Typical silicon-based timing detectors in high-energy physics applications have capacitances of
1
-
2\text{\,}\mathrm{pF}
and a readout bandwidth below
2\text{\,}\mathrm{GHz}
, in accordance with the length of the detector signal
Allaire et al. 2018
.
To increase the bandwidth (and decrease signal rise-times), either the detector capacitance or the input impedance of the amplifier has to be reduced.
For the latter, approaches using shunt-resistors exist, reducing the input impedance to
10\text{\,}\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolOhm}

Schumm, Bruce et al. 2024
.
However, resistances smaller than this are not practically feasible at
\mathrm{GHz}
frequencies, and most of the signal is lost through the shunt.
Another way to reduce the input impedance is to use active feedback.
Nevertheless, the bandwidths that can be achieved in this approach are limited due to signal transfer delays.
The detector capacitance is a result of the sensor geometry and, in general, for
50\text{\,}\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolMicro m}
thick semiconductor detectors, the electrically active area should be below
0.5\text{\,}\mathrm{mm}
to achieve capacitances of below
1\text{\,}\mathrm{pF}
, which corresponds to signal bandwidths above
3\text{\,}\mathrm{GHz}
for an input impedance of
50\text{\,}\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolOhm}
.

The following sections describe the 4H-SiC sensor used in the measurements, the high-frequency readout design, and a Monte-Carlo simulation framework based on technology computer-aided design (TCAD), Allpix
2
, and SPICE simulations to model the measured transient signals.

Silicon Carbide Sensors

350\text{\,}\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolMicro m}
substrate.
An image of the sensor cross-section is shown in Figure
1
.
Diodes with a small area (
0.016\text{\,}\mathrm{mm}
) were chosen to achieve a very low capacitance and to maximize the achievable bandwidth.

shows an image of the diode undergoing electrical characterization and the measured capacitance as a function of the bias voltage.

2.2
High-Frequency Readout Electronics

The core of the readout is an MMIC-based low noise amplifier (PMA3-14LN+) from Mini-Circuits, with a bandwidth of
50\text{\,}\mathrm{MHz}
-
10\text{\,}\mathrm{GHz}
, a flat gain of
22.6\pm 7\text{\,}\mathrm{dB}
and a low noise figure of around
1.1\text{\,}\mathrm{dB}
in the frequency range of interest
Mini-Circuits 2025
.
A printed circuit board (PCB) based on Rogers 4350B substrate was designed to accommodate the MMIC, depicted in Figure
3
.
The detector capacitance and inductance of the wire bonds form a low-pass filter defined by the amplifier’s input impedance, limiting the bandwidth that can be achieved.
Furthermore, the inductance of the wire bond (typically
1\text{\,}\mathrm{nH}
per mm wire length) can create a damped oscillator with the detector capacitance.
For the detector used in the measurements, this resonance frequency is estimated to be around
9\text{\,}\mathrm{GHz}
and can negatively influence the signal transients. These effects will be investigated in more detail with dedicated SPICE simulations in Section
2.3.3
.

The detector is wire bonded to a transmission line, where a
10\text{\,}\mathrm{nF}
capacitor and a
100\text{\,}\mathrm{k\SIUnitSymbolOhm}
resistor form a bias-tee.
The sensor die itself is glued to a high-voltage plane, which is connected to eleven 0805-size
2.2\text{\,}\mathrm{nF}
/
1\text{\,}\mathrm{kV}
SMD capacitors to ground on the back side of the PCB, providing a signal return path as short as possible.
Multiple capacitors are used in parallel to minimize the effects of parasitic inductance.
All measurements were performed with a Rohde&Schwarz RTP164 oscilloscope at a sample rate of
40\text{\,}\mathrm{GSa}\text{/}\mathrm{s}
and an analog bandwidth of
16\text{\,}\mathrm{GHz}
.
The measured voltage pulses
V(t)
were converted to current pulses using
I(t)={V(t)}/{(A\cdot$50\text{\,}\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolOhm}$)}
, assuming an amplification of
A=$13.5$
(
22.6\text{\,}\mathrm{dB}
) and a
50\text{\,}\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolOhm}
input impedance of the MMIC.

2.2.1
Feature Extraction

The transient current induced by the charge carrier drift between two electrodes can be defined by the Shockley-Ramo theorem
Shockley 1938
;
Ramo 1939

I=q\cdot\vec{v}\cdot\vec{E_{w}}\,,

(1)

where
q
is the charge,
\vec{v}
the drift velocity, and
\vec{E_{w}}
the gradient of the
weighting potential
.
For selective electron/hole injection at the top/bottom of the device, the resulting transient current will be a rectangular pulse, which can be directly used to measure the drift time (known as the ”time-of-flight” technique)
Martini et al. 1972
.
However, for very thin detectors, selectively injecting electrons or holes using ionizing radiation is challenging.
For example,
5.5\text{\,}\mathrm{MeV}
alpha particles already penetrate more than
15\text{\,}\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolMicro m}
into 4H-SiC
Gsponer et al.
.

Instead, a uniform charge deposition along the depth of the detector can be used.
For a constant electric field (i.e., constant drift velocity), the transient current will then be of triangular shape for the electrons and holes, respectively
Kramberger 2015
.
The electron and hole drift times (
t_{e}
and
t_{h}
) can thus be extracted by linear fits of the two falling slopes and their zero crossings, as depicted in Figure
4
.
For bias voltages
V
well above the full depletion voltage (where a constant electric field
E=V/d
can be assumed), the charge carrier drift velocities can be calculated according to
v_{e,h}(E)=d/t_{e,h}(E)
, with
d=$50\text{\,}\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolMicro m}$
being the epitaxial thickness of the detector.
The electron and hole mobilities are then finally obtained by
\mu_{e,h}(E)=v_{e,h}(E)/E
.

2.3
Simulation Framework

In order to verify the results and compare the measured transient currents with theoretical models, electric field simulations in Sentaurus TCAD, Monte-Carlo simulations in Allpix
2
, and SPICE simulations using QUCS-S have been performed.

2.3.1
TCAD Simulations

In a first step, the 4H-SiC diode was simulated using Synopsis Sentaurus TCAD in order to extract electrostatic potentials and electric field profiles intended for import into the Allpix
2
simulations.
The electric fields were simulated using a quasi 1-D geometry using the device depth and a width of
1\text{\,}\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolMicro m}
, as depicted in Figure
5
(a).
By omitting edge termination structures, which have a negligible effect on the electric field in the center of the diode, computation time could be significantly reduced.
The p
++
implant was specified using measurements performed by the manufacturer, and the doping concentration of the intrinsic epitaxial layer was extracted from the C-V measurements (see Figure
2
(b)).
At the bottom of the geometry, a
1\text{\,}\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolMicro m}
thick buffer layer with a doping concentration of
1\text{\times}{10}^{18}\text{\,}\mathrm{cm}
was added in the form of a box profile.
The simulation setup, including material parameters, material anisotropy, and solving criteria, matches previously published simulation work done by the authors
Gaggl 2023
;
Onder et al. 2025
.
From the simulations, the electrostatic potentials and corresponding electric fields at bias voltages from
0\text{\,}\mathrm{V}
up to
1100\text{\,}\mathrm{V}
were calculated.
Selected electric field distributions along a cut across the detector thickness are shown in Figure
5
, showing non-linear electric fields due to the non-constant epitaxial doping profiles.

2.3.2
Allpix
2
Simulations

In order to simulate the response of the detector to ionizing radiation (and to take into account the stochastic energy loss), the Allpix
2
framework
Spannagel et al. 2018
was used.
The electric fields and the electrostatic potentials from the TCAD simulations were exported, converted, and subsequently imported into Allpix
2
.
The weighting field was calculated by subtracting the electrostatic potential from two simulations with a
1\text{\,}\mathrm{V}
difference.

For the simulation of alpha particles hitting the sensor, the
DepositionGeant4
module was used, while the UV-laser signals were simulated using the
DepositionLaser
module.
To simulate the laser with a wavelength of
370\text{\,}\mathrm{nm}
, an absorption coefficient of
42.25\text{\,}\mathrm{c}\mathrm{m}^{-1}

Sridhara et al. 1998
was used, with a cylindrical beam geometry and a pulse duration of
25\text{\,}\mathrm{ps}
(approximately
58\text{\,}\mathrm{ps}
FWHM).

100\,000
photons were simulated, and the resulting signal was scaled to match the magnitude of the measurements.

Charge carrier propagation was performed using the
TransientPropagation
module, using
0.5\text{\,}\mathrm{ps}
time steps and charge carriers propagated in groups of 100 per step.
Finally, the charge carrier transport parameters of 4H-SiC were implemented in Allpix
2
using a custom mobility model corresponding to the Caughey-Thomas formula:

\isPreprints

\mu(E)=\frac{\mu_{\mathrm{low}}}{\left(1+\left(\frac{\mu_{\mathrm{low}}E}{v_{\mathrm{sat}}}\right)^{\beta}\right)^{1/\beta}}\,,

(2)

\isPreprints

with a fixed low field mobility
\mu_{\mathrm{low}}
of
950\text{\,}\mathrm{cm}
for electrons and
115\text{\,}\mathrm{cm}
for holes
Ishikawa et al. 2023
;
Ishikawa et al. 2024
.
The saturation velocity
v_{\mathrm{sat}}
and the empirical coefficient
\beta
are based on the measured parameters (see Section
3.4
).
4H-SiC features an anisotropy (parallel/perpendicular to the
c
-axis) in many of its material parameters, including the mobility
Burin et al. 2025
.
As the epitaxial layer of the detector is grown
4\text{\,}\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolDegree}
off-axis (to ensure polytype control), and the drift of the charge carriers is primarily in one axis (along the detector thickness), the mobility parallel to the
c
-axis was used.

2.3.3
QUCS simulations

QUCS (quite universal circuit simulator)
Qucs-S 2025
is an open-source SPICE (simulation program with integrated circuit emphasis) software.
In QUCS, the circuit schematic (shown in Figure

4\text{\,}\mathrm{nH}
.
The detector capacitance was specified to be
75\text{\,}\mathrm{fF}
, according to the C-V measurements in Figure

.
The attenuation of the SMA cable connecting the readout electronics to the oscilloscope was phenomenologically modeled by including a
2\text{\,}\mathrm{pF}
capacitance parallel to the
50\text{\,}\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolOhm}
input impedance of the oscilloscope.
The input current
\mathrm{I_{det}}
, provided by the TCAD and Allpix
2
simulations described in the previous chapters, was included in QUCS via a file-based current source.

3
Results

3.1
Pulsed UV-Laser

A PILAS PIL1-037-40FC picosecond pulsed laser with a wavelength of
370\text{\,}\mathrm{nm}
was used to inject charges inside the metallization opening in between the pad and the guard ring (compare with Figure
1
), with a 1-
\sigma
spot size of around
28\text{\,}\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolMicro m}
.
The attenuation length of 4H-SiC for this wavelength is around
240\text{\,}\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolMicro m}

Sridhara et al. 1998
, which implies that photons are absorbed (and electron hole pairs created) almost uniformly along the thickness of the epitaxial layer.
The FWHM of the laser pulse was measured by the manufacturer to be
58\text{\,}\mathrm{ps}
.
Additionally, the laser provides a very low jitter trigger output (verified to be less than
4\text{\,}\mathrm{ps}
relative to the laser pulse), which has been used to trigger the oscilloscope and average
1000
waveforms per acquisition, reducing noise.
By changing the trigger threshold, the acquisition can be shifted in time, allowing the real-time oscilloscope to be used as a synthetic sampling oscilloscope with a sample rate in excess of
40\text{\,}\mathrm{GSa}\text{/}\mathrm{s}
.

Figure

At the maximum of the waveform, a ”double-peak” can be observed, which can be attributed to a resonance of the wire bond inductance (discussed in further detail in Section

The amplitude of the signals increases even after full depletion is reached (around

3.2
Proton Beams at MedAustron

The MedAustron ion therapy center offers proton beams in the energy range between
62.4\text{\,}\mathrm{MeV}
and
800\text{\,}\mathrm{MeV}
.
In order to obtain the highest charge deposition in the detector, the lowest proton energy was chosen, corresponding to an energy loss of around
5
minimum ionizing particles (MIPs).
For the measurements, an additional
22\text{\,}\mathrm{dB}
ZX60-14LN-S+ low-noise amplifier from Mini-Circuits was used as a second stage to boost the signals above the noise floor of the oscilloscope.
Waveforms were recorded by splitting the signal into two channels, one channel recording the signal at the full analog bandwidth of
16\text{\,}\mathrm{GHz}
and the other serving as a trigger using a
500\text{\,}\mathrm{MHz}
low-pass filter.
For each bias voltage between
100\text{\,}\mathrm{V}
and
1.1\text{\,}\mathrm{kV}
,
10\,000
acquisitions were obtained.

Figure
8
shows a histogram of the transient current waveform amplitude, proportional to the sum of the electron and hole drift velocity.
Due to stochastic fluctuations in the interaction of ionizing radiation, the energy loss of the protons follows a
Landau
distribution, with a most probable value of around
120\text{\,}\mathrm{keV}
(or
15\text{\,}\mathrm{ke}

Gsponer et al.
) in
50\text{\,}\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolMicro m}
4H-SiC.
At a bias voltage of
1\text{\,}\mathrm{kV}
, an SNR of around
5
is achieved.

The shape of the transient current waveforms is strongly affected by fluctuations and non-uniformities in the energy loss of protons, and in order to compare the observed charge carrier drift to UV-TCT measurements, waveform averaging is required.
However, due to the poor SNR, the rising edge of the signals cannot be aligned sufficiently accurately to prevent a ”smearing-out” of the signals.
To overcome this limitation, waveform averaging was performed using only a subset of waveforms from the high SNR tail of the energy deposition distribution, as indicated by the gray shaded area (SNR ¿ 10) in Figure
8
.
Depending on the bias voltage, this resulted in about
500
waveforms available for averaging.
This ”cherry-picking” detaches the information about the amplitude of the waveforms, which was recovered by scaling the averaged waveforms by the most-probable prompt current obtained from histograms.

As expected, a good agreement with the data obtained using a UV-laser is observed.

3.3
Alpha Particles

Finally, measurements were performed using
5.5\text{\,}\mathrm{MeV}
alpha particles from a
{}^{241}\mathrm{Am}
source in vacuum.
Alpha particles are readily absorbed in 4H-SiC, depositing their charge in the first
15\text{\,}\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolMicro m}
of the detector.
Figure
10
shows the measured transient currents as a function of the bias voltage.
For each voltage, only around
100
waveforms were averaged, as the acquisition rate was limited by the small active area of the detector and the low activity (
1\text{\,}\mathrm{kBq}
) of the radioactive source.
At high bias voltages, the waveforms are shorter than
500\text{\,}\mathrm{ps}
(or
300\text{\,}\mathrm{ps}
FWHM), as the slower holes only need to drift a short distance to the anode at the top of the device, compared to traversing the entire device thickness in the case of a uniform charge deposition.
This results in an almost rectangular current pulse, as expected by a selective injection of electrons, in clear contrast to the triangular pulses obtained for UV-TCT or proton beams.

4
Discussion

Although the nominal bandwidth of the LNA MMIC is
10\text{\,}\mathrm{GHz}
, the detector capacitance and parasitic impedances can reduce the bandwidth.
Together with the input impedance of the MMIC (
50\text{\,}\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolOhm}
), the detector capacitance forms an
RC
low-pass filter.
However, with the detector capacitance of
75\text{\,}\mathrm{fF}
, the cut-off frequency of this filter is well above
10\text{\,}\mathrm{GHz}
.
Furthermore, together with the detector capacitance, the wire bond inductance of
4\text{\,}\mathrm{nH}
will form an
LC
-filter, with a pole at
1/(2\pi\sqrt{LC})\approx$9.2\text{\,}\mathrm{GHz}$
.
Damped oscillations at this frequency are visible in simulations and measurements, e.g., Figure
12
.
Furthermore, the inductance of the bond wire results in a limited effective bandwidth of
6\text{\,}\mathrm{GHz}
, calculated from a
10\text{\,}\mathrm{\%}
–
90\text{\,}\mathrm{\%}
rise time of
58\text{\,}\mathrm{ps}
.

In the UV-TCT laser measurements, charge was injected between the pad and the charge collection (guard) ring.
As this ring was left floating, the electric potential is expected to be identical to the pad (contact of the diode), therefore resulting in a uniform electric field.
As a result, these measurements are expected to yield accurate results for the charge carrier drift velocities, even though the charge is not injected directly in the center of the device.
As a cross-check, the analysis of charge carrier drift velocities based on the transient current waveforms was also performed for data obtained using a beam of
62.4\text{\,}\mathrm{MeV}
protons.
Here, saturation velocities of
1.54\pm 2\text{\times}{10}^{7}\text{\,}\mathrm{cm}\text{\,}{\mathrm{s}}^{-1}
for electrons and
0.55\pm 4\text{\times}{10}^{7}\text{\,}\mathrm{cm}\text{\,}{\mathrm{s}}^{-1}
for holes were obtained, with
\beta_{e}=$1.01\pm 2$
and
\beta_{h}=$1.36\pm 3$
.
If
\beta
is fixed to the values obtained from UV-TCT measurements (see Table
1
), the obtained saturation velocities are
1.58\pm 2\text{\times}{10}^{7}\text{\,}\mathrm{cm}\text{\,}{\mathrm{s}}^{-1}
for electrons and
0.68\pm 4\text{\times}{10}^{7}\text{\,}\mathrm{cm}\text{\,}{\mathrm{s}}^{-1}
for holes.
Both approaches are comparable to the UV-TCT measurement, albeit with slightly higher drift velocities for electrons and slightly lower for holes.
It is also possible that the error bars of the drift velocities extracted using the proton data are larger, because the transient current waveform in the proton beam measurements is estimated by averaging a small signal subset.

5
Conclusion

A high-frequency readout capable of resolving the transient current in
50\text{\,}\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolMicro m}
thin 4H-SiC PiN diodes was developed and applied to measurements using a pulsed UV laser, alpha particles, and a
62.4\text{\,}\mathrm{MeV}
proton beam.
The high effective readout bandwidth of
6\text{\,}\mathrm{GHz}
was used to measure the electron and hole drift velocities and to fit a high-field mobility model.
From pulsed UV laser measurements, saturation drift velocities parallel to the
c
-axis of
1.47\pm 2\text{\times}{10}^{7}\text{\,}\mathrm{cm}\text{\,}{\mathrm{s}}^{-1}
for electrons and
0.69\pm 4\text{\times}{10}^{7}\text{\,}\mathrm{cm}\text{\,}{\mathrm{s}}^{-1}
for holes were obtained, providing valuable input to TCAD simulations.
In order to understand the readout electronics in detail and to investigate the effect of parasitic impedances such as the wire bond inductance, simulations leveraging TCAD, Allpix
2
, and QUCS were performed, which agree well with the measurements.
Future studies will aim at applying the developed readout electronics to other detector types, such as silicon-based detectors and low-gain avalanche diodes (LGADs), and will exploit more targeted charge injection methods, such as two-photon absorption.
