Social support on social media may buffer associations of parental rejection with sexual minority young adults’ tobacco use outcomes
Authors: Katelyn F Romm, Mahalaxmi Das, Erin A Vogel
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Highlights•SMYA-reported parental rejection predicted higher odds of past-month cigarette use and greater nicotine dependence.•Social media-based social support buffered associations of SMYA-reported parental rejection with tobacco use outcomes.•Parent-reported warmth predicted lower odds of tobacco use, using fewer products, and less nicotine dependence.•Associations of parental warmth and tobacco use were not moderated by social media-based or in-person social support.Keywords: Tobacco use, Parental rejection, Parental warmth, Social support, Social media, Sexual minority young adults

Introduction

Despite declines in US young adults’ (YAs’) cigarette smoking over the past decade (Pierce et al., 2025), rates of other tobacco product use, particularly e-cigarettes, have increased (Bandi et al., 2023, Sanford et al., 2024). Young adulthood is a critical time for tobacco use initiation and escalation (Barrington-Trimis et al., 2020, Villanti et al., 2019), given greater independence, changes in parent-YA dynamics (e.g., less control/monitoring), and increased time spent with peers (Wood et al., 2018). Sexual minority-identifying (i.e., gay, lesbian, bisexual, pansexual, queer, and/or another non-heterosexual identity; SM) YAs report even higher cigarette and e-cigarette use rates (Truth Truth Initiative, 2024, Romm et al., 2022), along with polytobacco use (i.e., past-month use of ≥2 tobacco products) and greater nicotine dependence, relative to heterosexual YAs (Deyo et al., 2025, Romm et al., 2022).

According to minority stress theory, SMYAs commonly experience stress related to their SM identity, including discrimination and social rejection, which may be associated with coping-related tobacco use (Hatzenbuehler, 2009, Meyer, 2003). Parents may be a source of minority stress for SM youth and YAs (Mills-Koonce et al., 2018), with >50% of SM individuals reporting parental rejection (e.g., parental disdain, disapproval, invalidation, emotional withdrawal, shame, and/or disappointment) related to their SM identity (Keller, 2023). Parental rejection is associated with higher odds of tobacco and other substance use, whereas parental acceptance or warmth is associated with lower odds of such use among SM adolescents (Gamarel et al., 2020, Goldbach et al., 2014, Padilla et al., 2010, Parnes et al., 2023, Ryan et al., 2009) and YAs (Klein and Golub, 2016, Romm et al., 2023, 2024). Although most SMYAs and adults report coming out to their parents around age 17 (Dunlap, 2016), research suggests lasting impacts of parental rejection and support through young adulthood and adulthood (Fish et al., 2020, Puckett et al., 2014, Ryan et al., 2009).

Notably, social support is protective for SM individuals, as it may aid in coping with minority stress (Glick et al., 2025). Indeed, associations of minority stress, primarily operationalized as discrimination or internalized stigma, and SM youth and YAs’ health outcomes are weaker for those with higher vs. lower levels of social support (Fingerhut, 2018, Rogowska and Cisek, 2024, Trujillo et al., 2017). However, less research has focused on the role of social support on the impact of parental rejection on SMYAs’ tobacco use, specifically.

SM youth and YAs prioritize social support, particularly when faced with parental rejection (Fish, McInroy, et al., 2020). Extant research has primarily operationalized social support as in-person social support. This is a critical limitation, as SMYAs receive less social support when faced with parental rejection (Puckett et al., 2014, Ryan et al., 2010), and may turn to social media for such support (Berger et al., 2021, Berger et al., 2022, Charmaraman et al., 2021, Craig and McInroy, 2014, Karim et al., 2022, Lucero, 2017, Steinke et al., 2017). Although both in-person and social media-based social support may benefit SMYAs in the face of parental rejection, social media may facilitate connections with peers that these SMYAs may otherwise have difficulty making (Puckett et al., 2014, Ryan et al., 2010). Given the ubiquity of social media in SMYAs’ social environments, it remains imperative to examine the role of social support, as it occurs both in person and on social media, on associations of parental rejection and support with SMYAs’ tobacco use at a later timepoint. Doing so will shed light on future intervention targets to promote a supportive social environment among SMYAs who experience parental rejection and/or a lack of warmth and to ultimately reduce coping-related tobacco use.

The current study recruited parent-SMYA dyads to examine: 1) main effects of parent-reported and SMYA-reported parenting behaviors (i.e., rejection, warmth) and SMYA-reported social support (both in-person and social media-based) on SMYA-reported tobacco use outcomes (i.e., past-month cigarette use, e-cigarette use, number of products used, nicotine dependence) 6 months later; and 2) moderating effects of in-person and social media-based social support on associations of parenting behaviors with later tobacco use outcomes. Based on the aforementioned literature, it was hypothesized that greater parental rejection and less warmth would be associated with worse tobacco use outcomes 6 months later, but that these associations would be weaker for SMYAs with greater social media-based social support.

Methods

Participants and procedures

The current study analyzed 2 waves of survey data, which took place 6 months apart, among SMYAs (aged 18–22) and their parents. All SMYAs resided in Southern and Midwestern regions of the US (i.e., Oklahoma, Arkansas, Colorado, Kansas, Louisiana, Missouri, New Mexico, Texas), where tobacco use rates are highest (Cornelius et al., 2022). This study launched in October-December 2024 and was approved by the University of Oklahoma Health Sciences Institutional Review Board. These data have not been presented elsewhere.

The study was advertised on social media (e.g., Facebook, Instagram) and targeted both SMYAs and parents of SMYAs meeting eligibility criteria (i.e., YAs ages 18–22, residing in eligible states, and identifying as SM). Purposive, quota-based sampling was used to ensure ≥ 50% of SMYAs used a tobacco product in the past month, ∼50% reported male vs. female sex at birth, and ∼50% identified as racial and/or ethnic minority to meet the aims of the parent grant. After clicking on an ad, interested individuals were directed to a webpage with a study description, answered preliminary screening questions, and provided email/text message verification. Screening questions for YAs included age, state of residence, sex at birth, sexual identity, past-month tobacco use, and contact information for a parent or guardian who was aware of their sexual identity and willing to participate. Interested parents reported their child’s age, race, ethnicity, sex at birth, sexual identity, and the contact information for their SMYA child who was willing to participate. The dyad member (e.g., parent) of the interested individual deemed preliminarily eligible (e.g., SMYA) was then emailed a study description and contacted to confirm their relation to the individual deemed preliminarily eligible (e.g., SMYA). The interested dyad member (e.g., parent) was then emailed a link to the study consent form in Qualtrics. Those consenting then completed a screener in Qualtrics to confirm eligibility and were administered the baseline survey (∼20 min). Upon completion, the other dyad member (e.g., SMYA) was emailed a link to the study consent form in Qualtrics and directed to complete the online survey. Both parents and SMYAs were emailed a $10 Amazon e-gift card for participating. Fraud prevention efforts included withholding details of eligibility criteria before screening, examining data validity (e.g., duplicate internet provider addresses, e-mail addresses, phone numbers; illogical responses; survey completion time), and confirming validity of contact information before providing incentives (Heffner et al., 2021, Pratt-Chapman et al., 2021).

Of the 2,313 individuals who clicked on ads, 530 (22.9%) completed the pre-screening. Of those who completed the pre-screening, 469 (88.5%) were SMYAs and 61 (11.5%) were parents. Among SMYAs, 232 (49.5% of those who completed the pre-screening) were preliminarily eligible. Reasons for ineligibility included not providing contact information for a parent/guardian (N = 55, 21.9%) or email/text message verification (N = 120, 51.7%), age ineligibility (N = 32, 13.8%), state ineligibility (N = 23, 9.9%), reporting a heterosexual identity (N = 6, 2.6%), or already completing the survey (N = 1, 0.4%). Among parents, 7 (11.5% of those who completed the pre-screening) were preliminarily eligible. Reasons for ineligibility included not providing email/text message verification (N = 37, 68.5%) and indicating that their YA child was heterosexual (N = 3, 5.6%), fell outside of the eligible age range (N = 13, 24.1%), or resided in an ineligible state (N = 1, 1.9%).

The baseline survey was fully completed by 150 SMYAs and 175 parents, of which 150 represented parent-SMYA dyads. Among these 150 dyads, 140 (93.3%) completed the 6-month follow-up survey and were included in current analyses. The current analyses used baseline data assessing SMYA sociodemographic factors, parent- and SMYA-reported parenting behaviors, and SMYA-reported social support, as well as follow-up data assessing SMYA-reported tobacco use outcomes to understand the potential influence of parenting behaviors and social support on SMYAs’ tobacco use outcomes 6 months later.

Measures

Primary Predictors: Parenting Behaviors

SMYAs and parents reported on parental rejection and parental warmth. Among SMYAs, parental rejection was assessed with the 13-item Parental Rejection of Sexual Orientation scale (Kibrik et al., 2019). SMYAs rated the extent to which each item is true of the participating parent/guardian currently (1=Not at all true-5 = Very true, e.g., “My parent/guardian thinks that my sexual orientation is a wrong choice I made”, “My parent/guardian makes me feel that my sexual orientation has caused difficulties for them and my family”). Among parents, parental rejection was assessed with the 7-item Parental Acceptance of Sexual Orientation scale. Parents rated the extent to which each item is true of them in relation to the SMYA child (1=Not at all true-5 = Very true, e.g., “I offer my child support in their relationships”, “I have told my child I think they are intentionally hurting me”). Higher scores on each scale reflect higher levels of parental rejection (SMYA report: α = 0.95, parent report: α = 0.73) (Kibrik et al., 2019). Parental warmth was assessed with the 6-item Perception of Parents Scale (1=Not at all true-5 = Very true, e.g., “My parent(s) accepts me and likes me as I am”/ “I accept my child and like them as they are”). Higher scores indicate higher levels of parental warmth (SMYA report: α = 0.84, parent report: α = 0.84) (Grolnick et al., 1997).

Moderators: Social Support

SMYAs’ in-person social support was assessed with 8 items derived from the Patient-Reported Outcomes Measurement Information System (PROMIS). Participants were prompted to think about real-life, in-person social interactions when answering 8 items (1=Never-5 = Always; e.g., “I have someone who will listen to me when I need to talk”, “I have someone who makes me feel appreciated”). Higher scores reflect higher levels of in-person social support (α = 0.83) (Hahn et al., 2014). SMYAs’ social media-based social support was assessed using the 12-item Multidimensional Scale of Perceived Social Support adapted for social media asking participants to rate 12 items about social support received from interactions on social media (1=Strongly disagree-7=Strongly agree; e.g., “There is a special person around on social media when I am in need”, “There is a special person on social media with whom I can share my joys and sorrows”). Higher scores reflect higher levels of social media-based social support (α = 0.89) (Meshi & Ellithorpe, 2021).

Tobacco use outcomes

SMYAs reported the number of days in the past 30 they used cigarettes, e-cigarettes, large cigars, little cigars/cigarillos, smokeless tobacco, oral nicotine pouches, and hookah. Responses for each product were dichotomized into 0 (0 days) or 1 (≥1 day). We constructed 3 tobacco use variables: any current (i.e., past-month) vs. no cigarette use, any vs. no e-cigarette use, and total number of tobacco products used (0–7). Additionally, we assessed nicotine dependence using the Hooked on Nicotine Checklist, a 10-item instrument assessing the strength of nicotine dependence (e.g., “Have you ever tried to quit using tobacco or nicotine, but couldn’t?”; no/yes; α = 0.84) (Wellman et al., 2005). Scores were summed with higher scores indicating greater nicotine dependence severity. Tobacco products other than cigarettes and e-cigarettes were not examined as individual outcomes due to lower use prevalence.

Covariates

Covariates included SMYA age (continuous), sex at birth (female/male), sexual orientation (monosexual: gay/lesbian vs. bisexual+: bisexual, pansexual, queer), education (high school or less, some college, Bachelor’s degree or higher), living with the participating parent (no/yes), and SMYAs’ report of their parent’s tobacco use (no/yes) and discouragement of tobacco use (“Please indicate the extent to which your parent encourages you to not use cigarettes, e-cigarettes, and/or other tobacco products”; 0=Never-4 = Frequently). We also assessed SMYAs’ race (White, Black, another race), ethnicity (non-Hispanic/Hispanic), employment status (full-time, part-time, unemployed, student), and relationship status (single/in a relationship) in bivariate analyses. These variables were not included as covariates in primary analyses, as they were not associated with outcomes.

Data analysis

Analyses were conducted with SPSS v30. First, descriptive statistics characterized participants and examined response distributions. Next, bivariate analyses (i.e., independent-samples t-tests, Pearson’s correlations, one-way ANOVAs, chi-square tests) tested associations of parenting behaviors (i.e., rejection, warmth), social support (in-person, social media-based), and sociodemographic covariates with tobacco use outcomes 6 months later (i.e., past-month cigarette use, e-cigarette use, number of tobacco products used, nicotine dependence). Next, multivariable regressions were conducted separately for each outcome and examined associations of parenting behaviors, social support variables, and interactions between each parenting behavior and each social support variable with tobacco outcomes. To avoid multicollinearity, SMYA and parent reports of parenting behaviors were examined in separate models, as SMYA and parent report of rejection (r = 0.20) and warmth (r = 0.85) were significantly correlated at p < 0.05.

Results

Participant characteristics and bivariate associations of key study variables

Sample characteristics are reported in Table 1. Over half of participants were female, Black, currently students, and residing with the participating parent, and just under half reported at least a Bachelor’s degree. Most reported a monosexual identity and were not Hispanic. Almost half of participants indicated that their parent used tobacco in the past month. On average, SMYAs reported low-moderate levels of parental rejection, high levels of parental warmth, moderate levels of in-person social support, and moderate levels of social media-based social support. Parents also reported low-moderate levels of parental rejection and high levels of warmth. Regarding tobacco use at the 6-month follow-up survey, just under half of SMYAs reported past-month cigarette use and e-cigarette use, used fewer than 2 tobacco products on average, and reported low-moderate levels of nicotine dependence.

Bivariate analyses (Table 1) indicated that SMYAs reporting past-month cigarette and e-cigarette use (vs. no use) at follow-up experienced significantly higher levels of parental rejection (SMYA report only), lower levels of parental warmth (parent and SMYA report), and lower in-person social support at baseline. Both parent and SMYA reports of parental rejection were associated positively with number of tobacco products used and both reports of parental warmth were associated negatively with number of tobacco products used and nicotine dependence. SMYA-reported parental rejection and both in-person and social media-based social support were associated positively with nicotine dependence.

Associations of SMYA-reported parenting behaviors, social support, and tobacco use outcomes

Shown in Table 2, SMYA-reported parental rejection was associated with higher odds of past-month cigarette use and greater nicotine dependence, parental warmth was associated with lower odds of e-cigarette use, and social media-based social support was associated with higher odds of e-cigarette use, but less nicotine dependence 6 months later. In-person social support was no longer associated with tobacco use outcomes after accounting for covariates and other key study variables. Social media-based social support moderated associations of parental rejection with each tobacco use outcome. Specifically, rejection was associated with higher odds of cigarette and e-cigarette use, as well as using more tobacco products for those with lower (cigarette: aOR = 5.57, 95% CI = 2.23, 13.93; e-cigarette: aOR = 2.40, 95% CI = 1.01, 5.83; number of tobacco products: B = 0.72, SE = 0.19, p = .001), but not higher levels of social media-based social support (cigarette: aOR = 1.51, 95% CI = 0.54, 4.23; e-cigarette: aOR = 1.29, 95% CI = 0.49, 3.40; number of tobacco products: B = 0.08, SE = 0.24, p = .729). Parental rejection was also associated more strongly with greater nicotine dependence for those with lower (B = 2.05, SE = 0.29, p < .001) vs. higher (B = 1.47, SE = 0.37, p = .010) social media-based support.

Discussion

This study examined prospective associations of parenting behaviors and social support (both in person and on social media) with SMYAs’ tobacco use. As hypothesized, SMYAs who reported greater sexual identity-related parental rejection were more likely to report tobacco product use and had greater nicotine dependence at follow-up, but only if they received little social support on social media. SMYAs reporting greater parental warmth were less likely to report e-cigarette use at follow-up, regardless of social support.

Prior research has found that parental rejection of SM adolescents’ and SMYAs’ sexual identity is associated with higher odds of tobacco use, while warmth is associated with lower risk (Gamarel et al., 2020, Goldbach et al., 2014, Klein and Golub, 2016, Padilla et al., 2010, Parnes et al., 2023, Romm et al., 2023, Romm et al., 2024, Ryan et al., 2009). The Rejection Sensitivity model, as applied to SM individuals, posits that rejection from close others (e.g., parents) breeds anticipation of future rejection, the psychological distress associated with rejection from a close other, and efforts to avoid it (e.g., withdrawing from the relationship) (Baams et al., 2020, Feinstein, 2020). The resulting decline in social support may lead to psychological distress (Puckett et al., 2014) and SMYAs may use tobacco to cope with this distress (Li et al., 2024). Parent-reported parental rejection was not associated with SMYA tobacco use. Parents may have been hesitant to disclose not fully accepting their children, or their rejection may have been unintentional. Many parents have ambiguous responses when their child discloses an SM identity (e.g., avoiding the topic), and may not realize their child feels rejected (van Bergen et al., 2021). However, parental warmth, as reported by both SMYAs and parents, appears to have been protective against SMYAs’ tobacco use and nicotine dependence.

As hypothesized, the association of SMYA-reported parental rejection with tobacco use was attenuated among SMYAs who received high levels of social support on social media. In 2023, US 17-year-olds averaged 5.8 h per day (40.6 h per week) of social media use as they entered young adulthood (Rothwell, 2023). As such, social support received on social media may have strong impacts on tobacco use. Social media can be a lifeline for SMYAs who face rejection at home, as it enables connection with other SMYAs they may not have otherwise met (Escobar-Viera et al., 2020). Unlike in-person social support, social media-based social support can easily be targeted to a shared experience (e.g., being rejected for one’s sexual identity) by seeking out groups, profiles, or hashtags on social media.

Although social media-based social support may attenuate negative effects of parental rejection, it was also associated with higher odds of e-cigarette use. Social media content glamorizing e-cigarette use is abundant on platforms popular with young adults, such as TikTok, Instagram, and YouTube (Smith et al., 2023, Sun et al., 2021). Research consistently finds that greater exposure to tobacco content on social media is associated with tobacco use among adolescents and young adults (Donaldson et al., 2022). Moreover, experimental research has identified causal effects of pro-e-cigarette content on youth perceptions and intentions around e-cigarettes (Vogel et al., 2021). Major social media platforms restrict tobacco-related content (Kong et al., 2022), but such policies are inconsistently enforced (Donaldson et al., 2026, Jancey et al., 2023).

This study highlights the need for multi-level interventions to support SMYAs and their parents to prevent SMYA tobacco use. Parents may benefit from connecting with other parents of SMYAs on social media to learn more about SM identities and how to support their YA children (van Bergen et al., 2021). Parents and Friends of Lesbians and Gays (PFLAG), a long-standing group promoting understanding and acceptance, offers virtual community support groups for parents and other allies (Parents and Friends of Lesbians and Gays, 2026). Social media-based groups, moderated by a trained individual who can correct misinformation and remove hate speech, may offer a convenient option for parents who may be uncomfortable joining a video call. SMYAs may also benefit from moderated groups where they can receive support without encountering hate speech. Beyond the individual level, public service announcements correcting misperceptions of SM individuals that are tailored to a community’s characteristics and values (e.g., supporting one’s family) may help shift public opinion and help SMYAs feel more supported within their families and communities. At the policy level, increased protections for SM individuals can support SMYAs’ health and well-being (Movement Advancement Project, 2025). Stronger enforcement of social media platforms’ anti-tobacco policies is needed to protect SMYAs from exposure to harmful tobacco-related content.

Limitations

This study used two time points, six months apart, to examine prospective associations of parenting behaviors with SMYA tobacco use. This study design allows for temporal ordering, but not causal inference. Participants were asked to report on “real-life, in-person” social support to differentiate in-person support from social media-based social support. There may be overlap between the two; for example, an SMYA might communicate with a friend in-person, at school, via text or phone call, and via social media. Additionally, the sample was not representative of US SMYAs due to oversampling of specific groups (e.g., most SMYAs reported past-month tobacco use) and limited geographical reach. Estimates should not be interpreted as prevalence rates. Relatedly, because we recruited parent-SMYA dyads, our sample is likely not representative of SMYAs who experience strong levels of parental rejection. Parents who strongly reject their SMYA children are likely less willing to participate in a study aimed at better understanding parenting dynamics in relation to SMYAs’ tobacco use. Finally, we were unable to examine both parent- and SMYA-report of parenting behaviors within the same models and thus, cannot determine whether SMYA-report of parenting behaviors is predictive of tobacco use after accounting for parent-report. However, parent- and SMYA-report of parenting behaviors were significantly correlated, highlighting the quality of this dyadic data.

Conclusions

SMYAs who feel parental rejection based on their sexual identity may be at elevated risk for initiating or continuing tobacco use and developing nicotine dependence, which may contribute to disparities in tobacco use and tobacco-related disease observed between SM and heterosexual adults. Findings suggest the potential utility of incorporating social media into interventions for SMYAs, which may be a valuable outlet for SMYAs to obtain social support and ameliorate risk of coping-related tobacco use. It also remains important to shift public opinion, encourage parents to be accepting, and support SMYAs to the extent possible.
