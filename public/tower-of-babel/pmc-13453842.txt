Single-operator endoscopic lesser saphenous vein harvest using retractor-assisted leg suspension
Authors: Mahmoud Alshneikat, Ryan Carter, Rami Alaraj, Ahmed K Awad, Marijan J Koprivanac, Faisal G Bakaeen
Published: 2026

Endoscopic lesser saphenous vein harvesting setup with internal thoracic artery retractor.

Central Message

Using an internal thoracic artery retractor to vertically suspend the patient's leg provides convenient access to the lesser saphenous vein, allowing efficient single-operator harvesting.

The greater saphenous vein (GSV) remains the most commonly used conduit in coronary artery bypass grafting (CABG) because of its availability and ease of access.1 However, the GSV may be unavailable or unsuitable in several scenarios: if it was previously harvested for CABG, peripheral vascular procedures, or removed during vein stripping.2,3 It also may be of suboptimal quality as the result of varicosities. In these cases, the lesser saphenous vein (LSV) is a useful alternative when its bigger brother is not available. However, its use has been limited mainly because of harvesting challenges.

Historically, harvesting the LSV was performed through an open technique with a longitudinal incision along the posterior calf,4 but this approach led to greater morbidity and delayed mobility of patients. Endoscopic harvesting techniques for the LSV have been described to reduce these complications. However, most approaches require an assistant to stabilize or elevate the leg during harvest or necessitate placing the patient in the prone position, which can be cumbersome in the setting of CABG.

Supine endoscopic harvesting has been reported previously, with the operator positioned at the patient's waist.5 Our technique provides a single operator, who is positioned at the end of the table, easy access to the LSV through a small incision near the lateral malleolus.

Surgical Technique

Institutional review board approval was not required for this article. Informed consent was obtained from the patient. With the patient in supine position, a 2-cm incision is made approximately 2 cm lateral to the lateral malleolus toward, but not reaching, the Achilles tendon (Figure 1, A). Dissection is conducted until the LSV and the sural nerve are both visualized; the sural nerve is closer to the lateral malleolus (Figure 1, B). Next, the patient's foot is wrapped with a towel in a hammock-like fashion to hold the patient's leg. An internal thoracic artery retractor is then attached at the opposite side of the table, approximately 15 to 20 cm cranial to the patient's feet. Using the retractor, the patient's foot is raised to the desired height (Video 1). To optimize visualization during harvesting, the knee can be placed in slight flexion and the hip in slight external rotation. A scope is inserted through the incision and harvesting is completed in the same manner as harvesting the GSV (Figure 2). Blunt dissection is then performed, during which great care is taken to carefully visualize the sural nerve to prevent injury as it is pushed away with the fascia, and electrocautery is used to divide the LSV tributaries (Video 2). After the required length has been dissected, a second incision is made at that site. Blunt dissection is performed reaching the LSV, clamping it there. An Endoloop (J&J MedTech US, Johnson & Johnson) is used to ligate both the proximal and distal portions of the LSV, and the vein is cut with the harvesting system and removed. Standard vein prepping procedure is then conducted on the back table (Figure 3). Finally, the skin is closed before initiating cardiopulmonary bypass. The LSV can then be used in CABG in a similar manner to the GSV.

Discussion

Using the internal thoracic artery retractor to set up the patient's leg for LSV harvesting offers the following advantages: First, it allows for easy single-operator endoscopic harvesting through a small distal leg incision. This is important, because other methods of LSV endoscopic harvesting usually require an assistant to hold the leg. Second, the harvester stands at the end of the table so that sternotomy can be performed simultaneously. Third, harvesting is done in the exact manner for the GSV, which means that there will be no learning curve for harvesting the LSV.

Conclusions

Vertically suspending the patient's leg with an internal thoracic artery retractor provides convenient access to the LSV, enabling a single-operator endoscopic harvesting. This can help expand conduit options for CABG when the GSV is of suboptimal quality or is unavailable, and there is no suitable arterial conduit.
