Background

Unicornuate uterus (UU) with rudimentary horn (RH) is a rare congenital Müllerian duct anomaly, which results from incomplete fusion or development of the paramesonephric ducts during embryogenesis.

Among all Müllerian duct anomalies, which affect approximately 4.3% of the general female population, UU accounts for roughly 0.4% of cases, with up to 84% coexisting with a contralateral RH [1]. These prevalence figures are clinically important, as this condition elevates the risk of obstetric complications and gynecologic emergencies, necessitating increased clinical awareness.

Rudimentary horn pregnancy (RHP), a catastrophic complication of this anomaly, exhibits a wide epidemiological range, with reported incidences ranging from 1 in 10,000 in domestic clinical series to 1 in 76,000–150,000 in international cohorts [2]. This broad disparity may reflect differences in diagnostic capabilities, awareness of the condition, and completeness of reporting across different regions and healthcare systems.

This condition poses significant diagnostic challenges owing to its intricate anatomy and nonspecific clinical presentation. Consequently, more than 70% of cases go undetected until catastrophic rupture occurs.

The case report presents the diagnosis and management of a UU with RHP at our institution. It highlights critical imaging features and multidisciplinary therapeutic decision-making in this rare condition. By sharing our experience, we aim to enhance early recognition and outcomes for this life-threatening obstetric emergency.

Case presentation

General information

A 24-year-old nulligravid Asian female (G0P0) was admitted on 31 December 2021, with 48 days of amenorrhea and a newly detected pelvic mass. She had regular menses, with her last period on 13 November 2021, and denied typical pregnancy symptoms after amenorrhea onset. At 2 days before admission, scant brown vaginal discharge occurred, without abdominal pain or systemic symptoms. An external quantitative β-human chorionic gonadotropin (β-hCG) testing on 30 December 2021, demonstrated an elevated level of 11,537 mIU/mL. Transvaginal ultrasonography revealed a 2.5 × 1.8 cm heteroechoic right adnexal mass with a 1.2 × 0.5 cm anechoic area, suggestive of ectopic gestation. Notably, the patient had a prior ultrasound—diagnosed UU with a contralateral RH found during routine health screening. She declined the recommended surgical intervention.

Physical examination revealed minimal coffee-ground vaginal discharge, without lesions or anatomical abnormalities. The cervix had a closed os with mild ectropion and no motion tenderness. The uterus was anteverted, normotrophic, soft, and nontender. The right adnexal had mild tenderness and fullness on gentle compression without rebound guarding. The left adnexa was unremarkable.

Examine

Imaging findings (31 December transvaginal sonography)

The uterus was normomorphic, measuring 4.5 × 4.7 × 5.2 cm. The endometrium was laterally deviated and markedly thickened to 1.9 cm, with focal cavity distension (maximum transverse diameter 0.9 cm). No gestational sac or decidual reaction was seen (Fig. 1A). A 5.8 × 3.0 cm hypoechoic mass with homogeneous echogenicity was seen in the right lower uterine segment, broadly continuous with myometrium. At its periphery, a well-circumscribed 2.5 × 2.4 cm mixed-echogenicity satellite lesion was noted, showing a central area (0.9 × 0.8 cm) surrounding by a hyperechoic ring (Fig. 1B).

Synthesis of findings

The imaging constellation is consistent with: left UU with hematometra (endometrial thickening and cavity distension). Right noncommunicating RH containing a complex adnexal mass. RHP: supported by yolk sac visualization within the cystic component and peri-lesional hypervascularity, correlating with elevated β-hCG levels.

Diagnosis

Clinical diagnosis

The patient presented with a history of secondary amenorrhea. Based on serial quantitative β-HCG monitoring and transvaginal ultrasound findings, including a gestational sac with embryonic pole in the right uterine horn and the characteristic “banana-shaped” morphology of the left uterine cavity. The working diagnosis is a right RHP associated with a left UU (American Society of Reproductive Medicine Class II Müllerian anomaly).

Differential diagnoses

(i) Tubal ectopic pregnancy was considered. This was differentiated by the intra-myometrial location of the gestational sac and absence of ipsilateral Fallopian tube visualization on sonography. (ii) Angular pregnancy was ruled out owing to a myometrial mantle thickness less than 5 mm surrounding the gestational sac and the absence of interstitial line sign. (iii) Cornual heterotopic pregnancy was excluded following comprehensive evaluation of the endometrial cavity.

Treatment

Preoperative evaluation and surgical procedure

Following comprehensive preoperative evaluation, the patient underwent minimally invasive surgery on the same day. The surgery included three parts: 1) laparoscopic excision of right noncommunicating RH; 2) right partial salpingectomy (fimbrial preservation); 3) concurrent exploratory curettage.

Surgical specimen handling

The surgical specimen was resected en bloc using the cold scissors technique to minimize thermal artifact. Specimen retrieval was conducted via an endobag system, in line with oncological principles.

Histopathological examination

Histopathological examination confirmed the diagnosis of RHP. The key findings included abnormal placental implantation within the myometrium and the absence of chorionic components in the endometrial cavity. Microscopically, chorionic villi were seen infiltrating the myometrial vasculature, with focal implantation measuring 2.8 mm in depth. The endometrial curettage specimen showed pseudodecidualized stroma and no chorionic elements, further supporting the diagnosis.

Treatment outcomes

The surgical procedure was uneventful. Postoperatively, the patient received antiinflammatory therapy and regular wound dressings, followed by a satisfactory recovery leading to discharge on the fourth day. At the 6-month follow-up, she reported no subjective discomfort.

Discussion and conclusions

RHP is a rare and high-risk obstetric condition, frequently misdiagnosed owing to nonspecific symptoms resembling normal pregnancy. According to the 2023 ESHRE/ESGE classification, RHP occurs in a UU with a contralateral RH, most commonly noncommunicating (type II, 83% prevalence) [3], as confirmed in this case.

Clinical features vary with gestational age. In early pregnancy, symptoms may include abdominal pain, vaginal bleeding, or signs from hemorrhage; Mid-to-late pregnancy carries risks of fetal growth restriction, abnormal placentation, and uterine rupture. The risks of uterine rupture and hemorrhage increases with advancing gestational age. In this case, minimal brown vaginal discharge was observed, and placental implantation was confirmed postoperatively.

Preoperative diagnosis remains challenging, with an accuracy of just 22–29% [4], particularly with advancing gestational age [5]. Ultrasound is the first-line modality, especially when combined with 3D imaging and serum β-HCG monitoring [6]. Furthermore, magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) is reserved for inconclusive cases and offers the highest accuracy (98–100%) in detecting uterine anomalies[7, 8]. Emerging diagnostic modalities including contrast-enhanced ultrasound and molecular imaging are enabling novel therapies such as uterine artery embolization and advanced medical therapy, broadening future diagnosis options for uterine anomalies [9].

Early diagnosis, and surgical removal are pivotal in preventing severe complications, with maternal mortality declining dramatically from 23% to 0.5% today [10]. Some cases were originally misdiagnosed with ruptured ectopic pregnancy and underwent life-saving surgery after correct identification of RHP [11], underscoring the impact of improved diagnostic and treatment practices.

Treatment options for RHP include medical management with methotrexate (MTX) in stable patients, asymptomatic patients who decline surgery, or surgical intervention. Surgery provides both diagnosis and therapeutic purposes, with options including radical excision of the RH and ipsilateral Fallopian tube, or gestational tissue removal with uterine preservation. A combined MTX-laparoscopy strategy can reduce bleeding in noncommunicating cases [12]. It should be noted, however, that surgical removal of RH may lead to uterine damage and pelvic adhesions, potentially compromising subsequent pregnancy outcomes [6].

Pregnancies in RH rarely result in live births (∼2%) [13], contrasting with a 49.9% live birth rate in unicornuate uteri [14, 15]. A few live births from RHP have been documented historically, including the earliest report in 1946 [16]. In this present case, laparoscopic removal of the RH and ipsilateral Fallopian tube was performed. Diagnostic curettage was also conducted owing to suspected concurrent unicomuate pregnancy. At 6-month follow-up, the patient remained asymptomatic, though no subsequent conception had occurred. Comprehensive counseling was provided regarding future obstetric risk, including ectopic pregnancy and preterm birth.

In conclusion, RHP is a rare, high-risk condition frequently misdiagnosed due to nonspecific symptoms. Timely diagnosis relies on clinical suspicion and advanced imaging to prevent life-threatening hemorrhage. Surgical excision remains the standard treatment to avoid rupture and mitigate maternal risk. In addition, patient education regarding the nature of the condition, anticipated outcomes, and recognition of danger signs is essential to enhance vigilance and facilitate timely medical intervention.
