On the Implications of Verbose LLM Outputs: A Case Study in Translation Evaluation
Eleftheria Briakou, Zhongtao Liu, Colin Cherry, Markus Freitag

Abstract
This paper investigates the impact of verbose llm translations on evaluation. We first demonstrate the prevalence of this behavior across several llm outputs drawn from the wmt 2024 2024 general shared task on machine translation. We then identify the primary triggers of verbosity, including safety, copyright concerns, and insufficient context in short input queries. Finally, we show that ignoring this behavior unfairly penalizes more verbose llm s according to both automatic and human evaluations, highlighting the need to address this issue for more accurate future evaluations.



1
Introduction

Large Language Models (
llm
s) have transformed the landscape of
nlp
. In the past years, we have seen many
nlp
tasks being approached though prompting, where a natural language instruction and/or example demonstrations
guide an
llm
toward a desired output
Brown et al. (2020)
. While these demonstrations aim at implicitly structuring the
llm
’s output to align with traditional
nlp
tasks definitions,
llm
s might exhibit a tendency for verbosity and free-form responses, contextualizing the reasoning behind their choices
Wang and Zhou (2024)
. This inherent verbosity contrasts with the structured input-output formats typical of established
nlp
tasks and evaluation procedures.

Consider the task of machine translation (
mt
). Figure
1

llm
model responses when prompted to translate the given source texts (drawn from
wmt

2024

Kocmi et al. (2024)
). Unlike traditional
mt

llm
output might exhibit a more conversational approach:
refusing to translate
altogether,
providing multiple translations
, or even
offering reasoning insights
behind their translation choices. This tendency, which we term “verbosity”, poses significant challenges for evaluating
llm
s using existing evaluation frameworks. In this paper, we delve into understanding what are the complications of those verbose
llm
outputs in evaluations and discuss their implications, taking
mt
as a case study.

We conduct a thorough exploration of the verbosity factor using released outputs of
wmt

2024
data. Our exploration covers eight diverse language models, both open-source and proprietary, across eight language pairs. We begin by employing a lightweight prompting technique to annotate and analyse verbose outputs in the released
llm

llm
systems when evaluated using both automatic metrics and standardized human evaluation protocols of fine-grained error annotations. Our key findings are summarized as follows:

—

We establish that verbosity is a common trait among
llm
s, though its prevalence differs significantly across
llm
s and languages.
gpt
-4 and Aya
23
stand out as exceptions, exhibiting almost no verbosity in the
wmt

2024
tasks.

—

The most prevalent form of verbosity we observed across
llm
outputs is their refusal to translate. Our analysis points to various triggers: identifying potentially harmful or copyrighted content or encountering source text in non-natural language. Among the
llm
s studied, Gemini-1.5-Pro and Claude-3.5 are the ones demonstrating bigger cross-language consistency in refusing to translate. In contrast, Mistral-Large, Phi-3-Medium, and CommandR-
+
exhibit a more inconsistent pattern, choosing to refuse or translate depending on the language pair.

—

Beyond refusal to translate,
llm
s can exhibit a tendency to produce verbose outputs contextualizing their translation choices with alternative options or additional commentary. This behavior, observed in all but
gpt
-4 and Aya
23
, is by far most pronounced in Gemini-1.5-Pro, with the degree varying across language pairs. Our analysis reveals that short input segments lacking sufficient context are the primary reason for this verbose behavior.

—

Current evaluation metrics and human evaluation protocols fail to account for the nuances of contextualized translations, penalizing verbose
llm
s.

—

We discuss the need for both prompt-level interventions and adjustments to evaluation methodologies to ensure accurate and meaningful evaluation of
llm
s moving forward.

4
Discussion

The rise of verbose
llm
outputs raises questions regarding how this behavior should be addressed in evaluation. Two largely orthogonal interventions emerge: modifying
llm
outputs to conform to standardized evaluation paradigms, or adapting the evaluations themselves. The former could potentially be achieved through efforts to suppress verbosity
7
7

7

We have found that a simple prompt to “output the translation and nothing else” works quite well.
or to use structured output formats to isolate commentary and alternative translations from the core response. While these methods can improve evaluation fairness, they may not account for all verbosity-induced errors, especially refusal. Furthermore, they make no attempt to reward useful verbosity. Handling these scenarios potentially necessitates context-aware evaluations
Agrawal et al. (2024)
. The most immediate and challenging case is how to handle the refusal to translate, which should be considered when designing future evaluation datasets and protocols. We hope this paper raises awareness of the premises and pitfalls of evaluating
llm
outputs and inspires future studies to address them directly.

5
Conclusion

In this paper, we study the impact of the verbose behavior
llm
s may exhibit when prompted on translation evaluation. Based on extensive machine translation evaluations across seven language models and eight languages, we find that several
llm
s contextualize their responses with additional commentary or refuse to provide translations altogether. Ignoring this verbose behavior leads to over-penalizing more verbose
llm
s, such as
gemini
-
1.5
-
pro
. Furthermore,
llm
rankings vary significantly in both automatic and human evaluations when segments triggering potentially responses are excluded. These findings highlight the need for a shift in how we evaluate
llm
outputs for translation, and potentially other tasks.
